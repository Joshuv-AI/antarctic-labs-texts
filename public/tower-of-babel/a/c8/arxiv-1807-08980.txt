Abstract
The paper addresses a sequential changepoint detection problem for a general stochastic model, assuming that the observed data may be non-i.i.d. (i.e., dependent and non-identically distributed) and the prior distribution of the change point is arbitrary. Tartakovsky and Veeravalli (2005), Baron and Tartakovsky (2006), and, more recently, Tartakovsky (2017) developed a general asymptotic theory of changepoint detection for non-i.i.d. stochastic models, assuming the certain stability of the log-likelihood ratio process, in the case of simple hypotheses when both pre-change and post-change models are completely specified. However, in most applications, the post-change distribution is not completely known. In the present paper, we generalize previous results to the case of parametric uncertainty, assuming the parameter of the post-change distribution is unknown. We introduce two detection rules based on mixtures – the Mixture Shiryaev rule and the Mixture Shiryaev–Roberts rule – and study their asymptotic properties in the Bayesian context. In particular, we provide sufficient conditions under which these rules are first-order asymptotically optimal, minimizing moments of the delay to detection as the probability of false alarm approaches zero.

Introduction

Suppose
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are random variables observed sequentially, which may change statistical properties at an unknown point in time
\nu\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
, so that

X_{1},\dots,X_{\nu}
are generated by one stochastic model and
X_{\nu+1},X_{\nu+2},\dots
by another model. The value of the change point
\nu
is unknown and the fact of change
must be detected as soon as possible controlling for a risk associated with false detections.

More specifically, let
{\mathbf{X}}^{n}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
denote a sample of size
n
and
let
\{f_{\theta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of conditional densities of
X_{n}

given
{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1}
. If
\nu=\infty
, i.e., there is no change, then the parameter
\theta
is equal to
\theta_{0}
, so that
f_{\theta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})=f_{\theta_{0},n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
for all

n\geqslant 1
. If
\nu=k<\infty
, then
\theta=\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{0}
, so that
f_{\theta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})=f_{\theta_{0},n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
for
n\leqslant k
and

f_{\theta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})=f_{\theta_{1},n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
for
n>k

A sequential detection rule is a stopping time
T
with respect to an observed sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}

is an
integer-valued random variable, such that the event
\{T=n\}
, which denotes stopping and taking an action after observing the sample
{\mathbf{X}}^{n}

belongs to the sigma-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}=\sigma({\mathbf{X}}^{n})
generated by observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}

A false alarm is raised when the detection is declared before the change occurs,
T\leqslant\nu
.
The goal of the quickest changepoint detection problem is to develop a detection rule that stops as soon as possible after the real change occurs under a given risk of false alarms.

In early stages, the work focused on the i.i.d. case where
f_{\theta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})=f_{\theta}(X_{n})

II
The Shiryaev and Shiryaev–Roberts Mixture Rules

Let
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
denote the probability measure corresponding to the sequence of observations
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
when there is never a change (
\nu=\infty
) and,
for
k=0,1,\dots
and
\vartheta\in\Theta
, let
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\vartheta}
denote the measure corresponding to the sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
when
\nu=k<\infty
and
\theta=\vartheta

(i.e.,
X_{\nu+1}
is the first post-change observation), where
\theta\in\Theta
is a parameter (possibly multidimensional).
Further, let
p_{k,\vartheta}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})=p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|\nu=k,\theta=\vartheta)
denote a joint density of the sample
{\mathbf{X}}^{n}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, i.e., density
of the restriction
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\vartheta}^{(n)}
of the measure
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\vartheta}
to the sigma-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}=\sigma({\mathbf{X}}^{n})
with respect to a non-degenerate sigma-finite measure.
Let
\{g_{n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
and
\{f_{\theta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be two sequences of conditional densities of
X_{n}

given
{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1}
. With this notation, the general non-i.i.d. changepoint model, which we are interested in, can be written as

\begin{split}p_{\nu,\theta}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})&=p_{\infty}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(X_{i}|{\mathbf{X}}^{i-1})~~\text{for}~~\nu\geqslant n,\\
p_{\nu,\theta}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})&=\prod_{i=1}^{\nu}g_{i}(X_{i}|{\mathbf{X}}^{i-1})\times\prod_{i=\nu+1}^{n}f_{\theta,i}(X_{i}|{\mathbf{X}}^{i-1})\\
&~~\text{for}~~\nu<n.\end{split}

(1)

Therefore,
g_{n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
and
f_{\theta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
are the pre-change and post-change conditional densities.
Note that the post-change densities may depend on the change point
\nu
, i.e.,
f_{\theta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})=f_{\theta,n}^{(\nu)}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
for
n>\nu
. We omit the superscript
\nu
for brevity.
While often the pre-change density
g_{n}
belongs to the same parametric family as the post-change one
f_{\theta,n}
, i.e.,
g_{n}=f_{\theta_{0},n}
for some known value
\theta_{0}
,
this is not necessarily the case, so that we consider a more general scenario.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}_{k,\vartheta}
and
{\mathsf{E}}_{\infty}
denote expectations under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\vartheta}
and
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
, respectively.

The likelihood ratio (LR) of the hypothesis “
{\mathsf{H}}_{k}^{\vartheta}:\nu=k,\theta=\vartheta
” that the change occurs at
\nu=k
with the post-change parameter
\theta=\vartheta
against the no-change hypothesis
“
{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}:\nu=\infty
” based on the sample
{\mathbf{X}}^{n}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is given by the product

LR_{k,n}(\vartheta)=\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{f_{\vartheta,i}(X_{i}|{\mathbf{X}}^{i-1})}{g_{i}(X_{i}|{\mathbf{X}}^{i-1})},\quad n>k

and we set
LR_{k,n}(\vartheta)=1
for
n\leqslant k
.

Assume that the change point
\nu
is a random variable independent of the observations with prior distribution

\pi_{k}={\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k)
,
k=0,1,2,\dots
with
\pi_{k}>0
for
k\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. We will also assume that a change point may take negative values, which means that the change has
occurred by the time the observations became available. However,
the detailed structure of the distribution
{\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k)
for
k=-1,-2,\dots
is not important. The only value which matters is the total probability
q={\mathsf{P}}(\nu\leqslant-1)
of the change being in effect before the observations
become available.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(\vartheta)=f_{\vartheta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})/g_{n}(X_{i}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
. In
[
24
,
26
]
for detecting a change from

\{g_{n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}
to
\{f_{\vartheta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}
it was proposed to use the Shiryaev statistic

\begin{split}S_{n}(\vartheta)&=\frac{1}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}\left(q\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\vartheta)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\vartheta)\right),\\
&\quad n\geqslant 1,\quad S_{0}(\vartheta)=q/(1-q),\end{split}

(2)

where
\prod_{i=j}^{s}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}=1
for
s<j
.

When the value of the parameter is unknown there are two conventional approaches to overcome uncertainty – either to maximize or average over
\vartheta
.
The second approach is usually referred to as

Mixtures
. To be more specific, introduce a mixing measure
W(\theta)
,
\int_{\Theta}\rm{d}W(\theta)=1
, which can be interpreted as a prior distribution if needed.
Define the average (mixed) LR

\Lambda_{k,n}^{W}=\int_{\Theta}LR_{k,n}(\vartheta)\,\mathrm{d}W(\vartheta),\quad k<n

(3)

and the statistic

\begin{split}S_{n}^{W}&=\int_{\Theta}S_{n}(\vartheta)\,\mathrm{d}W(\vartheta)\\
&=\frac{1}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}\left(q\Lambda_{0,n}^{W}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\Lambda_{k,n}^{W}\right),\\
&\quad n\geqslant 1,~~S_{0}^{W}=q/(1-q),\end{split}

(4)

where
S_{n}(\vartheta)
is the Shiryaev statistic tuned to the parameter
\theta=\vartheta
defined in (
2
). We will call this statistic the
Mixture Shiryaev
(MS) statistic.

In the sequel, we study the MS detection rule that stops and raises an alarm as soon as the statistic
S_{n}^{W}
reaches a positive level
A
, i.e., the MS rule is nothing but
the stopping time

T_{A}=\inf\left\{n\geqslant 1:S_{n}^{W}\geqslant A\right\},

(5)

where
A>0
is a threshold controlling for the false alarm risk. In definitions of stopping times we always set
\inf\{\mathchoice{\mbox{\msbmx\char 63\relax}}{\mbox{\msbmx\char 63\relax}}{\mbox{\msbmvii\char 63\relax}}{\mbox{\msbmv\char 63\relax}}\}=\infty
.

Another popular statistic for detecting a change from
\{g_{n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}
to
\{f_{\vartheta,n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}
, which has certain optimality properties

[
10
,
11
,
9
,
33
]
, is the generalized Shiryaev–Roberts (SR) statistic

\begin{split}R_{n}(\vartheta)&=\omega LR_{0,n}(\vartheta)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}LR_{k,n}(\vartheta)\\
&=\omega\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\vartheta)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{i=k}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\vartheta),\quad n\geqslant 1\end{split}

(6)

with a non-negative head-start
R_{0}(\vartheta)=\omega
,
\omega\geqslant 0
.
The mixture counterpart, which we will refer to as the
Mixture Shiryaev–Roberts
(MSR) statistic, is

\begin{split}R_{n}^{W}&=\int_{\Theta}R_{n}(\vartheta)\,\mathrm{d}W(\vartheta),\\
&=\omega\Lambda_{0,n}^{W}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{k,n}^{W},\quad n\geqslant 1,~~R_{0}^{W}=\omega,\end{split}

(7)

and the corresponding MSR detection rule is given by the stopping time

\widetilde{T}_{A}=\inf\left\{n\geqslant 1:R_{n}^{W}\geqslant A\right\},

(8)

where
A>0
is a threshold controlling for the false alarm risk.

In Section
IV
, we show that the MS detection rule
T_{A}
is first-order asymptotically optimal, minimizing moments of the stopping time distribution for the low risk of false alarms
under very general conditions. In Section
V
, we establish asymptotic properties of the MSR rule, showing that it is also asymptotically optimal when the prior distribution
becomes asymptotically flat, but not in general.

III
Asymptotic Problems

Let
{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{\theta}({\mathcal{A}}\times\mathcal{K})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\,\pi_{k}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left({\mathcal{A}}\right)
denote the “weighted” probability measure and
{\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{\theta}
the corresponding expectation.

For
r\geqslant 1
,
\nu=k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and
\theta\in\Theta
, introduce the risk associated with the conditional
r
-th moment of the detection delay

{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,\theta}(T)={\mathsf{E}}_{k,\theta}\left[(T-k)^{r}\,|\,T>k\right].

(9)

In a Bayesian setting, the average risk associated with the moments of delay to detection is

\begin{split}{\bar{\mathcal{R}}}^{r}_{\pi,\theta}(T)&:={\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{\theta}[(T-\nu)^{r}|T>\nu]\\
&=\frac{{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}}\pi_{k}{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,\theta}(T){\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T>k)}{1-\mathsf{PFA}(T)},\end{split}

(10)

where

\mathsf{PFA}(T)={\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{\theta}(T\leqslant\nu)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k)

(11)

is the weighted probability of false alarm (PFA) that corresponds to the risk associated with a false alarm. Note that in (
10
) and (
11
) we used the fact that

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}(T\leqslant k)={\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k)
since the event
\{T\leqslant k\}
depends on the observations
X_{1},\dots X_{k}
generated by the pre-change probability measure
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
since
by our convention
X_{k}
is the last pre-change observation if
\nu=k
.

In Section
IV
, we are interested in the Bayesian optimization problem

\inf_{\{T:\mathsf{PFA}(T)\leqslant\alpha\}}\,{\bar{\mathcal{R}}}_{\pi,\theta}^{r}(T)\quad\text{for all}~\theta\in\Theta.

(12)

However, in general this problem is not manageable for every value of the PFA
\alpha\in(0,1)
. So we will focus on the asymptotic problem assuming that the PFA
\alpha
approaches zero.
Specifically, we will be interested in proving that the MS rule is first-order asymptotically optimal, i.e.,

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha,\pi)}{\bar{\mathcal{R}}}_{\pi,\theta}^{r}(T)}{{\bar{\mathcal{R}}}_{\pi,\theta}^{r}(T_{A})}=1\quad\text{for all}~\theta\in\Theta,

(13)

where
{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha,\pi)=\{T:\mathsf{PFA}(T)\leqslant\alpha\}
is the class of detection rules for which the PFA does not exceed a prescribed number
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
In addition, we will prove that the MS rule is uniformly first-order asymptotically optimal in a sense of minimizing the conditional risk (
9
) for all change point values
\nu=k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, i.e.,

\begin{split}&\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha,\pi)}{\mathcal{R}}_{k,\theta}^{r}(T)}{{\mathcal{R}}_{k,\theta}^{r}(T_{A})}=1\\
&\quad\text{for all}~\theta\in\Theta~\text{and all}~k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.\end{split}

(14)

In Section
VI
, we consider a “purely” Bayes problem with the average (integrated) risk, which is the sum of the PFA and the cost of delay proportional to the
r
-th moment of the detection delay and
prove that the MS rule is asymptotically optimal when the cost of delay to detection approaches
0
.

Asymptotic properties of the MSR rule
\widetilde{T}_{A}
will be also established.

(15)

and we say that

uniformly
r-
completely

\begin{split}&\hskip-14.22636pt\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{r-1}\sup_{0\leqslant k<\infty}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left\{\left|n^{-1}\lambda_{k,n}(\theta)-I_{\theta}\right|>\varepsilon\right\}<\infty\\
&\quad\text{for all}~~\varepsilon>0.\end{split}

(16)

Assume that there exists a positive and finite number
I_{\theta}
such that the normalized LLR
n^{-1}\lambda_{k,n+k}(\theta)
converges to
I_{\theta}

r-
completely.
Then it follows from

]
that when the parameter

is known the

)

To study asymptotic optimality we need certain constraints imposed on the prior distribution

The following two conditions are imposed on the prior distribution:

\mathbf{CP}\mathbf{1}
.
For some

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left|\log\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}\right|=\mu.

(17)

\mathbf{CP}{\mathbf{2}}

If

The following lemma provides the upper bound for the PFA of the MS rule.

\displaystyle\mathsf{PFA}(\widetilde{T}_{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\pi_{j}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(\widetilde{T}_{A}\leqslant j)

VI
Asymptotic Optimality with Respect to the Integrated Risk

Instead of the constrained optimization problem (
12
) consider now the unconstrained, “purely” Bayes problem with the loss function

L_{r}(T,\nu)={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{T\leqslant\nu\}}}+c\,(T-\nu)^{r}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{T>\nu\}}},

where
c>0
is the cost of delay per unit of time and
r\geqslant 1
. The unknown parameter
\theta
is now assumed random and the weight function
W(\vartheta)
is interpreted as the prior distribution of
\theta
.
The expected loss (integrated risk) associated with the detection rule
T
is given by

\rho_{\pi,W}^{c,r}(T)={\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}(T\leqslant\nu)+c\,\int_{\Theta}{\mathsf{E}}_{\vartheta}^{\pi}[(T-\nu)^{+}]^{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\vartheta).

Below we show that the MS rule
T_{A}
with a certain threshold
A=A_{c,r}
that depends on the cost
c
is asymptotically optimal, minimizing the integrated risk
\rho_{\pi,W}^{c,r}(T)

over all stopping times as the cost vanishes,
c\to 0
.

Define

{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi,W}^{r}(T)=\int_{\Theta}{\bar{\mathcal{R}}}_{\pi,\vartheta}^{r}(T)\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\vartheta).

Observe that, if we ignore
the overshoot, then
\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A})\approx 1/(1+A)
and that using approximation (
27
) we may expect that for a large
A

{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi,W}^{r}(T_{A})\approx\int_{\Theta}\left(\frac{\log A}{I_{\vartheta}+\mu}\right)^{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\vartheta)=(\log A)^{r}D_{\mu,r}

where

D_{\mu,r}=\int_{\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{I_{\vartheta}+\mu}\right)^{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\vartheta).

So for large
A
the integrated risk of the MS rule is approximately equal to

\displaystyle\rho_{\pi,W}^{c,r}(T_{A})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A})+c\,[1-\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A})]{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi,W}^{r}(T_{A})

\displaystyle\approx 1/A+c\,D_{\mu,r}(\log A)^{r}:=G_{c,r}(A).

The threshold value
A=A_{c,r}
that minimizes
G_{c,r}(A)

A>0
, is a solution of the equation

rD_{\mu,r}A(\log A)^{r-1}=1/c.

(44)

r=1

. Thus, it is reasonable to conjecture that threshold
A_{c,r}
optimizes the performance of the MS rule for a small
c
, and hence, makes this rule
asymptotically optimal as
c\to 0
.

\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}^{c}|\log\pi_{k}^{c}|^{r}}}{|\log c|^{r}}=0

(46)

and threshold
A=A_{c,r}
of the MS rule
T_{A}
is the solution of the equation

rD_{r}A(\log A)^{r-1}=1/c,

(47)

then, as
c\to 0
,

\begin{split}\inf_{T\geqslant 0}\rho_{\pi^{c},W}^{c,r}(T)\sim D_{r}\,c\,|\log c|^{r}\sim\rho_{\pi^{c},W}^{c,r}(T_{A_{c,r}}).\end{split}

(48)

Therefore, the MS rule
T_{A_{c,r}}
is asymptotically optimal as
c\to 0
.

(ii)
If the head-start
\omega_{c}
and the mean of the prior distribution
\bar{\nu}_{c}
approach infinity at such rate that

\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{{\log(\omega_{c}+\bar{\nu}_{c})}}{|\log c|}=0

(49)

and if
A=A_{c,r}
of the MSR rule
\widetilde{T}_{A}
is the solution of the equation

rD_{r}A(\log A)^{r-1}=(\omega_{c}b_{c}+\bar{\nu}_{c})/c,

(50)

then, as
c\to 0
,

\begin{split}\inf_{T\geqslant 0}\rho_{\pi^{c},W}^{c,r}(T)\sim D_{r}\,c\,|\log c|^{r}\sim\rho_{\pi^{c},W}^{c,r}(\widetilde{T}_{A_{c,r}}).\end{split}

(51)

Therefore, the MSR rule
\widetilde{T}_{A_{c,r}}
is asymptotically optimal as
c\to 0
.

Example 1

(
Detection of Signals with Unknown Amplitudes in a Multichannel System)
.

Assume there is a multichannel system with
N
channels (or alternatively an
N
-sensor system) and one is able to observe the output vector

X_{n}=(X_{n}^{1},\dots,X_{n}^{N})
,
n=1,2,\dots
, where the observations in the
i
th channel are of the form

X_{n}^{i}=\theta_{i}S_{n}^{i}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{n>\nu\}}}+\xi_{n}^{i},\quad n\geqslant 1.

Here
\theta_{i}S_{n}^{i}
is a deterministic signal with an unknown amplitude
\theta_{i}>0
that may appear at an unknown time
\nu
in additive noise
\xi_{n}^{i}
. For the sake of
simplicity, suppose that all signals appear at the same unknown time
\nu
.
Assume that noises
\{\xi_{n}^{i}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, are mutually independent
p^{i}
-th order Gaussian autoregressive processes AR
(p^{i})
, i.e.,

\xi_{n}^{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{p^{i}}\beta_{j}^{i}\xi_{n-j}^{i}+w_{n}^{i},\quad n\geqslant 1,

(54)

where
\{w_{n}^{i}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
are mutually independent i.i.d. normal
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
sequences and the initial values
\xi_{1-p^{i}}^{i}
,
\xi_{2-p^{i}}^{i}
,
\dots,\xi_{0}^{i}
are arbitrary random or
deterministic numbers, in particular we may set zero initial conditions
\xi_{1-p^{i}}^{i}=\xi_{2-p^{i}}^{i}=\cdots=\xi_{0}^{i}=0
.
The coefficients
\beta_{1}^{i},\dots,\beta_{p^{i}}^{i}
are known and all roots of the equations
z^{p^{i}}-\beta_{1}^{i}z^{p^{i}-1}-\cdots-\beta_{p^{i}}^{i}=0
are in the interior of the unit circle, so that
the AR(
p^{i}
) processes are stable. Let
\varphi(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\,e^{-x^{2}/2}
denote density of the standard normal distribution. Define the
p_{n}^{i}
-th order residual

\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i}=X_{n}^{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{p_{n}^{i}}\beta_{j}^{i}X_{n-j}^{i},\quad n\geqslant 1,

where
p_{n}^{i}=p^{i}
if
n>p^{i}
and
p_{n}^{i}=n
if
n\leqslant p^{i}
. Write
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N})
and
\Theta=(0,\infty)\times\cdots\times(0,\infty)
(
N
times).
It is easy to see that the conditional pre-change density is

g(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i})

and the post-change density is

f_{\theta}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i}-\theta_{i}\widetilde{S}_{n}^{i}),\quad\theta\in\Theta,

where
\widetilde{S}_{n}^{i}=S_{n}^{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{p_{n}^{i}}\beta_{j}^{i}S_{n-j}^{i}
. Obviously, due to the independence of the data across channels for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
n\geqslant 1
the LLR has the form

\lambda_{k,k+n}(\vartheta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\vartheta_{i}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{j}^{i}\widetilde{X}_{j}^{i}-\frac{\vartheta_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}(\widetilde{S}_{j}^{i})^{2}}{2}\right].

Under measure
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}
the random variables
\{\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i}\}_{n\geqslant k+1}

are independent Gaussian random variables with mean
{\mathsf{E}}_{k,\theta}[\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i}]=\theta_{i}\widetilde{S}_{n}^{i}

and unit variance, and hence, under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}
the normalized LLR can be written as

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k,k+n}(\vartheta,\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\vartheta_{i}\theta_{i}-\vartheta_{i}^{2}/2}{n}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}(\widetilde{S}_{j}^{i})^{2}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{n}\vartheta_{i}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{j}^{i}\eta_{j}^{i},\end{split}

(55)

where
\{\eta_{j}^{i}\}_{j\geqslant k+1}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, are mutually independent sequences of i.i.d. standard normal random variables.

Assume that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}|\widetilde{S}_{j}^{i}|^{2}=Q_{i},

(56)

where
0<Q_{i}<\infty
. This is typically the case in most signal processing applications, e.g., for harmonic signals
S_{n}^{i}=\sin(\omega_{i}n+\phi_{n}^{i})
.
Then for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\theta\in\Theta

\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}-\text{a.s.}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}}{2}=I_{\theta},

so that condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
holds. Furthermore, since all moments of the LLR are finite it is straightforward to show that conditions (
52
) and (
53
), and hence, conditions

{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{3}
hold for all
r\geqslant 1
. Indeed, using (
55
), we obtain that
I(\vartheta,\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\vartheta_{i}\theta_{i}-\vartheta_{i}^{2}/2)Q_{i}
and for any
\delta>0

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left(\sup_{\vartheta\in[\theta-\delta,\theta+\delta]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k,k+n}(\vartheta)-I(\vartheta,\theta)\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left(|Y_{k,n}(\theta)|>\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\right),

where

Y_{k,n}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{j}^{i}\eta_{j}^{i},\quad n\geqslant 1

is the sequence of normal random variables with mean zero and variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}(\widetilde{S}_{j}^{i})^{2}
,
which by (
56
) is asymptotic to
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}
.
Thus, for a sufficiently large
n
there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that
\sigma^{2}_{n}\leqslant\delta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}
and we obtain that for all large
n

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left(\sup_{\vartheta\in[\theta-\delta,\theta+\delta]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k,k+n}(\vartheta)-I(\vartheta,\theta)\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathsf{P}}\left(|\hat{\eta}|>\frac{\delta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{\delta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}}\right),

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathsf{P}}\left(|\hat{\eta}|>\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{\delta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}}\right),

where
\hat{\eta}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
is a standard normal random variable. Hence, for all
r\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,n^{r-1}\,\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\,\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{c}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\Big(\sup_{\vartheta\in[\theta-\delta,\theta+\delta]}\Big|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k,k+n}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle-I(\vartheta,\theta)\Big|>\varepsilon\Big)<\infty,

which implies (
53
) for all
r\geqslant 1
.

Thus, the MS rule minimizes as
\alpha\to 0
all positive moments of the detection delay. All asymptotic assertions for the MSR rule presented in Section
V
also hold with

I_{\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}/2
. In particular, by Theorem
4
, the MSR rule is also asymptotically optimal for all
r\geqslant 1
if the prior distribution of the
change point is either heavy-tailed or asymptotically flat.

Since by condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
the MS and MSR procedures are asymptotically optimal for almost arbitrary mixing distribution
W(\theta)
, in this example it is most convenient to
select the conjugate prior,
W(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}F(\theta_{i}/v_{i})
, where
F(y)
is a standard normal distribution and
v_{i}>0
, in which case the MS and MSR statistics can be computed explicitly.

[

.

Yet another challenging application area where the multichannel model is useful is cyber-security

Example 2

(
Detection of Changes in a Hidden Markov Model)
.

The following example, which deals with a two-state hidden Markov model with i.i.d. observations in each state may be of interest, in particular, for rapid detection and tracking of
sudden spurts and downfalls in activity profiles of terrorist groups that could be caused by various factors such as changes in the organizational dynamics of terrorist groups, counterterrorism
activity, changing socio-economic and political contexts, etc. In
[
52
]
, based on the analysis of real data from
Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia

(FARC) terrorist group from Colombia (RDWTI) it was shown that two-state HMMs can be
recommended for detecting and tracking
sudden changes in activity profiles of terrorist groups.
The HMM framework provides good explanation capability of past/future activity
across a large set of terrorist groups with different ideological attributes.

Specifically, let
\upsilon_{n}\in\{1,2\}
be a two-state Markov chain with the transition matrix

\displaystyle[P_{\theta}(\upsilon_{n-1}=i,\upsilon_{n}=l)]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1-\beta_{\theta}&\beta_{\theta}\\
\gamma_{\theta}&1-\gamma_{\theta}\end{array}\right]

and stationary initial distribution
{\mathsf{P}}_{\theta}(\upsilon_{0}=2)=1-{\mathsf{P}}_{\theta}(\upsilon_{0}=1)=\pi_{\theta}(2)=\gamma_{\theta}/(\beta_{\theta}+\gamma_{\theta})
for some

\beta_{\theta},\gamma_{\theta}\in[0,1]
, where the parameter
\theta
equals
\theta_{0}
in the pre-change mode (
\theta_{0}
is known) and
\theta\in\Theta
in the post-change mode (unknown).
Suppose that conditioned on
\upsilon_{n}
the observations
X_{n}
are i.i.d. with densities
p_{\theta}(X_{n}|\upsilon_{n}=l)=p_{\theta}^{(l)}(X_{n})
,
l=1,2
.

Introduce the probabilities
P_{\theta,n}:={\mathsf{P}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}^{n},\upsilon_{n}=2)
and

\widetilde{P}_{\theta,n}:={\mathsf{P}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}^{n},\upsilon_{n}=1)
. Straightforward computations show that for
n\geqslant 1

\displaystyle P_{\theta,n}

\displaystyle=\left[P_{\theta,n-1}\,(1-\gamma_{\theta})+\widetilde{P}_{\theta,n-1}\,\beta_{\theta}\right]\,p_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n});

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\theta,n}

\displaystyle=\left[P_{\theta,n-1}\,\gamma_{\theta}+\widetilde{P}_{\theta,n-1}\,(1-\beta_{\theta})\right]\,p_{\theta}^{(1)}(X_{n})

with initial values
P_{\theta,0}=\pi_{\theta}(2)
and
\widetilde{P}_{\theta,0}=\pi_{\theta}(1)=1-\pi_{\theta}(2)
. Denote
p_{0,\theta}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})=p_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})
and
p_{\infty}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})=p_{\theta_{0}}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})
. Since
p_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})=P_{\theta,n}+\widetilde{P}_{\theta,n}
we obtain that

p_{k,\theta}({\mathbf{X}}_{k+1}^{n}|{\mathbf{X}}_{0}^{k})=\frac{P_{\theta,n}+\widetilde{P}_{\theta,n}}{P_{\theta,k}+\widetilde{P}_{\theta,k}}

and

\lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)=\log\left(\frac{P_{\theta,k+n}+\widetilde{P}_{\theta,k+n}}{P_{\theta_{0},k+n}+\widetilde{P}_{\theta_{0},k+n}}\cdot\frac{P_{\theta_{0},k}+\widetilde{P}_{\theta_{0},k}}{P_{\theta,k}+\widetilde{P}_{\theta,k}}\right).

For the sake of simplicity, consider now the symmetric case where
\beta_{\theta}=\gamma_{\theta}=1/2
for all
\theta\in\Theta+\theta_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle P_{\theta,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}p_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left[p_{\theta}^{(1)}(X_{n})+p_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n})\right],

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\theta,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}p_{\theta}^{(1)}(X_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left[p_{\theta}^{(1)}(X_{n})+p_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n})\right]

and we obtain that the LLR is

\lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)=\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+n}\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta}^{(1)}(X_{i})+p_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{i})}{p_{\theta_{0}}^{(1)}(X_{i})+p_{\theta_{0}}^{(2)}(X_{i})}\right).

Condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
holds with

I_{\theta}=\int\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta}^{(1)}(x)+p_{\theta}^{(2)}(x)}{p_{\theta_{0}}^{(1)}(x)+p_{\theta_{0}}^{(2)}(x)}\right)\frac{p_{\theta}^{(1)}(x)+p_{\theta}^{(2)}(x)}{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

being the Kullback–Leibler information number since by the SLLN
n^{-1}\lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)\to I_{\theta}

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}
-a.s. (assuming that
I_{\theta}<\infty
).
Condition (
52
) usually holds if the
(r+1)
-th absolute moment of the increment of the LLR is finite:

\displaystyle\int\left|\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta}^{(1)}(x)+p_{\theta}^{(2)}(x)}{p_{\theta_{0}}^{(1)}(x)+p_{\theta_{0}}^{(2)}(x)}\right)\right|^{r+1}\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[p_{\theta}^{(1)}(x)+p_{\theta}^{(2)}(x)\right]\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

This is the case, for example, if the observations are Gaussian with unit variance and different mean values in
pre- and post-change modes as well as for different states, i.e.,

p_{\theta}^{(l)}(y)=\varphi(y-\mu_{\theta}^{(l)})
(
\theta=\theta_{0}
or
\theta\neq\theta_{0}
,
l=1,2
). It is easily verified that the Kullback–Leibler number

I_{\theta}
is finite and that condition (
52
) is satisfied for all
r\geqslant 1
. Therefore, in this case, the
MS and MSR detection rules are asymptotically optimal, minimizing asymptotically all positive moments of the detection delay.

VIII
Concluding Remarks

1. In the case where the increments
\{\Delta\lambda_{i}(\theta)\}
of the LLR

\lambda_{k,n}(\theta)=\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\Delta\lambda_{i}(\theta)
are independent (but not necessarily identically distributed), condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
in Theorem
1
and
Theorem
3
and condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{3}
in Theorem
2
and Theorem
4
can be relaxed in the following condition:
for all
\ell\geqslant k
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left(\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Gamma_{\delta,\theta}}\lambda_{\ell,\ell+n}(\vartheta)\,\mathrm{d}W(\vartheta)<I_{\theta}-\varepsilon\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(57)

More specifically, the MS rule asymptotically minimizes all moments of the delay to detection under the right-tail and left-tail conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
and (
57
). Also, assertions of
Theorem
2
and Theorem
4
for the MSR rule hold for all
r\geqslant 1
under conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
and (
57
).
The proof can be built by considering the cycles

[k+(n-1)N_{A},k+nN_{A}]
,
n=1,2,\dots
of the length
N_{A}=1+\lfloor\log A/(I_{\theta}+\mu-\varepsilon)\rfloor
and slightly modifying of the technique developed by
Tartakovsky
[
26
]
in the case of complete knowledge of the post-change distribution.

2. Since we do not assume a class of models for the observations such as Gaussian, Markov or HMM and build the decision statistics on the LLR process

\lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)
,
it is natural to impose conditions on the behavior of
\lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)
, which is expressed by conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{3}
, related to the law of large numbers for the LLR and
rates of convergence in the law of large numbers. The assertions of Theorems
1
–
4
hold if

n^{-1}\lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)
and
n^{-1}\log\Lambda_{k,k+n}^{W}
converge uniformly
r
-completely to
I_{\theta}
under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}
, i.e., when for all
\varepsilon>0
and
\theta\in\Theta

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,n^{r-1}\,\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)-I_{\theta}\right|>\varepsilon\right)<\infty,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,n^{r-1}\,\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\log\Lambda_{k,k+n}^{W}-I_{\theta}\right|>\varepsilon\right)<\infty.

(58)

However, verifying the
r
-complete convergence condition (
58
) for the weighted LLR
\log\Lambda_{k,k+n}^{W}
is typically much more difficult than checking
conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{3}
for the local values of the LLR in the vicinity of the true parameter value. For the simple post-change hypothesis, sufficient conditions for the class of
ergodic Markov models
are given in
[
29
]
and for HMMs in
[
27
]
. In these cases, the LLR process is a Markov random walk and one of the key conditions is finiteness of the

(r+1)
-th moment of its increment.

3. As expected, the results indicate that the MSR rule is not asymptotically optimal when the prior distribution of the change point has an exponential tail (i.e.,
\mu>0
),
but it is asymptotically optimal for heavy-tailed prior distributions (i.e.,
\mu=0
) and also when
\mu\to 0
with a certain rate.

4. The results show that first-order asymptotic optimality properties of the MS and MSR procedures hold for practically arbitrary weight function
W(\theta)
,
in particular for any prior that has strictly positive
values on
\Theta
. Therefore, the selection of
W(\theta)
can be based solely on the computational aspects. The conjugate prior is typically the best choice when possible. However,
if the parameter is vector and the parameter space is intricate, constructing mixture statistics may be difficult. In this case, discretizing
the parameter space and selecting the prior
W(\theta=\theta_{i})
concentrated on discrete points
\theta_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, suggested and discussed in
[
53
]

for the hypothesis testing problems, is perhaps the best option.
Then one can easily compute the MS and MSR statistics (as long as the LR
\Lambda_{k,k+n}(\theta)
can be computed) at the expense of losing optimality between the points
\theta_{i}
since
the resulting discrete versions are asymptotically optimal only at the points
\theta_{i}
.

Proof:

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
, define
N_{\alpha}=N_{\alpha}(\varepsilon,\delta,\theta)=(1-\varepsilon)|\log\alpha|/(I_{\theta}+\mu+\delta)
. By the Chebyshev inequality,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{k,\theta}^{r}(T)\geqslant{\mathsf{E}}_{k,\theta}[(T-k)^{+}]^{r}

\displaystyle\geqslant N_{\alpha}^{r}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}(T-k>N_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\geqslant N_{\alpha}^{r}\left[{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}(T>k)-{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}(k<T<k+N_{\alpha})\right],

where
