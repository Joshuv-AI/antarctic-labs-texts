On Quantitative Algebraic Higher-Order Theories
Ugo Dal Lago, Furio Honsell, Marina Lenisa, Paolo Pistone

Abstract
We explore the possibility of extending Mardare et al quantitative algebras to the structures which naturally emerge from Combinatory Logic and the λ \lambda -calculus. First of all, we show that the framework is indeed applicable to those structures, and give soundness and completeness results. Then, we prove some negative results which clearly delineate to which extent categories of metric spaces can be models of such theories. We conclude by giving several examples of non-trivial higher-order quantitative algebras.

Introduction

One way of seeing program semantics is as the science of program equivalence.
Each way of giving semantics to programs implicitly identifies which programs
are equivalent. Similarly, a notion of program equivalence can be seen as a way of
attributing meaning to programs (namely, the equivalence class to which the
program belongs). This point of view makes semantics a powerful source of ideas and techniques
for program transformation and program verification, with
the remarkable advantage that such techniques
can be defined in a
compositional and modular way.

However, there are circumstances in which equivalences between programs, being purely
dychotomous,
are just not informative enough: two programs are either equivalent or
not, period.
No further quantitative or causal
information can be extracted from two programs which are
slightly
different, although not equivalent. Furthermore, as program equivalences are usually congruences,
and therefore preserved by any context, programs that only differ in

peculiar
circumstances are also just non-equivalent ones. For these reasons, methods
alternative to program equivalence have to be looked for in all (very common)
situations involving transformations that replace a program by one which is
only
approximately
equivalent
[
29
]
, or when the
specifications are either not precise or not to be met precisely (e.g. in
modern cryptography
[
25
]
, in which most security properties
hold in an approximate sense, namely modulo a negligible probability).

The considerations above led the scientific community to question the
possibility of broadening the scope of program semantics from a science of equivalences to a
science of
distances
between programs. By the way, the possibility of interpreting programs
in domains having a metric structure has been known since the
1990s
[
17
,
18
]
. Recently, Mardare, Panangaden, and Plotkin have
introduced a notion of quantitative algebra
[
27
]
that generalizes usual equational reasoning to a setting in which the compared entities can be at a
certain distance. In this way, various notions of quantitative algebra have been shown to be
captured through a formal system,
à la
Birkhoff
[
8
]
.

Still, when the programs at hand are higher-order functional programs, the construction of a metric
semantics faces several obstacles. First, it is well-known that
the category
\mathrm{Met}
of metric spaces and non-expansive maps, providing the standard setting of the approaches just recalled, is
not
a model of the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus (more precisely, it is not cartesian closed).
Furthermore, finding relevant
sub-
categories of
\mathrm{Met}
enjoying enough structure to model higher-order programs can lead to trivial (i.e. discrete) models, and several (mostly negative) results have remained so far in the
folklore
(with a few notable exceptions, e.g.
[
20
]
).

In this paper we bite the tail of the dragon:
we apply
the framework of quantitative equational theories and
algebras from Mardare
et al
to the cases of
combinatory logic and the
\lambda
-calculus, and we try to highlight features and obstacles in the construction of higher-order quantitative algebras, at the same time showing the existence of
several interesting models.

There are various reasons for exploring combinatory algebras,
i.e.
applicative structures where the
\xi
-rule fails. The first is that these structures naturally arise in various contexts, most notably in Game Semantics and in particular in the “Geometry of Interaction”-construction as introduced by Girard and Abramsky, see
[
1
]
. The
\xi
-rule can then be enforced only by introducing a rather complex notion of equivalence relation, whose fine structure is usually rather awkward to grasp. The second reason is that combinatory algebras, being indeed algebras, might appear at first sight to be amenable straightforwardly in the first order framework of quantitative algebras of Mardare
et al
. We show that this is illusory, because the impact of the basic assumption that constructors are non-expansive,
i.e.
Axiom NExp (see Section
4
) is very strong, even in a context which could appear to be algebraically well-behaved. Finally, even if it is convenient to assume the
\xi
-rule, in reasoning on higher-order programming languages, showing that it holds in implementations is not at all immediate and, when side-effects are present, it needs to be carefully phrased.

The contributions of this paper are threefold:

(i) We introduce, following the framework defined by Mardare
et al
, quantitative
generalizations of the standard notions of
weak
\lambda
-theories
and

\lambda
-theories

[
6
]
, as well as their corresponding notions of algebra.
This is in
Section
3
, Section
4
, and Section
5
,
respectively.

(ii) We study properties and examples of algebras for such theories, as suitable sub-categories of
\mathrm{Met}
.
In particular, we highlight the relevance of
ultra-
metric and
injective
metric spaces in the construction of non-trivial (i.e. non discrete) algebras.
Some examples are discussed through Section
2
and Section
5
, further properties and examples are in Section
6
.

(iii) Finally, we discuss algebras obtained by relaxing the conditions from Mardare
et al
: either by
replacing metrics by
partial
metrics
[
9
,
32
]
,
i.e.
generalized metrics in which self-distances
d(x,x)
need not be zero, or by relaxing the non-expansiveness condition and introducing a class of
approximate
quantitative algebras.
This is in Section
7
and Section
8
.

2
Preliminaries on Metric Spaces

In this section we discuss a few properties of metric spaces and their associated categories, which provide the general setting for quantitative algebras in the sense of Mardare
et al

In particular, we recall the definition of ultra-metric spaces, as well as
partial ultra-metric spaces

[
9
,
32
]
. The latter is a class of generalized metric spaces in which self-distances
a(x,x)
are not required to be
0
but only smaller than any distance of the form
a(x,y)
.

Definition 1
.

A pair
(X,a)
formed by a set
X
and a function

a:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
is called:

(i) a
pre-metric space
if it satisfies, for all
x,y\in X
,
a(x,x)=0

\mathrm{(refl)}
and
a(x,y)=a(y,x)

\mathrm{(symm)}
;
(ii) a
(pseudo-)metric space
if it satisfies
\mathrm{(refl)}
,
\mathrm{(symm)}
, and, for all
x,y,z\in X
,
a(x,y)\leq a(x,z)+a(z,y)

\mathrm{(trans)}
;
(iii) an
ultra-metric space
if it satisfies
\mathrm{(refl)}
,
\mathrm{(symm)}
and, for all
x,y,z\in X
,
a(x,y)\leq\max\{a(x,z),a(z,y)\}

\mathrm{(trans^{*})}
;
(iv)
a
partial ultra-metric space
if it satisfies
\mathrm{(symm)}
,
\mathrm{(trans^{*})}
and, for all
x,y\in X
,

a(x,y)\geq a(x,x),a(y,y)

\mathrm{(refl^{*})}
.

Since all metrics we consider are “pseudo”, from now on we will omit this prefix.
Observe that an ultra-metric space is also a metric space.
Moreover, a partial ultra-metric space
(X,a)
also yields an ultra-metric space
(X,a^{*})
, with
a^{*}(x,y)=0
if
x=y
and
a^{*}(x,y)=a(x,y)
otherwise.
Usually, partial metric spaces are defined using a stronger version of the
triangular law, given by
a(x,y)\leq a(x,z)+a(z,y)-a(z,z)
. However, for partial
ultra-
metrics this condition is equivalent to
\mathrm{(trans^{*})}
(see e.g.
[
32
]
).

The standard morphisms between metric (ultra-metric, partial ultra-metric) spaces
(X,a)
and
(Y,b)
are the
non-expansive
functions, i.e. those functions

f:X\to Y
such that for all
x,y\in X
,
b(f(x),f(y))\leq a(x,y)
.
We let
\mathrm{Met}
(resp.
\mathrm{UMet}
,
\mathrm{PUMet}
) indicate the category of metric spaces
(resp. ultra-metric spaces, partial ultra-metric spaces) and non-expansive maps.
All categories
\mathrm{Met},\mathrm{UMet}
and
\mathrm{PUMet}
are cartesian, the product of
(X,a)
and
(Y,b)
being given by
(X\times Y,\max\{a,b\})
.
In
\mathrm{UMet}
and
\mathrm{PUMet}
the cartesian functors
\{-\}\times X
have right-adjoints given, respectively, by

(\mathrm{UMet}(X,\{-\}),\Phi_{a,\{-\}})
and
(\mathrm{PUMet}(X,\{-\}),\Phi_{a,\{-\}})
, where for all metric space
(Y,b)
,

\Phi_{a,b}(f,g)=\sup\{b(f(x),g(x))\mid x\in X\}
.
For this reason, both categories are cartesian closed.

By contrast,
\mathrm{Met}
is
not
cartesian closed.
Indeed, the functor
(\mathrm{Met}(X,\{-\}),\Phi_{a,\{-\}})
is right-adjoint in
\mathrm{Met}
(and thus also in
\mathrm{UMet}
) to the functor
(X\times\{-\},a+\{-\})
, but for all metric spaces
(Y,b)
,
(X\times Y,a+b)
is isomorphic to the cartesian product
(X\times Y,\max\{a,b\})
only when
X
and
Y
are ultra-metrics.
On the other hand, the exponential of
(X,a)
and
(Y,b)
in
\mathrm{Met}
, if it exists, is necessarily of the form

(\mathrm{Met}(X,Y),\Xi_{a,b})
, where

\displaystyle\Xi_{a,b}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\delta\mid\forall x,y\in X\ \max\{\delta,a(x,y)\}\geq b(f(x),g(y))\}

We use the Greek letter
\Xi
, since, as we’ll see, this metric is tightly related to the interpretation of the “
\xi
-rule” of the
\lambda
-calculus. Notice that in general
\Xi_{a,b}
is only a pre-metric. Indeed, the category of pre-metric spaces and non-expansive functions is cartesian closed, while the exponential of
(X,a)
and
(Y,b)
exists in
\mathrm{Met}
precisely when
\Xi_{a,b}
further satisfies
\mathrm{(trans)}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
(X,a)
and
(Y,b)
be metric spaces.
The exponential of
(X,a)
and
(Y,b)
in
\mathrm{Met}
, if it exists, is isomorphic to

(\mathrm{Met}(X,Y),\Xi_{a,b})
.

Proof.

By definition
(Z,c)
is the exponential of
(X,a)
of
(Y,b)
iff there exists a family of isomorphisms

(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{C},\mathbf{Ev}_{C}):\mathrm{Met}(C\times X,Y)\to\mathrm{Met}(C,Z)
, natural in
C
.
From this it follows that we can identify, up to bijection,
Z
with
\mathrm{Met}(X,Y)
, and we can let
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{C}(h)(z)(x)=h(z,x)
and
\mathbf{Ev}_{C}(k)(z,x)=k(z)(x)
.

It remains to show that
c(f,g)=\Xi_{a,b}(f,g)
, for all
f,g\in\mathrm{Met}(X,Y)
.
Let
\mathbf{Ev}_{X,Y}:=\mathbf{Ev}_{\mathrm{Met}(X,Y)}(\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{Met}(X,Y)})\in\mathrm{Met}(\mathrm{Met}(X,Y)\times X,Y)
.
Since
\mathbf{Ev}_{X,Y}
is non-expansive we have, for all
f,g\in\mathrm{Met}(X,Y)
and
x,y\in X
,

b(\mathbf{Ev}_{X,Y}(f)(x),\mathbf{Ev}_{X,Y}(g)(y))\leq\sup\{c(f,g),a(x,y)\}
, which shows that
c(f,g)\geq\Xi_{a,b}(f,g)
.

For the converse direction, let

\delta:=\Xi_{a,b}(f,g)
and

Q=(\{0,1\},d^{\delta})
be the two points metric space given by
d^{\delta}(p,q)=\delta
.
Let
h:Q\times X\to Y
be the function given by
h(0,x)=f(x)=\mathbf{Ev}_{1}(f)(x)

and
h(1,x)=g(x)=\mathbf{Ev}_{1}(g)(x)
.
Then for all
z,z^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
and
x,y\in X
,
|h(z,x)-h(z^{\prime},y)|\leq\sup\{\delta,a(x,y)\}
, which shows that

h\in\mathrm{Met}(Q\times X,Y)
.
Using the non-expansivity of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{Q}(h)

we deduce then

c(f,g)=c(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{Q}(h)(0),\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{Q}(h)(1)))\leq d^{\delta}(0,1)=\delta
,
that is,
c(f,g)\leq\Xi_{a,b}(f,g)
.
∎

Remark 3
.

The distances computed with
\Xi_{a,b}
are larger than those computed with
\Phi_{a,b}
. For example, given the functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(x)=x
and
g(x)=x+K
, one has
\Phi_{a,b}(f,g)=K
, while

\Xi_{a,b}(f,g)=\infty
, since for all
\delta
, there exist
x,y
such that
|x+K-y|>max\{|x+y|,\delta\}
.
Moreover, the distance function
\Xi_{a,b}
should not be confused with the similar function

\Xi^{\prime}_{a,b}(f,g)=\inf\{\delta\mid\forall x,y\in X\ a(x,y)\leq\delta\Longrightarrow b(f(x),g(y))\leq\delta\}
, which is also not in general a metric.
Indeed, consider the metric space consisting of the subset
\mathbb{N}
as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
, and the non-expansive functions
f(x)=x
and
g(x)=x+K
. While for
K<1
we have
\Xi^{\prime}_{a,b}(f,g)=K
, we have as above that for all
\delta
, there exist
x,y
such that
|x+K-y|>\max\{|x+y|,\delta\}
.

We will exploit the following useful characterization of exponentiable objects in
\mathrm{Met}

A
in a cartesian category
\mathbb{C}
is exponentiable when for all object
B
the exponential of
B
and

exists in
\mathbb{C}
. In particular
\mathbb{C}
is cartesian closed iff all its objects are exponentiable.

Theorem 4

(

)
.

A metric space
(X,a)
is exponentiable in
\mathrm{Met}

and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
such that

a(x_{0},x_{2})=\alpha+\beta
, the condition below holds:

\displaystyle\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists x_{1}\in X\text{ s.t. }a(x_{0},x_{1})<\alpha+\epsilon\text{ and }a(x_{1},x_{2})<\beta+\epsilon

(
*
)

Condition (

4
) intuitively requires
X
to have “enough points”. For example,
the set
\mathbb{N}
, as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}
, is not exponentiable in
\mathrm{Met}
(take

x_{0}=0,x_{1}=1
and
\alpha=\beta=1/2
: a point between
0
and
1
is “missing”).
Instead, condition (

∗

) always holds when
(X,a)
is

(see

,

\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
X
and positive reals
\{r_{i}\}_{i\in I}
such that
a(x_{i},x_{j})\leq r_{i}+r_{j}
, there is a point lying in the intersection of all balls
B(x_{i},r_{i})
.
This implies that the sub-category
\mathrm{InjMet}
of
\mathrm{Met}
formed by injective metric spaces is cartesian closed.
Since the Euclidean metric is injective, there is a cartesian closed sub-category of
\mathrm{Met}
formed by “simple types” over closed real intervals, that we’ll use as working example.

Example 5
.

Let
\mathrm{IntST}
be the set of
simple types over the intervals
, defined by
[a,b]\in\mathrm{IntST}
, for all intervals
[a,b]
(with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
and
a\leq b
) and
i,j\in\mathrm{IntST}\ \Rightarrow\ (i\times j),(i\to j)\in\mathrm{IntST}
.
For any
i\in\mathrm{IntST}
, the metric spaces
(\mathcal{I}_{i},d^{\mathcal{I}}_{i})
are defined by

\mathcal{I}_{[a,b]}:=[a,b]
,

\mathcal{I}_{i\times j}:=\mathcal{I}_{i}\times\mathcal{I}_{j}
,

\mathcal{I}_{i\to j}:=\mathrm{Met}(\mathcal{I}_{i},\mathcal{I}_{j})
,

d^{\mathcal{I}}_{[a,b]}(x,y):=|x-y|
,

d^{\mathcal{I}}_{i\times j}:=\max\{d^{\mathcal{I}}_{i},d^{\mathcal{I}}_{j}\}
and

d^{\mathcal{I}}_{i\to j}:=\Xi_{d^{\mathcal{I}}_{i},d^{\mathcal{I}}_{j}}
.

3
Many-Sorted Quantitative Theories and Algebras

In this section we introduce quantitative theories and algebras in the sense of
[
27
]
.
In order to cover both the typed and the untyped case, we consider
many-sorted
theories and algebras, hence combining the quantitative (but one-sorted) approach from
[
27
]
with the qualitative (but many-sorted) approach from
[
22
]
.

Notation.

For any set
I
, an
I
-sorted set

A
is an
I
-indexed family of sets
A=(A_{i})_{i\in I}
(i.e. an object of
\mathsf{Set}^{I}
), and an
I
-sorted function

f:A\to B
between
I
-sorted sets is an
I
-indexed family of functions

f=(f_{i}:A_{i}\to B_{i})_{i\in I}
(i.e. a morphism in
\mathsf{Set}^{I}(A,B)
).
For a set
I
, we denote by
I^{*}
the set of all finite (possibly empty) lists of elements of
I
, we let
w
range over
I^{*}
and use
*
for concatenation.
For
A
an
I
-sorted set and
w=i_{1}\dots i_{k}\in I^{*}
, we let
A_{w}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}A_{i_{j}}
.
We let
\mathrm{Var}
denote a distinguished
I
-sorted set such that for all
i\in I
,
\mathrm{Var}_{i}
is a countably infinite set of variables.
For any
I
-sorted set
A
,
I
-sorted function
f:\mathrm{Var}\to A
, pairwise disjoint variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, with
x_{j}\in\mathrm{Var}_{i_{j}}
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
with
a_{j}\in A_{i_{j}}
, we let

f_{\vec{x},\vec{a}}:\mathrm{Var}\to A
indicate the
I
-sorted function mapping
x_{j}
to
a_{j}
and being as
f
on all other variables.

Definition 6

(Many-Sorted Signature)
.

An
I
-sorted signature

\Sigma
is an
I^{*}\times I
-sorted set
\{\Sigma_{w,i}\mid w\in I^{*},i\in I\}
(i.e. an object of
\mathrm{Set}^{I^{*}\times I}
).

The objects
\sigma\in\Sigma_{w,i}
will be called
symbols
of the signature.

Definition 7

(
\Sigma
-Algebra)
.

A
\Sigma
-algebra
is a pair
(A,\Omega^{A})
where
A
is a
I
-sorted family and
\Omega^{A}
associates each symbol
\sigma\in\Sigma_{w,i}
with a function
\sigma_{A}:A_{w}\to A_{i}
, where

A_{w}=A_{i_{1}}\times\dots\times A_{i_{k}}
, for
w=i_{1}\dots i_{k}
.
For any object
A
of
\mathrm{Set}^{I}
, the
free
\Sigma
-algebra over
A
, noted
\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(A)
, is the
I
-sorted set defined
by the following conditions:
(i)
for all
x\in A_{i}
,
x\in\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(A)_{i}
;
(ii)
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{w,i}
and
v_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(A)_{w(1)},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(A)_{w(k)}
, then
\sigma_{\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(A)}(v_{1},\dots,v_{k}):=\sigma(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(A)_{i}
.

Intuitively,
\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(A)_{i}
is the set of “terms of sort
i
with parameters in
A
”.
Free algebras enjoy the following universal property:

Proposition 8
.

For any
\Sigma
-algebra
(A,\Omega^{A})
and map
f\in\mathrm{Set}^{I}(B,A)
there exists a unique
\Sigma
-homomorphism

f^{\sharp}:\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(B)\to A
extending
f
, that is, such that
f=f^{\sharp}\circ\eta_{B}
, where
\eta_{B}:B\to\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(B)
is the inclusion map.

Given a function
f\in\mathrm{Set}^{I}(B,A)
, if
t\in\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(B)_{i}
is some term of sort
i
with parameters
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
in
B
,
f^{\sharp}t\in\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(A)_{i}
is the result of “substituting” each parameter
b_{i}
in
t
with
f(b_{i})
.

Let us now introduce the equational language of quantitative theories.

Definition 9
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
I
-sorted signature.

(i) A
quantitative
\Sigma
-equation
over
\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var})
is an expression of the form

t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s
,
where
i\in I
,
t,s\in\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var})_{i}

and
\epsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
.

(ii)
For all
\epsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
, let
\mathcal{V}(\mathrm{Var})
be the set of
indexed
\Sigma
-equations
of the form
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}y
, for some
i\in I
and
x,y\in\mathrm{Var}_{i}
, and
\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var}))
be the set of indexed
\Sigma
-equations of the form
t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s
, where
i\in I
and

t,s\in(\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var}))_{i}
.

Definition 10
.

A
consequence relation
on the free
\Sigma
-algebra
\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var})
is a relation
\vdash\ \subseteq\wp(\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var})))\times\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var}))
closed under all instances of the following rules (where
\epsilon,\delta
vary over all
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
):

(Cut)

if
\Gamma\vdash\phi
for all
\phi\in\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}\vdash\psi
, then

\Gamma\vdash\psi
;

(Assumpt)

if
\phi\in\Gamma
, then
\Gamma\vdash\phi
;

(Refl)

\emptyset\vdash t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{0}}}t
;

(Symm)

\{t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s\}\vdash s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}t
;

(Triang)

\{t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s,s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\delta}}}u\}\vdash t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon+\delta}}}u
;

(Max)

\{t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s\}\vdash t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon+\delta}}}s
;

(Arch)

\{t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\delta}}}s\mid\delta>\epsilon\}\vdash t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s

(NExp)

\{t_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{1}}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s_{1},\dots,t_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{k}}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s_{k}\}\vdash\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}\sigma(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
, for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k},i}
;

(Subst)

if

, then
\Gamma\vdash t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}s
implies

f^{\sharp}\Gamma\vdash f^{\sharp}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}f^{\sharp}s
.

Notice that rule
(Arch)
has infinitely many assumptions.

We let
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var}))=\wp_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var})))\times\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var}))
indicate the set of
quantitative inferences on
\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var})
and

\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Var})=\wp_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{V}(\mathrm{Var}))\times\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{F}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{Var}))
indicate the set of
basic quantitative inferences
. Axioms for theories will be basic quantitative inferences.

Definition 11

(Many-Sorted Quantitative Theory)
.

As is well-known (see e.g.

Definition 15

Definition 16

Definition

5
Quantitative
\lambda
-Theories and Algebras

As we recalled, weak
\lambda
-theories do not fully capture the equational theory of the
\lambda
-calculus, as they fail to capture the so-called
\xi
-rule

[
5
]
.
In our quantitative setting, this rule can be expressed as the inference

t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}u\vdash\lambda x.t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i\to j}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}\lambda x.u

provided
the equation on the left of
\vdash
is
locally universally quantified
: the righthand equation holds under the condition that,
for all possible value of
x
, the lefthand equation holds. This kind of quantitative inferences differ from those seen so far. The reason for this proviso is that it involves the higher-order operator
\lambda
, which “binds” the variable
x
.
The example below shows that quantitative weak
\lambda
-algebras fail to capture this rule.

Example 26
.

The
\xi
-rule fails in the weak
\lambda
-algebra
\mathcal{I}
:
let
f,g:[0,b]\to[0,b+\epsilon]
(where
b\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\epsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
) be, respectively, the identity function
f=\mathrm{id}
and the function
g(x)=x+\epsilon
;
for any
s\in[a,b]
, we then have
|f(s)-g(s)|\leq\epsilon
, which shows

\mathcal{I}\vDash\overline{f}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle[a,b+\epsilon]}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}\overline{g}x
.
However, since
d^{\mathcal{I}}_{[a,b]\to[a,b+\epsilon]}(f,g)=b+\epsilon
, we deduce

\mathcal{I}\not\vDash\lambda x.\overline{f}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle[a,b]\to[a,b+\epsilon]}}{{\simeq_{\epsilon}}}\lambda x.\overline{g}x
.

In order to define quantitative
\lambda
-theories we could follow Curry
[
5
]
and “strengthen” the set of axioms, in fact mere equalities, satisfied by a
\Sigma^{\mathbf{CL}}
-algebra and essentially do away with the
\xi
-rule and all higher order features.
The alternative, that we develop in this section, is to take abstraction and the
\xi
-rule as first class elements of our theories and algebras. This will require a number of generalizations of the original approach of
[
27
]
.

At the level of syntax, the first step is to enrich the class of symbols with higher-order operators of the form
\lambda_{i}x
. The occurrence of the variable
x
is part of the symbol
\lambda_{i}x
itself.

Definition 27

(
\lambda
-Signature)
.

Given an applicative
T
-sorted signature
\Sigma
, let
\Sigma^{\lambda}
be the applicative
T
-sorted signature further including the symbols

and

.
The

\Sigma^{\lambda}
-algebra,

.

Terms

will be denoted by
\lambda_{i}x.t
or simply

.
Free and bound variables, open and closed

-terms are defined as usual.
For a

-term

In order to simplify the notation we deal with bound variables by implementing directly Barendregt’s “hygiene condition”.
For any function

.

In order to be able to express correctly the
\xi
-rule we generalize quantitative equations to expressions of the form

\Lambda^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma,\tau}(\lambda x.t)=[\lambda x.t]_{\beta\eta}
,

Then
{\cal NF}
is an applicative
\lambda

The signature of this algebra can be enriched with

providing the approximants of a given normal form. Intuitively, the

Definition 53
.

For all
\sigma\in T

n\in{\mathbb{N}}

\pi^{n}_{\sigma}:\mathit{NF}_{\sigma}\to\mathit{NF}_{\sigma}
is defined by induction on

for all
\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{m}.x_{i}t_{1}\ldots t_{k}\in\mathit{NF}_{\sigma}

\pi_{0}^{\sigma}(t)=\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{m}.\bot\ \ \ \ \pi_{n+1}^{\sigma}(t)=\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{m}.x_{i}(t_{1})_{n}\ldots(t_{k})_{n}\ .

In the sequel, we will denote the approximant
\pi_{\sigma}^{n}(t)

.

Definition 54

(Distance on Normal Forms)
.

We define a family of functions
d^{\mathit{NF}}=\{d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma}
, where
d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}:\mathit{NF}_{\sigma}\times\mathit{NF}_{\sigma}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

d^{\mathit{NF}}_{o}(t,s)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }t=s\\
1&\mbox{ otherwise }\end{cases}
and
d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma\rightarrow\tau}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\ ,

where
m
is the largest

, if it exists, such that

(1)

t_{n}=t^{\prime}_{n}
and
(2)

\forall s,s^{\prime}\in\mathit{NF}_{\sigma}.\ (d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}(s,s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\ \Longrightarrow\ d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\tau}(ts,ts^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}})

Lemma 55
.

For all
\sigma,\tau
,
(\mathit{NF}_{\sigma},d_{\sigma}^{\mathit{NF}})
is a partial ultrametric space and
\cdot_{\sigma,\tau}
is non-expansive.

Proof.

The fact that
\cdot_{\sigma,\tau}
is non-expansive follows immediately from the definition of
d_{\sigma}^{\mathit{NF}}
.

Conditions for
d^{\mathit{NF}}
to be an ultrametric are shown by induction on types. Symmetry is immediate. Now we show, by induction on types that, for all
\sigma
,

\forall t,t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}(\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}$}(t,t)\leq\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}$}(t,t^{\prime})\ \mbox{and}\ \mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}$}(t,t^{\prime})\leq\mbox{max}\{\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}$}(t,t^{\prime\prime}),\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}$}(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})\})\ .

(1)

For the base type
o
the thesis is immediate, since the only normal forms of type
o
are
\bot
and the variables. Then let
\sigma
be
\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}
. We show that the first conjunct of
1
holds. Let
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t,t^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
. We prove that

\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t,t)\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
. Condition 1 in Definition
54
is trivially verified. We prove condition 2.
Let
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}}$}(s,s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
.
By the hypothesis
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t,t^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
, we have
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(ts,t^{\prime}s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
. By induction hypothesis,
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}}$}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
, hence
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}$}(t^{\prime}s^{\prime},ts^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
. By induction hypothesis,
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(ts,ts^{\prime})\leq\mbox{max}\{\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(ts,t^{\prime}s^{\prime}),\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma}$}(t^{\prime}s^{\prime},ts^{\prime})\}
, hence
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(ts,ts^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
.
We are left to show the second conjunct of
1
,
i.e.
that
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t,t^{\prime})\leq\mbox{max}\{\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t,t^{\prime\prime}),\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})\})
.
Assume
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t,t^{\prime\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
and
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{m}}

m\geq k
. We show that
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t,t^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
. Condition 1 of Definition
54
is straightforward. We show condition 2. Let
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}}$}(s,s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
.
Since by hypothesis we have
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t,t^{\prime\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
then
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(ts,t^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
. But by hypothesis we have also
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}\to\sigma_{2}}$}(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{m}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
, then, using
reflexivity, by induction hypothesis,
i.e.

\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{1}}$}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
, we have
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(t^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime},t^{\prime}s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
. Again by induction hypothesis, we have
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(ts,t^{\prime}s^{\prime})\leq\mbox{max}\{\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(ts,t^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime}),\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(t^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime},t^{\prime}s^{\prime})\})
, and hence
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{NF}}_{\sigma_{2}}$}(ts,t^{\prime}s^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
.
∎

Remark 56
.

Notice that

is not reflexive.
E.g., let

of appropriate type

Remark 58

If we drop condition 2 in Definition

.

(iii) Let
\mbox{$d^{\mathit{\cal D}}_{\sigma}$}:D_{\sigma}\times D_{\sigma}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

\mbox{$d^{\mathit{\cal D}}_{o}$}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }a=b\\

\mbox{$d^{\mathit{\cal D}}_{\sigma\rightarrow\tau}$}(a,b)=\frac{1}{2^{m}}
,

(1)

Partial
\lambda
-Algebras with Approximants.
The two examples above of partial quantitative
\lambda
-algebras can be viewed as special cases of a general construction, which can be carried out on any applicative algebra which includes projection operators. Namely, using the system of approximants given by the projection operators, one can endow the algebra with an ultrametric, getting a quantitative applicative algebra. If moreover the algebra satisfies the
(\beta)
-rule, then it is a quantitative (weak)
\lambda
-algebra.

Definition 62

(Applicative Algebra with Approximants)
.

An applicative algebra
\mathcal{A}=(A,\Omega^{\mathcal{A}})
has approximants if it includes
projection operators
\pi_{\sigma}^{n}:A_{\sigma}\to A_{\sigma}
, for all
\sigma\in T
and
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
, satisfying the following property:

\mbox{for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}},\mbox{\ for all }a,b\in A_{\sigma},\ \pi^{n+1}_{\sigma}(a)=\pi^{n+1}_{\sigma}(b)\mbox{ implies }\pi^{n}_{\sigma}(a)=\pi^{n}_{\sigma}(b)\ .

Proposition 63
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(A,\Omega^{\mathcal{A}})
be an applicative
\lambda
-algebra with approximants. Let
d^{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}:A_{\sigma}\times A_{\sigma}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
be the family of functions defined by induction on types
as follows:

d^{\mathcal{A}}_{o}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }a=b\\
1&\mbox{ otherwise }\end{cases}
and
d^{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma\rightarrow\tau}(a,b)=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\ ,

where
m
is the maximal
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if it exists, such that

(1)

\pi_{\sigma\to\tau}^{n}(a)=\pi_{\sigma\to\tau}^{n}(b)
and
(2)

\forall c,d\in A_{\sigma}.\ (d^{A}_{\sigma}(c,d)\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\ \Longrightarrow\ d^{A}_{\tau}(ac,bd)\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}})
;

if the maximal
n
does not exist, then
d^{A}_{\sigma\rightarrow\tau}(a,b)=0
.

Then
(A,\Omega^{\mathcal{A}},d^{\mathcal{A}})
is a quantitative applicative algebra.

Using an argument similar to that for Lemma
55
, one can to prove that
d^{A}
is a partial ultrametric.

8
Approximate Quantitative Algebras

As we have seen, finding non-discrete quantitative (weak)
\lambda
-algebras is difficult. One difficulty arises from the non-expansiveness requirement on application.
In Section
7
we have shown how to define non-trivial ultrametric quantitative
\lambda
-algebras, still maintaining the non-expansiveness requirement for application, but at the price of the partiality of the metric.
Here we present a different approach: we relax rule (NExp) for application, so as to get quantitative
\lambda
-algebras with full pseudo-metric distances.
Namely, we introduce the notion of
approximate applicative algebra
: this amounts to an applicative algebra with approximants (see Def.
62
above), and operators
\{\cdot^{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
approximating application. Projection operators immediately induce an ultrametrics on the algebra, by just considering condition 1 in Prop.
63
(and dropping condition 2). In general, application is expansive w.r.t. this metric (see Remark
58
). However, the milder
uniform
non-expansiveness
condition for approximant operators is satisfied in many cases, including the term algebra of normal forms and the
D_{\infty}
model of Section
7
. This approach is quite general, since it works both for the typed and the untyped
\lambda
-calculus.

Lemma 64
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(A,\Omega^{\mathcal{A}})
be an applicative algebra with approximants. Let
e^{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}:A_{\sigma}\times A_{\sigma}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
be the family of functions defined by:

e^{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}(a,b)=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\ ,

where
m
is the maximal
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if it exists, such that
a_{n}=b_{n}
, otherwise we put
e^{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}(a,b)=0
.

Then for all
\sigma

(A,e^{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma})
is an ultrametric space.

Definition 65

(Approximate Quantitative Algebra)
.

(i) An approximate algebra
\mathcal{A}=(A,\Omega^{\mathcal{A}})
is an applicative algebra with approximants whose signature includes also a family of operators
\cdot^{n}_{\sigma,\tau}:A_{\sigma\to\tau}\times A_{\sigma}\to A_{\tau}
, for all
\sigma,\tau\in T
and
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
(the operators
\cdot^{n}_{\sigma,\tau}
will be simply denoted by
\cdot^{n}
).

(ii) An approximate quantitative algebra
\mathcal{A}=(A,\Omega^{\mathcal{A}},e^{\mathcal{A}})
is an approximate algebra where the operators
\cdot_{\sigma,\tau}^{n}
satisfy the following conditions:

(1)
for all
a\in A_{\sigma\to\tau}
,
b\in A_{\sigma}
,
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
,
e_{\tau}^{A}(a\cdot^{n+1}b,a\cdot b)\leq e_{\tau}^{\mathcal{A}}(a\cdot^{n}b,a\cdot b)
;

(2)
for all
a\in A_{\sigma\to\tau}
,
b\in A_{\sigma}
, for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
such that
e_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{A}}(a\cdot^{n}b,a\cdot b)\leq\epsilon
;

(3)
(uniform non-expansiveness)
\forall n>0\ \exists\epsilon_{n}>0
s.t.
\forall\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{n}
,
\forall a,b\in A_{\sigma\to\tau}
,
\forall c,d\in A_{\sigma}
,

e_{\sigma\to\tau}^{\mathcal{A}}(a,b)\leq\epsilon\mbox{ and }e_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{A}}(c,d)\leq\epsilon\mbox{ implies }e_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{A}}(a\cdot^{n}c,b\cdot^{n}d)\leq\epsilon
.

Conditions 1 and 2 above express the fact that the operators
\cdot^{n}
approximate the behaviour of application; condition 3 replaces rule (NExp) for application.

The Approximate
\lambda
-Algebra of the Term Model.
The
\lambda
-algebra
\mathcal{NF}
can be extended to an approximate quantitative
\lambda
-algebra by defining operators
\cdot^{n}_{\sigma,\tau}
as follows:

\mbox{ for all }t\in NF_{\sigma\to\tau},s\in NF_{\sigma},\mbox{ for all }n\in{\mathbf{N}},\ \ t\cdot^{n}_{\sigma,\tau}s=t_{n}\cdot_{\sigma,\tau}s_{n}\ .

One can check that the approximant operators satisfy all conditions of Definition
65
.

The Approximate
\lambda
-Algebra of
D_{\infty}
.

The
\lambda
-algebra
\mathcal{D}
can be extended to an approximate quantitative
\lambda
-algebra by defining operators
\cdot^{n}_{\sigma,\tau}
as follows:

\mbox{ for all }a\in D_{\sigma\to\tau},b\in D_{\sigma},\mbox{ for all }n\in{\mathbf{N}},\ \ a\cdot^{n}_{\sigma,\tau}b=a_{n+1}\cdot_{\sigma,\tau}b\ .

One can check that the approximant operators satisfy all conditions of Definition
65
.

Notice that the approximate algebra of
D_{\infty}
yields a
\lambda
-algebra for the untyped
\lambda
-calculus.

Finally, notice that in dealing with partial and approximate algebras we have considered applicative algebras over an extended signature.
For lack of space, we have not developed corresponding approximate theories including extra operators and the suitable rules on them.
In particular, rule (NExp) has to be replaced by a rule expressing uniform non-expansiveness of approximant operators.
We leave this as future work; here we just observe that the appropriate
language for reasoning on such structures would be the
indexed
\lambda
-calculus
together with
indexed reduction
, see
[
5
]
.

9
Conclusions

Contributions.

This paper addresses the problem of defining quantitative algebras, in the sense of Mardare
et
al
, capable to interpret terms of higher-order calculi.
Our contributions include both negative and positive results: on the one hand we identify the main mathematical obstacles to the construction of non-trivial quantitative higher-order algebras; on the other hand we introduce a quantitative variant of the traditional notions of (weak)
\lambda
-algebras, together with a sound and complete equational syntax, and we show that,
in spite of the limitations highlighted,
intriguing notions of distance for the
\lambda
-calculus do indeed exist.

Related Work.

Since
[
2
]
metric spaces have been exploited as an alternative, quantitative, framework to standard, domain-theoretic, denotational semantics
[
33
,
4
]
.
The possibility of giving a metric structure to
linear
or
affine
higher-order
programs is known, since
\mathrm{Met}
is an SMCC, even if not a CCC. In this sense it is worth
recalling the work by de Amorim
et al

[
3
]
, along with those of Reed and
Pierce
[
31
]
.
Moreover,
ultra-metrics have already been used to model PCF
[
20
]
.
More recently, Pistone has given a precise account of
cartesian closed structure in categories of generalized metric spaces
[
30
]
. In
particular, it is known that if the quantale that captures distances can vary as the types
vary, as for example in the so-called differential logical relations
[
16
]
,
categories of generalized metric spaces can become cartesian closed. The study of metric
semantics for imperative and concurrent programming languages has a long
tradition
[
17
,
18
]
. However, this very sophisticated apparatus is not
applicable to higher order programming languages.

Partial metrics have been well-studied since
[
9
]
and
[
24
]
(where they are called
M
-sets).

[
10
]
shows that these metrics are strongly related to Scott semantics.
The setting of
quantaloid-enriched
categories
[
23
,
32
]
provides an abstract unifying framework for the different metric structures discussed here.
In this setting,
[
13
,
14
]
provide a general characterization
of exponentiable morphisms and objects in categories of (generalized) metric spaces.

Finally, a somehow related approach to quantitative reasoning is provided by the use of fuzzy logic to reason about degrees of similarity between programs, as spelled out in Zadeh’s pioneering work
[
34
,
35
]
.
More recently, fuzzy algebraic theories in the style of Mardare
et al
have been studied
[
12
]
. However, such theories seem to lack a compositionality condition comparable to the one expressed by axiom (Nexp), hence apparently diverging from the idea of interpreting
programs as non-expansive functions.

Perspectives.

Our
focus here was on the simplest case, namely that of algebras
without
a barycentric structure,
thus putting ourselves in a simpler setting than the one studied by Mardare
et al.

Indeed, a natural continuation of this work would be to study quantitative algebras for
\lambda
-calculi enriched with operations having an intrinsically quantitative content, like e.g. probabilistic choice (see
[
15
]
) or some form of differentiation
[
19
,
11
,
26
]
.
Another direction to explore, already suggested by some of our models, is that of exploring quantitative algebras in categories of domains like, e.g. metric CPOs
[
3
]
, or continuous Scott domains (especially in virtue of their close connection with partial metric spaces
[
10
]
).

Finally, the approaches of partial and approximate algebras open new lines of investigation: suitable approximate theories are called for, and moreover the distances on programs which arise are worth to be studied in depth.
