Presentations for wreath products involving symmetric inverse monoids and categories
Chad Clark, James East

Abstract
Wreath products involving symmetric inverse monoids/semigroups/categories arise in many areas of algebra and science, and presentations by generators and relations are crucial tools in such studies. The current paper finds such presentations for M ≀ ℐ n M\wr\mathcal{I}_{n} , M ≀ Sing ⁡ ( ℐ n ) M\wr\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n}) and M ≀ ℐ M\wr\mathcal{I} . Here M M is an arbitrary monoid, ℐ n \mathcal{I}_{n} is the symmetric inverse monoid, Sing ⁡ ( ℐ n ) \operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n}) its singular ideal, and ℐ \mathcal{I} is the symmetric inverse category. Keywords : Presentations, wreath products, symmetric inverse monoids/semigroups/categories. MSC(2020): 20M05, 20M20, 20M50, 20M18, 18M05.

Introduction

Semigroups whose elements are fundamental mathematical entities such as partitions, transformations and relations have a natural representation using diagrams. Since the work of Brauer in the 1930s

, so-called diagram semigroups have appeared in a number diverse settings, including topology, invariant theory, quantum mechanics, representation theory, statistical mechanics and many other branches of mathematics and science. See for example

Finding presentations of diagram/transformation semigroups and their singular parts, which are ideals formed by complementing the group of units, has been of considerable interest since at least the late 1950s. This began with the work of Aĭzenštat
[

,
2
]
and Popova
[

,

]
, and can be traced back to Moore’s presentation for the symmetric group as a Coxeter group
[

]
; cf.
[

]
. With the notable exceptions of the semigroups of (partitioned) binary relations
[
43
,
39
]
, presentations are known for a great many important diagram semigroups. These include the full/partial transformation monoids, (dual) symmetric inverse monoids, Temperley-Lieb/Kauffman monoids, (partial) Brauer monoids, Motzkin monoids, (rook) partition monoids, and the singular ideals of many of the above; see for example
[

The symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}_{n}
is the set of all partial permutations of the set
{{\bf n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}}
, under the operation of relational composition. This monoid plays the same role in the theory of inverse semigroups as the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
plays in group theory; for more details on this, see for example
[
33
,
35
]
. The monoid
\mathcal{I}_{n}
is also known in the literature as the rook monoid because of the alternate characterisation of partial permutations by
\{0,1\}
-matrices with at most one non-zero entry in each row and each column; such matrices are in one-to-one correspondence with placements of non-attacking rooks on an
n\times n
chess board. While the representation theory of the rook monoid is well studied (see for example
[
49
,
42
]
), there has also been substantial recent interest in the representation theory of wreath products
G\wr\mathcal{I}_{n}
, where
G
is a group (definitions are given below). See especially the work of Steinberg
[
50
,
51
]
and Mazorchuk and Srivastava
[
40
]
. The paper
[
40
]
concerns the important special case that
G=C_{r}
is a finite cyclic group, which stems from analogous wreath products
C_{r}\wr\mathcal{S}_{n}
and their deformations, the cyclotomic Hecke algebras
[
10
]
. See also
[
6
]
, which computes the congruence lattice of
G\wr\mathcal{I}_{n}
for an arbitrary group
G
. As the title of that paper suggests,
G\wr\mathcal{I}_{n}
is in fact isomorphic to the monoid of partial automorphisms of a free
G
-act of rank
n
, providing another source of motivation for studying such products.

The current article studies more general structures still, and concerns
transformational
wreath products
M\wr\mathcal{I}_{n}
for an arbitrary
monoid

M
, as studied for example in
[
6
,
7
]
(full definitions are given in Subsection
2.3
). We also consider:

•

M\wr\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
, where
\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
is the singular ideal of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, consisting of all
strictly
partial permutations, and

•

M\wr\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the category of all partial bijections
{\bf m}\to{\bf n}
where
m
and
n
range over
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\dots\}
.

Our main results give presentations by generators and relations for each of these structures. Although several general results exist on presentations of wreath products of monoids (see
[
11
,
46
,
28
,
47
]
), these are not applicable to
M\wr\mathcal{I}_{n}
as they concern different kinds of wreath products to the transformational types considered here. As for
M\wr\mathcal{I}
, we are not aware of any previous results on presentations for wreath products involving categories, but we will be aided by tools developed in
[
17
]
, which allow presentations for certain classes of categories to be constructed from presentations of their endomorphism monoids. The situation for
M\wr\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
is rather more complex, as
\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
is not a monoid, and it is well known that semigroups tend to behave quite badly even for much simpler constructions such as Cartesian/direct products
[
48
]
. As a case in point, an entire 41-page paper
[
20
]
has been devoted to the related wreath product
M\wr\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, where
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is the full transformation monoid (consisting of all self-maps of
\mathbf{n}
). While the recent paper
[
7
]
deals with a vast class of semigroup products, and proves many general results, examples such as
M\wr\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
were singled out as a special kind of difficult case, and none of the results proved in
[
7
]
apply here. Thus, we hope that these methods of working with non-monoid wreath products will be of use in the study of other such complex structures.

The article is organised as follows. We begin with some preliminary material in Section

2.1
Categories

As in
[
17
]
, we are interested in very special kinds of categories. Throughout, the term
category
will always mean a small, strict tensor (a.k.a. monoidal) category with object set
\mathbb{N}
; these terms will all be defined below. Any such category
\mathcal{C}
will be identified with its morphism set. The domain and range of
a\in\mathcal{C}
are denoted by
\mathbf{d}(a)
and
\mathbf{r}(a)
, respectively. Morphisms are composed left-to-right, so that
ab=a\circ b
is defined if and only if
\mathbf{r}(a)=\mathbf{d}(b)
, in which case
\mathbf{d}(ab)=\mathbf{d}(a)
and
\mathbf{r}(ab)=\mathbf{r}(b)
. For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
\mathcal{C}_{m,n}=\{a\in\mathcal{C}:\mathbf{d}(a)=m,\ \mathbf{r}(a)=n\}
for the set of all morphisms
m\to n
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathcal{C}_{n,n}
for the endomorphism monoid at the object
n
, and we denote the identity of this monoid by
\iota_{n}
(or a similar symbol). To say that
\mathcal{C}
is a
strict tensor category
means that it has an additional (totally defined) operation
\oplus:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
for which

•

(\mathcal{C},\oplus)
is a monoid with identity
\iota_{0}
,

•

\mathbf{d}(a\oplus b)=\mathbf{d}(a)+\mathbf{d}(b)
and
\mathbf{r}(a\oplus b)=\mathbf{r}(a)+\mathbf{r}(b)
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{C}
,

•

\iota_{m}\oplus\iota_{n}=\iota_{m+n}
for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

(a\circ b)\oplus(c\circ d)=(a\oplus c)\circ(b\oplus d)
for all
a,b,c,d\in\mathcal{C}
with
\mathbf{r}(a)=\mathbf{d}(b)
and
\mathbf{r}(c)=\mathbf{d}(d)
.

A
congruence
on a category
\mathcal{C}
(as above) is an equivalence relation
\sigma
on
\mathcal{C}
for which

•

(a,b)\in\sigma\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{d}(a)=\mathbf{d}(b)
and
\mathbf{r}(a)=\mathbf{r}(b)
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{C}
,

•

(a,b)\in\sigma\ \Rightarrow\ (xa,xb),(ay,by)\in\sigma
for all
a,b,x,y\in\mathcal{C}
whenever the stated products are defined.

The
quotient category

\mathcal{C}/\sigma
is the set of all
\sigma
-classes under the induced operation. We say
\sigma
is a
tensor congruence
if it also preserves
\oplus
, meaning that

•

(a,b)\in\sigma\ \Rightarrow\ (x\oplus a,x\oplus b),(a\oplus y,b\oplus y)\in\sigma
for all
a,b,x,y\in\mathcal{C}
.

For a set
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
satisfying
(a,b)\in\Omega\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{d}(a)=\mathbf{d}(b)
and
\mathbf{r}(a)=\mathbf{r}(b)
, we denote by
\Omega^{\sharp}
the congruence on
\mathcal{C}
generated by
\Omega
. So
\Omega^{\sharp}
is the smallest congruence on
\mathcal{C}
containing
\Omega
. We also denote by
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}
the tensor congruence generated by
\Omega
; note that
\Omega^{\sharp}\subseteq\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}
, but the inclusion is sometimes strict.

Given categories
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
(as above), a
category morphism

\phi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is a functor that acts as the identity on objects, meaning that
\mathbf{d}(a\phi)=\mathbf{d}(a)
and
\mathbf{r}(a\phi)=\mathbf{r}(a)
for all
a\in\mathcal{C}
. The
kernel
of
\phi
is the congruence

\ker(\phi)=\big\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}:a\phi=b\phi\big\},

and we have
\mathcal{C}/{\ker(\phi)}\cong\im(\phi)
.

2.2
Symmetric inverse monoids and categories

A particularly important category for us is the
symmetric inverse category

\mathcal{I}
, defined as follows. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
{\bf n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, interpreting this to be empty when
n=0
. For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
\mathcal{I}_{m,n}
the set of all injective partial maps
{\bf m}\to{\bf n}
: i.e., all injective functions
A\to{\bf n}
with
A\subseteq{\bf m}
. We then define

\mathcal{I}=\big\{(m,\alpha,n):m,n\in\mathbb{N},\ \alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{m,n}\big\}.

For
m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and for
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{m,n}
and
\beta\in\mathcal{I}_{n,k}
, we define

\mathbf{d}(m,\alpha,n)=m,\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbf{r}(m,\alpha,n)=n\and(m,\alpha,n)\circ(n,\beta,k)=(m,\alpha\beta,k),

where
\alpha\beta=\alpha\circ\beta\in\mathcal{I}_{m,k}
is the ordinary relational composition. To avoid clutter in our notation, we will typically identify an element
(m,\alpha,n)
of
\mathcal{I}
with the partial map
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{m,n}
itself, but regard
m
and
n
as ‘encoded’ in
\alpha
, writing
\mathbf{d}(\alpha)=m
and
\mathbf{r}(\alpha)=n
. In this way, the morphism sets of
\mathcal{I}
are the
\mathcal{I}_{m,n}
(
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
), and the endomorphism monoids are the
symmetric inverse monoids

\mathcal{I}_{n}=\mathcal{I}_{n,n}
.

A partial bijection
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{m,n}
is typically represented by a graph consisting of a row of upper vertices labelled
1,\ldots,m
, a row of lower vertices labelled
1,\ldots,n
, and an edge from upper vertex
i
to lower vertex
j
if
i\in\dom(\alpha)
and
i\alpha=j
. Here as usual
\dom(\alpha)
denotes the domain of
\alpha
; we also write
\im(\alpha)
for the image of
\alpha
. Figure

We have already noted that the endomorphism monoids of the category
\mathcal{I}
are the symmetric inverse monoids
\mathcal{I}_{n}=\mathcal{I}_{n,n}
. The group of units of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
is the
symmetric group

\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Because the monoids here are finite, the singular part
\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n})=\mathcal{I}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}
is itself a semigroup; indeed, it is an ideal of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
. The set
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{S}_{n}

is of course a subcategory, but
\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I})=\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}
is not a category; for example, if
\alpha

\mathcal{I}_{0,1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1,0}
, respectively, then
\alpha,\beta\in\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I})
, while
\alpha\beta=\varnothing
is the unique element of
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\mathcal{S}_{0}
.

2.3
Wreath products

In this subsection we define the transformational wreath products that will be the objects of our study.

Let
M
be a monoid with identity
1
and let
0
be a symbol not belonging to
M
. We denote by
M_{0}=M\cup\{0\}
the monoid obtained from
M
by adjoining
0
as a multiplicative zero element, even if
M
already had a multiplicative zero element. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
M_{0}^{n}
for the set of all
n
-tuples over
M_{0}
, which is a monoid with identity
{\bf 1}_{n}=(1,\ldots,1)
. Note that
M_{0}^{0}=\{\varnothing\}
consists only of the empty tuple. We also write

\mathcal{M}=M_{0}^{0}\cup M_{0}^{1}\cup M_{0}^{2}\cup\cdots

for the set of all finite tuples over
M_{0}
, which is trivially a category (in our sense). For
{\bf a}\in\mathcal{M}
we write
\mathbf{d}({\bf a})=\mathbf{r}({\bf a})
for the unique

such that
{\bf a}\in M_{0}^{n}

{\bf a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in M_{0}^{m}
and

{\bf a}\oplus{\bf b}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in M_{0}^{m+n}
.

The
support

\operatorname{supp}({\bf a})
and where
{\bf 1}_{\dom(\alpha)}\in M_{0}^{\mathbf{d}(\alpha)}
denotes the
\mathbf{d}(\alpha)
-tuple over
\{0,1\}
with support equal to
\dom(\alpha)
.

Remark 2.1
.

With the above identifications, we note that

({\bf a},\alpha)={\bf a}\cdot\alpha\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for any $({\bf a},\alpha)\in M\wr\mathcal{I}$.}

More generally, if
{\bf a}\in M_{0}^{m}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{m,n}
are arbitrary, then
{\bf a}\cdot\alpha=({\bf b},\beta)
, where:

•

{\bf b}
is obtained from
{\bf a}
by replacing
a_{i}
by
0
for any
i\not\in\dom(\alpha)
, and

•

\beta
is the restriction of
\alpha
to
\operatorname{supp}({\bf a})
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any subsemigroup
S
of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
we also have the wreath product

M\wr S=\big\{({\bf a},\alpha)\in M_{0}^{n}\times S:\operatorname{supp}({\bf a})=\dom(\alpha)\big\},

which is a subsemigroup of
M\wr\mathcal{I}_{n}
. Later we will be especially concerned with the case that
S
is the singular subsemigroup
\operatorname{Sing}(\mathcal{I}_{n})=\mathcal{I}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

2.4
Presentations

We now fix the notation we will be using for (monoid, semigroup, category and tensor category) presentations. We also state two results from
[
17
]
that will be required.

Fix an alphabet
X
and let
X^{*}
be the
free monoid
over
X
. So
X^{*}
consists of all words over
X
, including the empty word, which we will typically denote by
\iota
. Let
R\subseteq X^{*}\times X^{*}
, and let
R^{\sharp}
be the congruence on
X^{*}
generated by
R
. We say a monoid
M
has
presentation

\textup{{Mon}}\langle{X}:{R}\rangle
if
M\cong X^{*}/R^{\sharp}
: i.e., if there exists a monoid surmorphism
X^{*}\to M
with kernel
R^{\sharp}
. If
\phi
is such a surmorphism, we say
M
has
presentation
\textup{{Mon}}\langle{X}:{R}\rangle
via
\phi
. Elements of
X
and
R
are called
generators
and
relations
, respectively, and a relation
(u,v)\in R
is typically displayed as an equation:
u=v
. (The same conventions hold for the other kinds of presentations discussed below.) We sometimes use the notation
\textup{{Mon}}\langle{X}:{R}\rangle
to denote the monoid
X^{*}/R^{\sharp}
itself.

Semigroup presentations
are defined analogously in terms of the
free semigroup

{X^{+}=X^{*}\setminus\{\iota\}}
. Relations in a semigroup presentation
\textup{{Sgp}}\langle{X}:{R}\rangle
always involve non-empty words, meaning that
{R\subseteq X^{+}\times X^{+}}
.

For any alphabet
X
, we denote by
\ell=\ell_{X}:X^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
the length function. The following basic result will be useful on a number of occasions.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be disjoint alphabets, let
\sim
be a congruence on
(X\cup Y)^{*}
or
(X\cup Y)^{+}
, and suppose one of the following two conditions holds:

(i)

for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
,
yx\sim uv
for some
u\in X^{*}
and
v\in Y^{*}
with
\ell(u)\leq 1
,

(ii)

for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
,
yx\sim uv
for some
u\in X^{*}
and
v\in Y^{*}
with
\ell(v)\leq 1
.

Then for all
w\in(X\cup Y)^{+}
we have
w\sim uv
for some
u\in X^{*}
and
v\in Y^{*}
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we assume that
(i)
holds, and we proceed by induction on
k=\ell(w)
. The result being clear for
k=1
, we assume that
k\geq 2
, so that
w=w^{\prime}z
for some
w^{\prime}\in(X\cup Y)^{+}
and
z\in X\cup Y
. By induction, we have
w^{\prime}\sim u^{\prime}v^{\prime}
for some
u^{\prime}\in X^{*}
and
v^{\prime}\in Y^{*}
, and so
w\sim u^{\prime}v^{\prime}z
. If
z\in Y
then we take
u=u^{\prime}
and
v=v^{\prime}z
, so we now assume that
z\in X
. Repeated application of condition
(i)
gives
v^{\prime}z\sim u^{\prime\prime}v
for some
u^{\prime\prime}\in X^{*}
and
v\in Y^{*}
(with
\ell(u^{\prime\prime})\leq 1
), so
w\sim u^{\prime}v^{\prime}z\sim u^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}v
, and we take
u=u^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

The assumption that
\ell(u)\leq 1
in condition
(i)
of Lemma
2.2
cannot be dropped (and similarly for condition
(ii)
). For example, suppose
X=\{x\}
,
Y=\{y\}
, and that
\sim
is the congruence on
\{x,y\}^{*}
generated by the single relation
yx=xxyy
. Then one can show by induction that any word
\sim
-equivalent to
yxx
must have the form
x^{2k}\cdot yxx\cdot y^{2k}
or
x^{2k}\cdot xxyyx\cdot y^{2k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, the conclusion of Lemma
2.2
does not hold in this case.

For category presentations, we must work with digraphs and paths in place of alphabets and words. Keeping in mind the conventions about categories from Subsection
2.1
, let
\Gamma
be a digraph with vertex set
\mathbb{N}
, possibly with multiple/parallel edges, and possibly with loops. We identify
\Gamma
with its edge set, and denote the source and target of an edge
x\in\Gamma
by
\mathbf{d}(x)
and
\mathbf{r}(x)
respectively. The
free category
over
\Gamma
is the set
\Gamma^{*}
of all paths in
\Gamma
under concatenation (where defined). The empty path at
n\in\mathbb{N}
will be denoted by
\iota_{n}
. Every other path can be thought of as a word of the form
w=x_{1}\cdots x_{k}
, where
k\geq 1
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in\Gamma
, and where
\mathbf{r}(x_{i})=\mathbf{d}(x_{i+1})
for all
1\leq i<k
. For such a word/path, we have
\mathbf{d}(w)=\mathbf{d}(x_{1})
and
\mathbf{r}(w)=\mathbf{r}(x_{k})
. Now let
\Omega\subseteq\Gamma^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
be a set of pairs of paths, such that
\mathbf{d}(u)=\mathbf{d}(v)
and
\mathbf{r}(u)=\mathbf{r}(v)
for all
(u,v)\in\Omega
. We say a category
\mathcal{C}
(over
\mathbb{N}
) has
presentation

\textup{{Cat}}\langle{\Gamma}:{\Omega}\rangle
if
\mathcal{C}\cong\Gamma^{*}/\Omega^{\sharp}
: i.e., if there exists a surmorphism
\Gamma^{*}\to\mathcal{C}
with kernel
\Omega^{\sharp}
. If
\phi
is such a surmorphism, we say
\mathcal{C}
has
presentation
\textup{{Cat}}\langle{\Gamma}:{\Omega}\rangle
via
\phi
.

Finally, we recall the formulation of (strict) tensor category presentations from
[
17
]
. Let
\Delta
be a digraph with vertex set
\mathbb{N}
, again identified with its edge set. We will denote the
free tensor category
over
\Delta
by
\Delta^{\hskip-1.42262pt\oast}
. It consists of all terms constructed in the following way:

(T1)

All empty paths
\iota_{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
) are terms, with
\mathbf{d}(\iota_{n})=\mathbf{r}(\iota_{n})=n
, acting as identities of
\Delta^{\hskip-1.42262pt\oast}
.

(T2)

All edges
x\in\Delta
are terms, with
\mathbf{d}(x)
and
\mathbf{r}(x)
the source and target of
x
, respectively.

(T3)

If
s
and
t
are terms, and if
\mathbf{r}(s)=\mathbf{d}(t)
, then the formal expression
s\circ t
is a term, with
\mathbf{d}(s\circ t)=\mathbf{d}(s)
and
\mathbf{r}(s\circ t)=\mathbf{r}(t)
.

(T4)

If
s
and
t
are terms, then the formal expression
s\oplus t
is a term, with
\mathbf{d}(s\oplus t)=\mathbf{d}(s)+\mathbf{d}(t)
and
\mathbf{r}(s\oplus t)=\mathbf{r}(s)+\mathbf{r}(t)
.

Note that
(T1)
–
(T4)
describe the elements of
\Delta^{\hskip-1.42262pt\oast}
, while
(T3)
and
(T4)
also give the definition of the
\circ
and
\oplus
operations. Now let
\Xi\subseteq\Delta^{\hskip-1.42262pt\oast}\times\Delta^{\hskip-1.42262pt\oast}
be a set of pairs of terms, such that
\mathbf{d}(u)=\mathbf{d}(v)
and
\mathbf{r}(u)=\mathbf{r}(v)
for all
(u,v)\in\Xi
. We say a tensor category
\mathcal{C}
(over
\mathbb{N}
) has presentation
\textup{{TCat}}\langle{\Delta}:{\Xi}\rangle
if
\mathcal{C}\cong\Delta^{\hskip-1.42262pt\oast}/\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}
: i.e., if there is a surmorphism
\Delta^{\hskip-1.42262pt\oast}\to\mathcal{C}
with kernel
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}
. If
\phi
is such a surmorphism, we say
\mathcal{C}
has presentation
\textup{{TCat}}\langle{\Delta}:{\Xi}\rangle
via
\phi
.

be the following set of relations, where for convenience we write
