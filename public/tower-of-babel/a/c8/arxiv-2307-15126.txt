Aether scalar tensor theory: Hamiltonian Formalism
Marianthi Bataki, Constantinos Skordis, Tom Zlosnik

Abstract
The Aether Scalar Tensor (AeST) theory is an extension of General Relativity (GR), proposed for addressing galactic and cosmological observations without dark matter. By casting the AeST theory into a 3 + 1 3+1 form, we determine its full non-perturbative Hamiltonian formulation and analyse the resulting constraints. We find the presence of four first class and four second class constraints and show that the theory has six physical degrees of freedom at the fully nonlinear level. Our results set the basis for determining the propagation of perturbations on general backgrounds and we present the case of small perturbations around Minkowski spacetime as an example stemming from our analysis.

We conclude this section by comparing the AeST model to other extensions of GR.
It is straightforward to extend GR with the addition of a scalar field and several scalar-tensor theories have been

all possible terms at that order that may arise from the metric perturbation, or a scalar field (typically written in the unitary gauge).
The majority of the terms which are part of DHOST, or EFTofDE, do not overlap with the AeST action (
1

The only term that may overlap with DHOST is

Nevertheless, AeST could in principle be extended with additional terms for
\phi

Extending GR with a vector field is another direction that has been considered. In

V.1
Poisson brackets

For a phase space coordinatized by fields
Q_{I}(t,{\vec{x}})
and
P_{I}(t,{\vec{x}})
(where here indices

label the different fields),
it is useful to introduce the Poisson bracket defined for quantities
A(Q_{I},P_{J})
,
B(Q_{I},P_{J})
.
If
\tau_{i\ldots j}^{k\ldots l}(x)
is a general tensor field and
{\cal F}[\tau_{i\ldots j}^{k\ldots l}(x)]

a functional of
\tau_{i\ldots j}^{k\ldots l}(x)
, then the functional derivative of
{\cal F}[\tau_{i\ldots j}^{k\ldots l}(x)]
wrt
\tau_{i\ldots j}^{k\ldots l}(x)
,
in three dimensions is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta{\cal F}[\tau_{i\ldots j}^{k\ldots l}({\vec{x}})]}{\delta\tau_{a\ldots b}^{c\ldots d}({\vec{y}})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigg\{{\cal F}\big[\tau_{i\ldots j}^{k\ldots l}({\vec{x}})

\displaystyle+\epsilon\delta^{(3)}({\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}})\delta^{k}_{S\{c}\dots\delta^{l}_{d\}}\delta^{S\{a}_{i}\dots\delta^{b\}}_{j}\big]

\displaystyle-{\cal F}\left[\tau_{i\ldots j}^{k\ldots l}({\vec{x}})\right]\bigg\}

(44)

with
\delta^{(3)}({\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}})
being the three-dimensional Dirac delta-function and
S\{ab..cd\}

applies the symmetries of the tensor field
\tau
e.g. if
\tau^{ijkl}=\tau^{jikl}
,
\tau^{ijkl}=-\tau^{ijlk}
then
S\{ijkl\}=(ij)[kl]
.
With this definition, the Poisson bracket of
A(Q_{I},P_{J})
and
B(Q_{I},P_{J})
is defined as

\displaystyle\left\{A,B\right\}\equiv\sum_{I}\int d^{3}x\left(\frac{\delta A}{\delta Q_{I}}\frac{\delta B}{\delta P_{I}}-\frac{\delta A}{\delta P_{I}}\frac{\delta B}{\delta Q_{I}}\right)

(45)

For time evolution according to any general Hamiltonian
H
(corresponding to general Hamiltonian density
\mathscr{H}
)

H=\int d^{3}x\mathscr{H}

(46)

we have Hamilton’s equations for quantities

on phase space

\displaystyle\dot{f}

\displaystyle=\left\{f,H\right\}.

(47)

In our case, we test the time evolution of the constraints according to
H_{\rm pri}
, that is,
letting
{\cal C}_{I}
being any of the constraints in the set
\{{\cal H},{\cal H}_{i},\Pi^{(\mu)},\Pi^{(\nu)}\}

with
H\rightarrow H_{\rm pri}
in (
46
)
and (
47

\displaystyle{{\cal H}}[{N}]\equiv\int d^{3}yN({\vec{y}})\;{\cal H}[Q_{I}({\vec{y}}),P^{I}({\vec{y}})].

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta\tau_{b_{1}\ldots b_{s}}^{a_{1}\ldots a_{r}}}\int d^{3}y\,\sigma^{d_{1}\ldots d_{s}}_{c_{1}\ldots c_{r}}\,{\cal L}_{\vec{N}}\tau_{d_{1}\ldots d_{s}}^{c_{1}\ldots c_{r}}

\{{\cal H}_{i},{\cal H},\Pi^{(\mu)},\Pi^{(\nu)},{\cal S}^{(\mu)},{\cal S}^{(\nu)}\}
, implying eleven brackets in total.

We set the index
A=\{\mu,\nu\}
and consider collectively the vector
{{\cal S}^{(A)}}[{u^{(A)}}]
. From equation (
52
) we have:

\displaystyle\left\{{{\cal S}^{(A)}}[{u^{(A)}}],{\vec{{\cal H}}}[{\vec{N}}]\right\}

\displaystyle=-{{\cal S}^{(A)}}[{{\cal L}_{\vec{N}}u^{(A)}}]

(67)

which therefore vanish weakly. Hence,
\vec{{\cal H}}
remains a first class constraint.
The remaining nine brackets do not vanish, meaning that all other constraints are second class. Consider first

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\Pi^{(A)}}[{\lambda^{(A)}}]\approx\sum_{B}\left\{{\Pi^{(A)}}[{\lambda^{(A)}}],{{\cal S}^{(B)}}[{u^{(B)}}]\right\}\approx 0.

(68)

The involved Poisson brackets are evaluated as

\displaystyle\left\{{\Pi^{(A)}}[{\lambda^{(A)}}],{{\cal S}^{(B)}}[{u^{(B)}}]\right\}=-{C^{AB}}[{\lambda^{(A)}u^{(B)}}]

(69)

where

\displaystyle C^{AB}\equiv\frac{\partial{\cal S}^{(B)}}{\partial A}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial{\cal S}^{(\mu)}}{\partial\mu}&\frac{\partial{\cal S}^{(\mu)}}{\partial\nu}\\
\frac{\partial{\cal S}^{(\nu)}}{\partial\mu}&\frac{\partial{\cal S}^{(\nu)}}{\partial\nu}\end{pmatrix}

(70)

Then (
69
) gives two homogeneous equations for two unknowns, the Lagrange multipliers
u^{(\mu)}
and
u^{(\nu)}
, and therefore implies that they must both vanish.

Consider now

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{{\cal H}}[{N}]\approx\sum_{B}\left\{{{\cal H}}[{N}],{{\cal S}^{(B)}}[{u^{(B)}}]\right\}\approx 0.

(71)

The involved Poisson brackets are evaluated as

{U^{B}}[{\xi^{(B)}}]\equiv\left\{{{\cal H}}[{N}],{{\cal S}^{(B)}}[{\xi^{(B)}}]\right\}=\left\{{{\cal H}}[{N}],\frac{\partial{{\cal H}}[{\xi^{(B)}}]}{\partial B}\right\}

(72)

for arbitrary functions
\xi^{(A)}
(not necessarily the Lagrange multipliers
u^{(A)}
),
which then allows us to use the
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
coefficients defined in (
55
) and (
56
) and displayed in appendix
B
.
Explicitly, after some integrations by parts and further computations we find

\displaystyle U^{A}

\displaystyle=N\bigg(-{\cal B}_{(\Pi^{ij})}\frac{\partial{\cal A}_{(q_{ij})}}{\partial A}+{\cal A}_{(A_{i})}\frac{\partial{\cal B}_{(\Pi^{i})}}{\partial A}-{\cal B}_{(\Pi^{i})}\frac{\partial{\cal A}_{(A_{i})}}{\partial A}

\displaystyle+{\cal A}_{(\phi)}\frac{\partial{\cal B}_{(\Pi)}}{\partial A}+D_{i}{\cal B}_{(\Pi)}\frac{\partial{\cal A}^{i}_{(\phi)}}{\partial A}\bigg).

(73)

It is crucial to note that
U^{(A)}
can vanish neither strongly nor weakly. This can be easily seen by focussing on the

{\cal B}_{(\Pi^{ij})}\frac{\partial{\cal A}_{(q_{ij})}}{\partial A}
term, which is the only term containing
\Pi^{ij}
and thus cannot be cancelled by other terms.
Back to (
72
), we set
\xi^{(B)}\rightarrow u^{(B)}\approx 0
which results in
\partial_{t}{{\cal H}}[{N}]={U^{(\mu)}}[{u^{(\mu)}}]+{U^{(\nu)}}[{u^{(\nu)}}]\approx 0
without requiring any additional constraints
or equations involving the Lagrange multipliers.

Lastly, consider
\partial_{t}{{\cal S}^{(A)}}[{\xi^{(A)}}]
. Using our definitions from (
69
) and (
72
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{{\cal S}^{(A)}}[{\xi^{(A)}}]=

\displaystyle-{U^{A}}[{\xi^{(A)}}]-\sum_{B}{C^{AB}}[{\xi^{(A)}\lambda^{(B)}}]

\displaystyle+\sum_{B}\left\{{{\cal S}^{(A)}}[{\xi^{(A)}}],{{\cal S}^{(B)}}[{u^{(B)}}]\right\}

(74)

Now, the last Poisson bracket depends linearly on
u^{(B)}
(and its derivative) and the same for
\xi^{(A)}
, hence, it vanishes when
u^{(B)}\approx 0
. Require then that

\partial_{t}{{\cal S}^{(A)}}[{\xi^{(A)}}]\approx 0
leads to the linear system of equations

\displaystyle U^{A}+\sum_{B}C^{AB}\lambda^{(B)}\approx 0

(75)

If the matrix
C^{AB}
is invertible then the two equations above
determine
the Lagrange multipliers
\lambda^{A}
which then become functions of all the phase-space variables.
Then the Hamiltonian analysis is complete and no further constraints in phase-space are required, with the conclusion that the theory possesses
three first class primary constraints
{\cal H}_{i}
,
three second class primary constraints
{\cal H}
and
\Pi^{(A)}
, and two second class secondary constraints
{\cal S}^{(A)}
. We list all the constraints and their Poisson brackets
in table
1
.
On the other hand, if the matrix has a vanishing determinant, there exists a left null eigenvector which when applied
to the consistency relation may produce further constraints, called
tertiary
constraints. Given the forms
of (
64
),(
65
) and equations of motion (
62
),(
63
) it is to be expected that
C^{AB}
is indeed invertible in general situations.

V.5
Hamiltonian evolution

Having found all the constraints and solved for the Lagrange multipliers, we may now form the
first class
Hamiltonian
H_{\rm FC}
. We find this as the secondary Hamiltonian with the
Lagrange multiplies subbed-in. Given that
u^{(A)}\approx 0
, we have that
H_{\rm sec}\approx H_{\rm pri}
, hence,

H_{\rm FC}=\int d^{3}x\left[N{\cal H}+N^{i}{\cal H}_{i}+\lambda^{(\mu)}\Pi^{(\mu)}+\lambda^{(\nu)}\Pi^{(\nu)}\right]

(76)

with
\lambda^{(\mu)}
and
\lambda^{(\nu)}
being functions of the phase-space variables
\{q_{ij},A_{i},\phi,\Pi^{ij},\Pi^{i},\Pi,\mu,\nu\}
, that is,
\Pi^{(\mu)}
and
\Pi^{(\nu)}
are absent from

\lambda^{(A)}
. Hamilton’s equations are then found using (
47
) with
H\rightarrow H_{\rm FC}
. We find

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{ij}\approx

\displaystyle N{\cal B}_{(\Pi^{ij})}+2D_{(i}N_{j)}

(77a)

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}^{ij}\approx

\displaystyle-N{\cal A}_{(q_{ij})}-2\Pi^{k(j}D_{k}N^{i)}+D_{k}\left(N^{k}\Pi^{ij}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{q}}{16\pi\tilde{G}}\left(D^{i}D^{j}N-q^{ij}\vec{D}^{2}N\right)

(77b)

\displaystyle\dot{A}_{i}\approx

\displaystyle N{\cal B}_{(\Pi^{i})}-\chi D_{i}N+N^{j}D_{j}A_{i}+A_{j}D_{i}N^{j}

(77c)

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}^{i}\approx

\displaystyle-N{\cal A}_{(A_{i})}-{\cal A}^{j}_{(A_{i})}D_{j}N+D_{k}\left(N^{k}\Pi^{i}\right)-\Pi^{j}D_{j}N^{i}

(77d)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}\approx

\displaystyle N{\cal B}_{(\Pi)}+N^{i}D_{i}\phi

(77e)

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}\approx

\displaystyle D_{i}\left(\Pi N^{i}-N{\cal A}^{i}_{(\phi)}\right)

(77f)

\displaystyle\dot{\mu}\approx

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\mu)}+N^{i}D_{i}\mu

(77g)

\displaystyle\dot{\nu}\approx

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\nu)}+N^{i}D_{i}\nu

(77h)

along with the constraints (
43
), (
62
) and (
63
), and where to reiterate,
\lambda^{(A)}
are functions of all the phase-space variables
obtained after solving (
75
).

VI
An example: Small perturbations around Minkowski spacetime

The Hamiltonian density is rather complicated in general so for illustration it is useful to have a look at a simple example.
We consider a background Minkowski spacetime and the linear evolution of small perturbations to this background.

VI.1
Background Minkowski solution

Before discussing the choice of function
{\cal U}(\nu,\mu)
, let us first determine what conditions a Minkowski background imposes on the fields, in the general sense.
Note that a cosmological constant
\Lambda
can always be absorbed into the definition of
{\cal U}(\nu,\mu)
, hence, we set
\Lambda=0
without any loss of generality.
We use an overbar to denote the values that fields take in the background and we fix the background gauge to

\displaystyle\bar{N}=1\qquad\bar{N}^{i}=0.

(78)

Moreover, for Minkowski spacetime we have
\bar{q}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
and then (
77a
) trivially gives
\bar{\Pi}^{ij}=0
(and so
\bar{{\cal B}}_{(\Pi^{ij})}=0
). Now, the background solution should not
violate the isometries of Minkowski spacetime, including rotational invariance, hence, all three dimensional vector fields should vanish:

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{i}=\bar{\Pi}^{i}=D_{i}{\bar{\phi}}=0

(79)

so that (
77c
) and (
77d
) are trivially satisfied
(and
\bar{{\cal B}}_{(\Pi^{i})}=\bar{{\cal A}}_{(A_{i})}=\bar{{\cal A}}^{i}_{(A_{j})}=0
,
while
\bar{{\cal B}}^{i}_{(\Pi^{j})}=-\delta^{i}_{\;\;j}
). The conditions (
79
)
in turn imply that
\bar{\chi}=1
and
{\bar{\phi}}={\bar{\phi}}(t)
leading in addition to
\bar{{\cal Y}}=0
. Hence, from (
32
) we find

\bar{\Xi}={\bar{\nu}}
and from (
38
) we get
\bar{C}_{1}=\bar{\Pi}
.

Meanwhile, (
79
) leads to
\bar{{\cal A}}^{i}_{(\phi)}=0
(and hence
\bar{{\cal A}}_{(\phi)}=0
) so that
from (
73
) we find
\bar{U}^{A}=0
and hence, (
75
) leads to
\lambda^{(A)}=0
. This last condition then implies
through (
77g
) and (
77h
) that
{\bar{\mu}}
and
{\bar{\nu}}
are functions of
\vec{x}
only.
Moreover, (
77f
) implies that
\bar{\Pi}
is also a function of
\vec{x}
only.

Now from (
77b
) we have that
\bar{{\cal A}}_{(q_{ij})}=0
and this imposes that

\bar{{\cal U}}=(8\pi\tilde{G})^{2}\bar{\Pi}^{2}/{\bar{\nu}}
. Finally, we have that
\bar{{\cal B}}_{(\Pi)}=8\pi\tilde{G}\bar{\Pi}/{\bar{\nu}}
is time-independent, so that
(
77e
) may be integrated to get
{\bar{\phi}}={\cal Q}_{0}t
, where
{\cal Q}_{0}=8\pi\tilde{G}\bar{\Pi}/{\bar{\nu}}
is a constant. We thus set

\displaystyle 8\pi\tilde{G}\bar{\Pi}={\cal Q}_{0}{\bar{\nu}}

(80)

without loss of generality, implying that
\bar{\Pi}
and
{\bar{\nu}}
have the same
\vec{x}
dependence, and that
{\cal Q}_{0}
, being a constant, is independent of
{\bar{\nu}}
.

Now (
64
) gives
\bar{{\cal Q}}={\cal Q}_{0}

so that the constraint (
63
) gives
\partial{\cal U}/\partial{\bar{\nu}}={\cal Q}_{0}^{2}
. Since we also have that
that
\partial{\cal U}/\partial{\bar{\mu}}=0
from (
63
), we find that

\displaystyle\bar{{\cal U}}={\cal Q}_{0}^{2}{\bar{\nu}}

(81)

which is consistent with our discussion in the previous paragraph. Note that the above condition, is a condition that
{\cal U}
must satisfy in order to have Minkowski solutions
but it does not completely determine the general form of the function
{\cal U}
.

Finally, before discussing function choices, let us connect with the original function
{\cal F}
. From (
15
) we find the relations

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\partial{\cal F}}{\partial{\cal Y}}

(82)

\displaystyle\nu=-\frac{\partial{\cal F}}{\partial{\cal Q}^{2}}

(83)

Hence, since
\bar{{\cal Y}}=0
and
\bar{{\cal Q}}={\cal Q}_{0}
is a constant, this implies that
{\cal F}
and its derivatives will also be, at best, constants. We immediately get that
both
{\bar{\mu}}
and
{\bar{\nu}}
are constants and hence, so is
\bar{\Pi}
. Incidentally, (
15
) returns
\bar{{\cal F}}=0
.

VI.2
Choice of function

We now turn to the choice of function
{\cal U}(\nu,\mu)
in order to pave the way for departures from Minkowski.
Our goal here is to compare to
Skordis and Zlosnik 2022
, hence, we restrict ourselves to cases where the function
{\cal F}({\cal Y},{\cal Q})
of (
1
) takes the form

\displaystyle{\cal F}({\cal Y},{\cal Q})

\displaystyle=(2-K_{{B}})\lambda_{s}{\cal Y}-2{\cal K}_{2}\left({\cal Q}-{\cal Q}_{0}\right)^{2}

(84)

where
\lambda_{s}
and
{\cal K}_{2}
are constants.
The above functional form is motivated by making sure that at the weak field quasistatic limit Newtonian gravity is recovered and that
the large-scale FLRW cosmology admits dust solutions. Exact Minkowski is recovered when
{\cal Y}=0
and
{\cal Q}={\cal Q}_{0}
in accordance with the previous subsection.

We then find that

\displaystyle\mu=(2-K_{{B}})\lambda_{s}

(85)

and

\displaystyle\nu=2{\cal K}_{2}\left(1-\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}}{{\cal Q}}\right)

(86)

which inverts to

\displaystyle{\cal Q}=\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}}{1-\frac{\nu}{2{\cal K}_{2}}}

(87)

so that

\displaystyle{\cal U}=

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}^{2}\nu}{1-\frac{\nu}{2{\cal K}_{2}}}

(88)

Using the above functional form into the constraint (
62
) leads to
{\cal Y}=0
, while constraint (
63
) returns back
(
87
). The last relation also leads to

\displaystyle{\bar{\nu}}=0,

(89)

and hence,

\displaystyle\bar{\Pi}=0.

(90)

VI.3
Hamiltonian density to 2nd order

We perturb the lapse function as

\displaystyle N=

\displaystyle 1+\Psi

(91)

where
\Psi
is a small perturbation and let the shift
N_{i}=h_{i}
be a pure perturbation (zero background).
Additionally, we perturb our phase-space variables as

\displaystyle q_{ij}=

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}+h_{ij}

(92)

\displaystyle\Pi^{ij}=

\displaystyle\varpi^{ij}

(93)

\displaystyle A_{i}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

(94)

\displaystyle\Pi^{i}=

\displaystyle\varpi^{i}

(95)

\displaystyle\phi=

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{0}t+\varphi

(96)

\displaystyle\Pi=

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}}{8\pi\tilde{G}}{\bar{\nu}}+\varpi

(97)

\displaystyle\nu=

\displaystyle{\bar{\nu}}+{\delta\nu}

(98)

where
h_{ij}
,
\alpha_{i}
,
\varphi
,
\varpi^{ij}
,
\varpi^{i}
,
\varpi
and
{\delta\nu}
are small perturbations,
and we further define
h\equiv h_{i}^{\;\;i}
. We raise and lower indices using the background metric
\delta_{ij}
.

The variable
\mu
is fixed to (
85
) as it is a constant. We keep
{\bar{\nu}}
in all calculations where the background otherwise vanishes and take the limit
{\bar{\nu}}\rightarrow 0

only at the end. We first calculate the secondary constraints, the first of which leads to
{\cal S}^{(\mu)}=0
for the chosen function.
To expand the constraint (
63
), that is
{\cal S}^{(\nu)}\approx 0
, to first order and determine
{\delta\nu}
we need
{\cal Q}
and
{\cal U}
to first order, however, since the latter is also needed for the Hamiltonian constraint
to second order, we compute that to get

\displaystyle{\cal Q}=

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{0}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}h-\frac{{\delta\nu}}{{\bar{\nu}}}+8\pi\tilde{G}\frac{\varpi}{{\cal Q}_{0}{\bar{\nu}}}\right),

(99)

\displaystyle{\cal U}=

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{0}^{2}\left[{\bar{\nu}}+{\delta\nu}+\frac{{(\delta\nu)^{2}}}{2{\cal K}_{2}}\right].

(100)

Thus, into (
63
) we find

\displaystyle{\delta\nu}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{{\bar{\nu}}}{2{\cal K}_{2}}}\left(8\pi\tilde{G}\frac{\varpi}{{\cal Q}_{0}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bar{\nu}}h\right).

(101)

We now compute the diffeomorphism constraint (
36
) which leads to

\displaystyle N^{i}{\cal H}_{i}\approx

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}{\bar{\nu}}}{8\pi\tilde{G}}h^{i}\vec{\nabla}_{i}\varphi-2h^{i}\vec{\nabla}_{j}\varpi^{j}_{\;\;i}

(102)

Finally, we compute the Hamiltonian constraint (
37
). Since
\vec{A}\cdot\vec{D}\phi
is second order, we need to zeroth order
C_{2}=(2-K_{{B}})(2+K_{{B}}\lambda_{s})/K_{{B}}

and
C_{1}
and
\Xi
to second order. These are calculated as

\displaystyle C_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}}{8\pi\tilde{G}}{\bar{\nu}}+\varpi-\frac{2-K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}\alpha^{i}\varpi_{i}

(103)

\displaystyle\Xi=

\displaystyle{\bar{\nu}}+{\delta\nu}+\left[{\bar{\nu}}-\frac{(2-K_{{B}})(2+K_{{B}}\lambda_{s})}{K_{{B}}}\right]\alpha_{i}\alpha^{i}

(104)

and into (
37
) we find

\displaystyle N{\cal H}=

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}^{2}}{8\pi\tilde{G}}{\bar{\nu}}+{\cal Q}_{0}\varpi+\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}^{2}}{8\pi\tilde{G}}{\bar{\nu}}\Psi+8\pi\tilde{G}\left[2\varpi^{ij}\varpi_{ij}-\hat{\varpi}^{2}+\frac{1}{2K_{{B}}}|\vec{\varpi}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4{\cal K}_{2}}\frac{1}{1+\frac{{\bar{\nu}}}{2{\cal K}_{2}}}\varpi^{2}\right]-\frac{2-K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}{\cal Q}_{0}\alpha^{j}\varpi_{j}

\displaystyle-\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}{\bar{\nu}}}{4{\cal K}_{2}}\frac{1}{1+\frac{{\bar{\nu}}}{2{\cal K}_{2}}}\varpi h-\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}^{2}{\bar{\nu}}}{16\pi\tilde{G}}\left[\frac{1}{8}\frac{1-\frac{{\bar{\nu}}}{2{\cal K}_{2}}}{1+\frac{{\bar{\nu}}}{2{\cal K}_{2}}}h^{2}+\frac{1}{4}h^{ij}h_{ij}+|\vec{\alpha}|^{2}\right]+{\cal Q}_{0}\Psi\varpi-\frac{{\cal Q}_{0}{\bar{\nu}}}{8\pi\tilde{G}}\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\phi+\Psi\vec{\nabla}_{i}\varpi^{i}-\frac{2-K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}\vec{\varpi}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\varphi

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{16\pi\tilde{G}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{4}|\vec{\nabla}h|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\vec{\nabla}_{i}h\vec{\nabla}_{j}h^{ij}-\frac{1}{4}\vec{\nabla}_{k}h^{ij}\left(\vec{\nabla}_{i}h^{k}_{\;\;j}+\vec{\nabla}_{j}h^{k}_{\;\;i}-\vec{\nabla}^{k}h_{ij}\right)+\vec{\nabla}_{i}\Psi\vec{\nabla}_{j}h^{ij}-\vec{\nabla}\Psi\cdot\vec{\nabla}h\bigg]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{16\pi\tilde{G}}\bigg[K_{{B}}|\vec{B}|^{2}+(2-K_{{B}})\frac{2+\lambda_{s}K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}|\vec{\nabla}\varphi+{\cal Q}_{0}\vec{\alpha}|^{2}\bigg].

(105)

We may now set
{\bar{\nu}}=0
and combine the two to get the second order Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\bigg\{8\pi\tilde{G}\left[2\varpi^{ij}\varpi_{ij}-\hat{\varpi}^{2}+\frac{1}{2K_{{B}}}|\vec{\varpi}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4{\cal K}_{2}}\varpi^{2}\right]-\frac{2-K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}\vec{\varpi}\cdot\left(\vec{\nabla}\varphi+{\cal Q}_{0}\vec{\alpha}\right)+\frac{1}{16\pi\tilde{G}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{4}|\vec{\nabla}h|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\vec{\nabla}_{i}h\vec{\nabla}_{j}h^{ij}-\frac{1}{4}\vec{\nabla}_{k}h^{ij}\left(\vec{\nabla}_{i}h^{k}_{\;\;j}+\vec{\nabla}_{j}h^{k}_{\;\;i}-\vec{\nabla}^{k}h_{ij}\right)+K_{{B}}|\vec{B}|^{2}+(2-K_{{B}})\frac{2+\lambda_{s}K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}|\vec{\nabla}\varphi+{\cal Q}_{0}\vec{\alpha}|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\Psi\left[{\cal Q}_{0}\varpi+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{\varpi}+\frac{1}{16\pi\tilde{G}}\left(\vec{\nabla}^{2}h-\vec{\nabla}_{i}\vec{\nabla}_{j}h^{ij}\right)\right]-2h^{i}\vec{\nabla}_{j}\varpi^{j}_{\;\;i}\bigg\}

(106)

Having found the second order Hamiltonian, we compare with the results from
Skordis and Zlosnik 2022
, which only find the scalar mode Hamiltonian.
The Lagrangian and corresponding Hamiltonian in
Skordis and Zlosnik 2022
is multiplied by
16\pi\tilde{G}
compared with what we have in this work, and we should do the same here
for proper comparison. Doing that leads to a rescaling of canonical momenta by
16\pi\tilde{G}
, that is, we define
\tilde{\varpi}^{ij}\equiv 16\pi\tilde{G}\varpi^{ij}
and similarly for
\varpi^{i}
and
\varpi
.

We define the traceless operator
D_{ij}\equiv\vec{\nabla}_{i}\vec{\nabla}_{j}-\frac{1}{3}\vec{\nabla}^{2}\delta_{ij}
and
expand the perturbations in scalar modes as

\displaystyle h_{ij}=

\displaystyle-2\Phi\delta_{ij}+D_{ij}\eta

(107)

\displaystyle\tilde{\varpi}^{ij}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{6}P_{\Phi}\delta^{ij}+\frac{3}{2\vec{\nabla}^{4}}D^{ij}P_{\eta}

(108)

\displaystyle A_{i}=

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}_{i}\alpha

(109)

\displaystyle\tilde{\varpi}^{i}=

\displaystyle-\vec{\nabla}^{i}\frac{1}{\vec{\nabla}^{2}}P_{\alpha}

(110)

\displaystyle h_{i}=

\displaystyle-\vec{\nabla}_{i}\zeta

(111)

so that
\{\Phi,P_{\Phi}\}
,
\{\eta,P_{\eta}\}
,
\{\alpha,P_{\alpha}\}
and
\{\varphi,P_{\varphi}\}
form canonical pairs, that is,

\int dtd^{3}x\left[\dot{h}_{ij}\varpi^{ij}+\dot{\alpha}_{i}\varpi^{i}\right]=\int dtd^{3}x\left[\dot{\Phi}P_{\Phi}+\dot{\eta}P_{\eta}+\dot{\alpha}P_{\alpha}\right]
.
Using the above expressions into (
106
) we find the scalar-mode Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\bigg\{-\frac{1}{24}P_{\Phi}^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\left|\frac{1}{\vec{\nabla}^{2}}P_{\eta}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{4K_{{B}}}|\vec{\nabla}\frac{1}{\vec{\nabla}^{2}}P_{\alpha}|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{2-K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}P_{\alpha}\left(\varphi+{\cal Q}_{0}\alpha\right)-2\left|\vec{\nabla}\left(\Phi+\frac{1}{6}\vec{\nabla}^{2}\eta\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{8{\cal K}_{2}}P_{\varphi}^{2}+(2-K_{{B}})\frac{2+\lambda_{s}K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}|\vec{\nabla}\left(\varphi+{\cal Q}_{0}\alpha\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle+\Psi\left[{\cal Q}_{0}P_{\varphi}-P_{\alpha}-4\vec{\nabla}^{2}\left(\Phi+\frac{1}{6}\vec{\nabla}^{2}\eta\right)\right]

\displaystyle+2\zeta\left(\frac{1}{6}\vec{\nabla}^{2}P_{\Phi}-P_{\eta}\right)\bigg\}

(112)

Our comparison is, however, not yet finished because in
Skordis and Zlosnik 2022
one of the variables used is not
\varphi
but the combination
\chi\equiv\varphi+{\cal Q}_{0}\alpha
.
We thus perform a canonical transformation to new canonical pairs
\{\chi,P_{\chi}\}
and
\{{\tilde{\alpha}},P_{\tilde{\alpha}}\}
defined through

{\tilde{\alpha}}=\alpha
,
P_{\chi}=P_{\varphi}
and
P_{\tilde{\alpha}}=P_{\alpha}-{\cal Q}_{0}P_{\varphi}
, and switch to Fourier space (where
\vec{k}
denotes the Fourier wavevector) to get

\displaystyle H^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg\{-\frac{1}{24}\left|P_{\Phi}\right|^{2}+\frac{3}{2k^{4}}\left|P_{\eta}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{8{\cal K}_{2}}\left|P_{\chi}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{4k^{2}K_{{B}}}\left|{\cal Q}_{0}P_{\chi}+P_{\tilde{\alpha}}\right|^{2}-\frac{2-K_{{B}}}{2K_{{B}}}[\chi\left(P_{\tilde{\alpha}}^{*}+{\cal Q}_{0}P_{\chi}^{*}\right)+c.c.]

\displaystyle-2k^{2}\left|\Phi-\frac{1}{6}k^{2}\eta\right|^{2}+(2-K_{{B}})\frac{2+\lambda_{s}K_{{B}}}{K_{{B}}}k^{2}|\chi|^{2}+\Psi C_{\Psi}^{*}+\Psi^{*}C_{\Psi}+\zeta C_{\zeta}^{*}+\zeta^{*}C_{\zeta}\bigg\}

(113)

where

\displaystyle C_{\Psi}\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}P_{\tilde{\alpha}}+2k^{2}\left(\Phi-\frac{1}{6}k^{2}\eta\right)\approx 0

(114)

\displaystyle C_{\zeta}\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{6}k^{2}P_{\Phi}-P_{\eta}\approx 0

(115)

are two constraints imposed by the Lagrange multipliers
\Psi
and
\zeta
, and where for brevity we use the same variable symbols in Fourier space as in real space.
The resulting Hamiltonian (
113
) is identical to the one found in
Skordis and Zlosnik 2022
, up to some symbol relabeling.

VII
Conclusions

In this article, we presented the general Hamiltonian analysis for AeST theory, which extends GR with the inclusion of a unit-timelike vector field
\hat{A}^{\mu}
and scalar field
\phi
in addition
to the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
. To simplify the computations we further introduced two auxiliary fields
\mu
and
\nu
in order to avoid inversion of the free function
{\cal F}
which is part of the theory.
Our analysis revealed the existence of
four first class constraints and four second class constraints.
The first class constraints consist of the three (primary) constraints
{\cal H}_{i}
defined in (
36
)
and the first class Hamiltonian
H_{\rm FC}
defined in (
76
),
which is the linear combination of the primary Hamiltonian constraint
{\cal H}
defined in (
37
) (by itself second class),

{\cal H}_{i}
and also
\Pi^{(\mu)}
and
\Pi^{(\nu)}
.
The four second class constraints are the two canonical momenta
\Pi^{(\mu)}
and

We may use the constraint analysis to count the number of physical degrees of freedom.
We have six variables in the spatial metric
q_{ij}
, three in
A_{i}
and one for each of
\phi
,
\mu
and
\nu
, that is
12
in total. Counting in the canonical momenta doubles this to
24
.
We subtract the four second class constraints and twice the number of first class constraints which remove the gauge redundant degrees of freedom, that is,
we subtract
12
degrees of freedom because of the constraints. We finally divide by two to find six physical degrees of freedom.
It is interesting to compare this number to the result found in the case of SVT theories, specifically the case of broken gauge-invariance
which also propagate six degrees of freedom
Heisenberg 2018
. However, as discussed in Section
II
, there is no obvious overlap of AeST with SVT
and there is no a-priori reason why the matching number of degrees of freedom would represent similar field structure.
We also note, that the additional degrees of freedom propagating may lead to interesting features, such as, additional polarization modes for gravitational waves, or perhaps,
new couplings to matter (which are not part of AeST). These could lead to important observational consequences which could then be used to put constraints on the theory.

Taking linear perturbations around a Minkowski background we expanded the Hamiltonian to quadratic order and recovered the same results found in
Skordis and Zlosnik 2022
using different methods.
In the process we showed that the number of perturbative degrees of freedom found in
Skordis and Zlosnik 2022
matches the number found here using the full non-linear theory.
Our formalism may be used to compute the quadratic Hamiltonian of AeST theory on other backgrounds in order to determine whether those backgrounds are stable or not. Of particular interest
are the case of de Sitter space and static spherically symmetric configurations which we leave for a future work.
