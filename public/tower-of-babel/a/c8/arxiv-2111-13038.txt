On the dimension and structure of the square of the dual of a Goppa code
Rocco Mora, Jean–Pierre Tillich

Abstract
. The Goppa Code Distinguishing (GD) problem asks to distinguish efficiently a generator matrix of a Goppa code from a randomly drawn one. We revisit a distinguisher for alternant and Goppa codes through a new approach, namely by studying the dimension of square codes. We provide here a rigorous upper bound for the dimension of the square of the dual of an alternant or Goppa code, while the previous approach only provided algebraic explanations based on heuristics. Moreover, for Goppa codes, our proof extends to the non-binary case as well, thus providing an algebraic explanation for the distinguisher which was missing up to now. All the upper bounds are tight and match experimental evidence. Our work also introduces new algebraic results about products of trace codes in general and of dual of alternant and Goppa codes in particular, clarifying their square code structure. This might be of interest for cryptanalysis purposes.



1.
Introduction

The McEliece scheme.

The McEliece encryption scheme
[
McE78
]
, which dates back to 1978, is a code-based cryptosystem built upon the family of binary Goppa codes. It is equipped with very fast encryption and decryption algorithms and has very small ciphertexts. It is also widely viewed as a viable quantum safe cryptosystem, since the best quantum algorithm for breaking it
[
KT17
]
has exponential complexity and the corresponding exponent barely improves the exponent of the best classical algorithm
[
BM17
]
by about
40
percent.

Over the years, the attempts to attack McEliece scheme moved in two main directions. One hand, we have
message-recovery attacks
. They consist in inverting the McEliece encryption without finding a trapdoor and make use of general decoding algorithms. Despite considerable improvements
[
LB88
,
Ste88
,
CC98
,
MMT11
,
BJMM12
,
MO15
,
BM17
]
, all these algorithms have exponential complexity. The parameters of McEliece-like schemes have then been intentionally chosen to thwart this attack, because it is considered as the main threat to the scheme. Despite all these efforts, the original McEliece cryptosystem
[
McE78
]
based on binary Goppa codes remains, after more than forty years, unbroken, be it by a classical or a quantum computer. It is now the oldest public-key cryptosystem with this feature.

The other way to attack the cryptosystem is by seeking to recover the private key.
For a long time it was widely believed that even a simpler task which is just to distinguish efficiently a generator matrix of a Goppa code from a randomly drawn generator matrix with non negligible probability was unfeasible. This is the so called
Goppa Code Distinguishing (GD) problem
as introduced by the authors of
[
CFS01
]
. The nice feature of this problem is that it is possible to devise a security proof for the McEliece scheme based solely on the intractability of this problem and decoding a generic linear code
[
Sen10
]
.
The belief about GD problem hardness was basically justified by the fact that Goppa codes behave like random codes in many aspects. For instance, they asymptotically meet the Gilbert-Varshamov bound, their weight distribution is roughly the same as those of random codes and they generally have a trivial permutation group. The absence of significant breakthrough in key-recovery attacks also strengthened the idea that the Goppa Code distinguishing problem is difficult. This problem was actually used for a long time as a problem which basically captures the hardness of recovering the private key of a Goppa code.

A distinguisher for high rate.

However, this belief was severely questioned in
[
FGO+11
,
FGO+13
]
which gave a polynomial time algorithm that distinguishes between Goppa codes (or more generally alternant codes) and random ones from their generator matrices at least for very high rate codes. It is based on the kernel of a linear system related to an algebraic system that encodes the key-recovery problem for McEliece cryptosystem instantiated with alternant or Goppa codes. Indeed, it was shown to have an unexpectedly high dimension. This distinguisher was later on given another interpretation in
[
MCP12
]
, where it was proved that this dimension is related to the dimension of the square of the dual of the public code.
The algebraic explanations given in
[
FGO+13
]
do not represent however a rigorous proof of the dimension of the kernel sought, but they rely on heuristic considerations. Indeed, while a set of vectors is proposed as candidate for the kernel basis, its elements are neither proved to be independent nor a set of generators.
Despite that the experiments run in
[
FGO+13
]
show a regular behavior when alternant codes are defined by picking at random support and multiplier vectors, it is possible to artificially choose alternant codes whose kernel dimensions are even larger than for random ones. Moreover, in the case of Goppa codes, even if a general formula for the dimension of the kernel was provided which matches the experimental evidence, an algebraic explanation was only provided in the case of binary Goppa codes with square free Goppa polynomials. This explanation crucially relies on the fact that binary Goppa codes are in this case also Goppa codes of a higher degree (with a Goppa polynomial being the square of the original polynomial). Clearly, this approach does not generalize
to non binary Goppa codes.

Our contribution

In the present article, we revisit the distinguisher for random alternant codes and Goppa codes. We do so by exploiting the link given by
[
MCP12
]
. Indeed we provide a rigorous upper bound on the dimension of the square code of the dual of an alternant or a Goppa code that coincides with the experiments. By using
[
MCP12
]
, this also gives a lower bound on the dimension of the kernel of the matrix considered in
[
FGO+13
]
. Together with results about the typical dimension of the square of random codes
[
CCMZ15
]
, this provides the first rigorous analysis about the effectiveness of the approach pioneered in
[
FGO+11
]
, because the typical dimension of the square of a random code
is way larger than this upper-bound on the dimension of the square of the dual of a Goppa or alternant code.

Our approach relies on several new ingredients

•

a new result about the square of trace codes showing that if essentially the square of a code is abnormally small then the square of its trace code is also abnormally small in a certain region of parameters. By interpreting the dual of an alternant code or a Goppa code as the trace of a generalized Reed-Solomon code (whose dimension of the square is known to be abnormally small
[
Wie10
]
) this shows that the square of a dual of an alternant code or a Goppa code is abnormally small.

•

While this approach explains rigorously why alternant codes or Goppa codes can be distinguished for extremely large rates, lower rates require a much delicate analysis, in particular in the Goppa case. We do so, by noticing that the square of a trace of a code
\mathscr{C}
can be interpreted as a sum of traces of products of
\mathscr{C}
with
\mathscr{C}^{q^{i}}
(which denotes
i
applications of the Frobenius map to
\mathscr{C}
). In the case of Goppa codes, we show that the traces of these products turn out to be duals of alternant codes of a remarkably low degree at least for small values of
i
(see Theorem
23
). This accounts for the remarkably low dimension of the square of the dual of Goppa codes in all cases considered in
[
FGO+13
]
.

Interestingly enough, the latter argument applies to
all
kinds of Goppa codes, be they binary or not and provides now not only a rigorous explanation of the distinguisher found in
[
FGO+11
]
, but also covers the non-binary Goppa code case as well.
Note that even if this approach is not able to distinguish the Goppa codes proposed in the NIST competition as shown in Table
1
, because it only works for very high rate Goppa codes, this still raises the issue whether this distinguishing approach can be improved to lower the dimension of the Goppa codes that can be distinguished by this approach. The better understanding of the distinguisher obtained here might help to address this issue.

of dimension

Vectors and matrices will be denoted by bold letters

,

2.1.
Subfield subcodes and trace codes

In this article we will often consider a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
and its subfield
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. It is therefore useful to fix a basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
:

\{\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m-1}\}.

We will also make use of a
normal basis

\{\beta,\beta^{q},\dots,\beta^{q^{m-1}}\}

whenever fruitful.

A useful linear map from
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
to its subfield
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the trace operator.

Definition 1
.

Given the finite field extension
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we define the
trace
operator
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
as

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x^{q^{i}}.

The definition extends to vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
so that the trace acts component-wise:

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=(\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(x_{1}),\dots,\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(x_{n}))

and to codes
\mathscr{C}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{C})=\{\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\boldsymbol{c})\mid\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{C}\}.

We remark that if
\mathscr{C}=\left\langle\,\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq k\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}
then
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{C})
is a linear code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{C})=\left\langle\,\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\alpha_{j}\boldsymbol{c}_{i})\mid 0\leq j<m,1\leq i\leq k\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}.

Informally, multiplying the generators of
\mathscr{C}
inside the trace by each element
\alpha_{j}
of the extension field basis takes into account the fact that
\mathscr{C}
is a code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
, while
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{C})
over the subfield
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. So, if
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}=k
then typically
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{C})=mk
, unless
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{C})
coincides with the ambient space.
From now on, we will omit the extension field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
and simply write
\operatorname{Tr}
, whenever the former is clear from the context.

The main classical result linking trace codes to subfield subcodes is Delsarte’s theorem.

Theorem 2
.

(Delsarte’s theorem
[
Del75
]
)
Let
\mathscr{C}
be a code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
. Then

(\mathscr{C}_{\rvert\mathbb{F}_{q}})^{\perp}=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C}^{\perp}),

where
\mathscr{C}_{\rvert\mathbb{F}_{q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathscr{C}\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}
denotes the subfield subcode over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of
\mathscr{C}
.

2.2.
Reed-Solomon, alternant and Goppa codes

We first recall the definitions of some well-known classes of algebraic codes. In the following we will always denote with
r
the dimension of a generalized Reed-Solomon code. We start with the definition of the latter, seen as an
evaluation code
:

Definition 3
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
be a vector of pairwise distinct entries and
{\boldsymbol{y}}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
a vector of nonzero entries. The
[n,r]

generalized Reed-Solomon (GRS) code
with
support

{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
multiplier

{\boldsymbol{y}}
is

\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{(y_{1}P(x_{1}),\dots,y_{n}P(x_{n}))\mid P\in\mathbb{F}[z],\deg P<r\}

This code can be conveniently generated by vectors that are component-wise (also called Schur) products of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}
. Recall that this product is defined as

Definition 4
.

The
component-wise product
of two vectors
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
is defined as

{\boldsymbol{a}}\star{\boldsymbol{b}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(a_{1}b_{1},\dots,a_{n}b_{n}).

Since any polynomial
P
in
\mathbb{F}[z]
of degree
<r
can be written as a linear combination over
\mathbb{F}
of powers of

of degree
<r

\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\langle\,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{a}{\boldsymbol{y}}\mid 0\leq a<r\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}},

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{a}
stands for the component-wise product

with itself repeated

The dual of a GRS code is also a GRS code, where the support and the multiplier are related to the ones of the primal code. In order to explicit such relation we introduce the polynomial

\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-x_{i})\in\mathbb{F}[z].

Proposition 5
.

[

Let

\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp}=\text{\bf GRS}_{n-r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\perp}),

{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\perp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left(\frac{1}{\pi^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(x_{1})y_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{\pi^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(x_{n})y_{n}}\right)

and
\pi^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is the derivative of
\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

An

can be defined as the subfield subcode of a GRS code:

Definition 6
.

, for some positive integer

The integer

is called

Note that by Proposition
5

\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{\bf GRS}_{n-r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\perp})\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}.

We use the same notation as in
[

We observe that for

From Delsarte’s theorem (Theorem

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\left(\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\right)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left((\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp})^{\perp}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\right).

The dimension of an alternant code of order

built upon an extension field of degree

has therefore dimension at least

.
There exists a subclass of alternant codes which is particularly attractive for cryptographic purposes:

Definition 7
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
be a support vector and
\Gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[z]

The reason why binary Goppa codes are preferable to instantiate McEliece-like schemes is that, if the Goppa polynomial has no multiple roots, there exists a polynomial time algorithm to decode up to

be a binary Goppa polynomial

Square of codes

GRS codes turn out to display a very peculiar property with respect to the component-wise/Schur product of codes which is defined from the component-wise/Schur product of vectors by

Definition 9
.

The
component-wise product of codes

\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}
over
\mathbb{F}
with the same length
n
is defined as

{\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\langle\,{\boldsymbol{c}}\star{\boldsymbol{d}}\mid\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{C},\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathscr{D}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}}.

If
\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{D}
, we call
\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}}
the
square code
of
\mathscr{C}
.

It is easy to see that if
\mathscr{C}=\left\langle\,\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{c}_{k_{1}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}}
and
\mathscr{D}=\left\langle\,\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{d}_{k_{2}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}}
, a generating set for
{\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}}
over
\mathbb{F}
is given by a set of
k_{1}k_{2}
vectors,

\{{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}}\star{\boldsymbol{d}_{j}}\mid 1\leq i\leq k_{1},1\leq j\leq k_{2}\}.

However, when
\mathscr{C}\cap\mathscr{D}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}
, some of the above elements are obviously redundant. In the extreme case, i.e. when
\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{D}
, the square code dimension is much smaller than
k^{2}
, where
k=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\mathscr{C}
. This is a consequence of the commutative property of the component-wise product:
{c_{i}}\star{c_{j}}={c_{j}}\star{c_{i}}
. Thus we can give the following folklore result appearing for instance in
[
CCMZ15
]
.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a linear code over
\mathbb{F}
of dimension
k
and length
n
. Then

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}\leq\min\left(n,\binom{k+1}{2}\right).

For a random linear code
\mathscr{C}
whose square does not fill the full space, the dimension of its square code is
\binom{k+1}{2}
with high probability, where
k
is the dimension of
\mathscr{C}
. However, there exist families of codes for which the inequality in Proposition
10
is strict. This clearly translates into a distinguisher from random codes. Generalized Reed-Solomon codes represent an example of such behavior
[
Wie10
]
. We namely have

Proposition 11
.

Let
\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
be a GRS code with support
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, multiplier
{\boldsymbol{y}}
and dimension
k
. We have
\text{\bf GRS}_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\star 2}=\text{\bf GRS}_{2k-1}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{2})
.
Hence, if
k\leq\frac{n+1}{2}
,

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}(\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))^{\star 2}=2k-1.

This follows immediately from the fact that the square of a
\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
can be written as

\displaystyle\text{\bf GRS}_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\star 2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\,{({\boldsymbol{x}}^{a}{\boldsymbol{y}})}\star{({\boldsymbol{x}}^{b}{\boldsymbol{y}})}\mid 0\leq a,b<k\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{a+b}{\boldsymbol{y}}^{2}\mid 0\leq a,b<k\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{c}{\boldsymbol{y}}^{2}\mid 0\leq c<2k-1\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}.

Note that the square code dimension is here
2k-1
, i.e. it is linear in
k
and not quadratic. Since the dual of a Reed-Solomon code is again a Reed-Solomon code, it turns out that this algebraic class is distinguishable for any rate. For other families, a square code-based distinguisher may occur only for certain rates. For instance Goppa codes (and more in general alternant codes) are distinguishable whenever the rate is high enough as we now recall. Again this is related to such square code considerations as we will explain.

The distinguisher of Goppa/alternant codes of
[
FGO+11
,
FGO+13
]
and its relationship with square code considerations

The dual of an alternant (or Goppa) code can also be distinguished from random codes when the primal code has a high enough rate, using the square code tool. The different behavior was already observed in
[
FGO+11
]
. Here however, the distinguisher was presented in terms of the kernel dimension
D
of a linear system obtained by linearizing in the proper way the algebraic system that encodes the key-recovery problem for McEliece cryptosystem endowed with alternant or Goppa codes. Indeed let
\boldsymbol{P}=(p_{ij})_{i,j}
be a generator matrix of an
[n,k]
alternant (or Goppa) code
\mathscr{C}
in systematic form, i.e. with its first
k
columns that form an identity block and consider the following linear system

\mathscr{L}_{p}=\left\{\sum_{k+1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq n}p_{ij}p_{ij^{\prime}}Z_{jj^{\prime}}=0\mid 1\leq i\leq k\right\}.

The dimension
D
of the solution space of this system turns out to be much smaller in the case of high rate Goppa or alternant codes than for random codes. A formula for
D
coinciding with experimental evidence was given in
[
FGO+13
]
together with a convincing algebraic explanation for alternant and binary Goppa codes.

It has been proved in
[
MCP12
]
that such
D
is related to the dimension of the square of the dual code
\mathscr{C}^{\perp}
. Indeed, from
[
MCP12
, Proposition 1]
,

(2)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left({\mathscr{C}^{\perp}}\right)^{\star 2}=\binom{\dim_{\mathbb{F}}({\mathscr{C}^{\perp}})+1}{2}-D.

In terms of dimensions of the square codes, the formula for
D
given in
[
FGO+13
]
together with (
2
) predicts for a generic alternant code
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of length
n
and extension degree
m
that

(3)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp})^{\star 2}=\min\left\{n,\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}(r-1)\left((2e_{\mathscr{A}}+1)r-2\frac{q^{e_{\mathscr{A}}}-1}{q-1}\right)\right\},

whereas for a generic Goppa code
\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)
of length
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with Goppa polynomial
\Gamma(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[X]
of degree
r
:

(4)

\displaystyle\dim(\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)^{\perp})^{\star 2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{n,\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}(r-1)(r-2)\right\},\;\;\text{if $r<q-1$}

(5)

\displaystyle\dim(\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)^{\perp})^{\star 2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{n,\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}r\left((2e_{\mathscr{G}}+1)r-2(q-1)q^{e_{\mathscr{G}}-1}-1\right)\right\},\;\;\text{else,}

where
{e_{\mathscr{A}}}
and
{e_{\mathscr{G}}}
are respectively defined by

\displaystyle e_{\mathscr{A}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\max\{i\in\mathbb{N}\mid r\geq q^{i}+1\}=\left\lfloor\log_{q}(r-1)\right\rfloor

\displaystyle e_{\mathscr{G}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}\mid r\leq(q-1)^{2}q^{i}\}+1=\left\lceil\log_{q}\left(\frac{r}{(q-1)^{2}}\right)\right\rceil+1.

As shown in
[
FGO+13
]
, these formulas agree with extensive experimental evidence.

3.
A general result about the square of a trace code

The dual of alternant codes and Goppa codes are trace codes of GRS codes. From Proposition
11
we know that square codes of GRS codes have an abnormally small dimension. A natural question is whether or not this implies that the
square of the trace of a GRS code has itself a small dimension. More generally, this raises the following fundamental issue
of whether or not when the product of two codes
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
of length
n
is smaller than
\min(n,\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{D})
(which is the dimension we expect for random codes
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
) then this property survives for trace codes, namely do we have in this case

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})}\star{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D})}<\min(n,\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D}))?

This is related to open questions raised in
[
Ran15
, C.4]
. This is indeed the case up to some extent, due to the following proposition:

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
be two linear codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
with the same length
n
. Then

{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})}\star{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D})}\subseteq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}^{q^{i}}}\right),\;\;\text{where $\mathscr{D}^{q^{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{d^{q^{i}}\mid d\in\mathscr{D}\}$.}

Proof.

It is readily verified that
\mathscr{D}^{q^{i}}
is a linear code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
. Let
c,d\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
. We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(c)\cdot\operatorname{Tr}(d)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{0\leq i\leq m-1}c^{q^{i}}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{0\leq i\leq m-1}d^{q^{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}0\leq i\leq m-1\\
0\leq j\leq m-1\end{smallmatrix}}c^{q^{i}}\cdot d^{q^{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j\leq m-1}\sum_{0\leq i\leq m-1}c^{q^{i}}\cdot d^{q^{(i+j\mod m)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j\leq m-1}\sum_{0\leq i\leq m-1}c^{q^{i}}\cdot d^{q^{i+j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\operatorname{Tr}(c\cdot d^{q^{i}}).

Because the trace acts component-wise on vectors, we also have for
\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,

\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{c})\star\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{d})=\sum_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{c}\star\boldsymbol{d}^{q^{i}}).

Hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\star\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\,\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{c})\star\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{d})\mid\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{C},\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathscr{D}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\,\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}\star{\boldsymbol{d}^{q^{i}}}\right)\mid\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{C},\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathscr{D}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\left\langle\,\operatorname{Tr}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}\star{\boldsymbol{d}^{q^{i}}}\right)\mid\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{C},\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathscr{D}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathscr{C}\star\mathscr{D}^{q^{i}}\right).

∎

Note that for an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
-linear code
\mathscr{C}
,
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\leqslant\min(m\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C},n)
, where
n
is the code length of
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
, and equality generally holds.
An easy corollary of this proposition is that

Corollary 13
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
be two
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
-linear codes of a same length and which are such that

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})=m\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}
and
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D})=m\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{D}
. We have

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\star\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D})\right)-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D})\leqslant m\cdot\left(\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}})-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{D}\right)

Proof.

We will drop in what follows the subscript indicating in the dimension if it is taken by considering the corresponding code as an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
subspace or as an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
subspace– it will be clear from the context.
We have

\displaystyle\dim\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\star\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\dim\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}^{q^{i}}}\right)\;\;\text{(by Prop. \ref{pr:general})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}m\cdot\dim\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}^{q^{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\left(\dim({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}})-\dim(\mathscr{C})\dim(\mathscr{D})\right)+m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim\mathscr{D}+m\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim\left(\mathscr{D}^{q^{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\left(\dim({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}})-\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim\mathscr{D}\right)+m^{2}\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim\mathscr{D}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\left(\dim({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}})-\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim\mathscr{D}\right)+\dim\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\cdot\dim\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D}).

∎

Remark 14
.

In particular, this result implies that if we have two codes
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
for which

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{D}})<\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{D}
, then the same property survives for the corresponding trace codes:

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}({\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})}\star{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D})})<\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{D}).

In the case
\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{D}
, namely if we consider square codes, Proposition
12
can be refined to give

Proposition 15
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a linear code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
. We have

(6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{m-u}}}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\sum_{u=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{m/2}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\frac{(\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}(\mathscr{C}))^{2}}{2}\;\;\text{if $m$ is even}

Proof.

Proof of (
6
).

Let
\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathscr{C}
. Since the trace acts component-wise on vectors and
\operatorname{Tr}(x)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(x^{q^{u}}\right)
for any
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
and natural number
u
,

\operatorname{Tr}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}\star{\boldsymbol{d}^{q^{u}}}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(({\boldsymbol{c}}\star{\boldsymbol{d}^{q^{u}}})^{q^{m-u}}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left({\boldsymbol{c}^{q^{m-u}}}\star{\boldsymbol{d}^{q^{m}}}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left({\boldsymbol{d}}\star{\boldsymbol{c}^{q^{m-u}}}\right)\in\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{(q^{m-u})}}\right).

This shows that
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\subseteq\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{m-u}}}\right)
. By replacing
u
by
m-u
in the equality above, we obtain the reverse inclusion, which finishes the proof of (
6
).

Proof of (
7
).

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\sum_{u=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\;\;\text{(by Prop. \ref{pr:general} )}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\sum_{u=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\;\;\text{(by \eqref{eq:symmetry}).}

Proof of (
8
).

Let
\{\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{c}_{r}\}
be a basis of
\mathscr{C}
where
r\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\dim(\mathscr{C})
.
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{m/2}}}\right)
is generated by the
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\beta^{q^{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}}\star{\boldsymbol{c}_{j}^{q^{m/2}}}\right)
where
\{\beta,\beta^{q},\cdots,\beta^{q^{m-1}}\}
is a normal basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
and
\ell
ranges over
\{0,\cdots,m-1\}
and
i
,
j
over
\{1,\cdots,r\}
. Since for any
0\leq\ell\leq\frac{m}{2}-1
,
1\leq i,j\leq r
,

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\beta^{q^{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}}\star{\boldsymbol{c}_{j}^{q^{m/2}}}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\beta^{q^{\ell+\frac{m}{2}}}{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{q^{m/2}}}\star{\boldsymbol{c}_{j}}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\beta^{q^{\ell+\frac{m}{2}}}{\boldsymbol{c}_{j}}\star{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{q^{m/2}}}\right),

this implies that
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathscr{C}*\mathscr{C}^{q^{\frac{m}{2}}}\right)
is generated by the (smaller) set
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\beta^{q^{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}}\star{\boldsymbol{c}_{j}^{q^{m/2}}}\right)
where
\ell
ranges over
\{0,\cdots m/2-1\}
and
i
,
j
over
\{1,\cdots,r\}
.
This is a set of cardinality
\frac{mr^{2}}{2}
.
∎

Proposition
15
has a corollary which is similar to Corollary
13
, namely that

Corollary 16
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
-linear code.
We have

(9)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}\leqslant m\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\binom{m}{2}\left(\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}\right)^{2}.

Furthermore if

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})=m\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}
then

(10)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}-\binom{\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})+1}{2}\leqslant m\left[\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}-\binom{\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}+1}{2}\right].

Proof.

Proof of (
9
):

by using (
7
) of Proposition
15
, we obtain

(11)

\dim\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}\dim\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{i}}}\right).

In the case of odd
m
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\dim\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}m\cdot\dim{\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{i}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+m\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{q^{i}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\left(\dim\mathscr{C}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\binom{m}{2}\left(\dim\mathscr{C}\right)^{2}.

On the other hand, if
m
is even, we have

\displaystyle\dim\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{m}{2}-1}m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{q^{i}}+\dim\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{m/2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\frac{m(m-2)}{2}\left(\dim\mathscr{C}\right)^{2}+\frac{m(\dim\mathscr{C})^{2}}{2}\;\;\text{(by using \eqref{eq:dimension} of Proposition \ref{pr:square_general})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\binom{m}{2}\left(\dim\mathscr{C}\right)^{2}.

Proof of (
10
):

\displaystyle\dim\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}-\binom{\dim\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})+1}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\binom{m}{2}\left(\dim\mathscr{C}\right)^{2}-\binom{\dim\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})+1}{2}\;\;\text{(by using \eqref{eq:cor_dimension})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\left(\dim\mathscr{C}\right)^{2}-\binom{m\dim\mathscr{C}+1}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\left[\frac{m-1}{2}\dim\mathscr{C}-\frac{m\dim\mathscr{C}+1}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}-m\cdot\dim\mathscr{C}\cdot\frac{\dim\mathscr{C}+1}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\left[\dim\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}-\binom{\dim\mathscr{C}+1}{2}\right].

∎

Similarly to Corollary
13
, Corollary
16
implies that if the dimension of a square code
\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
is smaller than what we expect from a random code, namely that

\dim\left(\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}\right)<\binom{\dim\mathscr{C}+1}{2}
(if
\binom{\dim\mathscr{C}+1}{2}
is smaller than the code length) then this property survives for the trace code:

\dim\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}<\binom{\dim\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})+1}{2}.

4.
Alternant case with
e_{\mathscr{A}}=0
and Goppa case with
{e_{\mathscr{G}}}=0

In this section, we are going to give a first upper bound on the dimension of the square of the dual of an alternant or Goppa code which is valid for all parameters and is tight when
e_{\mathscr{A}}=0
for random alternant codes and when
r<q-1
for Goppa codes.
This will a direct application of the general results of Section
3
from which we derive:

Theorem 17
.

Let
\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
be an alternant code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then

(12)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp}\right)^{\star 2}\leq\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}(r-1)(r-2).

Remark 18
.

Notice that the upper bound appearing in this theorem coincides exactly with the prediction of the dimension
of the square code derived from the predictions given in
[
FGO+13
]
used together with
[
MCP12
, Proposition 1]

in the alternant case corresponding to
{e_{\mathscr{A}}}=0
, see (
3
) or the Goppa case with
r<q-1
), see (
4
). In other words, this theorem shows that this prediction is actually an upper bound on the dimension and the experimental evidence gathered in
[
FGO+13
]
actually shows that the dimensions of random alternant or Goppa codes agree with this upper bound in such a case.

Proof.

We let
\mathscr{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
. Note that
\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp}=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})
. We apply Corollary
16
with such a
\mathscr{C}
and get that

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})\right)^{\star 2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle m\cdot\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}^{\star 2}+\binom{m}{2}\left(\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{C}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(2r-1)+\frac{m(m-1)r^{2}}{2}\;\;\text{(by Proposition \ref{pr: square_GRS})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2(2r-1)+(m-1)r^{2}\right)\frac{m}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(r(mr+1)-(r-1)(r-2)\right)\frac{m}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}(r-1)(r-2).

∎

5.
Alternant case with
e_{\mathscr{A}}>0

In this section, we will show new linear relationships arising for alternant codes (hence also for Goppa codes) of high enough order
r
. More precisely, the threshold value for which new relations are guaranteed is
r\geq q+1
, i.e.
e_{\mathscr{A}}>0
.
Our main result in this section is that

Theorem 19
.

Let
\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
be an alternant code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then

(13)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp})^{\star 2}\leq\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}(r-1)\left((2{e_{\mathscr{A}}}+1)r-2\frac{q^{{e_{\mathscr{A}}}+1}-1}{q-1}\right).

Remark 20
.

Note that the upper bound on the dimension coincides with the prediction (
3
) given for generic alternant codes. In other words, this theorem shows that this prediction is actually an upper bound on the dimension and the experimental evidence gathered in
[
FGO+13
]
actually shows that the dimensions of random alternant codes agree with this upper bound in such a case.

The reason that we have a refinement of the upper bound of Theorem
17
for values of
r
for which
{e_{\mathscr{A}}}>0
comes from the fact that when we apply
Proposition
15
with
\mathscr{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
(which is the relevant quantity here since

\operatorname{Tr}(\mathscr{C})=\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp}
) we get terms of the form
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)
which will have a smaller dimension than the generic upper bound
mr^{2}
. This is due to the fact that these
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)
will actually be duals of alternant codes for small values of
u
as shown by the
following lemma

Lemma 21
.

Let
\mathscr{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
and
f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\lfloor\log_{q}(r)\rfloor
. We have

(14)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{(r-1)(1+q^{u})+1}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{1+q^{u}})^{\perp}\;\;\text{for all non-negative integers $u$,}

(15)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{(r-1)(1+q^{u})+1}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{1+q^{u}})^{\perp}\;\text{for all integers $u$ in $\{0,\cdots,f\}$.}

Proof.

We first notice that

\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)=\left\langle\,\operatorname{Tr}\left(\alpha_{l}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{a+bq^{u}}{\boldsymbol{y}}^{1+q^{u}}\right)\mid 0\leq l<m,0\leq a,b\leqslant r-1\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}.

Clearly the powers
a+bq^{u}
are all smaller than or equal to
(r-1)(1+q^{u})
. This directly implies
(
14
).
For the equality case, we observe that we get all the powers in
\{0,\cdots,(r-1)(1+q^{u})\}
as long as

r-1\geqslant q^{u}-1
, that is
r\geqslant q^{u}
which is equivalent to
u\leqslant\lfloor\log_{q}(r)\rfloor
.
∎

The previous lemma implies that

Corollary 22
.

Let
\mathscr{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
. For all non-negative integers
u
, we have

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\leq m((r-1)(q^{u}+1)+1).

From this corollary we obtain directly Theorem
19
. The proof goes as follows.

Proof of Theorem
19
.

Let
\mathscr{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
.

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{A}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\perp})^{\star 2}

\displaystyle\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{u=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\;\;\text{(by Prop. \eqref{pr:square_general})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u=0}^{e}m((r-1)(q^{u}+1)+1)+\left(\frac{m-1}{2}-e\right)mr^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}(r-1)\left((2e+1)r-2\frac{q^{e+1}-1}{q-1}\right).

Here, this inequality holds for any integer
e
in
\{0,\cdots,\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor\}
. To get the sharpest upper-bound, we
minimize the function

\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}(r-1)\left((2e+1)r-2\frac{q^{e+1}-1}{q-1}\right)

with respect to
e
. Obviously, this is equivalent to find the maximum over
\{0,\cdots,\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor\}
of the function

T(e)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}er-\frac{q^{e+1}}{q-1}

in the discrete domain of non-negative integers.
We compute the discrete derivative
\Delta T:e\mapsto T(e+1)-T(e)
,

\displaystyle\Delta T(e)=T(e+1)-T(e)

\displaystyle=\left((e+1)r-\frac{q^{e+2}}{q-1}\right)-\left(er-\frac{q^{e+1}}{q-1}\right)

\displaystyle=r-q^{e+1}.

This function is decreasing with
e
and the smallest value of
e
for which
\Delta T(e)\leqslant 0
corresponds to its maximum.
It is the smallest value of
e
for which
r\leqslant q^{e+1}
, i.e.
e=\left\lfloor\log_{q}(r-1)\right\rfloor={e_{\mathscr{A}}}
.

∎

6.
Goppa case with
r\geq q-1

In the previous two sections, we found linear relationships within the individual
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)
subspaces, showing that they are spanned by less than
r^{2}m
vectors if
r
is large enough. We will see that the dimension of some
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)
is even smaller in the Goppa case with
r\geq q-1
(see (
17
) below). Moreover, they are no more disjoint, i.e.
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\cap\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)\right)>0
for some
0\leq u<v\leq{e_{\mathscr{G}}}
. We will namely prove that

Theorem 23
.

Let
\mathscr{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{\bf GRS}_{r}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
, where
y_{i}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(x_{i})}
and
\Gamma
is a polynomial of degree
r
.
Let us define for any positive integer
v

(16)

\mathscr{B}_{v}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathscr{A}_{r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{q^{v}+1})^{\perp},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{B}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathscr{A}_{2r-1}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{2})^{\perp}.

Let
f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\lfloor\log_{q}(r)\rfloor
. Then

(17)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{v}\quad\text{for all positive integers $v$,}

(18)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)=\mathscr{B}_{u}\quad\text{for $0\leqslant u\leqslant f$,}

(19)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}}\right)\subseteq\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q}}\right)\subseteq\dots\subseteq\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\quad\text{for $0\leqslant u\leqslant f$.}

Before proving this theorem it will be useful to state and prove two lemmas. The first one deals with the inclusion
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{v}
. For this, we first recall Lemma
21
which when applied to Goppa codes yields immediately that for all integers
v
we have:

(20)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)\mid\deg P<(r-1)(q^{v}+1)+1\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{(r-1)(q^{v}+1)+1}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{q^{v}+1})^{\perp}.

On the other hand, by definition of
\mathscr{B}_{v}
we have

(21)

\mathscr{B}_{v}=\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)\mid\deg P<r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)\right\}.

Depending on how
q
compares to
r
,
r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)
might be either greater or smaller than
(r-1)(q^{v}+1)+1
. The key for showing the inclusion

\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{v}
will be to show that under certain conditions a codeword of the form
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)
can be written as
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{Q({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)+\boldsymbol{c}
for some codeword
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{B}_{v}
with a polynomial
Q
such that

\deg Q<\deg P
. For performing this task we will use the following lemma.

Lemma 24
.

Let
P(X)
be a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[X]
and consider the Euclidean division of
P
by
\Gamma^{q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1}
:

P=A\Gamma^{q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1}+B
where
\deg B<\deg\Gamma^{q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1}
. We have

(22)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{A({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q}\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)+\boldsymbol{c},\;\text{for some $\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{B}_{v}$}

(23)

\displaystyle\deg A^{q}\Gamma

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\deg P\quad\text{if $\deg P<r(q^{v}+1)$.}

Proof.

For the first point, we just have to observe that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{A({\boldsymbol{x}})\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1}+B({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{A({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v-1}}}\right)+\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{B({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{A({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q}}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}}}\right)+\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{B({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)\quad\text{(since $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^{q})=\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha)$ for $\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{A({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q}\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\quad\quad\text{with $\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{B}_{v}$.}

The last equality follows immediately from the definition of
\mathscr{B}_{v}
(see (
21
)) and the fact that

\deg B<r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)
. For the second point, let us just observe that either
A=0
in which case (
23
) clearly holds or

\deg A=\deg P-r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)

in which case

\deg A^{q}\Gamma=q\deg P-qr(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)+r=q\deg P-r(q-1)(q^{v}+1).

Clearly

\displaystyle\deg A^{q}\Gamma<\deg P

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle q\deg P-r(q-1)(q^{v}+1)<\deg P

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\deg P<r(q^{v}+1).

∎

The second lemma shows that the
\mathscr{B}_{v}
’s form a nested family of codes:

Lemma 25
.

We have for all
v\geqslant 0
:

\mathscr{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{v}\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{v+1}\subseteq\cdots

Proof.

We will first prove that
\mathscr{B}_{v}\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{v+1}
for
v\geqslant 1
.
Let us notice that

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{v}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\,\operatorname{Tr}\left(\alpha_{l}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{c}{\boldsymbol{y}}^{q^{v}+1}\right)\mid 0\leq l<m,0\leq c<r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}

(24)

\displaystyle=\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right):P\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[X],\;\deg(P)<r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)\right\}

Consider now a codeword
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)
in
\mathscr{B}_{v}
and let us prove that it is
in
\mathscr{B}_{v+1}
. By (
24
) we know that

(25)

\deg P<r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1).

Then, let us notice that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v+1}-q^{v}}}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v+1}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{A^{q}({\boldsymbol{x}})\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v+1}+1}}\right)+\boldsymbol{c}

where
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{B}_{v+1}
,
A
is the quotient of the division of
P\Gamma^{q^{v+1}-q^{v}}
by
\Gamma^{q^{v+1}-q^{v}+1}
, and we used Lemma
24
(with
v+1
instead of
v
and
P\Gamma^{q^{v+1}-q^{v}}
instead of
P
). Either
A=0
and it follows immediately that

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)
belongs to
\mathscr{B}_{v+1}
, or else, if
v\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\deg A^{q}\Gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q\left(\deg P+\deg\Gamma(q^{v+1}-q^{v})-\deg\Gamma(q^{v+1}-q^{v}+1)\right)+\deg\Gamma

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle q\left(r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)+r(q^{v+1}-q^{v})-r(q^{v+1}-q^{v}+1)\right]+r\;\;\text{(by definition of $\mathscr{B}_{v}$ -see \eqref{eq:BCv_definition})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q\left(r(q^{v}-q^{v-1})\right)+r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r(q^{v+1}-q^{v}+1).

This shows that in this case too, we have that
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)
belongs to
\mathscr{B}_{v+1}
.
If
v=0
we proceed similarly, the only difference being that now the definition of
\mathscr{B}_{0}
is not obtained by plugging
v=0
into (
21
), but is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}}\right):P\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[X],\;\deg(P)<2r-1\right\}.

Consider a codeword
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}}\right)
in
\mathscr{B}_{0}
and let us prove that it belongs to

\mathscr{B}_{1}
. We proceed similarly as before and write

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q-1}}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q+1}}\right).

We can again apply Lemma
24
with
v=1
and
P({\boldsymbol{x}})\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q-1}
(for the
P
appearing in this lemma).
Notice that

\displaystyle\deg P({\boldsymbol{x}})\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q-1}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2r-1+r(q-1)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle r(q+1).

This shows that we can write

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q-1}}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q+1}}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{Q({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)+\boldsymbol{c}

with
\boldsymbol{c}
belonging to
\mathscr{B}_{1}
and
\deg Q<\deg P\Gamma^{q+1}
. We can carry on this process further on
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{Q({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)
by reducing the degree of
Q
with Lemma
24
until getting at the end a quotient
A
of Lemma
24
equal to
0
, thus showing that
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}}\right)
belongs to
\mathscr{B}_{1}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
23
.

Proof of Theorem
23
.

Proof of (
17
).

We start by using Lemma
21
which implies that

(26)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right):\deg P<(r-1)(q^{v}+1)+1\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{(r-1)(q^{v}+1)+1}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{q^{v}+1})^{\perp}.

We consider a codeword
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)
in
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)
and apply Lemma
24
to it. Since
\deg P\leqslant(r-1)(q^{v}+1)<r(q^{v}+1)
we can apply (
23
) and get that

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{Q({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)+\boldsymbol{c},

where
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{B}_{v}
and
\deg Q<\deg P
. We can continue this process by applying iteratively Lemma
24
to
Q
until the quotient
A
becomes eventually the zero
polynomial, which shows that

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{P({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})^{q^{v}+1}}\right)\in\mathscr{B}_{v}.

Proof of (
18
).

When
v\leqslant f
, Lemma
21
shows that the inclusion (
26
) is actually an equality.
We therefore have

\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)=\mathscr{A}_{(r-1)(q^{v}+1)+1}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{q^{v}+1})^{\perp}.

On the other hand, from (
17
), we know that

(27)

\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{v}=\mathscr{A}_{r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{q^{v}+1})^{\perp}.

Observe now that
r\geqslant q^{v}
implies that
(r-1)(q^{v}+1)\geqslant r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)-1
, since

\displaystyle(r-1)(q^{v}+1)-r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(r-q)q^{v-1}.

This shows that

\mathscr{B}_{v}=\mathscr{A}_{r(q^{v}-q^{v-1}+1)}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{q^{v}+1})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{(r-1)(q^{v}+1)+1}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{q^{v}+1})^{\perp}

which combined with the reverse inclusion (
27
) proves Equality (
18
).

Proof of (
19
).
This is a direct consequence of Lemma
25
and that the
\mathscr{B}_{v}
’s coincide with the

\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{v}}}\right)
’s in this range from (
18
).
∎

A direct consequence of Theorem
23
is that

Corollary 26
.

Let
\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)
be a Goppa code of order
r\geq q-1
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then

(\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)^{\perp})^{\star 2}\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{e}+\sum_{u=e+1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right),

for any non-negative integer
e
.

We can therefore conclude that

Corollary 27
.

Let
\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)
be a Goppa code of order
r\geq q-1
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)^{\perp})^{\star 2}\leq\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}r\left((2{e_{\mathscr{G}}}+1)r-2(q-1)q^{{e_{\mathscr{G}}}-1}-1\right).

Proof.

From Theorem
23
and Lemma
25
, we have
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{e}
, for all
u\leq e
. Therefore

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)^{\perp})^{\star 2}

\displaystyle\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\sum_{u=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{B}_{e})+\sum_{u=e+1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right)\;\;\text{(for arbitrary $e\in\{0,\cdots,\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor\}$)}

\displaystyle\leq rm(q^{e}-q^{e-1}+1)+\left(\frac{m-1}{2}-e\right)mr^{2}

\displaystyle=\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}r\left((2e+1)r-2(q-1)q^{e-1}-1\right).

We want now to minimize the function

\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}r\left((2e+1)r-2(q-1)q^{e-1}-1\right)

with respect to
e
. By removing the constant part in
e
, this clearly becomes equivalent to maximizing

T(e)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}er-(q-1)q^{e-1}

over
\{0,\cdots,\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor\}
.
We compute the discrete derivative
\Delta T:e\rightarrow T(e+1)-T(e)
,

\Delta T(e)=T(e+1)-T(e)=((e+1)r-(q-1)q^{e+1-1})-(er-(q-1)q^{e-1})=r-(q-1)^{2}q^{e-1}.

The maximum is attained at the least integer
e
such that

\Delta T(e)\leqslant 0
. This corresponds to the least integer
e
such that
e\geqslant\log_{q}\left(\frac{r}{(q-1)^{2}}\right)+1
,
i.e.
\left\lceil\log_{q}\left(\frac{r}{(q-1)^{2}}\right)+1\right\rceil={e_{\mathscr{G}}}
minimizes the function
T
and consequently

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathscr{G}({\boldsymbol{x}},\Gamma)^{\perp})^{\star 2}\leq\binom{rm+1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}r\left((2{e_{\mathscr{G}}}+1)r-2(q-1)q^{{e_{\mathscr{G}}}-1}-1\right).

∎

Remark 28
.

From the computation given in the proof of Corollary
27
and knowing that the upper bound is usually an equality, we also infer that with high probability

\mathscr{B}_{e_{\mathscr{G}}}=\sum_{u=0}^{{e_{\mathscr{G}}}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathscr{C}}\star{\mathscr{C}^{q^{u}}}\right).

This has been verified experimentally.

Conclusion

In this article we revisited the distinguisher for random alternant and Goppa codes presented for the first time in
[
FGO+13
]
through a different approach, namely using squares of codes. With this simple but powerful tool we were able to provide explicitly the linear relationships determining the distinguisher in a more straightforward way. We managed therefore to rigorously prove a tight upper bound for the dimension of the square of the dual of an alternant or Goppa code, while
[
FGO+13
]
only provides an algebraic explanation which does not however represent neither an upper or a lower bound. Our proof is also valid in the case of non-binary Goppa case, for which the conjectured distinguisher is only demonstrated experimentally in
[
FGO+13
]
. By doing this we got an unifying explanation for the behavior of all Goppa codes, which does not make use of specific features of the binary case. Finally, we illustrated an interesting property of the structure of the square of the dual of any Goppa code, relating it to the dual of another alternant code.

This connection could be of help for a potential key-recovery attack. Indeed a distinguisher can sometimes be turned into an attack. In the code-based cryptography setting, this is for instance the case for GRS codes. The uncommon dimension of the square of a GRS code leads to a successful key-recovery for several proposed variants of McEliece cryptosystem built upon this family of codes for any rate
[
CGG+14
]
. Despite the strong relation between generalized Reed-Solomon codes and alternant codes, the same attacks cannot be carried over from the former to the latter, because of the additional subfield subcode structure. A similar idea has been successfully exploited for Wild Goppa codes though
[
COT17
]
. But in this case, the distinguisher is based on considerations of square of Goppa codes themselves, which only apply to a very restricted class of parameters. Indeed the attack can only work for extensions of degree
m=2
and there is no way to go beyond it, because for
m>2
the square code fills the whole space. In our case, our distinguisher is based on squaring the
dual
of a Goppa code (or an alternant code) and works for
any
field extension degree.

However, the fact that the dual of a Goppa code is the trace of a generalized Reed-Solomon code rather than the subfield subcode of a generalized Reed-Solomon code seems to complicate significantly the attempts to turn this distinguisher into an attack. But again, having now a much better algebraic (and rigorous) explanation of why the distinguisher works, together with new algebraic results about products involved in the square of the dual of the Goppa code gives a much better understanding of the square code structure. This is clearly desirable and needed if we want to mount a key recovery attack based on these square code considerations. The hope is that this will ultimately lead to being able to attack McEliece schemes based on very high Goppa codes. As explained earlier, this will still not threaten the codes used in the aforementioned NIST competition, but this would break the 20 years old signature scheme
[
CFS01
]
that is based on very high rate Goppa codes.
