A (5/3+ ε \varepsilon )-Approximation for Tricolored Non-crossing Euclidean TSP
Júlia Baligács, Yann Disser, Andreas Emil Feldmann, Anna Zych-Pawlewicz

Abstract
In the Tricolored Euclidean Traveling Salesperson problem, we are given k = 3 k=3 sets of points in the plane and are looking for disjoint tours, each covering one of the sets. Arora (1998) famously gave a PTAS based on “patching” for the case k = 1 k=1 and, recently, Dross et al. (2023) generalized this result to k = 2 k=2 . Our contribution is a ( 5 / 3 + ε ) (5/3+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for k = 3 k=3 that further generalizes Arora’s approach. It is believed that patching is generally no longer possible for more than two tours. We circumvent this issue by either applying a conditional patching scheme for three tours or using an alternative approach based on a weighted solution for k = 2 k=2 .

Our results.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1

For every

3
Our structure theorem

In this section, we prove our structure theorem for
3\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
(cf. Theorem
4
) which, roughly speaking, states the following: For every
\varepsilon>0
, either, there is a two-tour presolution that induces a
\left(\frac{5}{3}+\varepsilon\right)
-approximation, or there is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution that fulfills some additional constraints.
Later, we will see that it is possible to find a good solution that fulfills these additional constraints and a good two-tour presolution in polynomial time.

Our final algorithm for
3\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
will preprocess the input such that the terminals remain distinct points and have integer coordinates.
This allows us to assume throughout this section that terminals lie in
\{0,\dots,L\}^{2}
for some integer
L
that is a power of 2. As the problem is not interesting for small
L
, we also assume
L\geq 4
.
In Section
5
, we explain in more detail how we preprocess the input and show that a near-optimal solution to the preprocessed input can be transformed in polynomial time to a near-optimal solution to the original input.

Following
[
10
]
, we assume without loss of generality that, for every
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
, there is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution to
3\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
whose tours consist of straight line segments, where each segment connects two points that each are at distance at most
\delta
from a terminal. To see this intuitively, interpret each tour of a solution as a sequence of terminals to visit or bypass. The cheapest way to realize such a sequence is by straight line segments with endpoints arbitrarily close to terminals. For this reason, we will assume from now on that all tours that we work with consist of such straight line segments.
Since we assume in this section that terminals lie in
\{0,\dots,L\}^{2}
, we have in particular, that the straight line segments have endpoints in
N_{\delta}\left(\{0,\dots,L\}^{2}\right)
, where
N_{\delta}(A):=\{\boldsymbol{x}:\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}\rVert<\delta\text{ for some }\boldsymbol{a}\in A\}
denotes the
\delta
-neighbourhood of a set
A
.

3.3
The patching technique for three disjoint tours

In the previous section, we have seen that a (reasonable) solution to
k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
can be modified such that it only intersects grid lines in portals. In this section, we investigate how the tours can further be modified to reduce the number of intersection points per portal.
This will be important for our algorithm because it considers all possible ways that a solution can cross the squares of

As briefly explained in the introduction, we cannot hope to show this for every solution, even for

Bounding the number of red and green groups with only one crossing.

After the previous step, every group contains now either one or two crossings.
Our next goal is to bound the number of red and green groups with only one crossing.

Next, note that, if we are given a red or green group

3.4
Structure theorem for three non-crossing tours

Now, we have all the prerequisites in place to state and prove our structure theorem (cf. Theorem
4
) for three non-crossing tours.

For this, given an instance of
k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
with terminals in
\{0,\dots,L\}^{2}
and a shift vector
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}^{2}
, we say that a solution
\Pi=(\pi_{c})_{c\in C}
is
(r,m,\delta)
-portal respecting
if, for every boundary
b
in
D(\boldsymbol{a})
, the intersection points
b\cap\bigcup_{c\in C}\pi_{c}
are contained in the the portals
\mathrm{grid}(b,r\log L,\delta)
and every portal is intersected in at most
m
points in total.

Theorem 14

(Structure Theorem for
3\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
)
.

Let an instance of
3\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
with

(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\textsc{Opt}^{\ast}

(\lceil(15\sqrt{2}+4)/\varepsilon\rceil,18,\delta)
-portal respecting two-tour presolution

Proof.

Let
\eta>0
be a small real number that will
be appropriately set later on in the proof. Let
\Pi=(\pi_{\mathrm{R}},\pi_{\mathrm{G}},\pi_{\mathrm{B}})

b
.

As the first step, we move all crossings to portals by applying the modifications of Lemma
10

3\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}

\mathbb{E}\left[l(\hat{\Pi})\right]\leq\left(1+\frac{7\sqrt{2}}{r}\right)\cdot\left(l(\pi_{\mathrm{R}})+l(\pi_{\mathrm{G}})+l(\pi_{\mathrm{B}})\right)\leq\left(1+\eta+\frac{7\sqrt{2}\cdot(1+\eta)}{r}\right)\cdot\textsc{Opt}^{\ast}.

(6)

Moreover, no crossing of

\delta>0
such that there exists a
(\lceil(15\sqrt{2}+4)/\varepsilon\rceil,36,\delta)
-portal respecting solution of cost at most
\left(\frac{5}{3}+\varepsilon\right)\cdot\textsc{Opt}^{\ast}
.

4
A dynamic programming algorithm

In the previous section, we have seen that there is a
\left(\frac{5}{3}+\varepsilon\right)
-approximate portal-respecting solution.
In this section, we give a polynomial-time algorithm that computes an “optimal” (in the sense of Theorem
5
) portal-respecting solution.

Our algorithm is based on the same ideas as Arora’s dynamic programming algorithm for Euclidean TSP
[
2
]
and the algorithm by Dross et al. for
2\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}

[
10
]
.
The difference to our work is that we need to solve a more general problem: First, we allow for any fixed number of colors of terminals and search for non-crossing tours. Second, we have weighted colors, i.e., the tours of different colors contribute differently to the total cost.

More precisely, by
k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}^{\prime}
we denote the following problem:
a set
C
of
k
colors is given together with an integer
L
that is a power of two. The input consists of a set of terminals
T_{c}\subseteq\{0,\dots,L\}^{2}
for each color
c\in C
, a color weight
w_{c}\geq 0
for each
c\in C
, a shift vector
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}^{2}
,
\delta>0
(sufficiently small) and two integers
r,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
We consider the dissection
D(\boldsymbol{a})
and, as before, we place
r\log L
portals on every boundary.
A
solution
to
k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}^{\prime}
is a
k
-tuple of tours
\Pi=(\pi_{c})_{c\in C}
such that
every terminal is visited by the tour of the same color (i.e.,
T_{c}\subseteq\pi_{c}
for every
c\in C
),
the tours are pairwise disjoint, and
(r,m,\delta)
-portal respecting.
The
cost of a solution
for
k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}^{\prime}
is then
l(\Pi):=\sum_{c\in C}w_{c}\cdot l(\pi_{c})
.
Similarly as for
k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
(cf. Figure
1
), a solution minimizing the cost does not necessarily exist.
By
\textsc{Opt}^{\ast}:=\inf\{l(\Pi)):\Pi\text{ is a solution}\}
, we denote the value that we want to approximate.

The aim of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 16
.

There is an algorithm that computes a parametric solution
\Pi(\lambda)
to
k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}^{\prime}
in time
L^{O(mr\log k)}
such that
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}l\left(\Pi(\lambda)\right)=\textsc{Opt}^{\ast}
.

The main idea is to use dynamic programming. More precisely, we consider the nodes of
D(\boldsymbol{a})
(i.e., squares in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) one by one from leaves to the root and consider all possible ways that a solution can cross the border of the square. For a non-leaf node, we will find these possibilities by combining the solutions for the four subsquares.

4.1
The multipath problem and the lookup table

In this subsection, we investigate how the problem of finding a portal-respecting solution can be decomposed into subproblems. For this, fix an instance
(T_{c})_{c\in C},(w_{c})_{c\in C},\boldsymbol{a},\delta,r,m
of
k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}^{\prime}
.

Recall that portals are subsegments of boundaries of
D(\boldsymbol{a})
of positive length
\delta
. For every portal in
D(\boldsymbol{a})
, we place
m
distinct points in it, called
subportals
, and we will consider solutions that only intersect boundaries in subportals. We place the subportals in a way that no subportal lies at the intersection of the gridlines. Note that each subportal is crossed at most once because the tours are pairwise disjoint and a subportal is a point.
Observe that every
(r,m,\delta)
-portal-respecting solution can be transformed into a subportal-respecting solution at cost at most
2\delta
. Since
\delta
will be chosen arbitrarily small, this cost is negligible. Therefore, the requirement that a solution only intersects portals in the subportals is not a restriction.

Let a node (i.e., a square)

of
D(\boldsymbol{a})
be given.
We consider all possible ways that the tours of a solution can leave and enter
S
through the subportals. For this, we color each subportal with one of the colors in
C
or leave it uncolored. This encodes which of the tours in a solution crosses through the subportal where uncolored means that none of them crosses.
We denote a coloring on all the subportals associated with
\partial S
by a
k
-tuple
(P_{c})_{c\in C}
of pairwise disjoint subsets of the subportals.
Given such a coloring, we also have to specify which subportals are crossed consecutively by a tour. For this, we consider matchings between the colored subportals (cf. Figure
13
). The fact that tours of different colors are non-crossing and that we can restrict ourselves to constructing simple tours

\boldsymbol{x}

, significantly reduces the number of matchings that need to be considered.

For this, we define a

as a
k

(M_{c})_{c\in C}

is a partition of

(\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{q}})

We will see later that the number of non-crossing matchings is bounded by

If

\pi_{c}
that we construct in the end are connected (i.e., do not consist of several disconnected cycles). For this reason, in the case
P_{c}\neq\emptyset
, we will require

.

Now, we have all the prerequisites in place to define the
multipath problem
(cf. Figure

k\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}^{\prime}
and a node

of

.
The input consists of a coloring
(P_{c})_{c\in C}

of

.
A solution to the multipath problem for

is a
k

(\Pi_{c})_{c\in C}
of sets of simple curves in

[a)]

1.

every terminal of color

, i.e.,

,

2.

the curves are disjoint,

3.

for every

\{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\}=\pi\cap\partial S

4.

the border of

\displaystyle\mathrm{LT}_{S}\left[(P_{c})_{c\in C},(M_{c})_{c\in C}\right]

\displaystyle:=\inf\Bigl\{\sum_{c\in C}w_{c}\cdot l(\Pi_{c}):(\Pi_{c})_{c\in C}\text{ is a solution to the multipath}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt\text{problem with inputs $(P_{c})_{c\in C}$ and $(M_{c})_{c\in C}$}\Bigl\},

As we will calculate every entry of the lookup-tables in a dynamic programming fashion, it is important to bound the
number of entries of
\mathrm{LT}_{S}
.
First, we bound the number of non-crossing matchings.

Lemma 17
.

For a node
S
of
D(\boldsymbol{a})
and a coloring
(P_{c})_{c\in C}
of the subportals in
\partial S
, the number of non-crossing matchings is at most

L^{O(mr)}
and we can list all the non-crossing matchings in time
L^{O(mr)}
.

Proof.

Let a coloring
(P_{c})_{c\in C}
of the subportals of
S
be given.
We count the number of non-crossing matchings of
\bigcup_{c\in C}P_{c}
, i.e., we ignore the colors and allow for all colored subportals to be matched as long as the straight line segments

\{\overline{p_{1}p_{2}}:\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}\text{ is in the matching}\}
are non-crossing.
It is immediate that this is an upper bound on the number of non-crossing matchings of
(P_{c})_{c\in C}
.

Let
i:=|\bigcup_{c\in C}P_{c}|
denote the number of colored subportals and let
f(i)
denote the number of non-crossing matchings between them.
Assume a non-crossing matching contains the pair of subportals
\{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\}
.
Note that
\overline{\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}}
separates the square into two faces.
Therefore, the matching cannot contain a pair
\{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\}
where
\boldsymbol{q}
and
\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}
lie in different faces.
Hence, if there are
j
subportals different from
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
in one of the faces, there are
i-j-2
subportals different from
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
in the other face, and the number of non-crossing matchings that contain the pair
\{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\}
is
f(i)\cdot f(i-j-2)
.
Summing up over all choices for
\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
, we obtain

f(i)\leq\sum\limits_{j=0}^{i-1}f(j)\cdot f(i-j).

(7)

Note that this sum is precisely the recursive definition of the well-known
Catalan numbers
(see, e.g.,
[
28
]
). Additionally using that
f(0)=1
and
f(1)=0
, we obtain that
f(i)
is upper bounded by the
i
-th Catalan number
C_{i}
. Since
C_{i}=O(4^{i})
(cf.
[
28
, Theorem 3.1]
), we have

f(i)\leq C_{i}=O(4^{i}).

As
i
is the number of colored portals, we have
i=O(mr\log L)
.
Together, this gives that the number of non-crossing matchings is upper bounded by

O(4^{O(mr\log L)})=2^{O(mr\log L)}=L^{O(mr)}.

Note that the above recursion also gives an efficient recursive procedure for listing all the non-crossing matchings.∎

Next, note that the number of subportals on the border of

is

so that the number of possible colorings of the subportals of

is
(k+1)^{O(mr\log L)}=L^{O(mr\log k)}
, and we can easily list all colorings in

Combining this with Lemma

, we obtain the following result.

Observation 18
.

For every node
S
of
D(\boldsymbol{a})
, the lookup-table
\mathrm{LT}_{S}
has

\leq L^{O(mr\log k)}

entries.

4.2
The algorithm

Next, we show that the entries of the lookup-table
can be computed in polynomial time.
We begin with computing the tables of leaves.

Lemma 19
.

For every leaf
S
of
D(\boldsymbol{a})
, the lookup-table
\mathrm{LT}_{S}
can be computed in time

L^{O(mr\log k)}
.

Proof.

Consider a leaf of
D(\boldsymbol{a})
, i.e., a square
S
of side length 1, and an instance of the multipath problem, i.e., a coloring of the subportals
(P_{c})_{c\in C}
and a non-crossing matching
(M_{c})_{c\in C}
.
Recall that terminals are distinct points in
\{0,\dots,L\}^{2}
so that
S
contains at most one terminal and, if
S
contains a terminal, it lies in the center of
S
.

If it contains no terminal, observe that an optimal solution is simply given by
\Pi_{c}=\{\overline{\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}:\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}\in M_{c}\}
(
c\in C
).
Therefore, assume from now on that
D(\boldsymbol{a})
does contain a terminal
\boldsymbol{t}
, say of color
c^{*}\in C
.
If there is no subportal colored
c^{*}
(note that this is only possible if
\boldsymbol{t}
is the only portal of color
c
), consider the solution given by
\Pi_{c}=\{\overline{\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}:\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}\in M_{c}\}
(
c\neq c^{*}
) and additionally letting
\Pi_{c^{*}}
consist of a single small cycle of length at most
\lambda
that visits
\boldsymbol{t}
and does not intersect any of the tours in
\Pi_{c},c\neq c^{*}
. In case one of the straight line segments in
\Pi_{c}
intersects
\boldsymbol{t}
, it can be bended by a sufficiently small amount at cost at most
\lambda
(the parameter that our solution depends on) so that it does not intersect
\boldsymbol{t}
or any other of the straight line segments. For
\lambda\to 0
, the cost of this solution, i.e.,
\sum_{\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}\in M_{c}}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{1}-\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\rVert
, gives the desired entry of the lookup-table.
Therefore, assume from now on additionally that there are subportals colored
c^{*}
.

For one of the matched pairs in
M_{c^{*}}
, the curve connecting the two subportals has to visit the terminal
\boldsymbol{t}
.
Say this pair is given by
\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}
. Then, we obtain a solution as follows: First, include the curve
\overline{\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\boldsymbol{t}}\cup\overline{\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}
in
\Pi_{c^{*}}
. Then, add the straight line segments
\overline{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}
to
\Pi_{c}
for all
\{\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{q}_{2}\}\in M_{c}
where
\overline{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}
does not intersect
\overline{\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\boldsymbol{t}}\cup\overline{\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}
(
c\in C
and
\{\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{q}_{2}\}\neq\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}
).
For each remaining pair
\{\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{q}_{2}\}\in M_{c}
that intersects
\overline{\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\boldsymbol{t}}\cup\overline{\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}
, there is a suitable point
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
at distance at most
\lambda
from
\boldsymbol{t}
such that
\overline{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}}\cup\overline{\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}
does not cross any other curves.
This is illustrated by one of the green pairs on the left side in Figure
13
.
We add
\pi:=\overline{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}}\cup\overline{\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}
to
\Pi_{c}
. Note that
l(\pi)=\lVert\boldsymbol{q}_{1}-\boldsymbol{t^{\prime}}\rVert+\lVert\boldsymbol{q}_{2}-\boldsymbol{t^{\prime}}\rVert=\lVert\boldsymbol{q}_{1}-\boldsymbol{t}\rVert+\lVert\boldsymbol{q}_{2}-\boldsymbol{t}\rVert
for
\lambda\to 0
. Therefore, given a choice of the pair
\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}
, we can efficiently compute the optimal cost of a solution.
To find the entry of the lookup-table, we compute the cost for every choice of the pair
\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}\in\bigcup_{c\in C}M_{c}
and check which choice minimizes the cost.

To summarize, there are
O(mr\log L)
subportals so that there are at most
O(m^{2}r^{2}\log^{2}L)
possible choices for the pair
\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\}
. Once a pair is chosen, computing the cost of a solution (as
\lambda\to 0
) can be done in time
O(mr\log L)
because the number of curves is bounded by the number of subportals.
Therefore, a single entry of the lookup-table of a leaf can be computed in time

O(m^{2}r^{2}\log^{2}L)\leq L^{O(mr)}
.
Combining this with the fact that the size of every lookup-table is at most
L^{O(mr\log k)}
(cf. Observation
18
), we obtain that
\mathrm{LT}_{S}
can be computed in time
L^{O(mr\log k)}
.
∎

Proof.

Let a square

of side length
>1

Note that each child

Given a combination of an entry of each lookup-table of the four children

\boldsymbol{p}\in P_{c}^{(j)}

\boldsymbol{p}\in P_{c}^{(i)}

\boldsymbol{p}\in P_{c}

\boldsymbol{p}_{1}=\boldsymbol{p}

\boldsymbol{p}_{N}=\boldsymbol{q}
,

3.

if

, for every maximal matched sequence of portals

\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{N}\in P

4.

Proof of Theorem

.

Observe that the value
\textsc{Opt}^{\ast}
for the given instance of

is given by the solution to the multipath problem of
C(\boldsymbol{a})
with
P_{c}=\emptyset
for all

.
Recall that
D(\boldsymbol{a})
is a full 4-ary tree with

L^{2}
leafs.
Therefore,
D(\boldsymbol{a})
contains in total at most
O(L^{2})
nodes.
As we have seen in Lemmas

and

, the lookup-tables of the nodes of

can be computed using dynamic programming and computing a single lookup-table takes time at most

As usual, a parametrized solution

5
Perturbation

In the previous section, we have focused on solving
3\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
when the terminals have integer coordinates. In this section, we show how an input for general
3\text{-}\mathrm{ETSP}
