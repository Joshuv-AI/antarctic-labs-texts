Error analysis of kernel EDMD for prediction and control in the Koopman framework
Friedrich Philipp, Manuel Schaller, Karl Worthmann, Sebastian Peitz, Feliks Nüske

Abstract
. Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD) is a popular data-driven method to approximate the Koopman operator for deterministic and stochastic (control) systems. This operator is linear and encompasses full information on the (expected stochastic) dynamics. In this paper, we analyze a kernel-based EDMD algorithm, known as kEDMD, where the dictionary consists of the canonical kernel features at the data points. The latter are acquired by i.i.d. samples from a user-defined and application-driven distribution on a compact set. We prove bounds on the prediction error of the kEDMD estimator when sampling from this (not necessarily ergodic) distribution. The error analysis is further extended to control-affine systems, where the considered invariance of the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space is significantly less restrictive in comparison to invariance assumptions on an a-priori chosen dictionary.



1.
Introduction

Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD, see, e.g.,
[
56
]
) and its predecessor DMD
[
48
]
have received much attention in recent years.
The reason is the capability to predict so-called
observables
along the flow of dynamical (control) systems in a data-driven manner.
These observable functions are quantities of interests, which can be used for prediction
[
20
]
, computation and analysis of key characteristics like attractors
[
27
]
or metastable sets
[
35
,
21
,
34
]
as well as control
[
18
]
, cf. the book
[
31
]
and the recent overview article
[
7
]
for further applications.
The particular appeal of the EDMD approach is due to several reasons: First, the implementation of the learning scheme is comparatively simple. Second, the resulting model is linear and, last, the Koopman framework provides a mathematically-sound foundation.

Application of the EDMD method usually requires two steps: First, the linear Koopman operator
[
23
]
(or its generator) is restricted to a subspace
\mathcal{D}
spanned by a finite set of observable functions. Second, the projection of this finite-rank linear operator to
\mathcal{D}
, also called
compression
, is approximated using samples, i.e., evaluations of the observables along the flow of the dynamical system. The first step usually requires the a-priori construction of a dictionary consisting of functions on the state space such as, e.g., monomials, which inevitably leads to very large dictionaries for high-dimensional state spaces.
Consequently, the respective data requirements to learn the compression of the Koopman operator grows rapidly due to the curse of dimensionality. Data-informed dictionaries using kernel methods have proven their potential to alleviate this issue.
In kernel EDMD (kEDMD), the dictionary is built up by kernel feature evaluations on the data set
[
57
,
22
]
which are contained in an infinite-dimensional ambient Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS, see, e.g.,
[
39
,
49
]
). Thus, the a-priori choice and tuning of the dictionary reduces to the mere choice of a suitable kernel.
As another advantage, one gains access to powerful tools from the rich theory of RKHSs such as the kernel trick. For further recent works on the use of reproducing kernels in the context of dynamical systems see also
[
5
,
12
,
25
,
26
]
.

To derive data-driven approximations of Koopman operator or generator, two fundamentally different sampling techniques are common. The first one is to draw independent-and-identically-distributed (i.i.d.) initial values and to propagate the flow by one time step. In this way, the dynamics is learned by means of more and more i.i.d samples and results from, e.g., Monte-Carlo integration, can be invoked.
The second approach, which is very appealing from a practical point of view, is to aqcuire data from one (or several) long trajectories. This approach is called ergodic sampling and is widely applied to systems stemming, for instance, from statistical physics problems, see
[
28
,
29
]
.
As these measurements are clearly not independent, the exploration of the state space has to be ensured by means of properties of the dynamical system such as ergodicity.
Ergodic sampling is often mandated if the target measure is a high-dimensional multi-modal distribution, where efficient algorithms to generate i.i.d samples are unavailable. It is also appealing due to its ease of implementation and its ability to reveal unknown ergodic measures and long-term behavior of the system. However, i.i.d. sampling also has a particular advantage:
In contrast to ergodic sampling, where one usually obtains characteristics of the behavior through the lens of the ergodic invariant measure, e.g., on an attractor of the system, i.i.d. sampling allows for learning the dynamics on any desired region of the state space.

For an a-priori chosen dictionary, i.e., the original EDMD approach, the approximation error was analyzed in
[
37
,
59
]
for systems governed by ordinary and stochastic differential equations.
However, for kEDMD, this analysis is so far only available if some invariant measure exists on the whole state space, see
[
42
]
. In this work, we complement these works by extending the results of
[
42
]
on the kernel- and data-based approximation of the Koopman operator to a much more flexible setting without (potentially restrictive) invariance assumptions on the sets and/or measures under consideration. Our main tool is a probabilistic error bound for (cross-)covariance operators in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm using Hoeffding’s inequality. The non-invariance of the considered subset of the state space leads to Koopman operators which no longer map the function space into itself, but onto another space in general.
Thus, the Koopman operators do not form a semigroup as it is the case under invariance assumptions.
Hence, we generalize the definition of the Koopman generator to establish our findings without modifying the kernel-based EDMD algorithm.

To model control systems in the Koopman framework, a popular approach consists of seeking a linear surrogate model in a lifted state space by means of augmenting the state by the control, cf.
[
24
]
.
Bi-linear models are an alternative
[
55
,
53
]
. This is motivated by the fact that
the Koopman generator inherits control affinity and can, thus, be constructed using Koopman generators of autonomous systems. While loosing linearity of the data-driven surrogate model, the bi-linear approach has proven to be favorable for nonlinear systems with state-control couplings
[
40
]
. For EDMD, a respective analysis of the approximation error for dynamical control systems can be conducted by splitting the error into its two sources, cf.
[
37
]
for the estimation error and
[
47
]
for the projection error.
In these works, error bounds for the generator approximation of autonomous systems were leveraged exploiting the control-affine structure.
In addition, for set-point stabilization, an error bound proportional to the size of the state and the control was established in
[
6
]
.
On the one hand, these error bounds were leveraged in
[
52
]
to design a robust controller of the bi-linear system using techniques based on linear matrix inequalities. On the other hand, they were exploited in
[
6
]
to rigorously ensure practical asymptotic stability of EDMD-based Model Predictive Control using cost controllability
[
10
]
of the original system. In this paper, we provide the first error bounds for the approximation of control-affine systems using kernel-based EDMD. Whereas many existing results concerning EDMD for control require the Koopman invariance of the finite-dimensional linear span of the pre-defined dictionary
[
16
,
6
]
, we are able to improve our approximation bounds by assuming that the Koopman operator leaves the (usually infinite-dimensional) ambient RKHS invariant. In contrast to the standard EDMD case with a finite-dimensional dictionary space, this assumption appears to be much more naturally satisfied in many relevant cases.

2.
Summary of the key results

We briefly highlight our two main findings. Our first result considers error bounds for kernel EDMD (kEDMD) of systems governed by a stochastic differential equation

dX_{t}=b(X_{t})\,dt+\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}

for initial values contained in a compact set
\mathcal{X}
. The Koopman operator is defined by the conditional expectation of observables along the flow, which is assumed to be contained in possibly unbounded set
\mathcal{Y}
. More precisely, for an observable function
\psi:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
and for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we define

(2.1)

\displaystyle(K^{t}\psi)(x)=\mathbb{E}\big[\psi(X_{t})|X_{0}=x\big].

We explicitly allow for
\sigma\equiv 0
such that ordinary differential equations are included. To approximate the Koopman operator from data, we collect samples
(x_{k},y_{k})_{k=0}^{m-1}
, where
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
is distributed according to a chosen
Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{Y}
such that
\mu(\mathcal{X})=1
holds and
y_{k}\sim\rho_{t}(x_{k},\cdot)
denotes the corresponding successor state. Here,
\rho_{t}(x,\cdot)
is the probability distribution associated with the transition kernel of the process, i.e.,
\rho_{t}(x,A)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in A\,|\,X_{0}=x)
.
As we are interested in approximations of the Koopman operator on Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
denoted by
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, respectively, the data matrices
\Psi(X)
and
\Psi(Y)
are then formed by kernel evaluations at the data points, i.e.,

\Psi(X)=\big(k(x_{i},x_{j})\big)_{i,j=0}^{m-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi(Y)=\big(k(x_{i},y_{j})\big)_{i,j=0}^{m-1}.

Correspondingly, the kEDMD estimator
\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t}
of the Koopman operator
K^{t}
on the function space
\mathcal{D}
spanned by the kernel features, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}=\operatorname{span}\{k(x_{0},\cdot),\ldots,k(x_{m-1},\cdot)\}
, is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}\quad\text{with\ matrix\ representation}\quad\widehat{M}^{m,t}_{r}=[\Psi(X)]^{\dagger}_{r}\Psi(Y)^{\top},

where
[\Psi(X)]^{\dagger}_{r}
is a pseudo-inverse of a rank-
r
truncation of
\Psi(X)
to avoid ill-conditioning.

First main result: error bound for autonomous systems
, cf. Theorem
4.2
. Let an error bound
\varepsilon>0
and a probabilistic tolerance
\delta\in(0,1)
be given. Then, under suitable assumptions to be specified later, there is a sufficient amount of data
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
,

(2.2)

\|P_{r}K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)}\,\leq\,c_{r}\varepsilon

holds with probability
1-\delta
. Here,
c_{r}\geq 0
is a constant depending on the truncation order
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and
P_{r}
is an orthogonal projection onto the first
r
components of the Mercer orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)
.

Further, if the chosen RKHS is invariant under the Koopman flow, then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)}\,\leq\,\sqrt{\lambda_{r+1}}\,\|K^{t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}}+c_{r}\varepsilon

with Mercer eigenvalues
\lambda_{r}\to 0
for
r\to\infty
.

As a second result, we provide bounds for kEDMD-based predictions of control-affine systems

(2.3)

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}u_{i}(t)g_{i}(x(t))

with a piecewise constant control function taking values in a compact set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
, resulting e.g. from sampling with zero-order hold. For a constant control function
u(t)\equiv u\in\mathcal{U}
, the counterpart of the Koopman operator (
2.1
) is given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle(K_{u}^{t}\psi)(x^{0}):=\psi\big(x(t;x^{0},u)\big),

where
x^{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
x(\cdot;x^{0},u)
is the solution to (
2.3
) with
x(0)=0
. Again,
\mathcal{Y}
is chosen in a way such that
x(t;x^{0},u)\in\mathcal{Y}
for
x^{0}\in\mathcal{X}
. As the generator of the Koopman operator (
2.4
) inherits the control affine structure of the underlying control system (
2.3
), we show that the Koopman operator is approximately control affine (for small times
t\geq 0
). Then, we define its approximation by

(2.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}:=\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\big(\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}\big),

where
\widehat{K}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}^{m,t}
,
\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}
are estimators of the Koopman operators corresponding to (
2.3
) for the constant controls
u=\gamma_{i}e_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,{n_{u}}
, and
u=0
, respectively, using the kEDMD method as outlined above for autonomous systems.

Second main result: error bound for control systems
, cf. Theorem
5.1
.
Let an error bound
\varepsilon>0
, a probabilistic tolerance
\delta\in(0,1)
, and a control
u\in\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathcal{U}
compact be given. Then, under suitable assumptions to be specified later, there is a sufficient amount of data
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
,

\|P_{r}K_{u}^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r,u}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)}\,\leq\,c_{r}\varepsilon+t^{2}\cdot C_{k}\widehat{\lambda}_{r}^{-1}.

The first term resembles the right-hand side of (
2.2
) and the second term stems from the
approximate control-affinity
of the Koopman operator.

5.1
, provides an error bound on this estimator. Finally, Section
6
concludes the paper with an exhibition of numerical results.

3.
Setting

In the following, we introduce the reader to the setting considered in this paper which involves stochastic differential equations (Section
3.1
), Koopman operators (Section
3.3
), symmetric positive definite kernels and their associated reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (Section
3.4
), and the kEDMD approximation method (Section
3.5
). In Section
3.2
we provide our assumptions and discuss them in detail.

3.1.
Stochastic differential equations

Let a stochastic differential equation (SDE) with drift vector field
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and diffusion matrix field
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be given, i.e.,

(3.1)

dX_{t}=b(X_{t})\,dt+\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t},

where
W_{t}
is
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. We assume that both
b
and
\sigma
are Lipschitz-continuous. Then
[
38
, Theorem 5.2.1]
guarantees the existence of a unique solution
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
3.1
). The case
\sigma\equiv 0
will be referred to as “the deterministic case” as the SDE then reduces to the ODE
\dot{x}=b(x)
.

The solution
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the SDE (
3.1
) is a continuous time-homogeneous Markov process, whose transition kernel is denoted by
\rho_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{B}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
\rho_{t}(x,\cdot)
is a probability measure for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for each
A\in\mathcal{B}
we have that
\rho_{t}(x,A)
is a representative of the conditional probability for
A
containing
X_{t}
, given
X_{0}=x
, i.e.,

\rho_{t}(x,A)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in A|X_{0}=x)\quad\text{for $\mathbb{P}^{X_{0}}$-a.e. $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

where
\mathbb{P}^{X_{0}}
denotes the law of
X_{0}
. Note that the law of
X_{t}
is determined by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{X_{t}}(A)=\int\rho_{t}(x,A)\,d\mathbb{P}^{X_{0}}(x),\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}.

It is well known
[
3
, Section 1.3]
that the transition kernel satisfies the so-called
Chapman-Kolmogorov equation

(3.3)

\rho_{t+s}(x,A)=\int\rho_{s}(y,A)\,\rho_{t}(x,dy),\qquad A\in\mathcal{B},\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;s,t\geq 0.

As it is usual, we agree to write
\rho_{t}(x,dy)
for
d\rho_{t}(x,\cdot)(y)
.

3.2.
Assumptions

In many works it is assumed that there is some set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is invariant under the flow of the SDE and that there is an invariant distribution
\pi
on
\mathcal{X}
such that the solution process
X_{t}
is stationary with respect to
\pi
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}^{X_{t}}=\pi
for all
t\geq 0
. In view of (
3.2
), this is equivalent to

\int\rho_{t}(x,A)\,d\pi(x)=\pi(A),\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}},\,t\geq 0.

Here,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the Borel sigma algebra on
\mathcal{X}
.

However, although the Krylov–Bogolyubov Theorem
[
11
]
ensures the existence of such an invariant measure
\pi
for a large class of SDEs, both the set
\mathcal{X}
as the measure
\pi
are often unknown in practice; even if
\pi
is known, generating independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) samples with respect to it remains, in general, a challenging problem.

In this paper, we relax both conditions in a way that the objects become both more flexible and more accessible for applications:

•

\mathcal{X}:=\overline{\Omega}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded and connected open set;

•

T>0
is a finite time horizon;

•

\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a closed set such that
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{Y}
and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0\,\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\rho_{t}(x,\mathcal{Y})=1.

•

\mu
is a finite Borel measure on
\mathcal{Y}
such that
\mu(\mathcal{X})=1
and there exists a constant
L>0
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho_{t}(x,A)\,d\mu(x)\,\leq\,L\cdot\mu(A),\quad A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Y}},\,t\in[0,T].

The set
\mathcal{X}
will later serve as the set, which initial data for the process is sampled from. The assumption (
3.4
) then assures that a subsequent sample after time
t\in(0,T]
of the process remains in
\mathcal{Y}
almost surely. Clearly,
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a possible choice.

Let us discuss the above assumptions in the following remark.

Remark 3.1
.

(a)
In the case
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}
, the set
\mathcal{X}
is invariant under the stochastic flow of the SDE (
3.1
), i.e.,

\forall t\geq 0\,\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\rho_{t}(x,\mathcal{X})=1.

A sufficient condition on the coefficients of the SDE (
3.1
) for the invariance of
\mathcal{X}
under the SDE flow is given by

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\mathcal{X}$ and}

(3.6b)

\displaystyle b(x)^{\top}\nu(x)

\displaystyle\leq 0\quad\text{for all $x\in\partial\mathcal{X}$ and all outer normal vectors $\nu(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$},

where an outer normal vector of
\mathcal{X}
at
x\in\partial\mathcal{X}
is any vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\nu\neq 0
, such that the open ball with center
x+\nu
and radius
\|\nu\|_{2}
and
\mathcal{X}
are disjoint
1
1

1

If
\Omega
has a
C^{1}
-boundary, the outer unit vector is unique. In case of Lipschitz continuous boundary, as a consequence of Rademacher’s theorem
[
13
, Theorem 3.2]
, it is unique up to a set of Lebesgue measure zero on the boundary.
. This follows by an application of the Bony-Brezis (also known as Nagumo) theorem, which is proven in
[
54
, Theorem 10.XVI, p. 117f]
for the deterministic case under the
tangent or interior pointing
-condition (
3.6b
). In the stochastic setting, continuity of solutions to the SDE ensures that any trajectory leaving
\mathcal{X}
has to cross the boundary. The condition (
3.6a
) implies that the noise has no influence at the boundary (
\sigma
is continuous) and in the exterior of
\mathcal{X}
such that the SDE reduces to an ordinary differential equation. Hence, the proof in
[
54
]
for the deterministic setting also applies to the SDE case.

(b)
Note that (
3.5
) means that the push-forward measures
\nu_{t}
of
\mu
under the dynamics, defined on
\mathcal{Y}
by
\nu_{t}(A)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho_{t}(x,A)\,d\mu(x)
,
t\in[0,T]
, are uniformly Lipschitz-continuous w.r.t.
\mu
, i.e.,
\nu_{t}(A)\leq L\cdot\mu(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, cf.
[

, Definition 3.1]
. In consequence,
\mu
-integrable functions on
\mathcal{Y}
are also
\nu_{t}
-integrable, and for
\psi\in L^{1}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
we have

(3.7)

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\psi(y)|\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\psi|\,d\nu_{t}\,\leq\,L\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\psi|\,d\mu.

(c)
In the deterministic case (i.e.,
\sigma\equiv 0
), (
3.4
) means that
F_{t}(x)\in\mathcal{Y}
for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, where
F_{t}
denotes the flow of the ODE
\dot{x}=b(x)
. Moreover, the condition (
3.5
) is equivalent to

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mu(F_{t}^{-1}(A))\leq L\cdot\mu(A),\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Y}},\,t\in[0,T].

In Section
A
we show that this condition is satisfied for a large class of systems and measures.

3.3.
Koopman operators

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, we let
\|\cdot\|_{p,\mathcal{Y}}
denote the
L^{p}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
-norm. In particular, for
p<\infty
,

\|\psi\|_{p,\mathcal{Y}}=\left(\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\psi(y)|^{p}\,d\mu(y)\right)^{1/p}.

Moreover, we define
\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{2,\mathcal{Y}}:=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\phi(y)\overline{\psi(y)}\,d\mu(y)
for
\phi,\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
. Similarly, we define the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p,\mathcal{X}}
on
L^{p}(\mathcal{X};\mu)
and the scalar product
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle_{2,\mathcal{X}}
on
L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)
, respectively.

Let
B(\mathcal{Y})
denote the set of all bounded Borel-measurable functions on
\mathcal{Y}
. For
t\in[0,T]
, we define the
Koopman operator

K^{t}
on
B(\mathcal{Y})
associated with the SDE (
3.1
) by

(K^{t}\psi)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\psi(y)\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)=\mathbb{E}\big[\psi(X_{t})|X_{0}=x\big],\quad x\in\mathcal{X},

for
\psi\in B(\mathcal{Y})
.

Remark 3.2
.

(a) For the definition of
K^{t}
to carry over to spaces of equivalence classes of functions that coincide a.e. on
\mathcal{Y}
, it must be ensured that
K^{t}\psi_{1}=K^{t}\psi_{2}

\mu
-a.e. on
\mathcal{X}
whenever
\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}

\mu
-a.e. on
\mathcal{Y}
. This is equivalent to the absolute continuity of the measure
\nu_{t}
w.r.t.
\mu
(see Remark
3.1
), which is a relaxation of (
3.5
). However, it is easy to see that
K^{t}
maps
L^{1}(\mu)
(boundedly) into itself if and only if
\nu_{t}\leq L\mu
for some
L>0
, i.e., if (
3.5
) holds.

(b) Usually, the Koopman operator of a dynamical system on
\mathcal{X}
is considered as a linear operator from a space of functions on
\mathcal{X}

into itself
. The price we pay by relaxing the invariance condition on
\mathcal{X}
is that our version of the Koopman operator is a linear map between
two different spaces
.

(c) Let us briefly consider the deterministic case. Here, we have
\rho_{t}(x,A)=\delta_{F_{t}(x)}(A)
, and thus
(K^{t}\psi)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\psi(y)d\delta_{F_{t}(x)}(y)=\psi(F_{t}(x))
for
t\in[0,T]
, cf. (
3.4
). This complies with the usual definiton of the Koopman operator for deterministic dynamical systems as a composition operator.

A proof of the following proposition can be found in the Appendix Section
B
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
. For every
t\geq 0
, the operator
K^{t}
extends uniquely to

•

a contraction from
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
to
L^{p}(\mathcal{X};\mu)
;

•

a bounded operator from
L^{p}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
to
L^{p}(\mathcal{X};\mu)
.

If

3.4.
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

In what follows, let
k:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous and bounded symmetric positive definite kernel, that is, we have
k(x,y)=k(y,x)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{Y}
and

\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k(x_{i},x_{j})c_{i}c_{j}\,\geq\,0

for all choices of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
. It is well known that
k
generates a so-called
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS)
[
2
,
4
,
39
]

(\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}})
of bounded continuous functions on
\mathcal{Y}
, such that for
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
the
reproducing property

(3.9)

\psi(x)=\langle\psi,\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x)\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}},\qquad x\in\mathcal{Y},

holds, where
\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the so-called
feature map
corresponding to the kernel
k
, i.e.,

\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x)=k(x,\cdot),\qquad x\in\mathcal{Y}.

In the sequel, we shall denote the norm on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}
and the kernel diagonal by
\varphi
:

\varphi(x)=k(x,x),\qquad x\in\mathcal{Y}.

Then for
x\in\mathcal{Y}
we have

\|\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x)\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}=\langle\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x),\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x)\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}=\langle k(x,\cdot),k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}=k(x,x)=\varphi(x).

We shall frequently make use of the following estimate:

|k(x,y)|=|\langle\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x),\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}|\leq\|\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x)\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\|\Phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}=\sqrt{\varphi(x)\varphi(y)}.

For example, it shows that
\|k\|_{\infty}=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
.

In contrast to other works, where the kernel is induced by an observation map
[
12
]
or is specifically designed by taking samples of the dynamics into account
[
5
]
, we admit any continuous kernel on
\mathcal{Y}
which satisfies the following

Compatibility assumptions:

(C1)

If
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
such that
\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\!\int_{\mathcal{Y}}k(x,y)\psi(x)\psi(y)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)=0
, then
\psi=0

\mu
-a.e.

(C2)

If
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
such that
\psi(y)=0
for
\mu
-a.e.
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, then
\psi=0
.

Since the functions in
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
are continuous, note that condition (C2) holds if the measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure with positive density. The condition allows us to consider
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
as a subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
, i.e., the inclusion operator
S_{\mathcal{Y}}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
, defined by

S_{\mathcal{Y}}\psi:=[\psi],\quad\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},

is injective. Here,
[\psi]
denotes the equivalence class w.r.t. a.e. equality. It is easily seen that

is bounded with norm
\|S_{\mathcal{Y}}\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{1,\mathcal{Y}}^{1/2}

\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is continuously embedded in

\|\psi\|_{2,\mathcal{Y}}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}},\quad\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}.

Furthermore, the condition (C1) is equivalent to the density of
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
in

[
42
]
. Hence,
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is continuously and densely embedded in

.

In fact, the embedding is even compact as
S_{\mathcal{Y}}
is a
Hilbert-Schmidt operator
with Hilbert-Schmidt norm
\|S_{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{HS}^{2}=\|\varphi\|_{1,\mathcal{Y}}
, see
[
42
]
. For this, recall that an operator

on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is

if for some (and hence for each) orthonormal basis (ONB)

of
\mathcal{H}

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle(T^{*}T)^{1/2}e_{i},e_{i}\rangle<\infty

for some (and hence for each) ONB

of
\mathcal{H}
. Trace-class and Hilbert-Schmidt operators are compact operators. In particular, their spectrum consists of discrete eigenvalues with the origin as the only accumulation point. As

is Hilbert-Schmidt, it follows that the
covariance operator

3.5.
Data samples and Kernel EDMD

Next, we briefly recall the Koopman approximation method called
Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition
(EDMD), where certain (usually continuous) functions from
L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
are evaluated on a set of sampled data.

Assumption 3.6
.

The data
(x_{k},y_{k})
,
k=0,\ldots,m-1
, are drawn i.i.d. from the joint distribution

d\mu_{0,t}(x,y):=\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)

on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where the time-step
t>0
is fixed. This then implies that the
x_{k}
are sampled i.i.d. with respect to
\mu
and
y_{k}
is drawn dependently of
x_{k}
from the probability distribution
\rho_{t}(x_{k},\,\cdot\,)
.

The EDMD Algorithm
[
56
]
.
In EDMD, one chooses functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
from a so-called
dictionary

\mathcal{D}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}
spanning the finite-dimensional space
\mathbb{V}=\operatorname{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
with the goal of approximating the compression
P_{\mathbb{V}}K^{t}|_{\mathbb{V}}
of
K^{t}
to
\mathbb{V}
. Here,
P_{\mathbb{V}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the space
\mathbb{V}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};\mu)
. The matrix representation of the compression operator with respect to the basis
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathbb{V}
is then given by
C^{-1}A
, where
C=(\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{\mu})_{i,j=1}^{N}
is the so-called mass matrix and
A=(\langle\psi_{i},K^{t}\psi_{j}\rangle_{\mu})_{i,j=1}^{N}
is called the stiffness matrix. In order to define empirical estimators for
C
and
A
, we replace the scalar products in the matrices’ entries by sums of
\psi_{i}(x_{k})\psi_{j}(x_{k})
and
\psi_{i}(x_{k})\psi_{j}(y_{k})
, respectively, which leads to the matrices

\widehat{C}_{m}=\tfrac{1}{m}\Psi(X)\Psi(X)^{\top}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{A}_{m}=\tfrac{1}{m}\Psi(X)\Psi(Y)^{\top},

where
\Psi=[\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}]^{\top}
and

\Psi(X)=[\Psi(x_{0}),\ldots,\Psi(x_{m-1})]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi(Y)=[\Psi(y_{0}),\ldots,\Psi(y_{m-1})].

Hence, the EDMD approximation of
P_{\mathbb{V}}K^{t}|_{\mathbb{V}}
is given by the estimator
\widehat{M}^{m,t}=\widehat{C}_{m}^{-1}\widehat{A}_{m}
. In
[
37
]
it was proved that
\widehat{M}^{m,t}
indeed converges in probability to
C^{-1}A
as
m\to\infty
, and bounds on the accuracy were provided.

The kEDMD Algorithm
[
57
,
22
]
.
In contrast to traditional EDMD, in
kernel EDMD
(kEDMD) the dictionary is not chosen a priori, but is given by the features sampled on the data points, that is,
\mathcal{D}=\{\Phi(x_{0}),\ldots,\Phi(x_{m-1})\}
. The data matrices
\Psi(X)
and
\Psi(Y)
are then easily seen to be given by

\Psi(X)=\big(k(x_{i},x_{j})\big)_{i,j=0}^{m-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi(Y)=\big(k(x_{i},y_{j})\big)_{i,j=0}^{m-1}.

Hence, since
\Psi(X)
is symmetric,

\widehat{C}_{m}^{-1}\widehat{A}_{m}=\big[\tfrac{1}{m}\Psi(X)\Psi(X)^{\top}\big]^{-1}\big[\tfrac{1}{m}\Psi(X)\Psi(Y)^{\top}\big]=\Psi(X)^{-1}\Psi(Y)^{\top}.

However, since the eigenvalues of
\Psi(X)
typically decay fast, in practice one often replaces the inverse of the ill-conditioned matrix
\Psi(X)
by a pseudo-inverse of a rank-
r
(
r<m
) eigendecomposition truncation of
\Psi(X)
. Hence, the kEDMD estimator for the Koopman operator compression becomes

(3.11)

\displaystyle\widehat{M}^{m,t}_{r}=[\Psi(X)]_{r}^{\dagger}\Psi(Y)^{\top}.

Now, the empirical estimators for the operators
C_{\mathcal{X}}
and
C_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}^{t}
are given by

\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\Phi(x_{k})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}^{m,t}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\Phi(y_{k}).

We have
\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}^{m,t}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
. In fact, both operators map to
\mathbb{V}=\operatorname{span}\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
. Since

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}\Phi(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}[\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\Phi(x_{k})]\Phi(x_{j})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}k(x_{j},x_{k})\Phi(x_{k}),

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}^{m,t}\Phi(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}[\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\Phi(y_{k})]\Phi(x_{j})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}k(x_{j},y_{k})\Phi(x_{k}),

the matrix representations of
\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}|_{\mathbb{V}}
and

3.10
))

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\langle C_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}}e_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\langle K^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle_{2,\mathcal{X}}e_{j}=P_{r}K^{t}\psi.

Hence, we have to estimate the deviation of
\sum_{j=1}^{r}\langle\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}^{m,t}\psi,\widehat{e}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}}\widehat{e}_{j}
from
\sum_{j=1}^{r}\langle C_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}}e_{j}
, which was done in
[
42
]
for the special case of
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The proof for the present case follows analogous lines. Also the estimate in the case
K^{t}\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
can be proved by following the lines of the proof of the counterpart in
[
42
]
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The assumption
K^{t}\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
is not too exotic. In fact, we prove in Section
D
in the Appendix that the condition holds for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, if
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}
is any compact set with non-empty interior, and
k
is a Gaussian RBF kernel.

5.
Extension to deterministic control-affine systems

In this part, we extend the error estimates to deterministic control systems of the form

(5.1)

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}u_{i}(t)g_{i}(x(t)),\qquad t\in[0,T],

with vector fields
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i\in[1:{n_{u}}]:=\{1,\ldots,{n_{u}}\}
,
{n_{u}}\in\mathbb{N}
, and a prediction horizon
T>0
. We let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and choose specifically

\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
|\mathcal{X}|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of
\mathcal{X}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We furthermore assume that the restrictions of the vector fields
f
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n_{u}}
to
\mathcal{Y}
are
C^{1}
. Admissible controls
u
in (
5.1
) are measurable functions
u:[0,T]\to\mathcal{U}
, where
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
is a compact, convex set satisfying
0\in\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{U})
. To avoid excessive notation, we assume that for all
x^{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and all admissible controls
u:[0,T]\to\mathcal{U}
,

•

[0,T]
is contained in the existence interval for the solution
x(\,\cdot\,;x^{0},u)
to (
5.1
) and

•

x(t;x^{0},u)\in\mathcal{Y}
for each
t\in[0,T]
.

If the first assumption is satisfied and
\mathcal{X}
is contained in the interior of
\mathcal{Y}
, then by continuity of the flow in time and the compactness of both
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{U}
, there exists a time horizon
T_{0}
such that the second assumption is satisfied for all
T\in(0,T_{0})
.

Here, we consider sampled-data systems with Zero-order Hold (ZoH), i.e., for a given, typically small sampling period
t>0
,
u(s)\equiv u
on
[kt,(k+1)t)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This system class is well established in systems and control, see, e.g.,
[
8
,
33
]
and the references therein.
Sampled-data systems can be used, e.g., for output-feedback stabilization of nonlinear systems
[
30
]
or in the redesign of continuous-time control signals
[
17
]
. In particular, there are many results showing that system-theoretic properties like stabilizability, or even cost controllability, are preserved under sampling
[
58
]
. The latter even is inherited by the EDMD-based surrogate model in the Koopman framework as recently shown in
[
6
]
analyzing EDMD-based Model Predictive Control.
Hence, we also restrict our consideration to sampled-data systems knowing that the analysis on one sampling period suffices to obtain similar results based on the concatenation of the control signal for multiple sampling periods.

For a constant control
u\in\mathcal{U}
we let
K_{u}^{t}
be the Koopman operator for the autonomous system (
5.1
), that is,

\displaystyle(K_{u}^{t}\psi)(x^{0}):=\psi\big(x(t;x^{0},u)\big),\qquad x^{0}\in\mathcal{X},\,\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Y}).

In order to control the system by piecewise constant controls, in principle, one could compute the kEDMD estimator for
K_{u}^{t}
for every applied control
u
, which, however, would require a lot of computation time in each step. As this is infeasible in practice, we circumvent this issue by approximating
K_{u}^{t}
by a linear combination of
{n_{u}}+1
Koopman operators
K_{u_{i}}^{t}
with quadratic precision. In this course, we leverage the affine representation of the Koopman generator.

The Koopman generator
\mathcal{L}^{u}
associated with (
5.1
) is given by

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{u}\psi=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{K_{u}^{t}\psi-\psi|_{\mathcal{X}}}{t}

for
\psi\in\operatorname{dom}\mathcal{L}^{u}
, where the limit is taken in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, and
\operatorname{dom}\mathcal{L}^{u}
consists of those functions
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Y})
for which this
L^{2}
-limit exists. It is easy to see that
C^{1}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\operatorname{dom}\mathcal{L}^{u}
for each
u\in\mathcal{U}
such that for
\psi\in C^{1}(\mathcal{Y})
and
x^{0}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}^{u}\psi)(x^{0})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\tfrac{1}{t}\big((K_{u}^{t}\psi)(x^{0})-\psi(x^{0})\big)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(K_{u}^{t}\psi)(x^{0})\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\psi(x(t;x^{0},u))\Big|_{t=0}

(5.3)

\displaystyle=\nabla\psi(x^{0})^{\top}\Big(f(x^{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}u_{i}g_{i}(x^{0})\Big).

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}
denote the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
and let

\gamma_{i}:=\inf\{\gamma>0:\gamma e_{i}\notin\mathcal{U}\},\qquad i=1,\ldots,{n_{u}}.

Then the
\gamma_{i}
are positive numbers thanks to
0\in\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{U})
, and we have
\gamma_{i}e_{i}\in\partial\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{U}
. Now, if
u=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}u_{i}e_{i}
is the coordinate representation of the vector
u\in\mathcal{U}
, the representation (
5.3
) reveals that, on
C^{1}(\mathcal{Y})
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{u}=\mathcal{L}^{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\mathcal{L}^{0}\right).

To transfer the error bounds from autonomous systems derived in this work to control systems, we will show that the restriction of the Koopman operator to
C^{2}(\mathcal{Y})
is also affine in the control—at least approximately for small
t>0
. For this, set

f_{u}(x):=f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}u_{i}g_{i}(x)

and let
\psi\in C^{2}(\mathcal{Y})
,
u\in\mathcal{U}
, and
x^{0}\in\mathcal{X}
. We apply Taylor’s theorem to
\psi\circ x(\,\cdot\,;x^{0},u)
at
t_{0}=0
and observe that

\displaystyle(K_{u}^{t}\psi-\psi|_{\mathcal{X}})(x^{0})

\displaystyle=\psi(x(t;x^{0},u))-\psi(x(0;x^{0},u))

\displaystyle=t\nabla\psi(x^{0})\cdot f_{u}(x^{0})+\frac{1}{2}r(\xi)t^{2},

for some
\xi\in[0,t]
, where, abbreviating
x(s)=x(s;x^{0},u)
,

\displaystyle r(s)=f_{u}(x(s))^{\top}\nabla^{2}\psi(x(s))f_{u}(x(s))+\nabla\psi(x(s))^{\top}Df_{u}(x(s))f_{u}(x(s)).

By compactness of
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathcal{Y}
and the continuity of all functions involved, we can bound the remainder term uniformly in
u\in\mathcal{U}
,
x^{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
s\in[0,T]
. More specifically,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\max_{s\in[0,T]}|r(s)|\leq c(f,g_{1},\ldots,g_{n_{u}})\cdot\|\psi\|_{C^{2}(\mathcal{Y})},

where
\|\psi\|_{C^{2}(\mathcal{Y})}:=\|\psi\|_{C(\mathcal{Y})}+\|\nabla\psi\|_{C(\mathcal{Y})^{d}}+\|\nabla^{2}\psi\|_{C(\mathcal{Y})^{d\times d}}
. Thus,

(5.6)

\displaystyle K_{u}^{t}\psi=\psi+t\mathcal{L}^{u}\psi+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})

where the constant in
\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
as defined in (
5.5
) can be chosen independently of
u
.

Invoking control-affinity of the generator (
5.4
), we thus get

(5.7)

\displaystyle K_{u}^{t}\psi=K^{t}\psi+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\left(K^{t}_{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-K^{t}\right)\psi+O(t^{2}),

which means that the Koopman operator is control affine up to second order in time. This is Equation (3.14) in
[
40
]
, where we stress however that the remainder term here depends on the chosen dictionary function.

The error bound (
4.2
) obtained in Theorem
4.2
can now be transferred to the controlled case. Thus, for
u\in\mathcal{U}
we define the estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}:=\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\big(\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}\big),

where the
\widehat{K}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}^{m,t}
are defined as in (
3.12
) for the system
\dot{x}=f(x)+\gamma_{i}g_{i}(x)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let the assumptions in Theorem
4.2
be satisfied. For fixed
r\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\delta_{r}
and
c_{r}
be as in (
4.1
). Further, let
\varepsilon\in(0,\delta_{r})
and
\delta\in(0,1)
be arbitrary and fix some
m\geq\max\{r,\frac{8\|k\|_{\infty}^{2}\ln(4(n_{u}+1)/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\}
. Then, for each
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\cap C^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, there is a constant
c_{\psi}\geq 0
such that with probability at least
1-\delta
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|P_{r}K_{u}^{t}\psi-\widehat{K}_{r,u}^{m,t}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)}\,\leq\,(1+2\|\Gamma^{-1}u\|_{1})c_{r}\varepsilon\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}+c_{\psi}t^{2}.

where
\Gamma=\operatorname{diag}_{i=1,\ldots,n_{u}}\gamma_{i}
. In the case where
K_{u}^{t}\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\partial}:=\{0,\gamma_{1}e_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n_{u}}e_{n_{u}}\}
, we obtain

(5.9)

\displaystyle\|K_{u}^{t}\psi-\widehat{K}_{r,u}^{m,t}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)}\,\leq\,(1+2\|\Gamma^{-1}u\|_{1})(\sqrt{\lambda_{r+1}}M+c_{r}\varepsilon)\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}+c_{\psi}t^{2},

where
M=\max\{\|K_{u}^{t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}}:u\in\mathcal{U}_{\partial}\}
.

If the kernel
k
enjoys
C^{2}
-regularity, for
m\geq\max\{r,\frac{8\|k\|_{\infty}^{2}\ln(4/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\}
data points we have with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\|P_{r}K_{u}^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r,u}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)}\,\leq\,c_{r}\varepsilon+t^{2}\cdot C_{k}\widehat{\lambda}_{r}^{-1}

with a constant
C_{k}
depending only on the kernel
k
. Here,
\widehat{\lambda}_{r}
denotes the
r
-th largest eigenvalue of the operator
\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}
. Again, if
K_{u}^{t}\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\partial}
, then

\|K_{u}^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r,u}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mu)}\,\leq\,\sqrt{\lambda_{r+1}}M+c_{r}\varepsilon+t^{2}\cdot C_{k}\widehat{\lambda}_{r}^{-1}.

Proof.

Using the approximate control-affinity (
5.7
), we compute

\displaystyle(P_{r}K_{u}^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r,u}^{m,t})\psi=(P_{r}K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t})\psi+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\left(P_{r}K^{t}_{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-P_{r}K^{t}+\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}\right)\psi+t^{2}\phi,

where
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}\leq c_{\psi}
for all choices of
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
t\in[0,T]
. By the choice of
m
, the respective bounds in Theorem
4.2
on
\|P_{r}K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t}\|
and
\|P_{r}K^{t}_{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}\|
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,{n_{u}}\}
, hold with probability at least
1-\delta(n_{u}+1)^{-1}
each. Hence, the probability for all
n_{u}+1
bounds to hold exceeds
1-\delta
, cf.
[
37
, Lemma 22]
. In that event,

\displaystyle\Big\|(P_{r}K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t})\psi+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\left(P_{r}K^{t}_{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-P_{r}K^{t}+\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}\right)\psi\Big\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{r}K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big(\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,{n_{u}}\}}\big\|P_{r}K^{t}_{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}+\|P_{r}K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{r}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\Big)\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\frac{|u_{i}|}{\gamma_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\big(1+2\|\Gamma^{-1}u\|_{1}\big)c_{r}\varepsilon\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}},

which proves the given estimate. The proof for the case
K_{u}^{t}\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\partial}
follows analogous lines, making use of (
4.2
).

Now, let
k\in C^{2}
and denote the flow of
\dot{x}=f_{v}(x)
by
F_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{U}
. Also, let
F:=F_{0}
. In addition to the random variables
y_{k}=F^{t}(x_{k})
,
k=0,\ldots,m-1
, let
y_{k,v}=F_{v}^{t}(x_{k})
,
i=1,\ldots,{n_{u}}
. Moreover, let
\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y},v}
be the empirical estimator of the cross-covariance operator
C^{t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y},v}
for the system
\dot{x}=f_{v}(x)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}

\displaystyle=\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\big(\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r}\big)=[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}\Big[\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\big(\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y},\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\Bigg[\Phi(F^{t}(x_{k}))+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\big(\Phi(F_{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}^{t}(x_{k}))-\Phi(F^{t}(x_{k}))\big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\Bigg[\mathbb{K}^{t}\Phi(x_{k})+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\big(\mathbb{K}_{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}^{t}\Phi(x_{k})-\mathbb{K}^{t}\Phi(x_{k})\big)\Bigg],

where
\mathbb{K}_{v}^{t}:L^{2}(\mathcal{Y},\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}})
for
v\in\mathcal{U}
is defined by
\mathbb{K}_{v}^{t}\Psi(x)=\Psi(F_{v}^{t}(x))
,
\Psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Y},\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}})
, and
\mathbb{K}^{t}:=\mathbb{K}^{t}_{0}
. As
\Phi\in C^{2}
, we can, analogously as in the discussion preceding the theorem, see that

\mathbb{K}^{t}\Phi+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{u}}}\frac{u_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\left(\mathbb{K}^{t}_{\gamma_{i}e_{i}}-\mathbb{K}^{t}\right)\Phi=\mathbb{K}_{u}^{t}\Phi+t^{2}\Psi_{u,t}

with some
\Psi_{u,t}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
C_{\Phi}=\sup\{\|\Psi_{u,t}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}})}:u\in\mathcal{U},\,t\in[0,T]\}<\infty
. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}

\displaystyle=[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\big[\Phi(F_{u}^{t}(x_{k}))+t^{2}\Psi_{u,t}(x_{k})\big]

\displaystyle=[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}\Big[\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y},u}+\frac{t^{2}}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\Psi_{u,t}(x_{k})\Big].

Note that
[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y},u}
is the estimator for
K^{t}_{u}
as defined in (
3.12
). Here, we shall denote it by
\widetilde{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}
. Now, if
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, then

\displaystyle\|P_{r}K^{t}\psi-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{r}K^{t}\psi-\widetilde{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}+\|\widetilde{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}\psi-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{r,u}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{r}\varepsilon\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}+\frac{t^{2}}{m}\Bigg\|\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}[\Phi(x_{k})\otimes\Psi_{u,t}(x_{k})]\psi\Bigg\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{r}\varepsilon\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}+\frac{t^{2}}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}|\langle\psi,\Psi_{u,t}(x_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}|\cdot\|[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}\Phi(x_{k})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{r}\varepsilon\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}+\|\varphi\|_{1,\mathcal{X}}^{1/2}\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}|\langle\psi,\Psi_{u,t}(x_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}|\|[\widehat{C}_{\mathcal{X}}^{m}]_{r}^{\dagger}\Phi(x_{k})\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{r}\varepsilon\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}+\|k\|_{\infty}^{1/2}\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\|\Psi_{u,t}(x_{k})\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\widehat{\lambda}_{r}^{-1}\sqrt{\varphi(x_{k})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{r}\varepsilon\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}+\widehat{\lambda}_{r}^{-1}C_{\Phi}\|k\|_{\infty}\cdot t^{2}\|\psi\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}},

which proves the claim with
C_{k}=C_{\Phi}\|k\|_{\infty}
.
∎

6.
Numerical results

In this part, we present two numerical examples. First we consider an autonomous stochastic system to illustrate the results of Section
4
. Second, we present numerical results for a controlled deterministic system to showcase the bounds deduced in Section
5
.

6.1.
Estimation Error for Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process

We consider a one-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process obeying the stochastic differential equation

(6.1)

dX_{t}=-\alpha X_{t}+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}dW_{t},

with
\alpha=1
and
\beta=2
in the numerical tests. The initial domain is chosen as
\mathcal{X}=[-1.5,1.5]
, the final domain is simply the entire real line
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
. The measure
\mu
is chosen as the invariant distribution of the OU process on
\mathcal{Y}
, which is normal with mean zero and standard deviation
(\alpha\beta)^{-1/2}
. We then use the restriction of
\mu
to the finite interval
[-1.5,1.5]
to generate initial conditions for the SDE (
6.1
).

Mirroring the analysis in
[
42
]
, we consider the approximation of the cross-covariance operator
C^{t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}
at lag time
t=0.05
, corresponding to five elementary time steps at integration time step
\Delta_{t}=0.01
. We use the Gaussian radial basis function kernel

k(x,y)=\exp\Big[-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big],

for bandwidths
\sigma\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5\}
. Setting the confidence level to
1-\delta=0.9
, we use Proposition
4.1
to bound the minimal guaranteed error that is achieved with this confidence, for a range of data sizes
m
between
m=20
and
m=5\cdot 10^{4}
. As a comparison, we estimate the actual incurred error for fifty independent collections of
m
pairs
(x_{k},y_{k})
each, and plot the
(1-\delta
)-percentile for this error as a function of
m
. To do so, we first observe that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the empirical covariance operator
\widehat{C}^{t,m}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}
can be calculated using the Mercer features and eigenvalues of the Gaussian kernel:

(6.2)

\|\widehat{C}^{t,m}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}(M^{m}_{ij})^{2},

using the following matrix elements for the Mercer features:

(6.3)

M^{m}_{ij}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}e_{i}(x_{l})e_{j}(y_{l}).

In practice, we truncate the infinite sum in (
6.2
) after
i_{\max}=j_{\max}=30
terms. We then first generate ten large reference collections
(x_{l},y_{l}),1\leq l\leq m_{\max}
, where
m_{\max}=10^{7}
, and average the matrix elements
M^{m_{\max}}_{ij}
over these large reference samples. The actual error for
m<m_{\max}
data pairs
(x_{k},y_{k}),1\leq k\leq m
is then estimated by the formula

\|C^{t}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}-\widehat{C}^{t,m}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}\approx\|\widehat{C}^{t,m_{\max}}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}-\widehat{C}^{t,m}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}=\|\widehat{C}^{t,m_{\max}}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}-2\left\langle\widehat{C}^{t,m_{\max}}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}},\,\widehat{C}^{t,m}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}+\|\widehat{C}^{t,m}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}.

The Hilbert-Schmidt inner product can be computed by the formula

\left\langle\widehat{C}^{t,m_{\max}}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}},\,\widehat{C}^{t,m}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}M^{\max}_{ij}M^{m}_{ij}.

The results are shown in Figure

over-estimate the actual incurred error by about one order of magnitude.

6.2.
Duffing Oscillator

To conclude this study, we illustrate that kernel EDMD, combined with state-of-the art model validation and low-rank approximation techniques, enables accurate prediction of a non-linear control system with very few necessary design choices on the modeler’s end. We study the two-dimensional Duffing oscillator, governed by the ordinary differential equation

(6.4)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}(t)\\
z_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}z_{2}\\
-\alpha z_{1}u-2\beta z_{1}^{3}\end{bmatrix},

which has been used as a model system in numerous previous studies. We set
\alpha=-1
,
\beta=1
. By

at time zero and the control

. We generate

uniformly sampled initial conditions

\mathcal{X}=[-1.5,1.5]^{2}

\bar{u}\in\{0,1\}
, we compute the successor state

for
t=0.025
via numerical integration with time step
\Delta_{t}=0.005
. The data pairs

serve as training data for the kEDMD algorithm corresponding to the flow with constant control
\bar{u}\in\{0,1\}

\mathcal{X}

The condition (
3.5
) for deterministic systems

Let
\mathcal{X}=\overline{\Omega}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is open and bounded. We consider the flow
F:[0,T]\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of a given autonomous ODE
\dot{x}=b(x)
. Let
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathcal{Z}:=F([0,T]\times\mathcal{X})

\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{Y}
which guarantee that condition (

\mu(F_{t}^{-1}(A))\,\leq\,L\cdot\mu(A)\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y}),\,\forall t\in[0,T].

First of all, we note that it is sufficient to prove the above for
\mathcal{Y}
replaced by
\mathcal{Z}
. Indeed, then for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y})
we have

\mu(F_{t}^{-1}(A))=\mu(F_{t}^{-1}(A\cap\mathcal{Z}))\leq L\cdot\mu(A\cap\mathcal{Z})\leq L\cdot\mu(A).

Proposition A.1
.

The condition (

•

The flow
F:[0,\widetilde{T}]\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the ODE
\dot{x}=b(x)
is defined for some
\widetilde{T}>T
and is
C^{1}
.

•

d\mu(x)=\varphi(x)\,dx
with a positive density
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})
satisfying
1/\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z})
.

Note that the first assumption is satisfied if

, cf.
[
1
, Theorem 10.3]
.

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

First of all, we show that

(A.1)

\displaystyle\delta:=\inf\big\{\det F_{t}^{\prime}(x):x\in\mathcal{X},\,t\in[0,T]\big\}>0.

Since
F_{0}(z)=z
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we have
F_{0}^{\prime}(z)=I_{d}
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Let
t_{1}:=\widetilde{T}-T

F:[0,t_{1}]\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined on
\mathcal{Z}
and is
C^{1}
. Hence, there exists some

for all

and all

. Choose
t_{0}
such that
T=nt_{0}
for some

. We prove by induction that for all

we have

(A.2)

\displaystyle\forall\,t\in[0,t_{0}]\,\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\det F_{kt_{0}+t}^{\prime}(x)\geq 2^{-k-1}.

Then (

) holds with
\delta=2^{-n}
. We have proved (
A.2
) already for

. So, let (
A.2

and

\det F_{(k+1)t_{0}+t}^{\prime}(x)=\det D_{x}F_{t_{0}}(F_{kt_{0}+t}(x))=\det F_{t_{0}}^{\prime}(F_{kt_{0}+t}(x))\cdot\det F_{kt_{0}+t}^{\prime}(x)\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2^{k+1}}=\tfrac{1}{2^{k+2}},

which concludes the proof of (

In order to prove (

F:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mu(\partial\Omega)=0

t\in[0,T]
, set
\mathcal{Z}_{t}:=F_{t}(\Omega)
, and let

be open. Note that
\mathcal{Z}_{t}
is open due to the invariance of domain theorem
3
3

3

or simply the inverse function theorem thanks to (

)
. Hence, also

is open. By the change of variables formula we have

\displaystyle\mu(F_{t}^{-1}(U))

\displaystyle=\mu(F_{t}^{-1}(U_{t}))=\int_{F_{t}^{-1}(U_{t})}\varphi(x)\,dx=\int_{U_{t}}\varphi(F_{t}^{-1}(y))|\det DF_{t}^{-1}(y)|\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{U_{t}}\frac{\varphi(F_{t}^{-1}(y))}{|\det F_{t}^{\prime}(F_{t}^{-1}(y))|\varphi(y)}\varphi(y)\,dy\,\leq\,\frac{\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|\frac{1}{\varphi}\|_{\infty}}{\delta}\mu(U_{t})\leq\frac{\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|\frac{1}{\varphi}\|_{\infty}}{\delta}\mu(U).

The claim now follows from the outer regularity of the measures

Proof of Proposition
3.3

Let
\psi\in B(\mathcal{Y})
. Then
|(K^{t}\psi)(x)|\leq\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\psi(y)|\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\leq\|\psi\|_{\infty}
, hence
K^{t}\psi\in B(\mathcal{X})
with
\|K^{t}\psi\|_{\infty}\leq\|\psi\|_{\infty}
and

\|K^{t}\psi\|_{p}\leq\|\psi\|_{\infty}
. Let now
1\leq p<\infty
. Then, by Hölder’s inequality and (
3.7
),

\int_{\mathcal{X}}|(K^{t}\psi)(x)|^{p}\,d\mu(x)\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\psi(y)|^{p}\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)\leq L\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\psi(y)|^{p}\,d\mu(y),

and hence
\|K^{t}\psi\|_{p,\mathcal{X}}\leq L^{1/p}\|\psi\|_{p,\mathcal{Y}}
.

Let
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{Y})
(the set of continuous functions on
\mathcal{Y}
with compact support). For fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
, denote the stochastic solution process of the SDE (
3.1
) with initial value
x
by
X_{t}^{x}
. Since
X_{t}^{x}(\omega)
is continuous in
t
a.s. (see
[
38
, Theorem 5.2.1]
) and
X_{t}^{x}\in\mathcal{Y}
a.s., we have
\psi(X_{t}^{x}(\omega))\to\psi(X_{0}^{x}(\omega))=\psi(x)
as
t\to 0
a.s. Hence, by dominated convergence,

K^{t}\psi(x)=\mathbb{E}[\psi(X_{t}^{x})]=\int\psi(X_{t}^{x}(\omega))\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega)\,\to\,\psi(x)

as
t\to 0
. By the first part of this proof,

|K^{t}\psi(x)-\psi(x)|\,\leq\,|K^{t}\psi(x)|+|\psi(x)|\leq 2\|\psi\|_{\infty},\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},

hence, dominated convergence implies that
\|K^{t}\psi-\psi|_{\mathcal{X}}\|_{p,\mathcal{X}}\to 0

.

If

Appendix C
An Example: Lack of contractivity of the Koopman semigroup

Consider the deterministic case with the scalar ODE

\displaystyle\dot{x}=x(2-x)\quad\text{on $\mathcal{X}=[1,2]$}.

The solution of this ODE is given by

\displaystyle x(t;x_{0})=\frac{2e^{2t}x_{0}}{2-x_{0}+e^{2t}x_{0}},\quad x_{0}\in\mathcal{X},

which is non-decreasing and approaches
2
as
t\to\infty
for any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
. In particular,
\mathcal{X}
is invariant under the flow of the ODE. Since
\mathcal{X}
is compact, the convergence towards the equilibrium
x=2
is uniform in
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
. We will use this fact to show that the Koopman semigroup is not a bounded semigroup on
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, i.e., it does not satisfy
\|K^{t}\|_{L(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq M
for some
M\geq 0
and all
t\geq 0
. To show this, it suffices to find
\psi\in L^{2}(1,2)
such that for any
M\geq 0
, there is
t\geq 0
such that
\|K^{t}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\geq M\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}
.

Let
\psi(x):=\frac{1}{(2-x)^{1/4}}
. Then
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
as

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{2}\psi^{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{1}^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-x}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x=2\sqrt{x}\Big|_{0}^{1}=2<\infty.

Further, we have that
\psi(x)\to\infty
as
x\to 2
and
\psi
is non-decreasing such that

(C.1)

\displaystyle\forall M\geq 0\,\,\exists\,\varepsilon>0:\,\psi(x)\geq M\qquad\forall x\in(2-\varepsilon,2).

Moreover, as the flow is non-decreasing towards
2
:

(C.2)

\displaystyle\forall\varepsilon\geq 0\,\,\exists\,t^{*}\geq 0\,\,\forall t\geq t^{*}\,\,\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\{2\}:\,x(t;x_{0})\in(2-\varepsilon,2).

Hence, for given
M
, choose
\varepsilon
as in (
C.1
) and correspondingly
t^{*}
as in (
C.2
). Then
x(t;x^{0})\in(2-\varepsilon,2)
for all
t\geq t^{*}
and
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\{2\}
, hence

\displaystyle\forall t\geq t^{*}:\quad\|K^{t}\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}=\int_{1}^{2}|\psi(x(t;x_{0}))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\geq M^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}M^{2}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}.

Appendix D
On the condition
K^{t}\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
in case of the Gaussian kernel and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process on
X=\mathbb{R}
is the solution of the SDE
dX_{t}=-\alpha X_{t}\,dt+dW_{t}
, where
\alpha>0
. The invariant measure
\mu
and the Markov transition kernel
\rho_{t}
,
t>0
, are known and given by

\displaystyle d\mu(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\pi}}\cdot e^{-\alpha x^{2}}\,dx\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{t}(x,dy)=\sqrt{\frac{c_{t}}{\pi}}\cdot\exp\Big[-c_{t}(y-e^{-\alpha t}x)^{2}\Big]\,dy,

where

c_{t}=\frac{\alpha}{1-e^{-2\alpha t}}.

We consider the Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) kernels on
\mathbb{R}
with bandwidth
\sigma>0
, i.e.,

k^{\sigma}(x,y)=\exp\left[-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right].

For
y\in\mathbb{R}
and a kernel
k
on
\mathbb{R}
set
k_{y}(x):=k(x,y)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}
be any set. By
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(X)
we denote the RKHS generated by the kernel
k^{\sigma}_{X}=k^{\sigma}|_{X\times X}
on
X
. Hilbert space norm and scalar product on
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(X)
will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\sigma,X}
and
\langle\cdot\,,\,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma,X}
, respectively. For two positive definite kernels on
X
we write
k_{1}\preceq k_{2}
if

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{1}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{2}(x_{i},x_{j})

for any choice of
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
x_{j}\in X
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. We also write
V\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}W
for two normed vector spaces
V
and
W
if
V\subset W
is continuously embedded in
W
.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}
and
0<\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}
. Then we have
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{2}}(X)\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{1}}(X)
with

\|\psi\|_{\sigma_{1},X}\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}}\cdot\|\psi\|_{\sigma_{2},X},\qquad\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{2}}(X),

and
k_{X}^{\sigma_{2}}\preceq\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}k_{X}^{\sigma_{1}}
.

Proof.

The first claim is Corollary 6 in
[
50
]
. The second follows from Aronszajn’s inclusion theorem
[
39
, Theorem 5.1]
.
∎

Theorem D.2
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
and let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}
be any compact set with non-empty interior. Then for each
t\geq 0
and all
\alpha,\sigma>0
we have

K^{t}\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X})\qquad\text{with}\qquad\big\|K^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X})}\leq e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}t}.

Proof.

First of all, it follows from
[
50
, Corollaries 4&5]
that to each
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X})
there exists a unique extension
\tilde{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})
such that
\langle\tilde{\psi},\tilde{\phi}\rangle_{\sigma,\mathcal{Y}}=\langle\psi,\phi\rangle_{\sigma,\mathcal{X}}
for all
\psi,\phi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X})
. Conversely, we have

(D.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X})=\{\psi|_{\mathcal{X}}:\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle\psi,\phi\rangle_{\sigma,\mathcal{Y}}=\langle\psi|_{\mathcal{X}},\phi|_{\mathcal{X}}\rangle_{\sigma,\mathcal{X}},\qquad\psi,\phi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y}).

For
t=0
the claim is obviously true. Hence, suppose that
t>0
. For
z\in\mathbb{R}
let us compute
K^{t}k^{\sigma}_{z}
. For this, we define

\tau=\sqrt{\frac{c_{t}\sigma^{2}}{1+c_{t}\sigma^{2}}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu=\frac{e^{\alpha t}}{\tau}\sigma\,>\,\frac{\sigma}{\tau}>\sigma.

Since for
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}>0
and
z,w\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}^{\sigma_{1}}(x)\,k_{w}^{\sigma_{2}}(x)\,dx=\sqrt{\pi}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(z-w)^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right),

with
\sigma_{t}=1/\sqrt{c_{t}}
we obtain for
x\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle(K^{t}k_{z}^{\sigma})(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}^{\sigma}(y)\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)=\sqrt{\frac{c_{t}}{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}^{\sigma}(y)\exp\Big[-c_{t}(y-e^{-\alpha t}x)^{2}\Big]\,dy

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{c_{t}}{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}^{\sigma}(y)k_{e^{-\alpha t}x}^{\sigma_{t}}(y)\,dy=\sqrt{c_{t}}\cdot\frac{\sigma\sigma_{t}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(z-e^{-\alpha t}x)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+1/c_{t}}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(e^{\alpha t}z-x)^{2}}{e^{2\alpha t}(\sigma^{2}+1/c_{t})}\right)=\tau\cdot k^{\nu}_{e^{\alpha t}z}(x).

Since
\nu>\sigma
, it follows from (
D.1
) and Lemma
D.1
that

K^{t}k_{z}^{\sigma}=\tau\cdot k^{\nu}_{e^{\alpha t}z}|_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{H}_{\nu}(\mathcal{X})\subset\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X}).

Note that

k^{\nu}(e^{\alpha t}x,e^{\alpha t}y)=\exp\left(-\frac{e^{2\alpha t}(x-y)^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}(x-y)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)=k^{\sigma/\tau}(x,y).

Now, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{j},x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, be arbitrary and let
\psi=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}k^{\sigma}_{x_{j}}\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})
. Then, again by (
D.1
) and Lemma
D.1
,

\displaystyle\|K^{t}\psi\|_{\sigma,\mathcal{X}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu}{\sigma}\|K^{t}\psi\|_{\nu,\mathcal{X}}^{2}=\frac{\nu}{\sigma}\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}K^{t}k^{\sigma}_{x_{j}}\Bigg\|_{\nu,\mathcal{X}}^{2}=\frac{\nu\tau^{2}}{\sigma}\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}k^{\nu}_{e^{\alpha t}x_{j}}\Bigg\|_{\nu,\mathcal{Y}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu\tau^{2}}{\sigma}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{\nu}(e^{\alpha t}x_{i},e^{\alpha t}x_{j})=\frac{\nu\tau^{2}}{\sigma}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{\sigma/\tau}(x_{i},x_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu\tau^{2}}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{\sigma}(x_{i},x_{j})=\frac{\nu\tau}{\sigma}\|\psi\|_{\sigma,\mathcal{Y}}^{2}=e^{\alpha t}\|\psi\|_{\sigma,\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

This shows that
K^{t}
maps
\mathbb{H}_{0,\sigma}(\mathcal{Y}):=\operatorname{span}\{k^{\sigma}_{y}:y\in\mathcal{Y}\}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})
boundedly into
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X})
. Since
\mathbb{H}_{0,\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})
is dense in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})
, it follows that
K^{t}|_{\mathbb{H}_{0,\sigma}}
extends to a bounded operator
T:\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X})
. In order to see that
T\psi=K^{t}\psi
for
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})
, let
(\psi_{n})\subset\mathbb{H}_{0,\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})
such that
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})
. Then
K^{t}\psi_{n}=T\psi_{n}\to T\psi
in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{X})
. Since
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Y})\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{Y})
, we have
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{Y})
and thus
K^{t}\psi_{n}\to K^{t}\psi
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})
. Also,
K^{t}\psi_{n}\to T\psi
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})
. Hence,
K^{t}\psi=T\psi

\mu
-a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
. But as both
K^{t}\psi
and
T\psi
are continuous and
\mu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue measure with a positive density, we conclude that
K^{t}\psi=T\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
.
∎
