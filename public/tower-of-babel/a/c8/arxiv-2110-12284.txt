A phase-field model for thermo-mechanical fracture with an open-source implementation of it using Gridap in Julia
Ved Prakash, Akash Kumar Behera, Mohammad Masiur Rahaman

Abstract
In this article, we propose a thermodynamically consistent phase-field model for thermo-mechanical fracture and provide an open-source implementation of the proposed model using a recently developed finite element toolbox, Gridap in Julia. Here, we have derived the balance equations for the thermo-mechanical fracture by invoking the virtual power principle and determined the constitutive relations for the thermodynamic fluxes based on the satisfaction of the thermodynamic laws. Our proposed formulation provides an equation of temperature evolution that can easily accommodate dissipative effects such as viscous damping. One may consider the proposed model as a non-trivial extension of a recently developed iso-thermal phase-field model by Dhas et al. [ 1 ] for the non-isothermal case. We provide very compact and user-friendly open-source codes for implementing the proposed model using Gridap in Julia that requires very low memory usage and gives a high degree of flexibility to the users in defining weak forms of the governing partial differential equations. We have validated the proposed model and its implementation against such standard results available in the literature as crack propagation in the cruciform shape material, single edge-notched plate, bi-material beam and a quenching test.

Introduction

Analysis of thermally induced stresses and cracks are of great importance in the design of engineering materials. When materials are exposed to a sudden change in ambient temperature, it can result in a non-uniform change of volume that induces thermal stresses and leads to the initiation of cracks. For many practical problems, an iso-thermal model may not be accurate enough to capture the experimental observations when the effect due to the difference between ambient and reference temperature is significant. Specifically, for problems related to thermal shock, materials with inhomogeneous thermal properties, materials subjected to cooling during manufacturing, etc., consideration of thermal effect becomes vital. Some of the practical examples are shrinkage cracks in the concrete, window materials for aircraft
[
2
]
, additive manufacturing
[
3

etc. Thermally-induced cracks may be complex and may follow paths very different from the one observed for mechanically induced cracks; typically, cracks propagate from higher heating area to lower heating area
[
4
]
. Thermo-mechanical coupling can significantly modify the behavior and can lead to unstable crack growth. Also, understanding the mechanism of thermally induced fracture is essential in the development of novel engineering materials. Hence, fracture analysis of materials under thermo-mechanical loading has become one of the contemporary topics of research interest.

Over the years, several experiments have been carried out to understand the behavior of thermally induced cracks and their effect on the strength or other essential parameters. Geyer and Nemat-Nasser
[
5
]
considered the crack growth behavior of a glass plate where cooling from the boundaries resulted in parallel cracks. They have observed stable crack growth for pre-cooling cracks. The effect of the width of a plate on speed and nature of crack propagation under the presence of thermal gradient was studied by Ronsin
et al.

[
6
]
. Shao
et al.

[
7
]
studied how the residual strength of ceramics varies with the stages of crack propagation. To understand the behavior of infrared window materials used in hypersonic aircraft Qu
et al.

[
2

conducted numerical (finite element analysis) and experimental studies on chemical vapor deposited zinc sulfide (CVD ZnS). Yousef
et al.

[

experimentally observed the microcrack evolution in large-grained polycrystalline alumina along with the finite element simulation. They also reported variation in macro-structural properties such as young’s modulus due to reduction in temperature. To study the stable crack growth in glass Grutzik and Reedy

Modeling of fracture involving realistic conditions is tackled with the help of numerical approaches. Broadly these approaches can be classified as discrete or discontinuous and diffusive or continuous. 2
Proposed phase-field model

In this section, we provide the details of our proposed model in a small deformation set-up. The proposed model can be considered as a non-trivial extension of a recently developed phase-field model by Dhas
et al.

[
1
]

2.1
Kinematics

Consider a material body defined by an open set
\Omega
with boundary
\partial\Omega
in the three-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. Corresponding to the displacement field, let
\partial\Omega_{u}
and
\partial\Omega_{t}
be the Dirichlet and Neumann boundaries, respectively. Corresponding to the temperature field, let
\partial\Omega_{T}
and
\partial\Omega_{q}
be the Dirichlet and Neumann boundaries, respectively. We define the displacement field as

\bm{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
at any instant of time
t\in\mathrm{R}^{+}
. Within a small deformation set-up, the strain tensor
\bm{\epsilon}
is given by

\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{u}+\nabla\bm{u}^{T}),

(1)

where
(\cdot)^{T}
denotes the transpose of a tensor and
\nabla
is the gradient operator. For thermo-mechanical problems,
\bm{\epsilon}
can be considered to be consisting of an elastic strain
\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}
and a thermal strain
\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{th}}
, which can be represented as

\bm{\epsilon}=\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}+\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{th}},

(2)

where

\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{th}}=\alpha(T-T_{0})\bm{I},

(3)

for a temperature change from an initial temperature
T_{0}
to current temperature
T
, considering
\alpha
as the thermal coefficient of the material body with
\bm{I}
denoting the second-order identity tensor.

(b)

\Omega
with Dirichlet boundaries (

,

) subjected to applied temperature, displacement, heat flux, and traction
\bar{T}
,

,

, respectively. Sub-figure (a) is the material body with sharp crack. For phase-field modeling of fracture a diffused representation of crack is considered in sub-figure (b) by introducing a length scale

In the PFM, sharp cracks are approximated by employing a diffused representation of the cracks via a length scale parameter
l_{s}
and a phase-field internal variable
s
(see Fig.

). Damaged, undamaged, and partially damaged states of matter can be described using the values of the phase-field variable as follows:
s=1
for undamaged state,
s=0
for fully damaged state and
s\in(0,1)
for a partially damaged state. Considering fully damaged states as the fracture, crack set may be defined as
\Omega_{s}=\{\bm{x}\in\Omega|s(\bm{x})=0\}

\bm{x}\in\Omega_{s}

as an additional kinematic descriptor along with the displacement vector
\bm{u}
and the temperature field
T
.

2.2
Virtual power principle and the balance equations

To describe the deformation of a material body under external loading, one can derive the force balances from a virtual power principle by considering a macro-force and a micro-force system
[
44
]
. While stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
, traction vector
\bm{t}(\bm{n})
and body force
\bm{b}
define the macro-force system, the micro-force system includes scalar micro-traction
\chi(\bm{n})
, vector micro-stress
\bm{\xi}
and scalar micro-stress
\pi
. It is important to note that the micro-system is also at the same continuum level of the macro-system and the coinage ‘micro-force’ may be a misnomer. One can identify
\dot{\bm{u}}
as the power conjugate of
\bm{t}(\bm{n})
and
\bm{b}
,
\dot{s}
as the power conjugate of
\chi(\bm{n})
and express the external mechanical power for any arbitrary sub-domain
\mathcal{P}\subset\Omega
as

P^{\text{ext}}=\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\biggl[\bm{t}\left(\bm{n}\right)\cdot\dot{\bm{u}}+\chi(\bm{n})\cdot\dot{s}\biggr]\,dA+\int_{\mathcal{P}}\bm{b}\cdot\dot{\bm{u}}\,dV,

(4)

where,
(\dot{\,\,})
denotes the time derivative of a variable,
\bm{n}
the unit normal vector to the boundary
\partial\mathcal{P}
,
dA
and
dV
are, respectively, measures on
\partial\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}
. The internal mechanical power
P^{\text{int}}
can be defined by the summation of power expenditure of
\bm{\sigma}
over
\dot{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}}
,
\bm{\xi}
over
\nabla\dot{s}
,
\pi
over
\dot{s}
and can be given by

P^{\text{int}}=\int_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\bm{\sigma}:\dot{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}}+\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{s}+\pi\,\dot{s}\right)dV.

(5)

We denote the virtual counterparts of
\dot{\bm{u}}
and
\dot{s}
by
\tilde{\bm{u}}
and
\tilde{s}
, respectively. Introducing the generalized virtual velocity vector
\mathcal{V}=\left(\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{s}\right)
, one can express the external virtual mechanical power
\tilde{P}^{\text{ext}}\left(\mathcal{P};\mathcal{V}\right)
and the internal virtual mechanical power
\tilde{P}^{\text{int}}\left(\mathcal{P};\mathcal{V}\right)
as

\tilde{P}^{\text{ext}}\left(\mathcal{P};\mathcal{V}\right)=\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\biggl[\bm{t}\left(\bm{n}\right)\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}+\chi(\bm{n})\cdot\tilde{s}\biggr]dA+\int_{\mathcal{P}}\bm{b}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}\,dV,

(6)

and

\tilde{P}^{\text{int}}\left(\mathcal{P};\mathcal{V}\right)=\int_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\bm{\sigma}:\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}}+\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla\tilde{s}+\pi\tilde{s}\right)dV,

(7)

respectively. At a given temperature from the symmetry of stress tensor one can show that
\bm{\sigma}:\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}}=\bm{\sigma}:\nabla{\tilde{\bm{u}}}
using of which in the virtual power principle, i.e.,
\tilde{P}^{\text{int}}\left(\mathcal{P};\mathcal{V}\right)=\tilde{P}^{\text{ext}}\left(\mathcal{P};\mathcal{V}\right)
, one can arrive at

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\biggl[\bm{t}\left(\bm{n}\right)\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}+\chi(\bm{n})\cdot\tilde{s}]dA+\int_{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{b}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}})\,dV=\int_{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{\sigma}:\nabla{\tilde{\bm{u}}}+\\
\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla\tilde{s}+\pi\tilde{s})\,dV.\end{split}

(8)

Appropriate choices of
\mathcal{V}=\left(\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{s}\right)
in Eq. (
8
) lead to the macro-force and the micro-force balances as described below.

2.2.1
Macro-force balance

Considering
\mathcal{V}=\left(\tilde{\bm{u}},0\right)
, i.e., by substituting
\tilde{s}=0
in Eq. (
8
), and using the divergence theorem the macro-force balance Eq. may be obtained as

\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\left(\bm{t}\left(\bm{n}\right)-\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\right).\tilde{\bm{u}}\,dA=-\int_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}+\bm{b}\right)\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}\,dV,

(9)

which holds for all
\tilde{\bm{u}}
and any arbitrary sub-domain
\mathcal{P}
. Applying the localization theorem on Eq. (
9
), one can get that

\bm{t}\left(\bm{n}\right)=\bm{\sigma}\,\bm{n},

(10)

which is the macro-traction condition and

\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}+\bm{b}=\bm{0}.

(11)

called the macro-force balance. Equations (
10
) and (
11
) may be identified as the classical traction condition and the local linear momentum balance equation, respectively.

2.2.2
Micro-force balance

Considering
\mathcal{V}=\left(\bm{0},\tilde{s}\right)
, i.e., by substituting
\tilde{\bm{u}}=\bm{0}
in Eq. (
8
), the equation for micro-force balance can be derived as

\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\biggl[\chi(\bm{n})-\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{n}\biggr]\cdot\tilde{s}\,dA=-\int_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{\xi}-\pi\right)\cdot\tilde{s}\,dV.

(12)

Since Eq. (
12
) holds for all
\tilde{s}
and any sub-domain
\mathcal{P}
, Eq. (
12
) may be localized as

\chi(\bm{n})=\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{n},

(13)

and

\nabla\cdot\bm{\xi}-\pi=0.

(14)

Equations (
13
) and (
14
) are called the micro-traction condition and the micro-force balance, respectively.

2.3
Thermodynamics laws and constitutive modeling

To determine constitutive relations for the thermodynamic fluxes, we have followed the work by Anand and Gurtin

[
45
]
. In presence of heat flux
\bm{q}
, and heat supply
Q_{\text{h}}
, the first law of thermodynamics, i.e., the law of conservation of energy, can be given by

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{P}e\,dV=P^{\text{int}}-\int_{\partial P}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{n}dA+\int_{P}Q_{h}dV,

(15)

where,
e
is the internal energy density and the power balance i.e.
P^{ext}=P^{int}
is used. From the second law of thermodynamics, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{P}\eta\,dV\geq-\int_{\partial P}\frac{\bm{q}\cdot\bm{n}}{T}\,dA+\int_{P}\frac{Q_{\text{h}}}{T}\,dV,

(16)

where
\eta
is the entropy per unit volume. The inequality given by Eq. (
16
) ensures that the rate of entropy increase is not less than the entropy input in the system. Using the divergence theorem on Eq. (
15
) and Eq. (
16
), and applying the localization theorem we get

\dot{e}=-\nabla\cdot\bm{q}+Q_{\text{h}}+\bm{\sigma}:\dot{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}}+\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{s}+\pi\,\dot{s},

(17)

and

\dot{\eta}\geq-\frac{1}{T}\nabla\cdot\bm{q}+\frac{1}{T^{2}}\bm{q}\cdot\nabla T+\frac{Q_{\text{h}}}{T}.

(18)

From the relation between Helmholtz free energy and internal energy density given by
\psi=e-T\eta
, we have

\dot{\psi}=\dot{e}-\dot{T}\eta-T\dot{\eta}.

(19)

Employing equations (
11
), (
14
), (
17
), (
18
) and (
19
), we get that

\dot{\psi}-\bm{\sigma}:\dot{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}}-\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{s}-\pi\,\dot{s}+\eta\,\dot{T}+\frac{1}{T}\bm{q}\cdot\nabla T\leq 0.

(20)

The inequality given by Eq. (
20
) needs to be satisfied for a thermodynamic process to be valid, and one can determine constitutive functions for
\bm{\sigma}
,
\pi
,
\eta
and
\bm{\xi}
on satisfying Eq. (
20
).

2.4
Constitutive response functions

We assume the Helmholtz free energy
\psi
is a function of
\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}},\,s,\,\nabla s
, and
T
, i.e.,

\psi=\hat{\psi}(\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}},s,\nabla s,T).

(21)

Using the chain rule, the rate of Helmholtz energy can be expressed as

\dot{\psi}=\partial_{\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}}\psi:\dot{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}}+\partial_{s}\psi\,\dot{s}+\partial_{\nabla s}\psi\cdot{\nabla\dot{s}}+\partial_{T}\psi\,\dot{T}.

(22)

Considering
\pi
as sum of an energetic part
\pi^{\text{en}}
and a dissipative part
\pi^{\text{dis}}
, i.e.
\pi=\pi^{\text{en}}+\pi^{\text{dis}}
, and employing Eq. (
22
), we can rewrite Eq. (
20
) as

\begin{split}(\bm{\sigma}-\partial_{\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}}\psi):\dot{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}}+(\pi^{\text{en}}-\partial_{s}\psi)\dot{s}+(\bm{\xi}-\partial_{\nabla s}\psi)\cdot{\nabla\dot{s}}+\\
\pi^{\text{dis}}\dot{s}-(\eta+\partial_{T}\psi)\dot{T}-\frac{1}{T}\bm{q}\cdot\nabla T\geq 0.\end{split}

(23)

Applying Collman and Noll
[
46
]
procedure to Eq. (
23
). we obtain the following constitutive relations

\bm{\sigma}=\partial_{\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}}\psi,

(24)

\bm{\xi}=\partial_{\nabla s}\psi,

(25)

\pi^{\text{en}}=\partial_{s}\psi,

(26)

\eta=-\partial_{T}\psi,

(27)

and the inequality given by (
23
) reduces to

\pi^{\text{dis}}\dot{s}-\frac{1}{T}\bm{q}\cdot\nabla T\geq 0.

(28)

Determination of the constitutive relation must be done in such a way that the inequality constraint given by Eq. (
28
) always satisfy. From the irreversiblity condition on damage i.e.
\dot{s}\leq 0
, it can be realised that a possible choice for
\pi^{\text{dis}}
could be
\pi^{\text{dis}}=\mathcal{G}\dot{s}
, where
\mathcal{G}
is a constitutive function with
\mathcal{G}\leq 0
. Now, for the satisfaction of the inequality, the second term also has to be positive. Hence, we define
\bm{q}=-k\,\nabla T
, where
k
is the thermal conductivity.

2.5
Specialized constitutive relations

We assume the Helmholtz free energy of the system as

\psi(\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}},s,\nabla s,T)=\psi_{\text{elas}}(\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}})+\psi_{\text{frac}}(s,\nabla s)+\psi_{\text{ther}}(T),

(29)

where
\psi_{\text{elas}}
is the elastic energy,
\psi_{\text{frac}}
the fracture energy and
\psi_{\text{ther}}
the thermal energy. For imposing the condition that cracks do not propagate under pure compression, we consider a decomposition of strain tensor into a deviatoric part
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{dev}}
and a volumetric part
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{vol}}
, i.e.,

\bm{\epsilon}=\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{dev}}+\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{vol}},

(30)

where

\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{dev}}=\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\bm{\epsilon};\,\,\,\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{vol}}=\mathbb{P}_{\text{vol}}\bm{\epsilon}.

(31)

In Eq. (
31
),
\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\text{vol}}
projects the strain tensor onto its deviatoric and volumetric parts, respectively. For the assumption of no damage under pure compression, the elastic energy can be expressed as

\psi_{\text{elas}}(\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}},s)=g(s)\,\psi_{\text{elas}}^{+}+\psi_{\text{elas}}^{-}.

(32)

In Eq. (
32

\psi_{\text{elas}}^{+}=\frac{1}{2}g(s)\left(\langle p\rangle_{+}\bm{I}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{vol}}+\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\mathbb{C}\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{dev}}\right),

(33)

\psi_{\text{elas}}^{-}=\frac{1}{2}\langle p\rangle_{-}\bm{I}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{dev}},

(34)

where

is the undamaged fourth order elasticity tensor,
\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{vol}}=\mathbb{P}_{\text{vol}}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}

and g(s) is a degradation function considered as
s^{2}
in this work,
\langle p\rangle_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\left(p+\lvert p\rvert\right)
and
\langle p\rangle_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left(p-\lvert p\rvert\right)
,
p=\frac{1}{3}\bm{I}:\mathbb{P}_{\text{vol}}\mathbb{C}\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}
. The fracture energy
\psi_{\text{frac}}(\nabla s,s)
may be postulated as

\psi_{\text{frac}}\left(\nabla s,s\right)=G_{c}\biggl(\frac{\left(1-s\right)^{2}}{2l_{s}}+\frac{l_{s}}{2}\nabla s\cdot\nabla s\biggr),

(35)

where
G_{c}
is the critical energy release rate and

is the phase-field length scale parameter.
Recognizing the fact that the heat flux may get affected by crack generation we express the thermal conductivity

k=g(s)\,k_{0},

(36)

where

is the undamaged thermal conductivity and the expression given by Eq. (

25
) and (
26

\bm{\sigma}=g(s)\left(\langle p\rangle_{+}\bm{I}+\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\mathbb{C}\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}\right)+\langle p\rangle_{-}\bm{I},

(37)

\bm{\xi}=G_{c}l_{s}\nabla s,

(38)

\begin{split}\pi^{en}=g^{\prime}(s)\left(\langle p\rangle_{+}\bm{I}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{vol}}+\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\mathbb{C}\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{dev}}\right)-\\
\frac{G_{c}}{l_{s}}(1-s).\end{split}

(39)

Using the expression for the above thermodynamic fluxes in Eq. (

) and Eq. (
14
), one can arrive at the governing equation for displacement field and phase-field, respectively.

2.6
Temperature evolution

To derive the temperature evolution equation, we are following the approach similar to the one adopted by Rahaman
et al.

[
47
]
in a small deformation set-up with the presence of fracture energy instead of energy contribution due to plasticity. Employing Eq. (
27
) and Eq. (
29
) in the relation
e=\psi+T\eta
, we get that

e=\psi_{\text{elas}}+\psi_{\text{frac}}+\psi_{\text{ther}}-T\frac{d\psi_{\text{ther}}}{dT}.

(40)

For the case of no mechanical strain, differentiation of Eq. (
40
) leads to

de=-T\frac{d^{2}\psi_{\text{ther}}}{dT^{2}}dT=\rho\,c\,dT,

(41)

where
c
is the specific heat. Employing Eq. (
40
) and Eq. (
41
), we get that

\dot{e}=\dot{\psi}_{\text{elas}}+\dot{\psi}_{\text{frac}}+\rho c\dot{T}.

(42)

Expressing
\dot{\psi}_{\text{elas}}
and
\dot{\psi}_{\text{frac}}
in their unspecialized form as

\dot{\psi}_{\text{elas}}=\bm{\sigma}:\dot{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\text{elas}},

(43)

\dot{\psi}_{\text{frac}}=\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{s}+\pi^{\text{en}}\dot{s},

(44)

and using Eq. (
17
) in Eq. (
42
) we get the temperature evolution equation as given by

\rho c\dot{T}=-\nabla\cdot\bm{q}+Q_{\text{h}}+\pi^{\text{dis}}\dot{s}.

(45)

As can be seen from Eq. (
45
), our proposed formulation provides a thermodynamically consistent accommodation of dissipative effects such as temperature change due to viscous damping through the
\pi^{\text{dis}}\dot{s}
in Eq. (
45
).

2.7
Boundary value problem and the strong form

Using the expressions derived in the previous section, one may write the strong form of the governing PDEs as a boundary value problem and derive the corresponding weak form of the governing PDEs for the finite element formulation of the proposed phase-field model. One can express the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
in terms of kinematic variables as
\mathbb{C}_{\text{mod}}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}
by defining
\mathbb{C}_{\text{mod}}
as

\mathbb{C}_{\text{mod}}=\begin{cases}g(s)\mathbb{C},\,\,\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,p\geq 0&\\
g(s)\left(\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\mathbb{C}\right)+\mathbb{P}_{\text{vol}}\mathbb{C},\,\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,p<0.\end{cases}

(46)

Using
\bm{\sigma}=\mathbb{C}_{\text{mod}}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}
, Eq. (
11
) can be re-written as

\nabla\cdot\biggl(\mathbb{C}_{\text{mod}}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}\biggr)+\bm{b}=\bm{0}.

(47)

Similarly, using Eq. (
14
), (
38
) and (
39
), we can re-write the force balance corresponding to phase field as

\begin{split}\nabla\cdot(G_{c}l_{s}\nabla s)-g^{\prime}(s)\left(\langle p\rangle_{+}\bm{I}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{vol}}+\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\mathbb{C}\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{dev}}\right)\\
+\frac{G_{c}}{l_{s}}\left(1-s\right)+\mathcal{G}\dot{s}=0.\end{split}

(48)

As it can be seen from the above equation, any dissipative effect can easily be incorporated in to the equation through the term
\mathcal{G}\dot{s}
, bypassing the empirical route by Miehe
et al.

[
27
]
. However, in the numerical examples of the present work, we have not considered any energy dissipation and implementation of this model for problems with dissipative effect can be considered as a future scope of the current work. To account for the irreversiblity condition on damage, a history function
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{H}(f)=\text{max}_{\tau\in[0,t]}f(\tau)
for any input argument
f
, of the so-called positive part of elastic energy
\mathcal{E}=\psi^{+}_{\text{elas}}(\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}})
is employed
[
48
]
. Using the history function
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{E})
, Eq. (
48
) may be expressed as

\nabla\cdot\biggl(G_{c}l_{s}\nabla s\biggr)-g^{\prime}(s)\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{E})+\frac{G_{c}}{l_{s}}\left(1-s\right)=0,

(49)

where

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{E})=\langle p\rangle_{+}\bm{I}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{vol}}+\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\mathbb{C}\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}:\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}_{\text{dev}}.

(50)

For
\pi^{\text{dis}}=0
and
\bm{q}=-k\,\nabla T
, the temperature evolution equation given by Eq. (
45
) can be re-written as

\rho c\dot{T}-\nabla\cdot(k\,\nabla T)=Q_{\text{h}}.

(51)

The governing PDEs (
47
), (
49
) and (
51
) are subjected to boundary conditions, such as prescribed displacement
\bm{u}=\bar{\bm{u}}
, prescribed temperature
T=\bar{T}
and applied traction
\bm{t}(\bm{n})=\bar{\bm{t}}(\bm{n})
on
\partial\Omega_{u}
,
\partial\Omega_{T}
,
\partial\Omega_{t}
, respectively. Equations (
47
), (
49
), and (
51
) together with the boundary conditions are called the strong form of the governing PDEs. For instance, in absence of body force i.e.
\bm{b}=\bf{0}
, one possible strong form can be given as

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}={\bf{0}}\,\,\,\,\,\left(\text{where}\,\,\bm{\sigma}=\mathbb{C}_{\text{mod}}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{\text{elas}}\right)\,\,\,\,\text{in}\,\,\,\,\Omega,

(52a)

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\bar{\bm{u}}\,\,\,\,\text{on}\,\,\,\,\partial\Omega_{u},

(52b)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}\,\bm{n}=\bm{0}\,\,\,\,\text{on}\,\,\,\,\partial\Omega\backslash\partial\Omega_{u},

(52c)

corresponding to the macro-force balance,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\biggl(G_{c}l_{s}\nabla s\biggr)-2s\,\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{E})+\frac{G_{c}}{l_{s}}\left(1-s\right)=0\,\,\,\,\text{in}\,\,\,\,\Omega,

(53a)

\displaystyle\nabla s\cdot\bm{n}=0\,\,\,\,\text{on}\,\,\,\,\partial\Omega\backslash\partial\Omega_{u},

(53b)

corresponding to the micro-force balance, and

\displaystyle\rho\,c\,\dot{T}-\nabla\cdot(k\,\nabla T)=Q_{\text{h}}\,\,\,\,\text{in}\,\,\,\,\Omega,

(54a)

\displaystyle{T}=\bar{{T}}\,\,\,\,\text{on}\,\,\,\,\partial\Omega_{T},

(54b)

\displaystyle\bm{q}\cdot\bm{n}=0\,\,\,\,\text{on}\,\,\,\,\partial\Omega\backslash\partial\Omega_{T},

(54c)

corresponding to the temperature evolution. Considering isotropic materials for the present study, we express the components of the fourth order elasticity tensor as
\mathbb{C}_{ijkl}=\lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\mu(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})
, where
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lamé parameters and
\delta
denotes the Kronecker delta. The Lamé parameters are related to Young’s modulus
E
and Poisson’s ratio
\nu
by
\lambda=E\nu/\left((1+\nu)(1-2\nu)\right)
and
\mu=E/\left(2(1+\nu)\right)
.

The weak form and an outline for finite element implementation in Julia

In this section, we have derived the weak form for the strong form given by equations (
52
), (
53
) and (
54
) and outlined the finite element formulation through a numerical example. Let
\bm{v}
,
\phi
and
w
be the test functions corresponding to displacement
\bm{u}
, phase-field
s
, and temperature field
T
, respectively. The trial spaces with the given displacement and temperature boundary condition may be given by

H_{u}=\{\bm{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d};\;\bm{u}=\bar{\bm{u}}\;\text{on}\;\partial\Omega_{u}\},

(55)

H_{s}=\{s\in H^{1}(\Omega)\},

(56)

H_{T}=\{T\in H^{1}(\Omega);\;T=\bar{T}\;\text{on}\;\partial\Omega_{T}\},

(57)

where
\bar{\bm{u}}
and
\bar{T}
are prescribed displacement and temperature on
\partial\Omega_{u}
and
\partial\Omega_{T}
, respectively, and
d
corresponds to the dimension of displacement vector. The test spaces may be defined as

V_{u}=\{\bm{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d};\;\bm{v}=0\;\text{on}\;\partial\Omega_{u}\},

(58)

V_{s}=\{\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\},

(59)

V_{T}=\{w\in H^{1}(\Omega);\;w=0\;\text{on}\;\partial\Omega_{T}\}.

(60)

One can obtain the following weak form: Find
\bm{u}\in H_{u}
,
s\in H_{s}
and
T\in H_{T}
such that for all
\bm{v}\in V_{u}
,
\phi\in V_{s}
and
w\in V_{T}

\displaystyle a_{\text{Disp}}((\bm{u},T),(\bm{v},w))=b_{\text{Disp}}(\bm{v},w),

(61a)

\displaystyle a_{\text{PF}}(s,\phi)=b_{\text{PF}}(\phi)

(61b)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}a_{\text{Disp}}((\bm{u},T),(\bm{v},w))=\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{v}):\bm{\sigma}_{T}(\bm{u},T)+w\,\rho\,c\,\dot{T}\\
+\nabla w\cdot(k\,\nabla T))\,d{\Omega},\end{split}

(62a)

\displaystyle b(\bm{v},w)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{v}):\bm{\sigma}_{F}+Q_{\text{h}}\right)\,d{\Omega},

(62b)

\displaystyle a_{\text{PF}}(s,\phi)=\int_{\Omega}\bigl(G_{c}\,l_{s}\,\nabla s\cdot\nabla\phi+2s\,\phi\,\mathcal{H}+\frac{G_{c}}{l_{s}}\,s\,\phi\bigr)\,d\Omega,

(62c)

\displaystyle b_{\text{PF}}(\phi)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{G_{c}}{l_{s}}\phi d{\Omega},

(62d)

with the definitions of

\bm{\sigma}_{T}(\bm{u},T)
and
\bm{\sigma}_{F}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{T}(\bm{u},T)=g(s)\left(\langle p\rangle_{+}\bm{I}+\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\mathbb{C}(\bm{\epsilon}-\alpha\bm{I}T)\right)+\langle p\rangle_{-}\bm{I},

(63a)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{F}=-g(s)\left(\langle p\rangle_{+}\bm{I}+\mathbb{P}_{\text{dev}}\mathbb{C}\alpha\bm{I}T_{0}\right)+\langle p\rangle_{-}\bm{I}.

(63b)

4
Numerical simulations

In this section, we have considered a few standard problems available in the literature for validating our proposed phase-field model and its Julia implementation. The validation problems include cruciform shaped material subjected to different boundary conditions, single edge notched specimen under tension, bi-material beam subjected to reduction of temperature, and a ceramic beam quenched to lower temperature. We have used constant strain triangle (CST) elements for finite element solutions of the problems. The simulation results show the capability of the proposed model to reproduce the results available in the literature.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we have developed a thermodynamically consistent phase-field model for brittle fracture under thermo-mechanical loading. We have provided an open-source implementation of the developed model using the finite element toolbox, Gridap in Julia. Our proposed model provides an equation for temperature evolution that can account for the effects of dissipative forces such as viscous damping whenever needed. We have validated the proposed model and its implementation against a few representative examples available in the literature. We have outlined all the steps to implement our proposed model in Julia for simulating fracture in a single edge notched plate that one can easily modify to generate results for other thermo-mechanical fracture problems. Numerical results for a cruciform-shaped plate show the capability of our proposed model to reproduce the behavior under steady-state conditions. We have reproduced the effect of thermo-mechanical coupling on the global response (load-displacement curve) for a single edge notched plate under tension. The proposed model shows excellent agreement with the experimentally observed crack paths for a bi-material beam and the quenching test.

Our proposed implementation is available with an open-source license. Hence, it eliminates the technical barrier for practitioners and researchers interested in exploring the phase-field model for solving a wide range of thermo-mechanical brittle fracture problems. Moreover, our proposed implementation will expose the users to many built-in packages of Julia that may be useful for researchers who want to extend the proposed implementation for the case of ductile fracture or other applications. The proposed multifield solution scheme can be implemented very easily for other coupled problems with the help of Gridap. Most importantly, the availability of an efficient and compact open-source code will provide better flexibility to the researchers and will establish a high standard for the research work.
