On subharmonic and entire functions of small order: after Kjellberg Thanks: 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 30D15, secondary 30F45, 31A05. Both authors were supported by the EPSRC grant EP/R010560/1.
P. J. Rippon, G. M. Stallard

Abstract
. We give a general method for constructing examples of transcendental entire functions of given small order, which allows precise control over the size and shape of the set where the minimum modulus of the function is relatively large. Our method involves developing a new technique to give an upper bound for the growth of a positive harmonic function defined in a certain type of multiply connected domain, giving a sharp estimate for the growth in many cases.



1.
Introduction

This paper concerns transcendental entire functions of small order. Such functions have been studied extensively in classical complex analysis, ever since Wiman
[
27
]
observed that such functions have properties which, in some ways, resemble those of polynomials. Subsequently, powerful results such as the version of the cos
\pi\rho
theorem due to Barry
[
2
]
showed that, for such functions, the minimum modulus of the function on many circles centred at the origin is comparable in size to the maximum modulus of the function.

More recently, these properties have led to functions of small order playing a key role in two major conjectures in complex dynamics: Baker’s conjecture explicitly concerns such functions (see
[
20
]
for recent progress on this conjecture) and they were also shown (in
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
) to have an unexpected link with Eremenko’s conjecture
[
11
]
, one of the main drivers of research in transcendental dynamics, arising from the fact that for functions of small order the escaping set of the function is often connected; see
[
21
]
and
[
25
]
.

In this paper we give a very general method for constructing examples of functions of given small order, including order 0, which allows precise control over the size and shape of the set where the minimum modulus of the function is relatively large. The original motivation for this work was related to further progress on Eremenko’s conjecture, which we report on in forthcoming work
[
22
]
, but our new results here have wider applications.

For a transcendental entire function
f
the
maximum modulus
and
minimum modulus
of
f
are denoted by

M(r)=M(r,f)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|\quad\text{and}\quad m(r)=m(r,f)=\min_{|z|=r}|f(z)|,

respectively. The
order
of
f
is

\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log M(r)}{\log r}

and we say that a function has
small order
if it has order less than 1/2. Functions of order 1/2,
minimal type
, that is, functions of order 1/2 with
\log M(r)=o(r^{1/2})
as
r\to\infty
, are sometimes included in this class, since they also have many circles on which the minimum modulus is relatively large (though far fewer such circles than for functions of order less than
1/2
).

Here we give a considerable generalisation of Kjellberg’s construction
[
17
]
of transcendental entire functions with orders in the range
(0,1/2)
, which he used to show that various theorems about the minimum modulus of transcendental entire functions of order less that 1/2 are in a certain sense best possible.

Kjellberg’s construction approximates continuous subharmonic functions with specified properties by functions of the form
\log|f|
, where
f
is a transcendental entire function, with similar properties. His construction is in two stages:

1.

a continuous subharmonic function
u
with the required properties is specified by using a positive harmonic function defined on a domain whose complement is a union of radial slits, on which
u
vanishes;

2.

the Riesz measure of
u
is discretised to produce an entire function
f
such that
\log|f|
is close to
u
away from the zeros of
f
.

In Kjellberg’s original construction, the radial slits were chosen to lie on a ray from the origin in such a way that the union of the slits is invariant under a scaling of the plane. This invariance was a key property to allow various parts of his reasoning to succeed.

Here we give a method which allows the slits to be chosen much more flexibly. This enables us to construct examples of entire functions with prescribed order
\rho
, for each
\rho
in the
closed
interval
[0,1/2]
, which also have bounded minimum modulus on as large a set as possible given the order. To achieve the necessary control, we introduce a new technique for estimating the growth of certain positive harmonic functions from above; this technique may well have applications beyond our current purpose.

We introduce a class of subharmonic functions named
{\mathcal{K}}
, after Kjellberg.

Definition 1.1
.

A subharmonic function
u
is in the class
{\mathcal{K}}
if
u
is continuous in
{\mathbb{C}}
and positive harmonic in
D={\mathbb{C}}\setminus E
, where
E\subset(-\infty,0]
is a closed set on which
u
vanishes. We assume that each point of
E
is regular for the Dirichlet problem in
D
.

We denote by
u_{\alpha,\beta}
the function in
{\mathcal{K}}
corresponding to the set

E=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}[-\alpha\beta^{n},-\beta^{n}],

where
1<\alpha<\beta
, and
u_{\alpha,\beta}(1)=1
.

Remarks
1. For each closed subset
E
of the negative real axis, there is exactly one corresponding function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
, up to positive scalar multiples, by a result of Benedicks
[
7
, Theorem 4]
, and such a
u
is unbounded and symmetric with respect to the real axis.

2. The functions
u_{\alpha,\beta}
correspond to the functions considered by Kjellberg in his original construction
[
17
, Chapter 3]
. His set
E
was of the form
E=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}[\beta^{n},\alpha\beta^{n}]
, where
1<\alpha<\beta
, whereas for us it is on balance more convenient to have the set
E
contained in the negative real axis.

To state our results, we recall that for a set
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
and
r>1
, the
upper logarithmic density
of
S
is

\overline{\Lambda}(S)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log r}\int_{S\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t},

and the
lower logarithmic density
of
S
is

\underline{\Lambda}(S)=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log r}\int_{S\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t}.

When
\overline{\Lambda}(S)=\underline{\Lambda}(S)
we speak of the
logarithmic density
of
S
, denoted by
\Lambda(S)
.

We also define, for a continuous subharmonic function
u
in
{\mathbb{C}}
,

A(r)=A(r,u)=\min_{|z|=r}u(z)\quad\text{and}\quad B(r)=B(r,u)=\max_{|z|=r}u(z),

and the
order
and
lower order
of
u
,

\rho(u)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log B(r)}{\log r}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda(u)=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log B(r)}{\log r},

respectively.

First, we give some basic properties of
all
functions in the class
{\mathcal{K}}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
, with
E
the corresponding closed subset of the negative real axis. Then
u
has the following properties.

(a)

Monotonicity properties:
for all
r>0
,

u(re^{i\theta})\;\;\text{is decreasing as a function of }\theta,\text{ for }0\leq\theta\leq\pi,

so, in particular,
B(r,u)=u(r)
and
A(r,u)=u(-r)
for all
r>0
. Also,

\frac{u(r)}{r^{1/2}}=\frac{B(r)}{r^{1/2}}\;\;\text{is decreasing for }r>0,

so, in particular,
\rho(u)\leq 1/2
.

(b)

Bounds for order and lower order:

\rho(u)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})\quad{\text{a}nd}\quad\lambda(u)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\underline{\Lambda}(E^{*}),

where
E^{*}=\{|x|:x\in E\}
.

It is natural to ask whether equality holds for the lower bounds in part (b). For the functions
u_{\alpha,\beta}
,
1<\alpha<\beta
, Kjellberg proved that

\frac{\log\alpha}{2\log\beta}\leq\rho(u_{\alpha,\beta})=\lambda(u_{\alpha,\beta})<\frac{1}{2}.

Here the lower bound is the one given by Theorem
1.2
, part (b) and Kjellberg showed that equality does not hold in general; see the discussion below, after Theorem
1.3
. He obtained the strict upper bound of
1/2
by using the invariant nature of his set
E
; see Section
6
, Remark 2 for a proof of this upper bound for the functions
u_{\alpha,\beta}
.

In our main result, which follows, we show that for many functions
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
, corresponding to sets
E
that are unions of closed intervals, the order and lower order of
u
can be expressed explicitly in terms of the geometric properties of the set
E
. We do this by developing a new technique to give upper bounds for the growth of positive harmonic functions defined in multiply connected domains, using a result about the relationship between the Harnack metric and the hyperbolic metric in such a domain
[
16
,
5
]
, together with the Beardon–Pommerenke estimate for the density of the hyperbolic metric
[
6
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
and

E=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}[-b_{n},-a_{n}],

where
0\leq a_{0}<b_{0}<a_{1}<b_{1}<\cdots,
and
a_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. If

(1.1)

a_{n}^{1/n}\to\infty\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

then

(1.2)

\rho(u)=\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})\quad{\text{a}nd}\quad\lambda(u)=\tfrac{1}{2}\underline{\Lambda}(E^{*}).

We note that some condition such as (
1.1
) is essential here. Indeed, as mentioned earlier, Kjellberg showed that the functions
u_{\alpha,\beta}\in{\mathcal{K}}
do not in general satisfy the identities in (
1.2
). He did this by proving that if the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
tend to 1 while
\frac{1}{2}\log\alpha/\log\beta
remains constant (so the intervals in the set
E
and their complementary intervals become ever slimmer while the logarithmic density of
E^{*}
remains fixed), then the order of
u_{\alpha,\beta}
must tend to 1/2.

By using a precise harmonic measure estimate in multiply connected domains due to Sodin
[
26
]
, we can construct an even more extreme example to demonstrate this phenomenon.

Example 1.4
.

There exists a function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
with the set
E
of the form

E=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}[-b_{n},-a_{n}],

where
0\leq a_{0}<b_{0}<a_{1}<b_{1}<\cdots,
and
a_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, such that
\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})=0
,

\rho(u)=\lambda(u)=1/2\quad\text{and moreover}\quad\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{u(r)}{r^{1/2}}>0.

In forthcoming work
[
22
]
we will use the results in this paper to construct entire functions of order 1/2, minimal type, with dynamically interesting properties related to their minimum modulus. Our next theorem is useful in any situation where we need to construct examples of order 1/2, minimal type.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
, with
E
the corresponding closed subset of the negative real axis. If

E^{c}\supset\bigcup_{n\geq 0}(-d_{n},-c_{n}),

where
0\leq c_{0}<d_{0}<c_{1}<d_{1}<\cdots,
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}d_{n}/c_{n}>1
, then

\frac{u(r)}{r^{1/2}}\to 0\;\;\text{as }r\to\infty.

Our final theorem is the result needed in the second stage of Kjellberg’s process, which shows how to approximate a function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
by
\log|f|
, where
f
is entire. This generalises the result given by Kjellberg in
[
17
, Chapter 4]
for his particular type of set
E
. Due to the much greater generality of the set
E
considered here, the proof is significantly more delicate, so we include full details.

Theorem 1.6
.

Suppose that
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
and

E=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}[-b_{n},-a_{n}],

where
0\leq a_{0}<b_{0}<a_{1}<b_{1}<\cdots,
and
a_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Put

D_{1}={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{z:\text{{\rm dist}}(z,E)\leq 1\}.

Then there exists a transcendental entire function
f
with only negative zeros, all lying in the set
E
, such that

(1.3)

\log|f(z)|-u(z)=O\left(\log|z|\right)\;\;\text{as }z\to\infty,\;\;\text{for }z\in D_{1}.

Moreover, if we also have

(1.4)

b_{n}/a_{n}\geq d>1,\;\;\text{for }n\geq 0,

then there exists
R=R(u)>0
such that

(1.5)

\log|f(z)|\leq u(z)+4\log|z|,\;\;\text{for }|z|\geq R.

Since the work of Kjellberg there have been many results on the approximation of subharmonic functions by logarithms of moduli of entire functions; see, for example,
[
14
, Chapter 10]
for subharmonic functions whose Riesz measure lies on a finite number of unbounded curves,
[
28
]
and
[
10
]
for functions subharmonic in
\mathbb{C}
of finite order, and
[
19
]
and
[
12
]
for functions subharmonic in
\mathbb{C}
of infinite order. These works all give estimates of the form (
1.3
) either outside certain exceptional sets or on average in a certain sense, with various error bounds, but we are not aware of earlier results that provide the type of control of the entire function
f
given in (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) simultaneously.

Indeed, the two estimates in Theorem
1.6
enable us to use any subharmonic function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
to obtain an entire function
f
with the same order, lower order and type class as
u
, and also with the property that
\log|f|
is uniformly bounded by
u
, provided (
1.4
) holds.

Finally, we recall that one aim of Kjellberg’s work in
[
17
]
was to show that a certain density estimate appearing in an early version of the
\cos\pi\rho
theorem is best possible. Recall the strong version of the
\cos\pi\rho
theorem due to Barry (see
[
2
]
or
[
14
, Theorem 6.13]
), which states that if
u
is a non-constant subharmonic function of order
\rho\in[0,1)
and
\rho<\alpha<1
, then

(1.6)

\underline{\Lambda}(\{r:A(r,u)>\cos(\pi\alpha)B(r,u)\})\geq 1-\rho/\alpha.

Kjellberg’s examples in
[
17
]
show that if
0\leq\rho<\alpha=1/2
, then the logarithmic density of the set in (
1.6
) can be arbitrarily close to the quantity
1-\rho/\alpha=1-2\rho
, thus demonstrating that the inequality in (
1.6
) for the lower logarithmic density is best possible in the case
\alpha=1/2
.

Theorem
1.3
shows that for a given
\rho<1/2
and
\alpha=1/2
the value
1-\rho/\alpha=1-2\rho
for the logarithmic density can in fact be
attained
by a subharmonic function
u
of order
\rho
. Moreover, Theorem
1.6
allows us to use such a subharmonic function to construct an entire function with the same properties.

Corollary 1.7
.

For each
\rho
,
0\leq\rho<1/2
, there is a transcendental entire function
f
of order
\rho
such that

\underline{\Lambda}(\{r:A(r,\log|f|)>0)\})=\overline{\Lambda}(\{r:A(r,\log|f|)>0)\})=1-2\rho.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we state a number of key results that are needed in our proofs. Then we prove Theorem
1.2
in Section
3
, Theorem
1.3
in Section
4
, Example
1.4
in Section
5
, Theorem
1.5
in Section
6
, and finally Theorem
1.6
and Corollary
1.7
in Section
7
.

2.
Preliminary results

Our results depend on two entirely different techniques that will enable us to estimate the growth of functions in class
{\mathcal{K}}
from below and from above. The first is a lemma of Beurling
[
9
, page 95]
, which gives estimates of growth from below and was a key tool in
[
17
]
, and in many other papers. Recall that for any subharmonic function
u
we write

B(r,u)=\max_{|z|=r}u(z),\;\text{where }r>0.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
u
is subharmonic in
{\mathbb{C}}
,
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
, and

E(r_{1},r_{2})=\{r\in[r_{1},r_{2}]:\inf_{|z|=r}u(z)\leq 0\},

then

(2.1)

B(r_{2},u)>\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{E(r_{1},r_{2})}\frac{dt}{t}\right)B(r_{1},u).

In particular, if
E(r_{1},r_{2})=[r_{1},r_{2}]
, then

B(r_{2},u)>\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right)^{1/2}B(r_{1},u).

The second technique, which will give us estimates from above, needs more preparation; we are not aware of this technique being used previously to estimate the growth of positive harmonic functions from above in multiply connected domains.

The
Harnack metric
is defined in a domain
G
by

(2.2)

d_{G}(z_{1},z_{2})=\sup\{|\log(u(z_{2})/u(z_{1}))|:u\text{ is positive and harmonic in }G\},

where
z_{1},z_{2}\in G
. This concept was introduced by Bear
[
4
]
and named the Harnack metric by König
[
18
]
. The Harnack metric in
G
has a close relationship with the hyperbolic metric in
G
, which we denote by
\rho_{G}
. Indeed, if
G
is simply connected then these two metrics are identical, provided that
\rho_{G}
is normalised so that the hyperbolic density in the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
is
d\rho_{{\mathbb{D}}}(z)=2/(1-|z|^{2}
), or equivalently
d\rho_{{\mathbb{H}}}(z)=1/\Re(z)
in the right-half plane
{\mathbb{H}}
.

In
[
16
]
, and also
[
5
]
, the relationship between the two metrics when
G
is multiply connected was investigated and, amongst other results, the following was obtained; see
[
16
, Theorem 6]
and
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
be a domain with Harnack metric
d_{G}
. Then

d_{G}(z_{1},z_{2})\leq\rho_{G}(z_{1},z_{2}),\;\;\text{for }z_{1},z_{2}\in G.

It follows that we can make good estimates for the growth of a positive harmonic function in a domain whenever we can obtain good estimates for the hyperbolic metric in that domain. To do this we shall use the following result of Beardon and Pommerenke; see
[
6
, Theorem 1]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a domain in
{\mathbb{C}}
that omits at least two finite points. Then the hyperbolic density in
G
satisfies

d\rho_{G}(z)\leq\frac{\pi/2}{{\rm dist}\,(z,\partial G)\beta_{G}(z)},

where

\beta_{G}(z)=\inf\{\left|\log|z-a|/|b-a|\right|:a,b\in\partial G,|z-a|={\rm dist}\,(z,\partial G)\}.

Note that the constant
\pi/2
in Lemma
2.3
appears as
\pi/4
in
[
6
, Theorem 1]
due to the different normalisation of the hyperbolic density in
[
6
]
.

In our situation, where we are estimating the growth of functions in class
{\mathcal{K}}
, the domains where the positive harmonic functions are defined always have their boundaries lying entirely in the negative real axis, so we can make use of the following special case of a deep result of Weitsman; see
[
14
, Theorem 9.16]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a domain in
{\mathbb{C}}
whose complement contains at least two points, and suppose that
G
has the symmetry property that for each
r>0
the set
\{z\in G:|z|=r\}
is either a circle or is of the form
\{re^{i\theta}:|\theta|<\pi\}
. Then
d\rho_{G}(re^{i\theta})
is an increasing function of
|\theta|
for
0\leq|\theta|<\pi
.

It follows easily from Lemma
2.4
that for such a domain
G
the hyperbolic geodesic joining any two points on the positive real axis is the line segment joining those two points.

In order to prove Example
1.4
we need two further results. The first is due to Sodin
[
26
, Lemma 4]
, who sharpened an earlier result of this type due to Benedicks
[
7
]
. In this result, cap
(.)
denotes logarithmic capacity.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
Q_{z}(h)
be the open square in the complex plane with center
z
and sidelength
h>0
, and let
Q(h)=Q_{0}(h)
. Let
E\subset Q(r)\cap{\mathbb{R}}
be a closed set such that, for some
\delta\in(0,1)
and
h\in(0,r)
,

{\rm cap}\left(\frac{1}{2h}(E\cap Q_{x}(h))\right)\geq\delta,\;\;\text{for }|x|<r-h.

Then

\omega(0)\leq\frac{Ch}{r}\log(1/\delta),

where
\omega
denotes the harmonic measure in
Q(r)\setminus E
of
\partial Q(r)
, and
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Finally, we need a special case of a beautiful theorem of Kjellberg
[
14
, Theorem 6.7]
, which generalised and sharpened earlier results of Wiman
[
27
]
and Heins
[
15
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
u
be a non-constant subharmonic function in
{\mathbb{C}}
of order 1/2. Then either

\limsup_{r\to\infty}A(r,u)=\infty,

or

\lim_{r\to\infty}B(r,u)/r^{1/2}=\alpha\;\text{ as }r\to\infty,\quad\text{where }0<\alpha<\infty.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is reasonably straightforward. The first statement in part (a) follows easily from the fact that any subharmonic function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
can be represented as a potential of the form

u(z)=u(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\log|1+z/t|\,d\mu(t),

where
\mu(t)
denotes the Riesz measure of
u
in the disc
\{z:|z|\leq t\}
, which is clearly entirely supported in the set
E
; see
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
, for example.

To prove the second statement in part (a) we use the fact that the function
U(z)=u(z^{2})
is positive harmonic in the right half-plane with continuous boundary values, so it has the Poisson integral representation

(3.1)

U(z)=cx+\frac{x}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{U(it)}{|z-it|^{2}}\,dt,\;\;\text{for }x=\Re(z)>0,

where
c\geq 0
; see, for example,
[
1
, Theorem 7.26]
. It follows that
U(x)/x
is decreasing for
x\in(0,\infty)
and hence that
u(r)/r^{1/2}
is decreasing for
r>0
.

To prove part (b) we use Lemma
2.1
. Since
B(r,u)=u(r)
for
r>0
, by Theorem
1.2
, part (a), this lemma gives

u(r)>\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{E^{*}\cap(1,r)}\frac{1}{t}\,dt\right)u(1),\;\;\text{for }r>1,

and hence

\frac{\log u(r)}{\log r}>\frac{1}{2\log r}\int_{E^{*}\cap(1,r)}\frac{1}{t}\,dt+\frac{\log u(1)-\log 2}{\log r},\;\;\text{for }r>1,

from which both statements in part (b) follow immediately.

Remark
The above proof can be adapted easily to show that if the set
E
is a sufficiently ‘thick’ subset of the negative real axis, then

(3.2)

u(r)\geq cr^{1/2},\;\;\text{for }r>1,

where
c
is a positive constant. For example, suppose that the sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
satisfy
a_{0}=1
and the recurrence relations

b_{n}=2a_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad a_{n+1}=b_{n}+b^{p}_{n},\;\;\text{for }n\geq 0,

where
0<p<1
, and
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
corresponds to the set
E=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}[-b_{n},-a_{n}]
. Then Lemma
2.1
gives, for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle u(b_{n})

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{E^{*}\cap(b_{0},b_{n})}\frac{1}{t}\,dt\right)u(b_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}\right)^{1/2}u(b_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{b_{j}}{b_{j-1}+b^{p}_{j-1}}\right)^{1/2}u(b_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{b_{n}}{b_{0}}\right)^{1/2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{1+b^{p-1}_{j-1}}\right)^{1/2}u(b_{0}),

from which (
3.2
) follows, since
b_{n}\geq 2^{n}
for
n\geq 0
and
u(r)/r^{1/2}
is decreasing for
r>0
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section we give the proof of Theorem
1.3
. First, however, we give a basic technical lemma that will be needed in the proof. Here we use the notation
\log^{+}x=\max\{\log x,0\}
for
x>0
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
x_{n}>0
for
n=1,2,\ldots
, and suppose that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\to\infty\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Then

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log^{+}x_{j}=o\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\right)\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

For
x\geq X\geq e
, we have
(\log x)/x\leq(\log X)/X
. Therefore, for
\varepsilon>0
and
X\geq e
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log^{+}x_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{j}\leq X}\log^{+}x_{j}+\sum_{x_{j}>X}\log x_{j}

\displaystyle\leq n\log X+\frac{\log X}{X}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}.

Hence, by further taking
X
so large that
(\log X)/X\leq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon
and then
n
so large that
n\log X\leq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}
, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log^{+}x_{j}\leq\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j},

as required.
∎

In order to prove Theorem
1.3
, given the results of Theorem
1.2
, part (b), we need only show that, under the given hypotheses,

(4.1)

\rho(u)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda(u)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\underline{\Lambda}(E^{*}).

By Theorem
1.2
, part (a), we have

\rho(u)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log u(r)}{\log r}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda(u)=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log u(r)}{\log r}.

Therefore, by Lemma
2.2
and the definition of the Harnack metric in (
2.2
), it is sufficient to show that

(4.2)

\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\rho_{D}(1,r)}{\log r}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})\quad\text{and}\quad\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\rho_{D}(1,r)}{\log r}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\underline{\Lambda}(E^{*}),

where
D={\mathbb{C}}\setminus E^{*}
as usual.

In view of the remark after Lemma
2.4
, we have

(4.3)

\rho_{D}(1,r)=\int_{1}^{r}d\rho_{D}(t)\,dt.

Therefore, to prove (
4.2
) we need to obtain good upper estimates for the hyperbolic density
d\rho_{D}(t)
, for
t>1
.

First, we have the basic hyperbolic density estimate

(4.4)

d\rho_{D}(t)\leq d\rho_{{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]}(t)=\frac{1}{2t},\;\;\text{for }t>0,

which follows from the standard monotonicity property of the hyperbolic metric, using the fact that
D\subset{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]
, together with an evaluation of
d\rho_{{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]}(t)
, for
t>0
, by conformal mapping from the right half-plane to the cut plane.

Second, we have the Beardon–Pommerenke estimate in Lemma
2.3
. To apply this it is convenient to assume, as we may by the monotonicity property of the hyperbolic metric, that
a_{0}=0
and
b_{0}\geq 1
; that is, the first interval of
E
contains
[-1,0]
. With this assumption on
E
the closest point of
\partial D
to any positive number
t
is 0, so we can apply Lemma
2.3
to obtain the estimate

(4.5)

d\rho_{D}(t)\leq\frac{\pi/2}{t\beta_{D}(t)},\;\;\text{for }t>0,

where

\beta_{D}(t)=\inf\{\left|\log(t/|b|)\right|:b\in\partial D\},\;\;\text{for }t>0.

The Beardon-Pommerenke estimate is more effective than (
4.4
) when
t
lies well inside an interval of the form
[b_{n},a_{n+1}]
. Indeed, putting
s_{n}=\sqrt{b_{n}a_{n+1}}
,
n\geq 0
, we deduce from (
4.5
) that, for
n\geq 0
,

(4.6)

\beta_{D}(t)=\begin{cases}\log t/b_{n},&b_{n}<t\leq s_{n},\\
\log a_{n+1}/t,&s_{n}\leq t<a_{n+1}.\end{cases}

To take advantage of this better estimate, we shall apply (
4.4
) for values of
t
lying in intervals of the form

[a^{\prime}_{n},b^{\prime}_{n}],\quad\text{where }a^{\prime}_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}a_{n}\;\text{and}\;b^{\prime}_{n}=2b_{n},\;\;\text{for }n\geq 0,

and the estimate (
4.5
) in the complementary intervals.

Together with (
4.3
), this gives

(4.7)

\rho_{D}(1,r)\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{E^{\prime}\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t}+\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{(1,r)\setminus E^{\prime}}\frac{dt}{t\beta_{D}(t)},

where

E^{\prime}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}[a^{\prime}_{n},b^{\prime}_{n}].

Observe that the complementary intervals of
E^{\prime}
are of the form
(b^{\prime}_{n},a^{\prime}_{n+1})
in the cases where
b^{\prime}_{n}<a^{\prime}_{n+1}
. Also, for such
n
, we have
s_{n}\in(b^{\prime}_{n},a^{\prime}_{n+1})
.

We now consider each of the two integrals in (
4.7
) separately.

Claim 1

\frac{1}{2}\int_{E^{\prime}\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})\log r\,(1+o(1))\;\text{ as }r\to\infty.

Proof.

We note that, for
a^{\prime}_{n}\leq r\leq a^{\prime}_{n+1}
, where
n\geq 0
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\int_{E^{\prime}\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{E^{*}\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t}+n\log 2.

Claim 1 now follows from the fact that

\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log r}\int_{E^{*}\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t},

together with the fact that
n=o(\log r)
as
r\to\infty
, since
r\geq a_{n}/2
and
a_{n}^{1/n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, by (
1.1
).
∎

Obtaining an upper bound for the second integral requires more work.

Claim 2

\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{(1,r)\setminus E^{\prime}}\frac{dt}{t\beta_{D}(t)}=o(\log r)\;\text{ as }r\to\infty.

Proof.

We consider the case that
a^{\prime}_{n}\leq r\leq a^{\prime}_{n+1}
, where
n\geq 1
. It follows from (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{(1,a^{\prime}_{n})\setminus E^{\prime}}\frac{dt}{t\beta_{D}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
b^{\prime}_{j}<a^{\prime}_{j+1}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\left(\int_{b^{\prime}_{j}}^{s_{j}}\frac{dt}{t\log t/b_{j}}+\int_{s_{j}}^{a^{\prime}_{j+1}}\frac{dt}{t\log a_{j+1}/t}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
b^{\prime}_{j}<a^{\prime}_{j+1}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\left(\log\frac{\log s_{j}/b_{j}}{\log 2}+\log\frac{\log a_{j+1}/s_{j}}{\log 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
b^{\prime}_{j}<a^{\prime}_{j+1}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\log\frac{\log a_{j+1}/s_{j}}{\log 2}

\displaystyle<\pi\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\log^{+}\log a_{j+1}/a_{j},

since
s_{j}/b_{j}=a_{j+1}/s_{j}=a^{1/2}_{j+1}/b^{1/2}_{j}\leq a^{1/2}_{j+1}/a^{1/2}_{j}
.

In view of condition (
1.1
), we can now apply Lemma
4.1
with
x_{j}=\log a_{j+1}/a_{j}
to give

\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{(1,a^{\prime}_{n})\setminus E^{\prime}}\frac{dt}{t\beta_{D}(t)}=o\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\log a_{j+1}/a_{j}\right)=o(\log a_{n})=o(\log r)\;\text{ as }r\to\infty.

This proves Claim 2 in the case that
a^{\prime}_{n}\leq r\leq b^{\prime}_{n}
.

It remains to show that, if
b^{\prime}_{n}\leq r\leq a^{\prime}_{n+1}
, then

(4.8)

\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{b^{\prime}_{n}}^{r}\frac{dt}{t\beta_{D}(t)}=o(\log r)\;\text{ as }r\to\infty.

We split this into two cases. First, if
b^{\prime}_{n}\leq r\leq s_{n}
, then it follows from (
4.6
) that

(4.9)

\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{b^{\prime}_{n}}^{r}\frac{dt}{t\beta_{D}(t)}=\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{b^{\prime}_{n}}^{r}\frac{dt}{t\log t/b_{n}}=\frac{\pi}{2}\log\frac{\log r/b_{n}}{\log 2}=o(\log r)\;\text{ as }r\to\infty.

Second, if
s_{n}<r\leq a^{\prime}_{n+1}
, then it follows from (
4.6
) together with (
4.9
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{b^{\prime}_{n}}^{r}\frac{dt}{t\beta_{D}(t)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{b^{\prime}_{n}}^{s_{n}}\frac{dt}{t\log t/b_{n}}+\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{s_{n}}^{a^{\prime}_{n+1}}\frac{dt}{t\log a_{n+1}/t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\log\frac{\log s_{n}/b_{n}}{\log 2}+\frac{\pi}{2}\log\frac{\log a_{n+1}/s_{n}}{\log 2}

\displaystyle=\pi\log\frac{\log s_{n}/b_{n}}{\log 2}<\pi\log\frac{\log r}{\log 2}=o(\log r)\;\text{ as }r\to\infty.

Together with (
4.9
), this shows that (
4.8
) is true. This completes the proof of Claim 2.
∎

It follows from Claim 1 and Claim 2 together with (
4.7
) that

\rho_{D}(1,r)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})\log r(1+o(1))\;\text{ as }r\to\infty.

The first estimate in (
4.2
) now follows.

A similar but somewhat simpler argument can be used to prove the second statement in (
4.2
). First recall that

\underline{\Lambda}(E^{*})=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log r}\int_{E^{*}\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t}.

It is easy to check that, for
n\geq 0
,

\min_{b_{n}\leq r\leq b_{n+1}}\frac{1}{\log r}\int_{E^{*}\cap(1,r)}\frac{dt}{t}

occurs at
r=a_{n+1},
so there must be a subsequence
a_{n_{k}},k=1,2,\ldots,
such that

\underline{\Lambda}(E^{*})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log a_{n_{k}}}\int_{E^{*}\cap(1,a_{n_{k}})}\frac{dt}{t}.

Hence, by similar reasoning to that used to prove Claim 1 and by Claim 2 (in the special case when
r=a_{n_{k}},k=1,2,\ldots
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\rho(1,a_{n_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{E^{*}\cap(1,a_{n_{k}})}\frac{dt}{t}+n_{k}\log 2+\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{(1,a_{n_{k}})\setminus E^{\prime}}\frac{dt}{t\beta_{D}(t)}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\underline{\Lambda}(E^{*})\log a_{n_{k}}(1+o(1))+o\left(\log a_{n_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\underline{\Lambda}(E^{*})\log a_{n_{k}}(1+o(1))\;\text{ as }k\to\infty.

The second estimate in (
4.2
) now follows.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

5.
Proof of Example
1.4

Our example is a function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
where

E=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}[-b_{n},-a_{n}],\quad\text{with }a_{n}=n,b_{n}=n+1/n,\;n=1,2,\ldots,

normalised so that
u(1)=1
. It is straightforward to check that
\overline{\Lambda}(E^{*})=0
in this case.

We shall show that, for this function,

(5.1)

u(-r)=o(1)\;\text{ as }r\to\infty.

It follows, by Barry’s theorem (see the end of Section
1
), that
u
must have order
1/2
and then, by Lemma
2.6
, that
u
cannot be of order 1/2, minimal type. Hence
\lim_{r\to\infty}u(r)/r^{1/2}>0
, by Theorem
1.2
, part (a); in particular,
u
has order and lower order
1/2
.

To prove (
5.1
), we first apply Lemma
2.5
to the part of
E
that lies in the square box of the form

Q_{z_{r}}(\tfrac{1}{2}r)=\{z:-2r<\Re z<-r:|\Im z|<\tfrac{1}{2}r\},\quad\text{where }z_{r}=-\tfrac{3}{2}r,r>1.

From now on we assume that
r>4
. Then

\max\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:-b_{n}\in Q_{z_{r}}(\tfrac{1}{2}r)\}\leq 2r,

so, for
r<a_{n}<b_{n}<2r
,

b_{n}-a_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\geq\frac{1}{2r}.

We shall apply Lemma
2.5
with
h=1
. In this case, for
|x-z_{r}|<\tfrac{1}{2}r-h
,

E\cap Q_{x}(h)\text{ contains at least one interval of }E,

so

{\rm cap}\left(\frac{1}{2h}(E\cap Q_{x}(h))\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2r}=\frac{1}{16r},\;\;\text{for }|x|<\tfrac{1}{2}r-h,

since cap
(I)\geq\frac{1}{4}|I|
for any interval
I
on the real line; see
[
23
, Corollary 9.10]
, for example. Therefore, by Lemma
2.5
, with
h=1
and
\delta=1/(16r)
, we have

(5.2)

\omega(z_{r})\leq\frac{C}{r}\log(16r),\;\;\text{for }r>4,

where
\omega
denotes the harmonic measure in
Q_{z_{r}}(\tfrac{1}{2}r)\setminus E
of
\partial Q_{z_{r}}(\frac{1}{2}r)
and
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Now note that, by Theorem
1.2
, part (a),

\max\{u(z):z\in\partial Q_{z_{r}}(\tfrac{1}{2}r)\}\leq u(3r)\leq(3r)^{1/2},\;\;\text{for }r>0.

It follows, by applying the maximum principle to
u
in
Q_{z_{r}}(\tfrac{1}{2}r)\setminus E
and using (
5.2
) that, for
r>4
,

\displaystyle u(z_{r})

\displaystyle\leq\max\{u(z):z\in\partial Q_{z_{r}}(\tfrac{1}{2}r)\}\omega(z_{r})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(3r)^{1/2}}{r}\log(16r)

\displaystyle=o(1)\;\text{ as }r\to\infty,

as required.

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

For the proof of Theorem
1.5
, we need the following result on positive harmonic functions defined in annuli; see
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
for a variation on Lemma
6.1
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
u
be positive and harmonic in
\{z:r_{1}<|z|<r_{2}\}
and suppose that
r_{1}<s_{1}\leq s_{2}<r_{2}
. Then there is a positive constant
K
depending only on
\mu:=\min\{\log(s_{1}/r_{1}),\log(r_{2}/s_{2})\}
such that

(6.1)

u(z^{\prime})\leq Ku(z),\;\;\text{for }|z^{\prime}|=|z|\in[s_{1},s_{2}].\overline{}

Proof.

The positive harmonic function
u(e^{t})
is defined in the infinite strip
S=\{t:\log r_{1}<\Re(t)<\log r_{2}\}
. We can apply Harnack’s inequality to this function in any disc of radius
\mu
whose centre lies in the rectangle

R=\{t:\log s_{1}\leq\Re(t)\leq\log s_{2},-\pi\leq\Im(t)\leq\pi\}\subset S,

to deduce that there is a positive constant
K=K(\mu)
such that

u(e^{t^{\prime}})\leq Ku(e^{t}),\;\;\text{for }t,t^{\prime}\in R,\;\Re(t^{\prime})=\Re(t)\in[\log s_{1},\log s_{2}],

and this gives (
6.1
).
∎

Theorem
1.5
states that if the function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
, with corresponding closed subset
E
of the negative real axis, satisfies

E^{c}\supset\bigcup_{n\geq 0}(-d_{n},-c_{n}),

where
0\leq c_{0}<d_{0}<c_{1}<d_{1}<\cdots,
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}d_{n}/c_{n}>1
, then

\frac{u(r)}{r^{1/2}}\to 0\;\;\text{as }r\to\infty.

We give two proofs, the first based on Lemma
2.6
and the other a direct one using the Poisson integral formula.

First proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By the hypotheses on
(c_{n})
and
(d_{n})
, we can assume that there exists
d>1
such that

(6.2)

d_{n}/c_{n}\geq d,\;\;\text{for }n\geq 0.

We will apply Lemma
6.1
with

r_{1}=c_{n},\quad s_{1}=c_{n}^{3/4}d_{n}^{1/4},\quad s_{2}=c_{n}^{1/4}d_{n}^{3/4},\quad r_{2}=d_{n}.

Then, by (
6.2
),

\frac{s_{1}}{r_{1}}=\frac{r_{2}}{s_{2}}=\left(\frac{d_{n}}{c_{n}}\right)^{1/4}\geq d^{\,1/4},\;\;\text{for }n\geq 0.

We deduce that there is a constant
K=K(d)>0
such that

(6.3)

u(r)\leq Ku(-r),\;\;\text{for }c_{n}^{3/4}d_{n}^{1/4}\leq r\leq c_{n}^{1/4}d_{n}^{3/4},\;n\geq 0,

and, in particular,
A(r)=u(-r),r>0,
is unbounded, by Theorem
1.2
, part (a).

The fact that
u(r)=B(r)=o(r^{1/2})
as
r\to\infty
now follows immediately from Lemma
2.6
and Theorem
1.2
, part (a), in the case that
\rho(u)=1/2
and is trivial if
\rho(u)<1/2
.
∎

Second proof of Theorem
1.5
.

The alternative direct argument uses the estimate

(6.4)

u(r)\geq\frac{r^{1/2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u(-s)}{s^{1/2}(s+r)}\,ds\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{1/2}}{\pi}\int_{c_{n}}^{d_{n}}\frac{u(-s)}{s^{1/2}(s+r)}\,ds,

which follows from the convergence of the integral in (
3.1
) after the change of variable
z\mapsto\sqrt{z}
; note that
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
is symmetric with respect to the real axis.

Now let
\alpha_{n}=c_{n}^{3/4}d_{n}^{1/4}
and
\beta_{n}=c_{n}^{1/4}d_{n}^{3/4}
. We deduce from (
6.4
) with
r=1
, together with (
6.2
), (
6.3
) and the fact that
u(r)/r^{1/2}
is decreasing (by Theorem
1.2
, part (a)) that

\displaystyle\pi u(1)

\displaystyle>\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{c_{n}}^{d_{n}}\frac{u(-s)}{2s^{3/2}}\,ds\geq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{\alpha_{n}}^{\beta_{n}}\frac{u(s)}{2s^{3/2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{u(\beta_{n})}{\beta_{n}^{1/2}}\log\left(\frac{\beta_{n}}{\alpha_{n}}\right)\geq\frac{\log d^{\,1/2}}{2K}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{u(\beta_{n})}{\beta_{n}^{1/2}}.

Hence
u(\beta_{n})/\beta_{n}^{1/2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, so
u(r)/r^{1/2}\to 0
as
r\to\infty
, as required.
∎

Remarks
1. The example in the remark following the proof of Theorem
1.2
and also Example
1.4
show that we cannot hope to significantly weaken the condition
\limsup_{n\to\infty}d_{n}/c_{n}>1
in Theorem
1.5
.

2. Theorem
1.5
can be used to show that the functions
u_{\alpha,\beta}
considered by Kjellberg have order strictly less than 1/2. Indeed, for these functions the gaps between the intervals in the set
E
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
1.5
, so we certainly have
u_{\alpha,\beta}(r)=o(r^{1/2})
as
r\to\infty
.

However, in view of the fact that the set
E
is invariant under scaling by
z\mapsto\beta z
and the uniqueness property of functions
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
, we have
u_{\alpha,\beta}(\beta z)=Cu_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, where
C=C(\alpha,\beta)>0
, and this property is incompatible with
u_{\alpha,\beta}
having order 1/2, minimal type.

7.
Proofs of Theorem
1.6
and Corollary
1.7

The proofs of Theorem
1.6
and Corollary
1.7
follow the structure of the reasoning in
[
17
, Chapter 4]
but require significant additional arguments due to the much greater generality of the sets
E
considered here.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Once again, we express the subharmonic function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
as a potential of the form

u(z)=u(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\log|1+z/t|\,d\mu(t),

where
\mu(t)
denotes the Riesz measure of the subharmonic function
u
in the disc
\{z:|z|\leq t\}
, clearly entirely supported in the set
E
. It is well known (see
[
13
, Chapter 3]
) that

(7.1)

\mu(r)=rI^{\prime}(r),\quad\text{where }I(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u(re^{i\theta})\,d\theta,\;\;\text{for }r\geq 0.

As noted earlier, the idea of Kjellberg’s method is to discretise the measure
\mu
and use the resulting discrete measure to construct a subharmonic function
u_{1}
which is close to
u
in much of the plane, and for which
u_{1}(z)=\log|f(z)|
, where
f
is an entire function.

Indeed, writing

u_{1}(z)=u(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\log|1+z/t|\,d[\mu(t)]=u(0)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log|1+z/x_{n}|,

where the sequence
(x_{n})
is positive and increasing, and
-x_{n}\in E
, we see that
u_{1}(z)=\log|f(z)|
, where
f
is the entire function

f(z)=C\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+z/x_{n}),\quad C=e^{u(0)}.

Now recall from the statement of Theorem
1.6
that

D_{1}={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{z:\text{{\rm dist}}(z,E)\leq 1\}.

We shall show that there exists a positive constant
R_{0}=R_{0}(u)
such that

(7.2)

|u(z)-u_{1}(z)|\leq 4\log|z|,\;\;\text{for }z\in D_{1},|z|\geq R_{0},

which gives (
1.3
).

To do this, we write

\displaystyle u(z)-u_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\log|1+z/t|\,d(\mu(t)-[\mu(t)])

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{x_{1}}+\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}\right)\log|1+z/t|\,d(\mu(t)-[\mu(t)])

(7.3)

\displaystyle=I_{1}(z)+I_{2}(z),

say, and estimate
I_{1}(z)
and
I_{2}(z)
in turn, for
z\in D_{1}
.

Since
\mu(x_{1})=1
, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\log|1+z/t|\,d\mu(t)-\log|1+z/x_{1}|

(7.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\log|t+z|\,d\mu(t)-\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\log t\,d\mu(t)-\log|1+z/x_{1}|,

provided that the two integrals in the latter expression are convergent. This is the first point at which our proof requires different reasoning from that in
[
17
]
.

To prove this convergence, we use the fact that
I(r)
, defined in (
7.1
), is a non-negative increasing convex function of
\log r
, that is,
\phi(s)=I(e^{s})=I(r),s\in{\mathbb{R}},
is a non-negative increasing convex function. Hence
rI^{\prime}(r)\log r=s\phi^{\prime}(s)\to 0
as
s\to-\infty
, by a simple argument. Therefore, by (
7.1
), we deduce that the integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\log t\,d\mu(t)

\displaystyle=[tI^{\prime}(t)\log t]^{x_{1}}_{0}-\int_{0}^{x_{1}}I^{\prime}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=x_{1}I^{\prime}(x_{1})\log x_{1}-I(x_{1})+I(0)

is convergent.

Since
|z+t|>1
for
z\in D_{1}
and
-t\in E
, we deduce that, for
z\in D_{1}
,

(7.5)

0<\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\log|t+z|\,d\mu(t)\leq\log(x_{1}+|z|)\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\,d\mu(t)=\log(x_{1}+|z|).

Hence, by (
7
) and (
7.5
), there exists
R_{1}>0
and
C_{1}>0
such that

(7.6)

\left|I_{1}(z)\right|\leq C_{1},\;\;\text{for }z\in D_{1},|z|\geq R_{1}.

Next we estimate
|I_{2}(z)|
for
z\in D_{1}
, and for this second integral we need to give considerably more detail than was given in
[
17
]
. Integrating by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}\log|1+z/t|\,d(\mu(t)-[\mu(t)])

(7.7)

\displaystyle=-\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}(\mu(t)-[\mu(t)])\,d\log|1+z/t|,

since
\mu(x_{1})=[\mu(x_{1})]
, so

(7.8)

|I_{2}(z)|\leq\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}\left|d\log|1+z/t|\right|.

Now, for
z
in the right half-plane and
t>0
, the function
t\mapsto\log|1+z/t|
,
t>0
, is decreasing (as
t
increases), so

(7.9)

\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}\left|d\log|1+z/t|\right|=-\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}d\log|1+z/t|=\log|1+z/x_{1}|.

Now we assume that
z=x+iy
lies in the left half-plane, and observe that

(7.10)

t\mapsto\log|1+z/t|\text{ is }\begin{cases}\text{decreasing for }0<t\leq|z|^{2}/|x|,\\
\text{increasing for }t\geq|z|^{2}/|x|;\end{cases}

the change from decreasing to increasing occurs at the value
t=|z|^{2}/|x|
where
1+z/t
is orthogonal to
z
. Hence, for
z
in the left half-plane with
|z|\geq x_{1}
,

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}\left|d\log|1+z/t|\right|

\displaystyle=-\int_{x_{1}}^{|z|^{2}/|x|}d\log|1+z/t|+\int^{\infty}_{|z|^{2}/|x|}d\log|1+z/t|

\displaystyle=\log|1+z/x_{1}|-2\log\left|1+z|x|/|z|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\log|1+z/x_{1}|-2\log|y|/|z|.

It follows from this estimate, and (
7.8
) and (
7.9
), that if
z\in D_{1}\setminus\{z=x+iy:x<0,|y|<1/2\}
and
|z|\geq x_{1}
, then

(7.11)

|I_{2}(z)|\leq\left|\log|1+z/x_{1}|\right|+2\log|z|+2\log 2.

Finally, suppose that
z\in D_{1}\cap\{z=x+iy:x<-b_{0},|y|<1/2\}
. Then
z
lies in a set of the form

\Omega_{n}=\{z:|z+b_{n}|>1,|z+a_{n+1}|>1,|y|<1/2\},\;\text{ where }n\geq 0,

and (
7
) can be written as

I_{2}(z)=-\int_{x_{1}}^{b_{n}}(\mu(t)-[\mu(t)])\,d\log|1+z/t|-\int_{a_{n+1}}^{\infty}(\mu(t)-[\mu(t)])\,d\log|1+z/t|,

since the measure
\mu
is supported in
E
.

Also, for
n
sufficiently large and
z\in\Omega_{n}
, we have
b_{n}<|z|^{2}/|x|<a_{n+1}
, so in this case,

\displaystyle|I_{2}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x_{1}}^{b_{n}}\left|d\log|1+z/t|\right|+\int_{a_{n+1}}^{\infty}\left|d\log|1+z/t|\right|

(7.12)

\displaystyle=\log|1+z/x_{1}|-\log\left|1+z/b_{n}\right|-\log\left|1+z/a_{n+1}\right|,

in view of (
7.10
).

Next we observe that, if
z\in\Omega_{n}
, for
n\geq 0
, then

(7.13)

|1+z/b_{n}|\geq 1/|z|\quad\text{and}\quad|1+z/a_{n+1}|\geq 1/(2|z|).

The first estimate is immediate since
|z|>|b_{n}|
and
|z+b_{n}|>1
for
z\in\Omega_{n}
. The second estimate holds because
|z||a_{n+1}+z|\geq|x|(a_{n+1}+|x|)>a_{n+1}/2
, for
z=x+iy\in\Omega_{n}
, by an elementary calculation.

Therefore, by (
7
) and (
7.13
), we deduce that (
7.11
) holds for all values of
z\in D_{1}
as long as
|z|
is large enough.

Combining (
7.6
) and (
7.11
), we deduce that there exist
R_{2}>0
and
C_{2}>0
such that

(7.14)

|u(z)-u_{1}(z)|\leq 3\log|z|+C_{2},\;\;\text{for }z\in D_{1},|z|\geq R_{2},

which gives (
7.2
). This proves the first part of Theorem
1.6
.

To prove the second part, we first claim that if (
1.4
) holds, that is,
b_{n}/a_{n}\geq d>1
for
n\geq 0
, then there exists
C_{3}>0
such that

(7.15)

u(z)\leq C_{3},\;\;\text{whenever }\text{{\rm dist}}(z,E)\leq 1.

We use Lemma
2.1
again. First, let
n
be so large that
a_{n}(1-1/\sqrt{d})>1
, and consider a general point
-s\in[-b_{n},-a_{n}]
. We have
s(1-1/\sqrt{d})>1
and
u=0
on at least one of the intervals
[-s\sqrt{d},-s]
or
[-s,-s/\sqrt{d}\,]
. Thus, if we apply Lemma
2.1
with the origin moved to
-s
and the radii
r_{1}=1
,
r_{2}=s-s/\sqrt{d}>1
, then we obtain

\max_{|z+s|\leq s-s/\sqrt{d}}u(z)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right)^{1/2}\max_{|z+s|\leq 1}u(z)=\frac{1}{2}s^{1/2}\left(1-1/\sqrt{d}\right)^{1/2}\max_{|z+s|\leq 1}u(z).

Also, by Theorem
1.2
, part (a),

u(z)\leq|z|^{1/2}u(1)\leq\left(s\sqrt{d}\right)^{1/2}u(1),\;\;\text{for }|z|\leq s\sqrt{d},

so the claim (
7.15
) follows, since
\{z:|z+s|\leq s-s/\sqrt{d}\,\}\subset\{z:|z|\leq s\sqrt{d}\,\}
.

Now let
v(z)=u_{1}(z)-(3\log|z|+C_{2})
. By (
7.14
) and (
7.15
), we deduce that

v(z)\leq u(z)\leq C_{3},

when
z
lies on the boundary of any complementary component of
D_{1}
. Since
v
is subharmonic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
, we deduce by the maximum principle that
v(z)\leq C_{3}
in each complementary component of
D_{1}
, except possibly one that contains 0, and hence there exists
R=R(u)>0
such that

(7.16)

\log|f(z)|=u_{1}(z)\leq u(z)+4\log|z|,\;\;\text{for }|z|\geq R.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.6
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.7
.

We shall assume that the required order
\rho\in(0,1/2)
and consider the function
u\in{\mathcal{K}}
with
E=\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}[-b_{n},-a_{n}]
, where

b_{n}=\exp(n^{2}/(4\rho))\quad\text{and}\quad a_{n}=b_{n}e^{-n},\;\;\text{for }n\geq 0,

and also
u(0)=1
. The proof for the case
\rho=0
us similar, but with
b_{n}=\exp(n^{3})
.

Then
a_{n}^{1/n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and it is easy to check that the logarithmic density of
E
is
2\rho
, so the order and lower order of
u
is
\rho
, by Theorem
1.3
. Also,

b_{n}/a_{n}\to\infty\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

so we can apply Theorem
1.6
to obtain an entire function of the form

f(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+z/x_{n}),

where the sequence
(x_{n})
is positive and increasing, and
-x_{n}\in E
, such that
f
has order and lower order
\rho
and

\log|f(z)|\leq u(z)+4\log|z|,\;\;\text{for }|z|\geq R,

where
R=R(u)>0
. If we then redefine
f
to be

f(z)=\prod_{n=6}^{\infty}(1+z/x_{n}),

then
f
again has order
\rho
and

\log|f(z)|\leq u(z),\;\;\text{for }|z|\geq R,

for some possibly larger
R
.

Since
u=0
on the set
E
, we deduce that
\{r:A(r,\log|f|)>0)\}
is a subset of
E^{c}\cup\{x:-R<x\leq 0\}
, and so has lower logarithmic density at most
1-2\rho
, and hence exactly
1-2\rho
, by Barry’s theorem (
1.6
), applied with
\alpha=1/2
.

The set
\{r:A(r,\log|f|)>0)\}
has upper logarithmic density at most
1-2\rho
also. Hence it has upper logarithmic density exactly
1-2\rho
by another theorem of Barry
[
3
]
, applied with
\alpha=1/2
again, which states that if
u
is a non-constant subharmonic function of lower order
\lambda\in[0,1)
and
\lambda<\alpha<1
, then

(7.17)

\overline{\Lambda}(\{r:A(r,u)>\cos(\pi\alpha)B(r,u)\})\geq 1-\lambda/\alpha.

This completes the proof of Corollary
1.7
.
∎
