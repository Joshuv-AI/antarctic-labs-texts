The spread of an epidemic: a game-theoretic approach
Sayar Karmakar, Moumanti Podder, Souvik Roy, Soumyarup Sadhukhan

Abstract
. We introduce and study a model stemming from game theory for the spread of an epidemic throughout a given population. Each agent is allowed to choose an action whose value dictates to what extent they limit their social interactions, if at all. Each of them is endowed with a certain amount of immunity such that if the viral risk/exposure is more than that they get infected. We consider a discrete-time stochastic process where, at the beginning of each epoch, a randomly chosen agent is allowed to update their action, which they do with the aim of maximizing a utility function that is a function of the state in which the process is currently in. The state itself is determined by the subset of infected agents at the beginning of that epoch, and the most recent action profile of all the agents. Our main results are concerned with the limiting distributions of both the cardinality of the subset of infected agents and the action profile as time approaches infinity, considered under various settings (such as the initial action profile we begin with, the value of each agent’s immunity etc.). We also provide some simulations to show that the final asymptotic distributions for the cardinality of infected set are almost always achieved within the first few epochs.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview of the paper

The primary motivation that fuels our work in this paper is the need to understand how an infectious disease spreads through a population comprising intelligent, pragmatically thinking individuals who decide upon their actions (such as distancing oneself from possibly infected acquaintances via voluntary confinement to one’s home) on a day-to-day basis, with the aim to maximize their respective
utility functions
. The key novelty of our work lies in being able to capture, via our model, the fact that the population we consider is made up of rational beings referred to as
agents
or
players
. We emphasize here the need for investigation in understanding the spread of a contagion through a population whose members are not just helpless entities exposed to the infection at the whim of nature alone (see §
1.2
for a brief discussion of the existing literature on models devised for studying the spread and control of epidemics, based on game theory).

Our model is firmly based on the premise of game theory, constituting a population
\displaystyle N=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
of
\displaystyle n
agents, each of whom is allowed to choose from a set
\displaystyle A=[0,1]
of available
actions
. Choosing action
\displaystyle 0
is equivalent to the agent confining themselves to their home and coming in contact with no other agent, whereas choosing action
\displaystyle 1
is tantamount to the agent going about their day as usual, with no restrictions imposed. An
action profile

\displaystyle a_{N}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
is an element of the set
\displaystyle A^{n}
, with
\displaystyle a_{i}
indicating the most recent action undertaken by agent
\displaystyle i
, for each
\displaystyle i\in N
. The agents are represented by the vertices of an undirected weighted graph, and the
interaction
between agent
\displaystyle i
and agent
\displaystyle j
, for distinct
\displaystyle i,j\in N
, is captured by the weight
\displaystyle g_{i,j}\in[0,1]
of the edge connecting the vertices
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
. We further endow agent
\displaystyle i
, for each
\displaystyle i\in N
, with an
immunity power

\displaystyle\tau(i)\in(0,1)
. We consider a discrete-time stochastic process indexed by
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the set of all non-negative integers. At the beginning of the
\displaystyle t
-th
epoch
of time, for each
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, an agent
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}
is chosen uniformly randomly out of
\displaystyle N
and permitted to update their action. The chosen agent decides upon their action by taking stock of the
state
the process is in at the beginning of that epoch, and their own
utility function
, both of which are formally defined in §
2
. We mention here that the state
\displaystyle S_{t}
of the process, at the start of epoch
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, is made up of two crucial components:

(i)

the set
\displaystyle I(S_{t})
comprising all the agents who have been infected up to and including epoch
\displaystyle t-1
,

(ii)

and the action profile
\displaystyle a_{N}(S_{t})
of the agents at the beginning of epoch
\displaystyle t
.

The process mentioned above shall, henceforth, be referred to as the
stochastic virus spread process
(SVSP). In addition, we shall consider, for some of our preliminary investigations of the SVSP, a
deterministic virus spread process
(DVSP) (see §
2
for a more formal definition) in which the sequence
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}=(\undertilde{v}_{t}:t\in\mathbb{N}_{0})
of agents is specified fully (i.e. the agent
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}
chosen to update their action at the start of epoch
\displaystyle t
, for each
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, is predetermined, and
not
random).

The principal questions we aim to answer in this work are those concerning the limiting distribution of the infected set
\displaystyle I(S_{t})
and the limiting distribution of the action profile
\displaystyle a_{N}(S_{t})
of all agents concerned, as
\displaystyle t\rightarrow\infty
, provided such limits exist. Such questions are pertinent not just theoretically, but also from a very practical perspective in that, in any country, the departments under the federal government that are tasked with overseeing the provision of healthcare for the population must be able to reliably predict the approximate proportion of citizens to get infected in the long run (i.e. when the epidemic has continued for a considerably long duration). This is necessary because such knowledge can aid in the decision of how much resources (medicines and medical equipment, hospital beds etc.) to set aside for the treatment of infected patients in the long run. The investigation of the limiting behaviour of the action profile
\displaystyle a_{N}(S_{t})
as
\displaystyle t\rightarrow\infty
goes on to reveal how, when such a limit exists, individuals in a population typically tend to behave once the epidemic has prevailed for a sufficiently long time.

In the next couple of paragraphs, we focus on citing a few of the articles out of the vast literature that concerns itself with applying the theory of mean field games to the study of the spread of an epidemic throughout a population. In

, motivated by models of epidemic control in large populations, a

In
[
22

[
26

, an agent’s decision as to whether to be socially active in the midst of an epidemic is modeled as a mean-field game with health-related costs and activity-related rewards. By considering the fully and partially observed versions of this problem, the role of information in guiding an agent’s rational decision is highlighted. In
[
25

, how the evolution of an infectious disease in a large heterogeneous population is governed by the self-interested decisions (to be socially active) of individual agents is studied based on a mean-field type optimal control model. The model is used to investigate the role of partial information on an agent’s decision-making, and study the impact of such decisions by a large number of agents on the spread of the virus in the population.

In
[
7
]
, a mean-field game model is proposed in which each of the agents chooses a dynamic strategy of making contacts, given the trade-off of gaining utility but also risking infection from additional contacts. Both the
mean-field equilibrium strategy

, which maximizes the total utility of the population, are computed and compared with each other. An additional cost is also included as an incentive to the agents to change their strategies, when computing the socially optimal strategies. The

[
11

, a mean field game model of SIR dynamics is proposed in which players choose when to get vaccinated. It is shown that this game admits a unique mean-field equilibrium that consists of vaccinating aggressively at a maximal rate for a certain amount of time and then not vaccinating, and it is shown that this equilibrium has the same structure as the vaccination strategy that minimizes the total cost. A very similar problem is studied in
[
12
]
that focuses on a virus propagation dynamics in a large population of agents, with each agent being in one of three possible states (namely, susceptible, infected and recovered) and with each agent allowed to choose when to get vaccinated. It is shown that this system admits a unique symmetric equilibrium when the number of agents goes to infinity, and that the vaccination strategy that minimizes the social cost has the same threshold structure as the mean field equilibrium, though the latter has a shorter threshold. In
[
14
]
, the newborn, non-compulsory vaccination in an SIR model with vital dynamics is studied, with the evolution of each individual modeled as a Markov chain and their decision to vaccinate aimed at optimizing a criterion depending on the time-dependent aggregate (societal) vaccination rate and the future epidemic dynamics. The existence of a Nash mean field game equilibrium among all individuals in the population is established. In
[
18
]
, techniques from the mean field game theory are used to examine whether, in an SIR model, egocentric individuals (i.e. whose actions are driven by self-interest when it comes to getting vaccinated) can reach an equilibrium with the rest of the society, and it is shown that an equilibrium exists. The individual best vaccination strategy (with as well as without discounting) is completely characterized, a comparison is made with a strategy based only on overall societal optimization, and a situation with a non-negative price of anarchy is exhibited. In
[
19
]
, individual optimal vaccination strategies in an SIR model are analyzed. It is assumed that the individuals vaccinate according to a criterion taking into account the risk of infection, the possible side effects of the vaccine and the overall epidemic course, that the vaccination capacity is limited, and that the individual discounts the future at a given positive rate. Under these assumptions, an equilibrium between the individual decisions and the epidemic evolution is shown to exist. In
[
31
]
, a model of agent-based vaccination campaign against influenza with imperfect vaccine efficacy and durability of protection is considered. The existence of a Nash equilibrium is proved and a novel numerical method is proposed to find said equilibrium. Various aspects of the model are also discussed, such as the dependence of the optimal policy on the imperfections of the vaccine, the best vaccination timing etc.

In
[
15
]
, a general mathematical formalism is introduced to study the optimal control of an epidemic via incentives to lockdown and testing, and the interplay between the government and the population, while an epidemic is spreading according to the dynamics given by a stochastic SIS model or a stochastic SIR model, is modeled as a principal–agent problem with moral hazard. Although, to limit the spread of the virus, individuals within a given population can choose to reduce interactions among themselves, this cannot be perfectly monitored by the government and it comes with certain social and monetary costs for the population. One way to mitigate such costs and encourage social distancing, lockdown etc., is to put in place an incentive policy in the form of a tax or subsidy. In addition, the government may also implement a testing policy in order to know more precisely the spread of the epidemic within the country, and to isolate infected individuals. It is verified via numerical results that if a tax policy is implemented, the individuals in the population are encouraged to significantly reduce interactions among themselves, and if the government also adjusts its testing policy, less effort is required on the part of the population to enforce social distancing, lockdown upon itself, and the epidemic is largely contained by the targeted isolation of positively-tested individuals. In
[
8
]
, a model for the evolution of sociality strategies in the presence of both a beneficial and costly contagion is investigated, and a social dilemma is identified in that the evolutionarily-stable sociality strategy is distinct from the collective optimum (i.e. the level of sociality that would be best for all individuals) – in particular, the level of social interaction in the former is greater (respectively less) than the social optimum when the good contagion spreads more (respectively less) readily than the bad contagion. Finally, we cite
[
29
]
, which provides a state-of-the-art update on recent advances in the mean field approach that can be used very effectively in analyzing a dynamical modeling framework, known as a continuous time Markov decision process, for epidemic modeling and control.

2.
Formal description of the model

Recall, from the second paragraph of §
1.1
, the brief introduction to the model we consider in this paper. Here, we formalize the model by providing mathematical definitions to the crucial quantities involved in it.

The process described in §
1.1
is said to be in the
state

\displaystyle S=(I,a_{N})
if
\displaystyle I\subseteq N
denotes the set of infected agents and
\displaystyle a_{N}
denotes the action profile at that time. Given a state
\displaystyle S
, we denote by
\displaystyle I(S)
the corresponding set of infected agents, and by
\displaystyle a_{\widehat{N}}(S)=(a_{i}(S):i\in\widehat{N})
the tuple in which
\displaystyle a_{i}(S)
represents the action of the
\displaystyle i
-th agent for all
\displaystyle i\in\widehat{N}
, for any subset
\displaystyle\widehat{N}
of
\displaystyle N
. In particular, if
\displaystyle\widehat{N}=N\setminus\{j\}
, we abbreviate the notation
\displaystyle a_{N\setminus\{j\}}(S)
by
\displaystyle a_{-j}(S)
, and for any
\displaystyle a\in A
, we denote by
\displaystyle(a\vee a_{-j}(S))
the tuple
\displaystyle(a_{1}(S),\ldots,a_{i-1}(S),a,a_{i+1}(S),\ldots,a_{n}(S))
. We denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
the set of all possible states.

The viral exposure
\displaystyle r_{i}(S)
that agent
\displaystyle i
is subjected to, when the process is in state
\displaystyle S
, is defined as

r_{i}(S)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in I\setminus\{i\}}g_{ij}a_{j}(S)}{\sum_{j\in N\setminus\{i\}}g_{ij}a_{j}(S)}\right)&\text{if }\sum_{j\in N\setminus\{i\}}g_{ij}a_{j}(S)\neq 0,\\
0&\text{if }\sum_{j\in N\setminus\{i\}}g_{ij}a_{j}(S)=0.\end{cases}

Such a definition can be motivated is as follows: the sum
\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N\setminus\{i\}}g_{ij}a_{j}(S)
equals
\displaystyle 0
if and only if, for every
\displaystyle j\in N\setminus\{i\}
, either
\displaystyle g_{i,j}=0
, thereby indicating that there is no interaction whatsoever between agent
\displaystyle i
and agent
\displaystyle j
, or else the action
\displaystyle a_{j}(S)=0
, which indicates that agent
\displaystyle j
has chosen to socially distance themselves from all other agents – in either of these two scenarios, agent
\displaystyle i
runs no risk of being infected by agent
\displaystyle j
. Therefore, the viral exposure agent
\displaystyle i
is subjected to is
\displaystyle 0
whenever
\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N\setminus\{i\}}g_{ij}a_{j}(S)=0
.

The utility function of agent
\displaystyle i
, when the process is in state
\displaystyle S
, is defined as

(2.1)

u_{i}(S)=\begin{cases}1+f(a_{i}(S))&\text{if }i\notin I(S)\text{ and }a_{i}(S)r_{i}(S)\leqslant\tau(i),\\
f(a_{i}(S))&\text{if either }i\in I(S)\text{ or }a_{i}(S)r_{i}(S)>\tau(i)\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a strictly increasing function. Intuitively, if agent
\displaystyle i
is neither already infected during the current epoch (which is indicated by the condition
\displaystyle i\notin I(S)
) nor runs the risk of being infected in the next epoch (which is indicated by the condition
\displaystyle a_{i}(S)r_{i}(S)\leqslant\tau(i)
, i.e. their action multiplied by the viral exposure they have been subjected to does not exceed their immunity power), they enjoy a ‘reward’ of amount 1 in addition to the utility
\displaystyle f(a_{i}(S))
that they receive because of their chosen action (note that the strictly increasing nature of
\displaystyle f
ensures that the more they go out in society, the more utility they get). Else, they are deprived of such a reward and must settle for the utility value
\displaystyle f(a_{i}(S))
.

We now formally describe how agent
\displaystyle i
responds if they are chosen to update their action at the beginning of an epoch when the system is in state
\displaystyle S
. We call this the
best response
by agent
\displaystyle i
at state
\displaystyle S
, denoted
\displaystyle b_{i}(S)
, and it is defined as

(2.2)

b_{i}(S)=\argmax_{a\in[0,1]}u_{i}\left(I(S),(a\vee a_{-i}(S))\right).

In words, this is the set of actions
\displaystyle a
by agent
\displaystyle i
that allow them to maximize their utility function (note that the utility function, as defined in (
2.1
), is a function of the state, and the state here constitutes
\displaystyle I(S)
as the infected set and
\displaystyle(a\vee a_{-i}(S))
as the action profile).

We now summarize the stochastic process we focus on in this paper. We denote by
\displaystyle S_{t}=(I(S_{t}),a_{N}(S_{t}))
the state of the process at the start of epoch
\displaystyle t
, for
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}
denote the agent chosen uniformly randomly out of
\displaystyle N
at the start of epoch
\displaystyle t
, and we define the infinite sequence
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}=(\undertilde{v}_{t}:t\in\mathbb{N}_{0})\in N^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. Given
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}=i
, agent
\displaystyle i
updates her action by playing the best response
\displaystyle b_{i}(S_{t})
as defined in (
2.2
). We are now at an
intermediate
state
\displaystyle\hat{S}_{t}=\left(I(S_{t}),(b_{i}(S_{t})\vee a_{-i}(S_{t}))\right)
that may be imagined to occur at the midpoint of epoch
\displaystyle t
. We now update the infected set to
\displaystyle I(S_{t+1})=I(S_{t})\cup\{j:a_{j}(\hat{S}_{t})r_{j}(\hat{S}_{t})>\tau(j)\}
, and finally, at the end of epoch
\displaystyle t
and the beginning of epoch
\displaystyle t+1
, the process is in state
\displaystyle S_{t+1}=\left(I(S_{t+1}),(b_{i}(S_{t})\vee a_{-i}(S_{t}))\right)
(which tells us that
\displaystyle a_{N}(S_{t+1})=(b_{i}(S_{t})\vee a_{-i}(S_{t}))
).

The following lemma summarizes the best response of an agent at a state depending on whether or not they are infected at that state.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle i\in N
be an agent and
\displaystyle S\in\mathcal{S}
be a state. Then,

b_{i}(S)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\in I(S),\\
1&\text{if }i\notin I(S)\text{ and }r_{i}(S)=0,\\
\min\left\{1,\frac{\tau(i)}{r_{i}(S)}\right\}&\text{if }i\notin I(S)\text{ and }r_{i}(S)\neq 0.\end{cases}

The proof of this lemma can be found in Appendix
A
. There are two key messages to take away from Lemma
2.1
. The first of these is that the best response of an agent
\displaystyle i
at any state
\displaystyle S
is unique, which is why we have, henceforth, presented
\displaystyle b_{i}(S)
as an element of
\displaystyle A
(which is more convenient than writing it as a singleton subset of
\displaystyle A
). The second is that once an agent is infected or runs no risk of becoming infected (i.e. the viral exposure is
\displaystyle 0
), they choose to go out with no restrictions imposed on their movements.

Although we alluded to it in §
1
, we recall here the definition of the deterministic virus spread process (DVSP). Given a (deterministic) agent sequence
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}
, the DVSP
\displaystyle S=(S_{t}:t\in\mathbb{N}_{0})
induced by
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}=(\undertilde{v}_{t}:t\in\mathbb{N}_{0})
, with
\displaystyle S_{t}
indicating the state of the process just before epoch
\displaystyle t
commences, is defined in a manner identical to the stochastic virus spread process (SVSP) described above, with the only difference being that, instead of choosing an agent randomly at the start of each epoch, the agent
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}
is chosen at the start of epoch
\displaystyle t
to update their action, for each
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Whenever the agent sequence
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}
is not clear from the context, we shall denote the DVSP
\displaystyle S
(induced by
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}
) by
\displaystyle S(\undertilde{v})
to emphasize its dependence on
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}
. In this case,
\displaystyle S_{t}(\undertilde{v})
will denote the state of the process at the start of epoch
\displaystyle t
.

In what follows, we make a few observations about the DVSP
\displaystyle S(\undertilde{v})
that we shall use frequently throughout the paper.

Observation 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle S
be the DVSP induced by
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}
. Then, for all
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(i)

\displaystyle I(S_{t})=I(\hat{S}_{t})
and
\displaystyle a_{N}(\hat{S}_{t})=a_{N}(S_{t+1})
,

(ii)

if
\displaystyle b_{\undertilde{v}_{t}}(S_{t})=a_{\undertilde{v}_{t}}(S_{t})
, then
\displaystyle S_{t}=\hat{S}_{t}=S_{t+1}
,

(iii)

if
\displaystyle I(S_{t})=I(S_{t+1})
, then
\displaystyle\hat{S}_{t}=S_{t+1}
.

Note here that
\displaystyle\hat{S}_{t}
indicates the intermediate state of the process at the midpoint of epoch
\displaystyle t
, for each
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Observation 2.3
.

For any fixed
\displaystyle i\in N
, if
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}\neq i
for some
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
\displaystyle a_{i}(S_{t})=a_{i}(\hat{S}_{t})=a_{i}(S_{t+1})
. By repeated applications of this observation, we are able to conclude the following: if
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}\neq i
for all
\displaystyle t\in[t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}]
with
\displaystyle t^{\prime}<t^{\prime\prime}
, then this yields
\displaystyle a_{i}(S_{t})=a_{i}(S_{t^{\prime}})
for all
\displaystyle t\in[t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}]
.

Observation 2.4
.

Since the best response of an infected agent is always
\displaystyle 1
(see Lemma
2.1
),
\displaystyle i\in I(S(t))
and
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}=i
together imply that
\displaystyle a_{i}(S_{t^{\prime}})=1
for all
\displaystyle t^{\prime}>t
.

Observation 2.5
.

Since the best response of an uninfected agent
\displaystyle i
is

b_{i}(S)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }r_{i}(S)=0,\\
\min\left\{1,\frac{\tau(i)}{r_{i}(S)}\right\}&\text{if }r_{i}(S)\neq 0,\end{cases}

by Lemma
2.1
, hence
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}=i
and
\displaystyle i\notin I(S_{t})
together imply that
\displaystyle i\notin I(S_{t+1})
as well.

Recall that our main goal in this paper is to explore the limiting behaviours of both the cardinality of the infected set of agents and the action profile of all the agents in our population. We now show that such limits are well-defined, at the very least, for a deterministic sequence of agents:

Lemma 2.6
.

The DVSP
\displaystyle S(\undertilde{v})
converges for each agent sequence
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. In other words, both
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}I(S_{t}(\undertilde{v}))
and
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}a_{N}(S_{t}(\undertilde{v}))
exist.

The proof of this lemma can be found in Appendix
A
. In view of Lemma
2.6
, we set
\displaystyle S_{\infty}(\undertilde{v})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}S_{t}(\undertilde{v})
.

The set
\displaystyle{N}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is the set of all agent-sequences indexed by
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We consider the probability space
\displaystyle(N^{\mathbb{N}_{0}},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
where
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
is the sigma-field generated by the cylindrical sets of
\displaystyle{N}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
is the uniform probability distribution.

Remark 2.7
.

Let
\displaystyle N_{\infty}
be the subset of
\displaystyle{N}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
consisting of the agent-sequences where each agent moves an infinite number of times. In other words,
\displaystyle{N}_{\infty}=\{\undertilde{v}\in{N}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}:\undertilde{v}_{t}=i\text{ for infinitely many }t,\text{ for all }i\in N\}
. It is straightforward to see that the set
\displaystyle N_{\infty}
has probability 1 under
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
, since the probability of the set
\displaystyle{N}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\setminus N_{\infty}
is
\displaystyle 0
.

In view of Remark
2.7
, for the rest of the paper, we shall work with the probability space
\displaystyle(N_{\infty},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Recall that in the stochastic virus spread process (SVSP), before each epoch commences, an agent is chosen randomly, following the discrete uniform distribution on the set
\displaystyle N
, and they are allowed to update their action by playing their best response (see (
2.2
)) to the current state. Consequently, the SVSP is a random variable
\displaystyle S
supported on the probability space
\displaystyle(N_{\infty},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.

For an agent
\displaystyle i\in N
, the random variable
\displaystyle t_{i}
is defined as follows with respect to
\displaystyle(N_{\infty},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
: for
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N_{\infty}
, we set
\displaystyle t_{i}(\undertilde{v})=l
if
\displaystyle l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is such that
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{l}=i
and
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{k}\neq i
for all
\displaystyle k<l
. Note that for any
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N_{\infty}
,
\displaystyle i\in N
, and
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
\displaystyle t\leqslant t_{i}(\undertilde{v})
, we have
\displaystyle a_{i}(S_{0})=a_{i}(S_{t}(\undertilde{v}))
. Let
\displaystyle N_{1}
be the measurable function on
\displaystyle(N_{\infty},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
that describes the (random) set of agents who had been chosen before agent 1 was chosen for the first time, that is,
\displaystyle N_{1}(\undertilde{v})=\{i\in N\mid t_{i}(\undertilde{v})<t_{1}(\undertilde{v})\}
.

We now establish that
\displaystyle|N_{1}|
follows the uniform distribution on
\displaystyle\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Lemma
2.8
will be used in the proofs of the main results of this paper.

Lemma 2.8
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|N_{1}|=l)=\frac{1}{n}
for all
\displaystyle l\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
is uniform and there are
\displaystyle n!
possible orderings of the random times
\displaystyle t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
, each ordering of
\displaystyle t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
has an equal probability of
\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}
to occur. We can choose
\displaystyle m-1
random variables from the set
\displaystyle\{t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}\}
of
\displaystyle n-1
random times in
\displaystyle\prescript{n-1\mkern-0.5mu}{}{C}_{m-1}
ways. Therefore, the number of orderings that correspond to the event
\displaystyle|\{i\in N\mid t_{i}<t_{1}\}|=m-1
is
\displaystyle\prescript{n-1\mkern-0.5mu}{}{C}_{m-1}\times(m-1)!\times(n-m)!
, and hence, the probability of the said event is
\displaystyle\frac{\prescript{n-1\mkern-0.5mu}{}{C}_{m-1}\times(m-1)!\times(n-m)!}{n!}
, which is
\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

with
\displaystyle i\neq j

We emphasize here that all results in §
3

\displaystyle g_{ij}=c

(ii)

\displaystyle m-1

\displaystyle i\in\{2,\ldots,n\}

A brief discussion is in order regarding some of the startling findings that may be deduced from the two theorems of §
3.1
. Theorem

reveals that, if we consider
any
two
different

\displaystyle J_{1}
and
\displaystyle J_{2}
of
\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(I(S_{\infty})=J_{1})
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(I(S_{\infty})=J_{2})
are the same as long as
\displaystyle J_{1}
and
\displaystyle J_{2}
have the same cardinality and either both of them contain agent
\displaystyle 1
or neither contains agent
\displaystyle 1
. We note that the number of subsets

\displaystyle N
with
\displaystyle 1\in J

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|I(S_{\infty})\right|=m\right)=n^{-1}
for
each

\displaystyle m\in[2,\alpha]
. These observations suggest a rather close resemblance that the limiting distribution of the infected set, as well as the limiting distribution of its cardinality, bears with suitably defined discrete uniform distributions. In fact, for
\displaystyle\tau\leqslant n^{-1}

\displaystyle\alpha=n
, reducing the distribution of
\displaystyle|I(S_{\infty})|
to precisely the discrete uniform distribution on
\displaystyle\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. This uniform structure is somewhat marred when
\displaystyle\tau>n^{-1}
. For example, when
\displaystyle n=5
,
\displaystyle a=0
and
\displaystyle\tau=0.25
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=1)=\frac{2}{5},\ \mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=2)=\frac{1}{5},\ \mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=3)=\frac{1}{5},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=4)=\frac{1}{5},\ \mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=5)=0,

whereas if
\displaystyle\tau=0.4
, the probability distribution changes to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=1)=\frac{3}{5},\ \mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=2)=\frac{1}{5},\ \mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=3)=\frac{1}{5},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=4)=0,\ \mathbb{P}(|I(S_{\infty})|=5)=0.

An intuitive explanation for this phenomenon is that with higher immunity, i.e. higher value of
\displaystyle\tau
, the disease is less likely to spread to the entire community, instead having a higher probability of remaining confined to the initial infected set.

Conclusions of a similar flavour can be drawn as a consequence of Theorem

. For
any
two
different
ordered tuples
\displaystyle\underaccent{\tilde}{x}
and
\displaystyle\underaccent{\tilde}{y}
that belong to the same

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(a_{N}(S_{\infty})=\underaccent{\tilde}{x})
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(a_{N}(S_{\infty})=\underaccent{\tilde}{y})

and
\displaystyle\tau<(n-1)^{-1}

\displaystyle|A_{m}|=\prescript{n-1\mkern-0.5mu}{}{C}_{m-1}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(a_{N}(S_{\infty})\in A_{m})=n^{-1}
for
every

\displaystyle m\in[\beta,\alpha]

and for every
\displaystyle m\in[1,n]
when
\displaystyle\tau<(n-1)^{-1}
. These are, once again, reminiscent of suitably defined discrete uniform distributions.

A connection may be established between Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
, for the case where
\displaystyle\tau\geqslant(n-1)^{-1}
, via the following fact whose justification has been included in the proof of Theorem

in §

has cardinality strictly less than

\displaystyle\underaccent{\tilde}{1}

\displaystyle 1

5.
Conclusion

In this article, we propose a graph-theoretic model to describe the spread of a contagious disease allowing for rational interventions from agents sitting at the nodes of the graph. The agents act based on a reasonable utility function and they may (or may not) get affected if their exposure increases. We obtain the asymptotic distribution of the cardinality of the infected set as well as that of the action profile. The results reveal several interesting patterns that exhibit proximity to uniformity, as well as results that are intuitively justifiable (such as if everyone’s immunity (
\displaystyle\tau
value) is low to begin with, then eventually the whole population gets affected). We have given an almost complete picture of how the values of
\displaystyle\tau
and
\displaystyle a
impact the final distribution of the infected set and the action profile. We also observe several fascinating phase transition phenomena in our results. Through exact enumeration of all possible sequences in which the agents are picked randomly, we also show that the empirical distributions obtained mimic the corresponding empirical distributions rather closely after only around
\displaystyle 10
epochs from the start of the process.

However, there are a number of questions that remain to be addressed that seem to be beyond the scope of this paper. We give the readers a brief overview of the questions we intend to pursue for similar or related models in the future. So far, we have been unable to obtain, theoretically at least, the limiting distributions for the case where
\displaystyle a/(n-1)<\tau<1/(n-1)
. While this is negligible for large
\displaystyle a
(keeping in mind that
\displaystyle a\in[0,1]
, so that a large value of
\displaystyle a
indicates its closeness to
\displaystyle 1
) or for large
\displaystyle n
, our simulation studies show that there are possibly only two different distributions that could lie in this space. Second, we want to relax the restriction that all agents start with the same initial action
\displaystyle a
or the same initial immunity
\displaystyle\tau
. Again, some numerical explorations revealed that for fixed
\displaystyle a
and uniform
\displaystyle\tau
or for fixed
\displaystyle\tau
and uniform
\displaystyle a
, the contagion tends to spread throughout the population, yielding a rather interesting phenomenon. However, the current mathematical tools will fail to encompass such levels of randomness and proving the occurrence of the phenomena mentioned above rigorously may require completely different mathematical machinery. Finally, we would also like to explore the situation where
\displaystyle g_{ij}
is allowed to change over time or have its own model of evolution. That would also bring significant changes to our computations and thus left for future persuasion.

\displaystyle i\in I(S_{\bar{t}})

Lemma B.2
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle I(S_{0})=\{1\}
and
\displaystyle a_{i}(S_{0})\leqslant\tau(i)
for all
\displaystyle i\in N
. Let
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N_{\infty}
and
\displaystyle\hat{t}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be such that
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{t}\neq 1
for all
\displaystyle t<\hat{t}
. Then,
\displaystyle I(S_{t})=\{1\}
for all
\displaystyle t\leqslant\hat{t}
.

Proof.

Note that if
\displaystyle\hat{t}=0
then there is nothing to show. So, assume
\displaystyle\hat{t}\geqslant 1
. We use induction to prove this. As the base case, we show that
\displaystyle I(S_{1})=\{1\}
. Let
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{0}=i
. Since
\displaystyle\hat{t}\geqslant 1
,
\displaystyle i\neq 1
. Agent
\displaystyle i
will choose her action as

b_{i}(S_{0})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }r_{i}(S_{0})=0,\\
\min\left\{1,\frac{\tau(i)}{r_{i}(S_{0})}\right\}&\text{if }r_{i}(S_{0})\neq 0.\end{cases}

If
\displaystyle r_{i}(S_{0})=0
then
\displaystyle r_{i}(\hat{S}_{0})=r_{i}(S_{0})=0
, and agent
\displaystyle i
will not get infected as
\displaystyle 1\times r_{i}(\hat{S}_{0})=0\leqslant\tau(i)
. Suppose
\displaystyle r_{i}(S_{0})>0
. Since
\displaystyle a_{i}(\hat{S}_{0})=b_{i}(S_{0})
,
\displaystyle r_{i}(\hat{S}_{0})=r_{i}(S_{0})
, and
\displaystyle b_{i}(S_{0})\leqslant\frac{\tau(i)}{r_{i}(S_{0})}
, this means agent
\displaystyle i
will not get infected at
\displaystyle t=1
. For any
\displaystyle j\notin\{1,i\}
,
\displaystyle a_{j}(\hat{S}_{0})=a_{j}(S_{0})\leqslant\tau(j)
, so, agent
\displaystyle j
will also not get infected at
\displaystyle t=1
. Thus,
\displaystyle I(S_{1})=\{1\}
. Next we introduce an induction hypothesis.

Induction Hypothesis:
Given
\displaystyle\bar{t}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
\displaystyle\hat{t}\geqslant\bar{t}>1
, we have
\displaystyle I(S_{1})=\cdots=I(S_{\bar{t}-1})=\{1\}
.

We show that
\displaystyle I(S_{\bar{t}})=\{1\}
. Let
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{\bar{t}-1}=i
. Since
\displaystyle\hat{t}\geqslant\bar{t}
, this means
\displaystyle i\neq 1
. Hence,
\displaystyle i\notin I(S_{\bar{t}-1})
. As
\displaystyle\bar{t}>1
, we have
\displaystyle I(S_{\bar{t}-2})=I(S_{\bar{t}-1})
. This together with Lemma
B.1
, implies
\displaystyle I(S_{\bar{t}})=I(S_{\bar{t}-1})=\{1\}
. Thus, by induction, we have
\displaystyle I(S_{\hat{t}})=\{1\}
. This completes the proof of the lemma. ∎

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{r_{i}(\hat{S}_{\bar{t}})}.

The following lemma provides an important property of the final action limit for both infected and uninfected agents. It shows that an infected agent will have the action limit 1 whereas any two uninfected agents will have the same action limit, that is, for
\displaystyle i,j\notin I(S_{\infty})

\displaystyle a_{i}(S_{\infty})=a_{j}(S_{\infty})

Lemma D.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N_{\infty}

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\undertilde{v}

\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N_{\infty}

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\undertilde{v}

\displaystyle k\in I(S_{\infty})

\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N_{\infty}

\displaystyle k
appears infinitely many times in
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}

\displaystyle a_{k}(S_{\infty})=1
.
Now consider
\displaystyle i,j\notin I(S_{\infty})
. Let
\displaystyle b=a_{i}(S_{\infty})
and consider
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
. This means there exists
\displaystyle t_{0}

\displaystyle a_{i}(S_{t})\geqslant b-\epsilon

\displaystyle I(S_{t_{0}})=I(S_{\infty})

\displaystyle\hat{t}
such that

(i)

\displaystyle\hat{t}>t_{0}
and
\displaystyle\undertilde{v}_{\hat{t}}=j
and

(ii)

\displaystyle\bar{t}\in[t_{o},\hat{t}]

\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N_{\infty}

\displaystyle a_{j}(S_{\hat{t}})\geqslant a_{i}(S_{\hat{t}})

The next lemma determines the common action limit of the uninfected agents.

\displaystyle\undertilde{v}\in N_{\infty}

\displaystyle a_{k}(S_{t_{0}})=1
