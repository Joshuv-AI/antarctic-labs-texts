Arturo Fredes, Jordi Vitrià

Abstract
. Causality is vital for understanding true cause-and-effect relationships between variables within predictive models, rather than relying on mere correlations, making them highly relevant in the field of Explainable AI. In an automated decision-making scenario, we can analyze the underlying data-generation process using causal inference methods, which enable us to explain a model’s decision by manipulating features and creating counterfactual examples. These counterfactuals explore hypothetical scenarios where a minimal number of factors are altered, providing end-users with valuable information on how to change their situation. However, interpreting a set of multiple counterfactuals can be challenging for end-users who are not used to analyze raw data records. In our work, we propose a novel multiple-step pipeline that uses counterfactuals and generates natural language explanations of actions that will lead to a change of outcome in classifiers of tabular data using LLMs. This pipeline was thought to guide the LLM through smaller tasks that mimic human reasoning when explaining a decision based on counterfactual cases. We conducted various experiments using a public dataset and proposed a method of closed-loop evaluation to assess the coherence of the final explanation with the counterfactuals as well as the quality of the content. Results show to be promising, although further experiments with other data sets as well as human evaluation should be carried out. The code produced for this work can be found in the following repository: https://github.com/arturofredes/LLM-4-CFs-Explanation

1.
Introduction

Figure 1.
Using LLMs to generate a natural language explanation from a set of counterfactuals, which will be more easily interpreted by end users

Machine learning systems are becoming increasingly influential in society, especially in critical decision-making areas such as healthcare, education, and justice. However, many of these models function as "black boxes"
(
Guidotti et al. 2018
)
, where inputs are transformed into outputs without transparency into the decision-making process, leading to issues with trust and interpretability for users. This gave birth to the research field of XAI (explainable Artificial Intelligence), where multiple techniques for extracting explanations for model predictions have been proposed. In this work we focus on contrastive explanations through sets of multiple counterfactual examples
(
Molnar 2019
)
, which are variants of user data with minimal changes in order to flip the prediction.

When a user is presented with a single counterfactual, extracting conclusions from it is straightforward. Nevertheless, this may not reflect the whole scope of possible actions the user can take. On the other hand, as we increase the number of counterfactuals, drawing conclusions from them becomes difficult for people who are not trained on a data analytic thinking
(
Bove et al. 2023
)
. The objective of this work is to evaluate the use of LLMs in generating clear, plain-language explanations for end users without the need for individual case analysis. We guide the LLM through the explanation generation task by breaking it into smaller processes, mimicking the steps a human would follow when explaining an event.

2.
Implementation

Our work aims to bridge the gap between the ML model’s decision and the end user understanding and accepting or contesting that decision. To do that, we would like to generate a clear and actionable explanation for a user who receives a negative outcome, like being denied a loan, using LLMs to automatize the process. Due to the limitations of these models when tackling problems that require planning and multiple step reasoning
(
McCoy et al. 2023
;
Stechly et al. 2024
)
, we broke down the problem into smaller ones, mimicking human reasoning
(
Miller 2019
)
. We hope that by doing so, the LLM will be capable of correctly identifying the causes that led to a change of class and synthesise them into a clear explanation which gives advice to the user on how to change to their desired output. In Figure
2
we can see a scheme of the different steps taken to generate the final text.

2.1.
Counterfactual Generation

One of the most natural approaches to answering a "why" question is to use a contrastive explanation. Humans generate a contrastive explanation by comparing the actual outcome with a hypothetical alternative scenario. The process begins with understanding the event or decision that occurred within a causal context. Then, we consider what could have happened differently by imagining a scenario where a key cause is altered. Finally, we focus on the specific factors or causes that lead to a different outcome in the alternative scenario.

We will simulate alternative scenarios by generating counterfactuals that will flip the decision of the classifier. In our case, we used the DiCE framework
(
Mothilal et al. 2020
)
, which is among the most popular, however, other frameworks could be used. DiCE utilizes user data and the ML model to generate counterfactuals through perturbations, approaching the problem as an optimization task. The optimization focuses on maximizing validity (examples that effectively change class), proximity (examples that are as close as possible to the original), and diversity (examples with different causes changing to offer various routes). Additionally, users can restrict the features to be changed in order to avoid some unfeasible counterfactuals (like changing race), and post-hoc processes are applied to enhance sparsity (changing the minimum number of features possible). By doing this, the obtained set of counterfactuals can provide the user with diverse, feasible paths to the desired outcome, provided they are understood properly. In Table
1
an example of an individual of the adult dataset predicted to earn less than 50k $ and five counterfactual examples predicted to earn above that threshold are shown.

This process can be viewed as employing abductive reasoning to generate possible causes. In Table

3.
Evaluation

3.1.
What makes a good explanation?

Before delving into how to evaluate the process of the LLM, it is important to introduce some concepts on what makes a good explanation from the social sciences perspective
(
Miller 2019
)
, which will help us create a better evaluation.

Contrastive Explanations:

People are usually not just interested in why something happened, but why it happened instead of something else. Counterfactual examples are contrastive by nature, which makes them a perfect starting point for building an explanation.

Biased Selection in Explanations:

People don’t usually expect to be presented with the whole causal chain that leads to an event happening. We often choose only one or two possible reasons out of many when explaining something, and these choices can be biased. This selective process is influenced by cognitive biases, meaning that we might prefer certain explanations over others, not because they are more accurate, but because they align better with our way of thinking. Some of these biases are selecting simpler and more general causes, selecting more recent and abnormal events as causes, or using necessary causes over sufficient ones. The latter is especially relevant in our work, because if a cause appears in all counterfactuals it has more chances of being necessary and therefore we should pay attention to it.

Social Dynamics of Explanations:

Explanations are social, functioning as a knowledge transfer mechanism within interactions or conversations. A person who receives an explanation will not be satisfied if it is not aligned with his or hers prior beliefs and if it is not given in the correct form. We assume that the LLM has captured this common knowledge in its training and that it will be capable of writing the explanation in a correct manner given the right instructions.

3.2.
Closed Loop Evaluation

As it is the case for most of LLM applications, evaluating how "good" these explanations are is not an easy task. It would be ideal to have a group of people manually analyzing the outputs of the LLM, but this very time consuming and expensive. Because of this, we decided to make a "closed loop" evaluation of LLM’s understanding of the counterfactuals and causes of the change. We did so by asking the LLM to generate a counterfactual example
(
Gat et al. 2023
)
at the end of the reasoning, using only the original case, information of the dataset and the final explanation. If the LLM is capable of generating a counterfactual example from the explanation it means that the important information was correctly synthesized in it. Additionally, we checked some metrics to evaluate the quality of the explanation. To obtain all of these metrics, a final prompt was used to create a table with all the information and save it in a csv file. After extracting and executing the code generated by the LLM we can apply external operations on the table
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
. The different metrics evaluated are the following:

•

Validity:
The final example produced by the LLM must belong to the desired class. This will indicate that the explanation contained the necessary information in order to produce the flip in classification.

•

Consistency with causes:

The example we generate must be consistent with the identified causes that were reasoned in the previous steps, so we will check how many of them it follows. As we mentioned, people prefer simpler explanations and only the main causes, so the ratio of causes used in the explanation and the use of the top ranked causes could be seen as a proxy of explanation quality.

•

Novelty:

Since the dataset we are using is quite well known and available on the internet, we should check that the LLM is not taking samples it has memorized from the original dataset.

•

Feasibility:

We would also like to check if the example given could be applied in the real world. Since the perturbation method can change the selected variables freely, it can produce some examples that may not be applicable in the real world, for example, suggesting to change to a job for which we are not qualified without changing our education. This step is optional and can only be done if one has access to the causal graph
(
Neal 2020
)
. It will be left as future work.

In Appendix A we show the prompts and the pipeline used for this evaluation.

4.
Experiments and Results

As in the illustrative example used to explain the method, in our experiments we worked with the "Adult" dataset
(
Becker and Kohavi 1996
)
. A Random Forest classifier was trained, to predict whether an individual will earn more than 50k$ a year and we selected 100 cases in which the system predicted that an individual would earn less than 50k$ per year. Explanations were generated with our proposed pipeline using GPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2024
)
as the LLM and DiCE allowing all features to vary freely to generate the counterfactuals. We evaluated the explanations with the method method mentioned in the previous section. We varied the size of the sets of counterfactuals between 1, 3 and 5 to observe the effects of adding more counterfactuals to the causes identified, and how the LLM selected them. Furthermore, we started by trying two different prompting techniques, Zero-Shot and Few-Shot
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, to see if there were improvements in the results.

Since generating the counterfactuals is an optimization problem with random initial conditions, the generated counterfactuals can be different each time the algorithm is called. On top of this, the responses of the LLM are also stochastic
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
, and vary in each generation. We used Tree of Thought (ToT)
(
Yao et al. 2023
)
to take advantage of this and try to create more diversity in the explanations. We used both of the prompting techniques previously mentioned and created three different explanations and sets of identified causes of the change starting from different sets of 5 counterfactuals. We then leveraged all the information to create a final explanation and hoped that this would add diversity to the causes and lead to a better final explanation. In Appendix B the prompts for this method are shown.

Validity was over 70% in all the experiments we did, showcasing the method proposed is able to generate explanations that would contain the necessary information to generate a counterfactual example that flips the decision of the classifier. Furthermore, we can see for the different prompt types that by using larger sets of counterfactual examples the validity improves. When more examples are given, more reasons for the given classification are found, and therefore a larger amount of different paths to obtain the desired outcome can be observed. This could be a cause of the increase in validity.

The best results in terms of validity were obtained when using a Zero-shot strategy. However, this does not mean that the explanation is better in terms of quality, and a human evaluation should be made to determine this. When using Tree of Thought, larger sets of causes were extracted, which is what we expected, although validity decreased with respect to the other experiments. Again, this does not mean that the quality is worse.

In all the cases, the final counterfactual generated by the LLM is consistent with the deduced causes, which indicates that they are contained in the explanation. Furthermore, we can see that not all the causes are applied to the example, which is what we expect if a good explanation was given. In addition to this, we see that the causes selected are consistent with the rank we made, and the most relevant cause is selected in most of the cases, having the highest percentage of selection followed by the second and then the third.

The percentage of generated examples present in the dataset is below 30% in all the experiments. This could be coincidences, since the dataset is large and there is not a lot of variability in the features which are usually changed like education level and profession. However, we cannot be certain about this and further experiments should be carried out with other less known data sets.

5.
Conclusions

In our work we present a method to produce
automatic explanations of a set of counterfactuals using LLMs. We have seen that the LLM is capable of correctly extracting possible causes that will lead to a flip of the decision of a classifier by analyzing these counterfactual examples, select the main reasons according to their relevance and generate a final explanation in natural language that can be interpreted by the final user. We have also observed that increasing the amount of counterfactuals increases the amount of times the LLM is capable of generating a counterfactual of the opposite class using only the natural language explanation, indicating better information given. Furthermore, using larger sets of counterfactuals and prompting techniques like Tree of Thought can lead to more diversity of causes in the final explanation.
Overall, our method shows to be a promising option to complement sets of counterfactual examples in order to make them more understandable for the final user. However, further experiments should be made with other datasets, and with human evaluators in order to assess the quality if the natural language explanations.
