Jennifer Paykin, Sam Winnick

Abstract
. This paper introduces a novel abstraction for programming quantum operations, specifically projective Cliffords , as functions over the qu d it Pauli group. 1.3.
Why Cliffords? Why Qu
d
its?

Why introduce programming paradigm for Clifford operations? It is well-known that Cliffords are not universal for quantum computing, and in fact are efficiently simulatable on classical computers
(
Aaronson and Gottesman, 2004
)
. Nevertheless, Cliffords play key parts in almost every quantum algorithm, and form the backbone of quantum error correction and fault tolerance
(
Gottesman, 2024
;
Nielsen and Chuang, 2002
)
. A key contribution of this work is to explore how expressing these algorithms in terms of their conjugation action on Paulis highlights reveals underlying structure and could inspire new algorithmic approaches.

The algebraic structure of projective Cliffords is already widely used via Pauli tableaux, which serve as intermediate representations in quantum compilers
(
Wu et al., 2023
;
Paykin et al., 2023
)
, simulation
(
Aaronson and Gottesman, 2004
;
Li et al., 2022
)
, circuit synthesis
(
Schneider et al., 2023
;
Schmitz et al., 2024
)
, and equivalence checking
(
Amy, 2019b
;
Kissinger and van de Wetering, 2020
;
Borgna et al., 2021
)
.
Another important contribution of this work is to elevate the tableau data structure into a full programming feature, supporting variables, functions, equational reasoning, polymorphism, and more.

Neither of these justifications get us to universal quantum computing, however; for this we turn to
qu
d
its
.
A qudit is a
d
-dimensional quantum system
\alpha_{0}\ket{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{d-1}\ket{d-1}
, generalizing a qubit when
d=2
. While an
n
-qubit state has dimension
2^{n}
, an
n
-
qu
d
it
state has dimension

. The Pauli and Clifford groups generalize naturally to

d
it
systems
(
de Beaudrap, 2013
)
, but for any fixed
d
, the
qu
d
it
Clifford group is still not universal.

However, universality
can
be achieved by combining Cliffords of different dimension. Consider the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) on
n
qubits. By interpreting that
n
-qubit state as a single
qu
d
it
where
d=2^{n}
, the QFT operator becomes Clifford and is simple to express in
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
. By combining QFT with qubit Cliffords, we obtain a universal set of unitaries.

Currently,
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
assumes a fixed dimension
d
, but future work will extend
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
to a universal quantum programming language by allowing polymorphism in
d
. To prepare for this, a major contribution of this work is establishing
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
not just for qubits, but for arbitrary
qu
d
its
.

2.
Background

The type system of
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
is inspired by Pauli tableaux, a binary encoding of qubit Cliffords used widely for circuit simulation, compilation, and optimization
(
Aaronson and Gottesman, 2004
)
.
A tableau represents an
n
-qubit Clifford operator by its projective action on the Pauli
X
and
Z
operators, and is well-formed only if this action preserves the canonical commutation relations of
X
and
Z
.

Pauli tableaux have been generalized to
qu
d
it
Cliffords, and are well-behaved for odd and odd prime dimension
d

(
de Beaudrap, 2013
)
. For event dimensions, however, encodings become more complex, and several alternative formulations have been proposed.

The goal of
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
’s type system is to ensure that functions correspond exactly to valid encodings of projective Cliffords. To do this, we need an encoding of qudit projective Cliffords in arbitrary dimension
d
whose well-formedness properties are enforceable by a type system.

For odd dimensions this is relatively straight-forward: encodings are pairs consisting of a linear map and a symplectomorphism (a linear map that respects the symplectic form). Linearity can be captured via techniques from linear type systems
(
Diaz-Caro and Dowek, 2024
)
, and the symplectomorphism property is compositional in a way that is suitable for type-checking.

When
d
is even, encodings of projective Cliffords are significantly more complicated. Projective Cliffords can be expressed as
X^{x}Z^{z}\mapsto e^{i\pi r(x,z)/d}X^{\psi_{1}(x,z)}Z^{\psi_{2}(x,z)}
where
(r,\psi)
satisfy certain constraints, but the constraint on
r
depends on
\psi
, and
r
need not even be linear.
In
(
Winnick and Paykin, 2024
)
we present
condensed encodings
, which characterize projective Cliffords in any dimension as pairs of a linear map and symplectomorphism, with an additional subtle
phase correction
. In
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
, this correction is handled internally in the
\beta
-reduction rules and is invisible to the programmer.

With this motivation in mind, the rest of this section reviews qudits in both even and odd dimensions and summarizes the main properties of condensed encodings.

2.3.
Encodings of Qudit Projective Cliffords

Similar to qubits, the qudit Clifford operators take the qudit Pauli group to itself under conjugation.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Clif}_{d,n}\triangleq\{U\in\mathcal{U}_{d^{n}}\mid\forall P\in\mathrm{Pauli}_{d,n}.~UPU^{\dagger}\in\mathrm{Pauli}_{d,n}\}

Two Cliffords
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathrm{Clif}_{d,n}
are
projectively equivalent
if they have the same conjugation action for all Paulis:
U_{1}PU_{1}^{\dagger}=U_{2}PU_{2}^{\dagger}
. This is the case exactly when
U_{2}=e^{i\theta}U_{1}
, meaning that
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are quantum-mechanically indistinguishable. The equivalence classes
[U]
of

form a group
\mathrm{PClif}_{d,n}
with composition
[U_{2}][U_{1}]=[U_{2}U_{1}]
.

In order to program projective Cliffords, we draw on a common practice of

each class
[U]
as a pair of functions that act on the vectors
v
in the representation of
\Delta_{v}
.

Definition 2.3 (
(
Winnick and Paykin, 2024
)
).

The
condensed encoding
of a projective Clifford is a pair of functions
(\mu,\psi)
where
\psi:\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}
is a symplectomorphism (respects the symplectic form on
\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}
), and
\mu:\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{d}
is a linear transformation. This encoding corresponds to a projective Clifford
[U]\in\mathrm{PClif}_{d,n}
defined by its conjugation action on
\Delta_{\underline{b}}
for every standard basis vector
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}
as follows:

\displaystyle U\Delta_{\underline{b}}U^{\dagger}=\zeta^{\mu(b)}\Delta_{\underline{\psi(b)}}

Let us write

for the group of homomorphisms
\mu:\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{d}
and
\mathrm{Sp}(\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n})
for the group of symplectomorphisms
\psi:\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}

i.e.,
for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}
,
\omega(\psi(v_{1}),\psi(v_{2}))=\omega(v_{1},v_{2})
.

Theorem 2.4 (
(
Winnick and Paykin, 2024
)
).

The assignment of the condensed encoding
(\mu,\psi)
to each projective Clifford

is a bijection between the underlying sets of
\mathrm{PClif}_{d,n}
and

The condensed encoding is not the only choice of encoding, but it has several advantages. First, both the condition that
\mu
is a linear transformation and the condition that

is a symplectomorphism can be enforced by the type system, leading to a robust Curry-Howard correspondence. Second, the condensed encoding is applicable for all dimensions of

5.1.
Linear/Non-linear Types

Up until now we have only worked with linear types and linear maps in the sense of linear logic. To make

(

In LNL type systems, there are two kinds each of terms, types, typing contexts, and typing judgments: linear and non-linear.
They are useful for adding programming features to a linear language in a way that does not conflict with properties of the linear system, and have specifically been used for quantum programming languages in several settings
(

(

)
. Non-linear types also include

in that they capture a first-order quantum operation inside a classical sub-language.

5.2.
Compiling to Pauli Tableaux and Circuits

The idea of encoding projective cliffords based on their basis elements is inspired by the use of Pauli tableaux
(
Aaronson and Gottesman, 2004
)
.
A tableau can be defined as a non-linear data type
\text{Tableau}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Tableau}(\textsc{Pauli},\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle\triangleq\lceil\tau^{\prime}\rceil\times\lceil\tau^{\prime}\rceil

\displaystyle\qquad\text{Tableau}(\tau_{1}\boxtimes\tau_{2},\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle\triangleq\text{Tableau}(\tau_{1},\tau^{\prime})\times\text{Tableau}(\tau_{2},\tau^{\prime})

Intuitively, a tableau is a list of pairs
(P_{i,x},P_{i,z})
of
n
-
qu

Paulis, indicating that
UX_{i}U^{\dagger}=P_{i,x}
and
UZ_{i}U^{\dagger}=P_{i,z}
for each

.

Not all terms of this type correspond to well-formed tableaux—it must be the case that each
(P_{i,x},P_{i,z})
pair satisfies

\omega(P_{i,z},P_{i,x})=1
and, for

,
\omega(P_{i,x},P_{j,x})=\omega(P_{i,z},P_{j,z})=0
. For this reason, programming directly with Pauli tableaux in other languages can be dangerous for the programmer, as the type system does not ensure tableaux are well-formed.
However, every projective Clifford
\Gamma\vdash^{\scalebox{0.5}{{N}}}n:\lceil\tau\multimap\tau^{\prime}\rceil
can be compiled to a well-formed Pauli tableau
\Gamma\vdash^{\scalebox{0.5}{{N}}}\text{compile}_{\tau}(n):\text{Tableau}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
by induction on
\tau
:

\displaystyle\text{compile}_{\textsc{Pauli}}(n)

\displaystyle\triangleq(\lceil n\textbf{X}\rceil,\lceil n\textbf{Z}\rceil)

\displaystyle\text{compile}_{\tau_{1}\boxtimes\tau_{2}}(n)

\displaystyle\triangleq\left(\text{compile}_{\tau_{1}}\left(\lambda\lceil x_{1}\rceil.n(\iota_{1}(x_{1}))\right),\text{compile}_{\tau_{2}}\left(\lambda\lceil x_{2}\rceil.n(\iota_{2}(x_{2}))\right)\right)

This compilation can then be used in conjunction with circuit synthesis from Pauli tableaux to compile Clifford functions all the way to circuits
(
Aaronson and Gottesman, 2004
;
Paykin et al., 2023
;
Schneider et al., 2023
;
Brandl, 2024
)
.

Basics of Stabilizer Codes

A stabilizer code is a quantum error-correcting code characterized by an Abelian group
\mathbf{S}\subseteq\mathrm{Pauli}_{d,n}
of stabilizers, with
\tau^{s}I\in\mathbf{S}
if and only if
s=0
.
The fact that
\mathbf{S}
is Abelian means that all of the Paulis in
\mathbf{S}
commute with each other.
The code space of such a stabilizer code is the set of states stabilized by
\mathbf{S}
,
i.e.,

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{S})=\{\ket{\phi}\mid\forall P\in\mathbf{S}.~P\ket{\phi}=\ket{\phi}\}
.

We use the meta-variable
n
to refer to the number of physical qubits,
k
for the number of logical qubits, and
r
for the number of stabilizer generators
S_{0},\ldots,S_{r-1}
in an
[[n,k]]
code. Note that we always have
k+r=n

(
Gottesman, 2024
, Chapter 3)
.
For additional details, see
Gottesman (2024)
.

7.
Related and Future Work

7.1.
Related Work in Quantum Programming Languages

The majority of quantum programming languages today are based on the quantum gate model, where programs are formed from primitive quantum gates together with classical
(
Selinger et al., 2009
;
Green et al., 2013
;
Altenkirch and Green, 2010
;
Khalate et al., 2022
)
or quantum
(
Bădescu and Panangaden, 2015
;
Chiribella et al., 2013
;
Voichick et al., 2023
)
control flow.
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
takes a different approach, inspired by quantum programming languages based the linear-algebraic structure of quantum computing.

Linear-algebraic Programming Languages

The
\lambda^{\mathcal{L}}
calculus is closely related to a line of work on linear-algebraic lambda calculi stemming from QML
(
Altenkirch and Grattage, 2005
)
and Lineal
(
Arrighi and Dowek, 2017
)
, where terms in the calculus correspond to linear transformations over vectors spaces.
More recently,
Diaz-Caro and Dowek (2024)
combined a linear-algebraic lambda calculus with a linear logic, which allows them to prove well-typed functions are linear instead of defining function application pointwise. In contrast to their work,
\lambda^{\mathcal{L}}
does not include all of the connectives of intuitionistic multiplicative linear logic, notably the tensor product
\otimes
. The tensor product is not necessary for programming projective Cliffords, and by excluding it we avoid much of the complexity of their calculus. For example, while sums of values of type
\alpha_{1}\oplus\alpha_{2}
can be combined e.g.
[v_{1},v_{2}]+[v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime}]\rightarrow[v_{1}+v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}+v_{2}^{\prime}]
, the same cannot be done for sums of values of type
\alpha_{1}\otimes\alpha_{2}
. As such, their type system does not allow reduction under
t_{1}+t_{2}
if
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
have type
\alpha_{1}\otimes\alpha_{2}
.
Both type systems have equational theories based on logical relations, and both satisfy the fact that
f(a_{1}+a_{2})=f(a_{1})+f(a_{2})
.

Linear-algebraic lambda calculi are closely related to quantum computing. QML
(
Altenkirch and Grattage, 2005
)
, Lambda-
S_{1}

(
Díaz-Caro et al., 2019
)
, and symmetric pattern matching calculi
(
Sabry et al., 2018
)
limit linear transformations to unitary ones by ensuring that the branches of a quantum case statement are appropriately orthogonal. These orthogonality checks mirror the symplectic form condition in
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
—we too are restricting linear transformations to a particular shape. However, as these other languages target unitary transformations, their orthogonality checks involve simulating a linear transformation on vectors of size
2^{n}
. In contrast, the orthogonality check on symplectic encodings only involves vectors of size
2n
.

Pauli-based Programming and Optimization

We can also compare this work to representations of quantum algorithms based on Paulis, such as Pauli tableaux
(
Aaronson and Gottesman, 2004
;
Schmitz et al., 2024
)
, Pauli exponentials/Pauli rotations
(
Li et al., 2022
;
Zhang and Chen, 2019
;
Paykin et al., 2023
)
, phase polynomials
(
Amy, 2019b
;
Amy, 2019a
)
, and the ZX calculus
(
Kissinger and van de Wetering, 2020
)
. These representations have been used in the verification, optimization, and synthesis of quantum algorithms, but are not necessarily well-suited for programming. For one, they do not generally support abstractions like data structures and parametricity. For another, while phase polynomials and ZX-calculus processes can be used for circuit synthesis, they also include operations that are not physically realizable: for example, synthesizing a circuit from a ZX diagram is #P-hard in general
(
de Beaudrap et al., 2022
)
. Pauli exponentials are synthesizable into circuits in a straightforward way, but they are only fully general for unitaries. Pauli exponentials can be extended beyond unitaries, for example in PCOAST
(
Paykin et al., 2023
;
Schmitz et al., 2023
)
, but only for primitive preparation and measurement operators.

The lines of work most closely-related to
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
are Gottesman types
(
Rand et al., 2020
)
and Heisenberg logic
(
Sundaram et al., 2023
)
. In those works, the projective action of Cliffords are used as a specification for Clifford circuits; for example, the Hadamard gate is given the specification that it maps
X
to
Z
and vice versa. The advantage of this approach is that it can be used for
partial
specification, allowing for properties like separability as well as non-Clifford gates
(
Rand et al., 2021
)
. While
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
focuses on describing high-level algorithms and allowing a compiler to efficiently generate the circuits, Gottesman types and Heisenberg logic focus on verification of circuits that already exist, which may have additional constraints such as limited connectivity or depth. These two approaches are complimentary however;
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
could be used to inspire a richer specification language for Gottesman types, and Heisenberg logic could be used to verify the correctness of a
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
compiler.

Qu
d
it Quantum Programming

In recent years there has been significant interest in higher-dimensional (qudit) systems, inspired by the capabilities of real hardware
(
Chi et al., 2022
;
Hrmo et al., 2023
;
Fischer et al., 2023
)
. Simulations confirm that qubit-based implementations of quantum algorithms can be significantly more efficient compared to their qubit versions
(
Litteken et al., 2022
;
Nadkarni and Garani, 2021
;
Keppens et al., 2025
)
. As such, researchers have investigated techniques for compiling qubit quantum programs to qudit circuits using a variety of techniques including gatewise transpilation
(
Drozhzhin et al., 2024
)
, phase polynomials
(
Heyfron and Campbell, 2019
)
, and unitary decomposition
(
Mato et al., 2023c
;
Volya and Mishra, 2023
;
Lysaght et al., 2024
;
Sharma and Achour, 2024
;
Kiktenko et al., 2025
)
.

While some gate-based programming frameworks support qudit simulation and development
(
Mato et al., 2023b
;
Chatterjee et al., 2023
;
Volya and Mishra, 2023
;
de Souza Farias et al., 2025
)
, to our knowledge
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
is the first in the style of linear-algebraic programming languages.

7.2.
Future Work

Implementation

The examples given in this paper are written in pseudocode, and an implementation of
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
is a next logical step. Such an implementation should include both an interpreter and a compiler to Pauli tableaux and thus circuits, as described in
Section

5.2
.

An efficient type checker is necessary for practical use of
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
, as checking the symplectomorphism relation is tricky by hand. Consider a judgment
x_{1}:\tau_{1},x_{2}:\tau_{2}\vdash^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{P}_{c}$}}\omega_{\tau^{\prime}}\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)\equiv r
. Naively checking this condition would require evaluating the symplectic form on each of the
2^{|\tau_{1}|}\times 2^{|\tau_{2}|}
basis values of type
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
respectively, where the cost of each evaluation is unknown.
Instead, we propose implementing symplectomorphism-checking via symbolic evaluation, which would require just a single call to an SMT solver.

Beyond Cliffords

As discussed in the introduction,
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
is not universal for quantum computing since it focuses solely on the Clifford group. However, we speculate that there are several directions in which to extend the language to encompass general quantum computation.

One avenue is to integrate
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
into representations of Pauli exponentials such as PCOAST
(
Paykin et al., 2023
)
. PCOAST is an optimization framework for universal quantum computation where typical gates are replaced with Pauli-based components—Pauli rotations (unitaries of the form
e^{-iP\theta}
), Pauli tableaux, and generalized versions of Pauli measurements and state preparation. Existing PCOAST representations are not well-suited for programming, as they are not parametric on the number of qubits or modular over their inputs. We hypothesize that by replacing the tableaux with
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
expressions, we could very naturally express programs for universal quantum computing in the style of PCOAST terms but with the added benefit of compositionality, modularity, and type checking.

PCOAST supports measurement and preparation, which we note
are
efficiently simulatable on Pauli tableaux, with subtleties for even dimensions
d
. Incorporating measurement into
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
, even without arbitrary Pauli rotations, would be extremely useful for applications like error correction.

Another approach towards universaility is to extend
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
to be parametric in the dimension
d
of
qu
d
its
(
Section

1.3
), which would extend
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
s computational power along the lines of mixed-dimensional quantum computing
(
Mato et al., 2023b
;
Mato et al., 2023a
;
Volya and Mishra, 2023
;
Chatterjee et al., 2023
;
Poór et al., 2023
)
.
Clifford unitaries in higher dimensions are not all Clifford in lower dimensions: for example, the 2 qubit Fourier transform
F_{4}
is single-
qu
d
it
Clifford (with
d=4
) but not multi-qubit Clifford. In fact,
F_{4}
together with
F_{2}=H
is universal.

A third avenue to explore is moving up the Clifford hierarchy. The Clifford hierarchy starts with the Pauli group, and the
(k+1)

\displaystyle\textit{Clf}~^{0}_{d,n}

\displaystyle\textit{Clf}~^{k+1}_{d,n}

\displaystyle\triangleq\{U\mid\forall P\in\mathrm{Pauli}_{d,n}.~UPU^{\dagger}\in\textit{Clf}~^{k}_{d,n}\}

Extending this to a programming abstraction, if a projective Clifford is a map from a single Pauli to the Pauli group, then a projective

We hypothesize that a

with

7.3.
Conclusion

This paper presents a type system for programming projective Clifford unitaries as functions on the Pauli group. We establish a Curry-Howard correspondence with the category
\mathcal{P}_{c}
of projective Clifford encodings built out of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
-linear maps and symplectic morphisms. We present the type systems of
\lambda^{\mathcal{L}}
and
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
and prove they are well-behaved with respect to the categorical semantics. Finally, we explore what it is like to program with
\lambda^{\mathcal{P}_{c}}
via extensions and examples.

A.2.
Strong Normalization

Next, we want to show that every well-typed expression
\cdot\vdash^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{L}$}}a:\alpha
(1) always evaluates to a value (does not get stuck or run forever); and (2) evaluates to a

Uniqueness is easy for this operational semantics, because it has a fixed evaluation order and is thus deterministic.

To prove that every expression evaluates to a value, we define the following logical relation on values and closed expressions respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}[\mathbb{Z}_{d}]

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{Z}_{d}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}[\alpha_{1}\oplus\alpha_{2}]

\displaystyle\triangleq\{[v_{1},v_{2}]\mid v_{1}\in\mathcal{V}[\alpha_{1}]\wedge v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}[\alpha_{2}]\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}[\alpha_{1}\multimap\alpha_{2}]

\displaystyle\triangleq\{\lambda x.a\mid\forall v\in\mathcal{V}[\alpha_{1}].~a\{v/x\}\in\mathcal{N}[\alpha_{2}]\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}[\alpha]

\displaystyle\triangleq\{a\mid\cdot\vdash^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{L}$}}a:\alpha\wedge\exists v\in\mathcal{V}[\alpha].~a\rightarrow^{\ast}v\}

We can extend this relation to open expressions. Let
\delta
be a map from variables to (closed) values; in other words, it is a partial function from the set of variables to the set of values. We write
x\mapsto v\in\delta
to mean that

In the same way, typing contexts

can be thought of as partial functions from the set of variables to the set of types. For example,

We now define

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{\delta\mid\forall x\in\text{dom}(\Delta),~x\in\text{dom}(\delta)\wedge\delta(x)\in\mathcal{V}[\Delta(x)]\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}[\Delta\vdash^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{L}$}}\alpha]

\displaystyle\triangleq\{a\mid\Delta\vdash^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{L}$}}a:\alpha\wedge\forall\delta\in\mathcal{V}_{\Delta}.~\delta(a)\in\mathcal{N}[\alpha]\}.

It is trivial to see that the following lemma holds about

Lemma A.1.

If
\cdot\vdash^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{L}$}}a:\alpha

Follows from the definition of

.

Lemma A.2 (Compatibility of constants and tuples).

For the

If

Let
\delta\in\mathcal{V}_{\Delta}

If

\displaystyle r_{1}+r_{2}\rightarrow_{\beta}r^{\prime}

Appendix B

Categorical Soundness for
\lambda^{\mathcal{L}}

B.1.
Properties of
\mathcal{L}

The (outer) direct sum
A\oplus A^{\prime}
is defined as the set of symbols
\{a\oplus a^{\prime}\mid a\in A~\text{and}~a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}\}

with basis
\{b^{A}_{i}\oplus b^{A^{\prime}}_{i}\}
, and
with addition and scalar multiplication defined by:

\displaystyle r(a\oplus a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(ra)\oplus(ra^{\prime})

\displaystyle(a\oplus a^{\prime})+(b\oplus b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(a+b)\oplus(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime})

The rank of
A\oplus A^{\prime}
is
\mathrm{rank}(A)+\mathrm{rank}(A^{\prime})
,
and it is easy to verify that
A\oplus A^{\prime}
is the categorical biproduct of
A
and
B
.

The tensor product
A\otimes B
of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
-modules
A
and
B
forms a monoidal product on
\mathcal{L}
.
The universal property of the tensor product in
\mathcal{L}
allows us to regard elements of
A\otimes A^{\prime}
as
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
-linear combinations of symbols
a\otimes a^{\prime}
with
a\in A
and
b\in A^{\prime}
, modulo the following relations:

\displaystyle r(a\otimes b)

\displaystyle=(ra)\otimes b=a\otimes(rb)

\displaystyle(a\otimes b)+(a^{\prime}\otimes b)

\displaystyle=(a+a^{\prime})\otimes b

\displaystyle(a\otimes b)+(a\otimes b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=a\otimes(b+b^{\prime})

The rank of
A\otimes B
is multiplicative in the ranks of
A
and
B
, and the basis of
A\otimes A^{\prime}
is the Kronecker basis
\{b^{A}_{i}\otimes b^{A^{\prime}}_{j}\}
.

The associator of
\otimes
is taken to be the isomorphism
A\otimes(B\otimes C)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}(A\otimes B)\otimes C
given by linearly extending
a\otimes(b\otimes c)\mapsto(a\otimes b)\otimes c
; this is natural in

Lemma C.1.

C.2.
Soundness

To prove the categorical semantics is sound, we first prove that substitution corresponds to composition.

Lemma C.7.

By induction on

Case C.7.2 (

\displaystyle\left\llbracket t\{v/x\}\right\rrbracket^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{P}_{c}$}}

(induction hypothesis)

\displaystyle=\left\llbracket t_{2}\right\rrbracket^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{P}_{c}$}}\circ\left\llbracket t_{1}\right\rrbracket^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{P}_{c}$}}\circ\left\llbracket v\right\rrbracket^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{P}_{c}$}}
