Resonance-enhanced optical nonlinearity in the Weyl semimetal TaAs
Shreyas Patankar, Liang Wu, Baozhu Lu, Manita Rai, Jason D. Tran, T. Morimoto, D. Parker, Adolfo G. Grushin, N. L. Nair, J. G. Analytis, J. E. Moore, J. Orenstein, Darius H. Torchinsky

Abstract




While all media can exhibit first-order conductivity describing current linearly proportional to electric field,
\displaystyle E
, the second-order conductivity,
\displaystyle\sigma^{(2)}
, relating current to
\displaystyle E^{2}
, is nonzero only when inversion symmetry is broken. Second-order nonlinear optical responses are powerful tools in basic research, as probes of symmetry breaking
shen1986surface
;
shen1989surface
;
dahn1996symmetry
;
petersen2006nonlinear
;
harter2017parity
, and in optical technology as the basis for generating currents from far-infrared
robinsonFIR
to X-ray wavelengths
chang1997generation
. The recent surge of interest in Weyl semimetals with acentric crystal structures has led to the discovery of a host of
\displaystyle\sigma^{(2)}
-related phenomena in this class of materials, such as polarization-selective conversion of light to dc current (photogalvanic effects)
ma2017direct
;
sun2017circular
;
osterhoudt2017colossal
;
ji2018spatially
and the observation of giant second-harmonic generation (SHG) efficiency in TaAs at photon energy 1.5 eV
wu2017giant
;
li2018
. Here, we present measurements of the SHG spectrum of TaAs revealing that the response at 1.5 eV corresponds to the high-energy tail of a resonance at 0.7 eV, at which point the second harmonic conductivity is approximately 200 times larger than seen in the standard candle nonlinear crystal, GaAs. This remarkably large SHG response provokes the question of ultimate limits on
\displaystyle\sigma^{(2)}
, which we address by a new theorem relating frequency-integrated nonlinear response functions to the third cumulant (or “skewness”) of the polarization distribution function in the ground state. This theorem provides considerable insight into the factors that lead to the largest possible second-order nonlinear response, specifically showing that the spectral weight is unbounded and potentially divergent when the possibility of next-neighbor hopping is included.

Second-harmonic generation (SHG) is a subset of
\displaystyle\sigma^{(2)}
-related phenomena in which light of frequency
\displaystyle 2\omega
is generated by excitation at frequency
\displaystyle\omega
. Figure 1 shows a schematic of the apparatus used to measure the spectrum of the SHG response tensor, which is formally written as
\displaystyle\sigma_{ijk}(2\omega;\omega,\omega)
and hereafter shortened to
\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{shg}}_{ijk}(\omega)
, or simply
\displaystyle\sigma_{ijk}
. The measurements are enabled by a laser/optical parametric amplifier system that generates pulses of duration
\displaystyle\sim
50 fs at a 5 kHz repetition rate in the range of photon energies 0.5 - 1.5 eV. As the crystals are opaque (optical penetration depth of approximately 200 nm

) the SHG intensity is measured in reflection. As shown in the Figure 1a., a polarizer
\displaystyle P
, and a halfwave plate in front of the sample select the orientation of the electric field (referred to by
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{e}}}
) of the fundamental light at frequency
\displaystyle\omega
, and a second analyzing polarizer
\displaystyle A
selects the polarization of the light at frequency
\displaystyle 2\omega
that reaches the detector.

The structure of TaAs (point group
\displaystyle 4mm
) contains two perpendicular glide planes, which are equivalent to mirror planes for optical response functions
TaAsWeng
;
li2016weyl
. The normal directions of these two planes determine the two equivalent directions of the tetragonal unit cell, which we label as
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
. The direction perpendicular to the
\displaystyle xy
plane is the polar, or
\displaystyle z
axis. The
\displaystyle 4mm
point group allows three independent nonvanishing components of the conductivity tensor,
\displaystyle\sigma_{zzz}
,
\displaystyle\sigma_{zxx}=\sigma_{zyy}
, and
\displaystyle\sigma_{xzx}=\sigma_{yzy}
. To avoid using a surface obtained by cutting and subsequent polishing, which are known to affect the nonlinear optical response, we perform nonlinear reflectance measurements using the (112) surface, as it forms a naturally flat and smooth growth facet. Figure
1

We performed two pairs of polarization scans at each wavelength. In the first pair,
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{e}}}
and
\displaystyle A

∘

These scans are primarily used to determine the high symmetry directions. However, as will be discussed below, they provide additional evidence for the existence of a sharp resonance in the nonlinear response function in the infrared. In the second pair of scans
\displaystyle A
is fixed parallel to either of the [1,-1,0] or [1,1,-1] high symmetry directions and
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{e}}}
is rotated over 360
∘

\displaystyle|\sigma_{zxx}|
,
\displaystyle|\sigma_{xzx}|

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}|\equiv|\sigma_{zzz}+2\sigma_{zxx}+4\sigma_{xzx}|

\displaystyle|\sigma_{zxx}|/|\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}|
and
\displaystyle|\sigma_{xzx}|/|\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}|

Measurements of absolute, as opposed to relative, magnitudes of
\displaystyle\sigma_{ijk}

The key features of the nonlinear conductivity in TaAs –– strong anisotropy and a single, sharp resonant peak –– suggest a description in terms of a one-dimensional (1D) two-band model, modified for a 3D system. Recently it was shown that the Rice-Mele (RM) tight-binding model of a polar semiconductor
RM82
provides an excellent description of the spectrum of the shift current response in single-layer monochalcogenide semiconductors
rangel2017large
. As we discuss below, for such models there is precise correspondence between shift current and SHG spectra
MN16
. Moreover, the energies probed in our experiment are much larger than either the Fermi energy
xu2015discovery
;
TaAsLv
or plasma frequency
xu2016optical
of TaAs, suggesting that the semimetallic part of its bandstructure is not relevant to the nonlinear response at photon energies of order
\displaystyle 1
eV.

These considerations lead us to compare the measured SHG spectra with the predictions for a system described by the RM Hamiltonian,

H_{RM}=t\cos(k_{z}a/2)\sigma_{z}+\delta\sin(k_{z}a/2)\sigma_{y}+\Delta\sigma_{z},

(1)

where the
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}
are the Pauli matrices. As shown in Figure
4
,
\displaystyle H_{RM}
describes a 1D chain of atoms along the
\displaystyle z
direction, in which inversion symmetry is broken by alternating on-site energies (
\displaystyle\pm\Delta
) and hopping amplitudes (
\displaystyle t\pm\delta
). Despite its relative simplicity, the RM model has played a key role in the development of the modern theory of polarization
resta2007theory
and recently in strategies to enhance nonlinear optical response functions
tan2017upper
.

The optical response of the RM Hamiltonian captures qualitative features of the nonlinear optical response in TaAs, as
\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{shg}}_{ijk}(\omega)
is polarized parallel to the polar axis and exhibits a sharp peak at the threshold for absorption. However, to allow a quantitative comparison with experiment the RM model must be modified to include an interchain coupling to smooth the 1D van Hove singularity that is obtained for an isolated chain.

To extend the model to 3D, we consider an array of RM chains parallel to
\displaystyle z
and allow electrons to hop laterally in the
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
atom layers (see Figure
4
). The interchain hopping adds an additional term
\displaystyle t_{AB}(\cos k_{x}a_{x}+\cos k_{y}a_{y})
to the coefficient of
\displaystyle\sigma_{z}
in
\displaystyle H_{RM}
, where
\displaystyle t_{AB}\equiv t_{\parallel,A}-t_{\parallel,B}
. The shift current spectrum in this model can be calculated following
SipeShkrebtii
(see
seeApp
),

\sigma_{zzz}^{\text{shift}}(\omega)=\frac{2e^{3}}{\hbar\Delta}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\right)^{3}\frac{c^{2}}{ab}F(\tilde{\omega};\tilde{\delta},\tilde{t},\tilde{t}_{AB}),

(2)

where
\displaystyle F
is a dimensionless function of the parameters of
\displaystyle H_{RM}
, and of frequency
\displaystyle\omega
(the tildes indicate normalization by
\displaystyle\Delta
);
\displaystyle a
,
\displaystyle b
, and
\displaystyle c
are lattice constants in the
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle y
, and
\displaystyle z
directions, respectively
seeApp
. The real part of the SHG conductivity can be expressed in terms of the shift conductivity as follows

\text{Re}\left\{\sigma^{\text{shg}}_{zzz}(\omega)\right\}=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{zzz}^{\text{shift}}(\omega)+\sigma_{zzz}^{\text{shift}}(2\omega)

(3)

Equation (
3

\displaystyle\hbar\omega=E_{\text{gap}}(k)
and the second (“two-photon”) term corresponds to
\displaystyle\hbar\omega=E_{\text{gap}}(\textbf{k})/2

\displaystyle E_{\text{gap}}(\textbf{k})
is the energy gap at wavevector
k

\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{shg}}_{zzz}
response integrates to zero, but we shall show momentarily that for some models each term has a geometrical interpretation in terms of the skewness or intrinsic asymmetry of the ground-state polarization distribution.

The theory discussed above helps to identify the factors that contribute to a large SHG response. The first is a polar crystal structure such as
\displaystyle 4mm
(TaAs, NbAs, etc.) or
\displaystyle mm2
(single layer monochalcogenides) consistent with a phenomenological description in terms of coupled ferroelectric chains. However, even when this description is possible there is a global bound on the frequency integrated second-order response,
\displaystyle\Sigma_{z}^{\text{shift}}
, of a system described by
\displaystyle H_{RM}
that is given by,

\Sigma_{z}^{\text{shift}}\equiv\int\sigma_{zzz}^{\text{shift}}(\omega)d\omega=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\frac{c^{2}}{ab}G(\tilde{t},\tilde{\delta}).

(4)

where
\displaystyle G
is also a dimensionless function of the dimensionless parameters, with a global maximum 0.604
seeApp
. Given this bound on
\displaystyle\Sigma_{z}^{\text{shift}}
, it is clear that the single most important factor in optimizing the second-order response of a 3D crystal is the effective aspect ratio parameter
\displaystyle c^{2}/ab
.

We conclude by addressing the question of ultimate bounds on
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{shift}}_{z}
, which is relevant not only to SHG, but to sensitivity of photodetectors and efficiency of solar cells based on Berry curvature generated intrinsic photogalvanic effects
MN16
;
MooreOrenstein
as well. We have discovered a new connection between
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{shift}}_{z}
and the modern theory of polarization that generalizes the formulae for spectral weight beyond the RM Hamiltonian. Specifically we show that
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{shift}}_{z}
is unbounded and potentially divergent when the possibility of next-neighbor hopping is included.

As demonstrated in the Supplementary Information,
\displaystyle\Sigma_{z}^{\text{shift}}
in two band systems, regardless of dimensionality
\displaystyle d
and range of electron hopping amplitude is given by,

\Sigma_{z}^{\text{shift}}=\frac{2\pi e^{3}}{V\hbar^{2}}C_{3},

(5)

where,

C_{3}\ =\ -\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int d^{d}k\;\text{Im}\left[c_{3}-3c_{2}c_{1}+2c^{3}_{1}\right],

(6)

\displaystyle c_{n}\equiv\langle u_{0}(k)|(i\partial_{k})^{n}|u_{0}(k)\rangle
, and the periodic gauge is assumed for the valence-band Bloch wavefunction
\displaystyle\psi_{0k}(r)=u_{0k}(r)e^{ikr}
. The quantity
\displaystyle C_{3}
is a member of a set of gauge invariant quantities,
\displaystyle C_{n}
, that are cumulants of the electronic polarization
hetenyi_cumulants
;
souza2000polarization
. The quantity
\displaystyle C_{1}
is exactly the average macroscopic polarization, which coincides with the first moment of the polarization distribution
resta2007theory
. Accordingly,
\displaystyle C_{3}
is the third cumulant, or “skewness” of the distribution, which vanishes in the presence of inversion symmetry.
\displaystyle C_{1}
is controlled by the center-of-mass location of the polarization, while
\displaystyle C_{3}
describes the intrinsic asymmetry in the shape of the polarization distribution, independent of its center-of-mass location.

In addition to providing a very satisfying connection between the nonlinear response and ground state fluctuations of the polarization, equation (
5
) speeds the search for effective Hamiltonians with large nonlinear response. For instance, we find that the bound on
\displaystyle\Sigma_{z}^{\text{shift}}
that is obtained in the 3D RM model is exceeded when next-neighbor hopping is introduced, and can diverge when the fundamental gap is driven to zero. This result is consistent with the recent analysis of Tan and Rappe
tan2017upper
, who suggested that
\displaystyle\Sigma_{z}^{\text{shift}}
can be enhanced when the range of inter-site hopping becomes larger than the lattice parameter.

In this work we reported SHG spectra of TaAs demonstrating a ten-fold resonant enhancement of the response at 0.7 eV as compared with the previously reported, and already quite large, response at 1.5 eV. We showed that the spectral dependence and absolute magnitude of the SHG response function is well captured by a model based on coupled ferroelectric Rice-Mele chains, suggesting that the giant nonlinear response in TaAs is related to its strong polar nature. The relationship between the RM phenomenology and the actual electronic wavefunctions and bandstructure of TaAs remains to be understood. Finally, we presented a new theorem linking the spectral weight of the nonlinear conductivity to the third gauge-invariant cumulant of the ground state. The cumulant
\displaystyle C_{3}
is likely to be more computationally tractable, and we anticipate that this theorem will facilitate algorithmic searches for materials with large nonlinear response functions.

(7)

A.2
Wavelength dependent correction factors

We provide a brief summary of the factors that must necessarily be taken into consideration to accurately determine the second harmonic susceptibility tensor elements
\displaystyle\chi_{ijk}^{\text{shg}}
and the procedure we followed to experimentally measure them.

The electric field of the incident laser pulse can be well approximated by a simple Gaussian field as

\mathbf{E}_{\omega}(\mathbf{r},t)=E_{0}e^{-i(k_{0}z-\omega t)}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}\right)\exp{\left[-\left(\frac{z-ct}{\tau}\right)^{2}\right]}\hat{x}

(15)

where
\displaystyle E_{0}
is the electric field amplitude,
\displaystyle k_{0}
is the field wavevector magnitude,
\displaystyle\omega
is the fundamental light frequency,
\displaystyle c
is the speed of light, and
\displaystyle w_{0}
is the electric field beam waist
Quesnel1998theory
, here taken as the point from which the SHG field arises. We have assumed that the beam is transversely polarized purely in the
\displaystyle\hat{x}
direction for convenience. As a consequence of Poynting’s Theorem, the relationship between the integrated intensity of the incident, fundamental frequency field
\displaystyle I_{\omega}
and the associated electric field can be given by

I_{\omega}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{0}\int\left\langle|\mathbf{E}_{\omega}(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}\right\rangle d\mathbf{r}

(16)

where the brackets denote time averaging. For the field as given by equation (
15
), the integration is easily performed to yield

I_{\omega}\propto E_{0}^{2}w_{0}\tau

(17)

The penetration depth of the wavelengths used in this study is relatively short in
TaAs
. Thus we may consider the limit of non-depleted incident fields. The radiated second harmonic electric field is then proportional to the second-order induced polarization as
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{2\omega}(\mathbf{r},t)\propto\mathbf{P}_{2\omega}(\mathbf{r},t)=\epsilon_{0}\overleftrightarrow{\bm{\chi}}^{\text{shg}}\mathbf{E}_{\omega}(\mathbf{r},t)\mathbf{E}_{\omega}(\mathbf{r},t)
. Hence, the intensity of the second harmonic field
\displaystyle I_{2\omega}
is given by

I_{2\omega}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{0}\int\left\langle|\mathbf{E}_{2\omega}(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}\right\rangle d\mathbf{r}\propto\int\left\langle|\overleftrightarrow{\bm{\chi}}^{\text{shg}}|^{2}|\mathbf{E}_{\omega}(\mathbf{r},t)|^{4}\right\rangle d\mathbf{r}\propto(E_{0}^{2}\chi_{ijk}^{\text{shg}})^{2}w_{0}\tau.

(18)

When this latter result is combined with equation (
17
) to eliminate the electric field
\displaystyle E_{0}
, we determine that

\displaystyle I_{2\omega}\propto(\chi_{ijk}^{\text{shg}})^{2}I_{\omega}^{2}/w_{0}\tau
. However, for Gaussian fields such as in equation (
15

\displaystyle w_{0}
can be described in terms of the incident beam diameter
\displaystyle d

\displaystyle f
and beam wavelength
\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle w_{0}\propto f\lambda/d
, yielding

I_{2\omega}\propto(\chi_{ijk}^{\text{shg}})^{2}\frac{I_{\omega}^{2}d}{f\lambda\tau}.

(19)

Therefore, accurate measurement of the elements of
\displaystyle\chi^{\text{shg}}
must be controlled for incident integrated pulse intensity (equivalently, energy), diameter, wavelength and duration as well as the extrinsic factor of the lens focal length. Since the value for
\displaystyle\chi_{ijk}^{\text{shg}}
at
\displaystyle 800\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
is known from previous study
wu2017giant

\displaystyle 800\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}

Quasi-One Dimensional Model for Second Harmonic Generation

B.1
Model details

This supplement describes the details of the phenomenological model of Rice-Mele chains used in the main text, and details the calculation of its second harmonic response. The model is inspired by the polar character of the
TaAs

\displaystyle\\
{\sigma_{zzz}}
is the dominant component of the SHG response. This is reminiscent of a 1D system, in which
\displaystyle\sigma_{zzz}
is the only non-zero component.
We consider a quasi-one dimensional model composed of 1D Rice Mele chains in the
\displaystyle z
-direction
RM82
with only weak couplings in the
\displaystyle xy
plane.
In momentum space it takes the form of a two band model
\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}=\bm{d}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}
with

\displaystyle d_{x}

\displaystyle=t\sin(ck_{z}/2)

(22)

\displaystyle d_{y}

\displaystyle=\delta\cos(ck_{z}/2)

\displaystyle d_{z}

\displaystyle=\Delta+t_{AB}(\cos(bk_{x})+\cos(bk_{y})).

Here
\displaystyle b
and
\displaystyle c
are the dimension of the unit cell in the
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle z
directions. Along lines in the
\displaystyle z
-direction there is a staggered onsite potential
\displaystyle\pm\Delta
and staggered hopping
\displaystyle t\pm\delta

\displaystyle t_{AB}=t_{\parallel,A}-t_{\parallel,B}\ll\Delta
represents the difference between the inter-chain hopping strengths. When
\displaystyle t_{AB}=0

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\tilde{t}=t/\Delta

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}=\delta/\Delta
, and
\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{AB}=t_{AB}/\Delta

The function

is bounded and attains its global maximum at
\displaystyle G(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2})=\pi 3^{-3/2}\approx 0.604
(see equation (4) of the main text).
This shows that the total spectral weight in the two-photon contribution is bounded, i.e. there is a maximum amount of SHG for Rice-Mele models.

Appendix C
Gauge-Invariant Cumulants and a new sum rule

This appendix reviews the theoretical machinery of gauge-invariant cumulants (GICs) that underlies the relationship between ground-state polarization distributions and the frequency-integrated nonlinear response described in the last part of the main text. We restrict the analysis to two-band tight-binding models, relevant for this work, to show that this connection can be made exact.
It generalizes the known relationships between the Berry connection and polarization, as well as the relation between the spread of Wannier functions with linear response. The last part of the supplement shows how this connection gives a guide to construct of models whose spectral weight exceeds the maximum spectral weight possible in the Rice-Mele model
RM82
.

C.1
The polarization distribution

Let us start with the macroscopic polarization of a solid
souza2000polarization
;
zak1989berry
;
king1993theory
;
resta1994macroscopic
;
resta2007theory
;
PhysRevB.49.14202
and Kohn’s theory of the insulating state
kohn1964theory
. Consider a solid with
\displaystyle N
electrons and
\displaystyle M
nuclei in any dimension
\displaystyle d
. The macroscopic polarization operator is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{P}}=\frac{1}{V}\left(e\widehat{\bm{X}}+q_{\text{nuc}}\widehat{\bm{X}}_{\text{nuc}}\right),

(44)

where
\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{X}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}
is the center-of-mass position of the electrons (resp.
\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{\text{nuc}}
of the nuclei). Within the clamped nuclei approximation, the nuclei do not move and we can set
\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{\text{nuc}}=0
by a choice of coordinates. The macroscopic polarization is the expectation
\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\bm{P}}\rangle
.

The GICs are a systematic way to extract further information from
\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{P}}
that we now exploit. Computing
\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\bm{P}}\rangle=\langle\Psi|\widehat{\bm{P}}|\Psi\rangle
, averages over many different centers of charge. Following Souza
et al

souza2000polarization
we define the
distribution
of those centers of charge, i.e. the spatial distribution of polarization, via

p(\bm{X})=\Braket{\Psi|\delta(\widehat{\bm{X}}-\bm{X})|\Psi}.

(45)

This should be interpreted as the probability that the center of charge is exactly at the position
\displaystyle\bm{X}
.
Different electronic properties of the solid have already been directly linked to
\displaystyle p(\bm{X})
, including the Berry connection, localization length of Wannier functions, and the f-sum rule
souza2000polarization
.We can now add the second-harmonic to that list. As a cautionary note, the polarization is distinct from other real-space distributions in solids;
\displaystyle p(\bm{X})
is an different object from the electron density, or the Wannier functions. The subtle relationship between these is discussed carefully by Souza
et. al.

souza2000polarization
.

The great utility of this distribution is tempered by the indirect way it must be computed, a complication due to the fact that the position operator
\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{X}}
is ill-defined

. This implies that, even knowing the exact eigenvectors of the system,
\displaystyle p(X)
cannot be straightforwardly computed and, moreover, it is not immediately clear if
\displaystyle p(X)
is even a well-defined distribution. A key result of Souza
et al
is that
\displaystyle p(X)
can be carefully defined, rendering its cumulants computable.

C.2
Definition of gauge-invariant cumulants

Recall that the first several cumulants
\displaystyle C_{n}
of a
distribution
\displaystyle p(X)
can be written in terms of the moments as

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{1}^{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle X^{i}\rangle

(46)

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{2}^{ij}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle X^{i}X^{j}\rangle-\langle X^{i}\rangle\langle X^{j}\rangle

(47)

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{3}^{ijk}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle X^{i}X^{j}X^{k}\rangle+2\langle X^{i}\rangle\langle X^{j}\rangle\langle X^{k}\rangle

(48)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\langle X^{i}X^{j}\rangle\langle X^{k}\rangle-\langle X^{i}X^{k}\rangle\langle X^{j}\rangle-\langle X^{j}X^{k}\rangle\langle X^{i}\rangle

where
\displaystyle i,j,k\in\left\{x,y,z\right\}
are spatial indices. The first cumulant is the same as the first moment, the second is the variance, and the third cumulant is often called the skew of the distribution. A schematic of the first three cumulants is shown in figure (

).

A convenient way to compute the moments is in terms of the characteristic function

\mathcal{C}(\bm{\alpha})=\langle e^{-i\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{X}}\rangle,

(49)

so that
\displaystyle\langle X^{i}X^{j}\cdots X^{k}\rangle=i\partial_{\alpha_{i}}i\partial_{\alpha_{j}}\cdots i\partial_{\alpha_{k}}C(\bm{\alpha})\big|_{\bm{\alpha}=0}
.
The cumulants are likewise obtained by differentiating the log of the characteristic function

C_{n}^{ij\dots k}=i\partial_{\alpha_{i}}i\partial_{\alpha_{j}}\cdots i\partial_{\alpha_{k}}\ln\mathcal{C}(\bm{\alpha})\big|_{\bm{\alpha}=0}.

(50)

Following
souza2000polarization
, we define

\ln\mathcal{C}(\bm{\alpha})=\frac{V}{\left(2\pi\right)^{d}}\int[d\bm{k}]\;\ln\langle\Psi_{\bm{k}}|e^{-i\bm{\alpha}\cdot\widehat{\bm{X}}}|\Psi_{\bm{k}+\bm{\alpha}}\rangle

(51)

where
\displaystyle V
is the volume of the system,
\displaystyle\int[d\bm{k}]
denotes the normalized integral over the Brillouin zone, and
\displaystyle|\Psi_{k}\rangle
is the many-body wavefunction with boundary conditions twisted by
\displaystyle e^{ikL}
.
In a single-particle description,
\displaystyle|\Psi_{k}\rangle=\prod_{n}u_{\bm{k}n}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}n}|0\rangle
where the Bloch wavefunctions
\displaystyle\psi_{\bm{k}n}(\bm{r})=u_{\bm{k}n}(\bm{r})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}
are chosen to satisfy a smooth, periodic gauge:
\displaystyle\psi_{\bm{k}n}\equiv\psi_{\bm{k}+\bm{G},n}
for reciprocal lattice vectors
\displaystyle\bm{G}
. The cumulants of the polarization distribution can be computed via differentiating (
51
). The first three are

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{1}^{i}\

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\ iV\int[d\bm{k}]\;\operatorname{Tr}\left[c_{1}^{i}\right]

(52)

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{2}^{ij}\

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\ i^{2}V\int[d\bm{k}]\;\operatorname{Tr}\left[c^{ij}_{2}-c_{1}^{i}c_{1}^{j}\right]

(53)

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{3}^{ijk}\

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\ i^{3}V\int[d\bm{k}]\;\operatorname{Tr}\left[c_{3}^{ijk}-c_{2}^{ij}c_{1}^{k}-c_{2}^{jk}c_{1}^{i}-c_{2}^{ki}c_{1}^{j}+2c_{1}^{i}c_{1}^{j}c_{1}^{k}\right]

(54)

where
the trace is over occupied bands, and the
\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{1}^{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=i\langle u_{kn}|\partial_{k_{i}}u_{km}\rangle,

(55)

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{2}^{ij}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=i^{2}\langle u_{kn}|\partial_{k_{i}}\partial_{k_{j}}u_{km}\rangle,

(56)

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{3}^{ijk}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=i^{3}\langle u_{kn}|\partial_{k_{i}}\partial_{k_{j}}\partial_{k_{j}}u_{km}\rangle.

(57)

One can show that the cumulants are gauge-invariant— hence the name — and purely real in the presence of time-reversal symmetry.

To compute the cumulants numerically, it is advantageous to use an alternative formulation (written here for a single occupied band in 1D)

where
\displaystyle k_{i+1}-k_{i}=\Delta k=2\pi/M

\displaystyle\psi_{k}

C.3
Interpretation and physical intuition

Each of the GICs has a dual interpretation: as a measure of the spatial properties of the electrons, or as an electromagnetic response. The first cumulant tells us the mean of the center of charge polarization, i.e. the total deviation of electrons from their companion nuclei
resta1994macroscopic
. Via equation (
44
), the polarization of the solid has a straightforward formula in terms of the first cumulant

\langle\widehat{\bm{P}}\rangle=\frac{e}{V}\bm{C}_{1}=e\sum_{n\text{ occ}}\int[d\bm{k}]\bm{A}_{\bm{k}n},

(59)

where
\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\bm{k}n}
is the Berry connection. The last expression is familiar from the modern theory of polarization. Equation (
59
) shows that, up to dimensionful prefactors, the first cumulant is the electrical response of the system in the absence of an external field, a purely static quantity.

The second cumulant measures the covariance of the polarization distribution. In an insulator, electrons are exponentially localized. Their localization is in the
\displaystyle i\;(=x,y,z)
direction is given by (no summation)
souza2000polarization

\xi_{i}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}C_{2}^{ii}}.

(60)

The second cumulant then gives a great deal of information about the character of the material. In a metal-insulator transition, for instance, the localization length diverges, and hence so does the second cumulant. Physically, the electrons in a metal are almost entirely delocalized, so the polarization distribution should be nearly flat, with variance on the order of the size of the system.

It is useful to contrast the information in
\displaystyle C_{2}
with the Wannier functions. In 1d, the maximally localized Wannier functions have the property that the their centers are governed by the centers of polarization, and their variance is proportional to the squared localization length. We stress, however, that this does
not
imply the Wannier density is the same as the polarization distribution. In dimensions greater than one, there are no unique maximally-localized single-particle Wannier functions, and all Wannier functions have variance strictly larger than the squared localization length.

As an optical response, the second cumulant encodes the all-frequency linear response of the system. A standard application of the flucutation-dissipation theorem shows that the fluctuations in the polarization distribution (
\displaystyle C_{2}
) is related to the total current response of the system:
souza2000polarization

\frac{\pi e^{2}}{V^{2}\hbar}C_{2}^{\alpha\beta}=\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\operatorname{Re}\sigma^{\alpha\beta}(\omega)

(61)

where
\displaystyle\sigma^{\alpha\beta}
is the linear conductivity.

C.4
A New Sum Rule

Now that we have seen the dual nature — spatial and optical — of the first two GICs, it is perhaps not too surprising that the third cumulant can give a non-linear sum rule. The third cumulant measures the skewness of the polarization distribution which, in one dimension, says if the left or right “shoulder” of the distribution is larger.

On the optical side, we find that the second cumulant is related to the second-harmonic response of the system. Given that the first cumulant determines the polarization and the second cumulant gives a linear sum rule, it is perhaps not too surprising that the third cumulant can give a non-linear sum rule. More precisely, we show that for a generic two-band model (see equation (5) of the main text)

\Sigma^{\text{shift}}_{a}=\frac{2\pi e^{3}}{V\hbar^{2}}C_{3}^{a}

(62)

where

\Sigma_{a}^{\text{shift}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\operatorname{Re}\sigma^{aaa}(0;-\omega,\omega)

(63)

and, specializing to the case of two bands,

C_{3}^{a}=-V\int\frac{d^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\operatorname{Im}\left[c_{3}-3c_{1}c_{2}+2c_{1}^{3}\right]

(64)

where
\displaystyle c_{n}=\langle 0|\left(i\partial_{k_{a}}\right)^{n}|0\rangle
for the valence band Bloch wavefunction
\displaystyle|0\rangle=|u_{0}(\bm{k})\rangle
.

Integrating (
25
), the spectral weight of the shift current in a two-band model is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{shift}}_{a}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\int[d\bm{k}]\frac{\operatorname{Im}[\langle 0|\partial_{k}H|1\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}^{2}H|0\rangle]}{(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{0})^{2}},

(65)

where
\displaystyle|0\rangle
and
\displaystyle|1\rangle
denote valence and conduction bands, respectively, and
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}(k)
are the band energies.

The Schrödinger equation and its
\displaystyle k
derivatives give

\displaystyle\displaystyle H|n\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\epsilon_{n}|n\rangle,

(66)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{k}H|n\rangle+H|\partial_{k}n\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\partial_{k}\epsilon_{n}|n\rangle+\epsilon_{n}|\partial_{k}n\rangle,

(67)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{k}^{2}H|n\rangle+\partial_{k}H|\partial_{k}n\rangle+H|\partial_{k}^{2}n\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\partial_{k}^{2}\epsilon_{n}|n\rangle+\partial_{k}\epsilon_{n}|\partial_{k}n\rangle+\epsilon_{n}|\partial_{k}^{2}n\rangle.

(68)

Taking inner products and applying
\displaystyle|0\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle\langle 1|=I
implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle 0|\partial_{k}H|1\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{0})\langle 0|\partial_{k}1\rangle,

(69)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle 1|\partial_{k}^{2}H|0\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{1})[\langle 1|\partial_{k}^{2}0\rangle-2\langle 1|\partial_{k}0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle]+2(\partial_{k}\epsilon_{0}-\partial_{k}\epsilon_{1})\langle 1|\partial_{k}0\rangle.

Substituting these into the integral in the spectral weight yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{0})^{2}}\operatorname{Im}[\langle 0|\partial_{k}H|1\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}^{2}H|0\rangle]

(70)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}\left\{\langle 0|\partial_{k}1\rangle\left[-\langle 1|\partial_{k}^{2}0\rangle+2\langle 1|\partial_{k}0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle]\}-\frac{\partial_{k}\epsilon_{0}-\partial_{k}\epsilon_{1}}{\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{1}}\operatorname{Im}[\langle 0|\partial_{k}1\rangle\langle 1|\partial_{k}0\rangle\right]\right\}.

We can drop the last term since
\displaystyle\langle 0|\partial_{k}1\rangle\langle 1|\partial_{k}0\rangle
is real.
Applying the resolution of the identity, the first term is

\displaystyle\langle 0|\partial_{k}1\rangle\langle 1|\partial_{k}^{2}0\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial_{k}0|\partial_{k}^{2}0\rangle+\langle\partial_{k}0|0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}^{2}0\rangle

(71)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{k}(\langle 0|\partial_{k}^{2}0\rangle)+\langle 0|\partial_{k}^{3}0\rangle-\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}^{2}0\rangle

\displaystyle=\partial_{k}c_{2}+ic_{3}-ic_{1}c_{2},

while the second term becomes

\displaystyle\langle 0|\partial_{k}1\rangle\langle 1|\partial_{k}0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial_{k}0|\partial_{k}0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle+\langle\partial_{k}0|0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle

(72)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{k}(\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle)\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle+\langle 0|\partial_{k}^{2}0\rangle\langle 0|\partial_{k}0\rangle-c_{1}^{3}

\displaystyle=+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}(c_{1}^{2})+ic_{1}c_{2}-ic_{1}^{3}.

The total derivatives vanish after integration, and we obtain equation (
62
).

This sum rule leads to intriguing conclusions. For two-band models, non-linear optical responses can be understood as a facet of the spatial distribution of polarization. This provides a clear physical picture that may be more intuitive than the normal expressions for SHG, which involve k-space sums over virtual transitions.
Moreover, the sum rule can
predict
the shift current — and hence the SHG response — of a material as a ground state property.
