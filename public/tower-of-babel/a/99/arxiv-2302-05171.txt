The Coxeter symmetries of high-level general sequential computation in the invertible-digital and quantum domains
H.E. Bez

Abstract
The article investigates high-level general invertible-sequential processing in the digital and quantum domains. In particular it is shown that: 1. invertible digital-sequential processes, constructed using a standard general-inversion procedure, and 2. sequential quantum processes, determine Coxeter groups. In each case the groups are seen to define all processes that may be constructed from the, given, elemental processes of the sequences. Explicit forms of the presentations of the Coxeter groups are given for all cases. The quantum processes are seen to define unitary representations of the associated Coxeter groups in tensor-product qubit spaces.



1

Introduction and overview

Using the well-known (see, for example,
[
2
]
) general invertible representation

\hat{f}(x,y)=(x,y\oplus f(x)),\text{ where }\oplus\text{ denotes the Boolean exclusive-or operator}

of a Boolean function
f
, a process is defined to step-wise invert a general non-invertible sequence of Boolean functions
f_{n}\circ f_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
. Each of the invertible component functions, which in the general case are denoted by
\phi_{i}
, of the invertible sequence
\phi_{n}\circ\phi_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}
so constructed, is an involution - and a Coxeter group is defined by
\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
. For a
2
-step sequence, i.e., the case
n=2
, the group is shown to be isomorphic to the
8
-element dihedral group
D_{8}
. For
n\geq 3
more general Coxeter groups are generated and their presentations are given for all cases.

In the quantum computing case it is shown, for example, that the quantisation of a
2
-step digital sequence
g(f(x))
, where
f:\B^{n}\rightarrow\B^{m}
and
g:\B^{m}\rightarrow\B^{k}
, leads to a linear representation of
D_{8}
in the unitary group
U(\otimes^{n+m+k}\C^{2})
of the tensor-product space
\otimes^{n+m+k}\C^{2}
.

2

Non-invertible sequential digital processing

The output of a sequential digital process depends on the initial input and the output of a sequence of previous computational steps. A
j
-step process of this type may therefore be specified as follows:

•

pre-process state:
x\in\B^{n}

•

post-process state:
(f_{j}\circ f_{j-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{2}\circ f_{1})(x)
for some functions
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{j}
.

In general such computations are not invertible; i.e., the pre-process state cannot be determined from the post-process state. It is implicit in the above that the functional composition
f_{1}\circ f_{2}\circ\cdots\circ f_{k}
is well-defined. A coded representation of the above may be expressed as:

\begin{array}[]{llllll}x\leftarrow(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{/* initialisation of the pre-process state */}\\
\textbf{begin}\\
\;\;y_{1}\leftarrow f_{1}(x)\\
\;\;y_{2}\leftarrow f_{2}(y_{1})\\
\;\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;y_{j}\leftarrow f_{j}(y_{j-1})\\
\textbf{end}\end{array}

3

Invertible sequential digital processing

3.1

The general-inversion process

For
f:\B^{n}\rightarrow\B^{m}
and
g:\B^{m}\rightarrow\B^{k}
, the functional composition
g\circ f
is defined, but as neither
f
nor
g
is invertible the function
g\circ f
will not be invertible. The general-invertible representations of
f
and
g
are:

\displaystyle\hat{f}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x))

\displaystyle\hat{g}(y,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y,p\oplus g(y))

where
\hat{f}:\B^{n+m}\rightarrow\B^{n+m},\hat{g}:\B^{m+k}\rightarrow\B^{m+k},x\in\B^{n},y\in\B^{m},p\in\B^{k}
. The functions
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}
are invertible representations of
f
and
g
but
\hat{g}\circ\hat{f}
is not defined and is not therefore representative of
2
-step invertible sequential digital processing.

3.2

A functional extension of the general-invertible process

Although
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}
represent individual invertible digital computations we have seen above that their functional composition is, in general, undefined. For sequential processing, functions that combine under the functional composition operator
\circ
are required.

One way of accomplishing an appropriate functional composition of
f
and
g
is to extend the
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}
functions by Cartesian product. This process extends their domains and enables a functional composition, representative of invertible
2
-step digital sequential processes, to be defined. We denote the extended functions by
\phi
and
\gamma
and define them below. The generalisation to
n
-step invertible digital sequences is then clear.

We define for
f
and
g
where
f:\B^{n}\rightarrow\B^{m}
and
g:\B^{m}\rightarrow\B^{k}
the invertible functions

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{f}\times e^{k}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{n}\times\hat{g}

where
e^{q}:\B^{q}\rightarrow\B^{q}
,
q=1,2,\ldots
, are identity functions. We have
\phi:\B^{n+m+k}\rightarrow\B^{n+m+k}
,
\gamma:\B^{n+m+k}\rightarrow\B^{n+m+k}
with:

\displaystyle\phi(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{f}\times e^{k}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\hat{f}(x,y),z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z)

and

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{n}\times\hat{g}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,\hat{g}(y,z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z\oplus g(y))

from which we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma\circ\phi(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y\oplus f(x),z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)))

and

\gamma\circ\phi(x,0,0)=(x,\;f(x),\;g(f(x))).

Hence the result of the non-invertible sequential digital process,
x\rightarrow g(f(x))
, may be obtained, by invertible means, from the 3rd component of
\gamma\circ\phi(x,0,0)
.

-step invertible digital sequences

Throughout we denote by
G(\B^{p})

4.1

Properties of the extended functions
\phi
and
\gamma

Lemma 1
.

The extended functions
\phi=\hat{f}\times e^{k}
and
\gamma=e^{n}\times\hat{g}
are both self-inverse.

Proof.

We have
\phi(x,y,z)=(x,y\oplus f(x),z)
hence

\displaystyle\phi\circ\phi(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(x,y\oplus f(x),z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,(y\oplus f(x))\oplus f(x),z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z)

Similarly for
\gamma
.
∎

Hence
\phi\in G(\B^{n+m+k})
and
\gamma\in G(\B^{n+m+k})
. It follows that the functional compositions
\phi\circ\gamma
and
\gamma\circ\phi
are also elements of the group
G(\B^{n+m+k})
; their properties, which are required to establish the main results of this article, are discussed below.

Properties of the composite functions
\gamma\circ\phi
and
\phi\circ\gamma

Lemma 2
.

Each is the inverse of the other, i.e.,

Proof.

Follows trivially from the fact that

and

.

Lemma 3

\gamma\circ\phi(x,y,z)=(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)))

Proof.

See section

∎

Lemma 4

\phi\circ\gamma\circ\phi(x,y,z)=(x,y,z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)))

Proof.

From Lemma

\displaystyle\phi\circ\gamma\circ\phi(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\phi(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)))

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x)\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))).

Lemma 5

(\gamma\circ\phi)^{2}=(x,y,z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))\oplus g(y))
.

Proof.

From Lemma

\displaystyle(\gamma\circ\phi)^{2}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y,z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))\oplus g(y))

Lemma 6
.

\phi\circ(\gamma\circ\phi)^{2}=(x,y\oplus f(x),[z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))]\oplus g(y))
.

Proof.

From Lemma
5
we have

\displaystyle\phi\circ(\gamma\circ\phi)^{2}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(x,y,z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))\oplus g(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))\oplus g(y))

∎

Lemma 7
.

(\gamma\circ\phi)^{3}(x,y,z)=(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y))
.

Proof.

Applying
\gamma
to the outcome of Lemma
6
gives:

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y^{+},z^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y^{+},z^{+}\oplus g(y^{+}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),([z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))]\oplus g(y))\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),([z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)]\oplus g(y))))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y)).

∎

Lemma 8
.

\phi\circ(\gamma\circ\phi)^{3}=\gamma
.

Proof.

Pre-multiplying the result of Lemma
8
by
\phi
we obtain:

\displaystyle\phi\circ(\gamma\circ\phi)^{3}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z\oplus g(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y,z).

∎

Denoting the identity function of
G(\B^{n+m+k})
by
e
we have:

Lemma 9
.

(\gamma\circ\phi)^{4}=e
,

Proof.

From Lemma
8
we have

.
∎

Equivalently

is of order

in the group
G(\B^{n+m+k})
. Given that the inverse of
\gamma\circ\phi

\phi\circ\gamma
we have:

Corollary 1
.

\displaystyle\phi\circ\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\gamma\circ\phi)^{3},

\displaystyle(\phi\circ\gamma)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\gamma\circ\phi)^{2},

\displaystyle(\phi\circ\gamma)^{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\circ\phi,

\displaystyle(\phi\circ\gamma)^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e.

and, hence:

Proposition 1
.

The elements
\gamma\circ\phi
and

Immediate from Lemma

.
∎

Proof.

4.3

The group generated by the functions
\phi
and
\gamma

The cyclic group
C_{\gamma,\phi}
is not the largest subgroup of
G(\B^{n+m+k})
generated by
\phi
and
\gamma
. We have:

Lemma 10
.

The functions
\phi=(\hat{f}\times e)
and
\gamma=(e\times\hat{g})
generate the
8
-element sub-group

G_{2s}=\{e,\phi,\gamma,\phi\circ\gamma,(\phi\circ\gamma)^{2},(\phi\circ\gamma)^{3},\phi\circ(\gamma\circ\phi),\gamma\circ(\phi\circ\gamma)\}

of
G(\B^{n+m+k})
.

Proof.

The functions
\phi
and
\gamma
clearly generate the elements of
G_{2s}
. The closure of
G_{\phi,\gamma}
under both inversion and product, are easily verified from the properties of
\phi,\gamma
, and their products, established above.
∎

Lemma 11
.

The subgroup
G_{2s}\subset G(\B^{n+m+k})
, defined in Lemma
10
, is isomorphic to the dihedral group
D_{8}
.

Proof.

Following, for example, Ledermann
[
3
]
the dihedral group
D_{8}
may be defined by:

\{e,A,A^{2},A^{3},B,AB,A^{2}B,A^{3}B\}

where
A
and
B
are such that

A^{4}=B^{2}=(AB)^{2}=e.

With the identifications

A=\phi\circ\gamma\text{ and }B=\gamma

the conditions
A^{4}=B^{2}=(AB)^{2}=e
are satisfied and we have:

\displaystyle\{e,A,A^{2},A^{3},B,AB,A^{2}B,A^{3}B\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{e,\phi\circ\gamma,(\phi\circ\gamma)^{2},(\phi\circ\gamma)^{3},\gamma,\phi,\phi\circ(\gamma\circ\phi),\gamma\circ(\phi\circ\gamma)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{2s}.

∎

We note that the group
G_{2s}
may also be expressed as

G_{2s}=\{e,\phi,\gamma,\gamma\circ\phi,(\gamma\circ\phi)^{2},(\gamma\circ\phi)^{3},\phi\circ(\gamma\circ\phi),\gamma\circ(\phi\circ\gamma)\}

and, from the digital-computational point of view, the elements
\phi,\gamma
and
\gamma\circ\phi
are sufficient to represent:

1.

the non-invertible computations defined by
f
and
g
, and

2.

the
2
-step sequence, or functional composition,
g\circ f

by invertible means. However
\{\phi,\gamma,\gamma\circ\phi\}
is not a subgroup of
G_{2s}
, and
\gamma\circ\phi
isn’t the only invertible digital sequence that may be constructed from the elements
\gamma
and
\phi
. The group
G_{2s}
is clearly representative of the complete set of invertible digital sequences (or invertible ‘programs’) that may be constructed from the invertible components
\phi
and
\gamma
.

4.4

Coxeter groups

In terms of group presentation the group
G_{2s}
, hence also
D_{8}
, may be defined by:

G_{2s}\equiv\langle A,B|A^{4}=B^{2}=(AB)^{2}=e\rangle

i.e., the set of strings over
\{A,B\}
consistent with the relations
A^{4}=B^{2}=(AB)^{2}=e
.
The Coxeter groups have been widely studied following their introduction by H. S. M. Coxeter in 1934
[
1
]
. They have applications in many areas of mathematics and are defined by presentation. We have for
n>1
in
\N
and
1<i,j\leq n
the Coxeter group generated by the elements
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
has the presentation:

\langle s_{1},\ldots s_{n}|(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=e\rangle

where, for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots n\}
, we have
m_{ij}\in\N
where
m_{ii}=1
and for
i\neq j
we have
m_{ij}>1
and
m_{ij}=m_{ji}

The dihedral groups
D_{2n}
are special cases of Coxeter groups with
n=2
. As a Coxeter group
G_{2s}
has the presentation:

\displaystyle G_{2s}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle s_{1},s_{2}|s_{1}s_{1}=e,s_{2}s_{2}=e,(s_{1}s_{2})^{4}=e\rangle,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\phi,\gamma|\phi^{2}=e,\;\gamma^{2}=e,\;(\phi\circ\gamma)^{4}=e\rangle,

i.e.,
m_{12}=m_{21}=4
.

5

The Coxeter groups of general
n
-step invertible digital sequences

5.1

The case of
3
sequential steps

In the case of
3
-step invertible digital computations we have, from the general invertible process, the
3
independent involutions,
\phi,\gamma,\rho
, defined by:

\displaystyle\phi(x,y,z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z,t)

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y,z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z\oplus g(y),t)

\displaystyle\rho(x,y,z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z,t\oplus h(z))

from the non-invertible digital functions
f,g
and
h
for which the functional product
h(g(f(x)))
is well-defined. We obtain

\displaystyle\rho\circ\gamma\circ\phi(x,y,z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho\circ\gamma(x,y\oplus f(x),z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)),t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)),t\oplus h(z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)))).

and have

\rho\circ\gamma\circ\phi(x,0,0,0)=(x,\;f(x),\;g(f(x)),\;h(g(f(x))))

i.e., the outcome of the digital sequence
h(g(f(x)))
, available in the last component of the above, is computed by invertible means.

It is straightforward to show that the functions
\phi,\gamma,\rho
satisfy the relationships:

\displaystyle\phi^{2}=\gamma^{2}=\rho^{2}=e,\;(\phi\circ\gamma)^{4}=e,\;(\gamma\circ\rho)^{4}=e,\;(\phi\circ\rho)^{2}=e

from which it follows that the group generated by
\phi,\gamma,\rho
is the Coxeter group with presentation:

G_{3s}\equiv\langle\phi,\gamma,\rho|\phi^{2}=e,\gamma^{2}=e,\rho^{2}=e,(\phi\circ\gamma)^{4}=e,(\gamma\circ\rho)^{4}=e,(\phi\circ\rho)^{2}=e\rangle.

5.2

The case of
n
sequential steps

For the
n
-step non invertible digital sequence
x\rightarrow f_{n}\circ f_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{2}\circ f_{1}(x)
we define, from the general invertible construction, the invertible functions

\hat{f}_{1},\hat{f}_{2},\ldots\hat{f}_{n}
and their extensions

\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{n}

such that the product

\phi_{n}\circ\phi_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}

is a well-defined functional product for the computing the non-invertible sequence

f_{n}\circ f_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{2}\circ f_{1}

by invertible means. The group
G_{ns}
for the
n
-step invertible digital sequence is the Coxeter group defined by the presentation:

G_{ns}\equiv\langle\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}|\phi_{1}^{2}=\phi_{2}^{2}=\cdots=\phi_{n}^{2}=e;\;(\phi_{k}\circ\phi_{k+1})^{4}=e;\;\text{else }(\phi_{p}\circ\phi_{q})^{2}=e\rangle

i.e., when
q\neq p+1
we have
(\phi_{p}\circ\phi_{q})^{2}=e
. Equivalently the presentation of
G_{ns}
may be expressed as:

\langle\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}|R_{1}\cup R_{2}\cup R_{3}\rangle

where

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{2}=\phi_{2}^{2}=\cdots=\phi_{n}^{2}=e

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{k}\circ\phi_{k+1})^{4}=e

\displaystyle R_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{p}\circ\phi_{q})^{2}=e\;\;\text{when}\;\;|q-p|\neq 1.

6

Quantum sequential processing

6.1

\B^{n+m+k}
as a
G_{2s}
-space

From sections
3
and
4
we have an action of
G_{2s}
, equvalently
D_{8}
, on
\B^{n+m+k}
defined by:

\displaystyle\phi(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z)

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z\oplus g(y))

\displaystyle\gamma\circ\phi(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))

\displaystyle(\gamma\circ\phi)^{2}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))\oplus g(y))

\displaystyle(\gamma\circ\phi)^{3}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),z\oplus g(y))

\displaystyle\phi\circ(\gamma\circ\phi)(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y,z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x)))

\displaystyle\gamma\circ(\phi\circ\gamma)(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x,y\oplus f(x),(z\oplus g(y))\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))).

From this we define corresponding actions on the basis elements:

B_{\otimes^{n+m+k}\C^{2}}=\{|x\rangle|y\rangle|z\rangle:x\in\B^{n},y\in\B^{m},z\in\B^{k}\}

of
\otimes^{n+m+k}\C^{2}
by

\displaystyle U_{\phi}|x\rangle|y\rangle|z\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x\rangle|y\oplus f(x)\rangle|z\rangle

\displaystyle U_{\gamma}|x\rangle|y\rangle|z\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x\rangle|y\rangle|z\oplus g(y)\rangle

\displaystyle U_{\gamma\circ\phi}|x\rangle|y\rangle|z\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x\rangle|y\oplus f(x)\rangle|z\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))\rangle

\displaystyle U_{\gamma\circ(\phi\circ\gamma)}|x\rangle|y\rangle|z\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x\rangle|y\oplus f(x)\rangle|(z\oplus g(y))\oplus g(y\oplus f(x))\rangle

the linear extensions of which, from
B_{\otimes^{n+m+k}\C^{2}}
to the whole of
\otimes^{n+m+k}\C^{2}
, determine unitary operators.

We note that:

U_{\gamma\circ\phi}|x\rangle|0\rangle|0\rangle=|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle|g(f(x))\rangle

which is a quantum implementation of the digital sequence
g(f(x))
. The value of
g(f(x))
being obtained from
U_{\gamma\circ\phi}|x\rangle|0\rangle|0\rangle
by measurement of the
k
qubits
|g(f(x))\rangle
.

From the above we also have

U_{\gamma\circ\phi}=U_{\gamma}U_{\phi}

and

Proposition 2
.

For
\psi\in G_{2s}
the mapping
\psi\rightarrow U_{\psi}
is a unitary representation of the group
G_{2s}
in the unitary group of the tensor-product space
\otimes^{n+m+k}\C^{2}
.

The quantisation of
n
-step digital sequences leads to similar conclusions.

7

Conclusion

Coxeter groups have been shown to occur in both high-level,
n
-step, invertible digital sequential processing and in a quantum representation of sequential processing. The groups, in both cases, have been shown to be representative of the set of all invertible processes that may be constructed from the given set of processing steps.

It has also been shown that, in the quantum case, particular unitary representations of the Coxeter groups occur; the general nature and properties of these representations is not known to the author.
