1.
Introduction

The maximal operator for the Schrödinger equation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has been studied extensively. In particular, the pointwise convergence of solutions to the linear Schrödinger equation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has been established for initial data in Sobolev spaces with sharp range of exponents
[
DZ19
]
.

For the Schrödinger equation on the one-dimensional torus, some partial results on the
L^{p}
estimates of the maximal operators were obtained.
[
MV08
]
proved that

\|\sup_{0<t<1}|\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}e(2\pi(nx+n^{2}t))|\|_{L^{6}([0,1])}\leq C_{\epsilon}N^{1/3+\epsilon}(\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2})^{1/2},

which is sharp up to a
C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}
factor. Some estimates for higher dimensional tori were obtained in
[
WZ19
,
CLS21
]
.

It is conjectured that for every
\epsilon>0,
there exists a constant
C_{\epsilon}>0
such that

(1)

\left\|\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e\left(x\frac{n}{N}+t\frac{n^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)\right|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N])}\leq C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{1}{2}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}},

where
b_{n}
are complex numbers. To recall,
e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}.

It is shown in
[
Bar20
]
that (
1
) holds when
b_{n}=1
for every
n.

Similarly we could consider the maximal operator in the
x
direction, and it seems plausible to conjecture that for every
\epsilon>0,
there exists a constant
C_{\epsilon}
such that

(2)

\left\|\sup_{x\in[0,N]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e\left(x\frac{n}{N}+t\frac{n^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)\right|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N^{2}])}\leq C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{1}{2}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}},

where
b_{n}
are complex numbers.

In this paper we describe some difficulties of this problem by showing both (
1
) and (
2
) cannot hold when we replace
\{\frac{n^{2}}{N^{2}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
by some uniformly convex sequences, whose definition we now give.

Definition 1.1
.

We say a sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
(with parameter
N
) is uniformly convex if it satisfies

(3)

a_{n+1}-a_{n}\in\left[\frac{1}{4N},\frac{4}{N}\right]

(4)

(a_{n+2}-a_{n+1})-(a_{n+1}-a_{n})\in\left[\frac{1}{4N^{2}},\frac{4}{N^{2}}\right].

This definition coincides with the definition of generalized Dirichlet sequences in
[
FGM21
]
. We use the name of uniformly convex sequence in this paper in hope of better clarity in this context.

We will construct examples of uniformly convex sequences to show the following two theorems.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist uniformly convex sequences (for every parameter
N\geq 10
)
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N},
and complex sequences
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
such that

(5)

\left\|\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e\left(x\frac{n}{N}+ta_{n}\right)\right|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N])}\geq CN^{\frac{7}{12}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}

for every
N\geq 10.

Moreover, for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
C_{\epsilon}>0
such that for any uniformly convex sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N},
and any complex sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
we have

(6)

\left\|\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e\left(x\frac{n}{N}+ta_{n}\right)\right|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N])}\leq C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{7}{12}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exist uniformly convex sequences (for every parameter
N\geq 10
)
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N},
and complex sequences
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
such that

(7)

\left\|\sup_{x\in[0,N]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e\left(x\frac{n}{N}+ta_{n}\right)\right|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N^{2}])}\geq CN^{\frac{5}{8}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}

for every
N\geq 10.

Moreover, for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
C_{\epsilon}>0
such that for any uniformly convex sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N},
and any complex sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
we have

(8)

\left\|\sup_{x\in[0,N]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e\left(x\frac{n}{N}+ta_{n}\right)\right|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N^{2}])}\leq C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{2}{3}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

The proof of the first parts of the theorems above relies on a family of examples of uniformly convex sequences having large intersections with an arithmetic progression.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every
\alpha\in[1/2,2],
there exists a uniformly convex sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
for every
N\geq 10
such that
|\{a_{n}\}\cap N^{-\alpha}\mathbb{Z}|\gtrsim N^{\frac{\alpha+1}{3}}.

For every
\alpha\in[0,1/2],
there exists a uniformly convex sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
for every
N\geq 10
such that
|\{a_{n}\}\cap N^{-\alpha}\mathbb{Z}|\gtrsim N^{\alpha}.

Conversely, for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
C_{\epsilon}>0
such that for every uniformly convex sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and every
\alpha\in[0,2],
we have

(9)

|\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\cap N^{-\alpha}\mathbb{Z}|\leq\begin{cases}C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{\alpha+1}{3}}&\text{ if }\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2},2],\\
100N^{\alpha}&\text{ if }\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]\end{cases}.

The inequality (
9
) is Corollary 4.7 of
[
FGM21
]
(when
\theta=1
). The construction of example part of Theorem
1.4
will be proved by Theorem
4.5
below. So the examples that we construct show that (
9
) is sharp up to a
C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}
factor.

A,B,
we use
A\lesssim B
to denote the statement
A\leq CB
for some constant
C.

A\lesssim_{q}B
will denote the statement that
A\leq C_{q}B
for some constant
C_{q}

A\sim B
means
A\lesssim B
and

x\in\mathbb{R}

, let

.

2.
The
L^{4}_{x}L^{\infty}_{t}
estimate

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
.

2.1.
Proof of (
5
)

Let
\{c_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be a uniformly convex sequence from Theorem
1.4
with the property that
|\{c_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\cap N^{-1}\mathbb{Z}|\gtrsim N^{2/3}.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be the sequence with
a_{n}=c_{n}-\frac{n}{N^{2}}.

Then
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
is a uniformly convex sequence because the sequence
\{-\frac{n}{N^{2}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
is an arithmetic progression with common difference
-\frac{1}{N^{2}}
.

Let

f(x,t)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq N:c_{n}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z}}e(\frac{n}{N}x+a_{n}t),

that is, in (
5
) we are taking
b_{n}=1
if
Nc_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b_{n}=0
otherwise.
We check that when
c_{n}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z},
we have

(\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\cdot(j,jN)\in\mathbb{Z}

for every
1\leq j\leq N
with
j\in\mathbb{Z}.
Indeed when
c_{n}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z},

(10)

\displaystyle(\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\cdot(j,jN)

\displaystyle=\frac{jn}{N}+jNa_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{jn}{N}+jN(c_{n}-\frac{n}{N^{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{jn}{N}+jNc_{n}-\frac{jn}{N}

\displaystyle=jNc_{n}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{Z}.

Therefore we have shown that

(11)

f(j,jN)=\#\{n\in\mathbb{N}:1\leq n\leq N,c_{n}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z}\}\gtrsim N^{2/3}.

The calculation in (
10
) in fact shows that for every
1\leq j\leq N
with
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, if
|(x,t)-(j,jN)|\leq\frac{1}{100}
we have

(12)

|f((x,t))|\gtrsim N^{2/3}.

Indeed, for every
1\leq n\leq N
with
c_{n}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z},
if
|(j,jN)-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100}
,

\displaystyle d\left((\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\cdot(x,t),\mathbb{Z}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\left((\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\cdot(j,jN),\mathbb{Z}\right)+\left|(\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\right||(j,jN)-(x,t)|

\displaystyle\leq 0+2|(j,jN)-(x,t)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{50}.

Therefore we have when
|(j,jN)-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100}
,

\mathrm{Re}(f(x,t))\gtrsim\frac{1}{100}\#\{n\in\mathbb{N}:1\leq n\leq N,c_{n}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z}\}\gtrsim N^{2/3},

which implies

(13)

|f(x,t)|\gtrsim N^{2/3}

when
|(j,jN)-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100}
for
1\leq j\leq N.

Because of (
13
), we have shown

\left\{(x,t):|(j,jN)-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100},1\leq j\leq N\right\}\subset\{(x,t):|f(x,t)|\gtrsim N^{2/3}\},

which implies for every
1\leq j\leq N
, if
x\in[j-\frac{1}{100},j+\frac{1}{100}]
then

\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}|f(x,t)|\gtrsim N^{2/3}.

Therefore,

\|\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}|f(x,t)|\|_{L^{4}([0,N])}\gtrsim N^{2/3}N^{1/4}=N^{7/12}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

This concludes the proof of (
5
).

2.2.
Proof of (
6
)

Throughout this subsection, let
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be a uniformly convex sequence, and let
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be a sequence of complex numbers.
The proof will utilize the
\ell^{2}L^{6}
decoupling inequality of Bourgain and Demeter
[
BD15
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
BD15
]
)
.

Suppose
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
C^{2}
and satisfies
|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq 4
,
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\in[\frac{1}{4},4]
for
x\in[0,1].
Let
\Theta
be a collection of disjoint subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by
\{\theta:\theta=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x\in[\frac{j}{N},\frac{j+1}{N}),|y-f(x)|\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\}\}.
Suppose
f
has Fourier transform supported on
\bigcup_{\theta\in\Theta}\theta,
and let
f_{\theta}
denote the Fourier projection
\int_{\theta}\hat{f}(\xi)e(x\cdot\xi)d\xi.
For every
\epsilon>0,
there exists
C_{\epsilon}
such that

\|f\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\|f_{\theta}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2})^{1/2}

for every
f
with
\mathrm{supp}\hat{f}\subset\bigcup_{\theta\in\Theta}\theta.

Another ingredient we need is the locally constant property. First we set up some notations. The functions we define below are in
1
or
2
dimensions, that is,
n=1,2.

Let
w(y)
denote the function

w(y)=\frac{1}{(1+d(y,[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{n}))^{100}}.

So
w(y)
is a smooth positive function which decays outside the
n
-dimensional cube
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{n}
. For a unit cube
Y
centered at
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
we write
w_{Y}(y)
to denote the function
w(y-z),
which decays outside the cube
Y.
For
X,
a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n},
we let
\bigsqcup Y
be the minimal covering of
X
by lattice unit cubes, and write
w_{X}(y)
to denote the function
\sum_{Y}w_{Y}(y).
For a single point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
we let
w_{x}(y)
denote the function
w_{\{x\}}(y).
We write
\|f\|_{L^{p}(w_{X})}
to denote the integral
(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(y)|^{p}w_{X}(y)dy)^{1/p}.

The following locally constant property is well-known (see e.g.
[
Dem20
]
).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
n=1,2.
Suppose
f
has Fourier transform supported in
B_{10}(0).
Then for
1\leq q<p\leq\infty,

\|f\|_{L^{p}(Y)}\lesssim_{p,q}(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f|^{q}w_{Y})^{\frac{1}{q}}.

Theorem
2.1
has a local version (for example can be found as Theorem 5.1 in
[
BD17
]
), which states that

(14)

\|f\|_{L^{6}(w_{B_{N^{2}}})}\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\|f_{\theta}\|_{L^{6}(w_{B_{N^{2}}})}^{2})^{1/2}

for every
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{supp}\hat{f}\subset\bigcup_{\theta\in\Theta}\theta.

Now we are ready to prove (
6
).
Denote the exponential sum
\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e(\frac{n}{N}x+a_{n}t)
by
f(x,t).
For every
\alpha>0,

let
U_{\alpha}
denote the level set

(15)

U_{\alpha}:=\{(x,t)\in[0,N]\times[0,N^{2}]:|f(x,t)|\in[\alpha/2,\alpha)\}.

The following proposition, which together with a dyadic pigeonholing argument will imply (
6
).

Proposition 2.3
.

For every
\alpha>0
we have

\alpha^{4}|\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim_{\epsilon}C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{7}{3}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{4}

where
\pi_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the projection along the
t
-axis.

Proof.

For every
x\in\pi_{t}U_{\alpha},
let
t(x)\in[0,N^{2}]
be such that

|f(x,t(x))|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}.

By the definition of
\pi_{t}U_{\alpha},
we have

(16)

\alpha^{6}|\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim\int_{\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}}|f(x,t(x))|^{6}dx.

Because of Proposition
2.2
, we have

|f(x,t(x))|\lesssim(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{t(x)}(t)dt)^{1/6},

combining which with (
16
) yields

\alpha^{6}|\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim\int_{\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{t(x)}(t)dxdt.

Noting the pointwise estimate

1_{\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}}(x)w_{t(x)}(t)\lesssim w_{U_{\alpha}}(x,t),

we obtain

\alpha^{6}|\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{U_{\alpha}}(x,t)dxdt\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{[0,N]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)dxdt.

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{[(j-1)N,jN]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)\lesssim w_{[0,N^{2}]\times[0,N^{2}]},
and the exponential sum
f(x,t)
is
N
-periodic in
x
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{[0,N]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)dxdt\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{[0,N^{2}]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)dxdt.

Since
f(x,t)
has Fourier support on the parabola, by (
14
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{[0,N^{2}]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|b_{n}e(\frac{n}{N}x+a_{n}t)\|_{L^{6}(w_{B_{N^{2}}})}^{2})^{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\epsilon}N^{4}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{6}.

Hence, we have

(17)

\alpha^{6}|\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{3}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{6}.

On the other hand we have the trivial bound

(18)

|\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}|\leq N.

Combining (
17
) and (
18
) we obtain

\alpha^{4}|\pi_{t}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim_{\epsilon}C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{7}{3}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{4}.

∎

To conclude (
6
), we combine Proposition
2.3
with a dyadic pigeonholing argument.

Proof of (
6
).

Without loss of generality we assume
\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}=1.
Then by the triangle inequality,
|f(x,t)|<N^{50}.

So we may write

(19)

\left\|\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}|f(x,t)|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N])}^{4}\\
\leq\sum_{\alpha\in[N^{-100},N^{100}],\,\alpha\text{ dyadic}}\left\|\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e\left(x\frac{n}{N}+ta_{n}\right)\right|\right\|_{L^{4}(\pi_{t}U_{\alpha})}^{4}+E

where

E=\int_{x\in[0,N],\,\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}|f(x,t)|\leq N^{-100}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}|f(x,t)|\right)^{4}dx\leq NN^{-400}\leq N^{-100}.

By Proposition
2.3
we have

\left\|\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e\left(x\frac{n}{N}+ta_{n}\right)\right|\right\|_{L^{4}(\pi_{t}U_{\alpha})}^{4}\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{7}{3}}

for every
\alpha>0.

Since the number of dyadic
\alpha
in
[N^{-100},N^{100}]
is
O(\log N),
we conclude from (
19
) that

\left\|\sup_{t\in[0,N^{2}]}|f(x,t)|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N])}^{4}\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{7}{3}}.

∎

3.1.
Proof of (
7
)

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be a uniformly convex sequence from Theorem
1.4
with the property that
|\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\cap N^{-1/2}\mathbb{Z}|\gtrsim N^{1/2}.

Let

f(x,t)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq N:a_{n}\in N^{-1/2}\mathbb{Z}}e\left(\frac{n}{N}x+a_{n}t\right),

that is, in (
7
) we are taking
b_{n}=1
if
N^{1/2}a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b_{n}=0
otherwise.
When
a_{n}\in N^{-1/2}\mathbb{Z}
we have

(\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\cdot(0,jN^{1/2})\in\mathbb{Z}

for every
1\leq j\leq N^{2}
with
j\in\mathbb{Z}.

Therefore

(20)

f(0,jN^{1/2})=\#\{n\in\mathbb{N}:1\leq n\leq N,a_{n}\in N^{-1/2}\mathbb{Z}\}\gtrsim N^{1/2}.

In fact, for every
1\leq j\leq N^{3/2}
, when
|(x,t)-(0,jN^{1/2})|\leq\frac{1}{100}
we have

(21)

|f((x,t))|\gtrsim N^{1/2}.

Indeed, for every
1\leq n\leq N
with
a_{n}\in N^{-1/2}\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle d\left((\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\cdot(x,t),\mathbb{Z}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\left((\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\cdot(0,jN),\mathbb{Z}\right)+\left|(\frac{n}{N},a_{n})\right||(0,jN^{1/2})-(x,t)|

\displaystyle\leq 0+2|(0,jN^{1/2})-(x,t)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{50}

if
|(0,jN^{1/2})-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100}.
Therefore we have when
|(0,jN^{1/2})-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100}
,

\mathrm{Re}(f(x,t))\gtrsim\frac{1}{100}\#\{n\in\mathbb{N}:1\leq n\leq N,a_{n}\in N^{-1/2}\mathbb{Z}\}\gtrsim N^{1/2},

which implies

(22)

|f(x,t)|\gtrsim N^{1/2}

when
|(0,jN^{1/2})-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100}
for
1\leq j\leq N^{3/2}.

Because of (
22
), we have shown

\left\{(x,t):|(0,jN^{1/2})-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100},1\leq j\leq N^{3/2}\right\}\subset\{(x,t):|f(x,t)|\gtrsim N^{1/2}\}

which implies

\sup_{x\in[0,N]}|f(x,t)|\gtrsim N^{1/2}

if
t\in[jN^{1/2}-\frac{1}{100},jN^{1/2}+\frac{1}{100}]
for every
1\leq j\leq N^{3/2}.

Therefore,

\left\|\sup_{x\in[0,N]}|f(x,t)|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N^{2}])}\gtrsim N^{1/2}N^{3/8}=N^{5/8}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

This concludes the proof of (
7
).

3.2.
Proof of (

)

Recall
U_{\alpha}
is the level set of
f(x,t)
defined in (

).

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
\alpha>0

\alpha^{4}|\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim_{\epsilon}C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{8}{3}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{4}

where
\pi_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

Proof.

For every
t\in\pi_{x}U_{\alpha},
let
x(t)\in[0,N]
be such that

|f(x(t),t)|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}.

By the definition of
\pi_{x}U_{\alpha},
we have

(23)

\alpha^{6}|\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim\int_{\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}}|f(x(t),t)|^{6}dt.

Because of Proposition
2.2
, we have

|f(x(t),t)|\lesssim(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{x(t)}(x)dx)^{1/6},

combining which with (
23
) yields

\alpha^{6}|\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim\int_{\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{x(t)}(x)dxdt.

Noting the pointwise estimate

1_{\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}}(t)w_{x(t)}(x)\lesssim w_{U_{\alpha}}(x,t),

we obtain

\alpha^{6}|\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{U_{\alpha}}(x,t)dxdt\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{[0,N]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)dxdt.

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{[(j-1)N,jN]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)\lesssim w_{[0,N^{2}]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t),
and the exponential sum
f(x,t)
is
N
-periodic in
x
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{[0,N]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)dxdt\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{[0,N^{2}]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)dxdt.

Since
f(x,t)
has Fourier support on the parabola, by (
14
) we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x,t)|^{6}w_{[0,N^{2}]\times[0,N^{2}]}(x,t)dxdt\lesssim_{\epsilon}C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{4}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{6}.

Hence, we have

(24)

\alpha^{6}|\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{3}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{6}.

On the other hand we have the trivial bound

(25)

|\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}|\leq N^{2}.

Combining (
24
) and (
25
) we obtain

\alpha^{4}|\pi_{x}U_{\alpha}|\lesssim_{\epsilon}C_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{8}{3}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{4}

∎

Proof of (
8
).

Without loss of generality we assume
\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}=1
. Similarly as in the proof of (
6
), Proposition
3.1
yields

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\in[0,N]}|f(x,t)|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N^{2}])}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in[N^{-100},N^{100}],\\
\alpha\ \text{dyadic}\end{subarray}}\left\|\sup_{x\in[0,N]}|f(x,t)|\right\|_{L^{4}(\pi_{x}U_{\alpha})}^{4}+N^{-100}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\epsilon}(\log N)N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{8}{3}}+N^{-100}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{8}{3}},

which completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Remark

Unlike Theorem
1.2
, we were not able to show a corresponding inequality where the example yielding (
5
) is essentially sharp. However, if we consider the domain
(x,t)\in[0,N^{2}]^{2}
instead of
[0,N]\times[0,N^{2}],
and we are also allowed to perturb the “periodic” term
\frac{n}{N}
, then we do have a “complete” version of Theorem
1.3
.

To be precise, we let
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be a uniformly convex sequence from Theorem
1.4
with the property that
|\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\cap N^{-1}\mathbb{Z}|\gtrsim N^{2/3}.
Let
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by

(26)

v_{n}=(\frac{n}{N}-\frac{a_{n}}{N},a_{n}).

Proposition 3.2
.

For every
N\geq 10,
there exists a complex sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
such that

(27)

\left\|\sup_{x\in[0,N^{2}]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}e((x,t)\cdot v_{n})\right|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N^{2}])}\geq CN^{\frac{5}{6}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}

where
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
is defined in (
26
).

Proof.

Let

f(x,t):=\sum_{1\leq n\leq N,a_{n}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z}}e\left((x,t)\cdot v_{n}\right),

i.e., take
b_{n}=1
for
n
such that
Na_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b_{n}=0
otherwise (so
\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}\sim N^{1/3}
).
For every
1\leq j\leq N^{2}
with
j\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\displaystyle v_{n}\cdot(jN,j)

\displaystyle=(\frac{n}{N}-\frac{a_{n}}{N},a_{n})\cdot(jN,j)

\displaystyle=jn-ja_{n}+ja_{n}

\displaystyle=jn

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{Z}.

So

|f(jN,j)|=\#\{n\in\mathbb{N}:1\leq n\leq N,\quad a_{n}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z}\}\gtrsim N^{2/3}.

By the same argument in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we actually have

|f(x,t)|\gtrsim N^{2/3}

when
|(jN,j)-(x,t)|\leq\frac{1}{100}
for
1\leq j\leq N^{2}.

Therefore, we have

\sup_{x\in[0,N^{2}]^{2}}|f(x,t)|\gtrsim N^{2/3}

if
t\in[j-\frac{1}{100},j+\frac{1}{100}]
for every
1\leq j\leq N^{2}.

We conclude

\left\|\sup_{x\in[0,N^{2}]}|f(x,t)|\right\|_{L^{4}([0,N^{2}])}\geq CN^{\frac{2}{3}}N^{\frac{1}{2}}=CN^{\frac{5}{6}}\|b_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

∎

4.
Construction of examples

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
C\geq 1
be a constant. Let
x_{i},y_{i},p_{i}
be increasing sequences of real numbers. Let
\Delta x_{i}=x_{i+1}-x_{i}
, and define
\Delta y_{i},\Delta p_{i}
analogously. Suppose
\Delta p_{i}\sim_{C}\Delta x_{i}
and
\frac{\Delta y_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}-p_{i}\sim p_{i+1}-\frac{\Delta y_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}
.

(1)

There exists a strictly convex
C^{1}
function
f
such that
f(x_{i})=y_{i}
,
f^{\prime}(x_{i})=p_{i}
, and
f^{\prime}
is piecewise linear with slope
\sim_{C}1
.

(2)

Let
D
be
\frac{\pi}{4}
times the inf of slopes in (a). We can further modify
f
to be a convex
C^{2}
function with
f^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})=D
for all
i
and
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\sim_{C}1
for all
x\in(x_{1},x_{n})
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the case
n=2
for both (a) and (b); the general case is obtained by concatenation (note that the hypotheses ensure continuity at the boundary). We approach (a) first. Let
s=\frac{y_{2}-y_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{1}}
and
c=\frac{s-p_{1}}{p_{2}-s}
; then
c\sim 1
. Choose
x_{0}
such that

\frac{x_{2}-x_{0}}{x_{0}-x_{1}}=c.

Choose
p_{0}
such that
(x_{1},p_{2}),(x_{0},p_{0}),(x_{2},p_{1})
are collinear; then
\frac{p_{2}-p_{0}}{p_{0}-p_{1}}=\frac{x_{0}-x_{1}}{x_{0}-x_{2}}=\frac{1}{c}
.
Let
f^{\prime}
be the piecewise linear function determined by lines from
(x_{1},p_{1})
to
(x_{0},p_{0})
and then to
(x_{2},p_{2})
. A calculation shows that the area under
f^{\prime}
from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
is precisely
y_{2}-y_{1}
, so
f(x_{2})-f(x_{1})=y_{2}-y_{1}
. Thus, if we choose
f(x_{1})=y_{1}
, then
f(x_{2})
must equal
y_{2}
.

To verify the slope condition, note that
p_{0}-p_{1}\sim p_{2}-p_{1}
and
x_{0}-x_{1}\sim x_{2}-x_{1}
, so
\frac{p_{0}-p_{1}}{x_{0}-x_{1}}\sim\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{1}}\sim 1
. Similarly,
\frac{p_{2}-p_{0}}{x_{2}-x_{0}}\sim 1
.

Finally, for (b), we observe the following fact, which follows from the Intermediate Value theorem: For every
y\in[0,\frac{\pi}{4}]
, there exists
x\in[\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2}]
with
y=x\cot x
.

Replace the linear function from
(x_{1},p_{1})
to
(x_{0},p_{0})
with a sinusoid
f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{p_{0}+p_{1}}{2}+\frac{p_{0}-p_{1}}{2\sin\alpha}\sin\frac{\alpha(x-(x_{0}+x_{1})/2)}{(x_{0}-x_{1})/2}
, where
\alpha
is chosen to satisfy
\alpha\cot\alpha=\frac{D(x_{0}-x_{1})}{p_{0}-p_{1}}
(which is allowed since the latter expression is
\leq\frac{\pi}{4}
by the definition of
D
). Make an analogous replacement for the linear function from
(x_{0},p_{0})
to
(x_{2},p_{2})
. Then
f^{\prime}
becomes a
C^{1}
function on
(x_{1},x_{2})
with the boundary condition verified by
\lim_{x\to x_{0}^{+}}f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lim_{x\to x_{0}^{-}}f^{\prime\prime}(x)=D
. Hence,
f
is
C^{2}
. Also, replacing the linear function with the sinusoid doesn’t change the area under the curve. Finally,
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{p_{0}-p_{1}}{x_{0}-x_{1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\cos\frac{\alpha(x-(x_{0}+x_{1})/2)}{(x_{0}-x_{1})/2}
. We have
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq\frac{p_{0}-p_{1}}{x_{0}-x_{1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\lesssim 1
(using
\frac{\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
), and
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq\frac{p_{0}-p_{1}}{x_{0}-x_{1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\cdot\cos\alpha=D
. In particular, we have
f^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
, showing that
f
is strictly convex.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

If
\{a_{n}\}
is a
C
-uniformly convex sequence, then there exists a
C^{2}
function
f
passing through
(\frac{n}{N},a_{n})
such that
f^{\prime}\sim_{C}1
and
f^{\prime\prime}\sim_{C}1
.

Proof.

We may define
a_{0}
and
a_{N+1}
such that
(a_{n})_{n=0}^{N+1}
is also
C
-uniformly convex, e.g.
a_{0}=2a_{1}-a_{2}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}
and
a_{N+1}=2a_{N}-a_{N-1}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}
. Apply Theorem
4.1
(b) to
x_{i}=\frac{i}{N}
,
y_{i}=a_{n}
,
p_{i}=\frac{N}{2}\cdot(a_{i+1}-a_{i-1})
. Then

\Delta p_{i}=\frac{N}{2}(\Delta a_{i+1}-\Delta a_{i-1})=\frac{N}{2}(\Delta^{2}a_{i}+\Delta^{2}a_{i-1})\sim_{C}\frac{1}{N}\sim\Delta x_{i}

and

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta y_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}-p_{i}=N(a_{i+1}-a_{i})-\frac{N}{2}\cdot(a_{i+1}-a_{i-1})=\frac{N}{2}(a_{i+1}-2a_{i}+a_{i-1})\sim\frac{1}{N},

\displaystyle p_{i+1}-\frac{\Delta y_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}=\frac{N}{2}(a_{i+2}-a_{i})-N(a_{i+1}-a_{i})=\frac{N}{2}(a_{i+2}-2a_{i+1}+a_{i})\sim\frac{1}{N}.

Thus,
\frac{\Delta y_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}-p_{i}\sim p_{i+1}-\frac{\Delta y_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}
, verifying the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The interpolation of a convex sequence by a
C^{2}
convex curve was considered in
[
Del89
,
MN98
]
etc. However, these results are mostly implicit statements of the existence. An explicit construction based on Bezier curve was mentioned in
[
Han20
]
. However, this Bezier cuve has zero second derivatives at the data points, and therefore it is not strictly convex. Our approach in Theorem
4.1
gives a simple construction and ensures strict convexity.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
x,y>0
be real numbers with
xy\geq 100
. The number of rational numbers
\frac{r}{s}
in
[x,2x]
with denominator
s\leq y
is
\sim xy^{2}
.

Proof.

Round up
y
to the nearest integer.

To prove
\lesssim xy^{2}
, we observe that the number of fractions in
[x,2x]
with denominator
k
is
\leq 1+kx
, so the number of fractions with denominator
\leq y
is
\leq y+xy^{2}\lesssim xy^{2}
. Now, we prove
\gtrsim xy^{2}
.

The number of fractions in
[0,1]
with denominator at most
n
is
\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}n^{2}+O(n\log n)
(see for example
[
GKPL89
]
). This result extends to any interval
[m,m+1]
(since if
x
has denominator
n
, then so does
x+m
), and so the number of fractions in
[0,m]
with denominator at most
n
is
\sim\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}mn^{2}
. This proves the lemma for
x\geq 1
.

Let
F_{n}(m,x)
be the number of fractions with denominator
n
in
[mx,(m+1)x)
, and
G_{n}(m,x)
be the number of fractions with denominator
\leq n
in
[mx,(m+1)x)
. We know
G_{n}(m,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{n}(m,x)
. Furthermore,
F_{n}(m,x)
equals the number of integers in
[mnx,(m+1)nx)
, so it equals
\lfloor nx\rfloor
or
\lceil nx\rceil
. Thus, we have
|F_{n}(a,x)-F_{n}(b,x)|\leq 1
, so
|G_{n}(a,x)-G_{n}(b,x)|\leq n
for all
a,b
. Let
s=\lceil\frac{1}{x}\rceil
and
G_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}G_{n}(k,x)
; then, we have
|G_{n}(a,x)-\frac{1}{s}G_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}|G_{n}(a,x)-G_{n}(k,x)|\leq n
. Since
G_{n}(x)\geq\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}n^{2}
, we see that as long as
xy\geq 100
, we have
G_{y}(1,x)\gtrsim xy^{2}
.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Choose
r_{i}
to be the fractions in
[\frac{N^{\alpha-1}}{3},\frac{2N^{\alpha-1}}{3}]
with denominator
\leq N^{(2-\alpha)/3}
. There are
\sim N^{(\alpha+1)/3}
many fractions, and
r_{i+1}-r_{i}\gtrsim\frac{1}{N^{(4-2\alpha)/3}}
. Define
a_{i}=\frac{r_{i}}{N^{\alpha}}
, and now we will define
k_{i},M_{i}
as follows: For a given
i
, let
r_{i}=\frac{a}{b}
and
r_{i+1}=\frac{c}{d}
, where we can choose
\Delta_{i}\leq b,d\leq 2\Delta_{i}
for
\Delta_{i}:=N^{2-\alpha}(r_{i+1}-r_{i})
(which is allowed since
\Delta_{i}\gtrsim N^{(2-\alpha)/3}
). Then let
M_{i}=a+c
and
k_{i}=b+d
. Define the sequences
x_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i}k_{j}
,
y_{i}=\frac{M_{i}}{N^{\alpha}}
,
p_{i}=Na_{i}
. Then there exists a convex
C^{2}
function
f
through
(x_{i},y_{i})
with
f^{\prime}(x_{i})=p_{i}
and
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\sim 1
.

Proof.

Write
r_{i}=\frac{m_{i}}{n_{i}}
in lowest terms. Then
r_{i+1}-r_{i}=\frac{m_{i+1}n_{i}-m_{i}n_{i+1}}{n_{i}n_{i+1}}\geq\frac{1}{n_{i}n_{i+1}}\gtrsim\frac{1}{N^{(4-2\alpha)/3}}
.

Now we check the conditions of Theorem
4.1
. First,
\Delta p_{i}=N(a_{i+1}-a_{i})=\frac{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}{N^{\alpha-1}}\sim\frac{k_{i}}{N}=\Delta x_{i}
. Next,

\displaystyle\frac{M_{i}}{k_{i}N^{\alpha}}-a_{i}=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{a+c}{b+d}-\frac{a}{b}\right)=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{bc-ad}{b(b+d)}\right),

\displaystyle a_{i+1}-\frac{M_{i}}{k_{i}N^{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{c}{d}-\frac{a+c}{b+d}\right)=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{bc-ad}{d(b+d)}\right).

This implies
\frac{\Delta y_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}-p_{i}\sim p_{i+1}-\frac{\Delta y_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}
, so Theorem
4.1
applies.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Our construction also gives a lower bound for the intersection of a short convex sequence with an arithmetic progression. An appropriate scaling of the short sequence yields a generalized Dirichlet sequence (see
[
FGM21
, Definition 3.1]
), which is a sequence
\{b_{n}\}
satisfying

b_{n+1}-b_{n}\in[\frac{1}{4N},\frac{4}{N}],\ \ \ (b_{n+2}-b_{n+1})-(b_{n+1}-b_{n})\in[\frac{\theta}{4N^{2}},\frac{4\theta}{N^{2}}],\ \ \ 0<\theta\leq 1.

Consequently, we can extend Theorem
1.4
to generalized Dirichlet sequences with parameter
\theta\not\equiv 1
.

Specifically, for any
0<\theta(N)\leq 1
and
\alpha\in[\tfrac{1}{2},2]
, there exists a generalized Dirichlet sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
with parameter
\theta
such that

(28)

\left|\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\cap N^{-\alpha}\mathbb{Z}\right|\gtrsim\theta^{\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{1+\alpha}{3}}.

To see this, for some fixed
0<\beta\leq 1
, we restrict our attention to the first
N^{\beta}
terms of a convex sequence. Note that for the construction
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
in Theorem
4.5
, the arithmetic progression is approximately equidistributed in the convex sequence. This yields that

(29)

\left|\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N^{\beta}}\cap N^{-\alpha^{\prime}}\mathbb{Z}\right|\gtrsim N^{\beta-1}N^{\frac{1+\alpha^{\prime}}{3}},\ \ \ \tfrac{1}{2}\leq\alpha^{\prime}\leq 2.

Let
\tilde{N}=N^{\beta}
, then
\{N^{1-\beta}a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}}
is a generalized Dirichlet sequence of length
\tilde{N}
with parameter
\theta=N^{\beta-1}=\tilde{N}^{1-\frac{1}{\beta}}
. Denote
\tilde{\alpha}=\tfrac{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta-1}{\beta}
, then rewriting (
29
) gives

\left|\{N^{1-\beta}a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}}\cap\tilde{N}^{-\tilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}\right|\gtrsim\tilde{N}^{\frac{\alpha^{\prime}+3\beta-2}{3\beta}}=\theta^{\frac{1}{3}}\tilde{N}^{\frac{1+\tilde{\alpha}}{3}},

which implies (
28
).
