An adaptive heavy ball method for ill-posed inverse problems
Qinian Jin, Qin Huang

Abstract
. In this paper we consider ill-posed inverse problems, both linear and nonlinear, by a heavy ball method in which a strongly convex regularization function is incorporated to detect the feature of the sought solution. We develop ideas on how to adaptively choose the step-sizes and the momentum coefficients to achieve acceleration over the Landweber-type method. We then analyze the method and establish its regularization property when it is terminated by the discrepancy principle. Various numerical results are reported which demonstrate the superior performance of our method over the Landweber-type method by reducing substantially the required number of iterations and the computational time.

65J20, 65J22, 65J15, 47J06

1.
Introduction

Consider an operator equation of the form

\displaystyle F(x)=y,

(1)

where
F:\mbox{dom}(F)\subset X\to Y
is an operator between two real Hilbert spaces
X
and
Y

with domain
\mbox{dom}(F)
. The operator
F
could be either linear or nonlinear. Throughout, we
always assume that (
1
) has a solution, i.e.
y\in\mbox{Ran}(F)
, the range of
F
.
We are interested in the situation that (
1
) is ill-posed in the sense that
its solution does not depend continuously on the right hand data. Such situation can happen in a
broad range of applications of inverse problems. In practical scenarios, data are acquired
through experiments and thus the exact data may not be available; instead we only have measurement
data corrupted by noise. Due to the ill-posed nature of the underlying problem, it is therefore
important to develop algorithms to produce stable approximate solutions of (
1
) using
noisy data.

In practical applications, some
a priori
information, such as non-negativity, sparsity and
piecewise constancy, on the sought solution, is often available. Utilizing such feature
information in algorithm design can significantly improve the reconstruction accuracy. By
incorporating the available
a priori
information, one may pick a proper, lower semi-continuous,
strongly convex function
{\mathcal{R}}:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
as a regularization function to determine
a solution of (
1
) with the desired feature. Let
y^{\delta}
be a noisy data satisfying

\|y^{\delta}-y\|\leq\delta

with a known noise level
\delta>0
. The Landweber-type method

\displaystyle\begin{split}\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}&=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}L(x_{n}^{\delta})^{*}(F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}),\\
x_{n+1}^{\delta}&=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta},x\rangle\right\},\end{split}

(2)

has been considered in
[
18
]
for solving (
1
), where
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}>0
is the
step-size and
\{L(x):x\in\mbox{dom}(F)\}
is a family of properly chosen bounded
linear operators. In case
F
is Fréchect differentiable at
x
, we may take
L(x)=F^{\prime}(x)
,
the Fréchet derivative of
F
at
x
; otherwise,
L(x)
should be appropriately chosen as a
substitute for the non-existent Fréchet derivative. This method has been thoroughly analyzed in
[
15
,
18
]
under
the constant step-size

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{\delta}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{L^{2}},

(3)

when an upper bound
L
of
\|L(x)\|
around the sought solution is available, and the adaptive
step-size

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{\delta}=\min\left\{\frac{\mu_{0}\|F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}\|^{2}}{\|L(x_{n}^{\delta})^{*}(F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta})\|^{2}},\mu_{1}\right\}

(4)

with suitable choices of the parameters
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, and the regularization property
has been established when the iteration is terminated by the discrepancy principle. Note that,
when
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2
, the corresponding method with
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
chosen by (
3
) is the
classical Landweber method which has been analyzed in
[
6
,
10
]
, and the corresponding
method with
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
chosen by (
4
) is the minimal error method (
[
19
]
).
Extensive numerical simulations have demonstrated that the Landweber-type method (
2
)
is a slowly convergent approach, often necessitating a large number of iteration steps before
termination by the discrepancy principle. It is therefore important to develop strategies
for accelerating the Landweber-type method (
2
) while maintaining its simple implementation
feature.

For linear ill-posed problems in Hilbert spaces, Brakhage used in
[
4
]
the Jacobi
polynomials to construct a method, which is known as the
\nu
-method, to accelerate the
classical Landweber iteration. Additionally, in
[
9
]
, a family of accelerated Landweber
iterations was developed by employing orthogonal polynomials and the spectral theory of self-adjoint
operators. However, the acceleration strategy using orthogonal polynomials is scarcely
applicable for the Landweber-type method (
2
) when the forward operator
F
is
nonlinear or the regularization function
{\mathcal{R}}
is non-quadratic. In
[
12
,
28
]
the
sequential subspace optimization strategies were employed to accelerate the Landweber iteration.
The implementation of this strategy however requires to solve an additional optimization
problem to determine the weighted coefficients of the search directions at each iteration
step which unfortunately has the drawback of slowing down the method.

On the other hand, to determine a solution of well-posed minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}f(x)

(5)

with a continuous differentiable objective function
f
, tremendous progress has been made
toward accelerating the gradient method

x_{n+1}=x_{n}-\alpha_{n}\nabla f(x_{n})

in optimization community. In particular, Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method (
[
22
]
) and
Polyak’s heavy ball method (
[
27
]
) stood out and had far-reaching impact on the development
of fast first order optimization methods. In Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method, to define the
next iterate, instead of using the current iterate, it uses a carefully chosen extrapolation point,
built from the previous two iterates (
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
23
]
). Based on Nesterov’s acceleration
strategy, an accelerated version of Landweber-type method has been proposed in
[
15
]
and a refined
version of the method takes the form (see also
[
16
,
31
]
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\xi}_{n}^{\delta}&=\xi_{n}^{\delta}+\frac{n-1}{n+\gamma}(\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n-1}^{\delta}),\qquad\quad\quad\,\,\hat{x}_{n}^{\delta}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\hat{\xi}_{n}^{\delta},x\rangle\right\},\\
\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}&=\hat{\xi}_{n}^{\delta}-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}L(\hat{x}_{n}^{\delta})^{*}(F(\hat{x}_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}),\quad x_{n+1}^{\delta}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta},x\rangle\right\},\end{split}

(6)

where
\gamma\geq 2
. The numerical results presented in
[
15
]
demonstrate its striking
acceleration effect. Inspired by the numerical observations in
[
15
]
, some efforts have
been devoted to analyzing the regularization property of (
6
) in the context of ill-posed
problems. When X is a Hilbert space,
F
is a bounded linear operator and
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2
,
the regularization property of the corresponding method, terminated by either
a priori

stopping rules or the discrepancy principle, has been established in
[
20
,
24
]
based on
a general acceleration framework in
[
9
]
using orthogonal polynomials. When
F
is nonlinear
or
{\mathcal{R}}
is non-quadratic, although some partial results are available in
[
14
,
16
]
,
the analysis on (
6
) is under developed and the understanding of its regularization property
is largely open. By modifying the first equation in (
6
) by
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}+\lambda_{n}^{\delta}(\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n-1}^{\delta})
with a connection coefficient
\lambda_{n}^{\delta}
to be determined,
a two-point gradient method has been considered in
[
13
,
31
]
. This method, however, requires
a discrete backtracking search algorithm to determine
\lambda_{n}^{\delta}
at each iteration step which can
incur additional computational time.

Different from Nesterov’s method, Polyak’s heavy ball method accelerates the gradient method by
adding a momentum term to define the iterates, i.e.

\displaystyle x_{n+1}=x_{n}-\alpha_{n}\nabla f(x_{n})+\beta_{n}(x_{n}-x_{n-1}).

(7)

The performance of (
7
) depends crucially on the property of
f
and the choices of
the step-size
\alpha_{n}
and the momentum coefficient
\beta_{n}
, and its analysis turns out to
be very challenging. When
f
is twice continuous differentiable, strongly convex and has
Lipschitz continuous gradient, it has been demonstrated in
[
27
]
, under suitable
constant choices of
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
, that the iterative sequence
\{x_{n}\}
enjoys a provable
linear convergence faster than the gradient descent. Due to the recent development of machine
learning and the appearance of large scale problems, the heavy ball method has gained renewed
interest and much attention has been paid on understanding its convergence behavior (
[
7
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
30
]
). Although many strategies have been proposed
to select
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
, how to choose these parameters to achieve acceleration effect
for general convex and non-convex problems remains an active research topic in optimization
community.

In this paper we will consider the iterative method

\displaystyle\begin{split}\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}&=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}L(x_{n}^{\delta})^{*}(F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta})+\beta_{n}^{\delta}(\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n-1}^{\delta}),\\
x_{n+1}^{\delta}&=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta},x\rangle\right\}\end{split}

(8)

for solving ill-posed inverse problem (
1
), where
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
denotes the step-size and

\beta_{n}^{\delta}
denotes the momentum coefficient. This method differs from (
2
) in that a
momentum term
\beta_{n}^{\delta}(\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n-1}^{\delta})
is added to define the iterates. The method
(
8
) has intimate connection with the heavy ball method. Indeed, when
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2

and
F
is Fréchet differentiable, the method (
8
) with
L(x)=F^{\prime}(x)
becomes

x_{n+1}^{\delta}=x_{n}^{\delta}-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}F^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})^{*}(F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta})+\beta_{n}^{\delta}(x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta})

which can be obtained by applying the heavy ball method (
7
) to the minimization problem
(
5
) with

\displaystyle f(x):=\frac{1}{2}\|F(x)-y^{\delta}\|^{2}.

(9)

We will further demonstrate that, when
F
is a bounded linear operator, the method (
8
)
with general strongly convex
{\mathcal{R}}
, can be derived by applying the heavy ball method to a dual problem.
Based on the successful experience with the acceleration effect of the method (
7
) for well-posed
optimization problems, it is natural to expect that, with suitable choices of the parameters
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}

and
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
, the method (
8
) can have superior performance over the method (
2
)
for ill-posed problems. In this paper we will provide a criterion for choosing
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
and
\beta_{n}^{\delta}

and investigate the convergence property of the corresponding method. Our choices of
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
and
\beta_{n}^{\delta}

are adaptive in the sense that only quantities arising from the computation are used. The formulae
for calculating
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
and
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
are explicit, thereby avoiding the need for a backtracking
search strategy and consequently saving computational time. The complexity per iteration of our proposed
method is comparable to one step of the Landweber-type method (
2
). However, extensive
computational results demonstrate that our method significantly accelerates the Landweber-type method
in terms of both the number of iterations and the computational time.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we first formulate the conditions on the
regularization function
{\mathcal{R}}
and the forward operator
F
, we then propose our adaptive heavy ball
method by elucidating how to choose the step-sizes and the momentum coefficients, we also show that
our method is well-defined and the iteration can be terminated in finite many steps by the discrepancy
principle. In Section
3
we focus on the convergence analysis and establish the regularization
property of our method. In Section
4
we perform various numerical simulations to demonstrate
the acceleration effect and superior performance of our method over the Landweber-type method.

2.
The adaptive heavy-ball method

In this section we will consider the iterative method (
8
) and provide a motivation on
choosing the step-size
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
and the momentum coefficient
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
adaptively. We will then
show that our proposed method, i.e. Algorithm
1
below, is well-defined.

2.1.
Assumptions

Note that the method (
8
) involves the regularization function
{\mathcal{R}}
and the forward
operator
F
. In order to carry out the analysis, certain conditions should be placed on
{\mathcal{R}}

and
F
. For the regularization function
{\mathcal{R}}
, we assume the following standard condition.

Assumption 1
.

{\mathcal{R}}:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a proper, lower semi-continuous, strongly convex function in
the sense that there is a constant
\sigma>0
such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(t\bar{x}+(1-t)x)+\sigma t(1-t)\|\bar{x}-x\|^{2}\leq t{\mathcal{R}}(\bar{x})+(1-t){\mathcal{R}}(x)

for all
\bar{x},x\in\emph{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
and
0\leq t\leq 1
.

To make use of Assumption
1
, let us recall a little convex analysis (
[
29
]
). For any
proper convex function
{\mathcal{R}}:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
we use
\partial{\mathcal{R}}
to denote its subdifferential, i.e.

\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\{\xi\in X:{\mathcal{R}}(z)\geq\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x)+\langle\xi,z-x\rangle\mbox{ for all }z\in X\}.

For any
x\in X
with
\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x)\neq\emptyset
, let
\xi\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x)
and define

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi}(z,x):={\mathcal{R}}(z)-{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi,z-x\rangle,\quad z\in X

which is called the Bregman distance induced by
{\mathcal{R}}
at
x
in the direction
\xi
. If
{\mathcal{R}}
is strongly
convex in the sense of Assumption
1
, it is easy to derive that

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi}(z,x)\geq\sigma\|z-x\|^{2}

(10)

for all
x\in X
with
\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x)\neq\emptyset
,
\xi\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x)
and
z\in X
. For a proper,
lower semi-continuous, convex function
{\mathcal{R}}:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
, its convex conjugate

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi):=\sup_{x\in X}\left\{\langle\xi,x\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}(x)\right\},\quad\forall\xi\in X

is also a proper, lower semi-continuous, convex function that maps from
X
to
(-\infty,\infty]
. Moreover

\xi\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x)\iff x\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)\iff{\mathcal{R}}(x)+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)=\langle\xi,x\rangle.

If
{\mathcal{R}}
satisfies Assumption
1
, then
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
is continuous differentiable, its gradient

\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
is Lipschitz continuous with

\displaystyle\|\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\bar{\xi})-\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)\|\leq\frac{\|\bar{\xi}-\xi\|}{2\sigma},\quad\forall\bar{\xi},\xi\in X;

(11)

see
[
29
, Corollary 3.5.11]
. Thus, under Assumption
1
, for any
\xi\in X
the minimization
problem

x=\arg\min_{z\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(z)-\langle\xi,z\rangle\right\}

has a unique solution given by
x=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)
. Clearly
\xi\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x)
.

For the forward operator
F
, we assume the following condition which has been widely used in
dealing with ill-posed problems, where
B_{\rho}(x_{0}):=\{x\in X:\|x-x_{0}\|\leq\rho\}
for
any
\rho>0
.

Assumption 2
.

(a)

X
and
Y
are Hilbert spaces.

(b)

There exist
\rho>0
,
x_{0}\in X
and
\xi_{0}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{0})
such that

B_{2\rho}(x_{0})\subset\emph{dom}(F)
and (
1
) has a solution
\bar{x}
such that

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(\bar{x},x_{0})\leq\sigma\rho^{2}
.

(c)

F
is weakly closed on
\emph{dom}(F)
, i.e. for any sequence

\{x_{n}\}\subset\emph{dom}(F)
satisfying
x_{n}\rightharpoonup x\in X
and
F(x_{n})\to v\in Y

there hold
x\in\emph{dom}(F)
and
F(x)=v
. Throughout the paper “
\to
’ and
“
\rightharpoonup
” are used to denote the strong and weak convergence respectively.

(d)

There exist a family of bounded linear operators
\{L(x):X\to Y\}_{x\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})}

such that
x\to L(x)
is continuous on
B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
and there is
0\leq\eta<1
such that

\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-L(\bar{x})(x-\bar{x})\|\leq\eta\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|

for all
x,\bar{x}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
. Moreover, there is a constant
L>0
such that
\|L(x)\|\leq L
for
all
x\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
.

We remark that, when
F
is a bounded linear operator, Assumption
2
holds automatically with

\rho=\infty
and
\eta=0
. When
F
is nonlinear, Assumption
2
(d) is known as the
tangential cone condition which gives certain restriction on the nonlinearity of
F
. Assumption

2
(d) clearly implies

\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\eta}\|L(\bar{x})(x-\bar{x})\|,\quad\forall x,\bar{x}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})

and thus the continuity of the mapping
x\to F(x)
on
B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
. Based on Assumption
1

and Assumption
2
, it has been shown in
[
18
, Lemma 3.2]
that (
1
) has a
unique solution
x^{\dagger}
satisfying

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(x^{\dagger},x_{0}):=\min_{x\in\mbox{dom}(F)}\left\{D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(x,x_{0}):F(x)=y\right\}.

(12)

By using (
10
) and Assumption
2
(b) it is easy to see that
\|x^{\dagger}-x_{0}\|\leq\rho
,
i.e.
x^{\dagger}\in B_{\rho}(x_{0})
.

2.2.
The method

Let us now turn to the consideration on the method (
8
). We first demonstrate that,
when
F
is a bounded linear operator, the method (
8
) can be derived by applying
the heavy ball method to the dual problem of

\displaystyle\min\{{\mathcal{R}}(x):x\in X\mbox{ and }Fx=y\}.

(13)

To see this, we recall that the associated dual function is given by

\displaystyle d(\lambda):=\inf_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\lambda,Fx-y\rangle\right\}=-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(F^{*}\lambda)+\langle\lambda,y\rangle

and hence the corresponding dual problem is
\max_{\lambda\in Y}d(\lambda)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in Y}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(F^{*}\lambda)-\langle\lambda,y\rangle\right\}.

(14)

Since
{\mathcal{R}}
satisfies Assumption
1
, the objective function in (
14
) is continuous
differentiable. Applying the heavy ball method to solve (
14
) gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1}=\lambda_{n}-\alpha_{n}(F\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(F^{*}\lambda_{n})-y)+\beta_{n}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n-1}).

By setting
\xi_{n}:=F^{*}\lambda_{n}
and
x_{n}:=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(F^{*}\lambda_{n})
, we then obtain

\displaystyle x_{n}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}),\quad\xi_{n+1}=\xi_{n}-\alpha_{n}F^{*}(Fx_{n}-y)+\beta_{n}(\xi_{n}-\xi_{n-1}).

In case only noisy data
y^{\delta}
is available, by replacing
y
by
y^{\delta}
and allowing
\alpha_{n}
,
\beta_{n}
to
be dependent on
y^{\delta}
in the above equation we may obtain the method

\displaystyle x_{n}^{\delta}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta}),\quad\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}F^{*}(Fx_{n}^{\delta}-y^{\delta})+\beta_{n}^{\delta}(\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n-1}^{\delta})

which is equivalent to (
8
) when
F
is bounded linear.

Note that the term
F^{*}(Fx_{n}^{\delta}-y^{\delta})
in the above equation is the gradient of the function

x\to\frac{1}{2}\|Fx-y^{\delta}\|^{2}
at
x_{n}^{\delta}
. When
F
is a nonlinear Fréchet differentiable operator,
we may use the gradient
F^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})^{*}(F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta})
of the function (
9
) at
x_{n}^{\delta}
to
replace
F^{*}(Fx_{n}^{\delta}-y^{\delta})
in the above equation; in case
F
is not differentiable at
x_{n}^{\delta}
,
we may use
L(x_{n}^{\delta})
to substitute
F^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})
. Consequently, it leads to the method (
8
) for solving (
1
).

In order for the method (
8
) to have fast convergence, it is natural to choose
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
and

\beta_{n}^{\delta}
at each iteration step such that
x_{n+1}^{\delta}
is as close to a solution of (
1
)
as possible. Let
\hat{x}
denote any solution of (
1
) in
B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
. We may measure the
closeness from
x_{n+1}^{\delta}
to
\hat{x}
by the Bregman distance

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}}(\hat{x},x_{n+1}^{\delta}):={\mathcal{R}}(\hat{x})-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n+1}^{\delta})-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta},\hat{x}-x_{n+1}^{\delta}\rangle

induced by
{\mathcal{R}}
at
x_{n+1}^{\delta}
in the direction
\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}
. Directly minimizing this quantity
becomes infeasible because it involves an unknown solution
\hat{x}
and a strongly convex function
{\mathcal{R}}

which may be non-quadratic. As a compromise, we may first derive a suitable upper bound for this
quantity and then take minimization steps.

We will elucidate the idea on choosing
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
and
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
below. For simplicity of
exposition, we introduce the notation

r_{n}^{\delta}:=F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta},\quad g_{n}^{\delta}:=L(x_{n}^{\delta})^{*}r_{n}^{\delta}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad m_{n}^{\delta}:=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n-1}^{\delta}

for all integers
n\geq 0
. Then (
8
) can be written as

\displaystyle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}g_{n}^{\delta}+\beta_{n}^{\delta}m_{n}^{\delta},\quad x_{n+1}^{\delta}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}).

(15)

Let
\Delta_{n}^{\delta}:=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}^{\delta}}(\hat{x},x_{n}^{\delta})
for
n\geq 0
. Since
\xi_{n}^{\delta}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})
,
we have

{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})=\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle

and thus

\Delta_{n}^{\delta}={\mathcal{R}}(\hat{x})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})-\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},\hat{x}\rangle.

Therefore, by using
x_{n}^{\delta}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})
, (
11
) and (
15
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\left({\mathcal{R}}(\hat{x})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n+1}^{\delta})-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta},\hat{x}\rangle\right)-\left({\mathcal{R}}(\hat{x})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})-\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},\hat{x}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n+1}^{\delta})-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}^{\delta},\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})\rangle+\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}\|\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sigma}\|\alpha_{n}^{\delta}g_{n}^{\delta}-\beta_{n}^{\delta}m_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\langle-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}g_{n}^{\delta}+\beta_{n}^{\delta}m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle.

By the polarization identity in Hilbert spaces, we then have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}\left((\alpha_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|g_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+(\beta_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|m_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle g_{n}^{\delta},m_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\quad\,-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\langle r_{n}^{\delta},L(x_{n}^{\delta})(x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x})\rangle+\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\alpha_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|g_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\beta_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|m_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle g_{n}^{\delta},m_{n}^{\delta}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\,-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\langle r_{n}^{\delta},r_{n}^{\delta}+y^{\delta}-y+y-F(x_{n}^{\delta})-L(x_{n}^{\delta})(\hat{x}-x_{n}^{\delta})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\,+\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle.

Assume
x_{n}^{\delta}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
. By using
\|y^{\delta}-y\|\leq\delta
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
and Assumption
2
(d), we further obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\alpha_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|g_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\beta_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|m_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle g_{n}^{\delta},m_{n}^{\delta}\rangle-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\delta\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|+\eta\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|\|y-F(x_{n}^{\delta})\|+\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\alpha_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|g_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+(1+\eta)\delta\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|+\eta\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle+\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\beta_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|m_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle g_{n}^{\delta},m_{n}^{\delta}\rangle.

(16)

Note that when
\beta_{n}^{\delta}=0
, the method (
8
) becomes the Landweber-type method (
2
) and
extensive analysis has been done on (
2
) with suitable choices of the step-size
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
,
including (
3
) and (
4
). For the method (
8
) we also choose
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
as
such, i.e.

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{\delta}:=\frac{\mu_{0}}{L^{2}}\quad\mbox{ or }\quad\alpha_{n}^{\delta}:=\min\left\{\frac{\mu_{0}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}}{\|g_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}},\mu_{1}\right\}

(17)

when
\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|>\tau\delta
, where
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
are two positive constants. Then, by plugging
the choice of
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
from (
17
) into (
16
) and using
\delta\leq\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|/\tau

we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq-\left(1-\frac{1+\eta}{\tau}-\eta-\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\sigma}\right)\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\beta_{n}^{\delta})^{2}\|m_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\beta_{n}^{\delta}\langle g_{n}^{\delta},m_{n}^{\delta}\rangle.

(18)

We next consider the choice of
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
. It seems natural to obtain it by minimizing the right
hand side of the above equation with respect to
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
. Let us fix a number
\beta\in(0,\infty]

and minimize the right hand side of (
18
) over
[0,\beta]
to obtain

\beta_{n}^{\delta}=\min\left\{\max\left\{0,\frac{\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\langle g_{n}^{\delta},m_{n}^{\delta}\rangle-2\sigma\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle}{\|m_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}}\right\},\beta\right\}

whenever
m_{n}^{\delta}\neq 0
. However, this formula for
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
is not computable because
it involves
\hat{x}
which is unknown. We need to treat the term

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}^{\delta}:=\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle

(19)

by using a suitable computable surrogate. Note that
\gamma_{0}^{\delta}=0
and, for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta}\rangle+\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n-1}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta}\rangle+\langle-\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}g_{n-1}^{\delta}+\beta_{n-1}^{\delta}m_{n-1}^{\delta},x_{n-1}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta}\rangle-\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\langle r_{n-1}^{\delta},L(x_{n-1}^{\delta})(x_{n-1}^{\delta}-\hat{x})\rangle+\beta_{n-1}^{\delta}\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta}\rangle-\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\,-\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\langle r_{n-1}^{\delta},y^{\delta}-F(x_{n-1}^{\delta})+L(x_{n-1}^{\delta})(x_{n-1}^{\delta}-\hat{x})\rangle+\beta_{n-1}^{\delta}\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta}\rangle-\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|\left(\delta+\|y-F(x_{n-1}^{\delta})-L(x_{n-1}^{\delta})(\hat{x}-x_{n-1}^{\delta})\|\right)+\beta_{n-1}^{\delta}\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}.

Assuming
x_{n-1}^{\delta}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
, we may use Assumption
2
(d) to further obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta}\rangle-\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|\left(\delta+\eta\|y-F(x_{n-1}^{\delta})\|\right)

\displaystyle\quad\,+\beta_{n-1}^{\delta}\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta}\rangle-(1-\eta)\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+(1+\eta)\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\delta\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|+\beta_{n-1}^{\delta}\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}.

(20)

This motivates us to introduce
\{\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{\delta}\}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{\delta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0&\mbox{ if }n=0,\\
\langle m_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n-1}^{\delta}\rangle-(1-\eta)\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|^{2}&\\
\qquad\qquad+(1+\eta)\alpha_{n-1}^{\delta}\delta\|r_{n-1}^{\delta}\|+\beta_{n-1}^{\delta}\tilde{\gamma}_{n-1}^{\delta}&\mbox{ if }n\geq 1\end{array}\right.

once
x_{k}^{\delta}
for
0\leq k\leq n
and
\alpha_{k}^{\delta},\beta_{k}^{\delta}
for
0\leq k\leq n-1
are defined.
Therefore, it leads us to propose Algorithm
1
below that will be considered in this paper.

Algorithm 1

(AHB: Adaptive heavy ball method)
.

Take an initial guess
\xi_{0}
, calculate
x_{0}:=\arg\min_{x\in X}\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{0},x\rangle\}
, and set
\xi_{-1}^{\delta}=\xi_{0}^{\delta}=\xi_{0}
. Pick the numbers
\tau>1
,
0<\beta\leq\infty
,
\mu_{0}>0
and
\mu_{1}>0
. For
n\geq 0
do the following:

(i)

Calculate
r_{n}^{\delta}:=F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}
. If
\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|\leq\tau\delta
, stop and output
x_{n}^{\delta}
;

(ii)

Calculate
g_{n}^{\delta}:=L(x_{n}^{\delta})^{*}(F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta})
and determine
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
according to (
17
);

(iii)

Calculate
m_{n}^{\delta}:=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n-1}^{\delta}
and determine
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{\delta}
by the formula (
2.2
);

(iv)

Calculate
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
by

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{\delta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\min\left\{\max\left\{0,\frac{\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\langle g_{n}^{\delta},m_{n}^{\delta}\rangle-2\sigma\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{\delta}}{\|m_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}}\right\},\beta\right\}&\emph{if}m_{n}^{\delta}\neq 0,\\
0&\emph{if}m_{n}^{\delta}=0;\end{array}\right.

(v)

Update
\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}
and
x_{n+1}^{\delta}
by

\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\alpha_{n}^{\delta}g_{n}^{\delta}+\beta_{n}^{\delta}m_{n}^{\delta},\quad x_{n+1}^{\delta}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta},x\rangle\}.

Note that, for the implementation of one step of Algorithm
1
, the most expensive parts are the
calculations of
r_{n}^{\delta}
,
g_{n}^{\delta}
and
x_{n+1}^{\delta}
which are common for Landweber-type method (
2
);
the computational load for other parts relating to
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
,
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{\delta}
and
\beta_{n}^{\delta}
can be
negligible. Therefore, the computational complexity per iteration of Algorithm
1
is marginally
higher than, but very close to, that of one step of the Landweber-type method (
2
).

In the design of Algorithm
1
, the discrepancy principle has been incorporated, i.e. the iteration
stops as long as
\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|\leq\tau\delta
is satisfied for the first time. In the following we will prove that
Algorithm
1
is well-defined by showing that the iteration indeed can stop in finite many steps.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
and let
k\geq 0

be an integer such that
x_{n}^{\delta}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
for all
0\leq n<k
. Define
\gamma_{n}^{\delta}
by
(
19
) for
0\leq n\leq k
. Then
\gamma_{n}^{\delta}\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{\delta}
for all
0\leq n\leq k
.

Proof.

Since
\gamma_{0}^{\delta}=\tilde{\gamma}_{0}^{\delta}=0
, the result is true for
n=0
. Assume next that

\gamma_{n}^{\delta}\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{\delta}
for some
0\leq n<k
. By noting that
\beta_{n}^{\delta}\geq 0
,
we may use (
20
) and the definition of
\tilde{\gamma}_{n+1}^{\delta}
to obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{n+1}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\langle m_{n+1}^{\delta},x_{n+1}^{\delta}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle-(1-\eta)\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+(1+\eta)\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\delta\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|+\beta_{n}^{\delta}\gamma_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\langle m_{n+1}^{\delta},x_{n+1}^{\delta}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle-(1-\eta)\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+(1+\eta)\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\delta\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|+\beta_{n}^{\delta}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\gamma}_{n+1}^{\delta}.

By the induction principle, this shows the result.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
with
\mu_{0}>0

and
\tau>1
being chosen such that

\displaystyle c_{0}:=1-\frac{1+\eta}{\tau}-\eta-\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\sigma}>0.

(24)

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer such that
\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|>\tau\delta
for all
0\leq n<k
. Then

x_{n}^{\delta}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
for all
0\leq n\leq k
and

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}\leq\Delta_{n}^{\delta}-c_{0}\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|r_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}

for all
0\leq n<k
, where
\Delta_{n}^{\delta}:=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}^{\delta}}(\hat{x},x_{n}^{\delta})
and
\hat{x}
denotes any
solution of (
1
) in
B_{2\rho}(x_{0})]\cap\emph{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
.

Proof.

We first show by induction that

\displaystyle x_{n}^{\delta}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}^{\delta}}(x^{\dagger},x_{n}^{\delta})\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(x^{\dagger},x_{0})

(25)

for all integers
0\leq n\leq k
. It is trivial for
n=0
as
\xi_{0}^{\delta}=\xi_{0}
and
x_{0}^{\delta}=x_{0}
.
Next we assume that (
25
) holds for all
0\leq n\leq l
for some
l<k
. According
to (
18
) and Lemma
2.1
we have for any solution
\hat{x}
of (
1
)
in
B_{2\rho}(x_{0})\cap\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
that

\displaystyle\Delta_{l+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{l}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq-c_{0}\alpha_{l}^{\delta}\|r_{l}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\beta_{l}^{\delta})^{2}\|m_{l}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\beta_{l}^{\delta}\gamma_{l}^{\delta}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\alpha_{l}^{\delta}\beta_{l}^{\delta}\langle g_{l}^{\delta},m_{l}^{\delta}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-c_{0}\alpha_{l}^{\delta}\|r_{l}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\sigma}(\beta_{l}^{\delta})^{2}\|m_{l}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\beta_{l}^{\delta}\tilde{\gamma}_{l}^{\delta}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\alpha_{l}^{\delta}\beta_{l}^{\delta}\langle g_{l}^{\delta},m_{l}^{\delta}\rangle.

Note that
\beta_{l}^{\delta}
is the minimizer of the function
t\to h_{l}(t)
over
[0,\beta]
, where

h_{l}(t):=-c_{0}\alpha_{l}^{\delta}\|r_{l}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\sigma}t^{2}\|m_{l}^{\delta}\|^{2}+t\tilde{\gamma}_{l}^{\delta}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\alpha_{l}^{\delta}t\langle g_{l}^{\delta},m_{l}^{\delta}\rangle,

we can conclude that

\displaystyle\Delta_{l+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{l}^{\delta}\leq h_{l}(\beta_{l}^{\delta})\leq h_{l}(0)=-c_{0}\alpha_{l}^{\delta}\|r_{l}^{\delta}\|^{2}.

(26)

By virtue of this inequality with
\hat{x}=x^{\dagger}
, the induction hypothesis, and Assumption
2
(b)
we have

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{l+1}}(x^{\dagger},x_{l+1}^{\delta})\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{l}^{\delta}}(x^{\dagger},x_{l}^{\delta})\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(x^{\dagger},x_{0})\leq\sigma\rho^{2}

which together with (
10
) implies that
\sigma\|x_{l+1}^{\delta}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\leq\sigma\rho^{2}

and hence
\|x_{l+1}^{\delta}-x^{\dagger}\|\leq\rho
. Since
\|x^{\dagger}-x_{0}\|\leq\rho
, we thus
have
\|x_{l+1}^{\delta}-x_{0}\|\leq 2\rho
, i.e.
x_{l+1}^{\delta}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
. We therefore complete the
proof of (
25
). As a direct consequence, we can see that
(
26
) holds for all
0\leq l<k
which shows the desired result.
∎

Based on Lemma
2.2
we now can prove that Algorithm
2
is well-defined, i.e. iteration
must terminate in finite many steps.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
with noisy data

y^{\delta}
satisfying
\|y^{\delta}-y\|\leq\delta
with noise level
\delta>0
. Assume that
\tau>1
and
\mu_{0}>0

are chosen such that (
24
) holds. Then the algorithm must terminate in finite many steps, i.e.
there exists a finite integer
n_{\delta}
such that

\displaystyle\|F(x_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}\|\leq\tau\delta<\|F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}\|,\quad 0\leq n<n_{\delta}.

(27)

Proof.

Let
l\geq 0
be an integer such that
\|F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}\|>\tau\delta
for all
0\leq n\leq l
. It then follows from Lemma
2.2
that

\displaystyle c_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{l}\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{l}\left(\Delta_{n}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{0}^{\delta}-\Delta_{l+1}^{\delta}\leq\Delta_{0}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(\hat{x},x_{0}).

According to the choice of
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}
and
\|L(x)\|\leq L
for all
x\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
, we can see
\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\geq\min\{\mu_{0}/L^{2},\mu_{1}\}
for all
0\leq n\leq l
. Thus

\displaystyle(l+1)c_{0}\min\{\mu_{0}/L^{2},\mu_{1}\}\tau^{2}\delta^{2}\leq c_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{l}\alpha_{n}^{\delta}\|F(x_{n}^{\delta})-y^{\delta}\|^{2}\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(\hat{x},x_{0})<\infty.

If there is no finite integer
n_{\delta}
such that (
27
) holds, then we can take
l
to be any positive integer. Letting
l\to\infty
in the above equation gives a contradiction. Thus, the algorithm must terminate in finite many steps.
∎

3.
Convergence analysis

In this section we will provide convergence analysis on Algorithm
1
. Let
n_{\delta}
be the integer
output by the discrepancy principle, we will analyze the behavior of
x_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}
as
\delta\to 0
. We first
prove a weak convergence result as stated below.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
using a
sequence of noisy data
\{y^{\delta_{k}}\}
satisfying
\|y^{\delta_{k}}-y\|\leq\delta_{k}
with
0<\delta_{k}\to 0

as
k\to\infty
. Assume that
\tau>1
and
\mu_{0}>0
are chosen such that (
24
) holds. Let

n_{k}:=n_{\delta_{k}}
be the output integer. Then there is a subsequence
\{y^{\delta_{k_{l}}}\}
of

\{y^{\delta_{k}}\}
such that

x_{n_{k_{l}}}^{\delta_{k_{l}}}\rightharpoonup x^{*}\quad\mbox{ as }l\to\infty

for some solution
x^{*}
of (
1
) in
B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
.

If in addition
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2
and
\emph{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))\subset\emph{Ker}(L(x))
for
all
x\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
, then
x_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}}\rightharpoonup x^{\dagger}
as
k\to\infty
,
where
x^{\dagger}
is the unique
x_{0}
-minimum norm solution of (
1
).

Proof.

By using Lemma
2.2
with
\hat{x}=x^{\dagger}
and (
10
), we have

\sigma\|x_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}}-\hat{x}\|^{2}\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}}}(\hat{x},x_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}})\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(\hat{x},x_{0})

which implies that
\{x_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}}\}
is a bounded sequence. Thus, we can find a subsequence

\{y^{\delta_{k_{l}}}\}
of
\{y^{\delta_{l}}\}
such that
x_{n_{k_{l}}}^{\delta_{k_{l}}}\rightharpoonup x^{*}

as
l\to\infty
for some
x^{*}\in X
. Because
\|F(x_{n_{k_{l}}}^{\delta_{k_{l}}})-y^{\delta_{k_{l}}}\|\leq\tau\delta_{k_{l}}
, we have
F(x_{n_{k_{l}}}^{\delta_{k_{l}}})\to y
as
l\to\infty
. Thus, by the
weak closedness of
F
given in Assumption
2
(c), we have
x^{*}\in\mbox{dom}(F)

and
F(x^{*})=y
. Furthermore, by the weak lower semi-continuity of norms we have

\displaystyle\|x^{*}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\leq\liminf_{l\to\infty}\|x_{n_{k_{l}}}^{\delta_{k_{l}}}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{0}}(x^{\dagger},x_{0})\leq\rho^{2}

which together with
x^{\dagger}\in B_{\rho}(x_{0})
implies that
x^{*}\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
.

When
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2
and
\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))\subset\mbox{Ker}(L(x))
for all
x\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
,
we show that
x_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}}\rightharpoonup x^{\dagger}
as
k\to\infty
, It suffices to show that

x^{\dagger}
is the unique weak cluster point of
\{x_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}}\}
. For the given
{\mathcal{R}}
we have

\xi_{n}^{\delta_{k}}=x_{n}^{\delta_{k}}
for all
n
and by the definition of
\xi_{n}^{\delta_{k}}
we can see that

\displaystyle x_{n}^{\delta_{k}}-x_{0}

\displaystyle\in\mbox{Ran}(L(x_{n-1}^{\delta_{k}})^{*})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mbox{Ran}(L(x_{0}^{\delta_{k}})^{*})

\displaystyle\subset\mbox{Ker}(L(x_{n-1}^{\delta_{k}}))^{\perp}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mbox{Ker}(L(x_{0}^{\delta_{k}}))^{\perp}

\displaystyle\subset\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))^{\perp}

for all
n
which in particular implies that
x_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}}-x_{0}\in\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))^{\perp}
.
Consequently, for any weak cluster point
\bar{x}
of
\{x_{n_{k}}^{\delta_{k}}\}
we have

\bar{x}-x_{0}\in\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))^{\perp}
. Since
x^{\dagger}
is the
x_{0}
-minimum norm solution
of (
1
), we must have
x^{\dagger}-x_{0}\in\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))^{\perp}
. Indeed, for any

w\in\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))
we have
x^{\dagger}+tw\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
for small
|t|
. Thus, it
follows from Assumption
2
(d) that

\displaystyle\|F(x^{\dagger}+tw)-F(x^{\dagger})\|

\displaystyle=\|F(x^{\dagger}+tw)-F(x^{\dagger})-L(x^{\dagger})(tw)\|

\displaystyle\leq\eta\|F(x^{\dagger}+tw)-F(x^{\dagger})\|

which implies
F(x^{\dagger}+tw)=F(x^{\dagger})=y
for small
|t|
as
0\leq\eta<1
. Since
x^{\dagger}
is the

x_{0}
-minimal norm solution of (
1
), we have
\|x^{\dagger}+tw-x_{0}\|^{2}\geq\|x^{\dagger}-x_{0}\|^{2}

for small
|t|
and thus
\langle x^{\dagger}-x_{0},w\rangle=0
for all
w\in\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))
. Therefore

\bar{x}-x^{\dagger}=(\bar{x}-x_{0})+(x_{0}-x^{\dagger})\in\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))^{\perp}

On the other hand, since both
\bar{x}
and
x^{\dagger}
are solutions of
F(x)=y
in
B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
,
we may use Assumption
2
(d) to obtain

\displaystyle\|L(x^{\dagger})(\bar{x}-x^{\dagger})\|\leq(1+\eta)\|F(\bar{x})-F(x^{\dagger})\|=0

which means
\bar{x}-x^{\dagger}\in\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))
. Therefore

\bar{x}-x^{\dagger}\in\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))^{\perp}\cap\mbox{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))=\{0\}

and thus
\bar{x}=x^{\dagger}
. The proof is complete.
∎

The above theorem gives a weak convergence result on Algorithm
1
for any

\beta\in(0,\infty]
. However, it remains uncertain if a strong convergence result can
be assured for general
\beta
. Nevertheless, by confining
\beta
to be less than 1, we
can establish a strong convergence result for Algorithm
1
in the remaining part
of this section. The proof of strong convergence is rather challenging. We must explore the
counterpart of Algorithm
1
utilizing the exact data
y
, which can be formulated
as follows.

Algorithm 2
.

Take an initial guess
\xi_{0}
, calculate

(31)

In view of (
30
) and (
31
), we can pick a sequence
\{n_{k}\}
of
integers by setting

, for each

It is easy to see that

(32)

For the above chosen
\{n_{k}\}
, we now show (
28
) for any solution
\hat{x}
of (

)
in

\displaystyle\xi_{n+1}-\xi_{n}=-\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}\beta_{j}\right)\alpha_{i}L(x_{i})^{*}r_{i}.

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{n+1}-\xi_{n},x_{n_{k}}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}\beta_{j}\right)\alpha_{i}\langle r_{i},L(x_{i})(x_{n_{k}}-\hat{x})\rangle.

Using Assumption

\displaystyle\|L(x_{i})(x_{n_{k}}-\hat{x})\|

, then we may use the

.
By using
r_{k}=0
and
m_{k}=0
we have
\xi_{k+1}=\xi_{k}
. Thus, by using
0\leq\alpha_{k}^{\delta_{l}}\leq\mu_{1}

and
0\leq\beta_{k}^{\delta_{l}}\leq\beta
, we have

\displaystyle\|\xi_{k+1}^{\delta_{l}}-\xi_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle=\|\xi_{k}^{\delta_{l}}-\xi_{k}-\alpha_{k}^{\delta_{l}}L(x_{k}^{\delta_{l}})^{*}r_{k}^{\delta_{l}}+\beta_{k}^{\delta_{l}}m_{k}^{\delta_{l}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\xi_{k}^{\delta_{l}}-\xi_{k}\|+\mu_{1}\|L(x_{k}^{\delta_{l}})^{*}r_{k}^{\delta_{l}}\|+\beta\|m_{k}^{\delta_{l}}\|

\displaystyle\to\mu_{1}\|L(x_{k})^{*}r_{k}\|+\beta\|m_{k}\|=0

as
\delta\to 0
. This shows that (
36
) is also true for
n=k+1
.

Putting the facts established in (
35
) and (
36
) together, the proof is
therefore complete.
∎

Finally we are ready to show the main strong convergence result on Algorithm
1
for
solving (
1
) using noisy data.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold and consider Algorithm
1
,
where
0\leq\beta<1
, and
\tau>1
and
\mu_{0}>0
are chosen such that (
24
) holds.
Let
n_{\delta}
be the output integer. Then there exists a solution
x^{*}
of (
1
) in

B_{2\rho}(x_{0})\cap\emph{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
such that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}}(x^{*},x_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta})=0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lim_{\delta\to 0}\|x_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}-x^{*}\|=0.

If, in addition,
\emph{Ker}(L(x^{\dagger}))\subset\emph{Ker}(L(x))
for all
x\in B_{2\rho}(x_{0})
,
then
x^{*}=x^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

Let
x^{*}
be the solution of (
1
) in
B_{2\rho}(x_{0})\cap\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
determined
in Theorem
3.5
such that
D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x^{*},x_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, where

\{x_{n}\}
denotes the sequence defined by the counterpart of Algorithm
1
using
the exact data. Let
\Delta_{n}^{\delta}:=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}^{\delta}}(x^{*},x_{n}^{\delta})
for
n\geq 0
. We will show
that
\Delta_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
by considering two cases.

Assume first that there is a sequence
\{y^{\delta_{l}}\}
of noisy data satisfying
\|y^{\delta_{l}}-y\|\leq\delta_{l}

with
\delta_{l}\to 0
such that
n_{l}:=n_{\delta_{l}}\to\hat{n}
as
l\to\infty
for some finite integer

\hat{n}
. Then
n_{l}=\hat{n}
for all large
l
. According to the definition of
n_{l}:=n_{\delta_{l}}

we have

\|F(x_{\hat{n}}^{\delta_{l}})-y^{\delta_{l}}\|\leq\tau\delta_{l}.

By taking
l\to\infty
and using lemma
3.6
, we can obtain
F(x_{\hat{n}})=y
. Thus,
we may use Lemma
3.3
(i) to obtain
x_{n}=x_{\hat{n}}
for all
n\geq\hat{n}
. Since

x_{n}\to x^{*}
as
n\to\infty
, we must have
x_{\hat{n}}=x^{*}
and thus, by Lemma
3.6

and the lower semi-continuity of
{\mathcal{R}}
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{l\to\infty}\Delta_{n_{l}}^{\delta_{l}}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{l\to\infty}\Delta_{\hat{n}}^{\delta_{l}}=\limsup_{l\to\infty}\left({\mathcal{R}}(x_{\hat{n}})-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{\hat{n}}^{\delta_{l}})-\langle\xi_{\hat{n}}^{\delta_{l}},x_{\hat{n}}-x_{\hat{n}}^{\delta_{l}}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}(x_{\hat{n}})-\liminf_{l\to\infty}{\mathcal{R}}(x_{\hat{n}}^{\delta_{l}})-\lim_{l\to\infty}\langle\xi_{\hat{n}}^{\delta_{l}},x_{\hat{n}}-x_{\hat{n}}^{\delta_{l}}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x_{\hat{n}})-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{\hat{n}})=0

which shows that
\Delta_{n_{l}}^{\delta_{l}}\to 0
as
l\to\infty
.

Assume next that there is a sequence
\{y^{\delta_{l}}\}
of noisy data satisfying
\|y^{\delta_{l}}-y\|\leq\delta_{l}

with
\delta_{l}\to 0
such that
n_{l}:=n_{\delta_{l}}\to\infty
as
l\to\infty
. Let
n
be any fixed integer.
Then
n_{l}>n
for large
l
. It then follows from Lemma
2.2
that

\Delta_{n_{l}}^{\delta_{l}}\leq\Delta_{n}^{\delta_{l}}.

By using Lemma
3.6
and the lower semi-continuity of
{\mathcal{R}}
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{l\to\infty}\Delta_{n_{l}}^{\delta_{l}}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{l\to\infty}\Delta_{n}^{\delta_{l}}=\limsup_{l\to\infty}\left({\mathcal{R}}(\hat{x})-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta_{l}})-\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta_{l}},x^{*}-x_{n}^{\delta_{l}}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n})-\langle\xi_{n},x^{*}-x_{n}\rangle=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x^{*},x_{n}).

Letting
n\to\infty
in the above equation gives
\limsup_{l\to\infty}\Delta_{n_{l}}^{\delta_{l}}\leq 0

which shows again
\Delta_{n_{l}}^{\delta_{l}}\to 0
as
l\to\infty
.
∎

i.e. Algorithm

5.
Conclusion

With the surge in machine learning advancements and the emergence of large-scale problems across
diverse domains, Polyak’s heavy ball method has experienced a resurgence of interest within the
optimization community in recent years. Much effort has been dedicated to comprehending its
acceleration effects. In this paper we
proposed an adaptive heavy ball method for solving ill-posed inverse problems, both linear and
nonlinear. This method differs from the Landweber-type method in that a momentum term is added to
define the iterates. Our method integrates a strongly convex function into the design of the algorithm
as a regularization function to detect the desired feature of the sought solution.
Moreover, we introduced novel techniques for adaptively selecting step-sizes and momentum coefficients,
aiming to achieve potential acceleration over the Landweber-type method. Notably, the updating formulae
for these parameters are explicit, obviating the need for a backtracking line search procedure and
thereby saving computational time. Under the discrepancy principle, we showed that our
method can be terminated after a finite number of iterations and established the corresponding
regularization property. We reported diverse numerical results which indicate the significant reduction
in both the required number of iterations and computational time, and thus demonstrate the superior
performance of our method over the Landweber-type method.
