Thin-Walled Higgs Assisted Q-balls from Pseudo-Nambu-Goldstone Bosons Preprint: OUTP-21-22P, DESY-21-155
Fady Bishara, Olivier Lennon

Abstract
We consider the question of whether Q-balls can exist in a chiral Lagrangian truncated at leading order when, in addition, the Standard Model Higgs boson couples to the pseudo-Nambu-Golstone bosons (pNGBs). In particular, we consider the so-called thin-wall limit where volume energy dominates over surface energy. It is known that the leading order chiral Lagrangian alone does not support such multi-field solutions. Augmented by the Higgs, however, we do indeed find that such solutions exist. We then study their properties numerically and, in various limits, analytically. Furthermore, since we consider a mirror-world-like model where the pNGBs are composite states of fundamental fermions, the question of Fermi repulsion in the high density bulk of the Q-ball plays a central role in determining its properties. The main effect is that when the parameter controlling the Fermi repulsion increases beyond a critical value, the radius of the Q-ball increase and continues to increase while the Q-ball becomes more weakly bound. As a result, there are Q-ball solutions with radii well exceeding a femtometer which would interact with nuclei in direct detection experiments via momentum-dependent form factors making their signatures striking. We leave the question of the production and direct detection of these Q-balls to a future study.



1
Introduction

Q-balls are non-topological solitons
Lee:1991ax
whose stability is ensured, simultaneously, by energy and Noether charge conservation
Coleman:1985ki
.
Specifically, a Q-ball is the most energetically-favourable state that stores charge in a theory.
In his classic analysis
Coleman:1985ki
, Coleman considered a single complex scalar field that carries a global
\displaystyle U(1)
charge.
The resulting extended objects have piqued theorists’ interests since then, as evidenced by the large body of work that ensued.
1
1

1

Coleman’s paper
Coleman:1985ki
alone has, at the time of writing, greater than
\displaystyle 800
citations.

The original Q-ball analysis has thus been expanded to accommodate multiple fields
Kusenko:1997zq
, more complicated symmetry groups
Safian:1987pr
, as well as gauging the stabilising symmetry
Lee:1988ag
;
Heeck:2021zvk
.

Finding the extremum, in field space, that corresponds to a minimum of the energy functional of the soliton can be formulated by studying a bounce equation, albeit at fixed and finite charge. This latter constraint is most naturally implemented with a Lagrange multiplier
Kusenko:1997si
.
Consequently, an exact analytical solution is generally not possible and one must resort to numerical techniques.
However, exact analytical solutions can be found in certain limits such as the ‘thin-wall’ limit
Coleman:1985ki
, the ‘thick-wall’ limit
Kusenko:1997ad
, and, more recently even beyond the strict thin-wall limit
Heeck:2020bau
.
More precisely, thin-walled Q-balls are spherically-symmetric solutions with parametrically large volumes such that the energy of the Q-ball is dominated by the bulk, or volume, energy rather than the surface energy.
2
2

2

By parametrically large, we mean here a volume per unit charge greater than the corresponding Compton wavelength of the constituent scalar.

Conversely, thick-walled Q-balls are appropriate to describe small Q-balls when the surface energy gives an important contribution to the overall rest mass of the object.

An interesting question to ask is whether the Standard Model (SM) itself, with its known field content, can admit Q-ball solutions, especially since the SM has many accidental global symmetries.
While only the matter fields are charged under these global symmetries, however, they are inherited by the scalar composite states they form below the QCD confinement scale.
This question was addressed in
Distler:1986ta
where the authors considered the leading order
\displaystyle SU(3)
-flavour-symmetric chiral Lagrangian.
The Q-balls in that study were envisioned to be composed of kaons which are stable in the limit of strangeness conservation.
It turns out, however, that no Q-ball solutions exist at leading order.

Nevertheless, Q-balls arise in many extensions of the SM, such as supersymmetric or mirror-world-like theories where additional scalar fields and global symmetries are aplenty
Kusenko:1997si
;
Kusenko:1997zq
;
Bishara:2017otb
, or in theories of extra dimensions
Demir:2000gj
;
Abel:2015tca
. They have long been considered as candidates for dark matter
Kusenko:1997si
;
Kusenko:2001vu
;
Graham:2015apa
;
Ponton:2019hux
owing to their stability against decay.
Q-balls can even play a role in baryogenesis
Krylov:2013qe
.
So far, the existence of Q-balls has not been confirmed experimentally, but, should they cross-paths with a sensitive detector, their signatures will be striking
Gelmini:2002ez
;
Kusenko:1997vp
;
Croon:2019rqu
.

In this work, we focus on a class of multi-field, thin-walled Q-balls that are stabilised by a global
\displaystyle U(1)
symmetry in a theory beyond the SM.
We have in mind a mirror-world-like scenario
Kobzarev:1966qya
;
Foot:1991bp
;
Foot:2014mia
, but leave the analogue of
\displaystyle U(1)_{Y}
ungauged. The leading order interaction between this hidden sector and the Standard Model is given through a portal interaction between the SM Higgs and a new scalar multiplet that acquires a vacuum expectation value (VEV).
Upon spontaneous symmetry breaking, the matter fields of the hidden sector acquire masses via Yukawa terms with the scalar.
The resulting breaking of an approximate chiral symmetry in the sector of hidden “quarks” leads to a number of stable scalars. The couplings of these pseudo-Nambu-Goldstone bosons (pNGBs) with the SM Higgs provide a non-trivial potential, as seen in Eq. (
2.8
), for which it is natural to ask if stable Q-ball states exist.
Here, the pNGBs are composite states of fundamental fermions and, therefore, at high density, Fermi repulsion must be taken into account as it modifies both the mass and volume of the Q-ball.

This scenario was explored in the thick-wall Q-ball limit in previous work
Bishara:2017otb
. The nature of the thick-wall limit is that the fields take on moderately low values in the Q-ball, and so higher order terms can be neglected in the Hamiltonian.
However, this is not the case in the thin-wall limit.
Higher order terms could render the potential unfavourable to Q-ball solutions.

The goal of this work is to show that Q-balls in the thin-wall limit of this theory exist and are classically stable against decay to the pions of the hidden sector.
We present numerical results, considering the effect of Fermi repulsion, as well as analytic expressions in certain limits.
These large Q-balls are interesting phenomenologically as they could form a component of dark matter.
Owing to their interactions with Standard Model states via the Higgs portal they can be detected in direct detection experiments.
Their signatures, however, are different to those of ordinary point particles due to form factor suppression at moderately-high momentum transfer
Gelmini:2002ez
. We leave this to future work on the phenomenology of this class of theories.

Note that while we have a particular UV model in mind, the only relevant states are the low energy ones, namely, the pNGBs and the SM higgs. The dynamics of the pNGBs are fully determined by the low energy effective field theory (EFT) according to the Callan-Coleman-Wess-Zumino
coset construction
Coleman:1969sm
;
Callan:1969sn
and, therefore, our analysis is more general than the model we describe. The only caveat is that in the model we have in mind, the couplings of the Higgs to the hidden sector pions are fixed by the breaking of scale invariance
Voloshin:1980zf
;
Voloshin:1985tc
;
Chivukula:1989ds
in addition to the portal coupling.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we describe the structure of the hidden sector of study. We introduce the Higgs portal term that leads to the coupling of the Standard Model Higgs with the pseudo-Nambu-Goldstone bosons that arise from the spontaneous symmetry breaking of approximate chiral symmetry in the “quark” sector of the theory. In Section
3
, we construct the Q-ball solution by analysing the scalar sector for states that minimise the energy for a given charge. We then present our numerical results, as well as regions of parameter space that allow for an analytic description. We finally discuss constraints on these solutions.

2.1
The Content of the Hidden Sector

In addition to the SM field content, we consider an
\displaystyle SU(3)^{\prime}\times SU(2)^{\prime}
gauge group in the hidden sector (HS) leaving the analogue of
\displaystyle U(1)_{Y}
ungauged.
The
\displaystyle SU(3)^{\prime}
sector is QCD-like, i.e., it is asymptotically free in the UV and
confines at energies below a scale
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\chi}^{\mathrm{HS}}
.

As for the
\displaystyle SU(2)^{\prime}
gauge group, we assume that it is spontaneously broken by a VEV of a scalar doublet,
\displaystyle S
.
A Higgs portal coupling between
\displaystyle S
and the SM doublet,
\displaystyle H
, gives the leading interaction between the two sectors.
We consider the following potential, which facilitates both spontaneous symmetry breaking in both sectors, as well as mass eigenstate mixing:

V(H,S)=-\mu_{h}^{2}H^{\dagger}H+\lambda_{h}(H^{\dagger}H)^{2}-\mu_{s}^{2}S^{\dagger}S+\lambda_{s}(S^{\dagger}S)^{2}+\lambda_{p}(H^{\dagger}H)(S^{\dagger}S)\,.

(2.1)

The final term above results in the mixing of the SM and HS Higgs gauge eigenstates
\displaystyle h^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
into the mass eigenstates
\displaystyle h
and
\displaystyle s
. This dimension-four operator is consistent with the symmetries of the theory, and could arise from integrating out higher modes.

We can determine the masses of the corresponding eigenstates of this theory. Writing the VEVs in the two sectors as
\displaystyle\left<H\right>=v_{h}/\sqrt{2}
and
\displaystyle\left<S\right>=v_{s}/\sqrt{2}
,
the gauge eigenstate
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
can be written in terms of the mass eigenstates as
\displaystyle s^{\prime}\approx s-\theta h
,
where the mixing angle
\displaystyle\theta
, in the small angle approximation, is given by,

\theta\approx\frac{\lambda_{p}v_{h}}{2\lambda_{s}v_{s}}.

(2.2)

The quartic Higgs coupling in the same approximation is, then,
\displaystyle\lambda\approx\lambda_{h}-\lambda_{p}^{2}/(4\lambda_{s})
,
where
\displaystyle\lambda_{h}
is its SM value; for more details regarding the scalar mass matrix diagonalization see
Barger:2008jx
and Appendix A in
Bishara:2017otb
.

Minimally, we mirror one full SM matter generation in the same representations and with the same charges under
\displaystyle SU(3)^{\prime}\times SU(2)^{\prime}
.
These states acquire masses through Yukawa couplings to the HS scalar doublet, S:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{HS}}\supset y_{ij}\overline{Q}_{L,i}Sq_{R,j}+\text{h.c.},

(2.3)

For concreteness, we assume that the resulting quark masses are ‘light’, i.e., below
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\chi}^{\mathrm{HS}}
, while the lepton masses are heavy.
These ‘light’ quarks will hadronise into stable pseudo-Nambu-Goldstone bosons (pNGBs).
Allowing for an arbitrary number of ‘heavy’ quark generations contributes to the HS scalar to pion couplings as in the SM via matching onto an
\displaystyle S\langle G\cdot G\rangle
operator
Chivukula:1989ds
.

2.2
Chiral Symmetry Breaking in the Hidden Sector

A necessary condition for the existence of Q-ball solutions is that the relative binding energy of the Q-ball per unit charge,
\displaystyle 1-M_{Q}/Qm_{\pi}
, be greater than zero. Furthermore, in the case of thin-wall Q-balls, the fractional binding energy scales as
\displaystyle\epsilon
with
\displaystyle\epsilon\ll 1
and thus the relevant degrees of freedom are indeed the pNGBs whose dynamics are described by a low-energy chiral Lagrangian.
The global flavour symmetry is spontaneously broken to its diagonal subgroup,
\displaystyle SU(n_{l})_{L}\times SU(n_{l})_{R}\to SU(n_{l})_{V}
, and explicitly broken by the Yukawa terms.
3
3

3

As in Ref.
Bishara:2017otb
, we ignore the fact that the symmetry group is generally
\displaystyle{U}(N_{f})_{L}\times{U}(N_{f})_{R}
since the one non-anomalous
\displaystyle U(1)
from the
\displaystyle{U}(N_{f})_{L}\times{U}(N_{f})_{R}
, that in the SM case corresponds to baryon number, acts trivially on the pNGBs, so it is not of interest to us here.

Provided that the mass matrix
\displaystyle M
is not proportional to the unit matrix, the remaining global symmetry acting on the pNGBs is
\displaystyle U(1)^{n_{l}-1}
, in the absence of other interactions. Under the assumption that the number of light quarks is 2, by Goldstone’s theorem there will be
\displaystyle 3
pNGBs: the HS pions, which transform under the unbroken
\displaystyle U(1)
symmetry. Below the chiral symmetry breaking scale of the HS, the correct effective description is the chiral Lagrangian in terms of these pNGBs.

To describe the pion sector of the theory, we conventionally define the unitary matrix field of unit determinant from the three pNGBs,
\displaystyle\pi^{a}
,

\Sigma=\exp(i\pi^{a}T^{a}/f),

(2.4)

where
\displaystyle T^{a}
are the generators of
\displaystyle SU(n_{l})
and
\displaystyle f
is a coefficient to be determined experimentally. Under the global vectorial symmetry,
\displaystyle\Sigma
transforms as

\Sigma\to\Sigma^{\prime}=V\Sigma V^{\dagger},

(2.5)

where
\displaystyle V
is given in terms of the Hermitian and traceless matrix
\displaystyle X
as
\displaystyle V=\exp(-iX)
. The leading order chiral Lagrangian is then

\mathcal{L}=\dfrac{f^{2}}{4}\mathrm{tr}\left(\partial_{\mu}\Sigma\partial^{\mu}\Sigma^{\dagger}\right)+\dfrac{B_{0}f^{2}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(M(\Sigma+\Sigma^{\dagger}-2)\right).

(2.6)

where
\displaystyle B_{0}
is another coefficient to be determined experimentally and
\displaystyle M
is the quark mass matrix.
This is the leading-order low-energy description of the HS pions, valid up to the chiral symmetry breaking scale
\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{HS}}_{\chi}\sim 4\pi f
. Despite the non-trivial potential contained within this Lagrangian, it does not admit thin-wall Q-ball solutions
Distler:1986ta
.

The HS pions of the theory are not the only light scalars which must be taken into consideration. The lightest mass eigenstate,
\displaystyle h
, associated to the scalar doublets couples to the chiral Lagrangian in a manner completely determined through the breaking of scale symmetry
Voloshin:1980zf
;
Voloshin:1985tc
;
Chivukula:1989ds
.
It is relevant to our analysis provided that its mass is smaller than those of the HS pions. Specifically, the leading order coupling of the gauge eigenstate,
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
, to the HS chiral Lagrangian is given through

\mathcal{L}=\left(1+\frac{4n_{h}}{3\beta_{0}}\frac{s^{\prime}}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{f^{2}}{4}\mathrm{tr}\left(\partial_{\mu}\Sigma\partial^{\mu}\Sigma^{\dagger}\right)+\left(1+\left[1+\frac{2n_{h}}{\beta_{0}}\right]\frac{s^{\prime}}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{B_{0}f^{2}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(M(\Sigma+\Sigma^{\dagger}-2)\right),

(2.7)

where
\displaystyle n_{h}
is the number of quarks with mass greater than the confinement scale, and
\displaystyle\beta_{0}
is the one-loop beta function. In principle, higher powers of
\displaystyle s^{\prime}/v_{s}
can also be included in Eq. (
2.7
). However, the resulting couplings to the light mass eigenstate,
\displaystyle h
, are suppressed by commensurate powers of the small mixing parameter
\displaystyle\theta
. Therefore we are justified in ignoring such terms. These additional terms originate from the Yukawa couplings of the fundamental quarks with the scalar
\displaystyle S
, and from integrating out the heavy quark degrees of freedom. The details of the numerical coefficients can be found in Ref.
Chivukula:1989ds
.

We assume that the heavier scalar,
\displaystyle s
, has a mass larger than the chiral symmetry breaking scale, and is thus irrelevant for this low-energy description. The full low-energy Lagrangian describing the HS scalars with masses less than
\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{HS}}_{\chi}
is then

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}=&\left(1-\theta\dfrac{2\eta}{3}\dfrac{h}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{f^{2}}{4}\mathrm{tr}\left(\partial_{\mu}\Sigma\partial^{\mu}\Sigma^{\dagger}\right)+\left(1-\theta\left(1+\eta\right)\dfrac{h}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{B_{0}f^{2}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(M(\Sigma+\Sigma^{\dagger}-2)\right)\\
&+\dfrac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}h\partial^{\mu}h-U(h),\end{split}

(2.8)

where we have defined
\displaystyle\eta=2n_{h}/\beta_{0}
and the Higgs potential is given by

U(h)=\dfrac{1}{2}m_{h}^{2}h^{2}+\lambda v_{h}h^{3}+\frac{1}{4}\lambda h^{4}.

(2.9)

3
Higgs assisted thin-wall Q-balls

The hidden sector theory with a Higgs portal to the SM described in the previous section was shown to admit thick-wall Q-ball solutions in Ref.
Bishara:2017otb
. In this paper, we turn our attention to the question of whether it admits
thin-wall
Q-ball solutions, where the volume energy dominates and the surface energy is negligible.

3.1
Minimising the Energy in a Sector of Fixed Charge

A Q-ball is a solution of minimum energy at fixed charge. The energy functional that should be minimized is given by

\mathcal{E}_{\omega}=H+\mathcal{E}_{F}+\omega\left(Q-\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,J^{0}\right),

(3.1)

where
\displaystyle H
is the Hamiltonian of the theory with Lagrange density given by Eq. (
2.8
) and
\displaystyle\omega
is a Lagrange multiplier that enforces the fixed charge constraint.
The second term on the right hand side,
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{F}
, is the total Fermi repulsion energy which must be included to account for the fact that the HS pions are fermion/anti-fermion composites states.
Therefore, the overlap between HS pion wave functions cannot be arbitrarily large.

The average energy contributed to the Q-ball per constituent fermion is

\frac{3}{5}E_{F}=\frac{3}{10m_{f}}(3\pi^{2}n)^{2/3}\,,

(3.2)

where
\displaystyle E_{F}
is the Fermi energy,
\displaystyle n
is the number density of a fermionic species, and
\displaystyle m_{f}
is the dressed quark mass, i.e., the mass of an excitation with the same quantum numbers as a quark, within the Q-ball medium. Typically,
\displaystyle m_{f}\sim\Lambda^{\mathrm{HS}}_{\chi}
, which is not set in our theory and so can in principle be large relative to other scales.
4
4

4

If the chiral symmetry breaking scale is sufficiently high, one might wonder if it might give rise to unnaturally large corrections to the Higgs mass through pion loops. The cubic Higgs-pion coupling gives rise to corrections that are logarithmic in
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\chi}^{\textsc{hs}}/m_{\pi}
, however, and therefore naturalness is not a problem in this case.
The total energy contributed to the Q-ball is thus

\mathcal{E}_{F}=\frac{1}{5m_{f}}\left(243\pi^{4}\frac{Q^{5}}{V^{2}}\right)^{1/3},

(3.3)

where
\displaystyle V
is the volume of the Q-ball, and
\displaystyle Q
the total charge.

The Noether charge (last term in Eq. (
3.1
)) is associated to the invariance of the Lagrangian in Eq. (
2.8
) under the transformation

\Sigma\to\exp(-i\alpha X)\Sigma\exp(i\alpha X)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad h\to h,

(3.4)

where
\displaystyle X
is a Hermitian charge operator.
The Noether charge functional is given by

\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,J^{0}=i\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\left(1-\theta\dfrac{2\eta}{3}\dfrac{h}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{f^{2}}{4}\mathrm{tr}\left(\dot{\Sigma}[\Sigma,X]+\dot{\Sigma}^{\dagger}[\Sigma^{\dagger},X]\right).

(3.5)

Hence, a Q-ball must be time-dependent solution of the classical equations of motion as is well known
Coleman:1985ki
. Explicitly isolating the time-dependent terms in the energy functional gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\omega}=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x

\displaystyle\left\{\left(1-\theta\dfrac{2\eta}{3}\dfrac{h}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{f^{2}}{4}\mathrm{tr}\left(|\dot{\Sigma}-i\omega[\Sigma,X]|^{2}-\omega^{2}[\Sigma,X][X,\Sigma^{\dagger}]+\nabla\Sigma\cdot\nabla\Sigma^{\dagger}\right)\right.

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left.-\left(1-\theta\left(1+\eta\right)\dfrac{h}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{B_{0}f^{2}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(M(\Sigma+\Sigma^{\dagger}-2)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dot{h}^{2}+\nabla h\cdot\nabla h\right)+U(h)\right\}+\omega Q+\mathcal{E}_{F}\,.

The two terms with explicit time-dependence are minimised if they vanish,
5
5

5

The field configuration
\displaystyle\Sigma(\vec{r},t)=1
leads to
\displaystyle Q=0
. Thus, a configuration with non-zero charge must differ from the vacuum in some domain.
and so

\Sigma(\vec{r},t)=\exp(-i\omega Xt)\Sigma(\vec{r})\exp(i\omega Xt)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad h(\vec{r},t)=h(\vec{r})\,.

(3.7)

After reinsertion of the Q-ball ansatz into the energy functional, what remains is known as a Euclidean bounce
Coleman:1977py
;
Callan:1977pt
;
Coleman:1977th
. This well-studied class of differential equations is generally intractable analytically, but progress can be made in certain limits. In previous work, the thick-wall limit
Kusenko:1997ad
was analysed for Q-ball solutions
Bishara:2017otb
. This work is concerned with the opposite limit: thin-wall Q-balls
Coleman:1985ki
.

A thin-wall Q-ball is characterised by a core of a homogeneous state, named Q-matter, and a thin outer shell. The mass of a thin-wall Q-ball is dominated by this core.
6
6

6

This statement holds apart from in the case where the underlying fields comprising the Q-ball take on configurations such that the potential energy vanishes inside the homogeneous core (see Ref.
Spector:1987ag
). In this case, the mass of the resulting Q-ball is dependent only on its surface energy.
We let
\displaystyle\Sigma(\vec{r})=\Sigma_{0}
and
\displaystyle h(\vec{r})=h_{0}
be constant spatial profiles of the fields inside the core of the Q-ball, such that

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\approx&-\omega^{2}\left(1-\theta\dfrac{2\eta}{3}\dfrac{h_{0}}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{f^{2}}{4}\mathrm{tr}\left([\Sigma_{0},X][X,\Sigma_{0}^{\dagger}]\right)V\\
&-\left(1-\theta\left(1+\eta\right)\dfrac{h_{0}}{v_{s}}\right)\dfrac{B_{0}f^{2}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(M(\Sigma_{0}+\Sigma^{\dagger}_{0}-2)\right)V\\
&+U(h_{0})V+\omega Q+\mathcal{E}_{F},\end{split}

(3.8)

where
\displaystyle V
is the volume of the core of the Q-ball.
To determine the mass of the resulting Q-ball, this expression must be minimised with respect to the field content, as well as the volume and the Lagrange multiplier.

So far, we have not specified the number of light flavours,
\displaystyle n_{l}
, in the analysis that led to Eq. (
3.8
). To make further progress, it is useful to specialize to our case of interest,
\displaystyle SU(n_{l})=SU(2)
, which will allow us to simplify the exponentials involving
\displaystyle\hat{n}\cdot\vec{T}
, where
\displaystyle T^{a}
are the generators of
\displaystyle SU(n_{l})
.
In this case,
\displaystyle T^{a}\propto\sigma^{a}
, where
\displaystyle\sigma^{a}
are the Pauli matrices.
Since
\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}
is now an element of
\displaystyle SU(2)
, we can write it as,

\Sigma_{0}=\exp\left(i\varphi\hat{n}\cdot\sigma\right)=\cos\varphi+i\left(\hat{n}\cdot\sigma\right)\sin\varphi,

(3.9)

where it is understood that
\displaystyle\cos\varphi
multiplies a unit
\displaystyle 2\times 2
matrix, and
\displaystyle\hat{n}=(n_{1},\,n_{2},\,n_{3})
is a unit vector,
\displaystyle\hat{n}^{2}=1
.
Furthermore, without loss of generality, we can choose the charge operator, which appears in Eq. (
3.7
),
\displaystyle X=\sigma_{3}/2
since the Lagrangian is invariant under
\displaystyle SU(2)
transformations.

For convenience, we introduce a small parameter,
\displaystyle\epsilon
, defined as,

\epsilon\equiv\theta\dfrac{h_{0}}{v_{s}}\,.

(3.10)

Here, we recall that
\displaystyle\theta
is the mixing angle between the SM and HS Higgses and is less than unity following our choice to work in the small angle limit. Furthermore, the ratio of VEVs
\displaystyle h_{0}/v_{s}
must also be small otherwise the effective field theory describing our system is not valid. Therefore, we also have that
\displaystyle\epsilon\ll 1
.
Minimising with respect to the Lagrange multiplier,
\displaystyle\omega
, yields

Q=\left(1-\dfrac{2\eta}{3}\epsilon\right)f^{2}\omega(1-n_{3}^{2})\sin^{2}\varphi V\,.

(3.11)

This expression corresponds precisely to the one for the charge as determined from Eq. (
3.5
). We use this to eliminate
\displaystyle\omega
, giving

\begin{split}E=&\dfrac{Q^{2}}{2\left(1-\dfrac{2\eta}{3}\epsilon\right)f^{2}(1-n_{3}^{2})\sin^{2}\varphi V}+\left(1-\left(1+\eta\right)\epsilon\right)m_{\pi}^{2}f^{2}(1-\cos\varphi)V\\
&+U(h_{0})\,V+\frac{1}{5m_{f}}\left(243\pi^{4}\frac{Q^{5}}{V^{2}}\right)^{1/3}.\end{split}

(3.12)

The only dependence on the direction of the VEV of
\displaystyle\Sigma
is the factor
\displaystyle n_{3}
in the first term. The energy of the Q-ball is minimized for
\displaystyle n_{3}=0
, which corresponds to zero VEV for the neutral pions. This behaviour is unsurprising since a neutral pion VEV inside the Q-ball contributes to its mass but not to its charge – unlike the SM Higgs, the neutral pion does not offer a way to reduce the mass of the resulting Q-ball for a given charge.

We further note that, in the expression that must be minimised,
\displaystyle E/Q
, the volume and charge of the Q-ball always appear in the form
\displaystyle V/Q
. Thus, we infer that
\displaystyle V
scales linearly with
\displaystyle Q
for thin-wall Q-balls, even in the presence of Fermi degeneracy pressure. This is in contrast with the thick-wall case, where
\displaystyle V\sim Q^{-3}
, for small

Before we continue, we note the complications in proceeding analytically. Due to the non-trivial dependence on
\displaystyle V
, it is not feasible to analytically minimise the energy of the Q-ball with respect to the volume exactly. This is entirely due to the presence of the Fermi degeneracy pressure.
Furthermore, the presence of
\displaystyle n_{h}
heavy quarks in Eq. (
3.12

\displaystyle n_{h}=0
to simplify our expressions – we will reintroduce this parameter in our numerical results. The Higgs potential is also a barrier to obtaining exact analytical expressions. We will discuss this as we proceed below.

3.2
Higgs-Assisted Thin-Wall Q-balls

For notational convenience, let us define the dimensionless variables,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{E}\equiv\frac{E}{m_{\pi}\,Q}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu\equiv\frac{m_{\pi}\,f^{2}\,V}{Q}\,,

(3.13)

together with the dimensionless parameters

\boxed{A\equiv\frac{(243\pi^{4})^{1/3}}{5}\left(\frac{f^{4}}{m_{f}^{3}\,m_{\pi}}\right)^{1/3}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad B\equiv\frac{1}{4}\frac{\lambda}{\theta^{4}}\frac{v_{s}^{4}}{m_{\pi}^{2}\,f^{2}}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad C\equiv\theta\frac{v_{h}}{v_{s}}.}

(3.14)

This equation is enclosed in a box for referential convenience since all of the ensuing analysis will be done using these dimensionless parameters defined here. These parameters effectively control the size of the contribution to the Q-ball mass due to the presence of Fermi degeneracy pressure, and the SM Higgs potential. Note that
\displaystyle C
is not entirely independent of
\displaystyle B
; they are related through

BC^{4}=\frac{\lambda}{4}\frac{v_{h}^{4}}{m_{\pi}^{2}f^{2}}.

(3.15)

This relation is always satisfied for some parameter values in each theory. Moreover, by Eq. (
2.2
), we see that
\displaystyle C\propto\theta^{2}
, and so it is related to the mixing between the SM and the HS. Thus, we see that
\displaystyle C
is naturally small.

Consider the example of
\displaystyle m_{\pi}\sim f\sim 4v_{h}
and
\displaystyle\lambda\sim 0.1
, then
\displaystyle BC^{4}\sim 10^{-4}
. Since, in the small angle approximation,
\displaystyle\theta\lesssim 10^{-1}
, we also have that

B\gtrsim 10^{3}\left(\frac{v_{s}}{f}\right)^{4},\quad C\lesssim 10^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{h}}{v_{s}}\right),\quad\frac{\lambda_{p}}{\lambda_{s}}\left(\frac{v_{h}}{v_{s}}\right)\lesssim 10^{-1},

(3.16)

where the final inequality comes from the definition of
\displaystyle\theta
in Eq. (
2.2
). If there is no hierarchy between the two parameters
\displaystyle\lambda_{p}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{s}
, then we see that
\displaystyle C\lesssim 10^{-2}
and
\displaystyle B\gtrsim 10^{4}
, which in turn requires that
\displaystyle v_{s}\gtrsim f
– this latter constraint is consistent with the idea that the “heavy Higgs” is massive enough that we need not include it in our effective theory. Realistically, the parameters can be different from these assignments, but we use values similar to these in our numerical study below.

Q-ball stability requires, at the minimum of the energy, that
\displaystyle\hat{E}<1
; that is, the mass of the Q-ball must be less than the product of the total charge and the HS pion mass such that it cannot classically decay into pions. With these definitions, the energy of the Q-ball reads,

\widehat{E}=\frac{1}{2\sin^{2}\varphi\,\nu}+(1-\epsilon)(1-\cos\varphi)\,\nu+B\left(4C^{2}\epsilon^{2}+4C\epsilon^{3}+\epsilon^{4}\right)\,\nu+\frac{A}{\nu^{2/3}}\,,

(3.17)

where we have set
\displaystyle\eta=0
, as discussed above. Minimising this expression with respect to
\displaystyle\epsilon
,
\displaystyle\varphi
and
\displaystyle\nu
yields, respectively,

\begin{split}4B\left(2C^{2}\epsilon+3C\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon^{3}\right)&=(1-\cos\varphi)\\
(1-\epsilon)\nu^{2}&=\frac{\cos\varphi}{(1-\cos^{2}\varphi)^{2}}\\
(1-\epsilon)(1-\cos\varphi)+B\left(4C^{2}\epsilon^{2}+4C\epsilon^{3}+\epsilon^{4}\right)&=\frac{1}{2(1-\cos^{2}\varphi)\nu^{2}}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{A}{\nu^{5/3}}.\end{split}

(3.18)

It is not possible to simultaneously solve these equations analytically due to the combination of the terms proportional to
\displaystyle\nu^{-2}
and
\displaystyle\nu^{-5/3}
, as well as the Higgs self-interactions.

One might naively expect that, in the large volume limit, we might ignore the term proportional to
\displaystyle\nu^{2}
, however, this becomes equivalent to the limits, either
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 1
,
\displaystyle\phi\to 0
, or
\displaystyle\nu\to 0
, which are all inconsistent with our assumptions (
\displaystyle\epsilon<1
) or the requirements for a stable Q-ball solution (
\displaystyle\phi\not=0
and
\displaystyle\nu>0
).
We must therefore proceed numerically obtaining, at best, analytical approximations in certain limits.

Naively, we might expect that, since they represent contributions to the Q-ball energy without a corresponding contribution to the charge, the parameters
\displaystyle A
,
\displaystyle B
and
\displaystyle C
(see Eq. (
3.14
)) should be as small as possible for a stable Q-ball to form.
However, note that if we set
\displaystyle B\to 0
in the above, then
\displaystyle\varphi\to 0
. This represents the vacuum solution, i.e., no stable Q-ball forms. Thus, counterintuitively, the Higgs potential is a necessary component in the stabilisation of these Q-balls; it is not enough for the Higgs to merely couple linearly to the pNGBs.

In the limit of
\displaystyle A\to 0
and
\displaystyle C\to 0
, with
\displaystyle B
non-zero, these equations can be readily solved for a stable Q-ball solution. We find that, to leading order,

\varphi^{2}\approx 8B\epsilon^{3},\quad\nu\approx\frac{1}{8B\epsilon^{3}}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\epsilon\approx\frac{1}{(8B)^{1/2}}.

(3.19)

The resulting physical properties of the Q-balls are given by

m_{Q}\approx m_{\pi}Q\left(1-\frac{1}{8}\frac{1}{(2B)^{1/2}}\right)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad V\approx\frac{Q}{m_{\pi}f^{2}}(8B)^{1/2}.

(3.20)

We see that the resulting Q-balls are stable, provided that
\displaystyle B>1/128
.
In fact,
\displaystyle B\gtrsim\mathcal{O}(1)
since the expansion is in terms of
\displaystyle\epsilon\ll 1
, and so this condition is always satisfied whenever the expansion in
\displaystyle\epsilon
is valid.

For the resulting Q-balls to be well-approximated by this idealised solution, we require that
\displaystyle A\ll 1
and that the Higgs potential be well-defined by its quartic term in the centre of the Q-ball, i.e., that
\displaystyle 4C\ll\epsilon
. Considering the definition of
\displaystyle C
and
\displaystyle\epsilon
given above, this translates to
\displaystyle v_{h}\ll h_{0}
, and so the VEV of the Higgs inside the Q-ball must be much greater than its VEV outside it. This occurs if the separation between the scale of the Higgs and the scale of the HS pNGBs is large. To corroborate this, we examine this inequality in terms of the solution above,

8\sqrt{2}B^{1/2}C\ll 1,

(3.21)

which, in terms of the fundamental parameters of the theory, is

\frac{v_{s}^{2}}{m_{\pi}f}\ll 1,

(3.22)

where we have assumed that no hierarchy exists between the parameters
\displaystyle\lambda_{s}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{p}
, and taken
\displaystyle\lambda\sim 0.1
. Thus, we see that, for this limit to be realistic, we require a hierarchy between the scale of the pNGBs and the VEV of the
heavier
HS Higgs. Moreover, since we ignored the HS Higgs from our analysis, for this to be self-consistent, we would require a large splitting between
\displaystyle m_{\pi}
and
\displaystyle f
. We thus see that this limit is highly idealised (and is contrary to our realistic parameter values discussed above).

Finally, we comment on the phenomenology of these Q-balls if they form a component of the observed dark matter abundance. In order to give an analytic understanding, we consider the idealised scenario of
\displaystyle A\to 0
and
\displaystyle C\to 0
in what follows. Q-balls may not have arbitrarily large charge

;
Sakai:2011wn
;
Tamaki:2011bx
. A constraint on the maximum charge of Q-balls comes from demanding that the radius is always larger than the corresponding Schwarzschild radius,
\displaystyle R_{Q}>2M_{Q}/M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}
. This gives a constraint on the charge to be

Q<\left[\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{16\pi}\right]^{1/2}\frac{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{3}}{m_{\pi}^{2}f}B^{1/4}.

(3.26)

Since both the volume and energy of the Q-balls we studied scale with the charge, this in turn sets an upper bound on the mass and volume of these Q-balls before they collapse into black holes:

m_{Q}<\left[\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{16\pi}\right]^{1/2}\frac{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{3}}{m_{\pi}f}B^{1/4}

(3.27)

and

R_{Q}<\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{54\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{3}}\right]^{1/6}\frac{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}{m_{\pi}f}B^{1/4}.

(3.28)

For some typical values for the idealised case of
\displaystyle m_{\pi}\sim\mathrm{TeV}
,
\displaystyle f\sim 10\mathrm{TeV}
,
\displaystyle v_{s}\sim 5\mathrm{TeV}
,
\displaystyle\theta\sim 0.01
and
\displaystyle\lambda\sim 0.1
, this sets an upper bound of
\displaystyle\sim 10
cm! These Q-balls can therefore be phenomenologically relevant, far below their upper bound in size, in dark matter experiments seeking direct detection, as they are in principle distinguishable from point-like particle states due to form factor suppression at moderately-high momentum transfer. The question, therefore, is how can such large Q-balls be formed in the early Universe? The build-up of Q-balls from collisions with constituent quanta, coined ‘solitosynthesis’
Frieman:1989bx
;
Griest:1989bq
, is unlikely to result in such large Q-balls, or even Q-balls that are large enough to be distinguished phenomenologically. This question is the subject of ongoing study.

4
Summary and conclusions

In this paper, we have shown that mirror-world-like theories, with the analogue of hypercharge ungauged, can support stable Q-ball states in the thin-wall limit. The stabilisation of these Q-balls is ensured by energy and charge conservation but also requires a portal coupling with the SM Higgs. This work is an extension of previous work on the so-called thick-wall limit, valid for small charges. Here, we focussed on the thin-wall limit which describes Q-balls with large charges. We have found several regimes that allow solutions to exist, each described by the interplay between the Fermi repulsion, Higgs potential, the mixing between the two sectors, and the number of heavy “quarks” in the hidden sector. For a wide range of parameter values, we have shown that these solutions are stable – however, in the case that the Higgs quadratic and cubic self-couplings are relevant, the Q-ball is rendered unstable if the Fermi repulsion is too large. In this latter case, the resulting Q-balls are sub-femtometre in size. However, if the quartic is the most relevant term in the Higgs potential, then the size of these Q-balls is primarily set by the Fermi repulsion itself and these Q-balls can be quite large in size.

In addition, if the Q-balls could contribute to the dark matter abundance inferred to exist in our Universe, the SM Higgs would provide a portal between the SM and the Q-balls. This coupling would allow for the direct detection of the Q-balls in existing dark matter experiments. Though this also requires a full phenomenological study of the evolution from formation to the detection of these objects. Such a study is warranted since the signatures in direct detection experiments would be striking. These extended objects can reach, in certain parts of parameter space, radii much greater than SM bound states, and thus the event rate can be sharply peaked at low momentum transfer due to form factor suppression. This study is the subject of future work.
