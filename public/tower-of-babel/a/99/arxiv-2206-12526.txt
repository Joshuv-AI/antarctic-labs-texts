The semigroup of endomorphisms with restricted range of an independence algebra. Thanks: This research has been carried out thanks to a PhD studentship from the department of Mathematics of the University of York.
Ambroise Grau

Abstract
. Since its introduction by Symons, the semigroup of maps with restricted range has been studied in the context of transformations on a set, or of linear maps on a vector space. Sets and vector spaces being particular examples of independence algebras, a natural question that arises is whether by taking the semigroup T ⁡ ( 𝒜 , ℬ ) T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}) of all endomorphisms of an independence algebra 𝒜 \mathcal{A} whose image lie in a subalgebra ℬ \mathcal{B} , one can obtain corresponding results as in the cases of sets and vector spaces. In this paper, we put under a common framework the research from Sanwong, Sommanee, Sullivan, Mendes-Gonçalves and all their predecessors. We describe Green’s relations as well as the ideals of T ⁡ ( 𝒜 , ℬ ) T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}) following their lead. We then take a new direction, completely describing all of the extended Green’s relations on T ⁡ ( 𝒜 , ℬ ) T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}) . We make no restriction on the dimension of our algebras as the results in the finite and infinite dimensional cases generally take the same form.

1.
Introduction

The full transformation monoid
\mathcal{T}_{X}
of a set
X
, the monoid of linear transformations
\mathrm{End}(V)
on a vector space
V
, as well as their generalisation to the endomorphism monoid
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
of an independence algebra
\mathcal{A}
have been the focus of a large amount of research during the last decades.
Following Malcev’s work on the automorphism group of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, Symons
[
15
]
investigated the automorphism group of the subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
consisting of all maps with range restricted to
Y\subseteq X
, denoted by
T(X,Y)
.
Later, Nenthein, Youngkhong and Kemprasit started the study of the properties of
T(X,Y)
which led to similar studies of
T(V,W)
, the semigroup of linear transformations of a vector space
V
with restricted range in a subspace
W
.
Since both sets and vector spaces lie in the general framework of independence algebras, we use this more generic structure to describe and extend these studies.
We give full details of independence algebras in Section
2
.
For the moment, it is enough for the reader to know they behave similarly to sets and vector spaces in terms of endomorphisms.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an independence algebra, and
\mathcal{B}
be a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
.
We make no global assumptions concerning the cardinalities of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, which means that these could be either finite or infinite dimensional algebras.
Define by
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
the semigroup of all endomorphisms from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
, that is, the set

T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\{\alpha\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})\mid\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathcal{B}\}.

Of course, if
\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
then
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\emptyset
and we will therefore exclude this case.
Otherwise the set
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is easily seen to be a subsemigroup of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
, and if
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
it is equal to the monoid
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
.
In fact, we will prove later in Corollary
2.2
that unless
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{B}
is a singleton, then
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is not isomorphic to the endomorphism monoid
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{C})
of any independence algebra
\mathcal{C}
.

In the case where
\mathcal{A}
is simply a set, Green’s relations on
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
have been studied by Sanwong and Sommanee in
[
13
]
, while Sullivan
[
14
]
carried out the same work when
\mathcal{A}
is a vector space, following the exhibition of the regular elements in both cases by Nenthein, Youngkhong and Kemprasit in
[
9
,
10
]
.
Later, Mendes-Gonçalves and Sullivan
[
7
,
14
]
gave the structure of the ideals of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
for both the set and the vector space case.
The sets
\left\{\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\mid\mathrm{rank}~\alpha<r\right\}
for any cardinal
r
are ideals of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
However, unlike the case for
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
these ideals are not the only ones present in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
in general, nor do they form a chain.
In
[
7
,
14
]
, the authors used the ideal structure when the dimension of
\mathcal{B}
is at least
3
in order to construct two ideals that are not comparable under containment. Doing this, they showed a weaker version of Corollary
2.2
, namely, that if
\dim\mathcal{B}\geq 3
then
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
cannot be isomorphic to
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{C})
for any appropriate
\mathcal{C}
since ideals of the latter always form a chain.
Even if the proof we give for this corollary does not rely on the ideal structure of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, we will give similar examples of ideals not forming a chain to give the reader an idea on this particular behaviour.

After recalling the notion of independence algebras used in this paper, the first sections will closely follow the work of previous authors.
We will thus directly state their common results and simply enhance their proofs to fit under the general framework of independence algebras.
We start by looking at the regular elements in Section
3
, and derive Green’s relations in Section
4
as well as the ideal structure in Section
5
.
An astute reader can retrace in the aforementioned papers the origin of the ideas used throughout the proofs present in these three sections.

The major part of this article is devoted to giving new results on the semigroup
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
where
\mathcal{A}
is an independence algebra and
\mathcal{B}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
.
From Corollary
3.2
, we have that
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is not a regular semigroup in general, and in order to further understand its structure, we turn ourselves to the extended Green’s relations
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
,
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
,
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
.
These equivalence relations were introduced to complement the study of regular semigroups since these only differ from the initial Green’s relations in non-regular semigroups.
While working on the structure of semigroups that are not necessarily regular, Fountain
[
3
]
introduced the notion of
abundant semigroups
, later extended further to the concept of
Fountain semigroups
(formerly called semi-abundant semigroups by El-Qallali
[
2
]
).
These are semigroups in which every
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
-class [resp.
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
] contains an idempotent, emulating the role that the relations
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
play in a regular semigroup.
It is well-known that the relations
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
commute.
However, this is not the case for
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
nor
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
and this presents extra difficulties in describing the joins
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}=\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\vee\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}=\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}\vee\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
.
Additionally, the equivalence relations
\mathop{\mathcal{J}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}}
induced by the principal
*
-ideals and
\sim
-ideals complete the list of studied relations and are shown to coincide with
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
.
All of these extended Green’s relations are fully characterised on
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
in Section
6
, and allow us to give a new example of right-abundant semigroups (that is, where all
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
-classes contain an idempotent), and thus right-Fountain semigroups, that are not abundant nor Fountain since not all
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-classes contain an idempotent.

2.
Preliminaries and notation

In order to allow this paper to be almost self-contained, we give here a short overview of the notion of independence algebras that will be necessary.

These were introduced by Gould in
[
4
]
in order to account for the similarities between endomorphisms of sets, vector spaces and free group acts, and to allow their study under a more general setting.
Incidentally, it was noted that independence algebras also corresponds to the
v^{*}
-algebras described by Narkiewicz
[
8
]
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\big\langle A;F\big\rangle
be a (universal) algebra, with
A
a non-empty set as its
universe
and
F
its (possibly infinite) set of
fundamental operations
that all have finite arity.
As a convention, we will always denote the underlying universe of an algebra, say
\mathcal{K}
, by the corresponding non-scripted capital letter, here
K
.
Fundamental operations can be composed as follows: if
f
is an
n
-ary operation and
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
are all
m
-ary operations, then
f\circ(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is an
m
-ary operation defined by
f\circ(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})(x)=f(g_{1}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x))
for all
x\in A^{m}
.
We also call
projections
the maps
\pi_{i}^{n}\colon A^{n}\to A
for all
1\leq i\leq n
sending the
n
-tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
to
x_{i}
.
With this, for an integer
n\geq 0
, an
n
-ary
term operation
t
is a map
t\colon A^{n}\to A
built by successive compositions of fundamental operations and projections, where a nullary term operation
f()=a
will be identified with its image
a\in A
and will be called a
constant
.
For any
X\subseteq A
, the universe of the
subalgebra generated by
X
will be denoted by
\langle X\rangle
and consists of all elements
t(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
where
t
is a term operation and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in X
.
The subalgebra generated by
\emptyset
consists of all the elements that can be obtained using nullary term operations, and thus
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle=\emptyset
if and only if
\mathcal{A}
has no constants.
In order to facilitate readability and to follow classical semigroup notation, terms of our algebras will be operating on the left while functions on our algebras will be operating on the right.
We will also give precedence to terms in the evaluation, so that fewer parentheses are needed.

A set
X\subseteq A
is said to be
independent
if for all
x\in X
we have that
x\notin\big\langle X\setminus\left\{x\right\}\big\rangle
, and it is a
basis
of
\mathcal{A}
(or
A
) if it is independent and generates
A
.
We say that an algebra
\mathcal{A}
satisfy the
exchange property
(EP) if all subsets
X
of
A
satisfy the following condition:

for all
a,b\in A
, if
b\in\big\langle X\cup\left\{a\right\}\big\rangle
and
b\notin\langle X\rangle
, then
a\in\big\langle X\cup\left\{b\right\}\big\rangle
.

An important point from
[
4
]
is that any algebra
\mathcal{A}
that satisfies (EP) has a basis, and in addition in such algebra, a set
X
is a basis if and only if it is a maximal independent set, if and only if it is a minimal generating set.
Additionally, in such an algebra, any independent set can be extended to a basis of
\mathcal{A}
and all bases have the same cardinality, called the
dimension
(or the
rank
) of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted by
\dim\mathcal{A}
.
Furthermore, for any
B\subseteq A
, the
dimension
(or
rank
) of
B
is the cardinality of a basis
X
of the subalgebra
\mathcal{B}=\langle B\rangle
and the
codimension
(or
corank
) of
\mathcal{B}
in
\mathcal{A}
, denoted
\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B
, is the cardinality of an independent set
Z
such that
X\sqcup Z
forms a basis of
\mathcal{A}
.

For
B,C\subseteq A
, a function
\alpha\colon B\to C
is a
homomorphism of
\mathcal{A}

if for all
n
-ary term operations
t
and all
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in B
, we have that
t(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\alpha=t(b_{1}\alpha,\dots,b_{n}\alpha)
.
Moreover,
\alpha
is an
isomorphism
between the subalgebras
\mathcal{B}=\langle B\rangle
and
\mathcal{C}=\langle C\rangle
if it is also bijective, which will be denoted by
\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{C}
.
Note that in particular, this forces any homomorphism to act as the identity on constants.
An
endomorphism of
\mathcal{A}
is a homomorphism
\alpha\colon A\to A
, and the set of all endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
is denoted by
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
, which is a monoid under composition of functions.

An algebra
\mathcal{A}
satisfying (EP) has the
free basis property
(F) if any map defined from a basis
X\subseteq A
to
A
can be extended to an endomorphism of
\mathcal{A}
.
An
independence algebra
is an algebra
\mathcal{A}
that satisfies both the exchange property and the free basis property.
Consequently, for
\mathcal{A}
an independence algebra, any subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
is an independence algebra in its own right.
Lastly, for an endomorphism
\alpha\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
, the
rank of
\alpha
is defined as the rank of the subalgebra
A\alpha
.
Independence algebras have been classified by Urbanik
[
16
]
and include, as previously mentioned, sets and vector spaces.

We let
\mathcal{A}
be an independence algebra with a non-empty subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
.
It is clear that
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, as defined in Section
1
, is a subsemigroup of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
, but not usually a monoid as given by the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

The semigroup
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is a monoid if and only if
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
, or
\mathcal{B}
is a singleton.

Proof
.

Clearly if
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
then
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
, and if
\mathcal{B}
is a singleton, say
\left\{b\right\}
, then
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\left\{c_{b}\right\}
, where
c_{b}
is the constant map with value
b
.
Moreover, both of these are indeed monoids.

Let us now assume that
\mathcal{B}\subsetneq\mathcal{A}
and let
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
.
Since
\mathcal{A}
satisfies (EP) we extend a basis of
B
to a basis
X
of
A
and take
a
to be one of the basis element in
A\setminus B
.
Define two maps
\alpha,\beta\colon X\to A
such that
a\alpha=b_{1}
,
a\beta=b_{2}
and
\alpha
,
\beta
act as identity maps on
X\cap B
(with elements of
X\setminus(B\cup\left\{a\right\})
arbitrarily sent to some elements in
B
).
From the free basis property we know that
\alpha
and
\beta
can be extended to endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
, and thus we can assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
belonged to
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
in the first place.
Suppose that
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is a monoid, and denote its identity by
\epsilon
.
Now let
c\in B
be such that
a\epsilon=c
.
Then, as
\epsilon
is a left identity for
\alpha
and
\alpha\lvert_{B}
is the identity on
B
, we need
b_{1}=a\alpha=a\epsilon\alpha=c\alpha=c
.
Similarly,
\epsilon
is a left identity for
\beta
and thus
b_{2}=a\beta=a\epsilon\beta=c
. Therefore
b_{1}=b_{2}
, which means that
\mathcal{B}
is a singleton.
This shows that for any proper subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
with at least two distinct elements, the semigroup
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
does not contain a two-sided identity, and thus is not a monoid.∎

From this as well as the fact that the trivial monoid is the endomorphism monoid of an algebra with a single element, we directly obtain the following result:

Corollary 2.2
.

The semigroup
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{C})
for
\mathcal{C}
an independence algebra if and only if
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
, or
\mathcal{B}
is a singleton.

In order to facilitate the reading of the proofs, the following notation will be used throughout the paper:

\bullet

the image of
\alpha\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
is
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=A\alpha
and its rank is
\rho(\alpha)=\dim(\mathrm{im\,}\alpha)
;

\bullet

the set
\{e_{i}\mid i\in I\}\subseteq A
is abbreviated as
\{e_{i}\}
without necessarily specifying the index set that will be denoted by capitalising the letter used as index, here
I
, and such a set will most of the time be non-empty and can be either finite or infinite;

\bullet

the closure operator is denoted by
\langle\cdot\rangle
and by writing
C=\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle
we mean that
C
is the subalgebra generated by
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
, while having
C=\langle\left\{b_{1},b_{2}\right\}\rangle
gives the additional information that
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
form a basis for
C
;

\bullet

similarly, by
B=\langle\left\{e_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{f_{j}\right\}\rangle
we mean that the sets
\{e_{i}\}
and
\left\{f_{j}\right\}
are independent sets and that together they form a basis for the subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
, and by
B=C\sqcup\langle\left\{x_{k}\right\}\rangle
we mean that
C
is a subalgebra of
B
and that a basis of
C
can be extended to a basis of
B
through
\left\{x_{k}\right\}
;

\bullet

for
A=\big\langle\{x_{i}\}\sqcup\{y_{j}\}\big\rangle
, by writing
\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&y_{j}\\
b_{i}&d_{j}\end{pmatrix}
we mean that
x_{i}\alpha=b_{i}
,
y_{j}\alpha=d_{j}
for all
i\in I,j\in J
, and that this map is the endomorphism obtained by extension of the underlying map on the basis using the free basis property of our independence algebra. We also notice that the sets
\{b_{i}\}
and
\{d_{j}\}
are not necessarily independent and that their intersection is not necessarily empty, and if both are subsets of the subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
, then
\alpha
lies in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
;

\bullet

by writing
t(\overline{x_{i}})
, we mean that the
n
-ary term
t
depends upon the set
\left\{x_{i}\right\}
, such that there exist a subset
\left\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{n}}\right\}\subseteq\left\{x_{i}\right\}
with
t(\overline{x_{i}})=t(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{n}})
. This will be used by saying that for any
c\in C=\langle\left\{x_{i}\right\}\rangle
there exists a term
t
such that
c=t(\overline{x_{i}})
;

\bullet

since
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is not a monoid in general as noted in Lemma
2.1
, we will denote by
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
the monoid obtained by adjoining an identity to
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

We finish this section by giving a few lemmas that provide some useful initial properties of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
inherited from those of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
.
The omitted proofs are well known and can be found in
[
1
,
4
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
and
\{x_{i}\}=X\subseteq A
. If
\{x_{i}\alpha\}
is independent, then
X
is an independent set.

Following this, we define a
preimage basis
of
\alpha\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
to be an independent set
X\subseteq A
such that
X\alpha
is a basis for
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
.
Notice that, by writing
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\langle\left\{x_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
, if
\mathcal{A}
has constants then we have that
\rho(\alpha)=0
if and only if
I=\emptyset
, in which case the empty set is a preimage basis for
\alpha
.

Lemma 2.4
.

In an independence algebra, all subalgebras of a given rank are isomorphic.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
be such that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}\beta
. Then there exist
\gamma,\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that:

\bullet

\mathrm{im\,}\gamma=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and
\ker\gamma=\ker\alpha
; and

\bullet

\mathrm{im\,}\mu=\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
and
\ker\mu=\ker\beta
.

Proof
.

Since
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}\beta
, there exists an isomorphism
\phi\colon A\alpha\to A\beta
.
Define
\gamma,\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by
\gamma=\alpha\phi
and
\mu=\beta\phi^{-1}
.
Then,
\mathrm{im\,}\gamma=A\gamma=(A\alpha)\phi=A\beta=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and similarly
\mathrm{im\,}\mu=\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
.
Additionally, we have that
\ker\gamma\supseteq\ker\alpha
by definition of
\gamma
and for the reverse inclusion, we have that for any
a,b\in A
,
(a,b)\in\ker(\gamma)
if and only if
(a\alpha,b\alpha)\in\ker\phi
.
However, since
\phi
is injective, this is equivalent to
a\alpha=b\alpha
, that is,
(a,b)\in\ker\alpha
and thus
\ker\gamma\subseteq\ker\alpha
.
Therefore
\ker\gamma=\ker\alpha
and using similar arguments we have
\ker\mu=\ker\beta
which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
. Then
\rho(\alpha\beta)\leq\min\left\{\rho(\alpha),\rho(\beta)\right\}
.

Proof
.

From
A\alpha\beta\subseteq B\beta\subseteq A\beta
, we get that
\rho(\alpha\beta)\leq\rho(\beta)
. Similarly,
\dim(A\alpha\beta)\leq\dim((A\alpha)\beta)\leq\dim(A\alpha)
and thus
\rho(\alpha\beta)\leq\rho(\alpha)
and the claim follows from the two inequalities.
∎

3.
Regular elements of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

It is well-known that for any independence algebra
\mathcal{A}
, its endomorphism monoid
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
is regular
[
4
, Prop. 4.7]
.
Thus in the case where
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
, it follows that
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is regular.
On the other hand, it is easy to see that if we take
\mathcal{B}
to be the constant subalgebra
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle
whenever this is non-empty, we have that
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is a left-zero semigroup.
Indeed, since any homomorphism has to be act as the identity on
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle
, then for any
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
we have that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle
and thus
\alpha\beta=\alpha
.
Besides, when
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle=\emptyset
and
\dim\mathcal{B}=1
we have that
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is isomorphic to
G\wr_{{}_{n}}\!\left\{c_{1}\right\}
, where
G
is the group of unary term operations which do not have constant image,
c_{1}\colon\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}\to\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
is the constant map with value
1
and the multiplication is given by

\displaystyle\left((g_{1},\dots,g_{n}),c_{1}\right)\left((h_{1},\dots,h_{n}),c_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left((g_{1}h_{1c_{1}},\dots,g_{n}h_{nc_{1}}),c_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left((g_{1}h_{1},\dots,g_{n}h_{1}),c_{1}\right).

In both these cases,
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is a regular semigroup but we will show that
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is not regular when
\mathcal{B}
does not satisfy the conditions given above.

To see that, first notice that if
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is any regular element, then there exists
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\alpha=\alpha\gamma\alpha
and from this, we obtain that

A\alpha=(A\alpha\gamma)\alpha\subseteq B\alpha.

We therefore define a set
Q
which will contain all regular elements as follows:

Q=\left\{\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\mid A\alpha\subseteq B\alpha\right\}.

It is clear that the condition
A\alpha\subseteq B\alpha
on the elements of
Q
can be rewritten as
A\alpha=B\alpha
or
(A\setminus B)\alpha\subseteq B\alpha
, and these equivalent conditions will be equally used throughout the paper to define an element of
Q
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The set
Q
is a right ideal and the maximal regular subsemigroup of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with respect to containment.

Proof
.

As per the remark above, any regular element of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
lies in
Q
.
Moreover, for any
\alpha\in Q,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
we have that

A(\alpha\beta)=(A\alpha)\beta\subseteq(B\alpha)\beta=B\alpha\beta,

and thus
Q
is a right ideal.

Let us now show that any
\alpha\in Q
is a regular element.
Let
B\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
, where, according to our convention,
\left\{b_{i}\right\}\subseteq B
is a preimage basis, and take an element
c\in\langle\left\{b_{i}\right\}\rangle
, which exists as
\mathcal{B}
is non-empty.
Since
A\alpha\subseteq B\alpha
, then for any
a\in A
there exists a term
t
such that
a\alpha=t(\overline{b_{i}\alpha})=t(\overline{b_{i}})\alpha
.
Now if
A=\langle\left\{b_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{j}\right\}\rangle
and we define
u_{j}=x_{j}\alpha=t_{j}(\overline{b_{i}\alpha})
, then we have that

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i}&x_{j}\\
b_{i}\alpha&u_{j}\end{pmatrix}.

Additionally, let
\left\{a_{j}\right\}
be such that
A=\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\sqcup\left\{a_{j}\right\}\rangle
, and define
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by:

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i}\alpha&a_{j}\\
b_{i}&c\end{pmatrix}.

Then we have that
\gamma\in Q
since
A\gamma=\langle b_{i}\rangle=B\gamma
.
Finally, we can see that
\alpha=\alpha\gamma\alpha
.
Indeed, this is clear for any
b\in\langle b_{i}\rangle
, but also for
a\in A
we have

a\alpha\gamma\alpha=t(\overline{b_{i}\alpha})\gamma\alpha=t(\overline{(b_{i}\alpha)\gamma})\alpha=t(\overline{b_{i}})\alpha=a\alpha,

which finishes the proof that any element in
Q
is regular.
∎

For ease of use, the set of non-regular maps, that is, the set
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\setminus Q
will be denoted by
Q^{c}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

For
\mathcal{B}\neq\mathcal{A}
, the semigroup
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is not regular if and only if
\dim B\geq 2
or
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle\neq\emptyset
and
\dim B=1
.

Proof
.

If
\dim B\geq 2
or
\dim B=1
and
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle\neq\emptyset
, there exists at least two elements
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
b_{1}\notin\langle b_{2}\rangle
since we can either take two independent elements, or take
b_{1}
to be an independent element and
b_{2}
a constant.
Now let
B=\langle\left\{x_{j}\right\}\rangle
,
A=\langle\left\{y_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{j}\right\}\rangle
and define
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}y_{i}&x_{j}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then we have that
B\alpha=\langle b_{2}\rangle\subsetneq\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle=A\alpha
and thus
\alpha
is not in
Q
.

Additionally, the comments at the beginning of Section
3
give us the contrapositive statement, hence the equivalence.
∎

Following Corollary
3.2
, we will restrict ourselves to the cases where
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is not regular, since if
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is regular, then we automatically obtain Green’s relations and the ideal structure from that of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
.
Therefore, from now on we assume that
\mathcal{B}\neq\mathcal{A}
and that
\dim B\geq 2
or
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle\neq\emptyset
and
\dim B=1
.
As a consequence, a similar argument to the one mentioned in the proof will be often used, namely, the fact that there exist two distinct elements
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
in
B
such that the former does not lie in the subalgebra generated by the latter.
This will allow us to treat in a similar way algebras of a given rank that contain constants with algebras of a rank one higher that do not have them, as long as we define our maps to be the identity on the element
b_{2}
whenever this is relevant.

Remark
.

A few notes on the structure of
Q
worth mentioning are the following:

\bullet

Q
is always nonempty:
let
B=\langle\left\{b_{i}\right\}\rangle
and
A=\langle\left\{b_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{a_{j}\right\}\rangle
, then the map
\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i}&a_{j}\\
b_{i}&b_{1}\end{pmatrix}
is an idempotent and is clearly in
Q
.

\bullet

Q
is not a left ideal:
let
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
b_{1}\notin\langle b_{2}\rangle
and define the algebras
C=\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle
,
B=C\sqcup\langle\left\{y_{j}\right\}\rangle
, and
A=B\sqcup\langle\left\{x_{i}\right\}\rangle
together with the following maps:

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&y_{j}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{2}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&y_{j}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{1}&b_{1}&b_{1}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then it follows that
\alpha\notin Q
and
\beta\in Q
are such that
\alpha\beta\notin Q
.

\bullet

Any map in
Q
has a preimage basis in
B
:
for
\alpha\in Q
we have that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=A\alpha=B\alpha
, so there exist
\left\{b_{i}\right\}\subseteq B
such that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
, and then
\left\{b_{i}\right\}
is a preimage basis for
\alpha
.
From now on, this fact will be used without explicit mention.

Since regular maps will be of utmost importance in the description of Green’s relations in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, some lemmas are given here for later use.
The first one gives us a sufficient and necessary condition on when the product of two maps lies in
Q
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
,
\alpha\beta\in Q
if and only if for all
x\in A
, there exists a
y\in B
such that
(x\alpha,y\alpha)\in\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
.

Proof
.

From the remarks above, if
\alpha\beta\in Q
, then there exists a preimage basis of
\alpha\beta
in
B
, say
\left\{b_{i}\right\}
.
Therefore, for any
a\in A
, we have that
a\alpha\beta\in\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\beta\right\}\rangle
and thus there exists a term
t
such that
a\alpha\beta=t(\overline{b_{i}\alpha\beta})=t(\overline{b_{i}})\alpha\beta
.
Since
t(\overline{b_{i}})\in B
and
\left(a\alpha,t(\overline{b_{i}})\alpha\right)\in\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
, the first direction is proved.

For the converse, we have that
A\alpha\beta=\left\{x\alpha\mid x\in A\right\}\beta
.
However, each element of the set
\left\{x\alpha\mid x\in A\right\}
lies in
B
and is
\ker\beta
-related to some element
y\alpha
in
B\alpha
by assumption.
Thus
A\alpha\beta=\left\{y\alpha\mid y\in B\right\}\beta=B\alpha\beta
which gives us
\alpha\beta\in Q
.
∎

The next lemma shows that we can always create a map in
Q
from a non-regular one with the same image, and that the converse is also true given that the rank is not minimal.
For this, let us denote by
e
the minimal rank of a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
, that is,
e=0
if
\mathcal{A}
has constants, and
e=1
otherwise.
As a direct consequence,
e
is also the minimal rank of any map in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any map
\alpha\in Q^{c}
, there exists a map
\alpha^{\prime}\in Q
such that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha^{\prime}=\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
.
Similarly, for any map
\beta\in Q
such that
\rho(\beta)>e
, there exists
\beta^{\prime}\in Q^{c}
such that
\mathrm{im\,}\beta^{\prime}=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
.

Proof
.

Let us assume first that
\alpha\in Q^{c}
.
Then
B\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
(with
I
possibly empty) and we extend this to a basis
\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\sqcup\left\{a_{j}\alpha\right\}
of
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
.
Since
\dim B\geq\lvert I\rvert+\lvert J\rvert
, we can write
B=\langle\left\{c_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{j}\right\}\sqcup\left\{y_{k}\right\}\rangle
where the set
K
is possibly empty.
Finally letting
A=\langle\left\{z_{s}\right\}\sqcup\left\{c_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{j}\right\}\sqcup\left\{y_{k}\right\}\rangle
and defining
\alpha^{\prime}
as the following:

\alpha^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}z_{s}&c_{i}&x_{j}&y_{k}\\
z_{s}\alpha&b_{i}\alpha&a_{j}\alpha&y_{k}\alpha\end{pmatrix},

we have that

A\alpha^{\prime}=\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\sqcup\left\{a_{j}\alpha\right\}\rangle=\langle\left\{c_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{j}\right\}\rangle\alpha^{\prime}\subseteq B\alpha^{\prime},

and thus
\alpha^{\prime}\in Q
with the same image as
\alpha
.

Now assume that
\beta\in Q
is such that
\rho(\beta)>e
.
Then, there exist two elements
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
b_{1}\beta\notin\langle b_{2}\beta\rangle
together with a set
\left\{x_{j}\right\}\subseteq B
such that
A\beta=B\beta=\langle b_{1}\beta,b_{2}\beta\rangle\sqcup\langle\left\{x_{j}\beta\right\}\rangle
, and we write
B=\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle\sqcup\langle\left\{x_{j}\right\}\rangle
,
A=\langle\left\{a_{i}\right\}\rangle\sqcup B
and define

\beta^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&\left\{b_{1},b_{2}\right\}&x_{j}\\
b_{1}\beta&b_{2}\beta&x_{j}\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Then
A\beta^{\prime}=\langle b_{1}\beta,b_{2}\beta\rangle\sqcup\langle\left\{x_{j}\beta\right\}\rangle
and
B\beta^{\prime}=\langle b_{2}\beta\rangle\sqcup\langle\left\{x_{j}\beta\right\}\rangle
.
By the exchange property, we have that
b_{1}\beta\notin B\beta^{\prime}
which gives us that
A\beta^{\prime}\neq B\beta^{\prime}
, that is
\beta^{\prime}\notin Q
, and also
\mathrm{im\,}\beta^{\prime}=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
as required.
∎

When we consider a non-regular map
\alpha
, even though we do not have that
A\alpha=B\alpha
, nevertheless we can have that
A\alpha\cong B\alpha
, which happens precisely when
A\alpha
and
B\alpha
have the same dimension.
As an example, consider
A=\langle\left\{x_{i}\right\}\rangle
,
B=\langle\left\{x_{i}\right\}\setminus\left\{x_{1}\right\}\rangle
where
I=\mathbb{N}
, and define
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by
x_{k}\alpha=x_{k+1}
.
Then clearly
x_{2}\in A\alpha\setminus B\alpha
, so that
\alpha\notin Q
but we still have
\rho(\alpha)=\dim B=\dim(B\alpha)
.
This peculiar behaviour of non-regular maps can however only happen if
\alpha
has infinite rank. This is made clear in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
be such that
A\alpha\cong B\alpha
. Then either
\alpha\in Q
, or
\rho(\alpha)\geq\aleph_{0}
.

Proof
.

Assume that
A\alpha\cong B\alpha
.
If
A\alpha=B\alpha
then
\alpha\in Q
.
Otherwise, we have that
\rho(\alpha\lvert_{B})=\rho(\alpha)
by Lemma
2.4
and we let
B\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{k}\alpha\right\}\rangle
.
Since
B\alpha\subsetneq A\alpha
, there exist elements
\left\{a_{j}\alpha\right\}\in\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\setminus B\alpha
and we can write
A\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{k}\alpha\right\}\sqcup\left\{a_{j}\alpha\right\}\rangle
with
\lvert J\rvert\geq 1
.
But then

\lvert K\rvert=\rho(\alpha\lvert_{B})=\rho(\alpha)=\lvert K\rvert+\lvert J\rvert,

which can only happen if
\lvert K\rvert=\rho(\alpha\lvert_{B})=\rho(\alpha)
is infinite.
∎

As an immediate consequence, the contrapositive statement tells us that a non-regular map
\alpha
with finite rank necessarily satisfy that
\rho(\alpha\lvert_{B})=\dim(B\alpha)<\dim(A\alpha)=\rho(\alpha)
.

The last lemma for this section concerns the equivalence between a map being regular and the existence of a map acting as a left identity on it.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\alpha
is regular;

(2)

\alpha=\eta\alpha
for some
\eta
idempotent in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
\rho(\eta)=\rho(\alpha)
; and

(3)

\alpha=\gamma\alpha
for some
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Proof
.

1\Rightarrow 2
:
Since
\alpha
is regular, then
\alpha=\alpha\beta\alpha
for some
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and taking
\eta=\alpha\beta
gives the desired result.

2\Rightarrow 3
:
Putting
\gamma=\eta
gives the result immediately.

3\Rightarrow 1
:
If
\alpha=\gamma\alpha
, then
A\alpha=(A\gamma)\alpha\subseteq B\alpha
and thus
\alpha\in Q
.
∎

4.
Green’s relations

In order to better understand the structure of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, as for any semigroup, we start by looking at Green’s relations.
There are three relevant semigroups in question here:
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
,
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
Q
.
To avoid confusion, where the relation is on
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
or
Q
, we use a subscript (we consider
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
as our base case and so do not use a subscript here).
For example,
\mathop{\mathcal{R}_{\!A}}
,
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}_{Q}}
denote Green’s relation
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
on
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
,
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
Q
respectively.
On the monoid
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
, the Green’s relations have been studied extensively (see for example
[
4
]
) where we have that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}_{\!A}}\beta
if and only if
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
,
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}_{\!A}}\beta
if and only if
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
, and
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{A}}\beta
if and only if
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
.
It is well known that a regular subsemigroup inherits Green’s relations
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
from the larger semigroup and since
Q
is a regular subsemigroup of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
, we get that
\mathop{\mathcal{R}_{Q}}=\mathop{\mathcal{R}_{\!A}}
,
\mathop{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}=\mathop{\mathcal{L}_{\!A}}
and thus also
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{Q}}=\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{A}}
.
The description of the Green’s relations in the semigroup
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is however slightly different.
This section will be devoted to proving adapted versions of the statements Sullivan, Sanwong and Sommanee gave in
[
13
,
14
]
in a more general approach that covers all types of independence algebras, using similar ideas to those they developed.

We start by showing that
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
behaves the same way in all of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
as given by:

Proposition 4.1
.

If
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, then
\alpha=\beta\mu
for some
\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if
\ker\beta\subseteq\ker\alpha
. Consequently,
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\beta
in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
.

Proof
.

Clearly if
\alpha=\beta\mu
then
\ker\beta\subseteq\ker\alpha
.
Now suppose that
\ker\beta\subseteq\ker\alpha
, and let
A\beta=\langle\left\{a_{i}\beta\right\}\rangle
.
Now let
A=\langle\left\{a_{i}\beta\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{j}\right\}\rangle
and define
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by:

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}\beta&x_{j}\\
a_{i}\alpha&x_{j}\beta\end{pmatrix}.

By definition of
\left\{a_{i}\beta\right\}
, for any
z\in A
, there exists a term
t
such that
z\beta=t(\overline{a_{i}\beta})=t(\overline{a_{i}})\beta
which shows that
(z,t(\overline{a_{i}}))\in\ker\beta\subseteq\ker\alpha
, and thus

z\beta\gamma=t\left(\overline{a_{i}\beta}\right)\gamma=t\left(\overline{(a_{i}\beta)\gamma}\right)=t\left(\overline{a_{i}\alpha}\right)=t(\overline{a_{i}})\alpha=z\alpha.

Therefore we have that
\alpha=\beta\gamma
.

It follows from this that if
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\beta
.
Conversely if
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\beta
then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}_{\!A}}\beta
and so
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
using the remarks made previously.
∎

Notice that regular maps can only be related to other regular maps since
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{D}}
and in any semigroup either all elements in a
\mathop{\mathcal{D}}
-class are regular, or none of them is.

The relation
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
, however, does not behave exactly as in
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
and is more restrictive on the non-regular part of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
\beta\in Q
, then
\alpha=\lambda\beta
for some
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\beta
.
Consequently,
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\beta
in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if
\alpha=\beta
or (
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and
\alpha,\beta\in Q
).

Proof
.

Let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
\beta\in Q
.
Clearly if
\alpha=\lambda\beta
, then
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\beta
.
Conversely, let us assume that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and write
A\alpha=\langle\left\{a_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
with
\left\{a_{i}\right\}\subseteq A
.
Since
\beta\in Q
, we have that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq B\beta
and thus there exist
\left\{f_{i}\right\}\subseteq B
such that
a_{i}\alpha=f_{i}\beta
for all
i\in I
, hence the set
\{f_{i}\beta\}
is independent.
Now we take
\{f_{j}\beta\}
such that
A\beta=\langle\{f_{i}\beta\}\sqcup\{f_{j}\beta\}\rangle
and let
\{x_{k}\}
and
\{y_{\ell}\}
subsets of
A
be such that
A=\langle\{a_{i}\}\sqcup\{x_{k}\}\rangle
and

A=\langle\{f_{i}\}\sqcup\{f_{j}\}\sqcup\{y_{\ell}\}\rangle
.
From this we can write the maps
\alpha
and
\beta
as:

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&x_{k}\\
a_{i}\alpha&t_{k}(\overline{a_{i}\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}f_{i}&f_{j}&y_{\ell}\\
f_{i}\beta&f_{j}\beta&y_{\ell}\beta\end{pmatrix}.

for some terms
t_{k}
.
By defining
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by

\lambda=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&x_{k}\\
f_{i}&t_{k}(\overline{f_{i}})\end{pmatrix},

we get that
a_{i}\lambda\beta=f_{i}\beta=a_{i}\alpha
and
x_{k}\lambda\beta=t_{k}(\overline{f_{i}})\beta=t_{k}(\overline{f_{i}\beta})=t_{k}(\overline{a_{i}\alpha})=x_{k}\alpha
.
Thus
\alpha=\lambda\beta
, which concludes the first statement of the proposition.

Now, let us assume that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\beta
in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
Then, there exist
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
such that
\alpha=\lambda\beta
and
\beta=\lambda^{\prime}\alpha
.
If
\lambda=1
or
\lambda^{\prime}=1
then we have that
\alpha=\beta
.
Otherwise, we have that

\alpha=\lambda\lambda^{\prime}\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\lambda^{\prime}\lambda\beta,

from which we get that
\alpha,\beta\in Q
using Lemma
3.6
.
From the first part of the proposition we also have that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and
\mathrm{im\,}\beta\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
, hence the equality.
The converse follows directly from the description of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}
given above whenever
\alpha\neq\beta
.
∎

From
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
we can deduce the description of the relation
\mathop{\mathcal{H}}
which is the equality relation on
Q^{c}
and consists of the set of maps with the same kernel and image on
Q
.
The relations
\mathop{\mathcal{D}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{J}}
are given by the following two propositions.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{D}}\beta
in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
or (
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
and
\alpha,\beta\in Q
).

Proof
.

Assume that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{D}}\beta
.
Then there exists
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\gamma\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\beta
.
By Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
we have that
\ker\alpha=\ker\gamma
, and either
\gamma=\beta
or
\gamma,\beta\in Q
have the same image.
In the case where
\gamma=\beta
we obtain that
\ker\alpha=\ker\gamma=\ker\beta
, as expected.
So let us now assume that
\mathrm{im\,}\gamma=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and
\gamma,\beta\in Q
.
Then
\alpha
is also in
Q
since
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\gamma
and we also have that

\rho(\beta)=\rho(\gamma)=\dim(A/\ker\gamma)=\dim(A/\ker\alpha)=\rho(\alpha),

as required.

Conversely, if
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
, then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\beta
and thus
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{D}}\beta
.
Otherwise, assume
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
and
\alpha,\beta\in Q
.
Therefore, by Lemmas
2.4
and
2.5
we have that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and then there exists
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\mathrm{im\,}\gamma=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
, and
\ker\gamma=\ker\alpha
.
Furthermore, since
\alpha\in Q
, we get that
\gamma\in Q
by the note following Proposition
4.1
, which finishes the proof that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\gamma\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\beta
and therefore
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{D}}\beta
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

If
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, then
\alpha=\lambda\beta\mu
for some
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
if and only if
\rho(\alpha)\leq\dim(B\beta)
. Consequently,
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{J}}\beta
in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if one of the following happens:

\bullet

\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
, or

\bullet

\rho(\alpha)=\dim(B\alpha)=\dim(B\beta)=\rho(\beta)
.

Proof
.

Let us assume first that
\alpha=\lambda\beta\mu
for some
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
.
Then
A\alpha=(A\lambda)\beta\mu\subseteq(B\beta)\mu
which gives that
\rho(\alpha)=\dim(A\alpha)\leq\dim\left((B\beta)\mu\right)\leq\dim(B\beta)
.
Conversely, let us assume that
\rho(\alpha)\leq\dim(B\beta)
.
If
\rho(\alpha)=0
, then clearly
\alpha=\alpha\beta
and we obtain the result.
Assuming now that
\rho(\alpha)\geq 1
and writing
A\alpha=\langle\left\{x_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
, we have by the first hypothesis on the rank of
\alpha
that there exists an independent set
\left\{b_{i}\right\}\subseteq B
such that
A\beta=\langle\{b_{i}\beta\}\sqcup\{y_{k}\beta\}\rangle
.
Thus we can write
A=\langle\{x_{i}\}\sqcup\{x^{\prime}_{j}\}\rangle=\langle\{b_{i}\}\sqcup\{y_{k}\}\sqcup\{y_{\ell}\}\rangle
and we get that

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&x^{\prime}_{j}\\
x_{i}\alpha&u_{j}(\overline{x_{i}\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i}&y_{k}&y_{\ell}\\
b_{i}\beta&y_{k}\beta&v_{\ell}(\overline{b_{i}\beta},\overline{y_{k}\beta})\end{pmatrix},

for some terms
u_{j}
and
v_{\ell}
. Now if we also extend
\{b_{i}\beta\}
into a basis of
A
via
\{z_{m}\}
and define
\lambda
and
\mu
in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by the following:

\lambda=\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&x^{\prime}_{j}\\
b_{i}&u_{j}(\overline{b_{i}})\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i}\beta&z_{m}\\
x_{i}\alpha&x_{1}\alpha\end{pmatrix},

then it can be easily seen that
\alpha=\lambda\beta\mu
.

In order to prove the second part of the proposition, let us first assume that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{J}}\beta
, that is, there exist
\lambda,\mu,\lambda^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
such that
\alpha=\lambda\beta\mu
and
\beta=\lambda^{\prime}\alpha\mu^{\prime}
.
If
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}=1
, then we have that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\beta
and thus Proposition
4.1
gives us that
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
.
If we have that only one of
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
is
1
, then we can find
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
such that
\alpha=\gamma\beta\delta
and
\beta=\gamma^{\prime}\alpha\delta^{\prime}
.
Indeed, assuming without loss of generality that
\lambda=1
, we have
\alpha=\beta\mu=\lambda^{\prime}\alpha\mu^{\prime}\mu=\lambda^{\prime}\beta(\mu\mu^{\prime}\mu)
and we thus can take
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}=\lambda^{\prime}
,
\delta=\mu\mu^{\prime}\mu
and
\delta^{\prime}=\mu^{\prime}
.
By the previous part of the proposition we therefore have that
\rho(\alpha)\leq\dim(B\beta)
and
\rho(\beta)\leq\dim(B\alpha)
.
Therefore we get that:

\dim(A\alpha)=\rho(\alpha)\leq\dim(B\beta)\leq\dim(A\beta)=\rho(\beta)\leq\dim(B\alpha)\leq\dim(A\alpha),

which forces the equalities.

Conversely, assume that
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
, or
\rho(\alpha)=\dim(B\beta)=\dim(B\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
.
If the first condition holds, then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\beta
by Proposition
4.1
and thus
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{J}}\beta
since
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{J}}
.
On the other hand, if the second condition holds, then we use the first part of the proof in both directions in order to obtain the desired result.
∎

Remark
.

In the proof of Proposition
4.4
given above, notice that the maps
\lambda
and
\mu
are constructed in such a way that
\mu\in Q
, and whenever
\left\{x_{i}\right\}\subseteq B
we also have
\lambda\in Q
.

From Lemma
3.5
, we know that the condition
\rho(\alpha)=\dim(B\alpha)
is equivalent to
\alpha\in Q
only if
\alpha
has finite rank, which means that
\mathop{\mathcal{D}}=\mathop{\mathcal{J}}
whenever
\mathcal{B}
is finite dimensional.
On the other hand, if
\mathcal{B}
is infinite dimensional, then there exist non-regular maps of infinite rank that are
\mathop{\mathcal{J}}
-related to regular maps of the same rank, which shows that in that case
\mathop{\mathcal{D}}\subsetneq\mathop{\mathcal{J}}
.
However, on the regular subsemigroup
Q
these relations always coincide and the description also corresponds to that of
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{A}}
relating maps of the same rank, as is given by the following:

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in Q
. Then
\alpha=\lambda\beta\mu
for some
\lambda,\mu\in Q
if and only if
\rho(\alpha)\leq\rho(\beta)
.
Consequently,
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{J}_{Q}}\beta
if and only if
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
, and thus
\mathop{\mathcal{J}_{Q}}=\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{Q}}
.

Proof
.

Clearly if
\alpha=\lambda\beta\mu
then
\rho(\alpha)\leq\rho(\beta\mu)\leq\rho(\beta)
by Lemma
2.6
.
Conversely, assume that
\rho(\alpha)\leq\rho(\beta)
.
Since both
\alpha
and
\beta
lie in
Q
, we have that
A\alpha=B\alpha
and
\rho(\beta)=\dim(A\beta)=\dim(B\beta)
.
Thus, using the same maps
\lambda
and
\mu
constructed in the proof of Proposition
4.4
together with the remark above, we get that
\alpha=\lambda\beta\mu
with
\lambda,\mu\in Q
.

The second part of the lemma now follow directly from the definition of maps being
\mathop{\mathcal{J}}
-related in
Q
together with the characterisation of
\mathop{\mathcal{D}}
-classes in
Q
given in Proposition
4.3
.
∎

5.
Ideals

In the same way that Section
4
was simply generalising the approach to Green’s relations exhibited in the cases of vector spaces and sets, this section is generalising the description of the ideals of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
using the same ideas developed by Sullivan and Mendes-Gonçalves
[
7
,
14
]
.
It is well-known
[
4
]
that the ideals of the endomorphism monoid of an independence algebra are precisely the sets
\left\{\alpha\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})\mid\rho(\alpha)<k\right\}
for each
k\leq(\dim A)^{+}
, where we denote by
\kappa^{+}
the successor cardinal of
\kappa
.
However, in the context of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, the ideals are not solely determined by ranks.
Nevertheless, in the subsemigroup
Q
, all ideals are in one-to-one correspondence with the cardinals smaller than
(\dim B)^{+}
.

Recall that
e
denotes the smallest rank of a non-empty subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
or equivalently the smallest rank of a map in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Proposition 5.1

(see
[
14
, Theorem 8]
)
.

The ideals of
Q
are precisely the sets

Q_{r}=\{\alpha\in Q\mid\rho(\alpha)<r\}

where
e<r\leq(\dim B)^{+}
.

Proof
.

Let
e<r\leq(\dim B)^{+}
. Let
\alpha\in Q_{r}
and
\beta\in Q
.
Then from Lemma
2.6
we have that
\rho(\beta\alpha),\rho(\alpha\beta)\leq\rho(\alpha)
and then both
\alpha\beta
and
\beta\alpha
lie in
Q_{r}
, which shows that
Q_{r}
is an ideal of
Q
.

Conversely, let us assume that
I
is an ideal of
Q
and let
r
be the least cardinal strictly greater than the rank of all maps in
I
, so that
e<r\leq(\dim B)^{+}
.
We show that
I=Q_{r}
. Clearly
I\subseteq Q_{r}
.
For the reverse inclusion, let
\beta\in Q_{r}
.If
\rho(\alpha)<\rho(\beta)
for all
\alpha\in I
, then this means that
\rho(\beta)\geq r
by minimality of
r
, which contradicts our assumption that
\beta\in Q_{r}
.
Thus, there exists some
\alpha\in I
such that
\rho(\beta)\leq\rho(\alpha)
.
By Lemma
4.5
, it follows that there exist
\lambda,\mu\in Q
such that
\beta=\lambda\alpha\mu
.
This gives us that
\beta\in I
since
I
is an ideal of
Q
and thus
Q_{r}\subseteq I
.
Therefore
I=Q_{r}
, completing the proof.
∎

Following the usual definition in
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})
, we define the sets
T_{k}
for any
k>e
by

T_{k}=\left\{\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\mid\rho(\alpha)<k\right\}
, which are easily seen to be ideals of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
using Lemma
2.6
.
It is obvious that for all
k\geq(\dim B)^{+}
we have that
T_{k}=T_{(\dim B)^{+}}=T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
Following
[
7
,
14
]
we define for any non-empty subset
S
of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
the cardinal
r(S)
and the subset
K(S)\subseteq T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
as follows:

\begin{gathered}r(S)=\min\{\kappa\leq(\dim B)^{+}\mid\dim(B\alpha)<\kappa,\ \forall\alpha\in S\},\\
\text{and }K(S)=\{\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\mid\ker\alpha\subseteq\ker\beta,\text{ for some }\alpha\in S\}.\end{gathered}

Using Proposition
4.1
, we can also express
K(S)
as

K(S)=\left\{\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\mid\beta=\alpha\mu,\text{ for some }\alpha\in S\text{ and }\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\right\}.

From this, it is clear that if
\beta\in K(S)
and
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, then we have that
\beta\lambda\in K(S)
and thus
K(S)
is a right ideal.

We now want to show that any ideal of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is of the form
T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
or
T_{r(S)^{+}}\cup K(S)
for some non-empty subset
S\subseteq T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
The ideals
T_{k}
are easily seen to satisfy this rule as
T_{k}=T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
for
S=T_{k}
.
Indeed, let
S=T_{k}
. Then, for any
s<k
,
T_{k}
contains the idempotent
\eta_{s}
with image a subalgebra
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{B}
that has rank
s
, from which we get that
r(S)=k
.
Also, if
\beta\in K(S)
, then
\beta=\alpha\mu
for some
\alpha\in T_{k}
and
\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
Hence
\rho(\beta)=\rho(\alpha\mu)\leq\rho(\alpha)<k
, which shows that
\beta\in T_{k}
and thus
K(S)\subseteq T_{k}
.
Therefore
T_{k}=T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
as claimed.

Before we can show that all ideals are of the form
T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
or
T_{r(S)^{+}}\cup K(S)
for some non-empty set
S
, we first need to show that such sets are indeed ideals.

Lemma 5.2
.

For each non-empty subset
S
of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, the sets
T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
and
T_{r(S)^{+}}\cup K(S)
are ideals of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Proof
.

Let
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, and consider
r(S)
and
K(S)
as in their definition.
As mentioned earlier, we first have that
K(S)
is a right ideal.
Now let
\beta\in K(S)
and
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
Then there exists
\alpha\in S
and
\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\beta=\alpha\mu
.
Thus

\rho(\lambda\beta)=\dim(A\lambda\beta)\leq\dim(B\beta)=\dim(B\alpha\mu)\leq\dim(B\alpha)<r(S),

and therefore
\lambda\beta\in T_{r(S)}
.
The result now follows from the fact that
T_{r(S)}\subseteq T_{r(S)^{+}}
and that
T_{\kappa}
is a two-sided ideal of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
for all
\kappa>e
.
∎

In order to show the reverse statement, we need a small lemma beforehand which will become handy in order to choose an adequate set
S
for each ideal of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
\alpha\in Q
and
e\leq s<\rho(\alpha)
, then there exists
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\lambda\alpha\notin Q
and
\dim(B\lambda\alpha)=s
.

Proof
.

Since
\alpha\in Q
, then
A\alpha=B\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
for some
\left\{b_{i}\right\}\subseteq B
, and for
A=\langle\left\{b_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{j}\right\}\rangle
the map
\alpha
can be written as

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i}&x_{j}\\
b_{i}\alpha&u_{j}(\overline{b_{i}\alpha})\end{pmatrix},

for some terms
u_{j}
.
Now take
\left\{b^{\prime}_{k}\right\}\sqcup\left\{b_{1}\right\}\subseteq\left\{b_{i}\right\}
such that
\lvert K\rvert=s
(which is possible by the assumption on the value of
s
), and take some basis element
z\in A\setminus B
.
Let
A=\langle\left\{b^{\prime}_{k}\right\}\sqcup\left\{z\right\}\sqcup\left\{y_{\ell}\right\}\rangle
, and define
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by

\lambda=\begin{pmatrix}b^{\prime}_{k}&z&y_{\ell}\\
b^{\prime}_{k}&b_{1}&c\end{pmatrix},

where
c
can be taken in
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle
whenever this is non-empty, and otherwise we can take
c
to be any element in
\left\{b^{\prime}_{k}\right\}\neq\emptyset
since
s\geq e=1
in that case.
Then
B\lambda\alpha=\langle\left\{b^{\prime}_{k}\alpha\right\}\rangle\neq\langle\left\{b^{\prime}_{k}\alpha\right\}\sqcup\left\{b_{1}\alpha\right\}\rangle=A\lambda\alpha
so
\lambda\alpha\notin Q
and
\dim(B\lambda\alpha)=\lvert K\rvert=s
as required.
∎

We can now finally show the characterisation of the ideals in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Theorem 5.4
.

The ideals of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
are precisely the sets
T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
and
T_{r(S)^{+}}\cup K(S)
where
S
is a non-empty subset of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Proof
.

Taking into account Lemma
5.2
, it suffices to show that for any ideal
I
of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, there exists a set
S
such that
I=T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
or
I=T_{r(S)^{+}}\cup K(S)
.
So let
I
be an ideal of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

If
I
is the smallest ideal of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, that is, if
I=T_{e^{+}}
, then we set
S=T_{e^{+}}
.
From the discussion preceding Lemma
5.2
, we obtain
I=T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
as required.

From now on, let us assume that
I\neq T_{e^{+}}
, and let
\alpha\in I\setminus T_{e^{+}}
.
Then
\rho(\alpha)>e
and
\alpha
has the property that there exist
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq B
such that
z_{1}\notin\langle z_{2}\rangle
.
If we can find two such elements with one of
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
not in
B\alpha
, then we have that
A\alpha\neq B\alpha
and thus
\alpha\notin Q
.
On the contrary, if for any two elements
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
satisfying the property above, both lie in
B\alpha
, then we get that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=B\alpha
and thus
\alpha\in Q
.
In this second case, since
\rho(\alpha)\geq e+1
we call upon Lemma
5.3
with
s=e
to get that that there exists
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\lambda\alpha\notin Q
.
Moreover, since
I
is an ideal, we also have that
\lambda\alpha\in I
.
Therefore in both cases, if we take
S=I\setminus Q
we have that
S\neq\emptyset
.
Now by setting
r=r(S)
, we show that
I
is equal to either
T_{r}\cup K(S)
or
T_{r^{+}}\cup K(S)
.

Firstly, we have that
T_{r}\cup K(S)\subseteq I
.
Indeed, let
\beta\in K(S)
, so that
\beta=\alpha\mu
for some
\alpha\in S\subseteq I
and
\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, and thus
\beta\in I
.
Similarly, let
\beta\in T_{r}
. If
\rho(\beta)>\dim(B\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in I
, then, in particular
\rho(\beta)>\dim(B\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in S
, which contradicts the minimality of
r
since
\rho(\beta)<r
.
Therefore, there exists
\alpha\in I
such that
\rho(\beta)\leq\dim(B\alpha)
.
By Proposition
4.4
, this means that
\beta=\lambda\alpha\mu
for some
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
,
\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
which shows that
\beta\in I
. Therefore
T_{r}\cup K(S)\subseteq I
.

It is also clear that
I\setminus Q=S\subseteq K(S)
.
Similarly, for any
\gamma\in I\cap Q
such that
\dim(B\gamma)<r
, we have that
r>\dim(B\gamma)=\rho(\gamma)
and therefore
\gamma\in T_{r}
.
We now need to distinguish between the case where
I=T_{r}\cup K(S)
and
I=T_{r^{+}}\cup K(S)
by looking at the possible values of
\dim(B\gamma)
for
\gamma\in I\cap Q
.

On one hand, if
\dim(B\gamma)<r
for all
\gamma\in I\cap Q
, then, using the argument in the previous paragraph, we have shown that
I=T_{r}\cup K(S)
.

On the other hand, assume that there exists at least one
\beta\in I\cap Q
such that
\dim(B\beta)\geq r
and set
\kappa=\rho(\beta)=\dim(B\beta)
.
We show in this case that
I=T_{r^{+}}\cup K(S)
.
If
\kappa>r
, then using Lemma
5.3
with
s=r
, we get that there exists
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\dim(B\lambda\beta)=r
and
\lambda\beta\notin Q
. But then
\lambda\beta\in I\setminus Q=S
and this contradicts the definition of
r=r(S)
.
Therefore we must have that
\kappa=r
from which we have that
\beta\in T_{r^{+}}
.
This gives us that
I\cap Q\subseteq T_{r^{+}}
and therefore
I\subseteq T_{r^{+}}\cup K(S)
.
In order to get the equality we only need to show that
T_{r^{+}}\subseteq I
since we already know that
T_{r}\cup K(S)\subseteq I
.
For this, consider
\gamma\in T_{r^{+}}
.
Since in the current case there exists a map
\beta\in I
with
\dim(B\beta)=r
, we have that
\rho(\gamma)\leq r=\dim(B\beta)
.
Then by Proposition
4.4
,
\gamma=\lambda\beta\mu
for some
\lambda\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}),\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
which shows that
\gamma\in I
and gives us that
T_{r^{+}}\subseteq I
.
Therefore, when there exists a map in
I\cap Q
which rank is equal to
r
, we have that
I=T_{r^{+}}\cup K(S)
.
∎

Remark
.

Notice that for two distinct sets
S
and
S^{\prime}
, one can have
T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)=T_{r(S^{\prime})}\cup K(S^{\prime})
.
Similarly, for an ideal
I
, there might exist sets
S
and
S^{\prime}
such that
I=T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
and
I=T_{r(S^{\prime})^{+}}\cup K(S^{\prime})
.
Indeed, if we take
I=T_{k}
, then we know that
I=T_{r(S)}\cup K(S)
for
S=T_{k}
, but we can also obtain
I
using the construction of the proof with the set
S^{\prime}=I\setminus Q
.
More precisely, if
k
is finite, then we have that
r(S^{\prime})=k-1
, and there exist many elements
\gamma\in T_{k}
for which
\dim(B\gamma)\geq k-1
, namely all idempotents on subalgebras of rank
k-1
. Thus we have that
T_{k}=I=T_{r(S^{\prime})^{+}}\cup K(S^{\prime})
.
On the other hand, if
k
is infinite, then we directly have that
r(S^{\prime})=k
and that all elements of
I
are such that
\dim(B\gamma)\leq\rho(\gamma)<k
, which gives us that
T_{k}=I=T_{r(S^{\prime})}\cup K(S^{\prime})
in this case.

Using the theorem above, we can give examples of the construction of two ideals that are not comparable under inclusion, as long as we are not in the case where the algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a set with
3
elements and its subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
has dimension exactly
2
.
In that specific case, the
8
elements of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
form three ideals
T_{2}
,
T_{2}\cup\left\{\alpha\in Q^{c}\mid\rho(\alpha)=2\right\}
and
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
itself, which make up a chain.

Example
.

Let us assume that there exist independent elements
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
, that
\mathcal{A}
does not have any constants and that
B
has codimension at least
2
in
A
.
Let
B=\langle\left\{b_{1},b_{2}\right\}\sqcup\left\{d_{j}\right\}\rangle
and
A=\langle\left\{b_{1},b_{2}\right\}\sqcup\left\{d_{j}\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{1},x_{2}\right\}\sqcup\left\{y_{k}\right\}\rangle
.
Define the following maps in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
:

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&\left\{b_{i},d_{j},x_{2},y_{k}\right\}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}&\left\{b_{i},d_{j},x_{1},y_{k}\right\}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

From this we have that
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)=2
,
\dim(B\alpha)=\dim(B\beta)=1
and
(x_{1},b_{1})\in\ker\beta\setminus\ker\alpha
while
(x_{2},b_{1})\in\ker\alpha\setminus\ker\beta
, which shows that
\ker\alpha
and
\ker\beta
are incomparable.
Since
r(\left\{\alpha\right\})=r(\left\{\beta\right\})=2
, we let
I_{\alpha}=T_{2}\cup K(\left\{\alpha\right\})
and
I_{\beta}=T_{2}\cup K(\left\{\beta\right\})
, which are both ideals from the previous theorem.
Now it is easy to see that
\alpha\in I_{\alpha}\setminus I_{\beta}
and
\beta\in I_{\beta}\setminus I_{\alpha}
, which shows that these two ideals are incomparable.

A similar trick can be used with
b_{2}
a constant, and another small modification can be used to find non comparable ideals when
B
contains at least three independent elements, or two independent elements and a constant.

6.
Extended Green’s relations

The study of extended Green’s relations was introduced by Pastjin in
[
11
]
and extended by El-Qallali
[
2
]
and Lawson
[
6
]
, and, since then, they have been widely used in order to generalise the notion of regular semigroups to abundant, Fountain and
U
-semiabundant semigroups.
For
a,b\in S
, these relations are given as follows:

\begin{gathered}a\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}b\Longleftrightarrow\left(ax=ay\Leftrightarrow bx=by\quad\forall x,y\in S^{1}\right),\\
a\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}b\Longleftrightarrow\left(xa=ya\Leftrightarrow xb=yb\quad\forall x,y\in S^{1}\right),\\
a\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}b\Longleftrightarrow\Big(af=a\Leftrightarrow bf=b\quad\forall f\in E(S)\Big),\\
a\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}b\Longleftrightarrow\Big(fa=a\Leftrightarrow fb=b\quad\forall f\in E(S)\Big),\\
\mathop{\mathcal{H}^{*}}=\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\wedge\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}},\quad\phantom{and}\quad\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}=\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}\wedge\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}},\\
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}=\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\vee\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}=\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}\vee\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}.\end{gathered}

Of course, there are analogues extended relations
\mathop{\mathcal{J}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}}
of Green’s relation
\mathop{\mathcal{J}}
but we delay their description which is less straightforward until these relations are needed.
Also, the description of the relations
\mathop{\mathcal{H}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}
will not be given as they can be easily deduced from the descriptions of the appropriate
*
and
\sim
-relations.
A well-known consequence of these definitions (see
[
3
]
) is that two elements are
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
related in a semigroup
S
if and only if they are
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
related in an oversemigroup
T
, and dually for
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
.
Also it is easy to see that by definition,
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
are right, respectively left, congruences and that we have the inclusions
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\subseteq\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\subseteq\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
.
These inclusions may be strict but it is well-known that they become equalities when we restrict our attention to regular elements as given in the next lemma (the proof of which is only included for completeness purposes).

Lemma 6.1

(
[
2
]
)
.

In a semigroup
S
, if
a
and
b
are both regular elements, then
a\mathop{\mathcal{R}}b\Leftrightarrow a\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}b
and
a\mathop{\mathcal{L}}b\Leftrightarrow a\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}b
.

Proof
.

By the remark above, we only need to prove the implication from right to left.
So assume that
a
and
b
are regular elements such that
a\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}b
.
Then there exist
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in S
such that
a=aa^{\prime}a
and
b=bb^{\prime}b
.
Thus
aa^{\prime}
and
bb^{\prime}
are idempotents and by the definition of being
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
related, we get that
a=bb^{\prime}a
and
b=aa^{\prime}b
, and thus
a\mathop{\mathcal{R}}b
.
A dual argument shows that the similar result holds for
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
.
∎

As a direct consequence of this lemma, we have that the relations
\mathop{\mathcal{R}_{Q}}
,
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}_{Q}}
, and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{Q}}
are equal (and similarly
\mathop{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}=\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{Q}}=\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}}
), but this is not true on the whole of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
Indeed, the following propositions shows that on
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
the relations
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
are equal but they differ from
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
, while the relations
\mathop{\mathcal{R}}
,
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
are all distinct.

Proposition 6.2
.

In
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
we have that
\alpha\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}\beta
if and only if
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
. Thus
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}=\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

Proof
.

Let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
\eta\in E
.
If
\alpha\eta=\alpha
, then it is clear that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\eta
.
Conversely, assume that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\eta
. Since
\eta
is an idempotent, it follows that
\eta\lvert_{\mathrm{im\,}\eta}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{im\,}\eta}
and then
\eta\lvert_{\mathrm{im\,}\alpha}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{im\,}\alpha}
which shows that
\alpha\eta=\alpha
.
Therefore for any
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
,
\eta\in E
, we have that
\alpha\eta=\alpha
if and only if
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\eta
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and assume that
\alpha\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}\beta
.
Now let
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\langle\left\{x_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
,
\mathrm{im\,}\beta=\langle\left\{y_{k}\beta\right\}\rangle
and
A=\langle\{x_{i}\alpha\}\sqcup\{x_{j}\}\rangle=\langle\{y_{k}\beta\}\sqcup\{y_{\ell}\}\rangle
. Define idempotents
\eta
and
\theta
as follows:

\eta=\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}\alpha&x_{j}\\
x_{i}\alpha&\tilde{x}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\theta=\begin{pmatrix}y_{k}\beta&y_{\ell}\\
y_{k}\beta&\tilde{y}\end{pmatrix},

for some elements
\tilde{x}\in\langle x_{i}\alpha\rangle
and
\tilde{y}\in\langle y_{k}\beta\rangle
.
It is clear that
\mathrm{im\,}\eta=\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
and
\mathrm{im\,}\theta=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
which gives us that
\alpha\eta=\alpha
and
\beta\theta=\beta
.
Since
\alpha\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}\beta
, it follows that
\alpha\theta=\alpha
and
\beta\eta=\beta
.
Hence, by the equivalence previously proved, we have that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\theta=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and
\mathrm{im\,}\beta\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\eta=\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
.
Therefore
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
.
Conversely, it can easily be seen that for any
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
, any idempotent
\eta
satisfying
\alpha\eta=\alpha
will be such that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\eta
and thus
\mathrm{im\,}\beta\subseteq\mathrm{im\,}\eta
and
\beta\eta=\beta
, so
\alpha\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}\beta
.

Finally, since
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}_{\!A}}\beta
implies
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\beta
, we get from the description of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}_{\!A}}
the following inclusions:

\{(\alpha,\beta)\mid\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\beta\}\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\subseteq\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}=\{(\alpha,\beta)\mid\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\beta\},

which forces the equalities, and so
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}=\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
.
∎

Whereas the description of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
follows from the description of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}
, the same cannot be said for
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
and their explicit descriptions are given by Propositions
6.3
and
6.4
.

Proposition 6.3
.

In
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, we have that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
if and only if one of the following occurs:

(1)

\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
and
\alpha,\beta\in Q
;

(2)

\ker\alpha\cap(B\times B)=\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
and
\alpha,\beta\notin Q
.

Proof
.

Let
\alpha\in Q
,
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and assume
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
.
From Lemma
3.6
, we know that there exists
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\alpha=\gamma\alpha
and therefore
\beta=\gamma\beta
, so
\gamma
is a left-identity for
\beta
and thus from Lemma
3.6
again, we have that
\beta\in Q
.
Since the case for
Q\times Q
is given by Lemma
6.1
, we only need to consider the case of two non-regular elements.

Notice that for any map
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, if
\gamma\alpha=\delta\alpha
with
\gamma\neq\delta=1
, it follows that
A\alpha=A\gamma\alpha\subseteq B\alpha
and then
\alpha\in Q
.
Therefore in what follows, either
\gamma=\delta=1
or
\gamma,\delta\neq 1
.

Now, let
\alpha,\beta\in Q^{c}
and assume that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
, that is,
\gamma\alpha=\delta\alpha
if and only if
\gamma\beta=\delta\beta
for
\gamma,\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1}
.
Then for any pair
(b_{1},b_{2})\in\ker\alpha\cap(B\times B)
with
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
, we construct specific maps
\gamma,\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
satisfying the relation
\gamma\alpha=\delta\alpha
.
To this end, let
B=\langle\left\{y_{k}\right\}\rangle
and
A=\langle\{y_{k}\}\sqcup\{u\}\sqcup\{x_{j}\}\rangle
and define
\gamma,\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by:

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}y_{k}&u&x_{j}\\
y_{k}&b_{1}&y_{1}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta=\begin{pmatrix}y_{k}&u&x_{j}\\
y_{k}&b_{2}&y_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

It is clear that
\gamma\alpha=\delta\alpha
, and since
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
, it follows that
\gamma\beta=\delta\beta
.
Therefore
b_{1}\beta=u\gamma\beta=u\delta\beta=b_{2}\beta
and thus
(b_{1},b_{2})\in\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
.
Using the same argument interchanging the roles of
\alpha
and
\beta
, we deduce that
\ker\alpha\cap(B\times B)=\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
.

Conversely, assume that
\ker\alpha\cap(B\times B)=\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
and that
\gamma\alpha=\delta\alpha
for some
\gamma,\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
Then for any
a\in A
we have
a\gamma\alpha=a\delta\alpha
from which
(a\gamma,a\delta)\in\ker\alpha
.
But since
a\gamma,a\delta\in B
, it follows that
(a\gamma,a\delta)\in\ker\beta
.
Therefore
a\gamma\beta=a\delta\beta
, and since this is true for any
a\in A
we get that
\gamma\beta=\delta\beta
.
Interchanging the roles of
\alpha
and
\beta
, we obtain that
\gamma\alpha=\delta\alpha
if and only if
\gamma\beta=\delta\beta
for any
\gamma,\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
Since this equivalence also holds when
\gamma=\delta=1
together with the note made earlier in the proof, we conclude that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
as required.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

In
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, we have that
\alpha\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}\beta
if and only if
\alpha,\beta\in Q^{c}
or (
\ker\alpha=\ker\beta
and
\alpha,\beta\in Q
).

Proof
.

From Lemma
3.6
we know that
\alpha=\eta\alpha
where
\eta\in E
if and only if
\alpha
is regular.
Using this and Lemma
6.1
, the proposition follows easily, since regular maps can only be
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
related to regular maps, and for two non-regular maps the condition in the definition of
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
is trivially realised.
∎

In order to compare kernels the following lemma will come in handy:

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
B=\langle\left\{b_{k}\right\}\sqcup\left\{b_{i}\right\}\rangle
and
A=B\sqcup\langle\left\{a_{j}\right\}\rangle
. Suppose that
\gamma,\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
can be expressed as follows:

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k}&b_{i}&a_{j}\\
f_{k}&t_{i}(\overline{f_{k}})&g_{j}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k}&b_{i}&a_{j}\\
x_{k}&t_{i}(\overline{x_{k}})&y_{j}\end{pmatrix},

for some terms
t_{i}
, where the sets
\left\{f_{k}\right\}
and
\left\{x_{k}\right\}
are respective bases of
\mathrm{im\,}(B\gamma)
and
\mathrm{im\,}(B\delta)
.
Then we have that
\ker\gamma\cap(B\times B)=\ker\delta\cap(B\times B)
. Consequently, if
\gamma,\delta\in Q^{c}
we also obtain that
\gamma\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\delta
.

Proof
.

Let us assume that the maps
\gamma,\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
are given as above.
Let
(a,b)\in\ker\gamma\cap(B\times B)
. Then there exist terms
u
and
v
such that
a=u(\overline{b_{k}},\overline{b_{i}})
and
b=v(\overline{b_{k}},\overline{b_{i}})
.
Then we have that
u(\overline{f_{k}},\overline{t_{i}(\overline{f_{k}})})=a\gamma=b\gamma=v(\overline{f_{k}},\overline{t_{i}(\overline{f_{k}})})
.
Now we extend
\left\{f_{k}\right\}
to a basis of
A
through
\left\{d_{m}\right\}
, and we define
\eta=\begin{pmatrix}f_{k}&d_{m}\\
x_{k}&x_{1}\end{pmatrix}\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
From this we get the following:

\displaystyle a\delta

\displaystyle=u(\overline{b_{k}},\overline{b_{i}})\delta=u(\overline{x_{k}},\overline{t_{i}(\overline{x_{k}}})=u(\overline{f_{k}\eta},\overline{t_{i}(\overline{f_{k}\eta})})=u(\overline{f_{k}},\overline{t_{i}(\overline{f_{k}})})\eta=a\gamma\eta

\displaystyle=b\gamma\eta=v(\overline{f_{k}},\overline{t_{i}(\overline{f_{k}})})\eta=v(\overline{x_{k}},\overline{t_{i}(\overline{x_{k}}})=b\delta,

which means that
(a,b)\in\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
.
Hence
\ker\gamma\cap(B\times B)\subseteq\ker\delta\cap(B\times B)
.
The converse inclusion works similarly by using the map
\theta=\begin{pmatrix}x_{k}&e_{m}\\
f_{k}&f_{1}\end{pmatrix}\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
Therefore we have that
\ker\gamma\cap(B\times B)=\ker\delta\cap(B\times B)
.
Moreover, if
\gamma,\delta\in Q^{c}
then Proposition
6.3
allows us to conclude that
\gamma\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\delta
.
∎

Recall from the introduction that a semigroup is called
left abundant
[resp.
left Fountain
] if each
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
-class [resp. each
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-class] contains an idempotent, and dually for the right-handed notion using
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
.
From the description of our extended Green’s relations, we can see that
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is right abundant (and thus right Fountain) but is not left abundant nor left Fountain since no idempotent lies in the
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
or
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-class of an element in
Q^{c}
.

As noticed at the beginning of this section
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
are respectively right and left congruences, and thus
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
is also a right congruence.
However, it is not always true that the same holds for
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
, as is shown by the following lemma.

Lemma 6.6
.

The equivalence
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
is a left congruence if and only if one of the following occurs:

\bullet

\dim B=2
and one-dimensional subalgebras are singletons; or

\bullet

\dim B=1
and
\left\lvert\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle\right\rvert=1
.

Proof
.

First, notice that if
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
are such that
\ker\alpha\cap(B\times B)=\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
, then for any
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, Lemma
3.3
tells us that
\gamma\alpha\in Q
if and only if
\gamma\beta\in Q
, and we also have that
(x\gamma,y\gamma)\in\ker\alpha
if and only if
(x\gamma,y\gamma)\in\ker\beta
(as
x\gamma,y\gamma\in B
) showing that
\ker\gamma\alpha=\ker\gamma\beta
.
From this we see that either
\gamma\alpha
and
\gamma\beta
are in
Q
and have the same kernel or neither of them is in
Q
, which gives us in both cases that they are
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-related.
Since
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{Q}}=\mathop{\mathcal{R}_{Q}}
and
Q
is a union of
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-classes, it follows that
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
when restricted to
Q\times Q
is a left congruence.
Therefore
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
can only fail to be a left congruence on
Q^{c}\times Q^{c}
.
To this extend, we need to find maps
\alpha,\beta\in Q^{c}
(which are then
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-related by Proposition
6.4
) and
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\gamma\alpha
and
\gamma\beta
are not
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-related.
That is, we need either that only one of
\gamma\alpha
and
\gamma\beta
lie in
Q
, or that both products are regular but have a different kernel.

With this in mind, let us assume that
\dim B=2
and that one-dimensional subalgebras are singletons and let
\alpha,\beta\notin Q
.
Then
B\alpha\subsetneq A\alpha\subseteq B
and similarly for
\beta
so
\rho(\alpha\lvert_{B})=\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})=1
.
Also, since one-dimensional subalegras are singleton, this forces
B\alpha=\left\{c\right\}
and
B\beta=\left\{c^{\prime}\right\}
for some
c,c^{\prime}\in B
and thus
\ker\alpha\cap(B\times B)=\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
.
Using the previous argument, it follows that for any map
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, we have that
\gamma\alpha\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}\gamma\beta
, and thus
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
is a left congruence in this case.

Similarly, if
\dim B=1
and the constant subalgebra only consists of a single element, say
0
, then it is clear that for any map
\alpha\notin Q
, we have that
B\alpha=\left\{0\right\}
, and thus the argument above works and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
is a left congruence in this case.

In order to show that outside of these cases
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
fails to be a left congruence, we exhibit counterexamples.
Let us assume first that
\dim B\geq 3
and let
B=\langle\left\{y_{m}\right\}\sqcup\left\{b_{i}\right\}\rangle
,
A=\langle\left\{x\right\}\sqcup\left\{a_{j}\right\}\sqcup\left\{y_{m}\right\}\sqcup\left\{b_{i}\right\}\rangle
where
\lvert M\rvert=3
and the sets
\left\{a_{j}\right\}
and
\left\{b_{i}\right\}
are possibly empty.
Consider the following maps:

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}x&y_{1}&y_{2}&y_{3}&a_{j},b_{i}\\
y_{1}&y_{3}&y_{3}&y_{3}&y_{3}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}x&y_{1}&y_{2}&y_{3}&a_{j},b_{i}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&y_{2}&y_{3}&y_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

Then it is clear that
\alpha,\beta\notin Q
and thus they are
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-related.
Also, we have that
\alpha^{2}\in Q
but
\alpha\beta\notin Q
, and thus they cannot be related.

Now assume that
\dim B=2
with
B=\langle\left\{b_{1},b_{2}\right\}\rangle
,
A=\langle\left\{a_{j}\right\}\sqcup\left\{b_{1},b_{2}\right\}\rangle
and suppose that one-dimensional subalgebras are not singletons, that is, that there exists a term
g
such that
g(b_{2})\neq b_{2}
.
Then for the maps

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}a_{j}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}a_{j}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{1}&g(b_{2})&b_{2}\end{pmatrix},\text{ and }\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a_{j}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{1}&b_{1}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix},

we have the following:

\bullet

\alpha,\beta\notin Q
and thus
\alpha\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}\beta
;

\bullet

\gamma\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}a_{j}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{2}&b_{2}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}
and thus
\gamma\alpha\in Q
;

\bullet

\gamma\beta=\begin{pmatrix}a_{j}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
g(b_{2})&g(b_{2})&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}
and thus
\gamma\beta\in Q
;

\bullet

(b_{1},b_{2})\in\ker\gamma\alpha
, but
(b_{1},b_{2})\notin\ker\gamma\beta
.

Therefore we have that
\gamma\alpha
and
\gamma\beta
are not
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
-related, whereas
\alpha
and
\beta
are.
Similarly, if
\dim B=1
and
\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle
contains at least two distinct elements, then by abusing notation on the counterexample where
\dim B=2
, we can take
b_{2}\in\big\langle\emptyset\big\rangle
and choose the term
g
such that
g(b_{2})\neq b_{2}
.
This shows that
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
is not a left congruence in these two cases.
∎

In any semigroup we can define the relation
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}
by being the join of the relations
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
.
A well-known characterisation (see for example in Howie
[
5
, Prop 1.5.11]
) tells us that
a\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}b
if and only if for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{2n}\in S
such that
a=c_{0}\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}c_{1}\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}c_{2}\dots\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}c_{2n-1}\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}c_{2n}=b
.
If
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
were to commute, this sequence of compositions would result in a single composition.
However Pastjin
[
11
]
noticed that in general this is not the case, as can be seen here by the following example.

Example
.

Let
A=\langle\left\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}\right\}\rangle
and
B=\langle\left\{x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}\right\}\rangle
.
Define
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by the following:

\begin{gathered}\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{3}&x_{4}&x_{5}\\
x_{3}&x_{3}&x_{3}&x_{4}&x_{5}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{3}&x_{4}&x_{5}\\
x_{3}&x_{3}&x_{5}&x_{5}&x_{5}\end{pmatrix},\\
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{3}&x_{4}&x_{5}\\
x_{3}&x_{4}&x_{5}&x_{5}&x_{5}\end{pmatrix}.\end{gathered}

Then we clearly have that
\alpha\in Q
,
\beta\notin Q
,
\gamma\notin Q
and from Propositions
6.2
and
6.3
these maps satisfy the relations
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\gamma
and
\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)=\ker\gamma\cap(B\times B)
.
Therefore
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
through
\gamma
.

Now, in order to have
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{L}^{*}}\beta
, we need to find
\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\mathrm{im\,}\delta=\mathrm{im\,}\beta=\langle\left\{x_{3},x_{5}\right\}\rangle
and
\ker\alpha=\ker\delta
(since
\alpha\in Q
).
However, for any such
\delta
we would need

\mathrm{im\,}\delta\cong A/\ker\delta=A/\ker\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}\alpha,

giving that
\dim(\mathrm{im\,}\delta)=3
which is impossible.
Therefore, no such
\delta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
can exist and
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
do not commute in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

When replacing in the example above the relation
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
by
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
, the same arguments hold since
\gamma,\beta\notin Q
implies that
\gamma\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}\beta
.
Therefore, similarly to
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
, we can see that the relations
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}=\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}
do not commute either in
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, exhibiting a different behaviour from the usual Green’s relations.

Nevertheless, in the case of
T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
it is possible to give a precise characterisation for
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}
which, surprisingly, depends on the corank of the subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
inside
\mathcal{A}
.
Some elements are easily seen to be
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}
-related, such as those of the same rank, as given by the following lemma.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
be such that
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
. Then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
and thus
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}\beta
.

Proof
.

For any
\alpha,\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
, Lemmas
2.4
and
2.5
give us that there exists
\mu\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\mu
and
\ker\beta=\ker\mu
(so that also
\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)=\ker\mu\cap(B\times B)
).
Thus
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\mu
by Proposition
6.2
.
Additionally, using the appropriate case on whether
\beta
and
\mu
are regular in Proposition
6.3
, we also have that
\mu\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
.
Therefore we have
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
and
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}\beta
.

However, the full characterisation of
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}
requires us to concentrate on the composition
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}
beforehand.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
. Then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
for some
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if one of the following happens:

(1)

\beta\in Q
and
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}\beta
;

(2)

\beta\notin Q
and
\mathrm{im\,}(B\beta)\cong\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
with
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})\geq\aleph_{0}
;

(3)

\beta\notin Q
and
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})<\rho(\alpha)\leq\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B
.

Exchanging the roles of
\alpha
and
\beta
, we have the dual characterisation for when
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{L}^{*}}\beta
.

Proof
.

Let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and suppose that
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is such that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
.
Then there exists
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\gamma
and
\gamma\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
. Therefore we always have that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\gamma
by Proposition
6.2
, and Proposition
6.3
tells us that either
\beta,\gamma\in Q
are such that
\ker\gamma=\ker\beta
, or
\beta,\gamma\notin Q
and then
\ker\gamma\cap(B\times B)=\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)
.
If
\beta\in Q
(and hence also
\gamma\in Q
), then we have that

\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\gamma\cong A/\ker\gamma=A/\ker\beta\cong\mathrm{im\,}\beta,

which gives us the first case.

We now assume that
\beta,\gamma\notin Q
.
Thus

\mathrm{im\,}\beta\lvert_{B}\cong B/\left(\ker\beta\cap(B\times B)\right)=B/\left(\ker\gamma\cap(B\times B)\right)\cong\mathrm{im\,}\gamma\lvert_{B},

so that
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})=\rho(\gamma\lvert_{B})
.
If
\rho(\gamma)=\rho(\gamma\lvert_{B})
, then
\rho(\gamma)\geq\aleph_{0}
by Lemma
3.5
and from
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})=\rho(\gamma)=\rho(\alpha)
we get that
\mathrm{im\,}(B\beta)\cong\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
which corresponds to the second case.
Otherwise,
\rho(\gamma\lvert_{B})<\rho(\gamma)
and then, setting
Z
to be a basis extension of
B
in
A
, we have that

\displaystyle\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})=\rho(\gamma\lvert_{B})

\displaystyle<\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\gamma)=\dim(A\gamma)\leq\dim(B\gamma)+\dim(Z\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\rho(\gamma\lvert_{B})+\dim Z=\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B,

giving us the remaining case.

For the converse, let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
We show that each case gives us that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
.

(1)

If
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and
\beta\in Q
, then Lemma
6.7
directly gives us that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
.

(2)

Suppose now that
\beta\notin Q
is such that
\mathrm{im\,}(B\beta)\cong\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
with
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})\geq\aleph_{0}
.
We then write
B\beta=\langle\left\{b_{k}\beta\right\}\rangle
,
B=\langle\left\{b_{k}\right\}\sqcup\left\{b_{i}\right\}\rangle
and
A=\langle\left\{b_{k}\right\}\sqcup\left\{b_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{a_{j}\right\}\rangle
.
By assumption, we have that
A\alpha=\langle\left\{x_{k}\alpha\right\}\rangle
for some
\left\{x_{k}\right\}\subseteq A
. Since
\lvert K\rvert=\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})\geq\aleph_{0}
we have that by setting
K^{\prime}=K\setminus\left\{1\right\}
there exists a bijection
\phi\colon K\to K^{\prime}
. For all
k\in K
we set
z_{k}=x_{k\phi}\alpha
and we now define
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
by:

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k}&b_{i}&a_{j}\\
z_{k}&t_{i}(\overline{z_{k}})&x_{1}\alpha\end{pmatrix},

where the terms
t_{i}
are such that
b_{i}\beta=t_{i}(\overline{b_{k}\beta})
.
Then, we have that
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\langle\left\{x_{k}\alpha\right\}\rangle=\mathrm{im\,}\gamma\neq\langle\left\{z_{k}\right\}\rangle=\mathrm{im\,}(B\gamma)
and thus
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\gamma
and
\gamma\notin Q
.
Also, looking at the expression of
\gamma
and noting that
\beta=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k}&b_{i}&a_{j}\\
b_{k}\beta&t_{i}(\overline{b_{k}\beta})&a_{j}\beta\end{pmatrix}
, we have that
\gamma\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
by Lemma
6.5
.
Therefore
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
as expected.

(3)

Last, assume that
\beta\notin Q
and
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})<\rho(\alpha)\leq\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B
. Keeping the same notation as above for
B\beta
,
B
and
A
, we write
A\alpha=\langle\left\{x_{k}\alpha\right\}\sqcup\left\{y_{\ell}\alpha\right\}\rangle
for some
\left\{x_{k}\right\},\left\{y_{\ell}\right\}\subseteq A
where
L\neq\emptyset
(since
\rho(\alpha)>\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})
). Since
\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B=\lvert J\rvert
, we can now rewrite the equation on the ranks in terms of the underlying sets, which gives us that

\lvert K\rvert<\lvert K\sqcup L\rvert\leq\lvert K\sqcup J\rvert.

Notice that if
\dim(B\beta)=\lvert K\rvert
is finite, then we directly obtain that
\lvert L\rvert\leq\lvert J\rvert
.
Otherwise
\lvert K\rvert
is infinite and from the equation above we have that
\lvert K\rvert<\lvert K\sqcup L\rvert=\max\left\{\lvert K\rvert,\lvert L\rvert\right\}
, so that
\lvert L\rvert>\lvert K\rvert
.
Similarly
\lvert J\rvert>\lvert K\rvert
, which put together gives us that
\lvert L\rvert=\lvert K\sqcup L\rvert\leq\lvert K\sqcup J\rvert=\lvert J\rvert
.
From the inequality on the cardinals of
L
and
J
in both the finite and the infinite dimensional cases, we can extract a subset
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
such that
\phi\colon J^{\prime}\to L
is a bijection. From this we define
\left\{z_{j}\right\}\subseteq B
for all
j\in J
by
z_{j}=y_{j\phi}\alpha
if
j\in J^{\prime}
and
z_{j}=y_{1}\alpha
(which necessarily exists) otherwise.
Under this definition, it is easy to see that
\langle z_{j}\rangle=\langle\left\{y_{\ell}\alpha\right\}\rangle
.
Now we define a map
\gamma\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
as follows:

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k}&b_{i}&a_{j}\\
x_{k}\alpha&t_{i}(\overline{x_{k}\alpha})&z_{j}\end{pmatrix},

where the terms
t_{i}
are such that
b_{i}\beta=t_{i}(\overline{b_{k}\beta})
. Then we have that
\mathrm{im\,}\gamma=\langle\left\{x_{k}\alpha\right\}\sqcup\left\{y_{\ell}\alpha\right\}\rangle=\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
, that is,
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\gamma
. Similarly as above, we can also see that
\gamma\notin Q
and that
\gamma\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
by Lemma
6.5
.
Therefore
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
, which concludes the last case of the proof.∎

If
\mathcal{B}
is a maximal proper subalgebra of a
\mathcal{A}
, then the set of maps that can be reached through a series of composition of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
starting from a map
\alpha
with finite rank is restricted to those having the same rank as
\alpha
.
This is stated formally and proved in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.9
.

Assume that
\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B=1
and let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
be a map of finite rank.
Then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
for some
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
.

Proof
.

Since the sufficient condition was given by Lemma
6.7
, we only need to show one direction.
So we let
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
be such that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
.

We first suppose that
\beta\in Q
.
Then by Proposition
6.8
,
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
if and only if
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}\beta
which is equivalent to
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
.

Now suppose that
\beta\notin Q
. Since
\alpha
has finite rank, only case 3 of Proposition
6.8
can occur. Hence we have that
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})<\rho(\alpha)\leq\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B=\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+1
.
This forces
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})
to be finite and using the contrapositive of Lemma
3.5
we get

\displaystyle\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})=\dim(B\beta)

\displaystyle<\rho(\beta)=\dim(A\beta)

\displaystyle\leq\dim(B\beta)+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B

\displaystyle=\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+1,

which in turn forces
\rho(\beta)=\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+1
.
Similarly,
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+1
, and so
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Conversely, if
\mathcal{B}
is not a maximal proper subalgebra then, by consecutive compositions of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
, we are able to go up and down the finite ranks as long as the map we started with does not have the minimal rank
e
where
e
was defined in Section
3
as the smallest rank of a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
.
This process is given formally by the following lemma.

Lemma 6.10
.

Assume that
\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B\geq 2
and let
\alpha\in Q
be such that
e<\rho(\alpha)<\aleph_{0}
.

If
\rho(\alpha)\geq e+2
then there exist
\delta_{1}\notin Q
and
\gamma_{1}\in Q
such that
\rho(\delta_{1})=\rho(\gamma_{1})=\rho(\alpha)-1
and
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\delta_{1}\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\gamma_{1}
.

If
\rho(\alpha)<\dim B
, then there exist
\delta_{2}\notin Q
and
\gamma_{2}\in Q
such that
\rho(\delta_{2})=\rho(\alpha)
,
\rho(\gamma_{2})=\rho(\alpha)+1
and
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\delta_{2}\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{L}^{*}}\gamma_{2}
.

Consequently, for all
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
e<\rho(\beta)<\aleph_{0}
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha\left(\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\right)^{n}\beta
.

Proof
.

Suppose that
\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B\geq 2
and consider
\alpha\in Q
such that
e<\rho(\alpha)<\aleph_{0}
.
Since
\alpha
is regular, we let
A\alpha=B\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
,
B=\langle\left\{b_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{c_{k}\right\}\rangle
and
A=\langle\left\{b_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{c_{k}\right\}\sqcup\left\{x_{j}\right\}\rangle
with
\lvert J\rvert\geq 2
.
Then we have that

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i}&c_{k}&x_{j}\\
b_{i}\alpha&u_{k}(\overline{b_{i}\alpha})&v_{j}(\overline{b_{i}\alpha})\end{pmatrix}

for some terms
u_{k}
and
v_{j}
.

For the first part, since
\lvert I\rvert=\rho(\alpha)\geq 2
, define
\delta_{1}
and
\gamma_{1}
as follows:

\delta_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i\geq 3}&\left\{b_{1},b_{2}\right\}&c_{k}&x_{j}\\
b_{i}\alpha&d&d&b_{2}\alpha\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i\geq 2}&b_{1}&c_{k}&x_{j}\\
b_{i}\alpha&b_{2}\alpha&b_{2}\alpha&b_{2}\alpha\end{pmatrix},

where the set
\left\{b_{i\geq 3}\right\}
is possibly empty and the element
d\in B
is taken as a constant if
e=0
, and
d=b_{3}\alpha
otherwise (which necessarily exists since
\lvert I\rvert=\rho(\alpha)\geq 3
in that case).
Thus
\delta_{1}\notin Q
,
\gamma_{1}\in Q
and we have that
\mathrm{im\,}\delta_{1}=\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\setminus\left\{b_{1}\alpha\right\}\rangle=\mathrm{im\,}\gamma_{1}
, so that
\delta_{1}\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\gamma_{1}
and
\rho(\delta_{1})=\rho(\gamma_{1})=\lvert I\rvert-1
.
Also
\rho(\delta_{1}\lvert_{B})=\lvert I\rvert-2<\lvert I\rvert=\rho(\alpha)
and
\rho(\delta_{1}\lvert_{B})+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B\geq\lvert I\rvert-2+2=\rho(\alpha)
, which shows that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\delta_{1}
by Proposition
6.8
and finishes the first part of the proof.

Now assume that
e<\rho(\alpha)<\dim B
, then there exists an element
z\in B
such that
z\notin\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
.
Using the same notations as above with this time
\lvert I\rvert=\rho(\alpha)\geq 1
, define
\delta_{2}
and
\gamma_{2}
by

\delta_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i\geq 2}&b_{1}&c_{k}&x_{j}\\
b_{i}\alpha&d&d&b_{1}\alpha\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i}&c_{k}&x_{j}\\
b_{i}\alpha&z&x_{j}\alpha\end{pmatrix},

where the set
\left\{b_{i\geq 2}\right\}
is again possibly empty and the element
d\in B
is defined in a similar way as before, that is,
d
is chosen as any constant if
e=0
and is set to
b_{2}\alpha
(which then exists) otherwise.
Then clearly
\delta_{2}\notin Q
and
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha=\mathrm{im\,}\delta_{2}
so
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\delta_{2}
.
Also
\left\{x_{j}\alpha\right\}\subseteq\langle\left\{b_{i}\alpha\right\}\rangle
so
\gamma_{2}\in Q
.
Finally,
\rho(\gamma_{2})=\lvert I\rvert+1\leq\rho(\delta_{2}\lvert_{B})+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B
and since
\rho(\delta_{2}\lvert_{B})=\lvert I\rvert-1<\rho(\gamma_{2})
we have that
\gamma_{2}\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\delta_{2}
from Proposition
6.8
, finishing the proof of the second part of the lemma.

Now consider
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
e<\rho(\beta)<\aleph_{0}
.
If
\beta\notin Q
, then by Lemma
3.4
, there exists
\beta^{\prime}\in Q
such that
\mathrm{im\,}\beta^{\prime}=\mathrm{im\,}\beta
and thus,
\beta^{\prime}\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}\beta\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
, so we can assume that
\beta\in Q
in the first place.
Similarly, we can assume that
\rho(\alpha)\geq\rho(\beta)
since otherwise we can exchange the role of
\alpha
and
\beta
.
If
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
, then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
by Lemma
6.7
, so we can assume that
\rho(\alpha)>\rho(\beta)
.
Set
m=\rho(\alpha)-\rho(\beta)
and construct
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}\in Q
by the process described in the first part of the lemma with the following properties:

\bullet

\rho(\gamma_{1})=\rho(\alpha)-1
and
\alpha\left(\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\right)^{2}\gamma_{1}
;

\bullet

\rho(\gamma_{m})=\rho(\beta)
; and

\bullet

\rho(\gamma_{r+1})=\rho(\gamma_{r})-1
and
\gamma_{r}\left(\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\right)^{2}\gamma_{r+1}
for all
1\leq r\leq m-1
.

Then we have that
\alpha\left(\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\right)^{2m}\gamma_{m}
and
\gamma_{m}\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
.
Therefore, in all cases, we have that
\alpha\left(\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\right)^{n}\beta
for some integer
n
.
∎

The two previous lemmas were concerned by maps of finite rank.
The following two on the other hand, are focused on maps of infinite rank, whenever this is possible.
We first show that if the subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
has a codimension smaller than its dimension, then the relation
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}
can only keep the higher ranks stable.

Lemma 6.11
.

Assume that
\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B<\aleph_{\kappa}\leq\dim B
and let
\alpha\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
be such that
\rho(\alpha)=\aleph_{\kappa}
. Then
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
for some
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if
\rho(\beta)=\rho(\alpha)
.

Proof
.

Since one direction is already given by Lemma
6.7
, we assume that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
and we go through the cases of Proposition
6.8
.
If
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}\beta
, then we directly have that
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(\beta)
.
On the other hand, if
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha\cong\mathrm{im\,}(B\beta)
then
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})=\aleph_{\kappa}
.
But then
\aleph_{\kappa}=\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})\leq\rho(\beta)\leq\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B=\aleph_{\kappa}
, and therefore
\rho(\beta)=\aleph_{\kappa}=\rho(\alpha)
.
Notice that the third case of the proposition cannot occur since we would have that
\aleph_{\kappa}=\rho(\alpha)\leq\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})+\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B
which forces
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})=\aleph_{\kappa}
and thus the condition
\rho(\beta\lvert_{B})<\rho(\alpha)
is not satisfied.
∎

In another way, if the dimension and codimension of
B
are both infinite cardinals, from a map with rank at least
\aleph_{0}
, we can reach maps with larger infinite rank through the relation
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}
, as long as we do not go further than the codimension of
B
.
This idea is given more formally in the following Lemma.

Lemma 6.12
.

Assume that
\dim B\geq\aleph_{0}
and
\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B=\aleph_{k}
and set
M=\min\{\dim B,\mathrm{codim_{A}\,}B\}
. Let
\alpha\in Q
be such that
\aleph_{0}\leq\rho(\alpha)<M
. Then for all
\nu
with
\rho(\alpha)<\nu\leq M
there exists
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that
\rho(\beta)=\nu
and
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
.

Proof
.

Let
\alpha\in Q
. Then we can write
A\alpha=B\alpha=\langle\left\{b_{k}\alpha\right\}\rangle
,
B=\langle\left\{b_{k}\right\}\sqcup\left\{c_{i}\right\}\rangle
and
A=\langle\left\{b_{k}\right\}\sqcup\left\{c_{i}\right\}\sqcup\left\{a_{j}\right\}\rangle
.
By assumption on the rank of
\alpha
, we have that
\lvert K\sqcup I\rvert=\dim B>\rho(\alpha)=\lvert K\rvert\geq\aleph_{0}
, and thus it follows that
\lvert I\rvert=\dim B>\aleph_{0}
.
Now, let
\nu
be such that
\rho(\alpha)<\nu\leq M=\min\{\lvert I\rvert,\lvert J\rvert\}
.
Then there exist sets
S\subseteq J
and
S^{\prime}\subseteq I
such that
\lvert S\rvert=\lvert S^{\prime}\rvert=\nu
, and we let
\phi\colon S\to S^{\prime}
be a bijection between them.
For all
j\in J
we now set elements
z_{j}\in B
by
z_{j}=c_{j\phi}
if
j\in S
and
z_{j}=c_{1}
otherwise, and we define the map
\beta\in T(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
as:

\beta=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k}&c_{i}&a_{j}\\
b_{k}\alpha&b_{1}\alpha&z_{j}\end{pmatrix}.

Clearly we have that
\beta\notin Q
and
\rho(\beta)=\lvert K\sqcup S\rvert=\lvert K\rvert+\nu=\rho(\alpha)+\nu=\nu
.
Also
\mathrm{im\,}(B\beta)=\langle\left\{b_{k}\alpha\right\}\rangle=\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
and from the second case of Proposition
6.8
we have that
\alpha\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{R}^{*}}\beta
, which concludes the proof.

We now have all the tools needed to prove the characterisation of

Remark
.

From the characterisation of
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}
in Theorem

The relation
\mathop{\mathcal{J}^{*}}

\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}

\alpha
that is the union of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}^{*}}
-classes and
\mathop{\mathcal{R}^{*}}
-classes of its elements.
The relation
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}}
is defined similarly on

using the
\sim

\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}

\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}

,
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}\subseteq\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}}
,
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}\subseteq\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{J}^{*}}\subseteq\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}}
.

Since we have determined the
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}
-classes, and
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{*}}
is a subset of
\mathop{\mathcal{J}^{*}}
,
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}}

is quite straightforward to determine.
In order to work with
\mathop{\mathcal{J}^{*}}

if and only if there exist

for all

An equivalent characterisation for
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}}
in terms of
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
has been given by Ren, Shum and Guo in
[

From this we have the short proposition that follows:

In
