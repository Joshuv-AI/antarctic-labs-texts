Abstract
. We consider initial boundary value problems with the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition. Given an initial value, we establish the uniqueness in determining a spatially varying coefficient of zeroth-order term by a single measurement of Dirichlet data on an arbitrarily chosen subboundary. The uniqueness holds in a subdomain where the initial value is positive, provided that it is sufficiently smooth which is specified by decay rates of the Fourier coefficients. The key idea is the reduction to an inverse elliptic problem and relies on elliptic Carleman estimates. Key words. inverse coefficient problem, parabolic equation, uniqueness, initial boundary value problem, inverse elliptic problem, Carleman estimate AMS subject classifications. 35R30, 35K15

Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
C^{2}-
boundary

\partial\Omega
.
We set
\nu=(\nu_{1}(x),\dots,\nu_{n}(x))
be an outward unit normal vector to

\partial\Omega
, and

\partial_{k}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}},\quad 1\leq k\leq n,\quad\partial_{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}.

We assume

a_{ij}=a_{ji}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\quad\mbox{for all $1\leq i,j\leq n$},\quad c_{1},c_{2}\in C(\overline{\Omega}),

and there exists a constant
\kappa_{1}>0
such that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\kappa_{1}|\xi|^{2}\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\overline{\Omega}$ and
$\xi=(\xi_{1},...\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

We set

\partial_{\nu_{A}}v:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(x)\nu_{i}(x)\partial_{j}v(x),\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

Moreover let

[formula]

A(x,D)v(x)=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}(x)\partial_{j}v(x))\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathcal{D}(A):=\{v\in H^{2}(\Omega);\,\partial_{\nu_{A_{0}}}v=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$}\}.

In this article, we consider the folowing

Inverse problem.

Let

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}u=-A(x,D)u+c_{1}(x)u\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\times(0,T)$},\\
&\partial_{\nu_{A}}u=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$},\\
&u(x,0)=a(x),\quad x\in\Omega,\end{array}\right.

and

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}v=-A(x,D)v+c_{2}(x)v\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\times(0,T)$},\\
&\partial_{\nu_{A}}v=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$},\\
&v(x,0)=a(x),\quad x\in\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Let an initial value
a
be suitably given and

be an arbitrarily chosen non-empty connected
relatively open subset of
\partial\Omega
.
Then

u=v
on
\gamma\times(0,T)
implies
c_{1}=c_{2}
in
\Omega
?

Most general results are obtained for the case when a time of observation

2.
Key Carleman estimate

The proof of Theorem 1 relies essentially on the reduction of our

After the reduction, we prove the uniqueness by the method developed in

[
2
]
or,
[

]

For the statement of Carleman estimate, we introduce a weight function.

We arbitrarily fix

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mbox{(i)}\,\,y\in\omega_{y},\quad\omega_{y}\subset\omega.\\

&\mbox{(iii)}\,\,\mbox{$\partial\omega_{y}\cap\Gamma$ has interior points

$x\in\overline{\omega_{y}}$}.\end{array}\right.

Indeed, since

For the proof of Theorem 1, we will show that if

This of course implies that

\omega

First we establish a Carleman estimate in

We know that there exists a function

[formula]

|\nabla d(x)|>0\quad\mbox{for $x\in\overline{\omega_{y}}$},\quad d(x)>0\quad\mbox{for $x\in\omega_{y}$},\quad d(x)=0\quad\mbox{for $x\in\partial\omega_{y}\setminus\Gamma$}.

]

For a constant

\partial_{0}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

[formula]

\alpha(x,t):=e^{\lambda(d(x)-\beta t^{2})},\quad(x,t)\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}

Lemma 2.1 (elliptic Carleman estimate).

There exists a constant

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}\left\{\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}|\partial_{i}\partial_{j}w|^{2}+s|\partial_{t}w|^{2}+s|\nabla w|^{2}+s^{3}|w|^{2}\right)e^{2s\alpha}dxdt

\displaystyle C\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}|\partial_{t}^{2}w-A_{1}w|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt

for all

and

Here we recall that

\partial_{t}^{2}-A_{1}w
. Since
(\nabla\alpha,\,\partial_{t}\alpha)=(\nabla d,\,-2\beta t)\neq(0,0)
on

[

[

\mathbb{N}_{1}

\mathbb{M}_{1}
as

\mathbb{N}_{1}=\{1,....,N_{1}\},\quad\mathbb{M}_{1}=\{1,....,M_{1}\},

where
N_{1}=\infty
or
M_{1}=\infty
may occur.
By
{}^{\sharp}\mathbb{N}_{1}
we mean the cardinal number of the set
\mathbb{N}_{1}
.
Wote that

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{N_{1}}\quad\mbox{if ${}^{\sharp}\mathbb{N}_{1}<\infty$}\quad\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots\quad\mbox{if ${}^{\sharp}\mathbb{N}_{1}=\infty$}

and

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{M_{1}}\quad\mbox{if ${}^{\sharp}\mathbb{M}_{1}<\infty$}\quad\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots\quad\mbox{if ${}^{\sharp}\mathbb{M}_{1}=\infty$}.

Assuming that
u=v
on
\gamma\times(0,T)
, by the time analyticity
of
u(t)
and
v(t)
for
t>0
(e.g., Pazy
[
14
]
), we obtain

[formula]

\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}e^{-\lambda_{k}t}P_{k}a=\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}}e^{-\mu_{k}t}Q_{k}a\quad\mbox{on $\gamma\times(0,\infty)$}.

We will prove that
\lambda_{1}=\mu_{1}
.
Assume that
\lambda_{1}<\mu_{1}
. Then

P_{1}a+\sum_{k=2}^{N_{1}}e^{-(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{1})t}P_{k}a=\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}}e^{-(\mu_{k}-\lambda_{1})t}Q_{k}a\quad\mbox{on $\gamma\times(0,\infty)$}.

Since
\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{1}>0
for
2\leq k\leq N_{1}
and

\mu_{k}-\lambda_{1}>0
for
1\leq k\leq M_{1}
, letting
t\to\infty
,
we see that
P_{1}a=0
on
\Gamma
.
Therefore

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&(A_{1}-\lambda_{1})P_{1}a=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$},\\
&P_{1}a|_{\Gamma}=0,\quad\partial_{\nu_{A}}P_{1}a|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{array}\right.

The unique continuation for the elliptic equation
A_{1}P_{1}a=\lambda_{1}P_{1}a
, (see e.g.
[
6
]
)
yields that

P_{1}a=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$}.

This is a contradiction by
1\in\mathbb{N}_{1}
.
Thus the inequality
\lambda_{1}<\mu_{1}
is impossible.
Similarly we can see that the inequality
\lambda_{1}>\mu_{1}
is impossible.
Therefore
\lambda_{1}=\mu_{1}
follows.

By (3.2) and
\lambda_{1}=\mu_{1}
, we have

P_{1}a-Q_{1}a=-\sum_{k=2}^{N_{1}}e^{-(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{1})t}P_{k}a+\sum_{k=2}^{M_{1}}e^{-(\mu_{k}-\lambda_{1})t}Q_{k}a\quad\mbox{on $\gamma\times(0,\infty)$}.

Hence, by
\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{1}>0
and
\mu_{k}-\lambda_{1}=\mu_{k}-\mu_{1}>0
for
all
k\geq 2
, letting
s\to\infty
we obtain
P_{1}a=Q_{1}a
on
\gamma
.

In view of (3.2), we obtain

\sum_{k=2}^{N_{1}}e^{-\lambda_{k}t}P_{k}a=\sum_{k=2}^{M_{1}}e^{-\mu_{k}t}Q_{k}a\quad\mbox{on $\gamma\times(0,\infty)$}.

Repeating the same argument as much as possible, we reach

[formula]

N_{1}=M_{1},\quad\lambda_{k}=\mu_{k},\quad P_{k}a=Q_{k}a\quad\mbox{on $\gamma\times(0,\infty)$ for
$1\leq k\leq N_{1}$}.

Second Step.

We consider two initial boundary value problems for elliptic equations:

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}^{2}w_{1}-A_{1}w_{1}=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\times(0,\tau)$},\\
&\partial_{\nu_{A}}w_{1}=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega\times(0,\tau)$},\\
&w_{1}(x,0)=a(x),\quad\partial_{t}w_{1}(x,0)=0,\quad x\in\Omega\end{array}\right.

and

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}^{2}w_{2}-A_{2}w_{2}=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\times(0,\tau)$},\\
&\partial_{\nu_{A}}w_{2}=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega\times(0,\tau)$},\\
&w_{2}(x,0)=a(x),\quad\partial_{t}w_{2}(x,0)=0,\quad x\in\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Since we have the spectral representations by (1.9), we can obtain

e^{-tA_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}}a=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}P_{k}a,\quad e^{tA_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}}a=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}P_{k}a\quad\mbox{in $L^{2}(\Omega)$ for $t>0$}

and similar representations hold for
e^{\pm tA_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}}a
.

Then by the assumption (1.9) on
a
, we see that

w_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}(e^{-tA_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}}a+e^{tA_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}}a),\quad w_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{2}(e^{-tA_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}}a+e^{tA_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}}a)

in
H^{2}(\Omega\times(0,\tau))
for
t\in(0,\tau)
, satisfy (3.4) and (3.5)
respectively if
\tau>0
is chosen sufficiently small.

In view of (3.3) and the definition (3.1) of
\mathbb{N}_{1}
,
the spectral representations imply

\displaystyle w_{1}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}(e^{-\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}P_{k}a+e^{\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}P_{k}a)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,...,N_{1}\}}(e^{-\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}P_{k}a+e^{\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}P_{k}a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}(e^{-\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}P_{k}a+e^{\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}P_{k}a)\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\times(0,\tau)$,}

and

w_{2}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}(e^{-\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}Q_{k}a+e^{\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}t}Q_{k}a)\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\times(0,\tau)$}.

Therefore (3.3) yields

[formula]

w_{1}=w_{2}\quad\mbox{on $\gamma\times(0,\tau)$.}

Now we reduce our inverse problem for the parabolic equations to the one
for elliptic equations for (3.4) and (3.5).
This is the essence of the proof.

Third Step.

By (1.9), we can readily verify further regularity
\partial_{t}w_{1},\partial_{t}w_{2}\in H^{2}(0,\tau;H^{2}(\Omega))
.
Setting
y:=w_{1}-w_{2}
and
R:=w_{2}
in
\Omega\times(0,\tau)

and
f:=c_{2}-c_{1}
in
\Omega
, by (3.4) -(3.6) we have

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}^{2}y-A_{1}y=f(x)R(x,t)\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\times(0,\tau)$},\\
&\partial_{\nu_{A}}y=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega\times(0,\tau)$},\\
&y=0\quad\mbox{on $\gamma\times(0,\tau)$},\\
&y(x,0)=\partial_{t}y(x,0)=0,\quad x\in\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Now we will prove that for any pair
(y,f)
solving problem (3.7) we have
y\notin\mbox{supp},f.
Since
y
was chosen as an arbitrary point from
\omega
this implies

f=0\quad\mbox{in}\quad\omega.

The argument relies on
[
9
]
.

We set

\widetilde{y}(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&y(x,t),\quad 0<t<\tau,\\
&y(x,-t),\quad-\tau<t<0\end{array}\right.

and

\widetilde{R}(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&R(x,t),\quad 0<t<\tau,\\
&R(x,-t),\quad-\tau<t<0.\end{array}\right.

Then we see that
\widetilde{y}\in H^{3}(-\tau,\tau;H^{2}(\omega_{y}))
by

y(\cdot,0)=\partial_{t}y(\cdot,0)=0
in
\omega_{y}
, and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}^{2}\widetilde{y}-A_{1}\widetilde{y}=f(x)\widetilde{R}(x,t)\quad\mbox{in ${\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}:=\omega_{y}\times(-\tau,\tau)$},\\
&\widetilde{y}=\partial_{\nu_{A}}\widetilde{y}=0\quad\mbox{on $(\partial\omega_{y}\cap\Gamma)\times(-\tau,\tau)$},\\
&\widetilde{y}(x,0)=\partial_{t}\widetilde{y}(x,0)=0,\quad x\in\omega_{y}.\end{array}\right.

Setting
\widetilde{z}:=\partial_{t}\widetilde{y}
, we have
\widetilde{z}\in H^{2}(Q_{\tau})
and

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}^{2}\widetilde{z}-A_{1}\widetilde{z}=f(x)\partial_{t}\widetilde{R}(x,t)\quad\mbox{in ${\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}$},\\
&\widetilde{z}=\partial_{\nu_{A}}\widetilde{z}=0\quad\mbox{on $(\partial\omega_{y}\cap\Gamma)\times(-\tau,\tau)$},\\
&\widetilde{z}(x,0)=0,\quad x\in\omega_{y}\end{array}\right.

and

[formula]

\partial_{t}\widetilde{z}(x,0)=f(x)a(x),\quad x\in\omega_{y}.

In the weight function
d(x)-\beta t^{2}
in Lemma 2.1, for
\tau>0
we choose

\beta>0
sufficiently large,

[formula]

\|d\|_{C(\overline{\omega_{y}})}-\beta\tau^{2}<0,

We choose constants

[formula]

\|d\|_{C(\overline{\omega_{y}})}-\beta\tau^{2}<0<\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad d(y)>\delta_{2}

and we define

[formula]

\chi(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&1,\quad d(x)-\beta t^{2}>\delta_{2},\\
&0,\quad d(x)-\beta t^{2}<\delta_{1}.\end{array}\right.

In particularl,

implies

Setting

on
\overline{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}
, we see that

[formula]

z=\partial_{\nu_{A}}z=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\omega_{y}\times(-\tau,\tau)$}

and

[formula]

z=\partial_{t}z=0\quad\mbox{on $\omega_{y}\times\{\pm\tau\}$}.

Indeed,
(x,t)\in(\partial\omega_{y}\setminus\Gamma)\times(-\tau,\tau)

For

.

\blacksquare

Consequently we prove that
z\in H^{2}_{0}(Q_{\tau})
.
Moreover, we can readily obtain

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}^{2}z-A_{1}z=\chi(\partial_{t}\widetilde{R})f+R_{0}(x,t),\quad(x,t)\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau},\\
&z=|\nabla z|=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}$},\end{array}\right.

where

is a linear combination of
\nabla\widetilde{z}
,
\partial_{t}\widetilde{z}
,
whose coefficients are linear combinations of
\nabla\chi
and

\partial_{t}\chi
. Therefore (3.12) implies

[formula]

R_{0}(x,t)\neq 0\quad\mbox{only if $\delta_{1}\leq d(x)-\beta t^{2}\leq\delta_{2}$}.

Therefore we can apply Lemma 2.1 to (3.15) with (3.16):

[formula]

\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}\left(\frac{1}{s}|\partial_{t}^{2}z|^{2}+s|\partial_{t}z|^{2}\right)e^{2s\alpha}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}|\chi(\partial_{t}\widetilde{R})f|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt+C\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}|R_{0}(x,t)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}\chi^{2}|f|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt+Ce^{2se^{\lambda\delta_{2}}}

for all large
s>0
.

On the other hand, since
\partial_{t}z(\cdot,-\tau)=0
in
\omega_{y}
by
(3.14), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\omega_{y}}|\partial_{t}z(x,0)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,0)}dx=\int^{0}_{-\tau}\partial_{t}\left(\int_{\omega_{y}}|\partial_{t}z(x,t)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}dx\right)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{0}_{-\tau}\int_{\omega_{y}}\{2(\partial_{t}z)(x,t)\partial_{t}^{2}z(z,t)+|\partial_{t}z|^{2}2s(\partial_{t}\alpha))\}e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}dxdt.

Since

|(\partial_{t}z)(\partial_{t}^{2}z)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(s|\partial_{t}z|^{2}+\frac{1}{s}|\partial_{t}^{2}z|^{2}\right)\quad\mbox{in $\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}$},

in terms of (3.17) we obtain

[formula]

\int_{\omega_{y}}|\partial_{t}z(x,0)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,0)}dx\leq C\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}\left(\frac{1}{s}|\partial_{t}^{2}z(x,t)|^{2}+s|\partial_{t}z|^{2}\right)e^{2s\alpha}dxdt

\leq C\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}|\chi|^{2}|f|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt+Ce^{2se^{\lambda\delta_{2}}}

for all large
s>0
.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z(x,0)=\partial_{t}(\chi\widetilde{z})(x,0)=(\partial_{t}\chi)(x,0)\widetilde{z}(x,0)+\chi(x,0)\partial_{t}\widetilde{z}(x,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi(x,0)f(x)a(x)

by (3.9) and
\widetilde{z}(x,0)=0
for
x\in\omega_{y}
in (3.8).
Therefore, in terms of (2.1)-(iv), we obtain

|\partial_{t}z(x,0)|\geq C|\chi(x,0)f(x)|,\quad x\in\overline{\omega_{y}}.

Consequently (3.18) implies

[formula]

\int_{\omega_{y}}|\chi(x,0)|^{2}|f(x)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,0)}dx\leq C\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}}|\chi f|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt+Ce^{2se^{\lambda\delta_{2}}}

for all large
s>0
.

Moreover, we see

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}}|\chi f|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt=\int^{\tau}_{-\tau}\int_{\omega_{y}}|\chi(x,0)f(x)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\omega_{y}}|\chi(x,0)f(x)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,0)}\left(\int^{\tau}_{-\tau}e^{2s(\alpha(x,t)-\alpha(x,0))}dt\right)dx.

Since

\int^{\tau}_{-\tau}e^{2s(\alpha(x,t)-\alpha(x,0))}dt=\int^{\tau}_{-\tau}e^{2se^{\lambda d(x)}(e^{-\lambda\beta t^{2}}-1)}dt\leq\int^{\tau}_{-\tau}e^{Cs(e^{-\lambda\beta t^{2}}-1)}dt=o(1)

as
s\to\infty
by the Lebesgue convergence theorem.
Hence, (3.19) yields

\int_{\omega_{y}}|\chi(x,0)f(x)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,0)}dx\leq o(1)\int_{\omega_{y}}|\chi(x,0)f(x)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,0)}dx+Ce^{2se^{\lambda\delta_{2}}},

and we can absorb the first term on the right-hand side into the
left-hand side to reach

[formula]

\int_{\omega_{y}}|\chi(x,0)f(x)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,0)}dx\leq Ce^{2se^{\lambda\delta_{2}}}

for all large
s>0
.

Henceforth we set
B(y,\varepsilon):=\{x;\,|x-y|<\varepsilon\}
.
Then, we can choose
\delta_{3}>\delta_{2}
and
a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
such that

B(y,\varepsilon)\subset\omega_{y}
and
d(x)\geq\delta_{3}
for all

x\in B(y,\varepsilon)
. This is possible, because
d(y)>\delta_{2}
in
(3.11) and
\omega_{y}
is an open set including
y
.

We shrink the integration region of the left-hand side of (3.20)
to
B(y,\varepsilon)
and obtain

\int_{B(y,\varepsilon)}|\chi(x,0)f(x)|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,0)}dx\leq Ce^{2se^{\lambda\delta_{2}}}

for all large
s>0
.
Since
d(x)\geq\delta_{3}>\delta_{2}
for
x\in B(y,\varepsilon)
, the condition (3.12)
yields
\chi(x,0)=1
and
\alpha(x,0)=e^{\lambda d(x)}\geq e^{\lambda\delta_{3}}
for all
x\in B(y,\varepsilon)
. Therefore,

\left(\int_{B(y,\varepsilon)}|f(x)|^{2}dx\right)e^{2se^{\lambda\delta_{3}}}\leq Ce^{2se^{\lambda\delta_{2}}},

that is,
\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B(y,\varepsilon))}\leq e^{-2s(e^{\lambda\delta_{3}}-e^{\lambda\delta_{2}})}
for all large
s>0
.

In terms of
\delta_{3}>\delta_{2}
, letting
s\to\infty
,
we see that the right-hand side
tends to

in
B(y,\varepsilon)

we reach

in

.

\blacksquare

Fourth Step.

We will complete the proof of Theorem 1.1 by demonstrating
that (1.7) implies (1.9).
Without loss of generality, we can assume

[formula]

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{\theta(\lambda_{k})}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty.

It suffices to prove that there exists a constant
\sigma_{1}>0
such that

[formula]

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{\sigma_{1}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|Q_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty,

with the assumption that the set of
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

Q_{k}a\neq 0
in
\Omega
is infinite.
For simplicity, we can consider the case where

P_{k}a\neq 0
in
\Omega
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We can argue similarly
in the rest cases.
Then, by Corollary which was already proved in First Step, we choose
a subset
\mathbb{M}_{1}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{M}_{1}},\quad Q_{j}a=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$ for $j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathbb{M}_{1}$}.

Now it suffices to prove (3.22) in the case where
Q_{k}a\neq 0
in

\Omega
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

After re-numbering, we can obtain

[formula]

\lambda_{k}=\mu_{k},\quad P_{k}a=Q_{k}a\quad\mbox{on $\gamma$ for all
$k\in\mathbb{N}$}.

The trace theorem and the a priori estimate for an elliptic operator
yields

[formula]

\|P_{k}a\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\leq C\|P_{k}a\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\|A_{1}P_{k}a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|P_{k}a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)})=C(\lambda_{k}+1)\|P_{k}a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Here and henceforth
C>0
denotes generic constants which are
independent of
s>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Since
A_{2}Q_{k}=\lambda_{k}Q_{k}
, by (3.23) we apply Lemma 2.2 to have

[formula]

s^{3}\int_{\Omega}|Q_{k}a|^{2}e^{2s\psi}dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{k}^{2}|Q_{k}a|^{2}e^{2s\psi}dx+Cs^{3}\int_{\gamma}(|Q_{k}a|^{2}+|\nabla(Q_{k}a)|^{2})e^{2s\psi}dx

\leq C\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{k}^{2}|Q_{k}a|^{2}e^{2s\psi}dx+Cs^{3}e^{2sM}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\gamma)}

for all large
s>0
. Here we set

M:=\max_{x\in\overline{\Gamma}}\psi(x)
.

We choose
s>0
sufficiently large and set

s_{k}:=s^{*}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, using (3.24),
we obtain

\displaystyle({s^{*}}^{3}\lambda_{k}^{2}-C\lambda_{k}^{2})\int_{\Omega}|Q_{k}a|^{2}e^{2s_{k}\psi}dx\leq C{s^{*}}^{3}\lambda_{k}^{2}e^{2s_{k}M}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C{s^{*}}^{3}\lambda_{k}^{2}e^{2s_{k}M}(\lambda_{k}+1)^{2}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Since
\psi\geq 0
in
\Omega
and we can take
s^{*}>0
sufficiently large, we see

{s^{*}}^{3}\lambda_{k}^{2}\|Q_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C{s^{*}}^{3}\lambda_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}^{2}e^{2s_{k}M}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

that is,

\|Q_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\lambda_{k}^{2}e^{C_{1}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where we set
C_{1}:=2s^{*}M
. Here we note that
s^{*}
and
M
, and so
the constant
C_{1}
are independent of
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Therefore, since we can find a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\eta^{2}e^{C_{1}\eta^{\frac{2}{3}}+\sigma_{1}\eta^{\frac{1}{2}}}\leq C_{2}e^{C_{2}\eta^{\frac{2}{3}}}
for all
\eta\geq 0
,
we see

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{\sigma_{1}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|Q_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}e^{C_{1}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}+\sigma_{1}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{C_{2}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Moreover,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\theta(\lambda_{k})}{\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}}=\infty
yields that for the constant
C_{2}>0
we can choose
N\in\mathbb{N}

such that
C_{2}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}\leq\theta(\lambda_{k})
for
k\geq N
.
Consequently,

\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}e^{\sigma_{1}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|Q_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}e^{\theta(\lambda_{k})}\|P_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty,

and so

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{\sigma_{1}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|Q_{k}a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty.

Thus (3.21) completes the proof of Theorem 1.1.

\blacksquare

&\ell(0)=\ell(T)=0,\\
&\mbox{$\ell$ is strictly increasing on $\left[0,\,\frac{T}{4}\right]$
and strictly decreasing on $\left[\frac{T}{4},\,T\right]$}.\end{array}\right.

In particular,
\ell(t)\leq 1
for
0\leq t\leq T
. Choosing
\lambda>0

sufficiently large, we set

\alpha(x,t):=\frac{e^{\lambda\rho(x)}-e^{2\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}}{\ell(t)},\quad\varphi(x,t):=\frac{e^{\lambda\rho(x)}}{\ell(t)},\quad(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

Then we know

Lemma 4.1

There exist constants
s_{0}>0
and
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{Q}(s\varphi|\nabla U|^{2}+s^{3}\varphi^{3}|U|^{2})e^{2s\alpha}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{Q}|\partial_{t}U-A_{2}U|^{2}e^{2s\alpha}dxdt+C\int_{\gamma}(|\partial_{t}U|^{2}+s\varphi|\nabla U|^{2}+s^{3}\varphi^{3}|U|^{2})e^{2\alpha}dSdt

for all
s\geq s_{0}
and
U\in H^{2,1}(Q)
satisfying

\partial_{\nu_{A}}U=0
on
\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
.

The proof is found in Chae, Imanuvilov and Kim
[
4
]
.

We apply Lemma 4.1 to
g(x)
satisfying
\partial_{\nu_{A}}g=0
on
\partial\Omega
to
obtain

[formula]

\int_{Q}(s\varphi(x,t)|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+s^{3}\varphi^{3}(x,t)|g(x)|^{2})e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}dxdt

\leq C\int_{Q}|A_{2}g|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}dxdt+C\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\gamma}(s\varphi(x,t)|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+s^{3}\varphi^{3}|g(x)|^{2})e^{2\alpha}dSdt

for all
s\geq s_{0}
.
Moreover, in terms of (4.1) and
e^{\lambda\rho(x)}=e^{2\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}\psi(x)
for
x\in\Omega
,
we have

[formula]

\int_{Q}(s\varphi(x,t)|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+s^{3}\varphi^{3}(x,t)|g(x)|^{2})e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}dxdt

\geq\int^{\frac{3}{4}T}_{\frac{T}{4}}\int_{\Omega}(se^{\lambda\rho(x)}|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+s^{3}e^{3\lambda\rho(x)}|g(x)|^{2})\exp(2s(e^{\lambda\rho(x)}-e^{2\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}))dxdt

\geq C\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Omega}(s|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+s^{3}|g(x)|^{2})e^{2s(e^{2\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}\psi(x))}dxe^{-2se^{2\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}}

\geq C\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Omega}(s|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+s^{3}|g(x)|^{2})e^{2s\psi(x)}dxe^{-2se^{2\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}}.

Here
C>0
depends on
\lambda
but not on
s>0
.
By
e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}\leq 1
in
Q
, (4.2) and (4.3), we obtain

[formula]

C\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Omega}(s|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+s^{3}|g(x)|^{2})e^{2s\psi(x)}dxe^{-2se^{2\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}}

\leq C\int_{Q}|A_{2}g|^{2}e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}dxdt+C\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\gamma}(s\varphi(x,t)|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+s^{3}\varphi^{3}(x,t)|g(x)|^{2})e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}dSdt.

Since
\sup_{(x,t)\in\gamma\times(0,T)}|(s\varphi)^{k}e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}|<\infty
for
k=1,3
and

e^{2s\psi(x)}=e^{2se^{\lambda(\rho(x)-2\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})})}}\geq e^{2se^{-\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}},

we can find a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\lambda)>0
such that

(s\varphi(x,t))^{k}e^{2s\alpha(x,t)}\leq C_{1}e^{2s\psi(x)},\quad(x,t)\in\gamma\times(0,T).

Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(s|\nabla g|^{2}+s^{3}|g|^{2})e^{2s\psi(x)}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{2se^{2\lambda\|\rho\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|A_{2}g|^{2}e^{2s\psi}dx+\int_{\gamma}(s|\nabla g|^{2}+s^{3}|g(x)|^{2})e^{2s\psi}dS\right).

Substituting (4.3) and (4.4) into (4.2), we complete the proof of
Lemma 2.2.

\blacksquare
