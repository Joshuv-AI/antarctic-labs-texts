Metric-valued regression
Dan Tsir Cohen, Aryeh Kontorovich

Abstract
We propose an efficient algorithm for learning mappings between two metric spaces, 𝒳 {\cal X} and 𝒴 {\cal Y} . Our procedure is strongly Bayes-consistent whenever 𝒳 {\cal X} and 𝒴 {\cal Y} are topologically separable and 𝒴 {\cal Y} is “bounded in expectation” (our term; the separability assumption can be somewhat weakened). At this level of generality, ours is the first such learnability result for unbounded loss in the agnostic setting. Our technique is based on metric medoids (a variant of Fréchet means) and presents a significant departure from existing methods, which, as we demonstrate, fail to achieve Bayes-consistency on general instance- and label-space metrics. Our proofs introduce the technique of semi-stable compression , which may be of independent interest.

Main results and overview of techniques

Our main result is the existence of a strong Universal Bayes-Consistent (UBC) learner for metric-valued regression.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a learning algorithm,
{\mathsf{MedNet}}
, with the following property.
Let
({\cal X},\rho)
and
({\cal Y},\ell)
be separable metric spaces
endowed with
a distribution
{\bar{\mu}}

supported on the product Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
,
such that

{\cal Y}
is bounded in expectation:

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}\ell(y_{0},Y)<\infty

for some
y_{0}\in{\cal Y}
.
Given a training sample
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[n]}\sim{\bar{\mu}}^{n}
as input,

{\mathsf{MedNet}}
outputs a predictor
f_{n}:{\cal X}\to{\cal Y}

that is strongly universally Bayes-consistent:

R(f_{n})\to R^{*}

almost surely (under
{\bar{\mu}}
)
as
n\to\infty
,
where

R(f)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}\ell(f(X),Y)

is the risk and
R^{*}
the
Bayes-optimal risk
(minimum risk achieved by any measurable
f
).

The proof proceeds via a sequence of incremental results, culminating in Theorem
12
,
which is a restatement of Theorem
1
.
A few remarks regarding our assumptions on

({\cal X},\rho)

and

({\cal Y},\ell)

are in order.
As per
Hanneke et al. 2021
,
the assumption of separability
may be weakened to

essential

separability (ES):
this is the condition that the
support of
{\bar{\mu}}

is contained in a separable subspace.
It was shown therein that the ES condition (on
{\cal X}
)
is also necessary for
any
learner to succeed,
and observed that
any actual metric space encountered in practice will be ES;
in fact, the existence of non-ES metric spaces is widely believed
to be independent of ZFC.
For countable
{\cal Y}
,
a variant of Theorem
1

— namely, Theorem
10
—
holds for
any

bounded
loss function
\ell:{\cal Y}\times{\cal Y}\to[0,L]
;
no metric structure is necessary.
Finally, a word about the computational efficiency
of
{\mathsf{MedNet}}
. The latter, conceptually, consists of two stages:
(I) computing a
\gamma
-net on the finite
training
sample (residing in
{\cal X}
)
and (II) for each Voronoi cell
C
induced on the sample by the
\gamma
-net,
finding a
medoid

y\in{\cal Y}
that minimizes
\sum_{c\in C}\ell(c,y)
.
The
\gamma
-net in stage (I)
can
indeed
be
efficiently
constructed

(
Gottlieb et al. 2014
;
Kpotufe and Verma 2017
)
.
At stage (II),

{\mathsf{MedNet}}
truncates
{\cal Y}

adaptively
to some finite
{\cal Y}^{\prime}

over which
the medoid can always be computed in a runtime linear in
|{\cal Y}^{\prime}|
.
When
additional structural information regarding
{\cal Y}

is available, it may be leveraged to obtain more efficient
medoid oracles.

To illustrate our significant departure from previous
techniques, let us provide some simple examples
where those fail to be Bayes-consistent for
various simple label metrics.
Let
{\cal X}=\left\{0\right\}
be the trivial singleton metric space
and
{\cal Y}=\left\{a,b,c,o\right\}
be the label space endowed with
the metric
\ell(a,b)=\ell(b,c)=\ell(c,a)=1
;
\ell(o,a)=\ell(o,b)=\ell(o,c)=1/2
,
and let the distribution
{\bar{\mu}}
be such that

\P_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}(Y=a)=\P_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}(Y=b)=\P_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}(Y=c)=1/3
.
We observe that any
majority-vote

based method, such as
k
-NN,
which takes a vote among the
k
nearest neighbors

(
Györfi et al. 2002
)
,
or

OptiNet
,
which takes a vote within each Voronoi cell

(
Hanneke et al. 2021
)
,
or the memory-based techniques of

Blanchard and Cosson 2021
;
Blanchard 2021
;
Blanchard et al. 2021
,
or the hybrid approach of
Györfi and Weiss 2021

cannot achieve Bayes-consistency in this case
—
for the simple reason that they can only output
the
observed
labels
a,b,c

(and hence, at best, achieve an asymptotic risk of
2/3
), while
the Bayes-optimal predictor

f^{*}\equiv o

achieves
R(f^{*})=1/2
.

The necessity of predicting labels that
never occurred in the sample required overcoming
a subtle challenge not present in

Hanneke et al. 2021
. As in that work, we obtain finite-sample generalization bounds via a sample compression scheme.
The latter selects a sub-sample

S_{I}=(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in I\subset[n]}
, based on which
the predictor will be constructed.
To mitigate the noise, we occasionally wish
to relabel a point
X_{i}\in S_{I}
with a label other than
Y_{i}
. One could do this using

b
bits of side information, but

Hanneke et al. 2021
sidestepped this issue by doubling the compression set size: the first
k
pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})
indicate which
X
’s to use (their
Y
’s are discarded) and the second
k

pairs indicate how to label those first
k

points
X_{i}
. This stratagem is not applicable
when we wish to relabel an
X
with a
Y\in{\cal Y}

not occurring in the sample.
Instead,
{\mathsf{MedNet}}
adaptively truncates
{\cal Y}

to a finite
{\cal Y}_{n}
, whose elements can be described in
b(n)=\log_{2}|{\cal Y}_{n}|
bits of side
information.
This solves two problems simultaneously: the concentration inequalities
we invoke require a bounded range, and our compression schemes require bounded side information.
We introduce a semi-stable
variant of the

stable

compression scheme

(
Bousquet et al. 2020
;
Hanneke and Kontorovich 2021
)

to analyze the behavior of our truncated medoid.

Finally, we recall
the family of regression techniques based on
Lipchitz-extension
and observe that it is not
suitable for learning general
metric-to-metric mappings.
A binary classifier
based on the McShane-Whitney
extension theorem
was shown to be Bayes-consistent

(
Kontorovich and Weiss 2014
)
;
this technique was also applied
by

Gottlieb et al. 2017
;
Ashlagi et al. 2021

to real-valued regression.
When
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Y}

are both Hilbert spaces,
the Kirszbraun extension
theorem
likewise provides a basis
for a regression algorithm

(
Biess et al. 2019
)
.
(While
the latter
three
works do not prove
Bayes-consistency,
the finite-sample generalization
bounds provided therein
are likely straightforwardly
adaptable to such a result
via an appropriate regularization schedule.)
Unfortunately, Lipschitz extension
is limited to
a small number of
metric spaces with a special structure;
besides the aforementioned cases,

Naor and Sheffield 2012

established one
for
{\cal X}

a
locally compact length space
and
{\cal Y}

a metric tree,
remarking that “It is rare for a pair of metric spaces […] to have the isometric extension property.”
As a concrete example, the spaces
({\cal X},\rho)=(\mathbb{R}^{3},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})

and

({\cal Y},\ell)=(\mathbb{R}^{2},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2})

fail to have this property

(
Naor 2015
, Counterexample 2.4)
.

Open problem.

The bounded in expectation (BIE)
condition on
{\cal Y}
is a natural
generalization of the real-valued
variant that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}|Y|<\infty

(or, more generally,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}|Y|^{p}<\infty

if
L_{p}
risk is being considered).
These conditions, while sufficient
for Bayes consistency, are clearly not always necessary.
Consider, for example,

{\cal X}={\cal Y}=\mathbb{R}
,
endowed with the standard metric,
where the distribution

{\bar{\mu}}

is such that
the
{\cal X}
-marginal is
Cauchy (i.e., has
density
f(x)=[\pi(1+x^{2})]^{-1}
) and
X=Y
almost surely.
In this case,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}|Y|=\infty

and the more general
BIE condition also fails.
Yet the identity predictor
h(x)=x
achieves the Bayes-optimal risk of
0
,
and various simple learning algorithms, including linear regression, achieve
Bayes consistency (we conjecture that

{\mathsf{MedNet}}
does as well).
Problem: formulate a necessary and sufficient condition
on the metric spaces

({\cal X},\rho)
and
({\cal Y},\ell)
,
and the joint distribution

{\bar{\mu}}
such that
{\mathsf{MedNet}}
(or some other learning algorithm)
is strongly Bayes-consistent.
A natural and optimistic candidate is the condition

R^{*}<\infty
.

Semi-stable compression

In this section, we expand the definition of
stable compression

and present our results for this notion.
First, we split the compression function
\kappa
into its two components.
For
S\in({\cal X}\times{\cal Y})^{+}
,
we write

\kappa(S)=(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S),\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S))\in({\cal X}\times{\cal Y})^{\leq k}\times\left\{0,1\right\}^{b}
;
these are the
compression set
and the
side information
.
We say that
(\kappa,\psi)
is
semi-stable
if the
\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}
component
is stable in the sense of
Bousquet et al. 2020
:
whenever
\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S)\subseteq S^{\prime}\subseteq S
, we have that

\displaystyle\psi(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S^{\prime}),\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S))=\psi(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S),\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S))=\psi(\kappa(S)).

We denote by

Proof.

Recall that

i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in[n]
there exists
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
such that
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subseteq I
. In particular,
Bousquet et al. 2020
construct a family
\mathcal{I}_{n}
satisfying the properties above with
T_{n}=k\lfloor\frac{n}{2k}\rfloor
, and with
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|=\binom{2k}{k}<4^{k}
: namely, let
D_{1},\dots,D_{2k}
be any partition of
[n]
with each
|D_{i}|\in\{\lfloor\frac{n}{2k}\rfloor,\lceil\frac{n}{2k}\rceil)\}
, and define
\mathcal{I}_{n}=\left\{\bigcup\{D_{j}:j\in\mathcal{J}\}:\mathcal{J}\subseteq[2k],|\mathcal{J}|=k\right\}
; that is,
\mathcal{I}_{n}
contains all unions of exactly
k
of the
2k
sets
D_{j}
.

Let there be a sample
S_{n}\sim{\bar{\mu}}^{n}
. Recall our notation that for any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
we have
S(I)\subseteq S_{n}
.
For any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, define
\bar{I}:=[n]\setminus I
. Let
\sigma:[n]\to[n]
be a uniformly random permutation of
[n]
, and for any
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})\subseteq[n]
define
\bm{\sigma}(I):=(\sigma(i_{1}),\dots,\sigma(i_{\ell}))
.

Next, for any
I\subseteq[n]
and any
\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{b}
, let
\hat{h}_{I,\bm{b}}:=\psi\left(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}\left(S\left(I\right)\right),\bm{b}\right)
. Now, since
S(\bar{I})
is independent of
S(I)
, Hoeffding’s Inequality (applied under the conditional distribution given
S(I)
) and the law of total probability imply that with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}
:

\displaystyle\left|R\left(\hat{h}_{I,\bm{b}}\right)-\widehat{R}\left(\hat{h}_{I,\bm{b}};S\left(\bar{I}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{2(n-|I|)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\left(\ln(|\mathcal{I}_{n}|)+b\ln(2)+\ln(4/\delta)\right)}{2(n-|I|)}}.

Applying this under the conditional distribution given
\sigma
, together with the union bound and the law of total probability, we have that with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, every
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
and every
\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{b}
has

\displaystyle\left|R\left(\hat{h}_{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right),\bm{b}}\right)-\widehat{R}\left(\hat{h}_{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right),\bm{b}};S\left(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right)}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\left(\ln(|\mathcal{I}_{n}|)+b\ln(2)+\ln(4/\delta)\right)}{2(n-|I|)}}.

In particular, let
\bm{i}^{*}
be the indices such that

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S_{n})=\left\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\in S_{n}:i\in\bm{i}^{*}\right\}=S_{n}(\bm{i}^{*}).

Now, by property (ii) of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
there must exist
I^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{i}^{*})\subseteq I^{*},

which means:

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\bm{i}^{*}\subseteq\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S_{n})=S_{n}(\bm{i}^{*})\subseteq S_{n}(\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})).

Due to the semi-stability property of
(\kappa,\psi)
and since
S_{n}(\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*}))\subseteq S_{n}
, this implies

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\psi(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S_{n}(\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*}))),\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S_{n}))=\psi(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S_{n}),\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S_{n}))=\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right)).

Thus, on the above event of probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
we get,
for
I=I^{*}
and
\bm{b}=\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S_{n})
,

\displaystyle\left|R(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right)))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})}))\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\left(\ln(|\mathcal{I}_{n}|)+b\ln(2)+\ln(4/\delta)\right)}{2(n-|I^{*}|)}}.

Furthermore, by property (i) of
I_{n}
we have that
n-|I^{*}|\geq T_{n}
, and so

\displaystyle\left|R(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right)))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})}))\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\left(\ln(|\mathcal{I}_{n}|)+b\ln(2)+\ln(4/\delta)\right)}{2T_{n}}}.

(C.1)

Next, we want to relate
\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})}))
to
\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S_{n})
. Let
\hat{h}:=\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right))
. For each
i\in[n]
, let
\ell_{i}:=\ell(\hat{h}(X_{i}),Y_{i})
. For any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, by Hoeffding’s inequality without replacement
(
Bardenet and Maillard 2015
)
applied under the conditional distribution given
S_{n}
, together with the law of total probability, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}
it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n-|I|}\sum_{i\in\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I)}}\ell_{i}-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S_{n})\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{2(n-|I|)}}.

By the union bound, this holds simultaneously for all
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
. In particular, taking
I=I^{*}
, and recalling that
n-|I^{*}|\geq T_{n}
, on this event we have that

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})}))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S_{n})\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{2T_{n}}}

(C.2)

By the union bound, the above two events (each of probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
) hold simultaneously with probability at least
1-\delta
, in which case (
C.1
) and (
C.2
) together imply

\displaystyle\left|R(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right)))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|R(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right)))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})}))\right|+

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})}))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\left(\ln(|\mathcal{I}_{n}|)+b\ln(2)+\ln(4/\delta)\right)}{2T_{n}}}+\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{2T_{n}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{4L^{2}\left(\ln(|\mathcal{I}_{n}|)+\ln(4/\delta)\right)}{2T_{n}}}+\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}b\ln(2)}{2T_{n}}}

The theorem now immediately follows from plugging the aforementioned family
\mathcal{I}_{n}
from
Bousquet et al. 2020
, having
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|=\binom{2k}{k}<4^{k}
and
T_{n}=k\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2k}\right\rfloor>\frac{n-2k}{2}
.
∎

C.3.2
Accounting for realizable case

Lemma 14
.

Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
be i.i.d random variables with values in
[0,L]
for some
L>0
, and let
\delta>0
. Define
{\bar{Z}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
. Then with probability at least
1-\delta
we have:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[{\bar{Z}}]-{\bar{Z}}\leq{\bar{Z}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(4/\delta)}{n-1}}+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(4/\delta)}{n-1}}+\frac{7L\ln(4/\delta)}{3(n-1)}.

Proof.

Let
{Z^{\prime}}_{i}:=\frac{Z_{i}}{L}\in[0,1]
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Using the empirical Bernstein inequality stated in
Maurer and Pontil 2009
, we get

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[{\bar{Z^{\prime}}}]-{\bar{Z^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bm{{Z^{\prime}}})\ln(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{7\ln(4/\delta)}{3(n-1)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[{\bar{Z}}]-{\bar{Z}}

\displaystyle\leq L\sqrt{\frac{2\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bm{Z})\ln(4/\delta)}{L^{2}n}}+\frac{7L\ln(4/\delta)}{3(n-1)}

(C.3)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bm{Z})\ln(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{7L\ln(4/\delta)}{3(n-1)},

where
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bm{Z})
is defined to be:

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bm{Z}):=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(Z_{i}-Z_{j})^{2}.

Observe that

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bm{Z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(Z_{i}-Z_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\cdot n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-{\bar{Z}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-{\bar{Z}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{2}-2{\bar{Z}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}+n{\bar{Z}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{2}-2n{\bar{Z}}^{2}+n{\bar{Z}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{2}-n{\bar{Z}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n-1}\left(nL^{2}-n{\bar{Z}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-1}\left(L^{2}-{\bar{Z}}^{2}\right).

Now plugging this back into (
C.3
):

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[{\bar{Z}}]-{\bar{Z}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(L^{2}-{\bar{Z}}^{2})\ln(4/\delta)}{n-1}}+\frac{7L\ln(4/\delta)}{3(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(L^{2}+{\bar{Z}}^{2})\ln(4/\delta)}{n-1}}+\frac{7L\ln(4/\delta)}{3(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq{\bar{Z}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(4/\delta)}{n-1}}+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(4/\delta)}{n-1}}+\frac{7L\ln(4/\delta)}{3(n-1)},

where the last inequality used
\sqrt{x+y}\leq\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}
for any
x,y>0
.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
S_{n}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim({\cal X}\times{\cal Y})^{n}
be a
given
sample. Let
\hat{h}:{\cal X}\to{\cal Y}
be a predictor, and
\ell:{\cal Y}\times{\cal Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a bounded loss function by some
L>0
. Let
I\subseteq[n]
be a random variable
sampled without replacement
from
[n]
. Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with confidence at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}\ell\left(\hat{h}(X_{i}),Y_{i}\right)-\widehat{R}(\hat{h};S_{n})\right|\leq\widehat{R}(\hat{h};S_{n})\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{|I|}}+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{|I|}}+\frac{2L\ln(2/\delta)}{3|I|}.

Proof.

Let
\ell_{i}:=\ell(\hat{h}(X_{i}),Y_{i})

for
i\in[n]
.
Treating
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}
as a given finite population, and
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i\in I}
as a random sample drawn without replacement from it, we can use
a version of Bernstein’s inequality
(
Bardenet and Maillard 2015
)
,

\displaystyle\P\left(\left|\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}\ell_{i}-\mu\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{|I|\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+\frac{2L}{3}\varepsilon}\right)\qquad\varepsilon>0,

where we have defined:

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}=\widehat{R}(\hat{h};S_{n})

(population mean)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\ell_{i}-\mu)^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(population variance)}.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, we get that with confidence at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}\ell_{i}-\mu\right|\leq\frac{2L}{3|I|}\ln(2/\delta)+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}\ln(2/\delta)}{|I|}}.

Now similarly to the analysis in the proof of Lemma
14
, we see that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\leq L^{2}+\widehat{R}^{2}(\hat{h};S_{n})

using that and that
\sqrt{x+y}\leq\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}
for any
x,y>0
we get the statement of the lemma.
∎

Theorem 16
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
b\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, let
(\kappa,\psi)
be any semi-stable compression scheme of size at most
k
using at most
b
bits of side-information. For any distribution
{\bar{\mu}}
over
{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
, any
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n>4k+4
, and any
\delta\in(0,1)
, for
S_{n}\sim{\bar{\mu}}^{n}
, with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle R(\psi(\kappa(S_{n})))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})\left(5\sqrt{\frac{8\left(\ln(\frac{4}{\delta})+k\ln 4\right)}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{8b\ln 2}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2L\sqrt{\frac{8\left(\ln(\frac{4}{\delta})+k\ln 4\right)}{n}}+\frac{(28+8L)\left(\ln(\frac{4}{\delta})+k\ln 4\right)}{3n}

\displaystyle\quad+L\sqrt{\frac{8b\ln 2}{n}}+\frac{28b\ln 2}{3n}.

Proof.

Similarly to
Hanneke and Kontorovich 2021
, this proof follows similar arguments as Theorem
13
, except using Lemma
14
in place of Hoeffding’s inequality in both places in the proof where such inequalities are used.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and
T_{n}
be as in the proof of Theorem
13
, and let
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
for and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let there be a sample
S_{n}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim{\bar{\mu}}^{n}
. As in Theorem
13
, for any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
we have
S(I)=\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):i\in I\}\subseteq S_{n}
, for any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, define
\bar{I}:=[n]\setminus I
. Let
\sigma:[n]\to[n]
be a uniform random permutation of
[n]
, and for any
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})\subseteq[n]
define
\bm{\sigma}(I):=(\sigma(i_{1}),\dots,\sigma(i_{\ell}))
.

For any
I\subseteq[n]
and any
\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{b}
, let
\hat{h}_{I,\bm{b}}:=\psi\left(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}\left(S\left(I\right)\right),\bm{b}\right)
. Now, since
S(\bar{I})
is independent of
S(I)
, Lemma
14
(applied under the conditional distribution given
S(I)
) and the law of total probability imply that with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}
:

\displaystyle R\left(\hat{h}_{I,\bm{b}}\right)-\widehat{R}\left(\hat{h}_{I,\bm{b}};S\left(\bar{I}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{R}\left(\hat{h}_{I,\bm{b}};S\left(\bar{I}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{n-|I|-1}}

\displaystyle\quad+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{n-|I|-1}}+\frac{7\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{3(n-|I|-1)}.

Applying this under the conditional distribution given
\sigma
, together with the union bound and the law of total probability, we have that with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, every
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
and every
\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{b}
has

\displaystyle R\left(\hat{h}_{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right),\bm{b}}\right)-\widehat{R}\left(\hat{h}_{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right),\bm{b}};S\left(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{R}\left(\hat{h}_{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right),\bm{b}};S\left(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right)}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{n-|I|-1}}

\displaystyle\quad+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{n-|I|-1}}+\frac{7\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{3(n-|I|-1)}.

In particular, let
\bm{i}^{*}
be the indices such that

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S_{n})=\left\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\in S_{n}:i\in\bm{i}^{*}\right\}=S_{n}(\bm{i}^{*})

Now, by property (ii) of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
there must exist
I^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{i}^{*})\subseteq I^{*},

which means:

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\bm{i}^{*}\subseteq\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S_{n})=S_{n}(\bm{i}^{*})\subseteq S_{n}(\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*})).

Due to the semi-stability property of
(\kappa,\psi)
and since
S_{n}(\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*}))\subseteq S_{n}
, this implies

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\psi(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S_{n}(\bm{\sigma}^{-1}(I^{*}))),\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S_{n}))=\psi(\kappa_{\mathsf{cs}}(S_{n}),\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S_{n}))=\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right)).

Thus, on the above event of probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
we can get for
I=I^{*}
and
\bm{b}=\kappa_{\mathsf{si}}(S_{n})
,

\displaystyle R\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right))\right)-\widehat{R}\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S\left(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I^{*}\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{R}\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S\left(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I^{*}\right)}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{n-|I^{*}|-1}}

\displaystyle\quad+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{n-|I^{*}|-1}}+\frac{7\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{3(n-|I^{*}|-1)}.

Furthermore, by property (i) of
I_{n}
we have that
n-|I^{*}|\geq T_{n}
, and that
\widehat{R}\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S\left(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I^{*}\right)}\right)\right)\leq\frac{n}{T_{n}}\widehat{R}\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S_{n}\right)
, so

\displaystyle R\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right))\right)-\widehat{R}\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S\left(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I^{*}\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{T_{n}}\widehat{R}\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S_{n}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{T_{n}-1}}

(C.4)

\displaystyle\quad+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{T_{n}-1}}+\frac{7\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\cdot 2^{b}}{\delta})}{3(T_{n}-1)}.

Now, given
S_{n}
, we define
\ell_{i}:=\ell(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}))(X_{i}),Y_{i})

for
i\in[n]
. Now for any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, under the conditional distribution given
S_{n}
we apply Lemma
15
and see that with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n-|I|}\sum_{i\in\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I\right)}}\ell_{i}-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{n-|I|}}

\displaystyle\quad+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{n-|I|}}+\frac{2L\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{3(n-|I|)}.

By the union bound, this holds simultaneously for all
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
. In particular, taking
I=I^{*}
, and recalling that
n-|I^{*}|\geq T_{n}
, on this event we have that

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I^{*}\right)}))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{T_{n}}}

(C.5)

\displaystyle\quad+L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{T_{n}}}+\frac{2L\ln(4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|/\delta)}{3T_{n}}.

By the union bound, the two events represented by (
C.4
) and (
C.5
) hold simultaneously with probability at least
1-\delta
, in which case together we get:

\displaystyle R(\psi(\kappa(S_{n})))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})

\displaystyle\leq R\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right))\right)-\widehat{R}\left(\psi(\kappa\left(S_{n}\right));S\left(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I^{*}\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S(\overline{\bm{\sigma}^{-1}\left(I^{*}\right)}))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})\left(\left(1+\frac{n}{T_{n}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}{\delta})}{T_{n}-1}}+\frac{n}{T_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{2b\ln 2}{T_{n}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2L\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}{\delta})}{T_{n}-1}}+\frac{(7+2L)\ln(\frac{4|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}{\delta})}{3(T_{n}-1)}+L\sqrt{\frac{2b\ln 2}{T_{n}-1}}+\frac{7b\ln 2}{3(T_{n}-1)}.

The theorem now follows from plugging the aforementioned family
\mathcal{I}_{n}
from
Bousquet et al. 2020
, with
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|=\binom{2k}{k}<4^{k}
and
T_{n}=k\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2k}\right\rfloor>\frac{n-2k}{2}

—
with the modification thatsince
n>4k+4
we have
\frac{n-2k}{2}-1>\frac{n}{4}
, meaning
T_{n}>T_{n}-1>\frac{n}{4}
:

\displaystyle R(\psi(\kappa(S_{n})))-\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{R}(\psi(\kappa(S_{n}));S_{n})\left(5\sqrt{\frac{8\left(\ln(\frac{4}{\delta})+k\ln 4\right)}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{8b\ln 2}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2L\sqrt{\frac{8\left(\ln(\frac{4}{\delta})+k\ln 4\right)}{n}}+\frac{(28+8L)\left(\ln(\frac{4}{\delta})+k\ln 4\right)}{3n}

\displaystyle\quad+L\sqrt{\frac{8b\ln 2}{n}}+\frac{28b\ln 2}{3n}.

∎

C.3.3
Semi-stable compression scheme of bounded sample-dependent size

Theorem 17

Appendix D
Diameter-truncating BIE
{\cal Y}

Recall the bounded-in-expectation
(BIE)
condition:

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}\ell(y_{0},Y)<\infty

for some
y_{0}\in{\cal Y}
.
Under BIE, the trivial
f_{0}(x)\equiv y_{0}

achieves
R(f_{0})<\infty
,
and a fortiori,
R^{*}=\inf_{f}R(f)<\infty

Lemma 18
.

If there exists some

y_{0}\in{\cal Y}
for which

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}\ell(y_{0},Y)<\infty
,
then this holds for all
y\in{\cal Y}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}\ell(y_{0},Y)<\infty

for some

y_{0}\in{\cal Y}
. Then,
by the triangle inequality,
for any other
y^{\prime}\in{\cal Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}\ell(y^{\prime},Y)\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}[\ell(y^{\prime},y_{0})+\ell(y_{0},Y)]=\ell(y^{\prime},y_{0})+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim{\bar{\mu}}}\ell(y_{0},Y)<\infty.

∎

Thus, the choice of
y_{0}\in{\cal Y}

is immaterial; let us fix one such
element once and for all.
For any sequence

L_{n}\uparrow\infty
,
let
\llbracket{\cal Y}\rrbracket_{n}:=B(y_{0},L_{n})

denote the “
L_{n}
-truncated”
space.

We observe that

\displaystyle f^{*}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{y^{\prime}\in{\cal Y}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(y^{\prime},Y)\,|\,X=x]

(where ties in
{\cal Y}
are broken lexicographically) achieves

R(f^{*})=R^{*}
,
since it is a pointwise minimizer of the non-negative risk integrand.
Let us
also
define a truncated version:

\displaystyle f_{n}^{*}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\hat{y}\in\llbracket{\cal Y}\rrbracket_{n}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(\hat{y},Y)\,|\,X=x].

Since
y_{0}\in\llbracket{\cal Y}\rrbracket_{n}
,
we have that

\displaystyle g_{n}(x):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(f_{n}^{*}(x),Y|X=x)]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(y_{0},Y|X=x)]=:h(x).

While one or both of

g_{n},h

may be infinite

x
,
BIE implies that
h

is integrable.
Next, we claim that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}(x)=g(x):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(f^{*}(x),Y|X=x)],\qquad x\in{\cal X}.

Indeed, this follows from a stronger

such that

for all

and all

this is immediate by construction

.
Applying Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem
to the sequence

yields

Theorem 19
.

If
{\cal Y}
is BIE,
f^{*}

is a Bayes-optimal predictor and
