On the Diffusion Time Evolution of Folding Chains in the Heteropolymer Model
Okezue Bell

Abstract
In this paper, we mathematically describe the time evolution of protein folding features via Iori et al.’s heteropolymer model. More specifically, we identify that the folding amino acid chain evolve according to a power law D ∼ t ν D\sim t^{\nu} . The power ν \nu decreases from 0 . 66 ¯ 0.\overline{66} to 0.5 0.5 when the randomness of the coupling constants in the Lennard-Jones potential increases.



1
Introduction

Protein folding is widely considered the milestone problem of biology gives its high disorder and computational complexity
[
2
]
. While there have been efforts to predict the shape of a protein given its amino acid sequence (e.g. Deepmind’s AlphaFold
[
4
]
and Alphafold 2
[
8
]
), there have been few studies that assess disordered amino acid linkages. Such chains often determine biological errors within protein folding mechanisms, such as misfolding into the incorrect minimum energy configuration
[
3
]
. In Shakhnovic and Gutin’s seminal work, a quantitative form, Replica Symmetry Breaking (RSB), is proposed to model amino acid disorder in protein folding
[
10
]
. The RSB treatment is later systematically applied to random manifolds in Mezard et al. In the heteropolymer model, RSB occurs when replicas collapse in groups onto particular polymer conformations. Groups sizes are balanced by the energies and entropies of the replicas’ interactions. In Iori et al., a heteropolymer of a ‘basic’ flavor is studied to differentiate between the static structures of spin glasses and native proteins
[
7
]
. This model highlighted the presence of dominant ground state features. In the two canon studies of the model, Fukugita et al. and Marinari et al.
[
6
,
9
]
, the
d=2
and Lennard-Jones homopolymer cases are studied, respectively.

The Hamiltonian
H
of the simple homopolymer is of the form

H=\sum_{i,j,I\neq j}\left[hd^{2}_{i,j}\delta_{i,j+1}-\frac{A}{d^{6}_{i,j}}+\frac{R}{2d^{1}_{i,j}}+\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\eta_{i,j}}{d^{6}_{i,j}}\right],

(1)

where
i,j
represent the number of sites on the polymeric chain, and therefore range between
1
and
N
inclusive, and
d^{2}_{i,j}\equiv\left(r_{i}-r_{j}\right)^{2}
. The first term
hd^{2}_{i,j}\delta_{i,j+1}
represents the harmonic force holding adjacent amino acids together. The attractive and repulsive —
A
and
R
—contributions’ proportionalities form a standard Lennard-Jones potential. The final square root term is the contribution of the randomness of the coupling constants in the potential; it represents nonuniform dipoles, where the values of
eta_{i,j}
are normalized, non-time-variant, independent, uncorrelated, and stochastic variables, with each set of coefficients characterizing a single native protein.

The most notable questions concerning these heteropolymeric chains lie within basic empirical behavior. More specifically, we observe quantities averaged on thermal noise and
\eta_{i,j}
couplings, from which we extrapolate short-time behaviors from Hamiltonian-define heteropolymeric chain dynamics. We study a diffusion
D_{2}
, the distance between two chain configurations, as derived from the Euclidean distance:

D_{2}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)\equiv\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\left(d_{i,j}^{(\alpha)}-d_{i,j}^{(\beta)}\right)^{2},

(2)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are chain configuration states at varying time points. The time evolution of
D_{2}
between the two states is indicative of shape and size diffusion of the protein folding structure. We base our study on the aforementioned noise strength
\epsilon
, which we suspect informs diffusion. We omit translational and rotational degrees of freedom in our definition of mean displacement by diffusion at equilibrium. Therefore, mean square displacement is maximized by a large time
t
, which implies both the amplitude of thermal fluctuations and spatial extent dependence.

In normal mode decomposition in Langevin dynamics modeling local polymeric diffusion cases, a power law for mean-square dependence,
t^{\frac{1}{2}}
is yielded. The Langevin model’s power law extends to collective size-affected and finite size invisible time ranges
[
12
]
. Such short-range interaction regimes are also applied to a surface aggregation context
[
1
]
, though they hold significance in studying long-range heteropolymeric chain interactions. Our case studies heteropolymers with long-range interaction on the chain, and a Lennard-Jones potential with a random frozen attractive contribution with non-local, disordered Hamiltonians. We bound the
i,j
range to
N=20
, as we aim to obtain results from finitely many sites within a chain. Given that there are all
20
amino acids within all basic proteins, it is likely that our results will be deterministic and broadly applicable to the secondary structure in protein folding.

In the nonlinear model with a local interaction in
(1+1)
dimensions, the mean squared displacement yields a power rule
t^{\frac{2}{3}}
. Therefore, we expect that externally-biased nonlinear systems will yield some crossover between the
\frac{1}{2}
and
\frac{2}{3}
power laws. We treat the apparent non-locality of the local nonlinear heteropolymer model describing nearest-neighbor interaction along the chain as a bias.

Nuts and Bolts

. In Subsection

and end-to-end chain length

1.72\pm 0.089

The values to explore the

We discover that in both the disordered and frozen heteropolymer regimes, the attractive contribution in the Lennard-Jones energy expectation value is approximately half the repulsive contribution’s energy when divided by
\epsilon

\frac{A}{2d^{6}}=\frac{R}{d^{12}}

The kissing problem asks to find the maximal number of equal size nonoverlapping spheres in three dimensions that can touch another sphere of the same size, known as the kissing number. It’s solution is to compute the maximum number of spheres of diameter
d
(in this case,
d
is not distance) tangent to a given sphere. For
\epsilon=0
in
\lim_{N\to\infty}
, chain site configuration lies on a face-centered cubic lattice. This lattice can be placed within the context of the maximization problem; it is made of the centroids of maximally-dense spheres with diameter
d
, where
d=4
. Therefore, within this set of cases, the harmonic energy is the determinant of the chain site ordering within the lattice. Fukugita et al. sufficiently demonstrate that within the
d=2
case, the lattice structure is maintained in
\epsilon=0
and holds in the disordered phase transition. In our
d=4
case, we find that the lattice structure is begins to fade at
N=20
and is lost at
N=30
. In
\epsilon=0
, the lattice structure again weakens, but survives when the chain in the strongly disordered potential is thermalized.

2.2
Time Dependence of the Equilibrium Shape

We introduce an additional definition of pairwise distance between configurations, called
D_{4}
which is synonymous to
D_{2}
:

D_{4}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)\equiv\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\left(d_{i,j}^{(\alpha)}-d_{i,j}^{(\beta)}\right)^{2},

(3)

which is derived from the difference of squares equation

\left(d^{2(\alpha)}_{i,j}-d^{2(\beta)}_{i,j}\right)=\left(d^{\alpha}_{i,j}-d^{\beta}_{i,j}\right)\left(d^{\alpha}_{i,j}+d^{\beta}_{i,j}\right),

(4)

where the first factor is much smaller than the second, thought the exponent that defines the power law short-time dependence of
D_{2}
and
D_{4}
remains the same. We then analyzed the ensemble average of
D_{4}
as a function of time separation between configurations to obtain diffusion diagnostics. As per our standard procedure, we average over time dynamics and each iteration of
\eta_{i,j}
couplings. It follows that the average is computed as
D_{4}(t)\equiv D_{4}\left(\alpha(t),\alpha(0)\right)
, where
\alpha(0)
is an equilibrium configuration and the fluctuations of the critical exponential governance of
D_{4}(t)
on a sample to sample are marginal, so
D_{4}
is average across hundreds of epochs. Each of our runs have different starting points and use standard Monte Carlo dynamics
[
11
]
.

We verify our determined universal behavior via validation from a simple harmonic chain. This chain presents a non-universal, short-time region where the discreteness of the Metropolis procedure is relevant and an asymptotic constant value for
D_{2}
with some universal approach to
D_{4}
in between the short-time region of interest. Within this region, scaling must be detected to validate our existing behavior conclusions, which can only be observed in
\lim_{N\to\infty}
. We check our results from the simple harmonic chain against the normal mode expansion of the Langevin, whose differential is of the form

\zeta\frac{dr_{i}}{dt}=-k\left(2r_{i}-r_{i+1}-r_{i-1}\right)+f_{i},

(5)

where
f
represents uncorrelated random forces satisfying
f_{i}(t_{1})f_{j}(t_{2})=2\zeta k_{B}T\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\delta_{i,j}
.
The friction constant
\zeta
defines the time scale, and the continuum limit
(2r_{i}-r_{i+1}+r_{i-1})
can be redefined as a second partial differential
\partial^{2}r\backslash\partial n^{2}
. The standard normal mode is defined as

x_{p}=\frac{1}{N}\int\limits^{N}_{0}r(n)\cos\left(\frac{p\pi n}{N}\right)dn.

(6)

Using Wick’s Theorem
[
5
]
, we evaluate the products of the coordinates
\langle x_{k}x_{l}x_{m}x_{n}\rangle
to obtain
D_{4}(t)

D_{4}(t)=\sum_{i,j}\left[\kappa_{i,j}(0)-\kappa_{i,j}(t)\right],

(7)

where

\kappa_{i,j}(t)=c\left[\sum_{p}e^{-p^{2}t\backslash\tau_{r}}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\left(\cos\left(\frac{p\pi i}{N}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{p\pi j}{N}\right)\right)^{2}\right]^{2},

(8)

and

c=64\frac{3k_{B}T}{h\pi^{2}};\hskip 7.22743pt\tau=\frac{N^{2}b^{2}}{kT3\pi^{2}}=\frac{\zeta N^{2}}{h\pi^{2}},\hskip 7.22743pt\tau_{r}\coloneqq\tau_{0}N^{2},\hskip 7.22743pt\tau_{0}\coloneqq\frac{\zeta}{h\pi^{2}}.

(9)

The asymptotic expectation value of
d^{2}_{i,i+1}
is
b^{2}=kT\backslash 3h
. In Figure
1(a)

We encounter an exclusion principle due to the discontinuous dynamics of the Metropolis algorithm: when it is known that the correct asymptotic equilibrium distribution is obtained, there is no direct correspondence with Langevin dynamics for short times. To exemplify this, we consider Metropolis dynamics with a random trial increment defined by a displacement vector
\vec{\delta}
chosen from some probability distribution
P(\vec{\delta})
, and accepted with a probability

P_{a}=\textnormal{min}\left(1,e^{\beta H(\vec{R})-H(\vec{r}+\vec{\delta})}\right).

We therefore define the average of a single step displacement

\langle\vec{r^{\prime}}-r\rangle\sim\left\langle\vec{\delta}\textnormal{min}\left(1,e^{\beta H(\vec{R})-H(\vec{r}+\vec{\delta})}\right)\right\rangle.

A large acceptance factor, however, has a small
\beta\vec{\delta}\cdot\vec{\nabla}H(r)
, and so its displacement is approximated as

-\beta\left\langle\vec{\delta}\Theta(\vec{\delta}\cdot\vec{\nabla}H(\vec{r}))^{2}\right\rangle\approx-\frac{\beta}{6}\delta^{2}\vec{\nabla}H(r),

(10)

where
\Theta
is the step function, identical to the Langevin step, with the exception of the scale factor, which now depends on the acceptance ration of the Metropolis procedure
\delta^{2}\beta
. However, the Metropolis algorithm is optimized for larger trial displacements, so the approximation in Equation
10
does not hold in general conditions. The nature of the Metropolis method also implies that it will only be reproducible in times multiple magnitudes higher than normal time of continuous dynamics needed to reach mean displacement
\delta
. This ambiguous time region is discriminated in our experiment prior to analysis of the dynamical critical exponent. Within this region, we discover that of times within bounds to ignore the non-universal details of the dynamics and not describing the relaxation to the asymptotic mean displacement, there is a critical exponent
\nu
where
D_{4}(T)\simeq t^{\nu}
.

With Langevin dynamics, we can relate
\nu
with the asymptotics of the dynamics linearized around local minima. We assume that asymptotically, in the continuum of large
N
and small

\lambda_{p}=p^{\alpha}
. We also assume that the fluctuations of all modes are the same and not correlated. With the generalizations, the width of the probability distribution of the
p

C\backslash\sqrt{\lambda_{p}}
, where

is a mode-independent constant. We can then more rigorously define
D_{4}(t)
and
\kappa_{i,j}(t)

\begin{split}D_{4}(t)&=\sum_{i,j}[\kappa_{i,j}(0)-\kappa_{i,j}(t)];\hskip 7.22743pt\kappa_{i,j}(t)=c\left[\sum_{p}\exp\left(\frac{-p^{\alpha}t}{\tau_{r}}\right)\frac{1}{p^{\alpha}}(c_{p,i}-c_{p,j})^{2}\right]^{2}\\
&\sim\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}p^{-\alpha}(1-e^{\frac{t}{\tau_{r}}p^{\alpha}})dp\sim t^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}},\end{split}

(11)

\epsilon

\lambda_{p}\sim(p\backslash N)^{\alpha}
that holds for small

3
Conclusion

There are four parameters that determine the Hamiltonian:
A
,
R
,
h
, and
\epsilon
, which inform
D_{4}(t)
behavior. At time scale
\tau_{r}
, we reach an initial point where the dynamical critical exponent power law does not hold, where
D_{4}(t)\simeq kt^{\nu}
,
t\ll\tau_{r}
is a decreasing function of interaction strength, and an increasing function of system size. The kissing number coefficient
k
is inversely proportional to the strength of the
\eta_{i,j}
coupling and proportional to temperature and system size. Based on our findings, we then demonstrate the behavior of a harmonic chain (Figure
1(a)
) and the time behavior of ordered systems for two different values of the parameters (Figure
1(b)
). The globular phase fits well to
\nu=0.\overline{66}
, aligning with our local interaction model predictions. Our exponent remains even when
h
increases with local interaction and
R
and
A
are fixed, suggesting that in the coil phase, the diffusion properties without frozen noise can be adequately described by a model of sites with a local interaction placed in an effective external field.

While investigating the dependence of
\nu
under
\epsilon
and non-dependence on frozen noise iterations. We obtain a qualitative portrait of critical slowing down systems in large-scale simulations. We show that for three different realizations of the noise, a similar power behavior
D_{4}(t)
is observed (Figure
2(b)
). Indeed, the non-universal coefficient of the power behavior depends on the given noise realization, though
\nu
is independent, as the energy of interaction does not determine the equilibrium shape, and consequently, does not determine
\nu
. We also show that for different
\epsilon
values of
D_{4}(t)
, we observe a pattern in increasing
\epsilon
where
\nu
is reduced: for
\epsilon=10
,
\nu=0.61\pm 0.04
(Figure
2(b)
). We also find that in accordance with Iori et al., the correlation time shows a dramatic dependence on
\epsilon
, which we find by plotting the distribution probability
P_{t}(D_{4})
for four values of
\epsilon
with the same time moment
[
7
]
. We obtain
P_{t}(D_{4})
by averaging over several hundred configurations. For
\epsilon=0
, the
P_{t}(D_{4})
at all times
tapprox10\theta
—where
\theta\equiv 10^{4}
updates of each chain site—is nearly stationery, while for
\epsilon
at times
10^{2}
longer approach a frozen state.

Ultimately, we construct a novel model of the behavior and time evolution of biological polymers by deriving a power law for the heteropolymer model. Tempering approaches from Monte Carlo dynamics such as in Marinari et al. may prove useful in large scale heteropolymeric behavior modeling, making our conclusions more generalize.
