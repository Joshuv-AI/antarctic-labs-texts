Quantizing rare random maps: application to flooding visualization
Charlie Sire

Abstract
Visualization is an essential operation when assessing the risk of rare events such as coastal or river floodings. The goal is to display a few prototype events that best represent the probability law of the observed phenomenon, a task known as quantization. It becomes a challenge when data is expensive to generate and critical events are scarce, like extreme natural hazard. In the case of floodings, each event relies on an expensive-to-evaluate hydraulic simulator which takes as inputs offshore meteo-oceanic conditions and dyke breach parameters to compute the water level map. In this article, Lloyd’s algorithm, which classically serves to quantize data, is adapted to the context of rare and costly-to-observe events. Low probability is treated through importance sampling, while Functional Principal Component Analysis combined with a Gaussian process deal with the costly hydraulic simulations. The calculated prototype maps represent the probability distribution of the flooding events in a minimal expected distance sense, and each is associated to a probability mass. The method is first validated using a 2D analytical model and then applied to a real coastal flooding scenario. The two sources of error, the metamodel and the importance sampling, are evaluated to quantify the precision of the method.



Keywords:
Visualization, Quantization, Flood maps, Rare events, Metamodel

Introduction

Political authorities typically support their decisions regarding flood risk management based of flood maps calculated with hydraulic simulations.

Beven
et al. 2015
provide a realistic application, and a review of the existing methods for graphical representations of flooding uncertainty is given by
Seipel and Lim 2017
.

The design and analysis of numerical experiments is a generic procedure to capture and explore the uncertainties related to such heavy numerical models (
Santner
et al. 2003
).
It consists in performing a series of runs of the hydrodynamic flow simulator
where the forcing conditions (the river flow rate and the offshore wave characteristics),
the breach characteristics (location and dimension of the breach) or the embankments characteristics (geometry, failures conditions, etc.) are one realization of a random law.

Rohmer
et al. 2018
provide an example of the setting up of the forcing scenarios in coastal simulations and specify a method to determine the probabilistic laws describing the natural phenomena.

Visualizing simulations for risk assessment is a real challenge because of their time-consuming aspect, the low probability of the flooding event, and the need to communicate the results to authorities who do not necessarily have a scientific background.
To this end, a simple-but-efficient approach consists in providing a small size set of representative flood maps.
Such prototype maps (
Hastie
et al. 2009
) are chosen to best represent the probability law associated to the flooding event.
Calculating the prototype maps is a quantization task (
Gray and
Neuhoff 1998
), implemented here through the Lloyd’s algorithm (
Pagès 2014
).
Lloyd’s algorithm searches for Voronoi cells centroids such that the mean distance (with respect to the uncertainty distribution) between an observation and its nearest centroid is minimized.

This procedure yields results that have both a probabilistic meaning and can be visually interpreted by a wide audience. For instance, in the flooding case tackled in this article, the four non-empty prototype maps and their probability masses expressed as frequencies are given in Figure

Lloyd’s algorithm is not a new quantization technique (
MacQueen 1967
), and has evolved since its introduction (
Bock 2008
).
This paper presents new extensions to quantization that address the following bottlenecks.
First, the quantization is done here in the context of costly numerical simulations, which makes the computation of the centroids more difficult. Indeed, the flood maps are computed with hydraulic codes that can take several hours to simulate the water levels associated to each set of input parameters.
Plus, flooding is a rare event. Classical Monte Carlo sampling is inefficient for extreme events with a very large return period (typically 1000-10000 years).
Finally, the quantization is performed in a space of pixelated maps (
64\times 64
) which raises metamodeling and storage questions when a very large number
(>10^{6})
of realizations has to be handled.

Finding prototype maps

2.1
Quantization

Let us consider a random vector
X\in\mathcal{X}
, with
\mathcal{X}
a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d},d\in\mathbb{N}
.
In our context,
X
is a parametric description of the offshore hydrodynamic (like the tide and surge parameters)
and the dyke breach conditions.
We define a random function
Y
from
\mathcal{X}
the space of offshore conditions and breach parameters to
\mathcal{Y}
the space of possible flood maps, with an inner product
\langle.,.\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}
, and a metric
\lVert.\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}
induced by the inner product:

\displaystyle Y\colon\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto Y(x).

In other terms, with this general formulation,
Y
associates a distribution of flood maps to every vector of input conditions
x
.
In practice,
Y
can be deterministic and thus associate a single flood map to every
x
which we write
y(x)
.

The quantization problem is to find a set
\Gamma_{\ell}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{\ell}\}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\ell}
of
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
representatives of
Y(X)
in a mathematically relevant way.
The notation
Y(X)
should be understood as the map
Y
conditioned by the random vector
X
as in regression,
Y(X)\equiv Y\!\!\mid\!\!X
.
The prototype maps will be these representatives in our study.
More precisely, we first define for
\Gamma_{\ell}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\ell}
the closest candidate representative as the function
q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}
:

\displaystyle q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}\colon\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle\to\Gamma_{\ell}

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\mapsto q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(y)=\underset{\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma_{\ell}}{\argmin}\lVert y-\gamma_{i}\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\penalty\ .

A measure of the quantization error is the average distance between a randomly chosen map and its closest representative
(
Pagès 2014
),

e(\Gamma_{\ell})=\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y(X)-q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(Y(X))\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\penalty\ .

To each centroid
\gamma_{j}
in
\Gamma_{\ell}
, we associate a Voronoi cell
C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}

which is the subset of points in
\mathcal{Y}
closest to
\gamma_{j}
,

C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}=\{y\in\mathcal{Y},q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(y)=\gamma_{j}\}\penalty\ .

The objective is to minimize this quantization error, i.e., to find

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star}=\{\gamma^{\star}_{1},\dots,\gamma^{\star}_{\ell}\}

\displaystyle\in\underset{\Gamma_{\ell}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\ell}}{\argmin}\>(e(\Gamma_{\ell}))

\displaystyle\in\underset{\Gamma_{\ell}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\ell}}{\argmin}\>\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y(X)-q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(Y(X))\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\in\underset{\Gamma_{\ell}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\ell}}{\argmin}\>\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{i\in\{1\dots\ell\}}{\min}\>\lVert Y(X)-\gamma_{i}\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\penalty\ .

Finding the optimal centroids is in general a NP hard problem (
Mahajan
et al. 2012
).
However, the solution has a strong attribute (
Pagès and
Yu 2013
):

Theorem 2.1

(Optimal quantization, Kieffer, Cuesta-Albertos)
.

If

\bullet

\mathcal{Y}
is of finite dimension m ,

\bullet

\forall y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y},\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}y_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}
with
\forall i,\lambda_{i}>0
,

\bullet

\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y(X)\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]<+\infty
,

then
\forall i\in\{1\dots\ell\},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[Y(X)\mid Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star}}_{i}\right]=\gamma^{\star}_{i}
.

This means that the representatives of an optimal quantification coincide with the Voronoi cell centroids.
The proof is given in Appendix
A
.
In our context, the distance induced by the scalar product
\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}
between two maps is the mean squared difference between the water depth at each pixel of the maps, weighted by
\lambda_{i}
which can represent the importance of the zone (for example the housing density, the presence of factories, etc.).

The popular Lloyd’s algorithm, also called K-means, is based on Theorem

.

2.2
Dealing with low probability events

As stated before, Lloyd’s algorithm requires to compute at each iteration and for each Voronoi cell
C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j},j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[Y(X)\mid Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{[k]}}_{j}\right]
. The most natural approach would be to sample
Y(X)
from its density and then to calculate the empirical mean of
Y(X)
given
Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{[k]}}_{j}
.
However, flooding being a rare event, we expect to have one Voronoi cell with almost only empty maps associated to a probability close to 1, and other Voronoi cells with more critical floodings associated to very low probabilities maps.
Thus, the straightforward sampling is not relevant to locate the Voronoi cells of low probabilities because their member maps will almost surely be missed.
Importance sampling is thus needed to perform a sampling less concentrated on a single cluster.

We will need to estimate centroids and probabilities, which reduces to calculating
\mathbb{E}\left[h(Y(X))\right]
with
h\colon\mathcal{Y}\mapsto\mathcal{H}
for some set
\mathcal{H}
.
To compute the probability that
Y
is in
A
,
P(Y(X)\in A)
,
h
is the indicator function
h\colon y\mapsto\mathds{1}_{y\in A}
with
A\subset\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{H}=\{0,1\}
.
Alternatively
h
is
h\colon y\mapsto y\mathds{1}_{y\in A}
with
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{Y}
to calculate the mean of
Y
when it belongs to
A
.

The importance sampling technique is based on the fact that the representation
of

\mathbb{E}\left[h(Y(X))\right]
as an expectation is not unique (
L’Ecuyer
et al. 2009
).
Indeed, if we consider
\tilde{X}
a random variable with density function
g
with a support such that
{\mathrm{supp}}(f_{X})\subset{\mathrm{supp}}(g)\subset\mathcal{X}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[h(Y(X))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[h(Y(\tilde{X}))\frac{f_{X}(\tilde{X})}{g(\tilde{X})}\right]\penalty\ .

The importance sampling estimator of the mean comes from this last representation:

\hat{E}_{n}^{IS}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{k=1}h(Y(\tilde{X}^{k}))\frac{f_{X}(\tilde{X}^{k})}{g(\tilde{X}^{k})}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\penalty\ ,

(1)

with
(\tilde{X}^{k})^{n}_{k=1}
i.i.d. random variables of density function
g
.
It is well known that such importance sampling estimator is unbiased and consistent (
Tabandeh
et al. 2022
) .

The covariance matrix of the estimator is

\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{E}_{n}^{IS}(h)\right)=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{V}\left(h(Y(\tilde{X}))\frac{f_{X}(\tilde{X})}{g(\tilde{X})}\right)\penalty\ .

(2)

The idea of the importance sampling method is to choose a relevant density function
g
, i.e., a density function that minimizes the terms of the diagonal of the above covariance matrix.

2.3
Importance sampling combined with quantization

In Lloyd’s algorithm, we need to repeatedly compute the expectations of
Y
within the candidate Voronoi cells. At the
p^{th}
iteration, these expectations are,

\gamma^{[p]}_{j}=\mathbb{E}\left[Y(X)\mid Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{[p-1]}}_{j}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[Y(X)\mathds{1}_{Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{[p-1]}}_{j}}\right]}{\mathbb{P}(Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{[p-1]}}_{j})}

(3)

As for a given
\Gamma_{\ell}
,
\mathbb{P}(Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}}\right]
is not known, we rely on the importance sampling estimators for both the numerator and the denominator and we introduce

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hat{P}_{n}(\Gamma_{\ell},j,Y)&=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{k=1}\mathds{1}_{Y(\tilde{X}^{k})\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}}\frac{f_{X}(\tilde{X}^{k})}{g(\tilde{X}_{k})}\penalty\ ,\\
\hat{E}_{n}(\Gamma_{\ell},j,Y)&=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{k=1}Y(\tilde{X}^{k})\mathds{1}_{Y(\tilde{X}^{k})\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}}\frac{f_{X}(\tilde{X}^{k})}{g(\tilde{X}_{k})}}{\hat{P}_{n}(\Gamma_{\ell},j)}\penalty\ ,\end{array}\right.

(4)

the estimators of
\mathbb{P}(Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j})
and
\mathbb{E}\left[Y(X)\mid Y(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}\right]
respectively.
The choice of the distribution
g
is delicate and will be further discussed in Section
6
.

The overall approach was described so far in the very general context of a random field
Y
. In the following, these estimators will be computed with
y
the deterministic output of the hydraulic simulators, and
\hat{y}
its predictor.

3
Dealing with time-consuming experiments

In order to have an estimator of
\mathbb{E}\left[{y}(X)\mid{y}(X)\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}\right]
of sufficiently small variance for all cells
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, we need to compute
{y}(\tilde{X}^{k})
for a sample
(\tilde{X}^{k})^{n}_{k=1}
of large size
n
, with
y
the deterministic output of the hydraulic simulator.
Realistic physical simulations would be too costly for that purpose,
and a metamodel is required to approximate
{y}(X)
.
Gaussian processes (GP) (
Williams and
Rasmussen 2006
) are used here to replace some of the simulations.
GPs are readily applicable to low dimensional outputs (most often scalars) but not to
{y}(X)
, the flood map, which is in
\mathbb{R}^{4096}
(
64\times 64
).
To overcome that,
Perrin et al. 2021
implemented a method based on functional principal component analysis (FPCA).
Their strategy, which we rely on, is to work with the projections of the maps in a space induced by a basis of
n_{\mathrm{pc}}\ll 4096
maps only.
The predictions are expressed as coordinates of a map in this basis, through a Gaussian process approach.
In the following, we call “metamodel“ the whole procedure: the FPCA followed by a Gaussian process regression.

3.1
Projection on a basis of maps

The objective here is to find a basis of
n_{\mathrm{pc}}
maps, with
n_{\mathrm{pc}}
small enough, and to compute the projection of the maps onto this basis,
thus representing every map
y(x)
as a linear combination of the
n_{\mathrm{pc}}
basis maps:

y^{\mathrm{proj}}(x):=t_{1}(x)y^{\mathrm{pca}}_{1}+t_{2}(x)y^{\mathrm{pca}}_{2}+\dots+t_{n_{\mathrm{pc}}}(x)y^{\mathrm{pca}}_{n_{\mathrm{pc}}}\penalty\ .

For this purpose, a functional principal components analysis (FPCA) is carried out on a training database of maps
({y}(x^{i}))_{i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}}
. The steps are the following, and further details are given in Appendix
B.1
. :

•

Find a decomposition of these maps, seen as realizations of a spatial function, on a functional orthonormal basis
\Phi=(\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{K})^{\top}
:
{y}(x^{i})=\Phi^{\top}\alpha(x^{i})

•

Reduce the number of basis functions by keeping the
\tilde{K}
most important ones,
\tilde{\Phi}
, in terms of their contribution to the energy of the coordinates in the database
(\tilde{\alpha}(x^{i}))_{i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}}
.

•

Perform a PCA on the selected coordinates to finally get the principal components
(t(x^{i}))_{i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}}
with
t(x^{i})=(t_{1}(x^{i}),\dots,t_{n_{\mathrm{pc}}}(x^{i}))^{\top}
and the
n_{\mathrm{pc}}\times\tilde{K}
projection matrix
\Omega
:
\tilde{\alpha}(x^{i})\simeq\Omega^{\top}t(x^{i})
.

The obtained FPCA-basis maps are
\Omega\tilde{\Phi}
.
The two basis maps
y^{\mathrm{pca}}_{1}
and
y^{\mathrm{pca}}_{2}
of the coastal flooding example are shown in Figure

.
Negative values can appear on these FPCA maps, but positive depths are recovered with the linear combinations of the principal components.

3.2
Gaussian process regression

We now aim at predicting the flood map for a new input
x^{\star}
by predicting its coordinates in the FPCA basis
(t_{1}(x^{\star}),\dots,t_{n_{\mathrm{pc}}}(x^{\star}))
.
For simplicity these coordinates are assumed independent, but the study of their dependency may be relevant in some cases (
Gu and Shen 2020
).
Therefore,
n_{\mathrm{pc}}
one-dimensional Gaussian processes define the metamodel for the flood maps.
Let
t\colon\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a deterministic function.

t
is approximated as a realization of a Gaussian process
Z(x)
of prior mean
\mu(x)
and prior covariance kernel
k(x,x^{\prime})
. The kernel contains the spatial dependencies between

y(x)
and
y(x^{\prime})
.
In our study we use the stationary Matérn 5/2 kernel (
Palar et al. 2023
).

We consider a design of experiments
\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}}=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n_{\mathrm{train}}})
and the observations
Z_{\mathrm{train}}=(z_{i})_{i\in I_{\mathrm{train}}}=(t(x^{i}))_{i\in I_{\mathrm{train}}}
with
I_{\mathrm{train}}=\{1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}\}
.
t
can be approximated using the distribution of
Z(x)\mid Z(\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}})=Z_{\mathrm{train}}
, which is a Gaussian process with the following mean (
Williams and
Rasmussen 2006
):

\hat{t}(x)=\mu(x)+k(x,\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}})k(\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}},\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}})^{-1}(Z_{\mathrm{train}}-\mu(\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}}))

where

k(\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}},\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}})=[k(x^{i},x^{j})]_{i,j\in I_{\mathrm{train}}}
and
k(x,\mathbb{X}_{\mathrm{train}})=[k(x,x^{i})]_{i\in I_{\mathrm{train}}}
.
This method is applied to all the FPCA components

i\in\{1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{pc}}\}
, for which the GP mean is a predictor
\hat{t}_{i}(x^{\star})
of
t_{i}(x^{\star})
.
Then,
\hat{y}(x^{\star})
, a predictor of
y(x^{\star})
is obtained by inverse FPCA as explained in Appendix
B.2
.
The metamodel errors are investigated in the following sections.

4.2
Performance metrics

We expect that Lloyd’s algorithm returns
\ell
prototype maps associated to the minimum quantization error, as well as
\ell
probabilities of maps to belong to each of the induced Voronoi cell. The errors will be evaluated both on the centroids and on each cluster probability.
To ensure the robustness of the metrics, we consider
(\Gamma_{\ell}^{r})_{r\in\{1,\dots,n_{\Gamma_{\ell}}\}}
a family of
n_{\Gamma_{\ell}}
different random quantizations chosen as random perturbations of a priori prototypes.
Details about the random quantizations are provided in
Sire et al. 2023b
.
We also introduce
\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star}
the optimal quantization obtained with the real maps coming from the simulators, and
\hat{\Gamma}_{\ell}^{\star}
the optimal quantization obtained with the predicted maps.

Metamodel performance metrics.

The following performance metrics quantify the errors caused by the metamodel (MM) on the quantization and on the cluster probability:

•

The excess in quantization error due to the metamodel,

\epsilon_{\Gamma}^{MM}=\frac{\hat{e}(\hat{\Gamma}_{\ell}^{\star})-\hat{e}(\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star})}{\hat{e}(\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star})}\penalty\ ,

The relative probability error,

Importance sampling performance metrics.

The effect of the importance sampling (IS) on the quantization is quantified, for a given Voronoi cell
C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{r}}_{j}
, through two metrics:

•

The IS coefficient of variation of the membership probability,
\epsilon_{P}^{IS}(\tilde{n},\Gamma_{\ell}^{r},j)

\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{P}_{\tilde{n}}(\Gamma_{\ell}^{r},j,{y})\right)}}{\mathbb{E}\left(\hat{P}_{\tilde{n}}(\Gamma_{\ell}^{r},j,{y})\right)}

It measures an error due to the importance sampling in the estimation of the probability
of the Voronoi cell number
j
. .

•

The IS centroid standard deviation,
\epsilon_{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{IS}(n_{\mathrm{maps}},\Gamma_{\ell}^{r},j)
,
which is the
90\%

\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{E}_{n_{\mathrm{maps}}}(\Gamma_{\ell}^{r},j,{y})\right)
.
It characterizes the error coming from importance sampling in the estimation of the centroid of the Voronoi cell number
j
.
Among other things, it will show potential variations due to the denominator being close to zero in the centroid formula (
3
).

These quantities are calculated for
r=1,\dots,n_{\Gamma_{\ell}}
and
j=1,\dots,\ell
and their distributions serve as the metrics for the importance sampling performance. Details about the computation of these errors are given in
Sire et al. 2023b
.

6
Application to flooding

6.1
Description of the flooding test case

Parameterization.

The main application of this article is about the Boucholeurs district located
on the French Atlantic Coast (Figure
8(a)
) near the city of La Rochelle.
This site was hit by the Xynthia storm in February 2010, which caused the inundation of several areas and severe human and economic damage (
Bertin
et al. 2012
).

The main coastal flooding processes correspond to overflow and are simulated with the numerical code MARS (
Lazure and
Dumas 2008
), where adaptations were made by the BRGM to take into account specificities of local water circulation flows around connections (nozzles, spillways, etc. and breaching phenomena).
We focus on the interplay between tide and storm surge on the spatial distribution of the maximum water depth after flooding in the urban area.
As shown in the Figure
8(b)
, the tide is simplified and assumed to be represented by a sinusoidal signal, parameterized by the high-tide level
T
.
The surge signal is modeled as a triangular function defined by four parameters: the peak amplitude
S
, the phase difference between the surge peak and the high tide
t_{0}
, the time duration of the rising part
t_{-}
and the time duration of the falling part
t_{+}
.
In total, 5 variables describe the offshore conditions,
X=(T,S,t_{0},t_{-},t_{+})
.

Each of these input variables is assumed to be independent and described by an empirically defined probability distribution based on the analysis performed by
Rohmer
et al. 2018
of tide gauge measurements from Ile d’Aix
1
1

1

data available on
data.shom.fr
(the tide gauge closest to the study site) and La Rochelle-La Pallice.
A constant surge level offset (chosen at 65 cm i.e., compatible with future sea level rise in this region
2
2

2

see e.g.
https://sealevel.nasa.gov/data_tools/17
)
) is added to its empirical distribution to ensure that a sufficient number of flooding events are induced. Appendix
C
shows the obtained probability distributions.

Regarding the variables representing a possible breach, two characteristics are taken into account.
First, the location of the breaches. Ten different locations are selected. Six of them (B1-B6, Figure
8(a)
) are related to natural dunes and are selected based on observations made during the Xynthia event (
Muller et al. 2016
). Four other locations (B7-B10) are related to artificial dykes and are added because they are near vulnerable zones.
The other breach-related variable is the topographic level after failure, parametrized as the fraction
p
(erosion rate) of the initial crest level left.

The distribution of the breach characteristics (location and erosion rate) depends on the offshore conditions.
If the maximum of the sum of the signals (tide and surge) over time is higher than
70\%
of the embankment’s height, the probability of breaching is
0.5
.
Vice versa, the probability of breaching is assumed to be
10^{-4}
.
Finally, we consider that if there is a breach, then the location and the erosion rate follow a uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}_{\{1,\dots,10\}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{]0,1]}
, respectively.
This breach model is a compromise between simplicity and realism.

Data set generation.

Our study relies on 1300 simulations of flood maps generated as follows:

•

The offshore conditions are sampled as the beginning of the Sobol Sequence in 5 dimensions (
Cheng and
Druzdzel 2013
).

•

500 of the 1300 simulations are run without breach, and the breach parameters of the 800 others are sampled uniformly
according to
\mathcal{U}_{\{1,\dots,10\}}
for a single breach location and
\mathcal{U}_{[0,1]}
for the associated erosion rate.

These 1300 maps form the training database, and each map belongs to the space
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{M}_{64,64}(\mathbb{R})
.
The inner product
\langle.,.\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}
of Theorem
2.1
is such that all
\lambda_{i}
’s are set to 1 so that
\forall(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}^{2},\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{64}\sum_{j=1}^{64}y_{i,j}y^{\prime}_{i,j}
.

IS sampling density.

The biased density function is
g=g_{1}\times\dots\times g_{7}
with
g_{i}=\frac{\mathds{1}_{I_{i}}}{\int_{I_{i}}dx},i=1,\dots,5
, where
I_{i}
is the support of the marginal density function of the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
input variable (cf. Appendix
C
),

g_{6}=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{k=1}^{10}\delta_{k}
and
g_{7}=\frac{5}{13}\delta_{0}+(\frac{8}{13})\mathds{1}_{]0,1]}
.

The idea is to choose a single density for all the estimators related to all the Voronoi cells.

The optimal biased density for which the IS estimators have zero variance is theoretically known (
L’Ecuyer
et al. 2009
) to be a normalized version of

\mathds{1}_{{y}(\tilde{x})\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}}f_{X}(\tilde{x})
or
y(\tilde{x})\mathds{1}_{{y}(\tilde{x})\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}}_{j}}f_{X}(\tilde{x})
.
It changes with each cell
j
, affecting a non-zero probability mass only to the cell.
It is not practical as it requires the knowledge of the result of the estimation (probability or mean map) for the normalization.
The choice of a uniform distribution for the variables related to the offshore conditions is an approximation to these optimal densities that doesn’t depend on the cell. The uniform density is relevant for quantization as it provides maps covering the whole domain irrespective of their occurrence probability.

Dealing with the concentration of empty maps.

One of the main difference between the Campbell function and the flooding case is a Dirac mass in the output distribution.
Indeed, the probability that the map is free of water (
0
at every pixel of the matrix) is close to 1.
As Gaussian processes are not adapted to predict empty maps, their direct use in the Prototype Maps Algorithm would overestimate the flooding probability.
As a counter-measure, a random forest classification step is added (
Ayyadevara 2018
), creating a hurdle model (
McDowell 2003
):

•

A first step predicts if the water level is below a threshold
t
(it is a parameter to tune) or not with the classification method.

•

If the water level is forecasted to be above
t
,
then use the spatial metamodel described in Section
3
.
Otherwise, the predicted map is empty.

A final post-processing step is to convert all negative water depth values into null values.

Another difference with the Campbell case is the existence of two behaviours with or without breach.
Two metamodels are built independently for each of these two situations.

Initializing Lloyd.

Another implementation aspect is the choice of the initial prototype maps at the beginning of Lloyd’s algorithm (Step 4, Algorithm
2
).
Our a priori is that the future Voronoi cells will be associated with diverse flooding levels.
The Prototype Maps Algorithm is first run with only the 1300 realistic maps instead of the
n_{\mathrm{maps}}=10^{6}
metamodeled maps. But this degraded version of the algorithm, which provides the initial centroids, also needs to be initialized,
which is done with the maps associated to the
\ell
equally spaced quantiles of the training water volumes.

7
Summary and perspectives

This work proposes a new visualization method based on quantization
adapted to rare events and for which a limited database is available, as it occurs with computationally costly simulations.
Without loss of generality, the method, called Prototype Maps Algorithm, is developed around an application to flood maps.
The visualization takes the form of
a set of prototype maps and their probability masses
that best represent the continuous probability distribution on the space of maps.
Importance sampling and a metamodel scheme based on Functional PCA combined with Gaussian process regression are used to
address the challenges of the scarcity of the (flooding) events and the small data set size.
The obtained prototype maps along with their probabilities are an intuitive representation of the flooding risk while remaining meaningful mathematically.
Indeed, the prototype maps are a discrete approximation of the continuous random flooding event.
The distance between the discrete and the continuous distribution is the quantization error,
which is easily interpretable as it is the expected squared distance between a random map and its closest prototype map.

Performance metrics used in the article validate the precision of our method on an analytical test problem and a real flooding case.

The following developments are worthwhile,
the first being related to the tuning of the algorithm, the others dealing with complementary approaches.

Tuning of the algorithm.

First, a more sophisticated biased density function of importance sampling could be sought.
We have integrated importance sampling inside Lloyd’s algorithm to deal with the low flooding probability when estimating the centroid and the probabilities of each cluster.
The choice of a uniform density
g
proposed here for the offshore conditions is a compromise between simplicity and the exploration of the whole domain and was empirically seen to keep the variance of the estimators small.
Yet a real optimization of this density has not been performed.
While it is a difficult problem with functional unknowns, the search for a better biased density, defined independently for each cell, is a relevant continuation to this work.

Second, the number of prototype maps,
\ell
, is chosen a priori to remain usable in practice while capturing sufficiently diverse floodings.
Strategies to identify the number of Voronoi cells could be implemented based, for instance, on the evolution of the quantization error with the number of representatives.

Complementary approaches.

The method described here characterizes representative floodings.
It would now be valuable to know more about the forcing and breaching conditions leading to these scenarios, i.e., to study how the inputs are related to each prototype.

An inherent feature of the obtained quantization is that none of the prototype maps is the no-flood map. The centroid of the first Voronoi cell has a negligible water depth (
3\times 10^{-5}
meters on average), but it is not really empty.
However, fortunately, it is known that the probability that a flood map is empty is close to 1.
The formulation of quantization could then be adapted so as to force the no-flood map (or more generally a Dirac of a continuous probability law) to be one of the representatives.

In this article, the metamodel is the kriging mean. The uncertainties of the Gaussian process were not considered.
Another future development could account for this metamodel uncertainty in the computation of the conditional expectations and the probabilities.

Pagès and
Yu 2013
) to random fields
Y
.
Let us consider a probability space
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathbb{P})
, a random variable
Y\colon\Omega_{0}\to\mathcal{Y}
and the optimal quantization of
\ell
prototype maps
\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star}=\{\gamma^{\star}_{1},\dots,\gamma^{\star}_{\ell}\}\in\underset{\Gamma_{\ell}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\ell}}{\argmin}\>(e(\Gamma_{\ell}))
.

We suppose that

•

\mathcal{Y}
is of finite dimension
m
,

•

\forall z,y\in\mathcal{Y},\langle z,y\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}z_{i}y_{i}
with
\forall i,\lambda_{i}>0
,

•

and
Y\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{F}_{0})=\{W\colon\Omega_{0}\to\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert W\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]<+\infty\}
.

Let us show that
\forall i\in\{1\dots\ell\},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid Y\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star}}_{i}\right]=\gamma^{\star}_{i}
.

We introduce the inner product of the space
L^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{F}_{0})
:
\langle W,Y\rangle=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle W,Y\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\right],\>W,Y\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{F}_{0})
.
We set
\mathcal{F}=\sigma(q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(Y))\subset\mathcal{F}_{0}
the
\sigma
-algebra induced by
q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(Y)
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
W\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{F}_{0})
,

\pi_{L^{2}(\mathcal{F})}(W)=\mathbb{E}\left[W\mid\mathcal{F}\right]
, with
\pi_{L^{2}(\mathcal{F})}(W)
the orthogonal projection of
W
on
L^{2}(\mathcal{F})
(
Durrett 2010
)

Proof.

Let
W\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{F}_{0}).\penalty\ \forall Z\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{F}),

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Z,W\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Z,W\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\mid\mathcal{F}\right]\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}z_{i}w_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}\right]\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}w_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}\right]\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}z_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[w_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}\right]\right]\>\mbox{because }\forall i,\>z_{i}\in\mathcal{F}\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Z,\mathbb{E}\left[W\mid\mathcal{F}\right]\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\right]\penalty\ .\\
\end{split}

∎

We have
q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\right]\in\mathcal{F}=\sigma(q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y))
,
so
Y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\right]
is orthogonal to
q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\right]
for the inner product of the space
L^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{F}_{0})
according to proposition
A.1
.
Then, the Pythagorean theorem gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y-q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\right]\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]+

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\right]\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]\penalty\ .

We set
\tilde{\Gamma_{\ell}}=\{\tilde{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\gamma}_{\ell}\}
such that
\forall i\in\{1\dots N\},\tilde{\gamma}_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid Y\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star}}_{i}\right]
.

•

\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y-q_{\tilde{\Gamma_{\ell}}}(Y)\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\right]\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]
:

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathcal{Y},\exists i\in\{1,\dots,N\},\lVert y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)=q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(y)\right]\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\lVert y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid Y\in C^{\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star}}_{i}\right]\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\lVert y-\tilde{\gamma}_{i}\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle\geq\lVert y-q_{\tilde{\Gamma_{\ell}}}(y)\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle\text{ by definition of }q_{\tilde{\Gamma_{\ell}}}.

by definition of
\Gamma_{\ell}^{\star}

Then,
\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y-q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid q_{{\Gamma_{\ell}}^{\star}}(Y)\right]\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]

Appendix B
Projection on a basis of maps

B.1
Functional Principal Components Analysis

Every flood map
y(x)
that we want to model can be considered as a matrix indicating the water depth at each pixel, i.e., as a function

\displaystyle y_{x}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle z=(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\longmapsto y_{x}(z).

We work with a training database of
n_{\mathrm{train}}
maps
(y_{x^{i}}(z))_{i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}}
and the objective here is to compute the projection of the flood maps in a FPCA basis of
n_{\mathrm{pc}}
maps.
We first consider a functional orthonormal basis
\Phi(z)=(\Phi_{1}({z}),\dots,\Phi_{K}({z}))^{\top}
. D4 Daubechies wavelets (
Daubechies 1988
), which are widely used in image processing, are chosen here, as
Perrin et al. 2021
shows their accuracy for a similar problem. The procedure consists in the following steps:

1.

Find the coordinates
(\alpha(x^{i}))_{i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}}
of the training maps
(y_{x^{i}}(z))_{i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}}
in the
\Phi
basis:

y_{x^{i}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}(x^{i})\Phi_{k}(z)=\Phi(z)^{\top}\alpha(x^{i})

2.

Compute
\lambda_{k}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\alpha_{k}(X)^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha_{j}(X)^{2}}\right],\>k=1\dots K
, order them by decreasing values
and select the
\tilde{K}\ll K
most important vectors of the basis (the
\tilde{K}
vectors associated with the highest
\lambda_{k}
) such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}}\lambda_{k}\geq p
, with
p
a given proportion of energy. Then, we have
y_{x^{i}}(z)\simeq\sum_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}}\alpha_{k}(x^{i})\Phi_{k}(z)=\tilde{\Phi}(z)^{\top}\tilde{\alpha}(x^{i})

3.

Apply PCA in
\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{K}}
on the dataset of coefficients evaluated at the design points
(\tilde{\alpha}(x^{i}))_{i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}}
.
Denote
t_{1}(x^{i}),\dots,t_{n_{\mathrm{pc}}}(x^{i}),i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}
, the coordinates of the first
n_{\mathrm{pc}}
principal components and
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n_{\mathrm{pc}}}
the associated eigenvectors in
\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{K}}.

We have
\tilde{\alpha}(x^{i})\simeq\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{pc}}}t_{j}(x^{i})w_{j}=\Omega^{\top}t(x^{i})
, with
\Omega
the
n_{\mathrm{pc}}\times\tilde{K}
projection matrix.

Finally we can write
y_{x^{i}}(z)\simeq(\Omega\tilde{\Phi}(z))^{\top}t(x^{i})
, with
\Omega\tilde{\Phi}
a basis of
n_{\mathrm{pc}}
maps.
\tilde{K}
(or, equivalently,
p
) and
n_{\mathrm{pc}}
are tuned by cross-validation.

B.2
Inverse FPCA

As described in Section
3
, Gaussian processes are used to predict
\hat{t}(x^{\star})
for a new input
x^{\star}
.
The following steps provide the map
\hat{y}(x^{\star})
from the coordinates
\hat{t}(x^{\star})
:

1.

Compute the coefficients of the vector
\alpha(x^{\star})

•

for
k=\tilde{K}+1,\dots,K,\hat{\alpha}_{k}(x^{\star})=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{train}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{train}}}\alpha_{k}(x^{i})

•

for
k=1,\dots,\tilde{K},\hat{\alpha}_{k}(x^{\star})=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{pc}}}\hat{t}_{j}(x^{\star})\omega_{j}

2.

Compute
(\hat{y}_{x^{\star}}(z))
with
\hat{y}_{x^{\star}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\alpha}_{k}(x^{\star})\Phi_{k}(z)

The computation of
y_{x^{\star}}(z)
at the targeted family of pixels provides the predicted map
\hat{y}(x^{\star})
.

Appendix D
Voronoi cells on the FPCA axes

In the flooding case, the two first FPCA axes summarize
98\%
of the inertia of the training 1300 maps. It is relevant to visualize the quantization cells through their two first FPCA coefficients. Among other things, this allows to have a better understanding of their size.
Figure
11(a)
shows the distribution of
10^{5}
maps sampled with the uniform density
g

and the prototype maps in the FPCA basis calculated for the maps without breach.
As expected, cell 1 is almost a Dirac at the empty map whose coefficients are
(-27,8)
.
Moreover, the largest cells are the ones associated to the most extreme floodings. For instance, cell 5 contains very diversified maps in terms of water depth magnitude and spatial distribution. It is an expected property of the optimal Voronoi cells: the cells with high probabilities are small and vice versa. However, this characteristic may not be true is some specific contexts, as shown in Appendix
E
.
Figure
11(b)
shows the probability density of the maps expressed in the 2 first FPCA coefficients.
It is a histogram where each bin has a colour corresponding to the weight
\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\text{bin}}}\frac{f(\tilde{X}^{k})}{g(\tilde{X}^{k})}\right)
associated to the IS density ratio of the maps,
n_{\text{bin}}
being the number of maps falling within each bin. It is essential to understand the position of the prototype maps and the probability mass of the cells.
It illustrates the concentration of the mass in cell 1,
with a high
\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\text{bin}}}\frac{f(X^{k})}{g(X^{k})}\right)
compared to the other cells.
It also explains the position of the centroid of the cell 5, which is located near the left limit. This is due to the low weight terms at the right of the cell.

Appendix E
Relation between cells size and probability

As the Lloyd’s algorithm minimizes the quantization error
e(\Gamma_{\ell})=\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y-q_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(Y)\lVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\right]\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we can expect that the largest Voronoi cells are associated to a small probability mass. However, it may not be the case with specific random variables
Y
.

For instance, let consider a random variable
Y\in[-20,20]
, with a density function
d=\frac{1}{10}\mathds{1}_{[-20,-10]}+\frac{9}{10}\mathds{1}_{[0,20]}
.

If we perform the Lloyd’s algorithm with two centroids
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, it gives
\gamma_{1}=-15
and
\gamma_{2}=10
and two Voronoi cells:
C_{1}=[-20,-2.5]
with probability mass
p_{1}=\frac{1}{10}
and
C_{2}=[-2.5,20]
with probability mass
p_{2}=\frac{9}{10}
.
