Numerical ranges of cyclic shift matrices
Mao-Ting Chien, Steve Kirkland, Chi-Kwong Li, Hiroshi Nakazato

Abstract
We study the numerical range of an n × n n\times n cyclic shift matrix, which can be viewed as the adjacency matrix of a directed cycle with n n weighted arcs. In particular, we consider the change in the numerical range if the weights are rearranged or perturbed. In addition to obtaining some general results on the problem, a permutation of the given weights is identified such that the corresponding matrix yields the largest numerical range (in terms of set inclusion), for n ≤ 6 n\leq 6 . We conjecture that the maximizing pattern extends to general n × n n\times n cylic shift matrices. For n ≤ 5 n\leq 5 , we also determine permutations such that the corresponding cyclic shift matrix yields the smallest numerical range.

Introduction

Let
M_{n}
be the set of
n\times n
complex matrices. The numerical range of
A\in M_{n}
is defined and denoted by

W(A)=\{x^{*}Ax:x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n},x^{*}x=1\},

and is a useful tool for studying matrices; for example, see
[
8
, Chaper 1]
.
In particular, there is an interesting interplay between the geometric properties of

W(A)
and the algebraic properties of
A
. The Töplitz-Hausdorff theorem
asserts that
W(A)
is a convex set. It is known that
W(A)=\{\lambda\}

if and only if
A=\lambda I
;
W(A)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
if and only if

A
is Hermitian;
W(A)
is a polygon (with interior) if and only if

A
is unitarily similar to
D\oplus B
for a diagonal matrix
D\in M_{k}
(for some
k=1,\ldots,n
) such that

W(B)\subseteq W(A)=W(D)={\rm conv}({\rm{eig}}(D))
, the convex hull of spectrum of
D
; e.g,
see
[
8
]
.

In this paper, we study the numerical range of a
cyclic shift matrix

defined as follows. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and let

A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}E_{j,j+1}+a_{n}E_{n,1}

be the corresponding cyclic shift matrix. Here

\{E_{1,1},E_{1,2},\dots,E_{n,n}\}
is the standard basis for
M_{n}
.
There are a number of interesting papers on properties of the numerical ranges of
cyclic shift matrices (see
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
7
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
).
In general, if
A\in M_{n}
is such that each of its
rows and columns has at most one nonzero entry,
then
A
is permutationally similar to
A_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{r}
(possibly with an additional direct summand that is a zero matrix),
where each
A_{j}
is a cyclic shift matrix. Hence, our results can be
used to describe
W(A)
in terms of
{\rm conv}(\cup_{j=1}^{r}W(A_{j}))
.

Now, suppose
A=S(a_{1}\dots,a_{n})
.
There is a diagonal unitary matrix
U
such that for some
t\in{\mathbb{R}},

U^{*}AU=e^{it}\hat{A}
, where
\hat{A}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|a_{j}|E_{j,j+1}+|a_{n}|E_{n,1},

so that
W(A)=e^{it}W(\hat{A})
.
Hence, we may focus our attention on the case that
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\geq 0
.
By the result in
[
10
]
, if
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}>0
, we have

W(A)=e^{i2\pi/n}W(A).

On the other hand,
if
a_{j}=0
for some
j
, then
W(A)
is a circular disk centered at the origin with
radius equal to the largest eigenvalue of
{\rm Re}(A)=(A+A^{t})/2
.
In fact, if
k
of the values
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are equal to zero, then
up to a permutation similarity,
A
has the form

A_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{k}
where each
A_{j}
has positive entries on the superdiagonal,
and
W(A_{j})
is a circular disk centered at the origin
with radius equal to the largest eigenvalue, i.e., the spectral radius,
of
(A_{j}+A_{j}^{t})/2
. If the largest eigenvalue of
A+A^{t}
corresponds to the
direct summand
A_{1}+A_{1}^{t}
, then
W(A)=W(A_{1})
.

Let
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
B=S(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})

for
a_{j},b_{j}\geq 0
,
j=1,\dots,n
. We are interested in comparing

W(B)
and
W(A)
. In particular, if

(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
is obtained from
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
by a
permutation
\sigma
of the entries, we write
B=A_{\sigma}
.

Let
r(A)=\max\{|\mu|:\mu\in W(A)\}
be the numerical radius of

A\in M_{n}
.
In
[
1
]
, it is shown that if
A=S(a_{1},a_{3},a_{5},\dots,a_{6},a_{4},a_{2})

with
0\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
, then

r(A_{\sigma})\leq r(A)\qquad\hbox{ for any permutation }\ \sigma.

In particular, if
a_{1}=0
, then
W(A_{\sigma})
is a circular disk, and

W(A_{\sigma})\subseteq W(A)\qquad\hbox{ for any permutation }\ \sigma.

(1.1)

We formulate the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Suppose that
n\geq 2,
that
0\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
, and let
A=S(a_{1},a_{3},a_{5},\dots,a_{6},a_{4},a_{2}).
Then
(
1.1
)
holds
for any
\sigma\in S_{n}
.

Conjecture
1.1
holds trivially
for
n=2,3
and in this paper, we confirm the conjecture for
n=4,5,6
.
We also study permutations
\tau
such that

W(A_{\tau})\subseteq W(A_{\sigma})
for all
\sigma
.
For
n=4
, we have
\tau=(1,3,2,4)
;
for
n=5
, we have
\tau=(1,4,3,2,5)
.

Note that
S(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
is permutationally similar to

S(a_{n},a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n-1}),
which is obtained by a clockwise permutation of the weights
(cf.
[
13
]
).
More generally,
letting
C=S(1,\dots,1)
,
we may replace
A_{\sigma}
by
C^{r}A_{\sigma}C^{-r}
(for some suitable choice of
r
) and
assume that the
(1,2)
entry of
A
is the minimum
of the values
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
.
In our discussion, we often assume that
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
with

a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}>0
. Letting

a_{j}\rightarrow 0
, may then recover the corresponding results when some
a_{j}=0
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we present some preliminary results. In Section 3, we consider
extreme matrices
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
attaining the maximum and minimum
numerical ranges under some restrictions on
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
.
In Sections 4–6, we compare
W(A)
and
W(B)
for

A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
B=S(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
with emphasis on the
cases that
(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
is a permutation of
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
.
We mention some related results and future research directions in Section 7.

2
Preliminaries

Denote by
\lambda_{1}(H)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(H)

the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix
H\in M_{n}
.
For
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
,
A\in M_{n}
, consider

{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)=(e^{i\theta}A+e^{-i\theta}A^{*})/2
.
We have the following; see
[
8
, Theorem 1.5.12]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A\in M_{n}
. Then

W(A)=\cap_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}\{\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}:{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}\mu)\leq\lambda_{1}({\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))\}.

As a result, for any
B\in M_{n}
,

W(B)\subseteq W(A)
if and only if
\lambda_{1}({\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}B))\leq\lambda_{1}({\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))
for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
be such that
a_{j}>0
(
j=1,2,\ldots,n
), and fix
\theta\in[0,2\pi).

Then each eigenvalue of
{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
has multiplicity at most 2.

(a)

Suppose
n=2k
is even. Then
{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)

is permutationally similar to a matrix of the form

\begin{pmatrix}0_{k}&R\cr R^{*}&0_{k}\cr\end{pmatrix}
, where
0_{k}
is the
k\times k
zero matrix. The eigenvalues of

{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
have the form
\pm s_{j}
, where
s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{k}\geq 0
are the
singular values of
R
.
If
s_{j}=s_{j+1}

for some
j
then
-s_{j}=-s_{j+1}
is another repeated eigenvalue.
Moreover,
if
\det(zI-(iA-iA^{*}))=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(z^{2}-\alpha_{j}^{2})
, then

\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(z^{2}-\alpha_{j}^{2})+(-1)^{k}2\prod_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}-2\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\Big)\cos(n\theta).

(b)

Suppose
n=2k+1
is odd. The matrix obtained from

{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
by deleting the last row and last column has

k
positive and
k
negative eigenvalues. The matrix

{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
has at least
k
positive and
k
negative eigenvalues.
Moreover, if

\det(zI-(iA-iA^{*}))=z\prod_{j=1}^{k}(z^{2}-\alpha_{j}^{2})
, then

\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))=z\prod_{j=1}^{k}(z^{2}-\alpha_{j}^{2})-2\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\Big)\cos(n\theta).

Proof.

Removing the first row and the last row of the matrix
\lambda I-{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)

yields an
(n-2)\times n
matrix in row echelon form
with positive
(j,j)
entry for
j=1,\dots,n-2
. So,
the kernel of
\lambda I-{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
has dimension at most 2.

(a) If we apply a permutation similarity to
{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
and put the rows and columns
with odd indices as the first
k
rows and columns,
we see that
{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
has the asserted block form. The assertions about
the eigenvalues follow.

To prove the formula for
\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))
,
let
D={\rm diag}\,(1,e^{i\theta},e^{2i\theta},\dots,e^{i(n-1)\theta})
.
Then
D(2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))D^{*}
is the same as
A+A^{t}
except that the
(1,n)

and
(n,1)
entries are equal to
a_{n}e^{-in\theta}
and
a_{n}e^{in\theta}
.
Now,
\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))=z^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{j}(\theta)z^{n-j}

such that
\gamma_{j}(\theta)
is the sum of the
j\times j
principal minors
of
2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
. One may readily check that

\gamma_{1}(\theta),\dots,\gamma_{n-1}(\theta)

are independent of
\theta
, and
\gamma_{n}(\theta)=\gamma_{n}(\pi/2)+(-1)^{k}2a_{1}\cdots a_{n}-2a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\cos(n\theta)
.
The result follows.

(b) Removing the last row and the last column of
{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
,
we get a tridiagonal Hermitian matrix that is permutationally similar to
a matrix of the form
\begin{pmatrix}0&R\cr R^{*}&0\cr\end{pmatrix}
.
We get the conclusion on the eigenvalues of the matrix.
We can use the arguments in the proof of (a) to conclude that

\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))=\det(I-(iA-iA^{*}))-2a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\cos(n\theta)
,
and
\det((iA-iA^{*}))=0
. The conclusion follows.

\Box

It is interesting to note that for
C=S(1,\dots,1)
, then

C+C^{t}
has eigenvalues
2\cos(2k\pi/n)
for
k=1,\dots,n
.
Thus, if
n\geq 3
is odd, there are
(n-1)/2
distinct
eigenvalues with multiplicity 2, while if
n\geq 4
is even, there are
(n-2)/2
distinct eigenvalues with multiplicity 2.
On the other hand, if
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1},a_{n})
with

a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}>0=a_{n}
, then
A+A^{t}
necessarily has
n
distinct
eigenvalues.

The following
proposition, which is of independent interest (especially in
the graph theory context) shows that
one can change

(a_{n-1},a_{n})
to produce a nonnegative matrix
\hat{A}

so that the smallest eigenvalue of
\hat{A}+\hat{A}^{t}

has multiplicity 2.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose
n
is odd, and

A=S(a_{1},\dots a_{n-1},a_{n})
with
a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}>0=a_{n}
.
Then there are
\hat{a}_{n-1},\hat{a}_{n}>0
such that
for
\hat{A}=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-2},\hat{a}_{n-1},\hat{a}_{n})
,
the smallest eigenvalue of
\hat{A}+\hat{A}^{t}

has multiplicity 2.

Proof.
Let
n=2k+1
, and
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1},a_{n})

with
a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}>0=a_{n}
. Then removing the last row and the
last column of the matrix
A+A^{t}
, we get
B
with
n-1
distinct eigenvalues.
Let
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
be an eigenvector of
B
corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue

\mu
. Then the first entry
v_{1}
and last entry
v_{n-1}
of
v

have opposite signs. (This follows from Perron–Frobenius theory: the graph associated with
B
is bipartite, so
-\mu
is the spectral radius of
B
; the conclusion that
v_{1}v_{n-1}<0
now follows from the fact that vertices
1
and
n-1
are in different subsets of the bipartition.)
Let
b=(|v_{n-1}|,0,\dots,0,|v_{1}|)^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
,
and form the matrix

M(x)=\begin{pmatrix}B&xb\cr xb^{t}&0\cr\end{pmatrix}
,
with
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
\mu

is an eigenvalue of
M(x)
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
with corresponding eigenvector
\begin{pmatrix}v\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}
.
For
x=0,M(0)

has same eigenvalues as
B
, with an extra 0 appended.
As
x\rightarrow\infty

one of the eigenvalues of
M(x)
goes to

-\infty
. By the Intermediate Value Theorem,
there must be a value
x_{0}>0
such that
M(x_{0})
has

\mu
as a double eigenvalue.

\Box

In connection to the comparison of the maximal eigenvalue of Proposition
2.1
,
the following simple lemma is useful in our study.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
f(t),g(t)
are monic polynomials of degree
n
and that each
has
n
real zeros.
Suppose further that
f(t)=g(t)-h(t)
for some polynomial
h(t)
of degree
smaller than
n
such that one of the following conditions holds:

(a)

h(t_{1})\geq 0
, where
t_{1}
is the largest zero of
g(t)
,

(b)

h(t)>0
for
t\geq t_{2}
, where
t_{2}
is the largest zero of
f(t)
.

Then the largest positive zero of
f(t)
is larger than or equal to that of
g(t)
.

Proof.

(a) Note that
f(t_{1})\leq 0
. Thus, the largest zero of
f(t)
is larger than or
equal to
t_{1}
.

(b) If
h(t)>0
for
t\geq t_{2}
, then
g(t)=f(t)+h(t)>0
for
t\geq t_{2}
and hence
t_{1}<t_{2}
.
\Box

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and

B=S(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
with
a_{j},b_{j}>0
for
j=1,\dots,n
.
Let
\alpha=\prod_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}
,
\beta=\prod_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}
,
There are monic polynomials
f(z),g(z)
of degree
n

independent of
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
such that for any such
\theta,

\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))=f(z)-2\alpha\cos n\theta\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}B))=g(z)-2\beta\cos n\theta.

Let
z_{t}

be the largest zeros of
f(z)-2\alpha t
for
t\in[-1,1]
and the largest zero of

g(z)+2\beta
is not larger than
z_{-1}
. Then
W(B)\subseteq W(A)
if and only if

g(z)/\beta\geq f(z)/\alpha
for
z\in[z_{-1},z_{1}]
.

Proof.

Note that for any
n\times n
matrix
M
,
\det(zI_{n}-M)=z^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}z^{n-j}

where

(-1)^{j}m_{j}
is the sum of the
j\times j
principal minors of
M
.
One readily checks that the coefficients of
z^{n-j}
of the polynomial
\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))
for
2\leq j\leq n-1

are independent of
\theta
and correspond to the (same) sum of principal minors.
The constant term is given by

\det(2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))=2\alpha\cos(n\theta)

for odd
n,
and

\det(2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))=(-1)^{n/2}(a_{1}^{2}a_{3}^{2}\cdots a_{n-1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}a_{4}^{2}\cdots a_{n}^{2})-2\alpha\cos(n\theta)

for even
n
. So we conclude that
\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))
and

\det(zI-2{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}B))
have the asserted forms.

Let
t=\cos n\theta\in[-1,1]
.
Then
f(x)-2\alpha t
has
n
real zeros for every
t\in[-1,1]
,
and
f(x)
is a convex increasing function for
x\in[z_{-1},\infty)
.
Similarly,
g(x)-2\beta t
has
n
real zeros for every
t\in[-1,1]
,
and
f(x)
is a convex increasing function for
x\in[\hat{z}_{-1},\infty)
,
where
\hat{z}_{-1}
is the largest zero of
g(x)+2\beta
.
By our assumption,
\hat{z}_{-1}\leq z_{-1}
.

Note that
W(B)\subseteq W(A)
if and only if
for every
t\in[-1,1]
, the
largest root of
f(x)-2\alpha t=0
always lies on the right side of that of

g(x)-2\beta t=0
. Equivalently, in
[z_{-1},z_{1}]
, the curve
g(x)/\beta
is always above
that of
f(x)/\alpha
, and the result follows.
\Box

Note that
f(z)
has the form
(x^{2}-c_{1}^{2})\cdots(x^{2}-c_{k}^{2})

or

or

3
Cyclic shift matrices with extreme numerical ranges

In the following, we show that some weighted cycles and paths will
yield the smallest numerical ranges under certain restrictions on the weights.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
such that

a_{1}\cdots a_{n}=1
and consider
C=S(1,\dots,1)
.
Then
W(C)\subseteq W(A)
. Equality holds if and only if

|a_{1}|=\cdots=|a_{n}|=1
.

Proof.
Note that
\det(\lambda I-A)=\lambda^{n}-1
.
Thus,
A
has eigenvalues
e^{i2k\pi/n}
for
k=1,\dots,n
.
By convexity of
W(A)
and the fact that
W(A)
contains all the
eigenvalues of
A
, we see that

W(C)={\rm conv}\{e^{i2k\pi/n}:1\leq k\leq n\}\subseteq W(A)
.
If
W(A)=W(C)
, then all eigenvalues of
A
are non-differentiable
boundary points of
W(A)
; e.g., see
[
8
]
.
Hence,
A
is normal, and equals
C
.

\Box

Below is a similar result for

A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
with
a_{n}=0
. In this case,
W(A)
is always a
circular disk centered at the origin with radius
r(A)
.
We will determine the smallest radius if
\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}=1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
for some

a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with

|\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}|=1
and
a_{n}=0
.
If
|A|=S(|a_{1}|,\dots,|a_{n-1}|,0)
, then
W(A)=W(|A|)
is a circular disk
centered at the origin with radius
\lambda_{1}(|A|+|A|^{t})/2

such that

\lambda_{1}(A+A^{*})=\lambda_{1}(|A|+|A|^{t})\geq 2^{(n-2)/(n-1)}.

The equality holds if and only if

|a_{1}|=|a_{n-1}|=2^{(n-3)/(2n-2)}
,
|a_{j}|=2^{-1/(n-1)}
for
j=2,...,n-2
,
so that

x=(1/\sqrt{2n-2},1/\sqrt{n-1},1/\sqrt{n-1},\ldots,1/\sqrt{n-1},1/\sqrt{2n-2})^{t}
is the corresponding
(Perron) eigenvector for
|A|+|A|^{t}
.

Proof.

We know that
W(A)=W(|A|)
is a circular disk centered at the origin with
radius
\lambda_{1}(|A|+|A|^{t})/2
. So,
suppose that
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})

gives rise to the minimum
\lambda_{1}(A+A^{t})=r
with

a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}>0=a_{n}
.
Consider the function

f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}x_{j}x_{j+1}.

The minimizing matrix
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
can be obtained by finding the minimax value

\min_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}}\max_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

over the conditions

a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n-1}=1
and

[formula]

[formula]

[formula]

7
Related results and further research

Note that the fourth author has verified Conjecture
1.1
for the cases
n=7,8
, by
extending the proof for the case
n=6
with the help of the symbolic calculation program Mathematica.
The computation is very involved. It would be interesting to prove or disprove the
conjecture for general
n
. Of course, a related question is to determine the
permutation
\tau
so that
A_{\tau}
yields the smallest

W(A_{\tau})
for
n\geq 6
, if such a
\tau
exists.
The fourth author also has some partial results in this direction for the case that
n=6
.

As pointed out in Section 1, our study is closely related to the maximum and
minimum eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of a given graph. In our case,
if
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
with positive numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, then
A+A^{t}
is the adjacency matrix of a weighted undirected cycle. One may consider a bound

\gamma
for the largest
eigenvalue of
A+A^{t}
for a general nonnegative matrix
A
and interpret it in terms of the
adjacency matrix of a weighted graph; then the numerical radius of
A
will satisfy
r(A)\leq\gamma
.
For example, that problem is solved in the case that
A+A^{t}
corresponds to the adjacency
matrix of a tree (and therefore a forest); see
[
2
]
.
Of course, in general it is more challenging to determine whether

W(A_{\sigma})
is maximum or minimum in terms of set inclusion.

Our study is also related to the study of positive linear maps on operator
algebras and operator subspaces (systems).
Recall that a linear map
\Phi:V_{1}\rightarrow V_{2}
from a subspace
V_{1}
of
M_{n}
to
a subspace
V_{2}
of
M_{m}
is
k
-positive for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
if

(\Phi(A_{ij}))\in M_{n}(V_{2})
is positive semidefinite whenever

(A_{ij})\in M_{n}(V_{1})
is positive semidefinite;

\Phi
is completely positive if
\Phi
is
k
-positive for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
One may connect the study of the inclusion of the numerical range
with positive linear maps and completely positive maps; see
[
5
]
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
A,B\in M_{n}
. Then
W(B)\subseteq W(A)
if and only if any one of the following holds.

(a)

\mu I-{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}B)
is positive definite whenever

\mu I-{\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A)
is positive definite.

(b)

The map
T:{\rm span}\,\{I,A,A^{*}\}\rightarrow{\rm span}\,\{I,B,B^{*}\}

defined by
\alpha I+\beta A+\gamma A^{*}\mapsto\alpha I+\beta B+\gamma B^{*}

send positive definite matrices to positive semidefinite matrices.

(c)

\lambda_{1}({\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}A))\geq\lambda_{1}({\rm Re}(e^{i\theta}B))
for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.

In the case that
A,B
are Hermitian, the above conditions are equivalent to the following.

(d)

There is a unital completely positive linear map
\Psi:{\rm span}\,\{I,A\}\rightarrow{\rm span}\,\{I,B\}
such that

\Psi(A)=B
.

Consequently, our results on
W(B)\subseteq W(A)
for

B=S(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
and
A=S(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
provide
necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a positive
linear map from
\{I,A,A^{*}\}
to
\{I,B,B^{*}\}
. Note that
there is a positive linear map from
\{I,A,A^{*}\}
to
\{I,B,B^{*}\}

if and only if there is a positive linear map from

\{I,U^{*}AU,U^{*}A^{*}U\}
to

\{I,V^{*}BV,V^{*}B^{*}V\}
for any unitary matrices
U
and
V
.
One may also consider matrices of different sizes, or infinite sizes
if we replace
W(X)
by its closure. So, there might be interest in
extending our results to general operators and infinite graphs.

Remark 7.2
.

One might seek to extend Conjecture
1.1
to find the minimal numerical range. Suppose that
n\geq 2,
and

W(B)\subseteq W(A_{\sigma})\qquad\hbox{ for any permutation }\ \sigma,

The inclusion (
7.8

and

We provide an example to show that the inclusion (

) is not true for

.

By letting

and
