Abstract
. We study semiflows generated via impulsive perturbations of Lorenz flows. We prove that such semiflows admit a finite number of physical measures. Moreover, if the impulsive perturbation is small enough, we show that the physical measures of the semiflows are close, in the weak* topology, to the unique physical measure of the Lorenz flow. A similar conclusion holds for the entropies associated with the physical measures.

1.
Introduction

Lorenz
[
31
]
studied numerically the three-dimensional vector field defined by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{x_{1}}=\sigma(x_{2}-x_{1}),\\
\dot{x_{2}}=rx_{1}-x_{2}-x_{1}x_{3},\\
\dot{x_{3}}=x_{1}x_{2}-bx_{3},\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

when
\sigma=10
,
r=28
and
b=8/3
, as a simplified model for atmospheric convection. By numerical calculations, Lorenz observed that the associated flow has a compact trapping region with a chaotic attractor. Later, a rigorous mathematical framework of similar flows, called geometric Lorenz flows, was introduced in
[
3
,
28
]
. In
[
37
]
, Tucker provided a computer-assisted proof that the classical Lorenz attractor is indeed a geometric Lorenz attractor. In particular, it is a singular-hyperbolic attractor
[
32
]
, namely a nontrivial robustly transitive attracting invariant set containing a singularity (equilibrium point). Moreover, it admits a unique physical measure; see for example
[
11
]
. Recently, many authors studied the stability of such physical measure and other statistical properties under small
smooth
perturbations of Lorenz flows
[
9
,
12
,
13
]
. It is know that invariant stable foliations of Lorenz flows persist under small smooth perturbations; see for instance
[
18
]
. In particular, the associated Poincaré map inherits an invariant
C^{1+\alpha}
,
\alpha>0
, stable foliation
[
10
]
and the Poincaré map can be represented by a two dimensional
skew product
, where the base map is a one-dimensional piecewise uniformly expanding and the fibre maps are uniformly contracting. Such properties were exploited in
[
9
,
12
,
13
]
.

Impulsive perturbations are very common in physical models. They capture phenomena characterised by sudden changes in the states of the system (e.g. when billiard balls collide, there is an impulse exchanged between them, resulting in abrupt changes in their velocities and directions). In this work, we are interested in studying impulsive perturbations of Lorenz flows, where the resulting perturbations generate discontinuous impulsive semiflows (see Subsection
1.2
for a precise definition of an impulsive semiflow). Our motivation is twofold, on the one hand is to investigate stability of statistical properties for Lorenz flows under discontinuous perturbations, and on the other hand to investigate smooth ergodic theoretic properties in the context of impulsive dynamical systems; in particular, existence and stability of
physical measures
. The latter systems have been mainly studied from a qualitative point of view: existence and uniqueness of solutions, sufficient conditions to ensure a complete characterization and some asymptotic stability of the limit sets
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
22
,
23
,
30
]
, with few exceptions on basic ergodic theoretic results
[
2
,
7
,
8
]
.

Our work demonstrates how introducing a small impulsive perturbation to Lorenz flows can pose non-obvious problems, requiring techniques different from those traditionally employed in classical scenarios. At the technical level, the main difficultly in our study is that the perturbed semiflow is not continuous. Hence, in general, it is not expected that it admits a smooth invariant foliation. Consequently, to prove existence of physical measures for the semiflow through a corresponding Poincaré map, one cannot use the standard technique of quotienting along stable leaves, since the Poincaré map is
not
necessarily a skew product. We believe that the current work will stimulate further research on statistical properties for impulsive semiflows.

In the rest of this introduction, we recall the definition of a physical measure and the notion of an impulsive dynamical system. Statements of our main results (Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
) on existence and stability of physical measures and the corresponding entropies for impulsive Lorenz semiflows are found in Subsection
1.3
.

1.1.
Physical measures and
u
-Gibbs measures

Let
M
be a finite dimensional compact Riemannian manifold (possibly with boundary) and
m
be the Lebesgue (volume) measure on the Borel sets of
M
. We say that
X:\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\times M\to M

is a
semiflow
if, for all
x\in M
and
s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
, we have

(1)

X_{0}(x)=x
,

(2)

X_{t+s}(x)=X_{t}(X_{s}(x))
.

The
trajectory
of
x\in M
is the curve defined by
X_{t}(x)
, for
t\geq 0
.
The semiflow is called a
flow
when we have
\mathbb{R}
playing the role of
\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
, which means that we can consider the past of the trajectories.

Given a semiflow
X
on
M
, we define the
basin

\mathcal{B}_{\nu}
of a probability measure
\nu
on the Borel sets of
M
as the set of points

x\in M
such that, for any continuous

\varphi:M\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(X_{t}(x))dt=\int\varphi\,d\nu.

(1.2)

We say that
\nu
is a
physical measure
for
X
if
m(\mathcal{B}_{\nu})>0
.
Since measures defined on the Borel sets of a compact metric space are determined by their integrals on continuous functions, it follows that distinct physical measures must have disjoint basins.

Physical measures for semiflows are frequently obtained considering discrete-time dynamical systems associated with these flows, e.g. via Poincaré return maps. In the discrete-time case of a dynamical system defined by a map
f:M\to M
, we define the basin
\mathcal{B}_{\mu}
of a probability measure
\mu
on the Borel sets of
M
as the set of points
x\in M
such that, for any continuous

\varphi:M\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\varphi(f^{j}(x))dt=\int\varphi\,d\mu.

(1.3)

We say that
\mu
is a
physical measure
for
f
if
m(\mathcal{B}_{\mu})>0
.
Note that a physical measure is necessarily
f
-invariant
.

A probability measure
\mu
on the Borel sets of
M
is called a
u
-Gibbs measure
(also known as an
SRB measure
) for a map
{f:M\to M}
if
i)

\mu
is
f
-invariant,
ii)

f
has positive Lyapunov exponents
\mu
almost everywhere, and
iii)
the conditional measures of
\mu
on local unstable manifolds are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measures on these manifolds.
A result due to Pesin
[
33
]

establishes that if
f
is a
C^{1+\alpha}
diffeomorphism whose Lyapunov exponents are all nonzero with respect to an ergodic invariant probability measure
\mu
, then the
stable holonomy
is absolutely continuous.
Using Birkhoff Ergodic
Theorem, it can be proved that
the basin of an ergodic probability measure
\mu
in a compact Riemannian manifold
M
contains
\mu
almost every point in
M
; see e.g.
[
5
, Proposition 2.12]
.
Since time averages with respect to continuous functions are constant on stable manifolds, it follows that every ergodic
u
-Gibbs probability measure for a
C^{1+\alpha}
diffeomorphism with non-zero Lyapunov exponents
\mu
almost everywhere is a physical measure.
These conclusions, relatively well known for diffeomorphisms of class
C^{1+\alpha}
, are also valid for the class of piecewise
C^{1+\alpha}
diffeomorphims considered in
[
35
,
36
]
which we will address in this work; see also
[
29
, Part I & Part II]
.

The existence of
u
-Gibbs measure for piecewise piecewise hyperbolic diffeomorphisms has been deduced in several situations under additional assumptions:

•

bounded derivative
[
24
,
19
,
38
,
39
]
;

•

conservative setting (billiards)
[
21
,
20
,
39
]
;

•

invariant stable foliation
[
11
,
26
]
;

•

stable cone field transverse to the singularity set
[
25
]
;

None of these assumptions are a priori verified for the Poincaré maps we consider. The most suitable work for our setting is that of Pesin
[
34
]
and Satayev
[
35
,
36
]
on piecewise hyperbolic diffeomorphisms.

1.2.
Impulsive semiflows

Consider a semiflow or a flow
X
on
M
and
\Sigma\subset M
a region such that

\inf\left\{t>0:X_{t}(x)\in\Sigma\right\}>0,\quad\forall x\in M.

Given a map

\varphi:\Sigma\to M
and
x\in M
, we define the
impulsive trajectory

{\gamma_{x}:[0,T(x))\to M}
, for some
T(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{\infty\}
,
and the
impulsive times

0=\tau_{0}(x)<\tau_{1}(x)<\tau_{2}(x)<\cdots
(a finite or infinite number of times) using the following inductive procedure:

(1)

Set

\tau_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}\inf\left\{t>0:X_{t}(x)\in\Sigma\right\},&\text{if }X_{t}(x)\in\Sigma\text{ for some }t>0;\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise;}\end{cases}

and for
0\leq t<\tau_{1}(x)

\gamma_{x}(t)=X_{t}(x).

Figure 1.
Impulsive trajectory

(2)

Assume that
\tau_{n}(x)
and
\gamma_{x}(t)
have been defined for
0\leq t<\tau_{n}(x)
and
n\geq 1
.

If
\tau_{n}(x)=\infty
, then set
T(x)=\infty
and stop the process.

If
\tau_{n}(x)<\infty
, set

\quad\quad\gamma_{x}(\tau_{n}(x))=\varphi(X_{\tau_{n}(x)-\tau_{n-1}(x)}(\gamma_{x}({\tau_{n-1}(x)}))),

\tau_{n+1}(x)=\tau_{n}(x)+\tau_{1}(\gamma_{x}(\tau_{n}(x)))

and for
\tau_{n}(x)<t<\tau_{n+1}(x)

\gamma_{x}(t)=X_{t-\tau_{n}(x)}(\gamma_{x}(\tau_{n}(x))).

This completes the inductive procedure.

In case
\tau_{n}(x)<\infty
for all
n\geq 1
, set

T(x)=\sup_{n\geq 1}\,\{\tau_{n}(x)\}.

In general we can have
T(x)=+\infty
or
T(x)<+\infty
. However, assuming that
\varphi(\Sigma)\cap\Sigma=\emptyset
, it follows that
T(x)=+\infty
for all
x\in M
; see
[
6
, Remark 1.1]
.
We say that
(M,X,\Sigma,\varphi)
is an
impulsive dynamical system
if

\tau_{1}(x)>0\quad\text{and}\quad T(x)=+\infty,\quad\text{for all $x\in M$}.

We call
\Sigma
the
impulsive region
and
\varphi
the
impulsive map
of the impulsive dynamical system. The
impulsive semiflow

Y:\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\times M\to M
of the impulsive dynamical system
(M,X,\Sigma,\varphi)
is given by

Y(t,x)=\gamma_{x}(t),\quad\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\times M,

where
\gamma_{x}(t)
is the impulsive trajectory of
x
determined by
(M,X,\Sigma,\varphi)
.
It follows from
[
14
, Proposition 2.1]
that
Y
is indeed a semiflow, though not necessarily continuous nor a flow, even if
X
is a flow. In fact, through the points of
\varphi(\Sigma)
more than one trajectory can pass, which means that we cannot consider the past of these trajectories. A different type of impulsive perturbations was considered in
[
27
]
.

1.3.
Lorenz flows

Our main results will be obtained not only for the flow
X
associated with the vector field in (
1.1
), but more generally for the broader class of geometric Lorenz flows introduced in
[
3
,
28
]
. We will refer to all these flows as
Lorenz flows
.
Many features of the flow
X
in the trapping region
M
have been proven over the last decades by studying the Poincaré return map to the plane
x_{3}=r-1
; see
[
3
,
4
]
.
As noted in
[
35
, Section 2.2]
and
[
36
, Section 2]
this return map fits in a certain set of conditions (L1)-(L4) that we describe in Subsection
2.2
. In particular, using an appropriate coordinate system, the Poincaré return map is given by a transformation
F:\Sigma\to\Sigma
, where

\Sigma=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x_{1}|\leq 1,\,|x_{2}|\leq 1,x_{3}=r-1\},

and has a discontinuity line
\Gamma\subset\Sigma
given by
x_{1}=0
, dividing
\Sigma
into the two subdomains
such that
F
is smooth in both subdomains. Strictly speaking, the transformation is not defined on the set
\Gamma
, but this is completely irrelevant for our purposes. It is well known that the return time
R:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
at a point
x=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{3})\in\Sigma
satisfies

R(x)\approx|\log|x_{0}||.

(1.4)

The return map
F
has been exhaustively studied by several authors from various perspectives. In particular, it is well known that it admits a stable invariant foliation by nearly vertical lines, which makes it possible to obtain some of its properties studying the one-dimensional transformation in the quotient space by the stable leaves. This strategy was particularly successful in obtaining a
u
-Gibbs measure
\mu
for
F
, which incidentally proves to be the unique physical measure for
F
, with its basin covering Lebesgue almost all of
\Sigma
. In the case of geometric Lorenz flows, the Poincaré return map
F
and the return time
R
enjoy these same properties by construction.

In this work we consider impulsive semiflows associated with Lorenz flows, having an impulsive map
\varphi:\Sigma\to M

close to the inclusion map, with the impulsive region
\Sigma
being the domain of a Poincaré return map as above. Allowing the impulsive map
\varphi
to send points
above

\Sigma
, it is relatively easy to think of examples for which the impulsive flow becomes essentially trivial. Indeed, consider a small
t_{0}>0
and the impulsive map
\varphi:\Sigma\to M
defined for each
x\in\Sigma
by

\varphi(x)=X_{-t_{0}}(x).

Since the trajectories under a Lorenz flow of Lebesgue almost all points in
M
hit the region
\Sigma
, it follows that the trajectories of Lebesgue almost all points in
M
enter a region where the flow is made up of periodic trajectories.
We therefore consider the impulsive maps having images in a flow box
below

\Sigma
, defined for some small
t_{0}>0
as

B^{+}(\Sigma)=\{X_{t}(z):0<t\leq t_{0},\;z\in\Sigma\}.

Let the inclusion map of
\Sigma
in
M
be denoted by
\operatorname{inc}_{\Sigma}
, and the space of
C^{1}
embeddings of
\Sigma
in
M
be denoted by
\operatorname{Emb}^{1}(\Sigma,M)
. Our first main result is the following theorem:

Theorem A
.

Let
X
be a Lorenz flow and
(M,X,\Sigma,\varphi)
be an impulsive dynamical system such that

\varphi(\Sigma)\subset B^{+}(\Sigma).

If
\varphi
is a
C^{2}
map sufficiently close to
\operatorname{inc}_{\Sigma}
in
\operatorname{Emb}^{1}(\Sigma,M)
, then the semiflow associated with
(M,X,\Sigma,\varphi)
has a finite number of physical measures whose basins cover Lebesgue almost all of
M
.

An interesting open problem is to investigate if the impulsive semiflow has a unique physical measure. Note that the uniqueness of the physical measure for Lorenz flows results from the uniqueness of the measure for the quotient transformation (because of transitivity), an instrument that we cannot use in our case. Uniqueness for the impulsive Lorenz semiflow will naturally follow from the uniqueness of the physical measures given by Corollary
3.4
for the Poincaré return map, something that is not guaranteed by the results in
[
35
,
36
]
.

A question that is of particular interest is whether the Lorenz flow is stable under small impulsive perturbations.
We say that the Lorenz flow
X
is
statistically stable by impulsive perturbations
on
\Sigma
if, for any sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n}
converging to
\operatorname{inc}_{\Sigma}
in the
C^{0}
topology and any sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n}
, where
\nu_{n}
is a physical measure for the impulsive flow of
(M,X,\Sigma,\varphi_{n})
,
we have
\nu_{n}\to\nu
in the weak* topology, as
n\to\infty
, where
\nu
is the physical measure for
X
. We say that
X
is
entropy stable by impulsive perturbations
on
\Sigma
if
h_{\nu_{n}}(Y_{n})\to h_{\nu}(X)
, as
n\to\infty
, where each
Y_{n}
is the impulsive semiflow of
(M,X,\Sigma,\varphi_{n})
and
h_{(\cdot)}(\cdot)
denotes the respective metric entropy. Our second main result is the following theorem:

Theorem B
.

Lorenz flows are statistically stable and entropy stable by impulsive perturbations on
\Sigma
.

(H1)

3.2.
Physical measures

In this subsection we lift each physical measure of the Poincaré map

to a physical measure of the impulsive semiflow

First of all, we define a class of functions that will help us in defining the lifting. Consider
\mathcal{F}
the class of bounded functions
\phi:M\to\mathbb{R}
such that,
for all
x\in\tilde{\Sigma}
, the function

\mathbb{R}\ni t\longmapsto\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)\in\mathbb{R}

has at most countably many discontinuity points. Clearly,
\mathcal{F}
contains all the compositions
\phi\circ Y_{s}
, with

and
\phi
continuous, which obviously includes the continuous functions.
Given an
F_{Y}
-invariant probability measure
\mu_{Y}
, set for
\phi\in\mathcal{F}
,

\nu_{Y}(\phi)=\frac{1}{\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}R_{Y}d\mu_{Y}}\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{R_{Y}(x)}\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x),

(3.10)

where

stands for the integral with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
, which is obviously well defined for all
\phi\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

\nu_{Y}
defines a probability measure on the Borel sets of
M
which is invariant under the semiflow
Y
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
\nu_{Y}
is a linear positive functional on the set of continuous functions and that
\nu_{Y}(1)=1
. It follows from Riesz-Markov Theorem that
\nu_{Y}
defines a probability measure on the Borel sets of
M
. The proof of the invariance of
\nu_{Y}
follows essentially as in the case of a continuous flow, we include it here for completeness. We need to check that

\int\phi\circ Y_{s}d\nu_{Y}=\int\phi\circ d\nu_{Y}

for all continuous
\phi
.
First of all, notice that the integral on the left hand side is well defined, since
\phi\circ Y_{s}\in\mathcal{F}
. Furthermore, setting
C=\left(\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}R_{Y}d\mu_{Y}\right)^{-1}
, we may write

\displaystyle\int\phi\circ Y_{s}d\nu_{Y}

\displaystyle=C\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{R_{Y}(x)}\phi\circ Y_{s}\circ Y_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x)

\displaystyle=C\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{R_{Y}(x)}\phi\circ Y_{s+t}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x)

\displaystyle=C\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{s}^{R_{Y}(x)+s}\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x)

\displaystyle=C\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{R_{Y}(x)}\!\!\!\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dt-\int_{0}^{s}\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dt+\int_{R_{Y}(x)}^{R_{Y}(x)+s}\!\!\!\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dt\right)d\mu_{Y}(x).

We are left to show that

\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{s}\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x)=\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{R_{Y}(x)}^{R_{Y}(x)+s}\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x).

(3.11)

Indeed, using the
F_{Y}
-invariance of
\mu_{Y}
we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{s}\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)d\mu_{Y}(x)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\phi\circ Y_{t}\circ F_{Y}(x)d\mu_{Y}(x)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{s}\phi\circ Y_{t}\circ F_{Y}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{s}\phi\circ Y_{t}\circ Y_{R_{Y}(x)}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{R_{Y}(x)}^{R_{Y}(x)+s}\phi\circ Y_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{Y}(x).

This gives (
3.11
), which concludes the proof.
∎

given by Corollary

Proposition 3.9
.

are physical measures for

Proof.

It is well known that with the exception of trajectories contained in the stable manifold of the singularity point 0, all other trajectories of points in the trapping region

. This implies that the trajectories of Lebesgue almost all points in

must intersect

. Taking into account the definition of the Poincaré map

for the impulsive semiflow

, we easily get that the trajectories of Lebesgue almost all points in

pass through
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Since the basin of a measure is invariant under the dynamics, it is enough to show that Lebesgue almost all points in
\Sigma^{\prime}
belong in the basin of one of the measures
\nu_{Y}^{i}
, for some
i=1,\dots,s
.

By Corollary
3.4
we know that Lebesgue almost all points in
\Sigma^{\prime}
belong in the basin of one of the ergodic measures
\mu_{Y}^{i}
, for some

.
Moreover, there exists a set
A_{i}
with
\tilde{\mu}^{i}_{Y}(A_{i})=1
such that Lebesgue almost every point in
\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{\mu}^{i}_{Y}}
belongs in the stable manifold of some point in
A_{i}
. Given a continuous function
\varphi:M\to\mathbb{R}
, consider
\varphi:\Sigma^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
given for

by

\hat{\varphi}(x)=\int_{0}^{R_{Y}(x)}\varphi(Y_{t}(x))dt.

By Corollary
3.7
and Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem we may assume that for
\mu_{Y}^{i}
almost every

we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}R_{Y}(F_{Y}^{j}(x))dt=\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}R_{Y}\,d\mu_{Y}^{i}

(3.12)

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat{\varphi}(F_{Y}^{j}(x))dt=\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\hat{\varphi}\,d\mu_{Y}^{i}.

(3.13)

Without loss of generality, we can also assume that

R_{Y}(F_{Y}^{n}(x))<\infty,\quad\text{for all $x\in A_{i}$ and $n\geq 0$.}

(3.14)

Given
x\in A_{i}
, set
T_{0}(x)=0
and

T_{n}(x)=R_{Y}(x)+R_{Y}(F_{Y}(x))+\cdots+R_{Y}(F^{n-1}(x)),\quad\text{for $n\geq 1$.}

Given
T>0
, consider

such that
T_{n-1}(x)<T\leq T_{n}(x)
. By (
3.14
), such an integer

always exists. Moreover,

T\to\infty\implies n\to\infty.

(3.15)

We may write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(Y_{t}(x))dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_{T_{j}(x)}^{T_{j+1}(x)}\!\!\!\varphi(Y_{t}(x))dt-\frac{1}{T}\int_{T}^{T_{n}(x)}\!\!\!\varphi(Y_{t}(x))dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_{0}^{R_{Y}(F_{Y}^{j}(x))}\!\!\!\varphi(Y_{t}(F_{Y}^{j}(x)))dt-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T_{n}(x)-T}\!\!\!\varphi(Y_{t}(F^{n}(x)))dt.

(3.16)

Using the definition of
\hat{\varphi}
, we get

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_{0}^{R_{Y}(F_{Y}^{j}(x))}\varphi(Y_{t}(F_{Y}^{j}(x)))dt=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat{\varphi}(F_{Y}^{j}(x))=\frac{n}{T}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat{\varphi}(F_{Y}^{j}(x)).

(3.17)

Now observe that

\frac{T_{n-1}(x)}{n}<\frac{T}{n}\leq\frac{T_{n}(x)}{n},

which together with (
3.12
) and (
3.15
) yields

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{T}{n}=\frac{1}{\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}R_{Y}\,d\mu_{Y}^{i}}.

(3.18)

Since the measure
\nu_{Y}^{i}
is related to
\mu_{Y}^{i}
through the formula in (
3.10
), it follows from (
3.13
) and (
3.18
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{n}{T}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat{\varphi}(F_{Y}^{j}(x))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}R_{Y}\,d\mu_{Y}^{i}}\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\hat{\varphi}\,d\mu_{Y}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}R_{Y}\,d\mu_{Y}^{i}}\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{R_{Y}(x)}\varphi(Y_{t}(x))dtd\mu_{Y}^{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\varphi d\nu_{Y}^{i}.

Recalling (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), we are left to show that

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T_{n}(x)-T}\!\!\!\varphi(Y_{t}(F^{n}(x)))dt=0.

(3.19)

Since

\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T_{n}(x)-T}\!\!\!\varphi(Y_{t}(F^{n}(x)))\right|\leq\frac{T_{n}(x)-T}{T}\sup|\varphi|\leq\frac{T_{n}(x)-T_{n-1}(x)}{T}\sup|\varphi|

it is enough to prove that

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{T_{n}(x)-T_{n-1}(x)}{T}=0

Indeed, writing

\frac{T_{n}(x)-T_{n-1}(x)}{T}=\frac{T_{n}(x)-T_{n-1}(x)}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{T},

and recalling (
3.18
), the conclusion follows from (
3.12
).
∎

4.1.
Statistical stability

In this subsection we obtain the statistical stability part of Theorem
B
.
Since
F
has a unique
u
-Gibbs measure, it
follows from Corollary
2.5
, Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
4.1
that

\tilde{\mu}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w*}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu,\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

(4.1)

Note that we may consider
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
as a measure on the set
\Sigma
, since
\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}\subset\Sigma
.
We are left to show that
\nu_{n}\to\nu
in the weak
∗
topology, as
n\to\infty
.
Since the measures
\nu
and
\nu_{n}
are given by (
3.1
) and (
3.10
) respectively, we just need to prove that

(1)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{n}}R_{n}d\mu_{n}\to\int_{\Sigma}Rd\mu
, as
n\to\infty
,

and for all continuous
\phi:M\to\mathbb{R}

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{n}^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{R_{n}(x)}\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{n}(x)\to\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dtd\mu(x)
, as
n\to\infty
.

This will be obtained in the Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
below. In the next lemma we give an auxiliary result which will be used several times below.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that the following conditions hold:

(c
1
)

there exists a sequence
(h_{n})_{n}
of continuous functions from
\Sigma
to
\mathbb{R}
converging
\mu
-almost everywhere to
h\in L^{1}(\mu)
, with
h\in L^{1}(\tilde{\mu}_{n})
, for all
n\geq 1
;

(c
2
)

there exists
g\in L^{1}(\mu)
such that
g\geq 0
and
|h_{n}|\leq g
, for all
n\geq 1
.

Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Sigma}hd\tilde{\mu}_{n}=\int_{\Sigma}hd\mu.

Proof.

We may write for any
n,k\geq 1

\int_{\Sigma}hd\tilde{\mu}_{n}=\int_{\Sigma}(h-h_{k})d\tilde{\mu}_{n}+\int_{\Sigma}h_{k}d\tilde{\mu}_{n}.

Since the densities of the conditionals of each
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
with respect to Lebesgue measure on local unstable manifolds are bounded from above and below by positive constants independent of
n
(recall Remark
2.2
), we may assume that the conditionals of
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
are those of Lebesgue measure in the first integral of the last sum. Since the conditionals of
\mu
with respect to Lebesgue measure on local unstable manifolds are also bounded from above and below by positive constants, it follows that
h_{k}
converges pointwise to
h
for Lebesgue almost every point on local unstable manifolds. Therefore,
\int_{\Sigma}(h-h_{k})d\tilde{\mu}_{n}
converges to zero, by dominated convergence theorem.
Hence, taking first limit in
n
and then limit in
k
in the second term, the conclusion follows by the weak* convergence in (
4.1
) and the dominated convergence theorem.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{n}}R_{n}d\mu_{n}\to\int_{\Sigma}Rd\mu
, as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

It follows from (
3.9
) that for all
n

R_{n}=R\circ\psi_{n}+t^{-}_{n},

which together with Corollary
3.4
yields

\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{n}}R_{n}d\mu_{n}=\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{n}}(R\circ\psi_{n}+t^{-}_{n})d(\psi_{n}^{-1})_{*}\tilde{\mu}_{n}=\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}}(R+t^{-}_{n}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1})d\tilde{\mu}_{n}.

Since
t^{-}_{n}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}
is uniformly small when
\varphi_{n}
converges to
\operatorname{inc}_{\Sigma}
in the
C^{0}
topology and the support of
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
is a subset of
\Sigma
, we just need to prove that

\int_{\Sigma}Rd\tilde{\mu}_{n}\to\int_{\Sigma}Rd\mu,\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

(4.2)

Set for each
k\geq 1

\hat{R}_{k}=\min\{R,k\}.

We conclude the proof using Lemma
4.2
with
h_{k}=\hat{R}_{k}
and
h=g=R
.
The conditions (c
1
) and (c
2
) are clearly verified, by Lemma
3.6
and recalling that
R
is integrable with respect to the measure
\mu
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For any continuous
\phi:M\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Sigma_{n}^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{R_{n}(x)}\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{n}(x)=\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dtd\mu(x).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{n}^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{R_{n}(x)}\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}(x)dtd\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma_{n}^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{R\circ\psi_{n}(x)+t^{-}_{n}(x)}\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}(x)dtd(\psi_{n}^{-1})_{*}\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}}\int_{0}^{R(x)+t^{-}_{n}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)}\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)

Since for any
x\in\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}

\int_{0}^{R(x)+t^{-}_{n}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)}\!\!\!\!\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)dt=\int_{0}^{R(x)}\!\!\!\!\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)dt+\int_{0}^{t^{-}_{n}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)}\!\!\!\!\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)dt

and
t^{-}_{n}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}
can be made uniformly small when
\varphi_{n}
converges to
\operatorname{inc}_{\Sigma}
in the
C^{0}
topology, we just need to prove that

\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)\to\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dtd\mu(x),\quad\text{when $n\to\infty$}.

Note that we may assume that
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
is a measure on
\Sigma
.
We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}

\displaystyle\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)-\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dtd\mu(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}(\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)-\phi\circ X_{t}(x))dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)

(4.3)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)-\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dtd\mu(x).

(4.4)

First we prove that the difference in (
4.4
) converges to 0 when
n\to\infty
. Indeed, set

h(x)=\int_{0}^{R(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dt

and for
k\geq 1
,

\hat{R}_{k}(x)=\min\{R(x),k\}\quad\text{and}\quad h_{k}(x)=\int_{0}^{\hat{R}_{k}(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dt.

We have

|h_{k}(x)|=\left|\int_{0}^{\hat{R}_{k}(x)}\phi\circ X_{t}(x)dt\right|\leq\int_{0}^{\hat{R}_{k}(x)}\left|\phi\circ X_{t}(x)\right|dt\leq C\hat{R}_{k}(x)\leq CR(x).

We conclude the proof using Lemma
4.2
with
g=CR
.
The conditions (c
1
) and (c
2
) are clearly verified, by Lemma
3.6
and recalling that
R
is integrable with respect to
\mu
.

We are left to prove that the term in (
4.3
) converges to 0 when
n\to\infty
. For simplicity, consider

g(t,x)=\phi\circ X_{t}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad g_{n}(t,x)=\phi\circ Y^{n}_{t}\circ\psi_{n}^{-1}(x),\quad\forall n\geq 1.

It is enough to show that

\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}|g_{n}(t,x)-g(t,x)|dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)\to 0

when
n\to\infty
.
Since
\hat{R}_{k}(x)\to R(x)
, when
k\to\infty
, for
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
-almost every
x
, by monotone convergence theorem

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{R(x)}|g_{n}(t,x)-g(t,x)|dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\hat{R}_{k}(x)}|g_{n}(t,x)-g(t,x)|dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{\hat{R}_{k}(x)}|g_{n}(t,x)-g(t,x)|dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x).

Since this last expression defines an increasing sequence in
k
of nonnegative numbers, we just need to show that each term in that sequence can be made arbitrarily small (for
n
sufficiently large).
So, fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. Given any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
n_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
(depending only on
k
) such that
for all
x\in\Sigma
and
0\leq t\leq\hat{R}_{k}(x)
(recall that
\hat{R}_{k}(x)\leq k
), we have

n\geq n_{\varepsilon}\implies|g_{n}(t,x)-g(t,x)|<\varepsilon.

Therefore, for any
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{\hat{R}_{k}(x)}|g_{n}(t,x)-g(t,x)|dtd\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}\hat{R}_{k}(x)d\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)\leq\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}R(x)d\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x).

Now, by (
4.2
), we may choose a uniform constant
C>0
such that
\int_{\Sigma}R(x)d\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)\leq C
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Entropy stability

In this subsection we obtain the entropy stability part of Theorem
B
.
Similar to (
3.2
), we have by Abramov formula

h_{\nu_{n}}(Y_{n})=\frac{h_{\mu_{n}}(F_{n})}{\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{n}}R_{n}d\mu_{n}}.

(4.5)

By (
3.2
) and (
4.5
), it is enough to prove that

\frac{h_{\mu_{n}}(F_{n})}{\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{n}}R_{n}d\mu_{n}}\longrightarrow\frac{h_{\mu}(F)}{\int_{\Sigma}Rd\mu},\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

Since
(F_{n},\mu_{n})
and
(\tilde{F}_{n},\tilde{\mu}_{n})
are isomorphic dynamical systems, by Lemma
4.3
we just need to prove that

h_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}(\tilde{F}_{n})\to h_{\mu}(F),\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

(4.6)

It follows from Theorem
2.1
, Theorem
2.3
and Lemma
3.2
that the entropy formula holds for both dynamical systems
(\tilde{F}_{n},\tilde{\mu}_{n})
and
(F,\mu)
. Therefore, the convergence in (
4.6
) can be rephrased as

\int_{\Sigma}\log|\tilde{J}^{u}_{n}|d\tilde{\mu}_{n}\to\int_{\Sigma}\log|J^{u}|d\mu,\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

(4.7)

where
J_{n}^{u}
and
J^{u}
stand for the unstable Jacobians of
\tilde{F}_{n}
and
F
, respectively.

Lemma 4.5
.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Sigma}\log|\tilde{J}^{u}_{n}|d\tilde{\mu}_{n}=\int_{\Sigma}\log|J^{u}|d\mu
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\log|\tilde{J}^{u}_{n}|d\tilde{\mu}_{n}-

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\log|J^{u}|d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\log|\tilde{J}^{u}_{n}|-\log|J^{u}|\right)d\tilde{\mu}_{n}+\int_{\Sigma}\log|J^{u}|d\tilde{\mu}_{n}-\int_{\Sigma}\log|J^{u}|d\mu.

(4.8)

We first prove that the integral of the difference above converges to zero when
n\to\infty
.
By Hadamard’s inequality and (H1), we have for all
x

|\tilde{J}^{u}_{n}(x)|\leq\|d_{x}F_{n}\|\leq A\rho(x,\Gamma)^{-\alpha};

(4.9)

recall that the constant
A
may be chosen uniform, by Lemma
4.1
.
Since the densities of the conditionals of each
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
with respect to Lebesgue measure on local unstable manifolds are bounded from above and below by positive constants independent of
n
(recall Remark
2.2
), we may assume that the conditionals of
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
are those of Lebesgue measure.
Moreover, it follows from (S5) that
\log|\tilde{J}^{u}_{n}|
converges pointwise to
\log|\tilde{J}^{u}|
, Lebesgue almost everywhere. Since
\log\rho(x,\Gamma)^{-\alpha}
is integrable with respect to Lebesgue measure, using (
4.9
) and dominated convergence theorem we conclude that the integral of the difference in (
4.8
) converges to zero when
n\to\infty
.

We are left to show that the difference of the integrals in (
4.8
) goes to zero when
{n\to\infty}
. Decomposing both integrals into integrals on
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, and noticing that
\log|J^{u}|
is continuous on both
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, the conclusion follows by the weak* convergence of
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
to
\mu
given by (
4.1
).
∎
