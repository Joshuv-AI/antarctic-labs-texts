Dynamics and steady state of squirmer motion in liquid crystal
Leonid Berlyand, Hai Chi, Mykhailo Potomkin, Nung Kwan Yip

Abstract
We analyze a nonlinear PDE system describing the motion of a microswimmer in a nematic liquid crystal environment. For the microswimmer’s motility, the squirmer model is used in which self-propulsion enters the model through the slip velocity on the microswimmer’s surface. The liquid crystal is described using the well-established Beris-Edwards formulation. In previous computational studies, it was shown that the squirmer, regardless of its initial configuration, eventually orients itself either parallel or perpendicular to the preferred orientation dictated by the liquid crystal. Furthermore, the corresponding solution of the coupled nonlinear system converges to a steady state. In this work, we rigourously establish the existence of steady state and also the finite-time existence for the time-dependent problem in a periodic domain. Finally, we will use a two-scale asymptotic expansion to derive a homogenized model for the collective swimming of squirmers as they reach their steady state orientation and speed.



Keywords
: Beris-Edwards model, squirmer, existence of solutions, two-scale analysis.

2
Model

2.1
Time dependent PDE system

Consider a rigid squirmer swimming in a liquid crystal with translational and angular velocities
{\boldsymbol{V}}(t)
and
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(t)
, respectively.
In the context of the Beris-Edwards model, the liquid crystal is described by
a velocity field
{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}

and a tensor order field
{\boldsymbol{Q}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
taking values in symmetric traceless
d\times d
matrices.
Here
d=2,3
is the spatial dimension.
The functions
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}=(Q_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}
satisfy the following system of partial differential equations and boundary conditions, written in the frame moving with velocity
{\boldsymbol{V}}(t)
, so the squirmer is always centered at
0
:

\displaystyle\rho(\partial_{t}+{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{u}}+\rho\frac{d{\boldsymbol{V}}}{dt}=\nabla\cdot(\sigma_{\text{hydro}}+\sigma_{\text{ela}}),\text{ in }\Pi\setminus{\mathcal{P}}(t)

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}=0,\text{ in }\Pi\setminus{\mathcal{P}}(t),

(2)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}=u_{\text{sq}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t),{\boldsymbol{x}})\boldsymbol{\tau}+{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(t)\times{\boldsymbol{x}},\text{ on }\partial{\mathcal{P}}(t),

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{Q}}+({\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{Q}}-S(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})=\Gamma\left(K\Delta{\boldsymbol{Q}}+\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})\right)+F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}),\text{ in }\Pi\setminus{\mathcal{P}}(t),

(4)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla p\quad\text{periodic in}\,\Pi

(5)

\displaystyle K\partial_{\nu}{\boldsymbol{Q}}=W({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}})\text{ on }\partial{\mathcal{P}}(t).

(6)

Here
\Pi=(-L,L)^{d}
is a periodic box,

{\mathcal{P}}(t)
and
\partial{\mathcal{P}}(t)
are the domain occupied by the squirmer
and its surface in the moving frame.
We assume that
\overline{\mathcal{P}(t)}\subset\Pi
for all
t\geq 0
. We will also use the notation
\Omega(t):=\Pi\setminus\mathcal{P}(t)

to denote the fluid region
.

Equation (
1
) is a modified Navier-Stokes equation for the
velocity
{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
which satisfies the divergence-free condition
(
2
). To this effect, we have

\sigma_{\text{hydro}}=\eta(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\text{T}})-p\mathbb{I}

to be the standard isotropic stress tensor
where
p({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
is the pressure of the liquid crystal with
uniform density
\rho
and viscosity
\eta
.
The internal structure of the liquid crystal, i.e., local preferred direction and order, affects the flow through an additional elastic stress
\sigma_{\text{ela}}
given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{ela}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\left[({\boldsymbol{Q}}\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{Q}}-\Delta{\boldsymbol{Q}}\,{\boldsymbol{Q}})-\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}\astrosun\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right]

(7)

\displaystyle-\xi\left[{\bf H}({\boldsymbol{Q}}+\dfrac{\mathbb{I}}{d})+({\boldsymbol{Q}}+\dfrac{\mathbb{I}}{d}){\bf H}-2({\boldsymbol{Q}}+\frac{\mathbb{I}}{d})\text{Tr}({\boldsymbol{Q}}{\bf H})\right].

Here
K
is the elastic constant and
\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}\astrosun\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}
is a
d\times d
matrix with the
(k,l)
component
\sum\limits_{i,j}\partial_{x_{k}}Q_{ij}\partial_{x_{l}}Q_{ij}
. The parameter
\xi
measures the ratio between
tumbling and aligning that a shear flow exerts on the liquid crystal molecules. The matrix-valued function
{\bf H}={\bf H}({\boldsymbol{Q}})
is defined as
{\bf H}({\boldsymbol{Q}})=\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})+K\Delta{\boldsymbol{Q}}
where
\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})
is

\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}}):=a{\boldsymbol{Q}}-c{\boldsymbol{Q}}\text{Tr}({\boldsymbol{Q}}^{2})=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Q}}\left(\dfrac{c}{4}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|^{4}-\dfrac{a}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|^{2}\right)=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Q}}\hat{\mathcal{F}}({\boldsymbol{Q}}).

(8)

The scalar potential
\hat{\mathcal{F}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})
is the polynomial part of the Landau-de Gennes free energy whose coefficients
a
and
c
depend on macroscopic parameters of the liquid crystal such as temperature. The potential
\hat{\mathcal{F}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})
attains minima at
{\boldsymbol{Q}}={\bf 0}
, corresponding to the isotropic state when the liquid crystal flows as a Newtonian fluid, and at tensor order parameters
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
with
q_{\infty}:=\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|=\sqrt{\dfrac{a}{c}}
, corresponding to the equilibrium liquid crystalline states.

Boundary conditions (
3
) describes how the squirmer interacts with the flow
{\boldsymbol{u}}
of the liquid crystal.
The orientation of the squrimer is described by a vector

\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
.
We also let
\boldsymbol{\tau}
to be a tangent vector field to the surface of the squirmer which can be chosen to be

\boldsymbol{\tau}:=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\times\boldsymbol{\nu})\times\boldsymbol{\nu}
where
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is the inward normal vector on the squirmer’s surface
\partial\mathcal{P}(t)
. A typical example of the slip velocity
u_{\text{sq}}
is given by
[
25
]
(which is also used in the
computational work
[
38
,
39
]
)

u_{\text{sq}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t),\boldsymbol{x})=v_{\text{prop}}\sin\theta(1+\beta\cos(\theta)),\text{ where }\theta=\cos^{-1}\left[\dfrac{\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}\right].

Here
\theta
is the azimuthal angle on the squirmer, the parameter
v_{\text{prop}}
is proportional to propulsion strength, and
\beta
quantifies the type of the squirmer (puller vs pusher; see
[
39
]
for details). In this work, we consider
u_{\text{sq}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t),\boldsymbol{x})=\sin(\theta)g(\theta)
with a smooth function
g(\theta)
. Note that such
u_{\text{sq}}
vanishes at points of singularity of the vector field
\boldsymbol{\tau}
.

The instantaneous angular velocity of the squirmer is denoted by
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(t)
.
Then any material point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
on the squirmer surface
\partial\mathcal{P}(t)

will move with velocity
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(t)\times{\boldsymbol{x}}
in the moving frame for which the system (
1
)-(
6
) is written. Then the boundary condition (
3
) states that there is a given slip velocity
u_{\text{sq}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t),{\boldsymbol{x}})\boldsymbol{\tau}
with the no-penetration condition:

\displaystyle\left[{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]\times\boldsymbol{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{\text{sq}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t),{\boldsymbol{x}})\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\boldsymbol{\nu},

\displaystyle\left[{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

The given non-zero slip velocity models self-propulsion of the squirmer. Such a condition was originally derived for micro-organisms swimming with the help of small elastic appendages (cilia) distributed on the surface
[
25
]
.

The matrix-valued equation (
4
) describes the dynamics of

{\boldsymbol{Q}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
. While the two first terms in the left-hand side of (
4
) is the advection derivative, the third term
S(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
describes how the flow gradient
\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}
rotates and stretches the order-parameter
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, and is given by

\displaystyle S(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})=(\xi{\boldsymbol{D}}+{\boldsymbol{A}})\left({\boldsymbol{Q}}+\frac{\mathbb{I}}{d}\right)+\left({\boldsymbol{Q}}+\frac{\mathbb{I}}{d}\right)(\xi{\boldsymbol{D}}-{\boldsymbol{A}})-2\xi\left({\boldsymbol{Q}}+\frac{\mathbb{I}}{d}\right)\text{tr}({\boldsymbol{Q}}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}),

(9)

where
{\boldsymbol{D}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\text{T}}\right]
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\text{T}}\right]
are symmetric and anti-symmetric parts of
\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}
, respectively. The right-hand side of (
4
) consists of the term leading to the minimization of the total Landau-de Gennes energy

\mathcal{E}_{\text{LdG}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})=\int\limits_{\Omega(t)}\hat{\mathcal{F}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})+\frac{K}{2}|\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}\,\text{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}

(10)

with the relaxation parameter
\Gamma>0
and the term
F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})
describing the aligning effect with an external field. This term imposes the equilibrium condition for liquid crystal, that is, in the absence of squirmer we have
{\boldsymbol{Q}}\equiv{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}
. We chose
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}=q_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{e}_{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{x}-\frac{\mathbb{I}}{d})
which means that if the liquid crystal is not perturbed by a squirmer then its molecules are oriented parallel to
\boldsymbol{e}_{x}
(the unit basis vector parallel to
x
-axis). In this work, we will use the example of
F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})
from
[
21
]
for
d=2
, given by

F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})=-\zeta{\boldsymbol{Q}}R_{\pi/2}\text{Tr}[{\boldsymbol{Q}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}R_{\pi/2}],

(11)

where
\zeta\geq 0
is the aligning parameter and
R_{\pi/2}
is the matrix of counterclockwise rotation by
\pi/2
. For
d=3
, the formula for
F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})
is

F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})=\zeta\left(\text{tr}({\boldsymbol{Q}}^{2}){\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}-\text{tr}({\boldsymbol{Q}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}){\boldsymbol{Q}}\right)

(12)

We note that if one considers dynamics
\dot{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}=F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})
then the Euclidean norm of
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
is preserved,
i.e.

\text{tr}({\boldsymbol{Q}}^{2})\equiv\text{const}
, and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}(t)
converges to a multiple of
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}
as
t
increases, so that
{\boldsymbol{Q}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}=\text{tr}({\boldsymbol{Q}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})>0
. One can also show that (
12
) is equivalent to (
11
) in the case of two-dimensional
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}
(with zero third row and column).

We impose anchoring boundary condition (
6
) on
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
along the squirmer surface
\partial\mathcal{P}(t)
which forces
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
to be close to a given tensor
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}=q_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{pref}}\otimes\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{pref}}-\frac{{\mathbb{I}}}{d})
. Here,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{pref}}=\boldsymbol{\nu}
in the case of homeotropic anchoring when the surface orients liquid crystal molecules perpendicular to it or equivalently, parallel to the normal vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
. On the other hand,

\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{pref}}=\boldsymbol{\tau}
in the case of the planar anchoring when molecules are aligned with the tangential vector field
\boldsymbol{\tau}
. The boundary condition (
6
) indeed penalizes the difference
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}
in the sense that if we drop all terms in (
4
) except
\Gamma\left(K\Delta{\boldsymbol{Q}}+\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})\right)
, then the solution
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
to this truncated version of (
4
) with boundary condition (
6
) minimizes
the energy

\mathcal{E}_{\text{LdG}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})+W\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}(t)}|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(13)

The coefficient
W
in front of the penalization term in the energy functional (
13
) and also the right-hand side of
(
6
) measures the anchoring strength. Mathematically,
depending on if
W\to\infty
or
0
, (
6
) reduces to
Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition for
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
.

To determine the trajectory of the squirmer, that is, its velocity
\boldsymbol{V}(t)
and angular velocity
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(t)
, we consider force and torque balances for the squirmer:

\displaystyle m\frac{\text{d}{\boldsymbol{V}}}{\text{d}t}=\int\limits_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}(t)}\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}\left[I(t)\boldsymbol{\omega}\right]}{\text{d}t}=\int\limits_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}(t)}{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\ell}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(15)

Here
\sigma=\sigma_{\text{hydro}}+\sigma_{\text{ela}}
is the total stress whereas
m
and
I(t)=\left\{I_{ij}\right\}_{i,j=1}^{d}
are the mass and inertia tensor of the squirmer, defined via

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathcal{P}}|\mathcal{P}(t)|,

\displaystyle I_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathcal{P}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{P}(t)}\left[\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\times{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]\cdot\left[\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\times{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]\,\text{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

Here
\rho_{\mathcal{P}}
is the squirmer’s density. The additional torque
\boldsymbol{\ell}
comes from the internal structure of the liquid crystal, namely, from that there is a preferred direction. It translates into the non-zero asymmetric part of the stress tensor
\sigma
. The formula for this additional torque is
[
39
]

\boldsymbol{\ell}=\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\nu},\text{ where }\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}\text{ and }\mu_{ij}=-2K\sum\limits_{m,l,k=1}^{d}\epsilon_{ilk}Q_{lm}Q_{mk,j}.

(16)

Here
\epsilon_{ilk}
is the Levi-Civita symbol. Finally, we note that the orientation
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)
and the angular velocity
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
are related via

\dot{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times\boldsymbol{\alpha}.

(17)

Remark 2.1
.

Note that the term
\ell
admits a simplified form:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\ell_{i}\,\text{d}S_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2K\sum\limits_{l,m,k=1}^{d}\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{ilk}Q_{lm}Q_{mk,j}\nu_{j}\,\text{d}S_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2W\sum\limits_{l,m,k=1}^{d}\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{ilk}Q_{lm}(Q_{\text{pref},mk}-Q_{mk})\,\text{d}S_{x}.

Here we used boundary conditions (
6
). Next, for any symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}
we have

\sum\limits_{l,m,k}\epsilon_{ilk}B_{lm}B_{mk}=0.

(18)

Indeed, from properties of the Levi-Civita symbol we have

\sum\limits_{l,m,k}\epsilon_{ilk}B_{lm}B_{mk}=-\sum\limits_{l,m,k}\epsilon_{ilk}B_{km}B_{ml}.

On the other hand, due to symmetry of
\boldsymbol{B}
we have

\sum\limits_{l,m,k}\epsilon_{ilk}B_{lm}B_{mk}=\sum\limits_{l,m,k}\epsilon_{ilk}B_{km}B_{ml}.

Thus, we have (
18
), from which we have the simplified form expression (simplified because it is linear in
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
as opposed to (
16
) which is quadratic in
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
):

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\ell_{i}\,\text{d}S_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2W\sum\limits_{l,m,k=1}^{d}\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{ilk}Q_{lm}Q_{\text{pref},mk}\,\text{d}S_{x}.

(19)

Remark 2.2
.

We end the introduction of the time-dependent problem with the energy identity satisfied by solutions of this problem.
First, consider the energy functional:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{m\boldsymbol{V}^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{I\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{2}+\dfrac{\rho}{2}\int\limits_{\Omega(t)}|\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{V}|^{2}\,\text{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}

(20)

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\Omega(t)}\hat{\mathcal{F}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})+\frac{K}{2}|\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}\,\text{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}+\dfrac{W}{2}\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}(t)}|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\mathrm{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}.

Note that in the absence of the squirmer
\mathcal{P}(t)=\emptyset

(or when the squirmer is passive,
i.e.,

u_{\mathrm{sq}}=0
)
and if the external field
F_{\mathrm{ext}}
equals zero,
then the system is dissipative, that is, the energy is non-increasing:

\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathcal{E}(t)=-\mathcal{D}(t)\leq 0,\quad\text{where }\mathcal{D}(t):=\eta\int\limits_{\Omega(t)}|\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}\,\text{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}+\Gamma\int\limits_{\Omega(t)}|{\bf H}|^{2}\,\text{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(21)

On the other hand, when the system experiences the energy input from the self-propulsion mechanism and external field
F_{\mathrm{ext}}
,
the energy identity takes the following form:

\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathcal{E}(t)=-\mathcal{D}(t)+\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}(t)}\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot u_{\text{sq}}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\int\limits_{\Omega(t)}{\bf H}:F_{\mathrm{ext}}\,\text{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(22)

Note that boundary integral
\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}(t)}\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot u_{\text{sq}}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}

contains nonlinear terms in
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
which in turn depends on the higher order
regularity property of
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
.
This causes difficulty in the analysis of the time-dependent problem.
Hence in this paper, we will only present a short time existence result and
leave the long time behavior to future work.

2.2
Steady state PDE system

In this work, we are also interested in the steady translational motion of the squirmer in the liquid crystal. In the context of the model (
1
)-(
6
) & (
14
)-(
15
), the steady motion is described by the stationary solution of this system:

\displaystyle\rho{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}=\nabla\cdot(\sigma_{\text{hydro}}+\sigma_{\text{ela}}),\text{ in }\Pi\setminus{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}},

(23)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}=0,\text{ in }\Pi\setminus{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}},

(24)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}=u_{\text{sq}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{st}},{\boldsymbol{x}})\boldsymbol{\tau},\text{ on }\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}},

(25)

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{Q}}-S(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})=\Gamma\left(K\Delta{\boldsymbol{Q}}+\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})\right)+F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}),\text{ in }\Pi\setminus{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}},

(26)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla p\quad\text{periodic in}\,\Pi

(27)

\displaystyle K\partial_{\nu}{\boldsymbol{Q}}=W({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}})\text{ on }\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}}.

(28)

Here, we assume that the squirmer moves with the velocity
V_{\text{st}}

with the orientation angle
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{st}}
,
both of which are independent of time. As equations
(
23
)-(
28
) are written in the squirmer’s frame,
the domain
\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}
occupied by the squirmer will then
be stationary. Similar to the time dependent case, we use

\Omega=\Pi\setminus\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}
to denote the fluid region in the
steady state case.

In this setting, the force and torque balances (
14
),
(
15
) become

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}=\int\limits_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}}}\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}},

(29)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}=\int\limits_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\ell}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(30)

The force balance (
29
), in view of periodic boundary conditions for
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
together with
{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}=0
on
\partial P_{\text{st}}
(follows from (
25
)), leads to

\displaystyle 0=\int\limits_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}}}\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\int\limits_{\partial\Pi}\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\rho\int\limits_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}\,\text{d}x=-\int\limits_{\partial\Pi}p\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\text{d}S_{x}.

(31)

Therefore, since
\nabla p

In this case, the force balance (

.

We note that if an external force
\textbf{F}^{(e)}=\left\{F^{(e)}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{d}

\int\limits_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}}}\sigma\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\textbf{F}^{(e)}=0

-\int\limits_{\partial\Pi}p\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\textbf{F}^{(e)}=0.

(34)

Using (

|\Pi|\boldsymbol{m}=\textbf{F}^{(e)}.

(35)

Therefore, an external force results in the pressure difference

F^{(e)}_{i}=\dfrac{L}{2}[p]_{i},\,i=1,...,d,\quad\text{where }[p]_{i}=p|_{x_{i}=L}-p|_{x_{i}=-L}.

(36)

{\boldsymbol{u}}=-\boldsymbol{V}_{\text{st}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}={\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty}
. Then
we would have obtained a Stokes-law-like force-velocity relation instead of the force-pressure relation (
36

In this work, the squirmer swims due to self-propulsion only, without an external force,
\textbf{F}^{(e)}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Thus, we impose periodicity for the
pressure

)-(
28

({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})

\displaystyle\eta\int\limits_{\Omega}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}\,\text{d}x+\rho\int\limits_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}\,\text{d}x+\int\limits_{\Omega}\sigma_{\text{ela}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}\,\text{d}x=0.

(37)

Suppose that

4.1

Here we adapt the strategy from

First, we introduce the number

whereas

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
be a solution of (
23
)-(
28

Then
\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\alpha
, where

\alpha:=\max\left\{|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\mathrm{pref}}|,q_{*}\right\}.

Proof.

Recall the equation for
{\boldsymbol{Q}}

({\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{Q}}-S(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})-K\Gamma\Delta{\boldsymbol{Q}}-\Gamma\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})-F_{\text{ext}}({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})=0.

By multiplying the above by
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, taking the trace of the resulting expression
and using (
40

\dfrac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2})-\dfrac{\Gamma K}{2}\left(\Delta(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2})-2|\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}\right)-\Gamma\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}}):{\boldsymbol{Q}}=0.

is non-negative, we obtain the inequality:

{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2})-\Gamma K\Delta(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2})-2\Gamma\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}}):{\boldsymbol{Q}}\leq 0.

(51)

Now introduce
\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}}):=(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}-\alpha^{2})_{+}
(
\alpha
is from (
49
)). Note that

\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\mathcal{D}(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2})=\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\mathcal{D}(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}-\alpha^{2})=\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\mathcal{D}\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}}),

where

is either
\Delta
or
\nabla
.

Next, we multiply (
51
) by
\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})
and integrate
over
\Pi\setminus\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}}{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nu\psi^{2}\,\text{d}s_{x}-\Gamma K\int\limits_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}}\dfrac{\partial\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})}{\partial\nu}\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\text{d}s_{x}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt+\Gamma K\|\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-2\Gamma\int\limits_{\Omega}(\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}}):{\boldsymbol{Q}})\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\text{d}x\leq 0.

(52)

The first term in the left hand side of the above inequality vanishes
due to (
25
) while the second term is negative:

\displaystyle K\int_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}}\dfrac{\partial\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})}{\partial\nu}\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\text{d}s_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2K\int_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}\cap\{|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|>\alpha\}}(\dfrac{\partial{\boldsymbol{Q}}}{\partial\nu}:{\boldsymbol{Q}})(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}-\alpha^{2})\,\text{d}s_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2W\int_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}\cap\{|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|>\alpha\}}(({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}):{\boldsymbol{Q}})(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}-\alpha^{2})\,\text{d}s_{x}

\displaystyle 2W\int_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}\cap\{|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|>\alpha\}}(({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}:{\boldsymbol{Q}})-|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2})(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}-\alpha^{2})\,\text{d}s_{x}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle W\int_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}\cap\{|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|>\alpha\}}(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}|^{2}-|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2})(|{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}-\alpha^{2})\,\text{d}s_{x}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0.

Hence,

K\|\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2\int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}}):{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right)\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\text{d}x.

Next, by (
48
), we have

\displaystyle K\|\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\hat{{\bf H}}({\boldsymbol{Q}}):{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right)\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\text{d}x\leq 0.

so that
\|\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{Q}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
. The Lemma is thus
proved.

7
Concluding remarks

In this work, we initiated the theoretical justification of the active microswimmer model, also known as a squirmer, in a liquid crystal. This model has been recently developed to explore a non-trivial response of the microswimmer to surrounding environment possessing a liquid crystalline structure.
As computational studies
[
38
,
39
]
clearly show that the squirmer eventually converges to an equilibrium, both time-dependent solutions and steady states are important and were in the focus of our work. In investigating well-posedness of the corresponding equations, we started with combining techniques from the two fields, the squirmer in Newtonian fluids and the Beris-Edwards model of liquid crystal. However, such a combination is not straightforward. As explained in Remark
2.2
, one of the main difficulties, besides that our model is complex and highly nonlinear, is that it is not dissipative: there is a permanent energy input (not necessarily constant)
coming from the activity of the squirmer. It makes application of a priori energy bounds established for the Beris-Edwards model not possible here. Therefore, the considered model requires novel approaches for its analysis.
For the steady state problem, using suitable offset functions,
we first proved the existence of a steady state for a truncated system via
Galerkin approximations and careful energy bounds using specific properties of the Beris-Edwards system (see, e.g., (
76
)),
and then extended the well-posedness from the truncated system to the original
one using the
L^{\infty}
result formulated in Lemma
49
.
For the time-dependent problem, in order to exploit the contraction mapping
principle, we considered higher regularity solutions (instead of weak ones)
which allowed us to obtain all the necessary bounds including the one for
integrals where activity of squirmer enters as well as the force and torque
balances for the squirmer (see, e.g., (
172
) & (
173
)).
Periodic settings, in which we considered our model, allowed us to pose a question of homogenization limit which would be a model describing a colony of synchronously moving squirmers. We found a scaling which, on the one hand, is consistent with experimental data (see Appendix
C
) and, on the other hand, allows for a non-trivial two-scale expansions so that the homogenized limit takes the form of Darcy’s law perturbed by an algebraic expression for the liquid crystal order parameter (see equations (
192
) and (
203
)).

Natural extensions of our work are:

(i)

Stability analysis of steady states.
Namely, we would like to find conditions on parameters when a steady state corresponding to swimming either parallel or perpendicular to the liquid crystal is stable. This analytical result will be compared with the main observation from
[
39
]
on bifurcations with respect to anchoring strength parameter
W
. It would be also important to show that there is no steady state other than corresponding to swimming parallel or perpendicular to the liquid crystal.

(ii)

Force-velocity relation for steady swimming.
Though in the squirmer’s frame and periodic conditions, squirmer’s velocity is not well-defined, we can consider the so-called superficial velocity
[
50
]

\boldsymbol{V}=-\fint_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{u}}\,\text{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}
, which can be understood as the velocity of the squirmer with respect to the surrounding flow, and show how it depends on propulsion force entering the problem via
u_{\text{sq}}
. Specifically, given the profile of the active slip velocity
u_{\text{sq}}
(with all other physical parameters fixed), what is the resulting velocity
\boldsymbol{V}
? This question is related to the evaluation of the squirmer efficiency in Stokes fluid as a function of
u_{\text{sq}}

[
28
,
32
]
.

(iii)

Rigorous justification of the homogenization limit.
We plan to justify the two-scale limit formally derived in Section
6
and in more general stochastic settings based on techniques developed for Newtonian fluids
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
.

{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}
and a function
\psi\in H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
:

\int\limits_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}\psi\,\text{d}x=\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n_{k}}\psi\nu_{i}\,\text{d}S_{x}-\int\limits_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{i}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n_{k}}\psi\,\text{d}x\to 0,\quad 1\leq i\leq d.

The convergence to
0
follows from strong convergences of
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n_{k}}
in
L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}})
and
\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n_{k}}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
which in turn follow from (
206
). Then

\int\limits_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}\partial_{x_{i}}\psi\,\text{d}x=0\quad\forall\psi\in H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega).

Using integration by parts we get

\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}\psi\nu_{i}\,\text{d}S_{x}-\int\limits_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{i}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}\psi\,\text{d}x=0\quad\forall\psi\in H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega).

By taking first
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
we get
\partial_{x_{i}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}\equiv 0
so the second integral in the equality above vanishes. Next, we get that the first integral is zero as well by taking
\psi
with various non-zero traces. We conclude
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}\equiv 0
. Moreover, since for any weakly converging sub-sequence the limit is
0
, then entire sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
weakly converges to
0
.

Note that
H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
and thus
H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n}
is strongly converging in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and since the weak limit is
0
, we conclude
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n}\to 0
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. However, it contradicts to (
206
) since it implies that if
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n}\to{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, then
\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=1
. Thus, inequality (
205
) is shown.

Finally, to prove (
204
) take
c_{P}=\dfrac{2\hat{c}_{P}}{\min\{K,W\}}
.
∎

Next we turn to the proof of Lemma
4.2
. We note that an equivalent way to define
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{m}
from (
63
) is via minimization of the following energy functional:

\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{Q}})=\int\limits_{\Omega}\dfrac{K}{2}|\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}+\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{M}({\boldsymbol{Q}})\,\text{d}x+\dfrac{W}{2}\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}}|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}|^{2}\,\text{d}S_{x}+\int\limits_{\Omega}{\bf H}_{m}:{\boldsymbol{Q}}\,\text{d}x

(207)

among functions
{\boldsymbol{Q}}\in H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
. The minimizer
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
of the energy functional
\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{Q}})
exists.

From (
204
), the Cauchy inequality, and
\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}\|^{2}\geq\dfrac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|^{2}-\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}\|^{2}
we obtain that
\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{Q}})
is bounded from below:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{Q}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\dfrac{K}{2}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\dfrac{W}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}})}-\dfrac{1}{2c_{P}}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\dfrac{c_{P}}{2}\|{\bf H}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\dfrac{W}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}})}-\dfrac{c_{P}}{2}\|{\bf H}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Thus, there exists a minimizing sequence
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{(\ell)}
weakly converging in
H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
. Then using the
\liminf
property of a weakly converging sequences we get the existence of minimizer
{\boldsymbol{Q}}={\boldsymbol{Q}}_{m}
.

From
\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{m})\leq\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\infty})
and (
204
) we get that there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\dfrac{K}{8}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\dfrac{1}{8c_{P}}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\dfrac{W}{4}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}})}\leq 3c_{P}\|{\bf H}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C.

(208)

This shows (
64
). Next, due to the elliptic regularity result (see Appendix
B
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta{\boldsymbol{Q}}=K^{-1}({\bf H}_{m}-\hat{{\bf H}}_{M}({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{m}))\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}{\boldsymbol{Q}}|_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{st}}}=WK^{-1}({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}})\end{array}\right.

we have

\quad\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\left(\dfrac{(W+K)^{2}}{W^{2}}\|{\bf H}_{m}-\hat{{\bf H}}_{M}({\boldsymbol{Q}}_{m})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\dfrac{W+K}{K}\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\text{pref}}\|_{C^{1}}^{2}\right).

(209)

Next, using (
62
) we get (
65
) and it completes the proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Appendix B
Elliptic regularity for the squirmer boundary conditions

Here, we consider the following auxiliary elliptic problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta q=F\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}q|_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}}=\beta(\gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})-q),\\
q\text{ is }\Pi\text{-periodic.}\end{array}\right.

(210)

Here
F=F({\boldsymbol{x}})
,
\gamma=\gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
\beta>0
are given.
We aim to prove the elliptic regularity for (
210
):

Theorem B.1
.

Let
q
be the solution of (
210
). Then

\|q\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\left(\frac{(1+\beta)^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+(1+\beta)\|\gamma\|_{C^{1}}^{2}\right)

(211)

for some constant
C
independent of
\beta
and
\gamma
.

This result is well-known from PDE textbooks
[
51
, Theorem 8.12]
and
[
48
, Theorem 4 in §6.3.2]
for the Dirichlet boundary conditions. However, we need to re-visit this result due to our specific boundary conditions for which the afore-mentioned results are not applicable. For the sake of clarity, the proof below is written for two-dimensional case,
d=2
.

Proof.

We first address a priori estimates for regions near the boundary
\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}
of the squirmer. Choose any point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
on
\partial\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}
. Suppose its vicinity on the boundary can be described by equation
x_{2}=\varphi(x_{1})
so the domain
x_{2}>\varphi(x_{1})
is the interior of domain
\Omega
. Then introduce change of variables

{\boldsymbol{y}}=\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})\Leftrightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{1}=x_{1},\\
y_{2}=x_{2}-\varphi(x_{1}).\end{array}\right.

(212)

In variable
{\boldsymbol{y}}
, the problem (
210
) has the form:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\cdot(L({\boldsymbol{y}})\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}q)=F,\quad y_{2}>0\\
L({\boldsymbol{y}})\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}q\cdot\nu_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\beta\sqrt{1+(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}}\,(\gamma-q),\quad y_{2}=0,\end{array}\right.

where

L=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-\varphi^{\prime}\\
-\varphi^{\prime}&(1+(\varphi^{\prime})^{2})\end{array}\right],\quad\nu_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right].

We note that
L
is a positive definite symmetric matrix with the smallest eigenvalue

\lambda_{\text{min}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(2+(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}-\sqrt{(2+(\varphi^{\prime})^{2})^{2}-4}\right)\geq 1.

(213)

Thus,
L
is uniformly positive definite:

(L({\boldsymbol{y}}){\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}})\geq|{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}
for all
{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(214)

Lemma B.1
.

Let
q
be the solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\cdot(L({\boldsymbol{y}})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}q)=\hat{F},\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}=\left\{(y_{1},y_{2}):y_{2}>0\right\}\\
L({\boldsymbol{y}})\partial_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}q\cdot\nu_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\hat{\beta}(\hat{\gamma}-q),\quad y_{2}=0,\end{array}\right.

(215)

for some
f\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2})
,
\hat{\gamma}=\hat{\gamma}(y_{1})\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\hat{\beta}(y_{1})\geq\beta_{0}>0
and matrix
L({\boldsymbol{y}})
satisfying uniform positivity condition (
214
). Denote also
U=B_{1}(0)\cap\left\{y_{2}>0\right\}
and
V=B_{1/2}(0)\cap\left\{y_{2}>0\right\}
.

Then we have the following bound:

\|q\|_{H^{2}(V)}^{2}\leq C\left(\|\hat{F}\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}+\|q\|_{H^{1}(U)}^{2}+\|q\|_{L^{2}(U_{0})}^{2}+\beta^{2}\|\gamma\|_{C^{1}}^{2}\right).

(216)

Here
U_{0}=\{y_{2}=0\}\cap U
.

Proof.

We adapt arguments from
[
48
]
. All gradients
\nabla
in the proof of this lemma are taken with respect to variable
{\boldsymbol{y}}
. First, we write the weak formulation of (
215
) for all
v\in H^{1}(U)
:

\int\limits_{\tilde{\Pi}}L\nabla q\cdot\nabla v\,\text{d}y-\int\limits_{\{y_{2}=0\}\cap\tilde{\Pi}}\hat{\beta}(\hat{\gamma}-q)v\,\text{d}S_{y}=-\int\limits_{\tilde{\Pi}}\hat{F}v\,\text{d}y.

(217)

Here
\tilde{\Pi}
is the image of
\Pi\setminus\mathcal{P}_{\text{st}}
under transformation (
212
). Next we introduce
\zeta({\boldsymbol{y}})
such that
\zeta\in C^{\infty}
and
\zeta\equiv 1
in
V
and
\zeta\equiv 0
outside of
W=B_{{3/4}}(0)\cap\left\{y_{2}>0\right\}
. Take test function
v=D_{1}^{-h}(\zeta^{2}D_{1}^{h}q)
, where
D_{1}^{h}
is the difference quotient operator:

D_{1}^{h}g=\dfrac{g(y_{1}+h,y_{2})-g(y_{1},y_{2})}{h}.

(218)

Integration by parts for the difference quotient allows us to rewrite the first integral in (
217
) as follows:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\tilde{\Pi}}L\nabla q\cdot\nabla v\,\text{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{U}\zeta^{2}\,(LD^{h}_{1}(\nabla q)\cdot D^{h}_{1}(\nabla q))\,\text{d}y-2\int\limits_{U}\zeta D_{1}^{h}q(\nabla\zeta\cdot LD^{h}_{1}(\nabla q))\,\text{d}y

(219)

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{U}\zeta^{2}(\left[D^{h}_{1}L\right](\nabla q)\cdot D^{h}_{1}(\nabla q))\,\text{d}y-2\int\limits_{U}\zeta D_{1}^{h}q(\nabla\zeta\cdot\left[D^{h}_{1}L\right](\nabla q))\,\text{d}y

\displaystyle\leq-\dfrac{1}{2}\int\limits_{U}\zeta^{2}|D^{h}_{1}(\nabla q)|^{2}\,\text{d}y+C\int\limits_{W}|D^{h}_{1}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y+C\int\limits_{U}|\nabla q|^{2}\,\text{d}y.

Here, to obtain the estimate we used (
214
), uniform boundedness of
\nabla\zeta
and
[D^{h}_{1}L]
, and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Similarly, we rewrite the second integral in (
217
):

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\{y_{2}=0\}\cap\tilde{\Pi}}\hat{\beta}(\hat{\gamma}-q)v\,\text{d}S_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{\beta}(\hat{\gamma}-q)D_{1}^{-h}(\zeta^{2}D_{1}^{h}q)\,\text{d}y_{1}

(220)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\,D_{1}^{h}(\hat{\beta}\hat{\gamma}-\hat{\beta}q)\,(D_{1}^{h}q)\zeta^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\,\zeta^{2}\left[D_{1}^{h}(\hat{\beta}\hat{\gamma})\right]D_{1}^{h}q\,\text{d}y_{1}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}q(D^{h}_{1}\hat{\beta})D^{h}_{1}q\,\text{d}y_{1}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{\beta}\zeta^{2}|D^{h}_{1}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{2}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}|D^{h}_{1}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}+C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}q^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}+C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}|D_{1}^{h}(\hat{\gamma}\hat{\beta})|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}.

Finally, we estimate the third integral in (
217
) using
[
48
, Theorem 3 from §5.8.2]
as follows:

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\tilde{\Pi}}\hat{F}v\,\text{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int\limits_{U}|\hat{F}|^{2}\text{d}y\right)^{1/2}\left(\int\limits_{U}|\nabla(\zeta^{2}D^{h}_{1}q)|^{2}\,\text{d}y\right)^{1/2}

(221)

\displaystyle\leq C\int\limits_{U}|\hat{F}|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\dfrac{1}{8}\int\limits_{U}|\nabla(\zeta^{2}D_{1}^{h}q)|^{2}\,\text{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\int\limits_{U}|\hat{F}|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\dfrac{1}{4}\int\limits_{U}\zeta^{2}|\nabla(D_{1}^{h}q)|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\dfrac{1}{4}\int\limits_{U}|\nabla(\zeta)^{2}\cdot D_{1}^{h}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\int\limits_{U}|\hat{F}|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\dfrac{1}{4}\int\limits_{U}\zeta^{2}|\nabla(D_{1}^{h}q)|^{2}\,\text{d}y+C\int\limits_{W}|D_{1}^{h}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y.

Combining (
217
) with estimates (
219
), (
220
), and (
221
), we get:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{4}\int\limits_{U}\zeta^{2}|D^{h}_{1}(\nabla q)|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{2}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}|D^{h}_{1}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt\leq C\left[\int\limits_{U}|\hat{F}|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\int\limits_{W}|D^{h}_{1}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\int\limits_{U}|\nabla q|^{2}\,\text{d}y\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 120.0pt\left.+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}q^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}|D_{1}^{h}(\hat{\gamma}\hat{\beta})|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}\right].

Using
[
48
, Theorem 3(i) from §5.8.2]
we get

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{4}\int\limits_{V}|D^{h}_{1}(\nabla q)|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{2}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\cap V}|D^{h}_{1}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt\leq C\left[\int\limits_{U}|\hat{F}|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\int\limits_{U}|\nabla q|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}q^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}|\partial_{y_{1}}(\hat{\gamma}\hat{\beta})|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}\right].

Then
[
48
, Theorem 3(ii) from §5.8.2]
implies

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{4}\int\limits_{V}|\partial_{y_{1}}(\nabla q)|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{2}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\cap V}|\partial_{y_{1}}q|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt\leq C\left[\int\limits_{U}|\hat{F}|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\int\limits_{U}|\nabla q|^{2}\,\text{d}y+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}q^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}|\partial_{y_{1}}(\hat{\gamma}\hat{\beta})|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}\right].

(222)

Now write

\partial_{y_{1}}(\hat{\gamma}\hat{\beta})=\beta\left(\dfrac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}\varphi^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1+(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}}}\gamma+\sqrt{1+(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}}(\gamma_{x_{1}}+\gamma_{x_{2}}\varphi^{\prime})\right)
. Thus,

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}|\partial_{y_{1}}(\hat{\gamma}\hat{\beta})|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{1}\leq C\beta^{2}\|\gamma\|_{C^{1}}^{2}.

Analogous estimate is valid for
\partial^{2}_{x_{2}}q
since

\partial^{2}_{y_{2}}q=-\dfrac{1}{1+(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}}\left[\hat{F}-\partial_{y_{1}}^{2}q+2\varphi^{\prime}\partial^{2}_{y_{1}y_{2}}q-\varphi^{\prime\prime}\partial_{y_{2}}q+2\varphi^{\prime}\varphi^{\prime\prime}\partial_{y_{2}}q\right]

and then

|\partial_{y_{2}}^{2}q|\leq C\left(|\hat{F}|+|\partial_{y_{1}}(\nabla q)|+|\nabla q|\right)

and thus (
216
) is proven.
∎

Next from from (
216
) and interior regularity
[
51
, Theorem 8.8]
we have that for and
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\subset\Omega
:

\|q\|_{H^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})}\leq C\left(\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

(223)

To obtain a bound on
\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, we will use that
q
from (
210
) minimizes the energy functional

\mathcal{E}_{0}(q)=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\Omega}|\nabla q|^{2}\,\text{d}x+\dfrac{\beta}{2}\int\limits_{\Omega}|\gamma-q|^{2}\,\text{d}S_{x}+\int\limits_{\Omega}Fq\,\text{d}x.

(224)

From
\mathcal{E}_{0}(q)\leq\mathcal{E}_{0}(0)
we get for all
\delta>0

\int\limits_{\Omega}|\nabla q|^{2}\,\text{d}x+\beta\int\limits_{\Omega}|q|^{2}\,\text{d}S_{x}\leq C\beta\|\gamma\|_{C}^{2}+C\delta^{-1}\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\delta\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Next, we use Poincaré estimate (
205
):

\displaystyle\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\beta}\right)\left(\beta\|\gamma\|_{C}^{2}+C\delta^{-1}\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\delta\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right).

Take
\delta:=\dfrac{1}{2C}\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\beta}\right)^{-1}
and we get

\displaystyle\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C(\beta+1)\|\gamma\|_{C}^{2}+C\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\beta}\right)^{2}\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Finally, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|q\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\beta+1)\|\gamma\|_{C}^{2}+C\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\beta}\right)^{2}\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

∎
