1.
Introduction

Several theories relate the spectrum of a passive tracer to the energy spectrum of the velocity that advects it.
When inertial effects dominate (tracer) diffusion, which happens at larger scales for sufficiently large velocity, the Batchelor–Corrsin–Obukhov theory
[
1
,
5
,
18
]
has the tracer spectrum scale as
|k|^{-({\hat{\beta}}+5)/2}
, for a (“turbulent”) energy spectrum scaling as
\mathcal{E}(k)\sim|k|^{\hat{\beta}}
.
On the other hand, tracer diffusion will inevitably dominate at smaller scales, where it was predicted by Batchelor, Howells and Townsend
[
2
]
, and later, using a different argument, by Kraichnan
[
14
,
13
]
that the tracer spectrum should scale as
|k|^{-4}\mathcal{E}(k)
.
The authors in
[
2
]
assumed that over this range, the characteristic time of velocity is much greater than that of the tracer.
With the source term in the equation for the tracer being approximately steady, they drop the time derivative.
Kraichnan’s argument has the velocity field fluctuating randomly in time with a correlation time much shorter than both the diffusive and convective time scales.
For synopses and later developments, see, e.g.,
[
23
,
19
,
24
,
8
,
20
]
.
There is also considerable computational evidence for the BHT spectrum
[
4
,
3
,
10
,
8
,
25
,
26
]
.

Following our earlier paper on the two-dimensional case
[
11
]
, in this paper we make rigorous the intuitive arguments of Batchelor–Howells–Townsend and Kraichnan, proving that the BHT scaling does hold probabilistically, asymptotically for large wavenumbers, for a class of small random synthetic three-dimensional incompressible velocity fields.
As in
[
11
]
, we also confirm the intuition of
[
2
,
14
]
that this holds whether or not the velocity is time dependent, subject to reasonable assumptions.
Relaxing some assumptions in
[
11
]
, here we allow a more general modal random variable, only requiring it to be circular, and a more general tracer (variance) source, only requiring sufficient Sobolev regularity.
The main limitation of our present result, specifically the convergence proof of the (unknown) remainder, is that it requires an energy spectrum no shallower than
|k|^{-2}
, which rules out an application to the hypothesized Kolmogorov
|k|^{-5/3}
energy spectrum.

Our general approach is similar to
[
11
]
, with some differences:
As in the two-dimensional case treated in
[
11
]
, our approach is to calculate precisely the expectation of the spectrum for the first iterate of a fixed point iteration of the tracer, and show that the error from the actual tracer can be made small by taking the velocity small. This, coupled with a certain bound on the variance, establish the BHT scaling.
Unlike the 2d case, however, here we consider a random tracer source at all scales, except for the bound on the variance.
Also different is that rather than randomizing the phases of the streamfunction in Fourier space, here we randomize components of the coefficients in the Craya–Herring 3d basis
[
6
,
9
,
12
]
.

Through its obvious relationship to various Sobolev norms, the tracer spectrum is related to the degree of tracer mixing and of the efficiency of mixing by the advecting velocity
[
22
,
7
,
21
,
16
,
15
,
17
]
.
A steep tracer spectrum such as the BHT suggests poor mixing, either throughout the entire range for small velocity (treated here), or beyond the so-called diffusive wavenumber
[
23
, (8.108)]
for larger velocity (with the Obukhov–Corrsin/Batchelor tracer spectrum at larger scales).

2.
Preliminaries

We consider the evolution of a passive scalar
\theta(x,t)
under
a prescribed velocity field
u(x,t)
and source
g(x)
,

(2.1)

\partial_{t}\theta+u\cdot\nabla\theta=\Delta\theta+g.

For simplicity, we take
x\in D:=[0,2\pi]^{3}
and assume periodic boundary conditions in all directions.
With no loss of generality, we assume that, for all
t

(2.2)

\int_{D}u(x,t)\;\mathrm{d}x=0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{D}\theta(x,t)\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

We note that for the latter to hold for all
t>0
, we must impose the same condition on
g
and
\theta(\cdot,0)
.

We expand
\theta(x,t)
in Fourier series as

(2.3)

\theta(x,t)={\textstyle\sum}_{k}^{\prime}\,\theta_{k}(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x},

where the prime indicates that the sum is taken over
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\backslash\{0\}
to satisfy (
2.2
), and denote spectral projection by

(2.4)

({\sf P}_{\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}}\theta)(x,t):=\sum\nolimits_{\kappa\leq|k|<\kappa^{\prime}}\theta_{k}(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}.

For the tracer (variance) source, we take either the deterministic

(2.5)

(\Delta^{-1}g)(x)={\textstyle\sum}_{k}^{\prime}\,\gamma_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}

where
\gamma_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\gamma_{-k}=\overline{\gamma_{k}}
,
\gamma_{0}=0
and

(2.6)

|\gamma_{k}|\leq c_{g}|k|^{\alpha}\qquad\text{when }|k|\geq\kappa_{g}

for some constants
c_{g}\geq 0
,
\kappa_{g}>1
and
\alpha<0
.
The case
c_{g}=0
gives the bandwidth-limited source considered in
[
11
]
, for which somewhat tighter estimates can be obtained below.
Alternately, and without altering the conclusion (see below), one may also consider the random

(2.7)

(\Delta^{-1}g_{r})(x)={\textstyle\sum}_{k}^{\prime}\,\gamma_{k}Z_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}.

The complex random variable
Z_{k}
is constructed as follows.
For a fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, we write
Z_{k}=R_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\zeta_{k}}
where the random phase
\zeta_{k}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,2\pi)
, implying that
Z_{k}
is
circular
, i.e.
{\sf E}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}Z_{k})={\sf E}Z_{k}
for any deterministic real
\phi
.
This in turn implies that
{\sf E}Z_{k}^{n}=0
for any integer
n\neq 0
.
We constrain the random modulus
R_{k}
so that

(2.8)

\Xi:=\sup\{s:P(R_{k}>s)>0\}<\infty,

implying that
Z_{k}
is bounded,
|Z_{k}|\leq\Xi
.
(With circularity, this means that
Z_{k}
is a
proper random variable
.)
With no loss of generality, we put as its variance and fourth moment

(2.9)

{\sf E}|Z_{k}|^{2}=1\quad\text{and}\quad{\sf E}|Z_{k}|^{4}=\varrho.

We note that by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
1=({\sf E}|Z_{k}|^{2})^{2}\leq{\sf E}|Z_{k}|^{4}=\varrho
, with equality (i.e.
\varrho=1
) attained iff
|Z_{k}|=1
a.s.
We denote
Z_{k}\sim\mathcal{R}_{\varrho}
.

Now for
g(x,t)
to be real-valued, we must require that
Z_{-k}=\overline{Z_{k}}
, but otherwise
Z_{k}
are assumed to be uncorrelated, so
{\sf E}Z_{j}\overline{Z_{k}}=\delta_{jk}
and
{\sf E}Z_{j}Z_{k}=\delta_{j,-k}
.
A convenient tool to handle this reality constraint is the wavenumber half-space

(2.10)

\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{3}:=\{(l,m,n):n>0\}\cup\{(l,m,0):m>0\}\cup\{(l,0,0):l>0\}

with
l
,
m
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
; we thus have
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{3}\cup(-\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{3})=\mathbb{Z}^{3}\backslash\{0\}
.
With this, we can write
{\sf E}Z_{j}Z_{k}=0
and
{\sf E}Z_{j}\overline{Z_{k}}=\delta_{jk}
for all
j
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{3}
.

To set up our velocity, we recall the Craya–Herring basis
[
6
,
9
,
12
]
:
Writing a wavevector
k=(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
, defining
k_{h}:=(k_{x},k_{y},0)
and using spherical coordinates
(1,\varphi,\phi)
, we define the (
k
-dependent) orthonormal vectors

\displaystyle d_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{|k|}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})}{|k|}

\displaystyle=(\sin\varphi\cos\phi,\sin\varphi\sin\phi,\cos\varphi),

\displaystyle e_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{k\times\hat{z}}{|k\times\hat{z}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k_{y},-k_{x},0)}{|k_{h}|}

\displaystyle=(\sin\phi,-\cos\phi,0),

\displaystyle f_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{k\times k\times\hat{z}}{|k\times k\times\hat{z}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k_{x}k_{z},k_{y}k_{z},-|k_{h}|^{2})}{|k|\,|k_{h}|}

\displaystyle=(\cos\varphi\cos\phi,\cos\varphi\sin\phi,-\sin\varphi)\;,

where
\hat{z}=(0,0,1)
.
With these basis vectors, any velocity field
v(x,t)
can be written as

(2.11)

v(x,t)={\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,[\tilde{U}_{d}(k,t)d_{k}+\tilde{U}_{e}(k,t)e_{k}+\tilde{U}_{f}(k,t)f_{k}]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}

for some
(\tilde{U}_{d},\tilde{U}_{e},\tilde{U}_{f})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
.
Now since
\text{div}\,v(x,t)=\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}\,\tilde{U}_{d}(k,t)|k|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}
, for
v(\cdot,t)
to be incompressible we must have
\tilde{U}_{d}(k,t)\equiv 0
.
We thus write our incompressible velocity field as

(2.12)

u(x,t)=\mathop{\smash{\mathop{{\sum}_{k}^{\prime}}}{\vphantom{\sum}}}|k|^{\beta}[U_{e}e_{k}V_{k}(t)+U_{f}f_{k}W_{k}(t)]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}

where
\beta<0
,
U_{e}
and
U_{f}
are real constants, and
V_{k}(t)
and
W_{k}(t)
complex random processes whose time behaviour will be precised below.

We note that in this paper
|u_{k}|\sim|k|^{\beta}
whereas in
[

|u_{k}|\sim|k|^{\beta+1}
, so the

s are different; in hindsight, we feel the present notation to be more natural.

For now, we require that, for each fixed
t
,
V_{k}(t)
and
W_{k}(t)\sim\mathcal{R}_{\varsigma}
, proper random variables with unit variance and
{\sf E}|V_{k}(t)|^{4}={\sf E}|W_{k}(t)|^{4}=\varsigma
, and bounded as
|V_{k}(t)|
,
|W_{k}(t)|\leq\Xi
.
As with

, for

to be real-valued, we must require that
V_{-k}(t)=\overline{V_{k}(t)}
and
W_{-k}(t)=\overline{W_{k}(t)}
.
Aside from this constraint, we assume that
V_{k}(t)
and
W_{k}(t)
are uncorrelated, so
{\sf E}V_{j}(t)\overline{V_{k}(t)}={\sf E}W_{j}(t)\overline{W_{k}(t)}=\delta_{jk}

{\sf E}V_{j}(t)\overline{W_{k}(t)}=0

Unlike

We turn to the energy spectrum.
First, we compute [suppressing dependence on
t
where no confusion may arise]

(2.13)

\displaystyle\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigl({\textstyle\sum}_{j}\,|j|^{\beta}(U_{e}e_{j}V_{j}+U_{f}f_{j}W_{j})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}j\cdot x},{\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,|k|^{\beta}(U_{e}e_{k}V_{k}+U_{f}f_{k}W_{k})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}\bigr)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=8\pi^{3}{\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,|k|^{2\beta}(U_{e}^{2}|V_{k}|^{2}+U_{f}^{2}|W_{k}|^{2}),

where for the second equality we have used the facts that
(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}j\cdot x},\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x})_{L^{2}}=(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{jk}
,
e_{k}\cdot e_{k}=f_{k}\cdot f_{k}=1
and
e_{k}\cdot f_{k}=0
.
Unlike in our previous work on the 2d case
[
11

\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u\|^{2}
contains the random variables
|V_{k}|^{2}
and
|W_{k}|^{2}
, so we compute

(2.14)

\displaystyle{\sf E}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=8\pi^{3}{\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,|k|^{2\beta}(U_{e}^{2}{\sf E}|V_{k}|^{2}+U_{f}^{2}{\sf E}|W_{k}|^{2})

\displaystyle=8\pi^{3}(U_{e}^{2}+U_{f}^{2})\,{\textstyle\sum}_{\kappa\leq|k|<2\kappa}|k|^{2\beta}.

Approximating the sum as an integral over the corresponding region in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we find this scales as
\kappa^{2\beta+d}
for sufficiently large
\kappa
, so for the classical Kolmogorov
-\frac{5}{3}

(i.e.

), we must take
\beta=-\frac{11}{6}
.
Next, we compute the variance
{\sf var}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, by first using (
2.9
) to obtain

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-6}{\sf E}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u\|_{L^{2}}^{4}={\sf E}\bigl({\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,|k|^{2\beta}(U_{e}^{2}|V_{k}|^{2}+U_{f}^{2}|W_{k}|^{2})\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\sum}_{jk}\,|j|^{2\beta}|k|^{2\beta}(U_{e}^{4}{\sf E}|V_{j}|^{2}|V_{k}|^{2}+2U_{e}^{2}U_{f}^{2}{\sf E}|V_{j}|^{2}|W_{k}|^{2}+U_{f}^{4}{\sf E}|W_{j}|^{2}|W_{k}|^{2})

\displaystyle={\textstyle\sum}_{j\neq k}\,|j|^{2\beta}|k|^{2\beta}(U_{e}^{4}{\sf E}|V_{j}|^{2}|V_{k}|^{2}+U_{f}^{4}{\sf E}|W_{j}|^{2}|W_{k}|^{2})

\displaystyle+{\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,|k|^{4\beta}(U_{e}^{4}{\sf E}|V_{k}|^{4}+U_{f}^{4}{\sf E}|W_{k}|^{4})+2\,{\textstyle\sum}_{jk}\,|j|^{2\beta}|k|^{2\beta}U_{e}^{2}U_{f}^{2}{\sf E}|V_{j}|^{2}|W_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\sum}_{j\neq k}\,|j|^{2\beta}|k|^{2\beta}(U_{e}^{4}+U_{f}^{4})+\varsigma\,{\textstyle\sum}_{j=k}\,|j|^{2\beta}|k|^{2\beta}(U_{e}^{4}+U_{f}^{4})

\displaystyle+2\,{\textstyle\sum}_{jk}\,|j|^{2\beta}|k|^{2\beta}U_{e}^{2}U_{f}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigl((U_{e}^{2}+U_{f}^{2}){\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,|k|^{2\beta}\bigr)^{2}+(\varsigma-1)(U_{e}^{4}+U_{f}^{4}){\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,|k|^{4\beta},

whence

(2.15)

\displaystyle{\sf var}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle={\sf E}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u\|_{L^{2}}^{4}-\bigl({\sf E}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{6}(\varsigma-1)(U_{e}^{4}+U_{f}^{4}){\textstyle\sum}_{\kappa\leq|k|<2\kappa}\,|k|^{4\beta}.

For large
\kappa
, this scales as
\kappa^{4\beta+d}
, so
({\sf var}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u\|^{2})^{1/2}/{\sf E}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}u\|^{2}\propto\kappa^{-d/2}
, giving asymptotic convergence (over dyads) to an energy spectrum that is
\kappa^{2\beta+d-1}
.

For the time dependence, we assume that,

\displaystyle{\sf E}V_{j}(s)\overline{V_{k}(t)}={\sf E}W_{j}(s)\overline{W_{k}(t)}=\delta_{jk}\Phi_{k}(s-t).

\Phi_{k}(t)=\Phi(\chi_{k}|t|)

,

is assumed not to grow too rapidly with

\displaystyle|\Phi(h)|

\displaystyle=|{\sf E}\,V_{k}(s)\overline{V_{k}(s+h)}|

\displaystyle\leq({\sf E}\,|V_{k}(s)|^{2})^{1/2}({\sf E}\,|V_{k}(s+h)|^{2})^{1/2}=\Phi(0)=1.

3.
Main Result and Discussion

As in the 2d case, it is both convenient and instructive to first consider the static case

(3.1)

u\cdot\nabla\theta=\Delta\theta+g.

Here the time-independent random velocity is

(3.2)

u(x)={\textstyle\sum}_{k}^{\prime}\,|k|^{\beta}[U_{e}e_{k}V_{k}+U_{f}f_{k}W_{k}]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}

with
V_{k}
and
W_{k}\sim\mathcal{R}_{\varsigma}
i.i.d. satisfying the usual reality constraints.

The strategy, as in
[
11
]
, is to solve (
3.1
) by a fixed-point iteration, show that the error from the first iterate
\vartheta
to the solution is, at most, of the same order, as
\kappa\to\infty
as
{\sf E}\|{\sf P}_{\kappa,2\kappa}\vartheta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. The latter will satisfy the BHT spectrum and the
relative
error can be made arbitrarily small by taking
U/U_{\text{max}}
small enough.
We put
\theta^{(0)}=-\Delta^{-1}g
and

(3.3)

\theta^{(n+1)}=\Delta^{-1}(u\cdot\nabla\theta^{(n)}-g).

We seek to prove that this iteration converges under some assumptions, and that the limit
\theta^{(\infty)}
asymptotes, dyad-wise as
\kappa\to\infty
, probabilistically to the BHT spectrum.
Unlike in
[
11
]
, however, here our source
g
may have a full spectrum, so
\vartheta:=\theta^{(1)}-\theta^{(0)}=-\Delta^{-1}(u\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}g)
has a remainder arising from high-frequency parts of
g
.

Denoting
\|{f}\|_{l_{1}}:={\textstyle\sum}_{k}\,|f_{k}|
and putting
U_{e}=U_{f}=U
, we have the following:

.

Let

) with
