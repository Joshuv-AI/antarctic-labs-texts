Deterministic Fast Scrambling with Neutral Atom Arrays
Tomohiro Hashizume, Gregory S. Bentsen, Sebastian Weber, Andrew J. Daley

Abstract
Fast scramblers are dynamical quantum systems that produce many-body entanglement on a timescale that grows logarithmically with the system size N N . We propose and investigate a family of deterministic, fast scrambling quantum circuits realizable in near-term experiments with arrays of neutral atoms. We show that three experimental tools – nearest-neighbor Rydberg interactions, global single-qubit rotations, and shuffling operations facilitated by an auxiliary tweezer array – are sufficient to generate nonlocal interaction graphs capable of scrambling quantum information using only 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ N ) \mathcal{O}(\log N) parallel applications of nearest-neighbor gates. These tools enable direct experimental access to fast scrambling dynamics in a highly controlled and programmable way and can be harnessed to produce highly entangled states with varied applications.

We have shown how deterministic, highly nonlocal iterated (Floquet) circuits can generate fast scrambling dynamics in a way that is amenable to direct experimental realization using fast shuffle operations on neutral atom qubits. This technique allows for rapid long-range spreading of entanglement, while minimizing errors from excitation of atoms to Rydberg states, and uses only shuffling operations, global single-qubit rotations, and parallel nearest-neighbor interactions. Building fast scrambling circuits in the laboratory opens connections to a wide range of ongoing areas, including fundamental limits on the spreading of quantum information

We note that these graphs might also be constructed by other means, including collisional gate implementations for neutral atoms, or via direct wiring of hypercubic coupling graphs in superconducting qubit systems.

V. Characterizing Scrambling via the Hayden-Preskill Experiment

Originally conceived in the context of the black hole information problem, the Hayden-Preskill thought experiment (Fig.
3
) can be viewed as a general conceptual tool useful for characterizing the scrambling properties of quantum channels
\mathcal{E}
. We focus for the moment on unitary channels
\mathcal{E}=U
and consider a local observer Alice who wishes to use this channel to encode some quantum information. To do so, she maximally entangles her qubits
A
with the channel
U
using
\left|A\right|

\left|\mathrm{EPR}\right\rangle_{A}=\prod_{\left|A\right|}(\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
as illustrated in Fig.
3

A
, Alice has complete control over the information entering the channel
U
; in particular, to send a quantum state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
into the encoding circuit
U
, Alice need only project her maximally-entangled qubits
A
onto the desired state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
.

Bob, another observer whose qubits
B
are maximally-entangled with the remaining channel inputs, attempts to recover Alice’s quantum information by collecting a subset
R
of the output qubits. Of course if Bob has access to all of the output qubits
\left|R\right|=N
then his ability to reconstruct Alice’s information is trivially guaranteed by the unitarity of
U
. Surprisingly, however, if the operator
U
is strongly scrambling then Bob can reconstruct Alice’s information using only a handful
\left|R\right|_{\mathrm{min}}=\left|A\right|+k
of output qubits, with
k

\mathcal{O}(1)
constant independent of
\left|R\right|,\left|A\right|,N
. We can see why by examining the information content available to the regions

The EPR pairs held by Alice and Bob in regions
A,B
serve to convert the operator
U
into a pure state
\left|U\right\rangle
via the channel-state correspondence
\left|U\right\rangle=U\left|\mathrm{EPR}\right\rangle

Hosur et al. 2016
as illustrated in Fig.
3
a. We may therefore use entropy measures on the state
\left|U\right\rangle
to quantify the amount of information that is accessible to various observers. In particular, the mutual information

\displaystyle I_{2}(A:RB)

\displaystyle=S_{A}+S_{RB}-S_{ARB}

\displaystyle=S_{A}+S_{A\overline{R}}-S_{\overline{R}}

\displaystyle=2\left|A\right|\ln 2-I_{2}(A:\overline{R})

(S17)

quantifies the amount of information shared between the region
A
in Alice’s control and the regions
R,B
in Bob’s (we have dropped the superscripts

for notational simplicity). In the last two lines we have used the fact that

;

;

and still recover Alice’s information, so long as he maintains control over regions

VIII.2
B. Simulating Probabilistic Decoding with Quantum Trajectories

While the Hayden-Preskill thought experiment tells under which conditions Bob can in principle recover the quantum state that has been sent by Alice into the scrambling circuit
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{s}}
, it does not tell how to do so in practice. One possibility to teleport a quantum state of Alice to Bob, is to add a probabilistic decoder
Yoshida and Kitaev 2017
;
Yoshida and Yao 2019
to the Hayden-Preskill thought experiment as shown in Fig.
4
a of the main text. The decoder circuit
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{s}}^{*}
is the complex conjugate of the scrambling circuit.

For the simulations presented in Fig.
4
, we use a scrambling circuit having
N=8
input qubits. Alice owns one qubit, referred to as
q_{A}
in the following. As described in section
V
of the supplemental material, Alice maximally entangles one input qubit of
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{s}}
with her qubit. Bob maximally entangles the remaining input qubits of
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{s}}
with qubits owned by him. The decoder circuit
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{s}}^{*}
acts on these qubits owned by Bob and one additional qubit that is maximally entangled with a further qubit of Bob, referred to as
q_{B}
. The qubits’ evolution under the scrambling and decoder circuits is calculated using the state vector simulator of the open-source framework Qiskit
Abraham and et
al 2019
. After the calculation, we sample over all subsystems of size
|R|
, and evaluate the projector on the corresponding EPR pairs. The projection succeeds with the probability
P_{\mathrm{EPR}}
. In case of success, we evaluate the projector on the EPR pair between qubit
q_{A}
and
q_{B}
. Such an EPR pair, which is measured with probability
F_{\mathrm{EPR}}
, is a resource for teleporting a quantum state of Alice to Bob.

To account for decoherence by an effective model, we add dephasing noise implemented via the depolarizing quantum error channel

D(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\frac{I}{2}

(S18)

to all qubits after each of the
2m=6
interaction layers of the circuits. Here,
p
is the error rate per qubit and per interaction layer. The error channel is treated with a quantum trajectory approach where we randomly sample over
6\times 10^{4}
realizations of the total circuit and average over the measurements.

For realizing the controlled-
Z
gate within the simulations, we use the following scheme described in
Jaksch et al. 2000
: We assume that
\left|1\right\rangle
can be excited globally to the Rydberg state
\left|r\right\rangle
by a
\pi
pulse, using a laser whose Rabi frequency is much larger than the van der Waals interaction
V_{\text{vdW}}(R)\sim 1/R^{6}
between adjacent atoms separated by the interatomic distance
R=R_{nn}
. For realizing the gate, we first transfer all the population from
\left|1\right\rangle
to
\left|r\right\rangle
by a global
\pi
pulse. After waiting for the time
\pi/V_{\text{vdW}}(R_{nn})
, the state
\left|rr\right\rangle
of two adjacent atoms has picked up the phase
\pi
and we bring back the population to
\left|1\right\rangle
by another global
\pi
pulse.
We separate neighboring atoms by
2R_{nn}
if we do not like to perform a controlled-
Z
gate between them. Hereby, the picked up phase gets strongly suppressed by the fast decay of the van der Waals interaction.
