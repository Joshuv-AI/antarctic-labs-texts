Equitable Continuous Organizations with Self-Assessed Valuations
Howard Heaton Sam Green Typal Research Semiotic AI Typal LLC

Abstract
Organizations are often unable to align the interests of all stakeholders with the financial success of the organization ( e.g. due to regulation). However, continuous organizations (COs) introduce a paradigm shift. COs offer immediate liquidity, are permission-less and can align incentives. CO shares are issued continuously in the form of tokens via a smart contract on a blockchain. Token prices are designed to increase as more tokens are minted. When the share supply is low, near-zero prices make it advantageous to buy and hold tokens until interest in the CO increases, enabling a profitable sale. This attribute of COs, known as investment efficiency, is desirable. Yet, it can yield allocative inefficiency via the “holdout problem,” i.e. latecomers may find a CO more valuable than early tokenholders, but be unable to attain the same token holdings due to inflated prices. With the aim of increasing overall equity, we introduce a voting mechanism into COs. We show this balances allocative and investment efficiency, and may dissuade speculative trading behaviors, thereby decreasing investment risk.

Proof-of-Vote

A voting rule property with potentially significant impact is the level of influence each mint can have on the aggregate assessment
\overline{a}
used by the ECO. Optimal design of this remains an open question, which we highlight as follows.
If the relative weight of an assessment
a
in obtaining
\overline{a}
is proportional to the volume of tokens minted, then a few wealthy and early tokenholders may effectively control the aggregate assessment
\overline{a}
.
Alternatively, each transaction assessment
a
could contribute equally to the aggregate
\overline{a}
(regardless of transaction size). Informally, we refer to this as the ‘‘proof-of-vote’’ property since (relatively) fixed frictional costs

involved for each mint gives a similar flavor to “proof-of-work” mechanisms.
In other words, the “have nots” can have equal contribution to defining the bonding curve as the “haves.”
This latter option may limit the ability to make short-term gains while also stabilizing the convergence of price to an aggregate average assessment (

The bound in (
21
) holds for all
s\in[0,S]
, thereby implying (
18
) holds upon taking
\tau_{-}=\overline{\tau}_{-}.

∎

Lemma
3.1
(Efficiency of Allocative Curves).
Given a tolerance
\varepsilon>0
and share supply
S>0
, if
q
is an allocative curve for a bonding curve
p
, then there are
\tau_{-},\tau_{+}\in(0,1)
such that

i)

for all

\tau\in[\tau_{+},1)
,
the curve

q(s;\ a,\tau,p)
is
\varepsilon
-allocative efficient on
[0,S]
;

ii)

for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{-}]
,
the curve

q(s;\ a,\tau,p)
is
(p-\varepsilon)
-investment efficient on
[0,S].

Proof.

We verify allocative efficiency (Step 1) and investment efficiency (Step 2) in turn. Let
\varepsilon>0
,
S>0
, and
a\geq 0
be given along with an allocative curve
q
for a bonding curve
p
.

Step 1.

By the definition of a limit, it suffices to show there is
\tau^{+}\in(0,1)
such that

q(s,a;\ \tau,p)\leq a+\varepsilon,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $s\in[0,S]$ and $\tau\in[\tau^{+},1).$}

(22)

By the second property of allocative curves, there is
\overline{\tau}^{+}\in(0,1)
such that

\left|q(S,a;\ \tau,p)-a\right|\leq\varepsilon,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $\tau\in[\overline{\tau}^{+},1).$}

(23)

With the monotonicity of
q
with respect to
s
, (
23
) implies

q(s,a;\ \tau,p)\leq q(S,a;\ \tau,p)\leq a+\varepsilon,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $\tau\in[\overline{\tau}^{+},1).$}

(24)

Thus, (
22
) holds, taking
\tau^{+}=\overline{\tau}^{+}.

Step 2.
In similar fashion, here it suffices to show there is
\tau^{-}\in(0,1)
such that

q(s,a;\ \tau,p)\geq p(s)-\varepsilon,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $s\in[0,S]$ and $\tau\in(0,\tau^{-}].$}

(25)

Since
q(\cdot,a;\ \tau,p)\rightarrow p
uniformly as
\tau\rightarrow 0^{+}
,
there is
\overline{\tau}^{-}\in(0,1)
such that

\|q(\cdot,a;\ \tau,p)-p(s)\|_{\infty}=\sup_{s\in[0,S]}\left|q(s,a;\ \tau,p)-p(s)\right|\leq\varepsilon,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $\tau\in(0,\overline{\tau}^{-}].$}

(26)

In particular, this implies (
25
) holds, taking
\tau^{-}=\overline{\tau}^{-},
and the proof is complete.
∎

A.2
Allocative Curves for Linear Bonding Curves

A core requirement in the implementation of bonding curves is efficient computation of the payment/reward
m
and the shares minted/burned
x
.
This is particularly important for smart contract implementations. The lemma below provides two bounds that may be used for estimating
m
and
x
.
We emphasize these bounds are given to ensure that transactions incur “positive friction,”
i.e.
the errors do not make it possible to game the system by repeating a mint/burn transaction pair profitably.

Remark A.1
.

When shares are burned,
x
and
m
are negative.
Thus, for burning shares, we seek an underestimate of
-m
or, equivalently, an overestimate of
m
.

Lemma A.1

(Allocative Mint and Burn Formulas with Linear Bonding Curves)
.

Let
s\geq 0
be a share supply,
a\geq 0
be an assessment, and
\tau\in(0,1)
be a tax rate.
Let
k>0
and
p(s)=k\cdot s
be a bonding curve and
q
be the allocative curve for
p
defined by (
7
).

1.

In a transaction where shares are burned (i.e.
x\in[-s,0)
), the reward
|m|
is satisfies

|m|=-m\geq\dfrac{\theta}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}\cdot\max\left(0,\ \ s\cdot|x|-\dfrac{x^{2}}{\max(2,1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)}\right),

(27)

and this lower bound on
|m|
is monotonically increasing as
x
decreases to
-s
.

2.

In a transaction where shares are minted (i.e.
x\in(0,\infty)
), the shares minted
x
satisfy

x\geq\dfrac{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}{2}\left[\sqrt{s^{2}+\dfrac{4m}{(1-\tau+\tau\cdot a)\cdot k}}-s\right].

(28)

Proof.

We first obtain a formula relating
m
and
x
via Taylor’s remainder theorem (Step 1).
This is used to establish (
27
) (Step 2) and then (
28
) (Step 3).

Step 1.
First observe

\displaystyle m=\int_{s}^{s+x}q(\zeta,a;\ \tau,p)\ \mbox{d}\zeta=\underbrace{(1-\tau+\tau\cdot a)\cdot k}_{\triangleq\theta}\cdot\int_{s}^{s+x}\dfrac{\zeta}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot\zeta}\ \mbox{d}\zeta.

(29)

Integrating yields

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{s+x}\dfrac{\zeta}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot\zeta}\ \mbox{d}\zeta

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\tau\cdot k}\cdot\int_{s}^{s+x}1-\dfrac{1}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot\zeta}\ \mbox{d}\zeta

(30a)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\tau\cdot k}\left[\zeta-\dfrac{\ln(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot\zeta)}{\tau\cdot k}\right]_{s}^{s+x}

(30b)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\tau\cdot k}\left[x-\dfrac{1}{\tau\cdot k}\cdot\ln\left(\dfrac{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot(s+x)}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}\right)\right]

(30c)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\tau\cdot k}\left[x-\dfrac{1}{\tau\cdot k}\cdot\ln\left(1+\dfrac{\tau\cdot k\cdot x}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}\right)\right].

(30d)

Combining (
29
) and (
30
) reveals

\displaystyle m=\dfrac{\theta}{\tau\cdot k}\left[x-\dfrac{1}{\tau\cdot k}\cdot\ln\left(1+\dfrac{\tau\cdot k\cdot x}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}\right)\right].

(31a)

Recall the Taylor series expansion relation

\ln(1+\xi)=\xi-\dfrac{\xi^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{\xi^{3}}{3}+\cdots=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-\xi)^{n}}{n},\ \ \ \mbox{for all $\xi\in(-1,1).$}

(32)

By Taylor’s remainder theorem, there is
\zeta_{x}
between
x
and
0
such that

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\theta}{\tau\cdot k}\left[x-\dfrac{x}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}+\dfrac{\tau\cdot k\cdot x^{2}}{(1+k\cdot\tau\cdot s)^{2}}-\dfrac{\tau^{2}\cdot k^{2}\cdot\zeta_{x}^{3}}{(1+k\cdot\tau\cdot s)^{3}}\right]

(33a)

\displaystyle=\theta\left[\dfrac{s\cdot x}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}+\dfrac{x^{2}}{(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)^{2}}-\dfrac{\tau\cdot k\cdot\zeta_{x}^{3}}{(1+k\cdot\tau\cdot s)^{3}}\right]

(33b)

Step 2.
Using (
33
), we see

m\leq\theta\left[\dfrac{s\cdot x}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}+\dfrac{x^{2}}{(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)^{2}}\right],\ \ \ \mbox{for all $x\in[-s,0),$}

(34)

noting
-\xi_{x}^{3}>0
for
x<0
.
More simply, the integral in (
29
) can also be bounded by

m\leq\dfrac{\theta}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}\cdot\int_{s}^{s+x}\zeta\ \mbox{d}\zeta=\dfrac{\theta\cdot(2\cdot s\cdot x+x^{2})}{2\cdot(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)},\ \ \ \mbox{for all}\ x\in[-s,0),

(35)

where we note
\theta>0
.
Combining (
34
) and (
35
) reveals,

m\leq\dfrac{\theta}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}\left[s\cdot x+\dfrac{x^{2}}{\max(2,1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)}\right],\ \ \ \mbox{for all $x\in[-s,0).$},

(36)

which establishes (
27
).
Lastly, note the vertex of the quadratic bound occurs at

x=-\dfrac{s\cdot\max(2,1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)}{2}\leq-s,

(37)

and so the bound (
27
) is increasing for all valid choices of
x
(
i.e.

x\geq-s
).

Step 3.
Setting

A=\dfrac{1}{(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)^{2}},\quad B=\dfrac{s}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s},\quad C=-\dfrac{m}{\theta}-\dfrac{\tau\cdot k\cdot\xi_{x}^{3}}{(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)^{3}},

(38)

reveals

\displaystyle Ax^{2}+Bx+C=0.

(39)

Consequently, if
x>0
, then, by the quadratic formula,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)^{2}}{2}\cdot\left[\sqrt{\dfrac{s^{2}}{(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)^{2}}-4AC}-\dfrac{s}{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}\right]

(40a)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}{2}\left[\sqrt{s^{2}+4\left(\dfrac{m}{\theta}+\dfrac{\tau\cdot k\cdot\xi_{x}^{3}}{(1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s)^{3}}\right)}-s\right]

(40b)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1+\tau\cdot k\cdot s}{2}\left[\sqrt{s^{2}+\dfrac{4m}{\theta}}-s\right],

(40c)

where we note
m>0
.
This gives (
28
), as desired.
∎
