Doubly robust nearest neighbors in factor models


Abstract
We introduce and analyze an improved variant of nearest neighbors (NN) for estimation with missing data in latent factor models. We consider a matrix completion problem with missing data, where the ( i , t ) (i,t) -th entry, when observed, is given by its mean f ⁡ ( u i , v t ) f(u_{i},v_{t}) plus mean-zero noise for an unknown function f f and latent factors u i u_{i} and v t v_{t} . Prior NN strategies, like unit-unit NN, for estimating the mean f ⁡ ( u i , v t ) f(u_{i},v_{t}) relies on existence of other rows j j with u j ≈ u i u_{j}\approx u_{i} . Similarly, time-time NN strategy relies on existence of columns t ′ t^{\prime} with v t ′ ≈ v t v_{t^{\prime}}\approx v_{t} . These strategies provide poor performance respectively when similar rows or similar columns are not available. Our estimate is doubly robust to this deficit in two ways: (1) As long as there exist either good row or good column neighbors, our estimate provides a consistent estimate. (2) Furthermore, if both good row and good column neighbors exist, it provides a (near-)quadratic improvement in the non-asymptotic error and admits a significantly narrower asymptotic confidence interval when compared to both unit-unit or time-time NN.

Introduction

Lataent factor models are useful tools for statistical inference with missing data. They are popular in the literature on recommender systems, e-commerce and panel data problems in econometrics, and more recently have been utilized for personalized decision-making in health, including sequential decision-making with adaptive algorithms like bandits. One of the canonical problem formulation include the matrix completion problem: Given a matrix of noisy outcomes with missing entries, estimate the denoised outcome for each (missing or not) entry in the matrix. In particular, our goal is to estimate the entries of a matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(T+1)}
with its
(i,t)
-th entry denoted by
\theta_{i,t}
, where for each
i\in[N+1]
and
t\in[T+1]
, we observe

\textstyle Y_{i,t}=\begin{cases}\theta_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}&\quad\text{if}\quad A_{i,t}=1\\
\star&\quad\text{if}\quad A_{i,t}=0\end{cases}

(2)

where
\varepsilon_{i,t}
denotes real-valued noise while
A_{i,t}
is a binary variable denoting which entries of the matrix are observed, and
\star
denotes that the entry is not observed. For recommender systems,
\theta_{i,t}
might denote a user
i
’s true rating for a product
t
, and a noisy value
Y_{i,t}
is available only when the user rates it (
A_{i,t}=1
). In an advertisement application,
Y_{i,t}
might denote whether user
i
bought a product
t
if a particular ad (
A_{i,t}=1
) is shown to the user. In digital health,
\theta_{i,t}
might denote a health outcome, e.g., step count, or heart rate, for user
i
at some decision time
t
, and
A_{i,t}=1
denotes whether a binary intervention, e.g., a reminder for physical activity, was delivered at that time. For clarity in our discussion (and partly motivated by the empirical application considered later), henceforth, we refer to the rows of the matrix as units and columns as time points.

Without any assumptions, estimating the matrix
\Theta
is an impossible task due to more number of unknown parameters than the number of (noisy) observations. A common modeling approach to make the task feasible is via a latent factor model. In particular, one assumes that the entries of
\Theta
admit a (non-linear) factorization in terms of two sets of (low)
d
-dimensional latent factors—
\mathcal{U}\triangleq\{u_{i},i\in[N+1]\}
and
\mathcal{V}\triangleq\{v_{t},t\in[T+1]\}
such that

\displaystyle\theta_{i,t}=f(u_{i},v_{t})\quad\text{for}\quad i\in[N\!+\!1],t\in[T\!+\!1].

(3)

Given our naming convention, we call the latent factors
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
to as unit and time latent factors respectively. The factorization
Eq.

3
essentially states that the outcome of interest
\theta_{i,t}
depends on two latent/hidden factors the unit
i
’s factor
u_{i}
and the time
t
’s factor
v_{t}
.
The model
Eq.

3
with bilinear
f
, i.e.,
f(u,v)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u,v}}\right\rangle
so that
\Theta=\mathcal{U}\mathcal{V}^{\top}
, is ubiquitous in matrix completion literature as well as the works in panel data settings
[
7
,
9
,
3
,
4
]
.
However, when
f
is non-linear and unknown, relatively fewer strategies are known to work.

A popular approach for inference with non-linear factor models is based on nearest neighbors (NN). Such estimators have shown empirically favorable performance in numerous applications in collaborative filtering, recommender systems
[
8
]
and counterfactual inference in panel data settings
[
7
]
. The estimates often come with entry-wise guarantees instead of averaged error guarantee, i.e., suitable bounds on
\|\widehat{\Theta}-\Theta\|_{\max}
are available for NN-based estimates, while typical matrix completion methods provide a bound on
\|\widehat{\Theta}-\Theta\|_{F}
. Consequently, the NN approach is especially suitable for tasks requiring fine-grained estimates with statistical guarantees, e.g., for assessing the quality of personalized recommendations in e-commerce
[
8
]
or the quality of interventions in digital health
[
7
]
.

For the matrix completion task, there are two natural variants of NN when trying to estimate
\theta_{i,t}
: (1)
unit nearest neighbor
(unit-NN) and (2)
time nearest neighbor
(time-NN) depending on how the neighbors are defined.
1
1

1

These two estimators are often called, user-user NN and item-item NN respectively, in the literature focusing on recommender system applications.

Let us briefly summarize the unit-NN approach, which involves two key steps. First, one computes the
unit neighbors

\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i}\subset[N+1]
by comparing the observed outcomes for unit
i
with that of the other units. Next, one averages the observed outcomes of units
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i}
at time
t
.
Roughly speaking, the quality of unit-NN estimate depends on the number of unit neighbors and how well these neighbors’ unit factors, i.e.,
\{u_{j}:j\in[\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i}]\}
, approximates
u_{i}
. Consequently, when there are few unit neighbors or the neighbors’ unit latent factors provide poor approximation to
u_{i}
, e.g., due to heterogeneity in latent unit factors so that
u_{i}
is an outlier, the estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}
provides a poor error.

The time-NN estimate, denoted by
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t}^{\textrm{time}}
is defined analogously to the unit-NN approach by flipping the roles of units and times, where
time neighbors

\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{t}\subset[T+1]
are estimated by comparing the outcomes at time
t
with that of other time points, and the estimate is given by an average of the observed outcomes of unit
i
across
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{t}
. As expected, the performance of the time-NN estimate is dictated by the size and the quality of the time neighbors
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{t}
.

To summarize, vanilla NN estimates (unit- and time-NN), while being favorable due to their interpretability and entry-wise guarantees with non-linear factor model, provide poor performance when the underlying factors are heterogeneous. The starting point of this work is to address this deficit of nearest neighbor estimates and make them more robust to lack of neighbors or equivalently the heterogeneity across latent factors.

Our contributions

We present a new NN estimate that brings together the unit and time-NN estimates to provide a significant improvement for providing entry-wise guarantee for estimating
\Theta
. First, we show that if both unit and time-NN provide a non-trivial non-asymptotic error, the new estimate provides an error that is strictly better than both of them. Moreover, the new estimate provides a non-trivial error decay rate as long as either the unit-NN or time-NN estimate provides a non-trivial error rate. We call this new estimate
doubly robust nearest neighbor estimate
(DR-NN) as its performance is doubly robust the performance of the two vanilla NN approaches. More precisely, for bilinear factor model and with suitable generative assumptions on the latent factors, we show that DR-NN error rate is a near-quadratic improvement over the error rates of the vanilla NN estimates. This improvement also translates to a near-quadratic improvement in the width of the asymptotic confidence intervals for DR-NN estimate when compared the vanilla NN estimates.

2.1
Data generating mechanism

Our observation model is given by
Eq.

2
and our goal is to design a robust NN algorithm for estimating
\theta_{i,t}
. We now state our main assumptions on the mean parameters, noise variables and the missingness pattern. Notably, our general guarantee is stated conditional on
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}
and does not make any distributional assumptions on these latent factors.

Assumption 1

(Latent factorization of mean parameters)
.

Conditioned on the latent factors
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, the mean parameters satisfy
\theta_{i,t}=f(u_{i},v_{t})
.

Assumption 2

(Missingness pattern)
.

Conditioned on the latent factors
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, the random variables
\{A_{i,t}\}
are drawn i.i.d from Bernoulli
(p)
distribution, and are independent of all other randomness.

Assumption 3

(Bounded noise)
.

Conditioned on
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, the noise variables
\{\varepsilon_{i,t}\}
are drawn i.i.d and independent of
\{A_{i,t}\}
and satisfy
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i,t}|\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}]=0
,
\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{i,t})=\sigma^{2}
and
\varepsilon_{i,t}\leq c_{\varepsilon}
almost surely.

All the assumptions are standard in the literature.
2
is often known as the MCAR (missing completely at random) assumption and is standard in the literature for nearest neighbors for matrix completion. The bounded noise assumption is made to simplify the analysis and can be easily relaxed to sub-Gaussian noise for high probability non-asymptotic analysis (
Theorem

3.1
) or noise with bounded high-order moments for asymptotic analysis (
Theorem

3.2
).

2.2.1
An intuitive construction of DR-NN

The notation, and definitions in this section should be treated as informal versions of the estimates defined formally in
Section

2.2.2
. We abuse notation to provide a parallel description behind a constructive intuition in building the improved nearest neighbor estimate.

Here we consider an idealized setting with no noise and a bilinear factor models so that
Y_{i,t}=\theta_{i,t}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{i},v_{t}}}\right\rangle
for all
i,t
. We assume that we observe all but one outcome
Y_{1,1}
and that our goal is to simply estimate the unobserved
\theta_{1,1}
. The two vanilla NN estimates are then defined as follows.

Unit-NN

First one compares the counterfactuals of unit
1
with all other units across all time points to identify the set
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}\subset[N+1]
such that
u_{j}\approx u_{1}
for each
j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}
. The unit-NN estimate is then given by averaging the unit neighbors’ outcomes at time
t
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{unit}}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}}Y_{j,1}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}|}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}}u_{j}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}|},v_{1}}}\right\rangle\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\widehat{u}_{1},v_{1}}}\right\rangle.

(4)

The quality of this estimate is then given by

\displaystyle\theta_{1,1}-\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{unit}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1}-\widehat{u}_{1},v_{1}}}\right\rangle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\theta_{1,1}-\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{unit}}|\leq\|{u_{1}-\widehat{u}_{1}}\|_{2}\|{v_{1}}\|_{2}.

(5)

Time-NN

Flipping the roles of time and unit in the unit-NN approach, first we identify the set
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}\subset[T+1]
such that
v_{t^{\prime}}\approx v_{1}
for each
t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}
. The time-NN estimate is then obtained by averaging the outcomes of unit
1
across these time neighbors:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{time}}=\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}}Y_{1,t^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}|}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1},\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}}v_{t^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}|}}}\right\rangle\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1},\widehat{v}_{1}}}\right\rangle,

(6)

for which the error is given by

\displaystyle\theta_{1,1}-\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{time}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1},v_{1}-\widehat{v}_{1}}}\right\rangle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\theta_{1,1}-\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{time}}|\leq\|{u_{1}}\|_{2}\|{v_{1}-\widehat{v}_{1}}\|_{2}.

(7)

Steps towards an improved nearest neighbors estimate

In this work, we aim to seek a suitable variant of nearest neighbors estimate, denoted by
\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{DR}}
, such that

\displaystyle\theta_{1,1}-\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{DR}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1}-\widehat{u}_{1},v_{1}-\widehat{v}_{1}}}\right\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad|\theta_{1,1}-\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{DR}}|\leq\|{u_{1}-\widehat{u}_{1}}\|_{2}\|{v_{1}-\widehat{v}_{1}}\|_{2},

(8)

where
\widehat{u}_{1}
and
\widehat{v}_{1}
are as defined in
Eqs.

4
and
6
.
Such an estimate, owing to its error bounded by the product of the error in estimating
u_{1}
, and
v_{1}
, would typically exhibit a better error than the previous
\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{unit}}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{time}}
.

“Incorrect” guesses

One might make two “natural” guesses for combining the unit- or time-NN estimates in order to find a better estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{unit}}+\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{time}}}{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:uhat_def},\ref{eq:vhat_def})}}{{=}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\widehat{u}_{1},v_{1}}}\right\rangle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1},\widehat{v}_{1}}}\right\rangle}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad

(9)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}}\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}}Y_{j,t^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}|\,|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}|}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}}u_{j}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}|},\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}}v_{t^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}|}}}\right\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:uhat_def},\ref{eq:vhat_def})}}{{=}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\widehat{u}_{1},\widehat{v}_{1}}}\right\rangle,

(10)

neither of which achieve the first equality in
Eq.

8
. Moreover,
simple algebra reveals that

\displaystyle|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1},v_{1}}}\right\rangle-\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{(1)}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|{u_{1}}\|_{2}\|{v_{1}-\widehat{v}_{1}}\|_{2}+\|{u_{1}-\widehat{u}_{1}}\|_{2}\|{v_{1}}\|_{2}}{2}\ \text{and}\

(11)

\displaystyle|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1},v_{1}}}\right\rangle-\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{(2)}|

\displaystyle\leq\|{u_{1}}\|_{2}\|{v_{1}-\widehat{v}_{1}}\|_{2}+\|{u_{1}-\widehat{u}_{1}}\|_{2}\|{v_{1}}\|_{2},

(12)

neither of which are analogous to our desired inequality from display
Eq.

8
.

The “correct” guess

Next, we make a constructive guess for
\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{DR}}
. Substituting
\theta_{1,1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1},v_{1}}}\right\rangle
and some algebra yields that

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{1,1}^{\textrm{DR}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{1},\widehat{v}_{1}}}\right\rangle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\widehat{u}_{1},v_{1}}}\right\rangle-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\widehat{u}_{1},\widehat{v}_{1}}}\right\rangle

(13)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:uhat_def},\ref{eq:vhat_def})}}{{=}}\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}}Y_{1,t^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}|}+\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}}Y_{j,1}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}|}-\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}}\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}}Y_{j,t^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}|\,|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}|}

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1},t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}}(Y_{1,t^{\prime}}+Y_{j,1}-Y_{j,t^{\prime}})}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{1}|\,|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{1}|},

(15)

which can be easily computed from the observed data. This intuitive construction turns out to be the “right” one and can be generalized to the case with noisy observations with missing data as well as a class of non-linear factor models. Of course, the construction here was informal and several key steps remain to be formalized, which we now turn our attention to.

2.2.2
Vanilla-NN and DR-NN for missing and noisy data

We begin by formally describing the two vanilla NN variants and conclude with DR-NN.

Unit-NN

Given a threshold
\eta_{1}
, define the unit neighbors
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}\triangleq\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\neq i\!\ \big|\ \!\widehat{\rho}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t}(j)\!\leq\!\eta_{1}}}\right\}\,\ \text{with}\ \,\widehat{\rho}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t}(j)\!\triangleq\!\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\neq t}A_{i,t^{\prime}}A_{j,t^{\prime}}(Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\!-\!Y_{j,t^{\prime}})^{2}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}\neq t}A_{i,t^{\prime}}A_{j,t^{\prime}}}\ \text{for}\ j\neq i.

(16)

In words,
\widehat{\rho}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t}(j)
denotes the mean squared distance of observed outcomes for unit
i
and
j
at time points except
t
and the set
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}
denotes the units that are within
\eta_{1}
distance from unit
i
. Then the unit-NN estimate is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}^{\textrm{unit}}\triangleq\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}}A_{j,t}Y_{j,t}}{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}}A_{j,t}},

(17)

Time-NN

Given the threshold
\eta_{2}
, define the time neighbors
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}\triangleq\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t^{\prime}\neq t\!\ \big|\ \!\widehat{\rho}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t}(t^{\prime})\!\leq\!\eta_{2}}}\right\}\,\ \text{with}\ \,\widehat{\rho}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t}(t^{\prime})\!\triangleq\!\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}A_{j,t}A_{j,t}(Y_{j,t}\!-\!Y_{j,t^{\prime}})^{2}}{\sum_{j\neq i}A_{j,t}A_{j,t^{\prime}}}\ \text{for}\ t^{\prime}\neq t.

(18)

In words,
\widehat{\rho}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t}(t^{\prime})
denotes the mean squared distance of observed outcomes for time
t
and
t^{\prime}
across units except unit
i
and the set
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}
denotes the time points that are within
\eta_{2}
distance from time
t
. Then the time-NN estimate is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}^{\textrm{time}}\triangleq\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}}A_{i,t^{\prime}}Y_{i,t^{\prime}}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}}A_{i,t^{\prime}}}.

(19)

DR-NN

We now formally define the DR-NN studied in this work. With the definitions
Eqs.

16
and
18
in place, and using the notation
\boldsymbol{\eta}\triangleq(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
, the doubly robust estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\textrm{DR}}
can be defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\textrm{DR}}\triangleq\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}}A_{i,t^{\prime}}A_{j,t}A_{j,t^{\prime}}(Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\!+\!Y_{j,t}\!-\!Y_{j,t^{\prime}})}{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}}A_{i,t^{\prime}}A_{j,t}A_{j,t^{\prime}}}.

(20)

Put simply, the DR estimate leverages both unit and time neighbors as each term in the numerator of definition
Eq.

20
is made of the sum of (i) the outcome of unit
i
at a similar time point
t^{\prime}
, and (ii) the outcome of a similar unit
j
at time
t
, (iii) with an offset term of the outcome of unit
j
and time
t^{\prime}
.

Confidence interval

Define

\displaystyle J_{i,t,\boldsymbol{\eta}}\triangleq\big(\frac{1}{\Sigma_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}}A_{i,t^{\prime}}}+\frac{1}{\Sigma_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}}A_{j,t}}+\frac{1}{\Sigma_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}}\Sigma_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}}A_{i,t^{\prime}}A_{j,t^{\prime}}A_{j,t}}\big)^{-1}.

(21)

Then our
(1\!-\!\alpha)
confidence interval for
\theta_{i,t}
is given by

\displaystyle\bigg(\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\textrm{DR}}\!\!-\!\!\frac{z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\,\widehat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{J_{i,t,\boldsymbol{\eta}}}},\,\,\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\textrm{DR}}\!\!+\!\!\frac{z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\,\widehat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{J_{i,t,\boldsymbol{\eta}}}}\bigg),

(22)

where
z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
denotes the
1\!-\!\frac{\alpha}{2}
quantile of standard normal random variable, and
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
is a consistent estimate of the noise-variance (which can be obtained, e.g., as the validation mean square error like in
[
7
, App. E.2]
).

Remark 1

(Sample split for theoretical analysis)
.

To prove our results, we assume that the doubly robust estimate was constructed using a data-split. In particular, we use do a
2\times 2
data split chosen uniformly at random: (i) Two equal splits of time points
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t^{\prime}\in[T]:t^{\prime}\neq t}}\right\}
into
\mathbf{T}_{1}
and
\mathbf{T}_{2}
, and (ii) two equal splits of units
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\in[N]:j\neq i}}\right\}
into
\mathbf{N}_{1}
and
\mathbf{N}_{2}
. We use time points
\mathbf{T}_{1}
to compute the unit distances
\widehat{\rho}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t}(j)
for
j\in\mathbf{N}_{1}
, and identify the unit-neighbors only in the set
\mathbf{N}_{1}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}\subset\mathbf{N}_{1}
. Next, we use units
\mathbf{N}_{2}
to compute the time distances
\widehat{\rho}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t}(t^{\prime})
for
t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{T}_{2}
, and identify the time-neighbors only in the set
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}\subset\mathbf{T}_{2}
.
On the one hand, this data split inflates the variance of our estimator.
On the other hand, it removes the (hard to analyze) bias that might arise due to data-reuse in estimating the two neighbors sets
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}
and
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}
without any data split.

(Estimates when there are no neighbors)
.

The definitions
Eqs.

17
,
19
,
20
, and
21
assume
that the corresponding denominator is not
0
and our analysis provides a guarantee also only for that case (see
Remark

3
. When the denominators in the estimates’ displays are

, we can simply use
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta_{2}}^{\textrm{time}}=Y_{i,t}
if
A_{i,t}=1

3
Main results

In this section, we present our main result, a unit
\times
time wise non-asymptotic guarantee for the doubly-robust nearest neighbor estimate and then discuss its consequences.

3.3.1
DR-NN estimate is doubly robust to heterogeneity in user and time factors

First, we show that the DR-NN estimate is (roughly) at least as good as the better of the two vanilla NN estimates.

Suppose
\widehat{\theta}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}^{\textrm{time}}
provides a vanishing error, so that at optimum we have
\mathrm{Err}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}^{\textrm{time}}\asymp\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}\asymp\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}=\Omega(N^{-1/2})

Next, choose
\eta_{1}
large enough, such that
\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{1}}=\Theta(1)

\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{1}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(N^{-1})

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\textrm{DR}}}^{\textrm{DR}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\mathrm{Err}_{(\eta_{1},\eta_{\textrm{time}})}^{\textrm{DR}}\precsim\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{1}}\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}+\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{1}}+\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\asymp}}\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}+\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}=\mathrm{Err}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}^{\textrm{time}}

(37)

where step (i) follows from optimality of
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\textrm{DR}}
and step (ii) from the fact that
\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}=\Omega(N^{-1/2})

Flipping the roles of

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\textrm{DR}}}^{\textrm{DR}}\leq\mathrm{Err}_{(\eta_{\textrm{unit}},\eta_{2})}^{\textrm{DR}}\precsim\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{2}}+\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}+\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\asymp}}\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}+\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\textrm{unit}}=\mathrm{Err}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}^{\textrm{unit}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\textrm{DR}}}^{\textrm{DR}}\precsim\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{Err}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}^{\textrm{unit}},\,\mathrm{Err}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}^{\textrm{time}}}}\right\}.

We now consider the setting where both time and unit neighbors estimate provides vanishing error.
Note that for the optimal choices of thresholds,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}^{\textrm{unit}}\asymp\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}\asymp\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}\ll 1,\quad\mathrm{Err}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}^{\textrm{time}}\asymp\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}\asymp\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}\ll 1.

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}\mathbb{B}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}\asymp\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}\ll\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}+\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:dr_decomp}}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\mathrm{Err}_{(\eta_{\textrm{unit}},\eta_{\textrm{time}})}^{\textrm{DR}}=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{unit}}_{\eta_{\textrm{unit}}}+\mathbb{V}^{\textrm{time}}_{\eta_{\textrm{time}}}}}\right).

Discussion

In this section, we present a detailed discussion of our results and its consequences in the context of broader literature on missing data, doubly robust estimation, and causal panel data problems. We start by illustrating in
Section

4.1
how the intuitive construction from
Section

2.2

4.1
“Triply robust” nearest neighbors for tensor denoising

The ideas considered in this work can also be extended to construct improved nearest neighbor estimates for denoising tensor latent factor model. Consider an
N\times T\times M
dimensional tensor
Y
that satisfies

\displaystyle Y=\Theta+E\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad Y_{i,t,a}=\theta_{i,t,a}+\varepsilon_{i,t,a}\quad\text{where}\quad\theta_{i,t,a}=\sum_{r=1}^{d}u_{i,r}v_{t,r}w_{a,r},

(61)

i.e.,
\theta_{i,t,a}=u_{i}\bigotimes v_{t}\bigotimes w_{a}
for
(i,t,a)\in[N]\times[T]\times[M]
. Here
u_{i},v_{t},w_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the latent factors associated with the three indices and all dimensions
N,T,M
are assumed to be large.
For simplicity, we assume the tensor is fully observed (so that we are doing tensor denoising) but our estimate can be easily generalized to the setting where entries in the tensor are missing completely at random (so that we are doing tensor completion).

Consider the task of estimating
\theta_{i,t,a}
via nearest neighbors and suppose we are given the neighbors along the three dimensions: (i) unit neighbors
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,a}\subset[N]
(ii) time neighbors
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{intervention}}_{i,t,a}\subset[T]
and (iii)
\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,a}\subset[M]
. Then the
nanilla nearest neighbor estimates for estimating
\theta_{i,t,a}
would correspond to averaging the outcomes (i)
\{Y_{j,t,a},j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,a}\}
, namely unit-NN, (ii)
\{Y_{i,t^{\prime},a},t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,a}\}
, namely time-NN, and (iii)
\{Y_{i,t,a^{\prime}},a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{intervention}}_{i,t,a}\}
, namely intervention-NN.

The ideas in the paper can be directly applied to build an estimator that provides good error as long as any of the three vanilla NN estimates provides good error, and an improved error if two or more of the estimates provide vanishing error. The starting point of building such an estimator is a calculation akin to
Eq.

15
. Suppose we consider a simplified noiseless setting where
Y_{j^{\prime},t^{\prime},a^{\prime}}=u_{j^{\prime}}v_{t^{\prime}}w_{a^{\prime}}
and we are missing the entry
Y_{i,t,a}=u_{i}v_{t}w_{a}
. Then our goal is to find an estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,a}^{TR}
such that

\displaystyle u_{i}v_{t}w_{a}-\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,a}^{TR}

\displaystyle=(u_{i}-\widehat{u}_{i})(v_{t}-\widehat{v}_{t})(w_{a}-\widehat{w}_{a})

(62)

\displaystyle=(u_{i}v_{t}+\widehat{u}_{i}\widehat{v}_{t}-u_{i}\widehat{v}_{t}-\widehat{u}_{i}v_{t})(w_{a}-\widehat{w}_{a})

(63)

\displaystyle=u_{i}v_{t}w_{a}+\widehat{u}_{i}\widehat{v}_{t}w_{a}-u_{i}\widehat{v}_{t}w_{a}-\widehat{u}_{i}v_{t}w_{a}-u_{i}v_{t}\widehat{w}_{a}-\widehat{u}_{i}\widehat{v}_{t}\widehat{w}_{a}+u_{i}\widehat{v}_{t}\widehat{w}_{a}+\widehat{u}_{i}v_{t}\widehat{w}_{a},

(64)

which motivates the following estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,a}^{TR}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,a},t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,a},a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{intervention}}_{i,t,a}}Y_{j,t,a}+Y_{i,t^{\prime},a}+Y_{i,t,a^{\prime}}+Y_{j,t^{\prime},a^{\prime}}-Y_{j,t^{\prime},a}-Y_{j,t,a^{\prime}}-Y_{i,t^{\prime},a^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{unit}}_{i,t,a}||\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{time}}_{i,t,a}||\mathbf{S}^{\textrm{intervention}}_{i,t,a}|}.

(65)

The analysis of this paper can be extended to show that this estimator is robust to hetergeoneity in the three sets of factors
\{u_{j}\}
,
\{v_{t^{\prime}}\}
and
\{w_{a^{\prime}}\}
. In particular, the performance of the estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,a}^{TR}
would be at least as good as the best of the three vanilla NN estimates whenever at least one of them provides a non-trivial error, and better than any of them as long as at least two of them provide a non-trivial error.

4.2
Alternative derivations of DR-NN

While we provided an intuitive justification of DR-NN estimator earlier in
Section

2.2.2
, we now show how it can also be derived using other commonly used ideas of bias-correction and orthogonal loss function minimization.

DR-NN as bias adjusted vanilla NN

Consider the following term appearing in the sum on the numerator of the RHS in the display
Eq.

20
:

\displaystyle Y_{i,t^{\prime}}+Y_{j,t}-Y_{j,t^{\prime}}.

(66)

Note that the term
Y_{i,t^{\prime}}
can be treated as the NN estimate for unit
i
at time
t
, estimated using the time neighbor
t^{\prime}
. With this view point, the term
Y_{j,t^{\prime}}-Y_{j,t}
approximates the bias of the time neighbor
t^{\prime}
, for estimating outcomes at time
t
(notably the bias is estimated using unit neighbors for unit
i
). Since the term
Eq.

66
is equal to the time-NN estimate minus the bias proxy, we can interpret it (and thereby its average, the DR-NN estimate) as a bias-adjusted time NN estimate, where the unit neighbors are being used to approximate the bias. By flipping the roles of time and unit neighbors, i.e., by treating the term
Y_{j,t}
as the unit-NN estimate, and the term
Y_{j,t^{\prime}}-Y_{i,t^{\prime}}
as the bias approximation, DR-NN estimate can be interpreted as bias-adjusted unit NN. This construction can be interpreted as a suitable adaptation of the bias-corrected matching estimators for average treatment effects. In particular,
[
1
]
used a covariate-based outcome prediction to approximate the bias and subsequently, use the estimate to debias their NN estimate for average treatment effect in a static setting with a single outcome for each unit
[
1
]
. In contrast, here we directly use suitably chosen outcomes for bias adjustment, and provide entry-wise guarantees in a setting with multiple observations for multiple units (without using any covariates).

DR-NN as minimizer of an orthogonal loss function

Since our main goal is estimating
\theta_{i,t}
, the latent factors
u_{i}
and
v_{t}
serve as nuisance parameters. We can define a loss function for estimating the parameter of interest as a function of the nuisance parameters:

\displaystyle L_{\star}(\theta_{i,t},(u,v))=(\theta_{i,t}-u^{\top}v)^{2}

(67)

A zeroth-order approximation of this loss function is given by
L_{0}(\theta,(u,v))=(\theta-u^{\top}v)^{2}
. So that for the nuisance estimates
\widehat{u}_{i}
and
\widehat{v}_{t}
, the estimate for
\theta_{i,t}
satisfies

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\theta}L_{0}(\theta,(\widehat{u}_{i},\widehat{v}_{t}))=\widehat{u}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{v}_{t}.

(68)

A first-order approximation to the true loss, also called the orthogonal loss
[
5
, Eq. (1)]
, is given by

\displaystyle L_{1}(\theta,(u,v))

\displaystyle\triangleq L_{0}(\theta,(u,v))+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla_{u,v}L_{0}(u,v),\begin{bmatrix}u_{i}-u\\
v_{t}-v\end{bmatrix}}}\right\rangle

(69)

\displaystyle=(\theta-u^{\top}v)^{2}+2(u^{\top}v-\theta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}v\\
u\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}u_{i}-u\\
v_{t}-v\end{bmatrix}}}\right\rangle

(70)

\displaystyle=(\theta-u^{\top}v)^{2}+2(u^{\top}v-\theta)(v^{\top}u_{i}+u^{\top}v_{t}-2u^{\top}v)

(71)

Now, given the nuisance estimates
\widehat{u}_{i}
and
\widehat{v}_{t}
, the minimizer of the
L_{1}
for
\theta_{i,t}
satisfies

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\theta}L_{1}(\theta,(\widehat{u}_{i},\widehat{v}_{t}))=\widehat{u}_{i}v_{t}+u_{i}\widehat{v}_{t}-\widehat{u}_{i}\widehat{v}_{t},

(72)

which matches the expression in
Eq.

13
. Thus, this construction can be treated as a particular instance of orthogonal machine learning, adapted to panel data settings.

4.3
Consequences for counterfactual inference and causal panel data problems

We now provide a brief illustration of how that our model and estimates are relevant also for counterfactual inference in panel data settings and on-adaptive experiments. For example, consider a setting with
N+1

\{0,1\}
over

denotes the outcome observed for unit

\textstyle Y_{i,t}^{(a)}=\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}+\varepsilon_{i,t}\quad\text{for}\quad a\in\mathcal{A}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{i,t}=Y_{i,t}^{(A_{i,t})}.

(73)

Here for each treatment
a\in\{0,1\}
, the tuple
(Y_{i,t}^{(a)},\theta_{i,t}^{(a)})
denotes the counterfactual outcome (also referred to as the potential outcome
[
10
,
12
]
) and its mean under treatment
A_{i,t}=a
and
\varepsilon_{i,t}
denotes exogenous and i.i.d zero mean noise with variance
\sigma^{2}
.
It is easy to see that
73
can be treated as two instances of the problem described in
Eq.

2
. That is, the estimates and the associated guarantees derived in this work directly apply for the task of estimating both
\theta_{i,t}^{(0)}
and
\theta_{i,t}^{(1)}
when the treatments are assigned independently with constant probability
p
. We note that such treatment patterns are commonly used for just in time interventions for experiments on digital platforms, like mobile health and online education. Such studies often form the basis of subsequent adaptive trials and are called exploratory trials . In prior work, NN-based estimates have been used for counterfactual inference in such settings and for these problems DR-NN strategy provides an improved set of estimates.

Relation to synthetic difference in differences (SDID)

We note that the SDID estimator
[
4
]
shares similarities with the DR-NN estimator in this work. SDID estimates the averaged outcome for a collection of units over a collection of time points, by combining two classical estimators (i) synthetic control and (ii) difference-in-difference and then debiasing them. The estimator has a same form as our DR-NN, once we map unit-NN as a special case of synthetic control estimator and time-NN as a special case of difference-in-difference estimator. While unit-NN, time-NN, and DR-NN each provide guarantees for a single outcome, the SC and DID estimators provide non-trivial error guarantees for an averaged outcome across time for a given unit and averaged outcome across units for a given time respectively, while SDID provides non-trivial error for averages of multiple units across multiple time points. Moreover, SC, DID and SDID do not handle missing data settings like considered here while NN estimators are well-suited for the settings considered by SC, DID and SDID, as well as the settings considered in this work where entries are stochastically missing (or the treatments are randomized). With these points in mind, we conclude that DR-NN can be interpreted as a generalization of the SDID estimator to obtain unit-time level treatment effect guarantees for a wide range of treatment patterns.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced and analyzed doubly robust variant of nearest neighbors. We provided non-asymptotic and asymptotic guarantees for entry-wise estimation for missing panel data under a latent factor model. We showed that the estimate’s double robustness to the underlying heterogeneity in latent factors makes it a superior algorithm compared to the unit-NN and time-NN algorithms.

The simplicity and interpretability of nearest neighbors makes them attractive approaches for estimation in a range of settings and consequently this work offers several venues of future research. We assumed that the data is missing in an exogenous manner. However, in many settings, like causal panel data, the missingness pattern is often endogenous as the likelihood of observing an outcome depends on the underling mean parameter. In sequential experimental settings, the missingness is determined by a learning policy which takes in historical observations to decide which entries to observe next. Vanilla NN variants or their suitable variations have been shown to be effective in both such settings
[
2
,
7
]
. Developing and analyzing suitable analogs of doubly robust NN for such settings is of significant interest. Furthermore, we assumed, like prior works on nearest neighbors, that the noise in the observations have identical variances. It is of natural interest to empower NN algorithms to handle heteroskedastic noise.
Finally, recent work
[
13
]
has established favorable results with nearest neighbors for matrix estimation with side information. In particular, assuming that one set of (unit or time) factors are known, they establish minimax optimality of a suitable variant of NN for a range of settings. A natural future direction is to design NN estimates that are also robust to imperfect knowledge of the side information in such problems.
