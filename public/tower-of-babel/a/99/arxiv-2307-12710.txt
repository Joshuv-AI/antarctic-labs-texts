Weakly distance-regular circulants, I
Akihiro Munemasa, Kaishun Wang, Yuefeng Yang, Wenying Zhu

Abstract
. We classify certain non-symmetric commutative association schemes. As an application, we determine all the weakly distance-regular circulants of one type of arcs by using Schur rings. We also give the classification of primitive weakly distance-regular circulants.

1.
Introduction

As a natural directed version of distance-regular graphs (see
[
3
,
4
]
for a background of the theory of distance-regular graphs), Wang and Suzuki
[
15
]
introduced the concept of weakly distance-regular digraphs. Both distance-regular graphs and weakly distance-regular digraphs can be regarded as association schemes.

A
d
-class association scheme

\mathfrak{X}
is a pair
(X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
, where
X
is a finite set, and each
R_{i}
is a
nonempty subset of
X\times X
satisfying the following axioms (see
[
2
,
24
,
25
]
for a background of the theory of association schemes):

(i)

R_{0}=\{(x,x)\mid x\in X\}
is the diagonal relation;

(ii)

X\times X=R_{0}\cup R_{1}\cup\cdots\cup R_{d}
,
R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset~(i\neq j)
;

(iii)

for each
i
,
R_{i}^{\rm T}=R_{i^{*}}
for some
0\leq i^{*}\leq d
, where
R_{i}^{\rm T}=\{(y,x)\mid(x,y)\in R_{i}\}
;

(iv)

for all
i,j,l
, the cardinality of the set

P_{i,j}(x,y):=R_{i}(x)\cap R_{j^{*}}(y)

is constant whenever
(x,y)\in R_{l}
, where
R(x)=\{y\mid(x,y)\in R\}
for
R\subseteq X\times X
and
x\in X
. This constant is denoted by
p_{i,j}^{l}
.

A
d
-class association scheme is also called an association scheme with
d
classes (or even simply a scheme). The integers
p_{i,j}^{l}
are called the
intersection numbers
of
\mathfrak{X}
. We say that
\mathfrak{X}
is
commutative
if
p_{i,j}^{l}=p_{j,i}^{l}
for all
i,j,l
. The subsets
R_{i}
are called the
relations
of
\mathfrak{X}
. For each
i
, the integer
k_{i}:=p_{i,i^{*}}^{0}
is called the
valency
of
R_{i}
. A relation
R_{i}
is called
symmetric
if
i=i^{*}
, and
non-symmetric
otherwise. An association scheme is called
symmetric
if all relations are symmetric, and
non-symmetric
otherwise. An association
scheme is called
skew-symmetric
if the diagonal relation is the
only symmetric relation.

Let
\mathfrak{X}=(X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
be an association scheme. For two nonempty subsets
E
and
F
of
\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d}
, define

EF:=\{R_{l}\mid\sum_{R_{i}\in E}\sum_{R_{j}\in F}p_{i,j}^{l}\neq 0\}.

We write
R_{i}R_{j}
instead of
\{R_{i}\}\{R_{j}\}
,
and
R_{i}^{2}
instead of
\{R_{i}\}\{R_{i}\}
.
If
R_{i^{*}}R_{j}\subseteq F
for any
R_{i},R_{j}\in F
, we say that
F
is
closed
. We say that
R_{j}

generates

\mathfrak{X}
if the smallest
closed subset containing
R_{j}
is equal to
\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d}
. We call
\mathfrak{X}

primitive
if every non-diagonal relation generates
\mathfrak{X}
. Otherwise,
\mathfrak{X}
is said to be
imprimitive
.

In this paper, we classify certain non-symmetric commutative association schemes. Our first main result is as follows. See Section 2 for precise definitions of a pseudocyclic scheme and a cyclotomic scheme.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{X}=(X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
be a commutative association scheme generated by a non-symmetric relation
R_{1}
with
k_{1}>1
satisfying

(1)

\displaystyle R_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{R_{1},R_{1^{*}},R_{2}\},

(2)

\displaystyle R_{1}R_{1^{*}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{R_{0},R_{1},R_{1^{*}},R_{2},R_{2^{*}}\},

(3)

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\notin\{1^{*},2^{*}\}.

If
k_{1}\geq k_{2}
, then
d=4
. Moreover, if
\mathfrak{X}
is pseudocyclic, then
\mathfrak{X}
is isomorphic to the cyclotomic scheme
{\rm Cyc}(13,4)
.

1.1
, we obtain the classification of weakly distance-regular circulants of one type of arcs. In order to state our results, we introduce some basic notations and terminologies about weakly distance-regular circulants. See

,

We always use
\Gamma
to denote a finite simple digraph, which is not undirected. We write
V\Gamma
and
A\Gamma
for the vertex set and arc set of

\Gamma
, respectively. For any

, let
\partial_{\Gamma}(x,y)
be the

from
x
to
y
in
\Gamma
, and
{\tilde{\partial}}_{\Gamma}(x,y):=(\partial_{\Gamma}(x,y),\partial_{\Gamma}(y,x))
the
two-way distance
from
x
to
y
in
\Gamma
. If no confusion occurs, we write
\partial(x,y)
(resp.
\tilde{\partial}(x,y)
) instead of
\partial_{\Gamma}(x,y)
(resp.
{\tilde{\partial}}_{\Gamma}(x,y)
). Denote
\tilde{\partial}(\Gamma)=\{\tilde{\partial}(x,y)\mid x,y\in V\Gamma\}
and
\Gamma_{\tilde{i}}=\{(x,y)\in V\Gamma\times V\Gamma\mid\tilde{\partial}(x,y)=\tilde{i}\}
. An arc
(u,v)
of
\Gamma
is of
type

(1,r)

\partial(v,u)=r
.

A strongly
connected digraph
\Gamma
is said to be
weakly distance-regular
if the configuration
\mathfrak{X}(\Gamma)=(V\Gamma,\{\Gamma_{\tilde{i}}\}_{\tilde{i}\in\tilde{\partial}(\Gamma)})
is an association scheme. This is a directed generalization of distance-regular graphs (see
[
3
]
). We call
\mathfrak{X}(\Gamma)
the
attached scheme
of
\Gamma
. We say that
\Gamma
is
primitive
(resp.

) if
\mathfrak{X}(\Gamma)
is primitive (resp. commutative). For each
(a,b)\in\tilde{\partial}(\Gamma)
, we write
\Gamma_{a,b}
(resp.
k_{a,b}
) instead of
\Gamma_{(a,b)}
(resp.
k_{(a,b)}
).

Let
G
be a finite, multiplicatively written, group with identity
e
, and
S
be a subset of
G
without identity. A
Cayley digraph
of a group
G
with respect
to the set
S
, denoted by
\textrm{Cay}(G,S)
, is the digraph with vertex set
G
, where

(x,y)
is an arc whenever
yx^{-1}\in S
. A digraph which is isomorphic to a Cayley digraph of a cyclic group is called a
circulant
. For a positive integer
n
, denote by
C_{n}
the Cayley digraph
{\rm Cay}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\{1\})
,
K_{n}
the complete graph
{\rm Cay}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus\{0\})

and
\overline{K}_{n}
the edgeless graph
{\rm Cay}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\emptyset)
, respectively.

A classical example of weakly distance-regular Cayley digraphs are Paley digraphs, defined as follows. For a prime power

q=p^{m}
with
q\equiv 3~(\textrm{mod}~4)
, let
\textrm{GF}(q)
denote the finite field of cardinality
q
, let
S
denote the set of all squares in the multiplicative group of
\textrm{GF}(q)
, and let
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m}
denote the additive group
of
\textrm{GF}(q)
, where
\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
is the cyclic group of order
p
, written additively. The
Paley digraph
P(q)
of order
q
is defined as the digraph
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
.

For the digraphs
\Gamma
and
\Sigma
, the
direct product

\Gamma\times\Sigma
is the digraph with vertex set
V\Gamma\times V\Sigma
such that
((u_{1},u_{2}),(v_{1},v_{2}))
is an arc if and only if
(u_{1},v_{1})\in A\Gamma
and
(u_{2},v_{2})\in A\Sigma
; the
lexicographic product

\Gamma[\Sigma]
is the digraph with vertex set
V\Gamma\times V\Sigma
such that
((u_{1},u_{2}),(v_{1},v_{2}))
is an arc if and only if either
(u_{1},v_{1})\in A\Gamma
, or

u_{1}=v_{1}
and
(u_{2},v_{2})\in A\Sigma
.

Our second main theorem is as follows, which classifies all weakly distance-regular circulants of one type of arcs.

Theorem 1.2
.

A digraph

\Gamma
is a weakly distance-regular circulant of one type of arcs,
if and only if

\Gamma
is isomorphic to one of the following digraphs:

(i)

C_{m}[\overline{K}_{l}]
;

(ii)

P(p)[\overline{K}_{l}]
;

(iii)

C_{3}\times K_{h}
;

(iv)

{\rm Cay}(\mathbb{Z}_{13},\{1,3,9\})[\overline{K}_{l}]
.

Here,
l\geq 1
,
m\geq 3
,
h,p>3
,
3\nmid h
and
p
is prime such that
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

In the forthcoming paper
[
11
]
, we shall classify weakly distance-regular circulants of more than one types of arcs. The following result is a consequence of Theorem
1.2
, which determines all primitive weakly distance-regular circulants.

Corollary 1.3
.

A digraph

\Gamma
is a primitive weakly distance-regular circulant,
if and only if

\Gamma
is isomorphic to
C_{p}
,
P(r)
or
{\rm Cay}(\mathbb{Z}_{13},\{1,3,9\})
, where
p,r
are primes with
r\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathfrak{X}=(X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
be a commutative association scheme with
|X|=n
. The
adjacency matrix

A_{i}
of
R_{i}
is the
n\times n
matrix whose
(x,y)
-entry is
1
if
(x,y)\in R_{i}
, and
0
otherwise. By the
adjacency
or
Bose-Mesner algebra

\mathfrak{U}
of
\mathfrak{X}
we mean the algebra generated by
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}
over the complex field. Axioms
(i)
–
(iv)
are equivalent to the following:

A_{0}=I,\quad\sum_{i=0}^{d}A_{i}=J,\quad A_{i}^{\rm T}=A_{i^{*}},\quad A_{i}A_{j}=\sum_{l=0}^{d}p_{i,j}^{l}A_{l},

where
I
and
J
are the identity and all-one matrices of order
n
, respectively.

Since
\mathfrak{U}
consists of commuting normal matrices, it has a second basis consisting of primitive idempotents
E_{0}=J/n,E_{1},\ldots,E_{d}
. The integers
m_{i}=\textrm{rank}E_{i}
are called the
multiplicities
of
\mathfrak{X}
, and
m_{0}=1
is said to be the trivial multiplicity. A commutative association scheme is called
pseudocyclic
if all the non-trivial multiplicities coincide with each other.

Now we list basic properties of intersection
numbers.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
2
, Chapter II, Proposition 2.2]
and
[
1
, Proposition 5.1]
)
.

Let the configuration
(X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
be an association scheme. The following hold:

(i)

k_{i}k_{j}=\sum_{l=0}^{d}p_{i,j}^{l}k_{l}
;

(ii)

p_{i,j}^{l}k_{l}=p_{l,j^{*}}^{i}k_{i}=p_{i^{*},l}^{j}k_{j}
;

(iii)

\sum_{j=0}^{d}p_{i,j}^{l}=k_{i}
;

(iv)

\sum_{\alpha=0}^{d}p_{i,j}^{\alpha}p_{f,\alpha}^{l}=\sum_{\beta=0}^{d}p_{f,i}^{\beta}p_{\beta,j}^{l}
;

(v)

{\rm lcm}(k_{i},k_{j})\mid p_{i,j}^{l}k_{l}
.

We call an association scheme
(X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
a
translation scheme
, if the underlying set
X
has the structure of an additive group, and for all relations
R_{i}
,

(x,y)\in R_{i}\Longleftrightarrow(x+z,y+z)\in R_{i}~\textrm{for all~$z\in X$}.

A classical example of translation schemes is the cyclotomic scheme which we describe now. Let
\alpha
be a primitive element of
\textrm{GF}(q)
, where
q
is a prime power. For a fixed divisor
d
of
q-1
, define

(x,y)\in R_{i}~~\textrm{if}~~x-y\in\alpha^{i}\langle\alpha^{d}\rangle,~1\leq i\leq d.

These relations
R_{i}
define a pseudocyclic scheme, denoted by
\textrm{Cyc}(q,d)
, called the
d
-class
cyclotomic scheme
over
{\rm GF}(q)
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
3
, Theorem 2.10.5]
)
.

Let
(X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
be a primitive translation scheme with
d\geq 2
. If
X
has some cyclic Sylow subgroup (and in particular if
X
is cyclic), then

|X|
is prime and
(X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
is cyclotomic.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
({\rm GF}(q),\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d})
be the
d
-class cyclotomic scheme. For
1\leq i\leq d
and
s\in{\rm GF}(q)\setminus\{0\}
, define

\displaystyle R_{i}^{(s)}=\{(sx,sy)\mid(x,y)\in R_{i}\}.

Then, for any fixed
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
,

\displaystyle\{R_{i}^{(s)}\mid s\in{\rm GF}(q)\setminus\{0\}\}=\{R_{j}\mid 1\leq j\leq d\}.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be a primitive element of
{\rm GF}(q)
. Then

\displaystyle\{s\alpha^{i}\langle\alpha^{d}\rangle\mid s\in{\rm GF}(q)\setminus\{0\}\}=\{\alpha^{j}\langle\alpha^{d}\rangle\mid 1\leq j\leq d\},

and the result follows immediately from this.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
2.3
, as digraphs, we have
(\textrm{GF}(q),R_{i})\cong(\textrm{GF}(q),R_{j})

for
1\leq i,j\leq d
. For example when
(q,d)=(13,4)
we have
(\textrm{GF}(13),R_{i})\cong\textrm{Cay}(\mathbb{Z}_{13},\{1,3,9\})
for
1\leq i\leq 4
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{X}=(X,\{R_{0},R_{1},R_{1^{*}},R_{2},R_{2^{*}}\})
be a
4
-class skew-symmetric and pseudocyclic association scheme. If
|X|>5
and
p_{1,1}^{2^{*}}=0
, then
\mathfrak{X}
is isomorphic to
{\rm Cyc}(13,4)
.

Proof.

The symmetrization
(X,\{R_{0},R_{1}\cup R_{1^{*}},R_{2}\cup R_{2^{*}}\})
of
\mathfrak{X}
is a pseudocyclic association scheme with 2 classes. Thus,
\mathfrak{X}
is a skew-symmetric fission of a conference graph (see
[
9
]
for definitions). By the proof of
[
9
, Theorem 3.3]
, there exist integers
u
and
v
such that

(4)

\displaystyle|X|

\displaystyle=u^{2}+4v^{2},

\displaystyle p_{1,1}^{2^{*}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|X|+1+2u\pm 8v}{16}.

Since
p_{1,1}^{2^{*}}=0
, we have
v^{2}=(|X|+1+2u)^{2}/64
. Substituting this into (
4
), we obtain

20u^{2}+4(|X|+1)u+|X|^{2}-14|X|+1=0.

Since
u
is an integer, the discriminant of the quadratic is not less than zero. Thus

(|X|+1)^{2}-5(|X|^{2}-14|X|+1)\geq 0.

It follows that
9-4\sqrt{5}\leq|X|\leq 9+4\sqrt{5}
. By
[
9
, Theorem 3.3]
, one gets
|X|\equiv 5~(\textrm{mod}~8)
, which implies
|X|=13
. In view of
[
6
, Result 1]
,
\mathfrak{X}
is isomorphic to
\textrm{Cyc}(13,4)
.
∎

We close this section with three results on weakly distance-regular digraphs.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a commutative weakly distance-regular digraph. Suppose that
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
is a path of length
m>0
. For each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
, there exists a path
(x_{0}=y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}=x_{m})
such that
\tilde{\partial}(x_{0},y_{1})=\tilde{\partial}(x_{i},x_{i+1})
.

Proof.

The desired result follows from the commutativity of
\Gamma
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a weakly distance-regular digraph. If
p_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}^{(2,q-2)}>0
with
q\geq 3
, then each arc of type
(1,q-1)
is contained in a circuit of length
q
consisting of arcs of type
(1,q-1)
.

Proof.

Let
(x_{0},x_{1})\in\Gamma_{1,q-1}
. Since
p_{(2,q-2),(q-1,1)}^{(1,q-1)}k_{1,q-1}=p_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}^{(2,q-2)}k_{2,q-2}>0
from Lemma
2.1

(ii)
, there exists a vertex
x_{2}\in P_{(2,q-2),(q-1,1)}(x_{0},x_{1})
. Pick a path
(x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{q-1},x_{0})
. The fact that
(x_{1},x_{3})\in\Gamma_{2,q-2}
implies that there exists a vertex
x_{2}^{\prime}\in P_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}(x_{1},x_{3})
. Then
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3},\ldots,x_{q-1})
is a circuit containing at least three arcs of type
(1,q-1)
. Repeating this process, there exists a circuit
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{q-1}^{\prime})
consisting of arcs of type
(1,q-1)
. Since
(x_{0},x_{1})\in\Gamma_{1,q-1}
was arbitrary, the desired result follows.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\Gamma
be a weakly distance-regular digraph, and
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1})
be a shortest circuit consisting of arcs of type
(1,q-1)
with
q\geq 3
. Assume
k_{1,q-1}\geq k_{a,b}
, where
(a,b)=\tilde{\partial}(x_{0},x_{2})
. Denote
Y_{i}=P_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}(x_{i-1},x_{i+1})
, where the indices are read modulo
m
. If
p_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}^{(2,q-2)}>0
or
|\Gamma_{1,q-1}^{2}|=1
, then
(x_{i-1},x_{i+1})\in\Gamma_{a,b}
, and

P_{(a,b),(q-1,1)}(x_{i-2},x_{i-1})=P_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}(x_{i+1},x_{i+2})=Y_{i}

for all
i
. Moreover, if
q>3
and
\Gamma
is a Cayley digraph of an additive group, then
Y_{i}-x_{i-1}=Y_{i+1}-x_{i}
for all
i
.

Proof.

Note that
m\geq q
. If
m=q
, then
\partial(x_{i},x_{i+j})=j
for
1\leq j<q
, and so
(a,b)=(2,q-2)
. If
m>q
, from Lemma
2.6
, then
p_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}^{(2,q-2)}=0
, and so
\Gamma_{1,q-1}^{2}=\{\Gamma_{a,b}\}
. Thus,
(x_{i-1},x_{i+1})\in\Gamma_{a,b}
for all
i
.

To complete the proof of this lemma, we only need to consider the case
i=1
only. For the first statement, since the proof of
Y_{1}=P_{(a,b),(q-1,1)}(x_{m-1},x_{0})
is similar, It suffices to show
Y_{1}=P_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}(x_{2},x_{3})
only. If
y\in Y_{1}
, then

(x_{0},y,x_{2},\ldots,x_{m-1})
is also a shortest circuit consisting of arcs of type
(1,q-1)
. Similarly,
(y,x_{3})\in\Gamma_{a,b}
, and so
y\in P_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}(x_{2},x_{3})
. Hence,
Y_{1}\subseteq P_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}(x_{2},x_{3})
, and so
p_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}^{(a,b)}\leq p_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}^{(1,q-1)}
. By Lemma
2.1

(ii)
, one gets
p_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}^{(1,q-1)}k_{1,q-1}=p_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}^{(a,b)}k_{a,b}
. Since
k_{1,q-1}\geq k_{a,b}
, we obtain
p_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}^{(1,q-1)}\leq p_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}^{(a,b)}
. It follows that
p_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}^{(a,b)}=p_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}^{(1,q-1)}
, and so
Y_{1}=P_{(q-1,1),(a,b)}(x_{2},x_{3})
. Thus, the first statement is valid.

Now suppose that
q>3
and
\Gamma
is a Cayley digraph of an additive group. From the first statement, we have

(5)

\displaystyle Y_{1}=P_{(a,b),(q-1,1)}(x_{m-1},x_{0}).

Let
y\in Y_{2}
. Since
m\geq q>3
,
(x_{1},y,x_{3},\ldots,x_{m-1},x_{0})
is a shortest circuit consisting of arcs of type
(1,q-1)
which contains two distinct vertices
y
and
x_{m-1}
. From the first statement again, one gets

(6)

\displaystyle P_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}(x_{0},y)=P_{(a,b),(q-1,1)}(x_{m-1},x_{0}).

Since
\Gamma
is a Cayley digraph and
(x_{1},y),(x_{0},x_{1})\in\Gamma_{1,q-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle x_{0}+y-x_{1}

\displaystyle\in\Gamma_{1,q-1}(x_{0})\cap\Gamma_{q-1,1}(y)

\displaystyle=P_{(1,q-1),(1,q-1)}(x_{0},y)

\displaystyle=Y_{1}

by (
5
) and (
6
). This proves
y-x_{1}\in Y_{1}-x_{0}
. Since
y\in Y_{2}
was arbitrary, one has
Y_{2}-x_{1}\subseteq Y_{1}-x_{0}
. The fact that
|Y_{1}|=|Y_{2}|
implies
Y_{1}-x_{0}=Y_{2}-x_{1}
. The second statement is also valid.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We note that the second statement of Theorem
1.1
follows immediately from Lemma
2.4
. To prove the first statement of Theorem
1.1
, we set
I:=\{i\mid R_{i}\in R_{1}^{2}\}
and
J:=\{i\mid R_{i}\in R_{1}R_{1^{*}}\}
. Then
I\subseteq\{1,1^{*},2\}
by (
1
). Since
R_{1}R_{1^{*}}=R_{1^{*}}R_{1}
from the commutativity of
\mathfrak{X}
, we get
J=J^{*}
. Since

4.
Schur rings

Let
G
be a finite, multiplicatively written, group with identity
e
. For a set
X\subseteq G
and an integer
m
, we set
X^{(m)}=\{x^{m}\mid x\in X\}
. The formal sum of the elements of
X
is denoted by
\underline{X}
, and we treat this element as an element of the group ring
\mathbb{Z}[G]
. Given
\mathcal{S}\subseteq 2^{G}
, we set
\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\{\underline{X}\mid X\in\mathcal{S}\}
.

A ring
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[G]
is called a
Schur ring
(briefly an
S-ring
) over the group
G
if there exists a
partition
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A})
of
G
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

the set
\underline{\mathcal{S}}
is a linear basis of
\mathcal{A}
;

(ii)

\{e\}\in\mathcal{S}
;

(iii)

X^{(-1)}\in\mathcal{S}
for all
X\in\mathcal{S}
.

It can be proved that a
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[G]
is an S-ring over
G
if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied:

•

\mathcal{A}
is closed with respect to the operation
\sum_{x}a_{x}x\mapsto\sum_{x}a_{x}x^{-1}
;

•

\mathcal{A}
is a ring with identity
\underline{\{e\}}
(resp.
\underline{G}
) with respect to the ordinary (resp. the componentwise) multiplication.

A partition
\mathcal{S}
of
G
is called a
Schur partition
if it is satisfies conditions
(ii)
,
(iii)
and
\mathcal{A}:=\langle\underline{\mathcal{S}}\rangle
is a
subalgebra of
\mathbb{Z}[G]
. One can see that there is a 1-1 correspondence between S-rings over
G
and Schur partitions of
G
. The elements (classes) of
\mathcal{S}
are called the
basic sets
of
\mathcal{A}
; the number
{\rm rk}(\mathcal{A}):=|\mathcal{S}|
is called the
rank
of
\mathcal{A}
.

Any union of basic sets of the S-ring
\mathcal{A}
is called an
\mathcal{A}
-set
. Thus
X\subseteq G
is an
\mathcal{A}
-set if and only if
\underline{X}\in\mathcal{A}
. A subgroup
H
of
G
is called an
\mathcal{A}

The connection between primitive S-rings and primitive weakly distance-regular Cayley digraphs is established through the following simple observation.

Proposition 4.3

Let

be a weakly distance-regular digraph with

The weakly distance-regular digraph

generates the group

The following statements are two properties of basic sets of

Lemma 4.5

Let

Since

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an orbit S-ring over a cyclic group

. Suppose that there exists a basic set

of

such that

generates

such that

for all

.

Proof.

Since

generates

,

contains a generator

of

. Since
-x\in X^{(-1)}\subseteq X+X

. Write

for some integer

. Since
\mathcal{A}
is an orbit S-ring, there exists a subgroup

such that every

is a

is a

For each

∎

Lemma 4.7

For an odd prime
