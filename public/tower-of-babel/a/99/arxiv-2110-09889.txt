Hydrodynamic limit of a stochastic model of proliferating cells with chemotaxis.
Radosław Wieczorek

Abstract
A hybrid stochastic individual-based model of proliferating cells with chemotaxis is presented. The model is expressed by a branching diffusion process coupled to a partial differential equation describing concentration of chemotactic factor. It is shown that in the hydrodynamic limit when number of cells goes to infinity the model converges to the solution of a nonconservative Patlak-Keller-Segel-type system. A nonlinear mean-field stochastic model is defined and it is proven that the movement of descendants of a single cell in the individual model converges to this mean-field process.

\displaystyle D\Delta\varrho(t,\mathbf{x})-r\varrho(t,\mathbf{x})+\alpha[\kappa\!*p(t,\cdot)](\mathbf{x}).

Movement of cells.

Equation for chemoreactant.

The concentration

\frac{\partial\varrho_{n_{0}}(t,\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}=D\Delta\varrho_{n_{0}}(t,\mathbf{x})-r\varrho_{n_{0}}(t,\mathbf{x})+\alpha\,\kappa\!*\!\xi^{n}_{t}\,(\mathbf{x}),

The measure

is the empirical measure of all cells
and function

is a mollifying kernel that represents the fact that cells are actually not points,
but have spatial size. The spatial convolution

\displaystyle\kappa\!*\xi^{n_{0}}_{t}\,(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\kappa(x-y)\xi^{n_{0}}_{t}(dy)=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\kappa(x-y)\delta_{X_{i,\mathbbm{j}}(t)}(dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\kappa(x-X_{i,\mathbbm{j}}(t))

is a mollified version of the empirical measure describing spatial positions of cells,
responsible for the production of the chemoreactant.
From the mathematical point of view this allows us to consider
classical solutions to equation (

Cell population dynamics.

We assume that cells may die or proliferate with rates depending on the chemoreactant.
Death means that a cell disappears and
proliferation means that a cell dies leaving two new daughter cells at the same place as the mother cell.
The birth rate of a cell placed at

Definitions of the processes

2.1
Definition of the microscopic processes

Note that if we use the empirical measure approach
even for a simple two Brownian particles case, then giving the initial condition
\delta_{X_{1}^{0}}+\delta_{X_{2}^{0}}

and two Brownian motions
W_{1}
and

does not guarantee pathwise uniqueness
— we need to know the order of particles.
The problem gets harder if the number of particles varies in time.
Since our goal is to obtain a pathwise propagation of chaos result,
we need to define a process in a more direct way.

Therefore, we construct the process in the following way.
We mark particles by means of a subtree of Ulam-Harris tree, namely let

\mathbb{J}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}{\{0,1\}}^{n}

(4)

with a convention that
{\{0,1\}}^{0}=\emptyset
means the root.
Elements of
\mathbb{J}
will be written as blackboard bold lowercase letters such as
\mathbbm{i},\mathbbm{j},\mathbbm{k}
.
If we write
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbbm{j}0
, we mean that
\mathbbm{k}
is longer by one then
\mathbbm{j}

and is created from
\mathbbm{j}
by adding
0
at the end.
Moreover, we will denote by

\mathbbm{j}^{\mkern-2.0mu\leftarrow}
element of
\mathbb{J}
obtained from
\mathbbm{j}
by removing last number,
eg. if
\mathbbm{j}=\emptyset 101
, then
\mathbbm{j}^{\mkern-2.0mu\leftarrow}=\emptyset 10
.
Moreover, let us assume that

(A.1)

\left(W_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N},\mathbbm{j}\in\mathbb{J}}
is an infinite array
of independent
d
-dimensional standard Wiener processes,
and
\left(\mathcal{N}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N},\mathbbm{j}\in\mathbb{J}}
is an array
of independent standard (i.e. such that intensity is Lebesgue measure) Poisson point processes on
[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)

on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.

These processes will be used as a source of randomness in all processes,
in particular in Eq. (
7a
) and (
9a
).

We actually define a sequence of processes indexed by the initial number of cell
n_{0}
.
Let us assume that we start with
n_{0}
cells and let each initial
cell be described by its position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the number
i
, and
\mathbbm{j}=\emptyset
denoting that it is the first cell in its own tree of inheritance.
The initial cells are located at
\mathbf{X}_{i,\emptyset}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

.
At each branching event, two new cells appear as daughters of a cell described by
\mathbf{X}^{n_{0}}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}

at the same place as the mother cell.
The daughter cells inherit the position, the cell line number

and obtain new subsequent indices

\mathbbm{k}_{1}=\mathbbm{j}0
and
\mathbbm{k}_{2}=\mathbbm{j}1
.
So, each cell is described by a triple

(\mathbf{x},i,\mathbbm{j})\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times\{1,\dots,n_{0}\}\times\mathbb{J}

and

Let

-th cell appears

-th cell dies

(5)

with a convention that

The times of birth

2.5
Other remarks

Usually, the aim of a rigorous proof of convergence of individual models to macroscopic ones is twofold:
from one hand side, it is convenient to be able to derive the macroscopic model of population
from the primitive rules that govern the individuals
and to know if and why the macroscopic model
properly approximates the collective behaviour.
On the other hand side,
often the individual based models are easier to simulate and the proofs of convergence
play significant role in justifying the use of
individual-based model simulations as Monte Carlo methods for complicated PDEs.
We have also the third motivation that justifies the hybrid mean-field model.
Sometimes (cf.
[
12
,
23
,
33
]
)
simulating the individual-based model is costly, as in our case where it demands simulating
branching diffusion coupled to PDE.
On the other hand side, solving only the macroscopic PDE can be insufficient, because we do not follow the geometry of trajectories.
In such a case we can use the hybrid mean-field model in the following way:
firstly solve numerically the non-stochastic macroscopic model
11

for density of cells and concentration of chemoreactant,
and then simulate the branching diffusion
17

with already given evolution of chemoreactant
\varrho
.

3
Assumptions and wellposedness

One of main goals of this paper is to make the definition of the individual model as strict as possible
while keeping it readable. To that aim, besides the description in section
2.1
,
we need to define the state space of the process, which can be done in various ways.
We add to the space of positions
\mathbb{R}^{d}
an additional state
\phi
denoting a nonexisting cell
and we describe the state of all particles as an infinite array of points from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\phi\}

indexed by

such that only finite numbers of elements are different then

,
that is

\mathbb{X}=\left\{\left(\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\right)_{(i,\mathbbm{j})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{J}}:\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\phi\},\text{ such that }\#\{\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}:\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\neq\phi\}\text{ is finite}\right\}

with a natural metrics

with a convention that
|\mathbf{x}-\phi|=1
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Because of the birth and death process the trajectories of the microscopic
process are not continuous. We apply the standard convention to use cádlág paths,
so the space of trajectories will be the Skorokhod space

.

Now, we can formally describe the branching diffusion component of the microscopic model
as the solution to the following system of SDEs

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{n_{0}}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\left(t\right)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{n_{0}}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}\left(0\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{X}^{n_{0}}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}(s),\nabla\varrho(t,\mathbf{X}^{n_{0}}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}(s)))\,ds+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{n_{0}}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}(s)\right)\,dW_{i,\mathbbm{j}}(s),

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\chi^{n_{0}}_{\rm b}(\mathbf{X}^{n_{0}}_{i,\mathbbm{j}^{\mkern-2.0mu\leftarrow}}(s^{-}),z)\mathcal{N}_{i,\mathbbm{j}^{\mkern-2.0mu\leftarrow}}(ds,dz)+\int_{0}^{t}\chi^{n_{0}}_{\rm d}(\mathbf{X}^{n_{0}}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}(s^{-}),z)\mathcal{N}_{i,\mathbbm{j}}(ds,dz)

(16)

for

,

with

\displaystyle\chi^{n_{0}}_{\rm b}(\mathbf{x},z)=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\phi+\mathbf{x},\text{ if }\mathbf{x}\neq\phi,\;z\leq\lambda_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{x},\varrho^{n_{0}}(\mathbf{x})),\\
0,\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

\displaystyle\chi^{n_{0}}_{\rm d}(\mathbf{x},z)=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\phi,\text{ if }\mathbf{x}\neq\phi,\;z\leq\lambda_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{x},\varrho^{n_{0}}(\mathbf{x}))+\lambda_{\text{d}}(\mathbf{x},\varrho^{n_{0}}(\mathbf{x})),\\
0,\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

where we use a convention that
\phi-\phi=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and
\mathbf{x}+\phi=\phi

for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and

In a similar way, to fully describe the branching diffusion of the hybrid model
