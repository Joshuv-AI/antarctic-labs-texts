Pseudo-Kleene algebras determined by rough sets Journal: International Journal of Approximate Reasoning
Jouni Järvinen, Sándor Radeleczki

Abstract
We study the pseudo-Kleene algebras of the Dedekind–MacNeille completion of the ordered set of rough set determined by a reflexive relation. We characterize the cases when PBZ and PBZ*-lattices can be defined on these pseudo-Kleene algebras.

Introduction

In rough set theory, introduced by Z. Pawlak
[
1

Each set

The pair

is called a rough set. We denote by
\mathrm{RS}

the set of all rough sets. It is proved in
[
2
]
that
\mathrm{RS}
is a Stone algebra.
This result was improved in
[
3
]
by showing that
\mathrm{RS}
forms a regular double
Stone algebra. The three-valued Lukasiewicz algebras defined by RS were considered for the first time
in
[
4

. P. Pagliani showed in
[
5
]
how a
semisimple Nelson algebra can be defined on
\mathrm{RS}
.

In the literature can be found studies in which the information about
the objects is given in terms of other types of relations than equivalences.
For instance, already in
[
6

rough approximations defined by an arbitrary binary relation

on

It is known that for an arbitrary tolerance (reflexive and symmetric binary relation),
the ordered set
\mathrm{RS}
is not necessarily a lattice; see
[
7

, for instance.
In
[
8

The work of Umadevi provides a starting point of for this study. Our aim is to find what
kind of logico-algebraic structures can be defined on
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
. We have
restricted ourselves to reflexive relations. Note that reflexivity is
equivalent to the fact that
X^{\blacktriangledown}\subseteq X\subseteq X^{\blacktriangle}
holds for any subset

which is a natural requirement for rough approximations.

Pseudo-Kleene algebras play an essential role in this study. They are bounded lattices equipped with a Kleene complement
\sim

Note that Kleene algebras are distributive pseudo-Kleene algebras. We show how to define a paraorthomodular pseudo-Kleene
algebra on
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
. Interestingly, in the literature
[
9

10

can be found studies in which approximation

[
11

, the authors

paraorthomodular pseudo-Kleene algebra. Therefore, our work has a connection also to the study of multigranular approximation spaces.
This study also continues our lattice-theoretical research of
\mathrm{RS}
. In
[
12
]
we proved that rough sets defined
by quasiorders (reflexive and transitive binary relations) are exactly the Nelson algebras defined on algebraic lattices.
In
[
13

we characterized the rough sets defined by a tolerance relation induced by an irredundant covering of
U
.

In this paper, we show that the pseudo-Kleene algebras defined on
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
have some distinctive properties.
We prove that the sharp and the complemented elements coincide.
We also note that if a complement of an element exists in
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
, it is unique.
The pseudo-Kleene algebra

is always paraorthomodular.
Central elements have a key role in direct decompositions of bounded lattices, and
we establish a connection between central elements and exact rough sets.

Brouwer–Zadeh posets and lattices were introduced by G. Cattaneo and G. Marino in

They were further investigated in

Together with D. Ciucci he studied BZ-lattices related to rough sets structures determined by tolerances in

Paraorthomodular BZ-lattices are called PBZ-lattices. In this work, we study PBZ-lattices defined on

If
R
a quasiorder or a tolerance induced by an irredundant covering, we

PBZ*-lattices were introduced in

2
Smallest completion of rough sets

The rough sets lattices defined by equivalences, tolerances induced by irredundant coverings, and quasiorders
are distributive, and they define a Kleene algebra. Pseudo-Kleene algebras
are non-distributive generalizations of Kleene algebras.
We show that for a reflexive relation, a paraorthomodular pseudo-Kleene algebra can be defined on
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
. In this algebra,
the sharp and the complemented elements coincide. Because this does not generally hold,
we have that there are pseudo-Kleene algebras that are not isomorphic to some
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.
We also show that if complements exist in
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
,
they are unique. The section ends by presenting some lattice-theoretical
properties of the ordered set of the complemented elements.

3
Central and exact elements

In this section, we consider the central elements of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}

and their relationship with the exact rough sets. The central elements of a bounded lattice
L

have an important role, because they correspond exactly to the
direct decompositions of
L

[
23
, Theorem III.4.1]
.

In the well-studied case of rough sets induced by equivalences, the central elements are the exact rough sets.
This observation can be found in

[
24
]
, where a precise structure theorem was
given by showing that
\mathrm{RS}
is always isomorphic to a product
of chains of two and three elements.

Here, we prove that the set of exact rough sets of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
equals
the intersection of the central elements of
\mathrm{DM}(\mathrm{RS})(R)
and
\mathrm{DM}(\mathrm{RS})(R^{-1})
;
see below for the description of the notation. This means that if
R
is a tolerance, then central elements
coincide with exact rough sets. We end this section by noting that
if
R
is a tolerance induced by an irredundant covering or a quasiorder,
then central elements are the same as sharp, complemented, or
exact rough sets.

We begin this section by recalling some definitions and facts from
[
23
]
.
Let
L
be a lattice. An element
a\in L
is called
neutral
if

(a\wedge x)\vee(x\wedge y)\vee(y\wedge a)=(a\vee x)\wedge(x\vee y)\wedge(y\vee a)

for all
x,y\in L
. It is known that
a\in L
is neutral if
and only if the sublattice generated by
a
,
x
, and
y
is distributive for any
x,y\in L
.

Let
L
be a bounded lattice. An element is said to be
central
if
it is complemented and neutral.
The set of central elements of
L
is denoted by
\mathrm{Cen}(L)
.
As noted in
[
23
]
,
0,1\in\mathrm{Cen}(L)

and
\mathrm{Cen}(L)
forms a Boolean sublattice of
L
.
Thus, the complement of a central element is both unique and also a central element.

In
[
25
, Theorem 4.13]
, the following conditions
are shown to be equivalent:

1.

a\in\mathrm{Cen}(L)
;

2.

there exists an element
a^{\prime}
such that

x=(x\wedge a)\vee(x\wedge a^{\prime})=(x\vee a)\wedge(x\vee a^{\prime})

for every
x\in L
.

We can now write a characterization of central elements in terms of the
operation
{\sim}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(L,\vee,\wedge,\sim,0,1)
be a bounded lattice with an antitone

2.

Let

is complemented and
{\sim}a
is a complement of

.
Suppose that

and

.
Because the sublattice generated by

{\sim}a
, and

is distributive,
we have

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=x\wedge 1=x\wedge(a\vee{\sim}a)=(x\wedge a)\vee(x\wedge{\sim}a)

and

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=x\vee 0=x\vee(a\wedge{\sim}a)=(x\vee a)\wedge(x\vee{\sim}a).

Thus, (ii) and (iii) hold. Because the lattice

Then, for

\displaystyle={\sim}((y\wedge a)\vee(y\wedge{\sim}a))={\sim}(y\wedge a)\wedge{\sim}(y\wedge{\sim}a)

By the equivalence of (M1) and (M2), we obtain that

Basic concepts of Brouwer–Zadeh lattices

In this section, we recall from the literature the essential facts
about Brouwer–Zadeh lattices. They are pseudo-Kleene
algebras provided with an additional negation
\daleth
resembling an intuitionistic
negation. The two negations are connected by the identity

{\sim}\daleth x=\daleth\daleth x
. In terms of the two negations, one can
define a necessity and a possibility operators. There is a close connection between the
set
\mathcal{N}
of clopen elements of these operators and the operation
\daleth
.

A
Brouwer–Zadeh lattice
is an algebra
(L,\vee,\wedge,{\sim},\daleth,0,1)

such that
(L,\vee,\wedge,\sim,0,1)
is a pseudo-Kleene algebra and for all
a,b\in L
the following conditions hold:

1.

a\wedge\daleth a=0
;

2.

a\leq\daleth\daleth a
;

3.

a\leq b
implies
\daleth b\leq\daleth a
;

4.

{\sim}\daleth a=\daleth\daleth a
.

Brouwer–Zadeh lattices are also called simply
BZ-lattices
.
A paraorthomodular BZ-lattice is called a
PBZ
-lattice.

Let
(L,\vee,\wedge,{\sim},\daleth,0,1)
be a BZ-lattice.
In
[
29
]
it is noted that the following facts hold for all
a\in L
:

5.

\daleth a\leq{\sim}a
;

6.

\daleth\daleth\daleth a=\daleth a
;

7.

\daleth a
and
{\sim}\daleth a
are complements of each other.

As in
[
14
]
, we define a pair of mappings on
L
by setting

\Diamond a:=\daleth\daleth a\quad\text{and}\quad\Box a:=\daleth{\sim}a.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(L,\vee,\wedge,{\sim},\daleth,0,1)
be a BZ-lattice and
a,b\in L
.

1.

\Box a\leq a\leq\Diamond a
;

2.

a\leq b
implies
\Box a\leq\Box b
and
\Diamond a\leq\Diamond b
;

3.

\Box\Box a=\Box a
and
\Diamond\Diamond a=\Diamond a
;

4.

\Box\Diamond a=\Diamond a
and
\Diamond\Box a=\Box x
;

5.

{\sim}\Diamond a=\Box{\sim}a
and
{\sim}\Box a=\Diamond{\sim}a
.

Proof.

(i) By (BZ2),
a\leq\daleth\daleth a=\Diamond a
. By (BZ5),

\Box a=\daleth{\sim}a\leq{\sim}{\sim}a=a
.

(ii) Let
a\leq b
. By (BZ3),
\daleth b\leq\daleth a
and
\Diamond a=\daleth\daleth a\leq\daleth\daleth b=\Diamond b
.
We have also
{\sim}b\leq{\sim}a
, which gives
\Box a=\daleth{\sim}a\leq\daleth{\sim}b=\Box b
.

(iii) By (BZ4),
\Box\Box a=\daleth{\sim}\daleth{\sim}a=\daleth\daleth\daleth{\sim}a=\daleth{\sim}a=\Box a
. We get

\Diamond\Diamond a=\daleth\daleth\daleth\daleth a=\daleth\daleth a=\Diamond a
by (BZ6).

(iv) We have by (BZ4) and (BZ6) that
\Box\Diamond a=\daleth{\sim}\daleth\daleth a=\daleth\daleth\daleth\daleth a=\daleth\daleth a=\Diamond a
.
Similarly,
\Diamond\Box a=\daleth\daleth\daleth{\sim}a=\daleth{\sim}a=\Box a
by (BZ6).

(v) Fact (BZ4) gives
{\sim\Diamond}a={\sim}\daleth\daleth a=\daleth\daleth\daleth a=\daleth a=\daleth{\sim}{\sim}a=\Box{\sim a}
.
Finally,
{\sim}\Box a={\sim}\daleth{\sim}a=\daleth\daleth{\sim}a=\Diamond{\sim}a
.
∎

Note that
\Box
and
\Diamond
can be defined in terms of each other, that is, for all
a\in L
,

\Box a={\sim}\Diamond{\sim}a\quad\text{and}\quad\Diamond a={\sim}\Box{\sim}a.

Let us recall from

A

.

.

A Kleene–Stone algebra such that its complemented and sharp elements coincide is a PBZ*-lattice.

Proof.

Trivially, the reduct

We show that

for any
(A,B)\in\mathrm{RS}
.

On the other hand, we know by Corollary
5.6
that
\mathcal{N}=\{\daleth x\mid x\in\mathrm{RS}\}
forms an
atomistic complete Boolean sublattice of
\mathrm{RS}
. Because each element of
\mathcal{N}
is complemented,
\mathcal{N}

consists of exact elements of
\mathrm{RS}
. Let
\mathrm{At}
denote the set of atoms of
\mathcal{N}
. Then

\mathrm{At}=\{(A,A)\mid A\in\mathcal{H}\}
for some
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathrm{Sat}(R^{e})
according to
Proposition
3.5
. As the meet of two different atoms of
\mathcal{N}
is
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
,
we have
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\neq\emptyset
for all
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
. Since the greatest element of
\mathcal{N}
is

(U,U)
, we have
(U,U)=\bigvee\mathrm{At}=(\bigcup\mathcal{H},\bigcup\mathcal{H})
. This means that
\mathcal{H}

forms a partition of
U
.

Let
E
be the equivalence corresponding to
\mathcal{H}
. We have that any
E
-class is a union of
R^{e}
-classes.
This means that
(x,y)\in R^{e}
implies
(x,y)\in E
and
R\subseteq R^{e}\subseteq E
. Thus,
E
extends
R
.

For
(C,D)\in\mathrm{RS}
,

\displaystyle\daleth(C,D)

\displaystyle=\Box^{\mathcal{N}}{\sim}(C,D)=\bigvee\{(X,X)\in\mathcal{N}\mid(X,X)\leq{\sim}(C,D)\}

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{(X,X)\in\mathcal{N}\mid(X,X)\leq(D^{c},C^{c})\}

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{(A,A)\in\mathrm{At}\mid(A,A)\leq(D^{c},C^{c})\}

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{(A,A)\mid A\in U/E\text{ and }(A,A)\leq(D^{c},C^{c})\}.

As
C\subseteq D
gives
D^{c}\subseteq C^{c}
, we have
(A,A)\leq(D^{c},C^{c})
if and only if
A\leq D^{c}
.
We obtain

\displaystyle\daleth(C,D)

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{(A,A)\mid A\in U/E\text{ and }A\subseteq B^{c}\}

\displaystyle=\big(\{\bigcup\{A\in U/E\mid A\subseteq B^{c}\},\{\bigcup\{A\in U/E\mid A\subseteq B^{c}\}\big)=(B^{c\downarrow},B^{c\downarrow}).\qed

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
E
be an equivalence extending a quasiorder
R
on
U
. The operation
\daleth
on
\mathrm{RS}

defined by
\daleth(A,B)=(B^{c\downarrow},B^{c\downarrow})
satisfies (
(BZ8)
) if and only if

(A\cup B^{c})^{\downarrow}\subseteq A^{\downarrow}\cup B^{c\downarrow}
for all
(A,B)\in\mathrm{RS}
.

Proof.

Condition (
(BZ8)
) means that

\daleth((A,B)\wedge(B^{c},A^{c}))\leq\daleth(A,B)\vee\daleth(B^{c},A^{c}).

Because
R
is a quasiorder,
\mathrm{RS}
is a complete sublattice of
\wp(U)\times\wp(U)
. We have

\daleth((A,B)\wedge(B^{c},A^{c}))=\daleth(A\cap B^{c},B\cap A^{c})=((B\cap A^{c})^{c\downarrow},(B\cap A^{c})^{c\downarrow})=((A\cup B^{c})^{\downarrow},(A\cup B^{c})^{\downarrow})

and

\daleth(A,B)\vee\daleth(B^{c},A^{c})=(B^{c\downarrow},B^{c\downarrow})\vee(A^{\downarrow},A^{\downarrow})=(A^{\downarrow}\cup B^{c\downarrow},A^{\downarrow}\cup B^{c\downarrow}).

Condition (
(BZ8)
) is thus equivalent to
(A\cup B^{c})^{\downarrow}\subseteq A^{\downarrow}\cup B^{c\downarrow}
.
∎

For our following theorem, we need to recall a couple of our older results. Let
R
be a quasiorder. It is proved in

[
30
, Proposition 4.2]
that a pair
(A,B)\in\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}\times\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
belongs to
\mathrm{RS}
if and only
if
A\subseteq B
and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq A\cup B^{c}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{S}
denotes the set of all singletons.
In addition, in
[
27
, Theorem 6.4]
it is showed that
\mathrm{R}
S is a Stone lattice if and only if
R^{-1}\circ R=R^{e}
.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
R
be a quasiorder on
U
. Then
(\mathrm{RS},\vee,\wedge,{\sim},\daleth,(\emptyset,\emptyset),(U,U))

is a distributive PBZ*-lattice if and only if the operation
\daleth
is defined
for any
(A,B)\in\mathrm{DM(RS)}
by

\daleth(A,B):=(B^{c\downarrow},B^{c\downarrow})

(14)

in terms of
R^{e}
. If
R\circ R^{-1}=R^{e}
, then
\daleth
is the pseudocomplementation.

Proof.

As we already mentioned, for any quasiorder,
\mathrm{RS}
is always a distributive lattice.
Let
E
be an equivalence extending
R
. In view of Theorem
5.8
, we can define a PBZ-lattice
on
\mathrm{RS}
if and only if
\daleth(A,B)=(B^{c\downarrow},B^{c\downarrow})
.

Let us now consider the equivalence
R^{e}
. For clarity, we denote in this proof
by
X^{\Downarrow}
the lower approximation of
X\subseteq U
determined by
R^{e}
.
We prove that
(A\cup B^{c})^{\Downarrow}\subseteq A^{\Downarrow}\cup B^{c\Downarrow}
.
Let us assume that this does not hold. Thus, there exists an element
x\in(A\cup B^{c})^{\Downarrow}
such that

x\notin(A^{\Downarrow}\cup B^{c\Downarrow})
. Therefore,
x/R^{e}\nsubseteq A
and
x/R^{e}\nsubseteq B^{c}
.
Because
A\subseteq B
, we have
A\cap B^{c}=\emptyset
. Now
x/R^{e}\nsubseteq A
implies
x/R^{e}\cap B^{c}\neq\emptyset
, because

x/R^{e}\subseteq A\cup B^{c}
. Similarly,
x/R^{e}\nsubseteq B^{c}
implies
x/R^{e}\cap A\neq\emptyset
.
Thus, there exist elements
a\in A
and
b\in B^{c}
such that
(a,b)\in R^{e}
.

Between any elements
a\in A
and
b\in B^{c}
there can be several
paths

z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
such that
z_{0}=a
,
z_{n}=b
and

(z_{i-1},z_{i})\in R\cup R^{-1}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, where
n
means the
length
of the path. Because the
length of each path is a nonnegative integer, there exists at least one path with the minimal length
n
connecting

a
and
b
. We say that the
distance
between the sets
A
and
B^{c}
is the minimal length between all possible paths between
some
a\in A
and
b\in B^{c}
.

Let the distance between
A
and
B^{c}
be
n
. In addition, let
z_{0}\in A
,
z_{n}\in B^{c}
and
z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
be the
corresponding connecting path. Because
A\cap B^{c}=\emptyset
,
n=0
is impossible. Similarly,

n=1
would imply that
(z_{0},z_{1})\in R\cup R^{-1}
. Thus,
(u,v)\in R
or
(v,u)\in R
.
As
(A,B)\in\mathrm{RS}
, we have
A=X^{\blacktriangledown}
and
B=X^{\blacktriangle}
for some
X\subseteq U
.
Now,
z_{0}\in A=X^{\blacktriangledown}
and
(z_{0},z_{1})\in R
imply
z_{1}\in R(z_{0})\subseteq X\subseteq X^{\blacktriangle}=B
.
We get
z_{1}\in B^{c}\cap B=\emptyset
, a
contradiction. Similarly,
(z_{1},z_{0})\in R
and
z_{0}\in A
yield
z_{1}\in A^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq X^{\blacktriangle}=B
. Again,

z_{1}\in B^{c}\cap B=\emptyset
, a contradiction.

Thus,
n\geq 2
. As the path
z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
connecting
A
and
B^{c}
is of the minimal length, we must have

z_{1}\notin A
and
z_{1}\notin B^{c}
.
Indeed, having
z_{1}\in A
would imply that
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}
is a path connecting

A
and
B
shorter than the path
z_{0},z_{1}\dots,z_{n}
of the minimum length. Similarly,
z_{1}\in B^{c}
would imply that
z_{0},z_{1}
is a
path of length
1
connecting
A
and
B^{c}
, which case we just managed to exclude.

Because
z_{1}\notin A\cup B^{c}
, we have
x/R^{e}\nsubseteq A\cup B^{c}
, contradicting the assumption
x\in(A\cup B^{c})^{\Downarrow}
.
This proves that for any
x\in(A\cup B^{c})^{\Downarrow}
, we must have
x\in A^{\Downarrow}\cup B^{c\Downarrow}
, that is,

(A\cup B^{c})^{\Downarrow}\subseteq A^{\Downarrow}\cup B^{c\Downarrow}
.

Let
E
be an equivalence extending
R
. Note that
R\subseteq E
is equivalent to

R^{e}\subseteq E
. We assume
R^{e}\subset E
and show that there is
(A,B)\in\mathrm{RS}

such that
(A\cup B^{c})^{\downarrow}\nsubseteq A^{\downarrow}\cup B^{c\downarrow}
, where

X^{\downarrow}
denotes the lower approximation of
X\subseteq U
determined by
E
.

Because
R^{e}\subset E
, there exists an
E
-class
H
that is a union of at least
two
R^{e}
-classes. Let
K
be the union of
R^{e}
-classes of the elements
in
\mathcal{S}\setminus H
, that is,

K=\bigcup\{z/R^{e}\mid z\in\mathcal{S}\setminus H\}
.
Notice that by definition

K\subseteq\bigcup\{z/E\mid z\in\mathcal{S}\setminus H\}\subseteq H^{c}
.

Let
x\in H
. We set

A:=x/R^{e}\cup K\quad\text{and}\quad B:=x/R^{e}\cup H^{c}.

It is clear that
A
and
B
belong to
\mathrm{Sat}(R^{e})
. Thus,
A^{\Downarrow}=A
and

B^{\Downarrow}=B
. By Lemma
5.2
,
A^{\blacktriangledown}=A^{\Downarrow\blacktriangledown}=A^{\Downarrow}=A
.
Thus,
A\in\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}
. Similarly, we can show
B\in\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
.
By definition,
A\subseteq B
.

We prove that
A\cap\mathcal{S}=B\cap\mathcal{S}
. Clearly,
A\cap\mathcal{S}\subseteq B\cap\mathcal{S}
. Suppose that
a\in B\cap\mathcal{S}
. If
a\in x/R^{e}
, then
obviously
a\in A\cap\mathcal{S}
. If
a\in H^{c}
, then
a\in\mathcal{S}\setminus H

and
a\in a/R^{e}
imply
a\in K
and
a\in A
. Hence,
a\in A\cap\mathcal{S}
and

A\cap\mathcal{S}=B\cap\mathcal{S}
holds. Thus,

\mathcal{S}=(B\cap\mathcal{S})\cup(B^{c}\cap\mathcal{S})=(A\cap\mathcal{S})\cup(B^{c}\cap\mathcal{S})\subseteq A\cup B^{c}
and
(A,B)\in\mathrm{RS}
.

Now,

\displaystyle A\cup B^{c}

\displaystyle=(x/R^{e}\cup K)\cup(x/R^{e}\cup H^{c})^{c}=(x/R^{e}\cup K)\cup((x/R^{e})^{c}\cap H)

\displaystyle=(x/R^{e}\cup K\cup(x/R^{e})^{c})\cap(x/R^{e}\cup K\cup H)=U\cap(x/R^{e}\cup K\cup H)

\displaystyle=x/R^{e}\cup K\cup H=K\cup H.

The last equality follows from the fact that
x/R^{e}\subset x/E=H
. We have that
x/E=H\subseteq K\cup H=A\cup B^{c}
and
x\in(A\cup B^{c})^{\downarrow}
. On the other
hand,
K\subseteq H^{c}
means
K\cap H=\emptyset
. Now
x/R^{e}\subseteq x/E
imply

x/E=H\nsubseteq x/R^{e}\cup K=A
and
x/E=H\nsubseteq(x/R^{e})^{c}\cap H=B^{c}
. Thus,
x\notin A^{\downarrow}

and
x\notin B^{c\downarrow}
. This means that

x\notin A^{\downarrow}\cup B^{c\downarrow}
.

As we noted, if
R\circ R^{-1}=R^{e}
, then
\mathrm{RS}
is a Stone algebra. Moreover,
it is a Kleene–Stone algebra in which complemented and sharp elements coincide. By Proposition
4.5
,

\mathrm{RS}
forms a PBZ*-lattice such that
\daleth(A,B)=(A,B)^{*}
.
∎

An
antiortholattice
is a PBZ*-lattice with the property that
0
and
1
are its only sharp elements.

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
R
be a quasiorder or a tolerance induced by an irredundant covering of
U
. We can define an antiortholattice on
\mathrm{RS}

if and only if
R^{e}=U\times U
. In that case, operation
\daleth
is given by

\daleth(A,B)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(U,U)&\text{if $(A,B)=(\emptyset,\emptyset)$,}\\
(\emptyset,\emptyset)&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(15)

Proof.

If
R
be a quasiorder or a tolerance induced by an irredundant covering of
U
,
\mathrm{RS}
is a complete distributive lattice.
By Theorem
5.8
,
(\mathrm{DM(RS)},\vee,\wedge,{\sim},\daleth,(\emptyset,\emptyset),(U,U))
is a
PBZ-lattice if and only if there exists an equivalence
E
extending
R
and
\daleth(A,B)
equals
(B^{c\downarrow},B^{c\downarrow})

for all
(A,B)\in\mathrm{RS}
.

Assume now that
\mathrm{RS}
forms an antiortholattice. Then
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
and
(U,U)

are the only sharp elements. In view of Proposition
3.7
and Corollary
3.11
, they coincide with the exact elements of
\mathrm{RS}
.
According to Proposition
3.5
,
(A,A)
is exact if and only if
A
is a union of
R^{e}
-classes.
This implies that there is just one
R^{e}
-class, which is
U
. Thus,
R^{e}=U\times U
.

Conversely, assume that
R^{e}=U\times U
. Then
R^{e}
is the only equivalence extending
R
.
By Theorem
5.8
, the only way to define an PBZ-lattice is by setting

\daleth(A,B)=(B^{c\downarrow},B^{c\downarrow})
, where
↓
is defined in terms of
R^{e}
.
Now
\daleth(\emptyset,\emptyset)=(\emptyset^{c\downarrow},\emptyset^{c\downarrow})=(U^{\downarrow},U^{\downarrow})=(U,U)
and
if
(A,B)\neq(\emptyset,\emptyset)
, then
B^{c}\subset U
,
B^{c\downarrow}=\emptyset
, and

\daleth(A,B)=(\emptyset,\emptyset)
. Thus,
\daleth
is defined as in (
15
).

Finally, we prove that
\daleth((A,B)\wedge{\sim}(A,B))\leq\daleth(A,B)\vee\daleth{\sim}(A,B)
holds for all
(A,B)\in RS
.
We have two possibilities: (i)
(A,B)\wedge{\sim}(A,B)\neq(\emptyset,\emptyset)
or
(ii)
(A,B)\wedge{\sim}(A,B)=(\emptyset,\emptyset)
.
In case (i),
\daleth((A,B)\wedge{\sim}(A,B))=(\emptyset,\emptyset)
and the condition holds trivially. In case (ii),
we have that
(A,B)
is complemented. By Proposition
3.7
and Corollary
3.11
,

(A,B)
is exact, that is,
(A,B)=(B,B)
. By Proposition
3.5
,
B
is a union of
R^{e}
-classes.
There is only one
R^{e}
-class, which is
U
. Thus, the only exact sets are
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
and
(U,U)
.
If
(A,B)=(\emptyset,\emptyset)
, then
\daleth(A,B)=(U,U)
. If
(A,B)=(U,U)
, then
\daleth{\sim}(A,B)=(U,U)
.
Also in this case, the required condition holds.
∎

Conclusions

In the case of rough sets induced by arbitrary binary relations, we knew quite a
little about their structure. Practically only the results presented in
[
8
]
about the completion
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
were known.
In this work, we have extended this knowledge in the case of a reflexive relation by showing that
\mathrm{DM(RS)}

forms a paraorthomodular lattice. Also, the connections between exact rough sets, central, sharp and complemented elements of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
were studied.

Since we have shown the one-to-one correspondence between atomistic complete Boolean
sublattices of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
and PBZ-lattices on
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
,
it is clear that PBZ-lattices can always be defined on
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
; there
always exists the Boolean sublattice
\{(\emptyset,\emptyset),(U,U)\}
.
This opens the possibility to study the properties of the complete lattice

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
for some particular reflexive relations different from the
known cases.
If
R
is a quasiorder or a tolerance induced by an irredundant covering,
then
\mathrm{RS}
itself is a completely distributive lattice and any PBZ-lattice
definable on it can be induced by an equivalence relation extending
R
. For
a quasiorder
R
, the only PBZ*-lattice which can be built on it is
induced by the least equivalence
R^{e}
containing
R
. We can see that in
these cases, the obtained algebraic structures are not richer than those
described in
[
30
]
and
[
13
]
.

In case of an equivalence relation
E
, the rough sets defined by
E
form a
PBZ*-lattice whose negation
\daleth
coincides to the pseudocomplementation operation
in the lattice
\mathrm{RS}
, and this is the only PBZ*-lattice which can be
defined on it.

As we have pointed out, not all pseudo-Kleene
algebras are isomorphic to
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
for some reflexive relation.
In the further studies, our aim is to find how pseudo-Kleene algebras
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
can be characterized among all the others.
