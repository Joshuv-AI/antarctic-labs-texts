1.
introduction

A knot diagram is a regular projection of a knot that has relative height information at each of the double points. In
[
1
,
2
]
, Adams introduced an
n
-crossing in a knot or link projection (also known as a multi-crossing when
n
is not specified), which is a point such that
n
strands pass straight through it and the relative heights of the strands are specified.
For each
n\geq 2
, every knot and link has a projection such that all crossings are
n
-crossings
[
1
]
. In
[
3
]
, it is proved that every knot has a projection with a single multi-crossing.

Definition 1.1
.

A
petal projection
of a knot
K
is a projection of
K
with a single multi-crossing such that there are no nesting loops. The
petal number
,
p(K)
, is the minimum number of loops among all petal projections of
K
, or equivalently, the minimum number of strands passing through the single multi-crossing.
Suppose we have a petal projection with
n
loops.
We label the strands passing through the
n
-crossing with
1,2,\ldots,n
from top to bottom.
From one end of the top strand we read the labels clockwise half way around the crossing.
This sequence of labels is called the
petal permutation
of the petal projection.

5

shows an example of a
5

The right one shows a petal projection of the left-handed trefoil with the petal permutation,
(1,4,2,5,3)
.

For a pair of relatively prime integers
r,s
with
2\leq r<s
, let
T_{r,s}
denote the torus knot of type
(r,s)
.
In
[
3
]
, Adams et al. showed that
p(T_{r,r+1})=2r+1
.
In this paper, we consider torus knots of type
(r,r+2)
and prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
r
be an odd integer and
r\geq 3
. Then

p\left(T_{r,r+2}\right)=2r+3
.

2.
Petal number and arc index

Given a petal projection of a knot
K
with
n
loops, we can embed the loops on
n
half planes with polar angles
0<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\cdots<\theta_{n}<2\pi
one by one.
Such an embedding of one arc on one half plane is called an arc presentation. The minimal number of half planes needed for an arc presentation is called the arc index, denoted by
\alpha(K)

[
4
]
.
It is obvious that
p(K)\geq\alpha(K)
for any knot
K
.

A grid diagram is a knot diagram which is composed of horizontal sticks and vertical sticks such that at every crossing the vertical stick crosses over the horizontal stick.
The arc index
\alpha(K)
is the same as the minimal number of vertical sticks among all grid diagrams of
K
.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
3
]

Let
K
be a nontrivial knot. Then

p(K)\geq\begin{cases}\alpha(K)\qquad&{\text{if \ }}\alpha(K){\text{ is odd}},\\
\alpha(K)+1\qquad&{\text{if \ }}\alpha(K){\text{ is even}}.\end{cases}

Proposition 2.2
.

[
5
]

Let
r
and
s
be relatively prime integers such that
2\leq r<s
. Then

\alpha\left(T_{r,s}\right)=r+s.

3.
Proof of the main theorem

By Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
, we know that

p(T_{r,r+2})\geq 2r+3
if
r\geq 3
is an odd integer.
Now we show that
p(T_{r,r+2})\leq 2r+3
.

Let
r=2n+1
for a positive integer
n
and let
\sigma_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,2n
, denote the standard generators of the braid group
B_{r}
of
r
strings.

Let
\Delta=\Delta_{r}
denote the positive half-twist of
r
strings and let

\tau=\tau_{r}=\prod_{i=1}^{2n}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{2n}.

We introduce some braid relations needed to prove Lemma
3.1
.

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}^{\varepsilon}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle|i-j|=1,\ \varepsilon=\pm 1,\ 1\leq i,j\leq 2n

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}^{\varepsilon}\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle|i-j|>1,\ \varepsilon=\pm 1,\ 1\leq i,j\leq 2n

The above are extended from the standard defining relations of
B_{r}
.

(2)

\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}\Delta^{2}=\Delta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}\qquad\varepsilon=\pm 1,\ 1\leq i\leq 2n

In fact, the positive full-twist
\Delta^{2}
commutes with any braid.

(3)

\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}\tau=\tau\sigma_{i-1}^{\varepsilon}\qquad\varepsilon=\pm 1,\ 2\leq i\leq 2n

An example of the above is shown below. Notice that we read braid words from the bottom.

Lemma 3.1
.

In
B_{r}
, the braid

\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{n+1}\sigma_{n+2}\cdots\sigma_{2n})(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1}\cdots\sigma_{n+1})

is a conjugate of
\Delta^{2}\tau^{2}
.

Proof.

In the cases
n=1
and
n=2
, the lemma is verified by the following computation.

\displaystyle(\sigma_{2}^{-1})\Delta^{2}\tau^{2}(\sigma_{2})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau\sigma_{1}^{-1}\tau(\sigma_{2})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{2})

\displaystyle(\sigma_{3}^{-1}\sigma_{4}^{-1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})\Delta^{2}\tau^{2}(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{3})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{3}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{3})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau\sigma_{2}^{-1}(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{3})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4})(\sigma_{4}\sigma_{3})

Let
n\geq 3
and let

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k+1}\sigma_{k+3}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k+1},\quad k=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau^{2}

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{-1}\beta_{0}c_{1}

\displaystyle\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=c_{k}^{-1}\beta_{k-1}c_{k},\quad k=2,\ldots,n.

We compute
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{n}
inductively.

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{2n}^{-1}\sigma_{2n-2}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1})\Delta^{2}\tau^{2}(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4}\cdots\sigma_{2n})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau\sigma_{1}^{-1}\tau(\sigma_{2n-2}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1})(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4}\cdots\sigma_{2n})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2n})

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{2n-1}^{-1}\sigma_{2n-3}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{3}^{-1})\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2n})(\sigma_{3}\sigma_{5}\cdots\sigma_{2n-1})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{2n-2}^{-1}\sigma_{2n-4}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1})\sigma_{1}^{-1}\tau(\sigma_{2n})(\sigma_{3}\sigma_{5}\cdots\sigma_{2n-1})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2n})(\sigma_{2n-3}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{3}^{-1})(\sigma_{3}\sigma_{5}\cdots\sigma_{2n-1})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1})

Suppose that

\beta_{k}=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{k}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k+1}),

for
k<n
. Then

\displaystyle\beta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{2n-k}^{-1}\sigma_{2n-k-2}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{k+2}^{-1})\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{k}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau

\displaystyle(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k+1})(\sigma_{k+2}\sigma_{k+4}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{2n-k-1}^{-1}\sigma_{2n-k-3}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{k+1}^{-1})(\sigma_{k}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau

\displaystyle(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k+1})(\sigma_{k+2}\sigma_{k+4}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{k+1}^{-1}\sigma_{k}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2n-k-2}^{-1}\sigma_{2n-k-4}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{k+2}^{-1})

\displaystyle(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k+1})(\sigma_{k+2}\sigma_{k+4}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{k+1}^{-1}\sigma_{k}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1}\cdots\sigma_{2n-k+1}\sigma_{2n-k})

Inductively, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{n}^{-1}\sigma_{n-1}^{-1}\cdots\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{-1})\tau(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1}\cdots\sigma_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\tau(\sigma_{n+1}\sigma_{n+2}\cdots\sigma_{2n})(\sigma_{2n}\sigma_{2n-1}\cdots\sigma_{n+1})

Since
\beta_{n}
is a conjugate of
\beta_{0}
, we are done.∎
