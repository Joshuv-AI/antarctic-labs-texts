Abstract
. A recent generalization of the Conley index to discrete multivalued dynamical systems without a continuous selector is motivated by applications to data–driven dynamics. In the present paper we continue the program by studying attractor–repeller pairs and Morse decompositions in this setting. In particular, we prove Morse equation and Morse inequalities.

Introduction

The present data–driven world sets new challenges to contemporary science. There is a growing interest in coarse theories capable to extract robust information hidden in noisy experimental data. A good example is the rapid development in persistent homology (cf.
[
15
]
) invented to investigate homological features of topological spaces known only from a cloud of points or samples. Conley theory (cf.
[
12
]
) provides a robust topological invariant for studying dynamical systems. It has been used to prove the existence of stationary and periodic solutions, heteroclinic connections and chaotic invariant sets. The potential of Conley theory in the context of data became apparent via a generalization to multivalued dynamical systems
[
21
]
proposed as a tool in computer assisted proofs in dynamics. Multivalued dynamics, important on its own right (cf. e.g.
[
1
]
), becomes in such proofs a tool in the study of single valued dynamics (cf. e.g.
[
28
,
30
,
40
,
35
,
22
]
). However, the practical use of the generalization
[
21
]
has been severely restricted by some strong assumptions difficult to be fulfilled in practice. Fortunately, a recent revision of the theory (cf.
[
5
,
3
]
) removes these limitations opening the way to applications in data–driven dynamics.

Assume time series data have been collected as a result of a measurement, an experiment, or an observation of an unknown dynamical system. Using similar techniques as in
[
29
,
39
,
7
,
4
]
one can construct a multivalued map that represents an unknown generator of the underlying system. Both, toy examples (cf.
[
5
,
3
]
) and constructions for real applications (cf.
[
7
,
8
]
) show that usually such a map does not admit a continuous selector. Nevertheless, we can identify isolated invariant sets, and hire the Conley index theory for multivalued maps, because the construction of the index works under minimal assumptions on a multivalued map; in particular, it does not require single valued selectors. Eventually, we extend the obtained results to the underlying unknown single valued dynamical system using continuation properties of the index.

In the present paper we continue the program that we started in
[
5
,
3
]
. Heading towards the comprehensive description of the dynamics we need to gain more insight into the internal structure of isolated invariant sets. One of the relevant descriptors is a Morse decomposition of an isolated invariant set and the associated Morse equation. Leaving mathematical sophistication aside, one can think that the Morse decomposition is a decomposition of a given isolated invariant set into a finite union of pairwise disjoint isolated invariant sets, called Morse sets, and connecting orbits between them, such that outside the Morse sets the dynamics is gradient–like.
There is a rich variety of approaches to global dynamics that involve
Morse decompositions; see for instance
[
17
,
19
,
18
,
2
]
or
[
11
,
27
]
for continuous-time dynamical
systems without uniqueness. For recent results that combine classical and combinatorial
dynamics we refer to
[
34
,
14
,
6
]
. An approach to the poset structure of Morse decomposition
with the emphasis on lattice structures of attractors, for both an underlying dynamical system
and its combinatorialization - a combinatorial multivalued map, is demonstrated in the series
of papers
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. The presented list of papers is not complete and does not pretend to be
complete in any sense.

The Morse equation describes the relationship between the Conley index of an isolated invariant set and the Conley indices of its Morse sets. In particular, information on (co)homologically nontrivial connections between Morse sets can be derived.
The classical Morse inequalities concern nondegenerate critical points of a gradient flow on a compact manifold, and show the correspondence between the
k
-th Betti number of the manifold and the number of critical points with Morse index
k
, that is critical points with the
k
-dimensional unstable invariant manifold (cf. e.g.
[
9
,
10
]
). One of the possible generalizations of the classical inequalities due to Morse and to Smale (cf.
[
37
]
) in the Conley index theory was developed by Conley and Zehnder to flows (cf.
[
13
]
). Afterwords, the Morse equation in the Conley index theory was proved by Rybakowski in
[
36
]
for semiflows, and in the discrete time case by Franks
[
16
]
and Mrozek
[
33
]
.

Assume that we are given a (continuous– or discrete–time) dynamical system on a locally compact metric space. A set
S
is said to be isolated invariant if it is the maximal invariant set contained in some compact neighborhood of itself. In the cohomological Conley index theory with such a set one associates a special pair of sets, called an index pair. Then, the cohomological Conley index of
S
is defined to be the Alexander–Spanier cohomology of the index pair. One proves that, in line with the need, this is an invariant of
S
. Thus, we can associate with
S
the Poincaré series
p(t,S)
, the power series in
t
with the ranks of the cohomology modules as coefficients. Now, assume that
\text{$\mathcal{M}$}:=\{M_{i}\ |\ i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}\}
is a Morse decomposition of
S
. Hence, in particular, the sets
M_{i}
are pairwise disjoint isolated and invariant subsets of
S
. Then, the Morse equation takes the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(t,M_{i})=p(t,S)+(1+t)Q(t),

where
Q
is a formal power series with nonnegative integer coefficients (cf.
[
13
]
). The terms in
Q
provide information about nontrivial connections between pairs of Morse sets. One can observe that the above equation generalizes the classical Morse inequalities
(cf. e.g.
[
36
,
38
]
).

The aim of this paper is to prove the Morse equation in the Conley index theory for discrete multivalued dynamical systems.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we provide basic definitions related to the Conley index theory for multivalued maps. Section
3
is devoted to Morse decompositions. We define repeller–attractor pairs and provide a characterization of Morse sets via the associated sequence of attractors. The key step for the prove of the Morse equation is the construction of the, so called, index triple for a given repeller–attractor pair. This is presented in Section
4
. Finally, we prove the Morse equation in Section
5
.

Preliminaries

Throughout the paper
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{N}

\mathbb{Z}

\mathbb{Z}

Given a topological space

\operatorname{int}_{X}A

we denote the
interior
of

in

closure
of

in

Let

F\colon X\ni x\mapsto F(x)\in\text{$\mathcal{P}$}(Y)

\operatorname{dom}(F):=\{x\in X\ |\ F(x)\neq\emptyset\}.

A multivalued map

if for any closed
B\subset Y
its large counter image under
F
, that is the set
F^{-1}(B):=\{x\in X\ |\ F(x)\cap B\neq\emptyset\}

Assume that a multivalued self-map
F\colon X\multimap X
is given.

Let
I
be an interval in
\mathbb{Z}
with

. We say that a single valued mapping

if
\sigma(n+1)\in F(\sigma(n))
for all

\sigma(0)=x
.
Given a subset

, the set

\operatorname{Inv}(N,F):=\{x\in N\ |\ \exists\sigma\colon\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to N\text{ a solution for }F\text{ through }x\}

is called the
invariant part
of

. A compact subset

is an

for

if
\operatorname{Inv}(N,F)\subset\operatorname{int}N
. A compact set

is said to be

with respect to

if
S=\operatorname{Inv}(S,F)
. It is called an
isolated invariant set
if it admits an isolating neighborhood

for

such that
S=\operatorname{Inv}(N,F)
(cf.
[
5
, Definition 4.1, Definition 4.3]
).

Definition 2.1

(cf.
[
5
, Definition 4.7]
)
.

Let
N\subset X
be an isolating neighborhood for
F
. A pair
P=(P_{1},P_{2})
of compact sets
P_{2}\subset P_{1}\subset N
is called a weak index pair in

if

(a)

F(P_{i})\cap N\subset P_{i}
for

,

(b)

\operatorname{bd}_{F}P_{1}:=\operatorname{cl}P_{1}\cap\operatorname{cl}(F(P_{1})\setminus P_{1})\subset P_{2}
,

(c)

\operatorname{Inv}(N,F)\subset\operatorname{int}(P_{1}\setminus P_{2})
,

(d)

P_{1}\setminus P_{2}\subset\operatorname{int}N
.

Given a weak index pair
P
in an isolating neighborhood
N\subset X
for

we set

T_{N}(P):=(T_{N,1}(P),T_{N,2}(P)):=(P_{1}\cup(X\setminus\operatorname{int}N),P_{2}\cup(X\setminus\operatorname{int}N)).

Assume
F
is determined by a morphism, which in particular holds true if
F
has acyclic values (cf.
[
20
]
). Following
[
5
]
recall that
F_{P}
, the restriction of

to the domain

, is a multivalued map of pairs,
F_{P}\colon P\multimap T_{N}(P)
; the inclusion
i_{P}:P\to T_{N}(P)
induces an isomorphism in the Alexander–Spanier cohomology; and the
index map

I_{F_{P}}
is defined as an endomorphism of
H^{*}(P)
given by

I_{F_{P}}=F_{P}^{*}\circ(i_{P}^{*})^{-1}.

Applying the Leray functor
L
(cf.
[

]
) to the pair
(H^{*}(P),I_{F_{P}})
we obtain a graded module
L(H^{*}(P),I_{F_{P}})
with its endomorphism, called the
Leray reduction of the Alexander–Spanier cohomology of a weak index pair

. By
[
5

, this is the
cohomological Conley index of

\operatorname{Inv}(N,F)
, which we denote by
C(\operatorname{Inv}(N,F),F)
.

We introduce the following notation for future use. Given
N\subset X
,
x\in N
and

we put:

\displaystyle F_{N,n}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{y\in N\mid\exists\,\sigma:[0,n]\to N\mbox{ a solution for }F

\displaystyle\mbox{\hskip 120.92421ptwith }\sigma(0)=x,\,\sigma(n)=y\},

\displaystyle F_{N,-n}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mbox{\hskip 129.46011ptwith }\sigma(-n)=y,\,\sigma(0)=x\},

\displaystyle F_{N}^{+}(x)

\displaystyle:=

3.
Morse decompositions and repeller–attractor pairs

Given a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to X
of a multivalued upper semicontinuous map
F:X\multimap X
,
we define its
\alpha
– and
\omega
–limit sets
respectively by

\alpha(\sigma):=\bigcap_{k\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}}\operatorname{cl}\sigma((-\infty,k]),\quad\quad\omega(\sigma):=\bigcap_{k\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}}\operatorname{cl}\sigma([k,+\infty)).

Let us point out that unlike in the single valued case, we define
\alpha
– and
\omega
–limit sets for a given solution
\sigma
through an
x\in X
, not for an
x
itself, because backward nor forward solutions through
x
need not be unique.

Definition 3.1

(cf.
[
6
, Definition 3.9]
)
.

Let
S
be an isolated invariant set of
F:X\multimap X
.
We say that the family
\text{$\mathcal{M}$}:=\{M_{r}\ |\ r\in\mbox{$\mathbb{P}$}\}
indexed by a poset
(\mbox{$\mathbb{P}$},\leq)

is a
Morse decomposition of
S
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

the elements of
\mathcal{M}
are mutually disjoint isolated invariant subsets of
S
,

(2)

for every full solution
\sigma
in
X
there exist
r,r^{\prime}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{P}$}
,
r\leq r^{\prime}
, such that
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{r^{\prime}}
and
\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{r}
,

(3)

if for a full solution
\sigma
in
X
and
r\in\mbox{$\mathbb{P}$}
we have
\alpha(\sigma)\cup\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{r}
, then
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset M_{r}
.

(a)

Morse decomposition
\text{$\mathcal{M}$}=\{M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}\}
, with
M_{3}=[0,a]
,
M_{2}=[g,h]
, and
M_{1}=[d,e]\cup[j,k]
, of an isolated invariant set
S=[0,k]
of
F
. Morse sets depicted as thick lines in orange, red and green, respectively, and the associated isolating neighborhoods as thin lines in the same colors.

(b)

Morse graph of
F
- a directed graph with the set of vertices
\mathcal{M}
and arrows presenting the existence of connecting orbits.

(c)

Repeller–attractor pair
(A_{1}^{*},A_{1})
in

. Attractor

and its trapping region
[c,f]\cup[i,l]
depicted as green lines. The dual repeller
A_{1}^{*}=[0,m]\cup[n,o]\cup[p,q]\cup[g,h]

(d)

The partial order
\leq
on
\mathbb{P}
will be called an
admissible
ordering of the Morse decomposition
\mathcal{M}
. Note that it is not uniquely determined. Moreover, there is an ”extremal” admissible ordering
\leq_{F}
, given by
p\leq_{F}q
if and only if there exists a sequence of distinct elements
p=r_{0},r_{1},\dots,r_{k}=q
of
\mathbb{P}
such that, for any
j\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
, there exists a solution
\sigma
for
F
with
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{r_{j}}
and
\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{r_{j-1}}
. The ordering
\leq_{F}
is extremal in the sense that any admissible ordering of
\mathcal{M}
is an extension of
\leq_{F}
. One can observe that for any admissible ordering
\leq
there exists its linear extension which is also admissible. In the latter case, that is whenever the admissible ordering is linear, for simplicity we write
\text{$\mathcal{M}$}=\{M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{n}\}
.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that an isolating neighborhood
T
for
F
is a
trapping region
if
F(T)\subset T
. A subset
A
of an isolated invariant set
S
is called an
attractor
(in

) if it admits a trapping region

which isolates

(relative to

). Given an attractor
A
in

, the set
A^{*}:=\{x\in S\ |\mbox{ there exists a solution }\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S\mbox{ through }x\mbox{ with }\omega(\sigma)\cap A=\emptyset\}
will be called the

, and the pair
(A^{*},A)
will be called the

Example 3.4
.

Consider Morse decomposition of an isolated invariant set
S=[0,k]
with respect to a multivalued map presented in Example
3.2
(see Figure
1
). Note that the pairs of sets
(A_{0}^{*},A_{0})=(S,\emptyset)
,
(A_{1}^{*},A_{1})=([0,m]\cup[n,o]\cup[p,q]\cup[g,h],[d,e]\cup[j,k])
(Figure
1
(C)),
(A_{2}^{*},A_{2})=([0,a],[d,k])
(Figure
1

(A_{3}^{*},A_{3})=(\emptyset,S)
are repeller-attractor pairs in

.

Observe that
\mathcal{M}
, the sequence of attractors
\emptyset=A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset A_{3}=S
, and the associated sequence of dual repellers
S=A_{0}^{*}\supset A_{1}^{*}\supset A_{2}^{*}\supset A_{3}^{*}=\emptyset
, satisfy all the assertions of upcoming Theorem

Assume that
A
is an attractor and
T
is a given trapping region of
A
. It is easily seen that if
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
is a solution for
F
through an
x\in T
, say
\sigma(0)=x
, then
\sigma(\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}^{+})\subset T
. Consequently,
\omega(\sigma)\subset T
according to the compactness of
T
. It follows that
\omega(\sigma)\subset A
, because
\omega(\sigma)
is invariant and
A=\operatorname{Inv}(T,F)
. We state this simple observation as a lemma for further references.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
A
be an attractor in
S
with a trapping region
T
. If
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
is a solution for
F
with
\operatorname{im}\sigma\cap T\neq\emptyset
then
\omega(\sigma)\subset A
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(A^{*},A)
be a repeller–attractor pair in
S
. Then
A^{*}
and
A
are disjoint isolated invariant sets. Moreover, if
T
is a trapping region for
A
then
N:=S\setminus\operatorname{int}T
is an isolating neighborhood for
A^{*}
.

Proof.

Directly by the definition,
A
is an isolated invariant set, and
A^{*}\cap A=\emptyset
. It is easy to see that
A^{*}\cap T=\emptyset
. Indeed, otherwise we would have an
x\in A^{*}\cap T
, and a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
with
\sigma(0)=x
with
\omega(\sigma)\cap A=\emptyset
, as
x\in A^{*}
. However,
x\in\operatorname{im}\sigma\cap T
, which according to Lemma
3.5
means that
\omega(\sigma)\subset A
, a contradiction.

We shall prove that
N
is an isolating neighborhood for
A^{*}
. Clearly,
N
is compact. Let
x\in A^{*}
. There exists a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
through
x
with
\omega(\sigma)\cap A=\emptyset
. Note that
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset\operatorname{int}N
, because otherwise we would have
\operatorname{im}\sigma\cap T\neq\emptyset
and in turn, by Lemma
3.5
,
\omega(\sigma)\subset A
, a contradiction. This shows the inclusions
A^{*}\subset\operatorname{Inv}N
and
A^{*}\subset\operatorname{int}N
. Now consider an
x\in\operatorname{Inv}N
and a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to N
through
x
. By compactness of
N
we get
\omega(\sigma)\subset N
, showing that
\omega(\sigma)\cap A=\emptyset
. Thus,
x\in A^{*}
, showing the inclusion
\operatorname{Inv}N\subset A^{*}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(A^{*},A)
be a repeller–attractor pair in
S
, let
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
be a solution for
F
through an
x\in S
, and let
T
be a trapping region for
A
. The following holds true:

(i)

if
x\notin T
then
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}
,

(ii)

if
x\notin A^{*}\cup A
then
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}
and
\omega(\sigma)\subset A
,

(iii)

if
\omega(\sigma)\cap A^{*}\neq\emptyset
then
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A^{*}
,

(iv)

if
\alpha(\sigma)\cap A\neq\emptyset
then
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A
.

Proof.

Set
N:=S\setminus\operatorname{int}T
and, without loss of generality, assume that
\sigma(0)=x
.

For the proof of (i), observe that
\sigma(\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}^{-})\subset N
. By the compactness of
N
we infer that
\alpha(\sigma)\subset N
. Consequently,
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}
, because
\alpha(\sigma)
is invariant and, according to Lemma
3.6
,
\operatorname{Inv}(N,F)=A^{*}
.

We shall verify (ii). Since
x\notin A^{*}
, we get
\omega(\sigma)\cap A\neq\emptyset
. Then
\operatorname{im}\sigma\cap T\neq\emptyset
, and by Lemma
3.5
,
\omega(\sigma)\subset A
. If
\operatorname{im}\sigma\not\subset T
then the inclusion
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}
follows from (i). We have left the case
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset T
. However, in that case we would have
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A
, and in turn
x\in A
, leading to a contradiction.

We verify (iii). For contradiction suppose that
\operatorname{im}\sigma\not\subset A^{*}
. By Lemma
3.6
,
N:=S\setminus\operatorname{int}T
is an isolating neighborhood for
A^{*}
, therefore we have
\operatorname{im}\sigma\not\subset N
. This means that
\operatorname{im}\sigma\cap T\neq\emptyset
. However, by Lemma
3.5
we get
\omega(\sigma)\subset A
, a contradiction.

For the proof of (iv) observe that the assertion holds whenever
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset T
. Thus, assume the contrary. But then, by (i), we get
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
K
be an isolating neighborhood of
S
, let
(A^{*},A)
be a repeller–attractor pair in
S
, and let
N\subset K
be a compact neighborhood of
A^{*}
disjoint from
A
. Then
N
is an isolating neighborhood for
A^{*}
.

Proof.

Since
A^{*}\subset\operatorname{int}N
and
N
is compact, it suffices to verify that
A^{*}=\operatorname{Inv}(N,F)
.

Inclusion
A^{*}\subset\operatorname{Inv}(N,F)
is straightforward, as
A^{*}
is invariant and
A^{*}\subset N
. To see the opposite inclusion consider an
x\in\operatorname{Inv}(N,F)
and a solution
\sigma
through
x
in
N\subset K
. Then, by compactness of
N
we have
\omega(\sigma)\subset N
. In particular,
\omega(\sigma)\cap A=\emptyset
, as
N
and
A
are disjoint. This along with Lemma
3.7
yields
x\in A^{*}
, and completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\text{$\mathcal{M}$}:=\{M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{n}\}

be a Morse decomposition of an isolated invariant set
S
with respect to an upper semicontinuous
F:X\multimap X
. Then there exists a family

\emptyset=A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\dots\subset A_{n}=S
of attractors in
S
such that
M_{j}=A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}
for
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, where
A^{*}_{j}
is a repeller dual to
A_{j}
.

Proof.

We set
A_{0}:=\emptyset
and
A_{k}:=\{x\in S\ |\ \mbox{there exists a solution }\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S\mbox{ through }x\mbox{ with }\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{1}\cup M_{2}\cup\dots\cup M_{k}\}
, for
k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

The inclusions
\emptyset=A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\dots\subset A_{n}=S
are straightforward.

We shall prove that the sets
A_{k}
are attractors. We proceed inductively. First note that directly by the definition we have
A_{n}=S
; hence, our claim holds true for
k=n
. Now suppose that, for given
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, the set
A_{k+1}
is an attractor. We need to verify that
A_{k}
is an attractor.

We begin by showing that
\operatorname{cl}A_{k}
and
M_{k+1}
are disjoint. For contradiction, suppose that there exists
y\in\operatorname{cl}A_{k}\cap M_{k+1}
, and consider a sequence
\{y_{t}\}
in
A_{k}
convergent to
y
. For each
t\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
let
\sigma_{t}:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
be a solution for
F
through
y_{t}
with
\alpha(\sigma_{t})\subset M_{1}\cup\dots\cup M_{k}
. Since
A_{k+1}
is an attractor,
\sigma_{t}
is a solution in
A_{k+1}
. We construct a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to A_{k+1}
through
y
.
Fix
m\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
, choose an increasing sequence
\{t_{p}\}\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
such that
\sigma_{t_{p}}(l)
is convergent in
A_{k+1}
for each
l\in[-m,m]
, and set
\sigma^{m}(l):=\lim_{p\to\infty}\sigma_{t_{p}}(l)
. Clearly,
\sigma^{m}(0)=y
. Moreover,
\sigma_{t_{p}}(l+1)\in F(\sigma_{t_{p}}(l))
for any
t_{p}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
and
l,l+1\in[-m,m]
. Therefore, by the closed graph property of
F
we obtain
\sigma^{m}(l+1)\in F(\sigma(l))
. This shows that
\sigma^{m}:[-m,m]\to A_{k+1}
is a solution for
F
through
y
. Proceeding inductively and using pointwise limits of solutions
\sigma_{t}
, we can extend
\sigma^{m}
to a full solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to A_{k+1}
for
F
through
y
(cf.
[
7
, Lemma 3.4]
).

Now, fix an arbitrary
l\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. Note that
F(M_{l})\cap M_{k+1}=\emptyset
, because otherwise by the invariance of
M_{l}
and
M_{k+1}
we would have a solution
\tau:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
with
\alpha(\tau)\subset M_{l}
and
\omega(\tau)\subset M_{k+1}
, which is in contradiction with the ordering of the Morse sets in
\mathcal{M}
. Clearly,
M_{l}
and
M_{k+1}
are compact. The set
F(M_{l})
is compact as an image of a compact set under an upper semicontinuous map. Therefore, by the upper semicontinuity of
F
we can take compact and disjoint neighborhoods
N_{l}
and
N^{\prime}_{k+1}
of
M_{l}
and
M_{k+1}
, respectively, such that
F(N_{l})\cap N^{\prime}_{k+1}=\emptyset
. It follows that there exists a compact neighborhood
N_{k+1}
of
M_{k+1}
such that

N_{l}\cap N_{k+1}=\emptyset\mbox{ and }F(N_{l})\cap N_{k+1}=\emptyset

for all
l\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. We set

V_{k+1}:=A_{k+1}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k+1}\operatorname{int}N_{i}

and

V_{k}:=A_{k+1}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{int}N_{i}.

Since
\sigma_{n}(0)
converges to
y\in M_{k+1}\subset\operatorname{int}N_{k+1}
, we may assume that
\sigma_{n}(0)\in N_{k+1}
for
n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. Fix an
n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. Observe that there exists a smallest
k_{n}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
such that
\sigma_{n}(-k_{n})\in V_{k+1}
, because
\alpha(\sigma_{n})\subset M_{1}\cup\cdots\cup M_{k}
and
F(N_{1}\cup\cdots\cup N_{k})\cap N_{k+1}=\emptyset
.

Case 1.
Sequence
\{k_{n}\}
is bounded. Then we can find an
l\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
such that
\sigma_{n}(-l)\in V_{k+1}
for all but finitely many
n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. Passing in
\sigma_{n}(-l)
with
n
to infinity we obtain
\sigma(-l)\in V_{k+1}
, as
V_{k+1}
is compact. Since
\sigma(0)\in M_{k+1}
and
M_{k+1}
is invariant, there exists a solution
\tau:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to M_{k+1}
through
\sigma(0)
. Now, the concatenation

\sigma^{\prime}(m):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\tau(m),&m\geq 0\\
\sigma(m),&m\leq 0\end{array}\right.

is a solution for
F
in
A_{k+1}
with
\omega(\sigma^{\prime})\subset M_{k+1}
and
\alpha(\sigma^{\prime})\subset M_{1}\cup\cdots\cup M_{k+1}
. However,
\sigma^{\prime}(-l)=\sigma(-l)\notin M_{k+1}
, thus we cannot have
\alpha(\sigma^{\prime})\subset M_{k+1}
. It follows that
\alpha(\sigma^{\prime})\subset M_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
, a contradiction.

Case 2.
Sequence
\{k_{n}\}
is unbounded. Without loss of generality we may assume that
k_{n}\geq n
. Observe that

\operatorname{dom}(F_{{V_{k}},{k_{n}}})\cap V_{k+1}\cap A_{k+1}\neq\emptyset

for all
n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. Taking into account that
\{\operatorname{dom}(F_{{V_{k}},{n}})\}
is a decreasing sequence of compact sets, and
V_{k+1}
and
A_{k+1}
are compact, we get

\bigcap\left\{\operatorname{dom}(F_{{V_{k}},{k_{n}}})\cap V_{k+1}\cap A_{k+1}\,|\,n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}\right\}\neq\emptyset.

By the identity
\operatorname{Inv}^{+}(V_{k},F)=\bigcap\left\{\operatorname{dom}(F_{{V_{k}},{n}})\,|\,n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}^{+}\right\}
(cf. e.g.
[
5
, Lemma 4.9]
,
[
21
, Lemma 2.8]
) we obtain

\operatorname{Inv}^{+}(V_{k},F)\cap V_{k+1}\cap A_{k+1}\neq\emptyset.

Take an
x\in\operatorname{Inv}^{+}(V_{k},F)\cap V_{k+1}\cap A_{k+1}
and a solution
\tau^{\prime}:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}^{+}\to V_{k}
for
F
through
x
. Then
\omega(\tau^{\prime})\subset V_{k}
, because
V_{k}
is compact. This, in turn, means that
\omega(\tau^{\prime})\subset M_{k+1}
, as
V_{k}\cap(M_{1}\cup M_{2}\cup\cdots\cup M_{k})=\emptyset
and
V_{k}\subset A_{k+1}
. Now, by the inclusion
x\in A_{k+1}
and the invariance of
A_{k+1}
, we can extend
\tau^{\prime}
to the full solution
\tau:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to A_{k+1}
. We have
\omega(\tau)\subset M_{k+1}
and
\alpha(\tau)\subset M_{1}\cup M_{2}\cup\cdots\cup M_{k+1}
. Moreover,
\operatorname{im}\tau\not\subset M_{k+1}
, as
x\in V_{k+1}
. Therefore, we cannot have
\alpha(\tau)\subset M_{k+1}
, showing that
\alpha(\tau)\subset M_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,2\dots,k\}
, a contradiction.

This completes the proof that
\operatorname{cl}A_{k}
and
M_{k+1}
are disjoint.

Take a compact neighborhood
W_{k}
of
A_{k}
such that
W_{k}\cap M_{k+1}=\emptyset
. We claim that
W_{k}
is an isolating neighborhood of
A_{k}
. Indeed, it suffices to verify the identity
A_{k}=\operatorname{Inv}(W_{k},F)
. To this end consider
x\in\operatorname{Inv}(W_{k},F)
and
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to W_{k}
, a solution through
x
. By compactness of
W_{k}
we have
\alpha(\sigma)\subset W_{k}
, and consequently
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{1}\cup\cdots\cup M_{k}
, showing that
x\in A_{k}
. We need to verify the other inclusion. It is straightforward to observe that
A_{k}
is invariant. Thus,
A_{k}=\operatorname{Inv}(A_{k},F)\subset\operatorname{Inv}(W_{k},F)
. This completes the proof that
W_{k}
is an isolating neighborhood for
A_{k}
. In particular, it follows that
A_{k}
is compact. Moreover, using the same reasoning, one can easily see that
\operatorname{Inv}^{-}(W_{k},F)=A_{k}
.

Observe that
F(A_{k})=A_{k}\subset\operatorname{int}W_{k}
and consider an open neighborhood
V
of
A_{k}
such that

(1)

F(V)\subset\operatorname{int}W_{k}.

Using
[
21
, Lemma 2.11]
we can pick a compact neighborhood
A
of
A_{k}
such that
T_{k}:=F^{+}_{W_{k}}(A)\subset V
. We shall show that
T_{k}
is a trapping region for
A_{k}
. First note that the set
T_{k}
is compact, because
\operatorname{Inv}^{-}(W_{k},F)=A_{k}\subset A\subset W_{k}
(cf.
[
21
, Lemma 2.9]
). Moreover,
A_{k}\subset\operatorname{int}A\subset\operatorname{int}T_{k}
. The identity
\operatorname{Inv}(T_{k},F)=A_{k}
is easily seen. Thus,
T_{k}
is an isolating neighborhood for
A_{k}
. There remains to verify that
F(T_{k})\subset T_{k}
. Note that, directly by the definition,
T_{k}
is positively invariant in
W_{k}
. Moreover, by (
1
) we have
F(T_{k})=F(F^{+}_{W_{k}}(A))\subset F(V)\subset W_{k}
. Consequently,
F(T_{k})=F(T_{k})\cap W_{k}\subset T_{k}
.

This completes the proof that
A_{k}
is an attractor.

We will show the identity
M_{j}=A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Fix
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let
x\in M_{j}
and let
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to M_{j}
be a solution through
x
. The set
M_{j}
is compact, therefore
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{j}
. This means that
x\in A_{j}
. We shall verify that
x\in A^{*}_{j-1}
. For contradiction suppose that
x\notin A^{*}_{j-1}
. Then, for any solution
\tau:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
we have
\omega(\tau)\cap A_{j-1}\neq\emptyset
. In particular,
\omega(\sigma)\cap A_{j-1}\neq\emptyset
, which in turn implies
\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{k}
for some
k\in\{1,2,\dots,j-1\}
. However,
\sigma
is a solution in
M_{j}
; hence,
\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{k}\cap M_{j}
, a contradiction. For the proof of the other inclusion take an
x\in A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}
. Then there exists a solution
\sigma^{\prime}:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
through
x
with
\alpha(\sigma^{\prime})\subset M_{1}\cup\cdots\cup M_{j}
. We also have a solution
\sigma^{\prime\prime}:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
such that
\omega(\sigma^{\prime\prime})\cap(M_{1}\cup\cdots\cup M_{j-1})=\emptyset
. Consequently, there is
k\geq j
such that
\omega(\sigma^{\prime\prime})\subset M_{k}
. Since both
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
are solutions through
x
, we can take their concatenation
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
so that
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{1}\cup\cdots\cup M_{j}
and
\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{k}
. Thus, by the ordering of the Morse sets
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{j}
and
\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{j}
. It follows that
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset M_{j}
, in particular
x\in M_{j}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\text{$\mathcal{M}$}:=\{M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{n}\}
be a Morse decomposition of an isolated invariant set
S
with respect to an upper semicontinuous
F:X\multimap X
, and let

\emptyset=A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\dots\subset A_{n}=S
be the sequence of associated attractors. Then
(M_{j},A_{j-1})
is a repeller–attractor pair in
A_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

Let
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and let
T_{j-1}
be a trapping region for
A_{j-1}
in
S
. One can observe that
T_{j-1}\cap A_{j}
is a trapping region for
A_{j-1}
in
A_{j}
, showing that
A_{j-1}
is an attractor in
A_{j}
. Now, the assertion of the proposition is straightforward according to the identity
M_{j}=A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}
.
∎

The following theorem may be viewed as a converse of Theorem
3.9
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
S
be an isolated invariant set with respect to an upper semicontinuous map
F:X\multimap X
, and let
\emptyset=A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\dots\subset A_{n}=S
be a sequence of attractors in
S
. Then

\text{$\mathcal{M}$}:=\{M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{n}\}
, where
M_{j}=A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}
and
A^{*}_{j}
is a repeller dual to
A_{j}
, for
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, is a Morse decomposition of
S
. Moreover,
A_{k}=\{x\in S\ |\ \mbox{there exists a solution }\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S\mbox{ through }x\mbox{ with }\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{1}\cup M_{2}\cup\dots\cup M_{k}\}
,
k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is similar to its single valued counterpart (cf. e.g.
[
36
]
), but the details differ. We present the entire proof for the sake of completeness.

We shall verify all the assertions of Definition
3.1
. Fix
i<j
. We have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}M_{i}\cap M_{j}&=&A_{i}\cap A^{*}_{i-1}\cap A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}\\
&\subset&A_{i}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}\subset A_{j-1}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}=\emptyset.\end{array}

Let
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
be fixed. We shall verify that
M_{j}
is isolated and invariant with respect to
F
. Let
T_{j}
be a trapping region for
A_{j}
. Recall that
N_{A^{*}_{j-1}}:=S\setminus\operatorname{int}T_{j}
is an isolating neighborhood for
A_{j-1}^{*}
(cf. Lemma
3.6
). We claim that
N_{j}:=T_{j}\cap N_{A^{*}_{j-1}}
is an isolating neighborhood for
M_{j}
. We have
M_{j}=A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}\subset A_{j}\subset\operatorname{int}T_{j}
and
M_{j}=A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}\subset A^{*}_{j-1}\subset\operatorname{int}N_{A^{*}_{j-1}}
. Thus,
M_{j}\subset\operatorname{int}T_{j}\cap\operatorname{int}N_{A^{*}_{j-1}}=\operatorname{int}(T_{j}\cap N_{A^{*}_{j-1}})=\operatorname{int}N_{j}
. There remains to show that
M_{j}=\operatorname{Inv}(N_{j},F)
. To this end, consider an
x\in M_{j}
. Since
x\in A_{j}
, we can take a solution
\eta:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to A_{j}
with
\eta(0)=x
. Clearly,
\alpha(\eta)\subset A_{j}
. On the other hand
x\in A^{*}_{j-1}
, therefore there exists a solution
\tau:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
with
\tau(0)=x
such that
\omega(\tau)\cap A_{j-1}=\emptyset
. Define
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
by

\sigma(k):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\eta(k),&k\leq 0,\\
\tau(k),&k\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

One easily sees that
\sigma
is a solution with respect to
F
through
x
. Note that
\omega(\sigma)=\omega(\tau)
; hence,
\omega(\sigma)\cap A_{j-1}=\emptyset
. Then, by Lemma
3.5
,
\operatorname{im}\sigma\cap T_{A_{j-1}}=\emptyset
and by Lemma
3.6
the inclusion
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A^{*}_{j-1}
follows. Moreover
\alpha(\sigma)=\alpha(\eta)\subset A_{j}
, and by Lemma
3.7
(iv),
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{j}
. Consequently,
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}=M_{j}
showing that
M_{j}
is invariant with respect to
F
. Therefore,
M_{j}=\operatorname{Inv}(M_{j},F)\subset\operatorname{Inv}(N_{j},F)
.
For the proof of the other inclusion consider an
x\in\operatorname{Inv}(N_{j},F)
and a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to N_{j}
through
x
. We have
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset N_{j}\subset T_{j}
, and in turn
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{j}
, as
T_{j}
is a trapping region for
A_{j}
. Similarly,
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset N_{j}\subset N_{A^{*}_{j-1}}
and we have the inclusion
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A^{*}_{j-1}
, because
N_{A^{*}_{j-1}}
is an isolating neighborhood for
A^{*}_{j-1}
. As a consequence,
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{j}\cap A^{*}_{j-1}=M_{j}
. In particular
x\in M_{j}
. We have proved that
M_{j}
is an isolated invariant set, which also justifies its compactness. The proof of Definition
3.1
(1) is complete.

In order to prove Definition
3.1
(2) consider a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
. Since the sequence of attractors is increasing and
A_{n}=S
, there exists a smallest positive integer
i
such that
\omega(\sigma)\subset A_{i}
. Similarly, there is a largest
j\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
,
j<n
, with
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}_{j}
, as the sequence of the dual repellers is decreasing and
A^{*}_{0}=S
. Then,
\omega(\sigma)\not\subset A_{i-1}
, which according to Lemma
3.5
implies
\operatorname{im}\sigma\cap T_{i-1}=\emptyset
. Hence, by Lemma
3.6
we get

(2)

\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A^{*}_{i-1}.

In particular,
\omega(\sigma)\subset A^{*}_{i-1}
. This, along with the inclusion
\omega(\sigma)\subset A_{i}
implies

(3)

\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{i}.

By the choice of
j
we have
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}_{j}
and
\alpha(\sigma)\not\subset A^{*}_{j+1}
. We claim that

(4)

\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{j+1}.

Indeed, otherwise we would have
\operatorname{im}\sigma\not\subset T_{A_{j+1}}
and, by Lemma
3.7
(i),
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}_{j+1}
, a contradiction.

Observe that
j+1\geq i
. If not, then by (
4
)
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{j+1}\subset A_{i-1}
, which along with (
2
) yields
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{i-1}\cap A^{*}_{i-1}=\emptyset
, a contradiction.

If
j+1=i
then by (
4
) we have
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{j+1}=A_{i}
. Now, using (
2
) we obtain
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{i}\cap A^{*}_{i-1}=M_{i}
.

If
j+1>i
then we have
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}_{j}
and, by (
4
),
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A_{j+1}
. Thus,
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A_{j+1}\cap A^{*}_{j}=M_{j+1}
. This along with (
3
) completes the proof of Definition
3.1
(2).

For the proof of Definition
3.1
(3) fix a
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and take a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
with
\alpha(\sigma)\cup\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{j}
. Then the inclusion
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{j}
follows from Lemma
3.7
(iv), as
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A_{j}
. Similarly, by Lemma
3.7
(iii) and the inclusion
\omega(\sigma)\subset A^{*}_{j-1}
we get
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A^{*}_{j-1}
. Consequently,
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset M_{j}
.

We have proved that the family
M
is a Morse decomposition of
S
.

There remains to justify the last assertion of the theorem. Fix a
k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Take an
x\in A_{k}
and a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to A_{k}
through
x
. Clearly,
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A_{k}
. Let
i\leq k
be the smallest integer such that
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A_{i}
. Then
\alpha(\sigma)\not\subset A_{i-1}
. This means that
\operatorname{im}\sigma\not\subset T_{i-1}
, which along with Lemma
3.7
(i) yields
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A^{*}_{i-1}
. Thus,
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A_{i}\cap A^{*}_{i-1}=M_{i}
. For the proof of the other inclusion consider an
x\in S
and a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
through
x
with
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{1}\cup M_{2}\cup\dots\cup M_{k}
. There is
i\leq k
such that
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{i}
. Then
\alpha(\sigma)\subset A_{i}
, which along with Lemma
3.7
(iv) shows that
\operatorname{im}\sigma\subset A_{i}
. In particular,
x\in A_{i}\subset A_{k}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Index triples

Throughout this section assume that
X
is a locally compact metric space and
F:X\multimap X
is an upper semicontinuous multivalued map determined by a morphism.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
N
be an isolating neighborhood for
F
and let a pair
P=(P_{1},P_{2})
of compact sets
P_{2}\subset P_{1}\subset N
satisfy conditions (a) and (d) of Definition
2.1
. Then
P
satisfies condition (b).

Proof.

First we show that
\operatorname{bd}_{F}P_{1}\subset\operatorname{bd}N
. For contradiction assume that there exists a
y\in\operatorname{bd}_{F}P_{1}\setminus\operatorname{bd}N
. Then
y\in\operatorname{cl}(F(P_{1})\setminus P_{1})
, and we can consider a sequence
\{y_{n}\}\subset F(P_{1})\setminus P_{1}
convergent to
y
. Observe that
y\in\operatorname{int}N
, because
y\in P_{1}\subset N
and
y\notin\operatorname{bd}N
. Therefore,
y_{n}\in\operatorname{int}N
for large enough
n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. This along with
y_{n}\in F(P_{1})
and (a) implies
y_{n}\in P_{1}
, a contradiction.

To prove inclusion (b) assume the contrary and consider an
x\in\operatorname{bd}_{F}P_{1}\setminus P_{2}
. Then
x\in P_{1}\setminus P_{2}\subset\operatorname{int}N
, by property (d). On the other hand,
x\in\operatorname{bd}_{F}P_{1}\subset\operatorname{bd}N
, a contradiction.
∎

The next lemma is straightforward.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A,B,N,M
be subsets of
X
. The following holds true:

(i)

if
A,B
are positively invariant with respect to
F
in
N
then so are
A\cup B
and
A\cap B
,

(ii)

if
A
and
B
are positively invariant with respect to
F
in
N
and
M
, respectively, then
A\cap B
is positively invariant with respect to
F
in
N\cap M
,

(iii)

The aim of this section is to prove the Morse equation.

Assume

is a repeller–attractor pair in an isolated invariant set

with respect to an upper semicontinuous map

p(t,A^{*})+p(t,A)=p(t,S)+(1+t)Q(t)

Proof.

The proof runs along the lines of the proof of

Theorem 5.2
.

Let

be a Morse decomposition of an isolated invariant set
S
with respect to an upper semicontinuous

(7)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(t,M_{i})=p(t,S)+(1+t)Q(t),

where

is a formal power series with nonnegative integer coefficients. Moreover,

Q(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}(t),

where

(8)

(1+t)Q_{i}(t)=p(t,M_{i})+p(t,A_{i-1})-p(t,A_{i}).

If

then there exists a solution
\sigma:\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\to S
with
\alpha(\sigma)\subset M_{i}
and
\omega(\sigma)\subset M_{j}
for some

.

Proof.

The proof runs similarly as the proof of
[

, Theorem 5.6]
. By Theorem
3.9
and Proposition

we can consider the associated sequence of attractors such that
(M_{i},A_{i-1})
is a repeller–attractor pair in

. Applying Proposition

to this pair we find a formal power series

with nonnegative integer coefficients satisfying

Summing these equations over

and taking into account that

and

In order to prove the second assertion, for contradictions suppose that there is no solution

with

and

, where

and

In particular,

and sum over

which, along with (
