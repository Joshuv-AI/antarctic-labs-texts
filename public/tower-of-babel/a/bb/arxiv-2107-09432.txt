Abstract
. We present a generalization of Descartes’ theorem for the family of polytopal sphere packings arising from uniform polytopes. The corresponding quadratic equation is expressed in terms of geometric invariants of uniform polytopes which are closely connected to canonical realizations of edge-scribable polytopes. We use our generalization to construct integral Apollonian packings based on the Platonic solids. Additionally, we also introduce and discuss a new spectral invariant for edge-scribable polytopes.

1.
Introduction

Apollonian packings and their generalizations have proven to be effective tools for studying various structures in natural science. They also appear in many branches of mathematics, such as geometric group theory
[
Zha23
]
, fractal geometry
[
MEG04
]
, discrete geometry
[
Che16
]
, knot theory
[
RR24
]
, and number theory
[
Gra+03
]
(see
[
She20
]
for an excellent survey). The process to construct them begins with an initial configuration of four pairwise tangent circles on the plane. By adding the inscribed circle to the interstice between each triple of circles and repeating this process ad infinitum, we obtain an
Apollonian packing of circles
, as illustred in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.
An Apollonian packing.

The connection with number theory was noted by Soddy in
[
Sod36
]
and relies on the existence of
integral
Apollonian packings, where the bends
1
1

1

also called curvatures
of the circles (the reciprocals of the signed radii) are all integers (see Figure
2
).

Figure 2
.
Two integral Apollonian packings. The labels are the bends, and the bends of the outer circles are
-3
(left) and
-6
(right).

Soddy’s observation arises from an old algebraic relation involving the bends of four pairwise tangent circles on the plane. This relation, known as
Descartes’ theorem
, was documented in the correspondence between Descartes and the Princess Elizabeth of Bohemia around 1643
[
Bos10
]
.

Theorem 1

(Descartes)
.

The bends of four pairwise tangent circles on the plane satisfy

(1)

\displaystyle(b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}+b_{4})^{2}=2(b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}+b_{3}^{2}+b_{4}^{2}).

Both Apollonian packings and Descartes’ theorem have been widely generalized. For example, the higher-dimensional analogues of Descartes’ theorem for configurations of
d+2
pairwise tangent spheres in
d
-dimensional Euclidean space were provided by Soddy for
d=3

[
Sod36
]
and by Gosset for every
d\geq 2

[
Gos37
]
. Another type of generalization in the plane involves modifying the initial configuration of circles according to the vertices of a polyhedron whose edges are tangent to the unit sphere. This approach leads to Apollonian packings derived from the tetrahedron, but it can also be used to obtain the
octahedral

[
Boy74
,
GM08
]
,
cubic

[
Sta15
]
, or
icosahedral

[
BBH18
]
analogues of Apollonian packings. Each of these works has its own generalization of Descartes’ theorem and can traced back to the most general version discussed by Boyd in
[
Boy74
]
. These generalizations fall under the family of
polyhedral crystallographic packings
described in
[
KN19
]
. In this paper, we explore a related family that we term
polytopal sphere packings
. The connection between polytopes and sphere packings has been previously investigated in the works of Boyd
[
Boy74
]
, Maxwell
[
Max82
]
, Eppstein, Kuperberg and Ziegler
[
EKZ03
]
, Chen
[
Che16
,
Che16a
]
and Chen and Labbé
[
CL15
]
. Our main contribution is the following generalization of Descartes’ theorem for the class of polytopal sphere packings arising from uniform polytopes in every dimension.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}
be a polytopal sphere packing where
\mathcal{P}
is a uniform
(d+1)
-polytope with
d\geq 2
. The polytopal curvatures of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}
with respect to the faces in any flag
(f_{0},\ldots,f_{d},f_{d+1}=\mathcal{P})
satisfy

(2)

\displaystyle(\kappa_{f_{0}}-\kappa_{f_{1}})^{2}+\ell_{f_{2}}^{2}(\kappa_{f_{1}}-\kappa_{f_{2}})^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{1}{\ell_{f_{i+1}}^{-2}-\ell_{f_{i}}^{-2}}(\kappa_{f_{i}}-\kappa_{f_{i+1}})^{2}=\ell_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}\kappa_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}

One of the special features of this generalization is that the quadratic equation (
2
), which relates the notions of
canonical lengths

\ell_{f_{i}}
with
polytopal curvatures

\kappa_{f_{i}}
, is expressed in terms of the geometry of the underlying polytope. Furthermore, the equation we derive encompasses several generalizations of Descartes’ theorem and allows us to determine the integrality conditions based solely on the combinatorial information of the polytope, without needing the coordinates of an initial packing. This approach is exemplified by our analysis of the truncated tetrahedron shown in Figure
3
.

Figure 3
.
Two polyhedral crystallographic packings based on the cube (left) and the truncated tetrahedron (right).

We apply our main theorem to study the crystallographic packings induced by the Platonic solids and rediscover their integral structure under a unified Descartes’ theorem expressed in terms of the corresponding Schläfli symbol (Proposition
1
). In higher dimensions, the second author classify all the crystallographic sphere packings induced by regular polytopes in
[
Ras24
]
and used our main theorem to examine their integrality. Beyond regular polytopes, our main theorem also applies to uniform polytopes, and we are confident it could be extended to other families, such as
quasi-uniform polytopes
. In dimension
3
, there is only one quasi-uniform polyhedron that is not uniform: the 37th Johnson solid
[
Grü+03
]
. We have verified that Theorem
2
holds for this polyhedron.

1.1.
Organization of the paper

In Section
2
, we provide the theoretical background on the Lorentzian and affine model of the space of spheres, and polytopes, which are essential for understanding the concepts of polytopal sphere packings. We then explore various aspects and endowed structures of these packings, including their dual arrangement, the full symmetry group, the Apollonian arrangement, and their uniqueness under Möbius transformations. Additionally, we introduce a novel spectral invariant of edge-scribable polytopes, which we term the
Möbius spectrum
.

In Section
3
, we prove the main theorem (Theorem
2
). Section
4
applies this theorem to derive formulas for Descartes’ theorem (Proposition
1
), integral matrix representations of the full symmetry groups (Proposition
2
), integrality conditions (Corollary
4
), and parametrizations of the integral Platonic crystallographic packings (Corollary
6
), in terms of the Schläfli symbol for each Platonic solid.

The Appendix contains a catalogue of the Platonic crystallographic packings, detailing all the properties discussed in this paper.

2.
Polytopal sphere packings: background

For any
d,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
d=m+n
, let
\mathbb{R}^{m,n}
denote the real vector space of dimension
d
, equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
of signature
(m,n)
. The
unit sphere

\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{R}^{m,n})
is the space
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m,n}\,|\,\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle=1\}
.
We will use
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{S}^{d}
to refer to
\mathbb{R}^{d,0}
and
\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
, respectively. An
oriented hypersphere
, or simply a
sphere
, in
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\infty\}
, is the image of a spherical cap on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
under stereographic projection. Depending on the position of the North Pole of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
relative to the spherical cap, there are three types of spheres:
solid sphere
(positive radius),
hollow sphere
(negative radius), or
half-space
(infinite radius).

Each sphere
S
is uniquely determined by its centre
c\in\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and its bend
b\in\mathbb{R}
(the inverse of the signed radius). If
S
is a half-space, it is determined by its unit normal vector
\widehat{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which points to the interior, and the signed distance
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
between its boundary and the origin.

2.1.
The Lorentzian model of the space of spheres

The space
\mathbb{R}^{d,1}
, along with its corresponding inner product, is referred to as the
Lorentzian space
and the
Lorentzian product
in dimension
d+1
, respectively. A vector
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d,1}
is
future-directed
(resp.
past-directed)
if
x_{d+1}>0
(resp.
x_{d+1}<0
). There is a well-known bijection between the space of spheres of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and the Lorentzian unit sphere
\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1})
(see
[
Wil81
]
as well as
[
RR21
, Section 2]
for further details on this bijection). For any sphere
S
of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, we denote by
X_{S}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1})
the Lorentzian vector corresponding to
S
. The
inversive product
of two spheres is defined as
\langle S,S^{\prime}\rangle:=\langle X_{S},X_{S^{\prime}}\rangle
. If
X_{S},X_{S^{\prime}}
are not both past-directed then

(3)

\langle S,S^{\prime}\rangle\begin{cases}<-1&\text{if }S\cap S^{\prime}=\emptyset,\\
=-1&\text{if $\partial S$ and $\partial S^{\prime}$ are tangent and }\mathrm{int}(S)\cap\mathrm{int}(S^{\prime})=\emptyset,\\
=0\quad&\text{if $\partial S$ and $\partial S^{\prime}$ are orthogonal,}\\
=1&\text{if $\partial S$ and $\partial S^{\prime}$ are tangent and }S\subseteq S^{\prime}\text{ or }S^{\prime}\subseteq S,\\
>1&\text{if }\partial S\cap\partial S^{\prime}=\emptyset\text{ and }S\subset S^{\prime}\text{ or }S^{\prime}\subset S.\\
\end{cases}

The
inversive coordinates
of
S
correspond to the coordinates of
X_{S}
, which are given by the
(d+2)
-dimensional real vector

(4)

\displaystyle X_{S}=\begin{cases}(bc,\dfrac{\overline{b}-b}{2},\dfrac{\overline{b}+b}{2})^{\top}&\text{ if }b\not=0,\\
\quad\\
(\widehat{n},\delta,\delta)^{\top}&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

where
\overline{b}=b\|c\|^{2}-\frac{1}{b}
is the
co-bend
of
S
. The co-bend is the bend of
S
after inversion through the unit sphere.
With this coordinate system, the inversive product is obtained by

(5)

\displaystyle\langle S,S^{\prime}\rangle=X_{S}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{d+2}X_{S^{\prime}}

where
\mathbf{Q}_{d+2}=\mathrm{diag}(1,\ldots,1,-1)
. The group of Möbius transformations of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
preserves the inversive product and acts linearly on the inversive coordinates as an orthogonal subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}_{d+2}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to
\mathbf{Q}_{d+2}
. An
arrangement
of spheres
\mathcal{A}=(S_{1},S_{2},\ldots)
of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, possibly infinite, is a
packing
if their interiors are mutually disjoint. The
Gramian
of a finite arrangement
\mathcal{A}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
is the matrix
\mathrm{Gram}(\mathcal{A})=(\langle S_{i},S_{j}\rangle)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
.

2.2.
The affine model of the space of spheres.

The Lorentzian unit sphere
\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1})
can be regarded within the
oriented projective space

\mathbb{P}_{+}\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1}\setminus 0\}/_{\sim}
,
where
X\sim Y
if there is a real number
\lambda>0
such that
X=\lambda Y
. This space
\mathbb{P}_{+}\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1}
is in bijection with the Euclidean unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d+1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1}
which, under the gnomonic projection, becomes the union of two affine hyperplanes
\Pi_{\pm 1}=\{x_{d+2}=\pm 1\}
, both of which can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, along with
\Sigma_{0}=\{(X,0)\mid X\in\mathbb{S}^{d+1}\}
. The composition of the isomorphism, which maps the space of spheres of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
to
\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1})
, with the projection

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1})&\longrightarrow&\Pi_{1}\cup\Sigma_{0}\cup\Pi_{-1}\\
X&\longmapsto&\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}X&\text{if }x_{d+2}=0\\
\frac{1}{|x_{d+2}|}X&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\end{array}

provides an isomorphism between the space of spheres of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and
\Pi_{1}^{\circ}\cup\Sigma_{0}\cup\Pi_{-1}^{\circ}
, where
\Pi_{\pm 1}^{\circ}
is the set of points in

\Pi_{\pm 1}
whose Euclidean norm is greater than 1. Such a point will be referred to as an
outer point
of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. We call
\Pi_{1}^{\circ}\cup\Sigma_{0}\cup\Pi_{-1}^{\circ}
the
affine model of the space of spheres
. In this manner, we can construct a bijection between the set of spheres whose Lorentzian vector is future-directed and the set of outer points of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. The reciprocal bijection between an outer point
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and a sphere
S_{v}
of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
can be obtained geometrically by positioning a
light source
which illuminates
\mathbb{S}^{d}
from
v
. The illuminated region on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is the spherical cap
\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\mid u\cdot v\geq 1\}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d}
, where
\cdot
denotes the Euclidean inner product of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. We denote by
S_{v}
the sphere obtained by the stereographic projection of the illuminated region of
v
, and call it the
stereographic sphere
of
v
. The
Lorentzian vector
of
v
is defined as
X_{v}:=X_{S_{v}}
(see Figure
4
).

Figure 4
.
(Left) An outer point of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and its stereographic sphere in
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
; (right) same setting in the affine model of the space of spheres, together with the corresponding Lorentzian vector.

The Lorentzian vector of
v
can be derived from
v
using the following equation

(6)

X_{v}=(\|v\|^{2}-1)^{-1/2}(v,1)^{\top}

This equation implies that for any two spheres, whose Lorentzian vectors are future-directed, their inversive product is related to the Euclidean inner product of their corresponding light sources via the following formula

(7)

\langle S_{u},S_{v}\rangle=((\|v\|^{2}-1)(\|v\|^{2}-1))^{-1/2}(u\cdot v-1).

2.3.
Polytopes

We recall some basic notions of polytopes needed for the rest of the paper. We refer the reader to
[
Sch04
]
for further details. A
d
-polytope

\mathcal{P}
is the convex hull of a finite collection of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
2
-polytope and a
3
-polytope are usually called
polygon
and
polyhedron
, respectively. For every
0\leq k\leq d
, we denote by
F_{k}(\mathcal{P})
the set of
k
-faces of
\mathcal{P}
and by
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{P})=\{\emptyset\}\cup\bigcup_{k=0}^{d}F_{k}(\mathcal{P})
where
F_{d}(\mathcal{P})=\{\mathcal{P}\}
. The elements of
V(\mathcal{P}):=F_{0}(\mathcal{P})
,
E(\mathcal{P}):=F_{1}(\mathcal{P})
,
F_{d-2}(\mathcal{P})
and
F_{d-1}(\mathcal{P})
are called
vertices
,
edges
,
ridges
and
facets
of
\mathcal{P}
, respectively. The
graph of

\mathcal{P}
is the graph induced by the vertices and the edges of
\mathcal{P}
. The
face lattice

(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{P}),\subset)
encodes all the
combinatorial information about
\mathcal{P}
. A
flag
of
\mathcal{P}
is a sequence of faces
\Phi=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{d-1},f_{d}=\mathcal{P})
where for each
k=0,\ldots,d-1
,
f_{k}\subset f_{k+1}
. Two polytopes
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
are combinatorially equivalent if there exists an isomorphism between their face lattices, and one is said to be a
realisation
of the other.

The
polar
of a subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as
\Omega^{*}=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid u\cdot v\leq 1\text{ for all }v\in\Omega\}
. If
\mathcal{P}
is a
d
-polytope containing the origin in its interior, then
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is also a
d
-polytope containing the origin in its interior and holds the dual relation
(\mathcal{P}^{*})^{*}=\mathcal{P}
. There is a bijection between
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{P})
and
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{P}^{*})
which reverses incidences and maps every facet
f
of
\mathcal{P}
to a vertex
v_{f}
of
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. For every vertex
u\in f
, one has

(8)

\displaystyle u\cdot v_{f}=1.

For every
0\leq k\leq d-1
, a
d
-polytope
\mathcal{P}
is
k
-
scribed
if all its
k
-faces are tangent to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. A
k
-scribed
d
-polytope is called
inscribed
,
edge-scribed
,
ridge-scribed
and
circumscribed
if
k=0,1,d-2,d-1
respectively. A
d
-polytope is said to be
k
-scribable
if it admits a realisation which is
k
-scribed. If
\mathcal{P}
is a
d
-polytope containing the origin in its interior then
\mathcal{P}
is
k
-scribed if and only if
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is
(d-k-1)
-scribed
[
CP17
]
. Concerning edge-scribability, all
d
-polytopes are edge-scribable for
d=2,3

[
BS93
]
. In dimension
d\geq 4
, there are examples of non-edge-scribable polytopes
[
Sch87
]
.

An edge-scribed
d
-polytope is said to be
canonical

[
Zie94
]
if the barycentre of all its tangency points with
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is the origin. It follows from the work of Springborn in
[
Spr05
]
, that for any edge-scribable polytope
\mathcal{P}
, there is a unique canonical realisation
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, up to Euclidean isometries. For
3
-polytopes, canonical realisations are also called
Springborn realisations

[
BJP22
]
.

Figure 5
.

4
-pyramid. The barycentre of the second one is the origin. In the third realisation, the barycentre of the contact points of the edges with the sphere is the origin, so it is canonical.

The
symmetry group

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{P})
of a
d
-polytope
\mathcal{P}
is defined as the group of Euclidean isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
preserving
\mathcal{P}
. A polytope is
regular
if its symmetry group acts transitively on the set of its flags. A
d
-polytope with
d\geq 2
is called
uniform
if it is regular for
d=2
and for
d>2
, its facets are uniform, and its symmetry group is vertex-transitive. It is well known that the only uniform
3
-polytopes are the 5 Platonic solids, the 13 Archimedean solids, and the infinite families of prisms and antiprisms
[
Grü+03
]
. Clearly, any regular
(d+1)
-polytope is
k
-scribable
for every
0\leq k\leq d
. With a little extra effort, it can be proved that uniform polytopes are inscribable, edge-scribable and, in general, non-circumscribable. Moreover, we have the following.

Lemma 1
.

Every edge-scribed uniform polytope is canonical and its barycentre is the origin.

Proof.

The
2
-dimensional case is trivial. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a uniform
d
-polytope with
d\geq 3
. Let us first clarify that
\mathcal{P}
admits a
midsphere
, i.e. a sphere tangent to every edge. Every symmetry of
\mathcal{P}
fixes its barycentre
\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})
. The vertex-transivity then implies that the distance from each vertex to
\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})
is constant. Since every
2
-face of
\mathcal{P}
is regular, every edge of
\mathcal{P}
has equal length. By combining these two facts, we get that for every
e\in E(\mathcal{P})
,
\|\mathrm{bar}(e)-\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})\|
is constant and
e
is orthogonal to the line passing through
\mathrm{bar}(e)
and
\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})
. Thus, the sphere centred at
\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})
with radius
\|\mathrm{bar}(e)-\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})\|
is tangent to every edge of
\mathcal{P}
at the barycentres of the edges, so it is the midsphere of
\mathcal{P}
.

Let us now suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is also edge-scribed, so its midsphere is the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Therefore,
\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})
is the origin
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. On the other hand, the vertex-transitivity implies that every vertex has same degree
\delta\geq d
, which gives us
2|E(\mathcal{P})|=\delta|V(\mathcal{P})|
. We show that
\mathcal{P}
is canonical by proving that the barycentre of the contact points of the edges of
\mathcal{P}
with
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is also the origin.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|E(\mathcal{P})|}\sum_{x\in E(\mathcal{P})\cap\mathbb{S}^{d}}x=\frac{1}{|E(\mathcal{P})|}\sum_{e\in E(\mathcal{P})}\mathrm{bar}(e)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|E(\mathcal{P})|}\sum_{u,v\in e\in E(\mathcal{P})}\frac{1}{2}(u+v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|E(\mathcal{P})|}\sum_{v\in V(\mathcal{P})}\delta v=\frac{\delta}{\delta|V(\mathcal{P})|}\sum_{v\in V(\mathcal{P})}v=\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})=\mathbf{0}\qed

We note that an edge-scribed polytope satisfying one of the properties of Lemma
1
might not necessarily satisfy the other one (see Figure
5
). As it is described in the proof above, uniform polytopes have equal edge lengths. We define the
canonical length

\ell_{\mathcal{P}}
of a uniform polytope
\mathcal{P}
as the half the edge length of its canonical realisation. The following lemma establishes a relation between the canonical lengths and the Lorentzian vectors of uniform polytopes.

Lemma 2
.

For each
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{P})
of an edge-scribed uniform
d
-polytope
\mathcal{P}
,

\langle X_{f},X_{\mathcal{P}}\rangle=-\ell_{\mathcal{P}}^{-2}

where
X_{f}:=\frac{1}{|V(f)|}\sum_{v\in V(f)}X_{v}.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
,
\mathcal{P}
is canonical and the barycentre of
\mathcal{P}
is the origin
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus, the Euclidean norm of any vertex
v
of
\mathcal{P}
and the canonical length of
\mathcal{P}
are related by
\|v\|^{2}=\ell_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}+1
. Therefore, by Equation (
6
), the Lorentzian vector
X_{v}=\ell_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}(v,1)^{\top}
which belongs to the affine hyperplane
\Pi=\{\langle X,\varepsilon_{d+1}\rangle=-\ell_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d,1}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
canonical of
\mathbb{R}^{d,1}
. Therefore, for each dimensional face
f
of
\mathcal{P}
,
X_{f}\in\Pi
, which implies that
X_{f}-X_{\mathcal{P}}\in\Pi
. On the other hand, since
\mathrm{bar}(\mathcal{P})=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
X_{\mathcal{P}}=(\mathbf{0},\ell_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1})^{\top}
. The line spanned by
X_{\mathcal{P}}
is orthogonal to
\Pi
. Therefore,
\langle X_{f}-X_{\mathcal{P}},X_{\mathcal{P}}\rangle=0
which implies that
\langle X_{f},X_{\mathcal{P}}\rangle=\langle X_{\mathcal{P}},X_{\mathcal{P}}\rangle=-\ell_{\mathcal{P}}^{-2}.

2.4.
Polytopal sphere packings

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
d
-polytope whose vertices are outer points. The
arrangement projection
of
\mathcal{P}
is defined as the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}
formed by the stereographic spheres of the vertices of
\mathcal{P}
(see Figure
6
). For any edge
uv
of
\mathcal{P}
, the spheres
S_{u}
and
S_{v}
are disjoint, tangent or have overlapping interiors, if and only if
uv
cuts transversely, is tangent or avoids
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, respectively. Therefore, if
\mathcal{P}
is edge-scribed, then
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a packing. For every
d\geq 2
, a sphere packing

2.5.
Möbius uniqueness and the Möbius spectrum

Polytopal sphere packings and all their associated structures (such as the dual arrangement, Apollonian arrangement, and full symmetry group) are unique up to Möbius transformations. As noted in
[
KN19
]
, this uniqueness follows from the Mostow Rigidity Theorem. Specifically, consider two polytopal sphere packings
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}_{1}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}_{2}}
in
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
for
d\geq 2
, where
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are edge-scribed realisations of an edge-scribable
(d+1)
-polytope. The polytopes
\mathcal{Q}_{1}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\mathcal{P}_{2}\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}^{*}
are inscribed in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and thus correspond to ideal hyperbolic polytopes in
\mathbb{H}^{d+1}
with finite volume. Moreover,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
are the fundamental domains of the hyperbolic reflection group generated by the reflections on their facets. Given that all dihedral angles of the facets are right angles due to polarity, these reflection groups are isomorphic. Therefore, by Mostow Rigidity Theorem, there exists a hyperbolic isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{d+1}
mapping
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
to
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, which extends to Möbius transformation of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
mapping
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}
to
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}_{2}}
.

For every
d\geq 3
, we define the
Möbius spectrum
of an edge-scribable
d
-polytope
\mathcal{P}
as the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{Gram}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}})
. Due to the Möbius uniqueness of edge-scribable polytopes with the invariance of the inversive product under Möbius transformations, the Möbius spectrum of
\mathcal{P}
is independant of the specific packing
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}
and thus it is a well-defined invariant of edge-scribable polytopes. According to Steinitz’s theorem
[
Ste28
]
, the graph of a
3
-polytope is a 3-connected simple planar graph, also known as a
polyhedral graph
. Given that all
3
-polytopes are edge-scribable, the Möbius spectrum can be defined for any polyhedral graph.

Question 1
.

For any
d\geq 3
, are there two combinatorially different edge-scribable
d
-polytopes with the same Möbius spectrum? In particular, are there two non isomorphic polyhedral graphs with the same Möbius spectrum?

The reader can find in the Appendix
A
the Möbius spectrum of each Platonic solid.

3.
A polytopal Descartes’ theorem

In this section, we shall prove our generalization of Descartes’ theorem for polytopal sphere packings induced by uniform polytopes, in terms of the polytope’s geometry. We present first some notions and a lemma needed for the statement and the proof. Boyd’s generalization of Descartes’ theorem, presented in
[
Boy74
]
, asserts that for any arrangement
\mathcal{A}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{d+2})
of spheres of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
with full-rank Gramian, the bend vector
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d+2})^{\top}
satisfies

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathrm{Gram}(\mathcal{A})^{-1}\mathbf{b}=0.

Let
X_{N}=\varepsilon_{d+1}+\varepsilon_{d+2}
be the Lorentzian vector corresponding to the North Pole of

in the affine model of the space of spheres, where
\varepsilon_{i}
denotes the
i
-th canonical vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1}
(see Figure
4
). Notice that
X_{N}
lies on the
light-cone
of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1}
, i.e.

(10)

\displaystyle\langle X_{N},X_{N}\rangle=0.

We define the
curvature
of any Lorentzian vector

as

(11)

\displaystyle\kappa(X):=-\langle X_{N},X\rangle

Notice that, by Equation

,
\kappa(X_{S})=b(S)
where

denotes the bend of
S
.
This notion of curvature, satisfies Boyd’s equation for any basis of Lorentzian vectors.

Lemma 3
.

For any basis of Lorentzian vectors
\mathcal{B}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d+2})
of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1}
the curvature vector
\mathbf{k}=(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{d+2})^{\top}

satisfies

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathrm{Gram}(\mathcal{B})^{-1}\mathbf{k}=0

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be the matrix of the Cartesian coordinates of
\mathcal{B}
. By combining Equations (
5
) (
11
) and the definition of the Gramian, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathrm{Gram}(\mathcal{B})^{-1}\mathbf{k}=(X_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{d+2}\mathbf{B}^{\top})((\mathbf{B}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{d+2}\mathbf{B}^{-1})(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}_{d+2}X_{N})=X_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{d+2}X_{N}=\langle X_{N},X_{N}\rangle=0

∎

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}
be a polytopal sphere packing. For every face

of

of

By linearity,

\displaystyle\kappa_{f}=\frac{1}{|V(f)|}\sum_{v\in V(f)}b(S_{v})

We now have all the ingredients to prove the main theorem, which we restate below.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}

(d+1)

\displaystyle(\kappa_{f_{0}}-\kappa_{f_{1}})^{2}+\ell_{f_{2}}^{2}(\kappa_{f_{1}}-\kappa_{f_{2}})^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{1}{\ell_{f_{i+1}}^{-2}-\ell_{f_{i}}^{-2}}(\kappa_{f_{i}}-\kappa_{f_{i+1}})^{2}=\ell_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}\kappa_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an edge-scribed uniform
(d+1)
-polytope with
d\geq 1
and let
\Phi=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{d},f_{d+1}=\mathcal{P})
be a flag of
\mathcal{P}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d+2})

, and

. Let us compute the Gramian of
\mathcal{B}
. Let

,

and let

be the other vertex of

By combining Equations (

\displaystyle\langle X_{v},X_{v}\rangle=1

\displaystyle\langle X_{v},X_{e}\rangle=\langle X_{v},\frac{1}{2}(X_{v}+X_{v^{\prime}})\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(1-1)=0

\displaystyle\langle X_{e},X_{e}\rangle=\langle\frac{1}{2}(X_{v}+X_{v^{\prime}}),\frac{1}{2}(X_{v}+X_{v^{\prime}})\rangle=\frac{1}{4}(\langle X_{v},X_{v}\rangle+2\langle X_{v},X_{v^{\prime}}\rangle+\langle X_{v^{\prime}},X_{v^{\prime}}\rangle)=\frac{1}{4}(1-2+1)=0

The classic Apollonian group introduced by Hirst in

Corollary 6
.

For every Platonic solid
\{p,q\}
, the triples in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega_{p},\omega_{q}]^{3}
satisfying (
64
) are given by
(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})=\frac{k}{g}(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})
where

(66)

\displaystyle h_{1}=(1+\omega_{q})(t_{2}+t_{3})t_{2},

\displaystyle h_{2}=(1+\omega_{q})(t_{2}+t_{3})t_{3},

\displaystyle h_{3}=t_{1}^{2}-(1+\omega_{q})(t_{2}-\omega_{p}t_{3})t_{3},

t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega_{p},\omega_{q}]
are coprimes and
k\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega_{p},\omega_{q}]
coprime with
g=\mathrm{gcd}(h_{0},h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})
with
h_{0}=(1+\omega_{q})(t_{2}+t_{3})t_{1}
.

Proof.

Solving (
65
) in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega_{p},\omega_{q}]^{4}
is equivalent to find all points in
\mathbb{Q}[\omega_{p},\omega_{q}]^{4}
contained in the projective hypersurface defined by

\mathcal{H}=\{Q(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=-x_{0}^{2}+(1+\omega_{q})(x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}x_{3}+x_{3}x_{1}-\omega_{p}x^{2}_{2})=0\}

The point

and we can find the other rational points by intersecting

with lines passing through

with rational parameters. We consider the affine quadric surface

. The parametric equation of a line passing through

is given by taking

and
x_{2}=t_{3}x_{0}
. Then we have that

and

\displaystyle f_{1}(t_{2},t_{3})=(1+\omega_{q})(t_{2}+t_{3})t_{2}

\displaystyle f_{2}(t_{2},t_{3})=(1+\omega_{q})(t_{2}+t_{3})t_{3}
