Wide-angle effects in multi-tracer power spectra with Doppler corrections
Pritha Paul, Chris Clarkson, Roy Maartens

Abstract
We examine the computation of wide-angle corrections to the galaxy power spectrum including redshift-space distortions and relativistic Doppler corrections, and also including multiple tracers with differing clustering, magnification and evolution biases. We show that the inclusion of the relativistic Doppler contribution , as well as radial derivative terms, are crucial for a consistent wide-angle expansion for large-scale surveys, both in the single and multi-tracer cases. We also give for the first time the wide-angle cross-power spectrum associated with the Doppler magnification-galaxy cross correlation, which has been shown to be a new way to test general relativity. In the full-sky power spectrum, the wide-angle expansion allows integrals over products of spherical Bessel functions to be computed analytically as distributional functions, which are then relatively simple to integrate over. We give for the first time a complete discussion and new derivation of the finite part of the divergent integrals of the form ∫ 0 ∞ d ​ r ​ r n ​ j ℓ ​ ( k ​ r ) ​ j ℓ ′ ​ ( q ​ r ) \int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}rr^{n}j_{\ell}(kr)j_{\ell^{\prime}}(qr) , which are necessary to compute the wide-angle corrections when a general window function is included. This facilitates a novel method for integrating a general analytic function against a pair of spherical Bessel functions.

The two-point correlation function (2PCF) in observed (redshift) space is often expressed in the plane-parallel or flat-sky approximation, in which the directions from the observer to galaxy pairs are assumed to be nearly equal,
\hat{\bm{r}}_{1}\approx\hat{\bm{r}}_{2}

1.1
Doppler contribution to the number counts

The galaxy number density contrast at the source is
\delta_{g}=(n_{g}-\bar{n}_{g})/\bar{n}_{g}=b\,\delta_{\rm m}
, where
b
is the clustering bias and
\delta_{\rm m}
is the matter density contrast. For brevity, we write
\delta_{g}\equiv\delta
and omit the
z
-dependence in our expressions. At first (linear) order in perturbations, the number density contrast at the source is related to the contrast
\delta^{s}
that is observed in redshift space by

{\delta^{s}(\bm{r}_{i})=\delta(\bm{r}_{i})-{1\over{\cal H}_{i}}{\partial\over\partial r_{i}}\big(\bm{v}_{i}\cdot\hat{\bm{r}}_{i}\big)-{\alpha_{i}\over{\cal H}_{i}}\,\big(\bm{v}_{i}\cdot\hat{\bm{r}}_{i}\big)\,,}

(1.1)

where we use Newtonian gauge.
Here
\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{v}(\bm{r}_{i})
is the peculiar velocity,
\bm{r}_{i}=r(z_{i})\hat{\bm{r}}_{i}
, where
r
is the comoving line-of-sight distance, and
{\cal H}_{i}={\cal H}(z_{i})
is the conformal Hubble rate. The second term on the right of (
1.1
) is the standard Kaiser redshift-space distortion (RSD), while the third term is a Doppler redshift effect. This Doppler term is suppressed relative to the Kaiser RSD term by a factor
\mathcal{H}/k
in Fourier space. It is therefore typically neglected in most work on galaxy clustering in redshift space.

The Doppler coefficient in (
1.1
) is given on a spatially flat background by
(e.g.
[
16
,
6
]

[
17

\alpha_{i}={2\over r_{i}}-{\cal H}_{i}\,b_{{\rm e}\,i}-{\mathrm{d}{{\cal H}_{i}}\over\mathrm{d}\ln(1+z_{i})}+2{\cal H}_{i}\,\mathcal{Q}_{i}\left(1-{1\over r_{i}{\cal H}_{i}}\right).

(1.2)

In the original RSD paper
[
18
]
, the Doppler coefficient (
1.2
) includes only the first 2 terms on the right. This is
followed in the pioneer wide-angle papers
[
1
,
2
]
and many subsequent papers. However, the second 2 terms on the right of (
1.2
) are required for a correct analysis
[
16
,
6
]
.
In (
1.2
), the ‘evolution bias’
b_{\rm e}=-\partial\ln\bar{n}_{g}/\partial\ln(1+z)
measures the deviation of the average comoving number density from constancy (due e.g. to galaxy mergers)
[
19
]
.
The third term on the right takes account of cosmic evolution.
The last term on the right arises from the Doppler correction to lensing convergence
[
20
,
21
]
in a flux-limited survey,
where
\mathcal{Q}=-\partial\ln\bar{n}_{g}/\partial\ln L_{\rm c}
is the magnification bias and
L_{\rm c}
is the luminosity cut
[
19
]
. (In the ideal case of no flux limit,
\mathcal{Q}=0
, and for line intensity mapping,
\mathcal{Q}=1
.)

In (
1.1
) we have omitted two contributions to the number density contrast:

(1)

the contribution from the standard lensing magnification term
2(\mathcal{Q}-1)\kappa
, where
\kappa
is a weighted integral of
\delta_{\rm m}
along the line of sight;

(2)

additional relativistic potential terms, including Sachs-Wolfe, integrated Sachs-Wolfe and time delay effects, which collectively scale as the gravitational potential
\Phi

The standard lensing magnification contribution (1) is typically only important at higher redshift – and it requires significant additional complexity to incorporate it into the Fourier power spectrum.
The additional relativistic terms in (2) are suppressed relative to the Doppler term in (
1.1
), since the Poisson equation shows that
\Phi\sim({\cal H}^{2}/k^{2})\delta_{\rm m}
. (See e.g.
[
16
,
6
,
10
]
for details of all these terms.)

There is a subtle point about the Doppler contribution relative to the potential contribution. In
\delta^{s}
, the Doppler term is clearly less suppressed than the potential contribution. However, this does not translate directly to the 2PCF in the case of a single tracer with correlations at equal redshifts. In this case, i.e. auto-correlations at equal
z
, the Doppler contribution is a square of the Doppler term, i.e., scaling as
(\mathcal{H}^{2}/k^{2})P_{\rm m}
, like the leading potential contribution.
Strictly, this means that it is inconsistent to neglect the potential contributions while including the Doppler term, when considering auto-correlations at equal redshifts. Including the potential terms is simple in principle, but we omit them in order to avoid additional complexity in the equations. Effectively, this means that we adopt a ‘weak field’ approximation
[
22
]
.

When considering correlations of two tracers (see e.g.

Furthermore, we highlight the fact that the leading wide-angle contribution to

1.2
Wide-angle multipole expansion – overview

Here we give a brief summary of the calculation of the wide-angle expansion.
In general the multipole decomposition of
\xi_{{g}}(\bm{d},\bm{r})
and
\xi_{\kappa}(\bm{d},\bm{r})
or their equivalent power spectra
P_{{g}}(\bm{d},\bm{k})
and
P_{\kappa}(\bm{d},\bm{k})
(defined below) are analytically intractable. We aim to produce a series expansion first in the 2PCF about the plane-parallel limit (
r\ll d
), and then for each term in that series expansion, we perform a multipole decomposition with respect to the angle between
\bm{r}
and
\bm{d}
, with cosine
\mu=\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot{\hat{\bm{r}}}
. Once translated to the power spectrum this becomes an expansion about
k^{-1}\ll d
with coefficients expanded in Legendre multipoles in
\mu_{k}=\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot{\hat{\bm{k}}}
. Clearly a sensible expansion variable in the 2PCF is
x=r/d
, but we need to be careful. The plane-parallel limit is not simply the limit as
r\to 0
with
d
fixed: in this limit,
\xi_{\rm pp}(r)\sim\int\mathrm{d}kj(kr)P_{m}(k)
, and therefore
\xi_{\rm pp}
is a function of
r
. Nor is it the limit
d\to\infty
with
r
fixed – since the coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
are functions of the two redshift shells
\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2}
we are looking at [see (
2.39
) below].
The plane-parallel limit is thus a mixture of both
r\to 0
and
d\to\infty
.

On inspection each term in (
1.5
) contains parts which depend on
|\bm{r}_{1}|,|\bm{r}_{2}|
– i.e., the
\alpha
and
\beta
coefficients only depend on the distance to each source, and parts which depend on
\hat{\bm{r}}_{1},\hat{\bm{r}}_{2},\hat{\bm{r}}
and
|\bm{r}|
but not on
|\bm{r}_{1}|,|\bm{r}_{2}|
– i.e., the terms which are integrals over the power spectrum,
S^{n}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}
, depend on the geometry of the triangle and the distance between the sources. These different contributions require slightly different series expansions around the plane-parallel limit:

•

In
S^{n}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}
, we may expand in a series in
x=r/d
around
x=0
with
r
fixed, because the direction vectors
\hat{\bm{r}}_{1},\hat{\bm{r}}_{2},\hat{\bm{r}}
only depend on the ratio
x
, not on
r
and
d
separately. In addition,
|\bm{r}|
in the exponential does not depend on
\mu
: hence it does not affect the multipoles and does not need expanding (the
\mu
dependence factors into Legendre polynomials on using a plane wave expansion below).

•

Functions of
|\bm{r}_{1}|,|\bm{r}_{2}|
which appear can sensibly be expanded in a series in
x
, but now with
d
fixed, so that the series coefficients come out in terms of
\alpha(d)
and
\beta(d)
and their derivatives
\mathrm{d}\alpha(d)/\mathrm{d}\ln d
,
\mathrm{d}\beta(d)/\mathrm{d}\ln d
evaluated at
r=0
. We start with a general
\bm{r}_{1}\neq\bm{r}_{2}
and then expand functions of these quantities around a median distance given by the shell
d
[see, e.g., (
2.45
)].

Putting this all together gives a multipole series of the form,

(1.10)

and the
\ell
-pole is given by

\sum_{p}\,\Xi_{\ell}^{(p)}(r,d)\left(\frac{r}{d}\right)^{p}\,.

(1.11)

We will denote analogous coefficients of the galaxy-magnification 2PCF with a tilde.
The plane-parallel approximation

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{21}b_{1}b_{2}\Big({7\beta_{1}}+{7\beta_{2}}+{6\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}\Big)\xi^{(0)}_{2},

2.1
Window function

A careful inspection of the terms in the functions

\Xi_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{(p,n)}

We can incorporate a window function

\big\langle\hat{P}_{L}^{{s}}\big\rangle=(2L+1)\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega_{{\bm{k}}}}{4\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\bm{r}_{1}\int\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\bm{r}_{2}\,\hat{P}_{{g}}\,\mathcal{L}_{L}(\hat{\bm{k}}\cdot\hat{\bm{d}}).

For simplicity we assume azimuthal symmetry for

Then in (

which leads to

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{{g}}(\bm{d},\bm{k})

Therefore we have for the wide-angle multipoles of the windowed power spectrum:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{(p)}_{\ell}(k,d)

In (

2.2.1
Hierarchy of terms

We briefly give an overview of the relative size of the contributions as we include wide-angle effects.
We are expanding in a series in
x=r/d
, corresponding to
1/kd
in the power spectrum, but once wide-angle effects are included like this there are a number of extra contributions which must be consistently included as we have discussed. Once we approach distances with
z\sim 1
,
d\sim 1/\cal H
, so
1/kd\sim{\cal H}/k
implying the need for the relativistic terms.
Therefore,
for a fully consistent approach, we need an expansion in powers of
p+n
where we consider terms

\left(\frac{r}{d}\right)^{p}\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}}{k}\right)^{n}\sim\left(\frac{1}{kd}\right)^{p}\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}}{k}\right)^{n}\sim\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}}{k}\right)^{p+n}\,,

(2.47)

together.

Furthermore, we include derivatives of the growth rate, biases, and other variables
since these derivatives are typically not negligible.
Consider the
\ln d
derivative of the grwoth rate:

\displaystyle xf^{\prime}(d)=r\frac{{\mathrm{d}}f}{{\mathrm{d}}d}={rH_{0}h(z)}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}f}{{\mathrm{d}}z}\,,

(2.48)

where
h(z)=H(z)/H_{0}
and
d=\int\mathrm{d}z/H
. This can be compared to

\displaystyle xf(d)=\frac{r}{d}f(z)=\frac{rH_{0}}{\int_{0}^{z}{\mathrm{d}}z/h(z)}f(z)\,,

(2.49)

leading to

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime}(d)}{f(d)}=\left[h(z)\int_{0}^{z}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}z}{h(z)}\right]{\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}}\approx z\,{\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}}\,.

(2.50)

The term in square brackets is found numerically to be
\approx z
. Therefore for variables whose derivative with respect to redshift is of the order of the variable itself, the derivatives appearing in the wide-angle expansion can be important.

In the case of the growth rate,

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}f}{{\mathrm{d}}z}=\frac{f}{1+z}\bigg[2+f-\frac{3}{2}\Omega_{m}\Big(1+\frac{1}{f}\Big)\bigg]\,,

(2.51)

where the right-hand side is a similar size to
f
– for a LCDM model we find the contribution from the derivative of the growth peaks at
\sim
25% at
z\sim 0.5
. Similarly, for a simple bias model
b\sim(1+z)^{\sigma}
, we have
b^{\prime}(z)\sim\sigma\,z(1+z)^{\sigma-1}
so
b^{\prime}(z)/b=\sigma\,z/(1+z)
. For
\alpha
it is more complicated, because the relative importance of
\alpha^{\prime}
is strongly dependent on the evolution and magnification biases. As an example, if
b_{e}=1=\mathcal{Q}
then the correction peaks at
\sim 50\%
above
z\sim 1
and can be more significant than this even at low redshift.

We now discuss the contributions to the most important multipoles in the cases of the line of sight being chosen either as the mid-point (

) or the equal angle bisector (
\phi=\theta
).
We give general formulas for any
t
in

3.1

3.2
Integration of the power spectrum

To complete our expansion of the multipoles of the power spectrum, we need to compute

P^{pn}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k)=k^{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}q}\,q^{2-n}\,P_{\rm m}(q)\,\mathcal{I}^{p}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)\,.

(3.34)

Having evaluated
\mathcal{I}^{p}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
in terms of relatively simple distributions means that these are straightforward to compute numerically, rather than having to compute highly oscillatory triple integrals. Given that they are distributions, there are some subtleties:

|\ell-\ell^{\prime}|+p
even:

The integrals consist of delta functions and derivatives thereof, together with step functions. The delta functions evaluate to sample the power spectrum and its derivatives at
k
, and the step functions sample the long wavelength part of the power spectrum for
\ell<\ell^{\prime}
and the short wavelength part for
\ell>\ell^{\prime}
.

|\ell-\ell^{\prime}|+p
odd:

The integrals consist of regular plus singular parts, all of the form

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\,\left\{f_{1}(q,k)\big[\ln|k-q|-\ln(k+q)\big]+\frac{f_{2}(q,k)}{(k-q)^{p+1}}\right\}\,,

(3.35)

which is singular at
k=q
and formally diverges. However what we need is the finite part – i.e. the Cauchy Principal Value in the case of the
1/(k-q)
singularities, and Hadamard regularisation for
p>0
. The stronger singularities for
p>0
can be evaluated integrating by parts, assuming
P_{\rm m}(q)
and its derivatives vanish sufficiently rapidly at
q=0
and
q=\infty
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\,\left\{f_{1}(q,k)\big[\ln|k-q|-\ln(k+q)\big]+\frac{f_{2}(q,k)}{(k-q)^{p+1}}\right\}

(3.36)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\,\bigg\{-f_{1}(q,k)\ln(k+q)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad~+(k-q)(1-\ln|k-q|)\left[-\frac{\partial f_{1}(q,k)}{\partial q}+\frac{(-1)^{p+1}}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p+2}f_{2}(q,k)}{\partial q^{p+2}}\right]\bigg\}.

In general we would use finite limits, which are most easily dealt with in this formula by cutting off
P(q)
with step functions – these then evaluate to delta functions in the integrand (and cannot be ignored). More details are given in
subsection C.2
.

An alternative approach which avoids the singular integrals is to use (
3.20
) and (
3.18
) to reduce the order in (
3.34
) down to
\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
. For example, for
p
even,

\displaystyle P^{pn}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k)=\left[\mathcal{D}^{(\ell)}_{k}\right]^{(p+2)/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}q}\,q^{2-n}\,P_{\rm m}(q)\,\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)\,,

(3.37)

where

\mathcal{D}^{(\ell)}_{k}=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}-\frac{2}{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial k}+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{k^{2}}\,.

(3.38)

For
p
odd,

\displaystyle P^{pn}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k)=\left[\mathcal{D}^{(\ell)}_{k}\right]^{(p+1)/2}\frac{1}{k^{2+\ell}}\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\left[k^{2+\ell}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}q}\,q^{2-n}\,P_{\rm m}(q)\,\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell+1,\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)\right]\,.

(3.39)

Therefore, given
P^{-2,n}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k)
, we can compute the rest simply by taking suitable derivatives.

There is a subtlety involved in swapping the derivative and integral because the integrals for
p\geq 0
are distributions and not convergent – yet the integrals in (
3.37
) and (
3.39
) are convergent and well defined. A full discussion of this is given in
subsection C.2
.

4
Conclusions

We have given for the first time the wide-angle corrections to the multi-tracer 2PCF and associated power spectrum, including the relativistic Doppler corrections which go beyond the normal redshift space distortion effect. We have also presented the wide-angle corrections for the density-magnification cross-power spectrum. The full-sky power spectrum is expanded as a series in powers of
1/kd
, with each term expanded into Legendre multipoles in the angle between the line-of-sight vector
\bm{d}
and the mode vector
\bm{k}
. The coefficients of this expansion are just the coefficients of the equivalent expansion in
r/d
of the 2PCF, weighted by an appropriate integral over the matter power spectrum. We have presented the coefficients for the equal-angle bisector and mid-point cases, as well as for an arbitrary line of sight.

A key result of our analysis has been to show the importance of the relativistic Doppler corrections
as well as the derivative terms in the expansion – both typically neglected in previous analysis. While the relativistic terms
enter the observed galaxy number density contrast at
O(\mathcal{H}/k)
, they only enter the 2PCF at
O[(\mathcal{H}/k)^{2}]
– except in the multi-tracer case, when the corrections appear at
O(\mathcal{H}/k)
, and is therefore a useful method for measuring relativistic effects. Similarly the density-magnification 2PCF has also been shown to be an important probe of relativistic effects. However, once we are interested in effects which appear at
O(\mathcal{H}/k)
, wide-angle effects need to be considered – since in the 2PCF they arise at
O(r/d)
, and in the power spectrum at
O([1/(kd)]
, which for large-scale surveys is potentially a similar size to
O(\mathcal{H}/k)
. Therefore, for a fully consistent approach we need an expansion in powers of
p+n
where we consider terms

\left(\frac{r}{d}\right)^{p}\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}}{k}\right)^{n}\sim\left(\frac{1}{kd}\right)^{p}\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}}{k}\right)^{n}\sim\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}}{k}\right)^{p+n}\,,

(4.1)

as similar size, and we need to keep derivative terms at each order for consistency
. In doing this we find, in the galaxy-galaxy case, that the even multipoles at
O(r/d)
receive relativistic corrections of
O(\mathcal{H}/k)
, which implies Newtonian wide-angle corrections at
O(x^{2})
are required
for consistency
. In addition, we find that for the odd multipoles in the multi-tracer case only, Newtonian wide-angle corrections are required for a consistent treatment of the plane-parallel limit.

This is clearly seen in Fig.
2
where the
O(1/kd)
terms are a significant correction for large
k
. This also shows the importance of the derivative terms in the wide-angle expansion.
This analysis implies that the relativistic calculations also require
n=2
potential terms for a fully consistent wide-angle expansion, which is a straightforward extension that we leave for future work.

In the galaxy-magnification cross-power case, we show that as a potential observable of relativistic effects, the dipole and octupole have wide-angle corrections which are suppressed by a factor
(\mathcal{H}/k)(r/d)
over the plane-parallel limit. For the even multipoles, the wide-angle corrections are of a similar size to the plane-parallel case.

Finally, we have given a full discussion of the resulting integrals that appear in the wide-angle expansion, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}^{p}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
. Although these are well known for some values of
p,\ell,\ell^{\prime}
, the full set of cases has not been discussed in this context
and given in terms of elementary functions
. Furthermore, in
Appendix C
we have given a new derivation of these integrals as distributions, together with a discussion of understanding the integrals as the Hadamard finite part, which allows us to give a general formula for an analytic function integrated against a pair of spherical Bessel functions (
C.39
).

Appendix C
Analysis of the integrals
\mathcal{I}^{p}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)

C.1
New derivation of
\mathcal{I}^{0}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)

Here we give a new derivation of the formulas for
\mathcal{I}^{0}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
, which starts from purely convergent integrals. We begin with

\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r\,j_{\ell}(kr)\,j_{\ell^{\prime}}(qr)\,.

(C.1)

Now, for large
r
,

\lim_{r\to\infty}j_{\ell}(kr)=\frac{\cos(kr-\pi(\ell+1)/2)}{kr}\,,

(C.2)

which implies that these integrals will converge absolutely, in contrast to the case with
p=0
, where the integral does not converge.

First, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\!\left({k},{q}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{4{k}}\left(\frac{q}{k}\right)^{\ell}\!\frac{\Gamma\left[\left(\ell+\ell^{\prime}+1\right)/2\right]}{\Gamma\left(\ell+3/2\right)\Gamma\left[1-\left(\ell-\ell^{\prime}\right)/2\right]}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\frac{\ell+\ell^{\prime}+1}{2},\frac{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}{2};\ell+\frac{3}{2};\frac{{q}^{2}}{{k}^{2}}\right)\,,

(C.3)

which is derived from the result
Maple
gives. However, this misses the full answer for all values of
\ell\ell^{\prime},k,q
. For
\ell-\ell^{\prime}
an odd number or zero, we have

\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)=\Theta(k-q)\,\tilde{g}_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\left({k},{q}\right)+\Theta(q-k)\,\tilde{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\left(q,k\right)\,,

(C.4)

while for
\ell-\ell^{\prime}
even, we have

\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)=\Theta(\ell-\ell^{\prime})\Theta(k-q)\tilde{g}_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\left({k},{q}\right)+\Theta(\ell^{\prime}-\ell)\Theta(q-k)\tilde{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\left(q,k\right)\,.

(C.5)

These formulas work for
k=q
provided that we use the definition of the step function:

\displaystyle\Theta\left({k}-{q}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\quad\mbox{for}&{k}>{q}\,,\\
{1}/{2}&\quad\mbox{for}&k=q\,,\\
0&\quad\mbox{for}&{k}<{q}\,.\end{array}\right.

The formulas can be converted to elementary functions; for example

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{0,0}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2q}\Theta(q-k)+\frac{\pi}{2k}\Theta(k-q)\,,

(C.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{1,0}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-q)(q+k)}{4qk^{2}}\ln\dfrac{q+k}{|k-q|}+\frac{1}{2k}\,,

(C.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{1,1}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{k\pi}{6q^{2}}\Theta(q-k)+\frac{q\pi}{6k^{2}}\Theta(k-q)\,,

(C.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{2,0}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(k-q)(q+k)}{4k^{3}}\Theta(k-q)\,,

(C.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{2,1}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-q)(q+k)\left(k^{2}+3q^{2}\right)}{16k^{3}q^{2}}\ln\dfrac{q+k}{|k-q|}-\frac{k^{2}-3q^{2}}{8qk^{2}}\,,

(C.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{2,2}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}\pi}{10q^{3}}\Theta(q-k)+\frac{q^{2}\pi}{10k^{3}}\Theta(k-q)\,.

(C.14)

These are similar in form to
\mathcal{I}^{0}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
, but without the
1/(k-q)
singular points. The points where
\ln|k-q|
causes problems in
\mathcal{I}^{0}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
always appear as
(k-q)\ln|k-q|
here, which is well behaved as
k\to q
. So these integrals always converge to finite values.

From these well-behaved non-singular formulas we can derive all
\mathcal{I}^{p}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
for
p\geq-1
, using the differentiation formulas given in the text. In terms of hypergeometric functions, these are not particularly helpful, although they are easy to derive (but painful to simplify!). However, for low values of
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
that we are interested in, it is straightforward in terms of elementary functions:

\bm{p=-1}

A single derivative of the
\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
formulas leaves weak singular points
\sim\ln|k-q|
. However, no delta functions appear because in
\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
the step functions are either symmetric in
k
and
q
(so that they cancel), or where they appear alone, they are accompanied by a factor
k-q
, so that any delta function appears as
(k-q)\delta(k-q)=0
. Step functions appear without being multiplied by
k-q
. We find for the first few:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{0,0}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2kq}\ln\frac{k+q}{|k-q|},

(C.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{1,0}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2k^{2}}\Theta(k-q),

(C.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{1,1}(k,q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(k^{2}+q^{2}\right)}{4k^{2}q^{2}}\ln\frac{|k-q|}{k+q}-\frac{1}{2qk},

(C.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{2,0}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{3q}{4k^{3}}\ln\frac{|k-q|}{(k+q)}+\frac{1}{4qk}\ln\frac{k+q}{|k-q|}+\frac{3}{2k^{2}},

(C.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{2,1}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{q\pi}{2k^{3}}\Theta(k-q),

(C.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{2,2}(k,q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(3k^{4}+2k^{2}q^{2}+3q^{4}\right)}{16k^{3}q^{3}}\ln\frac{|k-q|}{k+q}-\frac{3\left(k^{2}+q^{2}\right)}{8q^{2}k^{2}}.

(C.20)

\bm{p=0}

Another derivative implies that
\ln|k-q|\to 1/(k-q)
and the lone step functions now lead to the delta functions given in (
3.1
).

There are a variety of ways to check that these formulas make sense. For
p=-2,-1
we can just evaluate them numerically and check the results against these formulas. Alternatively, for low values of
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
, we can write
\int_{0}^{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}
, and perform the integral analytically.

For
p=0
these integrals are divergent, but we can check numerically that the formulas make sense for
q\neq k
. We give examples of simple cases, starting with
(\ell,\ell^{\prime},k,q)=1,1,2,3
, which is just an example of the closure relation – and thus should give zero. However, this is in fact not straightforward:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}j_{1}({2}r)j_{1}\left({3}r\right)r^{2}dr=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}j_{1}({2}r)j_{1}\left({3}r\right)r^{2}dr

(C.21)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{30t\sin t+6t\sin(5t)-5\cos t+5\cos(5t)}{360t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{30t\sin t+6t\sin(5t)+5\cos(5t)}{360t}\,,

which does not converge. However, the mean of this
is
zero, which is the result of the closure relation.
Trying other values of
(\ell,\ell^{\prime},k,q)
the general result works in the same way. For example,
(2,3,2,3)
gives numerically 0.07447888703 from the formulas above, so that numerically the integral does not converge as the upper limit
\to\infty
. Rewriting the spherical Bessel functions in terms of sin and cos and integrating gives a limit which oscillates between

-\frac{913}{1440}+\frac{233\ln 5}{3456}
and
\frac{163}{288}+\frac{233\ln 5}{3456},

with a mean value of 0.07447888703. From a distributional point of view, the oscillations cancel out, leaving only the mean.

For
p\geq 1
these results can be checked by integrating against a compact function in
k,q
to ensure their
distributional form
is correct. We have checked for small values of
|\ell-\ell^{\prime}|+p
even, where integrals such as

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2+p}\,j_{\ell}(kr)\,j_{\ell^{\prime}}(qr)\,k^{2}q^{2}{\rm e}^{-k^{2}-q^{2}}\,,

(C.22)

can be computed analytically by integrating over
k
and
q
first and then computing the
r
integral. These can then be compared with the same integrals computed with the distributions calculated here, which indeed agree.

C.2
Integrating the distributions – dealing with the singularities

As we saw by calculating everything from
\mathcal{I}^{-2}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
, the integrals
\mathcal{I}^{p}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)
should give meaningful answers for
p\geq 0
even though the integrals themselves are divergent. One way to see this is to write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}q}\,q^{2-n}\,P_{\rm m}(q)\mathcal{I}^{p}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)

\displaystyle=\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}-\frac{2}{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial k}+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{k^{2}}\right]^{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}q}\,q^{2-n}\,P_{\rm m}(q)\mathcal{I}^{p-2m}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,q)\,.

(C.23)

While the lhs appears to be divergent,
by differentiating the integral on the right with
2m=p+2
or
2m=p+1
appropriately we must get a finite answer. Where the integrals give distributions in the form of delta functions it’s clear what this means. For the other cases it’s a bit more subtle as a brute force numerical or analytical evaluation will give infinite answers (this is a key reason for treating the Fourier transforms as formal mathematical transforms rather than introducing cutoffs in
r
to try to remain within physical constraints). We know that all the singular points which are not delta functions come from derivatives of
(k-q)\ln|k-q|
, which give the singular terms of the form

\ln|k-q|,~~~\frac{1}{k-q},~~~\frac{1}{(k-q)^{2}},\cdots,\frac{1}{(k-q)^{p+1}}\,,

(C.24)

as we differentiate repeatedly.
The first is termed weakly singular, the second singular, and the higher powers, hyper-singular points. Consequently, what we need to understand is

\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}qf(q)(k-q)\ln|k-q|=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}qf(q)\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}\left[(k-q)\ln|k-q|\right]\,,

(C.25)

where we just focus on a region around
q=k
, so
0<a<k<b
.
On the left we have a regular expression but on the right we have now apparently created an integral with singular points, which diverges for
i>1

[
38
]
. How do we make sense of this? For
i=1
we can simplify,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f(q)\ln|k-q|=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,[f(q)-f(k)]\,\ln|k-q|+\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,[f(k)]\,\ln|k-q|

(C.26)

\displaystyle=f(k)\left[(b-k)\ln(b-k)+(k-a)\ln(k-a)+a-b\right]+\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,[f(q)-f(k)]\,\ln|k-q|,

where the remaining integral converges assuming that
f(q)
is analytic in the neighbourhood of
k=q
. On taking a derivative of this with respect to
k
we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f(q)\ln|k-q|=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)-f(k)}{k-q}+f(k)\ln\frac{k-a}{b-k}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{k-q}\,,

(C.27)

where
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int
represents the Cauchy Principal Value:

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{k-q}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left[\int_{a}^{k-\epsilon}+\int_{k+\epsilon}^{b}\right]\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{k-q}\,.

(C.28)

That is, a symmetric region about the singular point is removed and the limit taken to zero. Note all the integrals in (
C.27
) converge and terms involving
f^{\prime}(k)
cancel. We conclude that when we write an expression like

\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f(q)\ln|k-q|=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{k-q}\,,

(C.29)

swapping the derivative and integral means that we are only taking the principal value of the integral on the right; instead we should write

\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f(q)\ln|k-q|=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{k-q}\,.

(C.30)

We can take another derivative
[
39
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f(q)\ln|k-q|=\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\left[\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)-f(k)}{k-q}+f(k)\ln\frac{k-a}{b-k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)-f(k)-f^{\prime}(k)(q-k)}{(k-q)^{2}}+f(k)\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle=$}}\kern-7.3611pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle=$}}\kern-5.1528pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.29692pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.14937pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{1}{(k-q)^{2}}+f^{\prime}(k)\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{k-q}

\displaystyle=-\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)-f(k)-f^{\prime}(k)(k-q)}{(k-q)^{2}}+f(k)\left[\frac{1}{k-a}+\frac{1}{b-k}\right]+f^{\prime}(k)\ln\frac{k-a}{b-k}

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle=$}}\kern-7.3611pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle=$}}\kern-5.1528pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.29692pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.14937pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{(k-q)^{2}}\,.

(C.31)

Here, the notation
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle=$}}\kern-7.3611pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle=$}}\kern-5.1528pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.29692pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.14937pt}}\!\int
is the Hadamard finite part of the integral, which generalises the Cauchy Principal Value to hyper-singular points. For this we delete a small interval around
k=q
(it does not have to be symmetric), and take the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, and we ignore any diverging terms.
Alternatively, following
[
38
]
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f(q)\ln|k-q|=\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\left[\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)-f(k)}{k-q}+f(k)\ln\frac{k-a}{b-k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)-f(k)}{(k-q)^{2}}+f(k)\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle=$}}\kern-7.3611pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle=$}}\kern-5.1528pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.29692pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.14937pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{1}{(k-q)^{2}}=-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)-f(k)}{(k-q)^{2}}+f(k)\left[\frac{1}{k-a}+\frac{1}{b-k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle=$}}\kern-7.3611pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle=$}}\kern-5.1528pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.29692pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.14937pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{(k-q)^{2}}\,.

(C.32)

These expressions differ only by divergent terms which we can miraculously ignore (see
[
38
]
).
In a similar manner we can find the finite part of

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle=$}}\kern-7.3611pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle=$}}\kern-5.1528pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.29692pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle=$}}\kern-4.14937pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{1}{(k-q)^{p+1}}=\frac{(-1)^{p}}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p}}{\partial k^{p}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{1}{k-q}\,.

(C.33)

Numerically we find it straightforward to calculate these integrals using integration by parts, which naturally returns the finite part. For example,

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{f(q)}{k-q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f(q)\ln|k-q|=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f(q)\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\ln|k-q|

\displaystyle=-\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}q\,f^{\prime}(q)\ln|k-q|+~\text{boundary terms.}

(C.34)

In general, our integrals consist of regular parts plus singular parts and are of the form

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\,\left[f_{1}(q,k)[\ln|k-q|-\ln(k+q)]+\frac{f_{2}(q,k)}{(k-q)^{p+1}}\right]\,.

(C.35)

The singularities at
k=q
can be evaluated by parts, assuming that
P_{\rm m}(q)
and its derivatives vanish sufficiently rapidly at
q=0
and
q=\infty
. Integrating the last term by parts implies that the non-regular parts of this integral can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}q\!\left[f_{1}(q,k)\ln|k-q|+\frac{f_{2}(q,k)}{(k-q)^{p+1}}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\mathrm{d}q\ln|k-q|\!\left[f_{1}(q,k)+\frac{(-1)^{p+1}}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p+1}f_{2}(q,k)}{\partial q^{p+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\,(k-q)(1-\ln|k-q|)\left[-\frac{\partial f_{1}(q,k)}{\partial q}+\frac{(-1)^{p+1}}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p+2}f_{2}(q,k)}{\partial q^{p+2}}\right],

(C.36)

where we integrated by parts to remove the logarithmic singularity.
Note that the part of the integrand containing
(k-q)(1-\ln|k-q|)
is now regular
[
39
]
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}q\left\{f_{1}(q,k)\Big[\ln|k-q|-\ln(k+q)\Big]+\frac{f_{2}(q,k)}{(k-q)^{p+1}}\right\}

(C.37)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}q\left\{-f_{1}(q,k)\ln(k+q)-(k-q)(1-\ln|k-q|)\left[\frac{\partial f_{1}(q,k)}{\partial q}-\frac{(-1)^{p+1}}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p+2}f_{2}(q,k)}{\partial q^{p+2}}\right]\right\}.

In reality we are dealing with an integral over the power spectrum with finite limits so the boundary terms need to be taken into account. In general,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\!\mathrm{d}q\left[f_{1}(q,k)[\ln|k-q|-\ln(k+q)]+\frac{f_{2}(q,k)}{(k-q)^{p+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\!\mathrm{d}q\left\{-f_{1}(q,k)\ln(k+q)+(k-q)(1-\ln|k-q|)\left[-\frac{\partial f_{1}(q,k)}{\partial q}+\frac{(-1)^{p+1}}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p+2}f_{2}(q,k)}{\partial q^{p+2}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\frac{(p-(i+1))!}{p!(q-k)^{p-i}}\frac{\partial^{i}f_{2}(q,k)}{\partial q^{i}}\bigg|_{q=a}^{b}+\frac{(-1)^{p+1}}{p!}\ln|k-q|\frac{\partial^{p}f_{2}(q,k)}{\partial q^{p}}\bigg|_{q=a}^{b}

\displaystyle+(k-q)(1-\ln|k-q|)\left[-f_{1}(q,k)+\frac{(-1)^{p}}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p}f_{2}(q,k)}{\partial q^{p}}\right]\bigg|_{q=a}^{b}.

(C.38)

C.3
A general formula for
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r\,f(r)j_{\ell}(kr)\,j_{\ell^{\prime}}(qr)

Given an analytic function
f(r)
on
[0,\infty)
we can derive the general formula:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r\,f(r)j_{\ell}(kr)\,j_{\ell^{\prime}}(qr)

(C.39)
