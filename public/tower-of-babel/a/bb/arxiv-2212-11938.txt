On Boundedness of Isomerization paths for Non- and Semirelativistic Molecules
Ioannis Anapolitanos, Marco Olivieri, Sylvain Zalczer

Abstract
. This article focuses on isomerizations of molecules, i.e. chemical reactions during which a molecule is transformed into another one with atoms in a different spatial configuration. We consider the special case in which the system breaks into two submolecules whose internal geometry is solid during the whole procedure. We prove, under some conditions, that the distance between the two submolecules stays bounded during the reaction. To this end, we provide an asymptotic expansion of the interaction energy between two molecules, including multipolar interactions and the van der Waals attraction. In addition to this static result, we proceed to a quasistatic analysis to investigate the variation of the energy when the nuclei move. This paper extends [ 6 ] in two directions. The first one is that we relax the assumption that the ground state eigenspaces of the submolecules have to fulfill. The second one is that we allow semirelativistic kinetic energy as well.



An isomerization is a chemical reaction with the property that the reactant is a single molecule with the same atoms as the product, but in a different spatial configuration. One simple example is the isomerization HCN
\rightarrow
CNH. Isomerizations play, especially in organic and organometallic chemistry, an important role, see
e.g.

[
17
]
. The question of how much energy is needed for an isomerization to take place is very fundamental. The study of it occupies a large amount of the numerical computations in quantum chemistry, see for example
[
22
]
and references therein.

As explained in
[
29
, Chapter 17]
, real world chemical systems, involving a large number of particles, are very complicated and some approximations have to be made. We use here the Born-Oppenheimer approximation: we assume that electronic and nuclear motions occur at different time scales, so that one can first solve the Schrödinger equation for electrons with fixed nuclei, and then study the dynamics of the nuclei with a potential corresponding to the electronic energy. In this second step, we treat nuclei as classical particles. The Born-Oppenheimer approximation relies on the fact that nuclei are much heavier than electrons and it is customary
to use it for slow chemical reactions. The system is thus considered in a
quasistatic
way: the electrons are assumed to be in equilibrium at any moment of the motion of the nuclei.
The physical setting that we consider is for zero temperature, but it should be reasonable for small temperatures. A discussion about the Born-Oppenheimer approximation and its limits, from a chemistry point of view, can be found in
[
29
, Section 3.1]
. For a discussion from a mathematical point of view, we refer to
[
51
]
.

Another usual approximation is to neglect the relativistic effects. In the non-relativistic setting, the kinetic energy of a particle with mass
m
is given by the differential operator
-(\hbar^{2}/2m)\Delta
, where
\Delta
denotes the Laplace operator.
Nevertheless, the relativistic effects can be important, becoming stronger and stronger when the atomic numbers of involved atoms increase. Thus, if relativistic correction terms are needed only for very precise calculations for light atoms such as hydrogen, for atoms as heavy as silver or copper, relativistic effects cannot be neglected. For elements of the sixth row of the periodic table such as gold, lead or mercury, relativistic effects alter the chemical and physical behavior qualitatively, see
[
9
]
. Examples of relativistic effects are on molecular geometry, especially the length of covalent bonds, but they also give better values for dissociation energies of bonds and ionization potentials and explain common-life phenomena such as the yellow color of gold or the low melting point of mercury. Surveys about relativistic effects in structural chemistry can be found in
[
42
]
and
[
9
]
. The specific case of isomerizations has been studied for molecules containing heavy atoms such as platinum
[
45
]
, gold
[
41
]
or silver
[
15
]
and more generally for organometallic molecules
[
17
]
.

In order to be able to easily use variational formulations for relativistic systems, we will use the semirelativistic Hamiltonian, where the kinetic energy is obtained by applying to the classical relativistic kinetic energy
E=\sqrt{c^{2}p^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}}-mc^{2}
the standard quantization
p\rightarrow-{\rm i}\hbar\nabla
, which gives the pseudo-differential operator

T=\sqrt{-c^{2}\hbar^{2}\Delta+m^{2}c^{4}}-mc^{2}.

As it is well-known, the operator
T
is non local, which makes it more difficult to deal with than the standard Laplacian. Moreover, it is not possible to include external electromagnetic fields in a relativistically invariant way since, in the associated evolution equation, space and time derivatives are not symmetric. That is why Dirac introduced the operator bearing his name to describe relativistic quantum particles. Chemists usually use it in their computational study of relativistic effects, see for example
[
42
]
and
[
9
]
. Nevertheless, it is well known that the Dirac operator is not bounded from below, making it impossible to use a simple variational formulation, see for example
[
52
]
. That is why we consider here the semirelativistic kinetic energy described by the operator
T
.
Hamiltonians associated with this kinetic energy have been studied as early as in the 70s, for example by Herbst in the case of an hydrogenoid ion
[
24
]
. Lieb and Yau in
[
36
]
and Fefferman and de la Llave
[
19
]
discussed its stability in many-particle systems.

The general mathematical framework to study isomerizations is similar in the non-relativistic and in the semirelativistic settings. Two isomers of a molecule correspond to two stable configurations of the entire system. In these configurations, the ground state energy of the system has local minima with respect to the positions of the nuclei. In the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, the isomerization reaction is described by a path of the nuclei connecting these two local minima. The difference between the maximum and initial ground state energies along one path corresponds to the amount of energy needed to bring the system from one state to the other. The
activation energy
is the lowest energy needed for the reaction to happen, and it is obtained by minimizing the maximal energy over all possible paths linking the two stable configurations. Figure

provides a graphical representation of a hypothetical optimal path.

While chemists work hard to compute the activation energy, from a mathematics point of view even its existence, namely the existence of an optimal path, is an open problem.

From the point of view of critical point theory, an isomerization corresponds to a
mountain pass problem

[
3
,
48
,
28
]
, as Figure
1
illustrates. The difficulties in this situation are, however, very different from the ones present in most cases of the mathematical literature where mountain pass problems are considered. Usually, the mountain pass theorem is used to prove existence of a critical point of a functional, implying existence of a solution to a PDE. In this situation, the configuration space is infinite dimensional. In our case, the mountain pass problem has, in its simpler formulation, a finite dimensional configuration space. However, as we will explain, proving that an optimizing sequence of paths stays on a bounded set is in our situation often difficult, and it is the main issue that we deal with in this paper. This boundedness would physically mean that isomerizations take place without breaking the whole system infinitely apart. This was conjectured and proven in some specific cases in
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. Another particularity of the mountain pass problem that we consider is that it would be important to have an optimal path, as we discussed above.
The mountain pass theorem, however, does not imply the existence of such a path, even if the configuration space is finite dimensional.

One of the forces that play an important role in our analysis is the van der Waals force. It is a purely quantum and universal force that occurs when two molecules are placed at a distance
L
from each other, where
L
is not too small.
It is originated by an attractive potential of the order
1/L^{6}
and it is thus
an attractive force of the order of
1/L^{7}
. It is induced as a result of quantum correlations between the two molecules.
A first proof of existence of an asymptotic expansion of the interaction energy of two neutral atoms in powers of the inverse distance between their nuclei was given in
[
38
]
, assuming non-degeneracy of the ground state eigenspaces of the atoms.
An upper bound of the ground state energy of the system was proved in
[
35
]

using an intricate test function. It implies existence of an attractive potential that is, in the order of
L
, at least as strong as van der Waals potential for some orientations.
The exact expression in the leading order
has been more recently derived for individual non-relativistic atoms in
[
8
]
, assuming that the ground state energies of the atoms are irreducible. Irreducible roughly means that the ground states of the atoms are unique up to spin permutations, see
e.g.
Definition 1 and the related discussion in
[
6
]
.
In
[
4
]
, the entire remainder was analyzed in the spinless case in order to extract information on its dependence on the number of atoms. The long-range asymptotics was studied coupled with the number of atoms going to infinity. Progress in this direction would be important for the study of gases.
For the case of two atoms, the results of
[
8
]
were improved in
[
7
]
in the following way: the monotonicity and derivatives of the interaction energy were studied
and the irreducibility assumption was dropped for one of the two atoms. In
[
5
]
, the interaction energy of a molecule with a half infinite plate was analyzed.
In
[
11
]
, the van der Waals force was derived for atoms with semirelativistic kinetic energy, and the results also included an expansion with higher order terms including
e.g.
the Axilrod-Teller-Muto
three-body potential for atoms, see also
[
23
]
. In
[
13
]
the leading coefficient of the van der Waals interaction of two hydrogen atoms was studied and numerically approximated with rigorous proofs on the error estimates. In
[
12
]
the analysis was extended for coefficients of higher order terms. In
[
12
]
, it was moreover shown that the scattering states of the hydrogen atom, namely the states associated with the continuous spectrum of the Hamiltonian, have a major contribution to the leading coefficient of the van der Waals expansion. Note that the results described above are
static
,
i.e.
they do not consider any motion of the nuclei.

In
[
6
]
, the upper bound of
[
35
]
was improved and the results of
[
8
]
were extended to the case of molecules, still in a non-relativistic model.
The existence of the van der Waals force implies that
all
neutral molecules can bind in the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, that is, the energy always has a global minimum with respect to the nuclear positions. This is because the energy of a molecule which splits into pieces is necessarily higher than the lowest possible energy, due to the van der Waals force. This was already observed in
[
35
]
.

However, studying isomerizations has additional difficulties, even in the simplest case that the system splits into two solid submolecules, which is the case that we consider.
This is because if two molecules are far apart, there usually exist some orientations for which they repel, for example if they have dipole moments oriented in opposite directions. Here, the information that the static results described above provide is not sufficient to conclude and we need to conduct a quasistatic analysis, in which nuclei are allowed to move. This was already one of the main difficulties in the study of isomerizations in
[
30
,
32
,
6
]
.

In
[
30
]
, Lewin proved boundedness of all reaction paths, for a system containing two rigid molecules, each having a non-degenerate ground state with a non-vanishing dipole moment. A key point of the analysis is that the critical points of the dipole-dipole interaction with positive energy all have a Morse index greater than or equal to 2,
i.e.
at least two directions of decrease. In
[
32
]
, the completely different situation of a molecule with only one moving atom (like for the reaction HCN
\rightarrow
CNH) was treated. If the single atom escapes to infinity, then only the van der Waals force pertains since, by symmetry, the atomic ground state has no multipole moment in average.
All other cases were left open in
[
30
,
32
]
.

In
[
6
]
, the results of
[
30
,
32
]
were extended in many directions, and important progress was made for the case of a molecule composed of two rigid submolecules. The most important results were in the case when the two molecules have low order multipole moments, that is, such that the leading energy of the system is of the order
1/L^{p}
with
p<6
. In this case, the van der Waals force does not dominate in the long range asymptotics and the molecules may repel each other depending on their orientations. That was the main difficulty in
[
6
]
.
On the other hand, the authors proved that if sufficiently many of the multipole moments of the two molecules vanish, then the van der Waals force dominates in the long range asymptotics, as physically expected. In that case, the boundedness of reaction paths followed quite immediately from the dominance of the van der Waals force.

Here, we extend the results of
[
6
]
in two directions. The first one is that we relax the assumption of irreducibility of the ground state eigenspaces of the molecules. The new assumption is more natural and it states that the multipole moments of the molecules are independent of the choice of a normalized ground state. The second one is that we consider molecules with semirelativistic kinetic energy as well. As we explained above, isomerizations can involve molecules with heavy atoms, for which the relativistic effects of the electrons become important. Our approach requires to extend results of
[
6
]
and
[
11
]
to the case of semirelativistic molecules on the van der Waals force, and relax the irreducibility assumption made in
[
6
]
and
[
11
]
. This direction is of interest on its own. Further difficulties in extending the results of
[
6
]

arise on computing derivatives with respect to rotations of a variant of the ground state energy of the system, not only due to the nonlocality of the semirelativistic Laplacian, but also because, in the semirelativistic case, ground states have less regularity than in the non-relativistic case. In particular, in Section
3
, the approach of
[
6
]
to take into account the quasistatic behavior does not seem to work in the semirelativistic setting, while the results of
[
11
]
are purely static. We use ideas of
[
27
]
, which Hunziker used to prove smoothness of the ground state energy for bosonic non-relativistic systems. We substantially rework and refine these ideas so that we can quantify the derivatives and so that the analysis is applicable to the semirelativistic case as well. In the non-relativistic case, our strategy is simpler than the one of
[
6
]
and quantifies higher order derivatives as well.

On the other hand, the careful study of the multipole-multipole interactions in Section 4 of
[
6
]
does not depend on the kind of kinetic energy and is thus immediately applicable to our setting.

In the next section, we define the system properly and describe the main open questions. Then we state our new results. The rest of the paper is devoted to the proof of our theorems.

1.
Model and main results

1.1.
Schrödinger Hamiltonian for molecules

We consider a molecule composed of

•

N
electrons of charge
e
with position variables
x:=(x_{1},...,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3N}
;

•

M
nuclei of atomic numbers
\mathcal{Z}:=(Z_{1},...,Z_{M})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}
, and positions
Y:=(y_{1},...,y_{M})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3M}
with
y_{j}\neq y_{k}
for
j\neq k
.

The total nuclear charge will be denoted by

(1.1)

|\mathcal{Z}|:=\sum_{i=1}^{M}Z_{i}.

As explained in the introduction, we work in the Born-Oppenheimer approximation. In a first step,
the nuclei are fixed and pointwise, and we study the spectrum of a Hamiltonian
H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
only describing electrons. In a second step, we
consider the ground state energy of
H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
as an effective potential for the nuclei in order to investigate properties of reaction paths. To define
H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
, we need the Hilbert space associated with the electrons, which is

\mathcal{H}:=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\left\{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}\right),

with the inner product corresponding to the quadratic form given by

\langle\psi,\psi\rangle:=\sum_{s_{1}\in\{\pm 1/2\}}...\sum_{s_{N}\in\{\pm 1/2\}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}...\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\psi(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2},...,x_{N},s_{N})|^{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}...\mathrm{d}x_{N}.

The introduction of the set
\{-1/2,1/2\}
is due to the fact that an electron has 2 spin states.

The usual models of quantum mechanics do not consider relativistic effects.
However, as explained in the introduction, when the molecule contains heavy atoms, it is important to take these effects into account. Since we want to consider as well molecules without heavy atoms, we introduce both the non-relativistic and the semirelativistic kinetic energy operators, which are, in physical units and for the
j-
th electron,

(1.2)

T_{j}:=\begin{cases}\sqrt{-\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta_{j}+m^{2}c^{4}}-mc^{2},\quad&\text{ semirelativistic case {(SR)}},\\
-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta_{j},\quad&\text{ non-relativistic case {(NR)}},\end{cases}

where
\Delta_{j}
is the usual Laplace operator in the variable
x_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
\hbar
is the reduced Planck’s constant,
c
the speed of light and
m
the mass of the electron. The square root of the differential operator is defined through Fourier transformation: for
\psi\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
and almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, we have in the
(SR)
case

(1.3)

T\psi(x):=\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[(\sqrt{c^{2}\hbar^{2}|\cdot|^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}}-mc^{2})\mathcal{F}\psi\right]\right)(x),

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transformation on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})

(1.4)

(\mathcal{F}\psi)(p):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{e}^{-{\rm i}p\cdot x}\psi(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Of course,
T_{j}
can also be defined with the Fourier transformation as a sesquilinear form on
H^{\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.
Note that, due to the fermionic nature of electrons, we consider the cases where all kinetic energies are semirelativistic or all are non-relativistic. Since we consider a system with
N
electrons, we introduce
the
N
-particle Hamiltonian

(1.5)

H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}T_{j}+\hbar c\alpha I_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z}),

where
\alpha:=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}\hbar c}
is a dimensionless physical constant, which is called the fine structure constant (
\alpha\approx 1/137
). The electric potential is

(1.6)

I_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z}):=-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{i}}{|x_{j}-y_{i}|}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}\frac{1}{|x_{i}-x_{j}|}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant M}\frac{Z_{i}Z_{j}}{|y_{i}-y_{j}|},

where the summands respectively represent attraction between the electrons and the nuclei, repulsion between the electrons and repulsion between the nuclei.

In the non-relativistic case,
H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
is always self-adjoint with operator domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
and bounded from below: see for example
[
33
, Theorem 6.2]
, or
[
50
, Section 11.1]
, where the proof is given only in the case of atoms but can be easily adapted to molecules. In the semirelativistic case,
it was proved by Lieb and Yau in
[
36
]
and Fefferman and de la Llave in
[
19
]
that, if
\alpha
is small enough, including the physical case
\alpha\approx 1/137
, and, for all
k\in\{1,\dots,M\}
,

(1.7)

Z_{k}<\frac{2}{\pi\alpha},

then there exists
b<1
such that, for all
\psi\in H^{1/2}(R^{3N})
,

(1.8)

\langle\psi,I_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})\psi\rangle\leqslant b\Big\langle\psi,\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}\psi\Big\rangle,

uniformly in
Y
.
As a consequence,

H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
is bounded from below and self-adjoint with form domain
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
. We note here that the inequality (
1.8
) does not seem to provide information on the operator domain of
H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
. In particular, the operator domain may contain functions that are not in
H^{1}
. The
\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}
operator boundedness of
I_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
with relative bound less than 1 would guarantee that the operator domain of
H_{N}(Y,Z)
is
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3N})
. Such operator boundedness follows from the Hardy inequality if
\alpha
is small enough but it is not clear to us how small
\alpha
should be for large molecules. In particular, it is not clear if the physical value
\alpha\approx\frac{1}{137}
would be included. For this reason, we avoid this assumption. In fact, it is not certain that
H^{1}
regularity would make our analysis easier.

In the rest of the paper, we will assume that we have chosen units such that

(1.9)

\hbar=c=m=1.

Moreover, we assume that
\alpha
is small enough and that (
1.7
) holds, so that
H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
is bounded from below and we are not going to mention these assumptions explicitly.

For a more precise analysis, one needs to consider the fermionic nature of the electrons. To this end, we consider the permutation group
S_{N}
of
\{1,\ldots,N\}

We denote by

\bigwedge_{j=1}^{N}L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\left\{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}\right)

(1.11)

Q_{N}\Psi=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{N}}(-1)^{\pi}\pi\cdot\Psi,

Here

(\Gamma_{s})_{s\in\mathcal{I}}
is a family of functions such that there exists

\exists b,D>0

\|e^{b|.|}\gamma_{s}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3N})}\leqslant D.

Note that the fact that

is not assumed to be an exact eigenfunction of

can be useful, as one may prove with Theorem

that
an exponentially decaying function remains exponentially decaying after multiplication with a resolvent of
\hat{H}_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
.

Corollary 1.3

(Exponential decay of eigenfunctions)
.

Choosing
\Gamma=0
, the above theorem implies that any eigenfunction
\gamma
of
\hat{H}_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
with associated eigenvalue below its essential spectrum is exponentially decaying, in the sense that (

) holds.

The following theorem is an extension of Zhislin’s theorem to the
(SR)
case.

Theorem 1.4
.

If
|\mathcal{Z}|>N-1
, then
\hat{H}_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
has infinitely many eigenvalues below the infimum of its essential spectrum. In particular, it has a ground state and the ground state eigenspace is finite-dimensional.

We prove Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.4
in Appendix

.

Remark 1.5
.

Note that, in
[
11
]
, a variant of Theorem

was proven for
(SR)
atoms, but only the existence of at least one eigenvalue below the bottom of the essential spectrum was shown. Due to the nonlocality of the
(SR)
kinetic energy operator, we have to modify their argument in order to prove that there are infinitely many eigenvalues below the bottom of the essential spectrum.

From Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
, it follows that, for every

with

Moreover, the eigenspace associated with

has finite dimension.

1.2.
Isomerizations in the case of two rigid submolecules: the model

From now on, we consider only globally neutral systems,
i.e.
the case where

N=|\mathcal{Z}|.

Moreover, we assume that the molecule is composed of two rigid submolecules, which can only be rotated and translated with respect to each other. This restriction is unsatisfactory as this has no physical justification and, in many isomerizations, there is splitting to more than
two solid submolecules. However, removing the assumption seems to be very hard. In some particular cases,
e.g.
reaction HCN
\to
CNH, the assumption is reasonable. Some bigger organic molecules are made of 2 parts, which, if isomerization happens, should approximately remain rigid. This should be the case, for example, for the cis-trans isomerization of azobenzene, see
e.g.

[
49

. We refer to the introduction and appendix C of
[
6
]
for some discussion of the general case of several submolecules.

Let
Y_{1}:=(y_{1},...,y_{M_{1}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3M_{1}}
,
Y_{2}:=(y_{M_{1}+1},...,y_{M})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3M_{2}}
be the nuclear positions of the two solid submolecules, where
M_{1}+M_{2}=M
. The collections of their atomic numbers are denoted by
\mathcal{Z}_{1}:=(Z_{1},...,Z_{M_{1}})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M_{1}}
,
\mathcal{Z}_{2}:=(Z_{M_{1}+1},...,Z_{M})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M_{2}}
.

We assume that each of these molecules is neutral, which means that the
i
-th molecule has
N_{i}=|\mathcal{Z}_{i}|
electrons, where
|\mathcal{Z}_{i}|
is defined as in (
1.1
).
For
i=1,2
, we can define the Hamiltonian for the
i
-th molecule as in (
1.5
)

(1.19)

H_{i}:=H_{|\mathcal{Z}_{i}|}(Y_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{i}),

its version with statistics as in (
1.12
)

(1.20)

\hat{H}_{i}:=\hat{H}_{|\mathcal{Z}_{i}|}(Y_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{i})

and its ground state energy

(1.21)

E_{i}:=E_{|\mathcal{Z}_{i}|}(Y_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{i}),

in the same way as in (
1.15
).
We then consider all possible ways of placing these two molecules in space. Without loss of generality, we can place the first molecule at the origin and the second one at a distance
L
in the direction
e_{1}:=(1,0,0)
, and simply rotate the two molecules using

(see Figure

and denote by

the nuclear positions, as well as by

(1.23)

\boxed{\mathcal{E}_{\tau}:=E_{N}\big(Y(\tau),\mathcal{Z}\big)=\inf\sigma(\hat{H}_{N}(Y(\tau),\mathcal{Z}))}

the corresponding ground state energy.
The Hamiltonian has now the explicit expression

(1.24)

H_{\tau}:=H_{N}(Y(\tau),\mathcal{Z})=H_{1,\tau}+H_{2,\tau}+I_{\tau},

where
H_{1,\tau}

H_{2,\tau}
and
I_{\tau}
are, respectively, the Hamiltonians for the first and the second molecule and the interaction among them. Explicitly, they are given by

(1.25)

H_{1,\tau}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}T_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}}\frac{Z_{k}\alpha}{|x_{j}-Uy_{k}|}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{1}}\frac{\alpha}{|x_{j}-x_{i}|}+\sum_{1\leqslant k<l\leqslant M_{1}}\frac{Z_{k}Z_{l}\alpha}{|Uy_{k}-Uy_{l}|},

(1.26)

H_{2,\tau}:=\sum_{j=N_{1}+1}^{N}T_{j}-\sum_{j=N_{1}+1}^{N}\sum_{k=M_{1}+1}^{M}\frac{Z_{k}\alpha}{|x_{j}-Vy_{k}-Le_{1}|}+\sum_{N_{1}+1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}\frac{\alpha}{|x_{j}-x_{i}|}\\
+\sum_{M_{1}+1\leqslant k<l\leqslant M}\frac{Z_{k}Z_{l}\alpha}{|Vy_{k}-Vy_{l}|},

(1.27)

I_{\tau}:=-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{k=M_{1}+1}^{M}\frac{Z_{k}\alpha}{|x_{j}-Vy_{k}-Le_{1}|}-\sum_{j=N_{1}+1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}}\frac{Z_{k}\alpha}{|x_{j}-Uy_{k}|}\\
+\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{l={M_{1}+1}}^{M}\frac{Z_{k}Z_{l}\alpha}{|Uy_{k}-Vy_{l}-Le_{1}|}+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{j=N_{1}+1}^{N}\frac{\alpha}{|x_{i}-x_{j}|}.

\hat{H}_{\tau}:=H_{\tau}|_{\mathrm{Ran}Q_{N}},\qquad\hat{H}_{1,\tau}:=H_{1,\tau}|_{\mathrm{Ran}Q_{N_{1}}},\qquad\hat{H}_{2,\tau}:=H_{2,\tau}|_{\mathrm{Ran}Q_{N_{2}}},

Since we are interested in isomerizations, we have to assume that there exist different isomers for the considered molecule. This is done with the following condition.

Condition
.

(Min)
There exist two local minima
\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\in(0,\infty)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)
of
\tau\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
that are strict with respect to the relative displacements
\tau
that do not leave

invariant.

This condition is natural, as it means that the reactant and the product of the isomerization are stable molecules, in the sense that energy is needed to change their structure.

We consider the
mountain pass level

(1.29)

\boxed{c:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau(0)=\tau_{0},\\
\tau(1)=\tau_{1}\end{subarray}}\;\max_{t\in[0,1]}\;\mathcal{E}_{\tau(t)}}

where, as usual, it is understood that
\tau:[0,1]\to(0,\infty)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)
is continuous.
If
\tau_{0}
gives the shape of the reactant of the isomerization, the difference
c-\mathcal{E}_{\tau_{0}}
can be understood as the least energy needed for the isomerization to happen. The existence of a continuous path
\widetilde{\tau}
with
\widetilde{\tau}(0)=\tau_{0},\widetilde{\tau}(1)=\tau_{1}
and
c=\max_{t\in[0,1]}\;\mathcal{E}_{\widetilde{\tau}(t)}
would imply that the needed energy for the interaction can be indeed optimized. As we mentioned above, this is, to our best of knowledge, an open problem.

In our setting, we consider paths where the system is in a ground state at each time. However, it makes sense to let the electrons evolve along the path as well. For this reason, considering two local minima
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
as in Condition
, we choose two ground states
\overline{\Psi}_{0}
and
\overline{\Psi}_{1}
corresponding to the ground state energies

and
\mathcal{E}_{\tau_{1}}
, respectively, and define a new mountain pass level by

(1.30)

\boxed{c^{\prime}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\tau,\Psi)(0)=(\tau_{0},\overline{\Psi}_{0})\\
(\tau,\Psi)(1)=(\tau_{1},\overline{\Psi}_{1})\end{subarray}}\;\max_{t\in[0,1]}{\left\langle\Psi(t),H_{\tau(t)}\Psi(t)\right\rangle}}

which is independent of the choices of
\overline{\Psi}_{0}
,
\overline{\Psi}_{1}
and obviously larger than or equal to
c
. Recall that
H_{\tau(t)}
is defined by (
1.24
).
It is understood here that
\tau(t)
is a continuous path as before and that
\Psi(t)
is a continuous path of wavefunctions in the form domain of
\hat{H}_{\tau(t)}
(which is in fact independent of
t
, as stated in (
1.14
)), such that
\|\Psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}=1
for all

.

We note that the mountain pass problem (
1.30

1.29
) were known, existence of an optimizer for (
1.30
) would not immediately follow.
Nevertheless, it is proven in
[
30
, Theorem 4]
(see also
[

, Appendix A]
) that

Moreover, existence of a bounded minimizing sequence

for (

(\tau_{n},\Psi_{n})
for (

). If an optimal path
(\tau_{n},\Psi_{n})
exists, the point of its maximum is called a
transition state
in chemistry. In the
(NR)
setting, Theorem 4 of
[

]
provides, under condition
and boundedness of a minimizing sequence, existence of such a transition state in a more general setting: it is not necessary that the system splits into only two rigid submolecules. We conjecture that this holds in the
(SR)

1.3.1.
Expansion of the energy: multipole interactions and the van der Waals force

In this section, we expand the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}=\mathcal{E}_{(L,U,V)}
for large

and get the van der Waals energy as well as all the lower order multipolar energies. In particular, we prove that there always exists a non-vanishing term of order

Let

Furthermore, since we consider not only static but also quasistatic setting, we often need to control derivatives of quantities with respect to the position as well. To this purpose, let us introduce the set of matrices

(1.31)

\Gamma:=\{M\in\mathfrak{so}(3)\,|\;|M_{ij}|\leqslant 1,\;\forall i,j\in\{1,2,3\}\},

where recall that

We say that

(1.32)

such that, for all

such that, for all

,

and all

(1.33)

\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}t^{n}}g(L,e^{tA}U,e^{tB}V)\right\|_{W}\leqslant C_{n}f(L).

If
W={\mathbb{R}}
or
W=\mathbb{C}

is going to be omitted from the notation and we will write

or

When we write

The definition of regularity given here is formulated differently in

In order to be able to formulate our theorems, we need to introduce the multipolar interactions. We recall here the expansions introduced in

1.4.
Sketch of the proof of Theorem

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1.12

[
6
]

We will only consider the case where

E_{\infty}=c,

with

defined in (
1.29
), (
1.53

If (

[

We want to prove that there exists a min-maxing sequence
(\tau_{n})
for the mountain pass problem (
1.29

n

(1.67)

L_{n}(t)\leqslant L_{\mathrm{cut}},

Definition

, for which
\widetilde{L}_{n}(t)
might be unbounded. We will modify it to construct a min-maxing sequence

such that (
1.67

as a parameter and, for a given

start by looking at the first time

for which

. These times depend on
L_{\mathrm{cut}}
and

We will construct a path

which coincides with

outside

and for which

\max_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\mathcal{E}_{(L_{\mathrm{cut}},U(t),V(t))}\leqslant\max\big\{\mathcal{E}_{(L_{\mathrm{cut}},\widetilde{U}_{n}(t_{0}),\widetilde{V}_{n}(t_{0}))},\mathcal{E}_{(L_{\mathrm{cut}},\widetilde{U}_{n}(t_{1}),\widetilde{V}_{n}(t_{1}))},E_{\infty}\big\}.

Step 1. Replacing the two points by local (pseudo-)minima

Our first step is to rotate the molecules at each point
t_{0},t_{1}
, without changing the distance
L_{\mathrm{cut}}
, so that we reach a local minimum of the energy of the system with respect to rotations. In general, this is impossible if, in some directions, the energy takes values higher and lower than that of the current point, at arbitrarily small distances. Since we cannot exclude this pathological behavior, we introduce, as in
[
6
]
, the weaker notion of local pseudo-minimum.

Definition 1.16

(Local pseudo-minimum)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be any continuous function on
\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)
. A point
(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)
is called a

\mathcal{W}
if, for any
A,B\in\Gamma
, as defined in (
1.31
), there exists a sequence
t_{n}\to 0+
with
t_{n}\neq 0
such that

\mathcal{W}(e^{t_{n}A}\overline{U},e^{t_{n}B}\overline{V})\geqslant\mathcal{W}(\overline{U},\overline{V})
.

In other words, a local pseudo-minimum is a point at which one cannot find a direction in
\mathrm{SO}(3)^{2}
in which
\mathcal{W}
strictly decreases. If
\mathcal{W}
is
C^{2}
and has a local pseudo-minimum at
(\overline{U},\overline{V})
, one must then have

(1.69)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{W}(e^{tA}\overline{U},e^{tB}\overline{V})\big|_{t=0}=0,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\mathcal{W}(e^{tA}\overline{U},e^{tB}\overline{V})\big|_{t=0}\geqslant 0

for all
A,B\in\Gamma
, but
(\overline{U},\overline{V})
does not need to be a true local minimum.
A counterexample can be found even for functions of one variable: if

f(x)=x^{4}\sin(1/x)\chi_{\{x<0\}}(x)+x^{4}\chi_{\{x>0\}}(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},

where
\chi_{C}
denotes the characteristic function of the set
C
, then

has a local pseudo-minimum at zero but not a local minimum at 0.

The fact that we can reach a local pseudo-minimum can be shown with the help of the following lemma, already proven in
[

(
[
6
]

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a continuous function on
\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)

(U,V)
be a point that is not a local pseudo-minimum of

\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)
linking

\mathcal{W}(U,V)
.

2.68

2.78
), (
2.79
) and (

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,l=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{G}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{G}}_{2}}|Q_{N}K_{\tau}\chi_{L}^{\otimes N}\Psi_{k}\rangle(G_{\tau}^{kl}-g_{\tau}^{kl})\langle Q_{N}K_{\tau}W_{\tau}\Psi_{l}|

In order to prove Proposition

1.23

We treat both the
(SR)
and
(NR)
cases. We could not adapt the analysis of
[

and

in the
(NR)

In the
(SR)

[
6

cannot be adapted to this weaker regularity.
Moreover, in this case, it is not even clear whether the ground states are in

1.8

may contain functions that are not in

[

Appendix A
Some properties of the semirelativistic kinetic energy operator

In this appendix, we give some properties of the semirelativistic kinetic energy operator
T:=\sqrt{1-\Delta}-1
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, as defined in (
1.3
). Since it is a pseudodifferential and not a differential operator, it is not as easy to deal with as the Laplace operator. One reason is that it is not a local operator. The following lemma enables us to control this nonlocality.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
R>0
. Then, for all
f,g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
such that
\mathrm{dist}(\operatorname{supp}f,\operatorname{supp}g)\geqslant R
,

(A.1)

\langle f,Tg\rangle=-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{K_{2}(|x-y|)}{|x-y|^{2}}\overline{f(x)}g(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

where
K_{2}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind.
Moreover,
for all
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, the linear form
Tf\in H^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
can be extended to a bounded linear form on

\{g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\,\mathrm{dist}(\operatorname{supp}f,\operatorname{supp}g)\geqslant R\}.

Furthermore, there exists
C>0
such that, for
R
large enough, the above linear form has norm less than
Ce^{-R}\|f\|_{L^{2}}
.

Proof.

We know from
[
34
, Section 7.11]
that, for all
f
,
g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
and all
t>0
,

\langle f,e^{-tT}g\rangle=\frac{e^{t}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{t}{t^{2}+|x-y|^{2}}K_{2}\Big(\sqrt{t^{2}+|x-y|^{2}}\Big)\overline{f(x)}g(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

where the factor
e^{t}
originated from the 1 that was subtracted from the operator
\sqrt{1-\Delta}
in the definition of
T
.

If
f
and
g
have disjoint supports, we can write that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\left(\langle f,g\rangle-\langle f,e^{-tT}g\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t}\langle f,e^{-tT}g\rangle

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{-e^{t}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\operatorname{supp}g}\int_{\operatorname{supp}f}\frac{K_{2}\Big(\sqrt{t^{2}+|x-y|^{2}}\Big)}{t^{2}+|x-y|^{2}}\overline{f(x)}g(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Moreover, for all
t>0
,
x\in\operatorname{supp}f
and
y\in\operatorname{supp}g
,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{K_{2}\Big(\sqrt{t^{2}+|x-y|^{2}}\Big)}{t^{2}+|x-y|^{2}}\overline{f(x)}g(y)\right|\leqslant\frac{K_{2}(|x-y|)}{|x-y|^{2}}\left|\overline{f(x)}g(y)\right|,

because
K_{2}
is a positive and decreasing function, see
[
1
, Formula 9.6.29]
.
If
\mathrm{dist}(\operatorname{supp}f,\operatorname{supp}g)\geqslant R
, then, by Young’s inequality

(A.4)

\int_{\operatorname{supp}g}\int_{\operatorname{supp}f}\frac{K_{2}(|x-y|)}{|x-y|^{2}}\left|\overline{f(x)}g(y)\right|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\leqslant\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|g\|_{L^{2}}\int_{|x|\geqslant R}\frac{K_{2}(|x|)}{|x|^{2}}\mathrm{d}x.

But the integrand on the right hand side of (
A.4
) is continuous on the domain of integration. Moreover, as we can see for example in the result 9.7.2 of
[
1
]
,
K_{2}(x)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2x}}e^{-x}
for large
x>0
. It follows that the right hand side of (
A.4
) is finite. We can thus use the dominated convergence theorem to take the limit when
t\to 0
in (
A.2
) and find that for all such
f
and
g
,
\langle f,Tg\rangle
is well-defined and (
A.1
) holds. Moreover, if
R
is large enough then (
A.1
),(
A.4
) and the asymptotic behavior of
K_{2}(x)
for large
x
lead to

|\langle f,Tg\rangle|\leqslant Ce^{-R}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|g\|_{L^{2}},

as desired.
∎

Another difficulty with the semirelativistic kinetic energy operator is that there is no Leibniz rule. As a consequence, commutators between this operator and a multiplication operator cannot be explicitly computed. Nevertheless, we are able to bound some commutators with the following lemma.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\zeta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, with supp
\zeta\subset B_{1/4}(0)

and denote
\zeta_{R}(x):=\zeta\left(\frac{x}{R}\right)
. Then,
there exists
C>0
such that we have for all
R>1
, in operator norm,

\|[T,\zeta_{R}]\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2})}\leqslant\frac{C}{R}.

Proof.

We first consider
\psi\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. In view of the definition of
T
in (
1.3
) and of (
1.9
) we use the Fourier transformation as defined in (
1.4
). Let us denote, for all
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
\widetilde{T}(p):=\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}
. We define

\Xi_{R}:=\widetilde{T}\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R}\psi)-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\mathcal{F}({\zeta}_{R})*(\widetilde{T}\mathcal{F}(\psi)).

Then
\Xi_{R}=\mathcal{F}\big([T,\zeta_{R}]\psi\big)
so, by Plancherel identity,

(A.5)

\|[T,\zeta_{R}]\psi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}=\|\Xi_{R}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

For almost every
p
, we have

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3/2}\Xi_{R}(p)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}(p)(\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})*\mathcal{F}(\psi))(p)-\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})*(\widetilde{T}\mathcal{F}(\psi))(p)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}(p)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})(q)\ \mathcal{F}(\psi)(p-q)\mathrm{d}q-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})(q)\ \widetilde{T}(p-q)\ \mathcal{F}(\psi)(p-q)\mathrm{d}q

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})(q)\ \mathcal{F}(\psi)(p-q)\ (\widetilde{T}(p)-\widetilde{T}(p-q))\mathrm{d}q.

Therefore, since the function
\widetilde{T}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant 1,

(2\pi)^{3/2}|\Xi_{R}(p)|\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})(q)||\mathcal{F}(\psi)(p-q)||\widetilde{T}(p)-\widetilde{T}(p-q)|\mathrm{d}q\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|q||\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})(q)||\mathcal{F}(\psi)(p-q)|\mathrm{d}q

so we have

(2\pi)^{3/2}\|\Xi_{R}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\leqslant\||\cdot|\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\|\mathcal{F}(\psi)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

Since
\|\mathcal{F}(\psi)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}=\|\psi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}
, using (
A.5
) we find

\|[T,\zeta_{R}]\psi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\leqslant\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\||\cdot|\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

By density of
H^{1}
in
L^{2}
, we can extend this inequality to all
\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, which implies that

(A.6)

\|[T,\zeta_{R}]\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2})}\leqslant\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\||\cdot|\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

Let us estimate
\||\cdot|\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}
.
We have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|q||\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})(q)|\mathrm{d}q

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|q|\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-{\rm i}q\cdot x}\zeta\left(\frac{x}{R}\right)\mathrm{d}x\right|\mathrm{d}q=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left|\frac{p}{R}\right|\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-{\rm i}p\cdot y}\zeta(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|\mathrm{d}p,

where we have performed the changes of variable
y=\frac{x}{R}
and
p=Rq
. We find that the norm is equal to
R^{-1}(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|p|\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}e^{-{\rm i}p\cdot y}\zeta(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|\mathrm{d}p
. Since
\zeta
is a Schwartz function, we know that this integral is finite. As a consequence,

(A.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|q||\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{R})(q)|\mathrm{d}q\leqslant\frac{C}{R},

which together with (
A.6
) concludes the proof.
∎

One consequence of this result is that we can get a result analogous to what is called IMS localization formula in the non-relativistic case, see
e.g.

[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Recall that
\chi_{1}
was defined in (
1.38
).
Let us define
v_{1}:=\chi_{1}
,
v_{2}:=v_{1}(\cdot-e_{1})
and
v_{0}:=1-v_{1}-v_{2}
. We define too
V:=\sqrt{v_{0}^{2}+v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}}
, which by construction satisfies
V\geqslant 1/\sqrt{2}
. For
R>0
and
i=0,1,2
, we define the functions

(A.8)

J_{i,R}(x):=\frac{v_{i}(x/R)}{V(x/R)}.

We have that

\sum_{i=0}^{2}J_{i,R}(x)^{2}=1

for all
x
and
R
.

We define the
one-particle localization error
for
\psi\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
by

\text{Err}[\psi]:=\langle\psi,T\psi\rangle-\sum_{i=0}^{2}\langle J_{i,R}\psi,TJ_{i,R}\psi\rangle.

Lemma A.3
.

There exists
C>0
such that, for all
R>0
and
\psi\in H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
,

\left|\mathrm{Err}[\psi]\right|\leqslant\frac{C}{R}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}^{2}.

Proof.

We remark that
\langle\psi,T\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\langle\psi,TJ_{i,R}^{2}\psi\rangle
so

|\text{Err}[\psi]|\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{2}|\langle\psi,[T,J_{i,R}]J_{i,R}\psi\rangle|\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{2}\|[T,J_{i,R}]\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2})}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}^{2}

since
\|J_{i,R}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant 1
for all
i
and
R
.
For
i=1
or 2, the function
J_{i,R}
satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
A.2
so
\|[T,J_{i,R}]\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2})}\leqslant C/R
. Concerning the term
J_{0,R}
, we use the fact that
[T,J_{0,R}]=-[T,1-J_{0,R}]
. But
1-J_{0,R}
is compactly supported. Thus,
1-J_{0,R}
satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma
A.2
, and we can conclude with the same argument.
∎

This result can be extended to the case of
N
electrons.
Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{N})\in\{0,1,2\}^{N}

We define

(A.9)

where

was defined in (

We define the

Proposition A.4
.

There exists
C>0
such that, for all
R>0
and
\psi\in H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3N})
,

(A.10)

|\mathrm{Err}[\psi]|\leqslant\frac{C}{R}\|\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3N})}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one-particle case.
∎

Remark A.5
.

As explained in
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
, it is even possible to have a control of the localization error by
C/R^{2}
. Nevertheless, we do not need it in our work and it requires a longer proof.

The next lemma states that, even if the commutator is not local, we are able to control this nonlocality in the sense that, if we apply it to a function which has some decay at infinity, then we obtain something which is decaying, at least in a quadratic form sense.

Lemma A.6
.

Let us consider, for
R>0
, the function
\chi_{R}
defined in (
1.38
). Then there exists
C>0
such that,

, all

such that

,

|\langle\eta,[T,\chi_{R}]\xi\rangle|\leqslant C\max\left\{\frac{1}{R}\sup_{r\in[R/20,+\infty)}\frac{1}{F(r)},e^{-3R/80}\right\}\|F(|\cdot|)\eta\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\|\xi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

Remark A.7
.

Applying Lemma
A.6
for

,

we find

(A.11)

|\langle\eta,[T,\chi_{R}]\xi\rangle|\leqslant Ce^{-\min(a/20,3/80)R}\|e^{a|\cdot|}\eta\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\|\xi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

We introduce the function

On the one hand,

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{R}\left\|\frac{1}{F(|\cdot|)}\chi_{R/2}^{c}\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\|F(|\cdot|)\eta\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\|\xi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}

R
large enough, if
x_{i}
is in the support of
v_{0}
, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}
such that, for all
j
,
|x_{i}-y_{j}|\geqslant C^{\prime}R
. As a consequence, there exists
C>0
such that

(B.10)

\langle J_{\beta,R}\psi_{n},(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})-H_{\beta}(Y,\mathcal{Z}))J_{\beta,R}\psi_{n}\rangle\geqslant-\frac{C}{R}.

Let
K=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}
, namely
K
describes the number of electrons that are close to the origin. We observe that

J_{\beta,R}\psi_{n}
is antisymmetric with respect to the
K
variables
x_{i}
for which
\beta_{i}=1
. As a consequence,

(B.11)

\langle J_{\beta,R}\psi_{n},H_{\beta}(Y,\mathcal{Z})J_{\beta,R}\psi_{n}\rangle\geqslant E_{K}\|J_{\beta,R}\psi_{n}\|^{2}\geqslant E_{N-1}\|J_{\beta,R}\psi_{n}\|^{2}

since, by (
B.1
), the sequence
(E_{k})
is nonincreasing.
Inserting (
B.10
) and (
B.11
) into (
B.9
) and combining it with (
B.7
) and (
B.8
), we obtain that

\langle\psi_{n},H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})\psi_{n}\rangle\geqslant E_{N}\|J_{B,R}\psi_{n}\|^{2}+E_{N-1}\sum_{\beta\neq B}\|J_{\beta,R}\psi_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{C}{R},

and, since
\sum_{\beta\neq B}J_{\beta,R}^{2}=1-J_{B,R}^{2}
and
\|\psi_{n}\|=1
, we find that

\langle\psi_{n},H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})\psi_{n}\rangle\geqslant E_{N-1}+(E_{N}-E_{N-1})\|J_{B,R}\psi_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{C}{R}.

We will now prove that, for all
R>0
, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|J_{B,R}\psi_{n}\|=0

and this will conclude the proof of (
B.6
) and therefore of Theorem
1.1
.
This comes from the fact that, for
c>0
large, we have the following decomposition:

J_{B,R}\psi_{n}=\underbrace{J_{B,R}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}+c\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}}_{\text{compact operator }}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}+c\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+c)^{-\frac{1}{2}}}_{\text{ bounded operator }}\underbrace{(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+c)^{-\frac{1}{2}}}_{\text{ bounded}}\underbrace{(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+c)\psi_{n}}_{\rightharpoonup 0}.

The weak convergence
(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+c)\psi_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
follows from the decomposition

H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+c=(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})-\lambda)+(\lambda+c).

We know from the definition of
(\psi_{n})
that
(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})-\lambda)\psi_{n}\to 0
and that
(\lambda+c)\psi_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
. The operator
(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+c)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is bounded since
H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+c
is strictly positive for
c
large enough. The boundedness of

(\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}+c)^{\frac{1}{2}}(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+c)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
follows from (
1.8
). Finally, the operator
J_{B,R}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}+c\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is compact, since it is the norm limit of the sequence of Hilbert-Schmidt operators
J_{B,R}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}+c\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}+c<n\}}
. We thus obtain that

\|J_{B,R}\psi_{n}\|\rightarrow 0
, concluding the proof of the theorem.
∎

B.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We prove in the following exponential decay of approximate eigenfunctions for the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{N}
. The proof closely follows the proof of Theorem 2.1 in
[
11
]
.

Let
(\gamma_{s})
be as in the statement of Theorem
1.2
. For
\nu\geqslant 0
and
\epsilon\geqslant 0
,
we set for
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}

G_{\nu,\epsilon}(r):=\frac{\nu r}{1+\epsilon r}.

We then define on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3N}
the function
F_{\nu,\epsilon}
by

F_{\nu,\epsilon}(x_{1},...,x_{N}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{\nu,\epsilon}(|x_{i}|).

Moreover, for the function
\chi_{R}
defined in (
1.38
), we introduce

(B.12)

\xi_{\varepsilon}:=(1-\chi_{R}^{\otimes N})e^{F_{\nu,\varepsilon}}.

By the definition of
\widetilde{\Sigma}
in (
1.16
), there exists a function
\theta(R)
with
\lim_{R\rightarrow+\infty}\theta(R)=0
such that for all
s\in\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\Sigma}-\mu-\theta(R))\;\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s}\|^{2}\leqslant\langle\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s},(H_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z})-\mu)\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s}\rangle=\mathrm{Re}\langle\gamma_{s},\xi_{\varepsilon}^{2}\Gamma_{s}\rangle+\mathrm{Re}\left\langle\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s},\sum_{i=1}^{N}[T_{i},\xi_{\varepsilon}]\gamma_{s}\right\rangle,

where in the last step we used (
1.17
) and that
\xi_{\epsilon}
commutes with potentials.
Using the assumption that, for all
s\in\mathcal{I}
,
\|e^{a|\,\cdot\,|}\Gamma_{s}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})}\leqslant C_{1}
, we can bound, for all
R
,
\epsilon
,
\nu<\frac{a}{2\sqrt{N}}
and
s
, the first term on the right-hand side and find

(B.13)

(\widetilde{\Sigma}-\mu-\theta(R))\;\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s}\|^{2}\leqslant C_{1}\|\gamma_{s}\|+\mathrm{Re}\left\langle\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s},\sum_{i=1}^{N}[T_{i},\xi_{\varepsilon}]\gamma_{s}\right\rangle.

By
[
11
, Lemma C.10, Remark C.7]
, we know that for all
\nu\in(0,1)
, there exist
C_{\nu}>0
such that, for all
R
,

(B.14)

\left|\mathrm{Re}\left\langle\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s},\sum_{i=1}^{N}[T_{i},\xi_{\varepsilon}]\gamma_{s}\right\rangle\right|\leqslant NC_{\nu}\left(\frac{L_{\chi}}{R}+\nu\right)^{2}\left\|e^{F_{\nu,\varepsilon}}\gamma_{s}\right\|^{2},

where
L_{\chi}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\chi
, recall (
1.38
). Moreover,
C_{\nu}
goes to a finite value when
\nu
goes to 0.
Let us introduce the abbreviation
\delta:=NC_{\nu}\left(\frac{L_{\chi}}{R}+\nu\right)^{2}
.
Plugging (
B.14
) into (
B.13
) yields, in view of (
B.12
),

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\Sigma}-\mu-\theta(R))\;\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\|\gamma_{s}\|+\delta\left\|e^{F_{\nu,\varepsilon}}\gamma_{s}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\|\gamma_{s}\|+2\delta\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s}\|^{2}+2\delta\left\|\chi_{R}^{\otimes N}e^{F_{\nu,\varepsilon}}\gamma_{s}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\|\gamma_{s}\|+2\delta\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s}\|^{2}+2\delta e^{N\nu R/4}\|\gamma_{s}\|^{2},

where in the last line we used that, for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3N}
,

(B.15)

\chi_{R}^{\otimes N}(x)e^{F_{\nu,\varepsilon}(x)}\leqslant e^{N\nu R/8}.

We have therefore obtained

(B.16)

(\widetilde{\Sigma}-\mu-\theta(R)-2\delta)\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s}\|^{2}\leqslant C_{1}\|\gamma_{s}\|+2\delta e^{N\nu R/4}\|\gamma_{s}\|^{2}.

But

\lim_{R\to\infty}\theta(R)+2\delta=2NC_{\nu}\nu^{2},

so, for all
\nu
such that
2NC_{\nu}\nu^{2}<\widetilde{\Sigma}-\mu
, we can find a radius
R
such that

g:=(\widetilde{\Sigma}-\mu-\theta(R)-2\delta)>0,

and together with (
B.16
) we obtain:

g\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{s}\|^{2}<C_{1}\|\gamma_{s}\|+2\delta e^{N\nu R/4}\|\gamma_{s}\|^{2}\leqslant C_{R},

where in the last step we used the uniform boundedness of
\|\gamma_{s}\|
, see (
1.17
). The constant
C_{R}>0
does not depend on
\varepsilon
.
Since
\xi_{\varepsilon}
converges monotonically to
(1-\chi_{R}^{\otimes N})e^{\nu|x|}
for
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the monotone convergence theorem for the previous inequality gives that for all
s
and
\nu
, we have
\|(1-\chi_{R}^{\otimes N})e^{\nu|x|}\gamma_{s}\|^{2}\leqslant g^{-1}C_{R}
, which together with (
B.15
) concludes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
∎

B.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

We begin by showing that there exists at least one eigenvalue below
\sigma_{\text{ess}}(\hat{H}_{N}(Y,\mathcal{Z}))
. As
e.g.
in
[
11
, Appendix B]
, we prove it by induction on the number of electrons
N
.

Let us first consider the case
N=1
. Here, we do not need to take symmetry into account. Let
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
be such that
\|f\|_{L^{2}}=1
and
\operatorname{supp}f\subset\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}:\frac{5}{4}\leqslant|x|\leqslant\frac{7}{4}\}
. For
R>0
, we define

f_{R}(x):=\frac{1}{R^{\frac{3}{2}}}f\left(\frac{x}{R}\right).

Then, for all
R>0
,
\|f_{R}\|=1
and

\displaystyle\langle f_{R},H_{1}(Y,\mathcal{Z})f_{R}\rangle=

\displaystyle\left\langle f_{R},\left(T_{1}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{Z_{j}\alpha}{|x_{1}-y_{j}|}\right)f_{R}\right\rangle+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant M}\frac{Z_{i}Z_{j}\alpha}{|y_{i}-y_{j}|}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left\langle f_{R},\left(-\Delta_{1}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{Z_{j}\alpha}{|x-y_{j}|}\right)f_{R}\right\rangle+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant M}\frac{Z_{i}Z_{j}\alpha}{|y_{i}-y_{j}|}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R^{2}}\langle f,-\Delta_{1}f\rangle-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left\langle f,\frac{Z_{j}\alpha}{|Rx_{1}-y_{j}|}f\right\rangle+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant M}\frac{Z_{i}Z_{j}\alpha}{|y_{i}-y_{j}|},

where we have used that
T_{1}\leqslant-\Delta_{1}
and a change of variables in the second and third line, respectively.
With a Taylor expansion, we see that the second term decays as
-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha Z_{j}/R
when
R
becomes large. This proves that, for
R
large enough,

\langle f_{R},H_{1}(Y,\mathcal{Z})f_{R}\rangle<\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant M}\frac{Z_{i}Z_{j}\alpha}{|y_{i}-y_{j}|}=\inf\sigma_{\text{ess}}(H_{1}(Y,\mathcal{Z})),

implying that
\hat{H}_{1}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
has at least one eigenvalue below the infimum of its essential spectrum.

We consider now the induction step. Let us assume, for some
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
N<|Z|+1
,

E_{N-1}:=\inf\sigma(\hat{H}_{N-1}(Y,\mathcal{Z}))<\inf\sigma_{\text{ess}}(\hat{H}_{N-1}(Y,\mathcal{Z})),

which implies that there exists a ground state
\Psi_{N-1}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{H}_{N-1}(Y,\mathcal{Z}))
with
\|\psi_{N-1}\|=1
such that

H_{N-1}(Y,\mathcal{Z})\Psi_{N-1}=E_{N-1}\Psi_{N-1}
.
By the assumption of Theorem
1.4
,
|\mathcal{Z}|>N-1
so we can choose and fix
\delta>0
with

(B.17)

\delta<\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|+1-N}{2(N-1+|\mathcal{Z}|)}.

For
R
large enough,
\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}
is not identically 0, where
\chi_{\delta R}
was defined in (
1.38
).
Thus, we can define the sequence of trial functions:

(B.18)

\Phi_{N,R}:=\frac{Q_{N}((\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1})\otimes f_{R})}{\|Q_{N}((\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1})\otimes f_{R})\|}.

For all
R
, we have
\Phi_{N,R}\in H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3N})\cap\operatorname{Ran}Q_{N}
.

Arguing as in the proof of (
2.15
), we find that

(B.19)

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{N,R},(\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1})\Phi_{N,R}\rangle=\frac{\langle(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1})\otimes f_{R},(H_{N}-E_{N-1})(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1})\otimes f_{R}\rangle}{\|(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1})\otimes f_{R}\|^{2}}.

Let us first estimate the denominator of the right-hand side of (
B.19
). Since, by Corollary
1.3
,
\Psi_{N-1}
is exponentially decaying and we have chosen
\Psi_{N-1}
and
f_{R}
with norm 1, we find that

(B.20)

\|(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1})\otimes f_{R}\|=1+O(e^{-cR}).

To estimate the numerator of the right-hand side of (
B.19
), let us split

(B.21)

H_{N}-E_{N-1}=H_{N}-H_{N-1}+H_{N-1}-E_{N-1}.

Using that
(H_{N-1}-E_{N-1})
does not act on the coordinate
x_{N}
and that
(H_{N-1}-E_{N-1})\Psi_{N-1}=0
, we find

\displaystyle\langle(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}

\displaystyle\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R}),(H_{N-1}-E_{N-1})(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R})\rangle=\Big\langle\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1},\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}[T_{j},\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}]\Psi_{N-1}\Big\rangle.

As a consequence, since, by Corollary
1.3
,
\Psi_{N-1}
is exponentially decaying, we see, using (
A.11
), that

(B.22)

\langle(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R}),(H_{N-1}-E_{N-1})(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R})\rangle=O(e^{-dR}).

On the other hand, using
T\leqslant\frac{-\Delta}{2}
, we find

(B.23)

\langle(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R}),(H_{N}-H_{N-1})(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R})\rangle\\
\leqslant\left\langle\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R},\left(-\frac{\Delta_{N}}{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{Z_{j}\alpha}{|x_{N}-y_{j}|}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{\alpha}{|x_{N}-x_{j}|}\right)(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R})\right\rangle.

Assume that
R
is large enough such that for all
j
,
|y_{j}|\leqslant\delta R
. For
x_{N}
in the support of
f_{R}
, we have that
|x_{N}|=(1+\theta)R
, for some
\theta\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4}]
. Therefore,

(B.24)

\frac{1}{|x_{N}-y_{j}|}\geqslant\frac{1}{|x_{N}|+|y_{j}|}\geqslant\frac{1}{(1+\theta+\delta)R}.

Similarly, for
(x_{1},...,x_{N-1})
in the support of
\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}
, we have
|x_{j}|<\delta R
for all
j=1,...,N-1
. Therefore,

(B.25)

\frac{1}{|x_{N}-x_{j}|}\leqslant\frac{1}{|x_{N}|-|x_{j}|}\leqslant\frac{1}{(1+\theta-\delta)R}.

Using (
B.24
) and (
B.25
) and recalling (
B.18
) we find that, on
\operatorname{supp}\Phi_{N,R}
,

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{Z_{j}}{|x_{N}-y_{j}|}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{|x_{N}-x_{j}|}\leqslant\frac{(1+\theta)(N-1-|\mathcal{Z}|)+\delta(N-1+|\mathcal{Z}|)}{(1+\theta+\delta)(1+\theta-\delta)R}.

As a consequence, using (
B.17
), we find, on
\operatorname{supp}\Phi_{N,R}
,

(B.26)

-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{j}}{|x_{N}-y_{j}|}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{|x_{N}-x_{j}|}\leqslant\frac{(\frac{1}{2}+\theta)(N-1-|\mathcal{Z}|)}{(1+\theta+\delta)(1+\theta-\delta)R}\leqslant\frac{N-1-|\mathcal{Z}|}{8R},

where in the last step we used that
\frac{1}{2}+\theta\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
and that

(1+\theta+\delta)(1+\theta-\delta)\leqslant 4
for all
\theta\in[1/4,3/4]
as well as the assumed inequality
N-1-|\mathcal{Z}|<0
.

Inserting first
\langle f_{R},(-\Delta_{x})f_{R}\rangle=\frac{1}{R^{2}}\langle f_{1},(-\Delta_{x})f_{1}\rangle

and (
B.26
) into (
B.23
) and then combining it with (
B.22
) and (
B.21
), we find that

(B.27)

\langle(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R}),(H_{N}-E_{N-1})(\chi_{\delta R}^{\otimes N-1}\Psi_{N-1}\otimes f_{R})\rangle\leqslant-\frac{\alpha(|\mathcal{Z}|+1-N)}{8R}+\frac{D_{1}}{R^{2}},

for some constant
D_{1}>0
.

Using (
B.18
), (
B.20
) and (
B.27
), we find that there exists
C>0
such that, for
R
large enough,

(B.28)

\langle\Phi_{N,R},(\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1})\Phi_{N,R}\rangle\leqslant-\frac{C}{R}+\frac{D_{1}}{R^{2}},

which implies that
\hat{H}_{N}
has an eigenvalue below its essential spectrum since
E_{N-1}=\inf\sigma_{ess}(\hat{H}_{N})
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We will prove that
\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1}
has at least
n
negative eigenvalues. As far as we know, this part of the proof is new, even for atoms, since
[
11
]
does not consider this question. Let us consider a normed linear combination of
\Phi_{N,2^{k}}
’s with
k
between
2n+1
and
3n
:

(B.29)

\Xi:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}},\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|^{2}=1.

Then,

(B.30)

\langle\Xi,(\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1})\Xi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|^{2}\langle\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}},(\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1})\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}}\rangle\\
+\sum_{i\neq j}\overline{c_{i}}c_{j}\langle\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}},(\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1})\Phi_{N,2^{2n+j}}\rangle.

Using (
B.28
), we find that, if
n
is large enough,

(B.31)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|^{2}\langle\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}},(\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1})\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}}\rangle\leqslant-\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|^{2}\frac{C}{2^{2n+i}}+\frac{D_{1}}{4^{2n}}\leqslant-\frac{C}{8^{n}}+\frac{D_{1}}{16^{n}}.

For the terms where
i\neq j
, we know that the functions
\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}}
and
\Phi_{N,2^{2n+j}}
have disjoint supports in the
N
-th variable. As a consequence, the only parts which are not 0 are of the form

\langle\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}},T_{N}\Phi_{N,2^{2n+j}}\rangle=\langle f_{2^{2n+i}},Tf_{2^{2n+j}}\rangle.

Since the supports of
f_{2^{2n+i}}
and
f_{2^{2n+j}}
have distance at least
2^{2n}
, we get from Lemma
A.1
that there exists
C>0
such that

(B.32)

\langle\Phi_{N,2^{2n+i}},T_{N}\Phi_{N,2^{2n+j}}\rangle\leqslant Ce^{-2^{2n}}.

Inserting (
B.31
) and (
B.32
) into (
B.30
), we get that

\langle\Xi,(\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1})\Xi\rangle<0

for all such
\Xi
in the form (
B.29
), given that
n
is large enough.

The linear space spanned by the
\phi_{N,2^{k}}
for
k
between
2n+1
and
3n
is thus an
n
-dimensional space on which the quadratic form associated with
\hat{H}_{N}-E_{N-1}
is negative. Therefore,
\hat{H}_{N}
has at least
n
eigenvalues below its essential spectrum. Since this is true for arbitrarily large
n
, there are infinitely many eigenvalues below the bottom of the essential spectrum.
∎
