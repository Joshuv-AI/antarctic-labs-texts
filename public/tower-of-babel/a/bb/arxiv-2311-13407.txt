Abstract
. We study the limiting behavior of random lozenge tilings of the hexagon with a q q -Racah weight as the size of the hexagon grows large. Based on the asymptotic behavior of the recurrence coefficients of the q q -Racah polynomials, we give a new proof for the fact that the height function for a random tiling concentrates near a deterministic limit shape and that the global fluctuations are described by the Gaussian Free Field. These results were recently proved using (dynamic) loop equation techniques. In this paper, we extend the recurrence coefficient approach that was developed for (dynamic) orthogonal polynomial ensembles to the setting of q q -orthogonal polynomials. An interesting feature is that the complex structure is easily found from the limiting behavior of the (explicitly known) recurrence coefficients. A particular motivation for studying this model is that the variational characterization of the limiting height function has an inhomogeneous term. The study of the regularity properties of the minimizer for general variational problems with such inhomogeneous terms is a challenging open problem. In a general setup, we show that the variational problem gives rise to a natural complex structure associated with the same Beltrami equation as in the homogeneous situation. We also derive a relation between the complex structure and the complex slope. In the case of the q q -Racah weighting of lozenge tilings of the hexagon, our representation of the limit shape and their fluctuations in terms of the recurrence coefficients allows us to verify this relation explicitly.

Introduction

Random tilings of planar domains form a rich class of models that have been studied intensively in the past decades. Different techniques have been developed to study their asymptotic behavior when the domain grows large (or the mesh becomes small). We refer to
[
26
]
for a recent treatise on the subject with many references. In certain special examples, remarkable connections with orthogonal polynomials have been found. For example, the correlation function for uniformly distributed lozenge tilings of the hexagon can be expressed in terms of Hahn polynomials
[
27

31
]
. Uniformly distributed lozenge tilings of the Aztec diamond are related to Krawtchouk polynomials
[
32
]
. This connection bridges important models from statistical mechanics to classical objects of analysis. It also sets the stage for proving universality conjectures for these stochastic models, as these typically boil down to an asymptotic analysis (as the degree tends to infinity) for the orthogonal polynomials and their properties. An important example for this paper is the method developed in
[
12
,
25
]
for proving Gaussian Free Field fluctuations via the recurrence coefficients of the polynomials. The purpose of this writing is to extend this approach to more general classes of models that include
q
-orthogonal polynomials. The running example will be the probability measure on lozenge tilings of the hexagon introduced in
[
7
]
related to
q
-Racah polynomials, which we will first discuss.

Large hexagons

A tiling of the hexagon by lozenges can be viewed three-dimensional as cubes (or boxes) stacked in the corner of a room. This also allows us to view a random tiling as a random surface. We will be interested in the asymptotic behavior of this random surface when the size of the hexagon grows large. More precisely, for
b,c\in\mathbb{R_{+}},n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
q>0
or
q
on the complex unit circle, we set

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}=n,\quad\mathbf{b}=nb,\quad\mathbf{c}=nc,\quad\mathbf{q}=q^{\frac{1}{n}}.

and study the limit
n\to\infty
. Note that this means that we take
\mathbf{q}\to 1
simultaneously as the size of the hexagon goes to infinity.
In Figure
2
(also later in Figure
6
), we have plotted a sample of a random lozenge tiling for some special choice of parameters. The Figure(s) illustrates a common picture in tiling models: the randomness is contained in a region called the liquid region, but the corners are frozen. Moreover, a limit shape seems to appear for the random surface.

To make this more concrete, we define the height function
h_{n}
as

(4)

h_{n}(t,x)=\#\left\{j\mid y_{j}(\lfloor nt\rfloor)\leq nx\right\},

where
t
and

are rescaled variables inside the rescaled hexagon. In other words,

counts the number of the white points below and including the point
(\lfloor nt\rfloor,nx)
in Figure
1

n\to\infty

(5)

\frac{1}{n}h_{n}(t,x)\to h(t,x),

almost surely. The graph of

is the limit shape near which the random tilings concentrate. The limit shape

was rigorously computed using loop equations in
[
22
]
. Moreover, for fixed

, they also proved the fluctuations of

along vertical one-dimensional sections. Recently, the full two-dimensional fluctuation field was proved to be described by the Gaussian Free Field (GFF)
[
29
]
using time-dependent extensions of loop equation techniques. More precisely, after a change of coordinate, they prove that
h_{n}-\mathbb{E}h_{n}
converges to the Gaussian Free Field in the upper half-plane.

One of the main motivations of the current writing is to show that we can derive the limit shape and the Gaussian Free Field fluctuations by extending established principles for orthogonal polynomials in
[
12
,
25
,
38
]
to the
q
-orthogonal setting. More precisely, we will show that the limit shape and its fluctuations can be computed from the recurrence coefficients for the
q
-Racah polynomials. In our opinion, this is a rather direct way of studying the particular tiling model that fully exploits its integrable structure. Moreover, the conformal structure, particularly the diffeomorphism describing the pullback in the Gaussian Free Field for the height fluctuations, admits a very simple expression that can simply be read off from the asymptotic behavior of the recurrence coefficients.

Linear statistics and recurrence coefficients

The probability measure in (
2
) together with (
3
) is a special case of a more general class of models, and we will prove various results on their asymptotic behavior based on general principles. Indeed, we study point processes on
\mathbb{Z}
where one considers
n\in\mathbb{N}

with a joint probability distribution function proportional to

(6)

\sim\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\nu(y_{i})-\nu(y_{j}))^{2}w(y_{j}),

where
\nu:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly monotone and
w:\mathbb{Z}\to[0,\infty)
such that

(7)

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}|\nu(y)|^{k}w(y)<\infty,

for all

. If

has a finite support, then (
7
) is redundant. But in the case of infinite support, it ensures that all moments of

exist and, in particular, (
6
) can be normalized to a probability measure. Also, if
w
has a finite support, we need at least
n
points in that support for (
6

6

In case
\nu(y)=y
, (
6

[
39
]
associated with

. Such ensembles appear naturally in various contexts, and their name is explained by the fact that various quantities, such as the correlation functions, can be expressed in terms of the orthogonal polynomials with respect to the weight
w
. The process defined by (
6
) for general
\nu
can also be analyzed using extensions of these orthogonal polynomials, such as
q
-analogues. Let

be the unique polynomial of degree

and positive leading coefficient such that

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}p_{l}(\nu(y))p_{k}(\nu(y))w(y)=\delta_{l,k},

for
l,k=0,1,2\ldots
. We also set
r_{k}(y)=p_{k}(\nu(y))
, which is a polynomial in
\nu
. For
\nu(y)=y
, the polynomials

are the orthogonal polynomials with respect to

. For
\nu
and

), the polynomials are the

-Racah polynomials. From the orthogonality relation one can show that the polynomials

satisfy a three-term recurrence. That is, there exists two sequences
\{a_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
and
\{b_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
with

and

such that

(9)

\displaystyle\nu(y)r_{j}(y)=a_{j}r_{j+1}(y)+b_{j}r_{j}(y)+a_{j-1}r_{j-1}(y),

for

(where we also set

and

Variational problem

The weight (
1
)
falls outside the class of uniform or periodic weights but still has an elegant integrable structure. This makes it an attractive model to test extensions of established results for the uniform or periodic weights. For instance, for uniformly distributed random tilings, and more generally for tilings with periodic weight structure, there have been important works on characterizing the limit shape in terms of a variational problem, in particular
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
34
,
35
,
37
]
. Furthermore, the regularity of the minimizers for lozenge tilings, as well as general dimer models with periodic weight structure, has been classified in
[
3
]
. In addition,
[
3
]
shows that the
liquid regions
are endowed with a unique complex structure. It is a conjecture of Kenyon and Okounkov that the global fluctuations around the limit shape in the liquid region are governed by the Gaussian Free Field (which is proved for the uniform distribution on the hexagon in
[
25
,
42
]
). However, to observe the Gaussian Free Field, one must first identify the correct complex structure and make a change of coordinates by solving a Beltrami equation, typically pulling back the liquid region by a diffeomorphism to the unit disc in the case when the liquid region is a simply connected domain.

By including the parameters
q
and
\kappa
, one breaks the uniformity, and the complexity increases significantly. As conjectured in
[
7
]
, the limit shape is still characterized by a variational problem, and this is proven in
[
40
]
. The difference with the uniform measure is that the energy function now includes an additional linear term. A careful treatment of such a variational problem and a study of the regularity of the minimizer is an open problem that is significantly more complicated than the one for the uniform measure. We will discuss a general setup for this in Section
5
and will extend certain results in
[
3
]
. In particular, in a joint work with Astala, we show how the inhomogeneous Euler-Lagrange equation can be reduced to a first-order quasilinear inhomogeneous Beltrami equation. We will also identify the naturally associated complex structure and change of variables associated with the variational problem. In addition, we show how the complex structure can be expressed by the complex slope solving a complex Burger equation, connecting the complex structure to the results in
[
22
,
29
]
. That the complex structure associated with the variational problem is the same as the one giving the Gaussian Free Field for the regular hexagon is then verified in Section
6
, and we conjecture that they are always the same.

In greater detail, in Section
6
, we return to the variational problem for weight (
1
) for the hexagon. We recall that the diffeomorphism that gives the correct coordinates for the Gaussian Free Field fluctuation is a very simple function of the limiting behavior of the recurrence coefficients. The limit shape is computed using (
12
), but from the principles for the variational problem, it should also be computable directly from the diffeomorphism giving the Gaussian Free Field. We verify that both ways indeed provide the same result (as it should). Note that this is somewhat remarkable. Indeed, the diffeomorphism only depends on the limiting behavior of the recurrence coefficient
a_{j,n}
with
j/n\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, where the formula (
12
) depends on all
a_{j,n}
with
j/n\to\xi\in(0,1)
. We believe that the reason for this is that the recurrence coefficients for the
q
-Racah polynomials themselves satisfy difference equations (in both degree and time). It would be very interesting to see if these difference equations can be used to derive the variational problem.

Overview of the paper

In Section
2
, we state our main results for the asymptotic behavior of linear statistics for point processes of the type (
6
) and extensions. A Law of Large Numbers is formulated in Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
. Then, we state a CLT for the linear statistics in Theorem
2.4
and a time-dependent extension in Theorem
2.5
. It is imperative to underscore that the asymptotic behavior of the recurrence coefficients inherently drives all these theorems. The proofs of these theorems are presented in Section
4
, but we discuss the example of the
q
-Racah ensembles first in Section
3
. Our results include the real, imaginary, and trigonometric cases. The first two cases are dealt with simultaneously in Theorem
3.1
. The trigonometric cases require some special attention, and the limit density is computed separately in Theorem
3.2
. Then, in Section
5
, we discuss the variational problem for the tilings of the hexagon and propose a road map for studying the regularity properties. Finally, in Section
6
, we compute the limiting behavior of the height function for the lozenge tilings of the hexagon with weight (
1
). In particular, we compute the limiting height function and the Gaussian Free Field fluctuations. Then, we verify the relation between the complex slope and complex structure as predicted in Section
5
.

2.
Limit shapes and fluctuations for
q
-Orthogonal Polynomial Ensembles

In this section, we discuss general
q
-orthogonal polynomial ensembles and present our main results on the limiting behavior of their linear statistics.

2.1.
Scaled point process and large
n
behavior

We will be interested in the large
n
behavior of the point process defined by (
6
). To achieve a meaningful limit, we will allow the parameters in the model to depend on
n
. The assumptions we will pose imply that the point process will essentially have a global scale of order
\sim n
and it does make sense to rescale the process by
n
. That is, set
y=xn
and consider the point process on
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}
with the probability distribution function proportional to

(13)

\displaystyle\sim\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(\nu_{n}(nx_{i})-\nu_{n}(nx_{j})\right)^{2}w_{n}(nx_{1})\dots w_{n}(nx_{n}).

Note that we allow that
\nu=\nu_{n}
and
w=w_{n}
vary with
n
as
n\to\infty
, which also implies that
a_{j}=a_{j,n}
and
b_{j}=b_{j,n}
both depend on
n
. In fact, after setting

\mu_{n}(x)=\nu_{n}(nx),\qquad x\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}

we will assume that there exists a function
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
such that

(14)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(x)=\mu(x).

The precise mode of convergence in (
14
) will be specified in our main theorems. In a nutshell, for the limiting behavior of the empirical measure, we will require uniform convergence, but for the fluctuations, we will need a stronger Lipschitz condition.

We study the large
n
limit of the point process (
13
) by analyzing the asymptotic behavior of the linear statistics

(15)

X_{n}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(x_{j}),

for functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

2.2.
Limiting distribution of points

The first result we discuss is the fact that the linear statistic has an almost sure limit, implying that there is a deterministic density describing the limiting distribution of points as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider a discrete point process
\{x_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n-1}
with its joint distribution proportional to (
13
).
Suppose there exists real-valued continuous functions,
a(\xi),b(\xi)
, on
[0,1]
, where
a\geq 0
and
a\not\equiv 0
, such that the recurrence coefficients (
9
) associated with the orthonormal polynomials satisfy

(16)

\displaystyle\lim_{\frac{j}{n}\to\xi}a_{j,n}=a(\xi),\quad\lim_{\frac{j}{n}\to\xi}b_{j,n}=b(\xi),\quad\forall\xi\in[0,1],

where the limit
n\to\infty
is taken such that such that
j/n\to\xi
simultaneously.
Then, for any polynomial
p
we have that the linear statistics (
15
) satisfies

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}X_{n}(p(\mu_{n}))\to\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{b(\xi)-2a(\xi)}^{b(\xi)+2a(\xi)}\frac{p(x)}{\sqrt{4a(\xi)^{2}-(x-b(\xi))^{2}}}dxd\xi+\int_{a^{-1}(\{0\})}p(b(\xi))d\xi

as
n\to\infty
almost surely.

The proof of this theorem is given in Section
4
. Note that for
\mu_{n}(x)=x
, this result is already known in the literature. Indeed, in
[
38
]
, the authors prove the exact same limit result for the asymptotic zero distribution of the orthogonal polynomials. That the empirical measure for the orthogonal polynomial ensembles and the zero distribution of the
n
-orthogonal polynomial have the same limit (if it exists) is a well-known result and is, for example, discussed in
[
30
]
. The extension to general
\mu_{n}
is straightforward, but we will present a direct proof, both for completeness and because, to the best of our knowledge, the simple arguments we provide have not been published before.

Note that in Theorem
2.1
, we have not specified any condition on
\mu_{n}
. It may converge as in (
14
), but it does not have to. All that matters is that the recurrence coefficients are slowly varying. It is, however, a bit restrictive to only allow linear statistics with functions
f
that are polynomials in
\mu_{n}(x)
. It is desirable to allow for more general functions. Note that if the rescaled point process lives on a compact set (so
w
has finite support), then it is natural to extend the latter theorem to continuous functions. However, if
w
has infinite support then we need an additional condition that ensures that the points will accumulate on a compact subset (after rescaling) with high probability.

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider a discrete point process
\{x_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n-1}
with its joint distribution proportional to (
13
). Let the assumptions in Theorem
2.1
be satisfied. Assume further that there exists a bounded
K\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
with
\mu(K)
being compact such that

(1)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}\left(\left|\mu_{n}\right|^{k}\chi_{K^{c}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=O_{n}(n^{-\epsilon}),

where
\chi_{K^{c}}(x)=1
for
x\in K^{c}
and zero otherwise.

(2)

\mu_{n}
has a uniform limit
\mu
, i.e.

(19)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}\cap K}\left|\mu_{n}(x)-\mu(x)\right|=0,

and
\mu
is strictly monotone on
K
.

Then, for any
f
such that
f\circ\mu^{-1}
is continuous with at most polynomial growth at infinity, we have

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}X_{n}(f)\to\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mu^{-1}(b(\xi)-2a(\xi))}^{\mu^{-1}(b(\xi)+2a(\xi))}f(x)\frac{d\mu(x)d\xi}{\sqrt{4a(\xi)^{2}-(\mu(x)-b(\xi))^{2}}}+\int_{a^{-1}(\{0\})}f\circ\mu^{-1}(b(\xi))d\xi,

as
n\to\infty
almost surely.

Note that if the supports
S_{n}
of
x\mapsto w(xn)
are finite and contained in a compact interval, then condition (1) of Theorem
2.2
is redundant.

Although the extension to continuous functions is rather direct, the extension to piecewise continuous functions (note that the height function for the lozenge tilings, as discussed in the introduction, is a linear statistic with respect to a step function) does not hold without further conditions.

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider a discrete point process
\{x_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n-1}
with its joint distribution proportional to (
13
). Let all the assumptions in Theorem
2.2
be satisfied and
a(\xi)
and
b(\xi)
be the limiting continuous functions in (
16
). Additionally, assume that there exists a
\gamma>2
such that

(21)

\int_{0}^{1}|a(\xi)|^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}}d\xi<\infty.

Consider Riemann integrable functions
f
and
f\circ\mu^{-1}
on
K
and
\mu(K)
respectively, such that
f\circ\mu^{-1}
has at most polynomial growth at infinity. Then we have

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}X_{n}(f)\to\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mu^{-1}(b(\xi)-2a(\xi))}^{\mu^{-1}(b(\xi)+2a(\xi))}f(x)\frac{d\mu(x)d\xi}{\sqrt{4a(\xi)^{2}-(\mu(x)-b(\xi))^{2}}}

as
n\to\infty
almost surely.

The condition (
21
) implies that
a^{-1}(\{0\})
is a set of measure
0
. In many examples of interest,
a(\xi)
has at most finitely many zeros and their order is low. In such cases, condition (
21
) is satisfied. At first glance, condition (
21
) may look a bit odd, but it is the natural condition that makes the right-hand side of (
22
) a continuous linear function on the space
L^{p}(\mu(K),dx)
(up to composition with
\mu^{-1}
), as we will see in the proof.

Note that by changing the order of integration in (
20
), one obtains a formula for the limiting mean density of points. To this end, define

(23)

I(x)=\left\{\xi\in(0,1)\mid b(\xi)-2a(\xi)\leq\mu(x)\leq b(\xi)+2a(\xi)\right\}.

Then, we can rewrite the right-hand side of (
20
) as

\displaystyle\int f(x)\rho(x)dx,

where the density
\rho(x)
is defined as

(24)

\displaystyle\rho(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{I(x)}\frac{d\xi d\mu(x)}{\sqrt{4a(\xi)^{2}-(\mu(x)-b(\xi))^{2}}},

on the set

x\in\mu^{-1}\left(\min_{\xi\in[0,1]}\left(b(\xi)-2a(\xi)\right),\max_{\xi\in[0,1]}\left(b(\xi)+2a(\xi)\right)\right).

Remarkably, in various interesting cases, one can compute the integral over
\xi
and find an explicit expression for the density.

2.3.
Global fluctuations

In the next step, we study the fluctuations for the linear statistics
X_{n}(f)
around its mean
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(f)]
. In order to control the slope of the test function, we introduce the Lipschitz constant for any given function on a real domain
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
and subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}
,

(25)

\displaystyle L_{E}(f)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sup_{x\neq y\in E\cap I}\left|\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y}\right|.

If
E=I
we drop the subscript
E
, i.e.,
L(f)
. The following theorem is an extension of Theorem 2.3 in
[
12
]
. The main difference is in the presence of
\mu_{n}
. In particular, we need a non-trivial condition on the convergence of
\mu_{n}
to
\mu
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Consider a discrete point process
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
with its joint distribution proportional to (
13
).
Assume there exist a function
\mu
and a subset
K\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\mu(K)
is compact,
\sup_{x\in K}|\mu(x)|<\infty
,
L_{K}(\mu)<\infty
(as defined in (
25
)), and

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}\left(\left|\mu_{n}\right|^{k}\chi_{K^{c}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=o_{n}(n^{-1}),

as
n\to\infty
, and

(27)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}\cap K}\left|\mu_{n}(x)-\mu(x)\right|=0,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{K}(\mu\circ\mu_{n}^{-1}-id)=0,

where
id(x)=x
. Assume further that there exist two real numbers
a(1),b(1)
such that, for any fixed
j
,

(28)

\displaystyle a_{j+n,n}\to a(1),\quad b_{j+n,n}\to b(1)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Consider a test function
f
such that
f\circ\mu^{-1}
is continuously differentiable on
\mu(K)
and
f\circ\mu^{-1}
grows at most polynomially at infinity. Then, as
n\to\infty
, we have

(29)

\displaystyle X_{n}(f)-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(f)]

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{N}(0,\sum_{k\geq 1}k\left|\widehat{f\circ\mu^{-1}}_{k}\right|^{2}),

in distribution, where

(30)

\displaystyle\widehat{f\circ\mu^{-1}}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\circ\mu^{-1}(2a(1)\cos\theta+b(1))e^{-ik\theta}d\theta

and
\mu^{-1}(x)
is the inverse function of
\mu(x)
.

Note that the conditions for the Central Limit Theorem are weaker than the conditions from the Law of Large Numbers. We only require (
16
) to hold only for
\xi=1
for Theorem
2.4
and not necessarily for all
0\leq\xi\leq 1
. However, Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
need (
16
) to be satisfied for all
0\leq\xi\leq 1
.

What is different from the case of Orthogonal Polynomial Ensembles is the presence of
\mu
. Note that if
\mu
is differentiable then
f\circ\mu^{-1}\in C^{1}(I)
means that

(31)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)=\mu^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(\mu(x)),

for some
g\in C^{1}(I)
. In general, if
\mu
is differentiable with a strictly positive (or negative) derivative, then the above is valid for all
f\in C^{1}(I)
. However, if
\mu^{\prime}
vanishes at certain points, then so does
f
. It means that we are restricting our class of test functions to
C^{1}
functions whose derivative vanishes at the zeros of
\mu^{\prime}
. This will happen in some degenerate examples.

2.4.
Extended ensembles

The classical orthogonal polynomial ensembles have natural time-dependent extensions (for a general discussion on extended processes, we refer to
[
25
,
33
]
and the references therein). For such extensions, the global fluctuations can be analyzed in terms of the recurrence coefficients as above and the spectrum of the underlying generator that drives the dynamics
[
25
]
. As we will see for the tiling model of the hexagon from the Introduction, also the
q
-Racah ensembles have such a natural extension and this is why we will discuss the generalization of
[
25
]
to a class of processes that extend (
6
).

Let
N
be a fixed integer, and

t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{N}

be a real-valued sequence. Let
\mu_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
be a given real-valued function on
\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{N}\}\times\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}
and, for
m=1,2,\dots,N
, let
w_{n,t_{m}}(nx)
be an orthogonal weight on
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}
. For
m=1,\ldots,N
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
r_{k,t_{m}}(nx)
be defined as the polynomial of order
k
in
\mu_{n}(t_{m},x)
with positive leading coefficient such that

(32)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}}r_{j,t_{m}}(nx)r_{k,t_{m}}(nx)w_{n,t_{m}}(nx)=\delta_{j,k}.

To define the extended process we need transition functions

(33)

\displaystyle U_{t_{m}}(x_{i},x_{j})\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{c_{k,t_{m+1}}}{c_{k,t_{m}}}r_{k,t_{m}}(nx_{i})r_{k,t_{m+1}}(nx_{j}),

where
c_{k,t_{m}}>0
. The associated extended point process
\{(t_{m},x_{j}(t_{m}))\}_{m=1,j=0}^{N,n-1}
is then induced by the probability measure proportional to

(34)

\sim\det\left(c_{k,t_{0}}r_{k,t_{0}}(nx_{j}(t_{0}))\right)_{j,k=1}^{n}\prod_{m=0}^{N-1}\det\left(U_{t_{m}}\left(nx_{i}(t_{m}),nx_{j}(t_{m+1})\right)\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}\\
\times\det\left(\frac{r_{k,t_{N}}(nx_{j}(t_{N}))}{c_{k,t_{N}}}\right)_{j,k=0}^{n-1}\prod_{m=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{n}w_{n,t_{m}}(nx_{j}(t_{m})).

It is an interesting standard exercise, based on a repeated application of the Cauchy-Binet theorem, the orthogonality of the polynomials and the Vandermonde determinant, that the marginal densities for the point process
\{(t_{m},x_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
for fixed
m
are of the type (
6
) with
\nu_{m}
and weight
w_{n,t_{m}}
.

The linear statistics for this point process are the random variables

(35)

\displaystyle X_{n}(f)=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(t_{m},x_{j}(t_{m}))

for a test function
f
. As is the case for the one-dimensional marginals, the limiting behavior of these linear statistics can be studied using the coefficients in the recurrence relations

(36)

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(t_{m},x)r_{j,t_{m}}(nx)=a_{j,n}^{(t_{m})}r_{j+1,t_{m}}(nx)+b_{j,n}^{(t_{m})}r_{j,t_{m}}(nx)+a_{j-1,n}^{(t_{m})}r_{j-1,t_{m}}(nx),

but we will also need a condition on the limiting behavior of the coefficients
c_{k,t_{m}}
in the transition functions (
33
).

First, note that since the marginal density of (
34
) at any fixed
t_{m}
is the same as that of (
13
), Theorem
2.2
still holds for any fixed
t_{m}
and thus also gives the limiting distribution of points for the extended point process. The following is an extension of Theorem
2.4
and describes the two-dimensional fluctuations.

Theorem 2.5
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and consider a probability measure on
\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{N}\}\times\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}
of the form (

) with

t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{N}.

Suppose there exist a function
\mu
on
\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{N}\}\times\mathbb{R}
and compact sets
K^{(t_{m})}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

(1)

is compact for all

and thus, in particular,
\sup_{x\in K^{(t_{m})}}\left|\mu(t_{m},x)\right|<\infty.

(2)

we have

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}\left(\left|\mu_{n}\right|^{k}\chi_{K^{c}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=o(n^{-1}),

as

, where
K^{c}\coloneqq{K^{(t_{1})}}^{c}\times{K^{(t_{2})}}^{c}\times\dots\times{K^{(t_{N})}}^{c}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

(3)

for
m=1,\dots,N
, we have

(38)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}\cap K^{(t_{m})}}\left|\mu_{n}(t_{m},x)-\mu(t_{m},x)\right|=

\displaystyle 0,

(4)

for
m=1,\dots,N

(39)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{K^{(t_{m})}}\left(\mu(t_{m},\mu_{n}^{-1}(t_{m},\cdot))-id(\cdot)\right)=

\displaystyle 0,

where
id(x)=x

(5)

for
m=1,\dots,N
, we have

(40)

\displaystyle L_{K^{(t_{m})}}\left(\mu(t_{m},\cdot)\right)<\infty.

Suppose the recurrence coefficients (
36
) are such that, for any fixed
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
m=1,2,\dots,N
, we have

(41)

\displaystyle a_{j+n,n}^{(t_{m})}\to a(1;t_{m})\quad b_{j+n,n}^{(t_{m})}\to b(1;t_{m})\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

for some

and

(42)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c_{n+k,t_{m}}}{c_{n+l,t_{m}}}=e^{\tau_{m}(l-k)},

for some

such that, for each

is continuously differentiable on

with at most polynomial growth at infinity, we have

(43)

\displaystyle X_{n}(f)-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(f)]\to\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}ke^{-|\tau_{r_{1}}-\tau_{r_{2}}|k}\widehat{f\circ\mu^{-1}}^{(r_{1})}_{k}\widehat{f\circ\mu^{-1}}^{(r_{2})}_{k}\right),

where

(44)

\displaystyle\widehat{f\circ\mu^{-1}}^{(r)}_{k}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f\left(t_{r},\mu^{-1,(t_{r})}\left(b(1;t_{r})+2a(1;t_{r})\cos\theta\right)\right)\cos(k\theta)d\theta.

and

.

The proof of Theorem
2.5
will be given in Section
4
.

3.
A first example:
q
-Racah ensemble

In this section, we will discuss the
q
-Racah ensemble and illustrate some results from Section
2
. The computations here will also be relevant for our running example of lozenge tilings of the hexagon with the weight (
1
).

3.1.
q
-Racah ensemble

Our running example will be that of the
q
-Racah ensemble. For
M\in\mathbb{N}
set
-\log_{\bf q}\gamma_{n}=M+1
. The
q
-Racah ensemble of size
n<M
is then the probability density on
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
proportional to (
6
) with

(45)

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(y)=\mathbf{q}^{-y}+\delta\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q}^{y+1},

and weight function

(46)

\displaystyle w(y)=\frac{\left(\alpha\mathbf{q},\beta\delta\mathbf{q},\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q},\gamma_{n}\delta\mathbf{q};\mathbf{q}\right)_{y}\left(1-\gamma_{n}\delta\mathbf{q}^{2y+1}\right)}{\left(\mathbf{q},\alpha^{-1}\gamma_{n}\delta\mathbf{q},\beta^{-1}\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q},\delta\mathbf{q};\mathbf{q}\right)_{y}\left(\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}\right)^{y}\left(1-\gamma_{n}\delta\mathbf{q}\right)},\qquad y=0,\ldots,M,

where
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{j};\mathbf{q})_{k}=(x_{1};\mathbf{q})_{k}\cdots(x_{j};\mathbf{q})_{k}
and
(x;\mathbf{q})_{k}=(1-x)(1-x\mathbf{q})\cdots(1-x\mathbf{q}^{k-1})
is the
q
-Pochhammer symbol. One needs to specify the parameters
\alpha,\beta,\delta,\gamma_{n}
such that
w(y)
is non-negative for all
y
to become a weight. We do not intend to deal with the most general choice of parameters that do this but discuss a few special choices.
One readily verifies that
w(y)
is positive in the following cases:

case 1:

\displaystyle\qquad 0<\mathbf{q}<1,\qquad\alpha,\beta>0,\qquad\delta\geq 0,\qquad\beta\delta<1,\qquad\beta\geq\gamma_{n},\qquad\alpha\geq\gamma_{n}

case 2:

\displaystyle\qquad 1<\mathbf{q},\qquad\alpha,\beta>0,\qquad\delta\geq 0,\qquad\alpha^{-1}\delta<1,\qquad\beta\leq\gamma_{n},\qquad\alpha\leq\gamma_{n}

case 3:

\displaystyle\qquad 0<\mathbf{q}<1,\qquad\alpha,\beta>0,\qquad\delta<0,\qquad\beta\geq\gamma_{n},\qquad\alpha\geq\gamma_{n}

case 4:

\displaystyle\qquad 1<\mathbf{q},\qquad\alpha,\beta>0,\qquad\delta<0,\qquad\beta\leq\gamma_{n},\qquad\alpha\leq\gamma_{n}.

Note that the cases
0<q<1
and
q>1
are dual. Important is that in all four cases we have the following:

\log_{\mathbf{q}}\alpha\beta<2\log_{\mathbf{q}}\gamma_{n}=-2(M+1),

and this holds both for
0<q<1
and
q>1
.

Another interesting choice of parameters is known in the literature as the trigonometric case. Here
\mathbf{q},\alpha,\beta,\delta,\gamma_{n}
are on the unit circle. In this case, there are some natural conditions on the parameters for which the orthogonality weight
w(y)
becomes non-negative. Details can, for example, be found in Proposition 6.1. in
[
21
]
. Also note that in this case
\nu_{n}(y)=2\sqrt{\gamma_{n}\delta\mathbf{q}}\cos\left(\arg\left(\sqrt{\gamma_{n}\delta\mathbf{q}}\right)y\right)
is a monotone function after factoring out
\sqrt{\gamma_{n}\delta\mathbf{q}}
.

We recall that the
q
-Racah ensembles are our main interest, as they are directly related to the tiling model from the Introduction. Another important feature of the
q
-Racah polynomials is that they are on top of the Askey scheme. That means other classical families and their
q
-analogues can be obtained by taking special limits of the
q
-Racah polynomials. For instance, taking
\delta=0
,
w
becomes the
q
-Hahn polynomial weight. If one further sets
\alpha=\mathbf{q}^{\tilde{\alpha}}
,
\beta=\mathbf{q}^{\tilde{\beta}}
, where
\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}
, one gets

\lim\limits_{\mathbf{q}\to 1}w(y)=\binom{\tilde{\alpha}+y}{y}\binom{\tilde{\beta}+M-y}{M-y},

to be the weight for Hahn polynomials. Similarly, setting
\beta=-\alpha^{-1}p\mathbf{q}^{-1}
and
\delta=0
, we have

\lim\limits_{\mathbf{q}\to 1}\lim\limits_{\alpha\to 0}w(y)=\binom{M}{y}p^{-y},

to be the weight for Krawtchouk polynomials. For more about
q
-analogues of orthogonal polynomials, one may refer to
[
36
]
.

To apply the results of Section
2
, we first need to recall the recurrence relation for the
q
-Racah polynomials.

Denote
R_{j}(\nu_{n}(y))
to be the
q
-Racah orthogonal polynomial of order
j
which is given by the
q
-hypergeometric function
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
via the
q
-Pochhammer symbol
(\cdot;\mathbf{q})_{k}
as follows (for more details we refer to
[
36
]
),

(47)

\displaystyle R_{j}(\nu_{n}(y))

\displaystyle={}_{4}\phi_{3}\bigg(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{q}^{-j},\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}^{j+1},\mathbf{q}^{-y},\delta\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q}^{y+1}\\
\alpha\mathbf{q},\delta\beta\mathbf{q},\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q}\end{matrix};\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\bigg).

Instead of
R_{j}
we will work with the monic polynomials
p_{j}
defined by

p_{j}(\nu_{n}(y))=R_{j}(\nu_{n}(y))\frac{(\alpha\mathbf{q},\delta\beta\mathbf{q},\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q};\mathbf{q})_{j}}{(\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}^{j+1};\mathbf{q})_{j}}.

Then, the three-term recurrence relation can be written as

(48)

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(y)p_{j}(\nu_{n}(y))

\displaystyle=p_{j+1}(\nu_{n}(y))+(1+\delta\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q}-A_{j}-C_{j})p_{j}(\nu_{n}(y))+A_{j-1}C_{j}p_{j-1}(\nu_{n}(y)),

where

(49)

\displaystyle A_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q}^{j+1})(1-\alpha\mathbf{q}^{j+1})(1-\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}^{j+1})(1-\delta\beta\mathbf{q}^{j+1})}{(1-\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}^{2j+1})(1-\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}^{2j+2})},

(50)

\displaystyle C_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{q}\left(1-\mathbf{q}^{j}\right)\left(1-\beta\mathbf{q}^{j}\right)\left(\delta-\alpha\mathbf{q}^{j}\right)\left(\gamma_{n}-\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}^{j}\right)}{(1-\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}^{2j})(1-\alpha\beta\mathbf{q}^{2j+1})}.

In the previous sections, we have used recurrences for the normalized polynomials and not the monic polynomials. The recurrence relation for the normalized polynomials
r_{j}(y)
can be easily computed from the recurrence relation for the monic polynomials, giving

(51)

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(y)r_{j}(y)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A_{j}C_{j+1}}r_{j+1}(y)+(1+\delta\gamma_{n}\mathbf{q}-A_{j}-C_{j})r_{j}(y)+\sqrt{A_{j-1}C_{j}}r_{j-1}(y).

Now that we have the recurrence relations spelled out explicitly. We are ready to apply the results from Section
2
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{1}{n}}\qquad\text{for $0<q<1$ or $q>1$,}

\displaystyle x

4.
Proof of general limit theorems

In this section, we prove the main results of Section

.

4.1.
Preliminaries

For any random variable
X
, its cumulants
\mathbf{C}_{k}
are given by the expansion of its Laplace transform, if it exists,

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda X}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}\frac{\mathbf{C}_{k}}{k!}

The
k
-th moment is fully characterized by the first
k
cumulants, for example,
\mathbf{C}_{1}=\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\mathbf{C}_{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X)
. In particular, a random variable is Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
if and only if the first cumulant is
\mu
and the second
\sigma^{2}
and all cumulants of higher order are zero.

The important feature of orthogonal polynomial ensembles is that the cumulants for linear statistics with polynomial test functions can be expressed in terms of the Jacobi matrix associated with the orthogonality weight. This idea was originally introduced in
[
12
]
and further extended to extended processes in
[
25
]
. It is not hard to see that the same is true for the generalization to (
6
), but now for polynomials in
\mu_{n}
, as we will now explain. We will first discuss the one-dimensional ensembles of type (
6
) and then the extended processes of type (
34
) later. We will only state the main observations, as the proofs follow almost verbatim.

Let
J_{n}
be the Jacobi matrix

(62)

\displaystyle J_{n}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}b_{0,n}&a_{0,n}&\\
a_{0,n}&b_{1,n}&a_{1,n}&\\
&a_{1,n}&b_{2,n}&a_{2,n}&\\
&&a_{2,n}&b_{3,n}&a_{3,n}&\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

where
a_{j,n}
and
b_{j,n}
are the recurrence coefficients from (
9
) for the polynomials satisfying (
8
). We will also need the projection matrix
P_{n}
that is defined by
(P_{n})_{ij}=1
for
1\leq i=j\leq n
and
(P_{n})_{ij}=0
otherwise. The complementary projection is defined by
Q_{n}=I-P_{n}
.

The heart of the matter is that for
X_{n}(p\circ\mu_{n})
, where
X_{n}
is the linear statistic in (
15
) with
f=p\circ\mu_{n}
and
p
polynomial, we have the following expression:

(63)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X_{n}(p\circ\mu_{n})}]=\det\left(Q_{n}+P_{n}e^{\lambda p(J_{n})}P_{n}\right).

Indeed, a proof of this claim follows immediately after replacing
x
by
\mu_{n}(x)
everywhere in the proof of
[
25
, Lemma 4.1]
.
Then, by taking the log-derivatives with respect to
\lambda
, each cumulant can be expressed in terms of
J_{n}
and
P_{n}
. To start with, we have

(64)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(p\circ\mu_{n})]=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{n}p(J_{n})P_{n}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}[X_{n}(p\circ\mu_{n})]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{n}p(J_{n})Q_{n}p(J_{n})P_{n}\right),

and the general expression for the
k
-th cumulant is given by

(66)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{k}(X_{n}(p\circ\mu_{n}))=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\ldots+l_{j}=k\\
l_{i}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{l_{1}!\cdots l_{j}!}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}P_{n}p(J_{n})^{l_{1}}P_{n}\cdots P_{n}p(J_{n})^{l_{j}}P_{n}.

Here, all indices are integers. Note that from (
64
) we see that the
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(p\circ\mu_{n})]
depends on the recurrence coefficients
a_{j,n}
and
b_{j,n}
with
j/n\approx\xi
with
\xi\in[0,1]
. This explains the conditions in Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
. Moreover, the right-hand side of (
65
), and thus
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}X_{n}(p\circ\mu_{n})
, depends on only finitely many coefficients
a_{j,n}
and
b_{j,n}

The linear statistics for the extended process (

Then for any function

4.2.
Proof of Theorem

We say that a sequence

of

, if there exists a function

analytic in

on the unit circle and continuous in

such that for any

and any

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\frac{j}{n},\frac{k}{n}\to\xi}\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{j,k}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint a(\xi,z)\frac{dz}{z^{j-k+1}}.

It is important to note that the three diagonal Jacobi operator
J
, under the assumption (
16
), is a slowly varying matrix with
a(\xi,z)=a(\xi)z+b(\xi)+a(\xi)z^{-1}
. The proof of Theorem
2.1
is, in fact, a special case of a more general result for matrices that are slowly varying along the diagonals.

We start with a convenient consequence of the slowly varying assumption, which is the uniform boundedness in the following sense.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A^{(n)}
be the slowly varying operator satisfying (
69
). Assume its limit
a(\cdot,z)
is continuous on
[0,1]
. Let
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{j}{n}=\xi\in[0,1]
. Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}
be independent of
n
. Then we have

(70)

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup\limits_{j\leq n+\sqrt{n}}\left|\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{j+\Delta,j}\right|<\infty.

Proof.

Suppose, on the contrary, (
70
) does not hold. Then there exist two subsequences of
\mathbb{N}
, say
\{j_{m}\}_{m}
and
\{n_{m}\}_{m}
with
j_{m}\leq n_{m}+\sqrt{n_{m}}
and
n_{m}\to\infty
as

such that for all
m\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\left|\left(A^{(n_{m})}\right)_{j_{m}+\Delta,j_{m}}\right|>m.

Here we also have
0\leq\frac{j_{m}}{n_{m}}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{m}}}\leq 2
. Then, by compactness of the closed interval
[0,2]
, there exist another sub-sequence
\{m_{k}\}_{k}
of
\mathbb{N}
such that the limit exists as
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{j_{m_{k}}}{n_{m_{k}}}=\xi\in[0,1]
. Now we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\frac{j_{m_{k}}}{n_{m_{k}}}\to\xi}\left|\left(A^{(n_{m_{k}})}\right)_{j_{m_{k}}+\Delta,j_{m_{k}}}\right|=+\infty.

Recall that
a(\cdot,z)
is continuous on
[0,1]
and hence bounded. This contradicts assumption (
69
).
∎

Recall from (
64
) that we have, for any polynomial
p
, that
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(p\circ\mu_{n})]=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(P_{n}p(J_{n})P_{n})
, where

forms a sequence of slowly varying linear operator. The following two results thus prove the convergence of the mean

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\{A^{(n)}\}_{n}
be a sequence of slowly varying operator satisfying (

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{n}A^{(n)}P_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int^{1}_{0}\oint a(\xi,z)\frac{dz}{z}d\xi.

Proof.

Note that by the definition of the floor function, we can rewrite the trace into the following

(72)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{n}A^{(n)}P_{n}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}A^{(n)}_{\lfloor n\xi\rfloor,\lfloor n\xi\rfloor}d\xi

For any fixed

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}A^{(n)}_{\lfloor n\xi\rfloor,\lfloor n\xi\mathbb{\rfloor}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint a(\xi,z)\frac{dz}{z}

Now Lemma

shows that

∎

Proposition 4.2.1
.

Let
\{A^{(n)}\}_{n}
be a sequence of slowly varying operator satisfying (
69
). Suppose
A^{(n)}
is a band matrix with a bandwidth independent of
n
. We have for any polynomial
p
,

(73)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{n}p(A^{(n)})P_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int^{1}_{0}\oint p(a(\xi,z))\frac{dz}{z}d\xi.

Proof.

First, consider a monomial
p(x)=x^{k}
for any integer
k
. We have

\displaystyle\left(\left(A^{(n)}\right)^{k}\right)_{m,m}=

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\dots,l_{k-1}}\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{m,l_{1}}\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{l_{1},l_{2}}\dots\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{l_{k-2},l_{k-1}}\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{l_{k-1},m}.

Since
A^{(n)}
has fixed bandwidth, say
b
which is independent of
n
, we have
(A^{(n)})_{j,k}=0
for
|j-k|>b
. This means the non-zero entries in the sum must have indices
l_{j}
such that
|l_{j}-m|\leq kb
for all
j=1,2,\dots,k-1
. Hence, the total number of non-zero entries is independent of
n
. Consider
m
such that
\frac{m}{n}\to\xi\in[0,1]
. Then we have
\frac{l_{j}}{n}\to\xi
for those non-zero entries. By the slowly varying assumption (
69
), we have the following limits for the non-zero entries,

\displaystyle\lim_{\frac{m}{n}\to\xi}\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{m,l_{1}}=

\displaystyle\oint a(\xi,z)\frac{dz}{z^{m-l_{1}+1}}

\displaystyle\lim_{\frac{m}{n}\to\xi}\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{l_{j},l_{j+1}}=

\displaystyle\oint a(\xi,z)\frac{dz}{z^{l_{j}-l_{j+1}+1}}\quad\text{ for all }j=1,2\dots,k-2

\displaystyle\lim_{\frac{m}{n}\to\xi}\left(A^{(n)}\right)_{l_{k-1},m}=

\displaystyle\oint a(\xi,z)\frac{dz}{z^{l_{k-1}-m+1}}.

On the other hand, we expand
a(\xi,z)=\sum_{l^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint a(\xi,z_{k})\frac{z^{l^{\prime}}dz_{k}}{z_{k}^{l^{\prime}+1}}
and get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint a(\xi,z_{1})^{k}\frac{dz_{1}}{z_{1}}=\sum_{l_{1}^{\prime},l_{2}^{\prime},\dots,l_{k-1}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{k}}\oint\dots\oint\frac{a(\xi,z_{1})a(\xi,z_{2})\dots a(\xi,z_{k-1})a(\xi,z_{k})}{z_{1}^{1-l_{1}^{\prime}-l_{2}^{\prime}-\dots-l_{k-1}^{\prime}}z_{2}^{l_{1}^{\prime}+1}\dots z_{k-1}^{l_{k-2}^{\prime}+1}z_{k}^{l_{k-1}^{\prime}+1}}dz_{1}dz_{2}\dots dz_{k}

where we can reorder the indexes in the following way, i.e. setting

, and then recursively setting

for all

. Clearly,

and thus we have for all

\displaystyle\lim_{\frac{m}{n}\to\xi}\left(\left(A^{(n)}\right)^{k}\right)_{m,m}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint a(\xi,z)^{k}\frac{dz}{z}

the limit is uniform in

. This actually shows that the diagonal of

is also slowly varying. Thus we can apply Lemma
4.2
for

and we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{n}\left(A^{(n)}\right)^{k}P_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int^{1}_{0}\oint a(\xi,z)^{k}\frac{dz}{z}d\xi

for any
k
. By linearity of trace and integral, the limit above also works for any

being any polynomial. That is

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{n}p(A^{(n)})P_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int^{1}_{0}\oint p(a(\xi,z))\frac{dz}{z}d\xi,

and this proves the statement.
∎

Proof of Theorem

5.
Variational problem

It is well-established that random tilings of planar domains concentrate near a deterministic limit shape when the size of the domain grows large. For uniformly distributed random tilings, and more generally for tilings with periodic weight structure, there have been important works on characterizing the limit shape in terms of a variational problem, in particular
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
34
,
35
,
37
]
. Furthermore, a complete characterization of its regularity properties was achieved in
[
3
]
. Random tilings from probability measures different from the uniform measure or measures arising from a periodic weight structure are much less understood and this is an interesting open problem. As we will discuss below, non-uniform weights can give rise to variational problems whose Euler-Lagrange equation is non-homogeneous, and whose regularity theory is much more complicated than for the homogeneous one. The
q
-Racah weighting of the hexagon is an interesting case study from this point of view. Furthermore we associate a natural complex structure to the variational problem using a Beltrami equation approach, extending results in
[
3
]
. In section
6.5
we then verify that this complex structure is the same as the one occurring in the proof of the Gaussian free field in Theorem
6.4
. In addition we relate the complex structure to the complex slope solving a complex Burger equation. While the complex structure in the
q
-Racah weighting of the hexagon and similar trapezoid domains has also been studied in the works
[
22
,
29
]
, it has not before been connected to the variational problem.

5.1.

We start with some notation. The independent variables will be relabeled according to

The height function

\displaystyle h_{tiles}(x,y)=-h(x,y)+y.

In what follows we will only consider the height function for the tiles which is the one used in the variational problem. Furthermore we will omit the subscript

In particular we have the relation

Let

Central to the analysis of the variational problem and the regularity of its minimizer, is the first order non-linear Beltrami equation of the form

\displaystyle f_{\overline{z}}(z)=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\sigma}(f(z))f_{z}(z)+w(z)

where

(111)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(z)=(I-\nabla\sigma)\circ(I+\nabla\sigma)^{-1}(\overline{z})

Since

[

that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}:\text{Dom}(\mathcal{H}_{\sigma})=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\overline{z}\in(I+\nabla\sigma)(N^{\circ})\}\to\mathbb{C}

5.3.1.

The Euler-Lagrange equations for the variational problem amount to:

\displaystyle\text{div}\,\nabla\sigma(\nabla h(z))=w(z),

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open domain and assume that

and
\sigma
as in (

Then

(113)

solves the inhomogeneous
\mathbb{C}
-quasilinear Beltrami equation (

For

[

Proof.

Let
G
be a smooth vector field in
\mathcal{L}
such that
\text{div}\,G(z)=w(z)

in

[

]
. Set
\mathcal{A}=\nabla\sigma
. Define the divergence free vector field in
\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle B(x):=\mathcal{A}(\nabla u(x))-G(x).

We introduce that div-curl couple
\mathcal{F}=(B,E)=(\mathcal{A}(\nabla u)-G,\nabla u)

(locally, i.e. on simply connected subsets of
\mathscr{H}

\displaystyle\nabla v=\star B=\star(\mathcal{A}(\nabla u)-G),

where

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

We define

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle=2(u(z)+iv(z)).

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{+}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(E(z)+B(z)),\quad\mathcal{F}^{-}(z)=\frac{1}{2}(E(z)-B(z)).

A direct computation gives

\displaystyle F_{\overline{z}}

\displaystyle=u_{x}-v_{y}+i(u_{y}+v_{x}),

\displaystyle\overline{F_{z}(z)}

\displaystyle=u_{x}+v_{y}+i(u_{y}-v_{x}).

Using that

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{F}^{+}(z)

\displaystyle=(u_{x}-v_{y},u_{y}+v_{x}),

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{F}^{-}(z)

\displaystyle=(u_{x}+v_{y},u_{y}-v_{x}).

Thus we have the identifications

(114)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{+}(z)=\frac{1}{2}F_{\overline{z}}(z),\,\,\mathcal{F}^{-}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\overline{F_{z}(z)},

Since

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{F}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=E\pm B=E\pm\mathcal{A}(E)\mp G=(I\pm\mathcal{A})(E)\mp G

and

\displaystyle E=(I+\mathcal{A})^{-1}(2\mathcal{F}^{+}+G)

we get the algebraic relation

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{F}^{-}-G=(I-\mathcal{A})\circ(I+\mathcal{A})^{-1}(2\mathcal{F}^{+}+G)

\displaystyle F_{\overline{z}}=(I-\mathcal{A})\circ(I+\mathcal{A})^{-1}(\overline{F_{z}}+G)+G

Using the structure field
\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle F_{\overline{z}}=\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(F_{z}+\overline{G})+G.

\displaystyle f(z)=F_{z}(z)+\overline{G(z)}.

and note that even though

\mathcal{L}

Then

\displaystyle f_{\overline{z}}(z)

\displaystyle=F_{\overline{z}z}(z)+\overline{G_{z}(z)}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(f(z))+G(z))_{z}+\overline{G_{z}(z)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(f(z))f_{z}(z)+G_{z}(z)+\overline{G_{z}(z)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(f(z))f_{z}(z)+\text{div}\,G(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(f(z))f_{z}(z)+w(z).

Finally we verify that
f(z)=\overline{L_{\sigma}(\nabla u(z))}
.

We have

\displaystyle F_{z}=u_{x}+v_{y}+i(v_{x}-u_{y})

In addition

\displaystyle\nabla v(z)

\displaystyle=\ast(\mathcal{A}(\nabla u(z))-G(z))

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{1}(\nabla u(z))-G_{1}(z)\\

Next we discuss an intrinsic coordinate system, called

6.4.
Height Fluctuation and Gaussian Free Field

In this subsection, we will show how the fluctuation of the height function in the liquid region can be described by Gaussian Free Field on the strip
S=\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi)
, with Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Recall that on the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}_{0}(S)
we have a bilinear pairing

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\nabla}=\pi\int_{S}\langle\nabla f(x),\nabla g(x)\rangle dx.

This bilinear paring can be extended to a well-defined bilinear pairing between the space of distributions
W^{-1,2}(S)\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(S)
(where
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(S)
denotes the space of distributions on
S
in the sense of L. Schwartz) and
W^{1,2}_{0}(S)
which we still denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\nabla}
, (see
[
1
]
for definitions on negative order Sobolev spaces).
The Gaussian Free Field (we refer to
[
41
]
for more details) is a random variable
\Phi
with values in
W^{-1,2}(S)
such that for every
\phi\in W^{1,2}_{0}(S)
the
random variable

(152)

\langle\Phi,\phi\rangle_{\nabla}\sim N\bigg(0,\pi\int|\nabla\phi|^{2}\bigg).

For our statement, we need to introduce new coordinates for the liquid region

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\left\{(t,x):4a(1;t)^{2}-\left(\mu(t,x)-b(1;t)\right)^{2}>0\right\}.

To this end, with
S=\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi)
, we define

(153)

F:\mathcal{E}\to S:(t,x)\mapsto(\tau,\theta)=\left(\tau(t),\arccos\left(\frac{\mu(t,x)-b(1;t)}{2a(1;t)}\right)\right),

where
\tau
is as defined in (
139
). One readily verifies that
F
is a diffeomorphism with
inverse

(154)

F^{-1}:S\to\mathcal{E}:(\tau,\theta)\mapsto(t,x)=(t(\tau),\mu^{-1,(t(\tau))}\left(b\left(1;t(\tau)\right)+2a\left(1;t(\tau))\cos\theta\right)\right),

where
\mu^{-1,(t)}(x)
is the inverse of
x\mapsto\mu(t,x)
.

We will show that
h_{n}\circ F^{-1}-\mathbb{E}[h_{n}\circ F^{-1}]
converges to the Gaussian Free Field on
S
(with Dirichlet boundary conditions). More precisely, we will prove that

\langle h_{n}\circ F^{-1},\phi\rangle_{\nabla}-\mathbb{E}[\langle h_{n}\circ F^{-1},\phi\rangle_{\nabla}]\to\langle\Phi,\phi\rangle_{\nabla},

for all
C^{2}
functions
\phi
on
S
such that
\Delta\phi
has compact support and is piecewise constant in the vertical direction. That is, we assume there exists

0<t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}<b+c,

such that

(155)

\Delta\phi(\tau,\theta)=\begin{cases}\Delta\phi(\tau(t_{m}),\theta),&\tau\in(\tau(t_{m-1}),\tau(t_{m})],\qquad m=1,\dots,N\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

For such
\phi
, the pairing (
152
) can be written as

\displaystyle\langle\Phi,\phi\rangle_{\nabla}\coloneqq-\pi\sum_{m=1}^{N}(\tau(t_{m})-\tau(t_{m-1}))\int_{0}^{\pi}\Phi(\tau(t_{m}),\theta)\Delta\phi(\tau(t_{m}),\theta)d\theta.

Functions
\phi
that satisfy (
155
) are dense in the space of test functions with the Dirichlet norm.

Theorem 6.4
.

Consider a nonsingular
q
-Racah tiling model, i.e.,
\mu_{x}(t,x)\neq 0
for any
(t,x)
inside the hexagon. Let

be any twice differentiable function with compact support in

such that

Proof.

The proof is based on Theorem
2.5

To apply Theorem
2.5
we first show that

is a linear statistic. To this end, note that
h_{n}(t,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\mathbf{a}-1}\chi_{\{x_{j}(t)\leq x\}}
where we used the notation
\chi_{A}
for the characteristic function of the set

. Then,

\displaystyle\langle h_{n}\circ F^{-1},\phi\rangle=-\pi\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\tau(t_{m})-\tau(t_{m-1}))\int_{\arccos\left(\frac{\mu(t_{m},x_{j}(t_{m}))-b(1;t)}{2a(1;t)}\right)}^{\infty}\Delta\phi(\tau(t_{m}),\theta)d\theta.

If we thus define

f(t_{m},y)=-\pi(\tau(t_{m})-\tau(t_{m-1}))\int_{\arccos\left(\frac{\mu(t_{m},x_{j}(t_{m}))-b(1;t)}{2a(1;t)}\right)}^{\infty}\Delta\phi(\tau(t_{m}),\theta)d\theta,

then the above can be rewritten as a linear statistic for the point process
\{(t_{m},x_{j}(t_{m}))\}_{m=1,j=0}^{N,\mathbf{a}-1}
. Note that this is a marginal of (
128
), and the marginal density can be computed using repeated application of the Cauchy-Binet identity and using orthogonality of the polynomials. The result is exactly of the form (
34
)
and (
33
) with the

-Racah polynomials

and coefficients

as in (

Now, we are to check the conditions of Theorem
2.5
. First, the hexagon forms a compact domain; hence (
37

\mu_{n}(t,x)=q^{-x}+\kappa^{2}q^{-c-t+x+\frac{1}{n}}
, which converges uniformly to
\mu(t,x)=q^{-x}+\kappa^{2}q^{-c-t+x}
. Hence (
38
) and (
40
) is satisfied. Moreover

(156)

\displaystyle\mu(t,\mu_{n}^{-1,(t)}(y))-y=\frac{1}{2}q^{-1/n}\left(q^{1/n}-1\right)\left(\sqrt{y^{2}-4\kappa^{2}q^{-c-t+1/n}}-y\right),

and for any

, we have

is uniformly bounded. Moreover, for non singular

-Racah, we have
\mu_{x}(t,x)\neq 0
for any
(t,x)
inside the hexagon. Since
\mu(t,x)^{2}-4\kappa^{2}q^{-c-t}=(\log q)^{-2}\mu_{x}(t,x)^{2}
and compactness, this implies that

\inf_{(t,x)}\left(\mu(t,x)^{2}-4\kappa^{2}q^{-c-t}\right)>0.

Hence,

(157)
