Manifold Learning Using Kernel Density Estimation and Local Principal Components Analysis
Kitty Mohammed kittymohammed1985@gmail.com, Hariharan Narayanan hariharan.narayanan@tifr.res.in

Abstract
We consider the problem of recovering a d − d- dimensional manifold ℳ ⊂ ℝ n \mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} when provided with noiseless samples from ℳ \mathcal{M} . There are many algorithms (e.g., Isomap) that are used in practice to fit manifolds and thus reduce the dimensionality of a given data set. Ideally, the estimate ℳ put \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}} of ℳ \mathcal{M} should be an actual manifold of a certain smoothness; furthermore, ℳ put \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}} should be arbitrarily close to ℳ \mathcal{M} in Hausdorff distance given a large enough sample. Generally speaking, existing manifold learning algorithms do not meet these criteria. Fefferman et al. 2016 have developed an algorithm whose output is provably a manifold. The key idea is to define an approximate squared-distance function (asdf) to ℳ \mathcal{M} . Then, ℳ put \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}} is given by the set of points where the gradient of the asdf is orthogonal to the subspace spanned by the largest n − d n-d eigenvectors of the Hessian of the asdf. As long as the asdf meets certain regularity conditions, ℳ put \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}} is a manifold that is arbitrarily close in Hausdorff distance to ℳ \mathcal{M} . In this paper, we define two asdfs that can be calculated from the data and show that they meet the required regularity conditions. The first asdf is based on kernel density estimation, and the second is based on estimation of tangent spaces using local principal components analysis.

Introduction

It is often the case that high-dimensional data sets have lower-dimensional structure taking the form of a manifold. Manifold learning consists of algorithms that take a high-dimensional data set as input and output a fit of the manifold structure. Many of these algorithms (such as Isomap, Laplacian eigenmaps, locally linear embedding, etc.) are used in practice and have a theoretical literature supporting them.
Ma and Fu 2011
give a concise overview of these methods.

A drawback of most manifold learning algorithms is that if we are given data from a manifold, their output is not an actual manifold that is close to the original manifold.
Fefferman et al. 2016
develop an algorithm whose output is provably a manifold of certain smoothness. They start by defining an approximate squared-distance function (asdf) from the data in a manner that uses exhaustive search, utilizing the data only indirectly. Thus, a very large number of potential asdfs are examined before an approximately optimal one is chosen. In this paper, we do away with the exhaustive search, albeit in the specific case of noiseless data that is sampled uniformly from a manifold.
Fefferman et al. 2016
prove a key theorem that states that as long as we are able to define an asdf meeting certain general conditions, their algorithm outputs a set that is a manifold with bounded smoothness and Hausdorff distance to the original manifold. We demonstrate two different methods of estimating the true manifold via asdfs that can be calculated from the data. The two asdfs in our paper are based on 1) kernel density estimation, and 2) approximating the manifold using tangent planes which are in turn approximated with local principal components analysis (PCA).

Ozertem and Erdogmus 2011
learn manifolds by forming a kernel density estimator (KDE) from the data points and finding its
d
-dimensional ridges. We give a more precise definition later, but a ridge is essentially a higher-dimensional analog of the mode and is related to the output set from the algorithm of
Fefferman et al. 2016
.
Ozertem and Erdogmus 2011
give a practical method for finding the ridges through a variant of gradient descent where the descent is constrained to the subspace spanned by the largest eigenvectors of the Hessian of the KDE. We state their algorithm in Section
5
of our paper and use it to produce simulation results. Although they only apply subspace-constrained gradient descent to find ridges of the KDE, the method is more general and can be used to find ridges of both of our asdfs.

1.1
Related work

Manifold learning has existed as an area of statistics and machine learning since the early 2000s. Some classical manifold learning algorithms are Isomap
(
Tenenbaum et al. 2000
)
, locally linear embedding
(
Roweis and Saul 2000
)
, and Laplacian eigenmaps
(
Belkin and Niyogi 2003
)
. Many of these early algorithms rely on spectral graph theory and start off by constructing a graph which is then used to produce a lower-dimensional embedding of the data set. The theoretical guarantees are centered around proving that asymptotically, certain values such as the geodesic distance can be approximated to arbitrary precision.

More recently, there have been quite a few papers combining ridge estimation with manifold learning (including the work of
Ozertem and Erdogmus 2011
). Some early results on ridge estimation are due to
Eberly 1996
,
Hall et al. 1992
, and
Cheng et al. 2004
. Ridge sets can be constructed to estimate a probability density or an embedded submanifold. Theoretical guarantees in this setting have been given by
Genovese et al. 2012b
,
Genovese et al. 2012a
,
Genovese et al. 2014
, and
Chen et al. 2015
. Of these, the most relevant results for us are from
Genovese et al. 2014
. They prove that as the sample size goes to infinity, their ridge set gets arbitrarily close to an underlying manifold in Hausdorff distance.
Fefferman et al. 2016
also define a procedure related to ridge estimation methods that can be used to estimate an underlying manifold. For our purposes, the major advances of their work are twofold. First, their method is general; as long as a function meets a few conditions, it can be used to define an estimator that can be made arbitrarily close to an underlying manifold in Hausdorff distance. Furthermore, they show that this estimator is itself a manifold with bounded reach (which measures how rough a submanifold can be). Second, their proofs rely on using the implicit function theorem concretely, allowing them to make quantitative statements about the bounds of interest.

2.1
Manifolds

This subsection is adapted from
Fefferman et al. 2016
. In the current paper, we use the terms manifold and submanifold interchangeably with compact imbedded
d
-manifold. A closed subset
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
compact imbedded

d
-
manifold
if the following conditions hold. First,
\mathcal{M}
is compact. Next, there exists
r_{1}>r_{2}>0
such that for every
z\in\mathcal{M}
there exists a
d-
dimensional subspace
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{M}\cap B(z,r_{2})=\Gamma\cap B(z,r_{2})
for a patch
\Gamma
over
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
of radius
r_{1}
, centered at
z
and tangent to
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
at
z
. A
patch
of radius
r
over
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
is a subset
\Gamma:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x+\Psi(x)\,|\,x\in B_{d}(r)\subset T_{z}\mathcal{M}}}\right\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
\Psi(x):B_{d}(r)\rightarrow T_{z}^{\perp}\mathcal{M}
is a
C^{2}
-function that is zero at the origin.

The tangent space can be defined in the usual way (corresponding to
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
) or by using the following definition which applies to arbitrary closed sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. At a point
a\in A
,
\mathrm{Tan}^{0}(a,A)
is the set of vectors
v
such that for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
b\in A
such that
0<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert a-b}}\right\rvert<\epsilon
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v}}\right\rvert-\frac{b-a}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert b-a}}\right\rvert}}}\right\rvert<\epsilon
. Let the
tangent space

\mathrm{Tan}(a,A)
be the set of all
x
such that
x-a\in\mathrm{Tan}^{0}(a,A)
.

The geometric quantities of a submanifold
\mathcal{M}
that we are most concerned with are the
d
-dimensional volume
V
and the
reach

\tau
. The reach is the largest number such that all points within
\tau
of
\mathcal{M}
have a unique closest point on
\mathcal{M}
. Intuitively, the reach governs how “rough” an embedded submanifold is. For example, the reach of a line with a sharp cusp is zero, and the reach of a linear subspace is infinite.

The following theorem due to
Federer 1959
is useful for bounding the distance from a point on a manifold to the tangent space at a nearby point.

Theorem 1

(Federer’s reach condition)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{reach}(\mathcal{M})^{-1}=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\|b-a\|^{-2}\|b-\Pi_{a}b\|\;\big|\;a,b\in\mathcal{M},a\neq b}}\right\}.

In this paper, we assume regularity conditions on the manifold we draw samples from. We assume it is in
\mathcal{G}
, where
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(d,V_{max},\tau_{min})
is the family of boundaryless
C^{2}
-submanifolds of the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with dimension
d
, volume less than or equal to
V_{max}
, and reach at least
\tau_{min}
.

Let the
tubular neighborhood

\mathcal{M}_{\widebar{\tau}}
be the set of all points within a distance of
\widebar{\tau}
of
\mathcal{M}
. Now, for points
z\in\mathcal{M}
and
y\in\mathcal{M}_{\widebar{\tau}}
, denote the projection onto the tangent plane at
z
by

\displaystyle\Pi_{z}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow T_{z}\mathcal{M}.

A number of our proofs rely on defining the following sets:

\displaystyle U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}

\displaystyle:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y\;\big|\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-\Pi_{z}(y)}}\right\rVert\leq\widebar{\tau}}}\right\}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y\;\big|\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-\Pi_{z}(y)}}\right\rVert\leq\widebar{\tau}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z,\widebar{\tau}}

\displaystyle:=U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}\cap\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{z,\widebar{\tau}}

\displaystyle:=U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}\cap T_{z}\mathcal{M}.

U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}
is a cylinder centered at
z
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z,\widebar{\tau}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{z,\widebar{\tau}}
are nearby regions of the manifold and tangent space, respectively.
\mathcal{A}_{z,\widebar{\tau}}
can also be defined as the projection of the cylinder onto the tangent space; i.e., as
\Pi_{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}}}\right)
. These sets are especially useful because, as long as
\widebar{\tau}<\tau
, they allow us to work with a local parametrization of the manifold. As mentioned earlier, manifolds can be defined locally as functions from the tangent space to the normal space. The functions we are working with are in the class
C^{1,1}
; i.e., they are once continuously differentiable and have a Lipschitz gradient. This is summarized in the next theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{G}(d,V_{max},\tau_{min})
. Let
z\in\mathcal{M}
and
y\in\mathcal{M}_{\widebar{\tau}}
. When
\widebar{\tau}
is sufficiently small, there exists a
C^{1,1}
function

\displaystyle F_{z,U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{x}}:\mathcal{A}_{z,\widebar{\tau}}\rightarrow\Pi_{z}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Pi_{z}(0)}}\right)

such that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y+F_{z,U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}}(y)\;\big|\;y\in\mathcal{A}_{z,\widebar{\tau}}}}\right\}=\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z,\widebar{\tau}}.

Additionally, there exists a constant
C
such that
\mathrm{Lip}(\nabla F_{z,U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}})\leq C/\tau
.

The next theorem is from
Krantz and Parks 2012
. It states that
\mathcal{M}
has positive reach as long as it is embedded in a Euclidean space with strictly higher dimension.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
d
-dimensional
C^{2}-
submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
n>d
, then
\mathcal{M}
has positive reach.

Now, suppose we want a discrete approximation of a manifold
\mathcal{M}
at a certain resolution. Let
Y\subset\mathcal{M}
be an
\eta
-net for
\mathcal{M}
if for every
p\in\mathcal{M}
there is a
y\in Y
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert p-y}}\right\rVert<\eta
. The following theorem states that the size of an
\eta-
net depends on the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{G}(d,V_{max},\tau_{min})
, and let
\mathcal{M}
be equipped with the Euclidean metric from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any
\eta>0
, there exists an
\eta-
net of
\mathcal{M}
consisting of at most
CV\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/\tau^{d}+1/\eta^{d}}}\right)
points, where
C
is a universal constant.

How well a manifold approximates a point set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{i}}}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
can be quantified through the
empirical loss
, which is defined as

\displaystyle L_{emp}(\mathcal{M}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d(x_{i},\mathcal{M})^{2},

where
d(x_{i},\mathcal{M})
is the length of the projection from
x_{i}
onto
\mathcal{M}
.

Given two subsets
X
and
Y
of Euclidean space, we can measure the distance between them using the
Hausdorff distance

H(X,Y)
. This is defined as

\displaystyle H(X,Y):=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sup_{x\in X}\inf_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert,\sup_{y\in Y}\inf_{x\in X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert}}\right\}.

It can be shown that, given adequate sampling density, two manifolds that are close in empirical risk to a given point set are also close in Hausdorff distance.

2.2
Model

We assume that we are provided with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y_{i}}}\right\}_{1}^{N}
noiselessly sampled from the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{G}(d,V_{max},\tau_{min})
. We take this approach to simplify calculations. The analysis would be similar if the sample came from a (potentially Lipschitz) density bounded away from zero.

2.3
Approximate squared-distance functions

For our purposes the most important results from
Fefferman et al. 2016
are Theorem 13 and Lemma 14. We reproduce them below as Theorems
5
and
6
, and give an adapted proof of the latter. It is beyond the scope of this paper to discuss the proof of Theorem
5
. We merely note that it relies on the implicit function theorem, so there are concrete bounds on the constants
c_{2},\ldots,c_{7}
and
C
that control the geometry of the putative manifold.

Theorem
5
states that an
approximate squared-distance function
can be used to recover a manifold with arbitrary precision (with increasing sample size) as long as
F
, a scaled version of the asdf, meets three conditions related to smoothness and curvature. The notation
\partial^{\alpha}F(x)
means that given a set of vectors
\alpha:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert}}}\right\}
, the partial derivative is computed successively in the directions
v_{i}
. The third condition is the reason for the term asdf: for a small constant
\rho
,
F+\rho^{2}
is bounded both above and below by a multiple of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert^{2}+\rho^{2}
, the approximate squared distance to the manifold.

Note that the function
F
always has as its domain the unit ball (or a ball whose radius is not dependent on sample size).
F
is not the asdf itself, but a related function applied locally after the coordinate system has been scaled up by a constant. This constant is usually a kind of bandwidth parameter that we decrease in order to get a more precise estimate of the manifold. For example, in Section
3
, we have a scheme to decrease the bandwidth
\sigma
of the kernel density estimator, and
F
is the KDE applied to coordinates scaled up by
1/\sigma
.

The output set from Theorem
5
is locally a smooth graph
(x,\Psi(x))
that lies within a tubular neighborhood of the manifold. Theorem
6
uses bounds on the smoothness of
(x,\Psi(x))
to show that it lies away from the boundary of the tubular neighborhood, and so it is itself a manifold. We show that it is in fact very close to the original manifold, giving a bound on the Hausdorff distance in terms of a constant that can be made as small as desired.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose the following conditions hold for a function
F
:

1.

F:B_{n}(0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
C^{k}
-smooth.

2.

\partial_{x,y}^{\alpha}F(x,y)\leq C_{0}
, where
(x,y)\in B_{n}(0,1)
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|\leq k
.

3.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
and
(x,y)\in B_{n}(0,1)

\displaystyle c_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{2}+\rho^{2}}}\right)\leq F(x,y)+\rho^{2}\leq C_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{2}+\rho^{2}}}\right),

for
0<\rho<c
, where
c
is an arbitrarily small constant depending only on
C_{0},c_{1},C_{1},k,
and
n
.

Then there are constants
c_{2},\ldots,c_{7}
and
C
depending only on
C_{0},c_{1},C_{1},k,
and
n
such that:

1.

For
z\in B_{n}(0,c_{2})
, let
N(z)
be the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
spanned by the top
n-d
eigenvectors of
\partial^{2}F(z)
. Let
\Pi_{\mathrm{hi}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow N(z)
be the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
N(z)
. Then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\partial^{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathrm{hi}}(z)}}\right|\leq C
for
z\in B_{n}(0,c_{2})
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|\leq k-2
.

2.

There is a map

\displaystyle\Psi:B_{d}(0,c_{4})\rightarrow B_{n-d}(0,c_{3})

such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\Psi(0)}}\right|\leq C\rho
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\partial^{\alpha}\Psi}}\right|\leq C^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|}
for
1\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|\leq k-2
. The set of all
z=(x,y)\in B_{d}(0,c_{4})\bigtimes B_{n-d}(0,c_{3})
such that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\;\big|\;\Pi_{hi}(z)\partial F(z)=0}}\right\}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x,\Psi(x))\;\big|\;x\in B_{d}(0,c_{4})}}\right\}

is a
C^{k-2}
-smooth graph.

Theorem 6
.

Let
c_{1},C_{1}
, and
C_{0}
be the constants appearing in Theorem
5
. Assume that
C\rho
is sufficiently small compared to
r
. Define the putative submanifold

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in\mathcal{M}_{\min(c_{3},c_{4})r}\;\big|\;\Pi_{hi}(z)\partial F(z)=0}}\right\}.

Then,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
is a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which has a reach greater than
cr
, where
c
depends only on
C_{0},c_{1},C_{1},k,d,
and
n
. Furthermore, the Hausdorff distance
H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}}}\right)
is bounded above by
(C^{2}+C)\rho
.

The statement of this theorem assumes that we are provided with the output set from Theorem
5
; that is, we are working in the scaled-up coordinates. In the original coordinate system,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
is contained in
\mathcal{M}_{\min(c_{3},c_{4})\sigma r}
, where
\sigma
is the bandwidth. In this case, the reach is bounded below by
c\sigma r
, and
H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}}}\right)
is bounded above by
(C^{2}+C)\sigma\rho
.

{proof}

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
is locally the graph of a
C^{k-2}
-smooth function
\Psi
. To prove that it is a manifold, it is sufficient to show that it does not intersect the boundary of the tubular neighborhood
\mathcal{M}_{\min(c_{3},c_{4})r}
. Since Theorem
5
gives bounds on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\partial\Psi}}\right\rVert
, we can show by contradiction of the mean value theorem that every point on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
is within
\min(c_{3},c_{4})r/2
of
\mathcal{M}
.

Suppose there exists a point
\widehat{z}
on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
which is at a distance greater than
{\min(c_{3},c_{4})r}/{2}
from
\mathcal{M}
. Let
z:=\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}\widehat{z}
. By Theorem
5
, there is a point
\widetilde{z}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-\widetilde{z}}}\right\rVert<C\rho
. Let
\widetilde{v}\in T_{z}\mathcal{M}
be the vector
\Pi_{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{z}-\widetilde{z}}}\right)
. Let
\widetilde{\Psi}:[0,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\widetilde{v}}}\right\rVert]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
define a curve on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
whose endpoints are
\widehat{z}
and
\widetilde{z}
. The existence and smoothness of
\widetilde{\Psi}
are guaranteed by
\Psi
, the
C^{k-2}
-smooth function that locally defines
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
. The mean value theorem states that there exists a point
x\in[0,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\widetilde{v}}}\right\rVert]
such that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\partial\widetilde{\Psi}(x)}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\widetilde{v}}}\right\rVert}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\widetilde{\Psi}(z)-\widetilde{\Psi}(z+\widetilde{v})}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\widehat{z}-\widetilde{z}}}\right\rVert}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\widetilde{v}}}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-\widehat{z}}}\right\rVert-C\rho}{C\rho}

\displaystyle>\frac{\min(c_{3},c_{4})r/2-C\rho}{C\rho}.

Since
C\rho
is sufficiently small compared to
\min(c_{3},c_{4})r/2
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\partial\Psi(x)}}\right\rVert
can be made as large as desired. This contradicts the bound
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\partial\Psi(x)}}\right\rVert<C
and shows that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
lies away from the boundary of
\mathcal{M}_{\min(c_{3},c_{4})r}
. In fact, the expression in the third line above must be less than

, which shows that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-\widehat{z}}}\right\rVert<(C^{2}+C)\rho

\mathcal{M}
is within

of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
, so we have the desired bound on the Hausdorff distance.

By Theorem
1
, the reach of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{reach}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}}}\right)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\neq y\\
x,y\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-\Pi_{x}y}}\right\rVert}.

Let
c^{\prime}
be a constant depending on
C_{0},c_{1},C_{1},k,d,
and
n
. If
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert\geq r/c^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-\Pi_{x}y}}\right\rVert}\geq\frac{(r/c^{\prime})^{2}}{2(r/c^{\prime})}.

Now, suppose
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert<r/c^{\prime}
. If
x
and
y
are close together, this quantity is controlled by the second derivative of the

function locally defining
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
. That is,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-\Pi_{x}y}}\right\rVert
is on the order of
C^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}
, implying that

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\neq y\\
x,y\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert<r/c^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-\Pi_{y}x}}\right\rVert}\geq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}{2c^{\prime\prime}C^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}

for some constant
c^{\prime\prime}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{reach}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}})\geq\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r}{2c^{\prime}},\frac{1}{2c^{\prime\prime}C^{2}}}}\right).

2.4
Ridges and gradient descent

To actually find a putative manifold using an approximate squared-distance function
F
, we can use a method introduced by
Ozertem and Erdogmus 2011
. Let
\partial F
and
\partial^{2}F
be the gradient and Hessian of
F
, respectively. At a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}}}\right\}
be the eigenvectors of
\partial^{2}F
associated with the eigenvalues
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}}\right\}
(listed in decreasing order). Let
N(z)
be the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
spanned by the top
n-d
eigenvectors of
\partial^{2}F(z)
. Recall that
\Pi_{\mathrm{hi}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow N(z)
is the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
N(z)
. Note that
\Pi_{\mathrm{hi}}=VV^{\top}
, where
V
is a matrix whose columns are
[v_{1}|\dots|v_{n-d}]
.

Ozertem and Erdogmus 2011
give an algorithm to compute the set

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\;\big|\;\Pi_{hi}(z)\partial F(z)=0}}\right\},

which is termed the
d
-dimensional ridge
of
F
. This is, of course, the local definition of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{put}}
from Theorems
5
and
6
. In order to find a ridge, an initial set of points is chosen and then iteratively shifted in the direction
VV^{\top}\partial F
until a tolerance condition is met. This is essentially a subspace-constrained variant of gradient descent.

2.5
Empirical processes

In Section
3
, we need to bound various quantities that are functions of the kernel density estimator. This is difficult to do because they are empirical averages over a finite number of samples. It is easier to bound the expectation of these quantities and then bound their difference using results from empirical processes, which we summarize here.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a class of functions from
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. If
\mathcal{G}
consists of bounded functions, the
empirical Rademacher average
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{G})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\frac{1}{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}g(x_{i})}}\right)}}\right],

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{i}}}\right\}_{1}^{N}
is an i.i.d. sample from the distribution
\mathcal{P}
and
\sigma:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N}}}\right\}
is a vector of Rademacher random variables. (Rademacher random variables take the values
\pm 1
with equal probability). Letting
\mathcal{P}_{N}
denote the empirical distribution on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{i}}}\right\}_{1}^{N}
, the following holds for
0<\delta<1
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{N}}g-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}g}}\right|<2\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{G})+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{N}}}}\right]>1-\delta.

It is usually difficult to calculate
\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{G})
directly from the definition. However, the next theorem states an upper bound that is dependent on the size of
\mathcal{G}
, which is often easy to estimate. Let the
covering number

N(\eta,\mathcal{G},\|\cdot\|)
be the minimum number of elements in an
\eta
-net of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|
. Let the
metric entropy
be defined as
\log N(\eta,\mathcal{G},\|\cdot\|)
.
The Rademacher complexity can be bounded using a modified form of Dudley’s entropy integral
(
Sridharan and Srebro 2010
)
:

Theorem 7

(Modified Dudley’s integral)
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{G})\leq\inf_{\gamma\geq 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{4\gamma+12\int_{\gamma}^{\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|g\|_{\infty}}\sqrt{\frac{\log N(\eta,\mathcal{G},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{P}_{N})})}{N}}d\eta}}\right\}.

3
Kernel density estimation

Consider the kernel density estimator

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{N}(x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}G_{\sigma}(x;\,y_{i}),

where

G_{\sigma}(x;\,y):=C_{\sigma}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}/2\sigma^{2}},C_{\sigma}:={{(2\pi\sigma^{2})}^{-d/2}},

and
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
(the tubular neighborhood of
\mathcal{M}
with width
\sigma
). The denominator of
C_{\sigma}
has
2\pi\sigma^{2}
raised to the power
d/2
and not
n/2
because we are trying to estimate a
d
-dimensional surface. In Theorem
16
, we show that a function based on
\widetilde{p}_{N}
can recover a manifold
\mathcal{M}
when we are given noiseless samples from
\mathcal{M}
.

3.1
Definition of the asdf

Recall that Theorem
5
must actually be applied in a coordinate system scaled by a bandwidth parameter that becomes more precise with increasing sample size. For the kernel density estimator, this parameter is, of course,
\sigma
. (If we do not scale by
\sigma
, it is clear that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}\widetilde{p}_{N}}}\right\rvert
is bounded above by an increasing function of
1/\sigma
instead of a universal constant
C_{0}
). To this purpose, make the following transformations:

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto x/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto y_{i}/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\mapsto 1/{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

Note that the geometric properties of
\mathcal{M}
change in the obvious ways: the reach becomes
\tau/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})
and the volume is
O(V/\sigma^{d})
. In the transformed case, let
\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}
,
\widehat{\tau}
, and
\widehat{V}
denote the analogs of the obvious quantities. For
z
the projection of
x
onto
\mathcal{M}
, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z,\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{z}:={\mathcal{A}}_{z,\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}
. Recall that these are regions of
\mathcal{M}
and
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
, respectively, which are near the point
z\in\mathcal{M}
. Define the normalizing factor

\displaystyle N_{f}:={\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}}\right)}\bigg/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{z}}}\right)}\times\widehat{V}}}\right).

The appropriate estimator to analyze is any convenient function of
p_{N}/N_{f}
, where

\displaystyle p_{N}(x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}.

We choose to work with
-\log p_{N}(x)+\log N_{f}
as our potential asdf. The first condition from Theorem
5
follows immediately, as seen in the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

-\log p_{N}(x)
is
C^{k}
-smooth.

{proof}

G_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
is
C^{k}
-smooth, so by linearity,
p_{N}(x)
is
C^{k}
-smooth. By the chain rule,
-\log p_{N}(x)
is
C^{k}
-smooth.

In Lemmas
13
and
15
below, we show that
-\log p_{N}(x)+\log N_{f}
also satisfies the second and third conditions from Theorem
5
with high probability. Before detailing the proofs, we briefly discuss our scheme for selecting
\sigma
and
\widebar{\tau}
.

3.2
Selecting the bandwidth
\sigma

The procedure we assume is that a fixed value
\sigma_{1}
of
\sigma
is chosen by the experimenter as well as a value of
\widebar{\tau}
that depends on
\sigma
. Without making any claims about optimality, we choose
\widebar{\tau}:=\sigma^{5/6}
. We prove that there exists a lower bound
K_{1}
for
\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)
given
\sigma_{1}
. We then use empirical processes to show that
p_{N}(x)
concentrates around
\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)
, and is within
\varepsilon_{1}
of
\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)
with high probability. Since
\varepsilon_{1}
is a decreasing function of the sample size
N
, we can increase
N
until
\varepsilon_{1}<K_{1}
giving us a lower bound for
p_{N}
. This allows us to derive an upper bound for
\partial^{\alpha}(-\log p_{N}(x))
. We also find an expression for
\rho^{2}
in terms of
\sigma_{1}
and use this to show that condition 3 of Theorem
5
holds. If
\rho^{2}
is not small enough, we can repeat this procedure using a fixed value
\sigma_{i+1}:=\frac{\sigma_{i}}{2}
of
\sigma
in each subsequent iteration.

3.3
Bounding
p_{N}
in expectation

To prove the second and third conditions, we need upper and lower bounds for
p_{N}
. It is more convenient to work initially in the continuous setting, which amounts to bounding
\mathbb{E}p_{N}
. This is the Gaussian kernel integrated against
\mu_{\mathcal{M}}:=\frac{1}{\widehat{V}}d\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{M})
, the measure that is uniform with respect to the volume form. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y).

Points on
\mathcal{M}
that are far away from
x
do not contribute very much to the value of this integral. In fact, the value of
-\log\mathbb{E}p_{N}
is very close to

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}:=-\log\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y),

where
z
is the projection of
x
onto
\mathcal{M}
. Define its approximation

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}:=-\log\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}N_{f}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y),

where
\mathcal{L}_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure on
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
.

Decreasing
\sigma
corresponds to estimating
\mathcal{M}
with greater precision. Even though this expands the unit ball, leading to
\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}\rightarrow\infty
, the ratio
\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}/\widehat{\tau}\rightarrow 0
. This implies that
\mathcal{A}_{z}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}
are shrinking relatively closer and closer to
z
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}
is very close to an affine space. Thus, we expect
\widehat{F}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}
and
\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}
to grow closer together. To prove this, we first need to show that the pushforward of the uniform measure on the manifold has a density
p(y)
that is close to the uniform density on
B_{d}\big(\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}\big)
. Of course, we don’t want this to be a proper density on
B_{d}\big(\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}\big)
; we want it to have the same total measure as
\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})
. In the following lemma, we quantify how much
p(y)
can deviate from
N_{f}
on
\mathcal{A}_{z}
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{M}
, and let
y\in T_{z}\mathcal{M}
. The pushforward of
\mu_{\mathcal{M}}
to
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
has density
p(y)
on
\mathcal{A}_{z}
with respect to
\mathcal{L}_{d}
such that

\displaystyle N_{f}\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{\widehat{\tau}^{2}}}}\right)^{-d/2}\leq p(y)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{\widehat{\tau}^{2}}}}\right)^{d/2}\times N_{f}.

{proof}

Assume that
z
is the origin and the first
d
coordinates lie in
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
.
\mathcal{M}
is a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined locally by the function
G_{z,U_{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}^{z}}
that maps
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},F_{z,U_{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}^{z}}}}\right)
. Recall from Theorem
2
that
F_{z,U_{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}^{z}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
is a
C^{1,1}
function whose Jacobian
J\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-d)\times d}
has a Lipschitz constant bounded above by
C/\widehat{\tau}
.
J
evaluated at
z
is 0 since
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is tangent to
\mathcal{M}
at
z
; this implies
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert J}}\right\rVert_{F}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}}\right)/\widehat{\tau}
within a radius of
\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. We can find the desired bound on
p(y)
by finding the ratio of the volume elements of
\mathcal{A}_{z}
and
\mathcal{\widetilde{A}}_{z}
, normalizing this so it integrates to one over
\mathcal{A}_{z}
, and multiplying by
{\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}}\right)}\bigg/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{V}}}\right)
.
G_{z,U_{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}^{z}}
has Jacobian
[I|J^{\top}]^{\top}
, allowing us to write

\displaystyle p(y)=\frac{\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}}\right)}{\displaystyle\widehat{V}}\times\frac{\displaystyle\sqrt{\det(I+J^{\top}J)}}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}\sqrt{\det(I+J^{\top}J)}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)}.

Let
\lambda_{i}
be the eigenvalues of
J^{\top}J
.
J^{\top}J
is positive semidefinite, so
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\det(I+J^{\top}J)}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\lambda_{i}}}\right)}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert J^{\top}J}}\right\rVert_{F}}}\right)}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{\widehat{\tau}^{2}}}}\right)^{d/2}.

Since the map from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a contraction,
1\leq\sqrt{\det(I+J^{\top}J)}
. Clearly, we also have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{z}}}\right)\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}\sqrt{\det(I+J^{\top}J)}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)\leq\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{z}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{\widehat{\tau}^{2}}}}\right)^{d/2},

which is enough to show the lemma.

We can use this bound on
p(y)
to simplify the integration of functions over
\mathcal{M}
. As we mentioned earlier,
-\log\mathbb{E}p_{N}
is a function of an integral whose major contribution comes from the region
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}
. (A crude bound suffices for the contribution from the region
\mathcal{M}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}
). In the following lemma, we show that
\widehat{F}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}
and
\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}
are very close together. By using the pushforward we can perform both integrals over
\mathcal{A}_{z}
using Lebesgue measure. To do so we need a bound on the ratio of
p(y)
to
N_{f}
(which we have) as well as a bound on the ratio between the integrands. We find that
\widehat{F}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}
and
\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}
are within a constant
C_{f}
of each other. By decreasing
\sigma
,
C_{f}
can be made as small as desired.

Lemma 10
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}
, and let
z
be the projection of
x
onto
\mathcal{M}
. Then,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}-\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}}}\right\rvert\leq C_{f}
, where

\displaystyle C_{f}:=\frac{dC^{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{2\widehat{\tau}^{2}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{4}}{\widehat{\tau}^{2}}+\frac{2\sqrt{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{3}}{\widehat{\tau}}}}\right)\pi.

{proof}

Assume that
z
is the origin and
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
is identified with the first
d
coordinates. The following chain of inequalities holds, where
y\in T_{z}\mathcal{M}
,
y^{\prime}:=y+F_{z,U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}}
is a point on the manifold, and
J
is the Jacobian of
F_{z,U_{\widebar{\tau}}^{z}}
:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}-\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}N_{f}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)}{\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}N_{f}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)}{\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}}p(y)d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sup_{y\in\mathcal{A}_{z}}\log\frac{\displaystyle e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}N_{f}}{\displaystyle e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x-y^{\prime}}}\right\|^{2}}p(y)}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\mathcal{A}_{z}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{\widehat{\tau}^{2}}}}\right)^{d/2}}}\right\rvert+\sup_{y\in\mathcal{A}_{z}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert-(x-y)^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-y^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}}}\right\rvert\pi.

The first term in the last line comes from Lemma
9
. A Taylor expansion (valid for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert<1
) shows that

\displaystyle\log{(1+x)^{d/2}}=\frac{dx}{2}-\frac{dx^{2}}{4}+\frac{dx^{3}}{6}+O(x^{4}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{\widehat{\tau}^{2}}}}\right)^{d/2}}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{dC^{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{2\widehat{\tau}^{2}}

as long as
\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}/\widehat{\tau}
is smaller than a controlled constant. To bound the other term, we use the law of cosines in conjunction with Theorem
1
, which shows that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}-y}}\right\rVert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}-\Pi_{z}y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}-z}}\right\rVert^{2}}{2\widehat{\tau}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}}\right)^{2}}{2\widehat{\tau}}.

Let
\theta
be the angle between
y-y^{\prime}
and
x-y
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}-x}}\right\rVert^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-x}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\right\rvert\pi

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}-2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-x}}\right\rVert\cos\theta}}\right\rvert\pi

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{\widehat{\tau}}}}\right)^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}}{\widehat{\tau}}}}\right)(\sqrt{2}\widehat{\widebar{\tau}})}}\right)\pi.

Thus,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}-\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}}}\right\rvert\leq C_{f}
, where
C_{f}
is defined in the statement of the lemma.

To actually find the lower bound for
p_{N}
, we bound
\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}
in the next lemma by using a
d
-dimensional Gaussian concentration inequality. The upper bound is much simpler to derive; we include it as well. These bounds are important in verifying the second and third conditions of Theorem
5
. The third condition essentially says that our function is an approximate squared-distance function. That is, given a point
x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
and its projection
z\in\mathcal{M}
, we should have upper and lower bounds that are close to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}
. Since our putative asdf is
-\log p_{N}
, we need bounds for
p_{N}
that are within a multiplicative factor of
e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}
. In the proof of the following lemma we find these pointwise bounds.

The second condition requires that we find an upper bound for
\partial^{\alpha}(-\log p_{N})
. This derivative consists of terms that have powers of
p_{N}
in the denominator and combinations of powers of partial derivatives of
p_{N}
in the numerator. Thus, we need a uniform lower bound for
p_{N}
over
\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
; this follows by taking the infimum of the pointwise bound over the tubular neighborhood. We also need bounds for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\partial^{\alpha}p_{N}}}\right\rvert
, but we defer these to the proof of Lemma
13
.

Lemma 11
.

p_{N}(x)
is bounded in expectation. More precisely,
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}}\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)\geq K_{1}
, where

\displaystyle K_{1}:=N_{f}e^{-{1/2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2e^{-{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}-\sqrt{d/(2\pi)}}}\right)}^{2}}\pi}}}\right)e^{-C_{f}};

furthermore,
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}}\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)\leq K_{2}
, where

\displaystyle K_{2}:=e^{C_{f}}N_{f}+e^{-\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}\pi/2}.

{proof}

Let
z
be the projection of
x
onto
\mathcal{M}
, and let
y\in T_{z}\mathcal{M}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}N_{f}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)}}\right)e^{-C_{f}},

where the second inequality is due to Lemma
10
. By orthogonality,
(x-z)^{\top}(y-z)=0
, so we rewrite the integral over
\mathcal{A}_{z}
as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}N_{f}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)

\displaystyle=N_{f}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-y}}\right\rVert^{2}}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)

\displaystyle=N_{f}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-z}}\right\rVert\leq\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}}\right],

where the probability is with respect to a
d-
dimensional multivariate Gaussian with covariance
\frac{1}{2\pi}I
.
Letting
z
be the origin for simplicity, we know from standard Gaussian concentration results
(
Boucheron et al. 2013
)
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert}}\right\rvert\leq t}}\right]\geq 1-2e^{-t^{2}\pi}

for
t>0
. We can calculate
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|y\|^{2}}}\right]
and then get a bound for
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert}}\right]
by using Jensen’s inequality.

Make the substitutions

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{1}\mapsto r\cos{\phi_{1}},x_{2\leq i\leq d-1}\mapsto r\cos{\phi_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\sin{\phi_{j}},x_{d}\mapsto r\sin{\phi_{d-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin{\phi_{j}}}}\right\},

and let

\displaystyle dV:=r^{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin^{d-j-1}{\phi_{j}}drd\phi_{1}\dots d\phi_{d-1}.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert^{2}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d{\mathcal{L}_{d}}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d+1}e^{-r^{2}\pi}dr\times\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d-j-1}\phi_{j}d\phi_{j}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi_{d-1}

\displaystyle=\pi^{-(2+d)/2}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+d/2}}\right)\times\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((d-j)/2}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+(d-j-1)/2}}\right)}\times 2\pi

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2\pi}.

The product in the third line telescopes to
\pi^{(d-2)/2}\bigg/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma(d/2)}}\right)
; simplifying yields the fourth line.

It follows that
\mathbb{E}\|y\|\leq\sqrt{d/(2\pi)}
. Setting
t:=\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}-\sqrt{d/(2\pi)}
(and assuming that
\sigma
is small enough so
t>0
), we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert\leq\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}}\right]\geq 1-2e^{-{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}-\sqrt{d/(2\pi)}}}\right)}^{2}}\pi}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)\geq N_{f}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2e^{-{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}-\sqrt{d/(2\pi)}}}\right)}^{2}}\pi}}}\right)e^{-C_{f}}.

The first part of the lemma follows by taking the infimum over
\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
.

To find an upper bound, first write the expectation as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})+\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime}).

The first term can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq e^{C_{f}}\int_{{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}N_{f}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)

\displaystyle\leq e^{C_{f}}N_{f}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{z}}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-y}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(y)

\displaystyle\leq e^{C_{f}}N_{f}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}.

Now consider
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}
. Since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert\leq 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\leq\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert
, we have

(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert-\widehat{\widebar{\tau}})^{2}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}
. This gives us the following bound for the second term as long as
\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}
is large enough:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert-\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}}\right)^{2}\pi}\times\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}\pi/2}.

Thus, for
x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)

\displaystyle\leq e^{C_{f}}N_{f}+e^{-\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}^{2}\pi/2}.

Note that the values we chose for
\sigma
and
\widebar{\tau}
are appropriate given our calculations in this section. For decreasing
\sigma
, we would like for
p(y)
to grow closer to
N_{f}
in Lemma
9
and for
C_{f}
to tend to zero in Lemma
10
; we also need
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2e^{-{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}-\sqrt{d/(2\pi)}}}\right)}^{2}}\pi}}}\right)
, the Gaussian concentration probability, to grow closer to 1 in the previous lemma. Our choice of
\widebar{\tau}:=\sigma^{5/6}
is appropriate given these constraints. For
\sigma
small enough,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2e^{-{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}-\sqrt{d/(2\pi)}}}\right)}^{2}}\pi}}}\right)e^{-C_{f}}\approx 1
and
{\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}}\right)}\bigg/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{z}}}\right)}\times\widehat{V}}}\right)\approx{1}/{\widehat{V}}\approx{\sigma^{d}}/{V}
. Thus,
K_{1}\approx e^{-1/2}{\sigma^{d}}/{V}
and
K_{2}\approx{\sigma^{d}}/{V}
.

3.4
Finite sample bounds for
p_{N}
and
\partial^{\alpha}(-\log p_{N}(x))

In Lemma
11
, we proved a statement about
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}}\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)
whereas we really need a statement about
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}}p_{N}(x)
. We can use methods from empirical processes to relate these quantities. Let
\mathcal{F}
consist of functions
f:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow[0,1]
where each
f
has the form
e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}}
with
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
. Here,
x
is fixed, and each
x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
corresponds to a different
f
. Note that
p_{N}(x)
is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}_{N}f
and
\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)
is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}f
. We have the tools to prove that for
0<\delta<1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{N}f-\mathbb{E}f}}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon_{1}}}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

where
\varepsilon_{1}
is a function of
\delta
and
N
. We rewrite the form of the probability bound in part (a) of Lemma
12
so that it is in terms of
p_{N}
and
\mathbb{E}p_{N}
.

In part (b) of Lemma
12
, we prove a similar concentration bound for particular derivatives of
e^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}}p_{N}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\beta,v}
consist of functions
f:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
where each
f
is of the form
\partial^{\beta}_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}}}\right)e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}
with
x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
,
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
, and
v\in B_{n}(0,1)
. These functions are involved in finding an upper bound for
\partial^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{N}(x)}}\right)}}\right)
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the class of functions consisting of
e^{-\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}}
indexed by
x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
. For a given
\beta
and
v\in B_{n}(0,1)
, let
\mathcal{F}_{\beta,v}
be the class of functions consisting of
\partial^{\beta}_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}}}\right)e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}
indexed by
x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
.

(a)

For
0<\delta<1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert p_{N}(x)-\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon_{1}}}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}:=\frac{24}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi n}}{2}+\sqrt{\log C^{\prime}}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{N}}

and

\displaystyle C^{\prime}:=C\widehat{V}100^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\sqrt{{2\pi}/{e}}}}\right)^{n}.

(b)

For
0<\delta<1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\partial^{\beta}_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}}}\right)e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\partial^{\beta}_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}}}\right)e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}}}\right]}}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon_{1,\beta}}}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1,\beta}:=\frac{24}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi n}}{2}+\sqrt{\log C^{\prime}_{\beta}}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{N}}

and

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{\beta}:=C\widehat{V}100^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\sqrt{5+4\beta}}}\right)^{\beta+1}e^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-1-2\beta+\sqrt{5+4\beta}}}\right)/4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{\pi}}}\right)^{-(\beta+1)/2}}}\right)^{n}.

{proof}

We can bound
\sup_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p_{N}(x)-\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)}}\right|
through a method from empirical processes by first determining the covering number of
\mathcal{F}
and then using Dudley’s integral. Since
\mathcal{F}
is a class of Lipschitz functions parametrized by points in
\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}}
, we can relate its covering number to the covering number of this parameter space.

From empirical process theory, we know that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert p_{N}(x)-\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq 2\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F})+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{N}}}}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F})
is the Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}
, which can be bounded using Theorem
7
. Let
\mathcal{N}(\eta,\mathcal{F},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\cdot}}\right\rVert)
be the covering number at scale
\eta
with respect to norm
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\cdot}}\right\rVert
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\geq 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{4\varepsilon^{\prime}+12\int_{\varepsilon^{\prime}/4}^{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f^{2}]}}\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta,\mathcal{F},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\cdot}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(P_{N})}}}\right)}{N}}d\eta}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\geq 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{4\varepsilon^{\prime}+12\int_{\varepsilon^{\prime}/4}^{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F^{\prime}}}\sqrt{\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f^{2}]}}\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}\big(\eta,\mathcal{F},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\cdot}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\big)}{N}}d\eta}}\right\}.

The second inequality is well-known. Each
f\in\mathcal{F}
is parametrized by
x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}}
and is at most
L
-Lipschitz in this parameter. If we can calculate
L
, we can also bound the covering number of
\mathcal{F}
by relating it to the covering number of the tubular neighborhood.

That is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\big(\eta,\mathcal{F},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\cdot}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{N}\big(\eta/L,\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\cdot}}\right\rVert_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C\widehat{V}100^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\eta/L}+1}}\right)^{n},

where the second line follows from taking a 1/100-net of
\mathcal{M}
, placing unit
n
-balls at each net point, and then finding an
\eta/L
-net of those.

Now we find
L
. For simplicity, assume
x
has coordinates
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}}\right)
that have been centered around any point on the manifold. By the symmetry of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert
, we only need to consider one coordinate.

\displaystyle\text{Lip}(f)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla f}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}}}\right)}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=2e^{-x_{1}^{2}\pi}x_{1}\bigg|_{x_{1}={1}/{\sqrt{2\pi}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{e}}

\displaystyle=:L.

Since
\eta
ranges between 0 and 1,
L/\eta>1
. Define
C^{\prime}:=C\widehat{V}100^{d}(2\sqrt{{2\pi}/{e}})^{n}
; then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\big(\eta,\mathcal{F},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\cdot}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\big)\leq C^{\prime}\eta^{-n}.

Using the monotonicity of
\log
and the square root,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\leq 12\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log C^{\prime}}{N}}+\sqrt{\frac{-n\log\eta}{N}}}}\right)d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{12}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi n}}{2}+\sqrt{\log C^{\prime}}}}\right).

Thus, with high probability,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|p_{N}(x)-\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{24}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi n}}{2}+\sqrt{\log C^{\prime}}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{N}},

which proves (a).

The proof of part (b) is nearly the same, with the only difference being in the covering number of the parameter space. If each
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\beta,v}
is at most
L_{\beta}
-Lipschitz, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\big(\eta,\mathcal{F}_{\beta},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\cdot}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\big)\leq C^{\prime}_{\beta}\eta^{-n},

where
C^{\prime}_{\beta}:=C\widehat{V}100^{d}(2L_{\beta})^{n}
. Since

\displaystyle\partial^{\beta}_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}}}\right)e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}=e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}(2\pi)^{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y^{\prime\top}v}}\right)^{\beta}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi},

we have

\displaystyle\text{Lip}(f)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\beta,v}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla f}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},v\in B_{n}(0,1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}}}\right)(2\pi)^{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y^{\prime\top}v}}\right)^{\beta}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},v\in B_{n}(0,1)}(2\pi)^{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y^{\prime\top}v}}\right\rvert^{\beta}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\pi y^{\prime}_{i}e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{1/\sqrt{2\pi}},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},v\in B_{n}(0,1)}(2\pi)^{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y^{\prime\top}v}}\right\rvert^{\beta}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}2\pi e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}(2\pi)^{\beta+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{\beta+1}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi+\sqrt{2\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert}.

In the third line,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y^{\prime}_{1},\dots,y^{\prime}_{n}}}\right\}
are the components of
y^{\prime}
. The final line follows by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which shows that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y^{\prime\top}v}}\right\rvert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert
and
2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}\leq 2\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert
. Differentiating with respect to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert
and setting equal to zero shows that the supremum is achieved at
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert=(1+\sqrt{5+4\beta})/(2\sqrt{2\pi})
. We can substitute this back in to set

\displaystyle L_{\beta}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\sqrt{5+4\beta}}}\right)^{\beta+1}e^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-1-2\beta+\sqrt{5+4\beta}}}\right)/4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{\pi}}}\right)^{-(\beta+1)/2}.

It follows directly that
K_{1}-\varepsilon_{1}\leq p_{N}\leq K_{2}+\varepsilon_{1}
with high probability. For large enough
N
,
K_{1}/2\leq p_{N}\leq 2K_{2}
. In the next lemma, we prove that a corresponding result holds for
\partial^{\alpha}(-\log p_{N}(x))
(which is exactly the second condition from Theorem
5
). The derivation is more technical but the intuition is based on the arguments in Section
3.3
.

Lemma 13
.

\partial^{\alpha}(-\log p_{N}(x))\leq C_{0}
for
x\in B_{n}(0,1/\sqrt{2\pi})
,
|\alpha|\leq k
, and
C_{0}
depending only on
n
and
k
.

{proof}

Start by defining

\displaystyle q_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert z-y_{i}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}e^{-2(x-z)^{\top}(z-y_{i})\pi},

where
z
is the projection of
x
onto
\mathcal{M}
. Then,

\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}(-\log p_{N}(x))

\displaystyle=\partial^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}q_{N}(x)}}\right)}}\right).

A result due to
Nemirovski 2004
shows that

\displaystyle\sup_{|v_{i}|\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\partial_{v_{1}}\dots\partial_{v_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert}}F(x)}}\right\rvert\leq\sup_{|v|\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\partial_{v}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert}F(x)}}\right\rvert

for
C^{k}
-smooth
F
, implying that we do not need to bound mixed partials.

It is straightforward to calculate
C_{0,1}:=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-z}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right\rvert
. (The supremum is also over
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert\leq k
). To get an upper bound for
\partial^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\log q_{N}(x)}}\right)
, we first write it as an expression involving powers of
q_{N}(x)
and partials of
q_{N}(x)
. For example, if
\alpha=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1}}}\right\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{4}(-\log q_{N}(x))}{\partial x_{1}^{4}}

\displaystyle=6\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial q_{N}}{\partial x_{1}}}}\right)^{4}}{q_{N}(x)^{4}}-12\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial q_{N}}{\partial x_{1}}}}\right)^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial^{2}q_{N}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}}}\right)}{q_{N}(x)^{3}}+3\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial^{2}q_{N}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}}}\right)^{2}}{q_{N}(x)^{2}}

\displaystyle+4\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial q_{N}}{\partial x_{1}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial^{3}q_{N}}{\partial x_{1}^{3}}}}\right)}{q_{N}(x)^{2}}-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial^{4}q_{N}}{\partial x_{1}^{4}}}}\right)}{q_{N}(x)}.

Faa di Bruno’s formula is an explicit representation of this expression; the number of terms and the coefficients depend on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert
. We can find a suitable
C_{0}
if we can calculate a lower bound for
q_{N}
and an upper bound for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert{\partial^{\beta}_{v}q_{N}}}}\right\rvert
where
\beta\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert
. The first bound follows from two previous lemmas. Lemma
11
shows that
\mathbb{E}p_{N}(x)\geq K_{1}
, and Lemma
12
shows that
p_{N}
is within
\varepsilon_{1}
of its expectation with high probability. Since
q_{N}(x)\geq p_{N}(x)
and
\varepsilon_{1}
can be made smaller than
K_{1}/2
,
q_{N}\geq K_{1}/2
with high probability for
N
sufficiently large.
K_{1}
is a function of
d
, and
\varepsilon_{1}
is a function of
n
.

To bound the partials of
q_{N}
, we start off by using the second part of Lemma
12
, which shows

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert{\partial^{\beta}_{v}q_{N}}}}\right\rvert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathbb{E}{\partial^{\beta}_{v}q_{N}}}}\right\rvert+\varepsilon_{1,\beta}.

Let
z
be the origin and let the first
d
coordinates lie in
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
. We can write the expectation as

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathbb{E}{\partial^{\beta}_{v}q_{N}}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\int_{\mathcal{M}}\partial^{\beta}_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}}}\right)e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}(2\pi)^{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y^{\prime\top}v}}\right)^{\beta}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z}}e^{2\pi x^{\top}y^{\prime}}(2\pi)^{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y^{\prime\top}v}}\right)^{\beta}e^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{\prime}}}\right\rVert^{2}\pi}d\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime})}}\right\rvert.

4

A manifold
\mathcal{M}

.
Fefferman et al. 2016

and show that it is an asdf when coupled with a suitably constructed cylinder packet. In this section we show that we can estimate tangent spaces directly from the data to create a cylinder packet; this leads to the construction of an approximate squared-distance function that satisfies Theorems
5
and
6

on the order of

for a small value of

Let

be a proper rotation of

,

a translation, and

a composition of a proper rotation and translation that moves the origin to

and rotates the
d
-dimensional cross-section of
\mathrm{cyl}_{i}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define

\displaystyle F^{\bar{o}}(z):=\frac{\sum_{\mathrm{cyl}_{i}\ni z}\phi_{\mathrm{cyl}_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(o_{i}^{-1}(z)}}\right)\theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Pi_{d}(o_{i}^{-1}(z))/(2\widebar{\tau})}}\right)}{\sum_{\mathrm{cyl}_{i}\ni z}\theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Pi_{d}(o_{i}^{-1}(z))/(2\widebar{\tau})}}\right)},

where
\mathrm{cyl}_{i}\in C_{p}
,
z\in\bigcup\mathrm{cyl}_{i}
,
\phi_{\mathrm{cyl}_{i}}(z)
is the squared distance from
z
to the
d
-dimensional cross-section of
\mathrm{cyl}_{i}
and
\theta:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a bump function such that

1.

\theta(y)=0
for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert\notin(-1,1)

2.

\partial^{\alpha}\theta(y)=0
for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert\leq k
and
y=0
or
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert\notin(-1,1)

3.

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}\theta(y)}}\right\rvert<C
, a controlled constant for all
y

4.

\theta(y)=1
for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert<1/4
.

Note that whether or not
F^{\bar{o}}(z)
satisfies Theorem
5
depends on our choice of
C_{p}
; for convenience, we refer to the pair
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F^{\bar{o}},C_{p}}}\right\}
as a putative asdf.
F^{\bar{o}}(z)
measures the squared distances
\phi_{\mathrm{cyl}_{i}}
to the central cross-section of each cylinder containing a given point
z
, and averages them using the bump function
\theta
. Let
\widehat{F}_{z}:B_{n}(0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a related function defined by
\widehat{F}_{z}(w)=F^{\bar{o}}(z+\widebar{\tau}\Theta(w))/\widebar{\tau}^{2}
, where
\Theta
is an isometry that fixes the origin at
z
and identifies the first
d
coordinates with
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
.
\widehat{F}_{z}
is essentially
F^{\bar{o}}
analyzed in a coordinate system scaled up by
1/\widebar{\tau}
. This is analogous to our analysis of the kernel density estimator in the previous section, where we scaled the coordinate system by
1/\sigma
.

4.2
Cylinder packets

In order for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F^{\bar{o}},C_{p}}}\right\}
to be an asdf,
C_{p}
needs to be a cylinder packet, which is a collection of cylinders that satisfies the geometric constraints given below in Definition
17
. These conditions ensure that a cylinder packet doesn’t contain pairs of cylinders that overlap too much or intersect at too great of an angle. This is motivated by our desire to estimate a manifold with bounded reach.

Definition 17

(Cylinder packet)
.

Let
C_{p}
be a collection of cylinders as above.
C_{p}
is a cylinder packet if it satisfies the following conditions:

1.

The number of cylinders is less than or equal to a constant factor times
\frac{V}{\tau^{d}}
.

2.

Consider the set
S_{i}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{cyl}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathrm{cyl}_{i_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert S_{i}}}\right\rvert}}}}\right\}
of cylinders that intersect
\mathrm{cyl}_{i}
and perform the rigid-body motion
o_{i}
. For each
\mathrm{cyl}_{i_{j}}
, there exists a translation
Tr_{i_{j}}
and a proper rotation
U_{i_{j}}
fixing
x_{i_{j}}
so that

(a)

For
1\leq j\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert S_{i}}}\right\rvert
,
Tr_{i_{j}}U_{i_{j}}\mathrm{cyl}_{i_{j}}
is a translation of
\mathrm{cyl}_{i}
by a vector with norm at least
\widebar{\tau}/3
.

(b)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{i}}}\right\}\bigcup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Tr_{i_{j}}U_{i_{j}}x_{i_{j}}\;\big|\;1\leq j\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert S_{i}}}\right\rvert}}\right\}\bigcap\mathrm{cyl}_{i}
forms a
\widebar{\tau}/2-
net of the
d
-dimensional cross-section of
\mathrm{cyl}_{i}
.

(c)

For
1\leq j\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert S_{i}}}\right\rvert
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v-U_{i_{j}}v}}\right\rVert<2\frac{\widebar{\tau}}{\tau}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v-x_{i_{j}}}}\right\rVert
.

(d)

For
1\leq j\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert S_{i}}}\right\rvert
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert Tr_{i_{j}}(0)}}\right\rVert<\frac{\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{\tau}
.

In Lemmas 16 and 17 due to
Fefferman et al. 2016
, it is shown that
\widehat{F}_{z}
satisfies Theorem
5
when
C_{p}
is a cylinder packet, meaning that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F^{\bar{o}},C_{p}}}\right\}
is an asdf. We include this towards the end of this section as Theorem
23
and provide a sketch of the proof.

In the next lemma we construct a collection of cylinders
C_{p}^{Tan}
whose central cross sections are derived from the tangent planes of the manifold and show that it is indeed a cylinder packet. The putative manifold actually has reach
c\widebar{\tau}
, so the right-hand sides of conditions 2(c) and (d) in Definition
17
can be within a constant factor of what is given above.

Lemma 18
.

First, construct a set
\{x_{i}\}
of centers. Assume the sample size is large enough to contain a
\widebar{\tau}/2-
net of
\mathcal{M}
such that no two net points are within
\widebar{\tau}/2.9
of each other. Let
C_{p}^{Tan}
be the collection of cylinders with centers
\{x_{i}\}
and central cross sections contained in
T_{x_{i}}\mathcal{M}
. Then,
C_{p}^{Tan}
is a cylinder packet; we call it an ideal cylinder packet.

{proof}

We show that the conditions in Definition
17
hold. Fix an
x_{i}
and consider the set
S_{i}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{cyl}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathrm{cyl}_{i_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert S_{i}}}\right\rvert}}}}\right\}
of cylinders that intersect
\mathrm{cyl}_{i}
. Perform the rigid-body motion
o_{i}
so that we are working in a convenient coordinate system. For each
x_{i_{j}}\in S_{i}
, define
U_{i_{j}}
as a rotation fixing
x_{i_{j}}
and rotating the central cross section of
\mathrm{cyl}_{i_{j}}
so that it is parallel to
T_{x_{i}}\mathcal{M}
. Also, define
Tr_{i_{j}}
as the translation that subsequently moves the central cross section so that it lies in
T_{x_{i}}\mathcal{M}
. Lemma
4
implies the first condition.

Let
p
be the projection of
x_{i_{j}}
onto
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
. Federer’s reach condition implies that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{i_{j}}-p}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{i_{j}}-x_{i}}}\right\rVert^{2}}{2\tau}.

Condition 2(d) holds since the right hand side must be less than
4\widebar{\tau}^{2}/\tau
. Since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{i_{j}}-x_{i}}}\right\rVert\geq\widebar{\tau}/2.9
, we also have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{i}-p}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{i_{j}}-x_{i}}}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{i_{j}}-x_{i}}}\right\rVert^{4}}{4\tau^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{100\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{841}-\frac{2500\widebar{\tau}^{4}}{707281\tau^{2}}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{10\widebar{\tau}}{29}-\frac{125\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{24389\tau^{2}}+O(\widebar{\tau}^{4}),

where the last line follows by a Taylor expansion. Since this can be made arbitrarily close to
\widebar{\tau}/2.9
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{i}-p}}\right\rVert\geq\widebar{\tau}/3
and Condition 2(a) is satisfied. Condition 2(b) follows from the fact that we started off with a
\widebar{\tau}/2-
net of the manifold; projecting
\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert S_{i}}}\right\rvert}}\}
onto
T_{x_{i}}\mathcal{M}
contracts interpoint distances so we end up with a
\widebar{\tau}/2-
net of the tangent space.

To show the bound in 2(c), we need an expression for the angle between two nearby tangent spaces (in this case
T_{x_{i}}\mathcal{M}
and
T_{x_{i_{j}}}\mathcal{M}
). In Lemma B.3 from a paper by
Boissonnat et al. 2013
, it is shown that the sine of the largest principal angle
\theta_{1}
between
T_{x_{i}}\mathcal{M}
and
T_{x_{i_{j}}}\mathcal{M}
is less than or equal to
6\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{i}-x_{i_{j}}}}\right\rVert/\tau
, which is
12\sqrt{2}\widebar{\tau}/\tau
in our setup. Now, translate the origin to
x_{i_{j}}
and translate
T_{x_{i}}\mathcal{M}
so that it contains
x_{i_{j}}
. Without loss of generality, let
v\in T_{x_{i_{j}}}\mathcal{M}
. Let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e_{i}}}\right\}_{1}^{d}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\widehat{e}_{i}}}}\right\}_{1}^{d}
be orthonormal bases for
T_{x_{i_{j}}}
and
T_{x_{i}}
, respectively, so that the angle between
e_{i}
and
\widehat{e}_{i}
is the principal angle
\theta_{i}
. Define
U_{i_{j}}
as the rotation that maps
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e_{i}}}\right\}_{1}^{d}
onto
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\widehat{e}_{i}}}}\right\}_{1}^{d}
. Let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v_{i}}}\right\}_{1}^{d}
be the components of
v
and
U_{i_{j}}v
with respect to the appropriate bases. Then we have the following:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v-U_{i_{j}}v}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}e_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}\widehat{e}_{i}}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v_{i}e_{i}-v_{i}\widehat{e}_{i}}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v_{i}}}\right\rvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert e_{i}-\widehat{e}_{i}}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert e_{1}-\widehat{e}_{1}}}\right\rVert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert e_{1}-\widehat{e}_{1}}}\right\rVert\sqrt{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert.

Using the law of cosines,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert e_{i}-\widehat{e}_{i}}}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{2-2\cos\theta_{i}}
. From the bound on
\sin\theta_{1}
and a Taylor expansion of
\cos\arcsin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(12\sqrt{2}\widebar{\tau}/\tau}}\right)
, we can show

\displaystyle 2-2\cos\theta_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{288\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}+O(\widebar{\tau}^{4})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{576\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}

for large enough
N
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v-U_{i_{j}}v}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{24\sqrt{d}\widebar{\tau}}{\tau}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert,

which shows 2(c).

Corollary 19
.

First, construct a set
\{x_{i}\}
of centers. Assume the sample size is large enough to contain a
\widebar{\tau}/2-
net of
\mathcal{M}
such that no two net points are within
\widebar{\tau}/2.9
of each other. The collection of cylinders with centers
\{x_{i}\}
and central cross sections within
O(\widebar{\tau}/\tau)
of
T_{x_{i}}\mathcal{M}
in operator norm is a cylinder packet; we call it an admissible cylinder packet.

4.3
Constructing an admissible cylinder packet with local PCA

Usually we only have access to points sampled from the manifold and not their associated tangent spaces. It is easy to see that we can also construct a cylinder packet if we can estimate the tangent spaces accurately enough (as stated in Corollary
19
). Let
C_{p}^{\widehat{Tan}}
be a collection of cylinders constructed by using the same net points as in Lemma
18
and performing local PCA to estimate the
d
-dimensional cross-sections. In this section, we show that
C_{p}^{\widehat{Tan}}
is an admissible cylinder packet. The
d
-dimensional cross-sections are estimated as follows. Given a sample point
z\in\mathcal{M}
, we construct the PCA matrix
N_{z}^{-1}YY^{\top}
, where
Y
has columns consisting of the
N_{z}
sample points lying within
\widebar{\tau}(B_{d}\times B_{n-d})
. (We are using a coordinate system centered at
z
whose first
d
coordinates lie in
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
). Using the eigenvectors of
N_{z}^{-1}YY^{\top}
, we can get an estimate
\widehat{T_{z}\mathcal{M}}
of the tangent space at
z
. We show that this is close to the true tangent space by using the Davis-Kahan
\sin\theta
theorem. The version stated below is due to
Yu et al. 2015
.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denote the Frobenius norm of a matrix. Suppose
V,\widehat{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
both have orthonormal columns. Theorem
20
gives an upper bound on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\sin\theta(\widehat{V},V)}}\right\rVert_{F}
, where
\theta(\widehat{V},V)
is the
d\times d
diagonal matrix whose diagonal consists of the principal angles between the column spaces of
V
and
\widehat{V}
and
\sin\theta(\widehat{V},V)
is defined entrywise. The principal angles are given by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\cos^{-1}\zeta_{1},\dots,\cos^{-1}\zeta_{d}}}\right\}
, where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{d}}}\right\}
are the singular values of

\widehat{V}^{T}V
.

Theorem 20

(Davis-Kahan
\sin\theta
Theorem)
.

Let
\Lambda,\widehat{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric, with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\widehat{\lambda}_{n}
respectively. Let
1\leq d\leq n
and assume
\lambda_{d}-\lambda_{d+1}>0
. Let
V=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\widehat{V}=(\widehat{v}_{1},\dots,\widehat{v}_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
have orthonormal columns satisfying
\Lambda v_{j}=\lambda_{j}v_{j}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}\widehat{v}_{j}=\widehat{\lambda}_{j}\widehat{v}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,d
. Then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\sin\theta(\widehat{V},V)}}\right\rVert_{F}\leq\frac{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\widehat{\Lambda}-\Lambda}}\right\rVert_{F}}{\lambda_{d}-\lambda_{d+1}}.

We also make use of the following concentration inequality due to
Ahlswede and Winter 2002
. Let
A\preceq B
mean that
A-B
is positive semidefinite.

Theorem 21
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
be i.i.d. random positive semidefinite
d\times d
matrices with expected value
\mathbb{E}[a_{i}]=M\succeq\mu I
and
a_{i}\preceq I
. Then for all
\epsilon\in[0,1/2]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\not\in[(1-\epsilon)M,(1+\epsilon)M]}}\right]\leq 2D\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{-\epsilon^{2}\mu k}{2\ln 2}}}\right\}.

We now prove the key result of this section.

Theorem 22
.

Let
z
be a point sampled from
\mathcal{M}
. Translate
z
to the origin, and let the first
d
coordinates lie in
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
. Let
Y
be a matrix whose columns consist of the
N_{z}
sample points
\{y_{i}\}
lying within
\widebar{\tau}(B_{d}\times B_{n-d})
. Let
X
be a matrix whose columns are the projections
\{x_{i}\}
of
\{y_{i}\}
onto
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
. Construct the matrices
N_{z}^{-1}XX^{\top}
and
N_{z}^{-1}YY^{\top}
, and let
V
and
\widehat{V}
be their respective matrices of eigenvectors. Then, w.h.p.,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\sin\theta(\widehat{V},V)}}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\widebar{\tau}^{3}}{\tau}+\frac{2\widebar{\tau}^{4}}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)(d+2)}{(1-\epsilon)\widebar{\tau}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{-d/2}},

where
\epsilon\in[0,1/2]
.

{proof}

Clearly,
N_{z}
increases with
N
. We can assume the matrices in the statement of the theorem can be defined. We apply Theorem
20
with
N_{z}^{-1}XX^{\top}
and
N_{z}^{-1}YY^{\top}
corresponding to
\Lambda
and
\widehat{\Lambda}
, respectively.

We start off by bounding the numerator
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|N_{z}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(YY^{\top}-XX^{\top}}}\right)}}\right\|_{F}
. This is easiest if we consider
Y
as a perturbation of
X
by the matrix
P
since we can control
P
using Federer’s reach condition. This gives:

\displaystyle YY^{\top}

\displaystyle=(X+P)(X+P)^{\top}

\displaystyle=XX^{\top}+XP^{\top}+PX^{\top}+PP^{\top}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|N_{z}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(YY^{\top}-XX^{\top}}}\right)}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq N_{z}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|XP^{\top}+PX^{\top}+PP^{\top}}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq N_{z}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|XP^{\top}}}\right\|_{F}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|PX^{\top}}}\right\|_{F}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|PP^{\top}}}\right\|_{F}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq N_{z}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X}}\right\|_{F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P^{\top}}}\right\|_{F}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P}}\right\|_{F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}}}\right\|_{F}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P}}\right\|_{F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P^{\top}}}\right\|_{F}}}\right).

Because each column of X has norm less than or equal to
\widebar{\tau}
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert X}}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{N_{z}}\widebar{\tau}
. By Federer’s reach condition, we have

\displaystyle|y_{i}-x_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|z-y_{i}|^{2}}{2\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{2}\widebar{\tau}}}\right)^{2}}{2\tau},

which implies that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert P}}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{N_{z}{\widebar{\tau}^{4}}/({\tau^{2}})}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|N_{z}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(YY^{\top}-XX^{\top}}}\right)}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widebar{\tau}^{3}}{\tau}+\frac{\widebar{\tau}^{4}}{\tau^{2}}.

Now we need to bound
\lambda_{d}-\lambda_{d+1}
. Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
N_{z}^{-1}XX^{\top}
, and let
\mu_{1}\geq\dots\mu_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
M:=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[N_{z}^{-1}XX^{\top}}}\right]
. We see that
\lambda_{d+1}=\dots=\lambda_{n}=\mu_{d+1}=\dots=\mu_{n}=0
. So, we only need a lower bound for
\lambda_{d}
, which we can get by relating its value to
\mu_{d}
through a concentration inequality. Assuming the first
d
coordinates are aligned with the eigenvectors of
M
,
\mu_{d}
is the variance in the direction
x_{d}
.
M
is the population covariance matrix of the probability measure
\mathcal{P}
on
T_{z}\mathcal{M}\cap B_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widebar{\tau}}}\right)
that is the pushforward of the uniform measure on
\mathcal{M}\cap\widebar{\tau}(B_{d}\times B_{n-d})
. From Lemma
9
, we know
\mathcal{P}
has a density
p(x)
that is greater than or equal to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+{C^{2}\widebar{\tau}^{2}}/{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{-d/2}
multiplied by a normalizing factor, which in this case is just
\mathrm{Vol}(B_{d}(\widebar{\tau}))^{-1}
.

We have the following bound for
\mu_{d}
:

\displaystyle\mu_{d}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{d}(\widebar{\tau})}x_{d}^{2}d\mathcal{P}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B_{d}(\widebar{\tau})}x_{d}^{2}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{-d/2}}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{d}(\widebar{\tau}))}d\mathcal{L}_{d}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{{\widebar{\tau}}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\dots\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r\sin{\phi_{d-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin{\phi_{j}}}}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{-d/2}}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{d}(\widebar{\tau}))}dV

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(d/2+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{-d/2}}{\pi^{d/2}\widebar{\tau}^{d}}\int_{0}^{{\widebar{\tau}}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\dots\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}r^{d+1}\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\sin^{d-j+1}{\phi_{j}}dr\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}d\phi_{j}.

The third line follows by a change of coordinates. Substitute

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{1}\mapsto r\cos{\phi_{1}},x_{2\leq i\leq d-1}\mapsto r\cos{\phi_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\sin{\phi_{j}},x_{d}\mapsto r\sin{\phi_{d-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin{\phi_{j}}}}\right\},

and let

\displaystyle dV:=r^{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin^{d-j-1}{\phi_{j}}drd\phi_{1}\dots d\phi_{d-1}.

The integral in the fourth line can be evaluated by noting that
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\widebar{\tau}}r^{d+1}dr={\widebar{\tau}^{d+2}}\bigg/{(d+2)}
,
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}\phi_{d-1}d\phi_{d-1}=\pi
, and
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d-j+1}\phi_{j}d\phi_{j}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((d-j+2)/2}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+(d-j+1)/2}}\right)}
for
1\leq j\leq d-2
. We can simplify (by telescoping)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((d-j+2)/2}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+(d-j+1)/2}}\right)}=\frac{\pi^{(d-2)/2}}{\Gamma(1+d/2)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu_{d}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{d+2}\widebar{\tau}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{C^{2}\widebar{\tau}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{-d/2}.

N_{z}^{-1}XX^{\top}
and
M
are zero outside the upper left
d\times d
block. Call their nonzero blocks
\Xi
and
\widetilde{\Xi}
, respectively; clearly these matrices have eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{1}^{d}
and
\{\mu_{i}\}_{1}^{d}
.
\Xi
can be written as the empirical average
{N_{z}}^{-1}\sum_{1=1}^{N_{z}}x_{i,d}x_{i,d}^{\top}
, where the
\{x_{i,d}\}
are the first
d
coordinates of the
\{x_{i}\}
. Note that
x_{i,d}x_{i,d}^{\top}\preceq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x_{i,d}x_{i,d}^{\top}}}\right\|_{F}I\preceq\widebar{\tau}^{2}I
. For
N_{z}
large enough, this implies
x_{i,d}x_{i,d}^{\top}\preceq I
. Additionally, since
\mu_{d}>0
is the smallest eigenvalue of
\widetilde{\Xi}
, we have
\widetilde{\Xi}\succeq\mu_{d}I
. This is sufficient to apply Theorem
21
. So, for all
\epsilon\in[0,1/2]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Xi\notin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(1-\epsilon)\widetilde{\Xi},(1+\epsilon)\widetilde{\Xi}}}\right]}}\right]\leq 2d\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\mu_{d}N_{z}}{2\log 2}}}\right\}.

The matrix interval is in terms of the positive semidefinite ordering, so
\Xi\succeq(1-\epsilon)\widetilde{\Xi}
w.h.p. This implies
\lambda_{d}\geq(1-\epsilon)\mu_{d}

In this section, we present simulation results showing that the two asdfs considered in this paper can be used to find a discretized version of a putative manifold. All simulations were performed using the following gradient descent algorithm based on subspace-constrained mean shift
(

)
.

1.

Initialize a mesh of points on which to perform gradient descent. They can be sample points with or without added noise.

2.

(a)

Calculate the gradient

and the Hessian

f

(b)

Let

(c)

Calculate

6
Discussion

In this paper, we showed that if we are provided with data sampled from a manifold
\mathcal{M}
, we can use two different asdfs to construct an estimator of
\mathcal{M}
. The asdfs are based on kernel density estimation and local PCA, which are conceptually easy to understand and mainstays of nonparametric estimation. The estimator is a manifold itself, and there are concrete bounds on its geometry (for example, its reach). These bounds are derived from an application of the implicit function theorem and are given in a key theorem of
Fefferman et al. 2016
. Our contribution in this paper is to create asdfs that can be calculated directly from the data as well as to give bounds on the reach and Hausdorff distance that depend on the sample size and properties of the asdfs. In the future, we aim to work on several natural extensions of our results. It remains to be seen what can be said about an estimator derived from a sample contaminated with noise (potentially bounded or sub-Gaussian). Additionally, it would be of theoretical interest to see how precise we can make the constants in this paper, including the ones derived from
Fefferman et al. 2016
.
