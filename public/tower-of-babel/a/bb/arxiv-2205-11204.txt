Introduction

In 1990 we contributed a paper to the book

Open problems in Topology
,
[
139
]
, titled

Open problems on
\beta\omega
(
[
67
]
).

Through the years some of these problems were solved, some were shown to be
related to other problems, and some are still unsolved.
In the first years after the publication of the book there were regular
updates on the problems in the journal

Topology and its Applications
;
in 2004 these were collated and extended in a comprehensive status report,

[
97
]
,
by Elliott Pearl.

The COVID-19 pandemic provided a good opportunity to go through our original
paper again and provide a new update of the status of the problems as well as
to collect and formulate new questions on the fascinating object that
is
\beta\N
.

Many of the comments below incorporate information from Elliott Pearl’s
update mentioned above, but there have, of course, been many developments
in the years since.

The numbering of the problems is different from that in the first paper
because we have moved some questions around and combined related questions
into more comprehensive problems.
We have made no attempt to separate the solved problems from the unsolved ones.
We wanted to keep related problems together and even though we consider a
problem solved the reader may disagree and be inspired to investigate
variations or strengthenings of the answers.

We should also mention the book

Open problems in Topology II

[
98
]
,
edited by Elliot Pearl, that contains many more problems in topology,
and in particular a paper by Peter Nyikos,

Čech-Stone remainders of discrete spaces
,
that, as the title indicates, deals with problems on
\beta\kappa
for
arbitrary infinite cardinals
\kappa
.

1.
Preliminaries

The main objects of study in this paper are the space
\beta\N
and its
subspace
\N^{*}
.

For a quick overview of their properties we refer to Chapter D-18
of
[
66
]
; the paper
[
142
]
offers a more comprehensive
treatment.
Here we collect some of the basic facts about our spaces in order to fix
some notation that will be used throughout the paper.

To begin:
\beta\N
is the set of ultrafilters on the set
\N
of natural
numbers, endowed with the topology generated by the base

\{\overline{A}:A\subseteq\N\}
, where
\overline{A}
denotes the set of ultrafilters
that contain
A
.
The readily established equality

\beta\N\setminus\overline{A}=\overline{\N\setminus A}

confirms what the notation
\overline{A}
suggests:
the set
\overline{A}
is open
and
closed, and also equal to the closure
of
A
in
\beta\N
.

We identify an element
n
of
\N
with the ultrafilter
\{A:n\in A\}
and
thus consider
\N
to be a subset of
\beta\N
.
The complement
\beta\N\setminus\N
is the set of
free
ultrafilters
on
\N
and is denoted
\N^{*}
; we extend this notation to all subsets
of
\N
and write
A^{*}=\overline{A}\setminus A
whenever
A\subseteq\N
.

A map
\varphi
from
\N
to itself induces a map
\beta\varphi

from
\beta\N
to itself:
\beta\varphi(u)
is the ultrafilter
generated by
\{\varphi[A]:A\in u\}
.

2.
Autohomeomorphisms

The autohomeomorphisms of
\beta\N
correspond to the permutations of
\N
and
are, as such, not very interesting topologically.
The autohomeomorphisms of
\N^{*}
offer more challenges.

In what follows
\mathsf{Aut}
denotes the autohomeomorphism group of
\N^{*}
,
and
\mathsf{Triv}
denotes the subgroup of trivial autohomeomorphisms.
Here a
trivial
autohomeomorphism is one with an ‘easy’ description:
an autohomeomorphism
h
of
\N^{*}
is trivial if there are co-finite
subsets
A
and
B
of
\N
and a bijection
\varphi:A\to B
such that

h=\varphi^{*}
, where
\varphi^{*}
denotes the restriction of
\beta\varphi

to
\N^{*}
.

1
.

Can
\mathsf{Triv}
be a proper normal subgroup of
\mathsf{Aut}
, and if
yes
what is
(or can be) the structure of the factor group
\mathsf{Aut}/\mathsf{Triv}
;
and if
no
what is (or can be)
[\mathsf{Triv}:\mathsf{Aut}]
?

A very concrete first step would be to investigate what can one say about an
autohomeomorphism
h
that satisfies
h^{-1}\circ\mathsf{Triv}\circ h=\mathsf{Triv}
.

A related question: what is the minimum number of autohomeomorphisms
necessary to add to
\mathsf{Triv}
to get a generating set for
\mathsf{Aut}
?

Of course that number is
0
when
\mathsf{Aut}=\mathsf{Triv}
,
but can it be non-zero and finite?

If
h\in\mathsf{Aut}
then
I(h)
denotes the family of subsets of
\omega

on which
h
is trivial, that is,
A\in I(h)
iff there is a function

h^{\prime}:A\to\omega
such that
h(B^{*})=h^{\prime}[B]^{*}
whenever
B\subseteq A
.

If
I(h)
contains an infinite set then
h
is
somewhere trivial
,
otherwise
h
is totally non-trivial.

The ideal
I(h)
determines an open set
O_{h}
: the union

\bigcup\{A^{*}:A\in I(h)\}
; its complement
F_{h}
is closed and could be
said to be the set of points of
\N^{*}
where
h
is truly
non-trivial.

2
.
Does the existence of a (totally) non-trivial automorphism imply that

\mathsf{Aut}
is simple?

This question asks more than the opposite of question
2
;
a yes answer here would imply a no answer there, but a negative answer
there could go together with a negative answer here.

3
.
Is it consistent with
\mathsf{MA}+\neg\mathsf{CH}
that a totally non-trivial
automorphism exists?

The answer yes.
This was established by Shelah and Steprāns in
[
114
]
.
Consistency is the best one can hope for:
in
[
112
]
Shelah and Steprāns proved that
\mathsf{PFA}
implies
all autohomeomorphisms of
\N^{*}
are trivial; they also indicated how
the implicit large cardinal assumption can be avoided and
use
\diamond
on
\omega_{2}
to capture and eliminate any potential non-trivial
autohomeomorphisms in a countable support iteration of length
\omega_{2}
.
Though not stated explicitly by the authors it is clear that one can modify
the iteration so as to obtain a model that satisfies
\mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}}
as well.
In
[
145
]
Veličković showed that the conjunction
of
\mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}}
and
\mathsf{OCA}
implies that all autohomeomorphisms are trivial.

4
.
Is it consistent to have a non-trivial automorphism, while for every

h\in\mathsf{Aut}
the ideal
I(h)
is the intersection of finitely many prime ideals?

In topological terms: can one have non-trivial autohomeomorphisms but only
very mild ones; the set of points where an autohomeomorphism is truly
non-trivial is always finite.

5
.
Is every ideal
I(h)
a
P
-ideal?

This was asked explicitly in
[
112
]
*Question 2 in case every
autohomeomorphism is somewhere trivial, after it was shown that
\mathsf{PFA}

implies a yes answer.
However, as mentioned above,
\mathsf{PFA}
implies that all autohomeomorphisms
are trivial, so that
I(h)
is, in fact, always an improper ideal.

Of course this question only makes sense in case
I(h)
is not equal
to the ideal of finite sets.
Also, if
every
autohomeomorphism is somewhere trivial then
every
I(h)
is a tall ideal and hence the set of points of non-triviality
is nowhere dense.

Without the additional condition that every autohomeomorphism is somewhere
trivial the answer is consistently negative.
The Continuum Hypothesis lets one construct an autohomeomorphism
h
that is
trivial, in fact the identity, on the members of a partition
\mathcal{A}

of
\omega
into infinite sets, and so that there is a point
u
on the
boundary of
\bigcup\{A^{*}:A\in\mathcal{A}\}
such that
h
is not trivial on
each neighbourhood of
u
.
This implies there is no
B\in I(h)
such that
A\subseteq B

for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
.

6
.
If every automorphism is somewhere trivial, is then every automorphism
trivial?

This is undecidable.

Shelah proved the consistency of “all autohomeomorphisms are trivial”,
see
[
117
]
.
Shelah and Steprāns proved the consistency with
\mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}}

of “every autohomeomorphism is somewhere trivial, yet there is a non-trivial
autohomeomorphism” in
[
113
]
;
as noted above they proved in
[
114
]
that
\mathsf{MA}
does not imply
that all autohomeomorphisms are somewhere trivial.

Given a cardinal
\kappa
call an autohomeomorphism
h

weakly
\kappa
-trivial
if the set

\{p:p\not\mathrel{\equiv_{\mathbf{RK}}}h(p)\}
has cardinality less than
\kappa
.
Here
p\mathrel{\equiv_{\mathbf{RK}}}q
means that
p
and
q
have the same type, i.e.,

q=\pi^{*}(p)
for some permutation
\pi
of
\N
.

7
.
For what cardinals
\kappa
is it consistent to have that all
autohomeomorphisms are weakly
\kappa
-trivial?

Since a trivial autohomeomorphism is weakly
1
-trivial we see
that
\kappa=1
is a possibility.
And of course the candidates are less than or equal to
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
.

8
.
If
h
is weakly
1
-trivial is
h
then trivial?

This is a uniformization question: if for every
p\in\N^{*}

there is a permutation
\pi_{p}
such that
h(p)=\pi_{p}^{*}(p)
is there then
one (almost) permutation
\pi
of
\N^{*}
such that
h(p)=\pi^{*}(p)
for
all
p
?

9
.
(
\mathsf{MA}+\lnot\mathsf{CH}
)
if
p
and
q
are
P_{\mathfrak{c}}
-points
is there an
h
in
\mathsf{Aut}
such that
h(p)=q
?

This is undecidable.

Shelah and Steprāns proved the consistency of
\mathsf{MA}+\lnot\mathsf{CH}
with
“all autohomeomorphisms are trivial”
in
[
112
]
; in this model there are
\mathfrak{c}
many autohomeomorphisms
and
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
many
P_{\mathfrak{c}}
-points.

Steprāns proved the consistency of a positive answer in
[
126
]
.

In the investigations into the previous question the following
equivalence relation was used:
p\equiv q
means that there are two partitions

\{A_{n}:n\in\omega\}
and
\{B_{n}:n\in\omega\}
of
\N
into finite sets such that

(\forall P\in p)(\exists Q\in q)(\forall n)\bigl(\mathopen{|}P\cap A_{n}\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}Q\cap B_{n}\mathclose{|}\bigr)

The following question was left open.

10
.
Is
\equiv
different from
\mathrel{\equiv_{\mathbf{RK}}}
in
\mathsf{ZFC}
?

11
.
Are the autohomeomorphisms of
\N^{*}
induced by the shift map

\sigma:n\mapsto n+1
and by its inverse conjugate?

Recently Will Brian showed that the answer to this question is affirmative
assuming
\mathsf{CH}
, see
[
17
]
.

See
[
135
,
18
]
for earlier results.
Under
\mathsf{CH}
the autohomeomorphism group of
\N^{*}
is simple, yet it has
the maximum possible number of conjugacy classes:
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
.
This suggests questions about the number and nature of conjugacy classes
of this group, in
\mathsf{ZFC}
or under various familiar extra set-theoretical
assumptions, see also
[
68
]
.

12
.
Does
\N^{*}
have a universal autohomeomorphism?

This is a question with many possible variations.
The definition of universality that we adopt here is as follows:

f:\N^{*}\to\N^{*}
is universal if for every closed subspace
F
of
\N^{*}

and every autohomeomorphism
g
of
F
there is an embedding
e:F\to\N^{*}

such that
g=e^{-1}\circ(f\restr e[F])\circ e
.

One can ask whether there is a universal autohomeomorphism at all,
whether the shift
\sigma
is universal (for autohomeomorphisms without
fixed points),
whether there is a universal autohomeomorphism just for autohomeomorphisms
without fixed points.

The authors have shown that there is a universal autohomeomorphism of
\N^{*}

under
\mathsf{CH}
and that there is no trivial universal autohomeomorphism.
See
[
69
]
.

We also note that
\N
has a universal permutation:
take a permutation of
\N
that has infinitely many
n
-cycles, for every
n
,
and infinitely many infinite cycles (copies of
\Z
with the shift).
Every other permutation of
\N
can be embedded into this one.

3.
Subspaces

13
.
For what
p
are
\N^{*}\setminus\{p\}

and
\beta\N\setminus\{p\}
non-normal?

Originally this question had the word ‘equivalently’ after the ‘and’
(in parentheses).
Since
\N^{*}\setminus\{p\}
is closed in
\beta\N\setminus\{p\}
there is an
implication between the non-normality of these spaces but we do not know
whether that implication is reversible.
Thus this question may actually be two separate ones.

Under
\mathsf{CH}
the answer is, in both cases, “for every point”,
see
[
32
,
103
,
147
,
143
]
.
There are some results for some special types of points,
see, e.g., Błaszczyk and Szymański
[
27
]
,
Gryzlov
[
64
]
, and Logunev
[
87
]
, but
a general answer is wanting.

14
.
Is it consistent that there is a non-butterfly
point in
\N^{*}
?

We call
p
a butterfly point if there are disjoint sets
A
and
B
such
that
p
is the only common accumulation point of
A
and
B
,
that is:
A^{d}\cap B^{d}=\{p\}
.

The points used by Błaszczyk and Szymański
[
27
]

in their (partial) answer to the previous question are easy-to-describe
butterfly points.
Let
X=\{x_{n}:n\in\omega\}
be a discrete subset of
\N^{*}
,
let
A=\operatorname{cl}X\setminus X
and take
p\in A
.
Let
\{B_{n}:n\in\omega\}
be a partition of
\N
such that
B_{n}\in x_{n}
for
all
n
and let
q
be the ultrafilter
\{Q:p\in\operatorname{cl}\{x_{n}:n\in Q\}\}
.
The set
B=\bigcap_{Q\in q}\bigl(\bigcup\{B_{n}:n\in Q\}\bigr)^{*}
is closed
and
\{q\}=A^{d}\cap B^{d}
.
Thus butterfly points exist.

By contrast, in
[
14
]
Bešlagić and Van Douwen showed that
it is consistent with all consistent cardinal arithmetic that all points
of
\N^{*}
are butterfly points.

15
.
Is it consistent that
\N^{*}\setminus\{p\}
is
C^{*}
-embedded in
\N^{*}

for some but not all
p\in\N^{*}
?

The answer is yes: in
[
51
]
Alan Dow showed that
in the Miller model
\N^{*}\setminus\{p\}
is
C^{*}
-embedded iff
p
is not
a
P
-point.
There are
P
-points in the Miller model: every ground-model
P
-point
generates a
P
-point in the extension.

16
.
What spaces can be embedded in
\beta\omega
?

This is a very general question and a definitive answer looks out of reach
for now, even for closed subspaces.

The Continuum Hypothesis implies that the closed subspaces of
\beta\omega

are exactly the compact zero-dimensional
F
-spaces; in fact, these are also
exactly the closed
P
-sets in
\N^{*}
.
The implication does not reverse: in
[
37
]
it is shown that
every compact zero-dimensional
F
-space is a (closed) subspace of
\N^{*}

in any model obtained by adding
\aleph_{2}
many Cohen reals to a
model of
\mathsf{CH}
.

Dow and Vermeer proved in
[
39
]
that it is consistent that
the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets of the unit interval is not the quotient
of any complete Boolean algebra.
By Stone duality, this yields a compact basically disconnected space,
hence a compact zero-dimensional
F
-space, of weight
\mathfrak{c}
that cannot
be embedded into any extremally disconnected space, in particular
not into
\beta\N
.

Some
\mathsf{ZFC}
results are available.
For instance: if
X
is a compact space of countable cellularity
that is a continuous image of
\N^{*}
then its projective cover
E(X)

can be embedded in
\N^{*}
as a
\mathfrak{c}
-OK set (a weakening of the notion
of a
P
-set).
This was proved by van Mill in
[
141
]
and applies to all separable
compact extremally disconnected spaces as well as to the projective covers
of Suslin lines and of Bell’s ccc non-separable remainder
[
11
]
.

Van Douwen proved in unpublished work that every
P
-space of weight
\mathfrak{c}

(or less) can be embedded into
\beta\N
.
In fact he proved that for every infinite cardinal
\kappa
every
P
-space
of weight
2^{\kappa}
can be embedded in
\beta\kappa
.
The argument was sketched and extended in
[
44
]
and we summarize
it here for the reader’s convenience.

Let
X
be a
P
-space of weight
2^{\kappa}
and embed it into the Cantor cube

C=2^{2^{\kappa}}
of weight
2^{\kappa}
.
Next consider the projective cover
\pi:E(C)\to C
of this cube.
The Cantor cube is a group under coordinatewise addition modulo
2
, so
for every
p\in C
the map
\lambda_{p}:x\mapsto x+p
is a homeomorphism; this
homeomorphism lifts to a homeomorphism
\Lambda_{p}:E(C)\to E(C)
with the
property that
\pi\circ\Lambda_{p}=\lambda_{p}\circ\pi
.
Now take one point
u_{0}\in E(C)
that maps to the neutral element
0
of
C

and consider the subspace
X^{\prime}=\{\Lambda_{p}(u_{0}):p\in X\}
of
E(C)
.
Using the fact that regular open sets in
C
are, up to permutation of the
coordinates, of the form
U\times 2^{I}
where
U
is regular open
in the Cantor set
2^{\omega}
and
I=2^{\kappa}\setminus\omega
, one shows
that
\pi
is actually a homeomorphism from
X^{\prime}
to
X
.
Finally then, as
\pi
is irreducible and
C
has density
\kappa
,
the density of
E(C)
is equal to
\kappa
as well.
Therefore there is a continuous surjection
f:\beta\kappa\to E(C)
and one
can take a closed subset
F
of
\beta\kappa
such that
f\restr F
is
irreducible and onto.
As
E(C)
is extremally disconnected this restriction is a homeomorphism
and we find our copy of
X
in
F
.

The extension in
[
44
]
delivers more but at a cost:
one embeds
\beta X
in a suitable Cantor cube, possibly of a larger
weight than that of
X
itself.
What this delivers is that the copy of
X
in
\beta\lambda

(where
\lambda
may be larger than the
\kappa
above) is
C^{*}
-embedded.

Thus we get the general statement that every
P
-space can be
C^{*}
-embedded
in a compact extremally disconnected space.

This argument also shows that
2^{\aleph_{0}}=2^{\aleph_{1}}
implies that

\beta\omega_{1}
embeds into
\beta\N
.
For
\beta\omega_{1}
embeds into the Cantor cube
2^{2^{\omega_{1}}}
, which under
our assumption is the same as
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
.
The latter is a continuous image of
\beta\N
and an irreducible preimage
of
\beta\omega_{1}
will be homeomorphic to
\beta\omega_{1}
.
If
2^{\aleph_{0}}<2^{\aleph_{1}}
then
\beta\omega_{1}
can not be embedded
into
\N^{*}
because its weight, which is
2^{\aleph_{1}}
, is larger than that
of
\N^{*}
.

17
.
Describe the closed
P
-sets of
\N^{*}
.

This has a quite definitive answer under
\mathsf{CH}
: every compact zero-dimensional

F
-space of weight
\mathfrak{c}
can be embedded in
\N^{*}
as a
P
-set.
What we are looking for are properties that can be established in
\mathsf{ZFC}
,
or provably can not.
For example: one cannot prove in
\mathsf{ZFC}
that there is a
P
-set homeomorphic
to
\N^{*}
itself, see
[
78
]
, or that there is a
P
-set
that satisfies the ccc, see
[
59
]
.

One can ask if cellularity less than
\mathfrak{c}
is at all possible.

There are various nowhere dense closed
P
-sets that one can write down
explicitly.
To give two familiar examples, among many, we consider the density
ideal
\mathcal{I}_{d}
and the summable ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\Sigma}
.
The first is defined by

I\in\mathcal{I}_{d}\text{ \quad iff \quad}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}|A\cap n|=0

and
the second as

I\in\mathcal{I}_{\Sigma}\text{ \quad iff \quad}\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n}\text{ converges.}

These ideals have been studied widely but we would like to know: what are
the topological properties of the nowhere dense closed
P
-sets

F_{d}=\N^{*}\setminus\bigcup\{A^{*}:A\in\mathcal{I}_{d}\}\text{ \quad and \quad}F_{\Sigma}=\N^{*}\setminus\bigcup\{A^{*}:A\in\mathcal{I}_{\Sigma}\}

Rudin established in
[
105
]

that
F_{d}
contains no
P
-points and even that no countable
set of
P
-points accumulates at a point of
F_{d}
.
Indeed let
u
be a
P
-point and observe first that for every
n
there
is an
i_{n}<n
such that
U_{n}=\{m\in\N:m\equiv i_{n}\pmod{n}\}
belongs
to
u
.
Because
u
is a
P
-point there is then a
U\in u
such that
U\subseteq^{*}U_{n}

for all
n
.
But this implies
U\in\mathcal{I}_{d}
, so
u\notin F_{d}
.
Because
F_{d}
is a
P
-set this implies that no countable set of
P
-points
has accumulation points in
F_{d}
.

There are certain similarities between the two sets and
\N^{*}
itself.
Consider the map
f:\N\to\N
, defined by
f(n)=k
iff
k!<n\leq(k+1)!
.
It is an elementary exercise to show that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}|f^{\leftarrow}[X]\cap n|=1\text{ \quad en \quad}\sum_{n\in f^{\leftarrow}[X]}\frac{1}{n}=\infty

whenever
X
is an infinite subset of
\N
.
This implies that
\beta f
maps both
F_{d}
and
F_{\Sigma}
onto
\N^{*}

and it allows for the lifting of many combinatorial structures on
\N^{*}

to these sets.
It is clear that the restriction of
\beta f
to
\N^{*}
is an open map
onto
\N^{*}
itself, whether its restrictions to
F_{d}
and
F_{\Sigma}

are open as well is less clear.

Alan Dow showed in

This is known as the Katowice Problem, or rather the last remaining case of
this problem.
It was posed in full by Marian Turzański, when he was in Katowice
(hence the name of the problem).
The general question is:
if

\lambda

topology, and the remainders

and
\lambda^{*}
are homeomorphic
must the cardinals
\kappa

Since the weight of
\kappa^{*}
is equal to

it is immediate that
the Generalized Continuum Hypothesis implies a yes answer.
In joint work Balcar and Frankiewicz established that the answer is actually
positive without any additional assumptions,

except possibly for the first two infinite cardinals
.
More precisely, see
[

2

If
\langle{\kappa},{\lambda}\rangle\neq\langle{\aleph_{0}},{\aleph_{1}}\rangle

and
\lambda^{*}

are not homeomorphic.

The paper
[

and
\omega_{1}^{*}
being homeomorphic; all but one of these
can be made to hold in a single model of
\mathsf{ZFC}
.

By Stone-duality the Katowice problem can be formulated algebraically:

and
\mathcal{P}(\omega_{1})/\mathrm{fin}
ever isomorphic?
In this form the question even makes sense in
\mathsf{ZF}
: in models without
non-trivial ultrafilters the spaces
\omega_{0}^{*}
and
\omega_{1}^{*}
are empty
(and so trivially homeomorphic) but the structures of the algebras may still
differ.

Investigate ultrafilters as topological spaces.

This is a very general question, so let us discuss some specific ones that
may be investigated.
An ultrafilter can be viewed as a subspace of the Cantor set
\vphantom{2}{}^{\omega}2
,
if one identifies a subset of
\omega
with its characteristic function.

Of course this makes ultrafilters separable metric spaces, and hence
relatively well-behaved.
But not too well-behaved: free ultrafilters are non-measurable and do not
have the property of Baire.

To begin one can repeat many of the investigations into the Rudin-Keisler
order using more general kinds of maps.
We know
p\mathrel{\leq_{\mathbf{RK}}}q
means that there is a map
\varphi:\N\to\N
such
that
\beta\varphi(q)=p
.
The map
\varphi
determines a continuous map from
\vphantom{2}{}^{\omega}2
to itself, so
the following definition suggests itself at once: say
p\leq_{c}q
if there
is a continuous map
f:\vphantom{2}^{\omega}2\to\vphantom{2}^{\omega}2
such that
f[q]=p
.

One can ask whether
p\leq_{c}q
and
q\leq_{c}p
together imply that
p\equiv_{c}q
,
which means that there is a homeomorphism of
\vphantom{2}{}^{\omega}2
that maps
p
to
q
.
The structure of the partial order
\leq_{c}
, minimal elements, incomparable
elements, etc., would warrant investigation as well.

There is no reason to stop there of course: one can ask the same questions
about Borel maps of any specific order, or of maps of arbitrary Baire classes.

One need not work with maps on
\vphantom{2}{}^{\omega}2
, though that may make life easier,
one can investigate what it means for two ultrafilters to be homeomorphic,
or what it means that one is a continuous image of the other.
The methods of
[
50
]
may be of use in determining the possible
sizes of sets of ultrafilters that are incomparable in this sense.

We note that an ultrafilter can be homeomorphic to at most
\mathfrak{c}
many other
ultrafilters: if
f:p\to q
is a homeomorphism then Lavrentieff’s theorem
implies that

can be extended to a homeomorphism of
G_{\delta}
-subsets
of
\vphantom{2}{}^{\omega}2
, and the number of such homeomorphisms is equal to
\mathfrak{c}
.

The paper
[
92
]
contains many results on the topology of
ultrafilters.

This question is related to a conjecture/question of Kaplansky’s about
algebra norms on the spaces

The question is whether every algebra norm is equivalent to the
sup-norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{\infty}

The answer is positive if the norm is complete, hence the question became
whether every algebra norm on

is complete.

The book
[
35
]
surveys the solution to this problem:
under
\mathsf{CH}
every

carries an incomplete algebra norm
(Dales and Esterlé) and it is consistent
that every algebra norm on every

is complete (Solovay and Woodin).

The present question comes from the results that if
C(\beta\N,\C)
admits
an incomplete norm then so does every

if
some

carries an incomplete norm then so

.
In short it asks whether all compact spaces are equivalent for Kaplansky’s
conjecture.

The question can be translated into terms of individual ultrafilters and this
leads to some interesting subquestions.
A seminorm on an algebra is a function that satisfies all conditions of an
algebra norm except for the condition that non-zero elements should have
non-zero norm.
An algebra is semi-normable if it carries a non-trivial seminorm.

For a point
p
of
\beta\N
we let
A_{p}
denote the quotient algebra

M_{p}/I_{p}
, where
M_{p}=\{f\in C(\beta\N,\C):f(p)=0\}
, and

.
We also let

be the subalgebra of
C(\beta\N,\C)
of functions
that vanish on
\N^{*}
and we let

denote the quotient algebra

.

[
35
]
shows why we should be interested
in these algebras:
The algebra
C(\beta\N,C)
admits an incomplete norm iff for some
p

the algebra

is seminormable, and
C({\omega+1},\C)
admits an incomplete
norm iff for some
q
the algebra

is seminormable.

We see that
if
there is a
p
such that
A_{p}
is seminormable
then

there is a
q
such that
c_{0}/q
is seminormable.
The present question ask whether this implication can be reversed.

Further questions regarding these algebras suggest themselves:
is it the case that the seminormability of
A_{p}
implies that of
c_{0}/p
?
In other words can we get

in the previous paragraph?

Also, what is the answer to the stronger version of our question:

We recommend
[
35
]
*Chapters 1, 2 and 3 for more detailed
information on this question.

In
[

*XVIII §4 Shelah proved that it is consistent that
up to permutation there is one

-point.

We recall the definition of the Rudin-Frolík order: we say

To see this note that

readily seen to be equivalent to there being an embedding

61

Is every finite partial order embeddable in the Rudin-Keisler order?

See MathOverFlow
https://mathoverflow.net/questions/375365

To get a positive answer it suffices to embed every finite power set
into this order.
It is relatively easy to adapt the construction of two incomparable
ultrafilters to yield an embedding of the power set of
\{0,1\}

(see
[
152
]
),

\{0,1,2\}

difficulties.

This order is tree-like in that the predecessors of a point are linearly
