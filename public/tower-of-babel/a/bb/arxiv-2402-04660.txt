Adversarial Robustness Through Artifact Design
Tsufit Shua, Liron David, Mahmood Sharif

Abstract
Adversarial examples arose as a challenge for machine learning. To hinder them, most defenses alter how models are trained (e.g., adversarial training) or inference is made (e.g., randomized smoothing). Still, while these approaches markedly improve models’ adversarial robustness, models remain highly susceptible to adversarial examples. Identifying that, in certain domains such as traffic-sign recognition, objects are implemented per standards specifying how artifacts (e.g., signs) should be designed, we propose a novel approach for improving adversarial robustness. Specifically, we offer a method to redefine standards, making minor changes to existing ones, to defend against adversarial examples. We formulate the problem of artifact design as a robust optimization problem, and propose gradient-based and greedy search methods to solve it. We evaluated our approach in the domain of traffic-sign recognition, allowing it to alter traffic-sign pictograms (i.e., symbols within the signs) and their colors. We found that, combined with adversarial training, our approach led to up to 25.18% higher robust accuracy compared to state-of-the-art methods against two adversary types, while further increasing accuracy on benign inputs. Notably, a user study we conducted showed that traffic signs produced by our approach are also easily recognizable by human subjects.



I
Introduction

Adversarial examples
[
2
,
50
]
—mildly perturbed variants of
benign samples crafted to mislead machine learning (ML) models at
inference time—pose a risk to ML-based systems. For
instance, adversarial examples were shown to mislead face
recognition systems
[
42
]
, enabling adversaries to circumvent
access-control and surveillance systems. As another example,
adversarial examples were demonstrated to evade traffic-sign
recognition
[
10
]
, raising doubts about the safety of
autonomous vehicles.

Varied defenses against adversarial examples have been proposed.
Some defenses mainly intervene at training time, altering
how models are trained. For instance, adversarial training augments
training data with adversarial examples
to improve models’ adversarial
robustness
[
12
,
21
,
53
]
. Other
defenses primarily intervene during inference.
For example, randomized smoothing runs inference with
multiple samples augmented with noise to approximate a model with increased robustness and derive provable robustness
guarantees
[
7
,
5
]
. While
helpful at improving robustness,
existing approaches have exhibited limited improvements on standard
benchmarks in recent years, leading to calls for dramatically
different methods for boosting adversarial robustness
[
18
]
.

:
Traffic signs with original pictograms and colors (top)
vs. ones our method creates to defend against

This paper proposes such an approach, offering means to alter the
underlying data distribution to improve models’ adversarial robustness.
Our insight is that, in some domains such as traffic-sign and license-plate
recognition
[
23
,
52
]
, artifacts whose samples are ingested as
inputs are defined by human-created
standards

specifying how artifacts should be designed (see Definition
1
). Take
traffic signs, for instance—each sign is
implemented according to a standard specifying how its external
shape, pictogram (i.e., symbols within signs), and pictogram color should
appear (see Figure
1
). Guided by this
insight,
we explore means to apply minor changes to existing standards
(e.g., so that humans would still be able to recognize traffic signs),
redefining artifact designs, to enhance the adversarial robustness of
models trained and evaluated on data generated according to the
standards
.

We formalize the problem of artifact design as a robust optimization
problem. The goal of the optimization is to find a standard
specification and model that minimizes the maximum loss attainable by
adding worst-case adversarial perturbations to artifacts designed
per the standard. To solve the optimization, we offer two
approaches. The first approach leverages gradient-based optimization to
optimize artifact attributes differentiable with respect to the loss,
such as traffic-sign colors. The second approach adopts greedy
optimization to optimize discrete attributes, such as pictograms.
We tested our approach in the traffic-sign domain under two commonly
studied threat models (Figure
1
depicts results). One
threat model considers adversaries in the digital domain, adding
adversarial perturbations bounded in
\ell_{p}
-norm to evade
classification (e.g.,
[
12
]
). In contrast, the other threat
model considers physical domain adversaries introducing small,
inconspicuous patches to inputs to mislead models
(e.g.,
[
10
]
). In both settings,
our approach suggested traffic-sign pictograms and colors that
significantly improved adversarial robustness compared to
state-of-the-art methods (by up to 25.18% robust accuracy). As an
added benefit, our approach also enhances models’ accuracy on benign
inputs. Importantly, a user study we conducted showed that human subjects
recognized traffic signs produced by our approach as successfully as
standard traffic signs, for one threat model, or had slightly lower
accuracy for another threat model.

II
Background and Related Work

II-A

Evasion Attacks

Various attack types against ML, varying in attackers’ goals and
capabilities, exist. For example, in training-time attacks,
adversaries harm model accuracy by partially controlling the training
data or process (e.g.,
[
3
,
14
]
); in
privacy attacks, adversaries extract private information about
training data during or after training (e.g.,
[
43
]
); and, in availability attacks,
adversaries generate inputs that increase prediction or training time
(e.g.,
[
44
]
). Unlike these, this work deals with

evasion attacks
, where adversaries can only manipulate inputs
during inference to induce misclassifications.

Biggio et al.
[
2
]
and Szegedy et
al.
[
50
]
first proposed evasion methods against
ML models for image classification via
adversarial
examples
. To generate adversarial examples, they proposed methods to
find worst perturbations, with small
\ell_{p}
-norm for
imperceptibility, that increase models’ loss, thus leading to
misclassification. Since then, many studies offered more successful,
efficient, and imperceptible attacks
(e.g.,
[
12
,
6
,
29
,
21
,
9
]
). Typically, adversarial examples are
generated by solving an optimization of the form:

\argmax_{\delta}\mathcal{L}\big(\mathcal{F}(x+\delta^{x}_{\mathit{adv}}),y\big)\qquad\textit{s.t.}\ \lVert\delta^{x}_{\mathit{adv}}\rVert_{p}\leq\epsilon.

This optimization looks for the adversarial perturbation
(
\delta^{x}_{\mathit{adv}}
) with bounded
\ell_{p}
-norm (by

\epsilon
), whose addition to the benign sample (
x
) misleads the
model (
\mathcal{F}
), by increasing the loss (
\mathcal{L}
).

Adversaries vary, among others, in their
abilities and goals
[
30
]
.
In
white-box
settings
(e.g.,
[
21
]
),
attackers have access to the model parameters and
architectures. In contrast, in

black-box
settings (e.g.,
[
29
]
),
attackers are only allowed to query models and have no access to their
internals. Attacks may also target a particular class or seek an
arbitrary misclassification (a.k.a. untargeted
attacks)
[
30
]
. Intuitively, untargeted white-box
attacks are most potent;
thus, we primarily evaluate defenses’ robustness
against such attacks (Section
III
).

Adversarial perturbations bounded in
\ell_{p}
-norm are well-suited
for assessing robustness in the digital domain, where adversaries have
complete control over inputs. Yet, as adversaries may have
limited control over inputs in real-world scenarios (e.g., due to
sampling noise), researchers also explored adversarial examples
realizable in the physical domain
(e.g.,
[
42
,
4
,
1
,
10
,
56
]
). These
attacks are usually implemented in the form of
visible-yet-inconspicuous perturbations that are added to inputs (e.g., as stamps on traffic signs) to induce
misclassification. In this work, we also seek to defend against such
attacks (Section
III
).

II-B

Defenses Against Evasion Attacks

Defending models against adversarial examples is a chief problem in
adversarial ML. Adversarial training—augmenting the training data
with correctly labeled adversarial examples—is of the most
effective techniques for enhancing robustness
[
12
,
21
,
53
,
28
]
. Notably, Madry et al.
[
21
]
framed the
problem of training adversarially robust models as a robust
optimization problem:

\min_{\mathcal{F}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\max_{\delta_{\mathit{adv}}^{x}}\mathcal{L}\big(\mathcal{F}(x+\delta_{\mathit{adv}}^{x}),y\big)\Big]

(1)

where the objective is to find a classifier (
\mathcal{F}
) whose
expected maximum loss on adversarial examples is as low as possible. We offer a related
formalism to design standards conducive for robustness
(Equation
2
in Section
IV
).

Other defenses offer methods to detect adversarial
inputs
[
24
,
25
]
, transform inputs
prior to classification to sanitize adversarial
perturbations
[
37
,
54
]
, and certify
robustness within certain regions (e.g., by verifying that all samples
in them are classified the same)
[
7
,
47
,
5
]
. Still, despite remarkable advances in improving ML models’
adversarial robustness over the past few years, models remain highly
vulnerable to evasion attacks
[
18
]
. Our work aims to further enhance models’ adversarial robustness,
further closing the gap, via a novel technique.

Prior work mostly focused on offering alternative training and
inference techniques to improve robustness. In contrast, we propose
enhancing robustness by altering how artifacts are designed.
Somewhat related to our work, Salman et
al.
[
36
]
proposed creating a single artifact that is classified correctly with high
likelihood under varied imaging conditions that are not necessarily
adversarial (e.g., rain and fog).
Similarly, other researchers explored adding sample-specific
perturbations to enable robust classification
[
11
,
45
,
51
]
.
Unlike our work, however, previous work did
not consider defending against adversarial examples
[
36
]
, or altered
individual objects (e.g., airplanes) or digital inputs
to induce robutness
[
11
,
36
,
45
,
51
]
instead of altering standards
specifying how artifacts of all classes should be designed.

Several studies argue that data distribution is a crucial
factor impacting adversarial robustness
[
15
,
35
,
40
,
41
]
. In
line with these, we aim to change the data distribution in
domains where doing so is possible (e.g., traffic-sign recognition) to
facilitate adversarial robustness.

III
Threat Model

Our work seeks to develop a defense against adversaries perturbing
inputs to induce misclassifications by deep neural networks (DNNs).
The adversaries we
consider cannot alter the DNN weights or training process, as in
training-time attacks, but only have control over inputs ingested by
deployed models.
We evaluate our defense against worst-case
adversaries, as is standard in the
literature
[
8
,
53
]
. In
particular, to evaluate our defense, we consider adversaries with
white-box access to DNNs aiming to mislead them in an
untargeted manner. Intuitively, weaker adversaries with more
constrained access, or with the objective of targeting a specific
target class, would be less successful circumventing the defense.

We aim to defend against two types of commonly studied adversaries.
In the
digital threat model

[
9
]
,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
, we
consider adversaries producing adversarial inputs by adding
perturbations bounded in

\ell_{p}
-norm to benign inputs. Chiefly, we focus on perturbations bounded in

\ell_{\infty}
-norm, which are widely studied in the
literature
[
8
]
.
In the
physical threat model

[
10
]
,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
, we
consider adversaries introducing adversarial patches
covering a small area of the image (e.g.,
\sim
5% of
pixels
[
53
]
) with pixel values ranging anywhere

\in{}[0,1]
.
We clarify that, per standard practice, we consider defending against
one adversary type at a time. While a few approaches for defending
against varied adversary types concurrently exist
[
22
]
, such
defenses exhibit limited success, and, to our knowledge, no defense
exists for defending models under
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
. We thus
defer defending against both adversaries simultaneously to future work.

To deter attacks, we consider defenses that not only alter how models
are trained to improve their adversarial robustness, but can also
alter artifacts to prevent adversaries from carrying out
successful attacks. In particular, the defender may alter how
artifact designs are specified in standards to improve the model’s
robustness once the artifacts are implemented.
The changes to artifact designs the defender is allowed to
make are not without constraints. For example, they may need to be
sufficiently minor so that the resulting artifacts would be easily
identified by human observers.

IV
Technical Approach

We frame the problem of designing standards conducive for
adversarial robustness as a robust optimization problem,
similarly to Madry et al.’s adversarial training
process
[
21
]
(Equation
1
in
Section
II
). However,
while adversarial training seeks to find model weights that minimize
loss against worst-case perturbations of benign samples, we also aim
to find specifications of standards that minimize loss against such
perturbations. In other words, we seek to discover ways to define
how artifacts should be designed such that they would lend
themselves to training models with higher adversarial
robustness. Next, we provide definitions to help us formulate
the robust optimization objective.

Definition 1

A standard
\mathcal{S}{}
specifying the design of
n
artifact
classes is a set

\mathcal{S}{}=\{s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{n}\}
,
s.t. each
s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}{}

is a tuple specifying the design of artifacts in class
y_{i}
.

Conceptually, Definition
1
describes standards as sets
specifying how artifacts of
n
classes should be implemented, where
each tuple specifying an artifact class denotes the classes’
characteristics. Two canonical
domains where standards often specify object designs are traffic-sign
and license-plate recognition
[
23
,
52
]
. For
instance, in the traffic-signs domain,
s_{i}
may specify
the sign’s outer shape, where the shape can be circular for
prohibitory signs or triangular
for warnings; the pictogram, which is usually a symbol, picture, or
number within the sign; and the pictogram’s color, which is typically
black. As another example, standards for license plates may specify
font types; font colors; spacing; and background colors.

Once defined, standards are usually fabricated (many times) into
real-world artifacts, and these artifacts are placed within different
environment. The artifacts are then sensed (e.g., photographed) under
different environmental conditions (e.g., varied lighting and angles),
resulting in data samples or instances. We capture this complex
instantiation process of a standard by the instantiation operator:

Definition 2

\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{S})=\{x_{1},x_{2},...\}
is an instantiation
operator, mapping a standard
\mathcal{S}
to a set of instances, such that
\forall{}i
,

x_{i}\in[0,1]^{d}
(i.e., a
d
-dimensional vector).

Said differently,

\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{S})
is the set reflecting the data
distribution from which training and test samples are drawn. We reuse
this notation to refer to instances pertaining to a single artifact
class
y_{i}
by
\mathcal{I}(s_{i})
.

Robust Optimization

With these definitions, we can formulate the robust optimization for
uncovering a standard
\mathcal{S}
conducive for adversarial robustness
and an adversarially robust model
\mathcal{F}
by:

\min_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{F}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\footnotesize\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}s_{i}\sim\mathcal{S}\\
x\sim\mathcal{I}(s_{i})\end{array}}\Big[\max_{\delta_{adv}^{x}}\mathcal{L}\big(\mathcal{F}(x+\delta_{\mathit{adv}}^{x}{}),y_{i}\big)\Big].

(2)

Put simply, this optimization aims to find the standard (
\mathcal{S}
)
and model (
\mathcal{F}
) such that the worst-case adversarial
perturbation (
\delta_{\mathit{adv}}^{x}
) added to instances produced according to the standard
would still result in low loss (
\mathcal{L}
), in expectation. Note that, we may also
need to impose constraints on the standards that can be found by the
optimization. For instance, in the traffic-sign domain, sign shapes
have a particular meaning (e.g., triangles are used for warning),
thus, they may not be changed. Additionally, we may want to
specify standards that do not significantly deviate from widely used
ones to ensure they remain human-interpretable.

:
The dataset synthesis
process used for standard instantiation, as presented by Maletzeky et al.
[
23
]
.

To solve Equation
2
, it is necessary to model the
instantiation process captured by
\mathcal{I}
and to develop
effective and efficient search techniques to optimize

\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{F}
. As aforementioned, we show how this can be done in the
traffic-sign domain, as a case study. Because, in practice,
instantiation is a complex and slow process (requiring artifact
fabrication, placement, and sensing), we approximate it using
efficient computational techniques
(Section
IV-A
). We then present how to optimize
traffic signs’ colors and pictograms in standards to
solve Equation
2
(Section
IV-B
)

IV-A

Standard Instantiation

We estimate traffic-sign standard instantiation via a faithful
synthesis approach proposed by Maletzeky et al.
[
23
]
.
Figure
2
summarizes the complete synthesis process.
To synthesize images, Maletzky et al. started with patches contain a
prohibitory sign (round with red border) or a warning sign (triangular
with red border), called
context scenes
, that were extracted
from high-resolution photographs of traffic scenes on Austrian
highways (
\small{1}⃝
).
Next, they replaced the pictograms in the context scenes by the
pictograms of the traffic-sign classes (
\small{2}⃝
and
\small{3}⃝
).
Finally, they augmented the set of the images by applying an arsenal
of augmentation methods with varying intensities, all selected at
random (
\small{4}⃝
).
To enable gradient-based optimization of standards, it is necessary
that all operations employed by the instantiation process would be
differentiable. To this end, we ensured that all augmentation
operations applied to instantiate images can be differentiated.
We detail the augmentations’ implementation and synthesis parameters
when presenting the evaluation (Section
V-B
).

Standard Optimization

We explore optimizing two characteristics of traffic signs to improve
robustness—pictograms and their colors. Figure
3

depicts the full optimization processes of both characteristics.

IV-B
1
Pictogram Optimization

To optimize the pictograms, we leverage greedy discrete
search.
Given a pool of alternative pictograms per traffic-sign class, the
search algorithm
iterates over the traffic-sign classes one
at a time, and chooses the pictogram leading
to the highest robustness per class.
We explored iterating over classes in a random order or starting with
the classes exhibiting the lowest robust accuracy, and found the order
had little-to-no impact on the results.
More precisely, the pictogram optimization process works iteratively,
as follows:

(1)
For the previously optimized classes, use the chosen
pictogram with a black color (i.e. (0,0,0));

(2)
for classes that have not yet been optimized, use the
original pictogram with the original color;

(3)
instantiate different synthetic datasets, one per pictogram
found in the search pool for the class currently being optimized;

(4)
adversarially train models on each of the datasets
instantiated;

(5)
evaluate the robust accuracy of the models and select the
pictogram corresponding to the model with the highest robust accuracy.

As abovementioned (Section

), we employed
Maletzky et al.’s
[

[

, resulting in

To create images, the synthesis approach begins with 14 context

\small{1}⃝

augmentations with varied intensities

\small{4}⃝
in Figure

).
Eventually, the dataset consisted of 22,050 images,
equally distributed across source context scenes, classes,

To evaluate the generalization of models, we held the images

qualitatively and quantitatively

V-C

Attacks

To evaluate robustness under
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
, per standard practice,
we used the AutoAttack
[
9
]
—an ensemble of four,
advanced attacks, including two white-box attacks, and two black-box attacks.
Notably, for models attaining non-trivial levels of robust accuracy,
the AutoAttack was found to receive empirical robust accuracy within

<
1% from more recent, efficient
attacks
[
20
,
55
]
. We, however, opted to
use the AutoAttack in our evaluation as it is more commonly used in the
literature
[
8
]
, enabling a more direct
comparison with previous work. We limited the

\ell_{\infty}
-norm of perturbations to
\epsilon=\frac{8}{255}
, as
common
[
8
]
.

In
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
, we assessed robustness using four attacks. Three
of these attacks were previously established:

(1)
Brown et al.’s
[
4
]

adversarial patch
designs a position- and input-agnostic
(universal) patch leading to misclassification to an
a priori

selected class;

(2)
Wu et al.’s
[
53
]

rectangular occlusion attack (
\mathit{ROA}
)

generates an image-specific adversarial noise, shaped as a square that
is placed in a position most likely to produce
misclassification;
and
(3)
Eykholt et al.’s
[
10
]

Robust Physical Perturbations (
\mathit{RP}_{2}
)
posts
adversarially crafted multiple black and white stickers on signs to
induce misclassifications.
As the implementation of the process finding sticker
locations in Eykholt et al.’s attack is not publicly available, we
used the fixed locations published by the authors for a particular
traffic-sign class (specifically, stop signs). Unfortunately, however,
this resulted in poor attack success rates. Thus,
we combined elements of
\mathit{RP}_{2}
and
\mathit{ROA}
to produce a more
potent attack we denote by
\mathit{RP}_{2}^{m}
.
Similarly to
\mathit{RP}_{2}
,
\mathit{RP}_{2}^{m}
produces multiple
stickers. Specifically, it finds
m
square-shaped stickers, each
positioned at a location maximizing the loss during initialization (to
a fixed color), as found by the exhaustive-search approach employed by

\mathit{ROA}

[
53
]
. Once the locations are set, the
attack optimizes the sticker colors in the same manner as the

\mathit{RP}_{2}
, while relaxing the
constraint that each sticker is black and white.

We found that
\mathit{RP}_{2}^{4}
outperformed all other attacks
(Section
VI-F
), thus we
report models’ robust accuracy in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
against it.
In particular, we report results considering stickers covering

\sim
5% of pixels in the image, a setting that is commonly
studied
[
53
]
.
Figure
4
depicts attack examples.

attack, with stickers

We clarify that, in our evaluation, we only considered digital
representations of the physically realizable attacks. As physically
implementing the attacks would likely be affected by implementation
errors and sampling noise, our reported robust accuracy sets a lower
bound on the actual robust accuracy attained against attacks
implemented in the physical domain.

V-D

Adversarial Training

We considered two adversarial training approaches as baselines that we
later integrated into our standard-optimization approach. To select
these defenses, we searched for top-performing adversarial training
techniques that do
not
rely on external unlabeled data and have
open-source implementations. Following this criteria, for

\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
, we used the
SCORE
adversarial training
technique
[
28
]
, which ranks amongst the leading
defenses on the RobustBench benchmark
[
8
]
.
For
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
, we used
Defense against Occlusion Attacks
(
\mathit{DOA}
)

[
53
]
, a method performing adversarial
training using adversarial examples created by

\mathit{ROA}
.

We tuned the adversarial training methods’ parameters for best
performance. For
SCORE
, per the original work, we used a batch
size of 512 and trained the
model for 400 epochs using the SGD optimizer with Nesterov
momentum
[
27
]
(with a momentum factor of 0.9 and
weight decay 5
\times
10
-4
). We further used the
cyclic learning rate policy
[
48
]
, with cosine
annealing of the learning rate from an initial learning rate of

0.025
to maximum value of
0.01
and then to minimal value of
2.5
e
-6
.

For
\mathit{DOA}
, following the original work, we first ran 30 epochs of
standard training, using the SGD optimizer with initial learning rate
of 0.03 and a step learning-rate scheduler decaying the learning rate
by a factor of 0.1 every 15 epochs. Subsequently, we adversarially
trained the model for additional 100 epochs, using the Adam optimizer
and
SCORE
’s cyclic learning-rate scheduler. Similarly to the attack
phase (Section
V-C
), also in the training,
we used a sticker of size covering
\sim
5% of the image’s pixels.

V-E

Standard Optimization

Pictogram Optimization

For each traffic-sign class, we collected five alternative pictograms
for pictogram optimization via Internet search (e.g., by looking for
terms such as “bike silhouette”).
Figure
5
presents the original pictograms as well as the
pictograms included in the search space for pictogram optimization.
To address certain inconsistencies observed in the adversarial training
methods, we adversarially trained five models for each alternative
pictogram, using a different randomness seed in each run, and
selected the pictogram with the highest mean validation robust
accuracy.

Fig. 5
:
Original pictograms (leftmost column) and the alternatives used in
pictogram optimization.

Color Optimization

For each threat model, we performed a line search to find the
parameters (i.e., iterations,
epochs, and step size) for Algorithm
1
that
yield the highest robust accuracy.
In
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
, we ran 600 iterations of
Algorithm
1
, where, in each
iteration, we adversarially trained the model for 0.5 epoch (i.e., trained
one epoch using 0.5 of the training data) and updated the pictogram’s
color by a step size of 0.01.
Note that due to run-time limitations, in each iteration we ran 100
iterations of APGD-CE (one of the attacks employed by
AutoAttack)
[
9
]
to produce adversarial
examples (Line
9
of Algorithm
1
), instead of
the complete AutoAttack, which was left for the evaluation
(Section
V-C
). In
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
,
we ran 400 iterations of the algorithm with 0.25 training epochs per
iteration, and a color update step-size of 0.01.

Because the dataset generation process is randomized and
Algorithm
1
is stochastic (solving a highly
non-convex optimization), the results may vary across runs. Thus, to
enhance the reliability of the evaluation, we repeated each experiment
with Algorithm
1
ten times, each with a randomly
instantiated dataset, using a different randomness seed in each run.
We report the performance of the model corresponding to the highest
validation robust accuracy.

VI
Experimental Results

Our experiments sought to answer several research questions:

•

RQ1
: To what extent does standard optimization improve robust
accuracy? (Section
VI-A
);

•

RQ2
: Does adversarially training models on the resulting
standard from scratch yield equally robust models?
(Section
VI-B
);

•

RQ3
: Which is more effective, color or pictogram
optimization? (Section
VI-C
);

•

RQ4
: How does standard optimization compare to simpler means of
designing standards? (Section
VI-C
);

•

RQ5
: Is standard optimization effective with varied model
architectures? (Section
VI-D
).

•

RQ6
: Can human subjects accurately classify the traffic signs
resulting from standard optimization?
(Section
VI-E
).

We present the experiment results in the following sections, demonstrating
how standard optimization aids in improving adversarial robustness. We
also report results that further validate our findings and experiment
design, including, among others, evaluation of alternative adversarial
training methods and comparison of attacks in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
, in
Section
VI-F
.
Unless otherwise noted, the experiments report the performance of the
complete standard-optimization process, executing color optimization
after setting the pictograms to those found via the pictogram
optimization process.

VI-A

Defense Performance (
RQ1
)

To evaluate standard optimization, we started by measuring
the benign and robust accuracy of models adversarially trained on the original
pictograms and colors. Then, we gradually increased
the number of traffic signs whose pictograms and colors are optimized
using our approach.
Figure
6
presents the results, considering colors
initialized at random or to fixed values.
Compared to the adversarial training, the standard optimization with
random initialization improved robust accuracy by 25.18% under

\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
, and by 16.33% under
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
. An improvement of
1.30–5.63% was
also attained for benign accuracy. These results evidence that
standard optimization facilitates adversarial robustness. (The
optimized standards are shown in Figure
1
.)
Since the random initialization obtained comparable or better
performance than static initialization in most cases, we report
performance using random initialization in the following experiments.

VI-B

Training From Scratch (
RQ2
)

In addition to the new standard, the optimization process
produces a model that is optimized simultaneously with the standard.
It remains unclear, however, whether (adversarially) training models
from scratch on the resulting standard would result in models that
have comparable benign and robust accuracy to those trained
concurrently with standard optimization. To answer this question, we
adversarially trained models on datasets instantiated per standards
found by running the optimization with a varied number of pictograms.
Figure
7
presents the results. For
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
, the gap between the robust accuracy of the models
trained from scratch and those obtained from the optimization process
was small (
\pm
3%).
For
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
, a little less consistency was observed,
mostly due to large variance in robust accuracy when running vanilla
adversarial training. Still, when all pictograms labels were optimized,
the gap remained as small as 1.66%.
Hence, we conclude that the results of the standard
optimization process can be reproduced by adversarially training
models from scratch using the optimized standard.

(bottom).

VI-C

Baseline Comparisons and Ablation (
RQ3
&
RQ4
)

The standard-optimization process alters both the pictograms and
pictogram colors of traffic signs. To find which of these is more
conducive for robustness, we compared the full standard-optimization
process with pictogram optimization alone, where colors are set to
default ones (i.e., black or black and red, depending on the
sign). Additionally, we wanted to assess the utility in optimizing
pictogram colors via gradient-based optimization compared to less
elaborate approaches. To this end, we considered two baselines. The
first baseline sets the pictogram colors at random, by uniformly
sampling them from the RGB color space. The second baseline is
motivated by the observation that the color-optimization process finds
colors close the edges of the RGB color space excluding white
color (i.e. to the
colors in the group
C=\{c\in\{0,1\}^{3}:c\neq(1,1,1)\}
).
Intuitively, we expected that maximizing the distance between the
colors of different traffic-sign classes would enable distinguishing
them more successfully. Thus, in the second baseline, we set pictogram colors by randomly
permuting the seven possible values in
C
.

The results (Figure
8
)
demonstrate that color optimization is more helpful than pictogram
optimization for adversarial robustness, leading to
>
20% robust
accuracy.
Moreover, optimizing pictogram colors led to markedly
higher robustness than the baselines: Both setting colors at
random and selecting distinct edge colors for pictograms led to
lower robust accuracy than pictogram optimization when optimizing the
colors of all seven pictograms. These results demonstrate the utility
of the standard-optimization process, and particularly that of color
optimization.

:

compared to pictogram optimization alone and setting pictogram
colors at random or to edge colors, under both
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}

(top) and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
(bottom).

VI-E

Human Interpretability (
RQ6
)

We designed and ran an online user study to assess whether
human subjects can successfully recognize the artifacts resulting from
the optimized standards.

Study Design

We followed a between-subject design, where each participant was
assigned at random to one of three conditions and asked to classify
traffic-sign images. The conditions differed in the standard used to
create the traffic-sign images—one condition used the default
standard, another used the standard optimized under
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
, and
the last used a standard optimized under
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
. To maintain a
manageable participation time and reasonable cognitive load, we
presented each participants with 14 classification questions
two for each of the seven classes. To ensure fair comparison, we selected
the 14 images at random, from the respective synthesized datasets,
ensuring that identical embedding scenes and augmentations were
applied to the images from the different conditions. In each of the 14 questions, the
participants had to select the correct class label out of seven
possibilities in multiple choice questions.
To mitigate ordering effects, we randomized the questions’ order for
each participant. Additionally, at the
beginning of the study, we showed participants a legend to ensure
all participants were familiar with the traffic signs. Figure
9
depicts
an example legend for the
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
condition, and
Figure
10
presents the samples that participants in
the
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
condition had to classify.

We implemented the study in Qualtrics
[
33
]
and recruited
participants through Prolific
[
32
]
, limiting participation to
those who are at least 18-years-old and fluent in English. The study
protocol was reviewed and approved by our institution’s review board
(IRB).

In total, 118 participants completed the study. The median completion
time was roughly 4 minutes and participants were compensated by 1 USD.
Of the 118 participants, we excluded ten

exhibiting anomalous responses—their classification accuracy was more than
\times

than the lowest quartile
[

]
.
Thus, our
analysis focused on the responses of the remaining 108
participants. The participant ages ranged between 18 and
64 years, with a median of 29, and 47.22% of
the participants self-identifying as females, with the remainder
self-identifying as males, reflecting a standard Prolific
sample
[

. The median participant self-reported

The scatter plot in Figure

for participants in the different

\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
and

\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
are

\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}

VI-F

Additional Validation

We now present several experiments to further validate our findings
and methodology. Specifically, we report on an additional adversarial
training method and attempts to verify robustness with formal
methods. Then, we also compare the synthesized dataset with a
real-world dataset to assess the realism of the traffic-sign
synthesization method. Finally, we report run-time measurements.

Adversarial Training

We experimented with additional adversarial training techniques to
verify that our findings generalize approaches other than the ones we
considered thus far (i.e.,
SCORE
and
\mathit{DOA}
). Specifically, we
experimented with
\mathit{DAJAT}
, a high-performing adversarial training
method
[
8
]
tailored for
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
that
employs advanced data augmentations to improve adversarial robustness
while maintaining benign accuracy. We used the official implementation
published by the authors and trained ResNet-18 models with
\mathit{DAJAT}
’s
default parameters.
We trained models on the default dataset, based on the default
(unoptimized) standard, and the dataset optimized for

\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
. After training, we used the two datasets’ respective
test sets to measure benign accuracy and executed attacks in

\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
(i.e., using AutoAttack), similarly to the previous
experiments, to measure robust accuracy.

The results attained with
\mathit{DAJAT}
were consistent with those from
previous experiments. The model trained on samples based on the
optimized standard achieved 69.26% robust accuracy compared to
52.67% robust accuracy achieved by the other model (trained with
samples based on the default standard). In other words, standard
optimization led to a 16.59% increase in robust accuracy. Moreover,
we observed an increase in benign accuracy after standard optimization
(92.59% vs. 89.80%). This result shows that standard optimization
can also help improve robust accuracy when training models with
techniques other than those integrated into the standard-optimization
process.

Robustness-Verification Attempts

To set a lower bound on robust accuracy, we attempted to use
DNN-verification tools to verify that no (misclassified)
adversarial examples can be produced where attacks failed. To this
end, we adopted
\alpha{}
,
\beta
-crown
[
19
]
, a state-of-the-art verification
tool well-suited for verifying adversarial robustness under

\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
. While sound,
\alpha{}
,
\beta
-crown is incomplete. In other words,
the verification results shown that no adversarial examples exist
under given constraints, then none can be created. However, because
DNN verification is NP-hard, verification tools may in some
cases fail to verify robustness within finite time, even if no
misclassified adversarial examples cannot be created.

We executed
\alpha{}
,
\beta
-crown for samples where AutoAttack
failed to produce adversarial examples against either the ResNet or
VGG models on the standard optimized for
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
. (MobileNet does
not lend itself for verification with
\alpha{}
,
\beta
-crown due to incompatible
layers.) For the VGG model, we found that
\alpha{}
,
\beta
-crown ran out of memory
even with our largest GPU (NVIDIA A100 with 80GB or memory). For
ResNet,
\alpha{}
,
\beta
-crown did not find any counterexamples (i.e., samples
that could be misclassified), but also failed to verify robustness for
any sample, even when setting the wall-time to a permissive value of
10 minutes per sample. These results held for different
parameterizations of
\alpha{}
,
\beta
-crown.

We can conclude that established verification tools may be insufficiently mature for
verifying robustness under the threat models, DNNs, and datasets we
consider. However,
\alpha{}
,
\beta
-crown’s failure to demonstrate misclassified
samples after a long run time provides further validation of our
empirical robustness evaluation with the AutoAttack.

Run-Time Measurements

To measure run times, we ran adversarial training and standard
optimization on a single GPU—once on an NVIDIA A5000 and once on
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090. The average run time of the adversarial
training was
1.25 hours in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
, and 8.17 hours in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
.
Standard optimization, which includes adversarial model training as
part of the process (recall Algorithm
1
), took
longer to run, but still completed in
manageable time, requiring 28.92 hours in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
and 22.83 in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}
.
We highlight, however, that we have not taken particular measures to
optimize the time efficiency of standard optimization, and thus expect
that it would be possible to markedly improve the run
time. Furthermore, we note that, as highlighted by our evaluation
(Section
VI-B
), once a standard conducive for
adversarial robustness is found, it is
possible to train models more efficiently via adversarial training alone.

VII
Discussion

We now discuss our findings, drawing connections with related work,
addressing considerations for real-world deployment, and highlighting
directions for future work.

Distributions Conducive for Adversarial Robustness

Our work proposes an approach that optimize standards to enable the
development of more adversarially robust models when training and testing
on artifacts instantiated according to the standard.
Previous theoretical line of work showed that certain data
distributions lend themselves to training adversarially robust models
while other distributions do not
[
15
,
35
,
40
,
41
]
.
In a sense, our approach complements previous work by offering
arguably practical methods to alter the underlying distribution of
samples to enhance adversarial robustness.
In future work, it would be interesting to examine our approach from a
theoretical perspective to uncover in what ways the data distribution
is changed to enhance robustness.

Practical Considerations

Although this work paves new avenues for improving adversarial
robustness, it is critical to emphasize that it is not yet ready for
real-world deployment.
Although our experiments evidenced the promising potential of standard
optimization, we only considered a subset of classes (seven signs out
of at least 43
[
49
]
) in a specific application domain
(i.e., traffic-sign recognition).
To close the gap from actual deployment, future work should
demonstrate the viability of the approach for a larger number of
classes.
Moreover, one may consider other application domains, such as
license-plate recognition
[
17
]
, where artifact design is
potentially applicable.
Notably, such domains may be less constrained by economic and policy
considerations, rendering them more suitable for real-world deployment
of our approach.

Other Extensions

In this work, we consider mitigating a single family of attacks at a
time, defending against different threat models (i.e.,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{phys}}

and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{dig}}
) with different classification models.
A natural, critical extension would be to adapt standard optimization
to enable the development of a single model that is resilient against
different families of attacks. Indeed, this remains an open research
problem for other types of defenses too
[
22
]
.

VIII
Conclusion

We proposed a novel approach for hindering adversarial examples,
suitable for domains where standards specify the designs of
artifacts. Our approach slightly alters standards, re-specifying how
artifacts should be designed to enhance adversarial robustness. We
framed the problem of artifact design as a robust optimization,
proposed gradient-based and greedy methods to solve it, and evaluated
its viability in the traffic-sign domain against two well-studied
attack categories. The results demonstrate significant improvements
compared to state-of-the-art defenses (with up to 25.18% higher
robust accuracy attainable via artifact design).
Crucially, we also find that human subjects can recognize the
resulting traffic signs with relatively high accuracy.
