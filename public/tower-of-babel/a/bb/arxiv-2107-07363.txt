An oscillator driven by algebraically decorrelating noise Thanks: This work has been partially supported by the National Science Foundation under grants NSF DMS-1813943, DMS-1515187 to AN and HL, NSF DMS-1814147 to GI, and the Center for Nonlinear Analysis.
Chistophe Gomez, Gautam Iyer, Hai Le, Alexei Novikov

Abstract
. We consider a stochastically forced nonlinear oscillator driven by a stationary Gaussian noise that has an algebraically decaying covariance function. It is well known that such noise processes can be renormalized to converge to fractional Brownian motion, a process that has memory. In contrast, we show that the renormalized limit of the nonlinear oscillator driven by this noise converges to diffusion driven by standard (not fractional) Brownian motion, and thus retains no memory in the scaling limit. The proof is based on the study of a fast-slow system using the perturbed test function method.

1.
Introduction

Consider a stochastically forced nonlinear oscillator with 1 degree of freedom:

(1.1)

\ddot{x}_{t}+f(x_{t})=\varepsilon v(t),\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\dot{x}_{0}=y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Here
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given smooth function, and
v
is a stochastic process representing the noise.
Our interest is to study the asymptotic long-time behavior of
x
when the noise
v
has an algebraically decaying covariance function.
In this case, the rescaled noise converges to fractional Brownian motion (fBm), a process that has a memory.
We aim to study how the nonlinear dynamics affects the limiting behavior, and study whether or not the rescaled oscillator also converges to a process with memory.

A similar question was studied by Komorowski et al.
[
KNR14
]
in the case of a passive tracer particle advected by a periodic shear flow.
In this case, it turns out that there is a certain parameter regime where the time rescaled dynamics is Markovian and the memory effect of the noise is forgotten.
However, there is also a regime (namely the “very long-time” behavior when the Hurst index of the driving noise is larger than
1/2
) where the memory effect persists.
In contrast, for the oscillator (
1.1
), we will show that the memory effect
never
persists, and the effective long-time behavior is always Markovian.
The main reason for this is that the oscillatory nature of the deterministic dynamics cancels slowly decaying correlations, and destroys the memory effect.
To study (
1.1
) we cannot use the limit theorem for additive functionals fBm’s used by
[
KNR14
]
.
Instead, we recast (
1.1
) as a fast-slow system and use the perturbed test function method.
Even though this method was introduced in the ’76
[
PSV76
,
Kus84
]
, it has applications to many problems arising today
[
FGPSl07
,
DRV21
,
CV21
]
, and is the only method we presently know that can be used to prove our main result.

To describe our setup, we first introduce the Hamiltonian

H(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}y^{2}+\int_{0}^{x}f(s)\,ds\,,

and recast (
1.1
) as a stochastically perturbed Hamiltonian system

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}=y_{t}=\partial_{y}H(x_{t},y_{t})\,,

(1.2b)

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{t}=\ddot{x}_{t}=-f(x_{t})+\varepsilon v(t)=-\partial_{x}H(x_{t},y_{t})+\varepsilon v(t)\,.

Setting
X_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(x_{t},y_{t})^{T}
, we rewrite (
1.2a
)–(
1.2b
) compactly as

(1.3)

\dot{X}_{t}=\nabla^{\bot}H(X_{t})+\varepsilon v(t)e_{2},\quad X_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,.

where
\nabla^{\bot}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(\partial_{y},-\partial_{x})^{T}
, and
e_{2}=(0,1)^{T}
.

To study the long-time behavior,
we consider the time rescaled process
X^{\varepsilon}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}X(t/\varepsilon^{2})
, and observe

(1.4)

\dot{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla^{\bot}H(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)e_{2}\,,\quad X_{0}^{\varepsilon}=X_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

When the noise
v
is white, the behavior of
X^{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
is completely described by the averaging principle of Freidlin and Wentzell
[
FW93
,
FW94
,
FW98
]
.
To briefly explain this, we note that in the absence of noise (i.e. when
v\equiv 0
), the process
X^{\varepsilon}
travels very fast along level sets of the Hamiltonian.
In the presence of noise, the process
X^{\varepsilon}
will also diffuse slowly across these level sets.
To capture the limiting behavior we factor out the fast motion by projecting to the
Reeb graph
of the Hamiltonian, which has the effect of identifying all closed trajectories of the Hamiltonian system to points.
Now, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, this projection converges to a diffusion driven by a standard Brownian motion and we refer the reader to
[
FW93
,
FW94
,
FW98
]
for details.
(We also remark that when the noise
v
is white, one may also study the behavior of
X
on time scales shorter than
1/\varepsilon^{2}
.
In some scenarios, a robust and stable limiting behavior is also observed on these time scales and has been studied by several authors
[
You88
,
YJ91
,
Bak11
,
HV14a
,
HV14b
,
HKPG16
,
TV16
,
HIK+18
]
.)

In this paper, our interest is to study the case when the driving noise in (
1.4
) is colored in time and has algebraically decaying correlations.
It is well known that this noise can be renormalized to converge to a fractional Brownian motion (fBm), with Hurst parameter that is determined from the decay rate of the correlation function (see for instance
[
Taq75
,
Taq77
,
Mar05
]
).
When the Hurst parameter is not half (which happens when the correlation function decays like
1/t^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\neq 1
), the renormalized limit of the noise has memory and is a non-Markovian process.
We aim to study whether the memory effect is also present in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit of the nonlinear oscillator (
1.4
), where the noise is combined with the Hamiltonian dynamics.

At present, we are only able to analyze the scenario where the Hamiltonian is smooth with exactly one non-degenerate critical point.
In this case, it is convenient to think about the dynamics of
X^{\varepsilon}
in terms of action-angle coordinates.
The angular coordinate of
X^{\varepsilon}
changes very fast, and has no meaningful limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The action coordinate of
X^{\varepsilon}
, on the other hand, changes slowly as a result of the interaction between the noise and the averaged angular coordinate and has a non-trivial limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
To study this we use the Hamiltonian itself as a proxy for the action coordinate and state our results in terms of convergence of the process
H(X^{\varepsilon})
.
(To relate this to the Freidlin–Wentzell framework, we note that when
H
has exactly one non-degenerate critical point, the Reeb graph has exactly one vertex and one edge, and
H(X^{\varepsilon})
is precisely the projection of
X^{\varepsilon}
onto the Reeb graph.)

In this paper, we prove two results. They show that even though the driving noise has memory, the time correlations are destroyed by the oscillatory dynamics of (
1.4
), and
H(X^{\varepsilon})
converges to a diffusion driven by standard Brownian motion (a process without memory).
Roughly speaking our main results are as follows:

(1)

If the Hamiltonian
H
is quadratic, and the noise
v
is any stationary Gaussian process with an algebraically decaying covariance function, then
H(X^{\varepsilon})
converges to a diffusion process driven by a Brownian motion. In this particular case, the limiting diffusion is a rescaling of the square of the 2-dimensional Bessel process.

(2)

If the Hamiltonian is not quadratic (but still smooth with exactly one non-degenerate critical point), then we can show
H(X^{\varepsilon})
converges to a diffusion driven by Brownian motions, provided the noise
v
is chosen suitably.
(The noise
v
is still a stationary Gaussian process with an algebraically decaying covariance function.
However, it must be expressible as a superposition of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes as we make use of certain exponential mixing estimates in the proof.)

1.1.
Main results for a quadratic Hamiltonian.

We now state our results precisely when
H
is quadratic.
Hereafter we assume that the noise
v
is a stationary Gaussian process with covariance function

(1.5)

R(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{E}v(t)v(0)=\bm{E}v(t+s)v(s)\,.

Our main result when the Hamiltonian is quadratic is as follows:

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
H
be the quadratic Hamiltonian

(1.6)

H(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}}{2}\,,\qquad x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,,

and suppose the noise
v
is a stationary Gaussian process whose covariance function,
R
, is of the form

(1.7)

R(t)=\frac{L(t)}{t^{\gamma}}

for some
\gamma\in(0,2)
and a function
L
that is slowly varying at infinity.
1
1

1

Recall a function
L
is said to be slowly varying at infinity if for every
s>0
we have
L(st)/t\to 1
as
t\to\infty
.
If
\gamma\in(0,1]
, we further assume that
L
has the slow increase property

(1.8)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{L^{\prime}(t)}{L(t)/t}=0\,.

Then the family of processes
H(X^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
converges in distribution to
H(W^{D})
, where
W^{D}
is a 2D Brownian motion with
W_{0}=X_{0}
and covariance matrix
D
given by

(1.9)

D_{11}=D_{22}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\lim_{x\to\infty}\int_{0}^{x}R(z)\cos(z)\,dz\qquad\text{and}\qquad D_{12}=D_{21}=0\,.

Remark 1.2
.

For
\gamma\in(0,1]
we note that (
1.8
) implies that
R^{\prime}<0
near infinity.
Since
\cos(z)
oscillates periodically, the limit in (
1.9
) exists and is finite.
For
\gamma\in(1,2)
, we note
R\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and so the limit in (
1.9
) also exists and is finite.

As mentioned earlier, it is well known
[
Taq75
,
Taq77
,
Mar05
]
that the renormalized noise converges to a fractional Brownian motion.
For the reader’s convenience, we state a result to that effect next.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
v
be a stationary Gaussian process with covariance function
R
given by (
1.7
) for some
\gamma\in(0,2)
and a slowly varying function
L
.
If
\gamma\in(1,2)
, we additionally suppose

(1.10)

\int_{0}^{\infty}R(t)\,dt=0\,.

Let

(1.11)

\sigma(\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}L(\varepsilon^{-2})^{1/2}\varepsilon^{\gamma}&\gamma\neq 1\,,\\
L(\varepsilon^{-2})^{1/2}\varepsilon\lvert\ln\varepsilon\rvert^{1/2}&\gamma=1\,,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad u^{\varepsilon}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sigma(\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{t}v\left(\frac{s}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\,ds\,.

Then, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the family of processes
(u^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
converges in distribution
to
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}B^{{\mathcal{H}}}
, where
B^{\mathcal{H}}
is a standard fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
{\mathcal{H}}=1-\gamma/2
, and

(1.12)

\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}\lvert 2\mathcal{H}-1\rvert}&\mathcal{H}\neq\frac{1}{2}\,,\\
1&\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}\,.\end{cases}

To reiterate our main point, we note that Proposition
1.1
implies

(1.13)

H\Big(X_{0}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla^{\perp}H(X^{\varepsilon}_{s})\,ds+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v\Big(\frac{s}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)e_{2}\,ds\Big)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}H(W^{D}_{t})\,,

for a Brownian motion
W^{D}
with covariance matrix
D
.
However,

\frac{1}{\sigma(\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{t}v\Big(\frac{s}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\,ds\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}B^{H}_{t}\,,

for a fBm
B^{\mathcal{H}}
with Hurst index
\mathcal{H}
.
Of course,
H(W^{D})
is a Markov process with no memory, but
B^{\mathcal{H}}
is a non-Markovian process with memory.

Note further that when
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we see
\varepsilon\ll\sigma(\varepsilon)
and so the term
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v(s/\varepsilon^{2})\,ds
appearing in (
1.13
) diverges as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
On the other hand when
\gamma\in(1,2)
(and equation (
1.10
) holds), we see
\sigma(\varepsilon)\ll\varepsilon
, and so this term vanishes.
In both cases, the oscillatory Hamiltonian term contributes non-trivially and as a result, the time correlations are forgotten and
H(X^{\varepsilon})
converges to a memoryless Markov process.

We now explain the reason for the assumption (
1.10
), which is required in Proposition
1.3
when
\gamma\in(1,2)
, but not in Proposition
1.1
.
If
\gamma\in(1,2)
by the central limit theorem one can show that
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v(s/\varepsilon^{2})\,ds
converges to a Brownian motion.
The covariance of this Brownian motion is proportional to
\int_{0}^{\infty}R(t)\,dt
.
If this is
0
(as required by assumption (
1.10
)), then one can divide
\int_{0}^{t}v(s/\varepsilon^{2})\,ds
by the smaller factor
\varepsilon^{\gamma}=\sigma(\varepsilon)
, and look for a non-trivial limit.
This is precisely what is given by Proposition
1.3
.
The reason the assumption (
1.10
) is not needed for Proposition
1.1
is because in (
1.13
) the noise is only scaled by a factor of
1/\varepsilon
and not
1/\sigma(\varepsilon)
.

Finally, we briefly summarize the main idea of the proofs of Propositions
1.1
and
1.3
.
First, Proposition
1.3
only requires convergence of Gaussian processes and can be proved by directly computing covariances.
Similar results are well known
[
Taq75
,
Taq77
,
Mar05
]
.
Since the exact result we need isn’t readily available, we prove it in Appendix
B
.

The proof of Proposition
1.1
requires a little more work.
The main simplification obtained from the assumption that
H
is quadratic is that the evolution of the angle coordinate is independent of the action coordinate.
As a result one can perform a (time dependent) rotation in space and prove convergence of
H(X^{\varepsilon})
by studying integrals of the form

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v\Big(\frac{s}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\cos\Big(\frac{s}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\,ds\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v\Big(\frac{s}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{s}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\,ds\,.

These are now Gaussian and one can study convergence by computing covariances directly.
We note that the study of similar integrals arises in the study of random media where several authors
[
Mar05
,
FGPSl07
,
Gar97
]
have used oscillatory dynamics to decorrelate the medium.

1.2.
Main results for Hamiltonians with only one critical point.

We now study the case where the Hamiltonian has exactly one non-degenerate critical point but is not necessarily quadratic.
In this case we will need to work with a stationary Gaussian noise process
v
that can be obtained by super-imposing Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes and written in the form

v(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{S}e^{-\mu\lvert p\rvert^{2\beta}(t-u)}\mathcal{B}(du,dp)\,.

Here
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a bounded symmetric open interval,
\mu
,
\beta
are constants, and
\mathcal{B}
is a Gaussian random measure that is white in time and colored in space and will be constructed explicitly in Section
3
below to ensure that the covariance of
v
decays algebraically.
Our main theorem can now be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth Hamiltonian with exactly one non-degenerate minimum at
(0,0)
, and
v
be the stationary Gaussian noise described above.
Then

(1.14)

H(X^{\varepsilon})\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{X}\,,

where
\mathcal{X}
is a diffusion driven by standard Brownian motion, with infinitesimal generator

(1.15)

L=\frac{1}{2\Lambda(\mathcal{X})}\partial_{\mathcal{X}}(\Sigma(\mathcal{X})\partial_{\mathcal{X}})\,.

Here the coefficients
\Lambda
and
\Sigma
are defined by

\Lambda(\mathcal{X})=\oint_{\{H=\mathcal{X}\}}\,\frac{dl}{|\nabla H|}\,,

and

\Sigma(\mathcal{X})=2\int_{u=0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\oint_{\{H=\mathcal{X}\}}\partial_{y}H(\check{X}^{x,y}_{u})\partial_{y}H(x,y)\frac{dl(x,y)}{|\nabla H(x,y)|}\,,

where
\check{X}^{x,y}
is the solution to

\partial_{t}\check{X}^{x,y}_{t}=\nabla^{\perp}H(\check{X}^{x,y}_{t}),\qquad\text{with}\qquad\check{X}^{x,y}_{t=0}=(x,y)\,.

Theorem
1.4
follows immediately from the more general Theorem
4.3
, below, concerning fast-slow systems, and the proof of Theorem
1.4
is presented shortly after the statement of Theorem
4.3
.
Moreover, Theorem
4.3
also explicitly characterizes the limiting system and can be used to explicitly characterize the diffusion
\mathcal{X}
in Theorem
1.4
.

Note that when
H
is not quadratic the convergence of
H(X^{\varepsilon})
can’t be reduced to Gaussian integrals as in Proposition
1.1
, and so the proof of Theorem
1.4
is more involved.
We prove Theorem
1.4
by first switching to action-angle coordinates and converting (
1.4
) to a fast-slow system, where the slow variables are mean-zero in the fast variable.
We then use the perturbed test function (PTF) method
[
Kus84
]
to prove convergence of this system.
This approach provides more information than just the convergence of
H(X^{\varepsilon})
, as stated in Theorem
1.4
, and we refer the reader to Theorem
4.3
, below, for the precise statement.
Of course, the convergence of action coordinate is equivalent to convergence of the processes
H(X^{\varepsilon})
.
However, our proof also identifies an asymptotic diffusive behavior of the angle coordinate on the slow scale.

We remark that our fast-slow system is different from that recently considered by Hairer and Li
[
HL20
]
.
Indeed, in
[
HL20
]
, the authors proved an averaging principle for a coupled fast-slow system driven by fBm, with Hurst index
\mathcal{H}>\frac{1}{2}
, and they obtained convergence in probability to the naively averaged system.
However, they require the fast variable to be driven by an independent
standard
Brownian motion.
In the scenario that arises in the present paper, the fast variable is driven by noise that converges to a
fractional
Brownian motion when renormalized, and the same noise is also used to drive the slow variable.
Moreover the limiting system we obtain is not the naively averaged one, but a diffusion with an averaged generator akin to the limiting behavior of fast-slow systems driven by standard Brownian noise (see for instance
[
PS08
]
).

Theorem
1.4
is stated for Hamiltonians which have only one (non-degenerate) critical point.
The Reeb graph of such Hamiltonians is simply the half line, and the limiting process in Theorem
1.4
is a diffusion on the half line.
For general Hamiltonians, Reeb graph may have multiple vertices, and the limiting process should be a diffusion on this Reeb graph.
When the driving noise is a standard Brownian motion (as in the Freidlin–Wentzell theory), this diffusion is characterized through its generator on each edge of the Reeb graph, and certain gluing conditions on the vertices.
While the proofs in the Freidlin–Wentzell theory rely heavily on the Markov property, and do not apply to our situation, the form of the infinitesimal generator (
1.15
)
is similar to that in
[
FW98
, Equation 1.8]
.
As a result, we expect similar gluing conditions to hold in our context.

Explicitly, for a sufficiently general class of Hamiltonians, we expect that the process
(H(X^{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon>0}
converges in distribution to a diffusion process on the Reeb graph.
The generator of this diffusion should be

L_{j}h_{j}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{j}(\mathcal{X})}\partial_{\mathcal{X}}(\Sigma_{j}(\mathcal{X})\partial_{\mathcal{X}}h_{j})

on each edge
I_{j}
.
The coefficients
\Lambda_{j}
and
\Sigma_{j}
are obtained by taking line integrals as follows.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{j}(\mathcal{X})
the connected component of the level set
\{H=\mathcal{X}\}
corresponding to a point
\mathcal{X}\in I_{j}
.
Now
\Sigma_{j}
and
\Lambda_{j}
are given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{j}(\mathcal{X})=2\int_{u=0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\oint_{C_{j}(\mathcal{X})}\partial_{y}H(\check{X}^{x,y}_{u})\partial_{y}H(x,y)\,\frac{dl(x,y)}{|\nabla H(x,y)|}\,,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j}(\mathcal{X})=\oint_{C_{j}(\mathcal{X})}\frac{dl(x,y)}{\lvert\nabla H(x,y)\rvert}\,.

To describe the gluing condition, consider an interior vertex
O_{k}
corresponding to the saddle point
(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
and the level set
\{H=\mathcal{X}_{k}\}
, at which the collection of edges
\{I_{j}\}
meet.
(The typical situation is a “figure eight” scenario, which has three edges meeting at the vertex
O_{k}
.)
Now define

\beta_{kj}=\lim_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}_{k}}C_{j}(\mathcal{X})\,,

where the limit is taken along the edge
I_{j}
.
The gluing condition at the vertex
O_{k}
can then be written as

(1.16)

\sum_{j:\,I_{j}\sim O_{k}}\pm\beta_{kj}\,h^{\prime}_{j}(H(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k}))=0\,,

where sign before
\beta_{kj}
is positive if
\mathcal{X}>\mathcal{X}_{k}
along the edge
I_{j}
,
and negative if
\mathcal{X}<\mathcal{X}_{k}
along the edge
I_{j}
.

The main difference between our main result, Theorem
1.4
, and the standard Freidlin–Wentzell theory
[
FW98
]
is in the coefficient
\Sigma
.
The definition of
\Sigma
in Theorem
1.4
involves an average of
\partial_{y}H(\check{X}^{x,y}_{u})\partial_{y}H(x,y)
, with a shift of one factor to the point
\check{X}^{x,y}_{t=u}
.
In
[
FW98
]
the coefficient
\Sigma
depends only on
(\partial_{y}H(x,y))^{2}
.
The reason for this modification lies in the fact that
in our case the oscillations of the Hamiltonian system and the random
fluctuations in (
1.4
) are strongly coupled, and are both of order
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
.
The oscillatory behavior of the Hamiltonian system and random fluctuations fully interact
with each other and produce an effective diffusion coefficient involving
the correlation of
\partial_{y}H(\check{X}^{x,y}_{u})
with
\partial_{y}H(x,y)
for pairs of points on the same orbit.
If the oscillatory behavior of the Hamiltonian system is slower than the random fluctuations, then their coupling is weak and we would obtain the same generators as in
[
FW98
]
.
This will arise if, for example, we consider white noise in (
1.4
) as the driving random force.
This dichotomy of limiting behaviors between strongly and weakly coupled setups is
well-known in other approximation-diffusion limits of random differential equations with periodic
components, (for instance, compare and contrast Theorems 6.4 and 6.5 in
[
FGPSl07
]
). In the present work, we need the oscillations of the Hamiltonian system and the random fluctuations to be of the same order; otherwise, the shift
\check{X}^{x,y}_{u}
, and hence
\Sigma
, is not well-defined.
We hope to present a proof of this phenomenon in future work.

2.
Proof of convergence for a quadratic Hamiltonian.

This section is devoted to proving Proposition
1.1
.
The main idea behind the proof is that when
H
is quadratic, the deterministic dynamics rotates with constant angular speed in all trajectories.
Performing a spatial rotation will now reduce the problem to studying convergence of Gaussian processes, which can be resolved by computing covariances.

Proof of Proposition
1.1
.

When
H
is given by (
1.6
), we use Duhamel’s formula to write the solution of (
1.4
) as

X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}\cos(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}})&\sin(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}})\\
-\sin(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}})&\cos(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}})\end{pmatrix}X_{0}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v\Big(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\begin{pmatrix}\sin(\frac{t-\tau)}{\varepsilon^{2}})\\
\cos(\frac{t-\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}})\end{pmatrix}\,d\tau.

Let
M(t)
be the rotation matrix

M(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(t)&\sin(t)\\
-\sin(t)&\cos(t)\end{pmatrix}\,,

and define the rotated process
Y^{\varepsilon}
by
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=M(-t/\varepsilon^{2})X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
.
Clearly
H(X^{\varepsilon})=H(Y^{\varepsilon})
, and so convergence of the processes
H(X^{\varepsilon})
reduces to convergence of the processes
H(Y^{\varepsilon})
.

We claim that the processes
Y^{\varepsilon}
itself converge to a Brownian motion as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
To see this, observe

Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=X_{0}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v\Big(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\begin{pmatrix}-\sin(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}})\\
\phantom{-}\cos(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}})\end{pmatrix}\,d\tau\,.

We will now show that the second term above converges to a Brownian motion.
For this, define

(2.1)

\displaystyle w_{1}^{\varepsilon}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v\left(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)d\tau\,,

(2.2)

\displaystyle w_{2}^{\varepsilon}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}v\left(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)d\tau\,.

Note, the noise
v
(when normalized similarly by
\sigma(\varepsilon)
without the periodic terms) converges to fBm.
However, the expressions above use a normalization factor of
\varepsilon
instead of
\sigma(\varepsilon)
, and have an oscillatory factor.
For this reason we claim
(w_{1}^{\varepsilon},w_{2}^{\varepsilon})
converges to a Brownian motion.
To prove convergence of
w_{1}^{\varepsilon}
,
w_{2}^{\varepsilon}
, we first state two lemmas:

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
T>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
s,t\in[0,T]
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have

(2.3)

\bm{E}(w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t)-w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s))^{2}\leqslant\begin{cases}C|t-s|^{1-\gamma}&\gamma\in(0,1)\,,\\
C\lvert t-s\rvert^{2-\gamma}&\gamma\in[1,2)\,.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
s,t\geqslant 0
, and
i\in\{1,2\}
we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bm{E}(w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t)-w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s))^{2}=D_{11}|t-s|\,,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bm{E}(w^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t)-w^{\varepsilon}_{1}(s))(w^{\varepsilon}_{2}(t)-w^{\varepsilon}_{2}(s))=0\,.

The proofs of both Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
are lengthy, but direct computations.
Thus, for clarity of presentation, we postpone the proofs to Appendix
A
.
Once Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
are established, the proof of Proposition
1.1
follows quickly.
Indeed, Lemma
2.1
implies that the family
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is tight on
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{2})

To see this, it suffices to show that the processes
w^{\varepsilon}_{i}
are tight.
Without loss of generality suppose
T=1
, and suppose
\gamma\in(0,1)
(the case when
\gamma\in[1,2)
is similar).
Choose an integer
M
such that
M_{\gamma}=M(1-\gamma)>1
.
Since
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}
is Gaussian process, we know

\bm{E}(w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t)-w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s))^{2M}=C_{M}[\bm{E}(w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t)-w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s))^{2}]^{M}

for some constant
C_{M}
.
We will allow
C_{M}
to change from line to line as long as it only depends on
M
, and remains independent of
\varepsilon
.
The above implies

(2.6)

\bm{E}(w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t)-w^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s))^{2M}\leqslant C_{M}|t-s|^{M_{\gamma}}\,.

Since
M_{\gamma}>1
by choice, Kolmogorov’s criterion (see for instance
[
EK86
]
Proposition 10.3) implies that the family
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}
is tight.
This implies that the processes
Y^{\varepsilon}
converge in distribution along a subsequence to a continuous process
Y
.

Since each process
Y^{\varepsilon}
is a Gaussian process, the limiting process
Y
must also be a Gaussian process.
Now Lemma
2.2
implies
Y
is a Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
Y_{0}=X_{0}
and covariance matrix
D
.
Since the law of the limiting process is uniquely determined, the family
Y^{\varepsilon}
itself must converge in distribution, without having to select a subsequence.
Finally, since
H(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})=H(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})
, we obtain convergence of
(H(X^{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon>0}
as claimed.
∎

3.
Construction of the Noise.

In this section, we will construct noise
v
that will be used in Theorem
1.4
, and establish a few properties that will be used in the course of the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
We require the covariance function
R
to be of the form

(3.1)

R(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{S}r(p)e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}|t|}\,dp\,,

where
S=(-r_{s},r_{s})
is a symmetric, bounded, open interval,
\mu,\beta>0
are constants, and
r\colon S-\{0\}\to[0,\infty)
is defined by

r(p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\lambda(p)}{|p|^{2\alpha}}\,.

Here
\lambda\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth bounded even function such that
\lambda(0)\neq 0
and

\int_{S}r(p)\,dp>0\,,

and

(3.2)

\alpha<\frac{1}{2}\,.

We will now construct a stationary Gaussian process
v
with covariance function
R
.
The chosen form of
R
allows us to construct
v
by superimposing Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes as follows.
Let
\xi
be 2D white noise and define a Gaussian random measure
\mathcal{B}
by

\mathcal{B}(du,dp)=\sqrt{2\mu r(p)}\,|p|^{\beta}\mathbf{1}_{S}(p)\,\xi(du,dp)\,,

Clearly, the covariance of
\mathcal{B}
is given by

\bm{E}\mathcal{B}(du,dp)\,\mathcal{B}(du^{\prime},dp^{\prime})=2\mu\,r(p)\,|p|^{2\beta}\delta(u-u^{\prime})\delta(p-p^{\prime})\,du\,du^{\prime}\,dp\,dp^{\prime}\,.

Now define the measure-valued Gaussian random process
V
by

V(t,dp)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}(t-u)}\,\mathcal{B}(du,dp)\,,

and finally define the noise
v
by

v(t)=\int_{S}V(t,dp)\,.

We now establish a few properties of the process
v
that will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The process
v
defined above is a stationary Gaussian process with covariance function
R
.

Proof.

Clearly
v
is a Gaussian process.
To see that
v
is stationary with covariance function
R
we choose any
s\leqslant t
and compute

\displaystyle\bm{E}v(s)v(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\int_{-\infty}^{s}\int_{S}\exp\Big(-\mu\big(\lvert p\rvert^{2\beta}(s-u)+\lvert p\rvert^{2\beta}(t-u)\big)\big)\cdot 2\mu\lvert p\rvert^{2\beta}r(p)\,du\,dp\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\int_{S}r(p)e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}(t-s)}\,dp=R(t-s)\,.\qed

Lemma 3.2
.

If
R
is given by (
3.1
), then

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\gamma}R(t)=c_{0}

where
c_{0}
and
\gamma
are defined by

\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1-2\alpha}{2\beta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\lambda(0)\int_{p\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-\mu\lvert p\rvert^{2\beta}}}{\lvert p\rvert^{2\alpha}}\,dp\,.

Proof.

By a change of variable, note that

\displaystyle t^{\gamma}R(t)=t^{\gamma}\int_{S}\frac{\lambda(p)}{|p|^{2\alpha}}e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}t}\,dp

\displaystyle=\int_{(t^{1/(2\beta)}S)}\frac{1}{|p^{\prime}|^{2\alpha}}\lambda\Big(\frac{p^{\prime}}{t^{1/(2\beta)}}\Big)e^{-\mu|p^{\prime}|^{2\beta}}\,dp^{\prime}\,,

which converges to
c_{0}
as
t\to\infty
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Note that (
3.2
) already implies
\gamma>0
.
If additionally we assume

(3.3)

2\alpha+4\beta>1

then we will also have
\gamma<2
.
In this case the process
v
can be renormalized as in (
1.11
) to converge to a fBm.
For Theorem
1.4
, however, the assumption (
3.3
) is unnecessary.

Let
\{\mathcal{G}_{t}\}
be the augmented filtration generated by
V
.
That is,

(3.4)

\mathcal{G}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sigma\big(\mathcal{N}\cup\{\sigma(V(s,\cdot))\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\allowbreak 0\leqslant s\leqslant t\}\big)\,,

where
\mathcal{N}
is the set of all null sets known at time infinity.
Our next lemma computes conditional expectations of
V(\cdot,dp)
with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have for any
t,h\geqslant 0

(3.5)

\bm{E}\big[V(t+h,dp)|\mathcal{G}_{t}\big]=e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}h}\,V(t,dp)

and

(3.6)

\begin{split}\bm{E}\Big[V(t+h,dp)V(t+h,dq)\Big|\mathcal{G}_{t}\Big]&-\bm{E}\big[V(t+h,dp)|\mathcal{G}_{t}\big]\bm{E}\big[V(t+h,dq)|\mathcal{G}_{t}\big]\\
&=(1-e^{-2\mu|p|^{2\beta}h})r(p)\delta(p-q)\,dp\,dq\,.\end{split}

Our last result concerns the boundedness of our noise process.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
T>0
,
M>0
,

D_{k,M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}[0,T]\times L^{\infty}([0,T],W_{k,M})

with

W_{k,M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\big\{\varphi\in W^{1,k}(S):\quad\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,k}}\leqslant M\big\}

and where
W^{1,k}(S)
stands for the Sobolev space with
k\in(1,\infty]
. We have

(3.7)

\bm{E}\Big[\sup_{(t,\varphi)\in D_{k,M}}\Big|V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi(t,\cdot)\Big)\Big|\Big]\leqslant C+\frac{C(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\,,

and for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

(3.8)

\sup_{\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bm{E}\Big[\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}\Big|V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi\Big)\Big|^{n}\Big]\leqslant C_{n}\,,

where
C
,
C_{n}
and
C(\varepsilon)
are three positive constants where the latter satisfies

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}C(\varepsilon)=0.

Here the notation
V(t/\varepsilon^{2},\varphi)
denotes integral of a function
\varphi(p)
with respect to the measure
V(t/\varepsilon^{2},dp)
.
That is,

V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{S}\varphi(p)\,V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp\Big)\,,

and we will drop the
S
in the above notation for simplicity unless specified otherwise.
The proofs of these two Lemmas are computationally involved, and we relegate them to Appendices
C
and
D
respectively.

4.
The Main Theorem

One canonical way to analyze integrable Hamiltonian systems is to use a set of
action-angle coordinates
.
These coordinates separate the slow and fast motion, and preserves the Hamiltonian structure.

4.1.
Action-angle coordinates

The Liouville-Arnold theorem
[
Arn89
]
asserts that there exists a symplectic canonical transformation
\varphi\colon X=(x,y)\mapsto(I,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
, where the action variable
I
and the angle variable
\theta
satisfy

(4.1)

K(I)=H(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{I,\theta\}=1\,,

where
K
is a given one variable smooth enough increasing function such that
K(0)=0
,
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
stands for the standard Poisson bracket and is defined by

\{g,h\}=\partial_{x}g\partial_{y}h-\partial_{y}g\partial_{x}h\,,

for our Hamiltonian system with one degree of freedom.
The relation on the right-hand side of (
4.1
) can be also stated as

\nabla I\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\theta=1\,.

Note that in the action-angle coordinate the Hamiltonian is a function of the action coordinate alone.

The existence of such transformation
\varphi
is guaranteed by the Liouville-Arnold theorem, and can be constructed through a generating function
[
Arn89
, Section 50 pp. 281]
, and implicitly defined by the relations

(4.2)

y=\partial_{x}\mathcal{S}(I,x)\,,\qquad\theta=\partial_{I}\mathcal{S}(I,x)\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad H(x,\partial_{x}\mathcal{S}(I,x))=K(I)\,.

Such a construction is not unique.
For convenience in the forthcoming analysis we will choose
\mathcal{S}(I,0)=0
so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(I,x)=\int_{0}^{x}\partial_{x^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}(I,x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{I}\mathcal{S}(I,x)=\int_{0}^{x}\partial_{Ix^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}(I,x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\,,

and hence

\partial_{I}\mathcal{S}(I,0)=0\,.

The meaning of the second relation is that the zero angle corresponds to either the positive part of the
y
-axis or the negative part.
Consequently, we have for any
I>0

(4.3)

\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta=0)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta=0)\neq 0\,,

and in particular,

(4.4)

\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta=0)=0\,.

The identity (
4.3
) follows from the fact that
K
vanishes only for
I=0
.
For any
I>0
, the
x
and
y
components cannot simultaneously vanish.

Now let us write
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})
and define

I_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}I(X_{t})=\varphi_{1}(X_{t})\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\theta(X_{t})=\varphi_{2}(X_{t})\,.

In the absence of random fluctuations (i.e. when
v=0
), the Hamiltonian system
\dot{X}_{t}=\nabla^{\perp}H(X_{t})
becomes

\dot{I}_{t}=0\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot{\theta_{t}}=\omega(I_{t})\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\omega(I)=K^{\prime}(I)\,.

In the presence of random fluctuations, the Hamiltonian system (
1.3
) becomes

(4.5)

\dot{I}_{t}=\varepsilon v(t)a(I_{t},\theta_{t})\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot{\theta}_{t}=\omega(I_{t})+\varepsilon v(t)b(I_{t},\theta_{t})\,,

where

a=e_{2}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi^{-1}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad b=e_{2}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi^{-1}\,.

Example 4.1
.

For illustration purposes, we now explicitly compute the action-angle variables when the Hamiltonian is quadratic.
From (
4.2
), we have

x=\sqrt{\frac{I}{\pi}}\cos\Big(2\pi\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad y=\sqrt{\frac{I}{\pi}}\sin\Big(2\pi\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)

where
\theta
gives the angle of
X=(x,y)^{T}
from the negative part of the vertical axis. One can then see that the action variable is a multiple of the Hamiltonian,

K(I)=\frac{I}{2\pi}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\omega(I)=\frac{1}{2\pi},

while the angle variable corresponds to the angle on a trajectory, which is a circle in the case of
H(X)=|X|^{2}/2
, with period
1
.
In this case, the functions
a
and
b
are as follows

(4.6)

a(I,\theta)=2\sqrt{\pi I}\sin\Big(2\pi\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad b(I,\theta)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi I}}\cos\Big(2\pi\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)\,.

4.2.
The main theorem

To study the long-time behavior of (
4.5
), we let

I^{\varepsilon}_{t}=I_{t/\varepsilon^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\theta_{t/\varepsilon^{2}}\,.

Now (
4.5
) becomes

(4.7)

\dot{I}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)a(I_{t}^{\varepsilon},\theta_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,,\qquad\dot{\theta}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{\omega(I_{t}^{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)b(I_{t}^{\varepsilon},\theta_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,,

with
I^{\varepsilon}_{0}=I_{0}
and
\theta^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\theta_{0}
. Using the approach in
[
Gar97
]
to separate fast and slow motions, we study this system by splitting the angle variable
\theta_{t}^{\varepsilon}
into two parts

\theta_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\psi_{t}^{\varepsilon}+\tau_{t}^{\varepsilon}\,.

The evolution of
\tau_{t}^{\varepsilon}
(fast motion) is obtained by averaging (
4.7
) over the angular coordinate, and
\psi_{t}^{\varepsilon}
(slow motion) is the remainder.
That is we require

(4.8)

\dot{\tau}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{\omega(I_{t}^{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\langle b(I_{t}^{\varepsilon},\cdot)\rangle

with initial condition
\tau^{\varepsilon}_{0}=0
, and where we have the notation

\langle g\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\theta=0}^{1}g(\theta)\,d\theta\,.

Considering only the slow motion variables
(I_{t}^{\varepsilon},\psi_{t}^{\varepsilon})
, the system (
4.7
) becomes

(4.9)

\begin{split}\dot{I}_{t}^{\varepsilon}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)A(I_{t}^{\varepsilon},\psi_{t}^{\varepsilon},\tau_{t}^{\varepsilon})\\
\dot{\psi}_{t}^{\varepsilon}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)B(I_{t}^{\varepsilon},\psi_{t}^{\varepsilon},\tau_{t}^{\varepsilon})\end{split}

with

A(I,\psi,\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}a(I,\psi+\tau)\qquad\text{and}\qquad B(I,\psi,\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}b(I,\psi+\tau)-\langle b(I,\cdot)\rangle\,.

The above equations are coupled with the initial conditions
I^{\varepsilon}_{0}=I_{0}
, and
\psi^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\theta_{0}
. Note that
A
and
B
are both 1-periodic and mean-zero with respect to
\tau
. This latter property is mandatory to deal with the long-range correlation case as illustrated in Proposition
1.1
.
In fact, if the system possesses a component with zero frequency, a noise with long-range correlations will charge this component and cause the system to blow up as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

It is straightforward to see this mean-zero property in
\tau
for
B
, but for
A
we use that

\textrm{Jac}\,\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)=[\textrm{Jac}\,\varphi(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))]^{-1}

and that
\det\textrm{Jac}\,\varphi(X)=1
since
\varphi
is a symplectic transformation, to obtain the following relations

(4.10)

\begin{split}\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)&=\partial_{y}\varphi_{2}(X),\quad\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)=-\partial_{x}\varphi_{2}(X)\,,\\
\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)&=-\partial_{y}\varphi_{1}(X),\quad\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)=\partial_{x}\varphi_{1}(X)\,.\end{split}

Therefore, we have

a(I,\theta)=-\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)

which is clearly mean-zero with respect to
\theta
since we have the derivative of a periodic function. Note also that from these relations the mean-zero property in
\theta
for
b
is not clear. This is the reason why we introduce the compensation
\langle b\rangle
in the definition of
B
.

Our main result obtains the limiting behavior of (
4.9
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
under the following assumptions.

•

The function
K
is smooth, and

(4.11)

\inf_{I\geqslant 0}K^{\prime}(I)=\inf_{I\geqslant 0}\omega(I)>\omega_{0}>0

for some strictly positive number
\omega_{0}
.

•

There exist
r>0
, and positive constants
c_{1,r}
,
c_{2,r}>0
such that for any
I\in(0,r)

(4.12)

c_{1,r}I\leqslant K(I)\leqslant c_{2,r}I\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\omega^{\prime}(I)|\leqslant\frac{c_{2,r}}{I}\,.

Note that these conditions imply that
K(0)=0
and that
K
is an increasing function in
I
.
These assumptions are not too restrictive since any Hamiltonian satisfies (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) near non-degenerate critical points.
The following proposition is a consequence of the above assumptions, which concerns bounds on the function
a
.
The proof is given in Appendix
E
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) there exist
r>0
and a constant
C_{r}>0
such that for any
I\in(0,r)
we have

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}\Big(|a(I,\theta)|+|\partial_{\theta}a(I,\theta)|\Big)\leqslant C_{r}\sqrt{I}\,,

and

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}|\partial_{I}a(I,\theta)|\leqslant\frac{C_{r}}{\sqrt{I}}\,.

The proposition mainly describes the behavior of
a,\partial_{I}a
and
\partial_{\theta}a
around
I=0
. These bounds will allow us to prove the main result of the paper that characterizes the limiting process of the sequence of processes
(I^{\varepsilon},\psi^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) hold, the family
(I^{\varepsilon},\psi^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
(defined in (
4.8
)–(
4.9
)) converges in distribution in
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
to a process
(I_{t},\psi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, where
(I_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is the unique weak solution of the SDE

(4.13)

\begin{split}dI_{t}&=\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\,a(I_{t},\tau)\,dW_{t}(I_{t},\tau)\,d\tau\\
&\quad+\Big[\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\partial_{I}\big(a(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)_{|I=I_{t}}a(I_{t},\tau)\\
&\qquad\qquad+a(I_{t},\tau+\omega(I_{t})u)\partial_{I}a(I_{t},\tau)\,d\tau\,du\Big]\,dt\,,\end{split}

with initial condition
I_{t=0}=I_{0}
. Here,
W
is a real valued Brownian field with covariance function

(4.14)

\begin{split}\bm{E}[W_{t}(y,\phi_{1})W_{s}(y,\phi_{2})]&=t\wedge s\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\\
&\quad\times\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\phi_{1}(\tau+\omega(y)u)\phi_{2}(\tau)+\phi_{1}(\tau)\phi_{2}(\tau+\omega(y)u)\,d\tau\,du\,,\end{split}

for any
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
where

L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\big\{\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}):\langle\phi\rangle=0\big\}\,.

Explicitly, we can write

W_{t}(y,\tau)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}e^{2i\pi n\tau}R^{1/2}_{n}(y)(B^{1}_{t,n}-iB^{2}_{t,n})

where
B^{1}_{t,n}
and
B^{2}_{t,n}
are two independent real valued cylindrical Brownian motions satisfying
B^{1}_{t,-n}=B^{1}_{t,n}
,
B^{2}_{t,-n}=-B^{2}_{t,n}
, and
R_{n}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}R(u)\cos(2\pi n\omega(I)u)\,du
.

Also, we have

(4.15)

\begin{split}d\psi_{t}&=\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\,b(I_{t},\tau)\,dW_{t}(I_{t},\tau)\,d\tau\\
&\qquad+\Big[\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\partial_{I}\big(b(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)_{|I=I_{t}}a(I_{t},\tau)\\
&\qquad\qquad+b(I_{t},\tau+\omega(I_{t})u)\partial_{I}a(I_{t},\tau)\,d\tau\,du\,\Big]dt\,,\end{split}

with
\psi_{t=0}=\theta_{0}
.

Note that the action variable
I
does not depend on the slow angular motion
\psi
.
However, the distribution of the slow angular motion
\psi
is completely determined by the motion of the action variable
I
and the Brownian field.

We also note that the functions
\partial_{I}a
and
\partial_{I}b
appearing in (
4.13
) and (
4.15
) may be singular at
I=0
.
Nevertheless, since the minimum of
H
is non-degenerate, the function
a\partial_{I}a
has no singularity at
I=0
and there is no singularity on the right-hand side of equation (
4.13
).
The term
a\partial_{I}b
appearing in (
4.15

Existence of a solution

To construct solutions to equation

(3)

\displaystyle\int_{s=0}^{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}(\mathbf{a}(I_{s})+|\mathbf{b}(I_{s})|)\,ds<\infty\,,

Lemma 5.3

For any

we apply Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality
[
KS91
, Theorem 3.28 p. 166]
together with (
5.2
) from which we obtain

J_{T^{\prime}}\leqslant C\bm{E}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}\wedge\zeta_{M}}\mathbf{a}(I_{s})\,ds\leqslant C\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\bm{E}[\mathbf{a}(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}})]\,ds\\
\leqslant C_{\omega_{0}}\int_{S}|p|^{2\beta}|r(p)|\,dp\int_{s=0}^{T^{\prime}}\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\bm{E}[|a(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}},\tau)|^{2}]\,d\tau\,ds\,.

Moreover, using Proposition
4.2
, we have

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\bm{E}[|a(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}},\tau)|^{2}]\,d\tau&=\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(\bm{E}[|a(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}},\tau)|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}}\leqslant r)}]\\
&\qquad+\bm{E}[|a(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}},\tau)|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}}>r)}]\Bigr)\,d\tau\\
&\leqslant r\,C+C_{r}\bm{E}I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\\
&\leqslant r\,C+C_{r}\bm{E}\sup_{t\in[0,s]}I_{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\,,\end{split}

and hence

J_{T^{\prime}}\leqslant C_{1}\,T+C_{2}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\bm{E}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,s]}I_{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\Big)ds\,.

Now, for
K_{T^{\prime}}
, we use Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\begin{split}\bm{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}\mathbf{b}(I_{s})\,ds\Bigr)^{2}&\leqslant T\bm{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}\mathbf{b}^{2}(I_{s})\,ds\\
&\leqslant T\bm{E}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mathbf{b}^{2}(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}})\,ds\,.\end{split}

From Proposition
4.2
, for
I\leqslant r
, we again use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\begin{split}|\mathbf{b}(I)|&\leqslant C\,|\omega^{\prime}(I)|\,\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}|a(I,\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{\tau}a(I,\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau}\\
&+C_{\omega_{0}}\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}|a(I,\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{I}a(I,\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau}\\
&\leqslant C.\end{split}

As a result,

\begin{split}\bm{E}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mathbf{b}^{2}(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}})\,ds&=\bm{E}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mathbf{b}^{2}(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}})\mathbf{1}_{(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}}\leqslant r)}\,ds\\
&\qquad+\bm{E}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mathbf{b}^{2}(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}})\mathbf{1}_{(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}}>r)}\,ds\\
&\leqslant C_{1}\,T+C_{2,r}\bm{E}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{(I_{s\wedge\zeta_{M}}>r)}\,ds\\
&\leqslant C_{1}\,T+C_{2,r}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\bm{E}\Bigl(\sup_{t\in[0,s]}I_{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\Bigr)\,ds\,,\end{split}

and

K_{T^{\prime}}\leqslant C_{1}\,T^{2}+C_{2}\,T\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\bm{E}\Bigl(\sup_{t\in[0,s]}I_{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\Bigr)\,ds\,.

Finally, we have

\bm{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}I_{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\leqslant C_{1}(1+T+T^{2})+C_{2}(1+T)\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\bm{E}\Bigl(\sup_{t\in[0,s]}I_{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\Bigr)\,ds

and then by Gronwall’s inequality with
T^{\prime}=T

\bm{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t\wedge\zeta_{M}}^{2}\leqslant C_{1}(1+T+T^{2})e^{C_{2}(1+T)T}\,.

This concludes the proof of the Lemma by letting
M\to\infty
in (
5.2
). Consequently, the proof of Proposition
5.2
is also complete.
∎

5.3.
Uniqueness of solutions

The weak uniqueness property for equation (
4.13
) comes from the fact that this equation has strong uniqueness on
\mathcal{M}_{\zeta_{2,M}}
for any
M
, and then weak uniqueness on
\mathcal{M}_{\zeta_{2,M}}
for any
M

[
KS91
, Proposition 3.20 p. 309]
, since we avoid the lack of regularity at
0
for the action variable
I
. However, according to Proposition
5.2
, we have
\lim_{M\to\infty}\bm{P}^{0}_{I}(\zeta_{2,M}\leqslant T)=0
for any

which guarantees the weak uniqueness thanks to

.

6.
Proof of the main theorem (Theorem
4.3
)

6.1.
Truncated Process

We first describe the strategy used to prove Theorem
4.3
.
In addition to the difficulties concerning the behavior of
a
around
I=0
that we mentioned in Theorem
4.3
, we have no
apriori
estimates for the process
(I^{\varepsilon}_{t},\psi^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
that are uniform in
\varepsilon
, in probability. This is a problem when trying to apply
[
Kus84
, Theorem 4 p. 48]
directly to prove the tightness property, and when trying to identify the law of the subsequential limits.
To bypass this we follow the strategy developed in
[
SV06
, Chapter 11]
by introducing a
truncated process
that does not suffer from the above problems. To relate the truncated process with the original one we introduce a family of stopping times. As we will see, these times go to
\infty
when we remove the truncation and then finally obtain the weak convergence of the original processes.

Let
M>0
and consider the process
(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t},\psi^{\varepsilon,M}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
which is the unique solution to

(6.1)

\begin{split}\dot{I}^{\varepsilon,M}_{t}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\phi_{M}(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t},\psi^{\varepsilon,M}_{t})a(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t},\psi^{\varepsilon,M}_{t}+\tau^{\varepsilon,M}_{t})\\
\dot{\psi}^{\varepsilon,M}_{t}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\phi_{M}(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t},\psi^{\varepsilon,M}_{t})(b(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t},\psi^{\varepsilon,M}_{t}+\tau^{\varepsilon,M}_{t})-\langle b(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t},\cdot)\rangle)\end{split}

coupled with the initial conditions
I^{\varepsilon,M}_{0}=I_{0}
, and
\psi^{\varepsilon,M}_{0}=\theta_{0}
,
with

\dot{\tau}^{\varepsilon,M}_{t}=\frac{\omega(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t})}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\phi_{M}(I_{t}^{\varepsilon,M},\psi_{t}^{\varepsilon,M})\langle b(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t},\cdot)\rangle\,.

Here
\phi_{M}
is a smooth function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

0\leqslant\phi_{M}\leqslant 1,\quad\phi_{M}(I,\psi)=1\quad\text{if}\quad 1/M\leqslant I\leqslant M\quad\text{and}\quad|\psi|\leqslant M\,,

and

\phi_{M}(I,\psi)=0\quad\text{if}\quad I>2M\quad\text{or}\quad I<1/(2M)\quad\text{or}\quad|\psi|>2M\,.

Thanks to the truncation provided by the cutoff function
\phi_{M}
, for both action and angle variables, the process
(I^{\varepsilon,M}_{t},\psi^{\varepsilon,M}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
does not suffer from the regularity problem for the action variable around
0
as mentioned above. Also, its convergence in distribution in
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
can be proved using the perturbed-test function method and martingale properties
[
Kus84
,
PSV76
]
thanks to the boundedness of the process. All these points are developed more precisely in Section
7
.

To simplify the notation, we now omit the superscript
M
and denote

Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}I_{t}^{\varepsilon,M}\\
\psi_{t}^{\varepsilon,M}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Now the system (
6.1
) can be rewritten as

(6.2)

\dot{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)F(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\tau^{\varepsilon}_{t})

with

(6.3)

\dot{\tau}^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{\omega(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\Big(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,.

Here

(6.4)

\begin{split}F(Y,\tau)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{M}(I,\psi)a(I,\psi+\tau)\\
\phi_{M}(I,\psi)(b(I,\psi+\tau)-\langle b(I,\cdot)\rangle)\end{pmatrix},\\
G(Y)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\phi_{M}(I,\psi)\langle b(I,\cdot)\rangle\,,\end{split}

and
a,b
are defined in equation (
4.5
). Note that
F
is a smooth bounded function (with bounded derivatives) 1-periodic and mean-zero with respect to
\tau
(the truncation only affects
\psi
, not
\tau
), and
\omega(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})=\omega(I_{t}^{\varepsilon,M})
.

To relate the truncated and original processes we introduce first some notations. In the remaining of the paper, the space of all possible outcomes is
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and we denote the corresponding canonical filtration by
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\sigma(y_{s},0\leqslant s\leqslant t)
. The laws of the truncated and original processes on
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
will be denoted respectively by
\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}
and
\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}
. Now, we consider the stopping times

\eta_{1,M}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\inf\{t\geqslant 0:\|y_{t}\|\geqslant M\}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{2,M}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\inf\{t\geqslant 0:|y^{1}_{t}|\leqslant 1/M\}\,,

for any
y=(y^{1},y^{2})\in\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

(6.5)

\eta_{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\eta_{1,M}\wedge\eta_{2,M}\,.

It is not hard to see that
\eta_{1,M}
and
\eta_{2,M}
are lower semi-continuous so that
\eta_{M}
is also lower semi-continuous, that is their lower level sets are closed subsets of
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
The latter property will be used to obtain the convergence of the original process from the truncated one.

From the definition of the above stopping times and the cutoff function
\phi_{M}
it is clear that

(6.6)

\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}=\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{on}\quad\mathcal{M}_{\eta_{M}}\,.

This is the relation that links the truncated and original processes and that will be also used below in Section
6.2
to show the convergence of the original process.

For now, we have the following convergence results for the truncated process. We return the index
M
for a moment to emphasize the truncation.

Proposition 6.1
.

The family
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(I^{\varepsilon,M},\psi^{\varepsilon,M})_{\varepsilon>0}
converges in distribution in
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
to the unique solution
(Y_{t}^{M})_{t\geqslant 0}=(I_{t}^{M},\psi_{t}^{M})_{t\geqslant 0}
to the martingale problem with generator defined by

(6.7)

\mathcal{L}h(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\tilde{F}(0,y,\tau)\cdot\nabla(\tilde{F}(u,y,\tau)\cdot\nabla h(y))\,du\,d\tau\,,

where

\tilde{F}(u,y,\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}F(y,\tau+\omega(y)u)\,,

with
F
defined in (
6.4
) and starting point
(I_{0},\psi_{0})
.

From this characterization, we can deduce the SDEs satisfied by the limiting process
Y_{t}^{M}=(I_{t}^{M},\psi_{t}^{M})
up to the stopping time
\eta_{M}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Denoting
\tilde{I}^{M}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}I^{M}_{t\wedge\eta_{M}}
, we have

(6.8)

\begin{split}d\tilde{I}^{M}_{t}&=\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\,a(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau)\,dW_{t\wedge\eta_{M}}(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau)\,d\tau\\
&\quad+\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\Big(\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\partial_{I}\big(a(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)_{|I=\tilde{I}^{M}_{t}}a(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau)\\
&\qquad\qquad+a(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau+\omega(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t})u)\partial_{I}a(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau)\Big)\,d\tau\,du\,dt,\end{split}

with
\tilde{I}^{M}_{t=0}=I_{0}
, and where
W
is defined in (
4.14
). Also, we have

(6.9)

\begin{split}d\psi^{M}_{t\wedge\eta_{M}}&=\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\,b(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau)\,dW_{t\wedge\eta_{M}}(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau)\,d\tau\\
&\quad+\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\Big(\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\partial_{I}\big(b(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)_{|I=\tilde{I}^{M}_{t}}a(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau)\\
&\qquad\qquad+b(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau+\omega(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t})u)\partial_{I}a(\tilde{I}^{M}_{t},\tau)\Big)\,d\tau\,du\,dt,\end{split}

with
\psi^{M}_{t=0}=\theta_{0}
.

As we can see, this result is similar to the one of Theorem
4.3
, with coefficients
a
and
b
themselves, but involving the cutoff
M
through the stopping time
\eta_{M}
.
The proofs of Proposition
6.1
and
6.2
are presented in Sections
7
and
8
, respectively.
In the next section, we will show how these two propositions can be used to prove Theorem
4.3
.

6.2.
Convergence of
(I^{\varepsilon},\psi^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}

In order to prove Theorem
4.3
, we need to remove the cutoff
M
to show the convergence in distribution of
(I^{\varepsilon},\psi^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
to
(I_{t},\psi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, where
I
,
\psi
are defined by (
4.13
)-(
4.15
).
This is similar to Lemma 11.1.1 (p. 262) in
[
SV06
]
, and we adapt the proof to our situation.

In what follows, we denote by
\bm{P}^{M}
and
\bm{P}^{0}
the distribution of
Y^{M}=(I^{M}_{t},\psi^{M}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
(defined in Proposition
6.1
) and
Y=(I_{t},\psi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
(defined in Proposition
4.3
), respectively. Note that according to (
6.9
) and (
4.15
) the distributions
\bm{P}^{M}
and
\bm{P}^{0}
are completely determined by their first marginal
\bm{P}^{M}_{I}
and
\bm{P}^{0}_{I}
. Also, in view of (
6.8
) and (
6.9
), it is straightforward to see that (with
\eta_{M}
defined in (
6.5
))

(6.10)

\bm{P}^{0}=\bm{P}^{M}\qquad\text{on}\quad\mathcal{M}_{\eta_{M}}\,.

To prove this convergence, let
\Phi
be a continuous bounded function on
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and write

\displaystyle\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]-\bm{E}^{\bm{P}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]

\displaystyle=\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]-\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]

\displaystyle\qquad+\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]-\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{M}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]

\displaystyle\qquad+\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{M}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]-\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{0}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]\,.

Considering the following decomposition

\begin{split}\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]&=\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{M}\leqslant T\}}]\\
&\qquad+\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{M}>T\}}]\end{split}

with

\begin{split}&\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{M}>T\}}]=\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t\wedge\eta_{M}},t\in[0,T])\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{M}>T\}}]\\
&=\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}}[\Phi(y_{t\wedge\eta_{M}},t\in[0,T])\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{M}>T\}}]\\
&=\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{M}>T\}}]\\
&=\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]-\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{M}\leqslant T\}}]\,.\end{split}

Here we used (
6.6
) in the second line.
Using (
6.6
) again we note

\begin{split}\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]&-\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]\\
&\leqslant\|\Phi\|_{\infty}(\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}(\eta_{M}\leqslant T)+\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}(\eta_{M}\leqslant T))\\
&\leqslant 2\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}(\eta_{M}\leqslant T)\,.\end{split}

The same results hold for
\bm{P}^{0}
and
\bm{P}^{M}
instead of
\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}
and
\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}
respectively, but using (
6.10
) instead of (
6.6
). As a result, we obtain

\begin{split}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}|\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]&-\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{0}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]|\\
&\leqslant 2\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bm{P}^{\varepsilon,M}(\eta_{M}\leqslant T)\\
&+2\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\bm{P}^{0}(\eta_{M}\leqslant T)\,.\end{split}

Notice that the stopping time
\eta_{M}
is lower semi-continuous on
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
, so that
(\eta_{M}\leqslant M)\in\mathcal{M}_{\eta_{M}}
is a closed subset of
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
. According to the Portmanteau theorem
[
Bil99
, Theorem 2.1 p. 16]
we have

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}|\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]-\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{0}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]|\leqslant 4\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\bm{P}^{0}(\eta_{M}\leqslant T)\,,

for any
M
.
Moreover, we have

\bm{P}^{0}(\eta_{M}\leqslant T)\leqslant\bm{P}^{0}(\zeta_{M}\leqslant T)+\bm{P}^{0}(\zeta^{\prime}_{M}\leqslant T)\,.

where for any any
y=(y^{1},y^{2})\in\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{2})
we define

\zeta^{\prime}_{M}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\inf\{t\geqslant 0:|y^{2}_{t}|\geqslant M\}\,.

By Proposition
5.2
we know

\bm{P}^{0}(\zeta_{M}\leqslant T)=\bm{P}^{0}_{I}(\zeta_{M}\leqslant T)\underset{M\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\,.

For the other term, let
0<\eta<1
and write

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{0}(\zeta^{\prime}_{M}\leqslant T)\leqslant\bm{P}\Big(

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{t}|\geqslant M,\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}>\eta,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}<\frac{1}{\eta}\Big)

\displaystyle+\bm{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}\geqslant\frac{1}{\eta}\Big)+\bm{P}\Big(\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}\leqslant\eta\Big)\,,

then using Markov inequality and (
4.15
), we obtain the following for some
C_{\eta}>0

\begin{split}\bm{P}\Big(&\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{t}|\geqslant M,\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}>\eta,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}<\frac{1}{\eta}\Big)\\
&\leqslant\frac{1}{M^{2}}\bm{E}\Big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{t}|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}>\eta,\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}<\frac{1}{\eta}\}}\Big]\\
&\leqslant\frac{2}{M^{2}}\bm{E}\Big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\int_{s=0}^{t}b(I_{s},\tau)dW_{s}(I_{s},\tau)d\tau\Big|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}>\eta,\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}<\frac{1}{\eta}\}}\Big]\\
&\quad+\frac{C_{\eta}}{M^{2}}\,.\end{split}

Indeed, since we are on the event
\{\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}>\eta,\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}<\frac{1}{\eta}\}
we avoid the singularity of the function
b
at
I=0
and keep
I
bounded. Therefore, we can directly bound the drift term in (
4.15
) by bounding continuous functions
a,b,\partial_{I}a,\partial_{I}b
by some constant
C_{\eta}
.
We will subsequently let
C_{\eta}
be a constant that only depends on
\eta
whose value may change from line to line. The details are as follows.

Write the Fourier expansions of
a
and
b
as

a(I,\tau)=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}e^{2i\pi n\tau}a_{n}(I),\qquad b(I,\tau)=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{2i\pi n\tau}b_{n}(I)\,.

Considering only the first integrand in the drift term in (
4.15
) and substituting the above expansions, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\partial_{I}\big(b(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)_{|I=I_{t}}a(I_{t},\tau)\,d\tau\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\Big[\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big((\partial_{I}b)_{n}(I_{t})+2i\pi n\omega^{\prime}(I_{t})ub_{n}(I_{t})\Big)e^{2i\pi n(\tau+\omega(I_{t})u)}

\displaystyle\hskip 120.00018pt\cdot\Big(\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}a_{n^{\prime}}(I_{t})e^{2i\pi n^{\prime}\tau}\Big)\Big]\,d\tau\,du

(6.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\Big((\partial_{I}b)_{n}(I_{t})+2i\pi n\omega^{\prime}(I_{t})ub_{n}(I_{t})\Big)a_{-n}(I_{t})e^{2i\pi n\omega(I_{t})u}\,du

(6.12)

\displaystyle=R_{1}+R_{2}\,,

where

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}(\partial_{I}b)_{n}(I_{t})a_{-n}(I_{t})\cos(2\pi n\omega(I_{t})u)\,du

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}-\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}(\partial_{I}b)_{n}(I_{t})a_{-n}(I_{t})\cdot 2\pi n\omega^{\prime}(I_{t})u\sin(2\pi n\omega(I_{t})u)\,du\,.

Here (
6.11
) followed by expanding the double sum in
n
and
n^{\prime}
, integrating in
\tau
, and observing that the only terms that do not vanish are those with
n+n^{\prime}=0
.
The last equality (
6.12
) followed since the expression is real-valued and so must equal its real part.

Using (
5.2
) to bound the integral in
u
and bounding
(\partial_{I}b)_{n}(I_{t})
,
a_{-n}(I_{t})
by some constant
C_{\eta}
, we have that

|R_{1}|\leqslant C_{\eta}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\leqslant C_{\eta}\,.

Similarly, we can estimate that

\displaystyle\int_{u=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle R(u)\cdot 2\pi nu\sin(2\pi n\omega(I_{t})u)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{u=0}^{\infty}\Big(\int_{S}r(p)e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}u}\,dp\Big)\cdot 2\pi nu\sin(2\pi n\omega(I_{t})u)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\,dp\,r(p)\Big(\int_{u=0}^{\infty}2\pi nu\sin(2\pi n\omega(I_{t})u)e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}u}\,du\Big)

\displaystyle=2\int_{S}\,dp\,r(p)\frac{(2\pi n)^{2}\omega(I_{t})\mu|p|^{2\beta}}{(\mu^{2}|p|^{4\beta}+4\pi^{2}n^{2}\omega^{2}(I_{t}))^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{\omega_{0}}}{n^{2}}\int_{S}|p|^{2\beta}r(p)\,dp\,.

Therefore, we also have

|R_{2}|\leq C_{\eta}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\leq C_{\eta}\,.

Combining these estimates we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{u=0}^{\infty}R(u)\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\partial_{I}\big(b(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)_{|I=I_{t}}a(I_{t},\tau)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+b(I_{t},\tau+\omega(I_{t})u)\partial_{I}a(I_{t},\tau)\,d\tau\,du\,\Bigr|\leqslant C_{\eta}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\leqslant C_{\eta}\,.

For the stochastic integral term, we introduce a smooth function
b^{\eta}
such that for any
\tau\in(0,1)
we have

b^{\eta}(I,\tau)=b(I,\tau)\quad\text{if}\quad I>\eta\qquad\text{and}\qquad b^{\eta}(I,\tau)=0\quad\text{if}\quad I<\frac{\eta}{2}\,.

Then

\begin{split}\bm{E}\Big[&\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\int_{s=0}^{t}b(I_{s},\tau)dW_{s}(I_{s},\tau)d\tau\Big|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}>\eta,\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}<\frac{1}{\eta}\}}\Big]\\
&=\bm{E}\Big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|\int_{\tau=0}^{1}\int_{s=0}^{t}b^{\eta}(I_{s},\tau)dW_{s}(I_{s},\tau)d\tau\Big|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}>\eta,\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}<\frac{1}{\eta}\}}\Big]\\
&\leqslant C_{\eta}\,,\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality to bound the supremum of a continuous local martingale.
Finally, for any
0<\eta<1
we have

\limsup_{M\to\infty}\bm{P}^{0}(\zeta^{\prime}_{M}\leqslant T)\leqslant\bm{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}\geqslant\frac{1}{\eta}\Big)+\bm{P}\Big(\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}\leqslant\eta\Big),

and

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to 0}\bm{P}\Big(\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}\leqslant\eta\Big)

\displaystyle=\bm{P}\Big(\inf_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}=0\Big)=0\,,

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to 0}\bm{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}\geqslant\frac{1}{\eta}\Big)

\displaystyle=0\quad\quad(\text{by Lemma~\ref{bound_I}})\,.

As a result, we obtain

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{\varepsilon}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]=\bm{E}^{\bm{P}^{0}}[\Phi(y_{t},t\in[0,T])]\,,

which concludes the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

It remains to prove Proposition
6.1
, and we do this in the next section.

7.
Proof of Proposition
6.1

We will prove convergence of the family
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
by first showing tightness, and then showing all subsequential limits are solutions of a well-posed martingale problem with generator (
6.7
).
This follows the classical approach to prove weak convergence of a sequence of diffusions using martingale problem that is well developed in
[
SV06
]
.
However, because the processes involved are not diffusions (and often not even Markovian), we use pseudo-generators and the perturbed test function method (see for instance
[
Kus84
]
).

7.1.
Pseudo-generator and Perturbed test function method

In this section, we follow
[
Kus84
, Chapter 3]
.
Even though the pair of processes
(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon},\tau_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geqslant 0}
is Markov for each
\varepsilon>0
, the process
(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geqslant 0}
by itself is
not
Markov.
Since analyzing the pair
(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon},\tau_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geqslant 0}
is difficult, we study the process
(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geqslant 0}
on its own by using the pseudo-generator.
This allows us to apply martingale techniques to the non-Markovian process
(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.

7.1.1.
Pseudo-generator

Let

\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{G}_{t/\varepsilon^{2}}\,,

where
\mathcal{G}_{t}
is defined by (
3.4
),
and
\mathcal{S}^{\varepsilon}
be the set of all measurable functions
h(t)
, adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}_{t})
, for which
\sup_{t\leqslant T}\bm{E}[\lvert h(t)\rvert]<+\infty
, and where
T>0
is fixed. The
\plim
and the pseudo-generator are defined as follows. Let
h
and
h^{\delta}
in
\mathcal{S}^{\varepsilon}
for all
\delta>0
. We say that
h=\plim_{\delta}h^{\delta}
if

\sup_{t,\delta}\bm{E}\lvert h^{\delta}(t)\rvert<+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\bm{E}\lvert h^{\delta}(t)-h(t)\rvert=0\quad\forall t\geqslant 0\,.

We say that
h\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})
the domain of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}h=g
if
h
and
g
are in
\mathcal{S}^{\varepsilon}
and

\plim_{\delta\to 0}\Bigl(\frac{\bm{E}^{\varepsilon}_{t}h(t+\delta)-h(t)}{\delta}-g(t)\Bigr)=0\,,

where
\bm{E}^{\varepsilon}_{t}
is the conditional expectation given
\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}_{t}
. The difference with respect to the usual infinitesimal generator is that we have
average
limit (via
\plim
) rather than pointwise limit. This greatly helps us average out the noise in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. A useful result
[
Kus84
, Theorem 1, p. 39]
about the pseudo-generator
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
that will be central in our proof is the following.

Proposition 7.1

(Theorem 1, p. 30 in
[
Kus84
]
)
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})
. Then

M_{h}^{\varepsilon}(t)=h(t)-h(0)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}h(u)\,du

is a
(\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}_{t})
-martingale.

In other words, we introduce an operator
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
on functions of
Y^{\varepsilon}
having similar properties to those of an infinitesimal operator of a Markov process; more specifically, it satisfies a martingale problem.

7.1.2.
Perturbed test function method

The main idea behind the perturbed test function method is the following.
To characterize the limiting process
through a martingale problem with generator
\mathcal{A}
, we can then try to compare
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}h
with
\mathcal{A}h
for some test function
h
.
Unfortunately,
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}h
may have singular terms in
\varepsilon
, and we cannot proceed that way directly.
Instead, try and extract the effective statistical behavior of the system under consideration from
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
by introducing
h^{\varepsilon}
an appropriate perturbation of
h
.
In other words, if we can show
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bm{E}|h^{\varepsilon}(t)-h(t)|=0
and
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bm{E}|\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}h^{\varepsilon}(t)-\mathcal{A}h(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})|=0
for each
t
, then we can use Proposition
7.1
to conclude that the limiting process
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfies the martingale problem with generator
\mathcal{A}
.

To depict how the perturbation
h^{\varepsilon}
is built, let us briefly remind its construction in the simpler situation

\dot{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbf{F}\Big(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big),\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{F}(y,v,\tau)=v\,F(y,\tau)\,,

where
\nu
is a centered ergodic Markov process with invariant measure
\mu
, and generator
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
satisfying the Fredholm alternative. In this situation, to apply the perturbed test function method, we consider the extended Markov process
Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}=(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu(t/\varepsilon^{2}),\tau_{t/\varepsilon^{2}})
whose infinitesimal generator is given by

\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}Q+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{F}(y,v,\tau)\cdot\nabla_{y}\qquad\text{with}\qquad Q=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{\nu}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\Big)\,.

Looking for a perturbation
h^{\varepsilon}
of
h
of the form

h^{\varepsilon}(y,v,\tau)=h(y)+\varepsilon h_{1}(y,v,\tau)+\varepsilon^{2}h_{2}(y,v,\tau)\,,

we have

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}h^{\varepsilon}(y,v,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big(Qh_{1}(y,v,\tau)+\mathbf{F}(y,v,\tau)\cdot\nabla_{y}h(y)\big)

\displaystyle+Qh_{2}(y,v,\tau)+\mathbf{F}(y,v,\tau)\cdot\nabla_{y}h_{1}(y,v,\tau)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\,.

To cancel the singular term we have to solve a Poisson problem of the form

Qp=-q(y,v,\tau)\,.

Assuming the centering condition

\int_{0}^{1}\bm{E}_{\mu}[q(y,\nu(0),\tau)]\,d\tau=0\,,

where the expectation is taken with respect to the invariant measure
\mu
of the process
\nu
, the Poisson problem admits

p=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{uQ}q\,du

as a solution, so that

p(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{E}\Big[q\Big(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu\Big(u+\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big),u+\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\,\Big|\,\nu(s/\varepsilon^{2}),\,s\leqslant t\Big]\,du\,,

from the Markov property. Remembering that
\nu
is centered and
F
is mean zero with respect to
\tau
, we then have

(7.2)

h_{1}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{E}\Big[\mathbf{F}\Big(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu\Big(u+\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big),u+\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\cdot\nabla_{y}h(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,\Big|\,\nu(s/\varepsilon^{2}),\,s\leqslant t\Big]\,du\,.

To treat the term of order 1 in (
7.1
), which still has fast oscillations through
h_{1}
, we solve again the Poisson problem but this time with

(7.3)

q(y,v,\tau)=\mathbf{F}(y,v,\tau)\cdot\nabla_{y}h_{1}(y,v,\tau)-\int_{0}^{1}\bm{E}_{\mu}[\mathbf{F}(y,\nu(0),\tau^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{y}h_{1}(y,\nu(0),\tau^{\prime})]d\tau^{\prime}\,,

which satisfies the above centering condition. As a result, we can chose

(7.4)

\displaystyle h_{2}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{E}\Big[(\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla_{y}h_{1})\Big(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu\Big(u+\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big),u+\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\,\Big|\,\nu(s/\varepsilon^{2}),\,s\leqslant t\Big]du

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\bm{E}_{\mu}[\mathbf{F}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu(0),\tau^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{y}h_{1}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu(0),\tau^{\prime})]\,d\tau^{\prime}\,.

Finally, due to the centering in (
7.3
), the expansion (
7.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}h^{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{E}_{\mu}[\mathbf{F}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu(0),\tau)\cdot\nabla_{y}(\mathbf{F}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t},\nu(u),\tau+u)\cdot\nabla_{y}h(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}))]\,du\,d\tau

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\,,

and we obtain the same expression as in (
6.7
).

However, considering non-Markovian fluctuations, as well as long-range correlations, and a more complex periodic structure, make the construction of
h^{\varepsilon}
more delicate. The main concern is to handle the non-integrability at infinity of the correlation function of
V
. To overcome this difficulty we must take advantage of the periodic structure as in the proof of Proposition
1.1
. The details are in the next section where we will construct
h^{\varepsilon}
to show tightness of
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
, which implies imply weak subsequential convergence of stochastic processes.

Before going into the proofs, we remind the reader that
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is defined via a truncated process in equation (
6.2
) and there is an omitted index
M
in the functions

and
G
to indicate the truncation. This allows us to bound several expressions in the proofs in terms of

7.2.

In this section, we prove the tightness of the family

, seen as a family of continuous-time processes. Then, according to

it suffices to prove the tightness of

The proof of Proposition

Lemma 7.3

7.3.
Identification of the limit

In this section, we identify all the limit points of
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
via a well-posed martingale problem as stated in the following proposition. By abuse of notations, we still denote by
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
a converging subsequence and by
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
a limit point.

Proposition 7.5
.

All the limit points
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
of
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
are solutions of a well-posed martingale problem with generator

(7.9)

\mathcal{L}h(y)=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\tilde{F}(0,y,\tau)\cdot\nabla_{y}(\tilde{F}(u,y,\tau)\cdot\nabla_{y}h(y))

with

\tilde{F}(u,y,\tau)=F(y,\tau+\omega(y)u)\,.

To prove this proposition we use the notion of pseudo-generator introduced in Section
7.1.1
and the perturbed-test-function technique that we have already used in Section
7.2
for the proof of tightness. Thanks to the pseudo-generator we can characterize the subsequential limits of
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
as solutions of a well-posed martingale problem.

The outline of the proof is as follows.
Recall in Section
7.2
we saw that
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(h^{\varepsilon}_{0})
has a singular
1/\varepsilon
term, and modified the test function
h^{\varepsilon}_{0}
with a small perturbation
h^{\varepsilon}_{1}
to remove this singular term.
As a result,
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(h^{\varepsilon}_{0}+h^{\varepsilon}_{1})
is not singular any more.
However, it is not yet the generator of a martingale problem as
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(h^{\varepsilon}_{0}+h^{\varepsilon}_{1})
still has oscillatory terms in the form of
e^{2i\pi n\tau_{t}^{\varepsilon}}
.
We will show that these terms essentially vanish as
\varepsilon\to 0
by introducing a small perturbation
h_{2}^{\varepsilon}(t)
to cancel these oscillations, and then construct another perturbation
h_{3}^{\varepsilon}(t)
to cancel oscillations that result from computing
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(h_{2}^{\varepsilon})(t)
.
After we have constructed the perturbed test function

h^{\varepsilon}(t)=h_{0}^{\varepsilon}(t)+h_{1}^{\varepsilon}(t)+h_{2}^{\varepsilon}(t)+h^{\varepsilon}_{3}(t)\,,

we show that the pseudo-generator

\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(h_{0}^{\varepsilon}+h_{1}^{\varepsilon}+h_{2}^{\varepsilon}+h^{\varepsilon}_{3})(t)

has the desired form of a generator related to a martingale problem, plus a negligible term. Combining with tightness, we can show that the limiting process
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfies a martingale problem.

We now carry out this approach.
For convenience, the proofs of needed auxiliary lemmas will be deferred to Sections

-
7.8

\bm{E}|R^{\varepsilon}_{2}(t)|\leqslant\varepsilon\tilde{C}\,.

so that we only have to take care of
R^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t)
. For this term we consider the decomposition

\begin{split}R^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t)&=\sum_{q=0}^{[t/(\varepsilon\eta)]-1}\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}_{2}(u)-\mathcal{L}h(Y^{\varepsilon}_{u}))\,du\\
&=\sum_{q=0}^{[t/(\varepsilon\eta)]-1}\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}\,du\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\iint\Big(V\Big(\frac{u}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp\Big)V\Big(\frac{u}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp^{\prime}\Big)-r(p)\delta(p-p^{\prime})\,dp\,dp^{\prime}\Big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\cdot W_{n}(p,Y^{\varepsilon}_{q\varepsilon\eta})\\
&\qquad+\sum_{q=0}^{[t/(\varepsilon\eta)]-1}\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}\,du\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\iint\Big(V\Big(\frac{u}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp\Big)V\Big(\frac{u}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp^{\prime}\Big)-r(p)\delta(p-p^{\prime})\,dp\,dp^{\prime}\Big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\cdot\Big(W_{n}(p,Y^{\varepsilon}_{u})-W_{n}(p,Y^{\varepsilon}_{q\varepsilon\eta})\Big)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}R^{\varepsilon}_{11}(t)+R^{\varepsilon}_{12}(t)\,.\end{split}

For
R^{\varepsilon}_{12}(t)
, we have

\begin{split}\bm{E}|R^{\varepsilon}_{12}(t)|&\leqslant\frac{C_{h,\omega_{0},\nabla\omega,F}}{\varepsilon}\sum_{q=0}^{[t/(\varepsilon\eta)]-1}\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}du\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{u}du^{\prime}\int dp\,r(p)\leqslant\eta\tilde{C}\,,\end{split}

following the computations in the proof of Lemma
7.7
.
It only remains to treat
R^{\varepsilon}_{11}(t)
, and this term requires more care.
We have

\begin{split}\bm{E}|R^{\varepsilon}_{11}(t)|&\leqslant C_{h,\omega_{0},\nabla\omega,F^{M}}\sum_{q=0}^{[t/(\varepsilon\eta)]-1}\sqrt{I^{\varepsilon}_{q,\eta}}\,,\end{split}

with

I^{\varepsilon}_{q,\eta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{E}\Bigl[\Bigl|\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}du\iint\Big[V\Big(\frac{u}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp\Big)V\Big(\frac{u}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp^{\prime}\Big)-r(p)\delta(p-p^{\prime})\,dp\,dp^{\prime}\Big]\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr].

Using Gaussian property of
V
, we have

\begin{split}I^{\varepsilon}_{q,\eta}&=\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}du_{1}\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}du_{2}\\
&\qquad\cdot\int\Big(\bm{E}\Big[V\Big(\frac{u_{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp_{1}\Big)V\Big(\frac{u_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp_{2}\Big)\Big]\bm{E}\Big[V\Big(\frac{u_{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp^{\prime}_{1}\Big)V\Big(\frac{u_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp^{\prime}_{2}\Big)\Big]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\bm{E}\Big[V\Big(\frac{u_{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp_{1}\Big)V\Big(\frac{u_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp^{\prime}_{2}\Big)\Big]\bm{E}\Big[V\Big(\frac{u_{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp^{\prime}_{1}\Big)V\Big(\frac{u_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}},dp_{2}\Big)\Big]\Big),\end{split}

and by the symmetry between
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, we can write

\begin{split}I^{\varepsilon}_{q,\eta}&\leqslant C_{h,\omega_{0},\nabla\omega,F}\iint\,dp\,dp^{\prime}\,r(p)r(p^{\prime})\\
&\qquad\cdot\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}du_{1}\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{u_{1}}du_{2}\,e^{-\mu(|p|^{2\beta}+|p^{\prime}|^{2\beta})(u_{1}-u_{2})/\varepsilon^{2}}.\end{split}

Now, we split the integration domain of
p
into the region where
|p|\leqslant\nu
, and the region where
|p|>\nu
.
This leads to the decomposition

I^{\varepsilon}_{q,\eta}\leqslant J^{\varepsilon}_{1,q,\nu}+J^{\varepsilon}_{2,q,\nu}.

For the second term (when
|p|>\nu
), we have

\begin{split}\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{(q+1)\varepsilon\eta}\,du_{1}\int_{q\varepsilon\eta}^{u_{1}}\,du_{2}\,e^{-\mu(|p|^{2\beta}+|p^{\prime}|^{2\beta})(u_{1}-u_{2})/\varepsilon^{2}}&\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\mu|p|^{2\beta}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon\eta}(1-e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}u_{1}/\varepsilon^{2}})\,du\\
&\leqslant C_{\eta,\nu}\varepsilon^{3}\,,\end{split}

so that

\sum_{q=0}^{[t/(\varepsilon\eta)]-1}\sqrt{J^{\varepsilon}_{2,q,\eta}}\leqslant\varepsilon^{1/2}C_{\eta,\nu,T}\,.

For the first term (when
\lvert p\rvert\leqslant\nu
), we have

\begin{split}\sum_{q=0}^{[t/(\varepsilon\eta)]-1}\sqrt{J^{\varepsilon}_{1,q,\nu}}&\leqslant C_{T,\eta}\Big(\int_{\{|p|\leqslant\nu\}\times S}\,dp\,dp^{\prime}\,r(p)r(p^{\prime})\Big)^{1/2}\\
&\leqslant C_{T,\eta}\Big(\int_{\{|p|\leqslant\nu\}}\frac{dp}{|p|^{2\alpha}}\Big)^{1/2}\,.\end{split}

Since
\alpha<1/2
, the dominated convergence theorem implies that this converges to
0
.
This concludes the proof of Lemma
7.6
.

7.8.
Well-posedness of the martingale problem

We devote this section to showing that the martingale problem with generator
\mathcal{L}
(see equation (
7.9
)) is well-posed.
First, we rewrite
\mathcal{L}h(y)
as

\mathcal{L}h(y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,l=1}^{2}\mathfrak{a}_{jl}(y)\partial^{2}_{x_{l}x_{j}}h(y)+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\partial_{x_{j}}h(y)\mathfrak{b}_{j}(y),

where

(7.16)

\begin{split}\mathfrak{a}_{jl}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}R_{n}(y)F_{j,n}(y)F_{-l,n}(y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}R_{n}(y)F_{j,n}(y)\overline{F_{l,n}(y)}\end{split}

with (equivalently as in formula (
5.2
))

(7.17)

\begin{split}R_{n}(y)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int dp\,r(p)\Big(\frac{1}{\mu|p|^{2\beta}-2i\pi n\omega(y)}+\frac{1}{\mu|p|^{2\beta}+2i\pi n\omega(y)}\Big)\\
&=\int dp\,r(p)\frac{2\mu|p|^{2\beta}}{\mu|p|^{4\beta}+4\pi^{2}n^{2}\omega^{2}(y)}\\
&=2\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\bm{E}[v(u)v(0)]\cos(2\pi n\omega(y)u)\,,\end{split}

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{j}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\int dp\,\frac{r(p)}{\mu|p|^{2\beta}-2i\pi n\omega(y)}

(7.18)

\displaystyle\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{l=1}^{2}\Bigl(\partial_{x_{l}}F_{j,n}(y)F_{l,-n}(y)-\frac{2i\pi n}{\mu|p|^{2\beta}-2i\pi n\omega(y)}F_{j,n}(y)F_{l,-n}(y)\partial_{x_{l}}\omega(y)\Bigr)\Bigr).

The uniqueness of the martingale problem is guaranteed by
[
KS91
, Corollary 4.9 p. 317, Proposition 3.20 p. 309, and Theorem 2.5 p. 287]
together with
[
Fri06
, Theorem 1.2 p. 129]
, where the latter provides the locally Lipschitz behavior of a nonnegative square root. It is not hard to see that
\mathfrak{b}
is locally Lipschitz with respect to
y
, as well as
\mathfrak{a}(y)
is a symmetric nonnegative matrix, which is
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with respect to
y
. The smoothness property is direct from (
7.16
) and (
7.17
). Regarding the nonnegativity, we have for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(7.19)

x^{T}\mathfrak{a}(y)x=\big<{\sigma}^{*}(y)(x),{\sigma}^{*}(y)(x)\big>_{\mathscr{H}}\geqslant 0

with the Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}=\Big\{u:\mathbb{Z}^{*}\to\mathbb{C}:\overline{u_{-n}}=u_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}|u_{n}|^{2}<\infty\Big\}\,,

the adjoint
{\sigma}_{M}^{*}
is an operator from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathscr{H}
defined by

{\sigma}_{M}^{*}(y)(n,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}R^{1/2}_{n}(y)F_{1,n}(y)x_{1}+R^{1/2}_{n}(y)F_{2,n}(y)x_{2}\,,

and
\sigma
, from
\mathscr{H}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, is defined by

(7.20)

{\sigma}(y)(u,j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}R^{1/2}_{n}(y)F_{j,n}(y)u_{-n}\qquad j=1,2\,.

Hence, we have an explicit self-adjoint Hilbert-Schmidt square root for
\mathfrak{a}
that we use in Section
8
to derive a SDE for the process
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. It is also clear that
\sigma
is locally Lipschitz with respect to
y
, so that we can conclude the uniqueness of the martingale problem.

Let us finish by rewriting the martingale problem in a more convenient way and let us start with
\mathfrak{b}
. Using the fact that

\begin{split}\frac{-2i\pi n\partial_{x_{l}}\omega(y)}{(\mu|p|^{2\beta}-2i\pi n\omega(y))^{2}}&=\partial_{y_{l}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\mu|p|^{2\beta}-2i\pi n\omega(y))u}\,du\\
&=2i\pi n\partial_{x_{l}}\omega(y)\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,u\,e^{-(\mu|p|^{2\beta}-2i\pi n\omega(y))u}\,,\end{split}

we sum Fourier series in (
7.8
) to obtain

\mathfrak{b}(y)=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\textrm{Jac}_{y}[\tilde{F}(u,y,\tau)]\tilde{F}(0,y,\tau)\,,

where

\tilde{F}(u,y,\tau)=F(y,\tau+\omega(y)u)\,.

For
\mathfrak{a}
we have the following formulation

\begin{split}\mathfrak{a}_{jl}(y)&=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\Big(\tilde{F}_{j}(u,y,\tau)\tilde{F}_{l}(0,y,\tau)+\tilde{F}_{j}(0,y,\tau)\tilde{F}_{l}(u,y,\tau)\Big)\\
&=2\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\tilde{F}_{j}(u,y,\tau)\tilde{F}_{l}(0,y,\tau),\quad j,l\in\{1,2\}\,.\end{split}

Finally, to conclude, we just have to remark that according to (
6.4
), as well as the definition of
\mathfrak{a}
involving the integration of the periodic variable over a whole period, we obtain the formulation (
7.9
).

8.
Proof of Proposition
6.2

The proof of this proposition follows the treatment in
[
KS91
, Proposition 4.6 p. 315]
and consists of deriving the SDE corresponding to the martingale problem with generator (
6.7
). To prove this correspondence, we consider first the function
h_{j}(y)=y_{j}
for any
j\in\{1,2\}
, so that

(8.1)

M^{0}_{j}(t)=Y_{j,t}-Y_{j,0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{b}_{j}(Y_{s})\,ds

is a martingale, where
\mathfrak{b}
is defined as
\mathfrak{b}^{M}
in (
7.8
). Proceeding the same way choosing
h_{jl}(y)=y_{j}y_{l}
one can show that for any
j,l\in\{1,2\}

M^{0}_{j}(t)M^{0}_{l}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{a}_{jl}(Y_{s})\,ds

is a martingale.
(Recall,
\mathfrak{a}
is defined by (
7.16
).)
Therefore
M^{0}
is a martingale with quadratic variation given by

t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{a}_{jl}(Y_{s})\,ds\,.

Using (
7.19
), we note
\mathfrak{a}=\sigma{\sigma}^{*}
, where
\sigma
is defined by (
7.20
).
We can now apply a standard martingale representation theorem involving Hilbert spaces (see
[
DPZ96
, Theorem 8.2]
for instance).
This guarantees the existence of a complex valued cylindrical Brownian motion
B
(
B=(B^{1}-iB^{2})/\sqrt{2}
, with
B^{1}
and
B^{2}
being two independent real valued cylindrical Brownian motions) on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{T}
,
possibly defined on an extension of the probability space under consideration, and such that with probability one

M^{0}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(Y_{s})\,dB_{s}\\

for all
t\geqslant 0
. As a result, from (
8.1
) we obtain the following SDE for
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
,

dY_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})dB_{t}+\mathfrak{b}(Y_{t})dt\,.

Note that, because of symmetries, and that for the cylindrical Brownian motion on
\mathcal{T}
we necessarily have
B^{1}_{-n}=B^{1}_{n}
and
B^{2}_{-n}=-B^{2}_{n}
, this defines a real valued equation.

Regarding
\sigma
, one can write

(8.2)

\begin{split}\sigma_{j}(Y_{t})dB_{t}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\tilde{F}_{j,n}(Y_{t})R^{1/2}_{n}(Y_{t})(dB^{1}_{t,n}-idB^{2}_{t,n})\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\tilde{F}_{j}(Y_{t},\tau)\,dW_{t}(Y_{t},\tau)\end{split}

where

dW_{t}(y,\tau)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}e^{2i\pi n\tau}R^{1/2}_{n}(y)(dB^{1}_{t,n}-idB^{2}_{t,n})

is a real valued Brownian field with covariance function

\begin{split}\bm{E}W_{t}(y,\tau)W_{s}(y,\tau^{\prime})&=t\wedge s\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}e^{2i\pi n(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}R_{n}(y)\\
&=t\wedge s\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}e^{2i\pi n(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\int dp\,r(p)\\
&\qquad\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\Big[e^{-(\mu|p|^{2\beta}+2i\pi n\omega(y))u}+e^{-(\mu|p^{\prime}|^{2\beta}-2i\pi n\omega(y))u}\Big]\\
&=t\wedge s\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\\
&\qquad\cdot\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\Big[e^{2i\pi n(\tau-\tau^{\prime}+\omega(y)u)}+e^{2i\pi n(\tau-\tau^{\prime}-\omega(y)u)}\Big].\end{split}

so that for any test functions
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
in the Hilbert space

L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})=\Big\{\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}):\int_{0}^{1}\phi(\tau)d\tau=0\Big\}\,,

the covariance function reads

\begin{split}\bm{E}W_{t}(y,\phi_{1})W_{s}(y,\phi_{2})&=t\wedge s\int_{0}^{\infty}\,du\,R(u)\\
&\cdot\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\Big[\phi_{1}(\tau+\omega(y)u)\phi_{2}(\tau)+\phi_{1}(\tau)\phi_{2}(\tau+\omega(y)u)\Big]\,.\end{split}

To determine the SDE satisfied by
(I^{M}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
we just have to consider the first coordinate of
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. Then, we have

dI^{M}_{t}=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\,a^{M}(I^{M}_{t},\tau)\,dW_{t}(I^{M}_{t},\tau)+\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\tilde{A}(u,I^{M}_{t},\tau)\,dt

with

\begin{split}\tilde{A}(u,I,\tau)&=\partial_{I}\big(a^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)a^{M}(I,\tau)\\
&+\partial_{\tau}a^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big(b^{M}(I,\tau)-\langle b^{M}(I)\rangle\big)\,.\end{split}

Using the fact that
a^{M}
is mean-zero in
\tau
, and integrating by parts, we have

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\,\partial_{\tau}&a^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big(b^{M}(I,\tau)-\langle b^{M}(I)\rangle\big)\\
=&\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\partial_{\tau}a^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)b^{M}(I,\tau)-\int_{0}^{1}d\tau a^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\partial_{\tau}b^{M}(I,\tau)\,.\end{split}

Now, we restrict our study for
t\leqslant\eta_{M}
so that, according to (
4.10
), we have for
I\in(1/M,M)

\partial_{\tau}b^{M}(I,\tau)=\partial_{\tau}b(I,\tau)=\partial_{\tau}\partial_{I}\varphi_{1}^{-1}(I,\tau)=\partial_{I}\partial_{\tau}\varphi_{1}^{-1}(I,\tau)=-\partial_{I}a(I,\tau)\,,

so that

\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\tilde{A}(u,I,\tau)=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\,\partial_{I}\big(a(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)a(I,\tau)+a(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\partial_{I}a(I,\tau).

We then obtain the desired result for the stopped process
\tilde{I}^{M}_{t}=I^{M}_{t\wedge\eta_{M}}
. Now for the process
\psi^{M}_{t\wedge\eta_{M}}
, we look at the second coordinate of
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, and we have

d\psi^{M}_{t}=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\,b^{M}(I^{M}_{t},\tau)\,dW_{t}(I^{M}_{t},\tau)+\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,R(u)\tilde{B}(u,I^{M}_{t},\tau)\,dt

with

\begin{split}\tilde{B}(u,I,\tau)&=\partial_{I}\big(b^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)-\langle b^{M}(I)\rangle\big)a^{M}(I,\tau)\\
&\qquad+\partial_{\tau}(b^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)-\langle b^{M}(I)\rangle)\big(b^{M}(I,\tau)-\langle b^{M}(I)\rangle\big)\,.\end{split}

Using again that
a^{M}
and
\partial_{\tau}b^{M}
are mean-zero in
\tau
, together with an integration by parts, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\,\tilde{B}(u,I,\tau)&=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\,\partial_{I}\big(b^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\big)a^{M}(I,\tau)\\
&\qquad-\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\,b^{M}(I,\tau+\omega(I)u)\partial_{\tau}b^{M}(I,\tau)\,.\end{split}

Since
for
I\in(1/M,M)
we know

\partial_{\tau}b^{M}(I,\tau)=-\partial_{I}a(I,\tau)\,,

the proof of Proposition
6.2
is complete.

C(\varepsilon)>0
goes to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. To this end we first remark that there is a compact embedding of
\tilde{D}_{k,M}
into
[0,T]\times C^{0}(S)
equipped with the metric

(D.1)

\|(t,\varphi)\|_{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{|t|}/\varepsilon+\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\,.

Let us also recall
[
Bre11
, Theorem 8.8]
that there exists
C_{k}>0
such that for any
\varphi\in W^{1,k}(S)
, we have

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leqslant C_{k}\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,k}}\,.

We also remark that for the pseudometric

d_{V,\varepsilon}((t,\varphi),(s,\psi))=\bm{E}[|V(t/\varepsilon^{2},\varphi)-V(s/\varepsilon^{2},\psi)|^{2}]^{1/2}\,,

for which

(D.2)

\begin{split}d^{2}_{V,\varepsilon}((t,\varphi),(s,\psi))&=\int_{S}dp\,r(p)(\varphi^{2}(p)+\psi^{2}(p)-2\varphi(p)\psi(p)e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}|t-s|/\varepsilon^{2}})\\
&=\int_{S}dp\,r(p)(\varphi^{2}(p)+\psi^{2}(p))(1-e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}|t-s|/\varepsilon^{2}})\\
&+\int_{S}dp\,r(p)(\varphi(p)-\psi(p))^{2}e^{-\mu|p|^{2\beta}|t-s|/\varepsilon^{2}}\\
&\leqslant C_{k,M,r}\|(t,\varphi)-(s,\psi)\|^{2}_{\varepsilon}\,,\end{split}

we have

diam_{d_{V,\varepsilon}}(\tilde{D}_{k,M})\leqslant\sqrt{2C_{k,M,r}(T+M)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}c_{k,M,r}\,.

Note now that it is not easy to see if
\tilde{D}_{k,M}
is relatively compact equipped with
d_{V,\varepsilon}
, while it is required to apply
[
AT07
, Theorem 1.5.1 p. 41]
. The problem is that
d_{V,\varepsilon}
is only a pseudometric. For instance,
d_{V,\varepsilon}
does not separate well points, since we have for any
s
and
t

d_{V,\varepsilon}((t,0),(s,0))=0\,.

However, according to (
D.2
), the application
d_{V,\varepsilon}
provides a true metric on
\tilde{D}_{k,M}-[0,T]\times\{0\}
. Then, instead of working directly with
\tilde{D}_{k,M}
let us consider now the increasing sequence of
\|\cdot\|_{\varepsilon}
-relatively compact subset of
\tilde{D}_{k,M}

D^{n}_{k,M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\tilde{D}_{k,M}\cap[0,T]\times\big\{\varphi\in W^{1,k}(S):\,\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\geqslant 1/n\big\}\,.

Now, since
d_{V,\varepsilon}
is a metric on every
D^{n}_{k,M}
, it is straightforward to see from (
D.2
) that all the
D^{n}_{k,M}
are also
d_{V,\varepsilon}
-relatively compact from the sequential characterization of compactness.

We can now apply
[
AT07
, Theorem 1.5.1 p. 41 and Lemma 1.5.2 p. 44]
over all the
\overline{D^{n}_{k,M}}^{d_{V,\varepsilon}}
, we have

\begin{split}\bm{E}\sup_{(t,\varphi)\in D^{n}_{k,M}}V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi\Big)&\leqslant K\Big(c_{k,M,r}\ln(c_{k,M,r})+\int_{0}^{c_{k,M,r}}\sqrt{\ln(N_{\varepsilon}(r))}dr\Big)\,,\end{split}

where
N_{\varepsilon}(r)
is the smallest number of balls covering
D^{n}_{k,M}
with radius
r
, which are defined by

B(X,r)=\{Y\in D^{n}_{k,M}:\,d_{V,\varepsilon}(X,Y)<r\}\,.

Because of (
D.2
), it is clear that

N_{\varepsilon}(r)\leqslant\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\big(r/C^{1/2}_{k,M,r}\big)\,,

where
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(u)
stands for the smallest number of balls with radius
u
associated to the metric defined by the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\varepsilon}
. Since
D^{n}_{k,M}
is defined as a product space, one can determine the smallest number of balls with radius
r
that cover each of its components. For each parts, the metric is defined by the corresponding parts of the r.h.s of (
D.1
). Therefore, the smallest number of balls with radius
r
that cover
[0,T]
is of order
1/(r^{2}\varepsilon^{2})
, and for
\{\varphi\in W^{1,k}(S):\quad\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,k}}\leqslant M\}
it is of order
\exp(1/r)

[
BS67
, Theorem 5.2 p. 311]
. As a result, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

\begin{split}\bm{E}\sup_{(t,\varphi)\in D^{n}_{k,M}}V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi\Big)&\leqslant C_{1}+C_{2}\int_{0}^{c_{k,M,r}}\sqrt{\ln\Big(\frac{C\exp(1/r)}{r^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)}\,dr\,,\\
&\leqslant C^{\prime}_{1}+C^{\prime}_{2}\int_{0}^{c_{k,M,r}}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{r}}+\frac{C^{\prime}_{3}}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon c_{k,M,r}}\sqrt{\ln\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)}\,du\,.\end{split}

Finally, setting

C(\varepsilon)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}C^{\prime}_{3}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon c_{k,M,r}}\sqrt{\ln\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)}\,du\,,

and using the monotone convergence theorem, we have

E_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{E}\sup_{(t,\varphi)\in\tilde{D}_{k,M}}V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi\Big)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\bm{E}\sup_{(t,\varphi)\in D^{n}_{k,M}}V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi\Big)\leqslant C^{\prime\prime}_{1}+\frac{C(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\,.

We conclude the proof of the bound (
3.7
) using that

\bm{E}\sup_{(t,\varphi)\in\tilde{D}_{k,M}}\Big|V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi\Big)\Big|\leqslant 2E_{1}

by symmetry.

For proving (
3.8
), we first remark that by stationarity in
t
we have

\bm{E}\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}\Big|V\Big(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varphi\Big)\Big|^{n}=\bm{E}\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}|V(0,\varphi)|^{n}\,,

so that following the same lines as above by removing the
t
-dependence we have for the first order moment,

\bm{E}\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}|V(0,\varphi)|\leqslant 2\bm{E}\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}V(0,\varphi)\leqslant C_{1}\,.

Finally, for the arbitrary order moments we write

\begin{split}E^{\prime}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{E}\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}|V(0,\varphi)|^{n}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\bm{P}\Big(\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}|V(0,\varphi)|^{n}>u\Big)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\bm{P}\Big(\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}|V(0,\varphi)|>u^{1/n}\Big)\\
&\leqslant 2\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\bm{P}\Big(\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}V(0,\varphi)>u^{1/n}\Big)\,.\end{split}

Denoting

E^{\prime\prime}_{1}=\bm{E}\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}V(0,\varphi)\,,

we have

\begin{split}E^{\prime}_{n}&\leqslant 2{E^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{n}+2\int_{{E^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{n}}^{\infty}du\,\bm{P}\Big(\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}V(0,\varphi)-E^{\prime\prime}_{1}>u^{1/n}-E^{\prime\prime}_{1}\Big)\\
&\leqslant 2{E^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{n}+2\int_{{E^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{n}}^{\infty}du\,e^{-(u^{1/n}-E^{\prime\prime}_{1})^{2}/(2\sigma_{D}^{2})}\,,\end{split}

where the last line is given by
[
AT07
, Theorem 2.1.1 p. 50]

and

\sigma_{D}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}\bm{E}V(0,\varphi)^{2}=\sup_{\varphi\in W_{k,M}}\int dp\,r(p)\varphi(p)^{2}\leqslant C_{k}^{2}M^{2}\int dp\,r(p)\,.

As a result,

\begin{split}E^{\prime}_{n}&\leqslant 2{E^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{n}+2(n-1)\int_{E^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{\infty}dv\,v^{n-1}\,e^{-(v-E^{\prime\prime}_{1})^{2}/(2\sigma_{D}^{2})}\\
&\leqslant 2{E^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{n}+2(n-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\,v^{n-1}\,e^{-v^{2}/(2\sigma_{D}^{2})}\,,\end{split}

which concludes the proof of the bound (
3.8
).

\hfill\square

Appendix E
Proof of Proposition
4.2

The estimates for
a
,
\partial_{\theta}a
, and
\partial_{I}a
we prove in this section are based on the following estimates on the inverse map
\varphi^{-1}
.

Lemma E.1
.

There exist
r>0
small enough, and a constant
C_{r}>0
such that for any
I\in(0,r)

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}\Big(\|\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\|+\|\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\|\Big)\leqslant C_{r}\sqrt{I}\,,

and

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}\|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\|\leqslant\frac{C_{r}}{\sqrt{I}}\,.

The proof of this lemma is postponed to the end of this appendix.

In what follows we denote by

B_{r}=\varphi^{-1}((0,r)\times\mathbb{T})

with
r>0
small enough so that
B_{r}
describes a small neighborhood of
(0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

E.1.
Bound for
a
.

Let us remind the reader that

a(I,\theta)=e_{2}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{1}(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))=\partial_{y}\varphi_{1}(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))\,,

so that differentiating w.r.t.
y
the relation
K(I(x,y))=H(x,y)
we have

\partial_{y}I(x,y)=\partial_{y}\varphi_{1}(x,y)=\partial_{y}H(x,y)/\omega(I)\,.

Now, using that
\partial_{y}H(0,0)=0
, we have for any
I\in(0,r)

\begin{split}|a(I,\theta)|&\leqslant\omega^{-1}_{0}|\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))-\partial_{y}H(0,0)|\\
&\leqslant\omega^{-1}_{0}\sup_{B_{r}}\|\nabla\partial_{y}H\|\cdot\|\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\|\leqslant C_{r}\sqrt{I}.\end{split}

E.2.
Bound for
\partial_{\theta}a

Using that

(E.1)

a(I,\theta)=\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))/\omega(I)\,,

and now differentiating w.r.t.
\theta
, we obtain

\partial_{\theta}a(I,\theta)=\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\cdot\nabla\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))/\omega(I)\,.

Then, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\begin{split}|\partial_{\theta}a(I,\theta)|&\leqslant\omega^{-1}_{0}\|\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\|\sup_{B_{r}}\|\nabla\partial_{y}H\|\leqslant C_{r}\sqrt{I}\,.\end{split}

E.3.
Bound for
\partial_{I}a

Differentiating (
E.1
) w.r.t.
I
, we obtain

\begin{split}\partial_{I}a(I,\theta)&=-\frac{\omega^{\prime}(I)}{\omega^{2}(I)}\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))+\frac{1}{\omega(I)}\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\cdot\nabla\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}T_{1}+T_{2}\,.\end{split}

For
T_{1}
, using (
4.12
) and proceeding as for the function
a
to deal with the term
\partial_{y}H
we have

|T_{1}|\leqslant\frac{C_{r}}{\sqrt{I}}\,.

For
T_{2}
, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with Lemma
E.1
yields

|T_{2}|\leqslant\frac{C^{\prime}}{\sqrt{I}}\,,

which concludes the proof of Proposition
4.2
.

E.4.
Proof of Lemma
E.1

Before going into the proof of the lemma, let us remind the reader that
H
admits a unique minimum at
(0,0)
so that
\nabla^{2}H(0,0)
is a positive definite matrix

\nabla^{2}H(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{12}\\
\lambda_{12}&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}

with
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}

>0
. In this case, the inner product corresponding to this matrix defines a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which is equivalent to the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
. In other words, there exist two constants
\lambda
,
\bar{\lambda}>0
such that for any
X=(x,y)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(E.2)

\lambda|X|^{2}\leqslant X^{T}\nabla^{2}H(0,0)X\leqslant\bar{\lambda}|X|^{2}\,.

E.4.1.
Bound for
\varphi^{-1}

Since
(0,0)
is the unique minimum to
H
, a Taylor expansion at the second order gives us

(E.3)

K(I)=H(X)=\frac{1}{2}X^{T}\nabla^{2}H(0,0)X+o(|X|^{2})\,,

with
X=(x,y)^{T}
. For any
X\in B_{r}
we can have using (
E.2
) that

(E.4)

K(I)\geqslant\tilde{\lambda}|X|^{2}\geqslant\tilde{\lambda}y^{2}

with
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda/2-\mu>0
for some small
\mu
, yielding directly

|\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)|=|y|\leqslant\sqrt{K(I)/\tilde{\lambda}}=C\sqrt{K(I)-K(0)}\leqslant C\sup_{J\in(0,r)}\sqrt{\omega(J)}\sqrt{I}=C^{\prime}\sqrt{I}.

In the same way, we also have

|\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)|\leqslant C^{\prime}_{r}\sqrt{I}\,.

E.4.2.
Bound for
\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}

Differentiating w.r.t.
x
and then
y
the relation
K(I(X))=H(X)
we have

\partial_{x}\varphi_{1}(X)=\partial_{x}H(X)/\omega(I)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{y}\varphi_{1}(X)=\partial_{y}H(X)/\omega(I)\,,

so that using (
4.10
) we obtain

(E.5)

\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)=\frac{\partial_{x}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))}{\omega(I)}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)=\frac{-\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))}{\omega(I)}\,.

Therefore, we have

\|\partial_{\theta}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\|\leqslant\|\nabla H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))\|/\omega_{0}\,,

with

(E.6)

\begin{split}\|\nabla H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))\|&=\|\nabla H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))-\nabla H(0,0)\|\\
&\leqslant\sup_{B_{r}}\|\nabla^{2}H\|\cdot\|\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\|\leqslant C_{r}\sqrt{I}\,.\end{split}

E.4.3.
Bound for
\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}

Differentiating (
E.5
) w.r.t.
I
we obtain

\begin{split}\partial^{2}_{I\theta}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)&=-\frac{\omega^{\prime}(I)}{\omega^{2}(I)}\partial_{x}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))+\frac{1}{\omega(I)}\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\cdot\nabla\partial_{x}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))\\
\partial^{2}_{I\theta}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)&=-\frac{\omega^{\prime}(I)}{\omega^{2}(I)}\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))+\frac{1}{\omega(I)}\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta)\cdot\nabla\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))\,,\end{split}

so that using (
4.4
) and (
E.6
)

|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)|\leqslant\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{I}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\sup_{B_{r}}\|\nabla^{2}H\|\int_{0}^{\theta}\|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta^{\prime})\|d\theta^{\prime}\,,

and in the same way for
\varphi^{-1}_{2}

|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)|\leqslant\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{I}}+|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0)|+\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\sup_{B_{r}}\|\nabla^{2}H\|\int_{0}^{\theta}\|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta^{\prime})\|\,d\theta^{\prime}.

Letting

U(I,\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)|+|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)|\,,

and gathering the last two inequalities, we have

U(I,\theta)\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{I}}+|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0)|+C^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\theta}U(I,\theta^{\prime})\,d\theta^{\prime}\,.

Then, applying the Gronwall lemma, we obtain

\sup_{\theta\in(0,1)}U(I,\theta)\leqslant\Big(\frac{C}{\sqrt{I}}+|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0)|\Big)e^{C^{\prime}}\,.

Moreover, differentiating w.r.t.
I
the relation

H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))=K(I)

we have

\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)\partial_{x}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))+\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)\partial_{y}H(\varphi^{-1}(I,\theta))=\omega(I)

and setting
\theta=0
we have from (
4.3
) and (
4.4
)

\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0)\partial_{y}H(0,\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0))=\omega(I)\,.

Also, using that

\begin{split}\partial_{y}H(0,y)&=\partial_{y}H(0,0)+\partial^{2}_{yy}H(0,0)y+o(y)=\lambda_{2}y+o(y)\,,\end{split}

so that for any
(0,y)\in B_{r}
we have, for some small
\mu
,

|\partial_{y}H(0,y)|\geqslant(\lambda_{2}-\mu)|y|\,.

As a result,

|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0)|\leqslant\frac{\omega(I)}{(\lambda_{2}-\mu)|\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0)|}\,,

and from (
4.3
) together with (
E.3
), we have

K(I)=H(0,\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0))\leqslant\bar{\lambda}_{2}\big(\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0)\big)^{2},

with
\bar{\lambda}_{2}=\lambda_{2}/2+\mu
so that

|\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,0)|\geqslant\sqrt{K(I)/\bar{\lambda}_{2}}\geqslant\sqrt{c_{1}I/\bar{\lambda}_{2}}\,.

Finally, we obtain

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{1}(I,\theta)|+|\partial_{I}\varphi^{-1}_{2}(I,\theta)|\leqslant\frac{C_{r}}{\sqrt{I}}\,,

which gives the desired result and concludes the proof of the lemma.
