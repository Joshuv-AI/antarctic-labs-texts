Improving Generalization Bounds for VC Classes Using the Hypergeometric Tail Inversion
Jean-Samuel Leboeuf jean-samuel.leboeuf.1@ulaval.ca, Frédéric LeBlanc flebl035@uottawa.ca, Mario Marchand mario.marchand@ift.ulaval.ca

Abstract
We significantly improve the generalization bounds for VC classes by using two main ideas. First, we consider the hypergeometric tail inversion to obtain a very tight non-uniform distribution-independent risk upper bound for VC classes. Second, we optimize the ghost sample trick to obtain a further non-negligible gain. These improvements are then used to derive a relative deviation bound, a multiclass margin bound, as well as a lower bound. Numerical comparisons show that the new bound is nearly never vacuous, and is tighter than other VC bounds for all reasonable data set sizes.

Introduction

In their pioneering work,
Vapnik and Chervonenkis 1971
identified a key combinatorial property of classes of functions, now called the Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension, that characterizes the generalization properties of any learning algorithm operating on classes whose VC dimension is finite.
They showed that the difference between the true and empirical risks of a classifier is bounded from above in
{O}\big(\!\left(\frac{d}{m}\log\frac{m}{d}\right)^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}\big)

with high probability, where
m
is the sample size and
d
the VC dimension of the hypothesis class—a result known as
Vapnik’s pessimistic bound
.
A second result of
Vapnik and Chervonenkis 1971
(improved by
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
and
Vapnik 1998
), called
Vapnik’s relative deviation bound
, upper bounds the difference between the true and empirical risks of a classifier in
\smash[t]{\widetilde{{O}}\big(\frac{d+\sqrt{dk}}{m}\big)}
1
1

1

We use the
\widetilde{{O}}
notation to specify that
\log\frac{m}{d}
factors are ignored.
, where
k
is the number of errors made by the classifier on the sample.
An advantage of this bound is that it interpolates correctly between the asymptotic behaviors of the agnostic (
k\neq 0
) and the realizable (
k=0
) cases (the latter is known to be in
\widetilde{\Theta}\big(\frac{d}{m}\big)
), albeit at the cost of a larger universal constant than the pessimistic bound.

In parallel to these bounds, a complementary result showed that generalization bounds based on VC dimension were also bounded from below in
\Omega\big(\sqrt{d/m}\big)
,
i.e.
, without the logarithmic factor (see
Vapnik 1998
,
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
, recently improved to optimality by
Kontorovich et al. 2019
).
Not long after, some upper bounds making use of
Dudley 1978
’s chaining technique successfully matched the asymptotic behavior of the lower bound (
Li et al. 2001
;
Lugosi 2002
).

Rather than discouraging the community to pursue this line of work, these minimax results incited researchers to find new ways to improve the convergence rate of generalization bounds.
These so-called “fast rate” bounds enjoy a favorable asymptotic behavior in
\smash[t]{\widetilde{{O}}\big(\frac{1}{m}\big)}
at the expense of generality.
They all rely on some assumptions about the nature of the data labeling distribution, such as
Tsybakov’s margin condition

(
Tsybakov 2004
;
Massart and Nédélec 2006
;
Zhivotovskiy and Hanneke 2018
)
, the
Bernstein condition

(
Bartlett and Mendelson 2006
)
and the
central condition on proper learning

(
Van Erven et al. 2015
)
, or on some distribution-dependent quantities like local Rademacher complexities
(
Bartlett et al. 2002
)
and local VC dimension
(
Oneto et al. 2016
)
.
Some have even been able to obtain exact results on the generalization capabilities of VC classes, such as
Vayatis 2003
,
Vorontsov 2010
and
Bottou 2015
.
Even though these bounds can be very powerful in the right situation, they suffer from a limited scope of application—either because the assumptions made on the unknown generating distribution are hard to verify, or because they make use of quantities hard to compute or approximate (such as Rademacher complexities)
(
Bartlett and Mendelson 2002
)
.

While the community was mainly concerned with improving the convergence rate of generalization bounds, few actually tried to further decrease the constant factor of the original bounds of Vapnik.
Yet, it would be of great interest to have general distribution-independent bounds that are tight for real-life situations, where the sample size is relatively small and the distribution is unknown.
Indeed, to see that constant factors matter, one can show fairly easily (see Appendix
A
) that
Lugosi 2002
’s
\Theta(\sqrt{d/m})
chaining bound, which relies on
Haussler 1995
’s discretization trick, becomes tighter than
Vapnik 1998
’s pessimistic bound only when the sample size is on the order of
m\gtrsim 10^{335}d
.
Hence, even if it was possible to reduce the constant factor of
Lugosi 2002
’s bound, it is unlikely that this would lead to a tighter bound than Vapnik’s bound for reasonable data set sizes.

In fact, the work of
Catoni 2004
is the only one, to the best of our knowledge, to provide a meaningful improvement to the constant factors of Vapnik’s bounds.

Catoni 2004
achieves this by introducing the concept of “partially exchangeable” prior distributions on multiple “shadow” copies of the original sample in the transductive setting.
Thus, it feels as if bounds for VC classes are somewhat outdated compared to other types of bounds such as sample compression bounds
(
Floyd and Warmuth 1995
)
or PAC-Bayesian bounds
(
McAllester 1999
)
, which have received a fair amount of attention over the last years.
However, contrary to the case of sample compression, where
Langford 2005
and
Laviolette et al. 2009
have shown that the bounds are nearly as tight as one might hope, distribution-independent bounds for VC classes still have room for improvements.

While it is true that several recent research avenues indicate that the VC dimension is not the relevant complexity parameter to characterize the generalization error of over-parametrized deep neural networks (see for examples
Bartlett et al. 2019
and
Zhang et al. 2021
), neurals networks suffer from a lack of interpretability.
This shortcoming often prevents them from being used in areas where human safety, health, or well-being is at stake.
Interpretable classifiers most often take the form of hard threshold decisions such as those provided by Boolean formulas (for example DNFs), decision lists, decision trees, rule sets, etc.
For these models, the VC dimension is relevant and can be computed (or approximated), and a generalization upper bound can guide learning algorithms to achieve the proper bias-complexity trade-off, as well as make them certifiable.

In light of these observations, we derive a tight non-uniform distribution-independent generalization bound for VC classes by exploiting two new ideas that have appeared since Vapnik’s original work.
The proofs of Vapnik’s bounds rely on two critical steps, called the ghost sample trick (which replaces the dependence on the true risk by a dependence on the risk of a “ghost sample”) and the symmetrization trick (which allows simplifications by mixing the real sample with the ghost sample).
Taking inspiration from
Langford 2005
’s binomial tail inversion bound, we improve on the symmetrization step by considering the
hypergeometric tail inversion
.
Then, we optimize the size of the “ghost sample”—a step neglected by
Vapnik 1998
and
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
—to obtain noticeable gains, in agreement with the observations of
Catoni 2004
.
The new technique is general enough to also apply to a relative deviation bound, a margin bound, and a lower bound.

These improvements allow us to make very few approximations in the derivation of the bound; as such, the new bound is significantly tighter than Vapnik’s bounds, Catoni’s bound, and Lugosi’s chaining bound for various practical sets of parameters.
It is asymptotically optimal up to logarithmic factors for both the realizable case and the agnostic case.
Furthermore, as we show with numerical comparisons, it is almost never vacuous, even for a sample size as small as 100 examples or for large risks, contrary to the previous bounds.

The paper is divided as follows.
In the next section, we present the notation, definitions, and the mathematical setting.
The third section is dedicated to the main result, where we give the proof of the new upper bound and analyze its strengths and weaknesses in depth.
We also discuss the trade-off offered by the ghost sample size.
We then briefly present a relative deviation bound, a margin bound, and a lower bound derived following the same improvements.
We conclude by numerically comparing the multiple bounds in order to be able to appreciate the actual gain offered by the new bound.

2
Definitions and Notation

Throughout this paper, we consider the multi-class classification problem where
\mathcal{X}
denotes the instance space and
\mathcal{Y}=[n]
for
n\in\mathds{N}
, with
[n]\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, denotes the label space, although we will assume
n=2
in practical applications.
We also define
S\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\big((\mathbf{x}_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(\mathbf{x}_{m},y_{m})\big)\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{m}
as a collection of
m
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) examples sampled according to some unknown probability distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq[n]^{\mathcal{X}}
be some hypothesis class.
We consider the
0\hskip-2.0pt-\hskip-3.0pt1
loss function, from which we define the true and empirical risk of a classifier
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
as

R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\Expectation_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[h(\mathbf{x})\neq y\right]\right]\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad R_{S}(h)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[h(\mathbf{x}_{i})\neq y_{i}\right],

where
\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\cdot\right]
denotes the indicator function.
We adopt a notation where, given a random variable
Z
following some distribution,
\Expectation_{Z}\left[f(Z)\right]
denotes the expectation of
f(Z)
and
\Prob_{Z}\left[A(Z)\right]
denotes the probability that event
A(Z)
occurs.

To produce generalization bounds, VC theory makes use of the
growth function
to characterize a given hypothesis class
{\mathcal{H}}
.
It is defined by
\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\max_{S:\left|S\right|=m}\left|\left\{h|_{S}:h\in{\mathcal{H}}\right\}\right|
, where
h|_{S}\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}(h(\mathbf{x}_{1}),h(\mathbf{x}_{2}),\dots,h(\mathbf{x}_{m}))
, for
(\mathbf{x}_{j},y_{j})\in S
.
Note that this definition makes sense for any number of classes.
In the binary classification setting, one can use the
Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension
to approximate
\tau_{\mathcal{H}}
.
We define the VC dimension of
{\mathcal{H}}
as the largest integer
d
such that
\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(d)=2^{d}
.
This quantity can be used to upper bound the growth function by Sauer-Shelah’s lemma
(
Vapnik and Chervonenkis 1971
)
, which implies that
\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m)\leq\left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^{d}
whenever
m\geq d
.
In the case where
n>2
, one can use the Natarajan dimension
(
Ben-David et al. 1995
)
to characterize
{\mathcal{H}}
instead of the VC dimension so that a similar result holds for the growth function.

The main results of this paper rely on the hypergeometric distribution and its inverse.
We only give the essential definitions here; extensive detail is provided in Appendix
B
.

A
hypergeometric experiment
is the event of drawing
k
successes in a sample of
m
elements from a population of
M
elements containing
K
successes.
The
hypergeometric distribution
gives the probability of such an event, which is equal to
\textnormal{hyp}(k,m,K,M)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\binom{K}{k}\binom{M-K}{m-k}/\binom{M}{m}
.
We refer to the cumulative distribution function as the
hypergeometric tail function
, which we denote by
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\textnormal{hyp}(j,m,K,M)
.
Figure
3
of Appendix
B.1
presents the hypergeometric tail function in terms of its four parameters.
Our main result requires us to invert the tail seen as a function of the parameter
K
.
Since
K
is a discrete variable, the hypergeometric tail cannot be inverted straightforwardly.
Instead, one must use a pseudo-inverse, defined below.

Definition 1

(Hypergeometric tail pseudo-inverse)
.

We define the pseudo-inverse of the hypergeometric tail function, for any
k<m
and any
\delta\in(0,1)
, as

\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\min\left\{K:\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\leq\delta\right\}.

This choice of pseudo-inverse implies that
\textnormal{Hyp}\big(k,m,\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M),M\big)\leq\delta
, a fact which will be useful later on.
Figure
4
in Appendix
B.2
plots the pseudo-inverse as a function of its four parameters.
We also provide two algorithms to compute this pseudo-inverse in Appendix
B.3
, accompanied by an implementation in Python available on GitHub
2
2

2

https://github.com/jsleb333/hypergeometric_tail_inversion
.
Note that pseudo-inverses are generally not unique; in fact,
Le et al. 2016
also introduce an similar but slightly different pseudo-inverse for the hypergeometric tail in the context of validation of matching algorithms.

3
The Hypergeometric Tail Inversion Bound

The new technique takes inspiration from
Langford 2005
’s binomial tail inversion theorem, which states that with probability
1-\delta
on the choices of sample
S
of size
m
, the true risk of a classifier
h
is bounded from above by
\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(mR_{S}(h),m,\delta)
, where
\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}
is the binomial tail inverse (defined in Appendix
C
, similarly to
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}
).
The proof given by Langford feels more like an intuitive argumentation about why it should be true rather than an actual proof.
Therefore, we allow ourselves to present, in Appendix
C
, a new formal proof of the theorem, which has in fact inspired this work.
The binomial tail inversion bound is “essentially perfectly tight” since no approximation is made in deriving it, with a major caveat: it is a test bound, meaning it does not hold simultaneously for all classifiers in a hypothesis class
{\mathcal{H}}
.
While one can use the union bound to make it hold for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
, this strategy is limited to the cases where
{\mathcal{H}}
is countable.

In this section, we broaden the scope of
Langford 2005
’s strategy by generalizing it to hypothesis classes with polynomial growth function.
We first state and explain the bound, and then prove it in detail.
Next, we examine the improvements over the traditional derivations of
Vapnik 1998
and
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
, and analyze the weaker steps of the proof.
Finally, we discuss the effect of the ghost sample size and its implications.

3.1
The Main Bound

Typical
uniform
bounds require that the absolute difference between the true risk
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
and the empirical risk
R_{S}(h)
is bounded by a function
\epsilon(m,\delta)
, which depends only on the number
m
of examples and the confidence parameter
\delta
.
On the other hand,
non-uniform
bounds allow the upper bound
\epsilon
to depend on the particular classifier
h
in addition to
m
and
\delta
.
Both approaches have in common that they bound
\left|R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)\right|
.
This can provide interesting theoretical results, but in practice we are mainly concerned with finding an upper bound on the true risk.

The upper bound part of a uniform bound takes the form
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq R_{S}(h)+\epsilon(m,\delta)
.
The linear dependence on the empirical risk being arbitrary, we lift this constraint and we look for a bound of the general form
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\epsilon(R_{S}(h),m,\delta)
instead.
This transforms the bound into a non-uniform one, since
\epsilon
now depends on
h
, but only via its empirical risk—the only dependence on the data that we allow ourselves.
As such, our main theorem, stated below, proposes a kind of middle ground between the uniform and non-uniform settings, similar to what
Vapnik 1998
’s relative deviation bound offers.

Theorem 2

(Hypergeometric tail inversion bound)
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class.
Then for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, any confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
and any integers
m,m^{\prime}>0
, the following inequality holds:

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\forall\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\epsilon(mR_{S}(h),m,\delta)\right]>1-\delta,

where
\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=1
if
k=m
and otherwise

\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\max\left\{1,\;\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(k,m,\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}\big)-1-k\right\}.

For convenience, we let
\epsilon
depend on the number
mR_{S}(h)
of errors made by the classifier
h
instead of its empirical risk.
It is necessary to set
\epsilon(m,m,\delta)=1
, since
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}
is ill-defined for
k=m
.
We discuss the optimal choice of the parameter
m^{\prime}
in Section
3.3
.

The theorem is stated such that it holds for multi-class learning problems, as long as one possesses an upper bound for the growth function.
Moreover, the new bound is non-trivial only if the growth function is polynomial in the number
m
of examples (which is equivalent to ask that the VC dimension be finite in the case of binary classification).

Proof.

To prove the theorem, it is sufficient to show that
\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]<\delta
, where we mute some dependencies to alleviate the notation and we use
k_{S}(h)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}mR_{S}(h)
hereafter.
The left-hand side can be written as

\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]=\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right].

(1)

First, we proceed with the ghost sample trick as usual to transfer the dependence on the true risk
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
onto the empirical risk
R_{S^{\prime}}(h)
of a ghost sample
S^{\prime}
consisting of
m^{\prime}
i.i.d. examples drawn from
\mathcal{D}
.
Fix some
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
and notice that
k_{S^{\prime}}(h)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}m^{\prime}R_{S^{\prime}}(h)
is a random variable following a binomial distribution with parameters
m^{\prime}
and
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
.
Hence, we can use the inequality

\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[m^{\prime}R_{S^{\prime}}(h)\geq m^{\prime}R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right]>\frac{1}{4},

(2)

which holds provided that
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>1/m^{\prime}
, as proven by
Greenberg and Mohri 2014
(but claimed to be true by
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
).
Since we have that
\epsilon(k)\geq 1/m^{\prime}
according to its definition in the theorem, it is sufficient to assume that
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\epsilon(k_{S}(h))
to have

\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[m^{\prime}R_{S^{\prime}}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\geq\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[m^{\prime}R_{S^{\prime}}(h)\geq m^{\prime}R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right]>\frac{1}{4}.

This implies that for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
and all
S
, we have

\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]<4\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[m^{\prime}R_{S^{\prime}}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right].

Inserting this result inside Equation (
1
) and then using the fact that the supremum of an expectation is less than or equal to the expectation of the supremum, one has

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]

\displaystyle<4\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\Expectation_{S^{\prime}}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{S^{\prime}}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\!\Expectation_{S,S^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{S^{\prime}}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right].

(3)

Now, let
T\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}(S,S^{\prime})=((\mathbf{x}_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(\mathbf{x}_{m+m^{\prime}},y_{m+m^{\prime}}))
be an i.i.d. sample constructed by re-indexing
S
and
S^{\prime}
, and let
{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}=\left\{(h(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\dots,h(\mathbf{x}_{m+m^{\prime}})):h\in{\mathcal{H}}\right\}

be the restriction of
{\mathcal{H}}
to
T
.
Furthermore, let
\sigma\in\Sigma\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\{\sigma\subseteq[m+m^{\prime}]\>:\>\left|\sigma\right|=m\}
be some set of
m
indices taken from
[m+m^{\prime}]
, and denote its elements by
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
, …,
\sigma_{m}
such that they are in increasing order.
We define
T(\sigma)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}((\mathbf{x}_{\sigma_{1}},y_{\sigma_{1}}),\dots,(\mathbf{x}_{\sigma_{m}},y_{\sigma_{m}}))
to be the collection of examples in
T
whose indices are in
\sigma
.
With this notation, we have that
S=T([m])
, and the right-hand side of Inequality (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle 4\!\Expectation_{S,S^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{S^{\prime}}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right]\!=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{T([m])}(h))+k_{T([m])}(h)\right]\right].

We are then ready to apply the symmetrization trick.
Because the examples of
T
are i.i.d. and because we are taking the expectation over
T
, the right-hand side of the last equation would be the same if one were to replace
T([m])
with
T(\sigma)
for any set
\sigma\in\Sigma
.
Thus, we can take the expectation of this expression over all possible sets
\sigma\in\Sigma
with the uniform probability distribution on
\Sigma
to give

\displaystyle 4\!\Expectation_{S,S^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{S^{\prime}}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right]\!=4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{T(\sigma)}(h))+k_{T(\sigma)}(h)\right]\right]\right]\!.

Next, we decompose the (disjoint) events over the values that
k_{T(\sigma)}(h)
can take, taking into account that the term
k=m
of the sum vanishes by definition of
\epsilon(m)
, to obtain

\displaystyle 4\!\Expectation_{S,S^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{S^{\prime}}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt=4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T(\sigma)}(h)=k\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt\leq 4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T(\sigma)}(h)=k\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T(\sigma)}(h)=k\right]\right]\right].

(4)

Notice that
\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T(\sigma)}(h)=k\right]\right]
corresponds to the probability of choosing
m
examples (those of
T(\sigma)
),
k
of which are incorrectly classified by
h
, among the
m+m^{\prime}
examples of
T
(which contains
k_{T}(h)
examples incorrectly classified by
h
).
This is a hypergeometric experiment and thus
\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T(\sigma)}(h)=k\right]\right]=\textnormal{hyp}(k,m,k_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})
.
Moreover, note that
m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k
is increasing with
k
because
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)
is also increasing with
k
, as shown in Lemma
13
of Appendix
B.2
.
Hence, the indicator function
\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]
simply truncates the end of the summation.
Letting
\kappa(h)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\max\left\{k\in[m-1]:k_{T}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right\}
and substituting all results back into Inequality (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]

\displaystyle<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa(h)}\textnormal{hyp}(k,m,k_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\textnormal{Hyp}(\kappa(h),m,k_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})\right].

(5)

On the other hand, because
k_{T}(h)
and
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)
must both be integers, we have that
k_{T}(h)>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k
implies
k_{T}(h)\geq\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime})
.
Furthermore, since
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)
is decreasing with respect to parameter
K
as shown in Lemma
11
of Appendix
B.1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\textnormal{Hyp}(\kappa,m,k_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{Hyp}(\kappa,m,\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(\kappa,m,\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}),m+m^{\prime})

(6)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},

by the definition of
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}
.
Then Inequality (
5
) simplifies to

\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})}\right]\leq\delta\frac{\max_{T}\bigl|{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}\bigr|}{\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})}=\delta,

(7)

as desired.
∎

3.2
The Tightness of Theorem
2

The above proof improves upon the derivation of
Vapnik 1998
and
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
in three ways.
First, in the ghost sample trick, both
Vapnik 1998
and
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
set
m^{\prime}
equal to
m
.

Vapnik 1998
does so arbitrarily, while
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
require it to be able to use Hoeffding’s inequality.
However, as discussed in the next section, one can improve the bound significantly by making a better choice for
m^{\prime}
.
Note that
Catoni 2004
’s approach does not use a ghost sample in the same way as what is done here.
However, it requires a “shadow sample” which plays a similar role, and they also find that optimizing the shadow sample size is beneficial.

Second, we use the indicator function to truncate the summation on
k
in Equation (
4
).
This step is neglected by
Vapnik 1998
, and allows us to avoid summing unnecessary terms.
Furthermore, it enables us to proceed to a third improvement: the use of the hypergeometric tail pseudo-inverse, which
provides the tightest possible upper bound on the right-hand side of Equation (
5
).

The new bound is nearly optimal in the context of VC classes since few inequalities remain that could be improved.
Let us inspect the aforementioned inequalities.

The first inequality is introduced for the ghost sample trick.
It relies on the result (
2
) of
Greenberg and Mohri 2014
which bounds the probability of a binomial variable exceeding its expectation.
Tighter bounds with the same purpose exist, but they are distribution dependent
(
Pelekis and Ramon 2016
;
Doerr 2018
)
.
As
Cortes et al. 2019
observed, the bound of
Greenberg and Mohri 2014
presents a trade-off between the minimum value
\epsilon
can take and the factor
4
in the bound (for example, one could require
\epsilon\geq 2/m^{\prime}
instead of
1/m^{\prime}
to replace
4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}
in the bound by
3.375\tau_{\mathcal{H}}
).
However optimizing this trade-off appears negligible, since the best we could achieve is to replace the factor
4
by a factor
2
, and this factor contributes only logarithmically to the bound.

The second inequality is obtained by changing the order of the supremum over the classifiers and the expectation over the ghost sample in Equation (
3
).
Then, in order to use the union bound, a third inequality occurs where we replace the maximum over the classifiers by a sum.
The last inequality occurs at Equation (
7
), where we replace the expectation by a maximum.
These costly steps, which introduce the growth function, are required to be able to use the hypergeometric tail inverse.
To do better, we would need to consider distribution-dependent (possibly localized) complexity measures, such as in
Oneto et al. 2016
;
Zhivotovskiy and Hanneke 2018
.
Note that the use of the growth function here is not asymptotically optimal, as it introduces an “unnecessary”
\log m
factor in the bound.
However, typical chaining techniques used to avoid the growth function usually lead to very large constant factors, making the final bound worse for most practical applications, as discussed in Appendix
A
.

Note that the inequalities used in Equation (
6
) are not approximations, but rather consequences of the discrete nature of the hypergeometric distribution.
They are therefore unavoidable and minimal, and tend to disappear as the sample grows in size.

Finally, notice that we can recover known bounds by making approximations.
Indeed, one obtains
Vapnik 1998
’s pessimistic bound when applying Hoeffding’s inequality on the hypergeometric tail, which yields
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\leq e^{-2t^{2}m}
for
t\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\frac{k}{m}-\frac{K}{M}

(
Chvátal 1979
)
.
On the other hand, by definition of the pseudo-inverse, we have, for any
\delta
, that
\textnormal{Hyp}\big(k,m,K,M\big)>\delta
whenever
K\leq\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)-1
.
Combining both results implies
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)-1<M\sqrt{\frac{-\ln\delta}{2m}}+\frac{kM}{m}
.
Substituting this inequality in the expression of
\epsilon(k)
of Theorem
2
(neglecting the first part of the maximum), one obtains

\epsilon(k)<\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\sqrt{\frac{-\ln\delta+\ln(4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime}))}{2m}}+\frac{k}{m}\,.

Setting
m^{\prime}=m
, we end up with the pessimistic bound, but with an extra factor of
\sqrt{2}
in front of the square root, and without the
1/m
additive term.
Note that in
Vapnik 1998
’s derivation, this
1/m
term was originally introduced to satisfy the requirements of the ghost sample trick, whereas our approach handles it by using a maximum in the expression of
\epsilon
.

Furthermore, by inspecting the case where
k=0
(
i.e.
, the realizable case), we can also simplify the bound to recover the
\widetilde{\Theta}(d/m)
asymptotic behavior.
It is easy to verify that
\textnormal{Hyp}(0,m,K,M)\leq\left(\frac{M-K}{M}\right)^{m}
, which then implies that
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(0,m,\delta,M)\leq M(1-\delta^{1/m})+1
.
Substituting this approximation into Theorem
2
and then using
1-e^{x}\leq-x
leads to

\epsilon(0)\leq\left(1+\frac{m}{m^{\prime}}\right)\frac{1}{m}\ln\left(\frac{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})}{\delta}\right),

which shows that the new bound, similarly to Vapnik’s relative deviation bound, correctly interpolates between the realizable and agnostic cases.

3.3
The Ghost Sample Size Trade-off

The upper bound
\epsilon
of Theorem
2
presents an interesting dependence in
m^{\prime}
.
Indeed, increasing
m^{\prime}
makes the factor of
\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}
shrink, while
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)
decreases when
M
grows (keeping the other three parameters fixed), and increases when
\delta
becomes smaller, as shown in Appendix
B.2
.
Since the confidence parameter
\delta
is divided by the growth function
\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})
, which we assume is polynomial in
m+m^{\prime}
(for
m+m^{\prime}
large enough), we have that at some point, increasing
m^{\prime}
becomes less beneficial than decreasing it, thus presenting a non trivial trade-off.

Therefore, we can further tighten the new bound by carefully choosing the optimal value of
m^{\prime}
.
Due to the discrete nature of the hypergeometric tail pseudo-inverse, we must resort to using a numerical solver to do so.
By finding the best ghost sample size
m^{\prime}_{\text{best}}
for a variety of parameters, we can notice that
m^{\prime}_{\text{best}}
is increasing with
k
and
m
, and is decreasing with
\delta
and the VC dimension
d
of the hypothesis class
{\mathcal{H}}
.
We observe that for low empirical risk (
k/m\lesssim 10\%
),
m^{\prime}_{\text{best}}
is about four times greater than
m
(which can provide a good heuristic for finding
m^{\prime}_{\text{best}}
). But for some particular sets of parameters
k
,
m
,
\delta
and
d
,
m^{\prime}_{\text{best}}
can be almost as large as
100\,m
.
This shows that the choice of
Vapnik 1998
and of
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
of setting
m^{\prime}
equal to
m
can be very far from the optimal solution.
Computing the new bound for 880 different combinations of parameters, we gather that optimizing the bound leads to a relative gain of 8% to 10% over the non-optimized version where
m^{\prime}=m
, a substantial improvement.

A more detailed discussion of our findings is presented in Appendix
D
.
As a final note, we emphasize that Theorem
2
requires
m^{\prime}
to be chosen ahead of time; hence, we cannot optimize its value using the number of errors
k
made by our classifier.
We can, however, optimize it for an
anticipated
number of errors as some sort of prior knowledge.

4
More Results Using the Hypergeometric Tail Inversion

The approach we have exploited for deriving the new bound can be adapted to various other settings in a similar fashion, which shows the versatility of the technique.
We consider a relative deviation version of the new bound, a margin bound inspired by the work of
Anthony and Bartlett 1999
, as well as a lower bound.

4.1
The Relative Deviation Bound Revisited

It is known that relative deviation bounds, which bound
(R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h))/\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}
, often provide a better asymptotic rate than the pessimistic bound when the empirical risk is small.
Bounding the relative deviation with the new technique, we obtain Theorem
15
, presented and proved in Appendix
E
.
From this theorem directly follows the corollary below.

Corollary 3
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class.
Then for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, any confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
and any integers
m,m^{\prime}>0
, the following inequality holds:

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\forall\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq R_{S}(h)+\frac{\eta(mR_{S}(h))^{2}}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{4R_{S}(h)}{\eta(mR_{S}(h))^{2}}}\right)\right]>1-\delta,

where
\eta(k)=0
for
k=m
, and otherwise

\eta(k)=\max\left\{\scalebox{.9}{
$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{m^{\prime}}},\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\frac{u(k)-\frac{k}{m}}{\sqrt{u(k)}}$
}\right\}\text{ with }u(k)=\frac{\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(k,m,\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}\big)-1}{m+m^{\prime}}.

The steps of the proof of Corollary
3
follow closely those of Theorem
2
—the main exception being the ghost sample trick.
Indeed, to bound the relative deviation, we need to use a looser version of the result of
Greenberg and Mohri 2014
.
As such, this latest bound can only be looser, a phenomenon observed in Appendix
E.3
(although the difference between both bounds is small).
As a final note, Appendix
E.2
offers a brief analysis of the ghost sample trade-off for this latter bound, which shows that the benefit of optimizing
m^{\prime}
is even more important in this case.

4.2
A Bound for Multi-class Margin Classifiers

Taking inspiration in the work of
Anthony and Bartlett 1999
, we can also apply our framework to margin classifiers.
In margin theory, we consider hypotheses of the form
h_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x})=\argmax_{i\in[n]}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
(with arbitrary rule for breaking ties), where
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}\subseteq([0,1]^{n})^{\mathcal{X}}
is a vector-valued function that outputs a score between
0
and
1
for each class.
The
margin

of a classifier
h_{\mathbf{f}}
is
\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),y)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}f_{y}(\mathbf{x})-\max_{j\neq y}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})
and can be interpreted as the confidence of
h_{\mathbf{f}}
in its prediction.

Contrary to the previous settings, where the hypothesis class
{\mathcal{H}}|_{S}
restricted to a sample
S
is finite, here the class
\mathcal{F}|_{S}
is generally infinite.
This issue can be fixed by considering a discretization of
\mathcal{F}
instead.
Such a process is called a
cover
of
\mathcal{F}
, from which we can define uniform covering numbers (with respect to a mixed
L_{p}
-
L_{\infty}
metric
\ell
on
\mathds{R}^{n\times m}
) as follows.

Definition 4

(Uniform covering numbers)
.

Let
W\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n\times m}
and
c>0
.
Then, we say that
\widehat{W}\subseteq W
is a
c
-cover of
W
if and only if, for every
w\in W
, there exists
\widehat{w}\in\widehat{W}
such that
\ell(w,\widehat{w})<c
.

W
is totally bounded if, for each
c>0
, there exists a finite
c
-cover of
W
.
In this case, we define the
c
-covering number of
W
,
\mathcal{N}^{p}(c,W)
, to be the minimum cardinality of a
c
-cover of
W
w.r.t. a
L_{p}
-
L_{\infty}
metric.
Then, for any integer
m>0
and any
p>0
, the uniform
c
-covering number of
\mathcal{F}
is

\mathcal{N}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(c,m)=\!\max_{S:\left|S\right|=m}\mathcal{N}^{p}(c,\mathcal{F}|_{S}),

where
\mathcal{F}|_{S}\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\{(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{m})):\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}\}
denotes the restriction of
\mathcal{F}
to the sample
S
.

These uniform covering numbers are analogous to the growth function and play a similar role.
They allow us to prove the following theorem, as is done in Appendix
F
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq([0,1]^{n})^{\mathcal{X}}
be a vector-valued function class,
h_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x})=\argmax_{i\in[n]}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
be a classifier and
R^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f})\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in S}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})),y)<\gamma\right]
be the empirical margin risk.
Then for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, any confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, any margin
\gamma\in(0,1)
, any
p\geq 1
and any integers
m
and
m^{\prime}>0
,

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\forall\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})\leq\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}),m,\delta)\right]>1-\delta,

where
\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=1
if
k=m
and otherwise

\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\max\left\{1,\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\Bigl(k,\frac{\delta}{4\mathcal{N}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2^{2-1/p}},m+m^{\prime}\right)},m,m+m^{\prime}\Bigr)-1-k\right\}.

4.3
A Lower Bound

To derive Theorem
2
, we relaxed the requirement to bound the absolute value of the difference between the true and empirical risks of a classifier in order to gain more flexibility.
This lead us to consider only an upper bound on the true risk; however, one can use similar arguments to obtain a lower bound that holds simultaneously for all classifiers of a hypothesis class.
This section presents such a bound.

The main observation to be made is that the definition of the pseudo-inverse
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}
of the hypergeometric tail is no longer appropriate for a lower bound.
As we have already discussed below Definition
1
, the definition of a pseudo-inverse is not unique.
In fact, for the lower bound, the correct definition of the pseudo-inverse is the one used by
Le et al. 2016
, which is as follows.

Definition 6

(Hypergeometric tail lower pseudo-inverse)
.

We define the lower pseudo-inverse of the hypergeometric tail function, for any
0<k\leq m
and any
\delta\in(0,1)
as

\underline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\max\left\{K:\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\geq\delta\right\}.

Note that
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}
and
\underline{\textnormal{Hyp}}
differ at most by one.
It is straightforward to adapt the proof of the monotonic properties of
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}
of Lemma
13
to the lower pseudo-inverse.
Furthermore, while we had
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M),M)\leq\delta
, the new definition implies instead
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,\underline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M),M)\geq\delta
, which is more relevant for a lower bound.

Using the lower pseudo-inverse, our lower bound theorem is stated below.

Theorem 7

(Hypergeometric tail inversion lower bound)
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class.
Then for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, any confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
and any integers
m,m^{\prime}>0
, the following inequality holds:

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\forall\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\geq\epsilon(mR_{S}(h),m,\delta)\right]>1-\delta,

where
\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=0
if
k=0
and otherwise

\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\min\left\{m^{\prime}-1,\;\underline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(k-1,m,1-\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}\big)+1-k\right\}.

The proof is presented in Appendix
G.1
, and follows closely the steps of the proof of the upper bound.
Appendix
G.2
compares briefly the upper and lower bounds, where we can see that both are perfectly symmetric.

5
Numerical Comparison to Other Bounds

To show that the new bound is indeed tighter than previous existing VC bounds, we proceed with some numerical comparisons.
The bounds we consider are
Vapnik 1998
’s pessimistic (VP) bound, given by

\epsilon_{\textnormal{VP}}(R_{S}(h))=R_{S}(h)+\frac{1}{m}+\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(m)},

where
\mathcal{E}(m)=\frac{1}{m}(\ln 4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(2m)-\ln\delta)
,
Vapnik 1998
’s relative deviation (VRD) bound, given by

\epsilon_{\textnormal{VRD}}(R_{S}(h))=R_{S}(h)+2\mathcal{E}(m)\big(1+\sqrt{1+R_{S}(h)/\mathcal{E}(m)}\big),

the new hypergeometric tail inversion (HTI) bound (Theorem
2
),
Lugosi 2002
’s chaining bound (Theorem 1.16) given by

\epsilon_{\textnormal{Lugosi}}(R_{S}(h))=R_{S}(h)+24\sqrt{\frac{2d}{m}}\left(\sqrt{a}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{a}}{2}(1-\text{erf}(\sqrt{a}))\right)+\sqrt{\frac{-\ln\delta}{2m}},

with

a\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\frac{(d+1)(2+\ln 2)}{2d},

and finally
Catoni 2004

\epsilon_{C4.6}(R_{S}(h))=\left(1+\frac{2d^{\prime}}{m}\right)^{-1}\left(R_{S}(h)+\frac{d^{\prime}}{m}+\frac{1}{m}\sqrt{2d^{\prime}mR_{S}(h)(1-R_{S}(h))+d^{\prime 2}}\right),

d^{\prime}=\big(\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\big)^{2}\left(\ln\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})-\ln\delta\right)

and

0.5

Here,

m\approx 1250
, where Catoni’s bound becomes non-vacuous) for the agnostic case.

In the realizable case, the HTI bound becomes non-vacuous as soon as
m\approx 54
, which is only 4 more than the VC dimension, and it takes a value below
0.5
at around
m\approx 400
.
In contrast, Catoni’s C4.6 bound starts being informative at
m\approx 750
, almost twice the sample size needed for the HTI bound.
The situation is similar for the agnostic case, where the HTI bound is less than one for
m\geq 66
and is less than one half for
m\gtrsim 565
.
This is less than half of the sample size required (
m\approx 1260
) by Catoni’s bound to be non-vacuous.

Remark that in both cases, Lugosi’s chaining bound starts becoming non-vacuous only at
m\gtrsim 100
,
000
.
Furthermore, even if it possesses an optimal convergence rate for the agnostic case, recall from the introduction that it would become tighter than Vapnik’s pessimistic bound only for
m\gtrsim 10^{335}d
.
Let us also note that, even though the new HTI bound contains an extra unnecessary
\sqrt{\log(m/d)}
in the agnostic case, it is still more than 30 times smaller than Lugosi’s optimal-rate chaining bound at
m\approx 10^{6}
—a fact which highlights that constant factors matter more than logarithmic ones in practical settings.
As a final observation, the HTI bound has the non-negligible advantage that it has the correct asymptotic rate for the realizable case, something the chaining bound does not, as seen in Figure
2(a)
.

6
Conclusion

In conclusion, we proposed a new way to improve generalization bounds for VC classes.
Taking inspiration from
Langford 2005
, we considered the hypergeometric tail pseudo-inverse, from which we derived a novel non-uniform distribution-independent very tight bound with few approximations.
We tightened even more the bound by optimizing the ghost sample size.
Applying the new technique to other settings, we developed a relative deviation bound—which turned out less tight than the novel bound—as well as a bound for multi-class margin classifiers and a lower bound.
We provided numerical comparisons against the previously existing bounds of
Vapnik 1998
,
Catoni 2004
and
Lugosi 2002
, which has shown that the new generalization bound is significantly tighter for multiple settings, and that it is almost never vacuous.
These experimentations give evidence that constant factors are at the least as important as logarithmic factors in asymptotic rates for practical settings.

Even though the improvements to the constant factor achieved here have proven relevant for practical applications, the hypergeometric tail inversion bound is still not asymptotically optimal in the agnostic case.
Therefore, it would be very interesting to see if chaining techniques could be combined profitably with the hypergeometric tail inversion to obtain even further gain for (reasonably) large sample sizes.

Appendix A
Comparison between Lugosi’s chaining bound and Vapnik’s pessimistic bound

In the introduction, we have claimed that Lugosi’s chaining bound becomes better than Vapnik’s pessimistic bound only for extremely large sample sizes of
m\gtrsim 10^{335}d
.
We show the details of the computation here.

We use
Lugosi 2002
’s derivations of both bounds for the comparison.
These results are expressed as bounds on the expected deviation of the empirical risk from the true risk,
i.e.
, of the form

\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left|R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)\right|\right]\leq\beta.

These results can be converted to generalization bounds of the form

\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}}:\left|R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)\right|>\epsilon\right]\leq\delta

by using McDiarmid’s inequality (also called the bounded difference inequality) (Theorem 1.8 of
Lugosi 2002
), where
\epsilon\geq\beta+\sqrt{\frac{-\ln\delta}{2m}}
.
Therefore, comparing the bounds on the expected deviation
\beta
is equivalent to compare the bounds
\epsilon
.

For Vapnik’s pessimistic bound, we have
\beta_{\text{pessimistic}}=\sqrt{2\frac{\ln 2\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(2m)}{m}}
(Theorem 1.9 of
Lugosi 2002
without the last approximation of the proof).
Using Sauer-Shelah’s lemma to approximate the growth function by
\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(2m)\leq\big(\frac{2em}{d}\big)^{d}
, we get

\beta_{\text{pessimistic}}\leq\sqrt{2\frac{\ln(2)+d\ln(2em/d)}{m}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(d+1)\ln(4em/d)}{m}},

where we used
\ln 2\leq\ln(2em/d)
to simplify things (which will favor the chaining bound in the comparison).

For the chaining bound, we use
Lugosi 2002
’s Theorem 1.16:

\beta_{\text{chaining}}=\frac{24}{\sqrt{m}}\max_{S:|S|=m}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\sqrt{\ln\left(2\mathcal{N}(r,{\mathcal{H}}|_{S})\right)}\textrm{d}r,

(8)

where
\mathcal{N}(r,{\mathcal{H}}|_{S})
is the
r
-covering number of
{\mathcal{H}}|_{S}
.
When the VC dimension of
{\mathcal{H}}
is
d
, Haussler showed that

\mathcal{N}(r,{\mathcal{H}}|_{S})\leq e(d+1)\left(\frac{2e}{r^{2}}\right)^{d}.

Substituting this inequality in (
8
) and proceeding to the integration, one has (without further approximations):

\beta_{\text{chaining}}\leq 24\sqrt{\frac{2d}{m}}\left(\sqrt{a}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{a}}{2}(1-\text{erf}(\sqrt{a}))\right),

with

a\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\frac{(d+1)(2+\ln 2)}{2d}.

The term
\frac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{a}}{2}(1-\text{erf}(\sqrt{a}))
is always positive, and can be shown to be less than
\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}<1
.
Neglecting this term (which favors the chaining bound in the comparison) and setting it approximately equal to the pessimistic bound, we have

\displaystyle 24\sqrt{\frac{(d+1)(2+\ln 2)}{m}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{2(d+1)\ln(2em/d)}{m}}.

Evaluating constants, it simplifies to

\displaystyle 775.63\approx\ln(2em/d).

Taking the exponential on each side and changing to base 10 gives the claimed result.

Appendix B
Overview of the hypergeometric distribution

This Appendix is dedicated to the hypergeometric distribution.
We cover the formal definition of the tail and its pseudo-inverse, their monotonicity properties, and algorithms to compute the pseudo-inverse.

B.1
The hypergeometric distribution

The hypergeometric distribution is an elementary distribution that occurs often.
It deals with the probability of obtaining a given amount of successes out of some number of random draws (without replacement) from a population with a fixed total amount of successes.
This is similar to the binomial distribution, with the exception that the probability of success may change with each draw, since we draw without replacement when sampling according to a hypergeometric distribution.
The following definition gives the mathematical formula for the probability mass function of the hypergeometric distribution.

Definition 8

(Hypergeometric distribution probability mass function)
.

Given an
M
-element set containing
K
successes, the action of drawing
k
successes in a sample of
m
elements is called a
hypergeometric experiment
and its probability is given by the
hypergeometric distribution probability mass function
, defined by

\textnormal{hyp}(k,m,K,M)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\frac{\binom{K}{k}\binom{M-K}{m-k}}{\binom{M}{m}},

where
\binom{N}{n}
is a binomial coefficient.

The tail function, also called the cumulative distribution, is particularly useful in our work.
We give its formal definition below.

Definition 9

(Hypergeometric tail function)
.

Given a set of
M
elements containing
K
successes, the probability of drawing
k
successes or less in a sample of
m
elements is defined by the
hypergeometric tail function
(also called the cumulative distribution function) and is expressed as

\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\binom{K}{j}\binom{M-K}{m-j}}{\binom{M}{m}}.

Recently,

has found a new expression for the hypergeometric tail, which is stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 10

(Berkopec’s identity)
.

For any integers

,

,
K

M

\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{K}{j}\binom{M-K}{m-j}=\sum_{J=K}^{M-m+k}\binom{J}{k}\binom{M-J-1}{m-k-1},

where it is assumed that
\binom{N}{-n}=0
for any

and

.

With this identity, we have

\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)=\sum_{J=K}^{M-m+k}\frac{\binom{J}{k}\binom{M-J-1}{m-k-1}}{\binom{M}{m}}.

This will prove particularly useful when showing the monotonicity properties of the hypergeometric tail, since it allows us to exchange the sum over values of
k
with a sum over values of
K
.
This brings us to the following lemma.

Lemma 11

(Monotonicity of the hypergeometric tail function)
.

Let
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)
be the tail of the hypergeometric distribution as described in Definition
9
, and let
\binom{N}{-n}\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}0
for all integers
N\in\mathds{Z}
and
n>0
.
Then, for all
k\in\mathds{Z}
and
0\leq m,K\leq M
,
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)
is increasing in its first and last parameters
k
and
M
, and decreasing in its second and third parameters
m
and
K
.
Furthermore, the monotonicity is strict whenever the inequalities
0\leq k<\min(m,K)
and
m-k\leq M-K
hold simultaneously.

One can observe the monotonicity of the tail in Figure
3
.

Proof.

We begin with the first parameter,
k
, fixing
m
,
K
and
M
.
To show that the tail is monotonically increasing, we need to show that
\textnormal{Hyp}(k+1,m,K,M)-\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\geq 0
for all
k\in\mathds{Z}
. Starting from Definition
9
, we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)-\textnormal{Hyp}(k-1,m,K,M)

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{K}{k}\binom{M-K}{m-k}}{\binom{M}{m}}\geq 0.

Moreover, this is strictly greater than zero whenever
0\leq k\leq m
and
K-M+m\leq k\leq K

For the second and third parameters
m
and
K
, first observe that the tail is symmetric under the exchange of
m
and
K

i.e.
,
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)=\textnormal{Hyp}(k,K,m,M)
.
This can be shown by reorganizing the binomial coefficients of each term in the sum.
This tells us that monotonic decreasing in

implies monotonic decreasing in

and vice-versa.

We will show monotonicity in

with the help of Berkopec’s identity
(

stated in Proposition

We have

\displaystyle\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)-\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K+1,M)

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{K}{k}\binom{M-K-1}{m-k-1}}{\binom{M}{m}}\geq 0,

and we have strict monotonicity whenever
0\leq k\leq K
and
1\leq m-k\leq M-K
.
Monotonicity in

follows by exchanging

with

in the previous argument, and is strict when

and

.

Finally, to show the tail is monotonic increasing in

and decompose the probability

into two events: we picked the

-th element among the

The former case has a probability

of happening and must be multiplied by the probability of drawing

M
elements with

The latter case happens with probability

and we multiply it by the probability of drawing
k
successes among
m
elements within a population of
M
elements with
K
successes.
Putting everything together, we obtain

\displaystyle\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{M+1}\cdot\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m-1,K,M)+\frac{M+1-m}{M+1}\cdot\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m}{M+1}\cdot\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)+\frac{M+1-m}{M+1}\cdot\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M),

where we used the monotonicity of
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)
in the parameter

at the second step, which implies strict monotonicity in
M
for the same intervals as
m
.
Alternatively, note that it is possible to prove the first equality by using Pascal’s identity inside Definition
9
.

Taking the intersection of all the intervals of strict monotonicity, we find the intervals specified in the lemma, which concludes the proof.
∎

B.2
The pseudo-inverse of the hypergeometric tail

Our main result requires us to invert the tail over the parameter
K
.
This differs from the quantile function (also known as the “percent point” function), which inverts the tail with respect to the parameter
k
.
This section is concerned with this particular inverse and its subtleties.

Strictly Monotonic functions of a real variable enjoy the property of being invertible.
However, the hypergeometric tail having discrete parameters, defining an inverse is not as straightforward.
Instead, one must consider a pseudo-inverse.
Note that there exist many ways to define such an inverse; the next definition states the pseudo-inverse appropriate for our purpose.

Definition 12

(Hypergeometric tail pseudo-inverse)
.

The pseudo-inverse of the hypergeometric tail function, for any
k<m
and any
\delta\in(0,1)
, is defined as

\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\min\left\{K:\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\leq\delta\right\}.

We restrict the domain of the pseudo-inverse to
k<m
because
\textnormal{Hyp}(m,m,K,M)=1
for all
K
and
M
greater than or equal to
m
, and thus it is ill-defined for
k=m
.
Notice that the domain of
K
is restricted to
k<K\leq M-m+k+1
since
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)=1
for every
K\leq k
, and because we have that
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)=0
for every
K>M-m+k
.

In particular, this choice of pseudo-inverse implies
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M),M)\leq\delta
.

However, this is less evident for pseudo-inverses.
The following lemma shows that monotonicity properties still hold for the hypergeometric tail pseudo-inverse.

Lemma 13

(Monotonicity of the hypergeometric tail pseudo-inverse)
.

Let
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)
be the pseudo-inverse of the hypergeometric tail function as described in Definition
12
.
Then, for
\delta\in(0,1)
, for
0\leq k<\min(m,K)
and for
m-k\leq M-K
,
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)
is increasing in its first and last parameters
k
and
M
, and decreasing in its second and third parameters
m
and
\delta
.
Furthermore, the monotonicity in
k
is strict.

Proof.

We start by presenting an approach to showing the monotonicity properties of the pseudo-inverse in any of its parameters.
The approach is quite general, but for the sake of clarity, we apply it only for the parameter
k
and explain how to adapt it for the other parameters.
We then conclude by a more specific approach to show the pseudo-inverse is strictly increasing in
k
.

Let
m
,
\delta
and
M
take some values that satisfy the requirements of Lemma
13
and choose
k<m-1
.
Let
\mathcal{K}(k,m,\delta,M)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\left\{K:\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\leq\delta\right\}

\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)=\min\mathcal{K}(k,m,\delta,M)

We want to show that
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)
is increasing with
k
.
First observe that if, for some
K
, we have that
\textnormal{Hyp}(k+1,m,K,M)\leq\delta

\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\leq\delta

Therefore, for all

Hence, we have that
\mathcal{K}(k,m,\delta,M)\supseteq\mathcal{K}(k+1,m,\delta,M)
.
Then, muting dependence on

,

and

\displaystyle\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)

\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}(k,m,\delta,M)

.

\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)\leq\delta

\textnormal{Hyp}(k+1,m,K^{\prime},M)\leq\delta

\textnormal{Hyp}(k+1,m,K^{\prime}-1,\delta)>\delta

\textnormal{Hyp}(k+1,m,K^{\prime},M)\leq\delta<\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K-1,M).

(9)

Start from
\textnormal{Hyp}(k+1,m,K,M)

\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M)

\displaystyle\binom{M}{m}(\textnormal{Hyp}(k+1

\displaystyle,m,K,M)-\textnormal{Hyp}(k,m,K,M))

\displaystyle=\binom{K}{k+1}\binom{M-K}{m-k-1}

Appendix C
Langford’s binominal tail inversion theorem

In its 2005 tutorial on prediction theory,
Langford 2005
explores and compares elementary generalization bounds in the classification setting.
In particular, he presents a “unifying” framework for
test bounds
,
i.e.
, bounds that can only be used to evaluate the performance of a classifier on a test set, but cannot be used to learn a classifier.
In order to do so, he considers the binomial tail function

\textnormal{Bin}(m,k,p)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{m}{j}{p}^{\,j}(1-p)^{m-j},

(10)

which can be interpreted as the probability of making
k
errors or less on a sample of
m
examples if the true probability of making an error is
p\in(0,1)
.
Then, because
\textnormal{Bin}(m,k,p)
is a continuous one-to-one function with respect to parameter
p
, one can define the
inverse
of the binomial tail function
4
4

4

For our convenience, this definition differs from
Langford 2005
in that it uses the minimum instead of the maximum; but because
Bin
is a continuous strictly decreasing function with respect to
p
, both definitions are equivalent.
as follows:

\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k,\delta)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\begin{cases}\min\left\{p\in(0,1):\textnormal{Bin}(m,k,p)\leq\delta\right\}&\textnormal{if }k<m,\\
1&\textnormal{if }k=m.\end{cases}

(11)

We handle the case
k=m
separately, as
\textnormal{Bin}(m,m,p)=1
for all
p\in(0,1)
.

With this quantity at hand,
Langford 2005
states the following theorem (Theorem 3.3 in the tutorial).

Theorem 14

(Binomial tail inversion bound)
.

For any distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, for any classifier
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
, any integer
m>0
and for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,mR_{S}(h),\delta)\right]>1-\delta

(12)

Finally, by upper bounding
\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}
in different ways, one is able to recover several other known looser bounds.

Proof.

In the following, we let
k_{S}(h)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}mR_{S}(h)
be the number of errors made by the classifier
h
on the sample.
We start from the left-hand side of Equation (
12
) and we decompose the probability over the various values of
k
:

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k_{S}(h),\delta)\right]

\displaystyle=\Expectation_{S}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k_{S}(h),\delta)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\Expectation_{S}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k,\delta)\right]\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{S}(h)=k\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\Prob_{S}\left[k_{S}(h)=k\right]\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k,\delta)\right].

Here,
k_{S}(h)
is a binomial random variable with probability
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
, and therefore we have that
\Prob_{S}\left[k_{S}(h)=k\right]=\binom{m}{k}(R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))^{k}(1-R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))^{m-k}
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k_{S}(h),\delta)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-75.0pt=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))^{k}(1-R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))^{m-k}\,\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k,\delta)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-75.0pt=1-\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))^{k}(1-R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))^{m-k}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k,\delta)\right],

where we have used the fact that
\textnormal{Bin}(m,m,p)=1
for all values of
p
.

Because
\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k,\delta)
is strictly increasing with
k
, the indicator function implies that the sum is truncated at the largest value of
k
such that
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k,\delta)
.
Thus, define
\kappa(h)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\max\{k:R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k,\delta)\}
, so that the right-hand side can be written as

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k_{S}(h),\delta)\right]

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa(h)}\binom{m}{k}(R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))^{k}(1-R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))^{m-k}

\displaystyle=1-\textnormal{Bin}(m,\kappa(h),R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)).

Finally, because
\textnormal{Bin}(m,k,p)
is strictly decreasing with
p
and because we have that
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,\kappa(h),\delta)
, it follows that

\textnormal{Bin}(m,\kappa(h),R_{\mathcal{D}}(h))<\textnormal{Bin}(m,\kappa(h),\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,\kappa(h),\delta))=\delta

by definition of the inverse of the binomial tail.
Therefore, we end up with

\Prob_{S}\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}(m,k_{S}(h),\delta)\right]>1-\delta,

as desired
∎

Remark that every step of the proof holds with equality: the theorem is thus “essentially perfectly tight”, as written by
Langford 2005
.
Indeed, it is impossible to be tighter without further assumptions about the distribution
\mathcal{D}
at hand.

We would like to point out two critical steps of the proof which allow the approach to be perfectly tight, as opposed to other ones.
The first is the observation that one can truncate the summation over
k
using the indicator function, which eliminates superfluous terms leads to major improvements over other bounds.
The second, which follows from the first, is to notice that the truncated sum is nothing else than the distribution tail, which can be inverted quite easily with today’s computing power.

This bound is only valid for a single classifier at a time, and therefore cannot be used to select the best classifier
h
from a hypothesis class
{\mathcal{H}}
.
To mitigate this shortcoming,
Langford 2005
extends it to the case of a countable hypothesis class using the union bound.
However, machine learning algorithms usually consider uncountably infinite hypothesis classes, a challenge this present paper aims to tackle.

Appendix E
The hypergeometric tail inversion relative deviation bound

In this section, we consider the relative deviation version of the hypergeometric tail inversion bound.
We first state and prove the theorem from which Corollary
3
stems, and we then study the impact of the ghost sample size to numerically compare this version of the bound with the original of Theorem
2
.

E.1
Proof of the hypergeometric tail inversion relative deviation bound

Theorem
15
states that the difference between the true risk and the empirical risk is bounded from above as a function of the true risk with high probability.

Theorem 15
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class.
Then for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, any confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
and any integers
m
,
m^{\prime}>0
, the following inequality holds:

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\forall\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)\leq\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}\right]>1-\delta,

where

\eta(k)=\begin{cases}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{m^{\prime}}},\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\frac{u(k)-\frac{k}{m}}{\sqrt{u(k)}}\right\}&\text{if }0\leq k<m\\
0&\text{if }k=m\end{cases},

and

u(k)=\frac{1}{m+m^{\prime}}\left(\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(k,m,\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}\big)-1\right).

Notice that stating the theorem the way it is instead of in the form
\frac{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)}{\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}}\leq\eta(mR_{S}(h))
avoids the problem of division by zero completely.
This shows that dealing with
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=0
is not as complicated as was argued by
Cortes et al. 2019
.
To prove the theorem, we will make use of the following lemma, which will be used for the ghost sample trick.

Lemma 16
.

Consider the setting of Theorem
15
and let
S
and
S^{\prime}
be samples of sizes
m
and
m^{\prime}
respectively such that
T\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}(S,S^{\prime})
is i.i.d.
Then, for any hypothesis
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
and
\eta^{2}\geq\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}
, we have

\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}\right]<4\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right].

Note that similar results are offered by
Vapnik 1998
,
Anthony and Shawe-Taylor 1993
and
Cortes et al. 2019
, but differ in that
m^{\prime}
is set equal to
m
.

Proof.

of Lemma
16

Assume
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}
holds.
Then we need to show that

\frac{1}{4}<\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right].

To do so, we will make use of the theorem of
Greenberg and Mohri 2014
stating that

\frac{1}{4}<\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)\geq R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right],

(13)

provided that
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\geq\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}
.
Let us first confirm that our situation permits the use of this result.
From our assumption, we have that
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-\eta\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}>R_{S}(h)\geq 0
, which implies
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)>\eta^{2}\geq\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}
(from the definition of
\eta
as stated in the lemma).

If we can show that we have

\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)\geq R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right]<\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right],

then the lemma will follow directly from (
13
).
This inequality can be proven by showing that
R_{S^{\prime}}(h)\geq R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
implies
R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}
.
Therefore, assuming
R_{S^{\prime}}(h)\geq R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
holds, we will show that
\frac{R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)}{\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}}>\eta
.

Consider the function

f_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{\alpha x+\beta y}},

for
\alpha,\beta>0
,
x>0
and
y\geq 0
.
Taking the partial derivatives with respect to
x
and
y
, we have

\frac{\partial f_{\alpha,\beta}}{\partial x}=\frac{\alpha x+(\alpha+2\beta)y}{2(\alpha x+\beta y)^{3/2}}>0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\frac{\partial f_{\alpha,\beta}}{\partial y}=-\frac{(2\alpha+\beta)x+\beta y}{2(\alpha x+\beta y)^{3/2}}<0.

This informs us that
f_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)
is increasing with
x
and decreasing with
y
.

Applying this result to our original problem, we have

\displaystyle\frac{R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)}{\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)}{\sqrt{\frac{m^{\prime}R_{S^{\prime}}(h)+mR_{S}(h)}{m+m^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle=f_{\frac{m^{\prime}}{m+m^{\prime}},\frac{m}{m+m^{\prime}}}(R_{S^{\prime}}(h),R_{S}(h))

\displaystyle>f_{\frac{m^{\prime}}{m+m^{\prime}},\frac{m}{m+m^{\prime}}}(R_{\mathcal{D}}(h),R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-\eta\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}}{\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-\frac{m}{m+m^{\prime}}\eta\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}}}

\displaystyle>\eta,

where we have used both hypotheses to go from the second to the third line.
Note that the radicand at the fourth line is always greater than zero by our assumption that
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-\eta\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}>0
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the relative deviation bound.
Note that many steps of the proof are similar to the steps of our main theorem’s proof.
As such, we do not delve into details and justifications here unless the proof differs.

Proof.

of Theorem
15

We will prove the result by proving that the opposite event occurs with probability less than or equal to
\delta
,
i.e.
,

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}\right]\leq\delta.

We begin with the ghost sample trick, using Lemma
16
to replace the dependence on the true risk by the empirical risk of a second sample.
Notice that the definition of
\eta(k)
in Theorem
15
makes the probability zero whenever
k=m
and otherwise we have that
\eta(k)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}
so that the requirements of the lemma are always satisfied.

Starting with the left-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt=\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\leq 4\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\leq 4\Expectation_{S,S^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{T}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\frac{m^{\prime}}{m+m^{\prime}}\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right]\right],

where
{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}
is the restriction of
{\mathcal{H}}
to the sample
T
, as defined in the proof of Theorem
2
, and at the last line we have used the relation
R_{T}(h)=\frac{mR_{S}(h)+m^{\prime}R_{S^{\prime}}(h)}{m+m^{\prime}}
to express the inequality in terms of
R_{T}(h)
.

We can now proceed to the symmetrization trick, using the same notation as before, letting
\sigma\in\Sigma\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\{\sigma\subseteq[m+m^{\prime}]\>:\>\left|\sigma\right|=m\}
denote a set of
m
indices, and defining
T(\sigma)
as the examples of
T
pointed by the indices of
\sigma
, such that
S=T([m])
.
Again assuming the uniform distribution over
\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-R_{S}(h)>\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\leq 4\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{T}(h)-R_{T([m])}(h)>\frac{m^{\prime}}{m+m^{\prime}}\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\leq 4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{T}(h)-R_{T([m])}(h)>\frac{m^{\prime}}{m+m^{\prime}}\eta(mR_{S}(h))\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right]\right]

(14)

Before going further, let us take a closer look at the inequality inside the indicator function.
We will show that the expression of
\eta(k)
of Theorem

\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{T}(h)\hskip-1.0pt-\hskip-1.0pt\frac{k}{m}\!>\!\frac{m^{\prime}}{m+m^{\prime}}\eta(k)\sqrt{R_{T}(h)}\right]\hskip-1.0pt\leq\hskip-1.0pt\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{T}(h)\!\geq\!\frac{1}{m+m^{\prime}}\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(k,m,\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}\big)\!\right]\!,

or in other words, that the statement inside the left indicator function implies the statement inside the right indicator function.

Therefore, assume that

R_{T}-\frac{k}{m}>\frac{m^{\prime}}{m+m^{\prime}}\eta\sqrt{R_{T}}

(15)

is true, where we mute dependencies to alleviate the notation.
As a first step, we transform the inequality into a quadratic form by squaring both sides to get

According to the fundamental theorem of algebra, we can factor the left-hand side to obtain

(R_{T}-r_{-})(R_{T}-r_{+})>0,

r_{\pm}=\frac{k}{m}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\frac{m^{\prime 2}}{(m+m^{\prime})^{2}}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1+\frac{4k}{m}\frac{(m+m^{\prime})^{2}}{m^{\prime 2}\eta^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle R_{T}(h)

\displaystyle>r_{+}(\eta(k))

\displaystyle=r_{+}\left(\max\left\{\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{m^{\prime}}},\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\frac{u(k)-\frac{k}{m}}{\sqrt{u(k)}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\textstyle r_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m^{\prime}}}\right),r_{+}\left(\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\frac{u(k)-\frac{k}{m}}{\sqrt{u(k)}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq r_{+}\left(\textstyle\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\frac{u(k)-\frac{k}{m}}{\sqrt{u(k)}}\right)

\displaystyle=u(k)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa(h)}\textnormal{hyp}(k,m,(m+m^{\prime})R_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})\right]

Appendix F
Proof of the hypergeometric tail inversion margin bound

In this Appendix, we prove Theorem
5
, which bounds the risk of margin classifiers.
The bound is inspired from the work of
Anthony and Bartlett 1999
.

We assume the set-up of Section
4.2
, where classifiers are of the form

h_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x})=\argmax_{i\in[n]}f_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

with
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}\subseteq([0,1]^{n})^{\mathcal{X}}
a vector-valued function.
We define the margin
5
5

5

Note that we have adapted the setting of
Anthony and Bartlett 1999
to apply to multiclass problems, and therefore the definition of the margin is different.

of a classifier
h_{\mathbf{f}}
as

\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),y)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}f_{y}(\mathbf{x})-\max_{j\neq y}f_{j}(\mathbf{x}).

The mixed
L_{p}
-
L_{\infty}
metric
\ell
on
\mathds{R}^{n\times m}
we consider is explicitly defined as

\ell(A,B)=\max_{i\in[m]}\left\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{b}_{i}\right\rVert_{p}

where
\mathbf{a}_{i}
and
\mathbf{b}_{i}
are the
i
-th columns of
A
and
B
, respectively, and
p>0
.

We additionally define the true margin risk
R^{\gamma}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{f})
as the probability that the margin of a classifier is smaller than a margin parameter
\gamma
:

R^{\gamma}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{f})=\Prob_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\left[\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),y)<\gamma\right].

(20)

Since we have
\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[h(\mathbf{x})\neq y\right]\leq\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),y)\leq 0\right]
(with strict inequality only on tie breaks), it implies that
R_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{f})\leq R^{\gamma}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{f})
for any
\gamma>0
.

Below, we repeat the theorem for convenience.

Theorem
5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq([0,1]^{n})^{\mathcal{X}}
be a vector-valued function class,
h_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x})=\argmax_{i\in[n]}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
be a classifier and
R^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f})\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\Expectation_{S}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})),y)<\gamma\right]\right]
be the empirical margin risk.
Then for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, any confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, any margin
\gamma\in(0,1)
, any
p\geq 1
and any integers
m
and
m^{\prime}>0
,

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\forall\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})\leq\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}),m,\delta)\right]>1-\delta,

where
\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=1
if
k=m
and otherwise

\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\max\left\{1,\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(k,\frac{\delta}{4\mathcal{N}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2^{2-1/p}},m+m^{\prime}\right)},m,m+m^{\prime}\big)-1-k\right\}.

Note that we ask for the metric of the cover to have a
p\geq 1
rather than greater than 0.
It is possible to let
p\in(0,1)
, by considering a
\frac{\gamma}{2}
-cover instead of a
\frac{\gamma}{2^{2-1/p}}
-cover, but this can only worsen the bound.
The details are discussed in a footnote of the proof.

Many steps of the proof are similar to the proof of Theorem
2
.
Therefore, we shall not justify all steps and invite the reader to consult the aforementioned proof if needed.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
2
, we will prove the equivalent statement:

\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}),m,\delta)\right]\leq\delta.

We first proceed with the ghost sample trick using the same result of
Greenberg and Mohri 2014
as before.
It states that
\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}>\epsilon(k)\right]<4\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)>\epsilon(k)\right]
, provided that
\epsilon(k)\geq\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}
, which holds according to our definition of
\epsilon
.
We have, muting dependencies,

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}))\right]

\displaystyle=\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}))\right]\right]

\displaystyle<\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}}4\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(\mathbf{f})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}))\right]\right]

\displaystyle<4\Expectation_{S,S^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(\mathbf{f})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}))\right]\right].

Next, we continue with the symmetrization trick, using the same notation as before, letting
\sigma\in\Sigma\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\{\sigma\subseteq[m+m^{\prime}]\>:\>\left|\sigma\right|=m\}
denote a set of
m
indices, and defining
T(\sigma)
as the examples of
T
with index in
\sigma
, which means
S=T([m])
and
S^{\prime}=T([m+m^{\prime}]-[m])
.
Again assuming the uniform distribution over
\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}))\right]

\displaystyle<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-[m])}(\mathbf{f})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{T([m])}(\mathbf{f}))\right]\right]

\displaystyle=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-[m])}(\mathbf{f})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{T([m])}(\mathbf{f}))\right]\right]

\displaystyle=4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\mathbf{f})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{T(\sigma)}(\mathbf{f}))\right]\right]\right].

At this point, we must depart from the proof of our main theorem for two reasons: the supremum over
\mathcal{F}|_{T}
might be infinite, and we deal with standard and
\gamma
-margin risks at the same time.

To solve the problem, we aim to replace the dependence on
\mathcal{F}|_{T}
by a finite minimal
c
-cover
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n\times\left|T\right|}
, where
c=\frac{\gamma}{2^{2-1/p}}
.
By definition of a
c
-cover, we have that for each
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F}|_{T}
, there exists
\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}
such that
\max_{i}\lVert\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\hat{\mathbf{f}}^{i}\rVert_{p}<c
.
To limit possible confusion, we denote by
\hat{\mathbf{f}}^{i}
the
n
-dimensional vector associated to example
i
and by
\hat{f}_{j}^{i}
the
j
-th component of
\hat{\mathbf{f}}^{i}
.

We consider how the standard risk
R_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\mathbf{f})
is affected by this modification first.
Let
(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})
be any example in
T
and define
\Delta^{i}\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\hat{\mathbf{f}}^{i}
to be the cover error of
\hat{\mathbf{f}}^{i}
.
The condition
\argmax_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\neq y_{i}
implies
\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),y_{i})\leq 0
.
Furthermore, we have that
\max_{j\neq y_{i}}\hat{f}_{j}^{i}\geq\hat{f}_{u}^{i}
is true for any
u\neq y_{i}
, and this holds in particular for
l\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\argmax_{j\neq y_{i}}f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
Therefore, assuming
\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),y_{i})=f_{y_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-f_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\leq 0

\displaystyle\mu(\hat{\mathbf{f}}^{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=\hat{f}^{i}_{y_{i}}-\max_{j\neq y_{i}}\hat{f}^{i}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\hat{f}^{i}_{y_{i}}-\hat{f}^{i}_{l}

\displaystyle=(f_{y_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\Delta_{y_{i}}^{i})-(f_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\Delta_{l}^{i})

\displaystyle\leq-\Delta_{y_{i}}^{i}+\Delta_{l}^{i}.

(21)

-\Delta_{y_{i}}^{i}+\Delta_{l}^{i}
knowing that
\max_{i}\lVert\Delta^{i}\rVert_{p}<c

Let
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathds{R}^{2}

\displaystyle-\Delta_{y_{i}}^{i}+\Delta_{l}^{i}<\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p}<c}-z_{1}+z_{2}.

Notice that the strict inequality comes from the observation that no
\mathbf{z}\in\mathds{R}^{2}

One can use the constraint
\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{p}<c

Since we want to maximize

z_{2}\geq 0

\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{p}<c

We have

c^{p}>\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=(-z_{1})^{p}+z_{2}^{p}.

Isolating

\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p}<c}-z_{1}+z_{2}\leq\max_{z_{2}}(c^{p}-z_{2}^{p})^{1/p}+z_{2}.

Differentiating the right-hand side with respect to
z_{2}

.

6
6

6

This implies that, for the proof to carry over, the minimal cover of

This yields, using the definition of

Using this result in our original Equation (

R_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\mathbf{f})\leq R^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}).

Next, to address the margin risk

\mu(\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{i},y_{i})<\frac{\gamma}{2}

.
Using the same reasoning as before, we have

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),y_{i})

\displaystyle=f_{y_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\max_{j\neq y_{i}}f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq f_{y_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-f_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=(\hat{f}_{y_{i}}^{i}+\Delta_{y_{i}}^{i})-(\hat{f}_{l}^{i}+\Delta_{l}^{i})

\displaystyle<\frac{\gamma}{2}+\Delta_{y_{i}}^{i}-\Delta_{l}^{i}

\displaystyle<\frac{\gamma}{2}+\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p}<c}(z_{1}-z_{2})

\displaystyle=\gamma.

This shows that
\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mu(\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{i},y_{i})<\frac{\gamma}{2}\right]\leq\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mu(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),y_{i})<\gamma\right]
, and so we have
R^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T(\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})\leq R^{\gamma}_{T(\sigma)}(\mathbf{f})
.

Putting everything back together with the fact that
\epsilon(k)
is increasing with
k
, as discussed in the proof of the main theorem, we end up with

R^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})\geq R_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\mathbf{f})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{T(\sigma)}(\mathbf{f}))\geq\epsilon(R^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T(\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})),

which allows us to write

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}))\right]<4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})>\epsilon(mR^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T(\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}))\right]\right]\right].

The remainder of the proof is then identical to the proof of Theorem
2
with the exception that the empirical risks are replaced by margin risks.
Denoting
\left|T\right|\cdot R^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T}(\mathbf{f})
by
k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T}(\mathbf{f})
, it goes as follows.

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}))\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt<4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T(\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}))\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt<4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T(\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}))\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt=4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)\right]\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T(\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})=k\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt=4\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T(\sigma)}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})=k\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\textnormal{hyp}\big(k,m,k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}),m+m^{\prime}\big)\right]

Defining
\kappa(\hat{\mathbf{f}})\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\max\left\{k:k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})>m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right\}
, the last expression becomes

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{F},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathbf{f}})>\epsilon(mR^{\gamma}_{S}(\mathbf{f}))\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa(\hat{\mathbf{f}})}\textnormal{hyp}\big(k,m,k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}),m+m^{\prime}\big)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\textnormal{Hyp}\big(\kappa(\hat{\mathbf{f}}),m,k^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}_{T}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}),m+m^{\prime}\big)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\leq 4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\textnormal{Hyp}\big(\kappa(\hat{\mathbf{f}}),m,\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(\kappa(\hat{\mathbf{f}}),\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2^{2-1/p}},m+m^{\prime}\right)},m,m+m^{\prime}\big),m+m^{\prime}\big)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\leq 4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2^{2-1/p}},m+m^{\prime}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt<\max_{T}\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\textstyle\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2^{2-1/p}},m+m^{\prime}\right)}

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt<\delta.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix G
The hypergeometric tail lower bound

In this appendix, we prove the lower bound, and then we compare it to the upper bound.

G.1
Proof of the hypergeometric tail lower bound

In this section, we prove Theorem
7
.
For convenience, we reproduce below the statement of the theorem.

Theorem
7
(Hypergeometric tail inversion lower bound)
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class.
Then for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, any confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
and any integers
m,m^{\prime}>0
, the following inequality holds:

\Prob_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\forall\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\epsilon(mR_{S}(h),m,\delta)\right]>1-\delta,

where
\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=0
if
k=0
and otherwise

\epsilon(k,m,\delta)=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\min\left\{m^{\prime}-1,\;\underline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(k-1,m,1-\textstyle\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}\big)+1-k\right\}.

The proof being fairly similar to that of Theorem
2
, we do not give a thorough explanation of steps common to both proofs, and we focus of the differences instead.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
2
, we shall prove that the opposite event occurs with probability less than or equal to
\delta
,
i.e.
, we want to have

\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\leq\delta,

where we used
k_{S}(h)=mR_{S}(h)
.

To apply the ghost sample trick, the result of
Greenberg and Mohri 2014
used in the proof of the upper bound cannot be used; however, they also provide a similar result which holds for our current needs.
It states that, for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
,

\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[R_{S^{\prime}}(h)\leq R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right]>\frac{1}{4},

as long as
R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\frac{m^{\prime}-1}{m^{\prime}}
holds, a condition satisfied by the definition of
\epsilon
.
Following the same train of thoughts as in the proof of Theorem
2
, this inequality implies

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]

\displaystyle=\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right]

\displaystyle<4\Expectation_{S}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\Prob_{S^{\prime}}\left[k_{S^{\prime}}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\Expectation_{S,S^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{S^{\prime}}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]\right]

Next, let
T=(S,S^{\prime})
be an i.i.d. sample and let
{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}
be the restriction of
{\mathcal{H}}
to
T
.
Using the same notation as before, we have

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]

\displaystyle<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T([m+m^{\prime}]-[m])}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{T([m])}(h))\right]\right]

\displaystyle=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k_{T([m])}(h))+k_{T([m])}(h)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\max_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T([m])}(h)=k\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T([m])}(h)=k\right]\right].

Note that here the sum over
k
goes from
1
to
m
because the term
k=0
vanishes since
\epsilon(0)=0
.

For the symmetrization trick, let
\sigma\in\Sigma
be a combination of
m
indices chosen among
[m+m^{\prime}]
as in the proof for the upper bound.
Assuming the uniform distribution over
\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\Expectation_{\sigma}\left[\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T(\sigma)}(h)=k\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\hskip-2.5pt\left[k_{T}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right]\textnormal{hyp}(k,m,k_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})\right].

Now, define
\kappa(h)\overset{\smash{\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{=}\min\left\{k\in[m]:k_{T}(h)<m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k\right\}
.
Because
m^{\prime}\epsilon(k)+k
is an increasing function of
k
, this allows us to write

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]

\displaystyle<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\sum_{k=\kappa(h)}^{m}\textnormal{hyp}(k,m,k_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}1-\textnormal{Hyp}(\kappa(h)-1,m,k_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})\right].

(22)

On the other hand, the definition of
\kappa(h)
implies that we have (using the definition of
\epsilon
)

k_{T}(h)\leq\underline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(\kappa(h)-1,m,\textstyle 1-\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}\big).

Moreover,
\textnormal{Hyp}(k,K,m,M)
being decreasing with
K
as shown in Lemma
11
, implies

\displaystyle 1-\textnormal{Hyp}(\kappa(h)-1,m,k_{T}(h),m+m^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt\leq 1-\textnormal{Hyp}(\kappa(h)-1,m,\underline{\textnormal{Hyp}}\big(\kappa(h)-1,m,\textstyle 1-\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},m+m^{\prime}\big),m+m^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt\leq 1-\left(\textstyle 1-\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt=\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})},

where the third line holds by definition of the lower pseudo-inverse.

Substituting this result in Equation (
22
) yields

\displaystyle\Prob_{S}\left[\hskip 1.0pt\exists\hskip 1.0pth\in{\mathcal{H}},R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\epsilon(k_{S}(h))\right]<4\Expectation_{T}\left[\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}}\frac{\delta}{4\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})}\right]<\frac{\delta}{\tau_{\mathcal{H}}(m+m^{\prime})}\max_{T}\Big|{\mathcal{H}}|_{T}\Big|=\delta,

which concludes the proof.
∎

H.2
Comparison to sample compression

Sample compression bounds
(
Floyd and Warmuth 1995
)
exploit the idea that a classifier can be described with only a small subset of the sample.
Such a subset is called a compression set, and its size
d
acts as an indicator of the complexity of the chosen class of hypotheses.
In that sense, this parameter is analogous to the VC dimension.
Sample compression bounds are known to be very tight (although subject to the same optimal asymptotic rate as VC bounds).
Indeed, using the binomial tail inversion,
Langford 2005
and
Laviolette et al. 2009
have shown that they are nearly as tight as one might hope.

The Warmuth-Littlestone
(
Floyd and Warmuth 1995
)
conjecture states that any hypothesis class with VC dimension
d
admits a compression scheme with size in
{O}(d)
.
7
7

7

A strong version of the conjecture, stated by
Kuzmin and Warmuth 2007
, requires a compression size of at most
d
, which has been refuted by
Pálvölgyi and Tardos 2020
, although this result concerns unlabeled compression schemes.

If this conjecture was true, it would imply that one could use the (tighter) sample compression bounds instead of traditional VC bounds for most applications.
Yet, despite some significant progress
(
Moran and Yehudayoff 2016
)
, the weak version of the Warmuth-Littlestone conjecture is still an open problem.
Furthermore, it is not always clear how to construct an appropriate compression scheme with a reasonable message length, which has a compression size equal to (or slightly larger than) the VC dimension of the hypothesis class.
Therefore, there are still plenty of situations where VC bounds can prove useful.

Nevertheless, it is of interest to see how the new VC bound compares to sample compression bounds.
Thus, we assume a context where both generalization bounds apply, and we consider an
optimistic
situation where no reconstruction message is required, since it would be specific to the hypothesis class.
Note that such a reconstruction message would loosen the compression bound.
With these assumptions, we can use the following bound
(
Langford 2005
;
Laviolette et al. 2009
)
:

\epsilon_{\textnormal{SC}}(R_{S}(h))=\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}\left(mR_{S}(h),m-d,\textstyle\frac{\delta}{m\binom{m}{d}}\right),

where
\overline{\textnormal{Bin}}
is the binomial tail inversion (defined analogously to
\overline{\textnormal{Hyp}}
) and
d
is the size of the compression scheme.
Assuming the Warmuth-Littlestone conjecture holds, we set the compressed sample size equal to the VC dimension at a value of
50
.
