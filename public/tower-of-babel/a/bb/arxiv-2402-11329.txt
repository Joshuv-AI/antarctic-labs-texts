On a recent extension of a family of biprojective APN functions
Lukas Kölsch

Abstract
APN functions play a big role as primitives in symmetric cryptography as building blocks that yield optimal resistance to differential attacks. In this note, we consider a recent extension of a biprojective APN family by Göloğlu defined on 𝔽 2 2 ​ m \mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}} . We show that this generalization yields functions equivalent to Göloğlu’s original family if 3 ∤ m 3\nmid m . If 3 | m 3|m we show exactly how many inequivalent APN functions this new family contains. We also show that the family has the minimal image set size for an APN function and determine its Walsh spectrum, hereby settling some open problems. In our proofs, we leverage a group theoretic technique recently developed by Göloğlu and the author in conjunction with a group action on the set of projective polynomials. Keywords: APN function, biprojective functions, automorphism group, Walsh spectrum.



1
Introduction and preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
be the finite field with
2^{n}
elements. Vectorial Boolean functions
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
play an important role in the construction of many block ciphers as potential building blocks for S-Boxes of substitution-permutation networks (SPNs). To be secure against differential attacks, the differential uniformity of an S-Box must be low, for a comprehensive overview, we refer the reader to
[
5
, Section 3.4]
:

Definition 1.1
.

A function
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
has
differential uniformity

\delta
if

\delta=\max_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}},a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}}|\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\colon F(x+a)+F(x)=b\}|.

Clearly,
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
satisfies the equation
F(x+a)+F(x)=b
if and only if
x+a
does, so
\delta
must be even. In particular, the lowest, and thus best possible value, is
\delta=2
. Vectorial Boolean functions
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
satisfying the optimal bound
\delta=2
are called
Almost Perfect Nonlinear (APN)
. There are different ways to represent (APN) functions on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. We will mention two that are relevant to this paper: Firstly, every function mapping
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
to itself can be written (uniquely) as a univariate polynomial of degree at most
2^{n}-1
. Secondly, if
n=2m
is even, one can write a function
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
in a
bivariate
way, i.e.

F(x,y)=(F_{1}(x,y),F_{2}(x,y)),

(1.1)

where
F_{1},F_{2}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
.

Over the last two decades, several constructions of APN functions have been found, although constructing APN functions ”by hand” (i.e. without computer searches) remains a difficult task, and not many infinite families are known. All of the known theoretic constructions of APN functions can be written as momomials
x\mapsto x^{d}
or as so called quadratic functions.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
be a function written as a polynomial
F(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}x^{i}
. The
algebraic degree

\deg(F)
of
F
is defined by
\deg(F)=\max_{i\colon a_{i}\neq 0}\wt(i)
, where
\wt(t)
denotes the binary weight of the integer
i
, i.e., the number of ones written in the base
2
representation of
i
.

A function is called
quadratic
if its algebraic degree is
2
. Similarly, a function written in the bivariate way is quadratic if the functions
F_{1},F_{2}
from Eq. (
1.1
) only contain the monomials
x^{2^{i}}y^{2^{j}}
,
x^{2^{i}+1}
,
y^{2^{j}+1}
for some
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
. This is the case if and only if all of its discrete derivatives
\Delta_{a}F:=F(x+a)+F(x)
for
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
are
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear, see
[
5
]
. This also explains why finding quadratic APN functions is comparatively easier than non-quadratic ones: A quadratic function is APN if and only if all its (linear) discrete derivatives
\Delta_{a}F=F(x+a)+F(x)
have a one dimensional kernel over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Certain equivalence relations leave the differential uniformity and thus the APN property invariant.

Denote by
\Gamma_{F}=\{(x,F(x))\colon x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\}

the
graph
of
F
.

Definition 1.3
.

Two functions

are called

(i)

CCZ-equivalent
, if there exists an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-affine permutation

N&L\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\

v\end{pmatrix}

of

;

(ii)

if

and

are CCZ-equivalent with

;

(iii)

,

and

;

(iv)

,

if

and

are EA-equivalent with

(v)

if

All these relations preserve the APN property, see

. EL-equivalence between

Two quadratic APN functions

Determining equivalence

We fix for the rest of the paper the following terminology:
K=\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
,

Crucial objects that play a major role for the APN functions in Theorem

for a standard reference. Note that both

2.1
A useful group action on (bi)projective polynomials

We now define a group action by
G=K^{*}\times\GL(2,K)
on the set of
2\times 2
matrices
M_{2\times 2}(K)
as follows:
M=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}&c_{3}\\
c_{2}&c_{4}\end{pmatrix}\in\GL(2,K)
acts on a matrix
A\in M_{2\times 2}(K)
for a fixed field automorphism
\sigma
via

M\circ A=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\end{pmatrix}MA(M^{\sigma})^{t}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\sigma}\\
y^{\sigma}\end{pmatrix}

where
M^{t}
denotes as usual the transpose of
M
and
M^{\sigma}
is the matrix where
\sigma
is applied to every entry.
K^{*}
acts on
A\in M_{2\times 2}(K)
by regular multiplication.

This group action relates to biprojective polynomials in the following way: We can identify
f(x,y)=a_{1}x^{\sigma+1}+a_{2}x^{\sigma}y+a_{3}xy^{\sigma}+a_{4}y^{\sigma+1}\in K[x,y]
with
A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1}&a_{3}\\
a_{2}&a_{4}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in M_{2\times 2}(K)
and view the group
G
acting on the set of all biprojective polynomials via this identification. A technical but straightforward calculation then shows (see
[
9
]
) that using this identification, we get
Mf(x,y)=f(c_{1}x+c_{2}y,c_{3}x+c_{4}y)
and
af(x,y)=af(x,y)
. We want to note that this group action is less artificial than it might appear and has also been considered e.g. in
[
1
,
2
,
9
]
to deal with combinatorial objects constructed via projective polynomials since it essentially captures linear changes in a (bi)projective polynomial.

Counting the number of inequivalent APN functions in the extended family

In this section, we use group theoretical tools to determine the number of inequivalent function in the extended APN family. The machinery is a variant used by the author and Göloğlu in a series of papers to establish (in)equivalence of combinatoral structures

We start by spotting a simple equivalence:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\sigma\colon x\mapsto x^{2^{k}}
and
\overline{\sigma}\colon x\mapsto x^{2^{m-k}}
be field automorphisms on
K=\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
that are inverse to each other. Then
F_{\alpha,\sigma}
and
F_{\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}},\overline{\sigma}}
are linear equivalent.

Proof.

Recall
F_{\alpha,\sigma}=(f_{\alpha,\sigma},g_{\alpha,\sigma})
. We apply
\overline{\sigma}
to
f_{\alpha,\sigma}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{2}
to
g_{\alpha,\sigma}
, we get

F^{\prime}=(x^{\overline{\sigma}+1}+x^{\overline{\sigma}}y+\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}}y^{\overline{\sigma}+1},x^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}+1}+\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}}xy^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}}+(1+\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}})x^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}}y+\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}}y^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}+1}).

Now perform a shift
x\mapsto x+y
and we get

F^{\prime\prime}=(x^{\overline{\sigma}+1}+xy^{\overline{\sigma}}+\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}}y^{\overline{\sigma}+1},x^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}+1}+\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}}x^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}}y+(1+\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}})xy^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}}+\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}}y^{\overline{\sigma}^{2}+1}).

This is exactly
F_{\alpha^{\overline{\sigma}},\overline{\sigma}}
.
∎

Note that
p_{1}=X^{\sigma+1}+X+\alpha
has no roots in

if and only if
p_{2}=X^{\overline{\sigma}+1}+X+\alpha^{\sigma}
has no roots in

. Indeed, applying
\overline{\sigma}
to

and then a transformation

exactly yields

.

Theorem 3 in
[
9
]
gives a very strong and general tool to determine when two biprojective APN functions are equivalent or not. More precisely, it gives very strong conditions on the form of potential equivalences between biprojective functions. The result relies on group theoretic properties based on the very special automorphism groups of such functions. We will not explain the entire theory here, instead just focus on the parts needed to our case. The interested reader is invited to read the extended exposition in
[
9
]
. Still, we will need to introduce a bit of notation to explain this result.

We define the group of EL-mappings (i.e., the set of mappings that correspond to extended linear transformations on graphs) as

\ELM=\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}M&0\\
N&L\end{pmatrix}\in\GL(K^{2})\cong\GL(2m,\mathbb{F}_{2})\Bigg\}.

where
M,N,L
are linear mappings on
K
.

Further denote by

\Aut_{\EL}(F)=\{\mathcal{A}\in\ELM\colon\mathcal{A}(\Gamma_{F})=\Gamma_{F}\}

the group of EL-automorphisms of a function
F
. Clearly, if
F
and
G
are EL-equivalent, the corresponding EL-automorphism groups are conjugate in
\ELM
; this is essentially just a simple application of the orbit-stabilizer theorem.

Proposition 3.2
.

[
9
, Proposition 1]

Assume
F,G\colon\mathbb{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
are EL-equivalent via the EL-mapping
\gamma\in\ELM
, i.e.,
\gamma(\Gamma_{F})=\Gamma_{G}
. Then
\Aut_{\EL}(F)=\gamma^{-1}\Aut_{\EL}(G)\gamma
.

The important fact is that all biprojective functions have a big subgroup in the group of EL-automorphisms that can be written down in a simple and explicit way, this was the main fact leveraged in
[
9
]
. Since the functions
F_{\alpha,\sigma}
we investigate are a special case of biprojective function, we thus have the following.

Proposition 3.3
.

[
9
, Lemma 3]

Let
M_{a}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&0\\
0&a\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\GL(2,K)
,
L_{a}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a^{\sigma+1}&0\\
0&a^{\sigma^{2}+1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\GL(2,K)
for
a\in K^{*}
. Then

Z^{\sigma}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}M_{a}&0\\
0&L_{a}\end{pmatrix}\colon a\in K^{*}\right\}\leq\Aut_{\EL}(F_{\alpha,\sigma})

for any admissible
\alpha
.

Clearly,
Z^{\sigma}
is a cyclic group of order
|K^{*}|=2^{m}-1
.

Let
p
be a Zsygmondy prime of
2^{m}-1
, i.e. a prime number
p
such that
p|2^{m}-1
but
p\nmid 2^{r}-1
for any
r<m
. Such a
p
always exists by a theorem of Zsygmondy if
m>1
,
m\neq 6
, see
[
12
, Chapter IX., Theorem 8.3.]
. Since
Z^{\sigma}
is cyclic and
p
divides
|Z^{\sigma}|=2^{m}-1
, we have a unique Sylow
p
-group in
Z^{\sigma}
, which we will denote by
Z^{\sigma}_{p}
. If
R
is the unique Sylow
p
-subgroup of
K^{*}
then clearly

Z^{\sigma}_{p}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}M_{a}&0\\
0&L_{a}\end{pmatrix}\colon a\in R\right\}\leq\Aut_{\EL}(F_{\alpha,\sigma}).

We also denote by
C_{\alpha,\sigma}
the centralizer of
Z^{\sigma}
in
\Aut_{\EL}(F_{\alpha,\sigma})
.

With this notation in place, we may state the equivalence result from
[
9
]
.
Applied to the functions
F_{\alpha,\sigma}
from Theorem
1.6
, it states the following:

Theorem 3.4
.

[
9
, Theorem 3]

Assume
m>2
and
m\notin\{4,6\}
. Let
F_{\alpha_{1},\sigma}=(f_{1},g_{1})
and
F_{\alpha_{2},\tau}=(f_{2},g_{2})
be APN functions from Theorem
1.6
defined on
K^{2}=\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{2}
with field automorphisms
\sigma,\tau
.
Assume further that

p\text{ does not divide }|C_{\alpha,\sigma}|/(2^{m}-1).

(C)

Then
F_{\alpha_{1},\sigma},F_{\alpha_{2},\tau}
cannot be EL-equivalent unless
\sigma=\tau
or
\sigma=\tau^{-1}
.

We thus only have to verify Condition (
C
) for all
F_{\alpha,\sigma}
. Similar calculations are done in
[
9
]
, where it is shown that if
\gamma\in C_{\alpha,\sigma}
then
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}M&0\\
0&L\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where
M,L\in\GL(2,K)
and

is a diagonal matrix, this is shown in
[

, Proof of Lemma 8]
and essentially hinges on the fact that the centralizer of

0&a\end{smallmatrix}\right)\colon a\in R\}\leq\GL(2,K)
in the group
\GL(K^{2})\cong\GL(2m,\mathbb{F}_{2})
is precisely
\GL(2,K)
, which was shown in
[
10
, Lemma 5.7.]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

All elements in
C_{\alpha,\sigma}
are of the form

\begin{pmatrix}M&0\\
0&L\end{pmatrix},

where
M,L\in\GL(2,K)\leq GL(K^{2})
and
L
is a diagonal matrix. For each fixed value of
c\in K^{*}
there are exactly
3
different elements in
C_{\alpha,\sigma}
such that
\det(M)=c
.

In particular,
|C_{\alpha,\sigma}|=3(2^{m}-1)
and Condition (
C
) is satisfied if

.

Proof.

From the discussion above, it is already clear that all elements in
C_{\alpha,\sigma}
are of the form mentioned above. It remains to show that for each fixed value of
c\in K^{*}
there are exactly
3
different elements in
C_{\alpha,\sigma}
such that
\det(M)=c
.

So let us assume we have
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}M&0\\
0&L\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in C_{\alpha,\sigma}.
We then have
L\circ F_{\alpha,\sigma}=F_{\alpha,\sigma}\circ M
Set
M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c_{1}&c_{2}\\
c_{3}&c_{4}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
L=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&0\\
0&a^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Then
L\circ F_{\alpha,\sigma}=F_{\alpha,\sigma}\circ M
is equivalent to

\displaystyle af_{\alpha,\sigma}(x,y)

\displaystyle=f_{\alpha,\sigma}(c_{1}x+c_{2}y,c_{3}x+c_{4}y)

\displaystyle a^{\prime}g_{\alpha,\sigma}(x,y)

\displaystyle=g_{\alpha,\sigma}(c_{1}x+c_{2}y,c_{3}x+c_{4}y).

By Lemma

, if the first equation is satisfied, there is precisely one

that satisfied the second equation. We thus only have to check the first equation, which is equivalent to
(a,M)
being in the stabilizer of the group action defined by
G
in Section

. The result then follows immediately with Proposition
2.3
.

Note that the Zsygmondy prime
p
does not divide

for

∎

Having proven Condition (

Theorem 3.6
.

Let

and
m\notin\{4,6\}

and

be two APN functions from Theorem

or

, where
\overline{\tau}
is the inverse of
\tau
is the automorphism group of

.

There are in total
\varphi(m)/2
CCZ-inequivalent functions in the family from Theorem

defined on

.

Note that the result also holds in the

and

and (in the

and Proposition

4
The image sets and Walsh spectra of the APN functions

One of the most important properties of APN functions is their Walsh spectrum, which captures how a resistant a function is towards linear attacks when used as a function in a substitution permutation network, we again refer the reader to
[
5
]
for details.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
be a mapping. We define

W_{F}(b,a)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}}(-1)^{\Tr(bF(x)+ax)}\in\mathbb{Z}

for all
a,b\in\mathbb{F}
. We call the multisets

\{*W_{F}(b,a)\colon b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*},a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}*\}\text{ and }\{*|W_{F}(b,a)|\colon b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*},a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}*\}

the
Walsh spectrum
and the
extended Walsh spectrum
of
F
, respectively.

The extended Walsh spectrum is invariant under CCZ-equivalence. Most known APN functions in even dimension
n
have the so called
classical
(or Gold-like) extended Walsh spectrum, which contains the values
0,2^{n/2},2^{(n+2)/2}
precisely
(2^{n}-1)2^{n-2}
times,
(2^{n}-1)2^{n+1}/3
times and
(2^{n}-1)2^{n}/3
times, respectively.

Recently, the following simple criterion to determine the Walsh spectrum of an APN function from its value distributions was found.

Theorem 4.2
.

[
16
, Theorem 1]

Let
n
be even and
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
be a quadratic APN function such that

•

F(0)=0
, and

•

Every
y\in\image(F)\setminus\{0\}
has at least
3
preimages.

Then
F(x)=0
if and only
x=0
and every
y\in\image(F)\setminus\{0\}
has precisely
3
preimages (i.e.,
F
is
3
-to-
1
). Additionally,
F
has classical Walsh spectrum.

Note that APN
3
-to-
1
functions are the functions with the smallest possible image sets for APN functions in even dimension
[
16
]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

F_{\alpha,\sigma}
is
3
-to-
1
and has classical Walsh spectrum for all admissible values of
\alpha,\sigma
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
4.2
. It is immediate that
F_{\alpha,\sigma}(0,0)=(0,0)
.
Let us consider the equation
F_{\alpha,\sigma}\circ M=F_{\alpha,\sigma}
for
M\in\GL(2,K)\leq\GL(K^{2})
. This is equivalent to
(1,M)
being both in the stabilizer of
f_{\alpha,\sigma}
and
g_{\alpha,\sigma}
where we use again the group action defined in Section
2
. The matrix equations that characterize
(1,M)
being in the stabilizers are written down in Eqs. (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) and, taking again determinants, lead to
\det(M)^{\sigma+1}=\det(M)^{\sigma^{2}+1}=1
, which is equivalent to
\det(M)=1
.
By Proposition
2.3
, there are thus 3 distinct
M
(one of them being the identity matrix
I
) such that
(1,M)
is in the stabilizer of
f_{\alpha,\sigma}
. For these 3 matrices, we have
F_{\alpha,\sigma}(M(x,y))=F_{\alpha,\sigma}(x,y)
for all
x,y\in K
. Let us say the three matrices with this property are
I,M,N
. Clearly, if
M
has this property, then so does
M^{i}
for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, this immediately implies that
N=M^{2}
and
M^{3}=I
.

To show that
F_{\alpha,\sigma}
is
3
-to-
1
, it is enough to show that
M
has only
(0,0)
as a fixed point, i.e.
M(x,y)\neq(x,y)
for all
(0,0)\neq(x,y)\in K^{2}
, which is equivalent to
M
not having
1
as an eigenvalue. Since
M^{3}=I
the minimal polynomial of
M
has to be a divisor of
X^{3}+1=(X+1)(X^{2}+X+1)
but not
X+1
since
M\neq I
. Note that
X^{2}+X+1
has the roots
\omega\in\mathbb{F}_{4}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2}
, so it is an irreducible polynomial if
m
is odd. In this case, the minimal polynomial has to be
X^{2}+X+1
and
1
is not an eigenvalue. If
m
is even, then
X^{3}+1=(X+1)(X+\omega_{1})(X+\omega_{2})
where
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{4}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Assume
M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
has eigenvalue
1
, so the characteristic polynomial is
\chi_{M}(X)=(X+1)(X+\omega)=X^{2}+(\omega+1)X+\omega
for some
\omega\in\mathbb{F}_{4}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2}
. On the other hand
\chi_{M}(X)=\det\left(\begin{smallmatrix}X+a&b\\
c&X+d\end{smallmatrix}\right)=X^{2}+(a+d)X+ad+bc
, so comparing the constant coefficent we have
\omega=ad+bc
. This contradicts
ad+bc=\det(M)=1
. We conclude that
1
is not an eigenvalue of
M
, so
M(x,y)\neq(x,y)
for any
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
.

So
F_{\alpha,\sigma}(x,y)=F_{\alpha,\sigma}(M(x,y))=F_{\alpha,\sigma}(M^{2}(x,y))
for any

, where
(x,y)
,

are pairwise distinct if

. So all conditions of Theorem

are satisfied and the result follows.
∎

This is particularly interesting since

is

-

for

. Recall that by Theorem
2.4
different

(for the same

there are many

-to-

functions. More precisely, there are at least

or

(depending on

since this is the number of polynomials

with no roots in

[

.

5
Conclusion and open problems

In this note we proved that the recent extension of an APN family of Göloğlu only yields new APN functions on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}}
if
3|m
(Theorem
2.4
). In this case, we counted the number of inequivalent functions the extension yields (Theorem
3.6
) and we showed that all functions in the family are
3
-to-
1
and have classical Walsh spectrum (Theorem
4.3
). These results hinge on group theoretic tools developed in general for biprojective functions in
[
9
]
, based on large cyclic subgroups in the automorphism groups of these functions. We state some interesting open problems:

1.

Is it possible to generalize the group theoretic tools from
[
9
]
from biprojective functions to a wider class of functions? Note that a similar approach had already been used for power functions
[
7
,
18
]
before.

2.

Give a lower bound on the number of APN functions that is better than the current bound given in
[
15
]
.

Based on a similar conjecture for the (on a theoretical level) related combinatorial objects of semifields by Kantor
[
14
]
, in particular the explicit construction of semifields in
[
13
]
, we conjecture the following:

Conjecture 5.1
.

Let
N(Q)
be the number of APN functions on
\mathbb{F}_{Q}
.
N(Q)
is not bounded from above by a polynomial.

Note that the best current bound by Kaspers and Zhou
[
15
]
are not even linear in
Q
and thus quite far away from this bound. Better constructions or non-constructive arguments (for instance, using probabilistic methods) thus seem to be needed to tackle this conjecture.
