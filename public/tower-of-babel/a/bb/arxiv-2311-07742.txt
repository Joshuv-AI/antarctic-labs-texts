Abstract
. Self-attention (SA) mechanisms have been widely used in developing sequential recommendation (SR) methods, and demonstrated state-of-the-art performance. However, in this paper, we show that self-attentive SR methods substantially suffer from the over-smoothing issue that item embeddings within a sequence become increasingly similar across attention blocks. As widely demonstrated in the literature, this issue could lead to a loss of information in individual items, and significantly degrade models’ scalability and performance. To address the over-smoothing issue, in this paper, we view items within a sequence constituting a star graph and develop a method, denoted as 𝙼𝚂𝚂𝙶 \mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits , for SR. Different from existing self-attentive methods, 𝙼𝚂𝚂𝙶 \mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits introduces an additional internal node to specifically capture the global information within the sequence, and does not require information propagation among items. This design fundamentally addresses the over-smoothing issue and enables 𝙼𝚂𝚂𝙶 \mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits a linear time complexity with respect to the sequence length. We compare 𝙼𝚂𝚂𝙶 \mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits with ten state-of-the-art baseline methods on six public benchmark datasets. Our experimental results demonstrate that 𝙼𝚂𝚂𝙶 \mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits significantly outperforms the baseline methods, with an improvement of as much as 10.10%. Our analysis shows the superior scalability of 𝙼𝚂𝚂𝙶 \mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits over the state-of-the-art self-attentive methods. Our complexity analysis and run-time performance comparison together show that 𝙼𝚂𝚂𝙶 \mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits is both theoretically and practically more efficient than self-attentive methods. Our analysis of the attention weights learned in SA-based methods indicates that on sparse recommendation data, modeling dependencies in all item pairs using the SA mechanism yields limited information gain, and thus, might not benefit the recommendation performance 1 1 1 We will release the source code and processed datasets upon the acceptance of this paper. .

SR aims to

It has been widely employed in applications such as online retail

With the prosperity of deep learning,

From a graph perspective,
RNNs represent each interaction sequence using a sequential graph as shown in Figure

This design, however, may not
effectively capture the long-range dependencies within sequences as

.

To better capture the long-range dependencies, SA mechanisms
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)

have recently been utilized for
SR
(

;
Zhou et al. 2022
)
,
and demonstrated state-of-the-art performance
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
.
Given a sequence,
SA mechanisms learn attention weights for all pairs of items within sequences,
and aggregates items using these attention weights.
From a graph perspective, this is essentially
equivalent to viewing items
within a sequence as nodes
in a fully connected graph

The fully connected graph allows
each item node to propagate information
to all others in a single step,
thereby enabling SA-based methods to effectively capture long-range dependencies within a sequence.

Though promising,
modeling sequences as fully connected graphs
leads to two main issues.
First,
as will be demonstrated in our analysis (Section

),
SA-based SR methods substantially suffer from the

(

)

that the embeddings of items within a sequence could become more and more similar across attention blocks.
Learning similar embeddings for all items within a sequence leads to the loss of information on individual items, and thus,
could substantially degrade the model performance
(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Shi et al. 2022
;
Huang et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2018
)
.
In addition, as shown in Shi

(
Shi et al. 2022
)
, the over-smoothing issue also deteriorates the learning of deep models, thereby degrading the model scalability.
Second,
SA-based SR methods generally suffer from quadratic time complexities, which limit their utilities in large-scale recommendation applications.

Addressing the above issues while still effectively
capturing the long-range dependencies within
sequences represents a critical research challenge
in developing SR methods.
To tackle this challenge,
in this paper, we develop
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
,
a method that models user interaction sequences using star graphs for SR.
Specifically,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
stacks multiple blocks
to recursively capture users’ intent
from their interaction sequence
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
.

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
has an attention
layer and a feed-forward layer in each block.
In the attention layer,
as illustrated in Figure
1(c)
,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
models each sequence as a star graph, where edge weights are determined by the attention weights,
and introduces an additional internal node
c
to integrate information from all item nodes
v_{s_{t}}
, thereby capturing the user’s intent.
In the feed-forward layer,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
updates the embedding
of the internal node via non-linear
networks to more accurately estimate
the user’s intent.
Previous work
(
Chen et al. 2020
)

shows that requiring information propagation among all item nodes could be the essential reason for the over-smoothing issue.
Thus,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
does not propagate information among items.
Instead, it utilizes an internal node for information aggregation.
This design allows
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
to fundamentally address the over-smoothing issue (Section
6.3
).
Beyond addressing the
over-smoothing issue,
by modeling sequences as star graphs,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
also achieves a linear time complexity with respect to the sequence length as will be proven in Section
4.5
.
In addition,
similarly to that in SA mechanisms,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could effectively capture long-range dependencies within sequences as each item node could propagate information to the internal
node within one step.

We extensively compare
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
with ten state-of-the-art baseline methods on six benchmark datasets.
Our experimental results demonstrate that
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could remarkably outperform
the state-of-the-art baseline methods
on the six datasets,
with an improvement of up to 10.10% at Recall@
10
(Section
5.3.2
).
Our experimental results also show that

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
consistently outperforms
the state-of-the-art SA-based SR method on
users with different activity levels (Section
6.2
).
In addition,
our analysis reveals that existing
SA-based SR methods suffer from the over-smoothing issue (Section
6.3
),
which limits their scalability in learning
deep models (Section
6.4
).
Our complexity analysis (Section
4.5
) and run-time performance comparison (Section
6.5
)
together show that

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
is both theoretically and practically more efficient than the state-of-the-art
SA-based SR methods.
Our analysis also suggests that on sparse recommendation data, leveraging SA mechanisms to capture item dependencies could yield limited information gain.

We summarize our major contributions as follows:
1) To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work
demonstrating the over-smoothing issue in SA-based SR methods;
2) We develop
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
for SR in which we model sequences using star graphs to address
the over-smoothing issue and enable linear time complexity;
3)
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
outperforms ten state-of-the-art baseline methods on six benchmark datasets;
4) Our analysis suggests that
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could achieve both superior scalability and superior run-time performance
compared to SA-based SR methods;
5) Our analysis also indicates that
modeling item dependencies using the SA mechanism on sparse recommendation data could yield limited information gain, and thus, might not improve the recommendation performance.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Sequential Recommendation

Numerous SR methods have been developed,
particularly using Markov Chains (MCs)
and neural networks.
Specifically, Rendle
et al
.
(
Rendle et al. 2010
)
developed
\mathop{\mathtt{FPMC}}\limits
, a method
in which MCs are utilized to model the transitions among items.
In recent years, neural networks
such as RNNs
and convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
have been widely adapted for SR.
For instance,
Li
et al
.
(
Li et al. 2017
)
developed
\mathop{\mathtt{NARM}}\limits
, which
incorporates attention mechanisms into RNNs
to better capture users’ long-term preferences.
Tang
et al
.
(
Tang and Wang 2018
)
developed a CNNs-based model
\mathop{\mathtt{Caser}}\limits
that employs vertical and horizontal convolutional filters to capture
the synergies among items for better recommendation.
Besides RNNs and CNNs-based methods,
SA-based methods are also widely developed
for SR.
Kang
et al
.
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
developed a SA-based method
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
,
which stacks multiple SA blocks
to recursively learn users’ preferences.
Sun
et al
.
(
Sun et al. 2019
)
integrated the cloze objective and SA mechanisms, and developed a bidirectional sequence modeling method, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{Bert4Rec}}\limits
, for comprehensive user intent modeling.
Zhou
et al
.
(
Zhou et al. 2022
)
developed
\mathop{\mathtt{FMLP}}\limits
for SR, in which they replaced the attention map within each SA block with a predefined transformation matrix for more efficient item aggregation.
He
et al
.
(
He et al. 2021
)
constrained SA blocks to attend to local items, thereby enhancing the modeling of users’ short-term preferences.
Recently, simple shallow methods
have been introduced for SR, and shown promising performance.
For example, Ma
et al
.
(
Ma et al. 2019
)
developed
\mathop{\mathtt{HGN}}\limits
,
which uses a single gating layer to adaptively aggregate items and capture users’ preferences.
Peng
et al
.
(
Peng et al. 2021
)
developed
\mathop{\mathtt{HAM}}\limits
in which a single pooling layer is
employed to learn the
associations among items.

We notice that

\mathop{\mathtt{SGNN\text{-}HN}}\limits
developed in Pan
et al
.
(
Pan et al. 2020
)
also leverages star graphs for SR.
However,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
differs significantly from
\mathop{\mathtt{SGNN\text{-}HN}}\limits
.
Specifically,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
is developed to address the over-smoothing issue in SA-based SR methods.
To this end,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
utilizes the internal node to aggregate information from individual items,
and does not allow information propagation among items.
In contrast,
\mathop{\mathtt{SGNN\text{-}HN}}\limits
aims to improve graph neural networks (GNNs) in capturing long-range dependencies in sequences, and leverages the internal
node as an anchor node to better propagate information among items.

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{SGNN\text{-}HN}}\limits
are developed to address
different issues,
and have substantially different architectures.
Thus,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
is not an extension of
\mathop{\mathtt{SGNN\text{-}HN}}\limits
.

2.2.
Over-smoothing

The over-smoothing issue was firstly
identified in GNNs
(
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
by Li
et al
.
(
Li et al. 2018
)
.
They observed that as GNNs propagate and mix information
between neighboring nodes across layers,
all nodes could eventually have identical embeddings.
As a result, GNNs lose the information on individual nodes,
which could substantially deteriorate the model expressiveness
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
.
Recently,
Shi
et al
.
(
Shi et al. 2022
)
has proven that theoretically,
SA-based methods could also suffer from over-smoothing and
result in sub-optimal performance.
Numerous approaches have been introduced
to mitigate this issue.
For example,
Chen
et al
.
(
Chen et al. 2020
)

optimized the graph topology based on the model prediction to mitigate over-smoothing in GNNs.
Shi
et al
.
(
Shi et al. 2022
)
developed hierarchical fusion strategies (
\mathop{\mathtt{ConcatFusion}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{MaxFusion}}\limits
) to alleviate over-smoothing by fusing embeddings from both
shallow layers and deep layers as
final output.

4.1.
Sequence Representation

Following
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
, we focus on the most recent
n
items in users’ interaction histories to generate recommendations.
Specifically, we transform each interaction sequence
S
to a fixed-length sequence
B
=
\{v_{b_{1}},v_{b_{2}}\dots\}
, which contains the last
n
items in
S
(i.e.,
v_{b_{t}}
=
v_{s_{|S_{i}|-n+t}}
).
If
|S|
is shorter than
n
, we pad empty items, denoted as
v_{0}
,
at the beginning of
B
.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
, following the literature
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
, we represent items and positions in each sequence using learnable embeddings.
We learn an item embedding matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{V}|\times d}
in which the
j
-th row
\mathbf{v}_{j}

is the embedding of item
v_{j}
,
and
d
is the dimension of embeddings.
We use a constant zero vector as the embedding of padding items.
Similarly, we learn a position embedding matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, and
\mathbf{p}_{t}
is the embedding of the
t
-th position.
Given
V
and
P
, we represent the sequence
B
using a matrix
E
:

(1)

E=[\mathbf{e}_{1};\mathbf{e}_{2};\cdots;\mathbf{e}_{n}]=[\mathbf{v}_{b_{1}}+\mathbf{p}_{1};\mathbf{v}_{b_{2}}+\mathbf{p}_{2};\cdots;\mathbf{v}_{b_{n}}+\mathbf{p}_{n}],

where
\mathbf{v}_{b_{t}}
is the embedding of
v_{b_{t}}
.

4.2.
Modeling Sequences as Star Graphs

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
employs a multi-block architecture
to recursively model
users’ intent.
Distinct from existing SA-based SR methods
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
;
Fan et al. 2022
)
,
in each block,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
treats each item within
B

as a node in a star graph,
and introduces an additional internal node to
estimate the user’s intent by aggregating information from all item nodes using multi-head attention
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
.
Across blocks,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
updates only the embedding of the internal node to enhance the estimation of the user’s intent,
while maintaining fixed embeddings on item nodes to
preserve their individual information.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
, we have
n_{b}
blocks,
and each block contains an attention layer
and a feed-forward layer.
In what follows, we present the
attention layer and the feed-forward layer in each block in detail.

4.2.1.
Attention layer

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
employs multi-head attention to adaptively aggregate information from item nodes
to the central internal node in each block as follows:

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}_{k}&=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\left(\mathbf{c}^{m-1}Q^{m}_{k}\right)\left(EZ^{m}_{k}\right)^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),\quad\mathbf{head}^{m}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}_{k}(EA^{m}_{k}),\\
\mathbf{g}^{m}&=[\mathbf{head}^{m}_{1},\mathbf{head}^{m}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{head}^{m}_{n_{h}}]O^{m},\\
\end{aligned}

where
\mathbf{c}^{m-1}
is the embedding of the internal node from the
(m\text{-}1)
-th block, and

\mathbf{g}^{m}
is the output of the attention layer in the
m
-th block.
Each attention layer has
n_{h}
attention heads and
\mathbf{head}^{m}_{k}
is the output from the
k
-th attention head.

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}_{k}
is the attention weights learned in the
k
-th attention head.

Q^{m}_{k},Z^{m}_{k}\text{and }A^{m}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\frac{d}{n_{h}}}
are learnable parameters in the
k
-th attention head.

O^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a learnable parameter to integrate the attention heads.

Unlike SA-based methods, which require every item to aggregate information from all others to capture global information within the sequence,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
introduces an additional internal node to specifically capture the global information and estimate the user’s intent.
This approach results in a linear time complexity as will be shown in Section
4.5
, while could still effectively capture long-range dependencies within the sequence.

4.2.2.
Feed-forward layer

After each attention layer,
we include a feed-forward layer
to endow
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
with non-linearity,
and enable more expressive models
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
.
Particularly, given
\mathbf{g}^{m}
,
we stack two fully-connected networks (FCNs) as the feed-forward layer:

(3)

\mathbf{c}^{m}=(f(\mathbf{g}^{m}W^{m}_{1}+\mathbf{b}^{m}_{1}))W^{m}_{2}+\mathbf{b}^{m}_{2},

where
f(\cdot)
is an activation function such as ReLU
(
Nair and Hinton 2010
)
and GELU
(
Hendrycks and Gimpel 2016
)
,

W^{m}_{1}
,
W^{m}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbf{b}^{m}_{1}
,
\mathbf{b}^{m}_{2}
are learnable parameters.
It is worth noting that different from SA-based methods,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
updates only the embedding of the internal node across blocks,
while fixing the item node embeddings.
As a result,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could preserve the information on individual items and will not be affected by the over-smoothing issue, even when learning deep models (Section
6.3
).
Following
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits

(
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
,
we connect each layer with its previous layer using residual connections
(
He et al. 2016
)

to mitigate the degradation issue
(
Roy et al. 2018
)

during training.
Recent studies
(
Peng et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2018
)
show that the most recent interacted item could be a strong indicator of the next item of users’ interest.
Thus, for each sequence,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
employs
\mathbf{e}_{n}
,
the embedding of the last item in
E
(Equation
1
),
as the initial embedding of
the internal node in the first block
(i.e.,
\mathbf{c}^{0}
=
\mathbf{e}_{n}
).

4.3.
Comprehensive User Intent Modeling

As demonstrated in the literature
(
Peng et al. 2021
;
Ma et al. 2019
)
, both users’
short-term preferences and long-term preferences play important roles in generating accurate recommendations.
By considering
\mathbf{e}_{n}
,
the embedding of the most recent interacted item,
as the initial embedding of the internal node,
the internal node embedding should be able to
effectively capture the user’s short-term preferences across blocks.
However, it may not also fully capture the user’s
long-term preferences as the long-term preferences of the user could be different from her short-term preferences.
Thus, to enable comprehensive user intent modeling,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
learns user embeddings to specifically capture users’ long-term preferences.
In particular,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
learns an embedding matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{U}|\times d}
to represent the long-term preferences of all the users.
The
i
-th row
\mathbf{u}_{i}
in
U

represents the long-term preferences of user
u_{i}
.

4.4.
Recommendation Scores and Network Training

To generate recommendation scores for user
u_{i}
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
integrates the embedding of the internal node from the last block (i.e.,
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{n_{b}}
)
and the learned user embedding
\mathbf{u}_{i}
, as follows:

(4)

\hat{r}_{ij}=(\mathbf{c}^{n_{b}}_{i}+\mathbf{u}_{i})\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top},

where
\hat{r}_{ij}
is the recommendation score of user
u_{i}
on item
v_{j}
,
and
n_{b}
is the total number of blocks in
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
.
For each user, we recommend items with the top-
k
highest recommendation scores.
Following
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
,
we employ the binary cross-entropy loss
to minimize the negative log-likelihood of correctly recommending the ground truth next item as follows:

(5)

\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}-\sum\nolimits_{S_{i}\in\mathbb{T},v_{j}\not\in S_{i}}\left[\log(\sigma(\hat{r}_{ig}))+\log(1-\sigma(\hat{r}_{ij}))\right],

where
\mathbb{T}
is the set of all the training sequences;

\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is the set of learnable parameters (e.g.,
V
and
P
); and

\hat{r}_{ig}
is the recommendation
score of user
u_{i}
on her ground-truth next item

v_{g}(i)
.
For each training sequence, we randomly sample one negative item
v_{j}
for optimization.
All the learnable parameters are randomly initialized,
and are optimized in an end-to-end manner.

4.5.
Complexity Analysis

Previous work
(
Tay et al. 2020
;
wang2020linformer
;
katharopoulos2020transformers
)
shows that
the time complexity of each SA block is
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}d+nd^{2})
, where
n
is the length of the transformed sequence (Equation
1
)
and
d
is the dimension of embeddings.
The quadratic time complexity limits the utility of SA-based methods in latency-sensitive recommendation applications.
In contrast,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
models sequences as star graphs and could achieve
a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(nd^{2})
,
which is linear with respect to the sequence length
n
.
This allows
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
to be theoretically
more efficient than SA-based methods.
Particularly, each SA block requires
6nd^{2}+2n^{2}d+2nd
operations to compute,
whereas each block in
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
requires only
2nd^{2}+4d^{2}+2nd+2d
operations.
The difference amounts to
4d^{2}(n-1)+2d(n^{2}-1)
,
which is quadratic with respect to both
d
and
n
.
As will be shown in Section
6.5
,
the better time complexity could translate into
superior run-time performance of
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
over SA-based methods on modern GPUs.

\mathop{\mathtt{HGN}}\limits

(
Ma et al. 2019
)
.
We compare
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
with three state-of-the-art SA-based methods
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits

(
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
,
\mathop{\mathtt{Bert4Rec}}\limits

(
Sun et al. 2019
)

\mathop{\mathtt{FMLP}}\limits

We further compare
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
equipped with two hierarchical fusion strategies: 1)
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{ConcatFusion}}\limits
(
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec\text{-}Cat}}\limits

and 2)
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits

et al

to alleviate over-smoothing. Note that, we do not consider baseline methods that the implementation is not publicly available (e.g.,
\mathop{\mathtt{SGNN\text{-}HN}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{HGN}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
have been compared with
a comprehensive
set of other methods including
\mathop{\mathtt{GRU4REC}}\limits

Hidasi and Karatzoglou 2018

\mathop{\mathtt{NextItRec}}\limits

Yuan et al. 2019

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{HGN}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits

To ensure a fair comparison, we exhaustively tune the hyper-parameters of all the baseline methods.
Thus, the performance of baseline methods reported in our experiments is generally better than that reported in the literature
(
Ma et al. 2019

Rajput et al. 2023
)
.
For example, on the
\mathop{\texttt{Beauty}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
reported in our experiments is 20.3% higher than that reported in a recent work
(

)
.
We report the implementation detail of
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and baseline methods, and the search range for each hyper-parameter in Appendix

5.3.

5.3.1.
Training, validation and testing sets

Following
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits

6.1.1.
Comparing
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
to
\mathop{\mathtt{FPMC}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{Caser}}\limits

As presented in Table
1
,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
outperforms the MC-based method

\mathop{\mathtt{FPMC}}\limits
and CNNs-based method
\mathop{\mathtt{Caser}}\limits
by a significant margin
on all the six benchmark datasets.

\mathop{\mathtt{FPMC}}\limits
views the transitions among items as an MC and leverages only the most recent interacted items to predict the next item of users’ interest.
Consequently,
\mathop{\mathtt{FPMC}}\limits
may not be able to fully utilize the information in early interacted items, and result in sub-optimal performance.

\mathop{\mathtt{Caser}}\limits
employs CNNs to integrate items within sequences.
Though CNNs have been shown effective in capturing local structures, they may struggle to capture global information within sequences
(
Yamashita et al. 2018
)
.
In contrast, by modeling sequences as star graphs,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could effectively aggregate information from all items to capture the global information,
and thus, enable superior recommendation performance over
\mathop{\mathtt{FPMC}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{Caser}}\limits
.

6.1.2.
Comparison between
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{NARM}}\limits

Table
1
shows that compared to the RNNs-based method
\mathop{\mathtt{NARM}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
demonstrates superior performance on all the six datasets at all the evaluation metrics.

\mathop{\mathtt{NARM}}\limits
primarily uses RNNs to recurrently learn users’ preferences.
As demonstrated in the literature
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
, due to the recurrent nature, RNNs may struggle to
model long-range dependencies within sequences,
which could limit their ability
to capture global information.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
, as illustrated in Figure
1(c)
,
the central internal node could
aggregate information from every item node within one step.
Thus, compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{NARM}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could better capture long-range dependencies within sequences,
and achieve substantial performance improvement on all the datasets.

6.1.3.
Comparison between
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{HAM}}\limits

Table
1
also presents that

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
significantly outperforms the best-performing shallow method
\mathop{\mathtt{HAM}}\limits
on all the six datasets.
Compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{HAM}}\limits
,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
achieves a remarkable average improvement of 7.8% and 6.0%
at Recall@
10
and NDCG@
10
, respectively, across the six datasets.

\mathop{\mathtt{HAM}}\limits
employs the simple mean pooling to aggregate
items and estimate users’ intent.
Though efficient, this simple approach may not be
sufficiently expressive to accurately estimate users’ intent
from their diverse interactions.
Different from
\mathop{\mathtt{HAM}}\limits
,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
stacks multiple non-linear blocks to enable expressive models, which leads to a more accurate user intent modeling as compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{HAM}}\limits
.

6.1.4.
Comparison between
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits

Table
1

presents that both
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG\text{-}u}}\limits
substantially outperform the best-performing SA-based method
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
.
Similarly to
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
stacks multiple SA blocks to recursively
capture users’ intent, and has been demonstrated
state-of-the-art performance
(
Peng et al. 2021
;
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
in SR.
However, as shown in Table
1
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
consistently outperforms
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
on five out of six datasets (i.e.,
\mathop{\texttt{Beauty}}\limits
,
\mathop{\texttt{Toys}}\limits
,
\mathop{\texttt{Children}}\limits
,
\mathop{\texttt{Comics}}\limits
and
\mathop{\texttt{ML-1M}}\limits
).
Particularly, compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
, in terms of Recall@
10
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
achieves a significant average improvement of 14.2%
over the five datasets.
In terms of NDCG@
10
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
also outperforms
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
with a significant average improvement of 15.1%
over the five datasets.
Similarly,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG\text{-}u}}\limits
also remarkably outperforms
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
across the six datasets with an average improvement of 7.0% and 7.2% at Recall@
10
and NDCG@
10
, respectively.
From a graph perspective,
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
models sequences as fully connected graphs, which allow each item to aggregate information from other items within the sequence.
Though promising, this design results in quadratic time complexities and leads to the over-smoothing issue (Section
6.3
).
As shown in the literature
(
Cai and Wang 2020
;
Yang et al. 2020
)
, this issue could substantially limit the model scalability and degrade the recommendation performance.
In contrast,
by modeling sequences as star graphs,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could fundamentally address the over-smoothing issue while still being able to capture the long-range dependencies within sequences.
Consequently, as presented in Table
1
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could enable significant improvement over
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
on different recommendation scenarios.

6.1.5.
Comparison between
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec\text{-}Max}}\limits

Table
1
also shows that overall, both
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG\text{-}u}}\limits
achieve superior performance over
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec\text{-}Max}}\limits
on the six datasets.
For example, at Recall@10,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG\text{-}u}}\limits
achieves an average improvement of 11.4% and 7.7%, respectively, over the six datasets compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec\text{-}Max}}\limits
.

\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec\text{-}Max}}\limits
mitigates over-smoothing by fusing embeddings from both shallow layers and deep layers
using max pooling.
In contrast,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
fundamentally addresses
over-smoothing by modeling sequences using star graphs, thereby avoiding information propagation among items.
The substantial improvement of
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
over
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec\text{-}Max}}\limits
shows that our approach is more effective than hierarchical fusion
(
Shi et al. 2022
)
in both addressing over-smoothing, and improving recommendation performance.

6.3.
Analysis on Over-smoothing

We analyze if the state-of-the-art SA-based SR method
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
suffers from the over-smoothing issue.
Specifically, for each dataset,
we train a
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
model consisting of six SA blocks.
Apart from the number of blocks,

,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
on benchmark datasets as shown in Table

.

We conduct an analysis to investigate the information gain derived from the attention weights learned in
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
.
Particularly,
we measure the information gain by comparing the average entropy from the attention weight distributions and that from uniform distributions (i.e., weigh items equally).
We present more details on the calculation of the average entropy in Appendix
D.4
.
A higher difference between the average entropies
indicates a larger information gain derived from the learned attention weights.
Figure

shows the average entropy from attention weight distributions and uniform distributions on
\mathop{\texttt{Beauty}}\limits
,
\mathop{\texttt{Toys}}\limits
and
\mathop{\texttt{Children}}\limits
.
Following
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
, we focus on the attention weights learned in the first SA block in this analysis.

As shown in Figure

on all the three datasets,
the difference between the average entropy
from attention weight distributions

indicating that
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits

An important difference between
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
is that

\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
captures the dependencies in each pair of items by learning attention weights, while
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
does not explicitly model these dependencies.
As shown in Figure

(
Peng et al. 2021
)
, limited information gain could be achieved by capturing the dependency in each item pair.
As a result, as shown in Table

and Table

, without explicitly modeling dependencies in item pairs,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG\text{-}u}}\limits
could still achieve significant improvement over
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
on benchmark datasets.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper,
we identify the over-smoothing issue
in SA-based SR methods.
To address this issue,
we model sequences using star graphs and develop
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
for SR.
Different from SA-based methods in which each item could aggregate information from all others,

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
introduces an internal node for information aggregation
and does not propagate information among item nodes.
Consequently,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could fundamentally address the over-smoothing issue and achieve
a linear time complexity
with respect to the sequence length.
We extensively evaluate
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
against
ten state-of-the-art baseline methods on six benchmark datasets.
Our experimental results show that overall,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
outperforms baseline methods on all the datasets except for
\mathop{\texttt{ML-20M}}\limits
, with an improvement of up to 10.10%.
On
\mathop{\texttt{ML-20M}}\limits
, a variant of
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could still significantly outperform all the baseline methods.
Our analysis shows that
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
achieves superior scalability
with respect to both the number of blocks
and the embedding dimensions
compared to the state-of-the-art
SA-based SR method
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
.
This improved scalability could lead to enhanced recommendation performance in benchmark datasets.
In addition, our complexity analysis and run-time performance comparison together demonstrate that

\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
is both theoretically and practically more efficient than
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
.
Thus,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could be particularly
desirable in applications with limited
computing resources.
Our analysis also suggests that
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
achieves limited information gain by explicitly modeling dependencies between items using the SA mechanism.
Therefore, without modeling the dependencies,
\mathop{\mathtt{MSSG}}\limits
could still outperform
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
in benchmark datasets.

\mathop{\texttt{ML-1M}}\limits
,

D.2.
Calculating Average Similarities Between Item embeddings

Given the transformed sequence of user
u_{i}
:

B_{i}
=
\{v_{b_{1}}(i),v_{b_{2}}(i),\dots\}
(Section
4.1
),
we calculate the average similarity of the embeddings of items within a sequence for each SA block as follows:

(6)

a_{i}^{m}=\frac{1}{|B_{i}|^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{|B_{i}|}\sum_{k=1}^{|B_{i}|}\cos(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{m},\mathbf{e}_{k}^{m}),

where
|B_{i}|
is the length of
B_{i}
;

\mathbf{e}_{j}^{m}
is the embedding of the
j
-th item in
B_{i}
after
the
m
-th SA block;

\cos(\cdot)
is the cosine similarity;

a_{i}^{m}
is the average similarity between the embeddings of items in
B_{i}
after the
m
-th SA block.
Note that as illustrated in Figure
1(b)
,

\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
updates the embedding of each item within the sequence in each SA block.
Thus, we expect different embeddings
for the same item in different blocks.
We eliminate
i
in
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{m}(i)
in Equation
6
for simplicity.

Given
a_{i}^{m}
, we average it over all
the users in the dataset:

(7)

a^{m}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{U}|}\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathbb{U}}a_{i}^{m},

where
a^{m}
is the average similarity over all the users and
\mathbb{U}
is the set of all the users.

D.4.
Calculating Entropy
from Attention Weight Distributions

Given the attention map
A
from the
first SA block in
\mathop{\mathtt{SASRec}}\limits
,
we calculate the average entropy of
the attention weight distributions
over all the items
as follows:

(8)

\frac{-1}{\sum\limits_{u_{i}\in\mathbb{U}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(v_{b_{j}}(i)\neq v_{0})}\sum\limits_{u_{i}\in\mathbb{U}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(v_{b_{j}}(i)\neq v_{0})\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}(A_{ijk}\log(A_{ijk})),

where
\mathbbm{1}(x)
is an indicator function (i.e.,
\mathbbm{1}(x)=1
if
x
is
true, otherwise 0);

n
is the length of the transformed sequence (Section
4.1
);

v_{b_{j}}(i)\neq v_{0}
excludes the padding items;
and
-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}(A_{ijk}\log(A_{ijk}))
is the entropy calculated from the attention weights of
v_{b_{j}}(i)
.
