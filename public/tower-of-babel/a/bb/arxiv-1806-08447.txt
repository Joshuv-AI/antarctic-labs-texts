Abstract
. We study ℝ 2 ⊕ ℝ \mathbb{R}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{R} - separately convex hulls of finite sets of points in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , as in [ 11 ] . This notion of convexity, which we call 2 + 1 2+1 convexity, corresponds to rank-one convex convexity, or quasiconvexity, when ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} is identified with certain subsets of matrices. We introduce “ 2 + 1 2+1 complexes”, which generalize T n T_{n} constructions, define the “ 2 + 1 2+1 -complex convex hull of a set”, and prove that it is an inner approximation to the 2 + 1 2+1 convex hull. We also consider outer approximations to 2 + 1 2+1 convexity based in the locality theorem of rank convexity, by iteratively chopping off “ D D -prisms”. For many finite sets, this procedure reaches a “ 2 + 1 2+1 K K -complex” in a finite number of steps, and thus computes the 2 + 1 2+1 convex hull. We show examples of finite sets for which this procedure does not reach the 2 + 1 2+1 convex hull in a finite number of steps, but we show that there is always a sequence of outer approximations built with D D -prisms that converges to a 2 + 1 2+1 K K -complex. We conclude that K r ​ c K^{rc} is always a “ 2 + 1 2+1 K K -complex”, which has interesting consequences. Keywords. rank-one convex hull, 2+1 convex hull, 2+1 complex, D convex hull, quasiconvex hull, computation of D convex hulls

More often than not, rank-one convex hulls and quasiconvex hulls are estimated through inner or outer approximations.
A complete determination of the rank-one, or the quasiconvex hull, is possible if an inner approximation is shown to agree with an outer approximation.

Our approach is to start with a straightforward outer approximation

The

of a subset of

Definition 2.1

Let

•

The polyconvex
++

The determinant of the minors of the
2\times 3
matrices are polyconvex functions, which in our coordinates are the functions
xz
and

Any linear combination of them produces a polyconvex function.
In analogy with
[
21
]

[

]

[

]

Definition 3.1
.

A
shovel function
is a function

f(x,y,z)=\max\{l(x,y),0\}\cdot\max\{s(z-z_{0}),0\},

for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, an affine functional
l:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
s\in\{-1,1\}
.

A
shovel

L
is the set where a shovel function is positive:

L=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\>l(x,y)>0,s(z-z_{0})>0\}

A shovel function is a
2+1
convex function, hence its zero set is
2+1
convex, and shovels can be used to
carve

2+1
-convex sets.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a compact set
K
, its
polyconvex
++

K^{pc++}
is the complement of the union of all the shovels that do not intersect
K
.

Remark 3.3
.

Since the complement of shovels are
2+1
convex, the polyconvex
++

K^{rc}\subset K^{pc++}

Shovels are analogous to the quadrants used in section §4 of
[

]
to compute separately convex hulls in the plane.
Quadrants are zero sets of quadrant functions:

q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\max\{s_{1}(x_{1}-x^{0}_{1}),0\}\cdot\dots\cdot\max\{s_{n}(x_{n}-x^{0}_{n}),0\}

where
x^{0}=(x^{0}_{1},\dots,x^{0}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
s_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
for

.

At the end of section §4 of
[
13
]
, there is a counterexample showing that quadrants do not compute the separately convex hull of a finite set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

There is a limitation to that simple example: the separately convex hull of the six points is disconnected, and thus the separately convex hull can be computed for the subsets of points that generate each connected component alone, and then combined together to yield the whole hull using the “structure theorem” of rank one convex hulls: if
K^{rc}\subset U_{1}\cup U_{2}
for disjoint open sets

]

]

The quadrants are indeed enough to compute the separately convex hull of each subset of points.

However, in
[
13
]
there is also the
20

K^{rc}
, even if combined with the structure theorem.

We will see in section

4.
D
-prisms

The result that makes our outer approximations work is the following well-known theorem

Theorem 4.1

(Thm. 4.7 from
[
12
]
)
.

Let
B
be bounded and
K
compact in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

K^{rc}\cap B=\left[(B\cap K)\cup(\partial B\cap K^{rc})\right]^{rc}\cap B

We will use this theorem to remove an extremal point from an outer approximation
M
of the set

that does not belong to
K
, and reach a smaller outer approximation.
We will use the previous theorem only when
B
is a
2+1
-prism.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
K
be a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and let

be a subset that contains
K^{rc}
.
A
2+1
-prism
for
(M,K)
is the cartesian product
B=T\times I
of an open (horizontal) triangle
T
and an open (vertical) interval

such that

•

K\cap B=\emptyset

•

M\cap\partial B
is contained in the closure of the union of at most one of the three vertical rectangles and at most one of the two horizontal triangles that appear in the boundary of
B
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
M
be an outer approximation for
K^{rc}
and
B
be a
2+1
-prism for
(M,K)
.

Then
K^{rc}\subset M\setminus B
.

Furthermore, if

is
2+1
-convex, then
M\setminus B
is also
2+1
-convex.

Proof.

It follows from theorem
4.1
and
B\cap K=\emptyset
that

K^{rc}\cap B=\left[(B\cap K)\cup(\partial B\cap K^{rc})\right]^{rc}\cap B=(\partial B\cap K^{rc})^{rc}\cap B

Since
\partial B\cap K^{rc}\subset\partial B\cap M
is contained in the union of a closed vertical rectangle and a closed horizontal triangle, which is a
2+1
-convex set, it follows that

K^{rc}\cap B=(\partial B\cap K^{rc})^{rc}\cap B\subset(\partial B\cap M)\cap B\subset\partial B\cap B

Since
B
is open and
K^{rc}\subset M
, it follows that
K^{rc}\subset M\setminus B
.

If
M
is
2+1
-convex, then
(M\setminus B)^{rc}\subset M^{rc}=M
.
Thus
M
is an outer approximation for
(M\setminus B)^{rc}
, and
B
is a
2+1
-prism for
(M,M\setminus B)
.
We have just proved that this implies that
(M\setminus B)^{rc}\subset M\setminus B
, hence
M\setminus B
is
2+1
-convex.
∎

We will iteratively extract

-prisms from an initial

that contains

.
This produces a sequence of outer approximations

that often estabilizes at a certain number

M_{n^{*}}\underbrace{\subset}_{2.6}\left(Extr(M_{n^{*}})\right)^{rc}\underbrace{\subset}_{M_{n^{*}}\text{is 2+1-$K$ complex}}K^{rc}\underbrace{\subset}_{\text{outer approximation}}M_{n^{*}}

In example

.
If a point can be removed from a compact set with a shovel, it can also be removed by a

Notation 4.4
.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the vertical projection onto the plane

, and
let
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the horizontal projection onto the line

.

Definition 4.5
.

Let

be a finite set.
The crude outer approximation

to

is:

C(K)=\operatorname{co}(\pi(K))\times\operatorname{co}(\sigma(K)),

where

is the usual convex hull.

Definition 4.6
.

The local
2+1
-prism convex hull

is the complement of the set of points that can be removed from

by a finite sequence of applications of Theorem

.

p\notin K^{\text{prism}}\Leftrightarrow\begin{array}[]{c}\exists N\in\mathbb{N},D_{1},\dots,D_{N}\\
\text{ such that }\\

\text{ and }p\in D_{N}\end{array}

Remark 4.7
.

Any outer approximation that is bounded can be used instead of

.
The usual convex hull
\operatorname{co}(K)
works too, and we just used

because it is the start point of our algorithm.

5.
A limit of outer approximations

If we apply theorem
4.3
iteratively, it is straightforward to build a sequence of outer approximations to
K^{rc}
.
Since this is a sequence of nested compact sets, it will have a limit.
However, if the outer approximations are not taken carefully, the limit set may not be
K^{rc}
.
It is easy to build examples of this, by using
2+1
-prisms that cut smaller and smaller portions of the outer approximation.

In this section, we prove that we can start with the crude outer approximation, and apply theorem
4.3
in an specific way, in order to build a sequence of outer approximations
M_{k}
that will converge to
K^{rc}
, and we will also be able to prove that the limit is a
2+1

K
-complex.
This will prove theorem
1.1
.

In parallel, we will create a sequence of finite sets
A_{k}
that are related to the outer approximations
M_{k}
through the operator
\operatorname{hv}
, defined in
5.3
. We will genereate the sequence
A_{k}
by iteratively applying the operator
\operatorname{rah}_{K}
, which is defined in
5.7
.
The advantage of using the sequence
A_{k}
and the operator
\operatorname{rah}_{K}
is that is can be coded into an efficient computer program.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and let

.

The
h
-cut
of
M
,

, is the projection of
M\cap\{z=h\}
onto
\{z=0\}
.

M^{h}=\pi(M\cap\{z=h\})

Remark 5.2
.

A subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be specified by giving its
h
-cuts for all
h
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a finite set, and let
H=\{h_{1}<\dots<h_{n}\}=\sigma(A)
be the set of
z
-coordinates of all the points in
A
.

The
hv-convex hull
of
A
,
\operatorname{hv}(A)
, is the set that results from taking the convex hull only in horizontal planes, and then taking convex hulls in the vertical direction in order to fill only the intermediate heights. It can be defined by its
h
-cuts:

•

For
h_{j}\in H
:

\left(\operatorname{hv}(A)\right)^{h_{j}}=\operatorname{co}(A^{h_{j}}).

•

For
h\in(h_{j},h_{j+1})
:

\left(\operatorname{hv}(A)\right)^{h}=\operatorname{co}(A^{h_{j}})\cap\operatorname{co}(A^{h_{j+1}}).

•

For
h\not\in[h_{1},h_{n}]
,
\left(\operatorname{hv}(A)\right)^{h}
is empty.

This is not the same as taking the laminate convex hull for
\{z=0\}
and then the laminate convex hull for the
z
-axis, since for constructing
\operatorname{hv}(A)
vertical segments are only used in intermediate heights.

Remark 5.4
.

\operatorname{hv}(A)
is clearly a subset of

, the lamination convex hull obtained with only two iterations of the “add rank one connections” operation that builds

from

in definition

.

Lemma 5.5
.

\operatorname{hv}(A)
is a
2+1
-complex.

Proof.

\operatorname{hv}(A)
can be described as the union of one polygon at each height in
H
, which can be decomposed into triangles, and, for each
1\leqslant j<n
, one prism of the form
P=C\times(h_{j},h_{j+1})
for a finite 2D polygon

, and each prism

can also be decomposed into a finite list of elements.
∎

Definition 5.6
.

A
(2+1)

(or a

of finite subsets

.

The

is the product

of

of

Definition 5.7
.

Let

, and also

(

Lemma 5.9
.

Let

be a finite set such that
\operatorname{hv}(A)
is

-convex,

\operatorname{hv}\left(\operatorname{rah}_{K}(A,h_{j})\right)

Proof.

The goal is to prove that

obtained only by applications of theorem

In order to reach

We consider all prisms of the following types:

•

•

P_{down}=T\times(h_{j-1},h_{j+1}-\varepsilon)

of

\overline{q_{1}q_{3}}\cap\pi(M)=\overline{q_{1}q_{2}}\cap\pi(M)=\emptyset

p\notin\widetilde{M}_{j}\Leftrightarrow\begin{array}[]{c}\exists N\in\mathbb{N},P_{1},\dots,P_{N}\text{ such that }\\

\text{ and }p\in P_{N}\end{array}

,

is the intersection of a

1. Proof that

.

and is

.

Theorem 5.13
.

The outer sequence
\{M_{n}\}
of a finite set

2+1

Proof.

Since

Since

.

Since

from

Since

6.
Conclusions

6.1.
On
2+1
-prisms

We have chosen to use theorem
4.1
only with
2+1
-prisms, but that theorem can be used in other ways to improve an outer approximation.
If
K^{rc}\subset M
, then theorem
4.1
shows that

M^{\prime}=(M\setminus B)\cup\left(\left[(B\cap K)\cup(\partial B\cap M)\right]^{rc}\cap B\right)

is also a (hopefully smaller) outer approximation.

A natural generalization of
2+1
-prisms is to consider sets
B
such that
B\cap K=\emptyset
and
\partial B\cap M
is
2+1
convex.
This generalization can provide improvements over
2+1
-prisms, but it is not obvious how to turn this use of theorem
4.1
into an algorithm.
It is not clear whether generalization to a family with finitely many parameters can find
K^{rc}
exactly in finite time and memory.
Another approach that might work is to perform even more cuts with prisms in
each step.

On the one hand, the proof of theorem
5.13
limits the possible choices for points in each height, and hence the number of possible candidates for
K^{\text{prism}}=K^{rc}
.
However, this is not practical, and we have some empirical evidence that it will not be easy to find the finite object
K^{rc}
exactly in reasonable time and memory.
For any finite set
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
R^{\prime}
be the set of points of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that are the intersection of two segments with endpoints in
R
, and let
R^{(2)}=(R^{\prime})^{\prime}
and
R^{(n+1)}=(R^{(n)})^{\prime}
for any natural
n
.
We have found examples of finite sets
K
such that
K^{rc}
can be computed through the outer algorithm in finitely many steps, and it contains vertices whose first two coordinates lie in
R^{(n)}
for
n
up to
4

6.2.
Generalizations

The definitions
2.3
of
2+1
-complex, and
2.8
of
2+1
-complex convex hull, and also lemma
2.9
are easy to generalize to arbitrary wave cones
D
.
Definition
4.6
can be extended to arbitrary wave cones
D
in this way:
The local
D
-ball convex hull
K^{D-\text{ball}}
is the complement of the set of points that can be removed from
\operatorname{co}(K)
by a finite sequence of applications of Theorem
4.1
.
Another natural idea is to use only sets
B
such that
B\cap K=\emptyset
and
\partial B\cap M
is
D
convex.
Although this definition is unsuitable for computation, it might work for theoretical applications.

It does not seem difficult to prove that the
D
extremal points of
K^{D-\text{ball}}
would belong to
K
, but that knowledge would not be of much use.
The following, and more interesting conjectures do not seem at all trivial, and are possibly not true in their full generality:

(1)

For any wave cone
D
there is a family of semialgebraic sets (called
D
-prisms), parameterized by finitely many real numbers, such that
K^{D-\text{ball}}
is also the complement of the set of points that can be removed from
\operatorname{co}(K)
by a finite sequence of applications of an adequate generalization of theorem
4.3
(or a restriction of theorem
4.1
, as you wish to look at it).

(2)

K^{D-\text{ball}}=K^{rc}
.

(3)

K^{D-\text{ball}}
is a finite
D
-complex (defined as a semi-algebraic set that is foliated by lines of
D
).

(4)

For any wave cone
D
and finite set
K
there is a scaffolding
\widetilde{K}
of finite order
n
:
K^{rc}=\widetilde{K}^{lc,n}
.

The first of the above conjectures alone would give rise to an algorithm that could approximate
K^{rc}
from outside.
