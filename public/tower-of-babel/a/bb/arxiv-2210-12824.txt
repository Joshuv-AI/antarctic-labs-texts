Abstract
. Given two automorphisms of a group G G , one is interested in knowing whether they are conjugate in the automorphism group of G G , or in the abstract commensurator of G G , and how these two properties may differ. When G G is the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface, we present a uniform finiteness theorem for the class of pseudo-Anosov automorphisms. We present an explicit example of a commensurably conjugate pair of pseudo-Anosov automorphisms of a genus 3 3 surface, that are not conjugate in the mapping class group, and we also show that infinitely many pairwise non-commuting pseud-Anosov automorphisms have class number equal to one. In the appendix, we briefly survey the Latimer-MacDuffee theorem that addresses the case of automorphisms of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} , with a point of view that is suited to an analogy with surface group automorphisms.



1.
Introduction: commensurated conjugacy

When
G
is a group (or any structure), a natural problem is to classify the conjugacy classes in its automorphism group
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
. A familiar example is
G=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, for which
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}\simeq\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.

The
abstract commensurator

{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G\right)}
of
G
is the group of equivalence classes of isomorphisms between two finite index subgroups of
G
, where two such automorphisms
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
are declared to be equivalent if they agree on further finite index subgroups.

There is a natural homomorphism from
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
to
{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G\right)}
. It need not be injective, but in many cases of interest it is. In general,
{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G\right)}
is much larger than the image of
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
. In our familiar example,
{\mathrm{Comm}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right)}\simeq\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
. Of course, with linear algebra, conjugation in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
is more easily understood than in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. (See
[
27
]
for instance for more
details on commensurators in solvable Lie groups.)

For a group
G
, and
\phi\in{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
, let
[\phi]
denote the image of
\phi
in
{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G\right)}
. We define the
commensurated-conjugacy class
of
\phi

to be the set of automorphisms of
G
that are conjugate to
[\phi]
in
{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G\right)}
, and we say that these automorphisms are commensurably conjugate to
\phi
. Any commensurated-conjugacy class is a union of
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
-conjugacy classes.

Questions
.

When are the commensurated-conjugacy classes strictly larger than
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
-conjugacy classes? When do they consist of finitely many
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
-conjugacy classes?

We call the number of
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
-conjugacy classes in the commensurated-conjugacy class of
\phi
, the
class number
of
\phi
. This terminology is suggested and supported by our familiar example, as we explain now.

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, so that
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G\right)}=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
. Choose
\phi\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
such that its characteristic polynomial
\chi\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
. All
\psi\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with the same characteristic polynomial as
\phi
are conjugate to
\phi
in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, hence in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
as well. Thus, they are commensurably conjugate. The Latimer-MacDuffee theorem
[
17
,
28
]
states that the
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-conjugacy classes of such elements are in correspondence with the ideal classes of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[X]/(\chi)
. Their cardinality, which is the order of the ideal class monoid of the ring, is called the
class number
of the ring. In other words, the class number of
\phi
in our sense, is the class number of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[X]/(\chi)
.
It is finite for all
\phi\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, larger than the class number of the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}[X]/(\chi)}
, and it is greater than
2
if and only if the ring is not a principal ideal domain.

It is worth mentioning that finiteness of class numbers is not true in general. A countable group in which the class number of an automorphism is not finite is the infinite direct product of copies of
\mathbb{Z}/3
: the automorphisms that are identity on finitely many copies, and flips on all the other copies, are all commensurably equal, but not conjugate.

We turn our attention to non-abelian counterparts of the groups
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. There are two celebrated and important such classes: finite rank non-abelian free groups, and fundamental groups of surfaces of higher genus. In this work we will consider the latter.

Consider a closed orientable surface
\Sigma
of genus
g\geq 2
. The extended mapping class group of
\Sigma
is the homeomorphism group of
\Sigma
quotiented by its component of identity
\Homeo(\Sigma)/\Homeo_{0}(\Sigma)
.
By the Dehn-Nielsen-Baer theorem

[
11
, Chapter 8]
, it is isomorphic to the outer automorphism group of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
. However in order to make sense of commensuration, one must work at the level of automorphisms rather than outer automorphisms – in particular one needs a base point
p
in
\Sigma
. We have:

{\mathrm{Aut}\left(\pi_{1}(\Sigma,p)\right)}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathrm{Out}\left(\pi_{1}(\Sigma)\right)}\xrightarrow{\sim}MCG^{*}(\Sigma)

The Nielsen-Thurston classification of mapping classes of
\Sigma
distinguishes finite order mapping classes, reducible mapping classes, and pseudo-Anosov mapping classes.
It is appropriate to regard pseudo-Anosov mapping classes as the correct placeholders for truly irreducible mapping classes.

Definition 1.1
.

We will say that an automorphism is a
pseudo-Anosov
if its image in the mapping class group under the above surjective homomorphism is a pseudo-Anosov mapping class.

We establish the following uniform finiteness for class numbers of pseudo-Anosov automorphisms.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\phi
is a pseudo-Anosov automorphism of the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface of genus
g\geq 2
, its class number is finite, bounded above by
((168(g-1))!)^{2g}
.

We think that it is striking that our bound does not depend on
\phi
. For comparison, in the case of
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right)}\simeq\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, by
[
17
,
28
]
, one encounters class numbers of certain rings of integers of real quadratic fields, which are conjectured to have interesting (but elusive) behavior: Gauss’ class number problem conjectures that infinitely many such class numbers are
1
, but these numbers are unbounded when the field extension varies among real quadratic extensions, as proved by Montgomery and Weinberger
[
22
]
and quantitatively suggested by Cohen and Lenstra’s heuristics
[
9
]
, and its revision by Bartel and Lenstra
[
3
]
.
We explain in the appendix that the class numbers of elements of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
are indeed finite but unbounded, an argument explained to us by Sara Checcoli.

Finiteness of the class number of a pseudo-Anosov is not a surprise. Here is a simple argument. Given
\phi
a pseudo-Anosov automorphism on a surface
\Sigma
, the stretch factor, or entropy,
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\log|\phi^{n}(\gamma)|/n
does not depend on
\gamma\neq 1
in the fundamental group, nor on the metric up to quasi-isometry. Hence it is an invariant of the commensurated-conjugacy class of
\phi
. It also equals the translation length of
\phi
in the Teichmüller space of the associated surface, and there are finitely many conjugacy classes of a pseudo-Anosov that can have such translation length. (See
[
10
]
for much
finer results on counting closed geodesics in moduli space.) Hence there can only be finitely many conjugacy classes of
\phi
in the commensurated-conjugacy class of
\phi
. This argument does not, however, provide any uniform bound.

Our upper bound is very likely non-optimal. It seems that an optimal bound would be given in terms of the value of a subgroup-growth function of certain orbifolds. These are difficult to estimate sharply, see
[
19
]
.

Examples of commensurably conjugate automorphisms that are not conjugate are not immediate. Our result and methods however suggest a ‘recipe’ for producing examples of non-conjugate pseudo-Anosov automorphisms that are commensurably conjugate.
We look at the induced action on the abelianization: elements that are already conjugate in
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
would necessarily be conjugate in
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G/[G,G]\right)}
, and in the latter group, linear algebra tools may be applied to disprove the existence of such a conjugacy.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed orientable surface of genus
3
, with a base point
p
, and
G=\pi_{1}(\Sigma,p)
. There exist
\phi
and
\psi
pseudo-Anosov automorphisms of
G
that are commensurably conjugate but whose images in
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G/[G,G]\right)}
are not commensurably conjugate. In particular
\phi
and
\psi
are not conjugate in
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
.

Gauss famously conjectures (in his ‘class number problem’) that infinitely many real quadratic extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
have class number equal to one
[
26
]
.
We observe that in our non-abelian analogue, the corresponding question can be settled.

Theorem 1.4
.

There are infinitely many commensurated classes of automorphisms of hyperbolic
2
-orbifolds, that have class number one.

Summary of the paper.

After introducing the mapping tori involved and their relation to commensurated conjugacy (in Section 2), we discuss in Section 3 their representations as lattices in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. We show that, for different commensurably conjugate automorphisms, the groups of the mapping tori have representations that commensurate the image of the surface group in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. We then use a result of Leininger, Long and Reid
[
18
]
on the discreteness of the commensurator in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
of such an image, to obtain that all such representations land in a single lattice, and we obtain Proposition
3.6
. After finishing this work, we learned of the related, but more restrictive notion of fibered commensurability investigated by Calegari, Sun and Wang
[
7
, Section 2]
. We then prove our main theorem at the end of Section 3. In Section 4 we provide an explicit example, and a recipe for general examples. In Section 5 we address the class number one situation, proving Theorem
1.4
. We thought it worthwhile to attach an appendix, in which the algebraic number theory relevant to the situation of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is briefly presented, in a way that allows for an analogy with geometry and the case of surface group automorphisms. We briefly survey orders and ideal classes, provide an argument, explained to us by Sara Checcoli, as to why the class numbers of matrices in
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
are unbounded. We also explain a variant of Latimer-MacDuffee’s proof that allows for a geometric perspective on the situation, and is suited to an analogy with surface group automorphisms. Finally, we explain this analogy and its limitations.

2.
Fundamental groups of mapping tori

The aim of this section is to prove Lemma
2.3
which converts the problem of
understanding commensurated-conjugacy classes of automorphisms of a group
G
to commensurability of the associated semi-direct product by
\mathbb{Z}
. The one extra criterion that is vital in Lemma
2.3
is that the commensuration of the
associated semi-direct product also commensurates
G
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\phi,\psi
are automorphisms of
G
in the same commensurated-conjugacy class, there exists
H,H^{\prime}
of finite index in
G
,
and an isomorphism
\alpha:H\to H^{\prime}
such that,
\psi(H)=H,\phi(H^{\prime})=H^{\prime}
,
\psi|_{H}=\alpha^{-1}\circ\phi\circ\alpha|_{H}
, where
|_{H}
denotes restriction to
H
.

Proof.

Let
[\alpha]\in{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G\right)}
such that in

{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G\right)}
,

[\psi]={[\alpha]}^{-1} [\phi] [\alpha]
.
Realize
[\alpha]
by an isomorphism
\alpha:T_{1}\to T_{2}
for
T_{1},T_{2}
finite index subgroups of
G
.

Since
[\psi]=[\alpha]^{-1} [\phi] [\alpha]
, there is a further finite index subgroup
Y_{1}
of
T_{1}
on which
\alpha^{-1}\circ\phi\circ\alpha=\psi
. Let
Y_{2}=\alpha(Y_{1})
. Observe that
\psi(Y_{1})
must be in
T_{1}
but is perhaps not
Y_{1}
.

Let
H
denote the intersection of all subgroups in the
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
-orbit of
Y_{1}
, and
H^{\prime}
its image under
\alpha
.
Then
H,H^{\prime}
are also of finite index. By construction,
\psi
preserves
H
(as well as every automorphism). We still have
\alpha^{-1}\circ\phi\circ\alpha=\psi
after to restriction
H
. It follows that
\phi(H^{\prime})=H^{\prime}
.

∎

Given
\phi\in{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
, set
\langle t_{\phi}\rangle
to be an abstract infinite cyclic group, and consider
\Gamma_{\phi}=G\rtimes_{\phi}\langle t_{\phi}\rangle
. In this semi-direct product, we say that
G
is the
fiber
.

Remark 2.2
.

This terminology is inspired by the motivating example in this
paper, where
G=\pi_{1}(S)
with
S
a closed surface. Suppose
that
\Phi:S\to S
is a homeomorphism, so that
\phi
equals the automorphism of
G
induced by
\Phi
after choosing a base-point appropriately. Let
M
denote the 3-manifold given
by the
mapping torus
of
\Phi
.
Then
\Gamma_{\phi}
is the fundamental group of
M
. Thus, from
a topological point of view, the relevant object to be considered is the mapping torus
M
. But for the purposes of this
section, it suffices to consider only the fundamental group of
M
.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\phi,\psi
are automorphisms of
G
in the same commensurated-conjugacy class, then,

\Gamma_{\phi}
and
\Gamma_{\psi}
are commensurable by a homomorphism commensurating the fiber.

Proof.

Let
H,H^{\prime}
be the subgroups obtained in the previous Lemma. We make semi-direct products
H\rtimes_{\psi}\langle s\rangle
and
H^{\prime}\rtimes_{\phi}\langle q\rangle
. These two groups are isomorphic by an isomorphism restricting to
\alpha
on
H
and sending
s
to
q
. Moreover these two groups embed as finite index subgroups of
\Gamma_{\phi}
and
\Gamma_{\psi}
sending
s
and
q
respectively to
t_{\psi}
and
t_{\phi}
.
∎

3.
Surface groups and pseudo-Anosov automorphisms

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
. We provide a quick roadmap through the section for the benefit of the reader.
Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
allow us to upgrade the study of an abstract commensurated-conjugacy class of pseudo-anosov automorphisms to the study of hyperbolic 3-manifolds arising as mapping tori. This makes use of Lemma
2.3
. Lemma
3.3
then further upgrades the abstract commensurated-conjugacy class to the same conjugacy class in the automorphism group (and not just the outer automorphism group) of
G=\pi_{1}(S)
.

Propositions
3.5
and
3.6
ensure that the images of
G
under all
the representations into
PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
arising from Lemma
3.2
together generate
a
finite index
supergroup
F_{0}
of
G
, and hence the fundamental group of a hyperbolic 2-orbifold (Lemma
3.7
).

Corollary
3.8
, Lemma
3.9
and Corollary
3.10
furnish effective estimates on the number of subgroups of
F_{0}
of a fixed index.

These ingredients are finally assembled together to prove Theorem
1.2
at the end of the Section.

Recall that in a group
A
, an element
a
is in the
A
-commensurator of a subgroup
B
if
aBa^{-1}\cap B
has finite index in
B
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface of genus
g\geq 2
. If
\phi
and
\psi
are automorphisms of
G
in the same commensurated-conjugacy class, and if
\phi
determines a pseudo-Anosov mapping class, then
\psi
does as well.

Proof.

By Thurston’s hyperbolization of mapping tori

[
29
, Section 5]
(see case (iii) of
[
29
, Theorem 0.1]
), the group
\Gamma_{\phi}
is isomorphic to a lattice in
\PSL_{2}\mathbb{C}
, hence it is word-hyperbolic. Therefore
\Gamma_{\psi}
is as well, by the quasi-isometry induced by the commensuration. Hence
\psi
determines a pseudo-Anosov mapping class.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

There are faithful representations
\rho_{\phi}
,
\rho_{\psi}
of
\Gamma_{\phi}
and
\Gamma_{\psi}
into
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, such that for all
h\in H,\rho_{\psi}(h)=\rho_{\phi}(\alpha(h))
and
\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})=\rho_{\psi}(t_{\psi})
.

Moreover, given
\rho_{\phi}
, the representation
\rho_{\psi}
satisfying the above is unique.

Proof.

Consider the two uniform lattice representations
\rho_{\phi}
,
\rho_{\psi}
of
\Gamma_{\phi}
and
\Gamma_{\psi}
, respectively, into
\PSL_{2}\mathbb{C}
.

The images contain isomorphic uniform sublattices, images of
H\rtimes\langle s\rangle
and
H^{\prime}\rtimes\langle q\rangle
(following the notation of Lemma
2.3
). Through the isomorphism between these two groups, one may see them as lattice representations of the same group
H\rtimes\langle s\rangle
. Therefore, by Mostow rigidity
[
21
, Theorem 12.1]
, one may conjugate
\rho_{\psi}
so that for all
h\in H,\rho_{\psi}(h)=\rho_{\phi}(\alpha(h))
and also
\rho_{\psi}(q)=\rho_{\phi}(s)
.

Uniqueness follows also from Mostow rigidity
[
21
, Theorem 12.1]
: two such representations
\rho_{\psi},\rho^{\prime}_{\psi}
must be conjugate by a conjugator centralizing
\rho_{\psi}(H)
, but this latter group is Zariski dense in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, hence its centralizer is the center of
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
which is trivial.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
are both automorphisms of
G
, in the commensurated-conjugacy class of
\phi
.

If
\rho_{\psi_{1}}(G)=\rho_{\psi_{2}}(G)
, then
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
are in the same
Aut(G)
-conjugacy class.

Proof.

Since
\rho_{\psi_{1}}(t_{\psi_{1}})=\rho_{\psi_{2}}(t_{\psi_{2}})
, therefore

\rho_{\psi_{1}}^{-1}\circ\rho_{\psi_{2}}
is an automorphism of
G
that tautologically conjugates the automorphism of
G
given by
\rho_{\psi_{1}}^{-1}\circ{\rm ad}_{\rho_{\psi_{1}}(t_{\psi_{1}})}\circ\rho_{\psi_{1}}
to
\rho_{\psi_{2}}^{-1}\circ{\rm ad}_{\rho_{\psi_{1}}(t_{\psi_{1}})}\circ\rho_{\psi_{2}}
.
∎

The following lemma is not crucial, but we record it.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
are both automorphisms of
G
, in the commensurated-conjugacy class of
\phi
.
If
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
are in the same
Aut(G)
-conjugacy class of
\phi
, then
\rho_{\psi_{1}}(G)=\rho_{\psi_{2}}(G)
.

In particular, if
\psi
is conjugate to
\phi
by
\alpha\in{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G\right)}
, on the entire
G
, then
\rho_{\psi}=\rho_{\phi}\circ\alpha
on
G
, while in comparison with the previous case, the equality held only after restriction to
H
.

Proof.

The groups
\Gamma_{\psi_{1}}
and
\Gamma_{\psi_{2}}
are isomorphic by an isomorphism that sends the fiber to the fiber and
t_{\psi_{1}}
to
t_{\psi_{2}}
. Therefore, there is a representation
\rho^{\prime}_{\psi_{2}}
of
\Gamma_{\psi_{2}}
that has the same image as
\rho_{\psi_{1}}
. Further, the fibers have same image, as do
t_{\psi_{1}},t_{\psi_{2}}
. Since
\rho_{\psi_{1}}(t_{\psi_{1}})=\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})
, this representation satisfies the properties required for
\rho_{\psi_{2}}
(as defined in Lemma
3.2
). By uniqueness of
\rho_{\psi_{2}}
from Lemma
3.2
,
\rho_{\psi_{2}}=\rho^{\prime}_{\psi_{2}}
and we have the result.
∎

Let
\psi
be in the commensurated-conjugacy class of
\phi
. The representation
\rho_{\psi}
sends
G
to a subgroup of
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
that is normalized by
\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})
and intersects
\rho_{\phi}(G)
in a common finite index subgroup. After passing to a further common finite index subgroup
Y
, we may assume that
Y
is normal in the group generated by
\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)
, hence the quotient
\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\rangle/Y
is isomorphic to a quotient of the abstract free product
\rho_{\phi}(G)/Y*\rho_{\psi}(G)/Y
. In principle this could still be infinite. However, this is not the case, as we argue below in Proposition
3.5
and the first claim of
Proposition
3.6
.

Observe that the limit set of
\rho_{\phi}(G)
in the sphere at infinity is the whole sphere
\partial\mathbb{H}^{3}
, since it is a normal subgroup in a lattice. Since it has infinite index in the said lattice, it has infinite co-volume in its action on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. We may apply
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
, or equivalently
[
23
, Theorem 3.5]
, to obtain that the
(\PSL_{2}\mathbb{C})
-commensurator of
\rho_{\phi}(G)
is a uniform lattice
\Lambda_{\phi}
containing
\rho_{\phi}(\Gamma_{\phi})
.

Proposition 3.5
.

For all
\psi
in the commensurated-conjugacy class of
\phi
, the group

\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\cup\{\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})\}\rangle
is a uniform lattice that surjects onto
\mathbb{Z}
with kernel
\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\rangle
.

In particular,
\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\rangle
is virtually a hyperbolic surface group.

Proof.

Every element of
\rho_{\psi}(G)
is in the
(\PSL_{2}\mathbb{C})
-commensurator of
\rho_{\phi}(G)
since the latter has a finite index subgroup which is a normal finite index subgroup of
\rho_{\psi}(G)
. Therefore,
\rho_{\psi}(G)\subset\Lambda_{\phi}
, and
\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\cup\{\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})\}\rangle\subset\Lambda_{\phi}
.
Since it contains the uniform lattice
\rho_{\phi}(\Gamma_{\phi})
, it is also a uniform lattice.

The obvious map sending
\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})
to
1
and
\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)
to
0
extends to a homomorphism
h
to
\mathbb{Z}
.

By Selberg’s lemma, there is a torsion free finite index subgroup
F
of
\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\rangle
. Observe, though it is not directly useful, that the finite index of
F
is uniformly bounded, if Selberg’s lemma is applied to
\Lambda_{\phi}
. Considering the quotient of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
by

,
the Tameness Theorem
[
6
,
1
]
and Canary’s Covering Theorem
[

]
imply that

is a closed surface group.
Observe that the conclusion can be also drawn from Stallings’ Theorem
[
25
]
applied to a torsion-free finite index subgroup of
\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\cup\{\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})\}\rangle
, which projects onto
\mathbb{Z}
with finitely generated kernel.

It follows that
\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\rangle
is virtually the fundamental group of a hyperbolic surface. Further,
\langle\rho_{\phi}(G)\cup\rho_{\psi}(G)\rangle
is contained in a finite index supergroup
N
, the kernel of the homomorphism

h
constructed above. Hence the automorphism
{\rm ad}_{\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})}
of

extends both

\phi
and
\psi
.

∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Given
\rho_{\phi}
, there is a subgroup
F_{0}
of
\Lambda_{\phi}
that contains
\rho_{\phi}(G)
as a finite index subgroup that is normalized by
\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})
and that contains all images
\rho_{\psi}(G)
for
\psi
in the commensurated conjugacy class of
\phi
.

The subgroup
F_{0}
is virtually a hyperbolic surface group, and it contains finitely many finite index subgroups isomorphic to
G
.

This number is an upper bound for the class number of
\phi
.

Proof.

Take the collection of all
\rho_{\psi}(G)
for all possible representation
\psi
as above commensurating
\rho_{\phi}(G)
. Index them over the integers, let
G_{0}=\rho_{\phi}(G)
and for each

let
G_{i}=\langle G_{i-1}\cup\rho_{\psi_{i}}(G)\rangle
. This sequence of subgroups of
\Lambda_{\phi}
is a sequence of groups containing
\rho_{\phi}(G)
as a finite index subgroup, and normalized by
\rho_{\phi}(t_{\phi})
. The previous argument shows that, up to bounded finite index, this corresponds to a sequence of decreasing finite covers of hyperbolic surfaces, and thus must terminate, by decrease of the genus. Since the index is uniformly bounded, the sequence is stationary. Let
F_{0}
be the union of all

, thus the first part of the lemma holds. Considering Euler characteristic, all finite index subgroups of
F_{0}
that are isomorphic to

have the same index, and the second part is just counting subgroups with a given finite index. The third part is a consequence of Lemma

.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

The group
F_{0}
is a hyperbolic
2
-orbifold group.

More generally, anytime a group
F_{0}
is a finitely generated kernel of a homomorphism to
\mathbb{Z}
of a uniform lattice in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, it is a hyperbolic
2
-orbifold group.

Proof.

As already mentioned, such a group
F_{0}
contains as finite index subgroup, the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface of genus
\geq 2
.

Therefore there exists a finite index normal subgroup
K
of
F_{0}
that is a hyperbolic surface group. One has an exact sequence

1\to K\to F_{0}\to Q\to 1

where
Q
is finite. Choosing a set-theoretic section
\xi:Q\to F_{0}
, we obtain a map
Q\to{\mathrm{Aut}\left(K\right)}
by realizing each
\xi(q)
by
{\rm ad}_{\xi(q)}
. This is not necessarily a homomorphism (since
\xi(q)
could be changed by any element of
\xi(q)K
), but it descends to a homomorphism
\varphi:Q\to{\mathrm{Out}\left(K\right)}
.

We first argue that this homomorphism is injective. If

is sent to the identity, then
{\rm ad}_{\xi(q)}
equals conjugation by some

, hence

is in the centralizer of

. However, as

is Zariski dense in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
(which has trivial center), it has trivial centralizer.

By the Dehn-Nielsen-Baer theorem,
{\mathrm{Out}\left(K\right)}
is isomorphic to the extended mapping class group of the surface
\Sigma
of which

is the fundamental group. By the Nielsen realization theorem
[
14
]
, there is a hyperbolic metric on
\Sigma
, a subgroup
S
of the finite group of (metric) symmetries of
S
, an identification
K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\to}}\pi_{1}(\Sigma,v)
(for a base point

with trivial
S
-stabilizer), and an isomorphism
\varphi(Q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}S
in a way that the action of
S
by outer-automorphisms on
\pi_{1}(\Sigma,v)
is the equivariant image of the action of
\varphi(Q)
by outer-automorphisms on
K
.

Consider now the orbifold quotient
\overline{\Sigma}
of
\Sigma
under the action of
S
. We claim that its orbifold fundamental group (with base point
\bar{v}
)
is isomorphic to the extension
F_{0}
.
Indeed, it is also a group extension of the form

1\to\pi_{1}(\Sigma)\to\pi_{1}(\overline{\Sigma})\to S\to 1

with the same representation

as

for

. Since the center of

is trivial, the classification of extensions (see for instance

)
indicates that there is only one extension of

by

∎

Being commensurable with a hyperbolic surface group, the orbifold of which

is the fundamental group is an orbifold that carries a hyperbolic metric. It must be of area greater than that of the smallest orbifold (the quotient of

If

Proof.

The group

4.
Recipe for commensurably-conjugate mapping classes

In this Section we propose a recipe to construct examples, as suggested by the structure obtained in Proposition
3.6
. We will also produce an explicit example in Subsection
4.1
, thus proving Theorem
1.3
.

Start with a hyperbolic surface, or
2
-orbifold
\Sigma
with a base point
v
. Consider a single pseudo-Anosov mapping class
[\Phi]
, and realize it as an automorphism
\Phi
of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma,v)
.

Consider two finite sheeted characteristic covers that are of the same degree but do not correspond to subgroups in the same orbit under the automorphism group of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma,v)
. For instance, one can be an abelian cover while the other is not.
In fact, it is not necessary that the covers are characteristic, they only need to have fundamental groups preserved by
\Phi
. It could thus be any cover of degree
k
, up to replacing
\Phi
by
\Phi^{k!}
(which preserves all subgroups of index
k
).

Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be the subgroups of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma,v)
that are fundamental groups of the respective covers
(\Sigma_{1},v_{1})
and
(\Sigma_{2},v_{2})
.

The automorphism
\Phi
induces
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
, automorphisms of subgroups
G_{1},G_{2}
respectively.
Moving up to a common cover of
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
, corresponding to the intersection of the subgroups
G_{1},G_{2}
, (which is preserved by
\Phi
), we obtain a subgroup on which
\phi,\phi^{\prime}
coincide.
Turn
\phi^{\prime}
into an automorphism
\psi
of
G_{1}
by picking an isomorphism
\alpha:G_{1}\to G_{2}
.

Claim 4.1
.

The automorphisms
\phi
and
\psi
of the group
G_{1}
are in the same commensurated-conjugacy class in
{\mathrm{Comm}\left(G_{1}\right)}
.

Write
H=G_{1}\cap G_{2}
.
On
H
, the restrictions of
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
coincide. Since
\alpha\circ\psi\circ\alpha^{-1}=\phi^{\prime}
on
G_{2}
, we have that
\phi|_{H}=\alpha\circ\psi\circ\alpha^{-1}|_{H}
.
We thus have
\phi,\psi
commensurably conjugated by
\alpha^{-1}
on
H
.
It is not clear what conditions would ensure that they are not conjugate in
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G_{1}\right)}
, even though one might suspect that this happens somewhat generically: any such conjugation would be a hidden symmetry for the mapping class of
\phi
on its cover
\Sigma_{1}
, because it can then descend as
[\Phi]
to
\Sigma
through two different covering maps.

4.1.
Explicit example

5.
Infinitely many class number one pseudo-Anosovs

We start this Section by a lemma which is probably well known to specialists. Since we could not find an explicit reference, we include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists
V_{0}>0
, and infinitely many conjugacy classes of maximal non-arithmetic uniform lattices in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, with co-volume
\leq V_{0}
.

Proof.

Take
M_{0}
a non-compact, finite volume hyperbolic 3-manifold with one cusp (a complement of a hyperbolic knot for instance), and consider a sequence
M_{n}
of compact hyperbolic manifolds obtained by deeper and deeper hyperbolic Dehn filling on
M_{0}
. Their volumes
V_{n}
are bounded above by the volume
V_{0}
of the initial manifold, and accumulate on
V_{0}
. It follows from a theorem of Borel
[
4
, Theorem 8.2]
that, after passing to a subsequence, we can assume that they have non-arithmetic fundamental group
\Gamma_{n}
in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. By another theorem of Borel, on commensurability
[
4
, Main Theorem]
, for all these
n
, there exists a unique biggest uniform lattice
\Gamma_{n}^{+}
containing
\Gamma_{n}
, necessarily as a finite index subgroup. For each
n
, the co-volume of
\Gamma^{+}_{n}
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
divides
V_{n}
, and is larger than a positive constant
[
12
,
13
]
. Therefore, since
V_{n}
accumulates on
V_{0}
, the
\Gamma^{+}_{n}
are eventually all of different co-volume, hence non-conjugate.

∎

We finally prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\Lambda^{0}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}
a sequence of representatives of different conjugacy classes of maximal non-arithmetic uniform lattices in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
In each
\Lambda^{0}_{i}
, by Agol’s virtual fibration theorem
[
2
, Theorem 9.2]
, there is a finite index subgroup
\Lambda^{1}_{i}
that maps onto
\mathbb{Z}
with finitely generated kernel. Since the index of
\Lambda^{1}_{i}
in
\Lambda^{0}_{i}
is finite, the chains of strict inclusion of subgroups from
\Lambda^{0}_{i}
to
\Lambda^{1}_{i}
are finite. Therefore, there exists a maximal subgroup
\Lambda_{i}
of
\Lambda^{0}_{i}
, containing
\Lambda^{1}_{i}
, and surjecting on
\mathbb{Z}
(under a homomorphism
\pi_{i}:\Lambda_{i}\to\mathbb{Z}
). The index of
\Lambda^{1}_{i}
in
\Lambda_{i}
is necessarily finite, less than
[\Lambda^{1}_{i},\Lambda^{0}_{i}]
, and the kernel of
\pi_{i}
has therefore finite index in the fiber of
\Lambda^{1}_{i}
. In particular it is finitely generated. By Lemma
3.7
, it is therefore
the fundamental group of a hyperbolic
2
-orbifold. Let
G_{i}
be this subgroup, and
t_{i}\in\Lambda_{i}
such that
\Lambda_{i}=G_{i}\rtimes\langle t_{i}\rangle
.

Denote by
\phi_{i}
the automorphism of
G_{i}
given by conjugation by
t_{i}
.

Let us compute the class number of
\phi_{i}
. Let
\psi_{i}
be a commensurably conjugated automorphism. We want to prove that it is conjugate to
\phi_{i}
in
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G_{i}\right)}
. By Proposition
3.5
, the lattice
\Lambda_{i}
lives in a larger lattice containing a copy
{\rm M}_{i}
of the mapping torus of
\psi_{i}
and surjecting onto
\mathbb{Z}
with the fibers of
{\rm M}_{i}
and
\Lambda_{i}
in the kernel. By Borel’s Theorem on commensurability
[
4
]
, since
\Lambda_{i}
is non-arithmetic, there is a unique biggest lattice containing
\Lambda_{i}
, and it is therefore
\Lambda^{0}_{i}
. Therefore, by maximality of
\Lambda_{i}
,
\langle{\rm M}_{i}\cup\Lambda_{i}\rangle=\Lambda_{i}
, and it follows from Lemma
3.3
that
\phi_{i}
and
\psi_{i}
are conjugated in
{\mathrm{Aut}\left(G_{i}\right)}
. Therefore, the class number of
\phi_{i}
is one.

The same argument also reveals that for

,
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{j}
do not have any non-trivial power that are commensurably conjugate: if they did, the mapping tori would be represented in

in a common lattice that fibers with finitely generated kernel, and Borel’s Theorem again ensures that the maximal lattice containing them is unique, and therefore

.
∎

6.
Appendix: a discussion of the algebraic number theory for class number of matrices, and its analogous concepts

6.1.
Orders and ideal classes

We briefly discuss the algebraic number theory behind the class numbers of matrices in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with irreducible characteristic polynomial.
Recall that an order is a ring that is finitely generated as
\mathbb{Z}
-module.
In an algebraic number field
K
(a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}
), there is a maximal order which is the ring
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of algebraic integers.
Let
\chi
be a unitary irreducible polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}
, and
\xi
a root in
K=\mathbb{Q}[X]/(\chi)
. We are interested in the order
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
in
K
.

If
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}
are ideals in this ring, they are said to be equivalent if there exist
x,y
such that
x\mathfrak{a}=y\mathfrak{b}
. The ideal
\mathfrak{a}
is invertible if there is
\mathfrak{b}
such that
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}
is principal, hence equivalent to
(1)
. The monoid of equivalence classes of ideals is the ideal class monoid (or semigroup), and restricting to the invertible ideals, this constitutes the Picard group of the order
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
.

The Picard group of an order in a number field is finite (see discussion in
[
15
]
). The Ideal Class Monoid of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
is finite
[
30
, Thm. 8]
. It is a subset of a finite union of Picard groups of orders containing
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
(those orders are contained in the maximal order, the ring of algebraic integers).
When
n=2
, it is equal to this finite union of Picard groups (see
[
20
, Prop. 3.7]
).
The Latimer-MacDuffee theorem says the following.

Theorem 6.1
.

[
17
,
28
]

Let
\chi
be a unitary irreducible polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}
, and
\xi
a root in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{\chi}
be the set of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-conjugacy classes of matrices in
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
whose characteristic polynomial is
\chi
.
Then
\mathcal{C}_{\chi}
is in bijection with the Ideal Class Monoid of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
.

6.2.
Arbitrarily large class numbers

We owe to Sara Checcoli the following argument for showing that the class number is not bounded among elements of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proposition 6.2
.

The class numbers of elements in
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with irreducible characteristic polynomials are finite but unbounded.

Proof.

We already know that they are finite by Theorem
6.1
.
A theorem of Montgomery and Weinberger
[
22
]
ensures that there are infinitely many square-free integers
d>0
of the form
d=4n^{2}+1
such that the class number of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})}
is larger than
\sqrt{d}(\log\log d)/(\log d)
.

For each such
d
, consider Pell’s equation
a^{2}-db^{2}=4
, which has integer solutions by Lagrange’s theorem
[
16
]
.
Let
a,b
be a solution, and
\chi_{a}(X)=X^{2}+aX+1
. Since
a^{2}-4=db^{2}
is not a square,
\chi_{a}
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
, and it is obviously the characteristic polynomial of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-a&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Its splitting field is
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
, and since
d=4n^{2}+1\equiv 1\,mod\,4
, the ring of integers is
\mathbb{Z}[(1+\sqrt{d})/2]
. Its class number is larger than
\sqrt{d}(\log\log d)/(\log d)
, and therefore, the class number of the order
\mathbb{Z}[(a+b\sqrt{d})/2]
also. By the theorem of Latimer and MacDuffee, for our integer
a
, the class number of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-a&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is larger than
\sqrt{d}(\log\log d)/(\log d)
.
∎

6.3.
On the proof of Latimer-MacDuffee theorem

We reproduce a variation of the proof given in
[
28
]
, that will help us connect to the case of surfaces.
For
\xi\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, the dual of the
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
-module
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n}
is

Hom_{\mathbb{Z}[\xi]}(\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n},\mathbb{Z}[\xi])
, it is the module of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
-linear combinations of the
n
entries of vectors in
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n}
.
With the notation of Theorem
6.1
, it suffices to establish the following Proposition.

Proposition 6.3
.

For
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
of characteristic polynomial
\chi
, let
v_{M}
in
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n}
be an eigenvector for
\xi
, and

\mathfrak{a}_{v_{M}}=\{\alpha(v_{M}),\alpha\in Hom_{\mathbb{Z}[\xi]}(\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n},\mathbb{Z}[\xi])\}.

Then
\mathfrak{a}_{v_{M}}
is an ideal of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
, the ideal class of
\mathfrak{a}_{v_{M}}
depends only on
M
, every class is realized, and for all
M,N
, the ideal classes of
\mathfrak{a}_{v_{M}}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{v_{N}}
are equal if and only if
[M]=[N]
in
\mathcal{C}_{\chi}
.

Proof.

The module

\mathfrak{a}_{v_{M}}
is trivially a
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
-module. Therefore it is an ideal of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
. Any two choices of
v_{M}
differ by a scalar in the field of fractions
\mathbb{Q}[\xi]
. Observing that
\mathfrak{a}_{\lambda v}=\lambda\mathfrak{a}_{v}
, we obtain that
M
thus determines an ideal class of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
.

Let us show that every ideal of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
is realized in this way. If
\mathfrak{b}
is a non-zero ideal of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
, it contains and is contained in two rank
n
free abelian groups (respectively
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]b
for
b\in\mathfrak{b}\setminus\{0\}
, and
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
), hence it is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
n
.
Consider
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis.
Being moreover a
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
-module, each
\xi\omega_{i}
is a unique integral combination of
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
. Let
M
be the matrix whose rows are the integer coefficients of these combinations. Thus,
M
is a square matrix for which
\xi
is an eigenvalue, and therefore, by Irreducibility, its characteristic polynomial is
\chi
. Since
v_{M}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})^{\top}
is an eigenvector,
\mathfrak{a}_{v_{M}}
contains
\mathfrak{b}
, and is equal to it because the coordinate linear forms generate
Hom(\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n},\mathbb{Z}[\xi])
.

Two conjugate matrices
M,PMP^{-1}
(for
P\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
) produce the same ideal class: indeed, the eigenvector
v_{PMP^{-1}}
can be chosen as
Pv_{M}
, and since

Hom_{\mathbb{Z}[\xi]}(\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n},\mathbb{Z}[\xi])=Hom_{\mathbb{Z}[\xi]}(\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n},\mathbb{Z}[\xi])P,

the images
\mathfrak{a}_{v_{M}}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{Pv_{M}}
are the same.
Finally, if two matrices
M,N
produce the same ideal class, let
v_{M}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})^{\top}
and
v_{N}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})^{\top}
be the respective eigenvectors in
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n}
, scaled so that their entries generate the same ideal. The tuples
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
and
(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})
are two bases of the same
\mathbb{Z}
-module, therefore there exists
P\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
Pv_{M}=v_{N}
. Both matrices
N
and
PMP^{-1}
have
v_{N}
as eigenvectors for the eigenvalue
\xi
. Since the entries of
v_{N}
form a basis of the
\mathbb{Z}
-module they generate, there is a unique matrix
Q
with integer entries such that
Qv_{N}=\xi v_{N}
. So,
Q=N=PMP^{-1}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

6.4.
A connection to foliations: the torus

Let
T=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\mathbb{R}^{n}

be the
n
-dimensional unit torus.
Let
\alpha\in Hom(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbb{Z})
be a non-trivial linear form, and
\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}\alpha
its kernel.
Let
p
be the smallest positive value of
\alpha(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
.

The group
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
splits as
\ker\alpha\oplus\langle v_{\alpha}\rangle
for
v_{\alpha}
a preimage of
p
. Consider the extension of
\alpha
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the hyperplane
\ker_{\mathbb{R}}\alpha
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and its image in
T
: this is an embedded codimension 1 subtorus
T_{\alpha}
, the quotient of
\ker_{\mathbb{R}}\alpha
by the translation group
\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}\alpha
.

Let us say that
T_{\alpha}
carries a weight of
p
(the positive generator of
\alpha(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
).
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the space of weighted codimension 1 subtori that are images of kernels (in
\mathbb{R}
) of non-trivial forms in
Hom(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbb{Z})
.

Let us define the intersection form
\iota:\mathcal{H}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{N}
to be

\iota(T_{\alpha},v)=|\alpha(v)|.

First this is well defined: the weighted codimension 1 subtorus
T_{\alpha}
determines the form
\alpha
up to the sign.
Let us justify the name.
If
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
which is not a proper multiple, the image of the segment
[0,v]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
in
T_{\alpha}
is a simple closed curve. If
1\in Im(\alpha)
, this curve intersects exactly
|\alpha(v)|
times the codimension 1 subtorus
T_{\alpha}
, as seen when expressing
v
in the sum
\ker\alpha\oplus\langle v_{\alpha}\rangle
. If now
p
is the weight of
T_{\alpha}
, the quantity
|\alpha(v)|
counts each intersection point
p
times.

For general
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
\iota(T_{\alpha},v)=|\alpha(v)|
counts this geometric weighted intersection number multiplied by the largest
k
so that
w\in k\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
If now
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for all
m
let us choose a vector
v_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\|v_{m}\|_{\infty}<1
,
mv-v_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Observe that
\iota(T_{\alpha},v)=\lim_{m}\frac{1}{m}\iota(T_{\alpha},mv-v_{m})
. The intersection form counts an average of intersection numbers for the multiples of
v
. This justifies the denominator.

Geometrically, we associate, to an arbitrary vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, the image in the torus
T
of the line
\mathbb{R}v
. The foliation
\mathcal{F}_{v}
of the torus is then defined as the partition by the images of the parallel lines to
\mathbb{R}v
. If
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, this is a foliation by closed curves, and if
v\notin\mathbb{Z}^{n}
each line of the foliation is a geodesic that is infinite in both directions.

We may interpret the intersection form
\iota(\cdot,v)
as defining a measure
\mu
on each torus
T_{\alpha}
of weight
1
(i.e. for
\alpha
achieving
1
): for each Borel
\Omega
,
\mu(\Omega)
is
\lim_{m}\frac{1}{m}n(m)
for
n(m)
the number of intersection points of the image of
[0,mv]
in
T_{\alpha}
, with
\Omega
(counted with multiplicity if
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
). The intersection form thus defines a transverse measure to the foliation
\mathcal{F}_{v}
that endows each codimension 1 subtorus
T_{\alpha}
(for
\alpha
achieving
1
) of the total mass
\mu(T_{\alpha})=\iota(T_{\alpha},v)
. Choosing a multiple of
v
then multiplies the measure by the factor. The foliation has a well defined projective transverse measure.

Let now
M
be a matrix in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
as before, and
v_{M}
an eigenvector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the eigenvalue
\xi
.
We now relate this geometric intersection form to the considerations on the ideal class associated to the conjugacy class of
M
.

Lemma 6.4
.

If, for
\gamma\in Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbb{Z})
, one denotes by
\gamma^{\prime}
its linear extension to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\{\alpha(v_{M}),\alpha\in Hom_{\mathbb{Z}[\xi]}(\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n},\mathbb{Z}[\xi])\}&=&\{\alpha(v_{M}),\alpha\in Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),\alpha(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\subset\mathbb{Z}\}\\
&=&\{\gamma^{\prime}(v_{M}),\gamma\in Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbb{Z})\}\end{array}

Proof.

The equality between the two right hand sides is clear. In the first equality, the reverse inclusion is clear. To show the forward inclusion, let
v_{M}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
. It suffices to show that for each
i
,
v_{i}
and
\xi v_{i}
are in
\{\alpha(v_{M}),\alpha\in Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),\alpha(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\subset\mathbb{Z}\}
. Of course
v_{i}
is the scalar product
v_{i}=(e_{i},v_{M})
, and
\xi v_{i}
is
v_{i}=(M^{\top}e_{i},v_{M})
. Both linear forms
(e_{i},\cdot)
and
(M^{\top}e_{i},\cdot)
send
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

With Lemma
6.4
and Proposition
6.3
one recognizes the following geometric interpretation, with notations as above.

Corollary 6.5
.

The correspondence between
\mathcal{C}_{\chi}
and the ideal class monoid of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
is given by taking an eigenvector
v_{M}\in\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n}
and the ideal of all intersection numbers of
v_{M}
with weighted codimension 1 subtori of
T
— or in other words, the ideal of all evaluations of the transverse-measured foliation
\mathcal{F}_{v_{M}}
on codimension 1 subtori.

6.5.
A view to laminations: higher genus surfaces

In the case of a closed orientable surface
\Sigma
, of genus
g\geq 2
, and an automorphism of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
, one may define similar objects. For a pseudo-Anosov map
\phi
, the eigenvector
v_{M}
(and its associated foliation) should be replaced by a geodesic lamination
\lambda
on the surface that is preserved by
\phi
, with a transverse measure
\tau
. ( Recall that
\tau
is a measure on the arcs transverse to
\lambda
, such that
\tau
is
0
if the arc does not intersect
\lambda
.) Further,
\tau
is multiplied by some characteristic value
\xi
by the action of
\phi
. This transverse measure, when integrated on simple closed curves, provides an intersection number for the measured lamination.

Consider a train track chart
\mathcal{T}
carrying
\lambda
. To describe the transverse measure
\tau
it suffices to assign weights to each of the arcs of the train track, in such a way that at each node, a summation consistency holds. The mapping class
\phi
determines a map from
the underlying graph of
\mathcal{T}
to itself, and a matrix
M_{\phi,\mathcal{T}}
counting, in its columns, the number of occurrences of each arc of
\mathcal{T}
in the image of each given arc of
\mathcal{T}
. The vector
W
of weights of each arc must satisfy
M_{\phi,\mathcal{T}}W=\xi W
. Choosing
W
in
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]^{n}
thus allows us to define an ideal
\mathfrak{a}_{M_{\phi,\mathcal{T}}}
, as generated by the image of
W
under the collection of intersection forms.

Since the intersection forms depend only on
\lambda
, and not
\mathcal{T}
, we defined an ideal class of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
associated to
\phi
.
If
\psi
is conjugate to
\phi
in the mapping class group of
\Sigma
, then its associated lamination is the image of that of
\phi
by a conjugating mapping class, and therefore, they define the same ideal class.
If
\phi
and
\psi
are commensurably conjugate, the stretch factor
\xi
is the same for both, as easily seen in a finite cover of
\Sigma
.

Completing a picture analogous to the torus case would require understanding which ideal classes are realized, and understanding pseudo-Anosov mapping classes defining the same ideal class, in a given mapping class group.
