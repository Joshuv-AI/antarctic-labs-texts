Abstract
. Recent research has begun to assess people’s perceptions of voice user interfaces (VUIs) as dialogue partners, termed partner models. Current self-report measures are only available in English, limiting research to English-speaking users. To improve the diversity of user samples and contexts that inform partner modelling research, we translated, localized, and evaluated the Partner Modelling Questionnaire (PMQ) for non-English speaking Western (German, n=185) and East Asian (Japanese, n=198) cohorts where VUI use is popular. Through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), we find that the scale produces equivalent levels of “goodness-to-fit” for both our German and Japanese translations, confirming its cross-cultural validity. Still, the structure of the communicative flexibility factor did not replicate directly across Western and East Asian cohorts. We discuss how our translations can open up critical research on cultural similarities and differences in partner model use and design, whilst highlighting the challenges for ensuring accurate translation across cultural contexts.



1.
Introduction

Voice user interfaces, like Google Assistant, Apple’s Siri, and Amazon Alexa, are now commonplace. Much effort has gone into identifying and realizing ideal forms of engagement and user experience with these technologies
(
Clark et al. 2019a
;
Seaborn et al. 2022
)
, whilst also understanding people’s perceptions of voice user interfaces as dialogue partners
(
Doyle et al. 2021
;
Cowan et al. 2019
)
, termed
partner models

(
Brennan et al. 2010
;
Branigan et al. 2011
;
Cowan et al. 2019
;
Doyle et al. 2021
)
. This refers to the cognitive representation of a dialogue partner’s communicative competence and social relevance
(
Branigan et al. 2011
;
Cowan et al. 2017
)
. Recent work
(
Doyle et al. 2021
)
suggests three underlying dimensions for voice user interfaces: competence and dependability, human-likeness, and communicative flexibility
(
Doyle et al. 2021
)
. Partner models can affect user experience, impacting trust in the system
(
Luger and Sellen 2016
)
and knowledge expectations
(
Cowan et al. 2017
)
. They are also used to guide speakers toward appropriate speech and language choices for a given dialogue partner, increasing the chances of communicative success
(
Doyle et al. 2019
;
Branigan et al. 2011
)
.

In most research, partner model effects have been inferred based on language production during voice user interface interactions
(
Cowan et al. 2019
;
Branigan et al. 2011
)
. More recently, efforts have gone into creating a self-report scale to measure partner models more directly
(
Doyle et al. 2021
)
. This instrument, known as the Partner Modelling Questionnaire, has been validated for English-speaking Western audiences, showing good construct validity, convergent/divergent validity, and strong test-retest reliability
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)

. However, the Partner Modelling Questionnaire is currently only available in English, and has yet to be validated within other socio-cultural contexts. This validation is essential for future work to: (i) avoid generalizations about the structure and universality of partner models due to sampling exclusively from English-speaking
(
Levisen 2019
)
and/or Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic nations
(

)
; (ii) address the methodological gap in how user experience is measured in voice user interface research across international cohorts

)
; and (iii) promote diversity, equity, and inclusion in how voice user interfaces are designed by ensuring perceptions from diverse cohorts are assessed and influence the evaluation and design process.

To address this gap, we translated, localized, and evaluated the 18-item Partner Modelling Questionnaire
(

)
instrument for German and Japanese users of voice user interfaces: two non-English speaking cohorts from countries where voice user interfaces are popular. Our work contributes to the field by providing tools for reliable measurement of partner models about voice user interfaces for two divergent linguistic and cultural contexts: namely, German (Partner Modelling Questionnaire-DE) and Japanese (Partner Modelling Questionnaire-JP) cohorts

2.
Background

2.1.
Localization and Cross-Cultural Research

Humanity is diverse. Even so, much of human subjects research involves English-speaking
(
Levisen 2019
)
and/or Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic populations
(
Henrich et al. 2010
)
. human-computer interaction is no exception
(
Gasparini et al. 2011
;
Linxen et al. 2021
;
Seaborn et al. 2023
)
. A wealth of studies across the behavioural and social sciences has shown that English-speaking Westerners are not representative of all humanity
(
Henrich et al. 2010
)
. Still this pattern persists, reinforcing biases in global power structures based on language, culture, and nation. Suffice to say, this has implications for human knowledge, where diversity is flattened, sidelined, or left undiscovered, while findings from a rather small slice of humanity are treated as universal and transferable
(
Henrich et al. 2010
)
. Recognition of this within HCI has led to theorizing and taking action on matters of Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion, notably feminist HCI
(
Bardzell 2010
;
Bardzell and Bardzell 2011
;
Rode 2011
)
, intersectional HCI
(
Schlesinger et al. 2017
)
, critical race theory for HCI
(
Ogbonnaya-Ogburu et al. 2020
)
, decolonizing HCI
(
Alvarado Garcia et al. 2021
)
, disability justice in HCI
(
Sum et al. 2022
)
, to name a few.

Yet, more subtle challenges beyond the
“who”
of the human sample exist, including how research is framed (
epistemology
)
(
Markus et al. 1997
;
Kâğıtçıbaşı 1996
)
, what theories and models of the world are drawn on or developed (
ontology
)
(
Denzin and Lincoln 2005
;
Martín-Baró 1994
)
, and how phenomena are captured (
methodology
), i.e., evaluation frameworks, measures, and instruments
(
Adams and Stocks 2008
;
Denzin 2009
)
. Critical work on the linguistic and cognitive sciences has revealed a pattern of Anglocentrism, or English and Anglo culture as the default, which has driven and constrained global knowledge production
(
Levisen 2019
)
. Recent reviews of voice user interface research have highlighted a dire need for validated subjective measures of user perceptions and a need to move research outside of English-speaking and/or WEIRD contexts
(
Clark et al. 2019a
;
Seaborn and Urakami 2021
)
. We need localization, translation, and cross-cultural engagement
(
Bourges-Waldegg and Scrivener 1998
;
Gasparini et al. 2011
)
. To this end, Bourges-Waldegg and Scrivener
(
Bourges-Waldegg and Scrivener 1998
)
centred
meaning
as a key human factor, while Gasparini, Pimenta, and De Oliveira
(
Gasparini et al. 2011
)
also highlighted the need for consideration of evaluation measures and instruments.

Language is intimately tied to culture
(
Jiang 2000
;
Rock 2019
)
. By
culture
, we mean the sum total of knowledge, attitudes, norms, values, and beliefs that people within a certain society at a certain time share and develop together
(
Hofstede 2001
;
Hall 1976
;
Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner 2011
)
. Language transmits but also constructs and influences culture. Yet, culture is implicit, making it difficult to develop explicit concepts, measures, and instruments
(
Lee et al. 2008
)
. In response, several influential scholars, including Hofstede
(
Hofstede 2001
)
, Hall
(
Hall 1976
)
, and Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner
(
Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner 2011
)
developed broad frameworks of
cultural dimensions
for cross-cultural analyses. Importantly, there is a connection between language as a medium of culture and the resulting methodological (if not epistemological) implications for cross-cultural (and cross-linguistic) work in end-user evaluations with voice user interfaces. It is thus important to investigate voice user interfaces cross-culturally
(
Seaborn et al. 2024
)
. Yet few works exist on this topic. Ma et al.
(
Ma et al. 2022
)
discovered three varieties of user orientations towards emotionally-aware voice assistants linked to the cultural contexts of Germany, Egypt, and China. Huang and Zhang
(
Huang and Zhang 2020
)
found that UK and Taiwanese customers differed in terms of preference for
“media richness,”
the relative simplicity or complexity of voice assistant expressions, in transactional versus non-transactional contexts. In consideration of the Japanese context, Ouchi et al.
(
Ouchi et al. 2019
)
explored the importance of politeness in voice assistant expressions, finding that plain language was preferred when making travel plans, even when polite language may be typical in similar situations among people.
Seaborn et al. 2024
discovered language and cultural differences when comparing imagined dialogues and activities elicited by US and Japanese populations for voice user interfaces. Notably, Japanese participants imagined advanced social abilities, such as omoiyari (i.e., being mindful of those around you) and kūki wo yomu (i.e., reading the room), as well as linguistic patterns like tameguchi (i.e., plain form speech). We surmise that both
language
and
cultural
gaps exist, and tools are needed to inform voice user interface design and validate cross-cultural research.

2.2.
Concept and Measurement of Partner Models

A growing concept in voice user interface research is that of partner models. Inspired by research in psycholinguistics
(
Brennan et al. 2010
)
, partner models are
“an interlocutor’s cognitive representation of beliefs about their dialogue partner’s communicative ability”

(
Doyle et al. 2021
, p. 6)
. Conceptually, they are inspired by accounts of mental models
(
Johnson-Laird 1980
;
Johnson-Laird 2010
;
Norman 1983
, e.g.,)
and can be seen as
mental models for dialogue
that guide a speaker in choosing appropriate ways to converse with a dialogue partner
(
Brennan et al. 2010
;
Doyle et al. 2019
)
. This is said to enhance the chances of achieving communicative success
(
Brennan et al. 2010
;
Branigan et al. 2011
)
. Users tend to perceive voice user interface’s as
“at risk listeners”

(
Oviatt et al. 1998
)
or as basic communication partners
(
Branigan et al. 2011
)
with design influencing people’s perceptions of a voice user interface’s capabilities
(
Luger and Sellen 2016
;
Cowan et al. 2017
)
. Recent work suggests the concept is multidimensional, encompassing perceptions of competence and dependability, human-likeness and communicative flexibility
(
Doyle et al. 2021
)
. These models are also thought to have a significant impact on language production, driving adaptation and audience design in voice user interface interaction
(
Ho et al. 2018
;
Amalberti et al. 1993
;
Bell and Gustafson 1999
;
Oviatt et al. 1998
;
Branigan et al. 2010
)
.

Partner models are often inferred from audience design and adaptation effects rather than directly measured. But this hampers work on how voice user interface design and user experience may dynamically influence these models, as well as the nature of and causal links to audience design
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
. In response, a fully validated 18-item self-report measure, the Partner Modelling Questionnaire (Partner Modelling Questionnaire)
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
, was developed with three subscales for each partner model dimension
(
Doyle et al. 2021
)
:
perceived communicative competence and dependability; perceived human-likeness in communication;
and
perceived communicative flexibility
. The Partner Modelling Questionnaire has shown good construct validity by way of confirmatory factor analysis indices of good fit, strong test-retest reliability at 4- and 12-week intervals, generally strong internal reliability, and good convergent/divergent validity when compared to other scales
(
Doyle et al. 2021
;
Doyle et al. 2023
)
. Other work has shown discriminant validity and measure sensitivity by successfully identifying changes in partner models resulting from experiencing errors committed by a voice user interface
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
and differing perceptions toward adaptive and non-adaptive speech from a social robot
(
Axelsson et al. 2023
)
.

2.3.
Research Aims

Currently, the Partner Modelling Questionnaire and its underlying three factor model have only been validated in English
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
, thus limiting its use to English-speaking and/or Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic cohorts. We aimed to increase diversity and inclusion in voice user interface research by translating the Partner Modelling Questionnaire for German and Japanese users. These countries were chosen because of their voice user interface user base. Germany has the fourth largest number of VUI users (17.2%), while Japan is a leading market in East Asia, with an estimated 20% of smartphone and smart speaker owners actively using voice user interfaces
(
Secretariat of the Headquarters for Digital Market Competition,
Cabinet Secretariat 2022
)
. From a linguistic and cultural perspective, German is closer to English, the language of the original Partner Modelling Questionnaire, while Japanese is further
(
Chiswick and Miller 2005
)
. This allowed us to compare the Partner Modelling Questionnaire by relative similarity and distance across languages and cultures. We could also take a first step towards addressing the Anglocentric bias in linguistic and voice user interface research
(
Levisen 2019
)
, producing validated, non-English instruments. Moreover, we were able to identify more subtle Anglo biases within the Partner Modelling Questionnaire, notably for trust and reliance.

3.
Methods

Our research was conducted in three stages. First, we translated the Partner Modelling Questionnaire into German and Japanese. We then conducted a small-scale item review study wherein we piloted the questionnaire and identified issues with specific items. We then ran online questionnaire studies of voice user interface users (in German and Japanese). Our protocol was registered on OSF
3
3

3

https://osf.io/cd7jr
in advance of data collection on February 23
rd
, 2023. Ethics approval was obtained from all institutions.

3.1.
Stage 1: Translation

Guided by the
ITC Guidelines for Translating and Adapting Tests

(
International Test Commission 2018
)
, we used a double forward translation and reconciliation procedure followed by a back translation and tuning procedure.
First, two people independently translated the 18 Partner Modelling Questionnaire items from English into German/Japanese and then discussed and reconciled any discrepancies between the two translations.
Second, a third person back-translated the items into English; the results were discussed and updates made, if needed.
All translators were native or highly proficient speakers of the respective target language, native or highly proficient in English, and familiar with the respective target culture.
The translation process was overseen by and discussed with the author of the English Partner Modelling Questionnaire.
This process meets the criteria suggested by the ITC
(
International Test Commission 2018
)
.

3.2.
Stage 2: Pilot Study

Our first objective was to test whether the German and Japanese translations of the English Partner Modelling Questionnaire matched the original concepts of the Partner Modelling Questionnaire.
The German version of the Partner Modelling Questionnaire was tested remotely with a small sample of native German speakers who were also proficient in English (n=8).
All were doctoral students in linguistics or computer science at universities in Germany or Ireland.
The Japanese version was tested in-person in two stages: n=7 for the first, wherein we discovered an issue with the first set of trust and reliance items, and n=6 for the follow-up with updated items. Participants, recruited in the lab, were native Japanese speakers and bilingual Japanese and English speakers. All participants were asked to provide feedback on 1) the wording and level of detail in the instructions used throughout the questionnaire and 2) the terms used for the PMQ items. Participants responded in either German or Japanese.

Across both samples, a challenge arose for the concepts of
“trust”
and
“reliance”
.
This was not entirely unexpected because the English version of the PMQ uses the same base term—specifically, “reliable” and “unreliable”—split across two items, each paired with a similar concept—dependable/unreliable (as reliance), and reliable/uncertain (as trust). We realized that we needed to carefully translate these concepts, particularly in Japanese, where trust and reliance and their synonyms are often difficult to distinguish and even used interchangeably
(
Ueno et al. 2023
)
.

In the Japanese pilot tests, we first discussed the meaning of each term/concept with participants to ensure a shared understanding. Then, participants collaboratively developed lists of synonyms for each term/concept (refer to Supplementary Materials, Appendix 2). Next, the entire team met to discuss the meanings of each synonym in relation to each of the original terms/concepts. We decided to use opposite terms for each core term for both
“trust”
and
“reliance”
, and unique words for the opposite positive and negative correlates. For example, the Japanese version of “trust” uses
信頼
できる (trustable) and 疑わしい (doubtful/uncertain), while
“reliable”
uses 頼りになる (dependable/reliable) and
信頼
できない (not trustable), emphasis on the paired terms. After this, a second pilot test was run to test these pairings. Participants went through the entire questionnaire. They were first asked to provide freeform feedback, and then directly asked about the term choice in the PMQ section. Feedback confirmed that these pairings made sense and allowed each concept to be distinguishable from the other.

For the German version, we directly translated the English items, splitting the semantic counterparts denoting
“reliable”
(
zuverlässig
) and
“unreliable”
(
unzuverlässig
) over two items and pairing each with similar concepts—
verlässlich
for
“dependable”
and
unsicher
for
“uncertain.”

Pilot testers were able to rate these items without issue.

Stage 3: Main Study

We next conducted two independent online studies to test the construct validity of the Partner Modelling Questionnaire with a German and a Japanese sample, respectively. The data was analysed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the validity of the original three partner modelling factors among the German and Japanese samples.

3.3.2.
Procedure

We closely followed
Doyle et al. 2019
;
Doyle et al. 2021
;
Doyle et al. 2023
, with remote participants recruited via crowd-working platforms. The German sample was recruited using
Prolific
and the Japanese sample was recruited using
Yahoo! Crowdsourcing
. Each platform was used to provide participants with general information about the study, a link to the survey, and advice that they would need to use their own desktop/laptop device to complete the study.

After following the link, participants were first presented with a more detailed information sheet explaining the nature of the study, their role as a participant, what data was being collected and why, and their rights in relation to data use, storage, and access. They then provided informed consent. Next, they answered an open-ended question about experiences with voice user interfaces they
normally used
(refer to Supplementary Materials, Appendix 1). This was to make a voice user interface interaction salient before completing the Partner Modelling Questionnaire.

For the Partner Modelling Questionnaire, participants were asked to rate the voice assistant that they
interacted with most frequently
by indicating where they felt it sat between each of the poles presented (e.g., competent/incompetent, human-like/machine-like, flexible/inflexible, etc.). Specifically, they were prompted as follows:

Denken Sie an das sprachgesteuerte digitale System, mit dem Sie am häufigsten interagieren. Wie würden Sie dessen kommunikative Fähigkeiten auf den folgenden Skalen bewerten?

あなたが最も頻繁に接する音声インターフェースについて考えてみて下さい。そのコミュニケーション
能力を次の各極間の尺度でどのように評価しますか？

Think about the speech interface you interact with most frequently. How would you rate its communicative abilities on the following scales?

Participants used a 7-point radial dial scale placed between each pole. Finally, participants were asked to provide basic demographics before being debriefed and thanked for their participation. The study took
\sim
5 minutes, with participants compensated
\sim
1.50 British pounds (Prolific) or
\sim
100 yen (Yahoo! Crowdsourcing), respectively.

3.3.3.
Data Analyses

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to validate the factor structure, replicating efforts to quantify the Partner Modelling Questionnaire’s construct validity previously reported for the English instrument
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
. As highlighted above, data was assessed for Insufficient Effort Responding prior to conducting analyses using visual screening and by omitting anyone with a SD
\leq
.5
(
Field 2013
;
Steedle 2018
)
. We also omitted multivariate outliers according to Mahalanobis distances
(
Brown 2015
)
, resulting in data on 185 German participants and 198 Japanese participants being used in the respective analyses. Both samples were above the widely adopted 10:1 item-to-participant ratio
(
Nunnally 1967
;
Bentler and Chou 1987
)
, though below minimum recommendations of n=250 for confirmatory factor analysis using robust maximum likelihood estimators (MLE)
(
Hu and Bentler 1999
;
Yu and Muthén 2002
)
.
That said, our samples were deemed statistically suitable for confirmatory factor analysis (refer to
3.4
). Prior to running confirmatory factor analysis, assumption tests were also carried out for both samples, including assessment of bi-variate correlations, and multivariate normality and linearity. Data suitability for confirmatory factor analysis was also assessed based on the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin test for sampling adequacy, Bartlett’s test for sphericity, and a determinate figure was generated
(
Field 2013
)
. The results of these are presented for each sample in Section
3.4
.

In confirmatory factor analysis, a range of indices can be used to assess the statistical fit of a model. Following best practice
(
Brown 2015
)
, we used a combination of absolute fit indices, including a Chi-square test, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residuals (SRMR), alongside relative fit indices, including comparative fit index (CFI) and the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI). Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion statistics are also provided, though these have no cut-off threshold; instead, lower numbers are indicative of a better fitting model
(
Field 2013
)
. We also provide whole scale and by-factor descriptive statistics for each version, along with indices of internal reliability for the scale as a whole and for each factor.

3.3.4.
Assumption and Data Suitability Tests

Pearson’s bi-variate correlation matrices indicated no multicollinearity (
r
> .9)
(
Clark and Watson 2016
)
in either sample. Low mean bi-variate correlations (
r
< .15)
(
Clark and Watson 2016
)
were found for item 17 (interpretive/literal) in both samples, and for item 18 (spontaneous/predetermined) for the Japanese sample. However, as we were attempting to validate a predetermined model, these items were retained in the analysis. Data from both samples were non-normally distributed according to univariate (histogram and Shapiro-Wilk) and multivariate tests (Mardia, Henze-Zirkler, Royston H and Doornik-Hansen). However, deviation from normality is expected when analysing questionnaire responses
(
Field 2013
)
. Indeed, similar issues were also found in the confirmatory factor analysis analysis of the English Partner Modelling Questionnaire
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
. We therefore used robust parametric versions of confirmatory factor analysis. As per confirmatory factor analysis convention
(
Brown 2015
)
, we report both standard and robust outputs, though robust outputs should take precedence when being interpreted. Linearity for both samples was assessed using q-q plots, which revealed a relatively linear relationship between variables in both samples.

(
Kaiser 1974
)
. Results from Bartlett’s test for sphericity was statistically significant (
\chi^{2}
(153) = 1442.08,
p
< .001), also demonstrating that the data was suitable for confirmatory factor analysis
(
Field 2013
)
. This was further supported in producing a determinate figure of .00029, which is above the > .00001 threshold required for confirmatory factor analysis
(
Field 2013
)
.

Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin test of sampling adequacy was marvelous overall (.92), with 14 items deemed marvelous (> .9), three meritorious (.8-.9), and one (spontaneous/predetermined) middling (.76)

However, some differences existed across cohorts in terms of lower-order aspects of the scale that warrant discussion, particularly the relative weakness of factor 3:
perceived communicative flexibility
. In the original validation work
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
, and indeed for the PMQ-DE, internal reliabilities for factor 3 were acceptable, although in the PMQ-DE the loading of item 17 (interpretive/literal) is low. We felt it was important to retain this factor given its strong test-retest reliability at both 4- and 12-week intervals (icc = .80, CI: .72-.86) with the PMQ-EN, as well as the fact that communicative flexibility was deemed a key dimension of dialogue capability, particularly among participants in early Partner Modelling Questionnaire work
(
Doyle et al. 2019
;
Doyle et al. 2021
)
, and growing interest around issues like agent flexibility and pro-activity in conversational user interface research. It is also likely to become more important with the growth of generative AI, as these AI deliver more variation in responses. Poor internal reliability is also likely an artefact of the small number of items contained within factor 3: factors with less items tend to produce a lower Cronbach alpha and factor 3 contains the minimum recommended three items per factor
(
Field 2013
;
Kline 2000
)
.

This is, however, a more problematic position to take for the Japanese cohort. Items 17 (interpretive/literal) and 18 (spontaneous/predetermined) produced low factor loadings (.27 and .15, respectively), and Cronbach
\alpha
internal reliability for factor 3 was very low (.25). This might suggest that communicative flexibility, as it is represented and conceptualised in the PMQ-EN and PMQ-DE, may not replicate directly across to Japanese cohorts.
In short, there may be a distinction between Japanese and Western cohorts: that communicative flexibility may not be as prominent a feature of partner models toward voice user interfaces.
Before removing the factor from the PMQ-JP, we advise that work should be carried out to generate items to reinforce this factor before further evaluating its relevance to Japanese cohorts. In the meantime, we recommend that researchers administer the scale in its entirety (the 18-item, 3-factor model), but take account of the relative weakness of factor 3 when conducting and interpreting analysis.

4.
Discussion

Non-embodied voice user interfaces are now commonplace.
Understanding user perceptions of voice user interfaces as dialogue partners, i.e., partner models
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
, and how this informs user interaction
(
Cowan et al. 2019
)
has become a key issue. Here, we created and robustly analysed localized versions of the Partner Modelling Questionnaire for German and Japanese cohorts.
We found that the German version held a three-factor structure similar to the English version
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
, echoing the structure of partner models identified in English speaking cohorts
(
Doyle et al. 2021
)
. While the Japanese cohort achieved goodness of fit for the three-factor structure, we advise that future work investigates the relevance of communicative flexibility to Japanese and East Asian cohorts, and how it might be better represented across English, German, and Japanese cohorts. These findings contribute much needed linguistic–cultural variety to investigations into partner modelling, increasing diversity and inclusion in voice user interface research.

4.1.
Understanding Cultural Differences in Partner Model Use

The original three partner dimensions generally held goodness of fit across both German and Japanese contexts. Still, we do not know what dimensions are salient within particular contexts and to other non-WEIRD populations. It might be that only key features are activated
(
Johnson-Laird 2010
;
Norman 1983
)
, promoted by specific voice user interface designs or system behaviours. Indeed, the propensity for audience design to produce certain linguistic behaviours may be culturally dependent
(
Bell 1984
)
. For instance, the impact of perspective taking and linguistic adaptation can vary between individualist (e.g., Western) and collectivist (e.g., East Asian) cultures
(
Wu and Keysar 2007
;
Wu et al. 2013
;
Ross et al. 2002
)
. Those in more collectivist cultures may be more sensitive to partner modelling, i.e., take greater care before speaking with a dialogue partner, compared to those in individualist cultures, and thus their interaction with voice user interfaces may differ.
Such linguistic adaptation may also not be uniform across cultures.
We offer Partner Modelling Questionnaire researchers a localized tool to explore this by reliably measuring and monitoring user partner models
(
Doyle et al. 2021
)
.

4.2.
(Re)designing Conversational Agents based on Partner Modelling

The Partner Modelling Questionnaire as an evaluation tool enables greater precision on how specific aspects of voice user interface design impact user perceptions. With the PMQ-DE and PMQ-JP, we can now study this impact cross-culturally. For instance, such measures can be used to assess cultural differences in the impact of speech synthesis (e.g., disjointed speech or lack of affect), dialogue management architectures (e.g., turn-taking or response formats), dialogue events and culturally relevant stylistic variations (e.g., politeness, directness
(
House and Kasper 1981
)
, error handling, and use of common ground
(
Hershcovich et al. 2022
)
) on user partner models. Our work provides the tools needed to consistently measure partner models across different studies, time periods, and notably cultures, which has been highlighted as a key need for research synthesis and consensus-building in human-computer interaction
(
Rogers and Seaborn 2023
)
.

4.3.
Confusing Concepts and Tricky Terms: On
Trust
and
Reliance

Gasparini, Pimenta, and De Oliveira
(
Gasparini et al. 2011
, p. 16)
write that “[translation] is the central activity of localization.” Indeed, one of our key methodological findings relates to translation and the potential of subtle Anglocentric biases known to emerge when starting from an English base
(
Levisen 2019
)
. Translators are regularly faced with a choice between direct translation of the words according to dictionaries, assuming that the meaning is retained, and
transcreation
, which requires creatively deviating from options intended as direct translations so as to preserve the intended meaning
(
Díaz-Millón and Olvera-Lobo 2023
)
. Issues such as semantic equivalence are common when translating questionnaires across languages
(
Behling and Law 2000
)
. In our case, we had to grapple with the complex, culturally-situated relationship between trust and reliance. This was particularly tricky for the Japanese translation. Our solution was to brainstorm with native speakers and pilot test multiple times. This situation highlights that there is often no direct translation of an English word into another language
(
Levisen 2019
)
, which necessitates transcreative work that is neglected when translation is automated (e.g., using Google translate or other machine translators). This is not simply a matter of bias; it is also a matter of cross-cultural idea transmission through language. We highlight this challenge for others seeking to do cross-cultural and multi-linguistic work. Translation may not be a simple process, even for apparently simple concepts from our limited frames of reference.

4.4.
Three’s Company? Issues with Partner Modelling’s Three Factor Structure

Our comparisons of each Partner Modelling Questionnaire version revealed a consistent issue with the third factor,
perceived communicative flexibility
. It was weakest across the board, with sub-par levels of internal reliability and poor factor loadings on some items. Still, the results for the English and German versions indicate that it is viable, even while the Japanese version leans toward eliminating or reconstructing this factor. As such, despite the cross-cultural validation of the scale, we must emphasize the need to further validate the underlying three factor model itself. Future work should look into reinforcing this factor by following a similar processes used to create the original PMQ-EN (e.g.,
(
Doyle et al. 2019
)
), where a variety of qualitative and secondary research studies were used to generate an item pool specific to communicative flexibility, or by conducting novel studies with data analyzed for patterns related to communicative flexibility, similar to
Clark et al. 2019b
.
The Partner Modelling Questionnaire is by no means a monolithic account of partner models for any cultural group. There may be other factors that may differ considerably between groups and individuals. What the Partner Modelling Questionnaire currently offers is the most parsimonious account of the most common components of voice user interfaces partner models for English speaking Westerners, which also happens to offer a valid account of partner models for German and Japanese speaking cohorts.

In addition to testing the relevance of communicative flexibility and improving how it is represented, we also strongly advise work on other potentially important items and factors in these cultural contexts.
Drawing from work on what makes a good conversation
(
Clark et al. 2019b
)
, potential factors could be humourous/dry—an example of spontaneous joy and adaptation to error or unexpected occurrences—negotiative/headstrong—referring to more complex exchanges that hint at higher-order social competence and the ability to persuade as well as change “one’s mind”—and poetic/prosaic—indicating an ability to creatively express rhythm, metaphor, and drama tuned to the conversational moment.

4.5.
Limitations

Similar to the English version, our scope was limited to assessing partner models of non-embodied speech agents. Although used recently to assess robotic interactions
(
Axelsson et al. 2023
)
, partner models may need to include further dimensions that reflect the embodied nature of such agents to be comprehensive.

Although our work generally confirms the three factor structure for the items of the Partner Modelling Questionnaire within our two cohorts, we would encourage researchers to consider bolstering the current item sets with culturally specific items that may not be captured currently by the translated version of the Partner Modelling Questionnaire. Specific focus could be placed on further refining items to measure the culturally-situated issues of trust and reliance highlighted in our work. As mentioned, we also strongly encourage future research aimed at strengthening the communicative flexibility factor, along with work aimed at further investigating its validity as a core perception in partner modelling. With the advent of generative AI and proactive agents, our intuition is that this factor will become more important.

Our confirmatory factor analysis findings also highlight the measure’s construct validity across the languages. However, future work needs to confirm divergent/convergent validity, sensitivity, and test-retest reliability of the translated measures, in line with the English version of the Partner Modelling Questionnaire
(
Doyle et al. 2023
)
. We note though that significant challenges may exist due to the lack of translated measures that can act as comparators to adequately assess convergent/divergent validity.

Finally, we acknowledge limitations in our samples, notably that the German cohort was younger and had more women while the Japanese cohort was older and had more men. Future work capturing a wider array of demographics would lend more confidence to the generalizability of the results.
In particular, including a wider range of cultures would offer a more comprehensive view of global voice user interface interactions.

5.
Conclusion

Recent work has focused on understanding and measuring people’s partner models of voice user interfaces as conversational partners, generating an English self-report measure (called the Partner Modelling Questionnaire). So as to increase the cultural reach and improve diversity of partner modelling research our work has developed and evaluated the factor structure of a Japanese (PMQ-JP) and German (PMQ-DE) version of the measure. We have made these measures freely available to researchers with the hopes that such translations will increase the diversity of research on the concept, allowing the HCI and conversational user interface communities to expand the cultures and samples that can be included in their research.
