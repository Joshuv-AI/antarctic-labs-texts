The Entangled Quantum Polynomial Hierarchy Collapses
Sabee Grewal, Justin Yirka

Abstract
We introduce the entangled quantum polynomial hierarchy 𝖰𝖤𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QEPH} as the class of problems that are efficiently verifiable given alternating quantum proofs that may be entangled with each other. We prove 𝖰𝖤𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QEPH} collapses to its second level. In fact, we show that a polynomial number of alternations collapses to just two. As a consequence, 𝖰𝖤𝖯𝖧 = 𝖰𝖱𝖦 ⁡ ( 𝟣 ) \mathsf{QEPH}=\mathsf{QRG(1)} , the class of problems having one-turn quantum refereed games, which is known to be contained in 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} . This is in contrast to the unentangled quantum polynomial hierarchy 𝖰𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QPH} , which contains 𝖰𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 𝟤 ) \mathsf{QMA(2)} . We also introduce a generalization of the quantum-classical polynomial hierarchy 𝖰𝖢𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QCPH} where the provers send probability distributions over strings (instead of strings) and denote it by 𝖣𝗂𝗌𝗍𝗋𝗂𝖻𝗎𝗍𝗂𝗈𝗇𝖰𝖢𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{DistributionQCPH} . Conceptually, this class is intermediate between 𝖰𝖢𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QCPH} and 𝖰𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QPH} . We prove 𝖣𝗂𝗌𝗍𝗋𝗂𝖻𝗎𝗍𝗂𝗈𝗇𝖰𝖢𝖯𝖧 = 𝖰𝖢𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{DistributionQCPH}=\mathsf{QCPH} , suggesting that only quantum superposition (not classical probability) increases the computational power of these hierarchies. To prove this equality, we generalize a game-theoretic result of Lipton and Young (1994) which says that the provers can send distributions that are uniform over a polynomial-size support. We also prove the analogous result for the polynomial hierarchy, i.e., 𝖣𝗂𝗌𝗍𝗋𝗂𝖻𝗎𝗍𝗂𝗈𝗇𝖯𝖧 = 𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{DistributionPH}=\mathsf{PH} . These results also rule out certain approaches for showing 𝖰𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QPH} collapses. Finally, we show that 𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{PH} and 𝖰𝖢𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QCPH} are contained in 𝖰𝖯𝖧 \mathsf{QPH} , resolving an open question of Gharibian et al. (2022).

Introduction

The polynomial hierarchy
[
MS72
,
Sto76
]
is a hierarchy of complexity classes that are known to equal
\mathsf{P}
if and only if
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
.
The hierarchy, denoted by
\mathsf{PH}
, is a natural generalization of efficient proof verification and nondeterminism and plays a central role in complexity theory.
Given its significance, it is natural to explore quantum generalizations of
\mathsf{PH}
, yet such generalizations remain understudied.

Before discussing quantum polynomial hierarchies, let us first informally define
\mathsf{PH}
.
Intuitively,
\mathsf{PH}
is a hierarchy of complexity classes that can solve progressively harder problems, extending beyond both
\mathsf{NP}
and
\mathsf{coNP}
.
One can think of
\mathsf{PH}
as a public debate between Alice and Bob, who take turns presenting polynomial-sized proofs (bit strings) to a referee.
At the end of the debate, the referee takes the proofs, performs a polynomial-time classical computation, and decides a winner.

More formally, a problem is in the
k
-th level of the polynomial hierarchy
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{k}}
if there is a deterministic polynomial-time verifier
M
(the referee) that takes proofs
y_{1},\dots,y_{k}
and satisfies the following conditions. On yes-instances,
\exists y_{1}\forall y_{2}\exists y_{3}\ldots
such that
M(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=1
, and, on no-instances,
\forall y_{1}\exists y_{2}\forall y_{3}\ldots
such that
M(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=0
.
\mathsf{PH}
is comprised of every level
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{k}}
for all natural numbers
k
, and it is strongly believed that
\mathsf{PH}
is infinite.

Gharibian, Santha, Sikora, Sundaram, and Yirka
[
GSS+22
]
studied two quantum generalizations of
\mathsf{PH}
.
They generalized the class
\mathsf{QCMA}
to the quantum-classical polynomial hierarchy
\mathsf{QCPH}
, the class of problems for which a quantum verifier can efficiently verify solutions given a constant number of classical proofs from competing provers. Note that this is the same as
\mathsf{PH}
except the verifier can perform a polynomial-time
quantum
computation.
In the same work, they generalized the class
\mathsf{QMA(2)}
to the
unentangled
quantum polynomial hierarchy
\mathsf{QPH}
,
for which the verifier is still quantum, but the proofs are quantum mixed states and promised to be unentangled from each other.
Notably, Gharibian et al. did not introduce a hierarchy in which the proofs can be
entangled
, and they did not establish a relationship between
\mathsf{QPH}
and
\mathsf{QCPH}
(or even
\mathsf{QPH}
and
\mathsf{PH}
), leaving it unclear whether or not
\mathsf{QPH}
was at least as powerful as its classical counterpart.

While these containments are what one might guess to be true, proving them is nontrivial.

More generally, if
\mathsf{QCPH}
and
\mathsf{QPH}
are indeed more powerful, it prompts the question of why: is it quantum verification, quantum proofs, unentanglement, or some nuanced combination?

In this work, we address all of these questions related to quantum generalizations of the polynomial hierarchy.
First, we ask (and answer) what problems admit a
\mathsf{PH}
-style protocol where the provers can send potentially entangled proofs.
We show that this new hierarchy—the entangled quantum polynomial hierarchy (
\mathsf{QEPH}
)—behaves drastically differently from what we believe about
\mathsf{PH}
,
\mathsf{QCPH}
, and
\mathsf{QPH}
.

Second, we prove that
\mathsf{PH}\subseteq\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
,
confirming the intuitive relationship between these hierarchies.

Lastly, to understand the power of quantum proofs, we introduce a generalization of
\mathsf{QCPH}
where the provers send probability distributions over classical proofs and denote the class by
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
.
We prove that
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}=\mathsf{QCPH}
, despite the intuition from game theory that mixed strategies are helpful.
Note that the only difference between
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
and
\mathsf{QPH}
is that the proofs in
\mathsf{QPH}
involve quantum superposition.
Hence, our result establishes that the increased computational power of
\mathsf{QPH}
comes only from the quantum superposition in the proofs.

1.2
Main Ideas

Let us begin by explaining
\mathsf{QEPH}
on an intuitive level (see
Definition

3.2
for a formal definition).

\mathsf{QEPH}
can be thought of as a constant-round non-interactive game between two competing provers, Alice and Bob, who take turns sending quantum registers, i.e., collections of qubits, to a verifier. Alice and Bob are allowed to entangle their own quantum registers across turns.
The verifier then performs a polynomial-time quantum computation, measures a fixed output qubit in the computational basis, and, if the verifier sees
1
, they accept (Alice wins), and reject otherwise (Bob wins).

\mathsf{QEPH}
contains the decision problems for which Alice always wins with high probability on yes-instances and Bob always wins with high probability on no-instances.
We note that in this game the moves are
public
, which means that Alice knows the state of the quantum registers sent by Bob and vice versa.
See
Remark

3.4
for further discussion of public vs. private moves in a quantum world.

To highlight the key technique in our proof that
\mathsf{QEPH}
collapses (
Lemma

4.1
), we explain how to simulate the third level of
\mathsf{QEPH}
, denoted by
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{3}}
, inside of the second level
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{2}}
. The proof for higher levels proceeds by induction.
As we will explain formally in
Section

3
, a
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}
protocol can be written as an optimization problem with a value equal to the probability the verifier accepts when both players use optimal strategies.
In particular, Alice selects proofs that maximize the probability of the verifier accepting, while Bob selects proofs to minimize that probability.
For
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{3}}
, given a problem instance in which the verifier’s action is encoded by an observable
R
, the corresponding optimization problem is

\max_{\rho_{1}\in\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{X}_{1})}\min_{\sigma\in\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{Y})}\max_{\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{A}}\,\tr\left(R\left(\rho_{2}\otimes\sigma\right)\right),

where
\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the set of density operators on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

and
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\{\rho\in\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{X}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{2})\mid\tr_{\mathcal{X}_{2}}(\rho)=\rho_{1}\}
.
The restriction of the second maximization to the set
\mathcal{A}
is
to enforce that Alice’s second move is consistent with her first.

A straightforward analysis shows that when focusing on the inner two operators, a min-max theorem applies, allowing us to swap the ordering of the inner minimization and maximization. Then, because we allow entangled states, we can combine the two sequential maximization operators into one, leaving an optimization problem corresponding to a two-round protocol.
Notably, both the three-round and two-round protocols are over the same input and verifier, so the reduction does not increase the problem size or change the error parameters.

It is natural to ask why
our technique does not also collapse
\mathsf{PH}
. In short, the above approach fails immediately, since, for one, our collapse theorem relies on the fact that Alice and Bob are choosing quantum proofs from compact and convex sets (see
2.3
and
2.4
).
In contrast, the set of classical strings is neither compact nor convex.

To show that
\mathsf{QEPH}=\mathsf{QRG(1)}
, we build on a previous characterization of Gharibian et al.
[
GSS+22
]
where they showed that the second level of the
unentangled
quantum polynomial, denoted by
\mathsf{Q\Sigma_{2}}
, equals
\mathsf{QRG(1)}
.
We extend their result in
Proposition

3.3
to show that
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{2}}=\mathsf{Q\Sigma_{2}}=\mathsf{QRG(1)}
, which yields our characterization that
\mathsf{QEPH}=\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{2}}=\mathsf{QRG(1)}
.

\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{2}}=\mathsf{Q\Sigma_{2}}
because, after two turns, each prover has only sent a single proof, so there’s no distinction yet to be made between the entangled versus entangled hierarchies.

We now turn to the containment
\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
, which are both defined formally in
Section

2.3
.
In
\mathsf{QCPH}
, the verifier receives classical proofs, whereas the proofs in
\mathsf{QPH}
are unentangled quantum mixed states.
One naïve approach to simulating
\mathsf{QCPH}
inside of
\mathsf{QPH}
—which does not work—is for the verifier to immediately measure the quantum proofs to get classical strings and then run the
\mathsf{QCPH}
verification protocol.
The reason this fails is that the dishonest prover (i.e., the player without a winning strategy) can cheat by sending a quantum state, rather than a classical proof.
In more detail, while the honest prover has perfect knowledge of the quantum states sent by the dishonest prover, they do not know which particular classical strings the verifier will observe upon measurement, making it unclear what their response should be.
The definition of
\mathsf{QCPH}
guarantees the correct player has an effective response conditioned on any particular proof sent from the other player, but this does not generally guarantee the correct player can succeed against a mixture of potential moves.
Unfortunately, the equilibrium point of a zero-sum game which allows for such mixed moves will generally be mixed, rather than pure.

To overcome this, we simulate the
i
-th level of
\mathsf{QCPH}
in the
2ki
-th level of
\mathsf{QPH}
, for some constant
k
.
We ask the provers to send
k
copies of each of the proofs they would send in the
\mathsf{QCPH}
protocol, which increases the number of turns by a factor of
2k
.
Using the groups of
k
proofs, we give a simple test to ensure that no player cheats, which works as follows.
Measure each of the
k
proofs in the standard basis.
If the outcomes are all equal, then the test passes, and, otherwise, the test fails.
We prove that this is enough to force the provers to send computational basis states with high probability.

We remark that this bears some similarity to other protocols involving unentanglement.
Harrow and Montanaro
[
HM13
]
used unentanglement to force Merlin to send
k
-partite states, and, recently, Jeronimo and Wu
[
JW23
]
use unentanglement to force Merlin to send many copies of (approximately) the same quantum state.
Both of these results fundamentally rely on the swap test, which tests for equality between two quantum states
[
BCWdW01
]
.
In a similar fashion, we use unentanglement to force the provers to send standard basis states, i.e., classical strings.
With that, we design a simulation of any
\mathsf{QCPH}
protocol inside of
\mathsf{QPH}
.

Finally, we discuss our proof that
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}=\mathsf{QCPH}
(the same techniques will also show
\mathsf{DistributionPH}=\mathsf{PH}
).
In
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
, the provers send probability distributions over polynomial-length classical proofs.
For classical proofs of length
m
, these distributions can have a support of size exponential in
m
.
The key lemma for this result says that the provers can send
much simpler
distributions without changing the acceptance probability of the verifier too much.
In particular, we prove that the distributions sent by the provers can be
uniform
over
\poly(m)
many classical proofs and, even with this simplification, the acceptance probability of the verifier will change by at most a small constant.
This simplification lemma (
Lemma

6.4
) generalizes a result due to Lipton and Young
[
LY94
]
and Althöfer
[
Alt94
]
, where they showed that such a simplification works in the special case of a one-turn two-player game.
Our contribution is to generalize their result to the case where the two players alternate sending distributions any constant number of times.

With the simplification lemma, one can prove
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QCPH}
as follows.
To send a distribution in
\mathsf{QCPH}
, the provers send every classical string that is in the support of their distribution. By our simplification lemma, there are only a polynomial number of such strings, so all of them can be sent in a polynomially-sized classical proof.
Then, since the simplified distributions are uniform, the verifier can randomly sample one of the strings uniformly at random.
The other direction
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}\supseteq\mathsf{QCPH}
follows from the same techniques that prove
\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
.

There are several quantum complexity classes that involve provers sending possibly entangled proofs to a quantum polynomial-time verifier.
We do not attempt to survey them here, but, for convenience, we summarize quantum complexity classes involving entangled proofs (and their classical counterparts) in

.

Our work on
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
builds on previous game-theoretic characterizations in complexity theory (see e.g.,
[
FKS95
]
).

\mathsf{PH}
-style classes involve a debate with public communication (perfect information), and a non-interacting, passive referee.

\mathsf{RG}
-style classes involve private communication (imperfect information) with provers sending particular strings to the referee (perfect recall).
A consequence of imperfect information is that the players must model their competitor’s moves as probability distributions (mixed strategies) because they are never sure which move is made.
Our class
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
fits into this framework in a nuanced way.
Specifically, the distributions sent are public (similar to
\mathsf{PH}
); they represent a mixture of pure moves (similar to
\mathsf{RG}
); but, uniquely, the provers don’t know which string will be sampled by the referee (reminiscent of imperfect recall).
This is a novel game-theoretic model, and as we discuss further in
Section

6
, it is naturally motivated by a game of quantum mixed states sent to a non-interacting referee.

Finally, the concurrent and independent work of Agarwal, Gharibian, Koppula, and Rudolph

\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{pureQPH}

.

Preliminaries

We introduce notation, definitions, and background that are central to our results.
For the most part, we assume familiarity with common concepts and classes in quantum and classical complexity theory as well as quantum computing and quantum information.
For a thorough discussion of these topics, see

,

We will need the following version of Hoeffding’s inequality.

Fact 2.1

(Hoeffding’s inequality)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables subject to
a_{i}\leq X_{i}\leq b_{i}
for all
i
.
Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and let
\mu=\mathop{\bf E\/}[X]
.
Then it holds that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[X-\mu\geq t]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right)

and

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[X-\mu\leq-t]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right).

2.1
Quantum Information

A
quantum register
refers to a collection of qubits. Associated with each register is a complex Hilbert space, and the state of a quantum register is described by a Hermitian, positive semi-definite matrix with trace one called a
density matrix
.
We denote the set of
n
-qubit density matrices by
\mathbf{D}(n)
, and the sets of linear operators and density matrices on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
by
\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{H})
, respectively.

For two quantum registers
(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})
with Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, the combined space is the tensor product space
\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y}
.
The partial trace
\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}:\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y})\to\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{X})
is the unique linear map that satisfies
\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}(A\otimes B)=\tr(A)B
for all
A\in\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{X})
and
B\in\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{Y})
.
If the compound register
(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})
is in the state
\rho\in\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y})
, then the state of register
\mathsf{X}
is
\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}(\rho)\in\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{X})
.
That is, operationally speaking, the partial trace is the act of ignoring (or discarding) a quantum register.
We note that the partial trace
\tr_{\mathcal{X}}
can be defined similarly, and, in general, the context in which the partial trace is used should clarify which spaces are being “traced out”.

A
quantum measurement
of a quantum register is described by a finite collection of Hermitian, positive semi-definite matrices that sum to identity. Let
\mathsf{X}
be a quantum register with Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
whose state is described by
\rho
. Let
\mathcal{M}=\{E_{i}\mid i\in\Sigma\}
be a quantum measurement, where
\Sigma
is a finite alphabet. Upon measuring
\mathsf{X}
with
\mathcal{M}
, we observe
i\in\Sigma
with probability
\tr(E_{i}\rho)
.

2.2
A Min-Max Theorem

To prove our collapse theorem, we use a weaker version of Sion’s min-max theorem.

Theorem 2.2

(A weaker version of Sion’s min-max theorem
[
Sio58
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be complex Euclidean spaces, let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
be convex and compact subsets, and let
f:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bilinear function. Then

\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\min_{b\in\mathcal{B}}f(a,b)=\min_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}f(a,b).

It is a well-known fact that the space of density matrices is compact and convex.

Fact 2.3

(
[
Wat18
, Chapter 1]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of density matrices on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{H})
is compact and convex.

It is critical for us that, even if we impose partial trace constraints on the set of density matrices, the set remains compact and convex. We include a proof for completeness.

Fact 2.4
.

Let
\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y}
be two quantum registers with Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, respectively, and let
\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y})
be the corresponding set of density operators. Let
\rho^{\prime}\in\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{X})
be some fixed density matrix.
Then the set

\mathbf{S}=\{\rho\in\mathbf{D}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y})\mid\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}(\rho)=\rho^{\prime}\}

is compact and convex.

Proof.

Let
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathbf{S}
, and define
\sigma\coloneqq\theta\rho_{1}+(1-\theta)\rho_{2}
for for
\theta\in[0,1]
. Then

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}(\theta\rho_{1}+(1-\theta)\rho_{2})

\displaystyle=\theta\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}(\rho_{1})+(1-\theta)\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}(\rho_{2})

\displaystyle(\text{By the linearity of the partial trace.})

\displaystyle=\theta\rho^{\prime}+(1-\theta)\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\text{Because $\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathbf{S}$.})

\displaystyle=\rho^{\prime},

so
\mathbf{S}
is convex.

To show that
\mathbf{S}
is compact, we must show that it is closed and bounded.
Without loss of generality, let
\mathsf{X}
be an
n
-qubit register and
\mathsf{Y}
be an
m
-qubit register.
Then we can identify
\mathbf{S}
with the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{4^{n+m}}
and observe that all entries are bounded in magnitude by
1
. Therefore,
\mathbf{S}
is bounded.
To see that
\mathbf{S}
is closed, we need the following definitions.
For
x\in\mathbb{C}
, define
f_{x}:\mathbb{C}^{4^{n+m}}\to\mathbb{C}
as

f_{x}(A)=\langle x,Ax\rangle
,
which is continuous because the inner product is continuous;
define

g:\mathbb{C}^{4^{n+m}}\to\mathbb{C}^{4^{n+m}}
as

g(A)=A-A^{\dagger},

which is a polynomial and therefore continuous;
and, finally, define
h:\mathbb{C}^{4^{n+m}}\to\mathbb{C}^{4^{n}}
as

h(A)=\tr_{\mathcal{Y}}(A),
which is a linear map on a finite-dimensional vector space and therefore continuous.
Then

\mathbf{S}=\bigcap_{x\in\mathbb{C}}f_{x}^{-1}([0,\infty))\cap g^{-1}(\{0\})\cap h^{-1}(\{\rho^{\prime}\})\cap\text{tr}^{-1}(\{1\}).

The preimage of a continuous function on a closed set is closed, and the intersection of closed sets is closed. Therefore,
\mathbf{S}
is closed.
∎

Previously Studied Hierarchies

Here, we give formal definitions of the polynomial hierarchy
\mathsf{PH}
,
the quantum-classical polynomial hierarchy
\mathsf{QCPH}
,
and the unentangled quantum polynomial hierarchy
\mathsf{QPH}
, the latter two of which were both introduced by Gharibian et al.
[
GSS+22
]
.
These classes will appear again in
Section

5
when we prove
\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
and in
Section

6
when we prove
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}=\mathsf{QCPH}
.
We defer definitions of our new classes until later, with
\mathsf{QEPH}
studied in
Section

4
and
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
in
Section

6
.

Definition 2.5

(
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}
)
.

A language
L
is in the
i
-th level of the polynomial hierarchy
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}
if there exists a polynomial-time deterministic Turing Machine
M
such that for any
n
-bit input
x
,

\displaystyle x\in L

\displaystyle\iff\exists y_{1}\forall y_{2}\exists y_{3}\dots Q_{i}y_{i}\text{ such that }M(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{i})=1,

\displaystyle x\not\in L

\displaystyle\iff\forall y_{1}\exists y_{2}\forall y_{3}\dots\overline{Q_{i}}y_{i}\text{ such that }M(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{i})=0,

where
Q_{i}
denotes
\exists
if
i
is odd and
\forall
otherwise,
\overline{Q_{i}}
denotes the complement of
Q_{i}
, and
\left\lvert y_{i}\right\rvert\leq p(n)
for some fixed polynomial
p
for all
i
.

Definition 2.6

(The polynomial hierarchy (
\mathsf{PH}
)
[
Sto76
]
)
.

The Polynomial-time Hierarchy is defined as

\mathsf{PH}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}.

Note the union which defines
\mathsf{PH}
is over values of
i
which are constant, independent of a problem’s input size.
Observe also that for all
i
,
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}\subseteq\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i+1}}
. Additionally,
\mathsf{PH}
is closed under complement, in particular because
\mathsf{\overline{\Sigma}_{i}^{p}}\subseteq\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i+1}}\subseteq\mathsf{PH}
.
The complement of
\mathsf{\overline{\Sigma}_{i}^{p}}
is defined to be
\mathsf{\Pi^{p}_{i}}
, and for all
i
we have
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}\subseteq\mathsf{\Pi^{p}_{i+1}}\subseteq\mathsf{\Pi^{p}_{i+2}}
.

The definition of
\mathsf{PH}
is particularly robust.
The class can be defined equivalently by
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i+1}}=\mathsf{NP}^{\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}}
, giving a constant-height tower of
\mathsf{NP}
oracles.
The model of alternating nondeterministic Turing Machines also can be used to define each level of the hierarchy.
In another direction, the Sipser–Lautemann theorem shows
\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{2}}\cap\mathsf{\Pi^{p}_{2}}\subseteq\mathsf{PH}

[
Sip83
,
Lau83
]
.
So, natural bounded-error or probabilistic definitions of
\mathsf{PH}
collapse to the standard, deterministic definition given above. This is also true for oracle definitions, where we know
\mathsf{MA}^{\mathsf{MA}^{\ldots}}=\mathsf{PH}
.

Even a partial survey of results regarding
\mathsf{PH}
would be impossible to fit here. We finally note that
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}=\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i+1}}
or
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}=\mathsf{\Pi^{p}_{i}}
would both “collapse” the hierarchy so that
\mathsf{PH}=\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}
. These two events are analogous to
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
or
\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}
. Conversely, if
\mathsf{PH}
collapses to any finite level, it implies analogs of
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
and
\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}
must be true for some degree of nondeterminism, at some level of the hierarchy. So, the strongly-believed conjecture that
\mathsf{PH}
is not equal to any
\mathsf{\Sigma^{p}_{i}}
for fixed
i
is a generalization of those other strongly-believed conjectures.

The uniform circuit model is standard for quantum complexity classes, so we give the definition below.

Definition 2.7

(Polynomial-time uniform family of quantum circuits)
.

A polynomial-time uniform family of quantum circuits is a family
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that there exists a polynomial bounded function
t:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and a deterministic Turing machine
M
acting as follows.
For every
n
-bit input
x
,
M
outputs in time
t(n)
a description of a quantum circuit
V_{n}
, which has a designated output qubit.
We say
V_{n}
accepts when we observe a
1
upon measuring the designated output qubit in the standard basis.

We generally leave the subscript implicit and just write
V
.
Additionally, we often consider a single problem instance defined by an input
x
for the full length of an analysis.
So instead of writing
V(x,y)
for input
x
and proof
y
,
we simply refer to
V(y)
.

As with most quantum complexity classes, we will be working with promise problems.
Briefly, a promise problem
A
is a pair of non-intersecting subsets (
A_{\text{yes}}
,
A_{\text{no}}
) of
\{0,1\}^{*}
. A decision problem, or language, is a promise problem where
A_{\text{yes}}\cup A_{\text{no}}=\{0,1\}^{*}
.

We are now ready to define
\mathsf{QCPH}
.

We formally define the entangled quantum polynomial hierarchy.
The definition appears more technical than for

and

Definition 3.1

(
i
-th level of the entangled quantum polynomial hierarchy (
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}
))
.

A promise problem
L=(L_{\text{yes}},L_{\text{no}})
is in
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}(c,s)
for polynomial-time computable functions
c,s:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1]
if there exists a polynomial-time uniform family of quantum circuits
\left\{V_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for every
n
-bit input
x
,

takes quantum proofs, measures a fixed output qubit to decide to accept or reject, and satisfies

•

Completeness:
x\in L_{\text{yes}}\Rightarrow\exists\rho_{1}\forall\rho_{2}\exists\rho_{3}\dots Q_{i}\rho_{i}\text{ such that }\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[V(\rho_{i-1},\rho_{i})\text{ accepts}\right]\geq c
,

•

x\in L_{\text{no}}\Rightarrow\forall\rho_{1}\exists\rho_{2}\forall\rho_{3}\dots\overline{Q_{i}}\rho_{i}\text{ such that }\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[V(\rho_{i-1},\rho_{i})\text{ accepts}\right]\leq s
,

where each

\mathcal{A}_{j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\quad\left\{\rho\in\mathbf{D}\left(\mathcal{X}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{3}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{X}_{j}\right)\mid\text{if }j>1,\;\tr_{\mathcal{X}_{j}}\left(\rho\right)=\rho_{j-2}\right\}&\text{if $j$ is odd}\\
\quad\left\{\rho\in\mathbf{D}\left(\mathcal{X}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{4}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{X}_{j}\right)\mid\text{if }j>2,\;\tr_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}\left(\rho\right)=\rho_{j-2}\right\}&\text{if $j$ is even}\end{cases}.

Here,
Q_{i}
denotes
\exists
if

is odd and
\forall

denotes the complement of

. For all

is a space of at most

qubits for a fixed polynomial

.
When the completeness/soundness parameters are not specified, define

\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}\coloneqq\bigcup_{c-s\in\Omega\left(1/\poly(n)\right)}\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}(c,s).

Definition 3.2

(The entangled quantum polynomial hierarchy (
\mathsf{QEPH}
))
.

The entangled quantum polynomial hierarchy is defined as

\mathsf{QEPH}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}.

When introducing a complexity class, perhaps the first question one should ask is whether or not the choice of completeness and soundness parameters actually matter.
In
[
GSS+22
, Theorem 2.6]
, it was shown that
\mathsf{QCPH}
is robust to the choice of error parameters, but no such result is known for
\mathsf{QPH}
.
In
Section

4
, we show that the choice of parameters does not matter for any level of
\mathsf{QEPH}
, i.e., for
c,s
such that
c-s\geq 1/\poly(n)
,
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}(c,s)=\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3})
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
(see
Theorem

4.4
).

Let us also make several remarks on our definition.
As for
\mathsf{PH}
, the indices
i
in the definition of
\mathsf{QEPH}
are constants, independent of a problem’s input size, and, as one should expect,
\mathsf{BQP}=\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{0}}
and
\mathsf{QMA}=\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{1}}
.
One can also define
\mathsf{QE\Pi_{i}}\coloneqq\mathsf{\overline{\Sigma}_{i}^{qe}}
and
\mathsf{QE\Delta_{i}}\coloneqq\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}\cap\mathsf{QE\Pi_{i}}
.
The players also have no incentive to entangle their moves with their opponent because
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}
can be modeled as a zero-sum game.
Therefore, we may assume the even and odd indexed states are unentangled.

Informally,
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{i}}
can be thought of as the following game, where we assume
i
is even to simplify the exposition.
Alice has (possibly entangled) quantum registers
(\mathsf{A_{1}},\ldots,\mathsf{A_{i/2}})
,
and Bob has (possibly entangled) quantum registers
(\mathsf{B_{1}},\ldots,\mathsf{B_{i/2}})
,
where each register is a number of qubits that is polynomial in the input size.
The game commences as follows. In the first round, Alice reveals the state
\rho_{1}
of
\mathsf{A_{1}}
, and then Bob reveals the state
\sigma_{1}
of
\mathsf{B_{1}}
.
In the second round, Alice reveals the state
\rho_{2}
of
(\mathsf{A_{1}},\mathsf{A_{2}})
, and Bob reveals the state
\sigma_{2}
of
(\mathsf{B_{1}},\mathsf{B}_{2})
.
To ensure Alice and Bob do not change their “moves” from previous rounds, we demand that
\tr_{\mathsf{A_{2}}}(\rho_{2})=\rho_{1}
and

Remark 3.4

(Public vs. private quantum proofs)
.

While the quantum polynomial hierarchies are well-defined, some may object that the classes are unphysical because the provers have full knowledge of each other’s density matrices,
even though the verifier only receives a single copy of each proof.
The quantum no-cloning theorem also begs the question of how exactly the information is communicated between the provers.
This is not an issue for
\mathsf{PH}
because it’s trivial to learn classical proofs given a single copy, and, for
\mathsf{QRG}
, this is not an issue because communication is private.
In every quantum complexity class that we are aware of, the provers are “all-powerful” yet still bound by the laws of quantum mechanics.

Despite being unphysical, we are content with the definition for two reasons. First, it is a well-defined, useful theoretical tool for studying quantum information.
Second, in the case of
\mathsf{QEPH}
, we show that it collapses to
\mathsf{QE\Sigma_{2}}
, where it is known, by a min-max theorem, that public vs. private communication is irrelevant. So, despite starting with an unphysical definition, we show equivalence with a class that adheres entirely to the laws of quantum mechanics.

5
\mathsf{PH}
and
\mathsf{QCPH}
Are Contained in
\mathsf{QPH}

We prove that
\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
.
While this result is what one might expect, proving this containment was left as an option question by Gharibian et al.
[
GSS+22
]
.
It is trivial to see that
\mathsf{PH}\subseteq\mathsf{QCPH}
, and, combining these two containments, we have
\mathsf{PH}\subseteq\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
, establishing that quantifying over unentangled quantum proofs is at least as powerful as quantifying over classical proofs.

The central challenge in proving that
\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
is that the proofs in
\mathsf{QPH}
are allowed to be quantum states, which, upon measurement, give rise to a distribution over classical strings.
A flawed idea is to simply measure the quantum proofs to get classical proofs, and then run the
\mathsf{QCPH}
verification protocol with no modifications.
Suppose, however, that Alice has a winning strategy in the
\mathsf{QCPH}
protocol, so she always has a winning response to any classical proof that Bob sends.
When simulating this in
\mathsf{QPH}
, Bob can instead send a
quantum state
—a superposition over many classical proofs—preventing Alice from sending an optimal response. In particular, Alice may not know which response to send, since she doesn’t know which classical proof the verifier will observe upon measurement.

We prevent this potential cheating by requiring each player to send multiple copies of each of their proofs. We prove that this is enough to force both players to send classical strings with high probability.

Theorem 5.1
.

\mathsf{PH}\subseteq\mathsf{QCPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
.

The exact error parameters for
Theorem

5.1
are stated in
Eq.

3
below.
In particular, the reduction is only capable of producing a
\mathsf{QPH}
instance with a constant promise gap.
However, the containment does hold for any
\mathsf{QCPH}

\mathsf{QCPH}

[
GSS+22
]
.

Proof.

Consider any level
\mathsf{QC\Sigma_{i}}
of
\mathsf{QCPH}
.
We show that for any integer

\mathsf{QC\Sigma_{i}}(c,s)\subseteq\mathsf{Q\Sigma_{2ki}}\left(c\left(1-2^{-k}\right),s+2^{-k}\left(1-s\right)\right).

(3)

We simulate any
\mathsf{QC\Sigma_{i}}

\mathsf{Q\Sigma_{2ki}}

After the first

k

k
proofs from Bob, and the verifier discards all
k
proofs from Bob.
For the next
2k

\sigma_{1,1},\dots,\sigma_{1,k}
.
This is repeated
i

2ki

ki

\rho_{1,1},\dots,\rho_{1,k},\sigma_{1,1},\dots,\sigma_{1,k},\rho_{2,1},\dots,\rho_{2,k},\dots.

For each chunk of
k

k

k

If a player fails any check, then the other player is declared the winner.
If both players pass all checks, then the verifier keeps one copy of each classical proof from each chunk and runs the
\mathsf{QCPH}
verification procedure to determine the winner.

Let
A=(A_{\text{yes}},A_{\text{no}})
be a promise problem in
\mathsf{QC\Sigma_{i}}(c,s)

x
be some fixed input.
If
x\in A_{\text{yes}}

x\in A_{\text{no}}
then we consider Bob the honest prover.
We will define a strategy for the honest prover and show that no matter the strategy of the dishonest prover, the honest prover will win high probability.
In particular, the honest prover’s strategy will be to always send classical proofs, and when replying to a dishonest provers proof
\rho=\sum_{j}p_{j}\ket{j}\!\!\bra{j}

\hat{\text{\char 17\relax}}
with the maximum probability
p_{\hat{\text{\char 17\relax}}}
was sent (we arbitrarily choose to break ties by lexicographic order).

If the dishonest prover fails any check, they lose, so we assume now that the dishonest prover passes every check.
Then, since both provers pass every check, the verifier has the
i

In one case, suppose that each of the dishonest prover’s moves turns out to be as the honest prover expected.
Then the situation is identical to the original
\mathsf{QCPH}

In the second case, at least one chunk of
k

k

6
Distribution Hierarchies

We introduce another generalization of the polynomial hierarchy where the provers send probability distributions over bit strings.
This gives rise to two new hierarchies: the distributional polynomial hierarchy
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
and its quantum analogue
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
, which is the same as
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
but with a quantum verifier.
We will focus primarily on
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
since the techniques used to analyze
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
will work for
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
as well.

\mathsf{DistributionPH}
is similar to all of the hierarchies studied in this work.
In
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
, the distributions are public (the provers have full knowledge of the distributions that have been sent), but none of the distributions are sampled until every distribution has been sent.
One can think of this as a non-interactive game, where the players use public, mixed strategies.
Importantly, the distributions are not correlated across rounds.

While
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
is a classical complexity class, our motivation for studying it is to further understand the quantum polynomial hierarchies.
In particular,
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
involves proofs that are classical mixtures of bit strings.
This complements
\mathsf{pureQPH}
, where the proofs are quantum superpositions of bit strings, and
\mathsf{QPH}
, where the proofs are both (classical mixtures of quantum superpositions).
Does the computational power of the polynomial hierarchy increase when the proofs only involve classical probability distributions? Or does the increased computational power come only from the quantum superposition allowed in
\mathsf{QPH}
and
\mathsf{pureQPH}
?
In this section, we resolve these questions.

Theorem 6.1
.

\mathsf{DistributionPH}=\mathsf{PH}
.

That is, if the proofs are distributions over classical proofs,
\mathsf{PH}
does not increase in power.
The proof of

6.1

\mathsf{DistributionPH}
can be sparse and uniform. This lemma generalizes a result due to Lipton and Young
[
LY94
]
and Althöfer
[
Alt94
]
.

In the remainder of this section, we will formally define
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
, prove the technical lemma, and prove
Theorem

6.1
.
Finally, we will discuss
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
(the same as
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
but with a quantum verifier) and the power of classical versus quantum proofs.

We begin by formally defining
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{m}
denote the set of all probability distributions over
\{0,1\}^{m}

For a computation

.

Definition 6.3

(The distribution polynomial hierarchy (
\mathsf{DistributionPH}

The distribution polynomial hierarchy is defined as

\mathsf{DistributionPH}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{Distribution\Sigma_{i}}.

We make a few comments on our definition of
\mathsf{DistributionPH}

If we defined
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
without the bounded-error condition (i.e., no error probability), then it would be equal to

Finally, if one prefers, they can equivalently think of the provers sending quantum mixed states that are immediately measured in the computational basis (instead of probability distributions that are immediately sampled). This is why we choose to denote the probability distributions as
\rho_{i}
in our definition.

As we discussed in
Section

3

\mathsf{QEPH}

Additionally, just like with

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[M\text{ accepts}]=\max_{\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\min_{\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\ldots\operatorname*{Q^{i}}_{\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\mathop{\bf E\/}[M(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{i})],

The expectation is over the randomness in the distributions

\mathop{\bf E\/}[M(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{i})]=\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[M(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{i})=1]

The distributions sent in

Lemma 6.4
.

For any constant
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any classical randomized Turing Machine
M
accepting
k
length-
m
inputs, if

\max_{\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\min_{\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\max_{\rho_{3}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\dots\operatorname*{Q^{k}}_{\rho_{k}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[M(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k})=1\right]=v,

then
for any constant
\epsilon>0
,

\max_{\rho_{1}\in U_{t_{k}}}\min_{\rho_{2}\in U_{t_{k-1}}}\max_{\rho_{3}\in U_{t_{k-2}}}\dots\operatorname*{Q^{k}}_{\rho_{k}\in U_{t_{1}}}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[M(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k})=1\right]\in[v-k\epsilon,v+k\epsilon],

where
t_{i}\coloneqq\lceil m^{2i}/2\epsilon^{2}\rceil
,

U_{t}
denotes the set of uniform distributions over multi-sets of size at most
t
of strings in
\{0,1\}^{m}
,
and
\operatorname*{Q^{k}}
denotes
\max
if
k
is odd and
\min
otherwise.
The complement of this result also holds (i.e., when the sequence starts with
\min
instead of
\max
).

Proof.

We will prove the claim by induction. The base case
k=2
is precisely
[
LY94
, Theorem 2]
(see also
[
Alt94
]
). Our contribution is to generalize their result to larger
k
.

By way of induction, suppose the claim holds for
k-1
, and consider an instance with
k
rounds:

v\coloneqq\max_{\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\min_{\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\max_{\rho_{3}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\dots\operatorname*{Q^{k}}_{\rho_{k}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[M(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k})=1\right].

Since the complement of this result (where a
\min
is first instead of a
\max
) follows in the same way, we omit the details.

Fix
\rho_{1}
to a distribution that maximizes the acceptance probability (and think of
\rho_{1}
as hardcoded into the input).
Consider the inner
k-1
distributions
\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k}
. By the inductive hypothesis, we can simplify these distributions to

\min_{\rho_{2}\in U_{t_{k-1}}}\max_{\rho_{3}\in U_{t_{k-2}}}\dots\operatorname*{Q^{k}}_{\rho_{k}\in U_{t_{1}}}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[M(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k})=1\right],

while only changing the acceptance probability
v
by
\pm(k-1)\epsilon
.
In particular, we have that

v^{\prime}\coloneqq\max_{\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}\min_{\rho_{2}\in U_{t_{k-1}}}\max_{\rho_{3}\in U_{t_{k-2}}}\dots\operatorname*{Q^{k}}_{\rho_{k}\in U_{t_{1}}}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[M(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k})=1\right]\in[v-(k-1)\epsilon,v+(k-1)\epsilon].

We want to show that we can simplify the first distribution
\rho_{1}
in a similar fashion. Specifically, we want to show

v^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq\max_{\rho_{1}\in U_{t_{k}}}\min_{\rho_{2}\in U_{t_{k-1}}}\max_{\rho_{3}\in U_{t_{k-2}}}\dots\operatorname*{Q^{k}}_{\rho_{k}\in U_{t_{1}}}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[M(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k})=1\right]\in[v-k\epsilon,v+k\epsilon].

Observe that choosing
\rho_{1}
from
U_{t_{k}}
instead of
\mathcal{D}_{m}
can only hurt the maximizing player. That is, the probability that
M
accepts can only
decrease
, so
v^{\prime\prime}\leq v^{\prime}+\epsilon\leq v+k\epsilon
is trivial.
All that remains is to show that
v^{\prime\prime}\geq v-k\epsilon
.
To prove this, it suffices to show that
v^{\prime\prime}\geq v^{\prime}-\epsilon
.

Let
\rho_{1}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}
be a distribution that maximizes the acceptance probability of
M
.
Form a multi-set
S
by drawing
t_{k}
independent samples from
\rho_{1}^{*}
.
Consider a string
y\in S
. This gives rise to a random variable on the interval
[0,1]
:

\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k}}[M(y,\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k})],

where we are taking the expectation over optimal choices of
\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k}
.
In expectation over
\rho_{1}^{*}
, we have

\mathop{\bf E\/}_{y\sim\rho_{1}^{*}}\left[\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k}}[M(y,\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k}]\right]=v^{\prime}.

Therefore, by Hoeffding’s inequality (
2.1
),

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[\frac{1}{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\sum_{y\in S}\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k}}[M(y,\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k})]\leq v^{\prime}-\epsilon\right]\leq\exp\left(-2t_{k}\epsilon^{2}\right).

To complete the proof, we must count the number of sequences of distributions the minimizing player can send.
The minimizing player sends at most
k/2
of the distributions
\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k}
, each of which is a uniform distribution over at most
t_{k-1}
-sized subsets of
\{0,1\}^{m}
.
Therefore, in total, there are at most

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t_{k-1}}\binom{2^{m}}{i}\right)^{k/2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t_{k-1}}2^{im}\right)^{k/2}\leq\left(t_{k-1}2^{mt_{k-1}}\right)^{k/2}=t^{k/2}_{k-1}2^{kmt_{k-1}/2}

possible sequences.
We want to choose
t_{k}
so that

\exp\left(-2t_{k}\epsilon^{2}\right)<\frac{1}{t^{k/2}_{k-1}2^{kmt_{k-1}/2}},

(4)

which would imply that strictly less than
1
of the minimizing player’s sequences of distributions can decrease
v^{\prime}
by more than
\epsilon
.
Or, more directly, it would imply that there are no sequences the minimizing player can send to decrease
v^{\prime}
by more than
\epsilon
.
We will show that choosing
t_{k}=m^{2k}/2\epsilon^{2}
suffices.
By plugging in the definitions of
t_{k}
and
t_{k-1}
,
Eq.

4
becomes

\exp\left(-m^{2k}\right)<\frac{\epsilon^{k}}{m^{k(k-1)}}2^{\frac{k}{2}-\frac{km^{2k-1}}{4\epsilon^{2}}}\iff\exp\left(-m^{2k}\right)\frac{m^{k(k-1)}}{\epsilon^{k}}2^{\frac{km^{2k-1}}{4\epsilon^{2}}-\frac{k}{2}}<1.

(5)

We show that the inequality in
Eq.

5
holds, which proves that our setting of
t_{k}
is correct.

\displaystyle\exp\left(-m^{2k}\right)\frac{m^{k(k-1)}}{\epsilon^{k}}2^{\frac{km^{2k-1}}{4\epsilon^{2}}-\frac{k}{2}}

\displaystyle<\exp\left(-m^{2k}\right)m^{k^{2}}2^{\frac{km^{2k-1}}{4\epsilon^{2}}-\frac{k}{2}}

\displaystyle<m^{k^{2}}2^{\frac{km^{2k-1}}{4\epsilon^{2}}-\frac{k}{2}-m^{2k}}

\displaystyle=m^{k^{2}}2^{m^{2k-1}\left(\frac{k}{4\epsilon^{2}}-\frac{k}{2m^{2k-1}}-m\right)}

\displaystyle<m^{k^{2}}2^{-m^{2k-1}}

\displaystyle<1.

The first inequality holds because
m^{k}>\epsilon^{-k}
for constant
\epsilon>0
. The second-to-last inequality holds because
\left(\frac{k}{4\epsilon^{2}}-\frac{k}{2m^{2k-1}}-m\right)<-1
for constant
\epsilon>0
.

We conclude that
v^{\prime\prime}\geq v^{\prime}-\epsilon\geq v-k\epsilon
, which completes the proof.
∎

We can now prove that
\mathsf{DistributionPH}=\mathsf{PH}
.

Proof of
Theorem

6.1
.

\mathsf{PH}\subseteq\mathsf{DistributionPH}
follows from the proof that
\mathsf{PH}\subseteq\mathsf{QPH}
.
This only achieves containment in
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
with constant promise gap, and it puts the
k
-th level of
\mathsf{PH}
in some higher level of
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
(see
Theorem

5.1
for more detail).

To show
\mathsf{DistributionPH}\subseteq\mathsf{PH}
, we use
Lemma

6.4
.
Set
\epsilon<\frac{1}{12k}
.
For
\mathsf{Distribution\Sigma_{k}}
,
Lemma

6.4
implies that

\max_{\rho_{1}\in U_{t_{k}}}\min_{\rho_{2}\in U_{t_{k-1}}}\max_{\rho_{3}\in U_{t_{k-3}}}\dots\operatorname*{Q^{k}}_{\rho_{k}\in U_{t_{1}}}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[M(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k})\right]\in[v-k\epsilon,v+k\epsilon]\subseteq\left[v-\frac{1}{12},v+\frac{1}{12}\right].

Given the
\mathsf{Distribution\Sigma_{k}}
promise gap of
\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3}
, this modified game has a promise gap of
\frac{7}{12},\frac{5}{12}
.

We simulate this in
\mathsf{PH}
as follows. To send the distribution
\rho_{i}
, the prover sends every string in the support of
\rho_{i}
, which is only
\poly(n)
many bits by
Lemma

6.4
. The verifier can then take the list of strings and sample one uniformly at random.
This completes the proof since
\mathsf{PH}
can simulate randomness
[
Sip83
,
Lau83
]
.
∎

One can also define
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
in the same way, and it follows from
Theorem

6.1
that this class is equal to
\mathsf{QCPH}
.

Corollary 6.5
.

\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}=\mathsf{QCPH}
.

The
only
difference between
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
and
\mathsf{pureQPH}
is that the former involves proofs that are classical distributions over bit strings and the latter involves proofs that are quantum superpositions over bit strings.

\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}=\mathsf{QCPH}
is in the counting hierarchy
[
GSS+22
]
, while the best known upper bound for
\mathsf{pureQPH}
is
\mathsf{EXP}^{\mathsf{PP}}

[
AGKR
]
and it contains
\mathsf{QMA(2)}
and
\mathsf{QPH}
.
The conceptual takeaway is that it is only the quantum superposition in the proofs that gives the quantum hierarchies more computational power.

We also remark that if one allows the distributions in
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
and
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
to be correlated, then the techniques in
Lemma

4.1
can be used to collapse the resulting hierarchies to the second level. The correlated version of
\mathsf{DistributionPH}
collapses to
\mathsf{S_{2}P}
.
The correlated version of
\mathsf{DistributionQCPH}
collapses to a quantum-classical version of
\mathsf{QRG(1)}
, which, to our knowledge, has never been studied.

7
Open Problems

It is well-known that
\mathsf{PH}
can equivalently be defined via oracle Turing machines.
This suggests oracular definitions of quantum polynomial hierarchies, such as
\mathsf{QMAH}
discussed in
Section

1.3
.
One could similarly define
\mathsf{\mathsf{QCMAH}}
as
\mathsf{QCMA}^{\mathsf{QCMA}^{\mathsf{QCMA}^{\ldots}}}
and
\mathsf{QMA(2)H}
as
\mathsf{QMA(2)}^{\mathsf{QMA(2)}^{\mathsf{QMA(2)}^{\ldots}}}
.
We ask how these oracular hierarchies compare to the quantifier-based ones.

Question 7.1
.

Does
\mathsf{QEPH}=\mathsf{QMAH}
?
\mathsf{QPH}=\mathsf{QMA(2)H}
?
\mathsf{QCPH}=\mathsf{QCMAH}
?

It is unclear if these hierarchies are equal, as in the classical world, or if one version would be stronger than the other.
One immediate obstacle is the fact that
\mathsf{QEPH}
and
\mathsf{QPH}
are quantifying over quantum states, so perhaps it is easier to begin with
\mathsf{QCPH}
, which still quantifies over classical bits.
Alas, it is still unclear if an oracle machine definition of
\mathsf{QCPH}
would be equal to a quantifier definition, since, in the oracular case, queries can be made in superposition.

Answering
7.1
could yield progress towards characterizing
\mathsf{QRG(1)}
.
Jain and Watrous showed that
\mathsf{QRG(1)}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}
in 2009
[
JW09
]
, and, since then, no improved upper bounds have been proven despite effort
[
GW23
]
.
Our work shows that
\mathsf{QRG(1)}=\mathsf{QEPH}
, and, if one can show
\mathsf{QEPH}\subseteq\mathsf{QMAH}
, then that would imply
\mathsf{QRG(1)}\subseteq\mathsf{CH}
, since
[
Vin18
]
showed that
\mathsf{QMAH}\subseteq\mathsf{CH}
.

More broadly, proving better upper or lower bounds on the quantum polynomial hierarchies and finding more connections to other parts of complexity theory are important directions for future work.
For example, does any level of
\mathsf{QPH}
contain
\mathsf{PSPACE}
?
Can one improve the containment
\mathsf{QPH}\subseteq\mathsf{EXP}^{\mathsf{PP}}
?
Or, how can these hierarchies be used to better understand the relationships between
\mathsf{QCMA}
,
\mathsf{QMA}
, and
\mathsf{QMA(2)}
?
