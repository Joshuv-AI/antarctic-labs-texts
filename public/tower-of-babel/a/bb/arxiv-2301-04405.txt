1.
Introduction

Given a Riemannian locally symmetric space
X=\Gamma\backslash S
, it is a classical analytic problem to give pointwise bounds for (
L^{2}
-normalized) eigenfunctions
F
of the algebra of invariant differential operators
\mathcal{D}(S)
, uniformly in
S
in terms of the Laplace eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
of
F
. If
X
is compact, then Sarnak
[
Sar
]
proved the baseline bound

(1)

\|F\|_{\infty}\ll_{X}\lambda_{F}^{\frac{\dim(X)-\operatorname{rk}(X)}{4}}.

The exponent on the right-hand side is known to be sharp in general. It is also known in some special cases that if
X
is not compact, then
\|F\|_{\infty}
might be considerably larger (see
[
BT20
]
), but (
1
) still holds if
F
is retsriced to compact subsets of
X
. The sup-norm problem in the theory of automorphic forms asks if the exponent of (
1
) can be strengthened if
X
is an arithmetic manifold and
F
is an eigenfunction not only of
\mathcal{D}(S)
but of the full Hecke algebra of
X
.

An important motivation comes from quantum mechanics. Classical mechanics interprets a freely moving particle as a geodesic flow. The quantum mechanical interpretation of the same object is an
L^{2}
-normalized linear combination of eigenstates. Since the geodesic flow is ergodic with respect to the Liouville measure on the tangent space, the correspondence principle of quantum mechanics suggests that the masses of the eigenstates reproduce the invariant measure in the high-energy limit. Bounds on the sup-norm (or in general, the
L^{p}
norm for any
p>2
) of eigenstates control their mass concentration, and hence are in connection with the quantum unique ergodicity conjecture of Rudnick and Sarnak
[
RS94
]
. Other important connections of sup-norm bounds are towards the multiplicity problem, nodal domains of automorphic forms and bounds for
L
-functions, see e.g.
[
Sar
]
,
[
GRS13
]
,
[
BH10
]
.

In improving (
1
), there are (at least) two independent directions of research: one is to find as strong power-savings as possible among special circumstances, typically in rank one (see e.g.
[
IS95
]
and
[
BHMM20
]
), the other one is to find any power-saving in higher rank or among as general circumstances as possible (see e.g.
[
BP16
]
,
[
BM15
]
,
[
BM16
]
). We pick up the thread in the second theme, where the current limitation of our knowledge is an unpublished a manuscipt
[
Mar
]
, which proves power-saving for a wide class of symmetric spaces, more specifically, for arithmetic quotients of quasi-split groups with the exception of the type
\mathrm{SU}(n,n-1)
. (We note that even though
[
Mar
]
is not peer-reviewed, it is widely accepted in the community as being correct.)

In these notes, we introduce a new method, based and improved on that of
[
BM16
]
, to tackle the sup-norm problem. As a test application, we prove a saving over (
1
) in the case of the locally symmetric space

X:=\Gamma\backslash G/K,\qquad\Gamma:=\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i]),\qquad G:=\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad K:=\mathrm{PU}_{n}.

Admittedly, our result follows from the main result of
[
Mar
]
(see especially
[
Mar
, Corollary 1.4]
), but we strongly believe that the novelty of the method deserves attention, and might have the potential to address the type
\mathrm{SU}(n,n-1)
, the exceptional case in Marshall’s work.

All along the paper, we think of
n\geqslant 2
as being fixed, in particular, all implied constants below are allowed to depend on
n
.

Before stating our main result, we fix some notations. Since it is convenient to work with matrices instead of their projectivization, we shall realize elements of
G
as the rightmost nonzero entry of the bottom row is
1
. We will also talk about points of
X
or
G/K
as matrices, by which we mean any matrix which represents them. Let
A
stand for the diagonal subgroup of
G
consisting of positive real entries and let
N
be the upper-triangular unipotent subgroup. Then the Iwasawa decomposition reads as
G=NAK
. We write
\mathfrak{a}
for the Lie algebra of
A
,
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
for its dual and
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
for the complexification of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. Fixing bases in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
, we may view them as appropriate subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, respectively. Let
W
stand for the Weyl group,
\Sigma
for the set of roots and
\Sigma^{+}
for the set of positive roots corresponding to
N
. For
\alpha\in\Sigma
, let
m(\alpha)
be the real dimension of the corresponding root space. For
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, define

D(\lambda):=\prod_{\alpha\in\Sigma^{+}}(1+|\langle\alpha,\lambda\rangle|)^{m(\alpha)},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is induced by the Killing form.

In our special situation, define, for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, the function
e_{j}
on
\mathfrak{a}
as
e_{j}(\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))=a_{j}
, and then a set of positive roots is given by
e_{j}-e_{k}
with
1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n
. The corresponding root space is spanned by the matrices
E_{jk}
and
iE_{jk}
, where
E_{jk}
is the matrix which
1
at position
j,k
and otherwise zero, hence
m(e_{j}-e_{k})=2
.

A Hecke–Maaß cusp form
\phi
on
X
comes with archimedean Langlands parameters
\mu_{\phi}=(\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{n})\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}/W\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}/W
such that
\mu_{1}+\dotsc+\mu_{n}=0
and
\{\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{n}\}=\{\overline{\mu_{1}},\dotsc,\overline{\mu_{n}}\}
.
In our parametrization,
(\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
corresponds to the tempered spectrum, and we have that
\Im\mu_{1},\dotsc,\Im\mu_{n}=O(1)
. Let
\mu_{\phi}^{*}
stand for
\Re\mu_{\phi}
. Since for the Laplace eigenvalue
\lambda_{\phi}
of
\phi
,

\lambda_{\phi}\asymp 1+\|\mu_{1}\|^{2}+\dotsc+\|\mu_{n}\|^{2}

holds, we have

D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})\ll\lambda_{\phi}^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}.

The main result of this paper is the following.

Theorem 1
.

For any
n\geqslant 2
, there exists some
\delta=\delta(n)>0
with the following property. For any
L^{2}
-normalized Hecke–Maaß cusp form
\phi
on
X
and any compact
\Omega\subset X
,

\|\phi|_{\Omega}\|_{\infty}\ll_{\Omega}D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta}.

In particular,

\|\phi|_{\Omega}\|_{\infty}\ll_{\Omega}\lambda_{\phi}^{\frac{n(n-1)}{4}(1-2\delta)}.

Since
n(n-1)/4=(\dim(X)-\operatorname{rk}(X))/4
, this is a saving over (
1
). We note that the case
n=2
was solved in
[
BHM16
]
.

Our method closely follows the one introduced in
[
BM16
]
, however, the counting problem in this situation is more challenging (because of the too large maximal compact subgroup of
G
, as it will be briefly exposed below). This requires an improvement in the counting techniques, which is the heart of this paper (vaguely speaking, a combination of Lemma
4
and Lemma
8
). As a by-product, there is no need of Linnik type theorems about small primes in arithmetic progressions (i.e. zero repulsion of
L
-functions) any more.

We remark that with some work, the jungle of
O(1)
’s below (particularly in Section
4
) can be made explicit, hence an explicit subconvexity saving is available, see the work
[
Gil20
]
over the rational field (in fact, now it is easier, since the reference to Linnik type theorems is removed). We also note that the implied constant (which depends on
\Omega
) can be also made fully explicit, so the method in principle is effective, e.g. we do not need any reference to Siegel zeroes.

2.
Reduction to a counting problem

In this section, we reduce the problem to a matrix counting problem, following the lines of
[
BM15
]
and
[
BM16
]
, where
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{PO}_{n}
was treated. Since the spherical Hecke algebra is isomorphic in that case to ours at the archimedean and all relevant non-archimedean places, almost everything follows verbatim, so we give only a brief exposition, with some emphasis on the small difference coming from the fact that the maximal compact subgroup is the orthogonal group in the real case and the unitary group in the complex case.

Let
f_{\mu_{\phi}}:K\backslash G/K\to\mathbb{C}
be the spherical function constructed in
[
BM16
, pp.1276–1277]
(here we utilize that
A,\mathfrak{a}^{*},\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
are the same for
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
). Denoting by
\tilde{f}_{\mu_{\phi}}
its spherical transform, for any
x,y\in G
, we have the pre-trace formula

(2)

\int\tilde{f}_{\mu_{\phi}}(\mu_{\varpi})\overline{F_{\varpi}(x)}F_{\varpi}(y)\ d\varpi=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}f_{\mu_{\phi}}(x^{-1}\gamma y),

where the integral on the left is meant over the full spectrum of
\Gamma\backslash G
.

The idea of amplification in the current setup can be summarized as follows. Assume we want to estimate our form
\phi
at a point
g\in\Omega
. Then in (
2
), we set
x:=g
and we take
y
of the form
\gamma g
, where
\gamma
runs through right coset representatives corresponding to certain Hecke operators. An appropriate weighted sum of these results in a positive operator on
L^{2}(X)
, hence from the left-hand side, all but the one term corresponding to
F_{\varpi}=\phi
can be dropped (for this positivity argument, see also
[
BHMM20
, Section 3]
). All in all, we arrive at a bound of the form

C|\phi(g)|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}}f_{\mu_{\phi}}(g^{-1}\gamma g)

with
\Gamma^{\prime}
being a finite set of matrices arising from the Hecke operators utilized, and
C
is a positive constant coming from
\tilde{f}_{\mu_{\phi}}
and the corresponding Hecke eigenvalues. Then, using that
f_{\mu_{\phi}}
decays controllably away from
K
(see
[
BP16
, Theorem 2]
), to bound
|\phi(g)|
, we essentially have to count those
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}
for which
g^{-1}\gamma g
is close to
K
.

To be a little more concrete, let
L\geqslant 2
be a parameter, and assume that
\mathcal{P}
is a set of primes
\pi
lying above distinct split rational primes such that
L\leqslant N(\pi)<2L
with
N
standing for the norm. Then the discussion of
[
BM15
, Sections 4, 6]
(which in fact uses
[
BP16
, Theorem 2]
, and see also
[
BM16
, Section 2]
) leads to, for any number
M\geqslant 1
,

(3)

|\phi(g)|^{2}\ll_{M,\Omega}D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}}+D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})^{-\kappa}L^{K}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(\#\mathcal{P})^{2}}\sum_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{\#S(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\prime\nu},M)}{L^{\nu(n-1)}}\right),\qquad g\in\Omega,

where
K
is a fixed number depending only on
M
and
n
,
\kappa>0
is also a fixed number depending only on
n
; and

(4)

\begin{split}S(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\prime\nu},M):=\Bigl\{&\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i])\cdot\operatorname{diag}(1,\pi^{\nu},\dotsc,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\nu}\pi^{\prime\nu})\cdot\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i]):\\
&\||\det\gamma|^{-\frac{2}{n}}\cdot\gamma^{*}Q\gamma-Q\|_{\infty}\leqslant\max\left(N(\pi)^{-M},N(\pi^{\prime})^{-M}\right)\Bigr\}\end{split}

with
Q:=|\det g|^{2/n}\cdot(g^{*})^{-1}g^{-1}
, and by
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
applied to a matrix, we mean its largest entry in absolute value. Note that in
[
BM15
]
and
[
BM16
]
, the transpose of
\gamma
and
g
are taken instead of their adjoint.

By the prime number theorem, we can choose
\mathcal{P}
to satisfy
\#\mathcal{P}\gg_{\varepsilon}L^{1-\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore, if we were able to prove that

\#S(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\prime\nu},M)\ll_{\Omega,M}L^{\nu(n-1)-\eta}

holds for every
1\leqslant\nu\leqslant n
with some
\eta>0
, then (
3
) would imply Theorem
1
by setting
L
to be a very small positive power of
D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})
. We prove it in a weaker form, which still suffices for Theorem
1
.

Before formulating this weaker statement, we fix some notation. Introduce the notation
S_{n}
for the real vector space of self-adjoint matrices in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
P_{n}\subset S_{n}
for the open convex cone of positive definite matrices. Given a compact set
\Omega\subset X
as in Theorem
1
, we introduce

\Omega^{\prime}:=\left\{\left[\frac{1}{2},2\right]\cdot|\det g|^{\frac{2}{n}}\cdot(g^{*})^{-1}g^{-1}:g\in\Omega\right\}.

Then
\Omega^{\prime}
is a compact subset of
P_{n}
in the subspace topology.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. There exist positive numbers
\alpha(\varepsilon)\geqslant 1
and
M(\varepsilon)\geqslant 1
(both depending only on
n
and
\varepsilon
) with the following properties. For any compact
\Omega\subset X
, there exists a constant
L(\Omega)\geqslant 2
(depending only on
n
,
\alpha(\varepsilon)
,
M(\varepsilon)
and
\Omega
) such that the following holds. For any
L_{0}\geqslant L(\Omega)
, there exists some
L_{0}\leqslant L\leqslant L_{0}^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}
such that for any prime
\pi
lying above a split rational prime and satisfying
L\leqslant N(\pi)<2L
, we have

(5)

\#S(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\nu},M(\varepsilon))\ll_{\Omega,\varepsilon}L^{\nu(n-1)+\varepsilon},\qquad Q\in\Omega^{\prime},1\leqslant\nu\leqslant n;

and for any two distinct primes
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
lying above distinct split rational primes, we have

(6)

\#S(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\prime\nu},M(\varepsilon))=0,\qquad Q\in\Omega^{\prime},1\leqslant\nu\leqslant n.

This still suffices for the proof of Theorem
1
. Indeed, we apply Proposition
1
with any
0<\varepsilon<1/2
. There are implied numbers
\alpha:=\alpha(\varepsilon)\geqslant 1
and
M:=M(\varepsilon)\geqslant 1
, and together with the further input
\Omega
, one more number
L(\Omega)
by Proposition
1
. Then take
L_{0}:=D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})^{\omega}
, where
\omega>0
is a fixed constant to be specified later. By Proposition
1
, if
L_{0}\geqslant L(\Omega)
, we can find some with
D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})^{\omega}\leqslant L\leqslant D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})^{\alpha\omega}
such that the countings (
5
) and (
6
) hold for any primes
L\leqslant N(\pi),N(\pi^{\prime})<2L
. Then in every term of (
3
), we get a power-saving, as soon as
\omega>0
in the beginning is chosen sufficiently small. Now we return to the condition
L_{0}\geqslant L(\Omega)
. Apart from a finite set of Hecke–Maaß cusp forms depending only on
\Omega
, this is indeed satisfied by
L_{0}=D(\mu_{\phi}^{*})^{\omega}
. The finitely many exceptional forms are treated then by adjusting the implied constant (note that the implied constant might depend on
\Omega
, only the saving in the exponent must be absolute).

The rest of the paper is hence devoted to the proof of Proposition
1
.

We conclude this section by illustrating why this counting problem is harder for
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i])
than for
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
in the special case when
Q
is the unit matrix. Then in
S(\dotsc)
, we have a condition on the Smith normal form which is of the same complexity in both cases. However, the other condition is that
\gamma
is projectively equivalent to an orthonormal matrix, which intuitively happens more often over
\mathbb{C}
than over
\mathbb{R}
, since

\dim(\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))=2n^{2}-2,\qquad\dim(\mathrm{PU}_{n})=n^{2}-1=\frac{\dim(\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))}{2},

while

\dim(\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))=n^{2}-1,\qquad\dim(\mathrm{PO}_{n})=\frac{n^{2}-n}{2}<\frac{\dim(\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))}{2},

i.e.
\mathrm{PU}_{n}
is a “thicker” subgroup of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
than
\mathrm{PO}_{n}
of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

3.
Counting techniques in a special case

In this section, we introduce counting techniques for a situation which is very special in many different aspects. First, we will assume that
Q
is diagonal, moreover, its entries belong to the base field
\mathbb{Q}(i)
. Then these together imply that the diagonal entries are rational, i.e.
Q=\operatorname{diag}(q_{1},\dotsc,q_{n})
with
q_{1},\dotsc,q_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then
q_{j}\asymp_{\Omega}1
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, and we also assume that the numerator and the denominator of
q_{j}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
(in their simplest form) are both coprime to
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
. Our final simplification is that we allow no error term in (
4
), which we will denote by writing
\infty
in place of
M
.

Our convention will be that vectors, i.e. elements of
\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n},\mathbb{C}^{n}
, etc. are always meant as column vectors. We also introduce the notation
v_{\pi}(q)
, for any prime
\pi\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
and any
q\in\mathbb{Q}(i)
, which denotes the
\pi
-valuation of
q
. Occasionally, we may use this notation for vectors or matrices with entries from
\mathbb{Q}(i)
, then it means the minimal
\pi
-adic valuation attained by the entries.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\pi\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
be a prime lying above a split rational prime
p=\pi\overline{\pi}
,
\rho\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
A\in\mathbb{Q}(i)^{n\times n}
be a self-adjoint matrix such that
v_{\pi}(A)\geqslant 0
. Let
x=(\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{n})^{t},y=(\upsilon_{1},\dotsc,\upsilon_{n})^{t}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
be vectors satisfying
v_{\pi}(x)=v_{\pi}(y)=0
such that for any
1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n
,
\pi^{\rho}\mid(\xi_{j}\upsilon_{k}-\xi_{k}\upsilon_{j})
. Then the following statements hold.

(a)

For some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
coprime to
p
, we have
y\equiv ax\bmod\pi^{\rho}
, and this
a
is well-defined modulo
p^{\rho}
.

(b)

Choosing
a\in\mathbb{Z}
with this property in (
a
), there also exists a
b\in\mathbb{Z}
(unique modulo
p^{\rho}
) with
b\equiv ai\bmod\pi^{\rho}
, so we further have

(7)

2x^{*}Ay\equiv(a-bi)x^{*}Ax+(a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}i)y^{*}Ay\qquad\bmod p^{\rho},

where
a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
(resp.
b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
) stands for the multiplicative inverse of
a
(resp. of
b
) modulo
p^{\rho}
.

(c)

We have
\pi^{\rho}\mid a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}i
and
\overline{\pi}^{\rho}\mid a-bi
for the integers
a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
defined above.

Proof.

This is a variant of
[
BM16
, Lemma 3]
, but the proof in this case is a little more computational. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\pi\nmid\xi_{n}
. Then
\pi\nmid\upsilon_{n}
, for if not, then for some
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
,
\pi\nmid\upsilon_{j}
, and then
\pi\nmid(\xi_{n}\upsilon_{j}-\xi_{j}\upsilon_{n})
. Then
a\equiv\upsilon_{n}\xi_{n}^{-1}\bmod\pi^{\rho}
does the job, since
\upsilon_{j}\equiv\xi_{j}\upsilon_{n}\xi_{n}^{-1}\equiv a\xi_{j}\bmod\pi^{\rho}
for any
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
. Also,
a
can be chosen in
\mathbb{Z}
uniquely modulo
p^{\rho}
since
0,1,\dots,p^{\rho}-1
is a set of representatives modulo
\pi^{\rho}
(i.e. we have the isomorphism
\mathbb{Z}/(p^{\rho})\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\pi^{\rho})
). The proof of (
a
) is complete.

As for (
b
), fix any representative
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and let
b\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
b\equiv ai\bmod\pi^{\rho}
. Then
\pi^{\rho}
divides all the entries in
y-ax
whence
\overline{\pi}^{\rho}
divides all the entries in
(y-ax)^{*}
. So we deduce

0\equiv(y-ax)^{*}A(y-ax)=y^{*}Ay+a^{2}x^{*}Ax-ax^{*}Ay-ay^{*}Ax\qquad\bmod p^{\rho}=(\pi\overline{\pi})^{\rho}

hence

(8)

x^{*}Ay+y^{*}Ax\equiv ax^{*}Ax+a^{\prime}y^{*}Ay\qquad\bmod p^{\rho}

where
a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
is a multiplicative inverse of
a
modulo
p^{\rho}
. Similarly,
\pi^{\rho}
divides all the entries in
iy-bx
and
\overline{\pi}^{\rho}
divides all the entries in
(iy-bx)^{*}
as
ai\equiv b\bmod\pi^{\rho}
. So we compute

0\equiv(iy-bx)^{*}A(iy-bx)=y^{*}Ay+b^{2}x^{*}Ax-bix^{*}Ay+biy^{*}Ax\qquad\bmod p^{\rho}

whence

(9)

x^{*}Ay-y^{*}Ax\equiv-bix^{*}Ax-b^{\prime}iy^{*}Ay\qquad\bmod p^{\rho}

where
b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
is a multiplicative inverse of
b
modulo
p^{\rho}
. The statement follows by adding equations (
8
) and (
9
).

For part (
c
), we compute
\pi^{\rho}\mid i(b-ai)=ib+a
whence
\overline{\pi}^{\rho}\mid\overline{a+bi}=a-bi
. Similarly,
\pi^{\rho}\mid a^{\prime}b^{\prime}(b-ai)\equiv a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}i
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
A\in\Omega^{\prime}
, and assume that
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
are linearly independent vectors for some
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
. Then for any real number
\beta\geqslant 2
,

\#\{y\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}:y^{*}Ay=\beta^{2},\text{ and }x_{j}^{*}Ay=0\text{ for any }j=1,\dotsc,k\}\ll_{\Omega,\varepsilon}\beta^{2(n-k-1)+\varepsilon}

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

See
[
BM15
, Corollary 5.3]
.
∎

We slightly extend the notation
S(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\prime\nu},\infty)
for
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\nmid m\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
as

\begin{split}S_{m}(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\prime\nu},\infty):=\Bigl\{\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i]_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}})&\cdot\operatorname{diag}(1,\pi^{\nu},\dotsc,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\nu}\pi^{\prime\nu})\cdot\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i]_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}):\\
&m\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i]),|\det\gamma|^{-\frac{2}{n}}\cdot\gamma^{*}Q\gamma=Q\Bigr\},\end{split}

where by
\mathbb{Z}[i]_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}
, we mean the ring of elements
a\in\mathbb{Q}(i)
which satisfy
v_{\pi}(a),v_{\pi^{\prime}}(a)\geqslant 0
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\pi\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
be a prime lying above a split rational prime, and
\pi\nmid m\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
. Let
Q=\operatorname{diag}(q_{1},\dotsc,q_{n})
with
q_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
q_{j}\asymp_{\Omega}1
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
. Then for any
1\leqslant\nu\leqslant n
,

\#S_{m}(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\nu},\infty)\ll_{\Omega,\varepsilon}|m|^{2n^{2}-2+\varepsilon}\operatorname{den}(Q)^{\frac{(2n-1)(n-1)}{2}}N(\pi)^{\nu(n-1)+\varepsilon}.

for any
\varepsilon>0

denotes the least common multiple of the denominator of the diagonal entries of

(written in simplest form).

Proof.

By the definition of
S_{m}(Q,\pi^{\nu},\pi^{\nu},\infty)
, any matrix counted there must have a column not completely divisible by
\pi
, and we may assume that it is the first column
\gamma_{1}
. Since

, we have, by Lemma

that the number of possible

O_{\varepsilon}(|m|^{2(n-1)+\varepsilon}N(\pi)^{\nu(n-1)+\varepsilon})
. Now it suffices to prove that fixing
\gamma_{1}
, there are
O_{\Omega}(m^{2n(n-1)}\operatorname{den}(Q)^{(2n-1)(n-1)/2})
ways to finish the matrix.

We group the possible second columns
\gamma_{2}
according to their
\pi
-valuation. When
v_{\pi}(\gamma_{2})\geqslant\nu
, then
m\gamma_{2}/\pi^{\nu}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
and
\|m\gamma_{2}/\pi^{\nu}\|\asymp_{\Omega}\|m\gamma_{2}/\pi^{\nu}\|_{Q}=|m|
, hence there are
O_{\Omega}(|m|^{2n})
such choices for such a
\gamma_{2}
. Now fix

, and then it suffices prove that the number of possible
\gamma_{2}
’s satisfying

is
O_{\Omega}(|m|^{2n}\operatorname{den}(Q)^{(2n-1)/2})
, since the same can be repeated for all further columns.

Let

be second columns after the first column
\gamma_{1}
such that
v_{\pi}(x)=v_{\pi}(y)=\mu
. Consider then the vectors

and

. By Lemma
1
(

x^{\prime}
and

are both multiples of
m\gamma_{1}
modulo
\pi^{\nu-\mu}
, and then by transitivity, multiples of each other modulo
\pi^{\mu-\nu}
. Introduce then
Q^{\prime}:=\operatorname{den}(Q)Q
. With this notation, since
x^{\prime*}Q^{\prime}x^{\prime}=y^{\prime*}Q^{\prime}y^{\prime}=|m|^{2}q_{2}\operatorname{den}(Q)\pi^{\nu-\mu}\overline{\pi}^{\nu-\mu}=|m|^{2}q_{2}\operatorname{den}(Q)N(\pi)^{\nu-\mu}
, (

is divisible by

. On the other hand, viewing

as

, we obtain, for the

and

\sphericalangle_{Q}(x,y)=\arccos\frac{\Re(x^{*}Qy)}{\sqrt{(x^{*}Qx)(y^{*}Qy)}}=\arccos\frac{\Re(x^{\prime*}Q^{\prime}y^{\prime})}{\sqrt{(x^{\prime*}Q^{\prime}x^{\prime})(y^{\prime*}Q^{\prime}y^{\prime})}}=\arccos\frac{\Re(x^{\prime*}Q^{\prime}y^{\prime})}{|m|^{2}q_{2}\operatorname{den}(Q)N(\pi)^{\mu-\nu}}.

Since the numerator in the rightmost expression is divisible by

, as

\sphericalangle_{Q}(x,y)=\arccos\left(1-\frac{\ell}{|m|^{2}q_{2}\operatorname{den}(Q)}\right)\gg_{\Omega}|m|^{-1}\operatorname{den}(Q)^{-1/2},\qquad\text{if $\ell\neq 0$, i.e. $x\neq y$}.

This means that the number of possible second columns is
O_{\Omega}(|m|^{2n-1}\operatorname{den}(Q)^{(2n-1)/2})
(this is rather elementary, see also

). The proof is complete.
