Stein’s method for Conditional Central Limit Theorem
Partha S. Deyabel=e1, mark]psdey@illinois.edu, Grigory Terlovabel=e2, mark]gterlov2@illinois.edu

Abstract
In the seventies, Charles Stein revolutionized the way of proving the Central Limit Theorem by introducing a method that utilizes a characterization equation for Gaussian distribution. In the last fifty years, much research has been done to adapt and strengthen this method to a variety of different settings and other limiting distributions. However, it has not been yet extended to study conditional convergences. In this article, we develop a novel approach using Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs to find a rate of convergence in the Conditional Central Limit Theorem of the form ( X n ∣ Y n = k ) (X_{n}\mid Y_{n}=k) , where ( X n , Y n ) (X_{n},Y_{n}) are asymptotically jointly Gaussian, and extend this result to a multivariate version. We apply our general result to several concrete examples, including pattern count in a random binary sequence and subgraph count in Erdős-Rényi random graph.

Introduction

One of the oldest and most outstanding results in Probability theory is the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), which in classical form states that the sum of i.i.d. finite variance random variables, appropriately centered and scaled, converges to the standard normal distribution asymptotically. There are several generalizations and variations, such as Multivariate CLT, Martingale CLT, Local Limit Theorem (LLT), and Conditional CLT (CCLT), among others; however, the essence is the same as in the simplest classical CLT. Though the classical proof of CLT uses characteristic functions and their characterization of distributions, over the last century, various methods, such as the moment method and Lindeberg’s technique, have been developed to prove CLT even in highly dependent structures. State of the art in establishing CLT and getting a convergence rate is Stein’s method, which we will discuss in further detail in Section
1.2
. Researchers have applied Stein’s method to prove and find a rate of convergence in Multivariate CLT
[
3
,
12
,
5
,
28
,
29
,
41
,
44
]
, Martingale CLT
[
45
]
, Local Limit theorem
[
5
,
42
]
, and in other non-Gaussian limit theorems (see
[
1
,
11
,
13
,
14
,
35
]
among many others). Stein’s method has also been applied to prove concentration inequalities
[
8
,
10
,
24
,
26
]
, moderate deviation results
[
15
]
, and strong coupling
[
9
]
. For a more detailed overview of the topic, we refer to the books
[
16
,
20
]
and the survey
[
47
]
. However, to our knowledge, very little is known about proving and getting a convergence rate in CCLT of the form
X
given
Y=y
(see
[
7
,
19
,
32
,
50
]
), especially in structures with dependence.

The main focus of this article is to develop Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs to prove and get an explicit rate of convergence in Conditional Central Limit Theorems of the form
X_{n}
given
Y_{n}=k
. Our focus will be on the case where the random vector
(X_{n},Y_{n})
converges to a multivariate normal distribution as
n
tends to infinity, and
Y_{n}
is a discrete random variable. We believe that this is the first application of Stein’s method in proving CCLT and one of the first works that study the explicit rate of convergence in CCLT. In the rest of this section, we briefly discuss literature on CCLT and Stein’s method. We present the main idea behind the exchangeable pair approach on which our result is based. We state our main result in full generality in Section

1.1
Conditional Central Limit Theorem

It is common in Probability theory and Statistics to study conditional convergence of random variables. In general, the study of conditional probabilities played an important role in shaping the field as we know it and related to notions such as Markov chains and martingales. Even though Conditional Central Limit Theorem (CCLT) has been studied in the last forty years, it has been mostly looked at on particular examples or under specific assumptions. The strongest result is known for the sum of a stationary sequence
\Sigma_{i=1}^{n}(X\circ T^{i}),

{n\geqslant 1}
conditioned on a non-decreasing filtration
\mathcal{M}_{i}:=T^{-i}(\mathcal{M}_{0})
with a bi-measurable probability measure-preserving map
T
. The necessary and sufficient conditions for such CCLT were obtained by Dedecker and Merlevède in
[
19
]
. In
[
6
, Theorem 4]
, Bolthausen proved CCLT with explicit rate of convergence in Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance for a positively recurrent Markov chain with finite absolute third moment conditioned on the time of the
n^{\textrm{th}}
return to
0
. This result was recently extended by Guo and Peterson
[
31
, Theorem 4.2]
to a CCLT for sums of i.i.d. sequence of random vectors
(X_{i},Y_{i})
with
\E(|X_{1}|+|Y_{1}|)^{2+\delta}<\infty
conditioned on
\sum Y_{i}=k
. Another general CCLT was proved by Holst in
[
32
]
. In that work, he also considered an i.i.d. sequence of random vectors
(X_{i},Y_{i})
. Assuming that
\sum Y_{i}
is a sufficient statistic and a certain multivariate CLT holds, he derived CCLT for

(\sum{X}_{i}\mid\sum{Y}_{i}=k)
,
after appropriate centering and scaling. In all of the relevant results from
[
6
,
31
,
32
]
, the
authors used variations of the method of characteristic functions and thus crucially relied on the independence among the random variables.
Other settings in the literature include CCLT for conditionally independent random variables, which reduces to the classical setting see
[
7
,
50
]
, among others.

All of the above results use independence in one way or the other, which could lead one to believe that asymptotic independence and joint convergence to a multivariate normal are sufficient for CCLT to hold. However, the following artificial, but still insightful, example shows that it is not the case. Let
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
be centered Binomial
(n,1/2)
independent random variables. Define

\displaystyle\bigl(\widehat{X}_{n},\widehat{Y}_{n}\bigr)=\begin{cases}(X_{n},Y_{n})&\text{w.~p.}\quad 1-\alpha_{n}\\
(U,\delta_{0})&\text{w.~p.}\quad\alpha_{n},\end{cases}

where
n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\ll\alpha_{n}\ll 1
,
\delta_{0}
is Dirac measure at
0
, and
U
has arbitrary distribution. By Local Limit Theorem we have
\pr(Y_{n}=0)\approx n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\ll\alpha_{n}
and
(\widehat{X}_{n}\mid\widehat{Y}_{n}=0)\Rightarrow U
, even though
\widehat{X}_{n}
and
\widehat{Y}_{n}
are asymptotically independent and
(\widehat{X}_{n},\widehat{Y}_{n})
converges to the two dimensional standard normal distribution. Therefore a more careful characterisation of dependency is needed to have even heuristic understanding of when to expect CCLT to hold in a general setting.

1.2
Stein’s Method

Over the last few decades, Stein’s methods have become one of the essential tools to prove and get a rate of convergence in Central Limit Theorems for sums of dependent random variables. It was first introduced by Charles Stein in 1972
[
48
]
, who combined Gaussian Integration by parts or “Stein characterizing equation for standard normal distribution” with certain “noise robustness” property, which is now called the exchangeable pair approach. This method can now be applied using a variety of approaches, namely exchangeable pairs, dependency graphs or local dependencies
[
18
,
40
]
, size-bias
[
29
]
and zero-bias couplings
[
27
]
, Stein coupling
[
17
]
, and through Malliavin calculus
[
37
]
among others. The main underlying idea in Stein’s methods for CLT is as follows:

A random variable
W
is close to the standard normal distribution with respect to an appropriate metric if
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{D}}\E(f^{\prime}(W)-Wf(W))
is small for an adequately chosen class of functions
\mathcal{D}
depending on the metric.

Our work is built on the exchangeable pair approach, which we state here and refer to
[
16
,
20
,
47
]
for further details.

Definition 1.1
.

Two random variables
W
and
W^{\prime}
are said to be exchangeable if
(W,W^{\prime})\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{d}}}\,(W^{\prime},W)
.

While most works on Stein’s method using exchangeable pairs require exchangeability, this assumption can usually be relaxed to the requirement of
W
and
W^{\prime}
to be equidistributed as pointed out in
[
43
]
. This remark applies to the majority of our work as well. The only place where we use exchangeability to the fullest extent is in the last step in the proof of Theorem
2.4
and the analogous place in the multivariate result in Theorem
3.1
.

The method of exchangeable pairs is usually applicable in systems where small perturbations do not change the distribution significantly. Classical results due to Stein
[
48
,
49
]
can be stated in the following way.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(W,W^{\prime})
be an exchangeable pair of random variables defined on the same probability space. Suppose
\E W=0
,
\E W^{2}=1
,
\E\left|W\right|^{3}<\infty
, and
\Delta W:=W^{\prime}-W
almost surely satisfies

\displaystyle\E(\Delta W\mid W)=-\lambda(W+R_{1})\text{ and }\E\left(\Delta W^{2}\mid W\right)=2\lambda(1+R_{2})

for some constant
\lambda\in(0,1)
and random variables
R_{i}=R_{i}(W)
for
i=1,2
. Then

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}(W,Z)\leqslant\E|R_{1}|+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\E|R_{2}|+\frac{1}{3\lambda}\E|\Delta W|^{3},

where
Z
is a standard normal random variable and
d_{\mathcal{W}}(W,Z)
denotes the Wasserstein distance.

The outline of the proof is usually of the following form. One first finds an antisymmetric function, then using Taylor expansion and the properties of the conditional expectation one derives a bound on the Stein operator. In particular, given a bounded twice differentiable function
f
, one can find a function
F
with
F^{\prime}=f
. Then
\E(F(W^{\prime})-F(W))=0
and expanding the expression inside of the parenthesis around
W
, one gets

\displaystyle F(W^{\prime})-F(W)\approx\Delta W\cdot f(W)+\frac{1}{2}(\Delta W)^{2}\cdot f^{\prime}(W)+\frac{1}{6}(\Delta W)^{3}\cdot f^{\prime\prime}(W).

Using the tower property of the conditional expectation together with the assumptions on
\E(\Delta W\mid W)
and
\E(\Delta W^{2}\mid W)
one can derive the bound on
\left|\E(Wf(W)-f^{\prime}(W))\right|.

To derive the rate of convergence one aims to bound
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{C}}|\E h(W)-\E h(Z)|
for the corresponding class of functions
\mathcal{C}
and standard normal random variable
Z
. Given a function
h
, let
f
be the “bounded" solution to the following differential equation

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(w)-wf(w)=h(w)-\E h(Z).

(1.1)

The core idea behind Stein’s method is to work with the expectation of the left hand side in order to derive the desired bound on the distance. By inverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator one can see that
f
has one more derivative than
h
and that
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty},

\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\sqrt{2/\pi}\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty},
and
\left\|f^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}
.

1.3
Multivariate Stein’s method via exchangeable pairs

The definition of exchangeability can be extended to a multidimensional case in a natural way; we say that a pair of random vectors
(\bm{W},\bm{W}^{\prime})
is exchangeable if
(\bm{W},\bm{W}^{\prime})\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{d}}}\,(\bm{W}^{\prime},\bm{W})
. Multivariate versions of Stein’s method first appeared in
[
3
,
30
]
. It was extended to exchangeable pairs more than a decade later in
[
12
]
under the assumptions

\displaystyle\E(\Delta\!\bm{W}\mid\bm{W})=-\lambda\left(\bm{W}+\bm{R}\right)\text{ and }\E\left(\Delta\!\bm{W}\Delta\!\bm{W}^{T}\mid\bm{W}\right)=2\lambda(I+\Gamma),

where
\Delta\!\bm{W}:=\bm{W}^{\prime}-\bm{W}
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
I
is the identity matrix, and
\Gamma=\Gamma(\bm{W})
is a random matrix.
It was later extended in
[
39
]
to the case of a general covariance matrix
\Sigma
.

For the multivariate case one can derive an analogous relation to (
1.1
). Let
h
be a
1
-Lipschitz function on
\mathds{R}^{d}
,
i.e.,

\sup_{\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2}\in\mathds{R}^{d}}\frac{\left|h(\bm{w}_{1})-h(\bm{w}_{2})\right|}{\left|\bm{w}_{1}-\bm{w}_{2}\right|}\leqslant 1.

Here,
\left|\cdot\right|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
If
\Sigma
is a
d\times d
symmetric and positive definite matrix, there is a “bounded” solution
f
to the equation

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}f(\bm{w})=h(\bm{w})-\E h(\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{Z}),

(1.2)

where the operator

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}f(\bm{x}):=\langle\Sigma,\hess f(\bm{x})\rangle_{\hs}-\langle\bm{x},\nabla f(\bm{x})\rangle

(1.3)

defined for two times continuously differentiable function
f
characterizes
d
-dimensional normal distribution with mean zero and variance-covariance matrix
\Sigma
.

Both
[
12
,
39
]
bound the rate of convergence to the appropriate multivariate Gaussian vector in terms of smooth function metrics (also see
[
36
]
for a unified approach). A decade later
[
38
]
presented a smoothing scheme that allows one to go from a smooth test function to Lipschitz ones. These approaches of
[
12
,
39
,
38
]
were recently combined and extended in
[
23
]
, under additional assumption on the finiteness of the fourth moment, to apply Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs under Wasserstein distance. The result from
[
23
]
can be stated in the following form.

Theorem 1.2
(
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
).

Let
(\bm{W},\bm{W}^{\prime})
be an exchangeable pair of
d
-dimensional random vectors satisfying the linearity assumption

\E(\Delta\!\bm{W}\mid\bm{W})=-\Lambda(\bm{W}+\bm{R}),

where
\Delta\!\bm{W}:=\bm{W}^{\prime}-\bm{W}
. Assume that
\E\left|\bm{W}\right|^{4}<\infty
. Let
\Sigma
be a
d\times d
positive definite symmetric matrix such that

\displaystyle\E(\Delta\!\bm{W}\Delta\!\bm{W}^{T}\mid\bm{W})=2\Lambda(\Sigma+\Gamma)

(1.4)

for some symmetric matrix
\Gamma
.
Then

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{W},\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{Z})

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\text{ $1$-Lipschitz}}\left|\E h(\bm{W})-\E h(\Sigma^{1/2}\bm{Z})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\left|\bm{R}\right|+\left\|\Sigma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right\|_{\op}\E\left\|\Gamma\right\|_{\hs}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\Sigma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right\|_{\op}^{3/2}\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\max\left(\E\left|\bm{W}\right|^{2},\tr(\Sigma)\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\E\left(\left|\Lambda^{-1}\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|\cdot\left|\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|^{3}\right)}

where
Z
is a
d
-dimensional standard normal vector,
\left|\cdot\right|
denotes the Euclidean
\ell_{2}
norm,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\op}
denotes the operator norm and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\hs}
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt norm.

In particular, if
\E\bm{W}\bm{W}^{T}=\Sigma=I_{d}
the error bound reduces to

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{W},\bm{Z})

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\left|\bm{R}\right|+\E\left\|\Gamma\right\|_{\hs}+\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}d^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\E\left(\left|\Lambda^{-1}\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|\cdot\left|\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|^{3}\right)}.

(1.5)

1.4
Local Limit Theorem

While Central Limit Theorem provides the global limiting behavior for the distribution of the scaled sum of random variables, the Local Limit Theorem provides the behavior for the probabilities of the scaled sum of random variables to be equal to a particular number. The classical case for independent lattice distributed random variables is well understood (see
[
21
,
25
]
, among others) and can be stated in the following way.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random variables with mean zero, variance
\sigma^{2}
and having a common lattice distribution with span
1
,
i.e.,

\pr(X_{i}\in\zeta+\mathds{Z})=1
for some
\zeta\in\mathds{R}
. Let
W_{n}=n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}:=\left\{n^{-1/2}(n\zeta+\mathds{Z})\right\}
, then as
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}\left|\sqrt{n}\pr\left(W_{n}=x\right)-\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)\right|\to 0,

where is the density of the normal distribution with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
given by

\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}e^{-{x^{2}}/{2\sigma^{2}}}.

The analogous result for integer-valued dependent random variables was introduced in
[
34
]
under certain ‘smoothness’ conditions on the distribution. This approach was extended and connected to Stein’s method in
[
5
,
42
,
46
]
.
The main result of
[
5
]
is for general Stein coupling, which combines several approaches of Stein’s method. In this article, we focus on the exchangeable pair approach with an additional assumption that the change in the variable of interest takes values in
\{-1,0,1\}
. Hence we present in Theorem
1.4
a simplified version of the main result in
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
in combination with
[
5
, Remark 2.2]
. We refer to
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
for the general statement.

Theorem 1.4
(
[
5
, Theorem 2.1 and Remark 2.2]
).

Let
Y
be a mean zero random variable with variance
\sigma^{2}
and taking values in
\{\zeta\}+\mathds{Z}
for some
\zeta\in[0,1)
. Assume that
(Y^{\prime},Y)
is an exchangeable pair satisfying the linearity condition with some
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

\E(\Delta\!Y\mid Y)=-\lambda(Y+R_{1}),

(1.6)

and
\Delta\!Y\in\{-1,0,1\}
almost surely. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\{\zeta\}+\mathds{Z}}\left|\sigma\pr(Y=k)-\sigma\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(k)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{\sigma}+\frac{\sqrt{\E R_{1}^{2}}}{\sigma}\left(2+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2e}}+\sigma\sup_{k}\pr(Y=k)\right)

(1.7)

\displaystyle+\frac{\E R_{2}}{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2e}}+\frac{\E(R_{2}\left|Y\right|)}{\sigma^{3}}+\frac{2+\sup_{k}\E(R_{2}\mathds{1}_{Y=k})}{\sigma},

(1.8)

where
C
is some universal positive constant and
R_{2}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left|\pr(\Delta\!Y=1\mid Y)-\pr(\Delta\!Y=1)\right|
.

Notice that the original result in
[
5
]
is concerned with bounding the local distance to a translated Poisson random variable. However,
[
5
, Lemma 1.1]
yields that on the set of integers translated Poisson probability is within
\frac{C}{\sigma^{2}}
of the discretized normal probability with the same mean and variance. Hence the same holds after shifting the lattice by
-\E Y
; this additional step in the approximation is accounted for in the first error term in (
1.7
) with a universal constant
C
.

1.5
Strategy

The main strategy behind our result is similar to the one outlined under Theorem
1.1
, however we need to work with a bivariate function that is anti-symmetric in the first coordinate and symmetric in the second one. We need continuous approximation in terms of
(w^{\prime},w)
, but discrete approximation in terms of
(y^{\prime},y)
. More precisely, we will consider functions of the following form

\displaystyle(F(w^{\prime})-F(w))\cdot G(y^{\prime},y)

(1.9)

where
G
is a symmetric function of two variables. Note that, similar to the classical exchangeable pair approach, with
f=F^{\prime}
we have

\displaystyle F(w^{\prime})-F(w)\approx\Delta w\cdot f(w)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta w^{2}\cdot f^{\prime}(w)+\frac{1}{6}\Delta w^{3}\cdot f^{\prime\prime}(w).

We can choose
G
depending on the behavior of the exchangeable pair. We consider two cases:

Case I.

\pr(\Delta W\neq 0,\Delta\!Y=0)>0
. In this case we can take

\displaystyle G(y^{\prime},y):=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}_{y=k}+\mathds{1}_{y^{\prime}=k})\cdot\mathds{1}_{y^{\prime}-y=0}=\mathds{1}_{y^{\prime}=y=k}.

Here we need

\displaystyle\E(\Delta W\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y^{\prime}=Y=k}\mid W,Y)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\cdot(W+\text{``error''})\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y=k}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\E(\Delta W^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y^{\prime}=Y=k}\mid W,Y)

\displaystyle=2\lambda\cdot(\sigma_{W}^{2}+\text{``error''})\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y=k}

with some
\lambda\in(0,1)
for the classical idea to work.

Remark 1.5

(Classical techniques)
.

If the model falls into
Case I
, then conditioning on this event, one can derive CCLT with the explicit rate of convergence using standard techniques as in Theorem
1.1
. We apply this method in detail to various examples in Section
4.1
. In models where
\pr(X^{\prime}\neq X\mid Y^{\prime}=Y)=0
(Case II described below) one can usually consider
\bigl(\widehat{X}^{\prime},\widehat{Y}^{\prime}\bigr)
that is a result of the same Markov chain started at
(X,Y)
after two steps it then would fall into
Case I
. However, the computations get significantly more complicated and often are not feasible in applications.

Case II.

\pr(\Delta W\neq 0,\Delta\!Y=0)=0
. In this case we can take

\displaystyle G(y^{\prime},y):=g(y^{\prime}-k)\cdot\mathds{1}_{y^{\prime}-y=1}+g(y-k)\cdot\mathds{1}_{y^{\prime}-y=-1}

for some function
g
. Details are given in Section
5
.
To simplify notations we define for
\ell\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle M_{\ell,\pm}(W,Y):=\E((\Delta W)^{\ell}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Delta\!Y=\pm 1}\mid W,Y).

(1.10)

Using Taylor series expansion for the following mean zero random variable

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Theta_{f}(W,Y)\\
&\quad:=\E\left((F(W^{\prime})-F(W))\cdot(g(Y^{\prime}-k)\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Delta\!Y=1}+g(Y-k)\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Delta\!Y=-1})\mid W,Y\right),\end{split}

(1.11)

we get

\displaystyle\E(\ f(W)(M_{1+}(W,Y)\cdot g(Y-k+1)+M_{1-}(W,Y)\cdot g(Y-k))\ )

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\E(\ f^{\prime}(W)(M_{2+}(W,Y)\cdot g(Y-k+1)+M_{2-}(W,Y)\cdot g(Y-k))\ )\approx 0.

If we have

\displaystyle M_{1,\pm}(W,Y)\approx-\frac{1}{2}\lambda W\quad\text{ and }\quad M_{2,\pm}(W,Y)\approx\lambda\sigma_{W}^{2},

(1.12)

then to get the Stein characterizing equation for
(W\mid Y=k)
,

\displaystyle\left|\E\left((Wf(W)-\sigma_{W}^{2}f^{\prime}(W))\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y=k}\right)\right|\ll\pr(Y=k)

we
need a function
g
such that
g(y+1)+g(y)
is a constant multiple of
\mathds{1}_{y=0}
. Such a function is given by

\displaystyle g(y):=(-1)^{y}\cdot\mathds{1}_{y\leqslant 0}.

In many examples we have
M_{1,\pm}(W,Y)\approx-a_{\pm}\cdot\lambda W
where
a_{+}\neq a_{-}
are fixed positive constants, even though
M_{2,\pm}(W,Y)\approx\lambda\sigma_{W}^{2}
. Thus we need a way to remove the asymmetry in the two conditional means
M_{1,\pm}
. Similarly, even though we can make
W,Y
uncorrelated by subtracting an appropriate multiple of
Y
from
W
, in some examples,
M_{1,\pm}
involves non-trivial linear terms with
Y
. We will subtract an appropriate “small” random variable from
W
, which will not change variance behavior but introduce symmetry and remove
Y
dependence from the conditional mean computation. The change of variable is explained in Proposition
2.6
in Section
2
and applied in several concrete examples in Section
4.2
. The above setup can be generalized to high dimensions as given in Section
3
.

Remark 1.6
.

Note that, in general, the function

\displaystyle(f(W^{\prime},Y^{\prime})-f(W,Y^{\prime}))\cdot G(y,y^{\prime})+(f(W^{\prime},Y)-f(W,Y))\cdot G(y^{\prime},y)

is anti-symmetric in
(W^{\prime},Y^{\prime}),(W,Y)
for any
f
and
G
. So one can use different
f
and
G
to get the Stein operator for
W
multiplied by
\mathds{1}_{Y=k}
.

For such choices of function
g(y)
, one can get CCLT for
(W\mid Y=k)
. However, for an effective bound, one needs error terms with
\mathds{1}_{Y<k}
to be small in comparison with
\sigma_{Y}^{-1}
as one can see in Theorem
2.1
. To avoid this issue we first consider the same function as in
Case I
,
i.e.,

g(y)=\mathds{1}_{y=0}
, then the argument outlined in
Case II
yields a CCLT for
(W\mid Y\in\{k-1,k\})
. Thus it remains to compare
h(W)\mathds{1}_{Y=k-1}
with
h(W)\mathds{1}_{Y=k}
for appropriate smooth function
h(w)
. Using the intuition that exchangeable pair is most applicable in models where small perturbation does not change the system too much, we upper bound the difference between these two quantities by error terms involving
\E\left|W^{\prime}-W\right|
. We present details of this argument in the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

We now present a toy example to illustrate the ideas mentioned above.

1.6
Toy Example

Let
(\xi_{i},\omega_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
be independent random vectors with
\xi_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}\omega_{i}
independent with
\omega_{i}
for all
i\geqslant 1
for some sequence of real numbers
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
. We assume that
\omega_{i}
’s are i.i.d. Bernoulli
(p)
and
X_{i}:=\xi_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}(\omega_{i}-p)
’s are i.i.d. with mean zero variance one. Define

\displaystyle\overline{\omega}_{i}:=\omega_{i}-p,\quad Y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\omega}_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad W:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i}.

We will assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}=0
and
\varepsilon_{\max}:=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\varepsilon_{i}|\ll 1
. It is easy to check that the random vector
(W/\sqrt{n},Y/\sqrt{npq})
asymptotically converges to independent standard Gaussian rvs. We want to prove a CCLT for
(W\mid Y=k)
when
|k|\ll n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.

We consider the exchangeable pair created by independent re-sampling at a randomly chosen coordinate from
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
We have,

\displaystyle M_{1,+}(W,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E((\xi^{\prime}_{i}-\xi_{i})\cdot(1-\omega_{i})\omega^{\prime}_{i}\mid W,Y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E((X_{i}+\varepsilon_{i})(q-\overline{\omega}_{i})\mid W,Y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{pq}{n}\left(W-\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E((X_{i}+(1+q)\varepsilon_{i})\overline{\omega}_{i}\mid W,Y)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{pq}{n}\left(W-\frac{1}{q}\E\left(\frac{Y}{n}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}+(1+q)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i}\ \bigl|\ W,Y\right)\right).

Similarly, we get

\displaystyle M_{1,-}(W,Y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{pq}{n}\left(W+\frac{1}{p}\E\left(\frac{Y}{n}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-(1+p)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i}\ \bigl|\ W,Y\right)\right),

\displaystyle M_{2,+}(W,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{pq}{n}\left(2(n-Y/q)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E((X_{i}^{2}-1+\varepsilon_{i}^{2}-2\varepsilon_{i}X_{i})\cdot(1-\overline{\omega}_{i}/q)\mid W,Y)\right),

\displaystyle\text{ and }M_{2,-}(W,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{pq}{n}\left(2(n+Y/p)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E((X_{i}^{2}-1+\varepsilon_{i}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{i}X_{i})\cdot(1+\overline{\omega}_{i}/p)\mid W,Y)\right).

One can easily verify that
\sigma_{W}^{2}=\var(W)=n+o(n)
. Moreover, with
\lambda=2pq/n
, we have

M_{1,\pm}(W,Y)\approx-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\cdot(W+o_{p}(\sqrt{n}))\quad\text{ and }\quad M_{2,\pm}(W,Y)\approx\lambda\cdot(\sigma_{W}^{2}+O_{p}(\sqrt{n})),

as expected in (
1.12
).
This allows us to apply the idea in Case II to get an explicit rate of convergence for the CCLT.

We now write down all of the assumptions needed to state the main results.

1.7
Assumptions

It is natural to expect CCLT to hold for
(W\mid Y=k)
under reasonable structural assumptions when
W
and
Y
are asymptotically jointly Gaussian. Since for jointly Gaussian random variables uncorrelated implies independent the first assumption that we impose on
(W,Y)
is the following.

Assumption I
.

Assume that

I.1

\bm{W}=(W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{d})
, where
\bm{W}
is a mean
0
random vector with variance-covariance matrix
\Sigma
, which is invertible. In the one dimensional case we will assume the variance to be
1
.

I.2

Y
is a mean
0
random variable with variance
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
.

I.3

\bm{W}
and
Y
are uncorrelated.

I.4

The random vectors
(\bm{W},Y)
and
(\bm{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
are exchangeable.

In this paper, for simplicity, we focus on the case when
\Delta\!Y:=Y^{\prime}-Y\in\{-1,0,1\}
and the exchangeable pair approach is applicable, which we state in the following assumptions.
Recall the definition of
M_{\ell,\pm}(\bm{W},Y)
from (
1.10
).

Assumption II
.

Y
takes values in
\zeta+\mathds{Z}
for some
\zeta\in[0,1)
,
\Delta\!Y\in\{-1,0,1\}
almost surely, and

\displaystyle M_{0,\pm}(\bm{W},Y)=Q+R_{0,\pm},

where
Q=\lambda\sigma_{Y}^{2}
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Further, for any
k
such that
\pr(Y=k)>0
and
\pr(Y=k-1)>0
we define

r_{k}:=\frac{\pr(Y=k-1)}{\pr(Y=k)}\in(0,\infty).

(1.13)

The condition on
M_{0,\pm}
is natural because, in order to apply our method to
(\bm{W}\mid Y=k)
, we require the method of exchangeable pairs to be applicable to
Y
on its own. In other words, we expect
\E(\Delta Y\mid\bm{W},Y)\approx-\lambda Y
. Since we assume that
\Delta Y\in\{-1,0,1\}
, if
\pr(\Delta Y=1\mid\bm{W},Y)
is concentrated at
Q
,
\pr(\Delta Y=-1\mid\bm{W},Y)
has to also concentrate at
Q
. This also implies that
Q\approx\lambda\sigma_{Y}^{2}
as

\displaystyle 2\lambda\sigma_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle=\E(\E(\Delta Y^{2}\mid\bm{W},Y))

\displaystyle=\E(\pr(\Delta Y=1\mid\bm{W},Y)+\pr(\Delta Y=-1\mid\bm{W},Y))\approx 2Q.

It is important to highlight that
M_{0,\pm}(\bm{W},Y)=\pr(\Delta\!Y=\pm 1\mid\bm{W},Y)
, but it is reasonable to expect that the extra conditioning on
\bm{W}
does not affect this condition too much. In most examples that we consider,
Y
is the sum of independent Bernoulli random variables and
M_{0,\pm}(\bm{W},Y)\approx Q\mp\lambda a_{\pm}Y
for some
a_{\pm}
such that
a_{+}+a_{-}=1
.

Observation 1.7
.

Assumption
II
implies that
Y
satisfies the linearity condition

\E(\Delta\!Y\mid Y)=-\lambda(Y+R),

with
R=-Y-\frac{1}{\lambda}(R_{0,+}-R_{0,-})
.
Moreover, if
R_{0,\pm}=\mp\lambda a_{\pm}Y
, where
a_{+}+a_{-}=1
like in the case when
Y
is the sum of i.i.d. Bernoulli
(p)
random variables,
R
simplifies to

R=-Y-\frac{1}{\lambda}(-\lambda a_{+}Y-\lambda a_{-}Y)=-Y+(a_{+}+a_{-})Y=0.

Remark 1.8

(Ratio of probabilities and LLT)
.

Let
p_{k}(n):=\pr(Y=k)
for
Y=Y_{n}
. Suppose the random variable
Y
satisfies the LLT (see Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
) in the sense that as
n\to\infty

\sup_{k}\left|\sigma_{Y}\pr(Y=k)-\sigma_{Y}\varphi_{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}(k)\right|\to 0.

(1.14)

Then for any
\left|k\right|\ll~\sigma_{Y}
we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{k-1}(n)}{p_{k}(n)}=1,

see Lemma
A.2
for a proof.
Thus this ratio is uniformly bounded from above and from below by positive constants that depend only on the law of
Y
and the value of
k
. In particular, condition (
1.13
) of Assumption
II
is satisfied. Note that, for our results we only need
1+r_{k}
to be bounded away from
0
and
\infty
.

The next assumption corresponds to the linearity condition in Theorem
1.1
. However, since the change has been separated into two parts due to the change in
Y
, we state it as two separate equalities.

Assumption III
.

For a
d\times d
invertible matrix
\Psi
we have that

\displaystyle M_{1,\pm}(\bm{W},Y)=-\lambda\left(\frac{1}{2}\Psi\bm{W}+\bm{R}_{1,\pm}\right).

In one dimensional case this assumption takes form of

\displaystyle M_{1,\pm}(W,Y)=-\lambda\left(\frac{1}{2}\psi W+R_{1,\pm}\right),

(1.15)

for some number
\psi>0
.

Notice that, if
Y^{\prime}=Y
implies
W^{\prime}=W
, using the fact that
\Delta\!Y\in\{-1,0,1\}
a.s. and adding the equalities (
1.15
) together yields exactly the same linearity condition as in Theorem
1.1
, where
\psi
accounts for the difference in
\lambda
for
W
and
Y
. In general, the separation on the linearity condition based on the change to
Y
need not be symmetric, in the sense that the factor of
1/2
in front of
W
would be replaced by
a_{\pm}
with the property that
a_{+}+a_{-}=1
. We tackle this difficulty by an appropriate change of variable that accounts for this asymmetry.

Assumption IIIa
.

Assume that for a
d\times d
invertible matrix
\Psi
we have that

\displaystyle M_{1,\pm}(\bm{W},Y)=-\lambda\left(\Psi_{\pm}\bm{W}+\bm{b}_{\pm}Y+\bm{R}_{1,\pm}\right),

where
\Psi_{1,+}+\Psi_{1,-}=\Psi
,
\bm{b}_{+}+\bm{b}_{-}={\bf 0}
and
\bm{b}_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\Psi\bm{b}_{+}
. In the one dimensional case this assumption becomes

\displaystyle M_{1,\pm}(W,Y)=-\lambda\left(a_{\pm}\psi W+b_{\pm}Y+R_{1,\pm}\right).

for some number
\psi>0
, where
a_{+}+a_{-}=1
and
b_{+}+b_{-}=0
.

The last assumption has to do with the behavior of the conditional second moment of the change in
W
.

Assumption IV
.

With the same notations as above we assume that

\displaystyle\E\left(\Delta\!\bm{W}\Delta\!\bm{W}^{T}\mathds{1}_{\Delta\!Y=\pm 1}\mid\bm{W},Y\right)=\lambda\left(\Psi\Sigma+\Gamma_{2,\pm}\right),

(1.16)

for some random matrices
\Gamma_{2,\pm}=\Gamma_{2,\pm}(\bm{W},Y)
. In the one dimensional case this assumption becomes

\displaystyle M_{2,\pm}(W,Y)=\lambda\left(\psi+R_{2,\pm}\right).

(1.17)

2
Main results: Univariate Case

First, we state the simplest version of our main result.

Theorem 2.1
(Symmetric case).

Suppose
W
and
Y
are random variables satisfying Assumptions
I
,
II
,
III
and
IV
. Let
p_{k}:=\pr(Y=k)>0
. Then we have that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\bigl((W\mid Y=k),Z\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{\psi}\left(A_{k}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}C\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\psi^{2}}}\left(B_{k}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}D\right)+\frac{2}{3\lambda\psi p_{k}}E,

where

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|R_{1,-}\right|\,\big|\,Y=k\right),\qquad

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|R_{2,-}\right|\,\big|\,Y=k\right),

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\E\left|R_{1,+}-R_{1,-}\right|,\qquad

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\E\left|R_{2,+}-R_{2,-}\right|,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\text{ and }\quad E\leqslant\E|\Delta W|^{3}.

In the proof of Theorem
2.1
we consider
g(y):=(-1)^{y}\mathds{1}_{y\leqslant 0}
, so that

g(y+1-k)+g(y-k)=\mathds{1}_{y=k}.

This is the most straightforward function that satisfies all of the properties that we require.

Remark 2.2

(Asymmetry in the error terms)
.

One can notice that the bound in the conclusion of Theorem
2.1
is asymmetric with respect to the error terms
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
, namely only
R_{1,-}
and
R_{2,-}
appear, while
R_{1,+}
and
R_{2,+}
do not. The reason for this is that we chose the indicator
\mathds{1}_{y\leqslant 0}
inside of function
g(y)
. If instead one uses
\widehat{g}(y):=\left(-1\right)^{y}\cdot\mathds{1}_{y\geqslant 0}
then the similar argument would give a bound with only
R_{1,+}
and
R_{2,+}
appearing in the first term. Moreover, one could also consider their average to get the following bound

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\bigl((W\mid Y=k),Z\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\psi}\left(A^{\prime}_{k}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}C\right)+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi\psi^{2}}}\left(B^{\prime}_{k}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}D\right)+\frac{1}{3\lambda\psi p_{k}}E,

where

\displaystyle A_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|R_{1,+}+R_{1,-}\right|\ \big|\ Y=k\right)\quad\text{and}\quad B_{k}^{\prime}=\E\left(\left|R_{2,+}+R_{2,-}\right|\ \big|\ Y=k\right),

and the rest of the terms remain the same as in Theorem
2.1
.

There are two aspects in which Theorem
2.1
needs improvement. First, it is often the case that the term
\frac{1}{p_{k}}\E\left|R_{2,\pm}\right|
might not go to zero even though
\E\left|R_{2,\pm}\right|
are small on their own. To improve this bound we consider a different function
g(y)=\mathds{1}_{y=0}
. This adaptation with a similar proof to the one of Theorem
2.1
allows us to derive the following CCLT.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
W
and
Y
are random variables satisfying Assumptions
I
,
II
,
III
and
IV
. For any
k
such that
p_{k}:=\pr(Y=~k)>0
and
p_{k-1}:=\pr(Y=~k-1)>0
we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\bigl((W\mid Y\in\{k-1,k\}),Z\bigr)\leqslant\frac{2}{\psi(1+r_{k})}\widehat{A}_{k}+\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\psi(1+r_{k})}\widehat{B}_{k}+\frac{2}{3\lambda\psi}\widehat{E}_{k},

(2.1)

where
r_{k}=p_{k-1}/p_{k}
and

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|R_{1,-}\right|\,\mid\,Y=k\right)+r_{k}\cdot\E\left(\left|R_{1,+}\right|\mid Y=k-1\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|R_{2,-}\right|\,\big|\,Y=k\right)+r_{k}\cdot\E\left(\left|R_{2,+}\right|\,\big|\,Y=k-1\right),

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad\widehat{E}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E(|\Delta W|^{3}\mid Y\in\{k-1,k\}).

In most applications of our results we expect
r_{k}
to be uniformly bounded away from
0
and infinity, see Remark
1.8
and Lemma
A.2
.

As we mentioned in Section
1.2
, the exchangeable pair approach is most useful in models where a small perturbation does not change the observed quantity too much. Using this intuition it is natural that for a Lipschitz function
h(w)
the difference between of
\E(h(W)\mid Y=k)
and
\E(h(W)\mid Y=k-1)
should be negligible. To make this heuristic rigorous, we use the fact that
M_{0,\pm}
are concentrated at
Q
(Assumption
II
) and exchangeability of
W
and
W^{\prime}
(see Lemma
5.1
for the exact statement). Combining Lemma
2.3
with the fact that
W
conditioned on the event
\{Y=k-1\}
is almost the same as
W
conditioned on
\{Y=k\}
yields the improved version of the univariate result.

Theorem 2.4
(Improved symmetric case).

Suppose
W
and
Y
are random variables satisfying Assumptions
I
,
II
,
III
and
IV
. Let
k
be such that
\pr(Y=k)>0
and
\pr(Y=k-1)>0
. Then

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\bigl((W\mid Y=k),Z\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\psi}\widehat{A}_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi\psi^{2}}}\widehat{B}_{k}

\displaystyle\qquad+(1+r_{k})\left(\frac{1}{2Q}\widehat{C}_{k}+\frac{1}{2Q}\widehat{D}_{k}+\frac{2}{3\lambda\psi}\widehat{E}_{k}\right),

where
r_{k},

\widehat{A},

\widehat{B},
and
\widehat{E}
are as in Lemma
2.3
, while

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left((\left|W\right|+\E\left|Z\right|)(\left|R_{0,+}\right|+\left|R_{0,-}\right|)\,\big|\,Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right)

\displaystyle\text{and }\widehat{D}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|\Delta W\right|\,\big|\,Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right).

We now derive Theorem
2.4
using Lemmas
2.3
and
5.1
. The proofs of these lemmas are presented in Section
5
.

By Lemma
2.3
we have that for any
1
-Lipschitz function
h:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}

\displaystyle\left|\E(h(W)-h(Z))\mathds{1}_{Y\in\{k-1,k\}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{\psi}\left(\E|R_{1,-}|\mathds{1}_{Y=k}+\E|R_{1,+}|\mathds{1}_{Y=k-1}\right)

(2.2)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\psi^{2}}}\left(\E|R_{2,-}|\mathds{1}_{Y=k}+\E|R_{2,+}|\mathds{1}_{Y=k-1}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{3\lambda\psi}\E\left|\Delta W\right|^{3}\mathds{1}_{Y\in\{k-1,k\}}.

On the other hand by Lemma
5.1
we have that for any such function
h
and for any
k
such that
\pr(Y=k)>0
and
\pr(Y=k-1)>0
we have that

\displaystyle\left|\E(h(W)-h(Z))\left(\mathds{1}_{Y=k}-\mathds{1}_{Y=k-1}\right)\right|

(2.3)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{Q}\E\left(|\Delta W|\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y\in\{k-1,k\}}\right)+\frac{1}{Q}\E\left((\left|W\right|+\left|Z\right|)(\left|R_{0,+}\right|+\left|R_{0,-}\right|)\mathds{1}_{Y\in\{k-1,k\}}\right).

Adding the inequalities from Lemma
2.3
to the one in Lemma
5.1
gives us that

\displaystyle 2\cdot\left|\E(h(W)-h(Z))\mathds{1}_{Y=k}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{\psi}\left(\E|R_{1,-}|\mathds{1}_{Y=k}+\E|R_{1,+}|\mathds{1}_{Y=k-1}\right)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\psi^{2}}}\left(\E|R_{2,-}|\mathds{1}_{Y=k}+\E|R_{2,+}|\mathds{1}_{Y=k-1}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{3\lambda\psi}\E\left(\left|\Delta W\right|^{3}\mathds{1}_{Y\in\{k-1,k\}}\right)+\frac{1}{Q}\E\left(|\Delta W|\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y\in\{k-1,k\}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{Q}\E\left((\left|W\right|+\left|Z\right|)(\left|R_{0,+}\right|+\left|R_{0,-}\right|)\mathds{1}_{Y\in\{k-1,k\}}\right).

Dividing both sides of the inequality by
2p_{k}
and recalling that
r_{k}=\frac{p_{k-1}}{p_{k}}
we derive the desired result.

\blacksquare

One can see that Theorem
2.4
partially generalizes
[
31
, Theorem 4.2]
. While our result allows for the dependence among random variables, it also requires the third absolute moment to be finite (as in
[
6
, Theorem 4]
) and limits the change in
Y
to only
\{-1,0,1\}
. One can adapt our approach to models with finite
(2+\delta)
- th moment, although it might involve some technical computations. However, relaxing the assumption on the range of
\Delta\!Y
is of particular interest. In case when
\pr(\Delta\!Y\in\{-1,0,1\})
is sufficiently large, our techniques are still applicable but could yield a suboptimal rate of convergence. For further discussion see Section
7.2
.

Remark 2.5

(Explicit bound for the error terms with conditioning)
.

All of the error terms in Theorem
2.4
are of the form
\E(\left|R\right|\mid Y=i)
where
i\in\{k-1,k\}
. One can bound those terms using Hölder inequality in the following way

\displaystyle\E(\left|R\right|\mid Y=i)

\displaystyle=\pr(Y=i)^{-1}\E(\left|R\right|\mathds{1}_{Y=i})\leqslant\frac{\left\|R\right\|_{p}}{\pr(Y=i)^{1/p}}

for some
p>1
. Provided that the random variable
R
has
p^{\textrm{th}}
moment and is of order
n^{-\alpha}
,
i.e.,
for some constant
C(p)
depending on
p
we have
\left\|n^{\alpha}R\right\|_{p}\leqslant C(p)
, and if
\pr(Y=i)\approx~n^{-\beta/2}
, then

\E(\left|R\right|\mid Y=i)\leqslant C(p)n^{-\alpha+\frac{\beta}{2p}}.

In our applications, we can take
p
to be very large, so we will usually write

\E(\left|R\right|\mid Y=i)\lesssim n^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}

for some small
\varepsilon>0
.

The second aspect in which both Theorems
2.1
and
2.4
need improvement is that one would want to extend it to the models with asymmetries concerning the change in
Y
of the form

\displaystyle M_{1,\pm}(W,Y)=-\lambda\left(a_{\pm}\psi W+b_{\pm}Y+R_{1,\pm}\right),

and where the term
b_{\pm}Y
is not be negligible. In other words, we would like to apply this result to the models that satisfy Assumption
IIIa
instead of Assumption
III
. One can do it by applying a change of variable presented in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.6
(Univariate change of variable).

Suppose
X
and
Y
are random variables satisfying Assumptions
I
,
II
,
IIIa
and
IV
. Assume that

R_{0,\pm}=\mp\lambda a_{\pm}Y

and define the change of variable

\displaystyle W^{0}:=X+\lambda\,\psi\,\alpha XY+\frac{\lambda\,\theta}{2}\left(Y^{2}-\E Y^{2}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}(\psi+1)\alpha\theta}{3}(Y^{3}-\E Y^{3}),

(2.4)

where

\alpha=\frac{a_{+}-a_{-}}{2Q}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\theta=\frac{b_{+}}{Q}.

Let
W={W^{0}}/{\sigma_{W^{0}}}
. Then
\left(W,Y\right)
satisfies Assumptions
I
,
II
,
III
and
IV

with error terms
\widetilde{R}_{1,\pm}
and
\widetilde{R}_{2,\pm}
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{1,\pm}=\frac{\lambda\theta}{2}\left(1-\frac{\psi}{2}\right)\frac{\overline{Y^{2}}}{\sigma_{W^{0}}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{W^{0}}}(\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{0,\pm}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{1,\pm}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{2,\pm}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{3,\pm}),

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{0,\pm}

\displaystyle:=\lambda\psi^{2}\alpha a_{\pm}X(Y\pm 1)-\frac{\lambda\,\alpha\,\psi}{2}XY+R_{1,\pm}\biggl(1\pm\lambda\,\psi\,\alpha+\lambda\,\psi\,\alpha Y\biggr),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{1,\pm}

\displaystyle:=\lambda\left(\pm\lambda b_{\pm}\psi\alpha\mp\frac{a_{\pm}\theta}{2}+(\psi+1)\theta\alpha\left(Q-\frac{1}{3}\lambda a_{\pm}\right)\right)Y,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{2,\pm}

\displaystyle:=\mp\lambda^{2}\theta(\psi+1)\alpha a_{\pm}Y^{2},

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{3,\pm}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{3}\lambda\theta(\psi+1)\alpha Q-\lambda^{2}\theta(\psi+1)\alpha a_{\pm}Y^{3}-\frac{\lambda^{2}\psi(\psi+1)\alpha\theta}{6}\left(Y^{3}-\E Y^{3}\right),

and for
p\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{R}_{2,\pm}-\frac{R_{2,\pm}}{\sigma^{2}_{W^{0}}}\right\|_{2p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\psi\left|\alpha\right|\left(\frac{\sigma_{X}}{\sigma_{W^{0}}}\sqrt{\left\|\lambda(1+R_{2,\pm}/\sigma^{2}_{X})\right\|_{2p}\left\|Y\right\|_{2p}}+\frac{\left\|X\right\|_{p}}{\sigma_{W^{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left|\theta\right|\cdot\frac{\left\|Y\right\|_{p}}{\sigma_{W^{0}}}+\lambda^{3/2}(\psi+1)\left|\alpha\theta\right|\cdot\frac{\left\|Y^{2}\right\|_{p}}{\sigma_{W^{0}}}.

Assuming the particular form of
R_{0,\pm}
one can see this change of variable as the correction for the asymmetry created by the change of
Y
(
i.e.,
the
XY
term) and the approximation of the conditional mean up to the third order. Considering the examples where one can compute the exact conditional mean, we notice that the change of variable is indeed very close to the true value. We illustrate that in the Remarks
4.8
and
4.10
.

Now we explain the intuition behind each term in the change of variable.
One needs the term
\lambda\,\psi\,\alpha XY
to account for the asymmetry between
a_{+}
and
a_{-}
. Indeed, when
a_{+}=a_{-}
, we have that
\alpha=0
and this term is not present. In the computation of
M_{1,\pm}(W^{0},Y)
it produces the term
\mp\alpha QX
that in combination with
a_{\pm}X
gives us

\displaystyle(a_{\pm}\mp\alpha Q)X=\left(a_{\pm}\mp\frac{a_{+}-a_{-}}{2}\right)X=\frac{1}{2}X.

The square term has the exact form to cancel out
b_{\pm}Y
if it is non-negligible, while the cubic term is present to cancel everything created by the square term to match with the coefficient of
\E Y^{2}
.

Remark 2.7

(Parameter
\psi
)
.

In the majority of the examples for which we expect Theorem
2.4
to be used, one has
\psi=2
. When
\psi\neq 2
, we believe that one likely needs to use the multivariate version as in Theorem
3.1
, however, we presented the statement for general
\psi
, in case one has sufficient control on the quantity
\lambda\theta\sigma_{Y}
.

3
Main results: Multivariate Case

In this section, we present the extension of our main result to the multivariate setting.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\bm{W},Y
be, respectively, a
\mathds{R}^{d}
-valued random vector and a random variable satisfying Assumptions
I
,
II
,
III
, and
IV
. Let
k
be such that
p_{k}:=\pr(Y=k)>0
and
p_{k-1}:=\pr(Y=k-1)>0
. If
\E\left|\bm{W}\right|^{4}<\infty
then

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}((\bm{W}\mid Y=k),\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{Z})

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{1+r_{k}}\widehat{A}_{k}+\frac{1}{2(1+r_{k})}\left\|\Sigma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right\|_{\op}\widehat{B}_{k}+\frac{1+r_{k}}{2Q}\left(\widehat{C}_{k}+\widehat{D}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+(1+r_{k})\left\|\Sigma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right\|_{\op}^{3/2}\sqrt{c_{3}\cdot\widehat{E}_{k}\cdot\widehat{F}_{k}},

where
c_{3}=(2+8e^{-3/2})/\sqrt{2\pi}<2,

r_{k}=p_{k-1}/p_{k}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|\Psi^{-1}\bm{R}_{1,-}\right|\mid Y=k\right)+r_{k}\E\left(\left|\Psi^{-1}\bm{R}_{1,+}\right|\mid Y=k-1\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left\|\Psi^{-1}\Gamma_{2,-}\right\|_{\hs}\mid Y=k\right)+r_{k}\E\left(\left\|\Psi^{-1}\Gamma_{2,+}\right\|_{\hs}\mid Y=k-1\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left(\left|\bm{W}\right|+\sqrt{\tr(\Sigma)}\right)(\left|R_{0,+}\right|+\left|R_{0,-}\right|)\,\big|\,Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right)

\displaystyle\widehat{D}_{k}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|\,\big|\,Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right)

\displaystyle\widehat{E}_{k}

\displaystyle={\E\left(\left|(\lambda\Psi)^{-1}\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|\cdot\left|\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|^{3}\,\big|\,Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right)},

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad\widehat{F}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tr(\Sigma)+\E\left(\left|\bm{W}\right|^{2}\,\big|\,Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right)}.

In particular, if
\cov(\bm{W})=\Sigma=I_{d}
then the error bound reduces to

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}((\bm{W}\mid Y=k),\bm{Z})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{1+r_{k}}\left(\widehat{A}_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\widehat{B}_{k}\right)+\frac{1+r_{k}}{2Q}\left(\widehat{C}_{k}+\widehat{D}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+(1+r_{k})\cdot(d+\E(\left|\bm{W}\right|^{2}\mid Y\in\{k,k-1\}))^{\frac{1}{4}}\cdot\sqrt{\widehat{E}_{k}}.

On the other hand if only
\E\left|\bm{W}\right|^{3}<\infty
then

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}((\bm{W}\mid Y=k),\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{Z})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{1+r_{k}}\left(\widehat{A}_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Sigma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right\|_{\op}\widehat{B}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1+r_{k}}{2Q}\left(\widehat{C}_{k}+\widehat{D}_{k}\right)+c_{2}(1+r_{k})\cdot\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{2}\widehat{E}_{k}^{\prime}\cdot\widehat{F}_{k}^{\prime},

where
c_{2}=4/\sqrt{2\pi e}<1
,
\widehat{A}_{k},\widehat{B}_{k},\widehat{C}_{k},\widehat{D}_{k}
are as above and

\displaystyle\widehat{E}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left|(\lambda\Psi)^{-1}\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|\left|\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|^{2}\,\big|\,Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right),

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\widehat{F}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=1+\left|\log\left(c_{2}\cdot\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{2}\cdot{\widehat{E}^{\prime}_{k}}/{\widehat{F}_{k}}\right)\right|.

Remark 3.2
.

The case when
\Sigma
is the identity matrix, the proof of Theorem
2.4
can be easily adapted to get a bound on
\left|\E\left(\Delta f(\bm{W})-\bm{W}^{T}\nabla f(\bm{W})\right)\mathds{1}_{Y=k}\right|
. This case, in a way, corresponds to the setting of
[
12
]
. However if
\Sigma
is not an identity matrix one has to change the function to an expression that already resembles Taylor approximation and derive an upper bound to
\left|\E\left(\nabla^{T}\Sigma\nabla f(\bm{W})-\bm{W}^{T}\nabla f(\bm{W})\right)\mathds{1}_{Y=k}\right|
. This generalization is done in the spirit of
[
23
,
38
,
39
]
.
Moreover, in many applications
\left\|\Psi^{-1}\right\|_{\op}
is bounded by a constant
and thus
\left|\Psi^{-1}\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|
can be bounded by some constant multiple of
\left|\Delta\!\bm{W}\right|
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is analogous to the univariate case. We first establish a CCLT for
(\bm{W}\mid Y\in\{k-1,k\})
that we state in Lemma
5.2
and then combine it with the quantitative bound on the difference of
(\bm{W}\mid Y=k)
and
(\bm{W}\mid Y=k-1)
as in Lemma

. To derive the bound in terms of Wasserstein distance, we follow the smoothing technique as in
[
23
,
38
]
. We present the proof of Theorem
3.1
in Section
5.3
.

Similar to the univariate case, we introduce a change of variable that allows one to pass from Assumption

to Assumption

Proposition 3.3
(Multivariate change of variable).

Let
\bm{X},Y
be, respectively, a
\mathds{R}^{d}
-valued random vector and a random variable satisfying Assumptions
I
,
II
,
IIIa
, and
IV
. Define the following change of variable

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{0}:=\bm{X}+\lambda\mathrm{A}\bm{X}Y+\frac{\lambda\bm{\theta}}{2}\left(Y^{2}-\E Y^{2}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}(\mathrm{A}+\alpha)\bm{\theta}}{3}(Y^{3}-\E Y^{3}),

(3.1)

where

\mathrm{A}=\frac{\Psi_{+}-\Psi_{-}}{2Q},\quad\alpha=\frac{a_{+}-a_{-}}{2Q},\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bm{\theta}=\frac{\bm{b}_{+}}{Q}.

Then vector
\bm{W}:=\left(\bm{W}^{0}_{i}/\sigma_{\bm{W}^{0}_{i}}\right)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}
satisfies Assumptions
I
,
II
,
III
, and
IV
with error terms
\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{1,\pm}
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2,\pm}
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{1,\pm}=\Sigma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{W^{0}}(\widetilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{0,\pm}+\widetilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1,\pm}+\widetilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{2,\pm}+\widetilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{3,\pm}),

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{0,\pm}

\displaystyle:=\lambda a_{\pm}\Psi\mathrm{A}\bm{X}(Y\pm 1)-\frac{\lambda\,\Psi\,\mathrm{A}}{2}\bm{X}Y+\biggl(1\pm\lambda\,\mathrm{A}+\lambda\,\mathrm{A}Y\biggr)\bm{R}_{1,\pm},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1,\pm}

\displaystyle:=\lambda\left(\pm\lambda\mathrm{A}\bm{b}_{\pm}\mp\frac{a_{\pm}\bm{\theta}}{2}+(\mathrm{A}+\alpha)\bm{\theta}\left(Q-\frac{1}{3}\lambda a_{\pm}\right)\right)Y,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{2,\pm}

\displaystyle:=\mp\lambda^{2}a_{\pm}(\mathrm{A}+\alpha)\bm{\theta}Y^{2},

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{3,\pm}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{3}\lambda(\mathrm{A}+\alpha)\bm{\theta}Q-\lambda^{2}a_{\pm}(\mathrm{A}+\alpha)\bm{\theta}Y^{3}-\frac{\lambda^{2}\Psi(\mathrm{A}+\alpha)\bm{\theta}}{6}\left(Y^{3}-\E Y^{3}\right),

and

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2,\pm}-\Sigma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{\bm{W}^{0}}\Gamma_{2,\pm}\Sigma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{\bm{W}^{0}}\right\|_{p-\hs}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left(\frac{\left\|\Sigma_{\bm{X}}\right\|_{\hs}}{\left\|\Sigma_{\bm{W}^{0}}\right\|_{\hs}}\sqrt{\left\|\lambda(\Psi+\Sigma_{\bm{X}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Gamma_{2,\pm}\Sigma_{\bm{X}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\right\|_{p-\hs}\left\|Y\right\|_{2p}}+\left\|\Sigma_{\bm{X}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}X\right\|_{p}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left\|\bm{\theta}\right\|_{p}\cdot\left\|\Sigma_{\bm{W}^{0}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right\|_{\hs}\left\|Y\right\|_{p}+\lambda^{3/2}\left\|\bm{\theta}\right\|_{p}\left\|\Sigma_{\bm{W}^{0}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right\|_{\hs}\left\|Y^{2}\right\|_{p}.

Remark 3.4
.

Note that the change of variable (

) with

). The terms play analogous role as described in the univariate case. From the Assumption

4
Applications

4.1
Classical methods in CCLT

In this section we present several derivation of CCLT using classical Stein’s method. As we mentioned in Remark
1.5
to apply this technique to a random vector
(X,Y)
it is crucial to work with an exchangeable pair
((X,Y),(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}))
such that
\pr(\Delta X\neq 0,\Delta\!Y=0)>0.

In each of the following subsections we first describe the model and then state the relevant CCLT result.

4.1.1
Variant of an occupancy problem

Suppose there are three urns and
n
many distinct balls. At time
i
we put the
i^{\textrm{th}}
ball into an urn numbered
U_{i}
, where
U_{i}
’s are i.i.d. random variables that are equal to
1
with probability
p_{1}
, to
2
with probability
p_{2}
, and to
3
with probability
p_{3}:=1-p_{1}-p_{2}
. Define

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{U_{i}=2}\text{ and }W:=n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{1-p_{2}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{p_{1}}\mathds{1}_{U_{i}=1}-\frac{1}{p_{3}}\mathds{1}_{U_{i}=3}\right)

(4.1)

as the number of balls in the second urn and the scaled difference between the number of balls in the first and the third urn, respectively, at time
n
.
Notice that
\sigma_{V}^{2}=np_{2}(1-p_{2})
. We have the following CCLT result with an explicit rate of convergence for
(W\mid V=k)
when
|k-np_{2}|\ll n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
W
and
Y
be as defined in (
4.1
). For any
k\in\mathds{Z}
with
|k-np_{2}|\ll n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\left((W\mid V=k),\,Z\right)\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Notice that
W
is mean zero variance one random variable.
Now conciser the following Glauber dynamics Markov chain, for a given
(W,V)
we pick index of one of the balls uniformly at random,
i.e.,

I\sim\textrm{Uniform}\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, and then re-sample its placing,
i.e.,
place
I^{\textrm{th}}
ball into the urn
U_{I}
where
U_{I}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{d}}}\,U_{1}
and independent of everything else. Call the result
(W^{\prime},V^{\prime})
. Clearly
(W,V)
and
(W^{\prime},V^{\prime})
are equidistributed. Notice that, with
\lambda=(1-p_{2})/n
, we have

\displaystyle\pr(V^{\prime}=V\mid V,W)=\frac{1}{n}\left(p_{2}V+(1-p_{2})(n-V)\right)=1-p_{2}+(2p_{2}-1)\cdot\frac{V}{n},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\E\left(\Delta W\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}=V}\mid\bm{U}\right)=-\lambda W,

and

\displaystyle\E\left(|\Delta W|^{2}\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}=V}\mid\bm{U}\right)=2\lambda\left(1+R_{2}\right),

where
\bm{U}=(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n})
and

R_{2}=\frac{p_{1}p_{3}}{n(1-p_{2})}\E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{1}^{-2}(\mathds{1}_{U_{i}=1}-p_{1})+p_{3}^{-2}(\mathds{1}_{U_{i}=3}-p_{3})\ \bigl|\ \bm{U}\right).

For any piece-wise three times differentiable function
F
such that
F^{\prime}=f\in\mathcal{A}
, the random variable
(F(W^{\prime})-F(W))\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}=V=k}
has mean zero. Thus using standard techniques we have

\displaystyle\E\biggl(\bigl(\Delta W\cdot f(W)+\frac{1}{2}\left|\Delta W\right|^{2}\cdot f^{\prime}(W)+R\bigr)\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}=V=k}\biggr)=0,

where
R\leqslant\frac{\left|f\right|_{2}}{6}|\Delta W|^{3}
.
Simplifying, we arrive at

\displaystyle\E\biggl(\left(-\lambda Wf(W)+\lambda f^{\prime}(W)(1+R_{2})+R\right)\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}=V}\mathds{1}_{V=k}\biggr)=0.

Thus

\displaystyle\E\left((f^{\prime}(W)-Wf(W))\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}=V}\mid V=k,W\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\left|f\right|_{1}\cdot\E\left(\left|R_{2}\right|\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}=V}\mid V=k,W\right)+\frac{\left|f\right|_{2}}{6\lambda}\cdot\E\left(|\Delta W|^{3}\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}=V}\mid V=k,W\right)\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

By (
4.2
) we have,

\pr(V^{\prime}=V\mid V=k,W)\approx 1-p_{2}.

Thus if
k
is near the mean of
V
, more specifically if
|k-np_{2}|\ll\sigma_{V}
using relation (
1.1
) we conclude that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\bigl((W\mid V=k),Z\bigr)

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

4.1.2
Uniform darts given number of misses

Let
T\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a set, called target, of area
q\in(0,1)
. Suppose
U_{1},U_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. uniform random vectors taking values in
[0,1]^{2}
, called darts. In particular, a dart hits the target with probability
\pr(U_{i}\in T)=q
and misses with probability
\pr(U_{i}\notin T)=1-q=p
. Let
s:T\to\mathds{R}
be a bounded non constant score function such that
\E s(U_{1})\mathds{1}_{U_{1}\in T}=0
and
\E s(U_{1})^{2}\mathds{1}_{U_{1}\in T}=1
.

Define

Y:=\sum_{i}\mathds{1}_{U_{i}\notin T}-np\quad\text{ and }\quad W:=n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sum_{i}^{n}s(U_{i})\mathds{1}_{U_{i}\in T},

as the centered number of darts that missed the target and the total score, respectively. Notice that
Y
takes values in
\{-np\}+\mathds{Z}
and that
\sigma_{Y}^{2}=npq
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
W
and
Y
as above and any
k\in\{np\}+\mathds{Z}
with
|k|\ll n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, we have that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\left((W\mid Y=k),Z\right)\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

First, we notice that the
W
and
Y
are uncorrelated. As in Example
4.1
, we will consider Glauber dynamics conditioned on keeping the value of
Y
to remain the same. Namely at time
n
we choose a dart
I\sim\textrm{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,n\}
and define

W^{\prime}=W-n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}s(U_{I})\mathds{1}_{U_{I}\in T}+n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}s(U^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{U^{\prime}\in T},

where
U^{\prime}\sim\textrm{Uniform}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)
and independent of everything else. It is easy to see that resulting vectors
(W,Y)
and
(W^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
are exchangeable, and with a positive probability, the total score changes while the number of misses remains the same. Notice that we utilize that the score function
s(u)
is not constant on
T
; otherwise,
Y^{\prime}=Y
would have implied
W^{\prime}=W
.
First we compute that

\displaystyle\E(\Delta W\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y^{\prime}=Y}\mid\bm{U})

\displaystyle=-\frac{q}{n}W

\displaystyle\textrm{ and }\E(|\Delta W|^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y^{\prime}=Y}\mid\bm{U})

\displaystyle=\frac{2q}{n}\left(1+R_{2}\right),

where
\bm{U}=(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n})
and

R_{2}:=-\frac{1}{2nq}Y+\frac{1}{2n}\E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(s(U_{i})^{2}\mathds{1}_{U_{i}\in T}-1)\ \biggl|\ W,Y\right).

Since
s(x)
is bounded we can bound
|\Delta W|\leqslant\left\|s\right\|_{\infty}
. By application of standard technique, we derive that for any
k\in\{np\}+\mathds{Z}
with
|k|\ll n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\bigl((W\mid Y=k),Z\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{f\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\left|f\right|_{1}\cdot\E\left|R_{2}\right|+\frac{\left|f\right|_{2}}{6\lambda}\E\left(|\Delta W|^{3}\mathds{1}_{Y^{\prime}=Y}\mid Y=k\right)\right\}\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

4.1.3
Number of
01
’s given the number of
1
’s in a random binary sequence

Let
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n})
be a sequence of i.i.d. Bernoulli
\left(p\right)
random variables with
\omega_{n+1}=\omega_{1}
. Let
V:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}
be the number of
1
’s in it and define

\displaystyle U:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\omega_{i}=0}\mathds{1}_{\omega_{i+1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\omega_{i})\omega_{i+1}

be the number of times a zero is followed by a one. Define

W^{0}:=U-\E\left(U\mid V=m\right)\text{ and }W:={W^{0}}/{\sigma_{W^{0}}}.

One can easily compute that

\displaystyle\E(U\mid V=m)

\displaystyle=\frac{m(n-m)}{n-1}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\sigma_{W_{0}}^{2}=\var(U\mid V=m)

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{m}{2}\binom{n-m}{2}}{(n-1)\binom{n-1}{2}}\approx n\cdot(m/n)^{2}(1-m/n)^{2}.

Moreover, we have the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
W
and
V
be as above. For any
m\in\mathds{N}
with
m/n\in(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
, we have that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\left((W\mid V=m),Z\right)\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

We will work with the random variable
X:=V-U=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{i+1}
. Consider the following construction of an exchangeable pair. For a binary sequence
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n})
, pick two indices
I
and
J

uniformly at random from
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and create a new binary sequence by swapping the bits at those locations
\bm{\omega}^{\prime}=(\omega_{1}^{\prime},\omega_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,\omega_{n}^{\prime})

such that
\omega_{i}^{\prime}=\omega_{i}
for all
i\notin\{I,J\}
,
\omega_{I}^{\prime}=\omega_{J}
, and
\omega_{J}^{\prime}=\omega_{I}
. We have

\displaystyle\Delta X=(\omega_{I}-\omega_{J})(\omega_{J+1}+\omega_{J-1}-\omega_{I+1}-\omega_{I-1})-(\omega_{I}-\omega_{J})^{2}\mathds{1}_{|I-J|=1}.

(4.3)

One can easily check that
|\Delta X|\leqslant 2
and

\E(\Delta X\mid V=m)=-\frac{4(n-1)}{n^{2}}\left(X-\frac{m(m-1)}{n-1}\right)

This gives another proof of the fact that
\E\left(X\mid V=m\right)=\frac{m(m-1)}{n-1}
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\E(\Delta W\mid V=m)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4(n-1)}{n^{2}}W

\displaystyle\text{and }\E(\left|\Delta W\right|^{2}\mid V=m)

\displaystyle=\frac{8(n-1)}{n^{2}}(1+R_{2}),

where
R_{2}
is a centered random variable with
\E\left|R_{2}\right|\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
that can be computed explicitly using (
4.3
).
By a similar derivation as in the Lemma
4.1
we have that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\bigl((W\mid V=m),Z\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{f\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\left|f\right|_{1}\E\left|R_{2}\right|+\frac{\left|f\right|_{2}}{6\lambda}\E\left(|\Delta W|^{3}\mid V=m\right)\right\}\lesssim\frac{n}{n^{3/2}}=n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

4.1.4
Number of wedges in a uniform graph with
m
edges

Let
\mathcal{G}\sim G(n,m)
, a graph on
n
vertices with
m
edges chosen uniformly at random.
For Erdős–Rényi random graph
G_{n,p}
the number of edges
E
is a sufficient statistic for the parameter
p
, thus the
model
(G_{n,p}\mid E=m)
is equivalent to
G(n,m)
. In other words, deriving CCLT in
G_{n,p}
conditioned on the number of edges being
m
can be converted into deriving regular CLT in
G(n,m)
.

Let
d_{i}=\sum_{j}\mathds{1}_{i\sim j}
be the degree of vertex
i
in
\mathcal{G}
, note that
\sum_{i}d_{i}=2m
and
\overline{d}_{i}:=d_{i}-\frac{2m}{n}
. The number of wedges
U:=\sum\mathds{1}_{i\sim j}\mathds{1}_{j\sim\ell}
and can be written as

U=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}d_{i}(d_{i}-1)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}d_{i}^{2}-m.

Lemma 4.4
.

We have
\E(U)=2m(m-1)/(n+1)
. Moreover, for
m\in\mathds{N}
with
m/N\in(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
, we have that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}((U-\E U)/\sigma_{U},Z)\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Define
W:=(U-\E U)/\sigma_{U}
and
X:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\overline{d}_{i}^{2}
. Notice that

U=X+\frac{2m^{2}}{n}-m.

Similar to the example in Section
4.1.3
we will consider the Markov Chain that swaps two uniformly chosen edges to create an exchangeable pair preserving the total amount of edges. Then

\displaystyle\Delta d_{i}=\begin{cases}+1&\textrm{w.p.~}\frac{1}{4N\left(N-1\right)}(n-1-d_{i})(m-d_{i})\\
-1&\textrm{w.p.~}\frac{1}{4N\left(N-1\right)}d_{i}\left(N-m-(n-1-d_{i})\right)\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Simple computations show that, for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, we have

\displaystyle\E\left(\Delta d_{i}\mid\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\left(N-1\right)}\overline{d_{i}}\qquad\text{ and}

\displaystyle\E\left(|\Delta d_{i}|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\left(N-1\right)}\left(\frac{2(n-1)(n-2)}{n}\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)+\frac{n-4}{n}\left(1-\frac{2m}{N}\right)\overline{d}_{i}+\frac{2\overline{d}_{i}^{2}}{N}\right).

Therefore, using

\displaystyle\Delta X=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta d_{i}\left(\Delta d_{i}+2\overline{d}_{i}\right).

we have that

\displaystyle\E(\Delta X\mid\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2N}\left(X-\frac{n-1}{n+1}\cdot m\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\right).

In particular, this implies that

\E(X)=\frac{n-1}{n+1}\cdot m\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\text{ and }\E(U)=\frac{2m(m-1)}{n+1}.

So, after scaling by
\sigma_{U}\approx n^{3/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\E\left(\Delta W\mid\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2N}W

\displaystyle\text{and }\E\left(\left|\Delta W\right|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}2(1+R_{2}),

where
R_{2}
has mean zero and
\E\left|R_{2}\right|\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.
Letting
\lambda:=\frac{1}{2N}
. To derive CCLT it remains to upper bound the error term
\lambda^{-1}\E\left(|\Delta W|^{3}\mid E=m\right)
, which we do as follows

\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}\E\bigl(\left|\Delta W\right|^{3}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\E\left(|\Delta W|\cdot\Delta W^{2}\right)\lesssim\lambda^{-1}n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot 2\lambda\approx n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},

and get that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}(W,Z)\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\E\left|R_{2}\right|+\frac{2}{3\lambda}\E\left|\Delta W\right|^{3}\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

to complete the proof.

\blacksquare

4.2
Applications of main results in one dimension

In this section, we present several applications of Theorem

and

We bounded all of the error terms as described in Remark

4.2.1
Difference between the number of
11
patterns that start at odd and even bits in a two-species binary sequence

Let
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n})
be a sequence of independent Bernoulli
\left(p_{i}\right)
random variables. We assume that
n
is even and
\omega_{n+1}:=\omega_{1}
. Let
p_{i}
be equal to
p
if

if

Consider the random variable
V:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}
, the number of
1

\bm{\omega}
, and

11

Define

, notice that
\E Y=0
and
\sigma_{Y}^{2}=npq
. Also, notice that
Y
is not a sufficient statistic for
p
, making this example particularly interesting. The random variable
X
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}\omega_{i}\,\omega_{i+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}\overline{\omega}_{i}\,\overline{\omega}_{i+1}.

This representation of

is particularly convenient in computations for the variance
\sigma_{X}^{2}=np^{2}(1-p)^{2}
and a variety of terms in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X
and
Y
be as above, define
W:=\frac{X}{\sigma_{X}}
. For
k\in\mathds{Z}
with
|k|\ll n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, we have that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\left((W\mid Y=k)\,,\,Z\right)\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\varepsilon}.

Notice that
W
and
Y
are uncorrelated random variables,
\sigma_{X}^{2}=np^{2}q^{2}
, and consider the following construction of an exchangeable pair. Pick a position
I
uniformly at random and replace it with an independent
\omega_{I}^{\prime}\sim
Bernoulli
(p_{I})
.
It follows that
(W,Y)
satisfies Assumptions
I
,
II
,
III
and
IV
with
\lambda=\frac{1}{n}
and
\psi=2
In particular,

\displaystyle M_{1,\pm}(W,Y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}(p+q)W=-\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2W,

\displaystyle M_{2,\pm}(W,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(2+R_{2,\pm}\right),

where

\displaystyle R_{2,+}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i-1}^{2}\overline{\omega}_{i}+p_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i+1}^{2}\overline{\omega}_{i}+2pq\overline{\omega}_{i-1}\overline{\omega}_{i+1}-2p_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i-1}\overline{\omega}_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i+1}

\displaystyle\text{and }R_{2,-}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i-1}^{2}\overline{\omega}_{i}+p_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i+1}^{2}\overline{\omega}_{i}-2pq\overline{\omega}_{i-1}\overline{\omega}_{i+1}-2p_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i-1}\overline{\omega}_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i+1}.

Notice that
R_{2,+}
and
R_{2,-}
have means equal to zero and variances of order
n
.

For
\hat{k}\in\{k-1,k\}
with
\left|k\right|\ll\sigma_{Y}
, by LLT we have

\pr(Y=\hat{k})\approx\sigma_{Y}^{-1}\approx n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Thus the error terms from Theorem
2.4
can be upper bounded as follows

\displaystyle\E(\left|R_{2,\pm}\right|\mid Y=\hat{k})\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\E(\left|W\right|\left|R_{0,\pm}\right|\mid Y=\hat{k})\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\E(\left|\Delta W\right|\mid Y=\hat{k})\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\varepsilon},

and

\left|\E\left(\left|\Delta W\right|^{3}\mid Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right)\right|=\left|\E\left(|\Delta W|\cdot\Delta W^{2}\mid Y\in\{k-1,k\}\right)\right|\lesssim\lambda n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\varepsilon}.

In the last bound we used that
\left|\Delta W\right|\leqslant 2/\sigma_{X}
.
Thus by Theorem
2.4
we get that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\left((W\mid Y=k)\,,\,Z\right)\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\varepsilon}

where the constant in the right hand side depends on
p
and
\varepsilon
.

\blacksquare

Remark 4.6

,

4.2.2
Number of

takes values in

.

and

For

\displaystyle=\E\left(\left(1+\frac{2\alpha k}{N}\right)X\ \biggl|\ Y=k\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2Npq}{n+1}\left(1-\frac{4\alpha^{2}k^{2}}{N^{2}}-\frac{k^{2}}{Npq}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{N}k\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2\frac{k^{2}-Npq}{n+1}+\frac{4\alpha k^{3}}{N\left(n+1\right)}-\frac{8pq\alpha^{2}k^{2}}{N(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{N-1}\left(\frac{n-2}{N}\cdot(k^{2}-Npq)+2\cdot\frac{(n-2)}{N^{2}}\cdot\alpha k^{3}\right)-\frac{8pq\alpha^{2}k^{2}}{N(n+1)}.

This matches
Y^{2}
and
Y^{3}
terms in (
4.6
) with a
1+O(1/N)
factor.

4.4
General subgraph count given the number of edges

Stein’s method for normal approximation and Stein–Chen’s method for Poisson convergence were contextualized to random graphs by Barbour in
[
2
]
. The techniques for proving Poisson convergence described in
[
2
]
were used shortly after in many results, such as
[
33
]
where many distributional convergence theorems were established in different regimes of Erdős–Rényi random graph. Due to technical difficulties, this method for normal approximation in the context of random graphs was limited to particular examples until the following general theorem was shown in
[
4
]
. The original result in
[
4
]
is stated in terms of the Fortet–Mourier distance. However, the proof can be adopted to derive analogous statement in Wasserstein distance using standard machinery.

Theorem 4.13
(
[
4
, Theorem 2]
).

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a graph on
v
vertices with
m
edges,
H
be the number of its copies in
G_{n,p}
, then

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{W}}\left((H-\E H)/\sigma_{H},Z\right)=k_{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\begin{cases}r(n,p)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}&p\geqslant\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\\
n^{-1}(1-p)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}&p<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\end{cases}

where
k_{\mathcal{H}}
is a constant depending on
\mathcal{H}
,

r(n,p):=\min_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H},e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})>0}\left\{n^{v(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}p^{e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}\right\},

and
v(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
,
e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
denote the number of vertices and edges in a graph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, respectively.

Corollary 4.14
(
[
4
, Remark 1 on p.133]
).

For fixed
p\in(0,1)
we have

d_{\mathcal{W}}\left((H-\E H)/\sigma_{H},Z\right)\lesssim n^{-1}.

It is important to notice that this result is based on the dependency graph approach. In this section, we will derive an analogous CCLT as a consequence of Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
4.12
.

From the applications in Section
4.3.2
and the decomposition in (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), it is natural to expect that in order to derive CCLT for a graph
\mathcal{H}
given the number of edges, one would have to do it for a vector containing all possible sub-graphs of
\mathcal{H}
. Surprisingly it is not the case, as illustrated by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a graph on
v
vertices with
m
edges,
H
be the number of its copies in
G_{n,p}
and
E
be the number of edges, then

\displaystyle\var{(H)}\approx n^{2v-2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\var{\left(H-\sigma_{H,E}/{\sigma_{E}^{2}}\cdot E\right)}\approx n^{2v-3}.

Let
\bm{s}
denote a subset of edges in the complete graph
\mathcal{K}_{n}
on
n
vertices that forms an isomorphic copy of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{n}(H)
denote the collection of all such
\bm{s}
’s. Define
|\mathcal{S}|
to be the size of the collection
\mathcal{S}
. This allows us to rewrite the sub-graph count as

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\prod_{e\in\bm{s}}\omega_{e}=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\omega_{\bm{s}},

where
\omega_{\bm{s}}:=\prod_{e\in\bm{s}}\omega_{e}
. It is easy to check that

\displaystyle\sigma_{H,E}:=\cov(H,E)=mp^{m}q\cdot|\mathcal{S}|.

In particular we can write

\displaystyle\widehat{H}

\displaystyle:=H-\frac{\sigma_{H,E}}{\sigma_{E}^{2}}E=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\omega_{\bm{s}}-\frac{m}{N}p^{m-1}|\mathcal{S}|\cdot E.

(4.11)

We rewrite
\omega_{\bm{s}}
as

\displaystyle\omega_{\bm{s}}=\prod_{e\in\bm{s}}\omega_{e}=\prod_{e\in\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\omega}_{e}+p)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}p^{m-\ell}\sum_{\bm{s}^{\prime}\subseteq\bm{s},|\bm{s}^{\prime}|=\ell}\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}

where
\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}:=\prod_{e\in\bm{s}^{\prime}}\overline{\omega}_{e}
.
Given an edge-set
\bm{s}^{\prime}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}:=\{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}\mid\bm{s}^{\prime}\subseteq\bm{s}\}.

Given an edge
e
, size of the set
\{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}\mid e\in\bm{s}\}
is independent of
e
and thus by symmetry the common size is given by
\frac{m}{N}|\mathcal{S}|
. In particular,
\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{e\in\bm{s}}\overline{\omega}_{e}=\frac{m}{N}|\mathcal{S}|\cdot\overline{E}
and from (
4.11
) we get

\displaystyle\widehat{H}-\E\widehat{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=2}^{m}p^{m-\ell}\sum_{\bm{s}^{\prime}:|\bm{s}^{\prime}|=\ell}\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}\cdot|\mathcal{S}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}|.

(4.12)

It is easy to see that
\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}}
and
\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}
are uncorrelated whenever
\bm{s}\neq\bm{s}^{\prime}
.
Let
v^{\prime}
be the number of vertices of a graph whose edges form
\bm{s}^{\prime}
. Recall that
v=|V(\mathcal{H})|
. Thus it remains to estimate the following quantity for
\ell\geqslant 2
,

\displaystyle\var\left(\sum_{\bm{s}^{\prime}:|\bm{s}^{\prime}|=\ell}\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}\cdot|\mathcal{S}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\bm{s}^{\prime}|=\ell}|\mathcal{S}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}|^{2}\cdot(pq)^{\ell}

\displaystyle\approx n^{2(v-v^{\prime})}n^{v^{\prime}}\approx n^{2v-3},

where in the second equality we used the fact that
|\mathcal{S}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}|\approx n^{v-v^{\prime}}
for
\bm{s}^{\prime}\subseteq\bm{s}
,
|\{\bm{s}^{\prime}:|\bm{s}^{\prime}|=\ell\}|\approx n^{v^{\prime}}
; and in the last equality we used the fact that
\ell\geqslant 2
implies
v^{\prime}\geqslant 3
.

\blacksquare

Definition 4.16

(Extension function)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{n}
be the complete graph on
n
vertices, and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
be a sub-graph of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. For any fixed graph
\mathcal{H}
, we define
\mathrm{ext}_{n}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime},\mathcal{H})
to be the number of ways to extend the given graph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
to an isomorphic copy of
\mathcal{H}
inside of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.

For example if
\mathcal{T}
is the triangle and
\mathcal{K}_{4}
is the complete graph on four vertices then
\mathrm{ext}_{n}(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{K}_{4})=n-3
, while if
\mathcal{V}
is the wedge graph and
\mathcal{P}_{4}
is the path with four vertices then
\mathrm{ext}_{n}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{P}_{4})=2(n-3)
. As a consequence of Lemma
4.15
and a bit more analysis, we have the following lemma.

Let
\widetilde{T}
and
\widetilde{U}
are centered edge counts for triangles and wedges as in (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) respectively.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a graph on
v
vertices with
m

be the triangle and

.

\E|R_{H}|^{2\theta}
for an integer

. Let
\bm{s}^{\prime}
denote a subset of edges in the complete graph
\mathcal{K}_{n}
on
n
vertices that forms an isomorphic copy of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, such that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\E\left|R_{H}\right|^{2\theta}\lesssim n^{2\theta v-4\theta}\approx\sigma_{\widehat{H}}^{2\theta}n^{-\theta}.

\blacksquare

The decomposition from Lemma

yields that the terms involving

\displaystyle\lesssim p_{k}^{-1}\cdot(\E|R_{H}|^{2\theta})^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}\cdot p_{k}^{1-\frac{1}{2\theta}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma_{\widehat{H}}\cdot n^{-(\theta-1)/(2\theta)},

for all

\displaystyle\rho_{1}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{ext}_{n}(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{H})\cdot\sigma_{\widetilde{T}}}{\sigma_{\widehat{H}}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho_{2}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{ext}_{n}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{H})\cdot\sigma_{\widetilde{U}}}{\sigma_{\widehat{H}}}.

4.12

as in (

in (

) and derived CCLT given the number of edges for this random vector.
Lemma
4.12
together with Lemma
4.17
gives the following general result.

Theorem 4.18
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite graph,
H
be the number of times
\mathcal{H}
appears in
G_{n,p}
,
\widehat{H}:=H-\frac{\sigma_{H,E}}{\sigma_{E}^{2}}E

For any

with
|k|\ll n

There are several reasons why the upper bound of the rate of convergence in this theorem is slower than that in Corollary

7
Closing remarks and further work

Before our work, few results delve into CCLT in general settings. There are many aspects in which we would like to see our approach extended and improved, including generalizing the current approach, extending it to other dependency structures between random variables, and connecting it to concentration inequalities. In this article, we often utilize subtle, sometimes surprising, cancellations caused by exchangeability. Hence we believe that there is much more to understand in this area than we presently know. We discuss our results and possible future directions and state questions of particular interest in the remaining of this section.

7.1
Change of variable and the assumptions

For our main result, we require random variables to satisfy symmetric linearity conditions (Assumption
III
), and the second-moment condition (Assumption
IV
). It is important to notice that these assumptions together with Assumption
II
yield linearity condition for
(\bm{W},Y/\sigma_{Y})
. Hence if
R_{i,\pm}
are small for
i=0,1,2
the multivariate Stein’s method for exchangeable pair implies joint convergence to a
d+1
-dimensional Gaussian vector.

Given the non-symmetric linearity condition, one can make it symmetric by subtracting the product of random variables with an appropriate coefficient (the
XY
term in the change of variable). When working with counting random variables, it is often the case that the linearity condition is of the form
\lambda(\psi a_{\pm}W+b_{\pm}Y)
(Assumption
IIIa
); as we have seen in Section
4.2.2
on the sub-pattern count example and in Section
4.2.3
on the sub-graph count example. However, in such examples,
M_{0,\pm}
also has a particular form that we state in the form of the Assumption
II
. We utilized this fact to a great extent in the change of variable and the proof of Theorem
2.4
and
3.1
, leading to the following question.

Question 1
.

Is it possible to derive CCLT in the models with
|\Delta\!Y|=1
that satisfy Assumption
IIIa
but
M_{0,\pm}\approx Q+f_{\pm}(Y)
for non-linear
f
?

We believe that in such cases, our approach is still applicable, and if the conditional mean is not known, its approximation in the form of change of variable would be different, but analogous, from the one presented in (
2.4
) and (
3.1
).

7.2
Range of
\Delta\!Y

In this article, we focused on the case where
|\Delta\!Y|\leqslant 1
. When
Y
can change by more than
\pm 1
but
\pr(|\Delta\!Y|=1)
is sufficiently large, conditioning on this event, one can still apply our results similar to the way how we used the classical methods by conditioning on the event
\{\Delta\!Y=0\}
in the Section
4.1
. However, in the models where
\pr(|\Delta\!Y|=1)
is negligible, it remains open to extending our approach.

Question 2
.

Is it possible to derive CCLT with an explicit convergence rate for models where
\Delta\!Y
can take infinitely many values?

For example, consider the number of edges given the number of triangles in the Erdős–Rényi random graph. Another immediate application would be the extension of
[
6
, Theorem 4]
and
[
31
, Theorem 4.2]
, for
\delta=1
, to the dependent settings.

7.3
Other types of events than
\{Y=k\}

In this article, we condition on the simplest type of the event
\{Y=k\}
where
Y
is the sum of indicators, as it already required a considerable amount of effort and novel techniques. The next step is to extend our result to CCLT where one conditions on a vector
\{(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{d})=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{d})\}
sill under the assumption of joint Gaussian convergence. A natural application would be a joint CCLT for a sub-graph count given a value for several other sub-graph counts in the Erdős–Rényi random graph.

Question 3
.

How does the rate of convergence depend on the dimension of the vector
\bm{Y}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{d})
?

Another, the more complicated direction, is to relax the assumption of joint Gaussian convergence and condition on more complicated events such as some property of the trajectory of a random walk or random environment.

7.4
Sufficient statistic

In most of the applications presented in this paper, we condition on the sufficient statistic. We believe our theorem should reliably work without this condition, as we demonstrated in Lemma
4.5
. However, while writing this paper, we realized that we do not know of many natural examples satisfying our assumptions that will also not be sufficient statistics for the parameters of the model, especially in the univariate case. If one relaxes our condition on
\Delta\!Y
and allows it to range over an infinite set, this will create many natural examples with conditioning on non-sufficient statistics.

Besides the application presented in Section
4.2.1
, where
Y
was not a sufficient statistic for the parameter
p
, another application could be a “noisy" version of a sufficient statistic. For example, suppose we would like to condition on the number of edges in an inhomogeneous random graph where each of
N(1-\delta)
edges is present independently with probability
p
and each of the remaining
\delta N
edges is present with probability
p\pm\varepsilon
independently from everything else. One can estimate the difference between this model and the homogeneous random graph and work with the latter. However, depending on the
\delta
and
\varepsilon
, the error might be significant. Our approach provides an alternative that allows working directly with inhomogeneous models.

7.5
Other approaches

It is of interest to extend other existing approaches of Stein’s method to the conditional setting, particularly the dependency graph approach in relation to the subgraph counting problem (See Section
4.4
). Since, in this work, we assume that
(W,Y)
jointly converges to a Gaussian vector, a natural place to start would be adopting a multivariate extension of the dependency graph approach such as in
[
22
]
or some other variation of the method to the conditional setting.

Question 4
.

Is it possible to get the rate of converges of order
n^{-1}
in Theorem
4.18
using another approach than the exchangeable pair to match the bound in Corollary
4.14
?

7.6
Other distances between distributions

In this article, we use Wasserstein-
1
distance to quantify the rate of convergence. Suppose one is interested in bounding other metrics, such as Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance. Then one has to work with functions with fewer derivatives. In that case, one can usually approximate those functions by two or three times differentiable functions to apply our techniques. However, that would result in an extra loss in the rate of convergence. Thus it remains open to acquiring optimal bounds on the convergence rates in other distances directly if at all possible.

\E\left|\bm{W}\right|^{4}<\infty
, we get that

\displaystyle\left|\E(h(\bm{W})-h(\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{Z})\mid B)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant M_{1}(h)\cdot\left(A_{0}\cdot\sin\varepsilon+c_{0}\cdot A_{1}+\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}\cdot A_{2}+c_{3}\cdot\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{3}\cdot A_{4}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin\varepsilon}\right)

for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\pi/2]
. Taking
\sin^{2}\varepsilon=\min(c_{3}A_{4}\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{3}/A_{0},1)
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\E(h(\bm{W})-h(\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{Z})\mid B)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant M_{1}(h)\cdot\left(A_{1}+\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}\cdot A_{2}+\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{3/2}\cdot\sqrt{4c_{3}A_{0}A_{4}}\right).

Note that, when
c_{3}A_{4}\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{3}\geqslant A_{0}
, we can directly use the upper bound

M_{1}(h)\cdot A_{0}\sin(\pi/2)\leqslant M_{1}(h)\cdot\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{3/2}\cdot\sqrt{4c_{3}A_{0}A_{4}}.

When,
\E\left|\bm{W}\right|^{3}<\infty
, we similarly get

\displaystyle\left|\E(h(\bm{W})-h(\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{Z})\mid B)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant M_{1}(h)\cdot\left(A_{0}\cdot\sin\varepsilon+A_{1}+\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}\cdot A_{2}+c_{2}\cdot\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{2}\cdot A_{3}\cdot\log(1/\sin\varepsilon)\right)

for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\pi/2]
. Here we use the fact that

\int_{\varepsilon}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos^{2}\alpha}{\sin\alpha}d\alpha\leqslant\int_{\sin\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{dt}{t}=\log\frac{1}{\sin\varepsilon}.

Choosing
\sin\varepsilon=\min(c_{2}\cdot\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{2}\cdot A_{3}/A_{0},1)
, we get the bound

\displaystyle\left|\E(h(\bm{W})-h(\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{Z})\mid B)\right|\leqslant M_{1}(h)\cdot\biggl(A_{1}+\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}\cdot A_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+c_{2}\cdot\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{2}\cdot A_{3}\cdot\biggl(1+\left|\log\bigl({c_{2}\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\op}^{2}\cdot A_{3}}/{A_{0}}\bigr)\right|\biggr)\biggr).

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

The following lemma shows that under LLT
|p_{k}-p_{k-1}|\ll p_{k}
, and in particular,
r_{k}=p_{k-1}/p_{k}
is bounded away from
\infty
, for
|k|\ll\sigma_{Y}
.

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose
Y
is a random variable that take values in
\zeta+\mathds{Z}
for some
\zeta\in[0,1)
. Assume
Y
has mean
0
and variance
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
and is such that for all
k\in\zeta+\mathds{Z}

\left|\sigma_{Y}p_{k}-\sigma_{Y}\varphi_{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}(k)\right|\leqslant\varepsilon_{Y}<1,

where
p_{k}:=\pr(Y=k)
and
\varepsilon_{Y}
is some function. Then, for
|k|\ll\sigma_{Y}

\displaystyle\left|1-p_{k-1}/p_{k}\right|\lesssim(1-\varepsilon_{Y})^{-1}\cdot\max\left\{\varepsilon_{Y},{k}/{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right\}.

By LLT we have that

\left|\sigma_{Y}p_{k}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-{k^{2}}/{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right)\right|\leqslant\varepsilon_{Y},

where
\varepsilon_{Y}=o(1)
as
n\to\infty
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{p_{k-1}}{p_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\left|\exp\left(-{k^{2}}/{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right)-\exp\left(-{(k-1)^{2}}/{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right)\right|}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{Y}p_{k}}+\frac{2\varepsilon_{Y}}{\sigma_{Y}p_{k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1-\varepsilon_{Y})^{-1}\cdot\max\left(\varepsilon_{Y},{k}/{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right)

for
|k|\ll\sigma_{Y}

and the proof is complete.

\blacksquare

B
Computations for Lemma
4.9

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a graph on
v
vertices and
m
edges, and let
H
be the centered random variable that counts the number of its copies in
G_{n,p}
. Let
\bm{s}
denote a subset of edges in the complete graph
\mathcal{K}_{n}
on
n
vertices that form an isomorphic copy of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{n}(H)
denote the collection of all such
\bm{s}
’s. Define
|\mathcal{S}|
to be the size of the collection
\mathcal{S}
. Thus we can rewrite the sub-graph count as

H=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\prod_{e\in\bm{s}}\omega_{e}=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\omega_{\bm{s}},

It is often more convenient to work with centered edges as it makes as we did in (
4.7
) and (
4.8
). Thus define

\widetilde{H}:=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\prod_{e\in\bm{s}}\overline{\omega}_{e}=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}}.

Lemma B.1
.

With the notations as above for fixed
p\in(0,1)
we have that

\var{\widetilde{H}}=O\left(n^{v}\right).

Given two
\bm{s}
and
\bm{s}^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{S}
. The expectation
\E\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}}\widetilde{\omega}_{\bm{s}^{\prime}}
is nonzero if and only if each edge appears twice in the product. Since
\bm{s}
and
\bm{s}^{\prime}
induce isomporphic graphs this yields that
\bm{s}
has to be equal to
\bm{s}^{\prime}
. Thus
V(\bm{s}\cup\bm{s}^{\prime})=v
and thus the variance of
\widetilde{H}=O(n^{v})
.

\blacksquare

In this section, we present explicit computations that we used in the derivation of the CCLT for the number of wedges given the number of edges in a random graph in Lemma
4.9
.
Recall that
G_{n,p}
is the Erdős–Rényi random graph and
q:=1-p
.
Let
E=\sum_{x<y}\mathds{1}_{x\sim y}=\sum_{x<y}\omega_{xy}
be the number of edges in
G_{n,p}
, and

V=\sum_{x<y,z\neq x,y}\omega_{xy}\omega_{yz}
be the number of wedges. Define
X=V-2(n-2)pY-(n-2)\binom{n}{2}p^{2}
and
Y=E-\E E
. Finally recall we place bar above the random variable to denote the centered version of it.

It is straight forward to check that
X
is uncorrelated with
Y
and could be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j,k\neq i,j}\overline{\omega}_{ik}\overline{\omega}_{kj}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j,k\neq i,j}(\omega_{ik}-p)(\omega_{kj}-p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j,k\neq i,j}(\omega_{ik}\omega_{kj}-p\omega_{ik}-p\omega_{jk}+p^{2})

\displaystyle=V-2(n-2)pY-(n-2)\binom{n}{2}p^{2}=X.

In spirit of Lemma
B.1
, this representation is particularly helpful in computing the variance of
X

\sigma_{X}^{2}=\binom{n}{2}(n-2)p^{2}q^{2}=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)(n-2)p^{2}(1-p)^{2}.

To compute the first order terms we also work with centered edges and derive that

\displaystyle M_{1,+}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{N}\sum_{i<j}(q-\overline{\omega}_{ij})\left(\sum_{k\neq i,j}\overline{\omega}_{ik}+\overline{\omega}_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}(\overline{\omega}_{ij}-q)\sum_{k\neq i\neq j}\overline{\omega}_{ik}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{N}\sum_{i\neq j\neq k}\overline{\omega}_{ij}\overline{\omega}_{ik}+\frac{pq}{N}\sum_{i\neq k\neq j}\overline{\omega}_{ik}=-\frac{2}{N}(pX-pq(n-2)Y).

Similarly

\displaystyle M_{1,-}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{N}\sum_{i<j}(\overline{\omega}_{ij}+p)\left(-\sum_{k\neq i,j}\overline{\omega}_{ik}+\overline{\omega}_{jk}\right)=-\frac{2}{N}(qX+pq(n-2)Y).

For the second order terms we compute

\displaystyle M_{2,+}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{N}\sum_{i<j}(q-\overline{\omega}_{ij})\left(\sum_{k\neq i,j}\overline{\omega}_{ik}+\overline{\omega}_{jk}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{N}\sum_{i<j}(q-\overline{\omega}_{ij})\left(\overline{d}_{i}+\overline{d}_{j}-2\overline{\omega}_{ij}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}(q-\overline{\omega}_{ij})\left(\overline{d}_{i}^{2}+\overline{d}_{i}\overline{d}_{j}+2\overline{\omega}_{ij}^{2}-4\overline{\omega}_{ij}\overline{d}_{i}\right)

Letting

I_{ij}:=\overline{d}_{i}^{2}+\overline{d}_{i}\overline{d}_{j}+2\overline{\omega}_{ij}^{2}-4\overline{\omega}_{ij}\overline{d}_{i},

we get that

M_{2,+}(X,Y)=\frac{pq}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}I_{ij}-\frac{p}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\overline{\omega}_{ij}I_{ij}.

The second term counts centered-edge graphs that have at most four distinct vertices and hence by Lemma
B.1

\E\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\overline{\omega}_{ij}I_{ij}\right|\lesssim n^{2}.

Letting
\overline{d}_{i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}\overline{\omega}_{ij}
, the first summand gives the variance terms required in the Assumption (
IV
) in the following way

\displaystyle\frac{pq}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}I_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{pq}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\left(\overline{d}_{i}^{2}+\overline{d}_{i}\overline{d}_{j}+2\overline{\omega}_{ij}^{2}-4\overline{\omega}_{ij}\overline{d}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{pq}{N}\left((n-6)\sum_{i}\overline{d}_{i}^{2}+\left(\sum_{i}\overline{d}_{i}\right)^{2}+4\sum_{i<j}\overline{\omega}_{ij}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\left(\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{2}p^{2}q^{2}+(n-6)pqX+R_{2,+}\right),

where

R_{2,+}:=pq\left(2\left(E^{2}-Npq\right)+(n-4)\sum_{i<j}(\overline{\omega}_{ij}^{2}-pq)\right).

with

\E|R_{2,+}|^{2}\lesssim n^{5}.

M_{2,-}
is treated similarly as one can rewrite in as

M_{2,-}(X,Y)=\frac{pq}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}I_{ij}+\frac{q}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\overline{\omega}_{ij}I_{ij}.

The third order terms we can bound by

\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}\E(\left|\Delta W\right|^{3}\mid W,Y)

\displaystyle\leqslant\E(\left|\Delta W\right|\left|\Delta W\right|^{2}\mid W,Y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-1}\frac{n-2}{n^{3/2}}\lambda(\psi+\left|R_{2,+}\right|+\left|R_{2,-}\right|)\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.
