Onto extensions of free groups
Enric Ventura

Abstract
An extension of subgroups H ⩽ K ⩽ F A H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A} of the free group of rank | A | = r ⩾ 2 |A|=r\geqslant 2 is called onto when, for every ambient basis A ′ A^{\prime} , the Stallings graph Γ A ′ ​ ( K ) \Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K) is a quotient of Γ A ′ ​ ( H ) \Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H) . Algebraic extensions are onto and the converse implication was conjectured by Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil, and resolved in the negative, first by Parzanchevski–Puder for rank r = 2 r=2 , and recently by Kolodner for general rank. In this note we study properties of this new type of extension among free groups (as well as the fully onto variant), and investigate their corresponding closure operators. Interestingly, the natural attempt for a dual notion – into extensions– becomes trivial, making a Takahasi type theorem not possible in this setting.

Free group, subgroup extension, onto extension, algebraic extension, Stallings graph

The present paper is an elaborated version of Mijares
[
6
]
, the masters thesis defended by the first author at the Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya in July 2020. The goal of this masters thesis was to understand the recent interesting counterexample given by Kolodner
[
2
]
to a previous conjecture from Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil
[
5
]

The paper is organized as follows: in Section 1 we present the necessary context and background about free groups and Stallings graphs needed for the development; in Section 2 we reformulate the Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil conjecture and prove it, following an idea of Parzanchevski–Puder (who gave the first counterexample to the original version of the conjecture); in Section 3 we study the new concepts of onto and fully onto extensions of free groups arising from Kolodner’s more elaborated counterexample to the strengthened Parzanchevski–Puder version of the conjecture; in Section 4, we study onto and fully onto closures of subgroups, and observe that the natural attempt for a dual notion (that of into entensions) becomes trivial, eliminating the possibility of a new version of Takahasi’s theorem in this setting; finally, in Section 5, we state and comment some open questions arising naturally in this context.

g\mapsto g\alpha

1
Context and background

Let
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\}
be an alphabet of
r
letters, let
A^{\pm}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{r},a_{1}^{-1},\ldots,a_{r}^{-1}\}
be its formal involutive closure, and let
F_{A}
be the free group on
A
(formally, the free monoid on
A^{\pm}
modulo the equivalence relation generated by elementary reduction,
a_{i}a_{i}^{-1}\sim a_{i}^{-1}a_{i}\sim 1
).

In 1983, elaborating on previous ideas by several authors, Stallings
[
11
]
established the notion of so-called
Stallings
A
-automata
: oriented graphs (allowing loops and parallel edges) with labels from
A^{\pm}
at the edges, being
involutive
(i.e., for every edge
e
from
p
to
q
with label
a
, there is another one
e^{-1}
from
q
to
p
and labelled
a^{-1}
;
e
and
e^{-1}
are said to be inverse to each other), with a selected vertex called the
basepoint
(denoted
\circledcirc
), and being connected,
deterministic
(no two different edges with the same label from, or into, the same vertex) and
trim
(every vertex appears in some reduced closed path at the basepoint). Here, a path is called
reduced
if it has no backtracking, i.e., no crossings of an edge immediately followed by its inverse (equivalently in the deterministic case, a path is reduced if and only if its label is a reduced word).

With this notion, Stallings
[
11
]
established a bijection between the set of (free) subgroups of
F_{A}
and the set of isomorphism classes of
Stallings
A
-automata
. Further, finitely generated subgroups correspond to finite Stallings
A
-automata and, when restricted to these subsets, the Stallings bijection is algorithmic friendly, i.e., there are fast algorithms for both directions: given a set of words
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}
on
A
, one can compute the Stallings graph
\Gamma_{A}(H)
corresponding to the subgroup they generate,
H=\langle h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\rangle\leqslant F_{A}
. And, given a Stallings
A
-automaton
\Gamma
, one can compute a basis for its
language
subgroup
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)\leqslant F_{A}
(the set of labels of reduced closed paths at the basepoint in
\Gamma
). We assume the reader is familiar with this theory; see
[
1
,
5
,
11
]
for details.

{thm}

[Stallings,
[
11
]
]
The following is a bijection:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}St\colon\{H\leqslant F_{A}\}&\longrightarrow&\{\mbox{isom. classes of Stallings $A$-automata}\}\\
H&\mapsto&\Gamma_{A}(H)\\
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)&\mapsfrom&\Gamma.\end{array}

Furthermore,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
if and only if
\Gamma_{A}(H)
is finite; in this case, both directions are computable. ∎

From now on, we will mostly consider involutive
A
-automata and will describe and draw them just mentioning their positive part (i.e., those edges labelled by letters in
A
); next to each positive edge we implicitly assume its inverse is also there (even if we do not mention it), ready to be used by paths around the automaton. The positive subautomaton of
\Gamma
is denoted
\Gamma^{+}
(and it is obviouly not involutive, unless edgeless).

For later use, we briefly explain how the above mentioned algorithms work. Suppose we are given a finite set of reduced words,
W=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\}\subset F_{A}
. Draw the so-called
flower automaton

\mathcal{F}(W)
: for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, consider a circular graph with
A
-labels at the edges (usually called a
petal
) in such a way that, when travelled around, it spells the word
h_{i}=h_{i}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
(or its inverse if travelled in the opposite direction), and glue all of them together along their basepoints
\circledcirc
. Note that
\mathcal{F}(W)
is trim, deterministic except maybe at the basepoint, and satisfies
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}(W))=H
, where
H=\langle W\rangle\leqslant F_{A}
. To obtain a deterministic automaton, successively apply elementary foldings: whenever we have two edges with the same label from, or into, the same vertex, identify them into a single one (as well as their terminal, or initial, vertices). This process always terminates in a finite number of steps, and it can be proven that the final
A
-automaton obtained in this way, denoted
\Gamma_{A}(H)
, is deterministic, trim, reads the same language
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{A}(H))=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}(W))=H
, and, more significantly, is independent from the specific sequence of foldings applied, and even from the set of generators of
H
we started with: the Stallings graph
\Gamma_{A}(H)
only depends on the subgroup
H\leqslant F_{A}
(and on the ambient basis
A
chosen to work with). Conversely, given a finite Stallings
A
-automaton
\Gamma
, we can choose a spanning tree
T
(more precisely, the involutive closure of a spanning tree of
\Gamma^{+}
), and get the basis of
H=\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)\leqslant F_{A}
given by
\{h_{e}\mid e\in E\Gamma^{+}\setminus ET\}
, where
h_{e}=\operatorname{lab}(T[\circledcirc,\iota e]eT[\tau e,\circledcirc])
and
T[p,q]
stands for the unique reduced path in
T
from vertex
p
to vertex
q
, i.e., the
T
-geodesic
from
p
to
q
.

By
degree
of a vertex
p
in an involutive
A
-automaton
\Gamma
we mean the out-degree of
p
. Note that this equals the in-degree of
p
in
\Gamma
, and also the total (in- plus out-) degree of
p
in its positive part
\Gamma^{+}
.

For an
A
-automaton
\Gamma
, define its
core
, denoted
c(\Gamma)
, as its largest trim subautomaton, i.e., that determined by the vertices and edges appearing in some reduced closed path at
\circledcirc
; so,
\Gamma
is trim if and only if
c(\Gamma)=\Gamma
. It is easy to see that, when
\Gamma
is finite and connected, this is equivalent to saying that no vertex in
\Gamma
has degree 1 except maybe
\circledcirc
; in this case, one can get
c(\Gamma)
from
\Gamma
by applying finitely many times the trim operation: remove a vertex of degree one different from
\circledcirc
(together with the corresponding edge).

An
A
-automata homomorphism
(
A
-homomorphism
, for short) from
\Gamma
to
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a pair of maps
\theta=(\theta_{V},\theta_{E})
,
\theta_{V}\colon V\Gamma\to V\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\theta_{E}\colon E\Gamma\to E\Gamma^{\prime}
(where
V\Gamma
and
E\Gamma
denote the sets of vertices and edges of
\Gamma
, respectively), such that
\circledcirc\theta=\circledcirc^{\prime}
and, for every
a
-labelled edge
e
from
p
to
q
in
\Gamma
,
e\theta_{E}
is an
a
-labelled edge from
p\theta_{V}
to
q\theta_{V}
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
; we shall abuse language and write
\theta=\theta_{V}=\theta_{E}
. Such a
\theta
is called
onto
(resp.
into
) if both
\theta_{V}
and
\theta_{E}
are onto (resp. into). For an onto
A
-homomorphism we will use the notation
\theta\colon\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}
. Observe that, for
\Gamma
connected and
\Gamma^{\prime}
deterministic, there exists at most one
A
-homomorphism from
\Gamma
to
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Stallings bijection behaves well with respect to inclusions in the sense that, for two subgroups
H,K\leqslant F_{A}
,
H\leqslant K
if and only if there is an
A
-homomorphism from
\Gamma_{A}(H)
to
\Gamma_{A}(K)
which, in this case, is unique and will be denoted
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A}(H)\to\Gamma_{A}(K)
.

Since 1983, the Stallings bijection became central for the modern understanding and study of the lattice of subgroups of a free group. With the development of these graphical techniques, many new results have been obtained about free groups and their subgroups. Also, most of the results known before Stallings
[
11
]
have been reproven using graphical techniques, usually with conceptually simpler and more transparent proofs. Takahasi theorem is a typical example illustrating this fact.

An extension of subgroups
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
is called
free
(we also say that
H
is a
free factor
of
K
), denoted
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
, whenever some (so, any) basis of
H
can be extended to a basis for
K
; for example, it is easy to see that, for
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
, if
\Gamma_{A}(H)
is a subautomaton of
\Gamma_{A}(K)
then
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
(just extend a spanning tree for
\Gamma_{A}(H)
to a spanning tree for
\Gamma_{A}(K)
), while the converse is far from true. The notion of free factor is the non-abelian version of the notion of direct summand from commutative algebra. In a vector space, every pair of subspaces
E\leqslant F
is in direct sum position,
E\leqslant_{\oplus}F
, i.e.,
F=E\oplus E^{\prime}
for some complementary subspace
E^{\prime}
. When we consider, for example, free abelian groups (i.e., free modules over
\mathbb{Z}
) the exact same result is not true, but it works if we admit a bit of flexibility: every subgroup
H\leqslant\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is of finite index only in finitely many subgroups
H=H_{0}\leqslant_{\textsf{fi}}H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}\leqslant\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and, for every
K\leqslant\mathbb{Z}^{m}
containing
H
, there exists a unique
i
such that
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fi}}H_{i}\leqslant_{\oplus}K\leqslant\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. Of course, the situation in the free group seems much wilder, starting from the well known fact that
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
does not even imply
\mathrm{rk}(H)\leqslant\mathrm{rk}(K)
(to the extreme that
F_{\aleph_{0}}
can be viewed as a subgroup of
F_{2}
). However, back in the 1950’s, Takahasi
[
12
]
proved that, again, the same result adapts to the free group case, after admitting a little bit more of degeneration: we will have to restrict ourselves to finitely generated subgroups, and we will lose the finite index condition.

Takahasi theorem was proved 70 years ago using purely combinatorial and algebraic techniques. However, in more recent years, it was rediscovered independently, by Ventura
[
13
]
in 1997, by Margolis–Sapir–Weil
[
4
]
in 2001, and by Kapovich–Miasnikov
[
1
]
in 2002, in slightly different contexts; see also the subsequent paper Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil
[
5
]
joining the three points of view. These authors, independently, gave their own proofs of Takahasi’s theorem, and they happened to be essentially the same proof; we would say, the “natural” proof of this result using Stallings graphs. Let us sketch it here, since it will play a central role along the rest of the paper.

{thm}

[Takahasi,
[
12
]
]
Let
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
. Then
H
determines finitely many finitely generated extensions
H=H_{0},H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}\leqslant F_{A}
such that, for every
K\leqslant F_{A}
containing
H
, there exists
i
such that
H\leqslant H_{i}\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K\leqslant F_{A}
.

Sketch of proof.

Given
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
, consider its (finite) Stallings graph
\Gamma_{A}(H)
. Identifying certain sets of vertices, we get a new
A
-automaton which may very well be not deterministic; apply then a sequence of foldings until obtaining a deterministic one, say
\Gamma_{1}
, which will correspond to a finitely generated overgroup of
H
, say
H\leqslant\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{1})\leqslant F_{A}
. Repeating this operation for every possible partition on the set of vertices
V\Gamma_{A}(H)
(and possibly getting the same result for different initial partitions), we obtain a finite number of overgroups of
H
, say
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)=\{H=H_{0},H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}\}
, called the
A
-fringe of
H
in
[
13
]
, which satisfies the statement of Takahasi theorem. In fact, let
K
be a (non-necessarily finitely generated) subgroup with
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
, and look at the corresponding
A
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A}(H)\to\Gamma_{A}(K)
. Looking at the image
\operatorname{Im}(\theta_{H,K})
as a (finite and deterministic) subautomaton of
\Gamma_{A}(K)
, we see that: (1) the
A
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Im}(\theta_{H,K})
is onto and so,
\mathcal{L}(\operatorname{Im}(\theta_{H,K}))\in\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
; and (2)
\operatorname{Im}(\theta_{H,K})
is a subautomaton of
\Gamma_{A}(K)
and so,
\mathcal{L}(\operatorname{Im}(\theta_{H,K}))\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
. This proves that the
A
-fringe of
H
is a finite family of finitely generated overgroups of
H
satisfying the property stated in Takahasi theorem (a
Takahasi family
, for short).
∎

As done in Kapovich–Miasnikov
[
1
]
and in Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil
[
5
]
, it is possible to clean up the
fringe of
H
,
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
, in order to obtain a minimal Takahasi family, and gain uniqueness of the middle subgroup
H_{i}
in Theorem
1
; moreover, this minimal Takahasi family will have a clear algebraic meaning as follows. A subgroup extension
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
is called
algebraic
, denoted
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
, if
H
is not contained in any proper free factor of
K
; denote by
\mathcal{AE}(H)
the set of algebraic extensions of
H
(within
F_{A}
). By Takahasi’s theorem,
\mathcal{AE}(H)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
and so,
|\mathcal{AE}(H)|<\infty
for every finitely generated
H
. Furthermore it can be seen that, applying the following cleaning process to
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
, one obtains precisely
\mathcal{AE}(H)
:
for each pair of distinct subgroups
H_{i},H_{j}\in\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
, if
H_{i}\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}H_{j}
then delete
H_{j}
from the list
; see
[
5
]
for details.

Using the language of algebraic extensions, one can deduce the following variant (and slight improvement) of Takahasi’s theorem:

{thm}

[
[
5
, Thm. 2.6, Prop. 3.9, Thm. 3.16]
]
Let
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
. The set of algebraic extensions
\mathcal{AE}(H)
is finite and computable; further, it satisfies that, for every
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}K\leqslant F_{A}
, there exists a unique intermediate subgroup
L
such that
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}L\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K\leqslant F_{A}
. This
L
is called the
K
-algebraic closure of
H
, denoted
L=\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)
, and coincides both with the smallest free factor of
K
containing
H
, and with the largest algebraic extension of
H
contained in
K
. ∎

In Theorem
1
, the words
smallest
and
largest
make sense because of the following basic properties of free and algebraic extensions:

{prop}

Let
H_{i}\leqslant K_{i}\leqslant F_{A}
be a collection of subgroup extensions in
F_{A}
,
i\in I
. Then,

[(i)]

1.

(
[

)
H_{i}\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K_{i}

\Rightarrow

\cap_{i\in I}H_{i}\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}\cap_{i\in I}K_{i}
;

2.

(
[

)
H_{i}\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K_{i}
,
\forall i\in I

\Rightarrow

\langle H_{i},i\in I\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}\langle K_{i},i\in I\rangle
. ∎

{prop}

[

Let
H\leqslant M_{i}\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
, for

. Then,

[(i)]

Let us insist on the computability part in Theorem
1
. If we start with a finitely generated subgroup
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
(given by a finite set of generators), we can effectively compute: (1) its Stallings graph
\Gamma_{A}(H)
; (2) all its (finitely many) quotients, resulting from identifying vertices in
\Gamma_{A}(H)
in all possible ways followed by folding (i.e., we can effectively compute bases for all the members of the fringe
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
); and (3) the cleaning process until getting the set of (bases of all the) algebraic extensions of
H
. This last step requires an algorithm deciding whether a given extension
H\leqslant K
is free or not; this can be done using the classical Whitehead techniques (see Roig–Ventura–Weil
[
9
]
for a significant improvement on the time complexity, from exponential to polynomial), or using more modern algorithms based on Stallings automata (see Silva–Weil
[
10
]
, and Puder
[
8
]
). Therefore, for
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
, the set
\mathcal{AE}(H)
is finite and computable.

Another important observation is the following. The fringe of
H
strongly depends on the ambient basis
A
(reflected in the notation with the subscript
A
in
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
), while the set of algebraic extensions
\mathcal{AE}(H)
does not, and is canonically associated to the subgroup
H
, since it is defined completely in algebraic terms. To illustrate this fact, see Example 2.5 from Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil
[
5
]
, where the fringe of
H=\langle ab,acba\rangle\leqslant F_{A}
,
A=\{a,b,c\}
, is computed:
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)=\{H_{0},H_{1},H_{2},H_{3},H_{4},H_{5}\}
, where
H_{0}=H
,
H_{1}=\langle ab,ac,ba\rangle
,
H_{2}=\langle ba,ba^{-1},cb\rangle
,
H_{3}=\langle ab,ac,ab^{-1},a^{2}\rangle
,
H_{4}=\langle ab,aca,acba\rangle
, and
H_{5}=\langle a,b,c\rangle=F_{A}
; however, with respect to the new ambient basis
A^{\prime}=\{d,e,f\}
, where
d=a
,
e=ab
, and
f=acba
, the
A^{\prime}
-automaton
\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)
has a single vertex, and hence the
A^{\prime}
-fringe of
H
is much simpler,
\mathcal{O}_{A^{\prime}}(H)=\{H\}
. Of course, in this example,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}F_{A}
and
\mathcal{AE}(H)=\{H\}
. Alternatively, thinking the change of basis as an automorphism of the ambient free group, we can express the above fact by saying that, for every
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(F_{A})
,
\mathcal{AE}(H\varphi)=\{K\varphi\mid K\in\mathcal{AE}(H)\}
(this is to say that
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
if and only if
H\varphi\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K\varphi
), while
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
and
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H\varphi)
are unrelated in general (they may even have different cardinals). In the example above, considering the automorphism
\varphi\colon F_{A}\to F_{A}
,
a\mapsto a
,
b\mapsto ab
,
c\mapsto acba
, we have
H\varphi^{-1}=\langle b,c\rangle
,
\mathcal{AE}(H)=\{H\}
,
\mathcal{AE}(H\varphi^{-1})=\{H\varphi^{-1}\}
,
|\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)|=6
, and
|\mathcal{O}_{A}(H\varphi^{-1})|=1
.

We interpret the above fact by thinking that
\mathcal{AE}(H)
is what really carries relevant algebraic information about the subgroup
H
and its relative position within the lattice of subgroups of
F_{A}
. And
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
is the same set locally distorted with some accidental new members depending on the ambient basis used to draw and work with the graphs. From this point of view, Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil launched in
[
5
]
the following natural conjecture: the common subgroups in
\mathcal{O}_{A^{\prime}}(H)
, when
A^{\prime}
runs over every ambient basis might be, precisely, the algebraic extensions:

{conj}

[Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil,
[
5
]
]
Let
A
be a finite alphabet, and
F_{A}
be the free group on
A
. Then, for every
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
,

\mathcal{AE}(H)=\bigcap_{A^{\prime}\,\,\mbox{\scriptsize basis of }F_{A}}\mathcal{O}_{A^{\prime}}(H)=\bigcap_{\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(F_{A})}\big(\mathcal{O}_{A}(H\varphi)\big)\varphi^{-1}.

In
[
5
]
it was mentioned that this is clearly true in the two extremal situations
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fi}}F_{A}
and
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}F_{A}
, but nothing else was known at that time. Seven years later, in 2014, the paper Parzanchevski–Puder
[
7
]
appeared showing that the conjecture is not true as stated:

{prop}

[Parzanchevski–Puder,
[
7
, Prop. 4.1]
]
Let
A=\{a,b\}
and consider the free group
F_{A}
. The extension
H=\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant\langle a^{2}b^{2},ab\rangle=K\leqslant F_{A}
is free,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
(so, it is not algebraic), but it satisfies
K\in\mathcal{O}_{A^{\prime}}(H)
for every ambient basis
A^{\prime}
. ∎

They also proposed two possible reformulations making the conjecture more plausible. On one hand, the authors recognize that their counterexample exploits many idiosyncrasies of the (automorphism group of the) free group of rank two, and it could be that Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil conjecture holds true for ambient free groups of rank three or more (i.e.,
\mathrm{Aut}(F_{2})
is much “smaller” and easier in structure than
\mathrm{Aut}(F_{r})
for
r\geqslant 3
and so, the intersection of fringes with respect to all ambient basis is “too lax” in the case of rank two). On the other hand, they made the elementary but clever observation that the independence of
\mathcal{AE}(H)
from the ambient basis (i.e., the reason for the obvious inclusion
\mathcal{AE}(H)\subseteq\bigcap_{A^{\prime}}\mathcal{O}_{A^{\prime}}(H)
) has an even more restrictive consequence: adding new letters, extend
A
to a bigger (possibly infinite) new alphabet
A\subseteq B
, and consider the free extension
F_{A}\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}F_{B}
; viewed as subgroups of
F_{B}
, it is still true that
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
if and only if
H\varphi\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K\varphi
, for every
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(F_{B})
. So, the same reasoning gives us the stronger inclusion
\mathcal{AE}(H)\subseteq\bigcap_{B^{\prime}}\mathcal{O}_{B^{\prime}}(H)
, where
B^{\prime}
runs now over
all
the ambient bases of
all free extensions

F_{B}
,
B\supseteq A
. Parzanchevski and Puder finished their paper
[
7
]
by reformulating Conjecture
1
into the following two variations:

{conj}

[Parzanchevski–Puder,
[
7
]
]
Let
A
be a finite alphabet with
|A|\geqslant 3
, and
F_{A}
be the free group on
A
. Then, for every
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
,

\mathcal{AE}(H)=\bigcap_{A^{\prime}\,\,\mbox{\scriptsize basis of }F_{A}}\mathcal{O}_{A^{\prime}}(H)=\bigcap_{\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(F_{A})}\Big(\mathcal{O}_{A}(H\varphi)\Big)\varphi^{-1}.

{conj}

[Parzanchevski–Puder,
[
7
, Conj. 5.1]
]
Let
A
be a finite alphabet and
F_{A}
be the free group on
A
. Then, for every
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
,

\mathcal{AE}(H)=\bigcap_{B\supseteq A}\,\bigcap_{B^{\prime}\,\,\mbox{\scriptsize basis of }F_{B}}\mathcal{O}_{B^{\prime}}(H)=\bigcap_{B\supseteq A}\,\bigcap_{\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(F_{B})}\Big(\mathcal{O}_{B}(H\varphi)\Big)\varphi^{-1}.

They also observed that their counterexample to Conjecture
1
does not serve as a counterexample for either Conjecture
1
or Conjecture
1
. In fact,
H=\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle
and
K=\langle a^{2}b^{2},ab\rangle
live inside the free group of rank two
F_{A}
, with
A=\{a,b\}
; but if we extend this ambient free group with a third letter, say
B=\{a,b,c\}
, then the new ambient basis
B^{\prime}=\{x,y,z\}
, with
x=a
,
y=cb^{-1}
,
z=cbc^{-1}
, breaks the counterexample since
H=\langle x^{2}y^{-1}z^{2}y\rangle
,
K=\langle x^{2}y^{-1}z^{2}y,xy^{-1}zy\rangle
and
K\not\in\mathcal{O}_{B^{\prime}}(H)
. We shall further exploit this example below.

The last step in this story is the recent 2020 preprint Kolodner
[

, where the author definitively disproves the conjecture in all its mentioned forms. In fact, he shows the following stronger result:

{thm}

[Kolodner
[
2
, Thm. 1.4]
]
Let
A=\{a,b\}
. In
F_{A}
, the proper subgroup extension
H=\langle b^{2}aba^{-1}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}\langle b,aba^{-1}\rangle=K
is free (and so, not algebraic) but, for an arbitrary alphabet
B
, and for every homomorphism
\varphi\colon F_{A}\to F_{B}
with
a\varphi,b\varphi\neq 1
,
K\varphi\in\mathcal{O}_{B}(H\varphi)
. ∎

2
Closing the MVW conjecture

In this section, we want to elaborate more on Parzanchevski–Puder’s idea about possible natural modifications of the original Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil conjecture, which could make it true.

Let
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
.

Firstly, we can look above
F_{A}
, not just through free extensions but using
all
possible extensions. That is, consider a new free group
F_{B}
and an arbitrary injective homomorphism
F_{A}\hookrightarrow F_{B}
, not necessarily with
A\subseteq B
, i.e., with the image not necessarily being a free factor of
F_{B}
. In this situation,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
still implies that, for every
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(F_{B})
,
H\varphi\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K\varphi\leqslant F_{B}
; or, in other words,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
implies that, for every basis
B^{\prime}
of
F_{B}
, the
B^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(K)
is onto.

Secondly, we can look downwards instead of upwards:
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K\leqslant F_{A}
also implies that, for every subgroup
K\leqslant L\leqslant F_{A}
and every automorphism
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(L)
,
H\varphi\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K\varphi\leqslant L\leqslant F_{A}
; or, in other words,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
implies that, for every
K\leqslant L\leqslant F_{A}
and every basis
C^{\prime}
of
L
, the
C^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(K)
is onto. Note that, in general, there are plenty of automorphisms of
L
which do not extend to automorphisms of
F_{A}
; furthermore,
L
may very well be not finitely generated.

Thirdly, we can combine the two effects upwards/downwards:
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
also implies that, for every free group inclusion
F_{A}\hookrightarrow F_{B}
, every subgroup
L\leqslant F_{B}
containing
K
, and every automorphism
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(L)
,
H\varphi\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K\varphi\leqslant L\leqslant F_{B}
(note that this is more general than before since
L
is now not necessarily a subgroup of
F_{A}
); equivalently,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
implies that, for every free group inclusion
F_{A}\hookrightarrow F_{B}
, every subgroup
L\leqslant F_{B}
containing
K
, and every basis
C^{\prime}
of
L
, the
C^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(K)
is still onto.

As we show in the following result, all these generalizations of Parzanchevski–Puder’s idea are, in fact, (tautologically) equivalent to the algebraicity of the initial extension
H\leqslant K
.

{prop}

Let
A
be an alphabet,
F_{A}
be the free group on
A
, and let
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
. Then the following are equivalent:

[(a)]

1.

H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
;

2.

for every free group inclusion
F_{A}\hookrightarrow F_{B}
, every subgroup
L\leqslant F_{B}
containing
K
, and every basis
C^{\prime}
of
L
, the
C^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(K)
is onto;

3.

for every
B\supseteq A
, every subgroup
L\leqslant F_{B}
containing
K
, and every basis
C^{\prime}
of
L
, the
C^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(K)
is onto;

4.

for every subgroup
L\leqslant F_{A}
containing
K
, and every basis
C^{\prime}
of
L
, the
C^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(K)
is onto;

5.

for every basis
C^{\prime}
of
K
, the
C^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(K)
is onto.

Proof.

The implications (a)
\Rightarrow
(b)
\Rightarrow
(c)
\Rightarrow
(d)
\Rightarrow
(e) are obvious.

For the relevant one, (e)
\Rightarrow
(a), consider a free decomposition
K=K_{1}*K_{2}
with
H\leqslant K_{1}
; applying the hypothesis to a basis of
K
of the form
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}_{1}\sqcup C^{\prime}_{2}
, where
C^{\prime}_{i}
is a basis of
K_{i}
,
i=1,2
, we have that
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(K)
is onto. But, by construction,
\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(H)
contains only edges labelled by letters from
C^{\prime}_{1}
, while
\Gamma_{C^{\prime}}(K)
has a single vertex, and edges in bijection with
C^{\prime}
. Therefore,
C^{\prime}_{2}
must be empty and
K_{2}=1
. This proves that
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
.
∎

3
Onto extensions

Interestingly, the counterexample given by Kolodner in Theorem
1
opens up a possible new line of research considering and studying two new types of subgroup extensions within the lattice of subgroups of a free group (which do not coincide, in general, with algebraic extensions).

{defi}

Let
A
be an alphabet, and let
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
. We say that this is an
onto
extension of subgroups, denoted
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
, if
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\to\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
is onto, for every basis
A^{\prime}
of
F_{A}
; in other words, if
K\in\bigcap_{A^{\prime}}\mathcal{O}_{A^{\prime}}(H)
, where
A^{\prime}
runs over all possible basis for
F_{A}
. Further, we say that
H\leqslant K
is
fully onto
, denoted
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
, if
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(H)\to\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(K)
is onto, for every basis
B^{\prime}
of every free extension
F_{A}\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}F_{B}
,
B\supseteq A
. We denote by
\Omega(H)
(resp.,
f\Omega(H)
) the set of onto (resp., fully onto) extensions of
H
within
F_{A}
.

{prop}

Let
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
. The following implications are true, while the reverse implications are not true, in general:

H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K\,\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,\,H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K\,\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,\,H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K\,\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,\,K\in\mathcal{O}_{A}(H).

That is,
\mathcal{AE}(H)\subseteq f\Omega(H)\subseteq\Omega(H)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
.

Proof.

We have already seen the three implications in the previous section. As for counterexamples, take
A=\{a,b\}
, and observe that Kolodner example
H=\langle b^{2}aba^{-1}\rangle\leqslant\langle b,aba^{-1}\rangle=K\leqslant F_{A}
satisfies
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
but
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}K
(in fact, it is the extreme opposite,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
). Parzanchevski–Puder example
H=\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant\langle a^{2}b^{2},ab\rangle=K\leqslant F_{A}
satisfies
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
but
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
. Finally, in Example 2.5 from Miasnikov–Ventura–Weil
[
5
]
, we have
H=\langle ab,acba\rangle
and
H_{1}=\langle ab,ac,ba\rangle\in\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
, while
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}H_{1}
(see the discussion above).
∎

{cor}

For any finitely generated subgroup
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
, we have
|\mathcal{AE}(H)|\leqslant|f\Omega(H)|\leqslant|\Omega(H)|\leqslant|\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)|<\infty
.∎

Let us investigate now the properties of these two new types of extensions among free groups. To do this, we need to use an idea, which is not explicitly written in Kolodner
[
2
]
but it is reminiscent in the arguments there.

Let
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
be connected
A
-automata (neither necessarily deterministic, nor trim) and let
\theta_{0}\colon\Gamma_{0}\to\Delta_{0}
be an
A
-homomorphism; let
H=\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{0})
and
K=\mathcal{L}(\Delta_{0})
. We want to fold and trim both
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
until obtaining the Stallings graphs
\Gamma_{A}(H)
and
\Gamma_{A}(K)
, respectively, but in a synchronized way so that
\theta_{0}
keeps inducing
A
-homomorphisms down the tower of foldings, until
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A}(H)\to\Gamma_{A}(K)
; further, we shall pay attention to the preservation of surjectivity, whenever possible. Here is a way of doing this (used in Kolodner
[
2
]
to analyze the relation between the automata homomorphisms
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A}(H)\to\Gamma_{A}(K)
and
\theta_{H\varphi,K\varphi}\colon\Gamma_{B}(H\varphi)\to\Gamma_{B}(K\varphi)
, for
\varphi\colon F(A)\to F(B)
):

(0)

Start with
\theta_{0}\colon\Gamma_{0}\to\Delta_{0}
; let
H=\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{0})
and
K=\mathcal{L}(\Delta_{0})
.

(1)

For every pair of edges,
e_{1},e_{2}
, violating determinism in
\Gamma_{0}
, fold them in
\Gamma_{0}
and simultaneously fold their images
e_{1}\theta_{0}
and
e_{2}\theta_{0}
in
\Delta_{0}
; there is the possibility that
e_{1}\theta_{0}
and
e_{2}\theta_{0}
are already equal in
\Delta_{0}
, in which case we do nothing on the right hand side. Observe that after this (or these) folding operation(s), the
A
-homomorphism
\theta_{0}
determines naturally an
A
-homomorphism among the resulting
A
-automata. Repeat this process until having no more foldings to do at the left hand side, and denote the result by
\theta_{1}\colon\Gamma_{1}\to\Delta_{1}
. By construction,
\Gamma_{1}
is deterministic. Note also that if
\theta_{0}
is onto then
\theta_{1}
is also onto.

(2)

Now perform all possible foldings remaining to be done at the right hand side (and nothing on the left hand side): the homomorphism
\theta_{1}
naturally transfers to the new situation, and we get
\theta_{2}\colon\Gamma_{2}\to\Delta_{2}
, where
\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma_{1}
, and now both
\Gamma_{2}
and
\Delta_{2}
are deterministic (and not trim in general). Note that, again, if
\theta_{1}
is onto then
\theta_{2}
is also onto.

(3)

At this point, observe that the edges in the core of
\Gamma_{2}
must map through
\theta_{2}
to edges in the core of
\Delta_{2}
(this is because both
\Gamma_{2}
and
\Delta_{2}
are deterministic and
\theta_{2}
is an
A
-homomorphism). So, edges outside the core of
\Delta_{2}
can only be images of edges from outside the core of
\Gamma_{2}
. Hence, trimming all the edges from outside the core of
\Delta_{2}
, and trimming simultaneously all their
\theta_{2}
-preimages in
\Gamma_{2}
, we obtain
\theta_{3}\colon\Gamma_{3}\to\Delta_{3}
, where
\Delta_{3}=c(\Delta_{2})
is deterministic and trim, and
\Gamma_{3}
is deterministic (and not yet trim, in general). Again,
\theta_{2}
onto implies
\theta_{3}
onto. Moreover, observe also that
\mathcal{L}(\Delta_{3})=\mathcal{L}(\Delta_{2})=\mathcal{L}(\Delta_{1})=\mathcal{L}(\Delta_{0})=K
and so,
\Delta_{3}=\Gamma_{A}(K)
.

(4)

Finally, let us finish trimming
\Gamma_{3}
(and do nothing on the right hand side) to obtain
\theta_{4}\colon\Gamma_{4}\to\Delta_{4}
, where
\Delta_{4}=\Delta_{3}=\Gamma_{A}(K)
,
\Gamma_{4}=\Gamma_{A}(H)
since it is already a Stallings
A
-automaton with
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{4})=H
,
\theta_{4}=\theta_{H,K}
, and we are done. It is crucial to note, however, that in this
critical
last step we may very well lose surjectivity: in fact, even with
\theta_{3}
being onto, removing edges from
\Gamma_{3}
may result into some edges from
\Delta_{4}=\Delta_{3}
having no
\theta_{4}
-preimages in
\Gamma_{4}
.

This synchronized folding process is crucial for the proof of the next proposition. Observe that Propositions
3
and
3
express the fact that onto and fully onto extensions satisfy the same properties we already know for algebraic extensions so, they behave very similarly to them (compare with Propositions
1
and
1
).

{prop}

Let
H_{i}\leqslant K_{i}\leqslant F_{A}
be a collection of subgroup extensions in
F_{A}
,
i\in I
. Then,

[(i)]

1.

H_{i}\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K_{i}
,
\forall i\in I

\Rightarrow

\langle H_{i},i\in I\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle K_{i},i\in I\rangle
;

2.

H_{i}\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K_{i}
,
\forall i\in I

\Rightarrow

\langle H_{i},i\in I\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}\langle K_{i},i\in I\rangle
.

Proof.

(i). Fix a basis
A^{\prime}
of
F(A)
. The hypothesis tells us that, for
i\in I
,
\theta_{H_{i},K_{i}}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H_{i})\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K_{i})
is an onto
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism. Glue together all the
\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H_{i})
(resp.,
\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K_{i})
), for
i\in I
, along their basepoints, to get
\Gamma_{0}
(resp.,
\Delta_{0}
) and the natural onto
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{0}\colon\Gamma_{0}\twoheadrightarrow\Delta_{0}
induced by the
\theta_{H_{i},K_{i}}
’s. Observe that neither
\Gamma_{0}
nor
\Delta_{0}
is deterministic, in general, and that
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{0})=H
and
\mathcal{L}(\Delta_{0})=K
, where
H=\langle H_{i},i\in I\rangle
and
K=\langle K_{i},i\in I\rangle
.

Now let us apply the synchronized folding process described above, starting with the onto
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{0}\colon\Gamma_{0}\twoheadrightarrow\Delta_{0}
, until obtaining
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\to\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
. We will deduce that this last
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism is onto, after arguing that all the left hand side
A^{\prime}
-automata
\Gamma_{0}
,
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
, along the process (as well as the right hand side ones) are trim and so, neither step (3) nor the critical step (4) take place.

By construction,
\theta_{0}\colon\Gamma_{0}\twoheadrightarrow\Delta_{0}
is onto, and
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
are both trim, and not necessarily deterministic. In general, along the individual folding processes applied to
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
in steps (1) and (2), the fact of being trim can be lost, since some vertices along the process decrease their degrees and could eventually become degree one vertices. However, we claim that this will not be the case, neither for
\Gamma_{0}
, nor for
\Delta_{0}
. Observe that these two
A^{\prime}
-automata are trim in a stronger way: for every vertex
p\neq\circledcirc
, not only the degree is bigger than one,
|\{e\in E\mid\iota e=p\}|>1
, but also its
label-degree
,
|\{a\in(A^{\prime})^{\pm}\mid\exists\,e\in E\mbox{ s.t. }\iota e=p,\,\operatorname{lab}(e)=a\}|>1
. And, meanwhile the degree of a vertex could decrease along the folding process, its label-degree stays constant or increases. Therefore, at the end of step (2), the label-degree of all vertices
p\neq\circledcirc
in
\Gamma_{2}
and
\Delta_{2}
are bigger than one and hence, so are their degrees too. This means that steps (3) and (4) are empty and
\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma_{3}=\Gamma_{4}=\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)
,
\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{3}=\Delta_{4}=\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
, and
\theta_{2}=\theta_{3}=\theta_{4}=\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
is onto. Since this is valid for every initially fixed basis
A^{\prime}
of
F(A)
, we deduce that
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
.

(ii). Fix a free extension
F_{A}\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}F_{B}
,
B\supseteq A
, and a basis
B^{\prime}
for
F_{B}
. The hypothesis tells us that, each
B^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H_{i},K_{i}}\colon\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(H_{i})\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(K_{i})
, for
i\in I
, is onto. Glue them together along their basepoints and apply the synchronized folding process described above. The exact same argument as in (i) tells us that
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(K)
is onto, where
H=\langle H_{i},i\in I\rangle
and
K=\langle K_{i},i\in I\rangle
. Since this is valid for every basis
B^{\prime}
, we deduce that
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
.
∎

{rem}

The argument in the proof of Proposition
3
is not technically correct for the case
|I|=\infty
, since the processes of folding edges, pair by pair, in steps (1) and (2) could very well be infinitely long. This is not a conceptual obstacle, but only a matter of expression: one should do
all
these foldings in a single step (losing, of course, the algorithmic aspect of the proof, valid only when
|I|<\infty
). In a non-deterministic (possibly infinite)
A
-automata
\Gamma
, one can define the equivalence relation among vertices given by
p\sim q

\Leftrightarrow
there is a path
\gamma
in
\Gamma
satisfying
\iota\gamma=p
,
\tau\gamma=q
, and
\operatorname{lab}(\gamma)=1\in F_{A}
. It is straightforward to see that
\Gamma/\sim
is automatically deterministic and has the same language
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma/\sim)=\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
; further, for the case when
\Gamma
is finite,
\Gamma/\sim
equals the final output of the sequence of foldings. Similarly, when
\Gamma
is infinite, the trim process cannot be done edge by edge (there could even be no vertex of degree 1, and infinitely many edges to be trimmed out). Instead, one should delete, in a single step, all the edges not visited by any reduced closed path at
\circledcirc
.

{prop}

Let
H\leqslant M_{i}\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
, for
i=1,2
. Then,

[(i)]

1.

if
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{1}\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
, then
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
;

2.

if
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
, then
M_{1}\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
, while
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{1}
in general;

3.

if
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{1}
and
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{2}
, then
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle M_{1}\cup M_{2}\rangle
, while
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{1}\cap M_{2}
in general;

(i
′
)

if
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}M_{1}\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
, then
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
;

(ii
′
)

if
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
, then
M_{1}\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
, while
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}M_{1}
in general;

(iii
′
)

if
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}M_{1}
and
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}M_{2}
, then
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}\langle M_{1}\cup M_{2}\rangle
, while
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}M_{1}\cap M_{2}
in general.

Proof.

(i)-(i’). Let
A^{\prime}
be a basis for
F_{A}
. Since, by hypothesis, both
A^{\prime}
-homomorphisms
\theta_{H,M_{1}}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(M_{1})
and
\theta_{M_{1},K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(M_{1})\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
are onto, we have
\theta_{H,K}=\theta_{H,M_{1}}\circ\theta_{M_{1},K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(M_{1})\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
is onto as well. Hence,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
. The proof of (i’) is analogous.

(ii)-(ii’). Let
A^{\prime}
be a basis for
F_{A}
. Since, by hypothesis, the
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
is onto, and the inclusions
H\leqslant M_{1}\leqslant K
tell us that
\theta_{H,K}=\theta_{H,M_{1}}\circ\theta_{M_{1},K}
, we deduce that
\theta_{M_{1},K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(M_{1})\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
is onto as well. Hence,
M_{1}\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K
. The proof of (ii’) is analogous.

For a counterexample to the other assertion, consider
\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}\langle a,b\rangle=F_{\{a,b\}}
, which is an algebraic extension because
a^{2}b^{2}
is neither a proper power, nor a primitive element in
\langle a,b\rangle
; consequently,
\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}\langle a,b\rangle
and
\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle a,b\rangle
but, clearly,
\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\not\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle a^{2}b^{2},a^{3}b^{3}\rangle\leqslant\langle a,b\rangle
.

(iii)-(iii’). This is a direct application of Proposition
3
.

For a counterexample to the other assertion, consider
F_{\{a,b\}}
and its subgroups
H=\langle a^{6}b^{6}\rangle
,
M_{1}=\langle a^{2},b^{2}\rangle
, and
M_{2}=\langle a^{3},b^{3}\rangle
. Since
a^{6}b^{6}=(a^{2})^{3}(b^{2})^{3}
is neither a proper power nor a primitive element in
M_{1}
, we have
H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}M_{1}
and hence,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}M_{1}
and
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{1}
; similarly,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}M_{2}
and
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{2}
. However, we claim that
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{1}\cap M_{2}
. It is easier to show first that
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}M_{1}\cap M_{2}
, and then recycle the idea to strengthen the result to
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{1}\cap M_{2}
. In fact,
M_{1}\cap M_{2}=\langle a^{6},b^{6}\rangle
(where, by the way, the element
a^{6}b^{6}
is now primitive); adding a third ambient letter
\{a,b\}\subseteq\{a,b,c\}
, and using the ambient basis
B^{\prime}=\{x,y,z\}
where
x=ac^{-1}
,
y=cb
,
z=c
, it is straightforward to see that
\theta_{H,M_{1}\cap M_{2}}\colon\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(H)\to\Gamma_{B^{\prime}}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})
is not onto: the idea here is that, in this new basis,
a=xz
,
b=z^{-1}y
and so, in the middle point of the petal
a^{6}b^{6}=(xz)^{5}(x\not{z})(\not{z^{-1}}y)(z^{-1}y)^{5}
, an edge labelled
z
must be trimmed out provoking the lost of surjectivity. A bit trickier to find, but following the same idea, one can see that, in the ambient basis
A^{\prime}=\{x,y\}
with
x=ab^{-1}a^{-1}
and
y=ab^{2}
, we have
a=x^{2}y
,
b=y^{-1}x^{-1}y
, and the
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,M_{1}\cap M_{2}}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\to\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})
is not onto. Therefore,
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M_{1}\cap M_{2}
as claimed.
∎

The first examples of a fully onto extension not being algebraic (given by Kolodner in
Theorem
1
, namely
\langle b^{2}aba^{-1}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}\langle b,aba^{-1}\rangle\leqslant F_{\{a,b\}}
) or of an onto extension not being fully onto (given by Parzanchevski–Puder in Proposition
1
, namely
\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle a^{2}b^{2},ab\rangle\leqslant F_{\{a,b\}}
) were hard to establish. However, using the above properties, and combining with known examples of algebraic extensions, we can easily construct lots of new examples of onto and fully onto extensions.

{exa}

Take two copies of Kolodner’s example,
\langle b^{2}aba^{-1}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}\langle b,aba^{-1}\rangle\leqslant F_{\{a,b\}}
and
\langle c^{2}aca^{-1}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}\langle c,aca^{-1}\rangle\leqslant F_{\{a,c\}}
; clearly, both are also examples of fully onto extensions of subgroups of
F_{\{a,b,c\}}
so, by Proposition
3
(ii), we obtain
\langle b^{2}aba^{-1},c^{2}aca^{-1}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}\langle b,c,aba^{-1},aca^{-1}\rangle\leqslant F_{\{a,b,c\}}
, while it is a free extension so it is not algebraic. However, we have to be careful with a similar attempt to use Proposition
3
(i):
\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle a^{2}b^{2},ab\rangle
as subgroups of
F_{\{a,b\}}
, and
\langle a^{2}c^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle a^{2}c^{2},ac\rangle
as subgroups of
F_{\{a,c\}}
, but we cannot conclude that
\langle a^{2}b^{2},a^{2}c^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle a^{2}b^{2},ab,a^{2}c^{2},ac\rangle
as subgroups of
F_{\{a,b,c\}}
since, in the ambient basis
A^{\prime}=\{x,y,z\}
, with
x=a
,
y=cb^{-1}
,
z=cbc^{-1}
, the corresponding
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism is not onto (the problem being that
neither
of the two initial extensions is onto when viewed as subgroups of
F_{\{a,b,c\}}
).

As a second example, consider
\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle a^{2}b^{2},ab\rangle
and
\langle a^{-2}b^{-2}a^{2}b^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle a^{2},b^{2}\rangle
, both as subgroups of
F_{\{a,b\}}
(the first one is Parzanchevski–Puder example, and the second one is algebraic). Then, by Proposition
3
(i),
\langle a^{2}b^{2},b^{2}a^{2}\rangle\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\langle ab,a^{2},b^{2}\rangle
, while
\langle a^{2}b^{2},b^{2}a^{2}\rangle\not\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}\langle ab,a^{2},b^{2}\rangle
since, extending with a third letter
c
and using the ambient basis
B^{\prime}=\{x,y,z\}
, with
x=a
,
y=cb^{-1}
,
z=cbc^{-1}
, the corresponding
B^{\prime}
-homomorphism is not onto.

{cor}

For
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
, the finite sets
\mathcal{AE}(H)
,
f\Omega(H)
, and
\Omega(H)
, partially ordered by natural inclusion, form three lattices with the join operations given by
H_{1}\vee H_{2}=\langle H_{1}\cup H_{2}\rangle
, and the meet operations given by

H_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{AE}}H_{2}=\left\langle K\in\mathcal{AE}(H)\mid K\leqslant H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right\rangle,

H_{1}\wedge_{f\Omega}H_{2}=\left\langle K\in f\Omega(H)\mid K\leqslant H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right\rangle,

H_{1}\wedge_{\Omega}H_{2}=\left\langle K\in\Omega(H)\mid K\leqslant H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right\rangle,

respectively. Moreover, these lattices are not semimodular, in general (and so, not distributive either).

Proof.

By Propositions
1
(iii),
3
(iii’), and
3
(iii),
H\leqslant\langle H_{1}\cup H_{2}\rangle
is algebraic (resp., fully onto, onto) whenever
H\leqslant H_{1}
and
H\leqslant H_{2}
are so; and clearly it is the smallest such extension containing both
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, hence it is the join in the three lattices,
H_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{AE}}H_{2}=H_{1}\vee_{f\Omega}H_{2}=H_{1}\vee_{\Omega}H_{2}=\langle H_{1}\cup H_{2}\rangle
. By a similar reason, the subgroup generated by all the
K\in\mathcal{AE}(H)
(resp.,
K\in f\Omega(H)
,
K\in\Omega(H)
) contained in
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
(among which we always have
H
itself) is algebraic (resp., fully onto, onto) over
H
, and it is clearly the biggest such extension contained in
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
, hence it is the meet
H_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{AE}}H_{2}
(resp.,
H_{1}\wedge_{f\Omega}H_{2}
,
H_{1}\wedge_{\Omega}H_{2}
).

A lattice is
semimodular
when
H_{1}\wedge H_{2}<:H_{1}
implies
H_{2}<:H_{1}\vee H_{2}
(here,
H_{1}<:H_{2}
means that
H_{2}

covers

H_{1}
, i.e.,
H_{1}<H_{2}
but there is no
H_{3}
in between
H_{1}<H_{3}<H_{2}
). This property is not true, in general, for
\mathcal{AE}(H)
, neither
\Omega(H)
, nor
f\Omega(H)
, as the example computed in
[
6
, Ex. 3.7]
shows. Consider
H=\langle a^{2},b^{2},ab^{2}a\rangle\leqslant F_{A}
, where
A=\{a,b\}
. It is straightforward to see that
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)=\{H_{0},H_{1},H_{2},H_{3},H_{4},H_{5},H_{6}\}
, where
H_{0}=H
,
H_{1}=\langle a^{2},b,a^{-1}b^{2}a\rangle
,
H_{2}=\langle a^{2},b^{2},a^{-1}ba\rangle
,
H_{3}=\langle a^{2},b^{2},ab\rangle
,
H_{4}=\langle a,b^{2}\rangle
,
H_{5}=\langle a^{2},b,a^{-1}ba\rangle
, and
H_{6}=\langle a,b\rangle
. It is also straightforward to see that all these extensions are algebraic over
H
so,
\mathcal{AE}(H)=f\Omega(H)=\Omega(H)=\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
. Since all the inclusions among these subgroups follow by transitivity from
H_{0}\leqslant H_{1},H_{2},H_{3},H_{4}
,
H_{1},H_{2}\leqslant H_{5}
, and
H_{5},H_{3},H_{4}\leqslant H_{6}
, we have that
H_{3}
covers
H_{2}\wedge H_{3}=H_{0}
, but
H_{2}\vee H_{3}=H_{6}
does not cover
H_{2}
(since
H_{2}<H_{5}<H_{6}
). Hence, neither of the three lattices
\mathcal{AE}(H)
,
f\Omega(H)
, and
\Omega(H)
(here coinciding) is semimodular, in general. Since distributivity implies semimodularity, they are not distributive either.
∎

4
The onto and fully onto closures and triviality of into extensions

Similarly to what is done for algebraic extensions, given an extension
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
, one can define the onto and the fully onto closures of
H
relative to
K
.

{defi}

Let
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
. The
K
-onto closure
of
H
, denoted
\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
, is the join of all the onto extensions of
H
contained in
K
, i.e., the biggest onto extension of
H
inside
K
. Similarly, the
K
-fully onto closure
of
H
, denoted
f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
, is the join of all the fully onto extensions of
H
contained in
K
, i.e., the biggest fully onto extension of
H
inside
K
. Clearly, by construction, we have the inclusions
H\leqslant\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)\leqslant f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\leqslant\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\leqslant K
.

{rem}

Since, in general, the notions of algebraic, onto, and fully onto extensions do not coincide, the corresponding closure operators will also be different. In fact, we can illustrate this fact by recycling Kolodner’s and Parzanchevski–Puder’s examples above. Let
A=\{a,b\}
. We know that
H=\langle b^{2}aba^{-1}\rangle
and
K=\langle b,aba^{-1}\rangle
satisfy
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K\leqslant F_{A}
, while
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
. Hence,
\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)=H
,
f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)=K
, and
\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)=K
. On the other hand, we also know that
H=\langle a^{2}b^{2}\rangle
and
K=\langle a^{2}b^{2},ab\rangle
satisfy
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K\leqslant F_{A}
but
H\not\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
. It is straightforward to compute
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)=\{H_{0},H_{1},H_{2},H_{3},H_{4},H_{5},H_{6}\}
, where
H_{0}=H
,
H_{1}=\langle a,b^{2}\rangle
,
H_{2}=\langle a^{2},b\rangle
,
H_{3}=\langle a^{2},b^{2}\rangle
,
H_{4}=\langle ab,a^{2}b^{2}\rangle
,
H_{5}=\langle a^{2},b^{2},ab\rangle
, and
H_{6}=\langle a,b\rangle=F_{A}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{AE}(H)=\{H_{0},H_{6}\}
so,
\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)=H
. But, additionally, the only subgroups in
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
between
H
and
K
are
H_{0}=H
and
H_{4}=K
so,
f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)=H
and
\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)=K
.

{prop}

Let
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}K\leqslant F_{A}
be an extension of subgroups. Then, we have

H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)\!\begin{array}[]{l}\leqslant_{{}_{\textsf{f.ont}}}\\
\leqslant_{{}_{\textsf{ff}}}\end{array}\!\!f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\!\begin{array}[]{l}\leqslant_{{}_{\textsf{ont}}}\\
\leqslant_{{}_{\textsf{ff}}}\end{array}\!\!\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K.

Proof.

The chain of inclusions is clear by construction. Also, by construction, we have

H\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)
,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
and
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
therefore, by Proposition
3
(ii)(ii’),
\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
and
f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
.

To see the free factors, observe that
\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
and so, by Proposition
1
(ii’),
\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
and
\mathcal{C}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
. The remaining two free factors come from transitivity of onto and fully onto extensions (see Proposition
3
(i)(i’)) and from applying Theorem
1
to the corresponding extensions: there exists a subgroup
L
such that
f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}L\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
so,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}L\leqslant K
hence,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}L\leqslant K
and, by maximality,
f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)=L\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
. Similarly,
there exists a subgroup
M
such that
\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{alg}}M\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
so,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M\leqslant K
hence,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}M\leqslant K
and, again by maximality,
\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)=M\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}K
.
∎

{cor}

The three closures of a subgroup
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
with respect to the ambient free group
F_{A}
do coincide:
\mathcal{C}l_{F_{A}}(H)=f\mathcal{OC}l_{F_{A}}(H)=\mathcal{OC}l_{F_{A}}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}F_{A}
.

Proof.

It is enough to see the inclusion
\mathcal{OC}l_{F_{A}}(H)\leqslant\mathcal{C}l_{F_{A}}(H)
. In fact,
\mathcal{C}l_{F_{A}}(H)\leqslant_{\textsf{ff}}F_{A}
and we consider a basis
\{a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{r}\}
for
\mathcal{C}l_{F_{A}}(H)
and an extension of it to a basis
A^{\prime}=\{a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{r},a^{\prime}_{r+1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{n}\}
of
F_{A}
. By construction
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}\mathcal{OC}l_{F_{A}}(H)
so, in particular, the
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,\mathcal{OC}l_{F_{A}}(H)}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(\mathcal{OC}l_{F_{A}}(H))
is onto. But
H\leqslant\mathcal{C}l_{F_{A}}(H)=\langle a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{r}\rangle
so
\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)
, and hence
\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(\mathcal{OC}l_{F_{A}}(H))
, has no edges labelled by
a^{\prime}_{r+1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{n}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{OC}l_{F_{A}}(H)\leqslant\langle a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{r}\rangle=\mathcal{C}l_{F_{A}}(H)
.
∎

Arriving at this point, it seems natural to ask which could be the possible dual notions to the concepts of onto and fully onto, in the same way that free extensions are dual to algebraic extensions. Fulfilling this duality, Theorem
1
states that the
K
-algebraic closure of
H\leqslant K
can alternatively be reached by taking the biggest algebraic extension of
H
contained in
K
, or the smallest free factor of
K
containing
H
. Is there a dual notion for onto (resp., fully onto) extensions in this sense? i.e., is there a property
{\mathcal{P}}
of extensions for which
\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
(resp.,
f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
) is the smallest
{\mathcal{P}}
-subgroup of
K
containing
H
?

Since being onto is less restrictive than being algebraic, the dual notion should be a more restrictive notion than being a free factor. We presume the reader can easily imagine a very natural candidate for this possible dual notion: we could say that an extension
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
is
into
if
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\hookrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
is injective for every basis
A^{\prime}
of
F_{A}
. And similarly, for
fully into
.

However, somehow against intuition, this dualization project fails dramatically. After studying these two concepts, and proving some promising properties (very similar to those of free factors), we realized that the only into extension
H\leqslant K\leqslant F_{A}
is the equality
H=K
, by first proving that
H\leqslant_{\textsf{into}}K
implies that
\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)
must be a
full
subgraph of
\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
for every ambient basis
A^{\prime}
, and then seeing that this situation forces equality
H=K
. So, these notions of into and fully into are trivial and, by no means, can they provide a Theorem similar to
1
.

At the time of writing the present paper, a subsequent preprint Kolodner
[
3
]
appeared providing an easier proof for this same fact. We redirect the reader there.

{prop}

[Kolodner
[
3
, Thm. 21]
]
For every extension of finitely generated subgroups
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}K\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
, there always exists a basis
A^{\prime}
of the ambient group
F_{A}
such that the
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(H)\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
is onto. ∎

{cor}

[Kolodner
[
3
, Cor. 22]
]
Let
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}K\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
. If the
A^{\prime}
-homomorphism
\theta_{H,K}\colon\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}\hookrightarrow\Gamma_{A^{\prime}}(K)
is injective for every basis
A^{\prime}
of
F_{A}
then,
H=K
. ∎

5
Open questions

We conclude with a list of interesting related questions, which remain open as far as we know.

{qu}

Is there an algorithm to decide whether a given extension
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}K\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
is onto? and fully onto? Is there an algorithm to compute onto and fully onto closures of given extensions
H\leqslant K
of finitely generated subgroups?

{rem}

In the situation
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}K\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
, we can compute
\mathcal{O}_{A}(H)
and keep all those overgroups of
H
contained in
K
. This provides a finite list of subgroups containing both
\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
and
f\mathcal{OC}l_{K}(H)
. However, to finish deciding who they are among the candidates in the list, we would need an algorithm deciding whether a given extension
H\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}L\leqslant_{\textsf{fg}}F_{A}
is onto (resp., fully onto) or not. A procedure for this was designed by Kolodner in
[
2
]
, which may stop and give the correct answer, or may work forever (the example in Theorem
1
was obtained, precisely, as a stopping instance for this procedure). To make it into a true algorithm we would need a proof that it always stops, or an additional criterium to kill the process at certain point, and get the answer in finitely many steps.

{qu}

What is the algebraic meaning of an extension being onto, or fully onto? Is it possible to characterize the facts
H\leqslant_{\textsf{ont}}K\leqslant F_{A}
and
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K\leqslant F_{A}
without refereing to the bases of
F_{A}
?

{rem}

These two definitions are canonical in the sense that they do not depend on any prefixed ambient basis. It would be interesting, then, to characterize them in algebraic terms with respect to
H
and
K
, but not talking about ambient bases (like the definition of algebraic extension). It seems, however, that free factors will probably not help in this, since there exist onto, and even fully onto, extensions being simultaneously free factors. A tricky detail to take into account here is that fully onto transfers through free extensions while onto does not, i.e.,
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
as subgroups of
F_{A}
implies
H\leqslant_{\textsf{f.ont}}K
as subgroups of
F_{B}
for any
B\supseteq A
, while the same is not true in general for onto extensions.

{qu}

Is there a notion dual to onto, or dual to fully onto, which may lead to a Theorem similar to
1
?
