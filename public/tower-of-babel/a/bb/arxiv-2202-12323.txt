Introduction

The oriented chromatic number of an oriented graph is a natural generalization of the usual chromatic number. The chromatic number of an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, denoted
\chi(G)
, is the smallest
k
for which there is a function
c:V\to[k]
such that for every
uv\in E,\ c(u)\neq c(v)
. The chromatic number of
G
can equivalently be defined as the smallest
k
for which there is a graph homomorphism from
G
to a complete graph
K_{k}
. The oriented chromatic number of an oriented graph
\vec{G}
, denoted
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})
, is the smallest
k
for which there is a homomorphism (of directed graphs) from
\vec{G}
to some tournament of order
k
. An equivalent formulation is that
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})
is the least
k
for which there is a partition of
V
into
k
sets, called colour classes, so that each colour class contains no arcs and so that between every pair of colour classes, all arcs have the same direction. The notion of such colourings of oriented graphs was first introduced by Courcelle
[
14
]
in a series of papers studying graph properties described in monadic second order logic.

While
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})
is always bounded from below by the usual chromatic number of the underlying graph, these parameters can, in general, be far. It was noted by Kostochka, Sopena, and Zhu
[
34
]
that there is an orientation of the complete bipartite graph
K_{n,n}
with oriented chromatic number
2n
. Oriented graphs,
\vec{G}
, that have
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})
equal to the number of vertices in
\vec{G}
are sometimes called
oriented cliques
and are precisely the oriented graphs with diameter at most
2
. The problem of determining the minimum number of arcs in a directed graph with diameter at most
2
was proposed by Erdős, Sós, and Rényi
[
19
]
(see also
[
56
,
15
]
) and Füredi, Horak, Pareek, and Zhu
[
22
]
showed that this minimum is
(1+o(1))n\log n
(see also
[
33
]
). The maximum order of outerplanar and planar oriented cliques was studied by Klostermeyer and MacGillivray
[
31
]
and Sen
[
46
]
.

Another closely-related concept is the
acyclic chromatic number
of an oriented graph, denoted
\chi_{a}(\vec{G})
, which is the smallest number of colours needs to properly colour the vertices of an oriented graph so that every pair of colour classes induce an acyclic graph. Raspaud and Sopena
[
43
]
showed that
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})
can be bounded in terms of
\chi_{a}(\vec{G})
and Kostochka, Sopena and Zhu
[
34
]
gave bounds on
\chi_{a}(\vec{G})
in terms of
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})
.

There has been significant prior investigation into
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})
focusing on extremal results for graphs with bounded degree properties. In a recent series of papers
[
16
,
17
]
, Duffy, with others, prove that the oriented chromatic number of connected cubic graphs is at most eight. Oriented graphs with maximum degree
4
have an oriented chromatic number at most
69
(see
[
17
]
) although it is believed that this upper bound can be improved. In
[
34
]
, Kostochka, Sopena, and Zhu consider deterministic bounds on
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})
in terms of its maximum degree. They show that for any digraph
\vec{G}
with maximum degree
d
,
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})\leq 2d^{2}2^{d}
. They also prove that when
n
is sufficiently large, for every
d
-regular digraph
G
on
n
vertices, there is an orientation
\vec{G}
with
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})\geq\lceil 2^{d/2}\rceil
.

In this paper, we consider the question of bounding the oriented chromatic number of a random directed graph, extending results on the chromatic number of undirected random graphs. The random graph
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
was introduced in 1960 by Erdős and Rényi. In this model, each possible edge of a graph on
n
vertices is chosen independently with probability
p
. This model marks the beginning of the field of random graph theory, the study of random graph models and their properties. We direct the reader to the books
[
4
,
9
,
29
]
for results on random graphs and their connections to other fields. In addition to
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
, we consider the model
\mathcal{G}(n,d)
in which a
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices is selected uniformly at random.

The two models of random directed graphs that we consider in this paper are
\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
and
\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
. In each, after selecting an appropriate undirected graph according to
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
or
\mathcal{G}(n,d)
, respectively, each edge is given an orientation uniformly at random. In particular,
\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
and
\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
contain no directed loops or two-cycles as they are orientations of simple graphs.

The question of the chromatic number of a random graph was mentioned by Erdős and Rényi
[
18
]
and investigated as early as 1974 by Grimmet and McDiarmid
[
25
]
, but the topic has received considerable attention since 1987 following the breakthrough result of Shamir and Spencer
[
47
]
in which martingales were used to show
\chi(\mathcal{G}(n,p))
is concentrated in a small set of values with probability tending to
1
as
n
tends to infinity (which we refer to as
asymptotically almost surely
or a.a.s.). Bollobás
[
8
]
and later Łuczak
[
36
]
prove that for
pn\to\infty
, a.a.s.
\chi(\mathcal{G}(n,p))
is
(\frac{1}{2}+o(1))\log(1/(1-p))\frac{n}{\log n}
. Łuczak also proved
[
37
]
that when
p=d/n
, so that on average each vertex has degree
d
, there is a value
k_{d}
such that the chromatic number of
G(n,p)
is a.a.s.
k_{d}
or
k_{d}+1
. In 2005, Achlioptas and Naor
[
3
]
determined
k_{d}
to be the smallest integer such that
d<2k_{d}\log k_{d}
. Building on that work, Achloptas and Moore
[
2
]
showed that the
\chi(\mathcal{G}(n,d))
is a.a.s.
k_{d},k_{d}+1
, or
k_{d}+2
for the same
k_{d}
. Kemkes et al.
[
30
]
improved that result to
k_{d}
or
k_{d}+1
, and Coja-Oghlan et al.
[
13
]
proved that the chromatic number is concentrated at
k_{d}
for sufficiently large
d
.

Directed random graphs have received significantly less attention than undirected models. The aforementioned Kostochka, Sopena, and Zhu bound,
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{G})\leq 2d^{2}2^{d}
for digraphs with maximum degree
d
, also serves as an upper bound for
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,d))
. Chebolu and Frieze considered Hamilton cycles in random lifts of directed graphs
[
12
]
. Bensmail, Duffy, and Sen
[
6
]
noted that a.a.s.
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,p=\frac{1}{2}))=n
. (We expand on this observation in Proposition
8.4
.) For
d=1
or
2
,
\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
can be readily calculated exactly, and we do so in Section
8.1
. Unlike in any known undirected case,
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,2))
is not concentrated in a single value; instead, a.a.s.
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,2))\in\{4,5\}
and each value occurs with positive probability. We believe this difference suggests the oriented chromatic number of directed graphs deserves further study.

Our contribution.
We extend the literature on undirected graph models to the directed case by analyzing the chromatic number of the random graph models
\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,p=\frac{d}{n})
and
\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
.

A ‘doubly regular’ tournament
T
is a tournament in which every vertex has
out-degree
\frac{n-1}{2}
and for every pair of vertices
v,w
there are

\frac{n-3}{4}
vertices
u
such that
v\to u
and
w\to u
.

Now set

u_{k}=\frac{2\log k}{\log k-\log(\frac{k-1}{2})}<\frac{2}{\log 2}\log k

(1)

and

\ell_{k}=\frac{2(k-1)^{3}}{k(k+1)(k-2)}\log(k-1)\geq 2\left(1-\frac{2}{k+1}\right)\log(k-1).

(2)

The main results of this paper are

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d>0
be a real number and let
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that there exists a doubly-regular tournament of order
k_{2}
and

. Then a.a.s.
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,p=\frac{d}{n}))\in[k_{1},3k_{2}+11]
.

and

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and let
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that there exists a doubly-regular tournament of order
k_{2}
and

. Then a.a.s.
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,d))\in[k_{1},3k_{2}+11]
.

We also use Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
to prove the following corollary:

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
d>1
be given. Then a.a.s.

{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,p=\tfrac{d}{n}))\in(2^{d/2},6e^{d/2}+6d+17].

Furthermore, if
d
is an integer, a.a.s.

{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{G}}(n,d))\in(2^{d/2},6e^{d/2}+6d+17]

as well.

Corollary
1.3
allows us to contrast our results with the undirected case. For all

, we know
\mathcal{G}(n,p=\frac{d}{n})
and
\mathcal{G}(n,d)
are concentrated in at most two values. The gap in our directed result is exponential in

. While the bounds of Corollary

may be improvable, the case

suggests the oriented chromatic number may not be concentrated in as small a set of values as the unoriented cases.

The lower bound on
{\chi_{o}}
given by Kostochka, Sopena and Zhu
[
34
]
in fact showed that for

with

has some orientation with

in Corollary

As part of our argument, we maximize a specific function over doubly stochastic matrices. This is common in investigations of the chromatic number of regular graphs; see

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
k\geq 3
, let
G=(V,E)
be a connected regular graph on vertex set

, and let

Optimization Over Doubly Stochastic Matrices

In this section we prove Proposition
1.4
which describes how to maximize certain functions over doubly stochastic matrices. This optimization result generalizes that given by Achlioptas and Naor
[
3
]
in their proof of the concentration of the usual chromatic number in random graphs.

For every
k\geq 3
and constant
c
, Achlioptas and Naor define a function
g_{c}
on
k\times k
doubly-stochastic matrices
\text{\boldmath$A$}=(a_{ij})
by

g_{c}(\text{\boldmath$A$})=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}\log a_{ij}+c\log\left(1-\frac{2}{k}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}^{2}\right)

(3)

and show that for
c\leq\frac{(k-1)^{3}}{k(k-2)}\log(k-1)
,
g_{c}
is maximized in its domain by the matrix
\frac{1}{k}J_{k}
that has all entries equal to
\frac{1}{k}
. The first sum in the expression for
g_{c}
is viewed as the ‘entropy part’ of the function, while the second term is viewed as a function of the 2-norm of
A
:
||\text{\boldmath$A$}||_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}^{2}
. In their paper, Achlioptas and Naor, in fact, give a much more general result, replacing the first sum with one of the form
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}h(a_{ij})
where
h
satisfies a number of conditions involving its derivatives.

We use their techniques to show a generalization involving the second term,
replacing the 2-norm with an expression involving the ‘Lagrangian’ of a regular
graph, a function over its edges. The proof uses the key tools developed by Achlioptas and Naor in their
optimization result, which are stated here for reference with some of their
notation.

For any
k>1
, define a function
f:[1/k,1]\to\mathbb{R}
as follows. For any
r\in[1/k,1]
, set

x(r)=\frac{1+\sqrt{(k-1)(kr-1)}}{k},\qquad\text{and}\qquad y(r)=\frac{1-x(r)}{k-1}=\frac{k-1-\sqrt{(k-1)(kr-1)}}{k(k-1)}

defined to be the unique values

satisfying
x+(k-1)y=1
and
x^{2}+(k-1)y^{2}=r
. Set

f(r)=-x(r)\log x(r)-(k-1)y(r)\log y(r).

(4)

matrices by

\nu_{d}(\text{\boldmath$A$})=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V(G)}a_{v}\log a_{v}+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{k^{2}}\left(||\text{\boldmath$A$}||_{2}^{2}-1\right)+\frac{2|E|}{k^{4}}\right)

(11)

is maximized at
\text{\boldmath$A$}=\frac{1}{k}J_{k}
for a given value of
d
, then the same is true for the function
\gamma_{d}
. From here, the optimization tools developed in
[
3
]
are used to optimize the sum in the function
\nu_{d}
, subject to a fixed value of
||\text{\boldmath$A$}||_{2}^{2}
.
In order to show that for all doubly-stochastic
A
,
\nu_{d}(\text{\boldmath$A$})\leq\nu_{d}\left(\frac{1}{k}J_{k}\right)
, fix the value of
||\text{\boldmath$A$}||_{2}^{2}=\rho\in[1,k]
. By Theorem
2.1
, it suffices to show that for every choice of
\rho\in[1,k]
and
m\in\left[0,\frac{k(k-\rho)}{k-1}\right]
,

\frac{m\log k}{k}+\frac{(k-m)}{k}f\left(\frac{k\rho-m}{k(k-m)}\right)+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{\lambda(\rho-1)}{k^{2}}+\frac{2|E|}{k^{4}}\right)\leq\nu_{d}\left(\tfrac{1}{k}J_{k}\right)=\log k+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{2|E|}{k^{4}}\right).

Rearranging gives the condition

\displaystyle\frac{d}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\lambda(\rho-1)}{2|E|/k^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{m}{k}\right)\left[\log k-f\left(\frac{k\rho-m}{k(k-m)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{m}{k}\right)\left[f\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)-f\left(\frac{k\rho-m}{k(k-m)}\right)\right].

Using the inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
, it thus suffices to show that for every
\rho\in[1,k]
and
m\in\left[0,\frac{k(k-\rho)}{k-1}\right]
,

d\leq\frac{2\left(1-\frac{m}{k}\right)}{\frac{\lambda(\rho-1)}{2|E|/k^{2}}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)-f\left(\frac{k\rho-m}{k(k-m)}\right)\right]

Consider now the right hand side of the above equation.

\displaystyle\frac{2\left(1-\frac{m}{k}\right)}{\frac{\lambda(\rho-1)}{2|E|/k^{2}}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)-f\left(\frac{k\rho-m}{k(k-m)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left(1-\frac{m}{k}\right)}{\frac{\lambda(\rho-1)}{2|E|/k^{2}}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)-f\left(\frac{1}{k}+\frac{k\rho-k}{k(k-m)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4|E|\left(1-\frac{m}{k}\right)}{k^{2}\lambda(\rho-1)}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)-f\left(\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\rho-1}{k(1-m/k)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4|E|}{k^{3}\lambda}\left[\frac{f\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)-f\left(\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\rho-1}{k(1-m/k)}\right)}{\frac{\rho-1}{k(1-m/k)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4|E|}{k^{3}\lambda}\eta\left(\frac{\rho-1}{k(1-m/k)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4|E|}{k^{3}\lambda}\cdot\frac{k-1}{k-2}\log(k-1).

(12)

The final inequality in Equation (
12
) follows from Lemma
2.2
since
0\leq\frac{\rho-1}{1-m/k}\leq\frac{\rho-1}{(\rho-1)/(k-1)}=k-1
for
0\leq m\leq\frac{k(k-\rho)}{k-1}
and hence
0\leq\frac{\rho-1}{k(1-m/k)}\leq 1-\frac{1}{k}
is in the domain of
\eta
.
Thus,
d\leq\frac{4|E|}{k^{3}\lambda}\cdot\frac{k-1}{k-2}\log(k-1)
suffices to guarantee that
\nu_{d}
is maximized at
\text{\boldmath$A$}=\frac{1}{k}J_{k}
and hence that the same holds for the function
\gamma_{d}
.

Since the maximum in Theorem
2.1
and the minimum in Lemma
2.2
are unique, then
d>\frac{4|E|}{k^{3}\lambda}\cdot\frac{k-1}{k-2}\log(k-1)
, the function
\nu_{d}
attains a unique maximum at
\frac{1}{k}J_{k}
, and hence the same is true for the function
\gamma_{d}
.
∎

To see that Proposition
1.4
generalizes the optimization result for the function in Equation (
3
), consider the graph
G=K_{k}\otimes K_{k}
, the graph on vertex set
[k]^{2}
with
(u,v)\sim(a,b)
iff
u\neq a
and
v\neq b
. The graph
G
is a connected
(k-1)^{2}
regular graph. Since the adjacency matrix of
G
is
(J_{k}-I_{k})\otimes(J_{k}-I_{k})
it has as eigenvalues
(k-1)^{2}
,
1
, and
-(k-1)
. The eigenspace for
-(k-1)
is the set of all vectors of the form
1_{k}\otimes\text{\boldmath$v$}
or
\text{\boldmath$v$}\otimes 1_{k}
, where
\text{\boldmath$v$}\bullet 1_{k}=0
. For any such eigenvector and any doubly-stochastic matrix
A
,
(1_{k}\otimes\text{\boldmath$v$})\bullet\text{\boldmath$A$}=(\text{\boldmath$v$}\otimes 1_{k})\bullet A=0
and so, decomposing
A
into the basis of eigenvectors for the adjacency matrix of
G
gives

2\mathcal{L}_{G}(\text{\boldmath$A$})=(k-1)^{2}\cdot||\tfrac{1}{k}J_{k}||_{2}^{2}+1\cdot||\text{\boldmath$A$}-\tfrac{1}{k}J_{k}||_{2}^{2}=(k-1)^{2}+||\text{\boldmath$A$}||_{2}^{2}-1=k^{2}-2k+||\text{\boldmath$A$}||_{2}^{2}.

Since
G
has
\frac{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}{2}
edges, Proposition
1.4
says that for
k\geq 3
, if

c=\frac{d}{2}\leq\frac{2|E|}{k^{3}\cdot 1}\cdot\frac{(k-1)}{(k-2)}\log(k-1)=\frac{(k-1)^{3}}{k(k-2)}\log(k-1),

then the function

\gamma_{d}(\text{\boldmath$A$})=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V(G)}a_{v}\log a_{v}+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{2\mathcal{L}_{G}(\text{\boldmath$A$})}{k^{2}}\right)=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V(G)}a_{v}\log a_{v}+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{k^{2}-2k+||\text{\boldmath$A$}||_{2}^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)

is maximized at
\text{\boldmath$A$}=\frac{1}{k}J_{k}
.
For the result here, we shall take
G
to be the Kronecker product of a doubly-regular tournament with itself, as defined in Section
3
.

3
Properties of Doubly Regular Tournaments

In this section we develop several results regarding Kronecker products of tournaments with nice properties.

Doubly-regular tournaments were introduced by Brown and Reid
[
44
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A tournament
T
is said to be
doubly-regular
iff there are integers
k,\ell
so that for every
v\in V(T)
,
\deg^{+}(v)=k
and for every pair of vertices
v,w
,
|N^{+}(v)\cap N^{+}(w)|=\ell
.

Brown and Reid
[
44
]
note that if
T
is a doubly-regular tournament of order
n
, then
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
,
k=\frac{n-1}{2}
and
\ell=\frac{n-3}{4}
. They further relate the existence of a tournament of order
n
to the existence of a ‘skew Hadamard matrix’ of order
n+1
. A recent survey on the existence of skew-Hadamard matrices was given by Koukouvinos and Stylianou
[
35
]
.
A special case of a doubly regular tournament are the ‘Paley tournaments’ (see, eg
[
26
]
). For each prime
q\equiv 3\mod 4
, the
Paley
tournament
T_{q}
is defined on vertex set
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by directing the edge
\{u,v\}
from
u
to
v
iff
(v-u)
is a square modulo
q
and otherwise directing it from
v
to
u
. Standard number theoretic arguments give that exactly one of
v-u,u-v\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
, so the direction is well-defined, and as

|(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}|=\frac{q-1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}|\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}|

we see that
T_{q}
satisfies
d^{+}(v)=d^{-}(v)=\frac{q-1}{2}
for every
v\in V(T_{q})
.
As shown in
[
26
]
, for each pair of distinct vertices
u,v\in V(T_{q})
, there are exactly
\frac{k-3}{4}
vertices
w
that satisfy
u\to w
and
v\to w
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
T
is a doubly-regular tournament on
k
vertices with signed adjacency matrix
M
, then
M
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{C}
and has eigenvalues:

•

0
, with the eigenspace equal to the span of
\mathbf{1}
.

•

i\sqrt{k}
with multiplicity
\frac{k-1}{2}
, and

•

-i\sqrt{k}
with multiplicity
\frac{k-1}{2}

Proof.

Since the signed adjacency matrix satisfies
M^{T}=-M
, it is skew symmetric and real skew symmetric matrices are diagonalizable over
\mathbb{C}
.

For the eigenvalues of
M
, consider the product,
M^{2}
. If
M=(m_{ij})_{k\times k}
, the
(i,j)
entry of
M^{2}
is
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}m_{i\ell}m_{\ell j}
. If
i=j
, then
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}m_{i\ell}m_{\ell i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}-m_{i\ell}^{2}=-(k-1)
. If
i\neq j
, then for any
\ell\notin\{i,j\}
,
m_{i\ell}m_{\ell j}
is equal to
1
if
(i,\ell,j)
is a directed path (in either direction) and
-1
otherwise. By Lemma
3.2
, this is

\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}m_{i\ell}m_{\ell j}=-\frac{(k-3)}{4}+\frac{(k-3)}{4}+\frac{(k+1)}{4}-\frac{(k-3)}{4}=1.

Thus
M^{2}=J_{k}-kI_{k}
, which has eigenvalues
0
with multiplicity
1
and eigenvector
\mathbf{1}
and
-k
with multiplicity
k-1
. Thus, the eigenvalues for
M
are
0
with multiplicity
1
and eigenvector
\mathbf{1}
and complex eigenvalues
i\sqrt{k}
and
-i\sqrt{k}
. Since
tr(M)=0
, the eigenvalues
i\sqrt{k}
and
-i\sqrt{k}
occur with equal multiplicity.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

For any tournament
T
, define the Kronecker product of
T
with itself,
T^{\otimes 2}
, as the graph with vertex set
V(T)^{2}
and edges
(u,v)\sim(x,y)
iff either
u\to x
and
v\to y
or
x\to u
and
y\to v
. Note that while
T
is a directed graph, we have defined
T^{\otimes 2}
to be undirected. This is more convenient for our calculations, but it should be noted there is a natural orientation of
T^{\otimes 2}
where
(u,v)\to(x,y)
if
u\to x
and
v\to y
.

Proposition 3.5

Let

. Then the Kroenecker product graph
T^{\otimes 2}
is connected.

Proof.

First, consider two non-adjacent vertices

and
v\neq y
. Without loss of generality, assume that, in
T
,
u\to x
and
y\to v
. Define the following:

\displaystyle a_{++}

\displaystyle=|N^{+}(u)\cap N^{+}(x)|

\displaystyle a_{+-}

\displaystyle=|N^{+}(u)\cap N^{-}(x)|

\displaystyle a_{-+}

\displaystyle=|N^{-}(u)\cap N^{+}(x)|

\displaystyle a_{--}

\displaystyle=|N^{-}(u)\cap N^{-}(x)|

Since
T
is regular and
u\to x
, then
a_{++}+a_{+-}=\frac{k-3}{2}
,
a_{-+}+a_{--}=\frac{k-1}{2}
,
a_{+-}+a_{--}=\frac{k-3}{2}
, and
a_{++}+a_{-+}=\frac{k-1}{2}
. Similarly, define

\displaystyle b_{++}

\displaystyle=|N^{+}(v)\cap N^{+}(y)|

\displaystyle b_{+-}

\displaystyle=|N^{+}(v)\cap N^{-}(y)|

\displaystyle b_{-+}

\displaystyle=|N^{-}(v)\cap N^{+}(y)|

\displaystyle b_{--}

\displaystyle=|N^{-}(v)\cap N^{-}(y)|

Since
y\to v
, then
b_{++}+b_{+-}=\frac{k-1}{2}
,
b_{-+}+b_{--}=\frac{k-3}{2}
,
b_{++}+b_{-+}=\frac{k-3}{2}
, and
b_{+-}+b_{--}=\frac{k-1}{2}
.

If there are any
i,j\in\{+,-\}
with
a_{ij}>0
and
b_{ij}>0
, then there are vertices
w,z
(not necessarily distinct) so that the direction of
(u,w)
matches
(v,z)
and the direction of
(x,w)
matches
(y,z)
. Thus,
(u,v),(w,z),(x,y)
is a path in
T^{\otimes}
. To see that such pair always exists, suppose that one does not. If
a_{++}\neq 0
, then
b_{++}=0
and so
b_{+-}=\frac{k-1}{2}
and
b_{-+}=\frac{k-3}{2}
which implies that
b_{--}=0
. The fact that
b_{+-},b_{-+}>0
would imply that
a_{+-}=a_{-+}=0
. However, this is impossible since
a_{-+}+a_{--}=\frac{k-1}{2}\neq\frac{k-3}{2}=a_{+-}+a_{--}
.

Next, consider the case of vertices
(u,u)
and
(u,x)
. Note that the vertices

form a clique in

. Let

. Then,
(u,u)
is adjacent to
(z,z)
and by the previous case, there is a path between
(z,z)
and
(u,x)
.

For the final case, consider vertices
(u,v),(u,x)
with
v\neq x,v\neq u
, and
x\neq u
. By the previous case, there is a path between
(u,v)
and
(u,u)
and a path between
(u,x)
and
(u,u)
. Thus,
(u,v)
and
(u,x)
are in the same component.

Since the graph
T^{\otimes 2}
is isomorphic to itself under the operation of interchanging the order of pairs, this covers all cases and shows that every pair of vertices in
T^{\otimes 2}
are joined by a path.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Note that the result does not hold for

since the unique regular tournament on
3
vertices is the directed
3
-cycle, whose Kroenecker square is a disjoint union of three copies of
K_{3}
.

Throughout the paper, it is regularly assumed that
k>3
and part of the reason for this is that when the product graph
G
is a disjoint union of cliques, the optimization result from Proposition
1.4
holds without any assumptions on
d
in terms of
k
. Indeed, suppose that
G
is a graph on vertex set with
[k]^{2}
and
G\cong kK_{k}
, a disjoint union of
k
cliques with the property that the vertices of each clique form a transversal in
[k]\times[k]
. Fix
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}=k
and restrict to doubly-stochastic matrices with the property that the sum of entries on vertices,
V_{i}
, corresponding to the
i
-th copy of
K_{k}
is exactly
s_{i}
. Among such matrices,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{G}(\text{\boldmath$A$})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{v,w\in V_{i}}a_{v}a_{w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\left(\sum_{v\in V_{i}}a_{v}\right)^{2}-\sum_{v\in V_{i}}a_{v}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(s_{i}^{2}-\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{k}\right)

(by convexity)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-1)}{2k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}^{2},

Then, for any

Because both functions

and

are concave down, the upper bound for

is maximized when

, which gives precisely the value of
\gamma_{d}(\frac{1}{k}J_{k})
.

In particular, this means that the optimization result holds without condition on

for oriented

-colourings with the directed

-cycle. However, for the expected number of such

-colourings to be bounded away from

is required and this case is covered in Section

using structural results of underlying graphs and their possible colourings.

In this section we prove lower bounds on

and

using first moment arguments.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
c>0
be a real number and
k
be an integer such that
c>\frac{1}{2}u_{k}
. Then a.a.s. there are no proper oriented
k
-colourings of
\vec{\mathcal{M}}(n,m=cn)
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a random variable counting the number of proper oriented
k
-colourings of
\vec{\mathcal{M}}(n,m=cn)
. Fix a colouring of
n
vertices and a tournament on
k
vertices. Let
a_{i}
be the proportion of vertices that received colour
i
. Then the probability that a directed edge connects two vertices of colour
i
is
a_{i}^{2}
, so the probability that it connects different colours is
1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2}
and the probability it connects two different colours and its orientation matches the tournament is
\frac{1}{2}(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2})
. By convexity, since the colour proportions must add to one, this probability is at most
\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{k})
. As each edge is added independently, the probability that the resulting multigraph is properly coloured is at most
(\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{k}))^{m}
. Therefore

\mathbf{E}X\leq 2^{\binom{k}{2}}k^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{m}=2^{\binom{k}{2}}\left(k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{c}\right)^{n}.

Noting

c>\frac{1}{2}u_{k}\implies c\log\left(\frac{2k}{k-1}\right)>\log k\implies 1>k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{c},

we see
\mathbf{E}X=o(1)
and thus a.a.s. there are no proper
k
-oriented-colourings of
\vec{\mathcal{M}}(n,m=cn)
.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
k
be integers such that
d>u_{k}
. Then a.a.s. there are no proper oriented
k
-colourings of
\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)
.

Proof.

Let
X
be the number of oriented
k
-colourings of a random
d
-regular oriented multigraph
\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)
. We calculate
\mathbf{E}X
by summing over pairs
(\vec{C},{\chi_{o}})
, where
\vec{C}\in\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)
is a configuration with edges given orientations independently and uniformly at random and
{\chi_{o}}
is an oriented colouring that respects
\vec{C}
, then dividing by the total number of oriented configurations.

Starting with the denominator, each of the
|\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)|
many graphs
of the configuration model has
\frac{dn}{2}
edges, each with one of two
orientations, giving

|\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)|\cdot 2^{\frac{1}{2}dn}=\frac{(dn)!}{(\frac{1}{2}dn)!2^{\frac{1}{2}dn}}\cdot 2^{\frac{1}{2}dn}=\frac{(dn)!}{(\frac{1}{2}dn)!}

total oriented configurations.

Now we turn to the numerator. For each
i\in[k]
, let
a_{i}\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}
be the proportion of the vertices of
\vec{C}
that

{\chi_{o}}
stipulates receive colour
i
. We have

a_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i\in[k]\and\sum_{i\in[k]}a_{i}=1.

(13)

Set
{\bf a}=(a_{i})_{i\in[k]}
and note that
{\bf a}\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}^{k}
.

To be a proper oriented
k
-colouring, all edges between colour classes
must have the same orientation which may be chosen arbitarily. Fix a
k
-colouring of

K_{k}
and choose any of the
2^{\binom{k}{2}}
possible orientations uniformly
at random. Then orient any edges between vertices of colour
i
and
i^{\prime}

according to that orientation.

Having fixed the direction of all the edges, we now need only specify their
endpoints. To generate edges in the configuration model, we replace each vertex
with a collection of
d
vertices and select a perfect matching on the resulting

dn
vertices. The larger collection of vertices inherits a colouring from the
colouring of the base vertices. For each pair of distinct colours
i,i^{\prime}
, let

b_{i,i^{\prime}}\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}
denote the proportion of the vertices that

{\chi_{o}}
colours with colour
i
and are paired with a vertex of colour
i^{\prime}
. (As
{\chi_{o}}
is a
proper colouring there are no edges from a vertex of colour
i
to another
vertex of colour
i
.) Let
K
be the set
\{(i,i^{\prime})\in[k]^{2}\mid i\neq i^{\prime}\}
. We have

\displaystyle b_{i,i^{\prime}}\geq 0\quad

\displaystyle\quad\forall(i,i^{\prime})\in K

\displaystyle b_{i,i^{\prime}}=b_{i^{\prime},i}\quad

\displaystyle\quad\forall(i,i^{\prime})\in K

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}b_{i,i^{\prime}}=a_{i^{\prime}}\quad

\displaystyle\quad\forall i\in[k]

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}b_{i,i^{\prime}}=a_{i}\quad

\displaystyle\quad\forall i^{\prime}\in[k]

(14)

Set
{\bf b}=(b_{i,i^{\prime}})_{(i,i^{\prime})\in K}
and note that
{\bf b}\in\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}^{k(k-1)}
.

To generate a pair
(\vec{C},{\chi_{o}})
, we first fix
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
. We then colour the
n

vertices in accordance with
{\bf a}
, which can be done in

\frac{n!}{\prod\limits_{i\in[k]}(a_{i}n)!}

many ways. To add edges in accordance with
{\bf b}
we first select the
appropriate numbers of vertices in each colour class. The configuration model
blows up the

vertices of colour
i

a_{i}dn
vertices, so this
selection can be made in

\prod_{i\in[k]}\frac{(a_{i}dn)!}{\prod\limits_{i^{\prime}\neq i}(b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn)!}

many ways. Note that each pair of distinct colours
\{i,i^{\prime}\}
contributes two terms to the
denominator of this product. We then select a perfect matching between the
vertices of each pair of colours. For a given pair of coulours
(i,i^{\prime})
, we only
chose one matching, so there are

\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}(b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn)!

many such selections. Putting these steps together, we see there are

\frac{n!}{\prod_{i\in k}(a_{i}n)!}\prod_{i\in k}\frac{(a_{i}dn)!}{\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}(b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn)!}\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}(b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn)!=n!\prod_{i\in k}\frac{(a_{i}dn)!}{(a_{i}n)!}\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}\frac{1}{(b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn)!}

many colourings that satisfy
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
. Finally, colour the vertices of
K_{k}
with
[k]
and choose a random orientation of the edges, then orient the edges of
\vec{P}
in accordance with that colouring; there are
2^{\binom{k}{2}}
such orientations of
K_{k}
from which to choose.

Altogether, keeping in mind (
13
) and (
14
),

\sum_{i<i^{\prime}}b_{i,i^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i<i^{\prime}}2b_{i,i^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i<i^{\prime}}(b_{i,i^{\prime}}+b_{i^{\prime},i})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[k]}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}b_{i,i^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[k]}a_{i}=\frac{1}{2},

(15)

and applying Stirling’s approximation
x!\sim\sqrt{2\pi x}(x/e)^{x}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}X

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{{\bf a},{\bf b}}2^{\binom{k}{2}}\cdot n!\prod_{i\in k}\frac{(a_{i}dn)!}{(a_{i}n)!}\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}\frac{1}{(b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn)!}}{\frac{(dn)}{(\frac{dn}{2})!}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\sum_{{\bf a},{\bf b}}2^{\binom{k}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\pi n}(n/e)^{n}\prod_{i\in k}\frac{\sqrt{d}(a_{i}dn/e)^{a_{i}dn}}{(a_{i}n/e)^{a_{i}n}}\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn}(b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn/e)^{b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn}}}{\frac{(dn/e)^{dn}}{(dn/(2e))^{dn/2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{{\bf a},{\bf b}}2^{\binom{k}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\pi n}\prod_{i\in k}\sqrt{d}(a_{i})^{a_{i}(d-1)n}\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn}(b_{i,i^{\prime}})^{b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn}}}{2^{dn/2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf a},{\bf b}}\frac{2^{\binom{k}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\pi n}\cdot d^{k/2}}{\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}\sqrt{2\pi b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{dn/2}\prod_{i\in[k]}a_{i}^{a_{i}(d-1)n}\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}\frac{1}{b_{i,i^{\prime}}^{b_{i,i^{\prime}}dn}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf a},{\bf b}}\poly(n)\prod_{i<i^{\prime}}\left(\frac{(a_{i}a_{i^{\prime}})^{d-1}}{2(b_{i,i^{\prime}})^{d}}\right)^{b_{i,i^{\prime}}n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf a},{\bf b}}\poly(n)e^{nf({\bf a},{\bf b})}

where

f({\bf a},{\bf b})=\sum_{i<i^{\prime}}b_{i,i^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{(a_{i}a_{i^{\prime}})^{d-1}}{2(b_{i,i^{\prime}})^{d}}\right)

(16)

and
\poly(n)
is a function bounded above by a function that is polynomial in
n
.

Let
{\bf\hat{a}}=(\frac{1}{k})_{i\in[k]}
be a vector representing an equal
distribution of colours. Define
{\bf\hat{b}}=(\frac{1}{k(k-1)})_{(i,i^{\prime})\in K}
.

Due to Molloy and Reed
[
38

, the number of configurations respecting a fixed
{\bf a}
is maximized when
{\bf a}={\bf\hat{a}}
. Furthermore, we claim that if
{\bf a}={\bf\hat{a}}
, then
f({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf b})
is maximized at
{\bf b}={\bf\hat{b}}
.

Note that

f({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf b})=\sum_{i<i^{\prime}}-db_{i,i^{\prime}}\log(b_{i,i^{\prime}})-(d-1)\log k-\frac{d}{2}\log\left(2\right).

We maximize
f({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf b})
subject to the relaxed constraints

b_{i,i^{\prime}}\geq 0\ \forall(i,i^{\prime})\in K\and\sum_{(i,i^{\prime})\in K}b_{i,i^{\prime}}=1.

(17)

First we note that the boundary is closed but does not contain a maximum: if,
say,
b_{1,2}=0
and
b_{2,1}>0
, then setting
b^{\prime}_{1,2}=b^{\prime}_{2,1}=\frac{1}{2}b_{2,1}
increases

-b_{2,1}\log(b_{2,1})<-b_{2,1}\log(\tfrac{1}{2}b_{2,1})

where we take the convention

.

Using the method of Lagrange multipliers, we see

\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{i,i^{\prime}}}[-db_{i,i^{\prime}}\log(b_{i,i^{\prime}})]=-d(1+\log(b_{i,i^{\prime}}))=\lambda

for each
i\neq i^{\prime}
. Then

b_{i,i^{\prime}}=\exp\left(-1-\frac{\lambda}{d}\right)

and thus

1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\in[k]\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}b_{i,i^{\prime}}=k(k-1)\exp(-1-\frac{\lambda}{d})

or

\lambda=-d\left(1+\log\left(\frac{1}{k(k-1)}\right)\right)

which gives

b_{i,i^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{k(k-1)}

for each
i\neq i^{\prime}
.

Note that the constraints in Equation (
17
) are a relaxation of the
constraints in Equation (
14
). Therefore

\max_{{\bf b}\text{ s.t. }(\ref{eq:bcons})}f({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf b})\leq\max_{{\bf b}\text{
s.t. }(\ref{eq:brelaxedcons})}f({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf b})=f({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf\hat{b}})

which gives

\exp(nf({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf\hat{b}}))=\left(k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{n}.

Note that each of the
\frac{dn}{2}
edges of
G
is adjacent to two vertices of
different colours and determining those colours for each edge specifies
{\bf b}
.
Thus there are no more than

\left(\frac{dn}{2}\right)^{k(k-1)}=O\left(n^{k(k-1)}\right)

possible assignments for
{\bf b}
, so

\sum_{{\bf b}\text{ s.t.}(\ref{eq:bcons})}\exp(nf({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf b}))=O\left(n^{k(k-1)}\right)\left(k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{n}.

Similarly, there are at most
n^{k}
assignments of colours to vertices, each of
which determines an assignment to
{\bf a}
, so

\sum_{{\bf a}\text{ s.t. }(\ref{eq:acons})}\sum_{{\bf b}\text{ s.t. }(\ref{eq:bcons})}\exp(nf({\bf a},{\bf b}))=O(n^{k})\sum_{{\bf b}\text{ s.t. }(\ref{eq:bcons})}\exp(nf({\bf\hat{a}},{\bf b}))=O\left(n^{k^{2}}\right)\left(k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{n}.

Now recalling that
\poly(n)=O(n^{m})
for some
m
,

\mathbf{E}X=\sum_{{\bf a}\text{ s. t. }(\ref{eq:acons})}\sum_{{\bf b}\text{ s.t. }(\ref{eq:bcons})}\poly(n)e^{nf({\bf a},{\bf b})}=O(n^{k^{2}+m})\left(k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{n}.

Note that

d>u_{k}\implies d\log\left(\frac{2k}{k-1}\right)>2\log k\implies 1>k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{d/2}.

Therefore when
d>u_{k}
,
\mathbf{E}X=o(1)
and there are a.a.s. no
k
-colourings of
\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)
.
∎

5
Upper Bounds

In this section we use the second moment method to get upper bounds for

{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{M}}(n,m))
and
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d))
by finding
values of
k
for which oriented colourings are likely to exist. To simplify the
calculations, we narrow our consideration to particularly well-behaved oriented
colourings. If
T
is a tournament on
k
vertices, then a
T
-colouring of

\vec{C}
is a colouring with a homomorphism onto
T
. We call a
k
-colouring
(oriented or otherwise)
equitable
if there are
\frac{n}{k}
vertices
of each colour. Note that this condition requires that
n
is divisible by
k
.
We make that assumption throughout this section and discuss how to modify these
arguments for
n
not a multiple of
k
in Section
8.3
.

5.1
\vec{\mathcal{M}}(n,m)

Let
c>0
be real and
k\geq 3
be an integer such that there exists a doubly regular tournament of order
k
, denoted
T_{k}
. Let
Y
be the number of equitable
T_{k}
oriented colourings of
\vec{\mathcal{M}}(n,m=cn)
. The first moment is straightforward to calculate.

Lemma 5.1
.

\mathbf{E}Y\sim k^{k/2}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)/2}\left(k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{c}\right)^{n}.

Proof.

As we are only counting equitable colourings, the probability an arc chosen at random is correct is precisely
\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{k})
. Therefore the probability a colouring is correct is
(\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{k}))^{m}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}Y

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{((n/k)!)^{k}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\sqrt{2\pi n}}{(\sqrt{2\pi n/k})^{k}}\cdot\frac{(n/e)^{n}}{((n/ek)^{n/k})^{k}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{c}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=k^{k/2}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)/2}\left(k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{c}\right)^{n}.

∎

The second moment will require summing over the possible overlap matrices of
pairs of colourings. To accurately estimate the sum of this lattice, we use the
following Laplace summation technique of Pérez and the second author
[
40
]
based on
work by Greenhill, Janson, and Rucínski
[
24
]
:

Theorem 5.2

(
[
40
, Proposition 3.4]
)
.

Suppose the following:

(i)

\Gamma=(V_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma})
is a non-empty bipartite multigraph with at least one cycle.

(ii)

D
is the unsigned incidence matrix of
\Gamma
.

(iii)

\tau(\Gamma)
is the number of maximal forests in
\Gamma
.

(iv)

\mathbb{V}=\Ker(D)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}
is a vector space of dimension
r
.

(v)

\text{\boldmath$y$}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V_{\Gamma}|}
such that

D\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}

(18)

is a consistent linear system.

(vi)

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}
is a compact convex set with non-empty interior
K^{\circ}
.

(vii)

\phi:K\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function and the maximum of
\phi
in
K
subject to (
18
) is attained at a unique maximizer
\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}\in K^{\circ}
.

(viii)

\phi
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of
\hat{x}
and
H
is its Hessian matrix at
\hat{x}
.

(ix)

\psi:K_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function on some neighbourhood
K_{1}\subseteq K
of
\hat{x}
with
\psi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})>0
.

(x)

For each positive integer
n
,

\mathbb{X}_{n}=\left\{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in K\cap\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}:D\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}\right\}

is non-empty, and there is a positive real number
b_{n}
and a function
T_{n}:\mathbb{X}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=O(b_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(n)}),\quad\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}

and

T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=b_{n}(\psi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(1))e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})},\quad\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}\cap K_{1},

uniformly for
x
in the indicated sets.

Then provided
\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})\neq 0
, as
n\to\infty
,

\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})\sim\frac{\psi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}{\tau(\Gamma)^{1/2}\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})^{1/2}}(2\pi n)^{r/2}b_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}.

Using Theorem
5.2
and
Proposition
1.4
, we give the following asymptotic value for the
expected value of the square of the number of oriented colourings.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any integer
k>3
such that there exists a doubly regular tournament
T_{k}
of order
k
and
0<c<\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k}
,

\mathbf{E}Y^{2}\sim\left(\frac{(k-1)^{4}}{((k-1)^{2}-2c)^{2}-4c^{2}k^{2}}\right)^{(k-1)^{2}/4}(\mathbf{E}Y)^{2}.

Proof.

For any oriented graph
\vec{G}
with
n
vertices, let
h_{1},h_{2}:V(\vec{G})\to V(T_{k})
be any two equitable oriented colourings with the tournament
T_{k}
.
For every
i,j\in[k]
, let
na_{i,j}
be the number of vertices of
\vec{G}

that receive colour
i
with colouring
h_{1}
and that receive colour
j
with
colouring
h_{2}
. Let
\text{\boldmath$A$}=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{k}
be the
overlap matrix

for the pair of colourings. As the two colourings are both equitable,

\displaystyle\forall(i,j)\in[k]^{2},\qquad a_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall i\in[k],\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}

\displaystyle\forall j\in[k],\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}

(19)

Note that the overlap matrix can be prescribed first and then the arcs in the random oriented graph chosen.

Under the two colourings
h_{1},h_{2}
, every vertex of
\vec{G}
receives a pair
of colours in
[k]^{2}
. Let
x,y\in V(\vec{G})
be two vertices and set
u=(h_{1}(x),h_{2}(x))
,
v=(h_{1}(y),h_{2}(y))
. There can be an edge in
\vec{G}

between
x
and
y
only in the case that there are arcs in
T_{k}
between

h_{1}(x)
and
h_{1}(y)
and between
h_{2}(x)
and
h_{2}(y)
and that the direction
of the edges matches: either
h_{1}(x)\to h_{1}(y)
and
h_{2}(x)\to h_{2}(y)
or else

h_{1}(y)\to h_{1}(x)
and
h_{2}(y)\to h_{2}(x)
. Thus, in terms of the Kroenecker
product of tournaments (Definition
3.4
), arcs in

\vec{G}
between
x
and
y
can occur exactly when

and the direction of the arc is prescribed by the tournament
T_{k}
.

Thus, given an overlap matrix
\text{\boldmath$A$}=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{k}
, the probability an edge in a random graph
\vec{G}
is coloured correctly is
\sum\limits_{uv\in E(T^{\otimes 2})}a_{u}a_{v}
, so

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}Y^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$}\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:gnm_Acons}}\frac{n!}{\prod_{v\in V}(na_{v})!}\left(\sum_{uv\in E(T^{\otimes 2})}a_{u}a_{v}\right)^{m}

(20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$}\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:gnm_Acons}}\frac{\xi(n)(n/e)^{n}}{\prod_{v\in V}\xi(na_{v})(na_{v}/e)^{na_{v}}}\left(\sum_{uv\in E(T^{\otimes 2})}a_{u}a_{v}\right)^{cn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$}\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:gnm_Acons}}\xi(n)\left(\prod_{v\in V}\xi(na_{v})\right)^{-1}\left(\prod_{v\in V}a_{v}^{-a_{v}}\left(\sum_{uv\in E(T^{\otimes 2})}a_{u}a_{v}\right)^{c}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$}\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:gnm_Acons}}p(n,\text{\boldmath$A$})e^{nf(\text{\boldmath$A$})}

(21)

where

p(n,\text{\boldmath$A$})=\xi(n)\left(\prod_{v\in V}\xi(na_{v})\right)^{-1}

(22)

and

f(\text{\boldmath$A$})=-\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}+c\log\left(\sum_{uv\in E(T^{\otimes 2})}a_{u}a_{v}\right)

(23)

We seek to apply Theorem
5.2
to the function
f
. Assign to each constraint of Equation (
19
) of the form
\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}=\frac{1}{k}
a vertex
w_{1,i}
and to each constraint of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{ij}=\frac{1}{k}
a vertex
w_{2,j}
. Let
V_{\Gamma}=\{w_{1,i},w_{2,j}\}_{i,j=1}^{k}
,
E_{\Gamma}=\{\{w_{1,i},w_{2,j}\}\mid i,j\in[k]\}
, and
\Gamma=(V_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma})
. Then
\Gamma\cong K_{k,k}
, and as
k>3
,
\Gamma
contains a cycle. The equality constraints in Equation (
19
) are equivalent to
D\text{\boldmath$x$}=\frac{1}{k}\text{\boldmath$1$}_{2k}
where
D
is the unsigned incidence matrix of
\Gamma
. Onodera
[
41
]
proved that the number of spanning trees of
K_{m,n}
is
m^{n-1}n^{m-1}
, so
\tau(\Gamma)=k^{2k-2}
. As
\Gamma
is bipartite and connected, standard results (see e.g.
[
23
, Theorem 8.2.1]
) give
\dim D=|V_{\Gamma}|-1=2k-1
and therefore

r=\dim\mathbb{V}=k^{2}-\dim D=k^{2}-2k+1=(k-1)^{2}.

Setting
\text{\boldmath$y$}=\frac{1}{k}\text{\boldmath$1$}_{2k}
we see
D\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}
is a consistent linear system;
\text{\boldmath$x$}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\text{\boldmath$1$}_{k^{2}}
is a solution. Set

K=\{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{k^{2}}\mid 0\leq a_{v}\leq\tfrac{1}{k}\}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{1}=\{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{k^{2}}\mid\tfrac{0.9}{k^{2}}\leq a_{v}\leq\tfrac{1.1}{k^{2}}\}.

Then
K
is a compact convex set with nonempty interior,
K_{1}\subseteq K
, and
K_{1}
contains the point
\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}J_{k}
. Set
\alpha_{v}=ka_{v}
, let
\text{\boldmath$x$}=(\alpha_{v})_{v\in V}
, and define
\phi(\frac{1}{k}\text{\boldmath$x$})=f(\text{\boldmath$A$})
so that

\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V}\alpha_{v}\log\alpha_{v}+c\log\left(\frac{2\sum_{uv\in E}\alpha_{u}\alpha_{v}}{k^{2}}\right)+\log k-c\log 2.

Then by Corollary
3.8
, for
2c\leq\frac{2(k-1)^{3}}{k(k+1)(k-2)}\log(k-1)=\ell_{k}
,
\phi
, which is continuous on

, is uniquely maximized at
\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}\in K_{1}
. We have

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial a_{u}}=-\log a_{u}+1+c\cdot\frac{\sum_{w\sim u}a_{w}}{\sum_{xy\in E}a_{x}a_{y}}

and therefore

\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial a_{u}\partial a_{v}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\frac{1}{a_{u}}-c\left(\frac{\sum_{w\sim u}a_{w}}{\sum_{xy\in E}a_{x}a_{y}}\right)^{2}&v=u\\
\displaystyle-c\cdot\frac{(\sum_{w\sim u}a_{w})(\sum_{w\sim v}a_{w})}{\left(\sum_{xy\in E}a_{x}a_{y}\right)^{2}}&v\not\sim u\\
\displaystyle c\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{xy\in E}a_{x}a_{y}}-\frac{(\sum_{w\sim u}a_{w})(\sum_{w\sim v}a_{v})}{\left(\sum_{xy\in E}a_{x}a_{y}\right)^{2}}\right)&v\sim u\end{cases}.

Letting
B
denote the adjacency matrix for the graph
T^{\otimes 2}
. This gives

H|_{\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}}=-\left(k^{2}I_{k^{2}}+4cJ_{k^{2}}-\frac{4ck^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}B\right).

(24)

Then for any matrix
U
whose columns form a basis for
\ker(D)
,

\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})=\frac{\det(U^{T}(-H)U)}{\det(U^{T}U)}.

(25)

We give an explicit expression for the matrix
U
and the corresponding determinant of the Hessian over the vector space
\ker(D)
. Letting
e_{i}
denote the
i
-th unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and using the natural correspondence between
\mathbb{R}^{k^{2}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{k}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the following is a basis for
\ker(D)
:

\mathcal{B}=\left\{(e_{1}-e_{i})\otimes(e_{1}-e_{j}):\ 2\leq i,j\leq k\right\}.

Since the set

is linearly independent over
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, then
\mathcal{B}
is a set of
(k-1)^{2}
linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{k}
. With the appropriate indexing, these are naturally elements in
\ker(D)
. Recall that
D
is the incidence matrix of the graph
K_{k,k}
. Label the edges of
K_{k,k}
in a natural way with the elements of the set
[k]^{2}
. Then, the vector
(e_{1}-e_{i})\otimes(e_{1}-e_{j})
corresponds to the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{k^{2}}
whose entries corresponding to the edges
(1,1)
and
(i,j)
are
1
, entries corresponding to the edges
(i,1)
and
(1,j)
are
-1
and all other entries are
0
.

Define the
k\times(k-1)
matrix

V=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\text{\boldmath$1$}_{k-1}^{T}\\
\hline\cr-I_{k-1}\end{array}\right].

Then the matrix
U=V\otimes V
has columns that form a basis for
\ker(D)
. Note that

V^{T}V=\text{\boldmath$1$}_{k-1}\text{\boldmath$1$}_{k-1}^{T}+(-1)^{2}I_{k-1}=J_{k-1}+I_{k-1}

(26)

and since every column of
V
has entries summing to
0
,
J_{k}V=0_{k\times(k-1)}
.

With this in mind, consider the Hessian matrix in Equation (
24
). Recall that
M
is the signed adjacency matrix of the doubly-regular tournament
T_{k}
.

\displaystyle-H

\displaystyle=k^{2}I_{k^{2}}+4cJ_{k^{2}}-\frac{4ck^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}B

\displaystyle=k^{2}I_{k^{2}}+4cJ_{k^{2}}-\frac{4ck^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(M\otimes M+(J_{k}-I_{k})\otimes(J_{k}-I_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{2ck^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}\left(M\otimes M-J_{k}\otimes I_{k}-I_{k}\otimes J_{k}+J_{k}\otimes J_{k}\right)

Thus, considering the matrix expression in the numerator of Equation (

J_{k}V=0_{k\times(k-1)}

\displaystyle U^{T}(-H)U

\displaystyle=(V^{T}\otimes V)(-H)(V\otimes V)

\displaystyle=k^{2}\left(1-\frac{2c}{(k-1)^{2}}\right)(V^{T}V\otimes V^{T}V)-\frac{2ck^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}\left((V^{T}\otimes V^{T})(M\otimes M)(V\otimes V)\right)

\det(U^{T}(-H)U)=\det\left(k^{2}\left(1-\frac{2c}{(k-1)^{2}}\right)(V^{T}V\otimes V^{T}V)\right)\cdot\\
\det\left(I_{k}^{2}-\frac{2c/(k-1)^{2}}{1-2c/(k-1)^{2}}\cdot(M\otimes M)(V\otimes V)(V^{T}V\otimes V^{T}V)^{-1}(V^{T}\otimes V^{T})\right)

Note that

and hence

\displaystyle MV(kI_{k-1}-J_{k-1})V^{T}

\hline\cr$-1_{k-1}$&$I_{k-1}$\end{tabular}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$(k-1)^{2}$&$-(k-1)1_{k-1}^{T}$\\

\displaystyle\det(U^{T}(-H)U)

\displaystyle\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{k^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}\right)^{(k-1)^{2}}\cdot\left(((k-1)^{2}-2c)^{2}-4k^{2}c^{2}\right)^{(k-1)^{2}/2}.

Note that for

,

Let

. Then

is continuous on

and
\psi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})=k^{k^{2}}>0
. Set

and

,

so

and

Furthermore, for
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in K_{1}
,

p(n,\text{\boldmath$x$})=b_{n}(\psi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(1)).

Therefore Theorem

gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}Y^{2}=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\psi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}{\tau(\Gamma)^{1/2}\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})^{1/2}}(2\pi n)^{r/2}c_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{k^{2}}(2\pi n)^{(k-1)^{2}/2}(2\pi n)^{-(k^{2}-1)/2}}{k^{(k-1)}(\frac{k^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}})^{(k-1)^{2}/2}(((k-1)^{2}-2c)^{2}-4c^{2}k^{2})^{(k-1)^{2}/4}}e^{2n\log(k(\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{k}))^{c})}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(k-1)^{4}}{((k-1)^{2}-2c)^{2}-4c^{2}k^{2}}\right)^{(k-1)^{2}/4}k^{k}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)}\left(k\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{c}\right)^{2n}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{(k-1)^{4}}{((k-1)^{2}-2c)^{2}-4c^{2}k^{2}}\right)^{(k-1)^{2}/4}(\mathbf{E}Y)^{2}

∎

Proposition 5.4
.

For
k>3
an integer such that there exists a doubly regular tournament of order
k
and
0<c<\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k}
, with positive probability
{\chi_{o}}(\vec{\mathcal{M}}(n,m=cn))\leq k
.

Proof.

By the Paley-Zygmund inequality and Lemmas
5.1
and
5.3

\mathbf{Pr}[Y>0]\geq\frac{(\mathbf{E}Y)^{2}}{\mathbf{E}(Y^{2})}\sim\left(\frac{((k-1)^{2}-2c)^{2}-4c^{2}k^{2}}{(k-1)^{4}}\right)^{(k-1)^{2}/4}>0.

∎

.
In many investigations into the chromatic number of random graphs, however, this

As we will see in Section

5.2
\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)

In this section we use a second moment argument to get an upper bound on

We start with the first moment by adapting the following result of Kemkes et al.

Proposition 5.5

(
[
30
, Proposition 2(a)]
)
.

Fix integers
d,k\geq 3
. Let

be the number of equitable
k
-colourings of a random
d
-regular multigraph
\mathcal{C}(n,d)
(where
n
is restricted to the set of multiples of

\mathbf{E}Z\sim k^{k/2}\left(\frac{k-1}{2\pi(k-2)}\right)^{(k-1)/2}n^{-(k-1)/2}k^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{dn/2}.

Corollary 5.6
.

Fix integers
d,k\geq 3
and a tournament
T_{k}
on
k
vertices (not necessarily doubly regular). Then

\mathbf{E}Y\sim k^{k/2}\left(\frac{k-1}{2\pi(k-2)}\right)^{(k-1)/2}n^{-(k-1)/2}k^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{dn/2}.

Proof.

Each proper (unoriented)

-colouring of
\mathcal{C}(n,d)
has exactly one orientation that adheres to
T_{k}
, so there are the same number of proper
k
-oriented-colourings of
\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)
that are coloured by
T_{k}
as there are unoriented colourings. However, each
C\in\mathcal{C}(n,d)
has
2^{dn/2}
orientations. Thus we have

\displaystyle|\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)|\mathbf{E}Y

\displaystyle=|\{(\vec{C},\chi_{o}):\vec{C}\sim\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)\text{ and $\chi_{o}$ properly $T_{k}$-colours $\vec{C}$}\}|

\displaystyle=|\{(C,\chi):C\sim\mathcal{C}(n,d)\text{ and $\chi$ properly $k$-colours $C$}\}|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{C}(n,d)|\mathbf{E}Z

so

\mathbf{E}Y=\frac{|\mathcal{C}(n,d)|}{|\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)|}\mathbf{E}Z=\frac{1}{2^{dn/2}}\mathbf{E}Z.

∎

In order to prove Proposition

, we also need an estimate for the second moment

we give a counting argument similar to that in Section

5.2.1
Counting Argument

To calculate
\mathbf{E}(Y^{2})
, we count ordered triples
(\vec{C},h_{1},h_{2})
such that
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are balanced proper
T_{k}
-colourings of
\vec{C}\in\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)
. Let
A=(a_{ij})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\\
1\leq j\leq k\end{subarray}}
be the overlap matrix of
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
; that is,
a_{ij}
is the proportion of vertices
v
such that
h_{1}(v)=i
and
h_{2}(v)=j
. Note that
a_{ij}\geq 0
for each
i,j\in[k]
and, as
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are balanced,

\forall i\in[k]\quad\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}=\frac{1}{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall j\in[k]\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{ij}=\frac{1}{k}.

(28)

We introduce an (undirected) auxiliary graph
\Gamma
. The vertex set of
\Gamma
is
[k]^{2}
, so there is
v\in V(\Gamma)
corresponding to each
a_{ij}
, and we may, when convenient, refer to
a_{ij}
as
a_{v}
. The edge set of
\Gamma
is defined by

E(\Gamma)=\bigl\{\{u,v\}\mid u=(i,j),v=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),\ \text{and}\ i\to i^{\prime},j\to j^{\prime}\in A(T_{k})\bigr\}.

Note that if we partition edges in
\vec{C}
by the pairs of colours their vertices receive in
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, the set of equivalence classes is exactly the edges of
\Gamma
: in order for an edge to be directed from a vertex receiving colour
i
in
h_{1}
and colour
j
in
h_{2}
towards a vertex receiving colour
i^{\prime}
in
h_{1}
and
j^{\prime}
in
h_{2}
, we require that
i\to i^{\prime}
and
j\to j^{\prime}
in
T_{k}
. Note also that as
T_{k}
is doubly regular,
\Gamma
is regular of degree
\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{2}
.

Then let
B=(b_{uv})_{u=(i,j),v=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}
record the proportion of edges between a vertex
u
such that
h_{1}(u)=i
and
h_{2}(u)=j
and a vertex
v
such that
h_{1}(v)=i^{\prime}
and
h_{2}(v)=j^{\prime}
. Then for any tournament
T_{k}
,
B
satisfies

\displaystyle\forall u,v

\displaystyle\quad b_{uv}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall u,v

b_{uv}=0
unless
i\to i^{\prime}
and
j\to j^{\prime}
(or
i^{\prime}\to i
and
j^{\prime}\to j
) in
T_{k}

\displaystyle\forall u,v

\displaystyle\quad b_{uv}=b_{vu}

\displaystyle\forall v=(i,j)

\displaystyle\quad\sum\limits_{u\sim v}b_{uv}=a_{ij}.

(29)

To choose a
\vec{C}\in\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)
that respects
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, first select vertices to receive each pair of colours, which we do in one of

\frac{n!}{\prod_{i,j}(na_{ij})!}

ways. Then, for each pair of colour classes, select the vertices that will be connected by an edge, which we do in

\prod_{i,j}\frac{(dna_{ij})!}{\prod_{u\sim v=(i,j)}(dnb_{uv})!}

many ways. Finally, match the chosen vertices of each edge type in one of

\prod_{uv\in E}(dnb_{uv})!

ways. Noting that

\frac{\prod_{uv\in E}(dnb_{uv})!}{\prod_{v\in V}\prod_{u\sim v}(dnb_{uv})!}=\frac{1}{\prod_{uv\in E}(dnb_{uv})!},

repeating this process for each choice of
A
and
B
that satisfy (
28
) and (
5.2.1
), and dividing by
|\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)|
gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(Y^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{dn/2}(dn-1)!!}\sum_{A\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:Acons}}\frac{n!}{\prod_{ij}(na_{ij})!}\sum_{B\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:Bcons}}\prod_{i,j}\frac{(dna_{ij})!}{\prod_{u\sim v=(i,j)}(dnb_{uv})!}\prod_{uv\in E}(dnb_{uv})!

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Acons},\ B\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Bcons}}\frac{n!}{\prod_{ij}(na_{ij})!}\cdot\frac{\prod_{ij}(dna_{ij})!}{(dn)!}\cdot\frac{(\frac{dn}{2})!}{\prod_{uv\in E}(dnb_{uv})!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Acons},\ B\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Bcons}}\frac{\xi(n)(\frac{n}{e})^{n}}{\prod_{ij}\xi(na_{ij})(\frac{na_{ij}}{e})^{na_{ij}}}\cdot\frac{\prod_{ij}\xi(dna_{ij})(\frac{dna_{ij}}{e})^{dna_{ij}}}{\xi(dn)(\frac{dn}{e})^{dn}}\cdot\frac{\xi(\frac{dn}{2})(\frac{dn}{2e})^{\frac{dn}{2}}}{\prod_{uv\in E}\xi(dnb_{uv})(\frac{dnb_{uv}}{e})^{dnb_{uv}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Acons},\ B\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Bcons}}p(A,B,n)e^{nf(A,B)}

(30)

where

p(A,B,n)=\frac{\xi(n)\xi(\frac{dn}{2})\prod\limits_{v\in V}\xi(dna_{v})}{\xi(dn)\prod\limits_{v\in V}\xi(na_{v})\prod\limits_{uv\in E}\xi(dnb_{uv})}

(31)

and

f(A,B)=(d-1)\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}-d\sum_{uv\in E}b_{uv}\log(2b_{uv}).

(32)

5.2.2
Optimization

In order to estimate
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
, we will use a Laplace summation technique. As we are summing over a polynomial number of exponential terms, all but
o(1)
of the weight of the sum comes from terms with maximum exponential contribution. In this section we use Proposition
1.4
to determine that maximum value.

Lemma 5.7
.

Fix an integer
k
such that there exists a doubly regular tournament
T_{k}
of
order
k
and let
d<\ell_{k}
. Let
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{k}
be a
k\times k

real positive matrix satisfying Equation (
28
) and let
B=(b_{uv})_{u=(i,j),v=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}
be a
k^{2}\times k^{2}
real positive matrix satisfying
Equation (
5.2.1
). Define
f(A,B)
as in Equation (
32
). Then
f(A,B)
is uniquely
maximized at
f(\hat{A},\hat{B})=\log k^{2}+d\log(\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{k}))

where
\hat{A}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}J_{k,k}
and
\hat{B}=(\hat{b}_{uv})_{u=(i,j),v=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}
where

\hat{b}_{uv}=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}&u\sim v\\
0&u\not\sim v\end{cases}.

Proof.

We start by finding a more convenient expression of
f
. Note that using Equation (
5.2.1
),

\displaystyle(d-1)\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}-d\sum_{uv\in V}

\displaystyle b_{uv}\log(2b_{uv})=-\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}+d\left(\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}+\sum_{uv\in E}b_{uv}\log\left(\frac{1}{2b_{uv}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}+d\left(\sum_{v\in V}\left(\sum_{u\sim v}b_{uv}\right)\log a_{v}+\sum_{uv\in E}b_{uv}\log\left(\frac{1}{2b_{uv}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}+d\sum_{uv\in E}b_{uv}\left(\log a_{u}+\log a_{v}+\log\left(\frac{1}{2b_{uv}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}+d\sum_{uv\in E}b_{uv}\log\left(\frac{a_{u}a_{v}}{2b_{uv}}\right)

(33)

Using an idea from
[
40
]
, we now maximize
f
maintaining the constraints from Equation (
28
) but relaxing those in Equation (
5.2.1
) to
b_{uv}\geq 0
and

\sum_{u,v\in V}b_{uv}=1.

(34)

In view of this relaxation, we can regard
B
as an arbitrary probability distribution. We define another probability distribution given by

b^{*}_{uv}=\begin{cases}\frac{a_{u}a_{v}}{2\sum_{xy\in E}a_{x}a_{y}}&u\sim v,\\
0&u\not\sim v\end{cases},

and we write
B^{*}=(b^{*}_{uv})_{u,v\in V}
. Then

\sum_{uv\in E}b_{uv}\log\left(\frac{a_{u}a_{v}}{2b_{uv}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\sum_{xy\in E}a_{x}a_{y}\right)-D_{KL}(B\|B^{*}),

where
D_{KL}(B\|B^{*})=\sum_{uv}b_{uv}\log\left(\frac{b_{uv}}{b^{*}_{uv}}\right)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence from
B
to
B^{*}
. By Gibb’s inequality,
D_{KL}(B\|B^{*})\geq 0
with equality if and only if
B=B^{*}
. As a result,

\max_{B\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:Brelaxedcons}}}f(A,B)=-\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}\log a_{v}+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\sum_{xy\in E}a_{x}a_{y}\right)=:\tilde{f}_{d}(A),

(35)

with one unique maximizer at
B=B^{*}
. Note that if
A=\hat{A}
then
B^{*}=\hat{B}
.

Next we seek to apply Corollary
3.8
, but our matrix
A
is not currently doubly stochastic. We reparameterize, setting
\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}=kA
. Then

\tilde{f}_{d}(A)=\tilde{f}_{d}(\tfrac{1}{k}\text{\boldmath$\alpha$})=\log k-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V}\alpha_{v}\log\alpha_{v}+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{2\sum_{xy\in E}\alpha_{x}\alpha_{y}}{k^{2}}\right)-\frac{d}{2}\log 2.

Then as
\log k-\frac{d}{2}\log 2
is constant with respect to
\alpha
, Corollary
3.8
gives

\tilde{f}_{d}(A)\leq\log k+\log k+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{2|E|}{k^{4}}\right)-\frac{d}{2}\log 2=\log k^{2}+d\log\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)

(36)

whenever
d\leq\frac{2(k-1)^{3}}{k(k+1)(k-2)}\log(k-1)
. Now, using Equations (
35
) and (
36
), we have

\displaystyle f(A,B)\leq\max_{A\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Acons},\ B\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Bcons}}f(A,B)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{A\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Acons},\ B\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Brelaxedcons}}f(A,B)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{A\text{ s.t.}\eqref{eq:Acons}}\tilde{f}_{d}(A)=\log k^{2}+d\log\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)=f(\hat{A},\hat{B})

∎

Lemma 5.11

We start with a result regarding the oriented chromatic number of sparse digraphs.

Now we argue that a.a.s. subgraphs of

,

\mathbf{Pr}[X_{n}\leq\mathbf{E}[X_{n}]-t]\leq\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{dnb^{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{Pr}[X_{n}\geq\mathbf{E}[X_{n}]+t]\leq\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{dnb^{2}}\right).

As long as the value

does not depend on the orientations chosen for the new edges, we can extend Wormald’s result to directed configurations by “forgetting” the orientations, applying the result, and then reorienting the edges, including assigning new orientations to the new edges.

Our next step is to prove that as soon as there is some small probability that

-colour all but a small number of vertices.

Lemma 6.4

(
[
2

)

For a given function
\omega(n)
, let

be the smallest

With probability greater than

vertices of
\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)

Proof.

For
\vec{C}\sim\vec{\mathcal{C}}(n,d)
, let

be the minimal size of a set of vertices

\vec{C}-S
is
j

and

, switching two arcs of

can affect

by at most 4 as, in the worst case, we can add all four vertices to

and still
j
-oriented-colour
\vec{C}-S
. By Theorem

, with

\mathbf{Pr}[Y_{j}\leq\mu_{j}-\lambda\sqrt{n}]<e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{16d}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{Pr}[Y_{j}\geq\mu_{j}+\lambda\sqrt{n}]<e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{16d}}.

Choose

If

, then the event

∎

Lemma 7.1

8.1
Very sparse or very dense graphs

Throughout the majority of this paper, we have focused on the case of oriented graphs with edge density

What makes these cases more tractable for analysis is that there are, up to isomorphism, only two different tournaments on
3
vertices (
C_{3}

Proof of Proposition

Then
\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast}

Recall from Equation (

if and only if

8.4
Colouring with tournaments that are not doubly-regular

The exponential gap between the bounds in Theorem
1.2
comes from the different constants in
u_{k}
and
\ell_{k}
;
u_{k}
behaves like
\frac{2}{\log 2}\log k
while
\ell_{k}
grows as
2\log k
. While the argument for the lower bound is straightforward, one might reasonably ask if a better constant for
\ell_{k}
could be found by counting equitable colourings of a different tournament, hoping to improve Corollary
3.8
. In the calculations of the second moment for the number of equitable oriented colourings in Lemma
5.3
and Section
5.2.1
, the initial computation of the second moment would apply equally well to colouring by
any
tournament on
k
vertices, not just a doubly-regular tournament. Similarly, the function,
\gamma_{d}
, defined in Proposition
1.4
can be defined with the Lagrangian part given for any graph
G
. In the case where a tournament
T
is not regular, that is, not all vertices have the same in-degree or out-degree, the product graph
G=T^{\otimes 2}
will not be regular.

If
G
is not regular, then subject to the condition that the matrix
A
be doubly-stochastic, the point at
\text{\boldmath$A$}=\frac{1}{k}J_{k}
is not a critical point for the function
-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V(G)}a_{v}\log a_{v}+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{2\sum_{uv\in E}a_{u}a_{v}}{k^{2}}\right)
and so cannot be the location of the maximum value. This would mean that the maximum value is strictly greater and so the exponential part of the second moment of the number of equitable
T
-colourings has a larger base that the square of the expected value and a standard second moment method argument would not yield a positive probability of finding an equitable
T
-colouring.

Among the regular tournaments, one might seek a tournament whose Kronecker square has a smaller second-largest eigenvalue to improve the bound on
d
obtained from Proposition
1.4
. However, one can show that the signed adjacency matrix of any regular tournament of order
k
has an eigenvalue
\lambda
with
|\lambda|\geq\sqrt{k}
and use this fact to prove that the second-largest eigenvalue has size at least
\frac{1}{2}(1+\lambda^{2})\geq\frac{k+1}{2}
, precisely the value of the second-largest eigenvalue of the Kronecker square of a doubly regular tournament. The fact that the signed adjacency matrix of a regular tournament of order
k
has an eigenvalue of modulus at least
\sqrt{k}
follows from a result by Brauer and Gentry
[
11
]
on the unsigned adjacency matrices of regular tournaments, but one can also prove it directly by showing that if
M
is the signed adjacency matrix of a regular tournament of order
k
and
\mathbf{v}
is any column of
M
, then
||M\mathbf{v}||_{2}/||\mathbf{v}||_{2}\geq\sqrt{k}
. From this, one can show that the largest eigenvalue of
T^{\otimes 2}
is exactly
\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{2}
and the second-largest eigenvalue is at least
\frac{k+1}{2}
. Thus, for the approach of finding oriented colourings using the second-moment method, doubly-regular tournaments will give the best results.
