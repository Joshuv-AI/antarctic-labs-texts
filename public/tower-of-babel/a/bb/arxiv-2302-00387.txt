Cutting multi-control quantum gates with ZX calculus
Christian Ufrecht, Maniraman Periyasamy, Sebastian Rietsch, Daniel D. Scherer, Axel Plinge, Christopher Mutschler

Abstract
Circuit cutting, the decomposition of a quantum circuit into independent partitions, has become a promising avenue towards experiments with larger quantum circuits in the noisy-intermediate scale quantum (NISQ) era. While previous work focused on cutting qubit wires or two-qubit gates, in this work we introduce a method for cutting multi-controlled Z gates. We construct a decomposition and prove the upper bound 𝒪 ⁡ ( 6 2 ​ K ) \mathcal{O}(6^{2K}) on the associated sampling overhead, where K K is the number of cuts in the circuit. This bound is independent of the number of control qubits but can be further reduced to 𝒪 ⁡ ( 4.5 2 ​ K ) \mathcal{O}(4.5^{2K}) for the special case of CCZ gates. Furthermore, we evaluate our proposal on IBM hardware and experimentally show noise resilience due to the strong reduction of CNOT gates in the cut circuits.

The output of a single experimental shot on a quantum computer is typically a bitstring. The expectation value of an observable is then obtained by a classical post-processing function on the bitstrings of multiple runs. Modeling the outcome of each experimental run as i.i.d. random variables, the number of required samples to achieve a given additive error is determined via the variance of the post-processing function.
When a circuit is cut, the variance of the modified estimator for
\langle O\rangle

Consequently, more experimental runs are required to estimate the result of the original circuit to the same given additive error. More specifically, this sampling overhead is exponential (in the number of cuts).

(6)

\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{2})

3
ZX-calculus for circuit cutting

ZX calculus
[
33
,
34
]
is a tensor-network representation for quantum circuits that together with powerful transformation rules allows diagrammatic reasoning. Since ZX-calculus has been reviewed elsewhere
[
35
]
, we will only introduce those diagram types and transformation rules necessary for this article.
The basic diagrams are Z-spiders, defined as

\displaystyle{\scriptstyle m}\;\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[width]{figures/Z-spider.pdf}}}\;{\scriptstyle n}\;\;

\displaystyle=\underbrace{|0...0\rangle}_{\scriptstyle n}\underbrace{\langle 0...0|}_{\scriptstyle m}+\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha}|1...1\rangle\langle 1...1|

(7)

and X-spiders

\displaystyle{\scriptstyle m}\;\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[width]{figures/X-spider.pdf}}}\;{\scriptstyle n}\;\;

\displaystyle=\underbrace{|{\scriptstyle+...+}\rangle}_{\scriptstyle n}\underbrace{\langle{\scriptstyle+...+}|}_{\scriptstyle m}+\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha}|{\scriptstyle-...-}\rangle\langle{\scriptstyle-...-}|

(8)

where
|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
. For
\alpha=0
, the inset is commonly disregarded. A third tensor, a so-called H-box, is defined as

\displaystyle{\scriptstyle m}\;\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[width]{figures/H-Box.pdf}}}\;{\scriptstyle n}\;\;

\displaystyle=\sum(-1)^{i_{1}\cdot...\cdot i_{m}\cdot j_{1}\cdot...\cdot j_{n}}|j_{1}...j_{n}\rangle\langle i_{1}...i_{m}|

(9)

where the sum runs over all
i_{1},...,i_{m},j_{1},...,j_{n}\in\{0,1\}

Spiders and H-boxes are therefore maps from
m

n
-qubit states, signified by the number of wires ending at the right and the left of the diagrams.
When representing ZX diagrams as matrices, we will implicitly assume the computational basis. Then, for example, an H-box is a matrix filled with ones but a minus one in the lower right corner. H-boxes can be viewed as generalized Hadamard gates since

\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[width]{figures/H-Box-Hadamard_1.pdf}}}=\sqrt{2}\,\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[width]{figures/H-Box-Hadamard_2.pdf}}}\,.

(10)

As apparent from the definitions, spiders and H-boxes correspond to symmetric tensors in all indices. Any quantum circuit can be represented as a ZX diagram, the reverse statement, however, is incorrect since a ZX diagram not necessarily corresponds to a unitary matrix. To obtain the Hermitian conjugate of a ZX diagram, we move all wires ending at the left to the right and those ending at the right to the left and replace all angles with their negative values. Experiments on IBM Q

6
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed an approach for cutting multi-controlled Z gates by means of ZX-calculus based on the H-box fusion rule.
We derived the upper bound
\kappa=6
on the sampling overhead that is independent of the order of the gate. We validated the results on IBM hardware and found strong noise reduction due to the reduced amount of CNOT gates in the cut circuit. We anticipate the generalization of our method to multi-controlled rotation gates and extension to multi-qubit rotations.
The optimality of the decompositions constructed in this work at present remains an open question.

Appendix B
Proof of H-box fusion rule

We proof
Equation

12
from the right to the left. We first define the joint indices
i=(i_{1},...i_{m})
and
j=(j_{1},...,j_{n})
as well as

\pi_{i}=\Pi_{k=1}^{m}i_{k}
and
\pi_{j}=\Pi_{k=1}^{n}j_{k}
.
We then find in Dirac notation with the indices

and

running over
\{0,1\}
,

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,a,b}(-1)^{\pi_{i}a}|i\rangle\langle a|\otimes(-1)^{\pi_{j}b}|j\rangle\langle b|\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}(-1)^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}|a^{\prime}b^{\prime}\rangle

(37)

which is equivalent to the left-hand side of

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b}(-1)^{ab+\pi_{i}a+\pi_{j}b}

\displaystyle\quad\quad=(-1)^{-\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}\sum_{a,b}(-1)^{(a+\pi_{j})(b+\pi_{i})}

\displaystyle\quad\quad=2(-1)^{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}\,.

(40)

Alternatively, the H-box-fusion rule can be derived by the Schmidt decomposition of the vector

representing the diagonal elements of the unitary corresponding to the MCZ gate.

Appendix E
Derivation of sample-complexity overhead

This appendix investigates in more detail the sampling overhead associated with evaluating a cut circuit, closely following Refs.
[
24
,
7
]
. In
Section

E.1
we prove the statements on sampling overhead made in the main text for the case where in each experimental run a circuit from the decomposition is sampled from a probability distribution. Subsequently, in
Section

E.2
we bound the variance by pre-estimating the expectation values of the partitioned circuits.

E.1
Sample complexity for circuit sampling

Consider an
n
-qubit quantum circuit. Assume the initial state
|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
and consider the post-processing function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow[-1,1]
on the measured bitstring
s
. Furthermore, assume that
f(s)
can be efficiently calculated classically. These definitions describe the computational model of Refs.
[
42
,
7
]
.
The post-processing function gives rise to an observable
O
via

O=\sum_{s}f(s)P_{s}\,

(49)

where
P_{s}
is the projector on the computational basis state corresponding to bitstring
s
. Conversely, for instance, if the observable is a Pauli string, it can be written in the above form by local diagonalization and viewing the unitary diagonalization matrix
as part of the circuit.
The goal of the quantum computation is to approximate
\langle O\rangle
to additive error
\epsilon
with high probability for which we will define statistical estimators in the following.

Cutting a gate amounts to replacing its superoperator by a decomposition as in
Equation

1
.
We first consider the case where all
\mathcal{F}_{i}
in the decomposition correspond to unitary gates.
With this substitution, we find

\langle O\rangle=\sum_{i}a_{i}\langle O\rangle_{i}=\sum_{i,s}p(s|i)a_{i}f(s)

(50)

where
\langle.\rangle_{i}
denotes the expectation value with respect to the state evolved by circuit
i
, containing
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Moreover,

p(s|i)
is the probability to measure bitstring
s
given circuit
i
.
To evaluate the result on a quantum device, we sample
i
at each experimental run according to the probability distribution

p(i)=|a_{i}|/\kappa\,.

(51)

We therefore define a random variable
I
that takes values
i
with probability
p(i)
and the estimator
[
25
]

\hat{f}=\kappa\,\mathrm{sign}(a_{I})f(S_{I})

(52)

where the random variable
S_{I=i}
models the bitstring outcomes of the circuit
i
.
This estimator is unbiased since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,s}p(s,i)\kappa\,\mathrm{sign}(a_{i})f(s)

(53)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,s}\kappa\,\mathrm{sign}(a_{i})p(i)p(s|i)f(s)

(54)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,s}a_{i}p(s|i)f(s)

(55)

\displaystyle=\langle O\rangle\,.

(56)

Here, we substituted
Equation

51
into
Equation

54
. The final result is obtained by comparing
Equation

55
to
Equation

50
.
The estimator for
N
shots is given by the sample mean of
Equation

52
. Thus, to estimate
\langle O\rangle
to additive error
\epsilon
with probability
1-\delta
, Hoeffding’s inequality provides the required number of experimental repetitions as

N\geq 2\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\mathrm{ln}\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

(57)

where we used that
|\hat{f}|\leq\kappa
. The number of samples needed for the original circuit is obtained for
\kappa=1
in
Equation

57
from which we infer the sampling overhead
\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{2})
.

It is straightforward to generalize this derivation to
K
cuts. In this case, the expectation value of the observable is obtained as

\langle O\rangle=\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{K}}\sum_{s}p(s|i_{1},...,i_{K})a_{i_{1}}\cdot...\cdot a_{i_{K}}f(s)

(58)

and the estimator for
K
cuts becomes

\hat{f}=\kappa^{K}\mathrm{sign}(a_{I_{1}})\cdot...\cdot\mathrm{sign}(a_{I_{K}})f(S_{I_{1},...,I_{K}})

(59)

where we defined the independent random variables
I_{1}
,…,
I_{K}
that determine the specific circuit to run. To show equality between the expectation value of
Equation

59
and
Equation

58
, we follow
Equation

53
to
Equation

56
and make use of the independence of
I_{1}
,…,
I_{K}
. Since
|\hat{f}|\leq\kappa^{K}
, Hoeffding’s inequality applied to the sample mean of
Equation

59
, provides the bound
\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{2K})
on the sampling overhead.
If the observable factorizes over the partitions
A
and
B
of the original circuit, the post-processing function factorizes as well, that is
f(s)=f^{A}(s^{A})f^{B}(s^{B})
where
s^{A}
and
s^{B}
are the bitstring results on partition
A
and
B
. If the initial state factorizes and all gates connecting the two partitions are cut, the partitioned circuits can be evaluated on independent quantum computers or sequentially on the same device.

We now turn to a quantum circuit that contains projectors.
Consider the map
\mathcal{M}
consisting of a complete set of projectors
P_{l}
and
\xi_{l}\in[-1,1]
with

\mathcal{M}(\rho)=\sum_{l}\xi_{l}P_{l}\rho P_{l}

(60)

for state
\rho
. This map is neither positive since
\xi_{l}
can be smaller than zero, nor trace preserving since the state in
Equation

60
is not normalized after the projection and multiplied by
\xi_{l}
. Note that both
\mathcal{P}_{1...1}
and
\mathcal{P}
of
Equation

24
are instances of
\mathcal{M}
. For
\mathcal{P}_{1...1}
we set
\xi_{l}=1
only for
l=(1,...,1)
and zero otherwise. On the other hand for
\mathcal{P}
we have
\xi_{l}=-1
for
l=(1,...,1)
and
\xi_{l}=1
otherwise.
Even though
\mathcal{M}
does not correspond to a physical time evolution, we can nevertheless estimate

\chi=\mathrm{tr}(O\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{M}(\rho))

(61)

by repeated use of a quantum computer. In
Equation

61
, the density matrix

\rho
is the state right before the projectors and
\mathcal{U}
is the unitary channel corresponding to the unitary evolution
U
afterwards until the final measurement.
To estimate
\chi
on a quantum computer, we perform intermediate measurements, and sample according to the estimator

\hat{f}=\xi_{L}f(S)

(62)

where the random variable
S
describes the bitstring outputs of the final measurements as before and the random variable
L
models the outcomes of the intermediate measurements.
According to
Equation

62

we have to add
-f(S)
in the sample mean for
\hat{f}
if we found the all-one state in the intermediate measurement and
f(S)
otherwise.
This estimator is unbiased since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,l}p(s,l)\xi_{l}f(s)

(63)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,l}\xi_{l}\,\mathrm{tr}(P_{s}UP_{l}\rho P_{l}U^{\dagger})f(s)

(64)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\mathrm{tr}(P_{s}\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{M}(\rho))f(s)

(65)

\displaystyle=\chi\,.

(66)

The first line,
Equation

63
,
is the definition of the expectation value and in
Equation

64
we substituted
p(s,l)=\mathrm{tr}(P_{s}UP_{l}\rho P_{l}U^{\dagger})

[
1
]
. In the final step, we identified
O=\sum_{s}f(s)P_{s}
. The number of samples required to determine
\chi
to given additive error is again determined by Hoeffding’s inequality applied to the sample mean of
Equation

62
.
Since
|\xi_{l}|\leq 1
by definition,
Equation

57
remains unchanged when some of the circuits contain projectors.

E.2
Sampling complexity by pre-estimating circuits

In
Section

5
, we show experimental results for a circuit that disintegrates into two independent partitions
A
and
B
. After cutting the single gate that connects the two partitions, we have to evaluate

\langle O\rangle=\sum_{i}a_{i}\langle O^{A}\rangle_{i}\langle O^{B}\rangle_{i}\,.

(67)

Rather than sampling a circuit pair
i
for each experimental run as discussed in
Section

E.1
we pre-estimate the expectation values
\langle O^{A}\rangle_{i}
and
\langle O^{B}\rangle_{i}
for all the circuits first and subsequently restore the result of the original circuit with the help of
Equation

67
. In the following, it is shown that the standard deviation of
Equation

67
can be bounded by
\epsilon
for a total number of experimental runs

N\geq 4\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}

(68)

where we allocate

N_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{|a_{i}|}{\kappa}N

(69)

samples to each circuit of circuit pair
i
to determine the expectation values
\langle O^{A}\rangle_{i}
and
\langle O^{B}\rangle_{i}
. Note that
2\sum_{i}N_{i}=N
.
Next, we define the estimator

\hat{f}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\hat{f}_{i}^{A}\hat{f}_{i}^{B}

(70)

where
\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}_{i}^{A})=\langle O^{A}\rangle
and
\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}_{i}^{B})=\langle O^{B}\rangle
. Since
\hat{f}_{i}^{A}
and
\hat{f}_{i}^{B}
are independent, this estimator is unbiased. The variance of
\hat{f}
is calculated as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{i}^{A}\hat{f}_{i}^{B})

(71)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{i}^{A})\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f}_{i}^{B})^{2}]+\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{i}^{B})[\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}_{i}^{A})]^{2}

(72)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{i}^{A})+\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{i}^{B})

(73)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\frac{2}{N_{i}}

(74)

In
Equation

71
we first factorized the variance, valid for independent random variables.
Equation

73
makes use of the bounds
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f}_{i}^{B})^{2}]\leq 1
and
|\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}_{i}^{A})|\leq 1
since
|f_{i}^{A}|\leq 1
and
|f_{i}^{B}|\leq 1
. Finally,
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{i})\leq 1/N_{i}
for
N_{i}
independent samples.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{i}^{A}\hat{f}_{i}^{B})

(75)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}/N_{i}

(76)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{2}

(77)

using
Equation

70
,
Equation

74
and
Equation

69
.
