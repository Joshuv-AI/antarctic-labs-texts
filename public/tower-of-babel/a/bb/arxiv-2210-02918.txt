1.
Introduction

The study of eigenvalue problems in annular domains is, actually, a topic widely studied. In this kind of problems, usually, the shape of the hole is spherical and different conditions are imposed on the two components of the boundary. More precisely this problems are defined in annular domains having the following form
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
. Here
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, is an open, bounded, connected set with Lipschitz boundary and
B_{r}
is the ball of radius
r>0
centered at the origin such that
\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{0}
. Recently, the following Steklov-Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalue problem has attracted the attention of many mathematicians ( see for instance
[
20
,
21
,
8
,
16
,
11
,
18
,
9
]
and the references therein)

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\sigma u&\mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{0}\\
u=0&\mbox{on}\ \partial B_{r},\end{cases}

where
\nu
is the outer unit normal to
\partial\Omega
. The first eigenvalue to the problem (
1.1
) has the following variational characterization

(1.2)

\sigma_{D}(\Omega)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in H^{1}_{\partial B_{r}}(\Omega)\\
v\not\equiv 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v\right|^{2}\,dx}{\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}},

where
H^{1}_{\partial B_{r}}(\Omega)
is the set of those Sobolev functions vanishing on the boundary of the hole.
Since both Steklov and Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed, it is interesting to understand what properties
\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
inherits from them.
In
[
16
,
3
,
11
]
, the authors prove that the infimum is actually a minimum and that it is achieved by a function
u\in H^{1}_{\partial B_{r}}(\Omega)
which is a weak solution to problem (
1.1
) with constant sign and that

\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
is simple. This means that, even if a Steklov condition appears in (
1.1
),
\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
has the same properties of the first Dirichlet eigenvalue. Nevertheless, in
[
16
,
21
,
11
,
6
,
18
,
9
]
several shape optimization problems related to
\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
are studied. In this case it is possible to observe that the Steklov condition influences the behavior of
\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
. Indeed, one of the main property of
\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
for instance is that it is bounded from above when both the measure of
\Omega_{0}
and the radius of the hole are fixed (see for instance
[
16
,
8
]
), unlike the first Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalue.

Our aim is to study the eigenvalue problem obtained replacing the Dirichlet boundary condition in (
1.1
) with a Robin one. More precisely we deal with the following Steklov-Robin eigenvalue problem

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\sigma u&\mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{0}\\
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+\beta(x)u=0&\mbox{on}\ \partial B_{r},\end{cases}

where
\beta(x)\in L^{\infty}(\partial B_{r})
is a positive function such that
\|\beta\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{r})}=m>0
.

Definition 1.1
.

A real number
\sigma(\Omega)
and a function
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
are, respectively, called eigenvalue of (
1.3
) and associated eigenfunction to
\sigma(\Omega)
, if and only if

\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\;dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u\varphi\,d\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\sigma(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u\varphi\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

for every
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

The aim of this paper is to study the first eigenvalue
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
of (
1.3
) defined as (see Section 3 for the details)

(1.4)

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)=\inf_{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}\;dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}v^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}.

We prove that similarly to problem (
1.1
),
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
is actually a minimum, it is simple, and that the corresponding eigenfunctions have constant sign. Hence, also in this case, despite the Steklov condition,
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
is formally a Robin type eigenvalue.

When
\Omega
is a spherical shell, that is
\Omega=B_{R}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, and
\beta(x)=\beta
is a positive constant,
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and the corresponding eigenfunctions can be explicitly computed; in particular, in the radial case, it is straightforward to prove the asymptotic behaviors of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and the corresponding eigenfunctions when
\beta\to 0
and
\beta\to\infty
(see Section 4 for the details).

We observe that
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
depends clearly also on
\beta
and consequently on its
L^{1}
-norm
m
. When
\Omega
is a general open, bounded set with Liptschitz boundary, we expect that the same asymptotic behavior of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and the corresponding eigenfunctions still holds, when we let vary the
L^{1}-
norm
m
of
\beta(x)
.
In order to show this, we will prove some estimates on
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
in the spirit of the ones contained in
[
12
]
for the first Robin Laplacian eigenvalue (see also
[
2
]
for a more general case). More precisely, let us define the following quantities

(1.5)

\mu_{1}(\Omega):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}\\
\int_{\partial B_{r}}v\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0\end{subarray}}\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}\,dx}{\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}},

and if
u
is the eigenfunction corresponding to
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)

(1.6)

q_{\beta}(\Omega)=\inf_{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta w=0,\,w\neq 0\\
\frac{\partial w}{\partial\nu}=0\,\textit{on}\,\,\partial\Omega_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)w^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}w^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}},

We observe that
\mu_{1}(\Omega)
is the first nontrivial Steklov Laplacian eigenvalue in
\Omega
. Moreover if
\beta(x)=\beta
is constant, then

(1.7)

q_{\beta}(\Omega)=\beta q(\Omega),

where

(1.8)

q(\Omega)=\inf_{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta w=0,\,w\neq 0\\
\frac{\partial w}{\partial\nu}=0\,\textit{on}\,\,\partial\Omega_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{r}}w^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}w^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}.

Then our result is the following

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded set with Lipschitz boundary and let
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, where
B_{r}
is the ball centered at the origin and with radius
r
such that
\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{0}
. Then the following estimates hold

(1.9)

\frac{1}{\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}+\frac{P(\Omega_{0})}{m},\\

and

(1.10)

\frac{1}{\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{D}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{q_{\beta}(\Omega)},

where
\mu_{1}(\Omega)
and
q_{\beta}(\Omega)
are defined in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), and
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and
\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
are the first Steklov-Robin and Steklov Dirichlet eigenvalue of
\Omega
defined in (
1.4
) and (
1.2
) respectively.

As a consequence of the above estimates we can obtain the quoted asymptotic behaviour of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
with respect to
m
. We stress the fact that, when
\beta(x)=\beta
is a positive constant, Theorem
1.2
is a key point to prove the convergence of the Steklov-Robin eigenfunctions to the Steklov-Dirichlet eigenfunction as
\beta
goes to infinity. Indeed, our second main result is the following

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded set with Lipschitz boundary and let
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, where
B_{r}
is the ball centered at the origin and with radius
r
such that
\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{0}
. Let
\beta(x)=\beta
be a positive constant. Let
u_{\beta}
and
v
be positive eigenfunctions corresponding to
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and
\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
respectively. Then

(1.11)

\lim_{\beta\to+\infty}\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=0.

Remark 1.4
.

The previous result states that the first Steklov-Robin eigenfunctions converge to the Steklov-Dirichlet ones in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, when
\beta
goes to
\infty
. Actually, since they are smooth functions, by Theorem
1.2
and Morrey’s inequality we can say that the convergence result holds true in
C^{0}(\Omega).

The structure of the paper is the following: in Section 2 we recall some preliminary result and fix the notation we will use in the rest of the paper. In Section 3 we state and prove the main properties of the first eigenvalue
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and of the corresponding eigenfunctions. In Section 4 we focus on the radial case and finally Section 5 and 6 are devoted for the computation of some upper and lower bounds for
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and the proof of Theorems
1.2
-
1.3
.

2.
Preliminaries and notations

Throughout this paper, we denote by
B_{r}(x_{0})
and
B_{r}
the balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of radius
r>0
centered at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and at the origin, respectively. Moreover with
B
,
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\omega_{n}
we will denote respectively the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, its boundary and its volume. Let
r,R
be such that
0<r<R
, the spherical shell will be denoted as follows:

A_{r,R}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon r<|x|<R\}.

Moreover, the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
and the Euclidean scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open bounded set and let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a measurable set. For the sake of completeness, we recall here the definition of the perimeter of
E
in
D
(see for instance
[
14
]
), that is

P(E;D)=\sup\left\{\int_{E}{\rm div}\varphi\>dx:\;\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D;\mathbb{R}^{n}),\;||\varphi||_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}.

The perimeter of
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
P(E)
and, if
P(E)<\infty
, we say that
E
is a set of finite perimeter. Moreover, if
E
has Lipschitz boundary, it holds

P(E)=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E).

The Lebesgue measure of a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
V(E)
.
Moreover, we define the inradius of
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

(2.1)

\rho(E)=\sup_{x\in E}\;\inf_{y\in\partial E}|x-y|,

while the diameter of
E
is

\diam(E)=\sup_{x,y\in E}|x-y|.

2.1.
Some properties of convex sets

In what follows we recall some properties of the convex bodies, i.e. compact convex sets without empty interior. We refer to
[
17
]
for further properties and the details.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded convex body. The support function
h_{K}
of
K
is defined as
follows

h_{K}\colon\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R},\quad h_{K}(x)=\sup_{y\in K}(x,y).

If the origin belongs to
K
then
h_{K}
is non-negative and
h_{K}(x)\leq\text{diam}(K)
for every
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded convex body such that the origin is an interior point of
K
. The radial function of
K
is defined as follows

(2.2)

\rho_{K}(x)=\sup\{\lambda\geq 0\colon\lambda x\in K\},\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},

and it is a Lipschitz function. Moreover the boundary of
K
can be written as

(2.3)

\partial K=\{x\,\rho_{K}(x),x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\}.

Let us define the minimum and the maximum distance of
\partial K
from the origin as follows

(2.4)

R_{m}=\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\rho_{K}(x),\qquad\quad R_{M}=\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\rho_{K}(x).

Finally, if
f\colon\partial K\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-
integrable the following formula for the change of variable given by the radial map holds

\int_{\partial K}f\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(r_{K}(x))(\rho_{K}(x))^{n-1}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{|\nabla_{\tau}\rho_{K}(x)|}{\rho_{K}(x)}\right)^{2}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

where
\nabla_{\tau}
denotes the tangential gradient, which is the projection of the gradient on the tangent plane.

2.2.
Trace and Friedrich inequalities

Here we recall some known inequalities that will be useful in the sequel. Let
\Omega
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Lipschitz boundary, then by the classical Sobolev trace inequality (see
[
4
]
) we have that

(2.5)

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

for some positive constant
C>0
. Moreover the embedding operator of
H^{1}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is compact.

Another important embedding theorem is a consequence of the so-called Friedrich’s inequality (see for instance
[
5
,
15
]
and for a more general case
[
1
]
).
Let
H^{1}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)
the completion of the set of functions in
C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C(\bar{\Omega})
which have weak gradient in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, equipped with the following norm (see
[
15
]
for the details)

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)}=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

Fridrich’s inequality states that

(2.6)

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)})

for some positive constant
C>0
. Also in this case the embedding operator of
H^{1}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact (see Corollary 3, p. 392 in
[
15
]
).

3.
Existence and basic properties of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)

In this section we define and study the main properties of the first Steklov-Robin Laplacian eigenvalue in
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{B}_{r}
. Let
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
be the following quantity

(3.1)

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\\
v\not\equiv 0\end{subarray}}J[v],

where

(3.2)

J[v]=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}\;dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}v^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}

and
\beta(x)\in L^{\infty}(\partial B_{r})
is a positive function.

We observe that by (
3.1
) we immediately get

(3.3)

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\leq\sigma_{D}(\Omega),

where
\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
is the first Steklov-Dirichlet eigenvalue defined in (
1.2
).
In the next result we prove that
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
is the first eigenvalue of problem (
1.3
) and we show some basic properties of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and its corresponding eigenfunctions.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{B}_{r}
, where
\Omega_{0}
is an open, bounded and connected set with Lipschitz boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
B_{r}
a ball centered at the origin of radius
r>0
such that
\overline{B}_{r}\subset\Omega_{0}
. Then
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
is actually a minimum, that is

(3.4)

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\\
v\not\equiv 0\end{subarray}}J[v],

where
J[v]
is defined in (
3.2
). Moreover
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
is the first eigenvalue of (
1.3
), it is strictly positive and any minimizer has constant sign.

Proof.

Let us notice that the Rayleigh quotient
J[w]
defined in the previous proposition is always non-negative and
0
-homogeneous. Let us consider a minimizing normalized sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}=1
, i.e.
\lim_{n\to\infty}J[u_{n}]=\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
. By (
3.3
),
J[u_{n}]\leq\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
, then by Friedrich’s inequality (
2.6
),
\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is uniformly bounded from above. By the compactness of the embedding
H^{1}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a subsequence, still denoted by
u_{n}
, and a function
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with
||u||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}=1
, such that
u_{n}\to u
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, hence also almost everywhere, and
\nabla u_{n}\rightharpoonup\nabla u
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Moreover, by the compactness of the trace embedding theorem (
2.5
),

u_{n}
converges strongly to
u
also in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega
to
u
. Then, by weak lower semicontinuity we have

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}J[u_{n}]\geq J[u].

Hence the existence of a minimizer
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
follows.

It is obvious the fact that
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\geq 0
. By contradiction let us suppose that
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)=0
. This means that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0.

It follows that
\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and
\|\sqrt{\beta(x)}u\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{r})}
are both zero. From the first we have that
u
is constant a.e. in
\Omega
and then it must be
\beta(x)=0
a.e. on
\partial B_{r}
, which is an absurd. Therefore
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)>0
.

By classical arguments of Calculus of Variation it is easy to prove that (
1.3
) is the Euler-Lagrange equation corresponding to (
3.4
). Here we write down the proof for completeness. Let
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be minimum of the Rayleigh quotient (
3.2
) and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
its value, i.e.
J[u]=\lambda
. Let us now consider the first variation of
J[\cdot]
. If
v\in H^{1}(\Omega),
we define the following function

f(\varepsilon)=J[u+\varepsilon v].

It is clear that
f(0)=\lambda
and in particular we have that

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\cdot f^{\prime}(0)&=2\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\,dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)uv\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\bigg)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\
&-\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\bigg)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}uv\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0\end{split}

if and only if

\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\,dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)uv\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}uv\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}=\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}=\lambda.

Since the relation written above is valid for every
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, the proposition is proved by definition of weak solution.

In particular it follows that
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
is the smallest eigenvalue of problem (
1.3
). Indeed let us suppose that
v
is another eigenfunction of (
1.3
) with corresponding eigenvalue
\tilde{\sigma}
. Then an integration by parts gives

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\leq\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}=\dfrac{\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}=\tilde{\sigma}.

It only remains to show that any minimizer has constant sign. If
u
be an eigenfunction corresponding to
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
, then
J[u]=J[|u|]
. This implies that
u=|u|
on
\Omega
and therefore
u\geq 0
on
\Omega
. By Harnack inequality (see
[
19
, Thm 1.1]
),
u
is strictly positive on
\Omega
.

∎

Next propositions concern the simplicity of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and sign properties of the corresponding eigenfunctions.

Proposition 3.2
.

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
is simple, which means that there exists a unique corresponding eigenfunction up to multiplicative constants.

Proof.

Let us now suppose that
v
is another eigenfunction corresponding to
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
. Since
v>0
in
\Omega
, it follows

\int_{\Omega}v\,dx\neq 0.

Hence there exists a positive number
\xi>0
, such that

\int_{\Omega}(u-\xi v)\,dx=0.

Since
u-\xi v
is another eigenfunction corresponding to the same eigenvalue, it is necessary that
u=\xi v
in
\Omega
, which proves the simplicity.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, where
\Omega_{0}
is an open, bounded and connected set with Lipschitz boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
B_{r}
a ball centered at the origin of radius
r>0
such that
\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{0}
.
Any nonnegative function
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
that satisfies in the sense of definition (
1.1
)

(3.5)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\sigma u&\mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{0}\\
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+\beta(x)u=0&\mbox{on}\ \partial B_{R},\end{cases}

is a first eigenfunction of (
3.5
), that is
\sigma=\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
, and
v=u
(up to multiplicative constants), where
u
is the eigenfunction corresponding to the first eigenvalue
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Since
u
is a positive eigenfunction corresponding to
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
, it satisfies

(3.6)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\;dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Let us use the following function
z=u^{2}/(v+\varepsilon)
, for
\epsilon>0
,
as a test function in the definition
1.1
of weak solution for
v
.
We get

(3.7)

\int_{\Omega}\bigg[\frac{2u\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle}{v+\varepsilon}-\frac{u^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}}{(v+\varepsilon)^{2}}\bigg]\,dx+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{v}{v+\varepsilon}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\sigma\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}\frac{v}{v+\varepsilon}u^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

If we subtract (
3.6
) by (
3.7
), since
v/(v+\varepsilon)<1
, we get

\begin{split}0\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u-\frac{u\nabla v}{v+\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\bigg[|\nabla u|^{2}-&\frac{2u\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle}{v+\varepsilon}+\frac{u^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}}{(v+\varepsilon)^{2}}\bigg]\,dx\\
&\leq\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}\bigg[\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)-\sigma\frac{v}{v+\varepsilon}\bigg]u^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.\end{split}

Passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we get

[\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)-\sigma]\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\geq 0.

Since
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
is the smallest eigenvalue, the only possibility is that
\sigma=\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
and by the simplicity of the first eigenvalue, it must be
v=u
up to multiplicative constants.
∎

4.
The first Steklov-Robin eigenvalue in the spherical shell

Let us consider now
A_{r,R}=B_{R}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, where
B_{R}
and
B_{r}
are balls centered at the origin with radii
R>r>0
. Let
\beta(x)=\beta>0
be positive and constant on
\partial B_{r}
and let us consider the Steklov-Robin eigenvalue problem for the Laplacian in the spherical shell

(4.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\text{in}\;A_{r,R}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+\beta u=0&\text{on}\;\partial B_{r}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\sigma u&\text{on}\;\partial B_{R},\end{cases}

where
\nu
is the outer unit normal to
\partial A_{r,R}
.

We are going to compute the solutions to problem (
4.1
).

Theorem 4.1
.

The first Steklov-Robin eigenvalue of the problem (
4.1
) is

(4.2)

\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})\begin{cases}\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\frac{R}{\beta r}+R\log\frac{R}{r}}&n=2\\
\frac{\displaystyle n-2}{\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle n-2}{\beta}\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{n-2}+R\bigg[\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{n-2}-1\bigg]}&n\geq 3,\end{cases}

and the corresponding eigenfunctions are the following

(4.3)

u(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\log\frac{\left|x\right|}{r}+\frac{1}{\beta r}&n=2\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{n-2}}-\frac{1}{\left|x\right|^{n-2}}+\frac{n-2}{\beta}\frac{1}{Rr^{n-2}}&n\geq 3.\end{cases}

Proof.

Since the radial symmetry of the problem and the rotational invariance of the Laplacian, we look forward to a solution which is of the type
u(x)=v(\left|x\right|)=v(s)
, where
s=\left|x\right|
. Computing the Laplacian of
v
we get

v^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{s}v^{\prime}=0,

which is equivalent to

(s^{n-1}v^{\prime})^{\prime}=0.

So integrating twice we get

(4.4)

v(s)=\begin{cases}c_{1}\log s+c_{2}&n=2\\
\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}}{s^{n-2}}+c_{2}&n\geq 3.\end{cases}

We are going to find the solution to (
4.1
) by using the boundary conditions on
B_{r}
and
B_{R}
.
Let us begin by the bidimensional case. Using the boundary condition we get the following system in the unknown variables
c_{1}
and
c_{2}

(4.5)

\begin{cases}-\frac{c_{1}}{r}+\beta(c_{1}\log r+c_{2})=0\\
\frac{c_{1}}{R}-\sigma(c_{1}\log R+c_{2})=0.\end{cases}

Since this is a homogeneous system, the only way not to have
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
is that

\det\begin{pmatrix}-1/r+\beta\log r&\beta\\
1/R-\sigma\log R&-\sigma\end{pmatrix}=0.

From this we get that

\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})=\frac{1}{\frac{R}{\beta r}+R\log\frac{R}{r}}.

Since this choice of
\sigma
,
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
must be linearly dependents. Hence if we chose
c_{1}=1
, by using the second equation in (
4.5
) we have that

c_{2}=\frac{1}{\sigma R}-\log R=\frac{1}{\beta r}+\log\frac{R}{r}-\log R=\frac{1}{\beta r}-\log r.

Hence inserting
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
in (
4.4
), we have

u(x)=\log\frac{\left|x\right|}{r}+\frac{1}{\beta r}.

In higher dimensions the system becomes

(4.6)

\begin{cases}\frac{n-2}{r^{n-1}}c_{1}+\beta(\frac{c_{1}}{r^{n-2}}+c_{2})=0\\
-\frac{n-2}{R^{n-1}}c_{1}-\sigma(\frac{c_{1}}{R^{n-2}}+c_{2})=0.\end{cases}

Proceding in the same way as before, we find that

\sigma=\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})=\frac{n-2}{\displaystyle\frac{n-2}{\beta}\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{n-2}+R\bigg[\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{n-2}-1\bigg]}.

Hence chosing
c_{1}=-1

c_{2}=\frac{1}{R^{n-2}}+\frac{n-2}{\sigma R^{n-1}}=\frac{1}{r^{n-2}}+\frac{n-2}{\beta}\frac{1}{Rr^{n-2}},

and

u(x)=\frac{1}{r^{n-2}}-\frac{1}{\left|x\right|^{n-2}}+\frac{n-2}{\beta}\frac{1}{Rr^{n-2}}.

Eventually, in any dimension, with these choices of the constants
c_{1},c_{2}
, the corresponding eigenfunctions do not change sign and so they must be eigenfunctions corresponding to the first Steklov-Robin eigenvalue
\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})
.
∎

By the explicit form of
\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})
and the corresponding eigenfunctions in (
4.2
)-(
4.3
), we deduce the following properties
when we let vary
\beta
or the radii of the spherical shell.

•

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})=0
, and in particular

(4.7)

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})}{\beta}=\frac{P(B_{r})}{P(B_{R})}.

•

Recalling the explicit value of the first Steklov-Dirichlet eigenvalue of spherical shells (see
[
21
,
8
,
16
]
), we have

(4.8)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})=\sigma_{D}(A_{r,R}).

•

Finally we have

(4.9)

\lim_{r\to 0}\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})=\lim_{R\to 0}\sigma_{\beta}(A_{r,R})=0,

We will see in the next section that all of these behaviours will persist in the case of a generic
\Omega
.

5.
Asymptotic estimates of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
with respect to
\beta
and
r

In this section we will study the behaviour of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
when
m
(or
\beta
constant) and

5.1.
Behaviour with respect to the inner radius

We will prove that (
4.9
) continues to hold for a general annular domain
\Omega
by proving some suitable estimates in terms of the radius of the hole. Indeed let us consider the spherical shell
A_{r,R_{m}}
, where
R_{m}
is defined in (
2.4
), which is contained in
\Omega
. If we choose as a test function in the variational characterization of
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)

\varphi=\begin{cases}\displaystyle v(|x|)&\text{in}\,A_{r,R_{m}}\\
\displaystyle v(R_{m})&\text{in}\,\Omega\setminus\overline{A_{r,R_{m}}},\end{cases}

where
v
is the first eigenfunction in
A_{r,R_{m}}
with constant Robin parameter
\overline{\beta}=1/m
, then

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\leq\sigma_{1}(A_{r,R_{m}}).

As a consequence when
r\to 0
,

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\to 0.

A natural question, now, is asking if we have a lower bound in terms of the first Steklov-Robin eigenvalue of an opportune spherical shell. In the following result we prove that for starshaped set is possible to have an optimal lower bound
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
r>0
and
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded starshaped set such that
\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{0}
and let
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
. Then, if
\tilde{\beta}=\inf_{x\in\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)
, it holds

(5.1)

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\geq\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sigma_{\tilde{\beta}}\left(A_{r,R_{m}}\right)}{R_{M}^{n-1}\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{|\nabla_{\tau}\rho_{0}|^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}}\right)},

where
R_{m}
and
R_{M}
are defined in (
2.4
),
\rho_{0}
is the radial function of
\Omega_{0}
defined in (
2.2
) and
A_{r,R_{m}}
is the spherical shell with radii
r
and
R_{m}
.

Moreover, the equality case holds if and only if
\Omega_{0}
is also a ball
B_{R}
centered at the origin of radius
R>0
.

Proof.

We will follow an idea used in
[
13
]
for the planar case and in
[
7
,
20
]
for any dimension.
Let
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)

By using spherical coordinates and the notation introduced in Section
2
:

\partial\Omega_{0}=\{x\,\rho_{0}(x),x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\}

and

\partial B_{r}=\{xr,\,x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\}.

The integrals over the boundaries
\partial\Omega_{0}
and
\partial B_{R}
of
u^{2}
become

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}u^{2}\,\,\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{|\nabla_{\tau}\rho_{0}|}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{2}}\,(\rho_{0})^{n-1}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

(5.2)

\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\geq\tilde{\beta}r^{n-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}u^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

In particular we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq(R_{M})^{n-1}\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{|\nabla_{\tau}\rho_{0}|^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}u^{2}\,\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

We can parametrize

\Omega=\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon s=x\,\tilde{r},\,x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\,\tilde{r}\leq\tilde{r}\leq\rho_{0}(x)\},

by using spherical coordinates, where we denote by
R(y)=\rho_{0}(x(y))
, and
x\colon y\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to x(y)\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is a standard parametrization of the boundary of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then we get

(5.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\;ds=\displaystyle\int_{U}\int_{r}^{R(y)}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\tilde{r}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{2}}|\nabla_{\tau}u|^{2}\right\}\tilde{r}^{n-1}\sqrt{\tilde{g}}\,d\tilde{r}\,dy,\\

where
\sqrt{\tilde{g}}
is the determinant of the matrix
\tilde{g}_{ij}
, that is the standard metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\nabla_{\tau}u
is the component of
\nabla u
tangential to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Therefore

(5.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\;ds\geq\int_{U}\int_{r}^{R_{m}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\tilde{r}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{2}}|\nabla_{\tau}u|^{2}\right\}\tilde{r}^{n-1}\sqrt{\tilde{g}}\,d\tilde{r}\,dy,

Combining (
5.3
), (
5.2
) and (
5.5
) and recalling (
3.4
), we get

(5.6)

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\geq\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{U}\int_{r}^{R_{m}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\tilde{r}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{2}}|\nabla_{\tau}u|^{2}\right\}\tilde{r}^{n-1}\sqrt{\tilde{g}}\,d\tilde{r}\,dy+\tilde{\beta}r^{n-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\displaystyle(R_{M})^{n-1}\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{|\nabla_{\tau}\rho_{0}|^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}}\right)\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}u^{2}\,\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}\geq\\
\frac{\sigma_{\tilde{\beta}}(A_{r,R_{m}})}{\displaystyle(R_{M})^{n-1}\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{|\nabla_{\tau}\rho_{0}|^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}}\right)}.

Finally, we stress that the equality case implies that all the inequalities become equalities. So, we have that
\nabla_{\tau}\rho_{0}=0
and
\rho_{0}(x)=\overline{R}
, with
\overline{R}>R
constant.
∎

Theorem
5.1
tells us that as long
\Omega_{0}
is an open, bounded starshaped set, and
r>0
, then
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
remains away from zero. Is this property still true for any open, bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Lipschitz boundary? In general the answer is no, as showed in the following bidimensional counterexample, that is contained in
[
10
]
, that can be easily generalized in any dimension.

Counterexample 5.2
.

Let
\beta(x)=\beta
be a positive constant and let us consider a sequence of open, bounded and connected sets
\{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
as follows

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=B(x_{0})\cup R_{\varepsilon}\cup(B(x_{1})\setminus\overline{B_{r}(x_{1})}).

Here

R_{\varepsilon}=\bigg(-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg)\times\bigg(-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2},\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}\bigg)

is a rectangle centered at the origin with sides of length
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{3}
respectively,
B(x_{1})
,
B(x_{2})
are two dimensional balls of radius
1
centered at the points
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, chosen such that the rectangle
R_{\varepsilon}
is well glued and eventually
B_{r}(x_{2})
is a concentric ball in
B(x_{2})
of radius
0<r<1
.

Let us consider the following function

u(x,y)=\begin{cases}\sin\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{2\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)&\text{in}\,R_{\varepsilon}\\
0&\text{elsewhere},\end{cases}

which is a continuous test function for the first Steklov-Robin eigenvalue.

Let us evaluate
u
in the numerator and denominator the Rayleigh quotient. The denominator becomes

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&=\int_{\partial R_{\varepsilon}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=2\int_{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\sin^{2}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{2\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\,dx\\
&=4\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\sin^{2}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{4\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\,dx=4\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\frac{1-\cos\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{4\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)}{2}\,dx=\varepsilon\end{split}

Since

|\nabla u|^{2}=\bigg(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\bigg)^{2}=\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{2}\cos^{2}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{2\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg),

we have that the numerator is

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx\,dy&=\int_{R_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx\,dy=\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{2}\int_{-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}}\int_{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\cos^{2}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{4\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\,dx\,dy\\
&=2\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{2}\varepsilon^{3}\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\frac{1+\cos\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{4\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)}{2}\,dx=2\pi^{2}\varepsilon^{2}.\end{split}

In this way, since
u
is zero on
\partial B_{r}(x_{2})
, we get

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}=2\pi^{2}\varepsilon,

and

\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We stress the fact that the same proof can be exhibited even in the Steklov-Dirichlet case.

This counterexample gives us two information. The first is the one we already mentioned: if
\Omega_{0}
is not starshaped, the first eigenvalue could be arbitrarily close to zero. The second one is that when
\Omega
is not connected, then
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
could be zero, even though
r>0
.

5.2.
Behaviour with respect to
m
and
\beta

In this section we will give the proof of the Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Firstly we prove inequality (
1.9
). We observe that the claim is well posed since, by proceeding analogously as in the existence theorem,
\mu_{1}(\Omega)
is positive.
For any
w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, for simplicity, we will use the following notation

(5.7)

D(w):=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\,dx.

Let
u
be a positive eigenfunction corresponding to
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
. By the Minkowski inequality and the definition of
\mu_{1}(\Omega)
we have

\sqrt{\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}\leq\sqrt{\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}(u-c)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}+\sqrt{c^{2}P(\Omega_{0})}\leq\sqrt{\frac{D(u)}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}}+\sqrt{c^{2}P(\Omega_{0})},

where
c
is

(5.8)

c=\dfrac{1}{m}\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

Squaring and using the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality, we have

(5.9)

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&\leq\frac{D(u)}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}+c^{2}P(\Omega_{0})+2\sqrt{\frac{D(u)c^{2}P(\Omega_{0})}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}}\\
&=D(u)\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}+\frac{P(\Omega_{0})}{m}\right)+c^{2}m\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}+\frac{P(\Omega_{0})}{m}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}+\frac{P(\Omega_{0})}{m}\right)(D(u)+c^{2}m).\end{split}

Applying Hölder inequality in (
5.8
) we have

c^{2}m=\frac{1}{m}\bigg(\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\bigg)^{2}\leq\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

so that

(5.10)

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&\leq\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}+\frac{P(\Omega_{0})}{m}\right)\left(D(u)+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{1}(\Omega)}+\frac{P(\Omega_{0})}{m}\right)\left(\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right),\end{split}

which gives (
1.5
).

Now we prove inequality (
1.10
). Let us stress the fact that the quantity
q(\Omega)
defined in (
1.8
) is strictly positive. Indeed if
q(\Omega)=0
, then it would follow that
w=0
a.e. on
\partial B_{r}
and since
\partial w\backslash\partial\nu=0
on
\partial\Omega_{0}
, then it would imply
w\equiv 0
in
\Omega
, which is a contradiction. Let
u
be the eigenfunction corresponding to
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
, solution to problem (
1.3
). Let us observe that
u=v+h
, where
v
and
h
solve the following problems

\begin{cases}\Delta v=0&\text{in}\,\Omega\\
v=0&\text{on}\,\partial B_{r}\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}&\text{on}\,\partial\Omega_{0},\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\begin{cases}\Delta h=0&\text{in}\,\Omega\\
h=u&\text{on}\,\partial B_{r}\\
\frac{\partial h}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{on}\,\partial\Omega_{0}.\end{cases}

It is easy to check that

(5.11)

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v\right|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla h\right|^{2}\,dx.

Then, proceeding as the proof of inequality (
1.5
), by applying Minkowski inequality and using (
5.11
), (
3.7
) and (
1.6
), we get

\begin{split}\sqrt{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}&\leq\sqrt{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}v^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}+\sqrt{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}h^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}\\
&\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma_{D}(\Omega)}\displaystyle D(u)}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{q_{\beta}(\Omega)}\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}.\end{split}

Squaring both sides we have

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq\frac{D(u)}{\sigma_{D}(\Omega)}&+\frac{1}{q_{\beta}(\Omega)}\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\
&+2\sqrt{\frac{D(u)}{\sigma_{D}(\Omega)}\frac{1}{q_{\beta}(\Omega)}\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}\end{split}

Applying the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality and Hölder inequality we get

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&\leq\frac{D(u)}{\sigma_{D}(\Omega)}+\frac{D(u)}{q_{\beta}(\Omega)}\\
&+\frac{1}{q_{\beta}(\Omega)}\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{D}(\Omega)}\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\
&=\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma_{D}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{q_{\beta}(\Omega)}\bigg)\bigg(D(u)+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\beta(x)u^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\bigg)\end{split}

This gives (
1.10
).

∎

Remark 5.3
.

We stress that a rough but meaningful estimate can be obtained

) the constant function. In this case we have the following upper bound

) and the second by using (

Taking in (

) the limit for

In particular, if

Remark 5.4
.

We observe that if

6.
Asymptotic behaviour of the eigenfunctions

In the radial case it is easy to check that the eigenfunctions corresponding to
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)
converge pointwise to the corresponding eigenfunction of the Steklov-Dirichlet eigenvalue. In this section we will prove that the same happens even for a generic
\Omega
when
\beta
is a positive constant.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Without loss of generality, we suppose that
u_{\beta}
and
v
are normalized in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})
with unitary norm.
By their weak formulations we get

(6.1)

\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\;dx+\beta\int_{\partial B_{r}}u\varphi\,d\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u\varphi\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega),

and

(6.2)

\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla v,\nabla\varphi\rangle\;dx=\sigma_{D}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}v\varphi\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{\partial B_{r}}(\Omega).

In particular
v
is an admissible function for (
6.1
), hence

(6.3)

\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\;dx=\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}uv\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Now, by Friederich’s inequality (
2.6
), there exists a positive constant
C>0
such that

(6.4)

\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\bigg(\|\nabla u_{\beta}-\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\bigg).

Let us now estimate the terms inside the round brackets. Using the variational characterization of
u_{\beta}
and
v
, the fact that
\|u_{\beta}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}=\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}=1
and equation (
6.3
) we get

\begin{split}\|\nabla u_{\beta}&-\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\\
&=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\beta}|^{2}\,dx-2\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u_{\beta},\nabla v\rangle\,dx+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}\,dx+\\
&+\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}|u_{\beta}-v|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\partial B_{r}}u_{\beta}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\
&=(1-\beta)\int_{\partial B_{r}}u^{2}_{\beta}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}|u_{\beta}-v|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\
&+\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)-2\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}uv\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\sigma_{D}(\Omega).\end{split}

Since we want
\beta\to+\infty
, we can chose
\beta>1
. Therefore

\begin{split}\|\nabla u_{\beta}&-\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}<\\
&<\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}|u_{\beta}-v|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)-2\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}uv\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\sigma_{D}(\Omega).\end{split}

Since in the previous section we proved that
\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\to\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
as
\beta\to+\infty
, if we prove that
\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}\to 0
we have (
1.11
).

Let
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of Robin parameters such that
\beta_{k}>1
for every
k\geq 1
and such that
\beta_{k}\to+\infty
. Let
u_{k}\equiv u_{\beta_{k}}
be the eigenfunctions corresponding to the eigenvalues
\sigma_{\beta_{k}}(\Omega)
, such that
\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}=1
. We already know that
\sigma_{\beta_{k}}(\Omega)\to\sigma_{D}(\Omega)
as
k\to+\infty
.

Applying once more Friederich’s inequality (
2.6
), we get

\begin{split}\|u_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}&=\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)})\\
&\leq\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\beta_{k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{r})}+1)\\
&\leq\sigma_{D}(\Omega)+C(\sigma_{D}(\Omega)+1)=(C+1)\sigma_{D}(\Omega)+C.\end{split}

Hence we have that the sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega)
with respect to
\beta_{k}
. So there exists a function
\bar{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\nabla u_{k}\to\nabla\bar{u}
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and by the compactness of the trace operator we have that
u_{k}\to\bar{u}
in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and hence
u_{k}\to\bar{u}
a.e. on
\partial\Omega
. In particular, since for every
k\in\mathbb{N}

\beta_{k}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{r})}\leq\sigma_{D}(\Omega)<+\infty,

it is necessary that
\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{r})}\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
. Using Fatou’s Lemma we have that for
k\to+\infty

0\leq\int_{\partial B_{r}}\bar{u}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq\liminf_{k\to+\infty}\int_{\partial B_{r}}u_{k}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\to 0.

Therefore
\|\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=0
, and hence
\bar{u}\in H^{1}_{\partial B_{r}}(\Omega)
. We only need to show that
\bar{u}=v
. If we use
\bar{u}
as a test function in (
6.1
) we have that

\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u_{k},\nabla\bar{u}\rangle\;dx=\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}u_{k}\bar{u}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Now by the weak convergence of the gradients, the weak convergence of
u_{k}
on
L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})
and since
\sigma_{\beta_{k}}\to\sigma_{D}
, we get

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\bar{u}|^{2}\,dx=\sigma_{D}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}\bar{u}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

By the simplicity of
\sigma_{D}
and the fact that
\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}=\|\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}=1
, it follows that
\bar{u}=v
. This implies that
\|u_{\beta_{k}}-v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}\to 0
and so

\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0.

In particular the following

\begin{split}0&\leq\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\|\nabla u_{\beta}-\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u_{\beta}-v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)})\\
&\leq C\bigg(\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)-2\sigma_{\beta}(\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}uv\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\sigma_{D}(\Omega)\bigg)\to 0\end{split}

implies that
\|\nabla u_{\beta}-\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0
. This conclude the proof.
∎
