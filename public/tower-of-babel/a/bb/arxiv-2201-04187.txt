The hydrodynamics of a twisting, bending, inextensible fiber in Stokes flow
Ondrej Maxian, Brennan Sprinkle, Charles S. Peskin, Aleksandar Donev

Abstract
In swimming microorganisms and the cell cytoskeleton, inextensible fibers resist bending and twisting, and interact with the surrounding fluid to cause or resist large-scale fluid motion. In this paper, we develop a novel numerical method for the simulation of cylindrical fibers by extending our previous work on inextensible bending fibers [Maxian et al., Phys. Rev. Fluids 6 (1), 014102] to fibers with twist elasticity. In our “Euler” model, twist is a scalar function that measures the deviation of the fiber cross section relative to a twist-free frame, the fiber exerts only torque parallel to the centerline on the fluid, and the perpendicular components of the rotational fluid velocity are discarded in favor of the translational velocity. In the first part of this paper, we justify this model by comparing it to another commonly-used “Kirchhoff” formulation where the fiber exerts both perpendicular and parallel torque on the fluid, and the perpendicular angular fluid velocity is required to be consistent with the translational fluid velocity. Through asymptotics and numerics, we show that the perpendicular torques in the Kirchhoff model are small, thereby establishing the asymptotic equivalence of the two models for slender fibers. We then develop a spectral numerical method for the hydrodynamics of the Euler model. We define hydrodynamic mobility operators using integrals of the Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa tensor, and evaluate these integrals through a novel slender-body quadrature, which requires on the order of 10 points along a smooth fiber to obtain several digits of accuracy. We demonstrate that this choice of mobility removes the unphysical negative eigenvalues in the translation-translation mobility associated with asymptotic slender body theories, and ensures strong convergence of the fiber velocity and weak convergence of the fiber constraint forces. We pair the spatial discretization with a semi-implicit temporal integrator to confirm the negligible contribution of twist elasticity to the relaxation dynamics of a bent fiber. We also study the instability of a twirling fiber, and demonstrate that rotation-translation and translation-rotation coupling change the critical twirling frequency at which a whirling instability onsets by ∼ \sim 20%, which explains some (but not all) of the deviation from theory observed experimentally. When twirling is unstable, we show that the dynamics transition to a steady overwhirling state, and quantify the amplitude and frequency of the resulting steady crankshafting motion.

Introduction

The focus of this paper is on the simulation of cylindrical, slender, inextensible fibers immersed in a Stokes (zero Reynolds number) fluid, as found in cytoskeletal fibers
[
4

, bacterial flagella and cilia

,

44

In modeling twisted fibers immersed in a fluid, there are two largely independent choices to be made: the elastic rod model and the hydrodynamic mobility (force-velocity relationship). With regard to the first of these, most studies that explicitly (or fully) couple the rod to the surrounding fluid use some form of the Kirchhoff rod model
[
20
]
, in which the inextensible and unshearable rod resists both bending (along two principal axes) and twisting (along the third axis parallel to the fiber centerline). As we discuss, there is an ambiguity in the equilibrium equations of the Kirchhoff rod in which the torque density perpendicular to the fiber centerline can be recast into a force density; many studies refer to this parallel-torque-only version as “the Kirchhoff rod model”
[
69
, Sec. 2.3]
. In either case, the Kirchhoff model is a special case of the Cosserat model
[
61
,
26

, which also allows for extensibility and shear of the rod, and has recently been used to model cytoskeletal fibers without dynamics
[
25
]

For hydrodynamic coupling, the numerical methods used to simulate slender fibers fall into two broad categories: methods in which the fibers are represented by a discrete set of regularized singularities or “blobs,” and asymptotic methods, in which the true three-dimensional dynamics are approximated in the limit of very slender fibers. The blob-based class of methods includes the immersed boundary method

57
,
33

,

In some of these, at least for an unbounded domain, the Stokes equations can be solved analytically for the chosen regularization function, thereby analytically eliminating the fluid equations

,

51
,

[

or analytical solutions to the Stokes equations in the infinite cylinder limit

The numerical use of slender body theory came after regularized singularity methods to solve a problem in the latter: when the fiber is discretized as a chain of blobs, in order to accurately
hydrodynamically
model the fiber as a continuous curve, and not as a series of disconnected beads, the spacing between the blobs must be on the order of the fiber radius. This means that the number of degrees of freedom scales as
L/{\hat{a}}=\hat{\epsilon}^{-1}
. Slender body theory circumvents this problem, because the fiber is modeled as a continuous and smooth curve, with arclength parameterization
\boldsymbol{X}(s,t)
. The
hydrodynamics
can be reformulated in terms of integrals along the curve, and spectral methods can be used to achieve high accuracy
[
51
,
48
]
. Unfortunately, while SBT methods are the most efficient way to simulate the dynamics of smooth slender fibers, they have never been fully extended to include twist elasticity,
as discussed in
[
46
]
. As a result, a disconnect has emerged in the literature in which immersed boundary methods
[
40
]
, regularized Stokeslets
[
31
,
54
]
, and the force coupling method
[
62
]
, have accounted for the full hydrodynamics (including rotation-translation (rot-trans) and translation-rotation (trans-rot) coupling) of twisting fibers, while methods that use slender body theory
[
58
,
70
,
52
]
have (at best) included nonlocal hydrodynamics only in the translation-translation (trans-trans) mobility, with only local hydrodynamics in the rotation-rotation (rot-rot) mobility, and no trans-rot/rot-trans coupling.

A related, and perhaps more important, ambiguity is how the rod elasticity couples to the fluid mechanics, for which there are again two unreconciled choices in the literature. The main question is: If the rod moves with the local fluid velocity, does the cross section also rotate with the local angular fluid velocity? If it does, then for the rod to be unshearable, the fluid velocity has to be constrained so that the translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}(s)
and angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s)
are consistent with each other, where
s
denotes arclength. Specifically, there are two possible velocities of the tangent vector
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{X}
:
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}
, and
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}
. If we require that the two match, i.e., if we impose the constraint
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}
, we get what we refer to as the
Kirchhoff model
for historical reasons
[
62
,
31
,
74
]
. To avoid constraints, Lim et al.
[
40
]
introduced the
generalized
Kirchhoff rod model, in which the cross section is shearable and the fiber is extensible, both with an associated penalty. This model has been used frequently with the original IB method and with regularized Stokeslets
[
54
,
30
]
, but its consistency with the constrained Kirchhoff formulation as all penalty parameters go to infinity has not yet been established. Of course, imposing the constraint
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}
requires knowledge of the full rotational and translational fluid velocity, which so far has only been possible in regularized singularity methods, since SBT methods have not yet properly accounted for rot-trans coupling.

In the second class of fluid coupling methods, the redundancy in the possible motions is treated not by enforcing a constraint, but by ignoring the rotational fluid velocity in perpendicular directions. The rod then has only four possible motions: translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}(s)
and rotational velocity
\Omega^{\parallel}(s)
about its axis. In this model, the tangent vector
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is updated by the translational velocity, and then a local triad or some other measure of twist is rotated around
\boldsymbol{\tau}
by the velocity
\Omega^{\parallel}
. This decouples solving for the translational and rotational velocity, simplifying the problem and reducing the size of the linear system to be solved. This model, which we shall refer to as the
Euler model
, is presented in the review article by Powers
[
58
]
, and has been used in the simulation of both discrete
[
52
,
59
,
70
,
71
,
29
]
and continuous
[
45
]
elastic rods. All of these methods use an SBT-style mobility, in which nonlocal hydrodynamics is included in the translation-translation (trans-trans) coupling (to give the translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}
), but only local drag is included for rotation-rotation (rot-rot, to give the parallel rotational velocity
\Omega^{\parallel}
). Indeed, a perpendicular angular velocity is not available because no slender body theory exists for translation-rotation (trans-rot) or rotation-translation (rot-trans) coupling, so only parallel rotational velocity can be used.

This paper has two parts, each of which addresses one of the two main ambiguities in the literature. In part one, we compare the two approaches for coupling fluid mechanics and rod elasticity, which we refer to as the Euler and Kirchhoff models. For the fluid mechanics, in Section
2
we define the four components of the mobility operator (trans-trans, trans-rot, rot-trans, rot-rot) in terms of integrals of the Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa RPY kernel
[
72
]
, which is a popular, physics-inspired form of a regularized singularity. To compare the two models of fluid coupling, we use both asymptotic arguments and numerical tests to show that the Kirchhoff and Euler models give the same translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}
a distance
\mathcal{O}(1)
away from the fiber endpoints as the slenderness
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. The key idea is that the perpendicular torque in the Kirchhoff model, which enforces the constraint
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}
, can be as small as
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)
and still generate a total angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
that is consistent with
\boldsymbol{U}
. As a result, these Lagrange multipliers have strength of order
\epsilon^{2}
, and therefore have a minimal impact on
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}
, so they can be dropped in the Euler model without changing the resulting centerline velocity
\boldsymbol{U}
to
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)
. Thus the first part of this paper establishes the asymptotic equivalence of the Euler and Kirchhoff models as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. We will use the Euler model, since that choice makes it simpler to add twist to our previous model of inextensible bending fibers
[
48
]
as an extra degree of freedom.
The key equations are summarized in Section
3
.

In the second part of this paper and a companion paper
[
46
]
, we address the gap in the SBT literature with regard to the hydrodynamic mobility and rot-trans coupling. In
[
46
]
, we develop a slender body theory for twisting which is based on evaluating the RPY integrals asymptotically in the (regularized) slenderness
\hat{\epsilon}
.
Unfortunately, our asymptotic evaluation of the RPY integrals is still plagued by a longstanding problem in SBT-style methods: the ill-posedness of the trans-trans mobility, which has negative eigenvalues corresponding to force densities with lengthscales less than
\epsilon

[
28
,
67
,
50
]
. This is problematic in constrained problems, in which the mobility appears in a linear system to be solved for the constraint force. To avoid this problem with the asymptotic mobilities, in this paper we will instead use integrals of the RPY kernels along the centerline to define the action of the mobility operators (with the exception of rot-rot coupling). To evaluate these near-singular integrals to high accuracy, in Section
4
and Appendix
G
we develop “slender body” quadrature schemes which give three digits of accuracy for smooth fibers with on the order of 10 points discretizing the fiber centerline. This represents a significant improvement over existing regularized singularity approaches, which require
1/\hat{\epsilon}
points to resolve the

, and, unlike asymptotic methods, our approach reduces the required degrees of freedom
without
introducing negative eigenvalues in the mobility operators. Coupling our quadratures with the Euler model gives a spectral numerical method for the twisting and bending dynamics of an inextensible, slender, smooth filament with as few degrees of freedom as possible. In particular, in Section
4.4

we consider the relaxation of bent and twisted filaments with varying curvature, and show that our spectral discretization gives a more accurate velocity with
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}32-40
points on the fiber centerline than a standard second-order, blob-based method with
1/\hat{\epsilon}=1000
points
. We show empirically that the constraint force enforcing inextensibility converges weakly, even though it is not smooth near the fiber endpoints.

In Section
5
of this paper, we extend our numerical method to dynamic problems by introducing a (constrained) backward Euler temporal integration scheme. We apply this numerical method to two problems involving twisting fibers: a relaxing bent fiber, where we explore the feedback of twist on the fiber dynamics, and a twirling fiber, where we look at the critical frequency at which the fiber starts to whirl and
then transitions to a stable “overwhirling” state

[
75
,
41
,
70
,
39
]
. For fiber relaxation, we show that the assumption of twist being in quasi-equilibrium
[
8
,
45
]
is satisfied for materials in which the twist and bending moduli are comparable, as is the case in most materials (for inextensible isotropic elastic cylinders, the ratio is 2/3
[
52
]
). For a twirling fiber, we show how the systematic neglect of rot-trans coupling in prior studies based on the Euler model
[
75
,
70
]
leads to an overestimation in the critical frequency of 20%. Our results explain half of the deviation between prior theoretical/computational studies and what is observed experimentally
[

]

Model formulation and governing equations

In this section, we formulate the governing equations for both the Kirchhoff and Euler models in a viscous fluid. For brevity of notation, we omit explicit time dependence; it is implied that all functions of space also depend on time
t
. We also omit dependence on arclength
s
whenever there is no ambiguity. We start from the classical immersed boundary equations
[
57
]
, and take a continuum limit to model the rod as a line of regularized singularities. After introducing the hydrodynamic model in Section
2.1
, we discuss the two models of inextensible, bending, twisting fibers. This is necessarily preceded by a brief introduction to inextensibility and twist elasticity in Section
2.2
, where we introduce the Bishop frame
[
8
]
. This reference frame allows for a simple representation of twist as a scalar angle
\theta(s)
, instead of the full orthogonal triad used in previous formulations
[
40
,
54
]
. We use this scalar angle as our measure of twist in Section
2.3
, where we introduce the Kirchhoff rod model. A key feature of this model is the consistency condition between the angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s)
and translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}(s)
of the cross section. This requires a Lagrange multiplier force density with
three
independent components for the three directions of the velocity.

We compare the Kirchhoff formulation to the Euler model, which we present in Section
2.4
. In the Euler model, we solve for the translational velocity, which we use to evolve the tangent vectors
[
48
]
, then compute parallel rotational velocity to evolve the twist in a second, independent step. The Euler formulation completely disregards the perpendicular components
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\perp}
of the fluid angular velocity that could in principle be obtained from our hydrodynamic model. Since the Euler model does not enforce consistency of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\perp}
with the translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}
, it requires only a scalar tension as a Lagrange multiplier.
In Section
2.5
, we show that the two discarded perpendicular components of the Lagrange multiplier are small, thereby establishing the equivalence of the Kirchhoff and Euler models as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

The hydrodynamic mobility

In immersed boundary (IB) methods

{\hat{a}}=\epsilon e^{3/2}/4
. Substituting into (
12
) gives
8\pi\mu\Psi_{\text{n}}^{\parallel}\approx 1.79n^{\parallel}/a^{2}
, which is approximately 10% different from the formula for the rotational drag on a radius
a
cylinder,
8\pi\mu\Psi_{\text{n}}^{\parallel}=2n^{\parallel}/a^{2}

2.2
Fiber mechanics

We simulate inextensible fibers that resist bending and twisting. In this section, we discuss pieces of the fiber mechanics that are common to both the Kirchhoff and Euler models, in particular twist elasticity and inextensibility. The way bending elasticity appears in the equations depends on the underlying model of the fiber, and so we defer our discussion of bending to Sections
2.3
and
2.4
.

2.2.1
Twist elasticity

In previous immersed-boundary formulations of twist
[
40
,
54
]
, the rod is defined by an orthonormal material frame
\left(\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}(s),\boldsymbol{D}^{(2)}(s),\boldsymbol{D}^{(3)}(s)\right)
. In the standard model for slender fibers, it is assumed that the fibers are unshearable, so that cross sections of the fiber maintain their shape (e.g., circular) and remain perpendicular to the tangent vector. This implies the constraint

\boldsymbol{D}^{(3)}=\boldsymbol{\tau}.

(13)

The fiber cross sections are allowed to twist relative to the tangent vector. The twist density
\psi(s)
is defined as

\psi:=\boldsymbol{D}^{(2)}\cdot\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}

(14)

with boundary conditions

\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0

(15)

for a free filament
[
58
]
. The use of the material frame is somewhat cumbersome, however, since it requires us to keep track of three separate axes throughout the course of a simulation.

We will instead use a formulation based on the twist-free Bishop frame
[
8
]
, in which the state variables are the tangent vector
\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)
and a degree of twist
\theta(s)
relative to the Bishop frame. The Bishop frame is an orthonormal frame
\left(\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}(s),\boldsymbol{b}^{(2)}(s),\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)
which satisfies

\boldsymbol{b}^{(2)}\cdot\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}=-\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}\cdot\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{b}^{(2)}=0,

(16)

making it twist free. The Bishop frame along the entire fiber can be constructed by choosing
\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}(0)
and
\boldsymbol{b}^{(2)}(0)
such that
\left(\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}(0),\boldsymbol{b}^{(2)}(0),\boldsymbol{\tau}(0)\right)
is an orthonormal frame, and then solving the ordinary differential equation (ODE)
[
8
]

\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}=\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)\times\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)},

(17)

for
\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}(s)
, with
\boldsymbol{b}^{(2)}=\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}
. The material frame and Bishop frame are related by
[
8
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}\cos{\theta}+\boldsymbol{b}^{(2)}\sin{\theta},\qquad\boldsymbol{D}^{(2)}=-\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}\sin{\theta}+\boldsymbol{b}^{(2)}\cos{\theta},\qquad\boldsymbol{D}^{(3)}=\boldsymbol{\tau},

(18)

so that
\theta(s)
is the angle of rotation of the material frame relative to the Bishop frame.
Solving (
17
) is also called “parallel transporting” the vector
\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}
, since its tangential component is kept at zero by performing rotations about the binormal
[
8
]
.
Using (
18
), it can be shown that the twist (
14
) simplifies to
[
8
]

\psi=\partial_{s}\theta.

(19)

If
\theta
is a constant, the material frame is just a constant rotation of the twist-free Bishop frame, so the twist density
\psi=\partial_{s}\theta=0
as well.

2.2.2
Inextensibility

The fibers that we consider are inextensible, meaning that the constraint

\left\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\|^{2}=\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=1

(20)

has to preserved by the fiber evolution. This implies that the variable
s
is arclength. The form of the constraint (
20
) that we will use is the time derivative

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=0,

(21)

i.e., that the velocity of the tangent vector is orthogonal to itself; recall that we omit the explicit dependence on
t
for brevity. This can be enforced using either a penalty
[
40
,
54
]
or a constrained
[
67
,
62
,
31
,
48
]
approach, as we discuss further in what follows.

2.2.3
Evolution of the material frame and twist angle

Finally, we consider the time evolution of the material frame
\left(\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}(s),\boldsymbol{D}^{(2)}(s),\boldsymbol{D}^{(3)}(s)\right)
. Because the frame is orthonormal and stays orthonormal for all time, its velocity is constrained to satisfy

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{D}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{D}^{(i)},

(22)

for some
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(s)
. In the particular case of
i=3
, we have the evolution equation for
\boldsymbol{\tau}
,

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\tau},

(23)

which automatically satisfies the inextensibility constraint (
21
).

The evolution of the twist
density
can be written in terms of the angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi=\partial_{s}\Omega^{\parallel}-\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right),

(24)

where
\Omega^{\parallel}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}
. Equation (
24
) is derived using variational arguments in
[
58
]
; in Appendix
B
we rederive it using vector calculus. In the next two sections, we discuss how to use the Kirchhoff and Euler models to obtain
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, which closes the evolution equation for
\psi
.

2.3
The Kirchhoff model

The distinguishing feature of what we refer to as the
Kirchhoff model
is that the rotational velocity of the tangent vector must be exactly equal to the local angular fluid velocity (
8
),

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\rightarrow\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}.

(25)

However, because
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}
, the assumption (
25
) implies a local constraint that relates the translational and rotational velocity of the rod

\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}.

(26)

Thus the fluid velocity is (locally) constrained so that the integrity of the rod cross section is preserved, i.e., that the rod is unshearable
[
58
]
, and inextensible, since (
26
) implies the inextensibility constraint (
21
).

In the Kirchhoff model as presented in
[
40
,
54
,
62
]
, the total force density
\boldsymbol{f}(s)
and torque density
\boldsymbol{n}(s)
exerted by the fiber on the fluid are given by the force and torque balances
[
62
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{F},\qquad\boldsymbol{n}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{N}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\boldsymbol{F}.

(27)

Here the torque density
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{N}(s)
comes from the internal moments on the rod
\boldsymbol{N}(s)
, which are given in terms of the bending modulus
\kappa
and twist modulus
\gamma
, both of which we assume to be constant along the cylindrical rod. The internal moment
\boldsymbol{N}(s)
can be written in terms of the material frame as
[
40
,
54
,
62
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{N}

\displaystyle=\kappa\left(-\boldsymbol{D}^{(2)}\cdot\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}+\kappa\left(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}\right)\boldsymbol{D}^{(2)}+\gamma\psi\boldsymbol{\tau}

(28)

\displaystyle=\kappa\left(\left(\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}\times\boldsymbol{D}^{(2)}\right)\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)+\gamma\psi\boldsymbol{\tau}

\displaystyle=\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)+\gamma\psi\boldsymbol{\tau},

subject to the free fiber boundary conditions
[
58
]

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{N}(s=0,L)=\boldsymbol{0}\rightarrow\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s=0,L)=\boldsymbol{0},

(29)

and twist boundary conditions (
15
). The torque density
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{N}(s)
in (
27
) is

\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{N}=\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\partial_{s}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)+\gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}\partial_{s}\psi+\psi\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right),

(30)

with the remaining torque density coming from the force
\boldsymbol{F}(s)
, which is subject to the free fiber boundary conditions

\boldsymbol{F}(s=0,L)=\boldsymbol{0}.

(31)

As discussed in
[
62
, p. 5]
, the force
\boldsymbol{F}
is a Lagrange multiplier to enforce the position-based constraint
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{\tau}
. It turns out, however, that the final solution for
\boldsymbol{F}
depends on the internal torque (
30
), since the perpendicular torque from
\boldsymbol{F}

must
eliminate the perpendicular torque coming from
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{N}
for the constraint (
26
) to hold. This is because perpendicular angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{n}}^{\perp}\sim\hat{\epsilon}^{-2}\boldsymbol{n}^{\perp}
, while translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{n}}\sim\boldsymbol{n}^{\perp}\log{\hat{\epsilon}}
. Thus, it is impossible for (
26
) to hold if there is a
\mathcal{O}(1)
perpendicular torque on the fluid, so
\boldsymbol{F}
must cancel the
\mathcal{O}(1)
perpendicular torque in
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{N}
.

We now write an explicit formula for
\boldsymbol{F}
that yields a torque density
\boldsymbol{n}
which is only in the parallel direction, plus a Lagrange multiplier torque in the perpendicular direction. Because this formula eliminates perpendicular torque from the constitutive model, it also eliminates the dependency of torque on the bending modulus
\kappa
, and leaves only parallel torque depending on the twist modulus
\gamma
. The main step is to introduce the forces from bending
\boldsymbol{F}^{(\kappa)}(s)
and twisting
\boldsymbol{F}^{(\gamma)}(s)
, such that when we take their cross product with
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and add it to
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{N}
in (
35
), the perpendicular components of torque will be eliminated

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}^{(\kappa)}=-\kappa\partial_{s}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\qquad\boldsymbol{F}^{(\gamma)}=\gamma\psi\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right).

(32)

We then redefine the force
\boldsymbol{F}
in the Kirchhoff model as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{F}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{F}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda},

(33)

where now
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s)
is the Lagrange multiplier that enforces
the velocity-based constraint
(
26
). For free fibers, the force
\boldsymbol{F}
must vanish at the endpoints (see (
31
)), which, combined with (
29
), leads to the free fiber boundary conditions

\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s=0,L)=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\partial_{s}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(s=0,L)=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s=0,L)=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\psi(s=0,L)=0.

(34)

Substituting the new form of
\boldsymbol{F}
in (
33
) into (
27
), we have the torque density applied to the fluid as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{N}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{F}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)

(35)

\displaystyle=\left(\gamma\partial_{s}\psi\right)\boldsymbol{\tau}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\perp},

(36)

where we defined the parallel torque coming from twist as
[
58
]

n^{\parallel}=\gamma\partial_{s}\psi.

(37)

Throughout this section, we have assumed that the filament is intrinsically straight and untwisted; expressions for
n^{\parallel}
and
\boldsymbol{F}
in the case of an intrinsically bent and twisted rod are given in Appendix
C
.

Note that only the (two) perpendicular components of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
enter in the torque (
36
). The third Lagrange multiplier required to enforce (
26
) is the parallel component of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, which enters in the force density applied to the fluid as
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}=\partial_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{F}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)=\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda},\qquad\text{where}

(38)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}=-\kappa\partial_{s}^{3}{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\qquad\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}=\gamma\partial_{s}\left(\psi\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}.

In our reformulation of the Kirchhoff model, the force density on the fluid is given by (
38
) and the torque is given by (
36
).
This makes it obvious that the only perpendicular torque acting on the fluid comes from the Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\perp}
, while the parallel torque comes from twist elasticity.

2.3.1
Linear system for
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}

To derive a linear system for
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, we first write the constraint (
26
) in terms of the total force density
\boldsymbol{f}(s)
and torque density
\boldsymbol{n}(s)
applied by the rod to the fluid using the mobility operators defined in (
7
) and (
10
),

\partial_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}\boldsymbol{n}\right)=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\boldsymbol{n}\right)\times\boldsymbol{\tau}.

(39)

We then substitute the representations for
\boldsymbol{f}
and
\boldsymbol{n}
from (
38
) and (
36
), and define a linear operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\boldsymbol{x}\right)(s)=\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\boldsymbol{x}(s)
. This yields the second-order nonlocal boundary value problem (BVP)

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\partial_{s}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\partial_{s}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\right)\right]\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=

(40)

\displaystyle-\partial_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)\right)-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)\right)

which can be solved for the Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s)
. Using the force
\boldsymbol{f}
in (
38
) and torque in (
36
) then gives the resulting velocities in the Kirchhoff model,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right),

(41)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right).

(42)

In Appendix
D
, we show that these dynamics dissipate elastic energy by doing work on the fluid, with the Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
not doing any additional work as required by the principle of virtual work
2
2

2

For a
clamped rotating end, it can be shown that the motor rotating the fiber with angular frequency
\omega
does additional work with power
N_{0}\omega
, where
N_{0}
is the applied torque at the clamped end, see (

).

[

.

2.4
The Euler model

The main difference between the Kirchhoff model and what we refer to as the
Euler model
is how the rotation rate of the cross section (equivalently, of the tangent vector) is obtained. In the Kirchhoff model, it does not matter whether we use
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}
or
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}
to update the tangent vector, since by (
26
) they must be the same. In the Euler model, by contrast, we do not set
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
to be equal to
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
as in (
25
), and consequently do not enforce the constraint (
26
) that follows from (
25
). Instead, we relate
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\perp}(s)
to the
translational
velocity via

\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\perp}=\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\perp}\times\boldsymbol{\tau},

(43)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{E}^{\perp}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=0
, and, unlike the Kirchhoff model,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\perp}
is not necessarily equal to
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\perp}
. The evolution of the fiber can now be obtained by solving for
\boldsymbol{U}(s)
and specifying
\Omega^{\parallel}(s)=\Psi^{\parallel}(s)
.

In the Euler model, there is only one constraint, the inextensibility constraint (
21
). This means that the Lagrange multiplier to enforce this constraint should have only one independent component, as opposed to the three independent components for the Lagrange multiplier in the Kirchhoff model. Specifically, the Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s)
reduces to a scalar line tension
T(s)
,

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=T\boldsymbol{\tau},

(44)

with the free-fiber boundary condition (
34
) implying that

T(s=0,L)=0.

(45)

Since
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is tangential, the torque on the fluid in (
36
) reduces to
\boldsymbol{n}=n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}
, where
n^{\parallel}
is defined in (
37
). The force is still given by (
38
), but now with
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
having only one independent component.

In the Euler model, the translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}(s)
is obtained by solving a constrained system which includes only the parallel torque density. This constrained system is derived by substituting (
44
) into the Kirchhoff velocity (
41
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right),

(46)

where in the Euler model
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\partial_{s}\left(T\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)
. By substituting (
46
)
into (
21
)
, we can obtain a (second order, nonlocal) BVP for
T(s)

[
67
]
similar to BVP (
40
) for the Kirchhoff model. As discussed in Section
3.1
, in spectral numerical methods it is better to solve a saddle-point linear system for
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
instead of this tension equation for
T(s)
. The saddle point system is obtained from the principle of virtual work
[
48
, Sec. 3]
; see also Appendix
D
.

The evolution of the twist angle in (
24
) requires the rotation rate
\Omega^{\parallel}(s)
, which is computed in a post-processing step as the parallel component of the angular velocity in the Kirchhoff model (
42
),

\displaystyle\Omega^{\parallel}=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}.

(47)

The Euler model
does not
enforce the constraint (
26
), since only the translational velocity
\boldsymbol{U}
and parallel rotational velocity
\Omega^{\parallel}
are tracked. In Section
2.5
, we study the effect of this on the velocity of the fiber centerline.

We emphasize that our choice to name the model of this section the Euler model and that of the previous section the Kirchhoff model is historical and arbitrary. The reason we choose the naming we do is because the Kirchhoff model as formulated in Section
2.3
is what has been used before
[
40
,
54
,
62
]
, and the Euler model is the one that is closest to what we (and many others) have used before without twist
[
67
,
51
,
48
]
.

2.5
Comparing the Kirchhoff and Euler formulations

In this section, we analyze the difference between the Kirchhoff model of Section
2.3
and the Euler model of Section
2.4
. Because the Kirchhoff model accounts for the angular fluid velocity and has more degrees of freedom, we refer to the translational velocity obtained from (
41
) as the “exact” velocity
\boldsymbol{U}
, with corresponding tangent vector evolution
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\tau}
. We refer to the translational velocity from the Euler model (
46
) as
\boldsymbol{U}_{E}
and the parallel rotational velocity from the Euler model (
47
) as
\Omega^{\parallel}_{E}
. Since the rotation of the tangent vector in the Euler model is obtained from
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}_{E}
, the parallel rotational velocity
\Omega^{\parallel}_{E}
represents the only remaining degree of freedom. To examine the difference between the models, we substitute the definitions (
41
), (
46
), (
42
), and (
47
) to define

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{U}_{E}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{E}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right),

(48)

\displaystyle\Delta\Omega^{\parallel}=\Omega^{\parallel}-\Omega^{\parallel}_{E}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{E}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}:=\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}

(49)

The differences in velocity (
48
) and (
49
) depend on the difference in the constraint force

\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}:=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{E},

(50)

and its derivative, the force density
\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{E}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{E}=T\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{E}=\partial_{s}\left(T\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)
are the constraint force and force density from the Euler model.

To obtain
\Delta\boldsymbol{U}
and
\Delta\Omega^{\parallel}
, we solve the “mismatch” problem

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\Delta\boldsymbol{U}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}=-\boldsymbol{m}

(51)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}=\partial_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\left(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{F}_{E}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau})\right)+\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\left(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{F}_{E}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)\right),

(52)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{F}_{E}=\boldsymbol{F}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{F}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{E},

(53)

is the total force in the Euler model. This problem, which we obtain by substituting (
50
) into the Kirchhoff model (
40
), gives a second order nonlocal BVP for
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
as a function of the inconsistency in the evolution of the tangent vector in the Euler formulation
\boldsymbol{m}(s)
. Because the Euler formulation does not enforce (
26
), the mismatch
\boldsymbol{m}
is nonzero in general. We therefore need the Lagrange multiplier
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
to correct for it and make the total Kirchhoff velocities
\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{U}_{E}+\Delta\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{E}+\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}
consistent with (
26
). This Lagrange multiplier can be split into two components: a perpendicular part
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\perp}
that contributes to the torque
\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, and a parallel part
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\parallel}
which preserves the inextensibility of the velocity
\Delta\boldsymbol{U}
. Because the constraint force
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{E}=T\boldsymbol{\tau}
in the Euler model is tangential, we have
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\perp}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\perp}
and
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\parallel}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\parallel}-T\boldsymbol{\tau}
, where
T(s)
is the tension in the Euler model.

In Appendix
E
, we use the asymptotic slender-body scalings
[
46
]

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\sim\log{\epsilon},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}\sim\log{\epsilon},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\sim\log{\epsilon},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\sim\epsilon^{-2},

(54)

to show that the velocity differences
\Delta\boldsymbol{U}
and
\Delta\Omega^{\parallel}
are small in the fiber interior. Specifically, we show that

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{s}\Delta\boldsymbol{U}\right\|/\left\|\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{U}_{E}\right\|\sim\epsilon^{2}\log{\epsilon}\ll 1,

(55)

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta\Omega^{\parallel}\right\|/\left\|\Omega_{E}^{\parallel}\right\|\sim\begin{cases}\epsilon^{4}\left(\log{\epsilon}\right)^{2}&\gamma>0\\
\epsilon^{2}\log{\epsilon}&\gamma=0\end{cases}

(56)

away from the endpoints, so that the difference between the Kirchhoff and Euler translational and rotational velocities is very small for sufficiently slender fibers.
In Appendix
E
, we also verify these asymptotic estimates numerically and discuss how they change at the endpoints.

3
Euler model with dynamics

In this section, we formulate the full set of dynamical equations for the Euler model of an inextensible filament in Stokes flow. These are the equations of Section
2.4
, augmented with a parameterization of the set of inextensible fiber velocities, and an adjoint equation for the constraint forces
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

3.1
Continuum formulation for inextensible fibers

As discussed in Section
2.4
, most approaches to inextensibility for the Euler model use a scalar Lagrange multiplier for tension
T(s)

[
22
]
. This is problematic because it creates a complicated auxiliary integro-differential equation for the tension that is difficult to solve with spectral methods. The approach we follow here
[
9
]
is detailed in
[
48
, Sec. 3]
. We enforce the velocity constraint (
46
) by restricting the fiber velocity to the space of inextensible motions using (
20
). Specifically, for free fibers we let

\boldsymbol{U}(s)=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)(s)=\boldsymbol{U}_{0}+\int_{0}^{s}\boldsymbol{\Omega}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{\tau}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime},

(57)

for an arbitrary angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(s)
, where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\left\{\boldsymbol{U}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right\}

3.1.1
Modifications for clamped ends

In this work, we will consider two kinds of filaments: those with two free ends, and those with a clamped end at

4
Spectral spatial discretization

In this section, we formulate a spectral spatial discretization of the Euler model. This is an extension of our work
[
48
]
, where we designed a numerical method for the case of fibers
without
twist elasticity. In that work, we
followed the idea of Shelley et al.
[
63
,
64
,
67
]
in using slender body theory for the mobility, which decouples the number of degrees of freedom from the slenderness
\epsilon
.
However, we left open a few issues which have plagued numerical methods for SBT for quite some time
[
67
,
51
]
, including the ill-posedness of the continuum equations on lengthscales less than
\epsilon

[
28
]
, and singularities at the endpoints for non-cylindrical fibers
[
48
, Sec. 2.1]
. As has been mentioned in Section
2.1
, our solution to both of these problems is to take the action of the mobility operator to be an
integral
of the RPY kernel along the fiber centerline.

In Section
4.2
and Appendix
G
we develop efficient quadrature schemes to evaluate the trans-trans, rot-trans, and trans-rot RPY integrals with on the order of ten points along the fiber centerline. For the short-ranged rot-rot mobility, we use the local drag formulas of Section
2.1.3
, which are sufficiently accurate for our purposes. Since we expect the fiber shapes (and velocity on the fiber centerline) to be smooth, we represent the fiber centerline as a Chebyshev interpolant, and evaluate the mobility and forces at Chebyshev collocation points. Forces and torques at these points must be computed with proper treatment of the boundary conditions. Our choice is to use (a modified form of) rectangular spectral collocation
[
21
,
6
]
, as we discuss in Section
4.3
.

In Section
4.4
, we show that the solution of the Euler problem
with twist
can be well-approximated with a spectral method, which drastically lowers the number of degrees of freedom relative to previous methods
[
62
,
40
]
. We demonstrate this in detail in a sequence of steps, first computing the eigenvalues of the discrete mobility matrix, then the accuracy of the forward quadratures, and finally the convergence of the solution to the static Euler problem.

4.1
Spectral discretization

Our spectral spatial discretization of the Euler equation (
62
) is based on our previous work
[
48
]
. To discretize the fiber centerline, we introduce an
N
-point type 1 (not including the endpoints) Chebyshev collocation grid
\{s_{p}\}
for
p=1,\dots N
. In the discrete setting, we use
\boldsymbol{X}(s)
for the Chebyshev interpolant approximating the fiber centerline.

In this paper, we modify the discretization of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
as matrices
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
from
[
48
]
to robustly simulate more curved fibers (see example in Section
5.3
). In
[
48
]
, we introduced an orthonormal frame
\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s),\boldsymbol{n}_{1}(s),\boldsymbol{n}_{2}(s)\right)
at each collocation point, then parameterized
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\perp}
in terms of
\boldsymbol{n}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{n}_{2}
. This removes the null space from
\boldsymbol{K}
, but for curved fibers it leads to aliasing problems because products of Chebyshev polynomials of degree
N
are aliased on an
N
-point grid. In Appendix
F
, we describe a discretization of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
which is based on removing the null space of
\boldsymbol{K}

numerically
by doing all computations on a grid of size
2N
, then downsampling. That is the discretization we use in this paper.

4.2
Slender-body quadrature for RPY mobilities

We will impose (
62
) in the strong sense on the Chebyshev collocation grid. Since
\boldsymbol{K}
gives the inextensible velocity at each point on the grid, we need to evaluate the action of the mobility operators
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tr}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}
at each point on the Chebyshev grid, giving discrete mobility matrices
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}},\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{rt}},\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tr}}
, and
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{rr}}
. We discuss how to do this in this section.

Our approach is to use the translational mobility operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}
given by the RPY integral (
7
). If the integral is computed to sufficient accuracy, then the eigenvalues of the discrete mobility matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}
are guaranteed to be positive since the RPY kernel is SPD
[
72
]
.
Note, however, that while the RPY kernel acting on
forces
is symmetric, the matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}
that acts on force densities is not symmetric, since quadrature is applied on one side but not the other.
While (
7
) is still a “first kind” integral operator, and therefore has eigenvalues that cluster around zero, unlike the “second kind” integral operator proposed in
[
5
]
to regularize SBT, we will demonstrate that the ill-conditioning of
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}
is
not
a problem in practice. Furthermore, unlike
[
5
]
, we develop a nearly-singular quadrature scheme so that the RPY integrals for smooth fibers can be evaluated to 3 digits of accuracy with
10-40
collocation points, regardless of the slenderness
\hat{\epsilon}
.

4.2.1
Translational mobility

We define the translational velocity of the fiber centerline due to a force density

using (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}(s)

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\boldsymbol{f}\right)(s)=\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{M}_{\text{tt}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{D(s)}\left(\mathbb{S}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)+\frac{2{\hat{a}}^{2}}{3}\mathbb{D}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\right)\boldsymbol{f}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\int_{D^{c}(s)}\left(\left(\dfrac{4}{3{\hat{a}}}-\dfrac{3R\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)}{8{\hat{a}}^{2}}\right)\boldsymbol{I}+\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)}{8{\hat{a}}^{2}}\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}.

Here we have used the definition (

(69)

For the integral of the Stokeslet in (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{(S)}=

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\mathbb{S}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{f}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\,ds^{\prime}

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{|s-s^{\prime}|}\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\left(\mathbb{S}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{f}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{|s-s^{\prime}|}\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{(\text{inner, S})}(s)+\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{\text{(int, S)}}(s)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad 8\pi\mu\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{(\text{inner,S})}(s)=

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\begin{cases}\log{\left(\dfrac{(L-s)s}{4{\hat{a}}^{2}}\right)}&2{\hat{a}}<s<L-2{\hat{a}}\\

(71)

A set of corresponding singularity subtraction steps is given for the doublet integral in (

). In both cases, we obtain nearly singular integrals which are by design smoother than the integral of the Stokeslet/doublet by itself. We then apply specialized, “slender-body” quadrature schemes to these integrals, as discussed in Appendix
G.1
. The main framework of these schemes was first proposed by Tornberg, Barnett, and af Klinteberg
[
66

1

and used by us previously for the finite part integral in SBT
[
48

The idea is to write the integral as the product of a smooth function times a singular function, expand the smooth function in a set of basis functions, and precompute the integrals of the basis functions times the singularity analytically. Using an adjoint method, the calculation of the Stokeslet and doublet integrals can then be reduced to
N

N

\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
. Since all of these calculations are similar to previously-published methods, we discuss them in Appendix
G

After discretizing the Stokeslet and doublet integrals, we are left with the integral over

in the third line of (

(with appropriate modifications at the endpoints), and use

4.4
Well-posedness and convergence
of the static problem

In this section, we study the convergence of our numerical method for the case of a static filament (solving for velocity at
t=0
) in three steps. First, we show that our discrete mobility matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}
in Section
4.2
is indeed positive definite for larger
\hat{\epsilon}
, in contrast to
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}
in SBT, which has negative eigenvalues for as few as 15 collocations points on the fiber centerline when
\hat{\epsilon}=10^{-2}
. In Appendix

, we show that our quadratures for the forward evaluations of
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}\boldsymbol{f}
,
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{rt}}\boldsymbol{f}
,
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tr}}n^{\parallel}
, and
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{rr}}n^{\parallel}
give spectral accuracy in the velocity due to the bending and twisting elastic force/torque, even for a fiber with relatively high curvature. In particular, we find that we can obtain 3 digits of accuracy in the velocities with about 32 collocation points. Finally, we solve for the velocity
\boldsymbol{U}
and constraint force
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
in the Euler method and examine their convergence. This is where our method hits a snag: if we maintain a cylindrical fiber shape, there is a physical singularity that develops at the fiber endpoints, where the force required to produce a uniform motion becomes very oscillatory

. This means that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
fails to converge pointwise, which calls into question the convergence of

\boldsymbol{\lambda}
(i.e., the moments of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}

1/\hat{\epsilon}
points in a second-order method.

5.3
Twirling to whirling instability

[

To initialize a small perturbation of a straight fiber, we set

Since the publication of

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we studied a single, inextensible filament that resists bending and twisting while immersed in a viscous fluid. Our focus was on adding twist elasticity to the model that we used in previous work
[
48
]
for inextensible filaments with bending resistance. We did this in two parts: first, we compared two possible models for twist elasticity in the presence of a fluid, which we called the Kirchhoff and Euler models. While the two models have the same elastic constituitive laws, in the Kirchhoff model the angular velocity of the material frame is constrained to be equal to the angular fluid velocity, while in the Euler model the perpendicular components of the angular fluid velocity are discarded. The contribution of the first part of this paper was to reformulate the Kirchhoff model in a form similar to the Euler model, and use this to show that these two models produce dynamics (parallel and perpendicular angular velocities) that differ by
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
in the fiber interior, where
\epsilon
is the fiber aspect ratio. We chose to utilize the Euler model in the remainder of the paper, since the extra degree of rotational freedom (parallel torque and parallel rotational velocity) can be easily incorporated into the formulation we introduced in
[
48
]
. To compute the additional force and parallel torque due to twist, we used the Bishop (twist-free) frame and evolved the angle of twist relative to that frame
[
8
]
.

We defined the hydrodynamic mobility operators for translation-translation, translation-rotation, rotation-translation, and rotation-rotation coupling in terms of integrals of the corresponding Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa (RPY) kernels, then evaluated these integrals both asymptotically and numerically. Our preference for trans-trans, trans-rot, and rot-trans was to use the slender body
quadrature
schemes described in Appendix
G
to evaluate the integrals; we showed that this removes the spurious negative eigenvalues that result when the trans-trans integral is evaluated asymptotically (as in slender body
theory
). The only remaining uncontrolled approximation in our mobility evaluation is to use local drag, and not the full RPY integral, for the rot-rot mobility. We did this because the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
accuracy of this approximation was sufficient for our purposes. If necessary, the special quadrature scheme we developed for the doublet (in the context of trans-trans mobility) could also be applied to the rot-rot kernel to account for the nonlocal rot-rot hydrodynamics to higher accuracy. Using the RPY kernels to account for the fiber thickness undoubtedly gives some error relative to the true three dimensional geometry. Nevertheless, the equations we wrote are intended as a mathematical
model
of a thin elastic rod in fluid, in much the same spirit as the Kirchhoff rod model itself, which is not derived by solving any three-dimensional elasticity problem but which aims to capture the essence of a thin rod’s elastic behavior in a one-dimensional self-consistent continuum model. In the same way, our formulation of the fluid-structure interaction equations for a thin rod in fluid does not exactly correspond to the solution of any 3D Stokes problem with an internal moving tubular boundary
[
36
]
, but instead we work with the variables of the Kirchhoff rod model and couple them to the fluid in a physically reasonable way.

We applied our numerical method to two common single-filament problems in which twist elasticity has been traditionally neglected and included, respectively. We considered a relaxing bent filament and showed that twist elasticity has a negligible
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
effect, which justifies the assumption in many studies
[
51
,
48
]
to neglect it. By contrast, the spinning filament problem requires finite twist elasticity to drive the filament into an unstable trajectory for spinning frequencies
\omega>\omega_{c}
. We showed that our numerical method reproduces exactly the semi-numerical prediction of
[
75
]
for
\omega_{c}
when using ellipsoidally-tapered filaments with local drag, and that the shape of the filament and the addition of nonlocal trans-trans hydrodynamics modify the critical frequency by at most 5%
[
70
]
. We showed that rot-trans coupling is more significant, lowering the frequency by another 15% to about 80% of the value predicted for ellipsoidally-tapered local drag, which explains about half of the deviation observed experimentally
[
13
]
.

Above the critical frequency
\omega_{c}
, the fiber transitions to a steady crankshafting rotation in the shape of a shepherd’s crook (overwhirling)
[
41
]
. While we found this state to be stable up to the highest frequencies we investigated, getting to that state from an initially straight filament requires complex dynamics that can lead to self-intersections, suggesting that in practice plectonemes may be observed instead
[
13
]
.

Our numerical method cannot handle self intersections, both because of the quadrature scheme and because we do not include lubrication, frictional, or steric forces
[
10
]
.

Despite the length and complexity of this paper, we only considered a single filament! Yet it is quite interesting how many modeling and numerical choices we had to make for this seemingly simple problem. For instance, we chose
not
to regularize the shape of the filament, keeping the radius constant all the way up to the endpoints. Because we use integrals of the RPY tensor, the mobility for constant regularized radius
{\hat{a}}

is
well-defined at the fiber endpoints, although its lack of smoothness causes slow convergence in our spectral method. This is in contrast to SBT, in which the local drag coefficient is not well-defined for cylindrical fibers, and some kind of ellipsoidally-tapered radius function has to be assumed
[
48
, Sec. 2.1]
. Future work could develop a hybrid approach that uses the RPY kernel for
unequally-sized spheres

[
77
]
to simulate fibers with radius functions that decay smoothly to zero at the endpoints. This should both smooth out the mobility and keep the eigenvalues of the trans-trans mobility away from zero. The integrals involved are more complex than those for equally sized spheres because the integrand includes the radius function
{\hat{a}}(s)
. Still, because the singularities (Stokeslet, doublet, rotlet, etc.) in the polydisperse integral kernels are unchanged, it should be possible to account for non-constant
{\hat{a}}(s)
using the slender body quadrature schemes developed here.

Another choice we made for the single filament was to stick with a first kind integral equation for the force-velocity relationship, i.e., the trans-trans mobility. While this yielded sufficient accuracy for our purposes, the eigenvalues of the first kind mobility cluster around zero, leading to ill-defined solutions near the fiber endpoints. Of course, one argument around this (which we made in
[
48
]
) is that both the ill-conditioning and negative eigenvalues of the SBT mobility occur when
N\gtrsim 1/\hat{\epsilon}
, which is too large for slender body approaches to be efficient. Still, a better-conditioned formulation (for trans-trans mobility) would be to use a second-kind integral equation of the form given in
[
5
, Eq. (4)]
. To enable rapid dynamic simulation of the model in
[
5
]
, it is necessary to develop slender body quadratures to evaluate integrals of regularized Stokeslets and doublets along the centerline, as we did for the first kind RPY mobility in Appendix
G
.

An important future extension of this work is to consider the dynamics of multiple filaments in a suspension with twist, as we did without twist in
[
48
,
49
]
. The mobility of Section
2
directly generalizes to multiple filaments, since the same RPY kernel can be used between distinct filaments and a filament and itself. The challenge lies in efficiently numerically computing the solution. Focusing on the static case first, we need to develop an efficient numerical method to sum the rot-trans and trans-rot hydrodynamic interactions over many bodies, since our previous work
[
48
, Sec. 4]
already treated the trans-trans kernel with a combination of the spectral Ewald method
[
23
]
(for well-separated fibers)
6

A related even greater challenge for the future is to account for thermal fluctuations of elastic filaments in spectral approaches.

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\sim\log{\epsilon}

\Delta\Omega^{\parallel}\sim\epsilon^{2}\left(\log{\epsilon}\right)^{2}
. To non-dimensionalize this, we need to estimate the parallel rotational velocity
\Omega^{\parallel}_{E}
defined in (
47
). When there is no twist, there is no parallel torque, so
\Omega^{\parallel}_{E}=\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{F}_{E}\right)\sim\log{\epsilon}
, and the relative difference should be
\left\|\Delta\Omega^{\parallel}\right\|/\left\|\Omega_{E}^{\parallel}\right\|\sim\epsilon^{2}\log{\epsilon}
. When there is twist, the disparity is even larger, since
\Omega^{\parallel}_{E}\sim\epsilon^{-2}
, which explains the two different cases in (
56
).

Summarizing, the mismatch
\boldsymbol{m}
in (
51
) is compensated for by
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\perp}
, which grows as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, even as
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
decays. These are the components of the velocity that are discarded in the Euler model, so it does not matter if they differ from those of the Kirchhoff model. That said, because
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
must be zero at the endpoints by the boundary conditions (
31
), the perpendicular torques cannot generate angular velocity there, and the term
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\perp}
cannot compensate for the mismatch
\boldsymbol{m}
at the endpoints. Instead, the mismatch there has to be compensated for by terms involving
\partial_{s}\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, meaning that the derivative of
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
must be large near the boundaries, as we see in Fig
10
. This makes a spectral method a poor choice for the Kirchhoff rod formulation, and is the reason why we develop a second-order method to solve the Kirchhoff equations next.

E.2
Second-order numerical method

Following the method outlined in
[
62
]
, we introduce a number of links
N
and set
\Delta s=L/N
. The discretization of the fiber is
staggered
: the “nodes” are located at
s_{j}=j\Delta s
, where
j=0,\dots N
, so that there are actually
N+1
nodes. The “links” are centered on
s_{j+1/2}=(j+1/2)\Delta s
, where
j=0,\dots,N-1
, so that there are
N
links.

E.2.1
Euler model

In our second-order discretization of the Euler problem (
62
), we define
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}
, and
\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}
at link centers
(j+1/2)\Delta s
, for
j=0,\dots N-1
. All mobility operators in (
7
) and (
10
) (including the rot-rot mobility) are then replaced with the obvious discretizations of the integrals which sum over the links with weight
\Delta s
. For example,

\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{tt}}\boldsymbol{f}\right)(s_{i+1/2})\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{M}_{\text{tt}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i+1/2},\boldsymbol{X}_{j+1/2}\right)\boldsymbol{f}_{j+1/2}\Delta s:=\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}\boldsymbol{f}\right)_{i+1/2}.

(E.3)

Note that this discretization does not correspond to a more common discretization of an inextensible bead-link polymer chain, which would place forces on
N+1
blobs connected by
N
inextensible links, e.g.,
[
52
]
. We use the staggered discretization instead for a more direct comparison to the Kirchhoff model.

To complete the second-order discretization of (
62
), we have to define the matrices
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
. In the second-order case, these matrices are exact transposes of each other, so we only have to define one of them. The matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
is a midpoint discretization of the continuum operator (
57
), with action given by

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)_{j+1/2}=\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 2.15277pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{U}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}

\displaystyle\Delta s\left(\alpha_{1,k+1/2}\boldsymbol{n}_{1,k+1/2}+\alpha_{2,k+1/2}\boldsymbol{n}_{2,k+1/2}\right)

(E.4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta s}{2}\left(\alpha_{1,j+1/2}\boldsymbol{n}_{1,j+1/2}+\alpha_{2,j+1/2}\boldsymbol{n}_{2,j+1/2}\right).

The midpoint quadrature assigns a weight of
\Delta s
to all prior nodes
1/2,3/2,\dots j-1/2
, and then a weight of
\Delta s/2
to the current node
j+1/2
. The matrix
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
is the transpose of
\boldsymbol{K}
.

Finally, the parallel angular velocity (
64
) is obtained at the link centers by dotting the full angular velocity,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rt}}\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)
(computed at the link centers using the forces computing at the link centers), with the tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{\tau}
, which are also defined at the link centers.

E.2.2
Kirchhoff model

The discretization of the Kirchhoff model, which we take from
[
62
]
, is more complicated because the forces
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
are defined at the
nodes
, and not the link centers. To communicate between the nodes and links, we let
\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{lb}}
be the
N\times(N+1)
differentiation matrix, which gives derivates at the links from values at the nodes. We also define
\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{bl}}
as the
(N-1)\times N
differentiation matrix, which gives derivates at interior nodes from values at the link centers. Likewise the
N\times(N+1)
averaging matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{lb}}
gives averages at links from nodes, and the
(N-1)\times N
averaging matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{bl}}
gives averages at interior nodes from links. This gives the
(N-1)\times(N+1)
system of equations discretizing (
51
)

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{bl}}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{lb}}+\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tr}}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{lb}}\right)+\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{bl}}\boldsymbol{C}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{rt}}\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{lb}}+\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{rr}}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{lb}}\right)\right]\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=-\boldsymbol{m}

(E.5)

\displaystyle=-\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{bl}}\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tt}}+\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{bl}}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{rt}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{E}\right)-\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{bl}}\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{tr}}+\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{bl}}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{rr}}\right)\left(n^{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)

(E.6)

which we solve for
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
at the
N-1

interior blobs
away from the fiber boundaries. Because
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is defined at
N+1
points, the other two equations are the BCs
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{j}=\boldsymbol{0}
for
j=0,N
, see (
31
). The matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
represents cross products with
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{j+1/2}=\boldsymbol{\tau}\left((j+1/2)\Delta s\right)
, where in the second-order method
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{X}
is evaluated analytically at the links and
\left(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{X}\right)_{j+1/2}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{j+1/2}\times\boldsymbol{X}_{j+1/2}
. Notice that the calculation of the mismatch (
E.6
) requires calculation of the Euler quantities
\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}
and
\boldsymbol{f}^{(\gamma)}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{E}
at the
N
links, which we first obtain by solving the Euler problem to second order accuracy using the method in Section
E.2.1
.

Appendix F
Discretization of kinematic operators

In this appendix, we discuss the spectral discretization of the kinematic operators (
57
) and (
60
) on a type 1 Chebyshev grid of size
N
. For simplicity of notation, we will consider
s=0
to be a clamped end so that
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. The continuum operators
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
(c.f. (
57
) and (
60
)) in this case are defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}(s)=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\boldsymbol{\Omega}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{\tau}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime},

(F.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}:=\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\int_{s}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(F.2)

These operators must be discretized with care because of nonlinearities, which lead to aliasing on the spectral collocation grid. Our approach in this paper, which is somewhat different from what we have done previously
[
48
, Sec. 4.1.2]
, is to discretize these operators on a type 1 Chebyshev grid of size
2N
, and then appropriately upsample and downsample to a grid of size
N
. This is in contrast to
[
48
]
, where we set
\Omega^{\parallel}(s)=0
on the
N
point grid.

As in
[
48
, Sec. 4.1.2]
, we let
\boldsymbol{J}
be a matrix such that
\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is an inextensible motion
\int_{0}^{s}\boldsymbol{\Omega}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{\tau}(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}
on a grid of size
2N
. Unlike
[
48
, Sec. 4.1.2]
, however, we will use
\boldsymbol{J}
to project off extensible motions on the
2N
grid, rather than trying to write an explicit basis for inextensible motions on the
N
point grid. To define
\boldsymbol{J}
, we let
\boldsymbol{C}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
be the matrix that computes cross products with
\boldsymbol{\tau}
on the
2N
grid, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\boldsymbol{x}
, with
\boldsymbol{C}^{T}=-\boldsymbol{C}
. We use the pseudoinverse of the Chebyshev differentiation matrix
[
68
, c. 6]
for integration, with
\boldsymbol{D}^{\dagger}_{0}
denoting integration from 0 to
s
, which has
L^{2}
adjoint
\left(\boldsymbol{D}^{\dagger}_{0}\right)^{*}=-\boldsymbol{D}^{\dagger}_{L}
(integration from
s
to
L
). The discretization of
\boldsymbol{J}
and its
L^{2}
adjoint
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
on a grid of size
2N
can then be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{D}_{0}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{C},\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{J}^{*}=\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{D}_{L}^{\dagger}.

(F.3)

Here
\boldsymbol{J}
is a
6N\times 6N
matrix that discretizes (
F.1
), and
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
is a
6N\times 6N
matrix that discretizes (
F.2
).

To write a discretization on the
N
point grid, we introduce an extension matrix
\boldsymbol{E}
and restriction matrix
\boldsymbol{R}
. The extension matrix is well defined: to evaluate
\boldsymbol{E}f_{N}
, where
f_{N}
is a polynomial of degree
N-1
on the
N
point grid, we simply evaluate
f
on the grid of size
2N
. Following
[
48
, Eq. (62)]
, we let the restriction matrix
\boldsymbol{R}
be the matrix that gives the best possible (in the
L^{2}
sense) polynomial approximation of degree
N-1
on the
N
grid to a polynomial of degree
2N-1
on the
2N
point grid, i.e., if
f_{2N}
is a function on the
2N
point grid, we want to choose
\boldsymbol{R}
such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{R}f_{2N}-f_{2N}\right\|_{L^{2}}
is minimized. This can be accomplished by setting

\boldsymbol{R}=\left(\boldsymbol{E}^{T}\boldsymbol{W}_{2N}\boldsymbol{E}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{E}^{T}\boldsymbol{W}_{2N},

(F.4)

where
\boldsymbol{W}_{2N}
is a diagonal matrix of Clenshaw-Curtis weights on the
2N
point grid. We do this for both
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
to obtain the discretizations of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
,

\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{E},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{K}^{*}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\boldsymbol{E}

(F.5)

where
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
are both
3N\times 3N
matrices. It is straightforward to modify
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
to allow a nonzero
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
for a free end.

To update the fiber tangent vectors from the velocity
\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we follow (
63
). This update to the tangent vectors projects off any parallel components in
\boldsymbol{U}_{s}
, which come from numerical discretization artifacts. Therefore, the parallel component of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
on the
N
point grid does not affect the dynamics; it is included merely to lessen aliasing artifacts.

As discussed in Section
4.1
, the main difference between our discretization here and that of
[
48
, Sec. 4.1.2]
is the treatment of the null space of the continuum operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
. Because we project off the null space of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
on a grid of size
2N
and downsample the result, the rank of the matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
(and
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
) is a function of the fiber curvature. For a rigid fiber, the rank of
\boldsymbol{K}
is
2N+1
, which is the same as when we directly write a basis for inextensible motions on an
N
point grid. For curved fibers, the rank is larger because inextensible motions are aliased to extensible ones on the
N
point grid. For the fiber (
87
) with
q=7
for instance, for large
N
we observe
2N+23
nonzero singular values of
\boldsymbol{K}
, so there are 22 additional positive singular values on top of the
2N+1
nonzero singular values for a rigid fiber. Hence, typically the matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
does not have full rank, so the Schur complement
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}
that we use to solve the saddle point system (
62
) is singular. We therefore use the pseudo-inverse of
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}
to solve the saddle point system (
pinv
function in Matlab with default tolerance).

Appendix G
Nearly singular quadrature

In this appendix, we discuss the nearly singular quadrature scheme we use to evaluate the integrals of the RPY kernels in Section
4.2
to spectral accuracy. The idea behind this scheme has already been published in
[
1
,
66
,
48
]
, but in those works the nearly-singular integral is actually a singular finite part integral because the domain is
[0,L]
instead of
D(s)
as defined in (
69
). This section presents a review of the quadrature scheme and how we modify it when the domain changes. We do this sequentially for the Stokeslet integral, doublet integral, and rotlet integral.

G.1
Stokeslet integral

For the Stokeslet integral (
70
), the nearly-singular integral that we must evaluate is the Stokeslet minus the leading order singularity,

\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{\text{(int, S)}}(s)=\int_{D(s)}\left(\mathbb{S}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{f}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{|s-s^{\prime}|}\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right)\,ds^{\prime},

(G.1)

which has the same integrand as the finite part integral in slender body theory, but with the different domain of integration

D(s)=\begin{cases}\left(0,s-2{\hat{a}}\right)\cup\left(s+2{\hat{a}},L\right)&2{\hat{a}}\leq s\leq L-2{\hat{a}}\\
\left(s+2{\hat{a}},L\right)&s<2{\hat{a}}\\
\left(0,s-2{\hat{a}}\right)&s>L-2{\hat{a}}\end{cases}.

(G.2)

We employ a singularity subtraction scheme that ensures that the second term in the integral (
G.1
) cancels the leading order
1/|s^{\prime}-s|
singularity in the first. The next singularity is
\text{sign}(s^{\prime}-s)
, which means that the near singular integral (
G.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{\text{(int, S)}}(s)=\int_{D(s)}\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}(s,s^{\prime})\frac{s^{\prime}-s}{|s^{\prime}-s|}\,ds^{\prime}=\frac{L}{2}\int_{D^{\prime}(\eta)}\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\frac{\eta^{\prime}-\eta}{|\eta^{\prime}-\eta|}\,d\eta^{\prime},\qquad\text{where}

(G.3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}(s,s^{\prime})=\left(\mathbb{S}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{f}\left(s^{\prime}\right)|s^{\prime}-s|-\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right)\frac{1}{s^{\prime}-s}

is a smooth function,
\eta=-1+2s/L
is a rescaled arclength coordinate on
[-1,1]
, and
D^{\prime}(\eta)=[0,\eta_{\ell}]\cup[\eta_{h},L]
is defined from
D(s)
. The function
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}
is nonsingular at
s=s^{\prime}
with the finite limit

\lim_{s^{\prime}\to s}\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}(s,s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)+\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)+\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right).

(G.4)

Furthermore,
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}
is smooth, so we can express it in a truncated Chebyshev series on
[-1,1]
,

\frac{L}{2}\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\approx\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{c}_{k}(\eta)T_{k}(\eta^{\prime}),

(G.5)

where
\boldsymbol{c}_{k}
is a vector of 3 coefficients for each
\eta
. Substituting the Chebyshev expansion (
G.5
) into the integrand (
G.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{\text{(int, S)}}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{c}_{k}(\eta)\int_{D^{\prime}(\eta)}T_{k}\left(\eta^{\prime}\right)\frac{\eta^{\prime}-\eta}{|\eta^{\prime}-\eta|}\,d\eta^{\prime}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{c}_{k}(\eta)q^{(S)}_{k}(\eta)=\boldsymbol{c}^{T}(\eta)\boldsymbol{q}^{(S)}(\eta),

(G.6)

\displaystyle\text{where}\qquad q_{k}^{(S)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{D^{\prime}(\eta)}T_{k}(\eta^{\prime})\frac{\eta^{\prime}-\eta}{|\eta^{\prime}-\eta|}\,d\eta^{\prime}=-\int_{-1}^{\eta_{\ell}}T_{k}(\eta^{\prime})\,d\eta^{\prime}+\int_{\eta_{h}}^{L}T_{k}(\eta^{\prime})\,d\eta^{\prime},

(G.7)

are integrals that can be precomputed to high accuracy for each
\eta
on the Chebyshev collocation grid. The precomputed integrals in (
G.7
) can then be used in an adjoint method to accelerate the repeated evaluation of (
G.6
). Specifically, the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}(\eta)
for each
\eta
can be written as
\boldsymbol{c}(\eta)=\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
, where
\boldsymbol{V}
is the matrix that maps coefficients of the basis functions to values on the collocation grid. Then
\boldsymbol{c}^{T}(\eta)=\boldsymbol{g}^{T}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\boldsymbol{V}^{-T}
, and
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{\text{(int, S)}}(\eta)=\boldsymbol{g}^{T}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\boldsymbol{V}^{-T}\boldsymbol{q}^{(S)}(\eta)
. The matrix vector product
\boldsymbol{V}^{-T}\boldsymbol{q}^{(S)}(\eta)
can then be precomputed for each
\eta
, and so at each time step all that is needed to compute the integral (
G.1
) for each
\eta
are the values of
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tt}}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
on the collocation grid for
\eta^{\prime}
being another collocation point. Evaluating
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{\text{(int, S)}}
therefore has a cost of
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{2}\right)
.

We note that our use of Chebyshev polynomials differs from the approach of
[
66
,
48
]
, which uses a monomial basis for the expansion (
G.5
). Although the integrals (
G.7
) with monomials can be computed analytically, using the adjoint method with monomial coefficients requires inverting the Vandermonde matrix
\boldsymbol{V}
, which is too ill-conditioned with
N\gtrsim 40
points in double precision. The Chebyshev expansion is an improvement in the sense that computing coefficients from values on the Chebyshev grid is a well-conditioned problem, and so the technique we use to evaluate finite part integrals does not limit the number of points on the fiber or require the use of panels (composite quadrature).

G.2
Doublet integral

The next part of the translational mobility (
68
) is the integral of the doublet kernel. We begin by applying the same singularity subtraction technique we used in (
70
) for the Stokeslet integral

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{(D)}(s)=

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\mathbb{D}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{f}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\,ds^{\prime}

(G.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{I}-3\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{|s^{\prime}-s|^{3}}\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\left(\mathbb{D}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{f}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{I}-3\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{|s^{\prime}-s|^{3}}\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{(\text{inner, D})}(s)+\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{\text{(int, D)}}(s).

The term
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{(\text{inner, D})}(s)
is given for all
s
by

\displaystyle 8\pi\mu\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{(\text{inner,D})}(s)=\frac{2}{3}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-3\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{4}-\dfrac{{\hat{a}}^{2}}{2s^{2}}-\dfrac{{\hat{a}}^{2}}{2(L-s)^{2}}&2{\hat{a}}<s<L-2{\hat{a}}\\
\dfrac{1}{8}-\dfrac{{\hat{a}}^{2}}{2(L-s)^{2}}&s\leq 2{\hat{a}}\\
\dfrac{1}{8}-\dfrac{{\hat{a}}^{2}}{2s^{2}}&s\geq L-2{\hat{a}}\end{cases}

(G.9)

The nearly-singular doublet integral is handled in exactly the same way as the Stokeslet one, but the singular behavior is different, namely,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{f}}^{\text{(int, D)}}(s)=\int_{D(s)}\frac{(s^{\prime}-s)}{|s^{\prime}-s|^{3}}\,\boldsymbol{g}_{D}(s^{\prime},s)\,ds^{\prime},

\displaystyle\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{g}_{D}(s^{\prime},s)=\left(\mathbb{D}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{f}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{I}-3\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{|s^{\prime}-s|^{3}}\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right)\frac{|s^{\prime}-s|^{3}}{(s^{\prime}-s)}

(G.10)

is a smooth function with the finite limit

\lim_{s^{\prime}\rightarrow s}\boldsymbol{g}_{D}(s^{\prime},s)=\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{I}-3\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}(s)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)+\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right).

(G.11)

If we expand
\boldsymbol{g}_{D}
in terms of Chebyshev polynomials, the efficient evaluation of (
G.10
) requires precomputing integrals of the form

q_{k}^{\text{(D)}}(\eta)=\int_{D^{\prime}(\eta)}\frac{(\eta^{\prime}-\eta)}{|\eta^{\prime}-\eta|^{3}}T_{k}\left(\eta^{\prime}\right)\,d\eta^{\prime},

(G.12)

for all
\eta
on the Chebyshev grid. Notice that the integrals (
G.12
) are not defined if
\eta^{\prime}=\eta
is included in the integration domain, but
D^{\prime}(\eta)
does
not
contain
\eta
.

G.3
Rot-trans mobility

The long-range integral for the rot-trans mobility (
72
) is given by an integral of the rotlet kernel over the domain
D(s)
. Using singularity subtraction, we compute this integral as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{f}}^{\text{(R)}}(s)=

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{f}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{R}(s^{\prime})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{R(s^{\prime})^{3}}ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\left(\frac{\left(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{2|s-s^{\prime}|}\right)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\left(\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{f}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{R}(s^{\prime})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{R(s^{\prime})^{3}}-\frac{\left(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{2|s-s^{\prime}|}\right)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{f}}^{(\text{inner, R})}(s)+\Omega_{\text{f}}^{(\text{int, R})}(s),

(G.13)

where
\boldsymbol{R}(s^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{X}(s)-\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})
and
R=\left\|\boldsymbol{R}\right\|
. Since the leading order singularity in
\Omega_{\text{f}}^{(\text{inner, R})}
of
1/|s-s^{\prime}|
is the same as that of the Stokeslet, the expression for
\Omega_{\text{f}}^{(\text{inner, R})}
is also given by (
71
), but with
\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)
replaced by
1/2\left(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\boldsymbol{f}(s)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)=1/2\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(s)
.

The nearly singular integral
\Omega_{\text{f}}^{(\text{int, R})}(s)
in (
G.13
) can then be put in the form (
G.3
) (with scalar
g_{\text{rt}}(s,s^{\prime})
) by setting

g_{\text{rt}}(s,s^{\prime})=\left(\frac{|s-s^{\prime}|}{R(s^{\prime})^{3}}\left(\boldsymbol{R}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(s^{\prime})-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(s)}{2}\right)\frac{1}{s^{\prime}-s},

(G.14)

which has the finite limit

\lim_{s^{\prime}\rightarrow s}g_{\text{rt}}(s,s^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{f}(s)\cdot\left(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)-\frac{1}{6}\boldsymbol{f}(s)\cdot\left(\partial_{s}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(s)\times\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right).

(G.15)

Now the scalar function
g_{\text{rt}}(s,s^{\prime})
can be expanded in a Chebyshev series as in (
G.6
), and the precomputed integrals
q^{(S)}_{k}(\eta)
in (
G.7
) used once again to evaluate the second integral in (
G.13
).

G.4
Trans-rot mobility

For the trans-rot mobility (
74
), the singularity subtraction approach is to set

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{n}}^{(\text{R})}(s)=

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{R}(s^{\prime})\right)n^{\parallel}(s^{\prime})}{R(s^{\prime})^{3}}ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\left(\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)n^{\parallel}(s)}{2|s-s^{\prime}|}\right)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{D(s)}\left(\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{R}(s^{\prime})\right)n^{\parallel}(s^{\prime})}{R(s^{\prime})^{3}}-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)n^{\parallel}(s)}{2|s-s^{\prime}|}\right)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{n}}^{(\text{inner, R})}(s)+\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{n}}^{(\text{int, R})}(s).

(G.16)

where
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{n}}^{(\text{inner, R})}
is given by (
71
), but with
\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)\boldsymbol{f}(s)
replaced by
1/2\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)n^{\parallel}(s)
. The nearly singular integral
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{n}}^{(\text{int, R})}(s)
can then be put in the form (
G.3
) by setting

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tr}}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{|s-s^{\prime}|}{R(s^{\prime})^{3}}\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}(s^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{R}(s^{\prime})\right)n^{\parallel}(s^{\prime})-\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)}{2}\right)n^{\parallel}(s)\right)\frac{1}{s^{\prime}-s},

(G.17)

where

\displaystyle\lim_{s^{\prime}\rightarrow s}\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{tr}}(s,s^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{s}n^{\parallel}(s)\left(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\times\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right)-\frac{1}{3}n^{\parallel}(s)\left(\partial_{s}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(s)\times\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\right).

(G.18)

G.5
Rot-rot mobility

Although we use asymptotic evaluation for the rot-rot mobility as discussed in Section
2.1.3
, it is straightforward to derive a nearly singular quadrature scheme to evaluate the RPY integral for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{rr}}
. Substituting the tensor
\mathbb{M}_{\text{rr}}
from (
A.4
) into the integral (
10
) and using the approximation
\left\|\boldsymbol{X}(s)-\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right\|\approx s-s^{\prime}
when
s-s^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}({\hat{a}})
, the mobility is given by

\displaystyle 8\pi\mu\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{n}}(s)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{D(s)}\mathbb{D}\left(\boldsymbol{X}(s),\boldsymbol{X}(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{n}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}

(G.19)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{{\hat{a}}^{3}}\int_{D^{c}(s)}\left(\left(1-\frac{27R(s^{\prime})}{32{\hat{a}}}+\frac{5R(s^{\prime})^{3}}{64{\hat{a}}^{3}}\right)\boldsymbol{I}+\left(\frac{9}{32{\hat{a}}R(s^{\prime})}-\frac{3R(s^{\prime})}{64{\hat{a}}^{3}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}\right)(s^{\prime})\right)\boldsymbol{n}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}.

The first of these integrals can be evaluated to high accuracy using the doublet scheme of Section
G.2
, while the second integral can be computed using direct Gauss-Legendre quadrature on
D^{c}(s)
(split into two pieces). The parallel angular velocity
\Psi_{\text{n}}^{\parallel}
can then be obtained by taking the inner product of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{n}}
with
\boldsymbol{\tau}
.
