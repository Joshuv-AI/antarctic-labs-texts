1.
Introduction

The problem of triangle count deviations in the random graph
G(n,p)
has been extensively studied in recent decades. We give an overview of the literature in Section
1.2
. The majority of results focus on a particular range of deviations, such as large deviations (of the order of the mean), whereas we shall prove results which hold across essentially the whole range of possible deviations.

One may also consider these problems in the
G(n,m)
random graph model, in which the random graph
G\sim G(n,m)
is selected uniformly from all graphs with
n
vertices and
m
edges. In conceptual terms, the advantage of working in the
G(n,m)
model is that we have removed one potential cause of deviations – the variation in the number of edges.

There are also practical reasons to prefer the
G(n,m)
model – we may generate
G(n,m)
by revealing one edge at a time, for a fixed number of steps
m
, and this is particularly well suited to martingale techniques. Furthermore, the results proved in
G(n,m)
may be easily transferred to results in
G(n,p)
.

Let us begin then with a discussion of triangle count deviations in
G(n,m)
. After we have stated our results in this setting we return in Section
1.2
to the
G(n,p)
setting.

1.1.
Our results for triangle counts in
G(n,m)

We use the notation
N:=\binom{n}{2}
and set
t:=m/N
. Note that
t
is the proportion of edges which are present in
G_{m}\sim G(n,m)
. We use the falling factorial notation
(n)_{k}=n(n-1)\dots(n-k+1)
.

We remark that our approach is similar to that Goldschmidt, Scott and the third author
[
20
]
. However, we stress that we introduce new ideas and techniques which are needed to extend the results proved in
[
20
]
to a much wider range of densities.

We let
N_{\triangle}(G_{m})
denote the number of isomorphic copies of the triangle in
G_{m}
. For example
N_{\triangle}(K_{4})=24
. We set

L_{\triangle}(m)\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\left[N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\right]\,=\,\frac{(m)_{3}(n)_{3}}{(N)_{3}}\,,

and so

D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,:=\,N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,-\,L_{\triangle}(m)

is the deviation of this triangle count from its mean.

Our main result in the
G(n,m)
model will be to determine the rate associated with deviations up to a constant multiplicative factor across essentially the whole range of deviations, for all densities
t\gg n^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}
. That is, given a sequence
a=a_{n}
with
t^{3/2}n^{3/2}\sqrt{\log{n}}\,\ll\,a_{n}\,\ll\,t^{3/2}n^{3}
, we find a sequence
b=b_{n}
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\triangle}(G_{m})>a\right)=\exp(-\Theta(b))
.

In fact, we find that it is more intuitive to state these results in terms of the deviation
a
which has probability
\exp(-b)
. It will be clear that this is equivalent. Let
\scalebox{0.9}{{DEV}}_{\triangle}(b,t)
denote the triangle count deviation which has probability only
e^{-b}
. That is,
\scalebox{0.9}{{DEV}}_{\triangle}(b,t)
is the minimal value of
a
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,>\,\mathbb{E}\left[N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\right]\,+\,a\right)\,\leqslant\,e^{-b}\,.

It is said that a picture is worth a thousand words. So let us present a figure first, and then state our result more formally. In particular the figure will highlight the four different regimes which correspond to different potential “causes” of the triangle count deviation.

We may parameterise the deviation
a
as
\delta t^{3}n^{3}
, which is practically
\delta
times the mean. The figure considers densities
t
of the form
t=n^{\gamma}
, and
\delta
of the form
\delta=n^{\theta}
. For each
\gamma\in(-1/2,0)
we obtain results for deviations between the order of magnitude of the standard deviation (order
t^{3/2}n^{3/2}
) and the order of magnitude of the largest possible deviation (order
t^{3/2}n^{3}
). This explains the interval of values of
\theta\in(-3/2-3\gamma/2,-3\gamma/2)
.

Let us now discuss each of the four possible causes of deviations. Here and throughout let
\ell:=\log(1/t)
, the natural logarithm of
1/t
.

Pure good luck (normal):
The proof is based on a martingale related to the creation of triangles during the process (of adding edges). It is very likely that the sum of the conditional variances of the increments of the martingale is of the order of magnitude
t^{3}n^{3}
. It will follow (using Freedman’s inequalities) that a deviation of order

\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t)\,:=\,b^{1/2}t^{3/2}n^{3/2}

has probability
e^{-b}
. This behaviour is an extension of the central limit theorem, and the behaviour of deviations of the order of the standard deviation. For this reason we call it the “normal” regime.

A star:
Observe that a vertex with particularly large degree,
d
, is likely to be contained in
\Theta(d^{2}t)
triangles. And so a star with degree
d\gg tn
“causes”
\Theta(d^{2}t)
triangles, and occurs with probability approximately
t^{d}=e^{-d\ell}
. Clearly this only applies for degrees
d<n
. We see that this may cause a deviation of order

\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}(b,t)\,:=\,\frac{b^{2}t}{\ell^{2}}\,1_{b\leqslant n\ell}

with probability
e^{-b}
.

A hub:
The hub is the natural extension of the star once it has reached full degree. Observe that
k
vertices with degree of order
n
will be involved in
\Theta(ktn^{2})
triangles, and this occurs with probability approximately
t^{kn}=e^{-kn\ell}
. We see that this may cause a deviation of order

\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t)\,:=\,\frac{btn}{\ell}\,1_{b\geqslant n\ell}

with probability
e^{-b}
.

A clique:
A clique of
k
vertices creates
\Theta(k^{3})
triangles, and occurs with probability approximately
t^{k^{2}}=e^{-k^{2}\ell}
. We see that this may cause a deviation of order

\scalebox{0.8}{{CLIQUE}}(b,t)\,:=\,\frac{b^{3/2}}{\ell^{3/2}}

with probability
e^{-b}
.

Let
M(b,t)
be defined to be the maximum of these four,

(1.1)

M(b,t)\,=\,\max\{\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{CLIQUE}}(b,t)\}\,.

Our main theorem for the
G(n,m)
model states that the deviation
\scalebox{0.9}{{DEV}}_{\triangle}(b,t)
which has probability
e^{-b}
is given by
M(b,t)
, up to a multiplicative constant, across a large range of
t
and
b
. Note we restrict
t
to be at most
1/2
, even though the results certainly hold in all cases where the density is bounded away from
1
, see
[
20
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist absolute constants
c,C
such that the following holds.
For all
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
3\log{n}\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
we have

cM(b,t)\,\leqslant\,\scalebox{0.9}{{DEV}}_{\triangle}(b,t)\,\leqslant\,CM(b,t)\,.

Remark 1.2
.

The behaviour of deviations is likely to be different in sparse random graphs, with
t\ll n^{-1/2}
, as in this case most edges are not in any triangles. This is the so called Poisson regime. See
[
21
]
for large deviation results in this regime and
[
19
]
for results in the even sparser regime with constant average degree. It seems likely that our result should extend down to
t=Cn^{-1/2}
. The complication is that our results are based on the behaviour of codegrees. As the expected codegree is around
t^{2}n
it becomes more difficult to prove concentration results when
t^{2}n
is only a constant.

. Let us define

Corollary 1.3
.

There exist absolute constants
c,C
such that the following holds. For all
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
Ct^{3/2}n^{3/2}\sqrt{\log{n}}\leqslant a\leqslant ct^{3/2}n^{3}
, we have

\exp(-Cr(t,a))\,\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,>\,a\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-cr(t,a))\,.

We also mention that our methods give some results for the lower tail. When discussing the lower tail it is more difficult to talk about “causes” of deviations. Although some progress has been made in this direction by Neeman, Radin and Sadun
[
37
]
. In the dense setting, with
t
a constant, they show that the lower tail is closer related to the smallest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix. Our methods cannot really deal with this type of structure and so our lower tail result is restricted to the normal regime.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exist absolute constants
c,C
such that the following holds. For all
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
Ct^{3/2}n^{3/2}\sqrt{\log{n}}\leqslant a\leqslant ct^{3/2}n^{2}
, we have

\exp\left(\frac{-Ca^{2}}{t^{3}n^{3}}\right)\,\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,<\,-a\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-ca^{2}}{t^{3}n^{3}}\right)\,.

1.2.
Results for triangle counts in
G(n,p)

Understanding the behaviour of the upper tail for the number of triangles in the Erdős-Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
has been a major focus in probabilistic combinatorics in recent decades. We use the following notation to denote the asymptotic rate associated with a deviation of size
a=\delta p^{3}(n)_{3}
above the mean:

r(\delta,p,n)\,:=\,-\log\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\triangle}(G_{p})\,>\,(1+\delta)p^{3}(n)_{3}\right)\,,

where
G_{p}\sim G(n,p)
. This asymptotic rate was considered by Vu
[
41
]
and Janson and Ruciński
[
29
]
who gave a number of methods for bounding
r(\delta,p,n)
, in the early 2000s. A little later Kim and Vu
[
30
]
and Janson, Oleszkiewicz and Ruciński
[
28
]
independently arrived at the bounds

c(\delta)p^{2}n^{2}\,\leqslant\,r(\delta,p,n)\,\leqslant\,C(\delta)p^{2}n^{2}\log(1/p)\,

for some constants
c(\delta),C(\delta)
. In other words, the value of
r(\delta,p,n)
was determined up to a logarithmic error factor. Around 2010, the problem of finding the correct order of magnitude of
r(\delta,p,n)
was resolved independently by Chatterjee
[
8
]
and De Marco and Kahn
[
13
]
. They discovered that

c(\delta)p^{2}n^{2}\log(1/p)\,\leqslant\,r(\delta,p,n)\,\leqslant\,C(\delta)p^{2}n^{2}\log(1/p)\,.

Attention then turned to the precise asymptotic behaviour of
r(\delta,p,n)
.

In the case of constant
p\in(0,1)
, Chatterjee and Varadhan
[
11
]
showed that finding
r(\delta,p,n)
reduces to finding the solution of a variational problem for graphons

Recently, Harel, Mousset and Samotij

1.3.
Our results for cherry counts

The corresponding problem for cherries (paths of length two) is somewhat easier. And we would not be surprised if these results on cherries could be obtained by other methods. However, it is very useful to state and prove these results for cherries as it provides a more elementary application of our method. We hope this will help the reader understand the essential idea of the method in a simpler setting.

Let
N_{{\bigwedge}}(G)
be the number of isomorphic copies of the path of length two in the graph
G
(this is simply double the count without multiplicity).

Again we will see that the results break into various regimes. This time there are only three. The corresponding functions for cherry count deviations are:

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,

\displaystyle:=\,b^{1/2}tn^{3/2}\,,

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,

\displaystyle:=\,\dfrac{b^{2}}{\ell^{2}}1_{b<n\ell}\,,

and

\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,:=\,\dfrac{bn}{\ell}1_{b\geqslant n\ell}\,.

Let

M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)=\max\{\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\}.

and define
\scalebox{0.9}{{DEV}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)
as the minimal value of
a
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})>\mathbb{E}\left[N_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\right]+a\right)\,\leqslant\,e^{-b}\,.

That is,
\scalebox{0.9}{{DEV}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)
describes the size of deviation which has probability
e^{-b}
.

Our main theorem concerning the cherry count deviation determines the order of magnitude of
\scalebox{0.9}{{DEV}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)
across (essentially) the entire range of the parameters
b
and
t
.

Theorem 1.8
.

There exist absolute constants
c,C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
2n^{-1}\log{n}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and that
3\log n\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
. Then

cM_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\leqslant\scalebox{0.9}{{DEV}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\leqslant CM_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,.

We stress that this result is given as a secondary result to introduce methods which will be used in the upper bound part of Theorem
1.1
. For this reason we only give a sketch proof of the lower bound, see Section
8
.

1.4.
An informal discussion of our approach

As we have mentioned, the results for the
G(n,p)
setting will follow from results proved for the
G(n,m)
model. In this subsection we give a quick intuitive idea of the approach to bounding triangle count deviations in
G(n,m)
.

Naturally, the approach has some differences based on the regime in question. For definiteness, let us discuss the normal regime, i.e., the regime in which
\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t)=b^{1/2}t^{3/2}n^{3/2}
is the maximum of the four, and so
M(b,t)=\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t)
. In this context our objective is to prove that for some constants
c<C
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,cb^{1/2}t^{3/2}n^{3/2}\right)\,\geqslant\,\exp(-b)

and

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,Cb^{1/2}t^{3/2}n^{3/2}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,.

As the standard deviation
\sigma
of
N_{\triangle}(G_{m})
is of order
t^{3/2}n^{3/2}
, this corresponds to an (approximate) extension of central limit theorem. We recall that more precise bounds were proved in
[
20
]
, but these results seem to be difficult to extend to sparser random graphs.

In common with
[
20
]
our approach is based on a martingale expression for the deviation
D_{\triangle}(G_{m})
and then using Freedman’s martingale inequalities (which we state in the next section) to give both upper and lower bounds on the probability of deviations. This expression (discussed in more detail in Section
2.3
)

D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[3\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\right]\,,

expresses the triangle count deviation in terms of increments which are related to the created of cherries (
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
) and triangles (
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
) in the
i
th step of the process.

The challenges associated with this approach include:

•

understanding the variance, or more precisely, the likely quadratic variation, of the process, and

•

controling the effect of large increments on the course of the martingale.

The term
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
may cause a large increment if the new edge
e_{i}=uw
is such that one of the degrees
d(u)
or
d(v)
is far from the average. The term
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
may cause a large increment if the new edge
e_{i}=uw
is such that the codegree
d(u,w)
is far from its expected value.

We therefore require a good understanding of degrees, codegrees, and their deviations. We use truncations and the so-called divide and conquer martingale approach. Informally, this means that we use our understanding of degrees and codegrees to control the effect of large increments, while using the martingale approach to control the remaining increments.

Unfortunately, as we shall see, this approach becomes quite technical. We hope at least that this informal discussion puts the rest of the proof in context.

2.
Important auxiliary results and inequalities

In this section we give some important auxiliary results and inequalities which we shall use throughout the article.

We first remark that we write
N
for
\binom{n}{2}
, and set
t:=m/N
, which is the density of the random graph
G_{m}\sim G(n,m)
. We will sometimes consider the Erdős-Rényi random graph process for generating
G_{m}
. The process
G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{m}
may be generated from the empty graph
G_{0}
(with
n
vertices) by adding a uniformly random edge at each step, chosen from amongst the edges not already present. Equivalently, one may consider
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}
, a uniformly random permutation of the edges of
K_{n}
, and define the sequence of graphs
G_{0},\dots,G_{m}
by setting
E(G_{i})=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i}\}
. In this context we denote by
s
the density
s:=i/N
of the graph
G_{i}
, and we note that
G_{i}\sim G(n,i)
.

When it is convenient, and not likely to confuse, we ignore the problem of rounding to the nearest integer, and floor signs, and so on.

We now begin stating the required auxiliary results.

2.1.
Chernoff inequalities and related inequalities

We shall state Chernoff inequalities for binomial or hypergeometric random variables, see Theorems 2.1 and 2.10 of
[
26
]
. Note that the last two inequalities follow from (
2.1
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X
be a binomial or hypergeometric random variable and let
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]
. Then, for all
a>0
, we have

(2.1)

\mathbb{P}\left(X\,\geqslant\,\mu+a\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-a^{2}}{2\mu+2a/3}\right)

and

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}\left(X\,\leqslant\,\mu-a\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-a^{2}}{2\mu}\right)\,.

For
\theta\geqslant e
we have

(2.3)

\mathbb{P}\left(X\,\geqslant\,\theta\mu\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(-\theta\mu\big(\log(\theta)-1\big)\right)\,.

Consequently, for
j\geqslant 3
and any
\nu\geqslant\mu
we have

(2.4)

\mathbb{P}\left(X\,\geqslant\,2^{j}\nu\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(-j2^{j-2}\nu\right)\,.

It is sometimes also useful to consider random variables which are sums of binomial or hypergeometric random variables. If
X
is a sum of two such random variables then it follows from Lemma
2.1
that:

(2.5)

\mathbb{P}\left(|X-\mu|\,\geqslant\,a\right)\,\leqslant\,4\exp\left(\frac{-a^{2}}{8\mu+2a}\right)

and, for
j\geqslant 4
, and any
\nu\geqslant\mu

(2.6)

\mathbb{P}\left(X\,\geqslant\,2^{j}\nu\right)\,\leqslant\,2\exp\left(-j2^{j-3}\nu\right)\,.

In particular, this applies when we count edges between sets with multiplicity. Given a graph
G
we may define

e(U,W)\,:=\,\sum_{u\in U}\sum_{w\in W}1_{uw\in E(G)}\,.

Note that setting
X=e(U,W)
, and taking
G
with distribution
G(n,m)
or
G(n,p)
then
X
is a sum of two binomial or two hypergeometric random variables, and so inequalities (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) hold.

2.2.
Martingale deviation inequalities

We begin with the Hoeffding-Azuma inequality
[
23
,
2
]
. This inequality states that for a supermartingale
(S_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
with increments
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
we have

(2.7)

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{m}-S_{0}\,>\,a\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-a^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{2}}\right)\,.

for all
a>0
, where
c_{i}:=\|X_{i}\|_{\infty}
.

When increments are typically much smaller than their supremum the following inequality of Freedman gives better bounds.

Lemma 2.2

(Freedman’s inequality)
.

Let
(S_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
be a supermartingale with increments
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
with respect to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
, let
R\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\max_{i}|X_{i}|\leqslant R
almost surely, and for each
j\geqslant 1
let

V(j):=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\,\,\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\,.

Then, for every
\alpha,\beta>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\big(S_{j}-S_{0}\,\geqslant\,\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad V(j)\leqslant\beta\quad\text{for some }j\big)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-\alpha^{2}}{2(\beta+R\alpha)}\right)\,.

In particular,

\mathbb{P}\big(S_{j}-S_{0}\,\geqslant\,\alpha\big)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-\alpha^{2}}{2(\beta+R\alpha)}\right)\,+\,\mathbb{P}\left(V(j)>\beta\right).

The following converse result shows that the above bound is often close to best possible. Let
\tau_{\alpha}
be the least
j
such that
S_{j}\geqslant S_{0}+\alpha
(and infinity if this never occurs), and set

T_{\alpha}\,:=\,V(\tau_{\alpha})\,,

where we interpret
V(\infty)
as
\infty
. The above inequality states that

\mathbb{P}\left(T_{\alpha}\leqslant\beta\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-\alpha^{2}}{2(\beta+R\alpha)}\right)\,.

Freedman’s converse inequality
[
18
]
is as follows.

Lemma 2.3

(Converse Freedman inequality)
.

Let
(S_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
be a martingale with increments
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
with respect to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
, let
R
be such that
\max_{i}|X_{i}|\leqslant R
almost surely, and let
T_{\alpha}
be as defined above.
Then, for every
\alpha,\beta>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(T_{\alpha}\leqslant\beta\right)\,\geqslant\,\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{-\alpha^{2}(1+4\delta)}{2\beta}\right),

where
\delta>0
is such that
\beta/\alpha\geqslant 9R\delta^{-2}
and
\alpha^{2}/\beta\geqslant 16\delta^{-2}\log(64\delta^{-2})
.

We shall also prove a corollary of Freedman’s inequality applied to the
G(n,m)
setting. It is in the spirit of McDiarmid’s inequality
[
35
]
, which considers deviation probabilities for functions which satisfy a “Lipschitz” property. However, we work with a weaker “Lipschitz” property where the analogue of the “Lipschitz constant” depends on the edges in question.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n,m}
be the family of graphs with
n
vertices and
m
edges. We may consider an edit distance between graphs
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{n,m}
. We consider two graphs
G,G^{\prime}
to be adjacent (distance
1
) if
E(G^{\prime})=E(G)\setminus\{e\}\cup\{e^{\prime}\}
for some pair
e,e^{\prime}\in E(K_{n})
. Given a function
\psi:E(K_{n})\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we say that a function
f:\mathcal{G}_{n,m}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\psi
-Lipschitz
if for every adjacent pair of graphs
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{n,m}
we have

\big|\,f(G)\,-\,f(G^{\prime})\,\big|\,\leqslant\,\psi(e)\,+\,\psi(e^{\prime})\,,

where
E(G)\bigtriangleup E(G^{\prime})=\{e,e^{\prime}\}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
G_{m}\sim G(n,m)
with
t=m/N
. Given
\psi:E(K_{n})\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and a
\psi
-Lipschitz function
f:\mathcal{G}_{n,m}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(f(G_{m})\,-\,\mathbb{E}\left[f(G_{m})\right]\,\geqslant\,a\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-a^{2}}{24t\|\psi\|^{2}\,+\,6a\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)

for all
a\geqslant 0
, where
\|\psi\|^{2}:=\sum_{e\in E(K_{n})}\psi(e)^{2}
and
\psi_{\mathrm{max}}:=\max_{e}\psi(e)
.

Furthermore, the same bound holds for
\mathbb{P}\left(f(G_{m})\,-\,\mathbb{E}\left[f(G_{m})\right]\,\leqslant\,-a\right)
.

Remark 2.5
.

We have not attempt to optimise the constants.

Remark 2.6
.

We have stated this result for
G(n,m)
as this is the context in which we are working. It is clear that the result does not actually depend on the graph structure at all. In particular, if
f:[N]^{(m)}\to\mathbb{R}
then one may obtain the same result for the selection of a uniformly random
m
-element subset
G\subseteq[N]
.

We now present the proof of Proposition
2.4
. The proof is based on an application of Freedman’s inequality, and will rely on a bound on the increments in terms of the function
\psi
.

Proof.

We do not lose any generality in assuming
m\leqslant N/2
. Indeed, if
m>N/2
, then simply observe that
f
is also a function of the complementary graph
G_{m}^{c}\sim G_{N-m}
. The “Furthermore” statement follows by applying the inequality to
-f
.

We prove the result by considering the martingale

Z_{i}\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\left[f(G_{m})\,|\,G_{i}\right]\,\qquad i=0,\dots,m

where
G_{0},\dots,G_{m}
is the Erdős-Rényi random graph process. Equivalently, let
e_{1},\dots,e_{N}
be a uniform random ordering of the edges of
K_{n}
and for each
i
define
G_{i}
to be the graph with edges
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i}\}
. We require the following bound on the increments of this martingale

(2.8)

\big|Z_{i}\,-\,Z_{i-1}\big|\,\leqslant\,\psi(e_{i})\,+\,2\bar{\psi}\,,

where
\bar{\psi}=N^{-1}\sum_{e\in E(K_{n})}\psi(e)
.

Let us show that the proposition follows if we assume (
2.8
). In this case we may immediately bound the absolute value of increments,

\big|Z_{i}\,-\,Z_{i-1}\big|\,\leqslant\,\psi(e_{i})\,+\,2\bar{\psi}\,\leqslant\,3\psi_{\mathrm{max}}\,,

and we obtain the following bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\big(Z_{i}\,-\,Z_{i-1}\big)^{2}\,\middle|\,G_{i-1}\right]\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\psi(e_{i})+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{e\in E(K_{n})}\psi(e)\right)^{2}\,\middle|\,G_{i-1}\right]\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,2\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(e_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,+\,\frac{8}{N^{2}}\left(\sum_{e\in E(K_{n})}\psi(e)\right)^{2}\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{4}{N}\sum_{e\in E(K_{n})}\psi(e)^{2}\,+\,\frac{8}{N}\sum_{e\in E(K_{n})}\psi(e)^{2}\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{12}{N}\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\,\,.\phantom{\bigg|}

Summing the above bound over
i\leqslant m
we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(Z_{i}\,-\,Z_{i-1}\big)^{2}\,\mid\,G_{i-1}\right]\,\leqslant\,\frac{12m}{N}\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\,=\,12t\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\,.

We now apply Freedman’s inequality (Lemma
2.2
) with
\alpha=a
,
\beta=12t\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}
and
R=3\psi_{\mathrm{max}}
, which yields

\mathbb{P}\left(f(G_{m})\,-\,\mathbb{E}\left[f(G_{m})\right]\,\geqslant\,a\right)\,=\,\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{m}-Z_{0}\,\geqslant\,a\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-a^{2}}{24t\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\,+\,6a\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)\,,

as required.

All that remains is to justify (
2.8
), i.e., we must prove that for every possible sequence
(e_{1},\dots,e_{i})
we have that
|Z_{i}\,-\,Z_{i-1}|\,\leqslant\,\psi(e_{i})\,+\,2\bar{\psi}
. The conditional expectations
Z_{i}
and
Z_{i-1}
may be expressed explicitly as sums over the choices of the edges up to
e_{m}
,

Z_{i}\,=\,\frac{1}{(N-i)_{m-i}}\sum_{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m}}f(G_{i}\cup\{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m}\})

and

Z_{i-1}\,=\,\frac{1}{(N-i+1)_{m-i+1}}\sum_{e^{\prime}_{i},e^{\prime}_{i+1},\dots,e^{\prime}_{m}}f(G_{i-1}\cup\{e^{\prime}_{i},e^{\prime}_{i+1},\dots,e^{\prime}_{m}\})\,.

Note that the sums are over sequences of distinct edges of
K_{n}
disjoint from those already selected. We would like to pair up the terms in such a way that the graphs
E(G_{i})\cup\{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m}\}
and
E(G_{i-1})\cup\{e^{\prime}_{i},e^{\prime}_{i+1},\dots,e^{\prime}_{m}\}
are either equal or adjacent. The obvious problem is that the sums do not even have the same number of terms. We introduce a dummy edge
g
in the first sum, which may be any edge of
E(K_{n})\setminus\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i-1}\}
. We have

Z_{i}\,=\,\frac{1}{(N-i+1)_{m-i+1}}\sum_{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m};g}f(G_{i}\cup\{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m}\})\,.

We may now pair up terms of the two summations on a one-to-one basis. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the sequences of edges allowed in the above summation and
\mathcal{T}
the sequences allowed in the summation for
Z_{i-1}
. We define a bijection
\phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
as follows:

\phi(e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m};g)\,:=\,\begin{cases}(e_{i},e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m})&\text{if }g=e_{i}\\
(e_{i+1},\dots,e_{j-1},e_{i},e_{j+1},\dots,e_{m})\qquad&\text{if }g\in\{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m}\}\\
(g,e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m})&\text{if }g\not\in\{e_{i},\dots,e_{m}\}\,.\end{cases}

Note that in all cases the graphs
E(G_{i-1})\cup\{e_{i},\dots,e_{m}\}
and
E(G_{i-1})\cup\phi(e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m};g)
are either equal or adjacent (with symmetric difference
\{e_{i},g\}
). Now, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\big|

\displaystyle Z_{i}\,-\,Z_{i-1}\big|\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{1}{(N-i+1)_{m-i+1}}\sum_{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m};g}\big|f(G_{i-1}\cup\{e_{i},\dots,e_{m}\})\,-\,f(G_{i-1}\cup\phi(e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m};g))\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{1}{(N-i+1)_{m-i+1}}\sum_{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m};g}\psi(e_{i})+\psi(g)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\psi(e_{i})\,+\,\frac{1}{N-i+1}\sum_{g\in E(K_{n})}\psi(g)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\psi(e_{i})\,+\,2\bar{\psi}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Let us also mention a fairly trivial bound, which is simply a consequence of the triangle inequality and union bound:

(2.9)

\mathbb{P}\left(X+Y\geqslant\alpha+\beta\right)\,\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(X\geqslant\alpha\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geqslant\beta\right)

for any non-negative random variables
X,Y
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.3.
The martingale representations of the deviations of cherries and triangles

In this subsection we recall the martingale expressions for the subgraph count deviations given in
[
20
]
. We first state these results in general and then consider the specific cases, cherries and triangles, which interest us.

We begin by introducing some notation. For graphs
H
and
G
let
N_{H}(G)
denote the number of isomorphic copies of
H
in
G
. That is,
N_{H}(G)
is the number of injective functions from
V(H)
to
V(G)
which preserve the edges of
H
. This counts with multiplicity, so that, for example,
N_{K_{3}}(K_{4})=24
.

If
H
has
v(H)
vertices and
e(H)
edges, then the expected value of
N_{H}(G_{m})
is

L_{H}(m)\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\left[N_{H}(G_{m})\right]\,=\,\frac{(n)_{v(H)}(m)_{e(H)}}{(N)_{e(H)}}\,.

Thus the deviation of
N_{H}(G_{m})
from its mean is

D_{H}(G_{m})\,:=\,N_{H}(G_{m})\,-\,L_{H}(m)\,.

We will focus in particular on studying
D_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})
, the deviation for cherries, and
D_{\triangle}(G_{m})
, the deviation for triangles.

Let
G_{0},\dots G_{m}
be a realisation of the Erdős-Rényi random graph process. In a slight abuse of notation, when we write
G_{m}
we may assume that
G_{m}
also includes the information of the order in which the edges were added.

As in
[
20
]
, for each fixed graph
F
(although we only really care about the cases that
F
is a cherry or a triangle) we may define

A_{F}(G_{m})\,:=\,N_{F}(G_{m})\,-\,N_{F}(G_{m-1})

to be the number of copies of
F
created with the addition of the
m
th edge. We may also define a “centralised” version. Let

X_{F}(G_{m})\,:=\,A_{F}(G_{m})\,-\,\mathbb{E}\left[A_{F}(G_{m})\,\middle|\,G_{m-1}\right]\,=\,N_{F}(G_{m})\,-\,\mathbb{E}\left[N_{F}(G_{m})\,\middle|\,G_{m-1}\right]\,.

The general martingale representation for
D_{H}(G_{m})
given in
[
20
]
is

D_{H}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\,\sum_{F\subseteq E(H)}\frac{(N-m)_{e(F)}(m-i)_{e(H)-e(F)}}{(N-i)_{e(H)}}\,X_{F}(G_{i})\,,

where we sum over all
2^{e(H)}
subgraphs
F
with
V(F)=V(H)
and
E(F)\subseteq E(H)
. Since
X_{F}(G_{i})
is deterministically
0
if
F
has
0
edges or
1
edge, we obtain

D_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(N-m)_{2}}{(N-i)_{2}}\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})

and

D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[3\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\right]\,.

We say these are martingale representations as the random variables
X_{F}(G_{i})
behave as martingale increments, in the sense that
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{F}(G_{i})\middle|G_{i-1}\right]=0
.

2.4.
A bound on a certain summation

The following elementary inequality will be helpful for some estimates.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
r\geqslant d^{1/2}
and let
\beta\geqslant 1
. Then,

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geqslant r}\exp\left(-\beta\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\right)\,\leqslant\,(8\pi)^{d/2}e^{-\beta(r-d^{1/2})^{2}/2}\,.

For completeness a proof is given in Appendix
A
.

3.
Degrees in
G(n,m)

Many papers have studied degree sequences in random graphs and a great deal is known about the typical behaviour of the degrees. Bollobás
[
7
]
gives an overview which includes the results of Bollobás himself
[
5
,
6
]
and mentions other approaches, such as that of McKay and Wormald
[
36
]
. See also the recent article of Liebenau and Wormald
[
32
]
and the references therein.

Since we require results not only about the likely behaviour of degrees, but also the behaviour further into the tail (events with smaller probability), we state in this section the results we shall need. Most of the proofs are fairly standard and so are given in the appendix, see Appendix
B
.

After the results about degrees, we include a short subsection showing what we may deduce about the increments
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
.

We shall work in
G_{m}\sim G(n,m)
, although we remark that similar results could be easily deduced in
G(n,p)
. Let
d_{u}(G)
be the degree of a vertex
u
in a graph
G
and let

D_{u}(G_{m})\,:=\,d_{u}(G_{m})\,-\,\dfrac{2m}{n}

be the deviation of the degree of
u
(from its mean) in the graph
G_{m}\sim G(n,m)
.

The expected degree of each vertex is
2m/n\,=\,t(n-1)\approx tn
. We shall state bounds related to the number of vertices of large degree (at least
32tn
) and even larger degree (at least
2^{j}tn
for each
j\geqslant 5
). It is also helpful to state a bound on the maximum degree
\Delta(G_{m})
of
G_{m}
. These results are fairly standard. We also state bounds related to the sum of squares of degree deviations
\sum_{u}D_{u}(G_{m})^{2}
.

We now introduce some notation in order to state the bounds. We set
\ell_{b}:=\log(b/etn)
. Let
V_{j}
denote the set of vertices in
G_{m}\sim G(n,m)
with degree at least
2^{j}tn
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
t\geqslant 2n^{-1}\log{n}
and let
b\geqslant 4tn
. Then, except with probability at most
\exp(-b)
, we have

(i)

\Delta(G_{m})\,\leqslant\,2b/\ell_{b}\phantom{\bigg|}
, and

(ii)

\displaystyle|V_{j}|\,\leqslant\,\frac{b}{tnj2^{j-6}}\,\,
for all
j\geqslant 5
.

With respect to the sum of squares of degree deviations we see three different types of behaviours at different points in the tail. Let
\kappa(b,t)
be the function defined by

\kappa(b,t)\,:=\,\begin{cases}tn^{2}\qquad&1\leqslant b<t^{1/2}n\ell\\
b^{2}/\ell^{2}&t^{1/2}n\ell\leqslant b<n\ell\\
bn/\ell&n\ell\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell\,.\end{cases}

The three ranges correspond to three different “causes” of a large sum of squares of degree deviations. The first value corresponds to the contribution of order
n
vertices with degree deviation given by the standard deviation, which is of order
t^{1/2}n^{1/2}
. The second and third values are related to degree deviations caused by stars and hubs respectively.
We also define

\kappa^{+}(b,t)\,:=\,\begin{cases}b^{2}/\ell_{b}^{2}\qquad&32tn\leqslant b<n\ell\\
bn/\ell&n\ell\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell\,.\end{cases}

We may now state our bounds on the sum of squares of deviations.

Proposition 3.2
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Suppose that
t\geqslant 2n^{-1}\log{n}
and that
b\geqslant 32tn
. Except with probability at most
\exp(-b)
, we have

\sum_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}\,\leqslant\,C\kappa(b,t)

for all steps
i\leqslant m
, and

\sum_{u\,:\,d_{u}(G_{m})\geqslant 32tn}d_{u}(G_{m})^{2}\,\leqslant\,C\kappa^{+}(b,t)\,.

The following lemma, which bounds the contribution of vertices whose degree is not too large, covers the most interesting part of the proof of Proposition
3.2
. For this reason we give the proof of Lemma
3.3
here, while the rest of the proof of Proposition
3.2
is given in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 3.3
.

There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Suppose that
t\geqslant 2n^{-1}\log{n}
and that
b\geqslant n
. Except with probability at most
\exp(-b)
, we have

\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{i})\leqslant 32tn}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}\,\leqslant\,Cbtn

for all steps
i\leqslant m
.

We wish to use a martingale approach to bound the probability that the sum
{\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{i})\leqslant 32tn}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}}
is large. One thing which could complicate the analysis is the non-linearity of this expression. This following trick allows us to “linearise”. If the sum of squares of degree deviations is large then we may encode this using a vector
\sigma
which mimics the degree deviations, so that
\sum_{u\in V(G_{m})}\sigma_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})
is also large. As this function is linear, and with certain Lipschitz properties, we may bound the probability it is large using Proposition
2.4
. This idea, combined with a union bound, gives the desired result.

The idea of
\sigma
is to encode the (renormalised) degree deviation of each vertex up to a factor of two. So that the reader may think of
\sigma
as a dyadic rounding of the renormalised degree deviation . Values that are too small to matter are set to
0
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Given a vector
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{V(G)}
with entries which are all either
0
or
\pm 2^{j}
for some
j=0,\dots,\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}{tn}\rfloor
, we define, for each
i\leqslant m
, the event
E_{\sigma,i}
that

(3.1)

\sum_{u\in V(G_{i})}\sigma_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})\,\geqslant\,16\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2}t^{1/2}n^{1/2}\,.

Let
S
be the set of such vectors
\sigma
with
\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2}\geqslant 16b
. We make two claims:

Claim 1:
The event that
\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{i})\leqslant 32tn}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}>2^{20}btn
is contained in the union
\bigcup_{\sigma\in S}E_{\sigma,i}
.

Claim 2:

\mathbb{P}\left(E_{\sigma,i}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2})
for all
\sigma\in S
and
i\leqslant m
.

Let us observe that the lemma will follow from these two claims. By Claim 1, and a union bound, and then Claim 2, the probability that
\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{i})\leqslant 32tn}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}>2^{20}btn
occurs for some
i\leqslant m
, is at most
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\sigma\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(E_{\sigma,i}\right)\leqslant m\sum_{\sigma\in S}\exp(-\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2})
. Now, by Corollary
2.7
, with
r=4b^{1/2}
, we have

\displaystyle m\sum_{\sigma\in S}\exp(-\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\,n^{2}(8\pi)^{n/2}\exp\left(\frac{-(4b^{1/2}-n^{1/2})^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,n^{2}\exp(2n-9b/2)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,,

as required.

Proof of Claim I:
If the event
\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{i})\leqslant 32tn}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}>2^{20}btn
occurs for the graph
G_{i}
then we shall prove that
E_{\sigma}
occurs for the choice of
\sigma
given below. Let
U
denote the set of vertices with
|D_{u}(G_{i})|\in[32t^{1/2}n^{1/2},32tn]
. The contribution of vertices with deviation less than
32t^{1/2}n^{1/2}
is at most
2^{10}tn^{2}\leqslant 2^{10}btn
and so

(3.2)

\sum_{u\in U}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}\,>\,2^{19}btn\,.

Define
\sigma
by
\sigma_{u}=0
if
u\not\in U
and for
j\geqslant 0
, we set

\sigma_{u}\,:=\,\begin{cases}2^{j}\qquad&2^{j+5}t^{1/2}n^{1/2}\leqslant D_{u}(G_{i})<\min\{2^{j+6}t^{1/2}n^{1/2},32tn\}\\
-2^{j}\qquad&2^{j+5}t^{1/2}n^{1/2}\leqslant-D_{u}(G_{i})<\min\{2^{j+6}t^{1/2}n^{1/2},32tn\}\,.\end{cases}

It follows from the definition of
\sigma
that

\frac{D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}}{2^{12}tn}\,\leqslant\sigma_{u}^{2}\,\leqslant\,\frac{D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}}{2^{10}tn}\,

for all
u\in U
. And so, by (
3.2
), we have that

16b\,\leqslant\,2^{7}b\,\leqslant\,\|\sigma\|^{2}\,\leqslant\,\frac{1}{2^{10}tn}\sum_{u\in U}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}\,.

In particular, the lower bound confirms that
\sigma\in S
.
We also observe that
\sigma_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})\geqslant D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}/2^{6}t^{1/2}n^{1/2}
for all
u\in U
, and so

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in V(G_{i})}\sigma_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})\,

\displaystyle\geqslant\,\frac{1}{2^{6}t^{1/2}n^{1/2}}\sum_{u\in U}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\,16t^{1/2}n^{1/2}\|\sigma\|^{2}\,.

This shows that
E_{\sigma}
occurs, completing the proof of Claim 1.

Proof of Claim 2:
Let us fix
\sigma\in S
. Set

f_{\sigma}(G_{i})\,:=\,\sum_{u\in V(G_{i})}\sigma_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})\,.

So that the event
E_{\sigma}
is the event that the function
f_{\sigma}(G_{i})
is at least
16t^{1/2}n^{1/2}\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2}
. We use the inequality from Proposition
2.4
to bound this probability. In the language of that proposition our function
f_{\sigma}
is
\psi
-Lipschitz for the function
\psi(uw)=|\sigma_{u}|+|\sigma_{w}|
. Note that
\mathbb{E}\left[f_{\sigma}(G_{i})\right]=0
and that
\psi
satisfies
\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant 2n\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2}
and
\psi_{\mathrm{max}}\leqslant 2\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\leqslant 2t^{1/2}n^{1/2}
. Applying Proposition
2.4
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(E_{\sigma}\right)\,

\displaystyle=\,\mathbb{P}\left(f_{\sigma}(G_{i})\,\geqslant\,16\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2}t^{1/2}n^{1/2}\right)\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-256\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{4}tn}{48tn\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2}\,+\,192tn\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp(-\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2})\,.\phantom{\bigg|}

This completes the proof.
∎

3.1.
The increments
X_{\wedge}(G_{i})

We now deduce the following bound on increments from our results on the sum of squares of degree deviations.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
2n^{-1}\log{n}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
, that
b\geqslant 32tn
and that
i\leqslant m
. Then, except with probability at most
\exp(-b)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})^{2}\middle|G_{i-1}\right]\,\leqslant\,\frac{C\kappa(b,t)}{n}\,.

Proof.

Recall that
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
is defined by
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i}):=A_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})-\mathbb{E}\left[A_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\middle|G_{i-1}\right]
, which is the difference between
A_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
, the number of (isomorphic copies of) paths of length
2
created with the addition of the
i
th edge, and its expected value given
G_{i-1}
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})^{2}\middle|G_{i-1}\right]\,=\,\textup{Var}(X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})|G_{i-1})\,=\,\textup{Var}(A_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})|G_{i-1})\,.

We now use that for any constant
c
we have
\textup{Var}(X)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[(X-c)^{2}\right]
, so that

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})^{2}\middle|G_{i-1}\right]\,\leqslant\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(A_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,-\,\frac{8(i-1)}{n}\right)^{2}\,\middle|\,G_{i-1}\right]\,.

By counting the number of isomorphic copies of cherries created, we see that
A_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})=2d_{u}(G_{i-1})+2d_{w}(G_{i-1})
where
uw
is the
i
th edge included in
G_{i}
. Now, since
uw
is a uniformly selected pair from
E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{i-1})
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{{\bigwedge}}

\displaystyle(G_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}]\,\leqslant\,\frac{1}{N-i+1}\,\sum_{uw\not\in E(G_{i-1})}\left(2d_{u}(G_{i-1})\,+\,2d_{w}(G_{i-1})\,-\,\frac{8(i-1)}{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{8}{N-i+1}\,\sum_{uw\not\in E(G_{i-1})}\left(d_{u}(G_{i-1})\,-\,\frac{2(i-1)}{n}\right)^{2}\,+\,\left(d_{w}(G_{i-1})\,-\,\frac{2(i-1)}{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{32}{n^{2}}\,\sum_{uw\not\in E(G_{i-1})}D_{u}(G_{i-1})^{2}\,+\,D_{w}(G_{i-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{32}{n}\,\sum_{u}D_{u}(G_{i-1})^{2}\,.

The required inequality now follows immediately from Proposition
3.2
.
∎

4.
Upper bound on cherry count deviation in
G(n,m)

In this section we prove the upper bound part of Theorem
1.8
. This corresponds to Proposition
4.1
, stated below. We recall that

M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t):=\max\{\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\}

where
\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t):=b^{1/2}tn^{3/2},\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t):=b^{2}1_{b<n\ell}/\ell^{2}
and

\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t)\,:=\,bn1_{b\geqslant n\ell}/\ell\,
.
The following statement corresponds to the upper bound part of Theorem
1.8
.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. For all
2n^{-1}\log{n}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
3\log n\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,CM_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,.

The proof is a relatively straightforward divide and conquer martingale argument.

Before giving the proof it is useful to make some observations about
M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)
. It is easily checked that, for each choice of
t
, it is the normal term,
\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)
, which is largest when
b\leqslant t^{2/3}n\ell^{4/3}
, the star term,
\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)
, is largest if
t^{2/3}n\ell^{4/3}<b\leqslant n\ell
and the hub term,
\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t)\,:=\,bn1_{b\geqslant n\ell}/\ell
, is largest if
b>n\ell
. One may also easily verify that

(4.1)

M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,\geqslant\,btn

for all
b\geqslant 1
, and

(4.2)

M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)^{2}\,\geqslant\,btn\kappa(b,t)

for all
b\geqslant 1
, where
\kappa(b,t)
is as defined in Section
3
. To see (
4.2
), note that for
1\leqslant b\leqslant t^{1/2}n\ell
we have
M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)^{2}\geqslant\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)^{2}=bt^{2}n^{3}=btn\kappa(b,t)
, and for
b\geqslant t^{1/2}n\ell
we have
M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)=\kappa(b,t)
and so (
4.2
) follows from (
4.1
).

It will also be useful to note that the inequality

(4.3)

M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,\geqslant\,\frac{1}{9}\kappa^{+}(b,t)

holds for all
b\geqslant 32tn
. We consider three ranges. For
32tn\leqslant b\leqslant t^{2/3}n\ell_{b}^{4/3}
this follows from the fact that

M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,\geqslant\,\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,=\,b^{1/2}tn^{3/2}\,\geqslant\,\frac{b^{2}}{\ell_{b}^{2}}\,=\,\kappa^{+}(b,t)

in this interval. It is easily observed that for
b\geqslant 32tn
we have
\ell_{b}^{4/3}\geqslant e
, and so, for
b>\max\{32tn,t^{2/3}n\ell_{b}^{4/3}\}
we have that
\ell_{b}\geqslant\ell/3
. And so in the range
\max\{32tn,t^{2/3}n\ell_{b}^{4/3}\}\leqslant b\leqslant n\ell
we have

M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,\geqslant\,\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,=\,b^{2}/\ell^{2}\,\geqslant\,\frac{b^{2}}{9\ell_{b}^{2}}\,=\,\frac{1}{9}\kappa^{+}(b,t)

in this interval. Finally, for
b>n\ell
we have
M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)=\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)=\kappa^{+}(b,t)
.

We remark that, for the star and hub regimes, it would actually be possible to deduce these results more directly from Proposition
3.2
by expressing the cherry count in terms of the sum of squares of degree deviations. However, we prefer to present a unified proof which works across the regimes. One motivation for doing so is that the proof gives a good overview of proof techniques we shall use again later in the triangle case.

We recall the martingale expression for
D_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})
is given by

D_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\dfrac{(N-m)_{2}}{(N-i)_{2}}X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,.

To avoid any particular increment having too large an effect we consider truncating the increments. Let

X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,:=\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})1_{X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\leqslant 128tn}

and

Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i}):=X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})1_{X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})>128tn}\,.

It follows that

D_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,=\,D^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,+\,N^{*}_{\bigwedge}(G_{m})

where

(4.4)

D^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,:=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\dfrac{(N-m)_{2}}{(N-i)_{2}}X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})

and

(4.5)

N^{*}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,:=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\dfrac{(N-m)_{2}}{(N-i)_{2}}Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,.

And so, to prove Proposition
4.1
it suffices to prove that there exist absolute constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

(4.6)

\mathbb{P}\left(D^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,C_{1}M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)

and

(4.7)

\mathbb{P}\left(N^{*}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,C_{2}M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)

We may then take
C=C_{1}+C_{2}
and the required bound follows by the triangle inequality and union bound, as in (
2.9
).

4.1.
Bounding the probability that
D^{\prime}_{\wedge}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,C_{1}M_{\wedge}(b,t)

We shall use Freedman’s inequality, Lemma
2.2
, to prove (
4.6
).

As
X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\leqslant X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
, the sequence of partial sums of the summation in (
4.4
) form a supermartingale, with final value
D^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})
. We first investigate the behaviour of the increments
Y_{i}^{\prime}
, defined by

Y^{\prime}_{i}:=\big((N-m)_{2}/(N-i)_{2}\big)X_{{\bigwedge}}^{\prime}(G_{i})\,.

We observe immediately that, since the coefficient is at most
1
we have the deterministic upper bound
|Y^{\prime}_{i}|\leqslant 128tn
.

We may also bound the quadratic variation of the process. Note that
(Y_{i}^{\prime})^{2}\leqslant(X_{{\bigwedge}}^{\prime}(G_{i}))^{2}\leqslant X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})^{2}
. Thus, Lemma
3.4
applied with “
b
” being
\max\{4b,32tn\}
gives a constant
C_{3}
such that, for each
i\leqslant m
, there is probability at most
\exp(-4b)
that

\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{i}^{\prime})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]>C_{3}n^{-1}\kappa(b,t)\,.

And so, except with probability at most
\exp(-3b)
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{i}^{\prime})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\leqslant C_{3}\kappa(b,t)tn\,.

Set
\beta=C_{3}\kappa(b,t)tn
. We now apply Freedman’s inequality, Lemma
2.2
. We obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{{\bigwedge}}^{\prime}(G_{m})\geqslant C_{1}M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\dfrac{-C_{1}^{2}M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)^{2}}{2\beta+256C_{1}M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)tn}\right)\,+\,\exp(-3b)\,.

It now follows immediately from (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) that the first term is at most
\exp(-3b)
, provided
C_{1}
is taken sufficiently large. As
2\exp(-3b)\leqslant\exp(-2b)
, this completes the proof.

4.2.
Bounding the probability that
N^{*}_{\wedge}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,C_{2}M_{\wedge}(b,t)

To prove (
4.7
), we will bound
N^{*}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})
using our results on the behaviour of degrees proved in Section
3
. We begin by relating
N^{*}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})
to the degree sequence.

It follows from the definition of
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
that

X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,A_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,2\sum_{v\in e_{i}}d_{v}(G_{i-1})\,.

In particular, if
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})>128tn
then the vertex of larger degree has degree at least
32tn
. And so

N^{*}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})1_{X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})>128tn}\,\leqslant\,4\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{v\in e_{i}}d_{v}(G_{m})1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>32tn}\,.

Note that we used that
d_{v}(G_{i-1})\leqslant d_{v}(G_{m})
in the last inequality. Now, by double counting we get

\displaystyle N^{*}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,4\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}(G_{m})1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>32tn}\sum_{e\in E(G_{m})}1_{v\in e}

\displaystyle=4\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}(G_{m})^{2}1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>32tn}\,.

The quantity on the right hand side is automatically
0
if
\Delta(G_{m})\leqslant 32tn
. In particular, if
b\leqslant 16tn
then, by applying Lemma
3.1
with “b” being
32tn
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta(G_{m})>32tn\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-32tn)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)\,.

And so, in this case there is probability at most
\exp(-2b)
that
N^{*}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})
is non-zero, and (
4.7
) follows immediately.

For
b\geqslant 16tn
, we apply Proposition
3.2
to obtain that, except with probability at most
\exp(-2b)
, we have

N^{*}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{m})\,\leqslant\,C_{2}\kappa^{+}(b,t)\,\leqslant\,9C_{2}M_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,,

where the final inequality uses (
4.3
). This completes the proof of (
4.7
).

5.
Codegrees in
G(n,m)

Our proof for cherries in the previous section was reliant on some control of the degree sequence. The quadratic variation of the random variables
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
is a function of the sequence of codegrees. And so, to extend the approach to the triangle count we also need to prove bounds related to the behaviour of the sequence of codegrees. This turns out to be a much bigger challenge as we need to engage with the structure of the set of pairs with large codegrees.

After we have proved these results, we state, in Section
5.3
, what we can deduce about the quadratic variation of the associated increments
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
from these bounds on the behaviour of codegrees.

For each pair of distinct vertices
u,w\in V(G_{m})
(equivalently, for each
uw\in E(K_{n})
) we write
d_{uw}(G_{m})
for their codegree, i.e.,
d_{uw}(G_{m})=|N_{G_{m}}(u)\cap N_{G_{m}}(w)|
, the number of common neighbours of
u
and
w
in the graph
G_{m}
. Let us also define

D_{uw}(G_{m})\,=\,d_{uw}(G_{m})\,-\,\frac{m(m-1)(n-2)}{N(N-1)}\,,

which is the deviation of the codegree from its expected value in
G_{m}\sim G(n,m)
. We shall consider two ranges of codegree deviations

(i)

from
\Theta(tn^{1/2})
to
\Theta(t^{2}n)
, and

(ii)

larger than
\Theta(t^{2}n)
.

The first range considers deviations from the order of the codegree standard deviation to the order of the mean, while the second range considers genuinely large codegree deviations.

One observation is that a vertex
u
with large degree is likely to have large codegree with almost all vertices. For this reason we focus in this section on pairs
uw
which have large codegree which
cannot
be explained directly in terms of the degree of one of its vertices. Corresponding to (i) and (ii) above,

(i)

For
k\in K_{1}:=\{10,\dots,\lceil\log_{2}(tn^{1/2})+10\rceil\}
, we define

F_{k}(G)\,:=\,\{uw\,:\,|D_{uw}(G)|\in[2^{k}tn^{1/2},2^{k+1}tn^{1/2})\,,\,|D_{u}(G)|,|D_{w}(G)|\leqslant 2^{k-5}n^{1/2}\}

and set
f_{k}(G):=|F_{k}(G)|
.

(ii)

For
k\in K_{2}:=\{10,\dots,\lfloor 2\log_{2}(1/t)\rfloor\}
, we define

H_{k}(G)\,:=\,\{uw\,:\,d_{uw}(G)\in[2^{k}t^{2}n,2^{k+1}t^{2}n)\,,\,d_{u}(G),d_{w}(G)\leqslant 2^{k-5}tn\}

and set
h_{k}(G):=|H_{k}(G)|
.

Note that the intervals
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
have been chosen so that all dyadic intervals of possible codegrees from
2^{10}tn^{1/2}
up to
n
are covered. The alert reader will have noticed that we use the codegree
d_{uw}(G)
rather than the codegree deviation
D_{uw}(G)
in the definition of
H_{k}(G)
. We do so because the codegree is easier to discuss, and in applications we will use that
|D_{uw}(G_{i})|\leqslant d_{uw}(G_{i})
, whenever
d_{uw}(G_{i})\geqslant t^{2}n
.

We shall prove the following bounds.

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
t\geqslant Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}
and that
b\geqslant n
. Then, except with probability at most
\exp(-b)
, we have

f_{k}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,\frac{Cb^{2}}{2^{4k}}\phantom{\Bigg|}\qquad\qquad\text{for all }k\in K_{1}\,\text{and }i\leqslant m\,.

For
b\geqslant 3\log{n}
, except with probability at most
\exp(-b)
, we have

h_{k}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,\frac{Cb^{2}}{k^{2}2^{2k}t^{4}n^{2}}\phantom{\Bigg|}\qquad\qquad\text{for all }k\in K_{2}\,\text{and }i\leqslant m\,.

For the application to controlling the triangle count, and, in particular, the quadratic variation of the associated process, we will use the following corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
t\geqslant Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}
and that
b\geqslant n
. Then, except with probability at most
\exp(-b)
, we have

\sum_{uw}D_{uw}(G_{i})^{2}\,\leqslant\,C\max\{bt^{2}n^{2},b^{2}\}

for all
i\leqslant m
.

Proof.

We may fix
i\leqslant m
and prove the result holds with failure probability at most
4\exp(-2b)
. The corollary then follows by a union bound as
4m\exp(-2b)\leqslant 4n^{2}\exp(-2b)\leqslant\exp(-b)
.

We will consider four types of pairs which contribute to the sum
\sum_{uw}D_{uw}(G_{i})^{2}
.

First, pairs with
|D_{uw}(G_{i})|\leqslant 2^{10}tn^{1/2}
make total contribution at most
2^{20}t^{2}n^{3}\leqslant 2^{20}bt^{2}n^{2}
, so these may be safely ignored.

Second, the contribution of pairs with
|D_{uw}(G_{i})|\leqslant 64t\max\{|D_{u}(G_{i})|,|D_{w}(G_{i})\}
, may be controlled by our bound on the sum of squares of degree deviations. Indeed, by Proposition
3.2
, except with probability at most
\exp(-32b)
, the total contribution of these terms is at most

2^{12}t^{2}n\sum_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}\,\leqslant\,C_{1}t^{2}n\kappa(32b,t)\,\leqslant\,\frac{C^{\prime}_{1}bt^{2}n^{2}}{\ell}

for some constants
C_{1}
and
C^{\prime}_{1}
.

All remaining pairs appear in
\bigcup_{k\in K_{1}}F_{k}(G_{i})
or
\bigcup_{k\in K_{2}}H_{k}(G_{i})
. Except with probability at most
\exp(-2b)
, we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(G_{i})\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{C_{2}b^{2}}{2^{4k}}\phantom{\Bigg|}

for all

\displaystyle k\in K_{1},\,\text{and}

\displaystyle h_{k}(G_{i})\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{C_{2}b^{2}}{k^{2}2^{2k}t^{4}n^{2}}\phantom{\Bigg|}\qquad\qquad

for all

\displaystyle k\in K_{2}\,,

for some constant
C_{2}
. We will assume these bounds in what follows.

Let us first consider pairs in
\bigcup_{k\in K_{1}}F_{k}(G_{i})
. If
uw\in F_{k}(G_{i})
then
|D_{uw}(G_{i})|\leqslant 2^{k+1}tn^{1/2}
and so we may bound the total contribution of pairs in
F_{k}(G_{i})
by
2^{2k+2}t^{2}nf_{k}(G_{i})
.

We consider two cases. If
2^{2k}\leqslant b/n
then we use the trivial bound
f_{k}(G_{i})\leqslant n^{2}
, so that the total contribution of pairs in
F_{k}(G_{i})
is at most

2^{2k+2}t^{2}nf_{k}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,2^{2k+2}t^{2}n^{3}\,.

Summing these contributions gives at most
8bt^{2}n^{2}
. For
k
with
2^{2k}>b/n
we use the above bound on
f_{k}(G_{i})
, which gives us that the total contribution of pairs in
F_{k}(G_{i})
is at most

2^{2k+2}t^{2}nf_{k}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,\frac{4C_{2}b^{2}t^{2}n}{2^{2k}}\,.

Summing these contributions gives at most
8C_{2}bt^{2}n^{2}
. And so

\sum_{k\in K_{1}}\sum_{uw\in F_{k}(G_{i})}D_{uw}(G_{i})^{2}\,\leqslant\,8(C_{2}+1)bt^{2}n^{2}\,.

We now consider pairs in
\bigcup_{k\in K_{2}}H_{k}(G_{i})
. If
uw\in H_{k}(G_{i})
then
D_{uw}(G_{i})\leqslant d_{uw}(G_{i})\leqslant 2^{k+1}t^{2}n
and so we may bound the total contribution of pairs in
H_{k}(G_{i})
by
2^{2k+2}t^{4}n^{2}h_{k}(G_{i})
. Summing these contributions, over
k\in K_{2}
, gives at most

\sum_{k\in K_{2}}2^{2k+2}t^{4}n^{2}h_{k}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,\sum_{k\geqslant 10}\frac{4C_{2}b^{2}}{k^{2}}\,\leqslant\,C_{2}b^{2}\,.

Summing all the contributions of all types gives that the summation is at most

\big(2^{10}+C_{1}+9C_{2}+8\big)\,\max\{bt^{2}n^{2},b^{2}\}\,,

as required.
∎

In the remainder of the section we prove Proposition

5.1.
Controlling
h_{k}(G_{i})

We shall now prove the part of Proposition

related to

.

We remark that the bound is trivial if
k2^{k}\leqslant b/t^{2}n^{2}
, as
h_{k}(G)\leqslant n^{2}
. So let us fix
i\leqslant m
and
k\in K_{2}
such that
k2^{k}>b/t^{2}n^{2}
.

We may think of
H_{k}(G)
as an auxiliary graph associated with the graph
G
. Rather than work directly with
H_{k}(G)
it is useful to partition
H_{k}(G)
depending on the degrees of
u,w
in the pair
uw

We define

H_{k,0}(G)\,:=\,\{uw\,:\,d_{uw}(G)\in[2^{k}t^{2}n,2^{k+1}t^{2}n)\,,\,\max\{d_{u}(G),d_{w}(G)\}\leqslant 2^{k/2}tn\}\phantom{\bigg|}

and set
h_{k,0}(G):=|H_{k,0}(G)|
. And for
k/2<j\leqslant\min\{k-6,\log_{2}(1/t)\}
, we define

H_{k,j}(G)\,:=\,\{uw\,:\,d_{uw}(G)\in[2^{k}t^{2}n,2^{k+1}t^{2}n)\,,\,\max\{d_{u}(G),d_{w}(G)\}\in[2^{j}tn,2^{j+1}tn)\}\phantom{\bigg|}

and set
h_{k,j}(G):=|H_{k,j}(G)|
.

Let
J=\{0\}\cup\{j:k/2<j\leqslant\min\{k-6,\log_{2}(1/t)\}\}
be the set of indices. As every pair in
H_{k}(G)
occurs in some

with

(5.1)

h_{k}(G)\,=\,\sum_{j\in J}h_{k,j}(G)\,.

The following lemma bounds the size of certain structures within the auxiliary graphs
H_{k,0}(G)
and
H_{k,j}(G)
. In fact, the lemma will be stated in the context of a graph
G\sim G(n,p)
for

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that
t\geqslant n^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}
, that
p\in(0,t)
, that
b\geqslant 3\log{n}
. Let
10\leqslant k\leqslant 2\log_{2}(1/t)
and
k/2<j\leqslant\min\{k-6,\log_{2}(1/t)\}
and let
G\sim G(n,p)
. With probability at least
1-\exp(-b)
:

(i)

H_{k,0}(G)
contains no star with degree at least
32b/k2^{k}t^{2}n
,

(ii)

H_{k,j}(G)
contains no star with degree at least
32b/(k-j)2^{k}t^{2}n
,

(iii)

H_{k,0}(G)
contains no matching with at least
32b/k2^{k}t^{2}n
edges.

Proof.

A quick observation is that we may assume the above values,
32b/k2^{k}t^{2}n
for example, are integer, as for non-integers the result follows by increasing
b
until
32b/k2^{k}t^{2}n
reaches the next integer.

We begin with the proof of (i). For the duration of the proof of (i) let us write
j
for
k/2
. We first describe the event that
H_{k,0}(G)
contains a large star as a union of certain events. The event depends on

•

a vertex
u\in V
,

•

a subset
W\subseteq V\setminus\{u\}
with
|W|=32b/k2^{k}t^{2}n
, and

•

a subset
\Gamma\subseteq V\setminus\{u\}
with
|\Gamma|\leqslant 2^{j}tn
.

For each choice of the above, we set
F(u,W,\Gamma)
to be the event that
\Gamma
is the neighbourhood of
u
in
G\sim G(n,p)
, and that

e(W,\Gamma)\,\geqslant\,2^{k}t^{2}n|W|\,.

Observe that the event described in (i) may only fail if
F(u,W,\Gamma)
occurs for some trio
u,W,\Gamma
. Simply take
u
to be the centre of the star,
\Gamma
to be its neighbourhood, and
W
be
32b/k2^{k}t^{2}n
neighbours of
u
in
H_{k,0}(G)
.

For a fixed choice of
W
and
\Gamma
we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[e(W,\Gamma)\right]\leqslant 2^{j}t^{2}n|W|=:\nu
. The event
F(u,W,\Gamma)
implies that
e(W,\Gamma)\geqslant 2^{k-j}\nu
. And so, by (
2.6
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(F(u,W,\Gamma)|N(u)=\Gamma\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,2\exp\left(-(k-j)2^{k-j-3}\nu\right)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle=\,2\exp(-(k-j)2^{k-3}t^{2}n|W|)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,2\exp(-2b)\,.\phantom{\Big|}

For the last line, we used the definition of
|W|
and the fact that
j=k/2
. We will now complete the proof with a union bound. Note that there are
n
choices of the vertex
u
and at most

n^{|W|}\,=\,\exp\left(\frac{32b\log{n}}{k2^{k}t^{2}n}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{32b}{k2^{k}}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(b/2)

choices of
W
(we used
k\geqslant 10
). And so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{u,W,\Gamma}F(u,W,\Gamma)\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum_{u,W,\Gamma}\mathbb{P}\left(N(u)=\Gamma\right)\mathbb{P}\left(F(u,W,\Gamma)|N(u)=\Gamma\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,2\exp(-2b)\sum_{u,W}\sum_{\Gamma}\mathbb{P}\left(N(u)=\Gamma\right)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,2n\exp(-3b/2)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,,\phantom{\Big|}

as required (we used
b\geqslant 3\log{n}
).

The bound for (ii) works in exactly the same way. The only difference is that
j
is now taken in the interval
(k/2,\min\{k-6,\log_{2}(1/t)\}]
, and the
(k-j)
term cancels with the equivalent term in
|W|
.

For (iii), the argument is similar, though a little more involved as we now need to consider a set of disjoint pairs (a matching)
u_{1}w_{1},\dots u_{f}w_{f}
, where
f:=32b/k2^{k}t^{2}n
. Let us write
W
for
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{f}\}
. We also need to consider a sequence of sets
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{f}
rather than a single set
\Gamma
. Let
F(u_{1},\dots,u_{f},w_{1},\dots,w_{f},\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{f})
be the event that
N(u_{g})=\Gamma_{g}
for all
g=1,\dots f
, and

\sum_{g=1}^{f}|N(w_{g})\cap\Gamma_{g}|\,\geqslant\,2^{k}t^{2}nf\,.

It is clear that the event that
h_{k,0}(G)\geqslant f
is contained in the union of the events
F(u_{1},\dots,u_{f},w_{1},\dots,w_{f},\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{f})
, where the union is taken over matchings
u_{1}w_{1},\dots,u_{f}w_{f}
and sets
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{f}
each with cardinality at most
2^{k/2}tn
.

We abbreviate
F(u_{1},\dots,u_{f},w_{1},\dots,w_{f},\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{f})
to
F(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\Gamma})
and write
N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}
for the event that
N(u_{g})=\Gamma_{g}
for all
g=1,\dots,f
.

For each such choice,
\sum_{g=1}^{f}|N(w_{g})\cap\Gamma_{g}|
is a sum of indicator functions representing whether certain edges are included in
G
. As edges may occur twice, it might not necessarily be binomial, but can be written as a sum of two binomials. And so, just as in the “star” case above, by (
2.6
) we have

\mathbb{P}\left(F(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\Gamma})|N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)\,\leqslant\,2\exp(-2b)\,.

The union bound argument is also similar to that given in (i). There are at most

n^{2f}\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{32b\log{n}}{k2^{k}t^{2}n}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(b/2)

choices of the matching (we used
t^{2}n\geqslant\log{n}
and
k\geqslant 10
). And so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\Gamma}}F(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\Gamma})\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\Gamma}}\mathbb{P}\left(N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(F(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\Gamma})|N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,2\exp(-2b)\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}}\sum_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\mathbb{P}\left(N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,2\exp(-3b/2)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,,\phantom{\Big|}

as required.

5.2.
Controlling

We shall now prove the part of Proposition
5.1
related to

.

The approach we used to control
h_{k,0}(G_{i})
in the previous subsection may also be applied here. That approach, bounding the size of stars and matchings in the relevant graph (
H_{k,0}(G)
in that case, and

now) will give the required result for almost all values of

.

However, when

is small (when
2^{k}\leqslant 2^{8}\sqrt{\log{n}}
) the approach does not work. The problem is that the union bound argument breaks down when considering a large matching, as there are
\exp(\Theta(f\log{n}))
ways to choose a matching with

edges, and, as we shall see below, the probability bound for a fixed matching is
\exp(-\Theta(2^{2k}f))
. For this reason we give a slightly different argument for

.

We first give the argument for
k
such that
2^{k}\geqslant 2^{8}\sqrt{\log{n}}
. As in Section
5.1
we shall bound the size of stars and matchings. Again we state the result in
G(n,p)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
t\geqslant Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}
, that
p\in(0,t)
, and that
b\geqslant 4tn
. Let
k\in K_{1}
be such that
2^{k}\geqslant 2^{8}\sqrt{\log{n}}
and let
G\sim G(n,p)
. With probability at least
1-\exp(-b)
:

(i)

F_{k}(G)
contains no star with degree
Cb/2^{2k}
,

(ii)

F_{k}(G)
contains no matching with
Cb/2^{2k}
edges.

Proof.

We begin with the proof of (i). We include
C
in our argument and fix its value later.

We first describe the event that
F_{k}(G)
contains a large star as a union of certain events. The event depends on

•

a vertex
u\in V
,

•

a subset
W\subseteq V\setminus\{u\}
with
|W|=Cb/2^{2k+1}
, and

•

a subset
\Gamma\subseteq V\setminus\{u\}
with
\big||\Gamma|-pn\big|\leqslant 2^{k-4}n^{1/2}
.

For each choice of the above, we set
E(u,W,\Gamma)
to be the event that
\Gamma
is the neighbourhood of
u
in
G\sim G(n,p)
, and that

\big|e(W,\Gamma)-p^{2}n|W|\big|\,\geqslant\,2^{k}tn^{1/2}|W|\,.

In fact, if (i) fails then
E(u,W,\Gamma)
occurs for some trio
u,W,\Gamma
. Simply take
u
to be the centre of the star,
\Gamma
to be its neighbourhood in
G
, and
W
be
Cb/2^{2k+1}
neighbours of
u
in
F_{k}(G)
, chosen so that
D_{uw}(G)
has the same sign for all
w\in W
.

For a fixed choice of
W
and
\Gamma
let
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[e(W,\Gamma)\right]=p|W||\Gamma|
, and note that, by the condition on
|\Gamma|
we have

\big|\mu\,-\,p^{2}n|W|\big|\,\leqslant\,2^{k-4}pn^{1/2}|W|\,\leqslant\,2^{k-4}tn^{1/2}|W|\,.

And so, the event
E(u,W,\Gamma)
implies that

\big|e(W,\Gamma)-\mu\big|\,\geqslant\,2^{k-1}tn^{1/2}|W|\,.

We now bound the probability of
E(u,W,\Gamma)
using (
2.5
), we may of course use that
\mu\leqslant 2p^{2}n|W|\leqslant 2t^{2}n|W|
, and that for

, we have
2^{k}tn^{1/2}\leqslant 2^{10}t^{2}n
. So that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(E(u,W,\Gamma)|N(u)=\Gamma\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-2^{2k-2}t^{2}n|W|^{2}}{4t^{2}n|W|\,+\,2^{k}tn^{1/2}|W|}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big(-2^{2k-13}|W|\big)\,.\phantom{\Big|}

The union bound argument is similar to that in Section

. There are at most

ways to choose the pair

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{u,W,\Gamma}E(u,W,\Gamma)\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum_{u,W,\Gamma}\mathbb{P}\left(N(u)=\Gamma\right)\mathbb{P}\left(E(u,W,\Gamma)\,|\,N(u)=\Gamma\right)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big(-2^{2k-13}|W|\big)\sum_{u,W}\sum_{\Gamma}\mathbb{P}\left(N(u)=\Gamma\right)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big((2\log{n}-2^{2k-13})|W|\big)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big(-2^{2k-14}|W|\big)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big(-Cb/2^{14}\big)\,.\phantom{\Big|}

Note that we used the bound,

.

.

For the remaining cases, with
2^{k}\leqslant 2^{8}\sqrt{\log{n}}
, we use a slightly different lemma. Instead of considering stars and matchings we now consider a union of disjoint stars.

In the remaining cases, the graph
F_{k}(G_{i})
is potentially very dense. The upper bound we must prove on
f_{k}(G_{i})
is
Cb^{2}/2^{4k}
, which, provided
C\geqslant 2^{16}
, is at least
b^{2}/\log{n}\geqslant n^{2}/\log{n}
. The following lemma states that in reasonably dense graphs we may find a small number of stars which cover a relatively large number of vertices.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices with at least
n^{2}/r^{2}
edges. Then there exist
r
vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
of
G

such that

\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}N(v_{i})\right|\,\geqslant\,\frac{n}{r}\,.

Remark 5.6
.

While this lemma is incredibly elementary, we didn’t immediately find a reference for it. If it hasn’t already been studied, it could be of some interest to find “best possible” results along the same lines. This is discussed further in the concluding remarks.

Proof.

Clearly the result is trivial if some vertex has degree at least
n/r
so we may assume all degrees are less than
n/r
.

Consider the digraph obtained from
G
by replacing each edge by two oriented edges (one in each direction). It clearly suffices to find
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
such that the union of the out-neighbourhoods in
D
of these vertices has cardinality at least
n/r
. We note that all in and out degrees in
D
are at most
n/r
and that
e(D)=2e(G)\geqslant 2n^{2}/r^{2}
.

We may find
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
greedily. Let
v_{1}
be a vertex of maximum out-degree and let us write
d_{1}
for this degree and let
S_{1}:=N^{+}(v_{1})
be the set of out-neighbours of
v_{1}
. We now remove from the digraph all edges into
S_{1}
. In the remaining digraph we find a vertex of maximum out-degree
v_{2}
with out-degree
d_{2}
, set
S_{2}=S_{1}\cup N^{+}(v_{2})
, and remove any other edges into
S_{2}
. We continue.

At step
i
, we may assume the current set
S_{i}
has cardinality at most
n/r
(else we are already done) and so the total number of removed edges so far is at most
n^{2}/r^{2}
. It follows that at least
n^{2}/r^{2}
edges remain and so
d_{i}\geqslant n/r^{2}
.

It is clear this process terminates in at most
r
steps, as
|S_{i}|=d_{1}+\dots+d_{i}\geqslant in/r^{2}
.
∎

The required result bounding
f_{k}(G_{i})
in the remaining range,
k\in K_{1}^{-}
, will follow from the above statement and the following bound on unions of stars in
F_{k}(G)
.

Lemma 5.7
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
t\geqslant Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}
, that
p\in(0,t)
, and that
b\geqslant n
. Let
k\in K_{1}
be such that
2^{k}\leqslant 2^{8}\sqrt{\log{n}}
and let
G\sim G(n,p)
. With probability at least
1-\exp(-b)
we have that any union of
r:=2^{2k}
stars in

F_{k}(G)
contains less than
Cb/2^{2k}
vertices.

Let us see that the bound
f_{k}(G_{i})\leqslant Cb^{2}/2^{4k}
follows from these two lemmas. By transferring the result of Lemma
5.7
to
G_{i}\sim G(n,i)
, as we have done previously (using
n^{2}\exp(-2b)\leqslant\exp(-b)
), except with probability at most
\exp(-b)
we have that any union of
r:=2^{2k}
stars in

F_{k}(G_{i})
contains less than
C_{1}b/2^{2k}
vertices, for some constant
C_{1}\geqslant 1
. We claim that this implies a bound of the form
f_{k}(G_{i})\leqslant Cb^{2}/2^{4k}
. Indeed, suppose for contradiction that

f_{k}(G_{i})\,>\,\frac{C_{1}^{2}b^{2}}{2^{4k}}\,=\,\frac{C_{1}^{2}n^{2}(b/n)^{2}}{2^{4k}}.

It would then follow from Lemma
5.5
that
F_{k}(G_{i})
contains
r^{\prime}:=n2^{2k}/C_{1}b
vertices whose neighbourhoods cover at least
C_{1}b/2^{2k}
vertices. This is a contradiction, as
r^{\prime}\leqslant 2^{2k}
.

Now that we have explained how the required bound follows from the lemmas, we complete the section with a proof of Lemma
5.7
.

The proof is similar to those given for Lemmas
5.3
and
5.4
. The event considered is sort of an amalgam of the star and matching cases. It will be useful to use the following notation. Given two sequences of sets
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
and
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{r})
, we define

|\mathbf{A}|\,:=\,\sum_{j=1}^{r}|A_{j}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\,:=\,\sum_{j=1}^{r}|A_{j}||B_{j}|\,.

We recall, from Section
2
, that for two sets of vertices
U,W
, we let
e(U,W)
count the number of edges with multiplicity. We extend this definition to sequences of vertex subsets. We define
\mathbf{A}\cap\mathbf{B}:=(A_{1}\cap B_{1},\dots,A_{r}\cap B_{r})
and
e(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}):=\sum_{j=1}^{r}e(A_{j},B_{j})
. For example, in the random graph
G\sim G(n,p)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[e(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\right]\,=\,p\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\,-\,p|\mathbf{A}\cap\mathbf{B}|\,=\,p\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\,+\,O(p|\mathbf{A}|)\,.

Proof of Lemma
5.7
.

We include
C
in our argument and fix its value later. We continue to use
r:=2^{2k}
. As before we express the event in question as a union. This time we take a union over

•

a sequence of vertices
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{r})
, and sequences of

•

vertex subsets
\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\dots,W_{r})
such that
|\mathbf{W}|=Cb/2^{2k+1}
, and

•

vertex subsets
\mathbf{\Gamma}=(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{r})
with
\big||\Gamma_{j}|-pn\big|\leqslant 2^{k-4}n^{1/2}
for all
j=1,\dots,r
.

For each choice of the above, we set
E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})
to be the event that
\Gamma_{j}
is the neighbourhood of
u_{j}
in
G\sim G(n,p)
for all
j=1,\dots,r
, and that

\left|e(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})-p^{2}n|\mathbf{W}|\right|\,\geqslant\,2^{k}tn^{1/2}|\mathbf{W}|\,.

If the event of the lemma fails, i.e., if there are
r=2^{2k}
stars in
F_{k}(G)
which cover at least
Cb/2^{2k}
vertices, then
E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})
occurs for some trio
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma}
. Furthermore, the sets in
\mathbf{W}
may be taken to be disjoint and not intersect
\mathbf{u}
. Let us justify this assertion.

First note that, in this case, at least
Cb/2^{2k+1}
of these pairs have deviations of the same sign. Now, simply take
\mathbf{u}
to be the centres of the stars,
\mathbf{\Gamma}
to be their neighbourhoods in
G
, and define
\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\dots,W_{r})
by taking the sets
W_{j}
to be disjoint and with
W_{j}
chosen among the neighbours of
u_{j}
in
F_{k}(G)
with the favoured sign.

We now bound the probability of the event
E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})
. For a fixed choice of
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{\Gamma}
let
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[e(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})\right]=p\mathbf{W}\cdot\mathbf{\Gamma}+O(p|\mathbf{W}|)
. By the triangle inequality, and the bounds on the
|\Gamma_{j}|
, we have

\big|\mu\,-\,p^{2}n|\mathbf{W}|\big|\,\leqslant\,2^{k-3}pn^{1/2}|\mathbf{W}|\,\leqslant\,2^{k-3}tn^{1/2}|\mathbf{W}|\,.

And so, the event
E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})
implies that

\big|e(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})-\mu\big|\,\geqslant\,2^{k-1}tn^{1/2}|\mathbf{W}|\,.

We now bound the probability of
E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})
using (
2.5
), we may of course use that
\mu\leqslant 2p^{2}n|\mathbf{W}|\leqslant 2t^{2}n|\mathbf{W}|
, and that for
k\in K_{1}
, we have
2^{k}tn^{1/2}\leqslant 2^{10}t^{2}n
. So that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})|N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-2^{2k-2}t^{2}n|\mathbf{W}|^{2}}{4t^{2}n|\mathbf{W}|\,+\,2^{k}tn^{1/2}|\mathbf{W}|}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big(-2^{2k-13}|\mathbf{W}|\big)\,.\phantom{\Big|}

The union bound argument is similar to those used before, although we need to be a little more careful this time. There are at most
n^{r}\leqslant\exp((\log{n})^{2})
ways to choose
\mathbf{u}
, at most
\binom{n}{|\mathbf{W}|}\leqslant\exp\big(|\mathbf{W}|\log(en/|\mathbf{W}|)\big)
ways to choose the elements of
\mathbf{W}
and
r^{|\mathbf{W}|}
ways to assign these elements to the sets
W_{1},\dots,W_{r}
. The total number of choices of the pair
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W}
is therefore at most

\exp\left((\log{n})^{2}\,+\,|\mathbf{W}|\log\left(\frac{enr}{|\mathbf{W}|}\right)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left((\log{n})^{2}\,+\,4k|\mathbf{W}|\right)\,.

So that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma}}E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma}}\phantom{\Big|}\mathbb{P}\left(N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{\Gamma})|N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big(-2^{2k-13}|\mathbf{W}|\big)\sum_{u,W}\sum_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\mathbb{P}\left(N(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big((\log{n})^{2}\,+\,4k|\mathbf{W}|\,-\,2^{2k-13}|\mathbf{W}|\big)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big(-2^{2k-14}|\mathbf{W}|\big)\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp\big(-Cb/2^{14}\big)\,.\phantom{\Big|}

This proves the required bound provided
C\geqslant 2^{14}
.
∎

5.3.
The increments
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})

We now deduce the following bound on increments.

Lemma 5.8
.

There is an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Let
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
. For all
n\leqslant b\leqslant t^{2}n^{2}
and
i\leqslant m
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\triangle}(G_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,\geqslant\,Cbt^{2}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,\phantom{\Big|}

and, for
b>t^{2}n^{2}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\triangle}(G_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,\geqslant\,\frac{Cb^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,.\phantom{\Big|}

Proof.

The proof relies on relating
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
to codegree deviations. From the proof of Lemma 5.4 of
[
20
]
we have

(5.2)

X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,=\,6D_{uw}(G_{i-1})\,-\,\mathbb{E}\left[6D_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\,\mid\,G_{i-1}\right]\,

where
e_{i}=uw
is pair chosen as the
i
th edge. As
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
has conditional mean
0
given
G_{i-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\triangle}(G_{i})^{2}\mid G_{i-1}\right]\,

\displaystyle=\,\textup{Var}(X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\mid G_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\,36\textup{Var}(D_{uw}(G_{i-1})\mid G_{i-1})\,\leqslant\,36\mathbb{E}\left[D_{uw}(G_{i-1})^{2}\mid G_{i-1}\right]\,.

And so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\triangle}(G_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,\leqslant\,36\frac{1}{N-i+1}\,\sum_{uw\not\in G_{i-1}}D_{uw}(G_{i-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{144}{n^{2}}\,\sum_{u}D_{u}(G_{i-1})^{2}\,.

The two inequalities now follow immediately from Corollary
5.2
.
∎

6.
Triangle counts in
G(n,m)
– upper bounds on deviations

In this section we prove upper bounds on the probability of triangle count deviations in
G(n,m)
. That is, we prove the upper bound part of Theorem
1.1
. We state below (Proposition
6.1
) a result which implies this part of Theorem
1.1
.

In order to state Proposition
6.1
we begin by recalling the functions
\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t)
,
\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}(b,t)
,
\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t)
,
\scalebox{0.8}{{CLIQUE}}(b,t)
and
M(b,t)
defined in the introduction. We recall that

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t)\,

\displaystyle:=\,b^{1/2}t^{3/2}n^{3/2}\,,\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}(b,t)\,

\displaystyle:=\,\frac{b^{2}t}{\ell^{2}}\,1_{b\leqslant n\ell}\,,\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t)\,

\displaystyle:=\,\frac{btn}{\ell}\,1_{b\geqslant n\ell}\,,\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{{CLIQUE}}(b,t)\,

\displaystyle:=\,\frac{b^{3/2}}{\ell^{3/2}}\quad\text{and}\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle M(b,t)\,

\displaystyle:=\,\max\{\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t),\scalebox{0.8}{{CLIQUE}}(b,t)\}\,.\phantom{\bigg|}

We shall present some lower bounds on
M(b,t)
which will be useful later on in this section. First, we claim that

(6.1)

M(b,t)\,\geqslant\,bt^{3/2}n\,.

for all
b\geqslant 1
. This is easily verified, as we have
\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t)\geqslant bt^{3/2}n
for
b\leqslant n
,
\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}(b,t)\geqslant bt^{3/2}n
for
n<b\leqslant n\ell
and
\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t)\geqslant bt^{3/2}n
for
n\ell<b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
. In particular, (
6.1
) implies that

(6.2)

M(b,t)\,\geqslant\,bt^{2}n\,.

We also claim that

(6.3)

M(b,t)\,\geqslant\,t\kappa(b,t)\,

for all
b\geqslant 32tn
, where
\kappa(b,t)
is as defined in Section
3
. To verify this, note that
\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t)=b^{1/2}t^{3/2}n^{3/2}\geqslant t^{2}n^{2}=t\kappa(b,t)
whenever
32tn\leqslant b<t^{1/2}n\ell
. Also,
\scalebox{0.8}{{STAR}}(b,t)=t\kappa(b,t)
whenever
t^{1/2}n\ell\leqslant b<n\ell
and
\scalebox{0.8}{{HUB}}(b,t)=t\kappa(b,t)
if
n\ell<b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
.

We recall from Section
2.3
the martingale representation for the triangle count deviation,
D_{\triangle}(G_{m})
:

D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[3\,\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\right]\,.

We may now state the upper bound result which we prove in this section.

Proposition 6.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds.
For all
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
3\log{n}\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,CM(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,.

We use a divide and conquer martingale approach to prove Proposition
6.1
. The curious reader may wonder why we cannot simply use a direct martingale approach in which we use Freedman’s inequality to bound the probability of such a deviation. The problem is that during the process there may occasionally be increments which are very large. If we let these increments occur within the martingale argument then we get weaker bounds. For this reason we consider large increments separately.

We now introduce truncated versions
X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
and
X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})
of the increments
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
and
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
. The value at which we truncate these increments will be

K_{{\bigwedge}}\,=\,K_{{\bigwedge}}(b,t)\,:=\,2^{8}\,\frac{M(b,t)}{bt}

and

K_{\triangle}\,=\,K_{\triangle}(b,t)\,:=\,2^{16}\,\frac{M(b,t)}{b}

respectively. We set

X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,=\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})1_{X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\leqslant K_{{\bigwedge}}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,=\,X_{\triangle}(G_{i})1_{X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\leqslant K_{\triangle}}\,.\phantom{\Big|}

We will also consider the random variables

Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,=\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})1_{X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})>K_{{\bigwedge}}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,=\,X_{\triangle}(G_{i})1_{X_{\triangle}(G_{i})>K_{\triangle}}\,.\phantom{\Big|}

Note that
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})=X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})+Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
and
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})=X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})+Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})
.

We may now express the deviation
D_{\triangle}(G_{m})
as
D_{\triangle}(G_{m})=D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})+N^{*}_{\triangle}(G_{m})
where

(6.4)

D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[3\,\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\right]\phantom{\Big|}

and

N^{*}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[3\,\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\right]\,.\phantom{\Big|}

Since the
Z
random variables are non-negative and the coefficients are at most
3t
and
1
respectively, we have

(6.5)

N^{*}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\leqslant\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[3t\,Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\right]\,.

We have expressed
D_{\triangle}(G_{m})
as a sum of
D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})
, which is a supermartingale (as the truncation may only reduce the value), and
N^{*}_{\triangle}(G_{m})
which counts the contribution of “large” increments. In order to complete the proof of Proposition
6.1
it clearly suffices to prove that there exists absolute constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for all
\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}n^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
3\log{n}\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
we have

(6.6)

\mathbb{P}\left(D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,C_{1}M(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)

and

(6.7)

\mathbb{P}\left(N^{*}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,C_{2}M(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)\,.

We prove these two statements in the following subsections.

6.1.
Controlling
D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})

In this subsection we prove (
6.6
). Since (
6.4
) expresses
D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})
as the final value of supermartingale with increments

\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i}\,:=\,3\,\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,,\phantom{\Bigg|}

we may use Freedman’s inequality to bound the probability that
D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\geqslant C_{1}M(b,t)
. All we need is some control of the increments

\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i}
. Since the coefficients are at most
3t
and
1
respectively, we have

(6.8)

|\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i}|\,\leqslant\,3t|X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})|\,+\,|X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})|

deterministically. As the truncation limits these absolute values, we have that
|\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i}|\leqslant 2^{17}M(b,t)/b
deterministically.

On the other hand, using (
6.8
) and the fact that
(x+y)^{2}\leqslant 2x^{2}+2y^{2}
, we have

(6.9)

|\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i}|^{2}\,\leqslant\,18t^{2}\,|X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})|^{2}\,+\,2|X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})|^{2}\,.\phantom{\Big|}

This bound, together with the following lemma, will allow to control the quadratic variation of the process. It will be proved using (
6.9
) and Lemmas
3.4
and
5.8
, which bound the conditional second moment of the increments
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
and
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
respectively.

Lemma 6.2
.

There is an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Let
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
. For
n\leqslant b\leqslant t^{2}n^{2}
and
i\leqslant m
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,\geqslant\,Cbt^{2}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,

and, for
b>t^{2}n^{2}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,\geqslant\,\frac{Cb^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,.

Furthermore, for all
b\geqslant n
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,\geqslant\,C(bt^{3}n^{2}+b^{2}t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,.

Proof.

We first remark that the bounds of Lemmas
3.4
and
5.8
also hold for
X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
and
X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})
since a truncation may only reduce variance.

The first two bounds now follow immediately from (
6.9
), Lemmas
3.4
and
5.8
, and the fact that

t^{2}\kappa(b,t)\,\leqslant\,\max\{bt^{2},b^{2}/n^{2}\}

throughout the range
b\geqslant n
.

The furthermore statement follows easily by a union bound. Note that the bound is obtained as the sum of the other two, multiplied by the number of steps
m\leqslant tn^{2}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove (
6.6
). We shall apply Freedman’s inequality to the supermartingale (
6.4
) with final value
D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})
. We have already established that the increments
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i}
satisfy
|\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i}|\leqslant 2^{17}M(b,t)/b
deterministically. There are now two cases depending on the value of
b
.

For
3\log{n}\leqslant b\leqslant n
we set
\beta=8C^{\prime}t^{3}n^{3}
, where
C^{\prime}\geqslant 1
is the constant of Lemma
6.2
, so that
\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,\geqslant\,\beta\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2n)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)
. Note also that we may choose a constant
C_{1}\geqslant 16
such that
\beta\leqslant C_{1}\scalebox{0.8}{{NORMAL}}(b,t)^{2}/b\leqslant C_{1}M(b,t)^{2}/b
.

It follows from Freedman’s inequality (Lemma
2.2
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,C_{1}M(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-C_{1}^{2}M(b,t)^{2}}{2\beta\,+\,2C_{1}M(b,t)(2M(b,t)/b)}\right)\,+\,\exp(-2b)\phantom{Bigg|}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-C_{1}^{2}M(b,t)^{2}}{2C_{1}M(b,t)^{2}/b\,+\,2C_{1}M(b,t)(2M(b,t)/b)}\right)\,+\,\exp(-2b)\phantom{Bigg|}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\leqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-C_{1}b}{6}\right)\,+\,\exp(-2b)\phantom{Bigg|}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\leqslant\,2\exp(-2b)\phantom{bigg|}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\leqslant\,\exp(-b)\,.\phantom{bigg|}

The argument is similar in the case
b>n
. We now set
\beta=4C^{\prime}(bt^{3}n^{2}+b^{2}t)
, so that
\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{X}^{\prime}_{i})^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]\,\geqslant\,\beta\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)
. Note also that we may choose a constant
C_{1}\geqslant 16
such that
\beta\leqslant C_{1}M(b,t)^{2}/b
for all
b\geqslant n
. The required bound now follows from Freedman’s inequality by exactly the same sequence of inequalities given above. This completes our proof of (
6.6
).

6.2.
Controlling
N^{*}_{\triangle}(G_{m})

In this subsection we complete the proof of Propostion
6.1
by proving the bound (
6.7
). That is, we show that for some constant
C_{2}
the inequality

\mathbb{P}\left(N^{*}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,C_{2}M(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)\,

holds for all
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
3\log{n}\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
. As we saw above, (
6.5
), we have

N^{*}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\leqslant\,3\sum_{i=1}^{m}t\,Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,.

And so, by (
2.9
), i.e., triangle inequality and union bound, it suffices to show that there exist absolute constants
C_{3}
and
C_{4}
such that

(6.10)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}t\,Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,\geqslant\,C_{3}\,M_{\triangle}(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-3b)

and

(6.11)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,\geqslant\,C_{4}\,M_{\triangle}(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,2\exp(-3b)\,.

We prove (
6.10
) and (
6.11
) in the following subsections.

6.2.1.
Controlling
\sum_{i=1}^{m}t\,Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})

In this subsection we prove (
6.10
). The proof is similar to the argument of Section
4.2
. Again the main objective is to relate the summation to the sum of squares of degree deviations, we may then deduce (
6.10
) from Proposition
3.2
. First, for
i\leqslant m
, we have that

X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,A_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,=\,2\sum_{v\in e_{i}}d_{v}(G_{i-1})\,\leqslant\,2\sum_{v\in e_{i}}d_{v}(G_{m})\,\leqslant\,4\max_{v\in e_{i}}d_{v}(G_{i})\,.

Thus, the event
X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})>K_{{\bigwedge}}
may only occur if
d_{v}(G_{m})>K_{{\bigwedge}}/4
for one of the two vertices
v\in e
. It follows that

(6.12)

Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,=\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})1_{X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})>K_{{\bigwedge}}}\,\leqslant\,4\sum_{v\in e_{i}}d_{v}(G_{m})1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>K_{{\bigwedge}}/4}\,.

By summing this expression over
i\leqslant m
, we obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,4\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}(G_{m})1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>K_{{\bigwedge}}/4}\sum_{e\in E(G_{m})}1_{v\in e}

(6.13)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}(G_{m})^{2}1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>K_{{\bigwedge}}/4}\,.

Recall, from (
6.2
), that by definition
K_{{\bigwedge}}=2^{8}M(b,t)/(bt)\geqslant 2^{8}tn
. In particular,
K_{{\bigwedge}}/4\geqslant 64tn
, and so the above summation above is
0
if
\Delta(G_{m})\leqslant 64tn
.

Note that, by applying Lemma
3.1
with “
b
” being
96tn
we have that

(6.14)

\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta(G_{m})>64tn\right)\,\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta(G_{m})>\frac{96tn}{\log(96/e)}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-96tn)\,.

In particular, if
b\leqslant 32tn
then there is probability at most
\exp(-3b)
that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
is non-zero, and (
6.10
) follows in this case.

Now assume
b\geqslant 32tn
. Note that
d_{v}(G_{m})\leqslant 2D_{v}(G_{m})
for all vertices
v
such that
d_{v}(G_{m})\geqslant 32tn
, and so, by (
6.13
), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}tZ_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,16t\sum_{v\in V}D_{v}(G_{m})^{2}\,

deterministically. We now use Proposition
3.2
to bound the probability that this sum is large. We recall, (
6.3
) , that
t\kappa(b,t)\leqslant M(b,t)
. And so, by Proposition
3.2
, there exists a constant
C_{3}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ tZ_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,\geqslant C_{3}\,M(b,t)\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ tZ_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,\geqslant C_{3}\,t\kappa(b,t)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp(-3b)\,.

This completes our proof of (
6.10
).

6.2.2.
Controlling
\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})

In this section we prove (
6.11
). We recall that
Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})
is defined as

Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,=\,X_{\triangle}(G_{i})1_{X_{\triangle}(G_{i})>K_{\triangle}}

where
K_{\triangle}=2^{16}M(b,t)/b
. And that our objective is to prove that for some constant
C_{4}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,>\,C_{4}M_{\triangle}(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,2\exp(-3b)\,

for all
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
3\log{n}\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
.

For each
i\leqslant m
we may control
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
in terms of the codegree of the edge chosen at step
i
. Specifically,

X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,A_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,=\,6d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\,.

And so, for the event
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})>K_{\triangle}
to occur, it is necessary that

. It follows that

(6.15)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,6\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})>K_{\triangle}/6\}}\,.

We consider two different types of contribution to the sum, depending on whether the codegree is “explained” by degree deviations, or not. For each edge
e_{i}=uv
added during the process, which has

, we classify

as

i-1\leqslant j\leqslant m
such that

Otherwise
e_{i}
is
bad
.

As

(6.16)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,6\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{e_{i}\text{ is good}}+6\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{e_{i}\text{ is bad}}\,,

we have reduced our objective to proving the following two lemmas, modulo (
2.9
), i.e., the usual union bound and triangle inequality.

Lemma 6.3
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
3\log{n}\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
. Then, except with probability at most
\exp(-3b)
,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is good}\}}\,\leqslant\ CM(b,t)\,.

Lemma 6.4
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
and
3\log{n}\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
. Then, except with probability at most
\exp(-3b)
,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is bad}\}}\,\leqslant\ CM(b,t)\,.

We begin with Lemma
6.3
.

Proof of Lemma
6.3
.

As degrees may only increase during the process, for each good edge
e_{i}
, we have
K_{\triangle}/6\leqslant d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\leqslant 64t(d_{u}(G_{m})+d_{v}(G_{m}))
.

In particular if
e_{i}
is good then
d_{v}(G_{m})\geqslant K_{\triangle}/2^{10}t
for one of the two vertices
v\in e_{i}
. It follows that

(6.17)

d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is good}\}}\,\leqslant\,128t\sum_{v\in e_{i}}d_{v}(G_{m})1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>K_{\triangle}/2^{10}t}\,.

And so

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is good}\}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,128t\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}(G_{m})1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>K_{\triangle}/2^{10}t}\sum_{e\in E(G_{m})}1_{v\in e}

\displaystyle=128t\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}(G_{m})^{2}1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>K_{\triangle}/2^{10}t}.

We also recall, from (
6.2
), that
K_{\triangle}/2^{10}t=64M(b,t)/bt\geqslant 64tn
. In particular, the last summation above is automatically
0
if
\Delta(G_{m})\leqslant 64tn
. As we saw above, in (
6.14
), we have
\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta(G_{m})>64tn\right)\leqslant\exp(-96tn)
. In particular, if
b<32tn
, then there is most probability
\exp(-3b)
that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is good}\}}
is non-zero.

We may now assume that
b\geqslant 32tn
. As
d_{v}(G_{m})\leqslant 2D_{v}(G_{m})
for all
v
such that
d_{v}(G_{m})\geqslant 64tn
, it follows that

128t\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}(G_{m})^{2}1_{d_{v}(G_{m})>K_{\triangle}/2^{9}t}\,\leqslant\,256t\sum_{v\in V}D_{v}(G_{m})^{2}\,.

We shall now use Proposition
3.2
to bound the probability that this sum is large. We recall (
6.3
), which states that
t\kappa(b,t)\leqslant CM(b,t)
. Now, taking
C
to be
2^{10}
times the constant of Proposition
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is good}\}}\,>\,CM(b,t)\right)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is good}\}}\,>\,Ct\kappa(b,t)\right)\phantom{\Bigg|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(256t\sum_{v\in V}D_{v}(G_{m})^{2}\,>\,Ct\kappa(b,t)\right)\phantom{\Bigg|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v\in V}D_{v}(G_{m})^{2}\,>\,\frac{C}{256}\kappa(b,t)\right)\phantom{\Bigg|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\exp(-3b)\,.\phantom{\bigg|}

∎

We shall now bound the sum of codegrees of bad edges. We do so by showing that if
e_{i}
is bad then it belongs to one of the graphs
H_{k}(G_{m})
of pairs with large codegree, defined in Section
5
. This already gives us an upper bound on the number of such edges, using the bounds proved in Section
5
. This is sufficient for a large range of possible codegrees.

For codegrees just a little larger than
K_{\triangle}
, up to
K_{\triangle}\ell
in fact, a more careful analysis is required. We use the bounds mentioned, but we also need to consider the probability that a large number of such edges are selected during the process. This is achieved via an argument which corresponds to a coupling with a process with independent increments, which is then binomially distributed.

Proof of Lemma
6.4
.

Recall that we want to find an absolute constant
C
so that for all
{Cn^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2}
and all
3\log n\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell
we have

(6.18)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is bad}\}}\,>\,CM(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-3b)\,.

As we mentioned in the discussion before the proof, we shall consider separately contributions from codegrees between
K_{\triangle}/6
and
K_{\triangle}\ell
, and codegrees larger than
K_{\triangle}\ell
. We shall reduce the proof to the statement that, for some constant
C>0
, the following two claims hold:

(6.19)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is bad},d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})>K_{\triangle}\ell\}}\,>\,CM(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-4b)

and

(6.20)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is bad},d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\in[K_{\triangle}/6,K_{\triangle}\ell)\}}\,>\,CM(b,t)\right)\,\leqslant\,2\exp(-4b)\,.

Indeed, if both (
6.19
) and (
6.20
) hold then, except with probability at most
3\exp(-4b)\leqslant\exp(-3b)
, we clearly have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is bad}\}}\,\leqslant\,2CM(b,t)\,.

Proving (
6.19
) is relatively straightforward. We shall bound the number pairs of large codegree using results from Section
5
. These bounds are sufficiently strong that even if every edge with codegree this large is selected the contribution is still at most
CM(b,t)
.

Recall from Section
5
that we defined the sets

H_{k}(G)\,:=\,\{uw\,:\,d_{uw}(G)\in[2^{k}t^{2}n,2^{k+1}t^{2}n)\,,\,d_{u}(G),d_{w}(G)\leqslant 2^{k-5}tn\}

for
k\in K_{2}:=\{10,\dots,\lfloor 2\log_{2}(1/t)\rfloor\}
. And we set
h_{k}(G)=|H_{k}(G)|
.

By (
6.2
) and the definition of
K_{\triangle}
as
2^{16}M(b,t)/b
we have
K_{\triangle}/6\,\geqslant\,2^{10}t^{2}n
. Let
k_{0}\geqslant 10
be the maximum
k
such that
2^{k}t^{2}n\leqslant K_{\triangle}/6
, and let
k_{1}
be the maximum
k
such that
2^{k}t^{2}n\leqslant K_{\triangle}\ell
. It is also helpful to note that, as
K_{\triangle}=2^{16}M(b,t)/b\geqslant 2^{16}t^{3/2}n
by (
6.1
), we have

(6.21)

k_{1}\,\geqslant\,k_{0}\,\geqslant\,k_{0}-12\,\geqslant\,\log_{2}(t^{-1/2})\,\geqslant\,\ell/2\,.

Claim:
If
e_{i}
is bad and
d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\,\geqslant\,K_{\triangle}\ell
then
e_{i}\in\bigcup_{k=k_{1}}^{2\log_{2}(1/t)}H_{k}(G_{m})
.

Proof of Claim:
First, as codegree is non-decreasing through the process

d_{e_{i}}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\,\geqslant\,K_{\triangle}\ell\,\geqslant\,2^{k_{1}}t^{2}n\,.

And so
d_{e_{i}}(G_{m})
is in the range of one of the sets
H_{k}(G)
with
k\geqslant k_{1}
.

All that remains is to prove that the second condition in the definition of
H_{k}(G)
is satisfied. For this it suffices that
d_{e_{i}}(G_{m})\,>\,64t(d_{u}(G_{m})+d_{v}(G_{m}))
, and this follows immediately from the definition of “bad”. This completes the proof of the claim.

By Proposition
5.1
, except with probability at most
\exp(-4b)
, we have, for some constant
C_{1}
, that

h_{k}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,\frac{C_{1}b^{2}}{k^{2}2^{2k}t^{4}n^{2}}\phantom{\Bigg|}\qquad\qquad\text{for all }k\in K_{2}\,\text{and }i\leqslant m\,.

Let us assume these bounds hold and prove the required bound on the sum in (
6.19
). As
d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\leqslant d_{e_{i}}(G_{m})\leqslant 2^{k+1}t^{2}n
for edges
e_{i}\in H_{k}(G_{m})
we have, except with probability at most
\exp(-4b)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is bad},d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})>K_{\triangle}\ell\}}\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{2\log_{2}(1/t)}2^{k+1}t^{2}n\frac{C_{1}b^{2}}{k^{2}2^{2k}t^{4}n^{2}}\phantom{\Bigg|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{2\log_{2}(1/t)}\frac{2C_{1}b^{2}}{k^{2}2^{k}t^{2}n}\phantom{\Bigg|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{4C_{1}b^{2}}{k_{1}^{2}2^{k_{1}}t^{2}n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{8C_{1}b^{2}}{k_{1}^{2}K_{\triangle}\ell}

(6.22)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{2^{21}C_{1}b^{3}}{\ell^{3}M(b,t)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,2^{21}C_{1}M(b,t)\,.\phantom{\Big|}

Note that the penultimate inequality used the definition of
K_{\triangle}
and the inequality
k_{1}\geqslant\ell/2
observed above. The final inequality used that
M(b,t)^{2}\geqslant\scalebox{0.8}{{CLIQUE}}(b,t)^{2}=b^{3}/\ell^{3}
. This completes the proof of (
6.19
).

We now consider (
6.20
). This time a more subtle argument is needed. We will still use bounds on the cardinalities
h_{k}(G_{i-1})
, but we must also consider how many of the edges of large codegree are actually selected during the process.

Let
H^{*}(G_{i}):=\bigcup_{k=k_{0}}^{k_{1}}H_{k}(G_{i})
and note that, if
e_{i}
is bad and
d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\in[K_{\triangle}/6,K_{\triangle}\ell)
then
e_{i}\in H^{*}(G_{i-1})
. Let
a^{*}(G_{m})
be the number of edges
e_{i}
selected in the process such that
e_{i}\in H^{*}(G_{i-1})
. As

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})1_{\{e_{i}\text{ is bad},d_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\in[K_{\triangle}/6,K_{\triangle}\ell)\}}\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,K_{\triangle}\ell a^{*}(G_{m})

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{2^{16}M(b,t)\ell a^{*}(G_{m})}{b}\,,

it suffices to prove, for some constant
C
that

\mathbb{P}\left(a^{*}(G_{m})\,>\,Cb/\ell\right)\,\leqslant\,2\exp(-4b)\,.

Let
E
be the event that

h_{k}(G_{i})\,\leqslant\,\frac{C_{1}b^{2}}{k^{2}2^{2k}t^{4}n^{2}}\phantom{\Bigg|}\qquad\qquad\text{for all }k_{0}\leqslant k\leqslant k_{1}\,\text{and }i\leqslant m\,.

By Proposition
5.1
,
\mathbb{P}\left(E^{c}\right)\leqslant\exp(-4b)
. We now prove that, if
C
is sufficiently large, then
\mathbb{P}\left(a^{*}(G_{m})\,>\,Cb/\ell\,\text{and}\,E\right)\leqslant\exp(-4b)
. This will complete the proof of the lemma.

On the event
E
, at each step we have

\displaystyle|H^{*}(G_{i-1})|\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{k_{1}}\frac{C_{1}b^{2}}{k^{2}2^{2k}t^{4}n^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{2C_{1}b^{2}}{k_{0}^{2}2^{2k_{0}}t^{4}n^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{C_{2}b^{2}}{\ell^{2}K_{\triangle}^{2}}

(6.23)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{C_{2}b^{4}}{\ell^{2}M(b,t)^{2}}\,,

for some constant
C_{2}
, where we used the inequality
k_{0}\geqslant\ell/2
from (
6.21
). And so, the event “
a^{*}(G_{m})\,>\,Cb/\ell\,\text{and}\,E
” implies that

(i)

at each step there are at most
C_{2}b^{4}/\ell^{2}M(b,t)^{2}
pairs in
H^{*}(G_{i-1})
, and

(ii)

in at least
Cb/\ell
steps we select
e_{i}\in H^{*}(G_{i-1})
.

By (i), we have that, the conditional probability (given
G_{i-1}
) that
e_{i}
is selected in
H^{*}(G_{i-1})
is always at most

\frac{C_{2}b^{4}}{\ell^{2}M(b,t)^{2}(N-i+1)}\,\leqslant\,\frac{2C_{2}b^{4}}{\ell^{2}M(b,t)^{2}N}\,.

Therefore, given any sequence
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{j}
, with
j=Cb/\ell
, the probability that
e_{i}
is selected in
H^{*}(G_{i-1})
at all these steps is at most

\left(\frac{2C_{2}b^{4}}{\ell^{2}M(b,t)^{2}N}\right)^{j}\,.

By a union bound, over at most
\binom{m}{j}\leqslant(em/j)^{j}
choices of the sequence, it follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(a^{*}(G_{m})\,>\,Cb/\ell\,\text{and}\,E\right)\,\leqslant\,\left(\frac{2emC_{2}b^{4}}{j\ell^{2}M(b,t)^{2}N}\right)^{j}\,.

We now use that
m/N=t
, that
M(b,t)^{2}\geqslant\scalebox{0.8}{{CLIQUE}}(b,t)^{2}=b^{3}/\ell^{3}
, and the value of
j=Cb/\ell
to deduce that

\mathbb{P}\left(a^{*}(G_{m})\,>\,Cb/\ell\,\text{and}\,E\right)\,\leqslant\,\left(\frac{2eC_{2}t\ell^{2}}{C}\right)^{Cb/\ell}\,.

If
C
is taken sufficiently large then the expression in brackets is at most
t^{1/2}=\exp(-\ell/2)
, and so

\mathbb{P}\left(a^{*}(G_{m})\,>\,Cb/\ell\,\text{and}\,E\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-Cb/2)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-4b)\,.

∎

7.
Triangle counts in
G(n,m)
– lower bounds on deviations

We now prove the lower bound part of Theorem
1.1
. That is, in each of the four regimes, we prove that a deviation of the corresponding order (for example, at least
cN(b,t)
in the normal regime) has probability at least
\exp(-b)
. It is perhaps surprising that this is most challenging in the normal regime.

In the normal regime (Section
7.1
) we prove this result using the converse Freedman inequality (Lemma
2.3
). In the other three regimes (star, hub and clique) we provide an explicit structure (graph) which is present in
G(n,m)
with probability at least
\exp(-b)
and is directly responsible for the triangle count deviation. The details are given in Section
7.2
.

7.1.
The normal regime

In this subsection we use the converse Freedman inequality (Lemma
2.3
) to prove that, for some absolute constant
c>0
, we have

(7.1)

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,cb^{1/2}t^{3/2}n^{3/2}\right)\,\geqslant\,\exp(-b)

for all pairs
(b,t)
in the normal regime. This regime is displayed in green in Figure
2
. If we fix the value of
t\geqslant Cn^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}
then this range corresponds to values of
b
such that

3\log{n}\,\leqslant\,b\,\leqslant\,\min\{t^{1/3}n\ell^{4/3}\,,\,t^{3/2}n^{3/2}\ell^{3/2}\}\,.

We also remark that in this regime, the truncation values are

K_{\triangle}\,=\,2^{16}b^{-1/2}t^{3/2}n^{3/2}\quad\text{and}K_{{\bigwedge}}\,=\,2^{8}b^{-1/2}t^{1/2}n^{3/2}\,.

In the previous section we considered a process with truncated increments, with final value
D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})
, introduced in (
6.4
). Let us recall that

D^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[3\,\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\right]\phantom{\Big|}

uses the truncated random variables
X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,=\,X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})1_{X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\leqslant K_{{\bigwedge}}}
and
X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,=\,X_{\triangle}(G_{i})1_{X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\leqslant K_{\triangle}}
where
K_{{\bigwedge}}=2^{8}M(b,t)/bt
and
K_{\triangle}=2^{16}M(b,t)/b
.

Unfortunately, the truncations cause the process to be a supermartingale rather than a martingale and we cannot apply the converse Freedman inequality, Lemma
2.3
. This leaves us with a difficult decision, as we still want to have a bound on the size of increments.

We shall therefore“repair” the martingale property. Let us define
D^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{X}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
as the sum of the rebalanced increments

\mathbb{X}^{\prime\prime}_{i}\,:=\,3\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,,

where
X^{\prime\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
and
X^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})
, are defined by

X^{\prime\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,=\,X^{\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\mathbb{E}\left[X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})1_{X_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})>K_{{\bigwedge}}}|G_{i-1}\right]

and

X^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,=\,X^{\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,+\,\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\triangle}(G_{i})1_{X_{\triangle}(G_{i})>K_{\triangle}}|G_{i-1}\right]\,.

Note that these random variables have been rebalanced so that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{X}^{\prime\prime}_{i}|G_{i-1}\right]=0
. That is,
D^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{X}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
is a martingale representation of
D^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})
. Let us now state a lower bound for
D^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{m})
.

Proposition 7.1
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
n^{-1/2}(\log{n})^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
, and suppose that

Concluding Remarks

One natural type of question to consider is whether it is possible to understand the tail probabilities discussed in this article even more precisely. For example, can we find the asymptotic value of the log of deviation probabilities in general in the
G(n,m)
and
G(n,p)
models?

In both models, this has been achieved in certain regions. For example, in the dense case, such results for both models are given for the normal regime in
[
20
]
. These results also cover a part of the normal regime in slightly sparser random graphs. And, as we discussed in the introduction, asymptotically tight bounds for the log probability associated with large deviations were also found for the triangle count in
G(n,p)
. It would be of interest to know whether these results may be extended to cover the all localised regimes (star, hub and clique).

One may also ask about the typical structure of random graphs which exhibit certain deviations. It seems likely that, with a little more work, weak structural versions of our results on triangles could be proved. For example, given a pair
t,a
which corresponds to the clique regime, one may ask whether
G_{m}
conditioned on having
a
more triangles than expected is very likely to contain a somewhat dense subgraph on
\Theta(a^{1/3})
vertices. A stronger structural result stating that in this case
G_{m}
contains a clique (or at least a very dense subgraph) on
(1+o(1))a^{1/3}
vertices would be very interesting. See Theorem 1.8 of
[
21
]
for related results for large deviations in
G(n,p)
.

It is also natural to ask whether the results generalise to other graphs. These results were obtained for relatively dense random graphs in
[
20
]
, but it would be interesting to know how far such results extend.

Let us also mention an elementary question, which seems to be open in general, and which is related to Lemma
5.5
. We write
\mathcal{G}_{n,m}
for the set of all graphs with
m
edges on vertex set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Given a set
S
of vertices of a graph
G
, let us set
N(S):=\bigcup_{v\in S}N(v)
, the union of the neighbourhoods of the vertices
v\in S
.

Question 1
.

Let
2\leqslant k\leqslant n\leqslant m
. What is the value of
\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n,m}}\max_{S\subseteq[n]:|S|=k}|N(S)|
?

In particular, one may consider cases where
k
is a constant, where
n
is large and where
m\approx p\binom{n}{2}
, for some
p\in(0,1)
. Two natural candidates for the extremal graph which ought to be considered are:

(i)

the complete graph on
\approx p^{1/2}n
vertices,

(ii)

a random (or quasirandom) graph with density
p
.

In case (i) it is trivial to optimise
|N(S)|
, as for any
S
containing a vertex of the complete graph we have
|N(S)|\approx p^{1/2}n
. While in case (ii) the size of
|N(S)|
is roughly the same for all subsets
S
with
|S|=k
, indeed
|N(S)|\approx(1-(1-p)^{k})n
. So we may ask in particular:

Question 2
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
and let
p\in(0,1)
. In the limit as
n\to\infty
, is it true that

\min\left\{\max_{S\subseteq[n]:|S|=k}|N(S)|\,:\,G\in\mathcal{G}_{n,p\binom{n}{2}}\right\}\,=\,(1+o(1))\min\{p^{1/2}n,(1-(1-p)^{k})n\}\,?

Definition A.1

A function

Given a subset

Proposition A.2

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
and let

is non-increasing. Also, let

be a subset of

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geqslant r}{F(x)}\leqslant\int_{A(r-d^{1/2})}F(u)du

Proof.

We lose no generality in assuming
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geqslant r
for all

. Now, for each

, let
x_{-}
be the point obtained by reducing the absolute value of each coordinate of

Let

be the open cube defined by

lies between the corresponding coordinates in

Since the diameter of a unit cube is

What can we say if a family

We shall apply this result for the function

\displaystyle\int_{A(r)}\exp(-\beta\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2})\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,e^{-\beta r^{2}/2}\int_{A(r)}\exp(-\beta(u_{1}^{2}+\dots+u_{d}^{2})/2)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,e^{-\beta r^{2}/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-(u_{1}^{2}+\dots+u_{d}^{2})/2)du

\displaystyle=\,(2\pi)^{d/2}e^{-\beta r^{2}/2}\,.

It is clear that the same calculation in an

a:=b/tnj2^{j-6}
is an integer.

If the event
|V_{j}|\geqslant a
occurs then there exists a set
A
of
a
vertices whose degrees sum to at least
2^{j}atn
. Let
G[A]
denote the graph induced by a set
A
, and
G[A,B]
the bipartite induced graph between sets
A
and
B
. It follows that either

(i)

e(G_{m}[A])\,\geqslant\,2^{j-2}atn
, or

(ii)

e(G_{m}[A,V\setminus A])\,\geqslant\,2^{j-1}atn

In either case this random variable,
X
, has hypergeometric distribution and so we may use the bound (
2.4
) given in Lemma
2.1
. We note that in both cases
\mu\leqslant atn
, and so setting
\nu=atn
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(X\geqslant 2^{j-2}atn\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-(j-2)2^{j-4}atn)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-2b)\,,

as required.
∎

We now turn to Proposition
3.2
, which we restate. We recall the definitions

\kappa(b,t)\,:=\,\begin{cases}tn^{2}\qquad&1\leqslant b<t^{1/2}n\ell\\
b^{2}/\ell^{2}&t^{1/2}n\ell\leqslant b<n\ell\\
bn/\ell&n\ell\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell\end{cases}

and

\kappa^{+}(b,t)\,:=\,\begin{cases}b^{2}/\ell_{b}^{2}\qquad&1\leqslant b<n\ell\\
bn/\ell&n\ell\leqslant b\leqslant tn^{2}\ell\end{cases}

See
3.2

Proof.

Fix
t\geqslant 2n^{-1}\log{n}
, and
i\leqslant m
. We prove that the bounds fail with probability at most
\exp(-2b)
. The proposition then follows by a union bound. We begin with the second statement, about the restricted sum
\sum_{u\,:\,d_{u}(G_{m})\geqslant 32tn}d_{u}(G_{m})^{2}
. We shall show that the result follows from the bounds on
V_{j}
stated in Lemma
3.1
. Let us define
J=J(b)
to be maximal such that
2^{j}tn\leqslant 2b/\ell_{b}
, if
b\leqslant n\ell
, and
J=\log_{2}(1/t)
, if
b>n\ell
. By Lemma
3.1
, except with probability at most
\exp(-2b)
, we have

(i)

|V_{j}|\leqslant b/tnj2^{j-7}
for all
j\geqslant 5
, and

(ii)

V_{j}=\emptyset
for all
j>J
.

For the range
32tn\leqslant b\leqslant n\ell
: Assuming (i) and (ii), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{m})\geqslant 32tn}d_{u}^{2}(G_{m})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=5}^{J}2^{2j+2}t^{2}n^{2}|V_{j}|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{9}\sum_{j=5}^{J}\dfrac{2^{j}btn}{j}

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{2^{J+11}btn}{J}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\dfrac{2^{12}b^{2}}{\ell_{b}^{2}}\,.

Where we used for the last inequality that
2^{j}\geqslant b/tn\ell_{b}
, and so, in particular,

J\,\geqslant\,\log\left(\dfrac{b}{etn\ell_{b}}\right)\,=\,\ell_{b}-\log(\ell_{b})\,\geqslant\,\dfrac{\ell_{b}}{2}.

This completes the proof of the bound on
\sum_{u\,:\,d_{u}(G_{m})\geqslant 32tn}d_{u}(G_{m})^{2}
in this range provided
C
is chosen to be at least
2^{12}
.

For the range
b\geqslant n\ell
: In this case
J=\log_{2}(1/t)
, and so
\lfloor J\rfloor\geqslant\ell
and
2^{\lfloor J\rfloor}\leqslant t^{-1}
. Assuming (i) and (ii), we may argue as above and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{m})\geqslant 32tn}d_{u}(G_{m})^{2}\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum_{j=5}^{\lfloor J\rfloor}2^{2j+2}t^{2}n^{2}|V_{j}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\dfrac{2^{\lfloor J\rfloor+11}btn}{\lfloor J\rfloor}

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{2^{11}bn}{\ell}\,.

This proves the required bound in this range provided
C\geqslant 2^{11}
.

We now prove the first bound on the unrestricted sum
\sum_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}
. Note that
\kappa(b,t)
does not depend on
b
in the first range, so the result in this range follows from the beginning of the next range (as increasing
b
corresponds to a stronger result). Also, we may continue to assume the bounds proved in the first part of the proof. In particular, if
d_{u}(G_{i})\geqslant 32tn
then
d_{u}(G_{m})\geqslant 32tn
and we have
D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}\leqslant d_{u}(G_{m})^{2}
.

In the range
t^{1/2}n\ell\leqslant b<n\ell
we have from the above bound that

\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{i})\geqslant 32tn}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}\,\leqslant\,\dfrac{2^{12}b^{2}}{\ell_{b}^{2}}

and by Lemma
3.3
, there is a constant
C_{1}
such that except with probability
\exp(-2b)
we have
\sum_{u:d_{u}(G_{i})\leqslant 32tn}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}
is at most
Ctn^{2}
if
b\leqslant n
, and at most
Cbtn
if
n\leqslant b\leqslant n\ell
. As
\max\{tn^{2},btn,b^{2}/\ell^{2}\}=b^{2}/\ell^{2}=\kappa(b,t)
, we have

\sum_{u}D_{u}(G_{i})^{2}\,\leqslant\,(2^{12}+C_{1})\kappa(b,t)\,,

which gives the result in this range.

In the range
n\ell\leqslant b<tn^{2}\ell
we similarly bound the two contributions by
2^{11}bn/\ell
and
C_{1}btn
respectively. This is at most
C\kappa(b,t)
, provided we choose
C\geqslant 2^{11}+C_{1}
.
∎

Appendix C
Variance lower bound

We shall prove Lemma
7.3
which we restate for convenience. We also recall the definition

\mathbb{X}^{\prime\prime}_{i}\,:=\,3\frac{(N-m)_{2}(m-i)}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})\,+\,\frac{(N-m)_{3}}{(N-i)_{3}}\,X^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,,

See
7.3

We remark that if
t
is a constant, i.e., in the case of dense random graphs, then this follows easily from the variance bounds given in Section 7 of
[
20
]
. Since the coefficient of
X^{\prime\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})
is
O(t)
and the coefficient of
X^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})
is
\Omega(1)
, it is clearly suffices to prove that, except with probability
\exp(-\Omega(n))
we have

(C.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\prime\prime}_{{\bigwedge}}(G_{i})^{2}\,\mid\,G_{i-1}\right]\,=\,O\left(\frac{n}{\ell^{2}}\right)

and

(C.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})^{2}\,\mid\,G_{i-1}\right]\,=\,\Omega(t^{2}n)\,.

Note that (
C.1
) follows from Lemma
3.4
(as the truncation can only reduce variance), so we need only prove that (
C.2
) holds, except with probability
\exp(-\Omega(n))
.

The variance in the next value of
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
comes from the different codegrees present in the graph (the variance would be zero if all codegrees
d_{uw}(G_{i-1})
of non-edges
uw
were equal). Recall the notation
D_{uw}(G_{m})
for the codegree deviation of the pair
uw
relative to its expected value in
G_{m}\sim G(n,m)
. By (
5.2
) we have

(C.3)

X_{\triangle}(G_{i})\,=\,6D_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\,-\,\mathbb{E}\left[6D_{e_{i}}(G_{i-1})\,\mid\,G_{i-1}\right]\,

where
e_{i}
denotes the
i
th edge of the process. In order to turn this into a comparison of codegrees we recall the elementary fact that for
X,Y
iid random variables with mean
0
we have
\mathbb{E}\left[(X-Y)^{2}\right]=2\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]
. Similarly, if
X,Y
are iid random variables with
\mathbb{E}\left[X|G_{i-1}\right]=0
, then
\mathbb{E}\left[(X-Y)^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]=2\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}|G_{i-1}\right]
. In the case of
X_{\triangle}(G_{i})
the randomness comes from the selection of the edge
e_{i}\in E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{i-1})
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\triangle}(G_{i})^{2}\,\mid\,G_{i-1}\right]\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{2(N-i+1)^{2}}\sum_{e^{\prime},e^{*}\not\in E(G_{i-1})}\big(X_{\triangle}(G_{i-1}\cup\{e^{\prime}\})-X_{\triangle}(G_{i-1}\cup\{e^{*}\})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{18}{(N-i+1)^{2}}\sum_{e^{\prime},e^{*}\not\in E(G_{i-1})}\big(D_{e^{\prime}}(G_{i-1})\,-\,D_{e^{*}}(G_{i-1}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\,\Omega(n^{-4})\sum_{e^{\prime},e^{*}\not\in E(G_{i-1})}\big(D_{e^{\prime}}(G_{i-1})\,-\,D_{e^{*}}(G_{i-1}\big)^{2}\,.

This does not yet consider the truncation, however, the truncation only affects deviations larger than
3t^{2}n
, and so, by the same argument,

\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\prime\prime}_{\triangle}(G_{i})^{2}\,\mid\,G_{i-1}\right]\,=\,\Omega(n^{-4})\sum_{e^{\prime},e^{*}\in E_{0}(G_{i-1})}\big(D_{e^{\prime}}(G_{i-1})\,-\,D_{e^{*}}(G_{i-1}\big)^{2}

where
E_{0}(G)=\{uw\not\in E(G_{i-1}):D_{uw}(G_{i-1})\leqslant 3t^{2}n\}
.

It is therefore sufficient to prove that, except with probability
\exp(-\Omega(n))
, there are at least
\Omega(n^{2})
pairs
uw\in E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{i-1})
with

(C.4)

d_{uw}(G_{i-1})\,\leqslant\,sd_{med}(G)\,-\,sn^{1/2}

and
\Omega(n^{2})
pairs
uw\in E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{i-1})
with

(C.5)

sd_{med}(G)\,+\,sn^{1/2}\,\leqslant\,d_{uw}(G_{i-1})\,\leqslant\,1.1s^{2}n\,+\,2sn^{1/2}\,

where
d_{med}(G)
is the median degree. Note this gives
\Omega(n^{4})
pairs of pairs
uw,u^{\prime}w^{\prime}\in E_{0}(G_{i-1})
with
|D_{e^{\prime}}(G_{i-1})\,-\,D_{e^{*}}(G_{i-1})|\geqslant 2sn^{1/2}
, which is clearly sufficient (since
t/2\leqslant s\leqslant t
).

We will now give a proof that (
C.5
) holds for
\Omega(n^{2})
pairs
uw\in E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{i-1})
, except with probability
\exp(-\Omega(n))
. It will be clear that, up to minor variations, the same proof also gives (
C.4
).

We begin with a lemma about the codegrees
d_{uw}(G)
from the perspective of a single vertex
u
. We work with
G\sim G(n,p)
and the observe that it transfers to our setting. For a vertex
u\in V(G)
let
F_{u}
be the event that

(i)

d(u)\in[0.9pn,1.1pn]

(ii)

|\{w\in V(G)\setminus N(u)\,:\,d_{uw}(G)\in[pd(u)+pn^{1/2},pd(u)+2pn^{1/2}]\}|\,<\,n/100

Lemma C.1
.

There exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that the following holds. Let
n^{-1/2}/c_{1}\leqslant t\leqslant 1/2
, let
p\in(t/2,t)
and let
G\sim G(n,p)
. Then, for each vertex
u\in V(G)
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{u}\right)\,\leqslant\,\exp(-c_{1}n)\,.

Proof.

If the event
F_{u}
occurs then we must have that
d(u)\in[0.9pn,1.1pn]
. And so if we consider conditioning on
N(u)=S
, for some set of vertices
S
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(F_{u}\right)\,=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{0.9pn\leqslant|S|\leqslant 1.1pn}\mathbb{P}\left(N(u)=S\right)\cdot\phantom{\bigg|}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\{w\in V(G)\setminus S\,:\,|N(w)\cap S|\in[p|S|+pn^{1/2},p|S|+2pn^{1/2}]\}|\,<\,n/100\right)\phantom{\bigg|}\,,

where we have already used that edges in
G(n,p)
are independent, so that the edges between
w
and
S
are independent of the fact that
S
is the neighbourhood of
u
. Since the sum of the probabilities
\mathbb{P}\left(N(u)=S\right)
is at most
1
, and all
S
of the same cardinality are equivalent, it suffices to show, for all integers
r
in the range
0.9pn\leqslant r\leqslant 1.1pn
that the event

\Big|\{w\in V(G)\setminus\{1,\dots r\}:\big|N(w)\cap\{1,\dots,r\}\big|\in[pr+pn^{1/2},pr+2pn^{1/2}]\}\Big|\,<\,n/100\\

has probability at most
\exp(-\Omega(n))
.
For each fixed vertex
w\in V(G)\setminus\{1,\dots r\}
the random variable
B_{w,r}:=|N(w)\cap\{1,\dots,r\}|
has distribution
Bin(r,p)
. The central limit theorem states that
(B_{w,r}-rp)/(rp(1-p))^{1/2}
converges to a standard normal as
r\to\infty
(and so in particular as
n\to\infty
). And, setting
\sigma=(rp(1-p))^{1/2}
we see that
pn^{1/2}=\lambda\sigma
where
\lambda=(pn/r(1-p))^{1/2}
and one may check that
\lambda\in[0.6,1.1]
. It follows that the probability that
B_{w,r}\in[pr+pn^{1/2},pr+2pn^{1/2}]
converges to
\Phi(2\lambda)-\Phi(\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in[0.6,1.1]
, and this value is always at least
1/10
.

As this event is independent as we vary over the values of
w\in V(G)\setminus\{1,\dots r\}
we see that
|\{w\in V(G)\setminus\{1,\dots r\}\,:\,|N(w)\cap\{1,\dots,r\}|\in[pr+pn^{1/2},pr+2pn^{1/2}]\}|
stochastically dominates a random variable with distribution
Bin(n-r,1/10)
, and so stochastically dominates
Y\sim Bin(n/3,1/10)
. The required bound now follows, as the Chernoff’s inequality gives us that
\mathbb{P}\left(Y<n/100\right)\leqslant\exp(-\Omega(n))
.
∎

The lemma will clearly be very useful in proving (
C.5
) holds for
\Omega(n^{2})
pairs
uw\in E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{i-1})
. We note immediately that up to a polinomial factor, which is easily absorbed in the
\exp(-\Omega(n))
term, the same results apply for
G_{i-1}
, and we may also apply a union bound over vertices
u
. So that, except with probability at most
\exp(-\Omega(n))
, we have

(C.6)

|\{w\in V(G_{i-1})\setminus N(u)\,:\,d_{uw}(G)\in[sd(u)+sn^{1/2},sd(u)+2sn^{1/2}]\}|\,<\,n/100

for all vertices
u\in G_{i-1}
with degree in the interval
[0.9sn,1.1sn]
.

By Proposition
3.2
there is probability at most
\exp(-\Omega(s^{2}n^{2}\ell))\leqslant\exp(-\Omega(n))
that linearly many vertices of
G_{i}
have degree outside the range
[0.9sn,1.1sn]
. We may therefore assume that there are at least
n/10
vertices
u
with
0.9sn\leqslant d_{med}(G)\leqslant d_{u}(G_{i-1})\leqslant 1.1sn
and for such vertices there are at least
n/100
vertices
w
, given by (
C.6
), for which

sd_{med}(G)\,+\,sn^{1/2}\,\leqslant\,d_{uw}(G_{i-1})\,\leqslant\,1.1s^{2}n\,+\,2sn^{1/2}\,.

It follows immediately that (
C.5
) holds for
\Omega(n^{2})
pairs
uw\in E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{i-1})
.

As remarked above, a very similar argument shows that (
C.4
) holds for
\Omega(n^{2})
pairs
uw\in E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{i-1})
. And this completes the proof of Lemma
7.3
.
