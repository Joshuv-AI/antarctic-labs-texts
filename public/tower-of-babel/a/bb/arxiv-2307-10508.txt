& Scott W. McCue

Keywords: complex-plane singularities; exponential asymptotics; transseries, resurgence

Abstract:
Burgers’ equation is an important mathematical model used to study gas dynamics and traffic flow, among many other applications. Previous analysis of solutions to Burgers’ equation shows an infinite stream of simple poles born at
t=0^{+}
, emerging rapidly from the singularities of the initial condition, that drive the evolution of the solution for
t>0
.
We build on this work by applying exponential asymptotics and transseries methodology to an ordinary differential equation that governs the small-time behaviour in order to derive asymptotic descriptions of these poles and associated zeros.
Our analysis reveals that subdominant exponentials appear in the solution across Stokes curves; these exponentials become the same size as the leading order terms in the asymptotic expansion along anti-Stokes curves, which is where the poles and zeros are located. In this region of the complex plane, we write a transseries approximation consisting of nested series expansions. By reversing the summation order in a process known as transasymptotic summation, we study the solution as the exponentials grow, and approximate the pole and zero location to any required asymptotic accuracy.
We present the asymptotic methods in a systematic fashion that should be applicable to other nonlinear differential equations.

As mentioned, the application we are concerned with is Burgers’ equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\,t>0,

(1)

which is an extremely well-studied nonlinear partial differential equation (PDE).
On the real line, it illustrates advection-driven wave steepening competing with linear diffusion, and also acts as an idealised model for a number of physical processes including gas dynamics or traffic flow. This PDE was studied in the regime
\mu\ll 1
using exponential asymptotics by Chapman et al.
[
15
]
, where the authors described how singularities in the analytic continuation of the PDE solution generate behaviour known as “Stokes’ phenomenon”. By studying Stokes’ phenomenon in the solution, the authors explained the onset of smoothed shock fronts at a catastrophe point. Stokes’ phenomenon also plays an important role in explaining the small-time behaviour that we consider in the present study.

We consider the
\mu=\mathcal{O}(1)
regime of Burgers’ equation (
1
), supplemented with an initial condition that has simple poles in the complex
x
-plane, appearing as complex-conjugate pairs. As studied in VandenHeuvel et al.
[
48
]
in some detail (as well as Appendix B of
[
15
]
), in the small-time limit there is an inner problem near one of these poles governed by

-\frac{1}{2}U-\frac{\xi}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}\xi}+U\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}U}{\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}},

(2)

where
\xi
is a similarity variable, subject to the far-field condition

U\sim-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\xi}\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad\xi\to-\mathrm{i}\infty

(3)

(which turns out to act as two boundary conditions). The condition (
3
) is obtained by matching the solution of (
2
) to a small-time expansion on the real line in
x
. The main objective of our study is to use exponential asymptotics and transseries to estimate the location of the singularities and zeros of
U(\xi)
in the complex
\xi
-plane. To interpret the results in terms of the original model, these poles and zeros in the
\xi
-plane are poles and zeros of Burgers’ equation
(
1
) that emerge from the singularities of the initial condition
u(x,0)
with direction
\arg(\xi)
and speed
\mathcal{O}(t^{-1/2})
as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

The problem (
2
)–(
3
) provides an instructive example to apply our methodology for the following reasons. First, it serves as a prototype for inner problems that arise by treating PDEs in a small-time limit, or similarity solutions more generally. Second, all singularities of Burgers’ equation are simple poles, so our analysis of (
2
)–(
3
) avoids any complications from branch cuts and multiple Riemann sheets. And finally, there is an exact solution of (
2
)–(
3
) (which can be written in terms of parabolic cylinder functions; see (
13
)) that can be used to check the results, although this solution itself is difficult to resolve numerically. While our analysis turns out to be rather detailed in parts, we shall attempt to make the broader strategy accessible to those interested in other differential equations.

We provide a comprehensive background on exponential asymptotics and transseries in Appendix
A
. Additionally, a brief stripped-back summary of our methodology is as follows:

•

By direct substitution into (
2
), expand the solution
U(\xi)
as a divergent series

U\sim\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{(0)}}{\xi^{2m-1}}\quad\mbox{as}\quad\xi\rightarrow\infty,

(4)

which applies in a sector of the
\xi
-plane including the negative imaginary axis. This step is straightforward (see section
2
).

•

Apply techniques in exponential asymptotics to locate the Stokes curves (also known in the literature as Stokes lines) and determine the leading-order form of the exponentially small correction, which turns out to be

U_{\mathrm{exp}}\sim 2\pi\mathrm{i}\Lambda\xi^{-\mathrm{i}/2\mu}\mathrm{e}^{-\chi},

where
\chi=\xi^{2}/4\mu
and
\Lambda
is a constant (see section
3
). The important Stokes curves lie where
\chi
is real and positive
[
26
]
. This exponentially small term
U_{\mathrm{exp}}
is said to “switched on” as the Stokes curves are crossed in the complex
\xi
-plane.

•

Extend the series (
4
) to include full asymptotic expansions for the exponential correction and its exponential correction, and so on, to obtain a transseries

U\sim\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{(0)}}{\xi^{2m-1}}+\tau\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{(1)}}{\xi^{2m-1}}+\tau^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{(2)}}{\xi^{2m-1}}+\ldots=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{(n)}}{\xi^{2m-1}}

(5)

as
\xi\rightarrow\infty
,
where
\tau=\sigma\xi^{-(1+\mathrm{i}/2\mu)}\mathrm{e}^{-\chi}
and
a_{0}^{(0)}=0
; such an expansion remains well-ordered provided
|\tau|\ll 1
, i.e. away from the anti-Stokes curves (also knowns as anti-Stokes lines)
\mathrm{Re}(\chi)=0
(see section
4.1
).

•

Write the transseries (
5
) as a transasymptotic summation

U\sim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\xi^{2m-1}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau^{n}a_{m}^{(n)}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m}(\tau)}{\xi^{2m-1}},

(6)

where now
\tau
is no longer required to be small, and therefore (
6
) holds in neighbourhood of anti-Stokes curves (see section
4.2
)

•

By direct substitution, determine
A_{m}
(section
4.2
) and subsequently locate the singularities (section
5
) and zeros (section
6
) of
A_{0}(\tau)
,
A_{1}(\tau)
, etc., which ultimately provides an asymptotic approximation for the singularities and zeros of the original function
U(\xi)
.

As mentioned already, the above procedure does not rely on the exact solution of (
2
)–(
3
) and can be adopted to other nonlinear ODE problems
[
21
]
. We discuss our results in section
7
and conclude in section
8
.

2
Problem Formulation

In this section we briefly present the formulation of our problem (
2
)–(
3
), summarising relevant parts of Ref.
[
48
]
. We first derive the small-time asymptotic behaviour of Burgers’ equation (
1
) with an initial condition that is singular at
x=\pm\mathrm{i}
, and observe that the small-time expression ceases to be asymptotic in a small neighbourhood about each of the initial singularities. We then rescale (
1
) to study the behaviour in the neighbourhood of
x=\mathrm{i}
, and find that it is governed by (
2
)–(
3
) in a scaled similarity variable. Finally, for reference, we note the exact solution of (
2
)–(
3
) in terms of parabolic cylinder functions.

2.1
Small-time limit of Burgers’ equation

The background for (
2
)–(
3
) starts with Burgers’ equation (
1
) with the initial condition

u(x,0)=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(7)

This initial condition was chosen as it has a pair of simple poles at
x=\pm\mathrm{i}
, which allows us to more easily apply matched asymptotic expansions to study the small-time behaviour, especially the birth of infinitely many simple poles that are born at
x=\pm\mathrm{i}
at
t=0^{+}
and propagate in the complex
x
-plane for
t>0

[
48
,
15
]
(for initial conditions with other types of singularities, including branch points, the analysis is much more complicated
[
48
]
).

By applying a naive outer expansion of the form

u(x,t)\sim u_{0}(x)+tu_{1}(x)+t^{2}u_{2}(x)+\ldots\quad\mathrm{as}\quad t\to 0^{+},

(8)

we find by direct substitution that

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}},\qquad u_{1}=\frac{2(-\mu+x+3\mu x^{2})}{(1+x^{2})^{3}},

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{60\mu^{2}x^{4}-32\mu x+48\mu x^{3}-x^{2}(120\mu^{2}-7)+12\mu^{2}-1}{(1+x^{2})^{5}}.

In the limit that
x\to\mathrm{i}
, we have

u_{0}\sim-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\frac{1}{x-\mathrm{i}},\qquad u_{1}\sim\left(-\frac{1}{4}-\mu\mathrm{i}\right)\frac{1}{(x-\mathrm{i})^{3}}\qquad u_{2}\sim\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{5\mu}{2}-6\mathrm{i}\mu^{2}\right)\frac{1}{(x-\mathrm{i})^{5}}

(9)

(with similar expressions in the limit
x\to-\mathrm{i}
). Clearly the strongest singular behaviour for
u_{0}
,
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
,
\ldots
, increases in strength by two at each order, which suggests that (
8
) remains valid for
x\in\mathbb{C}
, except for a region in the neighbourhood of the singular points
x=\pm\mathrm{i}
.

2.2
Inner expansion near
x=\mathrm{i}

By comparing terms
u_{0}
with
tu_{1}
(or
tu_{1}
with
t^{2}u_{2}
, etc.) near
x=\pm\mathrm{i}
, we see the expansion (
8
) ceases to be well-ordered when
x\mp\mathrm{i}=\mathcal{O}(t^{1/2})
as
t\to 0^{+}
. Understanding what happens near the points
x=\pm\mathrm{i}
is important, because these points generate an infinite number of singularities in the solution
u(x,t)
of Burgers’ equation (
1
), which emerge from the singularities of
u_{0}(x)
rapidly for small time. For what follows, we will concentrate on the singular point
x=\mathrm{i}
, but our analysis applies in the same fashion to
x=-\mathrm{i}
.

The above argument suggests an inner region near
x=\mathrm{i}
, which, in terms of the new coordinates

\xi=\frac{x-\mathrm{i}}{t^{1/2}},\qquad u=\frac{1}{t^{1/2}}\bar{U}(\xi,t),

(10)

holds for
\xi=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
t\to 0^{+}
. In terms of (
10
), Burgers’ equation (
1
) is written exactly as

t\frac{\partial\bar{U}}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{U}-\frac{1}{2}\xi\frac{\partial\bar{U}}{\partial\xi}+U\frac{\partial\bar{U}}{\partial\xi}=\mu\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{U}}{\partial\xi^{2}}.

(11)

To leading order, we write
\bar{U}\sim U(\xi)
as
t\to 0^{+}
, so after substituting into (
11
) we end up with our main ODE (
2
). To match back onto the outer region (
8
), we rewrite (
9
) in terms of the inner variables (
10
), which gives

U\sim-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\xi}+\left(-\frac{1}{4}-\mathrm{i}\mu\right)\frac{1}{\xi^{3}}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}-\frac{5\mu}{2}-6\mathrm{i}\mu^{2}\right)\frac{1}{\xi^{5}}+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{-7})\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\xi\to-\mathrm{i}\infty.

(12)

To leading order, this condition reduces to (
3
). As demonstrated in Ref.
[
48
]
, a Liouville-Green (WKB) analysis of (
2
)–(
3
) shows that only the leading-term of (
12
) (i.e., (
3
)) is required to uniquely determine the solution; the correction terms in (
12
) shall be required for our transseries analysis later on (see (
4
) and (
41
)).

3
Stokes switching analysis

In the limit
|\xi|\to\infty
, the solution to (
2
)–(
3
) exhibits Stokes’ phenomenon in the complex
\xi
-plane. This means that subdominant exponential terms are present in different regions of the solution, and that these terms appear as particular curves, known as Stokes curves, are crossed. In this section, we will locate the Stokes curves, which are shown in Figure
2
, and determine the form of the exponential terms. Further, we will determine the location of anti-Stokes curves, or curves across which the exponential terms transition in size from exponentially small to exponentially large. In the neighbourhood of these anti-Stokes curves, an algebraic power series representation of the solution ceases to be valid, which will lead to a reordering of terms as part of our transasymptotic analysis in section (
4
).

Figure 2:
Stokes sectors for the inner problem (
2
)–(
3
). There are no exponential terms present in the sector containing the negative imaginary axis, where the boundary condition (
3
) is specified. Any exponentially small terms that could potentially appear in the expansion would be larger than the boundary behaviour on the negative imaginary axis. This region is shown as Sector ①. The anti-Stokes curves in the lower half-plane therefore have no effect on the solution. Exponentially small terms appear across the Stokes curves that follow the real axis, in sectors ② and ④. As the anti-Stokes curves in the upper half-plane are crossed into Sector ③, the exponential terms become large, and the asymptotic series terms re-order. The competing exponentials give rise to simple poles and zeros in the solution, represented by crosses and discs, respectively. The poles and zeroes in this figure actual examples computed for
\mu=1
.

3.1
Power series representation

To study the asymptotic solution to (
2
)–(
3
), we start by setting
\xi=z/\epsilon
and
U=\epsilon V
, and treat the solution when
z=\mathcal{O}(1)
. In these coordinates, studying the large-
|\xi|
behaviour is equivalent to taking the small-
\epsilon
limit while keeping
z=\mathcal{O}(1)
. The exponential asymptotics summarised in this section could be performed in terms of
\xi
, but it is more straightforward in the scaled coordinate
z
; since
\epsilon
is an artificial small parameter that is used to index the size of
|\xi|
, it will not appear in the final result (
38
) when written in terms of
\xi
.

In terms of
z
and
\epsilon
, our governing ODE (
2
) becomes

2\epsilon^{2}\left(\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}V}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}-V\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}z}\right)+z\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}z}+V=0.

(14)

Motivated by the far-field condition (
3
), we apply the algebraic power series ansatz

V\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2n}V_{n}(z)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0,

(15)

with

V_{0}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2z},

(16)

which describes the solution in a region of the complex plane containing the negative imaginary axis. From the Stokes structure of the solution, we will later be able to determine the broader sector in which this algebraic series is valid. By substituting (
15
)-(
16
) into (
14
) and matching powers of
\epsilon
, we can obtain a recurrence relation for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle z\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{n}}{\mathrm{d}z}+V_{n}=-2\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}V_{n-1}}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}+2\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{j}}{\mathrm{d}z}V_{n-1-j},\qquad V_{n}(z)={o}\left(\frac{1}{|z|}\right)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad z\to-\mathrm{i}\infty.

(17)

The first few terms of the series are therefore given by

V(z)\sim-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2z}+\left(\frac{z-2\mathrm{i}\mu}{2z^{3}}\right)\epsilon^{2}+\left(\frac{3\mathrm{i}z^{2}+16\mu z-36\mathrm{i}\mu^{2}}{6z^{5}}\right)\epsilon^{4}+\ldots\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(18)

Note that since
V_{0}
is singular at
z=0
, then so will
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
,
\ldots
. Now we must determine the region in which this power series approximation is valid, and how the solution behaves outside of this region. We therefore need to understand the Stokes’ phenomenon present in
V(z)
.

3.2
Late-order terms

A key step in our analysis is determining the asymptotic behaviour of
V_{n}
in the limit that
n\to\infty
. The presence of the summation term in (
17
) means that
V_{n}
cannot easily be evaluated exactly for arbitrary
n
. Instead, to determine the asymptotic behaviour of the late-order terms, we follow the method proposed by
[
16
]
and applied in numerous studies (eg.
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
15
,
25
,
35
,
36
]
) and assume that these terms diverge in a factorial-over-power fashion according to the ansatz

V_{n}\sim\frac{G(z)\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{\chi(z)^{n+\gamma}}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad n\to\infty\,,

(19)

where
\chi(z)=0
at the singular point in the leading-order term
V_{0}
(
z=0
) and
\gamma
is a constant to be determined. By substituting the ansatz (
19
)
into the recurrence relation (
17
) and matching in the limit that
n\to\infty
, we find at the first two orders:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(V_{n+1}):

\displaystyle\quad z=2\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathrm{d}z},

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(V_{n}):

\displaystyle\quad z\frac{\mathrm{d}G}{\mathrm{d}z}+G=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathrm{d}z}+4\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathrm{d}z}\frac{\mathrm{d}G}{\mathrm{d}z}+2\mu G\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\chi}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}.

(21)

Using the condition
\chi(0)=0
and solving both of these equations gives

\chi=\frac{z^{2}}{4\mu},\qquad G=\Lambda z^{-\mathrm{i}/2\mu},

(22)

where
\Lambda
is a constant.

Determining the constant
\Lambda
requires using matched asymptotic expansions to ensure that the late-order terms (
19
) are consistent with a local expansion of the solution in a small neighbourhood around the pole (
z=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
as
\epsilon\to 0
), identified as the region in which the series terms of (
15
) become comparable in size in the asymptotic limit, and the expansion therefore breaks down. From this matching, we can determine
\Lambda
, which depends on
\mu
. The technical details of this calculation are presented in Appendix
B
. Computed values are presented in Figure
3
. It is significant that
|\Lambda|
appears to grow indefinitely as
\mu\to 0^{+}
; this suggests that our analysis breaks down in this limit, as the asymptotic scalings are different to those of the small-
\mu
regime, studied in
[
15
]
. The two analyses, ours and that provided in
[
15
]
, combine to explain the solution behaviour for both
|\mu|\ll 1
and
\mu=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Figure 3:
The result of approximating
\Lambda
different values of
\mu
. This approximation was obtained by evaluating the ratio in (

) at each value of
\mu
for
n=1000
. This choice ensured that all values converged up to at least six decimal places. The value of

becomes large as

The late-order terms (
19
) therefore take the form

V_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda z^{-\mathrm{i}/2\mu}\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{(z^{2}/4\mu)^{n+\gamma}}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(23)

For this expression to be consistent with the leading-order (
16
), the strength of the singularity at
z=0
in (
23
) with
n=0
must be the same as the strength of the singularity in
V_{0}
(ie. equal to one). Applying this condition shows that
\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{\mathrm{i}}{4\mu}
.

3.3
Stokes switching

Given the divergent asymptotic expansion (
15
) and the late-order terms (
19
), it follows that an optimally truncated series has an exponential remainder of the form (
92
) (see (
29
) below). We can determine the location of Stokes curves, which are found where this exponential term is maximally subdominant compared to the leading-order term in the algebraic series
[
26
]
; in other words, where

\mathrm{Im}(\chi)=0\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathrm{Re}(\chi)>0.

(24)

These are the curves across which the exponential term is switched by the algebraic series, and hence behaviour on an exponentially small scale can appear or vanish as these curves are crossed. The condition on
\mathrm{Re}(\chi)
forces the changing contribution to be exponentially small in the asymptotic limit, rather than exponentially large.

Anti-Stokes curves are found where the exponential term becomes asymptotically comparable in size to the algebraic power series. These curves generally denote a change in dominance between two asymptotic contributions, and satisfy

\mathrm{Re}(\chi)=0.

(25)

If an anti-Stokes curve is crossed, the terms in the asymptotic series reorder, as the exponential terms are comparable in size to the algebraic terms. The power series approximation (
15
) is no longer valid in the region of the complex plane beyond the anti-Stokes curve. The Stokes structure is illustrated in Figure
2
.

Note that Figure
2
is presented in terms of
\xi
. While the exponential asymptotic calculations were performed in terms of
z
, we recall that taking
\epsilon\to 0
with
z=\mathcal{O}(1)
corresponds to the limit
|\xi|\to\infty
. For simplicity of interpretation, we show the Stokes structure in terms of
\xi
. We will use the fact that
\mathrm{arg}(z)=\mathrm{arg}(\xi)
to describe the Stokes structure in this section in terms of
\xi
.

In the red shaded regions of Figure
2
, the exponential term is larger than the algebraic series. In the blue shaded regions, the exponential is smaller than the algebraic series. In the unshaded regions, there are no exponential contributions in the solution.

In the region
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(-3\pi/4,-\pi/4)
, which contains the negative imaginary axis, any possible exponential term would necessarily dominate the algebraic leading-order solution in the asymptotic limit, as
\mathrm{Re}(\chi)<0
. We therefore conclude that there are no exponential contributions in this region, otherwise the boundary condition (
3
) cannot be satisfied. This region is bounded by an anti-Stokes curve that satisfies (
25
) along
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)=-\pi/4
.

In the region
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(-\pi/4,\pi/4)
, it can be seen that
\mathrm{Re}(\chi)>0
, and any exponential term present in the solution is small in the asymptotic limit. This region contains a Stokes curve that satisfies (
24
) along the positive real axis; the exponentially small behaviour in the solution must therefore appear as this curve is crossed into the upper half-plane.

Finally, the curves
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)=\pi/4
and
3\pi/4
are anti-Stokes curves that satisfy (
25
), across which the exponential behaviour becomes comparable in size to the algebraic power series. The series approximation (
15
) is no longer valid in this case, as the terms in the asymptotic series reorders as these curves are crossed. We will use transasymptotic analysis in Section
4
to study the asymptotic solution beyond this anti-Stokes curve, and to approximate the location of the poles, which are generally located in the region
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(\pi/4,3\pi/4)
.

3.4
Matched asymptotic expansions

There are a number of different methods for determining the behaviour that is switched on as the Stokes curve is crossed, such as hyperasymptotics or Borel summation methods (eg. in
[
11
,
10
,
42
,
37
,
38
,
45
]
). We apply the matched asymptotic expansion method of Ref.
[
41
]
. This approach is typical of Stokes switching analysis (eg. see similar analyses in
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
15
,
25
,
35
,
36
]
) but we include an outline of the details here in order to showcase our full methodology.

We first truncate the power series (
15
) after
N
terms to minimise the truncation error. We apply the heuristic from
[
14
]
and truncate after the smallest term in the series. For this purpose, we use the late-order ansatz (
19
) to identify the value of
N
at which consecutive terms of the series are the same size in the asymptotic limit that
\epsilon\to 0
and
n\to\infty
. This gives
N\sim|\chi|/\epsilon^{2}
as
\epsilon\to 0
. We therefore set the truncation point to be
N=|\chi|/\epsilon^{2}+\omega
, where
\omega\in[0,1)
is chosen so that
N
takes integer value. The truncated series is

V(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\epsilon^{2n}V_{n}(z)+R_{N}(z),

(26)

where
R_{N}(z)
is the truncation remainder. If the series is truncated optimally, this remainder will describe the exponentially small behaviour in the solution
[
10
]
. We substitute the truncated series (
26
) into the governing equation (
14
) and apply the recurrence relation (
17
) to simplify. This produces

\epsilon^{2}\left(2\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{N}}{\mathrm{d}z}-2V_{0}R_{N}+\ldots\right)+zR_{N}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sim z\epsilon^{2N}V_{N}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0,

(27)

where the omitted terms are subdominant to those which were retained in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
.

The right-hand side of (
27
) is exponentially small compared to the left-hand side except in an asymptotically small neighbourhood surrounding the Stokes curve. Applying a Liouville-Green (or WKB) ansatz to the homogeneous version of (
27
) shows that, away from the Stokes curve

R_{N}\sim kG\mathrm{e}^{-\chi/\epsilon^{2}}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0,

(28)

where
k
is some arbitrary constant. Motivated by this expression, we apply a variation of parameters approach to describe the solution to the inhomogeneous expression (
27
). We replace to constant
k
with a function
\mathcal{S}(z)
, known as a Stokes multiplier, to give

R_{N}=\mathcal{S}G\mathrm{e}^{-\chi/\epsilon^{2}}.

(29)

The Stokes multiplier
\mathcal{S}
captures the rapid variation in the exponential behaviour in the vicinity of the Stokes curve, while being asymtotically constant away from the Stokes curve. Substituting this expression into (
27
) and using the late-order ansatz (
19
) for the right-hand side gives

\epsilon^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}}{\mathrm{d}z}G\mathrm{e}^{-\chi/\epsilon^{2}}\sim\frac{z\epsilon^{2N}}{2\mu}\frac{G\Gamma(N+\gamma)}{\chi^{N+\gamma}}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(30)

We apply a change of variables to use
\chi
as the independent variable, and use (
20
) to simplify the expression. After some algebra, this gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}}{\mathrm{d}\chi}\sim\epsilon^{2N-2}\frac{\Gamma(N+\gamma)}{\chi^{N+\gamma}}\mathrm{e}^{\chi/\epsilon^{2}}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(31)

We now express the singulant in polar coordinates,
\chi=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
. The direction of rapid variation is perpendicular to the Stokes curve
[
41
]
, which corresponds to setting
r
to be constant and considering only angular variation. We therefore set
N=r/\epsilon^{2}+\omega
. This gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\sim\mathrm{i}r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\epsilon^{2r/\epsilon^{2}+2\omega-2}\frac{\Gamma(r/\epsilon^{2}+\omega+\gamma)}{\chi^{r/\epsilon^{2}+\omega+\gamma}}\exp\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\right)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(32)

Applying Stirling’s formula and simplifying this expression gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\sim\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2\pi r}}{\epsilon^{2\gamma+1}}\exp\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}-1-\mathrm{i}\theta\right)+\mathrm{i}\theta(1-\omega-\gamma)\right)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(33)

The right-hand side of this expression is exponentially small except in a small neighbourhood near
\theta=0
, which corresponds to the Stokes curve. To determine the variation near this curve, we define a local variable
\theta=\epsilon\vartheta
, and find that

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}}{\mathrm{d}\vartheta}\sim\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2\pi r}}{\epsilon^{2\gamma}}\exp\left(-\frac{r\theta^{2}}{2}\right)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(34)

Integrating this expression gives

\mathcal{S}\sim\int_{-\infty}^{\vartheta/\sqrt{r}}\mathrm{e}^{-s^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}s+C,

(35)

where
C
is some constant of integration. In fact, taking the limit that
\vartheta\to-\infty
corresponds to moving into the sector
\arg(\xi)<0
, in which the exponential is not present. Hence
C=0
. This gives the jump in exponential behaviour as the Stokes curve is crossed as

\left[\mathcal{S}\right]_{-}^{+}=\lim_{{\vartheta}\to\infty}\mathcal{S}-\lim_{{\vartheta}\to-\infty}\mathcal{S}\sim\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{\epsilon^{2\gamma}},

(36)

and therefore the exponential contribution for
\arg(\xi)>0
is given by

V_{\mathrm{exp}}\sim\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}G}{\epsilon^{2\gamma}}\mathrm{e}^{-\chi/\epsilon^{2}}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(37)

Returning to original coordinates using
\xi=z/\epsilon
and
U=\epsilon V
gives

U_{\mathrm{exp}}\sim 2\pi\mathrm{i}\Lambda\xi^{-\mathrm{i}/2\mu}\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^{2}/4\mu}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty.

(38)

This expression is the leading-order behaviour behaviour of the exponential terms in regions where these terms are present but small in the asymptotic limit (ie. Sectors ② and ④ in Figure
2
). Note that the
\epsilon
parameter disappears entirely from this expression. This is expected, as it was an artificial parameter introduced to index the size of
|\xi|
.

We have therefore determined the solution behaviour in the complex plane in Sectors ② and ④ from Figure
2
, or
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(0,\pi/2)
or
(3\pi/4,\pi)
. To continue beyond from these regions into Sector ③, or
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(\pi/4,3\pi/4)
requires understanding how the exponential terms behave as the anti-Stokes curve is crossed.

4
Transasymptotic Analysis

We wish to determine the location of the poles and zeros in the solution to (
2
)–(
3
), which requires studying the behaviour of the solution as the anti-Stokes curves on
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)=\pi/4
and
3\pi/4
are crossed. As these curves are crossed, the exponentially-small terms that appear across the Stokes curves, described in Section
3
, grow and become asymptotically large. This changes the asymptotic balance of the solution, and the series approximation in (
15
) is no longer valid. Poles are typically located along curves that asymptotically approach anti-Stokes curves in the large-
|\xi|
limit
[
22
]
, so locating the poles requires understanding the behavior of the solution in the region where the exponentials grow and become asymptotically large.

Approximating the solution behaviour in this region requires first writing the solution in the region where the exponential terms are small as a transseries, or a double series in multiple scales which describes both algebraic and exponential asymptotic contributions to the solution behaviour. The final step is to sum the transseries over all exponential terms at each algebraic order so that it remains valid even where the exponential terms reorder in size. This process is known as transasymptotic analysis, and it will produce an expression which we study to determine the location of poles caused by growing exponential terms in the asymptotic behaviour.

4.1
Formulating the transseries

We begin by integrating (
2
) and applying the boundary condition in (
3
), to obtain

\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=-\frac{1}{2}\xi U+\frac{1}{2}U^{2}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}.

(39)

This is not strictly necessary, but it reduces the order of the differential equation and makes the subsequent analysis more straightforward.

The transseries must contain an algebraic power series for
U
, as well as the exponentially small terms whose leading-order behaviour is given by (
38
). Furthermore, due to the nonlinearity in the problem, the transseries will contain exponential terms with exponents that are integer multiples of the exponential term in (
38
)
[
45
,
1
]
. The presence of these additional exponentials suggests that we apply a transseries of the form

U(\xi)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-n\xi^{2}/{4\mu}}U_{n}(\xi)\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad|\xi|\to\infty,

(40)

where
U_{n}(\xi)
can be represented as an algebraic series in
\xi
, and
\sigma
is a transseries parameter that will be determined from the exponential contribution derived in Section
3
, given explicitly in (
38
).

In Sector ① of Figure
2
, corresponding to
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(-\pi,0)
, there are no exponentially small contributions. We capture this behaviour by setting
\sigma=0
, which means that the asymptotic solution behaviour is entirely described by the power series (
41
). The analysis in Section
3
showed that exponentially small terms are present in sectors ② and ④, or
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(0,\pi/4)
and
(3\pi/4,\pi)
respectively, which have the leading-order behaviour given in (
38
). In these sectors we set
\sigma\neq 0
, which produces exponentially small transseries terms.

The power series expansion for
U_{0}
as
\xi\to\infty
may be found by direct substitution (or by matching with the outer expansion via (
12
)); we find that

U_{0}(\xi)\sim-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\xi}+\left(-\frac{1}{4}-\mathrm{i}\mu\right)\frac{1}{\xi^{3}}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}-\frac{5\mu}{2}-6\mathrm{i}\mu^{2}\right)\frac{1}{\xi^{5}}+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{-7})\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty.

(41)

Matching at higher orders of the exponential term gives equations for the subsequent transseries components,

\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=\frac{(n-1)}{2}\xi U_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n}U_{j}U_{n-j},\qquad n\geq 1.

(42)

We treat (
42
) differently for the case
n=1
, which causes the first term on the right-hand side to vanish. A Green-Liouville (or WKB) analysis of the solution for
n=1
gives

U_{1}\sim K_{1}\xi^{-\mathrm{i}/2\mu}\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad|\xi|\to\infty.

(43)

where
K_{1}
is a constant that has yet to be determined. If
n>1
, the first term on the right-hand side of (
42
) does not vanish, and the asymptotic dominant balance is obtained by balancing the terms on the right-hand side. This gives

U_{n}\sim K_{n}\xi^{1-n(1+\mathrm{i}/2\mu)}\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad|\xi|\to\infty,

(44)

where
K_{n}=(-1)^{n+1}K_{1}
. The power of
\xi
in (
44
) suggests that we write the transseries (
40
) as

U(\xi)\sim\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{a^{(0)}_{m}}{\xi^{2m-1}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma^{n}\xi^{-n(1+\mathrm{i}/2\mu)}\mathrm{e}^{-n\xi^{2}/4\mu}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{(n)}_{m}}{\xi^{2m-1}},

(45)

where
a^{(n)}_{m}
is the constant in the
m
th term in the power series associated with
U_{n}
. The range of index
m
is chosen for
a_{m}^{(0)}
so that the powers of
\xi
are consistent for corresponding choices of
m
across the
n=0
and
n>0
cases. Finally, we are able to write (
45
) as a typical transseries ansatz by defining a new variable
\tau
,

\tau=\sigma{\xi}^{-(1+\mathrm{i}/2\mu)}\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^{2}/4\mu},

(46)

Note that
\tau
is exponentially small in sectors ② and ④ of the solution, corresponding to regions in which the exponential terms are present and small. We collect the terms to write

U(\xi)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{(n)}_{m}}{\xi^{2m-1}}.

(47)

which is valid in the limit that
|\xi|\to\infty
in regions where the exponential contributions are asymptotically small, or
\tau\to 0
; this corresponds to
|\xi|\to\infty
in the sector
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(-5\pi/4,\pi/4)
.

We require that
a^{(0)}_{0}=0
to be consistent with (
45
) and the algebraic series solution, which is
\mathcal{O}(\xi^{-1})
as
|\xi|\to\infty
. The values of
a^{(0)}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
may be read directly from the series (
41
), giving

a^{(0)}_{1}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2},\qquad a^{(0)}_{2}=-\frac{1}{4}-\mathrm{i}\mu,\qquad a^{(0)}_{3}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}-\frac{5\mu}{2}-6\mathrm{i}\mu^{2}.

(48)

We may obtain all terms of the form
a^{(0)}_{m}
by directly calculating subsequent terms of the algebraic power series (
41
).

We determine
\sigma
by considering the term indexed by
a^{(1)}_{0}
, given by
\tau\xi a^{(1)}_{0}
. This term is the leading-order behaviour of the first exponential correction to the algebraic series, and is given by
U_{\mathrm{exp}}
from (
38
) in sectors ② and ④. By equating these expressions, we have

\tau\xi a^{(1)}_{0}=2\pi\mathrm{i}\Lambda\xi^{-\mathrm{i}/2\mu}\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^{2}/4\mu}.

(49)

Substituting the definition of
\tau
in (
46
) into (
49
) and simplifying the result shows

\sigma a^{(1)}_{0}=2\pi\mathrm{i}\Lambda.

(50)

We are free to set
\sigma=2\pi\mathrm{i}\Lambda
, which gives
a^{(1)}_{0}=1
. Recall that
\sigma=0
in sector ①. The value of
\sigma
is different on either side of Stokes curves in the complex plane – this is typical behaviour for the transseries parameter.

We now substitute the transseries (
47
) into the differential equation (
39
) and match at higher orders in
\tau
, recalling that
\tau\to 0
exponentially fast as
|\xi|\to\infty
. At higher orders, we find

\mathcal{O}(\tau):\,a_{1}^{(1)}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{8\mu},\qquad\mathcal{O}(\tau\xi^{-1}):\,a_{2}^{(1)}=\frac{3\mathrm{i}\mu}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{128\mu^{2}}.

(51)

We can continue to match in this fashion, and it is straightforward to compute an arbitrary number of coefficients
a_{m}^{(n)}
in both the
m
and
n
-indexed directions. Incrementing
m
gives

a_{3}^{(1)}=10\mathrm{i}\mu^{2}+\frac{55\mu}{12}-\frac{7\mathrm{i}}{16}+\frac{1}{24\mu}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{256\mu^{2}}+\frac{1}{3072\mu^{3}},

(52)

while incrementing
n
gives

a_{0}^{(2)}=-1,\qquad a_{1}^{(2)}=2\mu-\frac{1}{4\mu},\qquad a_{2}^{(2)}=-12\mu^{2}-7\mathrm{i}\mu+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{8\mu}-\frac{1}{32\mu^{2}}.

(53)

The values presented in (
48
) and (
51
) give sufficient information about the behaviour in sectors ② and ④ to approximate the pole locations in sector ③.

4.2
Transasymptotic analysis

The transseries (
47
) is a valid asymptotic expression for the solution behaviour in
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(-5\pi/4,\pi/4)
. In sector ①, or
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(-\pi,\pi)
, the exponential terms are absent, so
\sigma=0
. In sectors ② and ④, or
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(0,\pi/4)
and
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(-5\pi/4,-\pi)
, the exponential terms are small, and hence
\tau
is exponentially small in the limit that
|\xi|\to\infty
. To determine the location of poles in the solution, we must approximate the behaviour in
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)\in(\pi/4,3\pi/4)
, or sector ③. Continuing the solution into this region requires extending the expression (
47
) so that it remains valid as the anti-Stokes curves are crossed and the exponential terms in the transseries become asymptotically large.

The key idea of transasymptotic analysis is that the terms in the transseries (
40
) can be resummed to approximate the behaviour in regions where
\tau
is not exponentially subdominant compared to
\xi
. This is possible because the transseries (
40
) describes all of the subdominant exponential terms in the solution; hence, any change in the asymptotic balance of the problem corresponds to a rearrangement of the terms in the transseries.

For convenience, we rewrite the transseries as

U(\xi)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{n}a^{(n)}_{m}}{\xi^{2m-1}},

(54)

as
|\xi|\to\infty
and
\tau\to 0
, where we set
a_{0}^{(0)}=0
to be consistent with (
47
). This is a divergent asymptotic series, and we change the order of summation to obtain the asymptotically valid expression

U(\xi)\sim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\xi^{2m-1}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau^{n}a^{(n)}_{m}\right)

(55)

as
|\xi|\to\infty
and
\tau\to 0
. Finally, we sum the series over the index
n
. This is the resummation step, which allows us to describe the asymptotic solution behaviour even in regions where
\tau
is not small. We write the resultant expression as

U(\xi)\sim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m}(\tau)}{{\xi^{2m-1}}},

(56)

which is valid as
|\xi|\to\infty
, irrespective of the size of
\tau
. In regions of the complex
\xi
-plane where the inner sum of (
55
) converges, it is equal to
A_{m}(\tau)
; however,
A_{m}(\tau)
can also be evaluated in regions of the complex plane where the inner sum does not converge. It is a continuation of the inner sum from (
55
) to the entire complex plane.

Substituting (
56
) into the differential equation (
39
) gives an expression for the series terms

\mu\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\xi^{2m-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{m}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}-\frac{(2m-1)A_{m}}{\xi^{2m}}\right)=-\frac{\xi}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m}}{{\xi^{2m-1}}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{l}A_{m}}{{\xi^{2l+2m-2}}}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}.

(57)

Because
\tau
is a function of
\xi
, the chain rule gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{m}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}\xi}\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{m}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=-\tau\left[\frac{\xi}{2\mu}+\frac{1}{\xi}\left(1+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\mu}\right)\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{m}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}.

(58)

Applying (
58
) to (
57
) and matching terms as
|\xi|\to\infty
gives the recurrence relation

\displaystyle A_{0}-A_{0}^{2}-\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=0,

(59)

\displaystyle A_{1}+2\mu A_{0}-2A_{0}A_{1}-\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}-\left(\mathrm{i}+2\mu\right)\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}

\displaystyle=0,

(60)

\displaystyle A_{k+1}-2\mu\left(2k-1\right)A_{k}-\sum_{m=0}^{k+1}A_{m}A_{k+1-m}-\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{k}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}-\left(\mathrm{i}+2\mu\right)\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{k}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad k\geq 1.

(61)

Solving (
59
) gives

A_{0}(\tau)=\frac{\tau}{k+\tau},

(62)

where
k
is an arbitrary constant. To determine the value of this constant, we must match this expression to the leading exponential behaviour in sector ②, or (
38
), where
\tau
is small. In this region, the inner sum from (
55
) converges, and

A_{0}(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau^{n}a_{0}^{(n)}\sim a_{0}^{(0)}+\tau a_{0}^{(1)}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\tau\to 0.

(63)

Recall that
a_{0}^{(0)}=0
and
a_{0}^{(1)}=1
. Hence, we find that
A_{0}(\tau)\sim\tau
as
\tau\to 0
, so
k=1
and

A_{0}(\tau)=\frac{\tau}{1+\tau}.

(64)

This rational expression is singular at
\tau=-1
. This is sufficient for us to approximate the pole locations to leading order; however, it is straightforward to improve the approximation by calculating the first correction term,
A_{1}
.

The governing equation for
A_{1}(\tau)
is given by (
60
), and the boundary condition can be found by considering the behaviour of
A_{1}(\tau)
in sector ②, where
\tau\to 0
and the inner sum of (
55
) converges. In this region, we have

A_{1}(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau^{n}a_{1}^{(n)}\sim a_{1}^{(0)}+\tau a_{1}^{(1)}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\tau\to 0.

(65)

Using the values determined in (
51
), we find that

A_{1}(\tau)\sim 1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{8\mu}\right)\tau\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\tau\to 0.

(66)

Solving (
60
) subject to this boundary condition gives

A_{1}(\tau)=\frac{1}{(1+\tau)^{2}}\left[\frac{\tau(1-4\mu\mathrm{i})(1+4\mu\mathrm{i}\tau)}{8\mu}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\right].

(67)

The functions
A_{0}(\tau)
and
A_{1}(\tau)
provide sufficient information for us to approximate the pole locations in sector ③ up to the first correction term in the limit that
|\tau|\to 0
. We could continue this process to find further corrections using (
61
), with boundary conditions that depend on
a_{m}^{(0)}
and
a_{m}^{(1)}
. For instance, the next correction term is

\displaystyle A_{2}(\tau)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{128\mu^{2}(1+\tau^{3})}\left(32\mu^{2}\left(32\mu^{2}+12i\mu-1\right)\tau^{3}+32\mu^{2}(1+4i\mu)+\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left(192i\mu^{3}+32\mu^{2}-1\right)\tau+\left(1536\mu^{4}+704i\mu^{3}-128\mu^{2}-16i\mu+1\right)\tau^{2}\right).

(68)

Finally, we approximate the behaviour of
U(\xi)
in sector ③ using the resummed series (
56
), as well as (
64
) and (
67
),

U(\xi)\sim\frac{\tau\xi}{1+\tau}+\frac{1}{\xi}\frac{1}{(1+\tau)^{2}}\left[\frac{\tau(1-4\mu\mathrm{i})(1+4\mu\mathrm{i}\tau)}{8\mu}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(|\xi|^{-3})\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty.

(69)

This expression has the correct asymptotic behaviour in sector ②, and can be extended past the anti-Stokes curves at
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)=\pi/4
and
3\pi/4
.

Note that (
69
)is only valid in regions where
\sigma\neq 0
, or the upper-half complex plane. In the lower-half plane we instead have
\sigma=0
, and therefore
A_{k}(\tau)=0
for
k\geq 0
. We therefore do not predict any far-field poles to be located in sector ①, or the entire lower-half complex plane (poles close to the origin of the
\xi
-plane may lie in the lower-half plane, and indeed this does occur for
\mu\lessapprox 0.147

[
48
]
).

5
Pole Locations

From the form of (
64
), we see that that the singularities occur at
\tau\sim-1
as
|\xi|\to\infty
, which can be mapped back to coordinates in
(x,t)
. We can obtain a correction term to this expression which permits us to approximate the pole locations more accurately. We will write the corrected pole location as

\tau\sim\tau_{0}+\tau_{1}\xi^{-2}+\tau_{2}\xi^{-4}+\ldots\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty.

(70)

We know that all poles in the solution must be simple, and therefore write

\frac{U(\xi)}{\xi}=\frac{P(\tau)}{\tau-\tau_{0}-\tau_{1}\xi^{-2}-\tau_{2}\xi^{-4}-\ldots},

(71)

where
P(\tau)
is a polynomial expression. Expanding (
71
) as
|\xi|\to\infty
gives

\frac{U(\xi)}{\xi}\sim\frac{P(\tau)}{\tau-\tau_{0}}+\frac{\tau_{1}P(\tau)}{\xi^{2}(\tau-\tau_{0})^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(|\xi|^{-4}).

(72)

Now, we compare (
72
) with (
69
). Dividing (
69
) throughout by
\xi
gives

\frac{U(\xi)}{\xi}\sim\frac{\tau}{1+\tau}+\frac{1}{\xi^{2}(1+\tau)^{2}}\left[\frac{\tau(1-4\mu\mathrm{i})(1+4\mu\mathrm{i}\tau)}{8\mu}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\right]\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty.

(73)

Matching the first term of (
72
) with that of (
73
) near
\tau=\tau_{0}
gives

\tau_{0}=-1,\qquad P(\tau)=\tau.

(74)

We obtain
\tau_{1}
by matching the second term of (
72
) with that of (
73
) in the vicinity of
\tau=\tau_{0}
for large
|\xi|
. Matching (
72
) and (
73
) requires

P(\tau_{0})\tau_{1}=\left[\frac{\tau_{0}(1-4\mu\mathrm{i})(1+4\mu\mathrm{i}\tau_{0})}{8\mu}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\right],

(75)

and gives

\tau_{1}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{8\mu}-2\mu.

(76)

To obtain further corrections to the pole position, we would have to include further corrections to
U(\xi)
in (
63
). For instance, if we include
A_{2}(\tau)
from (
63
), a similar analysis shows that

\tau_{2}=8\mu^{2}-\frac{1}{128\mu^{2}}+\frac{9\mu\mathrm{i}}{2}.

(77)

We now have sufficient information for us to approximate the pole locations explicitly in terms of
\xi
. We need to invert the expression

\tau=\sigma\xi^{-\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^{2}/4\mu}\sim-1+\tau_{1}\xi^{-2}+\ldots\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty,

(78)

where we define
\alpha=1+\mathrm{i}/2\mu
for notational convenience, and
\tau_{1}
is given in (
76
). After taking logarithms of both sides and some further algebraic manipulation, we find

\displaystyle\log(\sigma)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\log(\xi^{2})-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4\mu}+(2M+1)\pi\mathrm{i}\sim\log\left(1-\frac{\tau_{1}}{\xi^{2}}+\ldots\right),

(79)

where
M
is any integer. For large values of
|M|
, the poles will be located at

\xi\sim\pm\sqrt{8\mu\mathrm{i}M\pi}\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad|M|\to\infty.

(80)

Here the positive (negative) sign is associated with positive (negative)
M
, so that the poles lie in the first (second) quadrant. The result is a string of poles along the rays
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)=\pi/4
and
\mathrm{arg}(\xi)=3\pi/4
, corresponding to the anti-Stokes curves labelled in Figure
2
. The expression (
80
) agrees with the leading-order approximation from
[
48
]
, obtained from a transcendental equation derived using properties of the exact solution (
13
).

The derivation of (
80
) only requires
A_{0}(\tau)
, which in turn requires only the explicit calculation of
a_{0}^{(0)}
and
a_{0}^{(1)}
. In order to improve the accuracy of (
80
), we will use the correction term
A_{1}(\tau)
. We set

r_{\mathrm{p}}=\log(\sigma)+(2M+1)\pi\mathrm{i},

(81)

and we will find an asymptotic expression for the pole location in the limit that
|r_{\mathrm{p}}|\to\infty
, which corresponds to
M\to\infty
. Although we are only determining the asymptotic behaviour of the pole locations in this limit, we will compare our results to numerical solutions in Section
7
and find that the approximations are accurate even for poles that are relatively close to the origin.

Note that (
79
) only contains
\xi^{2}
terms, so we can write a series expression for this variable. Due to the logarithmic term in (
79
), we expect that this series also contains logarithmic terms. We set

\xi^{2}\sim c_{0}r_{\mathrm{p}}+c_{1}\log(4\mu r_{\mathrm{p}})+c_{2}+\frac{c_{3}\log(4\mu r_{\mathrm{p}})}{r_{\mathrm{p}}}+\frac{c_{4}}{r_{\mathrm{p}}}+\ldots,

(82)

where the omitted terms are small in the limit that
|r_{\mathrm{p}}|\to\infty
, and the
4\mu
in the logarithmic terms is included for algebraic convenience. By substituting this into (
79
) and matching as
|r_{\mathrm{p}}|\to\infty
, we find

c_{0}=4\mu,\qquad c_{1}=-2\mu\alpha,\qquad c_{2}=0,\qquad c_{3}=\alpha^{2}\mu,\qquad c_{4}=\tau_{1}.

(83)

We combine these terms to obtain

\xi^{2}\sim 4\mu r_{\mathrm{p}}-2\mu\alpha\log(4\mu r_{\mathrm{p}})+\frac{\alpha^{2}\mu\log(4\mu r_{\mathrm{p}})+\tau_{1}}{r_{\mathrm{p}}}+\ldots,

(84)

where
r_{\mathrm{p}}
depends on
M
, which takes integer values that index the poles. The expression (
84
) gives an asymptotic expression for
\xi^{2}
, and hence it is straightforward to compute
\xi
. More terms could be obtained using the expression in (
77
) for
\tau_{2}
, but we do not require any further corrections here. Note that this approximation becomes more accurate as
|M|
grows, because
|r_{\mathrm{p}}|
grows correspondingly.

6
Zero Locations

We apply an essentially identical method to determine the asymptotic location of zeroes of
U(\xi)
. We write the location of the zeroes of
U(\xi)
as
\tau\sim\tau_{0}+\tau_{1}\xi^{-2}+\tau_{2}\xi^{-4}+\ldots
as
|\xi|\to 0
, as before. From (
64
), it is evident that
\tau_{0}=0
yields
A_{0}(\tau_{0})=0
.

Substituting the series expression for
\tau
into (
73
) and setting
U(\xi)=0
gives

\frac{\tau_{1}\xi^{-2}}{1+\tau_{1}\xi^{-2}}+\frac{1}{\xi^{2}(1+\tau_{1}\xi^{-2})^{2}}\left[\frac{\tau_{1}\xi^{-2}(1-4\mu\mathrm{i})(1+4\mu\mathrm{i}\tau_{1}\xi^{-2})}{8\mu}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(|\xi|^{-4})=0,

(85)

as
|\xi|\to\infty
. Matching terms in (
85
) at leading order as
|\xi|\to\infty
gives
\tau_{1}=\mathrm{i}/2
, and hence that the location of the zeroes of
U(\xi)
satisfies
\tau\sim\mathrm{i}/2\xi^{2}
as
|\xi|\to\infty
. We determine the location of zeroes by inverting the expression

\tau=\sigma\xi^{-\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^{2}/4\mu}\sim\tau_{1}\xi^{-2}+\ldots\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty.

(86)

By taking the logarithm of both sides and rearranging, we find that

\displaystyle\log(\sigma)-\frac{\alpha-2}{2}\log(\xi^{2})-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4\mu}+2M\pi\mathrm{i}\sim\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}+\ldots\right),

(87)

where
M
is any integer. This approximation is valid for large
|\xi|
, and hence
M
. For large values of
|M|
, this expression gives the asymptotic relation

\xi\sim\pm\sqrt{8\mu\mathrm{i}M\pi}\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad|M|\to\infty.

(88)

which is identical to the leading behaviour of the pole locations, given in (
80
). The description of the pole and zero locations in (
80
) and (
88
) is consistent with the behaviour seen in
[
48
]
, where it was shown that the poles and zeroes interlace. Consequently the difference between the pole zero locations is small compared to
|\xi|
, and should not appear at leading order as
|M|\to\infty
.

We use identical methods to the previous section to invert (
87
). First, comparing this equation with (
79
) we see that it is natural to define

r_{\mathrm{z}}=\log(2\sigma)+\left(2M-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\pi\mathrm{i},

(89)

and we then invert expression (
79
) to obtain

\xi^{2}\sim 4\mu r_{\mathrm{z}}-2(\alpha-2)\mu\log(4\mu r_{\mathrm{z}})-6\mu\pi\mathrm{i}+4\mu\log 2\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty.

(90)

Note that
\pi\mathrm{i}/2-\log 2=\log(\tau_{1})
. Because
\tau_{1}
is the leading contribution to the position of the zeros, we do not have sufficient accuracy to compute
r_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}
corrections for the variable
\xi
, unlike in the pole calculations. We could obtain more terms of accuracy in this expression using the value of
\tau_{2}
from (
77
) in the inverted expression for
\tau
(
86
), but (
90
) is sufficient to obtain reasonable approximations for the zero locations. Finally, note that there is some numerical imprecision for larger values of
|\xi|
, which is visible in Figure
4
for the case
\mu=0.5
.

8
Conclusions

We have studied solutions to (
2
)–(
3
), which represents the small-time solution to Burgers’ equation (
1
) near singular points of the initial condition (
7
). This work was motivated by VandenHeuvel et al.
[
48
]
, who used special function theory to show that the analytic continued solution (
13
) contains two rays of simple poles and simple zeros which rapidly stream out of the singularities of the initial condition (
7
) for
t\ll 1
.

We have presented a methodology (based on
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
) to approximate locations of poles and zeroes in the solution of (
2
)–(
3
) for
\mu=\mathcal{O}(1)
, which, in principle, allow us to derive asymptotic expansions to arbitrary asymptotic order. This approach does not rely on the exact solution or special function theory in any way, and can be applied to differential equations which do not have such a convenient formulation.

The approach used is as follows:

•

We applied exponential asymptotics to determine the Stokes structure of the solution, and to calculate the form of exponentially small terms (
38
) that appear across Stokes curves.

•

We expressed the solution as a transseries (
47
), which contains all of the subdominant exponential contributions to the solution. This transseries is asymptotically valid in regions where the exponential terms are small.

•

We switched the order of summation in the transseries, and summed the expression over all exponential terms at each algebraic order. This allowed us to extend the range of asymptotic validity so that the new series (
56
) describes the solution in regions where the exponentials are large.

Using this method, we calculated the location of poles and zeroes in the transseries expression, which we then inverted to obtain asymptotic expressions for the pole locations (
84
) and zero locations (
90
) in terms of the original coordinate
\xi
. These could be extended to include further corrections using additional terms in the transseries (eg. those in (
52
)–(
53
), or (
68
)).

Another purpose of this study was to illustrate the relationship between exponential asymptotics and transasymptotic analysis, and how they combine to describe the solution to differential equations. This connection is explicit in that the exponentially small contribution described in (
38
) was used to specify the transseries parameter
\sigma
. This parameter fixed the transseries coefficients
a_{m}^{(n)}
, and hence the boundary conditions used to solve the differential equations for
A_{0}(\tau)
and
A_{1}(\tau)
. Exponential asymptotics describes the small exponential contributions that appear across Stokes curves, and transasymptotic analysis allows us to find an asymptotic expression for the solution when these contributions become large across anti-Stokes cruves. Together, the methods provide a rather complete picture of the solution behaviour.

There are extensions to this analysis which were not considered here. As discussed in
[
48
]
, if the complex singularities in the initial condition for Burgers’ equation (
1
) are not simple poles, we must introduce an extra boundary layer into the small-time analysis. In general, we expect additional complications of this nature to arise when the strength of the singularity in the initial condition does not match the strength of singularities in the solution of the PDE for
t>0
predicted by dominant balance arguments. For Burgers’ equation, this further work will involve the prediction of branch point locations in the solution (see
[
3
]
).

Finally, we note that singularities and zeroes of solutions to ODEs are often arranged in more complicated lattices, rather than rays (as we have been dealing with here for (
2
)). This famously occurs in solutions to the Painlevé equations (eg.
[
23
,
29
]
), which frequently describe the solutions to partial differential equations near catastrophes, as in
[
28
]
. For solutions to ODEs with a lattice of singularities, the transasymptotic analysis for locating singularities and zeros becomes more complicated; we either have to resolve each ray of the lattice individually
[
23
]
, or use more sophisticated methods to determine the full lattice behaviour (see
[
4
]
).
