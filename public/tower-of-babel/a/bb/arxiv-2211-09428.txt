Abstract
. Let 𝒢 \mathcal{G} be a locally compact étale groupoid and ℒ ​ ( L 2 ​ ( 𝒢 ) ) \mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})) be the C ∗ C^{*} -algebra of adjointable operators on the Hilbert C ∗ C^{*} -module L 2 ​ ( 𝒢 ) L^{2}(\mathcal{G}) . In this paper, we discover a notion called quasi-locality for operators in ℒ ​ ( L 2 ​ ( 𝒢 ) ) \mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})) , generalising the metric space case introduced by Roe. Our main result shows that when 𝒢 \mathcal{G} is additionally σ \sigma -compact and amenable, an equivariant operator in ℒ ​ ( L 2 ​ ( 𝒢 ) ) \mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})) belongs to the reduced groupoid C ∗ C^{*} -algebra C r ∗ ​ ( 𝒢 ) C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}) if and only if it is quasi-local. This provides a practical approach to describe elements in C r ∗ ​ ( 𝒢 ) C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}) using coarse geometry. Our main tool is a description for operators in ℒ ​ ( L 2 ​ ( 𝒢 ) ) \mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})) via their slices with the same philosophy to the computer tomography. As applications, we recover a result by Špakula and the second-named author in the metric space case, and deduce new characterisations for reduced crossed products and uniform Roe algebras for groupoids.

1.
introduction

In the last few decades, there has been increased interest in the study of
C^{*}
-algebras associated to groupoids. The story should be traced back to Renault
[
33
]
, who introduced the notion of groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras as well as several counterparts for locally compact topological groupoids. These constructions are extremely powerful weapons to produce
C^{*}
-algebras, and provide a unified approach to a number of classic
C^{*}
-algebras including group
C^{*}
-algebras, crossed products,
C^{*}
-algebras associated to foliations, uniform Roe algebras,
etc
.

The groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra also provides a useful and concrete model for many classes of
C^{*}
-algebras, which is helpful to analyse their
C^{*}
-algebraic structures. Along this way, Renault
[
33
,
34
]
introduced the notion of Cartan subalgebras in
C^{*}
-algebras, motivated by Feldman and Moore’s works
[
15
]
on von Neumann algebras. Since then, the work of Kumjian, Renault, Barlak, Li and others (
e.g.
,
[
6
,
7
,
20
,
26
,
27
,
34
]
) showed that a large class of
C^{*}
-algebras can be realised as twisted groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras, based on the theory of Cartan subalgebras. Consequently, this allows people to use the groupoid models to study their ideal structure, nuclearity, the Universal Coefficient Theorem (UCT),
etc
.

On the other hand, the groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra plays a central role in (higher) index theory as a proxy for the algebra of continuous functions and its
K
-theory is the natural locus for generalised indices (see,
e.g.
,
[
40
]
).
For example, the equivariant index of a lifted elliptic differential operator on the universal cover of a closed manifold
M
belongs to the
K
-theory of the group
C^{*}
-algebra of
\pi_{1}(M)

[
18
,
37
]
; while the index of an elliptic differential operator on an open manifold belongs to the
K
-theory of its Roe algebra
[
35
]
, which is isomorphic to certain crossed product of the groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra for the associated coarse groupoid
[
38
]
.

These indices provide geometric and topological information of the underlying manifold. Hence the computation for the
K
-theory of groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras lies at the heart of (higher) index theory, and the famous Baum-Connes conjecture provides an efficient and practical approach (see
[
42
]
for an excellent introduction, and
[
17
]
for a more recent survey).
A number of excellent works have been made around this conjecture in the last few decades (see a compresensive list of reference in
[
17
]
), while the general case is still widely open. This suggests that studying the structure of groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras will help to understand the indices of various operators, and hence to enhance our understanding of geometric properties of the underlying manifolds.

In spite of the importance of groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras, it is usually hard to determine whether a given element belongs to this algebra. According to the definition (see Section
2.3
), we have to construct a sequence of compactly supported functions on the groupoid to approximate the given element in operator norm, while unfortunately there is no routine procedure for the construction.

The aim of this paper is to provide a practical approach to characterise elements in groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras using coarse geometry. For simplicity, we will only focus on locally compact étale groupoids (see Section
2.2
for a precise definition).

1.1.
A motivating example: the coarse groupoid

We start with an illuminating example which is the motivation of this work. Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space with bounded geometry. Regarding operators on
\ell^{2}(X)
as
X
-by-
X
matrices, we say that such an operator has
finite propagation
if the non-zero entries appear only in an
entourage
,
i.e.
, a band of finite width (measured by the metric
d
on
X
) around the main diagonal (see Section
2.5
for full details). The finite propagation operators form a
\ast
-subalgebra
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
of
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, and its closure
C^{*}_{u}(X)
is called the
uniform Roe algebra of
X
, introduced by Roe in
[
35
]
.

To characterise operators in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
, Roe
[
35
]
also introduced a notion of quasi-locality for operators in
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
. Roughly speaking, an operator is called
quasi-local
if for any
\varepsilon>0
we can find an entourage such that for any block sitting outside this entourage, its restriction on the block has norm less than
\varepsilon
(see Section
2.5
). The collection of all quasi-local operators on
\ell^{2}(X)
forms a
C^{*}
-algebra, called the
uniform quasi-local algebra of
X
and denoted by
C^{*}_{uq}(X)
.

Clearly
C^{*}_{u}(X)
is a subalgebra of
C^{*}_{uq}(X)
, and it is proved by Špakula and the second-named author
[
44
]
that they coincide when
X
has Property A. Quasi-locality is a coarse geometric property in the sense that the algebra
C^{*}_{uq}(X)
is preserved under coarse equivalence and moreover, this property is easier to verify than the case of the uniform Roe algebra. Therefore, the quasi-local characterisation is crucial in the work of Engel
[
12
,
13
]
on the index theory of pseudo-differential operators, and also in the work of White and Willett
[
45
]
on classifying Cartan subalgebras of Roe algebras, with some applications on the associated rigidity problem as well.

To build a bridge to the groupoid theory, Skandalis, Tu and Yu
[
38
]
introduced a locally compact étale groupoid for
(X,d)
, called the
coarse groupoid

G(X)
(see Example
2.6.3
). Note that any compact subset in
G(X)
is contained in the closure of some entourage, and hence it is clear that
C_{c}(G(X))
is
\ast
-isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
. Moreover taking closures, the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(G(X))
is
C^{*}
-isomorphic to the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. This suggests us to consider compactly supported operators in the place of finite propagation operators for general groupoids, and hence the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra should correspond to certain version of the uniform Roe algebra. Also recall from
[
38
]
that
X
has Yu’s Property A if and only if
G(X)
is amenable.

Now a natural question is to ask how to recover the notion of quasi-locality for operators in
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
using the language of coarse groupoids. This will also be our starting point to introduce quasi-locality for general locally compact étale groupoids. Parallel to the situation of uniform Roe algebras, the practical approach we are looking for to describe elements in groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras will be achieved by the coarse geometric property of quasi-locality.

1.2.
Our strategy for quasi-locality

To introduce the notion of quasi-locality for general groupoids, we would like to mimic the approach for metric spaces. Recall from Section
1.1
that for a discrete metric space
(X,d)
with bounded geometry, we have a natural ambient
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
. For operators in
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, we have the notion of finite propagation and quasi-locality to form the associated
C^{*}
-algebras. The main difficulty to generalise the notion of quasi-locality to general groupoids is that firstly we have to draw the border of a proper class of operators so that the notion of quasi-locality thereon has the chance to recover the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra.

Let us explain our strategy in details. For a locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, we follow the steps below:

Step I.
We need to choose a suitable candidate for the ambient
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
. The criterion is that
\mathcal{A}
should be large enough to include the
\ast
-algebra
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, while not too large so that a well-defined notion of quasi-locality has the chance to characterise the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
when
\mathcal{G}
is “well-behaved”. Moreover in the case of coarse groupoid, the ambient
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
should be (
almost

3.25
,

\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))

) the same as
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
.

Step II.
We would like to define the notion of compact support and quasi-locality for operators in the ambient

\mathcal{A}
chosen in
Step I
so that compactly supported operators are nothing but those from
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
. Moreover in the case of the coarse groupoid, the notion of quasi-locality should recover the uniform quasi-local algebra
C^{*}_{uq}(X)
introduced by Roe (see also
[
24
]
).

Step III.
Finally we aim to show that when
\mathcal{G}
is amenable (which is equivalent to Property A in the case of the coarse groupoid), elements in the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
can be characterised by the property of quasi-locality, which fulfils our task.

1.3.
Our main contributions

Following the strategy from Section
1.2
, now we introduce our main contributions to achieve each step above. Assume that
\mathcal{G}
is a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. For each
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, denote
\mathcal{G}_{x}
the set consisting of elements in
\mathcal{G}
with source
x
.

For
Step I
, there are two natural candidates for the ambient
C^{*}
-algebras coming from the definition of the reduce groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra. Recall that the
\ast
-algebra
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
can be faithfully represented as adjointable operators on the Hilbert
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, and the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
is the closure of
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with respect to the operator norm in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. Considering
\mathcal{G}
as a disjoint union of its source fibres
\{\mathcal{G}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
, this norm can also be recovered by representing
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
in
\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
(see Section
2.3
for full details). This suggests that either
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
or
\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
might be a suitable candidate for the ambient
C^{*}
-algebra.

By checking the case of the coarse groupoid, we realise that
\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
is too large due to the lack of control on different coordinates, and
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
seems more suitable because it contains certain topological information of
\mathcal{G}
. However, we are in a dilemma since elements in
\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
are much easier to describe than those in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
.

To overcome the issue, note that there is a natural embedding
\Phi:\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\to\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
, called the
slicing map
(see (
3.1
)). We realise that operators in the image of
\Phi
satisfy a condition which can be regarded as a vector-wise version of certain quasi-locality (Definition
3.12
). Hence consulting the notion of operator fibre space introduced by Austin and the second-named author, we achieve the following (see Section
3.2
for precise definitions):

Theorem A

(Theorem
3.13
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. For
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
belongs to
\Phi\big(\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\big)
;

(2)

the map
x\mapsto T_{x}
is a continuous section of the associated operator fibre space, and
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}},(T^{*}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
are vector-wise uniformly quasi-local.

Theorem
A
provides a characterisation for operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
via their slices with the same philosophy to the computer tomography, which describes an object using a family of its slices. This will be crucial in the proof of Theorem
E
below as well as in the computation of
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
for concrete examples. We also obtain a simplified version of Theorem
A
when the unit space contains a dense subset (see Proposition
3.18
and Corollary
3.19
), which happens in a number of cases including coarse groupoids.

By checking coarse groupoids again, we realise that the algebra
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
might still be too large. Note that elements in
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
admit an extra equivariant property: an operator in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
is called
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant
if it commutes with elements in the image of the right regular representation (see Definition
3.21
). Denote the subalgebra of
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
by
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))^{\mathcal{G}}
. Thanks to Theorem
A
, we manage to compute
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))^{\mathcal{G}}
for the coarse groupoid (see Example
3.25
). Moreover, we obtain the following realisation for equivariant operators:

Proposition B

(Proposition
3.31
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. For any
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, there exists a unique function
f_{T}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
such that
T
is the convolution operator by
f_{T}
.

The clues above suggest that
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))^{\mathcal{G}}
should be a suitable candidate for the ambient
C^{*}
-algebra. We remark that Proposition
B
will also be crucial to prove Theorem
E
below since it transfers operators to functions.

Next, we move to
Step II
. Recall that we need to define the notion of compact support and quasi-locality for operators in the ambient
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))^{\mathcal{G}}
so that those with compact support recover the algebra
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
. Indeed, the definition we discover can be naturally defined on the larger algebra
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. Inspired by the case of coarse groupoids, we introduce the following key notion of this paper:

Definition C

(Definition
4.1
and Definition
4.2
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid and
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. We define the following:

(1)

For a subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, functions
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
are called
K
-separated
if we have
\big(K\cdot\mathrm{supp}(f)\big)\cap\mathrm{supp}(g)=\emptyset
and
\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap\big(K\cdot\mathrm{supp}(g)\big)=\emptyset
.

(2)

T
is called
compactly supported
if there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that
gTf=0
for any
K
-separated functions
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
.

(3)

T
is called
quasi-local
if for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact subset
K\subset\mathcal{G}
such that for any
K
-separated functions
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
we have
\|gTf\|<\varepsilon\|g\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}
.

Thanks to Theorem
A
, we obtain a characterisation for compactly supported and quasi-local operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
using their slices (see Proposition
4.4
and Corollary
4.6
). In particular for a quasi-local operator, each of its slice is an ordinary quasi-local operator on a discrete metric space. Hence our notion of quasi-locality reflects the coarse geometry of the underlying metric family.
Applying this characterisation to the case of the coarse groupoid
G(X)
for some metric space
X
, we verify that Definition
C
is compatible with the notion of finite propagation and quasi-locality for operators in
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
as desired (see Example
4.9
).

Moreover under the assumption of equivariance, we reach the following as a consequence of Proposition
B
:

Proposition D

(Proposition
4.12
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. A
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant operator
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
is compactly supported
if and only if

T
belongs to the image of
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
under the left regular representation. Hence the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
can be recovered as the norm closure of all
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant compactly supported operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
.

Hence we fulfil the task of
Step II
.
For later use, we denote by
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]
the
\ast
-subalgebra in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
consisting of all compactly supported operators and define the
uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{G}
to be its norm closure in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. Also denote by
C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})
the set of all quasi-local operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, which forms a
C^{*}
-algebra and is called the
uniform quasi-local algebra of
\mathcal{G}
. We also consider their equivariant counterparts and denote by
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
,
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})^{\mathcal{G}}
and
C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})^{\mathcal{G}}
the subalgebras consisting of equivariant operators in
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]
,
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})
and
C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})
, respectively.

Having established all necessary ingredients, finally we reach
Step III
and manage to prove the following main result of this paper:

Theorem E

(Theorem
5.1
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact,
\sigma
-compact and étale groupoid.
If
\mathcal{G}
is amenable, then we have
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})=C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})^{\mathcal{G}}=C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})^{\mathcal{G}}
.

Theorem
E
shows that for a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant operator in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, it is quasi-local if and only if it belongs to the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
. Note that quasi-locality reflects the coarse geometry of the underlying object and is easier to verify than the situation of
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
, hence Theorem
E
provides a practical and coarse geometric approach to characterise elements in the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra for amenable groupoids as desired.

As an example when
\mathcal{G}
is the coarse groupoid associated to a discrete metric space with bounded geometry, Theorem
5.1
recovers the main result of
[
44
]
in the Hilbert space case (see Section
6.3
). Moreover when
\mathcal{G}
is the transformation groupoid associated to a discrete group acting on a compact Hausdorff space, we obtain a new characterisation for elements in the reduced crossed product (see Section
6.4
).

The proof of Theorem
E
relies heavily on the coarse geometry of groupoids (see
[
28
]
and
[
30
]
). Roughly speaking, we assign a length function on the groupoid thanks to the
\sigma
-compactness, which induces a metric on each source fibre. Hence we can appeal to the weapon of coarse geometry for a family of metric spaces, and consult the idea of
[
44
, Theorem 3.3]
. Here we would like to clarify that the proof for Theorem
E
is
not
merely a family version of that for
[
44
, Theorem 3.3]
since we have certain continuity restriction from the topology of the groupoid, and technical analysis is also required especially when the unit space is no longer compact.

1.4.
Beyond equivariance: uniform Roe algebras

Note that Theorem
E
only discusses the situation of equivariant operators, while our notion of quasi-locality (Definition
C

\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))

As revealed by the work of Anantharaman-Delaroche

-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})
is
C^{*}
-isomorphic

Theorem F

Let

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Standard notation

Here we collect the notation used throughout the paper.

To simplify the terminology, we always assume that locally compact spaces are Hausdorff. Given a locally compact space
X
, we denote by
C(X)
the set of complex-valued continuous functions on
X
, and by
C_{b}(X)
the subset of bounded continuous functions on
X
. Recall that the
support
of a function
f\in C(X)
is the closure of
\{x\in X:f(x)\neq 0\}
, written as
\mathrm{supp}(f)
, and denote by
C_{c}(X)
the set of continuous functions with compact support. We also denote by
C_{0}(X)
the set of continuous functions vanishing at infinity, which is the closure of
C_{c}(X)
with respect to the supremum norm
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup\{|f(x)|:x\in X\}
.

When
X
is discrete, denote
\ell^{\infty}(X):=C_{b}(X)
and
\ell^{2}(X)
the Hilbert space of complex-valued square-summable functions on
X
. To be compatible with the setting of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, we always assume that the Hilbert space inner product
\langle\eta,\xi\rangle
will be taken to be linear in the variable
\xi
and conjugate-linear in
\eta
. Denote by
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
the
C^{*}
-algebra of all bounded linear operators on
\ell^{2}(X)
, and
\mathscr{K}(\ell^{2}(X))
the
C^{*}
-subalgebra of all compact operators.

2.2.
Basic notions for Groupoids

Let us start with some basic notions and terminology on groupoids. For details we refer to
[
33
]
.

Recall that a
groupoid
is a small category, in which every morphism is invertible. Roughly speaking, a groupoid consists of a set
\mathcal{G}
, a subset
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
called the
unit space
, two maps
\mathrm{s},\mathrm{r}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
called the
source
and
range
maps respectively, a
composition law
:

\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\coloneqq\{(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}:\mathrm{s}(\gamma_{1})=\mathrm{r}(\gamma_{2})\}\ni(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\mapsto\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{G},

and an
inverse
map
\gamma\mapsto\gamma^{-1}
. These operations satisfy a couple of axioms, including the associativity law and the fact that elements in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
act as units. For
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, denote
\mathcal{G}^{x}:=\mathrm{r}^{-1}(x)
and
\mathcal{G}_{x}:=\mathrm{s}^{-1}(x)
.
A subset
Y\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is called
invariant
if
\mathrm{r}^{-1}(Y)=\mathrm{s}^{-1}(Y)
. For
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, we denote

\displaystyle A^{-1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}:\gamma^{-1}\in A\},

\displaystyle AB

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}:\gamma=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\text{ where }\gamma_{1}\in A,\gamma_{2}\in B\text{ and }\mathrm{s}(\gamma_{1})=\mathrm{r}(\gamma_{2})\}.

We say that
A\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is
symmetric
if
A=A^{-1}
.

A groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is
principal
if the map
(\mathrm{s},\mathrm{r})
from
\mathcal{G}
into
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\times\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is one-to-one, and
\mathcal{G}
is
transitive
if the map
(\mathrm{s},\mathrm{r})
is onto.

A
locally compact groupoid
is a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
endowed with a locally compact topology for which composition, inversion, source and range maps are continuous with respect to the induced topologies.

We say that a locally compact groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is
étale
if the range (and hence the source) map is a local homeomorphism,
i.e.
, for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
\gamma
such that
\mathrm{r}(U)
is open and
\mathrm{r}|_{U}
is a homeomorphism. In this case, fibers
\mathcal{G}_{x}
and
\mathcal{G}^{x}
with the induced topologies are discrete and
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is open in
\mathcal{G}
. Throughout the paper, we shall limit ourselves to the étale case.

For a locally compact groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is called a
bisection
if the restrictions of
\mathrm{s},\mathrm{r}
to
A
are injective.
It follows from definition that for a locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, all open bisections form a basis for the topology of
\mathcal{G}
. As a direct consequence, we obtain that for such a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, any function
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
can be written as a linear combination of continuous functions whose supports are contained in open precompact bisections.

2.3.
Groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras and Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules

Here we recall the notion of reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras in the étale case. Given a locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, note that the space
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
can be formed into a
*
-algebra with the following operations: for
f,g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle(f*g)(\gamma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{s(\gamma)}}f(\gamma\alpha^{-1})g(\alpha),

(2.2)

\displaystyle f^{*}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\overline{f(\gamma^{-1})}.

The function
f\ast g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
is called the
convolution
of
f
and
g
.

Recall that for each
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
the
left regular representation at
x
, denoted by
\lambda_{x}:C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
, is defined as follows:

(2.3)

\lambda_{x}(f)(\xi)(\gamma)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}f(\gamma\alpha^{-1})\xi(\alpha),\quad\text{where }f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\text{ and }\xi\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}).

It is routine to check that
\lambda_{x}
is a well-defined
\ast
-homomorphism.
The
reduced norm
on
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
is defined by

\|f\|_{r}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|\lambda_{x}(f)\|,

and the
reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra

C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
is defined to be the completion of the
*
-algebra
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with respect to the reduced norm
\|\cdot\|_{r}
. It is clear that each left regular representation
\lambda_{x}
can be extended automatically to a
C^{*}
-homomorphism
\lambda_{x}:C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
.

In the following, we would like to provide an alternative approach to define the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras using the language of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, which motivates our definition of quasi-locality for groupoids. Here we recall the necessary notions on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, and guide readers to
[
21
]
for details.

Definition 2.1

(
[
21
]
)
.

Let
A
be a
C^{*}
-algebra.
An
inner-product
A
-module
is a linear space
E
which is a right
A
-module (with compatible scalar multiplication:
\lambda(\xi a)=(\lambda\xi)a=\xi(\lambda a)
for
\xi\in E
,
a\in A
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
), together with a map
(\xi,\eta)\mapsto\langle\xi,\eta\rangle
from
E\times E
to
A
such that

(1)

\langle\xi,\alpha\eta+\beta\zeta\rangle=\alpha\langle\xi,\eta\rangle+\beta\langle\xi,\zeta\rangle
for
\xi,\eta,\zeta\in E
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
;

(2)

\langle\xi,\eta a\rangle=\langle\xi,\eta\rangle a
for
\xi,\eta\in E
and
a\in A
;

(3)

\langle\xi,\eta\rangle=\langle\eta,\xi\rangle^{*}
for
\xi,\eta\in E
;

(4)

\langle\xi,\xi\rangle\geq 0
for
\xi\in E
, and
\langle\xi,\xi\rangle=0
if and only if
\xi=0
.

For
\xi\in E
, we define its norm
\|\xi\|_{E}:=\|\langle\xi,\xi\rangle\|^{1/2}
, also denoted by
\|\xi\|
when there is no ambiguity.
An inner-product
A
-module which is complete with respect to this norm is called a
Hilbert
A
-module
.

Recall that an inner-product
A
-module
E
admits an
A
-valued norm
|\cdot|
given by
|\xi|=\langle\xi,\xi\rangle^{1/2}
. Note that
|\langle\xi,\eta\rangle|\leq\|\xi\|\cdot|\eta|
for
\xi,\eta\in E
(see
[
21
, Proposition 1.1]
), and hence
|\xi a|\leq\|\xi\|\cdot|a|
for
\xi\in E
and
a\in A
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
A
be a
C^{*}
-algebra and
E
be a Hilbert
A
-module. A map
T:E\rightarrow E
is called
adjointable
if there exists a map
T^{*}\colon E\rightarrow E
such that

\langle\eta,T(\xi)\rangle=\langle T^{*}(\eta),\xi\rangle\text{ for all }\xi,\eta\in E.

It is easy to see that adjointable operators are
A
-linear and bounded with respect to the norm on
E
. Denote by
\mathscr{L}(E)
the set of all adjointable operators on
E
, which forms a
C^{*}
-algebra.

The following standard result will be used in the sequel:

Proposition 2.3

(
[
21
, Proposition 1.2]
)
.

Let
E
be a Hilbert
A
-module.
For
T\in\mathscr{L}(E)
and
\xi\in E
, we have
|T(\xi)|^{2}\leq\|T\|^{2}|\xi|^{2}
and
|T(\xi)|\leq\|T\|\cdot|\xi|
.

Now we consider the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module associated to a locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
.
Let
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
be the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module over
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
obtained by taking completion of
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with respect to the
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-valued inner product:

\langle\eta,\xi\rangle(x)\coloneqq\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}\overline{\eta(\gamma)}\xi(\gamma),

and the right
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module structure is given by

(\xi f)(\gamma)\coloneqq\xi(\gamma)f(\mathrm{s}(\gamma)).

Due to étaleness, for any
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
we have

\|\xi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|\xi(\gamma)|^{2}\geq\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}|\xi(\gamma)|^{2}=\|\xi\|^{2}_{\infty}.

Hence we can regard
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
as a subspace in
C_{0}(\mathcal{G})
.

Note that all the regular representations
\lambda_{x}
defined in (
2.3
) can be put together to form a single representation
\Lambda:C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
by the formula:

(2.4)

\big(\Lambda(f)\xi\big)(\gamma)\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}}f(\gamma\alpha^{-1})\xi(\alpha)=((\lambda_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}f)\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}})(\gamma).

And it is easy to check that for
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, we have
\|f\|_{r}=\|\Lambda(f)\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))}
. Therefore,
\Lambda
can be extended to a faithful representation
\Lambda:C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, which is called the
left regular representation
.

It follows that the convolution operator
\ast:C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\times C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\to C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
can be extended to an operator (using the same notation)
\ast:C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
by (
2.1
), and by definition
\Lambda(f)\xi=f\ast\xi
for
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
and
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
.

There is a special class of operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, called multiplication operators. For
g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
, the
multiplication operator

M(g):L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
is defined by
(M(g)\xi)(\gamma)=g(\gamma)\xi(\gamma)
for
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
. It is clear that
M(g)\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, and we abbreviate
M(g)\xi
by
g\xi
.

For later use, here we briefly recall the notion of interior tensor products for Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules. Readers might go to
[
21
, Chapter 4]
for more details.

Let
A,B
be
C^{*}
-algebras,
E
be a Hilbert
A
-module and
F
be a Hilbert
B
-module. Let
\rho:A\rightarrow\mathscr{L}(F)
be a
*
-homomorphism, which turns
F
into a left
A
-module. Denote the algebraic tensor product of
E
and
F
over
A
by
E\otimes_{A}F
, which is a right
B
-module. According to
[
21
, Proposition 4.5]
,
E\otimes_{A}F
is an inner-product
B
-module under the inner product given on simple tensors by

\langle x_{1}\otimes y_{1},x_{2}\otimes y_{2}\rangle=\langle y_{1},\rho(\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle)(y_{2})\rangle\quad\mbox{where}\quad x_{1},x_{2}\in E\mbox{~and~}y_{1},y_{2}\in F.

The completion of the inner-product
B
-module
E\otimes_{A}F
is a Hilbert
B
-module, denoted by
E\otimes_{\rho}F
, which is called the
interior (
or
internal) tensor product
of
E
and
F
with respect to
\rho
.

Furthermore, given
T\in\mathscr{L}(E)
, we define the tensor product
T\otimes 1
on the algebraic tensor product
E\otimes_{A}F
by setting
(T\otimes 1)(x\otimes y):=T(x)\otimes y
for
x\in E
and
y\in F
. One can easily check that this is well-defined and can be extended to an operator in
\mathscr{L}(E\otimes_{\rho}F)
, denoted by
T\otimes_{\rho}1
.

2.4.
Amenable groupoids

Amenable groupoids comprise a large class of groupoids with relatively nice properties. Here we only focus on the case of étaleness, in which the notion of amenability behave quite well. A standard reference is
[
3
]
and another reference for the étale case is
[
9
, Chapter 5.6]
.

Definition 2.4

(
[
3
, Chapter 2.2]
)
.

A locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
amenable
if for any
\varepsilon>0
and compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, there exists a non-negative function
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
such that for any
\gamma\in K
we have:

•

\left|1-\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}f(\beta)\right|<\varepsilon
;

•

\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}|f(\beta)-f(\beta\gamma)|<\varepsilon
.

Using a standard normalisation argument, we have the following:

Lemma 2.5
.

A locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is amenable
if and only if
for any
\varepsilon>0
and compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, there exists a non-negative function
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
such that
\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}f(\beta)\leq 1
for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and for any
\gamma\in K
we have:

•

\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}f(\beta)=1
;

•

\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}|f(\beta)-f(\beta\gamma)|<\varepsilon
.

We also need another description for amenability in terms of positive type functions. Recall that for a locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, a function
h
on
\mathcal{G}
is of
positive type
if for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{x}
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, we have the following:

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\overline{\lambda_{i}}\lambda_{j}h(\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}^{-1})\geq 0.

We remark that if
h
is of positive type, then
h(\gamma^{-1})=\overline{h(\gamma)}
for
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
.

Combining Lemma
2.5
with
[
3
, Proposition 2.2.13]
, we obtain the following:

Lemma 2.6
.

A locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is amenable
if and only if
for any
\varepsilon>0
and compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, there exists a non-negative function
h\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
of positive type such that

•

h(x)\leq 1
for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and
h(x)=1
for any
x\in K\cap\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
;

•

|1-h(\gamma)|<\varepsilon
for any
\gamma\in K
.

2.5.
Notions from coarse geometry

Now we collect necessary notions from coarse geometry. For a discrete metric space
(X,d)
, denote the closed ball by
B_{X}(x,r):=\{y\in X:d(x,y)\leq r\}
for
x\in X
and
r\geq 0
. We say that
X
has
bounded geometry
if for every
r>0
, the number
\sup_{x\in X}\sharp B_{X}(x,r)
is finite. Here we use the notation
\sharp S
to denote the cardinality of a set
S
. For a subset
A\subseteq X
, we denote
\chi_{A}
the characteristic function of
A
. For a point
x\in X
, also denote
\delta_{x}:=\chi_{\{x\}}
.

Let us start with the notion of Property A, which was introduced by Yu in
[
47
]
. Property A plays a key role in Yu’s study on the coarse Baum-Connes conjecture, and it admits a number of equivalent definitions. Here we focus on the one in terms of kernels for later use.

For a set
X
, a
kernel
on
X
is a function
k\colon X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We say that a kernel
k
on
X
is of
positive type
if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
and any
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the following:

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 0.

We say that a kernel
k
has
finite propagation
if there is
S>0
such that
k(x,y)=0
whenever
d(x,y)>S
,
k
is
normalised
if
k(x,x)=1
for every
x\in X
, and
k
is
symmetric
if
k(x,y)=k(y,x)
for any
x,y\in X
. For
R>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, we say that a kernel
k
has
(R,\varepsilon)
-variation
if
|1-k(x,y)|<\varepsilon
whenever
d(x,y)<R
.

Associated to a normalised, symmetric and positive type kernel
k
on
X
, we have the so-called
Schur multiplier

m_{k}:\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))\rightarrow\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
. First note that every bounded linear operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
can be written as an
X
-by-
X
matrix
T=(T_{x,y})_{x,y\in X}
with coefficient
T_{x,y}:=\langle\delta_{x},T(\delta_{y})\rangle\in\mathbb{C}
. The Schur multiplier
m_{k}
is defined by

m_{k}(T)\coloneqq(k(x,y)T_{x,y})_{x,y\in X}

in the matrix form. It is a standard result that
m_{k}
is well-defined linear bounded operator with norm
1
(see,
e.g.
,
[
8
, Theorem C.1.4]
or
[
32
, Theorem 5.1]
),
i.e.
,
\|m_{k}(T)\|\leq\|T\|
for
T\in\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
.

Now we recall the definition of Property A (see,
e.g.
,
[
41
, Proposition 3.2(v)]
or
[
46
, Proposition 1.2.4]
for the equivalence to Yu’s original definition):

Definition 2.7

(
[
47
]
)
.

Let
X
be a discrete metric space with bounded geometry. We say that
X
has
Property A
if for any
R>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a normalised, finite propagation, symmetric kernel
k
on
X
of positive type and
(R,\varepsilon)
-variation.

In the following, we would like to recall
C^{*}
-algebras associated to a metric space. Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space. Recall that there is a
\ast
-representation
M:\ell^{\infty}(X)\to\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
defined by point-wise multiplication,
i.e.
,
(M(f)\xi)(x):=f(x)\xi(x)
for
f\in\ell^{\infty}(X),\xi\in\ell^{2}(X)
and
x\in X
. To simplify the notation, we will write
f\xi
instead of
M(f)\xi
in the sequel.

Definition 2.8
.

For a bounded linear operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, we say that
T
has
finite propagation
if there exists
R>0
such that for any
f_{1},f_{2}\in\ell^{\infty}(X)
with
d(\mathrm{supp}(f_{1}),\mathrm{supp}(f_{2}))>R
, then
f_{1}Tf_{2}=0
. Equivalently, there exists
R>0
such that
T_{x,y}=0
for
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)>R
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space with bounded geometry. The set of all finite propagation operators in
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
forms a
\ast
-algebra, called the
algebraic uniform Roe algebra
of
X
and denoted by
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
. The
uniform Roe algebra
of
X
is defined to be the operator norm closure of
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
in
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, which is a
C^{*}
-algebra and denoted by
C^{*}_{u}(X)
.

Now we move to the case of quasi-locality, which was introduced by Roe in
[
35
]
.

Definition 2.10
.

For a bounded linear operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
and
\varepsilon>0
, we say that
T
has
finite
\varepsilon
-propagation
if there exists
R>0
such that for any
f_{1},f_{2}\in\ell^{\infty}(X)
with
d(\mathrm{supp}(f_{1}),\mathrm{supp}(f_{2}))>R
, then
\|f_{1}Tf_{2}\|<\varepsilon\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}
.
We say that
T
is
quasi-local
if
T
has finite
\varepsilon
-propagation for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Similar to the case of Roe algebras, we form the following:

Definition 2.11

(
[
24
]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space with bounded geometry. The set of all quasi-local operators in
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
forms a
C^{*}
-algebra, called the
uniform quasi-local algebra
of
X
and denoted by
C^{*}_{uq}(X)
.

It is clear that finite propagation operators are quasi-local, hence taking closures, we obtain that
C^{*}_{u}(X)
is a
C^{*}
-subalgebra in
C^{*}_{uq}(X)
. For the converse, there have been a number of progresses mostly in the recent years
[
13
,
22
,
25
,
43
,
44
]
. Currently the most general answer is due to Špakula and the second-named author
[
44
]
as follows, while the general case is still open.

Proposition 2.12

(
[
44
, Theorem 3.3]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space with bounded geometry and Property A, then the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
coincides with the uniform quasi-local algebra
C^{*}_{uq}(X)
.

2.6.
Examples

We collect several classic examples of groupoids.

2.6.1.
Discrete groups

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group. Then
\Gamma
is a locally compact étale groupoid whose unit space consists of a single point
1_{\Gamma}
,
i.e.
, the unit of
\Gamma
. Obviously,
\Gamma
is amenable as a groupoid if and only if
\Gamma
is amenable as a group.

In this case, the
\ast
-algebra
C_{c}(\Gamma)
is just the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
and the left regular representation at
1_{\Gamma}
defined in (
2.3
) coincides with the left regular representation of the group
\Gamma
. Taking completion, the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra of
\Gamma
is the same as the reduced group
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\Gamma)
. We also record that the Hilbert
\mathbb{C}
-module
L^{2}(\Gamma)
is nothing but the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
, and hence
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\Gamma))=\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma))
.

2.6.2.
Pair groupoids

Let
X
be a set. The
pair groupoid
of
X
is
X\times X
as a set, whose unit space is
\{(x,x)\in X\times X:x\in X\}
and identified with
X
for simplicity. The source map is the projection onto the second coordinate and the range map is the projection onto the first coordinate. The composition is given by
(x,y)\cdot(y,z)=(x,z)
for
x,y,z\in X
. When
X
is a discrete Hausdorff space, then
X\times X
is a locally compact étale groupoid. It can be checked easily that the pair groupoid
X\times X
is always amenable.

Each
f\in C_{c}(X\times X)
can be regarded as an
X
-by-
X
matrix with finitely many non-zero matrix entries, which induces a map

(2.5)

\theta:C_{c}(X\times X)\longrightarrow\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))

by setting the matrix coefficients
\theta(f)_{x,y}:=f(x,y)
for
x,y\in X
. It is obvious that
\theta
is a
\ast
-monomorphism, and induces a
C^{*}
-isomorphism

(2.6)

\Theta:C^{*}_{r}(X\times X)\cong\mathscr{K}(\ell^{2}(X)).

2.6.3.
Discrete metric spaces

This example is the motivation for the entire work.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space with bounded geometry. The coarse groupoid
G(X)
on
X
was introduced by Skandalis, Tu, and Yu in
[
38
]
(see also
[
36
, Chapter 10]
) to relate coarse geometry to the theory of
groupoids. As a topological space,

G(X):=\bigcup_{r>0}{\overline{E_{r}}}^{\beta(X\times X)}\subseteq\beta(X\times X),

where
E_{r}:=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:d(x,y)\leq r\}
and
\beta(X\times X)
is the Stone-Čech compactification of
X\times X
. Note that
X\times X
is the pair groupoid with source and range maps
\mathrm{s}(x,y)=y
and
\mathrm{r}(x,y)=x
(see Section
2.6.2
). These maps extend to maps
\beta(X\times X)\to\beta X
, and hence to maps
G(X)\to\beta X
, still denoted by
\mathrm{r}
and
\mathrm{s}
.

Consider the map
(\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}):G(X)\to\beta X\times\beta X
. It was shown in
[
38
, Lemma 2.7]
that
(\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s})
is injective, and hence
G(X)
can be endowed with a groupoid structure induced by the pair groupoid
\beta X\times\beta X
, called the
coarse groupoid
of
X
. It was also shown in
[
38
, Proposition 3.2]
that the coarse groupoid
G(X)
is étale, locally compact and principal. Clearly, the unit space of
G(X)
is
\beta X
. We denote by
\partial_{\beta}X:=\beta X\setminus X
the Stone-Čech boundary of
X
. It was shown in
[
38
, Theorem 5.3]
that the coarse groupoid
G(X)
is amenable if and only if
X
has Property A.

Each
f\in C_{c}(G(X))
is a continuous function supported in
\overline{E_{r}}
for some
r>0
; equivalently, we can interpret
f
as a bounded continuous function on
E_{r}
. Define

\theta:C_{c}(G(X))\longrightarrow\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))

by setting the matrix coefficients
\theta(f)_{x,y}:=f(x,y)
for
x,y\in X
, which extends the map defined in (
2.5
). Hence we take the liberty of using the same notation. It is clear that
\theta
provides a
\ast
-isomorphism from
C_{c}(G(X))
to
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
. Moreover, it was shown in
[
36
, Proposition 10.29]
that
\theta
extends to a
C^{*}
-isomorphism

(2.7)

\Theta:C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\longrightarrow C^{*}_{u}(X).

This map extends the isomorphism in (
2.6
), and hence again we abuse the notation.

2.6.4.
Group actions

Let
X
be a locally compact space and
\Gamma
be a countable discrete group with an action
\Gamma\curvearrowright X
by homeomorphisms. The
transformation groupoid

X\rtimes\Gamma
is
X\times\Gamma
as a topological space, whose unit space is
X\times\{1_{\Gamma}\}
and identified with
X
for simplicity. The groupoid structure is given by
\mathrm{s}((x,\gamma))=\gamma^{-1}x
,
\mathrm{r}((x,\gamma))=x
and
(x,\gamma)(\gamma^{-1}x,\gamma^{\prime})=(x,\gamma\gamma^{\prime})
. Then
X\rtimes\Gamma
is a locally compact étale groupoid. It is not hard to check that
X\rtimes\Gamma
is amenable if and only if the action is amenable (see,
e.g.
,
[
9
, Chapter 4]
).

Denote by
C_{c}(\Gamma,C_{0}(X))
the
\ast
-algebra consisting of all finitely supported functions on
\Gamma
with values in
C_{0}(X)
(see
[
9
, Section 4.1]
for details). The
reduced crossed product

C_{0}(X)\rtimes_{r}\Gamma
is its norm closure with respect to a regular representation of
C_{c}(\Gamma,C_{0}(X))
(see
[
9
, Definition 4.1.4]
). We have the following
\ast
-isomorphism:

\theta:C_{c}(X\rtimes_{r}\Gamma)\longrightarrow C_{c}(\Gamma,C_{c}(X))

given by

F\mapsto\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}f_{\gamma}\gamma,\quad\mbox{where~}f_{\gamma}\in C_{c}(X)\mbox{~is~defined~by~}f_{\gamma}(x)=F(x,\gamma).

It is known that
\theta
can be extended to a
C^{*}
-isomorphism:

(2.8)

\Theta:C^{*}_{r}(X\rtimes\Gamma)\longrightarrow C_{0}(X)\rtimes_{r}\Gamma.

Finally, we remark that
L^{2}(X\rtimes\Gamma)\cong\ell^{2}(\Gamma)\otimes C_{0}(X)
.

3.
Characterisations for operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
and equivariance

In this section, we aim to provide a detailed and practical approach to describe operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
and discuss the notion of equivariance, which will play an important role in the sequel.
We will start with characterisations for vectors in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, and then move to operators.

Throughout this section, we assume that
\mathcal{G}
is a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

3.1.
Vectors in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})

Let us start by analysing vectors in the Hilbert
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
in terms of their “slices”.

Recall from Section
2.3
that vectors in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
can be regarded as continuous functions on
\mathcal{G}
vanishing at infinity. Hence for each
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, we have the restriction map

\phi_{x}:L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\longrightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\quad\text{by}\quad\phi_{x}(\xi)=\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}.

Lemma 3.1
.

With the notation as above, the map
\phi_{x}
is surjective.

Proof.

Given
\xi_{x}\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
, we assume that
\mathrm{supp}(\xi_{x})=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N}\}
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is étale, there exists an open subset
U
in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
containing
x
and an open bisection
A_{i}
containing
\gamma_{i}
for each
i=1,2,\ldots,N
such that
\mathrm{s}(A_{i})=U
.

Take a function
\rho\in C_{c}(U)\subseteq C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with range in
[0,1]
and value
1
at the given point
x
. Define a function
\xi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}
as follows: for
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
we set

\xi(\gamma)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\xi_{x}(\gamma_{i})\rho\left(\mathrm{s}(\gamma)\right),&\gamma\in A_{i}\text{ for }i=1,2,\ldots,N;\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is clear that
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
and
\phi_{x}(\xi)=\xi_{x}
, which implies that
\phi_{x}(C_{c}(\mathcal{G}))=C_{c}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
. Since
\phi_{x}
is contractive and
C_{c}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
is dense in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
, we conclude the proof.
∎

Collecting
\phi_{x}
together, we obtain the following
slicing map
:

\phi:L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\longrightarrow\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\quad\text{by}\quad\phi(\xi)\coloneqq\left(\phi_{x}(\xi)\right)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}},

which is clearly an isometric embedding. Here
\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
denotes the Banach space consisting of uniformly norm-bounded families
(\xi_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
with norm
\|(\xi_{x})_{x}\|:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|\xi_{x}\|
.

The following result characterises elements in the image of
\phi
, which essentially comes from
[
10
,
11
,
39
]
. For completeness, here we provide a proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

With the same notation as above, let
(\xi_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
be a family of vectors.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\left(\xi_{x}\right)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\phi\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)
;

(2)

the function
x\mapsto\|\xi_{x}-\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|
belongs to
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
for all
\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
;

(3)

the function
x\mapsto\left\langle\xi_{x},\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\right\rangle
belongs to
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
for all
\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, and the function
x\mapsto\|\xi_{x}\|
belongs to
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
.

Proof.

“
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
”: Let
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and we consider
(\xi_{x})_{x}=\phi(\xi)
. Note that condition
(2)
clearly holds when
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
. Since
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
with respect to the norm
\|\xi\|=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|
, condition
(2)
holds for general
\xi
by a standard approximating argument.

“
(2)

\Rightarrow

(1)
”: Taking
\eta=0
in condition
(2)
, we obtain that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\setminus K}\|\xi_{x}\|<\varepsilon
. For
x\in K
, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that there exists
\eta^{x}\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
such that
\|\xi_{x}-\eta^{x}|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|<\varepsilon
. By condition
(2)
, there exists an open neighbourhood
U_{x}
of
x
such that for
y\in U_{x}
we have
\|\xi_{y}-\eta^{x}|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}}\|<\varepsilon
.

Since
K
is compact, there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in K
such that
K\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}U_{x_{i}}
. Consider the open cover
\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\setminus K,U_{x_{1}},\ldots,U_{x_{N}}\}
of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and take a partition of unity
\{\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{N}\}
subordinate to
\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\setminus K
and
\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{i})\subseteq U_{x_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. Define

\eta\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}\cdot\eta^{x_{i}}.

It is easy to check that
\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
and
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|\xi_{x}-\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|<\varepsilon
. Hence we conclude condition
(1)
.

Finally, notice that “
(2)

\Rightarrow

(3)
” follows directly from the polarization identity, and “
(3)

\Rightarrow

(2)
” holds trivially. Hence we conclude the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

For those who are familiar with the notion of Hilbert bundles, we remark that the image of
\phi
generates a continuous field of Hilbert spaces
\mathfrak{S}
over
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
(see
[
10
, Proposition 10.2.3]
), and Lemma
3.2
is designed to show that
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
coincides with elements in
\mathfrak{S}
vanishing at infinity. Furthermore, it follows from
[
16
, Theorem II.13.18]
that there is a unique topology on
\bigsqcup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
which makes it into a Hilbert bundle over
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
are continuous sections.

3.2.
Operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))

Having established the characterisation for vectors in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
(see Lemma
3.2
), now we move to the case of operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
.

First note that similar to the slicing map
\phi
, we can construct a slicing map on
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. More precisely, for
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
and
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we define an operator
\Phi_{x}(T)
on
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
as follows:

\Phi_{x}(T)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\coloneqq T(\xi)|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\quad\text{for}\quad\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}).

It follows from Proposition
2.3
and Lemma
3.1
that
\Phi_{x}(T)
is well-defined and belongs to
\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
, called the
slice of
T
at
x
. Collecting them together, we define the
slicing map
:

(3.1)

\Phi\colon\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\longrightarrow\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))\quad\text{by}\quad\Phi(T)\coloneqq(\Phi_{x}(T))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}},

where
\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
denotes the direct product of
C^{*}
-algebras. It is easy to see that
\Phi
is a
C^{*}
-monomorphism, and hence an isometric embedding.

Remark 3.4
.

As pointed out by the anonymous referee, the maps
\phi_{x}
and
\Phi_{x}
constructed above can also be defined using the language of interior tensor products of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules (recalled in Section
2.3
). More precisely, for
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we consider the evaluation map
\mathrm{ev}_{x}\colon C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
given by
\mathrm{ev}_{x}(f)=f(x)
for
f\in C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
. Then the interior tensor product
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}\mathbb{C}
is a Hilbert space and isomorphic to
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
. It is not hard to verify that for
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, the vector
\phi_{x}(\xi)
is nothing but the image of
\xi\otimes 1
under this isomorphism, and the operator
\Phi_{x}(T)
can be identified with
T\otimes_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}1
. However, we stick to the original definitions for
\phi_{x}
and
\Phi_{x}
since the tools of interior tensor products and related theories seem only responsible to simplify the definitions rather than the proofs.

The rest of this subsection is devoted to establishing characterisations for the image of
\Phi
. Our philosophy is that although operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
are hard to describe, the family of their slices (
i.e.
, the image of
\Phi
) are usually more achievable and easy to handle. This idea will play a crucial role when we study the quasi-locality for groupoids later.

Let us start with the following elementary result:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
be a family of operators.
Then
\left(T_{x}\right)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
belongs to the image of
\Phi

if and only if
for any
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, the families
(T_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
and
(T_{x}^{*}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
belong to
\phi\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)
.

Proof.

The necessity is clear, and hence we only focus on the sufficiency. First define a map
T:C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
by

T(\xi)\coloneqq\phi^{-1}\big((T_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}))_{x}\big)\quad\text{for}\quad\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G}),

which is well-defined by assumption. It follows directly that
\|T(\xi)\|\leq\|(T_{x})_{x}\|\cdot\|\xi\|
, which implies that
T
can be extended to a bounded linear map
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, still denoted by
T
. Similarly, we have a bounded linear map
S:L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
such that
S(\eta)=\phi^{-1}\big((T^{*}_{x}(\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}))_{x}\big)
for
\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
. For
\xi,\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, it is clear that
\langle\eta,T(\xi)\rangle=\langle S(\eta),\xi\rangle
. By continuity, we obtain that
S=T^{*}
and hence conclude the proof.
∎

Combining Lemma
3.2
with Lemma
3.5
, we obtain the following:

Corollary 3.6
.

For
\left(T_{x}\right)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\left(T_{x}\right)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\Phi\left(\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)\right)
;

(2)

the functions
x\mapsto\|T_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})-\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|
and
x\mapsto\|T^{*}_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})-\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|
belong to
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
for all
\xi,\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
;

(3)

the functions
x\mapsto\left\langle\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}},T_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\right\rangle
,
x\mapsto\|T_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|
and
x\mapsto\|T^{*}_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|
belong to
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
for all
\xi,\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
.

Corollary
3.6
does provide a description for operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. However, either condition (2) or (3) is still not easy to verify. We would like to explore a more delicate characterisation for elements in the image of
\Phi
. To fulfil the task, we would like to consult the machinery of operator fibre spaces introduced by Austin and the second-named author in
[
4
, Section 3]
.

Consider the following disjoint union:

(3.2)

E\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right).

We write
\sigma_{x}
for an element in
\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)\subseteq E
to indicate the fibre in which it lives.
Following
[
4
, Section 3]
, we endow a topology on
E
as follows: a net
\{\sigma_{x_{i}}\}_{i\in I}
converges to
\sigma_{x}
in
E

if and only if

x_{i}\rightarrow x
in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and for any
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}\rightarrow\gamma^{\prime}
,
\gamma_{i}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow\gamma^{\prime\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}
with
\mathrm{s}(\gamma_{i}^{\prime})=x_{i}=\mathrm{s}(\gamma_{i}^{\prime\prime})
, we have

\left\langle\delta_{\gamma_{i}^{\prime\prime}},\sigma_{x_{i}}(\delta_{\gamma_{i}^{\prime}})\right\rangle\rightarrow\left\langle\delta_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}},\sigma_{x}(\delta_{\gamma^{\prime}})\right\rangle.

Definition 3.7

(
[
4
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

For a locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, the space
E
defined in (
3.2
) equipped with the above topology is called the
operator fibre space
associated to
\mathcal{G}
.

A
section
of
E
is a map
\sigma:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\to E
such that
\sigma(x)\in\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
for
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Denote by
\Gamma_{b}(E)
the set of continuous sections
\sigma
of
E
with
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|\sigma(x)\|<\infty
. Equipped with pointwise operations and norm
\|\sigma\|:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|\sigma(x)\|
,
\Gamma_{b}(E)
becomes a
C^{*}
-algebra. By definition, we have the inclusion map

(3.3)

\iota\colon\Gamma_{b}(E)\hookrightarrow\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right),\quad\sigma\mapsto(\sigma(x))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}.

The following lemma characterises continuous sections in terms of their slices, which can be deduced easily from the definition, hence we omit the proof.

Lemma 3.8
.

For
\left(\sigma_{x}\right)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\left(\sigma_{x}\right)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\iota\left(\Gamma_{b}(E)\right)
;

(2)

the function
x\mapsto\left\langle\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}},\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\right\rangle
belongs to
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
for all
\xi,\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
;

(3)

the function
\gamma\mapsto\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}})\right)(\gamma)
is continuous on
\mathcal{G}
for all
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
;

(4)

the function
\gamma\mapsto\left(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}})\right)(\gamma)
is continuous on
\mathcal{G}
for all
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
.

Combining Corollary
3.6
with Lemma
3.8
, we obtain that
\Phi\left(\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)\right)\subset\iota\left(\Gamma_{b}(E)\right)
. In other words, the homomorphism
\Phi
factors through
\Gamma_{b}(E)
,
i.e.
, there exists a
C^{*}
-homomorphism
\widetilde{\Phi}:\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)\to\Gamma_{b}(E)
such that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right).}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma_{b}(E)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\Phi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}

Remark 3.9
.

We remark that for a general element
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, it was pointed out by Exel
[
14
]
that the map
x\mapsto\|\Phi_{x}(T)\|
on
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
might not be continuous. However, Corollary
3.6
and Lemma
3.8
indicate that the map
x\mapsto\Phi_{x}(T)
is indeed continuous with respect to the topology in Definition
3.7
. That is the reason for us to consult the notion of operator fibre spaces.

Thanks to Lemma
3.8
, it remains to determine the image of
\widetilde{\Phi}
. The following result fulfils the task:

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid, and
E
be the associated operator fibre space.
For
\sigma\in\Gamma_{b}(E)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\sigma\in\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)\right)
;

(2)

for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap K=\emptyset
, we have

(3.4)

\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma(x)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|<\varepsilon\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma(x)^{*}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|<\varepsilon;

(3)

for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A_{x},B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(KB_{x})=\emptyset
, we have

\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma(x)\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|<\varepsilon\text{ and }\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma(x)^{*}\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|<\varepsilon.

Proof.

To save notation, we write
\sigma_{x}
instead of
\sigma(x)
where
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
for the given section
\sigma\in\Gamma_{b}(E)
.

“
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
”: By assumption, there exists
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
such that
\sigma=\widetilde{\Phi}(T)
. For any
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, we have
T(\xi)\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
T^{*}(\xi)\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
. Hence for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\eta,\zeta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
such that
\|T(\xi)-\eta\|<\varepsilon
and
\|T^{*}(\xi)-\zeta\|<\varepsilon
.

Take
K\coloneqq\mathrm{supp}(\eta)\cup\mathrm{supp}(\zeta)
. For any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap K=\emptyset
, we obtain:

\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|=\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(T(\xi)|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\big)\|\leq\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\big)\|+\varepsilon=\varepsilon.

Similarly, we obtain
\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma_{x}^{*}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|<\varepsilon
.

“
(2)

\Rightarrow

(1)
”: By assumption, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
satisfying condition
(2)
. Take a function
\rho\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
such that
\rho|_{K}\equiv 1
.

Consider
\zeta:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by
\zeta(\gamma)\coloneqq\rho(\gamma)\cdot\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}})\right)(\gamma)
for
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
. It follows from Lemma
3.8
that
\zeta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
. Moreover, for
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we have:

\|\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})-\phi_{x}(\zeta)\|=\|(1-\rho)|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|=\|(1-\rho)|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\cdot\chi_{\mathcal{G}_{x}\setminus K}\cdot\big(\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|,

which is less than
\varepsilon
by the assumption (
3.4
). Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain that
\big(\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
belongs to
\phi(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. Similarly,
\big(\sigma_{x}^{*}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
belongs to
\phi(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. Therefore, we conclude condition
(1)
thanks to Lemma
3.5
.

“
(2)

\Rightarrow

(3)
”: As remarked in the last paragraph of Section
2.2
, it suffices to prove condition
(3)
for
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
whose support is contained in a compact bisection
K_{\xi}
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, take a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
satisfying condition
(2)
. We set
\widetilde{K}\coloneqq KK_{\xi}^{-1}
. Since
\mathrm{supp}(\xi)
is a bisection, it follows that for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
we have:

\chi_{B_{x}}\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}=\begin{cases}\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}},&\text{ if }K_{\xi}\cap B_{x}\neq\emptyset;\\
0,&\text{ if }K_{\xi}\cap B_{x}=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Now for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A_{x},B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(\widetilde{K}B_{x})=\emptyset
, then
A_{x}\cap(KK_{\xi}^{-1}B_{x})=\emptyset
. If
K_{\xi}\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
, then:

\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma_{x}\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|=\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma_{x}\big)(\chi_{B_{x}}\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|=0

and similarly,
\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma_{x}^{*}\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|=0
. If
K_{\xi}\cap B_{x}\neq\emptyset
, then

KK_{\xi}^{-1}B_{x}\supseteq K\cdot\{x\}=K\cap\mathcal{G}_{x}.

Hence
A_{x}\cap K=A_{x}\cap(K\cap\mathcal{G}_{x})\subseteq A_{x}\cap(KK_{\xi}^{-1}B_{x})=\emptyset
. Applying condition
(2)
, we obtain:

\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma_{x}\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|=\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma_{x}\big)(\chi_{B_{x}}\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|=\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|<\varepsilon.

Similarly, we have
\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma_{x}^{*}\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|<\varepsilon
. Therefore, we conclude condition
(3)
.

“
(3)

\Rightarrow

(2)
”: Given
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, let
K_{\xi}\coloneqq\mathrm{supp}(\xi)
. By condition
(3)
, there exists a compact subset
\widetilde{K}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
satisfying the requirement therein. We set
K\coloneqq\widetilde{K}K_{\xi}
and take
B_{x}=\mathrm{supp}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})
. For any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap K=\emptyset
, we have

A_{x}\cap(\widetilde{K}B_{x})=A_{x}\cap\big(\widetilde{K}\cdot\mathrm{supp}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\subseteq A_{x}\cap(\widetilde{K}K_{\xi})=A_{x}\cap K=\emptyset.

Hence we obtain:

\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|=\|\left(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma_{x}\chi_{B_{x}}\right)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|<\varepsilon

and similarly,
\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma_{x}^{*}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|<\varepsilon
. Therefore, we conclude the proof.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Careful readers might already notice from Corollary
3.6
(3) and Lemma
3.8
(2) that in order to show that a continuous section

belongs to the image of

\Rightarrow

”.

Comparing with the notion of quasi-locality recalled in Definition

, we note that condition

can be regarded as a vector-wise uniform version of quasi-locality. Hence we introduce the following:

Combining Lemma
3.8
with Proposition

, we finally reach the following:

.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. For
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
, the following are equivalent:

(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}

;

(2)

the map

, and

are vector-wise uniformly quasi-local.

3.3.
The case of dense subsets

A lot of examples including the coarse groupoids (see Section
2.6.3
) come with natural dense open subsets in their unit spaces. In these cases, we now show that the characterisations established in Section
3.2
can be further simplified.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Assume that
X
is a dense subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and we set
\partial X\coloneqq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\setminus X
. Define

E_{X}:=\bigsqcup_{x\in X}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))\subseteq E

with the induced topology from
E
, and denote by
\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
the
C^{*}
-algebra consisting of all continuous norm-bounded sections of
E_{X}
. Consider the restriction map

\mathrm{Res}:\Gamma_{b}(E)\longrightarrow\Gamma_{b}(E_{X}),\quad\sigma\mapsto\sigma|_{X}.

Since
X
is dense and
\mathcal{G}
is étale, it is easy to verify that the map
\mathrm{Res}
is injective. To determine its image, we introduce the following:

Definition 3.14
.

A section
\sigma\in\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
is called
extendable
if for any
\omega\in\partial X
and
\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
, there exists a constant
c_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}
such that for any
x_{i}\in X
with
x_{i}\to\omega
and any
\gamma^{\prime}_{i},\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{i}}
with
\gamma^{\prime}_{i}\rightarrow\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}\rightarrow\gamma^{\prime\prime}
, then
\langle\delta_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}},\sigma(x_{i})(\delta_{\gamma^{\prime}_{i}})\rangle\to c_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}
.

The following lemma explains the terminology:

Lemma 3.15
.

The map
\mathrm{Res}:\Gamma_{b}(E)\rightarrow\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
is a
C^{*}
-monomorphism with image consisting of all extendable sections in
\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that an extendable section
\sigma\in\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
can be extended to a section
\tilde{\sigma}\in\Gamma_{b}(E)
. For
\omega\in\partial X
, define an operator
\sigma_{\omega}:\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{\omega})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{\omega})
by

\langle\delta_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}},\sigma_{\omega}(\delta_{\gamma^{\prime}})\rangle\coloneqq\lim_{i}\langle\delta_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}},\sigma({x_{i}})(\delta_{\gamma^{\prime}_{i}})\rangle\quad\mbox{for}\quad\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\omega},

where
x_{i}
is a net in
X
converging to
\omega
, and
\gamma^{\prime}_{i},\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{i}}
with
\gamma^{\prime}_{i}\rightarrow\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}\rightarrow\gamma^{\prime\prime}
. This is well-defined since
\mathcal{G}
is étale and
\sigma
is extenable. To show that
\sigma_{\omega}
is a bounded operator, we note that

\|\sigma_{\omega}\|=\sup\big\{|\langle\eta_{\omega},\sigma_{\omega}(\xi_{\omega})\rangle|:\xi_{\omega},\eta_{\omega}\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G}_{\omega})\mbox{~with~}\|\xi_{\omega}\|=\|\eta_{\omega}\|=1\big\}.

Hence using the étaleness and
\sup_{x\in X}\|\sigma(x)\|<\infty
, we obtain that
\sigma_{\omega}
is bounded (details are left to readers). Finally, we define a map
\tilde{\sigma}:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\to E
by

\tilde{\sigma}(z)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\sigma(z),&\text{ if }z\in X;\\
\sigma_{z},&\text{ if }z\in\partial X,\end{cases}

which clearly belongs to
\Gamma_{b}(E)
and extends
\sigma
. Hence we conclude the proof.
∎

The following result shows that in some special cases, sections in
\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
are always extendable. First recall that for a locally compact Hausdorff space
X
, its
Stone-Čech compactification
is a compact space
\beta X
together with a continuous map
i:X\rightarrow\beta X
such that
i:X\rightarrow i(X)
is a homeomorphsim with dense image in
\beta X
and satisfies the universal property: for any
f\in C_{b}(X)
there exists a continuous map
f^{\beta}:\beta X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that
f^{\beta}\circ i=f
. Note that in this case,
i(X)
is open in
\beta X
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and
X
be an open dense subset in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}=\beta X
is the Stone-Čech compactification of
X
(with the inclusion map). Then any section
\sigma\in\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
is extendable.

Proof.

Fix a section
\sigma\in\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
,
\omega\in\partial X
and
\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
. Using étaleness of
\mathcal{G}
, we can find two open neighborhoods
U_{\gamma^{\prime}}
and
U_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}}
of
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
, respectively, such that
\mathrm{s}|_{U_{\gamma^{\prime}}}
and
\mathrm{s}|_{U_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}}}
are homeomorphisms. Let
U_{\omega}\coloneqq\mathrm{s}(U_{\gamma^{\prime}})\cap\mathrm{s}(U_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}})
, and we take
\rho\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
such that
\rho(\omega)=1
and
\rho|_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\setminus U_{\omega}}=0
. Consider the function
f_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

f_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\rho(x)\langle\delta_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{x}},\sigma(x)(\delta_{\gamma^{\prime}_{x}})\rangle,&\text{ if }x\in X\cap U_{\omega};\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\gamma^{\prime}_{x}\in U_{\gamma^{\prime}}\cap\mathcal{G}_{x}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{x}\in U_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}}\cap\mathcal{G}_{x}
are uniquely determined. It is clear that
f_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}\in C_{b}(X)
. Thanks to the universal property of
\beta X=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,
f_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}
can be uniquely extended to a function
f^{\beta}_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}\in C(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
. Setting
c_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}\coloneqq f^{\beta}_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}(\omega)
, we conclude that
\sigma
is extendable.
∎

Combining Lemma
3.15
and
3.16
, we obtain:

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and
X
be an open dense subset in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}=\beta X
is the Stone-Čech compactification of
X
(with the inclusion map). Then the map
\mathrm{Res}:\Gamma_{b}(E)\rightarrow\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
is a
C^{*}
-isomorphism.

Now we are in the position to simplify Proposition
3.10
:

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and
X
be a dense subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.
For an extendable section
\sigma\in\Gamma_{b}(E_{X})
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\sigma\in\mathrm{Res}\circ\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)\right)
;

(2)

for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
x\in X
and
A_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap K=\emptyset
, we have

\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma(x)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|<\varepsilon\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\chi_{A_{x}}\cdot\big(\sigma(x)^{*}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|<\varepsilon;

(3)

for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
x\in X
and
A_{x},B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(KB_{x})=\emptyset
, we have

\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma(x)\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|<\varepsilon\text{ and }\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}\sigma(x)^{*}\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|<\varepsilon.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.15
, there exists
\tilde{\sigma}\in\Gamma_{b}(E)
such that
\mathrm{Res}(\tilde{\sigma})=\sigma
. Hence it suffices to show that condition (2) and (3) for
\sigma
are equivalent to those in Proposition
3.18
for
\tilde{\sigma}
, respectively. Again to save the notation, we write
\tilde{\sigma}_{x}
instead of
\tilde{\sigma}(x)
for
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

We only present the case for condition (2), while the other is similar. Assume that condition (2) holds for
\sigma
, we would like to verify Proposition
3.18
(2) for
\tilde{\sigma}
. Given
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, let
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
be a compact subset satisfying condition (2). For any
y\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and any finite subset
A_{y}\subset\mathcal{G}_{y}
with
A_{y}\cap K=\emptyset
, there exist
x\in X
and a finite subset
A_{x}\subset\mathcal{G}_{x}
such that
A_{x}\cap K=\emptyset
and

|\langle\tilde{\sigma}_{y}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}}),\eta|_{A_{y}}\rangle-\langle\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}),\eta|_{A_{x}}\rangle|<\varepsilon

for any
\eta\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with
\|\eta\|\leq 1
, since
\mathcal{G}
is étale and
\tilde{\sigma}
is the extension of
\sigma
. Note that
\|\chi_{A_{x}}\big(\sigma_{x}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)\|<\varepsilon
and

\|\chi_{A_{y}}\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{y}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}})\right)\|=\sup_{\|\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}}\|=1}|\langle\tilde{\sigma}_{y}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}}),\eta|_{A_{y}}\rangle|.

Hence we obtain
\|\chi_{A_{y}}\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{y}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}})\right)\|<2\varepsilon
, and similarly
\|\chi_{A_{y}}\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{y}^{*}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}})\right)\|<2\varepsilon
. Finally using a standard approximating argument, the above estimates hold for arbitrary
A_{y}\subset\mathcal{G}_{y}
with
A_{y}\cap K=\emptyset
, and therefore we conclude the proof.
∎

Analogous to Definition
3.12
, for a dense subset
X
of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we say that a family
(T_{x})_{x\in X}\in\prod_{x\in X}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
is
vector-wise uniformly quasi-local
if for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
x\in X
and
A_{x},B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(KB_{x})=\emptyset
, we have
\|\big(\chi_{A_{x}}T_{x}\chi_{B_{x}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\|<\varepsilon
.

Finally, we conclude the following for the case of dense subsets:

Corollary 3.19
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and
X
be a dense subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. For
(T_{x})_{x\in X}\in\prod_{x\in X}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

(T_{x})_{x\in X}
belongs to
\mathrm{Res}\circ\Phi\big(\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\big)
;

(2)

the map
x\mapsto T_{x}
is a continuous extendable section of
E_{X}
, and
(T_{x})_{x\in X},(T^{*}_{x})_{x\in X}
are vector-wise uniformly quasi-local.

Moreover, we can omit the requirement of extendableness in condition (2) when
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is the Stone-Čech compactification of
X
(with the inclusion map).

3.4.
Equivariant operators

In this subsection, we study an extra condition for operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
called
\mathcal{G}
-equivariance. It turns out that all operators in the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
satisfy this condition, which suggests us to restrict ourselves to the equivariant case when studying quasi-locality in Section
4
. This class of operators possess a huge advantage since they can always be represented by certain continuous functions (see Section
3.5
), which will play a key role in the proof of our main theorem.

Recall that the left regular representation
\Lambda:C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
is defined in Section
2.3
. As for groups, we can also consider the right regular representation as follows. Given a function
g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, define the map
\rho(g)\colon C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
by

\big(\rho(g)\xi\big)(\gamma):=(\xi\ast g)(\gamma)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}}g(\beta)\xi(\gamma\beta^{-1})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}g(\alpha^{-1}\gamma)\xi(\alpha)\text{ for }\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G}).

Note that in general,
\rho(g)
might
not
be a
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module homomorphism. However, we have the following:

Lemma 3.20
.

For
g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, the operator
\rho(g)
can be extended to a bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
(regarded as a Banach space), still denoted by
\rho(g)
.

Proof.

For any
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, we have:

\begin{split}\|\rho(g)\xi\|^{2}&=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}g(\beta)\xi(\gamma\beta^{-1})|^{2}\\
&\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}\big(\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|g(\beta)|\big)\cdot\big(\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|g(\beta)|\cdot|\xi(\gamma\beta^{-1})|^{2}\big)\\
&=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\big(\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|g(\beta)|\big)\cdot\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}\big(|g(\beta)|\cdot\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|\xi(\gamma\beta^{-1})|^{2}\big)\\
&\leq\big(\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|g(\beta)|\big)^{2}\cdot\|\xi\|^{2}.\end{split}

Note that

, hence the number

is finite. Therefore,

can be extended to a bounded operator on

.
∎

As a result, the convolution operator can also be extended to an operator (with the same notation)

Lemma 3.23
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. An operator

\mathcal{G}
-equivariant

V_{\gamma}\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(T)=\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(T)V_{\gamma}
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}

Proof.

For the necessity, we fix
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
and denote
x=\mathrm{s}(\gamma),y=\mathrm{r}(\gamma)
. By étaleness, choose a pre-compact neighbourhood
U
of
\gamma^{-1}
such that
\mathrm{s}|_{U}:U\to\mathrm{s}(U)
is a homeomorphism. Choose
g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
such that
g(\gamma^{-1})=1
and
g(\alpha)=0
for
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}\setminus U

\eta\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{y}

(\rho(g)\eta)(\gamma^{\prime})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{r}(\gamma^{\prime})}}g(\alpha^{-1}\gamma^{\prime})\eta(\alpha)=\eta(\gamma^{\prime}\gamma)=V_{\gamma}(\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})(\gamma^{\prime}),

which implies that
(\rho(g)\eta)|_{G_{y}}=V_{\gamma}(\eta|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})
. Hence for any
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big(V_{\gamma}\Phi_{x}(T)\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})

\displaystyle=V_{\gamma}(T(\xi)|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})=\big(\rho(g)T\xi\big)|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}}=\big(T\rho(g)\xi\big)|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}}=\Phi_{y}(T)\big((\rho(g)\xi)|_{\mathcal{G}_{y}}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\Phi_{y}(T)V_{\gamma}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}).

For the sufficiency, we need to show that
\rho(g)T=T\rho(g)
for any
g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\mathrm{supp}(g)
is a bisection (see Section
2.2
). Given
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
with
\mathrm{s}(\gamma)=x
and
\mathrm{r}(\gamma)=y
, the intersection
\mathcal{G}_{x}\cap\mathrm{supp}(g)
contains at most one point. If
\mathcal{G}_{x}\cap\mathrm{supp}(g)=\{\hat{\gamma}\}
, then

\displaystyle\big(\rho(g)T\xi\big)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}g(\beta)\cdot(T\xi)(\gamma\beta^{-1})=g(\hat{\gamma})\cdot(T\xi)(\gamma\hat{\gamma}^{-1})

\displaystyle=g(\hat{\gamma})\cdot\Big(\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\hat{\gamma})}(T)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\hat{\gamma})}})\Big)(\gamma\hat{\gamma}^{-1})=g(\hat{\gamma})\cdot\Big(\big(V_{\hat{\gamma}^{-1}}\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\hat{\gamma})}(T)\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\hat{\gamma})}})\Big)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=g(\hat{\gamma})\cdot\Big(\big(\Phi_{x}(T)V_{\hat{\gamma}^{-1}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\hat{\gamma})}})\Big)(\gamma),

where we use
V_{\hat{\gamma}^{-1}}\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\hat{\gamma})}(T)=\Phi_{x}(T)V_{\hat{\gamma}^{-1}}
in the last equality. On the other hand, note that for any
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{x}
we have

(\rho(g)\xi)(\gamma^{\prime})=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}g(\beta)\cdot\xi(\gamma^{\prime}\beta^{-1})=g(\hat{\gamma})\cdot\xi(\gamma^{\prime}\hat{\gamma}^{-1})=g(\hat{\gamma})\cdot V_{\hat{\gamma}^{-1}}(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\hat{\gamma}))}})(\gamma^{\prime}).

Hence we obtain

\big(T\rho(g)\xi\big)(\gamma)=\Phi_{x}(T)\big((\rho(g)\xi)|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\big)(\gamma)=g(\hat{\gamma})\cdot\Big(\big(\Phi_{x}(T)V_{\hat{\gamma}^{-1}}\big)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\hat{\gamma})}})\Big)(\gamma),

which shows that
\rho(g)T=T\rho(g)
. The case of

is similar but much easier, hence omitted.
∎

3.5.
Realisations for equivariant operators and left convolvers

This subsection is devoted to providing a concrete description for equivariant operators. Let us start with the case of groups,
i.e.
, the unit space consists of a single point.

Let
\mathcal{G}=G
be a discrete group, and hence
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(G))=\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(G))
. For
f,g\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, the convolution
f\ast g
is a well-defined bounded function on
G
thanks to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Recall that
f\in\ell^{2}(G)
is a
left convolver
(see
[
2
, Section 1.3]
) if
f\ast g\in\ell^{2}(G)
whenever
g\in\ell^{2}(G)
. In this case, the map
g\mapsto f\ast g
is a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(G)
(by the closed graph theorem), denoted by
\Lambda(f)
. It is shown in
[
2
, Theorem 1.3.6]
that an operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(G))
is
G
-equivariant
if and only if
there exists a left convolver
f_{T}\in\ell^{2}(G)
such that
T=\Lambda(f_{T})
.

In the following, we would like to explore an analogous description for equivariant operators on groupoids. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Similar to the assumption of square-summability in the group case, we consider functions
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
satisfying the following:

(c.1)

f|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
and
f^{*}|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})
for every
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
;

(c.2)

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|f|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})}<\infty
, and
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|f^{*}|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})}<\infty
,

where
f^{*}
is defined by the same formula (
2.2
).
Then for
g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that the convolution
f\ast g
and
f^{*}\ast g
by (
2.1
) are well-defined, and are bounded functions on
\mathcal{G}
.

Definition 3.27
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. A function
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
is called a
left convolver
if
f
satisfies
(1)
and
(2)
, and both
f*g
and
f^{*}*g
belong to
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
for every
g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
.

For a left convolver
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
, it is routine to check that the map
g\mapsto f\ast g
is an adjointable operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, still denoted by
\Lambda(f)
. It is clear that
\Lambda(f)^{*}=\Lambda(f^{*})
. Note that when
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, this coincides with the left regular representation defined by (
2.4
).

Remark 3.28
.

Comparing with the case of groups, readers might wonder why we need requirements on both
f
and
f^{*}
in
(1)
,
(2)
and Definition
3.27
. The reason lies in the fact that on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, a bounded module morphism might
not
be adjointable in general. We provide some examples below.

Example 3.29
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
be the pair groupoid from Section
2.6.2
.
Consider the function
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
given by
f((m,n)):=m^{-1}
. Direct calculations show that
f|_{\mathcal{G}_{(n,n)}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|f|_{\mathcal{G}_{(n,n)}}\|_{2}<\infty
. On the other hand, note that
f^{*}
is given by
f^{*}((m,n))=n^{-1}
, and hence
f^{*}|_{\mathcal{G}_{(n,n)}}\notin\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{(n,n)})
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, note that for the function
g:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
g((m,n))=(nm)^{-1}
, it is clear that
g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
while

f*g((m,n))=\sum_{m_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}f((m,m_{1}))g((m_{1},n))=(nm)^{-1}\sum_{m_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}m_{1}^{-1}=\infty.

This illustrates that the requirements on
f^{*}
in
(1)
and
(2)

cannot
be deduced from those on
f
, and both of them are used to ensure that the convolutions by
f
and
f^{*}
are well-defined.

The following example shows that even if a function
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
satisfies
(1)
and
(2)
, it might occur that the convolution by
f
is a bounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
while
not
adjointbale. This example essentially comes from
[
29
, Example 2.1.2]
, and explains that we need to consider both
f
and
f^{*}
in Definition
3.27
.

Example 3.30
.

We consider the groupoid
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\times[0,1]
with source and range maps given by
\mathrm{s}((m,n,x))=(n,n,x)
and
\mathrm{r}((m,n,x))=(m,m,x)
for
(m,n,x)\in\mathcal{G}
, respectively. The composition map is given by
(m,n,x)\cdot(n,k,x)=(m,k,x)
, and the inverse map is by
(m,n,x)=(n,m,x)
for
m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[0,1]
.

We take the product topology on
\mathcal{G}
, and then identify
C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
with

\{(f_{m,n})_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}}:f_{m,n}\in C([0,1])\text{ and }\sup_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}\|f_{m,n}\|_{\infty}<\infty\},

whose element can be regarded as an
\mathbb{N}
-by-
\mathbb{N}
matrix with entries in
C([0,1])
. Elements in the subspace
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
can be written as
(f_{m,n})_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
f_{m,n}\neq 0
for only finitely many
(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
.

Consider the function
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
given by

f=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}&\varphi_{2}&\cdots\\
0&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\text{ where }\varphi_{i}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{on }[0,\frac{1}{i+1}]\text{ and }[\frac{1}{i},1];\\
1,&\text{at the point }\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{i+1});\\
\text{linear},&\text{on }[\frac{1}{i+1},\frac{2i+1}{2i(i+1)}]\text{ and }[\frac{2i+1}{2i(i+1)},\frac{1}{i}].\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
f^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}&0&\cdots\\
\varphi_{2}&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}
and
f^{*}\notin L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
since the first coordinate of
\langle f^{*},f^{*}\rangle
is
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi_{n}^{2}
, which is not in
C([0,1])
. It is routine to check that
f
satisfies
(1)
and
(2)
.

Given
(\xi_{i,j})\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, we have:

f*\xi=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi_{k}\xi_{k,1}&\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi_{k}\xi_{k,2}&\cdots\\
0&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

which belongs to

using a standard approximating argument. This implies that the convolution
\Lambda(f)
is a linear operator on

\eta=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{[0,1]}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, and then
f^{*}*\eta=f^{*}\notin L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
.

Now we are in the position to provide the realisation for equivariant operators:

Proposition 3.31
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid.

(1)

For any left convolver
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
, we have
\Lambda(f)\in\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)^{\mathcal{G}}
.

(2)

Conversely, for any
T\in\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)^{\mathcal{G}}
there exists a unique left convolver
f_{T}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
such that
T=\Lambda(f_{T})
.

Proof.

(1). It is clear that
\Lambda(f)\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
for any left convolver
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
. Moreover, note that for any
g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
and
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, we have
\Lambda(f)\rho(g)\xi=f\ast(\xi\ast g)=(f\ast\xi)\ast g=\rho(g)\Lambda(f)\xi
. Hence
\Lambda(f)
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant.

(2). Given
T\in\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)^{\mathcal{G}}
, we consider the function
f_{T}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

(3.7)

f_{T}(\gamma)\coloneqq\big(\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(T)(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})\big)(\gamma)\quad\text{for}\quad\gamma\in\mathcal{G}.

Since
T
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant, then for any
\gamma,\alpha\in\mathcal{G}
with
\mathrm{r}(\alpha)=\mathrm{s}(\gamma)
we have

(3.8)

f_{T}(\gamma)=\big(\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(T)(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})\big)(\gamma)=\big(\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\alpha)}(T)(\delta_{\alpha})\big)(\gamma\alpha).

On the other hand, for
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
we have

\displaystyle f_{T^{*}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(T^{*})(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})(\gamma)=\left(V_{\gamma^{-1}}\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(T)^{*}V_{\gamma}\right)(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\left(\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(T)^{*}V_{\gamma}\right)(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})(\gamma\gamma^{-1})=\left\langle\delta_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)},\left(\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(T)^{*}V_{\gamma}\right)(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(T)(\delta_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}),V_{\gamma}(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})\rangle=\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(T)(\delta_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}),\delta_{\gamma^{-1}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(T)(\delta_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)})(\gamma^{-1})}=\overline{f_{T}(\gamma^{-1})}=(f_{T})^{*}(\gamma),

where
T
being
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant is used in the second equality. This shows that
(f_{T})^{*}=f_{T^{*}}
. Hence for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, we have
f_{T}|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}=\Phi_{x}(T)(\delta_{x})
and
(f_{T})^{*}|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}=\Phi_{x}(T^{*})(\delta_{x})
, which implies that
(1)
and
(2)
hold for
f_{T}
.

Since
x\mapsto\Phi_{x}(T)
is a bounded continuous section in
\Gamma_{b}(E)
, it follows that
f_{T}
is a bounded continuous function on
\mathcal{G}
. For any
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, note that
f_{T}\ast\xi
is well-defined since
f_{T}
satisfies
(1)
and
(2)
. Moreover, for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
with
\mathrm{s}(\gamma)=x
we have

\displaystyle(f_{T}\ast\xi)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}f_{T}(\gamma\alpha^{-1})\xi(\alpha)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}\big(\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\alpha)}(T)(\delta_{\mathrm{r}(\alpha)})\big)(\gamma\alpha^{-1})\xi(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}\big(\Phi_{x}(T)(\delta_{\alpha})\big)(\gamma)\xi(\alpha)=\big(\Phi_{x}(T)(\xi|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})\big)(\gamma)=(T\xi)(\gamma),

where we use (
3.8
) in the third equality. This implies that
f_{T}\ast\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\Lambda(f_{T})=T
. Similarly, we have
(f_{T})^{*}\ast\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\Lambda((f_{T})^{*})=T^{*}
. Therefore,
f_{T}
is a left convolver and we conclude the proof.
∎

, we provide an alternative picture for the isomorphism

in (

):

Example 3.32
.

Following the notation in Example

, for

f_{T}\in C_{b}(G(X))
such that
T=\Lambda(f_{T})
. Considering the restriction of
f_{T}
on
X\times X
, it is easy to see that the isomorphism
\Theta
in (
3.6
) can be written as follows:

\big(\Theta(T)(\xi)\big)(x):=\sum_{y\in X}f_{T}(x,y)\xi(y)\quad\text{for}\quad\xi\in\ell^{2}(X)\text{ and }x\in X.

Therefore as claimed in Example
3.25
,
\Theta
in (
3.6
) extends the isomorphism
\Theta:C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\to C^{*}_{u}(X)
in (
2.7
), which explains the notation.

We end this section with some discussions on multiplication operators.
Recall that for
g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
, the multiplication operator
M(g):L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
is defined by
(M(g)\xi)(\gamma)=g(\gamma)\xi(\gamma)
for
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
.

To simplify the statement, we say that a function
g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant
if
g(\beta)=g(\beta\gamma^{-1})
for any
\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
with
\mathrm{s}(\beta)=\mathrm{s}(\gamma)
.

Lemma 3.33
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid and
g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
. Then the multiplication operator
M(g)
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant
if and only if

g
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant.

Proof.

Assume that
g
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant, then for
h\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
,
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
, we have:

\displaystyle\big(\rho(h)M(g)\xi\big)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}}h(\beta)g(\gamma\beta^{-1})\xi(\gamma\beta^{-1})=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}}h(\beta)g(\gamma)\xi(\gamma\beta^{-1})

\displaystyle=g(\gamma)\cdot\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}}h(\beta)\xi(\gamma\beta^{-1})=\big(M(g)\rho(h)\xi\big)(\gamma).

Conversely, it follows from Lemma
3.23
that
V_{\gamma}\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(M(g))=\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(M(g))V_{\gamma}
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
. Hence for
\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}
, we have

g(\beta)\delta_{\beta\gamma^{-1}}=V_{\gamma}(g(\beta)\delta_{\beta})=V_{\gamma}\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(M(g))(\delta_{\beta})=\Phi_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}(M(g))V_{\gamma}(\delta_{\beta})=g(\beta\gamma^{-1})\delta_{\beta\gamma^{-1}},

which shows that
g
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant.
∎

On the other hand, the following lemma explores when an equivariant operator can be realised as a multiplication operator:

Lemma 3.34
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. For
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))^{\mathcal{G}}
, let
f_{T}
be the associated left convolver from Proposition
3.31
. Then
T=M(g)
for some
g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})

if and only if

\mathrm{supp}(f_{T})\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. In this case,
f_{T}=g|_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
and hence
M(g)=\Lambda(g|_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}})
.

Proof.

If
T=M(g)
for some
g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
, from (
3.7
) we have:

f_{T}(\gamma)=\big(\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(M(g))(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})\big)(\gamma)=g(\mathrm{s}(\gamma))\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(\gamma)=\begin{cases}g(\gamma),&\text{ if }\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)};\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence we obtain that
\mathrm{supp}(f_{T})\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
f_{T}=g|_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
. Conversely, assume that
\mathrm{supp}(f_{T})\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Then for any
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
, we have

(f_{T}\ast\xi)(\gamma)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}}f_{T}(\gamma\beta^{-1})\xi(\beta)=f_{T}(\mathrm{r}(\gamma))\xi(\gamma).

Setting
g(\gamma)=f_{T}(\mathrm{r}(\gamma))
for
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
, it follows that
g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant and
\Lambda(f_{T})=M(g)
. Hence we conclude the proof.
∎

4.
Compactly supported and quasi-local operators for groupoids

In this section, we introduce the key notion of this paper,
i.e.
, compactly supported and quasi-local operators for groupoids.
Moreover, we show that the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra can be recovered by equivariant and compactly supported operators.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid and
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
.
For
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
, we say that
f
and
g
are
K
-separated
if
\big(K\cdot\mathrm{supp}(f)\big)\cap\mathrm{supp}(g)=\emptyset
and
\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap\big(K\cdot\mathrm{supp}(g)\big)=\emptyset
,
i.e.
,
\gamma_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1}\notin K
and
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\notin K
for any
\gamma_{1}\in\mathrm{supp}(f)
and
\gamma_{2}\in\mathrm{supp}(g)
with
\mathrm{s}(\gamma_{1})=\mathrm{s}(\gamma_{2})
.

Recall that in the case of the coarse groupoids, compact subsets are always contained in closures of entourages. This inspires us to introduce the following:

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid and
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. We define the following:

(1)

T
is
supported in
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
if
gTf=0
for any
K
-separated functions
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
.

(2)

T
is
compactly supported
if
T
is supported in some compact subset of
\mathcal{G}
.

(3)

T
is
quasi-local
if for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact subset
K\subset\mathcal{G}
such that for any
K
-separated functions
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
we have
\|gTf\|<\varepsilon\|g\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}
.

It is clear that compactly supported operators are always quasi-local. Also note that
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
admits approximating units consisting of elements in
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, hence the following is straightforward:

Lemma 4.3
.

For an operator
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, we have the following:

(1)

T
is compactly supported
if and only if
there exists a compact subset
K\subset\mathcal{G}
such that
gTf=0
for any
K
-separated functions
f,g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
;

(2)

T
is quasi-local
if and only if
for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact subset
K\subset\mathcal{G}
such that for any
K
-separated functions
f,g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
we have
\|gTf\|<\varepsilon\|g\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}
.

Using the slicing map
\Phi
from (
3.1
), we provide the following “fibre-wise” characterisation for compactly supported and quasi-local operators:

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid and
T\in\mathscr{L}\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\right)
. We have the following:

(1)

T
is supported in a compact set
K\subset\mathcal{G}

if and only if

\chi_{A_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{B_{x}}=0
for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and subsets
A_{x},B_{x}\subset\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
;

(2)

T
is a quasi-local operator
if and only if
for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
K\subset\mathcal{G}
such that
\|\chi_{A_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{B_{x}}\|<\varepsilon
for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and subsets
A_{x},B_{x}\subset\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Here we only provide the proof for
(2)
, since the other is similar and simpler.

Necessity
: Given
\varepsilon>0
, we take a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
satisfying the condition in Definition
4.2
(3). Fix an
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. For any finite subsets
A_{x},B_{x}\subset\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
, take open subsets
U
and
V
in
\mathcal{G}
containing
A_{x}
and
B_{x}
, respectively, such that
UV^{-1}\cap K=\emptyset
and
VU^{-1}\cap K=\emptyset
. By Urysohn’s lemma, there exist
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
with range in
[0,1]
,
\mathrm{supp}(g)\subset U
and
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subset V
such that
g|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}=\chi_{A_{x}}
and
f|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}=\chi_{B_{x}}
. It is then clear that
f
and
g
are
K
-separated. Hence we obtain:

\|\chi_{A_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{B_{x}}\|=\|\Phi_{x}(gTf)\|\leq\|gTf\|<\varepsilon.

Using an approximating argument, the estimate above holds for any subsets
A_{x},B_{x}
in
\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
.

Sufficiency
: Given
\varepsilon>0
, we take a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
satisfying the assumption in (2). For any
K
-separated functions
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
, we have

\|gTf\|=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|\Phi_{x}(gTf)\|=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|g|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\cdot\Phi_{x}(T)\cdot f|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|.

For
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, denote
A_{x}:=\mathrm{supp}(g|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})
and
B_{x}:=\mathrm{supp}(f|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}})
. It is clear that
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
. Therefore, the assumption implies that

\|g|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\cdot\Phi_{x}(T)\cdot f|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|=\|g|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\cdot\chi_{A_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{B_{x}}\cdot f|_{\mathcal{G}_{x}}\|<\varepsilon\|g\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty},

which shows that
\|gTf\|\leq\varepsilon\|g\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}
.
∎

Inspired by Proposition
4.4
, we introduce the following:

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. A family
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
is said to be
compactly uniformly quasi-local
if for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A_{x},B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
, we have
\|\chi_{A_{x}}T_{x}\chi_{B_{x}}\|<\varepsilon
.

It is clear that the notion of compactly uniform quasi-locality implies vector-wise uniform quasi-locality introduced in Definition
3.12
. Hence combining Theorem
3.13
with Proposition
4.4
, we reach the following:

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. For
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

there exists a quasi-local operator
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
such that
\Phi(T)=(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
;

(2)

the map
x\mapsto T_{x}
is a continuous section of
E
introduced in (
3.2
) and
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
is compactly uniformly quasi-local.

A similar result also holds for the case of compactly supported operators.

As revealed in Section
3.3
, we can simplify Proposition
4.4
for dense subsets as follows. The proof is similar to that for Proposition
3.18
, hence omitted.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,
X
a dense subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. Then we have the following:

(1)

T
is supported in a compact set
K\subset\mathcal{G}

if and only if

\chi_{A_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{B_{x}}=0
for any
x\in X
and subsets
A_{x},B_{x}\subset\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
;

(2)

T
is quasi-local
if and only if
for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
K\subset\mathcal{G}
such that
\|\chi_{A_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{B_{x}}\|<\varepsilon
for any
x\in X
and subsets
A_{x},B_{x}\subset\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
.

Analogous to Definition
4.5
, for a dense subset
X
of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we say that a family
(T_{x})_{x\in X}\in\prod_{x\in X}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
is
compactly uniformly quasi-local
if for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
x\in X
and
A_{x},B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
, we have
\|\chi_{A_{x}}T_{x}\chi_{B_{x}}\|<\varepsilon
.

It is clear that in the case of dense subsets, the notion of compactly uniform quasi-locality also implies vector-wise uniform quasi-locality. Hence combining Corollary
3.19
with Lemma
4.7
, we reach the following:

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
X
be a dense subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. For
(T_{x})_{x\in X}\in\prod_{x\in X}\mathscr{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

there exists a quasi-local operator
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
such that
\mathrm{Res}\circ\Phi(T)=(T_{x})_{x\in X}
;

(2)

the map
x\mapsto T_{x}
is a continuous extendable section of
E_{X}
introduced in (
3.2
) and
(T_{x})_{x\in X}
is compactly uniformly quasi-local.

Moreover, we can omit the requirement of extendableness in condition (2) when
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is the Stone-Čech compactification of
X
(with the inclusion map).

A similar result also holds for the case of compactly supported operators.

The following example shows that our definition of quasi-locality for groupoids recovers Definition
2.10
in the case of metric spaces:

Example 4.9
.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space with bounded geometry and
G(X)
the associated coarse groupoid. Combining Example
3.25
and Corollary
4.8
, we obtain that a
G(X)
-equivariant operator
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(G(X)))
is quasi-local
if and only if
there exists a quasi-local operator (in the sense of Definition
2.10
)
T_{0}\in\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
such that
\mathrm{Res}\circ\Phi(T)=(T_{0})_{x\in X}
.

Analogous to the uniform Roe and quasi-local algebras for metric spaces introduced in Definition
2.9
and
2.11
, we define the following algebras for groupoids:

Definition 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid.

(1)

Denote by
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]
the set of all compactly supported operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, and define the
uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{G}
to be its norm closure in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
.

(2)

Denote by
C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})
the set of all quasi-local operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, which forms a
C^{*}
-algebra and is called the
uniform quasi-local algebra of
\mathcal{G}
.

We also consider the equivariant counterpart. For a locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, denote by
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
the
\ast
-subalgebra in
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]
consisting of
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant operators. Also denote by
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})^{\mathcal{G}}
and
C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})^{\mathcal{G}}
the
C^{*}
-subalgebras in
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})
and
C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})
, respectively, consisting of
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant operators.

Remark 4.11
.

Recall that Anantharaman-Delaroche introduced the notion of uniform
C^{*}
-algebra of a groupoid in
[
1
, Definition 6.1]
with the same notation
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})
. We will show in Section
7.2
that it coincides with Definition
4.10
(1).

The following proposition connects the algebra
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
with
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, which parallels the result that the equivariant part of the algebraic uniform Roe algebra for a group coincides with the group algebra.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. Then
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
is
\ast
-isomorphic to
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
. As a consequence, the norm closure of
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
is
C^{*}
-isomorphic to the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

Recall that we have the faithful representation
\Lambda:C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
defined in (
2.4
). Hence it suffices to show that the image
\Lambda(C_{c}(\mathcal{G}))
coincides with the
\ast
-algebra
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
.

Direct calculations show that if
h\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
has support in a compact set
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, then the operator
\Lambda(h)
has support in
K
. Together with Proposition
3.31
(1), this implies that
\Lambda(C_{c}(\mathcal{G}))\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
. On the other hand, for
T\in\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
with support in a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, Proposition
3.31
(2) implies that there exists a unique
f_{T}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
defined in (
3.7
) such that
T=\Lambda(f_{T})
. Recall that

f_{T}(\gamma)=\big(\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(T)(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})\big)(\gamma)=\langle\delta_{\gamma},\Phi_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}(T)(\delta_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)})\rangle\quad\text{for}\quad\gamma\in\mathcal{G}.

Hence Proposition
4.4
implies that
f_{T}
has support in
K\cup K^{-1}
, which concludes that
f_{T}\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

Note that
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
is contained in
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})^{\mathcal{G}}
, hence doing completing we obtain that the norm closure of
\mathbb{C}_{u}[\mathcal{G}]^{\mathcal{G}}
in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
is contained in
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})^{\mathcal{G}}
. However in general, it is unclear whether these two algebras are identical. We provide a sufficient condition in the next section.

To conclude this section, we discuss a relative commutant picture for quasi-local operators on groupoids, which generalises the metric space case proved in
[
43
, Theorem 2.8 “(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)”]
(see also
[
5
, Proposition 3.3]
). Recall the following notion:

Definition 4.14
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a groupoid,
K
a symmetric compact subset of
\mathcal{G}
and
\delta>0
. A function
h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
is said to have
(K,\delta)
–variation
, if for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}
with
\alpha\beta^{-1}\in K
, we have
|h(\alpha)-h(\beta)|<\delta
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid and
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

T
is quasi-local;

(2)

for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\delta>0
and a symmetric
compact
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
with

\|h\|_{\infty}=1
and
(K,\delta)
-variation, we have
\|[T,h]\|<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We follow the outline of the proof for
[
43
, Theorem 2.8 “(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)”]
.

“
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
”:
Given
\varepsilon>0
, choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1/n\leq\delta
and
take
K^{\prime}=K\cup K^{2}\cup\cdots\cup K^{n}
. For any
K^{\prime}
-separated
f,g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with norm
1
, there exists
h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
with
(K,\delta)
-variation and norm
1
such that
h
equals
1
on
\mathrm{supp}(f)
and
0
on
\mathrm{supp}(g)
. This can be achieved by Urysohn’s lemma. Then we have:

\|gTf\|=\|gThf\|=\|g[T,h]f\|<\varepsilon,

which implies that
T
is a quasi-local operator by Lemma
4.3
(2).

“
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
”: For simplicity, we assume that
\|T\|=1
. For any
0<\varepsilon<1
, take
\delta\leq\varepsilon/16
and choose
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\delta<1/N<\varepsilon/8
. Due to quasi-locality, there exists a symmetric compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
K
-separated
f,g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
with norm
1
, we have
\|gTf\|\leq\varepsilon/(2N^{2})
.

For
h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
with norm
1
and
(K,\delta)
-variation, we claim that
\|[T,h]\|<\varepsilon
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
h\geq 0
. Fixing
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, denote by
h_{x}
the restriction of
h
on
\mathcal{G}_{x}
. We set

A_{1}:=h_{x}^{-1}\big([0,\frac{1}{N}]\big),\quad A_{i}:=h_{x}^{-1}\big((\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}]\big)\quad\text{for}\quad i=2,\ldots,N.

It is clear that
(A_{i}A^{-1}_{j})\cap K=\emptyset
for
|i-j|>1
. Applying Proposition
4.4
(2), we have

\|\chi_{A_{i}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{A_{j}}\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2N^{2}}.

On the other hand, note that

\|h_{x}-\sum^{N}_{i=1}\frac{i}{N}\chi_{A_{i}}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/N.

Hence we obtain:

\displaystyle\|[\Phi_{x}(T),h_{x}]\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{N}+\|[\Phi_{x}(T),\sum^{N}_{i=1}\frac{i}{N}\chi_{A_{i}}]\|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}+\|(\sum^{N}_{j=1}\chi_{A_{j}})(\sum^{N}_{i=1}\frac{i}{N}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{A_{i}})-(\sum^{N}_{j=1}\frac{j}{N}\chi_{A_{j}}\Phi_{x}(T))(\sum^{N}_{i=1}\chi_{A_{i}})\|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}+\|\sum^{N}_{i,j=1}(\frac{i-j}{N})\chi_{A_{j}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{A_{i}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{N}+\sum_{|i-j|>1}\|\chi_{A_{j}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{A_{i}}\|+\|\sum_{|i-j|\leq 1}(\frac{i-j}{N})\chi_{A_{j}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{A_{i}}\|.

Each item in the first sum is dominated by
\varepsilon/(2N^{2})
, so the first item is less than
\varepsilon/2
. The second sum can be divided into two parts for
j=i+1
and
j=i-1
, each of which is dominated by
1/N
. Hence we have

\|[\Phi_{x}(T),h_{x}]\|\leq\frac{2}{N}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{2}{N}\leq\varepsilon,

which implies that

\|[T,h]\|=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|[\Phi_{x}(T),h_{x}]\|<\varepsilon.

Hence we conclude the proof.
∎

5.
Main theorem

In the previous section, we introduced the notion of quasi-locality for groupoids and constructed several associated

-algebras (see Definition

).
Now we are in the position to prove the following main result of this paper, showing that they coincide with each other under the assumption of amenability.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact,
\sigma
-compact and étale groupoid.
If
\mathcal{G}
is amenable, then we have

Note that when

\|\chi_{D_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{D_{x}}\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{12}

\gamma\in D_{x}
and
\beta\in C_{x}
, we have
\gamma\beta^{-1}\notin K_{\widetilde{n}}
. This implies that
\|\chi_{D_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{C_{x}}\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{12}
and
\|\chi_{C_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{D_{x}}\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{12}
. Hence

\|\chi_{D_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{D_{x}}+\chi_{C_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{D_{x}}+\chi_{D_{x}}\Phi_{x}(T)\chi_{C_{x}}\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{4},

which implies that

(5.7)

\|m_{k_{x}}(\Phi_{x}(T))-m_{\widetilde{k}_{x}}(\Phi_{x}(T))\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Finally combining (
5.5
), (
5.6
) and (
5.7
), we obtain

\|\Lambda(h\cdot f_{T})-T\|=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|m_{k_{x}}(\Phi_{x}(T))-\Phi_{x}(T)\|<\varepsilon.

Note that
h\cdot f_{T}\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, hence we conclude that
T\in C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
.
∎

6.
Examples

In this section, we apply Theorem
5.1
to the examples mentioned in Section
2.6
.

6.1.
Discrete groups

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete group with unit
1_{\Gamma}
. As mentioned in Section

, the reduced groupoid

-algebra of

is the same as the reduced group
C^{*}
-algebra

Writing in the matrix form, obviously an operator

is

-equivariant
if and only if

for any
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Gamma

Equipping

with an arbitrary proper word length metric, it is clear that

is compactly supported
if and only if

T
has finite propagation in the sense of Definition
2.8
.
Moreover, the notion of quasi-locality for
T
from Definition
2.10
coincides with that from Definition
4.2
(3). Hence in this case, Theorem
5.1
recovers the following known result:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete amenable group. Then for an operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma))
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

T
belongs to

(2)

is
\Gamma
-invariant and belongs to

;

(3)

is
\Gamma
-invariant and quasi-local.

Remark 6.2
.

The above theorem also holds for groups with the
approximation property
from
[
19
]
(see also
[
9
]
). In fact, it was shown in
[
48
]
that

if

has the approximation property. Moreover, it follows from
[
19
,
31
]
that amenable groups have the approximation property, and the approximation property implies Property A. Therefore combining with
[

, Theorem 3.3]
, we obtain that if

has the approximation property, then

.

6.2.
Pair groupoids

Let

be a set and we consider the pair groupoid

from Section
2.6.2
. By Theorem

, we know that the map

Lemma 6.3
.

Let

be a set and

be the pair groupoid. Then for some fixed

(6.1)

Analogous to the discussion in Example

6.3.

This example is our motivation of the whole work, and details have already been spread in Section

7.
Beyond equivariance

In Section
5
, we study the equivariant parts in uniform Roe and quasi-local algebras for groupoids, and our main result (Theorem
5.1
) provides a sufficient condition to ensure that they are the same. In this section, we will turn to these
C^{*}
-algebras themselves without the restriction of equivariance. Our approach is to consider the semi-direct product groupoids and transfer the general case to the equivariant one, inspired by the discussions in
[
1
, Section 6]
.

7.1.
Semi-direct product groupoids

Here we collect some prelimenary knowledge on groupoid actions and semi-direct product groupoids, and guide readers to
[
1
]
for more details.

Let
X
be a locally compact space. A
fibre space over
X
is a pair
(Y,p)
, where
Y
is a locally compact space and
p\colon Y\rightarrow X
is a continuous surjective map. For two fibre spaces
(Y_{1},p_{1})
and
(Y_{2},p_{2})
over
X
, we form their
fibred product
to be

Y_{1}\prescript{}{p_{1}}{\ast}_{p_{2}}Y_{2}\coloneqq\{(y_{1},y_{2})\in Y_{1}\times Y_{2}:p_{1}(y_{1})=p_{2}(y_{2})\},

equipped with the topology induced by the product topology.

Definition 7.1

(
[
1
, Definition 1.2]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact groupoid.
A
left
\mathcal{G}
-space
is a fibre space
(Y,p)
over
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, equipped with a continuous map
(\gamma,y)\mapsto\gamma y
from
\mathcal{G}\prescript{}{\mathrm{s}}{*}_{p}Y
to
Y
(called a

\mathcal{G}
-action on
(Y,p)
) which satisfies the following:

•

p(\gamma y)=\mathrm{r}(\gamma)
for
(\gamma,y)\in\mathcal{G}\prescript{}{\mathrm{s}}{*}_{p}Y
, and
p(y)y=y
;

•

\gamma_{2}(\gamma_{1}y)=(\gamma_{2}\gamma_{1})y
for
(\gamma_{1},y)\in\mathcal{G}\prescript{}{\mathrm{s}}{*}_{p}Y
and
\mathrm{s}(\gamma_{2})=\mathrm{r}(\gamma_{1})
.

Given a
\mathcal{G}
-space
(Y,p)
, the associated
semi-direct product groupoid

Y\rtimes\mathcal{G}
is defined to be
Y\prescript{}{p}{*}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}
as a topological space. For
(y,\gamma)\in Y\rtimes\mathcal{G}
, define its range to be
(y,\mathrm{r}(\gamma))
and its source to be
(\gamma^{-1}y,\mathrm{s}(\gamma))
. The product and inverse are given by

(y,\gamma)(\gamma^{-1}y,\gamma^{\prime})=(y,\gamma\gamma^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad(y,\gamma)^{-1}=(\gamma^{-1}y,\gamma^{-1}).

Clearly
(y,p(y))\mapsto y
is a homeomorphism from the unit space of
Y\rtimes\mathcal{G}
onto
Y
. Hence from now on, we regard Y as the unit space of the groupoid
Y\rtimes\mathcal{G}
. As shown in
[
1
, Proposition 1.4 and Proposition 1.6]
, the range map
\mathrm{r}:Y\rtimes\mathcal{G}\to Y
is always open, and
Y\rtimes\mathcal{G}
is étale when the groupoid
\mathcal{G}
itself is étale.

The class of groupoid actions we are interested in come from certain compactification of the given groupoid. For completeness, let us recall the following:

Definition 7.2
.

Let
(Y,p)
be a fibre space over a locally compact space
X
.

(1)

We say that
(Y,p)
is
fibrewise compact
if
p
is proper,
i.e.
,
p^{-1}(K)
is compact for any compact
K\subseteq X
.

(2)

A
fibrewise compactification
of
(Y,p)
is a fibrewise compact fibre space
(Z,q)
together with a continuous map
\varphi\colon Y\rightarrow Z
, which is a homeomorphism onto an open dense subset of
Z
and satisfies
q\circ\varphi=p
. Usually we regard
Y
as a subset of
Z
in this case, and the morphism
\varphi
is just the inclusion.

To introduce concrete fibrewise compactifications, we need refer to the Gelfand spectra. Let
(Y,p)
be a fibre space over a locally compact space
X
. Denote

p^{*}(C_{0}(X))\coloneqq\{f\circ p:f\in C_{0}(X)\}

and
C_{0}(Y,p)
to be the closure of the following set in
C_{b}(Y)
:

C_{c}(Y,p)=\{g\in C_{b}(Y):\text{ there is a compact }K\subset X,\,\mathrm{supp}(g)\subset p^{-1}(K)\}.

It was shown in
[
1
, Proposition A.2]
that the fibrewise compactifications of
(Y,p)
are in one-to-one correspondence with the
C^{*}
-subalgebras of
C_{0}(Y,p)
containing
p^{*}\left(C_{0}(X)\right)+C_{0}(Y)
. The fibrewise compactification associated to
C_{0}(Y,p)
is called the
fibrewise Stone-Čech compactification of
(Y,p)
, denoted by
(\beta_{p}Y,p_{\beta})
.

As a special case, we consider the following: Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. Then the groupoid multiplication of
\mathcal{G}
provides a
\mathcal{G}
-action on the fibre space
(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{r})
,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{G}\prescript{}{\mathrm{s}}{*}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
defined by
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\mapsto\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}
. Moreover, it was shown in
[
1
, Proposition 2.5]
that this
\mathcal{G}
-action extends in a unique way to a
\mathcal{G}
-action on the fibrewise Stone-Čech compactification
\left(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G},\mathrm{r}_{\beta}\right)
. Hence we can consider the semi-direct product groupoid
\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}
.

Following the terminology in
[
1
]
, a locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
strongly amenable at infinity
if
\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}
is amenable. As shown in
[
1
]
, this has a close relation with the notion of exactness for groupoids. By definition,
\mathcal{G}
is called
C^{*}
-exact
if the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
is exact. Also recall that
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
weakly inner amenable
if for any compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a continuous bounded positive definite function
f
on the product groupoid
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
which is properly supported such that
|f(\gamma,\gamma)-1|<\varepsilon
for all
\gamma\in K
.

Proposition 7.3

(
[
1
, Corollary 7.4 and Theorem 8.6]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact, second countable and étale groupoid. If
\mathcal{G}
is strongly amenable at infinity, then
\mathcal{G}
is
C^{*}
-exact. Conversely, if
\mathcal{G}
is weakly inner amenable and
C^{*}
-exact, then
\mathcal{G}
is strongly amenable at infinity.

Throughout the rest of this section, let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid and
(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G},\mathrm{r}_{\beta})
be the fibrewise Stone-Čech compactification of
(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{r})
. Consider the
\mathcal{G}
-action on
(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G},\mathrm{r}_{\beta})
induced by the groupoid multiplication in
\mathcal{G}
, and the associated semi-direct product groupoid
\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}
. In the next few subsections, we will transfer the uniform Roe and quasi-local algebras for
\mathcal{G}
to the equivariant part of the corresponding algebras for
\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}
.

7.2.
Compactly supported case

Here we deal with the case of compactly supported operators and the uniform Roe algebra, which was originally studied in

First we show that our notion of the uniform Roe algebra

A

\ast

\mathcal{T}:C_{t}(\mathcal{G}\ast_{\mathrm{s}}\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))

5
5

5

From equivariance to non-equivariance

\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{z}}:\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}))\longrightarrow\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}_{\beta}(z)})),\quad T\mapsto P_{z}TP_{z}^{*}.

Let us consider the map

(\iota\xi)(\gamma z,\gamma)\coloneqq\xi(\gamma)\quad\text{for}\quad(\gamma z,\gamma)\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}\quad\text{and}\quad\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G}).

\iota\xi
has support in
\mathrm{r}_{\beta}^{-1}(\mathrm{r}(K))\times K
, which is compact in
\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}
. Moreover for
\xi\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, we have

\|\iota\xi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})}=\sup_{z\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}}\|(\iota\xi)|_{(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}}\|^{2}=\sup_{z\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}_{\beta}(z)}}|\xi(\gamma)|^{2}=\|\xi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}.

Hence
\iota
can be extended to an isometry (with the same notation):

\iota:L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\longrightarrow L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}).

On the other hand, we define another map

\kappa:C_{c}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})\longrightarrow C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\quad\text{by}\quad(\kappa\eta)(\gamma)\coloneqq\eta(\gamma,\gamma)

for
\eta\in C_{c}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
. It is clear that
\kappa
is well-defined. Moreover, we have:

\displaystyle\|\kappa\eta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|(\kappa\eta)(\gamma)|^{2}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|\eta(\gamma,\gamma)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}_{\beta}(z)}}|\eta(\gamma z,\gamma)|^{2}=\|\eta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})}.

Hence
\kappa
can be extended to a contractive map (with the same notation):

\kappa:L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})\longrightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{G}).

Now we are in the position to extend the map
\Theta
from (
7.2
) to all
(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})
-equivariant operators in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))
. More precisely, we define

\Theta:\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))

by

(7.4)

\Theta(T):=\kappa\circ T\circ\iota\quad\text{for}\quad T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}.

In other words, the operator
\Theta(T)
makes the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(T)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\kappa}

We will show in Lemma
7.6
below that the image of
\Theta
is indeed contained in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
. First, note that Proposition
3.31
implies that
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}
can be written as
T=\Lambda(f_{T})
where
f_{T}
is the associated left convolver. Direct calculations show that
\Theta(T)
satisfies the following:

(7.5)

\big(\Theta(T)\xi\big)(\gamma)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}}f_{T}(\gamma,\gamma\alpha^{-1})\xi(\alpha)\quad\text{ for }\quad\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}).

Comparing (
7.1
) with (
7.5
), it is clear that
\Theta
in (
7.4
) indeed extends the isomorphism
\Theta:C^{*}_{r}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})
in (
7.2
). Hence again we use the same notation.

Remark 7.5
.

It is pointed out by the anonymous referee that the map
\Theta
from (
7.4
) can be defined in a more tidy way using interior tensor products of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules as follows. Let
\tau:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\to\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}
be the inclusion map, which induces a
*
-homomorphism
\tau^{*}:C_{0}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
. It is easy to see that
L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})
is isomorphic to the interior tensor product
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{\mathrm{r}_{\beta}^{*}}C_{0}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G})
as Hilbert
C_{0}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G})
-modules. Hence the interior tensor product
L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})\otimes_{\tau^{*}}C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
is isomorphic to
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
as Hilbert
C_{0}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules due to that the composition
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau}}{{\to}}\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{r}_{\beta}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is the identity. Moreover, direct calculations show that
\Theta(T)
in (
7.4
) is identified with
T\otimes_{\tau^{*}}1
under the isomorphism above.

On the other hand, using (
7.5
) it is straightforward to prove:

Lemma 7.6
.

With the same notation as above, we have:

(1)

for
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}
, the operator
\Theta(T)
is an adjointable operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
with adjoint
\Theta(T^{*})
;

(2)

for
T,S\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}
, we have
\Theta(TS)=\Theta(T)\Theta(S)
;

(3)

\Theta
is injective.

Note that item (1) and (2) can be deduced directly from the alternative viewpoint of
\Theta(T)
given in Remark
7.5
. However, we still need (
7.5
) to conclude item (3).

Combining the analysis above, we reach the following:

Corollary 7.7
.

The map
\Theta:\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
given by (
7.4
) is a
C^{*}
-monomorphism, which extends the isomorphism in (
7.2
).

Remark 7.8
.

A careful reader might already notice that the map
\Theta
can be further extended to
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))
using the same formula (
7.4
). However in this case, it is unclear whether Lemma
7.6
still holds since (
7.5
) is no longer available. Due to the same reason, it is unclear whether
\Theta(T)
coincides with
T\otimes_{\tau^{*}}1
given in Remark
7.5
for a general
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))
.

Finally, we provide an extra viewpoint on
\Theta
in terms of the slicing maps from Section
3.2
, which will be used later to characterise the quasi-local algebra. Recall from (
3.1
) that we have the slicing maps:

(7.6)

\Phi^{\rtimes}\colon\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}\longrightarrow\prod_{z\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z})),\quad T\mapsto(\Phi^{\rtimes}_{z}(T))_{z\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}}

and

(7.7)

\Phi\colon\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\longrightarrow\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})),\quad T^{\prime}\mapsto(\Phi_{x}(T^{\prime}))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}.

For
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}
and
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, we consider the operator
\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\Phi^{\rtimes}_{x}(T))\in\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
where
\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}
is from (
7.3
).
Using (
7.5
), it is straightforward to prove:

Lemma 7.9
.

With the same notation as above, for
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}
and
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we have

\Phi_{x}(\Theta(T))=\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\Phi^{\rtimes}_{x}(T)).

Remark 7.10
.

In fact, the map
\Theta
can be alternatively defined using the slicing maps above. More precisely, consider the map

(7.8)

\mathfrak{p}:\prod_{z\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}))\longrightarrow\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})),\quad(T_{z})_{z\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}}\mapsto(\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x}))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}.

Given

, we consider the family
\mathfrak{p}\circ\Phi^{\rtimes}(T)
. Then we can apply Theorem
3.13
to verify that
\mathfrak{p}\circ\Phi^{\rtimes}(T)
belongs to
\Phi(\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})))
, and hence it provides an operator
\Phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}\circ\Phi^{\rtimes}(T))
, which is defined to be
\Theta(T)
. More details will be provided in the next subsection, where Lemma

shows that these two definitions are the same.

7.4.
Quasi-local case

In this subsection, we consider the quasi-local algebras and the main result is the following:

Proposition 7.11
.

The restriction of the map
\Theta
given by (
7.4
) provides a
C^{*}
-isomorphism:

\Theta:C^{*}_{uq}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G}).

The proof is divided into several parts, making use of the slicing maps at the end of Section
7.3
.
First recall from Section
3.2
and
3.3
, the operator fibre spaces are given by

E^{\rtimes}\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{z\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z})),\quad E^{\rtimes}_{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}))

and

E\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})),

equipped with the topology from Definition
3.7
(note that
\mathcal{G}
is a dense subset in
\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}
). Also denote by
\Gamma_{b}(E^{\rtimes}),\Gamma_{b}(E^{\rtimes}_{\mathcal{G}})
and
\Gamma_{b}(E)
the associated algebras of continuous bounded sections. Similar to Lemma
3.16
, we have the following:

Lemma 7.12
.

Every section
\sigma\in\Gamma_{b}(E^{\rtimes}_{\mathcal{G}})
is extendable.

The proof is almost the same as the one for Lemma
3.16
. In fact, borrowing the notation therein, we can shrink the neighbourhoods
U_{\gamma^{\prime}}
and
U_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}}
to ensure that the function
f_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}\in C_{0}(\mathcal{G},r)
(see Section
7.1
to recall the definition). Hence we omit the details.

On the other hand, note that for the slicing maps (
7.6
) and (
7.7
), given
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}
the property of equivariance means the following:

V_{(\gamma z,\gamma)}\Phi^{\rtimes}_{z}(T)=\Phi^{\rtimes}_{\gamma z}(T)V_{(\gamma z,\gamma)}\quad\text{for}\quad(\gamma z,\gamma)\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G},

where
V_{(\gamma z,\gamma)}:\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z})\to\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{\gamma z})
is given by
V_{(\gamma z,\gamma)}(\delta_{(\alpha z,\alpha)})=\delta_{(\alpha z,\alpha\gamma^{-1})}
(see Section
3.4
). In the following, we will consider families
(T_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{G}}
satisfying:

(7.9)

V_{(\gamma z,\gamma)}T_{z}=T_{\gamma z}V_{(\gamma z,\gamma)}\quad\text{for}\quad(\gamma z,\gamma)\in\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}.

Applying Corollary
4.8
together with Lemma
7.12
, we obtain the following characterisations for the equivariant quasi-local algebras:

Corollary 7.13
.

The map

\mathrm{Res}\circ\Phi^{\rtimes}:C^{*}_{uq}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}\longrightarrow\prod_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z})),\quad T\mapsto(\Phi^{\rtimes}_{z}(T))_{z\in\mathcal{G}}

is a
C^{*}
-isomorphism with image consisting of
(T_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{G}}
such that
z\mapsto T_{z}
is a continuous section of
E_{\mathcal{G}}^{\rtimes}
,
(T_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{G}}
is compactly uniformly quasi-local and satisfies (
7.9
).

Corollary 7.14
.

The map

\Phi:C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})\longrightarrow\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})),\quad T\mapsto(\Phi_{x}(T))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}

is a
C^{*}
-isomorphism with image consisting of
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
such that
x\mapsto T_{x}
is a continuous section of
E
and
(T_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
is compactly uniformly quasi-local.

Recall from Lemma
7.9
and Remark
7.10
that we have the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Res}\circ\Phi^{\rtimes}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{p}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi}

Hence to prove Proposition
7.11
, it suffices to verify whether the map
\mathfrak{p}
provides a bijection between the images of quasi-local algebras characterised by Corollary
7.13
and Corollary
7.14
. First note that the equivariance condition (
7.9
) directly implies the following:

Lemma 7.15
.

The following restriction of
\mathfrak{p}
is a bijection:

\mathfrak{p}:\{(T_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\in\prod_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}))\text{ satisfying }(\ref{EQ:equiv for Phi})\}\longrightarrow\prod_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x})).

Now we turn to the condition of continuity for sections:

Lemma 7.16
.

For a family
(T_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\in\prod_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}))
satisfying (
7.9
), the map
z\mapsto T_{z}
is a continuous section of
E^{\rtimes}_{\mathcal{G}}

if and only if
the map
x\mapsto\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x})
is a continuous section of
E
.

Proof.

By definition, the map
z\mapsto T_{z}
is continuous if and only if for any
z_{i}\to z
,
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}\to\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}\to\gamma^{\prime\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}
with
\mathrm{r}(z_{i})=\mathrm{s}(\gamma^{\prime}_{i})=\mathrm{s}(\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i})
and
\mathrm{r}(z)=\mathrm{s}(\gamma^{\prime})=\mathrm{s}(\gamma^{\prime\prime})
, then

(7.10)

\langle\delta_{(\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}z_{i},\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i})},T_{z_{i}}(\delta_{(\gamma^{\prime}_{i}z_{i},\gamma^{\prime}_{i})})\rangle\to\langle\delta_{(\gamma^{\prime\prime}z,\gamma^{\prime\prime})},T_{z}(\delta_{(\gamma^{\prime}z,\gamma^{\prime})})\rangle

Setting
\mathrm{s}(z_{i})=x_{i}
and
\mathrm{s}(z)=x
, then (
7.9
) implies that

V_{(z_{i},z_{i})}T_{x_{i}}V_{(z_{i},z_{i})}^{*}=T_{z_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad V_{(z,z)}T_{x}V_{(z,z)}^{*}=T_{z}.

Hence (
7.10
) can be rewritten as:

\langle\delta_{(\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}z_{i},\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}z_{i})},T_{x_{i}}(\delta_{(\gamma^{\prime}_{i}z_{i},\gamma^{\prime}_{i}z_{i})})\rangle\to\langle\delta_{(\gamma^{\prime\prime}z,\gamma^{\prime\prime}z)},T_{x}(\delta_{(\gamma^{\prime}z,\gamma^{\prime}z)})\rangle.

Applying the isomorphisms
\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}
, the above is also equivalent to:

\langle\delta_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{i}z_{i}},\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x_{i}}}(T_{x_{i}})(\delta_{\gamma^{\prime}_{i}z_{i}})\rangle\to\langle\delta_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}z},\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x})(\delta_{\gamma^{\prime}z})\rangle,

which is (by definition) nothing but the continuity of the map
x\mapsto\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x})
.
∎

Finally, we consider the condition of compactly uniform quasi-locality.

Lemma 7.17
.

A family
(T_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\in\prod_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}))
satisfying (
7.9
) is compactly uniformly quasi-local
if and only if
the family
(\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x}))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
is compactly uniformly quasi-local.

Proof.

Necessity
: By definition, given
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
\tilde{K}\subseteq\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}
such that for any
z\in\mathcal{G}
and
\tilde{A}_{z},\tilde{B}_{z}\subseteq(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}
with
\tilde{A}_{z}\cap(\tilde{K}\cdot\tilde{B}_{z})=\emptyset
and
(\tilde{K}\cdot\tilde{A}_{z})\cap\tilde{B}_{z}=\emptyset
, we have
\|\chi_{\tilde{A}_{z}}T_{z}\chi_{\tilde{B}_{z}}\|<\varepsilon
. Take

K\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}:\text{ there exists }\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}\text{ such that }(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma)\in\tilde{K}\},

which is compact. Then for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A_{x},B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
, then we have
p_{x}^{-1}(A_{x})\cap(\tilde{K}\cdot p_{x}^{-1}(B_{x}))=\emptyset
and
(\tilde{K}\cdot p_{x}^{-1}(A_{x}))\cap p_{x}^{-1}(B_{x})=\emptyset
. Hence

\varepsilon\geq\|\chi_{p_{x}^{-1}(A_{x})}T_{x}\chi_{p_{x}^{-1}(B_{x})}\|=\|\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\chi_{p_{x}^{-1}(A_{x})}T_{x}\chi_{p_{x}^{-1}(B_{x})})\|=\|\chi_{A_{x}}\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x})\chi_{B_{x}}\|,

which shows that the family
(\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x}))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
is compactly uniformly quasi-local.

Sufficiency
: Given
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that for any
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A_{x},B_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{x}
with
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})=\emptyset
and
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
, we have
\|\chi_{A_{x}}\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x})\chi_{B_{x}}\|<\varepsilon
. Take

\tilde{K}\coloneqq\{(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma)\in\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}:\gamma\in K\},

which is compact. Then for any
z\in\mathcal{G}
and
\tilde{A}_{z},\tilde{B}_{z}\subseteq(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}
with
\tilde{A}_{z}\cap(\tilde{K}\cdot\tilde{B}_{z})=\emptyset
and
(\tilde{K}\cdot\tilde{A}_{z})\cap\tilde{B}_{z}=\emptyset
, we set
x=\mathrm{s}(z)
and note that
V_{(z,z)}T_{x}V^{*}_{(z,z)}=T_{z}
. Hence we have

\displaystyle\|\chi_{\tilde{A}_{z}}T_{z}\chi_{\tilde{B}_{z}}\|

\displaystyle=\|(V^{*}_{(z,z)}\chi_{\tilde{A}_{z}}V_{(z,z)})\cdot T_{x}\cdot(V^{*}_{(z,z)}\chi_{\tilde{B}_{z}}V_{(z,z)})\|

\displaystyle=\|\chi_{\tilde{A}_{z}\cdot(z,z)}\cdot T_{x}\cdot\chi_{\tilde{B}_{z}\cdot(z,z)}\|

(7.11)

\displaystyle=\|\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\chi_{\tilde{A}_{z}\cdot(z,z)})\cdot\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x})\cdot\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\chi_{\tilde{B}_{z}\cdot(z,z)})\|.

It is straightforward to check that
\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\chi_{\tilde{A}_{z}\cdot(z,z)})=\chi_{A_{x}}
and
\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\chi_{\tilde{B}_{z}\cdot(z,z)})=\chi_{B_{x}}
, where

A_{x}\coloneqq\{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{x}:(\alpha,\alpha)\in\tilde{A}_{z}\cdot(z,z)\}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{x}:(\alpha,\alpha z^{-1})\in\tilde{A}_{z}\}

and similarly,
B_{x}\coloneqq\{\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{x}:(\beta,\beta z^{-1})\in\tilde{A}_{z}\}
. If
A_{x}\cap(K\cdot B_{x})\neq\emptyset
, then there exists
\alpha\in A_{x}
,
\beta\in B_{x}
and
\gamma\in K
such that
\alpha=\gamma\beta
. Then we have

(\alpha,\alpha z^{-1})=(\alpha,\gamma)\cdot(\beta,\beta z^{-1}),

which is a contradiction to that
\tilde{A}_{z}\cap(\tilde{K}\cdot\tilde{B}_{z})=\emptyset
. Similarly, we have
(K\cdot A_{x})\cap B_{x}=\emptyset
. Hence combining with (
7.4
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\|\chi_{\tilde{A}_{z}}T_{z}\chi_{\tilde{B}_{z}}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\chi_{\tilde{A}_{z}\cdot(z,z)})\cdot\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x})\cdot\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(\chi_{\tilde{B}_{z}\cdot(z,z)})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\chi_{A_{x}}\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x})\chi_{B_{x}}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
7.11
.

Combining Corollary
7.13
, Corollary
7.14
, Lemma
7.15
, Lemma
7.16
and Lemma
7.17
, we conclude the proof.
∎

, we obtain an alternative proof for

.

Remark 7.19
.

Readers might wonder whether Lemma

, we can show that if a family
(T_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\in\prod_{z\in\mathcal{G}}\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2}((\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{z}))
satisfying (
7.9
) is vector-wise uniformly quasi-local, then the family
(\mathrm{Ad}_{P_{x}}(T_{x}))_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
is vector-wise uniformly quasi-local. Hence applying Theorem
3.13
together with Lemma
7.16
, we obtain that
\mathfrak{p}\circ\Phi^{\rtimes}(T)
belongs to
\Phi(\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G})))
for
T\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}
. As already mentioned in Remark
7.10
, this provides an alternative approach to define the map
\Theta
.

However, it is unclear whether the opposite direction still holds for vector-wise uniform quasi-locality. If it is true, then
\Theta
will provide a
C^{*}
-isomorphism between
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
.

7.5.
Conclusion

Combining the discussions in the previous subsections, we obtain that the
C^{*}
-monomorphism

\Theta:\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))

provides
C^{*}
-isomorphisms

\overline{\mathbb{C}_{u}[\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}]^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}}^{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}))}\big(\cong C^{*}_{r}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})\big)\cong C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})

and

C^{*}_{uq}(\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G})^{\beta_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{G}\rtimes\mathcal{G}}\cong C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G}).

Hence applying Theorem
5.1
, we reach the following quasi-local characterisation for the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})
:

Theorem 7.20
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact,
\sigma
-compact and étale groupoid. Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is either strongly amenable at infinity, or secondly countable weakly inner amenable and
C^{*}
-exact. Then we have
C^{*}_{u}(\mathcal{G})=C^{*}_{uq}(\mathcal{G})
.
