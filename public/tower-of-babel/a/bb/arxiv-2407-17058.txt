DiffCD: A Symmetric Differentiable Chamfer Distance for Neural Implicit Surface Fitting
Linus Härenstam-Nielsen Lu Sang Abhishek Saroha, [1mm] Nikita Araslanov Daniel Cremers

Abstract
Neural implicit surfaces can be used to recover accurate 3D geometry from imperfect point clouds. In this work, we show that state-of-the-art techniques work by minimizing an approximation of a one-sided Chamfer distance. This shape metric is not symmetric, as it only ensures that the point cloud is near the surface but not vice versa. As a consequence, existing methods can produce inaccurate reconstructions with spurious surfaces. Although one approach against spurious surfaces has been widely used in the literature, we theoretically and experimentally show that it is equivalent to regularizing the surface area, resulting in over-smoothing. As a more appealing alternative, we propose DiffCD , a novel loss function corresponding to the symmetric Chamfer distance. In contrast to previous work, DiffCD also assures that the surface is near the point cloud, which eliminates spurious surfaces without the need for additional regularization. We experimentally show that DiffCD reliably recovers a high degree of shape detail, substantially outperforming existing work across varying surface complexity and noise levels. † † Project code is available at https://github.com/linusnie/diffcd .

Introduction

Neural implicit surfaces parametrize a 3D surface as the zero-level set of a scalar field predicted by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}}):\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle S_{\theta}=\{x:f(\theta,x)=0\},

(1)

where
\theta
are the network parameters.
Neural implicit surfaces are naturally suited for surface reconstruction.
In contrast to meshes, they are continuous everywhere and can represent shapes of arbitrary topology;
in contrast to point clouds, they provide surface normal information and enable occlusion reasoning.
The implicit surface representation is also malleable and allows for complex shape manipulations, such as topological changes
[
34
]
. Yet, as
Fig.

1
illustrates, optimizing over such a flexible parametrization of the surface also comes with some difficulties.
State-of-the-art techniques
[
5
,
15
,
30
]
struggle to accurately recover surfaces in practical scenarios, especially when the sensor data is incomplete and noisy.

Problem setup.
We consider the problem of reconstructing an underlying surface
S
from an unoriented, sparse and possibly noisy point cloud
\mathcal{P}=\{\tilde{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, contained in a bounding region
\Omega
.
Our goal is to estimate the network parameters
\theta
such that the zero-level set
S_{\theta}
approximates the true surface,
i.e
.

S_{\theta}\approx S
.
We further require that
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
approximates a
Signed Distance Field
(SDF), characterized by the
eikonal equation
:

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}f(\theta,x)\|=1.

(2)

This ensures that the absolute value of the field at any point equals the distance to the surface.
Training
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
to satisfy the eikonal equation adds regularity to the optimization problem by preventing the degenerate solution
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})=0
.

Contributions.

Firstly, we show that the widely used approach of training the network output to vanish on the point cloud
[
15
,
30
,
6
,
32
,
35
]
corresponds to minimizing only one side of the Chamfer distance –
the average distance from the input point cloud to the implicit surface.
We will refer to this quantity as the
points-to-surface
Chamfer distance.
Although minimizing the one-sided Chamfer distance works in some scenarios, it may lead to
spurious surfaces
, see
Fig.

1(b)
.

Spurious surfaces are large surface chunks located in the “blind spot” of the one-sided Chamfer distance.
This observation suggests that minimizing the full,
symmetric
Chamfer distance could lead to a more accurate surface reconstruction.
As a second contribution, we therefore develop a novel loss term corresponding to the “missing” one-sided
surface-to-points
Chamfer distance.
This term measures the average distance from the implicit surface to the point cloud.
Our proposed loss function combines the two one-sided Chamfer distances into a single loss function, the
Diff
erentiable
C
hamfer
D
istance (DiffCD).
By minimizing the symmetric Chamfer distance, DiffCD reliably mitigates the issue of spurious surfaces, achieving a substantial improvement in surface reconstruction accuracy across various types of shapes and noise levels.

As our third contribution, we theoretically justify DiffCD against a popular alternative for mitigating spurious surfaces, the off-surface loss adopted by SIREN
[
30
]
.
We prove that minimizing the SIREN loss term is equivalent to regularizing the surface area of the implicit surface. This implies that the SIREN approach mitigates spurious surfaces at the cost of shrinking the surface area, resulting in over-smoothing, or even to the surface disappearing.

Overall, our work provides novel theoretical insights into surface reconstruction with neural implicit surfaces.
Building on these insights, we develop DiffCD.
It minimizes the
symmetric
Chamfer distance between the point cloud and the implicit surface, leading to a more reliable surface reconstruction.

Related work

The two dominant categories of techniques for reconstructing neural implicit surfaces from point clouds are
supervised
and
optimization-based
.
Here, we provide a condensed summary of the most relevant previous work.
For a broader overview, we refer interested readers to the recent survey by
Sulzer et al. [31]
.

Supervised methods.

Supervised methods approach the surface reconstruction problem by learning from a large collection of point clouds with corresponding ground-truth meshes.
Supervised methods typically extract feature vectors from the point cloud, which are used to predict either occupancy or a signed distance value.
Points2Surf
[
12
]
employs PointNet
[
29
]
and regresses the sign and distance separately by combining a fine-grained local descriptor with a coarse-grained global descriptor.
POCO
[
7
]
instead uses point cloud convolutions
[
8
]
.
Neural Kernel Surface Reconstruction (NKSR)
[
18
]
defines the surface as the zero-level set of a Neural Kernel Field
[
33
]
.
It relies on PointNet-like
[
29
]
encoder to extract a voxel hierarchy of kernel features from the point cloud.

Optimization-based methods.

More relevant to our work are optimization-based methods
[
27
,
3
,
30
,
4
,
5
,
23
]
.
They reconstruct the surface by formulating a loss function, which is optimized from scratch at inference time.
IGR
[
15
]
, Neural-Pull
[
5
]
and SIREN
[
30
]
all employ different loss functions to ensure that the zero-level goes through the point cloud points.
Leveraging normals in the input significantly simplifies the problem, leading to more accurate reconstruction with classical
[
19
]
and neural-based
[
15
,
30
,
4
,
28
]
formulations alike.
Surface reconstruction without normals – the scenario addressed in this work – is highly ill-posed.
It inspires interest in regularization techniques, which typically encourage smooth solutions by means of additional loss terms.
Several methods penalize deviations from the SDF properties, most notably the eikonal equation
[
15
,
30
]
.
A related property, which can be derived from the eikonal equation, is that the gradient of an SDF lies in the nullspace of the Hessian
[
25
]
.
This property has been used to motivate regularizing the determinant of the Hessian
[
35
]
and the norm of the Hessian-gradient product
[
32
]
.
Level-set alignment
[
24
]
also makes use of this property indirectly by regularizing the misalignment of the gradient in the query points and their level-set projections. DiGs
[
6
]
goes beyond SDF properties and minimizes the divergence of the scalar field, which increases surface smoothness by encouraging flat level sets.
PHASE
[
23
]
and, as we will see in
Sec.

5
, SIREN
[
30
]
both include loss functions that regularize the surface area of the implicit surface.
In contrast to regularization, our loss term is geometric in nature. In fact, we find that tuning the weight of the eikonal loss is sufficient for adapting our method to various noise levels.

Chamfer distance for shape reconstruction.

Point2Mesh
[
16
]
and SAP
[
28
]
also develop differentiable variants of the symmetric Chamfer distance, but use an intermediate mesh representation.
In contrast, our DiffCD is defined directly in terms of the implicit surface.

3
Background

3.1
Chamfer distance

As the base of our analysis and our novel loss formulation, we leverage a widely used metric in shape reconstruction, the Chamfer distance
1
1

1

The Chamfer distance is not a distance
metric
in the mathematical sense, as it does not satisfy the triangle inequality; it is an average over point-to-set distances.

[
13
]
.
The Chamfer distance between two point sets,
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, is given by:

\displaystyle\text{CD}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\text{CD}_{\rightarrow}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})+\text{CD}_{\rightarrow}(\mathcal{P}_{2},\mathcal{P}_{1})\Big).

(3)

The term
\text{CD}_{\rightarrow}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})
is the
one-sided
Chamfer distance from
\mathcal{P}_{1}
to
\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
It simply measures the average distance between each point in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and its corresponding closest point in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\text{CD}_{\rightarrow}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{1}|}\sum_{x_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\underset{x_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}{\text{min}}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|,

(4)

where
\|{{}\cdot{}}\|
is the
\ell^{2}
-norm and
|\mathcal{P}_{1}|
is the number of points in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
.
Although the one-sided Chamfer distance can be a reliable similarity metric if
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
have similar density and size, it can be misleadingly small even in cases of substantial shape mismatch.
Notably, points in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
which are not the nearest neighbor of any point in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
will have no impact on the value of
\text{CD}_{\rightarrow}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})
.

3.2
IGR and spurious surfaces

Let us recall implicit geometric regularization (IGR)
[
15
]
, which serves as the base loss function for several follow-up works
[
30
,
6
,
35
,
32
]
.
In the normal-free unoriented setting, IGR tackles the surface reconstruction problem by minimizing the following loss function:

\displaystyle L_{\text{IGR}}(\theta)\!=\!\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(\theta,\tilde{x}_{i})|+\lambda L_{\text{eikonal}}(\theta).

(5)

The first term in
Eq.

5
ensures that the network output is close to
0
on the points in the point cloud,
\tilde{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
.
The second term is the
eikonal loss
.
It ensures that
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
approximates an SDF by penalizing deviations from the eikonal equation in
Eq.

2
:

\displaystyle L_{\text{eikonal}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{P}_{s}}|}\sum_{{x_{s}}\in{\mathcal{P}_{s}}}(\|\nabla_{x}f(\theta,{x_{s}})\|\!-\!1)^{2}.

(6)

{\mathcal{P}_{s}}
is the set of sample points at which the eikonal loss should be computed.
The hyperparameter
\lambda
in
Eq.

5
is the
eikonal weight
and it determines the trade-off between the two terms.
As we discuss in
Appendix

0.E
, the value of
\lambda
can have a significant impact on the reconstructed surface.

IGR approximates a one-sided Chamfer distance.

Using the fact that
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
will approximate the signed distance field of
S_{\theta}
, we can adopt a geometric interpretation of
Eq.

5
.
Namely, when the eikonal loss is small, each term in the sum will approximate a distance to the implicit surface:

\displaystyle|f(\theta,\tilde{x})|\approx\min_{x\in S_{\theta}}\|\tilde{x}-x\|\text{, when }L_{\text{eikonal}}(\theta)\approx 0.

(7)

Plugging this approximation back into
Eq.

5
, it becomes clear that
L_{\text{IGR}}
in turn approximates the average distance between the point cloud and the implicit surface, which we can identify as a one-sided Chamfer distance (
cf
.

Sec.

3.2
).
That is, we have:

\displaystyle L_{\text{IGR}}(\theta)\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{x\in S_{\theta}}\|\tilde{x}_{i}-x\|^{p}=\text{CD}_{\rightarrow}(\mathcal{P},S_{\theta})\text{, when }L_{\text{eikonal}}(\theta)\approx 0.

(8)

Note that
Eq.

8
expands the definition of the Chamfer distance from
Eq.

4
: the minimum here is taken over a continuous surface
S_{\theta}
.
We will refer to
Eq.

8
as the
points-to-surface
Chamfer distance between the point cloud and the implicit surface.

Spurious surfaces.
A critical shortcoming of minimizing only one side of the Chamfer distance is that it cannot account for the surface parts far away from every point in
\mathcal{P}
.
As illustrated in
Fig.

1
and
Fig.

2
, this leads to
spurious surfaces
in the reconstructions produced by IGR.
Specifically, large blob-shaped artifacts tend to accumulate near repeated thin structures and in the areas where the point cloud has missing coverage.
Introduced next, our approach addresses this issue.

In order to resolve the issue of spurious surfaces, we propose extending the one-sided Chamfer distance in
Eq.

5
to a full
symmetric
Chamfer distance.
By doing so, we explicitly account for the spurious surfaces with the loss function.
To this end, we add the “missing” Chamfer term, namely the
surface-to-points
Chamfer distance.
The surface-to-points Chamfer distance is the average distance from the surface to the point cloud:

\displaystyle\text{CD}_{\rightarrow}(S_{\theta},\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{j=1,\ldots,N}\|x_{i}(\theta)-\tilde{x}_{j}\|,

(9)

where
x_{i}(\theta)
are points sampled uniformly from the implicit surface
S_{\theta}
.
Note that
Eq.

9
is an
explicit
loss; it is defined geometrically and does not assume
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
to be an SDF.
Below, we propose an efficient sampling procedure and a method for computing the gradients of the surface-to-points loss.

Fig.

2
illustrates a 2D example of jointly minimizing both sides of the Chamfer distance.
The sample points
x_{i}(\theta)
depend on the network parameters
\theta
and are constrained to lie on the implicit surface.
By minimizing
Eq.

9
, the gradient descent will tend to move each surface point
x_{i}(\theta)
toward the closest point in the point cloud.
This surface pulling toward the point cloud will eventually remove any spurious surfaces by molding them into the existing surface.

Having defined the surface-to-points loss, we introduce
Differentiable Chamfer Distance
, or
DiffCD
.
Similar to the Chamfer distance for point clouds, we take the average of the two one-sided Chamfer distances to obtain:

\displaystyle L_{\text{DiffCD}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(\theta,\tilde{x}_{i})|+\text{CD}_{\rightarrow}(S_{\theta},\mathcal{P})\bigg)+\lambda L_{\text{eikonal}}(\theta).

(10)

Using
Eq.

8
, we can confirm that
Eq.

10
indeed approximates the symmetric Chamfer distance when the eikonal loss is small:

\displaystyle L_{\text{DiffCD}}(\theta)\approx\text{CD}(S_{\theta},\mathcal{P})\text{, when }L_{\text{eikonal}}(\theta)\approx 0.

(11)

That is, minimizing our proposed loss function
Eq.

10
implicitly minimizes the fully symmetric Chamfer distance between the point cloud and the surface, as opposed to just the one-sided Chamfer distance.

4.1
Computing gradients

In order to minimize
Eq.

9
with backpropagation, we need a method for computing surface point derivatives,
\nabla_{\theta}x_{i}(\theta)
, where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar network parameter.
For this, we use the method for controlling level sets proposed by
Atzmon et al. [2]
.
The key idea is to make use of the level set equation
f(\theta,x_{i}(\theta))=0
, which must hold for all
\theta
. Differentiating both sides of the level set equation w.r.t.

\theta
and rearranging terms yields the constraint:

\displaystyle g^{T}\nabla_{\theta}x_{i}(\theta)=-f_{\theta},

(12)

where we have used the shorthands
g:=\nabla_{x}f(\theta,x_{i}(\theta))
and
f_{\theta}:=\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta,x_{i}(\theta))
.
That is, the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}x_{i}(\theta)
lies on a plane with normal
g
at a distance
|f_{\theta}|/\|g\|
from the origin. So we can set
\nabla_{\theta}x_{i}(\theta)=\xi-f_{\theta}g/\|g\|^{2}
, where
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is any vector orthogonal to
g
.

Similarly to
Atzmon et al. [2]
, we take the minimum norm solution,
\xi=0
.
While this choice may seem arbitrary, we find that it has a significant advantage when combined with gradient descent.
Namely, it will naturally lead to linearly interpolating surfaces when the input point cloud is sparse.
To see this, we note that the derivative of each term in
Eq.

9
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\|x_{i}(\theta)-\tilde{x}\|=-f_{\theta}\frac{(x_{i}(\theta)-\tilde{x})^{T}g}{\|x_{i}(\theta)-\tilde{x}\|\|g\|^{2}}.

(13)

Importantly,
Eq.

13
has the property that
\nabla_{\theta}\|x_{i}(\theta)-\tilde{x}\|=0
whenever the residual vector
x_{i}(\theta)-\tilde{x}
is orthogonal to the surface normal
g/\|g\|
.
This means that flat surfaces are stable solutions, in the sense that the gradient of
Eq.

9
will be 0 if the closest point to each sample point lies on the plane orthogonal to the surface normal at the sample point.

In order to compute

, we also need an efficient method for uniform sampling of points from
S_{\theta}
in batches. To this end, we build on the approach from
Yang et al. [34]
.
It operates by first sampling a large number of points
p_{i}
uniformly from
\Omega
and rejecting the points if
|f(\theta,p_{i})|>\tau
.
Then, it generates the surface samples by computing

\displaystyle x_{i}(\theta)=p_{i}-f(\theta,p_{i})\nabla_{x}f(\theta,p_{i}),

(14)

which equals to the closest point of
p_{i}
on
S_{\theta}
for the case when
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
is an SDF.
However, sampling
\Omega
densely enough to obtain a sufficient amount of points on
S_{\theta_{k}}
at each iteration
k
of network training is costly.
To improve efficiency, we leverage the observation that
S_{\theta_{k}}
changes gradually during training.
We extract a mesh
{M}_{k}
every
{K_{\text{mesh}}}
iterations using marching cubes, which approximates
S_{\theta_{k}}
.
On this mesh
{M}_{k}
, we uniformly sample a large bank of points
{\mathcal{P}_{\text{mesh}}}
.
It is from this
{\mathcal{P}_{\text{mesh}}}
, rather than
\Omega
, that we sample points
p_{i}
uniformly in
Eq.

14
for the next
{K_{\text{mesh}}}
training iterations.
This approach greatly decreases the number of rejected points at each iteration.
We use
{K_{\text{mesh}}}\!=\!1000
to strike the balance between the computational cost of marching cubes and the approximation quality of the implicit surface.

We further account for the fact that
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
may not be a good approximation of an SDF everywhere in
\Omega
.
Using
Eq.

14
when
f(\theta,x)
is not an SDF can produce points that are far from the zero-level set
S_{\theta}
, as demonstrated in
Fig.

.
As a solution, we propose to replace the projection in
Eq.

14
with
SDF-descent
by setting
x_{i}(\theta)=x_{i}^{M}
, where

\displaystyle x_{i}^{m+1}=x_{i}^{m}-f(\theta,x_{i}^{m})\frac{\nabla_{x}f(\theta,x_{i}^{m})}{\|\nabla_{x}f(\theta,x_{i}^{m})\|},\quad x_{i}^{0}:=p_{i},

(15)

which will take multiple steps using the SDF approximation.
Note that
Eq.

15
is equivalent to
Eq.

14
when
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
is an SDF.
We reject any sample point with
|f(\theta,x_{i}^{M})|>\epsilon
, which indicates that SDF-descent did not converge to a point on the implicit surface.
In our experiments, we set
\epsilon=0.001
and
M=4
.

5
The SIREN loss – a surface area regularizer

The off-surface term from SIREN
[
30
]
is a widely used alternative to mitigating spurious surfaces.
Here, we will examine this loss term and establish its link to the surface area, which explain some undersirable side-effects of this regularization in surface reconstruction.

Let us begin by recalling the regularization term from SIREN
[
30
]
:
2
2

2

Our definition varies slightly from
Sitzmann et al. [30]
in order to simplify the upcoming analysis:
(i)
We scale the loss by a factor
\alpha
/2, and
(ii)
integrate over
\Omega
instead of
\Omega\setminus S
, which better models the sampling-based implementation.

\displaystyle L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}e^{-\alpha|f(\theta,x)|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(16)

which penalizes having distance field values close to zero uniformly in
\Omega
.

Eq.

16
is commonly referred to as an off-surface or non-manifold loss
[
6
,
35
]
. However, our upcoming analysis reveals that
L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}
, in fact, behaves as a
surface area regularizer
, when it is combined with the eikonal loss.
For this reason, we opt to refer to it as the
SIREN Surface Area
(SSA) loss.
The full SIREN loss without normals is given by:

\displaystyle L_{\text{SIREN}}^{\alpha}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(\theta,x_{i})|+\lambda L_{\text{eikonal}}(\theta)+\tilde{\mu}L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta),

(17)

where
\tilde{\mu}
is a weighting parameter.
To ensure consistency of the weighting parameter with SIREN
[
30
]
, we will from now on work with the rescaled weight
\mu=\tilde{\mu}|\Omega|\alpha/2
.

Looking closer at
Eq.

16
, the value of the penalty term
e^{-\alpha|f(\theta,x)|}
will be close to 1 near the surface, and close to 0 otherwise,
with a cut-off distance at roughly
1/\alpha
from the surface.
We might therefore already expect the integral to be proportional to the area of the implicit surface, denoted by
|S_{\theta}|
.
The following theorem will confirm this intuition by reinterpreting
L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)
as a surface integral. In fact, the value of
L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)
approaches
|S_{\theta}|
in the limit of large
\alpha
– at least in the case when
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
is an SDF.

Theorem 5.1

If
L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)
is defined as in
Eq.

16
and
g(\theta,x)=\nabla_{x}f(\theta,x)
, then
L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)
approaches the surface integral of
1/\|g(\theta,x)\|
over the surface
S_{\theta}
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. That is:

\displaystyle L_{\text{SSA}}^{\infty}(\theta)=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow\infty}L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)=\int_{x\in S_{\theta}}\frac{1}{\|g(\theta,x)\|}dS.

(18)

Additionally, if
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
is an SDF,
L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)
approaches the surface area of
S_{\theta}
:

\displaystyle L_{\text{SSA}}^{\infty}(\theta)=|S_{\theta}|.

(19)

Proof

See
Appendix

0.B
.
The key idea is to recognize that the penalty term
\frac{\alpha}{2}e^{-\alpha|f(\theta,x)|}
approaches a Dirac delta centered on
S_{\theta}
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
.
This allows us to integrate out one spatial dimension, leaving a surface integral weighted by the factor
1/\|g(\theta,x)\|
. When
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
is an SDF, the surface integral reduces to the surface area since
\|g(\theta,x)\|=1
for all
x
. ∎

As a consequence of
Theorem

5.1
, minimizing
L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)
will tend to “shrink away” spurious surfaces. But it will also tend to remove details from the actual shape.
If the weight parameter
\mu
is too large, the zero-level set may even disappear fully.
We demonstrate this effect analytically for a toy example in
Appendix

0.D
, and empirically in
Fig.

8
.

We remark that
Theorem

5.1
is a variant of the “co-area” formula
[
14
,
26
]
adapted to the special case of neural implicit surfaces.
The idea of regularizing surface area using an integral over delta functions dates back to
Chan and Vese [10]
, where it was used for image segmentation.

Sample approximation.
In practice, the volume integral in
Eq.

16
cannot be computed exactly.
Instead, the loss term is computed using a sample average:

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)=\frac{|\Omega|}{K}\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{K}e^{-\alpha|f(\theta,x_{i})|},

(20)

where the points
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
are sampled uniformly from
\Omega
.
We numerically verify that
Eq.

20
is a good approximation of
Eq.

16
in
Appendix

0.C
.

7
Conclusion

We presented DiffCD, a method for fitting neural implicit surfaces to sparse and noisy point clouds.
We analyzed existing methods and showed that they tend to minimize only one side of the Chamfer distance, which leaves a blind spot for spurious surfaces.
We also showed that the SIREN approach to mitigating spurious surfaces comes at the cost of regularizing the surface area.
By instead minimizing the full symmetric Chamfer distance, our method explicitly mitigates spurious surfaces without being coupled with additional regularization.

Limitations and future work.

While DiffCD can be tuned to work well across various noise levels, it does not automatically adapt in cases where the noise level is unknown or varies significantly across the shape.
This stands in contrast to supervised methods, which can handle multiple noise levels using a single model, provided the training data is available.
A promising future direction would be to extend optimization-based methods with learned surface priors, which could help in guiding the optimization process.
We also hope that our analysis of the SIREN
[
30
]
loss term in
Sec.

5
can be extended to other kinds of sampling-based surface integrals for implicit surfaces.

Appendix 0.A
Additional implementation details

Network architecture and training.
We use the same coordinate MLP
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
for all loss functions, consisting of 8 fully connected layers with 256-dimensional features and softplus activations, using geometric initialization as introduced by
Gropp et al. [15]
. We also use a skip connection from the input to the 4th layer.
We train each method for 40K iterations of Adam
[
20
]
with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
for the “No Noise” and “Medium Noise” settings, and
5\!\times\!10^{-5}
for the “Max Noise” setting. We use cosine annealing and a linear warm-up spanning the first 1000 iterations.

Batch sampling.
Each training batch consists of 5K points
\tilde{x}_{i}
sampled uniformly from the input point cloud
\mathcal{P}
. These points are used to compute the points-to-surface loss.
For DiffCD, we sample an additional 5K surface points
x_{i}(\theta)
from
S_{\theta}
following our sampling scheme (
cf
.

Sec.

4
).
For the eikonal loss, we use a mixed sampling scheme similar to
Gropp et al. [15]
.
Specifically, we sample 625 “global” points uniformly from
\Omega
, along with 5K “local” points obtained from a normal distribution centered at each
\tilde{x}_{i}
.
The standard deviation of each normal distribution is determined by the distance from
\tilde{x}_{i}
to its
50^{\text{th}}
nearest neighbor in
\mathcal{P}
.
Inspired by the implementation of
Baorui et al. [5]
, we scale each standard deviation by a factor of
0.2
, as we find that it tends to improve surface detail.
For Neural-Pull
[
5
]
, we only use the 5K local sample points as in the original implementation.
For computing
\hat{L}_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}
, we sample 5K points uniformly from
\Omega
following the original paper.

Normalization.
Before training, we follow
Baorui et al. [5]
and compute a
XYZ
-aligned bounding box around each point cloud, and normalize it by subtracting the box center and scaling by the maximum side length.
As a result, the normalized point cloud fits into a
[-0.5,0.5]^{3}
bounding box.
We de-normalize all shapes to their original size and position before computing metrics.

Appendix 0.B
Proof of Theorem 1

Proof

Note that, in the limit of large
\alpha
, the function
\frac{\alpha}{2}e^{-\alpha|{\tau}|}
approaches the
Dirac delta distribution
3
3

3

The delta distribution is an idealized function, defined such that
\delta({\tau})=0
for
{\tau}\neq 0
, and
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta({\tau})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau}=1
.
For an introduction, see
e.g
.

Arfken et al. [1, Chapter 1]
.

\delta({\tau})
.
Using this fact, we can take the limit of
Eq.

16
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
to obtain:

\displaystyle L_{\text{SSA}}^{\infty}(\theta)=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow\infty}L_{\text{SSA}}^{\alpha}(\theta)=\int_{\Omega}\delta(f(\theta,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(21)

At this point, the theorem follows directly from
e.g
.

Hörmander [17, Theorem 6.1.5]
. Nevertheless, we will provide a proof in our notation with the purpose of providing a geometric interpretation.
The main idea is to decompose the volume integral over
\Omega
into a surface integral over
S_{\theta}
and a line integral over a short segment along the surface normal
\hat{g}(\theta,x)=g(\theta,x)/\|g(\theta,x)\|
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\delta(f(\theta,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in S_{\theta}}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\delta(f(\theta,x+{\tau}\hat{g}(\theta,x)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S.

(22)

This decomposition is valid since the surface normal is orthogonal to the surface element
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S
everywhere on the surface.
The particular value of
\epsilon
does not affect the result as long as it is chosen small enough to ensure there are no additional 0-level crossings of
f(x+\tau\hat{g}(\theta,x))
on the interval
\tau\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
.

We then use a property of the Dirac delta, namely that
\delta(h({\tau}))=\delta({\tau})/h^{\prime}(0)
for any function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
whose only zero occurs at
{\tau}=0
. In our case, we set
h_{x}(\tau)=f(\theta,x+{\tau}\hat{g}(\theta,x))
, which satisfies
h_{x}(0)=f(\theta,x)=0
and
h_{x}^{\prime}(0)\!=\!\|g(\theta,x)\|
, for all
x
on
S_{\theta}
.
We can then rewrite
Eq.

22
in terms of
h_{x}
to get:

\displaystyle L_{\text{SSA}}^{\infty}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\delta(f(\theta,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(23)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in S_{\theta}}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}h_{x}(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in S_{\theta}}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\frac{\delta({\tau})}{\|g(\theta,x)\|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in S_{\theta}}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\delta(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\frac{1}{\|g(\theta,x)\|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in S_{\theta}}\frac{1}{\|g(\theta,x)\|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S,

which completes the first part of the proof. If
f(\theta,x)
is an SDF, we additionally have
\|g(\theta,x)\|=1
for all
x\in S_{\theta}
, so:

\displaystyle L_{\text{SSA}}^{\infty}(\theta)=\int_{x\in S_{\theta}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S=|S_{\theta}|,

(24)

which completes the second part of the proof. ∎

In both cases, we can also note that increasing
\alpha

.

Appendix 0.D
A toy example

We can demonstrate how the SIREN
[
30
]
loss can lead to disappearing surfaces using the following toy example of a 2D circle. Suppose the points
\tilde{x}_{i}
all lie on a circle of unknown radius
r
in
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We then parameterize the neural SDF as
f(\theta,x)=\|x\|-\theta
.
We can confirm that, as
\nabla_{x}f(\theta,x)=\frac{x}{\|x\|}
, the field
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
satisfies the eikonal constraint everywhere except at
x=0
.
Furthermore, the 0-level set is a circle of radius
\theta
, so clearly the true shape is obtained by setting
\theta=r
.

Plugging
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
into
Eq.

17
, we obtain a closed-form expression:

\displaystyle L_{\text{SIREN}}^{\alpha}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\|\tilde{x}_{i}\|-\theta|^{p}+\mu\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-\alpha|f(\theta,x)|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(26)

\displaystyle=|r-\theta|^{p}+\pi\mu\alpha\Bigg(\int_{0}^{\theta}re^{-\alpha(\theta-r)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r+\int_{\theta}^{\infty}re^{-\alpha(r-\theta)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\Bigg)

\displaystyle=|r-\theta|^{p}+\mu(2\pi\theta+\frac{\pi}{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\theta}).

For a large
\alpha
, the loss function tends towards
L_{\text{SSA}}^{\infty}(\theta)=|r-\theta|^{p}+\mu 2\pi\theta
, which is minimized by

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\begin{cases}r&\mu<\frac{1}{2\pi}\\
0&\mu>\frac{1}{2\pi}\end{cases},\quad

\displaystyle\text{for $p=1$;}

(27)

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\max\{0,r-\pi\mu\},\;\quad

\displaystyle\text{for $p=2$}.

With the mean squared error (
p=2
), the estimated radius
\theta^{*}
is always strictly less than the actual radius
r
and shrinks linearly with the weighing parameter
\mu
until the surface collapses to a single point at
x=0
for
\mu\geq r/\pi
.
In the mean error case (
p=1
), the true radius will be recovered perfectly if
\mu
is below the threshold
1/2\pi
.
However, the radius will suddenly collapse to a point when
\mu
goes above the threshold. Although the threshold can be determined in closed form in this example, it will be generally unpredictable and depend on the magnitude of the gradient of the surface area w.r.t.
the surface parameters
\theta
.

We find that the value of the eikonal weight,
\lambda
, has a significant impact on the overall smoothness of reconstructed shapes.
In particular, larger
\lambda
tends to produce smoother shapes.
This is likely due to the fact that non-flat surfaces have more non-differentiable points in the distance field, where the gradient changes direction abruptly.
Such points are difficult to approximate without violating the eikonal constraint, and they are therefore naturally avoided by the optimization process if
\lambda
is sufficiently large.
We demonstrate this effect qualitatively in
Fig.

10
the noisy scenarios.
When
\lambda
is too small, the field collapses to the degenerate solution
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})=0
.
As
\lambda
increases, a distinct 0-level set eventually appears.
However, the reconstructed shapes are initially “wrinkly”, since nothing prevents the surface from overfitting to the noise.
With larger
\lambda
, the surfaces become smoother, and the gradient norms concentrate around the value 1.
On the other hand, setting
\lambda
too large results in over-smoothing, so the optimal value for any given shape will lie somewhere in between.
We can also note that, in contrast to
L_{\text{SIREN}}
, increasing
\lambda
does not lead to the surface disappearing.

We find that using the base settings of
\lambda\!=\!0.1
and a learning rate (LR) of
10^{-3}
works well for the noise-free setting.
However, in the “medium noise” and “max noise” settings, we observe that these parameter settings result in an under-regularized problem, and the surface tends to overfit the noise.

Medium noise.
In the medium noise setting, we observe that optimization-based methods tend to overfit to the point cloud by introducing “wrinkles” in the surface such that it passes through all the points.
We demonstrate this phenomenon quantitatively and qualitatively in
Tab.

3
.
The overfitting is particularly noticeable in the Chamfer angle (CA) metric, where all methods have a CA of above 35
∘
when using
\lambda\!=\!0.1
.
In order to reduce the degree of overfitting, we increase the eikonal weight to
\lambda\!=\!0.5
.
The larger eikonal weight results in smoother surfaces across all methods, with a substantial decrease in the CA metric, although at the cost of a slight increase in CD and CD
2
.

Max noise.
In the maximum noise setting, we observe a different kind of overfitting.
Namely, the surface tends to “fold” into several layers.
This folding does in fact result in a lower Chamfer distance (both symmetric and one-sided) between the implicit surface and the point cloud.
Therefore, mitigating this phenomenon again requires regularization.
In this case, we set
\lambda=1
and LR
=5\!\!\times\!\!10^{-5}
.
We verify in
Tab.

4
that these settings yield better results across all methods.

Neural-Pull
[
5
]
tackles the surface reconstruction problem by defining a pulling operation:

\displaystyle x_{\text{pull}}(\theta,x)=x-f(\theta,x)\frac{\nabla_{x}f(\theta,x)}{\|\nabla_{x}f(\theta,x)\|}

(28)

which “pulls” the point
x
a distance of
|f(\theta,x)|
along the direction of the gradient.
The pulled point will be close to the implicit surface
S_{\theta}
as long as
f(\theta,x)
approximates an SDF.
The idea behind Neural-Pull is then to sample a set of points,
{x_{s}}\in{\mathcal{P}_{s}}
, and minimize the average distance
4
4

4

While Eq. 2 in
Baorui et al. [5]
denotes the mean
squared
distance between the point correspondences, the released implementation uses the mean distance. We opt for the mean distance to match the implementation.
between the pulled location of each sample point and the corresponding closest point in the point cloud
\mathcal{P}
:

\displaystyle L_{\text{NP}}^{p}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{P}_{s}}|}\sum_{{x_{s}}\in{\mathcal{P}_{s}}}\|x_{\text{proj}}(\mathcal{P},{x_{s}})-x_{\text{pull}}(\theta,{x_{s}})\|.

(29)

Here,
x_{\text{proj}}(A,{x_{s}})
denotes the closest point to
{x_{s}}
in the set
A
:

\displaystyle x_{\text{proj}}(A,{x_{s}})=\min_{x\in A}\|x-{x_{s}}\|.

(30)

With this definition, we have
x_{\text{pull}}(\theta,{x_{s}})\!=\!x_{\text{proj}}(S_{\theta},{x_{s}})
in the case when
f(\theta,{{}\cdot{}})
is an SDF.
We can therefore view minimizing
Eq.

29
as a method for sampling correspondences between the point cloud and the implicit surface, and then minimizing the resulting distances.
5
5

5

Eq.

29
cannot be directly interpreted as a Chamfer distance, as
x_{\text{proj}}(S_{\theta},{x_{s}})
and
x_{\text{proj}}(\mathcal{P},{x_{s}})
are not necessarily the closest points w.r.t.
each other.

This approach works well in cases where
x_{\text{proj}}(\mathcal{P},x)
is a good approximation of the closest point on the true surface
S
, as is the case when
\mathcal{P}
densely samples
S
.
However, it introduces problems when the point cloud is noisy or sparse – the scenario we addressed in this work.

We illustrate the drawbacks of Neural-Pull for a sparse 2D point cloud in
Fig.

11
.
In the sparse scenario, the sample points
{x_{s}}
are more likely to end up
in-between
the training points, resulting in the surface being pulled apart and introducing “wrinkles”.
IGR
[
15
]
and DiffCD, which are based on the implicit points-to-surface loss (
cf
.

Eq.

5

We note that our surface-to-points loss used in DiffCD also “pulls” the surface towards the point cloud.
However, the choice of the minimum norm gradient in
Sec.

4
ensures that the pulling stops when the direction towards the closest point in the point cloud is orthogonal to the surface normal. Ensuring that a smooth surface is obtained even for sparse point clouds.
