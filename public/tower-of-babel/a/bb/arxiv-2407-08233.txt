Differentially Private Neural Network Training Under the Hidden State Assumption
Ding Chen ding.chen@my.cityu.edu.hk, Haochen Luo chester.hc.luo@my.cityu.edu.hk, Xiaofei Wang xwang4279-c@my.cityu.edu.hk, Chen Liu chen.liu@cityu.edu.hk

Abstract
Current differentially private learning paradigms face a severe utility bottleneck: DP-SGD degrades performance through noise accumulation over training steps, while aggregation-based approaches such as PATE suffer from data inefficiency due to disjoint data partitioning. We propose Differentially Private Decoupled Training (DP-DT) , a framework that decouples representation learning from privacy enforcement. DP-DT confines noise injection to the weight aggregation stage and employs auxiliary models, continuously synchronized with a global model, to perform noise-free feature extraction on private data shards. We prove that DP-DT converges globally to a limit point under non-convex objectives, via a Lyapunov potential analysis combined with the Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz property. Under the Hidden State Assumption, where adversaries observe only the final published model, we further prove that DP-DT’s privacy loss can converge to a constant bound rather than accumulating with iterations. Empirical results across vision and language benchmarks confirm that DP-DT significantly mitigates utility degradation, achieving state-of-the-art privacy-utility trade-offs.

Introduction

Deep neural networks have demonstrated exceptional performance and are now integral to applications such as face recognition
He et al. (2016)
and large language models
Kandpal et al. (2022)
. However, their success relies heavily on large-scale training data, which introduces serious privacy risks. Notably, research
Shokri et al. (2017)
indicates that these models can memorize training data, allowing adversaries to extract sensitive information from the model parameters.

To address data leakage, Differential Privacy (DP) provides a theoretical framework to bound the privacy loss of individuals. Due to its post-processing property and quantifiable guarantees, DP has become the de facto standard for protecting machine learning models. Generally, privacy-preserving training algorithms fall into two main categories:

Gradient Perturbation(e.g. DP-SGD)
Gradient perturbation methods, such as DP-SGD
Abadi et al. (2016)
, enforce differential privacy guarantees by injecting calibrated noise into clipped gradients. This mechanism inherently couples privacy preservation with the gradient-based learning process. However, this coupling introduces a severe utility-privacy trade-off: since neural network training requires many iterations, the privacy budget allocated to each step must be extremely small, necessitating the addition of large noise that significantly degrades model performance.

Output aggregation(e.g. PATE)
The
sample and aggregation
framework provides the DP guarantee to the final output rather than the training process. It trains multiple models on disjoint subsets of the private data and applies the DP guarantee only during the aggregation of their predictions. However, its utility is strictly constrained by the data volume. Splitting the dataset into many disjoint parts drastically reduces the training samples available for each teacher model, leading to weak individual predictors. Consequently, to achieve acceptable performance, this framework heavily relies on public unlabeled data to assist training.

To address these fundamental challenges, we propose
Differentially Private Decoupled Training (DP-DT)
. Our core motivation is to separate the
representation learning
capability from the
privacy-preserving mechanism
. Specifically, DP-DT maintains two distinct sets of parameters:

1.

Auxiliary Models:
These models are trained on disjoint private data shards without noise injection, serving as “clean learners” for precise data features.

2.

Global Model:
A single global model is maintained and updated by coordinating with the auxiliary models through a privacy-preserving consensus scheme, ensuring the final release remains differentially private.

Crucially, DP-DT employs an
iterative coordination protocol
by using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers(ADMM) algorithm
Boyd et al. (2011)
. Specifically, In each round, auxiliary models are first re-initialized with global parameters to facilitate knowledge sharing across data shards, mitigating the inefficiency of one-off aggregation methods like PATE. Next, these models train on private data without noise injection to capture precise features. Finally, the global model aggregates these auxiliary states via a privacy-preserving consensus mechanism. This design avoids the utility degradation associated with gradient noising in DP-SGD by confining privacy mechanisms to the aggregation step. We prove DP-DT can converge globally to a limit point under non-convex objectives.

Furthermore, DP-DT inherently aligns with the
Hidden State Assumption (HSA)
, which is proposed by recent works
(
Feldman et al., 2018
;
Altschuler and Talwar, 2022
;
Ye and Shokri, 2022
)
due to its benign property in privacy loss analysis. Specifically, HSA is a more realistic threat model where the adversary observes only the final published model rather than intermediate training states. This contrasts with standard DP-SGD analysis based on
composition theorems
, which assume
continuous adversarial access
to the entire optimization trajectory. The composition theorems are inherently pessimistic, as they accumulate privacy costs for intermediate updates that are never released. Prior works
(
Feldman et al., 2018
;
Altschuler and Talwar, 2022
;
Ye and Shokri, 2022
)
have attempted to apply HSA to DP-SGD; their research indicates that privacy loss under the HSA can converge to a stable bound, while the privacy loss under the composition theorem depends on the number of iterations. However, the benign property relies on strict
convexity and smoothness
assumptions, which prevents its application in deep learning. In contrast, our DP-DT framework overcomes these limitations. By proposing a more general privacy accounting method under the HSA, we theoretically prove that under our framework, the privacy loss
converges
to a tight constant bound, making HSA effective for general non-convex deep learning.

We summarize the contribution of this paper as follows.
(1) Decoupled Framework:
We propose
Differentially Private Decoupled Training (DP-DT)
, a framework that decouples feature learning from privacy noise. By confining noise injection solely to the weight aggregation stage and utilizing auxiliary models that are continuously synchronized with the global model to perform noise-free representation extraction, DP-DT substantially improves the utility of the private model. Convergence analysis indicates that DP-DT can converge to a limit point under non-convex objectives.
(2) Generalized Hidden State Accounting:
We derive a rigorous privacy accountant for DP-DT under the HSA, theoretically proving that the privacy loss converges to a stable bound. To the best of our knowledge, this
represents the first extension
of HSA’s applicability to deep learning.

(3) Empirical Superiority:
Experiments on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 demonstrate that DP-DT significantly outperforms baselines such as DP-SGD and PATE and achieves better privacy-utility trade-off.

Related Work

Differential Privacy Machine Learning.

While initially formulated for statistical database queries
(
Dwork, 2006
)
, DP has evolved into the standard framework for trustworthy machine learning.
Existing approaches predominantly follow two strategies.
The dominant method,
Gradient Perturbation
(e.g., DP-SGD
(
Song et al., 2013
)
), secures training by injecting noise into clipped gradients. While rigorous, it inherently couples privacy with learning dynamics; the cumulative noise required over thousands of iterations often overwhelms gradient signals, strictly bounding the privacy-utility trade-off
(
Jayaraman and Evans, 2019
)
. Some recent work tries to mitigate the trade-off by projecting the gradient to subspace
Yu et al. (2021)
;
Mulrooney et al. (2025)
.
Conversely, the
Sample-and-Aggregation
framework
Nissim et al. (2007)
(e.g., PATE) provides DP guarantee to the final output. Specifically, S&A framework decomposes a dataset into disjoint subsets, computes a target function on each subset independently, and subsequently aggregates individual results with DP guarantee. The PATE framework
Papernot et al. (2016)
;
Papernot et al. (2018)
applies this framework in deep learning. They first train teacher models on disjoint data partitions. Then, they apply a noisy voting mechanism to aggregate predictions, which are used to train a student model. Although this avoids direct noise injection during representation learning, it suffers from severe
data inefficiency
: partitioning the dataset dilutes the signal for individual teachers, necessitating large-scale public data for knowledge distillation to recover performance.

Differential Privacy Accounting.

Tracking privacy budget consumption is fundamental to DP mechanisms.
Early work relied on loose composition theorems
(
Dwork, 2006
)
, later refined by the Moments Accountant (MA)
(
Abadi et al., 2016
)
, Rényi DP
(
Mironov, 2017
)
and Gaussian DP
(
Dong et al., 2022
)
for tighter bounds.
However, these methods inherently assume a transparent optimization process where all intermediate states are visible to adversaries. Consequently, privacy loss increases with the number of iterations, limiting training duration. In practice, while the adversary typically lacks access to all intermediate states, the released model itself is often accessible. Thus, recent studies
(
Feldman et al., 2018
;
Chourasia et al., 2021
;
Altschuler and Talwar, 2022
;
Ye and Shokri, 2022
)
explore privacy accounting under the
Hidden State Assumption
(HSA), where intermediate dynamics are concealed.
The key finding is that privacy loss can
converge
rather than accumulate—but only if the loss function satisfies strict properties like strong convexity and
\beta
-smoothness. These conditions are incompatible with the non-convex nature of deep learning.
While variants like
Chien and Li (2024)
relax this to
(L,\lambda)
-Hölder continuity, they depend on estimating complex continuity constants, leaving a gap for a generalized, practical accountant for deep neural networks.

Augmented Lagrangian Method.
While SGD dominates unconstrained optimization, the Augmented Lagrangian Method (ALM)
(
Hestenes, 1969
)
remains pivotal for constrained learning problems
(
Zhang et al., 2018
)
.
To enable parallelization, the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
(
Boyd et al., 2011
)
was introduced to decompose global objectives into local sub-problems.
However, classical ADMM relies on sequential updates (Gauss-Seidel) and computationally expensive exact minimization, rendering it impractical for high-dimensional neural networks.
To overcome these bottlenecks, modern variants such as the
Auxiliary Problem Principle
(APP)
(
Zhu et al., 2024
)
and Linearized ADMM
Liu et al. (2019)
employ first-order approximations with proximal terms. Moreover, ADMM now is also widely used in quantization
(
Ye et al., 2018
)
and federative learning
(
Zhou et al., 2025
)
.

Methodology

3.1
Preliminary

Privacy Definitions.

Dwork (2006)
proposes
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP to quantitatively measure the privacy of an algorithm. That is, a random mechanism
{\mathcal{M}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP if
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{D})\in{\mathcal{S}})\leq e^{\epsilon}{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}})+\delta
holds for any neighboring datasets
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
and any possible output set
{\mathcal{S}}
.

(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP measures the bound in max-divergence between the output distributions of two neighboring inputs.

Mironov (2017)
proposes Rényi differential privacy (RDP) and use Rényi divergence to replace max-divergence.
Specifically, a mechanism
{\mathcal{M}}
is
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP if
D_{\alpha}({\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{D})||{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}))\leq\varepsilon
holds for any neighboring datasets
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
where metric
D_{\alpha}
represents the Rényi divergence of order
\alpha>1
.
For iterative algorithms like deep learning training, RDP enables tighter privacy accounting when it is converted back to
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.
Therefore, we adopt RDP for privacy accounting in this work.
For a given
\alpha
, we call
\varepsilon
the
privacy loss
if the algorithm satisfies
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP.

Notation and Threat Model.
We consider training neural networks of general architectures.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
denote the entire training set partitioned into
M
mini-batches
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},...,\mathcal{D}_{M}\}
.
We use
\ell(\cdot)
as the loss function, and
f(\bm{\theta},{\bm{x}})
to represent the output of a neural network parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
given the input
{\bm{x}}
.
For any dataset
\mathcal{D}
, the empirical risk minimization aim to solve the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}R_{\text{erm}}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathcal{D}}\ell(f(\bm{\theta},{\bm{x}}),y)

(1)

When training deep neural networks, we usually only save the final model parameters as checkpoints.
By contrast, the training trajectory, optimization states, and other variables beyond the model parameters are generally not included when models are published.
Consequently, for privacy analysis, we adopt
the hidden state assumption

(
Feldman et al., 2018
;
Altschuler and Talwar, 2022
;
Ye and Shokri, 2022
)
, which restricts the adversary’s view strictly to the final published model parameters.

3.2
Decoupling with Consistency

Our algorithm is inspired by the
sample-and-aggregation framework (S&A)

(
Nissim et al., 2007
)
, which decomposes a dataset into disjoint subsets and releases the aggregated result with a DP guarantee. However, rather than employing S&A for a one-shot model publication as in
Nissim et al. (2007)
, we utilize this framework for the iterative training phase. This adaptation allows us to address a significantly more challenging scenario: enabling deep learning optimization that strictly adheres to the
Hidden State Assumption
.

As demonstrated in Figure
1
, we propose a
decoupling with consistency
strategy: decoupling the privacy-sensitive training process from the final published model, with consistency between the two components enforced through soft constraints.
In addition to
the global publishable model
parameterized by
{\bm{\theta}}
, we maintain
K

auxiliary models
of the same architecture parameterized by
{\mathcal{A}}=\{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, which update their own parameters independently.
Specifically, we partition each mini-batch data into
K
disjoint micro-batches:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{m}=\{{\mathcal{D}}_{i}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K},~~~~m=0,1,...,M

(2)

Each auxiliary model
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
is trained on its micro-batches
\{{\mathcal{D}}_{i}^{k}\}_{i=1}^{M}
, while the publishable model serves as the consensus of the auxiliary models.
This design achieves the best of both worlds: on one hand, the auxiliary models are not published, eliminating the need for privacy-enhancing noise during their optimization and thus boosting their utility; on the other hand, the publishable model is updated based on the parameters of the auxiliary models rather than directly from the training data, bypassing the challenges of intractable non-convex optimization and thereby facilitating privacy accounting under the hidden state assumption.
Formally, our goal is to minimize the aggregate empirical risk across all auxiliary models while enforcing consistency between the auxiliary parameters in
\mathcal{A}
and the global parameters
\bm{\theta}
.
Therefore, training neural networks can be formulated as the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta},\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{MK}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{\text{erm}}(\bm{\theta}_{k};\mathcal{D}_{m}^{k})+r(\bm{\theta})\quad\text{s.t.}~~\bm{\theta}_{k}=\bm{\theta},~\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}.

(3)

Problem (
3
) ensure the consistency between the publishable model and the auxiliary models with hard constraints. Therefore, it is equivalent to empirical risk minimization in (
1
) with a regularization term
r(\bm{\theta})
which is an extended-valued convex regularization function, possibly non-smooth. In practice,
r(\bm{\theta})
is typically chosen as the
\ell_{2}
-norm penalty.

In our decoupling with consistency framework, we replace the hard constraints in (
3
) with soft ones and consider its augmented Lagrangian function as below:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(

\displaystyle\bm{\theta},\mathcal{A},\Pi,\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{MK}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{\text{erm}}(\bm{\theta}_{k};\mathcal{D}_{m}^{k})+r(\bm{\theta})+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\underbrace{\left[\langle\bm{\pi}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}\rangle+\frac{\rho_{k}}{2}\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}\|^{2}\right]}_{h(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\pi}_{k},\rho_{k})}

(4)

where
\Pi=\{\bm{\pi}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is the set of Lagrangian multipliers, and
\mathcal{P}=\{\rho_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is the set of penalty coefficients.
Based on the augmented Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}
in (
4
), the optimization objective of the auxiliary model parameters
\bm{\theta}_{k}
includes not only the empirical risk minimization term, the regularization term but also proximity terms
h(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\pi}_{k},\rho_{k})
, which ensure that
\bm{\theta}_{k}
does not deviate significantly from the global publishable parameters
\bm{\theta}
.
Furthermore, when optimizing the global publishable parameters
\bm{\theta}
with
{\mathcal{A}}
,
\Pi
and
{\mathcal{P}}
fixed, the function
\mathcal{L}
becomes a convex form.
This property facilitates the privacy analysis detailed in the following sections.

3.3
Differentially Private Decoupled Training (DP-DT)

For brevity, we suppress the fixed parameters in the notation for
\mathcal{L}
and

For example,

Top-Level Design.
Among the parameters in
\mathcal{L}
, only the global parameters
\bm{\theta}
are public and accessible to the adversary under the hidden state assumption, so we employ privacy-enhancing mechanisms when updating
\bm{\theta}
.
Considering that
r(\bm{\theta})
may be non-smooth, we utilize noisy proximal gradient descent for updating
\bm{\theta}
.
To ensure differential privacy, it is essential to bound the gradient magnitude of the remaining components apart from
r(\bm{\theta})
w.r.t.
\bm{\theta}
, i.e.,
\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{k})\|_{2}
must be bounded.
In this regard, we adopt two strategies: (1) reset the auxiliary model parameters to the global parameters before updating them, i.e.,
\forall\bm{\theta}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
, we set
\bm{\theta}_{k}\leftarrow\bm{\theta}
; (2) apply clipping to the updates of the auxiliary model parameters and multipliers. Both strategies aim to prevent divergence and control the sensitivity which is related to
\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}\|
.

As demonstrated in Algorithm
1
, each iteration of DP-DT involves several stages: alternative updates on auxiliary model parameters
{\mathcal{A}}
, multipliers
\Pi
, publishable parameters
\bm{\theta}
and penalty coefficients
{\mathcal{P}}
. Same as in Algorithm
1
, we use superscript
\cdot^{(t)}
to indicate the value of a variable in iteration
t
. The following are detailed discussions.

Stage 1: Auxiliary Model and Multiplier Updates.

The parameters and the multiplier of each auxiliary model can be updated in parallel, as the global publishable parameter
\bm{\theta}^{(t)}
remains fixed during this stage, and the data used for each auxiliary model is disjoint.
Without loss of generality, we focus on the
k
-th auxiliary model with parameters
\bm{\theta}_{k}
and the multiplier
\bm{\pi}_{k}
.
As outlined in Algorithm
1
,
\bm{\theta}_{k}
is set to
\bm{\theta}
before its update, i.e.
\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)}=\bm{\theta}^{(t)}
.
Therefore, the local gradient w.r.t.
\bm{\theta}_{k}
is
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}R_{\text{erm}}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)};\mathcal{D}_{m}^{k})+\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t)}
.
Considering the high dimensionality of the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{k}
, we usually employ optimizers with pre-conditioners, like RMSProp or Adam
(
Hinton et al., 2012
;
Kingma, 2015
)
, to update
\bm{\theta}_{k}
.
For the multiplier
\bm{\pi}_{k}
, we first apply
l_{2}
clipping to control its magnitude and then perform dual ascent as in ADMM using the updated parameter
\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)}
.
Therefore, we have the following generalized update scheme:

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)}-\Delta_{k}^{(t)}=\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)}-\eta_{k}^{(t)}{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)})

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{k}^{(t)}+\rho_{k}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=\frac{C^{(t)}\cdot\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t)}}{\max\{C^{(t)},\|\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t)}\|_{2}\}}-\rho_{k}^{(t)}\eta_{k}^{(t)}{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)})

where
C^{(t)}
,
{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}
and
\eta_{k}^{(t)}
are the clipped bound, the inverse preconditioner and the learning rate, respectively.
In addition,
\Delta_{k}^{(t)}
and
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{k}^{(t)}
represent the parameter update and the clipped multiplier, respectively.
The update rule in (
5
) is compatible with preconditioned gradient-based optimizers in deep learning.
The inverse preconditioner
{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}
is a diagonal and positive definite matrix. In Adam and RMSProp, the diagonal of
{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}
is the inverse of the moving average of squared gradients. In optimizers without a preconditioner like pure SGD,
{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}
is the identity matrix.
Without the loss of generality, we assume
0<{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}\leq\tau^{-1}{\mathbf{I}}
where
\tau
indicates the minimum estimated curvature. This is a benign assumption, because in practice, optimizers usually set a minimum value for preconditioners to avoid zero division.

As discussed in the top-level design, it is necessary to bound
\frac{1}{K}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(\bm{\theta}^{(t)},\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)})\|_{2}
to ensure differential privacy for the publishable parameter
\bm{\theta}
.
A sufficient condition for this is that
\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h(\bm{\theta}^{(t)},\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)})\|_{2}\leq C^{(t)}
holds for any
k
where
C^{(t)}
is the clipped bound in the update rule (
5
).
Here, we use
\bm{\theta}^{(t)}
,
\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)}
, because
\bm{\theta}
is updated after
\bm{\theta}_{k}
in Algorithm
1
.
We adjust the learning rate
\eta^{(t)}_{k}
in the update rule (
5
) to ensure the bounded sensitivity.
Specifically, the adaptive learning rate
\eta^{(t)}_{k}
is set as the largest value under a predefined threshold
\eta_{k}
to satisfy
\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h(\bm{\theta}^{(t)},\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)})\|_{2}\leq C^{(t)}
.
we have the following equation.

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h(\bm{\theta}^{(t)},\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\rho_{k}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)}-\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)})-\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t+1)}\|_{2}=\left\|\widehat{\bm{\pi}}^{(t)}-2\rho_{k}^{(t)}\eta_{k}^{(t)}{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)})\right\|_{2}

(6)

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{C^{(t)}\cdot\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t)}}{\max\{C^{(t)},\|\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t)}\|_{2}\}}-2\rho_{k}^{(t)}\eta_{k}^{(t)}{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)})\right\|_{2}

Therefore, the adaptive learning rate
\eta_{k}^{(t)}
is selected by the optimization problem below.

\displaystyle\max_{\eta_{k}^{(t)}\in[0,\eta_{k}]}\eta_{k}^{(t)},~s.t.~\left\|\frac{C^{(t)}\cdot\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t)}}{\max\{C^{(t)},\|\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t)}\|_{2}\}}-2\rho_{k}^{(t)}\eta_{k}^{(t)}{\mathbf{V}}_{k}^{(t)}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)})\right\|_{2}\leq C^{(t)}

(7)

Problem (
7
) involves a quadratic constraint, making it computationally efficient to solve.
Notably, since
\bm{\pi}_{k}^{(t)}
is clipped by its
l_{2}
norm, the feasible set of Problem (
7
) is always non-empty, ensuring that the problem is well-defined.

In summary, when updating the auxiliary model
\bm{\theta}_{k}
and its corresponding multiplier
\bm{\pi}_{k}
, we first solve Problem (
7
) to find the adaptive learning rate and then update
\bm{\theta}_{k}
and
\bm{\pi}_{k}
by rules in (
5
) and the selected optimizer.

Stage 2: Privacy-preserving Publishable Model Update.

The update rule in Stage 1 ensures bounded sensitivity for publishable parameters
\bm{\theta}
, i.e.,
\frac{1}{K}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(\bm{\theta}^{(t)},\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)})\|_{2}<C^{(t)}
.
In Stage 2, we use Gaussian mechanism to enforce differential privacy guarantees on
\bm{\theta}
.
Considering that the regularization term
r(\bm{\theta})
may be non-smooth, we employ noisy proximal gradient descent to update
\bm{\theta}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\text{Prox}_{\widehat{\eta},\text{r}}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(t)}-\widehat{\eta}^{(t)}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})+\mathbf{z}^{(t)}\right)\right)~\text{where}~h(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}h\left(\bm{\theta}^{(t)},\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t+1)}\right)

(8)

where
\mathbf{z}^{(t)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2}\mathbf{I})
denotes the Gaussian noise and
\widehat{\eta}^{(t)}=\frac{K\cdot\eta_{\bm{\theta}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho^{(t)}_{k}}
is the effective step size. Coefficient
\sigma_{\text{noise}}
and
\eta_{\theta}
denote the noise magnitude and the global learning rate.
In addition, the smooth component
h(\bm{\theta})
is a quadratic function whose Hessian can be analytically obtained as
(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{k}^{(t)})\mathbf{I}
.
Leveraging this curvature information, we can accelerate the convergence while maintaining training stability by setting the step size
\widehat{\eta}^{(t)}=\frac{K\cdot\eta_{\bm{\theta}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho^{(t)}_{k}}
.

Stage 3: Penalty Coefficient Update.

We increase the penalty parameter exponentially during training to enforce the consensus constraint gradually:

\rho_{k}^{(t+1)}=\rho_{k}^{(t)}/\gamma_{k},\quad\text{where }0<\gamma_{k}\leq 1,\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}

(9)

In practice, we periodically update the
\rho_{k}
to stabilize the training trajectory.
In our design, as the training progresses, the consistency constraint is gradually enforced, which is crucial to improve the utility of the published model.

4
Convergence Analysis

4.1
Proof Roadmap

We outline the proof strategy before presenting the technical details:

Section
4.2
bounds the mini-batch gradient deviation (Assumption
1
) and the bias from clipping the dual variables (Lemma
2
).

Section
4.3
then studies how each ADMM update affects the augmented Lagrangian (Lemmas
3
–
5
).

Section
4.4
addresses a structural obstacle: dual ascent (Lemma
4
), alongside residuals from mini-batch errors (Lemma
3
) and DP noise (Lemma
5
), prevents the Lagrangian from decreasing monotonically.
To resolve this, the section constructs a Lyapunov potential (Lemma
6
) that absorbs the conflicting terms; Theorem
7
then guarantees almost-sure convergence of the potential and square-summability of the parameter updates.

Section
4.5
addresses a second obstacle: square-summability implies
\|\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\theta}^{(t)}\|\to 0
, but does not rule out the iterates drifting along a flat region without approaching a stationary point.
The section closes this gap via the Attouch–Bolte–Svaiter framework
Attouch et al. (2013)
: Lemma
8
links the subgradient norm to the step size, and the Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz property translates the energy descent into finite path length, from which convergence to a unique limit point follows (Theorem
11
).

4.2
Error Quantification

Before analyzing the global optimization trajectory, we must properly bound the system errors introduced by local mini-batch updates and dual variable clipping. We observe that Algorithm
1
effectively decouples sensitivity control from the noise calibration process. Specifically, inspired by the work of
Zhou et al. (2025)

Assumption 1

(Bounded Gradient Deviation)
.

There exists a constant
\zeta<\infty
such that for all micro-batches
{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{k}
and all auxiliary model parameters
\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)}
encountered during training, the stochastic gradient deviation from the full-batch gradient is uniformly bounded:

\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}R_{\text{erm}}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)};{\mathcal{D}}^{k}_{m})-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}R_{\text{erm}}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(t)};{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{train}})\|\leq\zeta.

This is mild in practice: the loss
R_{\text{erm}}
is continuously differentiable, the ADMM penalty term
(\rho_{k}/2)\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}\|^{2}
and global weight decay
r(\bm{\theta})
jointly prevent parameters from diverging, and common losses (e.g., cross-entropy with softmax) have uniformly bounded gradients for compact input domains.

Moreover, the algorithm bounds the global model’s gradient sensitivity by adaptively controlling the learning rate of the auxiliary model and clipping the dual variables. We subsequently derive an algebraic bound for this clipping bias.

Lemma 2

(Bound for Clipping Bias)

Consider Algorithm

5

4.4

The augmented Lagrangian

Here,

is defined analogously.

To rigorously analyze the non-smooth optimization landscape, we first define the distance metric used for the limiting subdifferential set.
Specifically, we use the standard notation
\text{dist}(\mathbf{0},\partial\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{w}))=\inf_{\mathbf{g}\in\partial\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{w})}\|\mathbf{g}\|

is a limit point.

4.5
Global Convergence via the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz Property

Although Lemma
8
establishes a vital connection between the parameter displacement and the subgradient norm, it does not preclude the possibility of the sequence infinitely oscillating in a high-dimensional space. To mathematically eliminate this behavior, we leverage the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property.

Definition 9

(Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz Property)
.

A proper lower semicontinuous function
\widetilde{\Phi}
satisfies the KL property at a limit point
\mathbf{w}^{*}
if there exists a concave desingularizing function
\varphi\in C^{1}(0,r_{kl})
with
\varphi(0)=0
,
\varphi^{\prime}>0
, such that locally:

\varphi^{\prime}(\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{w})-\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{w}^{*}))\cdot\text{dist}(\mathbf{0},\partial\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{w}))\geq 1

(17)

In our context, since the augmented Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
and the potential function
\widetilde{\Phi}
are constructed via deep neural network components with typical activation functions (e.g., ReLU) and standard loss functions, they belong to the class of semi-algebraic functions. Any semi-algebraic function naturally satisfies the KL property with the desingularizing function taking the form
\varphi(s)=\frac{\bar{c}}{1-c_{kl}}s^{1-c_{kl}}
, where
\bar{c}>0
is a constant and
c_{kl}\in[0,1)
is the KL exponent.

By exploiting the geometric concavity of the KL desingularizing function, we elegantly translate the potential energy descent into a bounded path length.

Lemma 10

(Finite Length Property of the Sequence)
.

Suppose the subgradient is bounded as
\text{dist}\left(\mathbf{0},\partial\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})\right)\leq C_{2}\|\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{(t-1)}\|+\xi^{(t)}+\widetilde{C}
, with the constants satisfying
2C_{1}>C_{2}
. Under the KL-based potential difference bound established in Lemma
19
, the sequence
\{\mathbf{w}^{(t)}\}_{t\geq 0}
has a finite length, i.e.,

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{w}^{(t)}\|<\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

(18)

where
C_{1}=\min\{\min_{k}\frac{A_{k}}{1+2\rho_{k}^{2}},B\}
from Lemma
6
and
C_{2}=b
from Lemma
8
.

The proof of Lemma
10
is deferred to Appendix
B.8
. The condition
2C_{1}>C_{2}
required therein is the most restrictive among all convergence requirements. Together with
A_{k}>0
and
B>0
from Lemma
6

B.9

fundamentally constrains the topological behavior of the sequence, ensuring it forms a Cauchy sequence. Armed with this property and the summable subgradient bounds, we now present the main theorem of our analysis.

5
Privacy Loss Analysis

5.1
Preliminary

As described in Section
3.1
, our threat model is based on the hidden state assumption, where only the final state of the publishable parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
, is accessible to adversaries. In the DP-DT framework introduced in Section
3.3
, we employ the update rule in (
8
) to enhance the differential privacy of
{\bm{\theta}}
.
This update rule can be considered as a diffusion process
(
Balle et al., 2019
;
Chourasia et al., 2021
;
Ye and Shokri, 2022
)
consisting of three key steps: running clipped stochastic gradient descent, adding random calibrated noise, and applying proximal operators.

Differential privacy quantifies the distributional distance between outputs when given two adjacent input datasets.
To analyze its impact, we study how the update rule in (
8
) alters the distribution of the publishable parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
.
Specifically, let
\Theta
denote the distribution of
{\bm{\theta}}
. After applying the one-step update in (
8
), the updated distribution
\Theta^{\prime}
is:

\displaystyle\Theta^{\prime}=T_{\#}(F_{\#}(\Theta)*{\mathcal{N}}(0,2\widehat{\eta}\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}))

(19)

We define
\sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2}=2\widehat{\eta}\sigma^{2}
to align the noise scale with the canonical form of Langevin dynamics implied by (
8
). In addition,
F_{\#}
and
T_{\#}
denote push-forward mappings, and
*
represents the convolution operator between two distributions. These three operators directly correspond to the three steps above:
F_{\#}
applies gradient descent to each sample,
*
adds Gaussian noise, and
T_{\#}
applies the proximal mapping. Specifically,
F
corresponds to the gradient descent update:
F({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{\theta}}-\widehat{\eta}\nabla h({\bm{\theta}})
, while
T
represents the convex bijective proximal operator mapping:
T({\bm{\theta}})=\text{Prox}_{\widehat{\eta},r}({\bm{\theta}})
.

In DP-DT, we iteratively apply (
8
) to update the publishable parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
. Therefore, in privacy accounting, we investigate how the distributional distance between parameter distributions evolves when given two adjacent input datasets and applying (
19
) iteratively. To facilitate analysis, we adopt Rényi differential privacy (RDP), which can be translated to standard differential privacy
(
Mironov, 2017
)
.

5.2
Privacy Accounting

In order to track the trajectory of
\Theta
, we first establish the Lipschitz continuity properties of the operators
F
and
T
.

Lemma 12

(Lipschitz continuity for
F
)
.

The gradient descent update function
F({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{\theta}}-\widehat{\eta}\nabla h({\bm{\theta}})
is Lipschitz continuous with a constant
L_{F}=1-\eta_{{\bm{\theta}}}
, where
h({\bm{\theta}})
is defined in (
8
) and
\widehat{\eta}=\frac{K\cdot\eta_{\bm{\theta}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{k}}
is the effective step size.

Lemma 13

(Lipschitz continuity for
T
)
.

If
\widehat{\eta}>0
and
r({\bm{\theta}})
is a convex function, then the proximal operator function
T({\bm{\theta}})=\text{Prox}_{\widehat{\eta},r}({\bm{\theta}})=\argmin_{\widetilde{{\bm{\theta}}}}\left\{r_{\bm{\theta}}(\widetilde{{\bm{\theta}}})+\frac{1}{2\widehat{\eta}}\|\widetilde{{\bm{\theta}}}-{\bm{\theta}}\|^{2}_{2}\right\}
is Lipschitz continuous with a constant

.

The proof of Lemma
12
and
13
is deferred to Appendix
C.1
and
C.2
.
This work primarily employs
\ell_{2}
regularization (weight decay):
r({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}
. Under this setting, the proximal operator admits the following closed-form solution

\text{Prox}_{\widehat{\eta},r}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{1+\lambda\widehat{\eta}}{\bm{\theta}}
and is

Nevertheless, it is worth noting that Lemma

is general and supports other regularization schemes like

regularization.
In addition, when there is no regularization, we have

and

, which is also 1-Lipschitz.
Now we study the properties of the distribution of the publishable model
{\bm{\theta}}

Definition 14

(Log-Sobolev Inequality)
A distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim\nu}[|g({\bm{\theta}})|^{2}\log(|g({\bm{\theta}})|^{2})]-\frac{2}{c}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim\nu}[|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}g({\bm{\theta}})|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim\nu}[|g({\bm{\theta}})|^{2}]\log\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim\nu}[|g({\bm{\theta}})|^{2}]

Ye and Shokri, 2022
)
for the distribution of parameter.

(

)

Popular neural network initialization schemes

{\bm{\theta}}

7
Discussion and Future Work

The superior performance of DP-DT stems from its distinct threat model. While DP-SGD assumes an adversary who observes every gradient update—thereby accumulating privacy cost via composition theorems and necessitating a conservative privacy budget—DP-DT operates under the Hidden State Assumption. Under this assumption, the adversary only observes the final published model. Consequently, privacy loss is determined solely by the final state rather than the cumulative number of training steps, allowing the privacy cost to remain bounded rather than growing indefinitely. This threat model is well-suited for standard deployment scenarios where only the final model is released. However, it does not cover settings where intermediate states are exposed, such as federated learning with untrusted aggregators or systems that log intermediate checkpoints; in such cases, composition-based accounting remains necessary.

The primary computational overhead of DP-DT arises from training and storing
K
auxiliary models. While this cost is manageable on modern hardware for the moderate-scale architectures evaluated in this work (
K=40
for ResNet18 or ViT-Small), storing
K
full replicas becomes prohibitive for full-parameter fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs). To mitigate this bottleneck in our SST-2 experiments, we leverage LoRA, which restricts trainable parameters to low-rank adapters and substantially reduces the per-model memory footprint. Scaling DP-DT to full-parameter LLM training remains an important direction for our future work.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced
Differentially Private Decoupled Training (DP-DT)
to address the critical utility bottleneck in private deep learning. Departing from standard gradient perturbation paradigms, DP-DT leverages auxiliary models that are continuously synchronized with the global model to perform noise-free representation extraction. This strategy effectively isolates feature learning from privacy noise, ensuring that the final model retains high utility.
Theoretically, We derive a rigorous privacy accountant for DP-DT under the Hidden State Assumption (HSA), theoretically proving that the privacy loss converges to a stable bound. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first extension of HSA’s applicability to deep learning, moving beyond the convex assumptions of prior works.
Empirically, DP-DT establishes a new state-of-the-art on benchmarks such as CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, significantly outperforming existing baselines.

C.3
Change Rate of RDP

At each iterative step, we track the density evolution through the forward update
F
, Gaussian noise addition, and the proximal mapping
T
. To ensure tractable density transformations, we assume the proximal mapping
T
acts as a bijective affine transformation (i.e.,
T(x)=Ax+b
) with a corresponding density transition operator
P
. Note that the input LSI constant
c
at the current step implicitly encapsulates the geometric distortions from all prior iterative steps. We formalize the change rate of RDP in this process as follows:

Theorem 20

(Rate of RDP for Iterative Updates)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be intermediate distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the Log-Sobolev Inequality (LSI) with constant
c
. Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
L_{F}
-Lipschitz, and let
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bijective affine mapping bounded by a Lipschitz constant
L_{T}
. Define
p_{t}(x)
and
p^{\prime}_{t}(x)
as the probability density functions of the intermediate heat flows
F_{\#}(\mu)*\mathcal{N}(0,2t\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d})
and
F_{\#}(\nu)*\mathcal{N}(0,2t\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d})
. Let
H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=P(p_{t})({\bm{\theta}})
and
H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=P(p^{\prime}_{t})({\bm{\theta}})
where
P
be the linear density transition operator associated with the mapping
T
, we have the following bound for the Rényi divergence of order
\alpha
between
H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})
and
H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})
:

\frac{\partial R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}{\partial t}\leq-\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{c_{t}}\cdot\left[R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))+\alpha(\alpha-1)\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))\right]

(49)

where
c_{t}=\frac{1}{L_{T}^{2}}\left(\frac{L_{F}^{2}}{c}+2t\sigma^{2}\right)^{-1}
is the updated LSI constant for the output distributions.

Proof
Since
P
is assumed to be a linear function, we can represent this proximal mapping as an affine transformation
{\bm{\theta}}=Ax+b
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
resides in the original space before the mapping. The post-proximal density
H_{t}
is defined by the operator
P
such that
H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=P(p_{t})({\bm{\theta}})
. Explicitly, the push-forward density can be written as:

H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=p_{t}(A^{-1}({\bm{\theta}}-b))|\det A^{-1}|,\quad H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=p^{\prime}_{t}(A^{-1}({\bm{\theta}}-b))|\det A^{-1}|

Denote
E_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))=\int H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\cdot\frac{H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})^{\alpha}}{H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})^{\alpha}}d{\bm{\theta}}
to be the moment of the likelihood ratio function. By applying the change of variables
x=A^{-1}({\bm{\theta}}-b)
(where
dx=|\det A^{-1}|d{\bm{\theta}}
), we can see that the moment is invariant under this bijective affine mapping:

E_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))=\int p^{\prime}_{t}(x)\cdot\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha}}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)^{\alpha}}dx=E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)\|p^{\prime}_{t}(x))

Therefore, the Rényi divergence can be written as:

R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)\|p^{\prime}_{t}(x))

We compute the rate of Rényi divergence with regard to
t
in the original space
x
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\alpha-1)E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)\|p^{\prime}_{t}(x))}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\int\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha}{}}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)^{\alpha-1}}dx\right)

By exchanging the order of derivative and integration, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\alpha-1)E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)\|p^{\prime}_{t}(x))}\cdot

(50)

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha-1}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha-1}}\cdot\frac{\partial p_{t}(x)}{\partial t}-(\alpha-1)\cdot\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial p^{\prime}_{t}(x)}{\partial t}\right)dx

Since the
\mu*\mathcal{N}(0,2t\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d})
and
\nu*\mathcal{N}(0,2t\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d})
are heat flows at time
t\in\left[0,\sigma^{2}\right]

p_{t}(x)
and
p^{\prime}_{t}(x)
satisfy the following Fokker-Planck equations:

\frac{\partial p_{t}(x)}{\partial t}=\sigma^{2}\Delta_{x}p_{t}(x),\quad\frac{\partial p^{\prime}_{t}(x)}{\partial t}=\sigma^{2}\Delta_{x}p^{\prime}_{t}(x)

Then Equation (
50
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(\alpha-1)E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)\|p^{\prime}_{t}(x))}\cdot

(51)

\displaystyle\int\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha-1}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha-1}}\cdot\Delta_{x}p_{t}(x)-(\alpha-1)\cdot\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha}}\cdot\Delta_{x}p^{\prime}_{t}(x)\right)dx

We apply Green’s first identity to simplify the two terms in the integral. For the first term:

\displaystyle\int\alpha\cdot\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha-1}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha-1}}\cdot\Delta_{x}p_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=-\int\nabla_{x}\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha-1}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha-1}}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}p_{t}(x)dx

(52)

Using the same technique, we can rewrite the second term of Equation (
51
):

\displaystyle\int-(\alpha-1)\cdot\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha}}\cdot\Delta_{x}p^{\prime}_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int(\alpha-1)\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha}}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}p^{\prime}_{t}(x)dx

(53)

Because these expansions rigorously yield the exact same Fisher Information terms as in the
{\bm{\theta}}
space, plug Equation (
52
) and (
53
) into Equation (
51
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}{\partial t}&=\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)\|p^{\prime}_{t}(x))}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\int\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha}}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}p^{\prime}_{t}(x)dx\right.\\
&-\left.\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\int\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha-1}}{p_{t}^{\prime}(x)^{\alpha-1}}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}p_{t}(x)dx\right)\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)\|p^{\prime}_{t}(x))}&\left(\int\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha-1}}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)^{\alpha-1}}\cdot\left\langle\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x)}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)}\right),\nabla_{x}p^{\prime}_{t}(x)\right\rangle dx\right.\\
&-\left.\int\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha-2}}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)^{\alpha-2}}\cdot\left\langle\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x)}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)}\right),\nabla_{x}p_{t}(x)\right\rangle dx\right)\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =-\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)\|p^{\prime}_{t}(x))}\int\frac{p_{t}(x)^{\alpha-2}}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)^{\alpha-2}}\cdot\left\langle\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x)}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)}\right),\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x)}{p^{\prime}_{t}(x)}\right)\right\rangle p^{\prime}_{t}(x)dx\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =-\alpha\cdot\sigma^{2}\cdot\frac{I_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)||p^{\prime}_{t}(x))}{E_{\alpha}(p_{t}(x)||p^{\prime}_{t}(x))}=-\alpha\cdot\sigma^{2}\cdot\frac{I_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})||H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}{E_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})||H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}\end{aligned}

(54)

Based on
Vempala and Wibisono (2019, Lemma 16, Lemma 17)
, we can conclude
H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})
and
H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})
satisfy log-Sobolev inequality with a constant
c_{t}=\left(\frac{L_{F}^{2}}{c}+2t\sigma^{2}\right)^{-1}/L^{2}_{T}
.
In this regard, we can utilize
Ye and Shokri (2022, Lemma D.1)
and
Vempala and Wibisono (2019, Lemma 5)
to bound
\frac{I_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}{E_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}
as follows:

\frac{I_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}{E_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))}\geq\frac{2c_{t}}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot R_{\alpha}\left(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\right)+\frac{2c_{t}}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot\alpha(\alpha-1)\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}R_{\alpha}(H_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\|H^{\prime}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))

(55)

Combine Inequality (
55
) with Equation
54
, we conclude the proof.

Remark
Strictly speaking, because the algorithm terminates after the final proximal mapping
T
, there are no subsequent iterations that depend on its output’s LSI constant. Thus, propagating the
1/L_{T}^{2}
scaling for this terminal step is not rigorously required. However, for theoretical simplicity, we apply a uniform bound that assumes
c_{t}=c^{\prime}/L_{T}^{2}
across all steps. This uniform assumption has a strictly negligible impact on our overall guarantees: the Lipschitz constant
L_{T}
of the proximal mapping depends heavily on the regularization coefficient (e.g., explicitly set to a weight decay of
5\times 10^{-5}
in our experimental setup), making
L_{T}
extremely close to
1
(i.e.,
1/L_{T}^{2}\approx 1
).

Theorem

bounds the rate of Rényi divergence follows
Ye and Shokri (2022)
;
Vempala and Wibisono (2019)
. One assumption in Theorem

is

The function

For the three typical regularization function

In addition, the Lipschitz continuity of function
