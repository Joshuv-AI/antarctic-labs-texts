On the difficulty of learning chaotic dynamics with RNNs
Jonas M. Mikhaeil, Zahra Monfared, Daniel Durstewitz

Abstract
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are wide-spread machine learning tools for modeling sequential and time series data. They are notoriously hard to train because their loss gradients backpropagated in time tend to saturate or diverge during training. This is known as the exploding and vanishing gradient problem. Previous solutions to this issue either built on rather complicated, purpose-engineered architectures with gated memory buffers, or - more recently - imposed constraints that ensure convergence to a fixed point or restrict (the eigenspectrum of) the recurrence matrix. Such constraints, however, convey severe limitations on the expressivity of the RNN. Essential intrinsic dynamics such as multistability or chaos are disabled. This is inherently at disaccord with the chaotic nature of many, if not most, time series encountered in nature and society. It is particularly problematic in scientific applications where one aims to reconstruct the underlying dynamical system. Here we offer a comprehensive theoretical treatment of this problem by relating the loss gradients during RNN training to the Lyapunov spectrum of RNN-generated orbits. We mathematically prove that RNNs producing stable equilibrium or cyclic behavior have bounded gradients, whereas the gradients of RNNs with chaotic dynamics always diverge. Based on these analyses and insights we suggest ways of how to optimize the training process on chaotic data according to the system’s Lyapunov spectrum, regardless of the employed RNN architecture.

3
Theoretical analysis: Relation between RNN dynamics and loss gradients

In our analysis, we will cover all major types of asymptotic dynamics (fixed points, cycles, chaos, and quasi-periodicity), and mathematically investigate their implications for the loss gradients. We will do this for all major classes of RNNs, including standard RNNs with largely arbitrary activation function, LSTMs, GRUs, and PLRNNs.
The next section will first develop and illustrate the basic intuition behind the relations between RNN dynamics and loss gradients.

3.1
Preliminaries: RNN dynamics and loss gradients

Formally, all popular RNN architectures, including LSTMs, GRUs, or PLRNNs, are discrete time DS, defined by a (first-order-Markovian) recursive prescription for the temporal evolution of the latent states
{\bm{z}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
of the general form

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}=F_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{t-1},{\bm{s}}_{t}),\,

(1)

where
{\bm{s}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the input at time
t
and
\bm{\theta}
are RNN parameters. Map
F_{\bm{\theta}}
may be instantiated by
any of the common RNN architectures: For instance, for standard RNNs we have
F_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{t-1},{\bm{s}}_{t})=f\big({\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{B}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{h}}\big)
, where
f
is an element-wise activation function like
\tanh
or a rectified linear unit (ReLU),
{\bm{W}}
a connection matrix, matrix
{\bm{B}}
weighs the inputs, and
{\bm{h}}
is the usual bias term (see sect.
A.1.3
–
A.1.6
for the definition of other RNN models explored here).

Assuming we start at some initial value
{\bm{z}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and given a sequence of external inputs
{\bm{S}}=\{{\bm{s}}_{t}\}
, we can recursively rewrite eq. (
1
) as

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{T}=F_{\bm{\theta}}(F_{\bm{\theta}}(F_{\bm{\theta}}(...F_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{s}}_{2})...)))\,=:\,F_{\bm{\theta}}^{T-1}({\bm{z}}_{1},\{{\bm{s}}_{t}\}).

(2)

In DS theory, we characterize the long-term behavior of such sequences by its spectrum of Lyapunov exponents. The Lyapunov exponents estimate the exponential growth rates in different local directions of the system’s state space, and the largest Lyapunov exponent gives the dominant exponential behavior. Let us denote the system’s Jacobian at time
t
by

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}_{t}\,:=\,\frac{\partial F_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{t-1},{\bm{s}}_{t})}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-1}}=\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-1}}.

(3)

Then, the maximum Lyapunov exponent along an RNN trajectory
\{{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2},\cdots,{\bm{z}}_{T},\cdots\}
is defined as

\displaystyle\lambda_{max}\,:=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\log\left\lVert\prod_{r=0}^{T-2}{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\right\rVert,

(4)

where
{\rVert\cdot\lVert}
denotes the spectral norm (or any subordinate norm) of a matrix. If
\lambda_{max}<0
nearby trajectories will ultimately converge to a fixed point or cycle, while for
\lambda_{max}>0
(a necessary condition for chaos) initially nearby trajectories will exponentially separate, i.e. we will have divergence along one (or more) directions in state space. This accounts for the sensitive dependence on initial conditions in chaotic systems.

Now let
\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}},{\bm{B}},{\bm{h}})
be some loss function employed for RNN training that decomposes in time as
\mathcal{L}\,=\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{t}
.
Suppose we fancy BPTT as our training algorithm (similar derivations could be performed for Real Time Recurrent Learning [RTRL]), we recursively develop the loss gradients w.r.t. some RNN parameter
\theta

in time (i.e., across layers of the RNN unrolled in time) as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}\,=\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial\theta}\quad\textrm{with}\quad\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial\theta}\,=\,\sum_{r=1}^{t}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{r}}\,\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{r}}{\partial\theta},

(5)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{r}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-1}}\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-2}}\,\cdots\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{r+1}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{r}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\prod_{k=0}^{t-r-1}\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-k}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-k-1}}\,=\,\prod_{k=0}^{t-r-1}{\bm{J}}_{t-k},

(6)

where
\partial^{+}
denotes the immediate derivative. Now observe that the behavior of the loss gradients crucially depends on the
product series
of Jacobians in eqn. (
6
): If the maximum absolute eigenvalues of the Jacobians
{\bm{J}}_{t}
will, in the geometric mean, be larger than
1
(i.e.,
\left\lVert\prod_{r=0}^{T-2}{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\right\rVert^{1/T}>1
), gradients will explode as
T\rightarrow\infty
, while they will saturate if
\left\lVert\prod_{r=0}^{T-2}{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\right\rVert^{1/T}<1
. Thus, the key point to note is that the same terms that occur in the definition of the Lyapunov spectrum, eqn. (
4
), resurface in the loss gradients, eqn. (
5
) & (
6
). This accounts for the tight links between system dynamics and gradients.

3.2
Fixed points and cyclic dynamics

Let us start by considering the simplest types of limit dynamics that can occur in RNNs (or any discrete-time DS): fixed points and cycles. In fact, by far most of the literature on global stability in RNNs and on loss gradients focused on just fixed points

is a fixed point of the
k

27

\left\lVert\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{T}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert
will remain bounded for every
{\bm{z}}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma_{k}}
as
T\to\infty
.

Proof.

(i)\,
Assume that
\Gamma_{k}
is a stable
k
-cycle
(k\geq 1)
denoted by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\,=\,\{{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2},\cdots,{\bm{z}}_{T},\cdots\}\,=\,\{{\bm{z}}_{t^{*k}},{\bm{z}}_{t^{*k}-1},\cdots,

\displaystyle\hskip 25.6073pt{\bm{z}}_{t^{*k}-(k-1)},{\bm{z}}_{t^{*k}},{\bm{z}}_{t^{*k}-1},\cdots,{\bm{z}}_{t^{*k}-(k-1)},\cdots\}.

(7)

Then, the largest Lyapunov exponent of
\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\Gamma_{k}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\,\ln\left\lVert J^{*}_{t}\,J^{*}_{t-1}\,\cdots\,J^{*}_{2}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\,=\,\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{jk}\,\ln\left\lVert\bigg(\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}J_{t^{*k}-s}\bigg)^{j}\right\rVert.

By assumption of stability of
\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}J^{*}_{t}\,J^{*}_{t-1}\,\cdots\,J^{*}_{2}\,=\,\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\bigg(\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}J_{t^{*k}-s}\bigg)^{j}\,=\,0.

Now suppose that
\mathcal{O}_{{\bm{z}}_{1}}
is an orbit of the map eqn. (

We will now consider the all-important chaotic case. Let
F
be a recursive map and
\mathcal{O}_{{\bm{z}}_{1}}\,=\,\{{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2},{\bm{z}}_{3},\cdots\}
be an orbit of
F

[

bounded

two nearby orbits separate exponentially fast, but at the same time their mutual separation cannot go to infinity so that there are also folds. The following theorem states the sufficient condition for exploding gradients:

.

Suppose that an RNN
F_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{R}
(parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
) has a chaotic attractor
\Gamma^{*}
with
\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma^{*}}
as its basin of attraction.
Then, for almost every orbit
with
{\bm{z}}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma^{*}}
, (i) the Jacobians connecting temporally distal states
{\bm{z}}_{T}
and
{\bm{z}}_{t}
(
T\gg t
),
\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}
, will exponentially explode for
T\rightarrow\infty
, and (ii) the tangent vector
\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}
and so the gradients of the loss function,
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{T}}{\partial\theta}
, will diverge as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

Let the RNN
F_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{R}
have a chaotic orbit denoted by
\Gamma^{*}=\{{\bm{z}}_{1}^{*},{\bm{z}}_{2}^{*},\cdots,{\bm{z}}^{*}_{T},\cdots\}
.
Then, denoting by
\,J^{*}_{T}\,
the Jacobian of (
1

{\bm{z}}^{*}_{T}\in\Gamma^{*}

\Gamma^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle\lambda\,=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\ln\left\lVert J^{*}_{T}\,J^{*}_{T-1}\,\cdots\,J^{*}_{2}\right\rVert.

(12)

Since
\Gamma^{*}
is chaotic, so

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert J^{*}_{T}\,J^{*}_{T-1}\,\cdots\,J^{*}_{2}\right\rVert\,=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}^{*}_{t}}\right\rVert=\infty,\,\,\,\,T\gg t.

Now, according to Oseledec’s multiplicative ergodic Theorem, almost all the points in the basin of attraction of

3.4
Quasi-periodicity

Quasi-periodicity is a long-term behavior which occurs on a torus and, superficially, bears some similarity to chaos in the sense that, strictly speaking, orbits are also
aperiodic
. That is, as
T\rightarrow\infty
, trajectories will never close up with themselves. Moreover, every trajectory becomes arbitrarily close to any point on the torus, that is, it is dense. One important difference between quasi-periodic and chaotic systems is, however, that in a quasi-periodic system, as time passes, two close initial conditions are
linearly
diverging, while in a chaotic system the divergence is exponential.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that an RNN
F_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{R}
(parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
) has a quasi-periodic attractor
\Gamma
with
\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma}
as its basin of attraction. Then, for every
{\bm{z}}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\forall\,0<\epsilon<1\,\,\,\,\exists\,T_{0}>1\,\,\,s.t.\,\,\,\forall\,T\geq T_{0}\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)^{T-1}\,<\,\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert\,<\,(1+\epsilon)^{T-1}.

(14)

Proof.

See Appx.
A.2.3
.
∎

According to Theorem
3
, for every orbit converging to a quasi-periodic attractor, the Jacobians
\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}
may diverge or vanish as
T\rightarrow\infty
, but this will
not
occur exponentially fast as
T\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, even for bounded non-chaotic RNNs we may sometimes stumble into the problem of diverging gradients. Although this may be a less common scenario, we point out it may occur if we train RNNs on real data from oscillatory systems with incommensurate frequencies, as for instance encountered in electronic engineering.

In Appx.
A.3.2
we have collected further mathematical results on the connection between RNN dynamics and loss gradients that hold regardless of the RNN’s limiting behavior.

4
Empirical evaluation

Our theoretical results imply that chaotic time series pose a principle challenge for RNN training that cannot easily be circumvented through specifically designed architectures, constraints, or regularization criteria. If the underlying DS we aim to capture is chaotic, loss gradients propagated back in time will inevitably explode. Hence we need to curtail gradients in an ideal way. The issue arises especially in scientific ML where time series from chaotic systems are ubiquitous and the aim is to
reconstruct
the generating DS with its limiting behavior. Thus, our exposition will focus on this area.

4.1
Training on systems with exploding gradients by sparse teacher forcing

To illustrate the connections between theory and RNN training, we revive the old idea of TF
[
95
]
as a mechanism for truncating error gradients and keeping model-generated trajectories on track while training.
However, we would like to do this such that important information about the system dynamics does not get lost, for which Lyapunov theory offers some guidance. Specifically, we should not force the system back onto the true trajectory all or most of the time (as in “classical TF”), but should effectively “re-calibrate” it only at certain time points chosen wisely according to the system’s local divergence rates. This procedure will be referred to as
sparsely forced BPTT
in the following.
Assume we want to train an RNN with hidden states
{\bm{z}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and linear (or affine) output layer on a
time-series
\{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},\cdots,{\bm{x}}_{T}\}
generated by a chaotic system.
3
3

3

Note that in DS reconstruction one usually considers the data as observations (
unsupervised
problem).
The linear output layer
\hat{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{B}}{\bm{z}}_{t}
,
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, maps the RNN hidden states into the observation space. This allows us to modify the original TF procedure by constructing a control series
\{\tilde{\bm{z}}_{1},\tilde{\bm{z}}_{2},\cdots,\tilde{\bm{z}}_{T}\}
from the observations by ‘‘inverting’’ the linear output mapping

4
4

4

To ensure invertibility, one could add a regularizer
\lambda\mathbf{I}
to
{\bm{B}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\bm{B}}
in eqn. (
15
), as in ridge regression, but we did not find this necessary in any of our examples.

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{z}}_{t}=({\bm{B}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\bm{B}})^{-1}{\bm{B}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\bm{x}}_{t}.

(15)

The idea is to supply this control signal only sparsely, separated by the learning interval
\tau
between consecutive forcings. Hence, defining
\mathcal{T}=\{n\tau+1\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
as the set of all time points at which we force the RNN onto the ‘true’ values, the RNN updates can be written as

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\begin{cases}RNN(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{t})&\text{if $t\in\mathcal{T}$}\\
RNN({\bm{z}}_{t})&\text{else}\end{cases}.

(16)

This forcing is applied
after
calculation of the loss, such that
\mathcal{L}_{t}=\left\lVert{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}{\bm{z}}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
irrespective of whether
t
is in
\mathcal{T}
or not (and of course it is applied only during training, not at test time!).
Replacing hidden states
{\bm{z}}_{t}
with their teacher-forced signals
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{t}
simply breaks divergence between true and predicted trajectories at time points
t\in\mathcal{T}
, and also cuts off the Jacobians by breaking the temporal contingency (for details see Appx.
A.7
).
The learning interval
\tau
hence controls how many time steps are included in the gradient calculation and has to be chosen with care such as to balance the effects of exploding gradients vs. those of losing relevant time scales and long-term dependencies. While it is general wisdom that an optimal batch size will facilitate training,
5
5

5

It is also reminiscent of truncated BPTT, but with the all-important differences that we suggest 1) a theoretically informed choice of the optimal ‘truncation length’ (forcing interval) and 2) a specific procedure for replacing current latent states by control values. As shown in sect.
4.2
&
4.3
, both these aspects are indeed crucial to avoid diverging gradients and trajectories whilst not loosing relevant longer time scales.
the point here is thus much more specific: Ideally
\tau
should be chosen in accordance with the system’s Lyapunov spectrum, for instance based on the
predictability time

[
7
]

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{pred}}\,=\,\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda_{\max}}.

(17)

Various open-source packages exist for calculating the maximal Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{\max}
from empirical time series data (e.g., Julia: DynamicalSystems.jl
[
12
]
, C++: TISEAN
[
30
]
), potentially after delay-embedding the data (see Appx.
A.4
for more details). Note that this needs to be done on the
empirical
data and only once
before
RNN training, as
\lambda_{\max}
is an invariant characteristic of the empirical system that we are aiming to reconstruct by our RNN (i.e., after successful training the RNN should have the same invariant properties as the underlying DS).
We also emphasize that such a simple recipe for addressing the exploding gradient problem is based on modifying the training routine, and is thus in principle applicable to any model architecture.

5
Discussion and conclusions

In this paper we proved that RNN dynamics and loss gradients are intimately related for all major types of RNNs and activation functions. If the RNN is “well behaved” in the sense that its dynamics converges to a fixed point or cycle, loss gradients will remain bounded, and established remedies

[
35
,
80
]
can be used to refrain them from vanishing. However, if the dynamics are chaotic, gradients will always explode. This constitutes a
principle
problem in RNN training that cannot easily be mastered through architectural design or gradient clipping. This is because to avoid exploding gradients while training on time series from chaotic systems, one either needs to constrain the RNN so much that chaotic behavior is completely disabled to begin with (i.e., ultimately by forcing all Lyapunov exponents to be smaller or equal to zero), implying a very poor fit to such data. Or one needs to be a bit more lenient and thereby allow for the possibility of exploding gradients (as LSTMs or PLRNNs in fact do). This problem is furthermore practically highly relevant, as most time series we encounter in nature, and many from man-made systems as well, are inherently chaotic.

While we do not offer a full solution to this problem here, we suggest it might be tackled in training by taking a system’s local divergence rates as measured through the Lyapunov spectrum into account. Hence, rather than conquering the EVGP by structural design or specific constraints or regularization terms, we recommend to put the focus more on the training process itself.
We illustrated this point empirically using
sparsely forced BPTT
, a training technique that pulls trajectories back on track at times determined by the maximal Lyapunov exponent. Doing so leads to optimal reconstruction results for a variety of simulated and real-world benchmarks, regardless of the specific RNN architecture employed in training.

As noted in sect.
4.1
, fairly standard packages are available for computing maximal Lyapunov exponents from data. Some background knowledge, as provided in classical textbooks (e.g. Ch. 5 in
[
42
]
), may be required for properly reading the output from these packages: Essentially, one would be looking be for a linear scaling region as in Figs.
4
a &
12
a, ignoring both the initial noise transient as well as the plateau caused by reaching the full attractor extent. If unsure about the exact value, a moderate amount of jittering around the estimated mean value may help (see Appx. Fig.
15
). A further interesting direction for improvement might be to regulate the forcing interval through an annealing procedure
[
1
,
2
]
, for instance starting at
\tau=1
and ramping up to
\tau=\tau_{\text{pred}}
throughout training, similar as in adaptive schemes
[
5
]
.
6
6

6

We thank one of the referees for pointing this out.

The idea here would be to first get the short-term behavior right, and then challenge the system more and more for longer time spans until the predictability time is reached.

We stress that our goal here above all was to provide a mathematically grounded perspective on the problem, with the empirical section focused on elucidating the practical implications of the theoretical results.
We believe that a more thorough theoretical understanding is important and needed for guiding future research into more powerful training procedures that avoid exploding gradients
without
compromising expressiveness. In our application examples, we developed the case from the perspective of scientific machine learning, which by now is a broad area in its own right with huge societal relevance (e.g., climate or epidemiological time series), and where the reconstruction of geometrical or topological (invariant) properties is important, beyond mere prediction. Nevertheless, we believe that our theoretical results will also have implications for other domains, like NLP
[
36
]
. While scientific time series problems traditionally have been extensively considered from a DS perspective (e.g.,
[
42
]
), much more groundwork is needed, however, in areas like NLP, where, for instance, it may not even be immediately clear how to best define a Lyapunov spectrum.

A.1.2
RNN derivatives

Considering the loss function
\mathcal{L}\,=\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{t}\,
of an RNN
F_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{R}
parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}\,=\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial\theta},

(20)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial\theta}\,=\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial\theta}.

(21)

The tangent vector
\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}
has the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}\,=\,\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}\,+\,\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\bigg(\prod_{r=0}^{t-1}{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\bigg)\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{T-t}}{\partial\theta},

(22)

where
\partial^{+}
denotes the immediate partial derivative. Since for an RNN
F_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{R}
the activation function is element-wise, with
\theta
the
m
-th element of a parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
(or belonging to the
m
-th row of a parameter matrix
\bm{\theta}
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&\frac{\partial^{+}z_{m,T}}{\partial\theta}&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}.

(23)

For instance, let
\bm{\theta}\,=\,{\bm{W}}
be a weight matrix, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{11}}&\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{12}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{1M}}\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{21}}&\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{22}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{2M}}\\[4.30554pt]
\vdots\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{M1}}&\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{M2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{MM}}\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

In this case, for the standard RNN we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial w_{mk}}\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&z_{k,T-1}\,\xi_{mk}({\bm{z}}_{T-1})&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\,=\,\mathbf{1}_{(m,k)}\,\xi_{mk}({\bm{z}}_{T-1})\,{\bm{z}}_{T-1},

(25)

where
\,\xi_{mk}({\bm{z}}_{T-1})\,=\,f_{w_{m,k}}^{\prime}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{mj}\,z_{j,T-1}\,+\,\sum_{j=1}^{M}b_{mj}\,s_{j,T}+h_{m}\big)\,
, and
f_{w_{m,k}}^{\prime}
stands for the derivative of
\,f\,
with respect to
w_{m,k}
.

Therefore, for standard RNNs, (
22
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial w_{mk}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\mathbf{1}_{(m,k)}\,\xi_{mk}({\bm{z}}_{T-1})\,{\bm{z}}_{T-1}\,+\,\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\bigg(\prod_{r=0}^{t-1}{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\bigg)\mathbf{1}_{(m,k)}\,\xi_{mk}({\bm{z}}_{T-t-1})\,{\bm{z}}_{T-t-1}.

(26)

A.1.3
Piecewise-linear RNN (PLRNN)

The PLRNN has the generic form
[

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}\,=\,F({\bm{z}}_{t-1})\,=\,{\bm{A}}\,{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{W}}\phi({\bm{z}}_{t-1})\,+\,{\bm{C}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{h}}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},

where

is the element-wise rectified linear unit (ReLU) function,
{\bm{z}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the neural state vector,
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}

{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
is a matrix of connection weights,
{\bm{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
is the bias vector,

{\bm{C}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times K}
, and

\bm{\Sigma}
.

Equation (

) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}\,=\,({\bm{A}}+{\bm{W}}{\bm{D}}_{\Omega(t-1)}){\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{C}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{h}}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\,=:\,{\bm{W}}_{\Omega(t-1)}\,{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{C}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{h}}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},

where
{\bm{D}}_{\Omega(t)}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}({\bm{d}}_{\Omega(t)})

, and zeros otherwise.

For the PLRNN (

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}_{t}\,=\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-1}}\,=\,{\bm{W}}_{\Omega(t-1)},

(29)

and
\left\lVert{\bm{W}}_{\Omega(t-1)}\right\rVert\,\leq\,\left\lVert{\bm{A}}\right\rVert\,+\,\left\lVert{\bm{W}}\right\rVert
.

Furthermore, the derivatives (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial w_{mk}}\,=\,\mathbf{1}_{(m,k)}{\bm{D}}_{\Omega(T-1)}\,{\bm{z}}_{T-1}+\sum_{j=2}^{T-1}\,\,\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}{\bm{W}}_{\Omega(T-i)}\bigg)\,\mathbf{1}_{(m,k)}{\bm{D}}_{\Omega(T-j)}\,{\bm{z}}_{T-j}.

A.1.4
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)

The LSTM is defined by the equations

\displaystyle{\bm{i}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,\sigma\big({\bm{W}}_{ii}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{W}}_{hi}{\bm{h}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{b}}_{i}\big)

\displaystyle{\bm{f}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,\sigma\big({\bm{W}}_{if}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{W}}_{hf}{\bm{h}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{b}}_{f}\big)

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,\tanh{\big({\bm{W}}_{ig}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{W}}_{hg}{\bm{h}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{b}}_{g}\big)}

\displaystyle{\bm{o}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,\sigma\big({\bm{W}}_{io}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{W}}_{ho}{\bm{h}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{b}}_{o}\big)

\displaystyle{\bm{c}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,{\bm{f}}_{t}\,\odot\,{\bm{c}}_{t-1}+{\bm{i}}_{t}\,\odot\,{\bm{g}}_{t}

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,{\bm{o}}_{t}\,\odot\,\tanh{({\bm{c}}_{t})}

(31)

where
\{{\bm{s}}_{t}\}
is the input sequence,
{\bm{W}}
denotes weight matrices,
{\bm{b}}

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}\,=\,F_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{t-1})\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{o}}_{t}\,\odot\,\tanh{({\bm{f}}_{t}\,\odot\,{\bm{c}}_{t-1}+{\bm{i}}_{t}\,\odot\,{\bm{g}}_{t})}\\[4.30554pt]
{\bm{f}}_{t}\,\odot\,{\bm{c}}_{t-1}+{\bm{i}}_{t}\,\odot\,{\bm{g}}_{t}\end{pmatrix}.

(32)

The term

in (

can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial\theta}\,=\,\sum_{r=1}^{t}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}\,\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{r}}\,\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{r}}{\partial\theta}.

(33)

A necessary condition for LSTMs to have a chaotic orbit is given by:

Proposition 1
.

Let the LSTM given by (
31
) have a chaotic attractor
\Gamma^{*}
with
\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma^{*}}
as its basin of attraction. Then for every
{\bm{z}}_{1}\,=\,({\bm{h}}_{1},{\bm{c}}_{1})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle\gamma\,:=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[T]{\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{1}}\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert}\,>\,1.

(34)

Proof.

The Jacobian matrix of (
32
) for
t>1
can be written in the block form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t-1}}\,=\,J_{t}\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}\end{pmatrix}.

(35)

Further, due to the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle J_{t}\,J_{t-1}

\displaystyle\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-2}}\,+\,\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-2}}&&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-2}}\,+\,\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-2}}\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-2}}\,+\,\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-2}}&&\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-2}}\,+\,\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-1}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-2}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-2}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-2}}\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t-2}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t-2}}\end{pmatrix},

(36)

and by induction we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\,=\,J_{t}\,J_{t-1}\,J_{t-2}\cdots J_{2}\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{1}}\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{c}}_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.

(37)

Now assume that (
32
) has a chaotic orbit given by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{*}\,=\,\{{\bm{z}}_{1}^{*},{\bm{z}}_{2}^{*},\cdots,{\bm{z}}^{*}_{T},\cdots\}.

(38)

According to (
37
), the largest Lyapunov exponent of
\Gamma^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{\Gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\ln\left\lVert J^{*}_{T}\,J^{*}_{T-1}\,\cdots\,J^{*}_{2}\right\rVert\,=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\ln\,\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}^{*}_{1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{c}}^{*}_{1}}\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}^{*}_{1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{c}}^{*}_{1}},\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert.

Since
\Gamma^{*}
is chaotic, so
\lambda_{\Gamma^{*}}>0
, which gives

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[T]{\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}^{*}_{1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{c}}^{*}_{1}}\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}^{*}_{1}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}^{*}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{c}}^{*}_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert}\,>\,1.

(39)

Based on Oseledec’s multiplicative ergodic Theorem, (
39
) holds for every
{\bm{z}}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma^{*}}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

A.1.5
Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)

A GRU network is defined by the equations

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,\sigma\big({\bm{W}}_{z}\,{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{U}}_{z}{\bm{h}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{b}}_{z}\big)

\displaystyle{\bm{r}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,\sigma\big({\bm{W}}_{r}\,{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{U}}_{r}{\bm{h}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{b}}_{r}\big)

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}_{t}

\displaystyle\,=\,(1-{\bm{z}}_{t})\,\odot\,\tanh{\big({\bm{W}}_{h}\,{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{U}}_{h}({\bm{r}}_{t}\,\odot\,{\bm{h}}_{t-1})\,+\,{\bm{b}}_{h}\big)\,+\,{\bm{z}}_{t}\,\odot\,{\bm{h}}_{t-1}},

(40)

where
{\bm{r}}_{t}
represents the reset gate,
{\bm{z}}_{t}
the update gate,
{\bm{s}}_{t}
and
{\bm{h}}_{t}
denote the inputs and the hidden state respectively,
{\bm{W}}_{z},{\bm{W}}_{r},{\bm{W}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
and
{\bm{U}}_{z},{\bm{U}}_{r},{\bm{U}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
are weight matrices,
{\bm{b}}_{z},{\bm{b}}_{r},{\bm{b}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
are bias vectors, and
\sigma
is the element-wise logistic sigmoid function (for more details about GRUs see
[
10
]
).

A.1.6
Unitary evolution RNN (uRNN)

The uRNN, proposed in
[
4
]
, is defined as the nonlinear DS

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}\,=\,\sigma_{{\bm{b}}}\big({\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{V}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\big),

(41)

for which
{\bm{W}}\in U(M)
is an unitary matrix,
{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
,
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the bias parameter,
{\bm{s}}_{t}
is the real- or complex-valued input of dimension
N
, and

\displaystyle[\sigma_{{\bm{b}}}({\bm{z}})]_{i}\,=\,[\sigma_{\text{modReLU}}({\bm{z}})]_{i}\,=\,\begin{cases}\big(|z_{i}|+b_{i}\big)\frac{z_{i}}{|z_{i}|}\hskip 19.91684pt\text{if}\,\,|z_{i}|+b_{i}\,\geq\,0\\[4.30554pt]
\,\,0\hskip 68.28644pt\text{if}\,\,|z_{i}|+b_{i}\,<\,0\end{cases}.

(42)

Proposition 2
.

The uRNN given by (
41
)
cannot have any chaotic orbit.

Proof.

For any arbitrary orbit
\mathcal{O}_{{\bm{z}}_{1}}
of (
41
) we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert J_{T}\,J_{T-1}\,\cdots\,J_{2}\right\rVert\,=\ \left\lVert\prod_{k=0}^{T-2}{\bm{D}}_{T-k}\,{\bm{W}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert,

(43)

where
{\bm{D}}_{t}\,=\,diag\Big(\sigma^{\prime}_{{\bm{b}}}\big({\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{V}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\big)\Big)
. Since
{\bm{W}}
is unitary and so a norm preserving matrix, it is concluded that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\prod_{k=0}^{T-2}{\bm{D}}_{T-k}\,{\bm{W}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert\,\leq\,\prod_{k=0}^{T-2}\left\lVert{\bm{D}}_{T-k}\,{\bm{W}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert\,=\,\prod_{k=0}^{T-2}\left\lVert{\bm{D}}_{T-k}\right\rVert\,=\,1,

(44)

which implies

\displaystyle\lambda_{max}\,=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\ln\left\lVert J_{T}\,J_{T-1}\,\cdots\,J_{2}\right\rVert\,\leq\,0.

(45)

This rules out the existence of chaos (since
\lambda_{max}\,>\,0
is a necessary condition for
\mathcal{O}_{{\bm{z}}_{1}}
to be chaotic).
∎

Note that, more generally, any RNN which is constrained such as to exhibit global convergence to a fixed point or cycle, by definition must have a maximum Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{max}\leq 0
(in accordance with Theorem
1
), hence cannot exhibit chaotic behavior by definition.

(65)

Consequently, for

\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{kj-1}{\bm{W}}_{\Omega(t_{n+kj-1-i})}

\displaystyle\,=\,\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\bm{W}}_{\Omega(t_{k(\tilde{n}+j)+k-i})}\Big)\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\bm{W}}_{\Omega(t_{k(\tilde{n}+j-1)+k-i})}\Big)\cdots\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\bm{W}}_{\Omega(t_{k(\tilde{n})+k-i})}\Big)

A.2.3
Proof of theorem
3

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\,=\,\{{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2},\ldots{\bm{z}}_{T},\cdots\}
be a quasi-periodic attractor. Then, the largest Lyapunov exponent of
\Gamma
is

\displaystyle\lambda\,=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\ln\left\lVert J_{T}\,J_{T-1}\,\cdots\,J_{2}\right\rVert\,=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\ln\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert\,=\,0.

(72)

We prove for every
0<\epsilon<1

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1-\epsilon)^{T-1}\,<\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert\,<\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1+\epsilon)^{T-1}.

(73)

For this purpose, we show
\forall\,0<\epsilon<1

(I)

\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1-\epsilon)^{T-1}\,<\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert
, and

(II)

\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert\,<\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1+\epsilon)^{T-1}
.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that (I) does not hold. Then there exists some
0<\epsilon<1
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1-\epsilon)^{T-1}\,\geq\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert.

(74)

Therefore

\displaystyle\exists\,T_{0}>1\,\,\,s.t.\,\,\,\forall\,T\geq T_{0}\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,(1-\epsilon)^{T-1}\,\geq\,\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert,

(75)

and so

\displaystyle\exists\,T_{0}>1\,\,\,s.t.\,\,\,\forall\,T\geq T_{0}\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,\frac{\ln(1-\epsilon)^{T-1}}{T-1}\,\geq\,\frac{\ln\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert}{T-1}.

(76)

Consequently, due to (
72
), for
T\rightarrow\infty
we have
\,\ln(1-\epsilon)\,\geq\,0
. This implies
\epsilon\leq 0
, which is a contradiction.

Similarly if we assume (II) is not true, then there exists some
0<\epsilon<1
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert\,\geq\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1+\epsilon)^{T-1}.

(77)

Thereby

\displaystyle\exists\,T_{0}>1\,\,\,s.t.\,\,\,\forall\,T\geq T_{0}\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert\,\geq\,(1+\epsilon)^{T-1},

(78)

and thus

\displaystyle\exists\,T_{0}>1\,\,\,s.t.\,\,\,\forall\,T\geq T_{0}\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,\frac{\ln\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert}{T-1}\,\geq\,\frac{\ln(1+\epsilon)^{T-1}}{T-1}.

(79)

This means
\,\ln(1+\epsilon)\,\leq\,0
as
T\rightarrow\infty
, i.e.
\epsilon\leq 0
, which is a contradiction.

Therefore (
14
) holds for
\Gamma
and also, according to Oseledec’s multiplicative ergodic Theorem, for every
{\bm{z}}_{1}
in the basin of attraction of
\Gamma
.
∎

A.3
Additional results on relation between dynamics and gradients

A.3.1
Further results and remarks related to Theorem
2

Remark 4
.

The result of Theorem
2
also holds for unstable orbits
\,\{{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2},{\bm{z}}_{3},\cdots\}\,
with positive largest Lyapunov exponent. Trivially, for such orbits that diverge to infinity (unbounded latent states) gradients of the loss function will explode as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

Remark 5
.

For RNNs with ReLU activation functions
there are finite compartments in the phase space each with a different functional form. In such a case, to define the largest Lyapunov exponent of
\,\Gamma^{*}
, in the proof of Theorem
2
we assume that
\,\Gamma^{*}
never maps to the points of the borders.

Based on Theorem
2
, we can also formulate the necessary conditions for chaos and diverging gradients in standard RNNs with particular activation functions by considering the norms of their recurrence matrix, for which the following Corollary provides the basis:

Corollary 1
.

Let for a standard RNN

\displaystyle\,\left\lVert diag\big(f^{\prime}\big({\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{B}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{h}}\big)\big)\right\rVert\,\leq\,\gamma\,<\,\infty.

(80)

If the RNN is chaotic, then
\,\left\lVert{\bm{W}}\right\rVert\,\gamma\,>\,1\,
.

Proof.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\,\left\lVert{\bm{W}}\right\rVert\,\gamma\,\leq\,1\,
. From

\displaystyle\left\lVert\prod_{2<t\leq T}{\bm{W}}diag\big(f^{\prime}\big({\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{B}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{h}}\big)\big)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\prod_{2<t\leq T}\left\lVert{\bm{W}}diag\big(f^{\prime}\big({\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{B}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{h}}\big)\big)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\,\leq\,(\left\lVert{\bm{W}}\right\rVert\,\gamma)^{T-2},

(81)

it is concluded that

\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\prod_{2<t\leq T}{\bm{W}}diag\big(f^{\prime}\big({\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\,+\,{\bm{B}}{\bm{s}}_{t}\,+\,{\bm{h}}\big)\big)\right\rVert\,<\,\infty\,
, which contradicts (
13
). This means
\,\left\lVert{\bm{W}}\right\rVert\,\gamma\,>\,1\,
is a necessary condition for the standard RNN to be chaotic.
∎

Remark 6
.

For RNN with the tanh and
sigmoid
activation functions
\gamma=1
and
\gamma=\frac{1}{4}
, respectively. Thus, by Corollary
1
, the necessary conditions for chaos in these two cases are
\,\left\lVert{\bm{W}}\right\rVert\,>\,1\,
and
\,\left\lVert{\bm{W}}\right\rVert\,>\,4\,
, respectively.

A.3.2
Other connections between dynamics and gradients

There is a direct link between the norms of the Jacobians of the RNN along trajectories and the EVGP. By observing this link, we can formulate some general conditions that will have implications for the behavior of the gradients regardless of the limiting behavior of the RNN, as collected in the following theorem:

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{{\bm{z}}_{1}}\,=\,\{{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2},\ldots{\bm{z}}_{T},\cdots\}
be a sequence (orbit) generated by
an RNN
F_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{R}
parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
, and
{\bm{P}}_{T}\,:=\,{\bm{J}}_{T}-{\bm{I}},\,\,T=2,3,\cdots
.

(i)

Assume that
\mathcal{O}_{{\bm{z}}_{1}}
is an orbit for which
\,\left\lVert\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}\right\rVert\,\leq\,\xi\,\,\,\forall t
.
If
\,\sum_{T=2}^{\infty}\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\,<\,\infty
, then the Jacobian
\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}
, the tangent vector
\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}
and thus the gradient of the loss function,
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{T}}{\partial\theta}
, will be bounded for
T\rightarrow\infty
.

(ii)

If
\,\sum_{T=2}^{\infty}\left\lVert{\bm{P}}_{T}\right\rVert\,<\,\infty
, then the Jacobian
\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}
will neither vanish nor explode as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

(iii)

Let
\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\,\neq\,0,\,\,T\geq 2
, and
\,\sum_{T=2}^{\infty}\ln{\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert}
diverge to
\,-\infty
, then the Jacobian
\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}
vanishes as
T
tends to infinity.

Part
(i)
of Theorem
4
relaxes some of the conditions required in
Theorem
1
for bounded gradients by imposing a Lipschitz condition on the immediate derivatives. Part
(ii)
generalizes conditions satisfied, for instance, in orthogonal (unitary) RNNs
[
4
,
32
]
or fully regularized PLRNNs
[
80
]
.

Proof.

Let
\left\lVert.\right\rVert
be any matrix norm satisfying
\left\lVert{\bm{A}}_{1}{\bm{A}}_{2}\right\rVert\,\leq\,\left\lVert{\bm{A}}_{1}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{A}}_{2}\right\rVert
.

(i)\,
By boundedness of

\,\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\,=\,\left\lVert\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}\,+\,\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\bigg(\prod_{r=0}^{t-1}{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\bigg)\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{T-t}}{\partial\theta}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\xi\bigg(1\,+\,\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\left\lVert\prod_{r=0}^{t-1}{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\right\rVert\bigg)\,\leq\,\xi\bigg(1\,+\,\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\,\prod_{r=0}^{t-1}\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\right\rVert\bigg).

(82)

Moreover,

\displaystyle 1\,+\,\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\,\prod_{r=0}^{t-1}\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T-r}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\,\leq\,1\,+\,\sum_{p}\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{p}\right\rVert\,+\,\sum_{p<q}\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{p}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{q}\right\rVert\,+\,\sum_{p<q<r}\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{p}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{q}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{r}\right\rVert\,+\,\cdots

\displaystyle\,=\,\big(1+\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\big)\big(1+\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T-1}\right\rVert\big)\cdots\big(1+\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{2}\right\rVert\big)\,=:\,\prod_{t=2}^{T}\big(1+\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{t}\right\rVert\big).

(83)

Since
\,\sum_{T=2}^{\infty}\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert
converges, according to
[
94
]
, the infinite products
\prod_{T=2}^{\infty}\big(1+\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\big)
in (
83
) converge to a finite number
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\neq 0
. Consequently, by (
82
) and (
83
)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial\theta}\right\rVert\,\leq\,\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\,<\,\infty,

(84)

which implies
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{T}}{\partial\theta}
will be bounded for
T\rightarrow\infty
.

Furthermore

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert\,\leq\,\prod_{T=2}^{\infty}\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\,:=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\bigg(\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\,\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T-1}\right\rVert\,\cdots\,\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{2}\right\rVert\bigg)\,\leq\,\prod_{T=2}^{\infty}\big(1+\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\big)\,\leq\,\tilde{\mathcal{K}},

(85)

which completes the proof.

(ii)
Since
\,\sum_{T=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert{\bm{P}}_{T}\right\rVert\,<\,\infty
, due to
[
94
]
the infinite product

\displaystyle\prod_{T=2}^{\infty}\big({\bm{I}}+{\bm{P}}_{T}\big)\,=\,\prod_{T=2}^{\infty}{\bm{J}}_{T}\,:=\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}{\bm{J}}_{T}\,{\bm{J}}_{T-1}\,\cdots\,{\bm{J}}_{2},

(86)

converges to a matrix
{\bm{K}}\neq{\bm{O}}
, which implies

\displaystyle 0\,<\,\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert{\bm{K}}\right\rVert\,<\,\infty.

(87)

(iii)
For
\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\,\neq\,0,\,\,T\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle 0\,\leq\,\left\lVert\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\right\rVert\,\leq\,\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert\,\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T-1}\right\rVert\,\cdots\,\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{2}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=e^{\ln{\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert}}e^{\ln{\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T-1}\right\rVert}}\cdots e^{\ln{\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{2}\right\rVert}}=e^{\sum_{t=2}^{T}\ln{\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{t}\right\rVert}}.

(88)

Hence if
\,\sum_{T=2}^{\infty}\ln{\left\lVert{\bm{J}}_{T}\right\rVert}\rightarrow-\infty
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{1}}\,=\,{\bm{O}}.

(89)

∎

A.4
Empirical evaluation: Datasets

Lorenz attractor

The Lorenz system
[
56
]
is a simplified model for atmospheric convection, given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{~d}t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(y-x),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{~d}t}

\displaystyle=x(\rho-z)-y,

(90)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{~d}t}

\displaystyle=xy-\beta z.

The system is of particular interest for its chaotic regime and was studied here for
\sigma=16
,
\rho=45.92
and
\beta=4
. For these parameters the Lorenz system is known to have a maximal Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{\max}=1.5

[
72
]
. To generate a time series, the ODEs were integrated with a step size
\Delta t=0.01
using
scipy.integrate
.
Accordingly, the prediction time is
\tau_{pred}=\frac{\ln(2)}{\Delta t\ \lambda_{max}}=46.2
.

Duffing oscillator

The Duffing oscillator
[
15
]
is an example of a periodically forced oscillator with nonlinear elasticity

\displaystyle\ddot{x}+\delta\dot{x}+\beta x+\alpha x^{3}=\gamma\cos(\omega t).

(91)

Note that this system is non-autonomous, that is externally forced due to the r.h.s. of eqn.
91
. The following parameters were chosen to arrive at a chaotically forced oscillator:
\alpha=1.0
,
\beta=-1.0
,
\delta=0.1
,
\gamma=0.35
, and
\omega=1.4
. For these parameters the Duffing oscillator has a maximum Lyapunov exponent of
\lambda_{max}=0.0995
. The dataset used here was created with the code from
[
24
]
as a three dimensional embedding with step size
\Delta t=0.17
. The prediction time is
\tau_{pred}=39.28
.

Rössler system

Another prime textbook example for a chaotic system is the Rössler system
[
76
]
given by:

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}

\displaystyle=-y-z,

\displaystyle\frac{dy}{dt}

\displaystyle=x+ay,

(92)

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{dt}

\displaystyle=b+z(x-c).

For the parameters
a=0.15
,
b=0.2
and
c=10
, the maximal Lyapunov exponent is
\lambda_{\max}=0.09

[
72
]
. To arrive at a time series, a step size of
\Delta t=0.1
was chosen for integration. This gives us a prediction time of
\tau_{pred}=77.0
for this system.

Mackey-Glass equation

The Mackey-Glass equation
[
25
]
is a nonlinear time delay differential equation

\displaystyle\dot{x}=\beta\frac{x_{\rho}}{1+x_{\rho}^{n}}-\gamma x\,\,\,\,\text{ with }\beta,\gamma,\rho>0.

(93)

Here
x_{\rho}
represents the value of the variable
x
at time
t-\rho
(note that strictly, mathematically, this makes the system infinite-dimensional). Choosing the parameters to be
\beta=2
,
\gamma=1.0
,
n=9.65
, and
\rho=2.0
, leads to chaotic behavior with a maximum Lyapunov exponent of
\lambda_{max}=0.21
. The dataset was created as a 10-dimensional embedding with the code from
[
24
]
using
\Delta t=0.04
. This yields a prediction time of
\tau_{pred}=82.2
.

with

A.5
Empirical evaluation: measures of reconstruction quality

Attractor overlap

To asses the geometrical similarity of the chaotic attractor produced by the RNN to the one underlying the observations, we calculate the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the ground truth distribution
p_{\text{true}}({\bm{x}})
and the distribution
p_{\text{gen}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})
generated by RNN simulation. To do so in practice, we employ a binning approximation (see
[
50
]
)

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{stsp}}\left(p_{\mathrm{true}}(\mathbf{x}),p_{\mathrm{gen}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\right)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{p}_{\mathrm{true}}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\log\left(\frac{\hat{p}_{\mathrm{true}}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})}{\hat{p}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})}\right),

where
K
is the total number of bins, and
\hat{p}_{\text{true }}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})
and
\hat{p}_{\text{gen }}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})
are estimates obtained as relative frequencies through sampling trajectories from the observed time-series and the trained RNN, respectively.

Hellinger distance between power spectra

Since in DS reconstruction we mainly aim to capture invariant and time-independent properties of the underlying system, besides the geometrical agreement, we compare the similarity in true and RNN-reconstructed power spectra. To do so, we generate a time series of length
100,000
from the RNN and calculate its dimension-wise power spectra
S(\omega)

using the fast Fourier transform (
scipy.fft
).
By standardizing all trajectories prior to Fourier transforming them, we have
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(\omega)=1
due to the Plancherel theorem. This allows us to compare two power spectra,
S(\omega)
and
P(\omega)
, with the Hellinger distance

\displaystyle H(S(\omega),P(\omega))=\sqrt{1-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{S(\omega)P(\omega)}\ d\omega}\ \ \in\ [0,1].

(94)

To reduce the influence of noise we apply Gaussian kernel smoothing. The Hellinger distances between observed and generated spectra for all dimensions are then averaged to give the reported overall distance
D_{H}
.

A.6.4
Other initialization procedures:
Truncated BPTT with zero resetting or forward-iterated states

A common procedure in training RNNs is partitioning the time series into chunks of length
\tau
(as we did based on the Lyapunov spectrum), but then simply resetting the hidden states
{\bm{z}}_{1(k)}
at the beginning of each chunk (window)
k
to
\mathbf{0}
, or forward-iterating them from the previous chunk
k-1
, i.e.
{\bm{z}}_{1(k)}=F_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{\tau(k-1)})
. Formally this would mean that we do not force the trajectory back on track as in our approach, but instead may either kick it off track (zero resetting) or just let it freely evolve whilst still truncating the gradients (forward-iterating). To illustrate this, here we trained an LSTM on chunks (windows) with a length given by the optimal
\tau
(
\tau_{opt}=30
for the Lorenz system), but then initialized the hidden states to either
0
or to the forward-iterated last state at the beginning of each window. The performance obtained with zero-resetting is indicated by the dashed line in Fig.
10
a below, while the performance with forward-iterated states is shown in Fig.
10
c. As another control, we also checked dependence on window length (without forcing) in Fig.
10
b.

A.7
Sparsely forced BPTT

Loss truncation

One implicit consequence of the teacher forcing, eqn. (
16
), is the interruption of the hidden-to-hidden connections at these time points. More specifically, if the system is forced at time
t\in\mathcal{T}
, then there is no connection between
{\bm{z}}_{t}
and
{\bm{z}}_{t+1}
, that is

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}_{t+1}=\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t+1}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}=\frac{\partial RNN(\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t})}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}=0.

(95)

To see how these vanishing Jacobians truncate the loss gradients w.r.t to some parameter
\theta
, let us focus on the loss gradients immediately after the forcing,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t+1}}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t+1}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t+1}}\sum_{k=1}^{t+1}\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t+1}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{k}}\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{k}}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t+1}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t+1}}(\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{t+1}}{\partial\theta}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\underbrace{\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t+1}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{k}}}_{=0\,\text{, because of ~(\ref{eq:supp-van-jac})}}\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{k}}{\partial\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t+1}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t+1}}\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{t+1}}{\partial\theta}.

(96)

Eqn. (
96
) shows that sparsely forced BPTT implicitly truncates the loss gradients because it interrupts the hidden-to-hidden connection from
{\bm{z}}_{t}
to
{\bm{z}}_{t+1}
for
t\in\mathcal{T}
. More generally, defining
\widetilde{t}:=\max\{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}:t^{\prime}\leq t\}
, the overall loss gradients are truncated to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{k}}\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{k}}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{tr.}}}}{{=}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}\sum_{k=\widetilde{t}}^{t}\frac{\partial{\bm{z}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{k}}\frac{\partial^{+}{\bm{z}}_{k}}{\partial\theta}.

(97)
