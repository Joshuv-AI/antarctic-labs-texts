On elliptic equations involving surface measures
Marius Müller

Abstract
. We show optimal Lipschitz regularity for very weak solutions of the (measure-valued) elliptic PDE − div ( A ( x ) ∇ u ) = Q ℋ n − 1 Γ -\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u)=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma in a smooth domain Ω ⊂ ℝ n \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} . Here Γ \Gamma is a C 1 , α C^{1,\alpha} -regular hypersurface, Q ∈ C 0 , α Q\in C^{0,\alpha} is a density on Γ \Gamma , and the coefficient matrix A A is symmetric, uniformly elliptic and W 1 , q W^{1,q} -regular ( q > n ) (q>n) . We also discuss optimality of these assumptions on the data. The equation can be understood as a special coupling of two A A -harmonic functions with an interface Γ \Gamma . As such it plays an important role in several free boundary problems, as we shall discuss.

1.
Introduction

In this article we examine regularity of solutions of the following
measure-valued Dirichlet problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u)=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\qquad\qquad\quad\quad u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

on some smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
. Here,
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
denotes the
n-1
-dimensional Hausdorff measure restricted to an
interface

\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
for some suitably regular domain
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
and
Q:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is some suitably regular
density
. Further, the (nonconstant) coefficient matrix
(A(x))_{x\in\Omega}
is suitably regular as well as
symmetric and uniformly elliptic
in the sense that

(1.2)

a_{ij}=a_{ji}\quad\forall i,j=1,...,n\quad\textrm{and}\quad\exists\lambda>0:\xi^{T}A(x)\xi\geq\lambda|\xi|^{2}\quad\forall x\in\Omega\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Such equations appear often in the description of
free boundary problems
, as we shall illustrate in Section
5
.

The notion of solutions we study is the following

Definition 1.1

(Very weak solutions)
.

Let
A\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
be symmetric and uniformly elliptic and
Q\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
.
A function
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is called a
very weak solution
of (
1.1
) if

(1.3)

-\int_{\Omega}u(x)\;\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\phi(x))\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q(y)\phi(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\quad\forall\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}):\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

In Appendix
A
, we see that under some suitable further smoothness assumptions on the coefficients
Q,A,\Gamma
one can equivalently demand that
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and

(1.4)

\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u(x),\nabla\phi(x))\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q(y)\phi(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Solutions in the sense of (
1.4
) are called
weak solutions
, and might appear more intuitive at first sight — in particular since they can easily be seen to be unique.
The concept of very weak solutions is however the established definition of general
measure-valued Poisson problems
, cf.
[
Ponce
, Section 3.1]
and will also prove helpful during the course of our proof.

We are interested in the optimal regularity of solutions, under suitable further assumptions on the data
Q
,
A
,
\Gamma
.
The main result in this article is

Theorem 1.2

(Main Theorem)
.

Fix
\alpha>0
and
q>n
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth bounded domain and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be a
C^{1,\alpha}
-subdomain. If
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
,
Q\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)
and
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
satisfies (
1.2
), then the unique solution
u
of (
1.1
)
lies in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

The class
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
is the best expectable regularity in terms of Sobolev spaces. Indeed
W^{2,1}
-regularity is not possible, since for each
u\in W^{2,1}
and
A\in W^{1,q},(q>n)
the left hand side of (
1.1
) lies in
L^{1}
, whereas the (measure-valued) right hand side does not lie in
L^{1}
(unless
Q=0
). Therefore the optimal Sobolev class has to be below
W^{2,1}
. Moreover, we will see that
W^{1,p}
-regularity for each
p\in(1,\infty)
is true and a somewhat immediate consequence of the observations in
[
Auscher
]
, which deal with equations of the form
-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=\mathrm{div}(F)
. To make these results applicable, a needed prerequisite is
A\in VMO(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
. Recall that for any
q>n
one has
W^{1,q}\hookrightarrow C^{0,\gamma}\hookrightarrow VMO
with
\gamma=1-\frac{n}{q}
.
All in all,
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity is the only open question in terms of Sobolev regularity.

It could theoretically happen that
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity can be further improved upon in
classical function spaces

C^{k,\alpha}
(recalling that
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)=C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
). However, such improvement can not be obtained — we will show that
C^{1}
-regularity is impossible, cf. Section
2.3
.

If
A=Id_{n\times n}
(or
A
is smooth), (
1.1
) has already been studied by means of
potential theory
, cf.
[
Miranda
, Theorem 14.V]
and
[
Giraud
]
. In this special case the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
is already known to hold true.

We also show that the regularity assumptions on
Q,A,\Gamma
are optimal for the conclusion of the theorem. A previous article of the author (
[
PoissonMarius
]
) asserts (falsely) that if
A=Id_{n\times n}
then the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
holds true even if
\Omega^{\prime}
is only a Lipschitz subdomain. Such result is not true (by counterexample, cf.
[
PoissonMariusErratum
]
). Notice that the present article is self-contained and does not use any results in
[
PoissonMarius
]
.

We discuss the optimality of our assumptions by giving counterexamples to the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
if
\Omega^{\prime}
is only a Lipschitz domain and if
A\in W^{1,q}
only for some
q<n
, cf. Section
4
. This is remarkable in the following way: In
[
Kilpelainen
]
solutions of the general (Radon-)measure-valued problem

(1.5)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u(x))=\mu&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\quad\;u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

are studied and it is shown (cf.
[
Kilpelainen
, Theorem 2.9]
) that for each
\alpha\in(0,1)

(1.6)

\mu(B_{r}(x))\leq Cr^{n-2+\alpha}\;\forall r>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\textrm{The weak solution $u$ of \eqref{eq:measeqgen} lies in $C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})$}.

Our counterexample will reveal that “
\Rightarrow
” does in general not hold true in the case of
\alpha=1
. This reveals that growth properties of the measure are in this case not sufficient to make regularity assertions. The geometry of
\mu
plays a much bigger role.

Because of the mild requirements on our coefficient matrix we need an approach that is
detached
from potential theory. We will first establish results for smooth data
Q,A,\Gamma
by means of a comparison of solutions with the
(signed) distance function
. This comparison yields also
BV
-estimates for
\nabla u
, which are crucial for our approach.

We will then perform a blow-up analysis and use special test functions to obtain a-priori estimates in terms of the data. An approximation procedure will then imply the result.

Because of the absence of potential theory, our approach provides methodological progress that can in the future also be applied for nonlinear measure-valued equations.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

In the following we will always assume unless stated otherwise that
A
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic, cf. (
1.2
).
For an arbitrary closed set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
f\in C^{0,\alpha}(C)
if there exists some constant
H>0
such that

(2.1)

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq H|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in C.

We call
[f]_{C^{0,\alpha}(C)}:=\inf\{H>0:\eqref{eq:Hoeldiest}\textrm{ holds}\}
the
Hölder seminorm
of
f
and define the
Hölder norm

||f||_{C^{0,\alpha}(C)}:=\sup_{x\in C}|f(x)|+[f]_{C^{0,\alpha}(C)}
. We remark that if
C
is a
C^{1}
-submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then there would also be an alternative way to define the Hölder-seminorm, namely taking the
intrinsic distance

\mathrm{dist}_{C}(x,y)
instead of
|x-y|
in (
2.1
). These notions of Hölder continuity are only equivalent on
chord arc submanifolds
, cf.
[
Blatt
]
. We will thus consistently adhere to the notion in (
2.1
).

In the sequel
\Omega
always denotes a smooth bounded domain and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
always denotes a suitably smooth and compactly contained subdomain. Moreover,
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
denotes its boundary. Further we introduce the shorthand notation
\Omega^{\prime\prime}:=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
.

In what follows we will assume tacitly and without loss of generality that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
is connected, since otherwise we could look at the (finitely many) connected components of
\Gamma
separately. (If we do so, it however needs to be ensured that all connected components
\Gamma
can also be regarded as boundaries of subdomains of
\Omega
. This is true by the Jordan-Brouwer separation theorem.)

We say (for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\geq 0
) that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
is a
C^{k,\alpha}
-boundary
if it can be written as

(2.2)

\Gamma=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}O_{i}[(U_{i}\times V_{i})\cap\{(x^{\prime},f_{i}(x^{\prime})):x^{\prime}\in U_{i}\}]=:\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}\Gamma_{i}

where
M\in\mathbb{N}
is finite,
O_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are orthogonal matrices,
U_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
are open balls,
V_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}
are open intervals and
f_{i}\in C^{k,\alpha}(4\overline{U_{i}};V_{i})
are such that the subgraph of
f_{i}
lies in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Here
4U_{i}
denotes the ball with the same center and the quadruple radius of
U_{i}
. This enlargement of
U_{i}
will be needed for technical purposes, cf. Appendix
C
. We say that
\Gamma
is
represented
by
(O_{i},U_{i},V_{i},f_{i}
,
i=1,...,M)
if
O_{i},U_{i},V_{i},f_{i}
are as in (
2.2
) and
\Gamma_{i+1}\cap\Gamma_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all
i=1,...,M
. We remark that the condition that
\Gamma_{i+1}\cap\Gamma_{i}\neq\emptyset
can always be arranged by relabeling, since
\Gamma_{i}\subset\Gamma
is open for all
i
and
\Gamma
is connected. We set

(2.3)

\displaystyle[\Gamma]_{k,\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{M}(1+\sup_{x\in V_{i}}|x|+|U_{i}|+||f_{i}||_{C^{k,\alpha}(4\overline{U_{i}})}):\Gamma\textrm{ rep. by $O_{i},U_{i},V_{i},f_{i}$, $i=1,...,M$}\right\}.

We use the shorthand notation
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{k,\alpha}
if
\Gamma
is a
C^{k,\alpha}
-boundary of
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
and we denote the outward pointing unit normal
\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}
simply by
\nu
for the sake of simplicity. The signed measure
\mu=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
is defined via

(2.4)

\mu(A):=\int_{A\cap\Gamma}Q\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad\forall A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\textrm{ Borel set.}

From now on, also
(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denotes the standard Euclidean inner product.

2.2.
Existence, Uniqueness and
W^{1,p}
-regularity for
p<\infty

First we establish existence and
W^{1,p}
-regularity for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. To this end we employ the
duality method
. We show that the measure term on the right hand side of (
1.1
) lies the
dual space

(W_{0}^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega))^{*}
for any
p^{\prime}\in(1,\infty)
and then illustrate how this can be used to show that
u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
for
p\in(1,\infty)
such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. This is related to the study of the equation
-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=\mathrm{div}(F)
, conducted under minimal assumptions in
[
Auscher
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{0,1}
and
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. Then the distribution

(2.5)

T(\psi):=\int_{\Gamma}Q\psi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\quad(\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega))

extends to an element of
W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}
and
||T||_{(W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}}\leq C(n)[\Gamma]_{0,1}||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}
. Moreover there exists some
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma=\mathrm{div}(F)
distributionally, i.e.

(2.6)

\int_{\Gamma}Q\psi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\Omega}(F,\nabla\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Further, one can choose
F
in such a way that
||F||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(n)[\Gamma]_{0,1}||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}
.

Proof.

To prove that
T\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}
it suffices to show (by applying
[
Ponce
, Proposition 17.17]
with
\mu_{\pm}=Q^{\pm}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
and
k=1
and
[
Ponce
, Proposition B.3]
with
\delta=\infty
) that

(2.7)

\int_{\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)}|Q|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\leq Cr^{n-1}\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\quad\forall r>0.

We first estimate

(2.8)

\int_{\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)}|Q|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\leq||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)).

Notice that
\Gamma
is covered by finitely many sets of the form

(2.9)

E_{i}:=O_{i}[(U_{i}\times V_{i})\cap\{(x^{\prime},f_{i}(x^{\prime})):x^{\prime}\in U_{i}\}\quad i=1,...,M,

where
O_{i}
is a rotation matrix,
U_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is an open set and
V_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}
is open and
f_{i}\in C^{0,1}(4\overline{U_{i}})
are such that
\sum_{i=1}^{M}(1+||Df_{i}||_{L^{\infty}})\leq[\Gamma]_{0,1}+\epsilon
for some arbitrary but fixed
\epsilon>0
. Now let
r>0
and
x\in\Gamma
be arbitrary. For all
i=1,...,M
there holds (with
\tilde{x}_{i}:=O_{i}^{T}x
and
y_{i}^{\prime}
denoting the first
n-1
components of
\tilde{x}_{i}
)

(2.10)

\displaystyle O_{i}^{T}(E_{i}\cap(\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)))

\displaystyle\subset\{(x^{\prime},f_{i}(x^{\prime})):x^{\prime}\in U_{i},(x^{\prime},f_{i}(x^{\prime}))\in B_{r}(\tilde{x}_{i})\}

(2.11)

\displaystyle\subset\{(x^{\prime},f_{i}(x^{\prime})):x^{\prime}\in U_{i},x^{\prime}\in B_{r}(y_{i}^{\prime})\}.

Hence (with
\mathcal{L}^{n-1}
denoting the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
) one finds

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int_{U_{i}\cap B_{r}(y_{i}^{\prime})}\sqrt{1+|Df_{i}(x^{\prime})|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

(2.13)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sqrt{1+||Df_{i}||_{\infty}^{2}}\mathcal{L}^{n-1}(B_{r}(y_{i}))\leq\mathcal{L}^{n-1}(B_{1}(0))([\Gamma]_{0,1}+\epsilon)r^{n-1}.

Together with (
2.8
) we infer that

(2.14)

\int_{\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)}|Q|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\leq C(n)||Q||_{L^{\infty}}([\Gamma]_{0,1}+\epsilon)r^{n-1}.

This and
[
Ponce
, Proposition 17.17]
yield that
||T||_{W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}}\leq C(n)||Q||_{L^{\infty}}([\Gamma]_{0,1}+\epsilon)
. Arbitraryness of
\epsilon>0
yields the desired bound for
||T||_{W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}}
.
By
[
Torres
, Lemma 6.6]
we infer the existence of
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
||T||_{(W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega))^{*}}=||F||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
and

(2.15)

T(\psi)=\int_{\Omega}(F,\nabla\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall\psi\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega).

This together with the definition of
T
in (
2.5
) implies (
2.6
). Finally, we have

(2.16)

||F||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=||T||_{(W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega))^{*}}\leq C(n)[\Gamma]_{0,1}||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}.

The claim follows.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The proof exposes a special
growth property
of the measure
\mu=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
, namely
|\mu|(B_{r}(x))\leq Cr^{n-1}
, holding true whenever
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{0,1}
.

Remark 2.3
.

We recall that if
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{0,1}
then there exists a continuous
trace operator

\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}:W^{1,1}(\Omega^{\prime})\rightarrow L^{1}(\Gamma)
. One readily checks that the extension of
T
, defined as in (
2.5
) to
W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)
is given by

(2.17)

T(\psi)=\int_{\Gamma}Q\;\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

We will from now on write
\psi
instead of
\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\psi)
and tacitly regard the evaluation as an evaluation in the sense of Sobolev traces.

Next we prove the asserted
W^{1,p}
-regularity. To this end we will need that under suitable regularity requirements on
A
, very weak solutions of the equation are also
weak solutions
. Which regularity requirements are exactly needed is discussed in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{0,1}
,
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
,
q>n
. Then there exists a unique very weak solution
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
to

(2.18)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u)=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\qquad\qquad\quad\;\;\;u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

and it lies in
W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C=C(p,n,\Omega)
such that

(2.19)

||u||_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\leq C[\Gamma]_{0,1}||Q||_{L^{\infty}}.

Proof.

Step 1.
Existence. We show first that there exists
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that

(2.20)

\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad\forall\phi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega).

To this end we employ the Lax-Milgram lemma. One readily checks that (due to the fact that
A\in W^{1,q}\subset L^{\infty}
) one has that
a:W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\times W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

(2.21)

a(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u,\nabla v)\;\mathrm{d}x

defines a continuous and coercive bilinear form on the Hilbert space
H:=W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Next let
T
be the distribution in (
2.5
). The previous lemma shows that
T\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}\subset H^{*}
. Therefore the Lax-Milgram lemma yields that there exists a unique solution
u\in H
such that
a(u,v)=T(v)
for all
v\in H
. Using (
2.17
) we conclude that
u
solves (
2.20
). Lemma
A.2
shows that
u
is also a very weak solution.

Step 2.
Uniqueness. Suppose that
w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is another very weak solution. By Lemma
A.2
we have

(2.22)

-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}w\;\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=T(\phi)\quad\forall\phi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega),

yielding

(2.23)

\int_{\Omega}(u-w)\;\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=0\quad\forall\phi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega).

Next we let
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be arbitrary. Lemma
A.1
implies that there exists some
\phi_{g}\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi_{g})=g
pointwise almost everywhere. Using
\phi=\phi_{g}
in (
2.23
) we find

(2.24)

\int_{\Omega}(u-w)g\;\mathrm{d}x=0\quad\forall g\in L^{2}(\Omega).

This implies
u-w=0
a.e.

Step 3.
Regularity. Let
p\in[2,\infty)
be arbitrary. Let
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
be as in (
2.6
). By
[
Auscher
, Theorem 1]
there exists some
u_{p}\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
such that

(2.25)

\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u_{p},\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(F,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

We infer from linearity and (
2.6
)

(2.26)

\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla(u_{p}-u),\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(F,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Since
u_{p}-u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
A\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
we infer by approximation

(2.27)

0=\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla(u_{p}-u),\nabla(u_{p}-u))\;\mathrm{d}x=a(u_{p}-u,u_{p}-u),

yielding that
u_{p}=u
by coercivity of the bilinear form
a
. Hence
u=u_{p}\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
. Thus (
2.25
),
[
Auscher
, Theorem 1.1]
and Lemma
2.1
yield the estimate

(2.28)

||u||_{W_{0}^{1,p}}=||u_{p}||_{W_{0}^{1,p}}\leq C(p,n,\Omega)||F||_{L^{p}}\leq C(p,n,\Omega)||F||_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(p,n,\Omega)||Q||_{\infty}[\Gamma]_{0,1}.

∎

2.3.
Impossibility of
C^{1}
-regularity

In this section we discuss why
C^{1}
-regularity is impossible to obtain for this problem. This will be a consequence of the classical Hopf-Oleinik boundary point lemma for elliptic operators, discovered by Hopf and independently by Oleinik, cf.
[
Hopf
]
for elliptic equations in
C^{2}
-smooth domains. Meanwhile there have also been found versions of this lemma for less regular, e.g.
C^{1,\alpha}
-regular, boundaries, cf.
[
Mazya
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Lemma 2.5

(Hopf-Oleinik
[
Mazya
, Special case of Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Suppose that
D
is a
C^{1,\alpha}
domain for some
\alpha>0
and
A\in C^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic. Let

v\in C^{2}(D)\cap C^{0}(\overline{D})
be nonconstant and satisfy
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla v)=0
pointwise on
D
. Suppose that
x_{0}\in\partial D
is such that
v(x_{0})=\max_{x\in\overline{D}}v(x)
. Then

(2.29)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0-}\frac{v(x_{0}+t\nu_{D}(x_{0}))-v(x_{0})}{t}>0.

We remark that the existence of some
x_{0}\in\partial D
with the properties demanded in the previous lemma follows from the
elliptic maximum principle.
, cf.
[
Evans
, Theorem 1, Section 6.4.1]
. We also remark that in the situation of the previous lemma there holds for any
y_{0}\in\partial D

(2.30)

v(y_{0})=\min_{x\in\overline{D}}v(x)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0-}\frac{v(y_{0}+t\nu_{D}(y_{0}))-v(y_{0})}{t}<0.

This is readily checked when applying the previous lemma to
-v
.

Based on this lemma we prove the following result

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth domain and
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be the solution of (
1.1
) with
A\in C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
Q\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{1,\alpha}
. Then
u\not\in C^{1}(\Omega)
unless
Q=0
.

Proof.

Assume that
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and
Q\not\equiv 0
. We remark that also by Lemma
2.4

u\in C(\overline{\Omega}
). In particular
\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)
and
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)
are attained.
We claim that there holds
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=0
in
\Omega^{\prime}\cup\Omega^{\prime\prime}
. Indeed, for each
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
one has (after extending
\phi
by zero on
\Omega
)

(2.31)

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(A\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,

since
\phi|_{\Gamma}=0
. One infers that
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=0
weakly in
\Omega^{\prime}
. One can proceed analogously for
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime\prime})
.
We infer from
[
GilTru
, Theorem 9.19]
and the assumptions on
A
that
u\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega^{\prime\prime})
for each
q<\infty
. In particular, by Sobolev embedding,
u\in C^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap C^{2}(\Omega^{\prime\prime})
. As a consequence
u\in C^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap C(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
and
u\in C^{2}(\Omega^{\prime\prime})\cap C(\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}})
. Hence
u
is on both sets
\Omega^{\prime}
and
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
a classical solution of
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=0
. We next show the following

Intermediate claim.
One value
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)
or
\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)
is attained on
\Gamma
. By the elliptic maximum principle one has that
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}}u(x)
is attained on
\partial\Omega^{\prime}=\Gamma
and
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}}u(x)
is attained on
\partial\Omega^{\prime\prime}=\Gamma\cup\partial\Omega
. The same can be observed for the minima.
One concludes that
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)
and
\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)
must be attained on
\Gamma\cup\partial\Omega
. If both are only attained on
\partial\Omega
then
u|_{\partial\Omega}=0
yields

\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)=0,

and as a result
u\equiv 0
in
\Omega
. A contradiction to (
1.1
) as
Q\not\equiv 0
. The intermediate claim is shown. To proceed we assume without loss of generality that
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)
is attained at some
x_{0}\in\Gamma
. We note that
u\not\equiv\mathrm{const.}
in
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
. Indeed, assuming the opposite would yield
u\equiv 0
in
\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}
and hence also on
\Gamma
. This would however imply that
u|_{\Omega^{\prime}}
is a classical solution of the Dirichlet problem

(2.32)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=0&\textrm{in }\Omega^{\prime},\\
\qquad\quad\;\;\;u=0&\textrm{on }\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\end{cases}

and thus (by uniqueness for the Dirichlet problem)
u|_{\Omega^{\prime}}=0
, resulting in
u\equiv 0
and contradicting
Q\not\equiv 0
. Applying Lemma
2.5
with
D=\Omega^{\prime\prime}
and using that
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
we infer that

(2.33)

0<\lim_{t\rightarrow 0-}\frac{u(x_{0}+t\nu_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(x_{0}))-u(x_{0})}{t}=(\nabla u(x_{0}),\nu_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(x_{0})).

On the other hand we have

(2.34)

(\nabla u(x_{0}),\nu_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(x_{0}))=-(\nabla u(x_{0}),\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))=-\lim_{t\rightarrow 0-}\frac{u(x_{0}+t\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))-u(x_{0})}{t}\leq 0,

where the last inequality is due to the fact that
u
attains a maximum at
x_{0}
and thus
u(x_{0}+t\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))\leq u(x_{0})
for all
t\leq 0
.
Since (
2.33
) and (
2.34
) contradict each other we obtain that our assumption can not be satisfied. Hence
u\not\in C^{1}(\Omega)
.
∎

3.
Proof of the main theorem

3.1.
Regularity for smooth initial data

In this section we establish
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity of solutions for (suitably) smooth data
Q,A,\Gamma
. We will achieve this by comparison with a
prototype solution
that involves the
signed distance function

d_{\Gamma}
, a notion that we recall in Appendix
E
. The comparison with the signed distance reveals not just
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity but also that solutions
u
of (
1.1
) with suitably smooth initial data satisfy
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega)
. This will be important as it allows us to form
traces
of gradients of solutions in the sequel.

In this section we will always require that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{k}
for some
k\geq 2
. We further fix some
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
where
\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon_{0}(\Gamma)<\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega)
is chosen as in Appendix
E
, i.e. such that
d_{\Gamma}\in C^{k}(\overline{B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\Gamma)})
. Here we denote
B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\Gamma):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathrm{dist}(x,\Gamma)<\epsilon_{0}\}
.

We will make frequent use of the formula in Lemma
E.1
, i.e. that distributionally on
C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))^{\prime}
one has for any suitably smooth function
Q_{0}
(with
\nu:=\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}
)

(3.1)

\partial^{2}_{ij}\left(\frac{Q_{0}}{2}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)=Q_{0}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma+\partial^{2}_{ij}(\frac{Q_{0}}{2}d_{\Gamma})(\chi_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\prime}}).

Lemma 3.1

(Lipschitz-regularity for suitably smooth input data)
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{3}
,
Q\in W^{2,s}(\Omega)
and
A=(A_{ij})\in W^{2,s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
s>n
. Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be the unique very weak solution to (
1.1
). Then
u\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
Q,A,\Gamma
be as in the statement.
Let
\tilde{Q}:=\frac{Q}{(A\nu,\nu)}
and notice that
\tilde{Q}\in W^{2,s}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
as
\nu
possesses a
C^{2}
-extension on
B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)
(since
\nu=\nabla d_{\Gamma}|_{\Gamma}
and
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{3}
). Now we look at the following expression for arbitrary
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
.

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\left(A\nabla(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|),\nabla\phi\right)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.3)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(A_{ij}\partial_{j}\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.4)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\partial_{i}(A_{ij})\partial_{j}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\tfrac{1}{2}A_{ij}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\partial^{2}_{ij}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\partial_{j}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\partial_{i}A_{ij}\right)\phi\;\mathrm{d}x-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\tfrac{1}{2}A_{ij}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\partial^{2}_{ij}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Using Lemma
E.1
in the last summand (with
Q_{0}=\tilde{Q}A_{ij}
) we find

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\left(A\nabla(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|),\nabla\phi\right)\;\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\partial_{j}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\partial_{i}A_{ij}\right)\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.7)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma}A_{ij}\tilde{Q}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\partial^{2}_{ij}\left(\frac{\tilde{Q}A_{ij}}{2}d_{\Gamma}\right)(\chi_{\Omega^{\prime}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}})\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Notice next that

(3.8)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\tilde{Q}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}=(A\nu,\nu)\tilde{Q}=Q.

Further observe that Lipschitz continuity of
|d_{\Gamma}|
and the prerequisites on
Q
and
A
imply

(3.9)

\partial_{j}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\partial_{i}A_{ij}\right)\in L^{s}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))

as well as

(3.10)

\partial^{2}_{ij}\left(\frac{\tilde{Q}A_{ij}}{2}d_{\Gamma}\right)(\chi_{\Omega^{\prime}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}})\in L^{s}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)).

We infer from this and (
3.6
) that for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
one has

(3.11)

\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\left(A\nabla(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|),\nabla\phi\right)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}g\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

for
g:=\partial_{j}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\partial_{i}A_{ij}\right)-\partial^{2}_{ij}\left(\frac{\tilde{Q}A_{i}^{j}}{2}d_{\Gamma}\right)(\chi_{\Omega^{\prime}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}})\in L^{s}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
. Now let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be the solution of (
1.1
). With Lemma
A.2
and the previous formula we have that
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and for each
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
one has

(3.12)

\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}\left(A\nabla(u+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|),\nabla\phi\right)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)}g\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Elliptic regularity (cf.
[
GilTru
, Theorem 9.15]
) now implies that
u+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\in W^{2,s}_{loc}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))\subset W^{1,\infty}_{loc}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
. Since however also
\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\in W^{1,\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
we infer that
u=u+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|-\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\in W^{1,\infty}_{loc}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
. To complete the proof we show that
u\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))
. To this end we notice that on

\partial B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma)
we have

(3.13)

u=u+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Since
u+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\in W^{2,s}_{loc}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
we infer that
u\big|_{\partial B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))}
is a restriction of a
W^{2,s}
-function on
\partial B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma)
. Let now
\varphi:\partial B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma)\cup\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be given by

(3.14)

\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}u(x)&x\in\partial B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma),\\
0&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Then
\varphi
is the restriction of a function in
W^{2,s}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))
to
\partial(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))=\partial\Omega\cup\partial B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma)
.
Moreover

u\big|_{\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma)}
solves (weakly)

(3.15)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=0&\textrm{in }\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma),\\
\qquad\quad\quad\;\;u=\varphi&\textrm{on }\partial(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma)).\end{cases}

This and the regularity of
A,\varphi,\Gamma
imply (cf.
[
GilTru
, Lemma 9.15]
) that
u\in W^{2,s}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))\hookrightarrow W^{1,\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The prerequsite
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{3}
has been only used once in the beginning of the previous proof to deduce that
\tilde{Q}=\frac{Q}{(A\nu,\nu)}\in W^{2,s}
. If
A=\mathrm{Id}_{n\times n}
, we have that
\tilde{Q}=Q\in W^{2,s}
even if one has only
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{2}
.

The properties of the signed distance function and the findings in the previous proof also imply the announced
BV
-regularity statement.

Corollary 3.3

(
BV
-regularity of the gradient)
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{3}
,
Q\in W^{2,s}(\Omega)
and
A\in W^{2,s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
s>n
. Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be the very weak solution to (
1.1
). Then
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We know from the discussion after (
3.15
) that
u\in W^{2,s}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))
, and hence in particular
\nabla u\in W^{1,s}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))\subset BV(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))
. Next we show that
u\in BV(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
which will then prove the claim.
The discussion after (
3.12
) yields that
u+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\in W^{2,s}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
for some
\tilde{Q}\in W^{2,s}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
. Hence
\nabla\left(u+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)\in BV(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
. Using that by Corollary
E.2

\nabla\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)\in BV(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
we find

(3.16)

\nabla u=\nabla\left(u+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)-\nabla\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)\in BV(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)).

The claim follows.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

For the same reason as in Remark
3.2
, the prerequisite
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{3}
can be replaced by
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{2}
if
A=\mathrm{Id}_{n\times n}
.

Using the same comparison techniques one also obtains more regularity away from the interface
\Gamma
.

Corollary 3.5

(Further regularity away from the interface)
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{3}
and
Q\in W^{2,s}(\Omega)
,
A\in W^{2,s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
s>n
. Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be the weak solution to (
1.1
). Then
u\in W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime\prime})
.

Proof.

We have seen in (
3.12
) and the discussion below that for
\tilde{Q}=\frac{Q}{(A\nu,\nu)}\in W^{2,s}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
there holds

(3.17)

u+\tfrac{\tilde{Q}}{2}|d_{\Gamma}|\in W^{2,s}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))\subset W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))\cap W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)).

Moreover, Lemma
E.3
implies that

(3.18)

\tfrac{\tilde{Q}}{2}|d_{\Gamma}|\in W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))\cap W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)).

This implies that
u\in W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))\cap W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
. Since the discussion below (
3.15
) also yields that
u\in W^{2,s}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))\subset W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))\cap W^{2,s}(\Omega^{\prime\prime}\setminus B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\Gamma))
the claim follows.
∎

3.2.
Blow-up arguments

We have observed in the last section that for suitably regular data
Q,A,\Gamma
solutions of (
1.1
) lie in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and their gradients lie in
BV(\Omega)
. By Lemma
2.6
, better global regularity can not be expected. However on
\Omega^{\prime}
and
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
, solutions are
A
-harmonic and therefore more regular (cf. Corollary
3.5
). Hence there must occur a
loss of regularity
on
\Gamma
. In this section we use blow-up techniques to understand the behavior on
\Gamma
precisely.

Lemma 3.6

(A Taylor-type expansion)
.

Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{1},

A,Q\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
for some
\alpha>0
and
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is a solution of (
1.1
). If
u\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega)
then for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e.
x_{0}\in\Gamma
there exists some
\theta(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(3.19)

u(x)=u(x_{0})+(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{Q(x_{0})}{(A(x_{0})\nu(x_{0}),\nu(x_{0}))}|(x-x_{0},\nu(x_{0}))|+o(|x-x_{0}|).

Moreover, one has (for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e.
x_{0}\in\Gamma
)

(3.20)

\theta(x_{0})=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}x.

In particular, the map
\theta:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x_{0}\mapsto\theta(x_{0})
is Borel measurable and lies in
L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Define for
r>0
the function
u_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
via

(3.21)

u_{r}(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{u(x_{0}+rx)-u(x_{0})}{r}&x_{0}+rx\in\Omega,\\
-\frac{u(x_{0})}{r}&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Observe that
u_{r}
is Lipschitz continuous for all
r>0
,
u_{r}(0)=0
for all
r>0
and
||\nabla u_{r}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. We conclude by Lemma
B.5
that for each sequence
r_{j}\rightarrow 0
there exists a subsequence
r_{l_{j}}\rightarrow 0
and some
\bar{u}\in W^{1,\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\nabla\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
u_{r_{l_{j}}}\rightarrow\bar{u}
locally uniformly and
\nabla u_{r_{l_{j}}}\rightarrow\nabla\bar{u}
weakly in
W^{1,2}(B_{R}(0))
for all
R>0
.
As a shorthand notation we write
\bar{r}_{j}=r_{l_{j}}
.
We next describe
\bar{u}
by means of the equation it solves. For arbitrary
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
there holds

(3.22)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle(A(x_{0})\nabla\bar{u}(x),\nabla\phi(x))\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(A(x_{0})\nabla u_{\bar{r}_{j}}(x),\nabla\phi(x))\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.23)

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{x_{0}+\bar{r}_{j}x\in\Omega}(A(x_{0})\nabla u(x_{0}+\bar{r}_{j}x),\nabla\phi(x))\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.24)

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left(\int_{x_{0}+\bar{r}_{j}x\in\Omega}(A(x_{0}+\bar{r}_{j}x)\nabla u(x_{0}+\bar{r}_{j}x),\nabla\phi(x))\;\mathrm{d}x\right.

(3.25)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\left.+\int_{x_{0}+\bar{r}_{j}x\in\Omega}((A(x_{0})-A(x_{0}+\bar{r}_{j}x))\nabla u(x_{0}+\bar{r}_{j}x),\nabla\phi(x))\;\mathrm{d}x\right).

The absolute value of the second summand is now estimated by

(3.26)

[A]_{C^{0,\alpha}}\bar{r}_{j}^{\alpha}\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{spt}(\phi))^{\alpha}||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\phi|\;\mathrm{d}x=o(1)\quad(j\rightarrow\infty).

Hence we obtain

(3.27)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

Remark 3.8
.

The previous lemma reproduces a very classical result from potential theory, to be found in
[
Miranda
, Eq. (14.15)]
. For
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{1,\alpha}
and
Q\in C^{0,\alpha}
one can define the
single layer potential

\mathcal{P}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

(3.78)

\mathcal{P}(x):=\int_{\Gamma}F(x-y)Q(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\quad\textrm{with $F$ being the fundamental solution of $-\Delta$.}

The result is that for all
x_{0}\in\Gamma
there exist
\partial^{\pm}_{\nu}\mathcal{P}(x_{0}):=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0\pm}\frac{\mathcal{P}(x_{0}+t\nu(x_{0}))-\mathcal{P}(x_{0})}{t}
and one has the following
normal jump formula

(3.79)

\partial_{\nu}^{+}\mathcal{P}(x_{0})-\partial_{\nu}^{-}\mathcal{P}(x_{0})=-Q(x_{0}).

A similar result can be also obtained if one replaces
-\Delta
with an elliptic operator
-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla(\cdot))
, provided that
A
is smooth enough.
The observation of (
3.79
) is related to our findings in Lemma
3.7
.
If
A=\mathrm{Id}_{n\times n}
the solution
u
of (
1.1
) is (cf. Proposition
4.1
) given by

(3.80)

u(x)=\int_{\Gamma}G_{\Omega}(x,y)Q(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y),

where
G_{\Omega}
denotes Green’s function for
-\Delta
. Since
G_{\Omega}(x,y)
and
F(x-y)
coincide up to a smooth function in
\Omega
we obtain that
u=\mathcal{P}+g
for some
g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. In particular

(3.81)

\partial_{\nu}^{+}u(x_{0})-\partial_{\nu}^{-}u(x_{0})=\partial_{\nu}^{+}\mathcal{P}(x_{0})-\partial_{\nu}^{-}\mathcal{P}(x_{0})=-Q(x_{0}).

If one now replaces
\partial_{\nu}^{+}u
by
(\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(\nabla u),\nu)
and
\partial_{\nu}^{-}u
by
(\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\nabla u),\nu)
one can obtain the same normal jump with the formulas from Lemma
3.7
.

The previous remark says that the behavior of
normal derivatives
of solutions was previously understood by means of potential theory. The upshot in our new approach is that we also get an explicit understanding of the
tangential derivatives
, by means of the function
\theta
obtained in Lemma
3.6
. This helps us also characterize the regularity of the tangential derivatives.

Corollary 3.9
.

Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{3}
,
Q\in W^{2,s}(\Omega)
and
A\in W^{2,s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some

. Then

possesses an extension in

.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.5
one has that

which implies that
\nabla u\in W^{1,s}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap W^{1,s}(\Omega^{\prime\prime})
. In particular
\nabla u|_{\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}}
possesses an extension in
W^{1,s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(as
\Omega^{\prime}
is smooth enough for the existence of an extension operator).
Now observe that on
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
there holds by Lemma
3.7

(3.82)

\theta=\nabla u|_{\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{Q}{(A\nu,\nu)}\nu.

Since
\nu=\nabla d_{\Gamma}|_{\Gamma}
possesses a
C^{2}
-extension to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Q
possesses a
W^{2,s}
-extension the claim follows.
∎

3.3.
A priori bounds

In the following section we use the results of the previous blow-up analysis to bound
||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
in terms of our data
Q,A,\Gamma
, which are still assumed appropriately smooth in this section. We will however check carefully that the constants in our a priori estimates on
||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
depend only on
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
.
Notice in particular that this means that standard elliptic regularity estimates should not be used as the constants would then depend on
[\Gamma]_{2,0}
. However,
for equations with (left -and right hand side) in divergence form, some regularity estimates hold true with constants only depending on
[\Gamma]_{1,0}
, cf.
[
Auscher
, Eq. (3)]
.

A second key ingredient for our estimates is the
elliptic maximum principle
, which we however want to apply to the
differentiated equation
to obtain estimates for
\nabla u
. The problem here is that one needs to investigate in what sense
\nabla u
actually solves the differentiated equation: Since
\nabla u
is a priori only
BV
-regular, one has to investigate carefully in what way differentiation of the equation makes sense.
Further, there will appear very weak concepts of solutions, so called
BV-solutions
, cf. Definition
F.1
. For these types of solutions it needs to be carefully checked, whether an elliptic maximum principle is at all available. We have dedicated Appendix
F
to this question.

Lemma 3.10
.

Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}
,
Q\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)
and
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
q>n
. Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be a very weak solution of (
1.1
) with
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then for
r:=\frac{(n+q)q}{q-n}
there holds

(3.83)

||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(n,q,\Omega,[\Gamma]_{1,0})||DA||_{L^{q}(\Omega)}||\nabla u||_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+||\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}+||\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}.

Proof.

Define
w_{j}:=\partial_{j}u|_{\Omega^{\prime}}\in BV(\Omega^{\prime})\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
. Naturally,
\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(w_{j})=\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{j}u)
.
We intend to obtain an equation for
w_{j}
on
\Omega^{\prime}
and
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
to which the maximum principle can be applied.
To this end we compute for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})

(3.84)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}w_{j}\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\partial_{j}u\;\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.85)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}u\;\mathrm{div}(\partial_{j}A(x)\nabla\phi+A(x)\nabla(\partial_{j}\phi))\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.86)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{j}A(x)\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(A(x)\nabla u,\nabla(\partial_{j}\phi))\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.87)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{j}A(x)\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x,

where we have used in the last step that
u
solves (
1.1
).
Notice that by Hölder’s inequality

(3.88)

||(\partial_{j}A)\nabla u||_{L^{\frac{n+q}{2}}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq||\partial_{j}A||_{L^{q}(\Omega)}||\nabla u||_{L^{r}(\Omega)}.

Hence by
[
Auscher
, Theorem 1.1]
there exists a unique weak solution
\tilde{w}_{j}\in W_{0}^{1,\frac{n+q}{2}}(\Omega^{\prime})

(3.89)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\tilde{w}_{j})=\mathrm{div}((\partial_{j}A(x))\nabla u)&\textrm{in}\;\Omega^{\prime},\\
\tilde{w}_{j}=0&\textrm{on}\;\partial\Omega^{\prime},\end{cases}

in the sense that
\tilde{w}_{j}\in W_{0}^{1,\frac{n+q}{2}}(\Omega^{\prime})
and

(3.90)

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(A(x)\nabla\tilde{w}_{j},\nabla\eta)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{j}A(x)\nabla u,\nabla\eta)\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime}).

By
[
Auscher
, Eq. (3) and Theorem 1.1]
and (
3.88
) we infer also that

(3.91)

||\tilde{w}_{j}||_{W_{0}^{1,\frac{n+q}{2}}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq C([\partial\Omega^{\prime}]_{1,0})||\partial_{j}A\nabla u||_{L^{\frac{n+q}{2}}}\leq C([\Gamma]_{1,0})||DA||_{L^{q}}\;||\nabla u||_{L^{r}}.

Now defining
\psi:=w_{j}-\tilde{w}_{j}
we infer that
\psi\in BV(\Omega^{\prime})
and
\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\psi)=\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{j}u)
. Moreover (
3.87
) and (
3.90
) imply that for all
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
there holds

(3.92)

\int_{\Omega}\psi\;\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\eta)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(w_{j}-\tilde{w}_{j})\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\eta)\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

Hence
\psi
is (in the sense of Definition
F.1
) a
BV
-solution of

(3.93)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\psi)=0&\textrm{in }\Omega^{\prime},\\
\qquad\quad\quad\;\;\psi=\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{j}u)&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Therefore
\psi
satisfies all prerequisites for the announced elliptic maximum principle (Lemma
F.2
), from which we conclude that
||\psi||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq||\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{j}u)||_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega^{\prime})}
.
Using this and the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,\frac{n+q}{2}}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
(with embedding constant
D=D([\partial\Omega^{\prime}]_{1,0})
) we find

(3.94)

\displaystyle||w_{j}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq||\tilde{w}_{j}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})}+||\psi||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq D([\partial\Omega^{\prime}]_{1,0})||\tilde{w}_{j}||_{W^{1,\frac{n+q}{2}}(\Omega^{\prime})}+||\psi||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})}

(3.95)

\displaystyle\leq D([\Gamma]_{1,0})C([\Gamma]_{1,0})||DA||_{L^{q}(\Omega)}||\nabla u||_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+||\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{j}u)||_{L^{\infty}}.

For
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
one can derive the same bound and thereupon infers the claim.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}
,
Q\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)
and
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
q>n
. Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be a very weak solution of (
1.1
) with
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Let
\theta:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be as in Lemma
3.6
. Then

(3.96)

||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(n,q,\Omega,[\Gamma]_{1,0})||DA||_{L^{q}(\Omega)}||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}+2||\theta||_{L^{\infty}}+\frac{1}{\lambda}||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
and Lemma
3.10
we have that for
r:=\frac{q(n+q)}{q-n}

(3.97)

||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(n,q,\Omega,[\Gamma]_{1,0})||DA||_{L^{q}}||u||_{W^{1,r}}+||\theta-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{Q}{(A\nu,\nu)}||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}+||\theta+\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{Q}{(A\nu,\nu)}||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}.

Now notice that by Lemma
2.4
we have

(3.98)

||u||_{W^{1,r}}\leq C(r,[\Gamma]_{0,1})||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}.

Using this, the triangle inequality and
\frac{1}{(A\nu,\nu)}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}
the claimed estimate follows.
∎

The estimate (
3.96
) has (except for the second summand) constants depending only on
n,q,\Omega,[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha},||A||_{W^{1,q}},\lambda
and
||Q||_{C^{0,\alpha}}
.
We also will achieve such control in the second summand by estimating
||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}
in terms of the same quantities. We will obtain a control of
||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}
using the Taylor expansion in Lemma
3.6
and a special test function in (
1.1
).

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}
,
Q\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
and
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
q>n
. Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be a very weak solution of (
1.1
) such that
\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap BV(\Omega)
and let
\theta
be as in Lemma
3.6
. Then we have

(3.99)

||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq C(n,q,\alpha,\lambda,||Q||_{C^{0,\alpha}},||A||_{W^{1,q}},[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha},\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega)).

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\alpha<1-\frac{n}{q}
, so that
W^{1,q}\hookrightarrow C^{0,\alpha}
. (If this is not the case then regard
Q
as an element of
C^{0,\alpha^{\prime}}
for some
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
and work with
\alpha^{\prime}
instead).
Let
x_{0}\in\Gamma
be an arbitrary Lebesgue point of
\theta
such that the formula in Lemma
3.6
holds and
|\theta(x_{0})|\geq\frac{1}{2}||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}.

For this proof we set
A_{0}:=A(x_{0})
.
Fix
r\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega))
and define for
\epsilon>0
small
\eta_{\epsilon}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

(3.100)

\eta_{\epsilon}(s):=\begin{cases}1&0\leq s\leq r,\\
1-\frac{s-r}{\epsilon}&r<s\leq r+\epsilon,\\
0&r\geq r+\epsilon.\end{cases}

Let additionally
\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta}:=\eta_{\epsilon}*\psi_{\delta}
, where
(\psi_{\delta})_{\delta>0}
is a standard mollifier on
\mathbb{R}
. Define

(3.101)

\phi(x):=(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|),

where
A_{0}^{-1/2}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix such that
(A_{0}^{-1/2})^{2}=A_{0}^{-1}
. Using
\nabla|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|=\frac{A_{0}^{-1}(x-x_{0})}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|}
we compute

(3.102)

\nabla\phi(x)=\theta(x_{0})\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)+(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)\frac{A_{0}^{-1}(x-x_{0})}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|}.

By symmetry of
A_{0}
one has

(3.103)

\left(\frac{A_{0}^{-1}(x-x_{0})}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|},A_{0}\theta(x_{0})\right)=\frac{(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|}

and

(3.104)

\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|}\right)=\frac{n}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|}-\frac{(A_{0}^{-1}(x-x_{0}),x-x_{0})}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|^{3}}=\frac{n-1}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|},

whereupon one readily checks

(3.105)

\mathrm{div}(A_{0}\nabla\phi)=(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\left[(n+1)\frac{(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|}+(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime\prime}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)\right].

Hence there holds

(3.106)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}((A-A_{0})\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(A_{0}\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.107)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}u(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\Big[(n+1)\frac{(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|}+(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime\prime}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)\Big]\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.108)

\displaystyle\quad+S_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(r),

where

(3.109)

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(r)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}((A-A_{0})\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.110)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}((A-A_{0})\nabla u,\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)\theta(x_{0}))\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.111)

\displaystyle+\left((A-A_{0})\nabla u,(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)\frac{A_{0}^{-1}(x-x_{0})}{|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|}\right)\;\mathrm{d}x.

We remark that
S_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(r)
depends on
r
via the dependence of
\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta}
of
r
.
Notice that the supports of
(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(\cdot-x_{0})|)
and
(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime\prime}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(\cdot-x_{0})|)
lie in the closure of
x_{0}+A_{0}^{1/2}(B_{r+\epsilon}(0)\setminus B_{r}(0))\subset\subset\Omega
(if
\epsilon
is appropriately small). Hence we can substitute
y=A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})
and obtain

(3.112)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-S_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(r)

(3.113)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{det}(A_{0}^{1/2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x_{0}+A_{0}^{1/2}y)(A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)\Big[(n+1)\frac{(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(|y|)}{|y|}+(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime\prime}(|y|))\Big]\;\mathrm{d}y.

Looking at the second summand of integral we compute

(3.114)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x_{0}+A_{0}^{1/2}y)\big((A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime\prime}(|y|)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y

(3.115)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime\prime}(s)\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}u(x_{0}+A_{0}^{1/2}y)(A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\;\mathrm{d}s

(3.116)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(s)\frac{d}{ds}\left(\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}u(x_{0}+A_{0}^{1/2}y)(A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\right)\;\mathrm{d}s,

where (a.e.-)existence (and boundedness) of the derivative of the expression before is ensured by Lemma
B.3
.
We define now

(3.117)

h(s):=\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}u(x_{0}+A_{0}^{1/2}y)(A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

and observe that by (
3.112
)

(3.118)

\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(A_{0}^{1/2})}\left(\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-S_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(r)\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(s)h^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{\infty}(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}(s)\frac{n+1}{s}h(s)\;\mathrm{d}s.

Our goal is now to let
\delta\rightarrow 0
. We remark that
(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}
converges in
L^{1}((\frac{r}{2},2r))
to
\frac{1}{\epsilon}\chi_{[r,r+\epsilon]}
.
Since
h^{\prime}
and
s\mapsto\frac{n+1}{s}h(s)
are uniformly bounded in
(\frac{r}{2},2r)
and for
\epsilon,\delta
suitably small
(\eta_{\epsilon}^{\delta})^{\prime}
is only supported in
(\tfrac{r}{2},2r)
one can pass to the limit and obtains

(3.119)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(A_{0}^{1/2})}\left(\int_{\Gamma}Q(x)(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\eta_{\epsilon}(|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-S_{\epsilon}(r)\right)

(3.120)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{r}^{r+\epsilon}\left(h^{\prime}(s)-\frac{n+1}{s}h(s)\right)\;\mathrm{d}s,

where

(3.121)

S_{\epsilon}(r):=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}S_{\epsilon}^{\delta}(r).

The existence of this limit we will show later.
Letting also
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
we obtain for a.e.
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega))

(3.122)

h^{\prime}(r)-\frac{n+1}{r}h(r)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(A_{0}^{1/2})}\left(\int_{\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}_{r}(x_{0})}Q(x)(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+S(r)\right),

where
S(r):=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}S_{\epsilon}(r)
(which due to (
3.120
) must exist whenever
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{r}^{r+\epsilon}\left(h^{\prime}(s)-\frac{n+1}{s}h(s)\right)\;\mathrm{d}s
exists, i.e. at each Lebesgue point of
h^{\prime}
) and

(3.123)

\mathcal{E}_{r}(x_{0}):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|A_{0}^{-1/2}(x-x_{0})|<r\}=A_{0}^{1/2}B_{r}(A_{0}^{-1/2}x_{0}).

We infer

(3.124)

\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{h(r)}{r^{n+1}}\right)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(A_{0}^{1/2})}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}_{r}(x_{0})}Q(x)(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)+\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(A_{0}^{1/2})}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}S(r).

We claim next that (with
\omega_{n}:=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial B_{1}(0))
)

(3.125)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{h(r)}{r^{n+1}}\right)=\frac{\omega_{n}}{n}|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|^{2}.

To this end notice that by the Taylor expansion in Lemma
3.6
one has

(3.126)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{h(r)}{r^{n+1}}\right)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}u(x_{0}+A_{0}^{1/2}y)(A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(3.127)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}\left[u(x_{0})-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{Q(x_{0})}{(A_{0}\nu(x_{0}),\nu(x_{0})}|(A_{0}^{1/2}y,\nu(x_{0}))|+(\theta(x_{0}),A_{0}^{1/2}y)+o(r)\right]

(3.128)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot(A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

Since
y\mapsto\left[u(x_{0})-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{Q(x_{0})}{(A_{0}\nu(x_{0}),\nu(x_{0})}|(A_{0}^{1/2}y,\nu(x_{0}))|\right]
is even with respect to the transformation
y\mapsto-y
and
y\mapsto(A^{-1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)
is odd w.r.t this transformation we infer

(3.129)

\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}\left[u(x_{0})-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{Q(x_{0})}{(A_{0}\nu(x_{0}),\nu(x_{0})}|(A_{0}^{1/2}y,\nu(x_{0}))|\right](A^{-1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)=0.

Moreover,

(3.130)

\left|\frac{o(r)}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}(A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\right|\leq\frac{o(1)}{r^{n}}\omega_{n}r^{n-1}|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|r=o(1),

as
r\rightarrow 0
. Hence

(3.131)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{h(r)}{r^{n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}(A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0}),y)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(3.132)

\displaystyle=|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}(\tfrac{A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})}{|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|},y)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

We may now apply an orthogonal transformation that maps
\tfrac{A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})}{|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|}
to
e_{1}
and use the symmetry of
\partial B_{r}(0)
to find

(3.133)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{h(r)}{r^{n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}z_{1}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

(3.134)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}(z_{1}^{2}+...+z_{n}^{2})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

(3.135)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}r^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

(3.136)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\omega_{n}r^{n+1}=\frac{\omega_{n}}{n}|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|^{2}.

This shows (
3.125
). Using this and (
3.124
) we obtain for any
r_{0}\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega))

(3.137)

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{n}}{n}|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{h(r)}{r^{n+1}}\leq\frac{h(r_{0})}{r_{0}^{n+1}}+\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\left|\frac{d}{dr}\frac{h(r)}{r^{n+1}}\right|\;\mathrm{d}r

(3.138)

\displaystyle=\frac{h(r_{0})}{r_{0}^{n+1}}+\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\left|\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(A_{0}^{1/2})}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}_{r}(x_{0})}Q(x)(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\right|\;\mathrm{d}r

(3.139)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(A_{0}^{1/2})}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}|S(r)|=\textrm{(I) + (II) + (III)}.

We will estimate all the three summands appearing in this sum.

Term (I).

Estimating the integrand in (
3.117
) by its
L^{\infty}
-norm and using Lemma
2.4
(with
W^{1,p}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
for
p>n
) we find

(3.140)

\left|\frac{h(r_{0})}{r_{0}^{n+1}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{r_{0}^{n+1}}||u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}|A^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|\omega_{n}r_{0}^{n}\leq\frac{1}{r_{0}}C(\Omega,[\Gamma]_{0,1})|A_{0}^{1/2}\theta(x_{0})|\;||Q||_{L^{\infty}}.

Term (II).
Notice that

(3.141)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}_{r}(x_{0})}Q(x)(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)=\frac{Q(x_{0})}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}_{r}(x_{0})}(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

(3.142)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}_{r}(x_{0})}(Q(x)-Q(x_{0}))(\theta(x_{0}),x-x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

4.1.

The regularity requirement that

can not be substantially weakened. Indeed, we show that for some

the conclusion of Main Theorem

For our argument we will use an explicit representation for solutions in terms of the

for

Proposition 4.1

(
[

, p.44]
)
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{0,1},Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
and let
G_{\Omega}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be Green’s function for

in
\Omega
. Then the unique solution of

(4.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma,&\textrm{in }\Omega\\

(4.2)

Using this we find the following nonexistence result for Lipschitz solutions.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists a Lipschitz hypersurface
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{0,1}

\Omega=B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

(4.3)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma&\textrm{in }B_{1}(0),\\
\quad\;\;u=0&\textrm{on }\partial B_{1}(0)\end{cases}

admits no solution in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We look at the following rectangular equilateral triangle

(4.4)

\Gamma:=([0,\tfrac{1}{2}]\times\{0\})\cup(\{0\}\times[0,\tfrac{1}{2}])\cup\{(t,\tfrac{1}{2}-t):t\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]\}=:\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}\cup\Gamma_{3}.

Then clearly
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
for a domain

with Lipschitz boundary. Let now

be Green’s function for
-\Delta
in
B_{1}(0)
. Proposition
4.1
yields that then

(4.5)

u(x)=\int_{\Gamma}G(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\quad\forall x\in B_{1}(0).

Now we recall that

(4.6)

where

is the unique harmonic function such that

for all

. Let
\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{4})
be such that
B_{2\epsilon}(0)\cap\Gamma_{3}=\emptyset
. Since
x\mapsto h_{x}
depends smoothly on
x
in
B_{\epsilon}(0)
we have by (
4.5
) that

(4.7)

-2\pi u(x)=\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\log|x-y|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)+\int_{\Gamma_{2}}\log|x-y|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)+R(x)\quad\forall x\in B_{\epsilon}(0)

for some function
R
that is smooth on
\overline{B_{\epsilon}(0)}
. This implies that for
x\in B_{\epsilon}(0)\setminus(\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2})
there holds

(4.8)

-2\pi\partial_{1}u(x)=\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{x_{1}-y_{1}}{|x-y|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)+\int_{\Gamma_{2}}\frac{x_{1}-y_{1}}{|x-y|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)+\partial_{1}R(x).

Now for
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in B_{\epsilon}(0)\setminus\Gamma
one has

(4.9)

\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{x_{1}-y_{1}}{|x-y|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{x_{1}-t}{(x_{1}-t)^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}t=-\frac{1}{2}\log((x_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}+x_{2}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\log(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})

and

(4.10)

\int_{\Gamma_{2}}\frac{x_{1}-y_{1}}{|x-y|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}-t)^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}t=-\mathrm{arctan}\left(\frac{x_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}}{x_{1}}\right)+\mathrm{arctan}\left(\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\right).

Notice that the second integral lies in
L^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(0))
and the first integral takes the form
\log|x|+\tilde{R}
for some
\tilde{R}\in L^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(0))
(recalling that
\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}<\frac{1}{2}
). We infer that on
B_{\epsilon}(0)\setminus\Gamma
there holds

(4.11)

\partial_{1}u(x)=\log|x|+\bar{R}(x),

where
\bar{R}
lies in
L^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(0))
. It follows that
\partial_{1}u\not\in L^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

We have discussed in the introduction that for
\alpha\in[0,1)
one has that the
growth property

\mu(B_{r}(x))\leq Cr^{n-2+\alpha}
(for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r>0
) implies that the solution of the measure-valued Dirichlet problem (
1.5
) lies in
C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
. For Lipschitz manifolds, the growth property of the measure
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
is satisfied with
\alpha=1
, cf. Lemma
2.1
. However as we have seen, the solution does not necessarily lie in
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
. In
[
Kilpelainen
, Discussion after Theorem 2.9]
it is said that the limit case of the regularity statement for
\alpha=1
is not yet well-understood. Our counterexample shows that the growth property is not sufficient to deduce regularity.

Remark 4.4
.

This example can also be seen as an explicit example for failure of the
duality method
in the case of

p=\infty
, which we shall explain now. It is a classical result that for all
F\in L^{p}(\Omega)
,
1<p<\infty
the equation

(4.12)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\mathrm{div}(F)&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

has a unique weak solution
u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
, cf.
[
Auscher
]
and references therein. For
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, it is in general not true that
u\in W_{0}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
. This is shown by a very implicit contradiction argument in
[
PoissonMarius
, Remark 3.7]
. Using the insight above we can now give an explicit counterexample: Let
\Gamma\subset B_{1}(0)
be the triangle in the proof of Lemma
4.2
. By Lemma
2.1
there must exist some
F\in L^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))
such that in the sense of distributions
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma=\mathrm{div}(F)
. For such
F
we have seen in Lemma
4.2
that

(4.13)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\mathrm{div}(F)[=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma]&\textrm{in }B_{1}(0),\\
u=0&\textrm{on }\partial B_{1}(0)\end{cases}

has a solution which does not lie in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega).

4.2.
Optimality of the condition on
A

We show in this section that it is not possible to weaken the assumption of Main Theorem
1.2
to
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for any
q<n
. The case
A\in W^{1,n}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
is an interesting limit case and not yet fully understood, but the details would go beyond the scope of this article. Our example is just a slight modification of
[
Meyers
, Section 5]
, where regularity of (the gradient of)
A
-harmonic functions for irregular coefficients
A
is studied. The measure term actually has no major impact on the irregularity phenomenon exposed in
[
Meyers
, Section 5]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that
n=2
.
Then for each
q<n
there exists some
A\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
, some
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}
and some
Q\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
such that the solution to (
1.1
) does not lie in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We consider for
\mu\in(0,1)
the set
\Omega=B_{2}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\Gamma=\partial B_{1}(0)
. We define
A:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
via

(4.14)

A(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1-(1-\mu^{2})\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}+y^{2}}&(1-\mu^{2})\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\\
(1-\mu^{2})\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}&1-(1-\mu^{2})\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

One readily checks that
A
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic and
A\in W^{1,q}(B_{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
for all
q<2
.
Next we define

(4.15)

u_{1}(x,y):=\frac{x}{(x^{2}+y^{2})^{\frac{1-\mu}{2}}}\quad(x,y)\in B_{2}(0).

Moreover let
u_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B_{2}(0)}\setminus B_{1}(0))
be the unique solution to the
A
-harmonic Dirichlet problem

(4.16)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u_{2})=0&\textrm{in }B_{2}(0)\setminus\overline{B_{1}(0)},\\
\qquad\;\;u_{2}(x,y)=0&\textrm{on }\partial B_{1}(0),\\
\qquad\;\;u_{2}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2^{1-\mu}}x&\textrm{on }\partial B_{2}(0).\end{cases}

Such smooth solution exists as the restriction of
A
to
\overline{B_{2}(0)}\setminus B_{1}(0)
satisfies
A\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B_{2}(0)}\setminus B_{1}(0))
and the prescribed boundary data are smooth. We now extend
u_{2}
to the whole of
B_{2}(0)
by defining
u_{2}(x,y)=0
for all
(x,y)\in B_{1}(0)
. We will not rename this extension. Observe that this extension is Lipschitz continuous on
\overline{B_{2}(0)}
.
One readily checks that there holds (pointwise)
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u_{1})=0
on
B_{2}(0)\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u_{2})=0
on
B_{2}(0)\setminus\partial B_{1}(0)
.
Next we define
u:=u_{1}+u_{2}
, which clearly has zero boundary trace on
\partial B_{2}(0)
. As shown in
[
Meyers
, Section 5]
one has that
\nabla u_{1}\in L^{p}(B_{2}(0))
if and only if
p<\frac{2}{1-\mu}
. In particular
\nabla u=\nabla u_{1}+\nabla u_{2}\not\in L^{\infty}(B_{2}(0))
. It remains to show that
u
solves a problem of the form (
1.1
).
As
2<\frac{2}{1-\mu}
we obtain that
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(B_{2}(0))
and check that for
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{2}(0))
there holds

(4.17)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{2}(0)}(A\nabla u_{1},\nabla\phi)+\int_{B_{2}(0)\setminus B_{1}(0)}(A\nabla u_{2},\nabla\phi)

(4.18)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{B_{2}(0)\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}(A\nabla u_{1},\nabla\phi)+\int_{\partial(B_{2}(0)\setminus B_{1}(0))}(A\nabla u_{2},\nu)\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(4.19)

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}(A\nabla u_{1},\nu)\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}(A\nabla u_{2},(x,y)^{T})\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Noticing next that
A\in L^{\infty}(B_{2}(0);\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
we infer that

(4.20)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}(A\nabla u_{1},\nu)\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq||A||_{L^{\infty}}||\phi||_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}|\nabla u_{1}|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(4.21)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C||A||_{\infty}||\phi||_{\infty}}{\epsilon^{1-\mu}}(2\pi\epsilon)\rightarrow 0\quad(\epsilon\rightarrow 0).

In particular we obtain that for
Q:=-(A\nabla u_{2},(x,y)^{T})|_{\partial B_{1}(0)}\in C^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}(0))
one has

(4.22)

\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

By density and the fact that
A\in L^{\infty}
this also holds true for
\phi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.
In particular for arbitrary
\phi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
we may integrate by parts and obtain (as
u
has zero boundary trace)

(4.23)

\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad\forall\phi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega),

showing that
u
is a weak solution of an equation of type (
1.1
). The claim follows.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

The limit case
\mu\rightarrow 0
in the previous example shows actually that for general (symmetric and uniformly elliptic)
A\in L^{\infty}
no more regularity than
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
can be obtained.
In particular we observe that the best possible regularity depends vitally on the
W^{1,q}
-regularity of the coefficients in
A
. For
q<n
the optimal regularity is
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and for
q>n
the optimal regularity is
W_{0}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
. This jump shows again that the case
q=n
is very interesting.

5.
Applications to Free boundary problems

Equations like (
1.1
) appear in many free boundary problems, for example in the
thin obstacle problem
, cf
[
Fernandez
, Equation 3.8]
. Here we illustrate two further examples specifically, both of which consider
adhesive free boundary problems
.

5.1.
The Bernoulli problem

A solution of Bernoulli’s problem (in the sense of
[
Henrot
]
) is a pair consisting of a suitably smooth function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a suitably regular subset
S\subset\subset\Omega
that satisfies

(5.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\textrm{in }\Omega\setminus\overline{S},\\
\quad u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\quad u=1&\textrm{on }\partial S,\\
\;\;\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=Q&\textrm{on }S.\end{cases}

In
[
Henrot
]
it is observed that one can find a one-parameter family of solutions around a given (suitably regular, hyperbolic)
prototype solution
. This is done by means of a carefully chosen
evolution equation
. For the derivation of the evolution law it is possible to profit from
[
Henrot
, Proposition 4.3]
, saying that
u
is a solution of (
5.1
) if and only if

(5.2)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\partial S&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;u\equiv 1&\textrm{on }S.\end{cases}

This result requires some regularity of
S
and uses the
normal jump of the single layer potential
, cf. Remark
3.8
. Regularity of (
5.2
) can be studied with the methods developed in this article, with optimal regularity requirements on
S
. Possibly, this can also help for the study of the derived evolution equation under less regularity assumptions on the needed prototype solution. Moreover, the Bernoulli problem can be considered for other operators than
-\Delta
.

5.2.
The biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli Problem

Recently, the
biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli problem
has raised a lot of interest, cf.
[
Serena1
]
,
[
Serena2
]
,
[
AltCaffarelliMarius
]
,
[
AltCaffarelliMariusGrunau
]
. This is the minimization of

(5.3)

\mathcal{E}(u):=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta u)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{x\in\Omega:u(x)>0\}|

among all
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)
such that
u|_{\partial\Omega}=u_{0}
for some
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}),u_{0}>0
. Here
|\cdot|
denotes the Lebesgue measure. Minimizers of this problem have to find an optimal balance between
bending
(measured by the first summand) and
positivity
(measured by the second summand). Since
u_{0}>0
it will however require some bending to leave the positivity region, which is why the interests of both terms are conflicting.
The set
\Gamma:=\{u=0\}
is where the functional loses its regularity and is hence considered the
free boundary
of the problem. In
[
AltCaffarelliMarius
, Theorem 1.4]
it is shown that in dimension two (i.e. if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
) minimizers lie in
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
and satisfy
\nabla u\neq 0
on
\Gamma=\{u=0\}
. Moreover
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
for some
C^{2}
-smooth domain
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
. Additionally, each minimizer
u
satisfies the equation

(5.4)

\int_{\Omega}\Delta u\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall\phi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega).

Our results can therefore be used to study further regularity of minimizers. Indeed, if we define
w:=\Delta u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
then (
5.4
) yields that
w
is a very weak solution of

(5.5)

\begin{cases}-\Delta w=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
w=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

with
Q:=\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}\in C^{1}(\Gamma)
. We infer from our Main Theorem
1.2
that
w=\Delta u\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
. By elliptic regularity this also implies
u\in W^{3,p}(\Omega)
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. It will be subject of future research whether also
u\in W^{3,\infty}(\Omega)
. This would make
\Gamma=\{u=0\}
a
C^{2,1}
-manifold as
\nabla u
does not vanish on the level set
\Gamma
. We remark also that Theorem
1.2
paves the way for the study of the
A-
biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli problem
, i.e. the minimization of

(5.6)

\mathcal{E}(u):=\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u(x))|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{x\in\Omega:u(x)>0\}|

under the same boundary conditions as above.

Appendix A
Very weak solutions

In this appendix we show that (under mild conditions) very weak solutions are also weak solutions, as already discussed in the introduction. In the case of
A=Id_{n\times n}
this is already studied in
[
Ponce
, Section 3.1]
. The mild condition we need additionally is that
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
q>n
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth domain,
n\geq 2
. Suppose that
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
q>n
and let
p\in[2,n]
. Then for each
g\in L^{p}(\Omega)
there exists a unique
\psi\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
such that

(A.1)

\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\psi)=g\quad\textrm{pointwise a.e..}

Proof.

Let
(A^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
be such that
A^{(k)}\rightarrow A
in
W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
. We denote the coefficients of
A^{(k)}
by
a_{ij}^{(k)}
for
i,j=1,...,n
. By elliptic regularity (cf.
[
GilTru
, Theorem 9.15]
) there exist
\psi_{k}\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
such that (pointwise a.e.) there holds

(A.2)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}^{(k)}\partial^{2}_{ij}\psi_{k}+(\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)}),\nabla\psi_{k})=g,

i.e. pointwise a.e. there holds

(A.3)

\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)}\nabla\psi_{k})=g.

We define next the differential operator

(A.4)

L_{k}u:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}^{(k)}\partial^{2}_{ij}u.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
L_{k}
satisfies the prerequisites of
[
GilTru
, Lemma 9.17]
and therefore there exist constants
C_{k}
(cf.
[
GilTru
, Lemma 9.15]
) depending only on
\Omega
,
p
, the ellipticity constant of
A^{(k)}
and the
L^{\infty}
-norm of
A^{(k)}
and the moduli of continuity of
A^{(k)}
such that

(A.5)

||u||_{W^{2,p}}\leq C_{k}||L_{k}u||_{L^{p}}\quad\forall u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega).

As
A^{(k)}
converges to
A
in
C^{0,\gamma}
for
\gamma=1-\frac{n}{q}>0
the moduli of continuity and the ellipticity constants can all be uniformly controlled in
k
and hence one can find a uniform
\bar{C}>0
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
there holds

(A.6)

||u||_{W^{2,p}}\leq\bar{C}||L_{k}u||_{L^{p}}\quad\forall u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega).

The PDE (
A.2
) can now also be viewed as follows

(A.7)

L_{k}\psi_{k}=g-(\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)}),\nabla\psi_{k}).

We infer from (
A.6
) and Hölder’s inequality

(A.8)

\displaystyle||\psi_{k}||_{W^{2,p}}

\displaystyle\leq\bar{C}||L_{k}\psi_{k}||_{L^{p}}\leq\bar{C}||g-(\mathrm{div}(A^{k}),\nabla\psi_{k})||_{L^{p}}

(A.9)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{C}(||g||_{L^{p}}+||(\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)}),\nabla\psi_{k})||_{L^{p}})

(A.10)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{C}(||g||_{L^{p}}+||\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)})||_{L^{q}}||\nabla\psi_{k}||_{L^{\frac{qp}{q-p}}})

(A.11)

\displaystyle=\bar{C}(||g||_{L^{p}}+||\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)})||_{L^{q}}||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,\frac{qp}{q-p}}}),

We next intend to bound the right hand side uniformly in
k
.
Notice that
q>n
implies that
\frac{qp}{q-p}<\frac{np}{n-p}
and hence
W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
embeds compactly into

W_{0}^{1,\frac{qp}{q-p}}(\Omega)
. Since also
\frac{qp}{q-p}\geq p\geq 2
we observe that

(A.12)

W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\overset{c}{\hookrightarrow}W_{0}^{1,\frac{qp}{q-p}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)

forms a
Banach triple
(i.e. the first embedding is compact and the second is continuous). It follows that for each
\epsilon>0
there exists
D(\epsilon)>0
independent of
k
such that

(A.13)

||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,\frac{qp}{q-p}}}\leq\epsilon||\psi_{k}||_{W^{2,p}}+D(\epsilon)||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}.

Using this in (
A.11
) with
\epsilon:=\frac{1}{2\bar{C}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}||\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)})||_{L^{q}}+1}
(and calling
D:=D(\epsilon)=D(\bar{C},||A||_{W^{1,q}})
) we infer from (
A.11
) that

(A.14)

||\psi_{k}||_{W^{2,p}}\leq\frac{1}{2}||\psi_{k}||_{W^{2,p}}+\bar{C}||g||_{L^{p}}+\bar{C}D||\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)})||_{L^{q}}||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}.

In particular

(A.15)

||\psi_{k}||_{W^{2,p}}\leq 2\bar{C}(1+D)(||g||_{L^{p}}+||\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)})||_{L^{q}}||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}).

It remains to bound
||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}
uniformly in
k
. To that end we use (
A.3
),
which yields (if
\lambda_{k}
is the ellipticity constant of
A^{(k)}
)

(A.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi_{k}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\int_{\Omega}(A^{(k)}\nabla\psi_{k},\nabla\psi_{k})\;\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)}\nabla\psi_{k})\psi_{k}\;\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\int_{\Omega}g\psi_{k}\;\mathrm{d}x.

We infer

(A.17)

||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}^{2}\leq\frac{C_{1}(\Omega)}{\lambda_{k}}||g||_{L^{p}}||\psi_{k}||_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{2}(\Omega)}{\lambda_{k}}||g||_{L^{p}}||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}},

where
C_{i}(\Omega),i=1,2
depend on the volume and the optimal Poincare constant of
\Omega
. Since
A^{(k)}\rightarrow A
in
C^{0,\gamma}
one can also bound the reciprocals of the ellipticity constants
\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}
uniformly in
k
by
\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}}
. We infer

(A.18)

||\psi_{k}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}\leq\frac{C_{2}(\Omega)}{\tilde{\lambda}}||g||_{L^{p}}.

Using this uniform bound in (
A.15
) and the fact that
||\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)})||_{L^{q}}
is uniformly bounded in
k
we infer that
(\psi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in
W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
. Hence we may extract a weakly convergent subsequence (which we do not relabel) and a limit
\psi\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
such that
\psi_{k}\rightharpoonup\psi
weakly in
W^{2,p}
. We show now that
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\psi)=g
pointwise a.e.. To this end fix
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then using that
A^{(k)}\rightarrow A
in
C^{0,\gamma}
and
\psi_{k}\rightarrow\psi
in
W^{1,p}
we find

(A.19)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\psi)\eta

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla\psi,\nabla\eta)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}(A^{(k)}\nabla\psi_{k},\nabla\eta)\;\mathrm{d}x

(A.20)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(A^{(k)}\nabla\psi_{k})\eta\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}g\eta\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}g\eta\;\mathrm{d}x.

Since
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
was arbitrary we conclude
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\psi)=g
pointwise a.e..

Step 2.
Uniqueness. By linearity it suffices to show that for each
\psi\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
such that
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\psi)=0
a.e. in
\Omega
there must hold
\psi=0
a.e. in
\Omega
. To this end we choose
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
arbitrary and infer

(A.21)

0=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\psi)\eta\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla\psi,\nabla\eta)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Approximating
\psi
in
W_{0}^{1,2}
with
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
- functions we infer

(A.22)

0=\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla\psi,\nabla\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x\geq\lambda\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x,

whereupon we conclude that
\psi\equiv\mathrm{const}.
The fact that
\psi\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
yields that
\psi\equiv 0
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 2
be a smooth domain and
\Gamma\subset\subset\Omega
be a Lipschitz hypersurface. Further assume
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
and
A\in W^{1,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
. Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then the following are equivalent

(A.23)

\mathrm{(1)}\quad-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad\forall\phi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega),\quad\quad\quad

(A.24)

\mathrm{(1^{\prime})}\quad\;\;-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad\forall\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}):\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\quad\quad\;

(A.25)

\mathrm{(2)}\quad\textrm{ $u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)$ and }\quad\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).\;

Proof.

Equivalence of
(1)
and
(1^{\prime})
follows immediately by density, cf. Lemma
B.2
.
We show first
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. For fixed
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
observe that by Lemma
A.1
there exists some
\phi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, such that there holds (pointwise a.e.)

(A.26)

\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)=\mathrm{div}(f).

Multiplying by some
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and integrating we find

(A.27)

\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla\phi,\nabla\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(f)\psi\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Taking the closure, we find that this equation holds also true for all
\psi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.
We observe that

(A.28)

\lambda\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla\phi,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(f)\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(f,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields then
||\phi||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}=||\nabla\phi||_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}||f||_{L^{2}}
.
For the computation to come we recall that by Lemma
2.1

(A.29)

T(\psi):=\int_{\Gamma}Q\psi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad(\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega))

extends to a linear continuous functional in
(W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega))^{*}
. Using this we find

(A.30)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(f)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C_{1}||\phi||_{W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}||f||_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

In particular we infer that

(A.31)

S:C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad S(f):=-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(f)\;\mathrm{d}x

extends to a functional in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
, whereupon one finds
g\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

(A.32)

-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(f)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(g,f)\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Setting
f=ve_{j}
for
v\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
j=1,...,n
one readily checks that
u
is weakly differentiable and
\nabla u=g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We have obtained that
u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
. We next show (
A.25
). First notice that by smoothness of
\Omega
there exist
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
. By the classical Gauss divergence theorem we then infer for each
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
s.t.
\phi\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0
that

(A.33)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}u\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(A.34)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(-\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{n}(A(x)\nabla\phi,\nu_{\Omega})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u_{n},\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x\right)

(A.35)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega}(u)(A(x)\nabla\phi,\nu_{\Omega})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x,

where we have used continuity of the Sobolev trace operator in the last step. To simplify notation we write only
u
instead of
\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega}(u)
in the sequel.
Plugging in any
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
we infer that the integral formula in (
A.25
) holds true. It remains to show that
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, i.e.
\mathrm{tr}_{\partial\Omega}(u)=0
. To that end look at
\phi=\psi d_{\Omega}
, where
d_{\Omega}:B_{\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is the signed distance function for
\partial\Omega
,
\epsilon<\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega)
is chosen small enough such that
d_{\Omega}
is smooth, and
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(\partial\Omega))
is arbitrary. Then we have

(A.36)

0=\int_{\Gamma}Q(\psi d_{\Omega})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=-\int_{\partial\Omega}u(A(x)\nabla(\psi d_{\Omega}),\nu_{\Omega})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u,\nabla(\psi d_{\Omega}))\;\mathrm{d}x.

Since
\nabla d_{\Omega}=\nu_{\Omega}
on
\partial\Omega
we infer

(A.37)

\int_{\partial\Omega}u(A(x)\nabla(\psi d_{\Omega}),\nu_{\Omega})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\partial\Omega}u(A(x)\nu_{\Omega},\nu_{\Omega})\psi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Moreover, observe that
\psi d_{\Omega}\big|_{\Omega}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
and hence there exists
(\eta_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\eta_{m}\rightarrow\psi d_{\Omega}
in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
and also uniformly, see Lemma
B.1
.
Therefore we can rearrange the last summand in (
A.36
) (by using the already derived (
A.25
))

(A.38)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u,\nabla(\psi d_{\Omega}))\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u,\nabla\eta_{m})\;\mathrm{d}x

(A.39)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Gamma}Q\eta_{m}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\Gamma}Q(\psi d_{\Omega})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0.

Hence (
A.36
) yields

(A.40)

0=\int_{\partial\Omega}u(A(x)\nu_{\Omega},\nu_{\Omega})\psi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad\forall\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)).

This implies
u(A(x)\nu_{\Omega},\nu_{\Omega})=0
a.e. on
\partial\Omega
and thus by ellipticity of
A
one infers
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
. In particular
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and the claim is shown. Next we turn to
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. If
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
then there exists
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
u_{k}\rightarrow u
in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
. For each
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
s.t.
\phi\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0
we then infer

(A.41)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{k}\;\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u_{k},\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(A.42)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Now we observe that
\phi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
and we choose (due to Lemma
B.1
)
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\phi_{n}\rightarrow\phi
in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
and uniformly. Hence

(A.43)

\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla u,\nabla\phi_{n})\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

The previous two equations yield

(A.44)

-\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad\forall\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}):\phi\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

∎

Appendix B
Some approximation and regularity results

Here we collect some technical lemmas that have been useful for the course of our argument.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be
C^{1}
-smooth and
\eta\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
for some
q\in(1,\infty)
. Then there exists a sequence
(\eta_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\eta_{j}\rightarrow\eta
in
W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
and
\eta_{j}\rightarrow\eta
in
C(\overline{\Omega})
.

Proof.

For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
the function

(B.1)

\tilde{\eta}_{j}=\left(\eta-\frac{1}{j}\right)^{+}-\left(\eta+\frac{1}{j}\right)^{-}

has compact support in
\Omega
. Clearly,
\tilde{\eta}_{j}\rightarrow\eta
in
C(\overline{\Omega})
. Moreover, by the dominated convergence theorem and Stampacchia’s Lemma (cf.
[
KiStam
, Lemma A.4,Chapter 2]
) one infers

(B.2)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\tilde{\eta}_{j}-\nabla\eta|^{q}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\{|\eta|<\frac{1}{j}\}}|\nabla\eta|^{q}\;\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow\int_{\{\eta=0\}}|\nabla\eta|^{q}\;\mathrm{d}x=0.\quad(j\rightarrow\infty).

This also implies that
\tilde{\eta}_{j}\rightarrow\eta
in
W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
. Let now
(\psi_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
be the standard mollifier. For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
one can choose
\epsilon_{j}>0
such that
\eta_{j}:=\tilde{\eta}_{j}*\psi_{\epsilon_{j}}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and

(B.3)

||\tilde{\eta}_{j}*\psi_{\epsilon_{j}}-\tilde{\eta}_{j}||_{W^{1,q}(\Omega)}+||\tilde{\eta}_{j}*\psi_{\epsilon_{j}}-\tilde{\eta}_{j}||_{C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}<\frac{1}{j}.

A straightforward application of the triangle inequality and the previous observations shows that then

(B.4)

\eta_{j}:=\tilde{\eta}_{j}*\psi_{\epsilon_{j}}\rightarrow\eta\quad\textrm{in }W^{1,q}(\Omega)\;\textrm{and }C(\overline{\Omega}).

∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth domain. Then the closure of
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
with respect to the
W^{2,2}
-norm is
W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
v\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Then
v
lies in the domain of the
Dirichlet Laplacian

\Delta_{0}
, a densely defined and sectorial operator. Hence
\Delta_{0}
generates an analytic semigroup
(e^{t\Delta_{0}})_{t\geq 0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Next we set
v_{j}:=e^{\frac{1}{j}\Delta_{0}}v
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. We claim that
v_{j}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
v_{j}\rightarrow v
in
W^{2,2}(\Omega)
. The first assertion follows easily from the fact that by
[
Lunardi
, Proposition 2.1.1]

v_{j}
lies in the domain of
\Delta_{0}^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and hence actually in
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Moreover since
v
lies in the domain of
\Delta_{0}
,
[
Lunardi
, Proposition 2.1.4]
yields

(B.5)

||\Delta_{0}v-\Delta_{0}v_{j}||_{L^{2}}=||\Delta_{0}(e^{\frac{1}{j}\Delta_{0}}v-v)||_{L^{2}}\rightarrow 0\quad(j\rightarrow\infty).

Since
\Delta_{0}:W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is an isomorphism we infer that also
||v-v_{j}||_{W^{2,2}}\rightarrow 0
as
j\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be locally Lipschitz continuous. Then the map
I:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(B.6)

s\mapsto\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

is locally Lipschitz continuous on
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

(B.7)

\displaystyle I(r)-I(s)=\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)-\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(B.8)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)-\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{n-1}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(\tfrac{s}{r}y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(B.9)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{n-1}\right)\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)+\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{n-1}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}(f(y)-f(\tfrac{s}{r}y))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

Now for each
y\in\partial B_{r}(0)
one has

(B.10)

|f(y)-f(\tfrac{s}{r}y)|\leq||f||_{W^{1,\infty}(B_{s}(0)\setminus\overline{B_{r}(0)})}|y-\tfrac{s}{r}y|\leq||f||_{W^{1,\infty}(B_{s}(0)\setminus\overline{B_{r}(0)})}|r-s|

and

(B.11)

\displaystyle\left|\left(1-\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{n-1}\right)\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq||f||_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{r}(y))}\omega_{n}|r^{n-1}-s^{n-1}|

(B.12)

\displaystyle=||f||_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{r}(y))}\omega_{n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}r^{k}s^{n-2-k}\right)|r-s|.

With the previous two equations we infer

(B.13)

|I(r)-I(s)|\leq L(r,s)|r-s|,

where

(B.14)

L(r,s):=||f||_{W^{1,\infty}(B_{s}(0)\setminus\overline{B_{r}(0)})}+||f||_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{r}(y))}\omega_{n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}r^{k}s^{n-2-k}\right)

is clearly locally bounded in
(0,\infty)^{2}
. The local Lipschitz property follows.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

One has
BV(\Omega)\cap W^{1,1}_{loc}(\Omega)=W^{1,1}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

If
w\in BV(\Omega)\cap W^{1,1}_{loc}(\Omega)
then for all open sets
U\subset\subset\Omega
and
i=1,...,n
we have

(B.15)

\displaystyle\int_{U}|\partial_{i}w|\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sup_{\phi\in C_{0}^{1}(U),||\phi||_{\infty}\leq 1}\int_{U}\partial_{i}w\;\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\sup_{\phi\in C_{0}^{1}(U),||\phi||_{\infty}\leq 1}-\int_{U}w\;\partial_{i}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(B.16)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\phi\in C_{0}^{1}(U),||\phi||_{\infty}\leq 1}\int_{U}w\;\mathrm{div}(-\phi e_{i})\;\mathrm{d}x\leq|Dw|(U)\leq|Dw|(\Omega).

As
\Omega
can be monotonically exhausted by countably many sets
(U_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}},U_{j}\subset U_{j+1}\subset\subset\Omega
we infer by the monotonce convergence theorem that
|\partial_{i}w|\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. The claim follows.
∎

Lemma B.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain,
(v_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
be a sequence such that
(||v_{k}||_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in
k
. Then there exists a subsequence
(v_{l_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}
and some
v\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
such that
(v_{l_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
v
uniformly on
\overline{\Omega}
, weakly in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. Further, one has

(B.17)

||v||_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}||v_{l_{k}}||_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

One readily checks that
(v_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies all the prerequisites of the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, which explains the existence of a uniformly convergent subsequence and a limit
v\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. Choosing a further subsequence and using a standard diagonal sequence argument one can find a subsequence that converges weakly in
W^{1,r}(\Omega)
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
. One readily checks that the limit of this subsequence must also be
v
. Let now
(v_{l_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be this subsequence. If now
p\in(1,\infty)
is arbitrary we can choose some number
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r>p
. Observing that
W^{1,r}(\Omega)\subset W^{1,p}(\Omega)
(and hence
W^{1,p}(\Omega)^{*}\subset W^{1,r}(\Omega)^{*}
) we obtain that
v_{l_{k}}\rightharpoonup v
in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
. For the norm estimate (
B.17
) observe that by uniform convergence we have

(B.18)

||v||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}||v_{l_{k}}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Now for the gradients we observe

(B.19)

\displaystyle||\nabla v||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}||\nabla v||_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\limsup_{p\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}||\nabla v_{l_{k}}||_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

(B.20)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{p\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}||\nabla v_{l_{k}}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{p}}=\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}||\nabla v_{l_{k}}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

This and (
B.18
) yields

(B.21)

||v||_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}||v_{l_{k}}||_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}.

∎

Lemma B.6
.

Let
y\in C^{1}([a,b],\mathbb{R})
be such that
y^{\prime}(t)=f(t,y(t))
for all
t\in[a,b]
for some
f\in C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Then

(B.22)

||y||_{C^{1,\alpha}([a,b])}\leq||y||_{\infty}+[2+(b-a)^{1-\alpha}](1+||f||_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})})^{2}

Proof.

First derive the estimate

(B.23)

|y(t)-y(s)|=\int_{t}^{s}|f(u,y(u))|\;\mathrm{d}u\leq||f||_{\infty}|t-s|.

In particular

(B.24)

|y(t)-y(s)|\leq||f||_{\infty}(b-a)^{1-\alpha}|t-s|^{\alpha}.

Moreover we infer using (
B.23
)

(B.25)

\displaystyle|y^{\prime}(t)-y^{\prime}(s)|

\displaystyle=|f(t,y(t))-f(s,y(s))|\leq||f||_{C^{0,\alpha}}(|t-s|^{2}+|y(t)-y(s)|^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

(B.26)

\displaystyle=||f||_{C^{0,\alpha}}(1+||f||_{\infty}^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|t-s|^{\alpha}.

Now note that
(1+||f||_{\infty}^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\leq(1+||f||_{\infty})^{\alpha}\leq 1+||f||_{\infty}.
The previous computations yield

(B.27)

\displaystyle||y||_{C^{1,\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq||y||_{\infty}+||y^{\prime}||_{\infty}+[y]_{C^{0,\alpha}}+[y^{\prime}]_{C^{0,\alpha}}

(B.28)

\displaystyle\leq||y||_{\infty}+||f||_{\infty}+(b-a)^{1-\alpha}||f||_{\infty}+||f||_{C^{0,\alpha}}(1+||f||_{\infty}).

The claim follows estimating all
||f||_{\infty}
by
||f||_{C^{0,\alpha}}
and some elementary estimates.
∎

Lemma B.7
.

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be an open ball and
h\in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{B};\mathbb{R})
. For
z\in B
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m},|v|=1
we define
t_{+}(z,v)
to be the supremum of all
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
z+tv\in B
and similarly
t_{-}(z,v)
to be the infimum. Define

(B.29)

y_{z,v}(t):=h(z+tv)\quad t\in[t_{-}(z,v),t_{+}(z,v)].

Then

(B.30)

||h||_{C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{B})}\leq 2\sup_{z\in B,|v|=1}||y_{z,v}||_{C^{0,\alpha}([t_{-}(z,l),t_{+}(z,l)])}.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show that

(B.31)

||h||_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in B,|v|=1}||y_{z,v}||_{L^{\infty}([t_{-}(z,v),t_{+}(z,v)])}.

We next show the corresponding estimate for
[h]_{C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{B})}
. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in B
,
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
.
Define
z:=x_{1}
and
v:=\frac{x_{2}-x_{1}}{|x_{2}-x_{1}|}
now observe that by convexity of
B

(B.32)

z+tv=x_{1}+\tfrac{t}{|x_{2}-x_{1}|}(x_{2}-x_{1})\in B\quad\forall t\in[0,|x_{2}-x_{1}|].

this implies that
t_{-}(z,v)\leq 0\leq|x_{2}-x_{1}|\leq t_{+}(z,v)
. Now

(B.33)

\displaystyle|h(x_{2})-h(x_{1})|

\displaystyle=|y_{z,v}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)-y_{z,v}(0)|\leq[y_{z,v}]_{C^{0,\alpha}([t_{-}(z,v),t_{+}(z,v)]}|\;|x_{1}-x_{2}|-0|^{\alpha}

(B.34)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z\in B,|v|=1}[y_{z,v}]_{C^{0,\alpha}([t_{-}(z,v),t_{+}(z,v)]}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}.

We infer that
[h]_{C^{0,\alpha}(B)}\leq\sup_{z\in B,|v|=1}[y_{z,v}]_{C^{0,\alpha}([t_{-}(z,v),t_{+}(z,v)]}
and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma B.8
.

Let for
A\subset\mathbb{R}
closed and
\alpha>0

f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be
\alpha-
Hölder continuous in the sense that

(B.35)

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq H|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in A

for some
H>0
. Then there exists some
\bar{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\bar{f}|_{A}=f
and

(B.36)

|\bar{f}(x)-\bar{f}(y)|\leq H|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In particular
[\bar{f}]_{C^{0,\alpha}}=[f]_{C^{0,\alpha}}
.

Proof.

This is readily checked choosing

(B.37)

\bar{f}(z):=\inf_{x\in A}\left\{f(x)+H|z-x|^{\alpha}\right\}\quad(z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We remark that the subadditivity of
z\mapsto z^{\alpha}
plays an important role in this proof.
∎

Lemma B.9
.

Let
K\subset\Omega
be compact and
Q\in C^{0,\alpha}(K)
. Then there exists some
(Q_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
Q_{j}\rightarrow Q
uniformly on
K
and
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}||Q_{j}||_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}=||Q||_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
B.8
we may actually assume that
Q\in C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(without making the Hölder seminorm larger). Next let
Q_{\epsilon}:=Q*\phi_{\epsilon}
where
(\phi_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
is the standard mollifier. A standard result yields that
Q_{\epsilon}\rightarrow Q
uniformly on
K
. The lower semicontinuity of
[\cdot]_{C^{0,\alpha}}
with respect to uniform convergence yields

(B.38)

[Q]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}\leq\liminf_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}[Q_{\epsilon}]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}.

Now observe also that for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
there holds

(B.39)

\displaystyle|Q_{\epsilon}(x_{1})-Q_{\epsilon}(x_{2})|

\displaystyle=\left|\int Q(y)\phi_{\epsilon}(x_{1}-y)\;\mathrm{d}y-\int Q(y)\phi_{\epsilon}(x_{2}-y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|

(B.40)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int(Q(x_{1}-z)-Q(x_{2}-z))\phi_{\epsilon}(z)\;\mathrm{d}z\right|

(B.41)

\displaystyle\leq\int|Q(x_{1}-z)-Q(x_{2}-z)|\phi_{\epsilon}(z)\;\mathrm{d}z

(B.42)

\displaystyle\leq\int[Q]_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}|x_{1}-z-(x_{2}-z)|^{\alpha}\phi_{\epsilon}(z)\;\mathrm{d}z\leq[Q]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}.

This yields
[Q_{\epsilon}]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}\leq[Q]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}
and together with (
B.38
) we conclude that

[Q]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}[Q_{\epsilon}]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}
. All in all we infer that

(B.43)

||Q||_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}=||Q||_{\infty}+[Q]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}||Q_{\epsilon}||_{\infty}+[Q_{\epsilon}]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}||Q_{\epsilon}||_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)}.

∎

Appendix C
C^{1,\alpha}
-hypersurfaces

In this section we collect some facts about
C^{1,\alpha}
-boundaries that we use. In the following we will always look at
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
for a compact
C^{1,\alpha}
-domain
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

An important tool that we use is the
regularized signed distance function
,
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
introduced in
[
Lieberman
]
. This is a function
\rho\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Gamma)\cap C^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\rho<0
on
\Omega^{\prime}
,
\rho>0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
and

(C.1)

\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{|\rho(x)|}{\mathrm{dist}(x,\Gamma)}\leq 2,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\frac{1}{2}\leq|\nabla\rho(x)|\leq 2\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Gamma.

We see from
[
Lieberman
, Theorem 2.1]
(used with
\zeta(z)=z^{\alpha}
), that for
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{1,\alpha}
there exists a regularized signed distance function
\rho
, which fulfills in addition

(C.2)

|\nabla\rho(x)-\nabla\rho(y)|\leq 10[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(We remark that for obtaining the previous equation we need to demand that the graph representations
f_{i}
of
\Gamma
(cf. (
2.2
)) must lie in
C^{1,\alpha}(4\overline{U}_{i})
instead of just
C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{U}_{i})
. This is why the counterintuitive constant
4
appears in our definition of a representation).
In particular
\rho
extends to a
C^{1,\alpha}
-function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As
\rho\equiv 0
on
\Gamma
the derivative
\nabla\rho
must point in normal direction, which means that the outward unit normal
\nu:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies

(C.3)

\nu(x)=\frac{\nabla\rho(x)}{|\nabla\rho(x)|}\quad\forall x\in\Gamma.

This, (
C.2
) and the fact that
\frac{1}{2}\leq|\nabla\rho|\leq 2
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
implies

(C.4)

|\nu(x)-\nu(y)|\leq C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in\Gamma.

As a consequence one finds that if
\Pi_{x}
denotes the orthogonal projection on
T_{x}\Gamma
then (with
|\cdot|
denoting the operator norm) there holds

(C.5)

|\Pi_{x}-\Pi_{y}|\leq C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in\Gamma.

In the following we prove some further geometric estimates for
C^{1,\alpha}
-hypersurfaces and check that the constants appearing in them only depend on
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
. In the end we will show that one can approximate each
C^{1,\alpha}
-hypersurface
\Gamma
with
C^{\infty}
-hypersurfaces
(\Gamma_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
[\Gamma_{j}]_{1,\alpha}
is uniformly bounded in terms of
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
C^{1,\alpha}
-hypersurface. Then there exists
C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})>0
such that

(C.6)

(\nu(x),x-y)\leq C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})|x-y|^{1+\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in\Gamma.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\Gamma
be arbitrary.
The mean value theorem on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
yields that there exists some
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
on the line segement connecting
x
and
y
which satisfies

(C.7)

(\nabla\rho(\xi),x-y)=\rho(x)-\rho(y)=0.

We infer

(C.8)

(\nabla\rho(x),x-y)=(\nabla\rho(x)-\nabla\rho(\xi),x-y)\leq|\nabla\rho(x)-\nabla\rho(\xi)|\;|x-y|.

Using (
C.2
) we can bound the first factor by
10[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}|x-\xi|^{\alpha}
. Since
\xi
lies on the line segment connecting
x
and
y
we also have
|x-\xi|^{\alpha}\leq|x-y|^{\alpha}
. Hence we obtain

(C.9)

(\nabla\rho(x),x-y)\leq 10[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}|x-y|^{1+\alpha}.

The claim follows then from
\nu=\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}
and
|\nabla\rho|\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

Next we show that the intrinsic distance and the Euclidean norm are comparable on a
C^{1,\alpha}
hypersurface and the constant in the comparison estimate only depends on
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an
n-1
dimensional
C^{1,\alpha}
-hypersurface. Then there exists
C=C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})>1
such that for all
y,z\in\Gamma
one has
|y-z|\leq\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y,z)\leq C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})|y-z|
, where

(C.10)

\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y,z):=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{1}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|\;\mathrm{d}s:\gamma\in C^{1,\alpha}([0,1];\Gamma):\gamma(0)=y,\gamma(1)=z\right\}.

Proof.

We use
[
Blatt
, Theorem 1.1]
. For a compact
C^{1}
-submanifold
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
one can define

(C.11)

\gamma_{0}(S):=\max\{\gamma_{1}(S),\gamma_{2}(S)\}

where

(C.12)

\gamma_{1}(S):=\sup_{x\in S,R>0}\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},|v|=1}I_{x,R}(v),

(C.13)

I_{x,R}(v):=\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(S\cap B_{R}(x))}\int_{S\cap B_{R}(x)}|\nu(y)-v|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\quad(v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},|v|=1)

and

(C.14)

\gamma_{2}(S):=\inf_{x\in S,R>0}\sup_{y\in S\cap B_{R}(x),v\in\mathrm{arginf}I_{x,R}}\frac{(v,x-y)}{R},

where
\mathrm{arginf}
is meant among all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
s.t.
|v|=1
.
Now
[
Blatt
, Theorem 1.1]
states that there exists
\epsilon=\epsilon(n)>0
and
C=C(n)>0
such that
\gamma_{0}(S)\leq\epsilon
implies that

(C.15)

\left|\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(x,y)}{|x-y|}-1\right|\leq C(n)\gamma_{0}(S)\log\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{0}(S)}\quad\forall x,y\in\Gamma.

Next we look at the compact submanifold
S:=\Gamma\cap\overline{B_{s}(z)}\subset\Gamma
for some arbitrary
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
s>0
. Fix
x\in S
. One has (with
C=C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})
defined as in (
C.4
))

(C.16)

\displaystyle\inf_{|v|=1}I_{x,R}(v)\leq I_{x,R}(\nu(x))

\displaystyle=\fint_{S\cap B_{R}(x)}|\nu(y)-\nu(x)|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(C.17)

\displaystyle\leq C\fint_{S\cap B_{R}(x)}|y-x|^{\alpha}\leq C(2s)^{\alpha}.

and thus
\gamma_{1}(\Gamma\cap\overline{B_{s}(z)})\leq D([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})s^{\alpha}
. Moreover for each
v\in\mathrm{arginf}I_{x,R}
we have

(C.18)

\displaystyle|v-\nu(x)|

\displaystyle=\fint_{S\cap B_{r}(x)}|v-\nu(x)|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(C.19)

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{S\cap B_{r}(x)}|\nu(y)-v|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)+\fint_{S\cap B_{r}(x)}|\nu(y)-\nu(x)|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(C.20)

\displaystyle\leq 2\fint_{S\cap B_{r}(x)}|\nu(y)-\nu(x)|\leq 2D([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})s^{\alpha}.

Therefore we have that for each
v\in\mathrm{arginf}I_{x,R}
and
y\in S\cap B_{R}(x)
there holds

(C.21)

\displaystyle\frac{(v,x-y)}{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{(v-\nu(x),x-y)}{R}+\frac{(\nu(x),x-y)}{R}

(C.22)

\displaystyle\leq 2D([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})s^{\alpha}\frac{|x-y|}{R}+C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})\frac{|x-y|^{1+\alpha}}{R}

(C.23)

\displaystyle\leq 2D([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})s^{\alpha}\frac{|x-y|}{R}+C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})(2s)^{\alpha}\frac{|x-y|}{R}

(C.24)

\displaystyle\leq 2D([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})s^{\alpha}+C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})(2s)^{\alpha}.

This implies that

(C.25)

\gamma_{2}(\Gamma\cap\overline{B_{s}(z)})=E([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})s^{\alpha}.

From this and the discussion after (
C.17
) we infer that
s<s_{0}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})
implies that
\gamma_{0}(\Gamma\cap\overline{B_{s}(z)})\leq\epsilon(n)
and hence for all
s<s_{0}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})
one has

(C.26)

\left|\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma\cap\overline{B_{s}(z)}}(x,y)}{|x-y|}-1\right|\leq C(n)\sup_{t\in(0,\epsilon(n))}t\log\tfrac{1}{t}<\infty.

Now suppose that
x,y\in\Gamma
. If
|x-y|\geq\frac{1}{2}s_{0}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})
then one has

(C.27)

\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(x,y)}{|x-y|}\leq\frac{2\mathrm{diam}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma)}{s_{0}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})},

where
\mathrm{diam}_{\Gamma}
denotes the intrinsic diameter of
\Gamma
, which we bound later in terms of
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
. If
|x-y|<\frac{1}{2}s_{0}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})
then one has by (
C.26
)

(C.28)

\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(x,y)}{|x-y|}=\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma\cap B_{s}(x)}(x,y)}{|x-y|}\leq C(n)\sup_{t\in(0,\epsilon(n))}t\log\tfrac{1}{t}.

Choosing

(C.29)

C_{1}:=\frac{2\mathrm{diam}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma)}{s_{0}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})}+C(n)\sup_{t\in(0,\epsilon(n))}t\log\tfrac{1}{t}

we infer from the previous computations that
\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(x,y)\leq C_{1}|x-y|
. To show that
C_{1}
depends only on
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
it remains to prove that

\mathrm{diam}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma)
is bounded in terms of
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
. To this end pick a representation
(O_{i},U_{i},V_{i},f_{i}
,
i=1,...,M)
and let
\Gamma_{i}
be as in (
2.2
), i.e.
\Gamma_{i}\cap\Gamma_{i+1}\neq\emptyset
. Choose arbitrary
x_{i}\in\Gamma_{i}\cap\Gamma_{i+1}
for all
i=1,...,M
. Now let
y,z\in\Gamma
be arbitrary. Without loss of generality we may assume
y\in\Gamma_{i}
and
z\in\Gamma_{j}
for some
i\leq j
. Now we estimate

(C.30)

\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y,z)\leq\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y,x_{i})+\sum_{l=i}^{j-1}\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(x_{l+1},x_{l})+\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(z,x_{j}).

Observe that each summand is the distance of two points lying in the same subset
\Gamma_{i}
. For any two
y_{1},y_{2}\in\Gamma_{i}
, corresponding to the values
(z_{1},f(z_{1})),(z_{2},f(z_{2}))
for some
z_{1},z_{2}\in U_{i}
one sees that

(C.31)

\gamma(t):=O_{i}((z_{1}+t(z_{2}-z_{1}),f(z_{1}+t(z_{2}-z_{1})))

lies in
C^{1,\alpha}([0,1];\Gamma)
and connects
y_{1},y_{2}
. (Notice that we have used here that
U_{i}
is a ball). Therefore

(C.32)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\;\mathrm{d}t\leq|z_{1}-z_{2}|+|f(z_{1})-f(z_{2})|\leq(1+||Df_{i}||_{\infty})|z_{1}-z_{2}|

(C.33)

\displaystyle\leq(1+||Df_{i}||_{\infty})|y_{1}-y_{2}|\leq(1+||Df_{i}||_{\infty})\mathrm{diam}(\Gamma)\leq C([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}).

We infer from (
C.30
) (and the fact that
M
is bounded by
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
, cf. (
2.2
) f.) that

(C.34)

\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y,z)\leq MC([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})\leq\tilde{C}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}).

Since
y,z\in\Gamma
were arbitrary the diameter bound follows.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an
(n-1)
-dimensional
C^{1,\alpha}
-hypersurface. Then there exists some
r_{1}=r_{1}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})>0
such that for all
r<r_{1}
and for all
x_{0}\in\Gamma
the hypersurface
\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x_{0})
can be covered by a single graph of a
C^{1,\alpha}
-function
u:W\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with vanishing gradient at the preimage of
x_{0}
and Lipschitz-norm bounded by 1. The radius
r_{1}
and
||u||_{C^{1,\alpha}}
depend only on
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
.

Proof.

Let
C_{1}=C_{1}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})
be the constant from Lemma
C.2
and
C_{2}=C_{2}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})
be the constant from (
C.5
).
Next we let
r_{1}=r_{1}([\Gamma]_{1,\alpha})>0
be chosen such that

(C.35)

r_{1}^{\alpha}\min\{2^{\alpha}(1+C_{1})^{\alpha}C_{1}C_{2},C_{2}\}=\frac{1}{2}.

Now fix
x\in\Gamma
arbitrary. Let
\Pi_{x}^{\perp}=I-\Pi_{x}
be the orthogonal projection on
T_{x}\Gamma^{\perp}
. Let
r\leq r_{1}
and
y,z\in\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)
be arbitrary. Fix
\epsilon>0
and choose some
\gamma\in C^{1,\alpha}([0,1];\Gamma)
such that
\gamma(0)=y
,
\gamma(1)=z
,
L(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|\;\mathrm{d}s\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y,z)
.
Observe that for all
t\in[0,1]
one has
\gamma^{\prime}(t)\in T_{\gamma(t)}\Gamma
and
\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(\gamma(t),y)\leq L(\gamma|_{[0,t]})\leq L(\gamma)\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{dist}(y,z)
. We compute

(C.36)

\displaystyle|\Pi_{x}^{\perp}y-\Pi_{x}^{\perp}z|=\left|\Pi_{x}^{\perp}\int_{0}^{1}\gamma^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\Pi_{x}^{\perp}\gamma^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\Pi_{x}^{\perp}\Pi_{\gamma(s)}\gamma^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s\right|

(C.37)

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\Pi_{x}^{\perp}(\Pi_{\gamma(s)}-\Pi_{x})\gamma^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\int_{0}^{1}|\Pi_{x}^{\perp}||\Pi_{\gamma(s)}-\Pi_{x}||\gamma^{\prime}(s)|\;\mathrm{d}s\underset{\eqref{eq:C2}}{\leq}C_{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\gamma(s)-x|^{\alpha}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|\;\mathrm{d}s

(C.38)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\int_{0}^{1}(|\gamma(s)-y|+|y-x|)^{\alpha}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|\;\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(\gamma(s),y)+r)^{\alpha}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|\;\mathrm{d}s

(C.39)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}((1+\epsilon)\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(z,y)+r)^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|\;\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{2}((1+\epsilon)\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(z,y)+r)^{\alpha}(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y,z)

(C.40)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}((1+\epsilon)(\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(z,x)+\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(x,y))+r)^{\alpha}(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(y,z)

(C.41)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}C_{1}((1+\epsilon)C_{1}(|x-z|+|y-z|)+r)^{\alpha}(1+\epsilon)|y-z|

(C.42)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)C_{1}C_{2}(2(1+\epsilon)C_{1}+1)^{\alpha}r^{\alpha}|y-z|.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and using
r\leq r_{1}
we obtain

(C.43)

|\Pi_{x}^{\perp}y-\Pi_{x}^{\perp}z|\leq\frac{1}{2}|y-z|\leq\frac{1}{2}|\Pi_{x}y-\Pi_{x}z|+\frac{1}{2}|\Pi_{x}^{\perp}y-\Pi_{x}^{\perp}z|.

Rearranging we obtain

(C.44)

|\Pi_{x}^{\perp}y-\Pi_{x}^{\perp}z|\leq|\Pi_{x}y-\Pi_{x}z|\quad\forall y,z\in\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x),

and one also concludes immediately

(C.45)

|y-z|\leq\sqrt{2}|\Pi_{x}y-\Pi_{x}z|\quad\forall y,z\in\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x).

We conclude from (
C.44
) that
\Pi_{x}
is injective on
\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)
, call
W
its image, and infer from (
C.45
) that the inverse of
(\Pi_{x}|_{\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)})^{-1}:W\rightarrow\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)
is Lipschitz continuous. We infer that
\Pi_{x}:\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)\rightarrow W
is a homeomorphism.
Hence
\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)
is a Lipschitz graph over
T_{x}\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)
is a
C^{1,\alpha}
-submanifold, one obtains that this graph representation must be a
C^{1,\alpha}
-graph.
This means

(C.46)

\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)=O\{(x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime})):x^{\prime}\in W\}

for some
C^{1,\alpha}
function
f:W\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and some orthogonal matrix
O
such that
O\cdot\mathrm{span}(e_{1},...,e_{n-1})=T_{x}\Gamma
.
By the choice of
O
we also infer
\nabla f(x^{\prime})=0
if
x^{\prime}
is the preimage of
x
. The Lipschitz continuity with constant
1
of
f
follows immediately from (
C.44
). It only remains to show that the
C^{1,\alpha}
-norm of
f
can be bounded in terms of
[\Gamma]_{1,\alpha}
. Notice first that the Lipschitz estimate implies
||\nabla f||_{L^{\infty}(W)}\leq 1
. Define
h(z):=\frac{z}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}}
and observe that for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in B_{1}(0)
one has

(C.47)

|z_{1}-z_{2}|=|h^{-1}(h(z_{1}))-h^{-1}(h(z_{2}))|\leq||\nabla h^{-1}||_{\infty}|h(z_{1})-h(z_{2})|\leq\sqrt{2}|h(z_{1})-h(z_{2})|.

Therefore we obtain for
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in W

(C.48)

\displaystyle|\nabla f(x^{\prime})-\nabla f(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}|h(\nabla f(x^{\prime}))-h(\nabla f(y^{\prime}))|\leq\sqrt{2}|\nu((x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime})))-\nu((y^{\prime},f(y^{\prime}))|

Appendix D
The space
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})

In this section we prove some useful integral formulas that hold for functions in the set
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Here we derive some important tools to deal with functions in this class.

We first recall some facts about
BV
-functions.
For
v\in BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open one can define

(D.1)

|Dv|(U):=\sup_{\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(U;\mathbb{R}^{n}),||\phi||\leq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v\;\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x.

By
[
EvGar
, Chapter 5]

|Dv|
extends uniquely to a finite Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, called the
total variation measure
. This measure has the property that there exists a Borel measurable function
\sigma_{v}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
|\sigma_{v}|=1
a.e. and

(D.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v\;\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\phi,\sigma_{v})\;\mathrm{d}|Dv|\quad\forall\phi\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We further define for
v\in BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the
precise representative

(D.3)

v^{*}(x):=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}v(y)\;\mathrm{d}y,

which exists for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
[
EvGar
, Theorem 5.20]
(and not just Lebesgue almost-everywhere). If
(\phi_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
is the standard mollifier (defined as in
[
EvGar
, Section 4.2]
) one can define
v_{\epsilon}:=v*\phi_{\epsilon}
which then lies in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For
v\in BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})
it is shown in
[
EvGar
, Theorem 5.20]
that
v_{\epsilon}\rightarrow v^{*}
as
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e. as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
(and not just Lebesgue almost everywhere). We will need also the following

Lemma D.1
.

Let
v\in BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open. Then for any
\epsilon>0
there holds

(D.4)

\int_{U}|\nabla v_{\epsilon}(x)|\;\mathrm{d}x\leq|Dv|(B_{\epsilon}(U))

Proof.

Let
U,v
be as in the statement. Then

(D.5)

\displaystyle\int_{U}|\partial_{i}v_{\epsilon}(x)|\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(y)\partial_{x_{i}}\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{U}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(y)\partial_{y_{i}}\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|\;\mathrm{d}x

(D.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(y)\mathrm{div}(\phi_{\epsilon}(x-\cdot)e_{i})(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|\;\mathrm{d}x

(D.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)(\sigma_{v}(y),e_{i})\;\mathrm{d}|Dv|(y)\right|\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{U}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)\mathrm{d}|Dv|(y)\;\mathrm{d}x

(D.8)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{U}\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}x\right)\;\mathrm{d}|Dv|(y)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\chi_{\overline{B_{\epsilon}(U)}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}|Dv|(y)

(D.9)

\displaystyle\leq|Dv|(\overline{B_{\epsilon}(U)}),

where the last step is due to the fact that

(D.10)

0\leq\int_{U}\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}x=1

and if
y\not\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}(U)}
then the fact that
\mathrm{spt}(\phi_{\epsilon})\subset\overline{B_{\epsilon}(0)}
implies

(D.11)

\int_{U}\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

∎

Lemma D.2

(Radial integration for
BV
-functions)
.

Let
v\in BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
have compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

(D.12)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}v^{*}(x)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\;\mathrm{d}s

Proof.

Notice that
\fint_{B_{r}(x)}v(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\rightarrow v^{*}(x)
as
r\rightarrow 0
for Lebesgue a.e
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
|v^{*}(x)|\leq||v||_{L^{\infty}}\chi_{B_{1}(\mathrm{spt}(v))}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r\in(0,1)
. Thus the dominated convergence theorem yields

(D.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v^{*}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x)}v(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Now observe that for fixed
r\in(0,1)
the map
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},x\mapsto\fint_{B_{r}(x)}v(y)\;\mathrm{d}y
is continuous. Indeed this follows immediately from the estimate

(D.14)

|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|\leq\frac{|B_{r}(x_{1})\Delta B_{r}(x_{2})|}{|B_{r}(0)|}||v||_{L^{\infty}}.

Since radial integration is available for continuous functions one has

(D.15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x)}v(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\;\mathrm{d}s.

We can now use the dominated convergence theorem
again to switch the limit and the first two integrals (with the same dominating function as above) and obtain

(D.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}v^{*}(x)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\;\mathrm{d}s.

∎

Lemma D.3
.

Let
v\in BV(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

.
Let

Then
(0,\infty)\setminus A
is countable and the map
I:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(D.17)

I(r):=\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(x,v^{*}(x))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

is continuous on
A
.

Proof.

Since
t\mapsto|Dv|(B_{t}(0))
is monotone it can only have a countable set of discontinuities.
Hence
(0,\infty)\setminus A
is countable.
We notice that at each point

there holds

(D.18)

\displaystyle|Dv|(\partial B_{s}(0))

\displaystyle=|Dv|(\overline{B_{s}(0)}\setminus B_{s}(0))=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|Dv|(B_{s+1/n}(0)\setminus B_{s}(0))

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|Dv|(B_{s+1/n}(0))-|Dv|(B_{s}(0))=0.

. Let
(\phi_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
be the standard mollifier. Since by
[

and

\displaystyle I(r)-I(s)=\left|\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(x,v^{*}(x))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)-\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}f(x,v^{*}(x))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\left|\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)-\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\left|\frac{1}{r}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}(f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x))x,\tfrac{x}{r})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)-\frac{1}{s}\int_{\partial B_{s}(0)}(f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x))x,\tfrac{x}{s})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\right|

(D.24)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\left|\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}(0)}\mathrm{div}(f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x))x)\;\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{s}\int_{B_{s}(0)}\mathrm{div}(f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x))x)\;\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\left|\frac{n}{r}\int_{B_{r}(0)}f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x))\;\mathrm{d}x-\frac{n}{s}\int_{B_{s}(0)}f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x))\;\mathrm{d}x\right|

(D.26)

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\left|\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}(0)}(D_{x}f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x)),x)\;\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{s}\int_{B_{s}(0)}(D_{x}f(x,v_{\epsilon}(x)),x)\;\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{n}{r}\int_{B_{r}(0)}f(x,v^{*}(x))\;\mathrm{d}x-\frac{n}{s}\int_{B_{s}(0)}f(x,v^{*}(x))\;\mathrm{d}x\right|

(D.30)

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}(0)}(D_{x}f(x,v^{*}(x)),x)\;\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{s}\int_{B_{s}(0)}(D_{x}f(x,v^{*}(x)),x)\;\mathrm{d}x\right|.

\displaystyle\textrm{(III)}=

\displaystyle\leq M\left|1-\frac{r}{s}\right||Dv|(\mathbb{R}^{n})+M|Dv|(\overline{B_{s+\epsilon}(0)}\setminus B_{r-\epsilon}(0)).

-functions we find

(E.15)

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{j}\left(\tfrac{Q_{0}}{2}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{j}||\phi||_{\infty}\quad\forall\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma);\mathbb{R}^{n}),

implying that
\partial_{j}\left(\tfrac{Q_{0}}{2}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)\in BV(B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
. Since
j\in\{1,...,n\}
was arbitrary the claim is shown.
∎

Lemma E.3
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{k}
,
k\geq 2
and
Q_{0}\in W^{k,s}(\Omega)
,
s>n
. Then
\left(\frac{Q_{0}}{2}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)\in W^{k,s}(\Omega^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))\cap W^{k,s}(\Omega^{\prime\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the fact that on
\Omega^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)
there holds

(E.16)

\left(\frac{Q_{0}}{2}|d_{\Gamma}|\right)=-\frac{Q_{0}}{2}d_{\Gamma},

and
d_{\Gamma}
extends to a function in
C^{k}(\overline{\Omega}^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))=C^{k}((\Omega^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))\cup\Gamma)
. Hence all derivatives up to order
k
are bounded. The product rule in
W^{k,s}
yields then the desired
W^{k,s}(\Omega^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma))
-regularity.
On
\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}\cap B_{\epsilon}(\Gamma)
the same regularity can be obtained analogously.
∎

Appendix F
A maximum principle for
BV
-solutions

In this appendix we introduce a nonclassical maximum principle for so-called
BV
-solutions
of

(F.1)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u)=0&\textrm{in }D,\\
u=h&\textrm{on }\partial D.\end{cases}

This concept of solution is defined as follows.

Definition F.1
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{\infty}
-smooth domain,
A\in W^{2,s}(D;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})

(s>n)
and
h\in L^{1}(\partial D)
. We say
u\in BV(D)
is a
BV
-solution
of (
F.1
) if
\mathrm{tr}_{\partial D}(u)=h
and

(F.2)

\int_{D}u(x)\;\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\eta(x))\;\mathrm{d}x=0\quad\forall\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(D).

If a
BV
-solution
u
lies additionally in
W^{1,p}(D)
for some
p\in(1,\infty)
then it is easy to see that it is a usual weak solution of the problem and hence established methods like regularity theory, maximum principles etc. can be used. However it is not clear whether a
BV
-solution lies in some higher Sobolev space.
It was a longstanding conjecture by Serrin (cf.
[
Serrin
]
) under which conditions on the coefficient matrix
A(x)
further interior regularity can be obtained. In
[
Ancona
, Theorem A.1.2]
, Brezis presents a presumably optimal condition: For Dini-continuous coefficients
A(x)
further interior regularity can be obtained. We will use this further regularity to obtain a maximum principle.

Lemma F.2
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{\infty}
-smooth domain,
A\in W^{2,s}(D;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
,
(s>n)
and suppose that
\psi\in BV(D)
is a BV-solution of (
F.1
) for some
h\in W^{1,s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then

(F.3)

||\psi||_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leq||h||_{L^{\infty}(\partial D)}

Proof.

Observe first that by
[
Ancona
, Theorem A.1.2]

\psi\in BV(D)\cap W^{1,2}_{loc}(D)
. This in particular implies (cf. Lemma
B.4
) that
\psi\in W^{1,1}(D)
and

(F.4)

\int_{\Omega}(A(x)\nabla\psi(x),\nabla\eta(x))\;\mathrm{d}x=0\quad\forall\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

We infer that
\psi
is a
weak
W^{1,1}
-solution
of

(F.5)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla\psi(x))=0&\textrm{in }D,\\
\psi=h&\textrm{on }\partial D.\end{cases}

in the sense that

\mathrm{tr}_{\partial D}(\psi)=h
and (
F.4
) holds. We next observe that the concept of a
W^{1,1}
-solution just introduced is a notion that characterises
\psi
uniquely.
Indeed, if
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
are two
W^{1,1}
-solutions of (
F.5
) (i.e. satisfying (
F.4
) and having the same boundary trace) then
\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}
lies in
W_{0}^{1,1}(D)
and also satisfies (
F.4
). By
[
Ancona
, Theorem A5.1, second sentence]
we infer
\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}=0
.
We claim next that for each
h\in W^{1,s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
there exists a weak
W^{1,1}
-solution of (
F.5
) which additionally lies in
W^{1,s}(D)
. To this end notice that
A\nabla h\in L^{s}(D)
and hence by
[
Auscher
, Theorem 1.1]
there exists a unique
\tilde{\psi}\in W_{0}^{1,s}(D)
weak solution of

(F.6)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla\tilde{\psi})=\mathrm{div}(A\nabla h)&\textrm{in }D,\\
\tilde{\psi}=0&\textrm{on }\partial D.\end{cases}

We now define
\bar{\psi}:=\tilde{\psi}+h
and assert that
\bar{\psi}
is a weak
W^{1,1}
-solution. Indeed,
\mathrm{tr}_{\partial D}(\bar{\psi})=\mathrm{tr}_{\partial D}(\tilde{\psi}+h)=0+h|_{\partial D}=h
on
\partial D
. Moreover for each
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
there holds by (
F.6
)

(F.7)

\displaystyle\int_{D}(A(x)\nabla\bar{\psi}(x),\nabla\eta(x))\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{D}(A(x)\nabla\tilde{\psi}(x),\nabla\eta(x))\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{D}(A\nabla h(x),\nabla\eta(x))\;\mathrm{d}x

(F.8)

\displaystyle=-\int_{D}(A\nabla h(x),\nabla\eta(x))\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{D}(A\nabla h(x),\nabla\eta(x))\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

By the previous uniqueness discussion for
W^{1,1}
-solutions of (
F.5
) we have
\psi=\bar{\psi}
and thus
\psi\in W^{1,s}(D)
. In particular,
\psi\in C(\overline{D})
by Sobolev embedding. Now, the classical maximum principle implies the claim.
∎
