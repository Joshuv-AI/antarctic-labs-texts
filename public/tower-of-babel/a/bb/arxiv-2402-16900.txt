The time-fractional heat equation driven by fractional time-space white noise
Rahma Yasmina Moulay Hachemi & Bernt Øksendal

Abstract
We give an introduction to the time-fractional stochastic heat equation driven by 1+d-parameter fractional time-space white noise, in the following two cases: (i) With additive noise (ii) With multiplicative noise. The fractional time derivative is interpreted as the Caputo derivative of order α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ) \alpha\in(0,2) and we assume that the Hurst coefficient H = ( H 0 , H 1 , H 2 , … , H d ) H=(H_{0},H_{1},H_{2},...,H_{d}) of the time-space fractional white noise is in ( 1 2 , 1 ) 1 + d (\tfrac{1}{2},1)^{1+d} . We find an explicit expression for the unique solution in the sense of distribution of the equation in the additive noise case (i). In the multiplicative case (ii) we show that there is a unique solution in the Hida space ( 𝒮 ) ∗ (\mathcal{S})^{*} of stochastic distributions and we show that the solution coincides with the solution of an associated fractional stochastic Volterra equation.Then we give an explicit expression for the solution of this Volterra equation. A solution Y ⁡ ( t , x ) Y(t,x) is called mild if E ⁡ [ Y 2 ​ ( t , x ) ] < ∞ E[Y^{2}(t,x)]<\infty for all t , x t,x . For both the additive noise case and the multiplicative noise case we show that if α ≥ 1 \alpha\geq 1 then the solution is mild if d = 1 d=1 or d = 2 d=2 , while if α < 1 \alpha<1 the solution is not mild for any d d . The paper is partly a survey paper, explaining the concepts and methods behind the results. It is also partly a research paper, in the sense that some results appears to be new.

1
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to study the time-fractional heat equation driven by multi-parameter fractional Brownian motion. We discuss both the additive noise case and the case with multiplicative noise. Using basic methods of Laplace and Fourier transforms combined with white noise calculus we obtain explicit solution formulas in the sense of distribution for any order
\alpha\in(0,2)
of the time -derivative and for any space dimension
d
. Then we investigate for what values of
\alpha
and
d
the solution has finite second moment.

It was Niels Henrik Abel who first introduced the fractional derivative of a function.
In 1823 [1] he used the fractional derivative to solve the tautochrone (isochrone)
problem in mechanics.
Then in 1903 [22] Gosta Magnus Mittag-
Leffler introduced what was later called the the Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}(z)
. Subsequently it became clear that this function has a connection to
the fractional derivative introduced by Abel.

As a modelling tool the fractional derivative of order
\alpha\in(0,2)
is useful for many situations, e.g. in the
study of heat transfer and waves. For an example see Section 9. For many applications
of fractional derivatives we refer to the book by S. Holm [15].

- The
classical diffusion case
corresponds to
\alpha=1
. In that case our equation models the normal diffusion of
heat in a random or noisy medium, the noise being represented by the fractional
time-space white noise
W_{H}(t,x)
.

- The
superdiffusion case
corresponds to
\alpha>1
. Then the equation models a situation where
the particles spread faster than in regular diffusion. This may occur for example in some
biological systems.

- The
subdiffusion case
occures when
\alpha<1
. Then the equation models a situation in which travel times of the
particles are longer than in the standard case. Such situations may occur for example in some transport systems. For more details see
[
10
]
.

Specifically, in this paper we study the following two time-fractional stochastic heat equations driven by fractional time-space white noise:

Additive noise:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y(t,x)=\lambda\Delta Y(t,x)+\sigma W_{H}(t,x);\;(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.1)

Multiplicative noise:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y(t,x)=\lambda\Delta Y(t,x)+\sigma Y(t,x)\diamond W_{H}(t,x);\;(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.2)

where
d\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,...\}
and
\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}
is the Caputo derivative of order
\alpha\in(0,2)
, and
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
are given constants.
Here

\Delta Y=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}Y}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}(t,x)

(1.3)

denotes the Laplacian operator acting on
x
,
W_{H}(t,x)
is multiparameter fractional white noise, defined by

W_{H}(t,x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial^{d}B_{H}(t,x)}{\partial x_{1}...\partial x_{d}},

(1.4)

where
B_{H}(t,x)=B(t,x,\omega);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\omega\in\Omega
is time-space fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H=(H_{0},H_{1},...,H_{d})\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)^{d+1}
with probability law
\mathbb{P}
, and
\diamond
denotes the Wick product.

The boundary conditions are

\displaystyle Y(0,x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}(x)\text{ (the point mass at }0),

(1.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow+/-\text{ }\infty}Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=0.

(1.6)

We consider the equation (
1
) in the sense of distribution, and we find an explicit expression for the
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
-valued solution
Y(t,x)
, where
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is the space of tempered distributions. See Theorem
6.2
.

The equation (
1
) is considered in the Hida space
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
of stochastic distributions. We show that
Y(t,x)
solves (
1
) if and only if
Y(t,x)
solves a Volterra equation driven by the same fractional Brownian motion. See Theorem
7.2
.

Then we find an explicit expression for the solution of such Volterra equations. See Theorem
8.2
.

The solution is called
mild
if
Y(t,x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
for all
t,x
. It is well-known (see e.g. Y. Hu
[
14
]
) that in the classical case with
\alpha=1
, the solution is mild if and only if the space dimension
d=1
. We prove that if
\alpha\in(1,2)
the solution is mild if
d=1
or
d=2
.
If
\alpha<1
we prove that the solution is not mild for any
d
. This holds for both the additive noise and the multiplicative noise case and for all Hurst vectors
H=(H_{0},H_{1},...,H_{d})\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)^{1+d}
. See Theorem
6.5
and Theorem
8.3
.

A crucial base for our results is our
L^{2}(P)
-estimate for WIS-stochastic integrals with respect to fractional Brownian motion (Theorem
5.1

Preliminaries

In this section we summarise some of the background notation and results needed in this paper.

2.1
The space of tempered distributions

For the convenience of the reader we recall some of the basic properties of the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}
of rapidly decreasing smooth functions and its dual, the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of tempered distributions.
Let
n
be a given natural number. Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the
space of rapidly decreasing smooth real
functions
f

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

equipped with the family of seminorms:

\|f\|_{k,\alpha}:=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big\{(1+|y|^{k})|\partial^{\alpha}f(y)|\big\}<\infty,

where
k=0,1,...
,
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n})
is a multi-index with
\alpha_{j}=0,1,...

(j=1,...,n)
and

\partial^{\alpha}f:=\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}}{\partial y_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial y_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}f

for
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+...+\alpha_{n}
.

Then

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
Fréchet space.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be its dual, called the space of
tempered distributions
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the family of all
Borel subsets of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
equipped with the
weak* topology. If
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and
f\in\mathcal{S}
we let

\Phi(f)\text{ or }\langle\Phi,f\rangle

(2.1)

denote the action of
\Phi
on
f
.

Example 2.1

•

(Evaluations)
For
y\in\mathbb{R}
define the function
\delta_{y}
on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
by
\delta_{y}(\phi)=\phi(y)
. Then
\delta_{y}
is a tempered distribution.

•

(Derivatives) Consider the function
D
, defined for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
by
D[\phi]=\phi^{\prime}(y)
. Then
D
is a tempered distribution.

•

(Distributional derivative)

Let
T
be a tempered distribution, i.e.
T\in\mathcal{S}^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R})
. We define the distributional derivative
T^{{}^{\prime}}
of
T
by

T^{{}^{\prime}}[\phi]=-T[\phi^{{}^{\prime}}];\hskip 10.00002pt\phi\in\mathcal{S}.

Then
T^{{}^{\prime}}
is again a tempered distribution.

In the following we will apply this to the case when
n=1+d
and
y=(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.2
The Mittag-Leffler functions

Definition 2.2

(The two-parameter Mittag-Leffler function)
The Mittag-Leffler function of two parameters
\alpha,\;\beta
is denoted by
E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
and defined by:

E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}

(2.2)

where
z,\;\alpha,\;\beta\in\mathbb{C}
(the set of complex numbers),
Re(\alpha)>0\;and\;Re(\beta)>0,
and
\Gamma
is the Gamma function.

For
\beta=1
we obtain
the Mittag-Leffler function of one parameter
\alpha
denoted by
E_{\alpha}(z)
. This function is defined as

E_{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)}

(2.3)

where
z,\;\alpha\in\mathbb{C},\;Re(\alpha)>0.

Remark 2.3

Note that
E_{\alpha}(z)=E_{\alpha,1}(z)
and that

\displaystyle E_{1}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(k+1)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}=e^{z}.

(2.4)

2.3
The (Abel-)Caputo fractional derivative

In this section we present the definitions and some properties of the Caputo derivatives.

Definition 2.4

The (Abel-)Caputo fractional derivative of order
\alpha>0
of a function
f
such that
f(x)=0
when
x<0
is denoted by
D^{\alpha}f(x)
or
\frac{d^{\alpha}}{dx^{\alpha}}f(x)
and defined by

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f(x):

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{f^{(n)}(u)du}{(x-u)^{\alpha+1-n}};\quad n-1<\alpha<n\\
\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}f(x);\quad\alpha=n.\end{cases}

(2.5)

Here
n
is an smallest integer greater than or equal to
\alpha
.

If
f
is not smooth these derivatives are interpreted in the sense of distributions.

Example 2.5

If
f(x)=x
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
then

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f(x)=\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{(1-\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}.

(2.6)

In particular, choosing
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
we get

\displaystyle D^{\tfrac{1}{2}}f(x)=\frac{2\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

(2.7)

Laplace transform of Caputo derivatives

•

The 1-dimensional case.

Recall that the (1-dimensional) Laplace transform
L
is defined by

Lf(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)dt=\widetilde{f}(s)

(2.8)

for all
f
such that the integral converges.

Some of the properties of the Laplace transform that we will need are:

\displaystyle L[\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}f(t)](s)=s^{\alpha}(Lf)(s)-s^{\alpha-1}f(0)

(2.9)

\displaystyle L[E_{\alpha}(bx^{\alpha})](s)=\frac{s^{\alpha-1}}{s^{\alpha}-b}

(2.10)

\displaystyle L[x^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-bx^{\alpha})](s)=\frac{1}{s^{\alpha}+b}

(2.11)

Recall that the convolution
f\ast g

\displaystyle(f\ast g)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(t-r)g(r)dr;\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0.

The convolution rule for Laplace transform states that

L\left(\int_{0}^{t}f(t-r)g(r)dr\right)(s)=Lf(s)Lg(s),

\int_{0}^{t}f(t-r)g(r)dr=L^{-1}\left(Lf(s)Lg(s)\right)(t).

(2.13)

\mathcal{L}
of

\mathcal{L}\{g\}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}e^{-xy}g(x)dx=:\widehat{g}(y);\hskip 10.00002pty\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d};

\mathcal{L}\left(\int_{0}^{x}f(x-z)g(z)dz\right)(y)=\mathcal{L}f(y)\mathcal{L}g(y),

\int_{0}^{x}f(x-z)g(z)dz=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{L}f(y)Lg(y)\right)(x).

Time-space white noise

In this section we briefly review the basic notation and results of time-space white noise.

Let
n
be a fixed natural number. Later we will set
n=1+d
.
Define
\Omega={\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, equipped
with the weak-star topology. This space will be the base of our basic
probability space, which we explain in the following:

As events we will use the family
\mathcal{F}={\cal B}({\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
of Borel subsets of
{\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and our
probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is defined by the following result:

Theorem 3.1

(The Bochner–Minlos theorem)

There exists a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
{\cal B}({\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))

with the following property:

\mathbb{E}[e^{i\langle\cdot,\phi\rangle}]:=\int\limits_{\cal S^{\prime}}e^{i\langle\omega,\phi\rangle}d\mu(\omega)=e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\phi\|^{2}};\hskip 10.00002pti=\sqrt{-1}

for all
\phi\in{\cal S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, where

\|\phi\|^{2}=\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},\hskip 10.00002pt\langle\omega,\phi\rangle=\omega(\phi)
is the action of
\omega\in{\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
on

\phi\in{\cal S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}
denotes the expectation
with respect to
\mathbb{P}
.

We will call the triplet
({\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),{\cal B}({\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})),\mathbb{P})
the
white noise probability
space
, and
\mathbb{P}
is called the
white noise probability measure
.

The measure
\mathbb{P}
is also often called the (normalised)
Gaussian
measure
on
{\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. It is not difficult to prove that if
\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
we choose
\phi_{k}\in{\cal S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that
\phi_{k}\to\phi
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
then

\langle\omega,\phi\rangle:=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\langle\omega,\phi_{k}\rangle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{exists in}\hskip 10.00002ptL^{2}(\mathbb{P})

and is independent of the choice of
\{\phi_{k}\}
. In
particular, if we define

\widetilde{B}(x):=\widetilde{B}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n},\omega)=\langle\omega,\chi_{[0,x_{1}]\times\cdots\times[0,x_{n}]}\rangle;\hskip 10.00002ptx=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

where
[0,x_{i}]
is interpreted as
[x_{i},0]
if
x_{i}<0
,
then
\widetilde{B}(x,\omega)
has an
x
-continuous version
B(x,\omega)
, which
becomes an
n
-parameter Brownian motion
, in the following sense:

By an
n
-parameter Brownian motion
we mean a family

\{B(x,\cdot)\}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}
of random variables on a probability space

(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
such that

•

B(0,\cdot)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{almost surely with respect to }\mathbb{P},

•

\{B(x,\omega)\}
is a continuous and Gaussian stochastic process

•

For all
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
,

y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n}
,

B(x,\cdot),\,B(y,\cdot)
have the covariance

\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\wedge y_{i}
.

It can be proved that the process
\widetilde{B}(x,\omega)
defined above has a modification
B(x,\omega)
which satisfies all these properties.
This process
B(x,\omega)
then becomes an
n
-parameter Brownian motion
.

We remark that for
n=1
we get the classical (1-parameter) Brownian motion

B(t)
if we restrict ourselves to
t\geq 0
. For
n\geq 2
we get what is often
called the
Brownian sheet
.

With this definition of Brownian motion it is natural to define the

n
-parameter Wiener–Itô integral of
\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by

\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\phi(x)dB(x,\omega):=\langle\omega,\phi\rangle;\hskip 10.00002pt\omega\in{\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

We see that by using the Bochner–Minlos theorem we have obtained an easy construction of

n
-parameter Brownian motion that works for any parameter dimension
n
. Moreover, we get a representation of the space
\Omega
as the Fréchet space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This is an advantage in many situations, for example in the construction of the Hida-Malliavin derivative, which can be regarded as a stochastic gradient on
\Omega
.
See e.g.
[
11
]
and the references therein.

In the following we put
n=1+d
and let

B(t,x)=B(t,x,\omega);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\omega\in\Omega

denote the (1-dimensional) time-space Brownian motion (also called the Brownian sheet) with probability law
\mathbb{P}
. Since this process is
(t,x)
-continuous a.s., we can for a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
define its derivatives with respect to
t
and
x
in the sense of distributions.
Thus we define the time-space white noise
W(t,x)=W(t,x,\omega)
by

W(t,x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial^{d}B(t,x)}{\partial x_{1}...\partial x_{d}}.

(3.1)

In particular, for
d=1
and
x_{1}=t
and get the familiar identity

W(t)=\frac{d}{dt}B(t)\hbox{ in }\mathcal{S}^{{}^{\prime}}.

The definition (
3.1
) can also be interpreted as an element of the Hida space
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
of
stochastic distributions
, and in that setting it has been proved (see Lindstrøm, Ø. & Ubøe
[
20
]
and Benth
[
2
]
) that the Ito-Skorohod integral with respect to
B(dt,dx)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x,\omega)B(dt,dx)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x,\omega)\diamond W(t,x)dtdx,

(3.2)

where
\diamond
denotes the Wick product.

This is the interpretation we are using in (
1
) and elsewhere in this paper.

In particular, if
f(t,x,\omega)=f(t,x)
is deterministic, we can drop the Wick product and get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x)B(dt,dx)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x)W(t,x)dtdx.

(3.3)

4
Fractional Brownian motion and fractional white noise calculus

There are several ways to introduce fractional Brownian motion and fractional white noise calculus. One commonly used approach due to Hu et al
[
14
]
is recalled in the Appendix. For our purpose it is more convenient to follow the approach presented by Elliott and van der Hoek (see
[
38
]
and the references therein, including
[
41
]
). Hence we introduce the multi-parameter (time-space) fractional Brownian motion as obtained by applying an operator
M
to the classical multi-parameter Brownian motion. This approach has several advantages. For example, all the different fBm’s corresponding to different Hurst coefficients
H\in(0,1)
are defined on the same white noise probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{F},P)
introduced in Section 3. There are also several computational advantages, as shown later. See also e.g.
[
12
]
.

In the following we let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a function such that its Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}(f)(y):=\widehat{f}(y):=(2\pi)^{-\tfrac{n}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-ixy}f(x)dx;\hskip 10.00002pty\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\hskip 10.00002pt(i=\sqrt{-1})

(4.1)

exists.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
denote the inverse Fourier transform, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}g(x):=(2\pi)^{-\tfrac{n}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{ixy}g(y)dy,

(4.2)

for all functions
g
such that the integral converges.

Definition 4.1

(
[
38
]
,(A10))
Let
H=(H_{1},H_{2},...,H_{n})\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)^{n}
and let
\mathcal{S}
be the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The operator
M=M^{(H)}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mathcal{S}
is defined by

\displaystyle Mf(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\prod_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|^{\frac{1}{2}-H_{j}}(\mathcal{F}f)(y))(x)

(4.3)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\tfrac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{ixy}(\prod_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|^{\frac{1}{2}-H_{j}}(2\pi)^{-\tfrac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-ixy}f(x)dx)dy

(4.4)

If
H_{j}>\frac{1}{2}
for all

then the operator
M
can also be defined as follows:

\displaystyle M_{H}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|y_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(x+y)dy;\hskip 10.00002ptf\in\mathcal{S},

(4.5)

where

C_{H_{j}}=[2\Gamma(H_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2})\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}(H_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}))]^{-1},

(4.6)

\Gamma
being the classical Gamma function.

From now on we assume that
H\in(\frac{1}{2},1)^{n}
.

Since
H\in(\frac{1}{2},1)^{n}
is fixed, we will suppress the index
H
and write
M^{(H)}=M
in the following.

We can in a natural way extend the operator
M
from

to the space

\displaystyle L_{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R};Mf\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

(4.7)

Note that

is a Hilbert space when equipped with the inner product

(f,g)_{H}:=(f,g)_{L_{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=(Mf,Mg)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})};\hskip 10.00002ptf,g\in L^{2}_{H}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(4.8)

The following result is useful:

Lemma 4.2

Let
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\psi\in L^{2}_{H}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then

\displaystyle(\varphi,M\psi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=(M\varphi,\psi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

(4.9)

Proof.  Since

Mf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|y_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(x+y)dy;\hskip 10.00002ptf\in\mathcal{S},

(4.10)

we get by the Fubini-Tonelli theorem

\displaystyle(\varphi,M\psi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)M\psi(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|y_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}\psi(x+y)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(z-y)\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|y_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}\psi(z)dydz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(z)\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|y_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}\varphi(z+y)dydz=(M\varphi,\psi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

which completes the proof.

\square

The following property of the operator
M
will be used in the sequel.

Lemma 4.3

The operator
M
satisfies following property:

\displaystyle M^{2}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(y+z+x)dydz.

(4.11)

Proof.  Since

Mf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|y_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(x+y)dy

we get

\displaystyle M^{2}f(x)

\displaystyle=M(Mf)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}Mf(x+z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}|y_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(x+z+y)dydz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(y+z+x)dydz.

This completes the proof.

\square

Results from previous lemmas can be gathered in the extended equality which is given in following lemma.

Lemma 4.4

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)M^{2}g(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x)M^{2}g(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Mf(x)Mg(x)dx.

(4.12)

Proof.  From Lemma
4.3
it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)M^{2}g(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}g(y+z+x)dydzdx

(4.13)

.

If we substitute

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}u=x+y+z\\
v=y\\
w=z\\
\end{array}\right.\Leftrightarrow\begin{array}[]{l}x=u-v-w=:X(u,v,w)\\
y=v=:Y(u,v,w)\\
z=w=:Z(u,v,w),\\
\end{array}

then the Jacobian is

\begin{vmatrix}\frac{\partial X}{\partial u}&\frac{\partial X}{\partial v}&\frac{\partial X}{\partial w}\\
\frac{\partial Y}{\partial u}&\frac{\partial Y}{\partial v}&\frac{\partial Y}{\partial w}\\
\frac{\partial Z}{\partial u}&\frac{\partial Z}{\partial v}&\frac{\partial Z}{\partial w}\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}1&-1&-1\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{vmatrix}=1,

(4.14)

and we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)M^{2}g(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|u_{j}w_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(u-v-w)g(u)dudvdw

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(u)\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|u_{j}w_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(u-v-w)g(u)dudvdw

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(u)M^{2}f(u)du.

(4.15)

This proves the first identity. A similar argument proves the second identity.

\square

Now we can give a formal definition of multi-parameter fractional Brownian motion:

Definition 4.5

(Fractional Brownian motion revisited)

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
define

B_{H}(x):=B_{H}(x,\omega):=\langle\omega,M_{x}(\cdot)\rangle;\hskip 10.00002pt\omega\in\Omega

(4.16)

where we have put

\displaystyle M_{x}(y):=M(\chi_{[0,x_{1}]\times[0,x_{2}]\times...\times[0,x_{n}]})(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{-1}\Big(\frac{x_{j}-y_{j}}{|x_{j}-y_{j}|^{\frac{3}{2}-H}}+\frac{y_{j}}{|y_{j}|^{\frac{3}{2}-H}}\Big);\hskip 10.00002pty,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(4.17)

is a Gaussian process, and by the properties of the classical Bm we have

E[B_{H}(x)]=B_{H}(0)=0,

(4.18)

(4.19)

By the Kolmogorov continuity theorem the process
B_{H}
has a continuous version, which we will also denote by
B_{H}.
Except for a constant, this process coincides with the fractional Brownian motion

4.1
Fractional white noise calculus

By (

) it follows by approximating

B_{H}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\chi_{[0,x_{j}]}(y_{j})dB_{H}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}M_{x}(y)dB(y)=\langle\omega,M_{x}\rangle.

(4.21)

We know that the action of
\omega\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
extends from
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to

and that if
f\in\mathcal{S}
then

Therefore we can define

(4.22)

Using this, we can write

\displaystyle B_{H}(x,\omega)=\langle\omega,M_{x}\rangle=\langle M\omega,\prod_{j=1}^{n}\chi_{[0.x_{j}](\cdot)}\rangle=B(x,M\omega).

(4.23)

We define the

\displaystyle W_{H}(x,\omega)=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{1}...\partial x_{n}}B_{H}(x)\text{ (derivative in }(\mathcal{S})^{*}).

(4.24)

By the above it follows that

\displaystyle W_{H}(x,\omega)=W(x,M\omega),

(4.25)

where

\displaystyle W(x)=W(x,\omega)=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{1}...\partial x_{n}}B(x)\text{ is the classical white noise in }(\mathcal{S})^{*}.

(4.26)

Let
L_{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the set of measurable processes

\displaystyle E[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(Mf)^{2}(x)dx]<\infty.

(4.27)

Then we define the

(WIS)-integral of

as follows:

Definition 4.6

(The fractional Wick–Itô–Skorohod (WIS) integral)

Let

. Then we say that

is

-integrable and we define the Wick–Itô–Skorohod (WIS) integral of

with respect to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)dB_{H}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\diamond W_{H}(x)dx,

(4.28)

where the integral on the right is the (Pettis) Lebesgue integral with values in

By (

) and Lemma

we obtain the following isometry:

Theorem 4.7

\displaystyle E[(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)dB_{H}(x))^{2}]=E[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(Mf)^{2}(x)dx]=E[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)M^{2}f(x)dx]

(4.29)

5
An
L^{2}(P)
-estimate for fBm -integrals

The following theorem gives a fundamental
L^{2}(P)
-inequality for WIS-integrals with respect to fBm.
It is a multi-parameter extension of Theorem 3.1 in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 5.1

Let
f(x)=f(x,\omega)
be a measurable stochastic process such that

E\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(Mf)^{2}(x)dx\right]<\infty.

Assume that
f
is supported on the set

S=[0,\bar{x}_{1}]\times[0,\bar{x}_{2}]\times...\times[0,\bar{x}_{n}]
.
Define

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\bar{x}}f(x)dB_{H}(x)=\int_{S}f(x)dB_{H}(x)

(5.1)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\bar{x}_{1}}\int_{0}^{\bar{x}_{2}}...\int_{0}^{\bar{x}_{n}}f(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})dB_{H}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}),

(5.2)

where the integral is interpreted in the WIS sense.

Then

E[Y^{2}]\leq(\int_{S}E[f^{2}(x)]dx)\prod_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}\bar{x}_{j}^{2H_{j}-1},

(5.3)

where

\displaystyle K_{j}:=C_{H_{j}}^{2}\Big\{4(H_{j}-\tfrac{3}{2})^{-2}+2(H_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2})^{-1}(1-H_{j})^{-1}\Big\}.

(5.4)

Remark 5.2

Note that we do not assume that
f
is adapted.

Remark 5.3

Sufficient conditions (in terms of Malliavin derivatives) for the existence in
L^{2}(P)
of the WIS integral with respect to 1-parameter fractional Brownian motion can be found in Bender
[
33
]
, Corollary 3.5.
For general
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
-valued integrands, conditions for the existence of the WIS-integral as an element of
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
are given by Mishura
[
46
]
, Theorem 2.3.1, using an inequality of Vaage.

Proof.  By Theorem
4.7
we have

\displaystyle E[Y^{2}]

\displaystyle=E[(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)dB_{H}(x))^{2}]=E[(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(Mf)(x)dB(x))^{2}]

\displaystyle=E[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(Mf)^{2}(x)dx]=E[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)M^{2}f(x)dx].

(5.5)

Also, by Lemma
4.3

M^{2}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(y+z+x)dydz.

Hence, using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality twice we obtain

\displaystyle E[Y^{2}]

\displaystyle=E[\int_{S}f(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(y+z+x)dydzdx]

\displaystyle=E[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\int_{S}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}f(x)f(y+z+x)dx)dydz)]

\displaystyle=E[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}(\int_{S}f(x)f(y+z+x)dx)dydz]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}(\int_{S}E[f(x)f(y+z+x)]dx)dydz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}(\int_{S}E[f^{2}(x)]^{\tfrac{1}{2}}E[f^{2}(y+z+x)]^{\tfrac{1}{2}}dx)dydz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}(\int_{S}E[f^{2}(x)]dx\int_{S}E[f^{2}(y+z+x)]dx)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}dydz

\displaystyle=(\int_{S}E[f^{2}(x)]dx)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}(\int_{S}E[f^{2}(y+z+x)]dx)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}dydz

\displaystyle\leq(\int_{S}E[f^{2}(x)]dx)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{\{(y,z);y+z\in S\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}(\int_{S}E[f^{2}(y+z+x)]dx)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}dydz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{S}E[f^{2}(x)]dx\int_{\{(y,z);y+z\in S\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}dydz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{S}E[f^{2}(x)]dx\prod_{j=1}^{n}C_{H_{j}}^{2}\int_{0\leq y_{j}+z_{j}\leq\bar{x}_{j}}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}dy_{j}dz_{j}.

Here we have used that if
x+y+z\in S
and
x\in S
, then
y+z\in S
, i.e.
0\leq y_{j}+z_{j}\leq\bar{x}_{j}
for all
j
.

It remains to estimate the integral

\displaystyle J:=\int_{\{(y_{j},z_{j});0\leq y_{j}+z_{j}\leq\bar{x}_{j}\}}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}dy_{j}dz_{j}\leq J_{1}+J_{2},

(5.6)

where

\displaystyle J_{1}=\int_{\{(y_{j},z_{j});|y_{j}|\leq\bar{x}_{j},|z_{j}|\leq\bar{x}_{j}\}}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}dy_{j}dz_{j}

(5.7)

and

\displaystyle J_{2}=\int_{\{(y_{j},z_{j});|y_{j}|>\bar{x}_{j},|z_{j}|>\bar{x}_{j}\text{ }\&\text{ }0\leq y_{j}+z_{j}\leq\bar{x}_{j}\}}|y_{j}z_{j}|^{H_{j}-\frac{3}{2}}dy_{j}dz_{j}

(5.8)

By direct computation we get that

\displaystyle J_{1}=4(H_{j}-\tfrac{3}{2})^{-2}\bar{x}_{j}^{2H_{j}-1}.

(5.9)

It is also easy to see by first integrating wrt.

and then using the mean value theorem, that

\displaystyle J_{2}\leq 2\bar{x}_{j}(H_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2})^{-1}\int_{\bar{x}_{j}}^{\infty}z_{j}^{2H_{j}-3}dz_{j}\leq 2(H_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2})^{-1}(1-H_{j})^{-1}\bar{x}_{j}^{2H_{j}-1}.

(5.10)

We conclude that

\square

6

In this section we study the additive noise case, i.e. we study the equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y(t,x)=\lambda\Delta Y(t,x)+\sigma W_{H}(t,x);\;(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

(6.1)

\displaystyle Y(0,x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}(x)\text{ (the Dirac measure at }x),

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow+/-\text{ }\infty}Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=0,

(6.2)

Definition 6.1

We say that a process
Y(t,x)=Y(t,x,\omega):[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a solution of of the fractional stochastic heat equation (

(i)
\omega\mapsto Y(t,x,\omega)\in(\mathcal{S})^{*}

for all

Our first main result in this section is the following:

Theorem 6.2

The unique solution
Y(t,x)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of the fractional heat equation (
6.1
) - (
6
) is given by

\displaystyle Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ixy}E_{\alpha}(-\lambda|y|^{2}t^{\alpha})dy

\displaystyle+\sigma(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)^{\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{x}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x-z)y}E_{\alpha}(-\lambda|y|^{2}(t-r)^{\alpha})dy\Big)B_{H}(dr,dz).

(6.3)

Proof.  In the following we let

\displaystyle L\{f(\cdot,x)\}(s,x)=\widetilde{f(\cdot,x)}(s,x)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}f(r,x)dr;\hskip 10.00002pts\geq 0

(6.4)

denote the Laplace transform of
f(t,x)
as a function of
t
.

In the following we let

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\{f(t,\cdot)\}(t,y)=\widehat{f(t,\cdot)}(t,y)

(6.5)

denote the (multi-dimensional) Laplace transform of
f(t,x)
as a function of
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{d}).

First assume that
Y(t,x)
is a solution of (
6.1
). We apply the Laplace transform
L

to both sides of (
6.1
) and obtain

s^{\alpha}\widetilde{Y}(s,x)-s^{\alpha-1}Y(0^{+},x)=\beta\widetilde{\Delta Y}(s,x)+\sigma\widetilde{W}_{H}(s,x).

(6.6)

Applying the multi-dimensional Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}
to this equation we get, since
\widehat{Y}(0,y)=1
,
or, with
|y|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}^{2}
,

\left(s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}\right)\widehat{\widetilde{Y}}(s,y)=s^{\alpha-1}\widehat{Y}(0^{+},y)+\sigma\widehat{\widetilde{W}_{H}}(s,y)

(6.7)

Hence

\displaystyle\widehat{\widetilde{Y}}(s,y)=\frac{s^{\alpha-1}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}+\frac{\sigma\widehat{\widetilde{W}_{H}}(s,y)}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}.

(6.8)

Since the Laplace transform and the multi-dimensional Laplace transform commute, this can be written

\displaystyle\widetilde{\widehat{Y}}(s,y)=\frac{s^{\alpha-1}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}+\frac{\sigma\widetilde{\widehat{W}_{H}}(s,y)}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}.

(6.9)

Applying the inverse Laplace operator

to this equation we get

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}(t,y)

\displaystyle=L^{-1}\Big(\frac{s^{\alpha-1}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}\Big)(t,y)+L^{-1}\Big(\frac{\sigma\widetilde{\widehat{W}_{H}}(s,y)}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}\Big)(t,y)

\displaystyle=E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda|y|^{2}t^{\alpha})+L^{-1}\Big(\frac{\sigma\widetilde{\widehat{W}_{H}}(s,y)}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}\Big)(t,y),

(6.10)

\displaystyle E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}

(6.11)

It remains to find

L^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma\widehat{\widetilde{W}_{H}}(s,y)}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}\right)
:

Recall that the convolution
f\ast g
of two functions
f,g:[0,\infty)\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle(f\ast g)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(t-r)g(r)dr;\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0.

(6.12)

The convolution rule for Laplace transform states that

L\left(\int_{0}^{t}f(t-r)g(r)dr\right)(s)=Lf(s)Lg(s),

or

\int_{0}^{t}f(t-w)g(w)dw=L^{-1}\left(Lf(s)Lg(s)\right)(t).\\

(6.13)

Now (see
[
16
]
,p.312)

\displaystyle L^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda|y|^{2}}\right)(t)

\displaystyle=t^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{\alpha-1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda|y|^{2})^{k}t^{\alpha(k+1)-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha(k+1))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})^{k}t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha(k+1))}

\displaystyle=:\Lambda(t,y).

(6.14)

Theorem 8.1

Let
F(u):[0,\infty)^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
G(u,w):[0,\infty)^{n}\times[0,\infty)^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be locally bounded deterministic functions such that

F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n})

\displaystyle\|G(u,\cdot)\|^{2}

\displaystyle:=\|G(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}([0,u])}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{u}G^{2}(u,w)dw

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{u_{1}}\int_{0}^{u_{2}}...\int_{0}^{u_{n}}G^{2}(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{n},w_{1},w_{2}...w_{n})dw_{n}...dw_{2}dw_{1}<\infty

(8.1)

for all
u=(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}

Then there is a unique solution
Y(u)\in L^{2}(P)
of the stochastic Volterra equation

\displaystyle Y(u)=F(u)+\int_{0}^{u}G(u,w)Y(w)B_{H}(dw).

(8.2)

The solution is given by

\displaystyle Y(u)=F(u)+\int_{0}^{u}H(u,w)F(w)B_{H}(dw).

(8.3)

where

\displaystyle H(u,w)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}G_{m}(u,w)

(8.4)

with

given inductively by

\displaystyle G_{m+1}(u,w)

\displaystyle=\int_{w}^{u}G_{m}(u,v)G(v,w)B_{H}(dv);\hskip 9.24994ptm=1,2,...

\displaystyle G_{1}(u,w)

\displaystyle=G(u,w).

(8.5)

Proof.  The proof is based on a suitably adapted modification of the proof of Theorem 3.1 in
[
24
]
. We give an outline:

By induction we see that

\displaystyle G_{m}(u,w)=\underset{S(u,w)}{\int...\int}\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}G(z_{k},z_{k+1})B_{H}(dz_{1})B_{H}(dz_{2})...B_{H}(dz_{m-1}),

(8.6)

where
z_{0}=u,z_{n}=w
and the integration is taken over the set

\displaystyle S(u,w):=\Big\{(z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{m-1})\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n})^{m-1};w\leq z_{m-1}\leq z_{m-2}\leq...\leq z_{1}\leq u\Big\}

(8.7)

Here we use the notation

x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\leq y=(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{n})\text{ iff }x_{j}\leq y_{j};\text{for all }j=1,2,...,n.

By applying Theorem
8.1
repeatedly we get that

\displaystyle E[G_{m}(u,w)^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq A^{m-1}\int_{S(u,w)}\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}G^{2}(z_{k},k_{k+1})dz_{1}dz_{2}...dz_{m-1}

\displaystyle\leq A^{m-1}\|G(u,\cdot)\|^{2m}\frac{Vol(S(u,w))}{((m-1)!)^{n}}

(8.8)

where

\displaystyle A=\prod_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}\bar{x}_{j}^{2H_{j}-1}\text{ with }K_{j}:=C_{H_{j}}^{2}\Big\{4(H_{j}-\tfrac{3}{2})^{-2}+2(H_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2})^{-1}(1-H_{j})^{-1}\Big\}.

(8.9)

Hence

H(u,w)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}G_{m}(u,w)
converges in
L^{2}(P)
for all
u,w
.

By Theorem
8.1
it follows that

\displaystyle Y(u)=F(u)+\int_{0}^{u}H(u,w)F(w)B_{H}(dw)\in L^{2}(P)\text{ for all }u.

(8.10)

\square

(Solution of the fractional Volterra equation)

As before put

\displaystyle f(t,x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ixy}E_{\alpha}(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})dy=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ixy}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)}dy,

(8.11)

and

\displaystyle g(t-r,x-z)=\sigma(2\pi)^{-d}(t-r)^{\alpha-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x-z)y}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2})dy

(8.12)

The solution of the fractional Volterra equation ((

)):

\displaystyle Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=f(t,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}g(t-s,x-z)Y(s,z)B_{H}(ds,dz),

(8.13)

is

\displaystyle Y(t,x)=f(t,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}h(t,x,s,z)f(s,z)B_{H}(ds,dz),

(8.14)

where

\displaystyle h(t,x,s,z)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}g_{m}(t,x,s,z).

(8.15)

with

given inductively by

\displaystyle g_{m}(t,x,s,z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\Big(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{z}g_{m-1}(t,x,r,w)g_{1}(s,z,r,w)B_{H}(dr,dw)\Big)B_{H}(ds,dz);\hskip 9.24994ptm=2,3,...

\displaystyle=g(t-s,x-z),

(8.16)

i.e.

\displaystyle g_{m}(t,x,s,z)

\displaystyle=\underset{S((t,x),(s,z))}{\int...\int}\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}g(s_{k},z_{k},s_{k+1},z_{k+1})B_{H}(ds_{1},dz_{1})B_{H}(ds_{2},dz_{2})...B_{H}(ds_{m-1},dz_{m-1}).

Here
(s_{0},z_{0})=(t,x),(s_{m},z_{m})=(s,z)
and the integration is taken over the set

When is the solution mild?

We now apply the result above to the Volterra equation (

):

In this case we have
u=(t,x),w=(r,z)
and

\displaystyle G(u,w)=\sigma(2\pi)^{-d}(t-r)^{\alpha-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x-z)y}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2})dy.

(8.18)

Hence

\displaystyle\|G(u,\cdot)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(2\pi)^{-2d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}(t-r)^{2\alpha-2}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x-z)y}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2})dy\Big)^{2}drdz

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}(2\pi)^{-2d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}(t-r)^{2\alpha-2}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2})|dy\Big)^{2}drdz.

We split into two cases:

1) Assume
\alpha\geq 1
.

Then by Jia et al
[

]
we have

\displaystyle|E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2})|\leq e^{-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2}}.

(8.20)

Hence

\displaystyle\|G(u,\cdot)\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}(2\pi)^{-2d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}(t-r)^{2\alpha-2}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2}}dy\Big)^{2}drdz.

(8.21)

If we introduce the change of variable

we get

\displaystyle\|G(u,\cdot)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)^{2\alpha-2}(\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha})^{-d}(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-v^{2}}dv)^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}dr

which is finite if and only if

2) Assume

Then we have

\displaystyle\Big(E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})\Big)^{2}\sim(\lambda t^{\alpha})^{-2}|y|^{-4}

(8.23)

and by (
) we see that

Combining this with Theorem

Theorem 8.3

For all
H=(H_{0},H_{1},...,H_{d})\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)^{1+d}
the following holds for the stochastic fractional Volterra equation (
7.3
):

•

(i) If
\alpha=1
the solution

is mild if and only if
d=1
.

•

(ii) If
\alpha>1
the solution is mild if

or

.

•

(iii) If
\alpha<1
the solution is not mild for any

9
Application

9.1
Distillation columns

Distillation process separates between liquid and vapor in many fields, where it works by applying and removing a heat.
Heat causes components with lower boiling points and higher volatility to vaporize, leaving less volatile components in liquid form.

It separates binary mixtures. Many variables, such as column pressure, temperature, size, and diameter are determined by the properties of the feed and the desired products.

9.2
A model for the heat transfer in distillation columns

To model the transfer of heat in such a mixture we use the following fractional heat equation for the temperature
T(t,x)
at time
t
and at the position
x
in the
3-
dimensional space:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}T(t,x)=\lambda\Delta T(t,x)+\sigma T(t,x)\diamond W_{H}(t,x);\;(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)^{3}

(9.1)

with boundary conditions

\displaystyle T(0,x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}(x)\text{ (the Dirac measure at }x),

\displaystyle T(t,0)

\displaystyle=0\text{ when }t>0.

(9.2)

By Theorem
7.2
the solution of this fractional stochastic differential equation
coincides with the solution of the following fractional Volterra equation:

\displaystyle T(t,x)

\displaystyle=f(t,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}g(t-r,x-z)T(r,z)B_{H}(dr,dz),

(9.3)

where

\displaystyle f(t,x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{ixy}E_{\alpha}(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})dy=(2\pi)^{-3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ixy}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)}dy,

(9.4)

and

\displaystyle g(t-r,x-z)=\sigma(2\pi)^{-3}(t-r)^{\alpha-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i(x-z)y}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2})dy

(9.5)

A solution formula for this Volterra equation is given in Theorem
8.2
.

The question is:

For what values of
\alpha
do we obtain vaporisation, and for what
\alpha
do we obtain volatility?

Fractional calculus have an important role to describe real-world phenomena called memory effect. The Caputo derivative is used because the memory effect and a constant function derivatives yield zero. Or the distillation need the memory effect so we use it to interpret the situation.
In our previous paper
[
47
]
we show that in the case where
\alpha<1
the travel time of the particles are longer than the standard diffusion, this case models also the removal of heat.
Where
\alpha>1
the particles spread faster than in regular diffusion, in this case we obtain a vaporisation.
