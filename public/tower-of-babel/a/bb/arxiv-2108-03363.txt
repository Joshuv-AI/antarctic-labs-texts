Bringing discrete-time Langevin splitting methods into agreement with thermodynamics Preprint: To appear in Journal of Chemical Physics – DOI: 10.1063/5.0066008 – LA-UR-21-27590
Joshua Finkelstein, Chungho Cheng, Giacomo Fiorin, Benjamin Seibold, Niels Grønbech-Jensen

Abstract
Abstract In light of the recently published complete set of statistically correct Grønbech-Jensen (GJ) methods for discrete-time thermodynamics, we revise a differential operator splitting method for the Langevin equation in order to comply with the basic GJ thermodynamic sampling features, namely the Boltzmann distribution and Einstein diffusion, in linear systems. This revision, which is based on the introduction of time scaling along with flexibility of a discrete-time velocity attenuation parameter, provides a direct link between the ABO splitting formalism and the GJ methods. This link brings about the conclusion that any GJ method has at least weak second order accuracy in the applied time step. It further helps identify a novel half-step velocity, which simultaneously produces both correct kinetic statistics and correct transport measures for any of the statistically sound GJ methods. Explicit algorithmic expressions are given for the integration of the new half-step velocity into the GJ set of methods. Numerical simulations, including quantum-based molecular dynamics (QMD) using the QMD suite LATTE, highlight the discussed properties of the algorithms as well as exhibit the direct application of robust, time step independent stochastic integrators to quantum-based molecular dynamics.

The GJF and VRORV methods, corresponding to the GJ-I and GJ-II methods of the GJ set listed in Ref.

We seek in this paper to identify corrective measures to the splitting approach that can similarly lead to all methods in the GJ family. The reason for this interest is two-fold. First, such revisions would provide insight to the relationships between numerical Langevin methods and methodologies. Second, with a direct relationship between the methods and how they are derived, one may be able to take advantage of the analysis framework developed for one approach in the analysis of the methods arising from the other.

Building on the ABO splitting technique advanced by Ref.

From this combined analysis, we further identify a new GJ method, which is the result of equal time scaling throughout all three A, B, and O splitting operations. Finally, in Sec.

The results are validated in Sec.

Discrete-time algorithms

III
Generalized Splitting

The previous section suggests that the ABO splitting method needs at least one of two revisions in its operations before it can conform to the criteria defining the GJ methods, and before all the thermodynamic quantities Eqs. (
4
) and (
12
) can be satisfied. One possibility is to revise the one-time-step velocity attenuation parameter

; the other is to introduce scaling of the time step in operations A and B (similar to Ref.
[
27

). Here, we combine the above-mentioned observations to revise the splitting operations A, B, and O in Eq. (

The first generalization is to allow for a non-exponential, one-time-step velocity attenuation parameter
c_{2}
in the O operation. The thermodynamic O operation is only temporally additive as shown in Eqs. (

The configurational sampling is, however, not limited by the additivity of the O operation, since Ref.
[
34
]
has shown that no stochastic Verlet-type integrator can produce correct Boltzmann statistics with more than one independent stochastic number contributing to the configurational equation per time step. If O were not at the pivotal (i.e., central) position, but instead used as two symmetrically positioned

The second generalization is to introduce temporal scaling parameters,

III.1
Half-step velocities

As mentioned above, an essential feature of the pivotal location of the thermodynamic step O is that the two independent noise terms from the two sequential half-time steps OO combine into a single noise contribution
\beta^{n+1}
for the coordinates
r^{n}
and
v^{n}
seen in, e.g., Eq. (
28

, for any allowable functional form of
c_{2}=c_{2}(\gamma\Delta{t})

\displaystyle e^{\frac{\Delta{t}}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{O}}\;:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}r\\

\displaystyle{\overset{\widetilde{O}}{\longrightarrow}}\;\begin{pmatrix}r\\
\sqrt{c_{2}}\,v+\sqrt{(1-c_{2})\frac{k_{B}T}{m}}\sigma\end{pmatrix}\,,

(31)

where
\sigma
is drawn from a zero mean, unit variance Gaussian distribution. The half-step velocity
u_{s}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
, defined by the half-time-step sequence
\widetilde{\rm O}\widetilde{\rm A}\widetilde{\rm B}

25a

\displaystyle u_{s}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{c_{2}}\,(v^{n}+\frac{d_{B}\,\Delta{t}}{2m}f^{n})+\sqrt{(1-c_{2})\frac{k_{B}T}{m}}\,\sigma^{n}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{c_{2}}}{d_{A}\,c_{1}}\frac{r^{n+1}-r^{n}}{\Delta{t}}+\frac{1}{2c_{1}}\sqrt{(1-c_{2}^{2})\frac{k_{B}T}{m}}\,\underbrace{\frac{\sigma^{n}_{+}-\sqrt{c_{2}}\sigma^{n+1}_{-}}{\sqrt{1+c_{2}}}}_{\sigma^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{c_{2}}}{d_{A}\,c_{1}}\frac{r^{n+1}-r^{n}}{\Delta{t}}+\frac{1}{2m}\sqrt{\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}}\,\beta^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\,,

\displaystyle u^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{r^{n+1}-r^{n}}{\Delta{t}}\pm\frac{1}{m}\sqrt{\frac{1-c_{3}}{2\frac{\alpha\Delta{t}}{m}}}\,\beta^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\,,

IV
Method Equivalencies and statistical accuracy

Equivalencies amongst methods within the GJ set, and also with BAOAB, are very well illuminated by the generalized splitting form outlined above. In exponential operator parlance, the Langevin equation is numerically evolved using a GJ method over a time
\Delta t
by

\displaystyle e^{d_{AB}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{B}^{*}}e^{d_{AB}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{A}^{*}}e^{d_{O}\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{O}^{*}}e^{d_{AB}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{A}^{*}}e^{d_{AB}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{B}^{*}}\;,

(41)

which contrasts with the numerical evolution prescribed by BAOAB:

\displaystyle e^{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{B}^{*}}e^{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{A}^{*}}e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{O}^{*}}e^{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{A}^{*}}e^{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{B}^{*}}\;.

(42)

The remarks given in the appendix of
[
32
]
show that, when
0<\gamma\Delta t<2
, GJF and BAOAB are actually the same method (i.e., they yield identical trajectories and velocities for the same noise) if both methods use the same time step
\Delta t
but two
different
choices for
\gamma=\alpha/m
. The erroneous diffusion and transport results of the BAOAB method arise because of this rescaling of the damping parameter when comparing to the exact diffusion result
\frac{k_{B}T}{\alpha}
, where the damping parameter is not rescaled. The GJ methods, including GJF, are constructed to avoid this inconsistency. The viewpoint of friction rescaling between methods can be generalized to include the entire one-parameter family of GJ methods. In this spirit, we may view the splitting method in
Eq.

26
to be the BAOAB method with a time step
d_{AB}{\Delta t}
and a now re-scaled
\gamma
, the scaling factor being the ratio of
d_{O}
to
d_{AB}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle e^{d_{AB}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}}e^{d_{AB}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}}e^{(d_{O}/d_{AB})(d_{AB}\Delta t)\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}}e^{d_{AB}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}}e^{d_{AB}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}}\;,

(43)

because

\displaystyle(d_{O}/d_{AB})(d_{AB}\Delta t)\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}

\displaystyle=(d_{AB}\Delta t)\sum_{i}(\tfrac{d_{O}}{d_{AB}}\gamma)(\nabla_{v_{i}}v_{i}\cdot+\frac{k_{B}T}{m_{i}}\,\Delta_{v_{i}})\;.

(44)

Here,
Eq.

43
then has the standard splitting interpretation where each portion of the split vector field in
Eq.

14
has the same meaning of time. In this sense,
any
GJ method is mathematically equivalent with BAOAB as well as any other GJ method for some re-scaled
\gamma
within an appropriate domain of
\gamma\Delta t
. However, unlike BAOAB, the GJ friction rescaling is designed to be consistent with the known expressions for diffusion and drift, Eqs. (
4b
) and (
4c
). We note that the above attribution of the mutual equivalence of the GJ methods to a rescaling of the friction is not necessarily implying that there exists a universally correct way to describe the friction parameter in discrete time; i.e., a universally best choice of GJ method by choosing
c_{2}
. All GJ methods are designed to correctly give the same basic statistical properties.
Choosing which method is most appropriate for a given application depends on both the system and on the simulation objectives, which may require particular focus on, e.g., the statistical sampling efficiency or how the simulated frequency components of the system depend on the time step. However it may often be the case that unless warranted for some particular reason, the simplest choice where time is not scaled (i.e., the GJ-I method) may likely be the most appropriate for applications.

Given that this section establishes an equivalence between the GJ set and BAOAB, it is perhaps intuitively clear that the numerical steady state distribution for each GJ method, which we denote as
\rho_{\rm GJ}
, should have weak second order convergence in the time step
\Delta t
, since this is known to be true in the case of BAOAB
[
25
]
. This intuitive picture is explicitly shown in the Appendix where, through the use of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) formula, the weak second order nature of the limiting steady distribution,
\rho_{\rm GJ}
, is presented in a direct calculation. These calculations agree with the result in Ref.
[
40
]
, which concluded that the GJF method is weakly second order, and extends this order property to
all
GJ methods through the use of the splitting formalism we have developed. In addition to the formal argument given in the Appendix, we also numerically demonstrate this second order error scaling in the case of simple non-linear potentials for several GJ methods in the next section.

Two normalized potentials have been simulated:

VI
Discussion

This paper presents a direct connection between the most promising
[
25
,
27
]
of the traditional Langevin operator splitting approaches and the brute force GJ approach
[
29
,
34
]
, which has led to the complete set of statistically sound stochastic Verlet-type Langevin integrators. It is concluded that the splitting approach alone cannot lead to a method that produces the same number of correct statistical properties as what is found in the GJ set. However, the ABO splitting of Ref.
[
25
]
can be revised by a generalization of the time scaling that was proposed in Ref.
[
27
]
for a particular choice of functional attenuation parameter
c_{2}
, and we are here able to determine the specific time scaling,
d_{AB}
in Eq. (
29
), that brings the ABO splitting method into agreement with the entire GJ set.

Reviewing the three discrete-time operator steps, A, B, and O, given in Eq. (
15
) (as well as in Eq. (
25
)), shows that the thermodynamic O operation (Eq. (
15c
)) is a correct representation of the fluctuation-dissipation balance, and that the ABO time step inconsistencies arise from the Euler operations in the inertial A (Eq. (
15a
)) and the interactive B (Eq. (
15b
)) steps. This is consistent with the observation that the necessary corrective measure applies to those two operations, while the thermodynamic O step can be revised by changing
c_{2}
from one allowed functional form to another. Thus, the two approaches taken so far for correcting the transport properties of BAOAB can be illustrated by: 1) maintaining the time scaling of the A and B steps in BAOAB, while changing the attenuation parameter to the rational form
c_{2}=a
in the thermodynamic O step, thereby obtaining GJ-I (GJF)
[
26
]
, and 2) maintaining the exponential form of the attenuation parameter
c_{2}
in BAOAB’s thermodynamic O step, while adjusting the time scaling
d_{AB}
in BAOAB’s inertial A and interactive B steps to match
c_{2}
, thereby obtaining GJ-II (VRORV)
[
27
]
. The entire GJ set can then arise from BAOAB by, e.g., choosing a time scaling
d_{AB}
and then matching that scaling with an accompanying functional form for
c_{2}
that produces correct statistics; or vice versa. However, the choices of the useful
c_{2}
and
d_{AB}
combination in the revision of BAOAB do not emerge naturally from the ABO splitting method, and must be determined by a direct parameter determination similar to the one conducted in the derivation of the GJ set of methods
[
29
,
34
]
.

As illustrated in
Section

III
, allowing departure from the exact exponential operators or inclusion of time scaling enables a structural gain — namely correct (limiting) free particle and harmonic oscillator statistical behavior. This procedure can likely be generalized to other exact splittings in different orderings, which could possibly help to “repair” these methods by affording them the necessary flexibility to realize the desired statistical properties. This idea may prove fruitful whenever the algorithmic form of Eq. (
27
) is used, for example, in the numerical integration of quantum spin dynamics
[
54
]
.

Given the close connection between BAOAB and the GJ methods, we have been able to use the established analysis of accuracy for the LM family
[
39
]
, as shown in Appendix
A
, to the conclude that all the GJ methods are at least weakly second order accurate in the time step for nonlinear systems, consistent with the previously published analytical result for the GJF (GJ-I) method in Ref.
[
40
]
. The weak second order accuracy for the GJ methods also intuitively follows from the observation made in Section
IV
, that the GJ methods for any given time step can be described as one and the same method with different friction parameters.

This paper takes further advantage of the ABO splitting analogy to develop a new and improved definition of the half-step velocity for
any
of the GJ methods. This velocity, shown in Eq. (
38
), produces the correct kinetic statistics for the noisy harmonic oscillator
and
it produces the correct transport velocity. We have illustrated the integration of this new velocity into standard forms of the GJ methods. Although correct for the configurational coordinate in all GJ methods, the combination of correct velocity drift and Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics has previously only been possible for GJ-III
[
29
]
, but ought to be available in all stochastic schemes. This is especially true whenever the Langevin equation is used for its original purpose of modeling the kinetic effects of an implicit solvent on solute atoms and molecules simulated explicitly.

In addition to kinetics, the unbiased estimation of atomic velocities is also key to the computation of certain
thermodynamic
properties, such as the per-particle kinetic term of the virial stress
[
55
]
. There are several circumstances where a stochastic scheme with strong friction may be particularly beneficial. For example, when using many-body or quantum-mechanical interatomic potentials over short time scales
[
56
]
, or when simulating materials with a highly anisotropic distribution of forces and relaxation time scales
[
57
,
58
]
.

Apart from the now established mathematical connection between the ABO splitting and the GJ methods, the results of this paper have been numerically substantiated by simulating one-dimensional, nonlinear oscillators to verify the weak second order accuracy.
We also demonstrated the applicability of the GJ methods to condensed-phase systems by running molecular dynamics simulations at high friction for a small box of water described quantum mechanically. We show that the basic thermodynamic measures of potential and kinetic energies, along with their fluctuations, are robustly computed by the GJ methods within the entire stability range for the time step. These simulations have used the novel velocity definition presented in this paper, thus including the usability of this kinetic measure into the validation of the previously demonstrated statistically accurate GJ half-step velocities
[
29
]
.

Appendix A
Weak Second Order Accuracy

In this Appendix, we explicitly calculate the weak second order behavior for the GJ methods. The GJ splitting reformulation, as described in the main text, allows us to employ an established mathematical technique for operator splitting by using the operators defined in
Eq.

25
. We take advantage of this formulation to formally certify the weak second order — the order of convergence for averaged phase space quantities — of each GJ method. As was done in Ref.
[
25
]
, we use the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) formula to define a Fokker-Planck operator,
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}
, for each GJ method. We think of this operator as describing the evolution of the GJ methods’ probability density in continuous time such that at each moment of discrete time,
t_{n}=n\Delta t
, the probability density of the numerical method is given by
e^{t_{n}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}}\rho_{0}
. Using the BCH formula, we define
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}
through the following composition of linear operators

\displaystyle\begin{split}\exp(\Delta t{{\mathcal{L}^{*}}_{\text{GJ}}})&\equiv\exp(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}}_{B})\exp(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}}}_{A})\exp({\Delta t}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}}}_{O})\exp(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}}}_{A})\exp(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}}}_{B})\\
&=\exp(\tfrac{d_{AB}\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})\exp(\tfrac{d_{AB}\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A})\exp({d_{O}\Delta t}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O})\exp(\tfrac{d_{AB}\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A})\exp(\tfrac{d_{AB}\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})\;,\end{split}

(49)

where each exponential operator on the right hand side is well-defined. Fixing
\gamma
and expanding the time scaling factors in
\Delta t
,
d_{AB}=1+d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{AB}^{(2)}\Delta t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})
and
d_{O}=1+d_{O}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{O}^{(2)}\Delta t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})
, the
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}
operator can be calculated up to second order in
\Delta t
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}&=\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}+{\Delta t}(d_{O}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(1)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}))\\
&+{\Delta t}^{2}(d_{O}^{(2)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}))\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{16}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\\
&=:\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}+{\Delta t}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{1}+{\Delta t}^{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{2}+\mathcal{L}^{*}\;,\end{split}

(50)

where the result of applying the operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}
scales as
\Delta t^{3}
and
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\rm LD}=\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\rm A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\rm B}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\rm O}
is the Langevin dynamics Fokker-Planck operator. For specific details on arriving to
Eq.

50
from
Eq.

49
please see the supplementary material. Operating on the canonical distribution
\rho=\rho(r,v)\propto e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}(r,v)}
, leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}\;\rho

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{L}^{*}_{1}\rho\;+\Delta t^{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{2}\;\rho+\mathcal{L}^{*}\;\rho\;=\Delta t^{2}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{2}\;\rho+\mathcal{L}^{*}\;\rho\;,

(51)

because
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{1}\;\rho=0
, since
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}\;\rho=\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}\;\rho=(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})\;\rho=0
. The final equality follows from
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}
being the adjoint of the Liouvillian, which annihilates functions of the Hamiltonian. Therefore, up to second order in
\Delta t
, if
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{2}\;\rho=\rho^{\prime}
, we can re-write
Eq.

51
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}\;\rho=\Delta t^{2}\;\rho^{\prime}\;.

(52)

Then, assuming there exists a unique
\rho^{\prime\prime}
such that we can solve the equation
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}\;\rho^{\prime\prime}=\rho^{\prime}
, we are able to then write
Eq.

52
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}(\underset{\equiv\rho_{\rm GJ}}{\underbrace{\rho-\Delta t^{2}\rho^{\prime\prime}}})=0\;.

(53)

This shows that the steady-state distribution for the GJ methods,
\rho_{\rm GJ}
, satisfies
\rho_{\rm GJ}=\rho
up to second order in
\Delta t
, i.e.,
\rho_{\rm GJ}=\rho+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
, thereby demonstrating weak second order convergence in the time step if the potential is nonlinear. Of course, by construction there is no error in the distribution if the potential is linear. The above calculation further illuminates why it makes sense that the time scaling factors
d_{A}
and
d_{B}
of Eq. (
29
) are equal, since this allows for
d_{AB}^{(1)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})
to annihilate
\rho
. A small calculation shows that
(d_{A}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+d_{B}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})\rho=0
implies
d_{A}^{(1)}=d_{B}^{(1)}
, so that a second-order method of the type in
Eq.

27
must have
d_{A}=d_{B}
through first order in
\Delta t
.

II. Calculations in support of the Appendix

In this last section of the Supporting Information, we explicitly write out the calculations obtained from the definition of the
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{GJ}}^{*}
operator which were used in writing down Eq. (
50
) in the appendix. Using the formula on pg. 287 of
[
39
]
, which is based on the BCH expansion, we define
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\exp\big(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}\big)&=\exp\big(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}}\big)\exp\big(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}}\big)\exp\big(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}}\big)\\
&=\exp\bigg(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\bigg(d_{O}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+d_{AB}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t}{4}(d_{O}d_{AB}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]+d_{O}d_{AB}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]+d_{AB}^{2}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{16}(d_{O}d_{AB}^{2}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}(d_{AB}^{3}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+d_{AB}^{3}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+{d_{O}}^{2}d_{AB}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+{d_{O}}^{2}d_{AB}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]\\
&+d_{O}d_{AB}^{2}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]])\bigg)\bigg)\\
\end{split}

(54)

Similarly, we define
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\exp\big(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}}\big)&=\exp\big(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}}\big)\exp\big(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}}\big)\exp\big(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}}\big)\\
&=\exp\bigg(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\bigg(d_{AB}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}+d_{AB}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+d_{O}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t}{4}(d_{AB}^{2}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]+d_{O}d_{AB}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]+d_{O}d_{AB}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{16}(d_{O}d_{AB}^{2}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}(d_{O}d_{AB}^{2}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+d_{O}^{2}d_{AB}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+d_{AB}^{3}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+{d_{O}}d_{AB}^{2}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]\\
&+[d_{AB}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+d_{O}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[d_{AB}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+d_{O}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},d_{AB}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])\bigg)\bigg)\\
\end{split}

(55)

Expanding the time scaling factors up to second order in
\Delta t
, with
d_{O}=1+d_{O}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{O}^{(2)}\Delta t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})
and
d_{AB}=1+d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{AB}^{(2)}\Delta t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})
, leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}&=(1+d_{O}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{O}^{(2)}\Delta t^{2})\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+(1+d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{AB}^{(2)}\Delta t^{2})(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t}{4}((1+d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{O}^{(1)}\Delta t)[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]+(1+d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{O}^{(1)}\Delta t)[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]\\
&+(1+2d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t)[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{16}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]])\;.\end{split}

(56)

The zeroth order term is then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}=\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}\;,

(57)

which is the Fokker-Plank operator for Langevin dynamics. The first order term is given by

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{4}(4d_{O}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+4d_{AB}^{(1)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}])\;,

(58)

and the
\Delta t^{2}
term is given by

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{16}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+16d_{O}^{(2)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+16d_{AB}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})

(59)

\displaystyle+4(d_{AB}^{(1)}+d_{O}^{(1)})[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]+8d_{AB}^{(1)}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}])

(60)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{48}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]

(61)

\displaystyle+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]])\;.

(62)

Similarly, the reverse composition
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}}
leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}}&=(1+d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{AB}^{(2)}\Delta t^{2})(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})+(1+d_{O}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{O}^{(2)}\Delta t^{2})\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t}{4}((1+2d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t)[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]+(1+d_{O}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t)[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]\\
&+(1+d_{AB}^{(1)}\Delta t+d_{O}^{(1)}\Delta t)[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{16}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])\;,\end{split}

(63)

so that the low order terms are:

\displaystyle\Delta t^{0}

\displaystyle:\qquad\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}

(64)

\displaystyle\Delta t^{1}

\displaystyle:\qquad\tfrac{1}{4}\big(4d_{AB}^{(1)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})+4d_{O}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]\big)

(65)

\displaystyle\Delta t^{2}

\displaystyle:\qquad\tfrac{1}{16}\bigg(16d_{AB}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})+16d_{O}^{(2)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+8d_{AB}^{(1)}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]

(66)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+4(d_{AB}^{(1)}+d_{O}^{(1)})[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]\bigg)

(67)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\tfrac{1}{48}\bigg([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]

(68)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])\bigg)

(69)

With both
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}
being written out, we can now calculate the GJ operator,
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}
, through second order in
\Delta t
by once again applying the BCH formula,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\exp(\Delta t\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}})&=\exp(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}})\exp(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}})\\
&=\exp\bigg(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bigg(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}+\frac{\Delta t}{4}[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}},\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}]\\
&+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}([\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}},[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}},\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}]]-[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}},[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}},\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}]])+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\bigg)\bigg)\\
&=\exp\bigg({\Delta t}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}})+\frac{\Delta t}{8}[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}},\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}]\\
&+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{96}([\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}},[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}},\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}]]-[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}},[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}},\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}]])+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\bigg)\bigg)\;.\end{split}

(70)

Calculating each term in the expansion on the right hand side,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{BAO}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{OAB}}&=2\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}+2\Delta t(d_{O}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(1)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})\\
&+2\Delta t^{2}(d_{O}^{(2)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}))+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{16}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]])+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\\
\\
&=2\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}+2\Delta t(d_{O}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(1)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})\\
&+2\Delta t^{2}(d_{O}^{(2)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}))+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{16}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}(2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]])+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\\
\\
&=2\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}+2\Delta t(d_{O}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(1)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B})\\
&+2\Delta t^{2}(d_{O}^{(2)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}))\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}(2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]\\
&+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+2[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\end{split}

(71)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{BAO}}},\widetilde{\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{OAB}}}]&=[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}]+\Delta t(d_{O}^{(1)}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]+d_{AB}^{(1)}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]\\
&+\tfrac{1}{4}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]))\\
&+\Delta t(d_{O}^{(1)}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}]+d_{AB}^{(1)}[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}]\\
&+\tfrac{1}{4}([[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}],\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}]+[[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}],\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}]+[[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}],\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}]))+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})\\
\\
&=\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})\;,\end{split}

(72)

and,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{{BAO}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{{BAO}},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{{OAB}}]]-[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{{OAB}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{{BAO}},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{{OAB}}]]=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\;,

(73)

since only zero order terms are iterated commutators of
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\rm LD}
. Therefore, up to second order in
\Delta t
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{GJ}}&=\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}}+{\Delta t}(d_{O}^{(1)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(1)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}))\\
&+{\Delta t}^{2}(d_{O}^{(2)}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}+d_{AB}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}))\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{16}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}+\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\text{LD}},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])\\
&+\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{48}([\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]\\
&+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]]+[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A},[\mathcal{L}^{*}_{O},\mathcal{L}^{*}_{B}]])+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\;.\end{split}

(74)
