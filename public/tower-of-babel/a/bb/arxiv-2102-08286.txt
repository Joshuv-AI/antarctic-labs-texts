Guarded Kleene Algebra with Tests: Coequations, Coinduction, and Completeness
Todd Schmid, Tobias Kappé, Dexter Kozen, Alexandra Silva

Abstract
Guarded Kleene Algebra with Tests ( 𝖦𝖪𝖠𝖳 \mathsf{GKAT} ) is an efficient fragment of 𝖪𝖠𝖳 \mathsf{KAT} , as it allows for almost linear decidability of equivalence. In this paper, we study the (co)algebraic properties of 𝖦𝖪𝖠𝖳 \mathsf{GKAT} . Our initial focus is on the fragment that can distinguish between unsuccessful programs performing different actions, by omitting the so-called early termination axiom . We develop an operational (coalgebraic) and denotational (algebraic) semantics and show that they coincide. We then characterize the behaviors of 𝖦𝖪𝖠𝖳 \mathsf{GKAT} expressions in this semantics, leading to a coequation that captures the covariety of automata corresponding to these behaviors. Finally, we prove that the axioms of the reduced fragment are sound and complete w.r.t. the semantics, and then build on this result to recover a semantics that is sound and complete w.r.t. the full set of axioms.

Introduction

Kleene algebra with tests
(
\mathsf{KAT}
)
[
17
]
was introduced in the early 90’s as an extension of Kleene algebra (
\mathsf{KA}
), the algebra of regular expressions.
The core idea of the extension was simple: consider regular languages over a two-sorted alphabet, in which one sort represents Boolean tests and the other denotes basic program actions.
This seemingly simple extension enables an important application for regular languages in reasoning about imperative programs with basic control flow structures like branches (
if
-
then
-
else
) and loops (
while
).

\mathsf{KAT}
largely inherited the properties of
\mathsf{KA}
: a language model
[
22
]
, a Kleene theorem
[
19
]
, a sound and complete axiomatization
[
22
]
, and a
pspace
decision procedure for equivalence
[
8
]
.

In 2014, a specialized
\mathsf{KAT}
called
\mathsf{NetKAT}

[
4
]
was proposed to program software-defined networks.

\mathsf{NetKAT}
was later extended with a probabilistic choice operator that enabled the modelling of randomized protocols
[
9
]
.
Interestingly, there exists a decision procedure for
\mathsf{NetKAT}
program equivalence that enables practical verification of reachability in networks with thousands of nodes and links, which seems to scale almost linearly despite the
pspace
-completeness of this problem
[
10
,
34
]
.
This raised the question: do practical
\mathsf{NetKAT}
programs belong to a fragment of
\mathsf{KAT}
that has more favorable properties than the full language?

Recently, this question was answered positively
[
33
]
, in the form of
Guarded Kleene Algebra with Tests
(
\mathsf{GKAT}
), a fragment of
\mathsf{KAT}
obtained by adding a Boolean guard to the non-deterministic choice and iteration operators so that they correspond exactly to the standard
if-then-else
and
while
constructs.

\mathsf{GKAT}
is expressive enough to capture all programs used in network verification while allowing for almost linear time
1
1

1

O(n\alpha(n))
, where
\alpha(n)
is the inverse of Ackermann’s function
decidability of equivalence, thereby explaining the experimental results observed in
\mathsf{NetKAT}
.

The use of
\mathsf{GKAT}
as a framework for program analysis also raises further questions about recovering the properties of
\mathsf{KAT}
on the level of
\mathsf{GKAT}
.
Is there a class of automata that provides a Kleene theorem?
Is there a sound and complete axiomatization of
\mathsf{GKAT}
equivalence?
The original paper
[
33
]
gave incomplete answers to these questions.
First, it proposed a class of
well-nested
automata that can be used to describe the semantics of all
\mathsf{GKAT}
programs, but left open whether this class covered all automata that accept the behaviors of
\mathsf{GKAT}
programs.
Second,
\mathsf{GKAT}
was axiomatized under the assumption of
early termination
: intuitively, referring to a semantics of imperative programs where programs that fail immediately are equated to programs that fail eventually.
This semantics, though useful, is too coarse in contexts where program behavior prior to failure matters.

In this paper, we take a new perspective on the semantics of
\mathsf{GKAT}
programs and their corresponding automata, using coequations.
Coequations provide the right tool to characterize fragments of languages as they enable a precise way to remove unwanted traces.
We are then able to give a precise characterization of the behaviors of
\mathsf{GKAT}
programs and prove a completeness theorem for each of the fragments of interest.

Our contributions.

In a nutshell, the contributions of this paper are the following:

1.

We give a denotational model for
\mathsf{GKAT}
without early termination by representing the behavior as a certain kind of tree.
This allows us to design two coequations: one characterizing the behaviors denoted by
\mathsf{GKAT}
expressions, and another capturing only the behaviors of
\mathsf{GKAT}
expressions that terminate early.

2.

We obtain two completeness results for
\mathsf{GKAT}
: one for the model of the previous item and the axiomatization of
[
33
]
without the early termination axiom; and building on this, another for the full axiomatization.
The former is new; the latter provides an alternative proof to the completeness theorem presented in
[
33
]
.

3.

A concrete example of a well-nested
\mathsf{GKAT}
automaton with a non-well-nested quotient.
This settles an open question of
[
33
]
and closes the door on an alternative proof of completeness based on well-nested automata.

Guarded Kleene Algebra with Tests

At its heart,
Guarded Kleene Algebra with Tests
(
\mathsf{GKAT}

Expressions in
\mathsf{GKAT}
are concise formulas for

Formally, these expressions are drawn from a two-sorted language of

The tests are built from a finite set of

\operatorname{BExp}\ni b,c::=0\mid 1\mid t\in T\mid\bar{b}\mid b\wedge c\mid b\vee c.

Here,

denotes the negation of

\wedge
and
\vee
are conjunction and disjunction, respectively. We will use

where

is a fixed enumeration of

can be written equivalently as the disjunction of the atoms

that imply

under the laws of Boolean algebra. We will often identify each Boolean expression

with this set of atoms and write

or

.

Programs are built from tests and a finite set of
primitive programs
or
actions

Formally, programs are generated by the grammar

{\operatorname{Exp}}\ni e,f::=b\in\operatorname{BExp}\mid p\in\Sigma\mid e\cdot f\mid e+_{b}f\mid e^{(b)}

Here, a test

abbreviates the statement

is shorthand for

b

e

Some remarks are in order for axiom W3. The right-hand premise states that an expression
g
has some self-similarity in the sense that it is equivalent to checking whether
b
holds, in which case it runs
e
followed by recursing at
g
, and otherwise running
f
.
Intuitively, this says that
g
is loop-like, matching the conclusion that
g
is equivalent to
e^{(b)}\cdot f
.
However, this conclusion may not make sense when based on just the second premise.
Specifically, if we choose
e
,
f
,
g
and
b
to be
1
, we can show that the premise holds and derive
1\equiv 1^{(1)}\cdot 1
, which is to say that
assert true
is equivalent to
(while true do assert true); assert true
.
Intuitively, this should be false: the first program terminates successfully and immediately, but the second program does not.
The problem is that the loop body does not perform any actions that affect the state and make progress towards the end of the loop.

This is remedied by the left-hand premise, which distinguishes loop bodies that can accept immediately from those that cannot.
It plays the same role as the
empty word property
in Salomaa’s axiomatization of the algebra of regular events
[
31
]
. Formally, given
e\in{\operatorname{Exp}}
, the Boolean expression
E(e)
is defined inductively by setting
E(p)=0
,
E(b)=b
, and

\displaystyle E(e\cdot f)=E(e)\wedge E(f)\qquad E(e+_{b}f)=(b\wedge E(e))\vee(\bar{b}\wedge E(f))\qquad E(e^{(b)})=\bar{b}

We call
e

productive
if
E(e)\equiv 0
.
Axioms W2 and W3 are analogues of Salomaa’s axioms A
11
and R2
[
31
]
.
Specifically, W2 says that non-productive loop iterations do not contribute to the semantics.
This allows the use of W3 to reason about loops in general, for instance to prove
e^{(b)}\equiv e^{(b)}\cdot\overline{b}
, which says that the loop condition is false when a loop ends
[
33
]
.

Axiom S3 identifies a program that fails eventually with the program that fails immediately.
As a consequence,
\equiv
cannot distinguish between processes that loop forever, like
p^{(1)}
and
q^{(1)}
, even though they perform different actions
[
33
]
.
Consequently,
\mathsf{GKAT}
can be seen as a theory of
computation
schemata, i.e., programs that need to halt successfully to be meaningful.

In contrast, it is also useful to be able to reason about
process
schemata, i.e., programs that perform meaningful tasks, even when they do not terminate successfully.
To this end, we define the
reduced congruence
\equiv_{0}
generated by the axioms of
\cref
fig:GKAT axioms except S3.

Let
\left[\!\left[-\right]\!\right]:{\operatorname{Exp}}\to S
be a semantics of
\mathsf{GKAT}
. We say that
\left[\!\left[-\right]\!\right]
is
sound w.r.t.
\equiv
if for all

with
e\equiv f
, it holds that
\left[\!\left[e\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[f\right]\!\right]
.
Similarly,
\left[\!\left[-\right]\!\right]
is
sound w.r.t.
\equiv_{0}
if
e\equiv_{0}f
implies that
\left[\!\left[e\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[f\right]\!\right]
.

Since
\equiv
encodes common program laws, one might wonder whether there is a single interpretation in which programs are related by
\equiv
if and only if they have the same image.
Such an interpretation is called
free w.r.t.
\equiv
.
This question is not just of theoretical interest: a free interpretation can help decide whether programs are provably equivalent, and hence the same under any sound interpretation, by checking whether their free semantics coincide.
Naturally, the same question can be asked for
\equiv_{0}
: is there a semantics that is
free w.r.t.
\equiv_{0}
, i.e., where
e\equiv_{0}f
if and only if
e
and
f
have the same interpretation?

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In
\cref

\mathsf{GKAT}
expressions in terms of
\mathsf{GKAT}
-automata, as in
[
33
]
.
In
\cref

\mathsf{GKAT}
-automaton in which all other automata can be uniquely interpreted.
We then build a semantics that is sound w.r.t.
\equiv_{0}
in
\cref
sec:i_g_as_an_algebra.
In
\cref
sec:well_nested_coalgebras we relate our coequational description of
\mathsf{GKAT}
expressions to the
well-nested
\mathsf{GKAT}
-automata
of
[
33
]
.
In
\cref
sec:completeness, we prove that this semantics is in fact complete w.r.t.
\equiv_{0}
and, building on this, obtain a semantics that is complete w.r.t.
\equiv
.
Omitted proofs are included in the appendix.

An operational model:
\mathsf{GKAT}
-automata

In this section we discuss the small-step operational model for

[

The operational perspective provides us with the tools to describe a semantics that is complete w.r.t.

We can think of a
\mathsf{GKAT}

Depending on the most recently observed atomic test, the program either accepts, rejects, or emits an action label and changes to a new state.

Intuitively,

The final
\mathsf{GKAT}
-automaton

One way of assigning semantics to
\mathsf{GKAT}

\mathsf{GKAT}
-automaton

Concretely,

Essentially, this tree is an unfolding of the transition graph from that state.

Trees form an algebra

So far, we have seen that the behavior of a

\mathsf{GKAT}
-automaton

\mathsf{GKAT}

Intuitively, both of the examples above fail to exhibit the behaviors of

In

Whether this condition is also

Thus, a positive answer to the latter question amounts to showing that every

Such a class of automata closed under behavioral equivalence is known as a

In particular, they are closed under homomorphic images

27
,
12
,
3
]

Unfortunately, well-nested automata do not satisfy this property: we have found a well-nested automaton whose homomorphic image is not well-nested, depicted in
\cref

In other words, there exists a non-well-nested automaton whose behavior is still described by a
\mathsf{GKAT}
expression.
This also closes the door on a simpler approach to completeness described in
[
33
]

Thus, well-nested automata do not constitute a characterization of the

\mathsf{GKAT}

To obtain such a characterization, we take a slightly different approach: rather than describing shapes of these automata, we describe the shapes of the trees that they denote.

{\mathsf{U}}

It is easily shown that

Next, we present a completeness theorem w.r.t.

Theorem 17

This paper builds on

In contrast with

This explicit characterization was essential for proving completeness of the full theory of
\mathsf{GKAT}
, including the early termination axiom.

\mathsf{KAT}
equivalence without early termination has been investigated by Mamouras
[
24
]
.

Brzozowski derivatives
[

]
appear in the completeness proof of
\mathsf{KA}

[

,

,
14
]
.

[
32
]
.

[
28
]
and Pavlovic and Escardo
[

]

,

,
5
,

,
30
]
, and notably in the proof of generalized Eilenberg theorems

,

. The use of coequations in completeness proofs is, as far as we are aware, new.

9
Discussion

\mathsf{GKAT}
was introduced in
[
23
]
under the name
propositional while programs
and extensively studied in
[
33
]
as an algebraic framework to reason about simple imperative programs.
We presented a new perspective on the theory of
\mathsf{GKAT}
, which allowed us to isolate a fragment of the original axiomatization that captures the purely behavioral properties of
\mathsf{GKAT}
programs.
We solved an open problem from
[
33
]
, providing a proof that well-nested automata are not closed under homomorphisms, thereby making it unlikely that these automata can be used in a completeness proof that does not rely on uniqueness axioms.
Finally, we proved completeness for the full theory, respecting the early-termination property, in which programs that fail immediately are equated with programs that fail eventually.

There are several directions for future work that are worth investigating.
First, it was conjectured in
[
33
]
that the uniqueness axiom follows from
the other axioms of
\mathsf{GKAT}
.
This remains open, but at the time of writing we think this conjecture might be false.
Secondly, the technique we use, based on coequations, can serve as basis for a general approach to completeness proofs.
We plan to investigate other difficult problems where our technique might apply.
Of particular interest is an open problem posed by Milner in
[
25
]
, which consists of showing that a certain set of axioms are complete w.r.t. bisimulation equivalence for regular expressions.
Recently, Grabmeyer and Fokkink
[
11
]
provided a partial solution.
We believe our technique can simplify their proofs and shed further light on Milner’s problem.

We have chosen to adopt the axiomatization from
[
33
]
, which can be described as a Salomaa-style axiomatization—the loop is a unique fixpoint satisfying a side condition on termination.
We would like to generalize the results of the present paper to an axiomatization in which the loop is a least fixpoint w.r.t. an order.
The challenge is that there is no natural order in the language because the
+
of Kleene Algebra has been replaced by
+_{b}
.
However, we hope to devise an order
\leq
directly on expressions and extend the characterizations that we have to the new setting.
This new axiomatization would have the advantage of being algebraic (that is, sound under arbitrary substitution), which makes it more suitable for verification purposes as the number of models of the language would increase.

Topological Structure of

The space of trees

In this appendix, we will show that we can equip

with the compact metric

, defined

d(s,t)=\max\left\{2^{-|w|}\mathrel{\Big|}\begin{array}[]{c}w\in{\operatorname{dom}}(s)\cap{\operatorname{dom}}(t)\\
\text{ and }t(w)\neq s(w)\end{array}\right\},

where
\max\emptyset=0
.

Lemma 23
.

(Z,d)
is a metric space.

Proof.

Let
s,t\in Z
.
To show that
d
is a metric, we need to prove that
s=t
if and only if
d(s,t)=0
, and that
d
satisfies the triangle inequality.

We begin by making the observation that, if
w\in{\operatorname{dom}}(t)\setminus{\operatorname{dom}}(s)
, then
d(s,t)>2^{-|w|}
.
Let
w\in{\operatorname{dom}}(t)\setminus{\operatorname{dom}}(s)
.
Since
A\subseteq{\operatorname{dom}}(s)\cap{\operatorname{dom}}(t)
, there is a longest prefix
w^{\prime}a
of
w
such that
w^{\prime}a\in{\operatorname{dom}}(s)\cap{\operatorname{dom}}(t)
.
By assumption,
t(w^{\prime}a)\in\Sigma
, for otherwise
w
is a leaf of
t
and
w=w^{\prime}a
, contradicting the assumption that
w\mathbin{\not\in}{\operatorname{dom}}(s)
.
Moreover,
s(w^{\prime}a)\in 2
, for otherwise
w^{\prime}a
would be a node of
s
and we could find a prefix
w^{\prime}au
of
w
, for some
u\in A^{+}
, such that
wau\in{\operatorname{dom}}(s)\cap{\operatorname{dom}}(t)
, contradicting the assumption that
w^{\prime}a
is the longest prefix of
w
in
{\operatorname{dom}}(s)\cap{\operatorname{dom}}(t)
.
This means that
t(w^{\prime}a)\neq s(w^{\prime}a)
, because
\Sigma\cap 2=\emptyset
.
Hence,
d(s,t)\geq 2^{-|w^{\prime}a|}>2^{-|w|}
.

One consequence of this observation is that, if
d(s,t)=0
, then
{\operatorname{dom}}(s)={\operatorname{dom}}(t)
.
Since this means that
{\operatorname{dom}}(t)={\operatorname{dom}}(s)\cap{\operatorname{dom}}(t)={\operatorname{dom}}(s)
,
d(s,t)=0
implies that
s(w)=t(w)
for any

where either is defined.
Hence,
s=t
.

To see that
d
satisfies the triangle inequality, assume
d(s,t)=2^{-k}
.
Then there is a word
w\in{\operatorname{dom}}(s)\cap{\operatorname{dom}}(t)
such that
|w|=k
and
s(w)\neq t(w)
.
Now consider a third tree,

.
It cannot be the case that both

and

, so either

.
Whence,

Lemma 24
.

(Z,d)
is a compact metric space.

Proof.

Let
(t_{i})_{i>0}
be an infinite sequence in
Z
.
To show that
Z
is compact, we need to exhibit a convergent subsequence of
(t_{i})_{i>0}
.
This can be done as follows.

Let
\mathbf{t}^{(0)}=(t_{i})_{i>0}
, and for any

let
\mathbf{t}^{(k+1)}
be a subsequence of

(\forall i,j\in{\mathbb{N}})(\forall w\in A^{+})\ |w|\leq k+1\implies t_{i}^{(k)}(w)=t_{j}^{(k)}(w)

We claim that the subsequence

The intuitive candidate for the limit of

.
We need to show that this defines a tree in
Z

For the first domain rule, notice that if

By construction,

This means that for any

For the second domain rule, let

.
Where

By construction,

k\geq 0

.

∎

Indeed, every compact metric space is also complete, for every incomplete metric space contains a sequence with no convergent subsequence (consider an arbitrary nonconvergent Cauchy sequence).

In fact, at the time of writing, the finiteness of

Recall that any finite product of compact spaces is compact.
In particular,
Z^{n}
is compact for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Compact metric spaces are necessarily complete, so
Z^{n}
is complete as well.
This gives us access to the
Banach fixed-point theorem
, which states that any function
f:M\to M
from a complete metric space
(M,d_{M})
to itself that satisfies

(\exists z\in[0,1))(\forall x,y\in M)\ d_{M}(x,y)\leq zd_{M}(f(x),f(y))

has a unique fixed-point.
In the formula above, any
z\in[0,1)
witnessing this property is called a
contraction scalar
for
f
.

See
16

Proof.

Let
f:Z^{n}\to Z^{n}
be the function defined component-wise by

f(\mathbf{t})_{i}=s_{i1}\cdot t_{1}+_{b_{i1}}\cdots+_{b_{i(n-1)}}s_{in}\cdot t_{n}+_{b_{n}}c_{i}

where
\mathbf{t}=(t_{i})_{\leq n}\in Z^{n}
.
We are going to show that
f
is a contraction mapping in the product metric

d_{p}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}):=\max\{d(t_{i},t_{i}^{\prime})\mid i\leq n\}

on
Z^{n}
, with contraction scalar
1/2
, and deduce the result from the Banach fixed-point theorem.

To this end, let
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\in Z^{n}
be two
n
-tuples of trees, and fix an index
i\leq n
.
Clearly,

d(f(\mathbf{t})_{i},f(\mathbf{t}^{\prime})_{i})=\max\{d(s_{ij}\cdot t_{j},s_{ij}\cdot t_{j}^{\prime})\mid j\leq n\},

since any word
aw\in{\operatorname{dom}}(f(\mathbf{t})_{i})\cap{\operatorname{dom}}(f(\mathbf{t}^{\prime})_{i})
at which
f(\mathbf{t})_{i}(aw)\neq f(\mathbf{t}^{\prime})_{i}(aw)
must begin with an atom
a\in b_{ij}
for some
j\leq n
.
We argue below that, in fact,
d(s_{ij}\cdot t_{j},s_{ij}\cdot t_{j}^{\prime})\leq(1/2)d(t_{j},t_{j}^{\prime})
for any
j\leq n
.
It follows from this observation that

d(f(\mathbf{t})_{i},f(\mathbf{t}^{\prime})_{i})=\max\{d(s_{ij}\cdot t_{j},s_{ij}\cdot t_{j}^{\prime})\mid j\leq n\}\leq(1/2)\max\{d(t_{j},t_{j}^{\prime})\mid j\leq n\}=(1/2)d(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}),

which by definition of the product metric makes

a contraction scalar for
f
.

In general,
d(t,t^{\prime})\leq d(s\cdot t,s\cdot t^{\prime})
for any
s,t,t^{\prime}\in Z
, and
d(s,t)\leq(1/2)d(\partial_{a}s,\partial_{a}t)
when both derivatives are defined.
Thus, for a fixed
j\leq n
and atom

, if
s_{ij}(a)\in\Sigma
, we obtain

d(s_{ij}\cdot t_{j},s_{ij}\cdot t_{j}^{\prime})\leq(1/2)d(\partial_{a}(s_{ij}\cdot t_{j}),\partial_{a}(s_{ij}\cdot t_{j}))=(1/2)d(\partial_{a}s_{ij}\cdot t_{j},\partial_{a}s_{ij}\cdot t_{j}^{\prime})\leq(1/2)d(t_{j},t_{j}^{\prime}).

In either case,
d(s_{ij}\cdot t_{j},s_{ij}\cdot t_{j}^{\prime})\leq(1/2)d(t_{j},t_{j}^{\prime})
