Rectifying curves on a smooth surface immersed in the euclidean space
Absos Ali Shaikh ∗ and Pinaki Ranjan Ghosh

Abstract
. The main objective of the present paper is to investigate a sufficient condition for which a rectifying curve on a smooth surface remains invariant under isometry of surfaces, and also it is shown that under such an isometry the component of the position vector of a rectifying curve on a smooth surface along the normal to the surface is invariant.

1.
Introduction

In 2003, Bang-Yen Chen
[
3
]
introduced the notion of the rectifying curve in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as a curve whose position vector lies in the rectifying plane and such a curve classified by an unit speed curve in an unit sphere
S^{2}
and also obtained some of its characterization. For further properties of rectifying curves we refer the reader to see
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
. By motivating the above studies, the main goal of this paper is to investigate the nature of rectifying curves on a smooth surface
S
under an isometry to another smooth surface
\bar{S}
. Then we obtain a sufficient condition for which a rectifying curve on
S
remains invariant under isometry
F:S\rightarrow\bar{S}
. We also note that under isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, a rectifying curve on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is not necessarily transformed to a rectifying curve on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. It is also shown that the component of the position vector of a rectifying curve on a smooth surface along the normal to the surface is invariant under the rectifying curve preserving isometry of surfaces.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section 2 deals with the discussion of some rudimentary facts of Frenet-Serret equations and rectifying curves. Section 3 is devoted to the study of rectifying curves on a smooth surface and deduced the components of position vectors of such a curve along the normal to the surface. The last section is concerned with the main result (see Theorem
4.1
, Theorem
4.2
).

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some rudimentary facts of rectifying curves, isometry of surfaces and first fundamental form (for details see,
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
) which will be used throughout the paper.

Let
\gamma(s):I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
I=(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathbb{R}
, be a unit speed parametrized curve having at least fourth order continuous derivatives. Let the tangent vector of the curve
\gamma(s)
be denoted by
\vec{t}
. We consider
\vec{t^{\prime}}(s)\neq 0
, so that there is an unit normal vector
\vec{n}
along
\vec{t^{\prime}}(s)
and also a positive function
k(s)
such that
\vec{t^{\prime}}(s)=k(s)\vec{n}(s)
, where
\vec{t^{\prime}}
denote the derivative with respect to the arc length parameter
s
. The binormal vector field is defined by
\vec{b}=\vec{t}\times\vec{n}
. There is another curvature function
\tau(s)
, called torsion, and is given by the equation
\vec{b^{\prime}}(s)=\tau(s)\vec{n}(s)
. At each point on
\gamma(s)
,
\{\vec{t},\vec{n},\vec{b}\}
forms an orthonormal frame. At every point of the curve
\gamma(s)
, the planes generating by
\{\vec{t},\vec{n}\}
,
\{\vec{n},\vec{b}\}
and
\{\vec{b},\vec{t}\}
are called osculating plane, normal plane and rectifying plane respectively. The quantity
\|\vec{b^{\prime}}(s)\|
measures the rate of change of the neighboring osculating plane with the osculating plane at
s
. The Frenet-Serret equations are given by

\displaystyle\vec{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\vec{n},

\displaystyle\vec{n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k\vec{t}+\tau\vec{b},

\displaystyle\vec{b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tau\vec{n}.

A curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is called rectifying
[
3
]
if its position vector always lies in the rectifying plane of that curve. The position vector
\gamma(s)
satisfies the equation

\gamma(s)=\lambda(s)t(s)+\mu(s)b(s),

for some functions
\lambda(s)
and
\mu(s)
.

Let
\gamma(t)=\phi(u(t),v(t))
, where
t\in(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
, be a curve in a surface patch
\phi
. Then
\{\phi_{u},\phi_{v}\}
are linearly independent, and hence generates the tangent space
T_{p}\phi
at a point
p\in\phi
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\|\dot{\gamma(t)}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{u}\dot{u}+\phi_{v}\dot{v})\cdot(\phi_{u}\dot{u}+\phi_{v}\dot{v}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{u}\cdot\phi_{u})\dot{u}^{2}+2(\phi_{u}\cdot\phi_{v})\dot{u}\dot{v}+(\phi_{v}\cdot\phi_{v})\dot{v}^{2},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\dot{u}^{2}+2F\dot{u}\dot{v}+G\dot{v}^{2},

where
\dot{\gamma}(t)
denote the derivative with respect to the parameter
t
.

A surface
S
is said to be regular if, for each
p\in S
there exists a neighborhood
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a map
\psi:U\rightarrow V\cap S
of an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
onto
V\cap S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\psi
is differentiable, homeomorphism and the differential
d\psi_{q}
is one to one for all
q\in U
.

Definition 2.1
.

The first fundamental form of a regular surface
S
at a point
p
is a quadratic form
I_{p}:T_{p}S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

I_{p}(\dot{\gamma}(t))=<\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t)>=\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|.

Definition 2.2
.

A diffeomorphism
F:S\rightarrow\bar{S}
, where
S
and
\bar{S}
are smooth surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is an isometry if
F
takes a curve from
S
to a curve of same length on
\bar{S}
.

Isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is uniquely described as an orthogonal transformation followed by a translation. If we rotate the rectifying curve
\gamma(s)
by fixing a point
\gamma(s_{0})
then at
\gamma(s_{0})
, the Frenet-Serret frame transforms into another frame. Hence at
\gamma(s_{0})
the corresponding rectifying plane transforms into another rectifying plane. But the position vector of the curve
\gamma(s)
does not change before and after the rotation. Therefore, generally, rectifying curves are not invariant under the isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Main result

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

be an isometry, where
S
and
\bar{S}
are smooth surfaces and
\gamma(s)

\bar{\gamma}(s)

\bar{S}

(4)

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}(s)-F_{*}(\gamma(s))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(s)}{k(s)}\Big[u^{\prime 3}\Big(F_{*}\phi_{u}\times\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial u}\phi_{u}\Big)+2u^{\prime 2}v^{\prime}\Big(F_{*}\phi_{u}\times\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial u}\phi_{v}\Big)+u^{\prime}v^{\prime 2}\Big(F_{*}\phi_{u}\times\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial v}\phi_{v}\Big)

\displaystyle+u^{\prime 2}v^{\prime}\Big(F_{*}\phi_{v}\times\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial u}\phi_{u}\Big)+2u^{\prime}v^{\prime 2}\Big(F_{*}\phi_{v}\times\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial u}\phi_{v}\Big)+v^{\prime 3}\Big(F_{*}\phi_{v}\times\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial v}\phi_{v}\Big)\Big].

\bar{S}

\phi_{v}

\bar{\phi}_{v}

(5)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial u}\phi_{u}+F_{*}\phi_{uu},

\displaystyle F_{*}\phi_{u}\times\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial v}\phi_{v}

\displaystyle F_{*}\phi_{v}\times\frac{\partial F_{*}}{\partial v}\phi_{v}

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+2u^{\prime 2}v^{\prime}F_{*}(\phi_{u}\times\phi_{uv})+u^{\prime}v^{\prime 2}F_{*}(\phi_{u}\times\phi_{vv})+u^{\prime 2}v^{\prime}F_{*}(\phi_{v}\times\phi_{uu})+2u^{\prime}v^{\prime 2}F_{*}(\phi_{v}\times\phi_{uv})
