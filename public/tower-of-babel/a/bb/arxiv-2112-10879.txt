Quantitative homogenization for the obstacle problem and its free boundary
Gohar Aleksanyan, Tuomo Kuusi

Abstract
. In this manuscript we prove quantitative homogenization results for the obstacle problem with bounded measurable coefficients. As a consequence, large-scale regularity results both for the solution and the free boundary for the heterogeneous obstacle problem are derived.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
35B27, 35R35, 35B45, 60K37, 60F05

1.
Introduction

In this manuscript we investigate the stochastic homogenization of the obstacle problem. Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a given Lipschitz domain. Let
\varphi\in H^{1}(U)
be the given obstacle function and let
g\in H^{1}(U)
be boundary values, and assume that
g\geq\varphi
on
\partial U
. Assuming that the measurable coefficient field
\mathbf{a}
is uniformly elliptic, i.e.,
I_{d}\leq\mathbf{a}(x)\leq\Lambda I_{d}
for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider the following energy

(1.1)

J_{\varepsilon}[u]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{U}\mathbf{a}\big(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\big)\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)dx,

and the minimization problem

(1.2)

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{K}}J_{\varepsilon}[u],\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{K}:=\big\{u\in g+H_{0}^{1}(U)\,:\,u\geq\varphi\big\}\,.

Notice that the convex set
\mathcal{K}
is not empty, because we assume that
g\geq\varphi
on
\partial U
.

The existence and uniqueness of the minimizer can be shown via direct methods in the calculus of variation, see for example,
[
28
]
.
Via variational methods it is easy to verify that the minimizer
u^{\varepsilon}
satisfies the following system of inequalities in a weak sense:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\geq 0&&\mbox{ in }U\\
&-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}=0&&\mbox{ in }\{u^{\varepsilon}>\varphi\}\cap U\\
&u^{\varepsilon}\geq\varphi&&\mbox{ in }U\\
&u^{\varepsilon}=g&&\mbox{ on }\partial U.\end{aligned}\right.

Here, and in what follows, we denote
\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}:=\mathbf{a}\big(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}\big)
for small
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and assume that
\mathbf{a}
is symmetric and satisfies the following uniform ellipticity condition: There exists a constant
\Lambda\in[1,\infty)
such that, for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}(x)\xi\cdot\xi\geq|\xi|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt|\mathbf{a}(x)|\leq\Lambda.

The set
\varOmega(u^{\varepsilon}):=U\cap\{u^{\varepsilon}>\varphi\}
is called the
non-coincidence set
, and

\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon}):=\partial\varOmega(u^{\varepsilon})\cap U
is called the
the free boundary
. The
coincidence
or
contact set
is denoted by
\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon}):=\{u^{\varepsilon}\equiv\varphi\}
.
By (
1.3
), the minimizer
u^{\varepsilon}
is a weak supersolution over the whole domain
U
and
u^{\varepsilon}
is
\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}
-harmonic in the non-coincidence set. Observe that the free boundary
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
depends on the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
and is not known a priori. Moreover, we see that
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi)
in the interior of the set
\{u^{\varepsilon}=\varphi\}
, while

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=0
in the non-coincidence set. Hence, in general, the flux
\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}
is discontinuous along the free boundary.

The obstacle problem has numerous applications, from option pricing in financial mathematics to tumor growth problems in biology. Free boundary problems of similar nature appear in mathematical modeling of various problems in physics and life sciences. Our contribution is to study these type of problems in heterogeneous, possibly random, medium.

When investigating free boundary problems, we ask two main questions. First, what is the optimal regularity of the solution? Second, what can we say about the regularity of the free boundary? In the context of homogenization, we additionally ask the question whether we can approximate the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
with a solution to the corresponding homogenized obstacle problem as
\varepsilon\to 0
: Minimize

(1.5)

J[u]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{U}{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)dx,

over
u\in\mathcal{K}
. The symmetric matrix
{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}
is the
homogenized matrix
, which can be identified under suitable deterministic or stochastic
ergodicity
assumption. The exact assumptions on the coefficient field will be discussed in Subsection
2.3
. The subsequent question is if and when we can prove homogenization of the corresponding free boundaries?

Our goal is to give a rather general condition on the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{a}
, which allows to treat both
qualitative
and
quantitative
homogenization at the same time. Indeed, we show that under a suitable
sublinearity condition
for the correctors and the flux correctors, described in Section
2.3
, we obtain homogenization estimates. More precisely, for
m=\lceil-\log_{3}\varepsilon\rceil
and
{\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m}=(-\tfrac{1}{2}3^{m},\tfrac{1}{2}3^{m})^{d}
, we let
\phi_{m,e}\in H^{1}({\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m})
solve the equation

(1.6)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}(e+\nabla\phi_{m,e})=0\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in }{\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m},

and then find a
3^{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic skew-symmetric (
\mathbf{S}_{ij}=-\mathbf{S}_{ji}
) tensor
\mathbf{S}
solving

(1.7)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{S}_{m,ij}^{e}=\partial_{x_{j}}\big(\mathbf{a}(e+\nabla\phi_{m,e})\big)_{i}-\partial_{x_{i}}\big(\mathbf{a}(e+\nabla\phi_{m,e})\big)_{j},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{S}_{m,ij}^{e}\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}({\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m}),

and coarsened homogenized coefficients via

(1.8)

\displaystyle{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}_{m}e=\strokedint_{{\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m}}\mathbf{a}(e+\nabla\phi_{m,e}).

Our assumption then reads as that there exists a homogenized matrix
{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}
and a monotone increasing function
t\mapsto\mathcal{E}(t)
such that the quantity

(1.9)

|{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}_{m}-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}|+3^{-m}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\||\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(e_{k})}|+|\phi_{m,e_{k}}|\|_{\underline{L}^{2}({\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m})}\right)\leq\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon),\hskip 10.00002ptm:=\lceil-\log_{3}\varepsilon\rceil

is small. Our homogenization estimates are all given in terms of
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)
. Notice that
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)
is centered at the origin and it is in fact a function of the coefficient field
\mathbf{a}
in
{\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m}
. We suppress this dependence from the notation. However, one should keep in mind that
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{a}|_{{\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m}})
and we can thus extend the definition of
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)
for any given point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
using translation by setting

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,x)=\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{a}(x+\cdot)|_{{\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{m}}).

For example, in the random case a typical form would be that
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,x)=\mathcal{X}(x)\varepsilon^{\alpha}
for a stationary random field
x\mapsto\mathcal{X}(x)
with certain stochastic integrability. Our goal in this paper, in a nutshell, is to show that
smallness of
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)
implies large-scale regularity in the obstacle problem.
We would like to point out here, however, that we are not aiming at
optimal rates
of homogenization in the obstacle problem, since this would call much more careful analysis. We will not discuss this point any further in this manuscript.

For the regularity results, we need a stronger, more quantified control of the error. Indeed, for a given parameter
\nu\in(0,1)
,
we assume that, for every
s,t\in(0,\infty)
such that
s\leq t
, we have that

(1.11)

\displaystyle s^{-\nu}\mathcal{E}(s)\leq t^{-\nu}\mathcal{E}(t)\,.

In particular, this is satisfied with
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{X}\varepsilon^{\nu}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is a universal constant possibly depending on the realization of the coefficient field
\mathbf{a}
. Under the assumption (
1.11
), we also define the so called
minimal scale
: for every
\alpha,\sigma\in(0,1]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, set

(1.12)

\displaystyle R_{\sigma,\alpha}(x):=\inf\bigl\{r\in(0,\infty)\,:\,\bigl(\mathcal{E}\bigl(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r},x\bigr)\bigr)^{\alpha}<\sigma\bigr\},\hskip 20.00003ptR_{\sigma,\alpha}=R_{\sigma,\alpha}(0)\,.

This object is fundamental in the regularity theory, especially in the context of the stochastic homogenization, originating from the paper of Armstrong and Smart
[
8
]
in the random setting, see also
[
6
,
31
,
34
]
. The original ideas on large-scale regularity go back to seminal papers of Avellaneda-Lin
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

Now
let us proceed to more detailed discussion of the problem under investigation. Let
V^{\varepsilon}:=u^{\varepsilon}-\varphi\geq 0
, then we derive from (
1.3
) that
V^{\varepsilon}\geq 0
satisfies the following variational inequality in a weak sense

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla V^{\varepsilon})=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi)\chi_{\{V>0\}},

with corresponding boundary conditions. In order to be able to control the growth of the solution
V^{\varepsilon}
at free boundary points, we need some
control over
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi)
, which oscillates rapidly as
\varepsilon\to 0+
and is, in general, merely a distribution in
H^{-1}(U)
.
To remedy this, we notice that for a given obstacle
\varphi
and boundary values
g\in H^{1}(U)
, we obtain by integration by parts that the energy in (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

(1.13)

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}[u]

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla(u-\varphi)\cdot\nabla(u-\varphi)+(u-\varphi)(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\int_{U}\left((g-\varphi)(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi)+\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla(g-\varphi)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\varphi\right)\,.

The second integral on the right is null-Lagrangian for fixed obstacle and boundary data, so letting
v=u-\varphi\geq 0
, in fact we obtain that
v
is a nonnegative minimizer of the first integral on the right, with corresponding boundary data
g-\varphi
. This leads us to consider the so-called
normalized obstacle problem
: For given
\varphi\in H^{1}(U)
, define
\varphi^{\varepsilon}\in\varphi+H_{0}^{1}(U)
to be the solution of

(1.14)

-\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi^{\varepsilon}\right)=-\nabla\cdot\left({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla\varphi\right):=f\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in }U\,.

Letting
v^{\varepsilon}=u^{\varepsilon}-\varphi^{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
v=u-\varphi\geq 0
we derive the following Euler-Lagrange equations from the minimization problem, now with the normalized right-hand side:

(1.15)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla v^{\varepsilon})=f\chi_{\{v^{\varepsilon}>0\}}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla v)=f\chi_{\{v>0\}}\,.

In order to obtain regularity of the free boundary, and large-scale regularity, we need to impose more assumptions on
f
, such as uniform positivity and Hölder continuity. As a matter of fact, in Section
5
we simplify even further and take
f
to be a positive constant
\lambda
. There are counterexamples, showing that if we allow
f=0
or
f
to be merely continuous, even when
\mathbf{a}
is a constant matrix, we cannot expect regularity of the free boundary, see
[
13
]
for further discussion. The normalization described above, and the assumptions on
f
are widely used in the context of free boundary problems. In fact in most of the literature
f\equiv 1
, since the more general situation can be addressed via stability properties of the free boundary, see
[
13
]
for details.

In view of (
1.13
), studying the normalized obstacle problem described above is the same as if we study the minimizer of (
1.2
) over
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}:=\big\{u\in g+H_{0}^{1}(U)\,:\,u\geq\varphi^{\varepsilon}\big\}
. In other words, we intrinsically add the wild oscillations to the obstacle and only after that study the obstacle problem. The solution to the original obstacle problem (
1.2
), denoted now by
u^{\varepsilon}_{\varphi}
, is close to the solution of the obstacle problem with the new obstacle
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
.
Indeed, comparison principle yields that

(1.16)

\displaystyle\left\|u^{\varepsilon}_{\varphi}-u^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\left\|\varphi^{\varepsilon}-\varphi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=O(\varepsilon),

where the smallness of
\left\|\varphi^{\varepsilon}-\varphi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
follows from the homogenization theory, see Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
for details. Furthermore, the above estimate suggests that the homogenization of solutions of the normalized obstacle problem implies homogenization of solutions to the original obstacle problem (
1.2
), with a fixed obstacle. By the triangle inequality we have that

\displaystyle\left\|u^{\varepsilon}_{\varphi}-u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|v^{\varepsilon}-v\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+2\left\|\varphi^{\varepsilon}-\varphi\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

where
v^{\varepsilon}
and
v
solve equations in (
1.15
).
Consequently, it suffices to investigate the homogenization of solutions of the normalized obstacle problem. Moreover, observe that the homogenization of the free boundary does not hold when the obstacle is fixed due to wild oscillations of
\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}
for small
\varepsilon
even if
\mathbf{a}
is smooth. This can be seen already in 1D-examples as demonstrated in Appendix
A
.

Let us briefly discuss the illuminating examples of Appendix
A
. We actually give two examples in the appendix. In Example 2 we show that even if we have a fixed strictly concave smooth obstacle
\varphi
, then the we may not have homogenization of the free boundary due to rapid oscillations of
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi)~
as
\varepsilon\to 0+
, see Figure
1
for numerical illustration. On the contrary, Example 1 shows that the homogenization of the solution and of the free boundary holds for the normalized obstacle problem. Letting
v^{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
v\geq 0
, respectively, solve

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla v^{\varepsilon})=\chi_{\{v^{\varepsilon}>0\}}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla v)=\chi_{\{v>0\}}

in the interval
(0,4)
, and letting
\alpha^{\varepsilon}
and
\alpha
be the free boundary points for
v^{\varepsilon}
and
v
, respectively, we show that then
v^{\varepsilon}\to v
and
\alpha^{\varepsilon}\to\alpha
.

1.1.
The normalized obstacle problem

Let
f\in L^{q}
,
q>d/2
,
f>0
a.e., and consider the minimizer of the following convex functional

(1.17)

J_{\varepsilon}[v]:=\int_{U}\frac{1}{2}\nabla v(x)\cdot\mathbf{a}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\nabla v(x)+f(x)v(x)dx,

over
v\in H^{1}(U)
, satisfying
v\geq 0
in
U
,
v=g>0
on
\partial U
.
There is a unique minimizer
u^{\varepsilon}
, which we call the
heterogeneous solution
to the normalized obstacle problem. Then

u^{\varepsilon}
is a weak solution to

(1.18)

\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\right)=f\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}>0\}}.

Let
u
be the minimizer of the corresponding convex functional with the homogenized matrix
{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}
;

(1.19)

J[v]:=\int_{U}\frac{1}{2}\nabla v(x)\cdot{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla v(x)+f(x)v(x)dx,

over
v\in H^{1}(U)
, satisfying
v\geq 0
in
U
,
v=g>0
on
\partial U
.
Then

(1.20)

\nabla\cdot\left({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u\right)=f\chi_{\{u>0\}}.

The function
u
is called the
homogenized solution
to the normalized obstacle problem.

The corresponding free boundaries for
u^{\varepsilon}
and
u
are defined as follows

(1.21)

\displaystyle\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})=\partial\{u^{\varepsilon}>0\}\cap U\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\varGamma(u)=\partial\{u>0\}\cap U.

Concerning the regularity of the minimizer
u^{\varepsilon}
, for merely measurable coefficients, the solution is Hölder regular by De Giorgi-Nash-Moser type arguments, see for example
[
29
]
.
If coefficients are
C^{0,\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then the minimizer is
C^{1,\alpha}
-regular.
When the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{a}
is constant (or sufficiently smooth), both the regularity of the solution and of the free boundary are well understood. The optimal regularity of solutions is due to Frehse,
[
27
]
. When
f
is uniformly positive and Hölder continuous, the free boundary regularity is fully investigated by Caffarelli,
[
17
,
19
]
, see also some recent contributions such as
[
23
,
24
]
.
The more general case, when
f
is Dini-continuous, the regularity of the free boundary is derived in see
[
13
]
.

The obstacle problem with sufficiently regular variable coefficients have been studied using energy methods, see for example
[
39
,
26
]
. However, there are more challenges appearing when the coefficients are merely measurable. Blank, jointly with collaborators, studied free boundary problems with rough coefficients,
[
16
,
14
,
15
]
.
In
[
14
]
, the authors prove quadratic decay bounds and non-degeneracy at free boundary points assuming that
\mathbf{a}_{ij}
and
f
are bounded measurable and uniformly positive, see Lemma
2.7
. In particular the authors show that the free boundary has zero Lebesgue measure. In
[
16
,
14
,
15
]
the authors discuss the regularity of the free boundary, additionally assuming that
a_{ij},f\in VMO
.

1.2.
Summary of main results

Now let us refer to the results obtained in the current manuscript. We investigate the closeness of
u^{\varepsilon}
and
u
, the corresponding free boundaries
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
and
\varGamma(u)
, and derive large-scale regularity results for
u^{\varepsilon}
and
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
.

Following
[
6
]
, we denote by
\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
respectively the spaces of
{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}
-harmonic and
\mathbf{a}
-harmonic functions, with growth strictly less than polynomials of degree
k+1
. More rigorously,

(1.22)

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}_{k}:=\bigl\{u\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,:\,-\nabla\cdot({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u)=0,~\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{-k-1}\|u\|_{{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r})}}=0\bigr\},

and

(1.23)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{k}:=\bigl\{u\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,:\,-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}\nabla u)=0,~\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{-k-1}\|u\|_{{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r})}}=0\bigr\}.

By the Liouville theorem,
\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
is the space of affine functions.
Under the assumption (
1.11
), it turns out that
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
can be identified with the limits of
\phi_{m,e}
in (
1.6
) as
m\to\infty
. The dimension of
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
is
d+1
and it can be thought as a space of “heterogenous affine functions”. This space gives the right concept to measure
C^{1,1}
-regularity of solutions of the obstacle problem.
These type of large-scale regularity results were originally developed in the periodic setting by Avellaneda and Lin in a series of papers
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. As a matter
of fact, a version of the following theorem has been commented in the periodic non-divergence case already in
[
10
]
. Our contribution here is to prove the corresponding result also in the stochastic setting.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\nu\in(0,1]
and assume that
\mathcal{E}
satisfies (
1.11
). There exist constants
\alpha,\sigma\in(0,1]
and
C<\infty
, all depending only on
(\nu,d,\Lambda)
, such that the following claim holds. If
R_{\sigma,\alpha}
defined in (
1.12
) satisfies
R_{\sigma,\alpha}\leq 1
,
f\in C^{0,\beta}(B_{1})
and
u^{\varepsilon}
is a weak solution of

(1.24)

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=f\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}>0\}}\textrm{ in }B_{1},

then there exists
\psi\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
such that, for every
r\in[R_{\sigma,\alpha},1]
, we have

(1.25)

\|u^{\varepsilon}-\psi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\leq Cr^{2}\big(\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{1})}+C\|f\|_{C^{0,\nu}(B_{1})}\big).

Now let us discuss our results on the regularity of the free boundary
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
.
Let
\lambda>0
, and define the set of homogeneous half-space solutions to the homogenized obstacle problem
\nabla\cdot({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u)=\lambda\chi_{\{u>0\}}
, as follows

(1.26)

\mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{0}:=\left\{h(x)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{(x\cdot e)_{+}^{2}}{e\cdot{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}e}\,:\,e\in\partial B_{1}\right\}.

The following theorem is the second main result of this article.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\beta,\vartheta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
and
\lambda>0
. Let
\nu\in(0,1)
be such that the condition (
1.11
) holds. There exist constants
\alpha(d,\Lambda)\in(0,1)
,
C(\nu,\beta,\vartheta,\lambda,d,\Lambda)<\infty
, and
\sigma,r_{0}\in(0,1)
,
\gamma\in(0,r_{0}]
, all depending only on
(\beta,\vartheta,\lambda,d,\Lambda)
, such that the following claim is valid. Let
u^{\varepsilon}
solve the normalized obstacle problem

(1.27)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=\lambda\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}>0\}}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in }B_{1},

and assume that
0\in\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
. Suppose that

(1.28)

\displaystyle\inf_{r\in[\gamma r_{0},\gamma^{-1}r_{0}]}\frac{|\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon})\cap B_{r}|}{|B_{r}|}\geq\vartheta>0

and that
R_{\sigma,\alpha}\leq\gamma r_{0}
, where
R_{\sigma,\alpha}
is defined in (
1.12
). Then, for every
r\in[R_{\sigma,\alpha},\frac{1}{2})
, there exists
h^{(r)}\in\mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{0}
such that, for every
s\in[r,\frac{1}{2})
,

(1.29)

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}-h^{(r)}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{s})}\leq Cs^{2}\Bigr(s^{\beta}\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{0}}\|u^{\varepsilon}-h\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}+\bigl(\mathcal{E}(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r})\bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigl)\,.

Motivated by the result of Theorem
1.2
, we may give a definition of a regular free boundary points. Let
\alpha,\sigma,r_{0},\gamma
be from Theorem (
1.2
),

Definition 1.3

(Large-scale regular free boundary point)
.

Let
\lambda>0
, and
u^{\varepsilon}
solve

(1.30)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=\lambda\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}>0\}}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in }B_{2}\,.

We say that
x_{0}\in\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})\cap B_{1}
is a
large-scale regular free boundary point
if

(1.31)

\inf_{s\in[\gamma r,\gamma^{-1}r]}\frac{|\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon})\cap B_{s}(x_{0})|}{|B_{s}(x_{0})|}\geq\vartheta>0\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptr\in\big[\gamma^{-1}R_{\sigma,\alpha}(x_{0}),r_{0}\big]\,.

A few comments are appropriate. First, the heart of the matter is to observe that all constants are independent of
\varepsilon
. This justifies the use of the word “quantified” in the title. Second, there is no regularity assumed from the matrix
\mathbf{a}
apart from the uniform ellipticity. The homogenization guarantees that
\mathcal{E}(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r})
can be taken to be small and that it is moreover
quantified
.
In particular, in the periodic setting,
\mathcal{E}(t)=Ct
, so that by taking
r=C\delta^{-1}\varepsilon
, we see that
\|u^{\varepsilon}-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\leq\delta r^{2}
for every
r\in[C\delta^{-1}\varepsilon,c\delta^{1/\beta}]
. One could interpret this as “large-scale uniqueness of blow-ups” in the sense that the half space solution is fixed for all scales larger than
C\varepsilon
, which implies that also the normal direction of the free boundary is fixed in large-scale regularity sense. Similar types of statements remain valid in the stochastic case, but now the minimal scale
R_{\sigma,\alpha}
will be a
random object
.

Finally, there still remains a very interesting open problem, namely, under what condition one would be able to prove (or give a counterexample under some reasonable conditions) that the free boundary would be not only large-scale flat, but flat in the traditional sense. For example, as we were already discussing that in the periodic setting Theorem
1.2
yields large-scale regularity up to scales
C\varepsilon
. Assuming some smoothness of coefficients, Caffarelli’s results would imply regularity from
c\varepsilon
scale on. Thus, the missing range is
[c\varepsilon,C\varepsilon]
. In fact, we suggest the following open problem for future investigation. Assume that the matrix
\mathbf{a}
is periodic and sufficiently regular. Can we then obtain uniform regularity results for the free boundary
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
, in a neighborhood of a regular free boundary point?
In this setting, there exist blow-up solutions with respect to
u^{\varepsilon}
: for a fixed
x_{0}\in\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
and a subsequence
r_{j}\to 0+
there exists
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}(x):=\lim_{r_{j}\to 0+}\frac{u^{\varepsilon}(r_{j}x+x_{0})}{r_{j}^{2}}
, and
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}(x_{0})\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\lambda\chi_{\{u_{0}^{\varepsilon}>0\}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
However, the blow-ups of
u^{\varepsilon}
depend on
\varepsilon
, and a very careful analysis with respect to both heterogenous and homogeneous halfspace blow-up solutions will be needed.

1.3.
Organization of the manuscript

In Section 2 we gather the known homogenization estimates for the Dirichlet problem, and the known regularity theory for the obstacle problem. An expert in the field may skip this section.

In Section 3 we obtain a quantitative homogenization result for the solution of the obstacle problem, employing the well-known penalization method for partial differential equations with discontinuous right hand side. The idea is the following: First we show the closeness of the solution to the obstacle problem to the penalized/regularized problem in the
H^{1}
-norm. Then we prove that a quantitative homogenization result holds for the regularized problem by following the two-scale expansion argument, and employing the known estimates for first order correctors. In Section 4 we prove large-scale
C^{1,1}
- regularity for the solution of the obstacle problem with measurable coefficients.
The Section 5 is devoted to the large-scale regularity of the free boundary.
In the appendix we give two one-dimensional examples, with numerical illustrations, which further justify our assumptions.

Let

Endow
\Omega

\displaystyle:=\mbox{the~$\sigma$-algebra generated by the following family:}

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbf{a}\mapsto\int_{U}\mathbf{a}_{ij}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\,:\,\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(U),\ i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\right\}.

The largest of these

•

•

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{U}\ \mbox{and}\ \mathcal{F}_{V}\ \ \mbox{are~$\mathbb{P}$-independent for every pair~$U,V\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}$}

First we give a brief summary of the classical obstacle problem, following L. A. Caffarelli (
[
17
,
19
]
).
The main purpose here is to describe the main tools and techniques in investigation of the regularity of the free boundary.

As before
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a given Lipschitz domain and
\varphi\in H^{1}(U)
is the obstacle. Then the minimizer to the following functional

(2.17)

J[u]=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}dx,

over all functions

,
\varphi<g

\partial U

satisfies the following variational

(2.18)

\Delta u\leq 0,~u\geq\varphi,~\Delta u\cdot(u-\varphi)=0.

The following set
\varOmega(u):=U\cap\{u>\varphi\}
is called the non-coincidence set, and

\varGamma(u):=\partial\Omega(u)\cap U
is called the free boundary.

It is well known that the solution to the obstacle problem is as regular as the obstacle up to

,
[
27
]
.The solution

cannot obtain a better regularity, since
\Delta u
is discontinuous on

.
Let

, then
\Delta v=-\Delta\varphi\chi_{\{v>0\}}
. Denoting by

, we derive the

(2.19)

\Delta v=f\chi_{\{v>0\}}.

It is clear that the properties of the normalized obstacle problem above can be easily adapted for the following obstacle problem,
\nabla({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla\overline{u})=f\chi_{\{\overline{u}>0\}}
, with the homogenized matrix
{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}
.

[Frehse,

3.
Homogenization of the obstacle problem

In this section we will prove the necessary homogenization result for the obstacle problem.
Let
U
be a Lipschitz domain,
g\in W^{1,2+\delta}(U)
and
f\in L^{\infty}(U)
,
f>0
a.e..
We consider the nonnegative solutions
u^{\varepsilon}
and
u
to the following obstacle problems respectively

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}=f\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}>0\}}&&\mbox{ in }U,\\
&u=g&&\mbox{ on }\partial U,\end{aligned}\right.\hskip 20.00003pt\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\cdot{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u=f\chi_{\{u>0\}}&&\mbox{ in }U,\\
&u=g&&\mbox{ on }\partial U.\end{aligned}\right.

We will derive the homogenization result for the obstacle problem in two steps. First, we compare the solution of the obstacle problem with the solution of the penalized problem, for which merely measurable coefficients suffice. Second, we prove the actual homogenization estimates for the penalized problem, and after that tie these together simply by the triangle inequality.

The main theorem of this section reads as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
U
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
{\mathchoice{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\displaystyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\textstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptstyle\square$}}{\scalebox{1.2 }{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}_{0}
. Let
\delta\in(0,\infty]
. There exist constants
C(U,\delta,d,\Lambda)<\infty
and
\alpha(\delta,d,\Lambda)\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
such that the following statement is valid. Suppose that
g\in W^{1,2+\delta}(U)
and
f\in L^{\infty}(U)
are such that
g\geq 0
and
f>0
almost everywhere in
U
.
Let
u^{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
u\geq 0
solve the obstacle problems (
3.1
) respectively.
Then we have the estimate

(3.2)

\|u^{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}-\nabla u\|_{H^{-1}(U)}+\|\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla u^{\varepsilon}-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u\|_{H^{-1}(U)}\\
\leq C\big(\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\big)^{\alpha}(\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2+\delta}(U)}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}).

3.1.
Closeness of the two obstacle problems

We claim that the homogenization of the solution to the normalized obstacle problem imply homogenization of the solution to the original obstacle problem, i.e. with given obstacle
\varphi\in H^{1}(U)
.
For this, consider the problems

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla w^{\varepsilon}=\nabla\cdot{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla w&&\mbox{in }U,\\
&w^{\varepsilon}=w&&\mbox{on }\partial U\end{aligned}\right.

with
w\in W^{1,2+\beta}(U)\cap W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2,\infty}(U)
,
\beta\in(0,\infty]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
\beta\in(0,1)
and let
U\subseteq B_{1}
be a Lipschitz domain. Then there exist constants
\delta(\beta,d,\Lambda)\in(0,\beta]
and
C(U,\beta,d,\Lambda)<\infty
such that the following holds. If
w^{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2+\beta}(U)

and
w\in W^{1,2+\beta}(U)\cap W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2,\infty}(U)
solve (
3.3
), then, for every

,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|w^{\varepsilon}-w\|_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)+r^{\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}}\big)\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2+\delta}(U)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+C\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\big(r^{-1}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{r})}+\|\nabla^{2}w\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{r})}\big),

where

Proof.

First, define the two-scale expansion of
\varphi
as

\displaystyle\theta^{\varepsilon}(x)=w(x)+\varepsilon\eta\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{k}}w(x)\phi_{e_{k}}\big(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\big)

with
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
being a cutoff function such that
\mathds{1}_{U_{r}}\leq\eta\leq\mathds{1}_{U}
. By a direct computation (see
[
6
, Chapters 1 & 6]
or the computation in the proof of Proposition
3.5
)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\theta^{\varepsilon}=\nabla\cdot{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}_{r}\nabla w+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{g}_{\varepsilon}\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(1-\eta)(\mathbf{a}_{ij}^{\varepsilon}-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}_{r,ij})\partial_{x_{j}}w+\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{j}}(\eta\partial_{x_{k}}w)(-\mathbf{S}_{m,ij}^{\varepsilon,e_{k}}+\mathbf{a}_{ij}^{\varepsilon}\phi_{e_{k}}^{\varepsilon}).

By equations of
w^{\varepsilon}
and

w^{\varepsilon}=\theta^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\|w^{\varepsilon}-\theta^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(U)}\leq C\|\mathbf{g}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\,.

Therefore, by the triangle inequality and (
1.9
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\|w^{\varepsilon}-\theta^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(U)}\leq C\big(\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon){+}r^{\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}}\big)\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2+\delta}(U)}+C\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\big(r^{-1}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{r})}{+}\|\nabla^{2}w\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{r})}\big).

Applying then once more the bound (
1.9
) for

∎

We also record the comparison principle for the solutions.

Lemma 3.3

(Comparison principle)
.

Let
U
be a Lipschitz domain and
g\in H^{1}(U)
. Suppose that
\varphi
and
\widetilde{\varphi}
are two continuous obstacles in
H^{1}(U)
and let
u
and
\widetilde{u}
be solutions to the obstacle problem with boundary values
g
on
\partial U
and continuous up to the boundary. Then

\displaystyle\|u-\widetilde{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq\|\varphi-\widetilde{\varphi}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}.

Proof.

Let
\delta=\|\varphi-\widetilde{\varphi}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}
and suppose that
\delta>0
. Assume, on the contrary to the claim, that there is a point
x\in U
such that
u(x)>\widetilde{u}(x)+\delta
. Then, by continuity, there is a domain
V
in the interior of
U
such that
u>\widetilde{u}+\delta
in
V
and
u=\widetilde{u}+\delta
on
\partial V
. Indeed,
u=\widetilde{u}
on
\partial U
, and hence
\partial V
cannot touch
\partial U
. Now, in
V
we have that
u
is above
\varphi
and hence it is a weak solution in
V
. On the other hand,
\widetilde{u}+\delta
is a weak supersolution in
V
, and since
\widetilde{u}+\delta=u
on
\partial V
, the minimum principle implies that
\widetilde{u}+\delta\geq u
in
V
; a contradiction. Thus the claim follows.
∎

3.2.
Penalized obstacle problem

The penalization method is a well-known argument, which is widely used in the regularity theory of variational problems, see for example
[
28
]
. The core idea of the method is the following: Given a solution
u
to a partial differential equation with a discontinuous right hand side, we consider the solution of the corresponding partial differential equation with a regularized right hand side, with the same boundary values as the given function
u
. Showing that the given solution
u
is approximated by a more regular function, we then obtain regularity estimates for the given function
u
.

In this manuscript we use the penalization method in order to obtain quantitative homogenization for the obstacle problem. Below we describe the method for the obstacle problem, and derive the approximation lemma, even though we believe it can be found in the literature.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a given open bounded domain, with Lipschitz boundary, and
\mathbf{b}
be a given uniformly elliptic matrix, with measurable coefficients in
U
, satisfying the ellipticity condition
|\xi|^{2}\leq\mathbf{b}(\cdot)\xi\cdot\xi\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2}
. Let
f\in L^{\infty}(U)
be a given bounded function satisfying
f>0
a.e.. Denote by
u
the unique minimizer of the following functional

(3.5)

J[v,\mathbf{b}]=\int_{U}\frac{1}{2}\nabla v\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla v)+fv\,dx

over
v\in W^{1,2}(U)
,
v\geq 0
, satisfying the boundary condition
v=g>0
on
\partial U
.
Then
u~
satisfies the following variational inequality in a weak sense

(3.6)

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla u)=f\chi_{\{u>0\}},~u\geq 0\textrm{ in }U,~u=g\geq 0\textrm{ on }\partial U.

We see that the right hand side in the partial differential equation in (
3.6
) is a discontinuous function. The penalization method is studying an approximating problem for
(
3.6
)
, with regularized right hand side. For this, let
\beta:(-\infty,+\infty)\to[-1,1]
be an approximation of the function
\chi_{\{v>0\}}
, for simplicity, we take
\beta
to be the following Lipschitz continuous function,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\beta(t)=t~\textrm{ for }~|t|\leq 1\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\beta({t})=t/|t|~\textrm{ for }~|t|>1,

and denote by
\gamma\in C^{1,1}
its integral function
\gamma(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\beta(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}
.
Let

(3.8)

\displaystyle\beta_{s}(t):=\beta(\tfrac{t}{s})\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{s}(t)=s\gamma(\tfrac{t}{s})\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{ so that }\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{s}^{\prime}(t)=\beta_{s}(t).

Denote by
u^{s}
the unique minimizer of the following energy

(3.9)

J_{s}[v,\mathbf{b}]=\int_{U}\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla v\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla v)+f\gamma_{s}(v)dx

over
v\in H^{1}(U)
, satisfying the boundary condition
v=g\geq 0
on
\partial U
, or equivalently

(3.10)

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla u^{s})=f\beta_{s}(u^{s}),~u^{s}=g\geq 0\textrm{ on }\partial U.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
U
be a Lipschitz domain. Let
q\in(\frac{d}{2},\infty]
and let
f\in L^{q}(U)
be a given function such that
f
is positive almost everywhere in
U
and let
g\in H^{1}(U)
be a nonnegative function in
U
. Let
\mathbf{b}
be a symmetric matrix satisfying the ellipticity condition
|\xi|^{2}\leq\mathbf{b}(\cdot)\xi\cdot\xi\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2}
.
If
u
is the solution to the obstacle problem

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla u)=f\chi_{\{u>0\}},\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle u-g\in H_{0}^{1}(U)

and
u^{s}
solves the penalized equation

(3.11)

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla u^{s})=f\beta_{s}(u^{s}),~u^{s}-g\in H_{0}^{1}(U),

then
u^{s}
is a nonnegative continuous functions and

(3.12)

0\leq\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla u^{s})\leq f\textrm{ in a weak sense}.

Furthermore,
u^{s}\to u
as
s\to 0+
with the following quantitative estimates:

(3.13)

0\leq u^{s}-u\leq s\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{in }U

and

(3.14)

\|\nabla u^{s}-\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}(U)}s.

Proof.

Step 1.

We first argue that both
u
and
u^{s}
are Hölder continuous functions for every
s>0
. This is implied by the De Giorgi-Nash-Moser theory since both
f\chi_{\{u>0\}}
and
f\beta_{s}(u^{s})
belong to
L^{q}(U)
with
q>\frac{d}{2}
. Notice also that
x\mapsto\beta_{s}(u^{s}(x))
is Hölder continuous as well.

Step 2.
We next show that
u^{s}
is a nonnegative function, giving also (
3.12
). To this end, let
x_{0}~
be a point of minimum
for
u^{s}
and suppose, on the contrary, that
u^{s}(x_{0})<0
. This implies that
x_{0}
is an interior point of
U
since
g\geq 0
. By Hölder regularity of
u
and non-negativity of the boundary values, there exists positive
r=r(s,x_{0})
such that
u^{s}<0
in
B_{r}(x_{0})
. It thus follows that

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla u^{s})=f\beta_{s}(u^{s})<0~\textrm{ a.e. in }~B_{r}(x_{0}),

which means that
u^{s}
is a continuous weak supersolution in
B_{r}(x_{0})
with an interior minimum point. By the strong minimum principle, implied by the weak Harnack inequality, we hence obtain that
u^{s}
has to be a negative constant in
B_{r}(x_{0})
, which violates the fact that
f\beta_{s}(u^{s})<0
a.e. through the equation; a contradiction. Therefore
u^{s}
is nonnegative in
U
and, consequently, (
3.12
) is valid.

Step 3.

We then show that (
3.13
) holds. Let
w^{s}:=u^{s}-u
.
First we show that
w^{s}\leq s
. For this, let
E:=\{u^{s}>s\}\cap U
and observe that if
u^{s}(x)\leq s
for some
x\in U
, then
w^{s}(x)\leq s
as well since
u\geq 0
.
Thus
w_{s}\leq s
on
U\setminus E
. Moreover,
w^{s}\leq s
on
\partial E
and

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla w^{s})=f(1-\chi_{\{u>0\}})\geq 0~\textrm{ in }~E.

Therefore
w_{s}
is a continuous subsolution in an open set
E
and it attains its maximum on the boundary of
E
, which implies
w^{s}\leq s
in the whole
E
. In conclusion, (
3.13
) is valid.

Step 4.

We prove that
w^{s}=u^{s}-u\geq 0
. If
u(x)=0
for some
x\in U
, then
w^{s}(x)\geq 0
by Step 1. We thus need to show that
w^{s}\geq 0
in the open set

V=\{u>0\}\cap U
. Obviously,
w^{s}\geq 0
on
\partial V
, and

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}\nabla w^{s})=f(\beta_{s}(u^{s})-1)\leq 0~\textrm{ in }V,

which means that
w^{s}
is a weak supersolution in the set
V
and therefore it attains its minimum on the boundary of
V
and, therefore,
w^{s}\geq 0
.

Step 5.

It remains to prove (
3.14
). This follows by testing the subtracted equations of
u^{s}
and
u
, and from (
3.13
),

(3.15)

\displaystyle\|\nabla u^{s}-\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U}(\nabla u^{s}-\nabla u)\cdot\mathbf{b}(\nabla u^{s}-\nabla u)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U}f\left|\beta_{s}(u^{s})-\chi_{\{u>0\}}\right|(u^{s}-u)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}(U)}\|u-u^{s}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}(U)}s.

The proof is complete.
∎

Proposition 3.5

U

\displaystyle\int_{B_{h}}\zeta_{h}=1,~\|\nabla^{k}\zeta_{h}\|\leq C(k,d)h^{-k-d},~k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

\eta_{r}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)

\displaystyle 0\leq\eta_{r}\leq 1,~\eta_{r}=1\textrm{ in }U_{2r},~\eta_{r}\equiv 0\textrm{ in }U\setminus U_{r},\hskip 10.00002pt|\nabla^{k}\eta_{r}|\leq C(k,d,U)r^{-k},

and
U_{r}:=\{x\in U;~\dist(x,\partial U)>r\}

Step 1.
Bounds for

and for its convolution. First, since

C(U,d,\Lambda)<\infty

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}u_{s}\|_{L^{q}(U_{r/2})}\leq Cr^{-d/2}\left(r^{-1}\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\right).

Using Young’s inequality for convolutions, we have that, for every
h\in(0,r/2\wedge 1)

(3.21)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\big(\eta_{r}\nabla(\zeta_{h}\ast u_{s})\big)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{-d/2}\left(r^{-1}\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-d/q}r^{-d/2}\left(r^{-1}\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\right).

Moreover, using an elementary computation (see
[
6

(3.22)

\displaystyle\left\|\eta_{r}\nabla(u^{s}-\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})\right\|_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq C|U|^{1/2-1/q}h\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{q}(U_{r/2})}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{1-d/q}r^{-d/2}\left(r^{-1}\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\right).

Finally, by the Meyers’ estimate, see Lemma

which gives us, by Hölder’s inequality, that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u^{s}\right\|_{L^{2}(U\setminus U_{2r})}\leq Cr^{\frac{\sigma}{2+\sigma}}\left(\|g\|_{W^{1,2+\delta}(U)}+\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\right).

Step 2.

We derive the following estimate

(3.24)

\|\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)-f\beta_{s}(u^{s})\|_{H^{-1}(U)}\leq C\big(\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\big)^{\alpha}\left(\|g\|_{W^{1,2+\delta}(U)}+\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\right)

with constants
C(U,d,\Lambda)<\infty
and
\alpha(\delta,d,\Lambda)\in(0,1)

By a direct computation, we get

\displaystyle\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\nabla(u^{s}-\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})+(1-\eta_{r})\nabla\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\eta_{r}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\big(e_{k}+\nabla\phi_{e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\big)\partial_{x_{k}}\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s}+\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{d}\phi_{e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla\left(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s}\right).

Since

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\big(e_{k}+\nabla\phi_{m,e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\big)=0

(3.25)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})\cdot\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})(e_{k}+\nabla\phi_{m,e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}))

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\left((1-\eta_{r})\nabla u^{s}+\eta_{r}\nabla(u^{s}-\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\left(\phi_{m,e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})\right).

On the other hand, using the fact that

has constant coefficients together with the equation for

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})\cdot{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}e_{k}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(\eta_{r}{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})

\displaystyle=f\beta_{s}(u^{s})-\nabla\cdot({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\eta_{r}\nabla(u^{s}-\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s}))-\nabla\cdot((1-\eta_{r}){\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u^{s}).

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)-f\beta_{s}(u^{s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}u^{s})\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})(e_{k}+\nabla\phi_{m,e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}))-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}e_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\nabla\cdot\left((\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}})\big((1-\eta_{r})\nabla u^{s}+\eta_{r}\nabla(u^{s}-\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})\big)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla\cdot\left(\phi_{m,e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}u^{s})\right).

Furthermore, we write
\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})(e_{k}+\nabla\phi_{e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}))-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}e_{k}
by means of the finite volume skew-symmetric flux corrector

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{j}\mathbf{S}_{m,ij}^{(e_{k})}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})=\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})(e_{k}+\nabla\phi_{m,e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}))-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}e_{k}\right)_{i}.

By skew-symmetry of

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}u^{s})\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})(e_{k}+\nabla\phi_{m,e_{k}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}))-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}e_{k}\right)=\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(e_{k})}\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}u^{s})\right).

It follows that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)-f\beta_{s}(u^{s})\|_{H^{-1}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})-{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}})\big((1-\eta_{r})\nabla u^{s}+\eta_{r}\nabla(u^{s}-\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})\big)\right\|_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left\|\left(\mathbf{a}\phi_{m,e_{k}}+\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(e_{k})}\right)(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}u^{s})\right\|_{L^{2}(U)}.

Employing the definition of
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)
in , we finally derive

\displaystyle\|\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)-f\beta_{s}(u^{s})\|_{H^{-1}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\left\|\nabla(\eta_{r}\partial_{x_{k}}u^{s})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}+\left\|\nabla u^{s}\right\|_{L^{2}(U\setminus U_{2r})}+\left\|\eta_{r}\nabla(u^{s}-\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s})\right\|_{L^{2}(U)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(r^{-d/2-1}\big(\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)h^{-d/q}+h^{1-d/q}\big)+r^{\frac{\sigma}{2+\sigma}}\right)\left(\|g\|_{W^{1,2+\delta}(U)}+\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\right).

Optimizing in

and

with the exponent

, we obtain the desired inequality (
3.24

\alpha=\frac{\beta\sigma}{2+\sigma}
.

Step 3.
We show that

(3.26)

\|\nabla u^{s,\varepsilon}-\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}\leq C\|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}-f\beta_{s}(w^{s,\varepsilon})\|_{H^{-1}(U)}

\displaystyle\|\nabla u^{s,\varepsilon}-\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U}\left(\nabla u^{s,\varepsilon}-\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\mathbf{a}\left(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}\right)\left(\nabla u^{s,\varepsilon}-\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{U}\left(u^{s,\varepsilon}-w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)f(\beta_{s}(u^{s,\varepsilon})-\beta_{s}(w^{s,\varepsilon}))

\displaystyle+\|\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)-f\beta_{s}(w^{s,\varepsilon})\|_{H^{-1}(U)}\|\nabla u^{s,\varepsilon}-\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(U)}.

Step 4.

It follows from (

\displaystyle\|\nabla u^{s,\varepsilon}-\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla w^{s,\varepsilon}\right)-f\beta_{s}(u^{s})\|_{H^{-1}(U)}+C\|f\beta_{s}(w^{s,\varepsilon})-f\beta_{s}(u^{s})\|_{H^{-1}(U)}.

We have, by Hölder’s inequality and the Sobolev inequality, that, for every

with

\displaystyle\int_{U}(f\beta_{s}(w^{s,\varepsilon})-f\beta_{s}(u^{s}))g

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{d}(U)}\|\beta_{s}(w^{s,\varepsilon})-\beta_{s}(u^{s})\|_{L^{2}(U)}\|g\|_{L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{s}\|f\|_{L^{d}(U)}\|w^{s,\varepsilon}-u^{s}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{s}\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\|w^{s,\varepsilon}-u^{s}\|_{L^{2}(U)}.

Furthermore, we have by the definitions of
w^{s,\varepsilon}~
and
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)
, together with (

\displaystyle\|w^{s,\varepsilon}-u^{s}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq C\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\|\eta_{r}\nabla\zeta_{h}\ast u^{s}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq C\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)r^{-d/2}\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(U)}.

The proof is complete.

4.
Large-scale
C^{1,1}
-regularity of the solution

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
, which says that the solution to normalized obstacle problem satisfies
C^{1,1}
-estimate on large-scales. Let
u^{\varepsilon}\geq 0
solve the variational inequality:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=f\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}>0\}}\textrm{ in }U.

The idea of the proof is to combine boundedness of solutions close to the free boundary, provided by (
2.30
), together with the regularity of the equation
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=f
, proved in Lemma
4.2
below. Indeed, if
x_{0}\in\varGamma({u^{\varepsilon}})
, then, by (
2.30
),

(4.1)

\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}u^{\varepsilon}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}r^{2}

provided that

. Here the constant
C>0
depends on
\Lambda,d
. It is known that the quadratic decay bound above does not yet imply
C^{1,1}
-regularity of solutions, in general, if
f
belongs merely to
L^{\infty}(B_{1})
. In fact, even in the case of smooth coefficients, we need to impose further assumption on
f
in order to obtain
C^{1,1}
-regularity. Thus, we assume that
f
is Hölder continuous, which together with the homogenization then yields the large-scale
C^{1,1}
-regularity for
u^{\varepsilon}
. The terminology “large-scale” stems from the rescaling of the problem. If we set
R=\varepsilon^{-1}
, we may rescale
u^{\varepsilon}
to be the solution in a very large ball

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}\nabla u)=f(\varepsilon\cdot)\chi_{\{u>0\}}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ in }B_{2R}.

Next, recall definition of the linear space of
{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}

1.22
),

\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{k}:=\{u\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,:\,-\nabla\cdot({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u)=0,~\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{-(k+1)}\|u\|_{{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r})}}=0\}.

By the Liouville theorem,

consists only of

-harmonic polynomials of degree at most

. Recall also the definition in (

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{k}:=\bigl\{u\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,:\,-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}\nabla u)=0,~\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{-k-1}\|u\|_{{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r})}}=0\bigr\}.

As before, we use the notation
\psi^{\varepsilon}=\psi(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})
for the rescaled members of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
.

Denote the minimal scale by

(4.2)

\displaystyle R_{\sigma,\alpha}:=\inf\bigl\{r\in(0,\infty)\,:\,\bigl(\mathcal{E}\bigl(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r}\bigr)\bigr)^{\alpha}\leq\sigma.\bigr\}

The following proposition is essentially

We omit the rest of the details of the proof.

5.
Homogenization and large-scale regularity of the free boundary

In this section we discuss the regularity of the free boundary of the normalized heterogeneous problem. Throughout this section
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
is fixed. Let
u^{\varepsilon}~
and
u
be solutions to the normalized obstacle problem, respectively, with coefficients
\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}
and
{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}
,
with
f\equiv\lambda
, and recall that

\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
and
\varGamma(u)
denote the corresponding free boundaries.

Our goal is to answer the following two questions;

(1)

How close is
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
to
\varGamma(u)
?

(2)

What can we say about the regularity of
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
?

The discussion of the regularity of the free boundary for the homogenized obstacle problem evolves by studying the rescaled solutions around free boundary points, for
x_{0}\in\varGamma(u)
, denote
u_{r,x_{0}}=\tfrac{u(rx+x_{0})}{r^{2}}
. Geometrically, we zoom around a free boundary point, and investigate the limit as
r\to 0+
. By classifying the blow-ups and showing their uniqueness at a given free boundary point, we understand the local behavior of the free boundary. Furthermore, if we quantify the rate of the convergence to the blow-up solution as
r\to 0+
, we automatically obtain local estimates on the regularity of the free boundary.

The argument described above is no longer applicable, when we are given merely bounded measurable coefficients. Indeed,
let
\varepsilon>0
be a fixed number, and
x_{0}\in\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
be a free boundary point. Denote by

(5.1)

u^{\varepsilon}_{r,x_{0}}(x):=\frac{u^{\varepsilon}(rx+x_{0})}{r^{2}},\textrm{ where}~r<\dist(x_{0},\partial B_{1}).

Then
u^{\varepsilon}_{r,x_{0}}\geq 0
solves the following obstacle problem

(5.2)

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}_{x_{0},r}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{r,x_{0}}(x))=\lambda\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}_{r,x_{0}}>0\}},

where

\mathbf{a}_{x_{0},r}:=\mathbf{a}\left({rx+x_{0}}\right)~\textrm{ and }~\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}_{x_{0},r}:=\mathbf{a}\left(\tfrac{rx+x_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right).

The matrix
\mathbf{a}(rx+x_{0})
is uniformly elliptic with the given constants.

For a moment, assume that
\mathbf{a}
has continuous entries, i.e.
\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}_{x_{0},r}\to\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}({x_{0}})
as
r\to 0
.
If

u^{\varepsilon}_{r_{j},x_{0}}\to u_{0}^{\varepsilon}
as
r_{j}\to 0+
, then
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\geq 0
is a global solution to the following obstacle problem

(5.3)

\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}({x_{0}})\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{0})=\lambda\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}_{0}>0\}}.

If
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
is a half-space solution of the form

(5.4)

u_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x)=\lambda\frac{(x\cdot e)_{+}^{2}}{2e\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}(x_{0})e},

where
e\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is a unit vector, then
x_{0}
is called a regular free boundary point in a usual sense. If
u^{\varepsilon}_{r,x_{0}}\to u_{0}^{\varepsilon}
as
r\to 0+
, then
e
is the approximate unit normal vector to
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
at
x_{0}
.

In a more general situation, without additional assumptions imposed on the matrix
\mathbf{a}
, we cannot define the blow-up solution in the form (
5.4
). Instead we rely on an alternative definition for regular free boundary points, based on the density of the coincidence set
\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon})
, see Definition
1.3
.

A closely related quantity in the analysis of the free boundaries is so-called minimum diameter of a set.

Definition 5.1
.

Given
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the minimum diameter
of
U
, denoted
\mindiam(U)
, is the infimum among the distances between pairs of parallel hyperplanes enclosing
U
.

Notice that we have the following trivial lower bound for the minimum diameter by means of the density:

(5.5)

2\geq\frac{\mindiam(\varLambda\cap B_{r})}{r}\geq\frac{|\varLambda\cap B_{r}|}{d|B_{r}|}.

The Caffarelli’s alternative in the measure form,
[
16
,
15
]
says that if
\mathbf{a}\in VMO
, then there exists

(5.6)

\lim_{r\to 0+}\frac{|\varLambda(u)\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}=\frac{1}{2},

and
x_{0}
is a regular point,
or otherwise

(5.7)

\lim_{r\to 0+}\frac{|\varLambda(u)\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}=0,

and
x_{0}
is a singular point.
Let us mention that when
\mathbf{a}={\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}
, a stronger result holds: for any
\theta>0
small there exists an
0<r_{0}<1
and
0<\tau<1
such that if for some

t<r_{0}

\frac{|\varLambda(u)\cap B_{t}(x_{0})|}{|B_{t}(x_{0})|}\geq\theta,~\textrm{ then}~~\inf_{0<r\leq\tau t}\frac{|\varLambda(u)\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\theta,

where the parameters

are universal, i.e. they depend on the given parameters, but not on the solution

.

In the next easy example, we see that the
Caffarelli’s alternative does not hold, when
\mathbf{a}
is discontinuous, since we may have a contact set, with density
\frac{1}{4}
at a free boundary point.

Example:
Let in dimension 2,
\mathbf{a}=I
in

\mathbf{a}=2I

u^{0}=x^{2}\chi_{\{x>0\}}+y^{2}\chi_{\{y>0\}}

, in

has density

at the origin, the free boundary has a corner point. In other words, the blow-up of

at the origin is a homogeneous global solution, which is neither one-dimensional, nor a polynomial.

by Lemma
5.3
.

Next, we apply
[
19
, Lemma 6]
on the density of the level strip with the parameters
s\in[\gamma r,\gamma^{-1}r]
and
h=s^{1/3}\|u^{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}^{1/3}
,

\displaystyle|(\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon})\setminus\varLambda(u))\cap B_{s}(x_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq|\{0<u<h^{2}\}\cap B_{s}(x_{0})|+\frac{1}{h^{2}}\int_{\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon})\cap B_{s}(x_{0})}u

\displaystyle\leq C|B_{s}|\Bigl(\frac{h}{s}+\frac{1}{h^{2}}\|u^{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C|B_{s}|\bigl(\gamma^{-2}r^{-2}\big(\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\big)^{\alpha}\bigr)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq C|B_{r}|(\gamma^{-2}\sigma)^{1/3}.

Thus, by assuming that
C(\gamma^{-2}\sigma)^{1/3}\leq 2^{-d-1}
, we deduce the lower bound for the density of
\varLambda(u)
around the free boundary point
x_{0}\in\varGamma(u)
:

\displaystyle|\varLambda(u)\cap B_{s}(x_{0})|

\displaystyle\geq|\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon})\cap\varLambda(u)\cap B_{s}(x_{0})|

\displaystyle=|\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon})\cap B_{s}(x_{0})|-|(\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon})\setminus\varLambda(u))\cap B_{s}(x_{0})|

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-d-1}\theta|B_{s}|\,.

Observe then that
v(x)=u\bigl({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}^{1/2}(x-x_{0})\bigr)
solves
\Delta v=\lambda\chi_{v>0}
in
B_{1/\Lambda}
,
0\in\varGamma(v)
and that the above display and (
5.5
) imply that
\mindiam(\varLambda(u)\cap B_{s})\geq 2^{-d-1}\Lambda^{-1/2}\theta s
as well for every
s\in(\gamma r,\gamma^{-1}r)
. Thus, by choosing
\vartheta=2^{-d-1}\Lambda^{-1/2}\theta
and
\rho=(C^{-1}\delta\theta)^{2}/(4\Lambda)
, we find by Lemma
5.4
constants
s_{0},\tau\in(0,1)
, both depending only on
(\delta,\vartheta,\lambda,d,\Lambda)
, such that, for every
s\in(0,\gamma s_{0}\wedge r]
,

\displaystyle\inf_{e\in\partial B_{1}}\sup_{x\in B_{\tau\gamma^{-1}s/12}}\Big|v(x)-\frac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x)_{+}^{2}\Big|\leq\frac{\theta^{2}}{4\Lambda}\delta(\tau\gamma^{-1}s/12)^{2}\,.

Finally, taking
r_{0}=\gamma s_{0}
so that
r\leq r_{0}
implies that
\gamma^{-1}r\leq s_{0}
, changing variables back and taking
\gamma=\tau/(12\Lambda^{1/2})
and
s=r
above, we deduce that

\displaystyle\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}^{0}}\|u^{\varepsilon}-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}r^{2}\,.

Hence (
5.10
) follows by the triangle inequality by demanding further that the parameter
\sigma
is so small that
C\sigma^{\alpha}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
. The proof is complete.
∎

5.2.
Improvement of flatness

Next we show that flatness implies large-scale regularity. The flatness assumption does not involve the rescaled solutions, but it helps us to keep track of rescalings, thereby obtaining large-scale regularity of the free boundary.

We employ a flatness improvement argument, following J. Andersson,
[
2
]
, written for the Signorini problem.
The method can be compared to the De Giorgi’s flatness implies regularity theorem for minimal surfaces,
[
30
]
.
For the obstacle problem the argument takes a much easier form, see Lemma
5.5
, and it can be found in the lecture notes by J. Andersson
[
1
]
and by J. Serra
[
42
]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\beta,\gamma\in(0,1)
. There exist constants
r_{0},\delta\in(0,1)
, both depending only on
(\beta,\gamma,\lambda,d,\Lambda)
, such that if
u
is a solution of
\nabla\cdot{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u=\lambda\chi_{\{u>0\}}
in
B_{1}
, the origin is a free boundary point of
u
, that is,
0\in\varGamma(u)
, and that

(5.17)

\displaystyle\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\|u-h\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{1/2})}\leq\delta,

then there exists
h\in\mathbb{H}^{0}
such that, for every
r\in(0,r_{0}]
,

(5.18)

\|u-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\leq\gamma r^{2+\beta}\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\|u-h\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{1/2})}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
{\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}=I
, and hence
u
solves
\Delta u=\lambda\chi_{\{u>0\}}
. Assume that
0\in\varGamma(u)
. Fix
\beta,\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Our initial goal is to show that there exist small constants
\delta,s\in(0,1)
, both depending only on
(\beta,\gamma,\lambda,d)
, such that if

\displaystyle\inf_{(e,t)\in\partial B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\|u-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1/2})}\leq\delta,

then

(5.19)

\inf_{(e,t)\in\partial B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\|u-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{s})}\leq\gamma s^{2+\beta}\inf_{(e,t)\in\partial B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\|u-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{1/2})}.

We argue by contradiction, based on the following elementary argument in Step 1.

Step 1.

We show that, for every
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
c(\gamma,d)\in(0,1/2]
such that if
w\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(B_{1})
is the weak solution of the equation

\Delta w=0\textrm{ in }B_{1}^{+}:=B_{1}\cap\{x_{d}>0\},\textrm{ and }w\equiv 0\textrm{ in }B_{1}^{-}:=B_{1}\cap\{x_{d}<0\}.

and if
w
satisfies the conditions
\int_{B_{c}}w(x)(x_{d})_{+}\,dx=0
and
\int_{B_{c}}w(x)x_{k}(x_{d})_{+}\,dx=0
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
, then, for every
s\in(0,c]
,

(5.20)

\displaystyle\left\|w\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(B_{s}\right)}\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}s^{2+\beta}\left\|w\right\|_{\underline{L}^{2}\left(B_{1}\right)}.

The proof is based on the fact that we find by odd reflection a harmonic function
w^{\prime}
in
B_{1}
, which coincides with
w
in
B_{1}\cap\{x_{d}>0\}
. By harmonicity, there exists a radius
r(d)\in(0,1)
such that
w^{\prime}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}
in
B_{r}
, where
p_{k}
is a harmonic homogeneous polynomial of degree
k
. Now, the boundary condition
w^{\prime}=0
on
B_{1}\cap\{x_{d}=0\}
dictates that
p_{0}=0
,
p_{1}(x)=a_{0}x_{d}
and
p_{2}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}a_{k}x_{k}x_{d}
. Then the orthogonality conditions imply that
p_{1}=0
and
p_{2}=0
, and that, for every
s\in(0,c]
,
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{s})}\leq Cs^{3}\|w\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}
. We may then choose
c
small enough so that
c^{1-\beta}2C\leq\gamma
and obtain (
5.20
).

Step 2.
Fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
and let
c(\gamma,d)
be as in Step 2. We now prove (
5.23
) via contradiction.
We assume, on the contrary, that there exist a sequence
\{u^{j}\}_{j}
solving
\Delta u^{j}=\lambda\chi_{\{u^{j}>0\}}
with
0\in\varGamma({u_{j}})
and another sequence
\{h^{j}\}_{j}
with
h^{j}(x)=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e^{j}\cdot x+t_{j})_{+}^{2}
such that
\delta_{j}:=\|u^{j}-h^{j}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
while

(5.21)

\displaystyle\inf_{(e,t)\in\partial B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\|u^{j}-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{c})}>\gamma c^{2+\beta}\|u^{j}-h^{j}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}\left(B_{1}\right)}.

After rotation, assume, up to a relabeled subsequence, that
e^{j}\to e_{d}
as
j\to\infty
. Set

(5.22)

v^{j}=\frac{u^{j}-{h}^{j}}{\|u^{j}-{h}^{j}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}}.

Since both
u^{j}
and
h^{j}
are nonnegative minimizers of the energy
\int_{B_{1}}(\tfrac{1}{2}|\nabla w|^{2}+\lambda w)
, one actually obtains using admissible competitors that
v^{j}\Delta v^{j}\geq 0
and
\Delta(v^{j})^{2}\geq 0
weakly in
B_{1}
. The latter implies that
\|v^{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leq C(d)
. Using the first one, it is possible to show, up to passing to a subsequence, that there exists
v\in H_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(B_{1})
such that
v^{j}\to v
in
H_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(B_{1})
as
j\to\infty
. Now, since
0\in\varGamma({u^{j}})
, we have that
|v^{j}(0)|=\delta_{j}^{-1}(t^{j})_{+}^{2}\leq C
. The non-degeneracy, on the other hand, implies that
(t^{j})_{-}^{2}\leq C\delta_{j}
, and thus
t_{j}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Applying Lemma
2.7
, we may show that
v^{j}\to v=0
in
B_{1}\cap\{x_{d}<0\}
(using (
2.31
)) and that
\Delta v=0
in
B_{1}\cap\{x_{d}>0\}
(using (
2.30
)). Furthermore, for given
s\in(0,1)
, letting
h^{j,s}=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e^{s,j}\cdot x+t^{s,j})_{+}^{2}
be the minimizing half-space solution for
u^{j}
in
B_{s}
and taking variations with respect to
t
and
e
, we see that, for every
e^{\prime}\in(e^{s,j})^{\perp}
,

\int_{B_{s}}(h^{j,s})^{1/2}\left(u^{j}(x)-h^{j,s}\right)dx=0=\int_{B_{s}}(h^{j,s})^{1/2}(e^{\prime}\cdot x)\left(u^{j}(x)-h^{j,s}\right)dx\,.

Since
t_{j}\to 0
and
e^{j}\to e_{d}
as
j\to\infty
, the above display yields, after passing to the limit, that
\int_{B_{c}}v(x)(x_{d})_{+}\,dx=0
and
\int_{B_{c}}v(x)x_{k}(x_{d})_{+}\,dx=0
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
. Thus we are in position to use the result of Step 2 and obtain a contradiction with (
5.21
) for large enough

by the strong convergence in
L^{2}
.

Step 3.
Iterating (
5.23
) and using the sup bound for
(u-h)^{2}
gives us that, for every
s\in(0,c]
,

(5.23)

\inf_{(e,t)\in\partial B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\|u-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{s})}\leq\gamma s^{2+\beta}\inf_{(e,t)\in\partial B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\|u-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{1/2})}.

Let
e^{s}
and
t^{s}
realize the minimum on the right and denote
h^{s}=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e^{s}\cdot x+t^{s})_{+}^{2}
. Then, by the triangle inequality and simple manipulations,

\displaystyle s\bigl(s|e^{2s}-e^{s}|+|t^{2s}-t^{s}|\bigr)\leq C\gamma s^{2+\beta}\inf_{(e,t)\in\partial B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\|u-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{1/2})}

Thus both
\{e^{s}\}_{s}
and
\{t^{s}\}_{s}
are Cauchy sequences, and
e^{s}\to e
and
t^{s}\to t
as
s\to 0
. Since
u(0)=0
, we must have that
t=0
, and therefore, from the previous display,
s|e^{s}-e|+|t^{s}|\leq C\gamma s^{2+\beta}\inf_{(e,t)\in\partial B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\|u-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{1/2})}
. Defining hence
h=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(e\cdot x)_{+}^{2}
and taking

smaller, we conclude with (
5.18
). The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Fix
\beta\in(0,1)
. There exist constants
\rho,\omega\in(0,\tfrac{1}{4})
, both depending only on
(\beta,\lambda,d,\Lambda)
, such that if

u^{\varepsilon}
and
u
solve

(5.24)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=\lambda\chi_{\{u^{\varepsilon}>0\}}&&\textrm{ in }B_{1},\\
&\nabla\cdot({\overbracket{\mathbf{a}}}\nabla u)=\lambda\chi_{\{u>0\}}&&\textrm{ in }B_{1},\\
&u^{\varepsilon}=u&&\textrm{ on }\partial B_{1},\end{aligned}\right.

and if

\displaystyle\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\|u^{\varepsilon}-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}+\|u^{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leq\omega\,,

(5.26)

\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\|u^{\varepsilon}-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\rho})}\leq\rho^{2+\beta}\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\|u^{\varepsilon}-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+2\|u^{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\,.

Proof.

Fix
\beta\in(0,1)
and let
\gamma=2^{-2-\beta}
. Let
r_{0},\delta
be the parameters from Lemma
5.5
corresponding
(\beta,\gamma)
. Fix
\rho=\frac{1}{2}r_{0}

\theta\in(0,1)

. We assume that
\omega
is so small that
s\leq\rho
.
Suppose that
0\in\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
and that
h(x)=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\frac{(e\cdot x+t)_{+}^{2}}{e\cdot\overline{\mathbf{a}}e}
is the member of
\mathbb{H}
realizing the infimum in (
5.25
).

We first show that
|t|
in the definition of
h

2.31

\displaystyle cr^{2}\leq\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\leq\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}+\|u^{\varepsilon}-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\leq\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}+\omega\,.

Therefore, taking

\displaystyle\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}>0\hskip 10.00002pt\implies\hskip 10.00002ptt>-r=-2(\omega/c)^{1/2}\geq-C\theta s\,.

On the other hand, since

Using these we obtain that, for
h^{(r)}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{(e_{r}\cdot x)_{+}^{2}}{e_{r}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{a}}e_{r}}

\displaystyle\|h_{r}-h_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{s})}\leq Cs^{2}\Bigl(s^{2\beta}+r^{\beta}+\big(\mathcal{E}\bigl(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{s}\bigr)\big)^{2\alpha}+\big(\mathcal{E}\bigl(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r}\bigr)\big)^{\alpha}\Bigr)\,.

Combining this with (
5.28
) then concludes the proof.
∎

We finally remark that if the minimal scale is independent of the reference point, that is, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

(5.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,x)\leq\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

then we will obtain large-scale regularity of the free boundary. Notice that (
5.29
) is valid, for example, in the case of periodic or almost periodic homogenization.

Theorem 5.7
.

Assume that (
5.29
) is valid. Let
u^{\varepsilon}
be a solution to the heterogenous obstacle problem, with
f\equiv\lambda>0
. Assume that

0\in\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})
, and
\|u^{\varepsilon}-h\|_{L^{2}(B_{2})}=\delta
is small enough, then
\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})\cap B_{1/2}
is large-scale
C^{1,\beta}
-regular, in the sense that every
x_{0}\in\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})\cap B_{1/2}
is a large-scale regular free boundary point, (see Definition
1.3
), and (
1.29
) holds at
x_{0}
.

Proof.

The Lebesgue measure of the set
\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon}_{r})\cap B_{1}
can be estimated from below, employing Lemma
5.6
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that the half-space solution
h
in the
Lemma
5.6

depends on direction
e=e_{d}
. Hence

(5.30)

\|u^{\varepsilon}_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}\cap\{x_{d}<0\})}\leq Cr^{\beta}\delta+C\mathcal{E}(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r})^{\alpha}.

Now let
x^{0}\in B_{1}\cap\{x_{d}<0\}
be such that
u_{r}^{\varepsilon}(x^{0})>0
, and let
2\tau:=-x_{d}^{0}=-x^{0}\cdot e_{d}>0
, then
by the non-degeneracy of
u_{r}^{\varepsilon}
,

\displaystyle c\tau^{2}\leq\sup_{B_{\tau}(x_{0})}u^{\varepsilon}_{r}\leq Cr^{\beta}\delta+C\mathcal{E}(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r})^{\alpha}.

Therefore
we obtain

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\leq Cr^{\beta}\delta+C(\mathcal{E}(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r}))^{\alpha}.

Hence,

\displaystyle|\{u_{r}^{\varepsilon}>0\}\cap\{x_{d}<0\}|\leq c_{d}\tau

is small, and

\displaystyle\frac{|\varLambda(u^{\varepsilon}_{r})\cap B_{1}|}{|B_{1}|}\geq\frac{1}{2}-C\tau>\vartheta>0,

if
r\geq R_{\sigma,\alpha}
.
Applying Theorem
1.2
, we obtain (
1.29
).

Now if
x_{0}\in\varGamma(u^{\varepsilon})\cap B_{1/2}
, then we can apply Lemma
5.6
and Theorem
1.2
for the rescaled solutions
u^{\varepsilon}_{r,x_{0}}
.
∎

Appendix A
Examples in dimension one

In this appendix we briefly discuss homogenization of the obstacle problem and its free boundary in dimension one.
We give two important one-dimensional examples, which clarify the main assumptions and the setting of our problem.

A.1.
Homogenization of the normalized obstacle problem and its free boundary in 1D

Let the coefficient field
\mathbf{a}
be a continuous function taking values in
[1,2]
. Let
u_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
be the solution of the following
one-dimensional obstacle problem, with oscillating coefficients;

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &(a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})u^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})^{\prime}=\chi_{\{u_{\varepsilon}>0\}}\quad\mbox{in }(0,4),\\
&u_{\varepsilon}(0)=0,\;u_{\varepsilon}(4)=1.\end{aligned}\right.

Denote by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{x\in(0,4)\,:\,u_{\varepsilon}(x)>0\}

the free boundary point of the problem. This is a unique point since, by the minimum principle,
u_{\varepsilon}
is positive in
(\alpha_{\varepsilon},4)
and zero on
[0,\alpha_{\varepsilon}]
. After a direct computation, the solution can be written as

(A.1)

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{x\wedge\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t-\lambda_{\varepsilon})a^{-1}(\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon})\,dt,\hskip 10.00002pt

where
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
is chosen as

(A.2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}=\Big(\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{4}ta^{-1}(\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon})\,dt-1\Big)\Big(\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{4}a^{-1}(\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon})\,dt\Big)^{-1}

to guarantee the boundary condition
u_{\varepsilon}(4)=1
. Since
a
takes values less than
2
, we see that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
is positive. Employing then the fact that
u_{\varepsilon}
is
C^{1}
in the interior
(0,2)
, we also obtain that
u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\alpha_{\varepsilon})=0
, which gives us
\alpha_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}
and thus (
A.2
) gives us the equation to solve
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
.

Next, we let
\overline{a}_{\varepsilon}
to be the coarsened coefficient and
\phi_{\varepsilon}
the corrector:

\displaystyle\overline{a}_{\varepsilon}=\Big(\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{4}a^{-1}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})\,dx\Big)^{-1}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\overline{a}_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{x}a^{-1}(\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon})\,dt-x\,.

We assume the bound

(A.3)

\displaystyle|\overline{a}_{\varepsilon}-\overline{a}|+\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,4)}\leq\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)

and
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Now let

(A.4)

\overline{a}:=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}a^{-1}(t)dt\right)^{-1},

be the homogenized matrix, and
\overline{u}
be the solution of the obstacle problem
solves

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\overline{a}\,\overline{u}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\chi_{\{\overline{u}>0\}}\quad\mbox{in }(0,4),\\
&\overline{u}(0)=0,\;\overline{u}(4)=1.\end{aligned}\right.

As above, a straightforward computation yields that

\displaystyle\overline{u}(x)=\overline{a}^{-1}\int_{\overline{\alpha}}^{x\wedge\overline{\alpha}}(t-\overline{\alpha})\,dt,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\alpha}=4-\sqrt{2\overline{a}}

We will then verify that

(1)

\alpha_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\overline{\alpha}
a.s. as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,

(2)

u_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\overline{u}
a.s. as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Recall that
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
and
\overline{\alpha}
are determined by the following equations

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{4}(x-\alpha_{\varepsilon})_{+}a^{-1}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})dx=1\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{0}^{4}(x-\overline{\alpha})_{+}\overline{a}^{-1}dx=1.

On the one hand, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{4}(x-\overline{\alpha})_{+}\overline{a}^{-1}dx=\frac{1}{2}\overline{a}^{-1}(4-\overline{\alpha})^{2}.

By integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{4}(x-{\alpha_{\varepsilon}})_{+}a^{-1}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\overline{a}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(4-{\alpha_{\varepsilon}})^{2}-\overline{a}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{4}\phi(x)\,dx.

Therefore, it follows, after some manipulations, that

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}-\alpha_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{8-\overline{\alpha}-\alpha_{\varepsilon}}\biggl(\overline{a}_{\varepsilon}(\overline{a}^{-1}-\overline{a}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})+2\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{4}\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx\biggr)

The assumption (
A.3
) then implies that

\displaystyle|\overline{\alpha}-\alpha_{\varepsilon}|\leq C\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon).

Therefore
\alpha_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\overline{\alpha}
.
Using the explicit formulas for
u_{\varepsilon}
and for
\overline{u}
, together with the convergence

\alpha_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\overline{\alpha}
, we obtain the convergence

u_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\overline{u}
. Furthermore, the calculations show that in the periodic case

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}-\overline{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\varepsilon\textrm{ and }|\alpha_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\alpha}|\leq C\varepsilon.

A.2.
The homogenization of the free boundary does not hold with a fixed obstacle

We next show an example demonstrating the instability of the free boundary in homogenization with a fixed obstacle. Indeed, one cannot expect homogenization to hold even if the coefficients are smooth.

To this end, let
\varphi~
Be a given concave function in the interval
(-1,1)
, called an obstacle, we study the solution to the one-dimensional obstacle problem

(A.5)

\displaystyle u\geq\varphi,~(\overline{a}u^{\prime})^{\prime}\leq 0,\textrm{ and }~(\overline{a}u^{\prime})^{\prime}=0\textrm{ in }\{u>\varphi\}.

and the solutions
u_{\varepsilon}
to corresponding heterogenous obstacle problem

(A.6)

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}\geq\varphi,~({a^{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{\prime}\leq 0,\textrm{ and }~({a_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{\prime}=0\textrm{ in }\{u_{\varepsilon}>\varphi\},

with boundary condition
u^{\varepsilon}(x)=u(x)
when
x=\pm 1
.

In order to simplify our calculations, we can take
\varphi=\frac{1}{2}-{x^{2}}
, and the boundary conditions
u^{\varepsilon}(\pm 1)=u(\pm 1)=0
.

Figure 1.
Given obstacle
\varphi=\frac{1}{2}-x^{2}
, there are plotted both heterogeneous and homogenized solutions, together with
\varphi
and the free boundary. Also the difference, magnified by a factor of
6
, between heterogeneous and homogenized solutions is plotted.

By (
A.5
),
u
is a linear function in every connected component of the set
\{u>\varphi\}
, furthermore,
if
\overline{x}
is the free boundary point closest to the boundary point
-1
, then by straightforward calculations,
u(x)=\varphi^{\prime}(\overline{x})(x-\overline{x})+\varphi{(\overline{x})}
in the interval
(-1,\overline{x})
(The first order Taylor polynomial of
\varphi
at
\overline{x}
).
Letting
u(-1)=0
, we obtain
\overline{x}=-1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
.

Now let us see what similar calculations give for
u_{\varepsilon}
. Denote by
x_{\varepsilon}
the free boundary point closest to the boundary point
-1
,
then

\displaystyle(a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})u^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})^{\prime}=0\textrm{ in the interval }(-1,x_{\varepsilon}),

and at the free boundary point
x_{\varepsilon}
we get

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})=\varphi(x_{\varepsilon}),~u^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})=\varphi^{\prime}(x_{\varepsilon}),

Hence

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(x)=a(\tfrac{x_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon})\varphi^{\prime}(x_{\varepsilon})\int_{x_{\varepsilon}}^{x}a^{-1}(\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon})dt+\varphi(x_{\varepsilon}),\textrm{ in the interval }(-1,x_{\varepsilon}).

Letting
u_{\varepsilon}(-1)=0
, we get

(A.7)

\displaystyle x_{\varepsilon}a(\tfrac{x_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon})\int^{x_{\varepsilon}}_{-1}a^{-1}(\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon})dt+\frac{1}{2}-x_{\varepsilon}^{2}=0

Assuming that
x_{\varepsilon}\to\overline{x}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we get a contradiction in (
A.7
) due to the oscillation of
a(\tfrac{x_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon})
. Hence no homogenization of free boundary result can be expected given a fixed obstacle
\varphi
.
This happens, since we have no control over
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi)
as
\varepsilon\to 0+
.
This example fully justifies the normalization of the right hand side in the context of homogenization of the obstacle problem.
