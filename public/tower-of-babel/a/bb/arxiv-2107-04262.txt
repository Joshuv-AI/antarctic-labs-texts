Performance enhancements for a generic conic interior point algorithm
Chris Coey, Lea Kapelevich, Juan Pablo Vielma

Abstract
In recent work, we provide computational arguments for expanding the class of proper cones recognized by conic optimization solvers, to permit simpler, smaller, more natural conic formulations. We define an exotic cone as a proper cone for which we can implement a small set of tractable (i.e. fast, numerically stable, analytic) oracles for a logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barrier for the cone or for its dual cone. Our extensible, open source conic interior point solver, Hypatia, allows modeling and solving any conic optimization problem over a Cartesian product of exotic cones. In this paper, we introduce Hypatia’s interior point algorithm. Our algorithm is based on that of Skajaa and Ye 2015 , which we generalize by handling exotic cones without tractable primal oracles. With the goal of improving iteration count and solve time in practice, we propose a sequence of four enhancements to the interior point stepping procedure of Skajaa and Ye 2015 , which alternates between prediction and centering steps: (1) we use a less restrictive central path proximity condition, (2) we adjust the prediction and centering directions using a new third order directional derivative barrier oracle, (3) we use a single backtracking search on a quadratic curve instead of two line searches, and (4) we use a combined prediction and centering step. We implement 23 useful exotic cones in Hypatia. We summarize the complexity of computing oracles for these cones, showing that our new third order oracle is not a bottleneck, and we derive efficient and numerically stable oracle implementations for several cones. We generate a diverse benchmark set of 379 conic problems from 37 different applied examples. Our computational testing shows that each stepping enhancement improves Hypatia’s iteration count and solve time. Altogether, the enhancements reduce the shifted geometric means of iteration count and solve time by over 80% and 70% respectively.

Introduction

Any convex optimization problem may be represented as a conic problem that minimizes a linear function over the intersection of an affine subspace with a Cartesian product of primitive proper cones (i.e. irreducible, closed, convex, pointed, and full-dimensional conic sets).
Under certain conditions, a conic problem has a simple and easily checkable certificate of optimality, primal infeasibility, or dual infeasibility
(
Permenter et al. 2017
)
.
Most commercial and open-source conic solvers (such as CSDP
(
Borchers 1999
)
, CVXOPT
(
Andersen et al. 2011
)
, ECOS
(
Serrano 2015
)
, MOSEK
(
MOSEK ApS 2020
)
, SDPA
(
Yamashita et al. 2003
)
, Alfonso
(
Papp and Yıldız 2017
)
) implement primal-dual interior point methods (PDIPMs) based on the theory of logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barrier (LHSCB) functions.
Compared to first order conic methods (see
O’Donoghue et al. 2016
on SCS solver), idealized PDIPMs typically exhibit higher per-iteration cost, but have a lower iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\nu}\log(1/\varepsilon))
iterations to converge to
\varepsilon
tolerance, where
\nu
is the barrier parameter of the LHSCB.
We limit the scope of this paper to conic PDIPMs, but note that there are other notions of duality and PDIPMs for convex problems outside of the conic realm (see e.g.
Karimi and Tunçel 2018
).

1.1
Conic optimization with Hypatia solver

Hypatia
(
Coey et al. 2020
)
is an open-source, extensible conic primal-dual interior point solver.

Hypatia is available at

See

Hypatia is written in the Julia language

(

A key feature of Hypatia is a generic cone interface that allows users to define new

An exotic cone is a proper cone for which we can implement a small set of tractable LHSCB oracles (listed in
Section

1.2
) for either the cone or its dual cone.
Defining a new cone through Hypatia’s cone interface makes both the cone and its dual available for use in conic formulations.
We have already predefined 23 useful exotic cone types (some with multiple variants) in Hypatia.
Several cones are new and required the development of LHSCBs and efficient procedures for oracle evaluations (see
Coey et al. 2021c
;
Kapelevich et al. 2021

Advanced conic solvers such as MOSEK 9 currently recognize at most only a handful of standard cones: nonnegative, (rotated) second order, positive semidefinite (PSD), and three-dimensional exponential and power cones.
In
Coey et al. 2020
, we show across seven applied examples that modeling with the much larger class of exotic cones often permits simpler, smaller, more natural conic formulations.
Our computational experiments with these examples demonstrate the potential advantages, especially in terms of solve time and memory usage, of solving natural formulations with Hypatia compared to solving standard conic extended formulations with either Hypatia or MOSEK 9.
However, a description of Hypatia’s algorithms was outside the scope of
Coey et al. 2020
.
In contrast, the main purpose of this paper is to introduce some key features of our exotic conic PDIPM that enable it to be both general and performant.

1.2
The Skajaa-Ye algorithm

Most conic PDIPM solvers use efficient algorithms specialized for symmetric cones, in particular, the nonnegative, (rotated) second order, and PSD cones.
Although non-symmetric conic PDIPMs proposed by
Nesterov et al. 1996
;
Nesterov 2012
can handle a broader class of cones, they have several disadvantages compared to specialized symmetric methods (e.g. requiring larger linear systems, strict feasibility of the initial iterate, or conjugate LHSCB oracles).
The algorithm by
Skajaa and Ye 2015
, henceforth referred to as
SY
, addresses these issues by approximately tracing the central path of the homogeneous self-dual embedding (HSDE)
(
Andersen et al. 2003
;
Xu et al. 1996
)
to an approximate solution for the HSDE.
This final iterate provides an approximate conic certificate for the conic problem, if a conic certificate exists.
The SY algorithm relies on an idea by
Nesterov 2012
that a high quality prediction direction (enabling a long step and rapid progress towards a solution) can be computed if the current iterate is in close proximity to the central path (i.e. it is an approximate scaling point).
To restore centrality after each prediction step, SY performs a series of centering steps.

By using a different definition of central path proximity to
Nesterov 2012
, SY avoids needing conjugate LHSCB oracles.
2
2

2

Some proposed techniques such as the Hessian scaling updates and central path proximity definitions of
Myklebust and Tunçel 2014
;
Dahl and Andersen 2021
require conjugate LHSCB oracles.

Indeed, a major advantage of SY is that it only requires access to a few tractable oracles for the primal cone: an initial interior point, feasibility check, and gradient and Hessian evaluations for the LHSCB.
In our experience, for a large class of proper cones, these oracles can be evaluated analytically, i.e. without requiring the implementation of iterative numerical procedures (such as optimization) that can be expensive and may need numerical tuning.
Conjugate LHSCB oracles in general require optimization, and compared to the analytic oracles, they are often significantly less efficient and more prone to numerical instability.

1.3
Practical algorithmic developments

For many proper cones of interest, including most of Hypatia’s non-symmetric cones, we are aware of LHSCBs with tractable oracles for either the cone or its dual cone but not both.
Suppose a problem involves a Cartesian product of exotic cones, some with primal oracles implemented and some with dual oracles implemented (as in several example formulations described in
Coey et al. 2020
).
In this case, SY can solve neither the primal problem nor its conic dual, as SY requires primal oracles.
Our algorithm generalizes SY to allow a conic formulation over any Cartesian product of exotic cones.

The focus of
Skajaa and Ye 2015
is demonstrating that SY has the best known iteration complexity for conic PDIPMs.
This complexity analysis was corrected by
Papp and Yıldız 2017
, who implemented SY in their recent MATLAB solver Alfonso
(
Papp and Yıldız 2020
;
Papp and Yıldız 2021
)
.
It is well known that performant PDIPM implementations tend to violate assumptions used in iteration complexity analysis, so in this paper we are not concerned with iteration complexity.
Our goal is to reduce iteration counts and solve times in practice, by enhancing the performance of the interior point stepping procedure proposed by SY and implemented by Alfonso.

The basic SY-like stepping procedure computes a prediction or centering direction by solving a large structured linear system, performs a backtracking line search in the direction, and steps as far as possible given a restrictive central path proximity condition.
We propose a sequence of four practical performance enhancements.

Less restrictive proximity.

We use a relaxed central path proximity condition, allowing longer prediction steps and fewer centering steps.

Third order adjustments.

After computing the prediction or centering direction, we compute a
third order adjustment
(TOA) direction using a new
third order oracle
(TOO) for exotic cones.
We use a line search in the unadjusted direction to determine how to combine it with the TOA direction, before performing a second line search and stepping in the new adjusted direction.

Curve search.

Due to the central path proximity checks, each backtracking line search can be quite expensive.
Instead of performing two line searches, we use a single backtracking search along a particular quadratic curve of combinations of the unadjusted and TOA directions.

Combined directions.

Unlike SY, most conic PDIPMs do not use separate prediction and centering phases.
We compute the prediction and centering directions and their associated TOA directions, then perform a backtracking search along a quadratic curve of combinations of all four directions.

Our TOA approach is distinct from the techniques by
Mehrotra 1992
;
Dahl and Andersen 2021
that also use higher order LHSCB information.
3
3

3

To avoid confusion, we do not use the term ‘corrector’ in this paper.
In the terminology of
Mehrotra 1992
;
Dahl and Andersen 2021
our TOA approach is a type of ‘higher order corrector’ technique, but also our unadjusted centering direction is referred to by
Skajaa and Ye 2015
;
Papp and Yıldız 2017
as the ‘corrector’ direction.

Unlike these techniques, we derive adjustments (using the TOO) for both the prediction and centering directions.
Our TOO has a simpler and more symmetric structure than the third order term used by
Dahl and Andersen 2021
, and we leverage this for fast and numerically stable evaluations.
Whereas the method by
Mehrotra 1992
only applies to symmetric cones, and
Dahl and Andersen 2021
test their technique only for the standard exponential cone, we implement and test our TOO for all of Hypatia’s 23 predefined cones.
In our experience, requiring a tractable TOO is only as restrictive as requiring tractable gradient and Hessian oracles.
We show that the time complexity of the TOO is no higher than that of the other required oracles for each of our cones.
To illustrate, we describe efficient and numerically stable TOO procedures for several cones that can be characterized as intersections of slices of the PSD cone.

Although this paper is mainly concerned with the stepping procedures, we also outline our implementations of other key algorithmic components.
These include preprocessing of problem data, finding an initial iterate, the solution of structured linear systems for search directions, and efficient backtracking searches with central path proximity checks.
We note that Hypatia has a variety of algorithmic options for these components; these different options can have a dramatic impact on overall solve time and memory usage, but in most cases they have minimal effect on the iteration count.
For the purposes of this paper, we only describe and test one set of (default) options for these components.

1.4
Benchmark instances and computational testing

We implement and briefly describe 37 applied examples (available in Hypatia’s examples folder), each of which has options for creating formulations of different types and sizes.
From these examples, we generate 379 problem instances of a wide range of sizes.
Since there is currently no conic benchmark storage format that recognizes more than a handful of cone types, we generate all instances on the fly using JuMP or Hypatia’s native interface.
All of Hypatia’s predefined cones are represented in these instances, so we believe this is the most diverse conic benchmark set available.

On this benchmark set, we run five different stepping procedures: the basic SY-like procedure (similar to Alfonso) and the sequence of four cumulative enhancements to this procedure.
Our results show that each enhancement tends to improve Hypatia’s iteration count and solve time, with minimal impact on the number of instances solved.
We do not enforce time or iteration limits, but we note that under strict limits the enhancements would greatly improve the number of instances solved.
The TOA enhancement alone leads to a particularly consistent improvement of around 45% for iteration counts.
Overall, the enhancements together reduce the iterations and solve time by more than 80% and 70% respectively.
For instances that take more iterations or solve time, the enhancements tend to yield greater relative improvements in these measures.

is associated with the primal variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

4.2
Conic certificates

Under certain conditions, there exists a simple conic certificate providing an easily verifiable proof of infeasibility of the primal
Equation

7
or dual
Equation

8
problem (via the conic generalization of Farkas’ lemma) or optimality of a given primal-dual solution.

A primal improving ray

x
is a feasible direction for the primal along which the objective improves:

\displaystyle c^{\prime}x

\displaystyle<0,

(9a)

\displaystyle-Ax

\displaystyle=0,

(9b)

\displaystyle-Gx

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{K},

(9c)

and hence it certifies dual infeasibility.

A dual improving ray

(y,z)
is a feasible direction for the dual along which the objective improves:

\displaystyle-b^{\prime}y-h^{\prime}z

\displaystyle>0,

(10a)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}y+G^{\prime}z

\displaystyle=0,

(10b)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{K}^{\ast},

(10c)

and hence it certifies primal infeasibility.

A complementary solution

(x,y,z)
satisfies the primal-dual feasibility conditions
Equations

7b
,
7c
,
8b
and
8c
, and has equal and attained primal and dual objective values:

c^{\prime}x=-b^{\prime}y-h^{\prime}z,

(11)

and hence certifies optimality of
(x,y,z)
via conic weak duality.

One of these certificates exists if neither the primal nor the dual is
ill-posed
.
Intuitively, according to
MOSEK ApS 2020
, a conic problem is ill-posed if a small perturbation of the problem data can change the feasibility status of the problem or cause arbitrarily large perturbations to the optimal solution (see
Permenter et al. 2017
for more details).

4.3
Homogeneous self-dual embedding

The HSDE is a self-dual conic feasibility problem in variables
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{p},z\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\tau\in\mathbb{R},s\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
(see
Vandenberghe 2010
), derived from a homogenization of the primal-dual optimality conditions
Equations

7b
,
7c
,
8b
,
8c
and
11
:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
s\\
\kappa\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&A^{\prime}&G^{\prime}&c\\
-A&0&0&b\\
-G&0&0&h\\
-c^{\prime}&-b^{\prime}&-h^{\prime}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\\
\tau\end{bmatrix},

(12a)

\displaystyle(z,\tau,s,\kappa)

\displaystyle\in\bigl(\mathcal{K}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq}\times\mathcal{K}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq}\bigr).

(12b)

For convenience we let
\omega\coloneqq(x,y,z,\tau,s,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+p+2q+2}
represent a point.
We define the structured
4\times 6
block matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p+q+1)\times\dim(\omega)}
such that
Equation

12a
is equivalent to:

E\omega=0.

(13)

Here we assume
E
has full row rank; in
Appendix

A
we discuss preprocessing techniques that handle linearly dependent rows.
Note that
\omega=0
satisfies
Equation

12
, so the HSDE is always feasible.
A point
\omega
is an interior point if it is strictly feasible for the conic constraints
Equation

12b
, i.e.
\omega
satisfies
(z,\tau,s,\kappa)\in\intr\bigl(\mathcal{K}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq}\times\mathcal{K}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq}\bigr)
.

Suppose a point
\omega
is feasible for the HSDE
Equation

12
.
From skew symmetry of the square
4\times 4
block matrix in
Equation

12a
, we have
s^{\prime}z+\kappa\tau=0
.
From the conic constraints
Equation

12b
and the dual cone inequality
Equation

1
we have
s^{\prime}z\geq 0
and
\kappa\tau\geq 0
.
Hence
s^{\prime}z=\kappa\tau=0
.
We consider an exhaustive list of cases below.

Optimality.

If
\tau>0,\kappa=0
, then
(x,y,z)/\tau
is a complementary solution satisfying the primal-dual optimality conditions
Equations

7b
,
7c
,
8b
,
8c
and
11
.

Infeasibility.

If
\tau=0,\kappa>0
, then
c^{\prime}x+b^{\prime}y+h^{\prime}z<0
and we consider two sub-cases.

Of primal.

If
b^{\prime}y+h^{\prime}z<0
, then
(y,z)
is a primal infeasibility certificate satisfying
Equation

10
.

Of dual.

If
c^{\prime}x<0
, then
x
is a dual infeasibility certificate satisfying
Equation

9
.

No information.

If
\tau=\kappa=0
, then
\omega
provides no information about the feasibility or optimal values of the primal or dual.

Thus an HSDE solution
\omega
satisfying
\kappa+\tau>0
provides an optimality or infeasibility certificate (see
Skajaa and Ye 2015
and
Vandenberghe 2010
).

According to
Skajaa and Ye 2015
, if the primal and dual problems are both feasible and have zero duality gap, SY (their algorithm) finds an HSDE solution with
\tau>0
(yielding a complementary solution), and if the primal or dual (possibly both) is infeasible, SY finds an HSDE solution with
\kappa>0
(yielding an infeasibility certificate).
This implies that if SY finds a solution with
\kappa=\tau=0
, then
\kappa=\tau=0
for all solutions to the HSDE; in this case, no complementary solution or improving ray exists, and the primal or dual (possibly both) is ill-posed
(
Permenter et al. 2017
)
.
The algorithm we describe in
Section

5
is an extension of SY that inherits these properties.

5
Central path following algorithm

In
Section

5.1
, we describe the central path of the HSDE, and in
Section

5.2
we define central path proximity measures.
In

5.3

In

Finally in

5.1
Central path of the HSDE

We define the HSDE in
Equation

12
.
Recall that
\mathcal{K}
in our primal conic form
Equation

7
is a Cartesian product
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{K}_{K}
of
K
exotic cones.
We partition the exotic cone indices
\llbracket K\rrbracket
into two sets:
K_{\text{pr}}
for cones with primal oracles (i.e. for
\mathcal{K}_{k}
) and
K_{\text{du}}
for cones with dual oracles (i.e. for
\mathcal{K}_{k}^{\ast}
).
For convenience, we append the
\tau
and
\kappa
variables onto the
s
and
z
variables.
Letting
\bar{K}=K+1
, we define for
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathcal{K}_{k}&k\in K_{\text{pr}},\\
\mathcal{K}_{k}^{\ast}&k\in K_{\text{du}},\\
\mathbb{R}_{\geq}&k=\bar{K},\end{cases}

(14a)

\displaystyle(\bar{z}_{k},\bar{s}_{k})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}(z_{k},s_{k})&k\in K_{\text{pr}},\\
(s_{k},z_{k})&k\in K_{\text{du}},\\
(\kappa,\tau)&k=\bar{K}.\end{cases}

(14b)

For a given initial interior point
\omega^{0}=(x^{0},y^{0},z^{0},\tau^{0},s^{0},\kappa^{0})
, the central path of the HSDE is the trajectory of solutions
\omega_{\mu}=(x_{\mu},y_{\mu},z_{\mu},\tau_{\mu},s_{\mu},\kappa_{\mu})
, parameterized by
\mu>0
, satisfying:

\displaystyle E\omega_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mu E\omega^{0},

(15a)

\displaystyle\bar{z}_{\mu,k}+\mu g_{k}(\bar{s}_{\mu,k})

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket,

(15b)

\displaystyle(\bar{z}_{\mu},\bar{s}_{\mu})

\displaystyle\in\intr(\bar{\mathcal{K}}^{\ast}\times\bar{\mathcal{K}}).

(15c)

When all exotic cones have primal oracles (i.e.
K_{\text{du}}
is empty), our definition
Equation

15
exactly matches the central path defined in
Vandenberghe 2010
, and only differs from the definition in
Skajaa and Ye 2015
in the affine form (i.e. the variable names and affine constraint structure).
Unlike SY, our central path condition
Equation

15b
allows cones with dual oracles (
K_{\text{du}}
may be nonempty).

To obtain an initial point
\omega^{0}
, we first let:

\bigl(\bar{z}^{0}_{k},\bar{s}^{0}_{k}\bigr)=(-g_{k}(t_{k}),t_{k})\quad\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket,

(16)

where
t_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\bigr)
is the initial interior point oracle (note that
\tau^{0}=\kappa^{0}=1
).
Although
x^{0}
and
y^{0}
can be chosen arbitrarily, we let
x^{0}
be the solution of:

\displaystyle\textstyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad\lVert x\rVert

\displaystyle:

(17a)

\displaystyle-Ax+b\tau^{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(17b)

\displaystyle-Gx+h\tau^{0}-s^{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(17c)

and we let
y^{0}
be the solution of:

\displaystyle\textstyle\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\quad\lVert y\rVert

\displaystyle:

(18a)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}y+G^{\prime}z^{0}+c\tau^{0}

\displaystyle=0.

(18b)

In
Appendix

A
, we outline a QR-factorization-based procedure for preprocessing the affine data of the conic model and solving for
\omega^{0}
.

Like
Skajaa and Ye 2015
, we define the
complementarity gap
function:

\mu(\omega)\coloneqq\bar{s}^{\prime}\bar{z}/{\textstyle\sum_{k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket}}\nu_{k},

(19)

where
\nu_{k}
is the LHSCB parameter of the LHSCB
f_{k}
for
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
(see
Equation

2b
).
Note that
\mu(\omega)>0
if
(\bar{z},\bar{s})\in\bar{\mathcal{K}}^{\ast}\times\bar{\mathcal{K}}
, by a strict version of the dual cone inequality
Equation

1
.
From
Equation

16
,
\mu(\omega^{0})=1
, since in
Equation

19
we have
(\bar{s}^{0})^{\prime}\bar{z}^{0}={\textstyle\sum_{k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket}}t_{k}^{\prime}(-g_{k}(t_{k}))
, and
t_{k}^{\prime}(-g_{k}(t_{k}))=\nu_{k}
by logarithmic homogeneity of
f_{k}

(
Nesterov and Nemirovskii 1994
, Proposition 2.3.4)
.
Hence
\omega^{0}
satisfies the central path conditions
Equation

15
for parameter value
\mu=1
.
The central path is therefore a trajectory that starts at
\omega^{0}
with complementarity gap
\mu=1
and approaches a solution for the HSDE as
\mu
decreases to zero.

5.2
Central path proximity

Given a point
\omega
, we define the central path
proximity

\pi_{k}
for exotic cone
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
as:

\pi_{k}(\omega)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\big\lVert(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1/2}(\bar{z}_{k}/\mu(\omega)+g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))\big\rVert&\text{if }\mu(\omega)>0,\bar{s}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\bigr),\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(20)

Hence
\pi_{k}
is a measure of the distance from
\bar{s}_{k}
and
\bar{z}_{k}
to the surface defined by the central path condition
Equation

15b
(compare to
Skajaa and Ye 2015
and
Nesterov and Todd 1998
).

In
Lemma

5.1
, we show that for exotic cone
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
, if
\pi_{k}(\omega)<1
, then
\bar{s}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\bigr)
and
\bar{z}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}^{\ast}_{k}\bigr)
.
This condition is sufficient but not necessary for strict cone feasibility.
If it holds for all
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
, then
\omega
is an interior point (by definition) and
Equation

15c
is satisfied.
From
Equation

20
,
\pi_{k}(\omega)
can be computed by evaluating the feasibility check, gradient, and Hessian oracles for
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
at
\bar{s}_{k}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Given a point
\omega
, for each
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
,
\pi_{k}(\omega)<1
implies
\bar{s}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\bigr)
and
\bar{z}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}^{\ast}_{k}\bigr)
.

Proof.

We adapt
Papp and Yıldız 2017
.
Fix
\mu=\mu(\omega)
for convenience, and suppose
\pi_{k}(\omega)<1
for exotic cone
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
.
Then by
Equation

20
,
\mu>0
and
\bar{s}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\bigr)
.
By
Papp and Yıldız 2017
,
\bar{s}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\bigr)
implies
-g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}^{\ast}_{k}\bigr)
.
Let
f_{k}
be the LHSCB for
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
, and let
H^{\ast}_{k}\coloneqq\nabla^{2}f^{\ast}_{k}
denote the Hessian operator for the conjugate
f^{\ast}_{k}
(see
Equation

3
) of
f_{k}
.
By
Papp and Yıldız 2017
,
H^{\ast}_{k}(-g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))=(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1}
, so:

\displaystyle\big\lVert(H^{\ast}_{k}(-g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})))^{1/2}(\bar{z}_{k}/\mu+g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))\big\rVert

(21a)

\displaystyle=\big\lVert(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1/2}(\bar{z}_{k}/\mu+g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))\big\rVert

(21b)

\displaystyle=\pi_{k}(\omega)<1.

(21c)

So by
Papp and Yıldız 2017
,
\bar{z}_{k}/\mu\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}^{\ast}_{k}\bigr)
, hence
\bar{z}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}^{\ast}_{k}\bigr)
.
∎

We now define a proximity function that aggregates the exotic cone central path proximity values
\pi_{k}(\omega)\geq 0,\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
.
SY aggregates by taking the
\ell_{2}
norm:

\pi_{\ell_{2}}(\omega)\coloneqq\big\lVert(\pi_{k}(\omega))_{k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket}\big\rVert.

(22)

An alternative aggregated proximity uses the
\ell_{\infty}
norm (maximum):

\pi_{\ell_{\infty}}(\omega)\coloneqq\big\lVert(\pi_{k}(\omega))_{k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket}\big\rVert_{\infty}.

(23)

Clearly,
0\leq\pi_{k}(\omega)\leq\pi_{\ell_{\infty}}(\omega)\leq\pi_{\ell_{2}}(\omega),\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
.
Both conditions
\pi_{\ell_{2}}(\omega)<1
and
\pi_{\ell_{\infty}}(\omega)<1
guarantee by
Lemma

5.1
that
\omega
is an interior point, however using
\pi_{\ell_{2}}
leads to a more restrictive condition on
\omega
.

5.3
High level algorithm

We describe a high level algorithm for approximately solving the HSDE.
The method starts at the initial interior point
\omega^{0}
with complementarity gap
\mu(\omega^{0})=1
and approximately tracks the central path trajectory
Equation

15
through a series of iterations.
It maintains feasibility for the linear equality conditions
Equation

15a
and strict cone feasibility conditions
Equation

15c
, but allows violation of the nonlinear equality conditions
Equation

15b
.
On the
i
th iteration, the current interior point is
\omega^{i-1}
satisfying
\pi_{k}(\omega^{i-1})<1,\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
, and the complementarity gap is
\mu(\omega^{i-1})
.
The method searches for a new point
\omega^{i}
that maintains the proximity condition
\pi_{k}(\omega^{i})<1,\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
(and hence is an interior point) and either has a smaller complementarity gap
\mu(\omega^{i})<\mu(\omega^{i-1})
or a smaller aggregate proximity value
\pi(\omega^{i})<\pi(\omega^{i-1})
(where
\pi
is
\pi_{\ell_{2}}
or
\pi_{\ell_{\infty}}
), or both.
As the complementarity gap decreases towards zero, the RHS of
Equation

15a
approaches the origin, so the iterates approach a solution of the HSDE
Equation

12
.

To detect an approximate conic certificate and terminate the iterations, we check if the current iterate
\omega
satisfies any of the following numerical convergence criteria.
These conditions use positive tolerance values for feasibility
\varepsilon_{f}
, infeasibility
\varepsilon_{i}
, absolute gap
\varepsilon_{a}
, relative gap
\varepsilon_{r}
, and ill-posedness
\varepsilon_{p}
(see
Section

7.2
for the tolerance values we use in computational testing).

Optimality.

We terminate with a complementary solution
(x,y,z)/\tau
approximately satisfying the primal-dual optimality conditions
Equations

7b
,
7c
,
8b
,
8c
and
11
if:

\max\biggl(\frac{\lVert A^{\prime}y+G^{\prime}z+c\tau\rVert_{\infty}}{1+\lVert c\rVert_{\infty}},\frac{\lVert-Ax+b\tau\rVert_{\infty}}{1+\lVert b\rVert_{\infty}},\frac{\lVert-Gx+h\tau-s\rVert_{\infty}}{1+\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}}\biggr)\leq\varepsilon_{f}\tau,

(24a)

and at least one of the following two conditions holds:

\displaystyle s^{\prime}z

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{a},

(24b)

\displaystyle\min(s^{\prime}z/\tau,\lvert c^{\prime}x+b^{\prime}y+h^{\prime}z\rvert)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{r}\max(\tau,\min(\lvert c^{\prime}x\rvert,\lvert b^{\prime}y+h^{\prime}z\rvert)).

(24c)

Note that
Equations

24b
and
24c
are absolute and relative optimality gap conditions respectively.

Primal infeasibility.

We terminate with a dual improving ray
(y,z)
approximately satisfying
Equation

10
if:

b^{\prime}y+h^{\prime}z<0,\qquad\lVert A^{\prime}y+G^{\prime}z\rVert_{\infty}\leq-\varepsilon_{i}(b^{\prime}y+h^{\prime}z).

(25)

Dual infeasibility.

We terminate with a primal improving ray

approximately satisfying
Equation

9
if:

c^{\prime}x<0,\qquad\max(\lVert Ax\rVert_{\infty},\lVert Gx+s\rVert_{\infty})\leq-\varepsilon_{i}c^{\prime}x.

(26)

Ill-posed primal or dual.

If
\tau
and
\kappa
are approximately
0
, the primal and dual problem statuses cannot be determined (see
Section

4.3
).
We terminate with an ill-posed status if:

\mu(\omega)\leq\varepsilon_{p},\qquad\tau\leq\varepsilon_{p}\min(1,\kappa).

(27)

The high level path following algorithm below computes an approximate solution to the HSDE.

5.4
Search directions

At a given iteration of the path following method, let
\omega
be the current interior point and fix
\mu=\mu(\omega)
for convenience.
The stepping procedures we describe in
Section

5.5
first compute one or more search directions, which depend on
\omega
.
We derive the
centering
direction in
Section

5.4.1
and the
prediction
direction in
Section

5.4.2
.
The goal of centering is to step to a point with a smaller aggregate central path proximity than the current point, i.e. to step towards the central path.
The goal of prediction is to step to a point with a smaller complementarity gap, i.e. to step closer to a solution of the HSDE.
The centering and prediction directions match those used by SY.
We associate with each of these directions a new
third order adjustment
(TOA) direction, which depends on the TOO and helps to correct the corresponding unadjusted direction (which must be computed before the TOA direction).
Hence we derive four types of directions here.

Each direction is computed as the solution to a linear system with a structured square
6\times 6
block matrix left hand side (LHS) and a particular right hand side (RHS) vector.
The LHS, which depends only on
\omega
and the problem data, is the same for all four directions at a given iteration.
We let
r\coloneqq(r_{E},r_{1},\ldots,r_{\bar{K}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim(\omega)}
represent an RHS, where
r_{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+p+q+1}
corresponds to the linear equalities
Equation

15a
and
r_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{k}},\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
corresponds to the nonlinear equalities
Equation

15b
.
The direction
\delta\coloneqq(\delta_{x},\delta_{y},\delta_{z},\delta_{\tau},\delta_{s},\delta_{\kappa})\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim(\omega)}
corresponding to
r
is the solution to:

\displaystyle E\delta

\displaystyle=r_{E},

(28a)

\displaystyle\delta_{\bar{z},k}+\mu H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})\delta_{\bar{s},k}

\displaystyle=r_{k}\quad\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket.

(28b)

Since
E
is assumed to have full row rank and each
H_{k}
is positive definite, this square system is nonsingular and hence has a unique solution.
In
Appendix

A
, we describe a particular method for solving
Equation

28
.

5.4.1
Centering

The centering direction
\delta^{c}
is analogous to the definition of
Skajaa and Ye 2015
.
It reduces the violation on the central path nonlinear equality condition
Equation

15b
(and can be interpreted as a Newton step), while keeping the complementarity gap
\mu
(approximately) constant.
We denote the centering TOA direction
\delta^{ct}
.
To maintain feasibility for the linear equality condition
Equation

15a
, we ensure
E\delta^{c}=E\delta^{ct}=0
in
Equation

28a
.

Dropping the index
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
for conciseness, recall that
Equation

15b
expresses
\bar{z}+\mu g(\bar{s})=0
.
A first order approximation of this condition gives:

\displaystyle\bar{z}+\delta_{\bar{z}}+\mu(g(\bar{s})+H(\bar{s})\delta_{\bar{s}})

\displaystyle=0

(29a)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\delta_{\bar{z}}+\mu H(\bar{s})\delta_{\bar{s}}

\displaystyle=-\bar{z}-\mu g(\bar{s}),

(29b)

which matches the form of
Equation

28b
.
Hence we let the centering direction
\delta^{c}
be the solution to:

\displaystyle E\delta

\displaystyle=0,

(30a)

\displaystyle\delta_{\bar{z},k}+\mu H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})\delta_{\bar{s},k}

\displaystyle=-\bar{z}_{k}-\mu g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})\quad\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket.

(30b)

Similarly, a second order approximation of
\bar{z}+\mu g(\bar{s})=0
gives:

\displaystyle\bar{z}+\delta_{\bar{z}}+\mu\bigl(g(\bar{s})+H(\bar{s})\delta_{\bar{s}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{3}f(\bar{s})[\delta_{\bar{s}},\delta_{\bar{s}}]\bigr)

\displaystyle=0

(31a)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\delta_{\bar{z}}+\mu H(\bar{s})\delta_{\bar{s}}

\displaystyle=-\bar{z}-\mu g(\bar{s})+\mu\mathrm{T}(\bar{s},\delta_{\bar{s}}),

(31b)

where
Equation

31b
uses the definition of the TOO in
Equation

5
.
Note that the RHSs of
Equations

29b
and
31b
differ only by
\mu\mathrm{T}(\bar{s},\delta_{\bar{s}})
, which depends on
\delta_{\bar{s}}
.
To remove this dependency, we substitute the centering direction
\delta^{c}
, which we assume is already computed, into the RHS of
Equation

31b
.
Hence we let the centering TOA direction
\delta^{ct}
, which adjusts the centering direction, be the solution to:

\displaystyle E\delta

\displaystyle=0,

(32a)

\displaystyle\delta_{\bar{z},k}+\mu H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})\delta_{\bar{s},k}

5.5
Stepping procedures

A stepping procedure computes one or more directions from
Section

5.4
and uses the directions to search for a new interior point.
Recall from
5

Section

5.3
that on iteration

computes
\omega^{i}

\pi(\omega^{i})<1

\pi(\omega^{i})<\pi(\omega^{i-1})

In

In

The main purpose of our computational testing in
Section

Oracles for predefined exotic cones

Below we list 23 exotic cone types that we have predefined through Hypatia’s generic cone interface (see
Section

3
).
Each of these cones is represented in the benchmark set of conic instances that we introduce in
Section

7.1
.
Recall that we write any exotic cone
\mathcal{K}
in vectorized form, i.e. as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, where
q=\dim(\mathcal{K})\geq 1
is the cone dimension.
For cones typically defined using symmetric matrices, we use the standard svec vectorization (see
Section

2
) to ensure the vectorized cone is proper, to preserve inner products, and to simplify the dual cone definition.
Each cone is parametrized by at least one dimension and several cones have additional parameters such as numerical data.
For convenience, we drop these parameters from the symbols we use to represent cone types.
For several cones, we have implemented additional variants over complex numbers (for example, a Hermitian PSD cone), but we omit these definitions here for simplicity.
We defer a more complete description of Hypatia’s exotic cones and LHSCBs to
Coey et al. 2021a
;
Coey et al. 2021c
;
Kapelevich et al. 2021
.

Nonnegative cone.

\mathcal{K}_{\geq}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{d}
is the (self-dual) nonnegative real vectors (note for
d>1
,
\mathcal{K}_{\geq}
is not a primitive cone).

PSD cone.

\mathcal{K}_{\succeq}\coloneqq\bigl\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{\sdim(d)}:\mat(w)\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{\succeq}\bigr\}
is the (self-dual) PSD matrices of side dimension
d
.

Doubly nonnegative cone.

\mathcal{K}_{\dnn}\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{\geq}\cap\mathcal{K}_{\succeq}
is the PSD matrices with all nonnegative entries of side dimension
d
.

Sparse PSD cone.

\mathcal{K}_{\sppsd}
is the PSD matrices of side dimension
s
with a fixed sparsity pattern
\mathcal{S}
containing
d\geq s
nonzeros (including all diagonal elements); see
Section

C.4
.
The dual cone
\mathcal{K}_{\sppsd}^{\ast}
is the symmetric matrices with pattern
\mathcal{S}
for which there exists a PSD completion, i.e. an assignment of the elements not in
\mathcal{S}
such that the full matrix is PSD.
For simplicity, the complexity estimates in

1
assume the nonzeros are grouped under

supernodes, each containing at most

(
Andersen et al. 2013
)
.

Linear matrix inequality cone.

7.2
Methodology

We can assess the practical performance of a stepping procedure on a given benchmark instance according to several metrics: whether the correct conic certificate (satisfying our numerical tolerances, discussed below) is found, and if so, the PDIPM iteration count and solve time.
Across the benchmark set, we compare performance between consecutive pairs of the five stepping procedures outlined in
Section

5.5
.

basic.

The basic prediction or centering stepping procedure without any enhancements; described in
Section

5.5.1
, this is similar to the method in Alfonso solver
(
Papp and Yıldız 2021
)
, which is a practical implementation of the algorithm by
Skajaa and Ye 2015
;
Papp and Yıldız 2017
.

prox.

The
basic
procedure modified to use a less restrictive central path proximity condition; described in
Section

5.5.2
.

TOA.

The
prox
procedure with the TOA enhancement to incorporate third order LHSCB information; described in
Section

5.5.3
.

curve.

The
TOA
procedure adapted for a single backtracking search on a curve instead of two backtracking line searches; described in
Section

5.5.4
.

comb.

The
curve
procedure modified to search along a curve of combinations of both the prediction and centering directions and their corresponding adjustment directions; described in
Section

5.5.5
.

We perform all instance generation, computational experiments, and results analysis using double precision floating point format, with Ubuntu 21.04, Julia 1.7, and Hypatia 0.5.1 (with default options), on dedicated hardware with an AMD Ryzen 9 3950X 16-core processor (32 threads) and 128GB of RAM.
In
Appendix

A
, we outline the default procedures Hypatia uses for preprocessing, initial point finding, and linear system solving for search directions.
Simple scripts and instructions for reproducing all results are available in Hypatia’s benchmarks/stepper folder.
The benchmark script runs all solves twice and uses results from the second run, to exclude Julia compilation overhead.
A CSV file containing raw results is available at the Hypatia wiki page.

When Hypatia converges for an instance, i.e. claims it has found a certificate of optimality, primal infeasibility, or dual infeasibility, our scripts verify that this is the correct type of certificate for that instance.
For some instances, our scripts also check additional conditions, for example that the objective value of an optimality certificate approximately equals the known true optimal value.
We do not set restrictive time or iteration limits.
All failures to converge are caused by Hypatia ‘stalling’ during the stepping iterations: either the backtracking search cannot step a distance of at least the minimal value in the
\alpha
schedule, or across several prediction steps or combined directions steps, Hypatia fails to make sufficient progress towards meeting the convergence conditions in
Section

5.3
.

Since some instances are more numerically challenging than others, we set the termination tolerances (described in
Section

5.3
) separately for each instance.
Let
\epsilon\approx 2.22\times 10^{-16}
be the machine epsilon.
For most instances, we use
\varepsilon_{f}=\varepsilon_{r}=10\epsilon^{1/2}\approx 1.49\times 10^{-7}
for the feasibility and relative gap tolerances,
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{a}=10\epsilon^{3/4}\approx 1.82\times 10^{-11}
for the infeasibility and absolute gap tolerances, and
\varepsilon_{p}=0.1\epsilon^{3/4}\approx 1.82\times 10^{-13}
for the ill-posedness tolerance.
For 50 instances that are particularly numerically challenging, we loosen all of these tolerances by a factor of either 10 or 100, and for two challenging primal infeasible instances of the
contraction analysis
example, we set
\varepsilon_{i}=10^{-9}
.
This ensures that for every benchmark instance, at least one of the five stepping procedures converges.

Following
Fleming and Wallace 1986
, we define the
shifted geometric mean
with shift
s\geq 0
, for
d
values
v\in\mathbb{R}_{>}^{d}
, as:

M(v,s)\coloneqq\textstyle\prod_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(v_{i}+s)^{1/d}-s.

(44)

We always apply a shift of one for iteration counts.
Since different stepping procedures converge on different subsets of instances, in tables we show three types of shifted geometric means, each computed from a vector of values (

in

) obtained using one of the following approaches.

every.

Values for the 353 instances on which every stepping procedure converged.

Values for instances on which this stepping procedure (corresponding to the row of the table) converged.

Values for all instances, but for any instances for which this stepping procedure (corresponding to the row of the table) failed to converge, the value is replaced with two times the maximum value for that instance across the stepping procedures that converged.

Table

init.

Appendix

A
).

LHS.

Performed at the start of each iteration.
Includes updating data that the linear system solver (which has a fixed LHS in each iteration) uses to efficiently compute at least one direction (such as updating and factorizing the positive definite matrix in

RHS.

Performed between one and four times per iteration, depending on the stepping procedure.
Includes updating an RHS vector (see

.

Finally, in
Figures

3
and
6
we use
performance profiles

(
Dolan and Moré 2002
;
Gould and Scott 2016
)
to compare iteration counts and solve times between pairs of stepping procedures.
These should be interpreted as follows.
The
performance ratio
for procedure
i
and instance
j
is the value (iterations or solve time) attained by procedure
i
on instance
j
divided by the better/smaller value attained by the two procedures on instance
j
.
Hence a performance ratio is at least one, and smaller values indicate better relative performance.
For a point
(x,y)
on a performance profile curve for a particular procedure,
x
is the logarithm (base
2
) of performance ratio and
y
is the proportion of instances for which the procedure attains that performance ratio or better/smaller.
For example, a curve crosses the vertical axis at the proportion of instances on which the corresponding procedure performed at least as well as the alternative procedure.
We use the Julia package BenchmarkProfiles.jl
(
Orban 2019
)
to compute coordinates for the performance profile curves.

7.3.1
Less restrictive proximity

We compare

to evaluate the central path proximity enhancement introduced in

.

Figure

(first row) shows that the iteration count and solve time improve for nearly all instances.
From

, the shifted geometric means of iteration count and solve time improve by over 35%.

The similarity between the iteration count and solve time performance profiles in

and also between the per iteration subtimings in

suggests that the solve time improvement is driven mainly by the reduction in iteration count.
The per iteration

(because it tends to step further in the prediction directions, as evidenced by the smaller iteration counts).
These results suggest that the central path proximity restrictions in the algorithms by

;
Papp and Yıldız 2021
are too conservative from the perspective of practical performance, and that we need not restrict iterates to a very small neighborhood of the central path in order to obtain high quality prediction directions in practice.

We compare
prox
and
TOA
to evaluate the TOA enhancement introduced in

.

Figure

(second row) shows that the iteration count improves for all instances and by a fairly consistent magnitude, and the solve time improves for nearly 80% of instances.

, the shifted geometric means of iteration count and solve time improve by over 45% and over 20% respectively.

and

nearly double.

is still much faster than the other components.
Per iteration,
direc
and

also remain fast compared to
LHS
.
We see an overall solve time improvement because the reduction in iteration count usually outweighs the additional cost at each iteration.
This suggests that the TOO is generally relatively cheap to compute, and our TOA approach very reliably improves the quality of the search directions.

We compare

and

.

Appendix A
Preprocessing and solving for search directions

We discuss preprocessing and initial point finding procedures and solving structured linear systems for directions.
Although Hypatia has various alternative options for these procedures, we only describe the set of options we fix in our computational experiments in
Section

7
, to give context for these results.
These techniques are likely to be useful for other conic PDIPM implementations.

Given a conic model specified in the general primal conic form
Equation

7
, we first rescale the primal and dual equality constraints
Equations

7b
and
8b
to improve the conditioning of the affine data.
Next, we perform a QR factorization of
A^{\prime}
and check whether any primal equalities are inconsistent (terminating if so).
We use this factorization to modify
c,G,h
and eliminate all
p
primal equalities (removing dual variable
y
), reducing the dimension of the primal variable
x
from
n
to
n-p
.
Next, we perform a QR factorization of the modified
G
.
We use this factorization to check whether any dual equalities are inconsistent (terminating if so) and to remove any redundant dual equalities, further reducing the dimension of
x
.
This factorization also allows us to cheaply compute an initial
x^{0}
satisfying
Equation

17c
.
Since
y
is eliminated, we do not need to solve
Equation

18b
for
y^{0}
.

Starting from the initial interior point
\omega^{0}
defined in
Section

5.1
, we perform PDIPM iterations until the convergence conditions in
Section

5.3
(in the preprocessed space) are met.
Finally, we re-use the two QR factorizations to lift the approximate certificate for the preprocessed model to one for the original model.
The residual norms for the lifted certificate could violate the convergence tolerances, but we have not found such violations to be significant on our benchmark instances.

During each PDIPM iteration, we solve the linear system
Equation

28
for a single LHS matrix and between one and four RHS vectors, to obtain directions vectors needed for one of the stepping procedures described in
Section

5.5
.
Instead of factorizing the large square nonsymmetric block-sparse LHS matrix, we utilize its structure to reduce the size of the factorization needed.
Some of these techniques are adapted from methods in CVXOPT (see
Vandenberghe 2010
).

First we eliminate
s
and
\kappa
, yielding a square nonsymmetric system, then we eliminate
\tau
to get a symmetric indefinite system in
x
and
z
.
Most interior point solvers use a sparse LDL factorization (with precomputed symbolic factorization) to solve this system.
Although Hypatia can optionally do the same, we see improved performance on our benchmark instances by further reducing the system.
After eliminating
z
, we have a (generally dense) positive definite system, which we solve via a dense Cholesky factorization.
In terms of the original dimensions of the model before preprocessing (assuming no redundant equalities), the side dimension of this system is
n-p
.
Finally, after finding a solution to
Equation

28
, we apply several rounds of iterative refinement in working precision to improve the solution quality.

We note that this Cholesky-based system solver method does not require explicit Hessian oracles, only oracles for left-multiplication by the Hessian or inverse Hessian.
As we discuss in
Appendix

C
and
Coey et al. 2021c
, these optional oracles can be more efficient and numerically stable to compute for many exotic cones.
For cones without these oracles, Hypatia calls the explicit Hessian matrix oracle, performing a Cholesky factorization of the Hessian if necessary.
A deeper discussion of Hypatia’s linear system solving techniques and optional cone oracles is outside the scope of this paper.

Appendix B
Efficient proximity checks

Recall that each stepping procedure in
Section

5.5
uses at least one backtracking search (on a line or a curve) to find a point
\omega
satisfying an aggregate proximity condition:
\pi_{\ell_{2}}(\omega)\leq\beta_{1}
for the
basic
procedure in
Section

5.5.1
or
\pi_{\ell_{\infty}}(\omega)\leq\beta_{2}
for the procedures in
Sections

5.5.2
to
5.5.5
.
In
Section

5.2
, we define
\pi_{\ell_{2}}
and
\pi_{\ell_{\infty}}
in
Equations

22
and
23
.
For each primitive cone
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
,
0\leq\pi_{k}(\omega)\leq\pi_{\ell_{\infty}}(\omega)\leq\pi_{\ell_{2}}(\omega)
, and by
Lemma

5.1
,
\pi_{k}(\omega)<1
implies
\bar{s}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\bigr)
and
\bar{z}_{k}\in\intr\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{K}}^{\ast}_{k}\bigr)
.
We use a schedule of decreasing trial values for the step parameter
\alpha
and accept the first value that yields a candidate point satisfying the aggregate proximity condition.

Suppose at a particular iteration of the backtracking search, we have the candidate point
\omega
.
We check a sequence of increasingly expensive conditions that are necessary for the proximity condition to hold for
\omega
.
First, we verify that
\bar{s}_{k}^{\prime}\bar{z}_{k}>0,\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
, which is necessary for interiority (by a strict version of the dual cone inequality
Equation

1
).
Note that this condition implies
\mu(\omega)>0
.
Next, we verify that
\rho_{k}(\omega)<\beta,\forall k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
, where
\rho_{k}(\omega)
is:

\rho_{k}(\omega)\coloneqq\nu_{k}^{-1/2}\lvert\bar{s}_{k}^{\prime}\bar{z}_{k}/\mu-\nu_{k}\rvert\geq 0.

(45)

In
Lemma

B.1
below, we show that
\rho_{k}(\omega)
is a lower bound on
\pi_{k}(\omega)
, so if
\rho_{k}(\omega)>\beta
then then
\pi_{k}(\omega)>\beta
.
Computing
\rho_{k}
is much cheaper than computing
\pi_{k}(\omega)
as it does not require evaluating any cone oracles.

Next, we iterate over
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
to check first the primal feasibility oracle, then the optional dual feasibility oracle if implemented, and finally the proximity condition
\pi_{k}(\omega)<\beta
.
Before computing
\pi_{k}(\omega)
, we check that the gradient and Hessian oracle evaluations approximately satisfy two logarithmic homogeneity conditions
(
Nesterov and Nemirovskii 1994
, Proposition 2.3.4)
:

(g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{\prime}(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1}g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})=-\bar{s}_{k}^{\prime}g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})=\nu_{k}.

(46)

This allows us to reject
\omega
if the cone oracles and the proximity value
\pi_{k}(\omega)
are likely to be numerically inaccurate.

Lemma B.1
.

Given a point
\omega
for which
\mu(\omega)>0
, for each
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
,
0\leq\rho_{k}(\omega)\leq\pi_{k}(\omega)
.

Proof.

We fix
\mu=\mu(\omega)>0
for convenience.
Let
f_{k}
be the
\nu_{k}
-LHSCB for
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
, and let the conjugate of
f_{k}
be
f^{\ast}_{k}
(see
Equation

3
), which is a
\nu_{k}
-LHSCB for
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{k}^{\ast}
.
Let
g^{\ast}_{k}\coloneqq\nabla f^{\ast}_{k}
and
H^{\ast}_{k}\coloneqq\nabla^{2}f^{\ast}_{k}
denote the gradient and Hessian operators for
f^{\ast}_{k}
.
Using the logarithmic homogeneity properties from
Nesterov and Nemirovskii 1994
, and from the definition of
\pi_{k}(\omega)
in
Equation

20
, we have:

\displaystyle(\pi_{k}(\omega))^{2}

\displaystyle=(\bar{z}_{k}/\mu+g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{\prime}(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1}(\bar{z}_{k}/\mu+g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))

(47a)

\displaystyle=\mu^{-2}\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1}\bar{z}_{k}+2\mu^{-1}\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1}g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})+(g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{\prime}(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1}g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})

(47b)

\displaystyle=\mu^{-2}\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1}\bar{z}_{k}-2\mu^{-1}\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}\bar{s}_{k}+\nu_{k}.

(47c)

By
Papp and Yıldız 2017
,
(H_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))^{-1}=H^{\ast}_{k}(-g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))
.
Since
f^{\ast}_{k}
is a self-concordant barrier with parameter
\nu_{k}
, by
Nesterov et al. 2018
we have:
(\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}g^{\ast}_{k}(-g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k})))^{2}\leq\nu_{k}\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}H^{\ast}_{k}(-g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))\bar{z}_{k}
.
Furthermore,
g^{\ast}_{k}(-g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))=\bar{s}_{k}
.
Using these facts, from
Equation

47
we have
\rho_{k}(\omega)\geq 0
and:

\displaystyle(\pi_{k}(\omega))^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu^{-2}\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}H^{\ast}_{k}(-g_{k}(\bar{s}_{k}))\bar{z}_{k}-2\mu^{-1}\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}\bar{s}_{k}+\nu_{k}

(48a)

\displaystyle\geq\nu_{k}^{-1}\mu^{-2}(\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}\bar{s}_{k})^{2}-2\mu^{-1}\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}\bar{s}_{k}+\nu_{k}

(48b)

\displaystyle=\nu_{k}^{-1}(\bar{s}_{k}^{\prime}\bar{z}_{k}/\mu-\nu_{k})

(48c)

\displaystyle=(\rho_{k}(\omega))^{2}.

(48d)

Therefore,
\pi_{k}(\omega)\geq\rho_{k}(\omega)\geq 0
for all
k\in\llbracket\bar{K}\rrbracket
.
∎

As an aside, we can use similar arguments to
Lemma

B.1
to show that
\rho_{k}(\omega)
also symmetrically bounds a conjugate proximity measure
\pi_{k}^{\ast}(\omega)
, which we define as:

\pi_{k}^{\ast}(\omega)\coloneqq\big\lVert(H_{k}^{\ast}(\bar{z}_{k}))^{-1/2}(\bar{s}_{k}/\mu+g_{k}^{\ast}(\bar{z}_{k}))\big\rVert\geq\nu_{k}^{-1/2}\lvert\bar{z}_{k}^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{k}/\mu+g_{k}^{\ast}(\bar{z}_{k}))\rvert=\rho_{k}(\omega).

(49)

In general, we cannot check whether
\pi_{k}^{\ast}(\omega)<\beta
because as we discuss in
Section

1.3
we do not have access to fast and numerically stable conjugate barrier oracles (
g_{k}^{\ast}
and
H_{k}^{\ast}
).

Appendix C
Computing the TOO for some exotic cones

C.1
Intersections of slices of the PSD cone

First, we consider a proper cone
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
that is an inverse linear image (or slice) of the PSD cone
\mathbb{S}^{\jmath}_{\succeq}
of side dimension
\jmath
.
Suppose:

\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\Lambda(s)\succeq 0\},

(50)

where
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{S}^{\jmath}
is a linear operator, with adjoint linear operator
\Lambda^{\ast}:\mathbb{S}^{\jmath}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then the dual cone can be characterized as:

\mathcal{K}^{\ast}\coloneqq\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\exists S\succeq 0,s=\Lambda^{\ast}(S)\}.

(51)

We note that for
\mathcal{K}_{\succeq}
(the self-dual vectorized PSD cone), we can let
q=d=\sdim(\jmath)
,
\Lambda(s)=\mat(s)
, and
\Lambda^{\ast}(S)=\vect(S)
.
Given a point
s\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, strict feasibility for
\mathcal{K}
can be checked, for example, by attempting a Cholesky factorization
\Lambda(s)=LL^{\prime}
, where
L
is lower triangular.

For
\mathcal{K}
we have the LHSCB
f(s)=-\logdet(\Lambda(s))
with parameter
\nu=\jmath
.
Given a point
s\in\intr(\mathcal{K})
, we have
\Lambda(s)\in\mathbb{S}^{\jmath}_{\succ}
and its inverse
\Lambda^{-1}(s)\in\mathbb{S}^{\jmath}_{\succ}
.
For a direction
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, for
f
at
s
we can write the gradient, and the Hessian and TOO applied to
\delta
, as (compare to
Papp and Yildiz 2019
):

\displaystyle g(s)

\displaystyle=-\Lambda^{\ast}(\Lambda^{-1}(s)),

(52a)

\displaystyle H(s)\delta

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{\ast}(\Lambda^{-1}(s)\Lambda(\delta)\Lambda^{-1}(s)),

(52b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{\ast}(\Lambda^{-1}(s)\Lambda(\delta)\Lambda^{-1}(s)\Lambda(\delta)\Lambda^{-1}(s)).

(52c)

If we have, for example, a Cholesky factorization
\Lambda(s)=LL^{\prime}
(computed during the feasibility check), then the oracles in
Equation

52
are easy to compute if
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\ast}
are easy to apply.
We can compute the TOO
Equation

52c
using the following steps:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\coloneqq L^{-1}\Lambda(\delta)\Lambda^{-1}(s),

(53a)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\coloneqq Y^{\prime}Y=\Lambda^{-1}(s)\Lambda(\delta)\Lambda^{-1}(s)\Lambda(\delta)\Lambda^{-1}(s),

(53b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{\ast}(Z).

(53c)

We note
Equation

53a
can be computed using back-substitutions with
L
, and
Equation

53b
is a simple symmetric outer product.
We use this approach to derive simple TOO procedures for
\mathcal{K}_{\lmi}
in
Section

C.2
and for
\mathcal{K}_{\wsos}^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\matwsos}^{\ast}
in
Section

C.3
when
r=1
.

Now we consider the more general case of a cone
\mathcal{K}
that can be characterized as an intersection of slices of PSD cones, for example
\mathcal{K}_{\wsos}^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\matwsos}^{\ast}
when
r>1
.
Suppose:

\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\Lambda_{l}(s)\succeq 0,\forall l\in\llbracket r\rrbracket\},

(54)

where
\Lambda_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{S}^{\jmath_{l}}
, for
l\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
.
Then the dual cone can be characterized as:

\mathcal{K}^{\ast}\coloneqq\bigl\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\exists S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}\succeq 0,s={\textstyle\sum_{l\in\llbracket r\rrbracket}}\Lambda_{l}^{\ast}(S_{l})\bigr\}.

(55)

Feasibility for
\mathcal{K}
can be checked by performing
r
Cholesky factorizations.
If we let
f_{l}(s)=-\logdet(\Lambda_{l}(s)),\forall l\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
, then
f(s)=\sum_{l\in\llbracket r\rrbracket}f_{l}(s)
is an LHSCB for
\mathcal{K}
with parameter
\nu=\sum_{l\in\llbracket r\rrbracket}\jmath_{l}
.
Clearly,
g(s)
,
H(s)\delta
(and the explicit Hessian matrix), and
\mathrm{T}(s,\delta)
can all be computed as sums over
l\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
of the terms in
Equation

52c
.

C.2
LMI cone

We denote the inner product of
X,Y\in\mathbb{S}^{s}
as
\langle X,Y\rangle=\tr(XY)\in\mathbb{R}
, computable in order of
s^{2}
time.
For
\mathcal{K}_{\lmi}
parametrized by
P_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{s},\forall i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
, we define for
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
W\in\mathbb{S}^{s}
:

\displaystyle\Lambda(w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\textstyle\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}}w_{i}P_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{s},

(56a)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\ast}(W)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\langle P_{i},W\rangle)_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(56b)

Our implementation uses specializations of
Equations

52
and
53
for
\mathcal{K}_{\lmi}
.
For
w\in\intr(\mathcal{K}_{\lmi})
and direction
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

, we compute:

\displaystyle Q_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq L^{-1}P_{i}(L^{-1})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{s}\quad\forall i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,

(57a)

\displaystyle g(w)

\displaystyle=(-\tr(Q_{i}))_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket},

(57b)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\textstyle\sum_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}}\delta_{j}Q_{j}\in\mathbb{S}^{s},

(57c)

\displaystyle H(w)\delta

\displaystyle=(\langle Q_{i},R\rangle)_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket},

(57d)

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(w,\delta)

\displaystyle=(\langle Q_{i},R^{\prime}R\rangle)_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket},

(57e)

and we compute the explicit Hessian oracle as:

(H(w))_{i,j}=\langle Q_{i},Q_{j}\rangle\quad\forall i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket.

(58)

The symmetric form of
Q_{i}
and the use of a symmetric outer product
R^{\prime}R
in
Equation

57e
are beneficial for efficiency and numerical performance.

C.3
Matrix and scalar WSOS dual cones

Recall that Hypatia uses LHSCBs for
\mathcal{K}_{\wsos}^{\ast},\mathcal{K}_{\matwsos}^{\ast}

\mathcal{K}_{\wsos},\mathcal{K}_{\matwsos}
with tractable oracles are not known (see

Since the scalar WSOS dual cone

with

\mathcal{K}_{\matwsos}^{\ast}
here.
In general,
\mathcal{K}_{\matwsos}^{\ast}
is an intersection of
r
slices of

(see

54

(and

To enable convenient vectorization, we define
\rho_{i,j}
for indices

\rho_{i,j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=j,\\
\sqrt{2}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(59)

For
\mathcal{K}_{\matwsos}^{\ast}
parametrized by
P\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
and

, we define for

and
W\in\mathbb{S}^{st}

\displaystyle\Lambda(w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigl[P^{\prime}\Diag\bigl(\rho^{-1}_{i,j}w_{\max(i,j),\min(i,j),:}\bigr)P\bigr]_{i,j\in\llbracket t\rrbracket}\in\mathbb{S}^{st},

(60a)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\ast}(W)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\rho_{i,j}\diag(P(W)_{i,j}P^{\prime}))_{i\in\llbracket t\rrbracket,j\in\llbracket i\rrbracket}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sdim(t)d},

(60b)

where
w=(w_{i,j,:})_{i\in\llbracket t\rrbracket,j\in\llbracket i\rrbracket}
and
w_{i,j,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the contiguous slice of
w
corresponding to the interpolant basis values in the
(i,j)
th (lower triangle) position,
matrix
(S)_{i,j}
is the
(i,j)
th block in a block matrix
S
(with blocks of equal dimension),
and
[S_{i,j}]_{i,j\in\llbracket t\rrbracket}
is the symmetric block matrix with matrix
S_{i,j}
in the
(i,j)
th block.

We implement efficient and numerically stable specializations of the oracles in
Equations

52
and
53
.
Suppose we have
w\in\intr(\mathcal{K}_{\matwsos}^{\ast})
and direction
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\sdim(t)d}
, and a Cholesky factorization
\Lambda(w)=LL^{\prime}
.
For each
i,j\in\llbracket t\rrbracket:i\geq j
and
p\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
, we implicitly compute oracles according to:

\displaystyle(Q)_{i,j,p}

\displaystyle\coloneqq((L^{-1})_{i,j}P^{\prime})e_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{s},

(61a)

\displaystyle(g(w))_{i,j,p}

\displaystyle=-\rho_{i,j}Q_{i,:,p}^{\prime}Q_{:,j,p},

(61b)

\displaystyle(R)_{i,j,p}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(L^{-1}\Lambda(\delta)(L^{-1})^{\prime}Q)_{i,j}e_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{s},

(61c)

\displaystyle(H(w)\delta)_{i,j,p}

\displaystyle=\rho_{i,j}Q_{i,:,p}^{\prime}R_{:,j,p},

(61d)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{T}(w,\delta))_{i,j,p}

\displaystyle=\rho_{i,j}R_{i,:,p}^{\prime}R_{:,j,p}.

(61e)

Letting
Q^{2}_{i,j}\coloneqq(Q^{\prime}Q)_{i,j}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, we compute the Hessian oracle according to:

(H(w))_{(i,j,:),(k,l,:)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{i,j}\rho_{k,l}\bigl(Q^{2}_{i,k}\circ Q^{2}_{j,l}+Q^{2}_{i,l}\circ Q^{2}_{j,k}\bigr)\in\mathbb{S}^{d}\quad\forall i,j,k,l\in\llbracket t\rrbracket,

(62)

where
X\circ Y\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
denotes the Hadamard (elementwise) product of
X,Y\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

C.4
Sparse PSD cone

Let
\mathcal{S}=((i_{l},j_{l}))_{l\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}
be a collection of row-column index pairs defining the sparsity pattern of the lower triangle of a symmetric matrix of side dimension
s
(including all diagonal elements).
We do not require
\mathcal{S}
to be a chordal sparsity pattern (unlike
Andersen et al. 2013
;
Burer 2003
), as this restriction is not necessary for the oracles Hypatia uses.
Note
s\leq d\leq\sdim(s)
.
For
\mathcal{K}_{\sppsd}
parametrized by
\mathcal{S}
, we define
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{S}^{s}
as the linear operator satisfying, for all
i,j\in\llbracket s\rrbracket:i\geq j
:

(\Lambda(w))_{i,j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\rho^{-1}_{i,j}w_{l}&\text{if }i=i_{l}=j=j_{l},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(63)

where
\rho_{i,j}
is given by
Equation

59
.
Then
\Lambda^{\ast}
is the vectorized projection onto
\mathcal{S}
, i.e. for

:

\Lambda^{\ast}(W)\coloneqq(\rho_{i,j}W_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(64)

Consider
w\in\intr(\mathcal{K}_{\sppsd})
and direction
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

The gradient
Equation

52a
and Hessian product

52b

can be computed using
Andersen et al. 2013
.
To derive the TOO, we use the fact that:

-2\mathrm{T}(w,\delta)=\nabla^{3}f(w)[\delta,\delta]=\tfrac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\nabla f(w+t\delta)\big|_{t=0}.

(65)

In order to succinctly describe our TOO approach as an extension of the procedures in
Andersen et al. 2013
, we describe an approach based on a sparse LDL factorization of
\Lambda(w)
.
However, our current implementation in Hypatia uses a sparse Cholesky (

) factorization, which is very similar to the LDL-based approach here.
We compute the sparse Cholesky factors using Julia’s SuiteSparse wrapper of CHOLMOD
(
Chen et al. 2008
)

We note that Hypatia implements a
supernodal
generalization (see
Andersen et al. 2013

Before we describe the TOO procedure, we repeat useful definitions from
Andersen et al. 2013

As discussed in
Section

6
, Hypatia computes the feasibility check and gradient oracles before the TOO, and our TOO procedure reuses cached values computed for these oracles.

We define:

R\coloneqq\Lambda(\nabla f(w+t\delta)).

(66)

Let
LDL^{\prime}=\Lambda(w)
be a sparse LDL factorization, i.e.
L
is a sparse unit lower triangular matrix and

The sparsity pattern of

k

.

\ch(i)

i

denote the
i

(E_{I,J})_{i,j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }I(i)=J(j),\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(67)

be the update matrix for node

\displaystyle U_{i}\coloneqq-{\textstyle\sum_{k\in\ch(i)\cup\{i\}}}D_{k,k}L_{I_{i},k}L_{I_{i},k}^{\prime}.

(68)

Let
\dot{D}

\dot{U}

For convenience, we let:

\bar{L}_{j}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

(69)

Suppose we have computed

,

\begin{bmatrix}\ddot{D}_{j,j}&P_{j}^{\prime}\\[4.30554pt]

\displaystyle R_{I_{j},j}

\displaystyle=-R_{I_{j},I_{j}}L_{I_{j},j},

C.5
Euclidean norm cone and Euclidean norm square cone

Although
\mathcal{K}_{\ltwo},\mathcal{K}_{\sqr}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
are inverse linear images of
\mathbb{S}^{q}_{\succeq}
and hence admit LHSCBs with parameter
\nu=q
, we use the standard LHSCBs with parameter
\nu=2
, which have a different form (see
Vandenberghe 2010
).
For
\mathcal{K}_{\ltwo},\mathcal{K}_{\sqr}
, the LHSCB is
f(s)=-\log(s^{\prime}Js)
, where
J\in\mathbb{S}^{q}
is defined according to, for
i,j\in\llbracket q\rrbracket:i\geq j
:

\displaystyle J_{i,j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }j=1\text{ and }\bigl(i=1\text{ for }\mathcal{K}_{\ltwo}\text{ or }i=2\text{ for }\mathcal{K}_{\sqr}\bigr),\\[2.15277pt]
-1&\text{if }i=j\text{ and }\bigl(i>1\text{ for }\mathcal{K}_{\ltwo}\text{ or }i>2\text{ for }\mathcal{K}_{\sqr}\bigr),\\[2.15277pt]
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(80)

Consider
s\in\intr(\mathcal{K})
and direction
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, and let
\bar{J}=(s^{\prime}Js)^{-1}>0
.
The gradient, Hessian product, and TOO oracles for
\mathcal{K}
are:

\displaystyle g(s)

\displaystyle=-2\bar{J}Js,

(81a)

\displaystyle H(s)\delta

\displaystyle=2\bar{J}(2\bar{J}Jss^{\prime}J\delta-J\delta),

(81b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=\bar{J}(Js\delta^{\prime}H\delta+H\delta s^{\prime}J\delta-s^{\prime}H\delta J\delta).

(81c)

These oracles are computed in order of
