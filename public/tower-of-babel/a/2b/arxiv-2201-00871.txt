Thermonuclear and electron-capture supernovae from stripped-envelope stars
S. Chanlaridis, J. Antoniadis, D. R. Aguilera-Dena, G. Gräfener, N. Langer, N. Stergioulas

Abstract
Context. When stripped from their hydrogen-rich envelopes, stars with initial masses between ∼ \sim 7 and 11 M ⊙ \mathrm{\,M_{\odot}} may develop massive degenerate cores and collapse. Depending on the final structure and composition, the outcome can range from a thermonuclear explosion, to the formation of a neutron star in an electron-capture supernova (ECSN). It has recently been demonstrated that stars in this mass range may be more prone to disruption than previously thought: they may initiate explosive oxygen burning when their central densities are still below ρ c ≲ 10 9.6 \rho_{\mathrm{c}}\lesssim 10^{9.6} g ​ cm − 3 \mathrm{\,g\,cm^{-3}} . At the same time, their envelopes expand significantly, leading to the complete depletion of helium. This combination makes them interesting candidates for type Ia supernovae—which we call (C)ONe SNe Ia—and this might have broader implications for the formation of neutron stars via ECSNe. Aims. To constrain the observational counterparts of (C)ONe SNe Ia and the key properties that enable them, it is crucial to constrain the evolution, composition, and precollapse structure of their progenitors, as well as the evolution of these quantities with cosmic time. In turn, this requires a detailed investigation of the final evolutionary stages preceding the collapse, and their sensitivity to input physics. Methods. Here, we modeled the evolution of 252 single, nonrotating helium stars covering the initial mass range 0.8 – 3.5 ​ M ⊙ $0.83.5$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}} , with metallicities between Z = 10 − 4 Z=10^{-4} and 0.02 0.02 , and overshoot efficiency factors from f OV = 0.0 \rm f_{OV}=0.0 to 0.016 0.016 across all convective boundaries. We used these models to constrain several properties of these stars, including their central densities, compositions, envelope masses, and radii at the onset explosive oxygen ignition, as well as the final outcome as a function of initial helium star mass. We further investigate the sensitivity of these properties to mass loss rate assumptions using an additional grid of 110 models with varying wind efficiencies. Results. We find that helium star models with masses between ∼ \sim 1.8 and 2.7 M ⊙ \mathrm{\,M_{\odot}} are able to evolve onto 1.35 – 1.37 ​ M ⊙ $1.351.37$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}} (C)ONe cores that initiate explosive burning at central densities between log 10 ⁡ ( ρ c / g ​ cm − 3 ) ∼ 9.3 \log_{10}(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}/\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}})\sim 9.3 and 9.6. We constrained the amount of residual carbon retained after core carbon burning as a function of initial conditions, and conclude that it plays a critical role in determining the final outcome: Chandrasekhar-mass degenerate cores that retain more than approximately 0.005 ​ M ⊙ 0.005\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}} of carbon result in (C)ONe SNe Ia, while those with lower carbon mass become ECSNe. We find that (C)ONe SNe Ia are more likely to occur at high metallicities, whereas at low metallicities ECSNe dominate. However, both SN Ia and ECSN progenitors expand significantly during the final evolutionary stages, so that for the most extended models, a further binary interaction may occur. We constrain the relative ratio between (C)ONe SNe Ia and SNe Ib/c to be 0.17–0.30 at Z = 0.02 Z=0.02 , and 0.03–0.13 at Z ≤ 10 − 3 Z\leq 10^{-3} . Conclusions. We conclude with a discussion on potential observational properties of (C)ONe SNe Ia and their progenitors. In the few thousand years leading to the explosion, at least some progenitors should be identifiable as luminous metal-rich super-giants, embedded in hydrogen-free circumstellar nebulae.



1
Introduction

The relation between a star’s initial properties and the type of remnant it produces remains equivocal. In isolation, stars with zero-age main sequence
(ZAMS) masses above
\sim
11
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
are thought to produce
neutron stars (NSs) or black holes (BHs), while those with
masses below
\sim
7
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
leave behind white dwarfs (WDs). The
fate of stars with intermediate masses is less certain, as
predictions for their final size and composition remain
challenging
(
Siess 2007
;
Poelarends et al. 2008
;
Doherty et al. 2015
;
Farmer et al. 2015
, and references therein)
.

It is believed that most stars in this intermediate-mass regime evolve through a super-asymptotic giant branch (SAGB) phase, during which they burn carbon nonexplosively
(e.g.,
Ritossa et al. 1999
;
Siess 2006
;
Poelarends et al. 2008
;
Doherty et al. 2015
;
Farmer et al. 2015
;
Poelarends et al. 2017
, and references therein)
.
Prior to carbon ignition, the mass of the helium core can be substantially reduced due to
the second dredge-up (2DU), in which processed helium-rich matter is mixed throughout the stellar envelope. Subsequent shell burning is highly unstable and proceeds in a series of thermal pulses
(
Iben et al. 1997
;
Vassiliadis & Wood 1993
;
Poelarends et al. 2008
)
.
If the envelope is lost before the degenerate core reaches the Chandrasekhar-mass limit (
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}\simeq$1.351.37$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
, for the compositions investigated in this paper), then the final outcome is a WD. Conversely, if the core mass exceeds
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
, the star produces a supernova (SN), the properties
of which depend on the stellar structure and ignition conditions
1
1

1

Here, ignition refers to the time that the energy liberation rate per unit mass exceeds the local neutrino cooling rate,
\epsilon_{\rm nuc}-\epsilon_{\rm\nu}\geq 0
at the onset of the thermonuclear runaway
(
Rose 1969
;
Wheeler 1978
)
.
Most hydrogen-rich SAGBs are believed to lose their envelope before their core becomes sufficiently massive to collapse. Hence, an explosive ending is likely rare for noninteracting stars in this mass range
(
Iben & Renzini 1983
;
Poelarends et al. 2008
)
. However, if the hydrogen-rich envelope is lost before core helium ignition, both unstable shell burning and the 2DU are avoided, leading to more efficient core growth. This significantly broadens the mass range in which near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
SAGB cores may form
(e.g.,
Podsiadlowski et al. 2004
;
Poelarends et al. 2008
;
Woosley 2019
, and references therein)
.
The most important formation path for naked helium stars in this mass range is thought to be envelope stripping in binaries either through stable mass transfer, or in a common-envelope event
(
Tauris et al. 2015
;
Tauris et al. 2017
;
Siess & Lebreuilly 2018
;
Laplace et al. 2020
)
.

At the high densities expected inside near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
SAGB cores, the
ashes of carbon fusion participate in weak interactions, for instance Urca cycles and electron captures (henceforth
e
-captures), that can drastically influence the final evolutionary stages.
Owing to the large abundance of
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}Ne}
, the most
important of these is the
e
-capture sequence
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}Ne}
(\mathrm{e^{-}},\mathrm{\nu_{e}})
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}F}
(\mathrm{e^{-}},\mathrm{\nu_{e}})
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}O}
,
which—especially at densities above
10^{10}\mathrm{\,g\,cm^{-3}}
—rapidly consumes free electrons, leading to an
e
-capture supernova (ECSN). The result of the collapse is determined by the competition between
deleptonization and nuclear energy release, and can be either a NS formed in a low-energy core-collapse ECSN (ccECSN), or a thermonuclear explosion (tECSN), in which at least part of the star is disrupted
(
Miyaji et al. 1980
;
Nomoto 1984
;
Canal et al. 1992
;
Kitaura et al. 2006
;
Gutierrez et al. 1996
;
Jones et al. 2014
)
.
Whether one outcome prevails over the other depends on several uncertain factors, such as the ignition location and density
(
Leung et al. 2020
)
, the structure and chemical makeup of the star, the speed and size of the nuclear flame
(
Timmes & Woosley 1992
;
Schwab et al. 2020
)
, the reaction rates of
the various
e
-capture interactions
(
Kirsebom et al. 2019b
)
, and the efficiency of convective energy transport
(
Schwab & Rocha 2019
)
.

Multidimensional hydrodynamic simulations exploring some of these parameters

(
Jones et al. 2016
;
Jones et al. 2019
;
Leung et al. 2020
, e.g.,)

generally favor tECSNe for ignition densities around
10^{10}\mathrm{\,g\,cm^{-3}}
or smaller, even if the thermonuclear flame never transitions into a supersonic shock
(
Jones et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.
These models suggest that tECSNe may eject more than
\sim
1
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
of material, leaving behind only small bound remnants. Because of the small electron-to-baryon ratio (
Y_{\rm e}
) of the material
processed in nuclear statistical equilibrium, tECSNe have been proposed as candidate sources for neutron-rich elements such as
\mathrm{{{}^{48}}Ca}
and
\mathrm{{{}^{50}}Ti}
that are typically
underproduced in normal type Ia SNe
(
Jones et al. 2019
, SNe Ia;)
. Intriguingly, the chemical composition of some presolar meteoric grains compares well with the predicted nucleosynthetic signature of these events
(
Jones et al. 2019
)
. Some compact objects also seem consistent with the expectations for tECSN remnants
(
Raddi et al. 2019
;
Tauris & Janka 2019
)
.

Recent advances in our understanding of SAGB stars suggest that they might be even more prone to disruption than previously thought. Firstly, the second forbidden 2
+

\rightarrow
0
+

ground state transition in the
\beta
-decay of
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}F}
has now been measured
(
Kirsebom et al. 2019a
)
, and found to be several orders of magnitude stronger than previously predicted. This makes
e
-captures on
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}Ne}
a key energy source even at densities below
10^{10}\mathrm{\,g\,cm^{-3}}

(
Kirsebom et al. 2019b
;
Kirsebom et al. 2019a
;
Leung et al. 2020
)
. Secondly, state-of-the-art simulations consistently show that some carbon is retained after the carbon burning phase
(
Iben & Renzini 1983
;
García-Berro et al. 1997
;
Siess 2006
;
Denissenkov et al. 2013
;
Farmer et al. 2015
;
Doherty et al. 2015
;
Lecoanet et al. 2016
;
Jones et al. 2019
, see also Sect.
3
)
. Due to its lower ignition temperature compared to oxygen, residual carbon can significantly
influence the explosion conditions. In some cases, during the main carbon burning episode, the flame, which ignites off-center, may never reach the core, thereby creating a hybrid structure in which a CO-dominated core is surrounded by an
ONe-dominated envelope
(
Denissenkov et al. 2013
;
Farmer et al. 2015
, and references therein)
.

In a previous paper
(
Antoniadis et al. 2020
, henceforth
3
)
, we examin the evolution of two stripped helium star models with masses of 1.8 and 2.5
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
. We show that these stars initiate explosive
oxygen burning at central densities
\lesssim 6\times 10^{9}\mathrm{\,g\,cm^{-3}}
, thereby completely avoiding
e
-captures on
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}Ne}
. At the same time, their envelopes expand significantly, leading to intense mass loss and the complete depletion of helium. The combination of low ignition densities, and helium-free composition, make these stars atypical progenitor candidates for thermonuclear SNe Ia
(
Waldman & Barkat 2006
;
Waldman et al. 2008
, henceforth (C)ONe SNe Ia; see also)
. In addition, this behavior may also reduce significantly the mass range for which thermonuclear and core-collapse ECSNe may occur. To gauge which flavor of SN Ia this channel may be relevant to, as well as the broader consequences for ECSNe, it is important to constrain the range of masses for which this outcome is possible, the stellar structure and composition at the onset of collapse, as well as the sensitivity of these properties to underlying physical uncertainties.

In this work we present detailed one-dimensional stellar evolution models that cover a broad range of initial helium star
masses, compositions, and physical assumptions. The text is organized as follows: in Sect.
2
we describe our methodology and input physics. In Sect.
3
we present the results of our calculations and in Sect.
4
we discuss various physical uncertainties in the input physics. Finally, in Sect.
5
we conclude with a concise summary and a discussion on the ramifications of our models for ECSNe and SNe Ia.

We perform our numerical simulations using the implicit
one-dimensional code
M

for
E
xperiments in
S
tellar

(
Paxton et al. 2011
;
Paxton et al. 2013
;
Paxton et al. 2015
;
Paxton et al. 2018
,
mesa
v10398;)
. Our main grid (henceforth
Series I
) consists of 252
helium star models (
X=0
), covering the
$0.83.5$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
mass
range with a resolution of 0.1
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
, at three metallicities
(
Z=10^{-4},10^{-3}
, and 0.02).
Other physical assumptions in our models are identical to those described in
3
and are
only briefly summarized here for completeness

Our
mesa
configuration

.
To set the initial composition, we considered the abundances of
Grevesse & Sauval 1998
as a reference for the solar isotopic composition. Models of different metallicities have abundances that are scaled from the aforementioned reference for the solar composition. To better account for an enhanced carbon-oxygen mixture as a result of helium burning, we used the type-2 OPAL Rosseland mean opacity tables
(
Iglesias & Rogers 1996
)
.
We considered ion and electron screening corrections as described in
Potekhin et al. 2009
and
Itoh et al. 2002
, respectively.
We used the
HELM

(
Timmes & Swesty 2000
)
and
PC

(
Potekhin & Chabrier 2010
)
equations-of-state. For their blending, we followed the options suggested by
Schwab et al. 2017
so that the core is always treated using the
PC
equation-of-state.

Convection was treated according to the modified mixing-length theory (MLT) prescription of
Henyey et al. 1965
with a mixing-length parameter of
\alpha_{\text{ML}}=2.0
. Contrary to the default MLT option in
mesa
, this treatment allows for the convective efficiency to vary with the opaqueness of the convective element
(
Paxton et al. 2011
)
.
Stability against convection was evaluated using the Ledoux criterion.
Convective energy transport was computed using the
MLT++
variation of standard MLT
(
Paxton et al. 2015
)
. This artificially reduces large superadiabatic temperature gradients to improve numerical stability (for a discussion on the consequences of this choice we refer to Sect.
4.4
).
We also adopted an efficiency parameter of
\alpha_{\text{SEM}}=1.0
for semiconvection
(
Langer 1991
)
, and a diffusion coefficient of
D_{\text{TH}}=1.0
for thermohaline mixing
(
Brown et al. 2013
)
. Both semiconvection and thermohaline mixing are treated by
mesa
as diffusive processes
(
Kippenhahn et al. 1980
;
Langer et al. 1983
)
. We did not employ the predictive mixing implementation described in
Paxton et al. 2018
. Lastly, we considered the effect of overshooting across convective boundaries. Even though overshooting is likely not particularly strong in this mass range, especially between the CO-core and envelope interface
(
Lecoanet et al. 2016
)
, it might have a significant influence on the propagation of the carbon burning flame
(
Farmer et al. 2015
)
. Motivated by this, we computed models with small variations in overshooting efficiency. More specifically, following
Jones et al. 2013
, we considered three scaling factors of
f_{\rm OV}
= 0.0, 0.014 and 0.016, where
f_{\rm OV}
is defined as a fraction of the local pressure scale height,
H_{p}
, and relates to the diffusion coefficient,
D_{\text{OV}}
via,

\displaystyle D_{\text{OV}}=D_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{2z}{H_{v}}\right);\hskip 14.22636ptH_{v}=f_{\text{OV}}H_{p},

(1)

where
z
is the geometric distance from the edge of the convective zone
(
Herwig 2000
)
.

Our nuclear network consisted of 43
isotopes and considered all relevant weak reactions, using the
weak rates from
Suzuki et al. 2016
. We ensured that all these isotopes
were included in the
PC
equation-of-state calculation
(see
Schwab et al. 2017
, for details)
.
Mass loss rates due to stellar winds were implemented using the
Dutch
wind scheme
(
Glebbeek et al. 2009
)
, and assuming no further binary interactions take place following the formation of the helium star. In our case, this implied two different rates, depending on the effective temperature of the star: for
T_{\text{eff}}>10^{4}
K and a surface abundance of hydrogen of
X<0.4
by mass fraction (which is always satisfied in our models), we applied the prescription of
Nugis & Lamers 2000
with a scaling factor of
\eta=1
(default value). For
T_{\text{eff}}<10^{4}
K the mass loss rate followed the prescription of
de Jager et al. 1988
.

To probe the influence of stellar wind on core growth, we evolved additional stellar models with varying wind scaling factors within the
Dutch
scheme. This secondary grid (henceforth
Series II
) consists of 110 models with
(Z,f_{\rm OV})=(0.02,0.0)
, covering the
$1.43.5$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
mass range with a step size of 0.1
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
.

As we move to higher initial masses (

As long as the temperature at the base of the carbon burning region remains higher than the carbon ignition threshold, the
burning front advances inward. For stars that exhibit this behavior and have relatively low masses, the flame quenches before reaching the center.
In this case, the core exhibits a hybrid-like structure, where the inner layers are primarily composed of carbon and oxygen, while the outer layers have an oxygen-neon composition

.
In our

1.3\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}

\sim 1.2\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}

In the absence of further binary interactions after the hydrogen envelope removal, the final hybrid CONe WDs in our models have masses between 1.2 and 1.33
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}

3.4
Pre-explosion properties of (C)ONe cores: SNe Ia and ECSNe

Based on the results presented above, stars with a degenerate near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
core in the
$1.351.37$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
range may experience one of the following outcomes: firstly, for ONe cores, if oxygen ignites at low densities (
\log_{10}(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}/\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}})\lesssim 9.8
) and the core immediately expands as a response to the thermonuclear runaway, then
e
-captures on
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}Ne}
may be completely avoided. In this case, the star may undergo a thermonuclear runaway that resembles a SN Ia explosion. The kinetic energy of the ejecta and the composition of the material depend on the explosion conditions, for instance
Y_{\rm e}
and
E_{\rm bin}
.

Secondly, hybrid near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
cores ignite oxygen at even lower densities (
\log_{10}(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}/\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}})\lesssim 9.3
). These objects do not experience
e
-captures and may yield SN Ia-like explosions with higher kinetic energies compared to ONe cores.

Lastly, if an ONe core ignites oxygen at high densities (
\log_{10}(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}/\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}})\gtrsim 10
) then
e
-captures on
\mathrm{{{}^{20}}Ne}
lead to rapid deleptonization, and the star evolves toward an ECSN. Depending on the explosion conditions, the latter can be either a tECSN, in which part of the star is disrupted, or a ccECSN that forms a neutron star.

In the remainder of this section, we describe the pre-explosion properties of models that successfully initiated explosive oxygen burning. A more in-depth discussion on the possible final fates of near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models that terminated before reaching this stage follows in Sect.
4
.

Figure
12
summarizes the relation between
Y_{\rm e}
, central density, and binding energy at the onset of oxygen ignition for the twelve models that evolved past this phase.
As can be seen, with few exceptions, the binding energy of these models lies within
-5.8\leq E_{\text{bin}}/10^{50}\,\text{erg}\leq-5.4
and the average electron-to-baryon ratio is
0.494\leq Y_{e}\leq 0.499
.
Our simulations indicate that these stars respond to oxygen burning by expanding. As discussed in
3
, if one assumes that the thermonuclear runaway produces a typical SN Ia
composition (
\sim 0.7\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
of iron-peak and
\sim 0.8\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
of Si-group elements),
then the nuclear energy yield suffices to completely unbind the star and produce ejecta with kinetic energies

0.77\leq E_{\text{ej}}/10^{51}\,\text{erg}\leq 1.26
.
Consequently, these thermonuclear runaways would belong to the first category (ONe SNIa explosions).
The exact
\mathrm{{{}^{56}}Ni}
yield depends on when the deflagration transitions to a detonation,
and cannot be directly predicted by our simulations.
If nuclear statistical equilibrium is achieved when the cores are still around their maximum compactness,
then based on the core compositions shown in Fig.
12
, the resulting explosions would synthesize mostly stable iron-group
elements and only
\sim$0.10.3$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
of radioactive iron-group elements.
Such low nickel yields would translate to underluminous explosions with light curves resembling those of
SNe Iax
(
Jha 2017
, e.g.,)
.
On the other hand, if the cores expand before a detonation occurs,
then up to
1\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
of nickel may be synthesized (see
3
).

3.5
Core-collapse supernovae

If
M_{i}\gtrsim 2.8\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
(for models without overshooting), then the core reaches a
sufficiently high temperature to undergo on-center carbon ignition, under nondegenerate conditions, leading to more advanced burning stages. The evolution of these stars is similar to those described in
Jones et al. 2013
and
Woosley 2019
.
Models that fall within this category, develop a silicon core and evolve toward an iron core-collapse that ultimately forms a neutron
star. These models retain a
\mathrm{{{}^{4}}He}
-rich envelope, so assuming that a successful explosion follows the collapse of the core, it would
probably be observable as a SN Ib. Type Ic SNe are also expected to
form from stripped-envelope stars, but not in the mass range of our
models, unless additional mass loss takes place, either via winds,
pulsations
(
Aguilera-Dena et al. 2022
)
, or case BB mass transfer,
which may result in the formation of ultra-stripped SNe
(
Tauris et al. 2015
)
.

4
Physical uncertainties

4.1
Wind and mass loss uncertainties

There are two phases where mass loss through stellar winds affects the outcome of our model computations. Firstly,
because of its long duration, mass loss during the core helium burning
phase may influence both the total mass and the size of the degenerate core. In our
Series I
models the total mass removed in this phase is
\mathcal{O}(0.1\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}})
. Secondly, our models reach very high
luminosities toward the end of their evolution, when they expand and
their atmospheres become cooler (up to
\log_{10}\left(L/{\rm L}_{\odot}\right)\simeq 6.25

for the example in Fig.
8
). In this short phase, the
remaining
\mathrm{{{}^{4}}He}
-envelope is likely removed. The competition between envelope removal and core growth is critical for the final fate of the
star: if the envelope is lost before the core reaches the Chandrasekhar-mass limit, then the star becomes a WD. Contrarily, if enough of the envelope survives, helium might become observable in the resulting explosion.

In our models, we adopted the
Dutch

mesa
wind scheme which extrapolates empirical
mass loss relations for more massive Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars from
Nugis & Lamers 2000
for
\rm T_{\text{eff}}>10^{4}

In late phases, when the degenerate core is near its maximum mass, our models reach extremely high

4.3
Binary interactions

A key feature in the evolution of hydrogen-rich SAGB stars is the occurrence of the
2DU episode, during which the mass of the helium core is being reduced. Moreover, the existence of two
burning shells in the structure of these stars leads to periodic thermonuclear instabilities
that reduce the accretion of material onto the core
(
Denissenkov et al. 2013
)
.
These two effects make it harder for the
core to grow, leaving only a very narrow window, in the mass range, for which the cores of SAGB
stars can finally reach the Chandrasekhar-mass limit. Owing to the absence of a hydrogen envelope,
our models are not subject to any of those effects, although our models do experience a shallower dredge up which it does not seem to have the same impact as the 2DU to the
\mathrm{{{}^{1}}H}
-rich counterparts. However, since helium stars in our considered mass range are expected to be produced in binaries, some residual hydrogen may be present in the envelope when these stars detach from their Roche lobes
(
Sen et al. 2022
, and references therein)
. This thin layer of hydrogen might
influence the evolution, for instance by increasing the surface luminosity (and therefore mass loss rate) due to additional shell burning, or by inducing instabilities such as the ones described above. This could potentially inhibit the growth of the core and therefore reduce the expected mass range for core-collapse.

Similarly, binary interaction, both during and after core helium burning, can
potentially influence the final core mass. As can be seen in Fig.
10
, our models remain relatively compact (
\lesssim 0.5\mathrm{\,R_{\odot}}
) during this phase. Hence, additional mass stripping via Case BB mass transfer is only expected to play a significant role in very compact binaries.
Contrarily, we find significant radius expansion for all models with initial masses above
\sim 1.5\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
(see lower panel of Fig.
10
), meaning that late mass transfer likely affects the most extended SAGB stars, independently of their final fate ((C)ONe SNe Ia vs ECSNe vs iron core-collapse). While such interaction is not considered in our models, it will most likely influence our results by effectively shifting the mass boundaries shown in Fig.
4
.
Bearing in mind the importance of these caveats, we plan to investigate this further by performing detailed binary calculations in a future project.

4.4
Other sources of uncertainty

Besides the effects discussed previously in this section, there are several additional physical uncertainties that may influence the final fate of stars in this regime.
One of these uncertain factors is the propagation of the flame during the main carbon burning phase.
Both the ignition location of carbon and the flame propagation speed are sensitive to several physical mechanisms such as
thermohaline, convective and rotational mixing, the neutrino cooling timescale, and other uncertainties related to winds

(
Farmer et al. 2015
, and references therein)
.
Here, we only investigated one small component of this issue that is related to convective overshooting.
Our models confirm that the carbon flame propagation is indeed sensitive to assumptions related to mixing,
suggesting that other mixing mechanisms may play a significant role as well.

The accuracy of numerical calculations during the late stages of the evolution also contributes some uncertainty. It is unclear if our simulations can accurately reproduce the structure and properties of the outermost layers of stars with convective envelopes. For instance, our models reach a short phase of super-Eddington luminosities causing convection to become insufficient to transport the energy, due to the necessary high superadiabatic temperature gradient. This leads to increasingly short timesteps when one attempts to resolve this evolutionary stage.
In our calculations, we circumvented these issues by artificially reducing the superadiabatic temperature gradient using the
MLT++
option, which increases the efficiency of convective energy transport.
However, this approach also influences radii estimates, as it suppresses envelope inflation, thereby also affecting other properties such as the effective temperature and surface luminosity.

During the aforementioned super-Eddington phase, some of our models also achieve effective temperatures
that are below the lowest temperature covered by the
OPAL
opacity tables included in

mesa

\left(\log_{10}(T/\rm K)=3.75\right)
. This forces
mesa
to use other low-temperature opacity
tables that in principle assume different composition. This means that there might be significant
changes in opacity at the location of the blend due to the change in the assumed composition

(see
Schwab 2019
, for details)
. These issues motivate further investigations to
understand the complex underlying physics of stellar winds and envelope ejection.

Finally, we did not investigate in detail the impact of uncertainties in nuclear reaction rates.
For instance, the uncertain
\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C}({\rm\alpha},\mathrm{{{}^{16}}O})\gamma
,
\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C}(\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C},{\rm\alpha})\mathrm{{{}^{20}}Ne}
and

\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C}(\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C},{\rm p})\mathrm{{{}^{23}}Na}
rates
may substantially influence the pre-SN
\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C}
and
\mathrm{{{}^{23}}Na}
abundances.
Similarly, as
Strömberg et al. 2022
point out, the details of
e
-capture processes and the associated
weak reaction rates can also introduce substantial uncertainties. They argue that taking into consideration the second forbidden transition
4^{+}_{\text{g.s.}}\rightarrow 2^{+}_{1}
lowers the threshold density for
\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Na}
(\mathrm{e^{-}},\mathrm{\nu_{e}})
\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Ne}
, allowing it
to occur soon after the
\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Mg}
(\mathrm{e^{-}},\mathrm{\nu_{e}})
\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Na}
, for temperatures

\log_{10}(\rm T/{\rm K})\lesssim 8.5
. We did not consider this transition in our calculations.
Instead, the temperature in this regime increases in two separate steps, at slightly different
densities, owing to the allowed transition
0^{+}_{\text{g.s.}}\rightarrow 1^{+}_{1}
between

\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Mg}
and
\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Na}
, and the
1^{+}_{1}\rightarrow 0^{+}_{\text{g.s.}}
between the

\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Na}
and
\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Ne}
. For our models that feature residual carbon in their cores,
the close succession of those two reactions—forced by the forbidden transition—could have a
non-negligible effect on the overall outcome by reducing the carbon ignition density.

5
Summary & discussion

Antoniadis et al. 2020
presented a case study of two helium star models that develop a
degenerate (C)ONe core and subsequently experience a
thermal runaway when their central density is
9.2\lesssim\log_{10}(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}/\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}})\lesssim 9.8
. These stars likely avoid
e
-captures on

\mathrm{{{}^{20}}Ne}
and produce a (C)ONe SN Ia.
In this work, we build on this result by using
mesa
to model the evolution of
$0.83.5$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
helium stars, exploring the influence of initial composition, stellar-wind efficiency, and mixing assumptions.
Our models confirm that (C)ONe SNe Ia may occur for a sizeable fraction of helium stars in the
$1.72.6$\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
range. The salient properties of our models which we identify as responsible for enabling this outcome can be summarized as follows: firstly, unlike hydrogen-rich SAGB stars, our models do not experience a 2DU episode, nor undergo unstable shell burning. Consequently, their cores grow fast via stable
\mathrm{{{}^{4}}He}
-shell burning. This significantly broadens the mass range for which stars develop degenerate Chandrasekhar-mass cores. Our findings (Fig.
5
) are consistent with previous studies
(
Podsiadlowski et al. 2004
;
Poelarends et al. 2008
;
Poelarends et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
, confirming that ECSNe are mostly relevant to interacting binary systems. In Sects.
3.2
and
3.3
we argue that all (C)ONe cores originating from helium stars with initial masses
M_{\rm He,i}\gtrsim 2.1\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
are able to reach the Chandrasekhar limit, independently of overshooting efficiency and initial metallicity. At
Z=0.02
and
f_{\rm OV}>0
, near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
cores become possible for all stars with
M_{\rm He,i}\gtrsim 1.9\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
. This includes the entire mass range for which ONe cores are produced. Consequently, ONe WDs are not expected for these initial conditions.

Secondly, (C)ONe cores are formed via carbon burning that initiates off-center and propagates inward. This leaves a certain amount of unburned carbon distributed throughout the core.
In Sects.
3.2
and
4.2
we demonstrate that leftover carbon plays a critical role in determining the final fate of near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
(C)ONe cores. For hybrid CONe compositions, residual carbon ignites when
\log_{10}(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}/\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}})\simeq 9.3
leading to a thermonuclear runaway. Similarly, in ONe cores a carbon simmering phase is triggered by exothermic
e
-captures on
\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Mg}
and
\mathrm{{{}^{24}}Na}
. This may raise the temperature sufficiently to initiate explosive oxygen burning when
\log_{10}(\rho/\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}})\lesssim 9.8
.

Our models suggest that degenerate cores with a mass averaged mass fraction of
X(\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C})\gtrsim 0.004
produce ONe SNe Ia, whereas those with lower
\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C}
mass fractions yield ECSNe (Section
4.2
). This is approximately equal to 0.005
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
of residual carbon distributed in the degenerate core. Figure
6
illustrates how the residual
\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C}
mass fraction varies with initial mass, composition, and overshooting efficiency. Larger residual carbon mass fractions are favored for lower masses and high metallicities, especially for
f_{\rm OV}>0
. This means that (C)ONe SNe Ia are mostly relevant to high-metallicity environments: for
Z=0.02
, the initial mass range for which near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
degenerate cores have
X(\mathrm{{{}^{12}}C})\leq 0.004
is
(M^{\rm min}_{\rm He,i},M^{\rm max}_{\rm He,i})\simeq(2.1,2.7)
or
(1.8,2.3)
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
for
f_{\rm OV}=0.0
and 0.016, respectively. For
Z\leq 10^{-3}
, the corresponding range becomes
\sim(2.1,2.5)
and
(2.0,2.1)
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
for
f_{\rm OV}=0.0
and 0.016.

Thirdly, prior to collapse, near-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models undergo a period of vigorous shell burning, during which the envelope reaches super-Eddington luminosities. In Sect.
3
we demonstrate that this “hot-bottom
\mathrm{{{}^{4}}He}
-burning” episode leads to helium-free, metal-rich envelopes. In Sect.
4
we explore uncertainties related to the power of the stellar wind. We find that the final envelope mass depends on the strength of the stellar wind during the core helium burning phase. However, in all cases, no helium is present at the time of core-collapse.

In Sect.
4
we discuss several factors that could affect these explosions, including uncertainties in nuclear reaction rates, mixing processes, stellar winds, and binary interaction. If (C)ONe SNe Ia indeed occur in nature, they can significantly influence the properties of compact object populations, as well as the chemical evolution of galaxies. For instance, the formation of neutron stars via ECSNe in binaries would be affected, particularly at high metallicities. Such potentially important consequences raise the question of how (C)ONe SNe Ia and their progenitors could be identified. In Sect.
3.4
we estimated that (C)ONe SNe Ia have a sufficiently large reservoir of nuclear energy to appear similar to normal SNe Ia in terms of iron-group elements yields and kinetic energies. Evidently, more robust constraints on the nucleosynthesis and explosion energies require detailed multidimensional hydrodynamic simulations of the explosive oxygen burning phase. Another distinct characteristic that could help constrain their observational counterparts is the intense mass loss taking place during the few thousand years leading to the explosion. This mass loss history will likely create a dense metal-rich circumstellar material (CSM) that can subsequently interact with the SN ejecta. This presents some similarities to the “core-denegerate” SN Ia scenario
(
Wang et al. 2017
;
Soker 2019
;
Soker 2022
, e.g.,)
. However, binary interactions—involving these stars with the most extended envelopes—may occur leading to a more diverse CSM spectrum.

In terms of explosion sites, we expect (C)ONe SNe Ia to be relevant mostly to high-metallicity star-forming regions. Similarly to ECSNe, we expect a delay time distribution dominated by the main sequence lifetime of their progenitors, that is to be
\mathcal{O}
(200 Myr), although it might be possible to form similar ONe cores via WD mergers
(
Schwab et al. 2016
)
.

Antoniadis et al. 2020
, argued that the frequency of ONe SNe Ia can be comparable to the observed SNe Ib/c rates. Considering the results presented here, we update the relative ratio between ONe SNe Ia and SNe Ib/c to be
0.170.30
at
Z=0.02
and
0.030.13
at
Z=10^{-4}

(assuming a
Chabrier 2005
, initial mass function)
.

Interestingly, these population properties are very similar to what has been inferred for SNe Iax
(
Foley et al. 2013
;
Meng & Podsiadlowski 2018
;
Jha 2017
)
but also for some high-velocity
(
Pan 2020
;
Zeng et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
and normal SNe Ia
(
Maoz & Badenes 2010
;
Soker 2022
)
.
SNe Iax in particular, present some properties that make them particularly attractive as potential ONe SN Ia counterparts. While they are usually interpreted as CO or (C)ONe WD deflagrations
(
Kromer et al. 2015
;
Yamanaka et al. 2015
;
Magee et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
, at least a fraction of them occur inside a helium-rich CSM
(
Magee et al. 2019
;
Jacobson-Galán et al. 2019
)
, while none has been observed in an elliptical galaxy host
(
Foley et al. 2013
)
.
Lastly, at least one nearby SN Iax, SN 2012Z, has been securely associated with a luminous blue progenitor
(
McCully et al. 2014
;
Stritzinger et al. 2015
;
Yamanaka et al. 2015
;
McCully et al. 2022
)
, with properties similar to those inferred for our ONe models prior to collapse (Section
3
).

Besides the explosions themselves, the progenitors of (C)ONe SNe Ia might also be easily identifiable, particularly in the few thousand years leading to the collapse. This is because of their high luminosities (Fig.
2
), but most importantly due to their hydrogen- and helium-free, metal-rich atmospheres (Fig.
9
). Such peculiar atmospheric abundances are not easily produced via ordinary stellar evolution
paths. A unique object that presents some interesting similarities to our progenitor models is WS35
(
Gvaramadze et al. 2019
;
Oskinova et al. 2019
)
.
This star is luminous (
L\simeq 10^{4.5}
L
⊙
), hot (
\sim
200,000 K), as well as hydrogen- and helium-free
(
Oskinova et al. 2019
)
. It is also
embedded in a circular nebula
(
Gvaramadze et al. 2019
)
, that was likely produced via a
strong stellar wind. While our progenitor models are somewhat cooler prior to collapse, this could be due to artifacts related to, for instance, mixing assumptions or surface boundary conditions.

Other indirect, but distinct observational evidence could be related to the properties of compact-object populations at different metallicities. For instance if neutron star formation via ECSNe is suppressed at high-
Z
environments (Fig.
5
), as our results suggest, both the integrated and
Z
-dependent double NS (DNS) merger rates would be affected
(
Mandel & Broekgaarden 2022
)
. One would also expect to see differences between Galactic and extra-galactic DNSs (e.g., in their mass ratio distributions). Similarly, the Galactic pulsar population might also be affected, for example fewer ECSNe could lead to a decreased number of young radio pulsars in wide binary systems and globular clusters
(
Antoniadis 2021
;
Willcox et al. 2021
, and references therein)
. However, we note that there might be other core-collapse SN mechanisms that produce low-velocity pulsars
(see
Antoniadis, John et al. 2022
, and references therein)

Lastly, models that do not reach the Chandrasekhar limit, but become (C)ONe WDs instead, also
present some interest as possible thermonuclear SN and NS progenitors. Their high masses (up to 1.35
\mathrm{\,M_{\odot}}
; Section
3.1
)
imply that they only need to accrete very small amounts of mass to become unstable. Their collapse would then lead to a thermonuclear deflagration

(
Meng & Podsiadlowski 2014
;
Willcox et al. 2016
;
Bravo, E. 2019
)
, or the formation of a neutron star in an accretion-induced collapse
(
Nomoto & Kondo 1991
;
Tauris et al. 2013
;
Ruiter et al. 2019
;
Wang & Liu 2020
, e.g.,)
. Similarly to what has been discussed in this work, we expect the core composition—particularly the amount of residual carbon and its distribution in the core—to play a critical role in determining their fate
(
Brooks et al. 2017
)
. It is conceivable that if these WDs can achieve oxygen ignition conditions similar to those of (C)ONe SNe Ia, then the resulting explosions would also have similar appearance. In this case, (C)ONe WDs may contribute SNe Ia with much longer delay times.
