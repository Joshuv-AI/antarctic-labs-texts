Oracle-Augmented Prophet Inequalities
Sariel Har-Peled, Elfarouk Harb, Vasilis Livanos

Abstract
In the classical prophet inequality settings, a gambler is given a sequence of n n random variables X 1 , … , X n X_{1},\dots,X_{n} , taken from known distributions, observes their values in this (potentially adversarial) order, and select one of them, immediately after it is being observed, so that its value is as high as possible. The classical prophet inequality shows a strategy that guarantees a value at least half of that an omniscience prophet that picks the maximum, and this ratio is optimal. Here, we generalize the prophet inequality, allowing the gambler some additional information about the future that is otherwise privy only to the prophet. Specifically, at any point in the process, the gambler is allowed to query an oracle 𝒪 \mathcal{O} . The oracle responds with a single bit answer: YES if the current realization is greater than the remaining realizations, and NO otherwise. We show that the oracle model with m m oracle calls is equivalent to the Top- 1 1 -of- ( m + 1 ) (m+1) model when the objective is maximizing the probability of selecting the maximum. This equivalence fails to hold when the objective is maximizing the competitive ratio, but we still show that any algorithm for the oracle model implies an equivalent competitive ratio for the Top- 1 1 -of- ( m + 1 ) (m+1) model. We resolve the oracle model for any m m , giving tight lower and upper bound on the best possible competitive ratio compared to an almighty adversary. As a consequence, we provide new results as well as improvements on known results for the Top- 1 1 -of- m m model.

Introduction

The field of optimal stopping theory concerns optimization settings
where one makes decisions in a sequential manner, given imperfect
information about the future, in a bid to maximize a reward or
minimize a cost. A canonical setting in this area is the
prophet
inequality

[
KS77
,
KS78
]

gambler is presented with rewards
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}

decision to either accept
X_{i}

reject
X_{i}
and continue, losing the option to select
X_{i}
in the
future. The goal of the gambler is to maximize the selected reward
comparing against a
prophet
who knows all realizations in
advance and selects the maximum realized reward. Throughout, we
assume, without loss of generality, that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are
continuous random variables.

The performance of the gambler can be measured in terms of several
objectives. A common metric used in the literature is the

\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

Type of Adversary.

In the context of prophet inequalities, the distinction between an
offline adversary and an almighty adversary is crucial to
understanding the competitive ratio bounds of prophet inequalities. An
offline adversary, often considered less powerful, observes the

distributions
of the random variables, and chooses an
adversarial order based on the distributions. An almighty adversary is
stronger:

Definition 1.1
.

An
almighty adversary
possesses complete information,
including the algorithm’s random decisions, and can thus tailor
the ordering of the sequence to worst-case scenarios with perfect
foresight. In particular, the almighty adversary observes the
gambler’s algorithm, and the values of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, then
chooses a permutation order
X_{\sigma(1)},\dots,X_{\sigma(n)}

to show the algorithm the values in that order.

Consequently, while both types of adversaries present different levels
of challenge, the almighty adversary sets a far stricter benchmark,
typically leading to lower competitive ratios for prophet
inequalities. Unless stated otherwise, we work with the almighty
adversary. For a discussion on the (very subtle) differences between
the two adversaries for our model, see
Appendix
B
.

Model.

We introduce a new model that generalizes the standard prophet
inequality setting, and analyze it as a means to obtain new results
and improvements in the
Top-
1
-of-
m
model. Our model
allows the algorithm some information about the future that is
otherwise privy only to the prophet. Specifically, at any point in the
process, upon seeing a reward
X_{i}
, the algorithm is allowed to query
an oracle
\mathcal{O}
. The oracle
\mathcal{O}
responds with a single bit
answer:
YES
if the current realization is larger than the
remaining realizations, i.e.,
X_{i}>\max_{j=i+1}^{n}X_{j}
and

NO
otherwise. In other words, the oracle
\mathcal{O}
informs
the algorithm it should select
X_{i}
, or reject it, because there is a
reward coming up that is at least as good
2
2

2

There are

very subtle
differences between an oracle that answers
>

queries, vs
\geq
queries. See
Appendix
B
for a
discussion on this. In particular, the
>
oracle is weaker than
the
\geq
oracle; imagine a stream of
1
values, the
>
oracle
will always answer
NO
, while the
\geq
oracle answers
YES
on the
first query
. Clearly, with no queries available, one recovers the
classical prophet inequality setting, whereas with
n-1
queries,
the strategy of using a query on every
X_{i}
, for

i=1,\dots,n-1
, leads to the algorithm selecting the highest
realization always. Thus, this model interpolates nicely between the
two extremes of full or no information about the future.

Motivation.

Our oracle choice was driven by our initial effort to reformulate the
Top-
1
-of-
m
model in order to get a better understanding of that
settings and improve the known bounds. While we initially thought the
two models are the same, we later observed the subtle differences
between the two models. Thankfully, it turns out that one can still
use this oracle model to study the Top-
1
-of-
m
model and improve
the previously known bounds, which was our original goal. We did
consider a few other oracle models, which we briefly mention here: if
the oracle predicts the maximum value, then there is a trivial
solution of asking the oracle at
X_{1}
, and just waiting until the
maximum value
v
arrives. Another option is for the oracle to predict
a
range
for the maximum value, but formalizing this in a more
general setting turns out to be difficult without assuming something
about the support of each random variable. While we explored various
other oracle formulations, we chose the simplest version that improved
the lower bound for the Top-
1
-of-
m
model and for which we can also
achieve a tight competitive ratio. We leave exploring more complex
oracle models for future work.

Lemma 2.1

3.1
The exponent sequence

Definition 3.1
.

For every
m\geq 1
, let
\xi_{m}
denote the unique

positive
solution to the following equation:

1-e^{-\xi_{m}}=\frac{\Gamma(m+1,\xi_{m})}{m!},

where
\Gamma(m+1,x)=\int_{t=x}^{\infty}{t^{m}e^{-t}\mathop{dt}}

denotes the
upper incomplete gamma
function. The

exponent sequence
is
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
.

We show below that the optimal competitive ratio of

\mathcal{M}(\hyperref@@ii[def:prop:h:o:m]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{O}}}_{m},\hyperref@@ii[def:toiidornot]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\textsf{non-IID}}}},\hyperref@@ii[def:RoE]{\textsf{RoE}})
is exactly
1-e^{-\xi_{m}}
. It is

known

that, for
x\geq 0
and an integer
m+1>0
, we have

\Gamma(m+1,x)=m!\,e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{m}{\frac{x^{k}}{k!}}\leq m!e^{-x}e^{x}\leq m!.

(3.1)

As such, the above equation on the value of
\xi_{m}
, becomes

1-e^{-\xi_{m}}=e^{-\xi_{m}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}{\frac{\left(\xi_{m}\right)^{k}}{k!}}\qquad\iff\qquad\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}{\frac{\left(\xi_{m}\right)^{k}}{k!}}=1.

This readily implies that the exponent sequence is monotonically
increasing, and
m/e^{2}\leq\xi_{m}\leq m
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let

\mathsf{q}_{k+1}\left(x\right)=\frac{\Gamma(k+1,x)}{k!}=e^{-x}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}.

This implies
\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\xi_{m}\right)=1-e^{-\xi_{m}}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

\mathsf{q}_{m+1}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=-e^{-x}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}
.

Proof:

As
(e^{-x})^{\prime}=-e^{-x}
, we have

\mathsf{q}_{m+1}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=-e^{-x}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(e^{-x}\frac{x^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}-e^{-x}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}\right)=-e^{-x}+e^{-x}-e^{-x}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}=-e^{-x}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
m\geq 1
, we have

\left(m!\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{m}}}<\xi_{m}<\left((m+1)!\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{m+1}}}
.

Proof:

Guided by
Eq. (
3.1
)
, define

h(x)=\frac{\Gamma(m+1,x)}{m!}-1+e^{-x}=\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(x\right)-1+e^{-x}=e^{-x}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}-e^{x}+1\right)=e^{-x}\left(1-T(x)\right),

where

T(x)=\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}.

By
Lemma
3.3
, we have

h^{\prime}(x)=-e^{-x}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}-e^{-x}<0.

Namely,
h(\cdot)
is a strictly decreasing function. Thus,

\xi_{m}
the positive root of
h(x)=0
is unique, as
h(0)=1
,
and
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}h(x)=-1
.

Setting
\beta=\left((m+1)!\right)^{1/(m+1)}
, we have

T(\beta)>\frac{\beta^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}=\frac{(m+1)!}{(m+1)!}=1,

which readily implies
h(\beta)<0
. By the AM-GM inequality,
we have that

\gamma=\sqrt[m]{m!}<\sum_{i=1}^{m}i/m=\frac{m+1}{2}.

In particular, we have

\frac{\gamma^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}=\frac{m!\cdot\gamma}{(m+1)!}=\frac{\gamma}{m+1}<\frac{1}{2}.

As such, we have

T(\gamma)\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma^{i}}{i!}\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma^{m+1}\gamma^{i-m-1}}{(m+1)!(m+1)^{i-m-1}}<\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma^{j}}{(m+1)^{j}}<\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{((m+1)/2)^{j}}{(m+1)^{j}}=1.

Thus,
h(\gamma)>0
. We conclude that
\gamma<\xi_{m}<\beta
.

3.2
Background: Sharding, poissonization, and stochastic dominance

For a sequence of random variables
\mathbf{X}=X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, let

\left|{\alpha\leq\mathbf{X}\leq\beta}\right|=\left|{\left\{i\;\middle|\;\alpha\leq X_{i}\leq\beta\right\}}\right|
denote the number of realizations in this
sequence falling in the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
.

3.2.1
Sharding

For the lower bound, we use poissonization and sharding

[
Har24
]
. Given random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
with

cdf
s
F_{1},\dots,F_{n}
, instead of sampling
X_{i}
from
F_{i}
, we
instead replace it with a sequence of
K
independent random variables

\mathbf{H}_{i}=Y_{i,1},\dots,Y_{i,K}
, such that
\max_{j}Y_{i,j}
has
the same distribution as
X_{i}
. Specifically, the
cdf
of
Y_{i,j}
,
for all
j
, is
F^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{K}}}_{i}
. Thus, the distribution of

\max\left\{{Y_{i,1},\dots,Y_{i,K}}\right\}
is the same as
X_{i}
. This
creates a new sequence of
Kn
samples

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{H}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{H}_{n}
, where
\cdot
is the
concatenation operator. Observe that for any
\alpha\geq 0
and
integer
t
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left|{\mathbf{X}\geq\alpha}\right|>t\right]<\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left|{\mathbf{S}\geq\alpha}\right|>t\right].

This implies, that for threshold algorithms, running on
\mathbf{S}
instead
of
\mathbf{X}
can only generate worst results. We emphasize that this
sharding is done only for analysis purposes.

3.2.2
Poissonization

Definition 3.8
.

A random variable
X
has Poisson distribution with rate

\lambda
, denoted by
X\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)
, if

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X=i\right]=\lambda^{k}e^{-\lambda}/k!
. Conveniently,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{X}\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{V}}\!\left[{X}\right]=\lambda
.

The purpose of the sharding is to be able to bound quantities of the
form
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left|{\beta\leq\mathbf{S}\leq\tau}\right|=t\right]
. As
K
grows,
the underlying random variable
\left|{\beta\leq\mathbf{S}\leq\tau}\right|

has a binomial distribution that converges to a Poisson distribution.

Observation 3.9
.

For

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}x(1-c^{1/x})=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\exp(\log(c)/x)}{1/x}=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log(c)\exp(\log(c)/x)/x^{2}}{-1/x^{2}}=-\log c

Let
\tau
be a threshold such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[Y_{i,j}\geq\tau\right]=c

\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(1-\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{i}\leq\tau\right]^{1/K}\right)=c.

(3.2)

The limit of

)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\log\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{i}\leq\tau\right]=c
, by

. Equivalently, for
{Z}=\max\left\{{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}}\right\}
,

e^{-c}=\exp\left(\Bigl.\smash{\sum_{i=1}^{n}}\log\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{i}\leq\tau\right]\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{i}\leq\tau\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\leq\tau\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[{Z}\leq\tau\right].

In particular, the distribution of the number of indices

can be well approximated with a Poisson

\iff

, and consider the sum

. The variable

\mathrm{bin}(K,\psi_{i})

\psi_{i}=1-\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{i}\leq\tau\right]^{1/K}
.

Let
\lambda_{i}=\psi_{i}K
, and consider the random variable

(i.e.,
U_{i}
has a Poisson distribution
with rate

). Intuitively,
V_{i}
and

have similar

\left|{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[V_{i}\in T\right]-\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[U_{i}\in T\right]}\right|\leq 2K\psi_{i}^{2}=2\lambda_{i}^{2}/K\leq 2c^{2}/K,

by

)
. The later quantity goes to zero as

Thus, we get a variable

with a Poisson distribution for each

\lambda_{i}
, where

models

values larger than

models the total number of times

that values encountered are larger
than

.

3.2.3
The distribution in a range

Repeating the same process with a bigger threshold
\beta>\tau
,
would yield a similar Poisson random variable
U_{\beta}
with a

lower
rate
\lambda_{\beta}
. The quantity

\Delta=U_{\tau}-U_{\beta}
is the number of values in
\mathbf{S}
in the
range
[\tau,\beta]
. Furthermore,
\Delta
has a Poisson
distribution with rate
\lambda_{\tau}-\lambda_{\beta}
. Specifically,

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left|{\beta\leq\mathbf{S}\leq\tau}\right|=t\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\Delta=t\right]
.

The key to our analysis is that the variables
\Delta
and
U_{\beta}

are independent (in the limit as
K
increases).

3.2.4
Stochastic dominance

A standard observation is that for a non-negative random variable
X
,
we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{X}\right]=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X\geq x\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Thus, for
{Z}=\max\left\{{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}}\right\}
, and for an algorithm

\mathcal{A}
, if one can guarantee that there is
c\in[0,1]
, such that
for all
\nu\geq 0
,
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathcal{A}\geq\nu\right]\geq c\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[{Z}\geq\nu\right]
,
then

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}\geq x]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq c\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[{Z}\geq x]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq c\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{{Z}}\right].

And hence
c
is a lower bound on the competitive ratio of
\mathcal{A}
. This
argument is used in several results on prophet inequalities and is
often referred to as
majorizing

\mathcal{A}
with
{Z}
.

3.3
An optimal single-threshold algorithm (lower bound)

Here, we describe a single-threshold algorithm that achieves the
optimal competitive ratio in the oracle model.

Definition 3.10
.

A
single threshold
algorithm sets a threshold
\tau
, and
start reading the sequence. Whenever encountering a realization

>\tau
, the algorithm stops and consult with the oracle. The
oracle query is whether all the values remaining in the suffix of
the sequence are of value
\leq\tau
. If the oracle returns

YES
, the algorithm accepts the current value and stops. Otherwise
it raises its threshold to
\tau=X_{i}
and continues. If the oracle
runs out of oracle calls, it returns the first value encountered
after the last oracle call that is bigger than
\tau
(which
exists, since all oracle calls returned
NO
).

While technically, the querying threshold of the algorithm might
change during its execution, we call the algorithm a single-threshold
algorithm since it uses a single-threshold to decide whether to query
the oracle or not, and this threshold
does not change with
i
,
unlike for example the optimal DP for the
IID
prophet inequality or
the prophet secretary model. Our oracle model is quite different than
most other prophet inequality models in the sense that the algorithm
has some knowledge of the (true) future. Of course, any algorithm that
knows that the maximum of
X_{i+1},\dots,X_{n}
is larger than
X_{i}

would be wasting queries if it expended them on some
X_{j}<X_{i}
for

j>i
, and thus the spirit of it being a single-threshold algorithm
to decide whether to query the oracle or not remains.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let

\alpha=1-e^{-\xi_{m}}=1-e^{-\nicefrac{{m}}{{e}}+o\left(m\right)}
,

. For any finite sequence

of

\tau

\mathcal{M}(\hyperref@@ii[def:prop:h:o:m]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{O}}}_{m},\hyperref@@ii[def:toiidornot]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\textsf{non-IID}}}},\hyperref@@ii[def:RoE]{\textsf{RoE}})

Proof:

Let
\mathbf{X}=X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, and
{Z}=\max_{i}X_{i}
. The
threshold
\tau
is the

quantile of the maximum, i.e.

\mathbb{P}[Z\leq\tau]=e^{-\xi_{m}}

to denote the

\mathbf{X}
.

As suggested in
Section

(for the analysis), we imagine

. Some
counterintuitively, imagine first generating

. Thus,

\mathbf{S}
, let

\mathbf{S}_{\geq\tau}
denote the subsequence of elements of
\mathbf{S}

that their values are above
\tau

\mathbf{X}_{\geq\tau}

is a subsequence of
\mathbf{S}_{\geq\tau}
. Thus, we analyze the

\mathbf{S}
.

Let
\beta\in[0,\tau]

is equal to the probability it selects any
value. Thus,

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X})\geq\beta\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathcal{A}\geq\tau\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z\geq\tau\right]=1-e^{-\xi_{m}}\geq\bigl(1-e^{-\xi_{m}}\bigr)\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z\geq\beta\right].

(3.3)

For

By sharding and Poissonization, the number of shards in the range

(resp.

(resp.

with rate

(resp.
q

Section

. Critically,

and

in that

and

\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X})\geq\beta\right]}{\mathbb{P}[Z\geq\beta]}\geq\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[(U_{\beta}\geq 1)\cap(0\leq\Delta\leq m)\right]}{\mathbb{P}[Z\geq\beta]}=\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[U_{\beta}\geq 1\right]}{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z\geq\beta\right]}\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[0\leq\Delta\leq m\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[0\leq\Delta\leq m\right].

Now, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[0\leq\Delta\leq m\right]=\sum_{i=0}^{m}e^{-(\xi_{m}-q)}\frac{(\xi_{m}-q)^{i}}{i!}=\frac{\Gamma(m+1,\xi_{m}-q)}{m!}\geq\frac{\Gamma(m+1,\xi_{m})}{m!}=1-e^{-\xi_{m}}.

by

The above implies that, for any

Lemma 3.12

Observation 3.13

Theorem 3.14

Consider any choice of

\hyperref@@ii[def:RoE]{\textsf{RoE}}(\mathcal{A})\leq 1-e^{-\xi_{m}}+\delta

Proof:

First, we discuss the strategy that the almighty adversary
adopts. The adversary first observes all values. Suppose
k

nonzero values show up from
X_{2},...,X_{n}
at indices

U=\{i_{1},...,i_{k}\}

X_{\sigma(1)},\dots,X_{\sigma(n+2)}
where
\sigma

\sigma(1)=1
,
\sigma(j)=i_{j},j=2,\dots,k+1

\sigma(j)=\hat{i}_{j}

k

.

Now we consider any algorithm for this setting. We strengthen the
algorithm by telling it the almighty adversary’s strategy; this
can never reduce its expected reward. Hence, the algorithm knows
it will see
X_{\sigma(1)}=X_{1}
, then a stream of
k
ones (where
it does not know
k
), then
n-k
zeros, and finally

X_{\sigma(n+2)}=X_{n+2}
. The algorithm has two initial decisions
to make; either query at
X_{1}
and continue (if the answer is
NO

with
m-1
oracle calls,
or it can just proceed to
X_{\sigma(2)},\dots X_{\sigma(n+1)}

with
m
oracle calls. Thus, the only difference in the two cases
is that in the former, we have only
m-1
oracle calls for

X_{\sigma(2)},\dots,X_{\sigma(n+1)}
but we get an expected
reward of
1
if
X_{\sigma(2)}=\dots=X_{\sigma(n+2)}=0
, and in
the later case, we get
m
oracle calls for

X_{\sigma(2)},\dots,X_{\sigma(n+1)}
, but we get
0
reward if

X_{\sigma(2)}=\dots=X_{\sigma(n+2)}=0
.

Let
k
be the number of non-zeros in
X_{2},\dots,X_{n+1}

(i.e.,
X_{\sigma(k+1)}
is the last
1
). When the algorithm
starts reading the stream of
1
s from

X_{\sigma(2)},...,X_{\sigma(n+1)}
, it needs to decide indices

S\subseteq\left\llbracket 2:n+1\right\rrbracket,\left|{S}\right|\leq m
where it will expend
the oracle call. Further, it is suboptimal to use the oracle at a

0
value, since regardless, the algorithm will receive a value of

0
in the end if it fails. Consider what happens if the algorithm
decides to query at index

with

X_{\sigma(i)}=1
. If
X_{\sigma(i+1)}=\dots X_{\sigma(n+1)}=0
,
then the algorithm gets on expectation

1/{\varepsilon}\cdot{\varepsilon}+(1-{\varepsilon})\cdot 1\xrightarrow[{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0]{}2
reward on expectation. However, if

,
then the oracle will return

1=X_{\sigma(i)}\not>\max(X_{\sigma(i+1)},\dots,X_{\sigma(n+2)})

.

Hence, the crucial observation is that an algorithm starting at

X_{\sigma(2)}
that uses its query calls at indices

gets on expectation
2
if and only if

(k+1)\in S
, and

otherwise. Thus, for algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{1}
that

and uses its oracles at indices

, it satisfies

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle=2\cdot\sum_{i\geq 0,(i+1)\in S}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}+1\cdot\sum_{i\geq 0,(i+1)\notin S}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}+\sum_{i\geq 0,(i+1)\in S}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{(i+1)\in S}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}

\mathcal{A}_{2}
that uses its oracle at

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(e^{-\xi_{m}}\cdot 2\right)+\left(2\cdot\sum_{i\geq 0,(i+1)\in S^{\prime}}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}\right)+\left(1\cdot\sum_{i\geq 1,(i+1)\notin S^{\prime}}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\geq 0}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}\right)+e^{-\xi_{m}}+\sum_{(i+1)\in S^{\prime}}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}

\displaystyle=1+e^{-\xi_{m}}+\sum_{(i+1)\in S^{\prime}}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}.

First, we show that the expression

because

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}-\sum_{i=k-1}^{k+m-2}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}-\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+k-2}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}=1+\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\xi_{m}\right)-e^{-\xi_{m}},

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+e^{-\xi_{m}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}e^{-\xi_{m}}\frac{\xi_{m}^{i}}{i!}=1+\mathsf{q}_{m}\left(\xi_{m}\right).

\displaystyle\max(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\right],\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}_{2}}\right])

\displaystyle\leq 1-e^{-\xi_{m}}+\mathsf{q}_{m}\left(\xi_{m}\right)+e^{-\xi_{m}}\max\{1,\frac{\xi_{m}^{m}}{m!}\}

,

\displaystyle=1-e^{-\xi_{m}}+\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\xi_{m}\right)

Therefore, the competitive ratio of every algorithm is

\hyperref@@ii[def:RoE]{\textsf{RoE}}\leq\frac{2\left(1-e^{-\xi_{m}}\right)}{2}=1-e^{-\xi_{m}}.

4.2
A single-threshold algorithm for general

m

As we saw in the previous section, even for a simple, single-threshold
algorithm, the analysis of the winning probability gets tedious
quickly. In this section, we generalize our single-threshold algorithm
to the case of general
m
, and use the fact that the maximum of a
uniformly random permutation of
n
values changes
\mathcal{O}\left(\log{n}\right)

times with high probability to obtain a guarantee on the winning
probability that is super-exponential with respect to
m
.

As before, our algorithm selects a threshold
\tau
such that

p=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X\geq\tau\right]
and every time the algorithm observes a
realization above
\tau
, it uses an oracle query and asks
O

if the realization should be selected or not. If not, then it updates
the threshold to the new higher value. If the algorithm runs out of
oracle calls, then it selects the first element above the current
threshold
\tau
that is encounters, if any. In other words, the
algorithm uses the oracle calls greedily for all realizations above

\tau
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For sufficiently large
m,n
, and an instance of

\mathcal{M}(\hyperref@@ii[def:prop:h:o:m]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{O}}}_{m},\hyperref@@ii[def:toiidornot]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsf{IID}}},{{\hyperref@@ii[def:PbM]{\mathbb{P}_{\max}}}})
, there exists an algorithm that
selects the maximum realization with probability at least

1-\mathcal{O}\left(m^{-\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}}\right)
.

Proof:

Let
L=e^{\sqrt{m}}
. The idea is to set
\tau
so that

p=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X\geq\tau\right]=\nicefrac{{L}}{{n}}
. As before, let
Y
be the
number of realizations above
\tau
. By
Theorem
A.1
, we
have

\mathbb{P}[\left|{Y-L}\right|\geq\delta L]\leq 2e^{-\delta^{2}L/3}.

Setting
\delta=1
yields that
1\leq Y\leq 2L
with
probability at least

1-2e^{-L/3}=1-2e^{-\nicefrac{{e^{\sqrt{m}}}}{{3}}}\geq 1-m^{-\nicefrac{{m}}{{4}}}
for all
m
.

Next, let
X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{Y}
be the subsequence of all
realizations larger than
\tau
, according to their arrival order,
and let
Z_{i}=1
if
X^{\prime}_{i}>\max_{j=1}^{i-1}X^{\prime}_{j}
, in other
words if
X^{\prime}_{i}
is larger than all previous realizations, and

Z_{i}=0
otherwise. Observe that
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z_{i}=1\right]=\nicefrac{{1}}{{i}}
,
and that the random variables
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are
independent. Furthermore, let
M=\sum_{i}Z_{i}
be the number of
times that the maximum realization changes in the sequence

X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{Y}
. Observe that if
M\leq m+1
, then
m
oracle
queries are sufficient for the algorithm to always select the
maximum realization. Therefore, our goal is to bound the
probability that this event happens.

Conditioned on
1\leq Y\leq 2L
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{M}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{2L}{\frac{1}{i}}\leq\log\left(2L\right)+1\leq\sqrt{m}+2.

For
\delta=\nicefrac{{m+2}}{{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{M}\right]}}-1

Notice that for

If we instead use the tight Chernoff bound in

, we can show that

for all

and

{\varepsilon}>0
.

Putting everything together, for our algorithm to succeed, it
suffices to have

and

, both of
which happen together with probability at least

.

4.3
An (almost) tight upper bound

Now that we have presented a simple, single-threshold algorithm for
the
\mathcal{M}(\hyperref@@ii[def:prop:h:o:m]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{O}}}_{m},\hyperref@@ii[def:toiidornot]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsf{IID}}},{{\hyperref@@ii[def:PbM]{\mathbb{P}_{\max}}}})
setting, a reasonable question to ask
is how far it is from being optimal. As we show in this section, the

Theorem 4.3
.

There exists an instance of
\mathcal{M}(\hyperref@@ii[def:prop:h:o:m]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{O}}}_{m},\hyperref@@ii[def:toiidornot]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsf{IID}}},{{\hyperref@@ii[def:PbM]{\mathbb{P}_{\max}}}})

Proof:

To construct an instance in which no algorithm can achieve a high

m

n

0

Next, consider an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
\mathcal{M}(\hyperref@@ii[def:prop:h:o:m]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{O}}}_{m},\hyperref@@ii[def:toiidornot]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsf{IID}}},{{\hyperref@@ii[def:PbM]{\mathbb{P}_{\max}}}})

\bullet

m+1
non-zero values in

X_{i},\ldots,X_{n+1}
, then the algorithm gets at most

1+n\epsilon
. Hence, on expectation, the algorithm gets

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\nabla_{i}=0\right]\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{X_{n+2}}\right]+\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\bigl.\nabla_{i}\in\left\llbracket 1:m\right\rrbracket\right]\rho+\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\bigl.\nabla_{i}>m\right](1+n{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{q}_{1}\left(\beta\right)\Psi+\left(\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\beta\right)-\mathsf{q}_{1}\left(\beta\right)\right)(1+\Psi)+(1-\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\beta\right))\cdot\left(1+n{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0]{}\mathsf{q}_{1}\left(\beta\right)\Psi+\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\beta\right)+\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\beta\right)\Psi-\mathsf{q}_{1}\left(\beta\right)-\mathsf{q}_{1}\left(\beta\right)\Psi+1-\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\beta\right)

\displaystyle=1+\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\beta\right)\Psi-\mathsf{q}_{1}\left(\beta\right)=1+\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\beta\right)\Psi-e^{-\beta}.

Let

f(x)=\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(x\right)\Psi-e^{-x}.

By
Lemma
3.3
, we have

f^{\prime}(x)=-e^{-x}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}\Psi+e^{-x}=-e^{-x}\frac{x^{m}}{\xi_{m}^{m}}+e^{-x}.

As such,
f^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x\in[0,\xi_{m})
. Namely,
f
is
increasing in this range, and this range contains the value

\beta
. Since
\mathsf{q}_{m+1}\left(\xi_{m}\right)=1-e^{-\xi_{m}}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}}\right]\leq 1+f(\beta)\leq 1+f(\xi_{m})\leq 1+(1-e^{-\xi_{m}})\Psi-e^{-\xi_{m}}=(1-e^{-\xi_{m}})(1+\Psi).

This implies, that for all cases, the competitive ratio is

\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{\mathcal{A}}\right]}{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[{Z}\right]}\leq\frac{1-e^{-\xi_{m}}+(1-e^{-\xi_{m}})\Psi}{1+\Psi}=\frac{\left(1-e^{-\xi_{m}}\right)\left(1+\Psi\right)}{1+\Psi}=1-e^{-\xi_{m}}.

B.1
On the optimal multiple-threshold algorithms

Intuitively, a better strategy than single-threshold, is using
different thresholds (potentially based on the values seen so far).

New Input Instance

The input is a sequence made out of
n+2
random variables, for
n

sufficiently large. Each of these random variables can have only two
values – either zero or some positive value. Specifically, for

{\varepsilon}>0
sufficiently small (e.g.,
{\varepsilon}\ll 1/n^{4}
), let

X_{1}=1,\quad\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{i}=1+{\varepsilon}(i-1)\right]=\frac{c_{1,m}}{n},\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\left\llbracket 2:n+1\right\rrbracket,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{n+2}=\tfrac{c_{2,m}}{{\varepsilon}}\right]={\varepsilon},

Optimal algorithm.

For a fixed instance of the prophet inequality problem, one can
usually argue about the optimal algorithm for the instance using
reverse dynamic programming. The argument is standard, but we include
it here for the sake of completeness. Let

b_{i}=1+{\varepsilon}(i-1)
, for
i\in\left\llbracket 1:n+1\right\rrbracket
, and

b_{n+2}=c_{2,m}/{\varepsilon}
. Note that
b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq b_{n+2}
.
Similarly, let
p_{1}=1
,
p_{i}=c_{1,m}/n
for
i\in\left\llbracket 2:n+1\right\rrbracket
, and
finally
p_{n+2}={\varepsilon}
. Now, the input is the sequence of random
variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+2}
, with

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{i}=b_{i}\right]=p_{i},\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,n+2.

Let
Z_{i}=\max(X_{i},\ldots.,X_{n})
. Let
E_{t}(k)
be the expected
value of an
optimal algorithm
running on
X_{k},\ldots,X_{n}

having access to
t
oracle calls. Let
E_{t}^{\uparrow}(k)
be the
expected value of an
optimal algorithm
running on

X_{k},\ldots,X_{n}
with
t
oracle calls, given that
Z_{k}>0
. We have
the (mutual) recurrence

\displaystyle E_{t}(k)=\,\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X_{k}=0]E_{t}(k+1)

\displaystyle+\,\mathbb{P}[X_{k}>0]\begin{cases}\max\begin{cases}E_{t}(k+1)\\
\mathbb{P}[Z_{k+1}=0]b_{k}+\mathbb{P}[Z_{k+1}>0]E_{t-1}^{\uparrow}(k+1)\end{cases}&t>0\\
\max\left(E_{t}(k+1),b_{k}\right)&t=0.\end{cases}

Let

\alpha_{k}=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[X_{k}>0\;\middle|\;Z_{k}>0\right]=\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[(X_{k}>0)\cap(Z_{k}>0)\right]}{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z_{k}>0\right]}=\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{k}>0\right]}{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z_{k}>0\right]}.

Consider

\beta_{k+1}=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[Z_{k+1}>0\;\middle|\;(X_{k}>0)\cap(Z_{k}>0)\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[Z_{k+1}>0\;\middle|\;X_{k}>0\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z_{k+1}>0\right].

We now have

E_{t}^{\uparrow}(k)=(1-\alpha_{k})E_{t}^{\uparrow}(k+1)+\begin{cases}\alpha_{k}\max\begin{cases}E_{t}(k+1),\\
(1-\beta_{k+1})b_{k}+\beta_{k+1}E_{t-1}^{\uparrow}(k+1)\end{cases}&t>0\\
\alpha_{k}\max\left(E_{t}(k+1),b_{k}\right)&t=0\end{cases}

It is straightforward to argue by reverse induction on
k
that

E_{t}(k)
is the best any algorithm can get on expectation from

X_{k},\ldots,X_{n+2}
using
t
oracle queries, as the recurrence
includes all possible outcomes of these queries. Also note that the
recurrence can easily be evaluated in
O(nm)
time.
