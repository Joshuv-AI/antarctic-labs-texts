2.
Background

We now more-or-less follow the conventions of
[
BeD
]
.

Suppose that
(X,d_{X})
is a metric space.
Suppose that
U
and
V
are non-empty subsets of
X
with bounded diameter.
Throughout the paper we use the following notational convention.

d_{X}(U,V)=\operatorname{diam}_{X}(U\cup V)

We write
p=_{\mathsf{C}}q
if, for non-negative numbers
p
,
q
, and
\mathsf{C}
we have both
q\leq\mathsf{C}p+\mathsf{C}
and
p\leq\mathsf{C}q+\mathsf{C}
.
Also, we use the
cut-off
function:

[p]_{\mathsf{C}}
is equal to
p
if
p\geq\mathsf{C}
and is zero otherwise.

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are metric spaces.
A relation
f\colon X\to Y
is a
coarse map
if there is a constant
\mathsf{C}
so that, for all
x\in X
, the image
f(x)
is non-empty and has
\operatorname{diam}_{Y}(f(x))\leq\mathsf{C}
.
A coarse map
f\colon X\to Y
is a
coarse embedding
if there are functions
F,G\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
so that

•

\lim_{t\to\infty}F(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}G(t)=\infty
and,

•

for all
x,y\in X
, we have

F(d_{X}(x,y))\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq G(d_{X}(x,y))

A coarse map
f\colon X\to Y
is
coarsely onto
if there is a constant
\mathsf{C}>0
so that for all
y\in Y
there is a point
x\in X
with
d_{Y}(f(x),y)<\mathsf{C}
.

A coarse map
f\colon X\to Y
is
coarsely Lipschitz
if there is a constant
\mathsf{C}>0
so that for all
x,y\in X
we have

d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq\mathsf{C}\cdot d_{X}(x,y)+\mathsf{C}

That is, we have no lower bound, but we require the upper bound to be affine.

A coarse embedding
f
is a
quasi-isometric embedding
if the functions
F
and
G
are both affine (with positive coefficients).
The more usual definition is to require a constant
\mathsf{C}>0
so that
d_{X}(x,y)=_{\mathsf{C}}d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))
for all
x,y\in X
.
A quasi-isometric embedding
f
is a
quasi-isometry
if
f
is also coarsely onto.

2.1.
Asymptotic dimension

We now follow
[
Gr
, Section 1.E]
.
A metric space
X
has
asymptotic dimension

\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}(X)
at most
n
if for every
R>0
there is a
\mathsf{D}>0
and a cover
\mathcal{U}
of
X
so that

•

for all
U\in\mathcal{U}
we have
\operatorname{diam}_{X}(U)\leq\mathsf{D}
and

•

every metric
R
-ball in
X
intersects at most
n+1
sets in
\mathcal{U}
.

For example, trees have asymptotic dimension at most one.
More generally, following
[
BeD
, page 1272]
, a collection of metric spaces
\mathcal{X}
has asymptotic dimension at most
n
,
uniformly
, if for every
R
there is a constant
\mathsf{D}
so that every
X\in\mathcal{X}
has a cover as above.

If two metric spaces
X
and
Y
are quasi-isometric and
\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}(X)\leq n
, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}(Y)\leq n
. In view of this, we say a finitely generated group
G
has asymptotic dimension at most
n
if some (and thus all) of its Cayley graphs have asymptotic dimension at most
n
.

We list two well-known facts about asymptotic dimension.

Fact 2.2
.

[
BeD
, Theorem 32]

Suppose that
U
and
V
are metric spaces.
We give

the
\ell^{1}
product metric.
Then

\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}U\times V\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}U+\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}V\qed

Fact 2.3
.

[
BeD
, Proof of Proposition 22]

If
U
coarsely embeds into

then

\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}U\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}V\qed

2.4.
Quasi-trees of metric graphs

We quickly review the machinery of
quasi-trees of metric spaces
, as introduced in
[
BBF
, Section 4]
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a collection of metric graphs.
Suppose also that we have, for every pair of distinct graphs
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
, a non-empty subset
\pi_{B}(A)\subset B
.
Also, fix a sufficiently large constant
\mathsf{k}>0
.

With respect to the data
(\mathcal{F},\pi,\mathsf{k})
we require the following axioms.

Axiom 2.5

(Bounded projections)
.

For distinct
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
we have:

\operatorname{diam}_{B}(\pi_{B}(A))\leq\mathsf{k}\qed

For
A,B,C\in\mathcal{F}
, and if
A\neq B
and
B\neq C
, we adopt the following shorthand:

d_{B}(A,C)=d_{B}(\pi_{B}(A),\pi_{B}(C))

Axiom 2.6

(Behrstock inequality)
.

For distinct
A,B,C\in\mathcal{F}
at most one of the following is greater than
\mathsf{k}
:

d_{A}(B,C),\quad d_{B}(A,C),\quad d_{C}(A,B)\qed

Axiom 2.7

(Large links)
.

For distinct
A,C\in\mathcal{F}
the following set is finite:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{B\in\mathcal{F}\mathbin{\mid}\mbox{$A\neq B$, $B\neq C$, and $d_{B}(A,C)>\mathsf{k}$}}}\right\}\qed

We call these the
BBF axioms
.
These are called (P0), (P1), and (P2) in
[
BBF
]
.

Suppose that the data
(\mathcal{F},\pi,\mathsf{k})
satisfies the BBF axioms.
Then, by
[
BBF
, Theorem A]
, for every sufficiently large
\mathsf{K}\geq\mathsf{k}
there is a metric graph
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{C}_{\mathsf{K}}(\mathcal{F})
called the
quasi-tree of graphs
.
We denote the metric on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})
by
d_{\mathcal{C}}
.
We now list several properties of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})
.

Fact 2.8
.

[
BBF
, Theorem A and Definition 3.6]
.
By construction, every
A\in\mathcal{F}
isometrically embeds into
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})
.
The image is totally geodesic.
For distinct
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
their images in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})
are disjoint.
Finally, these images cover the vertices of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})
.
∎

Thus we may identify the vertices of
A
with their images in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})
.
We extend the definition of
\pi_{B}
as follows.
If
b\in B
then we take
\pi_{B}(b)=b
.
If
a\in A
, and
A
is distinct from
B
, then we take
\pi_{B}(a)=\pi_{B}(A)
.
One may think of
\pi_{B}\colon\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})\to B
as being a “closest points projection” map.
We adopt the shorthand

d_{B}(a,c)=d_{B}(\pi_{B}(a),\pi_{B}(c))

Fact 2.9
.

[
BBF
, Definition 4.1]

By construction, there is a constant
\mathsf{L}
(depending only on
\mathsf{K}
) with the following property.
Suppose that
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
are graphs so that the set of Axiom
2.7
is empty.
Then
d_{\mathcal{C}}(\pi_{A}(B),\pi_{B}(A))\leq\mathsf{L}
.
∎

We have the following
distance estimate
for
d_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Theorem 2.10
.

[
BBF
, Theorem 4.13]

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a family of metric graphs satisfying the BBF axioms.
Suppose that
\mathsf{K}
is sufficiently large.
Suppose that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathsf{K}}(\mathcal{F})
is the quasi-tree of graphs.
Then every
\mathsf{K}^{\prime}
, sufficiently larger than
\mathsf{K}
, has the follow property.
For any

we have

\frac{1}{2}\sum[d_{B}(a,c)]_{\mathsf{K}^{\prime}}\leq d_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F})}(a,c)\leq 6\mathsf{K}+4\sum[d_{B}(a,c)]_{\mathsf{K}}

where both sums are taken over all

. ∎

Suppose that
G
is a group acting on
\mathcal{F}
.
We further assume that, for any
g\in G
and for any
A\in\mathcal{F}
there is an isometry
g_{A}\colon A\to g\cdot A
.
We suppose that these isometries have the following consistency properties.
Suppose that
g,h\in G
are group elements and
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
are graphs with

.

•

For all

we have

.

•

For any

From
[

, Section 3.7]
we deduce the following:
there is an isometric action of

which extends the action of the isometries

.

We also have the following control on the asymptotic dimension of

Theorem 2.11
.

[

, Theorem 4.24]

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a family of metric graphs satisfying the BBF axioms.
Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
has asymptotic dimension at most

Then
\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F}))\leq D+1

Theorem 2.12
.

[

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a family of quasi-trees satisfying the BBF axioms.

\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F}))\leq 1

The notion of a

2.13.
Surfaces, curves, and arcs

Let
S=S_{g,b}
denote the connected, compact, oriented surface of genus
g
with
b
boundary components.
The
complexity
of
S
is defined to be
\xi(S)=3g-3+b
.
This counts the number of curves in any pants decomposition of
S
.
We will always assume that
\xi(S)\geq 1
.
We will also need the
modified complexity

\xi^{\prime}(S)=4g-3+b
.
If
S
is closed then we will simply write
S_{g}
for
S_{g,0}
.

Suppose that
\alpha
is an embedded arc or curve in
S
.
We call the embedding
proper
if
\alpha\cap\partial S=\partial\alpha
.
A properly embedded arc or curve
\alpha
in
S
is
essential
if it does not cut a disk off of
S
.
A properly embedded curve
\alpha
is
non-peripheral
if it does not cut an annulus off of
S
.

A
proper isotopy
is an isotopy through proper embeddings.
Let
[\alpha]
denote the proper isotopy class of
\alpha
.
Given
\alpha
and
\beta
, properly embedded arcs or curves, we define their
geometric intersection number
:

i(\alpha,\beta)=\min\{|\alpha^{\prime}\cap\beta^{\prime}|:\alpha^{\prime}\in[\alpha],\beta^{\prime}\in[\beta]\}

Note that
i(\alpha,\beta)=0
if and only if they have disjoint (proper isotopy) representatives.
To lighten the notation, we typically will not distinguish between a curve (or arc)
\alpha
and its proper isotopy class
[\alpha]
.

A connected, compact subsurface
X\subset S
is
essential
if every component of
\partial X
is either a component of
\partial S
or is essential and non-peripheral in
S
.
If
X
is essential we define the
relative boundary
of
X
to be
\partial_{S}X=\partial X-\partial S
.

Remark 2.14
.

Note that if
X\subset Y
are both essential subsurfaces of
S
, then
\xi^{\prime}(X)\leq\xi^{\prime}(Y)
.
Equality holds if and only if
X
and
Y
are isotopic.
∎

We say that a properly embedded curve or an arc
\alpha

cuts

X
if every
\alpha^{\prime}\in[\alpha]
intersects
X
.
If
\alpha
does not cut
X
then we say that
\alpha

misses

X
.
Suppose that
X
and
Y
are essential, and non-isotopic, subsurfaces of
S
.
We say that
X
is
nested
in
Y
if it is (perhaps after an isotopy) contained in
Y
.
We say that
X
and
Y

overlap
if
\partial_{S}X
cuts
Y
and
\partial_{S}Y
cuts
X
.

2.15.
Curve and arc graphs

We now define the
curve graph

\mathcal{C}(S)
.
Let
\mathcal{C}^{(0)}(S)
be the set of proper isotopy classes of essential, non-peripheral curves in
S
.
We have an edge
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{C}^{(1)}
exactly when
\alpha
and
\beta
are distinct and
i(\alpha,\beta)=0
.

We define the
arc graph

\mathcal{A}(S)
similarly:

\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(S)
is the set of proper isotopy classes of essential arcs in
S
.
Again we have an edge
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
exactly when
\alpha
and
\beta
are distinct and
i(\alpha,\beta)=0
.
Note that
\mathcal{A}(S)
is empty when
S
is closed.

Masur and Schleimer generalise the definition of the arc graph slightly, as follows
[
MS
, Definition 7.1]
.
Suppose that
\Delta\subset\partial S
is a non-empty collection of boundary components.
We define
\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)
to be the sub-graph of
\mathcal{A}(S)
spanned by the arcs having both endpoints in
\Delta
.
Note that
\mathcal{A}(S,\partial S)=\mathcal{A}(S)
.

We next define the
arc and curve graph

\mathcal{AC}(S)
:
the zero-skeleton is exactly
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(S)\cup\mathcal{C}^{(0)}(S)
.
Edges come from having disjoint representatives, as before.
Note that the inclusion of
\mathcal{C}^{(0)}(S)
into
\mathcal{AC}^{(0)}(S)
induces a quasi-isometry of graphs.

The definition of the curve complex must be modified when
\xi(S)\leq 1
: for
S_{1,1}
we use
i(\alpha,\beta)=1
and for
S_{0,4}
we use
i(\alpha,\beta)=2
.
For both of these surfaces the graph of curves is a copy of the
Farey graph
.
When
S
is an annulus we define
\mathcal{C}^{(0)}(S)
to be the set of proper isotopy classes of essential properly emebedded arcs where now isotopies are required to fix boundary points.
Two classes span an edge if they have representatives which are disjoint on their interiors.

All of the various curve, arc, and arc and curve graphs are connected when they are non-empty
[
MM99
, Lemma 2.1]
.
We make each of these into a metric graph by decreeing that all edges have length one.
It is then a theorem of Masur and Minsky that, for any surface
S
with
\xi(S)\geq 1
, the curve complex
\mathcal{C}(S)
is Gromov hyperbolic
[
MM99
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Masur and Schleimer proved that the same holds for
\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)

[
MS
, Theorem 20.3]
.

2.16.
I
–bundles

Suppose that
F
is a connected compact surface, possibly non-orientable, with non-empty boundary.
Let
\rho\colon T\to F
be an
I
–bundle.
We call
F
the
base surface
of the bundle.
We define
\partial_{v}T=\rho^{-1}(\partial F)
to be the
vertical boundary
of
T
.
We define the closure

\partial_{h}T={\overline{\partial T-\partial_{v}T}}

to be the
horizontal boundary
of
T
.
Also, we define the curves

\partial(\partial_{h}T)=\partial(\partial_{v}T)

to be the
corners
of
T
.
Finally, there is an involution
\tau\colon\partial_{h}T\to\partial_{h}T
associated to
\rho
obtained by swapping the ends of interval fibres.

We now define
\rho_{F}\colon T_{F}\to F
to be the
orientation

I
–bundle over
F
.
Here the preimage under
\rho_{F}
of a simple closed curve
\alpha
is an annulus or a Möbius band as
\alpha
is or is not, respectively, orientation preserving in
F
.
When
F
is non-orientable we call
T_{F}

twisted
and so
\partial_{h}T_{F}/\tau\mathrel{\cong}F
is non-orientable.
If
T_{F}
is not twisted then
T_{F}\mathrel{\cong}F\times[-1,1]
is a product.
In this case
\tau|(F\times\{-1\})
is a homeomorphism from
F\times\{-1\}
to
F\times\{1\}
.

2.17.
Compression bodies

References on compression bodies, of the type we are interested in here, include
[
Bo
, Appendix B]
and
[
Oe
, Section 1]
.

Suppose that
S=S_{g,b}
is a surface.
We assume that
S
is neither a disk nor a sphere.
We form
T=S\times I
.
We take
\partial^{+}T=S\times\{1\}
and
\partial^{-}T=S\times\{0\}
to be the
upper
and
lower
boundaries of
T
.
As before,
\partial_{v}T=\partial S\times I
is the
vertical boundary
.
We now attach a collection of three-dimensional two- and three-handles to the lower boundary of
T
to obtain a three-manifold
M
.
We define
\partial^{+}\!M=\partial^{+}T
as well as
\partial_{v}M=\partial_{v}T
.
Finally, we define

\partial^{-}\!M={\overline{\partial M-(\partial^{+}\!M\cup\partial_{v}M)}}

Thus
M
is a
compression body
.
If
\partial^{-}\!M
is not homeomorphic to
\partial^{+}\!M
, then
M
is
non-trivial
.
If
\partial^{-}\!M
has no sphere or disk components, then
M
is
spotless
.
To simplify the notation we take
S=\partial^{+}\!M
.
Note that if
\partial^{-}\!M
is empty then
M
is necessarily a handlebody of positive genus.

We now state the classification of compression bodies.

Theorem 2.18
.

Suppose that
M
and
N
are compression bodies.
Then
(M,\partial_{v}M)
is homeomorphic to
(N,\partial_{v}N)
if and only if
(\partial^{+}M,\partial^{-}\!M)
is homeomorphic to
(\partial^{+}N,\partial^{-}\!N)
. ∎

The proof is similar to that of the classification of surfaces
[
FaMa
, Theorem 1.1]
and of handlebodies
[
He
, Theorem 2.2]
.
The case of
\partial_{v}M=\emptyset
is discussed by Biringer and Vlamis
[
BiV
, Corollary 2.3]
.

2.19.
Disk graphs

Suppose that
(M,S)
is a non-trivial, spotless compression body.
Suppose that
(D,\partial D)\subset(M,S)
is a properly embedded disk.
We call
D

essential
if
\partial D
is essential in
S
.
We now define the
disk graph

\mathcal{D}(M,S)
.
The vertices of
\mathcal{D}(M,S)
are proper isotopy classes of essential disks in
(M,S)
.
A pair of distinct vertices
D
and
E
give an edge
(D,E)\in\mathcal{D}(M,S)
if they have disjoint representatives.

2.20.
Subsurface projection

We give one of the standard definitions of subsurface projection
[

, Definition 4.4]

Definition 2.21

Suppose that
X

S
.
The relation of

\pi_{X}\colon\mathcal{AC}(S)\to\mathcal{C}(X)

Let
\rho_{X}\colon S^{X}\to S

Note that the Gromov compactification of

This gives an identification of the graphs

For any
\alpha\in\mathcal{AC}(S)

We define
\alpha|X

If

Otherwise, for every

∎

Note that if
\alpha

\pi_{X}(\alpha)

Suppose instead that
\alpha

If
X
is an annulus, then the diameter of

If
X
is not an annulus, then the diameter of
\pi_{X}(\alpha)

3.

Let

Witnesses for the arc graph

Definition 3.2

for

We repackage a few results

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
X\subset S
is an essential subsurface, but not an annulus or a pair of pants.
The following are equivalent.

•

is a witness for
\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)
.

•

\Delta
.

•

For all arcs
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)

is non-empty.

•

The projection
\pi_{X}\colon\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)\to\mathcal{C}(X)
is coarsely Lipschitz with a constant of

. ∎

We have a useful corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are distinct witnesses with
\xi^{\prime}(X)=\xi^{\prime}(Y)
.
Then

and
Y
overlap.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
both

and

contain
\Delta
;
thus they intersect.
Since they have the same modified complexity, by Remark

they cannot be nested.
Thus they overlap.
∎

We let

be the mapping class group for the pair
(S,\Delta)
:
the group of mapping classes that preserve
\Delta

We say that a pair of arcs

have the same

(or more simply, the same

) if there is a mapping class

.

Lemma 3.5
.

The quotient
\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)/\operatorname{MCG}(S,\Delta)
has diameter at most two.

Proof.

Suppose that
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)
is an arc.
We break the proof into cases, depending on the number of components of
\partial S
and of
\Delta
.

Suppose that
\Delta
has at least two components.
Then
\alpha
is disjoint from some
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)
meeting two components of
\Delta
.
Since we made no assumptions on
\alpha
, we find that in the quotient, all vertices are at most distance one from
[\gamma]
.
Thus the diameter is at most two.

Suppose that
\Delta
has only one component, but
\partial S
has at least two.
Let
\delta
be some component of
\partial S-\Delta
.
Then
\alpha
is disjoint from some arc

so that
\gamma
separates

from the rest of

Suppose that
\partial S
has a single component, which necessarily equals

\alpha
is disjoint from some arc

∎

In this section we fix the constants

,
\mathsf{K}
, and

We then state and prove Theorem

The constant

into the product

of quasi-trees of curve graphs.

From this, and from Fact

, we deduce the following.

.

Suppose that

has non-empty boundary.
Suppose that

\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)\leq\frac{(4g+b)(4g+b-3)}{2}-2\qed

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
3.10
.
Fix a modified complexity

.

Definition 3.12
.

Suppose that
\beta\in\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)
is an arc.
Suppose that

is a witness, and
\beta

.

Note that

Recall, by Fact

embeds into

We now define a relation

\phi_{c}(\alpha)=\{\pi_{Y}(\beta)\mathbin{\mid}\mbox{$d_{\mathcal{A}}(\alpha,\beta)\leq 2$ and $Y\in\mathcal{F}_{c}$ carries $\beta$}\}

.

Proof.

Equivariance follows from the definition.

The set

is non-empty by Lemma
3.5
.
Suppose that
Y
,

, and

lie in
\mathcal{F}_{c}

\beta
and

are carried by

and

, respectively, and are distance at most two from

.

.
We deduce that

We now recall our choices (made above) of
\mathsf{k}
,
\mathsf{K}
, and
\mathsf{L}
.
In particular we have

.
Thus, by Fact

Furthermore, by

, Lemma 2.3]

Lemma 3.14
.

Suppose that

are arcs and

|d_{X}(\alpha,\gamma)-d_{X}(\phi_{c}(\alpha),\phi_{c}(\gamma))|\leq 12

Suppose that

\beta

Now, if

We now define
\phi\colon\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)\to\mathcal{P}(S,\Delta)

\phi(\alpha)=(\phi_{c}(\alpha))_{c}

All that remains is to prove that

\alpha
and
\gamma

\mathcal{A}(S,\Delta)

We now bound

Since

[

Suppose that
S
and
\Delta

Take

A,B\in\mathcal{F}_{c}
.
We now define
\pi_{B}(A)
.
(Note that we are overloading the notation
\pi_{B}
.
When the argument is a collection of curves we use Definition
2.21
.
When the argument is a witness we use Definition
4.10
.)
There are two cases.

(1)

Suppose that
B
is not a base surface.
Then we define
\pi_{B}(A)=\pi_{B}(\partial_{S}A)
.

(2)

Suppose that
B
is a base surface.
Suppose that
\rho_{B}\colon T_{B}\to B
is the
I
–bundle associated to
B
.
Then we isotope
\partial_{S}A
to meet
\partial_{h}T_{B}
minimally and we define
\pi_{B}(A)=\pi_{B}(\rho_{B}(T_{B}\cap\partial_{S}A))
.
∎

In the above definition, we are considering
\rho_{B}(T_{B}\cap\partial_{S}A)
as a set of arcs and curves in
B
.
We surger them one at a time to obtain a set of curves in
B
.
Also, in both parts of the definition, Corollary
4.6
implies that
\pi_{B}(A)
is non-empty.

Suppose that
A,B,C\in\mathcal{F}_{c}
.
Suppose further that
B
is distinct from both
A
and
C
.
Then we define

d_{B}(A,C)=d_{\mathcal{C}(B)}(\pi_{B}(A),\pi_{B}(C))

We will now abuse notation:
the reduced family
\mathcal{F}_{c}
contains surfaces
A
which index metric spaces, namely the curve graphs
\mathcal{C}(A)
, instead of being metric spaces themselves.

We now verify the three BBF axioms, as stated in Section
2.4
.
Recall that
(M,S)
is a spotless compression body, together with its upper boundary.
Also,
c\in\mathbb{Z}
is an integer.
Here is the proof of Axiom
2.5
.

Lemma 4.11
.

There is a constant
k>0
so that for every
(M,S)
, for every
c
, and for every
A,B\in\mathcal{F}_{c}
, we have that
\operatorname{diam}_{B}(\pi_{B}(A))\leq k
.

Proof.

Suppose that
B
is not a base surface.
Note that
\partial_{S}A
is a disjoint collection of curves.
By Corollary
4.6
, we have
\partial_{S}A
cuts
B
.
By
[
MM00
, Lemma 2.3]
the diameter of
\pi_{B}(A)
in
\mathcal{C}(B)
is at most two.

Suppose that
B
is a base surface.
Let
D
be either a compressing disk for
A
or a vertical disk for
T_{A}
, as provided by Theorem
4.3
or Remark
4.4
, respectively.
If
D
is a compressing disk then
\partial D
is disjoint from
\partial_{S}A
.
If
D
is a vertical disk then
\partial D
meets
\partial_{S}A=\partial_{S}\partial_{h}T_{A}
in exactly four points.
We now isotope
\partial D
to have minimal intersection with
\partial_{S}B
.
Applying
[
MS
, Lemma 12.20]
we deduce that
\pi_{B}(\rho_{B}(T_{B}\cap\partial D))
has bounded diameter.
Thus by the triangle inequality
\pi_{B}(\rho_{B}(T_{B}\cap\partial_{S}A))
also has bounded diameter, and we are done.
∎

We adopt the notation
d_{Y}(A,C)=d_{Y}(\partial_{S}A,\partial_{S}C)
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
k
be the constant of Lemma
4.11
.
Fix
(M,S)
and
c
.
Suppose that
A,B,C\in\mathcal{F}_{c}
where
B
is a base surface replacing the twinned surfaces
Y,Y^{\prime}
.
Then

d_{Y}(A,C)\leq d_{B}(A,C)\leq d_{Y}(A,C)+2k

and the same holds for
Y^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
4.9
, we have that
d_{Y}(A,C)
is defined.
The first inequality follows from the definitions.
The second inequality follows from two applications of Lemma
4.11
and the triangle inequality.
∎

Here is the proof of Axiom
2.6
.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
k
be the constant of Lemma
4.11
.
Fix
(M,S)
and
c
.
For every
A,B,C\in\mathcal{F}_{c}
, we have that at most one of the following is greater than
12+2k
:

d_{A}(B,C),\quad d_{B}(A,C),\quad d_{C}(A,B)

Proof.

It suffices to assume that
d_{B}(A,C)>12+2k
and bound
d_{A}(B,C)
from above.

Suppose that neither
B
nor
A
is a base surface.
Then we may apply the usual Behrstock inequality and deduce that
d_{A}(B,C)<10
; see
[
Ma
, Lemma 2.5]
.

Suppose instead
B
is not a base surface, but
A
is.
Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be the twins over
A
.
By Corollary
4.6
, both
X
and
X^{\prime}
overlap
B
.
Applying
[
MM00
, Lemma 2.3]
we have
d_{B}(X,C)>10+2k
.
The usual Behrstock inequality gives
d_{X}(B,C)<10
.
Applying Lemma
4.12
we deduce that
d_{A}(B,C)<10+2k
.

Suppose instead that
B
is a base surface but
A
is not.
Let
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
be the twins over
B
.
By Lemma
4.12
we have that
d_{Y}(A,C)\geq 12
and also
d_{Y^{\prime}}(A,C)\geq 12
.
By Lemma
4.9
both
\partial_{S}B
and
\partial_{S}C
cut
A
.
Suppose that
\partial_{S}Y
cuts
A
.
The usual Behrstock inequality gives
d_{A}(Y,C)<10
.
We deduce that
d_{A}(B,C)<12
, as desired.

Finally suppose that
B
and
A
are base surfaces.
Let
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
, and
X
and
X^{\prime}
, be the twins over
B
and
A
, respectively.
By Corollary
4.6
, we may assume that
X
and
Y
overlap.
Thus
d_{Y}(X,C)>10
, so
d_{X}(Y,C)<10
, and we are done as above.
∎

Here is the proof of Axiom
2.7
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
k
be the constant of Lemma
4.11
. Fix
(M,S)
and
c
. For every
A,C\in\mathcal{F}_{c}
, the following set is finite:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{B\in\mathcal{F}_{c}\mathbin{\mid}\mbox{$A\neq B$, $B\neq C$, and $d_{B}(A,C)>7+2k$}}}\right\}

Proof.

If
A
and
C
are not base surfaces, then this follows from
[
BBF
, Lemma 5.3]
and Lemma
4.12
.
If
A
is a base surface but
C
is not then suppose that
X
and
X^{\prime}
are the twins over

.
We may repeat the previous argument for the pairs
(X,C)
as well as

, paying at most an additional two
[
MM00
, Lemma 2.3]
in each case.
When both
A
and

are base surfaces there are four such pairs and the cost is at most an additional four in each case.
∎

\operatorname{\mathsf{dim}_{\mathsf{asym}}}\mathcal{D}(M,S)\leq\frac{(4g+b)(4g+b-3)}{2}-2\qed

The proof of Theorem

Third and lastly, we replace the distance estimate Theorem
3.15
with the following.

Theorem 4.22
.

Suppose that
M
and
S
are as above.
There is a constant
\mathsf{K}
so that for any
\mathsf{K}^{\prime}\geq\mathsf{K}
there is a constant
\mathsf{C}
with the following property.
For any disks
D
and

d_{\mathcal{D}}(D,E)=_{\mathsf{C}}\sum[d_{X}(D,E)]_{\mathsf{K}^{\prime}}

\mathcal{D}(M,S)

Proof.

[

bounds

\mathcal{D}(M,S)

B

d_{B}(D,E),\quad d_{Y}(D,E),\quad\mbox{and}\quad d_{Y^{\prime}}(D,E)
