1.
Introduction

Lévy processes form a class of stochastic processes that start from zero, that have càdlàg sample-paths, and that have stationary independent increments; see e.g. the general surveys
[
4
,
9
]
. They play a prominent role across various subdisciplines of applied probability, such as ruin theory
[
7
,
43
]
and queueing theory
[
18
]
, but they are also extensively used in mathematical finance
[
49
]
.

The current work aims to analyze the area under the graph of the Lévy process until a stopping time. More specifically, in the setting considered we give the process an initial push
V>0
at time
0
, and record the Lévy process (assumed to have a negative drift) until the first time
\tau
that the level
0
is hit. The main object of study is the joint distribution of the area under the graph of the Lévy process in the interval between
0
and
\tau
, the number of jumps of the Lévy process in the same interval, and the hitting time
\tau
itself. The objective is to characterize this joint distribution through a constructive scheme via which a family of joint moments can be recursively identified.

Our model is formally introduced as follows. We let
J(\cdot)
denote a Lévy process which is a superposition of a subordinator (i.e., a nondecreasing Lévy process) and a drifted Brownian motion (BM). For this specific subclass of spectrally positive Lévy processes it is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
43
, Theorem 2.1, Lemma 2.14]
) that the Laplace exponent of
J(\cdot)
necessarily takes the form

(1)

\varphi(\alpha)\equiv\varphi_{c,\sigma,\nu}(\alpha)\equiv-\log\mathbb{E}e^{-\alpha J(1)}=c\alpha-\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha^{2}}{2}-\int_{(0,\infty)}\left(e^{-\alpha y}-1\right)\nu({\rm d}y)\ \ ,\ \ \alpha\geqslant 0\,,

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma\geqslant 0
and
\nu
is a Borel measure on
(0,\infty)

such that

(2)

\int_{(0,\infty)}\left(1\wedge y\right)\nu({\rm d}y)<\infty\,.

This means that
J(\cdot)
could for instance be a compound Poisson process (CPP) with nonnegative jumps (by taking
\sigma=0
and
\nu\{(0,\infty)\}<\infty
) or BM (by taking
\nu\{(0,\infty)\}=0
). Importantly, however, our class of Lévy processes also includes processes with
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. infinitely many jumps over finite time intervals (i.e.,
\nu\{(0,\infty)\}=\infty
), such as the Gamma process and the inverse Gaussian process. For an account of some other examples, see e.g.
[
18
, Section 2.2]
and
[
43
, Section 1.2]
. In the sequel we denote by

(3)

\displaystyle\varrho\equiv\mathbb{E}J(1)=\varphi^{\prime}(0+)=c+\int_{(0,\infty)}y\,\nu\left({\rm d}y\right)=c+\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu\left\{(y,\infty)\right\}{\rm d}y\,

the process’ asymptotic drift, which we throughout assume to be negative.
In addition, we let
V
denote the initial push given to the process
J(\cdot)
, defined as a positive random variable that is independent from
J(\cdot)
. The resulting process is, for
t\geqslant 0
,

(4)

\displaystyle W(t)\equiv W_{V}(t)\equiv V+J(t),

which we consider between
0
and the stopping time

(5)

\tau\equiv\tau_{V}\equiv\inf\left\{t\geqslant 0:W_{V}(t)=0\right\}\,.

where we note that under
\varrho<0
and
\mathbb{E}V<\infty
we have
\mathbb{E}\tau<\infty
(see, e.g.,
[
42
, Section 2]
).
We proceed by stating the main objectives of this paper.
With Borel function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we define the following four functionals associated to the process
W(\cdot)
:

(6)

\displaystyle A_{f}\equiv A_{f}(V)\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}f\circ W(t)\,{\rm d}t\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}f(W(t))\,{\rm d}t\ \ ,

\displaystyle D_{f}\equiv D_{f}(V)\equiv\sum_{t\in[0,\tau]:W(t-)<W(t)}{f}\circ W(t-)\equiv\sum_{t\in[0,\tau]:W(t-)<W(t)}{f}(W(t-))\ .

Special cases include: (1) if
f=1
, then
A_{f}
equals the stopping time
\tau
, (2) if
f
is the identity
{\rm Id}
, then
A_{f}
equals the area under the graph of
J(\cdot)
until
\tau
, (3) if
{f}=1
, then
D_{{f}}
counts the number of jumps of
J(\cdot)
until
\tau
, (4) if
{f}={\rm Id}
, then
D_{{f}}
represents the aggregate of the values of the left-limit of
W(\cdot)
at the jump epochs happening until
\tau
.

Our concrete research question concerns joint moments of the functionals
A_{f_{i}}
and
D_{g_{i}}
, for given Borel functions
{f_{1}},{f_{2}},{g_{1}},{g_{2}}
. More specifically, we wish to derive explicit expressions of the high-order moments of the random vector

(7)

\left(A_{f_{1}},A_{f_{2}},D_{g_{1}},D_{g_{2}}\right)

in terms of (i) the moments of
V
, (ii) the moments corresponding to the measure
\nu(\cdot)
, and (iii) the parameters
c
and
\sigma
. Our findings demonstrate how this can be done for the case that
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
,
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are all of a polynomial form, the main result being a recursive scheme via which the joint expectation

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{A_{f_{1}}^{k_{1}}A_{f_{2}}^{k_{2}}D_{g_{1}}^{\ell_{1}}D_{g_{2}}^{\ell_{2}}\right\}

can be evaluated for any combination of nonnegative integers
(k_{1},k_{2},\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
and polynomials
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
,
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. For instance when picking
f_{1}=1
and
f_{2}={\rm Id}
, we obtain joint moments pertaining to the stopping time
\tau
and the area under the graph of
J(\cdot)
until
\tau.

An important special case, particularly from a queueing-theory perspective, is the one in which the process
J(\cdot)
is of CPP type and
V
has the same distribution as the jumps of
J(\cdot)
. We stress, though, that our results by no means do not require this choice; see also Remark
1
.

1.1.
Existing literature

Thus far, only in very specific cases explicit expressions (or computation schemes) have been developed for the high-order moments of (
7
). We proceed by providing a succinct overview, where we subsequently discuss the settings with spectrally positive Lévy input, CPP input, and BM input.

Spectrally positive Lévy input

In the case when
J(\cdot)
is a Lévy process with nonnegative jumps (i.e., a spectrally positive Lévy process), it is well-known
[
43
, Section 8.1]
that the Laplace-Stieltjes transform (LST) of
\tau
is given by

(9)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\beta\tau}={\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\varphi^{-1}(\beta)\,V},

with
\varphi^{-1}(\cdot)
the right inverse of the Laplace exponent
\varphi(\cdot)
. Thus, conceptually, the high order moments of the hitting time
\tau
(i.e.,
A_{f}
with
f\equiv 1
) can be computed via repeated differentiation of this LST. The paper
[
2
]
focuses on the characterization of the LST for a more general class of functionals. That is, consider some nonnegative real-valued measurable function
f(\cdot)
and assume that
V
equals to a constant
x\in(0,\infty)
. Then,
[
2
, Theorem 2.3]
states that

(10)

M_{f}(\lambda)\equiv M_{f}(\lambda;x)\equiv\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{-\lambda\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}}f\left[x+J(t)\right]{\rm d}t\right\}\ \ ,\ \ \lambda>0

is a solution of certain functional equation (equipped with boundary conditions) which is phrased in terms of the generator of
J(\cdot)
. This characterization of
M_{f}(\lambda)
was shown to be helpful in specific examples, e.g.:

(1)

When
\sigma>0
and there are no jumps,
M_{\text{Id}}(\cdot)
is a solution of a Schrödinger equation which can be expressed in terms of Airy functions.

(2)

When
\sigma=0
and the jump process is a Poisson process with rate
\theta\in(0,1)
(i.e., all jump sizes are equal to one), it was shown that

M_{\text{Id}}(\lambda)=\frac{\theta^{[x]+1}}{\prod_{j=0}^{[x]}[\theta+\lambda x-j]}\ \ ,\ \ \forall\lambda>0

where
[x]
is the integer part of
x
.

We note that, as stated in
[
2
, Example 5]
, the application of this approach to the more general case in which there are Brownian motions and jumps together will not yield any explicit solution even when considering the relatively simple functions
f\equiv 1
or
f\equiv\text{Id}
.

Compound Poisson input

Assume that
J(\cdot)
is a CPP with rate
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, nonnegative jumps (that are distributed as the initial push
V
) minus a unit drift. In addition, denote
N\equiv D_{g}
with
g\equiv 1
, i.e.,
N
is equal to the number of jumps that
J(\cdot)
has during the time interval
(0,\tau)
. For the case that
f\equiv 1
as well,
[
17
, Section II.4.4]
provides the joint LST of
N+1
and
\tau
in terms of a fixed point relation. In a similar fashion, we can derive the following fixed point relation for the joint LST of
N
and
\tau
: for
\alpha,\beta>0
,

(11)

\gamma(\alpha,\beta)\equiv\mathbb{E}\,e^{-\alpha N-\beta\tau}=\,\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\left\{\beta+\lambda\left[1-e^{-\alpha}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma(\alpha,\beta)\right]\right\}\,V}.

Suppose that we wish to compute
\mathbb{E}\tau^{k}N^{\ell}
.
To this end, apply the Faá di Bruno’s formula in combination with the general Leibniz rule in order to differentiate both sides of (
11
),
k
times with respect to
\beta
,
\ell
times with respect to
\alpha
, and then take
(\alpha,\beta)\downarrow(0,0)
. For every
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, this yields an equation from which one can isolate
\mathbb{E}\tau^{k}N^{\ell}
and get an expression in terms of
\lambda
, the first
\ell+k
moments of
G(\cdot)
and
\mathbb{E}N^{i}\tau^{j}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots\ell\}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. As a result, we can recursively determine all the joint moments of
N
and
\tau
.

Consider the case where
V
has the distribution
G_{V}(\cdot)
which is not the same as the jump distribution of
J(\cdot)
. In this case, denote the time until
W_{V}(\cdot)
hits the origin by
\tau_{V}
and the number of jumps it has until
\tau_{V}
by
N_{V}
. In this case, we know that given
V
, the number of records in the (decreasing) ladder process until
\tau
has a Poisson distribution with rate
\lambda V
. Thus, by the strong Markov property of
J(\cdot)
we shall apply the LST of a compound Poisson distribution in order to get, for
\alpha,\beta>0
,

(12)

\displaystyle\gamma(\alpha,\beta;G_{V})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbb{E}e^{-\alpha N_{V}+\beta\tau_{V}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{-V\left[\beta+\lambda\left(1-\mathbb{E}e^{-[\alpha(N+1)+\beta\tau]}\right)\right]\right\}=e^{-V\left\{\beta+\lambda\left[1-e^{-\alpha}\gamma(\alpha,\beta)\right]\right\}}\ \ .

This means that the high-order moments of the random vector
(N_{V},\tau_{V})
will all be linear combinations of the moments of
V
with coefficients determined by the high-order moments of
(N,\tau)
.

Complications arise, however, when considering functionals of
W(\cdot)
for which we lack a fixed-point relation for the LST, entailing that one cannot apply the procedure described above to recursively compute the corresponding moments. For example, in the case that
f(\cdot)={\rm Id}
, in which
A_{f}
represents the area beneath the sample path of
W(\cdot)
until
\tau
, no fixed-point relation for the relevant LST is known.
Following other techniques, a few partial results were found though. More specifically, with
\mu_{i}
denoting the
i
-th moment of the jump distribution of
J(\cdot)
, and
f(\cdot)={\rm Id}
,

(1)

Iglehart
[
28
]
showed that, applying the time normalization
c=1
,

(13)

\mathbb{E}A_{f}=\frac{\mu_{2}}{2(1-\rho)^{2}}\,,

where
\rho\equiv 1+\mathbb{E}J(1)=\lambda\mathbb{E}V<1
.
In addition, he stated the challenge of finding the other moments of
A_{f}
as an open problem.

(2)

Cohen
[
16
]
applied a level-crossing technique to derive that

(14)

\mathbb{E}A_{f}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{4}}{4(1-\rho)^{3}}+\frac{4\lambda\mu_{2}\mu_{3}}{3(1-\rho)^{4}}+\frac{5\lambda^{2}\mu_{2}^{3}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}4\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1-\rho)^{5}}\,.

Concerning moments of order greater than two, to our knowledge, the only relevant existing references are:

(1)

Borovkov, Boxma and Palmowski
[
12
]
, who showed that when the jump distribution of
J(\cdot)
is exponential, the LST of
A_{f}
(with
f(\cdot)
still the identity
{\rm Id}
) can be written in terms of Whittaker functions.

(2)

As mentioned above, Abundo
[
2
]
assumed that the jumps seize are all equal to one, and provided an explicit formula of the LST of
A_{f}
(with
f(\cdot)
still the identity
{\rm Id}
).

Thus, even when considering the simpler setup with
J(\cdot)
being a CPP, the computation of
\mathbb{E}A_{f}^{k}
for
k=3,4,\ldots
remained an open problem.

Another related work concerning the instance that
J(\cdot)
is a CPP with nonnegative jumps, is by Glynn
[
23
]
, relating
\mathbb{E}D_{g}(x)
to an associated Poisson’s equation. A consequence of the findings in
[
23
]
is that when
g(\cdot)
is a polynomial, then
\mathbb{E}D_{g}(x)
is a polynomial in
x
. To the best of our knowledge, there is no additional literature regarding
\mathbb{E}D_{g}^{\ell}(x)
when
\ell\geqslant 2
.

Brownian motion input

We proceed by considering the case where
V\equiv x
for some
x\in(0,\infty)
and
J(t)\equiv ct+B(t)
for every
t\geq 0
such that
B(\cdot)
is a standard BM and
c<0
. Abundo and Del Vescovo
[
3
]
derived a computational scheme to derive the precise expression of the joint moments

(15)

I_{k,\ell}(x)\equiv\mathbb{E}\tau_{x}^{k}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}}\left[x-J(t)\right]{\rm d}t\right)^{\ell}\ \ ,\ \ k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\,.

They showed how this scheme produced the expressions of

for small values of
k
and
\ell
. Furthermore, they proved
[
3
, Proposition 2.5]
that
I_{k,\ell}(x)
is a polynomial in
x
with a degree equal to

. Note that when
V
is a positive random variable independent of
B(\cdot)
and has a cdf

,
\int_{0}^{\infty}I_{k,\ell}(x){\rm d}G(x)
is a linear combination of the first
k+2\ell
moments of
V
.

More results on the joint distribution of the first passage time
\tau_{x}
and the so-called
first passage area

\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}}\left[x-J(t)\right]{\rm d}t

[
36
,

,

and the references therein).

1.2.
Motivations

The computation of high-order joint moments of the type (
8
) has applications across various domains. In this subsection we subsequently point out how our results are relevant in the context of Lévy fluctuation theory, in the context of insurance/risk models, and in a queueing setting.

Lévy processes with secondary jump input

Consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
equipped with a filtration
\mathbb{F}\equiv(\mathcal{F}_{t})
which is right-continuous increasing sequence of
{\mathbb{P}}
-complete sub-
\sigma
-fields of
\mathcal{F}
. In addition, assume that
X(\cdot)\equiv\{X(t):t\geqslant 0\}
is the right-continuous version of a Lévy process with respect to
\mathbb{F}
, i.e., it is adapted to
\mathbb{F}

is distributed as
X(t)-X(s)
and

is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}

(\tau_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a strictly increasing sequence of stopping times in
\mathbb{F}
such that
\tau_{0}\equiv 0
, and define a counting process

(16)

N(t)\equiv\sup\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\,\tau_{n}\leqslant t\right\}\,,

for
t\geqslant 0
.
If
(U_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of random variables adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}})_{n=0}^{\infty}
, then a general description of a
Lévy processes with secondary jump input
is given via

(17)

for
t\geqslant 0
.
For more details regarding this class of processes and their applications in the applied probability literature, see e.g. the seminal papers
[
41
,
42
]
and some consecutive works such as
[

Now, consider a regenerative process
\left\{Y(t):t\geqslant 0\right\}
with independent cycles that are distributed as
\left\{W_{V}(t):0\leqslant t<\tau\right\}
. The thus constructed process is a special case of a Lévy processes with secondary jump input, as introduced in the preceding paragraph. Since the distribution of
\tau
is non-arithmetic, we can use the classical theory regarding regenerative processes; see, e.g.,
[
6
, Chapter VI]
for a detailed account. It specifically implies that as
\varrho<0
, there exists a random variable
Y^{*}
such that

as

and

(18)

\mathbb{E}f\left(Y^{*}\right)=\frac{\mathbb{E}A_{f}}{\mathbb{E}\tau}\,,

for any nonnegative Borel function
f(\cdot)
.
A popular approach to quantify the steady-state mean
\mathbb{E}f\left(Y^{*}\right)
is via stochastic simulation, where a frequently used class of algorithms is of the
regenerative
type; see, e.g.,
[
8
, Chapter IV, Section 4]
. In the study of the probabilistic properties of the resulting estimators, one greatly benefits from the availability of high-order moments of
\left(A_{f},\tau\right)

(e.g. when constructing confidence intervals, or upper bounds on the tail distribution).

2.1.
General expressions.

In our analysis we denote, for each
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, with

the cumulative distribution function of the initial push
V
,

(27)

\mu_{i}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{i}\,{\rm d}G_{V}(y)\,.

We in addition observe that
\varrho
, as defined in (
3
), is negative and finite. As follows from, e.g.,
[
42
, Section 2]
, we thus have that the expected cycle length, given by

\mathbb{E}\tau=-\frac{\mu_{1}}{\varrho},

is finite. The following theorem provides a constructive method to compute the first moment
\mathbb{E}A_{f}
for a given Borel function

Theorem 1
.

Assume that

and
\zeta
are nonnegative random variables such that

(1)

V
,
U
and
\zeta
are independent.

(2)

U
is distributed uniformly on
[0,1]

(3)

The LST of
\zeta

(29)

\psi(\alpha)\equiv\mathbb{E}e^{-\alpha\zeta}=\frac{\alpha\varphi^{\prime}(0+)}{\varphi(\alpha)}\ \ ,\ \ \alpha>0\,.

Then, for any Borel function

,

(30)

\mathbb{E}A_{f}=-\frac{1}{\varrho}\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,\mathbb{E}f\left(\zeta+xU\right){\rm d}G_{V}(x)\,.

Proof.

First it is observed that it suffices to provide the proof under the assumption that
V=x
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for a given

. Then, the claim follows via an integration with respect to
{\rm d}G_{V}(\cdot)

Since
\mathbb{E}\tau=-{x}/{\varrho}<\infty
, there is a probability measure on
\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\right)
which is defined by, for any given

(31)

\mathbb{P}^{*}\{E\}\equiv\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\tau}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau}\textbf{1}_{\left\{W(t)\in E\right\}}{\rm d}t\Big]\ \ .

In addition, we note that, with
\mathbb{E}^{*}
denoting expectation with respect to

(32)

\mathbb{E}A_{f}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau}f\circ W(t)\,{\rm d}t\Big]=-\frac{x\,\mathbb{E}^{*}f}{\varrho}\,.

Now, let
\xi
be a random variable such that

for every

. Then
[

implies that, for any

\mathbb{E}e^{-\alpha\xi}=\frac{\alpha\varphi^{\prime}(0+)}{\varphi(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1-e^{-\alpha x}}{\alpha x}\,.

Hence,

.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 3

Consider a regenerative process

The second main result of this section concerns the relation between the first moment of

and the first moment of

Theorem 2
.

Let

be a nonnegative Borel function on

. Then,

(35)

\mathbb{E}D_{f}=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}A_{f}

with the convention that
0\cdot\infty=0
.

Remark that in the case of drifted Brownian motion, as discussed in the introduction, we have that, in (
35

is to be taken equal to

.

The proof of Theorem
2
(as well as proof of other claims in this section) is based on the Lévy-Itô decomposition
[
43
, Theorem 2.1]
which states that
J(\cdot)
can be expressed as the superposition of four independent processes:

(1)

A deterministic drift process
t\mapsto ct
with
c<0
.

(2)

B_{\sigma}(\cdot)\equiv\sigma B(\cdot)
which is a driftless Brownian motion with a volatility
\sigma\geqslant 0
.

(3)

\Psi_{0}(\cdot)
which is a CPP with an arrival rate
\lambda_{0}\equiv\nu\left\{[1,\infty)\right\}
and jump distribution
{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}\,{\nu({\rm d}y)}
.

(4)

A sum of a sequence of independent processes
\Psi_{1}(\cdot),\Psi_{2}(\cdot),\ldots
such that for each
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\Psi_{i}(\cdot)
is a CPP which has an arrival rate
\lambda_{i}\equiv\nu\left\{[2^{-(i+1)},2^{-i})\right\}
and jump distribution
{\lambda_{i}}^{-1}\,{\nu({\rm d}y)}
.

Note that in the above-mentioned description of the decomposition, we apply the convention that whenever
\lambda_{i}=0
, then
\Psi_{i}(\cdot)\equiv 0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
In addition,
\lambda\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}
.

The proof of Theorem
2
is based on the main result of
[
53
]
, a classical finding in the applied probability literature that has become known as the
Poisson arrivals see times averages
(
pasta
) property. Morally, the
pasta
result provides us with conditions under which a system’s stationary distribution at Poisson epochs coincides with the system’s long-run distribution. For convenience and completeness, we are now providing its precise statement.

Theorem 3

(
pasta)
.

Consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
with the following objects:

\circ

\mathbb{F}\equiv(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is an increasing right-continuous sequence of
{\mathbb{P}}
-complete sub-
\sigma
-fields of
\mathcal{F}
.

\circ

X(\cdot)\equiv\left\{X(t):t\geqslant 0\right\}
is a càdlàg stochastic process.

\circ

N(\cdot)\equiv\{N(t):t\geqslant 0\}
is a simple càdlàg counting process, which is adapted to
\mathbb{F}
and which has stationary increments.

\circ

For any
t,h\geqslant 0
, the increment
N(t+h)-N(t)
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

For every
n\geqslant 0
denote

(36)

T_{n}\equiv\inf\left\{t\in{\mathbb{N}}:N(t)\geqslant n\right\}.

Then, the (continuous-time) process
\{X(t):t\geqslant 0\}
and the (discrete-time) process
\{X(T_{n}-):n=0,1,2,\ldots\}
have the same ergodic distribution and hence for any nonnegative Borel function
f(\cdot)
, the limits

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f\circ X(s)\,{\rm d}s\ \ ,\ \ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\circ X\left(T_{n}-\right)\ \ ,\ \ \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{N(t)}\int_{0}^{t}f\circ X(s-)\,{\rm d}N(s)\,,

exist (possibly infinite) and they are equal
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

In what follows, we apply this theorem with
X(\cdot)
corresponding to a compound Poisson process with nonnegative jumps, and
N(\cdot)
corresponding to the underlying Poisson process that defines the jumps epochs in
X(\cdot)
. Now we are ready to provide the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof.

If
\lambda=0
, the result is trivial, so that it is left to provide a proof for the case
\lambda>0
(possibly infinite). For any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, let
N_{i}(\cdot)
be the arrival process corresponding to the jumps in
\Psi_{i}(\cdot)
, i.e., whenever
\lambda_{i}>0
,
N_{i}(\cdot)
is a Poisson process with rate
\lambda_{i}
and otherwise it equals zero
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. This entails that the superposition

(37)

N(t)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}N_{i}(t)=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:\lambda_{i}>0}N_{i}(t)\ \ ,\ \ t\geqslant 0

coincides with the arrival process of the jumps in
J(\cdot)
.

Now, let
\{Y(t):t\geqslant 0\}
be a regenerative process with independent cycles that are distributed as
\{W(t):0\leqslant t<\tau\}
such that
Y(t)=W(t)
for every
0\leqslant t<\tau
and note that
Y(\cdot)
is càdlàg.
Then, as
\tau
is positive and has a finite mean, the ergodic theorem for regenerative processes implies that

(38)

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f\circ Y(s)\,{\rm d}s\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}A_{f}}{\mathbb{E}\tau}\ \ ,\ \ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

As a result, due to the
pasta
property
[
53
]
, for any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that
\lambda_{i}>0
,

(39)

\frac{1}{N_{i}(t)}\int_{0}^{t}f\circ Y(s-)\,{\rm d}N_{i}(s)\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}A_{f}}{\mathbb{E}\tau}\ \ ,\ \ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

In addition, for each
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that
\lambda_{i}>0
, it is known that
N_{i}(t)/(\lambda_{i}t)\to 1
as
t\to\infty
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. (by the law of large numbers for renewal processes), and hence for that case the renewal reward theorem leads to

\displaystyle\frac{t}{N_{i}(t)}\cdot\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f\circ Y(s-)\,{\rm d}N_{i}(s)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}\,\mathbb{E}\tau}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau}f\circ Y(s-)\,{\rm d}N_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}\,\mathbb{E}\tau}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau}f\circ W(s-)\,{\rm d}N_{i}(s)\ \ ,\ \ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Thus, because this limit is unique, we conclude that, for any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,

(40)

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau}f\circ W(t-)\,{\rm d}N_{i}(t)=\lambda_{i}\,\mathbb{E}A_{f}\,.

Then, a summation of both sides of (
40
) over all indices
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lambda_{i}>0
yields

(41)

\mathbb{E}D_{f}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau}f\circ W(t-)\,{\rm d}N_{i}(t)=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}A_{f}\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Polynomial functionals

The first moment
{\mathbb{E}}A_{f}
, as identified in Theorem

is a polynomial. The expression for

with
\mathcal{P}_{k}(y)\equiv y^{k}
for

and

, is presented in
the next corollary. Recall the objects
\varphi(\cdot)
and

that were defined in respectively (

) and (

). For simplicity of notation, for any

, we define

(42)

\psi_{k}\equiv\psi^{(k)}(0+)\equiv\left.\frac{{\rm d}^{k}\psi(\alpha)}{{\rm d}\alpha^{k}}\right|_{\alpha\downarrow 0},\>\>\>\>\>\varphi_{k}\equiv\varphi^{(k)}(0+)\equiv\left.\frac{{\rm d}^{k}\varphi(\alpha)}{{\rm d}\alpha^{k}}\right|_{\alpha\downarrow 0}.

Corollary 1
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

(43)

\mathbb{E}A_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}=-\frac{1}{\varrho}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\frac{\mu_{k-i+1}}{k-i+1}(-1)^{i}\psi_{i}\,.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately by inserting

into the expression given in Theorem
1

∎

From a practical standpoint it is clear that, in order to apply Corollary
1
, it is necessary to know how to compute the first
k
right-derivatives of
\psi(\cdot)
at zero. Our model primitives, however, relate to right-derivatives of

at zero; recall our ambition to express the moments of interest in terms of the moments of

, the moments associated to

and the parameters

. This means that we require a procedure to express the right-derivatives of

in terms of those of

.
One special case in which an explicit solution can be found is that of

corresponding to a drifted Brownian motion. Then,

is the LST of an exponential random variable with rate

(see, e.g.,

). This means that the coefficients

are given by

\mathbb{E}A_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}=-\frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{k!\,\mu_{k-i+1}}{(k-i)!\,(k-i+1)}{\mathfrak{r}}^{-i}\,.

When

is a CPP minus a drift with rate one, Takacs’ recurrence relation
[

could be applied in order to recursively compute the coefficients

, in terms of the right-derivatives of

at

. The following result is a generalization of Takacs’ recursion. To streamline the flow of the exposition, we first provide in Subsection

Theorem 4
.

Let
\zeta
be defined as in Theorem

. For any

, denote

\eta_{i}\equiv\int_{(0,\infty)}y^{i}\nu\left({\rm d}y\right).

If

, for

, then

(-1)^{k}\mathbb{E}\zeta^{k}=\psi_{k}=-\frac{1}{(k+1)\varphi_{1}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k+1}{i}\psi_{i}\varphi_{k+1-i}\,.

Otherwise,
\mathbb{E}\zeta^{k}=(-1)^{k}\psi_{k}=\infty
.

Remark 4
.

Notice that (

) can be equivalently written as

(47)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\zeta^{k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\psi_{k}=-\frac{1}{(k+1)\varphi_{1}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k+1}{i}(-1)^{i}\psi_{i}(-1)^{k-i}\varphi_{k+1-i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varphi_{1}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k}{i}\frac{(-1)^{k-i}\varphi_{k+1-i}}{k+1-i}\mathbb{E}\zeta^{i}\,

from which it directly follows that Theorem

is a generalization of

Recall that

(48)

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}=0\,,\>\>\>\>\>\varphi_{1}=\varrho=c+\eta_{1}\,,\>\>\>\>\>\varphi_{2}=-\left(\sigma^{2}+\eta_{2}\right)\,,\>\>\>\>\>\varphi_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}\eta_{i}\ \ ,\ \ i\in\{3,4,\ldots\}\,.

In particular, for any

. As a result, the next corollary follows.

.

For any

\mathbb{E}\zeta^{k}=(-1)^{k}\psi_{k}<\infty

2.2.1.
Takacs’ recursion: informal derivation

Before providing the formal proof of Theorem
4
, we first present a more intuitive treatment. The starting point is the following evident identity: by
(
29
)
, for any
\alpha>0
,

(49)

\psi(\alpha)\,\varphi(\alpha)=\alpha\varphi_{1}\,.

Differentiating both sides of (
49
) once leads to
\psi^{(1)}(\alpha)\,\varphi(\alpha)+\varphi^{(1)}(\alpha)\,\psi(\alpha)=\varphi_{1}
, while for
k\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
we obtain, by the Leibniz rule, for any
\alpha>0
,

(50)

\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\psi^{(i)}(\alpha)\varphi^{(k-i)}(\alpha)=0\,.

As
\varphi(\cdot)
is either increasing or decreasing in a right neighborhood of zero, it is possible to isolate
\psi^{(k)}(\alpha)
in (
50
), i.e., for every
\alpha
in a right neighborhood of zero we have

(51)

\psi^{(k)}(\alpha)=-\frac{1}{\varphi(\alpha)}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k}{i}\psi^{(i)}(\alpha)\varphi^{(k-i)}(\alpha)\,.

Now,
\varphi(\alpha)
tends to zero as
\alpha\downarrow 0
. Thus, if the sum in (
51
) also tends to zero, we would want to take the limit of
\psi^{(k)}(\alpha)
as
\alpha\downarrow 0
according to l’Hopital’s rule from which the recursive scheme (
46
) follows.

When trying to rigorize this argument, the difficulty is that is not clear how to justify the convergence of the sum in (
51
) towards zero as
\alpha\downarrow 0
. In order to resolve this issue, we rely on the following construction. For any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
\Psi_{i,m}(\cdot)
as a CPP (with nonnegative jumps) such that
\Psi_{i,m}(0)=\Psi_{i}(0)
and
\Delta\Psi_{i,m}(t)=m\wedge\Delta\Psi_{i}(t)
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Note that the jumps of
\Psi_{i,m}(\cdot)
occur at the same times as in
\Psi_{i}(\cdot)
, and that the jumps sizes in
\Psi_{i,m}(\cdot)
are truncated values of the corresponding jumps sizes in
\Psi_{i}(\cdot)
. Then, it is possible to define a sequence (in
m
) of spectrally positive Lévy processes

(52)

J_{m}(t)\equiv-ct+B_{\sigma}(t)+\overline{\Psi}_{m}(t)\ \ ,\ \ t\geqslant 0\,,

where
\overline{\Psi}_{m}(\cdot)\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Psi_{i,m}(\cdot)
. Importantly, for each
t\geqslant 0
,
J_{m}(\cdot)\uparrow J(\cdot)
as
m\uparrow\infty
. Furthermore, for each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the analogues of
\psi^{(k)}(0+)
in the model with
J_{m}(\cdot)
replacing
J(\cdot)
are finite for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus, we first prove (
46
) under the assumption that
J(\cdot)\equiv J_{m}(\cdot)
, after which we let
m\uparrow\infty
. Appealing to the appropriate convergence theorems then completes the proof for the general case. In Subsection
2.2.2
we make these steps formal.

2.2.2.
Takacs’ recursion: proof

The proof of Theorem
4
consists of several steps, which are described in the next lemmas.
We start by providing sufficient conditions under which (
46
) is valid.

Lemma 1
.

If for
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
\mathbb{E}\zeta^{k+1}<\infty
, then (
46
) is valid.

Proof.

Differentiating both sides of (

(k+1)

It is given that

(55)

Finally, the result follows by isolating

in (

Lemma 2
.

For any

\psi_{k}(m)

The main idea behind the next two lemmas is the following. We consider (

Lemma 3
.

For any
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\psi_{k}(m)
be the analogue of
\psi_{k}
in the model with
W(\cdot)
replaced by
\widetilde{W}_{m}(\cdot)

(56)

\psi_{k}(m)\rightarrow\psi_{k}\ \ \text{as}\ \ m\uparrow\infty\,.

Proof.

By construction, we have that

(1)

as

(2)

for any

0\leqslant\widetilde{W}^{k}_{m}(t)\,\textbf{1}_{\left[0,\widetilde{\tau}(m)\right]}(t)\uparrow W^{k}(t)\,\textbf{1}_{\left[0,\tau\right)}(t)\ \ \text{as}\ \ m\uparrow\infty\\
\ ,\ \ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

As a result, by the monotone convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\widetilde{\tau}(m)}\widetilde{W}_{m}^{k}(t)\,{\rm d}t

Lemma 4
.

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
\varphi_{k}(m)
the analogue of
\varphi_{k}
in the model with
W(\cdot)
replaced by
\widetilde{W}_{m}(\cdot)
. If
\eta_{k}<\infty
, then
\varphi_{k+1}(m)\rightarrow\varphi_{k+1}
as

.

Proof.

Note that for each
u\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{K}_{u}\equiv(-1)^{u+1}\varphi_{u}
(resp.
\mathcal{K}_{u}(m)\equiv(-1)^{u+1}\varphi_{u}(m)
, with

J(1)
(resp.

, with

). In addition,
for each
u\in\mathbb{N}
denote
\mathcal{J}_{u}\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[J(1)\right]^{u}
(resp.
\mathcal{J}_{u}(m)\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[J_{m}(1)\right]^{u}
, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
). The following recursive relations are well known:

(60)

\mathcal{J}_{u}=\sum_{a=0}^{u-1}\binom{u-1}{a}\mathcal{K}_{u-a}\mathcal{J}_{a}

and

(61)

\mathcal{K}_{u}=\mathcal{J}_{u}-\sum_{a=1}^{u-1}\binom{u-1}{a}\mathcal{K}_{u-a}\mathcal{J}_{a}

with the initial condition

; see, e.g.,
[

By assumption we have
\eta_{k}<\infty

Observe that the Brownian motion

are independent, so that, for any

Note that
\mathcal{J}_{1},\mathcal{J}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{k}
are finite, the moments of

are finite, and therefore

Also, in a similar fashion, for any

By construction, we have

as
m\uparrow\infty
and hence for any

we have

as

as

. Thus, since

has finite moments and
\mathbb{E}\Psi^{u}(1)<\infty
for any

, standard limit arithmetic as

implies that
\mathcal{J}_{u}(m)\rightarrow J_{u}
as

for any

such that

are finite and
\mathcal{J}_{k+1}
is infinite iff
\mathbb{E}\Psi^{k+1}(1)=\infty
. This means that for any

,

(64)

\mathcal{J}_{u+1}-\sum_{a=1}^{u}\binom{u}{a}\mathcal{K}_{u+1-a}\mathcal{J}_{a}=\lim_{m\uparrow\infty}\left[\mathcal{J}_{u+1}(m)-\sum_{a=1}^{u}\binom{u}{a}\mathcal{K}_{u+1-a}(m)\mathcal{J}_{a}(m)\right]

and the limit is finite for any

and for

(65)

\displaystyle(-1)^{u+2}\varphi_{u+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{u+1}=\mathcal{J}_{u+1}-\sum_{a=1}^{u}\binom{u}{a}\mathcal{K}_{u+1-a}\mathcal{J}_{a}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\uparrow\infty}\left[\mathcal{J}_{u+1}(m)-\sum_{a=1}^{u}\binom{u}{a}\mathcal{K}_{u+1-a}(m)\mathcal{J}_{a}(m)\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\uparrow\infty}\mathcal{K}_{u+1}(m)=(-1)^{u+2}\lim_{m\uparrow\infty}\varphi_{u+1}(m)\,,

where for

we have

(66)

\varphi_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{J}_{1}=\lim_{m\uparrow\infty}\mathcal{J}_{1}(m)=\lim_{m\uparrow\infty}\mathcal{K}_{1}(m)=\lim_{m\uparrow\infty}\varphi_{1}(m)\,.

4.
The process

The functional

is determined by the choice of

together with the value of the initial condition
x>0
. In this section, we fix

and analyze the sensitivity of

with respect to

. Put differently, for given

we study the properties of the stochastic process

. A specific motivation for this sensitivity analysis lies in a storage system in which:

(1)

is the amount of stored work at time
0\leqslant t\leqslant\ \tau
,

(2)

f(w)
and is the holding cost of
w\geqslant 0
workload units per an infinitesimal unit of time.

In addition, let this storage system have a controller who knows at time
t=0
that the initial workload has the value
x_{1}\geqslant 0
. Suppose that this controller needs to decide whether or not to accept a new ‘external’ job of size
x_{2}\geqslant 0
at time
t=0
. We assume that it is impossible to introduce new external jobs during a busy period and hence the decision should be based on the distribution of the difference
A_{f}(x_{1}+x_{2})-A_{f}(x_{1})
representing the expected additional holding cost which is due to accepting the new job. For a few examples of papers on similar functionals, playing a pivotal role in the context of solving various types of social optimization problems in service systems, we refer to e.g.,
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
29
]
.

This example illustrates the relevance of studying the process
x\mapsto A_{f}(x)
. Note that this process has a clear economic interpretation: for each
x\geqslant 0
,

represents the total holding cost accumulated during the time interval
[0,\tau_{x}]
, i.e., during the first busy period. This interpretation explains that in the sequel we refer to it as the
cost process
. For other examples of related cost processes associated with the workload process in an M/G/1 queue, along with their motivations and analysis, we refer to
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
. For a recent work on such cost processes in a more general framework, along with additional application areas, see
[
11
]
.

The objective of this section is to apply results from the previous sections to prove that for any polynomial
f(\cdot)
the corresponding cost process has sample paths which are
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We also provide a method to compute the high-order moments of the finite-dimensional distributions of the cost process. Lastly, we discuss an integro-differential equation which is related to the infinitesimal generator of
x\mapsto A_{f}(x)
when
f(\cdot)={\rm Id}
, giving rise to alternative ways to evaluate the corresponding moments.

In the first step of our analysis of
A_{f}(\cdot)
we derive the following decomposition of
A_{f}(x+y)
(for some
y>0
):

(88)

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A_{f}(x+y)=\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}}f\left[W_{x}(t)+y\right]{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\tau_{x+y}-\tau_{x}}f\left[x+y+J\left(\tau_{x}+t\right)\right]{\rm d}t\,.

The validity of this decomposition can be seen via the following line of reasoning. First notice that
[0,\tau_{x+y}]=[0,\tau_{x}]\cup[\tau_{x},\tau_{x+y}]
and by definition
W_{x+y}(t)=y+W_{x}(t)
for every
t\in[0,\tau_{x}]
. Therefore, deduce that

(89)

\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}}f\left[W_{x+y}(t)\right]{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}}f\left[W_{x}(t)+y\right]{\rm d}t.

In addition,

\tau_{x+y}-\tau_{x}\geqslant y
and
W_{x+y}(\tau_{x})=y
. Thus, as
W_{x+y}(t)=x+y+J(t)
for every
t\in[0,\tau_{x+y}]
and
J(\tau_{x})=-x
, then a standard change in the integration variable implies that

(90)

\int_{\tau_{x}}^{\tau_{x+y}}f\circ W_{x+y}(t){\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{\tau_{x+y}-\tau_{x}}f\left[x+y+J(\tau_{x}+t)\right]{\rm d}t,

thus concluding (
88
).

Importantly, due to the strong Markov property of
J(\cdot)
and the fact that
W_{x+y}(\tau_{x})=y
(i.e.,
x+J(\tau_{x})=0
), the first term and the second term are independent entailing that the second term is distributed as
A_{f}(y)
. In the sequel, we restrict our attention to the case where
f(\cdot)
is a polynomial; for simplicity of notation, for any
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
we denote (with mild abuse of notation)
A_{k}(\cdot)\equiv A_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(\cdot)
. For this
f(\cdot)
, by the binomium, the first term in (
88
) simplifies to

(91)

\sum_{i=0}^{k\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\binom{k}{i}y^{k-i}A_{i}(x)\ \ ,\ \ x\geqslant 0\,.

As it turns out, this representation has several applications which are presented in the next subsections.

Remark 8
.

In our setup
A_{k}(\cdot)
is a cost process which is based on the cost functional
A_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(\cdot)
. As is readily checked, all of the analysis to follow for
A_{k}(\cdot)
can be translated to an analogue cost process
D_{k}(\cdot)
which is based on the cost functional
D_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(\cdot)
.

4.1.
Auto-covariance function

If
\eta_{2k+2}<\infty
, then Corollary
2
, in combination with (
85
), implies that the auto-covariance function of the process
A_{k}(\cdot)
is well defined. It is given by

(92)

\text{Cov}\left[A_{k}(x_{1}),A_{k}(x_{2})\right]=\mathbb{E}[A_{k}(x_{1})A_{k}(x_{2})]-\mathbb{E}A_{k}(x_{1})\,\mathbb{E}A_{k}(x_{2})\ \ ,\ \ 0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}

where the second term can be computed by applying Corollary
1
. Now, it is to be explained how to utilize the previous results in order to compute the joint moment appearing in (
92
).

To this end, we introduce a random variable
\widetilde{A}_{k}(x_{2}-x_{1})
which is distributed as
A_{k}(x_{2}-x_{1})
and which is independent from
A_{k}(x_{1})
. Then, the decomposition (
88
) implies that

(93)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A_{k}(x_{1})A_{k}(x_{2})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[A_{k}(x_{1})\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(x_{2}-x_{1}){}^{k-i}A_{i}(x_{1})+\widetilde{A}_{k}(x_{2}-x_{1})\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(x_{2}-x_{1}){}^{k-i}\mathbb{E}[A_{k}(x_{1})A_{i}(x_{1})]+\mathbb{E}A_{k}(x_{2}-x_{1})\,\mathbb{E}A_{k}(x_{1})\,.

Note that all of the expectations in the right-hand side of (
93
) can be computed by the method which was developed in the previous sections. More generally, such an approach is useful in order to compute all the joint moments of the finite-dimensional distributions of
A_{k}(\cdot)
, i.e., the high-order moments of the coordinates of the random vector

(94)

\big(A_{k}(x_{1}),A_{k}(x_{2}),\ldots,A_{k}(x_{m})\big)

for
0\leqslant x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{m}<\infty
and
m=2,3,\ldots
.

4.2.
Lipschitz continuity

The next theorem states that the cost functional is locally Lipschitz continuous with respect to the initial workload
x\geqslant 0
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and assume that
\eta_{k+1}<\infty
. Then,
x\mapsto A_{k}(x)
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. locally Lipschitz
continuous on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Proof.

Let
x
be an arbitrary positive number. Also, notice that, for any
0\leqslant x_{0}\leqslant x
,

(95)

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\limsup_{y\downarrow 0}\frac{A_{k}(x_{0}+y)-A_{k}(x_{0})}{y}

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=kA_{k-1}(x_{0})+\limsup_{y\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{y}\int_{0}^{\tau_{x_{0}+y}-\tau_{x_{0}}}\left[\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x_{0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y+J\left(\tau_{x_{0}}+t\right)\right]^{k}{\rm d}t\,.

In order to show that the
\limsup
in right-hand side of (
95
) is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. finite, consider the event

(96)

\mathcal{E}(x_{0})\equiv\left\{\omega\ :\ \limsup_{y\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{y}\int_{0}^{\tau_{x_{0}+y}-\tau_{x_{0}}}\left[\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x_{0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y+J\left(\tau_{x_{0}}+t\right)\right]^{k}{\rm d}t=\infty\right\}

and its complement by
\mathcal{E}^{c}(x_{0})
. Notably, for every
\omega\in\mathcal{E}(x_{0})
, there is a sequence
y_{n}\equiv y_{n}(\omega,x_{0})
with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
y_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
and

(97)

0\leqslant\frac{1}{y_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{x_{0}+y_{n}}-\tau_{x_{0}}}\left[\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x_{0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y_{n}+J\left(\tau_{x_{0}}+t\right)\right]^{k}{\rm d}t\,\uparrow\,\infty\ \ \text{as}\ \ n\to\infty\,.

Therefore, if
\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{E}(x_{0})\right\}>0
, the monotone convergence theorem yields that

(98)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{y_{n}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{x_{0}+y_{n}}-\tau_{x_{0}}}\left[\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x_{0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y_{n}+J\left(\tau_{x_{0}}+t\right)\right]^{k}{\rm d}t\Big]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}(x_{0})}}{y_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{x_{0}+y_{n}}-\tau_{x_{0}}}\left[\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x_{0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y_{n}+J\left(\tau_{x_{0}}+t\right)\right]^{k}{\rm d}t\Big]\rightarrow\infty\ \ \text{as}\ \ n\to\infty\,.

On the other hand, the assumption
\eta_{k+1}<\infty
, in combination with Corollaries
1
and
2
, imply that, for any
x,y>0
,

(99)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{y}\int_{0}^{\tau_{x+y}-\tau_{x}}

\displaystyle\left[\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x+y+J\left(\tau_{x}+t\right)\right]^{k}{\rm d}t\Big]=\frac{\mathbb{E}A_{k}(y)}{y}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varrho}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\frac{y^{k-i}}{k-i+1}(-1)^{i}\psi_{i}\xrightarrow{y\downarrow 0}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}\psi_{k}}{\varrho}<\infty\,.

As a result, a contradiction to (
98
) is obtained, and hence
\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{E}(x_{0})\}=0
. Therefore, there exists (random)
K_{x_{0}},\delta_{x_{0}}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
,

(100)

|A_{k}(z_{1})-A_{k}(z_{2})|<K_{x_{0}}|z_{2}-z_{1}|

for every
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
which belong to a set
B(x_{0})\equiv B_{\delta_{x_{0}}}(x_{0})\equiv(x_{0}-\delta_{x_{0}},x_{0}+\delta_{x_{0}})
. Now, notice that
\cup_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,x]}B(x_{0})
is an open cover of
[0,x]
. Therefore, the Heine-Borel theorem implies that there are
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{m}
in
\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,x]
such that
\cup_{i=1}^{m}B(q_{i})=[0,x]
. In addition, let
\beta
be the Lebesgue number of the open cover
\cup_{i=1}^{m}B(q_{i})
. Now, for every
0\leqslant z_{1}<z_{2}\leqslant x
there are numbers
s\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
and
z_{1}=u_{1}\leqslant u_{2}\leqslant u_{3}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant u_{s}=z_{2}
such that
u_{j+1}-u_{j}\leqslant\beta
for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,s-1\}
. As a result, with
K\equiv\max\left\{K(q_{i}):i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\right\}
, on the event
\mathcal{E}\equiv\cap_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{E}^{c}(q_{i})
we have

(101)

\displaystyle A_{k}(z_{2})-A_{k}(z_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}\left[A_{k}(u_{j+1})-A_{k}(u_{j})\right]\leqslant K\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}\left(u_{j+1}-u_{j}\right)\leqslant K(z_{2}-z_{1})\,.

Therefore, as
\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{E}\}=1
, we know that
A_{k}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous on
[0,x]
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. Finally, the result follows since
\mathbb{R}_{+}=\cup_{x=1}^{\infty}[0,x]
.
∎

Since Lipschitz continuity implies differentiability a.e., the following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem
6
.

Corollary 4
.

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. the limit

(102)

L(x)\equiv\lim_{y\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{y}\int_{0}^{\tau_{x+y}-\tau_{x}}\left[\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y+J\left(\tau_{x}+t\right)\right]^{k}{\rm d}t

exists a.e. on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Furthermore, wherever this limit exists,
A_{k}(\cdot)
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. differentiable with the derivative satisfying

(103)

A^{\prime}_{k}(x)=kA_{k-1}(x)+L(x)\,.

Remark 9
.

Note that
A_{k-1}(\cdot)
is nondecreasing, and that the distribution of
L(x)
does not depend on
x
. Therefore, it is tempting to conclude that the sample paths of
A_{k}(\cdot)
are
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. convex. This conclusion is, however, incorrect since it is possible to have a sequence of stochastically increasing random variables that with positive probability will not have increasing values. For an example of a slightly different cost process in an M/G/1 setup which has convex sample paths, we refer to
[
35
, Corollary 2]
. More examples of convex stochastic processes which arise in various applied probability models are found in
[
31
]
.

4.3.
Differential equation

In this subsection we discuss a specific (ordinary) differential equation by which we can characterize moments of
A_{f}(x)
in the case
f(\cdot)={\rm Id}
. For simplicity, we introduce it for the special case in which
k=1
and under the assumption that
\eta_{i}<\infty
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We conclude this section by pointing out possible interpretations which may
lead to possible directions for future research.

For
u\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce the set

(104)

\mathcal{I}(u)\equiv\left\{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in\mathbb{N}^{u}:\ i_{j}\leqslant 2j-1+\sum_{a=1}^{u-1}i_{a}\ ,\ j\in\{1,\ldots,u\}\ \text{and}\ \sum_{j=1}^{u}i_{j}=2u-1\right\};

which is not empty because
(0,0,\ldots,0,2u-1)\in\mathcal{I}(u)
. Also, define

(105)

\theta(u)\equiv u!(-\rho)^{-u}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{I}(u)}\prod_{j=1}^{u}\binom{2j-1-\sum_{a=1}^{j-1}i_{a}}{i_{j}}\frac{(-1)^{i_{j}}\psi_{i_{j}}}{2j-\sum_{a=1}^{j}i_{a}}.

Theorem 7
.

Assume that
\eta_{i}<\infty
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then, the following recursion in
\ell
applies:

(106)

\mathbb{E}A_{1}^{\ell}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{\ell}{i}\theta(\ell-i)\,\int_{0}^{x}\mathbb{E}A^{i}_{1}(y)\,{\rm d}y\ \ ,\ \ x\geqslant 0\,.

Proof.

Applying Theorem
5
and Corollary
3
, one can verify that, for any such
u
,

(107)

\theta(u)=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}A_{1}^{u}(y)}{y}\,.

Now, for an integer
\ell
the decomposition (
88
) yields that, for any
x,y>0
,

(108)

\mathbb{E}A^{\ell}_{1}(x+y)=\mathbb{E}\left[A_{1}(x)+y\tau_{x}+\widetilde{A}_{1}(y)\right]^{\ell}\,.

As a consequence,

(109)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}A^{\ell}_{1}(x+y)-\mathbb{E}A^{\ell}_{1}(x)}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{\ell}{i}\mathbb{E}\left[A_{1}^{i}(x)\left[y\tau_{x}+\widetilde{A}_{1}(y)\right]^{\ell-i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-i}\binom{\ell}{i}\binom{\ell-i}{j}y^{j}\mathbb{E}\big[A^{i}_{1}(x)\overline{\tau}^{j}_{x}\big]\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\widetilde{A}_{1}^{\ell-i-j}(y)}{y}\,.

Thus, by taking the limit as
y\downarrow 0
from both sides of (
109
), we thus obtain the differential equation

(110)

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\mathbb{E}A_{1}^{\ell}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{\ell}{i}\,\theta(\ell-i)\,\mathbb{E}A^{i}_{1}(x)\,.

An integration of both sides, using the initial condition
A_{1}^{\ell}(0)=0
, yields the recursion.
∎

Remark 10
.

Equation
(
110
) is particularly intriguing as it provides an expression for the infinitesimal generator of the process
A^{\ell}_{1}(\cdot)
in terms of the processes
A_{1}^{i}(\cdot)
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
. This argument may be repeated for each of the processes
A_{1}^{i}(\cdot)
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
, indicating that there is an ‘integro-differential relation’ between the underlying infinitesimal generators. Backed by the findings of
[
23
]
, one may anticipate that
there is a relation to a corresponding Poisson’s equation. In this respect, it is noticed that (
88
) shows that
x\mapsto\left(A_{1}(x),\tau_{x}\right)
is a Markov process, even though
A_{1}(\cdot)
is
not
a Markov process on its own.

Remark 11
.

The ‘integro-difference equation’ (
106
) allows an explicit assessment in the transform domain. Defining
\bar{a}_{\ell}(x)\equiv\mathbb{E}A_{1}^{\ell}(x)/\ell!
and
\bar{\theta}(\ell)\equiv\theta(\ell)/\ell!
, multiplying the entire equation (
106
) by
\beta e^{-\beta x}
and integrating over the positive halfline yields (by swapping the order of the two integrals)

(111)

\displaystyle a_{\ell}(\beta)

\displaystyle\equiv\beta\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta x}\bar{a}_{\ell}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\beta\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta x}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\bar{\theta}(\ell-i)\,\int_{0}^{x}\bar{a}_{i}(y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\bar{\theta}(\ell-i)\,a_{i}(\beta).

Now multiplying (
111
) by
z^{\ell}
(with
|z|<1
) and summing over
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, denoting
\Theta(z)\equiv\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}z^{\ell}\bar{\theta}(\ell)
,

(112)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{A}}(z,\beta)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}z^{\ell}a_{\ell}(\beta)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}z^{\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\bar{\theta}(\ell-i)\,a_{i}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{\infty}z^{\ell-i}\,\bar{\theta}(\ell-i)\right)\,z^{i}a_{i}(\beta)=\Theta(z)\,\left({\mathscr{A}}(z,\beta)+a_{0}(\beta)\right).

We conclude that the double transform
{\mathscr{A}}(z,\beta)
satisfies

(113)

{\mathscr{A}}(z,\beta)=\frac{\Theta(z)}{1-\Theta(z)}a_{0}(\beta)

for
|z|<1.
Here
a_{0}(\beta)=\beta\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta x}\,{\mathbb{E}}\tau_{x}\,{\rm d}x=-1/(\beta\varrho)
.

5.
The area beneath
\overline{W}_{V}(\cdot)
until an exponential time

In this paper, we devised an algorithm to evaluate (joint) high-order moments of
A_{f}
,
D_{g}
, and
\tau
, in the general Lévy-driven setup that we discussed. In this section we provide a potential application of this result. Namely, consider the case when: (1)
J(\cdot)
is a CPP with an arrival rate
\lambda
and nonnegative jumps having cdf
G(\cdot)
minus a unit drift. (2)
V
is distributed according to
G(\cdot)
. Recall the definition of the Skorokhod-reflected process
\overline{W}_{V}(\cdot)\equiv\overline{W}(\cdot)
from (
25
). We now show that the main results of this paper can be applied in order to identify the LST of

(114)

\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{V}(T_{\beta})\equiv\int_{0}^{T_{\beta}}\overline{W}_{V}(t)\,{\rm d}t\,=\int_{0}^{T_{\beta}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{W}_{x}(t)\,{\rm d}G(x)\,{\rm d}t,

where
T_{\beta}
is an exponentially distributed random variable with rate
\beta
which is independent from both
V
and
J(\cdot)
. By first dividing this quantity by
\beta
, and then (numerically) inverting it with respect to
\beta
, we obtain access to the distribution of
\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{V}(t)
.
Algorithms for numerical inversion can be found in e.g.
[
1
]
.

The line of reasoning is as follows.

\circ

Our goal is to identify the LST of (
114
), i.e.,

(115)

\Omega(\alpha,\beta)\equiv{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{V}(T_{\beta})}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(T_{\beta})}\,{\rm d}G(x).

\circ

For every
x,t\geqslant 0
define
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{x}(t)\equiv\int_{0}^{t}W_{x}(s)\,{\rm d}s
and
\overline{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{x}(t)\equiv\int_{0}^{t}\overline{W}_{x}(s)\,{\rm d}s
.
In particular, observe that

(116)

\Xi(\alpha,\beta)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(\tau_{x})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<T_{\beta}\}}{\rm d}G(x)={\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha A-\beta\tau}\,,

where
A\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}W_{V}(t)\,{\rm d}t.

Observe that we (in principle) have access to
\Xi(\alpha,\beta)
, as, by applying the results of the previous sections, we have an algorithm to compute the joint moments of
A
and
\tau
in terms of the model primitives; note that

(117)

\Xi(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{m}\beta^{n}}{m!n!}{\mathbb{E}}\,A^{m}\tau^{n}.

In practical terms, if one were able to numerically evaluate
\Xi(\alpha,\beta)
, a pragmatic approach is to approximate it by a truncated Taylor series, e.g.,

(118)

\Xi(\alpha,\beta)\approx\Xi_{M}(\alpha,\beta)\equiv\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:m+n\leqslant M}\frac{\alpha^{m}\beta^{n}}{m!n!}{\mathbb{E}}\,A^{m}\tau^{n}

for some
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
chosen such that a given accuracy level is achieved.

\circ

In Theorem
8
we succeed in expressing
\Omega(\alpha,\beta)
in terms of
\Xi(\alpha,\beta)
. We have thus developed a way to evaluate the transform
\Omega(\alpha,\beta)
.

In our analysis, we also use the transform

(119)

\Pi(\alpha,\beta)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\beta}{\alpha x+\beta-\bar{\varphi}(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}G(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta\,\widetilde{G}(\alpha s)\,e^{-(\beta-\bar{\varphi}(\alpha))s}\,{\rm d}s,

where
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha)\equiv\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha t)\,{\rm d}t
and
\widetilde{G}(\cdot)
is the LST of
G(\cdot)
. Especially, notice that the equality in (
119
) follows by first writing (in the rightmost expression, that is)
\widetilde{G}(\alpha s)
as an integral and then swapping the order of the two integrals.

Theorem 8
.

For any
\alpha,\beta>0
,

(120)

\Omega\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\frac{\displaystyle(\lambda+\beta)\Pi\left(\alpha,\beta\right)+\beta\,\Xi\left(\alpha,\beta\right)\left(1-\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\beta-\overline{\varphi}(\alpha)}\right)}{\displaystyle\lambda+\beta-{\lambda\,\Xi\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}}.

Proof.

First, for every
x\geqslant 0
, write

(121)

{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(T_{\beta})}={\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(T_{\beta})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<T_{\beta}\}}+{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(T_{\beta})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}\geqslant T_{\beta}\}}.

Owing to the underlying Markovian structure, and distinguishing between the scenario that a second busy period starts before the killing epoch
T_{\beta}
and the scenario that it does not, we can rewrite the first term on the right-hand side to

(122)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(T_{\beta})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<T_{\beta}\}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(\tau_{x})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<T_{\beta}\}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\beta}\Omega(\alpha,\beta)+\frac{\beta}{\lambda+\beta}\right).

The second term on the right-hand side of (
121
) equals

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(T_{\beta})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}\geqslant T_{\beta}\}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(T_{\beta})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}\geqslant T_{\beta}\}}={\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}{}_{x}(T_{\beta})}-{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}{}_{x}(T_{\beta})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<T_{\beta}\}}.

Then,

(123)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}{}_{x}(T_{\beta})}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta e^{-\beta s}\,{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{s}\left[x+J(t)\right]\,{\rm d}t\right){\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta e^{-(\alpha x+\beta)s}\,e^{\bar{\varphi}(\alpha)\,s}\,{\rm d}s=\frac{\beta}{\alpha x+\beta-\bar{\varphi}(\alpha)},

where in the penultimate equality
[
10
, Equation (4)]
has been used. Also, using the memoryless property of the exponential distribution,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}{}_{x}(T_{\beta})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<T_{\beta}\}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(\tau_{x})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<T_{\beta}\}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha A_{0}(T_{\beta})}={\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{A}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}_{x}(\tau_{x})}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<T_{\beta}\}}\,\frac{\beta}{\beta-\bar{\varphi}(\alpha)}.

Upon combining the above, we can express
\Omega(\alpha,\beta)
in itself. Indeed, by integrating the full expression with respect to
{\rm d}G(\cdot)
,

(124)

\Omega(\alpha,\beta)=\Xi(\alpha,\beta)\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\beta}\Omega(\alpha,\beta)+\frac{\beta}{\lambda+\beta}\right)+\Pi(\alpha,\beta)-\Xi(\alpha,\beta)\,\frac{\beta}{\beta-\bar{\varphi}(\alpha)}.

This finishes the proof. ∎

The next goal is to compute the second moment of

. To this end, apply (

\displaystyle(1-\rho)^{2}\mathbb{E}A_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}A_{\mathcal{P}_{0}}

7.
Discussion and directions for follow-up research

In this work we solved the problem of identifying the high-order moments of the vector (
7
). Specifically, we developed a recursive procedure that easily produces these moments in terms of the model primitives. Our findings in particular provide a solution for a long-standing problem posed by Iglehart
[
28
]
and Cohen
[
16
]
about existence of a constructive algorithm yielding the moments of
A_{f}
for the case that
f(\cdot)={\rm Id}
. As it was pointed out in Section
1
, being able to solve this open problem is relevant in the context of various applied probability models, e.g., (1) the computation of the conditional moments of the ‘area in red’ in a Lévy-driven model given the undershoot, and (2) the estimation of functionals of the limiting distribution of a Lévy process with secondary jump input.

We believe that our results, and the approach followed, naturally give rise to several follow-up questions. A few particularly interesting ones are:

\circ

The current results may be applied to approximate the LST of
A_{k}(x)
. We expect this approximation to be useful when assessing the tail probability
\mathbb{P}\left\{A_{k}(x)>u\right\}
as
u\to\infty
. For some existing works in this direction, see e.g.
[
5
,
10
,
12
]
. In case
x
is sampled according to the distribution function
G_{V}(\cdot)
, a large deviation of the area can be due to a non-trivial interplay between the secondary jump attaining an unusually high value and the driving Lévy process
J(\cdot)
being after time
0
systematically higher than usual.

\circ

More generally, let
f(\cdot)
be a nonnegative continuous function. The Weierstrass approximation theorem states that
f(\cdot)
can be approximated arbitrarily closely by polynomials (e.g., Bernstein polynomials). This suggests that our approach could be used in order to approximate the moments of
A_{f}
for any
f(\cdot)
(under some regularity conditions).

\circ

Another natural question concerns the identification of conditions under which the sequence

(133)

\frac{A_{k}(x)-\mathbb{E}A_{k}(x)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}\,A_{k}(x)}}\ \ ,\ \ x>0

converges as
x\to\infty
(for given
k
) to some limiting distribution.

\circ

Consider a sequence of cost processes

\left\{A_{k}^{(n)}(\cdot):n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

If
\varphi_{n}(\cdot)
is the Lévy exponent corresponding to the
n
-th model, what should be assumed on the sequence
\varphi_{n}(\cdot)
in order to ensure, in a proper space of functions, the weak convergence
A_{k}^{(n)}(\cdot)
to some limiting process
A(\cdot)
? For a similar analysis of a related process, we refer to
[
34
, Section 7]
and
[
35
, Section 6]
.

\circ

Note that in the current work, we only consider the case that the driving Lévy process is a superposition of a drifted Brownian motion and a subordinator, which is a special case of the broader class of spectrally positive Lévy processes. When trying to extend our results to the spectrally positive Lévy case, a major bottleneck concerns the generalization of the Takacs’ recursion of Theorem
4
; recall that in our proofs we intensively used that our driving Lévy process was the aggregate of a drifted Brownian motion and a subordinator.

\circ

In the context of Section
5
, one may be curious to know whether the relation (
120
) which appears in Theorem
8
can be generalized by considering classes of processes that are richer than just CPP. Furthermore, Theorem
8
is valid only if
f(\cdot)={\rm Id}
, but one could pursue analogous results for more general classes of functionals.
