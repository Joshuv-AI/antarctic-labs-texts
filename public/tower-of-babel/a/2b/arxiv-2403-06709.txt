Validation of the GreenX library time-frequency component for efficient GW and RPA calculations
Maryam Azizi, Jan Wilhelm, Dorothea Golze, Francisco A. Delesma, Ramón L. Panadés-Barrueta, Patrick Rinke, Matteo Giantomassi, Xavier Gonze

Abstract
Electronic structure calculations based on many-body perturbation theory (e.g. GW or the random-phase approximation (RPA)) require function evaluations in the complex time and frequency domain, for example inhomogeneous Fourier transforms or analytic continuation from the imaginary axis to the real axis. For inhomogeneous Fourier transforms, the time-frequency component of the GreenX library provides time-frequency grids that can be utilized in low-scaling RPA and GW implementations. In addition, the adoption of the compact frequency grids provided by our library also reduces the computational overhead in RPA implementations with conventional scaling. In this work, we present low-scaling GW and conventional RPA benchmark calculations using the GreenX grids with different codes (FHI-aims, CP2K and ABINIT) for molecules, two-dimensional materials and solids. Very small integration errors are observed when using 30 time-frequency points for our test cases, namely < 10 − 8 <10^{-8} eV/electron for the RPA correlation energies, and ≤ 10 \leq 10 meV for the G ​ W GW quasiparticle energies.

II
RPA and
GW
methodology in imaginary time and frequency

In this section, we define the Green’s functions, susceptibility and Fourier transformations from imaginary time to imaginary frequency and vice versa which are then used to compute the RPA correlation energy and
GW
quasiparticle energies.

Within the adiabatic-connection fluctuation-dissipation formalism
Gunnarsson and Lundqvist 1976
;
Dahlen et al. 2006
;
Dobson 1994
;
Eshuis et al. 2012
;
Ren et al. 2012
, the
\mathrm{RPA}
correlation energy can be written as

E_{c}^{\mathrm{RPA}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\mathrm{Tr}\{\mathrm{ln}[1-\chi(i\omega)V]+\chi(i\omega)V\},

(1)

where
\chi
stands for the RPA independent-particle susceptibility (or interacting density response) evaluated on the imaginary frequency axis (
i\omega
with
\omega
being real) and
V
denotes the frequency-independent Coulomb interaction. The spatial arguments have been omitted for brevity.
The RPA susceptibility is also an ingredient for the
GW
self-energy.

The RPA susceptibility can be computed from the independent-particle (irreducible) non-interacting susceptibility,
\chi^{0}
, through Dyson’s equation

\chi(i\omega)=\chi^{0}(i\omega)+\chi^{0}(i\omega)V\chi(i\omega)\,.

(2)

For
GW
, the screened Coulomb interaction
W
is then needed, followed by its frequency-convolution with the Green function
G
, as we will outline in more detail below.

In the Adler-Wiser formula
Adler 1962
;
Wiser 1963
, the non-interacting susceptibility in the imaginary-frequency domain can be obtained as follows

\displaystyle\chi^{0}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}},i\omega\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}^{\text{occ}}\sum_{a}^{\text{unocc}}\psi^{*}_{a}\left(\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\right)\psi_{j}\left(\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\right)\psi^{*}_{j}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)\psi_{a}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{2\left(\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{a}\right)}{\omega^{2}+\left(\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{a}\right)^{2}},

where indices
j
and
a
refer to occupied and unoccupied Kohn-Sham (KS) or Hartree-Fock states (
\psi(\mathbf{r})
) with energies

\epsilon
. The computational cost required to compute Eq. (
3
) in the frequency domain scales as
\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{at}}^{4})
, since the number of states (
j
and
a
indices) and discretized real space points
(
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{r^{\prime}}
) scale linearly with system size
N_{\text{at}}
. This is a significant bottleneck, which can be reduced by invoking the low-scaling space-time approach
Rojas et al. 1995
.

By Fourier transforming Eq. (
3
) in imaginary time (
i\tau
), the two summations decouple,

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}^{0}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j}^{\text{occ}}\psi_{j}\left(\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\right)\psi^{*}_{j}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)e^{\epsilon_{j}|\tau|}

(4)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sum_{a}^{\text{unocc}}\psi^{*}_{a}\left(\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\right)\psi_{a}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)e^{-\epsilon_{a}|\tau|}.

A circumflex accent is used to denote Fourier transformed functions of the imaginary time.
For completeness, we mention here that Eq. (
4
) is equivalent to expressing
\chi^{0}
as a product of two non-interacting Green’s functions

\hat{\chi}^{0}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)=\hat{G}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)\hat{G}^{*}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},-i\tau\right),

(5)

with

\displaystyle\hat{G}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}^{\mathrm{occ}}\psi_{j}(\mathbb{r})\psi^{*}_{j}(\mathbb{r^{\prime}})e^{-\epsilon_{j}\tau}\hskip 25.00003pt(\tau<0),

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{G}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{a}^{\mathrm{unocc}}\psi_{a}(\mathbb{r})\psi^{*}_{a}(\mathbb{r^{\prime}})e^{-\epsilon_{a}\tau}\hskip 10.00002pt(\tau>0).

(7)

The computational cost of the Green’s function scales cubically in
N_{\text{at}}
and so does
\hat{\chi}^{0}
. The space-time approach therefore saves one order in
N_{\text{at}}
compared to the aforementioned Adler-Wiser formalism.

With Fourier transformations
\chi^{0}
and
\chi
can be switched between (imaginary) time and frequency:

\displaystyle f\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\omega\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-i\omega\tau}\hat{f}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)d\tau,

\displaystyle\hat{f}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{i\omega\tau}f\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\omega\right)d\omega.

(8)

Since both susceptibilities are even functions in frequency and time (
\chi^{0}(i\omega)=\chi^{0}(-i\omega)
and
\hat{\chi}^{0}(i\tau)=\hat{\chi}^{0}(-i\tau)
),
these transforms simplify to cosine transformations,

\displaystyle\chi^{0}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\omega\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{i\omega\tau}\;\hat{\chi}^{0}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)d\tau

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(\mathrm{cos}(\omega\tau)+i\mathrm{sin}(\omega\tau))\;\hat{\chi}^{0}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{cos}(\omega\tau)\;\hat{\chi}^{0}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{cos}(\omega\tau)\;\hat{\chi}^{0}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)d\tau.

After a Fourier transforming
\chi
to imaginary frequency, the dielectric function can be calculated in the imaginary-frequency domain as

\displaystyle\epsilon\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\omega\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int d\mathbb{r^{\prime\prime}}V\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime\prime}}\right)\chi^{0}\left(\mathbb{r^{\prime\prime}},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\omega\right).

The screened Coulomb interaction
W
is then given by

W\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\omega\right)=\int d\mathbb{r^{\prime\prime}}\epsilon^{-1}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime\prime}},i\omega\right)V\left(\mathbb{r^{\prime\prime}},\mathbb{r^{\prime}}\right)

(11)

and cosine transformed into the time domain,

\displaystyle W(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{i\omega\tau}\,W(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\omega)\,d\omega\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\cos(\omega\tau)\,W(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\omega)\,d\omega\,.

(12)

The self-energy follows as a product with the Green’s function

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}\left(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau\right)=i\hat{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau)\hat{W}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r^{\prime}},i\tau).

(13)

The quasiparticle energies are then obtained from the matrix elements of the self-energy with respect to the single-particle wave functions of the corresponding states after Fourier transforming the self-energy from imaginary time to imaginary frequency. Since the self-energy is neither an odd nor an even function, both cosine and sine transformations are needed
Liu et al. 2016
,

\displaystyle\Sigma(i\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\,\hat{G}(i\tau)\hat{W}(i\tau)e^{i\omega t}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\hat{\Sigma}^{c}(i\tau)\cos(\omega\tau)

(15)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,2i\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\hat{\Sigma}^{s}(i\tau)\sin(\omega\tau),

(16)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{c}(i\tau)=-\frac{1}{2}[\hat{G}(i\tau)+\hat{G}(-i\tau)]\hat{W}(i\tau),

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{s}(i\tau)=-\frac{1}{2}[\hat{G}(i\tau)-\hat{G}(-i\tau)]\hat{W}(i\tau).

(18)

This is the last step in the low-scaling algorithm which will then be followed by the calculation of the
quasiparticle energy using analytic continuation
Golze et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2016
;
Wilhelm et al. 2021
.

III
Minimax time and frequency integration grids

III.1
Analytical form of functions in time and frequency

In Sec.
II
we have discussed that the computation of the susceptibility in the imaginary time domain
scales favorably.
However, the subsequent time-frequency Fourier transform of such a function is challenging if performed numerically.
Eq. (
3
) in the frequency domain is the exact (cosine) Fourier transform of
Eq. (
4
), in the time domain.
In a numerical approach, Eq. (
4
) has to be evaluated for a finite set of time points, where the set should be as small as possible for an efficient algorithm.
However, the functions introduced in the previous section in the imaginary time and frequency domains usually have long tails and very localized features.
As such, a usual Fast Fourier Transform, with homogeneously spaced integration grids, would need numerous sampling points.
Instead, a nonuniform Fourier transform (actually cosine and sine transforms) would yield a reduction by more than one order of magnitude in the computational requirements (both CPU time and memory).

The problem is thus to find the “best set” of time points, and associated weights, to discretize the integral in Eq. (
9
).
Similarly, the inverse transform Eq. (
12
) from frequency to time has to be performed on an equivalently “best set” of frequency points.
One option for defining a “best set” of points and weights is to exploit the functional form of the frequency dependence of Eq. (
3
), and the time dependence of Eq. (
4
).
For simplicity
Kaltak et al. 2014a
, we single out the frequency and time dependence of Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
)

\displaystyle\chi(i\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\chi_{\mu}\phi_{\omega}(x_{\mu}),

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}(i\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\chi_{\mu}\hat{\phi}_{\tau}(x_{\mu}),

(20)

thanks to the auxiliary functions

\displaystyle\phi_{\omega}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{2x}{x^{2}+\omega^{2}}

(21)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\tau}(x)

\displaystyle:=e^{-x|\tau|}\,,

(22)

where
\mu
in Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) runs over the occupied-state index

and the unoccupied-state index

,

is the energy difference between occupied and unoccupied states (

) and
\chi_{\mu}=\psi^{*}_{a}(\mathbb{r^{\prime}})\psi_{j}(\mathbb{r^{\prime}})\psi^{*}_{j}(\mathbb{r})\psi_{a}(\mathbb{r})

Note that

IV.2
FHI-aims

FHI-aims is an all-electron electronic structure code based on numerical atom-centered orbitals (NAOs)
Blum et al. 2009
.
All canonical RPA calculations were performed with FHI-aims. The RPA implementations in FHI-aims scale
\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{at}}^{4})
with respect to system size
N_{\text{at}}
and are based on the resolution-of-the-identity (RI) approach,
Whitten 1973
;
Dunlap et al. 1979
;
Mintmire et al. 1982
;
Vahtras et al. 1993a
refactoring the 4-center electron repulsion integrals in a product of three- and two-center integrals. For finite systems, the RPA implementation
Ren et al. 2012
relies on a global RI scheme with a Coulomb metric (RI-V), while for extended systems a localized variant (RI-LVL) is employed
Ihrig et al. 2015
;
Ren et al. 2021
.

Molecular RPA calculations were performed for the Thiel benchmark set using the cc-pVTZ Dunning basis set
Kendall et al. 1992
, which is an all-electron basis set of contracted spherical Gaussian orbitals. In FHI-aims, Gaussian basis sets are presented numerically to adhere to the NAO scheme. For the crystalline systems, we employed the hierarchical
tier 2
NAO basis functions
Blum et al. 2009
.
For both, molecules and crystal, the RI auxiliary basis functions (ABFs) were auto-generated during run-time following the approach described in Ref.
71
. For the crystalline systems, we have added additional
4f
and
5g
functions to the auto-generated ABFs to increase the accuracy of the RI-LVL approach implemented for solids
Ren et al. 2021
.

The RPA correlation energies were numerically computed on imaginary frequency grids
Ren et al. 2012
. We benchmarked three different grid types: the Gauss-Legendre (GL), modified Gauss-Legendre (mod-GL) and minimax quadrature grids. The modified Gauss-Legendre quadrature is described in more detail in Ref.
71
.

IV.3
CP2K

CP2K is an electronic-structure code that employs Gaussian basis sets for expanding the KS orbitals
Kühne et al. 2020
. CP2K can be used with Goedecker-Teter-Hutter pseudopotentials
Goedecker et al. 1996
(GTH) or as an all-electron code using the Gaussian and augmented plane-waves scheme (GAPW)
Lippert et al. 1999
. CP2K features a low-scaling
G_{0}W_{0}
implementation based on a local-orbital-basis adaptation of the space-time method
Rojas et al. 1995
, where sparsity is introduced by combining a global RI scheme with a truncated Coulomb metric
Wilhelm et al. 2021
;
Graml et al. 2024
. Low-scaling
GW
implementations for finite systems
Wilhelm et al. 2021
and recently also for extended systems
Graml et al. 2024
are available.

We used the low-scaling
G_{0}W_{0}
implementations in CP2K for performing the all-electron GW100 benchmark
van Setten et al. 2015
and for computing the
G_{0}W_{0}
bandgap of 2D materials with pseudopotentials. For the GW100 benchmark, we expanded molecular orbitals in the all-electron def2-QZVP
Weigend et al. 2003
basis set and used def2-TZVPPD-RIFIT
Hättig 2005
as ABFs.
For
G_{0}W_{0}
benchmark calculations on 2D materials, we used 10
\times
10 supercells, GTH pseudopotentials
Goedecker et al. 1996
with a TZV2P-MOLOPT basis set
VandeVondele and Hutter 2007
and corresponding ABFs
Graml et al. 2024
.
For molecules and 2D materials, we set a truncation radius of 3 Å
Vahtras et al. 1993b
;
Jung et al. 2005
for the truncated Coulomb metric.
Two- and three-center Coulomb integrals over Gaussian basis functions were computed with analytical schemes
Golze et al. 2017
;
Libint, A library for the evaluation of molecular
integrals of operators over Gaussian functions
.
The self-energy was analytically continued from the imaginary to the real-frequency domain using a Padé model
Vidberg and Serene 1977
;
van Setten et al. 2015
with 16 parameters.

While the primary purpose of the minimax grids is to facilitate low-scaling RPA and
GW
calculations, they can be also utilized to reduce the computational prefactor in conventional RPA implementations with
O(N_{\text{at}}^{4})
complexity. We demonstrate this in Fig.
2
for a single water molecule (top) and bulk MgO (bottom). Figure
2
reports the error of the RPA correlation energies calculated with GL and mod-GL grids with 6–50 points and minimax grids with 6–32 points. The RPA energy obtained with the 34-point minimax grid is used as reference.

Figure
2
shows that the GL quadrature converges slowly, while the mod-GL grids exhibit a faster convergence as already discussed in Ref.
71
. The minimax grids display the fastest convergence. We set a target accuracy of
10^{-6}
eV/electron, which is motivated by the size of the error introduced by the RI-V approach. RI-V is implemented in FHI-aims for finite RPA calculations and considered the most accurate RI scheme
Vahtras et al. 1993b
. Recently, we showed for MP2 correlation energies that RI-V introduces an average error of
5\times 10^{-6}
eV/electron compared to RI-free results
Delesma et al. 2024
. Similar RI errors are expected for RPA. The GL grids fail to achieve the desired accuracy for the grid sizes presented in Fig.
2
. In contrast, the mod-GL grids surpass the accuracy threshold with over 36 and 46 frequency points for the water molecule and bulk MgO, respectively. With minimax grids, errors below
10^{-6}
eV/electron are already achieved with 10 frequency points for water and 14 for MgO. Minimax frequency grids are therefore approximately 3.5-fold more efficient than mod-GL grids.

For even larger minimax grids of 20 points, the RPA integration errors drop below
10^{-10}
eV/electron in both cases, as is evident in Fig.
2
. The minimax error further decreases by two orders of magnitude to
10^{-12}
eV/electron before leveling out at around 32 points. We found that it is not possible to converge the RPA correlation energies as tightly with the mod-GL grids, which eventually yield fluctuating errors around
10^{-9}
-
10^{-10}
eV/electron. This is also the reason why we use the 34-point minimax grid as reference in Fig.
2
instead of a mod-GL grid with hundreds of points. The convergence behavior of the minimax grids for more than 20 grid points would not be adequately displayed.

Further benchmarks were performed with 10, 20 and 30 minimax grid points for molecules and crystalline systems as shown in Figs.
3
(a) and (b). Taking the mod-GL result with 200 frequency points as our reference, we find that minimax grids with 10 points fail to converge all molecular and extended systems below the threshold of the RI-V accuracy. However, we observe an excellent convergence for larger grids. All molecules from the Thiel set are converged within
10^{-9}
eV/electron for minimax grids with 20 or more frequency points; see Fig.
3
(a).

For solids shown in Fig.
3

10^{-8}
eV/electron for 30 minimax frequency points. For 20 minimax points, the RPA correlation energy of GaAs still deviates by
2.2\cdot 10^{-8}
eV/electron from the reference. This error is reduced by two orders of magnitudes for 30 minimax points instead. We note that even an error of
2.2\cdot 10^{-8}
eV/electron is excellent when taking the RI-V error as target accuracy. Minimax grids with 20 grid points can therefore be considered a reliable choice for conventional RPA calculations.

VI
Conclusion

The time-frequency component of the GreenX library
Azizi et al. 2023
, which provides minimax time-frequency grids along with corresponding quadrature weights to compute correlation energies and quasiparticle energies and weights for Fourier transforms, has recently been released. In the present work, we linked the GreenX library to three codes (FHI-aims, CP2K and ABINIT), and performed conventional RPA and low-scaling
GW
calculations for a variety of systems. Our test systems include the molecules of the Thiel and GW100 benchmark sets, four 2D-dimensional semiconductors (TMDC monolayer materials) and seven 3D bulk crystals. We found that the conventional RPA calculations are well converged within
<10^{-7}
eV/electron for minimax grids with 20 points, reducing the computational prefactor by a factor of
\approx 3
