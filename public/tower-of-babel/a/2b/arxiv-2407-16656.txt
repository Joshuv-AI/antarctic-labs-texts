Repeated Block Averages: entropic time and mixing profiles
Pietro Caputo, Matteo Quattropani, Federico Sau

Abstract
. We consider randomized dynamics over the n n -simplex, where at each step a random set, or block , of coordinates is evenly averaged. When all blocks have size 2, this reduces to the repeated averages studied in [ CDSZ22 ] , a version of the averaging process on a graph [ AL12 ] . We study the convergence to equilibrium of this process as a function of the distribution of the block size, and provide sharp conditions for the emergence of the cutoff phenomenon . Moreover, we characterize the size of the cutoff window and provide an explicit Gaussian cutoff profile. To complete the analysis, we study in detail the simplified case where the block size is not random. We show that the absence of a cutoff is equivalent to having blocks of size n Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{\Omega(1)} , in which case we provide a convergence in distribution for the total variation distance at any given time, showing that, on the proper time scale, it remains constantly 1 up to an exponentially distributed random time, after which it decays following a Poissonian profile.

1.
Introduction

The following process is commonly referred to as Repeated Averages. Given a probability vector
\eta=(\eta(1),\dots,\eta(n))
, one step of the dynamics consists in picking two distinct coordinates
x,y
uniformly at random and replacing both
\eta(x)
and
\eta(y)
by
(\eta(x)+\eta(y))/2
. Iteration of this single step defines a discrete time, random dynamical system on the
n
-simplex, converging with high probability to the fixed point given by the uniform distribution
\pi(x)\equiv 1/n
. Despite the simplicity of this process, a precise understanding of its convergence was obtained only very recently in
[
CDSZ22
]
, where the process was shown to exhibit a cutoff phenomenon at time
t=c\,n\log n
, with
c=1/2\log 2
, and with an explicit gaussian mixing profile in a time window
of size
n\sqrt{\log n}
. We refer to
[
CDSZ22
]
for a precise description of the result and for more background.

It is natural to ask what happens when the two–coordinate averaging at each step is replaced by a
k
-coordinate averaging, where
k\geq 2
is an integer. We refer to this as the Repeated Block Average (or, simply, Block Average) process. While an adaptation of the techniques in
[
CDSZ22
]
readily implies that, for fixed
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the analogous cutoff phenomenon takes place at time
t=c\,n\log n
with
c=1/k\log k
, cases where
k
is itself a random variable and/or is allowed to grow with
n
provide a richer phenomenology.

In this paper we develop a robust approach to the Block Average process, which allows us to derive a complete description of the total variation distance from stationarity in such contexts, including sharp criteria for the occurrence of cutoff and explicit descriptions of the mixing profile within the cutoff window in terms of the convolution of two normal random variables. Our approach is undoubtedly inspired by the methods introduced in
[
CDSZ22
]
, but our analysis departs from that work in several key places. In particular, we bypass the discretization technique, a crucial ingredient in the proof of the main results of
[
CDSZ22
]
, by viewing the Block Average process as the projection of a richer fragmentation process, which we call the
pile dynamics
, and which encodes the history of all averaging events.

If the block sizes at each step of the dynamics are i.i.d. copies of an integer random variable
X\geq 2
, we show that whenever cutoff occurs, then it takes place at the
entropic time

(1.1)

t_{\rm ent}=\frac{n\log n}{{\mathbb{E}}[X\log X]}\;\text{.}

Intuitively,

t_{\rm ent}
is the number of steps required by the Block Average process started at a Dirac mass to reach an entropy of order
\log n
.
It can be seen
as the analogue of the entropic time introduced in
[
BCS19
]
in the context of random walks on random graphs. In line with this perspective, we link the occurrence of cutoff to a Weak Law of Large Numbers for
\mu^{-1}\log n
i.i.d. copies of
\log Y
, where
Y
is the size-biased version of the block-size
X
, and
\mu={\mathbb{E}}\big[\log Y\big]
is its mean,
provided that
\mu^{-1}\log n\gg 1
.
We shall refer to this asymptotic inequality, which turns out to be necessary for cutoff to occur, as the
entropic product condition
(Proposition
2.6
and Theorem
2.7
).

We strengthen this result by determining the corresponding cutoff window and Gaussian profile in the case where
\log Y
additionally satisfies a Central Limit Theorem. Interestingly, we describe our profiles as arising from the interplay of two distinct sources of randomness. Roughly speaking, a source of Gaussian fluctuations arises from the randomness of the block sizes, while another stems from the random choice of the elements forming the block (Theorems
2.8
and
2.10
).

When the block size
X
is deterministically equal to
k=k(n)
, we show that cutoff occurs if and only if
k=n^{o(1)}
. In other terms, the product condition
\mu^{-1}\log n\gg 1

mentioned above becomes a necessary and sufficient condition for the occurrence of
cutoff in this case (Corollary
2.12
).
On the other hand, when
k=n^{\Omega(1)}
, the total-variation distance does not necessarily concentrate. We study this case by a suitable multi-scale analysis for Block Averages which successfully captures its limiting Poisson-like profile (Theorem
2.13
).

We end this introduction with some comments on related work.
The two-coordinate average process analyzed in
[
CDSZ22
]
is the complete graph (or mean field) version of the so called Averaging Process on a graph
G=(V,E)
, defined as above with the difference that the two coordinates
x,y
are now picked uniformly at random among the edges of
G
, that is, the unordered pairs
\{x,y\}\in E
. The systematic mathematical study of the Averaging Process on a graph has been initiated in
[
AL12
]
, where some general facts about convergence in
L^{2}
and in relative entropy where obtained. Repeated averages along the edges of a graph represents a natural toy model for stochastic processes on graphs with a richer structure, with remarkable applications to opinion dynamics, consensus finding, gossip algorithms, and even quantum computing, see
[
CDSZ22
,
MSW22
]
for further motivation and background. We also refer to
[
MSW22
]
,
[
QS23
]
,
[
CQS23
]
, and
[
Sau24
]
for further general results and for some mixing analysis on certain specific families of graphs.
The Averaging process can also be studied on hypergraphs, so that a single step now consists in a full average of all the masses on the vertices in a given hyperedge. This point of view was taken in
[
Spi22
]
, where the
L^{2}
convergence was analyzed. We note that the Block Average process introduced above can be seen as a mean field version of the Averaging Process on a hypergraph. Convergence in relative entropy for Block Averages can be deduced from the estimates in
[
BC24
]
.

2.
Model and main results

2.1.
Block Average process

Fix an integer
n\geq 2
, and write
V=[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let
\Delta
denote the set of probability vectors
\eta=(\eta(x))_{x\in V}
. We view
\eta
as a mass distribution over the vertex set
V
. Given
A\subset V
,
\eta\in\Delta
, we write
\varPsi_{A}\eta\in\Delta
for the mass distribution obtained after averaging in
A
,

(2.1)

x\in V\longmapsto\varPsi_{A}\eta(x)=\begin{cases}\textstyle{\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{y\in A}\eta(y)}&\text{if}\ x\in A\\
\eta(x)&\text{else}\;\text{.}\end{cases}

Given a random variable
X
with values in
\{2,\dots,n\}
, let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
denote i.i.d. copies of
X
and let
A_{1},A_{2},\dots
denote independent choices of uniformly random subsets such that
A_{t}\subset V
has size
|A_{t}|=X_{t}
. The
X
-Block Average process is defined as the discrete time evolution
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots
given by

(2.2)

\eta_{t}=\varPsi_{A_{t}}\varPsi_{A_{t-1}}\cdots\varPsi_{A_{1}}\eta\;\text{.}

We consider the total variation distance from stationarity

(2.3)

d_{\rm TV}(t)=\|\eta_{t}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\|v\|_{\rm TV}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in V}|v(x)|
, and
\pi
denotes the uniform distribution
\pi(x)\equiv 1/n
. It is not hard to check that for any initial mass distribution
\eta\in\Delta
, the sequence of random variables
d_{\rm TV}(t)
,
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is almost surely non-increasing and converging to
0
, and that the worst initial condition is obtained by placing all the mass in a single site
x_{0}\in V
; see, e.g.,
[
CQS23
]
. From now on, unless otherwise stated, we assume that the initial state is such a Dirac mass.

Our goal is to quantify this convergence in the limit
n\to\infty
.
To this end, we define the
\varepsilon
-mixing time
of the process as

(2.4)

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)=\inf\{t\in{\mathbb{N}}:\;\mathds{E}\left[d_{\rm TV}(t)\right]<\varepsilon\}\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\mathds{E}
denotes expectation over the Block Average process.
The process is said to exhibit the
cutoff phenomenon
if the mixing time is, to leading order, insensitive of the value of
\varepsilon
, that is, if

(2.5)

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)\sim t_{\rm mix}(1-\varepsilon)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\varepsilon\in(0,1)\;\text{,}\;\;

where, for two positive sequences
a=a(n),b=b(n)
, we write
a\sim b
for
a(n)/b(n)\to 1
.
We shall also adopt the standard asymptotic notation
a\ll b
for
a(n)/b(n)\to 0
, and
a\asymp b
if
a(n)/b(n)
and
b(n)/a(n)
are both bounded. Similarly, we write
a\lesssim b
if
a(n)/b(n)
stays bounded.

2.2.
Entropic time and other timescales

We shall identify the entropic time (
1.1
) as the relevant mixing timescale for Block Averages, regardless of the block size distribution, see Theorem
2.5
.
Before introducing this result, we review and introduce some other relevant timescales for the process.

As in the usual Averaging Process
[
AL12
]
, a simple duality relates the mass distribution
\eta_{t}
to the corresponding random walk.
In our case, a direct computation, see Appendix
A
, shows that if the process is started at the Dirac mass at
x_{0}\in V
, then for all
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in V
,

(2.6)

\mathds{E}[\eta_{t}(x)]=P_{\rm RW}^{t}(x_{0},x)\;\text{,}\;\;

where
P_{\rm RW}
is the transition matrix of the random walk on
V
obtained as follows. If the current state is
x
, the next state
y
is obtained by first sampling an integer
k
distributed as the random variable
X
, then a uniformly random subset
A\subset V
with size
k
, and finally taking
y\in A
uniformly at random if
x\in A
, otherwise
y=x
if
x\notin A
.
It is straightforward to check that
P_{\rm RW}
has one eigenvalue
1
, while all remaining ones are equal to
1-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[X]-1}{n-1}
. In particular, the inverse of the spectral gap,
referred to as the
relaxation time
is given by (see Appendix
A
)

(2.7)

t_{\rm rel}=\frac{n-1}{\mathds{E}[X]-1}\;\text{.}

Observe that
t_{\rm rel}\in[1,n-1]
, and the extremes
1
, and
n-1
are reached only in case
X
is deterministically equal to
n
and
2
, respectively. The relaxation time controls convergence to stationarity of the Block Average process in
L^{2}
norm, see
[
AL12
]

for the usual averaging process on graphs, and see
[
Spi22
]
for the hypergraph version. In our setup, this can be formulated as follows.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any initial distribution
\eta\in\Delta
, the
X
-Block Average process satisfies

(2.8)

\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]=\bigg(1-\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\bigg)^{t}\,\bigg\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\in{\mathbb{N}}\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\|v\|_{2}:=(\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)|v(x)|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
.

For the reader’s convenience, we record the proof of this estimate in the Appendix
A
.
Using Schwarz’ inequality and the fact that
\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}-1\|_{2}^{2}\leq n-1
, Proposition
2.1
implies a preliminary upper bound on mixing times:

(2.9)

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)\leq t_{\rm rel}\,\big(\log n-2\log\varepsilon\big)\ ,\qquad\varepsilon\in(0,1)\ .

An alternative upper bound can be obtained by using relative entropy. Let
t_{\rm ent}
denote the entropic time defined in (
1.1
), and let
D(\eta\|\pi)
denote the relative entropy (or KL divergence) of
\eta\in\Delta
with respect to
\pi
. The following is a direct consequence of
[
BC24
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, all initial
\eta\in\Delta
,

(2.10)

\mathds{E}[D(\eta_{t}\|\pi)]\leq\bigg(1-\frac{1}{t_{\rm ent}}\bigg)^{t}D(\eta\|\pi)\;\text{.}

Proof.

An equivalent formulation of
[
BC24
, Theorem 1.1]
states that if
A_{1}
is a random subset as in (
2.2
), then, for all

\eta\in\Delta
,

(2.11)

\mathds{E}[D(\varPsi_{A_{1}}\eta\|\pi)]\leq(1-\kappa)D(\eta\|\pi)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\kappa=\frac{1}{t_{\rm ent}}\;\text{.}

Iteration of the above estimate yields the conclusion.
∎

Since
D(\eta\|\pi)\leq\log n
, Pinsker inequality and Proposition
2.2
imply the upper bound

(2.12)

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)\leq 2t_{\rm ent}\,\big(\log\log n-\log(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon)\big)\ ,\qquad\varepsilon\in(0,1)\ .

Remark 2.3
.

The entropic time and the relaxation time always satisfy

(2.13)

1\leq\frac{t_{\rm ent}}{t_{\rm rel}}\leq\,\frac{n}{n-1}\frac{\mathds{E}[X]-1}{\mathds{E}[X]}\frac{\log n}{\log{\mathbb{E}}[X]}\ .

Indeed, the first inequality follows by integrating
(X-1)\,n\log n-(n-1)\,X\log X\geq 0
, while the second is
Jensen inequality
\mathds{E}[X\log X]\geq\mathds{E}[X]\log\mathds{E}[X]
.

Following
[
MSW22
]
, relative entropy may be used also to obtain a preliminary lower bound.

Proposition 2.4
.

For all
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, all initial
\eta\in\Delta
,

(2.14)

\mathds{E}[D(\eta_{t}\|\pi)]\geq D(\eta\|\pi)-\frac{t\,\log n}{t_{\rm ent}}\;\text{.}

The proof of Proposition
2.4
is a simple adaptation of the argument in
[
MSW22
]
, and its proof is reported in Appendix
A
.
When
\eta
is a Dirac mass one has
D(\eta\|\pi)=\log n
, so using a reversed Pinsker inequality, Proposition
2.4
yields a lower bound on mixing times:

(2.15)

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)\geq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon\,n}{n-1}\right)t_{\rm ent}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\varepsilon\in(0,1)\;\text{.}

Our first result shows that
t_{\rm ent}
is the right timescale for the decay of the random sequence
d_{\rm TV}(t)
.
In particular, the
lower bound (
2.15
) is rather sharp.

Theorem 2.5
.

For any block size distribution,

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)\asymp t_{\rm ent}
, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

2.3.
Cutoff phenomenon

We now turn our attention to
the occurrence of cutoff, as well as to the identification of the cutoff window and cutoff profile.

We start with a Block Average version of
the well-known
product condition
for reversible Markov chains, stating that if the relaxation time is not much smaller than the mixing time then there is no cutoff, see, e.g.,
[
LP17
, Section 18.3]
.

Proposition 2.6

(Entropic product condition)
.

Cutoff as in (
2.5
) implies
t_{\rm ent}\gg t_{\rm rel}
.

A natural question is whether this entropic product condition is sufficient for cutoff in our context. In the usual Markov chain setting, this equivalence holds for specific classes of Markov chains
[
DLP10
,
BHP17
,
Sal23
]
, but it is not true in general; see, e.g.,
[
LP17
, Example 18.7]
.
Counterexamples to this equivalence for Block Averages, akin to the example in the aforementioned reference, are discussed in Section
8
below. Nevertheless, in Theorem
2.7
, we establish a sufficient condition for cutoff based on a strengthened entropic product condition.

In order to state that, let us introduce one of the key quantities of our analysis.
Let
Y
be the size-biased version of
X
, so that the parameters
\mu=\mathds{E}[\log Y]
and
\sigma^{2}=\var(\log Y)

satisfy

(2.16)

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\mathds{E}[X\log X]}{\mathds{E}[X]}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\sigma^{2}=\frac{\mathds{E}[X(\log X)^{2}]}{\mathds{E}[X]}-\mu^{2}\;\text{.}

With this notation, the entropic time
t_{\rm ent}
in (
1.1
) reads

(2.17)

t_{\rm ent}=\frac{n\log n}{\mathds{E}[X]\,\mu}\;\text{,}\;\;

whereas the entropic product condition
t_{\rm ent}\gg t_{\rm rel}
in Proposition
2.6
becomes

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mu\ll\log(n)\;\text{.}

Our next result shows that this condition is sufficient if combined with a
Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) for the variables
\log Y
. We say that
\log Y
satisfies the WLLN if

(2.19)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathds{P}\bigg(\bigg|\frac{1}{\log n}{\sum_{s=1}^{\mu^{-1}\log n}\left(\log Y_{s}-\mu\right)}\bigg|>\delta\bigg)=0\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\delta>0\;\text{.}

Theorem 2.7

(Cutoff)
.

Assume the entropic product condition (
2.18
) and the WLLN (
2.19
). Then the Block Average process has cutoff,
with
t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)\sim t_{\rm ent}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

In words, Theorem
2.7
states that the abrupt convergence to equilibrium for Block Averages at time
t_{\rm ent}
is parallel to a WLLN — the simplest
concentration phenomenon in probability — for some auxiliary i.i.d. triangular arrays of length
\mu^{-1}\log n
. The next result (Theorem
2.8
) pushes this analogy further, establishing that the minimal assumption

(2.20)

\sigma^{2}\ll{\mu\log n}\;\text{,}\;\;

yields estimates on the size of the cutoff window.
This condition is minimal because, in general,
\sigma^{2}\leq\mu\log n
always holds true (cf. (
2.16
) and
X\leq n
). Note also that, by Chebyshev inequality, (
2.20
) alone clearly implies (
2.19
).

Theorem 2.8

(Cutoff window)
.

Assume (
2.18
) and (
2.20
).
Then, the quantity

(2.21)

t_{\rm w}=\left(1+\frac{\sigma}{\mu}\right)\frac{n\sqrt{\log n}}{\mathds{E}[X]\sqrt{\mu}}

satisfies
t_{\rm w}\ll t_{\rm ent}
, and

(2.22)

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)-t_{\rm mix}(1-\varepsilon)\lesssim t_{\rm w}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})\;\text{.}

Remark 2.9
.

The assumptions in Theorem
2.8
may be equivalently restated as

(2.23)

\displaystyle\frac{t_{\rm ent}}{t_{\rm rel}}\gg 1+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\;\text{,}\;\;

that is, as a strengthened version of the entropic product condition in (
2.18
).

To complete the picture, one is typically interested in capturing the second-order terms of the mixing times or, equivalently, determining the so-called cutoff profile. It turns out that, next to the assumptions of Theorem
2.8
, having a Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for
\log Y
— in place of the WLLN in (
2.19
) — suffices to grant a non-trivial Gaussian profile inside the window
t_{\rm w}
introduced in Theorem
2.8
. To simplify the exposition we consider two separate scenarios, according to whether the size of the blocks
X
is deterministic or not:

•

scenario 1: (
2.18
) holds, and
\sigma\equiv 0

•

scenario 2: (
2.18
) and (
2.20
) hold,
\sigma\neq 0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and the CLT holds:

(2.24)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathds{P}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\log n}}\;\sum_{s=1}^{\mu^{-1}\log n}\frac{\log Y_{s}-\mu}{\sigma}>\beta\bigg)=\Phi(-\beta)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{R}\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\Phi
is the CDF of a standard Gaussian.

Theorem 2.10

(Cutoff profile)
.

Assume either scenario 1 or scenario 2.
Letting
\varrho=\frac{\sigma}{\mu}
,

(2.25)

d_{\rm TV}(t_{\rm ent}+\beta t_{\rm w})-\Phi\bigg(-\beta\frac{1+\varrho}{\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}}}\bigg)\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}0\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{R}\;\text{.}

Here we use the notation
\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}
for convergence in probability. We also note that, as usual, all quantities in the above expressions (like
\mu,\sigma,\varrho
) are allowed to vary with
n
, and we do not necessarily assume convergence of the sequence
\Phi\big(-\beta(1+\varrho)/\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}}\big)
.

Figure 2.1.

The cutoff profile in (
2.25
) for three values of
\varrho
, namely
\varrho=0,1,5
(in blue, red and green, respectively).

Remark 2.11
.

In view of Feller’s theorem (see, e.g.,
[
AL06
, Theorem 11.1.5]
), simple manipulations show that, under (
2.18
) and (
2.20
), the CLT assumption in (
2.24
) is equivalent to the following Lindeberg condition for the triangular array
\log Y
:

(2.26)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\log Y-\mu}{\sigma}\bigg)^{2}\,{\bf 1}_{\left|\frac{\log Y-\mu}{\sigma}\right|>\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\mu}}}\bigg]=0\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\delta>0\;\text{.}

As already anticipated in the introduction, the factor
\varrho
appearing in the cutoff profile

(2.27)

\beta\in\mathbb{R}\longmapsto\Psi_{\varrho}(\beta)=\Phi\bigg(-\beta\frac{1+\varrho}{\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}}}\bigg)\in[0,1]\;\text{,}\;\;

accounts for the competition of two sources of fluctuations, one from the block sizes and one from the randomness of the block composition. In particular, when
\varrho=0
or
\infty
, only one of the two fluctuations asymptotically survives. Note that the factor
\varrho
crucially enters also in the definition of the cutoff window
t_{\rm w}
as defined in (
2.21
).

It is worth to point out the symmetry of the profile with respect to
\varrho
, that is,

(2.28)

\Psi_{\varrho}(\beta)=\Psi_{\varrho^{-1}}(\beta)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{R}\;\text{.}

As a consequence of this symmetry and the monotonicity of
\varrho\in[0,1]\mapsto(1+\varrho)/{\sqrt{{1+\varrho^{2}}}}
, the curve associated to
\varrho=0
is above (resp. below) the one associated to
\varrho=1
on the positive (resp. negative) half-line, whereas all other curves for
\varrho\not\in\{0,1,\infty\}
are sandwiched between these two; see Figure
2.1
.

2.4.
Deterministic block sizes

When the block size
X
(and, thus,
\log Y
) is deterministic,
Proposition
2.6
and Theorem
2.10
(assuming scenario 1) yield a full characterization of cutoff – including the cutoff profile – in terms of the entropic product condition only, as summarized by the following corollary. Note that, in the deterministic case

X\equiv k
, one has

(2.29)

t_{\rm rel}=\frac{n-1}{k-1}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t_{\rm ent}=\frac{n\log n}{k\log k}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t_{\rm w}=\frac{n\sqrt{\log n}}{k\sqrt{\log k}}\;\text{.}

Corollary 2.12

(Cutoff for deterministic sizes)
.

Assume
X\equiv k
, for some sequence
k=k(n)
. Then, the following conditions are equivalent:

•

\log k\ll\log n
;

•

cutoff occurs at time
t_{\rm ent}
and, further,

(2.30)

d_{\rm TV}(t_{\rm ent}+\beta t_{\rm w})\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}\Phi(-\beta)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{R}\;\text{.}

In particular, Corollary
2.12
includes the main result of
[
CDSZ22
]
, which corresponds to
X\equiv 2
.

We now turn to the analysis of mixing in the non-cutoff regime, in case of deterministic block sizes
X\equiv k
. By Corollary
2.12
, we, thus, consider the case where
k=n^{\delta}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
.

As usual, the initial mass
\eta\in\Delta
is assumed to be a Dirac delta at a single site
x_{0}\in V
. The next result shows that after the first random time at which the initial mass is updated, the total variation distance concentrates on the timescale
n/k
around the survival function of a Poisson process of unit rate.

Theorem 2.13

(Non-cutoff for deterministic sizes)
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
satisfying
\delta^{-1}\notin\mathbb{N}
, and take
X\equiv k
, with
k=n^{\delta}
.
Set
\bar{t}(\beta)={\beta n/k}
, for
\beta\geq 0
, and define the random time

(2.31)

\tau_{\rm start}=\inf\{t\geq 1:\;x_{0}\in A_{t}\}\;\text{.}

Then,

(2.32)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+\bar{t}(\beta))\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}\Pr\left(\tau_{\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor}>\beta\right)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\beta\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\tau_{j}
is the
j
-th arrival time of a Poisson process of unit rate.

t\mapsto d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+t)
in a single realization with
n=10^{7}
and

(right), up to the time

Remark 2.14
.

The assumption

is not of purely technical nature, and the reason of its appearance
will be further discussed in Section

; see
Remark

.

Since

\tau_{\rm start}
is geometrically distributed with mean
n/k
, we can restate (
2.32
) as a convergence in distribution for the random variable
d_{\rm TV}(t)
, that is,

(2.33)

d_{\rm TV}(\bar{t}(\beta))\overset{\rm d}{\longrightarrow}\mathds{1}_{S\geq\beta}+\mathds{1}_{S<\beta}\Pr\left(\tau_{\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor}>\beta-S\right)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\beta\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;

where
S\overset{\rm d}{=}{\rm Exp}(1)
is a unit rate exponential random variable, independent of
\tau_{\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor}
. Finally, taking expectations in (
2.33
), we readily get

(2.34)

\mathds{E}[d_{\rm TV}(\bar{t}(\beta))]\longrightarrow\Pr\big(\tau_{\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor+1}>\beta\big)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\beta\geq 0\;\text{.}

In words, the above results
state that the mixing of the process is articulated into two distinct phases. At first, mixing needs to be triggered, by redistributing the unit mass initially placed in a single site. This first time
\tau_{\rm start}
is random, and approximately
exponentially distributed with mean
n/k
. After this triggering time, the distance starts to decay (again on timescales
n/k
), now in a deterministic way, following a Poissonian profile. A similar two-phase decay has been recently observed on a related redistribution model
[
AHHN24
]
.

3.
Piles & infinitesimal chunks

In this section, we introduce the central objects employed in the proofs of our main results. Specifically, we present a richer representation of the block-average dynamics in terms of
piles
and
infinitesimal chunks
, rather than just the mass distribution
\eta_{t}
.
Following the evolution of sufficiently many of these piles will be key to measuring the flatness of the mass distribution. The notion of piles and chunks is introduced in Section
3.1
. In Sections
3.2
and
3.3
, we characterize the explicit distribution and corresponding asymptotic identities for the size of what we refer to as a “typical pile”. These findings are exploited and refined in later sections.

3.1.
Pile-chunk dynamics

The original process is characterized by the variables
\eta_{t}(x)
,
t\geq 0,x\in V
, where
\eta_{t}(x)
denotes the mass at vertex
x
at time
t
, with the initial distribution
\eta_{0}
given by the Dirac mass
{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}
at some
x_{0}\in V
. A finer description is obtained by keeping track of the distinct mass fragments produced by each averaging event, so that the mass
\eta_{t}(x)
is given by the sum of the masses of all fragments present at
x
at time
t
. These fragments are called
piles
, and their evolution can be formally defined as follows.
At time zero there is only one pile at
x_{0}\in V
, with mass equal to
\eta_{0}(x_{0})=1
. Whenever there is an averaging event at the block
A\subset V
with
|A|=k
, for every
x\in A
, each pile present at
x
is independently split into
k
labeled piles of equal mass, and the
k
new piles are assigned to the sites
y\in A
using a uniformly random permutation of
\{1,\dots,k\}
. In this way, at each time
t
, for each
x\in V
, if
\eta_{t}(x)>0
, then there is a finite number of piles at
x
, whose total mass equals
\eta_{t}(x)
, which we denote
(\zeta_{t}^{(1)}(x),\zeta_{t}^{(2)}(x),\ldots,\zeta_{t}^{(L)}(x))
, for some
L=L_{t}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
.
We refer to this new process
(\zeta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as the
pile dynamics
. Thus, letting
|\zeta_{t}^{(i)}(x)|\in[0,1]
denote the
size
of the pile
\zeta_{t}^{(i)}(x)
, at any time
t\geq 0
one has

(3.1)

\sum_{i}|\zeta_{t}^{(i)}(x)|=\eta_{t}(x)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;x\in V\,.

We further
enrich the pile dynamics
(\zeta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by adding a mark to one of the piles, which we interpret as the evolution
of an “infinitesimal chunk of mass”,
and which we denote by
U
.
The mark
U
belongs initially to the unique pile at
x_{0}
. At each averaging event at the block
A\subset V
with
|A|=k
, if the mark belongs to a pile sitting at one of the vertices of
A
, then the mark chooses uniformly at random one of the
k
fragments into which its own pile is split and sticks to it until the next averaging event. When the mark belongs to a pile that is not involved in the averaging, the mark does not move. Thus, at any time
t\geq 0
, the infinitesimal chunk
U
belongs to a single pile of the system, denoted, with slight abuse of notation,

\zeta_{t}(U)
, and has
a
position
, referred to as
e_{t}(U)\in V
, namely the vertex at which the pile
\zeta_{t}(U)
sits.
We also refer to
\zeta_{t}(U)
as the marked pile, or the
typical pile
.

It follows from the above construction that the evolution of the position
e_{t}(U)
of the infinitesimal chunk
U
, if we ignore all other variables, is simply the random walk with transition matrix
P_{\rm RW}
introduced in (
2.6
). Furthermore, if we condition on a realization of all the block updates, at each time
t\geq 0
, by construction the probability that
U
is at some
x\in V
at time
t
coincides with
\eta_{t}(x)
, i.e.,
\mathds{P}
-a.s.,

(3.2)

{\mathsf{P}}_{U}(e_{t}(U)=x)=\eta_{t}(x)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;x\in V\;\text{,}\;\;

where
{\mathsf{P}}_{U}
denotes the quenched law of the evolution of the infinitesimal chunk
U
.
We shall write
\mathsf{E}_{U}
for the corresponding expectation. Taking the
\mathds{E}
-expectation on both sides of (
3.2
), that is averaging over all block updates, we recover the duality relation (
2.6
).

The analysis of the evolution of the size
|\zeta_{t}(U)|
of the marked pile will be another key ingredient in our proofs. Specifically, we shall fully exploit the following identity

(3.3)

\mathds{P}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|\in\mathcal{A})=\mathds{E}\left[|\eta_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}|\right]\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;\mathcal{A}\subset[0,1]\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\eta_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}
is defined as the mass configuration obtained from
\eta_{t}
by considering only the piles
with size in
\mathcal{A}
, and
|\eta_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}|
stands for its total mass
\leq 1
. This is easily derived by taking the expectation with respect to
\mathds{P}
of the quenched identity

(3.4)

{\mathsf{P}}_{U}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|\in\mathcal{A})=|\eta_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}|\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;\mathcal{A}\subset[0,1]\;\text{.}

Indeed, by the definition of the marked pile dynamics, the probability that
U
is in a pile of a given size coincides with the total amount of mass in piles of that size. More generally, we combine (
3.2
) and (
3.4
) to obtain, e.g.,

(3.5)

{\mathsf{P}}_{U}(e_{t}(U)=x\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|\in\mathcal{A}\,,\,\eta_{t}\in\mathcal{B})={\bf 1}\{\eta_{t}\in\mathcal{B}\}\,\eta_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}(x)\;\text{,}\;\;

where
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{A}\subset[0,1]
,
\mathcal{B}\subset\Delta
, and
x\in V
.
An identity of this type allows us to follow selected portions of the mass evolving via block averages, depending on the size of the piles and their locations. As an example of its applications, see, e.g., (
4.13
) in Section
4.2
.

Moreover, we will gain refined information by following several infinitesimal chunks
U,U^{\prime},U^{\prime\prime}
, etc., and their piles at the same time. Their dynamics is defined as follows. All chunks belong initially to the same pile at
x_{0}
, and when an average event occurs with block
A
,
the chunks
independently
– albeit simultaneously – uniformly choose one among the newly generated sub-piles, and stick to it until the next averaging event. It is worth to mention that, as soon as different infinitesimal chunks join different piles (and, thus, relocate to different sites), they will never sit on the same pile at later times. This, however, does not prevent them to have the same position later on. In formulas, this means that
\zeta_{t}(U)=\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})
if and only if
\zeta_{s}(U)=\zeta_{s}(U^{\prime})
for all
s\leq t
, whereas
e_{t}(U)=e_{t}(U^{\prime})
does not necessarily imply
\zeta_{t}(U)=\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})
. We employ this approach in several places below; see, e.g., (
4.14
), Lemma
4.1
, Lemma
7.6
. In analogy with the notation used for a single mark, we will write, e.g.,
\mathsf{P}_{U,U^{\prime},U^{\prime\prime}}
and
\mathsf{E}_{U,U^{\prime},U^{\prime\prime}}
for the joint quenched law and expectation of the chunks
U,U^{\prime},U^{\prime\prime}
.

We remark that this pile-chunk representation may be viewed as a more flexible version of the “particle model” introduced in
[
CDSZ22
, Definition 2.5]
. Roughly speaking, the authors in
[
CDSZ22
]
start by first discretizing the mass by forming a finite number of equal-sized
piles, and then consider their evolution in a fashion similar to the one described above, with the important difference that piles which are either too small or that meet other piles coming from different locations get discarded.
These two operations are crucial when dealing with such a discretized version of the mass average dynamics. As we will show, our continuous pile-chunk dynamics avoids any discretization issue, and actually allows us to track the pile dynamics even when meeting events between piles occur. This will play a prominent role in Section
7
, where we consider block updates of such large size that cutoff is destroyed, and yet we are still able to identity the mixing profile.

3.2.
Distribution of the size of a typical pile

Consider an infinitesimal chunk
U
as described in the previous section.
Since our blocks are chosen uniformly at random and independently, the size
|\zeta_{t}(U)|
of its pile has a rather explicit distribution:

(3.6)

|\zeta_{t}(U)|\overset{\rm d}{=}\prod_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}^{-1}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 1\;\text{,}\;\;

where, letting
X_{s}
denote the size of the block chosen at step
s
, the random variables
(Z_{s})_{s\geq 1}
are i.i.d. with law given by

(3.7)

\mathds{P}(Z_{s}=1\mid X_{s})=1-\frac{X_{s}}{n}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\mathds{P}(Z_{s}=X_{s}\mid X_{s})=\frac{X_{s}}{n}\;\text{.}

Indeed,
Z_{s}=1
corresponds to the event “the uniformly-chosen block of size
X_{s}
did not involve the chunk
U
”, while
Z_{s}>1
encodes the information that the update of size
X_{s}
did hit the site
e_{s}(U)\in V
and, thus, split the pile
\zeta_{s}(U)
into
Z_{s}=X_{s}
sub-piles.
This readily implies (
3.6
) and (
3.7
).

Thus,
for all
t\geq 1
and
\theta\in(0,1)
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\big(|\zeta_{t}(U)|>\theta\big)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\log Z_{s}<-\log\theta\bigg)\;\text{,}\;\;

and part of our work will go into deriving
sharp asymptotics for the probability on the right-hand side.
For this purpose, let
T=T(t)
be the number of successful splittings, i.e.,

(3.9)

T=T(t):=\#\{s\leq t:Z_{s}>1\}\in\{0,1,\ldots,t\}\;\text{,}\;\;

which satisfies

(3.10)

T\overset{\rm d}{=}{\rm Bin}(t,\mathds{P}(Z_{1}>1))\overset{\rm d}{=}{\rm Bin}\bigg(t,\frac{\mathds{E}[X]}{n}\bigg)\;\text{.}

Further, let us define
Y_{s}:=Z_{s}\mid\{Z_{s}>1\}
, whose law is nothing but the size-biased distribution of
X_{s}
, that is, letting
p_{X}(k):=\mathds{P}(X_{s}=k)
, we have

(3.11)

p_{Y}(k):=\mathds{P}(Y_{s}=k)=\mathds{P}(Z_{s}=k\mid Z_{s}>1)=\frac{\mathds{P}(X_{s}=k)\frac{k}{n}}{\sum_{k=2}^{n}\mathds{P}(X_{s}=k)\frac{k}{n}}=\frac{k\,p_{X}(k)}{\mathds{E}[X]}\;\text{,}\;\;

for all
k\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
.
Hence, we obtain

(3.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\log(Z_{s})<-\log\theta\bigg)&=\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{T}\log Z_{s}<{-\log\theta}\,\bigg|\,Z_{s}>1\,,\,\forall\,s\leq T\bigg)\\
&=\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{T}\log Y_{s}<{-\log\theta}\bigg)\;\text{.}\end{aligned}

By collecting the above steps and
observing that, conditional to
T=T(t)\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, the variables
(Y_{s})_{s\geq 1}
are all i.i.d. and distributed as in (
3.11
), we just proved the following crucial result.

Lemma 3.1

(Size of a typical pile)
.

For all
t\geq 1
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, we have

(3.13)

\mathds{P}\big(|\zeta_{t}(U)|>\theta\big)=\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{T}\log Y_{s}<-\log\theta\bigg)\;\text{,}\;\;

where
T\overset{\rm d}{=}{\rm Bin}(t,\frac{\mathds{E}[X]}{n})
,
(Y_{s})_{s\geq 1}
are i.i.d. with distribution
p_{Y}
given in (
3.11
), and all random variables are mutually independent.

If one believes the heuristics
that the system is at equilibrium as soon as the typical pile has size
\approx\frac{1}{n}
, that is

(3.14)

t_{\rm mix}\approx\inf\left\{t\geq 0\mid\mathds{P}\left(|\zeta_{t}(U)|\approx\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\approx 1\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;

then Lemma
3.1
provides an efficient tool to guess the mixing time simply via a LLN for
\log Y
. Furthermore, in the cutoff regime, by controlling the fluctuations of
\log Y
(that is, having a CLT), one may hope that this same heuristics could yield a candidate for the size of the window, and, possibly even the corresponding profile. In the rest of the paper, we turn the heuristics in (
3.14
) into a rigorous framework. Moreover, we show how a Gaussian profile, with a variance depending on the interplay between the fluctuations of
T
and
\log Y
, arises in presence of cutoff.

Remark 3.2
.

Analogues of Lemma
3.1
, and of the heuristics in (
3.14
), appear in other works on random walks on random graphs
[
BHS17
,
BCS18
,
BCS19
]
, as well as in
[
CDSZ22
]
, with some remarkable differences. In
[
BHS17
,
BCS18
,
BCS19
]
,
Y_{s}^{-1}
is the (out-)degree of a random vertex sampled with probability proportional to its (in-)degrees, but
T\equiv t
deterministically. In
[
CDSZ22
]
,
T\overset{\rm d}{=}{\rm Bin}(t,\frac{2}{n})
, but
\log Y\equiv\log 2
deterministically.

2.8

The first lemma

.

Assume (

\psi=\gamma\sqrt{\mu\log n}
with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Thanks to the assumption (
2.20
), the expression in (

) is estimated, for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large and some

, by

(3.33)

\displaystyle f_{n}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{m(\alpha)}(\log Y_{s}-\mu)<-\left(\beta\left(1+\frac{\sigma}{\mu}\right)+\alpha+\gamma\right)\sqrt{\mu\log n}-\xi\bigg)

(3.34)

\displaystyle f_{n}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{m(\alpha)}(\log Y_{s}-\mu)<-\left(\beta\left(1+\frac{\sigma}{\mu}\right)+\alpha+\gamma\right)\sqrt{\mu\log n}+\xi\bigg)\;\text{.}

By Chebyshev inequality, we further get

(3.35)

\displaystyle f_{n}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{8}{\beta^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\frac{\sigma}{\mu}}{1+\frac{\sigma}{\mu}}\bigg)^{2}\geq 1-\frac{8}{\beta^{2}}\;\text{,}\;\;

\displaystyle\beta<-2(\alpha+\gamma)

(3.36)

\displaystyle f_{n}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8}{\beta^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\frac{\sigma}{\mu}}{1+\frac{\sigma}{\mu}}\bigg)^{2}\leq\frac{8}{\beta^{2}}\;\text{,}\;\;

\displaystyle\beta>2(\alpha+\gamma)\;\text{.}

Hence, taking
\beta\to-\infty
and
\beta\to\infty
, respectively, yields the desired claim for this case.

Case of Lemma
3.5
.
Let

(3.37)

g_{n}(\alpha)\coloneqq\begin{cases}{\bf 1}\{\alpha+\beta+\gamma<0\}&\text{if}\ \sigma=0\\
\Phi(-\mu/\sigma\left(\alpha+\beta\left(1+\sigma/\mu\right)+\gamma\right))&\text{if}\ \sigma>0\;\text{.}\end{cases}

By setting

Hence, the CLT assumption in (

This concludes the proof of the third case. ∎

We turn to a discussion of general upper and lower bounds for
\mathds{E}[d_{\rm TV}(t)]
,
which will be used several times in our proofs. The upper bound is based on an idea from
[
CDSZ22
]
, while the lower bound uses explicitly

3.4.
A general upper bound for
\mathds{E}[d_{\rm TV}(t)]

Given a fixed time
t
, we consider a sub-probability distribution
\tilde{\eta}_{t}\leq\eta_{t}
, obtained by removing some of the piles from the current configuration
\eta_{t}
. The precise definition of
\tilde{\eta}_{t}
will be specified in the applications and it is not important at this stage.
Next, we let the system evolve from time
t
to time
t+s
, for some fixed time
s
, with initial distribution
\tilde{\eta}_{t}
.
This can be coupled to
\eta_{t+s}
by using the same updates in the time interval
[t,t+s]
.
By the triangle inequality and the fact that
|\eta_{r}|=1
for all
r\geq 0
, we get

(3.41)

\displaystyle\|\eta_{t+s}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sum_{x\in V}\left|\eta_{t+s}(x)-\frac{1}{n}-\left(\tilde{\eta}_{t+s}(x)-\frac{|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|}{n}\right)\right|+\sum_{x\in V}\left|\tilde{\eta}_{t+s}(x)-\frac{|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|}{n}\right|\right\}

(3.42)

\displaystyle=1-|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|+|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t+s}}{|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|}-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}\leq 1-|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|+\frac{|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|}{2}\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t+s}}{|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|\pi}-1\right\|_{2}\;\text{.}

Taking expectation on both sides, letting
\mathcal{F}_{t}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra up to time
t
, Jensen inequalities and the
L^{2}
-bound in Proposition
2.1
yield

(3.43)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\|\eta_{t+s}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}]

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathds{E}[|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|]+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathds{E}\bigg[|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t+s}}{|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\bigg]\bigg]

(3.44)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathds{E}[|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|]+\frac{1}{2}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\bigg)^{s/2}\mathds{E}\bigg[|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|\bigg\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t}}{|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}\bigg]

(3.45)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathds{E}[|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|]+\frac{e^{-s/(2t_{\rm rel})}}{2}\,n\,\mathds{E}\Big[\max_{x\in V}\tilde{\eta}_{t}(x)\Big]\ .

For later reference, we rephrase the above argument in terms of a lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

For all initial sub-distributions
0\leq\xi\leq\eta_{0}
and times
s\geq 0
, we have

(3.46)

\mathds{E}[\|\eta_{s}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}]\leq 1-\mathds{E}[|\xi|]+\exp\left(-s/2t_{\rm rel}\right)n\,\mathds{E}\left[\|\xi\|_{\infty}\right]\ .

3.5.
A general lower bound for
\mathds{E}[d_{\rm TV}(t)]

Consider the process
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and the corresponding pile dynamics
(\zeta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. For a fixed time
t\geq 0
,
a>1
, let
w_{t}=w_{t}^{a}
denote the mass distribution
obtained after the removal of all piles having mass smaller than
\frac{a}{n}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For all
t\geq 0
,
a>1
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, almost surely

(3.47)

\|\eta_{t}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\geq{\bf 1}\{|w_{t}|>1-\varepsilon\}\left(1-\varepsilon-a^{-1}\right)\;\text{.}

Moreover,

(3.48)

\mathds{E}[\|\eta_{t}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}]\geq\big(1-\varepsilon^{-1}\mathds{P}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{a}{n})\big)(1-\varepsilon-a^{-1})\;\text{.}

Proof.

Note that

(3.49)

\|\eta_{t}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}=\sum_{x\in V}[\eta_{t}(x)-\tfrac{1}{n}]_{+}\geq\sum_{x\in V}[w_{t}(x)-\tfrac{1}{n}]_{+}=|w_{t}|-\tfrac{1}{n}|\{y\mid w_{t}(y)\geq\tfrac{a}{n}\}.

On the other hand,

|\{y\mid w_{t}(y)\geq\tfrac{a}{n}\}|\leq\frac{n}{a}
.
Thus, restricting to the event
|w_{t}|>1-\varepsilon
yields the claim (
3.47
).
Next, by Markov inequality, and using
(
3.3
),

(3.50)

\mathds{P}(|w_{t}|>1-\varepsilon)\geq 1-\frac{1-\mathds{E}[|w_{t}|]}{\varepsilon}=1-\frac{\mathds{P}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{a}{n})}{\varepsilon}\,.

Taking expectations on both sides of (
3.47
) we obtain (
3.48
).
∎

In this section we prove Theorem

4.1.
Proof of lower bound in Theorem
2.5

Recall from (
2.17
) the definition of
t_{\rm ent}
, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
. By applying the general lower bound in (
3.48
) with
t=\delta t_{\rm ent}
and
a=\varepsilon^{-1}>1
, we have

(4.1)

\mathds{E}[\|\eta_{t}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}]\geq\big(1-\varepsilon^{-1}\mathds{P}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon n})\big)\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\;\text{.}

To bound the above probability, we employ the result in Lemma
3.1
. Let
T\sim{\rm Bin}(t,\mathds{E}[X]/n)
and, independently, let
(Y_{s})_{s\geq 1}
be i.i.d. with
Y_{s}\sim p_{Y}
.
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon n})

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{T}\log Y_{s}>\log n+\log\varepsilon\bigg)\leq\sum_{m=0}^{t}\mathds{P}(T=m)\,\frac{m\mathds{E}[\log Y]}{\log n+\log\varepsilon}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{E}[T]\mathds{E}[\log Y]}{\log n+\log\varepsilon}=\delta\,\frac{\log n}{\log n+\log\varepsilon}\;\text{,}\;\;

where we used Markov inequality and the definition

.
Therefore, the desired result follows by taking first

\delta\to 0
, and finally
\varepsilon\to 0
.

4.2.
Proof of upper bound in Theorem
2.5

Fix
t=at_{\rm ent}
,
w=\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\mu}}\in[1,\sqrt{\log n}]
, and define

t_{\star}(a,b)=t+bt_{\rm rel}w=at_{\rm ent}+bt_{\rm rel}w

for some
b>0
. Observe that, since

\frac{n\log(n)}{\mu\mathds{E}[X]}=t_{\rm ent}\geq\frac{1}{2}t_{\rm rel}w=\frac{1}{2}\frac{n-1}{\mathds{E}[X]-1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(n)}{\mu}}\;\text{,}\;\;

and therefore, for all constant
a,b>0
,
t_{\star}(a,b)\asymp t_{\rm ent}
.

Let
\eta_{t}^{*}
be the restriction of
\eta_{t}
to those piles of mass smaller than
\frac{1}{n}
. Define
S(k):=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\log(Y_{s})
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By Lemma
3.1
for all
a>0
large enough,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[|\eta_{t}^{*}|]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n})=\mathds{P}\left(S(T)>\log(n)\right)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left(S(\lceil\tfrac{a\log(n)}{2\mu}\rceil)>\log(n)\;\text{,}\;\;T>\tfrac{a\log(n)}{2\mu}\right)

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(S(\lceil\tfrac{a\log(n)}{2\mu}\rceil)>\log(n)\right)\,\mathds{P}\left(T>\tfrac{a\log(n)}{2\mu}\right)\;\text{.}

Note that the second probability on the right-hand side of (
4.6
) goes to
1
provided
a>0
is large enough.
Similarly, the first probability on the right-hand side of (
4.6
)
satisfies, for any
a>6
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(S(\lceil\tfrac{a\log(n)}{2\mu}\rceil)>\log(n)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathds{P}\left(\left|S(\lceil\tfrac{a\log(n)}{2\mu}\rceil)-\tfrac{a}{2}\log(n)\right|\geq\tfrac{a}{3}\log(n)\right)

(4.8)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{2a\mu\log(n)}\;\text{.}

Since
\sigma^{2}\leq\mu\log(n)
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, taking
a=a(\varepsilon)
large enough,

(4.9)

\mathds{E}[|\eta_{t}^{*}|]=\mathds{P}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n})\geq 1-\varepsilon\;\text{.}

Next, for some
c>0
, define

(4.10)

\tilde{\eta}_{t}(x):=\eta_{t}^{*}(x)\mathds{1}\{\eta_{t}^{*}(x)\leq\tfrac{e^{cw}}{n}\}\ ,

and note that with this definition of
\tilde{\eta}_{t}
, by Lemma
3.7
we have

(4.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathds{E}[\|\eta_{t_{\star}(a,b)}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}]&\leq 1-\mathds{E}[|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|]+\exp\left(-bw/2+cw\right)\\
&=1-\mathds{E}[|\eta_{t}^{*}|]+\mathds{E}[|\eta_{t}^{*}(B)|]+\exp\left(-b\tfrac{w}{2}+cw\right)\;\text{,}\;\;\end{aligned}

where
B:=\{x\in V\mid\eta_{t}^{*}(x)>\frac{e^{cw}}{n}\}
, so that

(4.12)

\mathds{E}[|\tilde{\eta}_{t}|]=\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}\big[\eta_{t}^{*}(x)\mathds{1}\{\eta_{t}^{*}(x)\leq\tfrac{e^{cw}}{n}\}\big]=\mathds{E}[|\eta_{t}^{*}|]-\mathds{E}[\eta_{t}^{*}(B)]\;\text{.}

By (
4.9
) and (
4.11
), it suffices to prove
\mathds{E}[\eta_{t}^{*}(B)]\to 0
after taking the limits
n\to\infty
and, then,
c\to\infty
. To this end, we crucially exploit the identity (
3.5
) for the pile-chunk dynamics. We have

(4.13)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}^{*}(B)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{U}(e_{t}(U)\in B\,,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{U}(\eta_{t}^{*}(e_{t}(U))>\tfrac{e^{cw}}{n}\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{U}\big[{\bf 1}\{\eta_{t}^{*}(e_{t}(U))>\tfrac{e^{cw}}{n}\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n}\}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}\mathsf{E}_{U}\big[{\bf 1}\{\eta_{t}^{*}(x)>\tfrac{e^{cw}}{n}\,,\,e_{t}(U)=x\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n}\}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}{\bf 1}\{\eta_{t}^{*}(x)>\tfrac{e^{cw}}{n}\}\,\mathsf{P}_{U}\big(e_{t}(U)=x\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n}\big)\;\text{.}

By taking expectation in (
4.13
), applying Markov inequality, and reasoning as in Section
3.1
, we then deduce

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\eta_{t}^{*}(B)]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{e^{cw}}\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}\big[\eta_{t}^{*}(x)\,\mathsf{P}_{U}\big(e_{t}(U)=x\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{e^{cw}}\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}\big[\mathsf{P}_{U,U^{\prime}}(e_{t}(U)=e_{t}(U^{\prime})=x\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n}\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})|<\tfrac{1}{n})\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{e^{cw}}\,\mathds{P}\big(e_{t}(U)=e_{t}(U^{\prime})\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\tfrac{1}{n}\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})|<\tfrac{1}{n}\big)\;\text{.}

We now estimate the above probability.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
t\geq 0
,
\theta\in(0,1)
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large, we have

(4.15)

\mathds{P}\big(e_{t}(U)=e_{t}(U^{\prime})\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\theta\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})|<\theta\big)\leq\theta+\frac{1}{n}\;\text{.}

Proof.

First, observe that, under the event
\{|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\theta\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})|<\theta\}
, the probability that
U
and
U^{\prime}
sit on the same pile is at most
\theta
. Indeed,

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\zeta_{t}(U)=\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\theta\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})|<\theta\right)

(4.17)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(\zeta_{t}(U)=\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\theta\right)

(4.18)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}[{\bf 1}\{|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\theta\}\mathds{P}(\zeta_{t}(U)=\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})\mid|\zeta_{t}(U)|)]

(4.19)

\displaystyle\leq\theta\,\mathds{P}(|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\theta)\leq\theta\;\text{.}

Hence, up to errors of size
\theta
and recalling that initially we have
\{e_{0}(U)=e_{0}(U^{\prime})\}
, the probability that the event
\{e_{t}(U)=e_{t}(U^{\prime})\}
occurs is given by the probability of the following event: there exists a time
r\in[1,t)
at which the two chunks go to different sites, there exists a time
s\in[r+1,t]
in which they sit on the same vertex for the first time after
r
, and then we have
\{e_{\ell}(U)=e_{\ell}(U^{\prime})\,,\,\forall\ell\in[s,t]\}
. In formulas, this means

(4.20)

\mathds{P}\big(e_{t}(U)=e_{t}(U^{\prime})\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\theta\,,\,|\zeta_{t}(U^{\prime})|<\theta\big)\leq\theta+\mathds{P}(\exists\,s\leq t\ \text{s.t.}\ \mathcal{M}_{s-1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{M}_{s,t})\;\text{,}\;\;

where we define the events

\mathcal{M}_{r}\coloneqq\{e_{r}(U)=e_{r}(U^{\prime})\}
, and, for
s<t
,

\mathcal{M}_{s,t}\coloneqq\cap_{r=s}^{t}\,\mathcal{M}_{r}
. We now estimate the probability on the right-hand side of (
4.20
). A union bound yields

(4.21)

\mathds{P}(\exists\,s\leq t\ \text{s.t.}\ \mathcal{M}_{s-1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{M}_{s,t})\leq\sum_{s\leq t}\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s-1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{M}_{s,t})\ .

Furthermore, by the Markov property,

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s-1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{M}_{s,t})

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s+1,t}\mid\mathcal{M}_{s})\,\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s}\mid\mathcal{M}_{s-1}^{c})\,\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s-1}^{c})

(4.23)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s+1,t}\mid\mathcal{M}_{s})\,\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s}\mid\mathcal{M}_{s-1}^{c})

(4.24)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s+1,t}\mid\mathcal{M}_{s})\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\frac{k(k-1)}{n(n-1)}\frac{1}{k}

(4.25)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s+1,t}\mid\mathcal{M}_{s})\,\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\ ,\qquad s<t\ ,

where the second-to-last step follows from the fact that the probability of selecting a
k
-block containing the two vertices
e_{s-1}(U)\neq e_{s-1}(U^{\prime})
is
\binom{n-2}{k-2}/\binom{n}{k}=\frac{k\left(k-1\right)}{n\left(n-1\right)}
, while
\frac{1}{k}
is the probability of having them sitting on the same site after they have been both involved in a
k
-block update. Analogously, we have

(4.26)

\mathds{P}\left(\mathcal{M}_{t-1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{M}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\ .

By the Markov property, stationarity and
e_{0}(U)=e_{0}(U^{\prime})
,

(4.27)

\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{s+1,t}\mid\mathcal{M}_{s})=\prod_{u=s}^{t-1}\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{u+1}\mid\mathcal{M}_{u})=\left(\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{1})\right)^{t-s}\;\text{.}

Moreover,

(4.28)

\mathds{P}(\mathcal{M}_{1})=\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\left\{\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)+\left(\frac{k}{n}\frac{1}{k}\right)\right\}=1-\frac{n}{n-1}\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\leq 1-\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\;\text{.}

Summarizing,

(4.29)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(\exists\,s\leq t\ \text{s.t.}\ \mathcal{M}_{s-1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{M}_{s,t})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\sum_{s\leq t}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\right)^{t-s}\leq\frac{1}{n}\ .

This and (
4.20
) yield the desired result.
∎

(6.57)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathds{E}\bigg[\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{zn}\bigg|(X_{s})_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}\bigg)\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{z^{\prime}n}\bigg|(X_{s})_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}\bigg)\bigg]\\
&\qquad\sim\mathds{E}\bigg[\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{zn}\bigg|(X_{s})_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}\bigg)\bigg]\mathds{E}\bigg[\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{z^{\prime}n}\bigg|(X_{s})_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}\bigg)\bigg]\\
&\qquad\sim\mathds{P}(\mathcal{S}_{U})^{2}\;\text{.}\end{aligned}

We now separately prove both approximations in (
6.57
).

First approximation in (
6.57
).

In what follows, write
\bar{X}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t^{\prime}})
. In order to prove the first approximation in (
6.57
), by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it suffices to show

(6.58)

\sup_{z,t^{\prime}}{\rm Var}\big(f(\bar{X})\big)\ll 1\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{with}\ f(\bar{X})=f_{z,t^{\prime}}(\bar{X}):=\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{zn}\bigg|\bar{X}\bigg)\;\text{,}\;\;

where the supremum runs over
z\in[K^{-\theta},1]
and
t^{\prime}\in\{t-t_{\rm sep},\ldots,t\}
.
Since
\bar{X}
has i.i.d. components, we may estimate the above variance by Efron–Stein inequality (see, e.g.,
[
BLM13
, Theorem 3.1]
):

(6.59)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(f(\bar{X}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}\mathds{E}\big[\big(f(\bar{X})-f(\bar{X}^{(s)})\big)^{2}\big]=\frac{t^{\prime}}{2}\,\mathds{E}\big[\big(f(\bar{X})-f(\bar{X}^{(1)})\big)^{2}\big]\;\text{,}\;\;

where, given
\bar{X}
, we defined
\bar{X}^{(s)}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{s-1},X_{s}^{\prime},X_{s+1},\ldots,X_{t^{\prime}})
, with
X_{s}^{\prime}
being an independent copy of
X_{s}
, whereas the second step follows by the symmetry of the function
f
. Let us introduce some shorthand notation:
W:=\sum_{s=2}^{t^{\prime}}\log(Z_{s})
,
W_{1}:=\log(Z_{1})
,
W_{1}^{\prime}:=\log(Z_{1}^{\prime})
, and
h:=\log n-\psi+\log z
. By the tower property, we obtain

(6.60)

\displaystyle f(\bar{X})-f(\bar{X}^{(1)})

(6.61)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}(W_{1}+W>h\mid\bar{X})-\mathds{P}(W_{1}^{\prime}+W>h\mid\bar{X}^{(1)})

(6.62)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}[\mathds{P}(W_{1}+W>h\mid X_{1},W)-\mathds{P}(W_{1}^{\prime}+W>h\mid X_{1}^{\prime},W)\mid X_{2},\ldots,X_{t^{\prime}}]

(6.63)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=0}^{\infty}\mathds{P}(W=w\mid X_{2},\ldots,X_{t^{\prime}})\left\{\mathds{P}(W_{1}+w>h\mid X_{1})-\mathds{P}(W_{1}^{\prime}+w>h\mid X_{1}^{\prime})\right\}\;\text{.}

By Jensen inequality, we have

(6.64)

\displaystyle\big(f(\bar{X})-f(\bar{X}^{(1)})\big)^{2}

(6.65)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\infty}\mathds{P}(W=w\mid X_{2},\ldots,X_{t^{\prime}})\left(\mathds{P}(W_{1}+w>h\mid X_{1})-\mathds{P}(W_{1}^{\prime}+w>h\mid X_{1}^{\prime})\right)^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

and, further taking expectation with respect to
\bar{X}
and
\bar{X}^{(1)}
, we get, by the independence of the
X
-variables,

(6.66)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\big(f(\bar{X})-f(\bar{X}^{(1)})\big)^{2}\big]

(6.67)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\infty}\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{P}(W=w\mid X_{2},\ldots,X_{t^{\prime}})\big]\mathds{E}\big[\big(\mathds{P}(W_{1}>h-w\mid X_{1})-\mathds{P}(W_{1}^{\prime}>h-w\mid X_{1}^{\prime})\big)^{2}\big]

(6.68)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{w=0}^{h}\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{P}(W=w\mid X_{2},\ldots,X_{t^{\prime}})\big]\mathds{E}\big[\big(\mathds{P}(W_{1}>h-w\mid X_{1})\big)^{2}\big]\;\text{.}

Since, by (
3.7
) (recall
W_{1}\in\{0,\log(X_{1})\}
) and
w\leq h
, we have

(6.69)

\mathds{P}(W_{1}>h-w\mid X_{1})=\frac{X_{1}}{n}\,{\bf 1}\{\log(X_{1})>h-w\}\leq\frac{X_{1}}{n}\;\text{,}\;\;

the right-hand side of (
6.68
) is further bounded above by

(6.70)

\displaystyle 2\sum_{w=0}^{h}\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{P}(W=w\mid X_{2},\ldots,X_{t^{\prime}})\big]\frac{\mathds{E}\big[X^{2}\big]}{n^{2}}\leq 2\,\frac{\mathds{E}\big[X^{2}\big]}{n^{2}}\;\text{.}

In conclusion, plugging this back into (
6.59
), we obtain, uniformly over
z\in[K^{-\theta},1]
and
t^{\prime}\in\{t-t_{\rm sep},\ldots,t\}
,

(6.71)

{\rm Var}(f(\bar{X}))\leq t^{\prime}\,\frac{\mathds{E}[X^{2}]}{n^{2}}\lesssim t_{\rm ent}\frac{\mathds{E}[X^{2}]}{n^{2}}\;\text{,}\;\;

and, thus, the desired claim in (
6.58
) by Lemma
6.1
.

Second approximation in (
6.57
).
Recalling the definition of the event
\mathcal{S}_{U}
from (
6.12
), and the identity in Lemma
3.1
(see also (
3.12
)), we need to show, uniformly over
z\in[K^{-\theta},1]
and
t^{\prime}\in\{t-t_{\rm sep},\ldots,t\}
,

(6.72)

\mathds{P}\bigg(|\zeta_{t}(U)|<\frac{e^{\psi}}{n}\bigg)=\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{n}\bigg)\sim\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{zn}\bigg)=\mathds{P}\bigg(|\zeta_{t^{\prime}}(U)|<\frac{e^{\psi}}{zn}\bigg)\;\text{.}

Thanks to (
6.26
), we have
|\log z|\ll\psi\ll\log n
, ensuring

(6.73)

\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{zn}\bigg)\sim\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{n}\bigg)\;\text{.}

Similarly, since
t_{\rm sep}/t_{\rm rel}\gg 1
diverges arbitrarily slow (satisfying, in particular, (
6.14
)), for any
m\gg 1
satisfying
\mu\frac{t_{\rm sep}}{t_{\rm rel}}\ll\log m\ll\psi\asymp\sqrt{\mu\log n}
, Markov inequality yields

(6.74)

\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{s={t^{\prime}}+1}^{t}\log Z_{s}\geq\log m\bigg)\leq\frac{t_{\rm sep}\,\mathds{E}[\log Z]}{\log m}=\frac{t_{\rm sep}\,\mathds{E}[X\log X]}{n\log m}\ll 1\;\text{.}

Since
\log m\ll\psi
, we finally get

(6.75)

\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t^{\prime}}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{n}\bigg)\sim\mathds{P}\bigg(\prod_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}^{-1}<\frac{e^{\psi}}{n}\bigg)\;\text{.}

and this concludes the proof of
(
6.13
) and, thus, of (
6.8
).
∎

7.
Deterministic block size

In this section, we assume that the blocks’ sizes are deterministically equal to
k
. Since the case
\frac{\log k}{\log n}\to 0

is already covered by Theorem
2.10
, we focus on the alternative scenario, namely,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log k}{\log n}>0
. For simplicity of exposition, we shall pass to a converging subsequence, and assume

(7.1)

\exists\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log k}{\log n}>0\;\text{.}

By Propositions
2.6
and
2.5
, cutoff does not occur in this case, and
d_{\rm TV}
decays non-trivially on the timescale
n/k
, which coincides, at first order, with
t_{\rm rel}=\frac{n-1}{k-1}
.

A crucial role is played by the first time in which the site initially possessing all the mass is involved in a block update.
Letting
\tau_{\rm start}
denote this time, we have

(7.2)

\tau_{\rm start}\overset{\rm d}{=}{\rm Geo}(k/n)\;\text{,}\;\;

and, thus,

(7.3)

\mathds{P}(\tau_{\rm start}>\lfloor\beta\tfrac{n}{k}\rfloor)=\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\lfloor\beta\frac{n}{k}\rfloor}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\beta>0\;\text{.}

As a consequence, if
\frac{k}{n}\to 0
,
\frac{k}{n}\tau_{\rm start}\overset{{\rm d}}{\longrightarrow}{\rm Exp}(1)
; otherwise, if
\frac{k}{n}\to c\in(0,1]
,
\tau_{\rm start}\overset{{\rm d}}{\longrightarrow}{\rm Geo}(c)
.
The core of the mixing phenomenology begins after this first update. In what follows, for simplicity, we will often denote by
\eta^{\rm start}
a configuration having
k
vertices of mass
k^{-1}
(the labels of these vertices is not relevant to our scope).

We divide the analysis of the scenario in (
7.1
) into two regimes, dealt with in Sections
7.1
and
7.2
, respectively:

(7.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log k}{\log n}\in\left[5/6,1\right]\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log k}{\log n}\in\left(0,5/6\right]\;\text{.}

Remark 7.1
.

The value
5/6
in (
7.4
) does not have any particular meaning, and the proof actually works for any constant strictly larger than
1/2
. In other words, the approach employed for the first regime can be tuned to work for any value of
k>n^{1/2+o(1)}
. Nevertheless, we found more convenient to present the proof up to this arbitrary threshold, while incorporating the values of
n^{1/2}\ll k\ll n^{5/6}
into the second regime.

Before starting our analysis, let us recall the following elementary identity

(7.5)

\Pr\left(\tau_{j}>\beta\right)=\Pr\left({\rm Poi}(\beta)\leq j-1\right)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}\;\text{,}\;\;\beta>0\;\text{,}\;\;

where
{\rm Poi}(\beta)
stands for a Poisson random variable of parameter
\beta
.

7.1.
First regime in (
7.4
)

In this section, we consider
k=cn
, for some sequence
c=c_{n}\in(0,1)
satisfying

(7.6)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log k}{\log n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}1-\frac{\log\frac{1}{c}}{\log n}\geq 5/6\;\text{.}

Note that we skip the trivial case
c\sim 1
, for which, w.h.p., we have
\tau_{\rm start}=1
and
d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start})=1-c\to 0
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Letting
k=cn
, under the assumption in (
7.6
), we have, for all
\beta\geq 0
,

(7.7)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+\lfloor\beta\tfrac{n}{k}\rfloor-1)\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}\left(1-\bar{c}\right)^{\beta}\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{if}\ \lim_{n\to\infty}c\eqqcolon\bar{c}>0\;\text{,}\;\;

whereas

(7.8)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+\lfloor\beta\tfrac{n}{k}\rfloor-1)\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}e^{-\beta}\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{if}\ \lim_{n\to\infty}c=0\;\text{.}

Proof.

Right after time
\tau_{\rm start}
, the vertex set
V
can be partitioned into two subsets: the vertices having mass
k^{-1}
and those having mass zero. Call
B_{0}^{\rm big}
the former subset,
B_{0}^{\rm void}
the latter one. Clearly,
|B_{0}^{\rm big}|=k
, and therefore

(7.9)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start})=1-c\;\text{.}

Letting
A_{1}
be the subset of vertices sampled at time
\tau_{\rm start}+1
to perform an update, we further
define
A_{1}^{\star}\subset B_{0}^{\rm big}
as the subset of vertices in
A_{1}
having mass
k^{-1}
. Then,

(7.10)

|A_{1}^{\star}|\overset{\rm d}{=}{\rm HyperGeo}(n,k,k)\;\text{.}

Suppose that
|A_{1}^{\star}|=j
. Then, at time
\tau_{\rm start}+1
, there are
k-j
vertices with mass
k^{-1}
,
k
vertices with mass
jk^{-2}
, while the remaining vertices have mass zero.
By concentration of hypergeometric random variables we have (see, e.g.,
[
Ser74
]
), for all
n
large enough,

(7.11)

\mathds{P}(||A_{1}^{\star}|-c|B^{\rm big}_{0}||>k^{2/3})\leq\exp(-k^{1/4})\;\text{.}

In other words, there exists a random variable
E_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(7.12)

|A_{1}^{\star}|=\frac{k}{n}|B_{0}^{\rm big}|\left(1+E_{1}\right)=c^{2}n\left(1+E_{1}\right)\;\text{,}\;\;

and, by (
7.6
),

(7.13)

\mathds{P}(|E_{1}|>n^{-1/5})\leq p\coloneqq\exp(-k^{1/4})\;\text{.}

Therefore, after the
(\tau_{\rm start}+1)
-th update, the vertices in
V
can be partitioned into three sets:

(7.14)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} B_{1}^{\rm big}&\coloneqq\{x\in V\mid\eta_{\tau_{\rm start}+1}(x)=k^{-1}\}\;\text{,}\;\;\\
B_{1}^{\rm void}&\coloneqq\{x\in V\mid\eta_{\tau_{\rm start}+1}(x)=0\}\;\text{,}\;\;\\
B_{1}^{\rm eq}&\coloneqq\{x\in V\mid\left(1-|E_{1}|\right)n^{-1}\leq\eta_{\tau_{\rm start}+1}(x)\leq\left(1+|E_{1}|\right)n^{-1}\}\;\text{,}\;\;\end{aligned}

having sizes

(7.15)

\displaystyle|B_{1}^{\rm big}|

\displaystyle=k-|A_{1}^{\star}|\in\big[\left(1-|E_{1}|\right)c(1-c)n\,,\,\left(1+|E_{1}|\right)c(1-c)n\big]\;\text{,}\;\;

(7.16)

\displaystyle|B_{1}^{\rm eq}|

\displaystyle=k\;\text{.}

Henceforth,

(7.17)

|B_{1}^{\rm big}|(k^{-1}-n^{-1})\leq d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+1)\leq|B_{1}^{\rm big}|(k^{-1}-n^{-1})+|B_{1}^{\rm eq}|\frac{|E_{1}|}{n}\;\text{,}\;\;

and, in conclusion, with probability at least
p
(defined as in (
7.13
)),

(7.18)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+1)=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))(1-c)^{2}+O\big(n^{-1/5}\big)\;\text{.}

We can now argue by induction. Let us fix some
t\geq 2
, and assume that at time
t-1
, with probability at least
(1-p)^{t-1}
, we have

(7.19)

\displaystyle|B_{t-1}^{\rm big}|

\displaystyle=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t-1}c(1-c)^{t-1}n\;\text{,}\;\;

(7.20)

\displaystyle|B_{t-1}^{\rm eq}|

\displaystyle=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t-1}\left(1-(1-c)^{t-1}\right)n\;\text{,}\;\;

where we used a notation analogous to that in (
7.14
), that is,

(7.21)

\displaystyle B_{t-1}^{\rm big}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in V\mid\eta_{\tau_{\rm start}+t-1}(x)=k^{-1}\}

(7.22)

\displaystyle B_{t-1}^{\rm void}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in V\mid\eta_{\tau_{\rm start}+t-1}(x)=0\}\;\text{,}\;\;

(7.23)

\displaystyle B_{t-1}^{\rm eq}

\displaystyle\coloneqq V\setminus\{B_{t-1}^{\rm big}\cup B_{t-1}^{\rm void}\}\;\text{.}

Observe that this implies that, with probability at least
(1-p)^{t-1}
,

(7.24)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+t-1)=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))\,(1-c)^{t}+O(n^{-1/5})\;\text{.}

Under this event at time
t-1
, when we update vertices in the random block
A_{t}
, we get, with probability at least
1-p
again by concentration of hypergeometrics,

(7.25)

\displaystyle|A_{t}^{\star}|

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}\,(1+O(n^{-1/5}))\,|B_{t-1}^{\rm big}|=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}\,c^{2}(1-c)^{t-1}\,n\;\text{,}\;\;

(7.26)

\displaystyle|A_{t}^{\rm eq}|

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}\,(1+O(n^{-1/5}))\,|B_{t-1}^{\rm eq}|=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}\,c(1-(1-c)^{t-1})\,n\;\text{.}

Altogether, with probability at least
(1-p)^{t}
, each vertex in
A_{t}
has mass equal to

(7.27)

(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}\left[c^{2}(1-c)^{t-1}nk^{-1}+c(1-(1-c)^{t-1})\right]k^{-1}=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}\,n^{-1}\;\text{,}\;\;

and, furthermore,

(7.28)

\displaystyle|B_{t}^{\rm big}|

\displaystyle=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}\,[|B_{t-1}^{\rm big}|-|A_{t}^{\star}|]=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}\,c(1-c)^{t}n\;\text{,}\;\;

(7.29)

\displaystyle|B_{t}^{\rm eq}|

\displaystyle=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}(|B_{t-1}^{\rm eq}|-|A_{t}^{\rm eq}|+k)=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}\,(1-(1-c)^{t})\,n\;\text{.}

Consequently, with probability at least
(1-p)^{t}
, we have

(7.30)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+t)=(1+O(n^{-1/5}))^{t}\,(1-c)^{t+1}+O\big(n^{-1/5}\big)\;\text{.}

In conclusion, taking
\bar{t}(\beta)=\lfloor\beta\frac{n}{k}\rfloor=\lfloor\beta c^{-1}\rfloor
for any
\beta>0
, if
c\not\to 0
, (
7.30
) yields

(7.31)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+\bar{t}(\beta))\sim(1-c)^{\bar{t}(\beta)+1}\;\text{,}\;\;

and the proof is complete in this case.
If
c\to 0
(yet, satisfying (
7.6
), thus,
c\gg n^{-1/5}
), we rather obtain

\displaystyle d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+\bar{t}(\beta))\sim e^{-\beta}\;\text{.}

This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

7.2.
Second regime in (
7.4
)

In this section, we consider
k=n^{\delta}
, for some sequence
\delta=\delta_{n}\in(0,1)
such that the second condition in (
7.4
) holds true, that is,

(7.32)

\delta\longrightarrow\bar{\delta}\in(0,5/6]\;\text{.}

Proposition 7.3
.

Letting
k=n^{\delta}
, under the assumption in (
7.32
), we have, for all
\beta\geq 0
and w.h.p.,

(7.33)

\Pr\left({\rm Poi}(\beta)<\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right)\leq d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+\lfloor\beta\tfrac{n}{k}\rfloor)\leq\Pr\left({\rm Poi}(\beta)\leq\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right)\;\text{.}

In particular, if
\bar{\delta}^{-1}\notin\mathbb{N}
, then lower and upper bounds coincide, yielding, for all
\beta\geq 0
,

(7.34)

d_{\rm TV}(\tau_{\rm start}+\lfloor\beta\tfrac{n}{k}\rfloor)-\Pr\left({\rm Poi}(\beta)\leq\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right)\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}0\;\text{.}

Remark 7.4
.

For simplicity, we stated the convergence result in (
7.34
) for
\bar{\delta}^{-1}\notin\mathbb{N}
. Nevertheless, analogous convergence results hold also when
\bar{\delta}^{-1}\in\mathbb{N}
, provided that there exists some
\varphi
such that
\log\log(n)\ll\varphi\ll\log(n)
and

(7.35)

|j\delta-1|\gg\frac{\varphi}{\log(n)}\,,\qquad\forall j\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Clearly, under this additional assumption, we have, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, the following dichotomy

(7.36)

\text{either}\qquad k^{-j}\ll\frac{e^{-\varphi}}{n}\qquad\text{or}\qquad k^{-j}\gg\frac{e^{\varphi}}{n}\;\text{.}

In particular, under (
7.35
), (
7.34
) holds if
\delta\uparrow\bar{\delta}
, whereas
\delta\downarrow\bar{\delta}
yields (
7.34
) with “
<
” in place of “
\leq
”.

In cases where the condition (
7.35
) fails to hold, simulations suggest that neither one of the bounds in Proposition (
7.3
) is sharp; see Figure
7.1
for some simulations when
k=\sqrt{n}
, to be compared with Figure
7.2
, which considers an instance of the case
\bar{\delta}^{-1}\notin\mathbb{N}
.

Figure 7.1.
We simulate the process with
n=10^{6}
and
k=\sqrt{n}
, starting at
\eta_{0}=\eta^{\rm start}
. We plot the empirical CDF of the entries of
\eta_{t}
restricted to the entries for which
\eta_{t}\leq\frac{5}{n}
, when
t
is the first time in which the sequence
d_{\rm TV}(t)
assumes the values
0.7,0.5,0.3,0.1,0.01
. The final plot is the behavior of the TV distance and the bounds in Proposition
7.3
.

Figure 7.2.
Same as Figure
7.1
, but with
k=\lfloor n^{0.7}\rfloor=15849
. In this case, contrarily to the case
\varepsilon=1/2
in Figure
7.1
, the empirical mass distribution on the scale
1/n
is unimodal, having its bulk around
1
.

We split the proof of the above proposition into lower and upper bounds in the two subsequent subsections.

7.2.1.
Proof of the lower bound in Proposition
7.3

For any
t\geq 0
, we employ the notation in Section
3.5
and call
w_{t}
the sub-probability measure on
V
obtained by removing from
\eta_{t}
all the piles of size smaller than
e^{\varphi}/n
, for some prescribed
\log\log(n)\ll\varphi\ll\log(n)
. Thanks to Lemma
3.8
, it suffices to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 7.5
.

Fix
\beta>0
,
t=\bar{t}(\beta)\coloneqq\lfloor\beta\tfrac{n}{k}\rfloor
and consider the process starting at
\eta_{0}=\eta^{\rm start}
. Then,

(7.37)

|w_{t}|-\Pr\left({\rm Poi}(\beta)<\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right)\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}0\;\text{.}

Proof.

We start by showing that

(7.38)

\mathds{E}[|w_{t}|]-\Pr\left({\rm Poi}(\beta)<\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right)\overset{\mathds{P}}{\longrightarrow}0\;\text{.}

By specializing Lemma
3.1
to the particular case
Y\equiv k
, and recalling that
\eta_{0}=\eta^{\rm start}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[|w_{t}|]=\mathds{P}\left(|\zeta_{t}(U)|>\frac{e^{\varphi}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(k^{-T-1}>\frac{e^{\varphi}}{n}\right)=\mathds{P}\left(T<\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}+\frac{\varphi}{\log(k)}\right)\,,

where
T\overset{\rm d}{=}{\rm Bin}(t,k/n)
.
By Le Cam theorem, we have

(7.39)

\|T-{\rm Poi}(\beta)\|_{\rm TV}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}\leq\beta\frac{k}{n}\longrightarrow 0\;\text{,}\;\;

hence, being
\varphi\ll\log(k)
, (
7.38
) follows.

Using Chebyshev inequality, we conclude the proof of the lemma as soon as we prove

(7.40)

\mathds{E}[|w_{t}|^{2}]\leq\mathds{E}[|w_{t}|]^{2}+o(1)\;\text{.}

The proof of this estimate follows exactly the same lines of arguments employed in the proof of (
6.8
), with a single important difference: since here we start from
\eta_{0}=\eta^{\rm start}
, then
\tau_{\rm sep}=0
(cf. (
6.14
)) with probability
1-\frac{1}{k}
; therefore, we can set
t_{\rm sep}=0
and
\theta=1
in that proof. (Note that, with these choices, the event
\mathcal{E}_{3}
therein is irrelevant.) ∎

8.
Sharpness of the conditions in Theorems
2.7
and
2.10

In this section, we discuss a particularly simple parametric class of block-size distributions of the form

(8.1)

p_{X}(2)=1-\frac{a}{n}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad p_{X}(n)=\frac{a}{n}\;\text{,}\;\;

for some positive sequence
a=a_{n}
, which plays the role of a real parameter. This parameter, running over
[0,n]
, interpolates between the two following extreme cases: (i) when all the blocks have size
2
(i.e.,
a=0
), thus, there is cutoff with (standard) Gaussian profile; (ii) when the blocks all have size
n
(i.e.,
a=n
), so that a single update suffices to reach perfect equilibrium. In particular, case (i) coincides with the model in
[
CDSZ22
]
.

Since we are interested in the regime of small
a
, we assume
a\ll 1
all throughout this section. Under this condition, we have

(8.2)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[X]

\displaystyle\sim 2

(8.3)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[X\log X]

\displaystyle\sim 2\log 2+a\log n

(8.4)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[X(\log X)^{2}]

\displaystyle\sim 2(\log 2)^{2}+a(\log n)^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

and, thus,

(8.5)

\mu\sim\log 2+\frac{a}{2}\log n\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\sigma^{2}\sim\frac{a}{4}(\log n)^{2}\;\text{.}

As a consequence,
the entropic product condition in (
2.18
) is satisfied if and only
a\ll 1
, whereas condition (
2.20
) is verified if and only if
a\ll(\log n)^{-1}
. Finally, note that condition (
2.26
) holds true anytime
a\ll(\log n)^{-1}
.

We analyze this simple example with two distinct scopes:

•

Section
8.1
. We examine the robustness of the cutoff phenomenon. Indeed, our process corresponds to the regular block-size model in
[
CDSZ22
]
, but with the modification that, at any time and independently of everything else, there is a small probability that the system immediately reaches equilibrium. We show that a mixing trichotomy (see the dedicated section below for more details) takes place depending on the scaling of
a
, and comment on the universality of this behavior.

•

Section
8.2
. We investigate the sharpness of Theorem
2.10
by analyzing the regime
(\log n)^{-2}\lesssim a\ll(\log n)^{-1}
. This example shows that, even if (
2.18
) and (
2.20
) are in force, the convergence in (
2.25
) might fail in absence of (
2.24
) or, equivalently, (
2.26
) (see Remark
2.11
).

8.1.
Cutoff, half-cutoff, and metastability: a trichotomy

Consider the example in (
8.1
). Depending on whether
a
is much smaller than, larger than, or of the same order as
(\log n)^{-1}
, we observe three different behaviors:

Cutoff.
As already observed above, if
a\ll\log(n)^{-1}
, then Theorem
2.10
applies. In particular, we have cutoff at
\frac{n\log n}{2\log 2}
with a window of size
n\sqrt{\log n}
and, since
\varrho=\frac{\sigma}{\mu}\ll 1
, with a standard Gaussian profile.

Metastability.
If
(\log n)^{-1}\ll a\ll 1
, although the entropic product condition (
2.18
) holds, the condition (
2.20
) fails in this context. Actually, it is not difficult to check that even the weaker WLLN in (
2.19
) does not hold; therefore, cutoff as in Theorem
2.7
does not occur.

In this case, the random sequence
t\mapsto d_{\rm TV}(t)
shows a “cutoff at a random time”, on the scale
t_{\rm ent}\sim\frac{n}{a}\ll n\log n
. Indeed, w.h.p., the sequence
t\mapsto d_{\rm TV}(t)
will go from being almost one to be exactly zero in a single random step, coinciding with the random time

(8.6)

\tau\coloneqq\{t\geq 1\mid X_{t}=n\}\;\text{.}

which is geometrically distributed with mean
n/a
. Since this value coincides with the mixing timescale
t_{\rm ent}\sim n/a
, the sequence
t\mapsto\mathds{E}[d_{\rm TV}(t)]
does
not
exhibit cutoff. Furthermore, passing to the expected distance-to-equilibrium, we observe an exponential decay: for every
s\geq 0
,
\mathds{E}[d_{\rm TV}(st_{\rm ent})]\sim e^{-s}
.

This is the clear signature of a metastable behavior: the system goes to equilibrium because of the realization of a single event occurring at a random exponential time. Furthermore, this example shows that the entropic product condition (
2.18
) alone does not suffice to imply cutoff.

Half-cutoff.
In the intermediate regime
a=c(\log n)^{-1}
,
c>0
,
the sequence
t\mapsto d_{\rm TV}(t)
exhibits cutoff at a random point on the scale
n\log n
. However, since
t_{\rm ent}=\frac{n\log n}{2\log 2+c}\asymp n\log n
, such a random time is constrained to be at most
t_{\rm CDSZ}=\frac{n\log n}{2\log 2}
, the cutoff time for the
2
-block model in
[
CDSZ22
]
.
Indeed, under the event
\tau>(1+\varepsilon)t_{\rm CDSZ}
(which has probability
e^{-(1+\varepsilon)\frac{c}{2\log 2}}
), the sequence
d_{\rm TV}(t)
exhibits cutoff at
t_{\rm CDSZ}
. On the other hand, for all
s\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
,

(8.7)

\mathds{P}\big(d_{\rm TV}(st_{\rm CDSZ})>1-\delta\big)\sim e^{-s\,\frac{c}{2\log 2}}\;\text{,}\;\;

and therefore, for any sequence
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
sufficiently slow, we have

\mathds{P}\big(d_{\rm TV}((1-\varepsilon)t_{\rm CDSZ})>1-\delta\,,\,d_{\rm TV}((1+\varepsilon)t_{\rm CDSZ})<\delta\big)\sim e^{-\frac{c}{2\log 2}}\;\text{.}

We conclude that, for any fixed
s\neq 1
,

(8.8)

\mathds{E}[d_{\rm TV}(st_{\rm CDSZ})]\longrightarrow{\bf 1}_{s<1}e^{-s\,\frac{c}{2\log 2}}\;\text{.}

Such a trichotomy cutoff–half-cutoff–metastability is not a peculiar feature of this model, only. Indeed, several other parametric models (see, e.g.,
[
CQ21a
,
CQ21b
,
AGHH19
,
AGHHN22
]
), have been shown to exhibit the very same behavior.
In fact, when considering a certain model exhibiting cutoff (such as the case
X=2
a.s.) and a parametric perturbation of the model which allows the system to reach equilibrium as consequence of the realization of a certain event which occurs at a random exponential time (such as the event
X=n
, in our case), one should expect the existence of a critical scale for the perturbation parameter in which the two effects (cutoff
vs.
exponential decay) coexist, giving rise to a
half-cutoff
as in (
8.8
).

8.2.
The role of condition (
2.26
) in Theorem
2.10

Consider again the case
a\ll(\log n)^{-1}
.
Under this condition, the resulting Block Average process can be coupled, w.h.p., to the model with
a=0
up to times
t\gg t_{\rm ent}=\frac{n\log(n)}{2\log(2)}
. As a consequence, both models exhibit cutoff at
t_{\rm ent}
, with cutoff window and standard Gaussian profile as predicted for the model in
[
CDSZ22
]
, i.e., (
8.1
) with
a=0
.
This complete cutoff phenomenology does not always correspond, however, to the one that Theorems
2.8
and
2.10
would indicate.

Indeed, choosing, e.g.,
a=\log(n)^{-1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
in Theorem
2.8
would estimate the cutoff window by

(8.9)

t_{\rm w}\sim\left(1+\frac{\sigma}{\mu}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\mu}}\asymp(\log n)^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\;\text{,}\;\;

which is much larger than

\sqrt{\log n}
, the actual size of the cutoff window in
[
CDSZ22
]
. What goes wrong in Theorem
2.10
is the validity of (
2.24
) or, equivalently, (
2.26
). In this sense, conditions (
2.18
) and (
2.20
) without (
2.24
) do not suffice to determine the cutoff profile.

A.1.
Duality in (

)

For any
x\in V
and

, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\eta_{1}(x)]=\mathds{E}[\varPsi_{A}(\eta)(x)]=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{p_{X}(k)}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{|B|=k}\varPsi_{B}\eta(x)

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{p_{X}(k)}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{|B|=k}{\bf 1}_{B}(x)\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{y\in B}\eta(y)\right)+\eta(x)\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{p_{X}(k)}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{|B|=k}{\bf 1}_{B^{c}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in V:\,y\neq x}\eta(y)\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\left(\frac{k-1}{n(n-1)}\right)+\eta(x)\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\left(\frac{1}{n}+1-\frac{k}{n}\right)

A.2.
Proof of Proposition
2.1

Note that, for every
B\subset V
, we have

(A.9)

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{\varPsi_{B}\eta}{\pi}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}-\bigg\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|{\bf 1}_{B}\frac{\varPsi_{B}\eta}{\pi}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}-\bigg\|{\bf 1}_{B}\frac{\eta}{\pi}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

(A.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta(B)^{2}}{\pi(B)}-\bigg\|{\bf 1}_{B}\frac{\eta}{\pi}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

(A.11)

\displaystyle=n\sum_{x\in B}\eta(x)\,\big(\varPsi_{B}\eta(x)-\eta(x)\big)

(A.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{|B|}\sum_{x,y\in B}\eta(x)\,\big(\eta(y)-\eta(x)\big)\ .

We further get

(A.13)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg\|\frac{\eta_{1}}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]-\bigg\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

(A.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\,\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{|B|=k}\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{\varPsi_{B}\eta}{\pi}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}-\bigg\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\bigg)

(A.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\,\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{|B|=k}\frac{n}{k}\sum_{x,y\in B}\eta(x)\,\big(\eta(y)-\eta(x)\big)

(A.16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in V}\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\,\frac{1}{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}\sum_{|B|=k}{\bf 1}_{B}(x){\bf 1}_{B}(y)\,\eta(x)\,\big(\eta(y)-\eta(x)\big)

(A.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in V\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\eta(x)\,\big(\eta(y)-\eta(x)\big)\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\,\frac{1}{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}\sum_{|B|=k}{\bf 1}_{B}(x){\bf 1}_{B}(y)

(A.18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in V}\eta(x)\,\big(\eta(y)-\eta(x)\big)\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\,\frac{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}

(A.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{E}[X]-1}{n-1}\sum_{x,y\in V}\eta(x)\,\big(\eta(y)-\eta(x)\big)

(A.20)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\,\bigg\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

where for the last identity we used the definition in (
2.7
). Hence, we obtain

(A.21)

\mathds{E}\left[\bigg\|\frac{\eta_{1}}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\bigg(1-\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\bigg)\bigg\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\ ,

which, by iterating and using the Markov property, concludes the proof of Proposition
2.1
.

A.3.
Proof of Proposition
2.4

Observe that, for each block
B\subset V
, we have

(A.22)

D(\varPsi_{B}\eta\|\pi)-D(\eta\|\pi)=-\eta(B)\,{\rm ent}_{B}(\eta)\geq-\eta(B)\log|B|\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{with}\ \eta(B)=\sum_{y\in B}\eta(y)\;\text{.}

Here,
|B|
denotes the cardinality of
B\subset V
, while
{\rm ent}_{B}(\eta)\in[0,\log|B|]
the relative entropy of
\eta\,{\bf 1}_{B}

with respect to the uniform measure on
B
. Hence, we have

(A.23)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[D(\eta_{1}\|\pi)\right]-D(\eta\|\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\,\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{|B|=k}\left(D(\varPsi_{B}\eta\|\pi)-D(\eta\|\pi)\right)

(A.24)

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\,\frac{\log k}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{|B|=k}\eta(B)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=2}^{n}p_{X}(k)\,\log k\,\frac{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}{\binom{n}{k}}=-\frac{1}{n}\,\mathds{E}[X\log X]\;\text{.}
