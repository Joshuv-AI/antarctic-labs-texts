Abstract
We review the basic properties of effective actions of families of theories (i.e., the actions depending on additional non-perturbative moduli along with perturbative couplings), and their description in terms of operators (called check-operators), which act on the moduli space. It is this approach that led to constructing the (quantum) spectral curves and what is now nicknamed the EO/AMM topological recursion. We explain how the non-commutative algebra of check-operators is related to the modular kernels and how symplectic (special) geometry emerges from it in the classical (Seiberg–Witten) limit, where the quantum integrable structures turn into the well studied classical integrability. As time goes, these results turn applicable to more and more theories of physical importance, supporting the old idea that many universality classes of low-energy effective theories contain matrix model representatives.

Multiple solutions to the Virasoro constraints

2.1
Simplest example: the Hermitean matrix integral

Thus, we start with the simplest example of the Hermitean matrix model

\displaystyle Z=\int{\rm d}M\exp[\operatorname{Tr}V(M)],

(2.1)

where we parameterize

\displaystyle V(M)=\sum_{k=0}t_{k}M^{k}

so that there is a natural grading
[t_{k}]=k
and
{\rm d}M
is the Haar measure of integration over
N\times N
Hermitean matrices normalized to the volume of the unitary group
U(N)
.
t_{k}
are here coefficients in the potential
V(M)
which can be treated either as a formal series or as a polynomial of large enough degree (in the sense of the projective limit).

This integral satisfies an infinite set of Virasoro constraints (= loop equations)
[
10
,
25
,
31
,
42
,
61
,
76
,
91
]
:

\displaystyle L_{n}Z=0,\qquad n\geq-1,

(2.2)

\displaystyle L_{n}=\sum t_{k}{\partial\over\partial t_{k+n}}+\sum_{a+b=n}{\partial^{2}\over\partial t_{a}\partial t_{b}},

\displaystyle{\partial Z\over\partial t_{0}}=NZ.

These operators
L_{n}
form a Borel subalgebra of the Virasoro algebra.

2.2
Solutions as formal series

The matrix model partition function is defined in (
2.1
) by a formal integral, which is still to be defined. Instead of this, we define it as any solution to the constraints (
2.2
). We also need to fix a class of functions where we look for these solutions. Let us consider power series solutions in all
t_{k}
, i.e., we treat the integral (
2.1
) perturbatively with respect to the potential
V(M)
. Then, there are no solutions to (
2.2
) at all! This is because the moments
\langle M^{k}\rangle
all diverge. One has to regularize them.

The simplest way to do this is to consider the Gaussian integral, i.e., make the substitution
t_{2}\to t_{2}-\alpha
. Then,

\displaystyle Z=\int{\rm d}M\exp\big[{-}\alpha\operatorname{Tr}M^{2}+\operatorname{Tr}V(M)\big]=c_{0}+c_{1}t_{1}+c_{2}^{(1)}t_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{(2)}t_{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\hphantom{Z}{}=\sum_{n}\sum_{\Delta\colon|\Delta|=n}c_{n}^{\Delta}t_{\Delta},

where
c
’s are some coefficients of grading
n
, which are constructed from the moments

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}M\exp\big[{-}\alpha\operatorname{Tr}M^{2}\big]M^{k},

and
\Delta
is the Young diagram with lengths
\delta_{1}\geq\delta_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\delta_{k}
,
t_{\Delta}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}t_{\delta_{i}}
.
Note that

\displaystyle c_{i}\sim\alpha^{-i/2},

which is evident by dimensional argument.

Parameter
\alpha
is the simplest example of a non-perturbative modulus. It is treated differently from the perturbative couplings
t_{k}
, but not independent of them. It can appear in denominators of the coefficients, still
\frac{\partial Z(\alpha|t)}{\partial\alpha}=-\frac{\partial Z(\alpha|t)}{\partial t_{2}}
.

\displaystyle Z_{\rm DV}=\int{\rm d}M\exp[\operatorname{Tr}W(M)+\operatorname{Tr}V(M)],

Then, the coefficients are constructed from

Now one can enumerate the solutions to (

2.5
Technical tools: loop equations

One can rewrite the constraints (
2.2
) by introducing a generating functions of the connected correlators (resolvents) in the model (
2.3
):

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}(z)=\left\langle\operatorname{Tr}{1\over z-M}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{1\over z^{k+1}}\big\langle\operatorname{Tr}M^{k}\big\rangle={1\over Z_{\rm DV}}\hat{\nabla}_{z}Z_{\rm DV}=\hat{\nabla}_{z}{\cal F},

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}(z_{1},z_{2})=\left\langle\operatorname{Tr}{1\over z_{1}-M}\operatorname{Tr}{1\over z_{2}-M}\right\rangle_{c}=\hat{\nabla}_{z_{1}}\hat{\nabla}_{z_{2}}{\cal F},

\displaystyle\ldots,

where

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{z}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{1\over z^{k+1}}{\partial\over\partial t_{k}},\qquad Z_{\rm DV}=\exp{\cal F}.

Then, the generating function for the constraints (
2.2
)

\displaystyle[T(z)Z_{\rm DV}]_{-}=0,\qquad T(z)\equiv\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}{L_{n}\over z^{n+2}}

can be rewritten in the form of loop equation

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}(z)^{2}+\hat{\nabla}_{z}\rho^{(1)}(z)+W^{\prime}(z)\rho^{(1)}(z)+\underbrace{\big[W^{\prime}(z)\rho^{(1)}(z)\big]_{+}}_{\text{polynomial of degree }p-2}+\underbrace{\big[V^{\prime}(z)\rho^{(1)}(z)\big]_{-}}_{=0\text{ as }t_{k}\to 0}=0.

(2.4)

Here the indices “
+
” and “
-
” denote the non-negative power and negative power parts of the expression accordingly.

3.1

At all

3.2
Classical spectral curve

Now let us define the classical spectral curve that describes the family of solutions to the matrix model

Thus, the role of the check operator

Examples

Let us consider a couple of simplest examples.

7.3
An archetypical example

An archetypical example of duality is provided by the pair of operators constructed from the coordinate and momentum,
\hat{A}={\rm e}^{i\hat{\cal P}}
and
\hat{B}={\rm e}^{i\hat{\cal Q}}
, with the commutation relation

\displaystyle\hat{A}\hat{B}={\rm e}^{i\hbar}\hat{B}\hat{A}.

(7.2)

Then, their eigenfunctions are related by the Fourier transform in the eigenvalue space:

\displaystyle\hat{A}Z_{a}({\cal Q})={\rm e}^{ia}Z_{a}({\cal Q}),\qquad\hat{B}\tilde{Z}_{a^{\prime}}({\cal Q})={\rm e}^{ia^{\prime}}\tilde{Z}_{a^{\prime}}({\cal Q})\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{ABPQ})}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\quad Z_{a}({\cal Q})=\int{\rm e}^{\frac{iaa^{\prime}}{\hbar}}\tilde{Z}_{a^{\prime}}({\cal Q}){\rm d}a^{\prime},

which can be easily checked by the direct calculation of the eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle Z_{a}({\cal Q})={\rm e}^{\frac{ia{\cal Q}}{\hbar}},\qquad\tilde{Z}_{a^{\prime}}({\cal Q})=\delta({\cal Q}-a^{\prime}).

Check-operators.
One, however, does not need to calculate the eigenfunctions in order to determine what is the transformation kernel. Instead, one can substitute the two operators by their representatives in the eigenvalue space, which reproduce the right commutation relations:

\displaystyle\check{A}={\rm e}^{ia},\qquad\check{B}={\rm e}^{{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}}.

Then the transformation kernel
M(a,a^{\prime})={\rm e}^{\frac{iaa^{\prime}}{\hbar}}
is simply obtained from the equation

\displaystyle\check{A}(a)M(a,a^{\prime})=\check{B}(a^{\prime})M(a,a^{\prime}).

(7.3)

7.4
Conformal block as an eigenfunction

The conformal block turns out to be an eigenfunction of some operator
{\cal L}_{A}
:

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{A}G=\lambda G,\qquad{\cal L}_{B}G=\Lambda(\partial_{\lambda})G,

which is constructed, similarly to the previous subsection, from the canonical pair of operators
[
44

\beta
-ensemble) representation of the conformal block, it is natural that this pair is given by periods of the main check-operator, (
3.1

Claim.

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\gamma}={\rm e}^{b\oint_{\gamma}{\rm d}z\check{\nabla}_{z}}.

Since
[{\cal L}_{A},{\cal L}_{B}]=1

is the Fourier exponential
. This is what was obtained perturbatively
[

,
111

Subtlety.
Now one has to ask why (
7.1

a\to-a
, but there are two different main check-operators

\displaystyle\oint_{A}{\rm d}z\,\check{\nabla}_{z}^{(+)}Z^{(+)}_{a}=aZ^{(+)}_{a},\qquad\oint_{A}{\rm d}z\,\check{\nabla}_{z}^{(-)}Z^{(-)}_{a}=-aZ^{(-)}_{a},

and two different branches of the
\beta
-ensemble partition function, i.e.,
G
is globally defined but
Z(a)
is
not

and
a<0

that recalculate the action of
\check{\nabla}_{z}
from the partition function to the conformal block:

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\gamma}=\left[{1\over N(a)}{\rm e}^{b\oint_{\gamma}{\rm d}z\check{\nabla}^{+}_{z}}N(a)+{1\over N(-a)}{\rm e}^{-b\oint_{\gamma}{\rm d}z\check{\nabla}^{-}_{z}}N(-a)\right].

\displaystyle\oint_{A}{\rm d}z\check{\nabla}_{z}^{\pm}\to\pm 2\pi ia,\qquad\oint_{B}{\rm d}z\check{\nabla}_{z}^{\pm}\to\pm{1\over 2}{\partial_{a}}.

Thus, one obtains

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{B}={\Gamma(2ab)\Gamma(bQ+2ab)\over\Gamma(b\mu+2ab)\Gamma(b(Q-\mu)+2ab)}{\rm e}^{{b\over 2}\partial_{a}}+(a\to-a).

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sin 2\pi b(a-\mu/2)}{\sin 2\pi ba}{\rm e}^{-\frac{b}{2}\partial_{a}}+\frac{\sin 2\pi b(a+\mu/2)}{\sin 2\pi ba}{\rm e}^{\frac{b}{2}\partial_{a}}\right)K(a,a^{\prime})=\cos 2\pi ba^{\prime}K(a,a^{\prime}).

8
Conclusion

In this review, we introduced and explained the very important notion of check-operator: the operator that acts on the moduli space of theories (or vacua/solutions). We constructed the operator manifestly in the simplest example of the Hermitian matrix model and in a more involved example of the two-dimensional conformal field theory, and demonstrated its use by deriving the corresponding Seiberg–Witten structures and the quantum spectral curves. We also illustrated the usefulness of the concept by a simple evaluation of the kernel of modular transformation of the conformal blocks done in terms of the check-operators. The calculation used the wonderful relation (
3.1
), which provides the impressive example of the properties and the relevance of check-operators for the quantization theory.
