Landau-Zener transition between two quantum dots coupled by resonant tunneling
M. E. Raikh

Abstract
We consider the transition of electron between two quantum dots in which the discrete levels are swept past each other with a constant velocity. If a direct tunneling between the dot levels was allowed, an electron will be transferred between the dots when the levels cross. This transfer is described in terms of the conventional Landau-Zener theory. We assume that direct tunneling between the dots is forbidden. Rather, the transfer is due to the resonant tunneling via a discrete impurity level separating the dots. Then the description of the electron transfer reduces to a three-state (two dots plus impurity) Landau-Zener transition. Transition probability depends on the relative positions of the resonant level and the energy at which the levels cross. It also depends on the left-right asymmetry of tunneling between the impurity and the left(right) dots. We calculate the transition probability in different limits of the horizontal (in space) and vertical (in energy) impurity level positions.

II
Resonant tunneling current

Resonant-tunneling configuration is depicted in Fig. 1.
Let
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
be the amplitudes to find electron in the left and right dots, respectively, while
c_{0}
is the amplitude to find an electron on the impurity. These amplitudes are related by the system of equations

\displaystyle i{\dot{a}}_{1}=\frac{vt}{2}a_{1}+V_{1}c_{0},

(1)

\displaystyle i{\dot{a}}_{2}=-\frac{vt}{2}a_{2}+V_{2}c_{0},

(2)

\displaystyle i{\dot{c}}_{0}={\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}c_{0}+V_{1}a_{1}+V_{2}a_{2}.

(3)

Here
v
is the velocity with which the levels in the dots are swept past each other. The impurity level,
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
, is coupled to the dots with coupling coefficients

V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. As the levels are swept, they cross pairwise at time moments

t_{1}=\frac{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{v},~~~~t_{2}=-\frac{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{v},~~~~t_{12}=0.

(4)

Individual Landau-Zener transitions take place at
t=t_{1}
and at
t=t_{2}
(see Fig. 2),
while at
t=t_{12}
the transition does not occur, since the dot levels are decoupled.

If individual transitions take place
independently
, their respective probabilities are given by the Landau-Zener formula

{\cal P}_{1}=1-\exp\Bigg(-\frac{2\pi V_{1}^{2}}{\frac{1}{4}v}\Bigg),~~~~{\cal P}_{2}=1-\exp\Bigg(-\frac{2\pi V_{2}^{2}}{\frac{1}{4}v}\Bigg).

(5)

Velocity
\frac{v}{4}
in the denominator of the exponents is the arithmetic average of the velocity
\frac{v}{2}
of the dot level and the velocity of the impurity level, which is zero.

Another consequence of independence of the partial transitions is that the net probability,
{\cal P}
, of tunneling between the dots is equal to the product
{\cal P}_{1}{\cal P}_{2}
. Our goal is to calculate
{\cal P}
in the domains when the above decoupling is not permitted. Then the resonant current is expressed via
{\cal P}
as

I=\frac{e}{\tau}{\cal P},

(6)

where
\tau
is the period of the voltage sweep.

III
Semiclassical treatment

Assuming that
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
and
c_{0}
are proportional to the factor

\exp\left[i\int\limits_{\cal C}^{t}dt^{\prime}{\cal L}(t^{\prime})\right]
, while the prefactors are the slow functions of time. Substituting this form into the system Eq.
1
we obtain

\displaystyle a_{1}=-\frac{V_{1}c_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{{\cal L}+\frac{vt}{2}},~~a_{2}=-\frac{V_{2}c_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{{\cal L}-\frac{vt}{2}},

(7)

\displaystyle c_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}=-\frac{V_{1}a_{1}+V_{2}a_{2}}{{\cal L}+\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}.

(8)

Combining Eqs.
7
and
8
leads to the following cubic equation for
{\cal L}

\displaystyle\left[{\cal L}^{2}-\left(\frac{vt}{2}\right)^{2}\right]\left({\cal L}+\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\right)

\displaystyle=\Big(V_{1}^{2}+V_{2}^{2}\Big){\cal L}+\Big(V_{2}^{2}-V_{1}^{2}\Big)\frac{vt}{2}.

(9)

We will analyze how the solutions of this equation evolve upon gradual decrease of
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
.

III.1
\large{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}

In the lowest order in couplings
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
the roots of Eq.
III
are
{\cal L}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}=-\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
and
{\cal L}_{\pm}=\pm\Big(\frac{vt}{2}\Big)
. The first root corresponds to the resonant tunneling of electron via the impurity, while the other two roots describe the conventional tunneling. To find the coupling-dependent correction to the first root we set
{\cal L}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}=-\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}

in the square brackets and in the right-hand side of Eq.
III
. This yields

{\cal L}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}=-\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}+\frac{-\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}(V_{1}^{2}+V_{2}^{2})+\frac{vt}{2}(V_{2}^{2}-V_{1}^{2})}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}-\left(\frac{vt}{2}\right)^{2}}.

(10)

The assumption that the second term in Eq.
10
is a correction to
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
amounts to the condition
(V_{1}^{2}+V_{2}^{2})\ll\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}
, i. e. the condition that the impurity level is high enough.

The other two roots of Eq.
III
are obtained by neglecting
{\cal L}
in the bracket
\big({\cal L}+\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\big)
. Then the equation Eq.
III
becomes quadratic and can be cast in the form

\left[{\cal L}_{\pm}-\frac{V_{2}^{2}+V_{1}^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right]^{2}=\left[\frac{vt}{2}+\frac{V_{2}^{2}-V_{1}^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right]^{2}+\left(\frac{V_{1}V_{2}}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right)^{2}.

(11)

Upon an appropriate shifts of
{\cal L}
and
t

11
) can be reduced to a standard
form,
{\cal{\tilde{L}}}^{2}=\Big(\frac{v{\tilde{t}}}{2}\Big)^{2}+J^{2}
, corresponding to the gap
J=\frac{V_{1}V_{2}}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}
. The probability of the electron transfer is expressed via this gap in the form conventional for the Landau-Zener transition

\displaystyle{\cal P}=1-\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi J^{2}}{v}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\exp\left[-\frac{2\pi}{v}\Bigg(\frac{V_{1}V_{2}}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\Bigg)^{2}\right].

(12)

Naturally, if any of two couplings is zero, there is no electron transfer. It is less trivial that, the higher is the impurity level, the smaller is the transfer probability. Note that, within the
factor of the density of states square, the effective tunnel parameter
J^{2}
in the exponent can be identified with the cotunneling conductance. Recall that the result Eq.
III.1
was obtained in the semiclassical approximation, i.e. under the condition that the exponent in
Eq.
III.1
is big. It is known that, for conventional Landau-Zener transition, the exact result coincides with semiclassical. Concerning the result Eq.
III.1
, it emerges without semiclassics if we neglect
{\dot{c}}
in Eq.
1
and introduce instead of
a_{1}
and
a_{1}
the new variables

\displaystyle{\tilde{a}}_{1}=a_{1}\exp\left[{-i\left(\frac{V_{1}^{2}+V_{2}^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right)t}\right],

\displaystyle{\tilde{a}}_{2}=a_{2}\exp\left[-{i\left(\frac{V_{1}^{2}+V_{2}^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right)t}\right].

(13)

With these new variables, the system
1
takes the form

\displaystyle i{\dot{\tilde{a}}}_{1}=\left(\frac{vt}{2}-\frac{V_{1}^{2}-V_{2}^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right){\tilde{a}}_{1}-\frac{V_{1}V_{2}}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{\tilde{a}}_{2},

(14)

\displaystyle i{\dot{\tilde{a}}}_{2}=-\left(\frac{vt}{2}-\frac{V_{1}^{2}-V_{2}^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right){\tilde{a}}_{2}-\frac{V_{1}V_{2}}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{\tilde{a}}_{1},

(15)

for which the result Eq.
III.1
is exact. The condition that the gap is much smaller than

\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
ensures that
{\dot{c}}_{0}
in Eq.
1
can be neglected.

It is instructive to compare the probabilities of transfer in the cotunneling regime and in the independent crossing regime when these probabilities are small

{\cal P}\Big|_{\text{cotunnel}}\approx\frac{2\pi}{v}\left(\frac{V_{1}V_{2}}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right)^{2},~{\cal P}\Big|_{\text{independent}}\approx\left(\frac{8\pi V_{1}V_{2}}{v}\right)^{2}.

(16)

It follows from Eq.
16
that when the couplings
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
are small the criterion for cotunneling regime to apply is
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\gg v
.

III.2
Upon decreasing
{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}

To analyze the cubic equation Eq.
III
in this limit we eliminate the quadratic term by shifting the variable
{\cal L}
as follows

{\cal L}=-\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{3}+{\cal L}_{1}.

(17)

Then the exact equation for
{\cal L}_{1}
takes the form

{\cal L}_{1}^{3}-{\cal L}_{1}F=G,

(18)

where the functions

and

are defined as

\displaystyle F=\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}}{9}+\left(\frac{vt}{2}\right)^{2}+V_{1}^{2}+V_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle G=-\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{3}\left[\frac{2\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}}{9}-2\left(\frac{vt}{2}\right)^{2}+V_{1}^{2}+V_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{vt}{2}\Big(V_{2}^{2}-V_{1}^{2}\Big).

(19)

The left-hand side of Eq.
18
has a minimum at
{\cal L}_{1}={\tilde{\cal L}_{1}}=\left(\frac{F}{3}\right)^{1/2}
, see Fig. 3.
The minimal value is equal to

-2\left(\frac{F}{3}\right)^{3/2}
. Consider a particular case
V_{2}=V_{1}=V
and

. Then Eq.
18
takes the form

{}^{\prime}\left[1+\frac{1}{18}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{V}\right)^{2}\right]^{3/2}=\left(\frac{3}{8}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{V}\right)\left[1+\frac{1}{9}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{V}\right)^{2}\right].

(20)

The solution of the cubic equation is
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}=\tilde{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}=1.55V
.

To find the time dependence of
{\cal L}_{1}
, we restore the time dependence in the functions
F
and
G
and expand

{\cal L}_{1}^{3}-{\cal L}_{1}F
in Eq.
18
near the minimum. Then the time-dependent correction to the solution of Eq.
20
reads

\left[{\cal L}_{1}-\left(\frac{F}{3}\right)^{1/2}\right]^{2}=\frac{G+2\left(\frac{F}{3}\right)^{3/2}}{3\left(\frac{F}{3}\right)^{1/2}}.

(21)

At
t=0
the right-hand side of Eq.
21
turns to zero. Upon expanding
G
and
F
around
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
and restoring
\left(\frac{vt}{2}\right)^{2}
in
G
and
F
, Eq.
21
takes the form

\left[{\cal L}_{1}-\left(\frac{F}{3}\right)^{1/2}\right]^{2}=\alpha\left(\frac{vt}{2}\right)^{2}+\beta\Big(\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}-\tilde{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\Big),

(22)

where the coefficients of expansion
\alpha
and
\beta
are of the order of
1
. We see that, for
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}>\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
, two of the three solutions of the cubic equation Eq.
III
satisfy the quadratic equation having the Landau-Zener form Eq.
11
.

Comparing Eq.
22
to Eq.
III.1
leads immediately to the expression for the transition probability

{\cal P}=1-\exp\left[-\frac{2\pi}{v}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha^{1/2}}\right)\Big(\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}-\tilde{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\Big)\right].

(23)

To summarize our semiclassical analysis of the transmission probability,
{\cal P}
, for a symmetric position of the resonant level,
V_{1}=V_{2}=V
, it depends on the dimensionless ratio,
\Big|\frac{V}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\Big|
. When this ratio is small, the transport between the dots is due to cotunneling, and
{\cal P}
is small. As
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
is gradually shifted down,
{\cal P}
increases and passes through a maximum at
{\varepsilon}_{0}\sim V
. All three states, left dot, impurity level, and the right dot are represented in the wave-function approximately equally. As the level reaches a critical value
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}=\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}=1.55V
from the above, the transmission probability is small again, as follows from Eq.
23
. Upon further decrease of
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
, the transmission probability keeps dropping down to the value
\left({\cal P}\right)^{2}
, predicted by the independent crossing approximation.

III.3
Perturbative treatment

Perturbative treatment applies when the couplings of the impurity to the dots differ strongly. Then the picture of the electron transfer between the dots gets strongly simplified. Assume e.g. that
V_{2}\ll V_{1}
. This relation implies that beatings of electron
between the left dot and impurity is much faster than the beatings between the impurity
and the right dot. In this limiting case, the Landau-Zener transition between the left dot and impurity gets
completed
before electron accomplishes a
single
virtual tunneling from the impurity to the right dot. To quantify this scenario, the Landau-Zener time can be estimated as
\frac{V_{1}}{v}
, while the beating time can be estimated as
\frac{1}{V_{2}}
. Then the condition that the second time is longer reads
V_{1}V_{2}\ll v
. Under this condition, the probability of the transfer of the electron from the impurity to the second dot can be calculated perturbatively.

At time
t=t_{1}=2\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{v}
the dot level crosses the impurity level. To capture the vicinity of this crossing we introduce a shifted time

T=t-t_{1}

(24)

and the new variables

\displaystyle a_{1}=A_{1}(T)\exp\left(-i\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}T-\frac{vT^{2}}{8}\right),

\displaystyle a_{2}=A_{2}(T)\exp\left(-i\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}T-\frac{vT^{2}}{8}\right),

\displaystyle c_{0}=C_{0}(T)\exp\left(-i\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}T-\frac{vT^{2}}{8}\right).

(25)

With these variables the system Eq.
1
acquires the form

\displaystyle i{\dot{A}}_{1}=\frac{vT}{4}A_{1}+V_{1}C_{0},

(26)

\displaystyle i{\dot{A}}_{2}=-\frac{v}{2}\left(\frac{3T}{2}+\frac{4\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{v}\right)A_{2}+V_{2}C_{0},

(27)

\displaystyle i{\dot{C}}_{0}=-\frac{vT}{4}C_{0}+V_{1}A_{1}+V_{2}A_{2}.

(28)

If we neglect
V_{2}
, the system gets decoupled. Then the first and the third equations reduce to the system describing a conventional Landau-Zener transition with matrix element
V_{1}
and velocity
\frac{v}{4}
. A textbook expression for
A_{1}
and
C_{0}
, which satisfies the initial condition
C_{0}(-\infty)=0
, reads

A_{1}(T)=D_{\nu}\Big({\sqrt{\frac{v}{4}}}e^{\frac{\pi i}{4}}T\Big),~C_{0}(T)=-i{\sqrt{\nu}}D_{\nu-1}\Big({\sqrt{\frac{v}{4}}}e^{\frac{\pi i}{4}}T\Big),

(29)

where
D_{\nu}(z)
and
D_{\nu-1}(z)
are the parabolic cylinder functions, whereas the parameter
\nu
is defined as

\nu=-i\frac{V_{1}^{2}}{\Big(\frac{v}{4}\Big)}.

(30)

Perturbative incorporation of
V_{2}
reduces to solving the second equation of the system for
A_{2}
while assuming that
C_{0}(T)
is fixed. Upon imposing a natural initial condition
A_{2}(-\infty)=0
, we get

\displaystyle{\Big|}A_{2}(T){\Big|}^{2}

\displaystyle=V_{2}^{2}{\Bigg|}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{T}dT^{\prime}C_{0}(T^{\prime})\exp\left[-\frac{iv}{2}\Bigg(\frac{3T^{\prime 2}}{4}+\frac{4\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}T^{\prime}}{v}\Bigg)\right]{\Bigg|}^{2}.

(31)

Substituting
C_{0}(T)
from Eq.
29
and introducing a new variable

z=T\sqrt{\frac{v}{4}}\exp\Big(\frac{\pi i}{4}\Big)
, we find the following expression for
|A_{2}(\infty)|^{2}

\displaystyle|A_{2}(\infty)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4|\nu|V_{2}^{2}}{v}\Bigg|\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dzD_{\nu-1}(z)\exp\Big[-\frac{3z^{2}}{2}-\kappa z\Big]\Bigg|^{2},

(32)

where the parameter
\kappa
is defined as

\kappa=\frac{4\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{v^{1/2}}\exp\Big(\frac{\pi i}{4}\Big).

(33)

To evaluate the integral in Eq.
32
we use the following representation
Representation
of the parabolic cylinder function

D_{\nu-1}(z)=-\Bigl(\frac{2}{\pi}\Bigr)^{1/2}e^{\frac{z^{2}}{4}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}du~e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}}u^{\nu}\sin\left(zu-\frac{\pi\nu}{2}\right).

(34)

Upon substituting this representation into Eq.
32
, the integral over
z
becomes gaussian leading to

\displaystyle|A_{2}(\infty)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{16|\nu|V_{2}^{2}}{3v}\Bigg|\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}duu^{\nu}\exp\Big(-\frac{2}{3}u^{2}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{\kappa u}{3}+\frac{\pi\nu}{2}\Big)\Bigg|^{2}.

(35)

Comparing Eqs.
34
and
III.3
, we conclude that the integral
du
can also be expressed via the function
D_{\nu-1}

|A_{2}(\infty)|^{2}=\frac{2\pi|\nu|V_{2}^{2}}{v}\Bigg|D_{\nu-1}\Big(-\frac{\kappa}{2\sqrt{3}}\Big)\Bigg|^{2}.

(36)

Note now, that the combination
\frac{|\nu|V_{2}^{2}}{v}
in the prefactor with definition Eq. (
30
) of the parameter
\nu
assumes the form
\Big(\frac{V_{1}V_{2}}{v}\Big)^{2}
, which is the square of the parameter established above from the qualitative consideration. Smallness of this parameter ensures the applicability of the perturbative treatment.

The factor
\Big|D_{\nu-1}\Big(-\frac{\kappa}{2\sqrt{3}}\Big)\Big|^{2}
describes , via the argument

, the dependence of the tunnel conductance on the level position

. At resonance,

\Big|D_{\nu-1}\big(0\big)\Big|^{2}=\frac{\sinh^{2}\frac{\pi|\nu|}{2}}{\cosh\frac{\pi|\nu|}{2}}
.
Correction to the conductance at finite

follows from the expansion

\displaystyle\Bigg|\frac{D_{\nu-1}\Big(-\frac{\kappa}{2\sqrt{3}}\Big)}{D_{\nu-1}(0)}\Bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx 1-\left(\frac{32}{3}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}{v^{1/2}}\text{Re}\Bigg\{\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu}{2}+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{\nu}{2}+\frac{1}{2})}e^{\frac{\pi i}{4}}\Bigg\}.

(37)

As was discussed above, see Eq.
16
, it is the parameter
\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}}{v}
that should be large for cotunneling regime to unfold.

Note, that there is a formal correspondence between the standard Landau-Zener result Eq.
29
for

and our perturbative result

for

. It is well-known, see e.g.

;

, that the Landau-Zener transition is accompanied by the oscillations of the level occupation

contains an oscillating term

While the term Eq.

IV
Comparison to three-level bow-tie model

The paper Ref.
Carroll1
is entirely devoted to the transfer probability in the three-level model in the limit
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\rightarrow 0
. Quantitatively, this limit corresponds to
|\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}|\ll V_{1},V_{2}
. Similar to the present paper, it was assumed in Ref.
Carroll1
that the first and the third levels are completely decoupled.
Since in the limit
|\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}|\ll V_{1},V_{2}
, the position
{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
should drop out from the transfer probability, it can depend only on two dimensionless combinations:
\frac{V_{1}^{2}}{v}
and
\frac{V_{2}^{2}}{v}
. Using the contour integral representation,
Demkov1968
it was shown in Ref.
Carroll1
, see also Ref.
Carroll
, that the result
{\cal P}={\cal P}_{1}{\cal P}_{2}
, corresponding to the independent crossing, is valid. Thus, upon increasing of
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
, the probability,
{\cal P}
, crosses over from independent crossing to the cotunneling regime Eq.
III.1
, which applies for
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\gg(V_{1}^{2}+V_{2}^{2}).
The result Eq.
III.1
seems to be the only result predicting the
analytical
dependence of
{\cal P}
on the level position,
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
. This is certainly in agreement with conclusion of Ref.
dot1
, supported numerically, that the independent crossing approximation fails upon increasing of
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
. Equally, the result Eq.
36
of the perturbative treatment indicates the explicit dependence of the tunnel probability on
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
.

General approach in the papers
Carroll
;
Carroll1
;
Bow1
;
Bow2
;
Bow3
;
Bow5
is based on the representation of the solution of the system Eq.
1
in the form of the contour integral. The approach
adopted in the present paper is based on exploiting of the small parameter in the problem. In the three-state model, the semiclassical equation for the quasienergy,
{\cal L}
, has three solutions. Small parameter emerges
when one of the solutions is much bigger than the other two. Neglecting the fast solution and keepinng only two slow solutions
Rajesh0
;
Rajesh1
allows to reduce the problem to the effective Landau-Zener transition. Other small parameter emerges when the coupling of impurity level to one dot is much bigger than the coupling to the other dot.
