Abstract
. Let ℒ {\mathcal{L}} be a first-order two-sorted language and consider a class of ℒ \mathcal{L} -structures of the form ⟨ M , X ⟩ \langle M,X\rangle where M M varies among structures of the first sort, while X X is fixed in the second sort, and it is assumed to be a compact Hausdorff space. When X X is a compact subset of the real line, one way to treat classes of this kind model-theoretically is via continuous-valued logic, as in [ BYBHU ] . Prior to that, Henson and Iovino proposed an approach based on the notion of positive formulas [ HI ] . Their work is tailored to the model theory of Banach spaces. Here we show that a similar approach is possible for a more general class of models. We introduce suitable versions of elementarity, compactness, saturation, quantifier elimination and other basic tools, and we develop basic model theory.

1.
Introduction

As a motivating example for our work, consider a first-order structure
M
equipped with a bounded distance
d:M^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
, where
[0,1]
is the real unit interval. We regard
d
as a function in a two-sorted structure
\langle M,[0,1]\rangle
. We introduce a suitable two-sorted language
\mathcal{L}
and we require that the formulas in this language should remain classically (i.e. two-) valued, as opposed to what happens in continuous logic, where the set of truth values is the real unit interval
[0,1]
— see
[
BYBHU
]
for an introduction to continuous logic and
[
K
]
for a generalisation to structures without a metric.

More generally, we deal with structures of the form
\langle M,X\rangle
, where
M
is some first-order structure and
X
is a fixed compact Hausdorff space, also treated as a first-order structure, in a way that we detail in Section
2
.
In this regard, we point out again that — differently from
[
CK
]
, where formulas take values in an arbitrary compact Hausdorff space — in our setting formulas are classically valued.

Our goal is to be able to apply basic model-theoretic results, such as compactness and the existence of elementary and saturated extensions, to structures like
\langle M,X\rangle
, while requiring that
X
should stay fixed. Ours is an attempt to reconcile these two conflicting requirements to a certain extent. For example, with reference to our motivating example, when considering a saturated extension of
\langle M,[0,1]\rangle
, we would like the metric to still take values in
[0,1]
, rather than in an elementary extension that would possibly contain nonstandard elements.

We treat the class of structures described above with an approach similar to
[
HI
]
. As in
[
HI
]
, we isolate a suitable subclass of formulas
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
with respect to which we prove a compactness theorem and we formulate appropriate versions of notions such as elementary extension and saturation.
We point out a difference from
[
HI
]
.
In the class of formulas
\mathcal{F}
we explicitly allow quantification over elements of the topological space
X
.
Though we show that these quantifiers can be eliminated, their presence simplifies the comparison with real-valued logic–see Example
4
.
A second difference is discussed after Remark
3
.

We also borrow tools from nonstandard analysis, more precisely from nonstandard topology, to define what we call the
standard part
of a structure. The latter plays a crucial role in the proof of our compactness theorem. See
[
D
]
for a concrete introduction to nonstandard analysis.

We give a brief outline of the paper. In Section
2
, we describe our two-sorted language
\mathcal{L}
, and the subclass of
\mathcal{L}
-structures in which we are interested, namely those of the form
\langle M,X\rangle
, where
M
is any structure and
X
is a given nonempty compact Hausdorff space.

The language
\mathcal{L}
contains, but is not limited to, symbols for continuous function from
X^{n}
(equipped with the product topology) to
X
for
n\geq 0
– the case
n=0
gives a constant for each element of
X
– and predicate symbols for the closed (equivalently: compact) subsets of
X^{n}
, for all
0<n
.

We also describe the subset
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{L}
-formulas with respect to which we later define our versions of elementarity and saturation: the set
\mathcal{F}
restricts negations in such a way that the structure

stays the same in elementary extensions of
\langle M,X\rangle
. Section
3
introduces the notions of an
approximation
of a formula in
\mathcal{F}
, and of
approximate satisfiability
, used in Section
4
to define
\mathcal{F}
-elementary maps.
In order to partly recover the connective negation, in Section
3
we also introduce the notion of
strong negation
of an
\mathcal{F}
-formula.

In Section
5
, we use tools from nonstandard analysis to define the
standard part

of an elementary extension

of a structure

, thus providing the basis for the compactness theorem proved in Section
6
and the existence of saturated structures. In Section
6
we also prove that approximate satisfiability is the same as satisfiability in models that are
\omega
-saturated with respect to the class of
\mathcal{F}
-formulas.

These results allow us, in Section
7
, to work in a monster model and to formulate the analogue in our context of classical quantifier elimination. More precisely, we show that each
\mathcal{F}
-formula formula
\varphi
and each approximation
\varphi^{\prime}
of
\varphi
admit an interpolant
\mathcal{F}
-formula that does not contain quantifiers of the sort which is associated to the elements of
X
.

In Section

we formulate analogues of the Tarski-Vaught Test and the Löwenheim-Skolem Theorem. We also introduce some technical notions that allow us to obtain, among other results, a form of the Perturbation Lemma proved in
[

We conclude this section by applying these notions to bounded metric spaces.

2.
A class of two-sorted structures

We adopt standard model-theoretic notation and conventions.
In particular, when there is no ambiguity, we identify a language with the set of its formulas and a structure with its domain.

We fix a nonempty, compact Hausdorff space
X
. Recall that
X
is a normal space. We always assume that
X^{n}
is endowed with the product topology, for every
n>1
.

We associate to
X
a first-order language
\mathcal{L}_{\sf S}
. The subscript
\sf S
denotes the sort of this language, which we call the
space sort
.
The non-logical symbols of
\mathcal{L}_{\sf S}
are:

•

a set of
n
-ary predicate symbols

, one for each
n\geq 1
and each closed subset
C
of
X^{n}
: for each such symbol, we write
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\in C
instead of
C(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})
;

•

a set of function symbols

, one for every
n\geq 0
and every (uniformly) continuous

.

We also use

to denote the structure with domain

which interprets
\mathcal{L}_{\sf S}
-symbols as expected;
depending on the context,

and

denote either
\mathcal{L}_{\sf S}
-symbols or their interpretations in the structure

.
An example of a structure

as above is the real unit interval
[0,1]
, equipped with all closed sets

, all continuous functions

, and all constants

. We always assume that

is equipped with its standard topology.

Next we fix an arbitrary first-order language
\mathcal{L}_{\sf H}
, which we call the language of the
home sort

\sf H
.
Finally, we let

be a full two-sorted language that expands both

and

Definition 1
.

A

is a two-sorted
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure of the form
\langle M,X\rangle
, where
M
is some
\mathcal{L}_{\sf H}
-structure and

is the structure fixed above.

Since

From now on, the capital letters

Unless otherwise specified, we use the letters

.

The notion of standard structure is inspired by that of normed space structure introduced in
[
HI
]
.
In what follows, when comparing our setting with that of
[
HI
]
we always refer to the case of two-sorted structures therein, with our space sort
\sf S
playing the role of the real unit interval

. However, the component of sort

of a standard structure is an arbitrary first-order structure.

Since we are working in a

model-theoretic setting,

{\mathcal{L}}
-structures exist but, when

is infinite, they are not standard structures in the sense of Definition
1
.
For this reason, we introduce a set

of formulas such that

and every standard structure has an

-elementary,

-saturated extension which is itself a standard structure.

Definition 2
.

The set

is the least subset of

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

(iv)

Remark 3
.

In general, the negation of an
\mathcal{F}
-formula

is not logically equivalent to an
\mathcal{F}
-formula, unless all its

-atomic subformulas of the form

Nevertheless,

-formula is a well-formed

-formula and can be evaluated in any (standard) structure.

Formally, the set

The

In Section

The greater expressive power of

Somewhat surprisingly, we will also prove (Propositions

Approximations and strong negations

In this section we generalize the notion of

[

If

\mathcal{L}

\varphi\to\psi

Let

We let

containing a name for each element of

and

Definition 5
.

Let

be a standard structure, and

{\mathcal{F}}(M)
-formulas. We say that

is an

of

When no such atomic formula occurs in

.

Clearly,

also when all
\mathcal{F}
-atomic formulas of the form

occurring in

Moreover, an easy induction on the complexity of

for each approximation

Note also that

Definition 6
.

Let
M
be a standard structure, and

A

, which obviously extends the corresponding supremum metric on
M^{n}
. For tuples of variables
x=\langle x_{i}\rangle_{1\leq i\leq m}
and
\langle y\rangle_{1\leq i\leq m}
of the same finite length, we use
d(x,y)\leq\varepsilon
to abbreviate the conjunction
\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq m}d(x_{i},y_{i})\leq\varepsilon
.

The uniform continuity of a given function
f\colon M^{n}\to M
with
f\in\mathcal{L}
can be formulated using a set of
\mathcal{F}
-formulas. Indeed, by uniform continuity for all
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
there exists
0<\delta\leq 1
such that the following
\mathcal{F}
-formula is satisfied in
M
:

\forall x\forall y\,\Big(d(x,y)\in[\delta,1]\vee d(f(x),f(y))\in[0,\varepsilon]\Big).

The
\mathcal{F}
-type of all such sentences (one for each
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, paired with any suitable
0<\delta\leq 1
) is preserved from
M
to any monster model
\mathcal{U}
with
M\preccurlyeq^{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{U}
, hence the interpretation of
f
in
\mathcal{U}
remains uniformly continuous.
Similar considerations apply to uniformly continuous functions from
M^{n}
to
X
belonging to
\mathcal{L}
, as well as to the functions defined in
M
by
\mathcal{L}
-terms (which are necessarily uniformly continuous because by choice of
\mathcal{L}
they are compositions of finitely many uniformly continuous functions).

Lemma 48
.

Let
x
and
y
be tuples of variables of sort
\sf H
and of equal length
m\geq 1
. Then

x\sim_{\sf S}y\leftrightarrow d(x,y)=0.

28
.

Consider arbitrary
a,b\in\mathcal{U}^{m}
.
If
a\sim_{\sf S}b
, then
\forall z\,\ d(a,z)=d(b,z)
. Hence
d(a,a)=d(b,a)
, from which
d(a,b)=0
follows.

For the converse implication, assume that
d(a,b)=0
. Let
t(x,z)
be any term of sort
{\sf H}^{m+k}\rightarrow{\sf S}
for some
k\in\omega
.
As observed before this lemma, the term
t(x,z)
defines a uniformly continuous function in
\mathcal{U}
, hence for all
c\in\mathcal{U}^{k}
we have that
|t(a,c),t(b,c)|\leq 1/n
holds in
\mathcal{U}
for all
n>0
.
Therefore
\forall z\,\ t(a,z)=t(b,z)
, and since
t
was arbitrary we get
a\sim_{\sf S}b
.

Proposition 49
.

Let
N\preccurlyeq^{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{U}
be a model and let
m\geq 1
. For every
a\in\mathcal{U}^{m}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

p(x)={\mathcal{F}}\mbox{-tp}(a/N)
is a Cauchy type;

(2)

a\in\mathcal{U}^{m}
is a realization of an
\sim_{\sf S}
-invariant Cauchy type
q(x)\subseteq\mathcal{F}(N)
;

(3)

there is a sequence
\langle a_{k}\rangle_{k\in\omega}
in

that converges to
a
with respect to
d
.

29
.

(1)

\Rightarrow

(3)
.
As

is a Cauchy type, we have
p(x)\rightarrow x\sim_{\sf S}a
(see the first part of the proof of
(1)

\Rightarrow

in Lemma

-invariance).
By Lemma

Since for

in the space

28
(2)
applied to

and
d(a,x)\leq 2^{-k}
we get that for each

there is a formula
\varphi_{k}(x)\in p(x)
and an approximation
\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(x)\in p^{\prime}(x)
of it such that
\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(x)\rightarrow d(a,x)\leq 2^{-k+1}

p(x)

As

(3)

(2)
.
For each
k\in\omega
, let

from

, this means that

Proposition 50
.

For every model
N\preccurlyeq^{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{U}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

N
is Cauchy complete;

(2)

N
is
d
-Cauchy complete, that is, every

-Cauchy sequence in

30
.

(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)

-Cauchy sequence in

(2)
