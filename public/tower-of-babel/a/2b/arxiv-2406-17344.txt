Recurrence and transience for non-Archimedean and directed graphs
Matthias Keller, Anna Muranova

Abstract
. We introduce the notion of recurrence and transience for graphs over non-Archimedean ordered field. To do so we relate these graphs to random walks of directed graphs over the reals. In particular, we give a characterization of the real directed graphs which can arise is such a way. As a main result, we give characterization for recurrence and transience in terms of a quantity related to the capacity.

1.
Introduction

The study of random walks on graphs is a classical topic going back to the last century. The name was coined by Karl Pearson in 1905
[
11
]
to model a mosquito infestation in a forest. Since then this has become a vibrant field of mathematics connecting probability, potential theory, graph and geometric group theory, see e.g. the monographs
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
and references therein as well as
[
2
,
3
,
6
]
for recent developments.

In this work we take on the study of random walks arising from graphs over non-Archimedean fields. Such graphs have been recently introduced and studied in
[
8
,
9
,
4
]
. In particular, in
[
4
]
the notion of capacity on infinite graphs was studied in great depth. Classically the notion of capacity is closely related to questions of the behavior of random walks. Indeed vanishing capacity at a vertex is equivalent to fact that the random walk returns to this vertex over time infinitely often almost surely – a property which is known as recurrence. Conversely, if the capacity is positive the random walk only returns finitely many times almost surely which is known as transience.

However, for non-Archimedean graphs the situation is significantly more subtle. First of all, while the sequence of capacities along an exhaustion of an infinite graph is still monotone decreasing its limit does not necessarily exist. So, the classical dichotomy of zero or positive capacity is extended by the case of divergent capacity. Secondly, convergence to zero is much harder to realize in non-Archimedean fields as the sequence is not only required to be eventually smaller than any positive rational but also than any infinitesimal. On the other hand, in terms of non-Archimedean probability a property is said to hold almost surely if it holds outside of an event which has probability less than every positive rational
[
10
, p. 25]
. Thus, expecting to equate zero capacity with recurrence would be also conceptually problematic.

Thus, we have to take heed in two ways. First of all, we define random walks and the notions of recurrence and transience in accordance to the proper viewpoint of probability on non-Archimedean fields. This leads us to relate our non-Archimedean graphs to certain directed graphs over the reals. For such graphs, there is a rich theory of their corresponding random walks, see e.g.
[
14
]
, which we will exploit. However, it is crucial to align the proper notions in the non-Archimedean and the corresponding directed real setting. In particular, this relates to paths in the non-Archimedean graph with directed paths and consequently with (strongly) connected components. For directed graphs the study of connected components is more subtle and a specific importance comes to so called essential components. These are also called absorbing components and are characterized by the fact that they do not have paths leaving this component.
We use these notions to obtain a first result on determining whether a vertex is recurrent or transient. This result, Theorem
3.15
, says that vertices in non-essential components are necessarily transient. This basically leaves us with the study of the type of the vertex in the essential components of the graph. Furthermore, we characterize the directed graphs over the reals which arise from non-Archimedean graphs in Theorem
3.22
.

Secondly, we consider a quantity which relates the capacity to recurrence and transience. For real graphs the diagonal of the Green’s function is equal to the reciprocal of the capacity normalized by the measure. As discussed above neither of these quantities necessarily exists for infinite non-Archimedean graphs. However, as the normalized capacities of a vertex
a
along an exhaustion are bounded by values less than one, we can take their real part – that is the unique real number in the field which differs only by an infinitesimal. This gives not only a real number between zero and one but also the limit exists within the reals. Hence, also its reciprocal called
{G}(a)
exists and is finite or positive infinite. For vertices in the essential components this allows us to characterize recurrence and transience in terms of infiniteness or finiteness of
{G}(a)
, Theorem
5.4
. See also Corollary
5.5
which brings this together with the findings of Theorem
3.15
and therefore provides a full characterization of recurrence and transience. Moreover, for vertices in any non-essential component, one direction of this characterization remains true, Corollary
5.2
, namely that
{G}(a)<\infty
implies transience of
a
. Of course, this raises the question whether a full characterization of recurrence and transience is possible in terms of
{G}(a)
. That this is not the case is shown in Examples
5.6
and Example
5.7
.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section we provide all the basic notions and facts for graphs over non-Archimedean fields first and random walks over directed graphs over the reals second. In Section
3
we then construct directed real graphs from non-Archimedean ones together with their random walks. This also allows us to define recurrence and transience for vertices in the original graph. We then give a first criterion for recurrence and transience, Theorem
3.15
and also characterize the directed real graphs which can be obtained from non-Archimedean ones, Theorem
3.22
. In Section
4
, we then introduce the quantity
{G}(a)
for a vertex
a
discussed above and study its basic properties. Finally, in Section
5
we characterize recurrence and transience of vertices in the graph with the help of
{G}(a)
in Theorem
5.4
and Corollary
5.5
.

2.
Set up and preliminaries

2.1.
Weighted graphs over ordered field

An ordered field
(\mathbb{K},\succ)
is called
non-Archimedean
if there exists an
infinitesimal
, i.e.,
\tau\succ 0
in
\mathbb{K}
such that

{\tau}\prec\dfrac{1}{n}=\dfrac{1}{\underbrace{1+\dots+1}_{n\mbox{ \tiny{times}}}}

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}\subseteq\mathbb{K}
. Otherwise, the field is called
Archimedean
. Any element
\mathcal{N}
with
\mathcal{N}\succ N
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
is called
infinitely large
. Obviously, the field is non-Archimedean if and only if there exists an infinitely large element.

We denote the positive elements of
\mathbb{K}
by
\mathbb{K}^{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{K}\mid x\succ 0\}
. The
absolute value
|k|
of
k\in\mathbb{K}
is an element of
\mathbb{K}^{+}\cup\{0\}
defined as

|k|=\begin{cases}k,&:\mbox{if }k\succeq 0\\
-k&:\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly, also the rationals
\mathbb{Q}
are a subfield of any ordered field. For two elements
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
we fix the following notation:

We write

k_{1}\prec\hskip-2.0pt\prec k_{2}
if
\dfrac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}
is an infinitesimal, i.e.,

k_{1}\prec\hskip-2.0pt\prec k_{2}\hskip 17.00024pt:\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 17.00024pt\dfrac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\prec c\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{for all }c\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}.

We write

k_{1}\precsim k_{2}
if
\dfrac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}
is at most finite, i.e.,

k_{1}\precsim k_{2}\hskip 17.00024pt:\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 17.00024pt\dfrac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\prec c_{0}\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{for some }c_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}.

We write

k_{1}\simeq k_{2}
, if
k_{1}\precsim k_{2}
and
k_{1}\succsim k_{2}
, i.e.

k_{1}\simeq k_{2}\hskip 17.00024pt:\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 17.00024ptc_{1}\prec\dfrac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\prec c_{2}\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{for some }c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}

Note that
\simeq
is an equivalence relation. Further, we say that
k\in\mathbb{K}
is
at most finite
, if
k\precsim 1
, i.e.,
k
is not infinitely large. We denote by
\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}
the set of all at most finite elements in the field
\mathbb{K}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{K},\succ)
be an ordered field.
A weighted graph
over an at most countable set
V
, whose elements are called the
vertices
, is a symmetric map

b:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{K}^{+}\cup\{0\}
with vanishing diagonal, (i.e.,
b(x,y)=b(y,x)
and
b(x,x)=0
for all
x,y\in V
).

Having zero diagonal means that that graphs have no loops. We write
x\sim y
whenever
b(x,y)\succ 0
and say that there is an
edge
between
x
and
y
.

An
(undirected) path
between any vertices
x,y\in V
is a sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

x=x_{0}\sim x_{1}\sim x_{2}\sim\dots\sim x_{n}=y.

A graph is called
connected
, if there is a path between any two vertices.
Furthermore, a subset
U\subseteq V
is called
connected
if the restriction
b\left|{}_{U\times U}\right.
is connected.
A graph is called
locally finite
, if for all
x\in V
we have

\#\{y\in V\mid y\sim x\}<\infty.

Assumption 2.2
.

In the following,
b
always denotes a connected locally finite graph over an at most countable set
V
taking values in an ordered field
\mathbb{K}
.

For any
U\subseteq V
and
x\in V
, we denote

b(U)=\sum_{z\in U}\sum_{y\in V}b(z,y)\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 17.00024ptb(x):=b(\{x\})=\sum_{x\in V}b(x,y).

The
normalized weight

p:V\times V\to\mathbb{K}
of a graph
b
over
V
is defined as

p(x,y)=\dfrac{b(x,y)}{b(x)}.

Moreover, for
U\subseteq V
, we define the following set of functions:

\mathcal{F}(U)=\{f\mid f:U\to\mathbb{K}\}

and

C_{c}(V)=\{\phi\mid\phi:V\to\mathbb{K},\phi\mbox{ has finite support}\}

where the support of the function is the set of vertices where this function does not vanish. For the compactly supported functions
C_{c}(V)
, the
characteristic functions
1_{x}
of vertices
x\in V
form a basis.
We define a
transition operator
by

P:\mathcal{F}(V)\to\mathcal{F}(V),\hskip 17.00024ptPf(x)=\sum_{y\in V}f(y)p(x,y).

By local finiteness, we can define
P^{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
inductively and let
P^{0}=I
. The matrix elements of
P^{n}
with respect to the standard basis are denoted by
p^{(n)}(x,y)
. Note that, for
n\geq 2
,

p^{(n)}(x,y)=\sum_{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}\}\subseteq V}p(x,x_{1})p(x_{1},x_{2})\dots p(x_{n-1},y).

The capacity can be defined via limit involving solutions of Dirichlet problems or via the infimum of energies over compactly supported functions. The equivalence of either definition is shown in
[
4
]
and we present here the main concepts.

The
Laplace operator
is defined on the set of all functions
\mathcal{F}(V)

by

\Delta f(x)=\dfrac{1}{b(x)}\sum_{y\in V}b(x,y)(f(x)-f(y)).

and the corresponding quadratic form or
energy form
for
\phi\in C_{c}(V)
is defined as

Q(\phi)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}b(x,y)(\phi(x)-\phi(y))^{2},

which is connected to
\Delta
via
Green’s formula
for
f\in\mathcal{F}(V)
and
\phi\in C_{c}(X)
, cf.
[
8
, Theorem 22]

\sum_{x\in V}(b\phi\Delta f)(x)=\sum_{x\in V}(bf\Delta\phi)(x)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}b(x,y)(f(x)-f(y))(\phi(x)-\phi(y))=:Q(f,\phi).

Let
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq V
be a finite connected subset of
V
and
a\in K
. The Dirichlet problem for
K
and
a
then looks for a solution
v:V\to\mathbb{K}
of the equation

(DP)

\begin{cases}\Delta v=0&\mbox{ on }K\setminus\{a\},\\
v=1&\mbox{ on }\{a\},\\
v=0&\mbox{ on }V\setminus K.\end{cases}

Proposition 2.3

(Maximum principle
[
8
]
)
.

Let
K\subseteq V
be finite and connected and
a\in K
.
The Dirichlet problem (
DP
) has a unique solution
v
which satisfies

0\prec v\preceq 1\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{on }K

and

Q(v)=\Delta v(a)b(a)=-\sum_{x\in V\setminus K}\Delta v(x)b(x).

Moreover, for any function
f\in C_{c}(V)
with
f(a)=1
and
f=0
on
V\setminus K
,

Q(v)\preceq Q(f),

i.e., the solution of the Dirichlet problem minimizes the energy.

For a set
U\subseteq V
, we denote by
\pi_{U}:\mathcal{F}(V)\to\mathcal{F}(U)
the canonical projection and by
\iota_{U}:\mathcal{F}(V)\to\mathcal{F}(U)
the embedding via extension by zero. For an operator
A:\mathcal{F}(V)\to\mathcal{F}(V)
, we then denote by
A_{U}=\pi_{U}A\iota_{U}
the corresponding operator on
\mathcal{F}(U)
.
The matrix elements
p^{(n)}_{U}(x,y)
,
x,y\in U
of powers of the restricted transition operator
P_{U}^{n}
satisfy
p^{(1)}_{U}(x,y)=p_{U}(x,y)
and, for
n\geq 2
,

p^{(n)}_{U}(x,y)=\sum_{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}\}\subseteq U}p(x,x_{1})p(x_{1},x_{2})\dots p(x_{n-1},y).

However, observe that one does not longer have
\sum_{y\in U}p({x,y})=1
for
x\in U
but only
\sum_{y\in U}p({x,y})\preceq 1
which is a strict inequality whenever there is
y\in V\setminus U
with
x\sim y
.

For
K\subseteq V
finite and
a\in K
, the
renormalized Dirichlet problem
looks for
\widetilde{v}\in\mathcal{F}(K)
such that

(
\widetilde{\mathrm{DP}}
)

\Delta_{K}\widetilde{v}=1_{a}

and we call
\Delta_{K}
the

.
There is the following connection to the Dirichlet Problem (
DP
).

Lemma 2.4

(Renormalized Dirichlet problem, Lemma 2.5 and 4.5 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
K\subseteq V
be finite and connected and
a\in K
.
The Dirichlet problem (

~
DP

) has a unique solution
\widetilde{v}
which satisfies

\widetilde{v}=\dfrac{v}{\Delta v(a)}\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{and }\hskip 17.00024ptv=\dfrac{\widetilde{v}}{\widetilde{v}(a)},

where

is the solution of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
). Furthermore, the Dirichlet Laplacian

is invertible and

\widetilde{v}=\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{a}\succeq 0.

Definition 2.5

(Capacity, Definition 2.3
[
4
]
)
.

The

\operatorname{cap}_{K}

for a finite and connected set
K\subseteq V
and
a\in K
is defined as

\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)=\Delta v(a)b(a),

where
v
is the solution of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
).

From the definition, Lemma

and Green’s formula, we can immediately conclude at

\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)=\dfrac{b(a)}{\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{a}(a)}=Q(v).

Lemma 2.6

(Lemma 2.6 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let

be finite and connected and
x,y\in K
. Let

, respectively

, be solutions of (

) for

. Then,

\dfrac{v^{x}(y)}{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(x)}=\dfrac{v^{y}(x)}{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(y)}.

(Monotonicity of capacity)
.

If

, and

\operatorname{cap}_{L}(a)\preceq\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a).

Proof.

Follows from the maximum principle, Proposition

as this shows

Q(v_{L})\preceq Q(v_{K}),

where
v_{L}
and

are the solutions of Dirichlet problem (

) on

∎

We call an increasing sequence of finite connected subsets

an

.

Definition 2.8

(Effective Capacity, Definition 17 in

]
)
.

Let
(K_{n})

The following theorem shows that existence of the limit is independent of the base vertex

]
)

If

\operatorname{cap}(a)=0

)

2.2.
Random walks and directed graphs over the reals

In this subsection we recall the basic notions of random walks over the Archimedean field of the reals. We will use this theory later on to connect it to graphs over non-Archimedean fields.

Definition 2.11

(Transition matrix)
.

A
transition matrix
over an at most countable set
V
, called the
state space
, is a map

\pi:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}
such that

\sum_{y\in V}\pi(x,y)=1

for any
x\in V
.

We denote the corresponding operator on the space of compactly supported functions
V\to\mathbb{R}
by
\Pi
. For
\Pi
, we can take again powers and we denote the matrix elements of
\Pi^{n}
by
\pi^{(n)}(x,y)
for
x,y\in V
.
A transition probability
\pi
gives rise to a random walk
X=(X_{n})
via

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=y\mid{X}_{0}=x)=\pi^{(n)}(x,y).

Note that we can consider
\pi
as a
\emph{directedgraph}
over
V
which is said to be
locally finite
if
\#\{y\in V\mid\pi(x,y)\}<\infty
for all
x\in V
. We write
x\leadsto y
if
\pi(x,y)\neq 0
. We write
x\leftrightsquigarrow y
if
x\leadsto y
and
y\leadsto x
.

Further, we write
x\to y
, i.e.,
y
can be reached from
x
, if
x=y
or there exists a
directed path
from
x
to
y
, i.e., there exists
(x_{i})_{i=0}^{n}\subseteq V,n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

x=x_{0}\leadsto x_{1}\leadsto x_{2}\leadsto\dots\leadsto x_{n}=y.

We write
x\leftrightarrow y
if
x\to y
and
y\to x
.
Clearly,

\leftrightarrow
is an equivalence relation.
We call an equivalence class an
irreducible class
. In graph theoretical terminology, one also speaks of a
strongly connected component

[
14
, p.28]
.

We extend the use symbol
\to
to irreducible classes as follows: for two irreducible classes
C_{1},C_{2}\subseteq V
, we write
C_{1}\to C_{2}
if for some (all)
x\in C_{1}
and for some (all)
y\in C_{2}
we have
x\to y
. The following classical fact holds, see e. g.
[
14
, 2.4 Lemma]
:

Lemma 2.12
.

The set of all irreducible classes is a partially ordered set with respect to the relation
\to
.

Definition 2.13

(Essential classes)
.

A maximal element in the set of irreducible classes with respect to order
\to
is called
essential class
(or
absorbing class
).

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\pi
be a transition matrix over
V
. A state
x\in V
is in an essential class if and only if
x\to y
implies
y\to x
for any
y\in V
.

Proof.

This is clear from the definition.
∎

Definition 2.15

(Closed sets and absorbing states)
.

A subset
W\subseteq V
is called
closed
, if no element outside
W
can be reached from any state
x\in W
. A single state
x
, forming a closed set, is called
absorbing
.

Assumption 2.16
.

We assume that
\pi(x,x)>0
if and only if
\pi(x,x)=1
, i.e., we do not have loops, except for absorbing states.

The definition of recurrence and transience is classical, see e.g.
[
14
, Chapter 3]
, i.e.,
for a random walk
X=(X_{n})
on a state space
V
with transition matrix
\pi
, a state
x\in V
is called
recurrent
if

\mathbb{P}[X_{n}=x\mbox{ for infinitely many }n]=1

and
transient
otherwise. In fact, in case of transience we have

\mathbb{P}[X_{n}=x\mbox{ for infinitely many }n]=0,

see e.g.
[
14
, 3.2. Theorem]
.

The classical theory gives the following connection between the type of a state and convergence of sums over
\pi^{(n)}
, see e.g.
[
14
, 3.4. Theorem (a)]

Theorem 2.17
.

Let
\pi
be a transition matrix over
V
. Then the following holds:

(a)

A state
x\in V
is transient if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi^{(n)}(x,x)<\infty.

(b)

A state
x\in V
is recurrent if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi^{(n)}(x,x)=\infty.

It is known, that being recurrent or transient is a property of an irreducible class, see e.g.
[
14
, Theorem 3.4 and p. 45]

Theorem 2.18
.

All the vertices of the same class are recurrent or transient simultaneously.

Therefore, the type – recurrent or transient – is defined for irreducible classes. The following theorem is found in
[
14
, 3.4. Theorem (b) and (c)]

Theorem 2.19
.

Every
recurrent class is an essential class and
every finite essential class is recurrent.

Corollary 2.20
.

Let
V
be finite and
\pi
be a transition matrix over
V
. A state
x\in V
is recurrent if and only if
x\to y
implies
y\to x
for any
y\in V
.

Proof.

Clearly, if
x
is recurrent, then there must be a path to return to
x
from any
y
which can be reached by
x
. On the other hand, if
x\to y
implies
y\to x
for all
y\in V
, then
x
is in an essential class.
∎

3.
A correspondence between non-Archimedean graphs and random walks

It is known that real graphs stand in a one-to-one relationship to reversible random walks. In this section we identify a class of random walks which stand in a one-to-one correspondence to graphs over non-Archimedean fields. In turn many of the investigations for the probability theory on non-Archimedean graphs can draw from the rich theory of those random walks.

3.1.
Constructing a random walk for a non-Archimedean graph

In this subsection we construct a mapping between a non-Archimedean graph and a random walk. To this end recall the set
\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}
of at most finite elements which is an ordered ring and which we will map to
\mathbb{R}
as described below. To this end, we are looking for the unique real number, from which an element
k\in\mathbb{K}
differs by an infinitesimal. Note that
\mathbb{K}
includes
\mathbb{Q}
as
\mathbb{K}
is an ordered field.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}
, there is a unique real number
\rho(k)
, which differs from
k
only by an infinitesimal in an ordered field extension including
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be an ordered extension which includes
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{R}
, e.g. one can take the surreal numbers, which contains all non-Archimedean fields as subfields (see
[
1
, Theorem 24.29]
).
For
k\in\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}
, let

\displaystyle L(k)=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}\mid q\preceq k\}\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{and}\hskip 17.00024ptU(k)=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}\mid q\succeq k\}

Then,
L(k)
and
U(k)
are also subsets of
\mathbb{R}
in which they have a supremum
l=\sup_{\mathbb{R}}L(k)
and an infimum
u=\inf_{\mathbb{R}}U(k)
.
Since
l
and
u
both exist in
\mathbb{F}
, they differ from
k
only by an infinitesimal and are therefore equal in
\mathbb{R}
. Then, this is the number
\rho(k)
we are looking for. Uniqueness is clear by the triangle inequality in
\mathbb{F}
.
∎

Definition 3.2

(Real part)
.

We define the map
\varrho:\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}\to\mathbb{R}
so that
\varrho(k)
is the unique real number such that
k-\varrho(k)
is infinitesimal in an ordered field extension including
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{R}
.

We collect the following basic properties of the map
\rho
.

Lemma 3.3

(Basic properties of
\rho
)
.

The map
\rho
is an order preserving ring morphism, i.e., for
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}
, we have

\varrho(k_{1}+k_{2})=\varrho(k_{1})+\varrho(k_{2})
,
\varrho(k_{1}\cdot k_{2})=\varrho(k_{1})\cdot\varrho(k_{2})
and if
k_{1}\succeq k_{2}
, then
\varrho(k_{1})\geq\varrho(k_{2})
.
Furthermore, we have the following properties for
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}
.

(1)

\varrho
maps infinitesimal elements to zero.

(2)

if
\rho(k_{1})<\rho(k_{2})
, then
k_{1}\prec k_{2}.

(3)

if
\varrho(k_{1})\neq 0
, then
\varrho(1/k_{1})=1/\varrho(k_{1})
.

(4)

If a sequence
(k_{n})
in
\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}
converges to
k
in
\mathbb{K}
, then the sequence
(\rho(k_{n}))
is eventually constant and equal to
\rho(k)
in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The proof is straightforward.
∎

To relate a random walk to a non-Archimedean graph
b
we recall the normalized weight
p(x,y)=b(x,y)/b(x)
,
x,y\in V
. Clearly,
p
takes values in
\mathbb{K}_{\precsim 1}
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
U\subseteq V
. We let
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
be the
real transition matrix for
b
given by

\pi(x,y)=\begin{cases}\varrho(p(x,y)),&x\in U,\\
1,&x\in V\setminus U\mbox{ and }x=y,\\
0,&x\in V\setminus U\mbox{ and }x\neq y.\\
\end{cases}

Note, that if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
, then
\pi^{b}_{U}=p_{U}
is the classical transition matrix for graphs with boundary (or without boundary if
U=V
).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
U\subseteq V
. Then
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
is a transition matrix whose diagonal vanishes outside of the absorbing states which are exactly the elements of
V\setminus U
.

Proof.

For every
x\in U
, we obtain
0\leq\rho(p(x,y))\leq 1
by the properties of the mapping
\varrho
, since
0\preceq p(x,y)\preceq 1
. For
x\in U
, we have by linearity and local finiteness of
\varrho
that

\sum_{y\in V}\pi(x,y)=\sum_{y\in V}\varrho(p(x,y))=\varrho\left(\sum_{y\in V}p(x,y)\right)=\varrho(1)=1.

Hence,
\pi
is a transition matrix. Moreover, for
x\in U
, we have

\pi(x,x)=\rho(p(x,x))=\rho(b(x,x)/b(x))=\rho(0)=0
since
b
vanishes on the diagonal. Finally, for
x\in V\setminus U
, we have
\pi(x,x)=1
by definition of
\pi
which finishes the proof.
∎

The lemma shows that we are indeed dealing with a transition matrix which gives rise to a random walk.
This allows us to give a definition of whether a vertex in a weighted non-Archimedean graph is recurrent or transient.

Definition 3.6

(Recurrent and transient vertices)
.

Let
b
be a weighted graph over
V
,
U\subseteq V
and
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
be the corresponding transition matrix. We call a vertex
x\in U

recurrent
(respectively
transient
) with respect to
U
if
x
is a recurrent (respectively transient) state with respect to
\pi
.

3.2.
Strongly connected and essential components

We start by exploring some properties of the transition matrix
\pi
arising from a weighted non-Archimedean graph. Our aim is to relate directed paths with respect to
\pi
to specific paths with respect to
b
. This will lead us to our first criterion on recurrence and transience, Theorem
3.15
dependent on the type of component vertices are found in.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
,
U\subseteq V
and
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
be the corresponding transition matrix. Then
\pi(x,y)>0
(i.e.,
x\leadsto y
) if and only if one of the following condition holds:

(a)

x\in V\setminus U
and
x=y
,

(b)

x\in U
and
p(x,y)\simeq 1
.

In particular, for
x,y\in U
, we have
\pi(x,y)>0
if and only if
p_{U}(x,y)\simeq 1
. Moreover, if
b(x,y)\prec\hskip-2.0pt\prec b(x)
for
x,y\in V
,
x\neq y
, then

\pi(x,y)=0.

Proof.

If (a) holds then
\pi(x,x)=1
by definition. If (b) holds then
p(x,y)\succeq c
for some
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
. Hence,
\pi(x,y)\geq c
since
\rho
is order preserving.

Assume
\pi(x,y)>0
and (a) does not hold. If
x=y
and
x\in U
, then
p(x,y)=0
and, thus,
\pi(x,y)=0
. If
x\neq y
and
x\in V\setminus U
, then
\pi(x,y)=0
by definition. Hence,
x\neq y
and
x\in U
. Finally, from
0<\pi(x,y)=\rho(p(x,y))
, we infer that
p(x,y)\simeq 1
. This shows the equivalence.

The first “in particular” statement is immediate and the second is also clear as
p(x,y)=b(x,y)/b(x)
is infinitesimal in this case.
∎

Recall that we denote
x\to y
for two vertices if there is a directed path from
x
to
y
with respect to a transition matrix.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let a graph
b
over
V
, a subset
U\subseteq V
and
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
be given. For two vertices
x,y\in U
, we have
x\to y
if and only if there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
p^{(n)}_{U}(x,y)\simeq~1
.

Proof.

If
x=y
, then
p^{(0)}_{U}(x,x)=1
and
x\to x
by definition, so there is nothing to prove.

So, let
x\neq y
. Note that
p^{(n)}_{U}(x,y)\simeq 1
is equivalent to the existence of
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
such that
p^{(n)}_{U}(x,y)\succ~c
.

Let
x\to y
. Then there exists a directed path between
x
and
y

x=x_{0}\leadsto x_{1}\leadsto x_{2}\leadsto\dots\leadsto x_{n}=y,

and we can assume that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
for
i\neq j
on the path. Then by (b) of Lemma
3.7
and since
y\in U
we obtain that
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in U
and that there are
c_{0},\ldots,c_{n-1}\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
such that
p_{U}(x_{i},x_{i+1})=p(x_{i},x_{i+1})\succ c_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. Therefore, we obtain
p^{(n)}_{U}(x,y)\simeq~1
since

p_{U}^{(n)}(x,y)\succeq p(x,x_{1})p(x_{1},x_{2})\dots p(x_{n-1},y)\succ c_{0}\cdot c_{1}\dots c_{n-1}=:c.

Assume now
p^{(n)}_{U}(x,y)\simeq~1
, i.e.,
p_{U}^{(n)}(x,y)\succ c\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
. Assume, that for a fixed
n
all paths
x=x_{0}\sim\ldots\sim x_{n}=y
in
U
with respect to
b

one has

p_{U}(x,x_{1})p_{U}(x_{1},x_{2})\dots p_{U}(x_{n-1},y)\prec\hskip-2.0pt\prec 1.

Then, due to the local finiteness there exists an infinitesimal
\tau\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
such that for any such path we have

p_{U}(x,x_{1})p_{U}(x_{1},x_{2})\dots p_{U}(x_{n-1},y)\preceq\tau

and consequently

p_{U}^{(n)}(x,y)\preceq\#\{\mbox{undirected paths from $x$ to $y$}\}\tau

is infinitesimal, which is a contradiction. Therefore, there exists an undirected path
x_{0}\sim\ldots\sim x_{n-1}
in
U
from
x
to
y

such that

p_{U}(x,x_{1})p_{U}(x_{1},x_{2})\dots p_{U}(x_{n-1},y)\succ c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}.

Now, since all the factors are smaller than
1
, any of those factors can be bounded from below with a positive rational number. Hence,

x=x_{0}\leadsto x_{1}\leadsto x_{2}\leadsto\dots\leadsto x_{n}=y

which gives
x\to y
.
∎

The next theorem relates paths for
\pi
to paths for
b
with certain additional properties.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
,
U\subseteq V
,
x,y\in U
and
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
. Then the following holds:

{(a)}

We have

x\to y
if and only if there exists a path

x=x_{0}\sim x_{1}\sim\dots\sim x_{n}=y

in
U
(with respect to
b
) such that for all
i=0,\ldots,n-1

b(x_{i},x_{i+1})\simeq b(x_{i}).

In particular, in this case

{b(x_{i+1})\succsim b(x_{i})}
,
i=0,\ldots,n-1

and
{b(y)}\succsim{b(x)}
.
Furthermore, in this case this path is also a directed path (with respect to
\pi
)

x=x_{0}\leadsto x_{1}\leadsto\dots\leadsto x_{n}=y

and any such directed path is a path with respect to
b
with the properties above.

{(b)}

Let
x\to y
. Then
y\to x
if and only if
b(x)\simeq b(y).

{(c)}

We have
x\leftrightarrow y
if and only if
b(x)\simeq b(y)
and there exists a path

x=x_{0}\sim x_{1}\sim\dots\sim x_{n}=y

in
U
such that for all
i=0,\ldots,n-1

with

b(x_{i},x_{i+1})\simeq b(x).

In particular, this path is also a directed path form
x
to
y
and from
y
to
x
.

Proof.

Observe that if
v,w\in U
, then
p_{U}(v,w)=p(v,w)
which we will apply throughout the proof.

(a)
Assume
x\to y
. By Lemma
3.8

p_{U}^{(n)}(x,y)\simeq 1
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e,. there exist
n\in~\mathbb{N},c\in~\mathbb{Q}^{+}
such that
p_{U}^{(n)}(x,y)\succ c
. Then there exists a path

x=x_{0}\sim x_{1}\sim\dots\sim x_{n}=y

with
p_{U}(x,x_{1})\dots p_{U}(x_{n-1},y)\succ c^{\prime}
for some
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
(if all the paths would be infinitesimal, then
p_{U}^{(n)}
would be infinitesimal).
Since
p_{U}\preceq 1
, we have

1\succeq\dfrac{b(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{b(x_{i})}=p(x_{i},x_{i+1})=p_{U}(x_{i},x_{i+1})\succ c^{\prime}

and we immediately get
b(x_{i},x_{i+1})\simeq b(x_{i})
for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
.

For the other direction, assume there exists a path
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
as described. By finiteness of the path, there exist
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
such that

1\succeq p(x_{i},x_{i+1})=p_{U}(x_{i},x_{i+1})=\dfrac{b(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{b(x_{i})}\succ c.

Thus, we have
x_{i}\leadsto x_{i+1}
due to Lemma
3.7
and, hence,
x\to y
.

The “in particular” statement follows from the fact, that
b(x_{i+1})\succeq b(x_{i},x_{i+1})
for all
i=0,\ldots,n-1
and transitivity of
\succeq
. The final statement of (a) was shown along the proof.

(b)
Let
x\to y
.
Then, by Lemma
3.8
,
x\to y
implies the existence of
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
such that
p_{U}^{(n)}(x,y)\succ c
.
Since,

p_{U}^{(n)}(y,x)=\dfrac{b(y)}{b(x)}p_{U}^{(n)}(x,y)

a lower bound by a positive rational on
p_{U}^{(n)}(y,x)
is equivalent to a corresponding lower bound on
{b(y)}/{b(x)}
(as
p_{U}\succeq 1
). On the other hand, the “in particular” statement also yields an upper bound by a rational on
{b(y)}/{b(x)}
in the case
y\to x
.

(c) This follows directly from
(a)
and
(b)
.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

If
x\leftrightarrow y
and
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is a directed path from

to

, then
(x_{n},x_{n-1},\ldots,x_{0})
is a directed path from

to

, i.e.,

y=x_{n}\sim x_{n-1}\sim\dots\sim x_{0}=x.

This follows from proof of

of Theorem

, since

.

These relations between a graph

(Strongly connected components)
.

Let

.

Furthermore, we can now define the notion of an essential or absorbing component.

(Essential components)
.

Let

be a graph over

and

The next corollary shows that essential components are exactly the essential classes of the corresponding random walk.

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
b
be a graph over

and
U\subseteq V
.
The essential components of
\pi_{U}^{b}
, which are not absorbing states, are exactly the strongly connected components of

.

Proof.

This an immediate consequence of Theorem

and the definition of an essential class.
∎

This corollary allows for a first necessary condition on recurrence.

Theorem 3.15

(A first recurrence/transience criterion)
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
,

and
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
.

(a)

If a vertex is recurrent, then it is in an essential component. Furthermore, if an essential component is finite, then every contained vertex is recurrent.

(b)

If a vertex is not in an essential component, then it is transient.

Proof.

The result follows directly from Corollary
3.14
and Theorem
2.19
.
∎

3.3.
Characterizing the random walks obtained by the construction

In the previous section we constructed a transition matrix
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
of a random walk for a graph
b
over
V
and a subset
U\subseteq V
. These random walk are directed weighted graphs and we next turn to the question which kind of directed graphs are obtained in terms of combinatorics.

We start with a result which is a consequence of Lemma

and Theorem

. It shows that distance minimizing paths with respect to

and the specific paths identified above with respect to

coincide.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
and
U\subseteq V
. Let

such that

. Then the shortest directed paths from

to

and from

to

with respect to

Proof.

It follows immediately, since by Corollary
3.10
and Theorem

(c), all the directed paths from

to

to

.
∎

This theorem allows us to give an example to exclude certain phenomena.

Example 3.17

In a graph

over a vertex set

with

the directed graph

x_{1}
x_{2}
x_{3}
x_{4}

Indeed, by Corollary

Lemma 3.18

Let

Next, we state the criterion for a directed graph to arise from a non-Archimedean graph. To this end, we to need some basic facts on theory of partially ordered sets.
Note that irreducible classes of any directed graph together with relation
\to
form a partially ordered set. We treat the finite and infinite case separately.

Definition 3.19
.

For a partially ordered set
(A,\to)
and two elements
a,b\in A
a

from
a
to
b
is any finite sequence
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
in

such that

The
height

h(a)
of

is the supremum of the lengths of paths from any element of
A
to
a
.

Observe that if
A
is finite then
h(a)
is the maximal length of chains from minimal elements to

and, in particular, finite. In this case, it is immediate to see that

is order preserving, i.e., if

, then
h(a)<h(b)
.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
\pi
be a transition matrix over a finite set
V
. Then, there exists a weighted graph
b
over
V
with respect to a non-Archimedean ordered field
\mathbb{K}
and
U\subseteq V
such that
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
if and only if there is a function
\beta:V\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
for any irreducible class
C
and

\pi(x,y)\beta(x)=\pi(y,x)\beta(y)

and
\pi(x,x)=0
if
x
is not an absorbing state.
Furthermore, for a given transition matrix,
\beta
can be chosen such that
\beta(a)=1
for some
a\in C

\beta(x)=\rho(b(x)/b(a))
for all

.

Proof.

We construct

and

. First, we fix an infinitely large

, i.e.,

. Furthermore, we know that the set of irreducible classes is a finite partially ordered set and has a height function

. For
x\in V
contained in a irreducible class

, we denote
h(x)=h(C)
.
We let
U

be the subset of
V
which are not absorbing states. To define

, we let

on
(V\setminus U)\times(V\setminus U)
. Furthermore, we define
for

\displaystyle b(x,y)=\pi(x,y)\beta(x)\mathcal{N}^{h({x})}

\displaystyle b(x,y)=\max\{\pi(x,y)\beta(x),\pi(y,x)\beta(y)\}\mathcal{N}^{\min\{h({x}),h({y})\}}.

Then

is clearly symmetric and has zero diagonal.
Observe, for
x\in U

\displaystyle b(x,y)=\begin{cases}\pi(x,y)\beta(x)\mathcal{N}^{h({x})}&:x\leadsto y,\mbox{ i.e., $\pi(x,y)>0$}\\
\pi(y,x)\beta(y)\mathcal{N}^{h({y})}&:\mbox{else}.\end{cases}

Note that in the second case we have
b(x,y)=0
whenever

and

whenever
y\leadsto x
.
As
\pi(x,x)=0
for

\displaystyle b(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y:x\leadsto y}b(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y:x\not\leadsto y,\\
\;\;y\leadsto x\end{subarray}}b(x,y)=\sum_{y\in V}\pi(x,y)\beta(x)\mathcal{N}^{h({x})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y:x\not\leadsto y,\\
\;\;y\leadsto x\end{subarray}}\pi(y,x)\beta(y)\mathcal{N}^{h({y})}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y:x\not\leadsto y,\\
\;\;y\leadsto x\end{subarray}}\pi(y,x)\frac{\beta(y)}{\beta(x)}\mathcal{N}^{-(h(x)-h({y}))}\right)\beta(x)\mathcal{N}^{h(x)}=(1+\tau)\beta(x)\mathcal{N}^{h(x)}

for some infinitesimal
\tau
. We obtain for
\pi(x,y)>0
and
x\neq y
(which implies
x\in U
)

\displaystyle\pi^{b}_{U}(x,y)=\rho(p(x,y))=\rho\left(\frac{b(x,y)}{b(x)}\right)=\rho\left(\frac{\pi(x,y)\beta(x)\mathcal{N}^{h(x)}}{(1+\tau)\beta(x)\mathcal{N}^{h(x)}}\right)=\rho\left(\frac{\pi(x,y)}{(1+\tau)}\right)=\pi(x,y)

and for
\pi(x,y)=0
(which includes the case
x\in V\setminus U
and
x\neq y
) where we have either
\pi(y,x)=0
or
h(y)<h(x)

\displaystyle\pi^{b}_{U}(x,y)=\rho\left(\frac{\pi(y,x)\beta(y)\mathcal{N}^{h(y)}}{(1+\tau)\beta(x)\mathcal{N}^{h(x)}}\right)=\rho\left(N^{-(h(x)-h(y))}\frac{\pi(y,x)\beta(y)}{(1+\tau)\beta(x)}\right)=0=\pi(x,y).

Finally,
\pi_{U}^{b}(x,x)=1
by definition for
x\in V\setminus U
.

To prove the opposite direction we fix in each irreducible class
C
a vertex
x\in C
and put

\beta(x)=1.

Further, for any
y\in C
, we prescribe
\beta(y)=\varrho({b(y)}/{b(x)})
. Then, we have, for any
y,z\in~C
,

\displaystyle\pi(y,z)\beta(y)=\varrho(p(y,z))\varrho\left(\dfrac{b(y)}{b(x)}\right)=\varrho\left(\dfrac{p(y,z)b(y)}{b(x)}\right)=\varrho\left(\dfrac{p(z,y)b(z)}{b(x)}\right)=\pi(z,y)\beta(z),

where the second and last equality hold, since
p\preceq 1
and
{b(y)}/{b(x)}
as well as
{b(z)}/{b(x)}
have no infinitely large component, since
y,z
and
x
are all in the same irreducible class
C
, confer Theorem
3.9
.
∎

The idea of proof for infinite graph is similar, but there is a difference: we can not necessarily map an infinite partially ordered set to natural numbers, see e.g.
[
7
]
.
To this end, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
(A,\to)
be a countable partially ordered set. Then, there exists an order preserving mapping

h_{\infty}:A\to\mathbb{Q}.

Proof.

Since
A
is countable, we can enumerate it (in an arbitrary not necessarily order preserving way), i.e.,
A=\{a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\}
.
Let
A_{n}=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\}
and we define
h_{\infty}(a_{n})
inductively over
n
. Let
h_{\infty}(a_{0})=0
. Having defined
h_{\infty}(a_{0}),\ldots,h_{\infty}(a_{n})
in an order preserving way. Then, there exists an
q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

\max_{b\in A_{n},b\to a_{n+1}}h_{\infty}(b)<q<\min_{b\in A_{n},a_{n+1}\to b}h_{\infty}(b)

and we put
h_{\infty}(a_{n+1})=q
.
∎

We now come to an analogue of Theorem
3.20
for infinite graphs.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
\pi
be a locally finite transition matrix over an at most countable set
V
. Then, there exists a weighted graph
b
over
V
with respect to a real closed non-Archimedean ordered field and
U\subseteq V
such that
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
if and only if there is a function
\beta:V\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
for any irreducible class
C
and
x,y\in C

\pi(x,y)\beta(x)=\pi(y,x)\beta(y)

and
\pi(x,x)=0
if
x
is not an absorbing state. Furthermore,
\beta
can be chosen such that
\beta(a)=1
for some
a\in C
and
\beta(x)=\rho(b(x)/b(a))
for all
x\in C
.

Proof.

Lemma
3.21
allows us to reproduce the proof of Theorem
3.20
by replacing
h
with
h_{\infty}
. Note, that we need a real closedness in order to have rational powers of an infinitesimal
\mathcal{N}
.
∎

Remark 3.23
.

Given the height function
h
,
the proof of Theorem
3.20
is an explicit construction and also
h
can be obtained explicitly from a finite random walk. However, in the infinite case, the inductive construction of
h_{\infty}
often does not allow for an explicit construction. In consequence, the infinite graphs arising from
Theorem
3.22
are not as explicit as the crucial ingredient
h_{\infty}
is not.

4.
The reciprocal of the normalized capacity

In this section we study a quantity
G
which corresponds to the diagonal of the Green’s function. In order to define this quantity we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
. For finite connected sets
K\subseteq L\subseteq V
and
a\in K
,

\dfrac{\operatorname{cap}_{L}(a)}{b(a)}\preceq\dfrac{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)}{b(a)}\preceq 1.

Furthermore,

\dfrac{1}{\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{L}(a)}{b(a)}\right)}\geq\dfrac{1}{\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)}{b(a)}\right)}=\dfrac{1}{\sum_{x\in V}{(1-\varrho(v(x)))\varrho(p(a,x))}}\geq 1.

where
v
is the solution of Dirichlet problem (
DP
) for
K
and
a
.

Proof.

By monotonicity of the capacity, Lemma
2.7
, and the maximum principle, Proposition
2.3
, we get

\dfrac{\operatorname{cap}_{L}(a)}{b(a)}\preceq\dfrac{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)}{b(a)}=\Delta v(a)=\dfrac{1}{b(a)}\sum_{x\in V}{(1-v(x))}b(a,x)\preceq\dfrac{\sum_{x\in V}b(a,x)}{b(a)}\preceq 1,

where
v
is the solution of Dirichlet problem (
DP
) for
K
and
a
which satisfies
v\succ 0
. Thus, we can apply
\varrho
to the left hand side and the second statement follows as
\varrho
is an order preserving ring morphism and the definition of
p(a,x)=b(a,x)/b(a)
.
∎

This allows us to define the quantity which is vital for the further considerations of this paper.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
. For finite connected
K\subseteq V
and
a\in K
, we define

{G}_{K}(a)=\dfrac{1}{\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)}{b(a)}\right)}.

Furthermore, for an exhaustion
(K_{n})
of
V
and
a\in K_{0}
, we define

{G}(a)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}{G}_{K_{n}}(a)\in[1,\infty].

Observe that due to Lemma
4.1
, we have

1\leq{G}_{K_{n}}(a)\leq{G}_{K_{n+1}}(a)\leq{G}(a)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, the limit superior above is indeed a limit. Furthermore, if the graph has positive or null capacity, i.e.,
\operatorname{cap}(a)
exists, then

{G}(a)=\dfrac{1}{\varrho\left(\dfrac{\operatorname{cap}(a)}{b(a)}\right)},

where
1/0=\infty
. Next, we discuss a local Harnack inequality which will eventually allow us to conclude that
{G}
is either finite or infinite on strongly connected components.

Lemma 4.3

(Local Harnack inequality)
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
and let
v
be the solution of Dirichlet problem (
DP
) for a finite connected
K\subseteq V
and
a\in K
. If
x,y\in V
such that
x\to y
with respect to
\pi_{K}^{b}
, then

v(x)\succsim v(y)
. In particular, if
x
and
y
belong to the same irreducible class, then
v(x)\simeq v(y)
. Furthermore, the constants in the two sided estimates are uniform when increasing
K
.

Proof.

If
x=a
or if
y\not\in K
, then the statement is obvious since
0\preceq v\preceq 1=v(a)
by maximum principle, Proposition
2.3
. So assume
x\in K
, since otherwise
x
is an absorbing state and there is no
y\neq x
with
x\to y
.
By Theorem
3.9
there exists a path
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
in
K
and
b(x_{i})\simeq b(x_{i},x_{i+1})
, i.e.,
{b(x_{i},x_{i+1})}/{b(x_{i})}\succeq c
for some
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
.

If
x_{i}\neq a
for all
i
, we have by
\Delta v(x_{i})=0
that

v(x_{i})=\dfrac{\sum_{y\in V}b(x_{i},y)v(y)}{b(x_{i})}\succeq\frac{b(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{b(x_{i})}v(x_{i+1})\succeq cv(x_{i+1}),

since
v\succeq 0
by the maximum principle. We conclude
v(x)\succsim v(y)
by induction. Further observe that the path above is included in any finite set including
K
.

If there exists
i_{0}
such that
x_{i_{0}}=a
, then we can conclude from
\Delta v(x_{i})=0,i<i_{0}
, in the same way as above, that
v(x)\succeq cv(a)=c
. Since
v(y)\preceq 1
by maximum principle, Theorem
2.3
, the statement follows.
∎

Recall that a strongly connected component with respect to
U
is a maximal subset
W\subseteq U
such that for any
x,y\in W

there exists a path
x=x_{0}\sim\dots\sim x_{n}=y
in
U
such that

b(x)\simeq b(x_{i},x_{i+1})\simeq b(y)
for all
i
. These are exactly the strongly connected components of
\pi_{U}^{b}
which are not absorbing states, Corollary
3.12
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
and
x,y\in K
.

(a)

If
K\subseteq V
is finite and connected and if
x
and
y
belong to the same strongly connected component with respect to
K
, then
{G}_{K}(x)
and
{G}_{K}(y)
are either both finite or both infinite.

(b)

If
x
and
y
belong to the same strongly connected component with respect to
V
, then
{G}(x)
and
{G}(y)
are either both finite or both infinite.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
we have

\dfrac{v^{x}(y)}{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(x)}=\dfrac{v^{y}(x)}{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(y)}.

and by Lemma
4.3

v^{x}(y)\simeq v^{x}(x)=1=v^{y}(y)\simeq v^{y}(x)
. Hence
\operatorname{cap}_{K}(x)\simeq\operatorname{cap}_{K}(y)
. Since
b(x)\simeq b(y)
by Theorem
3.9
,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(x)}{b(x)}\simeq\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(y)}{b(y)}

and the first statement follows due to the definition of
{G}_{K}
. For the second statement we observe that for an exhaustion
(K_{n})
the capacity
(\operatorname{cap}_{K_{n}})
is monotone decreasing and the constants in the two sided estimate are uniform in the increasing sequence
(K_{n})
by the Harnack inequality, Lemma
4.3
. Thus, we infer the statement also for the limit.
∎

We end this section with a lemma that shows that the real part of the solution of the Dirichlet problem for a vertex
a
is constant on all irreducible classes on which
b
is infinitely larger than
b(a)
. Recall that the solution
v
of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
) satisfies
0\preceq v\preceq 1
by the maximum principle, Proposition
2.3
and we can therefore study
\rho(v)
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
and
v
be the solution of Dirichlet problem (
DP
) for a finite connected set
K
and
a\in K
. Let
x\in V
be such that
b(x)\succ\hskip-2.0pt\succ b(a)
. Then
\varrho(v)
is constant on the irreducible class which contains
x
.

Proof.

Let
C
be the irreducible class which contains
x
. Then
b(y)\simeq b(x)
for all
y\in C
by Theorem
3.9
.
Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in C
be such that
\pi(x_{1},x_{2})>0
. Then
b(x_{1},x_{2})\simeq b(x_{1})\succ\hskip-2.0pt\succ b(a)
. Assume that
\varrho(v(x_{1}))\neq\varrho(v(x_{2}))
, i.e.,
|v(x_{1})-v(x_{2})|\simeq 1
. Then, since
\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)=Q(v)
, we obtain

\dfrac{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)}{b(a)}=\dfrac{\sum_{x,y\in V}b(x_{1},x_{2})(v(x_{1})-v(x_{2}))^{2}}{b(a)}\succsim\dfrac{b(x_{1},x_{2})}{b(a)}\succ\hskip-2.0pt\succ 1,

which is a contradiction to Lemma
4.1
.
∎

5.
A characterization for recurrence and transience

In this section we study recurrence and transience in view of
{G}
. First of all notice that a first determination of whether a vertex is recurrent or transient can be made on whether it is contained in an essential component, Theorem
3.15
. Hence, the open question remains to determine the type of a state in essential components which will be completely characterized in terms of
{G}
in Theorem
5.4
. However, we will indeed prove more namely that the implication that finiteness of
{G}(a)
tells us that
a
is transient, Corollary
5.2
. One may wonder whether also the reverse direction holds outside of essential components. This is not the case and will be shown by counterexamples at the end of the section.

Before we start, we recall what is classically known for graphs
b
over the reals. Then
[
14
, 3.4 Theorem]
tells us that

\displaystyle\dfrac{b(a)}{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{K}^{n}1_{a}(a)\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{and}\hskip 17.00024pt\dfrac{b(a)}{\operatorname{cap}(a)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}1_{a}(a)

for any finite subset
K\subseteq V
and
a\in K
. In the second formula, the both sides are infinity if
\operatorname{cap}(a)=0
which is the case if
a
is a recurrent state.

For the non-Archimedean case, these equalities do only hold if right hand sides converge, see
[
4
, Theorems 8.2 and 8.5]
.

In this section we prove a more general statement, using
{G}_{K}(a)
(resp.
{G}(a)
) instead of capacity and the real transition matrix of the graph instead of the non-Archimedean one.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
, let
K\subseteq V
be finite and connected,
\pi_{K}=\pi_{K}^{b}
and
\pi=\pi_{V}^{b}
. Then, for any
a\in K

\displaystyle{G}_{K}(a)\geq\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi^{(n)}_{K}(a,a)\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{and}\hskip 17.00024ptG(a)\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi^{(n)}(a,a),

where infinity is allowed on both sides in the second inequality.

Proof.

We calculate using that
\Delta_{K}=I_{K}-P_{K}
is invertible and a telescopic argument

\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{a}(a)-\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{K}^{n}1_{a}(a)=\left(\Delta^{-1}_{K}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{K}^{n}\right)1_{a}(a)\\
=\Delta^{-1}_{K}\left(I_{K}-(I_{K}-P_{K})\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{K}^{n}\right)1_{a}(a)=\left(\Delta^{-1}_{K}P_{K}^{N+1}\right)1_{a}(a)\succeq 0,

for all
N
due to the non-negativity in
\mathbb{K}
of both matrices, Lemma
2.4
and the definition of
P_{K}
.
Hence, as a consequence of Lemma
2.4
and the definition of the capacity, we get

\frac{1}{\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)}{b(a)}}=\dfrac{b(a)}{\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)}=\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{a}(a)\succeq\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{K}^{n}1_{a}(a).

We take
\rho
on both sides and then take the limit
N\to\infty
. This gives the first inequality. For the second inequality, we let
K
be equal to an exhaustion
(K_{n})
and take the monotone limit.
∎

This immediately gives rise to the following corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
a\in V
.

(a)

If
a
is a recurrent state, then
{G}(a)=\infty
.

(b)

If
{G}(a)<\infty
, then
a
is a transient state.

Let a graph
b
and
\pi=\pi_{U}^{b}
for
U\subseteq V
. Then, Theorem
3.22
gives the existence of
\beta:V\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\pi(x,y)\beta(x)=\pi(y,x)\beta(y),

for
a\in C\subseteq V
in an irreducible class
C
, where
\beta
can be chosen such that for some fixed
a\in C
and all other
x\in C

\beta(a)=1\hskip 17.00024pt\mbox{and}\hskip 17.00024pt\beta(x)=\rho\left(\frac{b(x)}{b(a)}\right).

This allows us to define

\displaystyle\beta(x,y)=\pi(x,y)\beta(x)

for all
x,y\in V
. Then,
\beta

is symmetric on irreducible classes and as
\pi
is a transition matrix which sums up to
1
about each vertex

\displaystyle\beta(x)=\sum_{y\in V}\pi(x,y)\beta(x)=\sum_{y\in V}\beta(x,y).

Observe that for two irreducible classes
C
and
C^{\prime}
and
x\in C
and
y\in C^{\prime}
such that
x\leadsto y
we have
\beta(x,y)>0
but
\beta(y,x)=0
. Hence,
\beta
is not necessarily symmetric on
V\times V
whenever there is more than one irreducible class.

We let
\Lambda
be the operator for
f:V\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\Lambda f(x)=\frac{1}{\beta(x)}\sum_{y\in V}\beta(x,y)(f(x)-f(y)).

Then, the restriction
\Lambda_{C}
of
\Lambda
to any irreducible class
C
gives rise to a symmetric graph Laplacian over the reals, confer
[
5
]
. Hence, by the classical theory of graphs over the reals
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
or
[
5
, Section 6]
there is a Green’s function

\Gamma_{C}:C\times C\to(0,\infty].

According to this theory
\Gamma_{C}
is finite on all of
C
if it is finite on one vertex of
C
. Furthermore, if
C
is not an essential component, then there is are
x\in C
and
y\in V\setminus C
such that
x\leadsto y
in which case we have that
\Gamma_{C}
is finite.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
and
\beta
the real graph introduced above for
a\in V
in an essential component
C
. Then,

\displaystyle{G}(a)=\Gamma_{C}(a,a).

Proof.

Observe that for
x,y\in V

\displaystyle\frac{\beta(x,y)}{\beta(x)}=\pi(x,y)=\rho\left(\frac{b(x,y)}{b(x)}\right).

Since
C
is an essential component, we have
b(x,y)=0
for all
x\in C
and
y\in V\setminus C
.

Let
(K_{n})
be an exhaustion of
C
and
a\in K_{0}
. Let
v_{n}
be the solution of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
) for
K_{n}
and
a
. Since
0\preceq v_{n}\preceq 1
by the maximum principle, Proposition
2.3
, we can define

\nu_{n}=\rho(v_{n}).

Then,
\nu_{n}
satisfies
\nu_{n}(a)=1
,
\nu_{n}=0
on
V\setminus K_{n}
and for
x\in K_{n}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{C}\nu_{n}(x)=\sum_{y\in V}\frac{\beta(x,y)}{\beta(x)}(\nu_{n}(x)-\nu_{n}(y))=\rho\left(\Delta v_{n}(x)\right).

Hence,
\nu_{n}
solves the Dirichlet problem for
\Lambda_{C}
for
K_{n}\cap C
and
a
over the reals. Thus, by the theory of graphs over the reals, we have
\Lambda_{C}\nu_{n}(a)>0
and as
(K_{n}\cap C)
exhausts
C
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{C}(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{C}\nu_{n}(a)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\rho(\Delta v_{n}(a))}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\rho\left(\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{K_{n}}(a)}{b(a)}\right)}={G}(a).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Recall that the type of a vertex – recurrent or transient – is invariant within the strongly connected component. We come now to the last main result of this paper which characterizes the type of the states in the essential components.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
,
\pi=\pi_{V}^{b}
and
C
an essential component.

(a)

C
is recurrent if and only if
{G}(a)=\infty
for some (all)
a\in C
.

(b)

C
is transient if and only if
{G}(a)<\infty
for some (all)
a\in C
.

Proof.

The “if” direction follows from Corollary
5.2
and Theorem
4.4
.

For the “only if” direction follows from Lemma
5.3
and the fact that
\Gamma_{C}(a,a)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi^{(n)}(a,a)
by the theory of symmetric real graphs since
C
is assumed to be an essential component.
∎

We can summarize the findings of this paper on recurrence and transience in the following final corollary.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
b
be a graph over
V
,
\pi=\pi_{V}^{b}
and

a strongly connected component.

(a)

is recurrent if and only if

(b)

is transient if and only if

is non-essential or
{G}(a)<\infty
for some (all)

Proof.

This follows directly from Theorem

.
