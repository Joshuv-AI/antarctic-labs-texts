On the complexity of the word problem of the R.​ Thompson group V V
J.C. Birget

Abstract
We analyze the proof by Lehnert and Schweitzer that the word problem of the Thompson group V V is co-context-free, and we show that this word problem is the complement of the cyclic closure of a union of reverse deterministic context-free languages. For certain finite generating sets, this word problem is the complement of the cyclic closure of the union of four deterministic context-free languages. It follows that the deterministic time-complexity of the word problem of V V is at most quadratic.

The group

[

The word problem for groups

For an alphabet
A
, let
A^{-1}
be a (not necessarily disjoint)
copy of
A
. The elements of
A^{-1}
are called the
inverse letters
.
Inversion of letters is treated notationally as an involution, i.e.,

(a^{-1})^{-1}
denotes
a
. We denote
A\cup A^{-1}
by
A^{\pm 1}
.

A
generating set
(also called a
group generating set
) of a group

G
is a subset
A\subseteq G
such that every element of
G
can be
expressed as the product of a sequence of elements of
A^{\pm 1}

(where for all
a\in A
:
\,a^{-1}
is the inverse of
a
in
G
).
A
monoid generating set
of a group
G
is a subset
A\subseteq G
such
that every element of
G
can be expressed as the product of a sequence of
elements of
A
. Hence, if
A
is a group generating set then
A^{\pm 1}
is
a monoid generating set. If
G
has a finite group generating set then
G

also has a finite monoid generating set, and conversely. By default, for
groups, “generating set” means group generating set (here and in most of
the literature). We explicitly say “monoid” when a monoid generating set
is used. In this note we mainly use monoid generating sets, since Turing
machines do not have an inverse operation.
Note that here we only use “interior” generating sets
A
; i.e.,

A,A^{\pm 1}\subseteq G
, and
a^{-1}
is the inverse of
a
in
G
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group, with finite monoid generating set
A
.
If
u,v\in A^{*}
represent the same element of
G
we denote this by

\,u=_{G}v
. The
word problem
of
G
over
A
is defined to be the set

{\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)=\{w\in A^{*}:\,w=_{G}\varepsilon\}
.

The word problem is therefore a formal language over the alphabet
A
.
The languages that arise as word problems of groups have special properties,
some of which appear in the next lemma, and in Section 2.

Lemma 1.1

Let
\,L={\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\,\subseteq A^{*}\,
be the word problem of
any group
G
with finite monoid generating set
A
. Then
L
has the
following properties:

(1)

L
contains
\varepsilon
and is closed under
concatenation; i.e.,
L
is a submonoid of
A^{*}
.

(2)

L
has a unique-factorization property:
every
w\in L\smallsetminus\{\varepsilon\}
can be written in a unique way as a
concatenation of elements of
L\smallsetminus\{\varepsilon\}
that can themselves not be
factored. In other words,
L
is a free submonoid of
A^{*}
.

(3)
The set of free generators of

{\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)
is a bifix code (i.e., it is both a prefix code and a
suffix code).

Proof.
(1) is straightforward by the definition of the word problem.

(2) Suppose
w\in L\smallsetminus\{\varepsilon\}
has two maximal factorizations

w=u_{1}u_{2}\ \ldots\ u_{m}

=

v_{1}v_{2}\ \ldots\ v_{n}
, where

u_{i},v_{j}\in L\smallsetminus\{\varepsilon\}
.
Either
u_{1}=v_{1}
, or
u_{1}
is a strict prefix of
v_{1}
, or
v_{1}
is a
strict prefix of
u_{1}
.
If
u_{1}=v_{1}
then
u_{2}\ \ldots\ u_{m}

=

v_{2}\ \ldots\ v_{n}
, since

A^{*}
is cancellative; so the result follows by induction.
If
u_{1}
is a strict prefix of
v_{1}
then
v_{1}=u_{1}x
for some

x\in A^{+}
; since
u_{1}=_{G}\varepsilon=_{G}v_{1}
, it follows that

x=_{G}\varepsilon
. Hence,
v_{1}
has a factorization
v_{1}=u_{1}x
; this
contradicts maximality of the factorization
v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{n}
of
w
.
If
v_{1}
is a strict prefix of
u_{1}
, the reasoning is similar.

(3) Assume
u
is a strict prefix of
v
, where
u,v
are free
generators of
L
. Then
v=ux
for some
x\in A^{+}
, so since

u=_{G}\varepsilon=_{G}v
, it follows that
x=_{G}\varepsilon
. Hence,
v=ux
with

u,x\in L\smallsetminus\{\varepsilon\}
, which contradicts the assumption that
v
is a
free generator. Hence, no free generator is a prefix of another free
generator.
In a similar way one proves that no free generator is a suffix of another
free generator.

\Box

2
Cyclic closure and reverse of word problems of groups

The
co-word-problem
of a group
G
over the finite monoid generating
set
A
is defined by

{\sf cowp}_{A\!}(G)

\,=\,

A^{*}\smallsetminus{\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)
.

(About the spelling: We write “co-word-problem”, and not
“co-word problem”, because there is no such thing as a “co-word”.)

The
cyclic closure
of a word
w\in A^{*}
, or of a language

L\subseteq A^{*}
, or of a class
{\cal C}
, is defined by

{\sf cyc}(w)=\{yx\,:\,

x,y\in A^{*}\ {\rm and}\ w=xy\}
,

{\sf cyc}(L)\,=\,\bigcup_{w\in L}{\sf cyc}(w)
,

{\sf cyc}({\cal C})\,=\,\{{\sf cyc}(L):L\in{\cal C}\}
.

This is also called the closure under cyclic permutations.

A language
L
is said to be
cyclically closed
iff

L={\sf cyc}(L)
.
A class
\cal C
of languages is said to be cyclically closed
iff
{\sf cyc}({\cal C})\,\subseteq\,{\cal C}
.

The
reverse

\,w^{\rm rev}
of a word
w\in A^{*}
is
defined by induction on length as follows:

\varepsilon^{\rm rev}=\varepsilon
, and

(va)^{\rm rev}=a\,v^{\rm rev}
for all
a\in A
and

v\in A^{*}
.

For a language
L\subseteq A^{*}
, or a class
{\cal C}
, we
define

L^{\rm rev}\,=\,\{w^{\rm rev}:

w\in L\}
, and

{\cal C}^{\rm rev}\,=\,\{L^{\rm rev}:L\in{\cal C}\}
.

A language is said to be
closed under reversal
iff

L=L^{\rm rev}
. A class
\cal C
of languages is said to be closed
under reversal iff
{\cal C}^{\rm rev}\,\subseteq\,{\cal C}
.

(About terminology:
reversal
is an action, the result or outcome of
which is the
reverse
; i.e., applying reversal to
L
leads to the
reverse of
L
. Compare with complementation versus complement.)

Lemma 2.1

​​​.

(1)
If
L\subseteq A^{*}
is cyclically closed then
so is the complement
\,A^{*}\smallsetminus L
.

(2)
For any
L_{1},L_{2}\subseteq A^{*}:

{\sf cyc}(L_{1}\cup L_{2})\,=\,{\sf cyc}(L_{1})\cup{\sf cyc}(L_{2})
.

(3)
For any
L,L_{1},L_{2}\subseteq A^{*}:

\,(A^{*}\smallsetminus L)^{\rm rev}=A^{*}\smallsetminus L^{\rm rev}
, and

(L_{1}\cup L_{2})^{\rm rev}=L_{1}^{\rm rev}\cup L_{2}^{\rm rev}
.

Proof.
(1) Let
A^{*}\smallsetminus L=\overline{L}
. If
L=A^{*}
, then

\overline{L}=\varnothing
is closed under
{\sf cyc}(.)
. Otherwise, consider

w\in\overline{L}
. If
{\sf cyc}(w)\not\subseteq\overline{L}
then there exists

u\in{\sf cyc}(w)\cap L
. Since
L
is closed under
{\sf cyc}(.)
, this
implies
{\sf cyc}(u)\subseteq L
, hence

{\sf cyc}(w)={\sf cyc}(u)\subseteq L
, hence
w\in L
.
This contradicts
w\in\overline{L}
.

(2) If
x\in{\sf cyc}(L_{1}\cup L_{2})
then there exists

u\in L_{1}\cup L_{2}
such that
x\in{\sf cyc}(u)
. If
u\in L_{1}
then

x\in{\sf cyc}(L_{1})
; if
u\in L_{2}
then
x\in{\sf cyc}(L_{2})
. So,

x\in{\sf cyc}(L_{1})\cup{\sf cyc}(L_{2})
.
The converse is straightforward since
L_{1}\subseteq L_{2}
implies

{\sf cyc}(L_{1})\subseteq{\sf cyc}(L_{1}\cup L_{2})
, and similarly for

.

(3) is straightforward.

\Box

Lemma 2.2

(

Proposition 2.5

(non-closure under reversal).

(1)

\bullet

(2)

(1) If

Conversely, let

\,{\sf wp}_{A\cup\{c\}\!}(G)

\big({\sf wp}_{A\cup\{c\}\!}(G)\big)^{\rm rev}

.

Proof: The generator

.

[End, Proof.]

Claim 2:

Problem:
Does
every
finitely generated
non-commutative group
G
have some finite generating set
A

is closed under reversal?

Although in general the word problem is not equal to its reverse,

both the word problem and its reverse have the same complexity
.
To make this precise, we give some definitions.

Definition 2.7

(alphabet morphism).

Let
A
and
B
be two finite alphabets. An alphabet morphism is
a total function
h:A^{*}\to B^{*}
such that for all
u,v\in A^{*}
:

\ h(uv)=h(u)\,h(v)
.

It follows from the definition that
h(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
and that
h
is uniquely
determined by the restriction
h|_{A}
of
h
to
A
.

We have the following fact:
An alphabet morphism
h
is
injective

iff
h|_{A}
is injective and
\,\{h(a):a\in A\}\,

(
\,\subseteq B^{*})
is a
code
(see
[
1
]
).
Hence, an injective alphabet morphism is also called an
alphabetic
encoding
.

For an alphabet morphism
h:A^{*}\to B^{*}
and a language
L\subseteq A^{*}

we define
h(L)\,=\,\{h(w):w\in L\}
; and for a language

L\subseteq B^{*}
we define
h^{-1}(L)\,=\,\{x\in A^{*}:h(x)\in L\}
.

Definition 2.8

A set of languages
{\cal C}
is is closed under (injective)
alphabet morphisms iff for every alphabet
A
, every
L\subseteq A^{*}
with

L\in{\cal C}
, and every (injective) alphabet morphism
h
with domain

A^{*}:

h(L)\,\in\,{\cal C}
.

The set
{\cal C}
is is closed under inverse (injective) alphabet
morphisms iff for every alphabet
B^{*}
, every
L\subseteq B^{*}
with

L\in{\cal C}
, and every (injective) alphabet morphism
h
with image in

B^{*}:

h^{-1}(L)\,\in\,{\cal C}
.

The commonly used language classes are closed under injective alphabet
morphisms and under inverse injective alphabet morphisms:

P
,
NP
,
coNP
,
CF
,
coCF
,
DCF
,

{\sf DTime}(n^{k})
, etc.

Proposition 2.9

(reversal and complexity).

Let
{\cal C}
be a language class that is closed under inverse injective
alphabet morphisms.
(We do not assume that
{\cal C}={\cal C}^{\rm rev}
.)
Then for every finitely generated group
G
with a finite monoid generating
set
A\subseteq G:

{\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\in{\cal C}
iff

\big({\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\big)^{\rm rev}\in{\cal C}
.

Proof.
Obviously,
w=_{G}\varepsilon\,
iff
\,\varepsilon=_{G}w^{-1}
.

Let us first consider the case where
A=A^{-1}
.
Let
h:A^{*}\to A^{*}
be the alphabet morphism defined by
h(a)=a^{-1}
,
for every
a\in A
. Then
h
and
h^{-1}
are injective alphabet morphisms,
and
h=h^{-1}
. And for all

\,w^{-1}=h(w^{\rm rev})
.
Hence,
\big({\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\big)^{\rm rev}

=

h\big({\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\big)
; and this belongs to
\cal C
if

\cal C
is closed under injective alphabet morphisms, or under inverse
injective alphabet morphisms.

If
A\neq A^{-1}
, we first consider an alphabet morphism

\,a\in A\longmapsto i_{a}\in A^{*}
, where
i_{a}
is such that

i_{a}=_{G}a^{-1}
, and
i_{a}
has no subsegment that is
\,=_{G}\varepsilon
.
Since
i_{a}=i_{b}
implies
a^{-1}=_{G}b^{-1}
, hence
a=b
, it follows
that
a\mapsto i_{a}
is injective. (We use the fact that
A
is an interior
generating set, so different elements of
A
are also different in
G
.)

Next we define the alphabet morphism
h:A^{*}\to A^{*}
by

h(a)\,=\,a\,i_{a}\,i_{a}\,
.

Then
\,\{a\,i_{a}\,i_{a}:a\in A\}\,
(
\,\subseteq A^{+}
) is a
prefix code, since for any
a,b\in A
with
a\neq b
,
\,a\,i_{a}\,i_{a}\,
and

\,b\,i_{b}\,i_{b}\,
have no common prefix in
A^{+}
. Hence,
h
is an injective
alphabet morphism, and for all
w\in A^{*}
:
\,(w^{\rm rev})^{-1}=_{G}h(w)
. Then,

\,x\in\big({\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\big)^{\rm rev}\,
iff

\,\varepsilon=_{G}x^{\rm rev}
iff
\,\varepsilon=_{G}(x^{\rm rev})^{-1}=_{G}h(x)\,
iff

h(x)\in{\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)
.
So,
\big({\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\big)^{\rm rev}

=

h^{-1}\big({\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\big)
.
Hence, since
\cal C
is closed under inverse injective alphabet morphisms,

\big({\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\big)^{\rm rev}
belongs to
{\cal C}
.

\Box

Note that Prop.
2.9
does not assume nor

\,{\cal C}

=

{\cal C}^{\rm rev}
, nor that

\,{\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)

=

\big({\sf wp}_{A\!}(G)\big)^{\rm rev}

3
The deterministic complexity of the word problem of
V

Let
\Gamma
be a finite monoid generating set of the Thompson group
V\,

(i.e., every element of
V
is a product of elements of
\Gamma
).
For any
\varphi\in V
we define

{\rm maxlen}(\varphi)\,=\,\max\{\,|z|:

z\in{\rm domC}(\varphi)\,\cup\,{\rm imC}(\varphi)\,\}
;

here we follow the definition of
V
from
[
2
]
, based
on the prefix codes
{\rm domC}(\varphi)
and
{\rm imC}(\varphi)
;

{\rm domC}(\varphi)
is the maximal prefix code that generates the domain

{\rm Dom}(\varphi)
of
\varphi
as a right ideal, and
{\rm imC}(\varphi)

is the maximal prefix code that generates the image
{\rm Im}(\varphi)
of

\varphi
as a right ideal.
For a finite set
S\subseteq V
we define

{\rm maxlen}(S)\,=\,

\max\{\,{\rm maxlen}(\varphi):\varphi\in S\,\}
.

Remark.
The element of
V
generated by
w\in\Gamma^{+}

is denoted by
w(.)
; this is a maximally extended right-ideal morphism of

\{0,1\}^{*}
(by the definition of
V
in
[
2
,
Prop. 2.1 and
Def. 2.6]
). By
[
2
,
Cor. 3.7]
:

\,{\rm maxlen}(w(.))\leq|w|\ {\rm maxlen}(\Gamma)
. Hence, if

x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
satisfies
\,|x|\geq|w|\ {\rm maxlen}(\Gamma)
, then
not only is
w(x)
defined, but
w(x)
can be computed by successively
applying the generators in
w
(without applying extensions to maximum
right-ideal morphisms).

The following Lemma plays a crucial role in the proof by Lehnert and
Schweitzer that the word problem of

is in
coCF
; the Lemma is
intuitive and does not appear explicitly in
[

]
.

Lemma 3.1

(narrow point).

For any
w=a_{n}\ldots a_{1}\in\Gamma^{+}\,
(with
a_{i}\in\Gamma
for

1\leq i\leq n
), and any
\,x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, let

x=x_{0}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{1}}}{{\longmapsto}}

x_{1}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{2}}}{{\longmapsto}}

\ldots

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{i-1}}}{{\longmapsto}}

x_{i-1}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{i}}}{{\longmapsto}}

x_{i}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{i+1}}}{{\longmapsto}}

\ldots

Then there exist

(2)
The following is a computation of

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{1}}}{{\longmapsto}}

z_{1}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{2}}}{{\longmapsto}}

\ldots

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{i-1}}}{{\longmapsto}}

z_{i-1}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{i}}}{{\longmapsto}}

z_{i}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{i+1}}}{{\longmapsto}}

\ldots

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{n}}}{{\longmapsto}}

z_{n}=w(z_{0})
,

with
\,z_{i+1}\,
such that

z_{i+1}=a_{i+1}(z_{i})
, for

.

(3)
There exists
\,k\in

\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
such that
|z_{k}|\leq{\rm maxlen}(\Gamma)
.

Since
x_{0}=z_{0}s
and
x_{n}=z_{n}s:

and

is defined for every suffix

of

and
v(z_{0})
is defined for every

of
w
.

.

of length

\,\leq n\,{\rm maxlen}(\Gamma)\,

of

Definition 3.2

For any
\ell\geq 1
we define
\,\cup_{\ell\,}{\sf DCF}
to be the set
of formal languages that are the union of
\,\leq\ell
deterministic

Obviously,
\,\cup_{\ell\,}{\sf DCF}\subseteq{\sf CF}
, since

is
closed under union. And
\,\cup_{\ell\,}{\sf DCF}\neq{\sf CF}
, since every
language in
\cup_{\ell\,}{\sf DCF}
has bounded degree of ambiguity.

And
\,\cup_{\ell\,}{\sf DCF}\subseteq{\sf DTime}(n)
.
Indeed, every deterministic context-free language can be parsed in linear
time by a deterministic push-down automaton (dpda)
[
11
,
9
]
; and

{\sf DTime}(n)
is closed under finite union.

Let
\Gamma
is any finite monoid generating set of
V
. For any

, let
w(.)
be the element of
V
represented by
w
.
This is a maximally extended right-ideal morphism of
\{0,1\}^{*}

[

if

for every

, then

(
\,=y

The partial action of

on
\{0,1\}^{*}
, and the total action on

, are left (partial) actions.

The following Lemma is straightforward.

Lemma 3.3

For any
z\in\{0,1\}^{+}
and

we have:

s0^{\omega}\neq z0^{\omega}
iff

(1)

|s|\leq|z|\,
and

\,z\neq s0^{|z|-|s|}
; or

(2.1)

|s|\geq|z|\,
and

\,z
is not a prefix of
s
; or

(2.2)

|s|\geq|z|\,
and

\,s=zt\,
for some

.

\Box

Lemma 3.4

For any
z\in\{0,1\}^{+}
, we define the language

L_{z}\,=\,\{\,w\in\Gamma^{+}:\,

w(z0^{\omega})\neq z0^{\omega}\,\}
.

Then
L_{z}\subseteq{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma\!}(V)
; and

L_{z}^{\rm rev}
is deterministic context-free.

Proof.
The fact that
L_{z}\subseteq{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma\!}(V)

is obvious.

The reason why
L_{z}^{\rm rev}
is in
DCF
(and not
L_{z}
) is that in

w(.)=a_{n}\ldots a_{1}(.)
, the functions
a_{i}
are applied from right to
left, since
V
acts on
\{0,1\}^{*}
and
\{0,1\}^{\omega}
on the left.
Below, the action of
V
will be simulated by a deterministic push-down
automaton (dpda), which reads the letters in the order in which
V
acts on

\{0,1\}^{*}
, i.e., starting with
a_{1}
and ending with
a_{n}
.

A preliminary note:
An
endmarker language
consists of an alphabet
\Gamma
, a letter

\square\not\in\Gamma
(called
input endmarker
), and a subset of

\Gamma^{*}\square
; hence the language has the form
L\square
over the
alphabet
\Gamma\cup\{\square\}
, with
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
.
It is known that
L\square
is in
DCF
iff
L
is in
DCF
;
see e.g.,
[
9
,
Thm. 11.2.2]
,

[
16
,
(3rd. ed.) Thm. 2.43]
.
This is a special case of the closure of
DCF
under right quotient by
finite-state languages (since
L=(L\square)\{\square\}^{-1}
, and

\{\square\}
is of course a finite-state language), due to Ginsburg and
Greibach
[
7
]
; see also

[
11
,
Thm. 10.2]
,
[
9
,
Section 11.2]
.

We now construct a deterministic push-down automaton (dpda) that accepts the
endmarker language
L_{z}^{\rm rev}\square
; by the results of Ginsburg and
Greibach, we conclude that
L_{z}^{\rm rev}
is in
DCF
.
See the Appendix of for information about the pda’s used here.

The dpda has state set
\{q_{0},q_{1},q_{\rm a}\}
. The input alphabet is the
chosen monoid generating set
\Gamma
of
V
, and the stack alphabet is

\{0,1,\bot\}
, where
\bot
is the bottom marker of the stack. The start
configuration is
(q_{0},z\bot)
, where
z
is the word in
\{0,1\}^{+}
that
defines
L_{z}
. The dpda accepts the input read so far iff it reaches the
accept state
q_{\rm a}
(acceptance by final state).

A transition of the dpda applies the next input letter
\gamma\in\Gamma

to the current stack content
x\bot
where
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
.
The action in the Thompson group is based on prefix replacements in a
bitstring, and this is very similar to what a pda does on the stack; the
Thompson group elements treat their argument like a stack.
A complication arises from the possibility that
x
might be too short to
belong to
{\rm Dom}(\gamma)
; in that case the dpda treats
x
as
x0^{m}

where
m
is just large enough so that
x0^{m}\in{\rm domC}(\gamma)
.
The set
{\rm domC}(\gamma)
is a maximal prefix code; hence for every
x

that is too short to belong to
{\rm domC}(\gamma)
, there is exactly one

m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
x0^{m}\in{\rm domC}(\gamma)
. (See

[
2
,
Section 1]
for the concept of
domain code

\,{\rm domC}(\gamma)
.)

When the endmarker
\square
is encountered in the input, the dpda starts a
process, based on Lemma
3.3
, that checks whether the current
stack content
s\bot
, with
s\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, satisfies

s0^{\omega}\neq z0^{\omega}
. The dpda uses transitions that can read
several letters deep into the stack (but by a bounded amount, since the set
of transitions is finite and fixed). There are three cases:

(1)

Suppose
|s|\leq|z|
: If
z\neq s0^{|z|-|s|}
(which can be checked by
looking into the stack down to depth
|z|
), the dpda goes to state

q_{\rm a}
by a transition on input
\square
; if
z=s0^{|z|-|s|}

then the dpda rejects, by having no transition.

(2.1)

Suppose
|s|>|z|
: If
z
is not a prefix of
s
(which can be checked by
looking into the stack down to depth
|z|
), then the dpda goes to state

q_{\rm a}
by a transition on input
\square
.

(2.2)

Still assuming
|s|>|z|
: If
z
is prefix of
s
, i.e.,
s=zt
for some

t\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, then the dpda pops
z
on input
\square
and goes to
state
q_{1}
; then in state
q_{1}
the dpda pops all the letters of
t
one
at a time (with
\varepsilon
-transitions), to check whether
t
contains at least
one letter 1 (and accepts in that case) or encounters
\bot
after finding
that
t\in 0^{*}
(and rejects in that case by not having a transition).

In state
q_{\rm a}
the dpda does not have any transitions, hence no word
in
\,L_{z}\square\Gamma^{+}\,
will be accepted.

In detail, the set of transitions of the dpda is

\{\,(q_{0},r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma}}{{\to}}(q_{0},\gamma(r)):\,

\gamma\in\Gamma,\ r\in{\rm domC}(\gamma)\,\}

\,\cup\,

\{\,(q_{0},x\bot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma}}{{\to}}(q_{0},\,\gamma(x0^{m})\,\bot):

\,\gamma\in\Gamma,\ m\geq 1,\ x0^{m}\in{\rm domC}(\gamma)\,\}

\,\cup\,

\{\,(q_{0},s\bot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\square}}{{\to}}(q_{\rm a},s\bot):\,

s\in\{0,1\}^{*},\ |s|\leq|z|,\ z\neq s0^{|z|-|s|}\,\}

\,\cup\,

\{\,(q_{0},sa)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\square}}{{\to}}(q_{\rm a},a):\,

s\in\{0,1\}^{*},|s|=|z|,\ a\in\{0,1\},\ z\neq s\,\}

\,\cup\,

\{\,(q_{0},za)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\square}}{{\to}}(q_{1},a):\ a\in\{0,1\}\,\}

\,\cup\,

\{\,(q_{1},0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon}}{{\to}}(q_{1},\varepsilon)\,\}

\,\cup\,

\{\,(q_{1},1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon}}{{\to}}(q_{\rm a},\varepsilon)\,\}
.

The first two sets of transitions provide a simulation of the
action of
V
.
The remaining sets enable the dpda to recognize whether the stack content

s\bot
satisfies
s0^{\omega}=z0^{\omega}
, when the input endmarker

\square
is read.

Verification that this dpda accepts
L_{z}^{\rm rev}\square:

On input
w^{\rm rev}\square
with
w\in\Gamma^{*}
, the dpda first
reads
w^{\rm rev}
and reaches a configuration

\,w^{\rm rev}\,(q_{0},\,w(z0^{k})\,\bot)
, for some
k\in

{\mathbb{N}}
.
And
w(z0^{k}0^{\omega})=w(z0^{\omega})
, which is equal to

w(z0^{k})\,0^{\omega}
.
When
\square
is read, the dpda checks whether

w(z0^{k})\,0^{\omega}\neq z0^{\omega}
, i.e., whether
w^{\rm rev}

\in

L_{z}^{\rm rev}
.

is equivalent to
\,a_{j}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{j+1}(z_{j})

\neq

z_{j}
,
provided that
a_{j}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{j+1}(z_{j})
is defined; but we actually do not know whether

z_{j}\in{\rm Dom}(a_{j}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{j+1}(.)
.
To avoid the problem of undefinedness of the action on
\{0,1\}^{*}
, we use
the total action of
V
on
\{0,1\}^{\omega}
.
[End, Remark.]

Now,

w(z_{0})\neq z_{0}
iff for all
t\in\{0,1\}^{\omega}
:

w(z_{0}t)\neq z_{0}t
;

and for all
j
with
1\leq j\leq n
, and all

t\in\{0,1\}^{\omega}
:

w(z_{0}t)\neq z_{0}t
iff

a_{j}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{j+1}(z_{j}t)\,\neq\,z_{j}t
.

In particular, for all
j
:

w(z_{0})\neq z_{0}
implies
a_{j}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{j+1}(z_{j}0^{\omega})\,\neq\,z_{j}0^{\omega}
.

By Lem.
3.1
(3), for every
z_{0}\in

{\rm Dom}(w(.))
there exists
k\in[1,n]
such that
|z_{k}|

=

2

=

{\rm maxlen}(\Gamma_{\!H})
; in case
|z_{k}|<2
we can restrict the
domain of
a_{k}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{k+1}(.)
and lengthen

z_{k}=a_{k}\circ\,\ldots\,\circ a_{1}(z_{0})
so that
|z_{k}|=2
.
Hence, for this
z_{0}
and this cyclic permutation

a_{k}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{k+1}\in{\sf cyc}(w)
, we have:

|z_{k}|=2
, and

a_{k}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{k+1}(z_{k}0^{\omega})\,\neq\,

z_{k}0^{\omega}
.

Therefore,
w\in{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma_{\!H}\!}(V)
implies

(\exists z\in\{00,01,10,11\})[\,a_{k}\ldots a_{1}a_{n}\ldots a_{k+1}(z0^{\omega})\,\neq\,z0^{\omega}\,]
,

which implies that there exists
\,u\in{\sf cyc}(w)\,
such that

\,(\exists z\in\{00,01,10,11\})[\,u(z0^{\omega})

\neq z0^{\omega}\,]
.

This is equivalent to

there exists
\,u\in{\sf cyc}(w)\,
such that

\,(\exists z\in\{00,01,10,11\})[\,u\in L_{z}\,]
,

where
\,L_{z}=\{u\in\Gamma_{\!H}^{\ *}:\,

u(z0^{\omega})\neq z0^{\omega}\}
, as in Lem.
3.4
.
Therefore:

{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma_{\!H}\!}(V)

\subseteq

{\sf cyc}(L_{00})

\cup

{\sf cyc}(L_{01})

\cup

{\sf cyc}(L_{10})

\cup

{\sf cyc}(L_{11})
.

Moreover,
\,L_{z}\,\subseteq\,{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma_{\!H}\!}(V)
.
And by Lem.
2.1
(2):

{\sf cyc}(L_{00})

\cup

{\sf cyc}(L_{01})

\cup

{\sf cyc}(L_{10})

\cup

{\sf cyc}(L_{11})

\ =\

{\sf cyc}(L_{00}\cup L_{01}\cup L_{10}\cup L_{11})
.

Therefore,

L_{00}\cup L_{01}\cup L_{10}\cup L_{11}

\subseteq

{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma_{\!H}\!}(V)

\subseteq

{\sf cyc}(L_{00}\cup L_{01}\cup L_{10}\cup L_{11})

Since the word problem and the co-word-problem of a group are
cyclically closed (by Prop.
2.3
), we finally obtain:

{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma_{\!H}\!}(V)

\,=\,

{\sf cyc}(L_{00}\cup L_{01}\cup L_{10}\cup L_{11})

For every

L_{z}

3.4

\,{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma_{\!H}\!}(V)\,

By Cor.

, the co-word-problem of

over

\,L_{z}=L_{z}^{\rm rev}
.
Hence,
\,{\sf cowp}_{\Gamma_{\!H}\!}(V)\,

Appendix: Push-down automata

A push-down automaton (pda) is an algebraic structure
{\cal A}

=

(Q,A,

\Sigma,

{\cal T},

q_{0},

{\rm s}_{0},

Q_{\rm a})
, where

Q
(state set),
A
(input alphabet),
\Sigma
(stack alphabet),
{\cal T}

(set of transitions), and
Q_{\rm a}\subseteq Q
(set of accept states),
are finite sets;
q_{0}\in Q
is the start state; and
{\rm s}_{0}\in\Sigma^{+}

is the initial content of the stack.

The current
configuration
of
{\cal A}
(a.k.a. the instantaneous
description) is of the form
\,w\,(q,s)
, where
q\in Q
is the current
state,
s\in\Sigma^{*}
is the current stack content, and
w
is the input
that has been read so far.
A pda has one
start configuration
, namely
(q_{0},s_{0})
, where
q_{0}
and

s_{0}
are as above. No input has been read at this point, so
w
is
\varepsilon
in
the start configuration.
An
accept configuration
is of the form
\,w\,(q,s)
, such that

q\in Q_{\rm a}
.

Remark: Our definition of configuration is different from the one
in the literature
[
11
,
9
,
6
]
. There a configuration is of
the form
(q,x,s)\in

Q\hskip-1.8063pt\times\hskip-1.8063ptA^{*}\hskip-1.8063pt\times\hskip-1.8063pt\Sigma^{*}
, where
q
and
s
are the
same as for us, but
x
is a
future
input.

A
transition
in
{\cal T}
has the form

\ (q,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\to}}(p,s^{\prime})
, where

(q,s)\in Q\hskip-1.8063pt\times\hskip-1.8063pt\Sigma^{+}
,
(p,s^{\prime})\in Q\hskip-1.8063pt\times\hskip-1.8063pt\Sigma^{*}
, and
a\in

A\cup\{\varepsilon\}
. When
a=\varepsilon
, this is called an
\varepsilon
-transition: the state
and the stack may change, but no next input letter is being read.
There is no transition on an empty stack, since in a transition as above,

s\in\Sigma^{+}
. A pda has a finite set
{\cal T}
of transitions.

The above transition is
applicable
to a configuration
w\,(r^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\in

Q\hskip-1.8063pt\times\hskip-1.8063pt\Sigma^{+}\,
iff
\,r^{\prime}=q
,
\,s
is a prefix of
t^{\prime}
, and
either
a\in A
and the next input letter is
a
, or
a=\varepsilon
(and then
there is no requirement on the input). When this transition is
applied
to the configuration

w\,(q,st)
then the next configuration is

wa\,(p,s^{\prime}t)\,
(where
a
can be
\varepsilon
, in an
\varepsilon
-transition).
We extend the transition notation to configurations: when the transition

(q,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\to}}(p,s^{\prime})\,
is applied to the configuration

\,w\,(q,st)
, we write

\,w\,(q,st)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\to}}\,wa\,(p,s^{\prime}t)
.

Our transitions are a little more general than the ones commonly used in
the literature, but they do not lead to the acceptance of more languages

[
11
,
9
,
6
,
16
]
.

A
computation
of a pda
{\cal A}
on input
w=

a_{1}a_{2}\,\ldots\,a_{n}\in A^{*}
is a sequence of configurations and
applications of transitions

(q,s_{0})

\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\,(q_{1}^{\prime},s_{1}^{\prime})

\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{1}}}{{\to}}\,a_{1}\,(q_{1},s_{1})

\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\,a_{1}\,(q_{2}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime})

\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{2}}}{{\to}}\,a_{1}a_{2}\,(q_{2},s_{2})

\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \ \ldots\ \

\ \ \ldots\ \ \

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\,a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n-1}\,(q_{n}^{\prime},s_{n}^{\prime})

\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{n}}}{{\to}}\,a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n-1}a_{n}\,(q_{n},s_{n})

\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\,a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n-1}a_{n}\,(p,s)
,

where
\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\,
denotes a (possibly
empty) sequence of
\varepsilon
-transitions.

An
accepting computation
is a computation whose last configuration is
a accept configuration.

The same pda can be used as a
\exists
pda or a
\forall
pda; it depends on
the acceptance rule. The acceptance rule determines what inputs in
A^{*}
are
accepted by the pda.

A pda
{\cal A^{\prime}}
is used as a
\exists
pda iff the following acceptance
rule is used:

w\in A^{*}
is
accepted
iff
there exists
a computation of

{\cal A^{\prime}}
that reads the whole input
w
, that starts with the start
configuration
(q_{0},s_{0})
, and ends with a configuration of the form

\,w\,(q,t)
where
q\in Q_{\rm a}
.

A pda
{\cal A}
is used as a
\forall
pda iff the following acceptance
rule is used:

w
is
accepted
iff
every
computation of
{\cal A}
that
reads the whole input
w
, starting with the start configuration
(q_{0},s_{0})
,
ends in a configuration
\,w\,(q,t)
where
q\in Q_{\rm a}
.

A
deterministic pda
(dpda) is a pda such that in every configuration,
at most one transition is applicable. The acceptance rule is then the same
as in a
\exists
pda or a
\forall
pda.

We call a stack symbol
\bot\in\Sigma
a
bottom marker
iff

(1)
the start configuration is
(q_{0},\,p_{0}\bot)

for some
p_{0}\in(\Sigma\smallsetminus\{\bot\})^{*}
;

(2)
every transition with left-side
(q,s\bot)

has a right-side
(p,s^{\prime}\bot)
, for some
q,p\in Q
and

s,s^{\prime}\in(\Sigma\smallsetminus\{\bot\})^{*}
;

(3)
every transition with left-side
(q,s)

where
s\in(\Sigma\smallsetminus\{\bot\})^{+}
, has a right-side
(p,s^{\prime})
for
some
s^{\prime}\in(\Sigma\smallsetminus\{\bot\})^{*}
.

It is well known that the
\exists
pda’s accept exactly the languages in

CF
. Similarly, the
\forall
pda’s accept exactly the languages in

coCF
. By definition,
DCF
is the class of languages accepted
by dpda’s.
