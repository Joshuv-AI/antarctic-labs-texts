Minimal Constructible Sets
Jorge Garcia, Tyler Bongers, Jonathan Detgen, Walter Morales

Abstract
Given an initial family of sets, we may take unions, intersections and complements of the sets contained in this family in order to form a new collection of sets; our construction process is done recursively until we obtain the last family. Problems encountered in this research include the minimum number of steps required to arrive to the last family as well as a characterization of that last family; we solve all those problems. We also define a class of simple families ( n n -minimal constructible) and we analyze the relationships between partitions and separability (our new concept) that lead to interesting results such as finding families based on partitions that generate finite algebras. We prove a number of new results about n n -minimal constructible families; one major result is that every finite algebra of sets has a generating family which is n n -minimal constructible for all n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathds{N} and we compute the minimum number of steps required to generate an algebra. Another interesting result is a connection between this construction process and Baire’s Theorem. This work has a number of possible applications, particularly in the fields of economics and computer science.



1
Introduction

This paper examines the results of an iterative process of set construction using elementary set operations. Given a family
\mathcal{U}
of subsets of some universe
X
, we wish to characterize what can be built from
\mathcal{U}
by successive operations of union, intersection and complement. Interesting problems encountered in this work include finding methods of characterizing constructible sets and simple generating families for a given complicated family.

By examining the construction process and the forms of the families constructed, we have been able to prove several general theorems giving a class of simple generating families for any finite algebra. In particular, we have proven in this paper that every finite algebra has a generating family which is 1-minimal constructible; we have examined other properties of these families, and have determined how many steps are necessary to reach a last family (in which no new sets can be constructed) from our 1-minimal constructible generating families. Connections between this construction process and topology (Baire’s Theorem) and measurable sets are presented through some theorems regarding non-constructible sets, specifically, we prove that the rationals can not be constructed from the usual topology for the real numbers.

The concept of
constructibility
has been studied in a topological sense: a set is constructible if it belongs to the smallest algebra that contains the open intervals, see for example Chevalley’s Theorem: under certain conditions the image under a polynomial map of a constructible set is constructible
[
8
]
. Equivalently, a set is constructible if and only if it is a finite union of locally closed sets, that is, the intersection of a closed set and an open set (see Lemma
8
). Particularly when we start with a topology, in
[
1
]
, Allouche defines an operator on a set and concludes that a set is constructible if and only iff there is
n
such that the defined operator iterated
n
times on this set produces an empty set, in fact, he provides a canonical decomposition of the set into
n
locally closed sets, in other words, Allouche tells you how construct this set when the set is in indeed constructible. In a forthcoming paper
[
6
]
, we also analyze the decomposition of constructible sets. When our initial family
\mathcal{U}
consists of the open sets, this topological definition matches ours, hence our definition is a generalization of the existing one. In fact, we characterize the constructible sets when
\mathcal{U}
consists of the open sets, when
\mathcal{U}
consists of the open intervals and when
\mathcal{U}
consists of a finite partition. Therefore many of our results apply in the topological case.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the basic definitions and some examples to illustrate future tools and in Section
3
we study elementary results about algebras, we define our construction and “the last family”. These results will be needed through the whole paper. It is in Section
4
where we provide examples of sets that are not constructible and we characterize which sets are constructible in different cases, these are when the initial family is the open intervals and when the initial family is the usual topology. In addition, we provide a theorem that contrasts our finding with the characterization of a sigma-algebra. In Section
5
, we state the main concepts that give structure to our main results; particularly, we develop the concept of separability and analyze in depth the relationship between this concept and partitions in our construction process. Many of these results hold even if our universe set is infinite. Here we also define the minimum number of steps required to generate an algebra and we prove an important result of this paper, namely, we compute this number given any finite algebra. Finally in Section
6
, we analyze the concept of
n
-minimal constructibility and we prove two of the main results of this paper pertaining to the obtention of an
n
-minimal constructible family that generates a finite algebra in a minimum number of steps, we also compute this number. In Section
7
, we propose future work that extends some results that have been done here including counting certain number of families.

We would like to remark that often we provide some trivial examples because these examples inspire the definitions that follow them.

3
Elementary Results

In this section, we will present some basic results pertaining to set constructions; This will provide the necessary groundwork for the major results of this paper. For definitions of topology, closed sets, open sets, closure and interior refer to
[
4
]
.

Lemma 2
.

If
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{H}
are two families of subsets of
X
and
n\geq 0
, then

•

\mathcal{U}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{n+1}.

•

If
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
then
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{n}
and hence
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
.

•

(\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{H})_{n}\supseteq\mathcal{U}_{n}\cup\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{H})_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{n}\cap\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

Proof.

Clearly if
A\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
then
A=A\cup A,
hence
A\in\mathcal{U}_{n+1}.
The second part is obvious as anything that can be constructed from
\mathcal{H}
in
n
steps can also be constructed from
\mathcal{U}
in
n
steps because
\mathcal{H}
is part of
\mathcal{U}
, hence
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{n}
. Taking unions in this collection of sets over all
n
gives
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\infty}.
Finally, applying the second part to the collections
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{H},\ \mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{H}\cap\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H},\ \,\mathcal{H}\cap\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and taking the corresponding operation gives the desired result.
∎

We recall that every
\sigma
-algebra is an algebra (see
[
4
]
, Section 4, Chapter 1). The following two lemmas are immediate consequences of Lemma
2
and Definition
4
.

Lemma 3
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is an algebra of sets of

then
C_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\mathcal{A}.

Lemma 4
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is an algebra of sets with
\mathcal{A}\supseteq\mathcal{U}
then
\mathcal{A}\supseteq C_{n}\left(\mathcal{U}\right).

We will now prove two lemmas that will be useful.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{U}
is finite. Then
\mathcal{U}_{1}
has at most
|\mathcal{U}|^{2}+|\mathcal{U}|
elements, hence for any
n,\ \mathcal{U}_{n}
is finite. If in addition, there is
n_{0}
with
|\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}}}|=|\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}+1}}|
then
\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}}}
is an algebra of sets and
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}=\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}}}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{U}
has
N
elements, to build
\mathcal{U}_{1}
we need
\binom{N}{2}+N
unions (of two elements in
\mathcal{U}
),
\binom{N}{2}
intersections and
N
complements. This gives a total of at most
2N+2\binom{N}{2}=N^{2}+N
elements in
\mathcal{U}_{1}.
An inductive argument shows that
\mathcal{U}_{n}
is finite. Now, consider
n_{0}
with
|\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}}}|=|\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}+1}}|
. By Lemma
2
,
\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}+1}}.
Then,
\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}}}=\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}+1}}
and hence for all
n\geq n_{0},\ \mathcal{U}_{n}=\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}}},
hence
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}=\mathcal{U}_{{n_{0}}}
. By Lemma
1
,
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
is an algebra of sets.
∎

Lemma 6
.

C_{1}\left(\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\right)=\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
. If
\mathcal{U}
is finite, then
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
is a finite topology, an algebra and also a
\sigma
-algebra.

Proof.

C_{1}\left(\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\right)=\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
comes from Lemma
3
and Lemma
1
.
If
\mathcal{U}
is finite, then
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
is also finite with at most
2^{|X|}
elements. Therefore any countable (or arbitrary) union of elements in
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
really becomes a finite union of elements in
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, therefore
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
is also closed under countable (or arbitrary) unions, thus

is also a sigma algebra and a topology for

∎

4
Some Non-Constructible Sets and Their Characterizations

Through the construction process, only certain sets are constructible. In this section, we present a proof that the set
\mathbb{Q}
of rational numbers is not finitely constructible from the collection of open intervals. This example does provide a case in which the work on constructible sets has applications to analysis; it allows us to characterize various subsets of
\mathbb{R}
as expressible in terms of finite unions, finite intersections and complements of open sets. In some sense, this proof shows that the topological properties of
\mathbb{Q}
forbid it to be written in a finite sense using intersections, unions or complements of open or closed sets. In order to facilitate this proof, we begin with a few definitions and lemmas.

Notation 1
.

Throughout this section, we will consider
X=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{U}
the usual topology, that is, arbitrary unions of intervals of the form
(a,b)
in
\mathbb{R}
. Hence when we say open set or closed set, we are referring to the usual topology. We will also use the letter
F
to represent a closed set, and the letter
G
to represent an open set.

Even though the proof of the following lemma is somewhat simple, we will prove it as this technique will be used in two more results.

Lemma 7
.

Let
H
be a subset of
\mathbb{R},

G_{1},...,G_{n}
open subsets of
\mathbb{R}
and
F_{1},...,F_{n}
closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. If
H=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\left(G_{k}\cap F_{k}\right)
then there exist
\hat{G}_{1},...,\hat{G}_{m}
open sets and
\hat{F}_{1},...,\hat{F}_{m}
closed sets such that

\displaystyle H=\bigcap\limits_{j=1}^{m}\left(\hat{G}_{j}\cup\hat{F}_{j}\right).

Conversely, if
H=\bigcap_{k=1}^{n}\left(G_{k}\cup F_{k}\right)
, then there exist
\hat{G}_{1},...,\hat{G}_{m}
open sets and
\hat{F}_{1},...,\hat{F}_{m}
closed sets such that

\displaystyle H=\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{m}\left(\hat{G}_{j}\cap\hat{F}_{j}\right).

Proof.

We will only prove the first part by induction on
n
, the other part is analogous. When
n=1
, it clear that

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=G_{1}\cap F_{1}=\bigcap\limits_{j=1}^{2}\left(\hat{G}_{j}\cup\hat{F}_{j}\right)

where
\hat{G}_{1}=G_{1},\ \hat{F}_{1}=\emptyset,\ \hat{G}_{2}=\emptyset
and
\hat{F}_{2}=F_{1}
. Assume now that the results holds for
n=k
and consider the case
n=k+1.
Using our induction hypothesis, we conclude that there exists
\hat{G}_{1},...,\hat{G}_{m}
open sets and
\hat{F}_{1},...,\hat{F}_{m}
closed sets such that

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}\left(G_{k}\cap F_{k}\right)=\left[\bigcap\limits_{j=1}^{m}\left(\hat{G}_{j}\cup\hat{F}_{j}\right)\right]\cup\left(G_{n+1}\cap F_{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\bigcap\limits_{j=1}^{m}\left(\hat{G}_{j}\cup\hat{F}_{j}\right)\right)\cup G_{n+1}\right]\cap\left[\left(\bigcap\limits_{j=1}^{m}\left(\hat{G}_{j}\cup\hat{F}_{j}\right)\right)\cup F_{n+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}\left[\left(\hat{G}_{j}\cup G_{n+1}\right)\cup\hat{F}_{j}\right]\right)\ \cap\ \left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}\left[\hat{G}_{j}\cup\left(\hat{F}_{j}\cup F_{n+1}\right)\right]\right)

which is overall an intersection of sets of the form
G\cup F
where
G
is an open set and
F
is a closed set, as we wished.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
n\geq 0
be given and let
H\in\mathcal{U}_{n}.
Then there exist
G_{1},...,G_{N}
open sets and
F_{1},...,F_{N}
closed sets such that

H=\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{N}\left(G_{j}\cap F_{j}\right)

The proof of this lemma is done by induction and it is an immediate consequence of the previous one. We recall the following topological definition found in
[
4
]
where
\overline{A}
represents the closure of the set
A
.

Definition 5
.

We define a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
to be nowhere dense if
\overline{A}
has empty interior. That is,
A
is nowhere dense if its closure contains no nonempty open intervals.

These type of sets help us to define
first category
and
second category
sets which are important pieces of
Baire’s Theorem
. A full discussion and examples can be found in
[
4
]
, Chapter 7, Section 8. Here we have a simple implication of such a theorem that will be useful to our results.

Lemma 9
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2},...,A_{n}
be nowhere dense sets in
\mathbb{R}
. Then
\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\overline{A_{k}}\neq\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Baire’s Theorem as
A_{n}
being nowhere dense makes
\overline{A_{n}}
nowhere dense too and hence
\mathbb{R}
would be a set of the first category, a contradiction according to Baire’s Theorem.
∎

Theorem 1
.

The set of rational numbers is not constructible from
\mathcal{U}
. That is,
\mathbb{Q}\notin\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Assume otherwise. Since
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},
there is
n\geq 1
such that
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}.
By Lemma
8
, there exists
G_{1},...,G_{N}
open sets and
F_{1},...,F_{N}
closed sets such that
\mathbb{Q}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}\left(G_{k}\cap F_{k}\right)
.
Since
\mathbb{Q}
is a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
we must have

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{Q}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}\overline{\left(G_{k}\cap F_{k}\right)}=\mathbb{R}.

From Lemma
9
, it follows that one of the members of this union must not be nowhere dense. Therefore, there exists
k\geq 1
such that
\overline{G_{k}\cap F_{k}}
has nonempty interior; hence there is a non-empty open interval
(a,b)
such that
\left(a,b\right)\subseteq\overline{G_{k}\cap F_{k}}
. This implies that
(a,b)\subseteq\overline{F_{k}}=F_{k}
and also
\left(a,b\right)\subseteq\overline{G_{k}}
. Since
G
is open in the usual topology, there are
(a_{n},b_{n})
such that
G_{k}=\bigcup\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_{n},b_{n})
. Since
(a,b)\subseteq\overline{\bigcup\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_{n},b_{n})},
there is
n
with
(a,b)\cap(a_{n},b_{n})\neq\emptyset.
Therefore
G_{k}\cap(a,b)
is non-empty. Hence
G_{k}\cap(a,b)\subseteq G_{k}\cap F_{k}\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(G_{k}\cap F_{k}\right)=\mathbb{Q}
. But
\mathbb{Q}
does not have non-empty open subsets, a contradiction. We therefore conclude that
\mathbb{Q}\notin\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
.
∎

Theorem 2

Let

Theorem 3
.

If
\mathcal{V}=\big\{(a,b)\ :\ a,b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}\big\}
then a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{V}
constructible if and only if
A
can be written as

\displaystyle P\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i},b_{i})

(4.1)

where
n\geq 0,\ -\infty\leq a_{1}<b_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots a_{n}<b_{n}\leq\infty
and
P
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}
disjoint from
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i},b_{i}).

Proof.

Clearly for any real number
x
we have that

\{x\}=(-\infty,x)^{c}\cap(x,\infty)^{c}
, hence
\{x\}
is constructible (in fact it is 2-constructible). Now if
A
has the form
4.1
, it can be constructed in at most
n\vee(|P|+2)
steps. We will prove the converse by induction, more precisely, we will prove that if
A\in\mathcal{U}_{{m}}
then
A
has the special form
4.1
.

1.

When

5
Separability and Partitions

Through this work, it has become apparent that an examination of the forms of the sets in an initial family can give information regarding the sets in the last family. In the examples presented from finite initial families, it is worth to note that when constructing
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},..\mathcal{U}_{n},..
some elements of
X
are always grouped together; that is, there are groups of elements in
X
that either appear all together in each element of
\mathcal{U}_{n}
, or none appear.

Example 4
.

Consider
X=\{1,2,3,4\}
and the initial family
\mathcal{U}=\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\}
. We then have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}\cup\{\{1,2,3\},\emptyset,\{3,4\},\{1,2,4\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{1}\cup\{X,\{4\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\infty}=\mathcal{U}_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{2}.

Thus, it is clear that the elements of the set
\{1,2\}
are always paired; they never appear separately.

Examples such as this have motivated the following definition.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a family of subsets of
X
. Two elements
a,b\in X
are
separable
in
\mathcal{U}
if and only if there exists a set
A\in\mathcal{U}
such that either
a\in A,\,b\notin A
or
a\notin A,\,b\in A
. If any two different elements are separable in
\mathcal{U}
, we say that
X
is separable in
\mathcal{U}
. If two elements are not separable in
\mathcal{U}
, we say that they are unseparable in
\mathcal{U}
.

When the family
\mathcal{U}
is understood and
X
is separable in
\mathcal{U}
, we will simply say that
X
is
separable
. This definition is very useful through the whole paper. If our initial family is a topology
\tau
and
X
is separable in
\tau
then
X
satisfies the first axiom of separability (
T_{0}
) in the topological sense
[
4
]
. If the initial family
\mathcal{U}
is separable, then
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
is precisely the full power set, as noted in the following theorem:

Theorem 6
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is given and
X
is finite and separable then
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}=2^{X}
, that is,
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
consists of all possible subsets of
X.

Proof.

Clearly,
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
is finite. Let
a\in X
and consider
f(a)=\min\{|A|\,:\,A\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},\,a\in A\}
. We claim that
f(a)=1.
Suppose that
f(a)>1
and take
A
a set where this minimum is achieved, that is,
A\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
with
a\in A
and
f(a)=|A|.
Since
|A|>1
, there exist
b\in A
such that
b\neq a.
Since
X
is separable in
\mathcal{U}
there exists
B\in\mathcal{U}
such that
a\in B,\,b\in{B}^{\mathsf{c}}
or
a\in{B}^{\mathsf{c}},\,b\in B.
Define

{\displaystyle C}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllllllllllll}{\displaystyle A\cap B}&:\,\mathrm{\ if\ \,}{\displaystyle a\in B,\,b\in{B}^{\mathsf{c}}},\\[8.53581pt]
{\displaystyle A\cap{B}^{\mathsf{c}}}&:\,\mathrm{\ if\ \,}{\displaystyle a\in{B}^{\mathsf{c}},\,b\in B}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Then clearly
C\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},\,a\in C
and
|C|<|A|
as
b\in A-C,
contradicting the selection of
A.
Since
f(a)=1
and

was arbitrary, we conclude that
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
contains all singletons, hence it contains all possible unions of such and therefore
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
consists of all possible subsets of
X.

∎

We immediately state the following lemma which will help us to prove some future theorems.

Lemma 10
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is given and we define
a\sim b
in
X
if and only if
a
and
b
are unseparable, then the relationship
\sim
is an equivalence relationship.

Proof.

Clearly
a\sim a
as there is not
A\in\mathcal{U}
such that
a\in A
and
a\notin A.
From the definition we can see that if
a\sim b
then
b\sim a
. Now suppose that
a\sim b,\,b\sim c
and suppose that
a\not\sim c
hence
a,c
are separable. Then there is
A\in\mathcal{U}
such that either
a\in A,\,c\notin A
or
a\notin A,\,c\in A
. If

, since
a
and

are unseparable,
a,b\in A
; since
b
and

are also unseparable,

; Therefore

a contradiction. The other case

is treated in the same manner.
∎

From now on, we will denote by
[a]_{\mathcal{U}}
(or simply

) the equivalence class of

under this relationship. It is also possible, given an algebra, to look backwards at the generating families based on separable elements. We will in fact be able to correspond every algebra with a particular classification of elements based on separability.

Example 5

Consider the universe
X=\{1,2,3,4,5\}
and the algebra
\mathcal{A}=\{\emptyset,\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\{5\},\{1,2,3,4\},\{1,2,5\},\{3,4,5\},X\}

and

are unseparable,

and

are unseparable, but

consist of all possible combinations of the sets

, and

Examples such as this have led to much more general results on the relationship between partitions and finite algebras. As this example shows, it is possible to work backwards from an algebra and derive a small initial family which generates the algebra. These ideas have led to the following three results.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\{A_{1},\mathellipsis,A_{n}\}
be a finite partition of
X
. Then
\left|\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\right|=2^{n}
. Further,
A\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
if and only if there exists a set
\Lambda\subseteq\{1,\mathellipsis,n\}
such that

\displaystyle A=\bigcup\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda}A_{\lambda}.

(5.1)

Also
a,b
are unseparable in
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
if and only if
a,b
are unseparable in
\mathcal{U}.

Proof.

Clearly, if
A
has the form as in Equation
5.1
, then it can be constructed by unions and is thus an element of
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
.
By Lemma
6
,
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
is an algebra and a sigma algebra. It is well known (
[
2
]
, P.2.21) that if
A\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
then
A
is a union of some elements in the partition, hence
A
satisfies Equation
5.1
for some
\Lambda
subset of
\{1,2,...,n\}.

To find
|\mathcal{U}_{\infty}|
, note that every set
A\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
can be written as unions of
k

\mathcal{U}
and
k\in\{0,1,...,n\}
. The number of ways to select such possible sets is given by
\binom{n}{0}+\binom{n}{1}+\mathellipsis\binom{n}{n}=2^{n}.

Finally, suppose that
a,b
are unseparable in
\mathcal{U}
. If
a,b
were separable in
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, without loss of generality, there would exists a set
A
in
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
such that

and

Since

satisfies Equation
5.1
for some
\Lambda

there would exist only one

such that

contradicting the fact that

are unseparable in
\mathcal{U}.
Conversely, if

are separable in
\mathcal{U}
, since
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, by definition of separability,

are necessarily separable in
\mathcal{U}_{\infty},
the converse of the last statement finishes the proof of this lemma.
∎

Theorem 7
.

Let
X
be a finite universe. Let
\mathbf{A}
be the collection of nonempty algebras of subsets of
X
, and
\mathbf{P}
the collection of partitions of the set
X
. Then each element in
\mathbf{A}
corresponds to a unique element in
\mathbf{P}
, i.e. there exists a bijective mapping
f:\mathbf{A}\longrightarrow\mathbf{P}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbf{A}
be an arbitrary non-empty algebra; as previously, we define the relationship on
\mathcal{A}
by
a\sim b
if and only if
a
and
b
are unseparable in
\mathcal{A}
. By Lemma
10
, this becomes an equivalence relationship in
X
, hence it induces a partition in
X
. Denote this partition by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}
. Define the function
f:\mathbf{A}\longrightarrow\mathbf{P}
by

f\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}

Consider an arbitrary partition
\mathcal{P}=\{A_{1},\mathellipsis,A_{n}\}
of the universe
X
. Form the family
\mathcal{U}=\{A_{1},\mathellipsis,A_{n}\}
and consider the algebra
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
. By Lemma

, if a pair of elements
a,b
are unseparable in
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
then they are unseparable in
\mathcal{U}.
Therefore the classes of equivalence given by this relationship are precisely the elements of the partition
\mathcal{P}.
The way we are defining our function, we conclude that
f\left(\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\right)=\mathcal{P}.
Therefore
f
is surjective. Take
\mathcal{A},\,\mathcal{B}\in\mathbf{A}
with
f(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}},\ \,f(\mathcal{B})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}.
By using Lemma

, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=C_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)=C_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}\right)=\mathcal{B}.

Hence,

∎

\displaystyle A_{I}:=\big\{x\in X\,:\,I=\{i\in I_{n}\,:\,x\in E_{i}\}\big\}.

\displaystyle A_{I}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllllllllllll}{\displaystyle\bigcap_{i\in I}E_{i}-\bigcup_{i\notin I}E_{i}}&:\,\mathrm{\ if\ }I\neq\emptyset,\,I\neq I_{n}\\[17.07164pt]
{\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}}&:\,\mathrm{\ if\ }I=I_{n},\\[8.53581pt]
{\displaystyle X-\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}}&:\,\mathrm{\ if\ }I=\emptyset.\\
\end{array}\right.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\{E_{1},E_{2},...,E_{n}\}

\displaystyle E_{k}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq I_{n}\\

6
Minimal Constructibility

As noted before, it is frequently of use to be able to move backwards from complicated algebras to relatively simple generating families. The following example motives the definition that follows it.

Example 8
.

Consider
X=\{1,2,3\}
and the family

\mathcal{U}=\{\{1,2\},\{3\},\emptyset,X\}

The elements
\emptyset
and
X
are constructible in one step from the other two elements in the family; thus, in some sense, they are unnecessary. The family
\mathcal{H}=\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\}
is of smaller size, and
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}=\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. Thus, this allows for the construction of a simpler family that still generates the same algebra.

Definition 9
.

We say
\mathcal{U}
is
1-minimal constructible
if for any
\mathcal{H}\subsetneq\mathcal{U}
we have
\mathcal{U}\nsubseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

In other words, a family is 1-minimal constructible if it cannot be built in one step from any of its proper subsets. Thus, these families contain simple structure and lack much of the duplication and unnecessary elements encountered in algebras. It is possible to place a stronger condition on the families, and to require that there are no elements that can be constructed in any given finite number of steps. This desire leads to the following definition.

Definition 10
.

Let
n\geq 1
. We say that
\mathcal{U}
is
n
-minimal constructible if for any
\mathcal{H}\subsetneq\mathcal{U}
we have
\mathcal{U}\nsubseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

Example 9
.

Consider
X=\left\{1,2,3,4\right\}
, and
\mathcal{U}=\left\{\left\{1\right\},\left\{2\right\},\left\{3,4\right\}\right\}
. Then

\mathcal{U}_{1}=\left\{\left\{1\right\},\left\{2\right\},\left\{3,4\right\},\left\{1,2\right\},\left\{1,3,4\right\},\left\{2,3,4\right\},\emptyset\right\}.

We see that
\mathcal{U}
is 1-minimal constructible because none of
\left\{1\right\}
,
\left\{2\right\}
or
\left\{3,4\right\}
can be constructed from the remaining two in one step. Also note that
\mathcal{U}
is not 2-minimal constructible because there is
\mathcal{V},
a proper subfamily of
\mathcal{U},
such that
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{2}
, namely,
\mathcal{V}=\left\{\left\{1\right\},\left\{2\right\}\right\},
as

\mathcal{V}_{2}=\left\{\emptyset,\left\{1\right\},\left\{2\right\},\left\{1,2\right\},\left\{3,4\right\},\left\{1,3,4\right\},\left\{2,3,4\right\},\left\{1,2,3,4\right\}\right\}.

On the other hand,
\mathcal{U}_{1}
is not 1-minimal constructible because
\left\{1,2\right\}
can be constructed in one step through the union of
\left\{1\right\}
and
\left\{2\right\}
.

\mathcal{X}=\left\{1,2,3\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{2}

An alternative characterization of this idea can be formulated. If no set in the family can be constructed in one step from the remaining sets, then no proper subset can construct the entire family. This is formalized in the next lemma.

Lemma 14

\mathcal{U}
is
n
-minimal constructible if and only if for any

we have
\mathcal{U}\not\subseteq C_{n}(\mathcal{U}-\{A\}).

Proof.

We will prove the opposite of the statement, that is

\mathcal{U}\,\,\mathrm{is\ not\ }n\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{minimal\ constructible\ }\Leftrightarrow\ \,\exists\,A\in\mathcal{U}\,:\,\,\mathcal{U}\subseteq C_{n}(\mathcal{U}-\{A\}).

It is clear that if there exists
A\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathcal{U}\subseteq C_{n}(\mathcal{U}-\{A\}),
then
\mathcal{U}
would not be
n
-minimal constructible. Conversely, if
\mathcal{U}
is not
n
-minimal constructible, there exists
\mathcal{H}
a proper subfamily of
\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}.
Take simply
A\in\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{H},
then clearly
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}\subseteq C_{n}(\mathcal{U}-\{A\}).

∎

-minimal constructible. If

is not

\mathcal{U}
is not
k
-minimal constructible.

Combining our results, we have proven two general classification theorems that allow the construction of relatively simple generating families for any given finite algebra of sets. These theorems have deep connections to the results on partitions and separable elements. The first theorem gives a method of determining a family with a number of desirable properties relating to elements in
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
and the number of steps required to construct the last family; on the other hand, the second theorem gives a construction of a simplest generating family for a given algebra.

Theorem 11
.

\mathcal{A}

7
Future Work and Open Questions

There are a number of applications to research on constructible sets. In particular,
[
9
]
has examined some uses of the properties of set algebras in the context of economics; in this work, information can be modeled in terms of families of sets, while the exchange of information takes place through the mechanisms of union, intersection and complement. It is also possible that there exist applications in the areas of data storage and computer science; much of our work has been on generating large, complicated set algebras from small, simple families. This idea of finding simple generating families may have some use in data compression; a family representing some information could be replaced by a family which is 1- or
n
-minimal constructible.

There remain a number of open questions and possible areas of future work raised by our research. In particular, we have not arrived at a general formula for counting the number of
n
-minimal constructible families for a given finite universe. Connections between this counting problem and the conjecture of Frankl (see
[
7
]
) ought to be examined as well.

In addition to continuing to examine the finite case, more work in infinite families is necessary. Separability is a property that needs to be studied in the infinite case. In the finite case separability implies
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}=2^{X}
but in the infinite case, this is not the case because even though
\mathbb{R}
is separable in
\mathcal{U}=
usual topology, we do not have
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}=2^{\mathbb{R}}
since
\mathbb{Q}\notin\mathcal{U}_{\infty}.
In the upcoming paper
[
6
]
we count the number of
1
-minimal constructible families and we compute the number of
n
-minimal constructible families under some restrictions; however, a general formula needs to be found. Counting the families
\mathcal{H}
of smallest size that are
n
-minimal constructible and generate an algebra
\mathcal{A}
is also an interesting problem to be approached.
