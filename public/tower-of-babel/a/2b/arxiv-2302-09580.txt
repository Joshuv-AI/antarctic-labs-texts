Non-separable Covariance Kernels for Spatiotemporal Gaussian Processes based on a Hybrid Spectral Method and the Harmonic Oscillator PubID: pubid: 978-1-5386-5541-2/18/$31.00 ©2018 IEEE
Dionissios T. Hristopulos

Abstract
Gaussian processes provide a flexible, non-parametric framework for the approximation of functions in high-dimensional spaces. The covariance kernel is the main engine of Gaussian processes, incorporating correlations that underpin the predictive distribution. For applications with spatiotemporal datasets, suitable kernels should model joint spatial and temporal dependence. Separable space-time covariance kernels offer simplicity and computational efficiency. However, non-separable kernels include space-time interactions that better capture observed correlations. Most non-separable kernels that admit explicit expressions are based on mathematical considerations (admissibility conditions) rather than first-principles derivations. We present a hybrid spectral approach for generating covariance kernels which is based on physical arguments. We use this approach to derive a new class of physically motivated, non-separable covariance kernels which have their roots in the stochastic, linear, damped, harmonic oscillator (LDHO). The new kernels incorporate functions with both monotonic and oscillatory decay of space-time correlations. The LDHO covariance kernels involve space-time interactions which are introduced by dispersion relations that modulate the oscillator coefficients. We derive explicit relations for the spatiotemporal covariance kernels in the three oscillator regimes (underdamping, critical damping, overdamping) and investigate their properties. We further illustrate the hybrid spectral method by deriving covariance kernels that are based on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model.

INTRODUCTION

Gaussian processes are a data-driven, non-parametric machine learning method used for nonlinear regression and classification tasks
[
1
]
as well as adaptive control and reinforcement learning

2
]
. Gaussian processes define a prior over a class of functions or models. Hence, they provide a powerful framework for the analysis of time series as well spatial and spatiotemporal data

The core of Gaussian processes is the covariance kernel, which incorporates correlations that are learned from the data. Only non-negative definite functions can be used as covariance kernels. Various generic admissible models are available in the literature
[
5
,
1
,
6
]
. However, there is still
great interest in deriving kernels for spatiotemporal datasets
[
7
]
. Such functions, which are necessary for modeling

[

]
, systems identification

, mobile sensor networks

In spatial statistics, a predictive framework similar to Gaussian processes has been developed independently based on the theory of random fields and has found many applications in the natural sciences
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
4
]
. The covariance kernel is also instrumental in determining the properties of random fields. The main differences between the Gaussian process and the random field predictive frameworks are as follows: (i) in the case of random fields the input vector is restricted to the spatial (or space-time) coordinates; (ii) Gaussian processes are embedded in a Bayesian framework; (iii) the two approaches use different nomenclature
[
5
]
.
The results of this paper are applicable to both frameworks.

Covariance kernels for multidimensional input spaces often involve
separable models
which are formed as products or as linear superposition of lower-dimensional kernels
[
15
,
16
,
6
]
.
Separability is also invoked to construct simplified space-time covariance kernels by means of products or linear superposition of spatial and temporal components. In separable models, spatial and temporal correlations are decoupled; this behavior is not physically justifiable
[
17
]
and can lead to numerical instabilities in calculations of conditional means and variances
[
18
]
.
Non-separable, flexible and physically motivated covariance kernels are thus in great demand
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. Cressie and Huang
[
18
]
constructed kernels that involve space and time interactions by inverting admissible mathematical expressions for the spatial Fourier modes of the kernels. Gneiting
[
19
]
introduced a method for constructing admissible kernels directly in the space-time domain, thus avoiding the calculation of inverse Fourier transforms. His method takes advantage of the powerful properties of completely monotone functions and generates a broad class of functions. Kolovos
et al.

[
20
]
review various methods for generating non-separable space-time kernels including the use of Radon transforms and stochastic partial differential equations.

Properties, existing models, and open research questions for spatiotemporal covariance kernels are discussed in two recent reviews
[
21
,
7
]
. Ideally, space-time covariance kernels should be solutions of partial differential equations (PDEs) that characterize the particular system under study
[
4
]
. However, such PDEs are not amenable to explicit solutions except in certain special cases
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
17
,
25
]
. Hybrid approaches that use Gaussian processes and differential equations to combine data-driven modeling with a physical model have been proposed to address this issue
[
9
]
.

Motivated by the scarcity of solvable physics-based models for covariance kernels, we derive a new family of non-separable covariance kernels which are based on the stochastic, linear, damped harmonic oscillator (LDHO). The LDHO model is herein suitably generalized for spatially extended processes by means of the Cressie-Huang approach
[
18
]
. The spatial LDHO Fourier modes are generated by injecting intuitive dispersion relations in the oscillator’s coefficients. The dispersion relations then translate into physically meaningful covariance kernel hyperparameters. To our knowledge, this is the first non-separable space-time covariance kernel family in the literature which allows for oscillatory temporal correlations. Hence, the LDHO kernels are particularly interesting for geo-referenced data that exhibit periodicity (e.g., diurnal, weekly, monthly or yearly) in their correlation functions
[
26
]
. The LDHO covariance kernels are also applicable to temporal Gaussian processes with different input spaces, so long as the Euclidean distance measure is meaningful for the input space (excluding the time dimension).

II-B

Gaussian processes and random fields

A Gaussian process defines a prior distribution over functions, which can then be used for Bayesian regression
[
27
]
. We will denote the GP by
z(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}\left(m(\mathbf{x}),C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)
, where

[
1
]
. The matrix
\mathbf{C}
with elements
[\mathbf{C}]_{i,j}=C(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
, for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
(where

) is the

(Gram) matrix.

For geo-referenced data
\mathbf{x}=({\mathbf{s}},t)
where
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the spatial coordinate inside the spatial domain
\mathcal{D}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+,0}
is the time instant. In this case, the dimension of the input vector
\mathbf{x}
is
D=d+1
. To be more precise, given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\textsl{P})
, where
{\Omega}
is the sample space,
\mathcal{F}
is a
\sigma-
field of subspaces of
{\Omega}
, and
P
is a probability measure, the collection of real-valued, scalar random variables
\{z({\mathbf{s}},t;\zeta):{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{D},\,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+,0},\,\zeta\in\Omega\}
is a scalar, real-valued
spatiotemporal process

Z:\mathcal{D}\times R_{+,0}\times\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. The expectation operator over the probability space is denoted
by
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
. The functions (realizations) of this space are denoted by
z({\mathbf{s}},t)
.
Herein we focus on
weakly (second-order) stationary
spatiotemporal processes which have (i) constant mean and (ii) covariance that depends purely on the space-time lag, i.e.,

C(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=C(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2})=C({\mathbf{s}}_{1}-{\mathbf{s}}_{2},t_{1}-t_{2})
.
We use
{\bf r}={\mathbf{s}}_{i}-{\mathbf{s}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote the spatial lag and
\tau=t_{i}-t_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
for the temporal lag between two space-time points
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}
. The indices
i,j
are not needed for the space-time lags in the stationary case.

II-C

Covariance kernels

Definition 1
(Non-negative definite functions)

The function
C(\cdot,\cdot)
is
non-negative definite
(positive definite) if and only if for all sets
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and all real-valued vectors
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
it holds that
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}\geq 0

(resp.,
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}>0
) for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{v}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
, where

[\mathbf{C}]_{i,j}=C(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
.

Remark 1
(Units)

Covariance kernels depend on a vector of hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The box notation,
[\theta]
, denotes the units of a scalar hypeparameter
\theta
; e.g.,
[\theta]=[L]/[T]
implies that
\theta
units of length over time.

Definition 2
(Fourier transforms)

Let
C({\bf r},\tau):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
represent a space-time function which is absolutely integrable over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. Then, the
Fourier transform

\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)={\mathrm{FT}}[C({\bf r},\tau)]
and its inverse
C({\bf r},\tau)={\mathrm{IFT}}[{\widetilde{C}}({\bf k},\omega)]
exist. The FT is given by means of the multidimensional improper integral

\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{-\jmath({\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}+\omega\tau)}C({\bf r},\tau){\mathrm{d}}{\bf r}{\mathrm{d}}\tau,

(1)

where
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
is the
cyclic frequency
, and
{\bf k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
spatial frequency vector (wavevector)
in reciprocal (Fourier) space. The Euclidean norm
\|{{\bf k}}\|
is known as the
wavenumber
.

The inverse FT is given by means of the following integral

C({\bf r},\tau)=\frac{1}{(2\,\pi)^{d+1}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\jmath({\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}+\omega\tau)}{\widetilde{C}}({\bf k},\omega){\mathrm{d}}{\bf k}{\mathrm{d}}\omega.

(2)

Theorem 1
(Bochner’s theorem)

A function
C({\bf r},\tau)
is an
admissible covariance kernel
for a stationary random field if and only if the Fourier transform
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
of
C({\bf r},\tau)
exists, is non-negative, and its integral over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
is finite
[
28
]
.

Bochner’s theorem specifies conditions for
C({\bf r},\tau)
to be an admissible covariance kernel for some random field without requiring that the latter be normally distributed.

Definition 3
(Radial functions)

A function
C({\bf r},\tau)
is called a
radial function
if
C({\bf r},\tau)=C_{r}(r,\tau)
where
r=\|{{\bf r}}\|
is the Euclidean norm of
{\bf r}
. For simplicity of notation we drop the index
r
in
C_{r}(r,\tau)
. A covariance kernel defined by a radial function is called
isotropic
.

The Fourier transform of a radial function
C(r,\tau)
, if it exists, is also a radial function
\widetilde{C}(k,\omega)
, where
k=\|{{\bf k}}\|
; the converse is also true.

Definition 4
(Marginal covariance kernels)

The functions
C_{\mathrm{S}}(r)\triangleq C(r,\tau=0)
and
C_{\mathrm{T}}(\tau)\triangleq C(r=0,\tau)
represent the spatial and temporal
marginal covariance kernels
at zero space and time lags respectively.

III
Covariance Kernel of Harmonic Oscillator Driven by White Noise

We denote by
z(t;\zeta)
the displacement from equilibrium of a classical, linear, damped harmonic oscillator (LDHO) as a function of time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+,0}
. It is assumed that

m>0
is the inertial mass of the oscillator,
\gamma>0
is the friction coefficient, and
\kappa>0
is Hooke’s constant.

III-A

Equation of motion

The equation of motion (EOM) due to random forcing (e.g., if the oscillator is placed in a heat bath) is given by the following stochastic ordinary differential equation (SODE)

\frac{\text{d}^{2}{z}(t;\zeta)}{\text{d}t^{2}}+\frac{\gamma}{m}\frac{\text{d}{z}(t;\zeta)}{\text{d}t}+\frac{\kappa}{m}z(t;\zeta)=\sigma_{\eta}\eta(t;\zeta).

(3)

The function
\sigma_{\eta}\eta(t)
, where
\sigma_{\eta}>0
, models the random force acting on the oscillator. The
noise
\eta(t;\zeta)
represents a realization of a standard Gaussian white noise stochastic process,
i.e.,
d\eta(t;\zeta)=dW(t;\zeta)
where
dW(t;\zeta)
is the differential of the
Wiener process
; hence

\mathbb{E}[\eta(t;\zeta)]=0;\;\mathbb{E}[\eta(t;\zeta)\eta(t^{\prime};\zeta)]=\delta(t-t^{\prime}),\text{ for all }t,t^{\prime},\kern 5.0pt

(4)

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function. The LDHO hyperparameters

m,\kappa,\gamma,\sigma_{\eta}
can be replaced by the more intuitive quantities,
\omega_{0}
,
\tau_{c}
, and
\sigma
where

\omega_{0}\triangleq\sqrt{\kappa/m}

is the
natural frequency of the undamped oscillator
, and

\tau_{c}\triangleq m/\gamma

is the
characteristic damping time
and
\sigma\triangleq\sigma_{\eta}/m
.
The
natural frequency of the damped oscillator
is given by

\omega_{d}=\sqrt{\kappa/m-\gamma^{2}/(4m^{2})}=\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-1/(4\tau_{c}^{2})}.

(5)

The damped frequency (
5
) is real-valued if
\gamma^{2}<4m\kappa
; the value

\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}\triangleq 2(m\kappa)^{1/2}
represents the critical damping.

Remark 2
(RLC Oscillator)

The EOM (
3
) for the LDHO is parametrized for a mechanical oscillator. However, using the substitutions
m\to L
,
\gamma\to R
, and
\kappa\to 1/C
, the EOM describes current oscillations in an electrical RLC circuit in the presence of thermal noise.

III-B

Covariance equation of motion

Since the oscillator displacement
z(t;\zeta)
is governed by a second-order linear SODE, the displacement covariance
C(\tau)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[z(t+\tau;\zeta)\,z(t;\zeta)]-\mathbb{E}[z(t+\tau;\zeta)]\,\mathbb{E}[z(t;\zeta)]
is the fundamental solution (Green’s function) of a fourth-order, linear ordinary differential equation (ODE).

Corollary 1
(Green’s function equivalence of LDHO Covariance)

If the stochastic process
z(t;\zeta)
is governed by the second-order SODE (
3
), its covariance kernel is the fundamental solution (Green’s function); the latter satisfies the following fourth-order (biharmonic) generative ODE, where
\sigma=\sigma_{\eta}/m
:

\frac{d^{4}C(\tau)}{d\tau^{4}}+\left(2\,\omega_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{\tau_{c}^{2}}\right)\frac{d^{2}C(\tau)}{d\tau^{2}}+\omega_{0}^{4}C(\tau)=\sigma^{2}_{\eta}\delta(\tau).

(6)

Proof:

The proof is given in Appendix
A
.
∎

The connection between covariance kernels of stochastic processes satisfying linear SODEs and Green’s functions is well-known
[
29
,
30
,
4
]
.

Corollary 2
(Spectral density from generative ODE)

The covariance kernel which satisfies the ODE (
6
) corresponds to a
spectral density

\widetilde{C}(\omega)
. If
\tau_{c}>0
the latter is given by the following rational function of the cyclic frequency
\omega
:

\widetilde{C}(\omega)=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\eta}\,\tau_{c}^{2}}{\tau_{c}^{2}(\omega^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2})^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

(7)

Proof:

The
spectral density
of
z(t;\zeta)
is obtained according to Bochner’s theorem
[
28
]
from the Fourier transform of
C(\tau)
. We multiply both sides of (
6
) in Corollary
1
with
\tau_{c}^{2}
and apply the Fourier transform. Since the image of the time derivative operator in the Fourier domain is
{\mathrm{FT}}[{\mathrm{d}}/{\mathrm{d}}\tau]=\jmath\omega

[
31
]
, it follows that

{\mathrm{FT}}\left[\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{n}C(\tau)}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{n}}\right]=(\jmath\omega)^{n}\,\widetilde{C}(\omega),\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

(8)

The spectral density (
7
) then follows by recalling that
{\mathrm{FT}}[\delta(\tau)]=1
.
∎

Remark 3
(Admissibility of spectral density)

The function

\widetilde{C}(\omega)
defined in (
7
) is demonstrably non-negative for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and integrable over
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, it satisfies Bochner’s admissibility conditions.

III-C

Covariance kernel

The covariance kernel for the LDHO is given by calculating the
inverse Fourier transform of the spectral density. This can be explicitly evaluated
as shown in
[
32
]
(with slightly different parametrization). The results,
which correspond to three different LDHO damping regimes, are
reviewed below.

III-C
1
Underdamping

This regime is obtained for
\omega_{d}>0
, i.e., for
\omega_{0}\tau_{c}>1/2
. In this case,

C(\tau)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\omega_{0}^{2}\,\tau_{c}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tau_{c}}}\,\left(\cos\omega_{d}\tau+\frac{\sin\omega_{d}\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\omega_{d}\tau_{c}}\right)

(9a)

These covariance kernels oscillate with amplitudes that decrease exponentially with characteristic time
2\tau_{c}
.

III-C
2
Overdamping

This regime is obtained for
\omega_{d}
imaginary, i.e., for
\omega_{0}\tau_{c}<1/2
.

C(\tau)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\omega_{0}^{2}\,\tau_{c}}\left(\frac{1}{\lvert\omega_{d}\rvert\tau_{f}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lvert\tau\rvert/\tau_{s}}-\frac{1}{\lvert\omega_{d}\rvert\tau_{s}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lvert\tau\rvert/\tau_{f}}\right)\,.

(9b)

Hence, the covariance kernel decays as a superposition of two exponential functions with two characteristic times, a slow time,
\tau_{s}
, and a fast time,
\tau_{f}
:

\tau_{s}=\frac{2\tau_{c}}{1-2\tau_{c}\lvert\omega_{d}\rvert},\;\tau_{f}=\frac{2\tau_{c}}{1+2\tau_{c}\lvert\omega_{d}\rvert},\;\tau_{s}>\tau_{f}\,.

(9c)

Remark 4
(Admissible difference of exponential kernels)

Since
\omega_{d}
is imaginary in this regime, it follows from (
5
) that

2\omega_{0}\tau_{c}<1
and
2\lvert\omega_{d}\rvert\tau_{c}=\sqrt{1-(2\omega_{0}\tau_{c})^{2}}
. Hence, it holds that
0<2\lvert\omega_{d}\rvert\tau_{c}<1
. Therefore, the slow time

\tau_{s}
is a positive number. An interesting fact about the covariance (
9b
) is that it involves the difference of two admissible
(exponential) kernels, and it is admissible nonetheless. While this may seem trivial, one needs to recall that there are no simple, general conditions that render a linear superposition of kernels admissible unless the coefficients of the superposition are non-negative
[
33
]
.

III-C
3
Critical damping

This regime is obtained for
\omega_{d}=0
, i.e., for
\omega_{0}\tau_{c}=1/2
.

C(\tau)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\omega_{0}^{2}\,\tau_{c}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tau_{c}}}\,\left(1+\frac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tau_{c}}\right).

(9d)

The function (
9d
) is also known as the modified exponential kernel
[
34
,
35
]
.

IV
Hybrid Spectral Approach for Spatiotemporal Kernel Construction

In this section we present the hybrid spectral approach.
We assume that
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
is the spectral density of a spatiotemporal kernel.
The space-time inverse Fourier transform of
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
satisfies the
following decomposition property

\displaystyle C({\bf r},\tau)\triangleq

\displaystyle\,{\mathrm{IFT}}[\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)]={\mathrm{IFT}}_{{\bf k}}\left[{\mathrm{IFT}}_{\omega}[\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IFT}}_{{\bf k}}[\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau)].

(10)

In (
IV
),
{\mathrm{IFT}}_{\omega}
(
{\mathrm{IFT}}_{{\bf k}}
) is the inverse Fourier transform with respect to the temporal (spatial) dimension, and the function
\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau)\triangleq{\mathrm{IFT}}_{\omega}[\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)]
represents the
temporal Fourier modes
of the covariance kernel. The temporal modes are thus defined by means of the partial (with respect to
\omega
) inverse Fourier transform of
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
.

The hybrid spectral approach involves the following steps:

1.

Generative ODE:
A purely temporal covariance kernel,
C(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
, is derived as the fundamental solution (Green’s function) of a generative linear ODE with constant coefficients given by the vector
\mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\triangleq\left(c_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}),\ldots,c_{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\right)^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a hyperparameter vector. Thus,
C(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
satisfies the following equation (in terms of the linear differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\tau}
)

{\mathcal{L}}_{\tau}C(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})=\delta(\tau),\;{\mathcal{L}}_{\tau}=\sum_{p=0}^{P}c_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2p}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{2p}}.

(11)

2.

Spectral density:
The spectral density corresponding to
C(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
is given by

\widetilde{C}(\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})=\frac{1}{\Pi(\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})},

(12a)

where
\Pi(\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
is the characteristic polynomial of the differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\tau}
given by

\Pi(\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})=\sum_{p=0}^{P}(-1)^{p}\,c_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\omega^{2p},\;P\in\mathbb{N}\,.

(12b)

The equations (
12
) are obtained by applying the Fourier transform on both sides of (
11
), using (
8
) to calculate the FT of derivatives.
Bochner’s theorem requires that
c_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})>0
and
\Pi(\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})>0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.

The generative ODE coefficients are replaced by the vector
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}({\bf k};\boldsymbol{\theta})
. The latter incorporates
dispersion relations
which implement the space-time interactions.
Respectively, the characteristic polynomial becomes

\tilde{\Pi}(\omega,{\bf k};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{p=0}^{P}(-1)^{p}\,\tilde{c}_{p}({\bf k};\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\omega^{2p},\;P\in\mathbb{N}\,.

(13)

The dispersion relations must be compatible with the admissibility conditions of Bochner’s theorem
1
which specify that: (i)
\tilde{\Pi}(\omega,{\bf k};\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0
for
{\bf k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and (ii) the integral of
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
is finite.

4.

The spectral density of the temporal process,
\widetilde{C}(\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
, generates the spectral density
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta})
of the spatiotemporal kernel; the latter is obtained from
\widetilde{C}(\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
by replacing
c_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
with
\tilde{c}_{p}({\bf k};\boldsymbol{\theta})
for all
p=1,\ldots,P
.

5.

Temporal Fourier modes
of the associated spatiotemporal kernel,
\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta})
are obtained from
C(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
in (

) by replacing
\mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
with
{\bf k}
-dependent coefficients
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}({\bf k};\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top},{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}^{\top})^{\top}
is the augmented hyperparameter vector and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}

{\bf k}
dependence. The temporal modes
\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta})
are fundamental solutions of the
{\bf k}
-dependent generative ODEs:

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\tau}\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta})=\delta(\tau),\;\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\tau}=\sum_{p=1}^{P}\tilde{c}_{p}({\bf k};\boldsymbol{\theta})\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2p}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{2p}}\,.

(14)

The fundamental solution for each
{\bf k}
corresponds to a different coefficient vector
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}({\bf k};\boldsymbol{\theta})
. The assumption underlying (
14
) is that the mode for a given
{\bf k}
evolves in time independently of the modes for
{\bf k}^{\prime}\neq{\bf k}
.

Based on the decomposition property (

), proving the integrability of
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
is equivalent to proving that the modes
\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau)
are integrable over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

{\mathrm{IFT}}_{{\bf k}}[\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau)]
exists and is not singular at
{\bf r}=\mathbf{0}
(the lack of singularity implies that
\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau)
is integrable over
{\bf k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

6.

If the temporal Fourier modes are explicitly known by solving the generative ODE (
14
), the space-time covariance kernel
C({\bf r},\tau)
can be obtained, according to the decomposition property (
IV
), by calculating the IFT of
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega;\boldsymbol{\theta})
with respect to the wavevector
{\bf k}
. The latter is given by a multi-dimensional integral, which in certain cases can be exactly evaluated.

The spatiotemporal kernel
C({\bf r},\tau)
is physically motivated if the generative ODE that governs
\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau)
is associated with a SODE that, at least approximately, describes the equation of motion (EOM) of the studied process
[

,
4
]
.

We apply the hybrid spectral approach using the LDHO generative ODE given by (

Remark 5
(LDHO variance)

The variance
\sigma_{z}^{2}\triangleq C(0)
of the LDHO covariance in all three regimes is equal to
\sigma_{z}^{2}=\sigma^{2}/2\omega_{0}^{2}\,\tau_{c}
. This is straightforward for the kernels (
9a
) and (
9d
), while for the kernel (
9b
) it can be shown with simple algebraic manipulations. The variance
\sigma_{z}^{2}
can also be evaluated by integrating the spectral density
\widetilde{C}({\omega})
over all
\omega
, i.e.,

\sigma_{z}^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\omega\,\widetilde{C}({\omega})
, leading to

\sigma_{z}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\,\tau_{c}/\omega^{2}_{0}
. Equating the two expressions for the variance we obtain

\sigma^{2}_{\eta}=\sigma^{2}/\tau_{c}^{2}
. Then, the LDHO spectral density becomes

\widetilde{C}(\omega)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau_{c}^{2}(\omega^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2})^{2}+\omega^{2}}\,,

(15)

and the LDHO hyperparameter vector is
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\triangleq\left(\sigma^{2},\omega_{0},\tau_{c}\right)^{\top}
.

V-A

Dispersion relations

Dispersion relations link the LDHO hyperparameters with the spatial frequency
{\bf k}
. Let us assume the following general form for the dispersion functions:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\to\sigma^{2}({\bf k})=\sigma_{0}^{2}A({\bf k}),\,\tau_{c}\to\tau_{c}({\bf k})=\frac{\tilde{\tau}_{c}}{B({\bf k})},

\displaystyle\omega_{0}\to\omega_{0}({\bf k})=\tilde{\omega}_{0}\,B({\bf k}),

(16)

where
\tilde{\omega}_{0},\tilde{\tau}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
A({\bf k}),B({\bf k})>0
for all
{\bf k}
.
A({\bf k})
and
B({\bf k})
are dimensionless functions that allow considerable flexibility. We use physical considerations to constrain the form of these functions. We postulate the following principles:

(i)

A({\bf k})
is a bounded and decreasing function of
\|{{\bf k}}\|
, to ensure that the mode variance is finite and declines with increasing
\|{{\bf k}}\|
. If
A({\bf k})
increased with
\|{{\bf k}}\|
, the temporal modes would not be integrable. Non-exponential decline of
A({\bf k})
is possible, but it is not sufficient to ensure integrability for all
d
[cf. the dispersion functions (
19
) and the temporal Fourier modes given by (
B-A
), (
61
), (
68
)]. Exponential decline of the mode variance suppresses the high-
\|{{\bf k}}\|
modes and ensures integrability.

(ii)

B({\bf k})
increases with
\|{{\bf k}}\|
, implying an increase of the mode frequency and simultaneous decline of the damping time. Hence, for large
\|{{\bf k}}\|
(small spatial scales) the temporal mode oscillation frequency is high but the oscillations are rapidly damped. This behavior is combined with the fast decline of the oscillation amplitude due to
A({\bf k})
. Linking the oscillation frequency and damping time via
B({\bf k})
is crucial for mathematical convenience [cf. the comment accompanying (
18d
) below]. The reverse dependence, i.e., a damping time that increases with
\|{{\bf k}}\|
and concomitant decrease of the oscillation frequency, complicates the explicit integration of the temporal Fourier modes.

(iii)

Arbitrarily and without loss of generality, we assume that

A({\mathbf{0}})=B({\mathbf{0}})=1
so that
\tau_{c}({\mathbf{0}})=\tilde{\tau}_{c}
,
\omega_{0}({\mathbf{0}})=\tilde{\omega}_{0}
and

\sigma({\mathbf{0}})=\sigma_{0}
. Different values for
A({\mathbf{0}})
and
B({\mathbf{0}})
can be absorbed in
\sigma_{0}
and
\tilde{\omega}_{0}
.

In Step 3 of Section
IV
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
is augmented by the vector of the dispersion hyperparameters

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\triangleq(b,\epsilon)^{\top}
(see below).
According to Step 4, inserting the dispersion relations (
V-A
) in the spectral density (
15
) modifies the latter as follows

\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}A({\bf k})}{\omega^{2}+\left[\,\omega^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{0}^{2}\,B^{2}({\bf k})\,\right]^{2}\tilde{\tau}_{c}^{2}/B^{2}({\bf k})}\,.

(17)

The function (
17
) satisfies by construction
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)\geq 0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and
{\bf k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence, to confirm that (
17
) is an admissible spectral density for a stationary process (according to Bochner’s theorem) it suffices to provide conditions on
A({\bf k})
and
B({\bf k})
that render
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
integrable over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. Integrability conditions for radial dispersion functions are formulated in Section
V-B
.

The dispersive relations (
V-A
) lead to scaling relations for the damped natural frequency and for certain hyperparameter combinations that appear in the Fourier modes:

\displaystyle\omega^{2}_{0}({\bf k})\tau_{c}({\bf k})=\tilde{\omega}_{0}^{2}\,\tilde{\tau}_{c}B({\bf k}),

(18a)

\displaystyle\omega_{d}({\bf k})\tau_{c}({\bf k})=\tilde{\tau}_{c}\tilde{\omega}_{d},

(18b)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}({\bf k})}{\omega^{2}_{0}({\bf k})\tau_{c}({\bf k})}=\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\tilde{\omega}_{0}^{2}\,\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\frac{A({\bf k})}{B({\bf k})}\,.

(18c)

Tethering the dispersion relations for
\omega_{0}({\bf k})
and
\tau_{c}({\bf k})
to the same dispersion function in (
V-A
), i.e.,

\omega_{0}({\bf k})\propto B({\bf k})
and
\tau_{c}({\bf k})\propto B^{-1}({\bf k})
, enforces the linear dependence of the damped frequency on
B({\bf k})
:

\displaystyle\omega_{d}({\bf k})=\tilde{\omega}_{d}B({\bf k}),\,\text{where}\;\tilde{\omega}_{d}=\frac{1}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\,\Big\lvert\sqrt{4\tilde{\tau}_{c}^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{0}^{2}-1}\,\Big\rvert\,.

(18d)

This is a key property, since in combination with
B({\bf k})>0
it ensures that the LDHO regime is determined by
\tilde{\omega}_{d}
, and thus it is invariant for all
{\bf k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

V-B

Radial dispersion functions

Definition 5
(Dispersion functions with
k^{2}
dependence)

Let
A({\bf k})
and
B({\bf k})
be given by the following radial dispersion functions, where
k=\|{{\bf k}}\|
is the wavenumber:

\displaystyle B(k)=

\displaystyle 1+bk^{2},\;b>0,

(19a)

\displaystyle A(k)=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon k^{2}}\,B(k),\;\epsilon>0.

(19b)

The function
B(k)
in (

) implies that
\tau_{c}(k)\sim k^{-2}
, whereas
\omega_{0}(k)\sim k^{2}
for

The spectral density of the spatiotemporal LDHO kernel is obtained by inserting in (

Recalling Step 5, a comparison of (

The LDHO model hyperparameters

The kernel function (
23
) includes a hyperparameter vector with five independent components:
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\left(c_{0},\tilde{\tau}_{c},\tilde{\omega}_{d},\epsilon,b\right)^{\top}
.
The physical significance of the hyperparameters is as follows:

•

c_{0}
is a scaling factor which has units
[z]^{2}
and determines the kernel’s amplitude.

•

\tilde{\tau}_{c}
is a characteristic relaxation time that controls the temporal decay of the correlations.

•

\tilde{\omega}_{d}
is a cyclical frequency which controls the periodicity of damped temporal oscillations.

•

\epsilon
is the variance decay scale; it has dimensions of square length and determines how fast the mode variance declines at large
k
.

•

b
controls the space-time interaction strength; it also has dimensions of square length and determines the rate at which the non-damped resonance frequency increases and the damping time drops with
k
.

The hyperparameters
\tilde{\tau}_{c}
,
\tilde{\omega}_{d}
control the purely temporal dependence of the LDHO kernel. The hyperparameters
b,\epsilon
enter, along with
\tilde{\tau}_{c}
and
\tilde{\omega}_{d}
, in the three time-dependent functions
\kappa^{2},\lambda^{2},\phi
that control the space-time interactions as follows:

•

\kappa
: wavenumber that controls the spatial oscillations of the LDHO kernel;

•

\lambda
: inverse length controlling the decay of spatial correlations;

•

\phi
: phase factor modulating the correlations at
r=0
.

A preliminary discussion of the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from data is given in the Supplement (Section S1).

d
)

C(r,\tau)
depends on the spatial dimension

via the scaling factor

, the phase factor

, and the denominators in the damped oscillatory functions

and

.

VI
Properties of LDHO Kernels

Full symmetry

A stationary covariance kernel is
fully symmetric
if the following equalities hold for all
{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\tau\in\mathbb{R}

[
19
]
:

C({\bf r},\tau)=C({\bf r},-\tau)=C(-{\bf r},\tau)=C(-{\bf r},-\tau)\,.

Since the LDHO spatiotemporal kernels depend on
{\bf r}
and
\tau
only via
r=\|{{\bf r}}\|
and
\lvert\tau\rvert
, they are fully symmetric.

Full symmetry is not a suitable assumption for transport processes with a dominant advection velocity
[
19
,
38
]
. For such phenomena, the LDHO covariance kernels can be extended by invoking Taylor’s frozen field hypothesis
[
39
,
40
]
, according to which a non-symmetric spatiotemporal covariance
C({\bf r},\tau)
can be obtained from a purely spatial model
C_{s}({\bf r})
by means of

C({\bf r},\tau)=C_{s}({\bf r}-\mathbf{v}\tau)
, where
\mathbf{v}
is the uniform advection velocity, e.g.
[
38
]
. Full symmetry is broken in the frozen-field model because, except for
\tau=0
and
\mathbf{v}={\mathbf{0}}
, it holds that
{\bf r}-\mathbf{v}\tau\neq{\bf r}+\mathbf{v}\tau
; therefore, there exist
{\bf r}
and
\tau
such that
C({\bf r},-\tau)\neq C({\bf r},\tau)
. The “frozen field” assumption means that the correlation between two points at the same location separated by a time distance
\tau
is the same as the synchronous correlation between two points that lie apart by
{\bf r}=\mathbf{v}\tau
. If the spatial distance
{\bf r}
is replaced with the
composite space-time distance
{\bf r}-\mathbf{v}\tau
in the LDHO kernels, models that are not fully symmetric are generated. These models, however, do not respect the frozen-field condition, since they depend on
\tau
in addition to
{\bf r}-\mathbf{v}\tau
.

Hole effect

Commonly used isotropic covariance kernels, such as the exponential (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck), square exponential (Gaussian), and Whittle-Matérn models are admissible for input spaces of any dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
. They can be extended to space-time by means of a composite space-time distance
u=\sqrt{r^{2}+a^{2}\tau^{2}}
, where
a>0
, and
\mathbf{u}=({\bf r},a\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
is the composite lag vector. Isotropic kernels satisfy the inequality
C(u)\geq-C(0)/d

[
12
, p. 34]
. Hence, if the same functional form
C(\cdot)
is valid for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, by taking the limit of the lower bound as
d\to\infty
, it follows that
C(\cdot)
is non-negative everywhere, and therefore the hole effect is prohibited.

Gneiting’s non-separable kernels are fully-symmetric and expressed as
[
19
]
:

C(r,\tau)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left(\psi(\tau^{2})\right)^{d/2}}\,\phi\left(\frac{r^{2}}{\psi(\tau^{2})}\right),\;({\bf r},\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},

(34)

where
\phi(\cdot)
is a completely monotone function and
\psi(\cdot)
is a positive function with a completely monotone derivative (i.e., a Bernstein function).
Hence, this kernel family excludes negative values (i.e., the “hole effect”).

In contrast, the LDHO kernel in the underdamped regime (
23
) allows negative correlations at certain spatial and temporal lags even for large
d
. As evidenced in (
24
) and (
2
), oscillations are favored by (i)
\tau_{c}\uparrow
and (ii)
\epsilon\uparrow
, since (i) slows down the temporal and (ii) slows down the spatial decay. On the other hand, large values of
b
tend to suppress correlations as
\tau\uparrow
and thus also suppress oscillations. Fig.
3
illustrates a kernel with a “deep hole effect”: the normalized
C(r,\tau)
is plotted for the same hyperparameters as in Fig.
1
except that
\epsilon=3
instead of
\epsilon=1
. In this case, the most negative peak of
C(r,\tau)
is
C(0,0.7538)=-0.7853
.

Fig. 3:
Normalized
C(r,\tau)
and isolevel contours (red online) in the underdamped regime, obtained from (
23
) using

\tilde{\omega}_{d}=3\pi/2
,
\tilde{\tau}_{c}=3
,

Marginal kernels

All three LDHO
spatial
marginal kernels exhibit square exponential decay of the correlations [cf. (
24
), (
29
), (
32
)]. This is inherited from the square exponential spectral decay of the dispersion function
A(k)
(
V-A
) which is transferred to real space via the spectral integral (
57
).
The
temporal
marginal kernels in all three regimes behave as the respective purely temporal LHDO kernels (
9
) with renormalized coefficients.

Interactions

The LDHO kernels exhibit space-time interactions enabled by the hyperparameter
b
which determines (in all three regimes) to what extent the spatial and temporal lags are coupled in non-separable expressions [cf. (
23
), (
3
), (
4
)]. If
b=0
separable space-time covariance models are obtained. The presence of interactions in any given dataset can be tested using statistical separability tests which are based on the
interaction ratio

[
41
,
21
]
:

Q_{\textrm{int}}(r,\tau)\triangleq\frac{C(0,0)C(r,\tau)}{C(r,0)C(0,\tau)}=\frac{C(0,0)C(r,\tau)}{C_{\mathrm{S}}(r)\,C_{\mathrm{T}}(\tau)}\,.

(35)

The interaction ratio is equal to one for separable models
[
42
]
. From (
35
) it also follows that
Q_{\textrm{int}}(r,0)=Q_{\textrm{int}}(0,\tau)=1
. Within the framework of LDHO models, an initial estimate of
b
can be obtained by fitting the marginal temporal covariance kernels to the data, exploiting the fact that
b>0
modifies the
\tau
dependence of the temporal marginal kernels.
For nonzero
r,\tau
, it is shown empirically (cf. plots in Section S2 of the Supplement) that
Q_{\textrm{int}}(r,\tau)
can take both positive and negative values depending on the hyperparameter values and the space-time lags.

On the other hand, the LDHO temporal Fourier modes
\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau)
satisfy the linear generative ODE (
6
), albeit with
{\bf k}
-dependent coefficients. The linearity of the generative ODE implies a lack of interaction between different Fourier modes. Hence, the LDHO spatiotemporal kernel cannot capture nonlinear interactions that emerge in turbulent flows
[
43
]
and cosmological structure formation
[
44
]
. Nonetheless, this does not preclude the use of LDHO kernels as approximations, especially in the framework of variational Gaussian processes
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
.

Periodicity

Periodic kernels are suitable for physical processes that exhibit regular or quasi-regular variation in time such as stellar activity
[
48
]
. The MacKay periodic kernel
[
49
]

C(t,t^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}\,\exp\left[-a\sin^{2}\left(\pi(t-t^{\prime})/T\right)\right]
, where
T
is the period, is used in such cases
[
1
]
. The periodic kernel is often multiplied with a square exponential, in order to model quasi-periodic behavior
[
1
,
3
,
48
]
. In spatial processes, the product of cosine,
\cos({\bf k}_{0}\cdot{\bf r})
, and exponential,
\exp(-\|{{\bf r}}\|/\xi)
kernels is used for the same purpose
[
13
, p. 97]
. The MacKay kernel can also be used to construct products of separable terms which are periodic along each direction of the input space
[
4
, p. 120]
. Separable scale mixtures that comprise products of periodic components with squared exponential functions at multiple scales have also been considered
[
6
]
.
The LDHO covariance (
23
) is, by construction, a
quasi-periodic spatiotemporal
kernel. In contrast with the MacKay kernel which only takes positive values, the LDHO functions incorporates both positive and negative correlations. Furthermore, at the limit
\tau_{c}\to\infty
, the LDHO kernel (
23
) tends to a non-damped periodic function.

Connection with other models

The Matérn model comprises a family of flexible spatial kernels with smoothness properties controlled by a hyperparameter
\nu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

[
50
,
1
,
30
]
. Spatiotemporal extensions of the Matérn model have been derived
[
19
]
. The LDHO temporal kernel (
9d
) at critical damping is equivalent to the temporal Matérn model with
\nu=3/2
. However, the associated spatiotemporal LDHO kernel (
4
) exhibits different space-time interactions, inherited by the dispersion functions, than the spatiotemporal Matérn model. LDHO models with temporal smoothness orders
\nu=p+1/2
,
p\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
can be obtained using higher-order generative ODEs (see Section
VIII
).

Covariance Kernels based on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Model

In this section we investigate the application of hybrid spectral matching to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (O-U) process which satisfies the first-order SODE:

\frac{\text{d}{z}(t;\zeta)}{\text{d}t}+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}{z}(t;\zeta)=\sigma_{\eta}\eta(t;\zeta)\,,

where

is the standard Gaussian white noise. The covariance of the O-U process is given by

\tau_{c}\to\tau_{c}({\bf k})=\tilde{\tau}_{c}/B({\bf k}),\,\sigma^{2}\to\sigma^{2}({\bf k})=\sigma_{0}^{2}A({\bf k})

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}(k,\tau)

(41)

(42)

A(k)=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta k}
,

[

VIII
Conclusions

This paper responds to the need for non-separable covariance kernels that incorporate space-time interactions
[
21
]
by proposing a hybrid spectral approach. New spatiotemporal covariance kernels are then derived which can be used for regression and classification tasks involving Gaussian processes.

Our results are of interest for the analysis of spatiotemporal data obtained from processes whose evolution is governed by SPDEs. The covariance kernels in such cases should ideally be derived by solving a generative PDE associated with the SPDE that represents the EOM of the process. However, the explicit solution of PDEs is impossible except in simplified cases (e.g., linear models, constant coefficients, tractable initial/boundary conditions), thus hindering the development of physically consistent covariance kernels. In many cases, the generative PDE is not even known.

The novel hybrid spectral approach proposed herein overcomes the kernel solvability problem. It is based on the Cressie-Huang idea
[
18
]
, i.e., the construction of non-separable covariance kernels by inverting permissible spectral densities
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
. However, we introduce two important novel elements:

1.

Instead of
\widetilde{C}({\bf k},\omega)
, our starting point involves time-dependent kernels
C(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta})
which are derived from a generative ODE. Herein, we focus on the ODE associated with the stochastic, linear, damped harmonic oscillator.

2.

The
{\bf k}
-dependence is inserted by means of suitable dispersion relations that modulate the coefficients of the temporal kernels and lead to admissible Fourier modes
\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}({\bf k},\tau)\triangleq C\left(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta}({\bf k})\right)
; these are ultimately integrated (by means of the inverse Fourier transform) to obtain non-separable covariance kernels with space-time interactions.

The spatiotemporal interactions are thus inserted in the kernel by means of the dispersion relations which modify the oscillator hyperparameters at different spatial frequencies. A judicious choice of the dispersion relations leads to exactly solvable expressions for the LDHO spatiotemporal covariance kernels in the three oscillator regimes (underdamping, critical damping, and overdamping).

The LDHO kernel functions developed herein provide the first, to our knowledge, non-separable covariance kernels that exhibit both space-time interactions and consistent (that is, not subject to the “shallow hole” effect) oscillatory behavior in time, independently of the input space dimension
d
. In addition, the LDHO kernels have their underpinnings in the paradigmatic harmonic oscillator model and physically meaningful dispersion functions. The derived isotropic LDHO covariance kernels involve five hyperparameters (four in the critical regime). This already rich parametric dependence can be extended by means of scaling factors along each input dimension in the spirit of automatic relevance determination
[
53
]
.

The hybrid spectral approach can be investigated with generative ODEs other than LDHO for the temporal Fourier modes and different dispersion functions. For example, herein we also developed space-time kernels based on the first-order generative ODE that corresponds to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
[
54
]
. Higher-order generative ODEs are also useful, e.g. in calculations of background-error correlations in variational data assimilation
[
55
]
. The spatial roughness of the covariance kernels can be controlled by the asymptotic decay of the mode variance. This was demonstrated by deriving LDHO kernels based on dispersion functions with square exponential as well as exponential decay. Methods for consistent estimation of the LDHO kernel hyperparameters as well as novel algorithms and computational strategies for the efficient simulation of spatiotemporal LDHO Gaussian processes on large spatiotemporal grids, are needed. Particularly interesting is the extension of the hybrid spectral approach to multi-output (multivariate) Gaussian processes as well as Gaussian processes on manifolds (e.g., spherical surfaces).

Proof of Generative ODE for LDHO Covariance

Proof:

The EOM for the expectation of the harmonic oscillator’s displacement is expressed, based on (
3
), as follows

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\text{d}^{2}{z}(t;\zeta)}{\text{d}t^{2}}\right]+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\text{d}{z}(t;\zeta)}{\text{d}t}\right]+\frac{1}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}[z(t;\zeta)]=0.

(47)

Assume that the time derivatives and the expectation operator commute, being both linear operators
[
51
, p. 398]
. Then,
if
\overline{z}(t)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[z(t;\zeta)]
, the expectation
\overline{z}(t)
obeys the linear ODE

\frac{\text{d}^{2}}{\text{d}t^{2}}\overline{z}(t)+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\,\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\overline{z}(t)+\frac{\overline{z}(t)}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\,=0\,.

(48)

The solution of the ODE is the following damped harmonic function
[
56
, Chap. 24]

\overline{z}(t)=A\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/2\tau_{c}}\,\sin\left(\omega_{d}\,t+\phi_{0}\right),

where the constants
A
and
\phi_{0}
are determined by initial conditions. Without loss of generality we assume that
\overline{z}(t=0)=\text{d}\overline{z}(t=0)/\text{d}t=0
. Then it follows that
\overline{z}(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
and
C(\tau)=\mathbb{E}[z(t+\tau;\zeta)\,z(t;\zeta)]
.

LDHO covariance

To derive the EOM for the covariance function we use the SODE (
3
) at two different times,
t,t^{\prime}=t-\tau
, (i) we duplicate (
3
) for
t
and
t^{\prime}
; (ii) we multiply the respective sides of the two equations, and (iii) we calculate the expectation on both sides of the resulting EOM. The right-hand side includes the term
\sigma^{2}_{\eta}\,\eta(t;\zeta)\,\eta(t^{\prime};\zeta)
; upon calculating the expectation this leads to
\sigma^{2}_{\eta}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
in light of the noise covariance in (
4
). For brevity we use
z_{t}
and
\dot{z}_{t}
and
\ddot{z}_{t}
for
z(t;\zeta)
, and its first and second derivatives, respectively. The term on the left-hand side contains a sum of nine product pairs:

\displaystyle\ddot{z}_{t}\ddot{z}_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\ddot{z}_{t}\dot{z}_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{0}}\ddot{z}_{t}z_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\dot{z}_{t}\ddot{z}_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}^{2}}\dot{z}_{t}\dot{z}_{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}\omega^{2}_{0}}\dot{z}_{t}{z}_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{0}}z_{t}\ddot{z}_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{0}\tau_{c}}z_{t}\dot{z}_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\omega^{4}_{0}}z_{t}\,{z}_{t^{\prime}}\,.

(49)

The following lemma is used to evaluate the oscillator’s covariance EOM.

Lemma 1
(Covariance of process derivatives)

Let
z(t;\zeta)
be a stationary, stochastic process which admits derivatives up to order
n\in\mathbb{N}
in the mean-square sense. Then, the following
identity holds for the covariance of the derivatives of order
k
and
l
, where
\max(k,l)\leq n

[
51
, p. 407,417]
,
[
4
, p. 187]
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\text{d}^{k}z(t;\zeta)}{\text{d}t^{k}}\,\frac{\text{d}^{l}z(t^{\prime};\zeta)}{\text{d}{t^{\prime}}^{l}}\right]=(-1)^{l}\,\left.\frac{d^{k+l}C(\tau)}{d\tau^{k+l}}\right|_{\tau=t-t^{\prime}}\,.

Lemma
1
is used to evaluate the expectation of the summation (
A
) using
k,l=0,1,2
according to the order of derivatives in each product. The expectations of the following terms then cancel out: the second with the fourth and the sixth with the eighth. The remaining terms in the covariance EOM then include

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\ddot{z}_{t}\ddot{z}_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{\ddot{z}_{t}z_{t^{\prime}}+z_{t}\ddot{z}_{t^{\prime}}}{\omega^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\dot{z}_{t}\dot{z}_{t^{\prime}}}{\tau_{c}^{2}}+\frac{z_{t}\,{z}_{t^{\prime}}}{\omega^{4}_{0}}\right]=\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(50)

Finally, the LDHO covariance EOM (
6
) is obtained by applying Lemma
1
to the left-hand side of (
50

\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}(k,\tau)=c_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lvert\tau\rvert(1+bk^{2})}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}-\epsilon k^{2}}\,\left(1+\frac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}+\frac{b\,k^{2}\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\right).

(69)

Based on the linearity of the IFT we obtain

\displaystyle C(r,\tau)=

\displaystyle c_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}}\,\left(1+\frac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\right)C_{1}(r,\tau)

\displaystyle+\frac{c_{0}b\,\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}}\,C_{2}(r,\tau),

(70)

where

\displaystyle C_{1}(r,\tau)=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IFT}}\left[\,\mathrm{e}^{-k^{2}\left(\epsilon+\frac{b\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\right)}\right],

(71a)

\displaystyle C_{2}(r,\tau)=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IFT}}\left[\,k^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-k^{2}\left(\epsilon+\frac{b\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\right)}\right].

(71b)

The IFTs in (
71
) can be calculated using Lemma
3
; more precisely, by setting
a^{2}=4\epsilon+\frac{2b\lvert\tau\rvert}{\tilde{\tau}_{c}}
it follows that

C_{1}(r,\tau)=\left(\frac{\tilde{\tau}_{c}}{2\pi\,b\lvert\tau\rvert+4\pi\epsilon\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\right)^{d/2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}\tilde{\tau}_{c}/\left(2\,b\lvert\tau\rvert+4\epsilon\tilde{\tau}_{c}\right)}.

(72)

To calculate
C_{2}(r,\tau)
we use the fact that
\|{{\bf k}}\|^{2}
is the image of the Laplace operator
-\nabla^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial^{2}/\partial r_{i}^{2}
under the Fourier transform
[
31
]
. Hence, it follows from (
B-C
) that
C_{2}(r,\tau)=-\nabla^{2}C_{1}(r,\tau)
.

Lemma 4
(The Laplacian of Gaussian)

Let
r=\|{{\bf r}}\|,\,{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and assume that
C_{1}(r,\tau)=\mathrm{e}^{-c(\tau)r^{2}}
, where
c(\tau)>0
for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, the Laplacian of
C_{1}(r,\tau)
is given by

\nabla^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-c(\tau)r^{2}}=\left[4r^{2}\,c^{2}(\tau)-2d\,c(\tau)\,\right]\,\mathrm{e}^{-c(\tau)r^{2}}.

(73)

Proof:

The Laplacian of a radial function, i.e.,
C_{1}(r,\tau)
, is given by
[
4
, p. 190]

\nabla^{2}C_{1}(r,\tau)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}C_{1}(r,\tau)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}+\left(\frac{d-1}{r}\right)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C_{1}(r,\tau)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}.

The Laplacian of the square exponential (
73
) follows from the above and the independence of
c(\tau)
on
r
.
∎

Next, we apply Lemma
4
to
C_{2}(r,\tau)=-\nabla^{2}C_{1}(r,\tau)
, where
c(\tau)\to\tilde{\tau}_{c}/\left(2\,b\lvert\tau\rvert+4\epsilon\tilde{\tau}_{c}\right)
, to obtain

C_{2}(r,\tau)=\left(\frac{d\tilde{\tau}_{c}}{b\lvert\tau\rvert+2\epsilon\tilde{\tau}_{c}}-\frac{r^{2}\tilde{\tau}_{c}^{2}}{(b\lvert\tau\rvert+2\epsilon\tilde{\tau}_{c})^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{r^{2}\tilde{\tau}_{c}}{2\,b\lvert\tau\rvert+4\epsilon\tilde{\tau}_{c}}}.

(74)

Finally, by combining (
B-C
) with (
72
) and (
74
) the
critically damped LDHO kernel (
4
) is obtained.
∎

S.2.2
Estimation Method

The method of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is commonly used to determine kernel hyperparameters
[
1
]
since it is computationally efficient
[
21
]
. However, MLE is computationally intensive and thus impractical for the current dataset which contains
N\gtrapprox 10^{5}
sampling points. Hence, for large datasets approximations based on composite likelihoods are often used
[
57
]
.

Herein we opt for the method of moments in which the hyperparameters are fitted by fitting the “theoretical” kernel function to sample-based kernel estimates
[
58
]
. The method of moments is computationally more efficient and provides an easy visual assessment of the quality of the fit. More specifically, instead of the covariance we use the semi-variogram function (also known as structure function, henceforward “variogram” for short) defined by
[
58
,
13
]

\gamma({\bf r},\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\,\var\left[z({\mathbf{s}}+{\bf r},t+\tau)-z({\mathbf{s}},t)\right]\,,

(75)

where
\var[\cdot]\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\cdot^{2}]-\mathbb{E}^{2}[\cdot]
is the variance operator. For stationary processes, it holds that

\gamma({\bf r},\tau)=C({\mathbf{0}},0)-C({\bf r},\tau)\,.

Hence, the variogram is equivalent to the covariance for stationary processes. However, estimation of the former is often preferred because (i) the variogram is purely a function of the space-time lags for processes that are non-stationary but satisfy the
intrinsic hypothesis
—the process
z({\mathbf{s}},t)
is non-stationary but the increments
z({\mathbf{s}}+{\bf r},t+\tau)-z({\mathbf{s}},t)
are stationary—and (ii) if the mean of
z({\mathbf{s}},t)
is constant but unknown, the sample-based variogram is an unbiased estimator while the covariance is not.

The variogram fitting is performed by means of the approximate weighted least squares method
[
58
, Eq. (2.6.12]
. The Matlab
\circledR
constrained minimization function
fmincon
is employed using the interior point algorithm, a maximum of
10^{4}
function evaluations and iterations, and tolerances equal to
10^{-4}
.
We first fit the marginal spatial and temporal variograms
[
59
,
17
]
to obtain initial estimates of the hyperparameters followed by a fit of the full LDHO kernel to the space-time variogram. The main steps of the estimation procedure are as follows:

\displaystyle A_{r;i,j}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{true}\,\;\textrm{if}\;r-\delta r\leq\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{i}-{\mathbf{s}}_{j}\rVert\leq r+\delta r\,\textrm{and}\;A_{r;i,j}=\mathrm{false}\,\;\textrm{otherwise},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{T}}\,\sum_{t=1}^{N_{T}}\hat{\gamma}_{t}(r_{k})\;.

S2. The role of the interaction ratio on prediction

In Gaussian process regression, the prediction at an unmeasured point
({\mathbf{s}}_{\ast},t_{\ast})
is determined by the conditional mean
[
1
,
4
]
which involves the covariance kernel. Let us consider the following thought experiment: We assume a stationary process
z({\mathbf{s}},t)
with known constant mean
m
and covariance model
C(r,\tau)
. We aim to predict the values of the process
({\mathbf{s}}_{\ast},t_{\ast})
assuming that there is only one sampling point,
({\mathbf{s}}_{0},t_{0})
, in the “neighborhood” of
({\mathbf{s}}_{\ast},t_{\ast})
(this means that other sampling points are sufficiently far in space-time to consider their influence negligible. Then, the forecast can be expressed as
[
13
,
4
]

\hat{z}_{\ast}=m+\frac{C(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert,\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})}{C(0,0)}\,\,\left(z_{0}-m\right)\,,

(83)

where
\hat{z}_{\ast}
is the conditional mean at
({\mathbf{s}}_{\ast},t_{\ast})
,
z_{0}
is the sample value at
({\mathbf{s}}_{0},t_{0})
, and
C(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert,\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})
is the non-separable LDHO kernel.

The predictive equation can also be expressed as

\hat{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}=\frac{C(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert,\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})}{C(0,0)}\,\,{z^{\prime}}_{0}\,,

(84)

where
\hat{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}=\hat{z}_{\ast}-m
and
{z^{\prime}}_{0}={z}_{0}-m
are fluctuations of the process around the global mean.

Let us now consider that instead of using the non-separable covariance kernel, we use the separable model

K(r,\tau)=K_{1}(r)\,K_{2}(\tau)
. Furthermore, we assume that the separable model has the same marginal kernels as the non-separable model, i.e.,
K_{S}(r)=C_{\mathrm{S}}(r)
and
K_{T}(\tau)=C_{\mathrm{T}}(\tau)
. The following relations hold between the separable model and the marginals of the non-separable model:

C_{\mathrm{S}}(r)\triangleq K(r,0)=K_{2}(0)\,K_{1}(r)\,,

C_{\mathrm{T}}(\tau)\triangleq K(0,\tau)=K_{1}(0)\,K_{2}(\tau)\,,

Thus, the product of the non-separable model’s marginal kernels is given by

C_{\mathrm{S}}(r)C_{\mathrm{T}}(\tau)=K_{1}(0)\,K_{2}(0)K_{1}(r)\,K_{2}(\tau)=C(0,0)\,K_{1}(r)\,K_{2}(\tau)\,,

(85)

where in deriving the above we took into account that
C(0,0)=K(0,0)
.

The predictive equation for the separable model becomes

\tilde{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}=\frac{K(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert,\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})}{K(0,0)}\,\,{z^{\prime}}_{0}=\frac{K_{1}(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert)\,K_{2}(\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})}{C(0,0)}\,\,{z^{\prime}}_{0}\,,

(86)

where
\tilde{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}
is the conditional mean under the separable model.
Then, using (
85
) it follows that

\tilde{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{S}}(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert)\,C_{\mathrm{T}}(\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})}{C^{2}(0,0)}\,\,{z^{\prime}}_{0}\,.

(87)

Finally, assuming that
{z^{\prime}}_{0}\neq 0
, it follows from (
84
) and (
87
) that the ratio of the two predictors (i.e.,
\hat{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}
for the non-separable model over
\tilde{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}
for the separable model) is given by

\frac{\hat{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}}{\tilde{z}^{\prime}_{\ast}}=\frac{C(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert,\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})\,C(0,0)}{C_{\mathrm{S}}(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert)\,C_{\mathrm{T}}(\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})}\,=Q_{\mathrm{int}}(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert,\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})\,.

(88)

Hence, the
interaction ratio

Q_{\mathrm{int}}(\lVert{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}-{\mathbf{s}}_{0}\rVert,\tau_{\ast}-\tau_{0})
determines the relative change obtained by introducing a non-separable kernel with space-time interactions to the prediction obtained from the separable kernel. Note that the LDHO marginal temporal kernel given by (26) in the main text has a harmonic dependence that involves
\tau
but not
r
, while the space-time kernel given by (23) in the main manuscript involves a harmonic term that depends on both
r
and
\tau
.

C_{2}(r,\tau)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\,r^{\nu}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}kk^{d/2+1}\,J_{\nu}(kr)\,\mathrm{e}^{-a_{{\rm Re}}k},\;\textrm{where}\;a_{{\rm Re}}=\epsilon+\frac{\xi\lvert\tau\rvert}{2\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\,.

The latter can be expressed in terms of
C_{1}(r,\tau)
by noticing that the only difference between the two is an extra factor of
k
in the integral for
C_{2}(r,\tau)
. Therefore, by taking advantage that
C_{2}(r,\tau)
depends on
a_{{\rm Re}}
only via
\mathrm{e}^{-a_{{\rm Re}}k}
, it follows that

C_{2}(r,\tau)=-\frac{\partial C_{1}(r,\tau)}{\partial a_{{\rm Re}}}\,.

Thus, we obtain by differentiating
C_{1}(r,\tau)
in (
120
)

C_{2}(r,\tau)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})\,(d+1)\,a_{{\rm Re}}}{\pi^{(d+1)/2}\left(r^{2}+a_{{\rm Re}}^{2}\right)^{(d+3)/2}}\,.

(121)

This concludes the proof.
∎

S4. Kernels based on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck ODE

The covariance of the O-U process is given by

C(\tau)=\sigma^{2}\,\exp(-\lvert\tau\rvert/\tau_{c})
where
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\tau_{c}/{2}

[
51
, p. 448]
. The radial dispersion relations are given by

\sigma^{2}\to\sigma^{2}(k)=\sigma_{0}^{2}A(k),\,\tau_{c}\to\tau_{c}(k)=\tilde{\tau}_{c}/B(k)
. The O-U temporal Fourier modes for radial dispersion functions are thus given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{-\omega}(k,\tau)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{0}\,A(k)\,\exp\left[-\lvert\tau\rvert\,B(k)/\tilde{\tau}_{c}\right].

(124)

Since
A(k)
is dimensionless,
[\sigma_{0}^{2}]=[\mathrm{X}]^{2}[\mathrm{L}]^{d}
, where
\mathrm{L}
represents length, so that the FT (
124
) be dimensionally correct.
Based on the IFT (
22b
), the O-U covariance kernel is given by the integral
(\nu=d/2-1)
:

C({r},\tau)=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}r^{\nu}}\int_{0}^{\infty}k^{d/2}\,{J_{\nu}(kr)}A(k)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lvert\tau\rvert\,B(k)/\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\,{\mathrm{d}}k\,.

(125)

In the following, we derive spatiotemporal kernel expressions for two different choices of dispersion functions.

1.

A(k)=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta k^{2}},\;B(k)=a+bk^{2}
where
a,b,\beta>0
are hyperparameters with units
[b]=[\beta]=[L]^{2}
,
[a]=[L]^{0}
:

The spectral integral (
125
) becomes

C({r},\tau)=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}\,\mathrm{e}^{-a\,\lvert\tau\rvert/\tilde{\tau}_{c}}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\,r^{\nu}}\int_{0}^{\infty}k^{d/2}{J_{\nu}(kr)}\,\mathrm{e}^{-bk^{2}\lvert\tau\rvert\,/\tilde{\tau}_{c}-\beta k^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}k\,.

Using the table of integrals
[
52
, 6.631.4, p. 706]
it follows that

C(r,\tau)=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}\,\mathrm{e}^{-a\,\lvert\tau\rvert/\tilde{\tau}_{c}}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/4\left(\beta+b\,\lvert\tau\rvert/\tilde{\tau}_{c}\right)}}{\left(\frac{2b\lvert\tau\rvert}{\tilde{\tau}_{c}}+2\beta\right)^{d/2}}\,.

(126)

\sigma_{0}
,
\tilde{\tau}_{c}/a
,
\tilde{\tau}_{c}/b
and
\beta

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{S}}(r)=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}\,}{(4\pi\,\beta)^{d/2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/4\beta}\,,

(127a)

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{T}}(\tau)=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}\,\left(1+b\,\lvert\tau\rvert/\beta\tilde{\tau}_{c}\right)^{-d/2}}{(4\pi\,\beta)^{d/2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-a\,\lvert\tau\rvert/\tilde{\tau}_{c}}\,\,.

2.

A(k)=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta k}

(128)

The kernel (

\sigma_{0}
,
\tilde{\tau}_{c}/a

\tilde{\tau}_{c}/\xi
