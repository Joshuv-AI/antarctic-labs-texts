Wigner measures of electromagnetic waves in heterogeneous bianisotropic media Thanks: Corresponding author: É. Savin, ONERA–The French Aerospace Lab, 6 chemin de la Vauve aux Granges, FR-91123 Palaiseau cedex, France (eric.savin@onera.fr).
Jean-Luc Akian, Éric Savin

Abstract
. We study the propagation of high-frequency electromagnetic waves in randomly heterogeneous bianisotropic media with dissipative properties. For that purpose we consider randomly fluctuating optical responses of such media with correlation lengths comparable to the typical wavelength of the waves. Although the fluctuations are weak, they induce multiple scattering over long propagation times and/or distances such that the waves end up travelling in many different directions with mixed polarizations. We derive the dispersion and evolution properties of the Wigner measure of the electromagnetic fields, which describes their angularly-resolved energy density in this propagation regime. The analysis starts from Maxwell’s equations with general constitutive equations. We first ignore the random fluctuations of the optical response and obtain uncoupled transport equations for the components of the Wigner measure on the different propagation modes (polarizations). Then we use a multi-scale expansion of the Wigner mesure to obtain the radiative transfer equations satisfied by these components when the fluctuations are no longer ignored. The radiative transfer equations are coupled through their collisional parts, which account for the scattering of waves by the random fluctuations and their possible changes in polarization. The collisional kernels describing these processes depend on the power and cross-power spectral densities of the fluctuations at the wavelength scale. The overall derivation is based on the interpretation of Wigner transforms and Wigner measures in terms of semiclassical pseudo-differential operators in their standard quantization.



1.
Introduction

The analysis of electromagnetic wave propagation in complex, heterogeneous media has relevance to a wide range of engineering applications, including radar imaging and remote sensing
[
49
]
, optical imaging
[
13
]
, atmospheric physics
[
30
]
, or communications
[
25
]
, among others. As the complexity of the propagation media can not be known exactly but only assessed in a statistical sense, we model their inhomogeneities (or imperfections) as random fields and thus consider Maxwell’s equations with randomly fluctuating electromagnetic parameters. This approach pertains to waveguides filled with dielectric materials having numerous imperfections, atmospheres and clouds, colloidal suspensions, biological tissues, or plasmas, which is the type of media we have more particularly in mind here.

Asymptotic methods can be used to characterize both quantitatively and qualitatively the wave fields when some explicit scales can be identified and separated to describe the corresponding propagation regimes
[
3
,
46
,
48
]
. Recent mathematical works on electromagnetic waves in random media have considered layered half-spaces
[
22
]
, waveguides
[
4
]
, or beam propagation in open environments
[
11
]
, for example. In these works a preferred direction of propagation can be identified, and the electromagnetic field can be decomposed into plane wave components transverse to that direction. Their amplitudes are random fields of which evolution is driven by the random fluctuations of the dielectric parameters and can be studied in different asymptotic regimes. For example, the cumulative scattering effects induced by weak inhomogeneities at large distances of propagation in a waveguide or a beam are typically manifested in the exponential decay of the average fields, the enhancement of their random fluctuations, and depolarization.

We rather investigate in this paper the situation where no preferred direction of propagation can be identified beforehand as waves travel in open random media. The wavelength and the correlation length of the random fluctuations of the electromagnetic parameters are small and comparable, while the propagation distance and/or time lapse are large. The amplitude of the random fluctuations is also weak. This high-frequency scaling is of interest if the objective is for instance to probe some inhomogeneities from remote sensors. In this setting, waves are multiply scattered by the inhomogeneities as they travel in all directions, and their polarization and phase get randomized. It is then known that the energy density is the relevant quantity to focus on, and that it can be evaluated from the Wigner transform of the wave field and its non-negative high-frequency limit measure–the Wigner measure
[
23
,
36
,
38
,
54
]
. The evolution of the Wigner measure is described by radiative transfer equations
[
15
,
30
,
39
,
52
]
with linear collisional operators of which kernels depend on the correlation structure of the inhomogeneities
[
9
,
47
]
. Actually, the sole information needed to account for the fluctuations of the electromagnetic parameters is their power spectral densities,
i.e.
the spatial Fourier transforms of their auto-correlation functions. These radiative transfer equations for electromagnetic vector waves were already derived in
[
40
,
47
]
for random fluctuations in isotropic media, and subsequently in
[
18
,
19
]
for random fluctuations in bianisotropic media with homogeneous mean background parameters and no dissipation. Our aim is to extend these results to more general constitutive equations, for heterogeneous materials with dispersive and dissipative properties.

Radiative transfer equations have a geometrical interpretation
[
34
]
notably in terms of bicharacteristic rays that is, rays in phase space, which parallels classical asymptotic ray methods
[
7
,
32
]
. The latter are applicable to synthetic aperture radar (SAR) simulation for example
[
6
]
, including the investigation of multipath and vibration signatures in radar imaging and remote sensing or the computation of antenna transfer functions in heterogeneous media whenever the approximations of geometrical optics hold. Ray tracing solvers are needed in this respect
[
24
]
. One of the possible applications of this research concerns the development of ray tracing algorithms in phase space for complex media such as plasmas
[
51
]
. Ray tracing has been intensively developed in the last decade in the context of computer graphics
[
42
,
28
]
, notably, and one also expects that the application programming interfaces that have been implemented in the video game industry, among others, could be used in the context of computational electromagnetics alike.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sect.
2
we introduce the basic physical framework and notations used throughout, and we summarize our main results. The relevance of considering a Wigner transform and its non-negative limit measure for the analysis of high-frequency wave propagation phenomena is emphasized. We first consider the transport regime in the absence of fluctuations of the optical response that models instantaneous effects, and then the radiative transfer regime when weak random fluctuations are taken into account. The detailed derivation of the transport equations is outlined in Sect.
3
, and the detailed derivation of the radiative transfer equations is outlined in Sect.
4
. We use pseudo-differential calculus and the interpretation of Wigner transforms in terms of semiclassical pseudo-differential operators, choosing the same quantization for both of them. We argue that this choice clarifies the analyses as compared to other approaches where the Wigner transforms and the operators have different quantizations. A summary and outlook are finally drawn in Sect.
5
.

2.
Maxwell’s equations in bianisotropic media and main results

2.1.
Physical setting

We wish to analyze high-frequency electromagnetic wave propagation phenomena in general heterogeneous, bianisotropic dielectric media. The Ampère and Faraday laws read:

(1)

\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{D}}}{\partial t}={\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\times{\boldsymbol{H}}-{\boldsymbol{J}}\,,\quad\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{B}}}{\partial t}=-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\times{\boldsymbol{E}}\,,

where
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is the magnetic flux density,
{\boldsymbol{D}}
is the electric flux density, or electric displacement field,
{\boldsymbol{E}}
is the electric field,
{\boldsymbol{H}}
is the magnetic field, and
{\boldsymbol{J}}
is the density of electric current. Taking the divergence of Eq. (
1
) the fluxes are subjected to Gauss laws:

(2)

{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{D}}=\rho\,,\quad{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{B}}=0\,,

for
\rho
being the density of electric charge, which is then related to the current by the equation of continuity of charge:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{J}}=0\,.

Here we consider open media in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with no external charge (
\rho=0
) and no external current (
{\boldsymbol{J}}={\bf 0}
). Besides, Gauss laws hold for all times provided that they hold at some initial time. The Ampère and Faraday laws are supplemented with constitutive equations for general linear dispersive media with the properties of causality, time-invariance, and locality (see
e.g.

[
14
,
29
,
31
]
), which read:

(3)

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{D}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)&={\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{d}\star{\boldsymbol{E}})({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{H}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{d}\star{\boldsymbol{H}})({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\,,\\
{\boldsymbol{B}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)&={\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{d}\star{\boldsymbol{E}})({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{H}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{d}\star{\boldsymbol{H}})({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\,,\end{split}

where
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the spatially variable permittivity tensor,
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the spatially variable permeability tensor, and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})
are spatially variable magnetoelectric tensors. Also
\star
stands for the time convolution product:

({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{d}\star{\boldsymbol{E}})({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},\tau){\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t-\tau){\mathrm{d}}\tau

with similar expressions for the other terms. The upper bound
t
is because one may wish to consider initial data at
t=0
in the subsequent analyses; see Sect.
2.2
or Example
2
below. In Eq. (
3
)
{\boldsymbol{P}}=\smash{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{d}\star{\boldsymbol{E}}}
stands for the polarization field and
{\boldsymbol{M}}=\smash{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{d}\star{\boldsymbol{H}})}
stands for the magnetization field, but these constitutive equations also account for coupled magnetoelectric effects through the magnetoelectric tensors. Here we consider the subclass of bianisotropic materials for which the following symmetry relationships hold:
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{0}}=\smash{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{*}_{0}}
,
i.e.
the conjugate transpose of
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}
; and
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{0}}=\smash{{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}^{*}_{0}}
,
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}}=\smash{{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{*}_{0}}
. These symmetry conditions reduce the number of independent constitutive parameters in
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{0}
,
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}}
, and
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}
to 21.

Now plugging Eq. (
3
) into Eq. (
1
), we arrive at Maxwell’s
6\times 6
system for the electric field
{\boldsymbol{E}}
and magnetic field
{\boldsymbol{H}}
:

(4)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{0}&{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}\\
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{*}_{0}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{d}&{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{d}\\
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{d}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{d}\end{bmatrix}\star\right)\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{E}}\\
{\boldsymbol{H}}\end{pmatrix}+{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\times\begin{bmatrix}{\bf 0}&-{\boldsymbol{I}}\\
{\boldsymbol{I}}&{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{E}}\\
{\boldsymbol{H}}\end{pmatrix}={\bf 0}\,,

where
{\boldsymbol{I}}
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix. We introduce the following matrices of electromagnetic tensors:

(5)

{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})&{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})\\
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{*}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad{\boldsymbol{K}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)&{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\\
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\end{bmatrix}\,.

\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}}
is called the optical response, or (generalized) susceptibility tensor, and models instantaneous effects, while
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{d}}
is called the (generalized) susceptibility kernel, and models memory as well as dissipation effects
[
29
,
31
]
. We assume throughout the paper that
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}}
, which is Hermitian, is also positive definite, as in
[
18
,
19
]
. A negative definite optical response gives rise to a negative index of refraction, as in metamaterials
[
53
]
.

Example 1
.

An isotropic, lossless medium is
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})}=\smash{\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{I}},\mu_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{I}})}
and
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{d}}={\bf 0}
. A chiral medium is a reciprocal bi-isotropic medium with
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{0}}=\smash{\epsilon_{0}}{\boldsymbol{I}}
,
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}}=\smash{\mu_{0}}{\boldsymbol{I}}
, and a purely imaginary magnetoelectric tensor
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}=\mathrm{i}\chi{\boldsymbol{I}}
, where
\smash{\epsilon_{0}}
and
\smash{\mu_{0}}
are the permittivity and permeability constants, and
\chi\in\mathbb{R}
is the magnetoelectric constant. An example of a dissipative, local homogeneous material (in the terminology of
[
14
]
) is the Lorentz model with damping in
[
45
]
with:

(6)

{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_{0}{\boldsymbol{I}}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\mu_{0}{\boldsymbol{I}}\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad\widehat{{\boldsymbol{K}}}_{d}(\omega)=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\epsilon}_{d}(\omega){\boldsymbol{I}}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix}\,,

where
\widehat{\epsilon}_{d}(\omega)=\smash{\frac{\epsilon_{0}\omega_{p}^{2}}{-\omega^{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega\Gamma+\omega_{0}^{2}}}
is the susceptibility (in frequency domain),
\omega_{p}
is the plasma frequency such that
\smash{\omega_{p}^{2}}=\smash{\frac{Ne^{2}}{m\epsilon_{0}}}
,
\omega_{0}
is a characteristic frequency for the motion of an electron with mass
m
and charge
e
,
N
is the electron density, and
\Gamma
is a damping loss rate. The Fourier transform in time domain is
\smash{\widehat{{\boldsymbol{K}}}_{d}}(\omega)=\smash{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{d}(t){\mathrm{d}}t}
. Drude material is
\Gamma=0
and
\smash{\omega_{0}=0}
. Other examples are described in e.g.
[
14
,
18
,
19
,
31
]
.

We then introduce the scalar product
\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rangle={({\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{u}})}^{*}{\boldsymbol{v}}={{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{v}}
(for
\smash{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}^{*}_{0}}=\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}}
where
\smash{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}^{*}_{0}}
stands for the conjugate transpose of
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}}
), and the electromagnetic energy density
{\mathcal{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
and the energy flow density (Poynting vector)
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{F}}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
:

(7)

\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)&=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{E}}\\
{\boldsymbol{H}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{E}}\\
{\boldsymbol{H}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{2}{{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+\frac{1}{2}{{\boldsymbol{H}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{H}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\\
&\quad\quad+\Re\mathfrak{e}\{{{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{H}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\}\,,\\
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{F}}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)&=\overline{{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}\times{\boldsymbol{H}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\,,\end{split}

for
\Re\mathfrak{e}
standing for the real part, such that in the undamped case
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}\equiv{\bf 0}
:

\frac{\partial{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t}+{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{F}}}=0\,,\quad\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\int{\mathcal{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{x}}=0\,.

The conservation law above establishes how the energy density is spread in space, but it does not describe how it propagates. Our main objective in this paper is to describe how this quantity evolves both in space and direction for high-frequency waves induced by, say, strongly oscillating initial conditions, and for a randomly fluctuating optical response and a non-vanishing susceptibility kernel
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{d}}
. In the next section we show how this goal can be achieved using Wigner transforms of the wave fields and their high-frequency limits. We illustrate it with the wave equation with constant speed, before we turn to Maxwell’s system (
4
) and state our main results in Sect.
2.3
.

2.2.
High-frequency limit and Wigner transform

Maxwell’s equations (
4
) are subjected to highly oscillating initial data for the electromagnetic field
{\boldsymbol{u}}=({\boldsymbol{E}},{\boldsymbol{H}})
at
t=0
. Consider for example the WKB form
[
23
,
21
,
50
]
:

(8)

{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},0)={\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}^{\text{WKB}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\Re\mathfrak{e}\{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}})\operatorname{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}S_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}})}\}\,,

where
\smash{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}}
is a square integrable function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
; and
\smash{S_{I}}
is an integrable scalar function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(at least locally), as well as its derivative
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}S_{I}}
. The small parameter
\varepsilon
represents the typical wavelength of oscillations of these data. These oscillations are inherited by the actual electromagnetic field
{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
satisfying (
4
) at all times, which prevents it from converging nicely as the wavelength
\varepsilon
gets smaller and smaller in the high-frequency limit. A way to tackle this shortcoming is to consider quadratic observables of
{\boldsymbol{u}}
instead, as emphasized in
e.g.

[
1
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
26
,
27
,
44
,
47
]
for different types of waves. In particular, the Wigner transform
[
23
,
36
,
38
,
54
]
of the solutions of Eq. (
4
) shall be considered since its evolution in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
can be derived explicitly. It provides a phase space description of how the associated energy density propagates in this very limit. The advantages of this approach over more classical methods such as the WKB method, which is suggested by the type of data (
8
), are discussed in
[
21
,
50
]
for example. Notably, the Wigner transform needs much lower regularity assumptions on
\smash{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}}
and
\smash{S_{I}}
than in the WKB method, and evades the singularities (focal points or caustics) that can develop in finite times with the latter method.

Let
\vartheta\in[0,1]
. For tempered distributions
{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the Wigner transform is:

(9)

{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\varepsilon}^{\vartheta}[{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}]({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{y}}}{\boldsymbol{u}}\left({\boldsymbol{x}}-\varepsilon(1-\vartheta){\boldsymbol{y}}\right){{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{*}\left({\boldsymbol{x}}+\varepsilon\vartheta{\boldsymbol{y}}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{y}}\,.

We denote by
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\varepsilon}^{\vartheta}[{\boldsymbol{u}}]:={\boldsymbol{W}}_{\varepsilon}^{\vartheta}[{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{u}}]
the self Wigner transform of
{\boldsymbol{u}}
. Now let
({\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon})
be a bounded sequence in
\smash{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}
, the set of square integrable functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then it can be shown that, up to extracting a subsequence if need be,
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\varepsilon}^{\vartheta}[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}]
has a weak limit (in the sense of tempered distribution) as
\varepsilon\to 0
which is independent of the choice of
\vartheta
in Eq. (
9
). Also if
{\boldsymbol{W}}[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}]
is such a limit, it is a non-negative, Hermitian matrix-valued measure. These results are detailed in
e.g.

[
23
,
54
]
. Here the notation of
[
23
, p. 330]
is used for the limit
{\boldsymbol{W}}
(independent of
\varepsilon
) of the family
\smash{({\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon})}
(dependent of
\varepsilon
) but clearly the measure
{\boldsymbol{W}}[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}]
is independent of
\varepsilon
. The scalar Wigner measure is the trace of the latter
W[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}]=\operatorname{Tr}{\boldsymbol{W}}[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}]
and can be directly related to the energy density of the sequence
({\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon})
in the high-frequency limit. Taking the Wigner transform of the sequence of WKB data (
8
) for example, one obtains:

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{W}}[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}^{\text{WKB}}]({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}})&={\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}}){{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{*}_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}})\delta({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}S_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\,,\\
W[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}^{\text{WKB}}]({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}})&=|{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}\delta({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}S_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\,.\end{split}

If these data are propagated by the wave equation in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with constant speed
c
:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t^{2}}-\Delta{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}={\bf 0}\,,&\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\;t>0\,,\\
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{x}},0)={\bf 0}\,,\;\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}({\boldsymbol{x}},0)={\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}^{\text{WKB}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,,&\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\end{split}

then for
t>0
Kirchhoff’s formula yields
[
17
, §2.4]
:

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\int_{S^{2}}t{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}^{\text{WKB}}({\boldsymbol{x}}-c{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}t)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\Omega({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})}{4\pi}\,,

where
S^{2}
is the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
centered at
{\bf 0}
, and
{\mathrm{d}}\Omega
is its surface differential element. The scalar Wigner measure of
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)
reads
[
23
, §3.3]
:

W[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}]({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}},t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\pm}|\smash{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}}+\alpha c{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}}t)}|^{2}\delta({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}S_{I}({\boldsymbol{x}}+\alpha c{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}}t))\,,

with the usual notation
{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\smash{\frac{{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\left|{\boldsymbol{k}}\right|}}
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq{\bf 0}
. It provides a characterization of the evolution of the associated energy density both in space and direction. Because of the assumed low regularity of
\smash{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}}
and
\smash{S_{I}}
, a classical WKB method could not be used with these data. Note that more general data can be considered as well as shown in
[
23
]
.

2.3.
Main results

We derive the Wigner measure
{\boldsymbol{W}}
of the high-frequency electromagnetic field
{\boldsymbol{u}}
which obeys Maxwell’s
6\times 6
system (
4
) when the optical response
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}}
exhibits in addition random fluctuations. That is:

(10)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left({\boldsymbol{K}}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+\int_{0}^{t}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},\tau){\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t-\tau){\mathrm{d}}\tau\right)+\boldsymbol{M}({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)={\bf 0}

for
{\boldsymbol{x}},t\in{\mathcal{O}}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
in some open domain
{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\smash{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
and for highly oscillating initial conditions at
t=0
characterized by the small scale
\varepsilon\ll 1
. Here:

(11)

\boldsymbol{M}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf 0}&-{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}({\boldsymbol{k}})&{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix}

is the symmetric Maxwell operator for
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
being the skew-symmetric matrix such that
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}({\boldsymbol{k}}){\boldsymbol{a}}={\boldsymbol{k}}\times{\boldsymbol{a}}
for any vector
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\smash{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
, and accordingly:

\boldsymbol{M}({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf 0}&-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\\
{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\times&{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix}\,.

Also the actual optical response
{\boldsymbol{K}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
reads:

{\boldsymbol{K}}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})\left[{\boldsymbol{I}}+\sigma{\boldsymbol{V}}\left(\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\ell_{c}}\right)\right]\,,

where the dimensionless random matrix
{\boldsymbol{V}}
represents random fluctuations of the optical response
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}}
of the background medium, and is such that
\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}}{\boldsymbol{V}}={{\boldsymbol{V}}}^{*}\smash{{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}}
to preserve the Hermiticity of the actual optical response
{\boldsymbol{K}}
. It is assumed that
({\boldsymbol{V}}({\boldsymbol{y}}),\,{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\smash{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
is a second-order, mean-square homogeneous (spatially stationary) random field with zero mean
{\mathbb{E}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{V}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\right\}={\bf 0}
and integrable fourth-order autocorrelation tensor
{\boldsymbol{R}}
such that:

(12)

{\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}):={\mathbb{E}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{V}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\otimes{\boldsymbol{V}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime})\right\}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime})}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{R}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}){\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{k}}\,,

where
{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\cdot\right\}
stands for the mathematical expectation, or average. The second equality above stems from Bochner’s theorem and the fact that
{\boldsymbol{y}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{y}})
is positive semi-definite. The power spectral density of that random field is the matrix:

(13)

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{R}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{y}}}{\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{y}}

which is positive. The autocorrelation tensor is normalized such that
\smash{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{y}}}=\mathrm{O}(1)
and
{\boldsymbol{R}}({\bf 0})=\mathrm{O}(1)
. The length scale
\ell_{c}
is the correlation length and the dimensionless scalar
\sigma\geq 0
quantifies the amplitude of the fluctuations. These parameters are of the same order for all correlations of the fluctuations in the proposed model.

At first, the random fluctuations of the optical response are disregarded, setting
\sigma=0
. We state our main results formally in the open medium
{\mathcal{O}}
but emphasize here that they can be made rigorous by the method outlined in Remark
1
below. Possible boundary effects are evaded by this approach, though. This is a difficult topic that could be partly addressed by the analysis carried on in
[
1
]
. Let:

(14)

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}})&={\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{x}})\boldsymbol{M}({\boldsymbol{k}})\,,\\
{\boldsymbol{L}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)&=\mathrm{i}\omega{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{x}})\widehat{{\boldsymbol{K}}}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)\,,\end{split}

\smash{{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}}
being Hermitian for the scalar product
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle

\smash{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rangle}=\smash{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rangle}

(15)

{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}=\sum_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}

\smash{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\beta}\right\rangle}=\smash{{({\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})}^{*}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\beta}}=\smash{\delta_{\alpha\beta}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}}

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}&=\omega_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}\,,\\

\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}&={\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}\,.\end{split}

Then it is shown that:

{\boldsymbol{W}}=\sum_{\alpha}\delta(\omega+\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}\,,

\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{Tr}({\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{W}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}},t)){\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{k}}\,,\\

3.
Transport of high-frequency electromagnetic waves in bianisotropic media

19

3.1.
Semi-classical operators and Wigner measure

From now on let us introduce the space-time variable
{\boldsymbol{s}}=({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\in{\mathcal{O}}\times\mathbb{R}
and its dual variable
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
in the wave vector-frequency Fourier domain. Let
\boldsymbol{L}
be a smooth, compactly supported real matrix-valued function of both the space-time variable
{\boldsymbol{s}}
and impulse variable
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. For a vector field
{\boldsymbol{u}}
in
\smash{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}
endowed with the scalar product
\smash{\left({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{L^{2}}}=\smash{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{s}})\cdot\overline{{\boldsymbol{v}}}({\boldsymbol{s}})\,{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{s}}}
where
\smash{\overline{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}
stands for complex conjugation, consider the (semiclassical) pseudo-differential operator:

(24)

\boldsymbol{L}^{\vartheta}({\boldsymbol{s}},\varepsilon{\bf D}){\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}}\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}})}\boldsymbol{L}((1-\vartheta){\boldsymbol{s}}+\vartheta{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\varepsilon{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{\tau}})\,{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\,,

for
\vartheta\in[0,1]
. This parameter defines the so-called quantization of the operator
\boldsymbol{L}^{\vartheta}
. The case
\vartheta=0
corresponds to the standard quantization. It is simply denoted by
\smash{\boldsymbol{L}({\boldsymbol{s}},\varepsilon{\bf D})}
such that:

(25)

\boldsymbol{L}({\boldsymbol{s}},\varepsilon{\bf D}){\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{s}}}\boldsymbol{L}({\boldsymbol{s}},\varepsilon{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\widehat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\,,

where:

(26)

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\operatorname{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{s}}}{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{s}}){\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{s}}

stands for the space-time Fourier transform of
{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{s}})
. The case
\smash{\vartheta=1/2}
corresponds to Weyl quantization, which is usually denoted by
\smash{\boldsymbol{L}^{W}({\boldsymbol{s}},\varepsilon{\bf D})}
.

Now let
{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}
be tempered distributions defined on
\smash{\mathbb{R}^{4}}
and let
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\varepsilon}^{\vartheta}[{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}]({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
be their Wigner transform (
9

:

(27)

\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{\vartheta}({\boldsymbol{s}},\varepsilon{\bf D}){\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{L^{2}}=\operatorname{Tr}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}}\boldsymbol{L}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\varepsilon}^{\vartheta}[{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}]({\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{s}},{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,.

For a sequence
\smash{({\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon})}
uniformly bounded in
\smash{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}
, one can establish in particular that there exists a positive measure
{\boldsymbol{W}}[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}]
such that, up to extracting a subsequence if need be (see
e.g.

[
54

(28)

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{\vartheta}({\boldsymbol{s}},\varepsilon{\bf D}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}\right)_{L^{2}}=\operatorname{Tr}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}}\boldsymbol{L}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\boldsymbol{W}}[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}]({\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{s}},{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{L}\,,

independently of the quantization
\vartheta
. We thus recover the Wigner measure

invoked in Sect.

\smash{({\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon})}
in the phase space
\smash{\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}

20

dropping the superscript

Considering the leading-order term, one obtains as

for the Wigner measure

Evolution properties

Gathering the first order terms proportional to
\varepsilon

\varepsilon\to 0
:

(40)

\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{W}}+{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{W}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{{\boldsymbol{L}}}^{*}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{W}}-({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}){\boldsymbol{W}}+{\boldsymbol{L}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{W}}+{\boldsymbol{W}}{{\boldsymbol{L}}}^{*}_{1}={\bf 0}\,.

Next we multiply Eq. (
40

\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}
and on the right by
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{*}_{\alpha}
. From the property of projectors
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}
we first derive:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}&=\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}\sum_{\beta}\left((\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\beta})\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\beta}+\omega_{\beta}(\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\beta})\right)\\

The same holds for partial derivatives with respect to

\begin{split}(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{W}}){\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{*}_{\alpha}&=\partial_{k_{j}}({\boldsymbol{W}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{*}_{\alpha})-{\boldsymbol{W}}(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{*}_{\alpha})\\

where again the same holds for partial derivatives with respect to
x_{j}

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{W}}){\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{*}_{\alpha}&=(\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{k_{j}}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha})-(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})}^{*})\\
&\quad+(\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{W}}{(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})}^{*})\\

&\quad-(\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})((\partial_{k_{j}}(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}+(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}){\boldsymbol{W}}{(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})}^{*})\end{split}

since
(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}={\bf 0}
. Because
\partial_{k_{j}}
and
\partial_{x_{j}}
play symmetric roles, one also obtains:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{W}})(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{L}}}^{*}_{0}){\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{*}_{\alpha}&={(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{W}}){\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{*}_{\alpha})}^{*}\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}-(\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{(\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})}^{*})\\
&\quad-({\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}\partial_{x_{j}}(\omega_{\alpha}-{{\boldsymbol{L}}}^{*}_{0})+(\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{W}}(\omega_{\alpha}-{{\boldsymbol{L}}}^{*}_{0})){(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})}^{*}\,.\end{split}

Gathering these expressions, Eq. (
40
) yields:

(41)

\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}+\{\omega_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}\}+({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\omega_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}\\
-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\omega_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}+{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{{\boldsymbol{L}}}^{*}_{\alpha}={\bf 0}\,,

where
\{\omega_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}\}={\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\omega_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\omega_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}
and
\smash{{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\alpha}}=\smash{\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{1}}
.

Next consider the matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\alpha}=\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha}\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}-\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})
; then:

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\alpha}&=\partial_{k_{j}}[(\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}]-(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}-\partial_{x_{j}}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})\\
&=\partial_{k_{j}}[\cancel{\partial_{x_{j}}(\omega_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})}-\omega_{\alpha}\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}]-(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}-\partial_{x_{j}}[\cancel{\partial_{k_{j}}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})}-(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}]\\
&=-(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})-(\partial_{k_{j}}\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})-(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}+(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})\,.\end{split}

Letting (because again
(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}={\bf 0}

(42)

{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\alpha}={\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\omega_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{I}}\,,

Eq. (
41
) finally reads:

(43)

\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}+\{\omega_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}\}+({\boldsymbol{L}}_{\alpha}+{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}+{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{({\boldsymbol{L}}_{\alpha}+{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\alpha})}^{*}={\bf 0}\,.

Alternatively, one may wish to expand the Wigner measure
{\boldsymbol{W}}
of Eq. (
17
) onto the eigenvectors
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}
as:

{\boldsymbol{W}}=\sum_{\alpha}\delta(\omega+\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha}\delta(\omega+\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}{\bf w}_{\alpha}{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha}\,,

where
{\bf w}_{\alpha}={{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}
. The evolution properties of the so-called specific intensity, or coherence matrices
{\bf w}_{\alpha}
are derived multiplying Eq. (
43
) by
{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}
on the left-hand side, and by
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}
on the right-hand side. Firstly:

\begin{split}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}&=(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})[\partial_{x_{j}}{\bf w}_{\alpha}-(\partial_{x_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}-{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}]\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})[\partial_{x_{j}}{\bf w}_{\alpha}-(\partial_{x_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}{\bf w}_{\alpha}-{\bf w}_{\alpha}{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha}\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}]\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})[\partial_{x_{j}}{\bf w}_{\alpha}+{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}){\bf w}_{\alpha}+{\bf w}_{\alpha}(\partial_{x_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}]\end{split}

owing to the orthonormality condition
{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}
which yields
(\partial_{x_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\beta}=-{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\beta})
(and a similar property with
\partial_{k_{j}}

{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}=(\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})[\partial_{k_{j}}{\bf w}_{\alpha}+{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}){\bf w}_{\alpha}+{\bf w}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}]\,,

and consequently:

Secondly:

\begin{split}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}&=[\partial_{k_{j}}({{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})-(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}](\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}{\bf w}_{\alpha}\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha})({\boldsymbol{I}}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})[\partial_{x_{j}}({\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}]{\bf w}_{\alpha}\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha})({\boldsymbol{I}}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})[\partial_{x_{j}}(\omega_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}]{\bf w}_{\alpha}\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha})({\boldsymbol{I}}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}){\bf w}_{\alpha}\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha})(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}){\bf w}_{\alpha}\\
&=-{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}[\partial_{k_{j}}(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})](\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}){\bf w}_{\alpha}\,;\end{split}

and:

\begin{split}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{W}}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}&=(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})[\partial_{x_{j}}({{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha})-(\partial_{x_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}]{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}{\bf w}_{\alpha}\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{x_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha})({\boldsymbol{I}}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}{\bf w}_{\alpha}\\
&={\bf 0}\,.\end{split}

Thirdly:

\begin{split}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}\{\omega_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}\}-{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}[\partial_{k_{j}}(\omega_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})]\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}&={{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}[(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})-(\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})]\,.\end{split}

Letting:

(44)

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\alpha}&={{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\{\omega_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}\}+{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})\\
&={{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\omega_{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})-\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}\,,\end{split}

one can show that
{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\alpha}
is skew-symmetric owing to Eq. (
14

\begin{split}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}&={{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{0}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{x}})(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{M}({\boldsymbol{k}})){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}\\
&={{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{M}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}\\
&=(\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}\,.\end{split}

Then
(\partial_{x_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha})(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{M}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}+{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{M})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})=(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}
, or:

(45)

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}-{({{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{M})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}))}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{k_{j}}\omega_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}-{({{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0})(\partial_{x_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}))}^{*}\,;

besides:

(46)

\displaystyle(\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha}){{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}\partial_{k_{j}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{x_{j}}\omega_{\alpha})(\partial_{k_{j}}{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{*}_{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{c}}_{\alpha}\,;

thus one concludes with (
45
) and (
46
) that
{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\alpha}=-{{\boldsymbol{n}}}^{*}_{\alpha}
. Therefore, setting
\smash{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{\alpha}}=\smash{{{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{*}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\alpha}}

finally reads:

One can check in particular that for the eigenspace (

[

4.
Radiative transfer

In this section the radiative transfer equations (
23
) for a bianistropic dielectric medium are now derived. They describe the evolution of the high-frequency electromagnetic energy density accounting for random fluctuations of the optical response. We resort to the same formal pseudo-differential calculus as recalled in Sect.
3.1
. However additional rules are needed to account for the dependency of the optical response on both the slow scale of variation of the background medium, and the fast scale of variation of the random fluctuations. In Sect.
4.1
below we start by rewriting Maxwell’s system as a pseudo-differential operator when these random fluctuations are considered. In Sect.
4.2
we give the additional rules of pseudo-differential calculus for this situation, and we finally obtain in Sect.
4.3
the radiative transfer equations by a multiscale analysis.

5.
Summary and outlook

We have derived a system of coupled radiative transfer equations to describe the propagation of high-frequency electromagnetic waves in randomly fluctuating bianisotropic dielectric media including dispersive and dissipative effects. The fluctuations are weak and vary at the same length scales as the typical wavelength. We have used a Wigner functional approach and its interpretation in terms of semiclassical pseudo-differential operators to derive the transfer equations from a multiscale analysis. Radiative transfer equations have a geometrical interpretation in terms of bicharacteristic rays that is well suited for their numerical integration by ray methods and ray tracing solvers. Our results extend this approach to heterogeneous
bianisotropic
media or plasmas beyond the classical applications of ray tracing in homogeneous or piecewise homogeneous
isotropic
media. In future works one aims to derive the diffusion limit of the radiative transfer model, which describes the evolution of the energy density in physical space solely after the waves have travelled several scattering mean free paths and have lost the memory of their angular distribution at earlier times. The issue of deriving boundary conditions adapted to the Wigner distribution from the boundary conditions applied to the electromagnetic fields is also worth considering, for applications in radar imaging or remote sensing with scenes exhibiting sharp interfaces for example.

Likewise, one obtains:

\int_{S^{2}}\widehat{R}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{p}})\boldsymbol{X}({\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}})\boldsymbol{X}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}},{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}}){\mathrm{d}}\Omega(\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}})\\
=\pi\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\widehat{R}_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}+|{\boldsymbol{p}}|^{2}-2|{\boldsymbol{k}}||{\boldsymbol{p}}|\Theta}\right)(1+\Theta^{2}){\mathrm{d}}\Theta\right){\boldsymbol{I}}

provided that
{\boldsymbol{k}}\mapsto\smash{\widehat{R}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{k}})}
also depends on
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|
solely. Lastly:

\int_{S^{2}}\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon\mu}({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{p}})\boldsymbol{T}({\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}})\boldsymbol{X}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}},{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}})+\widehat{R}_{\epsilon\mu}({\boldsymbol{p}}-{\boldsymbol{k}})\boldsymbol{X}({\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}})\boldsymbol{T}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}},{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}})\right){\mathrm{d}}\Omega(\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}})\\
=4\pi\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\widehat{R}_{\epsilon\mu}\left(\sqrt{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}+|{\boldsymbol{p}}|^{2}-2|{\boldsymbol{k}}||{\boldsymbol{p}}|\Theta}\right)\Theta{\mathrm{d}}\Theta\right){\boldsymbol{I}}

provided that
{\boldsymbol{k}}\mapsto\smash{\widehat{R}_{\epsilon\mu}({\boldsymbol{k}})}
depends on
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|
solely. Now if the power spectral density functions
\smash{\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}}
,
\smash{\widehat{R}_{\mu}}
, and
\smash{\widehat{R}_{\epsilon\mu}}
are real, we see that the second integral (the Cauchy principal value) in Eq. (
73
) is real, proportional to the identity, and thus its contribution to the radiative transfer equation (
74
) vanishes. These conditions are equivalent to:

\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}(-{\boldsymbol{k}})\,,\quad\widehat{R}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\widehat{R}_{\mu}(-{\boldsymbol{k}})\,,\quad\widehat{R}_{\epsilon\mu}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\widehat{R}_{\epsilon\mu}(-{\boldsymbol{k}})\,,

which actually hold when these functions depend on
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|
solely. They also hold when the covariance functions
{\boldsymbol{y}}\mapsto\smash{R_{\epsilon}({\boldsymbol{y}})}
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}\mapsto\smash{R_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{y}})}
, and
{\boldsymbol{y}}\mapsto\smash{R_{\epsilon\mu}({\boldsymbol{y}})}
(see Eq. (
12
)) are even.

Gathering all these results, we conclude that the total scattering cross-sections
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\alpha}
of Eq. (
73
) are:

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\alpha}(|{\boldsymbol{k}}|)&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\delta(\omega_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{p}})-\omega_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\alpha\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{p}}):{\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{p}}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\delta(c_{0}|{\boldsymbol{p}}|-c_{0}|{\boldsymbol{k}}|)\left(\int_{S^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\alpha\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{p}}):{\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}{\mathrm{d}}\Omega(\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}})\right)|{\boldsymbol{p}}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}|{\boldsymbol{p}}|\\
&=\frac{\pi^{2}c_{0}|{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{4}}{4}\Bigg(\int_{-1}^{1}\Big[(1+\Theta^{2})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}\left(|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\sqrt{2(1-\Theta)}\right)+\widehat{R}_{\mu}\left(|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\sqrt{2(1-\Theta)}\right)\right)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+4\Theta\widehat{R}_{\epsilon\mu}\left(|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\sqrt{2(1-\Theta)}\right)\Big]{\mathrm{d}}\Theta\Bigg){\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}\,,\end{split}

ignoring the contribution from the Cauchy principal value. Indeed the latter reads:

\oint\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\alpha\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{p}}):{\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}}{\omega_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{p}})-\omega_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})}{\mathrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{p}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\varepsilon}^{+\infty}{\mathrm{d}}r\int_{\left||{\boldsymbol{p}}|-|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\right|=r}\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\alpha\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{p}}):{\boldsymbol{I}}_{A}}{c_{0}(|{\boldsymbol{p}}|-|{\boldsymbol{k}}|)}|{\boldsymbol{p}}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\Omega(\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}})

that is, integrals on spheres which are real and proportional to the identity. Once multiplied by
\mathrm{i}
, they do not contribute anymore to the radiative transfer equation (
74
) as already stated above.
