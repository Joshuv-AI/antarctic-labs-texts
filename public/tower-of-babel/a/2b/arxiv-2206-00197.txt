Equilibria and energy minimizers for an interaction model on the hyperbolic space
Razvan C. Fetecau, Hansol Park

Abstract
We study an intrinsic model for collective behaviour on the hyperbolic space ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} . We investigate the equilibria of the aggregation equation (or equivalently, the critical points of the associated interaction energy) for interaction potentials that include Newtonian repulsion. By using the method of moving planes, we establish the radial symmetry and the monotonicity of equilibria supported on geodesic balls of ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} . We find several explicit forms of equilibria and show that one such equilibrium is a global energy minimizer. We also consider more general potentials and utilize a technique used for ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} to establish the existence of compactly supported global minimizers. Numerical simulations are presented, suggesting that some of the equilibria studied here are global attractors. The key tool in our investigations is a family of isometries of ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} that we have developed for this purpose.



Keywords
: swarming on manifolds, hyperbolic space, energy minimizers, Newtonian potential

Introduction

In recent years there has been extensive work on investigating minimizers of nonlocal interaction energies, as motivated by a wide range of applications in a variety of disciplines, such as biology, physics, economics and social sciences
[
7
,
16
,
39
,
2
,
10
,
24
]
. With very few exceptions, such works have studied the Euclidean case, where individual particles in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
interact via an interaction potential that depends on the Euclidean distance between them. Since our goal in this paper is to consider nonlocal interaction energies set up on Riemannian manifolds (in particular, on the hyperbolic space), we will present the general setup from the start.

Consider an
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
M
, and denote by
\mathcal{P}(M)
the set of probability measures on
M
. Define the
interaction energy

E:\mathcal{P}(M)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by

E[\rho]=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{M\times M}K(x,y)\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}\rho(y),

(1)

where
K:M\times M\to\mathbb{R}
is an
interaction potential
that models attractive and repulsive interactions. More precisely, the negative manifold gradient
-\nabla_{M}K(x,y)
(the gradient with respect to
x
) of
K
provides the interaction force that a point mass located at
x
feels by interacting with a point mass located at
y

[
7
,
23
]
.

The interaction energy (
1
) is closely related to the following evolution equation for a population density
\rho
:

\partial_{t}\rho-\nabla_{M}\cdot(\rho\nabla_{M}K\ast\rho)=0,

(2)

where
\nabla_{M}\cdot
denotes the manifold divergence on
M
. Here, for a time-dependent measure
\rho_{t}
on
M
, the convolution
K\ast\rho_{t}
is defined by

K*\rho_{t}(x)=\int_{M}K(x,y)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(y).

(3)

Equation (
2
) is in the form of an active transport equation for the density
\rho
, with a nonlocal velocity field given by

\mathbf{v}[\rho](x,t)=-\nabla_{M}K*\rho_{t}(x).

(4)

Note that
\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}[\rho]=-\nabla_{M}\frac{\delta E[\rho]}{\delta\rho}}
, which sets (at least formally) equation (
2
) as the gradient flow of the energy (
1
). One can check by a direct calculation that indeed, formally, the energy
E
decays in time along solutions of equation (
2
)
[
23
]
. Equilibria (steady states) of the aggregation model (
2
) are the critical points of the interaction energy, and from a dynamical point of view, we expect that solutions to model (
2
) approach asymptotically as
t\to\infty
, local minimizers of the energy.

There has been extensive literature in recent years on interaction energies of type (
1
), with the vast majority of the works focusing on the case
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. One approach is to use the techniques developed in
[
1
]
and formulate the gradient flow of the energy
E
on the space of probability measures with finite second (or other order) moments, equipped with the
2
-Wasserstein metric
[
13
,
9
,
41
]
. Another approach is to use direct methods of the calculus of variations and establish the existence of global minimizers (ground states) of the interaction energy
[
16
,
39
,
24
]
. There are also various studies of quantitative and qualitative properties of minimizers, such as their regularity
[
8
]
and compactness and dimensionality of their support
[
7
,
2
,
10
]
.

Model (
2
) can exhibit a very diverse range of swarming or self-organized behaviour. We refer to
[
33
,
40
,
21
,
3
,
12
]
for explicit calculations and numerical illustrations of equilibria for the model in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which include aggregations on disks, annuli, rings, and soccer balls. The model on manifolds is far less investigated, but nevertheless, a similarly diverse set of equilibria was demonstrated in
[
23
]
for the hyperbolic plane and also, interesting aggregation patterns were shown in
[
19
]
for the model set up on the rotation group
SO(3)
. Applications of model (
2
) are numerous, e.g., to flocking and swarming of biological organisms
[
11
]
, material science and granular media
[
13
]
, self-assembly of nanoparticles
[
31
]
, robotics
[
25
,
32
]
and opinion formation
[
35
]
.

In our study we will follow the
intrinsic
approach pursued in various recent papers
[
23
,
19
,
22
]
and assume that
K(x,y)
only depends on the
geodesic
distance
d(x,y)
between the two points. By an abuse of notation we write
K(x,y)=K(d(x,y))
. In contrast, when
M
comes with a canonical embedding in a larger Euclidean space (e.g., a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
),
K(x,y)
can be assumed to depend on the
Euclidean
distance
|x-y|
in the ambient space between
x
and
y
; we refer to this case as the
extrinsic
approach. The two approaches yield very different models (unless
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, in which case the two models are the same). For extrinsic models we refer to
[
41
,
36
]
for studies on well-posedness by gradient flow techniques, and to various works on the emergent behaviours of the extrinsic models on manifolds, e.g., on sphere
[
15
]
, unitary matrices
[
34
,
30
,
29
]
, hyperbolic space
[
27
]
, and Stiefel manifolds
[
28
]
. Relevant to the present paper, we note the work in
[
27
]
, where the authors study synchronization behaviour on the hyperbolic space with an extrinsic quadratic potential (i.e.,
K(x,y)=|x-y|^{2}
) for the discrete analogue of (
2
). A related study is done in
[
37
]
. The emergence of self-synchronization has been widely studied in the literature, due to its numerous occurrences in biological, physical and chemical systems (e.g., flashing of fireflies, neuronal synchronization in the brain, quantum synchronization) – see
[
34
,
27
]
and references therein.

For intrinsic models, the sign of
K^{\prime}
determines whether the interactions are repulsive or attractive in nature. Indeed, provided
x
and
y
are not in the cut locus of each other, by chain rule one gets

-\nabla_{M}K(d(x,y))=K^{\prime}(d(x,y))\frac{\log_{x}y}{d(x,y)},

where
\log_{x}y
denotes the Riemannian logarithm map (i.e., the inverse of the Riemannian exponential map) on
M
. Therefore, by interacting with the point mass at
y
, the point mass at
x
is driven by a force of magnitude proportional to
|K^{\prime}(d(x,y))|
, to move either towards
y
(if
K^{\prime}(d(x,y))>0
) or away from
y
(if
K^{\prime}(d(x,y))<0
). To obtain non-trivial swarming behaviours, repulsion and attraction must balance each other. Typically, such interaction potentials have short-range repulsion and long-range attraction, i.e.,
K^{\prime}(r)<0
for small
r
and
K^{\prime}(r)>0
for large
r
(see Figure
2
).

The interaction energy for intrinsic models is invariant under isometries. Indeed, if
f:M\to M
is an isometry of
M
and
f_{\#}\rho
is the push-forward by
f
of the measure
\rho
, then it is immediate to show that

E[f_{\#}\rho]=E[\rho].

(5)

Consequently, the energy minimizers can only be expected to be unique up to isometries.

In this paper we are exclusively concerned with studying the nonlocal aggregation model on the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, represented as a one-sheeted hyperboloid in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
endowed with the Lorentzian inner product. Based on the Lorentz transform, we construct a family of isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and a binary operation on
\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}
, along with a concept of coordinates on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, that are the key tools in the results we establish in the present work.

In Section
3
we consider attractive-repulsive interaction potentials that include Newtonian repulsion, i.e., potentials for which the repulsion component is given by the Green’s function of the negative Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. We note here that the Green’s function on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
has an explicit expression
[
17
]
– see (
28
). For the model on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, potentials in this form have been given extensive attention in recent years
[
6
,
21
,
20
,
12
,
38
]
, and a potential with Newtonian repulsion was also considered on the hyperbolic plane
[
23
]
. In this section we only consider equilibria that are absolutely continuous with respect to the canonical volume measure on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. This choice is motivated by results in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which have established that for interaction potentials with Newtonian repulsion, the local minimizers of the interaction energy are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebegue measure
[
12
]
.

We study equilibria supported in geodesic balls of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and show that such equilibria must be radially symmetric and monotone with respect to the centre of the ball. To show this result (Theorem
3.3
) we use the method of moving planes, a well-known method for studying qualitative properties of positive solutions of elliptic equations
[
26
]
. More recently, the method found applications in integral equations
[
14
,
20
]
. The challenge here is to adapt the method to the hyperboloid; to the best of our knowledge this the first application of the method of moving planes to integral equations on manifolds. We also find several explicit forms of radially symmetric equilibria, corresponding to particular choices of the attractive part of the potential. For one such attractive potential, we show that the equilibrium we computed is in fact the global energy minimizer. We also present various numerical simulations in Section
5
.

In Section
4
we investigate the existence of compactly supported global minimizers of the energy (
1
) for
M=\mathbb{H}^{n}
. As we apply and follow closely the technique used by Cañizo
et al.

[
7
]
for the Euclidean case, we only highlight the main differences from their work. In other words, we are not aiming for a self-contained presentation in Section
4
, as some of results from
[
7
]
would transfer with no modifications to
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Nevertheless, we show how the family of isometries and the concept of coordinates on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
that we developed, enable a similar approach as for the Euclidean case. In particular, minimizers cannot have too large gaps, as otherwise, an isometric displacement of one of the components would decrease the energy (Lemma
4.3
).

Finally, we provide some numerical results in Section
5
. Using various interaction potentials, we illustrate the dynamical evolution to steady states of radially symmetric solutions to (
2
). The equilibria we present are qualitatively different in terms in their monotonicity, in consistency with the analytical findings. Numerics suggest these equilibria are global attractors for the dynamics (at least for radial initial densities).

The summary of the paper is the following. Section
2
provides key background needed for the paper, on the variational formalism and on the geometrical properties of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Section
3
contains analytical results for interaction potentials that contain Newtonian repulsion (characterization of monotonicity, explicit forms for equilibria, convexity of energy). In Section
4
we establish the existence of compactly supported global minimizers, following the approach for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
from
[
7
]
. Proof of various lemmas are deferred to the Appendix.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we provide some background on the variational formalism used in our paper, as well as on the various geometrical properties of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, in particular on a family of isometries of the hyperbolic space that plays a key role in our studies.

2.1
Variational approach: Euler-Lagrange equations

For the interaction energy set up on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a well-established approach to characterize its local minimizers (by Euler-Lagrange equations) in the topology of transport distances
[
2
]
. As this approach extends immediately to arbitrary manifolds, we will simply list below the main notations and results.

Take
p
to be a fixed point in
M
, and denote by
\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)
the set of probability measures that have finite moments of order
2
(with respect to
p
), i.e.,

\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(M):\int_{M}d^{2}(x,p)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty\right\}.

The
2
-Wasserstein distance between two measures
\mu,\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)
is given by:

d_{2}^{2}(\mu,\sigma)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\sigma)}\iint_{M\times M}d^{2}(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y),

where
\Pi(\mu,\sigma)\subset\mathcal{P}(M\times M)
is the set of transport plans between
\mu
and
\sigma
, i.e., the set of probability measures on the product space
M\times M
with first and second marginals
\mu
and
\sigma
, respectively.

A probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)
is a local minimizer of
E
with respect to the
d_{2}
-distance provided there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
E[\sigma]\geq E[\mu]
, for all
\sigma\in B(\mu,\epsilon)
, where
B(\mu,\epsilon)
denotes the open ball (in
d_{2}
) centred at
\mu
and of radius
\epsilon
.

We will pay particular attention in this paper to probability measures that are absolutely continuous with respect to the canonical volume measure
\mathrm{d}x
; we denote this space by
\mathcal{P}^{ac}(M)
.
Throughout the paper, we will refer to an absolutely continuous measure directly by its density
\rho
, and by abuse of notation write
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}(M)
to mean
\mathrm{d}\rho(x)=\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}(M)
. Finally, we denote by
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{c}(M)
the space of absolutely continuous probability measures with compact support in
M
.

The following result, stated originally for
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, extends trivially to arbitrary Riemannian manifolds.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
2
]
, Theorem 4 and Remark 4)
.

Assume the interaction potential
K
is bounded from below and lower semicontinuous. Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}(M)\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)
be a local minimizer of
E
with respect to
d_{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda,\qquad\text{ for a.e. }x\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho),

(6a)

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda,\qquad\text{ for a.e. on }M\setminus\mathrm{supp}(\rho),

(6b)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda(x):=\int_{M}K(x,y)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y,

(7)

and
\lambda=2E[\rho]
is a constant.

Condition (
6a
) is shown by taking perturbations of
\rho
supported in
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
. The condition is equivalent to
\rho
being a critical point of
E
, at which the first variation of the energy vanishes. The constant
\lambda
, which plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier in the derivation of (
6a
), has a physical interpretation
[
4
]
: it represents the energy per unit mass felt by a point mass at position
x
due to interaction with all points in
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
.

We also note that critical points of the interaction energy represent equilibrium solutions (steady states) of the aggregation model (
2
). This can be inferred from the fact that the velocity
\mathbf{v}(x)=-\nabla_{M}K\ast\rho(x)=-\nabla_{M}\Lambda(x)
vanishes for
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
, given that
\Lambda
is constant in
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
. We will use these concepts (critical point of the energy, equilibrium solution, steady state) interchangeably in the sequel.

The necessary condition (
6b
) is found by taking perturbations of
\rho
that can be supported anywhere in
M
, in particular outside
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
. The interpretation of (
6b
) is that transporting mass from the support of
\rho
into its complement increases the total energy (hence
\rho
is a local minimizer, being in a favourably energetic state).

2.2
Geometric properties of the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}

In this part, we present some geometric properties of the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
which will be used in the paper.

2.2.1
Hyperboloid model, polar coordinates and notations

We consider the space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
endowed with the (negative) Lorentzian inner product:

\langle x,y\rangle=x_{0}y_{0}-x_{1}y_{1}-\cdots-x_{n}y_{n},

(8)

where
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
y=(y_{0},y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The inner product (
8
) induces the (complex-valued) norm defined by

\|x\|=\langle x,x\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

(9)

We use the hyperboloid model of the hyperbolic space and take:

\mathbb{H}^{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:-x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}=-1,x_{0}>0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1},

or, written differently,

\mathbb{H}^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x^{\top}\eta x=-1,x_{0}>0\},

where
\eta=\mathrm{diag}(-1,\underbrace{1,1,\cdots,1}_{n-\text{times}})
is a matrix of size
(n+1)\times(n+1)
.

The geodesic distance
d(x,y)
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
between two points
x
and
y
, is given by:

d(x,y)=\cosh^{-1}(\langle x,y\rangle)=\cosh^{-1}(x_{0}y_{0}-x_{1}y_{1}-\cdots-x_{n}y_{n}).

(10)

For
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
R>0
, we denote by
B_{R}(x)
the open geodesic ball in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
centred at
x
of radius
R
, i.e.,

B_{R}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:d(x,y)<R\}.

We use the hyperbolic polar coordinates on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, given by:

\displaystyle(\theta,\xi)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\mapsto(\cosh\theta,\sinh\theta\,\xi)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1},

(11)

where
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
denotes the
(n-1)
-dimensional unit sphere embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We denote by
v
the vertex of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, corresponding to
\theta=0
, i.e.,
v=(1,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The vertex
v
plays the role of the origin in the Euclidean space.

In polar coordinates, the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}f(\theta,\xi)&=\sinh^{1-n}\theta\,\partial_{\theta}\left(\sinh^{n-1}\theta\partial_{\theta}f\right)+\sinh^{-2}\theta\Delta_{\xi}f\\
&=\partial_{\theta\theta}f+(n-1)\coth\theta\,\partial_{\theta}f+\sinh^{-2}\theta\Delta_{\xi}f,\end{aligned}

(12)

where
\Delta_{\xi}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, with respect to the variable
\xi
. Also, the canonical volume form on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
in these coordinates is given by

\mathrm{d}x=\sinh^{n-1}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\xi}.

(13)

We denote by
\alpha(n)
the volume of the
n
-dimensional unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; consequently,
n\alpha(n)
is the surface area of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Finally, we denote the standard Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
|\cdot|
, i.e.,

|w|=\left(w_{1}^{2}+\cdots+w_{n}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\text{ for }w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

2.2.2
Lorentz transform

For a fixed vector
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|w|<1
, consider the following matrix
B(w)
of size
(n+1)\times(n+1)
with indices
0,1,\dots,n
:

\displaystyle B(w)_{00}=\gamma,\quad B(w)_{i0}=B(w)_{0i}=-\gamma w_{i},\quad B(w)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+(\gamma-1)\frac{w_{i}w_{j}}{|w|^{2}},\quad\forall i,j=1,2,\cdots,n,

(14)

where
\gamma=(1-|w|^{2})^{-1/2}
. Equivalently, the matrix expression of
B(w)
is

B(w)=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma&-\gamma w^{\top}\\
-\gamma w&I_{n}+(\gamma-1)\frac{ww^{\top}}{|w|^{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

We denote this matrix by
B
, since the corresponding concept is the Lorentz boost.

Lemma 2.2
.

For a fixed
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|w|<1
,
B(w)
maps
\mathbb{H}^{n}
into
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, i.e.,

y=B(w)x\in\mathbb{H}^{n},\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

Here,
y
is the matrix product of
B(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Proof.

By direct calculations (see Appendix
A
for details) one can show:

y_{0}^{2}-y_{1}^{2}-\cdots-y_{n}^{2}=x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{n}^{2},

which implies the desired result.
∎

From the proof of Lemma
2.2
, for any fixed
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|w|<1
, it holds that:

x^{\top}\eta x=(B(w)x)^{\top}\eta B(w)x=x^{\top}(B(w)^{\top}\eta B(w))x,\qquad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

This yields

x^{\top}(\eta-B(w)^{\top}\eta B(w))x=0,\qquad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1},

and hence,

\displaystyle B(w)^{\top}\eta B(w)=\eta.

(15)

Identity (
15
) holds for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|w|<1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For a fixed
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|w|<1
, the map
F_{w}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
defined by

F_{w}(x):=B(w)x,

(16)

is an isometry. In particular,
F_{0}
is the identity map on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

Proof.

The fact that
F_{0}
is the identity map follows immediately from
B(0)=\text{Id}
. The general result follows from a simple calculation that uses (
15
); see Appendix
A
for details.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|w|<1
. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

F_{w}(1,0,\cdots,0)=(x_{0},-x_{1},\cdots,-x_{n})
,

2.

F_{w}(x)=(1,0,\cdots,0)
,

3.

w_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{x_{0}}
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Proof.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.

2.2.3
Translation on
\mathbb{H}^{n}

In this part, we define the translation on the hyperbolic space using the isometry
F_{w}
defined in (

). For any
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we define a

\hat{x}:=\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{0}},\cdots,\frac{x_{n}}{x_{0}}\right).

From the definition of
\hat{x}

F_{\hat{x}}(x)=v\qquad\text{ and }\qquad F_{\hat{x}}(v)=(x_{0},-x_{1},\cdots,-x_{n}).

Definition 2.1
.

We define a binary operation
+^{\prime}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
as follows:

x+^{\prime}y:=F_{-\hat{y}}(x)\in\mathbb{H}^{n},\qquad\forall~x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.

Since

is an isometry for all

, we know that the map

is also an isometry for all

. The following lemma provides the coordinate expression of

.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(x+^{\prime}y)_{0}=x_{0}y_{0}+x_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}y_{n},\\
&(x+^{\prime}y)_{j}=x_{0}y_{j}+x_{j}+\frac{y_{j}}{y_{0}+1}(x_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}y_{n}),\qquad\forall~1\leq j\leq n.\end{aligned}

(17)

Proof.

The proof follows from direct calculations, see Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

We can also express
(x+^{\prime}y)_{j}
for any
1\leq j\leq n
, as

\displaystyle(x+^{\prime}y)_{j}=x_{0}y_{j}+x_{j}+\frac{y_{j}}{y_{0}+1}((x+^{\prime}y)_{0}-x_{0}y_{0})=x_{j}+\frac{y_{j}}{y_{0}+1}((x+^{\prime}y)_{0}+x_{0}).

(18)

We investigate now the algebraic structure of the operator
+^{\prime}
.

(1) Identity of
+^{\prime}
.
Recall that
v=(1,\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{n-\text{times}})
denotes the vertex of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Since
\hat{v}
is the
n
-dimensional zero vector, we can easily find that

x+^{\prime}v=F_{0}(x)=x,

since
F_{0}
is the identity map on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Now, we calculate
v+^{\prime}x
. From the definition of
+^{\prime}
, we have

(v+^{\prime}x)_{0}=v_{0}x_{0}+v_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+v_{n}x_{n}=x_{0}

and

(v+^{\prime}x)_{j}=v_{0}x_{j}+v_{j}+\frac{x_{j}}{x_{0}+1}(v_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+v_{n}x_{n})=x_{j},\qquad\text{ for }j=1,\dots,n.

This implies that

v+^{\prime}x=x.

We conclude that
v
is an identity element for
+^{\prime}
. The uniqueness of identities can be shown easily, so
v
is the unique identity element of
+^{\prime}
.

(2) Inverse of
+^{\prime}
.
We want to find the inverse
-x
of
x
, which satisfies

x+^{\prime}(-x)=(-x)+^{\prime}x=v.

For
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
, set:

-x:=(x_{0},-x_{1},-x_{2},\cdots,-x_{n}).

(19)

It is easy to check that
-x
defined above is indeed the inverse of
x
. In polar coordinates, if
(\theta,\xi)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
are the coordinates of
x
, then
-x
has coordinates
(\theta,-\xi)
. So one can see that the inverse operator is quite natural. We will prove the uniqueness of the inverse later (see Corollary
2.9
).

Using the definition (
19
) of the inverse, we define the operation
-^{\prime}
by

x-^{\prime}y:=x+^{\prime}(-y),\qquad\forall~x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.

(20)

We have the following simple lemma which will be used in further calculations.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
x
and
y
be two points on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Then we have the following relation:

-(x+^{\prime}y)=(-x)+^{\prime}(-y).

Proof.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.
∎

(3) Non-commutativity of
+^{\prime}
.
Using (
17
), we can show easily that
+^{\prime}
is not commutative.

(4) Non-associativity of
+^{\prime}
.
By direct (but tedious) calculations, one can check that in general,
((x+^{\prime}y)+^{\prime}z)_{0}\neq(x+^{\prime}(y+^{\prime}z))_{0}
. Hence, the operation
+^{\prime}
is not associative.

To prove the uniqueness of the inverse, we provide the following lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we have

x=(x+^{\prime}y)-^{\prime}y.

Proof.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.
∎

From the previous lemma, we have the uniqueness of the inverse for
+^{\prime}
.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
x,y,z\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
that satisfy

x+^{\prime}y=v,\qquad\text{and}\qquad x+^{\prime}z=v.

Then, we have
y=z
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.8
, we have

(x+^{\prime}y)-^{\prime}y=x,\qquad(x+^{\prime}z)-^{\prime}z=x.

If we use
x+^{\prime}y=x+^{\prime}z=v
, we get

v-^{\prime}y=x,\qquad v-^{\prime}z=x.

This relation directly yields
y=z
.
∎

Note that one can express the geodesic distance on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
using
-^{\prime}
. Let
x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
,

and

Interaction potentials with Newtonian repulsion

In this section, we consider interaction potentials that include Newtonian repulsion, i.e., potentials for which the repulsion component is given by the Green’s function of the negative Laplacian on

\displaystyle\Phi(\theta):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left(\tanh\frac{\theta}{2}\right)&\text{ for }n=2,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\int^{\infty}_{\theta}\frac{1}{\sinh^{n-1}\zeta}\mathrm{d}\zeta\qquad&\text{ for }n\geq 3.\end{cases}

Remark 3.1
.

Similar to the Euclidean case, the energy (

\displaystyle\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\|\nabla u(x)\|_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u)(x)u(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where

satisfies (in the weak sense):
\displaystyle{-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u=\rho}
. Further, using the fundamental solution, write
\displaystyle{u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}G(x,y)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y}

\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})}^{2}=\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}G(x,y)\rho(x)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

(32)

Regarding the Euler-Lagrange equations, for an interaction potential in the form (

\Lambda(x)=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left(G(x,y)+A(d(x,y)\right)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y.

\Delta\Lambda(x)=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &-\rho(x)+\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\Delta_{x}A(d(x,y))\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y\qquad\text{ for }x\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho),\\
&\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\Delta_{x}A(d(x,y))\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y\hskip 66.86414pt\text{ for }x\notin\mathrm{supp}(\rho).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

3.1

We consider equilibria of the aggregation model (

Before we present the main result, we introduce the reflection on the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}

\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\mapsto(2a-x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}).

\displaystyle(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\mapsto(x_{1}-a,x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\mapsto(a-x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\mapsto(2a-x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}).

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(v)\cap\Sigma_{a}}F(d(x,y))\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{B_{R}(v^{a})\cap\Sigma_{a}}F(d(x^{a},y))\rho_{a}(y)\mathrm{d}y,

x=(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{0},y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
. Since
x,y\in\Sigma_{0}
, we have
x_{1},y_{1}\geq 0
. This yields that

\cosh\left(d(x,y)\right)=x_{0}y_{0}-x_{1}y_{1}-\cdots-x_{n}y_{n}\leq x_{0}y_{0}+x_{1}y_{1}-\cdots-x_{n}y_{n}=\cosh\left(d(x^{0},y)\right),

which implies the desired result.

Consider now the following two cases.

Case (i):
F
is decreasing.
Since
F
is decreasing and (
45
) holds, we have

F(d(x,y))-F(d(x^{a},y))\geq 0,\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma_{a}\cap B_{R}(v^{a})\text{ and }y\in\Sigma_{a}.

(46)

We will show that

\rho_{a}\leq\rho,\qquad\text{ on }\Sigma_{a},

for all
a\in(-R,0)
, which implies that
\rho
is monotonically decreasing about the vertex, in the
x_{1}
direction.

Assume first that there exists
a_{0}\in(-R,0)
such that

\rho_{a_{0}}(x)\leq\rho(x),\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma_{a_{0}},

(47)

and show that
a_{0}
can be extended to the origin. Indeed, suppose by contradiction that (
47
) holds, but
a_{0}
cannot be extended, and take
a\in(a_{0},a_{0}+\epsilon)
, with
\epsilon>0
. Define

\Sigma_{a}^{-}=\{x\in\Sigma_{a}:\rho(x)<\rho_{a}(x)\}.

Then, from (
44
) and (
46
), for any
x\in\Sigma_{a}^{-}
, we have:

0<\rho_{a}(x)-\rho(x)\leq\int_{\Sigma_{a}^{-}}(F(d(x,y))-F(d(x^{a},y)))(\rho_{a}(y)-\rho(y))\mathrm{d}y.

Therefore,

\|\rho_{a}-\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{a}^{-})}\leq\|\rho_{a}-\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{a}^{-})}\sup_{x\in\Sigma_{a}^{-}}\int_{\Sigma_{a}^{-}}(F(d(x,y))-F(d(x^{a},y)))\mathrm{d}y.

(48)

The function under the integral on the right-hand-side is locally integrable. Also, by (
44
),
it holds that
\rho(x)>\rho_{a_{0}}(x)
in the interior of
\Sigma_{a_{0}}
, implying that the
closure of
\Sigma_{a_{0}}^{-}
has measure
0
. As
\lim_{a\to a_{0}}\Sigma_{a}^{-}\subset\overline{\Sigma_{a_{0}}^{-}}
, we infer that the measure of
\Sigma_{a}^{-}
approaches

0
as
a\to a_{0}
. Therefore, we can choose
\epsilon
small enough such that

\sup_{x\in\Sigma_{a}^{-}}\int_{\Sigma_{a}^{-}}(F(d(x,y))-F(d(x^{a},y)))\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{1}{2}.

From (
48
) we then infer that
\|\rho_{a}-\rho\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Sigma_{a}^{-})}
=  0,
which implies that
\Sigma_{a}^{-}
is empty. Consequently,
a_{0}
is not maximal and we reached a contradiction.

To show that there exists
a_{0}\in(-R,0)
with the property (
47
), note
that (
47
) holds for
a_{0}=-R
. Then, by an argument entirely similar to the one made in the previous step, one can infer that the plane
a_{0}=-R
can be moved further to the right, while (
47
) still holds.

A similar argument can be made using hypersufaces with
a>0
. Finally, since the direction
x_{1}
can be chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that
\rho
is radially symmetric and decreasing about the vertex
v
. This concludes the proof for case (i).

Case (ii):
F
is increasing.
From a similar argument as for case (i), one can show that
\rho
is radially symmetric and increasing about the vertex
v
.
∎

We now turn the attention on radially symmetric equilibria that are local minimizers of the energy (see Lemma
2.1
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
K
be an interaction potential in the form (
31
), and let
\rho
be a bounded, radially symmetric equilibrium solution of model (
2
) that is supported in the geodesic ball
B_{R}(v)
, for some
R>0
.
Assume that
A:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\Delta_{x}A(d(x,y))\geq 0
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Then
\rho
satisfies the necessary condition (
6
) for being a local energy minimizer.

Proof.

Since
\rho
is an equilibrium solution, it satisfies the first equation of (
6
). It remains to show (
6b
). By using (
34
), we write:

\displaystyle\Delta\Lambda(x)=\begin{cases}0\qquad&\text{if }x\in B_{R}(v),\\[3.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\Delta_{x}A(d(x,y))\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y,\qquad&\text{if }x\not\in B_{R}(v).\end{cases}

(49)

As
K
and
\rho
are radially symmetric,
\Lambda
has radial symmetry as well. Hence, we can define
\tilde{\Lambda}:[0,\infty)\to R
by:

\tilde{\Lambda}(r):=\Lambda(x),

(50)

for some
x\in\partial B_{r}(v)
. In particular, by (
6a
),
\tilde{\Lambda}(r)=\lambda
for
0\leq r\leq R
.

For
r>R
, use the divergence theorem in
B_{r}(v)
to obtain that

\int_{B_{r}(v)}\Delta\Lambda(x)dx=\int_{\partial B_{r}(v)}\nabla\Lambda(x)\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{n}=|\partial B_{r}(v)|\tilde{\Lambda}^{\prime}(r).

(51)

From
\Delta_{x}A(d(x,y))\geq 0
and (
49
), it follows that
\Delta\Lambda(x)\geq 0
for all
x
. Consequently, using the equation above, we infer that
\tilde{\Lambda}
is a non-decreasing function, and conclude that

\begin{cases}\tilde{\Lambda}(r)=\lambda\qquad\text{if }0\leq r\leq R,\\[3.0pt]
\tilde{\Lambda}(r)\geq\lambda\qquad\text{if }r>R.\end{cases}

This shows that the necessary condition (
6b
) for a local minimizer is also satisfied.
∎

The following proposition shows that for potentials with
\Delta A>0
, radially symmetric local minimizers must have a geodesic ball as their support.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
K
be an interaction potential in the form (
31
), where
A:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\Delta_{x}A(d(x,y))>0
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. If
\rho
is a bounded, radially symmetric local minimizer of the interaction energy, then there exists
R>0
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)=B_{R}(v)
.

Proof.

As
\rho
is bounded and radially symmetric, its support can be expressed as

\mathrm{supp}(\rho)=\bigcup_{r\in C}\partial B_{r}(v),

where
C\subset[0,\infty)
is a compact set.

First note that
\Lambda
is radially symmetric (see (
50
)) and
\tilde{\Lambda}(r)=\lambda
for
r\in C
. Also, from

\Delta\Lambda(x)=\begin{cases}0\qquad&\text{if }x\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho),\\[3.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\Delta_{x}A(d(x,y))\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y>0\qquad&\text{if }x\not\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho),\end{cases}

and (
51
), we find that:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}^{\prime}(r)>0\qquad\text{if }r\not\in C.

(52)

Since
C
is a compact set in
[0,\infty)
, we can express
C
as a union of disjoint closed intervals as follows:

C=[a_{1},b_{1}]\cup[a_{2},b_{2}]\cup\cdots\cup[a_{k},b_{k}],

where
0\leq a_{1}<b_{1}<a_{2}<b_{2}<\cdots<a_{k}<b_{k}
. If
k\geq 2
, then

\tilde{\Lambda}(b_{1})=\tilde{\Lambda}(a_{2})\;(=\lambda).

However, this contradicts (
52
), so we conclude that
k=1
. Now assume that
0<a_{1}
. From (
52
), we have

\tilde{\Lambda}(0)<\tilde{\Lambda}(a_{1}),

but this cannot occur for a local minimizer (see (
6b
)).

We conclude that
a_{1}=0
, and
C=[0,b_{1}]
. By setting
R=b_{1}
, we then have

\mathrm{supp}(\rho)=\bigcup_{r\in[0,R]}\partial B_{r}(v)=B_{R}(v).

∎

3.2
Explicit forms of radially symmetric equilibria

We first find some explicit expressions of equilibria for certain interaction potentials in the form (
31
). These equilibria are radially symmetric and with
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)=D_{R}(v)
,
R>0
.

Denote

\tilde{\rho}(d(v,x)):=\rho(x).

(53)

Then, the integral equation (
35
) reads:

\tilde{\rho}(\theta_{x})=\int_{0}^{R}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\left(A^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{xy})+(n-1)\coth(\theta_{xy})A^{\prime}(\theta_{xy})\right)\tilde{\rho}(\theta_{y})\sinh^{n-1}\theta_{y}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{y}\mathrm{d}\theta_{y},

(54)

that holds for all
x\in D_{R}(v)
, where
\theta_{x}:=d(v,x)
and
\theta_{xy}:=d(x,y)
. Note that we used (
12
) to write:

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}A(d(x,y))=A^{\prime\prime}(d(x,y))+(n-1)\coth(d(x,y))A^{\prime}(d(x,y)).

(55)

The two main specific examples of attractive potentials investigated here are:

(i) Constant Laplacian attraction:

\displaystyle A_{c}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{\int_{0}^{\theta_{*}}\sinh^{n-1}\xi\mathrm{d}\xi}{\sinh^{n-1}\theta_{*}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{*}.

(56)

This is a very important example for applications;
A_{c}
has the property that

\Delta_{x}A_{c}(d(x,y))=1,

(57)

and leads to equilibria that are constant within their support.

(ii) Hyperbolic-cosine attraction:

\displaystyle A_{h}(\theta)=\frac{\cosh\theta-1}{n}.

(58)

This potential satisfies (use (
55
)):

\Delta_{x}A_{h}(d(x,y))=\cosh(d(x,y)).

We will show that the energy functional in this case is strictly convex, and we will find explicitly the global minimizer.

Constant Laplacian attraction.

Consider the interaction potential (
31
) with attraction given by (
56
). Since
A_{c}
satisfies (
57
), it is immediate to check that the following density distribution is a solution of (
54
):

\displaystyle\rho_{c}(x)=\begin{cases}1\qquad\text{ if }d(v,x)\leq R_{c},\\[2.0pt]
0\qquad\text{ if }d(v,x)>R_{c},\end{cases}

(59)

where
R_{c}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(D_{R_{c}}(v))=n\alpha(n)\int_{0}^{R_{c}}\sinh^{n-1}\theta\mathrm{d}\theta=1.

(60)

Hence,
\rho_{c}
is an equilibrium solution, and by Proposition
3.4
, it satisfies the necessary conditions for a local minimizer. This configuration was studied in
[
23
]
as an attractor for the aggregation equation (
2
).

Hyperbolic-cosine attraction.

We consider now the interaction potential (
31
) with attraction given by (
58
). We will show that the following density distribution is a global minimizer of the interaction energy:

\displaystyle\rho_{h}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\cosh(d(v,x))}{\alpha(n)\sinh^{n}R_{h}}&\qquad\text{ if }d(v,x)\leq R_{h},\\[10.0pt]
0&\qquad\text{ if }d(v,x)>R_{h},\end{cases}

(61)

where
R_{h}>0
satisfies

\displaystyle n\alpha(n)\int_{0}^{R_{h}}\sinh^{n-1}\theta\cosh^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=1.

(62)

We will show that (
61
) is a solution of (
54
). By rotational symmetry, use notation:

\tilde{\rho}_{h}(d(v,x)):=\rho_{h}(x).

The hyperbolic law of cosines on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
states:

\displaystyle\cosh(d(x,y))=\cosh(d(x,v))\cosh(d(y,v))-\sinh(d(x,v))\sinh(d(y,v))\cos\angle(xvy).

(63)

From (
63
) we compute, using polar coordinates for
y
and the rotational symmetry of
\rho_{h}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\cosh(d(x,y))\rho_{h}(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{R_{h}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\cosh(d(x,v))\cosh\theta-\sinh(d(x,v))\sinh\theta\cos\angle(xvy)\right)\tilde{\rho}_{h}(\theta)\sinh^{n-1}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{y}\mathrm{d}\theta,

where we also used that
\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{h})=D_{R_{h}}(v)
. Then, using

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\cos\angle(xvy)\mathrm{d}\sigma_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}(\cos\angle(xvy)+\cos\angle(xv(-y))\mathrm{d}\sigma_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}(\cos\angle(xvy)+\cos(\pi+\angle(xvy))\mathrm{d}\sigma_{y}

\displaystyle=0,

in the equation above, we have

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\cosh(d(x,y))\rho_{h}(y)\mathrm{d}y=n\alpha(n)\cosh(d(v,x))\int_{0}^{R_{h}}\cosh\theta\tilde{\rho}_{h}(\theta)\sinh^{n-1}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(64)

Consequently, to show that
\rho_{h}
is a solution of (
54
), we need to show:

\tilde{\rho}_{h}(d(v,x))=n\alpha(n)\cosh(d(v,x))\int_{0}^{R_{h}}\cosh\theta\tilde{\rho}_{h}(\theta)\sinh^{n-1}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Indeed, by substituting (
61
) into the right-hand-side above, one computes:

\displaystyle n\alpha(n)\cosh(d(v,x))\int_{0}^{R_{h}}\frac{\cosh^{2}\theta\sinh^{n-1}\theta}{\alpha(n)\sinh^{n}R_{h}}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\cosh(d(v,x))}{\alpha(n)\sinh^{n}R_{h}}\,\underbrace{n\alpha(n)\int_{0}^{R_{h}}\cosh^{2}\theta\sinh^{n-1}\theta d\theta}_{=1\text{ by \eqref{d-8-1}}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\rho}_{h}(d(v,x)).

This shows that
\rho_{h}
is an equilibrium solution of model (
2
). By Proposition
3.4
, it also satisfies the necessary conditions to be a local energy minimizer. In Section
3.3
we will prove that the energy functional corresponding to this potential is strictly convex and hence,
\rho_{h}
is a global energy minimizer.

3.3
Hyperbolic cosine attraction: convexity of the energy

In this subsection we consider the potential (
31
) with hyperbolic cosine attraction given by (
58
), and show that the equilibrium solution
\rho_{h}
from (
61
) is a global energy minimizer in
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{c}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap H^{-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
.

Consider the energy (
1
) defined on
\mathcal{A}
. Then use Remark
3.1
(see (
32
)) to write the energy as

\displaystyle E[\rho]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}(G(x,y)+A_{h}(d(x,y)))\rho(x)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho\|^{2}_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})}+\frac{1}{2n}\mathcal{K}[\rho]-\frac{1}{2n},

(65)

where the functional
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
is defined by

\mathcal{K}[\rho]=\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}\cosh(d(x,y))\rho(x)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

(66)

First note that the repulsion component of the energy is given in terms of the square of the
H^{-1}
norm, which is strictly convex. Since the last term in (
65
) is simply a constant, it remains to investigate the convexity of the functional
\mathcal{K}
.

By (
22
), we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\rho]=

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x_{0}y_{0}-x_{1}y_{1}-\cdots-x_{n}y_{n})\rho(x)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x,\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(y_{0},y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x\right\|^{2},

where we also used (
8
) and (
9
).

Denote

c_{\rho}:=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x,

(67)

and hence, write

\mathcal{K}[\rho]=\left\|c_{\rho}\right\|^{2}.

(68)

By a direct calculation, we then have

\displaystyle t\mathcal{K}[\rho_{1}]+(1-t)\mathcal{K}[\rho_{2}]-\mathcal{K}[t\rho_{1}+(1-t)\rho_{2}]=t\|c_{\rho_{1}}\|^{2}+(1-t)\|c_{\rho_{2}}\|^{2}-\|c_{t\rho_{1}+(1-t)\rho_{2}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=t\|c_{\rho_{1}}\|^{2}+(1-t)\|c_{\rho_{2}}\|^{2}-\|tc_{\rho_{1}}+(1-t)c_{\rho_{2}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=t(1-t)\|c_{\rho_{1}}\|^{2}+t(1-t)\|c_{\rho_{2}}\|^{2}-2t(1-t)\left\langle c_{\rho_{1}},c_{\rho_{2}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=t(1-t)\|c_{\rho_{1}}-c_{\rho_{2}}\|^{2},

(69)

for any density functions
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
. Note that the right-hand-side above is not necessarily non-negative, as the inner product (
8
) is not positive definite. So the positivity of this term needs to be shown, which is what we will focus on next.

Consider the following set:

\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n}):=\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}(\mathbb{H}^{n}):\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}x_{j}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x=0,\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq n\right\}.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, define
\hat{c}_{\rho}
to be the normalized
c_{\rho}
, i.e.,

\hat{c}_{\rho}=\frac{c_{\rho}}{\|c_{\rho}\|}.

Then,

f_{\#}\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),\qquad\text{ where }f(x)=x-^{\prime}\hat{c}_{\rho}.

Proof.

Write
c_{\rho}=(c_{0},c_{1},\cdots,c_{n})
using coordinates, i.e.,

c_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}x_{k}\,\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\text{ for all }0\leq k\leq n.

(70)

First note that
\langle c_{\rho},c_{\rho}\rangle>0
, so
\|c_{\rho}\|
is real-valued. Indeed, it is easy to show that
c_{\rho}\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, where

\mathrm{conv}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
denotes the convex hull of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, defined as:

\mathrm{conv}(\mathbb{H}^{n}):=\{(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{n}^{2}\geq 1\}.

Consequently,

\langle c_{\rho},c_{\rho}\rangle=c_{0}^{2}-c_{1}^{2}-\cdots-c_{n}^{2}\geq 1>0.

By a change of coordinates, one gets:

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}x_{j}f_{\#}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x-^{\prime}\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq n.

(71)

To have
f\#\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, the expression above needs to be
0
for all
1\leq j\leq n
.

By (
20
) and (
17
), we compute:

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x-^{\prime}\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}\rho(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left(x_{j}-x_{0}(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}+\frac{(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}}{(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{0}+1}(x_{1}(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{n})\right)\rho(x)dx.

From here, by the definition of
\hat{c}_{\rho}
and (
70
), we get:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x-^{\prime}\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}\rho(x)dx

\displaystyle=c_{j}-c_{0}(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}+\frac{(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}}{(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{0}+1}(c_{1}(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{n})

\displaystyle=\|c_{\rho}\|(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}-\|c_{\rho}\|(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{0}(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}+\frac{\|c_{\rho}\|(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}}{(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{0}+1}((\hat{c}_{\rho})_{1}^{2}+\cdots+(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{n}^{2})

\displaystyle=\|c_{\rho}\|\Big((\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}-(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{0}(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}+(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{j}((\hat{c}_{\rho})_{0}-1)\Big)

\displaystyle=0,

(72)

where for the third equal sign we used that
\hat{c}_{\rho}
has norm
1
, which implies
(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{1}^{2}+\cdots+(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{n}^{2}=(\hat{c}_{\rho})_{0}^{2}-1
.

The conclusion now follows from (
71
) and (
72
).
∎

From (
66
) and the fact that the operation
-^{\prime}
is an isometry, we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[f_{\#}\rho]

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}\cosh(d(x,y))f_{\#}\rho(x)f_{\#}\rho(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}\cosh(d(x-^{\prime}\hat{c}_{\rho},y-^{\prime}\hat{c}_{\rho}))\rho(x)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}\cosh(d(x,y))\rho(x)\rho(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}[\rho].

Since
\mathcal{K}[\rho]=\mathcal{K}[f_{\#}\rho]
, we can restrict
\mathcal{K}
to
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\subset\mathcal{P}^{ac}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
for the purpose of investigating its minimizers.

Lemma 3.7
.

The functional
\mathcal{K}
defined in (
66
) is convex on
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
.

Proof.

Take
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
; also note here that
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is a convex set. Then,
c_{\rho_{1}}=\alpha_{1}(1,0,\cdots,0)
and
c_{\rho_{2}}=\alpha_{2}(1,0,\cdots,0)
, for some positive constants
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
. Therefore,

\|c_{\rho_{1}}-c_{\rho_{2}}\|^{2}=(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})^{2}\geq 0,

and by using (
69
), we find that:

t\mathcal{K}[\rho_{1}]+(1-t)\mathcal{K}[\rho_{2}]\geq\mathcal{K}[t\rho_{1}+(1-t)\rho_{2}],\qquad\text{ for all }0<t<1.

This shows the claim.
∎

Next, we state and prove the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 3.8
.

Consider the interaction potential (
31
) with hyperbolic cosine attraction given by (
58
). Then, the equilibrium solution
\rho_{h}
from (
61
) is a global minimizer of the interaction energy on
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

As noted above, the square of the
H^{-1}
norm, representing the repulsion component of the energy, is strictly convex on
\mathcal{A}
. Since the attraction component
\mathcal{K}
is convex on
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, the sum of the two functionals (and hence
E
) is strictly convex on
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap\mathcal{A}
. We conclude from here that the critical point
\rho_{h}
given by (
61
) is the unique global minimizer of
E
in
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap\mathcal{A}
.

By Lemma
3.6
, any density can be translated by an isometry into
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. Since the interaction energy is invariant under isometries (see (
5
)), we infer that the global minimizer
\rho_{h}
of
E[\cdot]
on
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap\mathcal{A}
is also a global minimizer of
E[\cdot]
on
\mathcal{A}
itself.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

A similar result as in Theorem
3.8
holds in the standard Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
– see Theorem 2.4 in
[
16
]
. There, it is shown that for an interaction potential that consists of Newtonian repulsion and quadratic attraction (i.e.,
A(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
), the interaction energy is strictly convex and the global energy minimizer consists in a uniform density supported on a ball. However, for the model on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
we could not show the convexity of the energy for the attractive potential
A_{c}
(that leads to constant density equilibria), but we showed it instead for
A_{h}
.

4
Existence of compactly supported minimizers

In this section we study the existence of compactly supported global minimizers of the energy (
1
). The arguments we use here follow the layout and the general technique used by Cañizo
et al.

[
7
]
for the Euclidean case. For this reason, we use similar notations, and we only highlight the main differences.

Consider the interaction energy (
1
) defined on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. Retaining the numbering from
[
7
]
, we make the following assumptions on the interaction potential
K
:

(\mathcal{H}1)
:
K
is bounded from below by a finite constant
K_{min}<0
.

(\mathcal{H}2)
:
K
is locally integrable, i.e.,
\displaystyle{\int_{d(x,v)<R}|K(d(x,v))|dx<\infty}
, for any
R>0
.

(\mathcal{H}3)
: Interactions are symmetric, i.e.,
\displaystyle{K(x,y)=K(d(x,y))}
(by an abuse of notation), for all
x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

(\mathcal{H}4)
: The limit
\displaystyle{K_{\infty}=\lim_{\theta\to\infty}K(\theta)}
exists and
K
is unstable. Here,
K
is unstable if there exists
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
such that
E[\rho]<\frac{1}{2}K_{\infty}
.

(\mathcal{H}5)
:
K
is lower semi-continuous.

(\mathcal{H}6)
: There exists
R_{6}>0
such that
K
is strictly increasing on
[R_{6},\infty)
.

Note that hypothesis
(\mathcal{H}3)
has been used throughout the entire paper, but we list it here separately for consistency with the numbering in
[
7
]
. In the Euclidean case, this hypothesis shows in a weaker form in fact, specifically
K(x)=K(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Assumptions
(\mathcal{H}1)-(\mathcal{H}6)
were considered in
[
7
]
for the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Under such assumptions, the main result in
[
7
]
(Theorem 1.4) states that there exists a global minimizer of the energy in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover, there exists a uniform bound for the supports of all minimizers of
E
. A very similar result holds for
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. We state the result below and then we sketch its proof.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
K:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}1)-(\mathcal{H}6)
. Then, there exists a global minimizer
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
of the energy
E[\rho]
defined in (
1
). In addition, there exists
D>0
(depending only on
K
and the dimension
n
) such that every minimizer of
E
has compact support with diameter at most
D
.

The layout of the proof of the analogous theorem for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
from
[
7
]
is the following (we write it for
\mathbb{H}^{n}
instead):

Part 1.
Fix
R\geq 0
and set
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})=\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n}):\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subset\overline{B_{R}(v))}\}
. Show the existence of global minimizers on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. This can be done as follows
[
7
, Lemma 2.1]
. Consider a minimizing sequence
(\rho_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of
E
restricted to
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. As
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is tight and weakly-
*
closed in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, by Prokhorov’s Theorem, there exists a subsequence
(\rho_{k_{l}})_{l\geq 1}
that converges weakly-
*
to
\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. By the weakly-
*
lower semicontinuity of
E
,
\rho_{R}
is a global minimizer of
E
on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
.

Part 2.
Show that there exists
D>0
(depending only on
K
and
n
) such that for all
R\geq 0
and any global minimizer
\rho_{R}
of
E
on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, the diameter of the support of
\rho_{R}
is bounded by
D
. This is the core of the proof and follows from several key lemmas which we will discuss after presenting the layout.

Part 3.
Consider
\rho^{\prime}
a global minimizer on
\mathcal{P}_{D}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, where
D
is the upper bound on the diameters of minimizers from Part 2. Show that
\rho^{\prime}
is in fact a global minimizer on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. This can be argued as follows
[
7
, Lemma 2.10]
. First note that for any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with compact support,
E[\rho]\geq E[\rho^{\prime}]
. Indeed, for
R\geq 0
fixed, such that
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, take
\rho_{R}
a global minimizer of
E
on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. Then,
E[\rho]\geq E[\rho_{R}]\geq E[\rho^{\prime}]
, where the second inequality holds by the translation invariance of
E
(as
\rho_{R}
can be translated to a measure in
\mathcal{P}_{D}(\mathbb{H}^{n}))
. Second, for any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, approximate
\rho
by compactly supported measures
\rho_{k}
with
E[\rho_{k}]\to E[\rho]
as
k\to\infty
. Since
E[\rho_{k}]\geq E[\rho^{\prime}]
, for all
k
, infer
E[\rho]\geq E[\rho^{\prime}]
and conclude
\rho^{\prime}
global energy minimizer on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
.

As noted above, Part 2 is the core of the proof. It is based on two fundamental lemmas. The first lemma states that for any point in the support of a minimizer
\rho_{R}
on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, there has to be some mass not far from it. Specifically, the following result holds.

Lemma 4.2
.

[
7
, Lemma 2.6]

Suppose the potential
K
satisfies hypotheses
(\mathcal{H}1)-(\mathcal{H}5)
. Then there exist constants
r,m>0
(depending only on
K
) such that for all sufficiently large
R
, and all global minimizers
\rho_{R}
of the energy on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
we have

\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\mathrm{d}\rho_{R}(x)\geq m,\qquad\text{ for all }x_{0}\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R}).

Note that the constants
m
and
r
are universal (they do not depend on
R
). The proof of the lemma can be immediately adapted from that of
[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
. We only point out here that for its proof, Hypotheses
(\mathcal{H}1)
and
(\mathcal{H}4)
are used in a fundamental way, to bound the interaction potential
K
at short and long distances, respectively.

The second fundamental lemma states that minimizers
\rho_{R}
cannot have large gaps. The general idea of its proof is the same as for
[
7
, Lemma 2.7]
, but the details are more involved. In place of the projection on the
k
-th coordinate (as used in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
), we will use the map
\pi_{k}
defined in (
27
). This lemma is the only place where hypothesis
(\mathcal{H}6)
is being used.

Lemma 4.3
.

(Generalization of
[
7
, Lemma 2.7]
)

Assume that the potential
K
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}1)-(\mathcal{H}6)
. Let
R\geq 0
and suppose that
\rho_{R}
is a global minimizer of the energy (
1
) on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. Then the support of
\rho_{R}
cannot have “gaps” larger than
2R_{6}
in each coordinate (
R_{6}
is the constant from
(\mathcal{H}6)
): if
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
\displaystyle{\pi_{k}^{-1}([a_{k}-R_{6},a_{k}+R_{6}])\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}\backslash\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R})}
, then either
\displaystyle{\pi_{k}^{-1}((-\infty,a_{k}-R_{6}])\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}\backslash\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R})}
or

\displaystyle{\pi_{k}^{-1}([a_{k}+R_{6},\infty))\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}\backslash\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R})}
.

Proof.

Suppose by contradiction that there exists an index
k\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
and
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle{\pi_{k}^{-1}([a_{k}-R_{6},a_{k}+R_{6}])\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}\backslash\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R})}
, such that the support of
\rho_{R}
has nontrivial intersections with both the “left” and “right” parts, defined as

H_{\mathrm{L}}:=\pi_{k}^{-1}((-\infty,a_{k}-R_{6}])\quad\text{and}\quad H_{\mathrm{R}}:=\pi_{k}^{-1}([a_{k}+R_{6},\infty)),

respectively. Also, fix
0<\epsilon<R_{6}
and consider

\bar{\rho}_{R}:=\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}}+{T_{\epsilon}}_{\#}\bigl(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}\bigr),

where
T_{\epsilon}
is the isometry defined as
T_{\epsilon}(x)=x-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)
(recall (
23
)).

It follows immediately that

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}})\subset H_{\mathrm{L}}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\mathrm{supp}\bigl({T_{\epsilon}}_{\#}\bigl(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}\bigr)\bigr)\subset H_{\mathrm{R}}-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon).

From Corollary
2.13
we have

H_{\mathrm{R}}-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)=\pi_{k}^{-1}([a_{k}+R_{6},\infty))-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)=\pi_{k}^{-1}([a_{k}+R_{6}-\epsilon,\infty)).

Hence, we get

\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}})\subset\pi_{k}^{-1}((-\infty,a_{k}-R_{6}])\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\mathrm{supp}\bigl({T_{\epsilon}}_{\#}\bigl(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}\bigr)\bigr)\subset\pi_{k}^{-1}([a_{k}+R_{6}-\epsilon,\infty)).

From this fact and
0<\epsilon<R_{6}
, we infer that
\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}})
and
\mathrm{supp}\bigl({T_{\epsilon}}_{\#}\bigl(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}\bigr)\Bigr)
are disjoint. This yields the following calculation:

\displaystyle E(\bar{\rho}_{R})

\displaystyle=E(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}})+E({T_{\epsilon}}_{\#}\bigl(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}\bigr))+\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}K(d(x,y))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}{T_{\epsilon}}_{\#}\bigl(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}\bigr)(y)

\displaystyle=E(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}})+E(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}})+\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}K(d(x,y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}(y),

(73)

where for the second equal sign we used the invariance under isometries of the energy (see (
5
)) and the change of variable
z=y+^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)
in the integral.

Now take
x\in H_{\mathrm{L}}
and
y\in H_{\mathrm{R}}
, and recall (
22
), by which we write

\cosh(d(x,y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))=(x-^{\prime}(y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))_{0}.

Next, we will compare
(x-^{\prime}(y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))_{0}
with
(x-^{\prime}y)_{0}
.

Using (
17
), (
19
) and (
20
), we compute

\displaystyle(x-^{\prime}(y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))_{0}

\displaystyle=x_{0}(y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon))_{0}-\sum_{l=1}^{n}x_{l}(y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon))_{l}

\displaystyle=x_{0}(y_{0}\cosh\epsilon-y_{k}\sinh\epsilon)-\sum_{l=1}^{n}x_{l}(-y_{0}\delta_{kl}\sinh\epsilon+y_{l}+y_{l}\delta_{kl}(\cosh\epsilon-1))

\displaystyle=x_{0}(y_{0}\cosh\epsilon-y_{k}\sinh\epsilon)+x_{k}y_{0}\sinh\epsilon-\sum_{l=1}^{n}x_{l}y_{l}+(1-\cosh\epsilon)x_{k}y_{k}.

Furthermore, add and subtract
x_{0}y_{0}
to the right-hand-side above and use (
21
) to get

\displaystyle(x-^{\prime}(y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))_{0}-(x-^{\prime}y)_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\cosh\epsilon-1)(x_{0}y_{0}-x_{k}y_{k})+\sinh\epsilon(x_{k}y_{0}-x_{0}y_{k})

(74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)x_{0}y_{0}\left(1-\frac{x_{k}y_{k}}{x_{0}y_{0}}\right)+2\sinh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\cosh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)x_{0}y_{0}\left(\frac{x_{k}}{x_{0}}-\frac{y_{k}}{y_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sinh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)x_{0}y_{0}\left(\sinh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\left(1-\tanh(\pi_{k}(x))\tanh(\pi_{k}(y))\right)+\cosh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)(\tanh(\pi_{k}(x))-\tanh(\pi_{k}(y)))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sinh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)x_{0}y_{0}(1-\tanh(\pi_{k}(x))\tanh(\pi_{k}(y)))\left(\sinh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)+\cosh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\tanh(\pi_{k}(x)-\pi_{k}(y))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\sinh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)x_{0}y_{0}(1-\tanh\pi_{k}(x)\tanh\pi_{k}(y))}{\cosh(\pi_{k}(x)-\pi_{k}(y))}\right)\sinh\left(\pi_{k}(x)-\pi_{k}(y)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right).

(75)

Since

\frac{2\sinh\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)x_{0}y_{0}(1-\tanh\pi_{k}(x)\tanh\pi_{k}(y))}{\cosh(\pi_{k}(x)-\pi_{k}(y))}

is always positive for any
\epsilon>0
, we only have to study a sign of
\sinh\left(\pi_{k}(x)-\pi_{k}(y)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)
. As
x\in H_{L}
,
y\in H_{R}
, and
\epsilon<R_{6}
, we have

\pi_{k}(x)-\pi_{k}(y)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}<(a_{k}-R_{6})-(a_{k}+R_{6})+\frac{R_{6}}{2}=-\frac{3}{2}R_{6}<0,

which by (
75
) it implies that

(x-^{\prime}(y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))_{0}<(x-^{\prime}y)_{0}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d(x,y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon))<d(x,y).

(76)

Now apply Lemma
2.14
for
x
and
y-^{\prime}u_{k}(x)
, and use
\pi_{k}(x)<a_{k}-R_{6}
and
\pi_{k}(y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon))>a_{k}+R_{6}-\epsilon
, to get

d(x,y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon))>|(a_{k}+R_{6}-\epsilon)-(a_{k}-R_{6})|=2R_{6}-\epsilon>R_{6}.

(77)

By combining (
76
) and (
77
) we now get

R_{6}<d(x,y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon))<d(x,y),

and furthermore,

K(d(x,y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))<K(d(x,y)),

since
K
is strictly increasing on
[R_{6},\infty)
by Hypothesis
(\mathcal{H}6)
.

Finally, we substitute this result into (
73
) to get

\displaystyle E(\bar{\rho}_{R})

\displaystyle=E(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}})+E(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}})+\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}K(d(x,y-^{\prime}u_{k}(\epsilon)))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}(y)

\displaystyle<E(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}})+E(\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}})+\iint_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}}K(d(x,y))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{L}}}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{R}|_{{}_{H_{\mathrm{R}}}}(y)

\displaystyle=E(\rho_{R}).

However, this is a contradiction to the fact that
\rho_{R}
is a global minimizer on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
.
∎

Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
lead to the following last result, that concludes Part 2 of the proof of Theorem
4.1
. The statement and its proof are identical to those of
[
7
, Lemma 2.9]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

[
7
, Lemma 2.9]
Assume
K
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}1)-(\mathcal{H}6)
. Then there exists
D>0
(depending only on
K
and
n
) such that for all
R\geq 0
and any global minimizer
\rho_{R}
of the energy on
\mathcal{P}_{R}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, the diameter of the support of
\rho_{R}
is bounded by
D
.

Proof.

The proof of the lemma follows that of
[
7
, Lemma 2.9]
by replacing the
k
-th coordinate of a point
x
by
\pi_{k}(x)
as defined in (
27
). We sketch it here. First, given that there is at least an amount of mass
m
in any geodesic ball
B_{r}(x)
with
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R})
(by Lemma
4.2
), the support of
\rho_{R}
can be covered by a finite number of geodesic balls
B_{2r}(x_{i})
,
i=0,1,\dots,N
, with
N+1\leq[1/m]
(
[\cdot]
denotes the ceiling of a number). Note that the points
x_{i}
are chosen so that
x_{i}\notin B_{2r}(x_{j})
, for any
i\neq j
, so the balls
B_{r}(x_{i})
are disjoint.

With
k
fixed (
1\leq k\leq n
), order the points so that
\pi_{k}(x_{0})<\dots<\pi_{k}(x_{N})
. Then,

\pi_{k}(x_{i+1})-\pi_{k}(x_{i})\leq 4r+2R_{6},\qquad i=0,1,\dots N-1,

as otherwise this would contradict Lemma
4.3
(gaps larger than
2R_{6}
). From here, get that

\pi_{k}(x_{N})-\pi_{k}(x_{0})\leq N(4r+2R_{6})\leq([1/m]-1)(4r+2R_{6}).

(78)

Now set
x_{l}
and
x_{m}
such that

d(x_{l},x_{m})=\max_{0\leq i,j\leq N}d(x_{i},x_{j}).

Consider the geodesic curve between
x_{l}
and
x_{m}
, and the midpoint between the two points on this geodesic. Take the isometry
T
that maps the midpoint into the vertex
v
, and
x_{l}\mapsto z
,
x_{m}\mapsto-z
(see (
19
)), where
z=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha,0,\dots,0)
. Then,

d(x_{l},x_{m})=d(z,-z)=2\alpha,

where the second equal sign comes from the fact that (see (
10
))

d(-z,z)=2d(v,z)=2\cosh^{-1}(\cosh\alpha)=2\alpha.

On the other hand, by (
27
), we get

2\alpha=\pi_{1}(z)-\pi_{1}(-z).

Finally, by (
78
) (used for the image under the isometry
T
of
\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R})
), one has

\pi_{1}(z)-\pi_{1}(-z)\leq([1/m]-1)(4r+2R_{6}).

Combining these considerations, we find

\text{diam}(\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{R}))\leq([1/m]-1)(4r+2R_{6})+4r=:D.

∎

Numerical simulations

In this section, we provide numerical simulations of the model (
2
) for some specific interaction potentials in the form (
31
). For this purpose, we write model (
2
) in characteristic form; this form of the equation is widely used for both analysis and numerical simulations
[
5
,
18
,
20
,
23
]
.

Model (
2
) in characteristic form.

Consider the flow generated by the velocity field
\mathbf{v}[\rho]
given by (
4
). The characteristic paths
x(t)
then satisfy

\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{v}[\rho](x(t),t).

(79)

To reach the characteristic form of model (
2
), expand the divergence

\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}[\rho])=\nabla_{\mathbf{v}[\rho]}\rho+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}[\rho],

where
\nabla_{\mathbf{v}[\rho]}\rho
denotes the covariant derivative of
\rho
along
\mathbf{v}[\rho]
. Then write (
2
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{\mathbf{v}[\rho]}\rho

\displaystyle=-\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\rho(t,x)\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\Delta_{x}K(x,y)\rho(t,y)\mathrm{d}y,

(80)

where for the second equal sign we used (
4
). Note that the left-hand-side above represents the material derivative (along characteristic paths) of
\rho
.

For numerical simulations we consider only radially symmetric solutions. In such case, the characteristic paths
x(t)
are geodesic curves passing through the vertex
v
. Denote the radial coordinate

\theta_{x}:=d(x,v).

By writing the characteristic equations (
79
) in radial coordinates, we then find

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\theta_{x(t)}=-\hat{n}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{x}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}K(x,y)\rho(t,y)\mathrm{d}y,

(81)

where
\hat{n}_{x}:=\frac{x}{\theta_{x}}
.

The interaction potentials in the numerical simulations are in the form (
31
). Using (
31
), the radial symmetry of
\rho
, the divergence theorem and (
30
), we compute starting from (
81
):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\theta_{x(t)}=-\hat{n}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{x}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(G(x,y)+A(d(x,y))\rho(t,y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|\partial B_{v}(\theta_{x})|}\int_{\partial B_{v}(\theta_{x})}\hat{n}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{x}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}G(x,y)\rho(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\sigma_{x}-\hat{n}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{x}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}A(d(x,y))\rho(t,y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|\partial B_{v}(\theta_{x})|}\int_{B_{v}(\theta_{x})}\Delta_{x}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}G(x,y)\rho(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\sigma_{x}-\hat{n}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{x}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}A(d(x,y))\rho(t,y)\mathrm{d}y

Furthermore, using the notation

and the hyperbolic law of cosines, we write

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\theta_{x(t)}=\frac{1}{\sinh^{n-1}\theta_{x}}\int_{0}^{\theta_{x}}\tilde{\rho}(t,\lambda)\sinh^{n-1}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\partial_{\theta_{x}}A\left(\cosh^{-1}\left(\cosh\theta_{x}\cosh\theta_{y}-\sinh\theta_{x}\sinh\theta_{y}\cos\angle xvy\right)\right)\tilde{\rho}(t,\theta_{y})\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sinh^{n-1}\theta_{x}}\int_{0}^{\theta_{x}}\tilde{\rho}(t,\lambda)\sinh^{n-1}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\lambda-(n-1)\alpha(n-1)

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{\theta_{x}}A\left(\cosh^{-1}\left(\cosh\theta_{x}\cosh\theta_{y}-\sinh\theta_{x}\sinh\theta_{y}\cos\beta\right)\right)\tilde{\rho}(t,\theta_{y})\sinh^{n-1}\theta_{y}\sin^{n-2}\beta\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{y}\mathrm{d}\beta.

(82)

For the evolution of the density along characteristic paths, we calculate from (

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

From the definition of the matrix product, we have

y_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta=0}^{n}B(w)_{\alpha\beta}x_{\beta},\qquad\forall~\alpha=0,1,\cdots,n.

We can simplify it as follows:

y_{0}=\sum_{\beta=0}^{n}B(w)_{0\beta}x_{\beta}=B(w)_{00}x_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}B(w)_{0i}x_{i}=\gamma x_{0}-\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i},

and for
j=1,\dots,n
,

\displaystyle y_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta=0}^{n}B(w)_{j\beta}x_{\beta}=B(w)_{j0}x_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}B(w)_{ji}x_{i}

\displaystyle=-\gamma w_{j}x_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\delta_{ij}+(\gamma-1)\frac{w_{i}w_{j}}{|w|^{2}}\right)x_{i}

\displaystyle=-\gamma w_{j}x_{0}+x_{j}+(\gamma-1)\frac{w_{j}}{|w|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}.

This yields

\displaystyle y_{0}^{2}-y_{1}^{2}-\cdots-y_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\left(x_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}\right)^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(-\gamma w_{j}x_{0}+x_{j}+(\gamma-1)\frac{w_{j}}{|w|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}x_{0}^{2}-2\gamma^{2}x_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}+\gamma^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}\right)^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\gamma^{2}w_{j}^{2}x_{0}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}+(\gamma-1)^{2}\frac{w_{j}^{2}}{|w|^{4}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(-2\gamma w_{j}x_{j}x_{0}-2\gamma(\gamma-1)\frac{w_{j}^{2}x_{0}}{|w|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}+2(\gamma-1)\frac{x_{j}w_{j}}{|w|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}x_{0}^{2}-2\gamma^{2}x_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}+\gamma^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}\right)^{2}-\gamma^{2}x_{0}^{2}|w|^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{2}-(\gamma-1)^{2}\frac{1}{|w|^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2x_{0}\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}+2\gamma(\gamma-1)x_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}-2(\gamma-1)\frac{1}{|w|^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}x_{0}^{2}+\gamma^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}\right)^{2}-\gamma^{2}x_{0}^{2}|w|^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{2}-(\gamma^{2}-1)\frac{1}{|w|^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}(1-|w|^{2})x_{0}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{2}=x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{n}^{2}.

If we use
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, then we have
x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{n}^{2}=1
. This implies that

y_{0}^{2}-y_{1}^{2}-\cdots-y_{n}^{2}=1,

which is the desired result.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Let
a,b
be tangent vectors at
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Since
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, tangent vectors
a
and
b
can also be considered as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
which satisfy

a_{0}x_{0}-a_{1}x_{1}-\cdots-a_{n}x_{n}=b_{0}x_{0}-b_{1}x_{1}-\cdots-b_{n}x_{n}=0.

The induced metric defines the inner product between two tangent vectors as

a\cdot b=a_{0}b_{0}-a_{1}b_{1}-\cdots-a_{n}b_{n}=-a^{\top}\eta b.

From the definition of the push forward of a tangent vector, we have

dF_{w}(a)=B(w)a,\quad dF_{w}(b)=B(w)b.

This yields

dF_{w}(a)\cdot dF_{w}(b)=-(dF_{w}(a))^{\top}\eta\,dF_{w}(b)=-a^{\top}B(w)^{\top}\eta B(w)b=-a^{\top}\eta b=a\cdot b,

where we used (
15
) for the third equal sign. Finally, this implies that
F_{w}
is an isometry on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
for all
w
such that
|w|<1
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

By a simple calculation, for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|w|<1
, we know the vertex of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
maps to

F_{w}(1,0,\cdots,0)=(\gamma,-\gamma w_{1},\cdots,-\gamma w_{n}).

The equation above shows that
1\iff 3
.

Now, we investigate the relationship between
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that:

F_{w}(x)=(1,0,\cdots,0).

From the definition of
F_{w}
, we have

\displaystyle 1=\gamma x_{0}-\gamma(w_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+w_{n}x_{n}),

(92)

and

\displaystyle 0=-\gamma w_{i}x_{0}+x_{i}+(\gamma-1)\frac{w_{i}}{|w|^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}x_{j},\quad\forall~i=1,2,\cdots,n.

(93)

Equation (
93
) yields

\displaystyle w_{i}=\alpha x_{i},\qquad\forall~i=1,2,\cdots,n,

(94)

for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. We substitute (
94
) into (
92
) to get

1=\gamma x_{0}-\gamma\alpha(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})=\gamma x_{0}-\gamma\alpha(x_{0}^{2}-1),

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{x_{0}-\alpha(x_{0}^{2}-1)}.

(95)

By (
94
), we also have:

|w|^{2}=w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}+\cdots+w_{n}^{2}=\alpha^{2}(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})=\alpha^{2}(x_{0}^{2}-1),

where for the last equality we used that
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. From the definition of
\gamma

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}=\frac{1}{1-|w|^{2}}=\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{2}(x_{0}^{2}-1)}.

(96)

Finally, we compare (
95
) and (
96

(x_{0}-\alpha(x_{0}^{2}-1))^{2}=1-\alpha^{2}(x_{0}^{2}-1),

(x_{0}^{2}-1)(1-\alpha x_{0})^{2}=0.

(97)

If
w=0

is the identity map. So, in this case, we have
x=(1,0,\cdots,0)
. Now assume that
|w|>0
.
Then, (
94

1\leq i\leq n
, which yields

x_{0}^{2}=1+x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}\geq 1+x_{i}^{2}>1.

Hence, from (
97

\alpha=\frac{1}{x_{0}},

and this gives the relation

w=\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{0}},\cdots,\frac{x_{n}}{x_{0}}\right).

Note that this relation also holds for
w=0

2\iff 3
, which concludes the proof.

Proof of Lemma

\displaystyle=\tilde{x}_{0}\tilde{y}_{0}-\tilde{x}_{1}\tilde{y}_{1}-\cdots-\tilde{x}_{n}\tilde{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{\cosh^{-1}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+1)}\left(\pi b_{0}\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+2)\sinh\theta+(1-2\pi b_{0}\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}})\cosh\theta\sinh\theta\right)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=C\left(\pi b_{0}\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}^{2}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+2)+(1-2\pi b_{0}\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}})\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+2)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}C\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+2).

This implies that
C=\frac{1}{\pi\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+2)}
.

Finally, derive

\tilde{\rho}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle b_{0}+\left(\frac{1-2\pi b_{0}\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}}{\pi\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+2)}\right)\cosh\theta,\qquad&\text{if }0\leq\theta\leq\cosh^{-1}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+1),\\
0,\qquad&\text{if }\theta>\cosh^{-1}(\alpha_{b_{0}b_{1}}+1).\end{cases}

∎
