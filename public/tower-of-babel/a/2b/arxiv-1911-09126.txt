Incompressibility of classical distributions
Anurag Anshu, Debbie Leung, Dave Touchette

Abstract
In blind compression of quantum states, a sender Alice is given a specimen of a quantum state ρ \rho drawn from a known ensemble (but without knowing what ρ \rho is), and she transmits sufficient quantum data to a receiver Bob so that he can decode a near perfect specimen of ρ \rho . For many such states drawn iid from the ensemble, the asymptotically achievable rate is the number of qubits required to be transmitted per state. The Holevo information is a lower bound for the achievable rate, and is attained for pure state ensembles, or in the related scenario of entanglement-assisted visible compression of mixed states wherein Alice knows what state is drawn. In this paper, we prove a general and robust lower bound on the achievable rate for ensembles of classical states, which holds even in the least demanding setting when Alice and Bob share free entanglement and a constant per-copy error is allowed. We apply the bound to a specific ensemble of only two states and prove a near-maximal separation (saturating the dimension bound in leading order) between the best achievable rate and the Holevo information for constant error. This also implies that the ensemble is incompressible – compression does not reduce the communication cost by much. Since the states are classical , the observed incompressibility is not fundamentally quantum mechanical. We lower bound the difference between the achievable rate and the Holevo information in terms of quantitative limitations to clone the specimen or to distinguish the two classical states.

Introduction

1.1
Blind quantum data compression and related scenarios

A central goal of information theory is to capture the ultimate rate of transformation of resources. For example, we may want to minimize the communication cost of a task, which is an optimization problem over a potentially unbounded number of possible communication protocols. In some special cases, the best communication cost is given by a simple enough information theoretic quantity that can be computed. For example, this has been achieved in Shannon’s source coding theorem (data compression) and noisy channel coding
[
1
]
and some network analogues
[
2
]
. Quantum information theory shares the same goal, and similar understanding has been achieved in quantum noisy coding theorem, albeit with regularization issues in many scenarios.

This paper focuses on the problem of quantum data compression, which can be stated as follows.

In a powerful series of results

17

Is there a coding scheme that can even further reduce the rate

Note that since our lower bound holds for local

Since we consider entanglement-assisted protocols, our lower bound also applies to shared randomness assisted protocols, such as in

Our result may also provide insights to rate distortion theory

Main result, techniques and consequences

1.3
Conclusion

In this work, we study the problem of blind compression of quantum
data, in the regime of finite error. Our inspiration comes from two
sources. First is the visible scenario, where the trade-off between
global error and communication rate is very well understood (providing
a strong converse rate) and the trade-off between local error and
communication rate is relatively well understood. Second is the
Koashi-Imoto characterization, which gives the optimal rate of
communication as the error vanishes in the asymptotic limit and hence
shows a near maximal separation between the communication rate and the
Holevo information in the vanishing error regime.
In their converse bound for non-vanishing error, the error
dependence of the rate is not explicitly given, and we have observed
sensitivity to error in the appendix.
Thus the Koashi-Imoto characterization does not immediately
apply to the current regime of non-vanishing (global or local) error.
Our main result resolves this
problem, showing a near maximal separation of the rate
from the Holevo information
in the non-vanishing local error regime. For this, we prove a new
lower bound that is based on a variant of the no-cloning theorem for
classical distributions. Our technical proof builds on an approximate
version of the Birkoff-von Neumann theorem.

An immediate question raised by our work is to understand the error
vs communication rate trade-off for the blind compression scenario, for
the cases of global and local errors. Furthermore, we ask if a strong
converse rate exists for the blind compression scenario when the
global error is finite, which is known to hold for the visible
case. Finally, we highlight that our lower bound does not entirely
rely on the spatial separation between the sender and the receiver,
which leads to the question of further applicability of our techniques
to other problems.

\rho^{a}_{B}
on register
B
.
This convention allows a clear distinction between having a specimen
of the state
\rho^{a}_{B}
(having system
B
) and knowing what it is
(having system
A
). If all
\rho^{a}_{B}
are diagonal in the same basis,

\rho_{AB}
is called
classical-classical
or simply
classical
.

A quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{L}(A)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(B)
is a completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP) linear map. (We sometimes just call it a “map” in this paper.)
In particular, it takes quantum states in
\mathcal{D}(A)
to the quantum states in
\mathcal{D}(B)
.
A quantum measurement (or instrument)
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{L}(A)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(A^{\prime}C)
is characterized by a collection of operators
\{N_{c}:\mathcal{H}_{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{A^{\prime}}\}
that satisfy
\sum_{c}N_{c}^{\dagger}N_{c}=\mathrm{I}_{A}
and is given by

\mathcal{N}(\rho_{A})=\sum_{c}|c\rangle\langle c|_{C}\otimes N_{c}\rho_{A}N^{\dagger}_{c}.

A
unitary
operator
U_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{A}
is such that
U_{A}^{\dagger}U_{A}=U_{A}U_{A}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{I}_{A}
.

2.2
List of quantum information theoretic quantities

We consider the following information theoretic quantities. All logarithms are base
2
and only normalized quantum states are considered in the definitions below. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

1.

Trace distance:
. For
\rho_{A},\sigma_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
,

\Delta(\rho_{A},\sigma_{A}):=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{A}-\sigma_{A}\|_{1}.

2.

Fidelity:
For
\rho_{A},\sigma_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
,

\mathrm{F}(\rho_{A},\sigma_{A}):=\|\sqrt{\rho_{A}}\sqrt{\sigma_{A}}\|_{1}.

3.

\varepsilon
-ball:
For
\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
,

\mathcal{B}^{\varepsilon}\>\!\!\left(\rho_{A}\right):=\{\rho^{\prime}_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(A)|~\Delta(\rho_{A},\rho^{\prime}_{A})\leq\varepsilon\}.

4.

Von Neumann entropy:
(
[
8
]
) For
\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
,

\mathrm{S}(A)_{\rho}:=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A}).

5.

Conditional entropy:
For
\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}(AB)
,

\mathrm{S}\left(A|B\right)_{\rho}:=\mathrm{S}(AB)_{\rho}-\mathrm{S}(B)_{\rho}.

6.

Relative entropy:
(
[
36
]
) For
\rho_{A},\sigma_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
such that
\text{supp}(\rho_{A})\subset\text{supp}(\sigma_{A})
,

\mathrm{D}\>\!\!\left(\rho_{A}\middle\|\sigma_{A}\right):=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A})-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}\log\sigma_{A}).

7.

Mutual information:
For
\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}(AB)
,

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(A\>\!:\>\!B\right)_{\rho}:=\mathrm{S}(A)_{\rho}+\mathrm{S}(B)_{\rho}-\mathrm{S}(AB)_{\rho}=\mathrm{D}\>\!\!\left(\rho_{AB}\middle\|\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}\right).

8.

Conditional mutual information:
For
\rho_{ABC}\in\mathcal{D}(ABC)
,

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(A\>\!:\>\!B\>\!\middle|\>\!C\right)_{\rho}:=\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(A\>\!:\>\!BC\right)_{\rho}-\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(A\>\!:\>\!C\right)_{\rho}.

Basic facts used in our proofs

Fact 1

(Triangle inequality for trace distance,
[
37
]
, Chapter 9)
.

For quantum states
\rho,\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{D}(A)
,

\Delta(\rho,\sigma)\leq\Delta(\rho,\tau)+\Delta(\tau,\sigma).

Fact 2

(Data-processing inequality,
[
38
,
39
]
)
.

For the quantum states
\rho
,
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(A)
,
\theta\in\mathcal{D}(AC)
and the quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{L}(A)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(B)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\Delta(\mathcal{E}(\rho),\mathcal{E}(\sigma)),

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}\>\!\!\left(\rho\middle\|\sigma\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{D}\>\!\!\left(\mathcal{E}(\rho)\middle\|\mathcal{E}(\sigma)\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(A\>\!:\>\!C\right)_{\theta}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(B\>\!:\>\!C\right)_{\mathcal{E}(\theta)}.

Fact 3

(Pinsker’s inequality,
[
40
]
)
.

For the quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(A)
,

\Delta(\rho,\sigma)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}\>\!\!\left(\rho\middle\|\sigma\right).

Fact 4

(Dimension bound)
.

For the quantum state
\rho_{XAB}
, with classical register
X
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(A\>\!:\>\!X\>\!\middle|\>\!B\right)_{\rho}

\displaystyle\leq\log|X|.

Fact 5

(Alicki-Fannes-Winter inequality,
[
41
,
42
]
)
.

For quantum-classical states
\rho_{AB}
and
\sigma_{AB}
satisfying
\rho_{B}=\sigma_{B}
,

|\mathrm{S}\left(A|B\right)_{\rho}-\mathrm{S}\left(A|B\right)_{\sigma}|\leq\Delta\left(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB}\right)\cdot\log|A|+1,

|\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(A\>\!:\>\!B\right)_{\rho}-\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(A\>\!:\>\!B\right)_{\sigma}|\leq\Delta\left(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB}\right)\cdot\log|B|+1.

Fact 6

(Fano’s inequality,
[
43
]
)
.

For any classical state
\rho_{AA^{\prime}}=\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}p_{AA^{\prime}}(a,a^{\prime})|a,a^{\prime}\rangle\langle a,a^{\prime}|

a probability distribution, it holds that

\mathrm{S}\left(A|A^{\prime}\right)_{\rho}\leq 1+\Pr[A\neq A^{\prime}]\log|A|.

Note that we have stated weaker

simplify the expressions in our results.

3
Lower bound on entanglement-assisted blind distribution compression

For our lower bound on the compression rate, we focus on ensembles of
classical
states (these can be simultaneously diagonalized). We will henceforth refer to them as distributions. We begin with a formal definition of our task.

Definition 1

(Entanglement-assisted blind distribution compression)
.

Consider an ensemble
\{p(x),\rho^{x}_{C}\}
where all
\rho^{x}_{C}
’s are diagonal.
Let
\rho_{XC}
be as obtained through (
1
). Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be an error parameter and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let the initial joint state between the Referee and Alice be
\rho_{X_{1}C_{1}}\otimes\rho_{X_{2}C_{2}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{X_{n}C_{n}}
, with the Referee holding registers
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
(each
X_{i}\equiv X
) and Alice holding registers
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n}
(each
C_{i}\equiv C
). Alice and Bob share entanglement
|\theta\rangle_{E_{A}E_{B}}
, where
E_{A}
is with Alice and
E_{B}
is with Bob. An
(n,R,\varepsilon)
- entanglement-assisted blind distribution compression protocol is as follows. Alice applies an encoding map
\mathcal{E}:C_{1}C_{2}\ldots C_{n}E_{A}\rightarrow C_{1}C_{2}\ldots C_{n}T
, where
T
is a classical register of size
2^{nR}
. She communicates
T
to Bob (so the number of bits communicated in the protocol is
nR=\log|T|
). After receiving
T
, Bob applies a decoding map
\mathcal{D}:TE_{B}\rightarrow C^{\prime}_{1}C^{\prime}_{2}\ldots C^{\prime}_{n}
. Here, each
C^{\prime}_{i}\equiv C
. It is required that

\Delta\left(\mathrm{Tr}_{C_{1}\ldots C_{n}}\circ\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}\left(\rho_{X_{1}C_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{X_{n}C_{n}}\otimes\theta_{E_{A}E_{B}}\right),\rho_{X_{1}C^{\prime}_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{X_{n}C^{\prime}_{n}}\right)\leq\varepsilon.

(4)

The above definition involves a global error for the compression. Our lower bounds apply also to the more relaxed setting of the local error model:

\forall_{i}\;\Delta\left(\mathrm{Tr}_{C^{\prime}_{1}\ldots C^{\prime}_{i-1}C^{\prime}_{i+1}\ldots C^{\prime}_{n}C_{1}\ldots C_{n}}\circ\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}\left(\rho_{X_{1}C_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{X_{n}C_{n}}\otimes\theta_{E_{A}E_{B}}\right),\rho_{X_{i}C^{\prime}_{i}}\right)\leq\varepsilon.

(5)

Note that the definition uses classical communication, which is equivalent to quantum communication up to a factor of
2
when entanglement is free.

Since the ensemble is classical, Alice can always retain the information in the registers
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n}
, so, without loss of generality, we assume the following equality throughout the discussion.

\mathrm{Tr}_{T}\left(\mathcal{E}\left(\rho_{X_{1}C_{1}}\otimes\rho_{X_{2}C_{2}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{X_{n}C_{n}}\otimes\theta_{E_{A}}\right)\right)=\rho_{X_{1}C_{1}}\otimes\rho_{X_{2}C_{2}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{X_{n}C_{n}}.

(6)

The following theorem shows a lower bound on the rate of communication
R

required for the task.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\rho_{XC}
be as given in Definition
1
,
n
a
natural number, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. For any
(n,R,\varepsilon)
-
entanglement-assisted blind distribution compression, it holds that

R\;\geq\min_{\mathcal{F}:C\rightarrow CC^{\prime}}\left(\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\tau}\right)+\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X\>\!:\>\!C\right)_{\rho}-\varepsilon\log|X|-1,

where
\tau_{XCC^{\prime}}:=\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})
and the map
\mathcal{F}
must satisfy
\Delta\left(\tau_{XC^{\prime}},\rho_{XC^{\prime}}\right)\leq\varepsilon
and
\tau_{XC}=\rho_{XC}
.

Proof.

For brevity, set
\textbf{X}^{n}=X_{1}X_{2}\ldots X_{n}
,
\textbf{C}^{n}=C_{1}C_{2}\ldots C_{n}
and
\textbf{C}^{\prime n}=C^{\prime}_{1}C^{\prime}_{2}\ldots C^{\prime}_{n}
.
Let

\sigma_{\textbf{X}^{n}\textbf{C}^{n}TE_{B}}
be the state after Alice’s encoding,
and
\tau_{\textbf{X}^{n}\textbf{C}^{n}\textbf{C}^{\prime n}}
be the final quantum state at the end of the protocol.
Observe that

nR\;=\;\log|T|\;\geq\;\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(\textbf{X}^{n}\textbf{C}^{n}\>\!:\>\!T\>\!\middle|\>\!E_{B}\right)_{\sigma}\;\overset{(a)}{=}\;\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(\textbf{X}^{n}\textbf{C}^{n}\>\!:\>\!TE_{B}\right)_{\sigma}\;\overset{(b)}{\geq}\;\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(\textbf{X}^{n}\textbf{C}^{n}\>\!:\>\!\textbf{C}^{\prime n}\right)_{\tau}.

(7)

The equality
(a)
in (
7
) follows from the fact that

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(\textbf{X}^{n}\textbf{C}^{n}\>\!:\>\!E_{B}\right)_{\sigma}=0
. We apply the data processing
inequality to obtain
(b)
. Note also from this step onwards,
entanglement no longer plays a role in the proof. Now, consider

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(\textbf{X}^{n}\textbf{C}^{n}\>\!:\>\!\textbf{C}^{\prime n}\right)_{\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}C_{i}\>\!:\>\!\textbf{C}^{\prime n}\>\!\middle|\>\!X_{1}\dots X_{i-1}C_{1}\ldots C_{i-1}\right)_{\tau}

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}C_{i}\>\!:\>\!\textbf{C}^{\prime n}X_{1}\dots X_{i-1}C_{1}\ldots C_{i-1}\right)_{\tau}-\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}C_{i}\>\!:\>\!X_{1}\dots X_{i-1}C_{1}\ldots C_{i-1}\right)_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}C_{i}\>\!:\>\!\textbf{C}^{\prime n}X_{1}\dots X_{i-1}C_{1}\ldots C_{i-1}\right)_{\tau}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}C_{i}\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}_{i}\right)_{\tau}.

In (
8
), the equality
(c)
holds since (
6
) ensures that

\tau_{X_{i}C_{i}X_{1}\dots X_{i-1}C_{1}\ldots C_{i-1}}=\rho_{X_{i}C_{i}X_{1}\dots X_{i-1}C_{1}\ldots C_{i-1}}=\rho_{X_{i}C_{i}}\otimes\rho_{X_{1}\dots X_{i-1}C_{1}\ldots C_{i-1}},

and the last step follows from the data processing inequality.
From (
4
), we have
\Delta\left(\tau_{X_{i}C^{\prime}_{i}},\rho_{X_{i}C^{\prime}_{i}}\right)\leq\varepsilon
. Thus, using the Alicki-Fannes-Winter inequality (Fact
5
), we obtain

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}C_{i}\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}_{i}\right)_{\tau}=\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C_{i}\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}_{i}\>\!\middle|\>\!X_{i}\right)_{\tau}+\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}_{i}\right)_{\tau}\geq\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C_{i}\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}_{i}\>\!\middle|\>\!X_{i}\right)_{\tau}+\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}\>\!:\>\!C_{i}\right)_{\rho}-\varepsilon\log|X|-1.

(9)

Combining (
7
)-(
9
),
we obtain

nR\geq n\min_{i}\left(\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C_{i}\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}_{i}\>\!\middle|\>\!X_{i}\right)_{\tau}+\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X_{i}\>\!:\>\!C_{i}\right)_{\rho}-\varepsilon\log|X|-1\right).

We can now convert the above asymptotic inequality to a single-letter bound.
For an
i
that achieves the minimum, define
\mathcal{F}_{i}
to be the map that acts on register
C_{i}
as follows. It first creates the state
\rho_{X_{1}C_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{X_{i-1}C_{i-1}}\otimes\rho_{X_{i+1}C_{i+1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{X_{n}C_{n}}\otimes\theta_{E_{A}E_{B}}
. Then it applies
\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}
and traces out registers
X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1},X_{i+1},\ldots,X_{n}
,
C_{1},\ldots,C_{i-1},C_{i+1},\ldots,C_{n}
,
C^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,C^{\prime}_{i-1},C^{\prime}_{i+1},\ldots,C^{\prime}_{n}
. From (
4
), we conclude that

\Delta\left(\mathrm{Tr}_{C_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{i}(\rho_{XC_{i}}),\rho_{XC^{\prime}_{i}}\right)\leq\varepsilon.

Moreover,
\mathrm{Tr}_{C^{\prime}_{i}}\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC_{i}})=\rho_{XC_{i}}
, as the maps
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{D}
do not change the state in registers
XC_{i}
. Since
C_{i}\equiv C
,
C^{\prime}_{i}\equiv C^{\prime}
and
\rho_{XC_{i}}=\rho_{XC}
, the proof concludes.
∎

Theorem
2
shows that the communication cost for entanglement-assisted blind mixed distribution compression can exceed the Holevo information of the distribution
\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X\>\!:\>\!C\right)_{\rho}
.
We now show that this additional cost of
R-\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X\>\!:\>\!C\right)_{\rho}
can be quantitatively bounded by some measure of indistinguishability of the states in the ensemble, and also by some measure of the inability to clone the states.
To proceed with this, consider a simple example of compressing two equiprobable distributions, with

\rho_{XC}=\frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle\langle 0|_{X}\otimes\rho_{C}^{0}+|1\rangle\langle 1|_{X}\otimes\rho_{C}^{1})
. For a map
\mathcal{F}
satisfying
\Delta(\mathrm{Tr}_{C}\left(\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})\right),\rho_{XC^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon
and
\mathrm{Tr}_{C^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})=\rho_{XC}
, let
\tau_{XCC^{\prime}}=\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})
. We will prove the following:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})}}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(D(\tau^{0}_{CC^{\prime}}||\tau^{0}_{C}\otimes\tau^{0}_{C^{\prime}})+D(\tau^{1}_{CC^{\prime}}||\tau^{1}_{C}\otimes\tau^{1}_{C^{\prime}})\right)}

(10)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Delta(\tau^{0}_{CC^{\prime}},\tau^{0}_{C}\otimes\tau^{0}_{C^{\prime}})^{2}+\Delta(\tau^{1}_{CC^{\prime}},\tau^{1}_{C}\otimes\tau^{1}_{C^{\prime}})^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\geq}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\Delta(\tau^{0}_{CC^{\prime}},\tau^{0}_{C}\otimes\tau^{0}_{C^{\prime}})+\Delta(\tau^{1}_{CC^{\prime}},\tau^{1}_{C}\otimes\tau^{1}_{C^{\prime}})\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{\geq}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\Delta(\tau^{0}_{C}\otimes\tau^{0}_{C^{\prime}},\tau^{1}_{C}\otimes\tau^{1}_{C^{\prime}})-\Delta(\tau^{0}_{CC^{\prime}},\tau^{1}_{CC^{\prime}})\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(e)}{\geq}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\Delta(\rho^{0}_{C}\otimes\rho^{0}_{C^{\prime}},\rho^{1}_{C}\otimes\rho^{1}_{C^{\prime}})-\Delta(\tau^{0}_{CC^{\prime}},\tau^{1}_{CC^{\prime}})-2\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(f)}{\geq}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\Delta(\rho^{0}_{C}\otimes\rho^{0}_{C^{\prime}},\rho^{1}_{C}\otimes\rho^{1}_{C^{\prime}})-\Delta(\rho^{0}_{C},\rho^{1}_{C})-2\varepsilon\right).

Here,
(a)
uses the expansion

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\mathcal{F}(\rho)}=\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\tau}=\sum_{x}\,p(x)\,\mathrm{D}\>\!\!\left(\tau^{x}_{CC^{\prime}}\middle\|\tau^{x}_{C}\otimes\tau^{x}_{C^{\prime}}\right),

and
(b)
uses Pinsker’s inequality
(Fact
3
),

(c)
follows from the inequality
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq\frac{(a+b)^{2}}{2}
,
(d)
uses the triangle inequality for trace distance,
(e)
uses the identity
\tau^{x}_{C}=\rho^{x}_{C}
and the inequality
\Delta(\tau_{XC^{\prime}},\rho_{XC^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2}\Delta(\tau^{0}_{C^{\prime}},\rho^{0}_{C^{\prime}})+\frac{1}{2}\Delta(\tau^{1}_{C^{\prime}},\rho^{1}_{C^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon
and
(f)
uses the data-processing inequality (Fact
2
) to conclude that
\Delta(\tau^{0}_{CC^{\prime}},\tau^{1}_{CC^{\prime}})=\Delta\left(\mathcal{F}\left(\rho^{0}_{C}\right),\mathcal{F}\left(\rho^{1}_{C}\right))\leq\Delta(\rho^{0}_{C},\rho^{1}_{C}\right)
.

Furthermore, the above chain of inequalities quantitatively relate the gap between the communication cost and the Holevo information to other quantitative properties of the ensemble. Recall that
\tau^{x}_{C}=\rho^{x}_{C}
, so the RHS of the inequality (c) lower-bounds the gap by a “classical no-cloning bound”, which is the average distance between two copies of
\rho^{x}_{C}
and the actual Alice-Bob joint-output. Furthermore, the RHS of the inequality (f) says that the gap is lower-bounded by the
increase
in distinguishability of
\rho_{C}^{0}
and
\rho_{C}^{1}
if a second copy is available, which is a measure of the indistinguishability between
\rho_{C}^{0,1}
.

This gap can be strictly positive for some ensemble. For example, consider:

\rho^{0}_{C}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho^{1}_{C}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{3}&0\\
0&\frac{2}{3}\end{pmatrix}.

We evaluate

\Delta(\rho^{0}_{C},\rho^{1}_{C})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bigg|\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\bigg|+\bigg|\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{3}\bigg|\right)=\frac{1}{6}

and

\Delta(\rho^{0}_{C}\otimes\rho^{0}_{C},\rho^{1}_{C}\otimes\rho^{1}_{C})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bigg|\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{9}\bigg|+2\bigg|\frac{1}{4}-\frac{2}{9}\bigg|+\bigg|\frac{1}{4}-\frac{4}{9}\bigg|\right)=\frac{7}{36}.

We conclude that
\Delta(\rho^{0}_{C}\otimes\rho^{0}_{C},\rho^{1}_{C}\otimes\rho^{1}_{C})-\Delta(\rho^{0}_{C},\rho^{1}_{C})=\frac{1}{36}
. Thus,

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\mathcal{F}(\rho)}\geq\frac{(1-72\varepsilon)^{2}}{2\cdot 6^{4}}.

This example demonstrates a constant lower bound on the information defect.
In the next section, we develop a large lower bound that
is nearly maximal given the dimension of
C
.

4
Near maximal separation between information cost and the communication cost

In this section, we prove Theorem
1
. The proof will proceed in the following steps.

•

We will simplify the form of the map
\mathcal{F}
by observing that the input and the output are classical. This will also lead to a simpler lower bound than given in Theorem
2
, in terms of stochastic maps.

•

We will show the utility of the simpler lower bound first in the case of zero error. We will show that the stochastic map under consideration is actually doubly stochastic, since one of the two distributions in the ensemble is uniform. Then we apply the Birkoff-von Neumann theorem to show that the only relevant doubly stochastic map is the identity map, which implies our lower bound.

•

Finally, we will consider the case of non-zero error. We will show that the stochastic map under consideration is close to a doubly stochastic map. Then we will analyze the action of this doubly stochastic map on the distributions in the ensemble. Since the doubly stochastic map keeps the distributions approximately unchanged, we bound its distance from the identity map. This will lead to the desired lower bound for the communication cost.

4.1
Stochastic maps and the distribution

Recall the state
\rho_{XC}
, the map
\mathcal{F}
and the state
\tau_{XCC^{\prime}}
in Theorem
2
. We start with the observation that, despite the fact that the map
\mathcal{F}
involves entanglement, the state
\tau_{XCC^{\prime}}=\mathcal{F}\left(\rho_{XC}\right)
can be considered to be completely classical, without loss of generality. To see this, consider the constraints

\Delta(\mathrm{Tr}_{C}\left(\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})\right),\rho_{XC^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon

and
\mathrm{Tr}_{C^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})=\rho_{XC}
as in Theorem
2
. Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
be the map resulting from measuring the register
C^{\prime}
after applying
\mathcal{F}
to
\rho_{XC}
. Then it still holds that

\Delta(\mathrm{Tr}_{C}\left(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\rho_{XC})\right),\rho_{XC^{\prime}})\leq\Delta(\mathrm{Tr}_{C}\left(\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})\right),\rho_{XC^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon

and
\mathrm{Tr}_{C^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\rho_{XC})=\rho_{XC}
, so the constraints are still satisfied, and moreover

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\rho)}\leq\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\mathcal{F}(\rho)}

by the data processing inequality.
Therefore in Theorem
2
, it suffices to restrict to maps
\mathcal{F}
with completely classical
\tau_{XCC^{\prime}}
, which has the form

\tau_{XCC^{\prime}}=\sum_{x,c,c^{\prime}}p_{XCC^{\prime}}(x,c,c^{\prime})|x,c,c^{\prime}\rangle\langle x,c,c^{\prime}|.

(11)

Note that
p_{XCC^{\prime}}(x,c,c^{\prime})
satisfies the Markov chain condition
p_{XC^{\prime}|C=c}(x,c^{\prime})=p_{X|C=c}(x)\,p_{C^{\prime}|C=c}(c^{\prime})
, as the map
\mathcal{F}
produces
C^{\prime}
from
C
. Define a matrix
M
with

M_{c,c^{\prime}}=p_{C^{\prime}|C=c}(c^{\prime}).

(12)

Clearly,
M
is a stochastic matrix:
\forall_{c}\,\sum_{c^{\prime}}M_{c,c^{\prime}}=1
.
Therefore each distribution on
C
is mapped to a distribution on
C^{\prime}
.
The rest of the paper revolves about understanding this stochastic matrix
M
.
First, we view
M
as a ‘channel’ between Alice and Bob that inputs
C
and outputs
C^{\prime}
, and appeal to Fano’s inequality (Fact
6
) to bound the information between
C
and
C^{\prime}
in terms of the probability of
C
being equal to
C^{\prime}
. This is shown in the following claim.

Claim 1
.

Given the states
\rho
,
\tau
and the associated stochastic matrix
M
defined above, it holds that

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\tau}\geq\mathrm{S}\left(C|X\right)_{\rho}-2-\left(1-\sum_{c}p_{C}(c)M_{c,c}+\varepsilon\right)\log\left(|C|\right).

Proof.

Using the definition for conditional entropy, the Markov chain property

\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|CX\right)_{\tau}=\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|C\right)_{\tau}
on
\tau
,
and applying the Alicki-Fannes-Winter inequality along with the condition

\Delta(\mathrm{Tr}_{C}\left(\mathcal{F}(\rho_{XC})\right),\rho_{XC^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon
to

\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|X\right)_{\tau}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|X\right)_{\tau}-\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|CX\right)_{\tau}

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|X\right)_{\tau}-\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|C\right)_{\tau}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|X\right)_{\rho}-\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|C\right)_{\tau}-\Delta(\rho_{XC^{\prime}},\tau_{XC^{\prime}})\log(|C|)-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}\left(C|X\right)_{\rho}-\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|C\right)_{\tau}-\varepsilon\log(|C|)-1.

Using Fano’s inequality (Fact
6
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}\left(C^{\prime}|C\right)_{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\Pr[C\neq C^{\prime}]\log\left(|C|\right)=1+\left(1-\sum_{c}p_{C}(c)M_{c,c}\right)\log\left(|C|\right).

This concludes the proof.
∎

The ensemble.
Using Claim
1
, our strategy to obtain a large gap between the communication cost for compression and the Holevo information is to construct ensembles for which
\sum_{c}p_{C}(c)M_{c,c}\approx 1
, which implies
\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\tau}\approx\mathrm{S}\left(C|X\right)_{\rho}
.
We now define our ensemble using the following notations.
Let
d=|C|
,
c\in\{1,2,\ldots d\}
,
x\in\{0,1\}
, and
p_{X}(0)=p_{X}(1)=\frac{1}{2}
. We choose the states
\rho^{0}_{C}
and
\rho^{1}_{C}
to correspond to the uniform and ‘staircase’ distributions (see Figure
1
and also Theorem
1
), defined as follows.

p_{C|X=0}(c)=\frac{1}{d}\;{=}{:}\;u_{c}\,,\quad p_{C|X=1}(c)=\frac{d-c+1}{\eta}\;{=}{:}\;v_{c}\,,

(14)

where
\eta=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}
. We use
u
to denote the row vector with the
c
-th entry being
u_{c}
, and similarly for
v
.

4.2
Lower bound in the zero error case

To illustrate the principles in the general case, we
first consider the zero-error case where
\varepsilon=0
. In this case, we have
\tau_{XC^{\prime}}=\rho_{XC^{\prime}}
. From the definition of the stochastic matrix
M
in Equation
12
and the choice of distributions
p_{C|X=0},p_{C|X=1}
given above, we arrive at the following set of equations.

uM=u,vM=v\,,

(15)

so both
u,v
are fixed points of the transition matrix
M
. We have the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Using aforementioned definitions, (
15
) implies that

M_{c,c^{\prime}}=1\text{ iff }c^{\prime}=c
(i.e.,
M=\mathrm{I}
).

Proof.

Using the conditions
uM=u
and
u_{c}=\frac{1}{|C|}
for all
c
, (
15
) implies that

\forall_{c^{\prime}}\;\sum_{c}M_{c,c^{\prime}}=1.

Thus the matrix
M
is doubly stochastic. From the Birkoff-von Neumann theorem
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
,
there exist permutation matrices

\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\ldots\Pi_{k}
and a probability distribution
(q_{1},\ldots q_{k})
such that

M=\sum_{i}q_{i}\Pi_{i}.

(16)

Next, we show that
M=\mathrm{I}
.
Without loss of generality,
\Pi_{1}=\mathrm{I}
,
0\leq q_{1}\leq 1
.
Suppose, by contradiction,
q_{1}<1
, so there exists
i\geq 2

with
q_{i}>0
and
\Pi_{i}\neq\mathrm{I}
. Using
vM=v
and
applying (
16
),

\sum_{i}q_{i}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)=v.

Since
v\Pi_{1}=v
, we obtain

\frac{1}{1-q_{1}}\sum_{i=2}^{k}q_{i}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)=v.

(17)

We will argue that this is a contradiction for the vector
v
. A
permutation
\Pi_{i}
is said to act “non-trivially” on an index

j\in[d]
if
j
is not invariant under
\Pi_{i}
.
Let
j_{0}\in[d]
be the smallest index such that at least one of the permutations in
the set
\{\Pi_{i}\}_{i=2}^{k}
act non-trivially on
j_{0}
. Since
\Pi_{i}\neq\mathrm{I}
for

i=2,3,\cdots,k
, such an index
j_{0}
must
exist. This implies the following:

1.

\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)_{j}=v_{j}
for all
j<j_{0}
, that is, all the permutations act trivially on indices smaller than
j_{0}
.

2.

\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)_{j_{0}}\leq v_{j_{0}}
, as any permutation
\Pi_{i}
swaps the element
v_{j_{0}}
with a potentially smaller element
v_{j}
.

3.

There is a permutation
\Pi_{i_{0}}
such that
\left(v\Pi_{i_{0}}\right)_{j_{0}}<v_{j_{0}}
, by the definition of
j_{0}
.

But items
2,3
jointly contradict what is implied by the
j_{0}
-th entry of the vector equality in (
17
):

\frac{1}{1-q_{1}}\sum_{i=2}^{k}q_{i}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)_{j_{0}}=v_{j_{0}}.

Hence we must have
q_{1}=1
and
M=\mathrm{I}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We conclude this subsection with a lower bound on the rate in the zero
error case. When
\varepsilon=0
, Theorem
3
implies that

\sum_{c}p_{C}(c)M_{c,c}=1
. Substituting this into Claim
1
,

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\tau}\geq S(C|X)_{\rho}-\varepsilon\log(|C|)
.
Substituting this into Theorem
2
,

R\geq\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X\>\!:\>\!C\right)_{\rho}+S(C|X)_{\rho}-\varepsilon(\log(|C|)+1)-1\approx\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(X\>\!:\>\!C\right)_{\rho}+\alpha\log(|C|)

for some constant
\alpha
.
In the next subsection, we proceed to the case of small error
\varepsilon
.
We will generalize the intuition from the zero-error case in which
v

is a vertex of the polytope formed by the convex hull of

\{v\Pi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
.

4.3
Lower bound for the case of non-zero error

In this case, Theorem
2
ensures that
\Delta\left(\tau_{XC^{\prime}},\rho_{XC^{\prime}}\right)\leq\varepsilon
. Using the distribution
p_{XCC^{\prime}}
from Equation
11
, this translates to

\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)\sum_{c^{\prime}}\,\Bigl|\,p_{C|X=x}(c^{\prime})-p_{C^{\prime}|X=x}(c^{\prime})\Bigr|\leq 2\varepsilon.

For the rest of this paper, we continue to use the symbols
M,u,v
defined earlier (see Equations
12
and
14
).
Translating the above in terms of
M,u,v
,
and using the fact
p_{X}(0)=p_{X}(1)=\frac{1}{2}
,

\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\|u-uM\|_{1}+\|v-vM\|_{1}\bigr)\leq 2\varepsilon.

In particular,

\|u-uM\|_{1}\leq 4\varepsilon~~{\rm and}~~\|v-vM\|_{1}\leq 4\varepsilon.

(18)

We are ready to obtain our lower bound in spirits similar to the
zero-error case. First, we will use the condition concerning
u
in
(
18
) to approximate
M
by a doubly
stochastic matrix
N
(Lemma
1
part (a)). Then we
approximate
vM
by
vN
(Lemma
1
part (b)). With this
approximation, and with the condition on
v
in (
18
),
we lower bound the identity component of
N
which in turns lower
bound
N_{c,c}
and then
M_{c,c}
and the last bound gives us the
desired lower bound on the communication cost for the compression.

Lemma 1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
M
as defined before. There exists a doubly stochastic matrix
N
such that

\displaystyle{\bf{\rm(a)}}

\displaystyle\forall_{c,c^{\prime}}\;|N_{c,c^{\prime}}-M_{c,c^{\prime}}|\leq 12d\varepsilon\,,

\displaystyle{\bf{\rm(b)}}

\displaystyle\|vN-vM\|_{1}\leq 12d\varepsilon\,.

(19)

Proof.

Rewriting the condition
\|u-uM\|_{1}\leq 4\varepsilon
from (
18
)
using
u_{c}=\frac{1}{d}
for all
c
,

\sum_{c^{\prime}}\;\biggl|\sum_{c}M_{c,c^{\prime}}-1\biggr|\leq 4d\varepsilon\,.

(20)

Thus, the matrix
M
is a stochastic matrix, but “approximately” a doubly
stochastic matrix.
In particular, define
\alpha_{c^{\prime}}=\sum_{c}M_{c,c^{\prime}}-1
which measures
how far
M
deviates from being doubly stochastic.
Using (
20
),

\forall_{c^{\prime}}~~|\alpha_{c^{\prime}}|\leq 4d\varepsilon\,.

(21)

We can now follow the idea from
[
47
]
to find
a doubly stochastic matrix
N
that approximates
M
.
Define

N_{c,c^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(M_{c,c^{\prime}}+\frac{4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d}\right).

First, note that the entries of
N_{c,c^{\prime}}
are non-negative, as

M_{c,c^{\prime}}\geq 0
and

4d\varepsilon\geq\alpha_{c^{\prime}}
for all
c^{\prime}
(according to (
21
)).
Second,
N
is stochastic:

\displaystyle\sum_{c^{\prime}}N_{c,c^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{c^{\prime}}M_{c,c^{\prime}}+\sum_{c^{\prime}}\frac{4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(1+4d\varepsilon\right)=1,

where we have used the stochastic property of
M
to substitute

\sum_{c^{\prime}}M_{c,c^{\prime}}=1
, and also
\sum_{c^{\prime}}\alpha_{c^{\prime}}=\sum_{c,c^{\prime}}M_{c,c^{\prime}}-d=0
.
Next we show that
N
is doubly stochastic.

\displaystyle\sum_{c}N_{c,c^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{c}M_{c,c^{\prime}}+\sum_{c}\frac{4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(1+\alpha_{c^{\prime}}+4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}\right)=1.

Now, we bound the entry-wise difference between
M
and
N
. Consider

\displaystyle N_{c,c^{\prime}}-M_{c,c^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(M_{c,c^{\prime}}+\frac{4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d}\right)-M_{c,c^{\prime}}

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(\frac{4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d}-4d\varepsilon M_{c,c^{\prime}}\right)

Thus,

\displaystyle|N_{c,c^{\prime}}-M_{c,c^{\prime}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(\bigg|\frac{4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d}\bigg|+4d\varepsilon\bigg|M_{c,c^{\prime}}\bigg|\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}

\displaystyle 8\varepsilon+4d\varepsilon\leq 12d\varepsilon,

where
(a)
uses
|M_{c,c^{\prime}}|\leq 1
and

|4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}|\leq 8d\varepsilon
, in turns followed from
(
21
). This proved claim (a).

For claim (b), define a matrix
W
as

W_{c,c^{\prime}}=\frac{4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d(1+4d\varepsilon)}
.
Equation (
22
) states that

N-M=W-\frac{4d\varepsilon}{1+4d\varepsilon}\,M\,.

Thus,

\displaystyle\bigl\|vN-vM\bigr\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\;vW-\frac{4d\varepsilon}{1+4d\varepsilon}\;vM\;\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|vW\right\|_{1}+\frac{4d\varepsilon}{1+4d\varepsilon}\;\left\|vM\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}

\displaystyle\|vW\|_{1}+\frac{4d\varepsilon}{1+4d\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+4d\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{c^{\prime}}\,\bigg|\,\frac{4d\varepsilon{-}\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d}\,\biggl(\sum_{c}v_{c}\biggr)\,\bigg|\right)+\frac{4d\varepsilon}{1+4d\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{c^{\prime}}\bigg|\frac{4d\varepsilon-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}}{d}\bigg|+4d\varepsilon

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}

\displaystyle 8d\varepsilon+4d\varepsilon\leq 12d\varepsilon

In the above, step
(a)
uses the fact that
vM
is a probability distribution so
\|vM\|_{1}=1
and step
(b)
uses (
20
) to substitute
\sum_{c^{\prime}}|\alpha_{c^{\prime}}|\leq 4d\varepsilon
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove our main result, using the condition

\|v-vM\|_{1}\leq 4\varepsilon

from (
18
).

Theorem 4
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
M
as defined before.
It holds that

\forall_{c}~~M_{c,c}\geq 1-24d^{4}\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
N
be the doubly stochastic matrix as promised in Lemma
1
. We start with

\displaystyle\|v-vN\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|v-vM\|_{1}+\|vM-vN\|_{1}

(23)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}

\displaystyle 4\varepsilon+12d\varepsilon\leq 16d\varepsilon.

Here,
(a)
uses (
18
) and Lemma
1
.

Now, we apply the Birkoff-von Neumann theorem to write
N=\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\Pi_{i}
, where
\Pi_{1}=\mathrm{I}
and
\Pi_{2},\cdots,\Pi_{k}
are
permutation matrices not equal to the identity.
If
q_{1}=1
then we are done
so assume
q_{1}<1
, and there exists
i\geq 2
with
q_{i}>0
.
We lower bound
\|v-vN\|_{1}
as follows.

\displaystyle\|v-vN\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg\|v-\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)\bigg\|_{1}=\bigg\|v-q_{1}v-\sum_{i=2}^{k}q_{i}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)\bigg\|_{1}

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg\|(1-q_{1})v-\sum_{i=2}^{k}q_{i}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)\bigg\|_{1}=(1-q_{1})\bigg\|v-\sum_{i=2}^{k}\frac{q_{i}}{1-q_{1}}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)\bigg\|_{1}.

The last expression in (
24
) is lower bounded by the minimum
\ell_{1}
distance between the vector
v
and the convex hull of the vectors
\{v\Pi_{i}\}_{i=2}^{k}
. As shown in Claim
2
below, this distance is lower bounded by

\bigg\|v-\sum_{i=2}^{k}\frac{q_{i}}{1-q_{1}}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)\bigg\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{v_{1}}\left(\sum_{j}v^{2}_{j}-\max_{i>1}\left(\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)_{j}\right)\right).

From Claim
3
,

\max_{i>1}\left(\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)_{j}\right)\leq\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}.

This implies

\bigg\|v-\sum_{i=2}^{k}\frac{q_{i}}{1-q_{1}}\left(v\Pi_{i}\right)\bigg\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{v_{1}\eta^{2}}.

From (
24
), we conclude

\|v-vN\|_{1}\geq(1-q_{1})\frac{1}{v_{1}\eta^{2}},

and combining this with (
23
), we find

(1-q_{1})\frac{1}{v_{1}\eta^{2}}\leq 16d\varepsilon\implies 1-q_{1}\leq 16d\varepsilon v_{1}\eta^{2}\leq 16d^{4}\varepsilon.

(25)

Since
N_{c,c}\geq q_{1}
, we finally conclude from Lemma
1
and (
25
) that

M_{c,c}\geq N_{c,c}-12d\varepsilon\geq q_{1}-12d\varepsilon\geq 1-24d^{4}\varepsilon.

Here we used
d\geq 2
to substitute
16d^{4}\varepsilon+12d\varepsilon\leq 24d^{4}\varepsilon
. This completes the proof.
∎

The following claims were used in the proof.

Claim 2
.

Let
v,w_{1},\ldots w_{s}
be vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with non-negative entries. Suppose
v_{1}
is the largest entry of
v
, and
v_{1}>0
. It holds that

\min_{r_{1},\ldots r_{s}\in[0,1]:\sum_{i}r_{i}=1}\|v-\sum_{i}r_{i}w_{i}\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{v_{1}}\left(\sum_{j}v^{2}_{j}-\max_{i}\left(\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(w_{i}\right)_{j}\right)\right)

Proof.

We use the fact that
\|u\|_{1}=\max_{m_{1},\ldots m_{n}\in[-1,1]}\sum_{j}u_{j}m_{j}
to write

\displaystyle\min_{r_{1},\ldots r_{s}\in[0,1]:\sum_{i}r_{i}=1}\|v-\sum_{i}r_{i}w_{i}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\min_{r_{1},\ldots r_{s}\in[0,1]:\sum_{i}r_{i}=1}\quad\max_{m_{1},\ldots m_{n}\in[-1,1]}\left(\sum_{j}m_{j}\left(v_{j}-\sum_{i}r_{i}\left(w_{i}\right)_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{r_{1},\ldots r_{s}\in[0,1]:\sum_{i}r_{i}=1}\quad\max_{m_{1},\ldots m_{n}\in[-1,1]}\left(\sum_{j}m_{j}v_{j}-\sum_{i,j}r_{i}m_{j}\left(w_{i}\right)_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\max_{m_{1},\ldots m_{n}\in[-1,1]}\quad\min_{r_{1},\ldots r_{s}\in[0,1]:\sum_{i}r_{i}=1}\left(\sum_{j}m_{j}v_{j}-\sum_{i,j}r_{i}m_{j}\left(w_{i}\right)_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\max_{m_{1},\ldots m_{n}\in[-1,1]}\left(\sum_{j}m_{j}v_{j}-\max_{i}\left(\sum_{j}m_{j}\left(w_{i}\right)_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\geq}\frac{1}{v_{1}}\left(\sum_{j}v^{2}_{j}-\max_{i}\left(\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(w_{i}\right)_{j}\right)\right).

Here
(a)
uses the minimax theorem
[
48
]
,
(b)
chooses the optimal
\{r_{1},\ldots r_{s}\}
to maximize

\sum_{i,j}r_{i}m_{j}\left(w_{i}\right)_{j}=\sum_{i}r_{i}\left(\sum_{j}m_{j}\left(w_{i}\right)_{j}\right)

and
(c)
chooses
m_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{v_{1}}\in[0,1]
. This completes the proof.
∎

Claim 3
.

Let
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots v_{n}\}
be a vector and define
\gamma:=\min_{i\neq j}|v_{i}-v_{j}|
. It holds that

\max_{\Pi\neq\mathrm{I}}\left(\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(v\Pi\right)_{j}\right)=\sum_{j}v_{j}^{2}-\gamma^{2},

where the maximization is over all permutations
\Pi
not equal to identity. In particular, for the vector
v
in our ensemble (see Equation
14
, with
n
replaced by
d
), we have
\gamma=\frac{1}{\eta}
and

\max_{\Pi\neq\mathrm{I}}\left(\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(v\Pi\right)_{j}\right)=\sum_{j}v_{j}^{2}-\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}.

Proof.

We recall that
\Pi
can be decomposed as a product of disjoint cycles
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots C_{s}
. If
\Pi
leaves an index unchanged, we will view the action of
\Pi
on this index as a ‘trivial cycle’ of size
1
. Let
L_{k}
be the set of indices on which the cycle
C_{k}
acts. Since the cycles are disjoint,

\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(v\Pi\right)_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\sum_{j\in L_{k}}v_{j}\left(vC_{k}\right)_{j}.

(26)

Consider the expression
\sum_{j\in L_{k}}v_{j}\left(vC_{k}\right)_{j}
. Let
|L_{k}|
be the number of elements in
L_{k}
. Relabel the elements in
L_{k}
with integers
\{0,1,\ldots|L_{k}|-1\}
in a manner that
C_{k}(m)=m+s\mod|L_{k}|
, for some integer
s
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in L_{k}}v_{j}\left(vC_{k}\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{|L_{k}|-1}v_{m}v_{m+s\mod|L_{k}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{|L_{k}|-1}\left(v^{2}_{m}+v^{2}_{m+s\mod|L_{k}|}-\left(v_{m}-v_{m+s\mod|L_{k}|}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{|L_{k}|-1}v_{m}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{|L_{k}|-1}(v_{m}-v_{m+s\mod|L_{k}|})^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{|L_{k}|-1}v_{m}^{2}-\gamma^{2}\cdot\lfloor|L_{k}|/2\rfloor=\sum_{j\in L_{k}}v_{j}^{2}-\gamma^{2}\cdot\lfloor|L_{k}|/2\rfloor.

Here
(a)
follows by noticing that
(v_{m}-v_{m+s\mod|L_{k}|})^{2}\geq\gamma^{2}
. Combining with (
26
), we find that

\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(v\Pi\right)_{j}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{s}\left(\sum_{j\in L_{k}}v_{j}^{2}-\gamma^{2}\cdot\lfloor|L_{k}|/2\rfloor\right)=\sum_{j}v_{j}^{2}-\gamma^{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{s}\lfloor|L_{k}|/2\rfloor\right).

Since
\Pi
is not the identity permutation, there is a
C_{k}
of length at least
2
. Thus,
\sum_{k=1}^{s}\lfloor|L_{k}|/2\rfloor\geq 1
and

\max_{\Pi}\left(\sum_{j}v_{j}\left(v\Pi\right)_{j}\right)\leq\sum_{j}v_{j}^{2}-\gamma^{2}.

To show that the right hand side is achieved, choose
\Pi
to be any permutation that swaps a pair of consecutive indices and leaves every other index unchanged. This completes the proof.
∎

4.4
Final lower bound

Theorem
4
says that
M_{c,c}\geq 1-24d^{4}\varepsilon
for
all
c
. If we choose
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{24d^{4}\log d}
, we conclude that
for all
c

M_{c,c}\geq 1-\frac{1}{\log d}.

Thus, the lower bound in Claim
1
takes the form

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\tau}\;\geq\;\mathrm{S}\left(C|X\right)_{\rho}-2-\left(1-\sum_{c}p_{C}(c)M_{c,c}+\varepsilon\right)\log\left(d\right)\;\geq\;\mathrm{S}\left(C|X\right)_{\rho}-4.

The conditional entropy can be evaluated to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}\left(C|X\right)_{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{S}(C)_{\rho^{0}}+\mathrm{S}(C)_{\rho^{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log d+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{c}v_{c}\log\frac{1}{v_{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log d+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{c}v_{c}\log\frac{\eta}{d-c+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log d+\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\eta-\sum_{c}v_{c}\log(d-c+1)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\geq}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log d+\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\eta-\log\left(\sum_{c}v_{c}(d-c+1)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log d+\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\eta-\log\left(\sum_{c}\frac{(d-c+1)^{2}}{\eta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log d+\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\frac{d(d+1)}{2}-\log\left(\frac{d(d+1)(2d+1)}{3d(d+1)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log d+\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\frac{3d(d+1)}{4d+2}\right)\geq(\log d)-1.

Here
(a)
follows from concavity of log and
(b)
follows by substituting the value of
\eta
. This leads to the lower bound

\mathrm{I}\>\!\!\left(C\>\!:\>\!C^{\prime}\>\!\middle|\>\!X\right)_{\tau}\geq(\log d)-5.

Thus, from Theorem
2
, we have a state
\rho_{XC}
such that the asymptotic rate of communication for local error
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{24d^{4}\log d}
is at least
(\log d)-7
while the Holevo information is at most
1
.
Note that
d
is independent of the number of copies
n
, and
\varepsilon
is independent of
n
and only depends on
d
.

Appendix A
Koashi-Imoto characterization at non-vanishing error

Koashi and Imoto
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
provide a
characterization of the optimal rates of quantum communication,
classical communication, and entanglement for

(n,R,\varepsilon)
- blind compression of general quantum states
(defined similarly to the task of blind distribution compression) in
the limit where
n\rightarrow\infty
and the local error

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
These results apply to both the unassisted and the
entanglement-assisted scenarios.

The Koashi-Imoto characterization of
(n,R,\varepsilon)
- blind compression
is achieved by the following structure theorem for any ensemble

\{p_{i},\rho_{i}\}
. There exists a decomposition of the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
as
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{J}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{K}
,
in a manner that
\rho_{i}=\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{i,\ell}\rho^{(i,\ell)}_{J}\otimes\rho^{\ell}_{K}
.
Here,
\rho^{(i,\ell)}_{J}
and
\rho^{\ell}_{K}
are normalized density
matrices acting on
\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{J}
and
\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{K}
respectively,
and
q_{i,\ell}\geq 0
,
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{i,\ell}=1
. Furthermore,
for each
\ell
,
\{\rho^{(1,\ell)}_{J},\rho^{(2,\ell)}_{J},\cdots\}

cannot be expressed in a simultaneously block-diagonal form.
The register
K
is viewed
as redundant, as it has no dependence on
i
. Without loss of
generality, one can restrict the attention to ensembles which have no
redundant register
K
, as the register
K
can be removed by Alice
and later be generated by Bob without communication between them.
The average state
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{i}
can also be written as

\rho=\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{L}p^{(\ell)}\rho^{(\ell)}_{J}
where
p^{(\ell)}=\sum_{i}p_{i}q_{i,\ell}
and
\rho^{(\ell)}_{J}={1\over p^{(\ell)}}\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}q_{i,\ell}\rho^{(i,\ell)}_{J}\right)
. Furthermore, the authors proved that
any channel
\mathcal{E}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(\rho_{i})=\rho_{i}
acts as
the identity map on
J
.

The characterization in the case of zero error (
\varepsilon=0
) is now immediate. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the combined encoding and decoding map in the protocol. Since the protocol makes no error,
\mathcal{P}(\rho_{i})=\rho_{i}
for all
i
and hence
\mathcal{P}
acts as the identity map on the
J
register, leading to
\log(|J|L)
as the optimal rate in this case.

To address the regime
n\rightarrow\infty,\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,
Koashi and Imoto
[
16
]
define
I_{C}=-\sum_{\ell}p^{(\ell)}\log p^{(\ell)}
and

I_{Q}=\sum_{\ell}p^{(\ell)}S\left(\rho^{(\ell)}_{J}\right)
.
The ensemble can be compressed at the rates of
I_{C}
bits and

I_{Q}
qubits combined, whereas
I_{C}+I_{Q}=S(\rho)
. To prove optimality of this rate, they introduce two error functions

f(\mathcal{P})=1-\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathrm{F}(\rho_{i},\mathcal{P}(\rho_{i}))

and

g(\mathcal{P})=H(\lambda)+\lambda\log(d-1),

where
\lambda=1-\sum_{a}r(a)\mathrm{Tr}(|a\rangle\langle a|\,\mathcal{P}(|a\rangle\langle a|))
and
|a\rangle,r(a)
are the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the average state of the ensemble,
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{i}
. The parameter
f(\mathcal{P})
captures the error
\varepsilon
(up to the difference that we are working in trace distance, whereas they work in fidelity). The parameter
g(\mathcal{P})
appears in their lower bound
R\geq S(\rho)-g(\mathcal{P})
on the communication cost. They give a continuity argument that if
f(\mathcal{P})\rightarrow 0
, then
g(\mathcal{P})\rightarrow 0
.

For non-vanishing error, the dependence of
\lambda
on
f(\mathcal{P})
and
in turn, the dependence of
g(\mathcal{P})
on
f(\mathcal{P})
(via
\lambda
) is
important. While
\lambda\rightarrow 0
as
f(\mathcal{P})\rightarrow 0
,
it is unclear how quickly
\lambda
vanishes as
f(\mathcal{P})\rightarrow 0
. Consequently,
g(\mathcal{P})
need not vanish quickly enough to
provide a strong lower bound.
In our context, we construct a protocol in Theorem

5
exhibiting this sensitivity. We show an example
where
g(\mathcal{P})
becomes close to
\log d
, even if
f(\mathcal{P})\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
.
Thus we know from the example that for two distributions, the lower
bound proved by Koashi and Imoto only works for error substantially
smaller than
{1\over\sqrt{d}}
.

Another reason to expect that the Koashi-Imoto characterization is sensitive to errors, is as follows. Consider an equiprobable ensemble of two states
\rho
and
\sigma
. When the error
\varepsilon
(defined in (
5
)) is finite, we have the conditions
\Delta(\mathcal{P}(\rho),\rho)\leq 2\varepsilon,\Delta(\mathcal{P}(\sigma),\sigma)\leq 2\varepsilon
. Does this guarantee that
\rho
and
\sigma
are respectively close to states
\tilde{\rho}
and
\tilde{\sigma}
that are fixed points of
\mathcal{P}
? If this were the case, we could apply the Koashi-Imoto decomposition to
\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\sigma}
to obtain a large lower bound for the rate for finite
\varepsilon
. Unfortunately this is not true, as witnessed by the channel
\mathcal{E}(\tau)=(1-\varepsilon)\tau+\varepsilon\frac{\mathrm{I}}{d}
. This channel satisfies
\Delta(\mathcal{E}(\tau),\tau)\leq\varepsilon
for all
\tau
, but its only fixed point is
\frac{\mathrm{I}}{d}
.

Our arguments are made precise in the following protocol for the blind compression of any pair of commuting states
\rho,\sigma
, with rate significantly less than
\log(d)
for non vanishing local error.

Theorem 5
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}),\gamma\in(0,1)
. Consider the local error model. Given two commuting states
\rho,\sigma
, there exists an
(n,R,\delta+\gamma)
blind distribution compression protocol
\mathcal{P}
with
R=2\log\log(\frac{d}{\gamma})+2\log\frac{1}{\delta}+3
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality,
\rho,\sigma
are both diagonal in the
computational basis, so, they can be written as
\rho=\sum_{a}p(a)|a\rangle\langle a|
and
\sigma=\sum_{a}q(a)|a\rangle\langle a|
where
p(a),q(a)\geq 0
,
\sum_{a}p(a)=\sum_{a}q(a)=1
. Let

u=\Bigl\lceil\frac{\log\frac{d}{\gamma}}{\log\frac{1}{1-\delta}}\Bigr\rceil
, so that
(1-\delta)^{u}\leq\frac{\gamma}{d}
.
For
i,j\in\{1,2,\cdots,u\}
, define the sets

\displaystyle T_{i,j}

\displaystyle:=\{\,a\,:\,p(a)\!\in\!((1{-}\delta)^{i},(1{-}\delta)^{i-1}]\,,\;q(a)\!\in\!((1{-}\delta)^{j},(1{-}\delta)^{j-1}]\,\}\,,

\displaystyle T_{i,u+1}

\displaystyle:=\{\,a\,:\,p(a)\!\in\!((1{-}\delta)^{i},(1{-}\delta)^{i-1}]\,,\;q(a)\leq(1{-}\delta)^{u}\,\}\,,

\displaystyle T_{u+1,j}

\displaystyle:=\{\,a:\,p(a)\leq(1{-}\delta)^{u}\,,\;q(a)\!\in\!((1{-}\delta)^{j},(1{-}\delta)^{j-1}]\}

\displaystyle T_{u+1,u+1}

\displaystyle:=\{\,a:\,p(a)\leq(1{-}\delta)^{u}\,,\;q(a)\leq(1{-}\delta)^{u}\}\,.

The protocol
\mathcal{P}
is as follows.

•

Alice receives a sample
a
. She finds the unique
(i,j)
such that
a\in T_{i,j}
and communicates
(i,j)
to Bob.

•

Receiving
(i,j)
, Bob outputs an
a^{\prime}
drawn uniformly from
T_{i,j}
.

If the input given to Alice is drawn from
\rho
(
\sigma
), let the output produced by Bob be drawn from
\rho^{\prime}
(
\sigma^{\prime}
).
The analysis of the protocol is as follows.

•

Communication cost
: Since there are at most
(u+1)^{2}

(i,j)
’s, it suffices for Alice to communicate

2\log(u+1)\leq 2((\log u)+1)\leq 2\log\log\frac{d}{\gamma}+2\log\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{1{-}\delta}}+3\leq 2\log\log\frac{d}{\gamma}+2\log\frac{1}{\delta}+3

bits to Bob.
Note that we have used the inequality
\frac{1}{-\log(1-\delta)}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}
derived from the Taylor series expansion of
\log(1-\delta)
.

•

Error analysis
: Let
p(T_{i,j}):=\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}p(a)
and
q(T_{i,j}):=\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}q(a)
.
We can rewrite
\rho
and
\sigma
as

\rho=\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}p(T_{i,j})\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{p(a)}{p(T_{i,j})}|a\rangle\langle a|,\quad\sigma=\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}q(T_{i,j})\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{q(a)}{q(T_{i,j})}|a\rangle\langle a|.

Observe from the protocol that

\rho^{\prime}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}p(T_{i,j})\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}|a\rangle\langle a|,\quad\sigma^{\prime}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}q(T_{i,j})\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}|a\rangle\langle a|.

So,

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho,\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta\bigg(\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}p(T_{i,j})\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{p(a)}{p(T_{i,j})}|a\rangle\langle a|\,,\;\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}p(T_{i,j})\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}|a\rangle\langle a|\bigg)

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}p(T_{i,j})\;\Delta\bigg(\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{p(a)}{p(T_{i,j})}|a\rangle\langle a|\,,\;\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}|a\rangle\langle a|\bigg)

By definition of the set
T_{i,j}
with
i\leq u
, it holds that
1-\delta\leq\frac{p(a_{1})}{p(a_{2})}\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta}
for all
a_{1},a_{2}\in T_{i,j}
. Thus, for all
a\in T_{i,j}
with
i\leq u
,

(1-\delta)\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}\leq\frac{p(a)}{p(T_{i,j})}\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}\implies\bigg|\frac{p(a)}{p(T_{i,j})}-\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}\bigg|\leq\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}\,.

So,

\Delta\bigg(\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{p(a)}{p(T_{i,j})}|a\rangle\langle a|\,,\,\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}|a\rangle\langle a|\bigg)\leq{1\over 2}\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}\leq\frac{\delta}{2(1-\delta)}\,.

(28)

Furthermore,

\sum_{j=1}^{u+1}p(T_{u+1,j})=\sum_{a:p(a)\leq(1-\delta)^{u}}p(a)\leq(1-\delta)^{u}\cdot d\leq\gamma\,.

(29)

Applying (
28
), (
29
), and
\delta\leq{1\over 2}
to (
27
),

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{u}\sum_{j=1}^{u+1}\,p(T_{i,j})\,\frac{\delta}{2(1-\delta)}+\sum_{j=1}^{u+1}p(T_{u+1,j})\;\leq\;\frac{\delta}{2(1-\delta)}+\gamma\;\leq\;\delta+\gamma\,.

A similar argument shows that
\Delta(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\leq\delta+\gamma.
This completes the error analysis.

The correctness of the protocol concludes by running the above protocol independently for each copy to obtain an
(n,R,\delta+\gamma)
protocol.
∎

Theorem
5
immediately implies that
g(\mathcal{P})
is close to
S(\rho)
for constant
\delta,\gamma
, else the lower bound
R\geq S(\rho)-g(\mathcal{P})
would contradict the statement of the theorem. We can see this explicitly by evaluating the functions
f(\mathcal{P})
and
g(\mathcal{P})
. Let us continue using the notation in Theorem
5
. Since
\Delta(\rho,\mathcal{P}(\rho))\leq\delta+\gamma
, the Fuchs-van de graaf inequality implies that
\mathrm{F}(\rho,\mathcal{P}(\rho))\geq 1-2\delta-2\gamma
. Similarly,
\mathrm{F}(\sigma,\mathcal{P}(\sigma))\geq 1-2\delta-2\gamma
. Hence
f(\mathcal{P})\leq 2\delta+2\gamma
. Now, for the given ensemble,
r(a)=\frac{1}{2}p(a)+\frac{1}{2}q(a)
.
Then,

\displaystyle 1-\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a}r(a)\,\mathrm{Tr}(|a\rangle\langle a|\cdot\mathcal{P}(|a\rangle\langle a|))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}r(a)\,\mathrm{Tr}\left(|a\rangle\langle a|\cdot\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}\in T_{i,j}}\frac{|a^{\prime}\rangle\langle a^{\prime}|}{|T_{i,j}|}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}r(a)\cdot\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}\leq\max_{a^{\prime}}\,r(a^{\prime})\cdot\sum_{i,j=1}^{u+1}\sum_{a\in T_{i,j}}\frac{1}{|T_{i,j}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{a^{\prime}}\,r(a^{\prime})\cdot(u+1)^{2}\leq\frac{4\log^{2}\frac{d}{\gamma}}{\delta^{2}}\cdot\max_{a^{\prime}}\,r(a^{\prime}).

Suppose
p
and
q
are the uniform and the staircase distributions (Figure
1
). That is,
p(a)=\frac{1}{d}
for all

and
q(a)=\frac{2(d-a+1)}{d(d+1)}=\frac{2}{d}-\frac{2a}{d(d+1)}
(note that these distributions have no redundant part). Then

and

. So,

, even when

.
