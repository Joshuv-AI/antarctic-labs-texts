On a system of multi-component Ginzburg-Landau vortices
Rejeb Hadiji, Jongmin Han, Juhee Sohn

Abstract
We study the asymptotic behavior of solutions for n n -component Ginzburg-Landau equations as ε → 0 {\varepsilon}\to 0 . We prove that the minimizers converges locally in any C k C^{k} -norm to a solution of a system of generalized harmonic map equations.



MSC2000 : 35B40, 35J60, 35Q60.

n
-component Ginzburg-Landau equations, semi-local gauge field model, asymptotic behavior of solutions

Introduction

The classical Ginzburg-Landau model describes the macroscopic theory for phenomena on superconductivity at low temperature.
It is derived from the Helmholtz free energy that consists of a complex order parameter
u
and the magnetic potential
A

[
4
]
.
In the physical literature,
|u|^{2}
represents the density of the superconducting electron
pairs in the superconducting material.
The state
|u|^{2}=0
implies that material remains in normal conducting state.
Whereas the material is superconducting if
|u|^{2}=1
.

The Ginzburg-Landau theory also gives a good insight for understanding various topological defects arising from cosmology.
The superconducting state is achieved at the vacuum level of the potential and it can be regarded as a broken symmetry during a phase transition.
For instance, this idea is realized by the (special) relativistic extension of the planar Ginzburg-Landau theory, called the Abelian-Higgs model
[
8
]
.
This model describes a charged scalar field
\psi
interacting with a
{\mathrm{U}(1)}
gauge field
A
, and allows vortex solutions which are charged magnetically but electrically neutral.

The Abelian-Higgs model is considered in the (2+1)-dimensional Minkowski
space
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
with the metric
\text{diag}(-1,1,1)
. The metric is used to raise or lower indices.
The Lagrangian density for the model is defined as

{\mathcal{L}}_{0}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}D_{\mu}u\overline{D^{\mu}u}-\frac{1}{8{\varepsilon}^{2}}(|u|^{2}-1)^{2}.

(1.1)

Here,
\mu,\nu=0,1,2
,
{\varepsilon}>0
is the Higgs coupling constant,
u:\mathbb{R}^{1,2}\to\mathbb{C}
is the Higgs field and
A_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{1,2}\to\mathbb{R}
is
{\mathrm{U}(1)}
gauge fields.
In addition,
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
represents the electromagnetic field,
D_{\mu}u=\partial_{\mu}u+iA_{\mu}u
is the covariant derivative.
The Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is invariant under the local
{\mathrm{U}(1)}
gauge transform

u\mapsto e^{i\alpha}u,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{\mu}\mapsto A_{\mu}-\partial_{\mu}\alpha

for any smooth function
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{1,2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The Euler-Lagrange equations are

\left\{\begin{aligned} D_{\mu}D^{\mu}u&~=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}(|u|^{2}-1)u\\
\partial^{\nu}F_{\mu\nu}&~=\frac{i}{2{\varepsilon}^{2}}\big[u\overline{D_{\mu}u}-\overline{u}D_{\mu}u\big],\end{aligned}\right.

(1.2)

which are the (special) relativistic Ginzburg-Landau equations.

As a direct generalization of (
1.1
), Vachaspati and Achucarro proposed a new model in
[
12
]
that contains
n
scalar fields:

\Psi=\big(u_{1},\cdots u_{n}\big),{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{where}\hskip 10.00002pt}u_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{1,2}\to\mathbb{C}{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for}\hskip 10.00002pt}k=1,\cdots,n.

The Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is modified as

{\mathcal{L}}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}D_{\mu}\Psi\overline{D^{\mu}\Psi}-\frac{1}{8{\varepsilon}^{2}}\big(|\Psi|^{2}-n\big)^{2}.

(1.3)

It is easy to show that
{\mathcal{L}}
is invariant under the local
{\mathrm{U}(1)}
gauge transformation

\Psi\mapsto e^{i\alpha}\Psi,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{\mu}\mapsto A_{\mu}-\partial_{\mu}\alpha

for any smooth function
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{1,2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
It is also invariant under the global
{\mathrm{SU}(n)}
gauge transformation

\Psi\mapsto e^{i\alpha_{k}\tau^{k}}\Psi,

where
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
for
k=1,\cdots,n^{2}-1
and
\{\tau^{k}\}_{k=1}^{n^{2}-1}
are the generators of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(n)
.
The Euler-Lagrange equations are obtained as

\left\{\begin{aligned} D_{\mu}D^{\mu}u_{k}&~=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}|^{2}-n\Big)u_{k},\\
\partial^{\nu}F_{\mu\nu}&~=\frac{i}{2{\varepsilon}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(u_{j}\overline{D_{\mu}u_{j}}-\overline{u}_{j}D_{\mu}u_{j}\big),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.4)

which we will refer to as
n
-component Ginzburg-Landau equations.

The model (
1.3
) is useful in the study of some issues in cosmology, for instance, the formation of cosmic strings that have both local and global natures
[
12
]
.
So, the string solutions for (
1.3
) are called semilocal.
The semilocal string solutions for (
1.3
) are similar to those for (
1.1
) that reflect the local gauge transformation.
But they have additional features that have some resemblance to global defect.
For further physical implication of the model (
1.3
), one may refer to
[
7
,
12
]
.

In this article, we are interested in static solutions for (
1.2
) and (
1.4
).
In particular, we assume that the electromagnetic fields vanish, that is,
A_{\mu}=0
for
\mu=0,1,2
.
Then, the main topic is to study the asymptotic behavior of solutions for (
1.2
) and (
1.4
) as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
.
For the case (
1.2
), there have been lots of researches on this topic after the seminal work of Bethuel-Brezis-Helein
[
1
,
2
]
.
More specifically, let
{\Omega}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
be a smooth bounded simply connected domain.
Given a smooth map

g:\partial{\Omega}\to S^{1}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptd=\deg(g,\partial{\Omega})

the Ginzburg-Landau equations (
1.2
) with
A_{\mu}=0
reduce to

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}u(1-|u|^{2}){\quad\mbox{in}\quad}\Omega,\\
u&=g{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

The associated energy functional is

E_{{\varepsilon},{\Omega}}^{b}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(1-|u|^{2})^{2}dx.

(1.6)

Then,
E_{{\varepsilon},{\Omega}}^{b}
has a minimizer
u_{{\varepsilon},g}^{b}
in

H^{1}_{g}({\Omega};\mathbb{C})=\big\{u\in H^{1}({\Omega};\mathbb{C}):u=g\mbox{ on }\partial{\Omega}\big\},

that is

E_{{\varepsilon},{\Omega}}^{b}(u_{{\varepsilon},g}^{b})=\inf\{E_{{\varepsilon},{\Omega}}^{b}(u):u\in H^{1}_{g}({\Omega};\mathbb{C})\}.

(1.7)

We often write
H^{1}_{g}
instead of
H^{1}_{g}({\Omega};\mathbb{C})
if there is no confusion.

The asymptotic behavior of solutions of (
1.5
) as
{\varepsilon}\to 0

has attracted lots of interest for three decades.
The behavior of global minimizers for
u_{{\varepsilon},g}^{b}
was studied in detail by
Bethuel, Brezis and Hélein
[
1
,
2
]
.
If
d=0
,
u_{{\varepsilon},g}^{b}
converges to a harmonic map that minimizes

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dx

over the space

H^{1}_{g}({\Omega};S^{1})=\{u\in W^{1,2}({\Omega},S^{1}):u=g\mbox{ on }\partial{\Omega}\}.

This problem has a solution
u_{0}^{b}
satisfying

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u_{0}^{b}&=u_{0}^{b}|\nabla u_{0}^{b}|^{2}\quad\text{on}\quad{\Omega},\\
u_{0}^{b}&=g{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\partial\Omega,\\
|u_{0}^{b}|&=1\quad\text{ on }{\Omega}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.8)

When
d\neq 0
, the analysis is more delicate because
H^{1}_{g}({\Omega};S^{1})=\emptyset
.
There exists a set
\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{d}\}\subset{\Omega}
such that
up to a subsequence,
u_{{\varepsilon},g}^{b}
converges to the map
u_{*}
that satisfies the harmonic map equation
[
2
,
6
]

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u_{*}&=u_{*}|\nabla u_{*}|^{2}\quad\text{on}\quad{\Omega}\backslash\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{d}\},\\
u_{*}&=g{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\partial\Omega,\\
|u_{*}|&=1\quad\text{ on }~{\Omega}\backslash\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{d}\}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.9)

Moreover, we have

u_{*}(x)=\frac{x-a_{1}}{|x-a_{1}|}\cdots\frac{x-a_{d}}{|x-a_{d}|}e^{i\psi(x)},

(1.10)

where
\psi
is harmonic on
{\Omega}
and the singularities
a_{1},\cdots,a_{d}
of
u_{*}
minimizes the associated renormalized energy.
We refer to
[
10
]
for the study of the Ginzburg-Landau model with and without a magnetic fields that describe superconductivity.

Now, we turn to the
n
-component Ginzburg-Landau equations (
1.4
).
If we assume
A_{\mu}=0
, then (
1.4
) is reduced to

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u_{i}&=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}u_{i}\Big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}|^{2}\Big){\quad\mbox{in}\quad}\Omega,\\
u_{i}&=g_{i}{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.11)

for each
i=1,\cdots,n
.
One may regard (
1.11
) as a direct extension of (
1.5
) to
n
-component equations.
Here,
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}:\partial{\Omega}\to S^{1}
are smooth maps such that

\deg(g_{j}):=\deg(g_{j},\partial{\Omega})=d_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for}\hskip 10.00002pt}j=1,\cdots,n.

(1.12)

The system (
1.11
) is the Euler-Lagrange equations of the functional

E_{{\varepsilon},{\Omega}}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\Big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{2}dx

(1.13)

for a pair of maps
(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})\in H^{1}_{g_{1}}\times\cdots\times H^{1}_{g_{n}}
.
For simplicity, we write
E_{\varepsilon}^{b}
and
E_{\varepsilon}
instead of
E_{{\varepsilon},{\Omega}}^{b}
and
E_{{\varepsilon},{\Omega}}
if there is no confusion on the domain
{\Omega}
.
It is easy to check that

\inf\big\{E_{\varepsilon}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}):(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})\in H^{1}_{g_{1}}\times\cdots\times H^{1}_{g_{n}}\big\}

(1.14)

is achieved by some
(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\in H^{1}_{g_{1}}\times\cdots\times H^{1}_{g_{n}}
.
We also denote by
u_{j,{\varepsilon}}^{b}
a minimizer for
E_{\varepsilon}^{b}
on
H^{1}_{g_{j}}
.

The purpose of this paper is to study the asymptotic behavior of minimizers
(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})
as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
.
In this study, there is a remarkable difference between (
1.5
) and (
1.11
).
First, we consider the possible limit equation which could be a
n
-component generalization of (
1.9
) in some sense.
For (
1.5
), if
d\neq 0
, then the limit function
u_{*}
in (
1.10
) has
d
singularities.
However, even for the case
(d_{1},\cdots,d_{n})\neq(0,\cdots,0)
, the limit functions for (
1.11
) turns out to have no singularities.
This difference is related to the nonexistence of singular harmonic map in
H^{1}
.
In fact, we have
H^{1}_{g}({\Omega};S^{1})=\emptyset
for the single equation (
1.5
).
For the system (
1.11
), it is natural to define a function space analogously:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n};{\Omega})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})\in H^{1}_{g_{1}}({\Omega};\mathbb{C})\times\cdots\times H^{1}_{g_{n}}({\Omega};\mathbb{C})~:~\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}|^{2}=n~\text{ a.e. on }{\Omega}\Big\}.

We write
{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
if there is no confusion on domains.

The asymptotic behavior of minimizers for
E_{\varepsilon}
is closely related to the maps in
{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
.
Since it is expected that
|u_{1,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}+\cdots+|u_{n,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\to n
as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
, the limit functions, say,
(u_{1}^{*}\cdots,u_{n}^{*})
will satisfy
|u_{1}^{*}|^{2}+\cdots+|u_{n}^{*}|^{2}=n
a.e. on
{\Omega}
.
Thus, it is important to know whether
{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
is nonempty or not.
We can see that if
\Psi=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})\in{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
, then

\tilde{\Psi}=\Big(\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\cdots,\frac{u_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)~\in~S^{2n-1}.

Since the homotopy group
\pi_{1}(S^{2n+1})
is trivial, there is no topological obstruction and
we expect that the limit problem will not have singularities.
More precisely, the next theorem tells us that
{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
is nonempty if
n\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
d_{j}\geq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq n
, then
{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})\neq\emptyset
.
Furthermore, if

I(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}dx,

(1.15)

the minimization problem

\displaystyle\beta(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})

\displaystyle=\inf\big\{I(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}):(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})\in{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})\big\},

(1.16)

is achieved in
{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
.

Proof.

We choose smooth functions
v_{j}\in H_{g_{j}}^{1}({\Omega};\mathbb{C})
such that
\inf_{\Omega}(|v_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|v_{n}^{2}|)>0
on
{\Omega}
.
Set

w_{j}=\frac{\sqrt{n}v_{j}}{\big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|v_{k}|^{2}\big)^{1/2}}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for each}\hskip 10.00002ptj=1,\cdots,n.

Then,
(w_{1},\cdots,w_{n})\in{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})\neq\emptyset
.
Moreover,

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{j}|^{2}~dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{|\nabla v_{j}|^{2}}{\sum_{k}|v_{k}|^{2}}+\frac{|v_{j}|^{2}(\sum_{k}\nabla|v_{k}|^{2})^{2}}{(\sum_{k}|v_{k}|^{2})^{3}}\Big)dx\leq C.

Thus,
\beta(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})<\infty
and it is achieved in
{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
by a standard variational argument.
∎

The associated Euler-Lagrange equations for (
1.15
) are

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u_{j}&=\frac{1}{n}u_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{k}|^{2}{\quad\mbox{in}\quad}{\Omega},\quad u_{j}=g_{j}{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\partial{\Omega},\\
&\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}|^{2}=n{\quad\mbox{in}\quad}{\Omega}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.17)

Indeed, if
(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})
is a critical point of
I
, then we are led to

0=i^{\prime}(0)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\Big[\Delta u_{j}+\frac{1}{n}u_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{k}|^{2}\Big]\psi_{j}dx

by using the fact
\nabla(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}|^{2})=0
.
Here, we set
i(t)=I\big(u_{1}(t),\cdots,u_{n}(t)\big)
for
(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{n})\in H^{1}_{0}({\Omega})\times\cdots\times H^{1}_{0}({\Omega})
and

u_{j}(t)=\frac{n(u_{j}+t\psi_{j})}{\sqrt{n\sum_{k=1}^{n}|u_{k}+t\psi_{k}|^{2}}}~\in~{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}).

We will refer to (
1.17
) as
n
-component harmonic map equations.
The condition
|u_{*}|=1
in (
1.9
) makes its zeros singular.
Whereas the condition
|u_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|u_{n}|^{2}=n
in (
1.17
) do not force
their zeros singular since it does not mean
|u_{1}|=\cdots=|u_{n}|=1
.
We also note from (
1.17
) that
u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}
share the same singularities if any.

Now, we are ready to state the first main result of this paper.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})
be a minimizer for (
1.14
).
Then, as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
, up to a subsequence,

\displaystyle(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\to(u_{1}^{*},\cdots,u_{n}^{*}){\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in}\hskip 10.00002pt}C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})\times\cdots\times C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega}).

(1.18)

Here,
(u_{1}^{*},\cdots,u_{n}^{*})
is a minimizer of
I
defined by (
1.15
) and satisfies a system (
1.17
) of harmonic map equations.
Moreover, for any positive integer
k
,

\displaystyle(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\to(u_{1}^{*},\cdots,u_{n}^{*}){\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in}\hskip 10.00002pt}C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega})\times\cdots\times C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega}),

(1.19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\Big)\to\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j}^{*}|^{2}{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in}\hskip 10.00002pt}C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega})\times\cdots\times C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega}).

(1.20)

We will show that although the degrees of the minimizers
(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})
are not
0
, our energy
E_{\varepsilon}(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})
is still bounded as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
.
This gives a different situation for the Ginzburg-Landau energy
E_{\varepsilon}^{b}
in (
1.6
).
It is known that
E_{\varepsilon}^{b}(u_{{\varepsilon},g}^{b})
grows logarithmically in the limit
{\varepsilon}\to 0
if
\deg(g,\partial{\Omega})\neq 0
.
See
[
9
]
for optimal lower bounds for the energy
E_{\varepsilon}^{b}
and many applications.

The convergence (
1.19
) and (
1.20
) imply that
u_{1}^{*},\cdots,u_{n}^{*}
are smooth.
The condition
|u_{1}^{*}|^{2}+\cdots+|u_{n}^{*}|^{2}=n
makes each
u_{j}^{*}
smooth at its zeros.
It is an interesting question whether
|u_{j}^{*}|=1
on
\Omega
for some
j
.
This is not true if
d_{j}>0
.
As the following theorem says, we are not sure that this can happen even for the case
d_{j}=0
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})
be a minimizing sequence for (
1.13
).

(i)

If
d_{j}>0
for some
1\leq j\leq n
, then we have

\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(1-|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)^{2}dx\to\infty.

(ii)

Suppose that
d_{j}=0
for all
j=1,\cdots,n
.
If
{\alpha}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})>\beta(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
, then there is some
k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
such that

\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(1-|u_{k,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)^{2}dx\to\infty.

Here,
{\alpha}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
is defined by Remark
4.2
.

(iii)

For
n=2
, if either one of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
is positive or
{\alpha}(g_{1},g_{2})>\beta(g_{1},g_{2})
, then

\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(1-|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)^{2}dx\to\infty,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for each $j=1,2$.}

2
Interior Estimates

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.2
.
In what follows, we often use the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
1
, Lemma A.1]
)
.

Let
-\Delta u=f
in an open set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
.
Given any
K\Subset\Omega
, we have

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}^{2}\leq C_{K}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big),

where
C
depends only on
\Omega
and
K
.
In addition, if
u=0
on
\partial{\Omega}
, then

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}^{2}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},

where
C
depends only on
\Omega
.

The next lemma provides
L^{\infty}
-estimates for
u_{j,{\varepsilon}}
and their gradients for
j=1,\cdots,n
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})
be any solution of the system (
1.11
).
Then, for
0<{\varepsilon}<1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}<n\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{on}\hskip 10.00002pt{\Omega},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{{\varepsilon}}{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for}\hskip 10.00002pt}j=1,\cdots,n.

(2.2)

Here,
C_{0}
is a constant that depends only on
g_{j}
and
{\Omega}
.

Proof.

Let
f=n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}
.
Then,

\Delta f\leq\frac{2}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\Big)f-2\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\Big)f{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in}\hskip 10.00002pt}{\Omega}.

Since
f=0
on
\partial{\Omega}
, we have
f>0
in
{\Omega}
by the strong maximum principle.

To show (
2.2
), let us decompose
u_{j,{\varepsilon}}=u_{j,{\varepsilon}}^{1}+u_{j,{\varepsilon}}^{2}

where

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u_{j,{\varepsilon}}^{1}&=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}u_{j,{\varepsilon}}\big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big){\quad\mbox{in}\quad}\Omega,\quad u_{j,{\varepsilon}}^{1}=0{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\partial{\Omega},\\
-\Delta u_{j,{\varepsilon}}^{2}&=0{\quad\mbox{in}\quad}\Omega,\quad u_{j,{\varepsilon}}^{2}=g_{j}{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Then,

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})

Lemma 2.5

\|(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\|_{H^{2}_{loc}\times\cdots\times H^{2}_{loc}}\leq C

Proof.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D^{2}u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}=~

Hence, we can write

\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D^{2}u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\leq~\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta\Big(|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\Big)+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}|\Delta u_{k,{\varepsilon}}|,

Fix

.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2r}(x_{0})}\zeta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D^{2}u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle C\int_{B_{2r}(x_{0})}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}dx+C\int_{B_{2r}(x_{0})}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\zeta|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle C\int_{B_{2r}(x_{0})}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}dx+C\int_{B_{2r}(x_{0})}\zeta\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|\,|D^{2}u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|dx.

In the sequel, by (

\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2r}(x_{0})}\zeta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D^{2}u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}dx\leq C\int_{{\Omega}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}dx\leq C.

Now, the standard covering argument shows that each

is uniformly bounded in

.
∎

Let

f_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\Big).

(2.7)

Then, we can rewrite (

Lemma 2.6
.

For any

, we have

\|(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\|_{W^{2,p}_{loc}\times\cdots\times W^{2,p}_{loc}}\leq C

\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{0}_{loc}}\leq C

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, we may assume that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\geq 1
on
{\Omega}
.
Given
K\Subset{\Omega}

\displaystyle-{\varepsilon}^{2}\Delta F_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-{\varepsilon}^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\Delta{\varphi}-2{\varepsilon}^{2}\nabla{\varphi}\cdot\nabla f_{\varepsilon}-{\varepsilon}^{2}{\varphi}\Delta f_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\Big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\Big)\Delta{\varphi}+4\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j,{\varepsilon}}(\nabla{\varphi}\cdot\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}})

Now, given

Lemma 3.1
.

For any solution of problem (

) there is a constant

On the other hand,

Lemma 3.2

\|(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\|_{H^{2}({\Omega})\times\cdots\times H^{2}({\Omega})}\leq C

{\widetilde{u}}_{i,{\varepsilon}}&=\tilde{g}_{i,{\varepsilon}}{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\{y_{2}=0\}\cap\partial U.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,

\displaystyle L=\sum_{k,l=1}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{l}}\Big(a_{kl}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{k}}\Big)\mbox{ with $a_{11}=1$, $a_{12}=a_{21}=-h^{\prime}$ and $a_{22}=1+(h^{\prime})^{2}$}

is a strongly elliptic operator if
r
is small enough.
For simplicity, we write
{\widetilde{u}}_{j,{\varepsilon}}
as
u_{j}
in what follows.

Let
A=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}
.
By a direct calculation,

\displaystyle L[A]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k,l,r=1}^{2}\Big(a_{kl}(u_{j})_{y_{k}y_{r}}(u_{j})_{y_{l}y_{r}}+(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\cdot L\big[(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D^{2}u_{j}|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{r=1}^{2}(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\cdot L\big[(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\big],

where
{\alpha}
is the ellipticity constant of
L
.
We note that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{r=1}^{2}(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\cdot L\big[(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\big]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k,l,r=1}^{2}(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\cdot\Big\{\big(L[u_{j}]\big)_{y_{r}}-\big[(a_{kl})_{y_{r}}(u_{j})_{y_{k}}\big]_{y_{l}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}\Big(n-\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u_{i}|^{2}\Big)+\frac{2}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\sum_{r=1}^{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}\cdot(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\cdot(u_{i})_{y_{r}}\Big)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k,l,r=1}^{2}(u_{j})_{y_{r}}\big[(a_{kl})_{y_{l}y_{r}}(u_{j})_{y_{k}}+(a_{kl})_{y_{r}}(u_{j})_{y_{k}y_{l}}\big]

\displaystyle\geq~

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}\Big(n-\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u_{i}|^{2}\Big)-C\sum_{j=1}^{n}(|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}+|D^{2}u_{j}|^{2}).

By Lemma
2.4
, given
y\in U
, if
{\varepsilon}
is small, then we can find
k=k(y)
such that
|u_{k}(y)|\geq 1/2
.
Then,

|L[u_{k}](y)|=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}|u_{k}(y)|\Big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}(y)|^{2}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}(y)|^{2}\Big).

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Big(n-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j}(y)|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2|L[u_{k}](y)|=2\Big|\Big(a_{kl}(y)(u_{k})_{y_{k}}(y)\Big)_{y_{l}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{n}(|\nabla u_{j}(y)|+|D^{2}u_{j}(y)|).

As a consequence, by Young’s inequality, we are led to

-L[A]+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D^{2}u_{j}|^{2}\leq C\Big(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j}|^{4}\Big).

Let us choose
\zeta\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{r/2}(0),B_{r}(0))
.
Then,

\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{U}\zeta^{2}|D^{2}u_{j}|^{2}dx\leq C+C\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{U}\zeta^{2}|\nabla u_{j}|^{4}dx+\int_{U}\zeta^{2}L[A]dx.

(3.2)

By integrating by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{U}\zeta^{2}L[A]dx

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\zeta^{2}\big[a_{kl}A_{y_{l}}\big]_{y_{k}}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{U}AL[\zeta^{2}]dx+2\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}a_{12}A(\zeta^{2})_{y_{1}}+\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}a_{22}A(\zeta^{2})_{y_{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}\zeta^{2}A(a_{12})_{y_{1}}-\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}\zeta^{2}a_{22}A_{y_{2}}.

The first four terms are uniformly bounded by Lemma
3.1
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}\zeta^{2}a_{22}A_{y_{2}}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}\zeta^{2}a_{22}(u_{j})_{y_{1}}\cdot(u_{j})_{y_{1}y_{2}}+\zeta^{2}a_{22}(u_{j})_{y_{2}}\cdot(u_{j})_{y_{2}y_{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big((I)+(II)\big).

By Lemma
3.1
,

(I)=-\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}\Big((\zeta^{2}a_{22})_{y_{1}}(u_{j})_{y_{1}}\cdot(u_{j})_{y_{2}}+\zeta^{2}a_{22}(g_{j})_{y_{1}y_{1}}\cdot(u_{j})_{y_{2}}\Big)=O(1).

Since
Lu_{j}=0
on
\{y_{2}=0\}
and
a_{11}=1
, we have

\displaystyle(II)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}\zeta^{2}\Big((u_{j})_{y_{2}}\cdot(g_{j})_{y_{1}y_{1}}+(a_{12})_{y_{1}}|(u_{j})_{y_{2}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+~(a_{21})_{y_{2}}(u_{j})_{y_{2}}\cdot(g_{j})_{y_{1}}+(a_{22})_{y_{2}}|(u_{j})_{y_{2}}|^{2}\Big)-\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}\zeta^{2}a_{12}(|(u_{j})_{y_{2}}|^{2})_{y_{1}}

\displaystyle=O(1)+\int_{\{y_{2}=0\}}(\zeta^{2}a_{12})_{y_{1}}|(u_{j})_{y_{2}}|^{2}~=~O(1).

Here, we also used Lemma
3.1
.
In the sequel, we can rewrite (
3.2
) as

\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{U}\zeta^{2}|D^{2}u_{j}|^{2}dx\leq C+C\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{U}\zeta^{2}|\nabla u_{j}|^{4}dx.

Now, using this inequality and employing the same argument of the proof of Lemma
2.5
, we can show

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{B_{r/2}(x_{0})}|D^{2}u_{j}|^{2}dx\leq C.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
p\geq 1
, we have

\|(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\|_{W^{2,p}({\Omega})\times\cdots\times W^{2,p}({\Omega})}\leq C_{p}

Moreover, if
f_{\varepsilon}
is defined by (
2.7
), then

\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{0}(\Omega)}\leq C
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
and the Sobolev embedding,

\|(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\|_{W^{1,p}({\Omega})\times\cdots\times W^{1,p}({\Omega})}\leq C\hskip 10.00002pt\forall p\geq 1.

We keep the notation (
2.7
).
By Lemma
2.4
and (
2.9
), we obtain

-2{\varepsilon}^{2}\Delta f_{\varepsilon}+f_{\varepsilon}\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{on ${\Omega}$}.

(3.3)

For
q>1
, since
f_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\partial{\Omega}
, multiplying this equation by
f_{\varepsilon}^{q-1}
, we have

\int_{\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}dx\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}^{q-1}dx\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\nabla u_{j,{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{2q}\|f_{\varepsilon}\|^{q-1}_{q},

which implies that
\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{q}\leq C
for some
C=C(n,q)>0
.
Hence, each
\Delta u_{j,{\varepsilon}}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{q}({\Omega})
for any
q\geq 1
.
By applying the elliptic regularity to (
2.8
), we obtain that

\|(u_{1,{\varepsilon}},\cdots,u_{n,{\varepsilon}})\|_{C^{1,\alpha}({\Omega})\times\cdots\times C^{1,\alpha}({\Omega})}\leq C\hskip 10.00002pt\forall{\alpha}\in(0,1).

By the maximum principle, we obtain from (
3.3
) that
\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{0}(\Omega)}\leq C
.
In particular, the right hand side of (
2.8
) is uniformly bounded in
C^{0}({\Omega})
.
Hence, each
u_{j,{\varepsilon}}
is also uniformly bounded in
W^{2,p}({\Omega})
for any
p\geq 1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The proof of (
1.19
) follows readily from Proposition
2.3
and Proposition
3.3
.
To show (
1.20
), let
f_{\varepsilon}
be defined by (
2.7
) and set

h_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt}f_{*}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla u_{j}^{*}|^{2}.

Then,
-\Delta u_{j}^{*}=u_{j}^{*}f_{*}
and
\|h_{\varepsilon}-n\|_{C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega})}={\varepsilon}^{2}\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega})}\to 0
.
We note that

-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{j,{\varepsilon}}\Delta u_{j,{\varepsilon}}-u_{j}^{*}\Delta u_{j}^{*})=n(f_{\varepsilon}-f_{*})+f_{\varepsilon}(h_{\varepsilon}-n).

Hence, as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
,

\displaystyle\|f_{\varepsilon}-f_{*}\|_{C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\Big(\|f_{\varepsilon}(h_{\varepsilon}-n)\|_{C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega})}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}\Delta u_{j,{\varepsilon}}-u_{j}^{*}\Delta u_{j}^{*}\|_{C^{k}_{loc}({\Omega})}\Big)\to 0.

This completes the proof.
∎

4
Further Properties of Solutions

In this section, we study some properties of solutions of the
n
-component Ginzburg-Landau equations (
1.11
) and the generalized harmonic map equations (
1.17
).
First, the next proposition tells us that if
g_{j}
is a rotation of
g_{1}
for each
j
and
{\varepsilon}
is not so small, then
u_{j}
is a rotation of
u_{1}
for any solution pair
(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})
of (
1.11
).
Consequently, each of
u_{j}
and
u_{k}
is a rotation of the other.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})
be a solution of (
1.11
).
Assume that for each
j
, there exists
{\gamma}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
g_{j}=e^{i\gamma_{j}}g_{1}
.
Then,
u_{j}=e^{i\gamma_{j}}u_{1}
for all
{\varepsilon}>\sqrt{n/{\lambda}_{1}}
.
Here,
{\lambda}_{1}
is the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta
on
{\Omega}
with the Dirichlet boundary condition.

Proof.

Set
{\widetilde{u}}_{j}=e^{-i{\gamma}_{j}}u_{j}
.
Then,
{\widetilde{u}}_{j}
satisfies

\displaystyle-\Delta{\widetilde{u}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\widetilde{u}}_{j}\Big(n-\sum_{k=1}^{n}|{\widetilde{u}}_{k}|^{2}\Big){\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in}\hskip 10.00002pt}\Omega,

\displaystyle{\widetilde{u}}_{j}

\displaystyle=g_{1}{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{on}\hskip 10.00002pt}\partial\Omega.

If
w_{j}={\widetilde{u}}_{j}-u_{1}
, then

\displaystyle-\Delta w_{j}+\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|u_{k}|^{2}w_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}w_{j},{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in}\hskip 10.00002pt}\Omega,

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle=0{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{on}\hskip 10.00002pt}\partial\Omega.

Hence, it follows that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{j}|^{2}+\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|u_{k}|^{2}|w_{j}|^{2}=\frac{n}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|w_{j}|^{2}\leq\frac{n}{{\lambda}_{1}{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{j}|^{2}.

If
{\varepsilon}>\sqrt{n/{\lambda}_{1}}
, then
w_{j}
is a constant.
Since
w_{j}=0
on
\partial{\Omega}
, we have
w_{j}=0
.
∎

It is not clear whether the conclusion of Proposition
4.1
is valid for arbitrary
{\varepsilon}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Suppose that
d_{j}=0
for all
1\leq j\leq n
.
Since
H^{1}_{g_{j}}({\Omega};S^{1})\neq\emptyset
, the minimization problem

\alpha(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})=\inf\Big\{I(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}):u_{j}\in H^{1}_{g_{j}}({\Omega};S^{1})\Big\}

is achieved by a unique pair
(u_{1}^{0},\cdots,u_{n}^{0})
satisfying

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u_{j}^{0}&=u_{j}^{0}|\nabla u_{j}^{0}|^{2}&&\mbox{on }{\Omega},\\
|u_{j}^{0}|&=1&&\mbox{on }{\Omega},\\
u_{j}^{0}&=g_{j}&&\mbox{on }\partial{\Omega}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.1)

Since
d_{j}=0
for each
j=1,\cdots,n
, we have that
u_{j}^{0}=e^{i{\varphi}_{j}}
for a harmonic function
{\varphi}_{j}
in
{\Omega}
.
In general, we have
\alpha(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})\geq\beta(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
since
(u_{1}^{0},\cdots,u_{n}^{0})\in{\mathcal{X}}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
.
An interesting question is whether
\alpha(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})=\beta(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
or not.
The next proposition gives us a necessary condition that they are equal.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume that
d_{j}=0
for all
j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
.
If
\alpha(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})=\beta(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
and it is achieved by
(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})
, then
|\nabla u_{j}|=|\nabla u_{k}|
for all
j,k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
.

Proof.

By assumption,
u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}\in H_{g_{1}}^{1}(\Omega;S^{1})\times\cdots\times H_{g_{n}}^{1}(\Omega;S^{1})
and

\beta=I(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}).

Since
|u_{j}|=1
, we can write
u_{j}=e^{i{\varphi}_{j}}
.
By plugging this in (
1.17
), we are led to

-i\Delta{\varphi}_{j}=\Big(\frac{1}{n}-1\Big)|\nabla{\varphi}_{j}|^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\neq j}^{n}|\nabla{\varphi}_{k}|^{2}{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{in}\hskip 10.00002pt}{\Omega}.

Thus, for all
j=1\cdots,n

\Delta{\varphi}_{j}=0{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt}|\nabla{\varphi}_{j}|^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k\neq j}^{n}|\nabla{\varphi}_{k}|^{2}.

In particular,

|\nabla{\varphi}_{j}|^{2}-|\nabla{\varphi}_{1}|^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}(|\nabla{\varphi}_{1}|^{2}-|\nabla{\varphi}_{j}|^{2}),

which implies that
|\nabla{\varphi}_{j}|^{2}=|\nabla{\varphi}_{1}|^{2}
, or equivalently
|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}=|\nabla u_{1}|^{2}
for all
1\leq j\leq n
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Let us consider the case
n=2
for simplicity with
d_{1}=d_{2}=0
.
We can write
u_{j}=e^{i{\varphi}_{j}}
and
g_{j}=e^{i\tilde{{\varphi}}_{j}}
for
j=1,2
.
According to the proof of Proposition
4.3
, if
{\alpha}(g_{1},g_{2})=\beta(g_{1},g_{2})
, then it is necessary that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta{\varphi}_{1}=0=\Delta{\varphi}_{2}{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}{\Omega},\\
&|\nabla{\varphi}_{1}|=|\nabla{\varphi}_{2}|{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}{\Omega},\\
&{\varphi}_{1}=\tilde{\varphi}_{1},~{\varphi}_{2}=\tilde{\varphi}_{2}{\quad\mbox{on}\quad}\partial{\Omega}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.2)

So, if there are no solutions for (
4.2
), we may conclude that
{\alpha}(g_{1},g_{2})>\beta(g_{1},g_{2})
.
For instance, if
{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{1}
is a constant and
{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{2}
is not a constant function, then there are no solution of (
4.2
).
Another example is the case that
{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{1}={\lambda}{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{2}
with
|{\lambda}|\neq 1
.
Indeed, if
{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{1}={\lambda}{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{2}
, then

-\Delta\Big(\frac{{\varphi}_{2}}{{\lambda}}\Big)=0~~\mbox{on}~~{\Omega}{\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt}\frac{{\varphi}_{2}}{{\lambda}}={\widetilde{\varphi}}_{1}~~\mbox{on}~~\partial{\Omega}.

Since harmonic maps with the same boundary values are equal, we have
{\varphi}_{1}={\varphi}_{2}/{\lambda}
.
Thus,
|\nabla{\varphi}_{1}|=|\nabla{\varphi}_{2}|
only when
|{\lambda}|=1
.

As a final subject of this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
:

(i) Suppose to the contrary that there is
k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
such that
d_{k}>0
and

\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(1-|u_{k,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)^{2}dx\leq C.

By Theorem
1.2
, we have
u_{k,{\varepsilon}}\to u_{k}^{*}\in H^{1}({\Omega})
with
|u_{k}^{*}|=1
.
This implies that
u_{k}^{*}\in H^{1}_{g_{k}}({\Omega};S^{1})
, which is impossible since
H^{1}_{g_{k}}({\Omega};S^{1})=\emptyset
if
d_{k}>0
.

(ii) Suppose that
d_{1}=\cdots=d_{n}=0
.
If

\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(1-|u_{j,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)^{2}dx\leq C\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptj=1,\cdots,n,

then
u_{j,{\varepsilon}}\to u_{j}^{*}
in
H^{1}
and
|u_{j}^{*}|=1
for each
j=1,\cdots,n
.
So, we have
\alpha(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})=\beta(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})
that contradicts the assumption.

(iii)
If we set

X_{\varepsilon}^{2}=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(1-|u_{1,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)^{2}dx\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptY_{\varepsilon}^{2}=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\Big(1-|u_{2,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\Big)^{2}dx,

we are led by (
2.4
) and the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality that

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(2-|u_{1,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}-|u_{2,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=X_{\varepsilon}^{2}+Y_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\frac{2}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(1-|u_{1,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)\big(1-|u_{2,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\big)dx

\displaystyle\geq\big(X_{\varepsilon}-Y_{\varepsilon}\big)^{2}.

So, either both
X_{\varepsilon}
and
Y_{\varepsilon}
are bounded, or they are unbounded.
By (i) and (ii), at least one of
X_{\varepsilon}
and
Y_{\varepsilon}
is unbounded and we get the desired conclusion.
∎
