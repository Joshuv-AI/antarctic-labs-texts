Beatty Sequences for a Quadratic Irrational: Decidability and Applications
Luke Schaeffer, Jeffrey Shallit, Stefan Zorcic

Abstract
Let α \alpha and β \beta belong to the same quadratic field. We show that the inhomogeneous Beatty sequence ( ⌊ n ​ α + β ⌋ ) n ≥ 1 (\lfloor n\alpha+\beta\rfloor)_{n\geq 1} is synchronized, in the sense that there is a finite automaton that takes as input the Ostrowski representations of n n and y y in parallel, and accepts if and only if y = ⌊ n ​ α + β ⌋ y=\lfloor n\alpha+\beta\rfloor . Since it is already known that the addition relation is computable for Ostrowski representations based on a quadratic number, a consequence is a new and rather simple proof that the first-order logical theory of these sequences with addition is decidable. The decision procedure is easily implemented in the free software Walnut . As an application, we show that for each r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 it is decidable whether the set { ⌊ n ​ α + β ⌋ : n ≥ 1 } \{\lfloor n\alpha+\beta\rfloor\,:\,n\geq 1\} forms an additive basis (or asymptotic additive basis) of order r r . Using our techniques, we also solve some open problems of Reble and Kimberling, and give an explicit characterization of a sequence of Hildebrand et al.

3
Ostrowski
\alpha
-representation

In the famous Fibonacci (or Zeckendorf) system for representing natural numbers
[
34
,
48
]
, every integer is written uniquely as a sum of distinct Fibonacci numbers, subject to the condition that we never use two consecutive Fibonacci numbers. More precisely, if the Fibonacci numbers are (as usual)
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}
for
n\geq 2
, then a natural number
n
can be expressed as a binary string
e_{t}\cdots e_{1}
with interpretation

n=\sum_{1\leq j\leq t}e_{i}F_{i+1}
. Notice that the most significant digit is written at the left.

The Ostrowski numeration system is a generalization of this idea.
Let
\alpha>0
be an irrational real number, with continued
fraction
[a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
. Define, as usual, the

i
’th convergent
p_{i}/q_{i}
to be
[a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}]
.
Recall (e.g.,
[
23
]
) that
p_{i}=a_{i}p_{i-1}+p_{i-2}
and

q_{i}=a_{i}q_{i-1}+q_{i-2}
.

The
Ostrowski
\alpha
-numeration system
is a method for
uniquely expressing every natural number
n
as an integer linear
combination of the
q_{i}
, namely

\sum_{0\leq i\leq t}e_{i}q_{i}
. Here, to obtain uniqueness of the representation, the
e_{i}
are restricted by the following
inequalities:

(a)

0\leq e_{i}\leq a_{i+1}
for
i\geq 1
;

(b)

e_{i}=a_{i+1}
implies
e_{i-1}=0
for
i\geq 1
;

(c)

0\leq e_{0}<a_{1}
.

See
[
40
]
and
[
2
, §3.9]
.
We write the canonical Ostrowski
\alpha
-representation of
n
, starting with
the most significant digit, and satisfying the rules (a)–(c) above, as

(n)_{\alpha}
. The inverse mapping, which sends a string

x=e_{t}e_{t-1}\cdots e_{0}
to the sum
\sum_{0\leq i\leq t}e_{i}q_{i}
,
is denoted by
[x]_{\alpha}
.

In some cases we need to write the
\alpha
-representation of pairs or
tuples. We do so by working over a product alphabet, and padding the
shorter representation at the front with
0
’s, if needed.

4
Automata, synchronized sequences, and first-order logic

We say that a sequence
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
synchronized

if there is a finite automaton that takes the representations of

n
and
y
in parallel, and
accepts if and only if
y=s_{n}
. In this paper, the particular representations
we use are the Ostrowski
\alpha
-representations discussed in the
previous section.

Synchronized sequences have a number of nice closure properties. These can be found in
[
43
]
for automatic sequences, but the same proofs also suffice for sequences defined in terms of Ostrowski representations (provided one knows that the addition relation
is synchronized, as proved in
[
4
]
).

Proposition 1
.

(a)

The sequence
(\lfloor(bn+c)/d\rfloor)
is synchronized for integers
b,c,d
with
b\geq\max(1,-c)
and

d\geq 1
.

(b)

If
(f(n))_{n\geq 1}
and
(g(n))_{n\geq 1}

are both synchronized, then so is their termwise sum
(f(n)+g(n))_{n\geq 1}
.

(c)

If
(f(n))_{n\geq 1}
and
(g(n))_{n\geq 1}

are both synchronized then so is
their composition
f(g(n))_{n\geq 1}
.

From the results of
[
12
]
we immediately get the following result.

Theorem 2
.

There is an algorithm that takes as input
a first-order logical formula on natural
numbers for
f(n)
,
defined in terms of addition, subtraction, multiplication by constants,
and synchronized sequences and computes a synchronized
automaton for
f
.

Furthermore, the
Walnut
theorem-prover can compute these synchronized automata.
For more information about
Walnut
, see
[
36
,
45
]
.
Thus, to prove our main result, we only need to show that
the map
n\rightarrow\lfloor n\alpha+\beta\rfloor

is synchronized.

5
The main result and its implementation

We need a simple lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
x
be a real number and
c\geq 1
an integer.
Then
\lfloor x/c\rfloor=\lfloor{{\lfloor x\rfloor}\over c}\rfloor
.

Proof.

Using Euclidean division, write
x=qc+r
for
q\in\mathbb{Z}
and

0\leq r<c
. Since
0\leq\lfloor r\rfloor<c
, we have

{{\lfloor x\rfloor}\over c}={{qc+\lfloor r\rfloor}\over c}\\
=q+{{\lfloor r\rfloor}\over c}.

(1)

Hence

\lfloor{{\lfloor x\rfloor}\over c}\rfloor=q
.
On the other hand
x/c=q+r/c
, so

\lfloor x/c\rfloor=q
.
∎

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
0<\gamma<1
is a quadratic irrational
with purely periodic continued
fraction

[0,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m},\ldots].

Let
q_{i}
denote the denominator of the
i
’th convergent to this
continued fraction.
Then for all integers
n\geq 1
we have

[(n-1)_{\gamma}\,0^{m}]_{\gamma}=q_{m}(n-1)+q_{m-1}\cdot\lfloor n\gamma\rfloor.

Proof.

We use the
2\times 2
matrix approach to continued fractions,
pioneered by Hurwitz
[
28
]
, Frame
[
18
]
, and Kolden
[
33
]
, and also discussed (for example)
in
[
2
]
.

This approach uses the fact, easily proved by induction, that

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{0}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\cdots\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{i}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}p_{i}&p_{i-1}\\
q_{i}&q_{i-1}\end{array}\right]

(2)

for all
i\geq 0
.

Since
a_{i}=a_{i+m}
for all
i\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}p_{i+m}&p_{i+m-1}\\
q_{i+m}&q_{i+m-1}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{0}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\cdots\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{i+m}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{0}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\cdots\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{m}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\quad\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{m+1}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\cdots\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{i+m}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{0}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\cdots\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{m}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\quad\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\cdots\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{i}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}p_{m}&p_{m-1}\\
q_{m}&q_{m-1}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}q_{i}&q_{i-1}\\
p_{i}&p_{i-1}\end{array}\right],

where we have used the fact that
a_{0}=0
.

Let us now compare the entry in row
2
and column
1
of both sides.
We get

q_{i+m}=q_{m}q_{i}+q_{m-1}p_{i}.

(3)

Now suppose the Ostrowski
\gamma
-representation of

n-1
is
\sum_{0\leq i\leq t}e_{i}q_{i}
.
Then from Eq. (
3
), we get

\displaystyle[(n-1)_{\gamma}0^{m}]_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i\leq t}e_{i}q_{i+m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i\leq t}e_{i}(q_{m}q_{i}+q_{m-1}p_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(q_{m}\sum_{0\leq i\leq t}e_{i}q_{i}\right)+\left(q_{m-1}\sum_{0\leq i\leq t}e_{i}p_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=q_{m}(n-1)+q_{m-1}\lfloor n\gamma\rfloor,

as desired.
Here, in the last step, we have used
[
2
, Corollary 9.1.14]
.
∎

Corollary 5
.

Suppose
0<\gamma<1
is a quadratic irrational
with purely periodic continued
fraction.
Then the sequence
(\lfloor n\gamma\rfloor)_{n\geq 1}

is
\gamma
-Ostrowski synchronized.

Proof.

It is easy to see that for each

m\geq 1
there is a finite automaton

{\tt shift}_{m}
accepting
0^{m}y
and
y0^{m}
in parallel.
We sketch the details. The states are
m
-tuples of
integers appearing in the Ostrowski representation of

\mathbb{N}
, and the initial state and unique final state is
0^{m}
.
Here the “meaning” of a state named
f_{1}\cdots f_{m}
is
that these are the last
m
symbols of
y
that the automaton has read.
The transition function
\delta
is
defined by
\delta(f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{m},[f_{1},g])=f_{2}\cdots f_{m}g
.

Since
q_{m}
and
q_{m-1}
are constants, the
first-order formula

\exists u,v\ n=u+1\,\wedge\,{\tt shift}_{m}(u,v)\,\wedge\,v=q_{m-1}\cdot z+q_{m}\cdot u

expresses
z=\lfloor n\gamma\rfloor
as a synchronized function of
n
, for
n\geq 1
. The result now follows by Theorem
2
.

∎

Theorem 7
.

Suppose
0<\gamma<1
is a quadratic irrational
with purely periodic continued
fraction

[0,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m},\ldots],

and suppose
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)

such that
\alpha\geq 0
and
\alpha+\beta\geq 0
.
Then
(\lfloor n\alpha+\beta\rfloor)_{n\geq 1}

is synchronized in terms of
Ostrowski
\gamma
-representations.

Proof.

If
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)
satisfy the stated
inequalities, then we can write

\alpha=(a+b\gamma)/c
,

\beta=(d+e\gamma)/c

for some integers
a,b,c,d,e
with
b\geq 0
and
c\geq 1
.
So

\displaystyle\lfloor\alpha n+\beta\rfloor

\displaystyle=\lfloor n(a+b\gamma)/c+(d+e\gamma)/c\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor{{\lfloor bn\gamma+an+e\gamma+d\rfloor}\over c}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor{{\lfloor(bn+e)\gamma\rfloor+an+d}\over c}\right\rfloor.

Now from Corollary
5
, we know that

f(n)=\lfloor n\gamma\rfloor
is
\gamma
-Ostrowski
synchronized. Hence, by Proposition
1
, so is
f(bn+e)
. Hence so is

f(bn+e)+an+d
. Hence so is

\lfloor{{f(bn+e)+an+d}\over c}\rfloor

Next, we prove a sort of converse to Corollary
5
. We need
a lemma. Recall that the subword complexity (aka factor complexity) of a word is the function

\rho(n)
counting the number of distinct length-
n
factors.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose
0<\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}<1

are real numbers for
1\leq i\leq k
. Let

{\bf s}_{i}(n)=\lfloor(n+1)\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}\rfloor-\lfloor n\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}\rfloor

be the associated Sturmian word
with slope
\alpha_{i}
and
intercept
\beta_{i}
.
Form the word
\bf s
over the alphabet

\{0,1\}^{k}
by

{\bf s}[n]=({\bf s}_{1}[n],\ldots,{\bf s}_{k}[n])
. If the

k+1
numbers

1,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}

are linearly independent
over
\mathbb{Q}
, then

\bf s
has subword complexity

(n+1)^{k}
.

Proof.

By the multidimensional version of Kronecker’s theorem (see, e.g.,

[
23
, Thm. 442]
), the linear independence criterion implies that there are integers
q
and
p_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
such that

|\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}q\alpha_{i}-p_{i}+b_{i}|<\epsilon
.

It is known that, for all
n
, there exists a partition of
[0,1)
into

n+1
intervals such that the length-
n
factor of a Sturmian word starting at position
q
is completely determined by
which interval

q\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}
lies in, modulo 1
[
32
]
. In other words it is completely determined
by which of
n+1
(circular) intervals
q\alpha_{i}
lies in. Pick a particular length-
n
factor in each of the
k
Sturmian words. For all
i
,
1\leq i\leq k
, choose a point
b_{i}
in the
corresponding
intervals that is at least
\epsilon
away from the endpoints of the intervals, and choose
\epsilon
sufficiently small.
Then by Kronecker’s theorem some positive integer
q
exists that makes
q\alpha_{i}\bmod 1
at distance at most
\epsilon

from
b_{i}
, so it lies in the interior of the specified interval, so the
i
’th Sturmian word
beginning at position
q
is the desired one. Since this is true for each
i
,
1\leq i\leq k
,
all

(n+1)^{k}
possibilities for a factor of
\bf s
must occur.
∎

We can now prove a sort of converse to Corollary
5
.

Theorem 10
.

Suppose
0<\gamma<1
is a quadratic irrational with purely periodic continued fraction expansion.
Suppose the Beatty sequence

(\lfloor n\alpha+\beta\rfloor)_{n\geq 1}
is
\gamma
-Ostrowski synchronized. Then

\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)
. Furthermore, the expression of
\alpha
in terms of

\gamma
is computable.

Proof.

Suppose
1,\alpha,\gamma
are
linearly independent.
If the Beatty sequence

(\lfloor n\alpha+\beta\rfloor)_{n\geq 1}
is
\gamma
-Ostrowski synchronized, then
the corresponding Sturmian sequence
(possibly recoded over the alphabet

\{0,1\}
if necessary) is
\gamma
-Ostrowski automatic.
This is also true for the Sturmian
sequence associated with slope
\gamma
and intercept
0
. Then the product of these two sequences
(over the alphabet
\{0,1\}^{2}
)
is also
\gamma
-Ostrowski automatic. Hence this product
has subword complexity
\Theta(n^{2})
, but we know that the subword complexity of an automatic sequence is
O(n)
. This contradiction shows that
1,\alpha,\gamma
are linearly dependent, and hence there are
integers
a,b,c
such that

a\alpha=b\gamma+c
.

It remains to show that we can computably determine
a,b
, and
c
. To do so we iterate over all possible integer triples by increasing order of
|a|+|b|+|c|
.
For each triple we construct the an
\gamma
-Ostrowski automaton for the Sturmian word with slope
(b\gamma+c)/a
. We then check
(using the logical approach) whether the set of factors occurring in Sturmian word is the same as that for the Sturmian word with slope
\alpha
and intercept
\beta
. Since all Sturmian words with the same slope have the same set of factors, this suffices.
∎

6
Beatty sequences in
Walnut

We now explain how to use Beatty sequences of quadratic irrationals in

Walnut
. Suppose
\alpha
and
\beta
lie in a quadratic field
\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)
, where
\gamma
is a purely periodic continued fraction.

If the period of
\gamma
’s continued fraction starts with

1
, rotate the period to get
\gamma^{\prime}
with a period that begins with a number larger than
1
. Then
\gamma^{\prime}
generates the same
field as
\gamma
. This is necessary because
Walnut
assumes
that the continued fraction represents a number in the open
interval
(0,{1\over 2})
.
Rotation of the period only
fails when
\gamma=[0,1,1,1,\ldots]
, but in that
case we can just use the Fibonacci numeration system instead, based
on the real number
[0,2,1,1,1,\ldots]
.

Next, create the appropriate number system with a command
of the form

ost <name> [0] [period]:

This will create a number system called

msd_name
, where you replace
name
with any
name you like. For example

ost sqrt7 [0] [4 1 1 1]:

Use Theorem
4
to write a
Walnut
formula
for a synchronized automaton accepting
n
and

\lfloor n\gamma\rfloor
in parallel.
Finally, use the relationship between
\alpha,\beta,

and
\gamma
, and Theorem
7
to create a

Walnut
formula for

\lfloor n\alpha+\beta\rfloor
.

As an example, let us obtain the synchronized automaton
corresponding to the Beatty sequence
where

The additive theory of Beatty sequences

Given a sequence
(s_{i})_{n\geq 1}
or set
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots\}
of natural numbers,
a fundamental question of additive number theory is to decide
which integers have representations as
r
-fold sums of the
s_{i}

[
38
,
39
]
.
More precisely, we define the set of
r
-fold
sums
A_{s}(r)
as follows:

A_{s}(r)=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\exists i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{r}\text{ such that }n=s_{i_{1}}+s_{i_{2}}+\cdots+s_{i_{r}}\}.

We say that
(s_{i})_{n\geq 1}
forms an
additive basis of
order
r
if
A_{s}(r)=\mathbb{N}
. We say that
(s_{i})_{n\geq 1}
forms
an
asymptotic additive basis of order
r

if
\mathbb{N}\setminus A_{s}(r)
is finite.

In this section we generalize some recent results on the additive theory of Beatty sequences
[
29
,
41
,
44
,
13
]
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\gamma
be a real quadratic irrational, and suppose

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)
. Then
the inhomogeneous Beatty sequence
(\lfloor\alpha n+\beta\rfloor)_{n\geq 0}

forms an asymptotic additive basis of order
h
for some
integer
h\geq 1
. Furthermore, the smallest such
h
is
effectively computable, and one can also decide if

(\lfloor\alpha n+\beta\rfloor)_{n\geq 0}
forms an additive basis.

Proof.

By a classical theorem (see Nathanson
[
39
, p. 366, Theorems 11.6 and 11.7]
)
every Beatty sequence
forms an asymptotic additive basis where an upper bound on
the number of terms
needed to represent every sufficiently large number is (effectively) computable.
Now we just write first-order statements
that every sufficiently large integer is a sum of
h
terms of the
Beatty sequence, and evaluate them using the algorithm of Theorem
2
, until we find the smallest
h
. The algorithm
is guaranteed to terminate by Nathanson’s theorem.

Similarly we can write a first-order statement
that every natural number is a sum of some number of terms.
∎

Remark 12
.

The same result applies to the generalized Beatty sequences of Allouche and Dekking

[
1
]
.

We now illustrate the ideas for a particular example.
We use the free software
Walnut
, which can prove theorems
about Ostrowski-automatic sequences, if they are stated in first-order
logic. For more details about
Walnut
, see

[
36
,
45
]
.

Let
\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
, the golden ratio, and consider the sequence

{\bf c}=(n\varphi+{1\over 2})_{n\geq 0}
. This is sequence

A007067
in the OEIS
[
46
]
, and was originally studied by Johann Bernoulli
[
47
, pp. 66–69]
.

Theorem 13
.

The sequence
\bf c
is
\varphi
-synchronized.

Proof.

Starting from the known automaton (called
phin
) for the Beatty sequence

(\lfloor n\varphi\rfloor)_{n\geq 0}

(see, e.g.,
[
45
, §10.11]
), we can get
an automaton for
\bf c
as follows (in
Walnut
)

reg shift {0,1} {0,1} "([0,0]|[0,1][1,1]*[1,0])*":
def phin "?msd_fib (s=0&n=0) | Ex $shift(n-1,x) & s=x+1":

This produces the synchronized automaton for
\bf c
in Figure
2

Figure 2:
Synchronized automaton for

.

∎

Theorem 14
.

Every integer
n\not=1
can be written as the sum of two elements
of
\bf c

Proof.

We use the following
Walnut

. The result is proved.
∎

\alpha\geq 0

\gamma
-Ostrowski automatic.

The claim that

\alpha
.
