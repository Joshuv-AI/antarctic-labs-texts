Reducing the frequency of the Higgs mode in a helical superconductor coupled to an LC-circuit
Yao Lu, Stefan Ilić, Risto Ojajärvi, Tero T. Heikkilä, F. Sebastian Bergeret

Abstract
We show that the amplitude, or Higgs mode of a superconductor with strong spin-orbit coupling and an exchange field, couples linearly to the electromagnetic field. Furthermore, by coupling such a superconductor to an LC resonator, we demonstrate that the Higgs resonance becomes a regular mode at frequencies smaller than the quasiparticle energy threshold 2 ​ Δ 2\Delta . We finally propose and discuss a possible experiment based on microwave spectroscopy for an unequivocal detection of the Higgs mode. Our approach may allow visualizing Higgs modes also in more complicated multiband superconductors with a coupling between the charge and other electronic degrees of freedom.

In this letter, we propose a way of overcoming these difficulties.
We first demonstrate that the amplitude mode in helical superconductors

Helical superconductivity occurs in systems where both inversion and time-reversal symmetries are broken, for instance due to magnetic fields and spin-orbit coupling (SOC). Secondly, we exploit such a linear coupling and demonstrate the reduction of the Higgs frequency by coupling the superconductor to an LC resonator (Fig.

Summary of the results from a phenomenological model.-

In a conventional superconductor the Higgs-light coupling is characterized by the susceptibility

\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}=\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial\bm{A}\partial\Delta}=\frac{\partial\bm{J}}{\partial\Delta}\;,

(1)

where
S
is the action,
\bm{A}
is the vector potential, and
\bm{J}={\partial S}/{\partial\bm{A}}
is the supercurrent. Near the critical temperature,
\bm{J}\propto\Delta^{2}
, and the susceptibility is
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}\propto{J}/\Delta
. In other words, it is finite only in the presence of a supercurrent
Moor et al. 2017
.

The situation is different in
superconductors with broken time-reversal and inversion symmetries. These may correspond to superconductors with Rashba SOC and an in-plane exchange field, intensively studied in the context of magnetoelectric phenomena in superconductors, such as helical superconductivity
Agterberg 2003
;
Kaur et al. 2005
;
Dimitrova and Feigel’Man 2007
, Josephson
\phi_{0}
junctions
Buzdin 2008
;
Bergeret and Tokatly 2015
, and most recently supercurrent diode effects
Ando et al. 2020
;
Daido et al. 2022
;
Jiang and Hu 2022
;
Yuan and Fu 2022
;
Ilić and Bergeret 2022
;
He et al. 2022
. In this case, the action up to the fourth order in the order parameter is given by
He et al. 2022
;
Ilić and Bergeret 2022
,

\begin{split}S=&S_{0}+\int dtd\bm{r}\left(\bm{a}_{1}\tilde{\bm{Q}}+b_{1}\tilde{\bm{Q}}^{2}\right)|\Delta(t)|^{2}\\
+&\left(\bm{a}_{2}\tilde{\bm{Q}}+b_{2}\tilde{\bm{Q}}^{2}\right)|\Delta(t)|^{4},\end{split}

(2)

where
\tilde{\bm{Q}}=\bm{Q}+\bm{A}(t)
is the gauge-invariant condensate momentum and
\bm{Q}
is the phase gradient of the order parameter.
S_{0}
is the zeroth order term in
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
, and
\Delta(t)
the time dependent order parameter
\Delta(t)=\Delta_{0}+\delta\Delta(t)
.
The constants
b_{1,2}
are the usual Ginzburg-Landau coefficients appearing in even-power terms of
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
. Linear-in-
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
terms,
\bm{a}_{1}
and
\bm{a}_{2}
, are only allowed in superconductors with broken time-reversal and inversion symmetries, and are related to the Lifshitz invariant
Mineev and Samokhin 2008
;
Bauer and Sigrist 2012
.

The action, Eq. (
2
), describes a helical superconductor with a spatially varying order parameter in the ground state,
\Delta(\bm{r})=\Delta_{0}e^{i\bm{Q}_{0}\bm{r}}

Agterberg 2003
;
Kaur et al. 2005
;
Dimitrova and Feigel’Man 2007
. The amplitude of modulation
\bm{Q}_{0}
can be determined from the condition that the supercurrent
\bm{J}
in the ground state must vanish:
\partial S/\partial\bm{Q}|_{\bm{Q}=\bm{Q}_{0}}=0
. Thus,
\bm{Q}_{0}=-(\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{a}_{2}\Delta^{2})/[2(b_{1}+b_{2}\Delta^{2})]
.
Next, we calculate
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
by taking
\bm{Q}=\bm{Q}_{0}+\delta\bm{Q}
, where
\delta\bm{Q}
is a phase gradient generated by passing a supercurrent through the system. Substituting Eq. (
2
) into Eq. (
1
) we obtain the Higgs-light coupling susceptibility

\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}=\int d\bm{r}\left(4\Delta_{0}\delta\bm{Q}(b_{1}+2b_{2}\Delta_{0}^{2})+\frac{2(\bm{a}_{2}b_{1}-\bm{a}_{1}b_{2})\Delta_{0}^{3}}{b_{1}+b_{2}\Delta_{0}^{2}}\right).

(3)

The first term describes the linear Higgs-light coupling due to a finite supercurrent discussed above and established in Ref.
Moor et al. 2017
. The second term is an additional contribution that is only finite in helical superconductors for which

\bm{a}_{1}
and
\bm{a}_{2}
are non-zero. This is one of the main results of our work: helical superconductors support linear Higgs-light coupling even in the absence of an applied supercurrent.

Let us now investigate how the structure of the Higgs mode would change, if it were to be coupled linearly to an LC resonator (see setup in Fig.
1
). The Higgs mode is described by the fluctuations of the order parameter
\delta\Delta(t)
, whereas the LC mode is described by the time-varying voltage
V(t)
. In frequency domain, the effective equations of motion of this system can be written as

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\Omega_{H}-\Omega+i\Gamma_{H}}&\gamma_{1}\\
\gamma_{2}&\Omega_{LC}-\Omega+i\Gamma_{LC}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\delta\Delta(\Omega)\\
V(\Omega)\end{pmatrix}=0.

(4)

Here
\Omega_{H}
and
\Omega_{LC}
are the resonant frequencies of the Higgs and the LC mode, respectively, and
\Gamma_{H}
and
\Gamma_{LC}
are the damping parameters. The coupling coefficients
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are proportional to
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
. We assume a vanishing injected DC supercurrent, which is why only the second term in Eq. (
3
) contributes to the coupling.

In the absence of any coupling,
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0
, the Higgs mode is not a well-defined mode with a Lorentzian line-shape, as reflected in the square-root function in Eq. (
4
). On the other hand, the LC mode is a regular mode.
For a finite but small coupling such that
{\Gamma_{H}}\ll\text{Re}(\eta^{2})\ll{\Omega_{LC}-\Omega_{H}}
, where
\eta=\frac{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}{\Omega_{LC}+i\Gamma_{LC}-\Omega_{H}}
, and a low dissipation of the
LC
mode,
\Gamma_{LC}<\Omega_{LC}-\Omega_{H}
, we can approximate the eigenvalue equation near the Higgs frequency as

\sqrt{\Omega_{H}-\Omega+i\Gamma_{H}}-\eta=0.

(5)

If
\text{Re}(\eta)>0
, the eigenvalue equation can be linearized around the new resonance frequency and becomes

\left[\Omega_{H}-\real(\eta^{2})-\Omega\right]+i\left[\Gamma_{H}-\imaginary(\eta^{2})\right]=0.

(6)

The Higgs mode is shifted away from the branch cut at
\Omega_{H}
to a lower frequency
\Omega_{H}-\real(\eta^{2})
, and becomes a real mode with a potentially small linewidth
\Gamma_{H}-\imaginary(\eta^{2})

lin
.
Thus by coupling the two modes, the resonant response from the Higgs mode can be dramatically enhanced and its frequency reduced. This is our second main result. In what follows we derive our findings from a microscopic model.

Microscopic theory.-

One realization of helical superconductivity is a quasi two-dimensional superconductor with strong Rashba SOC and an in-plane magnetic field. To calculate the susceptibility
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
, we start with the generalized Eilenberger equation describing this system in the basis of the two helical bands labeled by the index
\lambda=\pm 1

Houzet and Meyer 2015
:

i\bm{n}\cdot\left(\bm{Q}v_{F}/2+\lambda\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}\times\hat{z}\right)[\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}]-\{\partial_{t}\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}\}\\
=[\Delta_{0}\tau_{1}+\Delta_{\Omega}e^{-i\Omega t}\tau_{1}+\hat{\Sigma}_{\lambda\bm{n}},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}].

(7)

Here,
\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}
is the quasiclassical Green function of the band
\lambda
with the momentum direction at the Fermi level given by
\bm{n}=\bm{p}/p_{F}
. It is a matrix in the Nambu space, spanned by the Pauli matrices
\tau_{1,2,3}
and the identity matrix 1. The normalization condition
\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{2}=1
holds.
\bm{Q}
is the phase gradient of the order parameter,
\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}
is the exchange field, and
\hat{z}
is the unit vector perpendicular to the plane of the superconductor.
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity,
\Delta_{0}
is the saddle point value of the order parameter and
\Delta_{\Omega}
is the Fourier amplitude of the external pairing potential with frequency
\Omega
.

\hat{\Sigma}_{\lambda\bm{n}}
is the disorder self-energy

\hat{\Sigma}_{\lambda\bm{n}}=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{1}{4\tau_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\left(\langle\hat{g}_{\lambda^{\prime}\bm{n}^{\prime}}\rangle_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}+\lambda\lambda^{\prime}\bm{n}\cdot\langle\bm{n}^{\prime}\hat{g}_{\lambda^{\prime}\bm{n}^{\prime}}\rangle_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}\right).

(8)

Here
\tau_{\lambda}
is the effective scattering time for the band
\lambda
,
\frac{1}{\tau_{\lambda}}=\frac{1}{\tau}(1+\lambda\frac{\alpha}{v_{F}})
, where
\tau
is the impurity scattering time.
\alpha
is the velocity associated with Rashba SOC. Note that Eq. (
S.1
) is valid when SOC is larger than all other energy scales except the chemical potential
\mu
, namely
\mu\gtrsim\alpha p_{F}\gg\Delta,h_{ex},\tau^{-1},Qv_{F}.
In the absence of disorder, the two bands are decoupled, but share the same order parameter. Disorder couples the bands by introducing interband scattering.

Within linear response to the external field, the Green function can be written as
\hat{g}=\hat{g}^{(0)}e^{i\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})}+\hat{g}^{(\Delta)}e^{i\omega_{1}t_{1}-i\omega_{2}t_{2}}
, with
\omega_{1}=\omega
and
\omega_{2}=\omega+\Omega
. Here
\hat{g}^{(0)}
is the unperturbative static Green function and
\hat{g}^{(\Delta)}
denotes the external field-induced dynamical Green function, which is of the first order in
\Delta_{\Omega}
. Once we solve the Eilenberger equation, the supercurrent can be determined from

\bm{J}^{(0,\Delta)}=\sum_{\lambda}N_{\lambda}v_{F}\text{Tr}[\tau_{3}\langle\bm{n}\hat{g}^{(0,\Delta)}_{\lambda\bm{n}}\rangle_{\bm{n}}].

(9)

Here,
\bm{J}^{(0)}
and
\bm{J}^{(\Delta)}
correspond to DC and AC supercurrents, respectively.
N_{\lambda}=N_{0}(1+\lambda\frac{\alpha}{v_{F}})
is the density of states of band
\lambda
, with
N_{0}
denoting the average density of states. From the condition
\bm{J}^{(0)}(\bm{Q}=\bm{Q}_{0})=0
, we first determine the modulation vector of the helical superconductivity
\bm{Q}_{0}
. Finally, we calculate
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
from

\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}=\bm{J}^{(\Delta)}/\Delta_{\Omega}.

(10)

First we consider
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
in two limiting cases, namely, the pure ballistic and the diffusive limits. In the ballistic limit
\tau^{-1}\to 0
, the system preserves a Galilean symmetry
Crowley and Fu 2022
;
Papaj and Moore 2022
, so that the current is time-independent despite the presence of an external pairing field, and hence
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}=0
. On the other hand, in the diffusive limit
\tau^{-1}\gg\Delta,T
, the two helical bands are strongly mixed by disorder, and both bands are described by the same Usadel equation derived in Ref.
Houzet and Meyer 2015
. This leads to a a suppression of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}=0
( see the supplementary material
sup
)

For intermediate degree of disorder one needs to solve the Eilenberger equation, Eq. (
S.1
). The weak exchange field allows for an analytic solution presented in the supplement
sup
;
cod
. We find a non-monotonic behavior of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
while increasing the disorder strength (Fig.

a). Without the disorder potential,
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
is zero, consistent with the above symmetry analysis. With increasing disorder strength,
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
rapidly reaches its maximum and then decays as a power law. We also verified that
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
vanishes in the diffusive limit, when
1/\tau\Delta_{0}\gg 1

The linear Higgs-light coupling leads to a modification of the admittance of the superconductor. To find the total admittance, we write the order parameter as
\Delta=\Delta_{0}+\delta\Delta(t)
and expand the action up to the second order in
\delta\Delta
and the external field
\bm{A}
,
S=S_{M}+S_{f}
, where
S_{M}
is the mean-field term and
S_{f}
is the fluctuation term given by

S_{f}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{n}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\delta\Delta(-\Omega_{n})\\
\bm{A}(-\Omega_{n})\end{array}\right]^{\intercal}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mkern-5.0mu-\chi_{\Delta\Delta}^{-1}&\chi_{\Delta\bm{A}}\\
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}&\chi_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\delta\Delta(\Omega_{n})\\
\bm{A}(\Omega_{n})\end{array}\right],

(11)

where
\Omega_{n}
is the bosonic Matsubara frequency
\Omega_{n}=2n\pi T
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
\chi_{\Delta\bm{A}}
is defined in Eq. (
10
), whereas

\chi_{\Delta\Delta}
and
\chi_{\Delta\bm{A}}
are defined as
\chi_{\Delta\bm{A}}=\partial F/\partial\bm{A}
, and
\chi_{\Delta\Delta}={\partial F}/{\partial\Delta_{\Omega}}
, where
F
is the pair correlation

\chi_{\Delta A}(\Omega)=\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}(-\Omega)^{*}=\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}(\Omega)

Finally, the field susceptibility is defined as
\chi_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}={\partial\bm{J}}/{\partial\bm{A}}

The pair susceptibility
\chi_{\Delta\Delta}
has a square root singularity at
\Omega=2\Delta_{0}

\tilde{\chi}_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}=\chi_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}+\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}\chi_{\Delta\Delta}\chi_{\Delta\bm{A}};,

(12)

which defines the total admittance
Y=\tilde{\chi}_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}/i\Omega

Conclusion.-

We have shown that the linear Higgs-light coupling exists in a helical superconductor
even without a supercurrent. From a phenomenological Ginzburg-Landau theory, we demonstrate that this linear Higgs-light coupling relies on the terms in the action related to the Lifshitz invariant.
We confirm this result by explicitly calculating the susceptibility
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
of a helical superconductor within a microsocopic theory. We find that
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
reaches its maximum at a weak disorder but vanishes in both clean and diffusive limits. We propose to reduce the mass of the Higgs mode by coupling it with an LC resonator. We demonstrate that the Higgs mode becomes an undamped regular collective mode when its frequency is reduced below the quasiparticle excitation energy
2\Delta_{0}
.

The linear Higgs-light coupling shows up in any system with a Lifshitz invariant. It may therefore be relevant also in the superconducting state of (twisted) multilayer graphene systems where the role of spin is replaced by the valley degree of freedom
Lin et al. 2022
;
Xie et al. 2022
. On the other hand, it would be interesting to study this mechanism in multiband superconductors, where it might allow for a direct visualization of the amplitude modes.

\bm{A}
.
We also show the frequency dependence of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
and explain the vanishing of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
in the diffusive limit.

S2
Calculation of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}

We consider a helical superconductor realized in a 2D superconductor with large Rashba spin-orbit coupling (SOC) under an in-plane magnetic field. To calculate the susceptibility
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
, we start with the generalized Eilenberger equation describing this system in the basis of two helical bands labeled by the index
\lambda=\pm 1

Houzet and Meyer 2015
:

i\bm{n}\cdot\left(\bm{Q}v_{F}/2+\lambda\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}\times\hat{z}\right)[\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}]-\{\partial_{t}\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}\}=[\Delta_{0}\tau_{1}+\Delta_{\Omega}e^{-i\Omega t}\tau_{1}+\hat{\Sigma}_{\lambda\bm{n}},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}].

(S.1)

Here,
\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}
is the quasiclassical Green function of the band
\lambda
with the momentum direction at the Fermi level given by
\bm{n}=\bm{p}/p_{F}
. It is a matrix in the Nambu space, spanned by the Pauli matrices
\tau_{1,2,3}
and the identity matrix 1. The normalization condition
\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{2}=1
holds.
\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}
is the exchange field, and
\hat{z}
is the unit vector perpendicular to the plane of the superconductor.
\bm{Q}
is the phase gradient of the order parameter,

\bm{Q}=\bm{Q}_{0}+\delta\bm{Q}
, where
\bm{Q}_{0}
is the anomalous phase gradient generated by
\bm{h}_{ex}
and
\delta\bm{Q}
is the supercurrent contribution. Here we assume zero DC supercurrent, so that
\bm{Q}=\bm{Q}_{0}
.
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity,
\Delta_{0}
is the saddle point value of the order parameter, and
\Delta_{\Omega}
is the Fourier transform of the time-dependent pairing potential driven by the external perturbation. The matrix
\hat{\Sigma}_{\lambda\bm{n}}
is the self-energy describing the scattering off impurities.

\hat{\Sigma}_{\lambda\bm{n}}=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{1}{4\tau_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\left(\langle\hat{g}_{\lambda^{\prime}\bm{n}^{\prime}}\rangle_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}+\lambda\lambda^{\prime}\bm{n}\cdot\langle\bm{n}^{\prime}\hat{g}_{\lambda^{\prime}\bm{n}^{\prime}}\rangle_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}\right).

(S.2)

Here
\tau_{\lambda}
is the effective scattering time for the band
\lambda
,
\frac{1}{\tau_{\lambda}}=\frac{1}{\tau}(1+\lambda\frac{\alpha}{v_{F}})
, where
\tau
is the impurity scattering time.
\alpha
is Rashba parameter describing the SOC. Note that Eq. (
S.1
) is valid when SOC is larger than all other energy scales except the chemical potential
\mu
, namely
\mu\gtrsim\alpha p_{F}\gg\Delta,h_{\mathrm{ex}},\tau^{-1},Qv_{F}.
In the absence of disorder, the two bands are decoupled, but share the same order parameter. Disorder couples the bands by introducing interband scattering.

Up to linear order in
\Delta_{\Omega}
, the Green function can be written as

\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}=\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(0)}e^{i\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})}+\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(\Delta)}e^{i\omega_{1}t_{1}-\omega_{2}t_{2}},

(S.3)

with
\omega_{1}=\omega
and
\omega_{2}=\omega+\Omega
. Here
\omega
is the fermion Matsubara frequency
\omega=(2n+1)\pi T
, where
n
is an integer and
T
is the temperature while
\Omega
is the boson Matsubara frequency
\Omega=2n\pi T
.
\hat{g}^{(0)}
is the static Green function and
\hat{g}^{(\Delta)}
denotes the external field induced dynamical Green function, which is linear in
\Delta_{\Omega}
. Here we consider a small exchange field limit and treat it as a perturbation. The zeroth order solution in
\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}
can be easily obtained

\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(0)}(t)=\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(00)}e^{i\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})}+\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(\Delta 0)}e^{i\omega t_{1}-i\omega_{2}t_{2}},

(S.4)

with

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(00)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega\tau_{3}+\Delta\tau_{1}}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\Delta^{2}}},

(S.5)

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(\Delta 0)}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\Omega}\frac{\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(00)}(\omega_{1})\tau_{1}\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(00)}(\omega_{2})-\tau_{1}}{s_{1}+s_{2}},

and

\displaystyle s_{1}\equiv s(\omega_{1})=\sqrt{\omega_{1}^{2}+\Delta^{2}},

(S.6)

\displaystyle s_{2}\equiv s(\omega_{2})=\sqrt{\omega_{2}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}.

Note that without the exchange field, the quasiclassical Green function is isotropic and the disorder self-energy has no effect on the Green functions.
Next, we determine corrections to the static and dynamical Green’s functions,
{\bm{g}}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(0h)}
and
\hat{\bm{g}}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(\Delta h)}
, in leading order with respect to
h_{ex}
.

For small
\bm{h}_{ex}
, the Green’s function is almost isotropic, and we can approximate the Green’s function up to the first two terms of the 2D harmonic expansion:

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\hat{g}^{(00)}+\hat{\bm{g}}_{\lambda}^{(0h)}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}},

(S.7)

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(\Delta)}

\displaystyle=\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)}+\hat{\bm{g}}_{\lambda}^{(\Delta h)}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}},

where
\hat{g}^{(00)}
and
\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)}
are the isotropic components of the Green functions defined in Eqs.(
S.5
), and we neglect here the indices
\lambda
and
\bm{n}
.
We first focus on the static part of the Green’s function,
\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}^{(0)}
. Linearization of Eq. (
S.1
) with respect to
\bm{h_{ex}}
gives

\displaystyle 2i\bm{v}_{F}\bm{Q}_{+}\frac{\Delta}{s}\tau_{2}

\displaystyle=s[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}]+\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\right)[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}]+\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}],

(S.8)

\displaystyle 2i\bm{v}_{F}\bm{Q}_{-}\frac{\Delta}{s}\tau_{2}

\displaystyle=s[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}]+\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}\right)[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}]+\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}].

For convenience, we have defined
\bm{Q}_{h}=\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}\times\hat{z}/v_{F}
and
\bm{Q}_{\pm}=\bm{Q}_{0}\pm\bm{Q}_{h}
. We have also used the fact that
Q_{0}
is of the same order of
\bm{h}_{ex}
.
The solution of these equations reads

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}

\displaystyle=\frac{iv_{F}\Delta\hat{g}^{(00)}\tau_{2}\bm{X}_{+}}{sY},

(S.9)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}

\displaystyle=\frac{iv_{F}\Delta\hat{g}^{(00)}\tau_{2}\bm{X}_{-}}{sY},

with

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{+}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(s+\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}\right)\bm{Q}_{+}-\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}\bm{Q}_{-}\right],

(S.10)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{-}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(s+\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\right)\bm{Q}_{-}-\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\bm{Q}_{+}\right],

and

Y=\left(s+\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\right)\left(s+\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}\right)-\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}.

(S.11)

Here
s=\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
. This static Green function is used to determine the anomalous phase gradient
\bm{Q}_{0}
above Eq. (10) in the main text.

To get the dynamic part of the Green’s function, we expand the Eilenberger equation. Eq. (
S.1
), in
\Delta_{\Omega}
and
\bm{Q}
. Linear terms give:

\displaystyle v\bm{Q}_{+}[\tau_{3},\hat{{g}}_{+}^{(\Delta 0)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(\Delta h)}]+\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau+}\right)[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(\Delta h)}]+\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau+}\right)[\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}]

(S.12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(\Delta h)}]+\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}[\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}]+[\Delta_{\Omega}\tau_{1},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}],

and

\displaystyle v\bm{Q}_{-}[\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{-}^{(\Delta 0)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(\Delta h)}]+\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\tau-}\right)[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(\Delta h)}]+\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\tau-}\right)[\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}]

(S.13)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}[\hat{g}^{(00)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(\Delta h)}]+\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}[\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}]+[\Delta_{\Omega}\tau_{1},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}].

The solution reads

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(\Delta h)}=\frac{\hat{g}^{(00)}\left[\left(s_{1}+s_{2}+\frac{1}{2\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}\right)\bm{Z}_{+}-\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}\bm{Z}_{-}\right]}{Y_{2}}\Delta_{\Omega},

(S.14)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(\Delta h)}=\frac{\hat{g}^{(00)}\left[\left(s_{1}+s_{2}+\frac{1}{2\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}\right)\bm{Z}_{-}-\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}\bm{Z}_{+}\right]}{Y_{2}}\Delta_{\Omega},

(S.15)

with

\displaystyle Y_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(s_{1}+s_{2}+\frac{1}{2\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}\right)\left(s_{1}+s_{2}+\frac{1}{2\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}\right)-\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}},

(S.16)

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v\bm{Q}_{+}[\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{+}^{(\Delta 0)}]-\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\right)[\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}]-\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}[\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}]+[\Delta_{\Omega}\tau_{1}e^{i\Omega t},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}]

(S.17)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(s_{2}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\right)\{\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}\}-\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}\{\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}\},

and

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v\bm{Q}_{-}[\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{-}^{(\Delta 0)}]-\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}\right)[\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}]-\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}[\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}]+[\Delta_{\Omega}\tau_{1}e^{i\Omega t},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}]

(S.18)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(s_{2}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}\right)\{\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(0h)}\}-\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\{\hat{g}^{(\Delta 0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(0h)}\}.

The pair-spin susceptibility is then given by

\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}=\sum_{\lambda}N_{\lambda}\text{Tr}\left[v_{F}\tau_{3}\hat{\bm{g}}_{\lambda}^{(\Delta h)}\right]/\Delta_{\Omega}.

(S.19)

The spin-pair susceptibility
\chi_{\Delta\bm{A}}
is related to
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
by
\chi_{\Delta\bm{A}}(\Omega)=\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}(-\Omega)^{*}=\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}(\Omega)
. These expressions of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
and
\chi_{\Delta\bm{A}}
are used in plotting Fig. (2-4) in the main text.

S3
calculation of
\chi_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}

In order to find
\chi_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}
we need to consider the Eilenberger equation with an external electromagnetic field
\bm{A}_{\Omega}

i\bm{n}\cdot\left(\bm{Q}_{0}v_{F}/2+\lambda\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}\times\hat{z}\right)[\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}]-\{\partial_{t}\tau_{3},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}\}=[\Delta_{0}\tau_{1}+\bm{A}_{\Omega}\cdot\bm{n}\tau_{3}e^{-i\Omega t}+\hat{\Sigma}_{\lambda\bm{n}},\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}].

(S.20)

Unlike calculating
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
, here we only consider the zeroth order term in
\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}
. We expand the Green function as

\hat{g}_{\lambda\bm{n}}=\hat{g}^{(0)}e^{i\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})}+\bm{n}\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\lambda}^{(A)}e^{i\omega_{1}t_{1}-i\omega_{2}t_{2}}.

(S.21)

where
\hat{g}^{(0)}
is the static Green function and
\hat{\bm{g}}_{\lambda}^{(A)}
is the dynamical Green function induced by
\bm{A}_{\Omega}
.
Keeping only the first order terms in
\bm{A}_{\Omega}
we have

\displaystyle s_{1}\hat{g}^{(0)}\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(A)}-s_{2}\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(A)}\hat{g}^{(0)}=\bm{A}_{\Omega}[\tau_{3},\hat{g}^{(0)}]-\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}\right)[\hat{g}^{(0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(A)}]-\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}[\hat{g}^{(0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(A)}],

(S.22)

\displaystyle s_{1}\hat{g}^{(0)}\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(A)}-s_{2}\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(A)}\hat{g}^{(0)}=\bm{A}_{\Omega}[\tau_{3},\hat{g}^{(0)}]-\left(\frac{1}{4\tau_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{-}}\right)[\hat{g}^{(0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(A)}]-\frac{1}{8\tau_{+}}[\hat{g}^{(0)},\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(A)}].

(S.22)

Solving these equations, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{g}}_{+}^{(A)}=\bm{A}_{\Omega}\frac{M_{+}\left[\hat{g}^{(0)}(\omega_{1})\tau_{3}\hat{g}^{(0)}(\omega_{2})-\tau_{3}\right]}{N_{+}},

(S.23)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{g}}_{-}^{(A)}=\bm{A}_{\Omega}\frac{M_{-}\left[\hat{g}^{(0)}(\omega_{1})\tau_{3}\hat{g}^{(0)}(\omega_{2})-\tau_{3}\right]}{N_{-}},

(S.23)

with

\displaystyle M_{\pm}

\displaystyle=s_{1}+s_{2}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{\pm}},

(S.24)

\displaystyle N_{+}

\displaystyle=\left(s_{1}+s_{2}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{-}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{\pm}}\right)\left(s_{1}+s_{2}+\frac{1}{4\tau_{\pm}}+\frac{1}{8\tau_{\mp}}\right)-\frac{1}{64\tau_{\pm}\tau_{\mp}}.

The field susceptibility
\chi_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}
is given by

\chi_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}=\sum_{\lambda}N_{\lambda}\text{Tr}\left[v_{F}\tau_{3}\hat{g}_{\lambda}^{(A)}\right]/A_{\Omega}.

(S.25)

This expression of
\chi_{\bm{A}\bm{A}}
is used in plotting Fig. (2-3) in the main text.

We have shown in the main text that in helical superconductors both the real part and imaginary part of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
can be finite at
\Omega=2\Delta_{0}
. Here, we calculate the full frequency dependence of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
, obtained from Eq. (
S.19
). The results are shown in Fig.
S1
.

Figure S1:
The pair spin susceptibility
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
as a function of frequency. The blue and red lines denotes the real and imaginary parts, respectively. The parameters used here are:

,

S5
Vanishing of
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}
in the diffusive limit

In the diffusive limit
\alpha p_{F}\gg\tau^{-1}\gg\Delta_{0},T,h_{ex}
, the two helical bands are strongly mixed by disorder. Both helical bands are described by the same quasiclassical Green’s function averaged over the Fermi surface,
\hat{g}=\langle\hat{g}_{\pm 1\bm{n}}\rangle_{\bm{n}}
, which satisfies the Usadel equation
Houzet and Meyer 2015

-\frac{D}{4}[\bm{Q}^{\prime}\tau_{3},\hat{g}[\bm{Q}^{\prime}\tau_{3},\hat{g}]]-\{\partial_{t}\tau_{3},\hat{g}\}=[\Delta_{0}\tau_{1}+\Delta_{\Omega}e^{-i\Omega t}\tau_{1}+\Gamma\tau_{3}\hat{g}\tau_{3},\hat{g}].

(S28)

Here, the role of the magnetic field is two-fold. First, it introduces the a shift to the gauge-invariant condensate momentum
\bm{Q}^{\prime}=\bm{Q}+\bm{A}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, where
\bm{A}_{\mathrm{eff}}=4\alpha\bm{h}_{\mathrm{ex}}\times\hat{z}/(\alpha^{2}+v_{F}^{2})
. Second, it introduces a depairing rate
\Gamma=2\tau h_{\mathrm{ex}}^{2}(v_{F}^{2}-\alpha^{2})/(v_{F}^{2}+\alpha^{2})
. The diffusion constant is
D=\tau(v_{F}^{2}+\alpha^{2})/2
.

In the ground state, the minimization of the energy requires that the gauge-invariant condensate momentum vanishes, and therefore
\bm{Q}_{0}+\bm{A}_{\mathrm{eff}}=0
. From here, we see that the only effect of the magnetic field is to introduce depairing rate
\Gamma
, and Higgs-light coupling is absent:
\chi_{\bm{A}\Delta}=0
.
