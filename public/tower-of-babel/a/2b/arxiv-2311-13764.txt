Work-Efficient Parallel Derandomization I: Chernoff-like Concentrations via Pairwise Independence
Mohsen Ghaffari, Christoph Grunau, Václav Rozhoň

Abstract
We present a novel technique for work-efficient parallel derandomization, for algorithms that rely on the concentration of measure bounds such as Chernoff, Hoeffding, and Bernstein inequalities. Our method increases the algorithm’s computational work and depth by only polylogarithmic factors. Before our work, the only known method to obtain parallel derandomization with such strong concentrations was by the results of [Motwani, Naor, and Naor FOCS’89; Berger and Rompel FOCS’89], which perform a binary search in a k k -wise independent space for k = poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) k=\operatorname{poly}(\log n) . However, that method blows up the computational work by a high poly ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{poly}(n) factor and does not yield work-efficient parallel algorithms. Their method was an extension of the approach of [Luby FOCS’88], which gave a work-efficient derandomization but was limited to algorithms analyzed with only pairwise independence. Pushing the method from pairwise to the higher k k -wise analysis resulted in the poly ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{poly}(n) factor computational work blow-up. Our work can be viewed as an alternative extension from the pairwise case, which yields the desired strong concentrations while retaining work efficiency up to logarithmic factors. Our approach works by casting the problem of determining the random variables as an iterative process with poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \operatorname{poly}(\log n) iterations, where different iterations have independent randomness. This is done so that for the desired concentrations, we need only pairwise independence inside each iteration. In particular, we model each binary random variable as a result of a gradual random walk, and our method shows that the desired Chernoff-like concentrations about the endpoints of these walks can be boiled down to some pairwise analysis on the steps of these random walks in each iteration (while having independence across iterations). Hence, we can fix the randomness of each iteration efficiently before proceeding to the next.

Introduction

Probability concentration results such as the Chernoff bound are the bread-and-butter of randomized algorithms
[
MR95
,
DP09
]
. There are known methods for derandomizing parallel algorithms that rely on these concentrations—see, e.g.,
[
MNN89
,
BR89
,
NN90
,
Har19
]
—but all these methods blow up the algorithm’s computational work by a large polynomial. Hence, they fall short of yielding work-efficient deterministic parallel algorithms. In this paper, we present the first generic nearly work-efficient parallel derandomization technique for algorithms that rely on these strong concentrations. This technique increases the algorithm’s computational work and depth by only polylogarithmic factors.

1.1
Background: parallel work-efficiency, and typical concentration setups

Work-efficiency in parallel algorithms:
Devising (nearly) work-efficient algorithms with small depth is the ultimate goal in designing parallel algorithms, as that leads to algorithms that can run faster than their sequential counterparts, using a reasonable number of processors. Let us briefly review this. We follow the standard work/depth terminology, see e.g.,
[
JáJ92
, Section 1.5]
or
[
Ble96
]
. For any algorithm, its
work
is defined as the total number of operations. Its
depth
is defined as the longest chain of operations with sequential dependencies, in the sense that the
(i+1)^{th}
operation depends on (and should wait for) the results of operations
i
in the chain.
Given
p
processors, an algorithm with work
W
and depth
D
can be run in
W/p+D
time
[
Bre74
]
. If a parallel algorithm has work polynomially larger than its sequential counterpart, we would need at least polynomially many processors so that the parallel algorithm runs faster than the sequential one. This would severely limit the relevance of such parallel algorithms. As such, the ultimate goal in parallel algorithms is to devise algorithms with work asymptotically equal (or close, e.g., equal up to logarithmic factors) to their sequential counterpart, which also have a small depth. Such algorithms are referred to as
work-efficient
(correspondingly,
nearly work-efficient
) parallel algorithms. See
[
KR89
,
JáJ92
]
for some of the classic examples, and
[
Fin18
,
JLS19
,
BGSS20
,
Li20
,
ASZ20
,
CFR20
,
DBS21
,
AB21
,
RGH+22
,
REGH22
]
for some of the more recent nearly work-efficient parallel algorithms.

Typical algorithmic setups where concentration bounds are used

Randomized (parallel) algorithms frequently use concentration bounds such as Chernoff, Hoeffding, and Bernstein inequalities. For instance, a simplified form of the Chernoff bound tells us that, for independent random variables
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
\dots
,
X_{\ell}\in[0,1]
and
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{j}
, and for any
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, we have
\textrm{P}[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X]]\leq 2\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]/3)
. The prototypical application setup is as follows: we have many independent random variables, and many constraints on these variables (often linear or even polynomial many constraints in terms of the number of variables), each of which requires the summation of a subset of the random variables being in a certain range. Chernoff bound allows us to conclude that each summation will be within a small (constant) factor of its expectation, assuming that the expectations are large enough to allow a union bound over different constraints/summations. Let us now have a brief look at two of the common scenarios.

•

Sampling.
One routine application is when we use randomness to find a small/sparse
sample
of the data, which provides good representations. Here is a concrete example. Consider an
n
-node undirected graph
G=(V,E)
. Using random sampling, along with Chernoff and union bound, we can sample a subset
T\subseteq V
of size
(1\pm\varepsilon)pn
that satisfies the following: for each node
v\in V
, whose degree is denoted by
d_{v}
, the number of neighbors of
v
in set
T
is upper and lower bounded by
(1\pm\varepsilon)d_{v}p
, assuming
d_{v}=\Omega((\log n)/p)
, and where
\varepsilon>0
is a desirably small constant.

•

Rounding.
Another common application is
randomized rounding
, e.g., for linear programs. Roughly speaking, we cast the problem at hand as some integer linear program, solve the fractional relaxation of it efficiently, and then use randomized rounding to turn these fractional solutions into integral solutions, with a small loss in the objectives. Here is a classic example
[
RT87
]
: Consider a multi-commodity routing instance where for each commodity
c
, we are given a collection
P_{c}
of paths from the commodity’s source node to its target node, and we should choose exactly one of these (for the integral solution). The congestion of an edge
e
is the number of chosen paths that include edge
e
, and we want to have small congestion in all edges. A fractional solution provides a probability distribution for each commodity
c
over
P_{c}
. Given a fractional solution where each edge has expected congestion
O(\log n)
, via a simple application of Chernoff and union bounds, we get that picking the paths according to their distributions yields an integral solution that has congestion
O(\log n)
.

1.2
State of the art in parallel derandomization

We next overview the known derandomization methods for algorithms that rely on the aforementioned concentrations. We first discuss the standard method of conditional probabilities, frequently used in sequential derandomizations, and then overview the parallel derandomization techniques.

Method of conditional probabilities

A basic and commonly used method in sequential derandomizations is via conditional probabilities/expectations
[
Rag86
]
. We use the expected number of violated constraints (e.g., in the sampling example above, the expected number of nodes whose number of neighbors in the sample is outside the allowed range) as a potential function. Initially, this expectation is strictly below
1
. We then iteratively fix the variables one by one (e.g., in the sampling example, determining whether the node is in the sample or not) while ensuring that the potential—i.e., the conditional expectation of the number of violated constraints—does not increase. Once all variables have been fixed, we have an assignment without any violated constraint. The computational efficiency of this approach is heavily dictated by how fast we can compute the conditional expectations in each iteration. Regardless, the method is inherently sequential and relies on fixing the variables one by one. As such, it cannot be used to obtain fast parallel algorithms, i.e., algorithms with depth polylogarithmic or even sublinear in the number of variables.

Using small spaces of randomness

One general theme in derandomizations, especially pioneered in and used for parallel algorithms, is to devise/revise the randomized algorithm such that its analysis relies on weaker properties of the randomness so that we can assume that the randomness comes from a small space. Prominent examples are algorithms analyzed using only pairwise independence
[
Lub86
,
ABI86
]
or
k
-wise independence for a constant
k

[
ABI86
]
, and algorithms using
\delta
-dependent
k
-wise random variables and small-bias spaces
[
NN90
]
. These methods ensure that the randomness of the algorithm comes from a polynomially sized space. Hence, one can enumerate and try all possibilities of randomness (in parallel) and find the one that satisfies all constraints. Of course, that increases the computational work by a factor linear in the space size. Unfortunately, it is well-known that this method requires spaces that are non-trivially large polynomials.
1
1

1

For instance, assuming
n
variables,
k
-wise spaces need size
\Theta(n^{k})
, and
\delta
-dependent
k
-wise spaces have size at least
\Omega({1/\delta})
where we usually need
\delta\leq 1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
for the desired concentrations to allow union bounds over all constraints
[
NN90
]
.
Hence, these methods blow up the algorithm’s work by a large polynomial, and cannot yield (nearly) work-efficient parallel algorithms.

Binary search through the small space of randomness

Luby
[
Lub88
]
pointed out that the above derandomization method—in particular the
k
-wise independence used in
[
Lub86
,
ABI86
]
—is far from work-efficient. He phrased this as having a prohibitively large penalty on the number of required processors. To fix that, he pioneered a method for parallel derandomization by using a binary search in the small space of randomness. He crafted this for two problems, maximal independent set, and
\Delta+1
coloring. Concretely, his approach is as follows: both cases can be analyzed using pairwise independence, and hence
\Theta(\log n)
bits of randomness suffice for them. However, instead of simply brute-forcing through all the
2^{\Theta(\log n)}=\operatorname{poly}(n)
possibilities of these random strings, Luby effectively performs a binary search in this space, by fixing the bits of randomness one by one. This takes
O(\log n)
steps, one for each bit. In each step, the method requires computing the conditional expectation of the number of violated constraints (or a suitable pessimistic estimator of it, which is still sufficiently small), for each of the two possible choices of the next bit. Then, we simply choose the bit so that we do not increase the conditional expectation. In a sense, this is an elegant combination of the two methods outlined above, conditional probabilities and small space. The computational efficiency of this method is dictated by how fast we can compute the conditional expectations in each step.

Luby
[
Lub88
]
showed that in the two particular cases of maximal independent set and coloring, the algorithms rely on certain pairwise analyses with extra nice properties.
2
2

2

See
Section

3
for a precise definition of these nice pairwise analyses.
He then used a well-structured pairwise independent space (where each random variable bit is the dot product of the variable’s characteristic vector with the seed of randomness) and showed that the conditional expectations in this space can be computed using near-linear work. We note that this is not always easy, because the
k
-wise independence is lost after some bits are fixed. This led to work-efficient parallel derandomizations for maximal independent set and coloring.
The method has been extended to some other problems analyzed with pairwise independence, including set cover approximations and minimum dominating set
[
BRS89
,
BEG+22
,
GGH+23
]
. We remark that these include some instances where the obtained concentration is better than what is implied directly by pairwise independence. However, that is only for a very limited
hitting
type of event

3

Hitting events are events of the following form: For a ground set
[n]
, we want to choose a small sample set
T\subset[n]
such that for every given set
S_{i}\subset[n]
for
i\in m
, we have
S_{i}\cap T\neq\emptyset
. However, these events do not provide good bounds on
|S_{i}\cap T|
. Moreover, the method used for proving them crucially relies on this simple event, as a single hit suffices and we can use repetitions to amplify the probability of having at least one single hit.
and does not address the wide range of Chernoff applications, including the two prototypical examples mentioned in

.

The clear shortcoming of the above is that pairwise independence does not yield strong concentrations. Whether there are (similar) nearly work-efficient parallel derandomizations for stronger concentrations, like the Chernoff bound, has remained open for the past 35 years.

The method of binary search in the space of randomness has been extended to
k
-wise analysis for higher values of
k
, up to
k=\operatorname{poly}(\log n)

[
MNN89
,
BR89
]
. However, this has been at the cost of a polynomial increase in work and does not yield work-efficient derandomizations. Notice that for such high values of
k
, without the binary search, the space size
O(n^{k})
would be super polynomial, and thus the brute-force approach in the space would require a superpolynomial blowup in computation work. Motwani, Naor, and Naor
[
MNN89
]
, and simultaneously and independently Berger and Rompel
[
BR89
]
, showed how to perform this binary search to obtain strong concentrations like those implied by the Chernoff bound (this requires choosing

m^{2}
factor where
m
is the number of constraints). Concretely, when computing the conditional expectations to perform a binary search in a
k
-wise space, once we adjust
k
to have a failure probability less than
1/m
for each of
m
constraints, the conditional expectation can have
\operatorname{poly}(m)
terms, and computing each of these terms incurs some work. The most recent and the best-known improvement in reducing this polynomial blow-up in the computational work was a paper of Harris
[
Har19
]
, but the improvement cuts the polynomial’s exponent by only a
2
factor and remains far away from work-efficiency.
4
4

4

Concretely, the computational work for simulating
k
-wise independence on
m
constraints and
n
variables is reduced from the

bound of
[

to

However, we note that this result has the advantage of getting the sharp concentration for all ranges of
\varepsilon

[
MRS01
]
.

Our technique can be viewed as a completely different approach to extending Luby’s results
[
Lub88
]
, which yields the desired strong concentrations while retaining the work-efficiency up to logarithmic factors.

1.3
Our results

A vanilla version of our derandomization result, as a starter

Our most general and strongest derandomization results are somewhat notation-heavy. Hence, before stating those, we state a weaker but cleaner form that captures some of the basic concentration setups in parallel derandomization.

Theorem 1.1

(a vanilla version of our derandomization)
.

Suppose we are given sets
S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{m}\subseteq[1,n]
, where
m\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
, and two given parameters
p\in[0,1]
and
\varepsilon\in[1/\operatorname{poly}(\log n),1]
such that for each
i\in[1,m]
, we have
|S_{i}|\geq\Omega(\frac{\log m}{p\varepsilon^{2}})
. Randomly including each element of
[1,n]
in a set
T
with probability
p
would provide a set
T
such that, for every
i\in[1,m]
, we have
|S_{i}\cap T|\in(1\pm\varepsilon)p|S_{i}|
.
5
5

5

We use the notation
z=(1\pm\varepsilon)z^{\prime}
as a shorthand for
z\in[(1-\varepsilon)z^{\prime},(1+\varepsilon)z^{\prime}]
.
There is a deterministic parallel algorithm that computes such a set
T
, using depth
\operatorname{poly}(\log nm)
and work
\tilde{O}(\sum_{i}|S_{i}|+n+m)
, which is linear in the input size up to logarithmic factors.

For instance, this theorem nicely captures the sampling example in a graph
G=(V,E)
that we discussed in
Section

1.1
.
There, we would have
m=n=|V|
sets, one for each node and simply equal to its set of neighbors. Then we get a sample set
T
such that each node
v
has
(1\pm\varepsilon)pd_{v}
neighbors in
T
. The algorithm would have depth
\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
and work
\tilde{O}(\sum_{j}|S_{j}|+m+n)=\tilde{O}(|E|+|V|).

Another remark is that the astute reader may have noticed that the theorem statement has a lower bound on
\varepsilon
by assuming
\varepsilon\in[1/\operatorname{poly}(\log n),1]
. This is unlike the Chernoff bound where we could allow
\varepsilon
to be an arbitrarily small value in
[0,1]
. We could phrase the theorem in a more general manner without a lower bound assumption on
\varepsilon
, but then the algorithm’s work and depth would increase by a
\operatorname{poly}(\log n/\varepsilon)
factor. This is indeed a drawback of our current method, though we note that many applications in parallel algorithms are fine with a constant or polylogarithmically sub-constant deviation factor
\varepsilon
. See
Section

1.4
for a follow-up work that removes this assumption and obtains an efficient parallel algorithm with the optimal concentration for all ranges of
\varepsilon
.

Generalizations, and the relevant notations

There are several dimensions to consider in generalizations. We next discuss these and gradually introduce the notations that we use in our statements.

(1)

Different random variables might have different probabilities. We consider
n
binary random variables and use the notation
p_{j}
to denote the probability of the
j^{th}
variable being equal to
1
. Hence, a part of the input to the problem is a vector
\mathbf{p}\in[0,1]^{n}
. The output is to compute a
0/1
setting of variables, i.e., a vector
\mathbf{q}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, such that
\mathbf{q}
is close to
\mathbf{p}
in the way that we discuss next.

(2)

We consider
m
different constraints on the variables, and each constraint might have different coefficients for different random variables that it involves. To encode these constraints, we use a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
, where
a_{ij}
denotes the entry in the
i^{th}
row and
j^{th}
colum. Given an
m
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
of the allowed deviations in different constraints, our ideal objective is to compute an output vector
\mathbf{q}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that for every
i\in[m]
we have
|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\Delta_{i}.
For instance, a common useful setting of
\mathbf{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
would be to set
\Delta_{i}=\varepsilon(\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}p_{j}),
for a desirably small deviation factor
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. In this case, our constraints would boil down to
A\mathbf{q}\in(1\pm\varepsilon)A\mathbf{p}
, in a coordinate-wise sense. Notice that the input size is
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m))
, where
nnz(A)
denotes the number of non-zero entries in
A
. Our derandomizations provide deterministic algorithms that have computational work nearly linear in this input size, and depth polylogarithmic in it.

(3)

We might want something weaker than satisfying all the constraints simultaneously. This is to widen the applicability range and it often brings useful flexibility to the algorithms. For instance, we could wish to have only most of the constraints satisfied. Even stronger, we can have the number of violated constraints upper bounded by the bound that we would have on the expected number of violations if we apply the Chernoff bound to each constraint.
6
6

6

Instead of just the number of violated constraints, we could generalize by assuming an importance score for each constraint and get a bound on the importance score of the violated constraints. We omit that generalization, as its proof is the same.
Concretely, for each constraint
i\in[m]
, we can provide an upper bound
prob^{bad}_{i}
on
\textrm{P}[|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|>\Delta_{i}]
, ideally similar to the upper bound provided by Chernoff or Hoeffding bounds, and we would ensure that for the set
I^{bad}\subseteq[m]
of violated constraints, we have
I^{bad}\subseteq[m]
with
|I^{bad}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad}_{i}
.

(4)

Finally, the concentration exponents that we obtain depend on a granularity parameter
k
to be chosen. We defer the reader to
Section

2
for more on this parameter. Our concentration exponents often have a term that grows linearly with
k
. We cannot set
k
arbitrarily large at no cost, because
k
appears also in the computational work and depth of our deterministic algorithms as a polynomial factor. For most applications, the reader can view
k
as a large
\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
. That usually gives strong enough concentrations that can be union bounded over polynomial many constraints, for allowed deviations that are an
\varepsilon
factor of the expectation for
\varepsilon\in[1/\operatorname{poly}(\log n),1]
. Furthermore, it keeps our algorithm’s computational work nearly linear in the input size and depth polylogarithmic.

Having introduced these notations, and the general setup for our derandomization results, we are now ready to state our main derandomization theorems.

Main results—Hoeffding-like concentrations

Our first result provides concentrations that are comparable to Hoeffding’s inequality
[
Hoe94
, Theorem 2]
. Recall that for independent random variables
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
\dots
,
X_{\ell}\in[0,a_{j}]
and
X:=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}X_{j}
, and given a
\Delta\geq 0
, Hoeffding shows that
\textrm{P}[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq\Delta]\leq 2\exp(-\Delta^{2}/(\sum_{j}a^{2}_{j})).
The following theorem presents our parallel derandomization analogue.

Theorem 1.2

(Hoeffding-Like Concentration)
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
n,m,k\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary and
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
. Also, let
\mathbf{p}\in[0,1]^{n}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
. For each
i\in[m]
, let

prob^{bad}_{i}:=c\exp\left(-(1/c)\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}^{2}},\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}}\right)\right).

There exists a deterministic parallel algorithm with
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k)
depth that outputs a vector
\mathbf{q}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and a set
I^{bad}\subseteq[m]
with
|I^{bad}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad}_{i}
such that for every
i\in[m]\setminus I^{bad}
it holds that

|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\Delta_{i}.

Let us provide some intuition for the provided guarantee and compare it to Hoeffding’s inequality. Notice that in the definition of
prob^{bad}_{i}
, the exponent of the exponential is equal to the minimum of two terms, up to a constant. The first term exactly matches the exponent provided by Hoeffdings inequality, as reviewed above. The second term is impacted by our granularity parameter
k
. For instance, for the intuitive and frequently used parameter range where we allow deviation
\mathbf{\Delta}=\varepsilon A\mathbf{p}
, and thus want
A\mathbf{q}\in(1\pm\varepsilon)A\mathbf{p}
, we can simplify the second exponent term as
\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}}=\varepsilon k
. Hence, our concentration exponent is the minimum of that of Hoeffding and
\varepsilon k
. As stated before, the main target regime for us is deviation factors
\varepsilon\in[1/\operatorname{poly}(\log n),1]
. Therefore, by setting
k=\Theta(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log n)\in\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
, we can easily ensure that the second exponent term is
\Omega(\log m)
, which means it would be good enough to union bound over all constraints and thus it is not a limiting factor. As such, we would recover the guarantees provided by Hoeffdings inequality while keeping the algorithm’s work bound
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m))
and its depth
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm))
.

Main results—Chernoff-like concentrations

Our second result provides concentrations that are comparable with Chernoff’s bound. See
Section

1.1
for a reminder on the statement. The following theorem has concentrations that match Chernoff’s in the case where the constraint coefficients are uniform. Thus, it proves the Vanilla version stated in
Theorem

1.1
. This statement is presented more generally, and itself will be used for proving our Bernstein-like concentration statement (
Theorem

1.4

Theorem 1.3

(Chernoff-Like Concentration)
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
n,m,k\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary and
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
. Also, let
\mathbf{p}\in[0,1]^{n}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
. For each
i\in[m]
, let

prob^{bad}_{i}:=c\exp\left(-(1/c)\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij})},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}},\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}}\right)\right).

There exists a deterministic parallel algorithm with
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k)
depth that outputs a vector
\mathbf{q}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and a set
I^{bad}\subseteq[m]
with
|I^{bad}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad}_{i}
such that for every
i\in[m]\setminus I^{bad}
it holds that

|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\Delta_{i}.

Again, let us discuss the provided guarantee intuitively. An interesting and simple regime to consider, which corresponds to
Theorem

1.1
, is when each nonzero
a_{ij}
is equal to
1
, and where we define
\mathbf{\Delta}=\varepsilon A\mathbf{p}
for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. Let us focus on one constraint
i\in[m]
and define
\mu=\sum_{j}a_{ij}p_{j}
, that is,
\mu
is the expectation of the value of the
i^{th}
constraint under the input random assignment. Then, in
prob^{bad}_{i}
, up to a constant factor, the concentration exponent is equal to
\min(\varepsilon^{2}\mu,\varepsilon\mu,\varepsilon k)=\min(\varepsilon^{2}\mu,\varepsilon k)
. Notice that
\varepsilon^{2}\mu
is the familiar exponent of the Chernoff bound. As before, the main target regime for us is deviation factors
\varepsilon\in[1/\operatorname{poly}(\log n),1]
. Therefore, by setting
k=\Theta(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log n)\in\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
, we can easily ensure that the second exponent term is
\Omega(\log m)
, which means it would be good enough to union bound over all constraints and thus it is not a limiting factor. Hence,
Theorem

1.1
is simply a special case of
Theorem

1.3
.

Main results—Bernstein-like concentrations

Our third result provides concentrations that are comparable with Bernstein’s bound
[
DP09
, Theorem 8.1]
(also
Lemma

3.2
), modulo a small loss. Recall that for independent random variables

2
An overview of our method

Our approach can be viewed as going opposite to the conventional derandomization wisdom of directly reducing the number of random bits in the randomized algorithm (and thus the space of the required randomness). Instead, we cast the task of fixing the random values as a gradual process with
\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
iterations. We use only pairwise independence inside each iteration, but full independence across different iterations. Hence, the total number of bits in the random process is a higher
\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
, in contrast with the
O(\log^{2}n)
bits of
O(\log n)
-wise independence, or the
O(\log n)
bits of
(1/\operatorname{poly}(n))
-dependent
O(\log n)
-wise independent space, which are often sufficient for the desired concentrations in randomized algorithms
[
MNN89
,
NN93
]
. In a sense, we will view each binary random variable as a gradual random walk, taking only a small step in each iteration in a random direction (we will use pairwise independence between the directions of different walks). Our slowed-down process gives us a lot of useful structure within each iteration: we can boil down the desired concentrations of the final random variables to some nice pairwise analyses inside each iteration. Utilizing this, we use known methods to fix the randomness of each iteration in a work-efficient manner, before proceeding to the next iteration.

Next, we provide a more concrete overview of our method, in the context of a simple concentration problem. This will be even weaker than our Vanilla result stated in
Theorem

1.1
, but it already delivers most of the key ideas. We then mention some of the ideas one can use to generalize from this and get
Theorem

1.1
. We will provide the actual proofs only for our main results, which are more general; this generality is needed in connecting different results. We do not attempt to summarize their ideas here.

Pairwise analysis of the deviation

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(|S_{i}|/6)^{2}}{4\max\left(100k^{2}\cdot|S_{i}|/k^{2},|S_{i}|/6\cdot|S_{i}|/k\right)}\right)

A sequential derandomization

Before explaining how we derandomize

Sequential derandomization of the concentration bounds

This sequential algorithm directly derandomizes the trivial randomized solution of sampling the value of each element randomly. This is using a certain method of conditional expectations
[
Rag86
]
, with pessimistic estimators coming from the proof of the Chernoff bound. In particular, we determine the values of random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\in\{-1,+1\}
, where
X_{j}=1
iff the element

is in class

At the time step

X_{1}=x_{1},X_{2}=x_{2},\dots,X_{t}=x_{t}

S_{i}
, we keep track of the following two potentials,
\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t}

\displaystyle\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq t^{\prime}\leq t\\
t^{\prime}\in S_{i}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\lambda x_{t^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}x_{t^{\prime}}^{2}\right)\cdot\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right)^{|S_{i}\cap[t+1,n]|}

(6)

\displaystyle\Phi^{(2)}_{i,t}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq t^{\prime}\leq t\\
t^{\prime}\in S_{i}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\lambda x_{t^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}x_{t^{\prime}}^{2}\right)\cdot\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right)^{|S_{i}\cap[t+1,n]|}

(7)

for

\displaystyle\lambda=1/10.

These potentials can be seen as corresponding to the ingredients of proof of a simple version of the standard Chernoff bound
[

]
: To understand the connection, note that one way to derive a version of Chernoff bound for a sum of independent random variables

, where
\textrm{P}(X_{j}=\pm 1)=1/2
, is to consider the expression
\prod_{j\in[n]}\textrm{e}^{\lambda X_{j}}
and apply Markov inequality to its expectation. Importantly, to (approximately) compute the value of
\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j\in[n]}\textrm{e}^{\lambda X_{j}}\right]=\prod_{j\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\textrm{e}^{\lambda X_{j}}\right]
, it is enough to approximate each partial term in the multiplication up to second order terms, i.e., the proof uses
\textrm{e}^{\lambda X_{j}}\leq 1+\lambda X_{j}+\lambda^{2}X_{j}^{2}

which are still uniformly random in

and remain to be decided.

We also define an overall potential

YES

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pot}_{t}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i\in[m]}\left(\frac{\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t}}{\Phi^{(1)}_{i,0}}+\frac{\Phi^{(2)}_{i,t}}{\Phi^{(2)}_{i,0}}\right)

Our deterministic algorithm will simply iterate over the elements of

1.

The overall potential

We start by noting that
\mathbb{E}[1\pm\lambda x_{t^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}x_{t^{\prime}}^{2}]=1\pm 0+\lambda^{2}
. This implies that
\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}[\mathrm{Pot}_{t}]=\mathrm{Pot}_{t-1}
, hence one of the two values of

2.

No bad event happens:

Note that we set up our potential in such a way that

. Our task is to prove that a bad event would lead to

. To see this, assume that the set

\displaystyle\Phi^{(1)}_{i,n}\geq\prod_{1\leq t^{\prime}\leq n}\left(1+\lambda x_{t^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}x_{t^{\prime}}^{2}\right)\geq\prod_{1\leq t^{\prime}\leq n}\textrm{e}^{\lambda x_{t^{\prime}}}=\textrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j}}\geq\textrm{e}^{|S_{i}|/30}

On the other hand, we compute that

\displaystyle\Phi^{(1)}_{i,0}=\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right)^{|S_{i}|}\leq\textrm{e}^{|S_{i}|/100}

2.3
Parallel derandomization, using pairwise independence per step

Let us now go back to our task – work-efficient parallel derandomization of the randomized process in

We do this using two ingredients. The first one is the method discussed above to derandomize Chernoff bounds; we apply it to derandomize Bernstein’s inequality used in the analysis of

One notable difference with the sequential Chernoff bound derandomization is this: in the sequential derandomization of Chernoff, in every step, we only needed to decide on two possible values of a binary random variable. In our parallel derandomization, one step of our process consists of assigning values to
n

[

such that

in small work and depth. In fact, the result of Luby is why we work with pairwise independent random variables in the first place.

Let us now write down the potential function that our deterministic algorithm keeps monotonically non-increasing. This is similar to the Chernoff bound analysis we have already seen and mimics the standard proofs of Bernstein’s inequality (see
[

).
We define

\displaystyle\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t}=\prod_{1\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\left(1+\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j,t^{\prime}}+\left(\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j,t^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\right)\cdot\left(1+\lambda_{i}^{2}|S_{i}|/k^{2}\right)^{T-t}

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{10^{10}}\min\left(1,\frac{k}{|S_{i}|}\right)

Next, we define

\displaystyle\Psi_{i,t}=

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\left(1-\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\left(\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\in S_{i}^{2}}(p_{j_{1},t}-p_{j_{2},t})^{2}\right)+\lambda^{\prime 2}_{i}\left(\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\in S_{i}^{2}}(p_{j_{1},t}-p_{j_{2},t})^{2}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(1-\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\frac{|S_{i}|^{2}}{100k^{2}}+\lambda^{\prime 2}_{i}\cdot 100\left(\frac{|S_{i}|^{3}}{k^{2}}+\frac{|S_{i}|^{4}}{k^{3}}\right)^{2}\right)^{T-t}

with

\displaystyle\psi_{i,t}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\in S_{i}^{2}}(p_{j_{1},t}-p_{j_{2},t})^{2}

analogously to

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{10^{10}}\min\left(\frac{1}{|S_{i}|},\frac{k}{|S|^{2}}\right).

Finally, we again define the overall potential to be the normalized sum of all partial potentials:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pot}_{t}=\frac{1}{3m}\sum_{i\in[m]}\left(\frac{\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t}}{\Phi^{(1)}_{i,0}}+\frac{\Phi^{(2)}_{i,t}}{\Phi^{(2)}_{i,0}}+\frac{\Psi_{i,t}}{\Psi_{i,0}}\right).

(11)

We can now analyze the deterministic algorithm that applies Luby’s pairwise derandomization in every step
t
to find values
x_{j,t}
for every
j\in[n]
such that
\mathrm{Pot}_{t}(x_{1,t},\dots,x_{n,t})\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Pot}_{t}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})]
. This turns out to be a little bit more technical because

. This would seem to make the pairwise derandomization inapplicable. However, it is possible to upper bound this term with a certain expression quadratic in
x_{1,n},\dots,x_{n,t}

\Psi
in

36

1.

First, we need to prove that
\mathrm{Pot}_{t}
is monotonically non-increasing, i.e., we always have
\mathrm{Pot}_{t}\leq\mathrm{Pot}_{t-1}
. This boils down to computing that the first and the second moments of
\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j,t}
are
0
and
|S_{i}|/k^{2}
, respectively in the case of
\Phi^{(1)}
and
\Phi^{(2)}

\frac{|S_{i}|^{2}}{100k^{2}}
and

, respectively. This computation was already discussed in the analysis of the randomized algorithm.

2.

Next, we prove that whenever a bad event happens, we have
\mathrm{Pot}_{t}>1
which contradicts
\mathrm{Pot}_{0}=1
. Here, we discuss this only for the case of the bad event of having one set

with
\varphi_{i,T}-|S_{i}|/2>|S_{i}|/6
(cf.

). Such an event implies

\displaystyle\Phi_{i,T}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq t^{\prime}\leq T}\left(1+\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j,t^{\prime}}+\left(\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j,t^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{1\leq t^{\prime}\leq T}\textrm{e}^{\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j,t^{\prime}}}

% As
z=\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j,t^{\prime}}\leq 1
and thus

.

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\lambda_{i}\sum_{1\leq t^{\prime}\leq T}\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j,t^{\prime}}\right\}=\exp\left\{\lambda_{i}\left(\varphi_{i,t}-|S_{i}|/2\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left\{\lambda_{i}|S_{i}|/6\right\}=\exp\left\{\Omega(\min(|S_{i}|,k))\right\}

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{i,0}^{(1)}=\left(1+\lambda_{i}^{2}|S_{i}|/k^{2}\right)^{T}\leq\exp\left\{T\cdot\lambda_{i}^{2}|S_{i}|/k^{2}\right\}=\exp\left\{O(\min(|S_{i}|,k^{2}/|S_{i}|)\right\}

2.4
Generalizing to prove the vanilla derandomization

We next discuss some of the ideas that would help to finish a proof of
Theorem

1.1
(beyond the sketch provided above for
Theorem

2.1
).

We review the additional ideas one can use to prove
Theorem

1.1
in three steps. First, we need to look back at the above proof sketch of
Theorem

2.1
and note that, with some work, it can prove a more general statement. Second, we sketch how to handle the problem that our argument keeps some values nonintegral (labeled as
MAYBE
for the purposes of
Theorem

2.1
). Third, we show how one can deal with sampling probabilities
p\ll 1/2
by repeatedly “subsampling” with probability
1/2
.

As the first step, we note that the argument for the above proof of
Theorem

2.1
can be extended. In particular, the argument allows for sublinear deviations between the
YES
es and
NO
s. Also, we do not need to require that the starting probability of every element is
1/2
, but an arbitrary value. With these extensions, one can get the following more general statement.

Lemma 2.2

(Simplified and unweighted version of the Partial Fixing Lemma (
Lemma

4.1
))
.

Let
n,m,\in\mathbb{N}
with
m=\operatorname{poly}(n)
. Also, let
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{m}\subseteq[n]
,
\mathbf{p}\in\left[0,1\right]^{n}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}
such that for every
i\in[m]
,

\Delta_{i}\geq\Omega(\max(\sqrt{|S_{i}|\log(n)},\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(\log n)}|S_{i}|)).

There exists a deterministic parallel algorithm with
\tilde{O}(n+\sum_{i\in[n]}|S_{i}|)
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
depth that outputs a vector
\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n}
such that for every
i\in[m]
,

|\sum_{j\in{S_{i}}}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\Delta_{i}

and

|\{j\in S_{i}\colon q_{j}\notin\{0,1\}\}|\leq 0.9|S_{i}|.

As the second step, we now show how we can repeatedly apply
Lemma

2.2
to get the same statement as in
Lemma

2.2
but where
q_{j}\in\{0,1\}
for every
j\in[n]
instead of the weaker condition
|\{j\in S_{i}\colon q_{j}\notin\{0,1\}\}|\leq 0.9|S_{i}|
.
To do this, we use the fact that we can repeatedly apply
Lemma

2.2
to compute a sequence of vectors
\mathbf{p}^{(0)}:=\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}^{(1)},\mathbf{p}^{(2)},\ldots
where
\mathbf{p}^{(\ell+1)}
is computed by using the algorithm of
Lemma

2.2
with
\mathbf{p}^{(\ell)}
as input, ignoring all 0/1 entries in
\mathbf{p}^{(\ell)}
. Moreover, we set
\Delta_{i}^{(\ell)}=0.99^{\ell}\cdot\Delta_{i}
.
This way, we inductively get

\displaystyle|\{j\in S_{i}\colon p_{j}^{(\ell)}\notin\{0,1\}\}|\leq 0.9^{\ell}|S_{i}|

which implies we are done after
O(\log n)
invocations of the lemma. Here, we are relying on the fact that the rate in which the number of nonintegral elements of
S_{i}
shrinks is much more rapid than the rate in which
\Delta_{i}
shrinks, which implies that for every invocation
\ell
of
Lemma

2.2
we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}^{\ell}\geq\Omega\left(\max(\sqrt{|S_{i}^{\ell}|\log(n)},\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(\log n)}|S_{i}^{\ell}|)\right),

and thus the lemma is always applicable. The total deviation we incur by all the calls to the lemma can be computed as the summation

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}+0.99\Delta_{i}+0.99^{2}\Delta_{i}+\dots=O(\Delta_{i}).

Hence, the final result of our new algorithm indeed satisfies all requirements of
Lemma

2.2
, up to constant factors; with the additional improvement that now all values of
\mathbf{q}
are integral.

We now discuss the third step. We informally sketched that by iteratively applying
Lemma

2.2
, we can obtain the same guarantees as in
Lemma

2.2
and where additionally
\mathbf{q}
is integral.
However, even this stronger version is not sufficient to prove our vanilla derandomization result
Theorem

1.1
- this would require us to replace the condition
\Delta_{i}=\Omega(\max(\sqrt{|S_{i}|\log(n)},\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(\log n)}|S_{i}|)
with the more relaxed condition
\Delta_{i}=\Omega(\max(\sqrt{\mu_{i}\log(n)},\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(\log n)}\mu_{i})
, where
\mu_{i}:=\sum_{j\in S_{i}}p_{j}
. Note that the conditions are equivalent as long as
p_{j}\geq 1/2
for every
j\in[n]
but for small sampling probabilities, the former condition is much stricter. To fix this, we consider repeatedly using
Lemma

2.2
to “subsample” with probability
1/2
. To illustrate the idea, we now give a proof sketch of
Theorem

1.1
using the integral version of
Lemma

2.2
. Recall that the input to
Theorem

1.1
is a uniform sampling probability
p\in[0,1]
and a precision parameter
\varepsilon\in[1/\operatorname{poly}\log n,1]
. Given that each set satisfies
|S_{i}|=\Omega\left(\frac{\log n}{p\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
, the goal is to compute a set
T\subseteq[n]
such that
|S_{i}\cap T|\in(1\pm\varepsilon)p|S_{i}|
for every
i\in[m]
.

For simplicity, we assume that
p=\frac{1}{2^{R}}\geq\frac{1}{n}
for some
R
.
We compute a sequence of sets
T_{0}:=[n]\supseteq T_{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq T_{R}:=T
.
Let
\varepsilon_{\ell}=\frac{\max(0.9^{R-\ell},1/\operatorname{poly}(\log n)}{100}\varepsilon
.
We compute
T_{\ell+1}
from
T_{\ell}
by using the integral version of
Lemma

2.2
as follows:
We choose
T_{\ell}
as our ground set and consider the sets
S_{i,\ell}:=S_{i}\cap T_{\ell}
for every
i\in[m]
. We set
p_{j}=1/2
for every
j\in T_{\ell}
and allow a deviation of
\Delta_{i,\ell}=\varepsilon_{i,\ell}|S_{i,\ell}|
.
Therefore, we can compute a set
T_{\ell+1}
satisfying

|S_{i}\cap T_{\ell+1}|\in(1\pm\varepsilon_{\ell})\frac{1}{2}|S_{i}\cap T_{\ell}|.

Setting
T=T_{R}
, a simple induction then gives

|S_{i}\cap T|\in\prod_{\ell\in[1,T-1]}(1\pm\varepsilon_{\ell})\frac{1}{2^{T}}|S_{i}|\in(1+\varepsilon)p|S_{i}|,

and thus
T
satisfies the condition of
Theorem

1.1
.

Recalling Bernstein’s inequality

Next, we state a version of Bernstein’s inequality. This inequality is a crucial tool to analyze

Lemma 3.2

(Bernstein’s inequality for martingales, a variant of Theorem 8.1 from
[
DP09
]
)
.

Let
Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{T}
be a martingale satisfying the conditions

\max_{1\leq t\leq T}|Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}|\leq c

and

\max_{1\leq t\leq T}\sup\textrm{Var}[Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}|Z_{t-1}]\leq v.

for some
c,v>0
.
For any
\Delta>0
we have

\textrm{P}(Z_{T}\geq Z_{0}+\Delta)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4\max(T\cdot v,\Delta\cdot c)}\right)

and

\textrm{P}(Z_{T}\leq Z_{0}-\Delta)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4\max(T\cdot v,\Delta\cdot c)}\right)

Moreover, the first inequality also holds if
(Z_{t})
is a submartingale and the second inequality holds also if
(Z_{t})
is a supermartingale.

Work-efficient derandomization for nice quadratic functions

Here, we recall Luby’s method
[
Lub88
]
for work-efficient derandomization of nice pairwise analysis. We comment that our terminology is slightly different.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that a function
Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a
nice quadratic term
if it can be written as

\displaystyle Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\left(\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_{i}x_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in B}\beta_{j}x_{j}\right)+\sum_{i^{\prime}\in C}\gamma_{i^{\prime}}x_{i^{\prime}}+\delta

(12)

for some sets
A,B,C\subseteq[n]
and
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j},\gamma_{i^{\prime}},\delta\in\mathbb{R}
for every
i\in A,j\in B,i^{\prime}\in C
. Moreover, the
complexity
of a nice quadratic term
||Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})||
is defined as

\displaystyle||Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})||=|A|+|B|+|C|+1.

(13)

Finally, whenever

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}Q_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

(14)

we write

\displaystyle||f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})||=\sum_{k=1}^{K}||Q_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})||

(15)

Lemma 3.4

(
Luby’s derandomization for nice quadratic functions
)
.

Let
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a function that can be written as

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}Q_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

(16)

where each
Q_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a nice quadratic term from
Definition

3.3
.
Then, there exist a probability space
\Omega
with pairwise independent binary random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
on it, and an algorithm with work

\displaystyle||f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})||\cdot\operatorname{poly}\log(n)

(17)

and depth
\operatorname{poly}(||f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})||,\log n)
that produces values
\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
such that

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{n})\leq\mathbb{E}\left[f(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\right].

(18)

Here, we assume that we get all constants of all nice quadratic terms
Q_{k}
as part of the input.

The result is implicit in the work of
[
Lub88
]
and has been used under different phrasings. See
[
BRS94
, Section 3.2]
or Harris
[
Har19
, Section 2]
. We provide a proof sketch in
Appendix

Overview of the analysis of potentials

For every potential

Analysis of

We will now analyze the potential

. Next, we prove that the value of

Claim 4.3
.

For every nonboring set
i
we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t}\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right)\right]\leq\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t-1}

Proof.

By definition of
\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t}
and
\Phi^{(1)}_{i,t-1}
from

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1+\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}X_{j}+\left(\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}X_{j}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\left(1+\lambda_{i}^{2}\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}^{2}/k^{2}\right).

That is, the first and the second moment of each

We first note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}X_{j}]=\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]=0.

(58)

by the linearity of expectation.
We continue with the second moment. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}X_{j}\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{2}]

On one hand, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{i,0}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq t\leq T}\left(1+\lambda_{i}^{2}\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}^{2}/k^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\sum_{1\leq t\leq T}\lambda_{i}^{2}\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{i,j}^{2}/k^{2}\right)

Applications of the partial fixing lemma

In this section, we present the proofs of our main theorems. The following result can be proven by repeatedly applying
Lemma

4.1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
n,m,k\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary and
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
. Also, let
\mathbf{p}\in[0,1]^{n}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
. For each

\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k)
depth that outputs a set
I^{bad}\subseteq[m]
with
|I^{bad}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad}_{i}
and a vector
\mathbf{q}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

We note that if we replace
\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}}
in the exponent with
\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}}
, then we would obtain the Hoeffding-like derandomization result
Theorem

1.2
. In particular,
Lemma

5.1
matches the guarantees of
Theorem

1.2
if

for every

5.1
to prove both
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.3
. We remark that the proof below is conceptually easy but tedious.

Proof.

First, observe that we can assume without loss of generality that for every
i\in[m]
,
prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}}_{i}<1
and therefore also that
\Delta_{i}\geq\frac{1000\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}}{k}
for every
i\in[m]

By our assumption, we have that

as the all-zero vector fulfills the conditions of

5.1
.
Now, assume that

We compute the output vector
\mathbf{q}
in the following way: First, let

by rounding each entry to the closest multiple of

. Note that

where the last inequality follows from our assumption
\Delta_{i}\geq\frac{1000\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}}{k}
.

By applying
Lemma

4.1
with input
A,\mathbf{\Delta}
and
\mathbf{p^{partial,in}}
, we can compute in
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k)
depth sets
(I^{ignore}_{i})_{i\in[m]}
and
I^{bad,partial}\subseteq[m]
and a vector
\mathbf{p^{partial,out}}\in[0,1]^{n}
such that
\sum_{j\in I^{ignore}_{i}}a_{ij}\leq\frac{\Delta_{i}}{1000}
for every

a_{ij},\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

e^{cher}_{i}:=\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij})},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}},\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}}\right).

There exists a deterministic parallel algorithm with
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k)
depth that outputs a set
I^{bad}\subseteq[m]
with

and a vector
\mathbf{q}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that for every

it holds that

Proof.

For a given
\mathbf{p}
, we define

. We prove

5.2
then follows as a simple corollary of that statement. During the induction, we think of

To state the precise statement, we first introduce two sequences of numbers. Namely, for each
\ell\in[0,k]

\varepsilon_{\ell}=\frac{1}{16}\max\left(0.8^{\ell},\frac{1}{k}\right)
and
\alpha_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-0.8^{\ell}+\frac{\ell}{k}\right)
. We have chosen
\varepsilon_{\ell}
and
\alpha_{\ell}
such that
\varepsilon_{\ell}
is sufficiently large for certain concentrations to hold and such that
\alpha_{\ell}
satisfies

1.

\alpha_{\ell}\in\left[\frac{1}{10},1\right]
for every

\alpha_{\ell}\geq\varepsilon_{\ell}+(1+\varepsilon_{\ell})\alpha_{\ell-1}
for every
\ell\in[1,k]

The first property follows immediately from the definition of
\alpha_{\ell}
. For the second one, using that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\ell}+(1+\varepsilon_{\ell})\alpha_{\ell-1}\leq\alpha_{\ell-1}+2\varepsilon_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-0.8^{\ell-1}+\frac{\ell-1}{k})+\frac{1}{8}\max(0.8^{\ell},\frac{1}{k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{4}0.8^{\ell}-\frac{5}{4}0.8^{\ell}+\frac{\ell}{k}\right)=\alpha_{\ell}.

The statement we prove by induction on
\ell
is that for
c
being sufficiently large, there exists a parallel algorithm with
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))(\ell+1)
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k))(\ell+1)
depth that outputs a set
I^{bad}\subseteq[m]
with
|I^{bad}|\leq\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}
and a vector
\mathbf{q}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that for every
i\in[m]\setminus I^{bad}
it holds that

|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\alpha_{\ell}\Delta_{i}.

Note that because
\alpha_{\ell}\leq 1
and
\ell\leq k
,
Lemma

5.2
follows as a simple corollary once we have proven the statement.

The base case
\ell\leq 1
is a simple corollary of
Lemma

5.1
for
c

\alpha_{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{10}
.

Now, assume that
\ell>1
. Note that we can assume without loss of generality that

for every
i\in[m]
. Let
J^{small}:=\{j\in[n]\colon p_{j}<0.5^{\ell-1}\}
and let
A^{small}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times J^{small}},\mathbf{p^{small,in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J^{small}}
,
\mathbf{\Delta^{small}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and

1.

a^{small}_{ij}=2p_{j}a_{ij}
for every

,

2.

p^{small,in}_{j}=1/2
for every

\Delta^{small}_{i}=\varepsilon_{\ell}\Delta_{i}
for every

By running the algorithm of

5.1

\mathbf{p^{small,out}}\in\{0,1\}^{J^{small}}

In particular, for

|\sum_{j\in J^{small}}p_{j}a_{ij}-\sum_{j\in J^{small}\cap J^{rec}}2p_{j}a_{ij}|\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}\Delta_{i}.

Let
A^{rec}\in\mathbb{R}^{I^{good,small}\times J^{rec}},\mathbf{p^{rec,in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J^{rec}}
and
\mathbf{\Delta^{rec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I^{good,small}}

1.

a^{rec}_{ij}=a_{ij}

,
j\in J^{rec}
,

2.

p^{rec,in}_{j}=\begin{cases}p_{j}&,\text{if $j\in[n]\setminus J^{small}$}\\
2p_{j}&,\text{if $j\in J^{small}\cap J^{rec}$}\end{cases}
and

3.

\Delta^{rec}_{i}=\left(1+\varepsilon_{\ell}\right)\Delta_{i}
for every
i\in I^{good,small}
.

Using the induction hypothesis, we can compute in
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))\ell
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k))\ell

I^{bad,rec}\subseteq I^{good,small}

|I^{bad,rec}|\leq\alpha_{\ell-1}\sum_{i\in I^{good,small}}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A^{rec},\mathbf{\Delta^{rec}},\mathbf{p^{rec,in}},k)

.

The algorithm runs in
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))(\ell+1)
work and
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k))(\ell+1)

We start by showing that the deviation
|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|

\alpha_{\ell}\Delta_{i}
for every
i\in[m]\setminus I^{bad}
. The first error comes from the fact that every

by calling

5.2
. We previously upper bounded the error from this step by

|\sum_{j\in J^{small}}p_{j}a_{ij}-\sum_{j\in J^{small}\cap J^{rec}}p^{rec,in}_{j}a_{ij}|=|\sum_{j\in J^{small}}p_{j}a_{ij}-\sum_{j\in J^{small}\cap J^{rec}}2p_{j}a_{ij}|\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}\Delta_{i}.

We also previously upper-bounded the error introduced by the recursive call by

|\sum_{j\in J^{rec}}a_{ij}(p^{rec,in}_{j}-q_{j})|=|\sum_{j\in J^{rec}}a^{rec}_{ij}(p^{rec,in}_{j}-p^{rec,out}_{j})|\leq(1+\varepsilon_{\ell})\alpha_{\ell-1}\Delta_{i}.

Hence, using that
q_{j}=0
for every
j\in[n]\setminus J^{rec}
,
p^{rec,in}_{j}=p_{j}
for every
j\in[n]\setminus J^{small}
and
\alpha_{\ell}\geq\varepsilon_{\ell}+(1+\varepsilon_{\ell})\alpha_{\ell-1}
, we can upper bound the overall error by

\displaystyle|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})=|\sum_{j\in J^{small}}p_{j}a_{ij}-\sum_{j\in J^{small}\cap J^{rec}}p^{rec,in}_{j}a_{ij}|+|\sum_{j\in J^{rec}}a_{ij}(p^{rec,in}_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\alpha_{\ell}\Delta_{i}.

Thus, it remains to upper bound the size of
I^{bad,small}
.
Below, we show that for every
i\in[m]
, we have
prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}}_{i}(A^{small},\mathbf{\Delta^{small}},k^{small})\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)
and for every
i\in I^{good,small}
, we have

prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A^{rec},\mathbf{\Delta^{rec}},\mathbf{p^{rec,in}},k)\leq prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)
. Thus, by using the fact that
\alpha_{\ell}\geq\varepsilon_{\ell}+\alpha_{\ell-1}
, we can conclude that

\displaystyle|I^{bad,small}|

\displaystyle\leq|I^{bad,small}|+|I^{bad,rec}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}}_{i}(A^{small},\mathbf{\Delta^{small}},k^{small})+\alpha_{\ell-1}\sum_{i\in I^{good,small}}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A^{rec},\mathbf{\Delta^{rec}},\mathbf{p^{rec,in}},k)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)+\alpha_{\ell-1}\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k).

We start by showing that
prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A^{rec},\mathbf{\Delta^{rec}},\mathbf{p^{rec,in}},k)\leq prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)
for every
i\in I^{good,small}
. For every
i\in I^{good,small}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J^{rec}}p^{rec,in}_{j}a^{rec}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus J^{small}}p_{j}a_{ij}+\sum_{j\in J^{rec}\cap J^{small}}2p_{j}a_{ij}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}+|\sum_{j\in J^{small}}p_{j}a_{ij}-\sum_{j\in J^{small}\cap J^{rec}}2p_{j}a_{ij}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}+\varepsilon_{\ell}\Delta_{i}\leq(1+\varepsilon_{\ell})\max\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij},\Delta_{i}\right).

Therefore,

\frac{\Delta^{rec}_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in J^{rec}}p^{rec,in}_{j}a^{rec}_{ij}}\geq\frac{(1+\varepsilon_{\ell})\Delta_{i}k}{(1+\varepsilon_{\ell})\max\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij},\Delta_{i}\right)}=\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}},k\right)

and

\frac{(\Delta^{rec}_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p^{rec,in}_{j}a^{rec}_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a^{rec}_{ij})}\geq\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij})},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}}\right).

The former inequality directly implies
e^{hoef}_{i}(A^{rec},\mathbf{\Delta^{rec}},\mathbf{p^{rec,in}},k)\geq\min(e^{hoef}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),k)
and both inequalities together directly imply
e^{cher}_{i}(A^{rec},\mathbf{\Delta^{rec}},\mathbf{p^{rec,in}},k)\geq\min(e^{cher}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),k)
.
Therefore,

prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A^{rec},\mathbf{\Delta^{rec}},\mathbf{p^{rec,in}},k)\leq prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k).

Thus, it remains to show that
prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}}_{i}(A^{small},\mathbf{\Delta^{small}},k^{small})\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)
for every
i\in[m]
.
For every
i\in[m]
, using the fact that
\varepsilon_{\ell}\geq 0.8^{\ell}
and
p_{j}\leq 0.5^{\ell-1}
for every
j\in J^{small}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{(\Delta^{small}_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in J^{small}}(a^{small}_{ij})^{2}}=\frac{(\varepsilon_{\ell}\Delta_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in J^{small}}(2p_{j}a_{ij})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{0.8^{2\ell}}{16\cdot 0.5^{\ell}}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1.2^{\ell}}{16}\max\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}^{2}},\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij})}\right).

Using the fact that
\varepsilon_{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{k}
and
k^{small}:=k^{3}
, we get

\frac{\Delta^{small}_{i}k^{small}}{\sum_{j\in J^{small}}a^{small}_{ij}}\geq\frac{\frac{1}{k}\Delta_{i}k^{3}}{\sum_{j\in J^{small}}2p_{j}a_{ij}}\geq\frac{k}{2}\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}}.

Thus, we get

\min\left(\frac{(\Delta^{small}_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in J^{small}}(a^{small}_{ij})^{2}},\frac{\Delta^{small}_{i}k^{small}}{\sum_{j\in J^{small}}a^{small}_{ij}}\right)\geq\min\left(\frac{1.2^{\ell}}{16},\frac{k}{2}\right)\max(e^{hoef}_{i},e^{cher}_{i}).

Thus, for
c
being sufficiently large, and using that
\varepsilon_{\ell}\gg(1/c)^{\min(1.2^{\ell},k)}
for
c
being sufficiently large and
prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\leq 1
, we get

\displaystyle prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}}_{i}(A^{small},\mathbf{\Delta^{small}},k^{small})

\displaystyle:=c^{L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}}\exp\left(-(1/c^{L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}})\min\left(\frac{(\Delta^{small}_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in J^{small}}(a^{small}_{ij})^{2}},\frac{\Delta^{small}_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in J^{small}}a^{small}_{ij}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq c^{L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}}\exp\bigg(-(1/c^{L\ref{lem:weighted_good_for_large_p}})\min\left(\frac{1.2^{\ell}}{16},\frac{k}{2}\right)\max(e^{hoef}_{i},e^{cher}_{i})\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq c\exp\left(-(1/c)\max(e^{hoef}_{i},e^{cher}_{i})\right)^{2\min\left(1.2^{\ell},k\right)}

\displaystyle=prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\cdot(prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)/c)^{2\min(1.2^{\ell},k)-1}

\displaystyle\leq prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\cdot(1/c)^{\min(1.2^{\ell},k)}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k).

This finishes the induction and therefore completes the proof of
Lemma

5.2
.

5.2
Bernstein-like concentration

Finally, we prove
Theorem

1.4
by using the algorithm of
Theorem

1.3
once. In particular, for each
i\in[m]
and
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define bucket
B_{i,\ell}=\{j\in[n]\colon a_{ij}\in(2^{\ell-1},2^{\ell}]\}
. We then assign each bucket
B_{i,\ell}
an allowed deviation
\Delta_{i,\ell}
such that the following is satisfied: on the one hand
\sum_{\ell}\Delta_{i,\ell}\leq\Delta_{i}
for every
i
and on the other hand
\sum_{\ell}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}\leq prob^{bad}_{i}
, where informally speaking
prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}
is the failure probability of
Theorem

1.3
to ensure that
|\sum_{j\in B_{i,\ell}}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\Delta_{i,\ell}
.
See
1.4

Proof.

Fix some
i\in[m]
. We define

\mu_{i}=\sum_{j\in[m]}p_{j}a_{ij}\text{ and }V_{i}:=\sum_{j\in[m]}p_{j}a_{ij}^{2}.

Also, for each
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
B_{i,\ell}:=\{j\in[n]\colon a_{ij}\in(2^{\ell-1},2^{\ell}]\}
. We also define

\mu_{i,\ell}=\sum_{j\in B_{i,\ell}}p_{j}a_{ij}\text{ and }V_{i,\ell}:=\sum_{j\in B_{i,\ell}}p_{j}a_{ij}^{2}.

We also define
\ell^{max}_{i}
as the largest
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
with
B_{i,\ell}\neq\emptyset
and
\gamma_{i}=\frac{V_{i}}{\Delta_{i}2^{\ell^{max}_{i}}}
.

Moreover, we define

\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}=\sqrt{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})\frac{V_{i,\ell}}{V_{i}}},\varepsilon^{(2)}_{i,\ell}=0.9^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell},\varepsilon^{(3)}_{i,\ell}=\frac{\mu_{i,\ell}}{\mu_{i}},\varepsilon_{i,\ell}=\max(\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell},\varepsilon^{(2)}_{i,\ell},\varepsilon^{(3)}_{i,\ell})\text{ and }\Delta_{i,\ell}:=\frac{\varepsilon_{i,\ell}}{100}\Delta_{i}

and we also define

prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}:=c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\exp\left(-(1/c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}})\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}^{2}}{V_{i,\ell}},\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}}{2^{\ell}},\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}c\log(nm)k}{\mu_{i,\ell}}\right)\right).

Now, assume we split the
i
-th constraint into multiple constraints, one for each nonempty bucket
B_{i,\ell}
. The corresponding row is defined by
a_{(i,\ell),j}=\begin{cases}a_{ij}&,\text{if $j\in B_{i,\ell}$}\\
0&,\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
for every
j\in[n]
and the deviation we allows is
\Delta_{i,\ell}
. Then, by using
Theorem

1.3
with
k^{\prime}=c\log(nm)k
, we get a set
I^{bad,bucket}\subseteq[m]\times\mathbb{Z}
with
|I^{bad,bucket}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}\sum_{\ell\colon B_{i,\ell}\neq\emptyset}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}
and a vector
\mathbf{q}
such that for every
(i,\ell)\notin I^{bad,bucket}
it holds that

|\sum_{j\in B_{i,\ell}}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\Delta_{i,\ell}.

We not output
I^{bad}=\{i\in[m]\colon\text{there exists an $\ell$ with $(i,\ell)\in I^{bad,bucket}$}\}
and
\mathbf{q}
. The algorithm runs in the desired work and depth. It thus remains to verify that the output is correct. To that end, we define

B^{big}_{i}=\{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}\colon B_{i,\ell}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}\geq 0.8^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}\}\text{ and }B^{small}_{i}:=\{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}\colon B_{i,\ell}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}<0.8^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}\}.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}\varepsilon_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}\colon\varepsilon_{i,\ell}=\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}}\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}+\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}\varepsilon^{(2)}_{i,\ell}+\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}\varepsilon^{(3)}_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}+\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}0.9^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}+\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}\frac{\mu_{i,\ell}}{\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}+10+1.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})}\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}\sqrt{\frac{V_{i,\ell}}{V_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})}\sqrt{|B^{big}_{i}|}\sqrt{\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}\frac{V_{i,\ell}}{V_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})|B^{big}_{i}|}.

Thus, we have to find a good upper bound on
|B^{big}_{i}|
.

Consider an arbitrary
\ell\in B^{big}_{i}
. From the definition of
\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}
and
B^{big}_{i}
, it follows that

\sqrt{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})\frac{V_{i,\ell}}{V_{i}}}\geq 0.8^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}

and therefore

\frac{V_{i}}{V_{i,\ell}}\leq\frac{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})}{0.64^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}}\leq 1.6^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}.

It directly follows from the way
\gamma_{i}
is defined that
\ell^{max}_{i}=\log\left(\frac{V_{i}}{\gamma_{i}\Delta_{i}}\right)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\ell^{max}_{i}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{V_{i}}{\gamma_{i}\Delta_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log(1/\gamma_{i})+\log\left(\frac{V_{i}}{\mu_{i,\ell}}\right)+\log\left(\frac{\mu_{i}}{\Delta_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log(1/\gamma_{i})+\log\left(\frac{V_{i}2^{\ell}}{V_{i,\ell}}\right)+\frac{1}{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\log(1/\gamma_{i})+\log\left(\frac{V_{i}}{V_{i,\ell}}\right)+\ell+\frac{1}{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\log(1/\gamma_{i})+\log\left(1.6^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}\right)+\ell+\frac{1}{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\log(1/\gamma_{i})+0.9(\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell)+\ell+\frac{1}{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}.

Thus, by rearranging terms, we get

\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell\leq 10\left(\log(1/\gamma_{i})+\frac{1}{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}\right).

Therefore,
|B^{big}_{i}|\leq 10\left(\log(1/\gamma_{i})+\frac{1}{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}\right)+1
and

\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}\leq\sqrt{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})|B^{big}_{i}|}\leq\sqrt{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})\left(10\left(\log(1/\gamma_{i})+\frac{1}{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}\right)+1\right)}\leq 50.

Thus, for
c
being sufficiently large, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}\Delta_{i,\ell}=\frac{\Delta_{i}}{100}\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}\varepsilon_{i,\ell}\leq\frac{\Delta_{i}}{100}\left(11+\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}\right)\leq\Delta_{i}.

Thus, for every
i\in[m]\setminus I^{bad}
, we get

|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}|\sum_{j\in B_{i,\ell}}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{max}_{i}}\Delta_{i,\ell}\leq\Delta_{i}.

Thus, it remains to show that
|I^{bad}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad}_{i}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}^{2}}{V_{i,\ell}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{i,\ell}}{100\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}}\right)^{2}\frac{(\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell})^{2}\cdot\Delta_{i}^{2}}{V_{i,\ell}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{i,\ell}}{100\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}}\right)^{2}\frac{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})\cdot\frac{V_{i,\ell}}{V_{i}}\Delta_{i}^{2}}{V_{i,\ell}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{i,\ell}}{100\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}}\right)^{2}\cdot\max(1,1/\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})\cdot\frac{c\cdot\alpha_{i}\min(1,\gamma_{i})\Delta_{i}^{2}}{V_{i}}

\displaystyle=c\cdot\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{i,\ell}}{100\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}}\right)^{2}\cdot\max(1,1/\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})\cdot\frac{\min(1,\gamma_{i})\Delta_{i}^{2}}{V_{i}}

\displaystyle=c\cdot\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{i,\ell}}{100\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}}\right)^{2}\cdot\max(1,1/\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})\cdot\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{V_{i}},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2^{\ell^{max}_{i}}}\right).

Also,

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}}{2^{\ell}}\geq\frac{1}{100}\frac{\varepsilon^{(2)}_{i,\ell}\Delta_{i}}{2^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{100}\frac{0.9^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}\Delta_{i}}{2^{\ell}}=\frac{(2\cdot 0.9)^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}}{100}\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2^{\ell^{max}_{i}}}\geq\frac{1.5^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}}{100}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2^{\ell^{max}_{i}}}

and

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}k\cdot c\log(nm)}{\mu_{i,\ell}}\geq\frac{c\log(nm)}{100}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{(3)}_{i,\ell}\Delta_{i}k}{\mu_{i,\ell}}\geq\log(nm)\cdot\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\mu_{i}}.

We now define

\beta_{i,\ell}=\min\left(c\cdot\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{i,\ell}}{100\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}}\right)^{2}\max(1,1/\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}),\frac{1.5^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}}{100},\log(nm)\right).

Using above, and that we can assume
prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\leq 1
without loss of generality, we get

\displaystyle prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle:=c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\exp\left(-(1/c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}})\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}^{2}}{V_{i,\ell}},\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}}{2^{\ell}},\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell}c\log(nm)k}{\mu_{i,\ell}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\exp\left(-(\beta_{i,\ell}/c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}})\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{V_{i}},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2^{\ell^{max}_{i}}},\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\mu_{i}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\right)\exp\left(-\alpha_{i}\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{V_{i}},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2^{\ell^{max}_{i}-1}},\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\mu_{i}}\right)\right)^{\frac{\beta_{i,\ell}}{2c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}\cdot prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\cdot\left(\alpha_{i}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\right)^{\frac{\beta_{i,\ell}}{2c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}}-1}

\displaystyle\leq c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}\cdot prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)^{\frac{\beta_{i,\ell}}{4c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}}}.

Consider an arbitrary
\ell\in B^{small}_{i}
. It holds that
\frac{\varepsilon_{i,\ell}}{\varepsilon^{(1)}_{i,\ell}}\geq\frac{0.9^{\ell^{max}_{i}-l}}{0.8^{\ell^{max}_{i}-l}}\geq 1.1^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}
and therefore,

\beta_{i,\ell}\geq\min\left(\frac{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}{100^{2}}1.2^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell},\frac{1.5^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}}{100},\log(nm)\right)\geq\min\left(\frac{c\cdot\alpha_{i}}{10000}1.2^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell},\log(nm)\right)

and thus for sufficiently large
c
,

c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)^{\frac{\beta_{i,\ell}}{4c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}}}\leq\frac{1}{100}\max\left(0.5^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell},\frac{1}{n}\right).

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in B^{small}_{i}}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}\leq\sum_{\ell B^{small}_{i}}\frac{1}{100}\max\left(0.5^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell},\frac{1}{n}\right)prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\leq\frac{1}{2}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k).

It always holds that
\beta_{i,\ell}\geq\min\left(\frac{c}{100^{2}}\dot{\alpha}_{i}\max(1,1/\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}),\frac{1.5^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell}}{100},\log(nm)\right)
.
Thus, it holds that

c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)^{\frac{\beta_{i,\ell}}{4c^{T\ref{thm:chernoff}}\alpha_{i}}}\leq\frac{\alpha_{i}}{c}\max\left(\min(1,\gamma_{i}),0.5^{\ell^{max}_{i}-\ell},\frac{1}{n}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{3}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)+|B^{big}_{i}|\frac{\alpha_{i}\min(1,\gamma_{i})}{c}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\Delta,\mathbf{p},k).

Therefore,

\displaystyle|I^{bad}|\leq|I^{bad,bucket}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}\left(\sum_{\ell\colon B_{i,\ell}\neq\emptyset}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}\left(\sum_{\ell\in B^{small}_{i}}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}+\sum_{\ell\in B^{big}_{i}}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i,\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k).

∎

Appendix A
A proof sketch of
Lemma

3.4

Proof Sketch of
Lemma

3.4
.

We provide only an informal sketch. The result is implicit in the work of
[
Lub88
]
and has been used under different phrasings. See
[
BRS94
, Section 3.2]
or Harris
[
Har19
, Section 2]
.

Let each random variable
x_{i}
, for
i\in[n]
, be defined by
x_{i}=-1+2(\vec{i}\bullet\vec{z})
. Here,
\vec{z}
is a random vector in
\{0,1\}^{\log n+1}
, and
\vec{i}
is the
L=(\log n+1)
dimensional vector indicating the binary representation of number
i
where we append a
1
in the least significant place to ensure that the vector is not all zeros. The sign
\bullet
is used for the dot product of the two vectors mod
2
. It is easy to see that
\textrm{P}[x_{i}=+1]=\textrm{P}[x_{i}=-1]=1/2
and that these variables are pairwise independent, i.e., any two
x_{i},x_{j}
—for
i,j\in[n]
such that
i\neq j
—are independent of each other. The task is to find a fixing of
\vec{z}\in\{0,1\}^{\log n+1}
such that the function
f()
is upper bounded by its expectation.

For that, we fix the bits of
\vec{z}
one by one. The core challenge is how to do one step: in such a step, some number
r\in[0,L-1]
of the bits of
\vec{z}
have already been fixed, and the task is to compute the conditional expectation of
f()
for the two possibilities of setting the next bit of
\vec{z}
equal to
0
or
1
. Thus, each of these two problems is to compute the conditional expectations for
f()
over the random space of
\vec{z}
, in the conditional space where the first
r+1
bits of
\vec{z}
are set in one particular way, and the rest remain random. Doing this computation for the constant term and the linear terms in
f()
is easy, in
||Q_{k}||\cdot\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
work and
\operatorname{poly}\log(n)
depth. It is the quadratic terms that require some ingenuity. Notice that, once some
r+1
bits of
\vec{z}
have been fixed and the rest remain random, the random variables
x_{i},x_{j}
are no longer necessarily pairwise independent. For instance, two variables
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
such that the binary representations of
\vec{i}
and
\vec{j}
have the same last
L-(r+1)
bits are deterministic functions of each other. Luby’s idea is that we can group
i\in[1,n]
based on the binary representation of
\vec{i}
in the remaining
L-(r+1)
bits (e.g., with sorting based on only these bits). Variables that are in different groups are still pairwise independent and thus their products cancel out from the computation. Variables inside one group can be broken into two subgroups, depending on the mod-
2
dot product of their binary representation in the first
(r+1)
bits with the chosen
r+1
bits of vector
\vec{z}
. Then the computation boils down to calculating the summation inside each subgroup of coefficients
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
respectively for
A
and
B
. This can be performed using
||Q_{k}||\cdot\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
work and
\operatorname{poly}\log(n)
depth. Please see
[
Lub93
, Section 2]
,
[
BRS94
, Section 3.2]
, or
[
Har19
, Section 2]
for details.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.3

Proof.

We now use
Lemma

5.2
to prove
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
. Note that it suffices to show that one can remove the assumption in
Lemma

5.2
that
p_{j}\geq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
for every
j\in[n]
and to remove the
k
in the exponent of the failure probability.
Let
A,\mathbf{p},\mathbf{\Delta},k
be some general input to
Theorem

1.2
or
Theorem

1.3
. We can assume without loss of generality that

\min(prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:hoeffding}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k))\leq 1.

Let
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
be defined as

1.

p^{\prime}_{j}=\max\left(p_{j},\frac{1}{n}\right)
for every
j\in[n]
,

2.

\Delta^{\prime}_{i}=\Delta_{i}/2
for every
i\in[m]
and

3.

k^{\prime}=\lceil c^{2}\log(2nm)\rceil k
.

Note that
p^{\prime}_{j}\geq\frac{1}{n}\geq\frac{1}{2^{k^{\prime}}}
for every
j\in[n]
. Thus, we can use
Lemma

5.2
to compute using computational work
\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k^{small}))=\tilde{O}(\max(nnz(A),n,m)\operatorname{poly}(k))
and depth bounded by
\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k^{small})=\operatorname{poly}(\log(nm),k)
a set
I^{bad}\subseteq[m]
with
|I^{bad}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},k^{\prime})
and a vector
\mathbf{q}
such that for every
i\in[m]\setminus I^{bad}
, it holds that

|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p^{\prime}_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\Delta^{\prime}_{i}.

Note that

|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-p^{\prime}_{j})|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}\leq\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}.

Moreover, for
c
being sufficiently large, it holds that
\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}\leq\Delta_{i}/2
because

1\geq\min(prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:hoeffding}}_{i},prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i})\geq c\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)\max\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij})^{2}},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}}\right)\right).

Therefore,

|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-q_{j})|\leq|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p_{j}-p^{\prime}_{j})|+|\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}(p^{\prime}_{j}-q_{j})|\leq\Delta_{i}/2+\Delta^{\prime}_{i}=\Delta_{i}.

Thus, it remains to verify that
|I^{bad}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:hoeffding}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)
in order to prove
Theorem

1.2
and
|I^{bad}|\leq\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)
in order to prove
Theorem

1.3
. To that end, we first show that for every
i\in[m]
and
c
being sufficiently large, it holds that

prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},k^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2}\min(prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:hoeffding}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k))+\frac{1}{2m}.

We have argued above that for
c
being sufficiently large, it holds that

\sum_{j\in[n]}p^{\prime}_{j}a_{ij}\leq\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}+\Delta_{i}\leq 2\max\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij},\Delta_{i}\right).

Therefore,

\frac{\Delta^{\prime}_{i}k^{\prime}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p^{\prime}_{j}a_{ij}}\geq\frac{\Delta_{i}c\log(2m)k}{\max(\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij},\Delta_{i})}\geq\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{i\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}},c\log(2m)\right).

Together with

\frac{(\Delta^{\prime}_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}^{2}},

this implies that

e^{hoef}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},k^{\prime})\geq\min\left(\frac{1}{4}e^{hoef}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),c\log(2m)\right).

Using again that
\sum_{j\in[n]}p^{\prime}_{j}a_{ij}\leq 2\max(\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij},\Delta_{i})
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{(\Delta^{\prime}_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p^{\prime}_{j}a_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\max\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}),\Delta_{i}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\min\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij})},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}e^{cher}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k).

Therefore,

\displaystyle e^{cher}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},k^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\min\left(\frac{(\Delta^{\prime}_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p^{\prime}_{j}a_{ij}(\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij})},\frac{\Delta^{\prime}_{i}}{\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}},\frac{\Delta^{\prime}_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p^{\prime}_{j}a_{ij}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\min\left(\frac{1}{8}e^{cher}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\max_{j\in[n]}a_{ij}},\frac{\Delta_{i}k}{\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}a_{ij}},c\log(2m)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\min\left(\frac{1}{8}e^{cher}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),c\log(2m)\right).

Thus, for sufficiently large
c
, we indeed get

\displaystyle prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=c^{L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{c^{L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}}\right)\min(\max\left(e^{hoef}_{i}\left(A,\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},k^{\prime}\right),e^{cher}_{i}\left(A,\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},k^{\prime}\right)\right),k^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\leq c^{L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{c^{L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}}\right)\min\left(\max\left(\frac{1}{4}e^{hoef}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),\frac{1}{8}e^{cher}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\right),c\log(2m)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0.5c\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)\max\left(e^{hoef}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),e^{cher}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\right)\right)+c^{L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{c^{L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}}\log(2m)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0.5\min(prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:hoeffding}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k))+\frac{1}{2m}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|I^{bad}|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\lfloor\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,L\ref{lem:hoeffding_chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta^{\prime}},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},k^{\prime})\bigg\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\lfloor\sum_{i\in[m]}\left(0.5\min(prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:hoeffding}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k))+\frac{1}{2m}\right)\bigg\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\lfloor 0.5+0.5\sum_{i\in[m]}0.5\min(prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:hoeffding}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k))\bigg\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\min\left(\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:hoeffding}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k),\sum_{i\in[m]}prob^{bad,T\ref{thm:chernoff}}_{i}(A,\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{p},k)\right).

Thus, we have proven both
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.3
.
∎
