Continuous Monitoring via Repeated Significance
Eric Bax, Arundhyoti Sarkar, Alex Shtoff

Abstract
Requiring statistical significance at multiple interim analyses to declare a statistically significant result for an AB test allows less stringent requirements for significance at each interim analysis. Repeated repeated significance competes well with methods built on assumptions about the test – assumptions that may be impossible to evaluate a priori and may require extra data to evaluate empirically. Instead, requiring repeated significance allows the data itself to prove directly that the required results are not due to chance alone. We explain how to apply tests with repeated significance to continuously monitor unbounded tests – tests that do not have an a priori bound on running time or number of observations. We show that it is impossible to maintain a constant requirement for significance for unbounded tests, but that we can come arbitrarily close to that goal.

Most group sequential methods use some form of

Dividing the budget more finely (while controlling type 1 error) makes conditions for ending the test more stringent, increasing test time and expense and increasing the likelihood of type 2 error.

To counter those effects, always-valid bounds and continuous monitoring methods often rely on correlation between

For example, a running average over observations combined with a limit on the value of each observation implies that the running average will have limited change as the number of observations grows large.

Another method to avoid extremely stringent conditions for large numbers of interim analyses is to require repeated statistically significant results
[
16

18

Compared to other methods, requiring repetition removes the need to know a priori, prove, or empirically evaluate whether side conditions on the data hold, because it does not rely on correlations among
p

[
16

, and as we show in this paper for unbounded tests.

II
Preliminaries

For a statistical test, a null hypothesis is an assertion about the (unknown) out-of-sample statistics or underlying distribution that generates the observations in the test. For a pre-specified metric measured on the in-sample observations, a
p
-value is the probability that the metric takes a value at least as extreme as the measured value under the null hypothesis.

A type 1 error occurs when a statistical test rejects the null hypothesis but the null hypothesis holds – a false positive. In test planning, we specify an acceptable upper bound for the probability of type 1 error. Call that bound
\alpha
. If the test rejects the null hypothesis, then we say it is rejected at confidence level
1-\alpha
.

A simple test plan specifies a metric and
\alpha
. After all in-sample observations are collected, the test measures the metric and produces a
p
-value based on the measured value. If
p\leq\alpha
, then the null hypothesis is rejected.

For example, in an AB test, the null hypothesis may be that both treatments A and B (one of which may be the control) have the same average for some metric. If the
p
-value for the test is below a specified
\alpha
, then we reject the null hypothesis, asserting that the difference in treatments has a statistically significant impact on the metric, with confidence
1-\alpha
.

For many AB tests, it is useful to allow early stopping – ending the test with a statistically significant result before collecting all planned observations. In tests of user interfaces or advertisements on apps and web pages, this can reduce time to market and save money. In medical tests, it can save lives by delivering useful treatments to the control group as well as the treatment group as soon as there is a positive result.

Early stopping can create the risk of data dredging or
p
-hacking
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
in time, by stopping when a metric is positive but continuing when it is negative. That can introduce large gaps between in-sample and out-of-sample results, due to a selection effect
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
7
]
.

In general, stopping when the
p
-value for the data collected from the start of the test to an interim analysis is less than
\alpha
fails to bound the type 1 error probability by
\alpha
. If the probability of type 1 error at each of
d
interim analyses is at most
\alpha
, then the probability of type 1 error at any interim analysis may be as high as
d\alpha
. (In the worst case, each interim type 1 error probability is
\alpha
, and those events are disjoint.)

A technique to avoid this problem is called
\alpha
spending – partitioning
\alpha
into
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
that sum to
\alpha
, and allowing early stopping when the
p
-value for interim analysis
k
(call it
p_{k}
) has
p_{k}\leq\alpha_{k}
. In this case, the probability of type 1 error at each interim analysis
k
is at most
\alpha_{k}
, so the overall probability of a type 1 error is at most
\alpha
, by the sum bound on the union of probabilities, also known as Boole’s inequality
[
25
,
26
]
. (Dividing evenly:
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{d}
is known as a Bonferroni correction
[
27
]
.) The method of
\alpha
spending has a long history in group sequential testing
[
1
,
28
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
, especially for medical trials.

As
\alpha
is partitioned more finely over more interim analyses, the
p
-value requirements for statistical significance become more stringent. Requiring repeated significance in order to stop the test is a way to counteract this effect, because it allows less stringent requirements for statistical significance while maintaining the
\alpha
bound on probability of type 1 error. If we require
p_{k}\leq\delta_{k}
for at least
r
interim analyses before ruling out the null hypothesis and ending the test, then the probability of type 1 error is at most
\frac{\delta_{1}+\ldots+\delta_{d}}{r}
, because the probability of type 1 error is then maximized by having
p_{k}\leq\delta_{k}
co-occur for exactly
r
different
k
values in
1,\ldots,d
when it occurs for any
k
.

Requiring
r
failures for a type 1 error allows us to divide the sum bound by
r
(see Figure
1
), or, equivalently to multiply each budgeted
\alpha_{k}
by
r
to get
p
-value requirements for interim analyses, since
\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{d}=\alpha
implies that if we set
p
-value requirements
\delta_{k}=\alpha_{k}r
, then
\frac{\delta_{1}+\ldots+\delta_{d}}{r}=\alpha
.

r=3

\delta

2\delta

An earlier paper
[
16
]
applies this insight to develop repetition-based test plans for tests with a priori limits on the number
d
of interim analyses. For that case, it is possible, for example, to spend
\alpha
evenly over interim analyses:
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{d}
for all
k
in
1,\ldots,d
. For unbounded
d
, that is impossible. This paper focuses on methods to spend
\alpha
and select the number of required repeats for unbounded tests.

III
Unbounded Tests

Following the notation from
[
16
]
, we will refer to interim analyses as decision points. For each decision point
t
, let
\delta_{t}
be the upper bound for the
p
-value at decision point
t
to be considered significant, and let
r_{t}
be the number of significant
p
-values over decision points
1
to
t
required to end the test and declare a significant result.

As an example, suppose that for each decision point
t
, if at least half of the decision points
k
from
1
to
t
have
p_{k}\leq\delta_{k}
, where
p_{k}
is the
p
-value at decision point
k
, then we end the test and declare a significant result – rejecting the null hypothesis. Then
r_{t}=\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil
for all

.

Theorem 1
.

Algorithm
1

\alpha

\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots

Proof.

Each decision point

\delta_{k}

\delta_{k}

from

1.

Use false significances as efficiently as possible by having as few of them occur simultaneously as needed to create type 1 error events, and

2.

Have those events be disjoint, to maximize the probability of their union.

Algorithm
1
finds

false significances for the earliest

, so at least as many false significances would be required to form a type 1 error event later. The algorithm sums the probability of those type 1 error events into

As an example, again consider requiring at least half of the decision points up to each decision point

For bounded tests – ones that have an priori limit

\alpha

\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{d}
, where

is the limit on the number of decision points. Similarly, it is possible to budget
\alpha

\delta_{t}=\alpha_{t}r_{t}

t\in\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}

For unbounded tests, geometric spending allocates the same fraction of the remaining
\alpha

\alpha_{2}=w(1-w)\alpha

\alpha_{t}

As an example applying Corollary
2

t

, then significance for each decision point
t
requires

p_{t}\leq w(1-w)^{t-1}\alpha\lceil ut\rceil

(8)

for a value
0<w<1

V
Nearly Impossible is Possible

Figures
2
to
6
all have
Z
score curves that increase after reaching a minimum, prompting the question: “Is there a way to spend
\alpha
that gives a flat curve for an unbounded test, keeping the required
p
-value from shrinking to zero, or, equivalently, the required
Z
score from becoming unbounded as
t\rightarrow\infty
?”

To answer that question, first consider that, in general, for an unbounded test we require

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}\leq\alpha

(10)

to avoid over-spending
\alpha
. If we let

\alpha_{t}=x_{t}\alpha,

(11)

then we require

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}x_{t}\leq 1,

(12)

so we need
x_{t}
to be a convergent series.

Now we show that the answer to the question is a negative of the form: “It is not quite possible, but nearly so.”

Theorem 2
.

It is impossible to have all
p
-value requirements
\delta_{t}
given by Corollary
2
equal for an unbounded test if
r_{t}=\lceil ut\rceil
for a constant
u>0
.

Proof.

Recall that

\delta_{t}=\alpha_{t}r_{t}=x_{t}\alpha\lceil ut\rceil.

(13)

Suppose these values are all equal to a constant
c
. Then, for all
t\in\left\{1,2,\ldots\right\}
,

x_{t}=\frac{c}{\alpha\lceil ut\rceil}>\frac{c}{\alpha(ut+1)}

(14)

=\frac{c}{\alpha u}\left(\frac{1}{t+\frac{1}{u}}\right).

(15)

We require the sum
x_{1}+x_{2}+\dots\leq 1
, but the sum of these terms diverges. Informally, it diverges because the terms are on the order of harmonic series terms:
\frac{1}{t}
, and the harmonic series diverges.

More formally, note that:

\frac{1}{t+\frac{1}{u}}=\frac{1}{t}-\frac{\frac{1}{u}}{t(t+\frac{1}{u})}.

(16)

So

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}x_{t}>

(17)

\frac{c}{\alpha u}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{u}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t(t+\frac{1}{u})}\right]

(18)

The second sum is less than

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}.

(19)

We know that the
p
-series

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{v}}

(20)

diverges for
0\leq v\leq 1
and converges for
v>1
. So in Expression
18
, the first sum diverges and the second converges, implying that the expression diverges.
∎

In short, we cannot have
x_{t}
proportional to
\frac{1}{t}
because its
p
-series diverges. However, we can use
x_{t}
proportional to any
\frac{1}{t^{v}}
for any
v>1
, since its
p
-series converges. So, in a sense, Theorem
2
is the most nearly possible impossible result possible.

Since

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{v}}=\zeta(v),

(21)

where
\zeta()
is the Riemann zeta function, we can set

\forall t:\hbox{ }x_{t}=\frac{1}{\zeta(v)t^{v}}

(22)

for any
v>1
. As
v\rightarrow 1
, we flatten the
\delta_{t}
curve, but
\zeta(v)
increases, making the initial
p
-value requirement more stringent. For example,
\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}
, so we can set

x_{t}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}t^{2}},

(23)

and get

\delta_{t}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}t^{2}}\alpha\lceil ut\rceil\geq\frac{6\alpha u}{\pi^{2}t}.

(24)

shows the

(26)

As

VI
Discussion

We have explored different ways to spend
\alpha
with repetition requirements for unbounded tests, showing that it is possible to adjust the levels of significance required at each decision point by requiring more repetition. We have also shown that it is impossible to have a significance requirement that does not eventually become more stringent for an unbounded test, but we can stave that off for an arbitrary number of decision points by setting parameters appropriately.

An earlier paper
[
16
]
that focused on bounded tests discussed how to balance control of type 1 and type 2 error, by spending a portion of
\alpha
on a single loose requirement for significance at the end of the test, which is reminiscent of traditional sequential testing strategies, such as the Haybittle-Peto boundary
[
28
,
2
]
. It is a challenge to imagine something similar for unbounded tests, since their ending decision points are not known a priori. Perhaps spending a portion of
\alpha
for loose requirements at a (possibly unbounded) subsequence of the decision points, either with or without a repetition requirement, would prove useful.

It would also be interesting to explore ways to combine repetition requirements with other approaches to continuous monitoring and always-valid bounds
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. In some cases, it may be possible to improve both types of bounds by combining their strategies. The challenge is that non-repetition methods sometimes make use of side conditions that are proven empirically in some sense with repetition requirements – we need to analyze how these approaches overlap to understand how they can complement each other beyond mere duplication, if possible.
