Koopman Reduced Order Modeling with Confidence Bounds
Ryan Mohr, Maria Fonoberova, Igor Mezić

Abstract
This paper introduces a reduced order modeling technique based on Koopman operator theory that gives confidence bounds on the model’s predictions. It is based on a data-driven spectral decomposition of the Koopman operator. The reduced order model is constructed using a finite number of Koopman eigenvalues and modes, while the rest of spectrum is treated as a noise process. This noise process is used to extract the confidence bounds. Additionally, we propose a heuristic algorithm to choose the number of deterministic modes to keep in the model.

Introduction

Koopman operator theory and Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) have become ubiquitous in the data-driven analysis of complex and often high-dimensional systems
[
19
,
24
,
5
,
4
]
. Given a vector-valued observable, the Koopman operator can be decomposed into eigenvalues, eigenfunctions, and Koopman modes plus the operator’s continuous spectrum
[
19
]
. Dynamic Mode Decomposition is an algorithm that can compute approximations to the Koopman modes. When the continuous spectrum exists, DMD tries to approximate it with modes and eigenvalues, when it would be better approximated by a stochastic process. We take this idea and try to find an optimal number of modes and model the rest as a stochastic process.

The result of this representation, first proposed in
[
19
]
, is that instead of only being able to make point predictions, we can make predictions in a distributional sense using the coherent part and the statistics of the residual process to get confidence bounds on the prediction.

Related literature.

The method in this paper constructs a reduced order model by projecting onto a low-dimensional subspace spanned by a finite number of Koopman modes and represents the unresolved dynamics with a stochastic process. Similar methods have been proposed in turbulence and climate studies
[

,

,

,

,

,

.

A related concept in Koopman operator theory is that of the stochastic Koopman operator associated with a random dynamical system
[
19
]
. Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},P)
and a family of random maps
\{T_{\omega}\}
parameterized by
\omega\in\Omega
the stochastic Koopman operator computes the expectation of an observable over realizations of the random maps by
\mathcal{K}f=\mathbb{E}_{P}[f\circ T_{\omega}(x)]=\int_{\Omega}f\circ T_{\omega}(x)dP
. The difference is that the stochastic Koopman operator starts from a random dynamical system and computes expectations of the evolution of the observable versus the method in this paper that starts from a dynamical system and represents it as a deterministic part plus a stochastic process.

Our method discards a subset of the computed DMD modes and eigenvalues in favor of representing those modes with a stochastic process. While our goal is to get confidence bounds on the predictions, one could argue that the discarded modes do not adequately represent the dynamics and a stochastic process better represents the unresolved dynamics. Previous methods,
[
9
,
8
,
7
]
truncate modes and eigenvalues by estimating the residual
\lVert Kg-\lambda g\rVert
and discarding those modes and eigenvalues whose residual is greater than a specified accuracy. The result of these methods is that they discard potentially useful, if noisy, parts of the dynamics.

In
[
17
]
, the authors investigate the prediction accuracy of Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD) on parabolic PDEs. They show that the local truncation error between the true solution and EDMD can be bound by a term that only depends on the number of snapshots used for EDMD and which goes to zero as the number of snapshots goes to zero.

The problem of approximating the Koopman operator in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) was taken up in
[
22
]
. A cross-covariance operator acting on the RKHS is defined via as an integral operator involving the Koopman operator and the reproducing kernel. Bounds between the cross-covariance operator and its empirical approximation are derived and, using these results, a bound on a certain norm between the Koopman operator and its empirical approximation are found.

In
[
16
]
, the authors define a Koopman operator over a Gaussian process
\mathsf{GP}(\mu,k)
. The Gaussian process is an infinite-dimensional distribution over the space of real-valued functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. The Gaussian process is characterized by specifying, a priori, the mean
\mu:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and the covariance functions
k:\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\to\mathbb{R}
. Assuming deterministic dynamics, the authors show that if an observable is distributed according to the Gaussian process,
f\sim\mathsf{GP}(\mu,k)
, then the Koopman operator applied to
f
is also a Gaussian process such that
U^{t}f=\mathsf{GP}(U^{t}\mu,U^{t}k)
. The limitations of this approach are that it requires the dynamics to be deterministic and the mean
\mu
and covariances
k
must be specified a priori rather than being learned from data.

Contributions of this paper.

We build off reduced order modeling techniques using the Koopman operator

. Much recent work has been done on algorithms that compute an approximation of the point spectrum of the Koopman operator

. These methods are purely data-driven and are based off computing a spectral decomposition of the operator without requiring an analytic representation of the infinite-dimensional operator. However, it is known that these algorithms can return spurious eigenvalues due to numerical issues or trying to capture the continuous part of the spectrum with eigenvalues.

In this paper, we develop a method, based on Koopman operator theory, that constructs a model and also gives confidence bounds on its predictions. For measure-preserving dynamical systems, the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
splits into a singular component,
\mathcal{K}_{s}
, and a regular component,
\mathcal{K}_{r}

[
19
]
. The singular part contains only the point spectrum while the regular part contains only the continuous spectrum. Low-dimensional projects can be obtained by retaining a finite number of modes and introducing stochastic terms to account for the neglected modes
[
19
]
. The algorithm in this paper implements the above idea by computing a Koopman Mode Decomposition on the training data; a finite number of the modes are selected as part of the ROM; the rest of the dynamics are modeled as a noise process, splitting the part of the noise in the subspace spanned by the Koopman modes used for the ROM (called “in-plane” for short) and the part orthogonal to those modes. The in-plane noisy dynamics are used to estimate confidence bounds on the predictions. In this paper, we are concerned in situations where we only have a single trajectory of the system and we wish to forecast future states.

The contributions of this paper can be summarized by the following points:

(1)

A method of providing confidence bounds on the predictions of a reduced order Koopman model based on the estimated noise distribution of the residual parts of the dynamics.

(2)

A heuristic method for determining the minimum number of modes to be used for the reduced order model. The heuristic is based on the Shapiro-Wilk hypothesis test comparing how well the empirical distribution is represented by a Gaussian distribution. More sophisticated methods could use Kernel Density Estimation or Gaussian mixture models.

(3)

Application of the method to stochastic systems with changing network topology.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In section
2
, we develop the mathematical framework for the reduced order modeling framework with confidence bounds. Section
3
applies the methodology to a sequence of examples, two of which have dynamically changing network topology. Section
4
gives a heuristic algorithm for selecting how many modes to keep for the nominal dynamics so one is not modeling the continuous spectrum with the point spectrum. We conclude the paper in section
5
.

Koopman Reduced Order Modeling

Consider a dynamical system

\mathbf{x}(t+1)=T(\mathbf{x}(t)),\quad t\in\mathbb{N}

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}(t)\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and
T:\Omega\to\Omega
is a (possibly) nonlinear map. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a linear space of real- or complex-valued functions on
\Omega
. The induced Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}
is defined by the composition operation

\mathcal{K}f(\mathbf{x})=f(T(\mathbf{x}))

(2)

If
f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, we define
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}
and define the action of
\mathcal{K}
on
\mathbf{f}
as
\mathcal{K}\mathbf{f}=(\mathcal{K}f_{1},\dots,\mathcal{K}f_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}
.

In this paper, we assume there is a spectral decomposition for
\mathcal{K}
(see
[
19
]
which states precise conditions for this to hold) that can be written as

\mathcal{K}^{t}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}c_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\int_{\mathbb{C}}z^{t}dE_{c}(z)f_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

(3)

where
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
is an eigenvalue,
\phi_{j}\in\mathcal{F}
is an eigenfunction, and
dE_{c}
is a projection-valued measure corresponding to the continuous part of the spectrum. For the vector-valued observable case, we have

\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}\mathbf{m}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\int_{\mathbb{C}}z^{t}dE_{c}(z)\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),

(4)

where
\mathbf{m}_{j}
is the
j
-th Koopman mode.

2.1
Model reduction

With finite data, we cannot compute the infinite expansion. Instead we compute a finite-dimensional approximation of the point spectrum and model the rest of the evolution of the observable as a stochastic process. That is

\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{m}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{r}(t),

(5)

where here
\mathbf{r}(t)
is the residual part of the dynamics that we cannot resolve with a finite number of modes and contains the unresolved point spectrum and the continuous spectrum. The finite sequence formed by the residual dynamics forms empirical distribution
\mathfrak{R}
(e.g. a Gaussian distribution). Additionally, we order the modes
\mathbf{m}_{j}
according to their norm so that
\lVert\mathbf{m}_{j}\rVert\geq\lVert\mathbf{m}_{j+1}\rVert
. In case the modes are normalized, the eigenvalues can be ranked by their associated reconstruction coefficient. We choose an order
J
for the ROM and take the highest ranked modes and eigenvalues. This implies that the modes and eigenvalues of a lower-order model are subsets of those of higher-order models. To make the notation more compact, let us denote the finite-mode expansion as

D^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{m}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x}).

(6)

So far we have split the evolution of the observable into a finite-mode expansion and the residual dynamics. Now we look into splitting the residual dynamics into a modal component and an innovation component
Let
M=\linspan\{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}\}
,
\mathbf{M}
the matrix having the Koopman modes as its columns, and
P_{M}
the orthogonal projection onto
M
. Then,

\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{m}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})+P_{M}\mathbf{r}(t)}_{\in M}+\underbrace{(I-P_{M})\mathbf{r}(t)}_{\text{orthogonal to $M$}}.

(7)

Consider the terms lying in
M
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{m}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})+P_{M}\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{M}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{t}\phi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{J}^{t}\phi_{J}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{+}\mathbf{r}(t),

(8)

where we have used the fact that
P_{M}
can be computed as
P_{M}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{+}
, where
\mathbf{M}^{+}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\mathbf{M}
.
We define the
modal noise

\rho(t)=(\rho_{1}(t),\dots,\rho_{J}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}
as

\rho(t)=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{+}\mathbf{r}(t).

(9)

The
innovation noise
is then defined as

\eta(t)=(I-P_{M})\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}(t)-P_{M}\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}(t)-\rho(t).

(10)

The innovation noise
\eta(t)
is orthogonal to
M
. With these definitions, (
7
) becomes

\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\mathbf{M}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{t}\phi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{J}^{t}\phi_{J}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}+\rho(t)\right)+\eta(t).

(11)

2.3
Forecasting with Confidence Bounds

Once the noise sequences
\mathbf{r}(t),\rho(t),\eta(t)
are computed, empirical probability density models need to be constructed for which drawing samples is easy, for example by fitting a parameterized density function like the Gaussian distribution or using other methods such as kernel density estimation techniques taking the form
p_{\rho}(x)=\frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\frac{x-\rho(t_{i})}{h})
, where
K
is the kernel function (such as a Gaussian),
h
is the bandwidth, and
\rho(t_{i})
are the samples of the residual sequence. Assume that the densities for
\rho
and
\eta
are computed.

Recall that
M
is the subspace spanned by the Koopman modes,
M=\linspan\{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}\}
, and
P_{M}
is the orthogonal projection onto
M
. By (
11
) and since the innovation sequence
\eta
is orthogonal to
M
we have

P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=D^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\rho(t)

(20)

as the form of the reduced order model. Note that the reduced-order model is split into a deterministic part and a stochastic part.

In our experiments, we assume that the resulting statistics of the modal sequence form a Gaussian distribution (which can be checked; see Section
4
) and construct a prediction interval at each time
t
. We use the finite-mode expansion to give the mean of the prediction and use
\pm
2 standard deviations of the estimated Gaussian to give the prediction interval at each point. Thus, the prediction interval defined by the reduced order model at each time is

I(t)=[D^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})-2\mathsf{std}(\rho),D^{t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+2\mathsf{std}(\rho)]

(21)

To measure the quality of the ROM, we compute both a reconstruction error and the percent time that the real trajectory were within 2 standard deviations of the deterministic model’s prediction. This residence time can be computed for every coordinate in
\mathbf{f}
.

In (
21
) and the experiments below, we plot
(K^{t}\mathbf{f})_{i}
and
(D^{t}\mathbf{f})_{i}\pm 2\sigma_{\rho,i}
, where
\sigma_{\rho,i}
is the estimated standard deviation of the
i^{th}
coordinate of the modal noise
\{\rho(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:t=0,\dots,T\}
. The true signal
\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}
generally lies within these bounds, despite the true signal containing contributions from both the modal and innovation noise:
\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}=D^{t}\mathbf{f}+\rho(t)+\eta(t)
. The below computation shows why we can only consider the modal noise.

Let
\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
) be the true evolution and
D^{t}\mathbf{f}
be the deterministic model in the modal subspace given by

D^{t}\mathbf{f}=M\Lambda^{t}c_{\mathbf{f}}

(22)

where
c_{\mathbf{f}}=M^{+}\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
,
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times p}
is the matrix of Koopman modes and
\Lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
is the diagonal matrix of Koopman eigenvalues.

The residual and modal noise are given, respectively, by

\displaystyle r(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-D^{t}\mathbf{f}

(23)

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle=P_{M}r(t)=P_{M}(\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-D^{t}\mathbf{f})=P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-D^{t}\mathbf{f},

(24)

where
P_{M}=MM^{+}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\linspan M
,
M^{+}
is the pseudoinverse, and
P_{M}D^{t}\mathbf{f}=D^{t}\mathbf{f}
since
D^{t}\mathbf{f}\in\linspan M
.

Modal noise standard deviation.

We estimate the standard deviation of the modal noise from the sequence
\{e_{i}^{*}\rho(t):t=0,\dots,T\}
, where
e_{i}
is the
i^{th}
canonical basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. From the definitions,

e_{i}^{*}\rho(t)=e_{i}^{*}P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}.

(25)

Assuming
\mathbb{E}e_{i}^{*}\rho(t)=0
, the unbiased estimator for the variance of the
i^{th}
coordinate is

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\rho,i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}\rho(t)\rvert^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert^{2}.

(26)

We plot
e_{i}^{*}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}
and
e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}
for some coordinate
i
and see if they are within
\pm 2\hat{\sigma}_{\rho,i}
. Therefore, we need to bound the error between the true signal and the deterministic part of the ROM in terms of the standard deviations of the modal noise.

\displaystyle\lvert e_{i}^{*}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert e_{i}^{*}(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}+e_{i}^{*}P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert e_{i}^{*}(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert+\lvert e_{i}^{*}P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert e_{i}^{*}(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert+\left(\lvert e_{i}^{*}P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(27)

Taking the expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert e_{i}^{*}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert\right]

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert+\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(\lvert e_{i}^{*}P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(28)

Applying Jensen’s inequality to the last term on the right gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert e_{i}^{*}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert+\left(\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}P_{M}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert+\hat{\sigma}_{\rho,i}.

(29)

Therefore, if

\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert\leq\hat{\sigma}_{\rho,i},

(30)

then
e_{i}^{*}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}
and
e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}
are within
\pm 2\hat{\sigma}_{\rho,i}
in expectation.

From the definition
\eta(t)=r(t)-\rho(t)
, we can show that
\eta(t)=(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}
. Using the same assumption as above (
\mathbb{E}e_{i}^{*}\eta(t)=0
) and Jensen’s inequality, it can be shown that

\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\lvert e_{i}^{*}(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert\leq\hat{\sigma}_{\eta,i}.

(31)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert e_{i}^{*}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\hat{\sigma}_{\eta,i}+\hat{\sigma}_{\rho,i}.

(32)

If
\mathbf{f}
is in the invariant subspace of Koopman eigenfunctions, then
(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}=0
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\eta,i}=0
. If
\mathbf{f}
is nearly invariant with respect to the subspace, then
\lVert(I-P_{M})\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{F}\rVert
is small on the training set and we can check numerically that
\hat{\sigma}_{\eta,i}\leq\hat{\sigma}_{\rho,i}
. In either case, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert e_{i}^{*}\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{f}-e_{i}^{*}D^{t}\mathbf{f}\rvert\right]\leq 2\hat{\sigma}_{\rho,i}.

(33)

3
Performance and Comparisons

3.1
Linear Modal System

We first test the algorithm for a system with known modes and eigenvalues. While in practice, it would not make sense to represent a known linear finite-dimensional model with the Koopman operator, this example acts as a sanity check. Additionally, in practice we only have trajectory data and may not know the underlying dynamics is linear. This example also shows that when lifting to a higher-dimensional space, spurious eigenvalues can be introduced. While everything was formulated with respect to deterministic dynamical systems, we will test both with a small amount of additive Gaussian noise to the dynamics. The general model of the system is given by

\mathbf{x}(t)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{m}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}\right)+\xi(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

(34)

where
\mathbf{m}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\xi(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a noise sequence with each coordinate independent and identically distributed with a normal distribution. We note that the evolution of this system is in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
rather than
\mathbb{R}^{n}
due to the choice of complex
\lambda_{j}
’s; therefore, the eigenvalues do not need to include the complex conjugates.

For the following numerical results, the system parameters were chosen as
J=20
,
n=40
, and
\xi_{i}(t)\sim N(0,0.25^{2})
. The values of the coordinates of each mode
\mathbf{m}_{j}
were chosen from a uniform distribution between -1 and 1 and afterwards the mode was normalized to have norm 1. The associated eigenvalues were chosen uniformly at random in the box
[-1,1]\times[-1i,1i]
in the complex plane, then scaled to be on the unit circle.

The trajectory was simulated for 401 time steps resulting in a simulation data matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{40\times 401}
. To construct the observable
\mathbf{f}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
used for the Koopman model, we take a delay-embedding of the trajectory
\mathbf{x}(t)
with a delay of 300. This gives a data matrix of size
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{C}^{12,000\times 101}
as input for the reduced-order modeling algorithm. Therefore, there are a total of 100 Koopman modes in
\mathbb{C}^{12,000}
for this model, even though
\mathbf{x}(t)
is constructed using only 20 modes in
\mathbb{C}^{40}
. For all examples, the Cauchy-Vandermonde version of DMD was used
[
10
]
.

Figure
1

4
A heuristic for estimating the minimum number of modes to use for the ROM

Above, we have computed an ablation study of reduced order models for 3 examples, from a minimum of 10 modes up to a full reconstruction (100 modes in all examples). The question remains how to choose the final number of modes to be used in the reduced order model (ROM); too few and we get poor prediction accuracy because we are not accurately capturing the nominal dynamics, too many modes and we are trying to capture noise and the continuous spectrum with eigenvalues and eigenfunctions.

In this section, we present a heuristic method to determine the minimum number of modes that must be taken.This heuristic is motivated by plots that show that by increasing the number of modes, the the distributions seem to become Gaussian. This method is based on testing the normality of the modal distribution that is computed for each ROM. The rationale is as follows. In our examples, we have structured dynamics plus added noise and a changing network topology. When we construct our model, we compute a deterministic model and then estimate the modal noise distribution. We assume that once we have adequately recovered the true structured dynamics with the computed deterministic part of the ROM the modal noise will have a Gaussian distribution. This can happen due to a variety of settings: the deterministic chaotic part has hyperbolic dynamics, or the stochastic elements are Gaussian distributed, or the number of dimensions is very large and projection yields the central limit theorem. If too few modes are used for the deterministic model, much of the structured part of the dynamics will be modeled as noise, destroying the normality of the modal distribution. By using too many modes in the deterministic model, the noisy part of the dynamics are subsumed into a deterministic model, possibly destroying the normality of the distribution. Since we are fitting a Gaussian to the distributions, the non-normality of the distribution can lead to inaccurate estimates for the confidence bounds, thereby degrading the model’s predictions.

5
Discussion and Conclusions

We have developed a method for reduced order modeling using Koopman operator theory that gives confidence bounds on the predictions of the reduced order model. While the reduced order model is by necessity a finite process, the spectral expansion of the Koopman operator is infinite. The reduced order model represents the process with a finite number of Koopman modes. The rest of the dynamics are modeled as a noise process. The part of the noise process that is in the subspace modeled by the ROM’s modes is called the modal process. This noise process is modeled as a Gaussian noise process and the estimated standard deviation of the noise is used to compute a confidence bound on the ROM’s predictions.

We have applied this modeling to a sequence of examples. The first was a synthetic example where we could directly specify the Koopman eigenvalues and modes, so the computed Koopman spectrum could be compared to ground truth. The next two examples represented networked dynamical systems that had changing network topology. The first was a network of noisy, anharmonic oscillators whose connections were switched at each integer time. This system was chosen due to the fact that a single, non-noisy, anharmonic oscillator has a purely continuous spectrum, except the eigenvalue at 1, and thus does not have an expansion in terms of Koopman modes and eigenfunctions. Despite this, the methodology was able to model the system’s evolution with a small number of computed modes plus the noise process. The actual trajectory of the system stayed within the confidence bounds of the ROM’s prediction a large percentage of the time. The last example consisted of a noisy Kuramoto model with random coupling strength between the oscillators. As with the anharmonic oscillator example, the true signal stayed within the confidence bounds of the ROM’s prediction the majority of the time.

As the number of modes increased, the ROM’s got closer to a deterministic plus Gaussian noise model. As we pass the threshold, the variance of the Gaussian decreases. This allows us to propose a heuristic algorithm that enables choosing the number of deterministic modes that should be kept.

In this paper, we assumed a Gaussian distribution of the modal noise when constructing the confidence bounds for the ROM predictions. Future work could relax this assumption and use something like kernel density estimation to construct the bounds.
