Nonlinear extension of the quantum dynamical semigroup
Jakub Rembieliński, Paweł Caban

Abstract
In this paper we consider deterministic nonlinear time evolutions satisfying so called convex quasi-linearity condition. Such evolutions preserve the equivalence of ensembles and therefore are free from problems with signaling. We show that if family of linear non-trace-preserving maps satisfies the semigroup property then the generated family of convex quasi-linear operations also possesses the semigroup property. Next we generalize the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad type equation for the considered evolution. As examples we discuss the general qubit evolution in our model as well as an extension of the Jaynes–Cummings model. We apply our formalism to spin density matrix of a charged particle moving in the electromagnetic field as well as to flavor evolution of solar neutrinos.



1
Introduction

In last decades many authors tried to generalize the standard quantum
mechanical evolution. Two most important approaches are based on
including nonlinear operations (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
35
,
36
]
)
and considering non-Hermitian Hamiltonians (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
28
]
).

Deterministic nonlinear evolutions are believed to allow for signaling

[
19
]
, which was for the first time explicitly shown
by Gisin in
[
18
]
, compare also

[
29
,
13
]
.
Such evolutions are usually defined for pure states:

f_{t}\colon|\psi\rangle\to|\psi(t)\rangle,

(1)

and consequently the evolution of ensembles is assumed to have
the following form:
If
f_{t}(|\psi_{1}\rangle)=|\psi_{1}(t)\rangle
,

f_{t}(|\psi_{2}\rangle)=|\psi_{2}(t)\rangle
then

f_{t}\colon\lambda|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|+(1-\lambda)|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|\to\\
\lambda|\psi_{1}(t)\rangle\langle\psi_{1}(t)|+(1-\lambda)|\psi_{2}(t)\rangle\langle\psi_{2}(t)|,

(2)

i.e., coefficients of the ensemble do not change under the evolution.
This assumption easily implies that deterministic nonlinear evolution
breaks the equivalence of ensembles corresponding to the same
mixed state and results in the possibility of arbitrary fast signaling

[
3
]
. In our recent paper

[
30
]
it was shown that if we replace
the assumption (
2
) by the
so called convex quasi-linearity, i.e., we allow the appropriate
change of the coefficients
[see Eq. (
3
)], then evolution satisfying
such a condition preserves equivalence of ensembles and
consequently such evolutions cannot be ruled out by the
standard Gisin’s argument.

Let us stress that in our approach we do not change anything else
in the quantum formalism but only extend admissible set of
quantum evolutions by including nonlinear deterministic evolutions
that do not admit superluminal signaling.
This is in contrast with such
nonlinear extensions of quantum mechanics that does not
allow
signaling but demand modifications of other quantum mechanical rules
(see, e.g.,
[
14
,
22
]
).
We also do not consider here stochastic nonlinear evolutions some of which
are free from the problems with signaling and have many important
applications (like in the collapse models
[
15
,
4
]
).

In this paper we demonstrate that there exist a large class of
convex quasi-linear evolutions.
These evolutions are generated by linear non-trace-preserving
quantum operations
and/or derived from generalized master equation.
What is interesting, recently considered evolutions
generated by non-Hermitian Hamiltonians
[
32
,
21
,
10
,
25
]

also belong to this class.
It shows that convex quasi-linearity might be used as a principle joining
nonlinear quantum mechanics and non-Hermitian quantum mechanics.

In Sec.
2
we remind the definition of
convex quasi-linearity
formulated in
[
30
]

and demonstrate that each
linear non-trace-preserving
quantum operation generates convex quasi-linear operation.
In Sec.
3
we show that if family of linear
non-trace-preserving maps satisfies the semigroup property then the
generated family of convex quasi-linear operations also possesses
the semigroup property. Next we consider
Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad type equation for
the considered evolution.
Sec.
4
is devoted to the discussion of qubit evolution
in our model
while in Sec.
5
we apply our
formalism to two physical systems.
We finish with some discussion and conclusions.

2
Admissible nonlinear quantum operations

We start with recalling the definition of convex quasi-linearity

[
30
]
.
Let us denote by
S
the convex set of density matrices.
We call a map
\Phi\colon S\to S
convex quasi-linear if
for all
\rho_{i}\in S
and
p_{i}\in\langle 0,1\rangle
,

\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
(
i=1,\dots,N
) there exist
\bar{p}_{i}
such that

\Phi\big[\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{i}\big]=\sum_{i}\bar{p}_{i}\Phi[\rho_{i}]

(3)

and
\bar{p}_{i}\in\langle 0,1\rangle
,

\sum_{i}\bar{p}_{i}=1
.
Below we show that there exists a class of convex quasi-linear
operations.

An example is discussed by Kraus
[
26
]
as a generalized
measurement. We considered
this example in details in our previous paper
[
30
]
.

Let us consider the most general linear quantum operation

\varphi
having the Kraus form

\rho_{in}\mapsto\varphi(\rho_{in})=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\alpha_{max}}K_{\alpha}\rho_{in}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger},

(4)

where
\tr(\rho_{in})=1
,
K_{\alpha}
are Kraus operators,

\alpha_{max}<N^{2}
,
N<\infty
is the dimension of the Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}
of the considered system.
Furthermore,
F=\sum_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}K_{\alpha}\leq I
.
To obtain a map into the convex set of density matrices we must normalize
the
\varphi(\rho_{in})
in the case
F<I
.
Consequently, the complete quantum operation has the form

\displaystyle\rho_{out}=\Phi(\rho_{in})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tr(\sum_{\beta}K_{\beta}\rho_{in}K_{\beta}^{\dagger})}\sum_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\rho_{in}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}

(5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tr(F\rho_{in})}\sum_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\rho_{in}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger},

(6)

i.e.,

\Phi(\rho_{in})=\frac{\varphi(\rho_{in})}{\tr[\varphi(\rho_{in})]}.

(7)

Of course
\tr[\varphi(\rho_{in})]=1
implies linearity of
\Phi
.
It is easy to see that
\Phi
is in general convex quasi-linear, i.e.,

\Phi(\lambda\rho_{in}^{a}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{in}^{b})=\bar{\lambda}\Phi(\rho_{in}^{a})+(1-\bar{\lambda})\Phi(\rho_{in}^{b}),

(8)

where
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
and thus

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{\tr(F\rho_{in}^{a})}{\tr(F\rho_{in})}\geq 0

(9)

\displaystyle 1-\bar{\lambda}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\frac{\tr(F\rho_{in}^{b})}{\tr(F\rho_{in})}\geq 0,

(10)

so
0\leq\bar{\lambda}\leq 1
. Of course, if
\tr[\varphi(\rho_{in})]=1

then
\bar{\lambda}=\lambda
(compare
[
20
]
).

From the above definition it follows that the frequently
used argument that two equivalent
ensembles after nonlinear map lost this equivalence does not apply
to the map
\Phi
.
This means that
\Phi
can be treated as the generalized form
of the acceptable quantum operation.

3
Admissible nonlinear evolutions

Now, the notion of convex quasi-linearity can be extended for
deterministic time evolution. Namely, the transformation

\rho(t)=\Phi_{t}[\rho_{0}],\qquad\rho_{0}=\rho(0),

(11)

is a convex quasi-linear time evolution if
\Phi_{t}
forms a semigroup, i.e.,

\Phi_{t_{1}}\circ\Phi_{t_{2}}=\Phi_{t_{1}+t_{2}}
and the condition
(
3
) holds for all times
t
, i.e., for all

\rho_{0i}\in S
,
p_{i}\in\langle 0,1\rangle
,

\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
there exist such
p_{i}(t)
that:

\Phi_{t}\big[\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{0i}\big]=\sum_{i}p_{i}(t)\Phi_{t}[\rho_{0i}]

(12)

and
p_{i}(t)\in\langle 0,1\rangle
,
\sum_{i}p_{i}(t)=1
.
In our paper
[
30
]

we have found a model of a convex quasi-linear
time evolution of a qubit. Here we show that there exists
a large class of natural evolutions fulfilling the above conditions.

3.1
Quasi-linear time evolution generated by linear transformations

According to the above discussion there are no formal objections to identify
time evolution of density operators with a family of time dependent extended
quantum operations.
Consequently we postulate the evolution of the density operator in the
form of the nonlinear map

\rho(t)=\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0})=\frac{\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})}{\tr[\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})]}=\frac{\sum_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}(t)\rho_{0}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(t)}{\tr[F(t)\rho_{0}]},

(13)

with

F(t)=\sum_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(t)K_{\alpha}(t).

(14)

Here
\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\alpha_{max}}K_{\alpha}(t)\rho_{0}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(t)
,
\tr(\rho_{0})=1
. Notice that
if
F(t)=I
then the evolution is linear.
The initial condition
\rho_{0}=\rho(0)
is related standardly with

K_{0}(0)=I
and
K_{\alpha}(0)=0
for
\alpha=1,2,\dots,\alpha_{max}
.

Let us assume that the family of linear positive maps
\varphi_{t}

satisfy
\varphi_{\tau}(\varphi_{t}(M))=\varphi_{\tau+t}(M)
for each

M
, i.e.,
\{\varphi_{t}\}
forms a one parameter semigroup.
Then using linearity of
\varphi_{t}
and the definition of
\Phi_{t}
we have
for each
\rho_{0}

\displaystyle\Phi_{\tau}(\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi_{\tau}(\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0}))}{\tr[\varphi_{\tau}(\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0}))]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\Big(\frac{\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})}{\tr[\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})]}\Big)}{\tr\Big[\varphi_{\tau}\Big(\frac{\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})}{\tr[\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})]}\Big)\Big]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi_{\tau}(\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0}))}{\tr[\varphi_{\tau}(\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0}))]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi_{\tau+t}(\rho_{0})}{\tr[\varphi_{\tau+t}(\rho_{0})]}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\tau+t}(\rho_{0}),

(15)

i.e.,
\Phi_{\tau}(\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0}))=\Phi_{\tau+t}(\rho_{0})
.
We conclude that under our assumptions the family of
quantum operations
\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0})
forms a nonlinear realization of the
one-parameter semigroup realized in the convex set of the density matrices.
In particular, subfamily of trace-preserving evolutions
(for
F(t)=I
) became linear.
Notice also that the above time evolution preserves convex quasi-linearity,
i.e.,

\Phi_{t}(\lambda\rho_{0}^{a}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{0}^{b})=\bar{\lambda}(t)\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0}^{a})+(1-\bar{\lambda}(t))\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0}^{b}),

(16)

where
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
and

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{\tr[\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0}^{a})]}{\tr[\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})]},

(17)

\displaystyle 1-\bar{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\frac{\tr[\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0}^{b})]}{\tr[\varphi_{t}(\rho_{0})]},

(18)

and
\bar{\lambda}(t)\leq 1
.

3.2
Nonlinear extension of the
Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad equation

The linear dynamics of an open quantum system is described
by Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) dynamical
semigroup which is generated by the GKSL generator.

Using the infinitesimal form of the global time evolution as well as the form of
the effect operator
F(t)
and defining

K_{0}(\delta t)\approx I+\delta t(G-iH)
,
where
G
and
H
are Hermitian while for

\alpha=1,2,\dots,\alpha_{max}
,
K_{\alpha}(\delta t)\approx\sqrt{\delta t}L_{\alpha}
,
we obtain the generalization of the action of this generator to the form

\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}[\rho]\equiv-i[H,\rho]+\{G,\rho\}+\sum_{\alpha=1}(L_{\alpha}\rho L_{\alpha}^{\dagger})\\
-\rho\tr\Big[\rho\big(2G+\sum_{\alpha=1}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha}\big)\Big]

(19)

(the detailed derivation of the above equation is given in Appendix).
Notice, that the nonlinearity of the dynamics of the density operator is rather weak:
It relies on the nonlinear coupling between
\rho
(operator) and the
\rho
-dependent
trace
\tr\Big(\rho\big(2G+\sum_{\alpha=1}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha}\big)\Big)
.

From the above dynamical equation it follows that infinitesimally
the operator
F(t)
is generated by

2G+\sum_{\alpha=1}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha}.

(20)

We observe that for
F(t)=I
, i.e., for

2G+\sum_{\alpha=1}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha}=0
,
we recover the standard form of the GKSL generator:

\mathcal{L}[\rho]=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{\alpha=1}\Big(L_{\alpha}\rho L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha},\rho\}\Big).

(21)

Notice, that vanishing of the Lindblad generators (only
K_{0}
is nonzero)
implies that the nonlinear Kraus evolution reduces to

\rho(t)=\frac{K(t)\rho_{0}K(t)^{\dagger}}{\tr[K(t)\rho_{0}K(t)^{\dagger}]}

(22)

with

K(t)=e^{(G-iH)t},

(23)

where
G^{\dagger}=G
,
H^{\dagger}=H

and we can restrict ourselves to the case of traceless generators
G
and
H
.
Thus, the family of
K(t)
operators forms an one-parameter subgroup of the

SL
(N,\mathbb{C})
linear group.
The corresponding GKSL equation reduces to the nonlinear generalization of
the von Neumann equation, i.e., (compare
[
32
]
)

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+\{G,\rho\}-2\rho\tr(G\rho)

(24)

with the initial condition
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
.
Let us observe that the pure states form for this evolution an invariant subset
in the convex set of density operators.
Indeed, we see that in this case

\rho_{0}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
with
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1
,
so the evolution equation takes the form

\rho(t)=\frac{K(t)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|K(t)^{\dagger}}{\langle\psi|K(t)^{\dagger}K(t)|\psi\rangle}.

(25)

Therefore

\rho(t)^{2}=\Bigg(\frac{K(t)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|K(t)^{\dagger}}{\langle\psi|K(t)^{\dagger}K(t)|\psi\rangle}\Bigg)^{2}=\rho(t),

(26)

i.e.,
\rho(t)
is a pure state.
Therefore, we can find a counterpart of Eq. (
24
) for
state vectors. However, the corresponding equation describing the evolution
of a state vector is not uniquely determined by Eq. (
24
).
Indeed, the whole family of equations of the form

\frac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle=\Big(-iH+G-\frac{\langle\psi|G|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}I+i\kappa I\Big)|\psi\rangle,

(27)

where
\kappa
is an arbitrary real function of
|\psi\rangle
,
leads to the evolution equation (
24
) for density matrices.
It is worth to mention here that in general the evolution equation for a state vector
also does not determine uniquely the evolution equation for density matrices.

Evolution of the form (
27
) for pure states with
\kappa=0

and
G=kH
was discussed by Gisin in
[
16
]

and subsequently with general
G
and
\kappa=0
in

[
17
]
.
Evolution similar to (
24
) was also considered in a specific
experimental context of interaction of a two-level atom with maser photons

[
9
]
.
Eq. (
24
) was also proposed in

[
10
]
as an evolution equation in presence of gain and loss while
in
[
25
]
the information retrieval
during such an evolution was discussed.

The fact that equation (
24
) preserves the coherence of pure
states means that
in the density matrix evolution
this part of the master equation (
19
)
plays different role than Lindblad operators.
We will discuss this question later.

The generalized GKLS master equation (
19
) admits
also evolutions with time-dependent generators;
in particular in (
24
) and (
27
)
H
and
G

can be time-dependent.
In such a case the global solutions have different form than those with
time-independent generators.
Such evolutions are very interesting because they can contain structural instability points

[
1
]
.

4
Nonlinear qubit evolution

In this section we discuss qubit evolution in our model.
Under the special choice of the von Neumann nonlinear equation it
was also investigated recently in
[
32
,
33
,
37
,
21
]
.
In this case the initial density matrix can be taken in the following form:

\rho(0)=\frac{1}{2}(I+\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}),

(28)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a real vector satisfying the condition

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{2}\leq 1
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the triple of the Pauli matrices.

4.1
The case of vanishing Lindblad generators (
L_{\alpha}=0
)

The
SL
(2,\mathbb{C})
nonlinear evolution of
\rho
in the case of vanishing of the Lindblad generators has the form

\rho(t)=\frac{1}{2}(I+\textbf{n}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\frac{K(t)\rho(0)K(t)^{\dagger}}{\tr[K(t)\rho(0)K(t)^{\dagger}]},

(29)

where the evolution matrix reads

K(t)=e^{(G-iH)t},

(30)

with
G=\tfrac{1}{2}\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,

H=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and
g
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}

are fixed real vectors. Moreover we assume
\textbf{n}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

This evolution can be represented on the Bloch ball as a nonlinear
realization of one-parameter subgroup of the orthochronous Lorentz group
homomorphic to
SL
(2,\mathbb{C})
.
Notice that the quantities
C_{1}=\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}
and

C_{2}=\textbf{g}^{2}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}
are invariant under inner automorphisms
of the
SL
(2,\mathbb{C})
. Therefore, values of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}

determine different types of evolution.

We get explicitly

K(t)=e^{(G-iH)t}=aI+b(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}),

(31)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\textbf{g}-i\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}\not=0
:

a=\cosh(\tfrac{t}{2}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}}),\quad b=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}}}\sinh(\tfrac{t}{2}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}}),

(32)

while for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}=0
:

a=1,\quad b=\tfrac{t}{2}.

(33)

The general form of
\textbf{n}(t)
can be found from
Eq. (
29
).
After simple but rather lengthy calculation we obtain:

\textbf{n}(t)=\Big\{aa^{*}+bb^{*}\big(g^{2}+\omega^{2}-2(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\big)+(ab^{*}+a^{*}b)(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})+i(ab^{*}-a^{*}b)(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\Big\}^{-1}\times\Big\{[aa^{*}-bb^{*}(g^{2}+\omega^{2})]\boldsymbol{\xi}\\
+[ab^{*}+a^{*}b+2bb^{*}(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})]\textbf{g}+[i(ab^{*}-a^{*}b)+2bb^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})]\boldsymbol{\omega}-2bb^{*}(\textbf{g}\times\boldsymbol{\omega})-i(ab^{*}-a^{*}b)(\textbf{g}\times\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
+(ab^{*}+a^{*}b)(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\xi})\Big\}.

(34)

If we restrict our attention to the case when

C_{1}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{g}=0

then
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
for the sake of brevity we introduce the following notation:

\Omega=\sqrt{|g^{2}-\omega^{2}|}.

(35)

The considered case divides into three sub-classes:

(i)
g^{2}=\omega^{2}
(
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}=0
),
a,b
are given
in Eq. (
33
) and

\textbf{n}(t)=\frac{(1-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega^{2}t^{2})\boldsymbol{\xi}+[t+\tfrac{1}{2}(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})t^{2}]\textbf{g}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})t^{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}+\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})+t(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\xi})}{1+t(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})+\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}(\omega^{2}-(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})},

(36)

(ii)
g^{2}>\omega^{2}
,
\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}}=\Omega

(compare Eq. (
35
)),

a=\cosh(\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega t)
,

b=\tfrac{1}{\Omega}\sinh(\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega t)
and

\textbf{n}(t)=\frac{\big(g^{2}-\omega^{2}\cosh(\Omega t)\big)\boldsymbol{\xi}+\Omega\sinh(\Omega t)(\textbf{g}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\xi})-\big(1-\cosh(\Omega t)\big)[(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\textbf{g}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{\omega}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g}]}{g^{2}\cosh(\Omega t)-\omega^{2}+\big(1-\cosh(\Omega t)\big)(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}+\Omega\sinh(\Omega t)(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})},

(37)

(iii)
\omega^{2}>g^{2}
,
\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}}=i\Omega

(compare Eq. (
35
)),

a=\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega t)
,

b=\tfrac{1}{\Omega}\sin(\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega t)
and

\textbf{n}(t)=\frac{\big(\omega^{2}\cos(\Omega t)-g^{2}\big)\boldsymbol{\xi}+\Omega\sin(\Omega t)(\textbf{g}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\xi})+\big(1-\cos(\Omega t)\big)[(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\textbf{g}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{\omega}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g}]}{\omega^{2}-g^{2}\cos(\Omega t)-\big(1-\cos(\Omega t)\big)(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}+\Omega\sin(\Omega t)(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})}.

(38)

To simplify our example, in what follow we put

\boldsymbol{\omega}=(0,0,\omega)
and
\textbf{g}=(g,0,0)
,
\omega,g>0
.
In this case the generator
H+iG
satisfies the
PT
-symmetry condition and was
used in
[
10
]
.

Let us calculate the time evolution of the probability
of finding the evolved state
\rho(t)
in eigenvectors
of the Hamiltonian
H
.
For
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(0,0,\omega)

the normalized eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian
H=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{3}

have the form:

a(0)_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad a(0)_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(39)

thus the corresponding projectors correspond to the

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\pm}=(0,0,\pm 1)
. We obtain:

g=\omega

\displaystyle p_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+n_{3}(t)}{2}=\frac{4+(gt)^{2}}{4+2(gt)^{2}},

(40)

\displaystyle p_{-}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-n_{3}(t)}{2}=\frac{(gt)^{2}}{4+2(gt)^{2}}.

(41)

In the asymptotic limit
p_{\pm}(\infty)=\frac{1}{2}
.

g>\omega

\displaystyle p_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+n_{3}(t)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{g^{2}\cosh(\Omega t)+g^{2}-2\omega^{2}}{g^{2}\cosh(\Omega t)-\omega^{2}},

(42)

\displaystyle p_{-}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-n_{3}(t)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{g^{2}\cosh(\Omega t)-g^{2}}{g^{2}\cosh(\Omega t)-\omega^{2}}.

(43)

In the asymptotic limit
p_{\pm}(\infty)=\frac{1}{2}
.

g<\omega

\displaystyle p_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+n_{3}(t)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\omega^{2}-g^{2}\cos(\Omega t)-g^{2}}{\omega^{2}-g^{2}\cos(\Omega t)},

(44)

\displaystyle p_{-}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-n_{3}(t)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{g^{2}-g^{2}\cos(\Omega t)}{\omega^{2}-g^{2}\cos(\Omega t)}.

(45)

The case
g<\omega
(when
\Omega=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-g^{2}}
)
is the most interesting one (compare with
[
21
]
).
In this case the probability of finding the evolved state in one of the eigenstates
of the Hamiltonian
H
,
a_{-}=(0,1)
, is given by (
45
).
For comparison, let us remind that the counterpart of the probability
(
45
) obtained for the standard Rabi oscillations
(i.e. in the case of evolution governed by the Hamiltonian

H=\tfrac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{3}+\tfrac{g}{2}\sigma_{1}
)
has the form
[
12
]

p_{-}^{\mathsf{Rabi}}=\frac{g^{2}}{2(g^{2}+\omega^{2})}\big(1-\cos(t\sqrt{g^{2}+\omega^{2}})\big).

(46)

It is interesting to notice that both probabilities,
p_{-}(t)
(
45
)
and
p_{-}^{\mathsf{Rabi}}
(
46
), for fixed
g
and
\omega

attain the same maximal value equal to

p_{-max}=\frac{g^{2}}{g^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

(47)

Below we illustrate the behavior of the probabilities
(
45
)

Asymptotes

In this subsection we discuss asymptotic behavior of quasilinear evolutions using
as a model the explicit global solution (
34
).
Analyzing time dependence of the Bloch vector
\textbf{n}(t)

we obtain the following classes of asymptotes:

(i)
\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{g}\not=0

\textbf{n}(\infty)=\frac{[x^{2}+y^{2}-(g^{2}+\omega^{2})]\boldsymbol{\xi}+2(x+\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\textbf{g}+2(-y+\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{\omega}+2[\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g}+y(\textbf{g}\times\boldsymbol{\xi})+x(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\xi})]}{[x^{2}+y^{2}+(g^{2}+\omega^{2})]-2[(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}-x(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})+y(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})]},

(48)

where

x=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{g^{2}-\omega^{2}+\sqrt{(g^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+(2g\omega\cos\vartheta)^{2}}},\quad y=\tfrac{-\mathrm{sign}\,(g\omega\cos\vartheta)}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\omega^{2}-g^{2}+\sqrt{(g^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+(2g\omega\cos\vartheta)^{2}}},

(49)

so

x^{2}+y^{2}=\sqrt{(g^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+(2g\omega\cos\vartheta)^{2}}

(50)

and
\vartheta
is the angle between
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
g
.

(ii)
\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{g}=0
and
g>\omega

\textbf{n}(\infty)=\frac{-\omega^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}+[\sqrt{g^{2}-\omega^{2}}+(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})]\textbf{g}+(\boldsymbol{\omega\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}})\boldsymbol{\omega}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})+\sqrt{g^{2}-\omega^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\xi})}{g^{2}-(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}+\sqrt{g^{2}-\omega^{2}}(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})}.

(51)

(iii)
\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{g}=0
and
g=\omega

\textbf{n}(\infty)=\frac{2(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})-(g^{2}+\omega^{2})\boldsymbol{\xi}+2(\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\textbf{g}+2(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{\omega}}{(g^{2}+\omega^{2})-2(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\textbf{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

(52)

(iv) In the case
\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{g}=0
and
g<\omega

the evolution is oscillatory and there are no asymptotes.

It is worth to stress that almost all quasilinear evolutions (
34
)
of qubit attain stationary states.
This mean that the operator
G=\tfrac{1}{2}\textbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
damps
oscillations generated by the Hamiltonian

H=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.
The only exception is the case when
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is perpendicular to
g

under condition
\omega>g
.
Notice that this damping does not increase entropy of pure initial states
(
\textbf{n}^{2}=1
is the evolution invariant).
Notice that in the neighborhood of the point given
by the conditions
\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{g}=0
and
\omega=g

the character of the evolution changes drastically. It means that this point
can be identified with the so called point of structural instability

[
1
]
.
This point is especially important in analysis of evolutions with time dependent

\boldsymbol{\omega}
and/or
g
(see Sec.
5
).
A physical meaning of the vectors
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
g
depends on
the physical context (see, e.g., Sec.
5
).

It can be shown that in the case (i) and for
\omega\gg g
the asymptotic state is
very close to the Bloch vector determining one of eigenstates of the Hamiltonian
H
,
i.e., the state of the system approaches to a stationary state.
In particular, for
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
g
parallel or anti-parallel and

\omega>g
the asymptotic state is exactly the stationary state
and it does not depend on the initial state
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

The case with nonzero Lindblad operators

Finally, we will discuss an example with nonzero Lindblad operators.

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+\{G,\rho\}+L\rho L^{\dagger}-\rho\tr[\rho(2G+L^{\dagger}L)].

Now, we consider an example of the Kraus form of the qubit evolution

\displaystyle\dot{n}_{3}(t)

\displaystyle\dot{n}_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=\Big[\Big(i\omega-\tfrac{l^{2}}{2}\Big)-\Big(g-\tfrac{l^{2}}{2}\Big)n_{3}(t)\Big]n_{+}(t),

(56)

where
n_{+}=n_{1}+in_{2}

(57a)

\displaystyle n_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi_{+}(\bar{l}+1)e^{(i\omega-g)t}}{(\bar{l}+\xi_{3})+(1-\xi_{3})e^{-2gt}},

(57b)

where
\bar{l}=\frac{2g+l^{2}}{2g-l^{2}}
.
Notice that

Moreover, for

,

4.4
The Jaynes–Cummings model

In this section we show that the standard Jaynes–Cummings
model
[
24
,
34
]

describing the interaction of a two-level atom with a single mode of
the electromagnetic (EM) field can be also generalized along the lines
discussed in the paper.
The standard Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian describing the system
atom + EM field has the form

H_{JC}=\omega_{f}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\tfrac{\omega_{a}}{2}\sigma_{3}+\tfrac{g}{2}(\hat{a}\sigma_{+}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\sigma_{-}).

(61)

The creation and annihilation operators
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
,
\hat{a}

fulfill the standard bosonic commutation relation.

In order to obtain a nonlinear generalization we replace
H_{JC}

with

H+iG=\big(\omega_{f}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\tfrac{\omega_{a}}{2}\sigma_{3}\big)+i\big(\tfrac{g}{2}(\hat{a}\sigma_{+}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\sigma_{-})\big).

(62)

Let us notice that subspaces

\mathcal{H}^{(n)}=\text{Span}\,\{|n\rangle\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},|n+1\rangle\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\}

(63)

are invariant under the action of both
H
and
iG
.
Therefore,
H+iG
can be written as the following direct sum:

H+iG=\bigoplus_{n}H^{(n)},

(64)

where

H^{(n)}=\omega_{f}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})I+\tfrac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{3}+i\tfrac{g}{2}\sqrt{n+1}\sigma_{1}.

(65)

Furthermore, assuming that the initial full density matrix can be written in
a similar way

\rho(0)=\bigoplus_{n}\lambda_{n}(0)\rho_{(n)}(0),

(66)

where the conditions
\tr[\rho(0)]=1
,
\tr[\rho_{n}(0)]=1
imply that

\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}=1
.
Now, according to Eq. (
22
),
during the evolution governed by
(
64
) the density matrix
(
66
) evolves to

\rho(t)=\frac{e^{(G-iH)t}\rho(0)e^{(G+iH)t}}{\tr[e^{(G-iH)t}\rho(0)e^{(G+iH)t}]}=\bigoplus_{n}\lambda_{n}(t)\rho_{(n)}(t),

(67)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-itH^{(n)}}\rho_{(n)}(0)e^{itH^{(n)\dagger}}}{\tr[e^{-itH^{(n)}}\rho_{(n)}(0)e^{itH^{(n)\dagger}}]},

(68)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}(0)\frac{\tr[e^{-itH^{(n)}}\rho_{(n)}(0)e^{itH^{(n)\dagger}}]}{\tr[e^{(G-iH)t}\rho(0)e^{(G+iH)t}]}.

(69)

Notice that choosing
\lambda_{0}=1
,
\lambda_{i}=0
for
i>0

we obtain the model considered in the subsection
4.1
.
From Eqs. (
67
–
68
) it follows that in each subspace

\mathcal{H}^{(n)}
the time evolution generator has the form (
65
),
i.e.,
the corresponding vectors
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{n}
and
\textbf{g}_{n}
are of the form

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{n}=\omega(0,0,1)
and
\textbf{g}_{n}=g(\sqrt{n+1},0,0)

so
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{n}\cdot\textbf{g}_{n}=0
.
Thus, according to Eqs. (
51
–
52
)
if
g>\omega
the oscillations are damped and
in each subspace states evolve to appropriate stationary states.
However, if
\omega\geq g
then for lower
n
satisfying
\omega\geq g\sqrt{n+1}

the states are oscillating but for
g\sqrt{n+1}>\omega

are evolving to stationary states.

5
Applications

In this section we present two applications of quasi-linear evolutions:
relativistic Dirac qubit in electromagnetic field and evolution of the flavor
state of solar neutrinos propagating through Sun in their way to Earth.
In both cases we utilize the nonlinear differential evolution equation (
24
)
generalizing the von Neumann equation.
In the former case we assume time independence of the evolution generators
so we can use the global solution (
34
).
In the latter case generators must be time(distant)-dependent which causes
more sophisticated and interesting dynamics but solutions are usually achievable
with use of numerical calculations only.

5.1
Evolution of the relativistic qubit carrying by a Dirac
particle in electromagnetic field

Let us consider an evolution of a spin
1/2
quantum charged particle
(e.g. electron) which motion is generated by the electromagnetic tensor

F^{\mu\nu}
.
We assume that state of the particle is determined by
its four-momentum
p^{\mu}
and the spin density matrix heaving in the spin
basis standard form
\rho(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\tfrac{1}{2}(I+\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})
,
where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is the particle polarization vector, so
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{2}\leq 1
.
For a sharp momentum particle it is possible to find this matrix in the manifestly
Lorentz-covariant spinorial basis
[
11
]

\Theta(p,w)=\tfrac{mc}{4p^{0}}(I+\tfrac{p_{\mu}}{mc}\gamma^{\mu})(I-2\gamma^{5}\tfrac{w_{\nu}}{mc}\gamma^{\nu})\gamma^{0}

(70)

and
\tfrac{p^{0}}{mc}\Theta(p,w)\gamma^{0}
is known in the literature

[
6
]
as the covariant spin density matrix for a Dirac particle.
Here
\gamma^{\mu}
are the gamma matrices while
w^{\mu}
is the counterpart
of the Pauli-Lubanski four-vector, called polarization pseudo-vector.
It is defined by the relations

w^{0}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\tfrac{1}{2}\textbf{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}
,

\textbf{w}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\tfrac{1}{2}(mc\boldsymbol{\xi}+\frac{\textbf{p}(\textbf{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})}{p^{0}+mc})

[
8
]
. Notice, that
\boldsymbol{\xi}
can be expressed by
p
and
w
:

\boldsymbol{\xi}=\frac{2}{mc}\Big(\textbf{w}-w^{0}\frac{\textbf{p}}{p^{0}+mc}\Big).

(71)

The four-vectors
p_{\mu}
and
w^{\mu}
are Minkowski-orthogonal, i.e.,

p_{\mu}w^{\mu}=0
. Moreover

p_{\mu}p^{\mu}=(mc)^{2}
,
w_{\mu}w^{\mu}=-(\tfrac{mc}{2})^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{2}
.
Under the bi-spinor representation of the Lorentz group
\Theta

transforms according to

\Theta^{\prime}=\frac{S\Theta S^{\dagger}}{\tr(S\Theta S^{\dagger})}

(72)

with

S=\exp(i\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu})
and

J^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac{i}{4}[\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}]
.
In the following we will use the Weyl representation of the gamma matrices
(see Appendix
B
). It is easy to prove that
\Theta
is Hermitian,
non-negative definite and
\tr\Theta=1
as well as it is related to the matrix

\rho(\boldsymbol{\xi})
by the formulas

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\overline{v(p)}\Theta(p,w)v(p),

(73)

\displaystyle\Theta(p,w)

\displaystyle=v(p)\rho(\boldsymbol{\xi})\overline{v(p)},

(74)

where
v(p)
satisfies the Dirac equation and intertwines spinorial and spin bases;
its explicit form in the Weyl representation is given in Appendix
B
.

Now, we define the evolution of
\Theta
, satisfying the nonlinear von Neumann equation
(
24
), by choosing its generator as follows

H+iG=\frac{\mu_{B}}{\hbar}F_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}=i\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}&0\\
0&-\textbf{F}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\end{pmatrix},

(75)

where
\mu_{B}=\tfrac{e\hbar}{2m}
is the Bohr magneton for a particle with
charge
e
. The complex vector

\textbf{F}=\textbf{E}+ic\textbf{B}

(76)

is known as the Riemann-Silberstein vector related to a self-dual tensor
while
E
,
B
are the electric and magnetic fields, respectively.
Note that

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{B}}{\hbar}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}&0\\
0&\textbf{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\end{pmatrix},

(77)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}&0\\
0&-\textbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\end{pmatrix},

(78)

so
H
coincides with the Hamiltonian (divided by
\hbar
)
twisting the spin and in the case of a constant
B
.

Assuming that the electromagnetic field is time independent we obtain the
result of integration of the von Neumann equation in the form (
22
).
More specifically, the evolution operator takes the form

K(\tau)=e^{-i\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{\hbar}F_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}&0\\
0&e^{-\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\end{pmatrix},

(79)

where we choose the proper time
\tau
as the dynamical parameter to guarantee
the Lorentz covariance of our formalism.
Taking into account that
\gamma^{0}K^{\dagger}(\tau)=K^{-1}(\tau)\gamma^{0}
,
and choosing the rest frame values of the four-momentum and the polarization
pseudo-vector as the initial conditions, i.e.,

p(0)=mc(1;0,0,0)
so
w(0)=mc(0;\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})
, we obtain that

\Theta(\tau)=\frac{K(\tau)\Theta(p(0),w(0))K^{\dagger}(\tau)}{\tr[K(\tau)\Theta(p(0),w(0))K^{\dagger}(\tau)]},

(80)

and

\rho(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau))=\frac{e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{\tr\big[e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\big]},

(81)

while
p(\tau)
as well as
w(\tau)
can be calculated by means of the following
equations

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}p^{\mu}(\tau)

\displaystyle=e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\sigma_{\mu}q^{\mu}e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}},

(82)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}w^{\mu}(\tau)

\displaystyle=e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\sigma_{\mu}\vartheta^{\mu}e^{\tau\frac{\mu_{B}}{c\hbar}\textbf{F}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}.

(83)

The solution of (
81
) is given in (
34
) under appropriate
identification of
F
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
with
\boldsymbol{\omega}
,
g
, and

n
.
On the other hand, from (
79
) with use of the explicit form
of the gamma matrices in the Weyl form (Appendix
B
) we see
that Eqs. (
82
-
83
)
imply the following differential equations for the four-momentum
p(\tau)
and
the polarization four-vector
w(\tau)
:

\displaystyle\frac{dp^{\mu}}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{m}F^{\mu}_{\phantom{\mu}\nu}p^{\nu},

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{dw^{\mu}}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{m}F^{\mu}_{\phantom{\mu}\nu}w^{\nu},

(85)

Equation (
84

5.2
Evolution of the flavor quantum state of solar neutrino

As is well known, neutrino oscillates in the leptonic flavor space
(space of fractional leptonic charges), i.e., it evolves between electric
muonic and taonic neutrino flavors.
However, neutrinos produced in the core of Sun undergo interaction
with the dense solar electron plasma and, as consequence, their flavor oscillations
are modified when neutrinos propagate through the Sun in their way to detectors

based on the Wolfenstein equation and form of the solar electron density function

6
Discussion and conclusions

In this paper we have discussed a generalization of the notion
of the quantum operations and the quantum time evolution.
The generalization depends on extending the linearity of quantum operations
to the quasi-linearity condition.
This condition is motivated by the appearance of such operations in a
“hidden” form (e.g. selective measurement
[
26
]
)
in quantum formalism.
On the other hand, convex quasi-linearity guarantees absence of
superluminal communication.
We have identified a natural class of operations satisfying this condition.
Moreover, we have generalized the GKSL master equation for
quasi-linear evolutions.
As an example we have considered nonlinear qubit evolution.
It is worth to stress
that some of these qubit evolutions were discussed
independently in the different
context of non-Hermitian quantum mechanics

[
10
,
32
,
21
]
.
We also discussed an appropriate modification of the Jaynes–Cummings model

[
24
,
34
]
describing the interaction
of a two-level atom with a single mode of the electromagnetic field.
In general, the nonlinear time development of qubit is related to an interaction
of the quantum system with an environment.
Depending on the interrelation with environment the evolution
of qubit can demonstrate different time dependence
but mostly it can be interpreted as a damping of oscillations
together with gain and loss of energy (Sec.
4.2
).
This is especially evident in presented physical applications of the introduced formalism:
evolution of spin of a relativistic Dirac particle in external electromagnetic field
and evolution of the flavor state of solar neutrinos propagating through the Sun.
In both cases we observe damping of oscillations of the state and its asymptotic
evolution to a stationary state. Moreover, the later case offers a new physical
interpretation of the process of transmutation of neutrinos belonging to different
flavor generations inside Sun.

To avoid misinterpretations of the introduced formalism we stress that
the generator
G
in the master equation (
19
) describes,
similarly as the Lindblad generators, influence of an environment on
a quantum system governed on the free level by the Hamiltonian
H
.
This means that the
H+iG
appearing in global solutions
(
23
), (
30
) of
the nonlinear von Neumann equation (
24
) or
in the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
27
) is
the evolution generator, however, in general, it cannot be interpreted as a non-Hermitian

PT
-symmetric Hamiltonian. Notice, that the spectrum of
H+iG

is in general complex while
PT
-symmetric Hamiltonians have real spectra,
i.e.,
PT
-symmetric Hamiltonians form a subset of the considered
set of generators
H+iG

while the corresponding trace-preserving,
PT
-symmetric evolutions belong to
the introduced by us class of quasi-linear, so admissible, evolutions.
This fact supports their physical acceptance because it shows that they do not lead to
arbitrary fast communication.

Let us stress here that our goal was to introduce nonlinear evolution
with minimal changes in the rest of quantum formalism.
In our approach we do not change Born rule or projection postulate
as it takes place in other nonlinear extensions of quantum mechanics
(
[
14
,
22
]
).
But of course
nonlinearity of evolution equations has implications—the superposition
principle is broken during the nonlinear evolution.

Appendix A
Derivation of Eq. (
19
)

To derive the infinitesimal form of the global time evolution (
13
) we proceed as follows:
First, we put

\Phi_{t+\delta t}(\rho_{0})\approx\Phi_{t}(\rho(0))+\delta t\dot{\Phi}_{t}(\rho_{0})=\rho(t)+\delta t\dot{\rho}(t),

(91)

where
\rho(t)=\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0})
.
On the other hand, using the composition law of the map
\Phi_{t}

we obtain

\Phi_{t+\delta t}(\rho_{0})=\Phi_{\delta t}\big(\Phi_{t}(\rho_{0})\big)=\Phi_{\delta t}(\rho(t)).

(92)

Next, with the help of the global form (
13
)
we get

\Phi_{\delta t}(\rho(t))=\frac{\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\alpha_{max}}K_{\alpha}(\delta t)\rho(t)K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\delta t)}{\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\alpha_{max}}\tr\big(K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\delta t)K_{\alpha}(\delta t)\rho(t)\big)}.

(93)

The initial condition implies

K_{0}(0)=I,\quad K_{\alpha}(0)=0\,\,\text{for}\,\,\alpha\geq 1.

(94)

Next, we expect that in the expansion of the sum

\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\alpha_{max}}K_{\alpha}(\delta t)\rho(t)K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\delta t)

the first correction is of the order
\delta t
. Therefore, we put

K_{0}(\delta t)=I+\delta t\kappa,\quad K_{\alpha}(\delta t)=\sqrt{\delta t}L_{\alpha}\,\,\text{for}\,\,\alpha\geq 1,

(95)

where
\kappa
and
L_{\alpha}
are linear operators.
Now, the nominator of Eq. (
93
) takes the form

\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\alpha_{max}}K_{\alpha}(\delta t)\rho(t)K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\delta t)=\rho(t)+\delta t\big(\kappa\rho(t)+\rho(t)\kappa^{\dagger}\big)\\
+\delta t\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\alpha_{max}}L_{\alpha}\rho(t)L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}.

(96)

Using this equation we obtain

\tr\Big(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\alpha_{max}}K_{\alpha}(\delta t)\rho(t)K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\delta t)\Big)=\\
1+\delta t\tr\Big[\rho(t)\Big(\kappa+\kappa^{\dagger}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\alpha_{max}}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha}\Big)\Big].

(97)

Next, with the help of the expansion
(1+\delta x)^{-1}\approx 1-\delta x
,
the inverse of the denominator of Eq. (
93
) takes the form

1-\delta t\tr\Big[\rho(t)\Big(\kappa+\kappa^{\dagger}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\alpha_{max}}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha}\Big)\Big].

(98)

Now, multiplying the right hand side of
Eq. (
96
) with
Eq. (
98
), leaving the terms of order
up to
\delta t
, inserting
\kappa=G-iH
,
and equating the result with (
91
)
we obtain equation (
19
).
