Current-Conserving Relativistic Linear Response for Collisional Plasmas
Martin Formanek, Christopher Grayson, Johann Rafelski, Berndt Müller

Abstract
We investigate the response of a relativistic plasma to electromagnetic fields in the framework of the Boltzmann equation incorporating a collision term in the relaxation rate approximation selected in a form assuring current conservation. We obtain an explicit solution for the linearized perturbation of the Fermi-Dirac equilibrium distribution in terms of the average relaxation rate κ \kappa . We study the resulting covariant, gauge invariant, and current conserving form of the polarization tensor in the ultrarelativistic and non-relativistic limits. We evaluate the susceptibility in the ultrarelativistic limit and explore their dependence on κ \kappa . Finally, we study the dispersion relations for the longitudinal and transverse poles of the propagator. We show that for κ > 2 ​ ω p \kappa>2\omega_{p} , where ω p \omega_{p} is the plasma frequency, the plasma wave modes are overdamped. In the opposite case, κ ≪ ω p \kappa\ll\omega_{p} , the propagating plasma modes are weakly damped.



I
Introduction

The response of relativistic plasmas to applied electromagnetic fields is of interest in many fields of physics
NAS_HED:2003
;
DOE-BRN:2009
, including astrophysics, intense laser interactions with matter, and relativistic heavy ion collisions. We develop and present a novel method of obtaining and studying exact solutions for the covariant polarization tensor in linear response limit. We incorporate the nonperturbative effect of collisional interaction in terms of relativistically covariant and invariant generalization of the average relaxation rate method. Our linear response polarization tensor satisfies current conservation requirements and is gauge invariant. In a schematic exploration of the effect of the strength of collisional interaction we demonstrate the physical relevance of these novel theoretical developments.

Several methods have been introduced to study the linear response of a collisionless ultrarelativistic plasma following the seminal work by Weldon
Weldon:1982aq
. Of particular importance to our effort are approaches using semiclassical transport theory based on the Boltzmann equation
Mrowczynski:1987jr
;
Mrowczynski:1989np
;
Blaizot:1993zk
;
Kelly:1994ig
;
Kelly:1994dh
. However, applications of this formalism have focused on dilute plasmas where collisions can be neglected
Blaizot:2001nr
.

The effects of the collision term were mainly studied for the purpose of deriving transport coefficients, such as the electrical conductivity, of interest is study of plasma response to long-wavelength perturbations
Mrowczynski:1988xu
;
Heiselberg:1993cr
;
Ahonen:1996nq
;
Baym:1997gq
;
Ahonen:1998iz
. Transport coefficients have also been calculated in quantum field theory using effective propagators that resum thermal modifications in order to avoid infrared divergences
Heiselberg:1994ms
;
Arnold:2002zm
;
Arnold:2003zc
.

In prior explorations of the plasma interaction the linear response of the plasma to an applied field is obtained while also linearizing the collision term and studying the relaxation back to the equilibrium distribution. This can be done in the schematic relaxation time approximation where an average relaxation time
\tau
is introduced
Mrowczynski:1988xu
;
Satow:2014lia
or by calculating the momentum dependent relaxation rate
\kappa(p)
with the input of perturbative matrix elements
Ahonen:1996nq
. Here, we use the average relaxation time approximation with momentum averaged
\kappa
to make all calculations analytically tractable.

The linearized relativistic collision term proposed by Anderson and Witting
Anderson:1974
takes the form

C[f]=(p^{\mu}u_{\mu})\kappa[f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)-f(x,p)]\,,

(1)

where
\kappa=1/\tau
is the average relaxation rate,
f(x,p)
is the phase space distribution of charged particles in the plasma,
f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)
is their equilibrium distribution and
u_{\mu}
is the four-velocity of the plasma rest frame. However, the interaction in equation  (
1
) violates current conservation. Of course, the full collision term respects all conservation laws; thus, this violation is an artifact of the linear approximation. In order to cure this deficiency, Bhatnagar, Gross and Krook (BGK) introduced a modification of the linearized collision term for nonrelativistic plasmas
Bhatnagar:1954zz
. In this work we generalize the BGK modification of the linearized collision term to relativistic plasmas using the Anderson-Witting form Eq. (
1
) and show that the resulting linear response functions satisfy current conservation and gauge invariance constraints.

Collective oscillations in an ultra-relativistic plasma have been explored previously in linear response formulation including binary collisions with the BGK correction in two earlier publications
Carrington:2003je
;
Schenke:2006xu
. While Carrington
et al.

Carrington:2003je
studied only collective modes in a massless equilibrium plasma, Schenke
et al.

Schenke:2006xu
also calculated the collective plasma response in the presence of non-equilibrium particle distributions as they arise in a rapidly expanding plasma. Both of these works were focused on the internal dynamics of the plasma, for which its quasiparticle modes are of primary interest. Our work is focused on the response of a relativistic plasma to time- and space-dependent
external
electromagnetic fields.

In line with this motivation, we explore the frequency and wavelength dependence of the plasma response away from its eigenmodes, in particular, the electric and magnetic susceptibilities and electric conductivity. Also, in contrast to these previous treatments which used the plasma rest frame as their starting point, our formulation is manifestly covariant and independent of the observer’s reference frame. Besides clarifying certain aspects, such as the relativistic invariance of the collision term, our treatment may be more convenient as starting point for further generalizations.

We note that the relativistic Anderson-Witting form of the scattering term with BGK modification, which ensures current conservation, does not in general assure that collisions preserve energy and momentum. In a recent development Rocha, Denicol, and Noronha
Rocha:2021zcw
aimed to ensure current and four-momentum conservation in the calculation of transport coefficients for a relativistic plasma using the Chapman-Enskog method.

In our derivation of the polarization tensor we follow the ideas and methods of Blaizot and Iancu
Blaizot:2001nr
for the collisionless relativistic plasma. We avoid some of the subtleties of the Blaizot-Iancu approach by computing the response functions directly in momentum space, where the equations are of algebraic rather than integro-differential type. This allows us to treat the current-conserving modification of the collision term exactly. We also show how the response functions for a collisionless plasma are recovered in the limit
\kappa\to 0
. Finally, we calculate the plasma normal modes including their damping rates.

For the sake of simplicity, our treatment here focuses on the relativistic electron-positron plasma, but it can be easily generalized to other relativistic plasmas, such as a weakly coupled quark-gluon plasma where it applies to the plasma response to both, electromagnetic fields and color fields. Our treatment can be also easily generalized to a momentum dependent relaxation rate
\kappa(p)
at the expense of requiring numerical evaluation of the momentum integrals.

II
Covariant kinetic theory

The covariant Boltzmann equation reads

(p\cdot\partial)f(x,p)+qF^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu}\frac{\partial f(x,p)}{\partial p^{\mu}}\\
=(p\cdot u)\kappa\left[f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\frac{n(x)}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}-f(x,p)\right]\,,

(2)

where on the right hand side we included a collision term adopted from
Bhatnagar:1954zz
. The meaning of the 4D scalar product dot notation is given by

p\cdot u\equiv p^{\mu}u_{\mu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}u^{\nu}

(3)

with the flat space-time metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}
with signature (+,-,-,-). The fluctuating and equilibrium densities are defined covariantly as

\displaystyle n(x)

\displaystyle\equiv 2\int(dp)(p\cdot u)f(x,p)\,,

(4)

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle\equiv 2\int(dp)(p\cdot u)f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\,.

(5)

The factor two accounts for the spin degrees of freedom. The covariant integration measure is defined as

(dp)\equiv\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}4\pi\delta_{+}(p^{2}-m^{2})=\left.\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}p^{0}}\right|_{p^{0}=\sqrt{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}+m^{2}}}\,,

(6)

where we consider only the positive energy particles. The covariant Fermi-Dirac distribution function reads
Becattini:2013fla
:

f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\equiv\frac{1}{\exp(p\cdot u/T)+1}\,.

(7)

where
u^{\mu}
is the 4-velocity of medium with respect to which the invariant
p\cdot u
is measured.
T
denotes the temperature in the medium rest frame.

The reason why we are implementing the collision term in the form (
2
) is that this formulation explicitly conserves the 4-current

j^{\mu}=2q\int(dp)p^{\mu}f(x,p)\,,

(8)

where the factor of two again accounts for the spin degrees of freedom. We can prove this statement by applying
\partial_{\mu}
on this expression and substituting back from the Boltzmann equation (
2
)

\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}=2q\int(dp)\left\{-qF^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu}\frac{\partial f(x,p)}{\partial p^{\mu}}\right.\\
\left.+(p\cdot u)\kappa\left[f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\frac{n(x)}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}-f(x,p)\right]\right\}\,.

(9)

The first term should naturally vanish because the collisionless Vlasov equation preserves 4-current. This can be seen upon integration by parts and use of the antisymmetry of
F^{\mu\nu}
. On the other hand, the collision term vanishes by design - see definitions (
4
,
5
).

We are writing the solution for the distribution function as

f(x,p)=f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)+\delta f(x,p)\,,

(10)

where the perturbation from the equilibrium comes because of the inclusion of the external EM field. To first order in the fields we can write

(p\cdot\partial)\delta f(x,p)+qF^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu}\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{\partial p^{\mu}}\\
=(p\cdot u)\kappa\left[\frac{f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\delta n(x)-\delta f(x,p)\right]\,,

(11)

where the quantity
\delta n(x)
is defined following the definitions (
4
,
5
) as

\delta n(x)\equiv 2\int(dp)(p\cdot u)\delta f(x,p)\,.

(12)

An explicit solution of the Boltzmann equation can be obtained more easily in momentum space after a Fourier transformation. We define the Fourier transform
\widetilde{g}(k^{\mu})
of a general function
g(x^{\mu})
of space-time coordinates as

\widetilde{g}(k)\equiv\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int d^{4}xe^{ik\cdot x}g(x)\,.

(13)

The Fourier transformation replaces partial derivatives
\partial_{\mu}
with the 4-momentum
k_{\mu}
:

\partial_{\mu}\rightarrow-ik_{\mu}\,,

(14)

and the Fourier transformed Boltzmann equation reads

-i(p\cdot k)\widetilde{\delta f}(k,p)+q\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu}\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{\partial p^{\mu}}\\
=(p\cdot u)\kappa\left[\frac{f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\widetilde{\delta n}(k)-\widetilde{\delta f}(k,p)\right]\,.

(15)

In the following we simplify the notation of derivatives of the equilibrium function with respect to momentum as

\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{\partial p^{\mu}}=\frac{df_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{d(p\cdot u)}u_{\mu}\equiv f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)u_{\mu}\,.

(16)

Solving (
15
) for
\widetilde{\delta f}(k,p)
we have

\widetilde{\delta f}(k,p)=\frac{i}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\bigg[-q(u\cdot\widetilde{F}\cdot p)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\\
\left.+(p\cdot u)\kappa\frac{f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\widetilde{\delta n}(k)\right]\,,

(17)

which can be readily integrated over
2(dp)(p\cdot u)
to obtain an equation for
\widetilde{\delta n}(k)

\widetilde{\delta n}(k)=R(k)-Q(k)\widetilde{\delta n}(k)\,,

(18)

where the integrals are defined

\displaystyle R(k)\equiv-2i\int(dp)(p\cdot u)\frac{q(u\cdot\widetilde{F}\cdot p)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\,,

(19)

\displaystyle Q(k)\equiv-2i\frac{\kappa}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\int(dp)(p\cdot u)^{2}\frac{f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\,.

(20)

This way the solution for
\widetilde{\delta n}(k)
in terms of the external fields is simply

\widetilde{\delta n}(k)=\frac{R(k)}{1+Q(k)}\,.

(21)

We can substitute this result back into (
17
) to obtain an explicit expression for
\widetilde{\delta f}(k,p)

\widetilde{\delta f}(k,p)=\frac{i}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\bigg[-q(u\cdot\widetilde{F}\cdot p)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\\
\left.+(p\cdot u)\kappa\frac{f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\frac{R(k)}{1+Q(k)}\right]\,,

(22)

where the right hand side contains only known quantities.

II.1
Induced current

If we subtract the antiparticle distribution
\widetilde{f}_{-}
from the particle distribution
\widetilde{f}_{+}
obtain the total current in the momentum space

\widetilde{j}^{\mu}(k)=2q\int(dp)p^{\mu}[\widetilde{f}_{+}(k,p)-\widetilde{f}_{-}(k,p)]\\
=4q\int(dp)p^{\mu}\widetilde{\delta f}(k,p)\,.

(23)

Whereas the equilibrium contributions cancel because of the opposite sign of the charges of particles and antiparticles, the perturbations add up due the change in sign of the external force
qF^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu}
.
Thus our final expression for the induced current is

\boxed{\widetilde{j}^{\mu}(k)=R^{\mu}(k)-\frac{R(k)}{1+Q(k)}Q^{\mu}(k)}

(24)

where the integrals
R^{\mu}(k)
and
Q^{\mu}(k)
are defined analogously to (
19
,
20
) as

\displaystyle R^{\mu}(k)\equiv-4q^{2}i\int(dp)p^{\mu}\frac{(u\cdot\widetilde{F}\cdot p)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}(k)\equiv-4qi\frac{\kappa}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\int(dp)(p\cdot u)p^{\mu}\frac{f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\,.

(26)

Note that we absorbed the factor
4q
from the current (
24
) into the definition of these integrals.

II.2
Current conservation

We can show that the result (
24
) satisfies the continuity equation. In the momentum space this expression has to be orthogonal to
k_{\mu}
. The first term

k_{\mu}R^{\mu}=-4q^{2}i\int(dp)[k\cdot p+i(p\cdot u)\kappa-i(p\cdot u)\kappa)]\\
\times\frac{(u\cdot\widetilde{F}\cdot p)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}=-i2q\kappa R\,,

(27)

where we added and subtracted
i(p\cdot u)\kappa
in the numerator. The integral for which the denominator cancels vanishes. On the other hand

k_{\mu}Q^{\mu}=-4qi\frac{\kappa}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\int(dp)[k\cdot p+i(p\cdot u)\kappa-i(p\cdot u)\kappa)]\\
\times\frac{(p\cdot u)f_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}=-2qi\kappa(1+Q)\,,

(28)

because the part where the denominator cancels integrates to
n_{\mathrm{eq}}
by definition (
5
). Substituting all into our result (
24
) we have

k\cdot\widetilde{j}(k)=-i2q\kappa R+\frac{R2qi\kappa}{1+Q}(1+Q)=0\,,

(29)

as expected.

II.3
Covariant polarization tensor

We can compare our result (
24
) with the covariant formulation of the Ohm’s law
Starke:2014tfa
. In the momentum space it reads

\widetilde{j}^{\mu}(k)=\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)\widetilde{A}^{\nu}(k)\,.

(30)

The Fourier transform of the electromagnetic tensor in terms of the 4-vector potential in momentum space
\widetilde{A}^{\mu}
is

\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}(k)=-ik^{\mu}\widetilde{A}^{\nu}(k)+ik^{\nu}\widetilde{A}^{\mu}(k)\,,

(31)

which we can substitute to the definition of
R^{\mu}(k)
(
25
) to obtain

R^{\mu}(k)=-4q^{2}\int(dp)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\\
\times\frac{(u\cdot k)p^{\mu}p_{\nu}-(k\cdot p)p^{\mu}u_{\nu}}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\widetilde{A}^{\nu}(k)\,,

(32)

from which we see that the contribution of
R^{\mu}
to the polarization tensor is

R^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)\equiv-4q^{2}\int(dp)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\\
\times\frac{(u\cdot k)p^{\mu}p_{\nu}-(k\cdot p)p^{\mu}u_{\nu}}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}.

(33)

Similarly we can address the contribution of the second term which is hidden in the
R(k)
scalar. In terms of the 4-vector potential in the momentum space
\widetilde{A}^{\nu}
we have

R(k)=-2q\int(dp)(p\cdot u)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\\
\times\frac{(u\cdot k)p_{\nu}-(k\cdot p)u_{\nu}}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\widetilde{A}^{\nu}(k)\,.

(34)

We can identify in this expression a 4-vector
H_{\nu}(k)
defined as

H_{\nu}(k)\equiv-2q\int(dp)(p\cdot u)f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(p)\\
\times\frac{(u\cdot k)p_{\nu}-(k\cdot p)u_{\nu}}{p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}

(35)

so that the polarization tensor is given by

\boxed{\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)=R^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)-\frac{Q^{\mu}(k)H_{\nu}(k)}{1+Q(k)},}

(36)

where the covariant quantities
R^{\mu}_{\nu}
,
Q^{\mu}
,
H_{\nu}
, and
Q
are given by the integrals (
33
,
26
,
35
,
20
) respectively.

We briefly mention the properties of the polarization tensor. The induced charges have to satisfy the continuity equation; in momentum space this implies

k_{\mu}\widetilde{j}^{\mu}=0=k_{\mu}\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}\widetilde{A}^{\nu}\,.

(37)

Since the current also has to be gauge invariant

\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{j}^{\prime\mu}_{\text{ind}}=\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}\widetilde{A}^{\prime\nu}=\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}(\widetilde{A}^{\nu}-ik^{\nu}\widetilde{\chi}),\\
\widetilde{j}^{\mu}_{\text{ind}}=\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}\widetilde{A}^{\nu}\end{array}\right\}\ \Rightarrow\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}k^{\nu}=0\,,

(38)

where
\widetilde{\chi}(k)
is a Fourier transform of an arbitrary gauge function. This implies that if we multiply the polarization tensor from the right with
k^{\nu}
it should vanish. This property is apparent in the covariant form of
H_{\nu}(k)
(
35
) and
R^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)
(
33
).

III
Polarization tensor

In order to evaluate the integrals
Q^{\mu}
,
H_{\nu}
,
Q
and
R^{\mu}_{\nu}
we choose to orient the vector
\boldsymbol{k}
along the
z
-axis

k^{\mu}=(\omega,0,0,k)\,.

(39)

With this choice the gauge condition imposed on the polarization tensor (
38
) becomes

\Pi^{0}_{z}=-\frac{\omega}{k}\Pi^{0}_{0},\quad\Pi^{z}_{z}=-\frac{\omega}{k}\Pi^{z}_{0}\,.

(40)

We choose to perform the calculations in the rest frame of the medium
u^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
and in two limits where, either the mass can be neglected (ultrarelativistic limit), or where the plasma particle move slowly compared with the speed of light (nonrelativistic limit).

III.1
Ultrarelativistic limit

In this limit we neglect the mass of the plasma particles as compared to their momentum. With this assumption the non-zero components of the polarization tensor are (see Appendix
A
for details):

\displaystyle\Pi^{0}_{0}

\displaystyle=-m_{D}^{2}L\left(1+\frac{i\kappa\Lambda}{2k-i\kappa\Lambda}\right)\,,

(41)

\displaystyle\Pi^{0}_{z}

\displaystyle=m_{D}^{2}\frac{\omega}{k}L\left(1+\frac{i\kappa\Lambda}{2k-i\kappa\Lambda}\right)\,,

(42)

\displaystyle\Pi^{z}_{0}

\displaystyle=-m_{D}^{2}\frac{\omega}{k}L\left(1+\frac{i\kappa\Lambda}{2k-i\kappa\Lambda}\right)\,,

(43)

\displaystyle\Pi^{z}_{z}

\displaystyle=m_{D}^{2}\frac{\omega^{2}}{k^{2}}L\left(1+\frac{i\kappa\Lambda}{2k-i\kappa\Lambda}\right)\,,

(44)

\displaystyle\Pi^{x}_{x}

\displaystyle=\Pi^{y}_{y}=\frac{m_{D}^{2}\omega}{4k}\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime 2}}{k^{2}}\Lambda-\Lambda-\frac{2\omega^{\prime}}{k}\right)\,.

(45)

The quantities
\omega^{\prime}
,
\Lambda
, and
L
are defined as

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\equiv\omega+i\kappa,\quad\Lambda\equiv\ln\frac{\omega^{\prime}+k}{\omega^{\prime}-k},\quad L\equiv 1-\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{2k}\Lambda\,,

(46)

and the Debye screening mass
m_{D}^{2}
is given by integral (
85
):

m_{D}^{2}\equiv\frac{q^{2}T^{3}}{3}\,.

(47)

III.2
Nonrelativistic limit

In this limit we assume that the velocity of the plasma particles is much smaller than the speed of llight, i. e., we work in the low temperature limit
T\ll m
. We also assume that
|\boldsymbol{p}|k/m\omega^{\prime}\ll 1
in order to be able to perform the expansion in the momentum integrals. We refer to Appendix
B
for the derivation of the results. The non-zero components of the polarization tensor are:

\displaystyle\Pi^{0}_{0}

\displaystyle=m_{L}^{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\omega^{\prime 2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{i\kappa}{\omega^{\prime}}\left(1+\frac{Tk^{2}}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\right)}\,,

(48)

\displaystyle\Pi^{0}_{z}

\displaystyle=-m_{L}^{2}\frac{\omega k}{\omega^{\prime 2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{i\kappa}{\omega^{\prime}}\left(1+\frac{Tk^{2}}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\right)}\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\Pi^{z}_{0}

\displaystyle=m_{L}^{2}\frac{\omega k}{\omega^{\prime 2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{i\kappa}{\omega^{\prime}}\left(1+\frac{Tk^{2}}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\right)}\,,

(50)

\displaystyle\Pi^{z}_{z}

\displaystyle=-m_{L}^{2}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega^{\prime 2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{i\kappa}{\omega^{\prime}}\left(1+\frac{Tk^{2}}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\right)}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\Pi^{x}_{x}

\displaystyle=\Pi^{y}_{y}=-m_{L}^{2}\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\,.

(52)

Here we have introduced the mass scale
m_{L}^{2}
through the integral (
127
)

m_{L}^{2}\equiv q^{2}\left(\frac{2mT}{\pi}\right)^{3/2}\frac{e^{-m/T}}{2m}\,.

(53)

We shall see in Section
IV.3
below that
m_{L}
is the nonrelativistic plasma frequency.

III.3
Current conservation in components

The current conservation relation (
37
) reads in explicit component notation:

0=k_{0}\Pi^{0}_{0}\widetilde{A}^{0}+k_{0}\Pi^{0}_{z}\widetilde{A}^{z}+k_{z}\Pi^{z}_{0}\widetilde{A}^{0}+k_{z}\Pi^{z}_{z}\widetilde{A}^{z}\,.

(54)

To do show that the right-hand side vanishes as required, we invoke the Lorentz gauge condition
k\cdot\widetilde{A}=0
which allows us to express
\widetilde{A}^{z}
in terms of
\widetilde{A}^{z}
:

\widetilde{A}^{z}=\frac{\omega}{k}\widetilde{A}^{0}\,.

(55)

The current conservation condition now becomes a constraint on the polarization tensor alone:

0=\Pi^{0}_{0}+\frac{\omega}{k}\Pi^{0}_{z}-\frac{k}{\omega}\Pi^{z}_{0}-\Pi^{z}_{z}\,.

(56)

If we invoke the gauge invariance constraint (
40
) we can simplify

0=\left(1-\frac{\omega^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)\left(\Pi^{0}_{0}-\Pi^{z}_{0}\frac{k}{\omega}\right)\,.

(57)

The current conservation condition is now reduced to the constraint

\Pi^{z}_{0}=\frac{\omega}{k}\Pi^{0}_{0}\,,

(58)

which is obviously satisfied in both limits. In conclusion, the polarization tensor
\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}
conserves the electric current and ensures its gauge invariance. With both covariant indices
\Pi_{\mu\nu}
is even symmetric.

IV
Collective Plasma Behavior

The equivalent dielectric and susceptibility tensors, through which one can study collective plasma effects, are defined through the spatial portion of the polarization tensor
\Pi^{i}_{j}
as in
Starke:2014tfa
;
Melrose:2008
,

K^{i}_{j}(\omega,k)=\varepsilon^{i}_{j}/\varepsilon_{0}=1+\frac{\Pi^{i}_{j}(\omega,k)}{\omega^{2}}=1+\chi^{i}_{j}(\omega,k)

(59)

For an isotropic plasma the spatial part can be written in terms of two independent response functions, one (
\Pi_{T}
) transverse to
\mathbf{k}
, the other (
\Pi_{L}
) parallel to
\mathbf{k}
. These are immediately recognizable in the spatial components of
\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}
, evaluated in the rest frame, given that
\mathbf{k}
was chosen to be in the
\hat{z}
direction
Melrose:2008
. The longitudinal and transverse response functions are given by

\Pi_{L}=\Pi^{z}_{z}=-\frac{\omega^{2}}{k^{2}}\Pi^{0}_{0},\quad\Pi_{T}=\Pi^{x}_{x}=\Pi^{y}_{y}\,.

(60)

Both prescriptions for
\Pi_{L}
are equivalent because of current conservation. They arise from solving the Maxwell equations in medium for either
A_{0}
or
A_{\parallel}
. Reference frame independent definitions of
\Pi_{L}
and
\Pi_{T}
can be given with the help of manifestly Lorentz invariant projection operators
Melrose:2008
.

IV.3
Dispersion Relations

The propagator of electromagnetic perturbations of the plasma is obtained by inverting Maxwell’s equations including the induced current:

-ik_{\mu}\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}=\mu_{0}(\widetilde{j}_{\mathrm{ind}}^{\nu}+\widetilde{j}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\nu})

(71)

Including the induced current on the left-hand side of the equation and writing the expression in-terms of
A^{\mu}
one finds,

(k^{2}g^{\mu\nu}-k^{\mu}k^{\nu}+\mu_{0}\Pi^{\mu\nu})\widetilde{A}_{\nu}=-\mu_{0}\widetilde{j}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\nu}\,.

(72)

The propagator
D^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)
is obtained by inverting the equation:

\widetilde{A}_{\nu}(k)=-D^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)\,\widetilde{j}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\nu}(k)\,.

(73)

For an isotropic medium the poles in the propagator can be expressed in terms of
\Pi_{L}
and
\Pi_{T}
; the result is
Melrose:2008
:

\left[(k\cdot u)^{2}+\mu_{0}\Pi_{L}(\omega,k)\right]\left[k^{2}+\mu_{0}\Pi_{T}(\omega,k)\right]^{2}=0\,,

(74)

where again
u
is the 4-velocity of the medium rest frame. Note that the transverse mode has duplicate solutions as it describes modes in a plane perpendicular to
k
.

In the limit of
k\to 0
both the transverse and longitudinal roots of the dispersion relation reduce to the frequency of plasma oscillations (Figure
9
):

\omega_{\pm}=-\frac{i\kappa}{2}\pm\sqrt{\omega_{p}^{2}-\frac{\kappa}{4}^{2}}\,,

(75)

where the plasma frequency
\omega_{p}
is explicitly given in the ultrarelativistic and non-relativistic limits, respectively, by:

\omega_{p}^{2}=\frac{1}{3}m_{D}^{2}\quad(\mathrm{UR})\,,\qquad\omega_{p}^{2}=m_{L}^{2}\quad(\mathrm{NR})\,.

(76)

This expression gives a physical meaning to the mass
m_{L}
introduced in (
127
).

For oscillatory waves of the form
E=E_{0}e^{-i\omega t}
, in the case of
\kappa\ll\omega_{p}
the waves are weakly damped. For
\kappa>2\omega_{p}
, the square root is imaginary and the wave becomes overdamped. For
\kappa\gg\omega_{p}
the weakly damped solution has a long lifetime
\tau\approx\kappa/\omega_{p}^{2}
. The plasma frequency is plotted below as a function of
\kappa
.

Figure 9:
Real and imaginary part of the plasma frequencies
\omega_{\pm}
as a function of
\kappa
.
The region
\kappa>2\omega_{p}
corresponds to overdamping, with one mode becoming more quickly damped
and the other more slowly damped.

In the static limit
\omega\rightarrow 0
the solutions to the longitudinal root of the dispersion relation go to

k=\pm im_{D}\,.

(77)

The positive imaginary root describes the Debye screening of a stationary charge within the plasma. The analysis of the real and imaginary parts of the zeros for the
\kappa
-dependent dispersion relation for both transverse and longitudinal modes can be found in
Carrington:2003je
;
Schenke:2006xu
. Our approach starting from manifestly covariant formulation with arbitrary medium 4-velocity matches these results.

V
Summary and Conclusions

The main result of this work is a manifestly Lorentz covariant description of the dynamics of perturbations in a relativistic plasma created by external or intrinsically generated electromagnetic fields in the presence of collisional damping within the range of conditions where linear plasma response applies. The covariant polarization tensor, which is gauge invariant and current conserving, agrees in the ultra-relativistic limit with earlier derivations in the plasma rest frame
Carrington:2003je
;
Schenke:2006xu
. In the limit of vanishing collisional damping (
\kappa\to 0
) our results reduce to the well known expressions originally obtained by Weldon
Weldon:1982aq
. For non-zero values of the collisional damping rate our results provide for an effective analytical description of the dynamics of relativistic plasmas under more realistic conditions, extending their applicability to a much wider range of phenomena.

Our original motivation for undertaking this work was

1.

The study of quark-gluon plasma response to the extreme electromagnetic fields produced in heavy ion collisions.

Of particular relevance here is that the results for electric conductivity (see Figures
8
and
7
) show strong frequency dependence governed by the average collision rate
\kappa
.

Moreover, we anticipate that the approach developed here and its clarification of prior results will serve as a point of departure for the exploration and study of several other distinct domains of physics. Notable examples are:

2.

Plasma physics characterizing the dynamics of cosmological evolution from the big-bang to the recombination era;

3.

Stellar evolution from the formation of stars to terminal processes such as supernovae;

4.

Plasma particle acceleration and/or high intensity laser interaction with matter generated in the laboratory in response to transient extreme electric and magnetic fields.

For most of these applications, multiple plasma components, i. e. heavy (ions) and light (electrons) charged particles, will need to be considered. For simplicity, we focused here on a one-component plasma. In many situations, chemical potentials are required to properly describe the asymmetry between particle and antiparticle distributions or to account for not fully saturated phase space distributions. Again for simplicity, here we assumed particle-antiparticle symmetry and fully saturated phase space. Also, in a multi-component plasma some plasma components may be better described in the nonrelativistic limit (see Appendix
B
) while others may remain relativistic as studied in the main body of this work.

As we already mentioned, our treatment can be generalized and extended in multiple ways, which generally require a modest amount of numerical computation. Specific examples of such generalizations are: a 4-momentum conserving extension of the collision term; a momentum-dependent collisional relaxation rate
\kappa(p)
; plasma kinetics that is neither nonrelativistic nor ultrarelativistic; degenerate or undersaturated phase-space populations. We intend to pursue these extensions and applications in future publications.

Appendix B
Non-relativistic limit

We now consider the low-temperature non-relativistic limit. For low temperatures (
T\ll m
) the Fermi distribution can be replaced by the Boltzmann factor

f_{\mathrm{eq}}\approx\exp\left(-\frac{p\cdot u}{T}\right)

(114)

and in the non-relativistic limit we approximate the energy in the rest frame by its nonrelativistic expansion

(p\cdot u)=p^{0}=m\left(1+\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}}{2m^{2}}+\ldots\right)\,.

(115)

In the following we will keep only the first term of this expansion. For example we can evaluate the
n_{\mathrm{eq}}
integral (
5
) as

n_{eq}=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}d|\boldsymbol{p}|}{\pi^{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{m}{T}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)\right]\,.

(116)

The angular integration is trivial and the rest gives us a gaussian integral

n_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{1}{4}e^{-m/T}\left(\frac{2mT}{\pi}\right)^{3/2}\,.

(117)

The denominator of all the integrals reads

p\cdot k+i(p\cdot u)\kappa=p^{0}\left(\omega-\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|}{p^{0}}k\cos\theta+i\kappa\right)

(118)

and in the first order

\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|}{p^{0}}\approx\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|}{m}\,.

(119)

Unfortunately, as we will see below, the angular and magnitude integration no longer factorize. We will resolve this problem by computing all angular integrals up to the quadratic order in the small parameter
|\boldsymbol{p}|/m
.

B.1
Evaluation of
R^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)

The definition of
R^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)
is given in (
33
). In non-relativistic and low-temperature limit we have

R^{\mu}_{\nu}(k)=-2q^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}d|\boldsymbol{p}|}{\pi^{2}}f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(|\boldsymbol{p}|)\\
\times\frac{1}{p^{0}}\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{\omega p^{\mu}p_{\nu}-(k\cdot p)p^{\mu}\delta_{0\nu}}{k\cdot p+i(p\cdot u)\kappa}\,.

(120)

Introducing the abbreviation

g(\cos\theta)=\omega-|\boldsymbol{p}|k\cos\theta/m+i\kappa\,,

(121)

the angular integrals are easily evaluated in up to quadratic order of
|\boldsymbol{p}|/m
:

\displaystyle I^{0}_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|k\cos\theta}{g(\cos\theta)m}\approx\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|k}{m\omega^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\,,

(122)

\displaystyle I^{0}_{z}

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{\omega|\boldsymbol{p}|\cos\theta}{g(\cos\theta)m}\approx-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\omega|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}k}{\omega^{\prime 2}m^{2}}\,,

(123)

\displaystyle I^{z}_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}k\cos^{2}\theta}{g(\cos\theta)m^{2}}\approx\frac{1}{3}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}k}{\omega^{\prime}m^{2}}\,,

(124)

\displaystyle I^{z}_{z}

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}\omega\cos^{2}\theta}{g(\cos\theta)m^{2}}\approx-\frac{1}{3}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}\omega}{m^{2}\omega^{\prime}}\,,

(125)

\displaystyle I^{x}_{x}

\displaystyle=I^{y}_{y}=-\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{\omega|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\varphi}{g(\cos\theta)m^{2}}\approx-\frac{1}{3}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}\omega}{m^{2}\omega^{\prime}}\,.

(126)

In order for the expansion to be possible
|\boldsymbol{p}|k/m\omega^{\prime}\ll 1
which places also limit on
k
and
\omega^{\prime}
. All the magnitude integrals are now of the type

m_{L}^{2}\equiv\frac{2q^{2}}{3m^{2}\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\boldsymbol{p}|^{4}d|\boldsymbol{p}|\left(\frac{1}{T}e^{-p^{0}/T}\right)\\
=q^{2}\left(\frac{2mT}{\pi}\right)^{3/2}\frac{e^{-m/T}}{2m}\,,

(127)

where
m_{L}^{2}
defines a mass scale. Altogether

R^{0}_{0}=m_{L}^{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\omega^{\prime 2}}\,,\quad R^{0}_{z}=-m_{L}^{2}\frac{\omega k}{\omega^{\prime 2}}\,,

(128)

R^{z}_{0}=m_{L}^{2}\frac{k}{\omega^{\prime}}\,,\quad R^{x}_{x}=R^{y}_{y}=R^{z}_{z}=-m_{L}^{2}\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\,.

(129)

B.2
Evaluation of
Q(k)

In the low temperature and non-relativistic limit the integral for
Q(k)
(
20
) becomes

Q(k)=-i\frac{\kappa}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}d|\boldsymbol{p}|}{\pi^{2}}f_{\mathrm{eq}}(|\boldsymbol{p}|)\\
\times\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\omega-|\boldsymbol{p}|k\cos\theta/m+i\kappa}\,,

(130)

in which again the integrals over angles and magnitude of the momentum
\boldsymbol{p}
not quite separate. Up to quadratic terms in
|\boldsymbol{p}|/m
the angular integral is

\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{1}{g(\cos\theta)}\approx\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}}\left(1+\frac{1}{3}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}k^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{\prime 2}}\right)\,,

(131)

giving the result:

\boxed{Q(k)=-\frac{i\kappa}{\omega^{\prime}}\left(1+\frac{Tk^{2}}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\right)\,.}

(132)

B.3
Evaluation of
Q^{\mu}(k)

In the low temperature and non-relativistic limit the integral for
Q^{\mu}(k)
(
26
) becomes

Q^{\mu}(k)=-2qi\frac{\kappa}{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}d|\boldsymbol{p}|}{\pi^{2}}f_{\mathrm{eq}}(|\boldsymbol{p}|)\\
\times\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{p^{\mu}/m}{\omega-|\boldsymbol{p}|k\cos\theta/m+i\kappa}\,,

(133)

where the only non-zero angular integrals are up to second order in
|\boldsymbol{p}|/k

\displaystyle I^{0}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{1}{g(\cos\theta)}\approx\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{3}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}k^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{\prime 3}}\,,

(134)

\displaystyle I^{z}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|\cos\theta}{g(\cos\theta)m}\approx\frac{1}{3}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}k}{m^{2}\omega^{\prime 2}}\,.

(135)

Finally, after integration over the magnitude we have for the two non-zero components of
Q^{\mu}(k)

\boxed{Q^{0}=-\frac{2qi\kappa}{\omega^{\prime}}\left(1+\frac{Tk^{2}}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\right)\,,\quad Q^{z}=-2qi\kappa\frac{Tk}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\,.}

(136)

B.4

In the low temperature and non-relativistic limit the integral for
H_{\nu}(k)
(

H_{\nu}(k)=-q\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}d|\boldsymbol{p}|}{\pi^{2}}f^{\prime}_{\mathrm{eq}}(|\boldsymbol{p}|)\\
\times\frac{1}{p^{0}}\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{\omega p_{\nu}-(k\cdot p)\delta_{0\nu}}{\omega-|\boldsymbol{p}|k\cos\theta/m+i\kappa}\,.

(137)

The only non-zero angular integrals up to the second order in
|\boldsymbol{p}|/k
read

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|k\cos\theta}{g(\cos\theta)m}\approx\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{|\boldsymbol{p}|k}{m\omega^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\,,

(138)

\displaystyle I_{z}

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{\omega|\boldsymbol{p}|\cos\theta}{g(\cos\theta)m}\approx-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\omega|\boldsymbol{p}|^{2}k}{m^{2}\omega^{\prime 2}}\,.

(139)

After integration over
|\boldsymbol{p}|
we have two non-zero components of
H_{\nu}(k)

\boxed{H_{0}=\frac{1}{2q}m_{L}^{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\omega^{\prime 2}}\,,\quad H_{z}=-\frac{1}{2q}m_{L}^{2}\frac{\omega k}{\omega^{\prime 2}}\,.}

(140)

(145)

The gauge conditions for the components (

\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{k}\left[1-\frac{i\kappa}{\omega^{\prime}}\left(1+\frac{Tk^{2}}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\right)\right]+i\kappa\frac{Tk}{m\omega^{\prime 2}}\\

\displaystyle\Pi^{z}_{0}

(147)
