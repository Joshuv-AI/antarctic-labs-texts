Probabilistic Parameter Estimators and Calibration Metrics for Pose Estimation from Image Features
Romeo Valentin 1 4 , Sydney M. Katz 1 , Joonghyun Lee 1 , Don Walker 2 , Matthew Sorgenfrei 2 , and Mykel J. Kochenderfer 1

Abstract
This paper addresses the challenge of probabilistic parameter estimation given measurement uncertainty in real-time. We provide a general formulation and apply this to pose estimation for an autonomous visual landing system. We present three probabilistic parameter estimators: a least-squares sampling approach, a linear approximation method, and a probabilistic programming estimator. To evaluate these estimators, we introduce novel closed-form expressions for measuring calibration and sharpness specifically for multivariate normal distributions. Our experimental study compares the three estimators under various noise conditions. We demonstrate that the linear approximation estimator can produce sharp and well-calibrated pose predictions significantly faster than the other methods but may yield overconfident predictions in certain scenarios. Additionally, we demonstrate that these estimators can be integrated with a Kalman filter for continuous pose estimation during a runway approach where we observe a 50% improvement in sharpness while maintaining marginal calibration. This work contributes to the integration of data-driven computer vision models into complex safety-critical aircraft systems and provides a foundation for developing rigorous certification guidelines for such systems.

II-A

Camera Model for Point Projections

Consider the pose estimation problem for an aircraft approaching a runway approach using image features with known corresponding world points.
We assume a (pinhole) camera located on the aircraft, positioned such
that the entire runway is in view of the camera lens.

Figure

1
presents an overview.

Let the camera projection function
\mathbf{y}=f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\upbeta})

map a world point

\mathbf{x}=[x_{[\text{alongtrack}]},x_{[\text{crosstrack}]},x_{[\text{altitude}]}]

to an image projection

\mathbf{y}=[y_{[\text{right}]},y_{[\text{up}]}]

given a camera position

\bm{\upbeta}=[\beta_{[\text{alongtrack}]},\beta_{[\text{crosstrack}]},\beta_{[\text{altitude}]}]

Consider now a focal-point-centric coordinate system rotated such that
the
x
-axis goes from the focal point through the center of the camera
projection plane towards the world points, and the
y
- and
z
-axes are
parallel to the projection plane’s edges.
If we write the location of the world points in this coordinate system as

[x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}]
, we can compute the
camera projection coordinate simply as

\mathbf{y}=\lambda_{\text{focal length}}\cdot\frac{1}{x^{\prime}}\begin{bmatrix}y^{\prime}\\
z^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}.

(1)

We will use the notation

\mathbf{y}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(y_{i}\right)_{[1]}\\
\left(y_{i}\right)_{[2]}\end{bmatrix},\quad i\in\mathcal{I}

(2)

to denote the first and second
components of the
i
-th observation
\mathbf{y}_{i}
, with

\mathcal{I}=\left\{1,2,3,4\right\}
for the four runway corners.
We denote random variables with a hat and write

{\bar{\xi}}^{(k)}\sim p_{\bar{\xi}},

(3)

to denote the
k
-th sample
of the random variable
\bar{\xi}

that follows the distribution
p_{\bar{\xi}}
.
Upright bold variables such as
\mathbf{y}
denote vectors, and matrices are uppercase such as
\Sigma
.

II-B

Pose-from-N-Points (PNP)

To solve the PNP problem in its basic form, it is now sufficient to
solve the nonlinear least-squares problem

\bm{\upbeta}=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\upbeta}}\sum_{i}{d\left(\mathbf{y}_{i},f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\upbeta}\right)\right)}^{2}

(4)

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Euclidean distance.
For other problem settings,
d
and
f
can take a variety of forms.
For example, if
f
computes sidelines angles other geometric features, such as sideline angles,
an appropriate distance functions may be chosen.
We also point out that for point projections, due to the nonlinearity of the projection function
f(\cdot,\cdot)
,
this problem is numerically challenging to solve.
In particular, for low altitudes, the inverse problem becomes very sensitive to small changes in
the projection. Nonetheless, for approach angles of at least

1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
above ground, the PNP problem can typically be solved
using, e.g., the Newton-Raphson, Levenberg-Marquardt, or trust region
algorithms.

II-C

Least-squares Under Measurement Noise

If weights
w_{i}
for each observation are available, we may rewrite

Eq.

4
as

\bm{\upbeta}=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\upbeta}}\sum_{i}w_{i}\cdot{d\left(\mathbf{y}_{i},f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\upbeta}\right)\right)}^{2}

(5)

where weights are typically chosen as the inverse variance of each
observation, i.e.,
w_{i}=1/{\sigma_{y}^{2}}_{i}

[
21
]
.
For noise that is
correlated across observations, we can instead concatenate all components
into a single vector

{\mathbf{r}}(\bm{\upbeta})=\begin{bmatrix}d\left(\mathbf{y}_{1},f\left(\mathbf{x}_{1},\bm{\upbeta}\right)\right)\\
d\left(\mathbf{y}_{2},f\left(\mathbf{x}_{2},\bm{\upbeta}\right)\right)\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}

(6)

where each
component
d(\cdot,\cdot)
may be further expanded if it has
multiple components (such as component-wise distances). Then we can rewrite

Eq.

5
as

\bm{\upbeta}=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\upbeta}}{\mathbf{r}(\bm{\upbeta})}^{\top}W\mathbf{r}(\bm{\upbeta})

(7)

where
W
denotes the weight matrix which can be chosen as the inverse
variance-covariance matrix of the observation noise, e.g.

\Sigma_{\mathbf{y}}^{-1}
for the case of Gaussian noise
[
21
]
.

Notice that we can also rewrite
Eq.

7
as

\displaystyle\bm{\upbeta}

\displaystyle=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\upbeta}}{\tilde{\mathbf{r}}}(\bm{\upbeta})^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}(\bm{\upbeta})

(8)

\displaystyle=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\upbeta}}\sum_{i}{\tilde{r}}_{i}^{2}(\bm{\upbeta})

by LU-factorizing

W=LU
and setting

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=U{\mathbf{r}}
in
the general case, or
\Sigma=LU
and

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=L^{-\top}{\mathbf{r}}

in the Gaussian noise case. This representation is sometimes preferable
for mathematical convenience and solver interfaces.

II-D

Representing Measurement and Pose Uncertainty

If our world points

\left\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
and measurements

\left\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
are known, we
can solve the pose estimation problem as a nonlinear least squares
optimization as shown above. However, our measurements

\mathbf{y}_{i}
and world points
\mathbf{x}_{i}
can be noisy or
uncertain, and may therefore be provided through a probabilistic
description, e.g., a Gaussian or other distribution, or samples drawn
from a distribution.

In this scenario, our pose estimate will be a random variable
\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}
, or more precisely,
\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
. We therefore need to represent the pose either (i) through samples (to which a distribution may be fitted) or (ii)
directly as a (multivariate) distribution. In these cases, we write

\left({\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}^{(1)},{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}^{(2)},\ldots\right)\overset{\text{ i.i.d.}}{\sim}p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}

(9)

with realizations

\left(\bm{\upbeta}^{(1)},\bm{\upbeta}^{(2)},\ldots\right)
, or

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}\approx{\mathcal{N}}_{(\mu,\Sigma)},

(10)

respectively. Notice that due to the non-linearity in the projection in

Eq.

1
, the distribution

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}

will not in general be Gaussian, even for Gaussian noise in the measurements. However, we
will see that approximating

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}

as a Gaussian
{\mathcal{N}}_{(\mu,\Sigma)}
does yield good results for
our problem.

II-E

Calibration and
Sharpness

Calibration gives us a way to quantify whether the uncertainty in a
series of predictions is faithful to the distribution of prediction
errors. Given a set of predicted probability distributions

p_{{\bar{\xi}}_{i}}
and corresponding measurements
\xi_{i}
, we
call the predictions
(marginally) calibrated
if

\text{Pr}\left(\bar{\xi}_{i}\leq q(p_{\bar{\xi}_{i}},\rho)\right)\approx\rho\quad\forall\rho\in(0,1),

(11)

where
q(p_{\bar{\xi}_{i}},\rho)
denotes the quantile
function for the random variable
{\bar{\xi}}_{i}
evaluated at

\rho
. Notably, however, the quantile function is not defined for
multivariate distributions such as

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}

in
Eq.

9
and
Eq.

10
. We must therefore
generalize
Eq.

11
to remove the dependence on
the quantile function and instead rely on the more general notion of
prediction sets. We present such a solution in

Section

IV
.

Further, we note that a series of predictions may be perfectly
calibrated yet still be “bad” in the sense that each prediction has
large uncertainty. Therefore, we must also measure sharpness, a measure
of the “conciseness” of the predictions. We propose a measure of
sharpness for multivariate normal distributions in

Section

IV-D
and refer to

[
22
]
for an overview of the
trade off between calibration and sharpness.

III
Three Probabilistic Estimators

Given an observation function
f(\cdot,\cdot)
and measurement
tuples
{\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, we have seen how to estimate the parameter
\bm{\upbeta}
,
incorporating measurement uncertainties through a weighting scheme given in

Section

II-C
. However, this process will only give us the maximum likelihood estimator for

\bm{\upbeta}
, i.e.

\bm{\upbeta}=\arg\max\limits_{\bm{\upbeta}}p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}(\bm{\upbeta}).

(12)

Instead, we are now interested in constructing a probabilistic
description of the parameter
\bm{\upbeta}
as a distribution or a
set of samples. We propose three estimators for this task: (i) the
least-squares sampling estimator, (ii) the linear approximation
estimator, and (iii) the MCMC estimator. We show that although the three methods differ in mathematical rigor
and runtime cost, their performance often matches closely.

III-A

The LSQ Estimator via Noise
Sampling

The simplest approach to constructing a sampled representation of

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}
is to repeatedly sample simulated noise and
solve the least-squares formulation for each noise sample.
To construct the
k
th pose estimate
\bm{\upbeta}^{(k)}
,
we draw a noise sample
\bm{\upvarepsilon}_{i}^{(k)}
for each
corner projection
\mathbf{y}_{i}
and solve the least squares
formulations from
Eq.

8
where we substitute

\mathbf{y}_{i}\leftarrow\mathbf{y}_{i}-\bm{\upvarepsilon}_{i}^{(k)}

(13)

for each
i
. Repeating this a number of times will result in a set of estimates

\{\bm{\upbeta}^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}=\{\bm{\upbeta}^{(1)},\bm{\upbeta}^{(2)},\ldots,\bm{\upbeta}^{\left(|\mathcal{K}|\right)}\}

(14)

which we may optionally approximate as a distribution
p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}
by fitting a
multivariate normal distribution to the samples.
For the pose estimation application we find that a number of samples
|\mathcal{K}|

between 100 and 500 is typically sufficient.

Notably, the LSQ Estimator does not require us to make any assumptions
about the prior distribution
p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}
or the shape of the posterior
p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}

(which is in general not a perfect Gaussian), nor does it require strong assumptions
about the function
f(\cdot,\cdot)
. Indeed, even multimodal
posterior distributions can be approximated with this method.

The downside of this method is that the computational cost is rather high, as the full
least-squares problem has to be solved for every sample of

\bm{\upbeta}^{(k)}
. However, starting from the second sample,
setting the initial guess for
\bm{\upbeta}^{(k)}
to the solution
of
\bm{\upbeta}^{(k-1)}
can significantly reduce the runtime cost.

III-B

Linear Approximation Estimator

The LSQ Estimator suffers from high computational cost, since it has to
solve the least squares problem many times. However, if we approximate
the projection function
f\left(\mathbf{x},\bm{\upbeta}\right)
as
a linear function around some sample
\bm{\upbeta}^{(1)}
, and
assume the noise to be Gaussian, we can construct

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}
in a single step by directly propagating
the uncertainty in the observations
\mathbf{y}
.
We call this estimator the Linear Approximation Estimator.

Let
J_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_{i}\left(\mathbf{x},\bm{\upbeta}\right)}{\partial\beta_{j}}

be the Jacobian of our projection function. We can relate changes in

\bm{\upbeta}
and
\mathbf{r}
or
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}
as

\displaystyle J\Delta\bm{\upbeta}

\displaystyle=-\Delta\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},

(15)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left(J^{\top}WJ\right)\Delta\bm{\upbeta}

\displaystyle=-J^{\top}W\Delta\mathbf{r}.

With

\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i}-f\left(\mathbf{x},\bm{\upbeta}\right)

and

\mathbf{y}_{i}=f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\upbeta}\right)+\bm{\upvarepsilon}_{i}

we can model
\Delta\mathbf{r}=-\bm{\upvarepsilon}
and therefore
treat
\Delta\bm{\upbeta}
as a random variable

\mkern 4.0mu\overline{\mkern-4.0mu\Delta\bm{\upbeta}\mkern-4.0mu}\mkern 4.0mu
with distribution

p_{\mkern 4.0mu\overline{\mkern-4.0mu\Delta\bm{\upbeta}\mkern-4.0mu}\mkern 4.0mu}=\left(J^{\top}WJ\right)^{-1}J^{\top}W{p_{\bar{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}}}_{i}.

(16)

Using this insight, we can now construct our parameter estimate

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}
as follows.
We first estimate
\mu_{\bm{\upbeta}}
by solving
Eq.

8
once,
without any noise perturbation.
Then, we compute

\displaystyle p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\bm{\upbeta}}+p_{\mkern 4.0mu\overline{\mkern-4.0mu\Delta\bm{\upbeta}\mkern-4.0mu}\mkern 4.0mu}

(17)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\bm{\upbeta}}+\left(J^{\top}WJ\right)^{-1}J^{\top}Wp_{\bar{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}}

which yields the desired result.
For example, if

p_{\bar{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}}={\mathcal{N}}_{\left(\mathbf{0},\Sigma_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}\right)}

and
W=\Sigma_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}^{-1}
then

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}={\mathcal{N}}_{\left(\mu_{\bm{\upbeta}},\Sigma_{\bm{\upbeta}}\right)}

(18)

with

\displaystyle\mu_{\bm{\upbeta}}=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\upbeta}}{\mathbf{r}(\bm{\upbeta})}^{\top}\mathbf{r}(\bm{\upbeta})

(19)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\bm{\upbeta}}=\left(J^{\top}\Sigma_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}^{-1}J\right)^{-1}J^{\top}\Sigma_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}^{-1}J\left(J^{\top}\Sigma_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}^{-1}J\right)^{-1}.

(20)

Further, if
J
is square and invertible then

\Sigma_{\bm{\upbeta}}
reduces to

\Sigma_{\bm{\upbeta}}=J^{-1}\Sigma_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}\left(J^{\top}\right)^{-1}.

(21)

Finally, we may replace
p_{\bar{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}}
in
Eq.

17
with

\left(p_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}-f\left(\mathbf{x},\theta^{\text{est}}\right)\right)

to slightly relax the assumption that

\mathbf{y}_{i}=f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\upbeta}\right)+\bm{\upvarepsilon}_{i}
,
which can yield more robust results.

The Linear Approximation Estimator
is highly efficient, only requiring to solve the least squares problem
once and compute the Jacobian of
f
one additional time. This property makes
it more suitable for real-time systems such as a visual positioning
system. However, in cases where the assumption of Gaussian noise does
not hold (see e.g.
Section

V-C
), or if the
function is highly nonlinear, the estimator may produce poor results.

III-C

MCMC Estimator

The previous two approaches have relied on a least-squares formulation
of the problem, carefully constructing a weighting scheme to incorporate
uncertainties correctly. Now, we present an approach based on a
Bayesian formulation that can create samples
\bm{\upbeta}^{(k)}

directly using probabilistic programming.

Our formulation is as follows. We choose a weakly informative prior

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}
as a diagonal multivariate normal
distribution such that our entire operational domain is contained within
one standard deviation of the prior. Of course, if a better prior is
available it may be chosen instead. We further recall that we assume we
have a model of the observation noise
{p_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}}_{i}

(which is now not constrained to any particular family of distribution),
provided by our sensor for every sample.
Then our Bayesian formulation is simply

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}

\displaystyle\sim p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}

(22)

\displaystyle{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}

\displaystyle\sim f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\upbeta}\right)+{p_{\bar{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}_{i}}}

which can be directly
solved using Markov Chain Monte Carlo
[
23
,
24
]
, yielding samples

\left(\bm{\upbeta}^{(1)},\bm{\upbeta}^{(2)},\ldots\right)
.

This formulation is remarkably simple, necessitating no further thoughts
about how to integrate the knowledge of uncertainties into any kind of
weighting scheme, how to set up the least squares problem, or
assumptions about the distributions of inputs or outputs. Further, this
formulation can indeed be more computationally efficient than the noise
sampling approach from
Section

III-A
(see e.g.

Table

I
) and can represent arbitrary distribution shapes just like the LSQ Estimator.

III-D

Integrating Probabilistic Estimators Into a Kalman Filter Framework

Finally, we demonstrate how we can integrate the proposed probabilistic parameter estimators into a Kalman filter framework.
Recall the Kalman filter equations (without inputs) given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{[t+1]}

\displaystyle=A\mathbf{x}^{[t]}+\mathbf{w}^{[t]}

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{[t+1]}

\displaystyle=C\mathbf{x}^{[t+1]}+\mathbf{v}^{[t+1]}

with process noise

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{w}^{[t^{\prime}]}{\mathbf{w}^{[t]}}^{\top}\right]=\delta_{t^{\prime}t}Q^{[t]}

and measurement noise

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}^{[t^{\prime}]}{\mathbf{v}^{[t]}}^{\top}\right]=\delta_{t^{\prime}t}R^{[t]}
,
i.e., Gaussian noise uncorrelated in time with covariances
Q^{[t]}
and
R^{[t]}

[
25
]
.

Often it is difficult to choose
R^{[t]}
dynamically for each time step, and instead a single fixed
R
is chosen
for all time steps.
Using one of the proposed estimators, however, we can dynamically compute
\mathbf{y}^{[t]}
and
R^{[t]}
for each time step.
Consider the probabilistic parameter
\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}^{[t]}
to represent our measurements
\mathbf{y}^{[t]}
at time step
t
, with

p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}^{[t]}\mid{\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{[t]}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{[t]}_{i})\}}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}\approx{\mathcal{N}}_{(\mu^{[t]},\Sigma^{[t]})}.

(24)

Then,
for each time step, we can set

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{[t]}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\bm{\upmu}^{[t]}

(25)

\displaystyle R^{[t]}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\Sigma^{[t]}.

Notice how this formulation differs from typical nonlinear Kalman filter formulations such as the Extended Kalman filter
[
25
,
26
]
.
Our measurement uncertainty is not restricted to relations
\mathbf{y}=h(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{w}
for some known
h(\mathbf{x})
, but instead may arise without modeling
h(\mathbf{x})
, such as measured image features with predictive uncertainty perceived from state
\mathbf{x}
.

One important additional consideration is that the Kalman filter assumes
noise uncorrelated between time steps, which is typically not the case for high-frequency
sensors, such as a computer vision model.
We consider two possible solutions:
(i)
Use only measurements far enough apart in time that their error correlation can be assumed to be low; or
(ii) use a Kalman filter formulation that is able to deal with “colored”
noise.
We refer to
[

for an overview of the latter.

IV
Measuring Calibration and Sharpness for Multivariate Normal Distributions

Evaluating the quality of a probabilistic estimator is not trivial.
For example, consider predictions somewhat close to the truth but overly confident and predictions
further from the truth but with appropriately high uncertainty.
The latter is often preferable due to it not misleading us with high confidence.
Conversely, predictions that are “probabilistically correct” but include an overly large number of possibilities can be equally problematic.

These two properties have been formalized as “calibration” and “sharpness” and are crucial for assessing and comparing the reliability of estimators.
In
Section

V
we will see that, depending on the problem setup, some of the proposed estimators can be sharp but not calibrated, or calibrated but not sharp, or even marginally calibrated in each component but not jointly calibrated.
However, as introduced in
Section

II-E
, computing calibration is typically restricted to univariate distributions due to its reliance on the quantile or cumulative density function, and therefore not applicable to our predictions
p_{\bar{\bm{\upbeta}}}
.

To remedy this, in this section we introduce efficient methods for measuring calibration and sharpness specifically tailored for multivariate normal distributions through the use of “centered prediction sets” in a diagonalizing basis.

IV-A

Centered Prediction Sets

In this section, we construct a scheme to efficiently compute
calibration for multivariate normal distributions using centered
prediction sets
\mathcal{P}_{\rho}
.
Consider for now the univariate probability distribution
p_{\bar{\xi}}
of a random variable
\bar{\xi}
and a
sample
\bar{\xi}_{i}
drawn from
p_{\bar{\xi}}
.
Then we define a prediction set

\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(p_{\bar{\xi}})
at coverage level

\rho
such that the probability of
\bar{\xi}_{i}
being contained in

\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(p_{\bar{\xi}})
is approximately
equal to
\rho
, i.e.

\text{ Pr}\left(\bar{\xi}_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(p_{\bar{\xi}})\right)\approx\rho.

(26)

We can see we recover
Eq.

11
exactly
by setting

\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(p_{\bar{\xi}})=\bigl(-\infty,\ q(p_{\bar{\xi}},\rho)\bigr].

(27)

However, we can also choose different constructions.
For example, we can also choose the centered construction

\mathcal{P}_{\rho}=\left[q(p_{\bar{\xi}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}}),\ q(p_{\bar{\xi}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}})\right]

(28)

or even the disjoint (and centered) construction

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(p_{\bar{\xi}})=\phantom{\cup}\

\displaystyle\bigl(-\infty,q(p_{\bar{\xi}},\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}})\bigr]

(29)

\displaystyle\cup\

\displaystyle\bigl[q(p_{\bar{\xi}},1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}}),\infty\bigr).

(30)

Notice that each construction satisfies
Eq.

26
but typically differs significantly in size.
Specifically, if
p_{\bar{\xi}}
is Gaussian then
Eq.

28
constructs the smallest set to satisfy
Eq.

26
for any
\rho
because it accumulates always those points with highest probability density.
We will call this construction
centered
and
cumulative
,
as opposed to
off-centered
such as
Eq.

27
or
disjoint
such as
Eq.

29
.

In this work we will proceed by considering only the centered and cumulative construction from
Eq.

28
.
Although the other constructions would also suffice for our upcoming multivariate construction,
we find that the central and cumulative construction more accurately reflects a small calibration error given a small mean prediction error and is further symmetric about the direction of the error.

IV-B

Constructing Cumulative Centered Prediction Sets for
Multivariate Normal Distributions

The construction of central and cumulative prediction sets introduced above is still not applicable for multivariate distributions.
However, by making two key observations about multivariate normal distributions we will be able to efficiently construct
prediction sets
\mathcal{P}({\mathcal{N}}_{(\mu,\Sigma)})
that satisfy
Eq.

26
just as above.

First, any multivariate normal distribution

{\mathcal{N}}_{(\mu,\Sigma)}
with
d
dimensions can be “diagonalized” without
fundamentally changing the probability density by considering a new distribution

{\mathcal{N}}_{\left(\widetilde{\mu},\widetilde{\Sigma}\right)}

in a rotated basis such that the covariance matrix

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=Q\Sigma Q^{\top}

(31)

\displaystyle=\text{ diag}\left(\left[{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{[1]}^{2},\ldots,{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{[d]}^{2}\right]\right)

(32)

is diagonalized with a rotation matrix
Q
and
appropriately transformed samples

\widetilde{\bm{\upxi}}=Q^{\top}\left(\bm{\upxi}-\bm{\upmu}\right)

(and thus

\widetilde{\bm{\upmu}}=Q^{\top}\left(\bm{\upmu}-\bm{\upmu}\right)=\bm{0}
).

Second, the prediction set

\mathcal{P}_{p}({\mathcal{N}}_{(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\Sigma})})

for diagonalized normal distributions can simply be constructed by
considering each dimension individually as a univariate case, with

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rho}\left({\mathcal{N}}_{(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\Sigma})}\right)=\bigl\{\widetilde{\bm{\upxi}}\,\mid\,{\widetilde{\xi}}_{[i]}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sqrt[d]{\rho}}(\mathcal{N}_{(0,{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{[i]}^{2})})

(33)

\displaystyle\forall\left\{i\in 1,\ldots,d\right\}

\displaystyle\bigr\}.

Notice that we have replaced the probability
\rho
of each set with
\sqrt[d]{\rho}

because the probability of
d
conditions each with probability
\sqrt[d]{\rho}
to hold simultaneously is exactly
\rho
.

For the univariate case we now recall the construction from
Eq.

28
and further exploit the symmetric structure of Gaussians to rewrite
Eq.

28
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rho}\left(\mathcal{N}_{\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[q(\mathcal{N}_{\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}}),\ q(\mathcal{N}_{\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}})\right]

(34)

\displaystyle=\left\{\xi\mathrel{\Big|}\frac{\xi-\mu}{\sigma}<q\left(\mathcal{N}_{\left(0,1^{2}\right)},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\right)\right\}.

Notably, when considering samples normalized by the
prediction’s mean and standard deviation, we find that the prediction set can
be written only as a function of
\rho
, which makes this construction
very efficient. To give an example of the construction, consider

p_{\bar{\xi}}=\mathcal{N}_{(\mu,\Sigma)}=\mathcal{N}_{(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\Sigma})}
with

\mu=[\frac{1}{3}.\frac{1}{4}]
and

\Sigma=\text{diag}([1.2^{2},1.5^{2}])
,
and pick
\rho=0.68^{2}\approx 0.46
. Then we can construct

\mathcal{P}_{0.46}(p_{\bar{\xi}})=\left\{\xi\mathrel{\Big|}\frac{\mspace{9mu}\left(\xi_{[1]}-\frac{1}{3}\right)}{1.2}\leq 1.0\land\frac{\xi_{[2]}-\frac{1}{4}}{1.5}\leq 1.0\right\}.

(35)

IV-C

Evaluating Calibration

Given the above constructions of prediction sets for multivariate normal distributions, it
is now straightforward to compute the calibration, or coverage rate, for any
\rho
, which we will be able to plot as a calibration curve.
This calibration curve will essentially tell us how much
Eq.

26
is violated given predictions and actual observations.

Given a sequence of Gaussian predictions

\{p_{{\bar{\bm{\upxi}}}_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
and
observations
\{{\bm{\upxi}}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}

with

{p_{\bar{\bm{\upxi}}}}_{i}={\mathcal{N}}_{\left(\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}\right)}

we can compute the ratio of observations contained in the
prediction set
\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(p_{\bar{\bm{\upxi}}})
, i.e.,

\text{ coverage }\coloneq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left[{\bar{\bm{\upxi}}}_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho}({p_{\bar{\bm{\upxi}}}}_{i})\right],

(36)

where
[\ \cdot\ ]
denotes the Iverson bracket.

implements this evaluation scheme.
Plots of this coverage rate are shown in

.

IV-D

Defining and Computing
Sharpness

Finally, we discuss how to efficiently compute sharpness for
multivariate normal distributions. Unfortunately, sharpness does not
have a single definition comparable to
Eq.

26
for
calibration. However, it is still useful for comparing
predictions.

We define sharpness of a single prediction as the
hyper-volume of the set of points within one standard deviation of the
mean prediction. Specifically, if we consider again the diagonalized
covariance matrix

\widetilde{\Sigma}=\text{ diag}([{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{[1]}^{2},\ldots,{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{[d]}^{2}])

we can compute sharpness as

\text{ sharpness }\coloneq V_{d}=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}\prod_{i}{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{i}

(37)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function. Notice that, for
example, with
d=1

Eq.

37
reduces to
2\widetilde{\sigma}
, and
with
d=2
and

\Sigma=\text{ diag}([{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{1}^{2},{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{2}^{2}])

reduces to
\pi{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{1}{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{2}
.

As our first experiment, we consider multivariate uncorrelated normal noise for each observation with

\begin{gathered}\bm{\upvarepsilon}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{(\mathbf{0},\Sigma_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}})},\\

V-B

Multivariate Correlated Normal Noise

Next, we verify that the three different weighting strategies for incorporating correlated noise all produce correct results.
For this, we consider multivariate correlated normal noise

\begin{gathered}\bm{\upvarepsilon}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{\left(\mathbf{0},\Sigma_{\bm{\upvarepsilon}}\right)},\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\upvarepsilon}_{i}\bm{\upvarepsilon}_{j}^{\top}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}0.7&0\\
0&0.7\end{bmatrix}\quad\forall i\neq j,\end{gathered}

(39)

i.e., the error of the “right” components of projections are correlated
across corners, and the same for the “up” components.
This reflects observations we have found in real sensor measurements,
where sometimes errors in components are correlated across observations
\mathbf{y}_{i}
.

We find that the results closely resemble the results from the previous section, although with increased sharpness outliers.
We conclude that all three estimators incorporate the correlation terms correctly into their respective weighting schemes, even though the details of the algorithms differs between estimators.

We also contrast these results with another experiment where we sample noise according to
Eq.

39
but do not model the correlation terms between observations, i.e., we model the problem according to
Eq.

38
.
We find that for all estimators the results (misleadingly) improve sharpness but produce overconfident results, which manifest as calibration curves with a “U” shape (we will see a similar result in the next experiment).
These results show the importance of correctly estimating and modeling the noise including the covariate terms.

V-C

Long Tail Noise

Finally, we examine a case where the three estimators differ.
We consider univariate “long tail” noise, modeled as a superposition of a low- and a
high-variance Gaussian, with

\left(\varepsilon_{i}\right)_{[l]}\sim\left[\frac{3}{4}\mathcal{N}_{\left(0,1^{2}\right)}+\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{N}_{\left(0,3^{2}\right)}\right]\quad\forall i,l,

(40)

i.e. there are no correlations between measurements or components, but
each component’s noise is either sampled from a “concise” or “long
tail” distribution.
Notice that this noise violates the assumptions of the Linear Approximation Estimator,
which assumes Gaussian noise.

VI
Conclusion

We presented how to incorporate knowledge of measurement
uncertainties into a parameter estimation pipeline to yield a
distribution instead of a point estimate, and applied this to
aircraft pose estimation using image features corresponding to known
runway features, although other geometric properties such as sideline
angles can be incorporated just the same. To this end, we have presented
three estimators with trade-offs in computational speed, mathematical
assumptions, and ease of implementation.

We have also highlighted that evaluating and comparing probabilistic
estimators is no simple task and requires analyzing calibration and
sharpness properties, which are not obvious for multivariate
predictions. To this end, we have put forth a new definition of
calibration and sharpness specifically for multivariate normal
distributions.

We have used this new definition to compare the three estimators given
different noise models and have found that the Linear Approximation
Estimator performs on par with the other two estimators when the noise
is Gaussian with known covariance. However, for non-Gaussian noise, the
Linear Approximation Estimator performs poorly and is outperformed in
particular by the MCMC-based approach, which still manages to produce
well-calibrated and sharp results. The MCMC-based approach is faster than the LSQ-based
approach while also being easy to implement.

Finally, we have shown that the estimator outputs can be used as inputs
to a Kalman filter using a case study with real measurements, improving
sharpness by about
2\times
while maintaining approximate marginal
calibration for all three position variables. Somewhat surprisingly,
filtering seems to introduce errors in the covariate terms of the
predictions, which leads to poor calibration of the joint distributions.

This work aims to aid adoption of machine learning-based
sensors into safety-critical applications by enabling rigorous
integration and careful evaluation of uncertainty estimates coming from
the sensor. Further, we hope to give guidance to practitioners trying to
choose a suitable estimator for probabilistic parameter estimation and
provide reference implementations in the Julia language.
