Lagrangian Cobordisms in Liouville manifolds
Valentin Bosshard

Abstract
. Floer theory for Lagrangian cobordisms was developed by Biran and Cornea in a series of papers [ BC13 , BC14 , BC17 ] to study the triangulated structure of the derived Fukaya category of monotone symplectic manifolds. This paper explains how to use the language of stops to study Lagrangian cobordisms in Liouville manifolds and the associated exact triangles in the derived wrapped Fukaya category. Furthermore, we compute the cobordism groups of non-compact Riemann surfaces of finite type.



1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to give a detailed proof that Lagrangian cobordisms in Liouville manifolds induce cone decompositions in the wrapped Fukaya category. As an application, we compute the cobordism groups of non-compact Riemann surfaces.

Fukaya
[
Fuk93
]
discovered that Lagrangian submanifolds in a symplectic manifold
W
can be organized as the objects of an
A_{\infty}
-category, the
Fukaya category
, whose morphisms are intersections between Lagrangian submanifolds. Higher compositions are given by counting pseudo-holomorphic disks with boundary on the Lagrangian submanifolds.

Any
A_{\infty}
-category can be algebraically completed to its
derived category
. This category is
triangulated
, in particular, all morphisms admit mapping cones. One can try then to decompose objects as iterated mapping cones of other objects and find a minimal generating set of objects.

However, the definition of a cone is purely algebraic. In the 80’s Arnold
[
Arn80a
,
Arn80b
]
introduced and studied
Lagrangian cobordisms
between Lagrangian submanifolds. Biran and Cornea
[
BC13
,
BC14
]
showed that Lagrangian cobordisms in fact give rise to cone decompositions in the Fukaya category. A Lagrangian cobordism is a Lagrangian submanifold living inside the product symplectic manifold
\mathbb{C}\times W
which, over the complement of a compact set of
\mathbb{C}
, only consists of a union of product Lagrangian submanifolds
\bigcup_{j=0}^{n}(\gamma_{j}\times L_{j})\subset\mathbb{C}\times W
, where
\gamma_{j}\subset\mathbb{C}
are horizontal rays in
\mathbb{C}
.

To define the Fukaya category one has to restrict to a set of admissible Lagrangian submanifolds. Biran and Cornea considered closed
monotone
Lagrangian submanifolds in monotone symplectic manifolds. Lagrangian cobordisms are in general flexible
[
Eli84
,
Aud85
]
, but become rigid when restricted to monotone Lagrangian cobordisms
[
Che97
,
BC14
]
.

To study a Liouville manifold
W
it is useful to enlarge the Fukaya category by also including exact non-compact Lagrangian submanifolds that are conical at infinity. This
A_{\infty}
-category is called the
wrapped Fukaya category

\mathcal{W}(W)
. Compared to non-wrapped Floer theory, morphisms between transverse Lagrangian submanifolds are not only intersection points but also Reeb orbits at infinity.

Biran and Cornea’s cone decomposition result
[
BC14
]
has the following analog in a Liouville manifold
(W,\lambda)
:

Put a Liouville structure on
\mathbb{C}
which induces the standard orientation. Let
V\subset\mathbb{C}\times W
be an exact Lagrangian submanifold which is conical outside a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}\times W
. By making
K
larger we can assume that
K
is the product
K=\mathbb{C}^{int}\times W^{int}
of Liouville domains
\mathbb{C}^{int}\subset\mathbb{C}
and
W^{int}\subset W
.
Let
\pi_{\mathbb{C}}:\mathbb{C}\times W\to\mathbb{C}
be the projection. For any subset
S\subset\mathbb{C}
we denote
V|_{S}=(V\cap\pi_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(S))\cap(\mathbb{C}\times W^{int}).
Note that we take the preimage and additionally intersect with
\mathbb{C}\times W^{int}
. Because
V
is conical we do not lose any information by the latter intersection.

The Lagrangian submanifold
V
is called a
Lagrangian cobordism
if
V|_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}^{int}}
consists of finitely many
conicalizations
(see Remark
2.6
for details) of product Lagrangian submanifolds
{\gamma_{0}\times L_{0}},

\ldots,\gamma_{n}\times L_{n}
, where the rays
\gamma_{j}
are invariant under the Liouville flow in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}^{int}
and the
L_{j}
are exact conical Lagrangian submanifolds in
W
, called the
ends
of
V
.

The first main theorem of this paper is the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{C}\times W
be a Lagrangian cobordism with ends
L_{0},\ldots,L_{n}
(ordered counterclockwise). Then there is an iterated cone decomposition

L_{0}\cong[L_{n}\to L_{n-1}\to\cdots\to L_{2}\to L_{1}]

in the derived wrapped Fukaya category
D\mathcal{W}(W).

We use the notation
[M\to N]
to describe the
A_{\infty}
-mapping cone of a homomorphism
M\to N
between two
A_{\infty}
-modules
M
and
N
. Theorem
1.1
is stated in the ungraded setting, where taking mapping cones is associative up to quasi-isomorphism (see
[
BC17
]
in section 3.1.1). Adding gradings requires to specify brackets in the iterated mapping cone. A graded cobordism
V
induces preferred gradings on the ends
L_{j}
, see section
4.6
, and leads to a graded version of Theorem
1.1
which is formulated as Theorem
4.5
.

One may ask for the minimal set of objects needed to write all objects as iterated cone decomposition. The strongest known generation results are that

•

thimbles generate the Fukaya category of Lefschetz fibrations
[
Sei08
]
,

•

Lagrangian cocores generate the wrapped Fukaya category of Weinstein manifolds
[
CGGR19
,
GPS19
]
.

Some information on the triangulated structure of an
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{A}
is captured by the
Grothendieck group

K_{0}(D(\mathcal{A}))
of its derived category
D(\mathcal{A})
. This group is the quotient of the free abelian group generated by objects of
D(\mathcal{A})
modulo the relation
A_{1}-A_{2}+A=0
whenever
A
is the mapping cone of a morphism
A_{1}\to A_{2}
in
D(\mathcal{A})
.

On the other hand Biran and Cornea introduced the group
\Omega(W)
, called the
Lagrangian cobordism group
of a Liouville manifold
W
. It is the abelian group freely generated by admissible Lagrangian submanifolds in
W
modulo the relation
L_{0}=L_{1}+\cdots+L_{n}
whenever there is a Lagrangian cobordism with ends
L_{0},\ldots,L_{n}
. According to Theorem
1.1
, Lagrangian cobordisms produce cone decompositions in the Fukaya category. So all relations between Lagrangian submanifolds defining the cobordism group also exist in the relations that define the Grothendieck group. In other words, the cobordism group
\Omega(W)
surjects onto the Grothendieck group
K_{0}(D\mathcal{W}(W))
of the derived wrapped Fukaya category of
W
.

One may wonder under what assumptions the cobordism group is actually isomorphic to its Grothendieck group. The only known cases are closed surfaces: Haug
[
Hau15
]
proved that it is indeed an isomorphism in case of the torus, when working with the set of admissible Lagrangian submanifolds being non-contractible embedded curves. Perrier
[
Per19
]
studied higher genus surfaces and proved partial results for the case of unobstructed immersed curves.

In surfaces with boundary exact conical Lagrangian submanifolds are either embedded arcs connecting boundary points or exact closed curves in the interior. The second result of this text is:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a Riemann surface with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, obtained from a closed Riemann surface by removing finitely many open discs. Then there are isomorphisms

\Omega(\Sigma)\cong K_{0}(D\mathcal{W}(\Sigma))\cong H_{1}(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma).

A priori this statement may depend on the Liouville structure that we chose on
\Sigma
. However, on Riemann surfaces with boundary any two Liouville structures which induce the same orientation are equivalent as Liouville manifolds after completing the Liouville flow. Hence the result does not depend on the choice of a Liouville form on
\Sigma
.

Finally, note that Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
will be proved in the generalized setting with stops allowed on the Liouville boundary, see Theorem
4.5
and Theorem
5.3
, respectively.

1.1.
Cobordism groups in higher dimensions

In some cases the full classification of exact conical Lagrangian submanifolds is known. Most examples that we state here come from where the Nearby Lagrangian conjecture (for the zero section) or the analogous Nearby Lagrangian conjecture for fibers in cotangent bundles are known.

Denote
\Omega^{c}(W)
the Lagrangian cobordism group of
W
with admissible Lagrangian submanifolds being closed and exact. The following groups are known:

•

Gromov
[
Gro85
]
proved that there are no compact exact Lagrangian submanifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
for
n>0
. Hence:

\Omega^{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})=0.

•

More generally, for subcritical Weinstein manifolds
W
:

\Omega^{c}(W)=0

by general position and by applying Theorem
4.5
to the null-cobordisms coming from Proposition
3.11
.

•

The Nearby Lagragian conjecture is known for the cotangent bundle of the sphere
S^{2}
(see Hind
[
Hin12
]
, and
[
LS91
,
Vit97
,
Rit09
]
for earlier related results) and the cotangent bundle of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
(
[
DRGI16
]
). That is, all closed exact Lagrangian submanifolds are Hamiltonianly isotopic to the zero section. Hence:

\Omega^{c}(T^{*}S^{2})\cong\mathbb{Z}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\Omega^{c}(T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{2})\cong\mathbb{Z}

are generated by the zero sections, respectively (they cannot be torsion as the zero section in the Grothendieck group is not torsion and the cobordism group maps to the Grothendieck group of the Fukaya category.)

Let
\Lambda
be a Legendrian submanifold in the Liouville boundary
\partial W
of a Liouville manifold
W
. Denote
\Omega(W,\Lambda)
the Lagrangian cobordism group of
W
with admissible Lagrangian submanifolds being exact and having boundary
\partial L=\Lambda\subset\partial W
at infinity and relations given by Lagrangian cobordisms having admissible Lagrangian ends.

For the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\Sigma
of an open surface
\Sigma
the Nearby Lagrangian conjecture is known, i.e. every conical exact Lagrangian which is equal to a fiber outside a compact set is Hamiltonianly isotopic to the fiber by a compactly supported Hamiltonian (Côté and Rizell
[
CR20
]
; see also
[
EP96
]
for earlier related work). Let
\Lambda
be the Legendrian boundary of a fiber in
\partial W
. Hence

\Omega(T^{*}\Sigma,\Lambda)\cong\mathbb{Z}

is generated by the fiber. In subsection
5.4
we discuss some more techniques to find interesting relations in cobordism groups.

1.2.
Strategy of the proofs

A refinement of the wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville manifold is the
partially
wrapped Fukaya category. A
stop
is a closed subset of the Liouville boundary
\partial W
of a Liouville manifold
W
. The name stop already suggests that in the space of morphisms we do not take into account Reeb orbits that intersect the stop. Put differently, we do not wrap Lagrangian submanifolds over stops. This extra structure turns out to be very useful in the context of Lagrangian cobordisms. Given a Lagrangian cobordism
V\subset\mathbb{C}\times W
with
n+1
ends we can put a stop
\mathfrak{f}_{n}
on the Liouville boundary
\partial\mathbb{C}
which consists of
n+1
points, one point between each end.

In
[
GPS19
]
it is shown that for
\mathbb{C}
equipped with the stop
\mathfrak{f}_{n}
and any stopped Liouville manifolds
(W,\mathfrak{g})
there is a cohomologically full and faithful Künneth
A_{\infty}
-bifunctor

\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{f}_{n}}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{g}}(W)\to\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{h}}(\mathbb{C}\times W)

where
\mathfrak{h}
is the product stop

\mathfrak{h}=(\mathfrak{f}_{n}\times\mathrm{sk}(W))\cup(\mathfrak{f}_{n}\times\mathfrak{g})\cup(\mathrm{sk}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathfrak{g}).

The set
\mathrm{sk}(W)
denotes the skeleton of
W
. The functor sends a pair
(\alpha,L)
to the conicalization
\alpha\tilde{\times}L
of the product Lagrangian
\alpha\times L
in the product Liouville manifold
\mathbb{C}\times W
. We refer to section
4.7.1
for the precise formulation.

A Lagrangian cobordism
V
defines an object in
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{h}}(\mathbb{C}\times W)
. Let
\mathcal{Y}(V)
be the associated Yoneda module. Each arc
\alpha
in
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{f}_{n}}(\mathbb{C})
induces a functor
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{g}}(W)\to\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{h}}(\mathbb{C}\times W)
. Pulling back the Yoneda module
\mathcal{Y}(V)
along this functor, yields a
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{g}}(W)
-module
M_{\alpha,V}
that depends only, up to a quasi-isomorphism, on where (between which stops) the conical ends of the arc
\alpha
lie.

This organizes as a functor

\mathrm{ev}_{V}:\mathcal{W}_{f_{n}}(\mathbb{C})^{opp}\to mod_{W_{\mathfrak{g}}(W)}

sending
\alpha
to
M_{\alpha,V}
. The partially wrapped Fukaya category
\mathcal{W}_{f_{n}}(\mathbb{C})^{opp}
serves as the universal model of cone decompositions. Exact triangles of arcs in
\mathbb{C}
give rise to exact triangles of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{g}}(W)
-modules under the
A_{\infty}
-functor
\mathrm{ev}_{V}
, as
A_{\infty}
-functors respect exact triangles. Picking and isotoping the right arcs in
\mathbb{C}
as in
[
BC14
]
concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For Theorem
1.2
we follow
[
HKK17
]
. The argument is based on the contractibility of the arc complex for surfaces with boundary
[
Har85
,
Har86
]
, it is completely topological.
A
full arc system

A
on a Riemann surface
\Sigma
with boundary is a set of non-isotopic arcs such that when cutting the surface along all arcs in
A
we are left with topological disks. In
[
HKK17
]
it is shown that the Grothendieck group of the derived wrapped Fukaya category of a Riemann surface is generated by the elements of an arc system. In fact, a full arc system generates any exact closed curve and any arc in
\Sigma
as an iteration of the following operations

•

Two arcs
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are identified if they are isotopic relative
\partial\Sigma,

•

An arc
\gamma
is
0
if it can be isotoped into the boundary of
\partial\Sigma
,

•

\gamma=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
if
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are curves intersecting exactly once and transversely with intersection index
1
, and the resulting surgery is
\gamma
.

All these operations can be realized by cobordisms. Thus the Grothendieck group and the cobordism group coincide. Note that it is not necessary to include Hamiltonian isotopies of closed curves, only isotopies of arcs (which are always Hamiltonian). This is possible because the surgery of a closed curve that intersects an arc only once is an arc, i.e. we can present any closed curves as the sum of two arcs in the cobordism group.

1.3.
Relation to other work

Independently of Biran and Cornea’s work, Nadler and Tanaka proposed a theory of Lagrangian cobordisms in Liouville manifolds in
[
NT20
]
. Tanaka developed this theory further in various follow-up papers
[
Tan21
,
Tan16a
,
Tan16b
,
Tan18
,
Tan20
]
.

Biran and Cornea’s goal was from the beginning motivated by Floer theory culminating in the functorial way that cobordisms induce exact triangles. On the other hand, Nadler and Tanaka a priori view Lagrangian cobordisms as morphisms in a stable
\infty
-category, where mapping cones exist intrinsically.

This work is Floer-theoretically oriented. That is, we try to redo Biran and Cornea’s work in the setting of Liouville manifolds with (possibly non-compact) exact conical Lagrangian and its consequences in wrapped Floer theory. Moreover, the definition of cobordism given here suffices to compute the cobordism group of all stopped Riemann surfaces.

Tanaka’s arguments relating Lagrangian cobordisms to Floer theory
[
Tan16a
,
Tan16b
]
are given without using the language of stops. We incorporate stops in our arguments following
[
SS21
,
GPS20
,
GPS19
]
to advertise this more recent notion. We also show how and when products of Lagrangian submanifolds should be conicalized in Lagrangian cobordism theory.

In our definition, a Lagrangian cobordism consists of conicalized products of Lagrangian submanifolds over the complement of a large compact set of
\mathbb{C}
. On
\mathbb{C}
we put any Liouville structure. Nadler and Tanaka work with
\mathbb{C}
as the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathbb{R}
. Their cobordism have a time direction. Ours have a rotational symmetry (also discussed in
[
Tan19
]
). The topology of their Lagrangian cobordism are only not allowed to go arbitrarily far into the positive cotangent direction (they call this
skeleton-avoiding
or
non-characteristic
). Cobordisms come there with a direction from negative real part to positive real part. So they are empty in the far lower half plane part, consist of two Lagrangian ends on the real line and are arbitrarily wild in the upper half plane of
\mathbb{C}
.
These cobordism induce not directly cone decompositions, they are regarded as morphisms. Furthermore, their Lagrangian cobordism
\infty
-category has higher morphisms living in
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}\times W
. The papers
[
Tan16a
,
Tan16b
]
prove that these morphisms induce Floer theoretic morphisms.

To unify these two approaches one may want to jump to section
4.5.6
for references on how stabilization of Liouville manifolds, cone decompositions and Lagrangian cobordisms fit into a larger picture.

1.4.
Structure of the text

In section
2
we briefly revise some basic constructions in Liouville manifolds that prepare the definition of Lagrangian cobordisms. In section
3
we define Lagrangian cobordisms in the framework of Liouville manifolds and give some examples. Section
4
recalls the construction of partially wrapped Fukaya categories, in particular of a disk with stops on the boundary and proves the cone decomposition stated in Theorem
1.1
. The Lagrangian cobordism groups of non-compact Riemann surfaces are discussed in section
5
.

2.
Some Constructions in Liouville Manifolds

A
Liouville domain

W^{int}
is a compact exact symplectic manifold
(W^{int},\omega)
with contact boundary
\partial W
. More precisely, we fix a 1-form
\lambda
, called
Liouville form
, such that
\omega=d\lambda
. The contact boundary assumption means that the vector field
Z
, called the
Liouville vector field
, uniquely defined by
\iota_{Z}\omega=\lambda
, points strictly outward the boundary
\partial W
of
W^{int}
. Denote
\alpha=\lambda|_{\partial W}
the contact form. A
stop

\mathfrak{f}
is a closed subset of the boundary
\partial W
. As the name suggests, all the dynamics that we are going to consider avoid the stop.

By flowing inwards from the boundary
\partial W
along the Liouville vector field
Z
with negative time
t
one obtains the negative part of the symplectization
(0,1]\times\partial W\subset W^{int}
of
(\partial W,\alpha)
. The first coordinate is called the radial coordinate
r=e^{t}\in(0,1]
. To make the vector field
Z
complete we glue the positive part
W^{cone}=[1,\infty)\times\partial W
of the symplectization (called
conical part
) to
W^{int}
and denote this (non-compact) manifold by

W=W^{int}\cup_{\partial W}W^{cone}.

On the symplectization part
(0,\infty)\times\partial W
the Liouville form is given by
\lambda=r\alpha
.
All information on the boundary
\partial W
should extend to the cone
W^{cone}
by using the Liouville flow. In presence of a stop
\mathfrak{f}\subset\partial W
we denote its extension
[1,\infty)\times\mathfrak{f}
to the cone
W^{cone}
also simply by
\mathfrak{f}
.

Manifolds that are completions of Liouville domains are called
Liouville manifolds
. It is convenient to be flexible on where the conical part
W^{cone}
starts. Our main object of interest are stopped Liouville manifolds
(W,\lambda,\mathfrak{f})
without fixing the Liouville domain
W^{int}
and the start of the stop
\mathfrak{f}
. If a property is true on the cone
W^{cone}
for some choice of Liouville domain
W^{int}\subset W
then we say the property holds
at infinity
. Note also that the manifold
W
has no boundary. The
Liouville boundary

\partial W
is just a (non-fixed) choice of a contact hypersurface on which the conical part of
W
is modelled.

The set

\mathrm{sk}(W)=W\setminus\big((0,\infty)\times\partial W\big)

is called the
skeleton
of
W
, i.e. the points that do not escape to infinity under the Liouville flow. The skeleton is compact, possibly singular, but independent of the choice of the Liouville boundary
\partial W
.

2.1.
Linear Hamiltonians

As the conical part of a Liouville manifold is the symplectization of the contact manifold, we restrict to Hamiltonian functions which induce contact dynamics at infinity.

Let
(W,\lambda)
be a Liouville manifold and
(\partial W,\alpha)
a choice of a contact manifold that models the conical part of
W
. Denote
\xi=\ker\alpha
the contact structure on
\partial W
.
Let us recall the relation of contact dynamics on the Liouville boundary
\partial W
to dynamics on the Liouville manifold
W
at infinity.
The following spaces are in bijection:

•

Functions
h_{t}
on
\partial W
,

•

Vector fields
v_{t}
on
\partial W
that are lifts of sections of
T(\partial W)/\xi
,

•

An isotopy
\psi_{t}
of
\partial W
starting at
\psi_{0}=id_{\partial W}
such that
\psi_{t}
preserves the contact structure
\xi
for all times
t
.

They are called
contact Hamiltonians
h_{t}
, contact vector fields
v_{t}
and contact isotopies
\psi_{t}
, respectively.

The relations among them are given by:

\alpha(v_{t})=h_{t}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\iota_{v_{t}}d\alpha=dh_{t}(R)\alpha-dh_{t}

where
R
is the
Reeb vector field
of
(\partial W,\alpha)
, i.e. the vector field uniquely defined by
\alpha(R)=1
and
\iota_{R}d\alpha=0
. The isotopy
\psi_{t}
is the flow of the vector field
v_{t}
.
As all the
\psi_{t}
preserve
\xi=\ker\alpha
, we must have that
\psi_{t}^{*}\alpha=f_{t}\alpha
for some positive function
f_{t}:\partial W\to\mathbb{R}.
We can recover
f_{t}
by
f_{0}=1
and
\partial_{t}f_{t}=dh_{t}(R).

In the situation of a Liouville manifold
(W,\lambda)
with Liouville flow
Z
the following spaces are in bijection:

•

Functions
H_{t}:W\to\mathbb{R}
such that
Z(H_{t})=H_{t}
at infinity,

•

Symplectic vector fields
X_{t}
on
W
that commute with
Z
at infinity,

•

An isotopy
\varphi_{t}
of
W
starting at
\varphi_{0}=id_{W}
such that the
1
-form
\varphi_{t}^{*}\lambda-\lambda
is exact everywhere and vanishes at infinity.

They are called
linear Hamiltonians
H_{t}
, Hamiltonian vector fields of contact type
X_{t}
and Hamiltonian isotopies of contact type
\varphi_{t}
, respectively. We use the convention that the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{t}
associated to a function
H_{t}:W\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
\omega(X_{t},\cdot)=-dH_{t}
and denote the induced flow on
W
by
\varphi_{t}
.

Any contact Hamiltonian
h_{t}
on
\partial W
induces a linear Hamiltonian
H_{t}
on
W
by defining
H_{t}=rh_{t}
at infinity and extending it to
W^{int}
by a cutoff function. Actually, the definition of linear Hamiltonian is equivalent to Hamiltonians that have the form
H_{t}=rh_{t}
at infinity by choosing a cone
W^{cone}
with conical coordinate
r
. Its Hamiltonian vector field
X_{t}
at infinity is
X_{t}(r,z)=(\partial_{t}f_{t},v_{t}(z))
where
v_{t}
is the contact vector field associated to
h_{t}
and the induced Hamiltonian diffeomorphism is of the form
\varphi(r,z)=(rf_{t}(z),\psi_{t}(z))
.

Example 2.1
.

Linear Hamiltonians of the form
H_{t}=r
at infinity (i.e.
h=1
in the previous notation), induce the Hamiltonian vector field
X
which is the Reeb vector field
R
in each of the contact manifolds
({r}\times\partial W,r\alpha)
for fixed
r
.

In case of a stopped Liouville manifold
(W,\lambda,\mathfrak{f})
we demand
H_{t}
to vanish on the set
\mathfrak{f}
at infinity. The corresponding flow
\varphi_{t}
is the identity on
\mathfrak{f}
. So if a subset
L\subset W
does not intersect
\mathfrak{f}
at infinity then
\varphi_{t}(L)
stays disjoint from
\mathfrak{f}
at infinity for all time
t
.

2.2.
Conical Lagrangian submanifolds and conicalizations

Similarly to the choice of linear Hamiltonians, we restrict to Lagrangian submanifolds which are modeled at infinity by Legendrian submanifolds for some choice of a contact Liouville boundary.

In this paper we only consider
exact
Lagrangian submanifolds
L
in Liouville manifolds
(W,\lambda)
. By exact we mean that there is a function
f:L\to\mathbb{R}
, such that

\lambda|_{L}=df.

Such a function
f
is called a
primitive
of
L
.

Given a choice of a Liouville domain
W^{int}\subset W
define

\partial L=L\cap\partial W,\qquad L^{int}=L\cap W^{int}\qquad\text{and}\qquad L^{cone}=L\cap W^{cone}

for any Lagrangian submanifold
L\subset W
.

In the contact manifold
(\partial W,\alpha=\lambda|_{\partial W})
we are interested in submanifolds
\Lambda
of
\partial W
which are
Legendrian
, i.e.
T\Lambda\subset T(\partial W)
lies in the contact distribution
\xi=\ker\alpha
.

The corresponding asymptotic notion for Lagrangian submanifolds
L
in
W
is characterized by the following equivalent conditions:

•

\lambda|_{L}
vanishes at infinity,

•

L
is invariant under the Liouville flow at infinity,

•

any primitive of
L
is locally constant at infinity.

These Lagrangian submanifolds are called
conical
1
1

Remark 2.9

Remark 3.3

Let

In this section we argue that the choice of a Liouville structure on

does not restrict the set of cobordisms. More specifically:

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose
\lambda_{0}
is an admissible Liouville structure on
\mathbb{C}
, i.e. has compact skeleton and induces the standard orientation. Suppose
V_{0}
is a Lagrangian cobordism that is exact and conical with respect to a Liouville structure
\lambda_{\mathbb{C}}^{0}+\lambda_{W}
on
\mathbb{C}\times W
and let
\lambda_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
be another admissible Liouville structure on
\mathbb{C}
. Then there is a Lagrangian cobordism

that is exact and conical with respect to the Liouville structure
\lambda_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}+\lambda_{W}
on
\mathbb{C}\times W
with the same ends as
V
.

More precisely,
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
are isotopic through a family of cobordisms that are defined with respect to
\lambda_{\mathbb{C}}^{t}+\lambda_{W}
, where
\lambda_{\mathbb{C}}^{t}
is a smooth family of admissible Liouville structures connecting
\lambda^{0}_{\mathbb{C}}
to
\lambda^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Note that we do not claim that this isotopy is unique in any sense. We are solely interested in its existence so that we can switch between Liouville forms chosen for
\mathbb{C}

Proof.

The strategy of the proof is to first deform the ends of

and then deform the interior of

Fix a Liouville domain

as in the definition of

3.5.
Examples of cobordisms

Remark 3.9

4.
(Partially) Wrapped Floer Theory and Cobordisms

In this section we recall the relevant Floer theory following
[
GPS20
,
GPS19
]
and add some comments about grading. Then before proving the main theorem
4.5
we discuss the partially wrapped Fukaya category of the disk as it serves as a standard model of iterated mapping cones.

4.1.
Floer theory of non-compact Lagrangian submanifolds

The first instance of the open string analog of symplectic homology was when Abbondandolo and Schwarz in
[
AS06
]
defined wrapped Floer homology for fibers in cotangent bundles using a quadratic Hamiltonian. Two possible definitions of wrapped Floer homology and the wrapped Fukaya category of Liouville manifolds, were first sketched in
[
FSS08
]
. The approach using a direct limit over linear Hamiltonians was systematically introduced in
[
AS10
]
, the approach over quadratic Hamiltonians in
[
Abo10
]
. Auroux
[
Aur10a
,
Aur10b
]
introduced a refinement by not allowing to wrap over certain subsets in the contact boundary of the Liouville manifold. This refined version is called partially wrapped Fukaya category (see also
[
Syl19
]
). A third method to define the partially wrapped Fukaya category was introduced in
[
GPS20
]
. The strategy resembles the linear setup from
[
AS10
]
, however, its definition does not involve any Hamiltonian functions. It is directly defined over all exact Lagrangian deformations which are positive at infinity and then localizes with respect to Floer continuation elements. Moreover, the setting in
[
GPS20
]
also allows certain Liouville manifolds with boundary near which the wrapping stops. These manifolds are called
Liouville sectors
. All these constructions are expected to produce equivalent
A_{\infty}
-categories. However, this is not yet rigorously established according to the introduction in
[
CGGR19
]
.

In dimension
2
, the discussion reduces to closed exact curves and arcs in surfaces with boundary. The boundary may be equipped with stops. Stops are points on the boundary, over which arcs are not allowed to wrap. The partially wrapped Fukaya categories for some symmetric products of surfaces are calculated in Auroux’s papers
[
Aur10a
,
Aur10b
]
by relating the computation to Lefschetz fibrations and Seidel’s generation result by thimbles
[
Sei08
]
. There is another equivalent approach
[
Boc16
,
HKK17
]
, called the topological Fukaya category, which is, as the name suggests, purely combinatorial and topological. Moreover,
[
DK18
]
show that triangulations of a surface lead very naturally to the definition of the topological Fukaya category and its derived triangulated structures. Some explicit computation and pictures of partially wrapped Fukaya categories of some Riemann surfaces can be found in
[
LP18
]
.

For our purpose, we mainly rely on the wrapped Fukaya category and theory developed in
[
GPS20
]
. The only explicit calculation we need are exact triangles in the partially wrapped Fukaya category of a disk stopped at finitely many boundary points (see section
4.5
).

4.2.

We give an overview of the construction of partially wrapped Floer theory assuming the definition and the properties of Floer homology

in Floer homology which for small positive exact Lagrangian isotopies corresponds to the unit in

defined as postcomposing with the continuation element

.

We define wrapped Floer homology of exact conical Lagrangian submanifolds

and

as the direct limit

HW(L,K):=\varinjlim_{L\rightsquigarrow L^{+}}HF(L^{+},K)

where the direct limit is taken over all exact Lagrangian isotopies

, where

and

are transverse. The isotopies are partially ordered by the ordering at infinity on Legendrian isotopies
\partial L\rightsquigarrow\partial L^{+}
, the maps given by continuation maps.

Wrapped Floer homology can equivalently be defined by negatively wrapping

or even by simultaneously wrapping
L

K

is invariant under exact conical Lagrangian deformations

. Similarly, we can also vary

through exact conical Lagrangian submanifolds.

4.2.2.
Vanishing of wrapped Floer homology

In this section we record two important vanishing results for wrapped Floer homology.

Two disjoint Lagrangian submanifolds may not have vanishing wrapped Floer homology. However, if the wrapping can be done in a disjoint way, then Floer homology vanishes. In more detail: In
[
GPS20
]
it is shown that for each Lagrangian submanifold

there is a countable

(4.1)

\displaystyle L=L^{(0)}\rightsquigarrow L^{(1)}\rightsquigarrow L^{(2)}\rightsquigarrow\ldots

in the partially ordered set of Lagrangian isotopies

such that Floer homology can be calculated as

for every test Lagrangian

which is transverse to all

. In case there is a cofinal wrapping of

and

to

that avoid the stop

Another vanishing property of wrapped Floer homology that does not follow directly from the definition is the following vanishing result stated in

4.3.1.
Grading

We discuss first the general case and then restrict to the two-dimensional case to complement section

Let
\Lambda_{n}
be the Grassmanian of all linear Lagrangian subspaces in

gives rise to a unitary basis of

with

\det:U_{n}\to S^{1}
descends to a map
\det^{2}:\Lambda_{n}\cong U_{n}/O_{n}\to S^{1}
. The preferred generator

of

is called the

Given a symplectic manifold
(W^{2n},\omega)
one has a fiber bundle
\mathcal{L}^{W}\to W
with fibers
\mathcal{L}^{W}_{z}=\Lambda_{n}(T_{z}W,\omega_{z})
.
To calculate determinants, let

be an
\omega
-compatible almost complex structure on

. Then

is a complex

-bundle and

is a complex line bundle, called the
determinant bundle.

The line bundle

is trivial iff the Chern class
c_{1}(\det(W)^{\otimes 2})=0\in H^{2}(W,\mathbb{Z})
, or equivalently
2c_{1}(W)=0\in H^{2}(W,\mathbb{Z})
. If this condition is satisfied, the line bundle
\det(W)^{\otimes 2}
admits a non-vanishing section
\Theta:W\to\det(W)^{\otimes 2}
, called a
grading
of

Moreover, we get a map

by

\alpha_{\Theta}(z,\Lambda)=\frac{\Theta_{z}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n})^{2}}{|\Theta_{z}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n})^{2}|},

called the
squared phase map
.

Any Lagrangian embedding

induces a map
s_{L}:L\to\mathcal{L}^{W}|_{L}
called the
Gauss map
, given by
z\mapsto D_{z}i_{L}(T_{z}W)
. A

on

is a map
\theta_{L}:L\to\mathbb{R}
which lifts

. Such a lift exists iff
(\alpha_{\Theta}\circ s_{L})_{*}\pi_{1}(L)=0\subset\pi_{1}(S^{1}),
i.e. if the Maslov class
\mu_{L}:=(\alpha_{\Theta}\circ s_{L})^{*}(\mu)\in H^{1}(L)

A grading
\theta_{L}
on
L
can be shifted: Denote
L[1]
the Lagrangian submanifold

equipped with the grading
\theta_{L}-1.

Remark 4.2
.

(Dimension 2)
Let
\Sigma
be an orientable non-compact surface. To get a grading we are interested in non-vanishing holomorphic quadratic differentials, i.e. sections of
(\bigwedge^{1,0}T^{*}W)^{\otimes 2}
. As holomorphic line bundles on non-compact surfaces are trivial, there always exists a non-vanishing quadratic differential
\Theta
. If this section is locally given by
q(z)dz\otimes dz
, it induces a volume form
|q(z)|dx\wedge dy
and a Riemannian metric
\sqrt{|q(z)|}dx\otimes dy
.

One can perform a coordinate change

such that the quadratic differential is

. Transition functions on overlaps of two charts are then forced to be

z\mapsto\pm z+c
for

. The induced metric is flat. Moreover, it comes with a choice of horizontal foliation given by horizontal lines in a local chart
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. See the textbook
[
Str84
]
for more details.

In fact, there is a bijection between the following structures

•

complex structures together with a non-vanishing holomorphic quadratic differential

•

flat Riemannian metrics together with a covariantly constant foliation and an orientation.

The stronger assumption of admitting a holomorphic differential leads to the notion of a
translation surface
instead of a
half-translation surface
. On overlapping charts the transitions are
z\mapsto z+c
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, the corresponding horizontal foliation lifts to an oriented foliation, a vector field. To avoid the use of local coefficients with respect to an orientation double cover on
\Sigma
(see
[
HKK17
]
) we can restrict to foliations coming from a vector field (see
[
LP20
]
for a characterization when a particular foliation lifts).

A grading of a curve
\gamma:L\to\Sigma
is a map
\theta:L\to\mathbb{R}
, such that
\Theta(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t))\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}e^{2\pi i\theta(t)}
(when identifying the holomorphic cotangent bundle with the real cotangent bundle). More generally, in terms of a foliation

, a grading is a homotopy class of paths between
\eta(\gamma(t))
and
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
varying continuously with

. The grading induced by a holomorphic quadratic differential is given by the paths from
\eta(\gamma(t))
to
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
by
s\mapsto e^{\pi i\theta(t)s}
for every
t
assuming that the foliation
\eta
is horizontal. A graded curve is oriented by
t\mapsto e^{\pi i\theta(t)}
. Note that the shift
\gamma[1]
has reversed orientation.

An intersection point

of two transversely intersecting graded curves
(\gamma_{1},\theta_{1})
and

can be

-graded by

where

4.8.

Biran and Cornea

]

The objects of the
cobordism category

of a stopped Liouville manifold
(W,\lambda,\mathfrak{f})
are tuples
(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})
of exact conical Lagrangian submanifolds

that avoid

and are equipped with some extra structure. A morphism

that has ends

(in counterclockwise order) and avoids

which will be decomposed by the other ends
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}
.
A morphism
[V]:(L_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,L^{\prime}_{m})\to(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})
from a general tuple is an ordered tuple
([V_{1}],\ldots,[V_{m}])
of morphisms

By the discussion in section
3.3

live in

with the same Liouville structure on
\mathbb{C}
, are disjoint and if projected to

ordered from lower half plane to upper half plane in
\mathbb{C}
. Hence,
V
is a cobordism on its own with ends

L^{\prime}_{m},\ldots,L^{\prime}_{1},L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}.

The composition of two morphisms
[V]
and

is given by gluing ends, see section

to an end in a Lagrangian cobordism in

[
BC14
]
we can make this category into a strict monoidal one by quotiening by void Lagrangians and void cobordisms.

in this section for notational convenience. See

A morphism

In this section we prove Theorem

5.1.
Cobordism group of the cylinder

As a warm-up let us calculate the cobordism group of the cylinder
T^{*}S^{1}
. We claim that all the maps in (
5.1
) are isomorphisms.

As the cylinder is a cotangent bundle its Grothendieck group is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
and is generated by a fiber (see
[
Abo11
]
). On the other hand, there are four smooth isotopy classes of embedded non-oriented curves and arcs. They can be represented by the zero section, a fiber, an arc
\gamma_{+}
with both ends at
+\infty
in the cotangent coordinate and an arc
\gamma_{-}
with both ends at
-\infty
. In
T^{*}S^{1}
any closed exact Lagrangian submanifold is in fact exact Lagrangian isotopic to the zero section. For arcs in a surface, smooth isotopies and exact Lagrangian isotopies are the same as
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})=0.
This classifies conical exact Lagrangian submanifolds up to exact Lagrangian isotopy.

The arcs
\gamma_{-}
and
\gamma_{+}
are both null-cobordant as Proposition
3.11
shows. Moreover, when we perform a surgery between the zero section and a fiber we get a Dehn twist which is Hamiltonianly isotopic through a linear Hamiltonian to the fiber again. Consequently, the cobordism group is at most one dimensional, generated by a fiber. As
K_{0}(\mathcal{W}(T^{*}S^{1}))\cong\mathbb{Z}
is generated by the fiber the maps in (

) must all be isomorphisms.

5.2.
Surfaces with boundary

Before looking at surfaces with boundary, let us state the known results that relate the Grothendiek group of the Fukaya category of closed surfaces to its cobordism group.

The cobordism group of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
has been calculated in
[
Hau15
]
(a further discussion of higher genus surfaces can be found in
[
Per19
]
). When the Lagrangian submanifolds are non-contractible embedded circles in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
then

\Omega(\mathbb{T}^{2})\cong K_{0}(\mathrm{Fuk}(\Sigma))\cong H_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\oplus\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}.

The latter identification is not canonical. The extra term
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
roughly arises as flux by fixing a circle and measure the area swept out when isotoping to another circle. This term will not arise in the case of surfaces with boundary as we will restrict to exact curves. Exact Lagrangian isotopies of circles do always sweep out zero area.

Suppose now that
\Sigma
is a closed Riemann surface with finitely many open disks removed, called a Riemann surface of finite type with boundary
\partial\Sigma
. Moreover, let
\mathfrak{f}
be a finite subset of
\partial\Sigma
, where arcs are stopped.
The wrapped Fukaya category of punctured surfaces has been calculated in
[
AAE+13
,
Boc16
]
and was generalized in
[
HKK17
]
to include stops on the boundary.
Open Riemann surfaces are Stein (see
[
CE12
]
), so we can equip each surface
\Sigma
with a Weinstein structure and speak about exact Lagrangian submanifolds. Recall that the space of Liouville structures on an oriented punctured surface is convex. By
[
CE12
]
any two complete Liouville structures are exact symplectomorphic. So it is enough to calculate the partially wrapped Fukaya category with respect to one particular complete Liouville structure.

A
full arc system

A
is a finite set of pairwise disjoint, non-isotopic arcs in
\Sigma
such that
A
cuts
\Sigma
into topological disks that each contain at most one point of the stop
\mathfrak{f}
. In
[
HKK17
]
it is described how to deduce from the contractibility of the arc complex (see
[

,
Har86
,
Hat91
]
) that any full arc system generates the partially wrapped Fukaya category
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)
and that there is an isomorphism

K_{0}(\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma))\cong H_{1}(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f}).

Following the argument in
[
Hat91
]
we can show that the relations that define
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)
are enough to recover the relations in the Grothendieck group of the partially wrapped Fukaya category
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)
. In particular, the cobordism group and the Grothendieck group agree.

Theorem 5.3
.

We have isomorphisms of groups

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\cong\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\cong K_{0}(\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma))\cong H_{1}(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f}).

sending an arc to its class in relative homology.
Moreover, any full arc system
A
in
\Sigma
generates each of these groups.

Proof.

The proof is purely topological and combinatorial. There is a well-defined surjective map of groups

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\to H_{1}(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f})

taking an arc or an exact closed curve to its homology class. Arcs that can be flowed away from any compact set by the Liouville flow are isotopic to an arc in
\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f}
and are therefore zero in relative homology. Note also that surgery of two Lagrangian submanifolds intersecting transversely at one point is equivalent to their sum in the first homology group.

Fix a full arc system
A
on
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
. As the complement of
A
in
\Sigma
consists only of disks,
A
can be shown to generate
H_{1}(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f})
by considering cellular homology and relations
L_{1}+\cdots+L_{n}=0
for any disk which is bounded by
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}
(and some boundary arcs in
\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f}
). Furthermore, all the relations in cellular homology can be realized by surgeries close to the boundary
\partial\Sigma
(see Figure

Remark 5.4
.

In section
5.2
and hence Theorem
5.3
we assumed that the stop
\mathfrak{f}
is a finite subset of
\partial\Sigma
. The cases where the stop
\mathfrak{f}
contains entire boundary circles is not covered by Theorem
5.3
but interesting as well. For example, the case
\mathfrak{f}=\partial\Sigma
is equivalent to allow only compact exact curves in the Fukaya category, no arcs. This case is not covered by Theorem
5.3
and needs to be analyzed more carefully.

In the case of the cylinder
\Sigma=T^{*}S^{1}
with stop
\mathfrak{f}=\partial\Sigma
the statement

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\cong\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\cong K_{0}(\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma))\cong H_{1}(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f})\cong H_{1}(\Sigma)\cong\mathbb{Z}

is still true with generator the zero section.

However, already for the pair of pants
\Sigma
the three isotopy classes coming from the three boundary components
\partial\Sigma
contain each a representative of an exact circle. But these three circles can never intersect pairwise in only one transverse intersection, we do not get a relation by surgery. Hence

\mathbb{Z}^{3}\cong\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\not\cong H_{1}(\Sigma)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

Sometimes it is helpful to have a full arc system with minimal number of arcs. That is, we would like to have an arc system that divides a stopped surface into a minimal number of topological disks.

Corollary 5.5
.

For any connected stopped surface
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
with
\mathfrak{f}\neq\emptyset
there is a full arc system
A
such that cutting the surface along the arcs in
A
results in
|\mathfrak{f}|
topological disks with each disk containing exactly one point in
\mathfrak{f}
. In this case

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{|A|}.

We call such a full arc system
A

minimal
.

To construct a minimal arc system, note that any surface with boundary can be obtained by identifying sides starting from one polygon. Add one arc for each two identified sides plus add linking disks for all but one stop.

Using these minimal full arc systems we can conclude the following:

Corollary 5.6
.

(Stop removal)
Suppose
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
is a stopped surface with stop
\mathfrak{f}\neq\emptyset
. Let
p\in\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f}
. Then

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}\cup\{p\}}(\Sigma)\cong\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\oplus\mathbb{Z}.

On the other hand if
\mathfrak{f}=\emptyset
then adding a point
p\in\partial\Sigma
to
\mathfrak{f}
does not change the cobordism group:

\Omega_{\{p\}}(\Sigma)\cong\Omega(\Sigma).

The second assertion follows because any full arc system for
\Sigma
stays a full arc system for
\Sigma
stopped at a point which is not lying on any of the arcs in the system.

5.3.
Gluing surfaces with boundary

Wrapped Fukaya categories are expected to be well-behaved under gluing. The major known result is the homotopy pushout formula of
[
GPS19
]
where a Liouville sector is divided along a hypersurface into two Weinstein sectors. A special case of this is the operation of attaching Weinstein handles. For surfaces, more concrete tools are at hand. In dimension two, gluing of two surfaces can be understood from the point of view of gluing the skeletons. This is entirely topological. The works of
[
DK18
,
Dyc17
,
PS19
]
illustrate how gluing wrapped Fukaya category in dimension two boils down to gluing graphs along half-infinite edges. This corresponds to attaching a one-handle connecting two points in the boundary of stopped surfaces. We have seen in section
5.2
that for surfaces with boundary the cobordism group and the Grothendieck group of the wrapped Fukaya category both agree with the first homology group. The statement of the following section are compatible with these isomorphisms. The given arguments are a version of relative cellular homology using disjoint 1-cells, namely the arcs.

5.3.1.
Linear gluing

We use the following model for attaching a Weinstein one-handle in dimension two: A one-handle is described by the unit disk
D\subset\mathbb{C}
with stop given by two points
\pm i
on the boundary. Suppose
\Sigma
is a (possibly disconnected) surface with boundary
\partial\Sigma
and stop
\mathfrak{f}\subset\partial\Sigma
containing at least two points
q_{\pm}
. We can deform the Liouville vector field on each of the surfaces
D
and
\Sigma
such that it is parallel to the boundary in a neighbourhood of
\pm i
and
q_{\pm}
, respectively. Then we can identify a small interval in
\partial D
containing
+i
with a small interval in
\partial\Sigma
containing
q_{+}
, and similarly for
-i
and
q_{-}
. The intervals in the resulting stopped surface
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{f}\setminus\{q_{\pm}\}
) are called the
cocores
of the one-handle. As they are Hamiltonianly isotopic we speak of one cocore of the one-handle.

The effect of a handle attachment on cobordism groups (and analogously on the Grothendieck group as well as the relative homology) is the following:

Proposition 5.7
.

Suppose the connected stopped surface
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
arises by attaching a one-handle to stopped surface
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
at the points
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathfrak{f}
.

Then if
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
there is an exact sequence

0\to\Omega_{\pm i}(D)\to\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\to\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}}(\Sigma^{\prime})\to 0,

where the second map comes from the inclusion of stopped surfaces. Denoting by
\gamma
an arc generating
\Omega_{\pm i}(D)
, then
\gamma
is mapped to a linking disk
\gamma_{-}
of
q_{-}
minus a linking disk
\gamma_{+}
of
q_{+}
. The second map sends both of
\gamma_{\pm}
to the cocore
\gamma^{\prime}
of the handle attachment.

In the case
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}=\emptyset
the inclusion
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})\to(\Sigma^{\prime},\emptyset)
induces an isomorphism

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\to\Omega(\Sigma^{\prime}).

Proof.

We will give a proof by using minimal full arc systems as defined in Corollary
5.5
. By abuse of notation we identify arcs in
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
and
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
which is possible whenever the arcs do not intersect the cocore
\gamma^{\prime}
. In particular, to conclude the statement for non-empty stop
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}
we construct a minimal arc systems for
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
such that adding one more arc chosen from
\{\gamma_{\pm}\}
is a minimal arc systems for
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
. The stopped surface
(\Sigma^{\prime}\setminus\gamma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
is homeomorphic to
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f}\setminus\{q_{\pm}\})
by definition of the handle attachment.

Suppose first that
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
and
\Sigma
is connected. In this case
(\Sigma^{\prime}\setminus\gamma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
admits a minimal full arc system
A^{\prime}
by Corollary

. Note that
A^{\prime}\cup\{\gamma^{\prime}\}
is a minimal full arc system for
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
and
A^{\prime}\cup\{\gamma_{\pm}\}
is a minimal full arc system for
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
.

Again suppose that
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
but now that
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
has two connected components
(\Sigma_{-},\mathfrak{f}_{-}\cup\{q_{-}\})
and
(\Sigma_{+},\mathfrak{f}_{+}\cup\{q_{+}\})
. Suppose
\mathfrak{f}_{-},\mathfrak{f}_{+}
are both non-empty. Then
(\Sigma_{-},\mathfrak{f}_{-})
and
(\Sigma_{+},\mathfrak{f}_{+})
both admit minimal full arc systems
A_{-}
and
A_{+}
, respectively. In this case
A_{-}\cup A_{+}\cup\{\gamma^{\prime}\}
is a minimal full arc system for
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
and
A_{-}\cup A_{+}\cup\{\gamma_{\pm}\}
is a minimal full arc system for
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
. We are missing the case when one of
\mathfrak{f}_{-}
,
\mathfrak{f}_{+}
is empty, say
\mathfrak{f}_{+}=\emptyset
. Then we can find a minimal full arc system
A_{-}
for
(\Sigma_{-},\mathfrak{f}_{-})
and a minimal full arc system
A_{+}
for
(\Sigma_{+},\{q_{+}\})
. Then

is a minimal full arc system for
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
and
A_{-}\cup A_{+}\cup\{\gamma_{-}\}
is a minimal full arc system for
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
.

Finally, we reduce the case
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}=\emptyset
to the previous cases by enlarging the stop
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}
(and hence

) by one point

. We have an exact sequence

But according to Corollary

there are isomorphisms

which are compatible with all maps as we only deal with inclusions of stopped surfaces.
∎

5.3.2.
Circular gluing

Another way to decompose surfaces with boundary are pair of pants decompositions. Put differently, we are interested in what happens when we glue two surfaces along a circle.
[
PS19
,
Lee15
]
show that Wrapped Fukaya categories behave well under these decompositions.

We take here the following model: let
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
be a (possibly disconnected) stopped surface and
C_{-},C_{+}\subset\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f}
two disjoint circles. Gluing these two circles can be done in the following three steps: Add two points
q_{-}\in C_{-},q_{+}\in C_{+}
to the set of stops
\mathfrak{f}
. Connect
q_{-}
and
q_{+}
by attaching a one-handle as described in section
5.3.1
and call the resulting circle
C
. Glue in a disk by identifying its boundary with
C
.

To prove the circular gluing result we need the following lemma:

Lemma 5.8
.

Suppose a stopped surface
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{f})
arises by gluing a disk
D
to a boundary circle
C\subset\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f}
of a stopped surface
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
. Then

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\cong\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma^{\prime})\oplus\mathbb{Z}.

The effect of this operation on the skeleton of the surface is collapsing a circle to a point.

Proof.

By adding a point to
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}
we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. Let
A^{\prime}
be a minimal full arc system for
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime})
. Thinking of
\Sigma
as a subset of
\Sigma^{\prime}
we can assume that
A^{\prime}
is disjoint from the disk
D
as
D
is contractible. Cutting
\Sigma
along the arcs in
A^{\prime}
leaves topological discs containing exactly one point of the stop, except of an annulus with one point of the stop (the component that contains
D
). Then adding one more arc which cuts this annulus into a disk to
A^{\prime}
is a minimal full arc system for
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
.
∎

The discussion at the beginning of this subsection concludes the following statement for circular gluing by first applying Corollary
5.6
, then Proposition
5.7
and finally Lemma
5.8
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose a connected stopped surface
(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{f})
arises by identifying two disjoint boundary circles
C_{-},C_{+}\subset\partial\Sigma\setminus\mathfrak{f}
of a stopped surface
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{f})
.

If either
\Sigma
is connected or
\Sigma
has two connected components and each of them has a non-empty stop then

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\cong\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}}(\Sigma^{\prime}).

If
\Sigma
has two connected components with at most one component having a non-empty stop, then

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(\Sigma)\cong\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}}(\Sigma^{\prime})\oplus\mathbb{Z}.

5.4.
More on Cobordism groups in higher dimensions

In this section we discuss two more techniques to get relations in cobordism groups in higher dimensions and give some examples.

5.4.1.
Relations induced by surgeries and Hamiltonian isotopies

Let
M=S^{n}
and let
F_{q}=T_{q}^{*}S^{n}
be the fiber over a point
q\in S^{n}
. The Dehn twist
\tau(F_{q})
of
F_{q}
is given by the time-
1
flow of the fiber
F_{-q}
with Hamiltonian flow coming from the Hamiltonian
H(q,p)=\rho(|p|)
where
\rho:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth increasing function with
\rho(0)=\rho^{\prime}(0)=0
and
\rho(r)=\frac{r}{2}
for
r>>0
. Note that the Hamiltonian
H
is linear at infinity.

An alternative description of the Dehn twist
\tau(F_{q})
is given by doing Polterovich surgery of the fiber
F_{q}
with the zero section
S^{n}\subset T^{*}S^{n}
.

These two different descriptions produce the following identities in the cobordism group:

F_{q}=F_{-q}=\tau(F_{q})=F_{q}+S^{n}.

We conclude:

Proposition 5.10
.

The zero section
[S^{n}]
is zero in
\Omega(T^{*}S^{n})
.

Let
S\subset M
be a closed submanifold of a closed submanifold
M
. Recall that the conormal bundle

N^{*}S=\{(q,\xi)\in T^{*}_{q}M\text{ where }\xi|_{T_{q}S}=0\}\subset T^{*}M

describes a strongly exact (see page
1
) Lagrangian submanifold of
T^{*}M
.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
(S_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a smooth family of spheres in a manifold
M
such that

a point in

Proof.

Denote

5.4.2.
Künneth formula for cobordism groups

Cobordism groups satisfy a Künneth formula. If
(W_{1},\mathfrak{f}),(W_{2},\mathfrak{g})
are two stopped Liouville manifolds then there is a group homomorphism

(5.3)

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}(W_{1})\times\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}(W_{2})\to\Omega_{\mathfrak{h}}(W_{1}\times W_{2})

given by
(L,K)\mapsto L\tilde{\times}K
, where the product stop

. Indeed, for any Lagrangian cobordism

with ends

and a Lagrangian submanifold

the conicalized product

.

Applying (

Appendix A
The Grothendieck group and cone decompositions

We collect here some remarks about triangulated categories, the Grothendieck group and iterated cone decompositions. More can be found in
[
Wei95
]
,
[
Sei08
]
,
[
BC14
]
(Section 2.6) and
[
Hen20
]
(Appendix A).

The
Grothendieck group

K_{0}(\mathcal{A})
of a triangulated
\mathcal{A}
is the group freely generated by objects in
\mathcal{A}
modulo the relation
A_{1}-A_{2}+A=0
whenever there is an exact triangle

A_{1}\to A_{2}\to A\to A[1]

in
\mathcal{A}
. Every morphism
A_{1}\overset{a}{\to}A_{2}
can be completed to an exact triangle
A_{1}\to A_{2}\to A\to A[1]
where
A
is unique up to isomorphism. In this case, we call
A
the
cone
over
a:A_{1}\to A_{2}
and write
A\cong[A_{1}\overset{a}{\to}A_{2}]
.

An (
n
-step)
iterated cone decomposition
of an object
A
in
\mathcal{A}
is a sequence of exact triangles

(A.1)

\displaystyle A_{j}[-1]\to X_{j-1}\to X_{j}\to A_{j}

for
j=1,\ldots,n
with objects
X_{1}=0,X_{2},\ldots,X_{n-1},X_{n}=A
in
\mathcal{A}
.

See
[
BC14
]
for a more detailed discussion, e.g. when two cone decompositions are isomorphic. Let us record here some simple facts about cone decompositions related to grading which directly follow from the axioms of triangulated categories and the given definitions above:

(1)

An iterated cone decomposition (
A.1
) induces a relation
A=A_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}
in the Grothendieck group
K_{0}(\mathcal{A})
.

(2)

Substituting isomorphic objects we can write the exact triangles (
A.1
) as cones
X_{j}\cong[A_{j}[-1]\to X_{j-1}]
and the iterated cone can be written as

(A.2)

\displaystyle A\cong\big[A_{n}[-1]\to\big[A_{n-1}[-1]\to\cdots\to\big[A_{2}[-1]\to A_{1}\big]\cdots\big]\big].

(3)

Rotating exact triangles and using the isomorphic decomposition

(A.3)

\displaystyle[[X\to Y]\to Z]\cong[X[1]\to[Y\to Z]]

the iterated cone decomposition (
A.2
) can be rotated as

(A.4)

\displaystyle A_{n}\cong\big[A_{n-1}\to\big[A_{n-2}\to\cdots\to\big[A_{1}\to A\big]\cdots\big]\big].

The other rotations of the iterated cone decompositions yield grading shifts with less insight.

(4)

If one of the objects in
A_{j}
admits itself an iterated cone decomposition

A_{j}\cong\big[B_{m}[-1]\to\big[B_{m-1}[-1]\to\cdots\to\big[B_{2}[-1]\to B_{1}\big]\cdots\big]\big]

then we can combine it with (
A.2
) and get an iterated cone decomposition

\displaystyle A\cong\big[A_{n}[-1]\to\cdots\to[A_{j+1}[-1]\to\big[B_{m}[-1]

\displaystyle\to\cdots\to\big[B_{1}[-1]\to[A_{j-1}[-1]\cdots\to A_{1}\big]\cdots\big]\big]\big]\big]

using (
A.3
) several times.
