Low-Complexity Voronoi Shaping for the Gaussian Channel
Shen Li, Ali Mirani, Magnus Karlsson, and Erik Agrell

Abstract
Voronoi constellations (VCs) are finite sets of vectors of a coding lattice enclosed by the translated Voronoi region of a shaping lattice, which is a sublattice of the coding lattice. In conventional VCs, the shaping lattice is a scaled-up version of the coding lattice. In this paper, we design low-complexity VCs with a cubic coding lattice of up to 32 32 dimensions, in which pseudo-Gray labeling is applied to minimize the bit error rate. The designed VCs have considerable shaping gains of up to 1.03 1.03 dB and finer choices of spectral efficiencies in practice. A mutual information estimation method and a log-likelihood approximation method based on importance sampling for very large constellations are proposed and applied to the designed VCs. With error-control coding, the proposed VCs can have higher achievable information rates than the conventional scaled VCs because of their inherently good pseudo-Gray labeling feature, with a lower decoding complexity.

Forney generalized this concept in 1989 to VCs based on an arbitrary lattice partition

[
23

In our conference paper
[
24
]
, we studied VCs with a cubic coding lattice, for which we compared Feng’s, Ferdinand’s, and Kurkoski’s encoding and decoding algorithms. To minimize the BER, we applied pseudo-Gray labeling to these algorithms, and evaluated the performance of the designed VCs in terms of the Gray penalty
[
25
,
26
]
and BER performance for some common multidimensional shaping lattices both in uncoded and coded systems. In our proposed scheme, coding is completely separated from shaping and performed using error-control coding, outside the Voronoi shaping.

We study VCs with a cubic coding lattice for the following three reasons. First, the decoding algorithm is much simpler than for VCs with rescaled coding and shaping lattices
[
11
,
13
]
, since the search for the closest lattice point, which dominates the decoding complexity for high-dimensional lattices, is simple dimension-wise integer rounding for a cubic coding lattice. Second, although the proposed VCs have no coding gain, the shaping gain is still achievable, and the lack of coding gain can be compensated by error-correction coding that is usually performed anyway. After error-correction coding, the proposed VCs can have better BER performance than the conventional scaled VCs because of their inherently good pseudo-Gray labeling feature. Third, for mapping integers to bits, the proposed VCs allow for improved granularity in spectral efficiencies.

In this paper, we extend our work
[
24
]
on the VCs with a cubic coding lattice. We propose a new method based on the concept of importance sampling to estimate the mutual information (MI) for very large constellations, and exemplify it for VCs. This has been considered as a challenging issue for such large constellations since the exact MI calculation requires enumerating all constellation points. The MI of VCs is evaluated here for the first time to our knowledge. The AIR performance of VCs in combination with a low-density parity-check (LDPC) code is also presented. For mapping integers to bits, we improve the granularity in spectral efficiencies by rotating the shaping lattice by
[formula]
and scaling it by
\sqrt{2}
. We also investigate the trade-off between shaping gain and decoding complexity in higher dimensions.

V
Achievable Information Rates

AIRs indicate the maximum amount of information that can be transmitted reliably over the underlying channel. It assumes a perfect channel code with an infinite blocklength, thus can be used as an upper bound of the performance of coded modulation
[
37
]
. For very large VCs, the calculation of MI is challenging, since it requires the coordinates of all constellation points. In this section, we propose an MI estimation method and a log-likelihood (LLR) estimation method for very large constellations and apply these methods to VCs with a cubic coding lattice. The MI of our designed VCs is investigated, and their AIRs are compared with conventional scaled VCs when an LDPC code is applied.

V-A

MI estimation method for very large constellations

For a memoryless discrete channel, the MI between the equally probable transmitted symbols
\boldsymbol{X}
and the received noisy symbols
\boldsymbol{Y}
can be written as

\displaystyle I(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{Y})\triangleq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\log\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}{f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})}d\boldsymbol{y},

(24)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,M
form the constellation
\mathcal{X}
.
By applying Monte Carlo integration, the MI can be approximated as

\displaystyle I(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{\text{s}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{s}}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\log\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}{f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})}d\boldsymbol{y}

(25)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{\text{s}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{s}}}\log\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}{f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})},

(26)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{\text{s}}
are
N_{\text{s}}
symbols drawn uniformly from
\mathcal{X}
, and given a certain
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
,
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is drawn from the conditional distribution of the channel
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
.

To numerically estimate (
26
), the key point is to calculate the marginal distribution of
\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}).

(27)

The exact calculation of
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})
using (
27
) requires storing all
M
constellation points, which is infeasible when
M
is very large. Furthermore, approximating
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})
by standard Monte-Carlo techniques is very inaccurate, because for most realistic channel laws
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
, only a tiny fraction of all constellation points
\boldsymbol{x}
contribute significantly to the sum in (
27
).

To approximate (
27
), we propose a method based on
importance sampling
, which is a weighted sampling method that oversamples from the important region we are interested in, thus making Monte Carlo feasible
[
38
, Ch. 9]
. This concept has been used in the machine learning field to estimated the MI between the original data and the learned representation
[
39
]
, but as far as we know never to estimate the MI of a communication channel. The classic importance sampling approach is to sample from a new importance distribution that is proportional to the product of the distribution of the
integral variable and the integrand
[
38
, Eq. (9.3)]
, to resemble the true expectation. For example, to simulate (
27
) in our case, the importance distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
should be proportional to
(1/M)f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
. For the MI estimation problem, we refine this concept by using a step-wise sampling rule, in which we only sample symbols from an important set
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
which contains points that have an important contribution to
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})
, and no samples are sampled from the complementary set
\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
since their contribution to
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})
is negligible compared with
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
. The important set
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
is divided into
D
disjoint subsets
\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})
for
d=1,\ldots,D
. The contribution to
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})
from each subset
\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})
can be either calculated by enumerating every symbol in
\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})
, or estimated by performing a uniform Monte Carlo sampling in
\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})
. Thus, the sum in (
27
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})

(28)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{|\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})|}{K_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{d}}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}_{d,j}),

(29)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{d,j}
for
j=1,\ldots,K_{d}
are all points from
\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})
if
{K_{d}=|\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})|}
, or
K_{d}
uniform random samples from
\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})
if
{K_{d}<|\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})|}
. For accurate results, the sampling sets
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
and
\mathcal{I}_{d}(\boldsymbol{y})
, but not necessarily their sizes
K_{d}
, should be chosen as functions of
\boldsymbol{y}
.

We propose (
29
) as a very general way to estimate
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})
and thereby the MI and related quantities. The proposed MI estimation method is a special case of importance sampling. Unlike the importance distribution in classical importance sampling, which should be more “continuous”, our refined step-wise sampling rule makes random generation of
\boldsymbol{x}
easier. As the number of the elements in
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
increases, the estimated distribution
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})
in (
28
) should converge to the exact value. As a special case, setting
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{X}
and
D=1
in (
29
) yields the exact expression (
27
) if
K_{1}=M
and a standard Monte Carlo estimate thereof if
K_{1}<M
. The same idea can be applied to other structured constellations, or other similar problems. The readers can derive their own estimation rules from (
29
) for a specific channel and constellation.

V-B

MI estimation for the designed VCs

We consider an
n
-dimensional real AWGN channel, which has the conditional distribution

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2}/n)^{n/2}}\exp{(-\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}/n})},

(30)

where
\sigma^{2}
is the total noise power. We define the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) as
E_{\text{s}}/\sigma^{2}
.

For estimating the MI of our VCs with a cubic coding lattice, first, given a received noisy symbol
\boldsymbol{y}
, we define a Euclidean ball
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y},R)
containing all
n

R
from the nearest integer vector of
\boldsymbol{y}+

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y},R)\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}+\left\lfloor\boldsymbol{y}+\right\rceil\|\leq R,~\boldsymbol{x}+\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\},

(31)

where the squared radius
R^{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. The Euclidean ball
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y},R)
consists of
R^{2}+1
Euclidean “shells”, each of which contains all
n

r
from
\left\lfloor\boldsymbol{y}+\right\rceil
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{y},r)\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}+\left\lfloor\boldsymbol{y}+\rceil\right\|=r,~\boldsymbol{x}+\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\},

(32)

for

. The number of points in each shell

\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y},R)

D

0.5\%

f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})

29

D

f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(D)}(\boldsymbol{y})

D

\displaystyle\max\left(\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(D+1)}(\boldsymbol{y})-f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(D)}(\boldsymbol{y})}{f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(D)}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right)<0.5\%,

(34)

where the maximum is taken over multiple random vectors
\boldsymbol{y}
and multiple Monte-Carlo realizations of (

34

D
throughout the SNR range. Alternatively, (
34

29

f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})
accurately for VCs with up to

points for all SNRs. For larger constellations where we have no benchmarks to compare with, we conjecture that this method is still valid. After having reliable estimated values of
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})

26

V-C

LLR approximation for very large constellations

We propose an LLR approximation method for very large constellations and exemplify it for our designed VCs and the scaled VCs.

For transmission of a constellation
\mathcal{X}
through a given channel with conditional distribution
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
, upon receiving a noisy symbol
\boldsymbol{y}
, the LLR of a certain bit
b_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,\log_{2}(|\mathcal{X}|)
, is defined as

\displaystyle\text{LLR}(b_{i}|\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\triangleq\log{\frac{\text{Pr}(b_{i}=0|\boldsymbol{y})}{\text{Pr}(b_{i}=1|\boldsymbol{y})}}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{(i,0)}}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{(i,1)}}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})},

(35)

where
\mathcal{X}^{(i,0)}
and
\mathcal{X}^{(i,1)}
are the sets of constellation points with
0
and
1
at position
i
, respectively. The exact LLR calculated using the whole constellation is accurate but too complex to compute, which can be approximated by only considering the most likely constellation point with
b_{i}=0
(or
b_{i}=1
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\text{LLR}(b_{i}|\boldsymbol{y})\approx\log\frac{\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{(i,0)}}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{(i,1)}}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}.

(36)

However, for very large constellations, searching for the most likely constellation point with
b_{i}=0
and
b_{i}=1
from the whole constellation is still infeasible.

We propose a similar technique as our MI estimation method in
V-A
to further approximate the LLR. Instead of searching points from
\mathcal{X}
, we only search the closest point from an important set
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
. Specifically, the LLR in (
36
) is further approximated as

\displaystyle\text{LLR}(b_{i}|\boldsymbol{y})\approx\log\frac{\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}^{(i,0)}(\boldsymbol{y})}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}^{(i,1)}(\boldsymbol{y})}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})},

(37)

where
\mathcal{I}^{(i,0)}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{X}^{(i,0)}\cap\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
and
\mathcal{I}^{(i,1)}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{X}^{(i,1)}\cap\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})
. If there is no constellation point in
\mathcal{I}^{(i,0)}(\boldsymbol{y})
(or
\mathcal{I}^{(i,1)}(\boldsymbol{y})
), we assume the most likely constellation point has a small probability, i.e., setting
\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}^{(i,0)}(\boldsymbol{y})}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
(or
\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}^{(i,1)}(\boldsymbol{y})}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
) to a small
default value.

For the AWGN channel, the widely-used approximate LLR derived from (
36
) is

\displaystyle\text{LLR}(b_{i}|\boldsymbol{y})\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}/n}\left(\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{(i,0)}}(\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2})-\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{(i,1)}}(\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2})\right),

(38)

where only the nearest constellation point with
b_{i}=0
(or
b_{i}=1
) to
\boldsymbol{y}
are considered
[
40
, Eq. (6)]
. Analogously with (
37
), we further approximate (
38
) as

\displaystyle\text{LLR}(b_{i}|\boldsymbol{y})\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}/n}\left(\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}^{(i,0)}(\boldsymbol{y})}(\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2})-\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}^{(i,1)}(\boldsymbol{y})}(\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2})\right),

(39)

For our designed VCs with a cubic coding lattice, the important set is defined as in (
33
) with a radius
R
. This parameter provides a trade-off between low computational complexity (small
R
) and good decoding performance (high
R
). If
{\mathcal{I}^{(i,0)}(\boldsymbol{y})=\varnothing}
, then setting
{\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}^{(i,0)}(\boldsymbol{y})}f_{\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}
to a small probability is equivalent to setting
{\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}^{(i,0)}(\boldsymbol{y})}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}}
to a large default value
q
which is larger than
R^{2}
, and the same rule applies to
\mathcal{I}^{(i,1)}(\boldsymbol{y})
. A default value close to the boundary of the important region (e.g.,
q=R^{2}+1
) is usually not a good choice, neither a very large
q
(e.g.,
q=100R^{2}
). The decoding performance can be roughly optimized by testing different
q
for a given
R
.

Similarly, for very large scaled VCs, the important set can be generalized as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{y})\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}+\mathcal{Q}_{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{y}+)\|\leq R,\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma\}.

(40)

Then (
37
) can be used to estimate the LLR for the scaled VCs.

VI
Conclusion

We proposed Voronoi constellations with a cubic coding lattice, for which we follow Kurkoski’s encoding and decoding algorithms and apply pseudo-Gray labeling to minimize the BER. As a structured geometric shaping method, this class of VC has high shaping gains of up to
1.03
dB and low complexity. Thanks to its cubic coding lattice, an MI estimation method for very large constellation size based on importance sampling is proposed for the first time, which enables us to observe an up to
0.85
dB shaping gain for medium SNR values. Our proposed LLR approximation method makes the analysis of AIRs of our VCs in coded systems at high spectral efficiencies possible, which is infeasible for the scaled VCs. Compared with the conventional scaled VCs, our designed VCs have the advantages that 1) the decoding algorithm is simpler, 2) the spectral efficiency for mapping integers to bits is improved to
1
[bit/symbol/dimension-pair] realized by rotating and scaling the shaping lattices, 3) the AIRs after combining with an LDPC code can be higher due to our better pseudo-Gray labeling, and 4) analysing the MI and AIR becomes feasible for very large constellation sizes.
