On the dimension of exceptional parameters for nonlinear projections, and the discretized Elekes-Rónyai theorem
Orit E. Raz, Joshua Zahl

Abstract
We consider four related problems. (1) Obtaining dimension estimates for the set of exceptional vantage points for the pinned Falconer distance problem. (2) Nonlinear projection theorems, in the spirit of Kaufman, Bourgain, and Shmerkin. (3) The parallelizability of planar d d -webs. (4) The Elekes-Rónyai theorem on expanding polynomials. Given a Borel set A A in the plane, we study the set of exceptional vantage points, for which the pinned distance Δ p ​ ( A ) \Delta_{p}(A) has small dimension, that is, close to ( dim A ) / 2 (\dim A)/2 . We show that if this set has positive dimension, then it must have very special structure. This result follows from a more general single-scale nonlinear projection theorem, which says that if ϕ 1 , ϕ 2 , ϕ 3 \phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3} are three smooth functions whose associated 3-web has non-vanishing Blaschke curvature, and if A A is a ( δ , α ) 2 (\delta,\alpha)_{2} -set in the sense of Katz and Tao, then at least one of the images ϕ i ​ ( A ) \phi_{i}(A) must have measure much larger than | A | 1 / 2 |A|^{1/2} , where | A | |A| stands for the measure of A A . We prove analogous results for d d smooth functions ϕ 1 , … , ϕ d \phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d} , whose associated d d -web is not parallelizable. We use similar tools to characterize when bivariate real analytic functions are “dimension expanding” when applied to a Cartesian product: if P P is a bivariate real analytic function, then P P is either locally of the form h ⁡ ( a ⁡ ( x ) + b ⁡ ( y ) ) h(a(x)+b(y)) , or P ⁡ ( A , B ) P(A,B) has dimension at least α + c \alpha+c whenever A A and B B are Borel sets with Hausdorff dimension α \alpha . Again, this follows from a single-scale estimate, which is an analogue of the Elekes-Rónyai theorem in the setting of the Katz-Tao discretized ring conjecture.



1
Introduction

We consider four related problems. (1) Obtaining dimension estimates for the set of exceptional vantage points for the pinned Falconer distance problem. (2) Nonlinear projection theorems, in the spirit of Kaufman, Bourgain, and Shmerkin. (3) The parallelizability of planar
d
-webs. (4) The Elekes-Rónyai theorem on expanding polynomials. We will briefly discuss each of these items.

We will begin with projection theory, and specifically the study of exceptional parameters for nonlinear analogues of the linear projection function
\pi_{e}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is the orthogonal projection onto
\operatorname{span}(e)
. A direction
e\in S^{1}
is called exceptional for a planar set
A
if the dimension of the projection
\pi_{e}(A)
is smaller than the dimension of the projection in a “typical” direction. Results of Kaufman and Bourgain quantify the size of the set of exceptional directions. There are several results concerning nonlinear generalizations of the projection function
\pi_{e}
. An important example of a nonlinear projection is the pinned distance function
d_{p}(q)=|p-q|
. The theme of many of these results is that under suitable constraints, nonlinear projections are as well-behaved as linear projections, with respect to the size of the set of exceptional parameters. We discover a new and unexpected phenomenon, which says that often nonlinear projections are much better behaved than linear ones.

Our results can also be expressed in the discretized setting of
(\delta,\alpha)_{2}
sets, which were introduced by Katz and Tao
[
25
]
as a single-scale model for planar Borel sets that have Hausdorff dimension
\alpha
. In brief, a
(\delta,\alpha)_{2}
set is a union of about
\delta^{-\alpha}
interior-disjoint squares of side-length
\delta
, which satisfy a Frostman-type non-concentration condition. It is straightforward to construct an example of a
(\delta,\alpha)_{2}
set
A
that has projection of size about
|A|^{1/2}
in many different directions, or equivalently, a set that has small image under may different linear functions
\pi_{e}
. We prove that morally speaking, this phenomenon can only occur for linear functions.

The fibers of each function
\pi_{e}
are parallel lines; more generally, we say a set of smooth functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}
determine a
p
arallelizable (planar)
d
-web if after a change of coordinates, the fibers of each function
\phi_{i}
are parallel lines. We prove that if a set of smooth functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}
do not determine a parallelizable planar
d
-web (in a certain quantitative sense), and if
A
is a
(\delta,\alpha)_{2}
set, then at least one of the images
\phi_{i}(A)
must be much larger than
|A|^{1/2}
.

Finally, we shall discuss the connection with the Elekes-Rónyai theorem on expanding polynomials. If
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}
are smooth functions, then after a change of coordinates we can locally write
\phi_{1}(x,y)=x
,
\phi_{2}(x,y)=y
, and
\phi_{3}(x,y)=P(x,y)
for some smooth function
P
. Suppose that
A
is a
(\delta,\alpha)_{2}
set, and that
\phi_{1}(A)
and
\phi_{2}(A)
each have size about
|A|^{1/2}
. If we write
A_{1}=\phi_{1}(A)
and
A_{2}=\phi_{2}(A)
, then
A\subset A_{1}\times A_{2}
, and the sets
A
and
A_{1}\times A_{2}
have comparable size. Following the discussion above, we would like to show that
|P(A)|
is much larger than
|A|^{1/2}
, or almost equivalently, that
P(A_{1}\times A_{2})
is much larger than
|A|^{1/2}
. If
P
was a polynomial and if
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
were discrete sets of cardinality
n
, then Elekes and Rónyai
[
14
]
proved that either
P(A_{1}\times A_{2})
is much larger than
n
, or
P
has a very specific form (for example
P(x,y)=x+y
). We will reduce the results discussed above about non-parallelizable
d
-webs to a discretized analogue of the Elekes-Rónyai theorem for smooth functions.

1.1
Exceptional directions for nonlinear projections

In
[
23
]
, Kaufman proved that if
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a Borel set and if
\pi_{e}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\operatorname{span}(e)
, then
\pi_{e}(A)
can only have small dimension for a small exceptional set of directions
e\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Kaufman)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Borel set and let
s\leq\dim A
. Then

\dim\{e\in\mathbb{S}^{1}:\dim\pi_{e}(A)<s\}\leq s.

(1.1)

Here and throughout the paper,
\dim
refers to Hausdorff dimension. Recently, Orponen and Shmerkin
[
34
]
obtained a quantitatively small (but qualitatively very significant) improvement to the RHS of (
1.1
) whenever
0<s<\dim A
and
s<1
. See also
[
35
]
for related results on this theme. In a slightly different direction, Bourgain
[
4
]
(see also
[
32
]
) obtained a large improvement over (
1.1
) when
s
is close to
(\dim A)/2
. As a special case of his result, we have the following.

Theorem 1.2

(Bourgain)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Borel set. Then

\dim\{e\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\colon\dim\pi_{e}(A)\leq(\dim A)/2\}=0.

(1.2)

An example by Kaufman and Mattila
[
24
]
shows that (
1.2
) cannot hold if
(\dim A)/2
is replaced by
(\dim A)/2+c
for any
c>0
.

Estimates similar to those in Theorem
1.1
hold for a broad class of generalized projections (see
[
36
]
and the references therein), and recently Shmerkin
[
45
]
proved an analogue of Theorem
1.2
where the set of orthogonal projections
\{\pi_{e}\}_{e\in S^{1}}
is replaced by a family
\{F_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
of nonsingular
C^{2}
functions
F_{\lambda}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, referred to as
nonlinear projections
, that satisfy a natural transversality condition.

A particularly interesting class of nonlinear projections are the pinned distance functions
d_{p}(q)=|p-q|
. In
[
33
]
, Orponen proved a packing dimension version of the Falconer distance conjecture in the plane for Ahlfors-regular sets by showing that the nonlinear projection
d_{p}(q)
could be analyzed using multi-scale averages of suitably chosen linear projections, and thus the problem of computing the size of the pinned distance set
\Delta_{p}(E)=\{d_{p}(q)\colon q\in E\}
was closely related to the problem of computing the size of certain orthogonal projections of
E
at various scales and locations. This powerful new idea has been extended in subsequent works
[
26
,
43
,
45
]
. In particular, Shmerkin
[
45
]
proved an analogue of Theorem
1.2
for pinned distances.

Theorem 1.3

(Shmerkin)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Borel set. Then

\dim\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\dim\Delta_{p}(A)\leq(\dim A)/2\}=0.

(1.3)

The following example due to Elekes
[
13
]
shows that the quantity
(\dim A)/2
on the LHS of (
1.3
) cannot be replaced by
(\dim A)/2+c
for any
c>0
.

Example 1
.

Let
0<\alpha<2
and let
c>0
. Let
X,Y,S\subset[0,1]
be Borel sets (see e.g.
[
24
]
) so that
\dim(X\times Y)=\alpha
,
\dim(S)>0
, and
\dim(X-sY)<\alpha/2+c
for each
s\in S
.
For each
(a,b)\in X\times Y
, define
q_{a,b}=(a/2,\sqrt{1+b-a^{2}/4})
, and define
A=\{q_{a,b}\colon a\in X,\ b\in Y\}
. We have
\dim A=\alpha
. Define
K=S\times\{0\}
.
Then for each
(x,y)=q_{a,b}\in A
and
s\in S
, we have
|(s,0)-q_{a,b}|^{2}=(s^{2}+1)+(b-sa)
. Thus for each
p=(s,0)\in K
, we have
\dim(\Delta_{p}(A))=\dim(B-sA)<\alpha/2+c
.
We conclude that

\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\dim\Delta_{p}(A)\leq(\dim A)/2+c\}\supset K,

(1.4)

so in particular the set on the LHS of (
1.4
) has positive dimension.

In Example
1
, the set
K
is contained in a line. It is possible to construct slightly more complicated multi-scale examples where the set (
1.4
) is not contained in one line, but can be contained in a union of countably many lines.
The next theorem says that if
c>0
is small, then this is essentially the only way that the set (
1.4
) can have positive dimension. To make this statement precise, we will need the following definition.

Definition 1.4
.

We say a Borel set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
curved
if there exist
\beta>0
and
C>0
, and a Borel probability measure
\mu
supported on
K
so that for all
r>0
,

\mu^{3}(\{(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})\in K^{3}\colon|T(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})|<r\})\leq Cr^{\beta},

(1.5)

where
|T(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})|
is the area of the triangle spanned by
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
. If
K
is not curved, we say it is
flat
. In particular, if
\dim(K)=0
then
K
is flat.

For example, if
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is contained in a countable union of lines, then it is flat. If
K
has positive dimension and is contained in a smooth plane curve that has nonvanishing curvature (or more generally, whose curvature never vanishes to infinite order), then
K
is curved. If
K=K_{1}\times K_{2}
, where
K_{1},K_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}
are Borel sets of positive dimension, then
K
is curved. If
K^{\prime}\subset K
has positive dimension and
K^{\prime}
is curved, then
K
is curved.

Theorem 1.5
.

For each
0<\alpha<2
there exists
c(\alpha)>0
so that the following holds. Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Borel set, with
\dim(A)=\alpha.
Then the set

\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\dim\Delta_{p}(A)\leq\dim(A)/2+c\}
is flat.

Corollary 1.6
.

For each
0<\alpha<2
there exists
c(\alpha)>0
so that the following holds. Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Borel set, with
\dim(A)=\alpha.
Let
\gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth curve whose curvature never vanishes to infinite order. Then

\dim\{p\in\gamma\colon\dim\Delta_{p}(A)\leq\dim(A)/2+c\}=0.

Theorem
1.5
is an example of a general phenomenon, which says that at a single scale, it is impossible for a set to have small image simultaneously under three nonlinear projections, provided these projections satisfy a certain non-degeneracy condition. This condition will be described below.

1.2
A single-scale estimate: the role of Blaschke curvature

Definition 1.7
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a connected open set, and let
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth functions whose gradients are non-vanishing and pairwise linearly independent at each point of
U
. Blaschke
[
2
]
defined the curvature form

2\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{2}}\log\frac{\partial\phi_{3}/\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial\phi_{3}/\partial\phi_{2}}d\phi_{1}\wedge d\phi_{2}.

(1.6)

This curvature form is invariant under change of coordinates, and it captures important information about the 3-web given by the level-sets of the functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}
on
U
. Indeed, Blaschke
[
2
, Section 9]
(see also
[
21
]
for the exact formulation given above) showed that the curvature form (
1.6
) depends only on the 3-web (i.e. the triple of foliations defined by the level sets
\{\phi_{i}=c\}_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
), and not on the choice of
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}
.

If each of
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}
is linear, then the curvature form (
1.6
) vanishes identically. Conversely, if the curvature form (
1.6
) vanishes identically on
U
, then around each point
p\in U
there is a local change of coordinates so that

\phi_{1}(x,y)=x;\quad\phi_{2}(x,y)=y;\quad\phi_{3}(x,y)=x+y.

(1.7)

This observation is important in the context of projection theory for the following reason: If
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}
are of the form (
1.7
), and if
E=A\times A
, where
A\subset\mathbb{R}
is an arithmetic progression, then
\#\phi_{i}(E)\leq 2(\#E)^{1/2}
for each index
i=1,2,3
.
Thus if the curvature form (
1.6
) vanishes identically, then for each integer
N\geq 1
we can construct a set
E\subset U
with
\#E=N
so that
\#\phi_{i}(E)\leq 2N^{1/2}
for
i=1,2,3
. Similarly, it is possible to construct discretized sets
E\subset U
satisfying a Frostman type non-concentration condition so that
\phi_{1}(E),\phi_{2}(E)
, and
\phi_{3}(E)
each have size roughly
|E|^{1/2}
. In this section we will describe several converses to this statement, which are manifestations of the following principle: If the curvature form (
1.6
) does not vanish identically, then one of
\phi_{1}(E),\phi_{2}(E)
, or
\phi_{3}(E)
must be substantially larger than
|E|^{1/2}
.

Returning to the pinned Falconer distance problem, let
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
be points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
\phi_{i}(q)=|p_{i}-q|^{2}
. As we will show in Section
5
, the curvature form (
1.6
) vanishes identically if and only if
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are collinear. If
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are not collinear, then the gradients of
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{j}
are linearly dependent precisely on
\ell_{i,j}
, the line spanned by
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
. We can now state the following single-scale version of Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem 1.8
.

For each
0<\alpha<2
, there exists
c=c(\alpha)>0
such that the following holds for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small. Let
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\subset[0,1]^{2}
be three points that span a triangle with area at least
\delta^{c}
. Let
X\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a union of
\delta
-squares with
|X|\geq\delta^{2-\alpha+c}
, and suppose that for all balls
B
of diameter
r\geq\delta
,
X
satisfies the Frostman-type non-concentration condition

\begin{split}|X\cap B|&\leq r^{\alpha}\delta^{2-\alpha-c}.\end{split}

(1.8)

Then for at least one index
i,
we have

|\Delta_{p_{i}}(X)|\geq\delta^{1-\alpha/2-c}.

(1.9)

Remark 1.9
.

Theorem
1.8
fails if we remove the condition that the three points span a triangle with large area. Indeed, both Example
1
and the “railroad tracks” example from
[
25
, Figure 1]
shows that if the three points
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are collinear (or almost collinear), then (
1.9
) can fail for all three points, or indeed, any number of points
1
1

1

more precisely, for any integer
N
, we can construct an example where an estimate of the form (
1.9
) fails for
N
points.
.

Remark 1.10
.

Theorem
1.8
is a discretized version of
[
15
, Theorem 32]
(see also
[
44
]
), which bounds the number of “triple points” determined by three families of circles. See
[
13
,
38
]
for further discussion of this problem.

The next result is a version of Theorem
1.8
for arbitrary 3-webs.

Theorem 1.11
.

For each
0<\alpha<2,
there exists
c=c(\alpha)>0
so that the following holds. Let
K\subset U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
K
is compact and
U
is open and connected. Let
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth functions whose gradients are pairwise linearly independent at each point of
U
, and suppose the Blaschke curvature form (
1.6
) is non-vanishing on
U
. Then the following is true for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small.

Let
X\subset K
be a union of
\delta
-squares, with
|X|\geq\delta^{2-\alpha+c}
, and suppose that for all balls
B
of diameter
r\geq\delta
,
X
satisfies the non-concentration condition

\begin{split}|X\cap B|&\leq r^{\alpha}\delta^{2-\alpha-c}.\end{split}

(1.10)

Then for at least one index
i
we have

|\phi_{i}(X)|\geq\delta^{1-\alpha/2-c}.

(1.11)

Theorem
1.11
is a discretized projection theorem in the spirit of
[
4
,
45
]
. In
[
4
]
, Bourgain proved that if
X\subset[0,1]^{2}
is a set satisfying (
1.10
), and if
\Theta\subset S^{1}
is a large (and non-concentrated) set of directions, then there must exist a direction
e\in\Theta
so that the associated orthogonal projection
\pi_{e}(q)
satisfies (
1.11
). The set
\Theta
must be large, since if we define
X=[0,1]^{2}\cap(\delta^{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})^{2}
, then whenever
s
and
t
are integers with
|s|
and
|t|
small, the projection
(x,y)\cdot(s,t)
will have small cardinality and thus fail to satisfy (
1.11
). Thus it is new, and rather surprising, that Theorem
1.11
only requires three projections.

1.3
A single-scale estimate when Blaschke curvature vanishes: the role of convexity

Let us now consider a quadruple
of functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{4}
where each triple has vanishing Blaschke curvature. To simplify our discussion, we will work in coordinates where
\phi_{1}(x,y)=x,

\phi_{2}(x,y)=y
,
\phi_{3}(x,y)=x+y
, and
\phi_{4}(x,y)=u(x)+v(y)
. In this setting, we can still expect expansion provided that at least one of the functions
u
or
v
is strictly convex.

Theorem 1.12
.

For each
0<\alpha<2,
there exists
c=c(\alpha)>0
so that the following holds. Let
u,v\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth. Suppose
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}
, and
u^{\prime\prime}
are nonzero on
[0,1]
. Let
\phi_{1}(x,y)=x,

\phi_{2}(x,y)=y,

\phi_{3}(x,y)=x+y
, and
\phi_{4}(x,y)=u(x)+v(y)
. Then the following is true for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small.

Let
X\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a union of
\delta
-squares, with
|X|\geq\delta^{2-\alpha+c}
, and suppose that for all balls
B
of diameter
r\geq\delta
,
X
satisfies the non-concentration condition

\begin{split}|X\cap B|&\leq r^{\alpha}\delta^{2-\alpha-c}.\end{split}

(1.12)

Then for at least one index
i
we have

|\phi_{i}(X)|\geq\delta^{1-\alpha/2-c}.

(1.13)

Theorem
1.12
can be viewed as a continuous analogue of
[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
, and indeed our proof is inspired by certain ideas from
[
22
]
.

Finally, we remark that Theorem
1.12
allows us to prove similar expansion results for
d
-tuples of functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}
for
d>4
. In brief, suppose that
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}
are smooth functions, and that the Blaschke curvature of each triple
\phi_{i},\phi_{j},\phi_{k}
is identically 0. Then after a change of coordinates we can write
\phi_{1}(x,y)=x,

\phi_{2}(x,y)=y,

\phi_{3}(x,y)=x+y
, and
\phi_{i}(x,y)=u_{i}(x)+v_{i}(y)
for each
i\geq 4
. We can then apply Theorem
1.12
for each
i\geq 4
. If
u_{i}^{\prime\prime}
and
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}
are identically 0 for each index
i\geq 4
, then the
d
-web
(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d})
is parallelizable. Thus Theorem
1.12
is a quantitative version of the principle that if
d
functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}
determine a non-parallelizable
d
-web and if
X
is a
(\delta,\alpha)_{2}
set, then
|\phi_{i}(X)|
must be substantially larger than
|X|^{1/2}
for at least one index
i
.

1.4
The discretized Elekes-Rónyai theorem

Theorems
1.5
and
1.11
are closely related to the Elekes-Rónyai theorem, which we will now briefly describe. In
[
16
]
, Erdős and Szemerédi proved that if
A\subset\mathbb{R}
is a finite set, then either the sum set
A+A=\{a+a^{\prime}\colon a,a^{\prime}\in A\}
or the product set
A.A=\{aa^{\prime}\colon a,a^{\prime}\in A\}
must have cardinality much larger than that of
A
.

Theorem 1.13

(Erdős-Szemerédi)
.

There exists
c>0
so that for all finite sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}
, we have

\#(A+A)+\#(A.A)\gtrsim(\#A)^{1+c}.

(1.14)

The Erdős-Szemerédi Theorem quantifies the principle that a subset of
\mathbb{R}
cannot be approximately closed under both addition and multiplication. In
[
14
]
, Elekes and Rónyai developed this idea in a slightly different direction, and proved the following:

Theorem 1.14

(Elekes-Rónyai)
.

Let
P
be a bivariate real polynomial. Then either
P
is one of the special forms
P(x,y)=h(a(x)+b(y))
or
P(x,y)=h(a(x)b(y))
, where
h,a,b
are univariate real polynomials, or that for all finite sets of real numbers
A,B
of cardinality
N
, we have

\#P(A,B)=\omega(N).

(1.15)

If
P(x,y)=h(a(x)+b(y))
or
P(x,y)=h(a(x)b(y))
, where
h,a,b
are univariate real polynomials, then we call
P
a (polynomial) special form. If
P
is not a (polynomial) special form then we call it an expanding polynomial. Theorem
1.14
has since been generalized in several directions. See
[
15
,
39
,
40
,
46
,
30
,
41
,
1
]
, and the references therein.

Similar questions can be asked for metric entropy. In this direction, Katz and Tao proposed the discretized ring conjecture
[
25
]
, which was solved by Bourgain
[
3
]
. The discretized ring conjecture (now a theorem) is similar to the Erdős-Szemerédi theorem, except cardinality has been replaced by metric entropy. It was an important ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.2

Theorem 1.16

(Bourgain)

For each

We prove a discretized version of the Elekes-Rónyai theorem for analytic functions, in the spirit of Theorem

. Before stating our result, we will define what it means for an analytic function to be a special form (cf.

Definition 1.17
.

Let

be analytic on a connected open set

. We say

is an (analytic) special form if on each connected region of
U\backslash\big(\big\{\frac{d}{dx}P=0\big\}\cup\big\{\frac{d}{dy}=0\big\}\big)
, there are univariate real analytic functions

,

P(x,y)=h(a(x)+b(y))
.

Note that every polynomial special form is also an analytic special form. We are now ready to state our main result on expanding functions.

Theorem 1.18
.

Let
0<\kappa\leq\alpha<1
, let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a connected open set that contains
[0,1]^{2}
, and let
P\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
be analytic (resp. polynomial). Then either
P
is an analytic (resp. polynomial) special form, or there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\alpha,\kappa)>0
and
\eta=\eta(\alpha,\kappa,P)>0
so that the following is true for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small.

Let
A,B\subset[0,1]
be unions of
\delta

,

and

satisfy the non-concentration conditions

\begin{split}|A\cap J|&\leq|J|^{\kappa}\delta^{1-\alpha-\eta},\\
|B\cap J|&\leq|J|^{\kappa}\delta^{1-\alpha-\eta}.\end{split}

(1.17)

Let

be a union of
\delta
-squares, with
|E|\geq\delta^{2-2\alpha+\eta}
. Then we have the growth estimate

|P(E)|\geq\delta^{1-\alpha-\varepsilon}.

(1.18)

Remark 1.19
.

Note that the growth exponent
\varepsilon
in Theorem
1.18
is independent of
P
, and in particular independent of the degree of
P
when
P
is a polynomial. This is interesting for the following reasons. First, if
P
is a polynomial then it might be possible (though likely difficult) to prove an analogue of Theorem
1.18
using Bourgain’s discretized sum-product theorem (Theorem
1.15
) and tools from Ruzsa calculus. For example, these ideas were used in
[
7
]
to prove expansion over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for a certain explicitly specified polynomial. However, the entropy growth exponent
\varepsilon
arising from such a strategy would necessarily depend on the polynomial
P
(the degree of
P
, the number of monomial terms, etc).

Second, there is a general intuition in additive combinatorics that states that one should expect a quantity such as
P(A\times B)
to be large when there are few solutions to
P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, and conversely. Concretely, if
A
and
B
have cardinality
\delta^{-\alpha}
, then the statements “there are at about
\delta^{-3\alpha+\varepsilon}

\delta
-separated solutions to

” and “

has measure about
\delta^{1-\alpha-\varepsilon}
” are often morally equivalent. In the present setting, however, this intuition is not entirely correct—there exists a sequence of polynomials
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots
of increasing degree so that there are
\delta^{-3\alpha+o(1)}
solutions to
P_{j}(x,y)=P_{j}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, and yet the measure of
P_{j}(A\times B)
remains small.

We will also prove the following “dimension expansion” version of Theorem

, which is an analogue of Theorem

Theorem 1.20
.

For every
0<\alpha<1
, there exists

so that the following holds. Let

and let

be analytic (resp. polynomial). Then either

is an analytic (resp. polynomial) special form, or for every pair of Borel sets

of dimension at least

1.5
Proof ideas

If
P(x,y)
is a smooth function, we will define an auxiliary function
K_{P}(x,y)
that measures the extent to which
P
“looks like” a special form (in the sense of Definition
1.17
) near the point
(x,y)
.
K_{P}(x,y)
is closely related to the Blaschke curvature of the 3-web defined by the functions
\phi_{1}(x,y)=x,

\phi_{2}(x,y)=y
, and
\phi_{3}(x,y)=P(x,y)
at the point
(x,y)
. We will show that if
P
is analytic and
K_{P}
vanishes identically, then
P
is an analytic special form.

At the other extreme, if
K_{P}
is bounded away from 0, then we will prove that the set of solutions to
P(a,b)=P(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, where
a,a^{\prime}\in A
and
b,b^{\prime}\in B
has small
\delta
-covering number. We will call this an “energy dispersion” estimate, since it is an upper bound on the
L^{2}
norm of the multiplicity function
m(z)=\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(P^{-1}(z)\cap(A\times B))
, where
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(\cdot)
denotes the
\delta
-covering number. This energy dispersion estimate will be stated precisely in Proposition
3.4
, and it is one of the main technical results of this paper. It is closely related to the
L^{2}
flattening lemma that was used by Bourgain and Gamburd
[
5
,
6
]
to establish expansion in the Cayley graph of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
and the existence of a spectral gap in certain free subgroups of
SU(2)
.

Theorem
1.11
follows from Proposition
3.4
and a formula that relates the auxiliary function
K_{P}
to Blaschke curvature. Theorem
1.8
and Theorem
1.5
are proved by combining Proposition
3.4
with tools from real algebraic geometry, while Theorem
1.18
is proved by combining Proposition
3.4
with tools from semi-analytic geometry.

Proposition
3.4
is proved using ideas from additive combinatorics. We will give a brief sketch of the proof, which is inspired by the proof of the Elekes-Rónyai theorem in
[
40
]
. The goal is to prove an estimate of the form
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(\{P(a,b)=P(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\})\leq\delta^{-3\alpha+\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\alpha)>0
. Here and for the rest of this proof sketch, we will always suppose that
a,a^{\prime}\in A
and
b,b^{\prime}\in B
. Suppose instead that
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(\{P(a,b)=P(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\})\geq\delta^{-3\alpha+\varepsilon}
; if
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small we will obtain a contradiction.

For the purpose of this sketch, we will suppose that for each
x,x^{\prime}
and
y
, there is a unique
g(x,x^{\prime},y)
so that
P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},g(x,x^{\prime},y)).
In particular, if
P(a,b)=P(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}),
then
g(a,a^{\prime},b)\in B
. This means there are many triples
(a,a^{\prime},b)\in A\times A\times B
for which
g(a,a^{\prime},b)\in B
. Concretely, we have an estimate of the form

|\{(a,a^{\prime},b)\colon g(a,a^{\prime},b)\in B\}|\geq\delta^{3-3\alpha+O(\varepsilon)}.

(1.19)

Next, we will write
g(a,a^{\prime},b)=g_{b}(a,a^{\prime})
; we will think of
\{g_{b}\}_{b\in B}
as a family of (nonlinear) projections from
\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. The bound (
1.19
) implies that for many
b\in B
, there is a set
E_{b}\subset A\times A
with
|E_{b}|\geq\delta^{O(\varepsilon)}|A\times A|
so that
g_{b}(E_{b})\subset B
, and in particular,
|g_{b}(E_{b})|\leq\delta^{-O(\varepsilon)}|E_{b}|^{1/2}
. This is precisely the setting where Shmerkin’s projection theorem (discussed in Section
1.1
) can be applied. Shmerkin’s projection theorem says that if the family of projection maps
\{g_{b}\}
satisfies a certain non-degeneracy condition (which is indeed satisfied if the auxiliary function
K_{P}
is bounded away from 0), then it is impossible for
|g_{b}(E_{b})|
to be small for many different projection maps
g_{b}
. This is a contradiction, which completes the proof.

In what follows, it will be helpful to introduce some additional notation. If

(2.1)

(Shmerkin)

be a union of
\delta
-squares, and let

be a union of
\delta
-intervals.

(2.2)

Finally, let

(2.19)

Combining (

(2.20)

We will establish the bound (

) by applying Theorem

to

. To do so, we must verify that

,

,

Verifying the conditions for Theorem
2.1

Let

with this choice of

(2.21)

Define

Since

where on the last line we used (

Let

2.2
Reformulating Theorem
2.1
as an energy dispersion estimate

As the title suggests, in this section we will reformulate Theorem
2.1
as an energy dispersion estimate. The basic idea is that we can count the number of solutions to
P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
with
x,x^{\prime}\in A,\ y,y^{\prime}\in B
by counting the size of the intersection

\{(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}):P(x,y)-P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=0\}\cap(A^{2}\times B^{2}).

(2.28)

We will express the surface
Z(P(x,y)-P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))
as the graph
y^{\prime}=G(x,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),
and then use Lemma
2.2
to estimate the size (or more accurately,
\delta
-covering number) of the set (
2.28
). We now turn to the details.

Definition 2.3
.

If
F(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})
is a function (that is at least twice differentiable on its domain), define

\begin{split}H_{F}(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})&=(\partial_{x}F)(\partial_{y^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{x^{\prime}y}F)-(\partial_{x}F)(\partial_{y}F)(\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}F)\\
&\qquad-(\partial_{x^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{y^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{xy}F)+(\partial_{x^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{y}F)(\partial_{xy^{\prime}}F).\end{split}

(2.29)

We will be particularly interested in functions
F
of the form
F(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=P(x,y)-P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. In this case
H_{F}
simplifies to

H_{F}(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=\partial_{x}P(x,y)\partial_{y}P(x,y)\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-\partial_{x^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\partial_{y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\partial_{xy}P(x,y).

(2.30)

Definition 2.4
.

Let
F\colon I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}\times I_{4}\to\mathbb{R}
, and let
\pi
be the projection to the first three coordinates. We say
F
has full projection if
\pi(Z(F))=I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For every
0<\alpha<1
and
0<\kappa\leq\alpha,
there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\alpha,\kappa)>0
and
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\alpha,\kappa)
such that the following holds for all
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
.

Let
I=I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}\times I_{4}\subset[0,1]^{4}
be intervals and let
F\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function with full projection. Suppose that

|H_{F}|,\ |\partial_{x}F|,\ |\partial_{y}F|,\ |\partial_{x^{\prime}}F|,\ |\partial_{y^{\prime}}F|\geq\delta^{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{on}\ Z(F)\cap I,

(2.31)

and

\|F\|_{C^{2}(I)}\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}.

(2.32)

Let
A_{i}\subset I_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,4
be sets. Suppose that for each index
i=1,\ldots,4
and each interval
J
of length at least
\delta
,
A_{i}
satisfies the non-concentration condition

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A_{i}\cap J)\leq|J|^{\kappa}\delta^{-\alpha-\varepsilon}.

(2.33)

Then we have the energy dispersion estimate

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big((A_{1}\times A_{2}\times A_{3}\times A_{4})\cap Z(F)\big)\leq\delta^{-3\alpha+\varepsilon}.

(2.34)

Proof.

By the bound (
2.31
) for
\partial_{y^{\prime}}F
and the assumption that
F
has full projection, we have that for each
(x,x^{\prime},y)\in I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}
, there is precisely one
y^{\prime}\in I_{4}
so that
F(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=0
, and

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big((A_{1}\times A_{2}\times A_{3}\times&A_{4})\cap Z(F)\big)\\
&\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\{(x,x^{\prime},y)\in A_{1}\times A_{2}\times A_{3}\colon\ \exists\ y^{\prime}\in A_{4}\ \textrm{s.t.}\ F(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=0\}.\end{split}

In particular, to establish (
2.34
) it suffices to prove the estimate

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\Big(\pi\big((A_{1}\times A_{2}\times A_{3}\times A_{4})\cap Z(F)\big)\Big)\leq\delta^{-3\alpha+2\varepsilon}.

(2.35)

Since
F
has full projection, there is a smooth function
G\colon I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}\to I_{4}
so that
F(x,x^{\prime},y,G(x,x^{\prime},y))=0
for all
(x,x^{\prime},y)\in I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}
.
By the implicit function theorem, (
2.32
), and (
2.31
), we have the bound

\delta^{2\varepsilon}\leq\min_{I}\left|\frac{\partial_{x}F}{\partial_{y^{\prime}}F}\right|\leq\max_{I}\left|\frac{\partial_{x}F}{\partial_{y^{\prime}}F}\right|\leq\delta^{-2\varepsilon},

and similarly for
\left|\partial_{x^{\prime}}/\partial_{y^{\prime}}F\right|
and
\left|\partial_{y}F/\partial_{y^{\prime}}F\right|
. Thus

\delta^{2\varepsilon}\leq\min_{(x,x^{\prime},y)\in I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}|\nabla G_{(y)}(x,x^{\prime})|.

(2.36)

By continuing to differentiate our expression for
G
and using (
2.31
) for
\partial_{y^{\prime}}F
and (
2.32
), we obtain the bound

\|G_{(y)}\|_{C^{2}(I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3})}\lesssim\delta^{-10\varepsilon}.

(2.37)

Define

\varphi_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y):=\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}G(x,x^{\prime},y)}{\partial_{x}G(x,x^{\prime},y)}=\tan(\theta_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)).

We have

\varphi_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)=\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}F}{\partial_{x}F},

(2.38)

and

\begin{split}\partial_{y}&\varphi_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)\\
&=\frac{(\partial_{x}F)(\partial_{y^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{x^{\prime}y}F)-(\partial_{x}F)(\partial_{y}F)(\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}F)-(\partial_{x^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{y^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{xy}F)+(\partial_{x^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{y}F)(\partial_{xy^{\prime}}F)}{(\partial_{y^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{x}F)^{2}}\\
&=\frac{H_{F}}{(\partial_{y^{\prime}}F)(\partial_{x}F)^{2}},\end{split}

(2.39)

where in (
2.38
) and (
2.39
),
F
(and its partial derivatives) and
H_{F}
are evaluated at the point
(x,y,G(x,x^{\prime},y))
. Thus by (
2.31
) and (
2.32
), we have

|\varphi_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)|\leq\delta^{-2\varepsilon},

(2.40)

while by (
2.31
) and (
2.39
), we have

|\partial_{y}\varphi_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)|\geq\delta^{6\varepsilon}|H_{F}|\geq\delta^{7\varepsilon}.

(2.41)

Next, we compute

\begin{split}|\partial_{y}\varphi_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)|&=|\partial_{y}(\tan(\theta_{(x,x^{\prime})}))|\\
&=|(1+\tan^{2}(\theta_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y))||\partial_{y}\theta_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)|,\end{split}

and thus

|\partial_{y}\theta_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)|=\frac{|\partial_{y}\varphi_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y)|}{1+(\varphi_{(x,x^{\prime})}(y))^{2}}\geq\delta^{11\varepsilon}.

(2.42)

The estimates (
2.36
), (
2.37
), and (
2.42
) are precisely the requirements needed to apply Lemma
2.2
to
G
and the sets
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},
and
R=A_{4}
.
If
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{1}(\alpha,\kappa)>0
and
0<\delta<\delta_{0}(\alpha,\kappa)>0
are selected sufficiently sufficiently small, then there exists
\sigma=\sigma(\alpha,\kappa)>0
so that

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\Big(\pi\big((A_{1}\times A_{2}\times A_{3}\times A_{4})\cap Z(F)\big)\Big)\leq\delta^{-3\alpha+\sigma}.

(2.43)

We conclude that (
2.35
) hold for some
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\alpha,\kappa)>0
.
∎

3
The auxiliary function
K_{P}

In this section we will define and study the auxiliary function
K_{P}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P(x,y)
be smooth. Define

K_{P}(x,y)=\nabla P\wedge\nabla\Big(\frac{\partial_{x}P\partial_{y}P}{\partial_{xy}P}\Big).

A computation shows that (formally),

\begin{split}\big(\partial_{x}P\partial_{y}P\big)^{2}&\partial_{xy}\left(\log\left(\frac{\partial_{x}P}{\partial_{y}P}\right)\right)\\
&=(\partial_{y}P)^{2}\big(\partial_{x}P\partial_{xxy}P-\partial_{xx}P\partial_{xy}P\big)-(\partial_{x}P)^{2}\big(\partial_{y}P\partial_{xyy}P-\partial_{xy}P\partial_{yy}P\big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\big(\partial_{xy}P\big)^{2}\Big(\nabla P\wedge\nabla\Big(\frac{\partial_{x}P\partial_{y}P}{\partial_{xy}P}\Big)\Big).\end{split}

(3.1)

Then the middle expression is well-defined on the domain of
P
. The first equality holds on the domain of the LHS of (
3.1
) (here we extend
\log
to
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
by defining
\log(-x)=\log(x)
for
x
positive), while the second equality holds on the domain of the RHS of (
3.1
). The auxiliary function
K_{P}
is useful because of the following result from Elekes and Ronyai
[
14
]
. A detailed proof can be found in
[
37
, Lemma 10]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Elekes and Rónyai)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a connected open set and let
P\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
be analytic. If
P_{x}
or
P_{y}
vanishes identically on
U
, then
P
is an analytic special form, in the sense of Definition
1.17
. If neither
P_{x}
nor
P_{y}
vanishes identically on
U
, then
P
is an analytic special form if and only if
\partial_{xy}\left(\log\left(\frac{\partial_{x}P}{\partial_{y}P}\right)\right)
vanishes everywhere on
U
that it is defined (i.e. everywhere
\partial_{x}P
and
\partial_{y}P
are non-zero).

The identity (
3.1
) and Lemma
3.2
have the following consequence.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a connected open set and let
P\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
be analytic (resp. polynomial). Suppose that none of
\partial_{x}P
,
\partial_{y}P
, and
\partial_{xy}P
vanishes identically on
U
. Then
K_{P}
vanishes identically on
U
if and only if
P
is an analytic (resp. polynomial) special form.

Next, we will show that if
K_{P}
is far from 0, then
P
satisfies an energy dispersion estimate.

Proposition 3.4
.

For every
0<\alpha<1
and
0<\kappa\leq\alpha,
there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\alpha,\kappa)>0
and
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\alpha,\kappa)>0
such that the following holds for all
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
. Let
I\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a rectangle, and let
P\colon I\to[0,1]
be smooth. Suppose that

|\partial_{x}P|,\ |\partial_{y}P|,\ |\partial_{xy}P|,\ |K_{P}|\geq\delta^{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{on}\ I,

(3.2)

and

\|P\|_{C^{3}(I)}\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}.

(3.3)

Let
A,B
be sets with
A\times B\subset I
. Suppose that for all intervals
J
of length at least
\delta
,
A
and
B
satisfy the non-concentration condition.

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A\cap J)&\leq|J|^{\kappa}\delta^{-\alpha-\varepsilon},\\
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(B\cap J)&\leq|J|^{\kappa}\delta^{-\alpha-\varepsilon}.\end{split}

(3.4)

Then

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big(\{(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\in A\times A\times B\times B\colon P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\}\big)\leq\delta^{-3\alpha+\varepsilon}.

(3.5)

Proof.

Define
F(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=P(x,y)-P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. If
I=I_{1}\times I_{2}
, then
F
has domain
I^{\prime}=I_{1}\times I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{2}
. By (
3.2
),
\partial_{y^{\prime}}F
never changes sign on
I^{\prime}
, and thus for each
(x,x^{\prime},y)\in I_{1}\times I_{1}\times I_{2}
, there is at most one
y^{\prime}\in I_{2}
with
F(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=0
. Our next task is to cut the domain of
F
into smaller rectangles, so that on most rectangles, for each
x,x^{\prime},y
, there is precisely one
y^{\prime}
with
F(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=0
. The size of (
3.5
) will be bounded by the corresponding sum taken over these sets (plus a small error term corresponding to the rectangles where the above property fails).

Let
t_{0}=t_{0}(\kappa)>0
be a small constant to be specified below. Cover
I^{\prime}
by interior-disjoint rectangles, each of whose dimensions is
~\frac{t_{0}}{6}\delta^{2\varepsilon+5\varepsilon/\kappa}
(a slight adjustment might be needed if
6|I_{i}|/(t_{0}\delta^{2\varepsilon+5\varepsilon/\kappa})
is not an integer).
For each such rectangle
J=J_{1}\times J_{2}\times J_{3}\times J_{4}
, let
\tilde{J}_{4}
be an interval of length
t_{0}\delta^{5\varepsilon/\kappa}
, with the same midpoint as
J_{4}
. Define
\tilde{J}=J_{1}\times J_{2}\times J_{3}\times\tilde{J}_{4}
.

We claim that if
J\cap Z(F)\neq\emptyset
and if
\tilde{J}\subset I^{\prime},
then for each
(x,x^{\prime},y)\in J_{1}\times J_{2}\times J_{3}
, there is
y^{\prime}\in\tilde{J}_{4}
with
F(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=0
. To prove this claim, observe that the assumption
J\cap Z(F)\neq\emptyset
implies there exists
(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})\in J
with
P(x_{0},y_{0})=P(x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0}^{\prime})
. By (
3.3
), we thus have

\displaystyle|P(x,y)-P(x^{\prime},y_{0}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|P(x,y)-P(x_{0},y_{0})|+|P(x_{0},y_{0})-P(x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0}^{\prime})|+|P(x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0}^{\prime})-P(x^{\prime},y_{0}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}|(x,y)-(x_{0},y_{0})|+0+\delta^{-\varepsilon}|x_{0}-x^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}\operatorname{diam}(J)\leq\frac{t_{0}}{2}\delta^{\varepsilon+5\varepsilon/\kappa}.

By (
3.2
), there exists

and
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(B)\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon-\alpha}
from (

By (

(3.6)

for each rectangle
J

.

For the remainder of the argument we will fix such a rectangle

(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\in J

Recall Definition

\begin{split}H_{F}(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})&=\partial_{xy}P(x,y)\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\Big(\frac{\partial_{x}P(x,y)\partial_{y}P(x,y)}{\partial_{xy}P(x,y)}-\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\partial_{y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}{\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\Big).\end{split}

Our next task is to control the size of the region where
H_{F}(x,x^{\prime},y,G(x,x^{\prime},y))
is small. Fix
s_{0}>0

Fix
(x_{0},y_{0})\in J_{1}\times J_{2}
and suppose there is a point
x_{0}^{\prime}\in J_{3}
with
|H(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0},G(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0}))|\leq s_{0}
. Consider the curve

\gamma=\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in J_{3}\times\tilde{J}_{4}\colon P(x_{0},y_{0})=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\}=\{(x^{\prime},G(x_{0},x^{\prime},y_{0}))\colon x^{\prime}\in J_{3}\}.

Then
\gamma
is a smooth curve, and, by (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), at each point the tangent to this curve has slope
\leq\delta^{-2\varepsilon}
. Let
\gamma(t)\colon S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a unit speed parameterization of
\gamma
, where
S\subset\mathbb{R}
is an interval,
y_{0}^{\prime}=G(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0})
, and
\gamma(0)=(x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0}^{\prime})
.

For each
t\in S
, the unit tangent vector to
\gamma
at
t
is given by
\frac{\nabla P^{\perp}(\gamma(t))}{|\nabla P(\gamma(t))|}
. Thus for all
t\in S
we have

\begin{split}\Big|\partial_{t}\Big(\frac{\partial_{x}P(\gamma(t))\partial_{y}P(\gamma(t))}{\partial_{xy}P(\gamma(t))}\Big)\Big|&=\Big|\frac{\nabla P^{\perp}}{|\nabla P|}\cdot\nabla\big(\frac{(\partial_{x}P)(\partial_{y}P)}{\partial_{xy}P}\big)(\gamma(t))\Big|\\
&=\frac{1}{|\nabla P|}\Big|\Big(\nabla P\wedge\nabla\big(\frac{(\partial_{x}P)(\partial_{y}P)}{\partial_{xy}P}\big)\Big)(\gamma(t))\Big|\\
&=\frac{|K_{P}(\gamma(t))|}{|\nabla P(\gamma(t))|}\\
&\geq\delta^{2\varepsilon}.\end{split}

Therefore if
\gamma(t)=(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))
, then

\Big|\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(0),y^{\prime}(0))\partial_{y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(0),y^{\prime}(0))}{\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(0),y^{\prime}(0)}-\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))\partial_{y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))}{\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))}\Big|\geq\delta^{2\varepsilon}t,

and hence

\begin{split}&\Big|\frac{\partial_{x}P(x_{0},y_{0})\partial_{y}P(x_{0},y_{0})}{\partial_{xy}P(x_{0},y_{0})}-\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))\partial_{y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))}{\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))}\Big|\\
&\geq\Big|\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))\partial_{y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))}{\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))}-\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(0),y^{\prime}(0))\partial_{y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(0),y^{\prime}(0))}{\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(0),y^{\prime}(0))}\Big|\\
&\qquad-\Big|\frac{\partial_{x}P(x_{0},y_{0})\partial_{y}P(x_{0},y_{0})}{\partial_{xy}P(x_{0},y_{0})}-\frac{\partial_{x^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(0),y^{\prime}(0))\partial_{y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))}{\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(0),y^{\prime}(0))}\Big|\\
&\geq\delta^{2\varepsilon}t-\delta^{-2\varepsilon}s_{0}.\end{split}

(3.7)

We conclude that

|H_{F}(x_{0},x^{\prime}(t),y_{0},y^{\prime}(t))|\geq\big|\partial_{xy}P(x,y)\partial_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}P(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t))\big|\big(\delta^{2\varepsilon}t-s\big)\geq\delta^{2\varepsilon}(\delta^{2\varepsilon}t-2^{-\varepsilon}s_{0}).

(3.8)

Using (
3.3
), one can easily verify that
|\nabla H_{F}|\leq 12\delta^{-3\varepsilon}
. Combined with (
3.8
), we have that
if
(x,y)\in J_{1}\times J_{2}
with
|x-x_{0}|\leq\delta
and
|y-y_{0}|\leq\delta
, then

|H_{F}(x,x^{\prime}(t),y,y^{\prime}(t))|\geq\delta^{2\varepsilon}(\delta^{2\varepsilon}t-\delta^{-2\varepsilon}s_{0})-12\delta^{1-3\varepsilon}.

(3.9)

Recall that
\gamma(t)
is a unit speed parameterization, and so
|(x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0}^{\prime})-\gamma(t)|\leq t
. We conclude that for each
(x_{0},y_{0})\in J_{1}\times J_{2}
, if there exists a point
x_{0}^{\prime}\in J_{3}
with
|H(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0},G(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0}))|\leq s_{0}
, then for each

we have that the set

\{x^{\prime}\in J_{3}\colon|H_{F}(x,x^{\prime},y,G(x,x^{\prime},y))|\leq s\ \quad\textrm{for some}\ x\in J_{1}\cap[x_{0}-\delta,x_{0}+\delta],\ y\in J_{2}\cap[y_{0}-\delta,y_{0}+\delta]\}

is contained in an interval of length at most
2\delta^{-6\varepsilon}s
(this interval necessarily contains

).

Since there are at most
\delta^{-2\alpha-2\varepsilon}

\delta
-separated points
(x_{0},y_{0})\in J_{1}\times J_{2}
for which there exists
x_{0}^{\prime}\in J_{3}
with
|H(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0},G(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0}))|\leq s_{0}
, we can use (
3.4
) to bound the covering number of each corresponding interval; setting

, we conclude that

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\Big\{(x,x^{\prime},y)\colon|H(x,x^{\prime},y,G(x,x^{\prime},y))|\leq s_{0}\Big\}&\lesssim\delta^{-2\alpha-2\varepsilon}\big((\delta^{-6\varepsilon}(2s_{0}))^{\kappa}\delta^{-\alpha-\varepsilon}\big)\\
&\lesssim\delta^{-3\alpha-9\varepsilon}s_{0}^{\kappa}.\end{split}

(3.10)

Since
\|\nabla G\|\leq\delta^{-2\varepsilon}
, this implies

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big(\{(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\in J_{1}\times J_{2}\times J_{3}\times J_{4}\colon P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),\ |H(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})|\leq s_{0}\}\big)\lesssim\delta^{-3\alpha-11\varepsilon}s_{0}^{\kappa}.

(3.11)

Next, let

W=\Big\{(x,x^{\prime},y)\in J_{1}\times J_{2}\times J_{3}\colon|H(x,x^{\prime},y,G(x,x^{\prime},y))|\leq c_{0}\delta^{5\varepsilon}s_{0}\Big\},

(3.12)

Indeed, if

If we select

s_{0}

J_{1}\times J_{2}\times J_{3}\subset\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}}Q\ \cup\ \Big\{(x,x^{\prime},y)\colon|H(x,x^{\prime},y,G(x,x^{\prime},y))|\leq s_{0}\Big\}.

4
Blaschke curvature and discretized projections

In this section we will prove Theorem
1.11
. First, we will show that if a non-concentrated set has small image under three different maps whose gradients are pairwise linearly independent at each point, then the image of this set under these maps must also be non-concentrated.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Q\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a square, let
\alpha,\eta>0
,
C\geq 1
. Then the following is true for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small. Let
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\in C^{2}(Q)
. Suppose that for each
i=1,2,3
we have

\|f_{i}\|_{C^{2}(Q)}\leq C,

(4.1)

and for each
i\neq j
and each
z\in Q
we have

|\nabla f_{i}(z)\wedge\nabla f_{j}(z)|\geq C^{-1}.

(4.2)

Let
X\subset Q
with with
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X)\geq\delta^{-\alpha}
. Suppose that for all balls
B
of radius
r\geq\delta
we have

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X\cap B)\leq r^{\alpha}\delta^{-\alpha-\eta}.

(4.3)

Suppose that for each
i=1,2,3
we have

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(f_{i}(X))\leq\delta^{-\alpha/2-\eta}.

(4.4)

Then there exists
X^{\prime}\subset X
, with

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X^{\prime})\gtrsim(\delta^{\eta}C^{-1})^{O(1)}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X),

(4.5)

such that for all intervals
J
of length at least
\delta
, and each index
i=1,2,3
, we have

\begin{split}&\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(f_{i}(X^{\prime})\cap J)\leq(C|\log\delta|\delta^{-\eta})^{O(1)}|J|^{\alpha/2}\delta^{-\alpha/2}.\end{split}

(4.6)

Proof.

First, we claim that the set
X
cannot be too concentrated near the boundary of
Q
, i.e.

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big(X\cap N_{C\delta}(\operatorname{bdry}(Q))\big)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X).

(4.7)

Indeed, if (
4.7
) failed, then there is a line segment
\ell\subset[0,1]^{2}
(one of the four edges of the square
Q
) with
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X\cap N_{C\delta}(\ell))\geq\frac{1}{8}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X)
. But then by (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we can find a segment
\ell^{\prime}\subset\ell
with
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X\cap N_{C\delta}(\ell^{\prime}))\geq C^{-O(1)}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X)
and an index
i
so that
|\nabla f\cdot v|\geq C^{-O(1)})
on
\ell
, where
v
is the unit vector parallel to
\ell
. But this implies

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(f_{i}(X\cap N_{C\delta}(\ell^{\prime})))\geq C^{-O(1)}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X),

which contradicts (
4.4
), provided
\delta>0
is sufficiently small (depending on
\alpha,\eta
, and
C
).

Next, by (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we can select a square
Q^{\prime}\subset Q\backslash N_{C\delta}(\operatorname{bdry}(Q))
with

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X\cap Q^{\prime})\geq C^{-O(1)}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X)

so that for each
i\neq j
,
f_{i}\otimes f_{j}\colon Q^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is injective on
N_{C\delta}(Q^{\prime})\subset Q
, where
N_{C\delta}(Q^{\prime})
is the
C\delta
-neighborhood of
Q^{\prime}
. In particular, since (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) continue to hold on
Q^{\prime}
, if
z,z^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
, then

|(f_{i}(z),f_{j}(z))-(f_{i}(z^{\prime}),f_{j}(z^{\prime}))|\geq C^{-O(1)}|z-z^{\prime}|.

(4.8)

Next, let
X_{0}\subset X\cap Q^{\prime}
be a maximal
C^{O(1)}\delta
separated subset of
X\cap Q^{\prime}
, where the implicit constant is chosen so that if
z,z^{\prime}\in X_{0}
, then for each
i\neq j
we have

|(f_{i}(z),f_{j}(z))-(f_{i}(z^{\prime}),f_{j}(z^{\prime}))|\geq 2\delta.

(4.9)

Note that
\#X_{0}\geq C^{-O(1)}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X),
so

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X_{0})\gtrsim(\delta^{\eta}C^{-1})^{O(1)}\delta^{-\alpha}.

(4.10)

In the argument that follows, we will show that if
X_{0}
is a set satisfying (
4.3
), (
4.4
), (
4.8
), (
4.9
), and (
4.10
), then there is a set
X_{1}\subset X_{0}
that continues to satisfy these inequalities (with slightly worse implicit constants for (
4.10
)), and that also satisfies (
4.6
) for
i=1
. We can then repeat this argument to find a set
X_{2}\subset X_{1}
that continues to satisfy the above inequalities, and also satisfies (
4.6
) for
i=2
, and then a set
X_{3}\subset X_{2}
that satisfies (
4.6
) for
i=3
. To complete the proof of the lemma, we define
X^{\prime}=X_{3}
.

Our next goal is to construct the set
X_{1}
described above. Let
A_{0}\subset f_{1}(X_{0})
and
B_{0}\subset f_{2}(X_{0})
be maximal
\delta

5.1

In this section we will compute the Blaschke curvature for the pinned distance functions from three points. Define

p_{1}=(t,0),\quad p_{2}=(-t,0),\quad p_{3}=(a,b).

Define

\begin{split}\phi_{1}(x,y)&=(x-t)^{2}+y^{2},\\
\phi_{2}(x,y)&=(x+t)^{2}+y^{2},\\
\phi_{3}(x,y)&=(x-a)^{2}+(y-b)^{2}.\end{split}

(5.1)

We have

\begin{split}x&=\frac{\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}}{4t},\\
y&=\Big(\frac{\phi_{1}}{2}+\frac{\phi_{2}}{2}-t^{2}-\frac{(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})^{2}}{16t^{2}}\Big)^{1/2},\end{split}

(5.2)

and thus

\phi_{3}=\frac{1}{16}\Big(\Big(\frac{4at+\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}}{t}\Big)^{2}+\Big(-4b+\sqrt{8\phi_{1}+8\phi_{2}-(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})^{2}/t^{2}-16t^{2}}\ \ \Big)^{2}\Big).

We therefore have

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\phi_{3}}{\partial\phi_{1}}&=\frac{-bx+(t+a)y-bt}{2ty},\\
\frac{\partial\phi_{3}}{\partial\phi_{2}}&=\frac{\phantom{-}bx+(t-a)y-bt}{2ty},\end{split}

i.e.

p_{3}=(a,b)

Next, we compute

d\phi_{1}\wedge d\phi_{2}=(-8ty)dxdy,

and finally, we compute

\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{2}}\log\frac{\partial\phi_{3}/\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial\phi_{3}/\partial\phi_{2}}\Big)\ d\phi_{1}\wedge d\phi_{2}=b\frac{f(x,y)}{g^{2}(x,y)}dxdy,

&+\big(-2ab\big)y^{5},\end{split}

(5.5)

and

We see that

8.1
Analytic varieties do not concentrate on fractal sets

In this section we will introduce several technical results showing how products of non-concentrated sets can intersect thin neighborhoods of varieties
Z(f)
, where
f
is an analytic function. Our setup will be as follows. Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}\subset[0,1]
be sets and let
A=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{d}
. Let
\delta>0
and suppose that for all intervals
J
of length at least
\delta
, each set
A_{i}
satisfies the non-concentration condition

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A_{i}\cap J)\leq\delta^{-\eta}|J|^{\kappa}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A_{i}).

We would like to show that if
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is analytic, then
|N_{t}(Z(f))\cap A|
must be much smaller than
|A|
. Results of this type, combined with Whitney’s cube decomposition, will allow us to find a (reasonably) small collection of interior disjoint cubes whose union covers most of
A
, so that
f
is not too small on each cube. Results of this type will help us control the size of various “bad” regions where discretized expansion might fail.

Theorem 8.1

(Stratification of the vanishing locus of an analytic function
[
28
]
)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set that contains
[0,1]^{d}
and let
\phi\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
be real analytic and not identically zero. Then there is a finite set
\mathcal{M}
of smooth (proper) submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
Z(\phi)\cap[0,1]^{d}\subset\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
M
be a smooth (proper) submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and suppose
M\cap[0,1]^{d}
is compact. Then there is a constant
C>0
so that the following holds.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}\subset[0,1]
be sets and let
A=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{d}
. Let
\delta>0
and suppose that for all intervals
J
of length at least
\delta
, each set
A_{i}
satisfies the non-concentration condition

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A_{i}\cap J)\leq\delta^{-\eta}|J|^{\kappa}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A_{i}).

Then
for each
s>\delta
we have

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big(A\cap N_{s}(M)\big)\leq C\delta^{-\eta}s^{\kappa}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A).

(8.1)

Proof.

For each
p\in M\cap[0,1]^{d}
, let
i(p)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
be an index so that the coordinate unit vector
e_{i(p)}
is not contained in the tangent space

. If

is selected sufficiently small, then the set

is a smooth manifold, and there exists a constant
C_{p}
so that for all

, we have

|e_{i(p)}\cdot q-e_{i(p)}\cdot q^{\prime}|\leq C_{p}|\pi_{p}(q)-\pi_{p}(q^{\prime})|,

(8.2)

where
\pi_{p}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
is the orthogonal projection to the
(d-1)
-dimensional subspace

.
Indeed, the above inequality follows from the fact that
\inf_{v\in T_{p}(M)}\angle(e_{i(p)},v)>0
, and the map
q\mapsto T_{q}(M)
is continuous.

Since
M\cap[0,1]^{d}
is compact, we can cover
M\cap[0,1]^{d}
by a finite set
\mathcal{M}
of manifolds of the form

. It suffices to prove (
8.1
) for each of these manifolds. Let

.

Let

. Let
s\geq\delta
and let
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of cubes of side-length

that intersect

. We claim that at most

cubes from

can have the same projection under

, for some

that depends only on

and

. Indeed, if
C_{1}>2
cubes have the same projection under
e_{i(p)}
, then select points

from two cubes that have greatest distance. Since there are at least
C_{1}-2
cubes in-between the cubes containing

and
q^{\prime}
, we have
|e_{i(p)}\cdot q-e_{i(p)}\cdot q^{\prime}|\geq(C_{1}-2)s
. On the other hand, since all of the cubes (and in particular, the cubes containing

and

) have the same projection under

, we have that

. Thus by (

), we must have

For each such cube

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(Q\cap A)\leq\delta^{-\eta}s^{\kappa}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A_{i(p)})\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(\pi_{p}(A\cap Q)).

(8.3)

Summing (

and using the fact that at most

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(N_{s}(M)\cap A)&\leq C_{1}\delta^{-\eta}s^{\kappa}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A_{i(p)})\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(\pi_{p}(A))\\
&\leq C_{1}\delta^{-\eta}s^{\kappa}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A).\qed\end{split}

Remark 8.3
.

Note that in the special case where where exists an index

is not contained in

to require the non-concentration condition only for

Combining Lemma

and Theorem

Let

8.2
Energy Dispersion implies Entropy Growth

Corollary
8.6
allows us to find a large square

are bounded away from zero. Proposition

Lemma 8.7
.

Let

be a square, and let

Let

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(P(X))\gtrsim\frac{c\big(\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X)\big)^{2}}{\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big(\{(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in X^{2}\colon P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\}\big)}.

Proof.

Since
X
is a union of squares of side-length
\delta
, we can select a
\delta
-separated set
\tilde{X}\subset X
with
N_{\delta/2}(\tilde{X})\subset X
and

.

Define

\tilde{P}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}c\delta\lfloor 2(c\delta)^{-1}P(x,y)\rfloor.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of quadruples
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{x}^{\prime},\tilde{y}^{\prime})\in(\tilde{X})^{2}
with
\tilde{P}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\tilde{P}(\tilde{x}^{\prime},\tilde{y}^{\prime}).
For such a quadruple we have

|P(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})-P(\tilde{x}^{\prime},\tilde{y}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{2}c\delta.

(8.7)

Since

on

, which is impossible.
∎

\geq\dim(A)-\varepsilon

. When
d=1
the result follows from Corollary
9.3
and the fact that since
f
is analytic and not identically zero on a neighborhood of
[0,1]
,
Z(f)\cap[0,1]
is finite. Now suppose the result is true for
d-1
and let
f,

\varepsilon
, and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}
be as in the statement of the lemma. Since
f
is analytic and not identically zero on a neighborhood of
[0,1]^{d}
, there are finitely many points
p_{d}\in[0,1]
so that the (truncated) hyperplane
\{x_{d}=p_{d}\}\cap[0,1]^{d}
is contained in
Z(f)\cap[0,1]^{d}
. Use Corollary
9.3
to select a point
p_{d}\in(0,1)
so that
A_{d}
has local dimension
\geq\dim(A_{d})-\varepsilon
at
p_{d}
, and the truncated hyperplane
\{x_{d}=p_{d}\}\cap[0,1]^{d}
is not contained in
Z(f)\cap[0,1]^{d}
. Then the function
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})\mapsto f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},p_{d})
is analytic and not identically zero on a neighborhood of
[0,1]^{d-1}
. We now find the point
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d-1},p_{d})
by applying the induction hypothesis to this function and the sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{d-1}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.20
. For the reader’s convenience we will restate it here.

Theorem
1.20
.

For every
0<\alpha<1
, there exists
c=c(\alpha)>0
so that the following holds. Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a connected open set that contains
[0,1]^{2}
and let
P\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
be analytic (resp. polynomial). Then either
P
is an analytic (resp. polynomial) special form, or for every pair of Borel sets
A,B\subset[0,1]
of dimension at least
\alpha
, we have

\dim P(A\times B)\geq\alpha+c.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\alpha)
and
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\alpha,P)
be the quantities obtained by applying Proposition
3.4
to
P
with parameters
\alpha
and
\kappa=\alpha/2
. We will prove that

\dim(P(A,B))>\alpha+\varepsilon/2.

(9.1)

Without loss of generality we can suppose that
P(A,B)\subset[0,1]
. Suppose that
P
is not a special form and (
9.1
) is false. Since
P
is not a special form, we have that none of
\partial_{x}P
,
\partial_{y}P
or
\partial_{xy}P
vanish identically. By Lemma
3.3
,
K_{P}
does not vanish identically. Define

f=(\partial_{x}P)(\partial_{y}P)(\partial_{xy}P)^{3}(K_{P}).

Note that initially
f
is not defined on
Z(\partial_{xy}P)
, but if we define
f
to be
0
on this set, then
f
is analytic on a neighborhood of
[0,1]^{2}
, so we can use Lemma
9.4
to select a point
p=(p_{1},p_{2})\in(0,1)^{2}\backslash Z(f)
so that
A
and
B
have local dimension
\geq\alpha-\varepsilon/20
at
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, respectively. Let
I_{0},J_{0}\subset[0,1]
be closed intervals containing
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
respectively so that
(I_{0}\times J_{0})\cap Z(f)=\emptyset
and
p
is contained in the interior of
I_{0}\times J_{0}
. Then there is a number
c>0
so that

|\partial_{x}P(x,y)|\geq c,\ |\partial_{y}P(x,y)|\geq c,\ |\partial_{xy}P(x,y)|\geq c,\ |K_{p}(x,y)|\geq c\quad\forall\ (x,y)\in I_{0}\times J_{0}.

(9.2)

Define
A^{\prime}=A\cap I_{0}
and
B^{\prime}=B\cap J_{0}
. Let
C=P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
.

Since
\dim(A^{\prime})\geq\alpha-\varepsilon/20
and
\dim(B^{\prime})\geq\alpha-\varepsilon/20
, by Frostman’s lemma (see e.g.
[
31
, Theorem 8.8]
), there are Borel measures
\mu
and
\nu
, supported on
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
respectively, with
\mu(A^{\prime})>0,
and
\nu(B^{\prime})>0
, so that

\begin{split}&\mu(A\cap I)\leq|I|^{\alpha-\varepsilon/10}\ \textrm{for every interval}\ I,\\
&\nu(B\cap J)\leq|J|^{\alpha-\varepsilon/10}\ \textrm{for every interval}\ J.\end{split}

(9.3)

In particular,
\mu
and
\nu
have no atoms. Let
\lambda
be the pushforward of
\mu\times\nu
by
P
, i.e. for each Borel set
U\subset\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lambda(U)=(\mu\times\nu)(\{(x,y)\in A\times B:P(x,y)\in U\}).

Observe that
\lambda
is a measure supported on
C
, and
\lambda(C)>0
. Let
\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{0}
be a number of the form
2^{-k_{1}}
for some positive integer
k_{1}
. In what follows, all implicit constants will be independent of
\delta_{1}
. If
\delta_{1}>0
is selected sufficiently small (depending only on
\alpha
),
then we will eventually show that (
9.1
) must hold.

Let
\{[x_{i},x_{i}+r_{i}]\}
be a covering of
C
by intervals of length at most
\delta_{1}
, with

\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\alpha+(3/5)\varepsilon}<1.

(9.4)

Such a covering must exist, since by assumption we have
\dim(C)\leq\alpha+\varepsilon/2
. Without loss of generality we can suppose that each interval is of the form
[n2^{-k},(n+1)2^{-k}]
for some
k\geq k_{1}
and some integer
1\leq n<2^{-k_{1}}
.

For each
k\geq k_{1}
, let
m_{k}=\sum\lambda([x_{i},x_{i}+r_{i}])
, where the sum is taken over all intervals of length
r_{i}=2^{-k}
. Since
\sum_{k\geq k_{1}}m_{k}\geq\lambda(C)>0
and
\sum_{k\geq k_{1}}k^{-2}\leq\pi^{2}/6
, there must exist an index
k_{2}\geq k_{1}
with
m_{k_{2}}\gtrsim\lambda(C)k_{2}^{-2}
. Define
\delta=2^{-k_{2}}
, so
0<\delta\leq\delta_{1}
.

At this point it will be helpful to introduce some additional notation. We say
X\lessapprox Y
if there is an absolute constant
K_{1}>0
and a constant
K_{2}>0
(which may depend on
\lambda(C)
) so that
A\leq K_{2}|\log\delta|^{K_{1}}B
. If
A\lessapprox B
and
B\lessapprox A
, we say
A\approx B
. In the arguments that follow, we can always take
K_{1}\leq 100
. With this notation we have
1\lessapprox m_{k_{2}}
.

Let
C^{\prime}=\bigcup[x_{i},x_{i}+r_{i}]
, where the union is taken over all intervals of length
r_{i}=\delta
in the covering. Then
\lambda(C^{\prime})=m_{k_{2}}\gtrapprox 1
, and
C^{\prime}
is a union of interior-disjoint intervals of the form
[n\delta,(n+1)\delta]
, where
1\leq n<\delta^{-1}
is an integer. After dyadic pigeonholing, we can select a set
C^{\prime\prime}\subset C^{\prime}
that is again a union of
\delta
-intervals with

\lambda(C^{\prime\prime})\gtrapprox 1,

(9.5)

and each
\delta
-interval
I\subset C^{\prime\prime}
has measure
\lambda(I)\sim\lambda(C^{\prime\prime})(\delta|C^{\prime\prime}|^{-1})
.
Note that

\delta^{1-\alpha+\varepsilon/10}\lessapprox|C^{\prime\prime}|\leq\delta^{1-\alpha-(3/5)\varepsilon}.

(9.6)

The lower bound on
|C^{\prime\prime}|
follows from (
9.3
) and (
9.5
), while the upper bound follows from (
9.4
).

Cover
I_{0}\times J_{0}
by squares of the form
[n\delta,(n+1)\delta]\times[m\delta,(m+1)\delta]
, and let
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
be the set of squares for which
P(Q)\cap C^{\prime\prime}\neq\emptyset
. We have

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}(\mu\times\nu)(Q)\geq\lambda(C^{\prime\prime})\gtrapprox 1.

By dyadic pigeonholing, there is a set
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and numbers
m_{x},m_{y}
so that

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}(\mu\times\nu)(Q)\gtrapprox 1,

(9.7)

m_{x}\leq(\mu(\pi_{x}(Q))\leq 2m_{x}
, and
m_{y}\leq(\mu(\pi_{x}(Q))\leq 2m_{y}
for each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. In particular,

\#\mathcal{Q}\approx(m_{x}m_{y})^{-1}.

Our next goal is to show that
\mathcal{Q}
resembles (a dense subset of) a Cartesian product. Let
A_{1}=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\pi_{x}(Q)
and let
B_{1}=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\pi_{y}(Q).
We have that
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are interior-disjoint unions of
\delta
-intervals contained in
I_{0}
and
J_{0}
, respectively;
m_{x}\leq\mu(I)<2m_{x}
for each
\delta
interval
I\subset A_{1}
;
m_{y}\leq\nu(J)<2m_{y}
for each
\delta
interval
J\subset B_{1}
; and
Q\subset A_{1}\times B_{1}
for each square
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
.

Clearly
A_{1}\times B_{1}
is a union of
\delta
-squares, and there are at least
\#\mathcal{Q}\approx(m_{x}m_{y})^{-1}
squares in this union. On the other hand, we have
m_{x}m_{y}\leq(\mu\times\nu)(Q)<4m_{x}m_{y}
for each
\delta
-square
Q\subset A_{1}\times B_{1}
, so
A_{1}\times B_{1}
is a union of
\lesssim(m_{x}m_{y})^{-1}

\delta
-squares.

Our final task is to show that
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
each have size roughly
\delta^{1-\alpha}
. First, since
\mathcal{Q}
contains
\gtrapprox(m_{x}m_{y})^{-1}
squares and
A_{1}\times B_{1}
contains
\lesssim(m_{x}m_{y})^{-1}
squares, there exists
y_{0}\in J_{0}
so that the line
y=y_{0}
intersects
\gtrapprox\delta^{-1}|A_{1}|
squares from
\mathcal{Q}
. By (
9.6
) and (
9.2
), we have

\delta^{1-\alpha-\varepsilon/10}\gtrapprox|C^{\prime\prime}|\geq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{Q}\\
Q\cap\{y=y_{0}\}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}P(Q)\gtrsim\delta\#(\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}\colon Q\cap\{y=y_{0}\}\neq\emptyset\})\gtrapprox|A_{1}|,

and an identical argument shows that

|B_{1}|\lessapprox\delta^{1-\alpha-\varepsilon/10}.

But (
9.3
) and (
9.7
) implies that
|A_{1}|,|B_{1}|\gtrapprox\delta^{1-\alpha+\varepsilon/10}.

We conclude that

\delta^{1-\alpha+\varepsilon/10}\lessapprox|A_{1}|,|B_{1}|\lessapprox\delta^{1-\alpha-\varepsilon/10}.

(9.8)

Next, if
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}
are two squares with the property that
P(Q)
and
P(Q^{\prime})
intersect a common
\delta
-interval from
C^{\prime\prime}
, and if
c>0
is the constant from (
9.2
), then there are points
(x,y)\in N_{c^{-1}\delta}(Q)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in N_{c^{-1}\delta}(Q^{\prime})
so that
P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. In particular, if we define
A_{2}=N_{c^{-1}\delta}A_{1}
and
B_{2}=N_{c^{-1}\delta}B_{1}
, then

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big(&\{x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in A_{2}^{2}\times B_{2}^{2}\colon P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)\\
&\gtrsim\#\{(Q,Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Q}^{2}\colon P(Q)\ \textrm{and}\ P(Q^{\prime})\ \textrm{intersect a common $\delta$-interval from}\ C^{\prime\prime}\}\\
&\gtrsim(\#\mathcal{Q})^{2}(\delta|C^{\prime\prime}|^{-1})\\
&\gtrapprox\delta^{-3\alpha+(4/5)\varepsilon},\end{split}

i.e. there is an absolute constant
K_{1}>0
and a constant
K_{2}>0
(which may depend on
\lambda(C)
) so that

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big(\{x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in A_{2}^{2}\times B_{2}^{2}\colon P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)\geq K_{2}^{-1}|\log\delta|^{-K_{1}}\delta^{-3\alpha+(4/5)\varepsilon}.

(9.9)

Finally, by (
9.3
), the sets
A_{2}
and
B_{2}
obey the hypotheses of Proposition
3.4
with parameters
\alpha
and
\kappa=\alpha/2
. Since
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
, there exists a constant
K_{3}
(which may depend on
\alpha
) so that

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\big(\{x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in A_{2}^{2}\times B_{2}^{2}\colon P(x,y)=P(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)\leq K_{3}\delta^{-3\alpha+\varepsilon}.

(9.10)

Thus if
\delta_{1}
is selected sufficiently small (depending on
K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}
), then (
9.10
) contradicts (
9.9
). We conclude that (
9.1
) must hold.
∎
