Abstract
. We propose a distributionally robust classification model with a fairness constraint that encourages the classifier to be fair in view of the equality of opportunity criterion. We use a type- ∞ \infty Wasserstein ambiguity set centered at the empirical distribution to model distributional uncertainty and derive a conservative reformulation for the worst-case equal opportunity unfairness measure. We establish that the model is equivalent to a mixed binary optimization problem, which can be solved by standard off-the-shelf solvers. To improve scalability, we further propose a convex, hinge-loss-based model for large problem instances whose reformulation does not incur any binary variables. Moreover, we also consider the distributionally robust learning problem with a generic ground transportation cost to hedge against the uncertainties in the label and sensitive attribute. Finally, we numerically demonstrate that our proposed approaches improve fairness with negligible loss of predictive accuracy.

The method we propose in this paper can be viewed as an adversarial approach pertaining to the third category.
More specifically, we consider the training problem of a general linear classifier, which is arguably one of the most popular classification methods in the statistical learning literature
[
36
]
. The classifier aims to establish a deterministic relationship between a feature vector
X\in\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a binary response, or label, variable
Y\in\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}
. Without any loss of generality, we associate the positive response with the “advantaged” outcome, such as “being hired” or “receiving a loan approval.” We also assume that there is a single sensitive attribute
A\in\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
. In a real-world setting, this sensitive attribute can represent information such as the race, gender, or age of a person, and it distinguishes the privileged from the unprivileged individuals. Throughout this paper, we assume that we possess a training data set containing
N

\{(\hat{x}_{i},\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and these samples are generated independently from a single data-generating probability distribution. In the setting, a classifier
\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is parameterized by a slope parameter
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an offset
b\in\mathbb{R}
, and the classification output is determined through an indicator function of the form

\mathcal{C}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }w^{\top}x+b\geq 0,\\
-1&\text{if }w^{\top}x+b<0.\end{cases}

Throughout, we consider the privileged learning setting in which the sensitive information is only available at the training stage but not at the testing stage
[
58
,
68
]
. It is therefore reasonable to consider only classifiers
\mathcal{C}
that do not take the sensitive attribute as input. In the context of linear binary classification problems, we need to find a classifier that maximizes the correct classification probability. To this end, we can consider the
correct classification probability
with respect to the distribution
\mathbb{Q}
as

\mathbb{Q}\left(Y(w^{\top}X+b)>0\right).

Complementarily, the
misclassification probability
with respect to
\mathbb{Q}
is defined as

\mathbb{Q}\left(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\leq 0\right).

Notice that by definition, we consider that any
x
falling exactly on the hyperplane
w^{\top}X+b=0
as misclassified irrespective of the true label of
x
. The linear classifier can be trained by solving the
misclassification probability minimization problem

(1)

\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},b\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathbb{Q}\left(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\leq 0\right).

To make the linear classifier fair, we can incorporate a measure of fairness into problem (
1
), either in the form of a constraint or in the form of an objective regularization. There are a plethora of fairness measure that we can utilize to promote fairness in this case, including the Equal Opportunity
[
34
]
, Demographic Parity
[
15

[
34
,
75
]
among many others. We refer the reader to the references
[
7

19
,
50
]
for comprehensive treatments of fairness in machine learning in general and in the classification problem in particular. In this paper, we focus on the Equal Opportunity (EO) criterion, which requires the true positive rate of the classifier is the same across the sensitive groups. The EO unfairness measure is challenging to formulate due to its non-convexity. Moreover, one can verify that the EO unfairness constraint leads to an open feasible set, which prohibits exact mixed binary programming reformulations. To alleviate intractability, simple convex functions such as linear functions have been used to approximate the unfairness measure
[
1
,
23
]
. Recently, the paper
[
72
]
proposes a mixed binary model that incorporates non-convex approximations of the fairness measures as a regularization term to enhance fairness. In our paper, we consider two different approximation schemes. We first propose a conservative approximation of the EO unfairness measure. The approximation admits a mixed binary reformulation, and it allows users to bound the EO unfairness measure explicitly. As the mixed binary model might not be efficiently solvable for large instances, we also develop a convex approximation based on the hinge loss function.

The existing notions of fairness proposed in the literature necessitate precise knowledge about the joint probability distribution that governs
(X,A,Y)
. In practice, this distribution is rarely available to the decision makers and is typically estimated using the empirical distribution generated from the imbalanced—and possibly biased—historical observations. While the empirical-based methods may work well on the observed data set, they often fail to yield complete fairness in practice because they do not generalize to out-of-sample data that have not been observed. For example, since there are fewer females in the technical positions at Amazon, relying on the empirical distribution can give rise to severe overfitting that yields an unfair hiring decision. On the other hand, even if the true underlying distribution is available, computing the fairness of the decision is generically intractable (#P-hard
[
25
]
) because it involves evaluating a multi-dimensional integration (e.g., computing the probability of getting hired conditionally on being an unprivileged person).

Fundamentally, promoting fairness in machine learning algorithms needs to balance among conflicting objectives including predictive accuracy, fairness, computational efficiency, while at the same time having to deal with mismatches between the training and the test data. In this paper, we endeavor to explore the trade-offs between these objectives using the ideas of
distributionally robust optimization (DRO)
. The DRO approach does not impose a single distribution of the features, the attributes and the response label of the entities in the population. Instead, it constructs a set of plausible probability distributions that are locally consistent with the available data set. The DRO approach then optimizes for a safe classifier that performs best in view of the most adverse distribution from within the prescribed distribution set. This approach thus may yield a fair classifier that has provable guarantees on the out-of-sample data.

Our paper belongs to an emerging class of fairness-aware distributionally robust algorithms. Previously, a repeated loss minimization model with a

When only the labels are noisy, robust fairness constraints based on a total variation ambiguity set is described in
[
69

[

[

[

]

[

In this paper, we consider the worst-case unfairness measure and the worst-case misclassification probability under the most unfavorable distribution from within the type-
\infty
Wasserstein ambiguity set constructed around the empirical distribution. If the radius of the ambiguity set vanishes to zero, our formulation recovers the unfairness measure evaluated at the empirical distribution. As such, our proposed conservative estimate can be leveraged as a regularization of the empirical-based method.

Contributions.
The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

•

Conservative approximation reformulation:
We robustify a recent unfairness measure motivated by the EO criterion and incorporate this unfairness measure into the distributionally robust misclassification probability minimization problem as a constraint. As the original model does not admit an exact reformulation, we propose a conservative approximation that can be reformulated as a mixed binary optimization program. Compared with existing approximations, ours is the first to guarantee an upper bound on the in-sample EO unfairness measure. Additionally, we illustrate how to generalize the conservative approximation model to handle ambiguity in the marginal distribution and obtain finite-sample guarantees.

•

Hinge-loss-based fairness-aware model:
To enhance scalability, we propose a
convex
distributionally robust fairness-aware classification model: this model uses the convex hinge loss function to approximate the unfairness measure and the objective function. Experimental results demonstrate that this classifier generates a marked improvement in terms of fairness, with a negligible loss of predictive accuracy. Interestingly, we find that minimizing the expected hinge loss, also known as the Support Vector Machine (SVM), is exactly the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) approximation of the misclassification probability minimization problem.

•

Training with the label and sensitive attribute uncertainties using type
\infty
-Wasserstein ambiguity sets:
We also consider the case where there are uncertainties in the label and sensitive attribute. To reduce the conservativeness, we develop a type-
\infty
Wasserstein ambiguity set with a side constraint that restricts the proportion of training samples whose sensitive attributes and labels can be ‘flipped.’ We then derive a mixed binary conic program reformulation and a linear program reformulation for training the conservative approximation model and the hinge-loss-based model with this ambiguity set, respectively.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
describes the distributionally robust fairness-aware classification problem. Section
3
proposes a conservative approximation to the original problem, and provides a binary optimization reformulation for training the model. Section
4
further proposes a convex fairness-aware model for large instances, and a convex optimization reformulation is derived for training. Section
5
discusses the situation where there are uncertainties in the sensitive attribute and label. Finally, Section
6
reports on the numerical experiments.

2.
Distributionally Robust Fairness-aware Linear Classifiers

Throughout this section, we focus on promoting fairness of a linear classifier with respect to the criterion of equal opportunity, or also known as equality of opportunity
[
33
]
. This criterion is formally defined as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Equal opportunity)
.

A classifier
\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
satisfies the equal opportunity criterion relative to
\mathbb{Q}
if

\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{C}(X)=1|A=1,Y=1)=\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{C}(X)=1|A=0,Y=1).

The definition indicates that the true positive rate is the same across the privileged and unprivileged groups. Based on this definition, we then further define the
equal opportunity unfairness measure
by

(2)

\mathds{U}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})\triangleq\left|\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{C}(X)=1|A=1,Y=1)-\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{C}(X)=1|A=0,Y=1)\right|,

which measures how biased the classification result is across the privileged and unprivileged groups.

We say that a classifier is
trivial
if it is parametrized by
(w,b)=(0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. In this case,
\mathcal{C}(x)=1
for any input
x\in\mathcal{X}
. It is easy to verify that the trivial classifier is also fair with respect to any possible distribution
\mathbb{Q}
. Our goal in this paper is to search for a
non-trivial
classifier that strikes a balance between promoting fairness and achieving superior predictive power.
To this end, suppose that
\mathbb{P}^{\star}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y})
is the data-generating distribution of the joint random vector
(X,A,Y)
. The fair linear classifier solves the constrained misclassification probability minimization problem

(3)

\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\mathbb{P}^{\star}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\leq 0)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\mathds{U}(w,b,\mathbb{P}^{\star})\leq\eta.\end{array}

The objective function of (
3
) minimizes the misclassification probability, while the constraint of (
3
) imposes an upper bound
\eta
on the unfairness measure with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
.
Unfortunately, the data-generating distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
is elusive to the decision maker. Even if
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
is known, the probabilistic program (
3
) is computationally intractable.

1

Formally, the problem of computing the probability of an event involving multiple random variables belongs to the complexity class #P-hard
[

]
—which is perceived to be ‘harder’ than the class NP-hard.
In a data-driven setting, we assume that we have access to
N
training samples generated from
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
. Let
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
be the empirical distribution supported on
\{(\hat{x}_{i},\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we will construct an ambiguity set around
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
using the Wasserstein distance.

Definition 2.2

(Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
c
be a metric on
\Xi
. The type-
p

(1\leq p<+\infty)
Wasserstein distance between
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
is defined as

\mathds{W}_{p}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})\triangleq\inf\left\{\big(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})^{p}]\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}:\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})\right\},

where
\Pi(\Xi\times\Xi)
is the set of all probability measures on
\Xi\times\Xi
with marginals
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
, respectively.
The type-
\infty
Wasserstein distance is defined as the limit of
\mathds{W}_{p}
as
p
tends to
\infty
and amounts to

\mathds{W}_{\infty}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})\triangleq\inf\left\{\mathrm{ess}\sup\limits_{\pi}\big\{c(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\Xi\times\Xi\big\}:\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})\right\}.

We let
\Xi=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
be the joint outcome space of the covariate, the sensitive attribute and the label. The ground metric on
\Xi
is supposed to be separable, meaning that
c
can be written as a sum of three components as

c\big((x^{\prime},a^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,a,y)\big)=\|x-x^{\prime}\|+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a-a^{\prime}|+\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y-y^{\prime}|

for some parameters
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}\in[0,+\infty]
and
\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}\in[0,+\infty]
.
Moreover, let
\hat{p}_{ay}=\hat{\mathbb{P}}(A=a,Y=y)
denote the empirical marginals constructed from the training samples. We will consider the following marginally-constrained ambiguity set

(4)

\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}):\begin{array}[]{l}\mathds{W}_{\infty}(\mathbb{Q},\hat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\rho,\\
\mathbb{Q}(A=a,Y=y)=\hat{p}_{ay}\quad\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\end{array}\right\},

which is a neighborhood around the empirical distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
. Intuitively,
\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
contains all the distributions of
(X,A,Y)
which is of a type-
\infty
Wasserstein distance less than or equal to
\rho
from
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
, and at the same time has the same marginal distribution on
(A,Y)
as
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
. The ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
is thus parametrized by
\rho
and the marginals
\hat{p}
; however, the dependence on these parameters is made implicit. Adding a marginal constraint to the ambiguity set is an expedient practice to achieve tractable reformulation, especially when dealing with conditional expectation constraints that are prevalent in fairness
[
66
]
. Indeed, conditional expectation is typically a non-linear function of the probability measure. However, when confining inside the set
\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
, we have

\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{C}(X)=1|A=a,Y=y)=\hat{p}_{ay}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{x:\mathcal{C}(x)=1\}}(X)\mathbbm{1}_{(a,y)}(A,Y)]\qquad\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y},

which are linear functions of
\mathbb{Q}
and conveniently simplifies the problem.

Equipped with the ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
, we can consider the fairness-aware distributionally robust linear classifier

(5)

\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{Q}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\leq 0)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathds{U}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})\leq\eta.\end{array}

The constraint of problem (
5
) depends on a tolerance
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
: it requires that the difference between the correct positive classification rates in two groups
A=0
and
A=1
to be smaller than
\eta
, uniformly over all distributions in the ambiguity set. It is easy to verify that the trivial classifier with
(w,b)=(0,0)
is feasible for (
5
) with an objective value of 1.

Unfortunately, it is challenging to transform problem (
5
) into an exact reformulation for the off-the-shelf solvers.
To see this, consider the simplest case where
\rho=0
, which implies that
\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})=\{\hat{\mathbb{P}}\}
, and the constraint of (
5
) becomes
\mathds{U}(w,b,\hat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\eta
. Let us define the index set
\mathcal{I}_{a1}=\left\{i\in[N]:\hat{a}_{i}=a,\hat{y}_{i}=1\right\}
containing indices of the samples with sensitive attribute
a
and label
1
. Fixing any pair
(a,a^{\prime})\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{U}(w,b,\hat{\mathbb{P}})=

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\mathbb{P}}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0|A=a,Y=1)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0|A=a^{\prime},Y=1)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}[\hat{p}_{a1}^{-1}\mathbbm{I}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)\mathbbm{1}_{(a,1)}(A,Y)-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)\mathbbm{1}_{(a^{\prime},1)}(A,Y)]\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\left(\hat{p}_{a1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1}}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+b\geq 0)+\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+b<0)-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}|\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}|\right)\right|.

Thus, for any pair
(a,a^{\prime})\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}
, its corresponding fairness constraint can be written as

\left|\frac{1}{N}\left(\hat{p}_{a1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1}}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b\geq 0)+\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b<0)-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}|\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}|\right)\right|\leq\eta

Consider now the simplest case where
\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}
is empty. Then the constraint only involves the first part

\frac{1}{N}\left(\hat{p}_{a1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1}}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+b\geq 0)\right)\leq\eta.

It can be verified that for
\eta\in(0,1)
, the feasible region of
(w,b)
with such constraints is an open set that cannot be exactly reformulated to a solvable form even with the big-M constraints. For example, if
\eta<1/(N\hat{p}_{a1})
, then
(w,b)
must satisfy
w^{\top}x_{i}+b<0~\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1}
to be feasible to the constraint. In the following sections, we will develop approximations to problem (
5
) that are amenable to solutions using off-the-shelf solvers.

3.
\varepsilon
-Distributionally Robust Fairness-aware Classifier

In this section, we propose a conservative approximation of the original problem (
5
). Notice that the openness of the feasible set as previously described is because the function
\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+b\geq 0)
is an upper-semicontinuous function in the variable
(w,b)
. In order to generate a closed approximation of the feasible set, it requires to change the inequality sign to a
strict
inequality. Moreover, to guarantee robustness, we also require an inner approximation by modifying the right-hand side to a negative quantity
-\varepsilon
.
To proceed, given any probability measure
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y})
, we can leverage the finite cardinality of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Y}
to decompose
\mathbb{Q}
using its conditional measures
\mathbb{Q}_{ay}(X\in\cdot)=\mathbb{Q}(X\in\cdot|A=a,Y=y)
.
We now define the
\varepsilon
-
un
fairness measure
\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}
as

\displaystyle\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})\triangleq\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w^{\top}X+b>-\varepsilon)-\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0),\\
\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w^{\top}X+b>-\varepsilon)-\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)\end{array}\right\},

which is parametrized by a strictly positive value
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
. Similarly, as the objective function of (
5
) does not admit an exact reformulation, we replace it with
\mathbb{Q}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)<\varepsilon)
, which is a conservative approximation of the misclassification probability for any
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
. The next proposition demonstrates that these approximations are tight in the limit as
\varepsilon
tends to zero.

Proposition 3.1

(Convergence)
.

Fix a measure
\mathbb{Q}
, the
\varepsilon
-unfairness measure
\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}
converges to the EO unfairenss measure
\mathds{U}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, i.e.,

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})=\mathds{U}(w,b,\mathbb{Q}).

And similarly, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{Q}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)<\varepsilon)=\mathbb{Q}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\geq 0).

3.1
.

See Appendix
A.1
.

Now, consider the following
\varepsilon
-distributionally robust fairness-aware classification (
\varepsilon
-DRFC) problem

(8)

\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{Q}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)<\varepsilon)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})\leq\eta.\end{array}

We remark that when defining the
\varepsilon
-DRFC model (
8
), we can also use two separate parameters of
\varepsilon
: one for the objective function and one for
\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}
. Nevertheless, we opt for a single parameter
\varepsilon
to simplify the notation, and also to alleviate the burden for parameter tuning.
The next result shows that problem (
8
) is well-defined in the sense that its feasible set contains a non-trivial classifier.

Proposition 3.2

(Feasibility)
.

For any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, there exists a non-trivial classifier that is feasible for problem (
8
).

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

It suffices to show that problem (
8
) is feasible for
\eta=0
. Let us consider a hyperplane parameterized by
(w_{s},b_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
such that
w_{s}^{\top}x+b_{s}>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
, where
\mathbb{X}
is defined as in Lemma
A.1
. Because
\mathbb{X}
is compact and convex, the existence of the hyperplane
(w_{s},b_{s})
is a direct result of the separating hyperplane theorem
[
14
, §2.5.1]
. In this case, one can verify that for any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w_{s}^{\top}X+b_{s}>-\varepsilon)-\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w_{s}^{\top}X+b_{s}\geq 0)=1-1=0

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w_{s}^{\top}X+b_{s}>-\varepsilon)-\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w_{s}^{\top}X+b_{s}\geq 0)=1-1=0,

where
\mathbb{Q}_{ay}
are the conditional distributions of
X
given
(A=a,Y=y)
.
This implies that

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathds{U}(w_{s},b_{s},\mathbb{Q})\leq 0,

and thus
(w_{s},b_{s})
is feasible for problem (
8
) at
\eta=0
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now show that the
\varepsilon
-DRFC problem (
8
) is a conservative approximation of (
5
).

Proposition 3.3

(Conservative approximation)
.

For any
\eta\in[0,1]
, let
(w^{\star},b^{\star})
be the optimal solution to problem (
8
). Then
(w^{\star},b^{\star})
is feasible for problem (
5
). Moreover, let
v^{\star}
be the corresponding optimal value of (
8
), then

\mathbb{Q}(Y(({w^{\star}})^{\top}X+b^{\star})\leq 0)\leq v^{\star}\qquad\forall\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}).

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

By definition, we find

\displaystyle\mathds{U}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})=

\displaystyle|\mathbb{Q}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0|A=1,Y=1)-\mathbb{Q}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0|A=0,Y=1)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)-\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0),\\
\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)-\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w^{\top}X+b>-\varepsilon)-\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0),\\
\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w^{\top}X+b>-\varepsilon)-\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}(w,b,\mathbb{Q}),

for every possible value of the classifier parameter
(w,b)
and any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
. As a consequence, the feasible region of problem (
8
) is an
inner
approximation of the feasible region of problem (
5
) for any
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
. This implies that the optimal solution
(w^{\star},b^{\star})
of problem (
8
) is also feasible for problem (
5
).

Furthermore, one can verify that for any
(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and any distribution
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\leq 0)\leq\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\leq 0)\leq\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)<\varepsilon).

Thus, by plugging in the optimal solution
(w^{\star},b^{\star})
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(Y((w^{\star})^{\top}X+b^{\star})\leq 0)

\displaystyle\leq v^{\star}\qquad\forall\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}),

which completes the proof.
∎

The
\varepsilon
-DRFC model (
8
) enables decision makers to bound the unfairness measure in the training set explicitly using
\eta
. Moreover, as shown in Proposition
3.3
, the optimal value of problem (
8
) constitutes an upper bound on the misclassification probability.

Remark 3.4

(Out-of-sample guarantee)
.

We also investigate the out-of-sample performance of the model (
8
). Note that the ambiguity set (
4
) contains marginal constraints that require probability measures in the ambiguity sets to have the same marginal distribution as the empirical distribution. This constraint invalidates the finite sample guarantees unless the true distribution shares the same marginal distribution with the empirical distribution. In Appendix
B
, we illustrate that relaxing the marginal constraints does admit a solvable model with attractive theoretical results; however, the model is more computationally intensive as we add an extra layer of robustness.

For any
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
, problem (
8
) admits a mixed binary reformulation, which is our next focus. We first consider the case where we have absolute trust in sensitive attributes and labels, i.e., we use the ground metric

(9)

c\big((x^{\prime},a^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,a,y)\big)=\|x-x^{\prime}\|+\infty|a-a^{\prime}|+\infty|y-y^{\prime}|,

where
\|\cdot\|
is an arbitrary norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Notice that in this setting, we have set
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}=\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}=\infty
, which indicates that we have absolute trust in the value of the sensitive attribute
A
and the label
Y
. When
c
is chosen as in (
9
), a simple modification of the proof of
[
65
, Theorem 3.2]
shows that any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
with
\mathds{W}_{\infty}(\mathbb{Q},\hat{\mathbb{P}})<\infty
should satisfy
\mathbb{Q}(A=a,Y=y)=\hat{p}_{ay}
for all
(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
. As a consequence, the marginal constraint in the definition of the set
\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
becomes redundant and can be omitted. This simplification with absolute trust in the sensitive attribute and label has been previously exploited to derive hypothesis tests for fair classifiers
[
65
]
and to train fair logistic classifier
[
66
]
.

Theorem 3.5

(
\varepsilon
-DRFC reformulation)
.

Suppose that the ground metric is prescribed using (
9
), then the
\varepsilon
-DRFC model (
8
) is equivalent to the conic mixed binary optimization problem

(10)

\begin{array}[]{cll}\min&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;b\in\mathbb{R},\;t\in\{0,1\}^{N},\;\lambda^{0}\in\{0,1\}^{N},\;\lambda^{1}\in\{0,1\}^{N},\\
&-\hat{y}_{i}(w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+b)+\rho\|w\|_{*}\leq Mt_{i}-1&\forall i\in[N],\\
&\hskip-5.69054pt\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{a1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1}}\lambda_{i}^{a}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}}\lambda_{i}^{a}-1\leq\eta,\\
w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+\rho\|w\|_{*}+b+\varepsilon\leq M\lambda_{i}^{a}\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1},\\
-w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+\rho\|w\|_{*}-b\leq M\lambda_{i}^{a}\qquad\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}\end{array}\right\}&\forall(a,a^{\prime})\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\},\end{array}

where

is the big-M parameter.

For notational simplicity, we present the reformulation (
10
) with
3N
binary variables. A closer investigation into problem (
10
) reveals that it suffices to use
N+2|\mathcal{I}_{1}|
binary variables, where
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{i\in[N]:\hat{y}_{i}=1\}
is the index set of training samples with positive labels. If
\|\cdot\|
is either the 1-norm or the
\infty
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

) is a linear mixed binary optimization problem. If
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm, problem (

[
53
]
.

For the remainder of this section, we will provide the proof for Theorem
3.5
. This proof relies on the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 3.6

(Indicator function reformulation)
.

Fix any index set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
, a radius
\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, a classifier
(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and a collection of samples
\{\hat{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
. For any
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\sup\limits_{x_{k}:\|x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k}\|\leq\rho}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{k}+b>\varepsilon)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\lambda_{k}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\lambda\in\{0,1\}^{N},\\
&w^{\top}\hat{x}_{k}+\rho\|w\|_{*}+b-\varepsilon\leq M\lambda_{k}\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{K},\end{array}\right.

where
M
is the big-M parameter.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Using an epigraphical formulation of each supremum term, we find

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\sup\limits_{x_{k}:\|x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k}\|\leq\rho}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{k}+b>\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\lambda_{k}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\lambda\in\{0,1\}^{N},\\
&\sup\limits_{x_{k}:\|x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k}\|\leq\rho}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{k}+b>\varepsilon)\leq\lambda_{k}\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{K}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\lambda_{k}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\lambda\in\{0,1\}^{N},\\
&\max\limits_{x_{k}:\|x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k}\|\leq\rho}w^{\top}x_{k}+b-\varepsilon\leq M\lambda_{k}\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{K},\end{array}\right.

where
M
is the big-M constant. The dual norm definition implies that

\max\limits_{x_{k}:\|x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k}\|\leq\rho}w^{\top}x_{k}=w^{\top}\hat{x}_{k}+\rho\|w\|_{*},

where
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is the dual norm of
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem

.

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})

Define the following index sets

\mathcal{I}_{a1}=\left\{i\in[N]:\hat{a}_{i}=a,\hat{y}_{i}=1\right\}~\forall a\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\min&\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{a1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1}}\lambda_{i}^{a}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}}\lambda_{i}^{a}-1\\

where the last equality follows by applying Lemma

twice and by noticing that

The deterministic reformulation (
10
) may encounter computational difficulties as the sample size
N
grows large because it involves
\mathcal{O}(N)
binary variables. Thus, there is merit in studying tractable approximations with better scalability properties. In the next section, we will propose a convex model that is efficiently solvable for large problem instances.

4.
Hinge Distributionally Robust Fairness-aware Classifier

Throughout this section, we propose a convex approximation of (
5
) which requires no binary variables in the reformulation. Towards this end, we replace the probability quantity
\mathbb{Q}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\leq 0)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\mathbbm{I}(Y(w^{\top}X+b)\leq 0)\right]
in the objective function of (
5
) by the expected hinge loss

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\max\left\{0,1-Y(w^{\top}X+b)\right\}\right],

which is a convex approximation of the misclassification probability. To have a convex approximation of the EO unfairness measure (
2
), we can rewrite this quantity as

\displaystyle\mathds{U}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)+\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w^{\top}X+b<0)-1,\\
\mathbb{Q}_{11}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)+\mathbb{Q}_{01}(w^{\top}X+b<0)-1\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}\left[\mathbbm{I}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}\left[\mathbbm{I}(w^{\top}X+b<0)\right]-1,\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}\left[\mathbbm{I}(w^{\top}X+b\geq 0)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}\left[\mathbbm{I}(w^{\top}X+b<0)\right]-1\end{array}\right\}.

Then, similar to the objective function, we propose a hinge-loss-based unfairness measure to approximate the EO criteria. The
hinge unfairness measure
is defined as

\mathds{H}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})\triangleq\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}\left[\max\{0,1+w^{\top}X+b\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}\left[\max\{0,1-w^{\top}X-b\}\right]-1,\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}\left[\max\{0,1+w^{\top}X+b\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}\left[\max\{0,1-w^{\top}X-b\}\right]-1\end{array}\right\}.

Combining the hinge loss objective and the hinge unfairness measure, we arrive at the following hinge distributionally robust fairness-aware classification (HDRFC) problem:

(11)

\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\max\{0,1-Y(w^{\top}X+b)\}]\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathds{H}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})\leq\zeta.\end{array}

The constraint of problem (
8
) depends on a tolerance
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
: it requires that the hinge unfairness measure to be smaller than
\eta
, uniformly over all distributions in the ambiguity set. It can be easily verified that the upper bound of
\mathds{H}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})
is
\infty
. The following proposition shows that the lower bound of
\mathds{H}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})
is 1. Moreover, when it achieves the minimum, the expected distance to the classification hyperplane is uncorrelated with respect to the sensitive attribute conditioned on the label being positive.

Proposition 4.1

(Lower bound)
.

For any
(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
, we have

\mathds{H}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})\geq 1.
And when
\mathds{H}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})=1
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}[w^{\top}X+b]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}[w^{\top}X+b]
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

See Appendix
A.2
.
∎

The hinge unfairness measure
\mathds{H}
is a convex approximation of the EO unfairness measure. Compared with the
\varepsilon
-unfairness measure
\mathds{U}_{\varepsilon}
defined in Section
3
, the hinge unfairness measure does not provide a tight upper bound for the EO unfairness measure. Nevertheless, the hinge unfairness measure is an attractive formulation because it is amenable to a convex reformulation, which is essential for solving large-scale problems.

Remark 4.2

(SVM and CVaR)
.

To obtain a conservative approximation of the misclassification probability minimization problem (
1
), one could also employ the popular Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR)
[
60
]
, which is the best-known convex approximation of probabilistic constraints
[
54
]
. Interestingly, we find that the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model, which minimizes the expected hinge loss, is exactly the CVaR approximation of problem (
1

C
.

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathds{H}(w,b,\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}\left[\max\{0,1+w^{\top}X+b\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}\left[\max\{0,1-w^{\top}X-b\}\right]-1,\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}\left[\max\{0,1+w^{\top}X+b\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}\left[\max\{0,1-w^{\top}X-b\}\right]-1\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}\left[\max\{0,1+w^{\top}X+b\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}\left[\max\{0,1-w^{\top}X-b\}\right]-1,\\
\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{11}}\left[\max\{0,1+w^{\top}X+b\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{01}}\left[\max\{0,1-w^{\top}X-b\}\right]-1\end{array}\right\}.

Fixing any pair
(a,a^{\prime})\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathds{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}_{a1}}\left[\max\{0,1+w^{\top}X+b\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{a^{\prime}1}}\left[\max\{0,1-w^{\top}X-b\}\right]-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\hat{p}_{a1}^{-1}\max\{0,1+w^{\top}X+b\}\mathbbm{1}_{(a,1)}(A,Y)+\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\max\{0,1-w^{\top}X-b\}\mathbbm{1}_{(a^{\prime},1)}(A,Y)\right]-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left(\hat{p}_{a1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1}}\sup\limits_{x_{i}:\|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho}\max\{0,1+w^{\top}x_{i}+b\}+\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}}\sup\limits_{x_{i}:\|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho}\max\{0,1-w^{\top}x_{i}-b\}-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}|\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\min&\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{a1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1}}\lambda_{i}^{a}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}}\lambda_{i}^{a}-1\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\lambda^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+},\\
&1+w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+\rho\|w\|_{*}+b\leq\lambda_{i}^{a}&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{a1},\\
&1-w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+\rho\|w\|_{*}-b\leq\lambda_{i}^{a}&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1},\end{array}\right.

where the last equality follows by applying Lemma
3.6
twice and by noticing that
\mathcal{I}_{a1}\cap\mathcal{I}_{a^{\prime}1}=\emptyset
. Setting the optimal value of the above minimization problem to be less than
\eta
completes the proof.
∎

In the remainder of this section, we will provide the proof of Theorem

Lemma 5.2
.

(Strong duality)
Let
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Borel measurable loss function. Then for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
, the semi-infinite program

(16)

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\phi(X,A,Y)]

admits the following dual form

\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\nu_{i}+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\hat{p}_{a,y}\mu_{a,y}-\theta(1-\gamma)&\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{N},~\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+},~\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2},\\
&\sup\limits_{x:\|x-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y-\hat{y}_{i}|}\phi(x,a,y)\leq\mu_{ay}-\theta\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a,y)+\nu_{i}&\forall i\in[N]\quad\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y},\end{array}

where the supremum value is considered to be
-\infty
if the corresponding feasible set is empty.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Using the definition of the type-
\infty
Wasserstein distance, we can re-express the ambiguity set
\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})=

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}):\begin{array}[]{l}\exists\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y})~\forall i\in[N]\text{ such that }\mathbb{Q}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i},\\
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}(A=a,Y=y)=\hat{p}_{ay}\quad\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y},\\
\|x-\hat{x}_{i}\|+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a-\hat{a}_{i}|+\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y-\hat{y}_{i}|\leq\rho\quad\forall(x,a,y)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{i})\quad\forall i\in[N],\\
\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(A=\hat{a}_{i},Y=\hat{y}_{i})\geq(1-\gamma)N\\
\end{array}\right\}.

The worst-case expected loss can now be written as

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\phi(X,A,Y)]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\sup&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}~\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}[\phi(X,A,Y)]\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y})&\forall i\in[N],\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(A=a,Y=y)=N\hat{p}_{ay}&\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y},\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(A=\hat{a}_{i},Y=\hat{y}_{i})\geq(1-\gamma)N,\\
&\|x-\hat{x}_{i}\|+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a-\hat{a}_{i}|+\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y-\hat{y}_{i}|\leq\rho&\forall(x,a,y)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{i})\quad\forall i\in[N].\end{array}\right.

Any
\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y})
can be decomposed as

\pi_{i}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}\times\mathrm{d}y^{\prime})=\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\tau_{iay}\pi_{iay}(\mathrm{d}x)\delta_{(a,y)}(\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}\times\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}),

where
\pi_{iay}
is the conditional distribution of
X
given that
(A,Y)=(a,y)
and the nonnegative weights
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N\times|\mathcal{A}|\times|\mathcal{Y}|}
satisfy

\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\tau_{iay}=1\qquad\forall i\in[N].

Moreover, define the following optimal values

v_{iay}=\sup\{\phi(x,a,y):x\in\mathcal{X},~\|x-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y-\hat{y}_{i}|\}

for each
i\in[N]
and
(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Denote momentarily the feasible set of the above optimization problem as
\mathcal{X}_{iay}
. Notice that
\mathcal{X}_{iay}=\emptyset
if
\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y-\hat{y}_{i}|<0
and in this case we set
v_{iay}=-\infty
. By definition, we also have

v_{iay}=\sup\limits_{\pi_{iay}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X})}\int_{\mathcal{X}_{iay}}\phi(x,a,y)\pi_{iay}(\mathrm{d}x)

whenever
\mathcal{X}_{iay}
is non-empty.
Using this definition of
v
and by the above decomposition of
\pi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\phi(X,A,Y)]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\sup&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}~\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\tau_{iay}v_{iay}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\tau_{iay}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}&\forall i\in[N]\quad\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y},\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\tau_{iay}=1&\forall i\in[N],\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\tau_{iay}=N\hat{p}_{ay}&\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y},\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\tau_{i\hat{a}_{i}\hat{y}_{i}}\geq(1-\gamma)N,\end{array}\right.

which is a finite-dimensional linear program. Strong duality result from linear programming asserts that

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\phi(X,A,Y)]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\nu_{i}+\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\hat{p}_{ay}\mu_{ay}-\theta(1-\gamma)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{N},~\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|\times|\mathcal{Y}|},~\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\\
&v_{iay}\leq\nu_{i}+\mu_{ay}-\theta\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a,y)&\forall i\in[N]\quad\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}.\end{array}\right.

Substituting the definition of
v
into the above optimization problem completes the proof.
∎

In this paper, we developed a new principled approach to fair classification by incorporating the equality of opportunity criterion as a constraint and robustifying the resulting optimization problem using the framework of Wasserstein min-max learning. We utilize the type-
\infty
Wasserstein ambiguity set, which generally enables a more scalable conic programming reformulation while providing the same statistical performance guarantees as the models based on the type-1 Wasserstein ambiguity sets. In addition, our proposed model can handle problem instances with noisy, adversarial sensitive attributes and labels.

Since the original problem cannot be reformulated exactly, we propose a conservative approximation. We remark that this conservative approximation is amenable to a mixed binary linear programming reformulation. Moreover, this approximation, for the first time, enables decision makers to bound the EO unfairness measure explicitly. However, experimental results indicate that the reformulation is not as efficiently solvable as plain-vanilla models such as SVM and logistic regression. To address this issue, we further approximate both the objective function and the unfairness measure using the hinge loss function to obtain a convex model. We find that the hinge-loss-based distributionally robust fairness-aware model plays favorably compared to the state-of-the-art method DOB+ and DRFLR in the numerical experiments. In summary, we propose a tight conservative fairness-aware classifier for moderate-size problems and an efficient, high-quality fairness-aware classifier for large instances.

\textup{Prob}\left(\mathds{U}(w,b,\mathbb{P}^{\star})\leq\eta\right)\geq 1-C_{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(N\delta_{p}-\sqrt{2kN\delta_{p}-k^{2}}\right)}-C_{3}\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}|\mathcal{I}_{ay}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}},

where
C_{1}
is a constant which depends on the true distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and
\alpha
,
C_{2}
is a constant which depends on the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
, and
C_{3}
is an universal constant.

The proof of Theorem
B.3
relies on the following theorem, which provides the concentration inequality for the type
\infty
-Wasserstein distance.

Theorem B.4

(
\infty
-Wasserstein concentration, Theorem
1.1
in
[
67
]
)
.

Assume that the probability distribution of
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
has a density function
\tau:\bar{\Xi}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, where
\bar{\Xi}\subseteq\Xi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open, connected, and bounded set with a Lipschitz boundary, and there exists a constant
\lambda\geq 1
such that
1/\lambda\leq\tau(\xi)\leq\lambda
for all
\xi\in\bar{\Xi}
. Then, for any fixed
\alpha>2
,

\textup{Prob}\left(\mathds{W}_{\infty}(\mathbb{P}^{\star},\hat{\mathbb{P}})>C\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\log(N)^{3/4}}{N^{1/2}},&\textup{if}\ d=1\\
\frac{\log(N)^{1/d}}{N^{1/d}},&\textup{if}\ d\geq 2\end{array}\right.\right)=\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}),

where
C
is a constant which depends only on
\alpha
,
\bar{\Xi}
, and
\lambda
.

Equipped with Theorem
B.4
, we are now ready to show the proof of Theorem
B.3
.

Proof of Theorem
B.3
.

For any
(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
,
\hat{p}_{ay}
is an estimator given by

\hat{p}_{ay}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{(a,y)}(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i}).

Let
p_{ay}
denotes the mass of the true marginal distribution on
(a,y)
. It can be verified that
N\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}(p_{ay}-\hat{p}_{ay})^{2}/p_{ay}
asymptotically converges to
\chi^{2}
with degree
k=3

[
13
]
. To obtain an explicit and concise result, we employ the
\chi^{2}
test-statistic upper tail bound (Lemma 1 in
[
45
]
), which can be written as

\textup{Prob}\left(Z\geq k+2\sqrt{kt}+2t\right)\leq e^{-t},

where
Z\sim\chi^{2}_{k}
and
t\geq 0
. By letting
N\delta_{p}=k+\sqrt{kt}+2t
and solving for
t
, we further obtain

\textup{Prob}\left(N\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}(p_{ay}-\hat{p}_{ay})^{2}/p_{ay}>N\delta_{p}\right)\leq C_{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(N\delta_{p}-\sqrt{2kN\delta_{p}-k^{2}}\right)},

where
C_{2}
is a constant that depends on the underlying distribution. We assume the conditions of Theorem
B.4
hold and
d\geq 2
. Then there exists
C_{3}>0
such that setting
\rho_{ay}=C_{1}\frac{\log(|\mathcal{I}_{ay}|)^{1/d}}{|\mathcal{I}_{ay}|^{1/d}}
implies

\textup{Prob}\left(\mathds{W}_{\infty}(\mathbb{P}_{ay}^{\star},\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{ay})>\rho\right)\leq C_{3}|\mathcal{I}_{ay}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\qquad\forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}.

By union bound, we further obtain

\textup{Prob}\left(\mathbb{P}_{ay}^{\star}\in\mathbb{B}_{ay}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{ay})\ \forall(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\ \textup{and}\ p\in\Delta\right)\geq 1-C_{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(N\delta_{p}-\sqrt{2kN\delta_{p}-k^{2}}\right)}-\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}C_{3}|\mathcal{I}_{ay}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

This result immediately leads to the statements in the theorem.
∎

Theorem
B.3
requires choosing the radius

based on the constants

and

, and the probabilistic guarantee also depends on the universal constant

. However, these parameters depend on the properties of the underlying distribution

Appendix C
Relationship between SVM and CVaR

In this section, we show that the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model is exactly the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) approximation of the misclassification probability minimization problem (
1
). SVM is a linear classifier obtained by determining the parameters
(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
that minimizes the empirical expected
hinge loss
:

(38)

\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},b\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\left\{0,1-Y(w^{\top}X+b)\right\}\right].

Observe that problem (
1
) under the empirical distribution can be equivalently written as

(39)

\begin{array}[]{cl}\inf&t\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~t\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\hat{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y(w^{\top}X+b)>0\right)\geq 1-t.\end{array}

The optimization problem above can be regarded as an extension of chance constrained programs, with the quantile
t
being a decision variable. However, the feasible set of such a chance constraint is non-convex, which makes optimization problematic in the face of large instances. A natural way to overcome this difficulty is to replace the chance constraint with a tractable approximation. To obtain an efficient model for large sample sizes, we require the approximation to be convex. Moreover, we also like the approximation to be conservative, i.e., if a solution is feasible in the approximated problem then it is also feasible to the original problem. If these two conditions hold, we refer to the approximation as a convex conservative approximation.

To this end, the well-known CVaR can be used to derive such an approximation. The core idea lies in the fact that the chance constraint in (
39
) can be written as a Value at Risk (VaR) constraint. This yields the equivalent reformulation

\begin{array}[]{cl}\inf&t\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~t\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\textup{VaR}_{(t,\hat{\mathbb{P}})}(-Y(w^{\top}X+b))<0,\end{array}

where
\textup{VaR}_{(t,\hat{\mathbb{P}})}(Z))\triangleq\inf\{\tau\in\mathbb{R}:\hat{\mathbb{P}}(Z\leq\tau)\geq 1-t\}
can be interpreted as the
1-t
quantile of
Z
. Recall that the CVaR of a random variable
Z
is defined as

\textup{CVaR}_{(t,\hat{\mathbb{P}})}(Z)\triangleq\inf\left\{\tau\in\mathbb{R}:\tau+\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\{0,Z-\tau\}\right]\right\}.

It can be verified that
\textup{VaR}_{(t,\hat{\mathbb{P}})}(Z)
is a minimizer of the right hand side problem
[
54
]
. Thus, the relation
\textup{VaR}_{(t,\hat{\mathbb{P}})}(Z))\leq\textup{CVaR}_{(t,\hat{\mathbb{P}})}(Z)
holds for any distribution, and we can simply replace the VaR term using CVaR, and obtain the following convex conservative approximation to the chance constrained program (
39
):

(40)

\begin{array}[]{cl}\inf&t\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~t\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\textup{CVaR}_{(t,\hat{\mathbb{P}})}(-Y(w^{\top}X+b))<0.\end{array}

Interestingly, we find that the CVaR approximation problem (
40
) is exactly equivalently to the SVM model (
38
). That is, the optimal value of (
40
) coincides with the optimal value of (
38
), and the two problems yield the same optimal classifiers. This result connects the well-known SVM model with the misclassification minimization problem from the perspective of conservative approximation.

Theorem C.1

(CVaR equivalence)
.

The CVaR approximation (
40
) is equivalent to the SVM model (
38
).

Proof of Theorem
C.1
.

Let
t^{\star}
be the optimal value of (
40
). When
t^{\star}=0
, one can verify that this condition implies the dataset is linearly separable, and the optimal value of the SVM model (
38
) will also be zero. Now, without loss of generality, we assume
t^{\star}>0
. By the definition of CVaR, we have

\displaystyle t^{\star}

\displaystyle=\inf~\left\{t:\begin{array}[]{l}w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{++},\\
\textup{CVaR}_{(t,\hat{\mathbb{P}})}(-Y(w^{\top}X+b))<0\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf~\left\{t:\begin{array}[]{l}w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{++},\\
\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}-\beta+\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\{\beta-Y(w^{\top}X+b),0\}\right]<0\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf~\left\{t:\begin{array}[]{l}w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~\beta\in\mathbb{R},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{++},\\
-\beta+\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\{\beta-Y(w^{\top}X+b),0\}\right]<0.\end{array}\right\}.

Notice that when
\beta\leq 0
, the constraint is always infeasible; hence, we can restrict
\beta
to be positive without changing the feasible region, which yields

\displaystyle t^{\star}

\displaystyle=\inf~\left\{t:\begin{array}[]{l}w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{++},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{++}\\
-\beta+\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\{\beta-Y(w^{\top}X+b),0\}\right]<0\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf~\left\{t:\begin{array}[]{l}w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{++},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{++}\\
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\{\beta-Y(w^{\top}X+b),0\}\right]<\beta t\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf~\left\{t:\begin{array}[]{l}w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b\in\mathbb{R},~\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{++},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{++}\\
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\left\{1-Y\left(\left(\frac{w}{\beta}\right)^{\top}X+\left(\frac{b}{\beta}\right)\right),0\right\}\right]<t\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf~\left\{t:\begin{array}[]{l}w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{++}\\
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\left\{1-Y\left(w^{\prime\top}X+b^{\prime}\right),0\right\}\right]<t\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\max\left\{1-Y\left(w^{\prime\top}X+b^{\prime}\right),0\right\}\right]\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},\end{array}\right.

where the penultimate equality holds by setting
w^{\prime}=w/\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}=b/\beta
. Thus, the optimal value of these two problem coincides. Furthermore, by noticing that
w^{\prime}=w/\beta
and
b^{\prime}=b/\beta
, the corresponding optimal hyperplanes
w^{\top}X+b=0
and
w^{\prime\top}X+b^{\prime}=0
are also the same. This completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\forall x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}:\|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a^{\prime}-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|}-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b\geq 0)\leq\mu_{a^{\prime}1}^{a}-\theta^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a^{\prime},1)+\nu_{i}^{a}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\inf\limits_{\|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a^{\prime}-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b\geq 0)\leq\mu_{a^{\prime}1}^{a}-\theta^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a^{\prime},1)+\nu_{i}^{a}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\left(1-\sup\limits_{\|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a^{\prime}-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b<0)\right)\leq\mu_{a^{\prime}1}^{a}-\theta^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}^{\prime},\hat{y}_{i})}(a^{\prime},1)+\nu_{i}^{a}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda_{a^{\prime}i}^{a}\in\{0,1\},\\
\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}(\lambda_{a^{\prime}i}^{a}-1)\leq\mu_{a^{\prime}1}^{a}-\theta^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a^{\prime},1)+\nu_{i}^{a},\\
-w^{\top}\hat{x}_{i}+(\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a^{\prime}-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|1-\hat{y}_{i}|)\|w\|_{*}-b\leq M\lambda_{a^{\prime}i}^{a}.\end{array}\right.

(3)

Case 3:
(\bar{a}_{i},\bar{y}_{i})=(a,-1)
. There is an active constraint if
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a-\hat{a}_{i}|+\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|-1-\hat{y}_{i}|\leq\rho
, the constraint is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\forall x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}:\|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|-1-\hat{y}_{i}|}\hat{p}_{a1}^{-1}\mathbbm{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b>-\varepsilon)\mathbbm{1}_{(a,1)}(a,-1)-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b\geq 0)\mathbbm{1}_{(a^{\prime},1)}(a,-1)

\displaystyle\hskip 312.9803pt\leq\mu_{a,-1}^{a}-\theta^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a,-1)+\nu_{i}^{a}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle 0\leq\mu_{a,-1}^{a}-\theta^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a,-1)+\nu_{i}^{a}.

(4)

Case 4:
(\bar{a}_{i},\bar{y}_{i})=(a^{\prime},-1)
. There is an active constraint if
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a^{\prime}-\hat{a}_{i}|+\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|-1-\hat{y}_{i}|\leq\rho
, the constraint is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\forall x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}:\|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\|\leq\rho-\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a^{\prime}-\hat{a}_{i}|-\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|-1-\hat{y}_{i}|}\hat{p}_{a1}^{-1}\mathbbm{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b>-\varepsilon)\mathbbm{1}_{(a,1)}(a^{\prime},-1)-\hat{p}_{a^{\prime}1}^{-1}\mathbb{I}(w^{\top}x_{i}+b\geq 0)\mathbbm{1}_{(a^{\prime},1)}(a^{\prime},-1)

\displaystyle\hskip 312.9803pt\leq\mu_{a^{\prime},-1}^{a}-\theta\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a^{\prime},-1)+\nu_{i}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle 0\leq\mu_{a^{\prime},-1}^{a}-\theta^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{(\hat{a}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})}(a^{\prime},-1)+\nu_{i}^{a}.

Notice that at least one of the above four conditions will be satisfied, because when
\bar{a}_{i}=\hat{a}_{i}
and
\bar{y}_{i}=\hat{y}_{i}
, we have

\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}|a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i}|+\kappa_{\mathcal{Y}}|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|=0\leq\rho

for any
\rho\geq 0
. Combining all four cases leads to the second set of constraints.

The last constraint in the reformulation is obtained by setting the optimal value of the dual problem to be less than
\eta
for each value of
a\in\mathcal{A}
. This completes the proof.
