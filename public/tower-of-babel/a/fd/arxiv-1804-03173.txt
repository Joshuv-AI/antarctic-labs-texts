On the Increasing Tritronquée Solutions of the Painlevé-II Equation This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Painlevé Equations and Applications in Memory of Andrei Kapaev. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Kapaev.html
Peter D. MILLER

Abstract
The increasing tritronquée solutions of the Painlevé-II equation with parameter α \alpha exhibit square-root asymptotics in the maximally-large sector | arg ⁡ ( x ) | < 2 3 ​ π |\arg(x)|<\tfrac{2}{3}\pi and have recently appeared in applications where it is necessary to understand the behavior of these solutions for complex values of α \alpha . Here these solutions are investigated from the point of view of a Riemann–Hilbert representation related to the Lax pair of Jimbo and Miwa, which naturally arises in the analysis of rogue waves of infinite order. We show that for generic complex α \alpha , all such solutions are asymptotically pole-free along the bisecting ray of the complementary sector | arg ⁡ ( − x ) | < 1 3 ​ π |\arg(-x)|<\tfrac{1}{3}\pi that contains the poles far from the origin. This allows the definition of a total integral of the solution along the axis containing the bisecting ray, in which certain algebraic terms are subtracted at infinity and the poles are dealt with in the principal-value sense. We compute the value of this integral for all such solutions. We also prove that if the Painlevé-II parameter α \alpha is of the form α = ± 1 2 + i ​ p \alpha=\pm\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}p , p ∈ ℝ ∖ { 0 } p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} , one of the increasing tritronquée solutions has no poles or zeros whatsoever along the bisecting axis.

Introduction

The Painlevé-II equation with parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}u}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}=xu+2u^{3}-\alpha,\qquad u=u(x;\alpha)

(1.1)

has been the object of intense study ever since it was identified by Painlevé more than a century ago as one of only 6 formerly unknown second-order ordinary differential equations of the form
u^{\prime\prime}=F(x,u)
with
F
rational in
u
and meromorphic in
x
having what is now called the
Painlevé property
: the only singularities of a solution
u
whose location in the
x
-plane depends on initial conditions are poles. In fact, it is now known that every solution of (
1.1
) is a meromorphic function of
x
all of whose poles are simple and of residue
\pm 1
. Since its original discovery in the context of the solution of an abstract classification problem for differential equations, the equation (
1.1
) and its particular solutions have become very important in numerous applications. For instance, similarity solutions of the modified Korteweg–de Vries equation satisfy (
1.1
)
[
16
]
. The oscillations appearing near the leading edge of the dispersive shock wave generated from a wide class of initial data in the weakly-dispersive Korteweg–de Vries equation have a universal profile corresponding to the Hastings–McLeod solution of (
1.1
) with
\alpha=0

[
12
]
. The real graphs of the rational solutions of (
1.1
) for integer values of
\alpha
determine the locations of kinks in space-time near a point where generic initial data for the semiclassical sine-Gordon equation crosses the separatrix in the phase portrait of the simple pendulum in a transversal manner
[
9
]
. In mathematical physics there are also many applications of solutions of (
1.1
). Perhaps the most famous one concerns the distribution functions for the largest eigenvalue of random matrices from certain ensembles, which in the scaling limit can in some cases can be written in terms of again the Hastings–McLeod solution
[
28
]
.

The Hastings–McLeod solution of (
1.1
) is especially important in applications because it is a global solution for real values of
x
, i.e., it has no singularities for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It is an example of a so-called
tronquée
solution, namely one having no poles near
x=\infty
in one or more sectors of opening angle
\tfrac{2}{3}\pi
of the complex plane. In fact, the Hastings–McLeod solution has no poles near infinity in two disjoint sectors:
|\arg(x)|<\tfrac{1}{3}\pi
and
|\arg(-x)|<\tfrac{1}{3}\pi
. The intervening sectors symmetric about the imaginary axis are filled with poles for large
|x|
, and the poles form a locally regular lattice. In general, there are two distinct types of tronquée solutions of the Painlevé-II equation (
1.1
). The
increasing tronquée
solutions satisfy
u(x;\alpha)\sim\pm\big({-}\tfrac{1}{2}x\big)^{1/2}
as
|x|\to\infty
with
|\arg(-x)|<\tfrac{1}{3}\pi
(arising from a dominant balance between the terms
xu
and
2u^{3}
in (
1.1
)), and the
decreasing tronquée
solutions satisfy
u(x;\alpha)\sim\alpha x^{-1}
as
|x|\to\infty
with
|\arg(x)|<\tfrac{1}{3}\pi
(arising from a dominant balance between the terms
xu
and
-\alpha
in (
1.1
)). The Hastings–McLeod solution is uniquely determined as being both an increasing and a decreasing tronquée solution. Otherwise, the tronquée solutions are not generally determined by their leading asymptotics. However, for each choice of sign there is a unique solution of (
1.1
) for which
u(x;\alpha)\sim\pm\big({-}\tfrac{1}{2}x\big)^{1/2}
holds as
|x|\to\infty
with
-\tfrac{1}{3}\pi<\arg(-x)<\pi
and another unique solution for which the same asymptotic holds for
-\pi<\arg(-x)<\tfrac{1}{3}\pi
. These solutions are related by the rotation symmetry of the Painlevé-II equation in which whenever
u(x)
is a solution, then so is
\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}u\big(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}x\big)
. Thus there is also for each choice of sign a unique solution for which
u(x;\alpha)\sim\pm\mathrm{i}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}x\big)^{1/2}
as
|x|\to\infty
with
|\arg(x)|<\tfrac{2}{3}\pi
. These six solutions are called the
increasing tritronquée
solutions of (
1.1
) in the nomenclature of
[
17
, Chapter 11]
that can be traced back to the terminology introduced by Boutroux
[
7
,
8
]
.

The Painlevé-I equation also has tritronquée solutions, and these have been conjectured and/or shown to describe critical phenomena in several different situations
[

Riemann–Hilbert Problem 2.1

(Jimbo–Miwa Painlevé-II problem)
.

Let
y,p,\tau\in\mathbb{C}
be related by
\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
. Seek a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)=\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y,p,\tau)
with the following properties.

Analyticity:

\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)
is analytic for
\zeta
in the five sectors
S_{0}
:
|\arg(\zeta)|<\tfrac{1}{2}\pi
,
S_{1}
:
\tfrac{1}{2}\pi<\arg(\zeta)<\tfrac{5}{6}\pi
,
S_{-1}
:
-\tfrac{5}{6}\pi<\arg(\zeta)<-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi
,
S_{2}
:
\tfrac{5}{6}\pi<\arg(\zeta)<\pi
, and
S_{-2}
:
-\pi<\arg(\zeta)<-\tfrac{5}{6}\pi
. It takes continuous boundary values on the excluded rays and at the origin from each sector.

Jump conditions:

\mathbf{W}_{+}(\zeta;y)=\mathbf{W}_{-}(\zeta;y)\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PII}}(\zeta;y)
, where
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PII}}(\zeta;y)
is the matrix defined on the jump contour shown in Fig.
3
.

Normalization:

\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)\zeta^{\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}}\to\mathbb{I}
as
\zeta\to\infty
uniformly in all directions.

Note that the condition
\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
ensures that the cyclic product of the jump matrices at the origin is the identity, which is a necessary condition for the continuity of the boundary values from each sector at
\zeta=0
. Noting also that all jump matrices have unit determinant, it is therefore a simple consequence of Liouville’s theorem that this problem has at most one solution, and if it exists it must have unit determinant. If
p\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
then all jump matrices become the identity so given the continuity of the boundary values at the origin from each sector the solution
\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)
would need to be entire; but this yields a contradiction with the normalization condition unless also
p=0
. Hence there is no solution for
p\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. If
p=0
it is easy to check that
\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)\equiv\mathbb{I}
is
the unique solution. For all other values of
p\in\mathbb{C}
, the solution will exist for generic values of
y\in\mathbb{C}
that avoid certain poles.

2.1
Differential equations

Given parameters
p
and
\tau
with
\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
, and assuming solvability of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
in the neighborhood of some value of
y\in\mathbb{C}
, we can derive from the solution certain differential equations via the dressing construction. It is a consequence of the exponential decay to the identity of the jump matrix
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PII}}(\zeta;y)
as
\zeta\to\infty
that the normalization condition on
\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)
holds in the stronger sense that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)\sim\left(\mathbb{I}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{W}^{j}(y)\zeta^{-j}\right)\zeta^{-\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}},\qquad\zeta\to\infty,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}}{\partial y}(\zeta;y)\sim\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}}{\mathrm{d}y}^{j}(y)\zeta^{-j}\right)\zeta^{-\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}},\qquad\zeta\to\infty,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}}{\partial\zeta}(\zeta;y)\sim-\left(\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}\zeta^{-1}+\!\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\!\big[(j-1)\mathbf{W}^{j-1}(y)+\mathrm{i}p\mathbf{W}^{j-1}(y)\sigma_{3}\big]\zeta^{-j}\right)\zeta^{-\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}},\qquad\zeta\to\infty.

It is easy to see that the “undressed” matrix
\mathbf{Z}(\zeta;y):=\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\zeta^{3}+y\zeta)\sigma_{3}/2}
is analytic in the same domain that
\mathbf{W}(\zeta;y)
is, and satisfies analogous jump conditions except that the factors
\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}(\zeta^{3}+y\zeta)}
in the jump matrix
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PII}}(\zeta;y)
have been replaced in all cases by
1
. It follows easily that

\mathbf{Z}_{\zeta}(\zeta;y)\mathbf{Z}(\zeta;y)^{-1}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{y}(\zeta;y)\mathbf{Z}(\zeta;y)^{-1}
are both entire functions of
\zeta
whose asymptotic expansions as
\zeta\to\infty
are easily computed from (
2.1
). Applying Liouville’s theorem shows that these entire functions are polynomials:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{\zeta}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}=\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}\zeta^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{i}\big[\mathbf{W}^{1}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]\zeta

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathbf{Z}_\zeta\mathbf{Z}^{-1}=}{}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}y\sigma_{3}+\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{i}\big[\mathbf{W}^{2}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]-\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{i}\big[\mathbf{W}^{1}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]\mathbf{W}^{1}(y),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{y}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}\zeta+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\big[\mathbf{W}^{1}(y),\sigma_{3}\big],

and from the necessarily vanishing coefficient of
\zeta^{-1}
in the expansion of
\mathbf{Z}_{\zeta}(\zeta;y)\mathbf{Z}(\zeta;y)^{-1}
one finds that

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\big[\mathbf{W}^{3}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]-\frac{3}{2}\big[\mathbf{W}^{2}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]\mathbf{W}^{1}(y)+\frac{3}{2}\big[\mathbf{W}^{1}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]\big(\mathbf{W}^{1}(y)^{2}-\mathbf{W}^{2}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{1}{2}y\big[\mathbf{W}^{1}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]=p\sigma_{3},

(2.3)

while setting to zero the coefficient of
\zeta^{-1}
in the expansion of
\mathbf{Z}_{y}(\zeta;y)\mathbf{Z}(\zeta;y)^{-1}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}^{1}}{\mathrm{d}y}(y)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\big[\mathbf{W}^{2}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\big[\mathbf{W}^{1}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]\mathbf{W}^{1}(y)=\mathbf{0}.

(2.4)

The latter allows (
2.2
) to be rewritten in terms of the matrix coefficient
\mathbf{W}^{1}(y)
alone:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{\zeta}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}=\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}\zeta^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{i}\big[\mathbf{W}^{1}(y),\sigma_{3}\big]\zeta+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}y\sigma_{3}-3\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}^{1}}{\mathrm{d}y}(y).

(2.5)

It is convenient to use the representation (
2.2
) for the diagonal terms and (
2.5
) for the off-diagonal terms. Thus, if

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(y):=W^{1}_{12}(y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{V}(y):=W^{1}_{21}(y),

(2.6)

then
\mathbf{Z}(\zeta;y)
is a simultaneous fundamental solution matrix of the two Lax pair equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{Z}}{\partial\zeta}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z},\qquad\mathbf{A}:=\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}\zeta^{2}+3\mathrm{i}\begin{bmatrix}0&-\mathcal{U}\\
\mathcal{V}&0\end{bmatrix}\zeta+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\begin{bmatrix}y+6\mathcal{U}\mathcal{V}&6\mathrm{i}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\\
6\mathrm{i}\mathcal{V}^{\prime}&-y-6\mathcal{U}\mathcal{V}\end{bmatrix},

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{Z}}{\partial y}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Z},\qquad\mathbf{B}:=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}\zeta+\mathrm{i}\begin{bmatrix}0&-\mathcal{U}\\
\mathcal{V}&0\end{bmatrix}.

These equations constitute the Lax pair for Painlevé-II found by Jimbo and Miwa
[
19
]
. The existence of a simultaneous fundamental solution matrix of both differential equations implies compatibility of the Lax pair, i.e., the coefficient matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
necessarily satisfy the zero-curvature condition
\mathbf{A}_{y}-\mathbf{B}_{\zeta}+[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]=\mathbf{0}
, which by direct computation is equivalent to the following coupled system for the functions
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(y)
and
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}(y)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{U}}{\mathrm{d}y^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}y\mathcal{U}-2\mathcal{U}^{2}\mathcal{V}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{V}}{\mathrm{d}y^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}y\mathcal{V}-2\mathcal{U}\mathcal{V}^{2}=0.

(2.7)

Combining the diagonal part of (
2.3
) with the off-diagonal part of (
2.4
) and using the identity
W^{1}_{11}(y)+W^{1}_{22}(y)=0
following from
\det(\mathbf{Z}(\zeta;y))=1
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\mathrm{i}p=\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(y)\mathcal{V}(y)-\mathcal{U}(y)\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(y).

(2.8)

Since the left-hand side is independent of
y
, so must be the right-hand side, and indeed it is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(y)\mathcal{V}(y)-\mathcal{U}(y)\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(y)
is a constant of motion for the system (
2.7
). It follows immediately that the logarithmic derivatives

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(y):=\frac{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(y)}{\mathcal{U}(y)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{Q}(y):=\frac{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(y)}{\mathcal{V}(y)}

(2.9)

satisfy uncoupled inhomogeneous Painlevé-II equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{P}}{\mathrm{d}y^{2}}+\frac{2}{3}y\mathcal{P}-2\mathcal{P}^{3}+\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{i}p-\frac{1}{3}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{Q}}{\mathrm{d}y^{2}}+\frac{2}{3}y\mathcal{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}^{3}-\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{i}p-\frac{1}{3}=0.

(2.10)

These two equations are simply rescaled forms of the standard Painlevé-II equation (
1.1
). Indeed, the function
u(x):=-\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)^{1/3}\mathcal{P}\big({-}\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)^{1/3}x\big)
is a solution of (
1.1
) with parameter
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}-\mathrm{i}p
. Likewise, the function
u(x):=-\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)^{1/3}\mathcal{Q}\big({-}\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)^{1/3}x\big)
satisfies (
1.1
) with
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}p
.

2.2.3
Solution identification

Recall that the function
u(x):=-\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)^{1/3}\mathcal{Q}\big({-}\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)^{1/3}x\big)
is a solution of the Painlevé-II equation in standard form (

[
17

there is
exactly one
solution of (
1.1
) consistent with the asymptotic formula (
1.4
) (for each choice of the sign
\pm
). Thus
u(x)=u_{\mathrm{TT}}^{-}\big(x;\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}p\big)

-plane, the solutions

are two of the six increasing tritronquée solutions of (
1.1
) (four others are obtained by
\pm\tfrac{2}{3}\pi
rotation symmetry in the complex

-plane). Each of these six is determined by its leading asymptotic behavior in a sector of maximal opening angle
\tfrac{4}{3}\pi-\epsilon

[
17

that the leading asymptotic (

in consecutive terms, the coefficients of which can be determined by formal substitution into the differential equation (

\displaystyle u(x)=-\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\alpha}{2x}+O\big(|x|^{-5/2}\big),\qquad x\to\infty,\qquad|\arg(x)|<\frac{2}{3}\pi.

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(y)=\mathrm{i}\left(-\frac{y}{3}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}-\left(\frac{1}{4}+\mathrm{i}\frac{p}{2}\right)\frac{1}{y}+O\big(|y|^{-5/2}\big),\qquad y\to\infty,\qquad|\arg(-y)|<\frac{2}{3}\pi.

(2.20)

The solution

characterized by the asymptotic behavior (

3.2
Parametrix construction

3.2.1
Outer parametrix

We construct an outer parametrix
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)
to satisfy the latter jump conditions as follows.
Since
\xi^{-\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}}
satisfies exactly the normalization condition
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)\xi^{\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}}\to\mathbb{I}
as
\xi\to\infty
, as well as the jump condition for
\mathbf{Z}
across the negative real axis, we seek the outer parametrix in the form
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi):=\mathbf{J}(\xi)\xi^{-\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}}
and we require that
\mathbf{J}(\xi)\to\mathbb{I}
as
\xi\to\infty
and that
\mathbf{J}(\xi)
be analytic except on
\Sigma
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{+}(\xi)=\mathbf{J}_{-}(\xi)\begin{bmatrix}0&-\tau^{-1}\xi^{-2\mathrm{i}p}\\
\tau\xi^{2\mathrm{i}p}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\xi\in\Sigma^{+},

(3.11)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{+}(\xi)=\mathbf{J}_{-}(\xi)\begin{bmatrix}0&\tau\xi^{-2\mathrm{i}p}\\
-\tau^{-1}\xi^{2\mathrm{i}p}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\xi\in\Sigma^{-}.

(3.12)

These choices guarantee that
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)
exactly satisfies the jump conditions of
\mathbf{Z}
described by the jump matrix in (
3.7
), (
3.8
), and (
3.9
). Given
p\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
, observe that there exists a unique number
\ell\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\tau=\mathrm{e}^{\ell}
solves the equation
\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
and such that the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle-1<\mathrm{Re}\left(2\mathrm{i}p+\frac{2\ell}{\mathrm{i}\pi}\right)\leq 1.

(3.13)

Indeed, the relation
\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
determines
\ell
modulo
\mathrm{i}\pi\mathbb{Z}
, and hence a unique definite value of
\ell
can be chosen so that
\mathrm{e}^{2\ell}=\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
and the inequalities (
3.13
) hold. We are thus breaking the symmetry
\tau\mapsto-\tau
mentioned in the remark at the end of Section
2.1
in order to facilitate subsequent asymptotic analysis. We note that in the application of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
to rogue waves in
[
3
]
, it is necessary to have
\tau=1
for
p=\ln(2)/(2\pi)
; this amounts to choosing
\ell=0
which is consistent with the inequalities (
3.13
), so we can be assured that the analysis that follows applies to that special case.

Now, given
p
and
\ell
as above, let
j(\xi)
be defined by

\displaystyle j(\xi):=\frac{pR(\xi)}{\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\log(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}{R_{+}(s)(s-\xi)}+\mathrm{i}\eta

\displaystyle\hphantom{j(\xi):=}{}+\frac{\ell R(\xi)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\left[\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{R_{+}(s)(s-\xi)}-\int_{\Sigma^{-}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{R_{+}(s)(s-\xi)}\right],\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma,

where the constant
\eta
is defined by

\displaystyle\eta:=\frac{p}{\mathrm{i}\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\log(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}{R_{+}(s)}.

Here,
\log(s)
refers to the principal branch, and
R_{+}(s)
refers to the boundary value from the left by orientation of the integration contour (the direction of which is irrelevant for making
j
and
\eta
well-defined given the factor
R_{+}(s)
in the integrand). Thus, taking the integration in the upward direction, and parametrizing by
s=\mathrm{i}t
, we have
R_{+}(\mathrm{i}t)=-\sqrt{\nu^{2}-t^{2}}
, and hence

\displaystyle\eta=-\frac{2p}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}}\frac{\ln(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}-t^{2}}}=\frac{p}{2}\ln(6).

(3.14)

The function
j
is analytic for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
and the value of the constant
\eta
is determined so that
j(\xi)\to 0
as
\xi\to\infty
. In fact, it is not difficult to establish that

\displaystyle j(\xi)=-\mathrm{i}\nu\left(p-\frac{\ell}{\pi}\right)\xi^{-1}-\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{i}p\xi^{-2}+O\big(\xi^{-3}\big),\qquad\xi\to\infty.

(3.15)

The boundary values
j_{\pm}(\xi)
taken on
\Sigma^{+}
and
\Sigma^{-}
are continuous except at the origin, but including the other endpoints
\xi=\pm\mathrm{i}\nu
. Since
R(\xi)
changes sign across
\Sigma=\Sigma^{+}\cup\Sigma^{-}
, the sum of the boundary values is easy to compute directly:

\displaystyle j_{+}(\xi)+j_{-}(\xi)=\begin{cases}2\mathrm{i}p\log(\xi)+\ell+2\mathrm{i}\eta,&\xi\in\Sigma^{+},\quad\mathrm{Im}(\log(\xi))=\tfrac{1}{2}\pi,\\
2\mathrm{i}p\log(\xi)-\ell+2\mathrm{i}\eta,&\xi\in\Sigma^{-},\quad\mathrm{Im}(\log(\xi))=-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi.\end{cases}

Let
D(0)
denote a disk of sufficiently small radius (less than
\tfrac{1}{2}\nu=1/\sqrt{6}
will do) centered at the origin. It is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle j(\xi)=\left(2\mathrm{i}p+\frac{\ell}{\mathrm{i}\pi}\right)\log(\xi)+k(\xi),\qquad\xi\in D(0),\qquad\mathrm{Re}(\xi)>0,

(3.16)

where
\log(\xi)
again denotes the principal branch, and where
k(\xi)
is a function analytic in the full disk
D(0)
. Letting
R_{1}(\xi)
denote the analytic continuation of
R(\xi)
from the right half of
D(0)
to all of
D(0)
, a formula for
k(\xi)
that admits direct evaluation for any
\xi\in D(0)
reads

\displaystyle k(\xi)=\mathrm{i}\eta+\frac{p}{\pi}R_{1}(\xi)\left[\int_{-M}^{\mathrm{i}\nu}\frac{\log(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}{R(s)(s-\xi)}+\int_{-\mathrm{i}\nu}^{-M}\frac{\log(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}{R(s)(s-\xi)}\right]

\displaystyle\hphantom{k(\xi)=}{}+\frac{\ell}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}R_{1}(\xi)\left[\int_{-M}^{\mathrm{i}\nu}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{R(s)(s-\xi)}-\int_{-\mathrm{i}\nu}^{-M}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{R(s)(s-\xi)}\right]

\displaystyle\hphantom{k(\xi)=}{}-\left(2\mathrm{i}p+\frac{\ell}{\mathrm{i}\pi}\right)\log(\xi+M)+\left(2\mathrm{i}p+\frac{\ell}{\mathrm{i}\pi}\right)R_{1}(\xi)\int_{-M}^{0}\left[\frac{1}{R_{1}(s)}-\frac{1}{R_{1}(\xi)}\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{s-\xi},

where
M>0
is any sufficiently large constant, and where the contour integrals on the first two lines are taken over straight line segments. It is an exercise to show from this formula that

\displaystyle k(0)=\mathrm{i}\eta-\mathrm{i}\left(p-\frac{\ell}{\pi}\right)\ln(2\nu).

(3.17)

Now set
\mathbf{K}(\xi):=\mathbf{J}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-j(\xi)\sigma_{3}}
. Then
\mathbf{J}(\xi)\to\mathbb{I}
as
\xi\to\infty
implies that also
\mathbf{K}(\xi)\to\mathbb{I}
as
\xi\to\infty
, and
\mathbf{J}
analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
implies that the same is true for
\mathbf{K}
. According to (
3.11
),
\mathbf{K}(\xi)
satisfies the jump condition

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{+}(\xi)=\mathbf{K}_{-}(\xi)\begin{bmatrix}0&-\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\eta}\\
\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\eta}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\xi\in\Sigma\;\;\text{(here taken with upward orientation).}

We take for
\mathbf{K}(\xi)
the following solution:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(\xi):=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\eta\sigma_{3}}\mathbf{S}\left(\frac{\xi-\mathrm{i}\nu}{\xi+\mathrm{i}\nu}\right)^{\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\eta\sigma_{3}},\qquad\mathbf{S}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-\mathrm{i}\\
-\mathrm{i}&1\end{bmatrix}.

(3.18)

This function takes continuous boundary values along
\Sigma
except at the endpoints, where it exhibits negative fourth-root singularities. Moreover, its boundary values from the left and right half-planes admit analytic continuation into the full neighborhood
D(0)
. The outer parametrix is thus defined by the formula

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi):=\mathbf{K}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{j(\xi)\sigma_{3}}\xi^{-\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}},\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\Sigma\cup\mathbb{R}_{-}).

(3.19)

and the fact that

since

lies along

is taken to the positive real axis. Then, the identity
\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
implies that the jump conditions satisfied by the product

(here

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{+}(\xi;y)\tau^{-\sigma_{3}/2}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})=\mathbf{Z}_{-}(\xi;y)\tau^{-\sigma_{3}/2}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{+}(\xi;y)\tau^{-\sigma_{3}/2}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})=\mathbf{Z}_{-}(\xi;y)\tau^{-\sigma_{3}/2}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\

From the definition (

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)\tau^{-\sigma_{3}/2}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)W_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)^{\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)y^{-\sigma_{3}/4}\lambda^{\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{S},\qquad\xi\in D(\mathrm{i}\nu),

where

denotes the same eigenvector matrix defined in (

and define a matrix
\mathbf{A}(\lambda)
as the solution of the following Riemann–Hilbert Problem.

Riemann–Hilbert Problem 3.2

(Airy parametrix)
.

Seek a
2\times 2
matrix function
\mathbf{A}(\lambda)
defined for
0<|\arg(\lambda)|<\tfrac{2}{3}\pi
and
\tfrac{2}{3}\pi<|\arg(\lambda)|<\pi
with the following properties:

Analyticity:

\mathbf{A}(\lambda)
is analytic in the four sectors indicated above, and takes continuous boundary values from each sector including at the origin.

Jump conditions:
The boundary values
\mathbf{A}_{\pm}(\lambda)
are related by exactly the same jump conditions satisfied by
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)\tau^{-\sigma_{3}/2}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})

(
see (
3.20
)–(
3.21
)
)
but with the indicated directions extended to infinite rays in the
\lambda
-plane oriented in the direction of increasing real part.

Normalization:

\mathbf{A}(\lambda)\mathbf{S}^{-1}\lambda^{-\sigma_{3}/4}\to\mathbb{I}
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

This problem is well-known to have a unique solution constructed from Airy functions. The solution has the additional property that the normalization condition is strengthened to

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\lambda)\mathbf{S}^{-1}\lambda^{-\sigma_{3}/4}=\mathbb{I}+\begin{bmatrix}O\big(\lambda^{-3}\big)&O(\lambda^{-1})\\
O\big(\lambda^{-2}\big)&O\big(\lambda^{-3}\big)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\lambda\to\infty.

(3.22)

We then define an inner parametrix for
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi;y):=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)y^{-\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{A}(yW_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi))(-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})\tau^{\sigma_{3}/2},\qquad\xi\in D(\mathrm{i}\nu).

Since

is bounded away from zero and hence

is proportional to

when

, we then get from (

) that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)^{-1}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)y^{-\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{A}(yW_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi))\mathbf{S}^{-1}(yW_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi))^{-\sigma_{3}/4}y^{\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)^{-1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{in}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{out}(\xi)^{-1}}{}=\mathbb{I}+\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)y^{-\sigma_{3}/4}\begin{bmatrix}O\big(y^{-3}\big)&O\big(y^{-1}\big)\\
O\big(y^{-2}\big)&O\big(y^{-3}\big)\end{bmatrix}y^{\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)^{-1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{in}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{out}(\xi)^{-1}}{}=\mathbb{I}+\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)\begin{bmatrix}O\big(y^{-3}\big)&O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)\\
O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)&O\big(y^{-3}\big)\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi)^{-1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{in}_{\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{out}(\xi)^{-1}}{}=\mathbb{I}+O\big(y^{-3/2}\big),\qquad\xi\in\partial D(\mathrm{i}\nu),

(3.23)

where the estimate on the last line holds in the uniform sense on the circle

in the limit

.

A very similar construction gives an explicit parametrix

D(0)
by two different conditions:

\displaystyle h(\xi)-h(0\pm)=\pm\frac{1}{2}W_{0}(\xi)^{2},\qquad\xi\in D(0),\qquad\pm\mathrm{Re}(\xi)>0.

(3.24)

Here,
h(0\pm)
denotes the limiting value of
h(\xi)
at
\xi=0
from the domain
\pm\mathrm{Re}(\xi)>0
; see (
3.4
). These conditions determine
W_{0}(\xi)
up to an overall sign as a conformal mapping of the disk
D(0)
with the property that
W_{0}(0)=0
. We fix the sign by insisting that
W_{0}^{\prime}(0)>0
. In fact, by taking two derivatives in (
3.24
) and setting
\xi=0
we see that

\displaystyle W_{0}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu}}=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{1/4}>0.

(3.25)

We deform the jump contour within
D(0)
to ensure both that
\arg(W_{0}(\gamma^{\pm}))=\pm\tfrac{1}{4}\pi
and that
\arg(W_{0}(\beta^{\pm}))=\pm\tfrac{3}{4}\pi
. The conformal mapping is real, and therefore it automatically holds that
\arg(-\xi)=0
corresponds to
\arg(-W_{0}(\xi))=0
.

Consider the matrix
\mathbf{X}(\xi;y)
explicitly related to
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
for
\xi\in D(0)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(\xi;y):=\begin{cases}\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)\begin{bmatrix}0&\tau^{-1}\\
-\tau&0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}),&\tfrac{1}{2}\pi<\arg(\xi)<\pi,\\
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)\begin{bmatrix}0&\tau\\
-\tau^{-1}&0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}),&-\pi<\arg(\xi)<-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi,\\
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}),&|\arg(\xi)|<\tfrac{1}{2}\pi.\end{cases}

(3.26)

From this definition, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{X}_{-}(\xi;y),\qquad\xi\in\Sigma^{\pm},

(3.27)

so that
\mathbf{X}(\xi;y)
may be considered to be analytic on
\Sigma^{\pm}
. The remaining jump conditions satisfied by
\mathbf{X}(\xi;y)
are easily expressed in terms of the rescaled conformal mapping with a new independent variable
\lambda:=y^{3/4}W_{0}(\xi)
. They read as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{X}_{-}(\xi;y)\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
\tau^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}}&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\arg(W_{0}(\xi))=\frac{1}{4}\pi,

(3.28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{X}_{-}(\xi;y)\begin{bmatrix}1&\tau^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\arg(W_{0}(\xi))=-\frac{1}{4}\pi,

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{X}_{-}(\xi;y)\begin{bmatrix}1&\tau\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\arg(W_{0}(\xi))=\frac{3}{4}\pi,

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{X}_{-}(\xi;y)\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
\tau\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}}&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\arg(W_{0}(\xi))=-\frac{3}{4}\pi,

and finally,

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{X}_{-}(\xi;y)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p\sigma_{3}}\tau^{-2\sigma_{3}},\qquad\arg(-W_{0}(\xi))=0,

(3.29)

where all contours are oriented in the direction of increasing real part (so
\beta^{\pm}
have been re-oriented). To confirm these jump conditions, it is necessary to use the fact that
h(0-)+h(0+)=0
.

The jump conditions (
3.27
)–(
3.29
) match those of the parabolic cylinder function parametrix described in the appendix, provided we replace the parameters
(p,\tau)
with
(q,\mu)
, where (recall that
\ell
is a specific number for which
\tau=\mathrm{e}^{\ell}
)

\displaystyle q:=p-\frac{\ell}{\pi}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu:=\frac{1}{\tau}.

(3.30)

It is easy to check that the condition
\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
guarantees that also
\mu^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi q}-1
. It then follows from the definition (
3.19
) of the outer parametrix
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)
and from (
3.16
) that there exists a matrix function
\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)
, independent of
y
and having unit determinant, that is analytic in
D(0)
such that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}W_{0}(\xi)^{-\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{out}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_3}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_1)}{}=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}y^{3\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}/4}\lambda^{-\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}},\qquad\xi\in D(0),\qquad\mathrm{Re}(\xi)>0.

Note that, in particular,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\eta\sigma_{3}}\mathbf{S}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\eta\sigma_{3}}\mathrm{e}^{k(0)\sigma_{3}}W_{0}^{\prime}(0)^{-\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}).

(3.31)

Similarly (comparing with (
3.26
)),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)\begin{bmatrix}0&\tau^{-1}\\
-\tau&0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}y^{3\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}/4}\lambda^{-\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}},

\displaystyle\qquad\xi\in D(0),\qquad\frac{1}{2}\pi<\arg(\xi)<\pi,\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)\begin{bmatrix}0&\tau\\
-\tau^{-1}&0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}y^{3\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}/4}\lambda^{-\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}},

\displaystyle\qquad\xi\in D(0),\qquad-\pi<\arg(\xi)<-\frac{1}{2}\pi.

Taking into account the final factor of
\lambda^{-\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}}
to obtain a good match on the boundary
\partial D(0)
, we are led to construct an inner parametrix near the origin by the following formulæ

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{0}(\xi;y):=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}y^{3\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{P}(y^{3/4}W_{0}(\xi);q,\mu)(-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}},

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{in}_0(\xi;y):=}{}\xi\in D(0),\qquad\mathrm{Re}(\xi)>0,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{0}(\xi;y):=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}y^{3\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{P}(y^{3/4}W_{0}(\xi);q,\mu)(-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}\begin{bmatrix}0&-\tau^{-1}\\
\tau&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{in}_0(\xi;y):=}{}\xi\in D(0),\qquad\frac{1}{2}\pi<\arg(\xi)<\pi,\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{0}(\xi;y):=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}y^{3\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{P}(y^{3/4}W_{0}(\xi);q,\mu)(-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}\begin{bmatrix}0&-\tau\\
\tau^{-1}&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{in}_0(\xi;y):=}{}\xi\in D(0),\qquad-\pi<\arg(\xi)<-\frac{1}{2}\pi.

Here
\mathbf{P}(\lambda;\cdot,\cdot)
is the solution of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
A.1
given in the appendix. We emphasize that
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}(\xi;y)
is an exact local solution of the jump conditions for
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
within the disk
D(0)
. It satisfies the mismatch condition

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{0}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)^{-1}=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}y^{3\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}/4}\mathbf{P}(\lambda;q,\mu)\lambda^{\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}}y^{-3\mathrm{i}q\sigma_{3}/4}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{in}_0(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^\mathrm{out}(\xi)^{-1}=}{}\times\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)\sigma_{3}}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1},\qquad\xi\in\partial D(0),

(3.32)

where
\lambda=y^{3/4}W_{0}(\xi)
.

3.3
Error analysis

The
global parametrix

\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y)
for
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
is defined as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y):=\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{\pm\mathrm{i}\nu}(\xi;y),&\xi\in D(\pm\mathrm{i}\nu),\\
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{0}(\xi;y),&\xi\in D(0),\\
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi),&\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\big(\overline{D(\mathrm{i}\nu)}\cup\overline{D(0)}\cup\overline{D(-\mathrm{i}\nu)}\big).\end{cases}

In a preliminary attempt to gauge the accuracy of the approximation of
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
by
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y)
, we define the error matrix as
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y):=\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y)^{-1}
wherever both factors are defined (note that by definition
\det(\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y))=1
holds everywhere). Because the inner parametrices are in each case exact local solutions of the jump conditions for
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
, it is easy to apply Morera’s theorem to deduce that
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)
can be viewed as a function analytic within all three disks:
D(\pm\mathrm{i}\nu)
and
D(0)
. Similarly, because the outer parametrix exactly satisfies the jump condition for
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
on the part of the negative real axis outside of
D(0)
and the parts of
\Sigma^{\pm}
lying outside of all three disks,
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)
may be considered to be analytic in a neighborhood of these. Therefore, the only points of non-analyticity for
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)
correspond to jump discontinuities (i) across the portions of
\alpha^{\pm}
,
\beta^{\pm}
,
\gamma^{\pm}
and
\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}\setminus\Sigma
lying outside all three disks (where
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
is discontinuous while
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y)=\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)
is analytic) and (ii) across the boundaries of the three disks (where the parametrix
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y)
is discontinuous while
\mathbf{Z}(\xi;y)
is analytic).

For jumps of type (i), we directly compute that
\mathbf{E}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{E}_{-}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)^{-1}
, where
\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{Z}}(\xi;y)
is defined on the relevant arcs by (
3.5
)–(
3.10
). The placement of the jump contour
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}
relative to the sign chart for
\mathrm{Im}(h(\xi))
as shown in Fig.
7
, the fact that for jumps of type (i),
\xi
is bounded away from the points
\xi=0,\pm\mathrm{i}\nu
, and the fact that the conjugating factors are uniformly bounded for such
\xi
and independent of
y
, show that
\mathbf{E}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{E}_{-}(\xi;y)(\mathbb{I}+\text{exponentially small in $y$ and~$\xi$})
.

For jumps of type (ii), if we take the disk boundaries to be oriented in the clockwise direction, it is easy to see that
\mathbf{E}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{E}_{-}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{\xi_{0}}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{out}}(\xi)^{-1}
holds when
\xi\in\partial D(\xi_{0})
, for all three cases
\xi_{0}=0,\pm\mathrm{i}\nu
. For
\xi_{0}=\pm\mathrm{i}\nu
we have from (
3.23
) and its analogue on
\partial D(-\mathrm{i}\nu)
that
\mathbf{E}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{E}_{-}(\xi;y)\big(\mathbb{I}+O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)\big)
holds uniformly on
\partial D(\pm\mathrm{i}\nu)
. For
\xi_{0}=0
, we combine (
3.32
) with formulæ (
A.7
)–(
A.9
) from the appendix and the fact that
W_{0}(\xi)
is bounded away from zero on
\partial D(0)
to conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{E}_{-}(\xi;y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\left(\mathbb{I}+\frac{1}{W_{0}(\xi)}\begin{bmatrix}0&c_{12}(y)\\
-c_{21}(y)&0\end{bmatrix}\right.

(3.33)

\displaystyle\left.{}+\begin{bmatrix}O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)&O(y^{-9/4+3\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)/2})\\
O(y^{-9/4-3\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)/2})&O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)\end{bmatrix}\right)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1},\qquad\xi\in\partial D(0),\qquad y\to+\infty,

where we have used
\big|\mathrm{e}^{\pm 2\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)}\big|=1
(following from (
3.4
)) and where

\displaystyle\begin{split}&c_{12}(y):=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)}y^{-3/4+3\mathrm{i}q/2}r(q,\mu)=\frac{\tau\sqrt{\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\mathrm{e}^{\pi q/2}2^{\mathrm{i}q}}{\Gamma(\mathrm{i}q)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(2y/3)^{3/2}}y^{-3/4+3\mathrm{i}q/2},\\
&c_{21}(y):=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}y^{3/2}h(0+)}y^{-3/4-3\mathrm{i}q/2}s(q,\mu)=\frac{q\Gamma(\mathrm{i}q)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi q/2}2^{-\mathrm{i}q}}{2\tau\sqrt{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(2y/3)^{3/2}}\!y^{-3/4-3\mathrm{i}q/2}.\end{split}\!\!\!\!\!\!

(3.34)

Now the inequalities (
3.13
) and the definition (
3.30
) of
q
show that
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, which implies that all matrix elements in the error term on the second line of (
3.33
) are
O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)
as
y\to+\infty
. However, the terms on the first line are larger; indeed,
c_{12}(y)
does not decay to zero at all as
y\to+\infty
if
\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)=\tfrac{1}{2}
. So it will be necessary to take that term into account explicitly in order to arrive at a small-norm problem, and it will also be convenient to remove the effect of
c_{21}(y)
, since for
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
it can decay arbitrarily slowly as
y\to+\infty
for
\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)
near the lower end of the allowed range.

3.3.1
First parametrix for the error

Since
c_{12}(y)
does not decay when
\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)=\tfrac{1}{2}
, to cover the whole range
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
we must construct a parametrix for
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)
itself, which we will denote by
\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)
. We want it to have the following properties. It should be analytic for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\partial D(0)
, taking continuous boundary values on
\partial D(0)
from the interior and exterior. It should converge to the identity as
\xi\to\infty
. Finally, it should satisfy the jump condition

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{+}(\xi;y)=\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{-}(\xi;y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\left(\mathbb{I}+\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\sigma_{+}\right)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1},\qquad\xi\in\partial D(0),\qquad\sigma_{+}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

in which the circle is taken with clockwise orientation. Given that
W_{0}(\xi)
has a simple pole at the origin, a reasonable ansatz for
\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)
is that its exterior boundary value is a function with a simple pole at
\xi=0
and that satisfies the normalization condition at infinity:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbb{I}+\xi^{-1}\mathbf{R}(y).

(3.35)

We then attempt to determine the residue
\mathbf{R}(y)
by insisting that the corresponding boundary value
\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{-}(\xi;y)
obtained from the jump condition admit analytic continuation to the full interior of the disk
D(0)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{-}(\xi;y)=\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{+}(\xi;y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\left(\mathbb{I}+\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\sigma_{+}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\dot{\mathbf{E}}_-(\xi;y)}{}=\left(\mathbb{I}+\xi^{-1}\mathbf{R}(y)\right)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\left(\mathbb{I}-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\sigma_{+}\right)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1}

needs to be analytic at
\xi=0
, where we have used
\sigma_{+}^{2}=\mathbf{0}
and (
3.35
) to obtain the second line. Noting that
\det(\mathbf{H}_{0}(0))=1
, the Laurent expansion of
\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{-}(\xi;y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)
about
\xi=0
is therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{-}(\xi;y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)=-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{R}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)\sigma_{+}\xi^{-2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\left(-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)}\sigma_{+}-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{R}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}(0)\sigma_{+}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad{}{}+\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{R}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)\sigma_{+}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle\left.\qquad{}+\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{R}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)\left(\mathbb{I}+\frac{c_{12}(y)W_{0}^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2W_{0}^{\prime}(0)^{2}}\sigma_{+}\right)\right)\xi^{-1}+O(1),\qquad\xi\to 0.

To remove the coefficient of
\xi^{-2}
, we will again use
\sigma_{+}^{2}=\mathbf{0}
to express
\mathbf{R}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)
in the rank-
1
form

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)=\mathbf{a}(y)\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{e}_{2}:=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\quad\implies\quad\mathbf{R}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)\sigma_{+}=\mathbf{0},

(3.36)

and
\mathbf{a}(y)
is a vector unknown yet to be determined. Using this form, two terms in the coefficient of
\xi^{-1}
are automatically cancelled, and the condition that this coefficient also vanish becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)}\sigma_{+}-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{a}(y)\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}(0)\sigma_{+}+\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{a}(y)\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}=\mathbf{0}.

The first column is an identity, and after multiplying on the left by
\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)
, the second column reads

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)\mathbf{e}_{1}-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)}\mathbf{a}(y)\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}(0)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{a}(y)=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbf{e}_{1}:=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}(0)\mathbf{e}_{1}
is a scalar, the solution is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}(y):=\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)\mathbf{e}_{1},\qquad\widetilde{c}_{12}(y):=\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)-c_{12}(y)\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}(0)\mathbf{e}_{1}},

(3.37)

provided the denominator of the coefficient
\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)
is nonzero. Since
W_{0}^{\prime}(0)>0
and
\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)
are independent of
y
, while
c_{12}(y)=O\big(y^{-3/4+3\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)/2}\big)
as
y\to+\infty
, it is clear that
\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)
will be well-defined and will also satisfy
\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)=O\big(y^{-3/4+3\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)/2}\big)
if
\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)<\tfrac{1}{2}
. This is a decaying estimate as
y\to+\infty
. Only in the case
\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)=\tfrac{1}{2}
is it even possible for
\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)
to fail to exist for sufficiently large positive
y
. In the latter case, by excluding neighborhoods of certain points, we may still assume that
\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)=O(1)
as
y\to+\infty
. Thus it is certainly true that
\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)=O(1)
as
y\to+\infty
with the above caveat if
\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Since
\det(\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y))=1
the same holds for the inverse matrix.

Using the rational expression (
3.35
) together with (
3.36
) and (
3.37
) and the identity
\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}\!=\!\sigma_{+}
then shows that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)=\mathbb{I}+\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)\sigma_{+}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\xi^{-1},\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{D(0)}.

(3.38)

3.3.2
Second parametrix for the error

We now compare the initial error matrix
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)
with its parametrix
\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)
by setting
\mathbf{F}(\xi;y):=\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)^{-1}
. Clearly,
\mathbf{F}(\xi;y)
is analytic in the same domain as is
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)
, and takes its boundary values on the jump contour in the same continuous fashion. Moreover, since we require
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)\to\mathbb{I}
as
\xi\to\infty
, we also have the corresponding condition on
\mathbf{F}(\xi;y)
because
\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)\to\mathbb{I}
as
\xi\to\infty
. For all points
\xi
on the jump contour for
\mathbf{E}
omitting the circle
\partial D(0)
, we use the uniform estimate on
\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)
and its inverse to conclude that the previously obtained estimates of the deviation of the jump matrix for
\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)
from the identity persist. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{F}_{-}(\xi;y)\big(\mathbb{I}+O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)\big),\qquad\xi\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{F}}\setminus\partial D(0),

(3.39)

where the estimate is in the
L^{\infty}
sense, but the error term also decays exponentially to zero in both
\xi\to\infty
and
y>0
along unbounded portions of
\Sigma_{\mathbf{F}}
. On the circle
\partial D(0)
where
\mathbf{E}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{E}_{-}(\xi;y)\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)
and
\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{+}(\xi;y)=\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{-}(\xi;y)\mathbf{V}^{\dot{\mathbf{E}}}(\xi;y)
, we have
\mathbf{F}_{+}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{F}_{-}(\xi;y)\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{F}}(\xi;y)
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{F}}(\xi;y)=\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{+}(\xi;y)\mathbf{V}^{\dot{\mathbf{E}}}(\xi;y)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)\dot{\mathbf{E}}_{+}(\xi;y)^{-1},\qquad\xi\in\partial D(0).

Now, using
c_{12}(y)=O(1)
along with
\sigma_{+}^{2}=\mathbf{0}
and the fact that over the whole range
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
we have
c_{12}(y)c_{21}(y)=O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)
as
y\to+\infty
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\dot{\mathbf{E}}}(\xi;y)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\left(\mathbb{I}-\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\sigma_{+}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathbf{V}^{\dot{\mathbf{E}}}(\xi;y)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)=}{}\times\left(\mathbb{I}+\frac{c_{12}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\sigma_{+}-\frac{c_{21}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\sigma_{-}+O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)\right)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathbf{V}^{\dot{\mathbf{E}}}(\xi;y)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^\mathbf{E}(\xi;y)}{}=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\left(\mathbb{I}-\frac{c_{21}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\sigma_{-}+O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)\right)\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1},\qquad\xi\in\partial D(0),

where
\sigma_{-}:=\sigma_{+}^{\top}
.
Therefore, using (
3.38
) and defining
\mathbf{B}(\xi):=\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{F}}(\xi;y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\left(\mathbb{I}+\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)\sigma_{+}\xi^{-1}\right)\mathbf{B}(\xi)\left(\mathbb{I}-\frac{c_{21}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\sigma_{-}+O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)\right)\mathbf{B}(\xi)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{I}-\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)\sigma_{+}\xi^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\mathbb{I}-\frac{c_{21}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\mathbf{B}(\xi)\sigma_{-}\mathbf{B}(\xi)^{-1}+O\big(y^{-3/2}\big),\qquad\xi\in\partial D(0),

where again we used
\sigma_{+}^{2}=\mathbf{0}
and took into account that
\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)c_{21}(y)=O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)
and that
\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)=O(1)
. Therefore conjugating again by
\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{F}}(\xi;y)=\mathbb{I}-\frac{c_{21}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\sigma_{-}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1}+O\big(y^{-3/2}\big),\qquad\xi\in\partial D(0),\qquad y\to+\infty.

(3.40)

This form suggests that we might finally arrive at a small-norm target problem with jump matrices uniformly deviating from the identity by
O\big(y^{-3/2}\big)
if we choose to remove the term proportional to
c_{21}(y)
by a second parametrix for the error, which we denote by
\dot{\mathbf{F}}(\xi;y)
. Technically, since
c_{21}(y)=O\big(y^{-3/4-3\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)/2}\big)
which is

as
y\to+\infty

,
\mathbf{F}(\xi;y)
already satisfies the conditions of a small-norm problem, but it is convenient to first construct
\dot{\mathbf{F}}(\xi;y)
and then compare to
\mathbf{F}(\xi;y)
before obtaining estimates from small-norm theory.

Therefore, we define
\dot{\mathbf{F}}(\xi;y)
as the matrix analytic for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\partial D(0)
, normalized to the identity as
\xi\to\infty
, and whose continuous boundary values on
\partial D(0)
are related by the jump condition

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{F}}_{+}(\xi;y)=\dot{\mathbf{F}}_{-}(\xi;y)\left(\mathbb{I}-\frac{c_{21}(y)}{W_{0}(\xi)}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)\sigma_{-}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\xi)^{-1}\right),\qquad\xi\in\partial D(0).

(3.41)

Of course, the solution of this problem is very similar to that for
\dot{\mathbf{E}}(\xi;y)
, and we obtain the result that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{F}}(\xi;y)=\mathbb{I}-\widetilde{c}_{21}(y)\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)\sigma_{-}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\xi^{-1},

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{21}(y):=\frac{c_{21}(y)}{W_{0}^{\prime}(0)+c_{21}(y)\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}(0)\mathbf{e}_{2}},\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{D(0)},

(3.42)

and one can check directly that
\dot{\mathbf{F}}_{-}(\xi;y)
, obtained from evaluating the above expression on
\partial D(0)
as
\dot{\mathbf{F}}_{+}(\xi;y)
and applying the jump condition (
3.41
), admits analytic continuation to the interior of
D(0)
. Unlike
\widetilde{c}_{12}(y)
for which existence may require conditions on arbitrarily large

if
\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)=\tfrac{1}{2}
,

always exists as long as
y>0
is sufficiently large and
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.

\displaystyle y\to+\infty,\qquad-\frac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)<\frac{1}{2},

we compare with (
4.2
) and eliminate
\mathcal{V}(y)
to find

\displaystyle\exp\left(\int_{-\infty}^{a}\left[\mathcal{Q}(\eta)-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{-\frac{\eta}{3}}+\left(\frac{1}{4}+\mathrm{i}\frac{p}{2}\right)\frac{1}{\eta}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\eta\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\quad{}+\int_{C(a,b)}\mathcal{Q}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta+\int_{b}^{y}\left[\mathcal{Q}(\eta)-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}p\right)\frac{1}{\eta}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\eta\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}(-a/3)^{3/2}}}{\tau p\Gamma(\mathrm{i}p)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi(1/2+\mathrm{i}p)/2}\left(b\sqrt{-\frac{a}{3}}\right)^{1/2+\mathrm{i}p}(1+o(1)),

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}\ y\to+\infty,\quad-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)<\frac{1}{2}.

(4.3)

Taking the limit
y\to+\infty
in (
4.3
) yields the total integral identity

\displaystyle\exp\left(\int_{-\infty}^{a}\left[\mathcal{Q}(y)-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{-\frac{y}{3}}+\left(\frac{1}{4}+\mathrm{i}\frac{p}{2}\right)\frac{1}{y}\right]\,\mathrm{d}y\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\quad{}+\int_{C(a,b)}\mathcal{Q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{b}^{+\infty}\left[\mathcal{Q}(y)-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}p\right)\frac{1}{y}\right]\,\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}(-a/3)^{3/2}}}{\tau p\Gamma(\mathrm{i}p)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi(1/2+\mathrm{i}p)/2}\left(b\sqrt{-\frac{a}{3}}\right)^{1/2+\mathrm{i}p},\qquad-\frac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}q)<\frac{1}{2},

in which the integral over the interval
(b,+\infty)
is not generally absolutely convergent, but it necessarily makes sense as an improper integral. Suppose that we take for
C(a,b)
a real path with infinitesimal semicircular indentations in the lower half-plane centered at each real pole of
\mathcal{Q}
. It is well-known that all poles of
\mathcal{Q}
are simple and have residue
\pm 1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{C(a,b)}\mathcal{Q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{Q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\mathrm{i}\pi(N_{+}-N_{-}),

where “P.V.” denotes the Hadamard principal value and

is the number of real poles of
\mathcal{Q}
of residue
\pm 1
.

Setting
A:=-(\tfrac{2}{3})^{1/3}b<0
and
B:=-(\tfrac{2}{3})^{1/3}a>0
so all real poles of

1.10
) for the increasing tritronquée solution

of (
1.1
). This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3

Consider the contour shown in Fig.
9
and the indicated jump matrix defined thereon.

The jump contour in the
\lambda
-plane and jump matrix
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PC}}
.

Riemann–Hilbert Problem A.1

(parabolic cylinder parametrix)
.

Let
p,\tau\in\mathbb{C}
be related by
\tau^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi p}-1
. Seek a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
\mathbf{P}(\lambda)=\mathbf{P}(\lambda;p,\tau)
with the following properties.

Analyticity:

\mathbf{P}(\lambda)
is analytic for
\lambda
in the five sectors
S_{0}\colon|\arg(\lambda)|<\tfrac{1}{4}\pi
,
S_{1}\colon\tfrac{1}{4}\pi<\arg(\lambda)<\tfrac{3}{4}\pi
,
S_{-1}\colon-\frac{3}{4}\pi<\arg(\lambda)<-\tfrac{1}{4}\pi
,
S_{2}\colon\tfrac{3}{4}\pi<\arg(\lambda)<\pi
, and
S_{-2}\colon-\pi<\arg(\lambda)<-\tfrac{3}{4}\pi
. It takes continuous boundary values on the excluded rays and at the origin from each sector.

Jump conditions:

\mathbf{P}_{+}(\lambda)=\mathbf{P}_{-}(\lambda)\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PC}}(\lambda)
, where
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PC}}(\lambda)
is the matrix function defined on the jump contour shown in Fig.
9
.

Normalization:

\mathbf{P}(\lambda)\lambda^{\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}}\to\mathbb{I}
as
\lambda\to\infty
uniformly in all directions.

To solve Riemann–Hilbert Problem
A.1
, first note that there can be at most one solution, and if it exists it must have unit determinant. This problem has no solution if
p\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
for the same reason as in the case of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
, and its solution is
\mathbf{P}(\lambda)\equiv\mathbb{I}
if
p=0
.

Therefore we now assume that
p\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
, and consider the matrix
\mathbf{Q}(\lambda):=\mathbf{P}(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}\sigma_{3}/2}
. This change of variables removes the exponentials
\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}}
from the jump conditions, from which it follows that
\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}(\lambda)
and
\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)
satisfy exactly the same jump conditions. Appealing to invertibility and the specified regular behavior of
\mathbf{P}
near
\lambda=0
we see that
\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}(\lambda)\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)^{-1}
is an entire function of
\lambda
. If
\mathbf{P}(\lambda)
has an asymptotic expansion as
\lambda\to\infty
consistent with the normalization condition and hence of the form
\mathbf{P}(\lambda)=\big(\mathbb{I}+\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{[1]}+\cdots\big)\lambda^{-\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}}
, the additional assumption that the series is differentiable term-by-term with respect to
\lambda
shows by a Liouville argument that
\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}(\lambda)\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)^{-1}=-\mathrm{i}\lambda\sigma_{3}+\mathrm{i}\big[\sigma_{3},\mathbf{P}^{[1]}\big]
; therefore
\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)
is a matrix solution of the first-order system

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathrm{i}\lambda&r\\
s&\mathrm{i}\lambda\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q},\qquad r:=2\mathrm{i}P^{[1]}_{12},\qquad s:=-2\mathrm{i}P^{[1]}_{21}.

(A.1)

Eliminating the second row of
\mathbf{Q}
and rescaling by
t=\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\lambda
shows that the first row elements of
\mathbf{Q}
satisfy Weber’s equation for parabolic cylinder functions
[
26
, equation (12.2.2)]

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}Q_{1k}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}-\left(\frac{1}{4}t^{2}+a\right)Q_{1k}=0,\qquad a:=\frac{1}{2}(1+\mathrm{i}rs),\qquad k=1,2.

This differential equation has particular solutions
U(a,\pm t)
and
U(-a,\pm\mathrm{i}t)
, and the special function
U(\cdot,\cdot)
is described in detail in
[
26
, Section 12]
. In each of the five sectors, the general solution can be written as a linear combination of a suitable “numerically satisfactory” fundamental pair:

\displaystyle Q_{1k}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}rA^{(0)}_{k}U(a,t)+rB^{(0)}_{k}U(-a,\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{0},\\
rA^{(1)}_{k}U(a,t)+rB^{(1)}_{k}U(-a,-\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{1},\\
rA^{(-1)}_{k}U(a,-t)+rB^{(-1)}_{k}U(-a,\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{-1},\\
rA^{(2)}_{k}U(a,-t)+rB^{(2)}_{k}U(-a,-\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{2},\\
rA^{(-2)}_{k}U(a,-t)+rB^{(-2)}_{k}U(-a,-\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{-2}.\end{cases}

The first row of the matrix differential equation (
A.1
) then gives the elements of the second row explicitly in terms of those of the first and their derivatives; however the derivatives can be eliminated using
[
26
, equations (12.8.2)–(12.8.3)]
, and we therefore obtain

\displaystyle Q_{2k}(\lambda)=\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\begin{cases}-A^{(0)}_{k}U(a-1,t)+\mathrm{i}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B^{(0)}_{k}U(1-a,\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{0},\\
-A^{(1)}_{k}U(a-1,t)-\mathrm{i}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B^{(1)}_{k}U(1-a,-\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{1},\\
A^{(-1)}_{k}U(a-1,-t)+\mathrm{i}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B^{(-1)}_{k}U(1-a,\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{-1},\\
A^{(2)}_{k}U(a-1,-t)-\mathrm{i}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B^{(2)}_{k}U(1-a,-\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{2},\\
A^{(-2)}_{k}U(a-1,-t)-\mathrm{i}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B^{(-2)}_{k}U(1-a,-\mathrm{i}t),&\lambda\in S_{-2}.\end{cases}

In each sector, the asymptotic expansion
[
26
, equation (12.9.1)]
:

\displaystyle U\left(a,t\right)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4}t^{2}}t^{-a-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\frac{{\big(\frac{1}{2}+a\big)_{2j}}}{j!(2t^{2})^{j}},\qquad t\to\infty,\qquad|\arg(t)|<\frac{3}{4}\pi

(A.2)

can be used to obtain the asymptotic behavior of the matrix elements of
\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)
. Since the normalization condition on
\mathbf{P}(\lambda)=\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}\sigma_{3}/2}=\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}\sigma_{3}/4}
forbids exponential growth, it is necessary that
A_{1}^{(i)}=0
and
B_{2}^{(i)}=0
for all sector indices
i=0,\pm 1,\pm 2
. From the jump conditions it follows that the second column of
\mathbf{Q}
must match between sectors
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
, as well as between sectors
S_{-1}
and
S_{-2}
. This implies that
A_{2}^{(0)}=A_{2}^{(1)}
and that
A_{2}^{(-1)}=A_{2}^{(-2)}
. Similarly, the first column of
\mathbf{Q}
must match between sectors
S_{0}
and
S_{-1}
, as well as between sectors
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. This implies that
B_{1}^{(0)}=B_{1}^{(-1)}
and that
B_{1}^{(1)}=B_{1}^{(2)}
. Lastly, the jump condition between sectors
S_{2}
and
S_{-2}
implies that
B_{1}^{(-2)}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}B_{1}^{(2)}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}B_{1}^{(1)}
and that
A_{2}^{(2)}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}A_{2}^{(-2)}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}A_{2}^{(-1)}
. Therefore, if
\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}(\lambda)
denotes the restriction of
\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)
to
\lambda\in S_{i}
, we have so far found that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(0)}(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}rB_{1}^{(0)}U(-a,\mathrm{i}t)&rA_{2}^{(0)}U(a,t)\\
\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B_{1}^{(0)}U(1-a,\mathrm{i}t)&\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{3\mathrm{i}\pi/4}A_{2}^{(0)}U(a-1,t)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\lambda\in S_{0},

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(1)}(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}rB_{1}^{(1)}U(-a,-\mathrm{i}t)&rA_{2}^{(0)}U(a,t)\\
\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-3\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B_{1}^{(1)}U(1-a,-\mathrm{i}t)&\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{3\mathrm{i}\pi/4}A_{2}^{(0)}U(a-1,t)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\lambda\in S_{1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(-1)}(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}rB_{1}^{(0)}U(-a,\mathrm{i}t)&rA_{2}^{(-1)}U(a,-t)\\
\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B_{1}^{(0)}U(1-a,\mathrm{i}t)&\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}A_{2}^{(-1)}U(a-1,-t)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\lambda\in S_{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(2)}(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}rB_{1}^{(1)}U(-a,-\mathrm{i}t)&\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}rA_{2}^{(-1)}U(a,-t)\\
\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-3\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B_{1}^{(1)}U(1-a,-\mathrm{i}t)&\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}A_{2}^{(-1)}U(a-1,-t)\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathbf{Q}^{(2)}(\lambda)=}{}\ \lambda\in S_{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(-2)}(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}rB_{1}^{(1)}U(-a,-\mathrm{i}t)&rA_{2}^{(-1)}U(a,-t)\\
\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi p}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-3\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)B_{1}^{(1)}U(1-a,-\mathrm{i}t)&\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}A_{2}^{(-1)}U(a-1,-t)\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathbf{Q}^{(-2)}(\lambda)=}{}\ \lambda\in S_{-2}.

(A.4)

Now taking
\lambda\to\infty
in each sector we find that the matrix
\mathbf{P}(\lambda)=\mathbf{Q}(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}\sigma_{3}/2}
satisfies the required normalization condition provided that

\displaystyle a=\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{i}p\qquad\text{which implies that}\quad rs=-2p,

(A.5)

and that the remaining unknown coefficients are subject to the following:

\displaystyle rB_{1}^{(0)}=\mathrm{e}^{-\pi p/4}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}p\ln(2)/2},\qquad

\displaystyle A_{2}^{(0)}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-3\mathrm{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi p/4}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}p\ln(2)/2},

\displaystyle rB_{1}^{(1)}=\mathrm{e}^{3\pi p/4}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}p\ln(2)/2},\qquad

\displaystyle A_{2}^{(-1)}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{e}^{3\pi p/4}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}p\ln(2)/2}.

(A.6)

Only the values of the constants
r
and
s
remain undetermined. It is interesting to observe that if one tries to use the definitions in (
A.1
) to find
r
and
s
from the formulæ (
A.3
)–(
A.4
) after substituting from (
A.5
) and (
A.6
) and using the asymptotic expansion (
A.2
) for the off-diagonal entries, one finds simply the truisms
r=r
and
s=s
. These constants must therefore be determined from the only other information we have not yet used: the nontrivial jumps captured by the first (resp. second) column of the jump matrix
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PC}}
for
\arg(\lambda)=\tfrac{1}{4}\pi,-\tfrac{3}{4}\pi
(resp. for
\arg(\lambda)=-\tfrac{1}{4}\pi,\tfrac{3}{4}\pi
). In particular, the jump condition for
\arg(\lambda)=\tfrac{1}{4}\pi
requires that

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}_{11}(\lambda)=Q^{(0)}_{11}(\lambda)+\tau Q^{(0)}_{12}(\lambda),

which given all available information is equivalent to the condition

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi(a+1/2)/2}U(-a,-\mathrm{i}t)+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi(1+1/2)/2}U(-a,\mathrm{i}t)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\tau r\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi p/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}p\ln(2)}U(a,t),\qquad a=\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{i}p,\qquad\arg(t)=0.

But it is easy to check that this matches the connection formula
[
26

for the parabolic cylinder function

provided that

\displaystyle r=r(p,\tau):=2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\pi p/2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}p\ln(2)}}{\tau\Gamma(\mathrm{i}p)}.

(A.7)

Using (
A.5
)
one then deduces that

\displaystyle s=s(p,\tau)=-\frac{2p}{r(p,\tau)}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{3\mathrm{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\tau p\Gamma(\mathrm{i}p)\mathrm{e}^{-\pi p/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}p\ln(2)}.

(A.8)

At last all free parameters have been determined, and it is straightforward to verify with the use of connection formulæ for
U(\cdot,\cdot)
that the other jump conditions that we have not yet enforced are automatically satisfied.

Remark A.2
.

In fact, the unenforced jump conditions cannot contain any further information, because the cyclic product of the jump matrices for
\mathbf{Q}
is the identity (as must be the case for consistency at the origin). This matrix identity modulo unit determinant amounts to three conditions, which determine all of the off-diagonal entries of
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{PC}}
given any one of them.

This completes the solution of Riemann–Hilbert Problem

. We pause to point out that the normalization condition on
\mathbf{P}(\lambda)=\mathbf{P}(\lambda;p,\tau)
indeed holds in every sense we assumed at the start in order to derive the differential equation (

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\lambda;p,\tau)\lambda^{\mathrm{i}p\sigma_{3}}=\mathbb{I}+\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}\lambda}\begin{bmatrix}0&r(p,\tau)\\
-s(p,\tau)&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}O\big(\lambda^{-2}\big)&O\big(\lambda^{-3}\big)\\
O\big(\lambda^{-3}\big)&O\big(\lambda^{-2}\big)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\lambda\to\infty.

(A.9)

In fact, (

In the special case that
