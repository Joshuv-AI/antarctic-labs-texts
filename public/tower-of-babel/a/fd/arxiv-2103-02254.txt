The exact value of Hausdorff dimension of escaping sets of class ℬ \mathcal{B} meromorphic functions
Volker Mayer, Mariusz Urbański

Abstract
. We consider the subclass of class ℬ \mathcal{B} consisting of meromorphic functions f : ℂ → ℂ ^ f:{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}} for which infinity is not an asymptotic value and whose all poles have orders uniformly bounded from above. This class was introduced in [ 7 ] and the Hausdorff dimension HD ​ ( ℐ ​ ( f ) ) \text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f)) of the set ℐ ⁡ ( f ) \mathcal{I}(f) of all points escaping to infinity under forward iteration of f f was estimated therein. In this paper we provide a closed formula for the exact value of HD ​ ( ℐ ​ ( f ) ) \text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f)) identifying it with the critical exponent of the natural series introduced in [ 7 ] . This exponent is very easy to calculate for many concrete functions. In particular, we construct a function from this class which is of infinite order and for which HD ​ ( ℐ ​ ( f ) ) = 0 \text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=0 .

1.
Introduction

We deal with the dynamics of meromorphic functions from class
\mathcal{B}
. This class consists in all meromorphic functions
f:{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
for which the
set
S(f)
of finite asymptotical and critical values is bounded. For entire functions, class
\mathcal{B}
was introduced by Eremenko and Lyubich in
[
9
]
.

Recalling standard definitions, the
Fatou
set
\mathcal{F}(f)
of a function
f\in\mathcal{B}
consists in all points in
{\mathbb{C}}
that admit a neighborhood on which all iterates of
f
are defined and normal in the sense of Montel. Then, the
Julia
set
\mathcal{J}(f)
of
f
is the complement of the
Fatou
set
\mathcal{F}(f)
.

In this paper we focus on another dynamically significant set, namely the
escaping
set
of
f

(1.1)

\mathcal{I}(f)=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;f^{n}(z)\to\infty\;\;\text{as}\;\;n\to\infty\}

which is also the intersection over all
R>0
of the sets

(1.2)

\mathcal{I}_{R}(f)=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;\liminf_{n\to\infty}|f^{n}(z)|\geq R\}.

If
f
is a polynomial then
\mathcal{I}(f)
is simply the basin of (immediate) attraction to infinity which is a central object in the study of the dynamics of polynomials.
If
f\in\mathcal{B}
is transcendental then
\mathcal{I}(f)\neq\emptyset
and
\mathcal{I}(f)\subset\mathcal{J}(f)
(see
[
9
]
and
[
21
]
) and we are interested in

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f)),

where
\text{{\rm HD}}(E)
denotes the
Hausdorff dimension
of a set
E
contained in a metric space.

McMullen showed in
[
15
]
that the Julia set of any exponential function
z\mapsto\lambda e^{z}
,
\lambda\neq 0
, has Hausdorff dimension equal to
2

and that the Julia set of any sine function
z\mapsto\sin(\alpha z+\beta)
,
\alpha\neq 0
, has even positive Lebesgue measure. His result has been extended by Barański

[
3
]
and Schubert
[
22
]
to all entire functions in
class
\mathcal{B}
that have finite order.
As it is mentioned in
[
7
]
, their proof actually shows that

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=2

for all such functions and in fact for all meromorphic functions in the Eremenko-Lyubich class
\mathcal{B}
that have finite order and
for which
\infty
is an asymptotic value. Bergweiler, Karpińska, and Stallard showed in
[
6
]
that this result
even also holds for some functions
f\in\mathcal{B}
having infinite order.
The situation is totally different when
\infty
is not an asymptotical value as was shown by Bergweiler and Kotus in
[
7
]
who, improving former results for elliptic functions by Kotus and Urbański (see
[
12
]
, comp.
[
13
]
), obtained the following upper estimate.

Theorem 1.1

(Bergweiler and Kotus)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}
be a transcendental meromorphic function having finite order
\rho<\infty
, for which
\infty
is not an asymptotic value and
such that there exists
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that the multiplicity of all poles, except possibly finitely many, is at most M. Then

(1.3)

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))\leq\frac{2M\rho}{2+M\rho}

and

(1.4)

\lim_{R\to\infty}\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}(f))\leq\frac{2M\rho}{2+M\rho}.

In this article we determine the exact values of these Hausdorff dimensions and we do this for the whole class of functions in Theorem
1.1
, and beyond.
The exact value of the Hausdorff dimension of the escaping set has been so far known only in some special cases, mainly for elliptic functions (see
[
13
]
), for the functions of the form
R(e^{z})
, where
R
is a rational function, obtained in the paper
[
11
]
by Galazka and Kotus, and for Nevanlinna functions obtained in the paper
[
8
]
by Cui, where the special case
z\mapsto\lambda\tan z
has been treated in
[
17
]
.
Notice that for this later case one has strict inequality

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))<\frac{2M\rho}{2+M\rho}.

Equality holds for all elliptic functions and for some interesting examples considered in
[
7
]
.
Finally, there are also original new examples provided by Aspenberg and Cui in
[
2
]
which consist in some functions
that have finite order and a finite singular set, that belong to Speiser class
\mathcal{S}
, and whose escaping set may have any Hausdorff dimension in
[0,2]
.

Let
f
be like in Theorem
1.1
,
let
(a_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
be the sequence of all its poles, and let
m_{j}
be the multiplicity of
a_{j}
,
j\geq 1
. Then

(1.5)

\limsup_{j\to\infty}m_{j}=M<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad f(z)\sim\left(\frac{b_{j}}{z-a_{j}}\right)^{m_{j}}\quad\text{as}\quad z\to a_{j}

for every
j\geq 1
with some
b_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
.
Bergweiler and Kotus considered the series

(1.6)

\sum_{j\;s.t.\;a_{j}\neq 0}\left(\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+1/M}}\right)^{t},

and an essential step in their proof of Theorem
1.1
was to show the convergence of it for all
t>\frac{2M\rho}{2+M\rho}
.

Definition 1.2
.

The critical exponent
\delta
of the series (
1.6
) is the infimum over all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
for which the
series (
1.6
) converges.

Notice that
\delta
does depend on
M
which itself does depend on
f
. Also, as explained above, this critical exponent is at most
\frac{2M\rho}{2+M\rho}

but in general it is strictly smaller. On the other hand, the functions under consideration have infinitely many poles and thus
\delta\geq 0
.

The main result of this article is the following result. Not only that it holds for all functions of Theorem
1.1
but it also applies to functions of infinite order.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f
be a meromorphic function in class
\mathcal{B}
for which
\infty
is not an asymptotic value
and whose poles have uniformly bounded multiplicities. Then

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=\lim_{R\to\infty}\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}(f))=\delta,

where
\delta
is the critical exponent of the series (
1.6
).

Rempe-Gillen and Stallard called in
[
19
]
the number
\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{R}(f)
the
eventual dimension
of
f
and asked if it can differ from

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))
. Theorem
1.3
shows that this is not the case for any function considered therein.

Combining our result with the aforementioned results by Barański and Schubert, one obtains the following full description for the Hausdorff dimension of escaping sets.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}
be a meromorphic function of finite order with poles having bounded multiplicity. Then

(1)

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=2
if
\infty
is an asymptotic value of
f
and

(2)

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=\delta
otherwise.

The upper bound in Theorem
1.1
does depend on the order
\rho
of
f
and converges to
2
when
\rho\to\infty
.
This suggests that
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=2
whenever
f
has infinite order, and this is the reason why in this case the behavior of
\mathcal{I}(f)
with respect
to Lebesgue measure has been studied rather than the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{I}(f)
(see
[
7
, Theorem 1.3]
and the discussion preceding it). However, our result, i.e. Theorem
1.3
, shows that
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))
does not really depend on the order. It certainly depends on the distribution of the poles but is also influenced
by the coefficients
b_{j}
.

It is quite easy to determine the critical exponent
\delta
as soon as the poles
a_{j}
and the coefficients
b_{j}
are known.
We exploit this in providing some quite simple explicit examples in Section
7
. Among them are functions having the following properties
which, up to our best knowledge, have not appeared in the literature.

Theorem 1.5
.

There exist meromorphic functions
f\in\mathcal{B}
of positive finite and also of infinite order for which
\infty
is not an asymptotic value, whose all poles have bounded multiplicities, and

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=0.

Our paper is organized as follows. First we present some background in Section
2
.
In the next section, Section
3
, we collect some properties of the behavior of the functions from Theorem
1.3
, near poles.
The first step of the proof of Theorem
1.3
is also done therein.
This part provides an upper estimate of
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))
which is a
straightforward adaption of the proof from
[
7
]
. Indeed,
Bergweiler and Kotus used the most natural dynamical covers of the escaping set, in fact of
\mathcal{I}_{R}(f)
,
from which the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
follows. The starting point of the present paper is the observation that their argument
gives in fact that

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))\leq\lim_{R\to\infty}\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}(f))\leq\delta.

Establishing a precise lower bound is the new and delicate step. For all specific functions for which
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))
was known, such as elliptic and Nevanlinna functions mentioned above, the precise lower bound was obtained using McMullen’s method in
[
15
]
. However, for this method to give sharp estimates the poles must be very
uniformly distributed in addition with a uniform behavior of coefficients
b_{j}
,
j\geq 1
. This is clearly the case for elliptic and Nevanlinna functions as well
as for the examples in
[
7
]
.
In order to treat our general case we had to proceed totally differently. Our idea was to use the concept of non-autonomous iterated function systems and some results proved for them. Indeed, in Section
4
, we briefly summarize some facts from this theory. Then, in Section
5
, we prove the inequality

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))\geq\delta,

by associating to the escaping set
\mathcal{I}(f)
some non-autonomous conformal iterated function system. This allows us to apply then some results of Rempe-Gillen and the second named author obtained in
[
20
]
. We construct our non-autonomous iterated function system
by carefully choosing appropriate inverse branches of the third iterate of the function
f
, and by doing some desingularizing change of variables which moves infinity to zero. Theorem
1.3
is then proved.

In Section
6
we slightly generalize Theorem
1.3
. Our method being of local nature, we can consider arbitrary functions of class
\mathcal{B}

having infinitely many poles and determine the dimension of the points whose orbit escape through poles having multiplicity bounded by any given number
M
. We illustrate this by considering Euler Gamma functions which have in the same time a logarithmic tract and infinity many simple poles.
From Barański’s

[
3
]
and Schubert’s
[
22
]
work we know that the set of points of the escaping set whose orbit eventually are in the logarithmic tract has full dimension two. Here we get the additional information that the set of points escaping to infinity through poles is zero.

Finally in Section
7
we prove Theorem
1.5
by providing simple concrete constructions.

2.
Preliminaries

The disk centered at
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
and of radius
R>0
will be denoted by
{\mathbb{D}}(a,R)
and we define

{\mathbb{D}}_{R}^{*}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;|z|>R\}.

If for some constant
C\geq 1
we have
A/C\leq B\leq CA
then this will be denoted by
A\asymp B
. If we need quantitative estimates, we write more precisely

A\asymp^{C}B.

The inverse of a transcendental meromorphic function
f
can have two types of singularities: asymptotic and critical values.
A point
b\in\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
is an asymptotic value of
f
if there exists a curve
\gamma\subset{\mathbb{C}}
tending to infinity such that
f(z)\to b

as
z\to\infty
along
\gamma
. A critical value of
f
is any point of the form
f(c)
where

c\in{\mathcal{C}_{f}}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\;f\text{ is defined at $z$ and }f^{\prime}(z)\neq 0\},

the set of critical points of
f
. The set of all finite singular values of
f
is denoted by
S(f)
and is commonly referred to as the singular set of
f
. A detailed description of singular values of meromorphic functions can be found for example in
[
5
]
.

We repeat from the introduction that class
\mathcal{B}
consists in all those meromorphic functions
f
for which the singular set
S(f)
is bounded. This class goes back to
Eremenko and Lyubich
[
9
]
who introduced it for entire functions. The functions in
\mathcal{B}
are also frequently called of
bounded type
.

Unless stated otherwise, we consider in this paper transcendental meromorphic functions
f
having the following properties:

(i)

f\in\mathcal{B}
.

(ii)

\infty
is not asymptotic value of
f
.

(iii)

All poles
a_{j}
of
f
have uniformly bounded multiplicities denoted by
m_{j}
, so that the number
M
in (
1.5
) is well defined, and consequently so is the critical exponent
\delta
. Also, we can and we will assume without loss of generality that
0
is not a pole of
f
.

One should always have in mind that a transcendental meromorphic function
f
for which
\infty
is not an asymptotic value
always has infinitely many poles. Moreover, we can use them to characterize the order
\rho=\rho(f)
of the function
f
as the the critical exponent of the series

(2.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|a_{j}|^{t}},

meaning that this series converges if
t>\rho
and diverges if
t<\rho
; all these facts are in
[
7
, Section 2 ]
and they go back to Iversen and Teichmüller.

We fix a number
R_{0}>0
such that

(2.2)

S(f)\subset{\mathbb{D}}(0,R_{0}/2)\ \text{ and }\ f(0)\subset{\mathbb{D}}(0,R_{0}/2).

Let
R\geq R_{0}
.
Since
\infty
is not an asymptotic value, Lemma 2.1 in
[
21
]
asserts that
f^{-1}({\mathbb{D}}_{R}^{*})
is a countable union of simply connected bounded components

U_{j}(R)
,
j\geq 1
, each of which contains exactly one pole
a_{j}\in U_{j}(R)
. When
R\geq R_{0}
is fixed then we often denote these components simply
U_{j}
.

All relevant information on the dynamics of transcendental functions, in particular the definition of the Julia set, can be found in the survey
[
4
]
by Bergweiler, see also
[
13
]
. We deal here only with the escaping set
\mathcal{I}(f)
and with the sets
\mathcal{I}_{R}(f)
which have been introduced in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
).

Falconer’s book
[
10
]
,
[
18
]
, and
[
13
]
contain all relevant information on fractal dimensions we need; especially the two latter of them are designed for dynamical needs. One can find the definition of Hausdorff dimension in Section 3
and we recall that the Hausdorff dimension of a set
E
will be denoted by
\text{{\rm HD}}(E)
. Many results on fractal dimensions for meromorphic functions have been obtained by several
authors, the interested reader can find an overview of them in the survey
[
23
]
by Stallard.

The restriction of every conformal map
g:{\mathbb{D}}(0,2r)\to{\mathbb{C}}
to
{\mathbb{D}}(0,r)
has
bounded distortion
in the sense that there exists an absolute constant
K\geq 1
such that

(2.3)

|g^{\prime}(z_{1})|\leq K|g^{\prime}(z_{2})|\quad\text{for all $z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{D}}(0,r)$.}

This is the well known Koebe’s distortion theorem.

We will need its more sophisticated version. It relies on the concept of modulus of ring domains that we briefly recall now,
see for example
[
1
]
for more details.

The modulus of the round annulus
A(r,R)=\{r<|z|<R\}
is

mod(A(r,R))=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\frac{R}{r}\quad,\quad 0<r<R

and the modulus is a conformal invariant which can be used to define the modulus of general annuli.
These are domains

\mathcal{A}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
such that, for some radii
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
,
\mathcal{A}
is the conformal image of
A(r_{1},r_{2})
and thus in fact the conformal image
of
A(1,R)
where
R=\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}
. Set then

mod(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log R\,.

We need the following important fact which results from Theorem 2 in
[
1
, p.10]
.

Fact 2.1
.

If
\mathcal{A}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is an annuli containing a round annuli

A(r,R)\subset\mathcal{A}
for some
0<r<R<\infty

then

mod(\mathcal{A})\geq\operatorname{mod}A(r,R)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log R/r.

The following generalization of (
2.3
) is contained in Theorem 2.9 in
[
16
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For every
m>0
there exists
K(m)\geq 1
such that the following holds.
Let
U
and
V
be open domains in
{\mathbb{C}}
both conformally equivalent to
{\mathbb{D}}
such that
U\subset\overline{U}\subset V
and

\operatorname{mod}(V\setminus\overline{U})\geq m.

If
F:V\to{\mathbb{C}}
is holomorphic univalent map. Then

|F^{\prime}(z_{2})|\leq K(m)|F^{\prime}(z_{1})|\quad\text{for all $z_{1},z_{2}\in U$.}

3.
Estimates and upper bound

This part is a straightforward adaption
of
[
7
]
. Throughout the whole section
f
is a meromorphic function satisfying all the hypotheses of Theorem
1.3
.
We first give a fairly complete description of the behavior of the function
f
near its poles and then we give the required upper estimate
of the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{I}_{R}(f)
for large
R
.

3.1.
Estimates near poles

The behavior of a function
f
near a pole
a_{j}
follows from (
1.1
). So, let us recall it:

f(z)\sim\left(\frac{b_{j}}{z-a_{j}}\right)^{m_{j}}

as
z\to a_{j}
for some complex number
b_{j}\neq 0
and with

m_{j}
being the multiplicity of
f
at
a_{j}
that satisfies
1\leq m_{j}\leq M
except for at most finitely many poles. Since we will only consider poles that lie outside some large disk we can assume that

(3.1)

1\leq m_{i}\leq M=\limsup_{j\to\infty}m_{j}.

for every
i\geq 1
. We recall that
R_{0}>0
has been fixed large enough so that (
2.2
) holds. We shall prove the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
j\geq 1
there exists a conformal homeomorphism
\Psi_{j}:U_{j}(R_{0})\to{\mathbb{D}}(0,R_{0}^{-1/m_{j}})
such that
\Psi_{j}(a_{j})=0
,
\Psi_{j}^{\prime}(a_{j})=1/b_{j}
, and

f|_{U_{j}(R_{0})}=1/\Psi_{j}^{m_{j}}

Proof.

Let
\Psi_{j}:U_{j}(R_{0})\to{\mathbb{D}}(0,R_{0}^{-1/m_{j}})
be any conformal homeomorphism with
\Psi(a_{j})=0
. It follows from the asymptotic behavior of
f
near
a_{j}
that the function
f\,\Psi_{j}^{m_{j}}
is well defined, holomorphic, and non-zero on
U_{j}(R_{0})
and in addition

\lim_{U_{j}(R_{0})\ni z\to\partial U_{j}(R_{0})}|f(z)\,\Psi_{j}^{m_{j}}(z)|=1.

Thus, the maximum principle implies that

f\,\Psi_{j}^{m_{j}}
is constant and of modulus one.
The required conformal map is now just this
\Psi_{j}
normalized by a rotation and then clearly
\Psi_{j}^{\prime}(a_{j})=1/b_{j}
.
∎

As the first application of this lemma, we can now describe the geometry of the domains
U_{j}
. In the next lemma, the constant
K
is the absolute constant from Koebe’s distortion property (
2.3
).

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
R\geq 2^{M}R_{0}
,

(3.2)

{\mathbb{D}}\Big(a_{j},\frac{1}{K}R^{-1/m_{j}}|b_{j}|\Big)\subset U_{j}(R)\subset{\mathbb{D}}\Big(a_{j},KR^{-1/m_{j}}|b_{j}|\Big).

Moreover, there exists
R_{1}\geq 2^{M}R_{0}
such that for every
R\geq R_{1}
there exists
\kappa_{R}>1
such that

\kappa_{R}\leq 2
for every
R\geq R_{1}
,
\lim_{r\to\infty}\kappa_{R}=1
, and

(3.3)

\frac{1}{\kappa_{R}}\leq\frac{|z|}{|a_{j}|}\leq\kappa_{R}

for every
z\in U_{j}(R)
.

Proof.

We have
\Psi_{j}(U_{j}(R))={\mathbb{D}}(0,R^{-1/m_{j}})
where
\Psi_{j}
is
the conformal map of Lemma
3.1
. Since
m_{j}\leq M
, we have that

R^{-1/m_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{2}R_{0}^{-1/m_{j}}
. This allows us to apply the bounded distortion property (
2.3
) to
\Psi_{j}^{-1}
from which (
3.2
) follows.

First let
R:=2^{M}R_{0}
. By (
2.2
),
0\not\in U_{j}(R)
and, using the first inclusion of (
3.2
), we see that

(3.4)

|a_{j}|-\frac{|b_{j}|}{KR^{1/m_{j}}}>0,\quad\text{thus}\quad|b_{j}|\leq KR|a_{j}|=K2^{M}R_{0}|a_{j}|.

Now, take an arbitrary
R\geq 2^{M}R_{0}
and then
z\in U_{j}(R)
. Then,

|z-a_{j}|\leq\frac{K}{R^{1/m_{j}}}|b_{j}|\leq\frac{K}{R^{1/M}}K2^{M}R_{0}|a_{j}|,

and thus

\Big|\frac{z}{a_{j}}-1\Big|\leq 2^{M}K^{2}\frac{R_{0}}{R^{1/M}}

which implies (
3.3
) if
R
is large enough, which determines
R_{1}
.
∎

The next technical result provides uniform estimates for the derivative
f^{\prime}
in the domains
U_{j}(R_{1})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
j\geq 1
we have that

|f^{\prime}|\asymp^{KM}\frac{1}{|b_{j}|}|f|^{1+\frac{1}{m_{j}}}\quad\text{in $\;\;U_{j}(R_{1})$.}

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
we have
1/f=\Psi_{j}^{m_{j}}
in
U_{j}
. Differentiating this relation yields

|f^{\prime}|=m_{j}|\Psi_{j}^{m_{j}-1}\Psi_{j}^{\prime}f^{2}|=m_{j}|\Psi_{j}^{\prime}||f|^{1+\frac{1}{m_{j}}}\asymp m_{j}\frac{1}{|b_{j}|}|f|^{1+\frac{1}{m_{j}}}\quad\text{in $\;U_{j}$}

by (
2.3
) and since
\Psi^{\prime}(a_{j})=1/b_{j}
. This proves the lemma since
m_{j}\leq M
.
∎

3.2.
Upper estimate

Let us recall that this part is taken from
[
7
]
except that we alter the final conclusion.
We include it for the sake of completeness and as a preparation for the proof of the lower bound. Here is the precise statement.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f
be a meromorphic function of class
\mathcal{B}
for which
\infty
is not an asymptotic value
and with poles having bounded multiplicity. Then

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))\leq\lim_{R\to\infty}\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}(f))\leq\delta,

where
\delta
is the critical exponent of the series (
1.6
).

Proof.

For every
R\geq R_{1}
define the set

\mathcal{I}_{R}^{0}(f):=\big\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:f^{n}(z)\geq R\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq 0\big\}

and, given an integer
l\geq 0
,

4.
Non autonomous IFS

A conformal iterated function system, IFS for short, in the complex plane is given by a family
\Phi=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of conformal contractions

\Phi_{i}:\Omega\to\Omega
, where
I
is an arbitrary countable set and where

\Omega
is a bounded simply connected domain in
{\mathbb{C}}
having some geometric properties that we will describe below.
We assume that the functions
\Phi_{i}
are in fact defined and continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
and that
\Phi_{i}(\overline{\Omega})\subset\Omega
.

One is interested by the dynamics of
all possible compositions

\Phi_{i_{1}}\circ...\circ\Phi_{i_{n}}:\Omega\longrightarrow\Omega\;\;,\quad i_{k}\in I\;\text{ for every }\;1\leq k\leq n.

Non-autonomous dynamical systems vary in time. In the setting of IFS this means that
for each
k
the functions
\Phi_{i_{k}}
in the above composition belong to a different family of functions. For example, consider the family
\Phi=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}

and define for each
n\geq 1
a set

I^{(n)}\subset I
hence a family
\Phi^{(n)}=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I^{(n)}}
.
The corresponding non autonomous dynamical system is given by the compositions

\Phi_{i_{1}}\circ...\circ\Phi_{i_{n}}:\Omega\longrightarrow\Omega\;,\quad(i_{1},...,i_{n})\in\mathcal{I}_{n}=I^{(1)}\times...\times I^{(n)}\;,\quad n\geq 1,

and the limit set of this non-autonomous IFS is
\Phi^{(n)}=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I^{(n)}}
,
n\geq 1
, is defined to be

\mathcal{J}:=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\bigcup_{(i_{1},...,i_{n})\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}\Phi_{i_{1}}\circ...\circ\Phi_{i_{n}}(\Omega).

We only consider the case where all the sets
I^{(n)}
are finite but their cardinalities are allowed to vary and grow with
n
, however only subexponentially, meaning that

(4.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\#I^{(n)}=0\,.

We then say that the non–autonomous IFS
\Phi^{(n)}=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I^{(n)}}
,
n\geq 1
, is of subexponential growth.
We are interested in the Hausdorff dimension
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{J})
of the limit set
\mathcal{J}
of this system, and it is shown in
[
20
]
that this dimension is given by
a Bowen’s Formula provided the system
\Phi
has the following properties.

(1)

\Omega
is bounded and convex.

(2)

Open Set Condition, meaning that

\Phi_{i}(\Omega)\cap\Phi_{j}(\Omega)=\emptyset

for all
n\geq 1
and all
i,j\in I^{(n)}
.

(3)

Bounded Distortion: all the compositions
\Phi_{i_{1}}\circ...\circ\Phi_{i_{n}}:\Omega\longrightarrow\Omega
,
(i_{1},...,i_{n})\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
have the property (
2.3
) on
\Omega
.

(4)

Uniform contraction: there exists
s\in(0,1)
such that

|\Phi^{\prime}(z)|\leq s
for every
z\in\Omega
and
i\in I
.

Also Bowen’s Formula is not true in general but we have from Theorem 1.1 in
[
20
]
:

Theorem 4.1

(Bowen’s Formula)
.

If a non–autonomous IFS
\Phi^{(n)}=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I^{(n)}}
,
n\geq 1
, has all the properties listed above and is of subexponential growth, then

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{J})=\inf\{t\geq 0\;:\;\;\underline{\text{{\rm P}}}(t)<0\},

where

\underline{\text{{\rm P}}}(t):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{(i_{1},...,i_{n})\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}\|(\Phi_{i_{1}}\circ...\circ\Phi_{i_{n}})^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{t}

is the, commonly called, lower pressure at
t
of the non–autonomous system
\Phi^{(n)}=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I^{(n)}}
,
n\geq 1
.

5.
Lower bound

Given Proposition
3.4
, Theorem
1.3
will be proved if we can show that

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))\geq\delta.

This will be done here and the strategy is to associate to

a non–autonomous
IFS consisting of compositions of some selected holomorphic inverse branches of

,
for which Theorem

(Bowen’s Formula) will be applicable. First of all we have to determine a good domain

on which our non–autonomous IFS is to act.

5.1.
The Domain for the IFS

For
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<2\pi
let

\mathcal{C}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}):=\exp\left(\big\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;\alpha_{1}<\Im z<\alpha_{2}\big\}\right)

be the cone delimited by the directions
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
. We recall that
\delta
is the critical exponent of (

).

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists a cone
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\alpha,\alpha+2\pi\,3/4)
such that

\sum_{j\;s.t.\;a_{j}\in\mathcal{C}}\left(\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+1/M}}\right)^{t}=\infty

for all
0\leq t<\delta
and such that
\mathcal{C}
contains infinitely many poles
a_{j}
of maximal multiplicity
m_{j}=M
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
can be covered by five cones
\mathcal{C}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
with
(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})/2\pi=1/4
, at least one of them
must contain infinitely many poles of maximal multiplicity. Denote it by
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

The plane minus the origin can also be covered by two cones
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
of the form

\mathcal{C}(\alpha,\alpha+2\pi\,3/4)
each of them containing
\mathcal{C}_{0}
so that both
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}

contain infinitely many poles of maximal multiplicity. Now, let
t_{0}<\delta
. Then the series in Lemma
5.1

diverges if
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}
or if
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Furthermore, if this is the case, say for

, then the

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}
diverges for all

. The conclusion now comes by considering
a sequence

with all

.
∎

For
R\geq R_{1}
let

\mathcal{C}_{R}:=\mathcal{C}\cap{\mathbb{D}}_{R}^{*}

where
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\alpha,\alpha+2\pi\,3/4)
is the cone of Lemma

.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists
R_{2}\geq 2K^{2M}R_{1}
such that for every pole

we have that

U_{j}(R_{2})\subset U_{j}\left(\frac{R_{2}}{2K^{2M}}\right)\subset\hat{\Omega}=\mathcal{C}_{R_{2}}(\alpha-\pi/8,\alpha+2\pi\,13/16).

Proof.

This follows immediately from the inequality (
3.3
) in Lemma
3.2
.
∎

In the following,
R_{2}
will be the number from this lemma and let

I
be the set of all
j
such that the pole
a_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{2R_{2}}
.

Since
\hat{\Omega}
is a simply connected subdomain of
{\mathbb{D}}_{R_{2}}^{*}
and
{\mathbb{D}}_{R_{2}}^{*}
is disjoint from
S(f)
, all holomorphic inverse branches of
f
are well defined on
\hat{\Omega}

and, for every
j\in I
, there exists
m_{j}
such branches mapping
\hat{\Omega}
into
U_{j}=U_{j}(R_{2})\subset\hat{\Omega}
. Denote by

f_{j}^{-1}:\hat{\Omega}\to U_{j}

one, arbitrarily chosen, of these inverse branches. We already gave a precise expression for the derivative of this function in the formula (
3.5
). Here we repeat it.

(5.1)

|(f_{j}^{-1})^{\prime}(z)|\asymp^{KM}\frac{|b_{j}|}{|z|^{1+\frac{1}{m_{j}}}}\quad,\quad z\in\hat{\Omega}.

Since, by Lemma
5.2
,
f_{j}^{-1}(\hat{\Omega})\subset\hat{\Omega}
for all
j\in I
,
we could build an IFS out of this family of functions. However it would fail to have some necessary properties. Especially, given the above expression
for the derivative of
f_{j}^{-1}
, these functions have no bounded distortion property on
\hat{\Omega}
. Nevertheless, there is a somewhat miraculous solution
to this problem by applying, what we would call, a singular change of variables.

5.2.
Desingularization and Derivative Estimates

Consider the map
u:\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
given by the formula

u(\xi)=\xi^{-M}.

It maps
{\mathbb{D}}\left(0,R_{2}^{-\frac{1}{M}}\right)\setminus\{0\}
onto
{\mathbb{D}}_{R_{2}}^{*}
and admits all holomorphic inverse branches on
\hat{\Omega}
. Choose again arbitrarily
one of them, denote it by
u_{*}^{-1}
, and define

\Omega:=u_{*}^{-1}(\hat{\Omega}).

Notice that
\Omega
is bounded and convex.

Transfer then the maps
f_{j}^{-1}
to
\Omega
by conjugation to get

\varphi_{j}:=u_{*}^{-1}\circ f_{j}^{-1}\circ u:\Omega\longrightarrow V_{j}:=u_{*}^{-1}(U_{j})\subset\Omega\quad\text{for all}\quad j\in I.

After having done this change of variables, not all new maps will have bounded distortion yet. However, the maps
\varphi_{j}
associated to poles with maximal multiplicity will
do have this property. The following lemma along with Lemma
5.4
are for us the two main ingredients to get, in Lemma
5.5
, the required bounded distortion.

Lemma 5.3
.

For every
j\in I
, we have that

|\varphi_{j}^{\prime}(\xi)|\asymp^{2KM}\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}|z|^{\frac{1}{M}-\frac{1}{m_{j}}}=\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}|\xi|^{\frac{M}{m_{j}}-1},\;\xi\in\Omega\text{ and }z=u(\xi).

In particular, when
a_{j}
is a pole with maximal multiplicity
m_{j}=M
then

|\varphi_{j}^{\prime}(\xi)|\asymp^{2KM}\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}\ ,\ \xi\in\Omega.

Proof.

Let
\xi\in\Omega
and set
z:=u(\xi)=\xi^{-M}
. since
u\circ\varphi_{j}=f_{j}^{-1}\circ u
, we get

(\varphi_{j}(\xi))^{-M}=f_{j}^{-1}(\xi^{-M}).

So, taking derivatives, gives

|\varphi_{j}^{\prime}(\xi)|=|(f_{j}^{-1})^{\prime}(z)|\frac{|\varphi_{j}(\xi)|^{M+1}}{|\xi|^{M+1}}=|(f_{j}^{-1})^{\prime}(z)|\frac{|z|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}{|f_{j}^{-1}(z)|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}

Since
f_{j}^{-1}(z)\in U_{j}
, Lemma
3.2
implies that
|f_{j}^{-1}(z)|\asymp^{2}|a_{j}|
. The affirmations in Lemma
5.3
now result from (
5.1
).
∎

5.3.
Non-autonomous IFS

Our goal now is to build an appropriate non–autonomous IFS out of the functions
\varphi_{j}
.
In order to do this, we first define an appropriate family of holomorphic maps on
\Omega
and verify that they have all necessary properties listed in Section
4
.
Then we explain how to choose for each
n\geq 1
its subfamily, depending on
n
, so that all these subfamilies taken together define a conformal non–autonomous IFS that fulfills all the requirements of Bowen’s Formula in Theorem
4.1
.

There are several constraints for a construction of such IFS. First of all, it must consist in maps that are uniformly contracting and have bounded
distortion. Secondly, its limit set must be, up to the change of variables
u
,
a subset of the escaping set of
f
, and it must have a sufficiently large Hausdorff dimension. It turns out that the multiplicity of the poles play a crucial role and
we get limit sets of large dimension only if the dynamics of the IFS pass sufficiently often through poles of maximal multiplicity. Since these poles are so important we introduce a second notation for them. Let
(j_{l})_{l=1}^{\infty}
be a strictly increasing sequence of elements in
I
such that
(a_{j_{l}})_{l=1}^{\infty}
are all the poles with multiplicity
m_{j_{l}}=M
in the set
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. Define then

{\bf a}_{l,M}:=a_{j_{l}},\,{\bf b}_{l,M}:=b_{j_{l}},\,{\bf V}_{l,M}:=u^{-1}({\bf U}_{l,M})=u^{-1}(U_{j_{l}})\text{ and }{\bf\varphi}_{l,M}:=\varphi_{j_{l}}.

Also, since
I
is infinite, we can assume without loss of generality that
I={\mathbb{N}}
.

The maps
\varphi_{j}
cannot be used directly but it turns out that there is a way to
compose these maps carefully in order to obtain a good family of generators for an IFS we want.
So, consider the following maps that correspond to inverse branches of the third iterate of
f
:

(5.2)

\Phi_{l,j}:={\bf\varphi}_{l,M}\circ\varphi_{j}\circ{\bf\varphi}_{l,M}:\Omega\longrightarrow\Omega\quad,\quad l\geq 1\;,\;j\in I.

These have indeed bounded distortion on
\Omega
and are uniformly contracting:

Lemma 5.4
.

For every
j\in I
, every
l\geq 1
, and every
z\in\Omega
, we have

|\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}(z)|\asymp\frac{|{\bf b}_{l,M}|^{2}}{|{\bf a}_{l,M}|^{2+\frac{1}{m_{j}}+\frac{1}{M}}}\,\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}

and

|\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Proof.

First of all

\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}=\Big({\bf\varphi}_{l,M}\circ\varphi_{j}\Big)^{\prime}\circ{\bf\varphi}_{l,M}\;{\bf\varphi}^{\prime}_{l,M}.

Lemma
5.3
and the fact that

{\bf a}_{l,M}
is a pole of maximal multiplicity yield

(5.3)

|{\bf\varphi}^{\prime}_{l,M}|\asymp^{2KM}\frac{|{\bf b}_{l,M}|}{|{\bf a}_{l,M}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}\quad\text{on}\quad\Omega\,.

Let
\xi\in\Omega
and
\eta={\bf\varphi}_{l,M}(\xi)\in{\bf V}_{l,M}
. We have to estimate the other factor of
\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}

at
\eta
. Since
u(\eta)\in{\bf U}_{l,M}
, we have that

|\eta|^{-M}=|u(\eta)|\asymp^{2}|{\bf a}_{l,M}|.

Using now Lemma
5.3
once more and remembering that
{\bf a}_{l,M}
has maximal multiplicity, we get

\begin{array}[]{rl}\Big|\Big({\bf\varphi}_{l,M}\circ\varphi_{j}\Big)^{\prime}(\eta)\Big|&\asymp^{(2KM)^{2}}\frac{|{\bf b}_{l,M}|}{|{\bf a}_{l,M}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}|\eta|^{\frac{M}{m_{j}}-1}\\
&\asymp^{2}\frac{|{\bf b}_{l,M}|}{|{\bf a}_{l,M}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}|{\bf a}_{l,M}|^{\frac{1}{M}-\frac{1}{m_{j}}}=\frac{|{\bf b}_{l,M}|}{|{\bf a}_{l,M}|^{1+\frac{1}{m_{j}}}}\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}.\end{array}

Multiplying this with (
5.3
) gives the required estimate for
|\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}|
.

It remains to verify the contraction property. The, already proved, first formula of our lemma gives

|\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}|\leq 2L^{3}\frac{|{\bf b}_{l,M}|^{2}}{|{\bf a}_{l,M}|^{2+\frac{1}{m_{j}}+\frac{1}{M}}}\,\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}

where
L=2KM
. By (
3.4
) we have
|b_{i}|\leq K2^{M}R_{0}|a_{i}|
for all
i
. So, since
|a_{i}|>R_{1}
and
m_{i}\leq M
,

|\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}|\leq 2L^{3}(K2^{M}R_{0})^{3}\frac{1}{R_{1}^{3/M}}.

Along with (
3.7
), this implies that
|\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}|\leq 1/4
. The proof is complete.
∎

We also need bounded distortion for arbitrary compositions of the maps
\Phi_{l,j}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

There exists
\hat{K}\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq 2
and all

(l_{k},i_{k})\in I
,

|F^{\prime}(z_{2})|\leq\hat{K}|F^{\prime}(z_{1})|\quad\text{for all $\;\;z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega$.}

Proof.

Write
F=F_{n-1}\circ\Phi_{l_{n},i_{n}}
where
F_{n-1}=\Phi_{l_{1},i_{1}}\circ...\circ\Phi_{l_{n-1},i_{n-1}}
.
The map
\Phi_{l_{n},i_{n}}
satisfies (
5.4
) with some constant
\hat{K}
, say
\hat{K}_{1}\geq 1
, by Lemma
5.4
.
Therefore, Lemma
5.5
will hold if we can show that there exists
\hat{K}_{2}
such that

|F^{\prime}_{n-1}(z_{1})|\leq\hat{K}_{2}|F^{\prime}_{n-1}(z_{2})|

for all

, all
(l_{k},i_{k})\in I
with
1\leq k\leq n
, and all

.

But this follows from the general Koebe distortion theorem, i.e. Theorem
2.2
, if we can find an open disk
D
such that
\Phi_{l_{n},i_{n}}(\Omega)\subset D
, the map
F_{n-1}
has a holomorphic univalent extension to
D
, and

\operatorname{mod}\bigl(D\setminus\overline{\Phi_{l_{n},i_{n}}(\Omega)}\bigr)\geq m

where

is a constant independent of all

,
(l_{k},i_{k})\in I
, and

.

We have that
\Phi_{l_{n},i_{n}}(\Omega)\subset{\bf V}_{l_{n},M}=u_{*}^{-1}({\bf U}_{l_{n},M})=u_{*}^{-1}(U_{j_{l_{n}}})
and, by Lemma

The map

is well defined, holomorphic, and univalent on the disk

It will be our disk

, and as

\operatorname{mod}\left(u_{*}^{-1}(U_{j_{l_{n}}}(R_{2}/Q)\setminus\overline{{\bf V}_{l_{n},M}}\right)=\operatorname{mod}\left(U_{j_{l_{n}}}(R_{2}/Q)\setminus\overline{U_{j_{l_{n}}}(R_{2})}\right),

it remains to estimate from below the modulus of the annulus

\mathcal{A}:=U_{j_{l_{n}}}(R_{2}/Q)\setminus\overline{U_{j_{l_{n}}}(R_{2})}.

This can be done by applying Lemma
3.2
. Indeed, it follows from this lemma that

U_{j_{l_{n}}}(R_{2})\subset{\mathbb{D}}\Big(a_{j_{l_{n}}},KR_{2}^{-1/m_{j_{l_{n}}}}|b_{j_{l_{n}}}|\Big)

and

{\mathbb{D}}\Big(a_{j_{l_{n}}},\frac{1}{K}(R_{2}/Q)^{-1/m_{j_{l_{n}}}}|b_{j_{l_{n}}}|\Big)\subset U_{j_{l_{n}}}(R_{2}/Q).

Thus,
\mathcal{A}
contains the round annulus

A\Big(KR_{2}^{-1/m_{j_{l_{n}}}}|b_{j_{l_{n}}}|,\frac{1}{K}(R_{2}/Q)^{-1/m_{j_{l_{n}}}}|b_{j_{l_{n}}}|\Big),

which implies, by Fact
2.1
, that

\operatorname{mod}(\mathcal{A})\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\frac{Q^{1/m_{j_{l_{n}}}}}{K^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2\pi M}\log 2.

So, the proof is complete by putting
m:=\frac{1}{2\pi M}\log 2>0
.
∎

Having now the right family of contractions defined on an appropriate domain
\Omega
we are ready to define the
non-autonomous system.
In order to do so, we will choose for each
n\geq 1
a set
I^{(n)}\subset{\mathbb{N}}\times I
and then
consider compositions

\Phi_{l_{1},i_{1}}\circ\Phi_{l_{2},i_{2}}\circ...\circ\Phi_{l_{n},i_{n}}:\Omega\longrightarrow\Omega

with
(l_{k},i_{k})\subset I^{(k)}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. The choice of these index sets does depend on the parameter
t
. So, fix
t<\delta
arbitrary. Then, by the definition of
\delta
, the series (
1.6
) is divergent for this
t
. Lemma
5.4
then implies that

\sum_{j\geq j_{l}}\inf|\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}|^{t}=+\infty

for every
l\geq 1
, where
(j_{l})_{l=1}^{\infty}
is again the sequence corresponding to the poles of maximal multiplicity

{\bf a}_{l,M}=a_{j_{l}}
. Therefore, for every
l\geq 1
, there exists
k_{l}>j_{l}
with

\sum_{j\geq j_{l}}^{k_{l}}\inf|\Phi^{\prime}_{l,j}|^{t}\geq 2

and, adding some terms in these sums if necessary, we may assume that

k_{l+1}-j_{l+1}\geq k_{l}-j_{l}

for all
l\geq 1
.
Define now

\hat{I}^{(l)}:=\{(l,j)\;:\;j_{l}\leq j\leq k_{l}\},\ \ l\geq 1.

\#\hat{I}^{(l)}
, the number of elements of
I^{(l)}
, increases with
l
but may vary a lot. This is the reason why we define the sets
I^{(n)}
as follows. They interpolate
the sets
\hat{I}^{(l)}
but in such a way that

\#I^{(n+1)}\leq\#I^{(n)}+1

for all
n\geq 1
. One way of doing this is the following. Let

I^{(1)}:=\hat{I}^{(1)}.

If
\#\hat{I}^{(2)}=\#I^{(1)}
, then put

I^{(2)}:=\hat{I}^{(2)}.

Otherwise, we have
\#\hat{I}^{(2)}=\#I^{(1)}+N_{1}
for some
N_{1}\geq 1
. We then define inductively

I^{(2)}:=I^{(1)}\cup\{(1,k_{1}+1)\},\,I^{(3)}:=I^{(2)}\cup\{(1,k_{1}+2)\},....,

I^{(N_{1}+1)}:=I^{(N_{1})}\cup\{(1,k_{1}+N_{1})\},

and then

I^{(N_{1}+2)}:=\hat{I}^{(2)}.

By continuing inductively this definition, we obtain a sequence of sets
\bigl(I^{(n)}\bigr)_{n=1}^{\infty}
having the following properties.

Lemma 5.6
.

For every
t<\delta
there exist
\bigl(I^{(n)}\bigr)_{n=1}^{\infty}
a sequence of sets, and
(l_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, a diverging sequence of positive integers, such that for every
n\geq 1
the following hold:

(1)

\displaystyle I^{(n)}=\{(l_{n},j):j_{l_{n}}\leq j\leq h_{n}\}\quad\text{with some}\quad h_{n}\geq k_{l_{n}}.

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{(l_{n},j)\in I^{(n)}}\inf|\Phi^{\prime}_{l_{n},j}|^{t}\geq 2\;
.

(3)

\#I^{(n+1)}\leq\#I^{(n)}+1
.

The last property immediately implies that the system has subexponential growth, i.e. it satisfies (
4.1
), meaning that

(5.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\#I^{(n)}=0\,.

whereas the second property yields positivity of the lower pressure for the parameter
t
:

(5.6)

\underline{\text{{\rm P}}}(t)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{(i_{1},...,i_{n})\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}\|(\Phi_{(l_{1},i_{1})}\circ...\circ\Phi_{(l_{n},i_{n})})^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{t}\geq\log 2>0.

5.4.
Conclusion of the Proof of Theorem
1.3

Theorem
1.3
results from Proposition
3.4
and the next result.

Proposition 5.7
.

If
\mathcal{J}
is the limit set of the non-autonomous conformal IFS
\Phi^{(n)}=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I^{(n)}}
,
n\geq 1
, constructed in the previous subsection, then

u(\mathcal{J})\subset\mathcal{I}(f)

and

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{J})\geq\delta.

Proof.

The first assertion of this proposition follows immediately from the construction of the non-autonomous IFS
\Phi^{(n)}=\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I^{(n)}}
done in the previous subsection.

The second assertion follows immediately from Lemma
5.4
, Lemma
5.5
, Formula (
5.5
), Formula (
5.6
), and Bowen’s Formula, i.e. Theorem
4.1
.
∎

6.
General functions of class
\mathcal{B}

As mentioned in the Introduction, McMullen and various other authors who extended his work
[
15
]
estimated the Hausdorff dimension
of the Julia set by considering the escaping set and, more precisely, the set of points whose orbit escapes through a tract of logarithmic singularity.
Here we consider, for general functions of class
\mathcal{B}
, orbits that escape to infinity “through poles”.

To make this more precise, let
f\in\mathcal{B}
be an arbitrary transcendental meromorphic function and let again
R_{0}>0
be such that (
2.2
) holds
so that in particular
S(f)\subset{\mathbb{D}}(0,R_{0}/2)
. For
R\geq R_{0}
let again
U_{j}(R)
be the connected component of
f^{-1}({\mathbb{D}}_{R}^{*})
that contains a pole
a_{j}
. Consider then the set

\quad\mathcal{U}_{M,R}:=\bigcup_{j\,:\,m_{j}\leq M}U_{j}(R)

where, as always,
m_{j}
is the multiplicity of
a_{j}
and where
M\geq 1
. Consider now the subsets

\mathcal{I}(f,\mathcal{U}_{M,R})

of the escaping set of
f
consisting of all those points
z\in\mathcal{I}(f)
for which there exists
N\geq 1
such that

f^{n}(z)\in\mathcal{U}_{M,R}

for all
n\geq N
. Clearly, these subsets of the escaping set are only
relevant if
f
has infinitely many poles. If
f
is entire or has only finitely many poles then the sets
\mathcal{I}(f,\mathcal{U}_{M,R})
are empty.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}
be a transcendental meromorphic function and assume that there exists
M_{f}
such that

f
has infinitely many poles
a_{j}
,
j\geq 1
, with multiplicities
m_{j}\leq M_{f}
. Then, for every
M\geq M_{f}
,
there exists
R_{M}>R_{0}
such that for all
R\geq R_{M}

\text{{\rm HD}}\left(\mathcal{I}(f,\mathcal{U}_{M,R})\right)=\delta_{M},

where
\delta_{M}
is the critical exponent of the series

\sum_{j\;s.t.\;m_{j}\leq M}\left(\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+1/{\mu}}}\right)^{t}

and where
\mu
is the upper limit of all the numbers
m_{j}
for which
m_{j}\leq M
.

Proof.

The proofs of the Propositions
3.4
and
5.7
are both
local. Indeed, let
M\geq M_{f}
and consider the restriction of
f
to the set
\mathcal{U}_{M,R_{0}}
.
All inverse branches
f_{j}^{-1}:{\mathbb{D}}_{R_{0}}^{*}\to U_{j}(R_{0})
,
m_{j}\leq M
, are well defined
and we can proceed exactly as in these proofs using this family of inverse branches in place of the full family of inverse branches considered in the proofs of the Propositions
3.4
and
5.7
.
∎

Theorem
6.1
contains Theorem
1.3
as its special case. Also, it is clear that it gives the following estimate valid for all functions in class
\mathcal{B}
having infinitely many poles.
The critical exponents
\delta_{M}
are increasing with
M
so that
\sup_{M}\delta_{M}=\lim_{M\to\infty}\delta_{M}
. Denote this limit by
\delta_{\infty}
.

Corollary 6.2
.

If
f\in\mathcal{B}
is a transcendental meromorphic function having infinitely many poles, then

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))\geq\delta_{\infty}\,.

Let us recall that the results of Barański (
[
3
]
) and Schubert (
[
22
]
) show that

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=2
if
f\in\mathcal{B}
and if
\infty
is asymptotic value. Therefore, Corollary
6.2

is meaningful for the functions of
\mathcal{B}
for which
\infty
is not asymptotic value.

We finally provide a class of examples that illustrate
Theorem
6.1
. Let

\Gamma:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}

be the Euler Gamma function and, for every
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
, let
\Gamma_{a}:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}
be the translated version of it defined by

\Gamma_{a}(z)=\Gamma(z+a)\quad\text{, $\;z\in{\mathbb{C}}$. }

It is well known and easy to check that
\Gamma
and thus all the functions
\Gamma_{a}
,
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
, are in class
\mathcal{B}

and have precisely one logarithmic tract over infinity. Moreover, the poles of a function
\Gamma_{a}
are

a_{j}=-j-a
, all these poles are simple and the residue of
\Gamma_{a}
at
a_{j}
is

b_{j}=(-1)^{j}/j!\;\;
,
\;\;j\geq 1
.

Since
\Gamma_{a}
has a logarithmic tract over infinity, again
[
3
]
and
[
22
]
imply that

(6.1)

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(\Gamma_{a}))=2.

In fact, it follows from these papers that the Hausdorff dimension of the
subset of the escaping set consisting of all points
z\in\mathcal{I}(\Gamma_{a})

whose orbits are eventually
contained in the tract is equal to
2
.

Theorem
6.1
allows to determine the dimension of the points that escape to infinity through the poles.
We recall that the later are simple poles so that
M_{\Gamma_{a}}=1
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For all
R
large enough
\text{{\rm HD}}\big(\mathcal{I}(\Gamma_{a},\mathcal{U}_{1,R})=0
.

Proof.

Let
R>0
be such that Theorem
6.1
applies. Then
\text{{\rm HD}}\big(\mathcal{I}(\Gamma_{a},\mathcal{U}_{1,R})
equals the critical
exponent
\delta_{1}
of the series

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{|b_{j}|}{|a_{j}|^{1+1/1}}\right)^{t}\asymp\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{j!j^{2}}\right)^{t}

which clearly is
\delta_{1}=0
.
∎

7.
Examples

Using our main result, i.e. Theorem
1.3
, it is particularly easy to determine the value of the Hausdorff dimension of the escaping set
as soon as one has a sufficiently good information about the poles of the function. We illustrate this here by providing some simple examples. The first class of examples consists in functions with
finite order, in fact order two (but this can be easily generalized to any finite order), such that
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))
realizes any value in
[0,2)
. Then we show, by explicit construction, that
functions of infinite order fulfilling the hypotheses of Theorem
1.3
can have
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))<2
and even
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=0
.

Theorem
1.5
follows from the Theorems
7.1
and
7.6
proven below.

7.1.
Examples of Finite Order

We consider functions of the form

f(z)=\sum_{|j|,|k|\geq N}\left(\frac{b_{j,k}}{z-a_{j,k}}\right)^{M}

with poles
a_{j,k}=j+ik
,
j,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, all of same multiplicity
M\geq 1
. Remember that the neighborhood
U_{j,k}
of a pole
a_{j,k}
is approximately a disk
of size
|b_{j,k}|
; see Lemma
3.2
. Hence, these numbers can not be too large and we take

0<b_{j,k}\leq|a_{j,k}|^{-\alpha}\quad\text{for some fixed $\alpha>2/M$}.

Since
\alpha M>2
, we can fix the integer
N\geq 1
sufficiently large so that

(7.1)

\sum_{|j|,|k|\geq N}|a_{j,k}|^{-\alpha M}\leq\frac{1}{2^{M+1}}.

Denote

{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}:=\{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:|j|,|k|\geq N\}

Theorem 7.1
.

Under these assumptions,
f
is a well defined meromorphic function in class
\mathcal{B}
and of order two for which
\infty
is not an asymptotic value and all of its poles have multiplicity
M
. furthermore:

(1)

If
b_{j,k}=|a_{j,k}|^{-\alpha}
for all
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=\frac{2}{\alpha+1+\frac{1}{M}}\in\left(0,\frac{2}{1+\frac{3}{M}}\right).

(2)

If
b_{j,k}=\exp(-|a_{j,k}|)
for all
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
, then
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=0
.

Proof.

Fix
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
distinct from all the poles. For all those
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
for which
|a_{j,k}|\geq 2|z|
(keep in mind that the set of
j,k
failing to satisfy this inequality is finite), we have

\left|\frac{b_{j,k}}{z-a_{j,k}}\right|^{M}\leq\left|\frac{|a_{j,k}|^{-\alpha}}{|a_{j,k}|-|z|}\right|^{M}\leq 2^{M}|a_{j,k}|^{-(1+\alpha)M}.

So, since
\alpha M\geq 2
, the series defining
f
converges absolutely and uniformly on some neighborhood of each such point
z
and, moreover,
f:{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
is a well defined meromorphic function with poles being equal to the numbers
a_{j,k}
,
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
. Furthermore,
f

is of order two since the series (
2.1
) converges if and only if
t>2
.

Consider the disjoint disks
{\mathbb{D}}(a_{j,k},b_{j,k})
,
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
, and let

\mathcal{U}:=\displaystyle\bigcap_{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}}{\mathbb{D}}^{c}(a_{j,k},b_{j,k}).

Claim 7.2
.

The set
{\mathcal{C}_{f}}
of critical points of
f
satisfies

{\mathcal{C}_{f}}\subset\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

Indeed, since

f^{\prime}(z)=-M\sum_{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}}\frac{b_{j,k}^{M}}{(z-a_{j,k})^{M+1}}

we have for all
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
, and all
\displaystyle z\in{\mathbb{D}}(a_{j,k},b_{j,k})\setminus\{a_{j,k}\}
that

\begin{array}[]{rl}|f^{\prime}(z)|&\geq M\left(\frac{b_{j,k}^{M}}{|z-a_{j,k}|^{M+1}}-\sum_{(u,v)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}\setminus\{(j,k)\}}\frac{b_{u,v}^{M}}{|z-a_{u,v}|^{M+1}}\right)\\
&\geq M\left(b_{j,k}^{-1}-\sum_{(u,v)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}\setminus\{(j,k)\}}2^{M+1}b_{u,v}^{M}\right)\end{array}

since
|z-a_{u,v}|\geq 1/2
if
(u,v)\neq(j,k)
. Thus,

|f^{\prime}(z)|\geq M\Big(|a_{j,k}|^{\alpha}-2^{M+1}\sum_{(u,v)\neq(j,k)}|a_{u,v}|^{-\alpha M}\Big)\geq M\left((\sqrt{2}N)^{\alpha}-1\right)>0.

∎

Claim 7.3
.

|f(z)|\leq 2
for every
z\in\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathcal{U}
. If there exists
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
such that
|z-a_{j,k}|\leq 1/2
, then
|z-a_{u,v}|\geq 1/2
for all
(u,v)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}\setminus\{(j,k)\}
and

\begin{array}[]{rl}|f(z)|&\leq\left(\frac{b_{j,k}}{|z-a_{j,k}|}\right)^{M}+\sum_{(u,v)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}\setminus\{(j,k)\}}\left(\frac{b_{u,v}}{|z-a_{u,v}|}\right)^{M}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{b_{j,k}}{b_{j,k}}\right)^{M}+\sum_{(u,v)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}\setminus\{(j,k)\}}\left(\frac{b_{u,v}}{1/2}\right)^{M}\\
&\leq 1+2^{M}2^{-(M+1)}\leq 2\end{array}

since
b_{u,v}\leq|a_{u,v}|^{-\alpha}
and since we have (
7.1
).

In the other case, i.e. if
|z-a_{j,k}|>1/2
for all
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
, then

|f(z)|\leq\sum_{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}}\left(\frac{b_{j,k}}{1/2}\right)^{M}\leq 2^{M}2^{-(M+1)}\leq 2

also since
b_{j,k}\leq|a_{j,k}|^{-\alpha}
and since we have (
7.1
). Thus the claim holds.
∎

Claim 7.4
.

S(f)\subset\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}(0,2),

and consequently
f\in\mathcal{B}
and
\infty
is not asymptotic value.

Proof.

It directly follows from Claims
7.2
and
7.3
that

f({\mathcal{C}_{f}})\subset\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}(0,2).

Concerning the asymptotic values, if
\gamma\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is a curve tending to infinity whose image under
f
converges to an asymptotic value
b\in\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, then there are arbitrary large values
z\in\gamma
that are in
\mathcal{U}
. Claim
7.3
implies that for such values
z
we have
|f(z)|\leq 2
, consequently
|b|\leq 2
. All of this shows that
S(f)\subset\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}(0,2)
and that
\infty
is not an asymptotic value. We are done.
∎

Thus the function
f
satisfies all hypotheses of our Theorem
1.3
, and thus it suffices to determine the critical exponent
\delta

of (
1.6
) in order to determine
\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))
.

Case (1):

b_{j,k}=|a_{j,k}|^{-\alpha}
for all
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
. Then the series

\sum_{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}}\left(\frac{b_{j,k}}{|a_{j,k}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}\right)^{t}=\sum_{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}}|a_{j,k}|^{-\left(\alpha+1+\frac{1}{M}\right)t}

converges if and only if
t\left(\alpha+1+\frac{1}{M}\right)>2
. This, with the help of Theorem
1.3
, shows that

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=\delta=\frac{2}{\alpha+1+\frac{1}{M}}.

Case (2):

b_{j,k}=\exp(-|a_{j,k}|)
for all
(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}
. Then the series

\sum_{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}}\left(\frac{b_{j,k}}{|a_{j,k}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}\right)^{t}=\sum_{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{2}}\left(\frac{\exp(-|a_{j,k}|)}{|a_{j,k}|^{1+\frac{1}{M}}}\right)^{t}

converges for all
t>0
which, in the same way as in Case 1 shows that

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=\delta=0.

∎

Remark 7.5
.

In both cases, i.e. if
b_{j,k}=|a_{j,k}|^{-\alpha}
or if
b_{j,k}=\exp(-|a_{j,k}|)
, we see that the series (
1.6
) diverges at the critical exponent
t=\delta
.
This in fact gives further information on the dimension
\dim\mathcal{I}_{R}(f)
since it was observed in
[
7
]
that one can then employ

[
14
]
in order to get

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}(f))>\delta

for every
R>0
although, by Theorem
1.3
,
\lim_{R\to\infty}\dim\mathcal{I}_{R}(f)=\delta
.

7.2.
Examples of Infinite Order

There are many ways to modify the preceding example in order to get such functions having infinite order. Here is one way to do it. Let

a_{j}:=\log j\,\text{,}\quad b_{j}:=e^{-j},

and define

(7.2)

f(z):=\sum_{j\geq 8}\frac{b_{j}}{z-a_{j}},\ \ z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Theorem 7.6
.

The function
f
defined above in the formula (
7.2
) is a well defined meromorphic function of class
\mathcal{B}
of infinite order infinity for which
\infty
is not an asymptotic value.
Furthermore, all poles of
f
have multiplicity one and the escaping set of
f
satisfies

\text{{\rm HD}}(\mathcal{I}(f))=0.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}=\frac{e^{-j}}{\log j}
,
j\geq 8
, the same argument, even easier, as in the proof of Theorem
7.1
, shows that the series of (
7.2
) defines a meromophic function from
{\mathbb{C}}
to
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
with poles being equal to the numbers
a_{j}
,
j\geq 8
. Furthermore, since
a_{j}=\log j
,
j\geq 8
, the series (
2.1
) associated with our present function
f
diverges for all
t>0
. This shows that the order
\rho
of
f
is equal to
\infty
.

The other estimates needed to prove Theorem
7.6
are (also) similar and even simpler than in the proof of Theorem
7.1
. Consider again the sets

{\mathbb{D}}(a_{j},b_{j}),\ j\geq 8,\ \ {\rm and}\ \ \mathcal{U}:=\bigcap_{i\geq 8}{\mathbb{D}}^{c}(a_{i},b_{i}).

Let
z\in\mathcal{U}
. If
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\left(a_{j},\frac{1}{3j}\right)
for some
j\geq 8
then, by an elementary calculation,

(7.3)

|z-a_{k}|\geq\frac{1}{3k}\ \ {\rm for\ all}\ \ k\neq j,k\geq 8.

This implies

|f(z)|\leq\frac{b_{j}}{|z-a_{j}|}+\sum_{k\neq j,\,k\geq 8}\frac{e^{-k}}{|z-a_{k}|}\leq 1+3\sum_{k\geq 8}ke^{-k}\leq 2.

If
z\not\in{\mathbb{D}}\left(a_{j},\frac{1}{3j}\right)
for all
j\geq 8
, then

|f(z)|\leq\sum_{j\geq 8}\frac{b_{j}}{|z-a_{j}|}\leq 3\sum_{j\geq 8}je^{-j}\leq 1.

This shows that

|f(z)|\leq 2
for every
z\in\mathcal{U}
.

The next step is to show that
{\mathcal{C}_{f}}\subset\mathcal{U}
. We have

f^{\prime}(z)=-\sum_{j\geq 8}\frac{b_{j}}{(z-a_{j})^{2}}.

Let
z\in\mathcal{U}
. Then
z\in{\mathbb{D}}(a_{j},b_{j})\setminus\{a_{j}\}
for some
j\geq 8
. So, using (
7.3
) we get

|f^{\prime}(z)|\geq\frac{b_{j}}{|z-a_{j}|^{2}}-\!\!\sum_{k\neq j,\,k\geq 8}\frac{b_{k}}{|z-a_{k}|^{2}}\geq\frac{b_{j}}{b_{j}^{2}}-9\sum_{k\geq 8}k^{2}e^{-k}\geq e^{8}-1>0.

Therefore we have again
{\mathcal{C}_{f}}\subset\mathcal{U}
and
f({\mathcal{C}_{f}})\subset\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}(0,2)
.
Arguing exactly as in the proof of Theorem
7.1
, it also follows that all possible asymptotic values of
f
belong to
