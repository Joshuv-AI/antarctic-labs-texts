Design optimization in unilateral contact using pressure constraints and Bayesian optimization
Jingyi Wang, Jerome Solberg, Mike A. Puso, Eric B. Chin, Cosmin G. Petra

Abstract
Design optimization problems, e.g. , shape optimization, that involve deformable bodies in unilateral contact are challenging as they require robust contact solvers, complex optimization methods that are typically gradient-based, and sensitivity derivations. Notably, the problems are nonsmooth, adding significant difficulty to the optimization process. We study design optimization problems in frictionless unilateral contact subject to pressure constraints, using both gradient-based and gradient-free optimization methods, namely Bayesian optimization. The contact simulation problem is solved via the mortar contact and finite element methods. For the gradient-based method, we use the direct differentiation method to compute the sensitivities of the cost and constraint function with respect to the design variables. Then, we use Ipopt to solve the optimization problems. For the gradient-free approach, we use a constrained Bayesian optimization algorithm based on the standard Gaussian Process surrogate model. We present numerical examples that control the contact pressure, inspired by real-life engineering applications, to demonstrate the effectiveness, strengths and shortcomings of both methods. Our results suggest that both optimization methods perform reasonably well for these nonsmooth problems.



1
Introduction

Contacting bodies are ubiquitous in engineering problems, covering,
e.g.
, crash worthiness test, part assembly, metal forming
[
14
]
, etc.
Many of these problems are solved with finite element methods
[
32
]
.
However, combining design optimization and finite element methods to
design
contacting systems rather than
analyze
them received little attention.
This is partially due to the complexity of combining state-of-the-art mortar contact solvers with optimization algorithms and the nonsmoothness that exists in such problems
[
11
]
.

Finite element contact solvers have evolved over the years and many techniques have been developed to solve contact problems in a robust and efficient manner
[
10
,
22
]
. Among them, the combined Uzawa-augmented Lagrangian method (ALM) has proven reliable. Recently, researchers have used the interior-point method, to solve frictionless contact problems
[
28
]
.
The constraint function,
i.e.
, gap function, can be computed using node-on-node, node-on-segment or mortar method.
The mortar segment-on-segment method is preferred because it passes the patch test and eliminates element locking
[
22
,
23
,
35
]
.
In this work, we use the popular interior-point library Ipopt and the mortar method to solve the contact problem.

Previous shape and topology optimization work has applied gradient-based optimization methods to design systems with contact.
In
[
7
]
, the authors applied topology optimization to design nonlinear elastic bodies in contact using
Uzawa-ALM and mortar methods, adjoint sensitivities and Ipopt.
In
[
6
]
, the authors used the ALM method to deal with stress-based constraints and contact boundary conditions. The nonsmoothness of the solution to the contact problems is highlighted as the reason for the design to be highly dependent on initial design and optimization algorithms.
In
[
20
]
, the design problems of sheets in contact are shown to be nonsmooth and solved approximately through a subgradient method.
In
[
2
]
, a stress-based approach is presented for the design of truss-like elastic structures through imposing stress constraints.
Sensitivities and gradient-based methods are used in topology optimization problems in unilateral contact in
[
26
]
.
In
[
19
]
, the authors study crashworthiness optimization of frame structures using a topology optimization formulation and sensitivities.
Contact pressure distribution in plane stress with friction is optimized in
[
13
]
through the smoothing of a pressure function. While the study is limited to obstacle contact problems, the authors argued for the necessity of compliance constraint in addition to the objective function consisting of the mean and variance of pressure.
In
[
17
]
, the authors solved shape optimization with uniform contact pressure constraints. While the potential contact surface is not allowed to change, the actual contact region is affected by the shape design.
Though nonsmoothness caused by changing contact region is not discussed in the paper, the results show promise for such problems in two dimension with method of moving asymptotes
[
27
]
.
It is worth pointing out that most papers on design optimization in contact use node-on-segment contact or other simplified treatment of contact analysis, and not mortar contact formulation, rendering the methods less accurate.
Moreover, design objective or constraints that are explicit in pressure are rare.

Despite the success and advancement of these important works, there remain a few challenges that hinder the further development of a robust and efficient gradient-based optimizer.
The first one is the problem-dependent sensitivity computation based on complex contact solvers. Not only are derivation and implementation of sensitivities time-consuming, the necessary information to compute them is often difficult to obtain. For instance, many design variables change the shape and geometry of the bodies and thus the discretized mesh. The sensitivities of the mesh with respect to the design variables might be unavailable, particularly if third-party mesh tools are employed.
Second, the design optimization problem in contact can be nonsmooth, especially when considering explicit pressure objective and constraints
[
11
]
, leaving researchers with little convergence guarantee even with the most successful optimizers such as Ipopt or MMA
[
36
]
.
Here, a smooth function means that it is continuously differentiable with Lipschitz first-order derivatives
[
18
]
.
During the optimization, the surface region in contact constantly changes with updated design variables, which could render the objective and constraint functions non-differentiable.

To address the first challenge, we propose using the Bayesian optimization method for design problems in contact.
Bayesian optimization is a gradient-free method that treats the objective and constraints as ‘black-box’ functions that can be approximated with surrogate models,
e.g.
, Gaussian Process (GP)
[
8
]
.
Bayesian optimization has been widely applied to structural design
[
16
]
, process optimization of additive manufacturing
[
30
]
, inertial confinement fusion design
[
29
]
, machine learning
[
1
]
, etc.
In
[
12
]
, a gradient-free subproblem approximation method is used for the optimization of an insole for lower plantar fascia stress.
To build the surrogate model, at each iteration, a sample point is collected by running the finite element simulation, where no sensitivity computation is necessary.
Yet Bayesian optimization, particularly in the constrained case, has its own shortcomings.
Without sensitivities, the algorithm is less likely to produce a local minimum that satisfies first-order optimality conditions
[
18
]
. In addition, for large scale problems, while the sampling can be easily parallelized, the surrogate model,
e.g.
, Gaussian Processes (GPs) can become computationally expensive to train with increasing number of samples.

To study the second challenge, we derive, design and implement sensitivities for problems with pressure objective and constraints, and solve them using the well-established interior-point solver Ipopt.
The sensitivities are computed based on the differentiation of the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) systems of the contact solutions.
While no new algorithm is proposed to provide convergence theory, we demonstrate the validity of the gradient-based approach through numerical examples with changing contact region and pressure constraints that are inspired by real-life applications.
Further, we compare both gradient-based and gradient-free approaches on our numerical examples. Our main contributions are

1.

derivation and implementation of a gradient-based method using sensitivities for design optimization in unilateral contact with pressure constraints

2.

Design and implementation of constrained Bayesian optimization on design optimization in unilateral contact

3.

Novel design and insights produced by our approach in numerical examples.

The paper is organized as follows. In section
2
, we briefly discuss the finite element contact mechanics models in the optimization form. In section
3
, we formulate the design optimization problem and its sensitivities based on the finite element model. We propose the constrained Bayesian optimization method in section
4
. The numerical examples are presented in section
5
. Finally, the conclusion is given in section
6
.

2
Contact problem in optimization formulation

By way of introduction, consider a solid body
B
in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{n}
and let
{\bm{X}}
denote the location of its material points in the undeformed/reference configuration
\Omega
with boundary
\partial\Omega
.
The body is subjected to an external load field
f_{ext}
and as such
the same material points are displaced to locations in the deformed configuration
\omega
such that
{\bm{x}}={\bm{X}}+{\bm{u}}({\bm{X}})
, where
{\bm{u}}:\Omega\to\mathbb{E}^{3}
. The deformation gradient tensor
{\bm{F}}
is defined as
{\bm{F}}=\mathbf{1}+\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial{\bm{X}}}
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the mixed identity tensor.
We assume
B
undergoes infinitesimal deformations and is comprised of an elastic material with internal energy function
\phi_{e}({\bm{F}},{\bm{x}})
. We also assume the external force
f_{ext}
is conservative such that it is derivable from an external energy function
\phi_{ext}({\bm{u}},{\bm{X}})
.

Now consider two distinct bodies
\Omega^{(1)}
and
\Omega^{(2)}
with material points
{\bm{X}}^{(i)},i=1,2
that are potentially in contact. The internal and external energies for
\Omega^{(i)}
are
\phi^{(i)}_{e}({\bm{F}}^{(i)},{\bm{X}}^{(i)})
and
\phi_{ext}^{(i)}({\bm{u}}^{(i)},{\bm{X}}^{(i)})
.

The boundary
\partial\Omega^{(i)}
of
\Omega^{(i)}
is decomposed into the complementary surfaces
\Gamma_{u}^{i},\Gamma_{t}^{i}
and
\Gamma_{c}^{i}
of Figure
1
.
Displacement is prescribed over
\Gamma_{u}^{(i)}
and
{\bm{u}}^{(i)}({\bm{X}}^{(i)})=\bar{{\bm{u}}}^{(i)}({\bm{X}}^{(i)})
.
The surface traction is prescribed over
\Gamma_{t}^{(i)}
:
{\bm{t}}^{(i)}({\bm{X}}^{(i)})=\bar{{\bm{t}}}^{(i)}({\bm{X}}^{(i)})
. The surface
\Gamma_{c}^{(i)}
denotes regions that will potentially contact with the other body.
In the deformed configurations
\omega^{(i)}
with boundary
\partial\omega^{(i)}
, these three surfaces occupy regions

\partial\omega^{(i)}
,
\gamma_{u}^{(i)}
,
\gamma_{t}^{(i)}
and
\gamma_{c}^{(i)}
, respectively.

Figure 1
:
Reference and deformed configurations of bodies in potential contact.

The unilateral contact constraint is based on the principle of impenetrability of matter,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\omega^{(1)}\cap\omega^{(2)}=\emptyset.

(1)

We impose (
1
) via the gap function
g:\gamma_{c}^{(2)}\to\mathbb{R}
and constraint

\displaystyle g({\bm{x}}^{(2)})\geq 0,\ \forall{\bm{x}}^{(2)}\in\gamma_{c}^{(2)}.

(2)

To define
g
, we assume that each location
{\bm{x}}^{(2)}\in\gamma_{c}^{(2)}
has a unique outward unit normal
{\bm{n}}^{(2)}({\bm{x}}^{(2)})
.
The projection along
{\bm{n}}^{(2)}({\bm{x}}^{(2)})
is used to identify a potential contact point
{\bm{x}}^{(1)}\in\gamma_{c}^{(1)}
.
The signed distance between these points define the gap,
i.e.
,
g={\bm{n}}^{(2)}\cdot({\bm{x}}^{(1)}-{\bm{x}}^{(2)})

[
24
,
22
]
.
The constraint (
2
) is one-sided and can bias the analysis
[
25
]
, but in general is effective and widely adopted.

To simplify the ensuing presentation, we define
{\bm{u}}=({\bm{u}}^{(1)},{\bm{u}}^{(2)})
,
{\bm{X}}=({\bm{X}}^{(1)},{\bm{X}}^{(2)})
and
{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}^{(1)},{\bm{x}}^{(2)})
.
As such, the frictionless contact for elastic bodies can be posed as an optimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{u}}\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle f({\bm{F}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{X}})

(3)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle g({\bm{x}})\geq 0,\ \forall{\bm{x}}\in\gamma_{c}^{(2)},

where
f
is the total energy,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle f({\bm{F}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{X}})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega^{(i)}}\phi^{(i)}_{e}({\bm{F}}^{(i)},{\bm{X}}^{(i)})dv+\int_{\Omega^{(i)}}\phi_{ext}^{(i)}({\bm{u}}^{(i)},{\bm{X}}^{(i)}).

(4)

We apply standard Galerkin finite element discretization to where in the displacement field is discretized with piecewise smooth interpolation functions
\Phi^{(i)}_{j}:\Omega^{(i)}\to\Omega
, local coordinates
{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, nodal displacement vectors
{\bm{u}}_{h,j}^{(i)}
and
{\bm{u}}_{h,j}

such that

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}({\bm{\xi}},{\bm{u}}_{h,j})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Phi_{j}({\bm{\xi}}){\bm{u}}_{h,j},

(5)

where
n
is the number of nodes. The subscript
h
is used to denote nodal vectors and other discretized quantities,
e.g.
,
\gamma_{h}^{(i)},i=1,2
.
The discretized energy objective for an elastic material is

\displaystyle f_{h}({{\bm{u}}}_{h})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2}{\bm{u}}_{h}^{(i),T}K^{(i)}{\bm{u}}_{h}^{(i)}-{\bm{f}}_{ext}^{(i),T}{\bm{u}}_{h}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{u}}_{h}^{T}K{\bm{u}}_{h}-{\bm{f}}_{ext}^{T}{\bm{u}}_{h}.

(6)

where
K
is the stiffness matrix and an integrated quantity, and
{\bm{f}}_{ext}
is the external force vector.

There are multiple ways to discretize and compute the gap function.
We choose the mortar method
which integrates the pointwise gap function
g
on
\gamma_{c,h}^{(2)}
.
Let
S
be the index set of all nodes on the discretized contact boundary
\gamma_{c,h}^{(2)}
, the mortar gap function is defined as a vector function
{\bm{g}}_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},m=|S|
, with components

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}_{h,j}({\bm{u}}_{h})=\int_{\gamma_{c,h}^{(2)}}\Phi_{j}({\bm{\xi}})g({\bm{\xi}},{\bm{u}}_{h})d{\bm{\xi}},

(7)

for all
j\in S
. Note that the local coordinates reduce to two dimensions on the surface.
Therefore, the discretized optimization problem of (
3
) with mortar contact formulation is

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{u}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle f_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h})

(8)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{g}}_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h})\geq\mathbf{0}.

The KKT conditions of (
8
) is that there exist Lagrange multipliers
{\bm{\lambda}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle\nabla f_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h})-{\bm{\lambda}}_{h}\nabla{\bm{g}}_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h})=0

(9)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{g}}_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h})\geq 0,\ {\bm{\lambda}}_{h}\geq 0,\ \lambda_{h,i}g_{h,i}=0,\ i=1,...,m.

Equations (
9
) comprise the standard contact finite element problem
[
5
]
and
is the basis for further deriving sensitivities needed for gradient-based design optimization.
The Lagrange multiplier
{\bm{\lambda}}_{h}
is often interpreted as the pressure on the contact surface
[
32
]
.

3
Design optimization and sensitivities

In this section, we consider design optimization problems that are subjected pressure constraints.
Let
{\bm{\rho}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be the design variable. The mathematical form of our target problems is

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\rho}}\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle a({\bm{\rho}},\bar{{\bm{u}}},\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}})

(10)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{c}}({\bm{\rho}},\bar{{\bm{u}}},\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}})\geq\mathbf{0},

where
a({\bm{\rho}},\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
and
{\bm{c}}({\bm{\rho}},\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
are the objective and constraint functions, respectively.
The displacement
\bar{{\bm{u}}}
and contact pressure
\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}
are obtained from the solutions to a contact mechanics problem (
3
).

Applying the same finite element discretization,
the parameterized and discretized forms of (
10
) can be written as

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\rho}}\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle a_{h}({\bm{\rho}},\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h})

(11)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{c}}_{h}({\bm{\rho}},\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h})\geq\mathbf{0},

where given
{\bm{\rho}}
,

\displaystyle\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}=

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{u}}_{h}\end{subarray}}{\text{argmin}}\

\displaystyle f_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})

(12)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{g}}_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})\geq\mathbf{0},

and
\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with
\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}
.
In many engineering applications such as shape optimization,
{\bm{X}}_{h}
can be a function of
{\bm{\rho}}
, which results in (
12
) being dependent on
{\bm{X}}_{h}
implicitly.

In order to use gradient-based optimization methods to solve (
11
), we need to compute the state sensitivities against
{\bm{\rho}}
given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}},\ \frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}.

(13)

For a low-dimensional design space where the dimension
p
is small, a primal method suffices, while adjoint methods can be used when
p
is large.
Denote the derivative of
f_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
{\bm{g}}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
with respect to the
i
th variable by
\partial_{i}f_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\partial_{i}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
, respectively, where
i=1,2
.
The solutions
\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}
satisfy the parameterized KKT conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{1}f_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})-\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}\partial_{1}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})=\mathbf{0},

(14)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})\geq\mathbf{0},\ \bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}\geq\mathbf{0},\ \bar{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{h}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})=\mathbf{0},

where
\bar{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{h}
is a diagonal matrix whose values are the vector
\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}
.

Next, denoting the second-order derivatives of a function by
\partial^{2}_{i,j},i,j=1,2
and differentiating the optimality conditions (
14
) with
{\bm{\rho}}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{1,1}^{2}f_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}+\partial_{1,2}^{2}f_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})-[\partial_{1}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})]^{T}\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}-

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}^{T}[\partial_{1,1}^{2}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})]^{T}\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}-[\partial_{1,2}^{2}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})]^{T}\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})+\bar{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{h}\partial_{1}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}+\bar{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{h}\partial_{2}{\bm{g}}_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle.

Solving the linear system of (
15
) gives us (
13
).
We can then solve (
10
) with gradient-based optimization methods by treating
\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}
and
\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}
as implicit functions of
{\bm{\rho}}
. The gradients and Jacobian of the objective and constraint functions with respect to
{\bm{\rho}}
are computed with

\displaystyle\frac{da_{h}}{d{\bm{\rho}}}=\frac{\partial a_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}+\frac{\partial a_{h}}{\partial\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}}\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}+\frac{\partial a_{h}}{\partial\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}}\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}},

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{c}}_{h}}{d{\bm{\rho}}}=\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}+\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{h}}{\partial\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}}\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}+\frac{\partial{\bm{c}}_{h}}{\partial\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}}\frac{\partial\bar{{\bm{\lambda}}}_{h}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}.

The second-order derivatives such as
\partial_{1,2}^{2}f_{h}(\bar{{\bm{u}}}_{h},{\bm{\rho}})
are arrived at directly through the chain rule.
In many cases,
{\bm{\rho}}
could affect
{\bm{X}}_{h}
and the calculation of the second-order derivatives of the gap function is obtained again through chain rule.

To solve the contact problem (
12
), we employ the interior-point solver Ipopt and HiOp
[
33
,
21
]
, also referred to as the forward solver, with the full analytical Hessian.
To solve the design optimization problem (
11
) using gradient-based optimization methods, the sensitivities are computed by solving the linear system (
15
) and the derivatives in (
16
) are calculated.
For a robust and efficient outcome, we use Ipopt with its Hessian approximation option. The gradient-based optimization is implemented in MATLAB.

4
Constrained Bayesian optimization

For the gradient-free optimization approach, we design and apply constrained Bayesian optimization with Gaussian Process (GP).
Bayesian optimization is an iterative black-box optimization method that does not require the analytic forms of the objective functions or their derivatives. At each iteration, GP surrogate models replace both the objective and constraint functions and an acquisition function is used to select new sample points. Applied to design optimization, each sample point requires a solution to the parameterized contact problem (
12
). The optimization variables are typically bounded by a closed and bounded set
{\bm{\rho}}\in C
.

A Gaussian process surrogate model assumes a multivariate joint Gaussian distribution between the variables
{\bm{\rho}}
and an output function
a_{h}({\bm{\rho}})
, written as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}a_{h}({\bm{\rho}}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
a_{h}({\bm{\rho}}_{T})\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}m({\bm{\rho}}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
m({\bm{\rho}}_{T})\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}k({\bm{\rho}}_{1},{\bm{\rho}}_{1})\dots k({\bm{\rho}}_{1},{\bm{\rho}}_{T})\\
\vdots\\
k({\bm{\rho}}_{T},{\bm{\rho}}_{1})\dots k({\bm{\rho}}_{T},{\bm{\rho}}_{T})\end{bmatrix}\ \right).

(17)

Here,
\mathcal{N}
is the normal distribution with
T
samples. The design variable samples are denoted as
{\bm{\rho}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\rho}}_{T}
.
The function
m:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is a user-chosen mean function for the distribution. The function
k:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the covariance function.
The posterior Gaussian probability distribution at a new sample point
{\bm{\rho}}
can be inferred using Bayes’ rule:

\displaystyle a_{h}({\bm{\rho}})|a_{h}({\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu({\bm{\rho}}),\sigma^{2}({\bm{\rho}}))

(18)

\displaystyle\mu({\bm{\rho}})\ =\ k({\bm{\rho}},{\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})k({\bm{\rho}}_{1:T},{\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})^{-1}\left(a_{h}({\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})-m({\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})\right)+m({\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}({\bm{\rho}})\ =\ k({\bm{\rho}},{\bm{\rho}})-k({\bm{\rho}},{\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})k({\bm{\rho}}_{1:T},{\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})^{-1}\sigma({\bm{\rho}}_{1:T},{\bm{\rho}})\ ,

where the vector
{\bm{\rho}}_{1:T}
is the notation for
[{\bm{\rho}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\rho}}_{T}]
and

\displaystyle k({\bm{\rho}}_{1:T},{\bm{\rho}}_{1:T})=[k({\bm{\rho}}_{1}),\dots,k({\bm{\rho}}_{T});\dots;k({\bm{\rho}}_{T},{\bm{\rho}}_{1}),\dots,k({\bm{\rho}}_{T},{\bm{\rho}}_{T})].

(19)

The functions
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
are the posterior mean and variance, respectively.
It is common to set the mean function
m
to constant
0

[
8
]
.
The covariance functions, or kernels, of the GP have significant impact on the accuracy of the surrogate model.
A widely adopted kernel, the Squared Exponential Covariance Function, also
known as the power exponential kernel, is defined as

\displaystyle k({\bm{\rho}},{\bm{\rho}}^{\prime};\theta)\ =\ \text{exp}\left(-\frac{\left\|{\bm{\rho}}-{\bm{\rho}}^{\prime}\right\|^{2}}{\theta^{2}}\right)\ ,

(20)

where
\theta
denotes the hyperparameters of the kernel. The hyperparameters of the GP surrogate model
often are optimized during the training or fitting step by maximizing the
log-marginal-likelihood with an optimization algorithm.

The choice of the acquisition function is critical to an efficient and effective Bayesian optimization algorithm.
If the acquisition function emphasizes on exploration, then typically sample points are chosen to reduce variance of the model.
Otherwise, sample points are chosen to improve objective function value.
One popular acquisition function is the expected improvement (EI)
[
1
]
, whose form can be written as

\displaystyle EI({\bm{\rho}})\ =\ \begin{cases}(\mu({\bm{\rho}})-a_{h}({\bm{\rho}}^{+})-\xi)\Phi(Z)+\sigma(x)\phi(Z),&\text{if}\ \sigma({\bm{\rho}})>0,\\
0,&\text{if}\ \sigma({\bm{\rho}})=0,\end{cases}

(21)

where

\displaystyle Z\ =\ \begin{cases}\frac{\mu({\bm{\rho}})-a_{h}({\bm{\rho}}^{+})-\xi}{\sigma({\bm{\rho}})},&\text{if}\ \sigma({\bm{\rho}})>0,\\
0,&\text{if}\ \sigma({\bm{\rho}})=0\ .\end{cases}

(22)

In the unconstrained setting, the variable
{\bm{\rho}}^{+}
in (
21
) is defined as

\displaystyle{\bm{\rho}}^{+}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\rho}}_{i}\in{\bm{\rho}}_{1:T}\end{subarray}}{\text{argmax}}\ a_{h}({\bm{\rho}}_{i})

(23)

of existing
T
samples, where
C
is the bounds on
{\bm{\rho}}
.
The trade-off parameter
\xi\geq 0
affects the trade-off between global search and local optimization
[
15
]
.
The functions
\phi
and
\Phi
are the probability density function (PDF) and cumulative
distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution, respectively.
The next sample point is chosen by maximizing the acquisition function.
At the
k
th iteration, this leads to

\displaystyle{\bm{\rho}}_{k}=

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\rho}}\in C\end{subarray}}{\text{argmax}}\ EI_{k}({\bm{\rho}}),

(24)

where
EI_{k}
is the
EI
function using samples accumulated up to the
k
th iteration.
In order to solve (
24
), optimization algorithms including L-BFGS or random
search can be used.

For constrained Bayesian optimization, the choices of acquisition functions are limited.
Fortunately,
EI
can be extended to constrained expected improvement
EI_{C}
, by incorporating feasibility penalties to the improvement function
[
9
]
.
The
{\bm{\rho}}^{+}
in (
23

T

\displaystyle{\bm{\rho}}^{+}=

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\rho}}_{i}\in{\bm{\rho}}_{1:T}\\
{\bm{c}}_{h}({\bm{\rho}}_{i})\geq 0\end{subarray}}{\text{argmax}}\ a_{h}({\bm{\rho}}_{i}).

(25)

The expected constrained improvement acquisition function is

\displaystyle EI_{C}({\bm{\rho}})=PF({\bm{\rho}})EI({\bm{\rho}}),

(26)

where
PF({\bm{\rho}})=Pr[c_{h}({\bm{\rho}})\geq 0]
is the probability of the constraints being satisfied.
For multiple inequality constraints, each constraint function is modeled as an independent GP surrogate model and is often simplified to be conditionally independent given
{\bm{\rho}}
.
That is, the probability of a feasible
{\bm{\rho}}
for constraints
{\bm{c}}_{h}({\bm{\rho}})\geq 0
satisfies

\displaystyle Pr({\bm{c}}_{h}({\bm{\rho}})\geq 0)=\prod_{i=1}^{q}Pr({\bm{c}}_{h,i}({\bm{\rho}})\geq 0).

(27)

The constrained Bayesian optimization algorithm is given in Algorithm

.

5
Numerical examples

To demonstrate and compare the effectiveness of the gradient-based and gradient-free optimization methods,
we present two numerical examples that are inspired by real-life engineering problems.
These model problems can be easily extended to more complicated and larger-scale problems using the same optimization method we presented in this paper.

Wedge problem

5.2
Marman clamp problem

The second example considers the design of a Marman clamp
[
3
]
. These devices are commonly used to connect two cylindrical sections together, see Figures
10
and
11
. In a general Marman clamp, the cylinders terminate in flanges which meet at a plane. The two flanges interface with a retainer, which is forced against the flanges by the action of a clamp. The retainers are commonly manufactured in segments to allow for inwards displacement without generating significant hoop stresses. The shape of the retainer and the flanges forces the flanges together.
In
[
34
]
, the authors optimized clamp band joint using the so-called constrained sequential approximate optimization method, which bears some similarity to the constrained Bayesian optimization method. In
[
4
]
, uncertainty analysis for the clamp band joints is incorporated into a mathematical model.

In the manifestation considered here, the clamp is in the form of a band which is forced against the retainer by designing the components such that there is an interference fit between them. The hoop stress generated in the band is reacted by a contact pressure between the band and the retainer, which is further reacted by contact pressure between the retainer and the flanges, and ultimately the two flanges are forced together, inducing contact pressure between them.

Figure 10
:
View looking down the cylindrical axis of a generic Marman clamp. The flange is part of the cylindrical object that is being clamped together. The gap between the segments of the retainer ensures that hoop stresses in the retainer are minimized. The band is either stretched over the retainer such that the induced hoop stresses in the band induce compressive force on the retainer, or the compression may be induced by splitting the band at some point and pulling the pieces together using a screw mechanism.

The cylindrical structures can be treated as axisymmetric so that only a small portion of the clamp is simulated and the angular coordinates (
\theta
) are fixed in the cylindrical coordinate system. Given a
\theta
, the bodies in the radial and longitudinal plane,
x-y
plane, is shown in Figure

Figure 11

The retainer is forced against the flanges by the band, causing the flanges to be clamped against each other. Right: the contact surfaces and boundary conditions on an example design. The pressure involved in the constraints are marked.

Similarly, only the top flange (referring to Figure

Three contact planes are defined – contact between the band and the retainer, between the retainer and the top flange, and between the top flange and the vertical symmetry plane, represented by an artificial box which is held rigid. The vertical displacement at the vertical symmetry plane of the retainer and the band is held fixed in accordance with typical symmetry boundary conditions.
As mentioned earlier, hoop stress in the band is generated through an initial interference fit, represented as an initial overlap in the meshes.

Both the flange and the band are given isotropic linear elastic materials with the elastic modulus at

To ensure robustness of the design against separation of the flange, the objective is chosen to be minimization of the compliance, that is maximizing the stiffness of the overall system.
Meanwhile, the maximum contact pressure between the retainer and the flange is constrained by user-defined upper and lower bounds.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we solved design optimization problems in unilateral contact under pressure constraints, using both gradient-based optimization approach and a gradient-free constrained Bayesian optimization algorithm.
The contact finite element simulation uses the mortar gap function formulation and interior-point forward solver.
Through smoothing of nodal pressure and careful design of the bound constraints, we successfully find optimal solutions using the gradient-based method and sensitivities, even though the problems are considered nonsmooth.
The Bayesian optimization approach produced relatively good results and could be suitable for problems without reliable sensitivity information. To solve our target problems more efficiently and robustly, we should address the nonsmoothness directly and develop algorithms that might overcome it. We aim to work on this challenge in the future.
