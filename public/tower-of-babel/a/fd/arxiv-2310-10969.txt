On the spectrum of the Hodge Laplacian on sequences
Hannah Santa Cruz Baur, Vladimir Itskov

Abstract
Hodge Laplacians have been previously proposed as a natural tool for understanding higher-order interactions in networks and directed graphs. Here we introduce a Hodge-theoretic approach to spectral theory and dimensionality reduction for probability distributions on sequences and simplicial complexes. We demonstrate that this Hodge theory has desirable properties with respect to natural null-models, where the underlying vertices are independent. We prove that for the case of independent vertices in simplicial complexes, the appropriate Laplacians are multiples of the identity and thus have no meaningful Fourier modes. For the null model of independent vertices in sequences, we prove that the appropriate Hodge Laplacian has an integer spectrum, and describe its eigenspaces. We also prove that the underlying cell complex of sequences has trivial reduced homology. Our results establish a foundation for developing Fourier analyses of probabilistic models, which are common in theoretical neuroscience and machine-learning.

While Hodge Laplacians are well-suited for a spectral theory on binary vectors (or subsets), many scientific fields, such as genomics, theoretical neuroscience and language models study data that come in the form of a collection of sequences. This kind of data can be thought of as a generalization of directed graphs, similar to how simplicial complexes and hypergraphs generalize undirected graphs.
The sequence data can be formalized in terms of a cell complex
\mathcal{X}
, that comprises all possible sequences, and a probability distribution

p:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
that describes the statistical properties of the data. Here, the role of similarity, or correlations is played by the probability; an equivalent formulation can be made in terms of the higher-order correlations.

Similar to the case of binary vectors, the interpretation and machine-learning pipelines for sequential data require tools such as dimensionality reduction and the notion of convolution. This necessitates a Laplacian formalism for sequences. Importantly, such a Laplacian needs to have particularly simple properties in the case of
null models
, where the statistical properties of sequences can be explained by a simple probabilistic model with no other underlying structure.

To this end we developed a Hodge Laplacian formalism associated with probability distributions on sequence complexes. We accompany this construction with an analogous one for the case of simplicial complexes. We consider natural null models of
independent vertices
and prove that for these models the Hodge Laplacians have particularly simple properties.

2
Hodge theory on abstract cell complexes

2.1
Abstract Cell Complexes

Sequence complexes are a special type of abstract cell complexes; thus start with their formal definition.

Definition 2.1
.

An
Abstract Cell Complex
is a quadruple
\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{X},\leq,\dim,\kappa)
that consists of a poset
(\mathcal{X},\leq)
along with two functions, the dimension function
\operatorname{dim}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, and the incidence function
\kappa:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
that satisfy the following conditions:

\displaystyle 1.\hskip 2.84526pt\dim\text{ is a poset morphism;}

\displaystyle 2.\hskip 2.84526pt\text{For all }\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},\hskip 28.45274pt\kappa\left(\xi,\xi^{\prime}\right)\neq 0\quad\implies\quad\xi^{\prime}\leq\xi,\text{ and }\operatorname{dim}(\xi)=\operatorname{dim}\left(\xi^{\prime}\right)+1;

\displaystyle 3.\hskip 2.84526pt\text{For all }\xi,\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{X},\hskip 28.45274pt\sum_{\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\kappa\left(\xi,\xi^{\prime}\right)\kappa\left(\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}\right)=0

Note that we will only consider abstract cell complexes where
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\dim^{-1}(n)
is a finite set for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The most popular example of abstract cell complexes are simplicial complexes.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
V
be a finite set of vertices,
an
Abstract Simplicial Complex
,
K\subseteq 2^{V}
, is a collection of subsets of
V
, that is closed under inclusion, meaning that

\forall\xi\in K,\quad\xi^{\prime}\subseteq\xi\implies\xi^{\prime}\in K.

Here the poset structure is chosen to be the set inclusion
\subseteq
, and the dimension function is
\dim(\xi)=|\xi|-1,

where
|\xi|
is the cardinality of the set
\xi
.
The incidence function is defined using a particular choice of a total order
v_{1}<v_{2}<\dots<v_{m}
on the vertex set
V
. Given a set
\xi=\{u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\}
, where
u_{j}<u_{j+1}
for all
j=0,\dots n-1
, and a subset
\xi^{\prime}\subseteq\xi
such that
\dim(\xi^{\prime})=\dim(\xi)-1
,

\kappa\left(\xi,\xi^{\prime}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(-1)^{j},\quad\text{where }\xi^{\prime}=\xi\setminus\{u_{j}\}.

If
\dim(\xi^{\prime})\neq\dim(\xi)-1
, or
\xi^{\prime}\not\subseteq\xi
, then
\kappa\left(\xi,\xi^{\prime}\right)=0
.

Another example of interest is a collection of sequences. Let
V
be a finite set of vertices, denote by
\mathcal{X}_{n}[V]
the set of all length
(n+1)
sequences with vertices in
V
. Here the same vertex can appear in the sequence more than once, and sequences with different orderings are distinct. We say that a sequence
\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n-k}[V]
is a subsequence of a sequence
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}[V]
, with the notation
\tau\leq\sigma
, if
\tau
can be obtained by removing
k
vertices (in any position) from the sequence
\sigma
. For example,
(a,b,c)\leq(c,a,b,b,c,a)
. We also denote by
\mathcal{X}_{-1}=\{\varnothing\}
the set consisting of the empty sequence in
V
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
V
be a finite set of vertices. A
Sequence Complex
is a collection of sequences

\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{n\geq-1}\mathcal{X}_{n},\quad\text{ where }\mathcal{X}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{n}[V],

which is closed under inclusion, i.e.

\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{X},\,\,\tau\leq\sigma\implies\tau\in\mathcal{X}
.

This is a special case of an abstract cell complex, where the poset structure is the subsequence relation
\leq
, and the dimension is defined as
\dim(\sigma)=n=|\sigma|-1
for
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}[V]
. The incidence function
\kappa
is defined for all
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{X},
with
\tau\leq\sigma
and
\dim(\tau)=\dim(\sigma)-1
, as

\kappa(\sigma,\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i:{\sigma}^{\times_{i}}=\tau}(-1)^{i},

where
{\sigma}^{\times_{i}}
denotes the sequence, obtained by removing the
i
’th vertex from sequence
\sigma
.
If
\dim(\tau)\neq\dim(\sigma)-1
, or
\tau\not\leq\sigma
then
\kappa\left(\sigma,\tau\right)=0
.

In this paper, we will only consider
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\mathcal{X}_{n}[V]
the full collection of sequences over the vertex set
V
. We refer to the corresponding sequence complex as the full sequence complex, even though all the properties and results, except for Theorem
3.1
, and its corollary hold in the more general case.

2.2
Distributions on Simplicial and Sequence Complexes

A probability distribution on a sequence complex
\mathcal{X}
, is a function
p:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
that satisfies the condition

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}}p(\xi)=1.

We introduce weights
w_{p}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
, that are
induced by the distribution
p
,
as the conditional probability given the sequence length:

w_{p}(\sigma)=p(\sigma\bigm||\sigma|=n+1)={p(\sigma)}\left(\sum_{|\tau|=|\sigma|}p(\tau)\right)^{-1},\qquad\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}.

(1)

An immediate consequence of this weight function construction is that all sequences of same length have weights that sum to one.

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}w_{p}(\sigma)=1,\hskip 5.69054pt\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(2)

In parallel to the distribution induced weight function on sequences, we introduced two weight functions on simplicial complexes. A simplicial distribution on an abstract simplicial complex
K\subseteq 2^{[m]}
, is a probability distribution
p:K\to[0,1]
that satisfies the condition

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi\in K}\hskip 2.84526pt

\displaystyle p(\xi)=1

We consider either the simplicial distribution directly as a weight function on the simplicial complex, or the associated moment map as the weight function.
The moment map
m_{p}:K\to[0,1]
, associated to a simplicial distribution,
p
is defined for all
\xi\in K
as:

\displaystyle m_{p}(\xi):=\mathbb{E}_{p}\Bigg(\prod_{i\in\xi}x_{i}\Bigg)=\sum_{\zeta\supseteq\xi}p(\zeta)

Here we use the notation
x_{i}:2^{[m]}\to\{0,1\},x_{i}(\xi)=1\text{ iff }i\in\xi
, to refer to the indicator function, viewed as a random variable on
2^{[m]}
. The equivalence of the two definitions for the moment map is easy to see,

\mathbb{E}_{p}\Bigg(\prod_{i\in\xi}x_{i}\Bigg)=\sum_{\zeta\supseteq\xi}p(\zeta)\prod_{i\in\xi}x_{i}(\zeta)+\sum_{\zeta\not\supset\xi}p(\zeta)\prod_{i\in\xi}x_{i}(\zeta)=\sum_{\zeta\supseteq\xi}p(\zeta)\cdot 1+\sum_{\zeta\not\supseteq\xi}p(\zeta)\cdot 0=\sum_{\zeta\supseteq\xi}p(\zeta)

Notice the moment map, as opposed to the simplicial distribution, takes value one on the empty set,
m_{p}(\varnothing)=1
, and
is monotone decreasing on the faces of the simplicial complex,

\zeta\subseteq\xi\implies m_{p}(\zeta)>m_{p}(\xi).

2.3
The independence models for simplices and sequences

We define null models on sequence and simplicial complexes as weight functions arising from an assumption that the weights assigned to the sequences (or simplices) depend solely on the vertices involved, and not the precise position of each vertex.
We will refer to complexes with such weight functions as following the Independent Vertices Model. Notice we would have one such model for the sequence complex context, and another for the simplicial complex context. We make this formal in the following two definitions.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a vertex set
V
, and vector
(w_{v})_{v\in V}\in(0,1)^{|V|}
which satisfies:

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}w_{v}

\displaystyle=1

(3)

We define the associated
independent vertices model
over a sequences complex
\mathcal{X}
, with
\mathcal{X}_{0}=V
, as the weighted sequence complex
(\mathcal{X},w)
with weight function
w
, such that:

\displaystyle w((v_{0},...,v_{n}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{n}w_{v_{i}},\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\text{for all }(v_{0},...,v_{n})\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle w(\varnothing)

\displaystyle=1

It is quick to check that this weight function satisfies the condition

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}w(\sigma)=1\quad\text{ for all }n\geq-1.

One can interpret this weight function as a probability distribution on sequences of a fixed length
=n+1
, where the appearance of a vertex in each sequence position is independent from the vertices in all the other positions, and
w_{v}
is the probability of the vertex
v
appearing in each position.

In the case of simplicial complexes, we define the independent simplicial distribution associated to a vector
(p_{1},...,p_{m})\in(0,1)^{m}
as follows. For each face
\xi\in 2^{[m]}
, interpreted as a binary vector on
m
vertices, we define the probability
p(\xi)
, as the joint probability of each of the vertices
i\in[m]
taking the prescribed binary value in
\xi
following a Bernoulli distribution given by
p_{i}
. In other words, the probability is defined as:

\displaystyle p(\xi)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\xi}p_{i}\prod_{j\not\in\xi}(1-p_{j}),\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\text{ for all }\xi\in K.

Here
p_{i}
has the meaning of probability of the
i
-th vertex appearing in a subset
\sigma
. Note that these probabilities do
not
sum to
1
.
We define the independent vertices model on simplicial complexes, inspired in the moment map weight function associated to the independent simplicial distribution.

Observation 2.5
.

The moment map
m_{p}
, on a simplicial complex
K\subseteq 2^{[m]}
induced by the independent simplicial distribution associated to
(p_{1},...,p_{m})\in(0,1)^{m}
, is defined as:

\displaystyle m_{p}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\xi}p_{i},\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\text{ for all }\xi\in K

\displaystyle w(\varnothing)

\displaystyle=1

We give the definition for the independent vertices model in the context of simplicial complexes, for a general vector
(w_{1},...,w_{m})\in(0,1)^{m}
determining the weight of each vertex. The definition is thus very reminiscent of the one in the sequence complex context.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a vertex set
[m]
, and vector
(w_{1},...,w_{m})\in(0,1)^{m}
(we allow in this context the possibility of
\sum_{i\in[m]}w_{i}\neq 1
), we define the associated
independent vertices model
over a simplicial complex
K\subseteq 2^{[m]}
, as the weighted simplicial complex
(K,w)
with weight function
w
, such that:

\displaystyle w(\xi)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\xi}^{n}w_{i},\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\text{for all }\xi\in K

\displaystyle w(\varnothing)

\displaystyle=1

In applications, we may interpret this weight function as either the moment map associated to an independent simplicial distribution (as in observation
2.5
), or alternatively as the weight function that normalizes the distribution by the probability of the empty set, as in the following observation
2.7
. We will show, that in this null hypothesis setting, both weight functions yield an equivalent result, which is what we would like to see.

Observation 2.7
.

Given a simplicial complex
K
, and the independent simplicial distribution,
p
, associated to the vector
(p_{1},...,p_{m})\in(0,1)^{m}
, consider the weight function
p_{\varnothing}
defined over each face as it’s probability normalized by the probability of the empty set.

\displaystyle p_{\varnothing}(\xi)=\dfrac{p(\xi)}{p(\varnothing)},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ for all }\xi\in K

In the case of the independent simplicial distribution, this yields

\displaystyle p_{\varnothing}(\xi)=\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\xi}p_{i}\cdot\displaystyle\prod_{i\not\in\xi}(1-p_{i})\cdot\Bigg(\displaystyle\prod_{i\in[m]}(1-p_{i})\Bigg)^{-1}=\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\xi}p_{i}(1-p_{i})^{-1}

Consequently the weighted simplicial complex associated to this weight function,
(K,p_{\varnothing})
, follows an independent vertices model associated to the vector

\Big(p_{1}(1-p_{1})^{-1},...,p_{m}(1-p_{m})^{-1}\Big)\in(0,1)^{m}
.

2.4
Homological algebra of Abstract Cell Complexes

Given an abstract cell complex
\mathcal{X}
, we denote the set of
n
-dimensional elements as
\mathcal{X}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\dim^{-1}(n)
, and identify the space of
n
-dimensional cochains
C^{n}(\mathcal{X})
with
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}_{n}
:

C^{n}(\mathcal{X})=\left\{f:\mathcal{X}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Note that if
\mathcal{X}_{n}
is empty for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, then by convention,
C^{n}(\mathcal{X})=\{0\}
. We will refer to the graded space of cochains as,

C^{*}(\mathcal{X})=\bigoplus_{n=-1}^{+\infty}C^{n}(\mathcal{X})

(4)

Note that we restrict ourselves to working over the field of real numbers; this is because we will make use of inner products on
C^{*}(\mathcal{X})
.

The coboundary operator
\delta_{n}:C^{n}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow C^{n+1}(\mathcal{X})
, is defined on
f\in C^{n}(\mathcal{X})
via

(\delta_{n}f)(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\kappa\left(\xi,\xi^{\prime}\right)f\left(\xi^{\prime}\right).

The coboundary operator satisfies
\delta_{n+1}\delta_{n}=0
for all
n
; this is because of the condition (3) in Definition
2.1
. This property enables the usual definition of cohomology

H^{n}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{\ker\delta_{n}}{\im\delta_{n-1}}
of the chain complex

\displaystyle\{0\}{\longrightarrow}\hskip 2.84526ptC^{-1}(\mathcal{X})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta_{-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}C^{0}(\mathcal{X})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta_{0}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta_{n-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}C^{n}(\mathcal{X})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta_{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}C^{n+1}(\mathcal{X}){\longrightarrow}\ldots.

2.5
Hodge Theory on Abstract Cell Complexes

Here we expose the Hodge theory on abstract cell complexes. The definitions and results we present here are classical results for simplicial complexes, dating back to
[
6
]
and expanded in e.g.
[
7
,
9
]
. We present them in the context of abstract cell complexes, for sake of self-containment and notation consistency. The results and proofs of this section are not new.

Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be an inner product on

C^{*}(\mathcal{X})
, such that the direct sum decomposition (
4
) is an orthogonal decomposition with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Denote by
\delta_{n}^{*}
the adjoint of
\delta_{n}
, with respect to the chosen inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. That is, for every
f,g\in C^{*}(\mathcal{X})
,

\langle\delta_{n}^{*}f,g\rangle=\langle f,\delta_{n}g\rangle.

It is easy to see that
\delta_{n+1}\delta_{n}=0
if and only if
\delta^{*}_{n}\delta^{*}_{n+1}=0
. Therefore there are two versions of the cohomology space, one for
\delta_{n}
and the other for
\delta_{n}^{*}
, and these two are isomorphic. We introduce the Laplacian operator, which is closely related to the cohomology,
H^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, of the coboundary operator
{\delta}_{n}
.

Definition 2.8
.

The Laplacian of a triple
(C^{*}(\mathcal{X}),\delta,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is an operator over
C^{n}(\mathcal{X})\to C^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}
.

\displaystyle\text{defined as, }L_{n}

\displaystyle=L^{\mathrm{up}}_{n}+L^{\mathrm{down}}_{n}

\displaystyle\text{where, }L^{\mathrm{up}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n}^{*}\delta_{n},\hskip 5.69054ptL^{\mathrm{down}}_{n}=\delta_{n-1}\delta_{n-1}^{*}.

For the reader familiar with the combinatorial Laplacian,
L_{\text{comb}}
, we note this operator corresponds to the
0
-dimensional up-Laplacian associated to a weighted graph G,

L_{\text{comb}}=L_{0}^{\text{up}}:C^{0}(G)\to C^{0}(G).

We refer to Section
5
for a deeper discussion of this.

As a direct result of Definition
2.8
and standard Linear Algebra results, one can easily prove the following properties.

Lemma 2.9
.

The Laplacians
\ L^{\mathrm{up}}
,
\ L^{\mathrm{down}}
and
\ L
are self-adjoint and positive semi-definite with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Moreover, the following identities hold for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}
:

\displaystyle\ker\ L_{n}=

\displaystyle\left(\ker\ L^{\mathrm{up}}_{n}\right)\cap\left(\ker\ L^{\mathrm{down}}_{n}\right)=(\ker\delta_{n-1}^{*})\cap(\ker\delta_{n}).

\displaystyle\im(\delta_{n}^{*})=

\displaystyle\im(\delta_{n}^{*}\delta_{n})\text{ and }\im(\delta_{n+1})=\im(\delta_{n+1}\delta_{n+1}^{*}).

As we mentioned earlier, the Laplacian is intimately related to the cohomology of the coboundary operator
{\delta}_{n}
. This theorem dates back to 1944
[
6
]
, in the context of simplicial complexes, and is easily extended to the case of abstract cell complexes with no changes to the proof.

Theorem 2.10
.

The kernel of the Laplacian
L_{n}
associated to an abstract cell complex
\mathcal{X}
, referred to as the space of “harmonic vectors”, is isomorphic to the cohomology of
\delta_{n}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}
:

\ker L_{n}\simeq H^{n}(\mathcal{X}).

Theorem
2.10
is a specific case of a more detailed theorem below, we refer to
[
7
]
for a proof. We use the following notations in what follows:

B^{n}_{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\im\delta_{n}^{*},\quad B^{n}_{-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\im\delta_{n-1}.

Theorem 2.11
.

Given an abstract cell complex,
\mathcal{X}
, and a fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}
, the space
C^{n}(\mathcal{X})
admits the following
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
-orthogonal decomposition,:

C^{n}(\mathcal{X})=\ker L_{n}\oplus B^{n}_{-}\oplus B^{n}_{+}

Theorem
2.11
Has many nice spectral interpretations, let us introduce some notation so we may discuss them. Denote the multiset (set with multiplicity) of strictly positive eigenvalues of
L_{n}
,
L^{\mathrm{up}}_{n}
and
L^{\mathrm{down}}_{n}
, by
{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L_{n})
,
{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\mathrm{up}}_{n})
and
{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\mathrm{down}}_{n})
, respectively. For each
\lambda\in{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L_{n})
,
{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\mathrm{up}}_{n})
and
{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\mathrm{down}}_{n})
, denote the corresponding eigenspace by
E^{n}_{\lambda}
,
E_{\lambda}^{n,+}
and
E_{\lambda}^{n,-}
, respectively.

Corollary 2.12
.

Given an abstract cell complex
\mathcal{X}
, we may decompose the spectrum of it’s associated Laplacian
L_{n}
, for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}
, as:

{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L_{n})={\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\text{up}}_{n})\sqcup{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\text{down}}_{n})={\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\text{up}}_{n-1})\sqcup{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\text{down}}_{n+1})

Moreover, the eigenspaces associated to it’s eigenvalues may be described as:

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}^{n,+}=E^{n}_{\lambda}\cap B^{n}_{+}\hskip 5.69054pt\text{ and }\hskip 5.69054ptE_{\mu}^{n,-}=E^{n}_{\mu}\cap B^{n}_{-},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for }\lambda\in{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\text{up}}_{n}),\mu\in{\text{Spec}}_{*}(L^{\text{down}}_{n})

And isomorphically mapped onto the eigenspaces of the adjacent dimension Laplacian by

E_{\lambda}^{n,+}\underset{{\delta}^{*}}{\overset{{\delta}}{\rightleftarrows}}E_{\lambda}^{n+1,-}
.

The proof to the corollary is a straightforward linear algebra exercise.

Example 3.2

\displaystyle E(1,L_{n})

4.1
Computation of the Laplacian on Sequences

We begin by providing the formulae for the coboundary operator and its conjugate on the sequence complex.
We will consider a weighted sequence complex
(\mathcal{X},w)
with weight function
w:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}

\mathcal{X}_{0}=V

e_{\sigma}(\tau)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }\sigma=\tau\\

The coboundary operator can be written as

{\delta}_{n}e_{\tau}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n+1}}\kappa({\sigma},{\tau})e_{\sigma}.

We define an inner product on
C^{*}(\mathcal{X})
, associated to a positive weight function,

\{e_{\sigma}\}

\langle e_{\sigma},e_{\tau}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}w(\sigma),&\text{ if }\sigma=\tau\\

The decomposition
C^{*}(\mathcal{X})=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{n\geq-1}C^{n}(\mathcal{X})
is an orthogonal decomposition with respect to this inner product.

Given this inner product, consider the adjoint of
{\delta}_{n}
, the operator
{\delta}_{n}^{*}:C^{n+1}(\mathcal{X})\to C^{n}(\mathcal{X})

\langle{\delta}_{n}f,g\rangle=\langle f,{\delta}_{n}^{*}g\rangle

and

{\delta}_{n}^{*}e_{\sigma}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\alpha_{\sigma}(\tau)e_{\tau}

. Following this notation, for any

\displaystyle\langle{\delta}_{n}^{*}e_{\sigma},e_{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\alpha_{\sigma}(\tau)\langle e_{\tau},e_{\eta}\rangle=\alpha_{\sigma}(\eta)w(\eta)

On the other hand, by definition of the adjoint, we may rewrite the inner product as:

\displaystyle\langle{\delta}_{n}^{*}e_{\sigma},e_{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle e_{\sigma},{\delta}_{n}e_{\eta}\rangle=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{X}_{n+1}}\kappa({\omega},{\eta})\langle e_{\sigma},e_{\omega}\rangle=\kappa({\sigma},{\eta})w(\sigma)

We conclude the adjoint of the coboundary operator
{\delta}_{n}
for all

{\delta}_{n}^{*}e_{\sigma}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\frac{w(\sigma)}{w(\tau)}\kappa({\sigma},{\tau})e_{\tau}

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{n}\times[0:n]\times\mathcal{X}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{n}\times[0:n]

Of interest to us, is that the cochains
\{f(\eta)\}_{\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}
form a basis for
C^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, the proof is quite simple, we give it in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Given a sequence complex
\mathcal{X}
, and any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
the cochains
\{f(\eta)\}_{\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}
form a basis for
C^{n}(\mathcal{X})
.

Proof.

We will show
\mathrm{Span}\big\{f(\eta)\mid\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{n}\big\}\ni e_{\sigma}
, for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We do the proof by induction over
n
.

Consider n=0, then
\mathcal{X}_{0}=V=\{a,b,c,...\}
. By definition we have:

\displaystyle f({(a)})

\displaystyle=\big(f_{0}(a)\big)(a)\cdot e_{(a)}+\big(f_{0}(a)\big)(b)\cdot e_{(b)}+\big(f_{0}(a)\big)(c)\cdot e_{(c)}+\dots=e_{(a)}+e_{(b)}+e_{(c)}+\dots

\displaystyle f({(b)})

\displaystyle=\big(f_{0}(b)\big)(a)\cdot e_{(a)}+\big(f_{0}(b)\big)(b)\cdot e_{(b)}+\big(f_{0}(b)\big)(c)\cdot e_{(c)}+\dots=w(b)\cdot e_{(a)}-w(a)\cdot e_{(b)}

\displaystyle f({(c)})

\displaystyle=\big(f_{0}(c)\big)(a)\cdot e_{(a)}+\big(f_{0}(c)\big)(b)\cdot e_{(b)}+\big(f_{0}(c)\big)(c)\cdot e_{(c)}+\dots=w(c)\cdot e_{(a)}-w(a)\cdot e_{(c)}

As we have
w(v)\neq 0
for all
v\in V
, we may take the following linear combination of cochains:

\displaystyle f({(a)})+\sum_{x\neq a}w(a)^{-1}\cdot f({(x)})=\sum_{x\in A}e_{(x)}+\sum_{x\neq a}\Big(\dfrac{w(x)}{w(a)}\cdot e_{(a)}-e_{(x)}\Big)=\Bigg(1+\frac{1-w(a)}{w(a)}\Bigg)\cdot e_{(a)}=w(a)^{-1}\cdot e_{(a)}

Notice that in the second equality, we made use of
\sum_{x\in V}w(x)=1
. Proving
e_{(a)}\in\mathrm{Span}\big\{f(\eta)\mid\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{n}\big\}
.

On the other hand, by definition, we may write
f({(y)})
for any
y\in V
,
y\neq a
as:

f({(y)})=w(y)\cdot e_{(a)}-w(a)\cdot e_{(y)}

Solving for
e_{(y)}
and plugging in our identity for
e_{(a)}
, we get

\displaystyle e_{(y)}=-w(a)^{-1}\cdot\big(f({(y)})-w(y)\cdot e_{(a)}\big)=-w(a)^{-1}\cdot f({(y)})+w(y)\cdot\Bigg(f({(a)})+\sum_{x\neq a}w(a)^{-1}\cdot f({(x)})\Bigg)

Proving for all
y\in X_{0}
,
e_{(y)}\in\mathrm{Span}\big\{f(\eta)\mid\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\big\}
, concluding the base induction case.

Consider
\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
and denote
\eta_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
it’s first vertex and
\eta\mid_{[1,n]}\in\mathcal{X}_{n-1}
it’s restriction to it’s other
n
vertices. By definition,
f(\eta)=f({\eta_{0}})\otimes f({\eta\mid_{[1,n]}})
, allowing us to immediately apply our induction hypothesis:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Span}\{f(\eta)\mid\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{n}+1\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Span}\{f({(x)})\mid x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\}\otimes\mathrm{Span}\{f(\tau)\mid\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{(x)}\mid x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\}\otimes\mathrm{Span}\{e_{\tau}\mid\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}\}=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{\eta}\mid\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{n+1}\}

proving the lemma.
∎

\lambda\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)=0=\left[(n+2)-\sum_{i=0}^{n}w(\tau_{i})\right]\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma:\sigma\bowtie\tau,\\
\tau_{j}\neq\sigma_{j}\end{subarray}}w(\sigma_{j})\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\sigma)

Proving the cochain
f(\eta)
satisfies the system of equations corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
, on sequences
\tau
with
\tau_{i}\neq a,\eta_{i}
for
i\in[0:n]\setminus I_{a}
. We will now show the system is also satisfied for any other
\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
. In this case for all
i\in[0:n]\setminus I_{a}
the vertices
\tau_{i}
are forced to be either
a,\eta_{i}
. Hence we deal as promised with the case of
\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, such that
\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)=0
.

We use the swap operation from Definition
4.1
to rewrite the sum:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma:\sigma\bowtie\tau,\\
\tau_{j}\neq\sigma_{j}\end{subarray}}w(\sigma_{j})\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\sigma)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{y\neq\tau_{j}}w(y)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)({{\tau}^{\mathrlap{\times}{+}_{j}:{y}}})

(9)

We have assumed,
\eta_{j}=a
for all
j\in I_{a}
. As such, for all
y\in V
:

\displaystyle\big(f(\eta)\big)({{\tau}^{\mathrlap{\times}{+}_{j}:{y}}})=\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)\hskip 14.22636pt\forall j\in I_{a}

Meaning we can reduce part of the sum in (
9
), when restricted to
j\in I_{a}
, to:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in I_{a}}\sum_{y\neq\tau_{j}}w(y)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)({{\tau}^{\mathrlap{\times}{+}_{j}:{y}}})=\sum_{j\in I_{a}}\sum_{y\neq\tau_{j}}w(y)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)=\sum_{j\in I_{a}}\left(1-w(\tau_{j})\right)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)

(10)

Notice in the last line we used the fact that
\sum_{y\in V}w(y)=1
.

On the other hand, for
j\in[0:n]\setminus I_{a}
we have:

\displaystyle\big(f(\eta)\big)({{\tau}^{\mathrlap{\times}{+}_{j}:{y}}})

\displaystyle=\big(f_{0}(\eta_{j})\big)(y)\cdot\prod_{s\neq j}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{s})\big)(\tau_{s})

In particular, as we have assumed
\eta_{j}\neq a
, for
j\not\in I_{a}
, this means

\displaystyle\big(f(\eta)\big)({{\tau}^{\mathrlap{\times}{+}_{j}:{y}}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{j})\big)(y)\cdot\displaystyle\prod_{s\neq j}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{s})\big)(\tau_{s})\neq 0\hskip 8.53581pt\text{if }y=\eta_{j},a\\
0\hskip 142.26378pt\text{ if }y\neq\eta_{j},a\end{cases}

We recall that for
j\in[0:n]\setminus I_{a}
the vertices
\tau_{j}
are forced to be either
a,\eta_{j}
. Consider the case
\tau_{j}=a
, then we can reduce part of the sum in (
9
) to:

\displaystyle\sum_{y\neq\tau_{j}}w(y)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)({{\tau}^{\mathrlap{\times}{+}_{j}:{y}}})=w(\eta_{j})\cdot\big(f_{0}(\eta_{j})\big)(\eta_{j})\cdot\displaystyle\prod_{s\neq j}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{s})\big)(\tau_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w(\eta_{j})\cdot(-w(a))\cdot\displaystyle\prod_{s\neq j}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{s})\big)(\tau_{s})=\big(f_{0}(\eta_{j})\big)(\tau_{j})\cdot(-w(a))\cdot\displaystyle\prod_{s\neq j}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{s})\big)(\tau_{s})=-w(\tau_{j})\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)

Similarly, consider the case where
\tau_{j}=\eta_{j}
, again we can reduce the sum to:

\displaystyle\sum_{y\neq\tau_{j}}w(y)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)({{\tau}^{\mathrlap{\times}{+}_{j}:{y}}})=w(a)\cdot\big(f_{0}(\eta_{j})\big)(a)\cdot\displaystyle\prod_{s\neq j}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{s})\big)(\tau_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w(a)\cdot w(\eta_{j})\cdot\displaystyle\prod_{s\neq j}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{s})\big)(\tau_{s})=-\big(f_{0}(\eta_{j})\big)(\tau_{j})\cdot w(\eta_{j})\cdot\displaystyle\prod_{s\neq j}\big(f_{0}(\eta_{s})\big)(\tau_{s})=-w(\tau_{j})\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)

Putting both these cases together we can rewrite the sum in (
9
) restricted to
[0:n]\setminus I_{a}
, as:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\not\in I_{a}}\sum_{y\neq\tau_{j}}w(y)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)({{\tau}^{\mathrlap{\times}{+}_{j}:{y}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\not\in I_{a}}-w(\tau_{j})\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)

(11)

Putting the restricted sums (
10
) and (
11
) together, and plugging into the right hand side of (
6
), we see that for
\tau
for which all vertices
\tau_{i}
, for
i\not\in I_{a}
, are forced to be either
a,\eta_{i}
, we have:

\displaystyle\left[(n+2)-\sum_{j=0}^{n}w(\tau_{j})\right]\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma:\sigma\bowtie\tau,\\
\tau_{j}\neq\sigma_{j}\end{subarray}}w(\sigma_{j})\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)({\sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[(n+2)-\sum_{j=0}^{n}w(\tau_{j})\right]\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)+\sum_{j\not\in I_{a}}w(\tau_{j})\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)-\sum_{j\in I_{a}}\left(1-w(\tau_{j})\right)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)=(k+2)\cdot\big(f(\eta)\big)(\tau)

Notice in the last equality we used
|I_{a}|=n-k
, as per our assumption. With this we conclude
f(\eta)
satisfies the system of equations corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda=k+2
for all sequences
\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
. Proving
f(\eta)
is an eigenvector for
\lambda=k+2
for
\eta
such that
\#\{a\in\eta\}=n-k
.

As we know the cochains
f(\eta)
form a basis for
C^{n}
, they must span the eigenspaces of the Laplacian,
L_{n}
. Hence the multiplicity of each eigenvalue
\lambda=1,...,n+2
is easily computed to be,

\displaystyle\text{mult}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\#\Big\{\eta\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\#\{a\in\eta\}=n+2-\lambda\Big\}=\binom{n+1}{\lambda-1}\cdot(|V|-1)^{\lambda-1}

With this, we finish the proof of the main theorem.
∎

In this section we prove the analogous results to the previous section’s, in the setting of simplicial complexes. As we mentioned earlier, the results in this section, if at all new, are simple consequences of classical results for the Hodge Laplacian. We present here results, old and new, for completion, and to provide a parallel to the sequence complex setting.

5.1
Laplacian over Simplicial Complexes

We will consider a weighted simplicial complex
K\subseteq 2^{[m]}
with weight function
w:K\to(0,+\infty)
and associated inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
C^{*}(K)
, defined via its values on the usual basis
\{e_{\xi}\}
as:

\langle e_{\xi},e_{\xi^{\prime}}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}w(\xi),&\text{ if }\xi=\xi^{\prime}\\
0&\text{ if }\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}\end{cases}

In this setting the coboundary
{\delta}
and coboundary adjoint
{\delta}^{*}
operators are defined for any face
\xi=\{i_{0},...,i_{n+1}\}\in K_{n+1}
, with vertices labeled such that
i_{0}<\dots<i_{n+1}
, as:

\displaystyle{\delta}_{n}e_{\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]\setminus\xi,\\
\{i\}\cup\xi\in K\end{subarray}}\kappa({\{i\}\cup\xi},{\xi})\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi}

\displaystyle{\delta}_{n}^{*}e_{\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi^{\prime}\in K_{n}}\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi^{\prime})}\kappa({\xi},{\xi^{\prime}})e_{\xi^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{j}\})}(-1)^{j}e_{\xi\setminus\{i_{j}\}}

Where the incidence function
\kappa
is defined as following Definition
2.2
. In this setting, the Laplacian is easily computed as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
2^{[m]}
be a weighted full simplex, with weight function
w:2^{[m]}\to(0,1]
. For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}
and faces
\xi=\{i_{0},...,i_{n}\}\in K_{n}
, with vertices labeled such that
i_{0}<\dots<i_{n}
, the Laplacian operator on the basis elements
e_{\xi}\in C^{n}(2^{[m]})
, is given by:

\displaystyle L_{n}(e_{\xi})=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\dfrac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w(\xi)}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}\right)\cdot e_{\xi}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\cdot\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\},\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})\cdot\Bigg(\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}-\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w((\{i\}\cup\xi)\setminus\{i_{k}\})}\Bigg)\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}

Proof.

We compute
L_{n}^{down}
and
L_{n}^{up}
separately, by plugging in our computations for
{\delta}
and
{\delta}^{*}
each time. Consider a face
\xi=\{i_{0},...,i_{n}\}\in K_{n}
, with vertices labeled such that
i_{0}<\dots<i_{n}
:

\displaystyle L_{n}^{up}e_{\xi}=

\displaystyle{\delta}^{*}{\delta}_{n}e_{\xi}={\delta}^{*}\left(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\kappa({\{i\}\cup\xi},{\xi})\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\kappa({\{i\}\cup\xi},{\xi})\cdot\Bigg[\sum_{j\in\{i\}\cup\xi}\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w((\{i\}\cup\xi)\setminus\{j\})}\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi,\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{j\})\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{j\}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\kappa({\{i\}\cup\xi},{\xi})\cdot\Bigg[\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w(\xi)}\kappa({\{i\}\cup\xi},{\xi})\cdot e_{\xi}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{k}\in\xi,\\
i_{k}<i\end{subarray}}\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w((\{i\}\cup\xi)\setminus\{i_{k}\})}(-1)^{k}\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{k}\in\xi,\\
i_{k}>i\end{subarray}}\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w((\{i\}\cup\xi)\setminus\{i_{k}\})}(-1)^{k+1}\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}\Bigg]

(12)

Here we made use of the simple observation:

\displaystyle\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi,\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k}\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{if }i_{k}<i\\
(-1)^{k+1}\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{if }i_{k}>i\end{cases}

On the other hand, notice:

\displaystyle\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\},\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})=\begin{cases}-\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi,\xi)\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{if }i_{k}<i\\
\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi,\xi)\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{if }i_{k}>i\end{cases}

We can use this, together with
\Big(\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi,\xi\Big)^{2}=1
, to simplify the expression from (
5.1
) :

\displaystyle L_{n}^{up}e_{\xi}=\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w(\xi)}\cdot e_{\xi}+\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w((\{i\}\cup\xi)\setminus\{i_{k}\})}\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\},\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})\cdot(-1)^{k+1}\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}

Now let us look at
L_{n}^{down}
,

\displaystyle L_{n}^{down}e_{\xi}={\delta}{\delta}_{n-1}^{*}e_{\xi}={\delta}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}(-1)^{k}\cdot e_{\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}(-1)^{k}\Bigg[\kappa(\xi,\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})\cdot e_{\xi}+\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\},\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}(-1)^{2k}\cdot e_{\xi}+\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}(-1)^{k}\cdot\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\},\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}

Putting
L_{n}^{down}
and
L_{n}^{up}
together, we get

\displaystyle L_{n}(e_{\xi})=

\displaystyle L^{\text{down}}_{n}(e_{\xi})+L^{\text{up}}_{n}(e_{\xi})=\left(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\dfrac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w(\xi)}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}\right)\cdot e_{\xi}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\cdot\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\},\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})\cdot\Bigg(\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}-\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w((\{i\}\cup\xi)\setminus\{i_{k}\})}\Bigg)\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}

Proving the expression in the proposition statement.

An immediate consequence of this lemma, tells us that scaling the weight function, by the same factor over all dimensions, has no impact on the associated Laplacian.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

be a weighted full simplex, with weight function
w:2^{[m]}\to(0,1]
. Consider a different weight function
w^{\prime}:2^{[m]}\to(0,1]
, defined as
w^{\prime}(\xi)=\alpha\cdot w(\xi)
for all

, for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The respective associated Laplacians
L^{w}_{n}
and
L^{w^{\prime}}_{n}

L^{w}_{n}=L^{w^{\prime}}_{n}\quad\text{ for all }n\geq-1.

It is also sometimes useful to think of the matrix representation of

directly in terms of the matrix representation of
{\delta}
. This particularly comes in useful when relating to the combinatorial Laplacian. Let
D_{n}
be the matrix representation of
{\delta}_{n}
and
W_{n}
the diagonal matrix representing the inner product on
C^{n}(K)
. Then we may express the
L_{n}^{\text{up}},L_{n}^{\text{down}}
operators as:

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\text{up}}

\displaystyle=W_{n}^{-1}D_{n}^{T}W_{n+1}D_{n}

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\text{down}}

\displaystyle=D_{n-1}W_{n-1}^{-1}D_{n-1}^{T}W_{n}

Consider
K=G
to be a weighted graph. Denote by
\mathbb{A}
the diagonal matrix of vertex weights, following our definition
\mathbb{A}=W_{0}
. On the other hand, let
\mathbb{W}
be the

:
\mathbb{W}_{ij}=w(\{i,j\})
, and
\mathbb{D}

given by,
\mathbb{D}_{ii}=\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{W}_{ij}
. It is easy to show that,

\mathbb{D}-\mathbb{W}=D_{0}^{T}W_{1}D_{0}.

Which implies

5.2
Laplacian on Simplicial Complexes for the Independent Vertices Model

Here we now show the Laplacian for the independent vertices model is “boring"; we recall the theorem we stated in section
3
:

Theorem
3.4
.

Let
(2^{[m]},w)
be a weighted simplicial complex, with weight function
w:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
w(\varnothing)=1
. Then the following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)

(2^{[m]},w)
is an independent vertices model;

(ii)

the associated Laplacians are multiples of identities:

L_{n}=\alpha_{n}\cdot I_{C^{n}},\quad\forall n\geq-1,

(13)

where
I_{C^{n}}
denotes the identity on
C^{n}(2^{[m]})
, and
\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Moreover, if (
13
) holds, then
\alpha_{n}
is constant with respect to
n
, and

\alpha_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i},

where
(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})
is the vector determining the independent vertices model associated to the weight function
w
.

Proof.

We start by proving the reverse direction: we assume our simplicial complex follows the independent weights model, and wish to prove the associated Laplacian
L_{n}=\alpha_{n}\cdot I_{C^{n}(2^{[m]})}
. Notice that for
n=-1,m-1
, we have
|(2^{[m]})_{n}|=1
, making the statement trivial. We now focus on the cases for
n=0,...,m-1
: Fix
e_{\xi}\in C^{n}(2^{[m]})
, we call upon Lemma
5.1
:

\displaystyle L_{n}(e_{\xi})=\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\cdot\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\},\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})\cdot\Bigg(\frac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}-\frac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w((\{i\}\cup\xi)\setminus\{i_{k}\})}\Bigg)\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\dfrac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w(\xi)}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}\right)\cdot e_{\xi}\hskip 5.69054pt=\hskip 5.69054pt\left(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}w(i)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}w(i_{k})\right)\cdot e_{\xi}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus\xi}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\cdot\kappa(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\},\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})\cdot\Big(w(i_{k})-w(i_{k})\Big)\cdot e_{\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\}}\hskip 5.69054pt=\hskip 5.69054pt\left(\sum_{i\in[m]}w(i)\right)\cdot e_{\xi}+0

Proving
L_{n}=\alpha_{n}\cdot I_{C^{n}(2^{[m]})}
for
\alpha_{n}=\sum_{i\in[m]}w(i)
for all
n=0,...,m-2
. In the cases of
n=-1,m-1
, the computation of
L_{n}
easily shows that
\alpha_{n}
must also be
\alpha_{n}=\sum_{i\in[m]}w(i)
.

We now proceed to prove the forward direction, we assume for all
n=-1,\dots,m
, it holds that
L_{n}=\alpha_{n}\cdot I_{C^{n}(2^{[m]})}
. Putting this assumption together with Lemma
5.1
tells us that for all
n\in[-1:m]
, face
\xi\in\left(2^{[m]}\right)_{n}
, and vertices
i\in[m]\setminus\xi,i_{k}\in\xi
it holds that

\dfrac{w(\{i\}\cup\xi)}{w(\{i\}\cup\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}=\dfrac{w(\xi)}{w(\xi\setminus\{i_{k}\})}

We will use this consequence of our hypothesis to prove the weights follow the independent vertices model by applying induction over
n
. The statement is assumed in the proposition statement for
n=-1
and vacuous for
n=0
, let us set
n=1
.
Consider
\{i,j\}\in\left(2^{[m]}\right)_{1}
, by hypothesis:

\displaystyle\dfrac{w(\{i\}\cup\{j\})}{w(\{i\}\cup\{j\}\setminus\{j\})}=\dfrac{w(\{j\})}{w(\{j\}\setminus\{j\})}\hskip 5.69054pt\iff\hskip 5.69054pt\dfrac{w(\{i,j\})}{w(\{i\})}=\dfrac{w(\{j\})}{w(\varnothing)}

As the proposition statement assumes
w(\varnothing)=1
, we conclude
w(\{i,j\})=w(\{i\})w(\{j\})
, proving the base case. Now assume the result holds for faces of dimension
n
, we will show it holds for faces of dimension
n+1
.

Consider
\{i_{0},\dots,i_{n+1}\}\in\left(2^{[m]}\right)_{n+1}
, by hypothesis:

\displaystyle\dfrac{w(\{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\}\cup\{i_{n+1}\})}{w(\{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\}\cup\{i_{n+1}\}\setminus\{i_{0}\})}=\dfrac{w(\{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\})}{w(\{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\}\setminus\{i_{0}\})}\hskip 5.69054pt\iff\hskip 5.69054pt\dfrac{w(\{i_{0},\dots,i_{n+1}\})}{w(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{n+1}\})}=\dfrac{w(\{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\})}{w(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{n})}

Applying the induction hypothesis, we get

\displaystyle w(\{i_{0},\dots,i_{n+1}\})=\dfrac{\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}w(i_{k})\prod_{k=0}^{n}w(i_{k})}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}w(i_{k})}=\prod_{k=0}^{n+1}w(i_{k})

proving the induction step, and the proof for the theorem.
∎

Conclusion

We introduced a general framework for weighted sequence complexes, where the weights are derived from probability distributions on sequences, and extended the Hodge Laplacian formalism to this setting.
An important requirement for such a Laplacian is that it has a
“simple” spectrum in the null models with independent vertices.

We completely characterized the spectrum of the associated Laplacian for the independent vertices model, both in the sequence and simplicial complex setting. For simplicial complexes we found (Corollary
3.5
) that the appropriate Laplacians are multiples of the identity. This happens with both natural choices of the weights: (i) if the weights are the probabilities and (ii) if the weights are the moments, and confirms a “common sense” intuition that there should not be any
preferred
eigenbasis in the independent model, as the data does not have any “interesting” structure beyond the independence of vertices.

We found, that unlike in the simplicial complex case, the Laplacian of the independent vertices model for the sequence complex has a stereotypical structure (Theorem
3.1
). Specifically, it has distinct integer eigenvalues, with multiplicities that scale exponentially in eigenvalues. Here the weight function on the sequence complex was chosen to be the conditional probability per equation (
1
). This choice is natural in this context, as the length of the sequence is another random variable that does not depend on the assumption that vertices appear in each sequence position independently. The eigenbasis for this independent vertices model provides the analogue of the Fourier transform in the context of the Laplacians in a Euclidean domain.

There are many open questions that stem from our results.
For example, both of our independent vertices models can be thought of as maximal entropy distributions that are constrained by the first moments. What is special about the spectrum of common maximal entropy distributions (such as the Ising model), constrained by the higher moments? What is the interpretation of the diffusion equation for the Hodge Laplacian on the weighted sequence complex? What can be said about the probability distributions on sequence complexes that are Markovian? Can one utilize the Hodge Laplacian on sequences for construction of the language models, similar to how the Hodge Laplacian was used in simplicial neural networks
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
? We believe that our results establish a foundation for dimensionality reduction and Fourier analyses of probabilistic models, that are common in theoretical neuroscience and machine-learning.
