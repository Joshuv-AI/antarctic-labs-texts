Generalized quasi-shuffle products
Masataka Satoh

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the notion of generalized quasi-shuffle products and give a criterion for their associativity. These extend the quasi-shuffle products introduced by Hoffman, which are often used to describe the stuffle and shuffle product for multiple zeta values. For q q -analogues of multiple zeta values, the description of an analogue for the shuffle product can often not be described with the classical notion of quasi-shuffle products. We show that our generalization gives a natural extension to also include these types of products and we prove a generalization of a duality between the q q -shuffle product and the q q -stuffle product.

1.
Introduction

In this work, we will discuss the algebraic structure of multiple zeta values and their
q
-analogues. The main result is the introduction of generalized quasi-shuffle products, which we will use to describe the shuffle product for
q
-analogues and to give a new interpretation of the so-called dual stuffle product of multiple zeta values. The results obtained in this work are based on the authors master thesis at Nagoya University.

Multiple zeta values
are real numbers defined for
k_{1}\geq 2,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
by

\displaystyle\zeta(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=\sum_{m_{1}>m_{2}>\cdots>m_{r}>0}\frac{1}{m_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots m_{r}^{k_{r}}}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

In particular, for
r=1
the
\zeta(k_{1})
are the
Riemann zeta values
.
The multiple zeta values in depth two were first considered by Goldbach and Euler (
[
K
]
). In 1776s, the multiple zeta values were introduced by Euler (
[
E
]
). In 1992s, Hoffman defined multiple zeta values for any depth as above (
[
H1
]
).
The multiple zeta values satisfy various relations
(
e.g., the
double shuffle relation
. (
[
AK
]
,
[
IKZ
]
), the
duality relation
. (
[
Z2
, p. 510]
,
[
AK
, p. 13]
)
)
. In addition, among them, there are also known relations obtained through knot invariants (e.g.,
[
LM
]
).
Furthermore, it is known that the multiple zeta values also appear in mathematical physics (e.g.,
[
BK
]
). In the following, we introduce some of the properties of multiple zeta values.

The product of two multiple zeta values can be expressed as a linear combination of multiple zeta values. For example, by the definition as an iterated sum, we have

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\zeta(2)\zeta(3)&=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{m}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{n}^{3}}=\left(\sum_{m>n>0}+\sum_{n>m>0}+\sum_{m=n>0}\right)\frac{1}{{m}^{2}{n}^{3}}\\
&=\zeta(2,3)+\zeta(3,2)+\zeta(5)\,.\end{split}

To describe this product algebraically, one can use the notion of quasi-shuffle products introduced by Hoffman (
[
H2
]
,
[
HI
]
). We will recall this classical notion of quasi-shuffle products in Section
2.1
, where we introduce a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
\mathfrak{H}^{0}
spanned by words in the letters
z_{k}
for
k\geq 1
, which do not start with
z_{1}
. For example, an element in
\mathfrak{H}^{0}
is the word
z_{3}z_{4}z_{2}
, which will correspond to the multiple zeta value
\zeta(3,4,2)
. The space
\mathfrak{H}^{0}
can be equipped with the
stuffle product

\ast
and we have, for example,

(1.2)

\displaystyle z_{2}\ast z_{3}

\displaystyle=z_{2}z_{3}+z_{3}z_{2}+z_{5}\,.

But since multiple zeta values can also be expressed as iterated integrals, we also have

(1.3)

\displaystyle\zeta(2)\zeta(3)=3\zeta(3,2)+6\zeta(4,1)+\zeta(2,3).

This can also be described by using the theory of quasi-shuffle products, by defining the
shuffle product

\shuffle
on
\mathfrak{H}^{0}
. For example,

(1.4)

\displaystyle z_{2}\shuffle z_{3}

\displaystyle=3z_{3}z_{2}+6z_{4}z_{1}+z_{2}z_{3}.

We then can view the multiple zeta values as a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map
\zeta:\mathfrak{H}^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which is a algebra homomorphism from
\mathfrak{H}^{0}
to
\mathcal{Z}
with respect to both products
\ast
and
\shuffle
. In particular, applying
\zeta
to (
1.2
) we obtain (
1.1
) and from (
1.4
) we deduce (
1.3
).
we describe double shuffle relation
by comparing
\ast
with
\shuffle
and
define the
double shuffle
element
\operatorname{ds}(w,v)=w\shuffle v-w\ast v\in\ker\zeta
for
w,v\in\mathfrak{H}^{0}
. Conjecturally all relations among multiple zeta values are a consequence of the double shuffle relations after some regularization process (
[
AK
, p. 51]
,
[
IKZ
, Conjecture 1]
).
We also define an involution
\tau:\mathfrak{H}^{0}\rightarrow\mathfrak{H}^{0}
and show that
\zeta\circ\tau=\zeta
, which is called the
duality relation
.
In Section
2.2
, we discuss the algebraic structure of
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values. In general, by a
q
-analogue, we mean some
q
-series, which evaluates to multiple zeta values as
q\rightarrow 1
.
In recent years, different models of
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values were studied (e.g.,
[
Ba1
]
,
[
CEM
]
,
[
EMS
]
,
[
T1
]

[
Zh
]
). In this work, we consider the Schlesinger-Zudilin
q
-multiple zeta values defined for
k_{1}\geq 1,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0
by

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=\sum_{m_{1}>\cdots>m_{r}>0}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{q^{k_{j}m_{j}}}{[m_{j}]_{q}^{k_{j}}},

where
[m]_{q}=\frac{1-q^{m}}{1-q}
. Here we see that for
k_{1}\geq 2,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
we have
\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=\zeta(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
.
Notice that by definition, the product of two
q
-analogues can also be expressed by the stuffle product formula, e.g., similar to (
1.1
)

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(2)\zeta_{q}(3)=\zeta_{q}(2,3)+\zeta_{q}(3,2)+\zeta_{q}(5)\,.

To describe this product algebraically, we will define the
q
-stuffle product

*_{q}
as a quasi-shuffle product on a space
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}^{0}
, which can be seen as an extension of
\mathfrak{H}^{0}
.

Since for multiple zeta values, the double shuffle relations conjecturally give all relations, it is natural to ask how this situation is for
q
-analogues and if there is an analogue of the shuffle product. To describe an analogue of the shuffle product, there have been several different approaches for different models of
q
-analogues. Some of these use Rota-Baxter operators, and Jackson integrals (e.g.,
[
CEM
]
,
[
Z1
]
), and others use more algebraic approaches (e.g.,
[
Ba1
]
,
[
EMS
]
,
[
T1
]
,
[
T2
]
). In analogy to the conjecture for multiple zeta values, it is expected that the corresponding
q
-shuffle relations also give all relations among
q
-analogues (c.f.
[
Ba1
]
,
[
T1
]
).

In this work, we follow
[
EMS
]
and
[
T2
]
and replace the iterated integrals with a certain action, which we will describe in Section
2.3
. This analogue, and also all other variants for different models in the literature, can not be described by using the classical notion of quasi-shuffle products. This was one of the main motivations for this paper.

The main result of this paper is to introduce a natural generalization of the notion of quasi-shuffle products (Definition
3.4
) and show when this product is associative (Theorem
3.12
).
Through the generalized quasi-shuffle product, we can describe an analogue of the shuffle product for
q
-analogues, called the
q
-shuffle product
\shuffle_{q}
(Definition
3.16
). For example, this will lead to the following analogue of (
1.3
)

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(2)\zeta_{q}(3)=

\displaystyle\,\,3\zeta_{q}(3,2)+6\zeta_{q}(4,1)+\zeta_{q}(2,3)

\displaystyle+(1-q)\left(2\zeta_{q}(2,2)+7\zeta_{q}(3,1)+3\zeta_{q}(4,0)\right)

\displaystyle+(1-q)^{2}\left(2\zeta_{q}(3,0)+\zeta_{q}(2,1)\right)\,.

We give some examples regarding Theorem
3.12
after defining the generalized quasi-shuffle product. They contain the integral shuffle product (c.f.
[
T1
]
section 2.3).

Finally, we explain a certain duality between the
q
-stuffle product and the
q
-shuffle product, which was also discussed in
[
EMS
]
. More precisely, we introduce an involution
\sigma:\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
(Definition
3.21
), such that for
w,v\in\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}^{0}
, we have

\displaystyle w\shuffle_{q}v=\sigma(\sigma(w)\ast_{q}\sigma(v)).

At the end, we consider a multiple zeta value version and consider the dual stuffle product, defined by
\tau(\tau(w)\ast\tau(v))
for
w,v\in\mathfrak{H}^{0}
. Our final result will be to show that this dual stuffle product can also be described as a generalized quasi-shuffle product. Finally, using this generalized quasi-shuffle product, we get another expression for the double shuffle relations.

Multiple zeta values & q-analogues of multiple zeta values

2.1.
Quasi-shuffle products

In this section, we recall quasi-shuffle products, which were introduced by Hoffman in
[
H2
]
and later studied in more detail in
[
HI
]
. We will use this to describe the algebraic setting of multiple zeta values. The results in this sections are well-known and they can be found in
[

[

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a commutative ring (with unit) containing
\mathbb{Q}
and let
L
be an arbitrary set. We refer to the element in
L
as

\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle

\mathcal{R}

Let

-bilinear product on

Definition 2.1

For words

\ast_{\diamond}

(2.1)

\displaystyle aw\ast_{\diamond}bv=a(w\ast_{\diamond}bv)+b(aw\ast_{\diamond}v)+(a\diamond b)(w\ast_{\diamond}v)

and
{\bf 1}\ast_{\diamond}w=w\ast_{\diamond}{\bf 1}=w
for any
w\in\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle
.

Definition 2.2

For words
w,v\in\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle
,
a,b\in L
we define the
dual product

\overline{\ast_{\diamond}}

(2.2)

\displaystyle wa\overline{\ast_{\diamond}}vb=(w\overline{\ast_{\diamond}}vb)a+(wa\overline{\ast_{\diamond}}v)b+(w\overline{\ast_{\diamond}}v)(a\diamond b)

and
{\bf 1}\overline{\ast_{\diamond}}w=w\overline{\ast_{\diamond}}{\bf 1}=w
for any
w\in\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle
.

One can show that these two products actually coincide:

Theorem 2.3

(
[
Zu2
, Theorem
9
]
)
For words
w,v\in\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle
, we have

w\ast_{\diamond}v=w\overline{\ast_{\diamond}}v.

We omit this proof but later we will describe the more generalized version of Theorem
2.3
in Section
3.3
. The next theorem (
[
H2
]
,
[

]
) states that the quasi-shuffle product is associative and commutative when

Theorem 2.4

(
[

, Theorem 2.1]
,
[
HI
, Theorem 2.1]
)

-algebra.

We omit this proof because we can see more generalized version of this theorem later. It is also worth mentioning that

Next, we will give two examples of quasi-shuffle products, namely the shuffle product and the stuffle product.

q-analogue of multiple zeta values

3.1.
Definition and results

In this section, we introduce the generalized quasi-shuffle product, which is the main result of this paper. Also, we organize the conditions of the generalized quasi-shuffle product. Later, we define the
q
-shuffle product using the generalized quasi-shuffle product. Furthermore, we check that
\zeta_{q}

\mathcal{C}
-algebra homomorphism using

First, we introduce some notation and definitions for explaining the generalized quasi-shuffle product.

In the following, we recall that
\mathcal{R}
is a commutative ring and
L
is a letter of set.

Definition 3.1

We define the
\mathcal{R}
-linear map
F
by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rccc}F\colon&\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{R}L+\mathcal{R}{\bf 1}\\
&\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}&\longmapsto&\alpha_{1}\\
&{\bf 1}&\longmapsto&{\bf 1}\end{array}

and we define

for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and

F^{k}(x):=\overbrace{F(F(\cdots F}^{k}(x)\cdots)).

In the same way as above, we define the
\mathcal{R}
-linear map
\overline{F}
by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rccc}\overline{F}\colon&\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{R}L+\mathcal{R}{\bf 1}\\
&\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}&\longmapsto&\alpha_{n}\\
&{\bf 1}&\longmapsto&{\bf 1}\end{array}

Definition 3.2

We define the map
R
by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rccc}R\colon&\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\alpha_{1,i}\cdots\alpha_{n_{i},i}&\longmapsto&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\beta_{2,i}\cdots\beta_{n_{i},i},\end{array}

where
c_{i,j}\in\mathcal{C}
,
\alpha_{1,1},\cdots,\alpha_{n_{k},k},\beta_{1,1},\cdots,\beta_{n_{l},l}\in L
.
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\beta_{2,i}\cdots\beta_{n_{i},i}
is a collection of similar terms of
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{2,i}\cdots\alpha_{n_{i},i}
with the coefficient and the first letter removed.
In particular,
R(c\alpha)=R(c{\bf 1})={\bf 1}
for all
c\in\mathcal{R}
,
\alpha\in L
.
We define
R^{k}(x)
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
x\in\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle
by

\displaystyle R^{k}(x):=\overbrace{R(R(\cdots R}^{k}(x)\cdots)).

In the same way as above, we define the map
\overline{R}
by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rccc}\overline{R}\colon&\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\alpha_{1,i}\cdots\alpha_{n_{i},i}&\longmapsto&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\beta_{1,i}\cdots\beta_{n_{i}-1,i},\end{array}

The map
F
can be seen as taking the first letter of a word and the map
R
takes the rest of the word. In contrast, the map
\overline{F}
can be seen as taking the last letter of a word and the map
\overline{R}
takes the rest of the word.
Notice that by definition of

and
R

\overline{R}(w)\overline{F}(w)=w

\diamond
on the vector space of letters.
To define the generalized quasi-shuffle product we consider bilinear maps
\diamond:\mathcal{R}L\times\mathcal{R}L\rightarrow\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle

Definition 3.3

We call a
\mathcal{R}
-bilinear map

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rccc}\diamond\colon&\mathcal{R}L\times\mathcal{R}L&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{R}\langle L\rangle\\
\end{array}

(i)

decomposable

\alpha,

\beta\in L
with
R(\alpha\diamond\beta)\notin\mathcal{R}L

(ii)

commutative

\alpha,\beta\in L

Next, we also define the generalized dual product.

Definition 3.5

For a

Next, we describe the multiple zeta values version of Proposition

through the generalized quasi-shuffle product. Furthermore, we get a different expression for the double shuffle relations.
Let
L

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rccc}\diamond_{Sh}\colon&\mathbb{Q}L\times\mathbb{Q}L&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{Q}\langle L\rangle\\

&(y,y)&\longmapsto&-yy\\

for

and

a
and

and we have as a consequence of Proposition

Corollary 3.24

For
w,v\in\mathfrak{H}^{0}
we have

\displaystyle\tau(\tau(w)*\tau(v))=w\shuffle_{Sh}v.

Furthermore, we consider the definition of
\shuffle_{Sh}
, we can divide
\shuffle_{Sh}
as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha w\shuffle_{Sh}\beta v=\alpha w\shuffle\beta v+D(\alpha w,\beta v).

For

the

Proposition 3.25

For
w,v\in\mathfrak{H}^{0}
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{ds}(w,v)=-\tau(D(\tau(w),\tau(v))).

Proof.

This follows from the following direct calculation

\displaystyle\operatorname{ds}(w,v)

\displaystyle=w\shuffle v-w*v

\displaystyle=w\shuffle v-\tau(\tau(w)\shuffle_{Sh}\tau(v))

\displaystyle=w\shuffle v-\tau(\tau(w)\shuffle\tau(v)+D(\tau(w),\tau(v)))

\displaystyle=-\tau(D(\tau(w),\tau(v))).

Here we used that
w\shuffle v=\tau(\tau(w)\shuffle\tau(v))
, which follows from the definition of
\tau

∎
