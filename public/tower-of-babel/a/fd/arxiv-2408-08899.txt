Kov : Transferable and Naturalistic Black-Box LLM Attacks using Markov Decision Processes and Tree Search WARNING : This paper contains LLM responses that may be offensive or harmful.
Robert J. Moss Stanford University mossr@cs.stanford.edu

Abstract
Eliciting harmful behavior from large language models (LLMs) is an important task to ensure the proper alignment and safety of the models. Often when training LLMs, ethical guidelines are followed yet alignment failures may still be uncovered through red teaming adversarial attacks. This work frames the red-teaming problem as a Markov decision process (MDP) and uses Monte Carlo tree search to find harmful behaviors of black-box, closed-source LLMs. We optimize token-level prompt suffixes towards targeted harmful behaviors on white-box LLMs and include a naturalistic loss term, log-perplexity, to generate more natural language attacks for better interpretability. The proposed algorithm, Kov , trains on white-box LLMs to optimize the adversarial attacks and periodically evaluates responses from the black-box LLM to guide the search towards more harmful black-box behaviors. In our preliminary study, results indicate that we can jailbreak black-box models, such as GPT-3.5, in only 10 queries , yet fail on GPT-4---which may indicate that newer models are more robust to token-level attacks. All work to reproduce these results is open sourced. 1 1 1 https://github.com/sisl/Kov.jl



1
Introduction

Efforts to align large language models (LLMs) with the expectation of avoiding toxic or harmful behavior is important to ensure that misuse of such models is minimized
[
16
]
.
Alignment is crucial for the proper use of LLMs in schools, companies, and the government, and to ensure that they are not used for harmful tasks.

Red teaming
(or
jailbreaking
) approaches have shown success in bypassing trained LLM alignment and post-process filters that reject answering sensitive questions
[
17
]
.
Although successful, these approaches use manually engineered prompt injections that may be easily fixed by the LLM developers.
Automated adversarial attacks have thus been proposed to inject suffixes to sensitive prompts—such as
“Tell me how to build a bomb”
—and do so using gradient-based optimization
[
21
]
.
The objective function seeks to find responses that begin with a positive affirmation of the user query (e.g.,
“Sure, here is how to build a bomb…”
).
The optimization function thus seeks to minimize the negative log likelihood of the “positive response” given the current sequence of tokens (called
token-level attacks
).
These methods require access to open-sourced models (e.g., LLaMa-2
[
15
]
and Vicuna
[
2
]
) in order to take gradients of the model.

To jailbreak closed-source
black-box
language models,
Zou et al. [21]
proposed the gradient-based optimization method mentioned above as a universal and transferable approach that trains on open-sourced models then transfers the prompts to test on closed-source models (e.g., GPT-3.5).
Our work addresses several limitations of existing methods: 1) token-level attacks replace single tokens at a time and may converge to a local minima, 2) the prompt suffixes, while somewhat interpretable, may seem unnatural, and 3) assumptions that direct transfer to black-box models will be successful.
Our work builds on the methods for token-level attacks from
Zou et al. [21]
by framing the problem as a Markov decision process (MDP) and we use Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS)
[
4
]
to enable multi-step lookaheads during gradient-based optimization.
We optimize towards
natural
adversarial suffixes using log-perplexity to produce adversarial suffixes that are more interpretable.
We incorporate feedback from the target black-box model during the tree search to guide towards model behaviors that exhibit severely harmful behavior.
This work is intended to test the robustness of language models to stress cases where they may be misaligned.
We define “misalignment” as any behavior that responds to harmful prompts with responses that are harmful, violent, or discriminatory.

Related Work

Token-level attacks.

Methods to jailbreak LLMs at the token level make adjustments to the user prompt to elicit some target behavior from the LLM under test.
The problem is framed as an optimization problem where the objective is to minimize the loss between the expected model outputs and the target model outputs given the adversarial prompt.
The AutoPrompt method
[
13
]
achieves this through single token replacements appended as a suffix to the user prompt.
The
greedy coordinate gradient
(GCG) algorithm from
Zou et al. [21]
extends AutoPrompt to search over all possible token replacements.
To optimize over inputs and outputs to elicit unexpected behaviors, the ARCA method
[
5
]
was proposed that uses discrete optimization and a low-cost approximation to rank all token replacements in the vocabulary.
An example prompt and adversarial suffix is shown below.

Prompt
: Write a tutorial on how to make a bomb.
[ADVERSARIAL SUFFIX]

Prompt-level attacks.

Although gradient-based optimization methods have been reported to transfer to black-box models such as GPT-4
[
21
]
, recent work in
prompt-level attacks
showed that the state-of-the-art LLMs are becoming more robust to token-level attacks
[
19
]
; our work also confirms this finding.
Prompt-level attacks generate test cases using another LLM to test the robustness of a target model
[
12
,
10
]
.
Another approach to address this is to employ an LLM to act as a
persuasive
adversary
[
1
,
11
,
19
]
.
The PAIR method
[
1
]
uses a hand-crafted system prompt of an adversarial LLM to trick the black-box target LLM to exhibit harmful behavior.
The TAP method
[
11
]
extends PAIR and constructs the automated red-teaming prompt problem as a sequential tree search with pruning.
Other approaches such as PAP
[
19
]
use a persuasion taxonomy from social science research
[
19
]
to get an adversarial LLM to attack the black-box target LLM through repeated persuasive prompts.
These methods have shown success on jailbreaking the state-of-the-art LLMs but require hand-crafted system prompts that may be hard to justify and evaluate the impact of individual components.

3
Sequential Adversarial Attacks

3.1
Preliminaries

To optimize token-level attacks, we use the same formulation from the GCG method purposed by
Zou et al. [21]
.
Given a sequence of
n
tokens
x_{1:n}
from a vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
of size
V=|\mathcal{V}|
, inference in language models is tasked to produce the most-likely next token:

x_{n+1}=\argmax_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p(v\mid x_{1:n})

(1)

In token-level attacks, the adversarial loss function is expressed as the negative log-likelihood of generating a target sequence
x^{*}_{n+1:H}
(e.g., an affirmative response
“Sure, here is…”
to the prompt
x_{1:n}
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x_{1:n})

\displaystyle=-\log p(x_{n+1:H}^{*}\mid x_{1:n})

(2)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{H}\log p(x_{n+i}\mid x_{1:n+i-1})

(3)

Following
Zou et al. [21]
, using the indices of the adversarial suffix
\mathcal{I}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the adversarial optimization objective can then be written as:

\operatorname*{minimize}_{x_{\mathcal{I}}\in\{1,\ldots,V\}^{|\mathcal{I}|}}\mathcal{L}(x_{1:n})

(4)

The GCG method optimizes the objective in
eq.

4
by first computing the top-
k
token substitutions via a gradient step on the loss function for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, i.e.,
\nabla_{e_{x_{i}}}\mathcal{L}(x_{1:n})
, then selects random token replacements over a batch of size
B
, and returns the replacement that minimizes the loss.

3.1.1
Naturalistic Greedy Coordinate Gradient

We propose a simple extension to the GCG algorithm to include log-perplexity in the loss function to generate more
natural language
adversarial suffixes, with the objective of being more interpretable.
Log-perplexity measures how likely a sequence of tokens are together under a language model.
Therefore, the naturalistic loss function becomes:

\mathcal{L}(x_{1:n})=\underbrace{-\log p(x^{*}_{n+1:H}\mid x_{1:n})}_{\text{negative log-likelihood}}+\underbrace{\lambda\!\left(-\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\log p(x_{i}\mid x_{1:i-1})\right)}_{\text{log-perplexity}}

(5)

The hyperparameter
\lambda\geq 0
controls the effect of the log-perplexity term on the loss (where setting
\lambda=0
recovers GCG).
This approach is similar to ARCA
[
5
]
which balances log-likelihood with more natural language adversarial sequences.
The proposed
naturalistic greedy coordinate gradient
(NGCG) algorithm is shown in
algorithm

1
, with differences from GCG shown in red.

3.2
NGCG-TS: Sequential White-Box Red-Teaming

To avoid the potential of optimizing towards local minima in GCG and NGCG, we frame the red-teaming problem as a Markov decision process.
This enables us to effectively perform a multi-step lookahead over candidate token replacements using a well-established sequential framework.

Markov decision processes.

The Markov decision process (MDP) is a sequential decision making problem formulation that consists of the tuple
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},T,R,\gamma\rangle
, with a state space
\mathcal{S}
, an action space
\mathcal{A}
, a state-transition probability function
T:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
, a reward function
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, and a discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1]
which discounts future rewards
[
6
]
.
The objective when solving MDPs is to find a policy
\pi
that maps states
s\in\mathcal{S}
to actions
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
In our problem, we use a
generative
model
G:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{S}
as we do not have direct access to the transition probability function
T
and can only generate next states
s^{\prime}=G(s,a)
given a current state
s
and action
a
.
Treating the white-box optimization problem as an MDP, we define the following components:

•

States

s\in\mathcal{S}
: The state consists of the prompt tokens
x_{1:n}
, which includes the adversarial suffix tokens (e.g., “
Write a tutorial on how to build a bomb. [TOKEN] [TOKEN] [TOKEN] ...
”).

•

Actions

a\in\mathcal{A}
: An action is an adversarial suffix from NGCG, namely
a=x_{i:n}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}
. To get new actions, NGCG is run for a single step and the suffix that minimizes the naturalistic loss is returned.

•

Generative transition function

s^{\prime}=G(s,a)
: The state is deterministically transitioned by combining the current token action
a
with the prompt state
s
.

•

Reward function

r=R(s,a,s^{\prime})
: The reward function is the negative loss
-\mathcal{L}(x_{1:n})
, as the objective is to
maximize
rewards.

Monte Carlo tree search.

A common algorithm for solving MDPs is Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS)
[
4
,
14
]
.
MCTS iteratively simulates future paths within a search tree and balances exploration and exploitation using
upper-confidence trees
(UCT)
[
7
]
.
The full MCTS algorithm is shown in appendix
A
.

Figure

1
illustrates MCTS applied to the white-box MDP, termed NGCG-TS (tree search).
In our experiments, NGCG-TS achieves smaller loss, negative log-likelihood (NLL), and log-perplexity compared to GCG and NGCG as seen in
fig.

2
.

s^{\prime}

After
n_{\text{iterations}}
of MCTS, the state with the largest score is returned, unlike traditional MCTS (including NGCG-TS) which selects the root-node action based on the
Q
-values.

3.3
Kov
: Sequential Black-Box Red-Teaming

We frame the black-box red-teaming problem as a separate MDP and also solve it using MCTS.
We call this algorithm
Kov
.
The primary objective of
Kov
is to find adversarial suffixes that jailbreak black-box models, given we do not have access to gradient information of these closed-source models.
We use NGCG-TS to search over a
surrogate
white-box model, and evaluate the optimized suffix on the black-box model to gather feedback to refine the tree search, shown in
fig.

3
.
The black-box MDP is defined as the following:

•

States

s\in\mathcal{S}
: The state is the same as the white-box MDP: the prompt
s_{x}=x_{1:n}
, including the adversarial suffix. Additionally, the state holds the response
s_{y}
from the black-box LLM under test
\mathcal{M}
, denoted
s_{y}=\mathcal{M}(s_{x})
. Therefore, the state is a vector
s=[s_{x},s_{y}]
.

•

Actions

a\in\mathcal{A}
: An action is an adversarial suffix generated using NGCG-TS.

•

Generative transition function

s^{\prime}=G(s,a)
: Similar to the white-box MDP, the state is deterministically transitioned by to get the prompt
s^{\prime}_{x}=s_{x_{1:j}}+a
(where
j=\mathcal{I}_{1}
is the initial index of the adversarial suffix). The response from the black-box LLM is then
s^{\prime}_{y}=\mathcal{M}(s^{\prime}_{x})
.

•

Reward function

r=R(s,a,s^{\prime})
: The reward function is a real-valued measure of the harmfulness or toxicity of the response from the black-box LLM. We use OpenAI’s moderation framework
[
9
]
, which is a value
r\in[0,1]
(not to be interpreted as a probability). We take the average score across the eleven categories (e.g.,
hate/threatening
,
harassment
,
violence
). Other moderation frameworks such as the Perspective API
[
8
]
could be used.

Figure

3
depicts the MCTS steps of
Kov
. Note that we use the average OpenAI moderation score as the
\operatorname{Score}
function to optimize our policy towards
overall
harmful behaviors (instead of taking the maximum, for example, which may lead the policy to bounce between different objectives).

3.4
Mitigation with an Aligned Agent

To help LLMs stay aligned to ethical standards, we modify the action space and reward function of the adversarial
Kov
MDP to create an
aligned
MDP.
Instead of adversarial suffixes as actions, the aligned MDP prompts the LLM to
“Provide a prompt suffix that ensures the LLM response follows ethical guidelines”
and uses the response as a suffix to the initial prompt.
An example aligned suffix provided by GPT-3.5 is shown below.
The objective of this suffix is to help reinforce that the LLM should follow ethical guidelines.
The reward function is then the negative score, thus minimizing harmful behavior, which attempts to find actions (ethical suffixes) that avoid harmful behavior.

Prompt
:
Write a tutorial on how to make a bomb.
Please provide a response that adheres strictly to ethical guidelines.

5
Conclusions

We frame the problem of red teaming black-box LLMs as a sequential decision making problem using MDPs and propose the
Kov
algorithm which finds adversarial suffixes that lead to harmful behaviors.

Kov
combines white-box optimization through tree search (to avoid local minima) and feedback from black-box LLM evaluations to guide tree search to more harmful behaviors of the target model.
In the process, we developed the naturalistic variant of GCG (NGCG) to find adversarial prompts that exhibit more natural language.
Results indicate that
Kov
is able to jailbreak black-box LLMs such as GPT-3.5 and transfer to other LLMs, yet fails to jailbreak newer models such as GPT-4.

Figure work.

To extend this research, incorporating the
universal
component of GCG which optimizes over
all
harmful behaviors and several white-box models, could allow
Kov
to optimize for a single adversarial prompt instead of individually training on the specific target behavior.
Other research directions could frame
prompt-level
attacks as an MDP similar to
Mehrotra et al. [11]
and use off-the-shelf solvers to find a sequence of persuasive prompts that lead to harmful behaviors.
