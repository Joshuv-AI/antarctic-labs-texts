The cost of solving linear differential equations on a quantum computer: fast-forwarding to explicit resource counts
David Jennings, Matteo Lostaglio, Robert B. Lowrie, Sam Pallister, Andrew T. Sornborger

Abstract
How well can quantum computers simulate classical dynamical systems? There is increasing effort in developing quantum algorithms to efficiently simulate dynamics beyond Hamiltonian simulation, but so far exact running costs are not known. In this work, we provide two significant contributions. First, we provide the first non-asymptotic computation of the cost of encoding the solution to linear ordinary differential equations into quantum states – either the solution at a final time, or an encoding of the whole history within a time interval. Second, we show that the stability properties of a large class of classical dynamics can allow their fast-forwarding, making their quantum simulation much more time-efficient. We give a broad framework to include stability information in the complexity analysis and present examples where this brings several orders of magnitude improvements in the query counts compared to state-of-the-art analysis. From this point of view, quantum Hamiltonian dynamics is a boundary case that does not allow this form of stability-induced fast-forwarding. To illustrate our results, we find that for homogeneous systems with negative log-norm, the query counts lie within the curves 11900 ​ T ​ log ⁡ ( T ) 11900\sqrt{T}\log(T) and 10300 ​ T ​ log ⁡ ( T ) 10300T\log(T) for T ∈ [ 10 6 , 10 15 ] T\in[10^{6},10^{15}] and error ϵ = 10 − 10 \epsilon=10^{-10} , when outputting a history state.

Introduction

I.1
Setting the stage

One of the original motivations for quantum computing was that simulating quantum dynamics efficiently requires quantum systems. However, a substantial fraction of today’s high-performance computing resources are used to simulate classical physics, such as fluid dynamics and high-temperature plasmas, which may be represented as dynamical systems of coupled degrees of freedom
1
1

1

For example, the DOE INCITE 2023 projects allocated over
55\times 10^{6}
core-hours over 56 projects, of which over 40% focused on plasma and fluid-dynamics problems, see https://www.doeleadershipcomputing.org/awardees/.

. It is then crucial to investigate to what extent these simulations may be sped up by quantum computing.

Assessing the potential of quantum computing in this computational space requires moving from the current asymptotic counts analysis to detailed resource estimates of quantum algorithms for differential equations. This is what we set out to do in this work, see e.g. Fig.
1
. We leverage recent non-asymptotic resource estimates for quantum linear solvers
Costa et al. 2022
;
Jennings et al. 2023
to compute for the first time rigorous upper bounds on the non-asymptotic cost of running a quantum algorithm for solving dynamical problems beyond Hamiltonian simulation. What is more, we also find that the stability properties of a large class of classical dynamical systems give rise to costs
sublinear
(at best, square-root) in the target simulation time.
For example, for a natural class of classical dynamics, we find
1
–
4
orders of magnitude improvement over best available analysis, for reasonable parameter regimes and including constant prefactors, see Fig.
1
. Hamiltonian dynamics is a boundary case in our stability analysis, hence preventing this form of fast-forwarding, in agreement with the no-fast-forwarding theorem.

We study systems of linear ordinary differential equations (ODEs):

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)=A\boldsymbol{x}(t)+\boldsymbol{b},\quad\textrm{ with }\;\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{x}^{0},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
is the
N

is an
N\times N

\boldsymbol{b}
is an
N
-dimensional vector, also taken to be time-independent. When

, with
H
Hermitian, and
\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the problem in Eq. (
1
) reduces to the well-known quantum Hamiltonian dynamics.

The main interest of linear ODE systems is as a component of a computational pipeline. Linear ODE systems are obtained after spatial discretization of linear partial differential equations (PDEs) – e.g., the Schrödinger, heat, Fokker-Planck or linearized Vlasov equations, among many others. Linear ODEs are also obtained after applying linear representation techniques such as Carleman linearization
Liu et al. 2021
, level-sets
Jin and Liu 2022
, Koopman von Neumann/Liouville embeddings
Koopman 1931
;
Kowalski 1997
;
Joseph 2020
;
Jin et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Gonzalez-Conde and Sornborger 2023
, and perturbative homotopy methods
Xue et al. 2021
for nonlinear problems.

There are different approaches that could be employed to encode the solution of Eq. (
1
) in a quantum computer. For example, one such method is to find a mapping to a Hamiltonian simulation problem
Babbush et al. 2023
or to a linear combination of Hamiltonian simulation problems
Jin et al. 2022
;
An et al. 2023
;
Apers et al. 2022
. Another approach is to encode the problem into a matrix equation of the form
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{c}
, which can be solved with a quantum linear solver algorithm
Harrow et al. 2009
;
Berry 2014
;
Berry et al. 2017
;
Costa et al. 2019
;
Childs and Liu 2020
;
Linden et al. 2022
;
Berry and Costa 2022
;
Krovi 2022
;
Bagherimehrab et al. 2023
. A more recent development is to solve the system of equations via a time-marching algorithm
Fang et al. 2023
. Here we shall follow the most widely-studied approach of embedding the dynamical problem into a linear system of equations. This has been used to analyze the heat equation
Linden et al. 2022
, the wave equation
Costa et al. 2019
, the linearized Vlasov equation
Ameri et al. 2023

For none of the above approaches are detailed resource estimates available. Our work is significant in that it provides ready-to-use, analytical estimates that can be leveraged to evaluate the cost for all of these use-cases, with the extra advantage that our analysis unlocks better scaling in the simulation time
T
compared to state-of-the-art in certain regimes.

We distinguish two different types of solutions to the dynamical problem: on the one hand, given
\epsilon>0

\epsilon

\ket{x_{F}}\propto\boldsymbol{x}(T),\quad\textrm{at some time final }T\geq 0,

(2)

[0,T]
. Then

\epsilon

\ket{x_{H}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M+1}}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\ket{x(t_{m})}\otimes\ket{m},

(3)

I.2
Stability

Our analysis relates stability properties of the dynamical system to algorithm complexity, so let us recall some notions of stability (also see Appendix
A.1
).
The
spectral abscissa
is the largest real part of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
of
A
:

\alpha(A):=\max_{i}\textrm{Re}\,\lambda_{i}(A)\;.

(4)

For finite-dimensional systems, we have
stable dynamics
when
\alpha(A)<0
, in which case the
Lyapunov inequality
is known to hold:

PA+A^{\dagger}P<0,\quad\textrm{ for some }P>0.

(5)

The stability of the system can be quantified via the condition number of
P
, given by
\kappa_{P}:=\|P\|\|P^{-1}\|
, and the
P
-log-norm

\mu_{P}(A)=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\neq 0}\textrm{Re}\frac{\langle A\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{P}}{\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{P}},

(6)

where
\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{P}:=\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}P\boldsymbol{y}
.
In terms of these quantities, one can bound the exponentiated matrix as (Theorem 3.35 in
Plischke 2005
).

\|e^{At}\|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{P}}e^{\mu_{P}(A)t},\quad\textrm{for all $t>0$.}

(7)

If in particular the above holds with
P=I
, then we have the special class of dynamics with negative (Euclidean) log-norm
\mu(A):=\lambda_{\textrm{max}}(A+A^{\dagger})/2<0
, where
\lambda_{\textrm{max}}(X)
is the largest eigenvalue of
X

Söderlind 2006
.

The case where
\alpha(A)\geq 0
includes systems for which the solution may grow arbitrarily large as we increase
T
, but it also contains cases where this does not occur. Notably quantum Hamiltonian dynamics is a boundary case, for which
\alpha(A)=\alpha(-iH)=0
. As in Ref.
Krovi 2022
, we shall bound the dynamics in the case
\alpha(A)\geq 0
with a constant
C_{\textrm{max}}
satisfying

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\|e^{At}\|\leq C_{\textrm{max}},

(8)

where
C_{\textrm{max}}=1
for all times
T\geq 0
for quantum Hamiltonian dynamics. In terms of the mathematical conditions, the
\alpha(A)\geq 0
case can be understood as the formal limit
\mu_{P}(A)\rightarrow 0^{-}
and
\kappa_{P}\rightarrow C_{\textrm{max}}^{2}
. In summary, we assume
(\kappa_{P},\mu_{P}(A))
, or a bound thereof, as input parameters.

I.4
Summary of the algorithm

The solution of Eq. (
1
) can be formally written as

\boldsymbol{x}(t)=e^{At}\boldsymbol{x}^{0}+\int_{0}^{T}dt^{\prime}e^{At^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{b}.

(10)

By discretizing time, we can recursively approximate the solution at time
m
from that at time
m-1
via a Taylor series truncated at degree
k
:

\boldsymbol{x}(m)\approx\boldsymbol{x}^{m}:=T_{k}(A)\boldsymbol{x}^{m-1}+S_{k}(A)\boldsymbol{b},

(11)

where
T_{k}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}x^{j}/j!
and
S_{k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}x^{j-1}/j!
. One can upper bound the required
k
such that the time-discretization relative error is at most
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
, meaning

\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}-\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|\leq\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|,\quad m=0,\dots,T.

(12)

Exploiting Theorem 3 in Ref.
Krovi 2022
, we find a value for the truncation degree,
k
, that suffices, see Appendix
A.2
. In particular,
k=O\left(\log\frac{\lambda(T)}{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\right)
.

The recursive equations are embedded, as in Ref.
Berry et al. 2017
, into a linear system
L
whose blocks only contain matrices proportional to
A
or the identity. This linear system takes the form
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{c}
, where
\boldsymbol{y}
encodes the dynamical solution at all times
[0,T]
. In fact, an idling parameter
p
in the linear system allows us to embed the trivial evolution generated by
A=0
for
p
time-steps at the end, which increases the amplitude
of the final state component. This is useful if we are trying to output
\ket{x_{F}}
.

This linear system of equations is solved via QLSA, see the schematics in Fig.
2
. The unitaries O1, O2, O3 are used to construct a block-encoding of the linear system matrix
L
and the vector of constants
\boldsymbol{c}
. The number of times these need to be called in the QLSA algorithm is classically precomputed as we shall see in Theorem
2
.
The output solution is post-processed via amplitude amplification as needed, to get either a quantum state encoding an approximation of the final state (
2
) or of the history state (
3
).

To compute the (non-asymptotic) query count upper bounds of the ODE algorithm we need to construct the linear system block-encoding, determine the QLSA parameters, analyze the output processing success probability and how exactly the various error sources (time-discretization, finite precision of the QLSA) compound. Here we report the main results.

I.5
Asymptotic complexity and fast-forwarding.

The asymptotic complexity obtained through our new analysis improves over state-of-the-art as shown
in Table
2
. Note that Berry
et al.

Berry et al. 2017
assume that
A
has
\alpha(A)\leq 0

whose condition number

(\kappa_{P},\mu_{P})=(\kappa_{V}^{2},0)

However for stable systems,
\alpha(A)<0

One might expect that simulating ODE systems could in general be harder than the Schrödinger equation, since one incurs various overheads in transforming the problem into a form suitable for a quantum computer. Here we sharpen this relation by showing that quantum dynamics are at the boundary of stable dynamics, and that for the latter the condition number of the linear system associated to the ODE scales
sublinearly
in the target simulation time
T
:

Theorem 1

(Fast-forwarding of stable systems)
.

Let
A
be a stable matrix and let
P>0
be any matrix that satisfies the Lyapunov inequality
PA+A^{\dagger}P<0
. Then outputting the final state requires

\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa_{P}}\bar{g}(T)T^{3/4}\log[\lambda(T)/\epsilon]),

(13)

queries to the oracles for
A
,
\boldsymbol{b}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}
,
while outputting the history state requires

\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa_{P}}T^{1/2}\log[\lambda(T)/\epsilon]),

(14)

queries to these oracles.

See Appendix
G
for the full technical statement and proof. This gives sublinear scaling in

\textrm{Pr}_{H}\geq\frac{K}{K-1+I_{0}(2)}\geq\begin{cases}0.058\quad\boldsymbol{b}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\\
0.438\quad\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{0},\end{cases}

(17)

and for outputting the final state compute

\displaystyle\textrm{Pr}_{F}\geq\frac{1}{(1-\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{(p+1)K})+(T+1)\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{(p+1)K}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}{1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\right)^{2}\bar{g}^{2}}.

(18)

[7]

Compute
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}=\epsilon\frac{\textrm{Pr}_{H,F}}{4+\epsilon}
.

[8]

Compute
\mathcal{Q}_{QLSA}=\mathcal{Q}_{QLSA}(\omega_{\tilde{L}},\kappa_{L},\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}})
, the best available query counts for QLSA given the parameters
\omega_{\tilde{L}},\kappa_{L},\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
. Here we use the closed formula for
\mathcal{Q}_{QLSA}(\omega,\kappa,\epsilon)
from Ref.
Jennings et al. 2023
, also provided in Appendix
H
.

[9]

Compute
\mathcal{A}(\textrm{Pr}_{H,F})
, the query counts for amplitude amplification from success probability
\textrm{Pr}_{H,F}
(see, e.g., Ref.
Yoder et al. 2014
), or
\mathcal{A}(\textrm{Pr}_{H,F})=1/\textrm{Pr}_{H,F}
if we sample till success.

[10]

Finally,
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}_{QLSA}\times\mathcal{A}
.

The total logical qubit count for the algorithm is
a+12+\lceil\log_{2}[((T+1)(k+1)+p)N]\rceil
.

This gives the first detailed counts for solving ODEs on a quantum computer, a crucial step to determining what problems of interest could be run on early generations of fault-tolerant quantum computers. These results also provide explicit targets for future work to improve on.

II
The algorithm

The algorithm is based on the novel analysis of Ref.
Berry et al. 2017
.

(1) Linear system encoding:
We introduce
3
registers
\ket{m,j,n}
, and proceed similarly to Ref.
Berry et al. 2017
. Register
m
is a clock degree of freedom, labeling the solution at each time-step within
[0,T]
, plus potentially further idling steps to increase the amplitude of the solution at the final state, if necessary. It takes values
m=0,\dots,T+p/(k+1)
, where
p
is a multiple of
k+1
to be fixed. The
j
register is used to compute Taylor sums,
j=0,\dots,k
. Finally,
n
labels the components of the vector
\boldsymbol{x}
,
n=1,\dots,N
. We encode data about the solution to the ODE problem into the solution
\boldsymbol{y}:=L^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}
of a linear system (Appendix
B
):

L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{c},

(19)

where
L=L_{1}+L_{2}+L_{3}
, and

\displaystyle L_{1}:=

\displaystyle I\otimes I\otimes I,

(20)

\displaystyle L_{2}:=

\displaystyle-\sum_{m=0}^{T-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ket{m,j}\!\!\bra{m,j-1}\otimes\frac{A}{j}

\displaystyle-\theta(p)\sum_{m=T}^{T+\frac{p}{k+1}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ket{m,j}\!\!\bra{m,j-1}\otimes I,

(21)

\displaystyle L_{3}:=

\displaystyle-\sum_{m=0}^{T-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\ket{m+1,0}\!\!\bra{m,j}\otimes I,

(22)

with the Heaviside function
\theta(p)=0
for
p=0
and
\theta(p)=1
for
p>0
.
The term
L_{2}
generates Taylor components, which are summed at each
m
by
L_{3}
to generate a truncated Taylor series that approximates the dynamics up to
m=T
. If
p\geq k+1
, from
m=T
to
m=T+p/(k+1)-1
and for all
j=0,\dots,k
, so
p
steps overall, the term
L_{2}
generates ‘idling’, which allows for an increase of the amplitude of the final state component. In Eq. (
19
) we set

\boldsymbol{c}=[\overbrace{\boldsymbol{x}^{0},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{0}}_{k}}^{m=0},\dots,\overbrace{\boldsymbol{0},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{0}}_{k}}^{m=M-1},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{0}}_{1+p}].

(23)

It can be shown that the solution to this system can be split up as
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}+\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{junk}}
, where

\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{0},\boldsymbol{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x}^{1},\boldsymbol{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{0},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{x}^{T},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}^{T}}_{1+p})

(24)

and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{junk}}
is an orthogonal ‘junk’ component, that is unwanted, and can be removed by post-selection.
When we output the history state, we set
p=0
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}
(as a normalized quantum state
\ket{x^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}_{H}}
)
provides a discrete approximation to the exact history state encoded in Eq. (
3
) (Appendix
A.2
):

\|\ket{x^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}_{H}}-\ket{x_{H}}\|\leq 2\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}\;.

(25)

Instead, when we output the final state, we set
p>0
and target the components
\boldsymbol{x}^{T}
with amplitude amplification.

(2) Solving the linear system:
Eq. (
19
) is tackled using the best available asymptotic
Costa et al. 2022
and non-asymptotic
Jennings et al. 2023
query complexity upper bound for QLSA. The running cost depends on three parameters:

(a)

The cost of constructing a block-encoding of
L
from that of
A
.

(b)

An upper bound on the condition number of
L
, i.e., on
\kappa_{L}:=\|L\|\|L^{-1}\|
.

(c)

The QLSA does not output the ideal solution
\ket{y}
, but a quantum state
\rho
which is
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
-close to it in
1-
norm. The cost depends on
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
.

All three will be determined from the ODE parameters.

(a) Constructing the linear system block-encoding.

We prove that
\|L\|\leq\omega_{k}:=\sqrt{k+1}+2
. Hence we define
\tilde{L}=L/\omega_{k}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}=\boldsymbol{c}/\omega_{k}
and solve the problem
\tilde{L}\boldsymbol{y}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}
. This has
\|\tilde{L}\|\leq 1
, as normally required by QLSA.

The detailed running cost of a QLSA is in terms of calls to a unitary block-encoding
U_{\tilde{L}}
of
\tilde{L}
and a state preparation unitary
U_{\tilde{c}}
of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}
. These need to be constructed from the available unitaries
U_{A}
,
U_{b}
,
U_{0}
(details in Appendix
C
). Concerning
U_{\tilde{c}}
, it can be constructed with a single call to both
U_{0}
and
U_{b}
. As for
U_{\tilde{L}}
,
we construct an
(\omega_{\tilde{L}},a+6,0)
-block-encoding of
\tilde{L}
with a single call to
U_{A}
, where
\omega_{\tilde{L}}=(1+\sqrt{k+1}+\omega)/\omega_{k}
, as given in Theorem
2
. The QLSA requires
6
further ancilla qubits, as well as
\lceil\log_{2}[((T+1)(k+1)+p)N]\rceil
qubits to encode
\tilde{L}
.

(b) Condition number bound.
Recall that the Lyapunov analysis leads to stability parameters
(\kappa_{P},\mu_{P}(A))
. In Appendix
D
we find the upper bound on
\kappa_{L}
in terms of the stability properties as given in Theorem
2
, inequality (
16
).

We set
p=0
for the case where we output the history state. For the case where we output the final state, we take
p=\lceil T^{1/2}/(k+1)\rceil(k+1)
in the case that
A
is stable, and
p=\lceil T/(k+1)\rceil(k+1)

otherwise. For both final and history state, we then have
\kappa_{L}=O(T^{1/2}k)
in the stable case and
\kappa_{L}=O(Tk)
otherwise. The better scaling of this bound leads to the improvements in Table
2
.

Also note that in Appendices
B
and
D
we provide a broader analysis of idling, where we allow varying amplifications at each discrete time-step, specified by integers
\{p_{m}:m=0,1,\dots,T\}
. This allows us to increase the amplitude of selected terms
\ket{x(t_{m})}
in the history state (
3
), which can be understood as applying a filter to the dynamical data.

(c) Required precision.
To determine
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
, we first need to analyze the output processing.

(3) Output processing:
We first present an analysis assuming we access the idealized linear system solution
\ket{y}
, and then consider the effect of finite precision.

Idealized analysis.
The ideal solution of the linear system encodes ODE data in the sense that we recover a state close to
\ket{x_{H,F}}
by performing a projective measurement
\{\Pi_{H,F},I-\Pi_{H,F}\}
on the auxiliary/clock registers. Specifically, if

\Pi_{H}=I\otimes\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\otimes I,\quad\Pi_{F}=\sum_{m=T}^{T+p/(k+1)}\ket{m}\!\!\bra{m}\otimes I\otimes I,

we have

\Pi_{H}\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}\Pi_{H}=\textrm{Pr}_{H}\ket{x^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}_{H}}\!\!\bra{x_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}},

(26)

where
\|\ket{x^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}_{H}}-\ket{x_{H}}\|\leq 2\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
from Eq. (
3
) and

\textrm{Tr}_{CA}[\Pi_{F}\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}\Pi_{F}]=\textrm{Pr}_{F}\ket{x^{T}}\!\!\bra{x^{T}},

(27)

where the trace discards clock and ancilla qubits and
\|\ket{x^{T}}-\ket{x_{F}}\|\leq 2\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
(Appendix
A.2
).

The values
\textrm{Pr}_{H,F}
are success probabilities of the corresponding measurements.
For outputting the history state we obtain the success probability in (
17
),
so the overhead to repeat the measurement until success or via amplitude amplification is
O(1)
.
For output of the final state we have the expressions given in Theorem
2
, which imply

\textrm{Pr}_{F}\geq\begin{cases}O(\bar{g}^{-2}T^{-1/2})\quad\textrm{for $A$ stable,}\\
O(\bar{g}^{-2})\quad\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(28)

Using amplitude amplification we have an overhead of
O(1/\sqrt{\textrm{Pr}_{F}})
repetitions.

Error analysis.
We now consider the effect of the finite precision
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
of the QLSA and go back to point (c) of the previous section, i.e. answer the question of what linear solver error
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
suffices, as well as what discretization error
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
we need to target, given a total error budget
\epsilon
.

In Appendix
F
, we find that the protocol presented (including the output processing) returns a quantum state
\tilde{\sigma}_{H,F}
satisfying

\|\sigma_{H,F}-\ket{x_{H,F}}\!\!\bra{x_{H,F}}\|_{1}\leq\frac{2\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}}{\textrm{Pr}_{H,F}-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}}+4\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}},

(29)

with a success probability at least
\textrm{Pr}_{H,F}-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
. If we choose the two terms in (
29
) to have equal magnitude, then it suffices to take errors as given in Theorem
2
.

III
Outlook

Quantum computers are expected to be a disruptive technology for the simulation of quantum mechanical problems. We presently do not know if quantum computing will have a similar impact on important classes of classical dynamical problems. What we do know, however, is that there is no scarcity of computational problems of broad relevance, such as in fluid dynamics and plasma physics, which are often under-resolved on today’s largest computers and will remain so in the foreseeable future. If we could identify such use cases and provide a fully compiled quantum algorithm with reasonable resource estimates, then the scientific and technological value of quantum computers would be substantially increased.
If this development follows a similar line to that for quantum chemistry, increasingly better algorithms and resource estimates will be required, likely starting from very high resource counts and then pushing numbers down for specific problem instances. We see the present work as a step in this direction. In giving the first detailed query count analysis for the general problem of solving linear differential equations on a quantum computer, we hope to set a signpost for improved resource estimates in the future, and also set the stage for a detailed analysis of the cost of solving
nonlinear
differential equations. The fast-forwarding results, furthermore, suggest these algorithms may be significantly less costly than previously thought in certain regimes.

Appendix A
Analysis of the dynamics

A.1
Stability and Lyapunov relations

For this discussion we refer to Ref.
Plischke 2005
;
Van Loan 2006
for further details, but many more sources can be found on the topic.

In general, we do not want the dynamics
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
to blow-up in terms of norm as
t\rightarrow\infty
. A necessary condition for this to happen for every initial condition is that the matrix
A
does not have any eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(A)
with positive real parts. This motivates the definition
\alpha(A):=\max_{i}\{Re(\lambda_{i}(A))\}
, and if
\alpha(A)<0
, then
A
is said to be
stable
. We have that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|e^{At}\|=0,

(30)

if and only if
A
is stable. A necessary and sufficient condition for stability is that there is a matrix
P>0
(strictly positive) such that

PA+A^{\dagger}P=-Q,

(31)

for some
Q>0
. This is known as the
Lyapunov equation
. This condition is related to the existence of quadratic Lyapunov functions for the dynamics generated by
A
. Indeed, it can be shown that for the homogeneous linear system
\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}(t)}{dt}=A\boldsymbol{x}(t)
, Eq. (
31
) holds if and only if the quadratic form
V(\boldsymbol{x}):=\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}P\boldsymbol{x}
obeys

\frac{d}{dt}V(\boldsymbol{x}(t))=-\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}(t)Q\boldsymbol{x}(t)<0,

(32)

along all trajectories
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
. The non-negative quadratic form
V(\boldsymbol{x})
can be viewed as a form of energy function, and from the fact that
\dot{V}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}(t)Q\boldsymbol{x}(t)\leq-cV(\boldsymbol{x})
, for some constant
c>0
it follows from Grönwall’s inequality that there is another constant
C
such that

\|\boldsymbol{x}(t)\|\leq Ce^{-ct/2}\|\boldsymbol{x}(0)\|,

(33)

for all
t\geq 0
. This is called global exponential stability for the dynamics.
Note that the above discussion still holds in the
\alpha(A)=0
case, but the strict inequality
c>0
is replaced with the non-strict one
c\geq 0
, allowing
c=0
and
C
not constant.

Global exponential stability relates to the trajectories of the dynamics, but for the analysis of our algorithm, we instead require conditions on the exponentiated matrix
A
. While, asymptotically, homogeneous stable systems decay, they can still display transient increases in the norm
\|e^{At}\|
for finite times. We can provide sharper measures of the early-time regime via the
logarithmic norm
(or log-norm for short) of
A
, denoted
\mu(A)
and defined as the initial growth rate

\mu(A):=\frac{d}{dt^{+}}\|e^{At}\|_{t=0}=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}(\|I+Ah\|-1).

(34)

The definition depends on the particular choice of norm used, and unless otherwise stated we shall assume the operator norm is used.
A significant property of the log-norm is that it is the smallest
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\|e^{At}\|\leq e^{\beta t}
for all
t\geq 0
. In the case of the operator norm, its value can also be calculated from

\mu(A)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\textrm{max}}(A+A^{\dagger})=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\neq 0}\textrm{Re}\,\frac{\langle A\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}{\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle},

(35)

where
\lambda_{\textrm{max}}(X)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of any Hermitian matrix
X
. If
\mu(A)<0
then the system is stable, but even if
\mu(A)>0
stability can still occur. However, in this case the bound
\|e^{At}\|\leq e^{\mu(A)t}
is no longer useful. To account for this regime one can introduce a different log-norm:

\mu_{P}(A)=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\neq 0}\textrm{Re}\,\frac{\langle PA\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}{\langle P\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle},

(36)

the
log-norm with respect to the elliptical norm induced by

P
. This can be seen as an initial growth rate with respect to the norm induced by
P
[Lemma 3.31, Ref.
Plischke 2005
]. Note that stability implies
\mu_{P}(A)<0
for some
P>0
. In particular, denote the condition number of
P
by
\kappa_{P}
. One then has [Theorem 3.35, Ref.
Plischke 2005
]

\|e^{At}\|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{P}}e^{\mu_{P}(A)t},

(37)

which accounts for potential increases of
\|e^{At}\|
in a transient regime.

Finally, we note that in the case of Schrödinger evolution under
A=-iH
for some Hamiltonian
H
, we have a constant norm for the dynamics, and so the system is not stable in the above sense – in the literature this case is called “marginally stable". Quantum dynamics, or any other linear dynamics with
A
having eigenvalues with vanishing real parts, are not stable under arbitrarily small perturbations in
A
, while stable systems do allow small perturbations while still remaining stable.

A.2
Time discretization error analysis

We now describe the formal solution to the ODE, and the discretized solution that our algorithm will return.
The solution of Eq. (
1
) can be formally written as

\boldsymbol{x}(t)=e^{At}\boldsymbol{x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{At^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{b},

(38)

for any
t\in[0,T]
.
We proceed by splitting the time interval up into
M
equal-sized sub-intervals of width
h
, so that we can approximate the dynamics at each of these time points. The exact solution data at these points are
\boldsymbol{x}(0),\boldsymbol{x}(h),\dots,\boldsymbol{x}(mh),\dots,\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)=\boldsymbol{x}(T)
. We shall approximate
\boldsymbol{x}(mh)
with
\boldsymbol{x}^{m}
, which is a vector obtained from a discrete sequence of evolutions obtained from a truncated Taylor series for the exponential
e^{At}
. In particular we use

\displaystyle T_{k}(Ah)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{j!}(Ah)^{j}

\displaystyle S_{k}(Ah)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j!}(Ah)^{j-1},

(39)

to approximate the exponentials needed to order
k
.
The discretized dynamics is now defined recursively by
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}:=\boldsymbol{x}(0)
, and

\boldsymbol{x}^{m}:=T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{x}^{m-1}+S_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{b},

(40)

for all
m=1,\dots,M
. The following result gives the condition on the truncation order
k
so as to obtain a target relative error
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
.

Lemma 3

(Time-discretization error, Theorem 3 of Ref.
Krovi 2022
)
.

Let
r(T)
be a constant such that
r(T)\geq\|\boldsymbol{b}\|/\|\boldsymbol{x}(t)\|
for all
t\in[0,T]
. If we choose the integer
k
such that

(k+1)!\geq\frac{Me^{3}}{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}(1+Te^{2}r(T)),

(41)

then it follows that the relative time-discretization error obeys

\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)-\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|\leq\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|,

(42)

for all
m=0,1,\dots M
.

We can provide a sufficient value of
k
required from the following lemma.

Lemma 4

(Sufficient truncation scale)
.

The choice

k=\left\lceil\frac{3\log(x_{*})/2+1}{1+\log(\log(x_{*})/2e+1/e)}-1\right\rceil

(43)

with
x^{*}=\frac{Te^{3}}{h\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}(1+Te^{2}r(T))
suffices to obtain a relative time-discretization error
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
over all
M=T/h
time-steps of the dynamics. In particular
k=O(\log\left(\lambda(T)/\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}\right))
.

Proof.

From Lemma
3
it suffices to take
(k+1)!\geq x_{*}
.
From the Stirling approximation we have that

n!\geq\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n},

(44)

so it suffices to take
(\frac{k+1}{e})^{k+1}\geq x^{*}
, i.e.,
k=\lceil f^{-1}(x_{*})\rceil-1
where we define
f(x)=\left(\frac{x}{e}\right)^{x}
. However we have that for any
x>0

f^{-1}(x)=\exp\left[W\left(\frac{\log x}{e}\right)+1\right],

(45)

where
W(x)
is the principal branch of the Lambert W-function. We now use the upper bound

e^{W(x)}\leq\frac{x+y}{1+\log y},

(46)

which holds for any
x>-1/e
and any
y>1/e

Hoorfar and Hassani 2008
.

In our case this reads

e^{W(\log(x)/e)}\leq\frac{\log(x)/e+y}{1+\log y}

(47)

We now take
y=1/e+\log(x)/2e
, and therefore have that

f^{-1}(x)-1\leq e^{W(\log(x)/e)+1}-1\leq\frac{3\log(x)/2+1}{1+\log(\log(x)/2e+1/e)}-1=O(\log(x)).

(48)

We now set
x=x_{*}
and round up to the nearest integer to obtain the result claimed.
∎

This expression shows that
k
grows at worst logarithmically in time,
k=O(\log(T))
, for the homogeneous ODE case, while for the inhomogenous case one can generally obtain
k=O(T)
if
\lambda(T)
decreases exponentially in
T
, as we discuss later.

For homogeneous regimes of interest the value of
k
is quite mild. For example, taking
\lambda(T)=0
,
M=10^{6}
and
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}=10^{-9}
the bound returns
k=19
(rounding up
18.2
). Directly solving numerically the original bound in Lemma
3
for
k
gives
k=18
, so the analytical bound is relatively tight.

The recursively defined dynamics generates terms such as
T_{k}(Ah)^{m}
, which approximate
(e^{Ah})^{m}=e^{Amh}
for all
m=0,1,\dots,M
. For the condition number analysis we need an estimate on how well the truncated expression
T_{k}(Ah)^{m}
approximates the exact exponential
e^{Amh}
for the continuous dynamics. The next result determines the relative error for these matrices in the discrete dynamics.

Lemma 5

(Truncation error for dynamics, Lemma 13 of Ref.
Krovi 2022
)
.

Let
k
obey the time-discretization condition given by Equation (
41
). Then for all
m=0,1,\dots M
we have the following relative error for the norm
\|T_{k}(Ah)^{m}\|
relative to
\|e^{Ahm}\|
.

\|T_{k}(Ah)^{m}\|\leq\|e^{Ahm}\|(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}),

(49)

Note that so far we discussed time-discretization errors at the level of unnormalized solution vectors, but these can be readily translated into errors on the normalized quantum states.

Lemma 6
.

For any two vectors

\ket{z}=\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|}(\boldsymbol{z}^{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}^{J}),\quad\quad\ket{\tilde{z}}=\frac{1}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},\dots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}^{J}),

(50)

if the relative error of each component is upper bounded as
\|\boldsymbol{z}^{j}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}^{j}\|\leq\delta\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\|
for all
j=1,\dots,J
, then it follows that for the corresponding normalized vectors we have

\|\ket{z}-\ket{\tilde{z}}\|\leq 2\delta.

(51)

Proof.

\displaystyle\|\ket{z}-\ket{\tilde{z}}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\boldsymbol{z}}{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|}-\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}\right\|\leq\left\|\frac{\boldsymbol{z}}{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|}-\frac{\boldsymbol{z}}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}\right\|+\left\|\frac{\boldsymbol{z}}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}-\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}\right\|

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\|\boldsymbol{z}\|-\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|\right|}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{z}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}\leq 2\frac{\|\boldsymbol{z}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|}.

(53)

Since

\|\boldsymbol{z}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|=\left(\sum_{j}\|\boldsymbol{z}^{j}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}^{j}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\delta\left(\sum_{j}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}^{j}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\delta\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}\|,

(54)

the result follows.
∎

Recall the definitions (we set
p=0
for the history state)

\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{0},0,\dots,0,\boldsymbol{x}^{1},0,\dots,0,\boldsymbol{x}^{M}),

(55)

\boldsymbol{x}_{H}=(\boldsymbol{x}(0),0,\dots,0,\boldsymbol{x}(h),0,\dots,0,\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)),

(56)

and recall that we denote by kets the corresponding normalized states. Then

Corollary 7
.

We have

\|\ket{x^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}_{H}}-\ket{x_{H}}\|\leq 2\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}},\quad\|\ket{x^{M}}-\ket{x_{F}}\|\leq 2\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}.

(57)

Proof.

The result follows by applying Lemma
6
to the time-discretization error Lemma
3
.
∎

We next turn to embedding the ODE system into a linear system of equations.

Appendix B
Linear system embedding of the dynamics.

In what follows we assume tuneable idling parameters at each step
m=0,1,\dots,M
so as to cover both the case where we want to output the history state, the case where we want to output the final state, as well as generalized settings, as anticipated in the main text. These idling parameters are specified by integers
p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{M}
. The case
p_{m}=0
except for
p_{M}=p
corresponds to the final state output case, while
p_{m}=p
for all
m
corresponds to the history state output case.

The linear system reduction for the ODE protocol in Ref.
Berry et al. 2017
, here in a generalized version, has the form
L=L_{1}+L_{2}+L_{3}
where

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=0}^{p_{m}+k}\ket{m,j}\!\!\bra{m,j}\otimes I+\theta(p_{M})\sum_{j=1}^{p_{M}}\ket{M,j}\!\!\bra{M,j}\otimes I,

\displaystyle L_{2}

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{m=0}^{M}\theta(p_{m})\sum_{j=1}^{p_{m}}\ket{m,j}\!\!\bra{m,j-1}\otimes I-\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ket{m,p_{m}+j}\!\!\bra{m,p_{m}+j-1}\otimes\frac{Ah}{j},

\displaystyle L_{3}

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\ket{m+1,0}\!\!\bra{m,p_{m}+j}\otimes I.

(58)

Here we use the Heaviside function
\theta(p)=1
for
p>0
and zero otherwise.
The integers
\{p_{m}\}
govern the filtering mentioned, as they amplify the solution vector at specified times. Note by setting
p_{m}=0
for all
m=0,\dots,M-1
and
p_{M}=p
we recover an alternative form of the matrix
L
given in the main text. The difference compared to the main text is that here the idling phase is entirely encoded in the
j
index, whereas in the main text it was more efficiently encoded in all
m\geq M
and
j=0,\dots,k
indices. This amounts to a simple relabeling. To compute the condition number bound it is convenient to use the form above, whereas to construct the block-encodings it is convenient to adopt the form used in the main text.

It can be useful to visualize the matrix
L
in the computational basis:

L=\begin{bmatrix}I\\
-I&I\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&-I&I\\
&&&-Ah&I&&&&&&&&\\
&&&&-\frac{Ah}{2}&I&&&&&&&\\
&&&&&\frac{-Ah}{3}&I&&&&&&\\
&&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&&&&&\\
&&&&&&&-\frac{Ah}{k}&I\\
&&&-I&-I&-I&\dots&-I&-I&I&\\
&&&&&&&&&-I&I\\
&&&&&&&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&&&&&&&&&-I&I\\
&&&&&&&&&&&&-Ah&I&&\\
&&&&&&&&&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&&\end{bmatrix}.

(59)

L
has
M
repeated blocks. The
m^{th}
block contains
p_{m}
repetitions of the
-I,I
‘idling’ and
k
sub-blocks
-Ah/j
,
j=1,\dots,k
, generating the Taylor series components. Note that
A
only appears to the first power, and hence (
59
) is a relatively simple encoding compared to alternative ones involving matrix exponentiation
Berry and Costa 2022
or matrix inversion subroutines
Krovi 2022
.

A simple extension of Eq. (16)-(24) in Ref.
Berry et al. 2017
shows that if we consider
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{c}
with the vector
\boldsymbol{c}
given in terms of the following subvectors

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}^{(0,0)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{0}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}

\displaystyle=h\boldsymbol{b}\mbox{ for all }m=0,\dots,M-1

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}^{(m,r)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\mbox{ otherwise},

(60)

then we get

\boldsymbol{y}=L^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}=[\underbrace{\boldsymbol{y}^{(0,0)},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(0,p_{0})}}_{p_{0}+1},\boldsymbol{y}^{(0,p_{0}+1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(0,p_{0}+k)},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{y}^{(1,0)},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(1,p_{1})}}_{p_{1}+1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(M-1,p_{M+1}+k)},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,0)},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,p_{M})}}_{p_{M}+1}]^{T},

(61)

where for each
m
we have
\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}=\boldsymbol{x}^{m}
for all
j=0,\dots,p_{m}
. Here
\boldsymbol{x}^{m}
are the components of the history state in the truncated Taylor series approach (Eq. (
40
)). Instead,
\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}
for
j\neq 0
is, with the above labeling, junk data that can be removed by a post-selection measurement on the qubits labeling the indices
(m,j)
. We show that this form of
\boldsymbol{y}
is the result of the matrix inversion inside the proof of Theorem
2
. Note that for the choice
p_{m}=0
for all
m
we recover the history state output for the
(m,0)
labels, and the desired history state can be extracted via post-selecting. For the choice
p_{m}=0
for
m<M
and
p_{m}=p
for
m=M
we obtain a vector that can be post-processed to output the final state with bounded probability.

Appendix C
Block-encoding of the linear system matrix

Formally, an
(\omega_{X},a_{X},\epsilon_{X})
block-encoding of a matrix
X
is a unitary
U_{X}
such that

\|(\bra{0^{a_{X}}}\otimes I)U_{X}(\ket{0^{a_{X}}}\otimes I)-X\|\leq\epsilon_{X}.

(62)

Here we denote by
\ket{0^{a}}
the state
\ket{0}^{\otimes a}
, for any integer
a
.
We assume oracle access to
A
via an
(\omega,a,0)
block-encoding of
A
. However from this we need to construct a block-encoding of the matrix
L
. The following result shows this can be done without too much overhead.

Theorem 1
.

Given a Taylor truncation scale
k

we can construct a
(1+\omega+\sqrt{k+1},a+6,0)
-block-encoding
U_{L}
of
L
(as given in the main text) using a single call to an
(\omega,a,0)
block-encoding
U_{Ah}
of the matrix
Ah
.

Proof.

We consider the problem separately for
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
and
L_{3}
and then combine them with linear combination of unitaries.

First,
L_{1}
: this is just the identity matrix, hence the block-encoding is trivial – we have a
(1,0,0)
-block-encoding of
L_{1}
.

Second,
L_{2}
:

\displaystyle L_{2}:=

\displaystyle-\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ket{m,j}\!\!\bra{m,j-1}\otimes\frac{Ah}{j}-\theta(p)\sum_{m=M}^{M+\frac{p}{k+1}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ket{m,j}\!\!\bra{m,j-1}\otimes I,

(63)

which we rewrite as

\displaystyle L_{2}=\omega L^{\prime}_{2}L^{\prime\prime}_{2}L^{\prime\prime\prime}_{2},

(64)

where

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{2}:=-\left[\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\ket{m}\!\!\bra{m}\otimes I\otimes\frac{Ah}{\omega}+\sum_{m=M}^{M+\frac{p}{k+1}}\ket{m}\!\!\bra{m}\otimes I\otimes I\right]

(65)

\displaystyle L_{2}^{\prime\prime}=\left[-\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ket{m,j}\!\!\bra{m,j}\otimes\frac{I}{j}-\theta(p)\sum_{m=M}^{M+\frac{p}{k+1}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ket{m,j}\!\!\bra{m,j}\otimes\frac{I}{\omega}\right]

(66)

\displaystyle L^{\prime\prime\prime}_{2}=I\otimes\Delta^{\dagger}\otimes I,\quad\quad\Delta^{\dagger}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\ket{j+1}\!\!\bra{j}.

(67)

L_{2}^{\prime}
can be
(1,a,0)
-block-encoded by
U_{L^{\prime}_{2}}
, where

U_{L^{\prime}_{2}}=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\ket{m}\!\!\bra{m}\otimes I\otimes U_{Ah}+\sum_{m=M}^{M+p/(k+1)}\ket{m}\!\!\bra{m}\otimes I\otimes I

(68)

L^{\prime\prime}_{2}
can be
(1,1,0)
-block-encoded as

U_{L^{\prime\prime}_{2}}\ket{0}\ket{mj}=\begin{cases}\left(-\frac{1}{j}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{j^{2}}}\ket{1}\right)\ket{mj}\quad\textrm{ if }(m<M\textrm{ and }j>0),\\
\left(-\frac{1}{\omega}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}}\ket{1}\right)\ket{mj}\quad\textrm{ if }(m\geq M\textrm{ and }j>0),\\
\ket{1}\ket{mj}\quad\textrm{ if }j=0,\end{cases}

(69)

where we assume that
\omega\geq 1
.
This gives a
(1,1,0)
-block-encoding of
L_{2}^{\prime}
.
Finally,
L^{\prime\prime\prime}_{2}
can be block-encoded as

U_{L_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}}=I\otimes U_{\Delta^{\dagger}}\otimes I
, where
U_{\Delta^{\dagger}}
acts on the space where
\Delta^{\dagger}
acts augmented by an extra qubit ancilla:

U_{\Delta^{\dagger}}=\Delta^{\dagger}\otimes\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}+\Delta\otimes\ket{1}\!\!\bra{1}+\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\otimes\ket{0}\!\!\bra{1}.

(70)

Using
\Delta^{\dagger}\Delta=I-\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}
and
\Delta\Delta^{\dagger}=I
, one can check that
U_{\Delta^{\dagger}}
is unitary. Hence, we have a
(1,1,0)
-block-encoding of
I\otimes\Delta^{\dagger}\otimes I
.

Overall, we set
U_{L_{2}}:=U_{L^{\prime}_{2}}U_{L_{2}^{\prime\prime}}U_{L_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}}
,where each one of the three block-encoding unitaries has implicit identity operators on the auxiliary qubits of the other block-encodings. This gives an
(\omega,a+2,0)
-block-encoding of
L_{2}
involving a single query to
U_{Ah}
.

Third,
L_{3}
: we rewrite

L_{3}=-\sqrt{k+1}(\tilde{\Delta}^{\dagger}\otimes I\otimes I)(I\otimes\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\otimes I)(I\otimes U^{\dagger}_{USP}\otimes I),

(71)

where
\tilde{\Delta}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\ket{m+1}\!\!\bra{m}
,

U_{USP}\ket{0}=\ket{+_{k}}

(72)

and
\ket{+_{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\ket{j}
.
Here
U_{USP}
is a uniform state preparation circuit
Lee et al. 2021
(if
k
is a power of
2
, this is just the tensor product of
\log_{2}k
Hadamards). Furthermore, the projector onto
\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}
can be decomposed as

\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}=\frac{1}{2}I+\frac{i}{2}e^{i\pi Z/2},

(73)

and so one can obtain a
(1,1,0)
-block-encoding of
I\otimes\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\otimes I
via Linear Combination of Unitaries (LCU)
Lin 2022
. Finally, we can construct a
(1,1,0)
-block-encoding of
\tilde{\Delta}^{\dagger}
exactly in the same way as we constructed one for
\Delta^{\dagger}
above. Overall, we obtain a
(\sqrt{k+1},2,0)
-block-encoding of
L_{3}
. Finally, consider

\frac{1}{1+\omega+\sqrt{1+k}}L=\frac{1}{1+\omega+\sqrt{1+k}}\left[L_{1}+\omega\left(\frac{L_{2}}{\omega}\right)+\sqrt{1+k}\left(\frac{L_{3}}{\sqrt{1+k}}\right)\right].

(74)

This can be realized by the LCU

\frac{1}{1+\omega+\sqrt{k}}(U_{L_{1}}+\omega U_{L_{2}}+\sqrt{k}U_{L_{3}})

(75)

using
2
extra qubits. We hence constructed a
(1+\omega+\sqrt{1+k},a+6,0)
-block-encoding of
U_{L}
using a single call to
U_{Ah}
.

∎

Appendix D
New condition number upper bound of the linear embedding matrix.

We next turn to the condition number of the matrix
L
, which determines the time-complexity of the algorithm. Previously Ref.
Berry et al. 2017
presented upper bounds on the condition number of
L
for a special choice of
\{p_{m}\}
and under several assumptions about
A
, in particular: that
A
has
\alpha(A)\leq 0
; that
A
is diagonalizable; and that one has a bound on the condition number
\kappa_{V}
of the matrix
V
that diagonalizes
A
. Here we extend the scope of that analysis and strengthen the bounds. This will give exponential savings in
\log N
for certain classes of matrices (similarly to Ref.
Krovi 2022
, but in the context of a simpler linear embedding), as well as a potential sublinear scaling with time for a large class of dynamics.

Theorem 2

(Condition number bound - General case)
.

Given
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, let
C(t)
be an upper bound to
\|e^{At}\|
. Assume a Taylor truncation scale
k
which obeys the time discretization condition given by Eq. (
41
). Choose idling parameters
p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{M}
and for convention set
p_{-1}=0
. Then the condition number of
L
in Eq. (
58
) is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\kappa_{L}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sum_{m=0}^{M}\left((1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}(p_{m}+I_{0}(2))\sum_{l=0}^{m}C(lh)^{2}\left(1+p_{l-1}(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}C(h)^{2}+g(k)\right)+\frac{p_{m}(p_{m}+1)}{2}\right)+(\sum_{m=0}^{M}p_{m}+Mk)(I_{0}(2)-1)\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times(\sqrt{k+1}+2).

(76)

Here
I_{0}(2)\approx 2.2796
, with
I_{0}(x)
the modified Bessel function of order zero, and where
g(k):=\sum_{s=1}^{k}\left(s!\sum_{j=s}^{k}1/j!\right)^{2}\leq ek
.

Proof.

We consider the construction of the history state with idling parameters in between each Taylor expansion step. A general vector will be divided up into sectors as follows

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=[\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,0)}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,1)}\,\cdots\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,p_{0})}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,p_{0}+1)}\,\cdots\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,p_{0}+k)}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1,0)}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1,1)}\,\cdots\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1,p_{1})}\,\cdots\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1,p_{1}+k)}\dots\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(M,p_{M})}],

(77)

where we use the idling parameters
(p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{M})
.
We can bound
\|L\|
via
\|L\|\leq\|L_{1}\|+\|L_{2}\|+\|L_{3}\|
. Firstly, it is clear that
\|L_{1}\|=1
. Secondly,

L_{2}L_{2}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\theta(p_{m})\sum_{r=1}^{p_{m}}\ket{m,r}\!\!\bra{m,r}\otimes I+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ket{m,p_{m}+j}\!\!\bra{m,p_{m}+j}\otimes\frac{(Ah)^{2}}{j^{2}},

(78)

and so
\|L_{2}\|=\max\{\|Ah\|,1\}=1
. Similarly (see
Berry et al. 2017
, proof of Lemma 4) we have
\|L_{3}\|=\sqrt{k+1}
. Therefore, we have
\|L\|\leq\sqrt{k+1}+2
.

We next estimate the norm
\|L^{-1}\|
which is obtained from

\|L^{-1}\|=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|=1}\|L^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|.

(79)

By writing
L\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\beta}
we have that
\|L^{-1}\|=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|=1}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|
, where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is implicitly a function of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. We now provide an upper bound on
\|L^{-1}\|
via bounding
\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|
. For
m
obeying
0\leq m<M
we have the following system of equations:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,0)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0,0)}

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,1)}

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,2)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,2)}

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}-1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m})}

\displaystyle-(Ah)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}

\displaystyle-\frac{(Ah)}{2}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}

\displaystyle-\frac{(Ah)}{3}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+3)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+3)}

\displaystyle-\frac{(Ah)}{k}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+k-1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+k)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+k)}

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}\cdots-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+k)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m+1,0)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m+1,0)}.

(80)

While for
m=M
we only have the
p_{M}
equations for idling parameters and no Taylor series terms.

We can formally solve for
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
in the above system of equations to get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,2)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,2)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m})}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}-1)}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,2)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)},

(81)

for the idling parts. Thus, for any
m=0,\dots,M
and any
r=1,\dots,p_{m}
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,r)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}+\sum_{s=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,s)}.

(82)

For the Taylor series parts we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}+(Ah)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}+\frac{(Ah)}{2}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+3)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+3)}+\frac{(Ah)}{3}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+k)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+k)}+\frac{(Ah)}{k}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+k-1)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m+1,0)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m+1,0)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+k)}.

(83)

Solving the previous expression in terms of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}
gives

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}+(Ah)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}+\frac{(Ah)}{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}+\frac{(Ah)^{2}}{2}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+3)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+3)}+\frac{(Ah)}{3}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}+\frac{(Ah)^{2}}{3\cdot 2}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}+\frac{(Ah)^{3}}{3\cdot 2\cdot 1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+k)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+k)}+\frac{(Ah)}{k}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+k-1)}

(84)

\displaystyle+\cdots+\frac{(Ah)^{k-2}}{k\cdot(k-1)\cdots 3}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+2)}+\frac{(Ah)^{k-1}}{k\cdot(k-1)\cdots 3\cdot 2}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}+\frac{(Ah)^{k}}{k!}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}.

(85)

This implies that for any
m=0,1,\dots,M-1
and any
j=1,\dots,k
we have

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+j)}=\frac{1}{j!}(Ah)^{j}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}+\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}(Ah)^{j-s}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+s)}.

(86)

We now iteratively solve over the
m
index as follows. Firstly,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:taylorsumproof}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m-1,p_{m-1}+j)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,0)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:jsumproof}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{j!}(Ah)^{j}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m-1,p_{m-1})}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}(Ah)^{j-s}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m-1}+s)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,0)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:idle-copying}}}{{=}}T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m-1,0)}+\sum_{r=1}^{p_{m-1}}T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m-1,r)}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}(Ah)^{j-s}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m-1,p_{m-1}+s)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,0)}.

(87)

We define

\boldsymbol{B}^{(m-1)}:=\sum_{r=1}^{p_{m-1}}T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m-1,r)}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}(Ah)^{j-s}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m-1,p_{m-1}+s)},

(88)

so that for any
m=1,2,\dots,M
we have

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}=T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m-1,0)}+\boldsymbol{B}^{(m-1)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,0)}.

(89)

Since we have the condition
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,0)}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0,0)}
it follows that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1,0)}

\displaystyle=T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0,0)}+\boldsymbol{B}^{(0)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,0)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(2,0)}

\displaystyle=T_{k}(Ah)(T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0,0)}+\boldsymbol{B}^{(0)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,0)})+\boldsymbol{B}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(2,0)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(3,0)}

\displaystyle=T_{k}(Ah)[T_{k}(Ah)(T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0,0)}+\boldsymbol{B}^{(0)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,0)})+\boldsymbol{B}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(2,0)}]+\boldsymbol{B}^{(2)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(3,0)},

(90)

and so iterating we find that

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}T_{k}(Ah)^{m-r}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,0)}+\boldsymbol{B}^{(r-1)}\right),

(91)

where we set
\boldsymbol{B}^{(-1)}=\boldsymbol{0}
by setting
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(-1,r)}\equiv 0
for all
r
.

We can use the above to show that the ideal inversion of
L
on the claimed form for
\boldsymbol{c}
gives the claimed output. Specifically, we choose
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{c}
via the conditions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0,0)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{0}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}

\displaystyle=h\boldsymbol{b}\mbox{ for all }m=0,\dots,M-1

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,r)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\mbox{ otherwise}.

(92)

The idling terms will produce copies of data with this choice of input. More precisely, from Equation (
82
) we have that

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,r)}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)},

(93)

for
r=0,\dots p_{m}
since
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,s)}=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
m
and all
s=1,\dots,p_{m}
.

The conditions
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+1)}=h\boldsymbol{b}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,r)}=\boldsymbol{0}
on other indices, imply that we have (Eq. (
88
))

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}^{(m-1)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{r=1}^{p_{m-1}}T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m-1,r)}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}(Ah)^{j-s}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m-1,p_{m-1}+s)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1!}{j!}(Ah)^{j-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m-1,p_{m-1}+1)}=S_{k}(Ah)h\boldsymbol{b}.

(94)

Therefore, Equation (
89
) implies that

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}=T_{k}(Ah)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m-1,0)}+S_{k}(Ah)h\boldsymbol{b},

(95)

for
m=1,\dots,M
and the initial condition
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,0)}=\boldsymbol{x}^{0}
. This recursion relation coincides with the one for the discrete dynamics and therefore
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}=\boldsymbol{x}^{m}
for all
m=0,\dots,M
, as required. Moreover, idling implies
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,r)}=\boldsymbol{x}^{m}
for all
m=0,\dots,M
and all
r=0,\dots,p_{m}
. The remaining indices correspond to junk data. This shows that
L^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}
returns the claimed form of discrete solution.

Returning to the condition number calculation, we now make use of Lemma
5
to estimate the following vector norm.

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=0}^{m}\|T_{k}(Ah)^{m-r}\|\left(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,0)}\|+\|\boldsymbol{B}^{(r-1)}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\sum_{r=0}^{m}\|e^{Ah(m-r)}\|\left(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,0)}\|+\|\boldsymbol{B}^{(r-1)}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\sum_{r=0}^{m}C(mh-rh)\left(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,0)}\|+\|\boldsymbol{B}^{(r-1)}\|\right),

(96)

and since
\|Ah\|\leq 1
we have that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}\|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\sum_{r=0}^{m}C(mh-rh)\left[\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,0)}\|+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r-1,p_{r-1}+s)}\|+\sum_{w=1}^{p_{r-1}}\|T_{k}(Ah)\|\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r-1,w)}\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\sum_{r=0}^{m}C(mh-rh)\left[\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,0)}\|+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r-1,p_{r-1}+s)}\|+(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})C(h)\sum_{w=1}^{p_{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r-1,w)}\|\right]

(97)

where we used
\|T_{k}(Ah)\|\leq C(h)(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})
, again from Lemma
5
. Now,

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+s)}\|\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{s=1}^{1}\frac{s!}{1!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+s)}\|+\sum_{s=1}^{2}\frac{s!}{2!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+s)}\|+\dots+\sum_{s=1}^{k}\frac{s!}{k!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+s)}\|\right]

\displaystyle=\left[1!\left(\frac{1}{1!}+\frac{1}{2!}+\dots+\frac{1}{k!}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+1)}\|\right.

\displaystyle\left.+2!\left(\frac{1}{2!}+\dots+\frac{1}{k!}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+2)}\|+\dots+k!\frac{1}{k!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+k)}\|\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{k}s!\sum_{j=s}^{k}\frac{1}{j!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+s)}\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{k}f(s,k)\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,p_{r}+s)}\|,

(98)

where we define

f(s,k):=s!\sum_{j=s}^{k}\frac{1}{j!}=\sum_{j=s}^{k}\frac{1}{j(j-1)\cdots(s+1)}.

(99)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}\|}{(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})}\leq\sum_{r=0}^{m}C(mh-rh)\left[\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,0)}\|+\sum_{s=1}^{k}f(s,k)\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r-1,p_{r-1}+s)}\|+(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})C(h)\sum_{w=1}^{p_{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r-1,w)}\|\right].

(100)

The RHS of this is in the form of an inner product
\boldsymbol{V}^{(m)}\cdot\boldsymbol{W}^{(m)}
, between vectors with components,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}^{(m)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\oplus_{r=0}^{m}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r,0)}\|\oplus_{r=0}^{m}\oplus_{w=1}^{p_{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r-1,w)}\|\oplus_{r=0}^{m}\oplus_{s=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r-1,p_{r-1}+s)}\|\right)

(101)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}^{(m)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\oplus_{r=0}^{m}C(mh-rh)\oplus_{r=0}^{m}\oplus_{w=1}^{p_{r-1}}(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})C(mh-rh)C(h)\oplus_{r=0}^{m}\oplus_{s=1}^{k}\left[C(mh-rh)f(s,k)\right]\right)

(102)

where we set
p_{-1}=0
, and use direct sum notation so that, for example,
(\oplus_{r=0}^{3}z_{r})=(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
. Since
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|=1
it follows that
\|\boldsymbol{V}^{(m)}\|\leq 1
for any
m=1,\dots,M
. We now use Cauchy-Schwarz on
\boldsymbol{V}^{(m)}\cdot\boldsymbol{W}^{(m)}
and get that for any

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{m}C(rh)^{2}\left(1+p_{r-1}(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}C(h)^{2}+g(k)\right)

(103)

where

g(k)=\sum_{s=1}^{k}f(s,k)^{2}=\sum_{s=1}^{k}\left(s!\sum_{j=s}^{k}1/j!\right)^{2}.

(104)

For
m=0
we have that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0,0)}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0,0)}
and so

. If we define

then we can alternatively use the bound given in (

, which is slightly weaker. Note that since

We next bound

and also

for

.

Firstly, from Eq. (

),

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,r)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,0)}\|+\sum_{s=1}^{r}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,s)}\|,

(105)

for

. We follow a similar line to before, and have from Eq. (

):

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,r)}\|\leq(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\sum_{l=0}^{m}C(mh-lh)\left[\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(l,0)}\|+\sum_{s=1}^{k}f(s,k)\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(l-1,p_{l-1}+s)}\|+(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})C(h)\sum_{w=1}^{p_{l-1}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(l-1,w)}\|\right]+\sum_{s=1}^{r}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,s)}\|,

and using Cauchy-Schwarz in an analogous way to Eq. (

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,r)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}\sum_{l=0}^{m}C(lh)^{2}\left(1+p_{l-1}(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}C(h)^{2}+g(k)\right)+r,

(107)

for any

Finally, using Eq. (

) and Eq. (

),

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+j)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{j!}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m})}\|+\sum_{s=1}^{j}\frac{s!}{j!}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m,p_{m}+s)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}}{j!^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{m}C(lh)^{2}\left(1+p_{l-1}(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}C(h)^{2}+g(k)\right)+\frac{p_{m}}{j!^{2}}+\sum_{s=1}^{j}\left(\frac{s!}{j!}\right)^{2}.

(109)

We now have that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|^{2}=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{p_{m}}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,r)}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(m,p_{m}+j)}\|^{2}\right)+\sum_{r=0}^{p_{M}}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(M,r)}\|^{2}.

(110)

Using the above bounds in Eq. (

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{M}\left((1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{p_{m}}\sum_{l=0}^{m}C(lh)^{2}\left(1+p_{l-1}(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}C(h)^{2}+g(k)\right)+\sum_{r=0}^{p_{m}}r\right)

(111)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}}{j!^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{m}C(lh)^{2}\left(1+p_{l-1}(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}C(h)^{2}+g(k)\right)+\frac{p_{m}}{j!^{2}}+\sum_{s=1}^{j}\left(\frac{s!}{j!}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{M}\left((1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}(I_{0}(2)-1)\sum_{l=0}^{m}C(lh)^{2}\left(1+p_{l-1}(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}C(h)^{2}+g(k)\right)\right)+\left(\sum_{m=0}^{M}p_{m}+Mk\right)(I_{0}(2)-1)

(112)

(113)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(j!)^{2}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(j!)^{2}}=I_{0}(2)-1,

(114)

where

is the order-zero modified Bessel function of the first kind, and also that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\left(\frac{s!}{j!}\right)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{j}\left(\frac{1}{(j-s)!}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{w=1}^{j}\frac{1}{(w!)^{2}}\leq k(I_{0}(2)-1).

(115)

From numerical tests, we find the latter inequalities cannot be substantially improved. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 8
.

The matrix
L
with
p_{m}=0
for all
m<M
and
p_{M}=p
satisfies the following condition number bounds obtained by direct replacement in Eq. (
116
):

•

If
A
is diagonalizable via
VAV^{-1}=D
, a diagonal matrix, then we may take
P=V^{\dagger}V
and so
\kappa_{P}=\kappa_{V}^{2}
(with
\kappa_{V}
the condition number of
V
) and
\mu_{P}(A)=\alpha(A)
. In particular, if
A
is a normal matrix then
\kappa_{P}=1
and
\mu_{P}(A)=\alpha(A)
.

•

If
A
has negative log-norm then we may take
P=I
and so
\kappa_{P}=1
and
\mu_{P}(A)=\mu(A)
(with
\mu(A)
the Euclidean log-norm of
A
).

The first corollary implies, for all
\alpha(A)<0
, a strengthened version of the result of
Berry et al. 2017
. In fact, for
\alpha(A)<0
with
A
diagonalizable via a matrix
V
, the result in
Berry et al. 2017
gives a condition number scaling
O(\kappa_{V}Tk)
, whereas the bound above scales as
O(\kappa_{V}\sqrt{T}k)
. The second corollary implies, for all matrices with negative log-norm
\mu(A)<0
, a stronger upper bound on the linear system condition number compared to
Krovi 2022
. In fact, the condition number in
Krovi 2022
scales as
O(Tk^{1/2})
(since
C_{\textrm{max}}\leq 1
). Note that an extra factor of
k
must be included to account for the implicit matrix inversion required in Ref.
Krovi 2022
so the effective scaling is
O(Tk^{3/2})
. Instead, the above bound scales as
O(\sqrt{T}k)
.

Proof.

If
A
is diagonalizable and
A=V^{-1}DV
for some diagonal
D
, then we may take
P=V^{\dagger}V>0
. In fact,

\displaystyle PA+A^{\dagger}P

\displaystyle=V^{\dagger}VA+A^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}V=V^{\dagger}(D+D^{\dagger})V.

However, since
A
cannot have any eigenvalues with nonnegative real parts, it follows that
V^{\dagger}(D+D^{\dagger})V=-Q
for some Hermitan operator
Q>0
. We then have that
\kappa_{P}=\|V^{\dagger}V\|\|V^{-1}(V^{-1})^{\dagger}\|=\kappa_{V}^{2}
, since
\|X^{\dagger}X\|=\|X\|^{2}
for any
X
(in the particular case of
A
normal, the operator is diagonalizable via a unitary
V
for which
\kappa_{V}=1
, so
\kappa_{P}=1
). To show that
\mu_{P}(A)=\alpha(A)
note that

\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\neq 0}\textrm{Re}\,\frac{\langle PA\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}{\langle P\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\neq 0}\textrm{Re}\,\frac{\langle VA\boldsymbol{x},V\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}{\langle V\boldsymbol{x},V\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\neq 0}\textrm{Re}\,\frac{\langle VAV^{-1}V\boldsymbol{x},V\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}{\langle V\boldsymbol{x},V\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|\neq 0}\textrm{Re}\,\frac{\langle D\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\rangle}=\alpha(A),

(126)

where we replace
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=V\boldsymbol{x}
.

Suppose now that we choose
P=I
and have that
PA+A^{\dagger}P=A+A^{\dagger}
has strictly negative eigenvalues. This occurs if and only if
A
has negative log-norm with respect to operator norm. In this case we have
\kappa_{P}=1
and
\mu_{P}(A)=\mu(A)
.
∎

Special case:
A
with negative log-norm

We can analyse the behaviour of these bounds in some more detail for the case where
P=I
, which corresponds to the case of
A
having negative log-norm. Firstly, the transition from the
O(\sqrt{M})
scaling to the
O(M)
scaling is determined by the square root of

\frac{e^{2\mu(A)(M+2)h}+M+1-e^{2\mu(A)h}(2+M)}{(1-e^{2h\mu(A)})^{2}}.

(127)

In the regime
\mu<0
the scaling is
O(M)
, but one can wonder how small the constant prefactor can be. We can in fact derive a lower bound for it. Note that for any matrix
A
we have
-\|A\|\leq\mu(A)\leq\|A\|
, and since stability requires
\|A\|h\leq 1
this implies that
e^{-1}\leq e^{\mu h}\leq 1
. The expression in Eq.
127
is equal to the double sum
\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{r=0}^{m}(e^{\mu h})^{r}
. Letting
x=e^{\mu h}
this sum can be written as

\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{r=0}^{m}(e^{\mu h})^{r}=\sum_{s=0}^{M}c_{s}x^{s},

(128)

where
c_{s}>0
. A sum of strictly positive monomials
c_{s}x^{s}
with
c_{s}>0
, is monotone increasing for
x>0
, and therefore on the interval
[e^{-1},1]
the sum attains its minimum when
x=e^{\mu h}=e^{-1}
. We therefore have that

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2\mu(A)(M+2)h}+M+1-e^{2\mu(A)h}(2+M)}{(1-e^{2h\mu(A)})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left(e^{4}(M+1)-e^{2}(M+2)+e^{-2M}\right)}{\left(e^{2}-1\right)^{2}}=\frac{e^{4}-2e^{2}+e^{-2M}}{(e^{2}-1)^{2}}+\frac{e^{2}}{e^{2}-1}M.

(129)

The smallest value the double sum can take among all systems with negative log-norm is hence smaller than

1+\frac{e^{2}}{e^{2}-1}M

(130)

Overall then in the best case scenario

\displaystyle\kappa_{L}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}\left(1+g(k)\right)I_{0}(2)\left(1+\frac{e^{2}}{e^{2}-1}M\right)+k(I_{0}(2)-1)M}

(131)

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{(1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})^{2}\left(1+g(k)\right)I_{0}(2)\frac{e^{2}}{e^{2}-1}+k(I_{0}(2)-1)}\times\sqrt{M}.

(132)

For example, for small
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
and
k=19
, the pre-factor constant before
\sqrt{M}
is approximately
9.8
.
This optimal case occurs when
\mu(A)=-\|A\|
, which in turn occurs if

\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=1}Re(\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}A\boldsymbol{x})=-\max_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=1}\|A\boldsymbol{x}\|.

(133)

We can show this occurs if and only if
A=-\|A\|I
in the case of negative log-norm.

Lemma 9
.

Let
A
have a negative log-norm. The optimal linear scaling
Me^{2}/(e^{2}-1)
in
\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{l=0}^{m}C(lh)^{2}
occurs if and only if
A=-\|A\|I
.

Proof.

The ‘if’ direction is clear. Suppose conversely that
\mu(A)=-\|A\|
. Let
\lambda_{i}(X)
denote the
i
’th largest eigenvalue of the Hermitian matrix
X
. Consider
\mu_{i}:=\lambda_{i}(Re(A)):=\lambda_{i}((A+A^{\dagger})/2)
, and let
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
be a corresponding unit eigenvector for this eigenvalue. By definition of log-norm,
\mu(A)=\mu_{1}
. Furthermore,
\mu_{N}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{2}\leq\mu_{1}<0
and so

|\mu_{N}|\geq\dots\geq|\mu_{1}|.

(134)

Also,

|\mu_{i}|=\left|\lambda_{i}\left(\frac{A+A^{\dagger}}{2}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\|A\|+\|A^{\dagger}\|}{2}=\|A\|

(135)

Hence, we have the following chain of inequalities for all
i=1,\dots,N

\displaystyle\|A\|\geq|\mu_{i}|=|\lambda_{i}(Re(A))|

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\dagger}Re(A)\boldsymbol{v}_{i}|=|Re(\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\dagger}A\boldsymbol{v}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\dagger}A\boldsymbol{v}_{i}|=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\dagger}(A^{\dagger}A)\boldsymbol{v}_{i}}\leq\|A\|.

(136)

These inequalities must be saturated. The equality
|Re(\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\dagger}A\boldsymbol{v}_{i})|=|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\dagger}A\boldsymbol{v}_{i}|
implies
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\dagger}Im(A)\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=0
for all
i
. Since
(A+A^{\dagger})/2
is a Hermitian matrix, it follows that the vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
form a complete basis, and so we find that
(A-A^{\dagger})/2=0
. The equality
|\mu_{i}|=\|A\|
implies
Re(A)\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=-\|A\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
for all
i
, since we are given that
\mu_{i}<0
. Since
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
are a basis, this implies
(A+A^{\dagger})/2=-\|A\|I
. Hence

A=\frac{A+A^{\dagger}}{2}+\frac{A-A^{\dagger}}{2}=-\|A\|I.

(137)

∎

This implies that the bounds are near-optimal when
A
has a relatively uniform spectrum with small imaginary components.

Appendix E
Success probabilities for post-selection.

Recall that we denote by
\ket{y}
a quantum state proportional to the solution to the linear system problem,
\boldsymbol{y}=L^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}
. Furthermore,
\ket{x_{H}}
and
\ket{x_{F}}
are proportional to

\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}=[\boldsymbol{x}^{0},0,\dots,0,\boldsymbol{x}^{1},0,\dots,0,\boldsymbol{x}^{M}]

(138)

and

\boldsymbol{x}_{F}=[0,\dots,0,\boldsymbol{x}^{M}],

(139)

respectively. We then have,

Theorem 10

(Success probability of ODE-solver – history state)
.

Let
h
be such that
\|A\|h\leq 1
. Let
\ket{y}
be the normalized solution to the linear system
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{c}
with
p_{m}=0
for all
m
and

\boldsymbol{c}=[\overbrace{\boldsymbol{x}^{0},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{b},0,\dots,0}_{k}}^{m=0},\dots,\overbrace{0,\underbrace{\boldsymbol{b},0,\dots,0}_{k}}^{m=M-1},0].

(140)

We define
\ket{x_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}
as the state obtained by successful post-selection on the first outcome of the projective measurement
\{\Pi_{H},I-\Pi_{H}\}
on the auxiliary register:

\Pi_{H}=I\otimes\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\otimes I,\quad\ket{x_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}:=\frac{\Pi_{H}\ket{y}}{\|\Pi_{H}\ket{y}\|}.

(141)

The success probability is lower bounded as

\textrm{Pr}_{H}\geq\max\left\{\left(1+\left(1+\frac{\lambda(T)}{(1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\|A\|}\right)^{2}(I_{0}(2)-1)\right)^{-1},\left(1+\frac{(I_{0}(2)-1)}{K}\right)^{-1}\right\}

(142)

where
K=(3-e)^{2}
if
\boldsymbol{b}\neq 0
and
K=1
for
\boldsymbol{b}=0
.

Proof.

The desired data is given by

\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}=[\boldsymbol{y}^{(0,0)},0,\dots,0,\boldsymbol{y}^{(1,0)},0,\cdots,0,\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,0)}]

(143)

which is obtained by post-selecting success (first outcome) in the projective measurement
\{\Pi_{H},I-\Pi_{H}\}
(as it follows from the expression for
\boldsymbol{y}
, Eq. (
61
)).
Moreover the success probability is given by
\textrm{Pr}_{H}=\|\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\|^{2}/\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}
.

From Eq. (
59
) with
p_{m}\equiv 0
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(Ah)^{j-1}}{j!}\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}

\displaystyle=Ah\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}+h\boldsymbol{b},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{(m+1,0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{(Ah)^{j-1}}{j!}\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}.

(144)

•

We first show that
\textrm{Pr}_{H}\geq\left(1+\frac{(I_{0}(2)-1)}{K}\right)^{-1}
.

We compute

\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}:=\|\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\|^{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}.

(145)

We start with the simple case where
\boldsymbol{b}=0
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\|\frac{(Ah)^{j-1}}{j!}\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\right\|^{2}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j!^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(I_{0}(2)-1)\sum_{m=0}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}=(I_{0}(2)-1)\|\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\|^{2}.

Hence,
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq I_{0}(2)\|\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\|^{2}
and the result follows.

We next consider the case
\boldsymbol{b}\neq 0
. Using
\|A\|h\leq 1
we have that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m+1,0)}\|

\displaystyle\geq\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|-\left\|\sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac{(Ah)^{j}}{j!}\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\right\|\geq\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|-\sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac{1}{j!}\left\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\right\|

(146)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|\geq(3-e)\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|.

(147)

So,
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}\geq(3-e)^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|^{2}
. From that and
\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|\leq\frac{1}{j!}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{j!}\right)^{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|^{2}\leq(I_{0}(2)-1)\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|^{2}

(148)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{(3-e)^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}\leq\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{(3-e)^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\|^{2}.

(149)

We conclude

\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{(3-e)^{2}}\right)\|\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\|^{2},

(150)

and hence,

\textrm{Pr}_{H}\geq\left(1+\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{(3-e)^{2}}\right)^{-1},

(151)

as required.

•

We next show that
\textrm{Pr}_{H}\geq\left(1+\left(1+\frac{\lambda(T)}{(1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\|A\|}\right)^{2}(I_{0}(2)-1)\right)^{-1}
.

We choose a function
\zeta(t)
such that

\|\boldsymbol{b}\|\leq\zeta(mh)\|A\|\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|,

(152)

for all
m=0,1,2,\dots,M
, and define a constant
\zeta_{*}
to be an upper bound on
\zeta(mh)
for all
m
, which we specify shortly. Then,

\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|\leq\|Ah\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|+\|h\boldsymbol{b}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|+\zeta(mh)\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|=(1+\zeta(mh))\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|.

(153)

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(j!)^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(I_{0}(2)-1)\sum_{m=0}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,1)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(I_{0}(2)-1)(1+\zeta_{*})^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|^{2}=(I_{0}(2)-1)(1+\zeta_{*})^{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\|^{2}.

(154)

This upper bounds the norm of the unwanted part of
\boldsymbol{y}
.
The probability of success is hence lower bounded as

\textrm{Pr}_{H}\geq\frac{1}{1+(I_{0}(2)-1)(1+\zeta_{*})^{2}}.

(155)

Now recall that
\lambda(T)
is required to obey

\lambda(T)\geq\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}(t)\|},

(156)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. We also have that
\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)-\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|\leq\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|
for all
m
by (reverse) triangle inequality. This implies that
\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|\geq(1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|
for all
m
. Therefore,

\frac{1}{(1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})}\frac{\lambda(T)}{\|A\|}\geq\frac{1}{(1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|A\|\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|}\geq\frac{1}{\|A\|}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|},

(157)

for all
m=0,1,\dots,M
. Therefore we can take

\zeta_{*}=\frac{1}{(1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})}\frac{\lambda(T)}{\|A\|}.

(158)

This implies,

\textrm{Pr}_{H}\geq\left(1+\left(1+\frac{\lambda(T)}{(1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}})\|A\|}\right)^{2}(I_{0}(2)-1)\right)^{-1}.

(159)

∎

A comment about the above bounds. Recall that we have

\boldsymbol{x}(t)=e^{At}\boldsymbol{x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{At^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{b},

(160)

and so for non-zero
\boldsymbol{b}
we can have
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=0
whenever

\boldsymbol{x}(0)=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{A(t^{\prime}-t)}\boldsymbol{b}.

(161)

Therefore, in general
\lambda(T)
is unbounded from above, and depends on the relations between
\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{x}(0)
and
A
. Despite this, it can be shown that we can still bound the success probability above
0.05
even in the case of no idling.

We now turn to the final state problem:

Theorem 11

(Success probability of ODE-solver – final state)
.

Let
h
be such that
\|A\|h\leq 1
. Let
\ket{y}
be the normalized solution to the linear system
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{c}
with
p_{m}=0
for all
m<M
,
p_{M}=p
a positive multiple of
k+1
and

\boldsymbol{c}=[\overbrace{\boldsymbol{x}^{0},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{b},0,\dots,0}_{k}}^{m=0},\dots,\underbrace{\overbrace{\underbrace{0,0,\dots,0}_{k+1}}^{m=M},\dots,\overbrace{\underbrace{0,0,\dots,0}_{k+1}}^{m=M+\frac{p}{k+1}}}_{1+p}].

(162)

We define
\ket{x_{F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}
as the state obtained by successful post-selection on the first outcome of the projective measurement
\{\Pi_{F},I-\Pi_{F}\}
on the clock register:

\Pi_{F}=\sum_{m=M}^{M+p/(k+1)}\ket{m}\!\!\bra{m}\otimes I\otimes I,\quad\ket{y_{F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}:=\frac{\Pi_{F}\ket{y}}{\|\Pi_{F}\ket{y}\|}=\ket{x^{M}}\otimes\sum_{m=M}^{M+\frac{p}{k+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\ket{mj}.

(163)

The success probability is lower bounded as

\textrm{Pr}_{F}\geq\frac{1}{(1-\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{(p+1)K})+(M+1)\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{(p+1)K}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}{1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\right)^{2}\bar{g}^{2}}.

(164)

where

\bar{g}^{2}=\frac{1}{M+1}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)\|^{2}},

(165)

with
K=1
in the homogeneous case,
K=(3-e)^{2}
in the general case.

Proof.

The equality
\ket{y_{F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}=\ket{x^{M}}\otimes\sum_{m=M}^{M+\frac{p}{k+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\ket{mj}
follows from the expression for
\boldsymbol{y}
(Eq. (
61
)), together with the form of the projector in Eq. (
163
).

The rest of the proof is similar to the previous theorem. First,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}=\sum_{m=M}^{M+p/(k+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}=(p+1)\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,0)}\|^{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}.

(166)

For the unwanted part (the second piece), using the same bound as in the previous theorem we get

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,j)}\|^{2}\leq\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}

(167)

with
K=(3-e)^{2}
for the inhomogeneous case and
K=1
if
\boldsymbol{b}=0
.

The success probability is then lower bounded as in the general case as

\displaystyle\textrm{Pr}_{F}=\frac{(p+1)\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,0)}\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p+1)\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,0)}\|^{2}}{(p+1)\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,0)}\|^{2}+\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}}

(168)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K(p+1)})+\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K(p+1)}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,0)}\|^{2}}}

(169)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(1-\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K(p+1)})+\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K(p+1)}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}{1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\right)^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)\|^{2}}}

(170)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K(p+1)})+(M+1)\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K(p+1)}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}{1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\right)^{2}\bar{g}^{2}}.

(171)

Note that the inequality in the above sequence follows from the time-discretization lemma (Lemma
3
):

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(m,0)}\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{(M,0)}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}^{M}\|^{2}}\leq\frac{(\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|+\|\boldsymbol{x}^{m}-\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|)^{2}}{(\|\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)\|-\|\boldsymbol{x}^{M}-\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)\|)^{2}}\leq\frac{(\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|)^{2}}{(\|\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)\|-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}\|\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)\|)^{2}}

(172)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}{1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\right)^{2}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}(mh)\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}(Mh)\|^{2}}.

(173)

The result then follows.
∎

Note that the success probability for the history state behaves quite differently from that of the final state output. One way to understand this is in terms of the changes in norm of the solution. For the final state output we have that if
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
gets very close to zero then we have a much lower success probability. But this does not affect the history state in the same way: it simply means that the amplitudes within the history state decay, but the norm of the history state does not – in particular we have that
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{H}\|\geq\|\boldsymbol{x}(0)\|
.

As we discuss below, we do not want the choice of
p
that we make to affect the scaling of
\kappa
with time. Therefore if
A
is stable we have a complexity
\kappa=O(\sqrt{M})
, otherwise we have
\kappa=O(M)
. Since
\kappa=O(p)
we can choose either
p=\sqrt{M}
or
p=M
, respectively, without affecting the time complexity of
\kappa
.

If we set
p=M^{1/2}
,

\textrm{Pr}_{F}\geq\frac{1}{(1-\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{M^{1/2}K})+\frac{(M+1)}{M^{1/2}}\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}{1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\right)^{2}\bar{g}^{2}}.

(174)

With amplitude amplification, we need
O(1/\sqrt{\textrm{Pr}_{F}})
queries to the whole ODE linear solver protocol to obtain success probability
O(1)
. This number is
O(\bar{g}M^{1/4})
.

Instead, if we set
p=M

\displaystyle\textrm{Pr}_{F}\geq\frac{1}{(1-\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K(M+1)})+\frac{I_{0}(2)-1}{K}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}{1-\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}\right)^{2}\bar{g}^{2}}.

(175)

With amplitude amplification, we need
O(\bar{g})
queries to the whole ODE linear solver protocol to obtain success probability
O(1)
.

Appendix F
Error propagation

The quantum linear solver applied to
L
returns a mixed quantum state
\rho
such that

\|\rho-\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}},

(176)

where
\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
is the linear solver error,
\ket{y}=\boldsymbol{y}/\|\boldsymbol{y}\|
and
\boldsymbol{y}=L^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}
. The solution of the linear system is then projectively measured. Upon success the target (history or final) state is returned. The success probability in the ideal case has been analyzed in Sec.
E
.

In the non-ideal case, however, both the success probability and the post-measurement state are perturbed due to the finite precision of the QLSA. This is quantified by the following continuity statement with respect to the
\ell_{1}
norm.

Lemma 12

(Continuity of success probability and post-selected state)
.

Let
\Pi
be a Hermitian projector,
\rho\geq 0
,
\tilde{\rho}\geq 0
with
\|\tilde{\rho}-\rho\|_{1}\leq\epsilon
and
\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\rho})>\epsilon
. Then

\left\|\frac{\Pi\rho\Pi}{\mathrm{tr}({\rho\Pi})}-\frac{\Pi\tilde{\rho}\Pi}{\mathrm{tr}({\tilde{\rho}\Pi})}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{2\epsilon}{\mathrm{tr}({\rho\Pi})-\epsilon}

(177)

and
\mathrm{tr}({\tilde{\rho}\Pi})\geq\mathrm{tr}({\rho\Pi})-\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

First note that

\|\Pi(\rho-\tilde{\rho})\Pi\|_{1}\leq\|\Pi\|^{2}\|\rho-\tilde{\rho}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon,

(178)

and

|\mathrm{tr}({\rho\Pi})-\mathrm{tr}({\tilde{\rho}\Pi})|=|\mathrm{tr}({(\rho-\tilde{\rho})\Pi})|\leq\|\rho-\tilde{\rho}\|_{1}\|\Pi\|\leq\epsilon.

(179)

It follows that

\mathrm{tr}({\tilde{\rho}\Pi})\geq\mathrm{tr}({\rho\Pi})-\epsilon.

(180)

Note that
\|\Pi\rho\Pi\|_{1}=\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\rho})
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\Pi\rho\Pi}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\rho})}-\frac{\Pi\tilde{\rho}\Pi}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\tilde{\rho}})}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\Pi\rho\Pi}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\rho})}-\frac{\Pi\rho\Pi}{\mathrm{tr}({P\tilde{\rho}})}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\frac{\Pi\rho\Pi}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\tilde{\rho}})}-\frac{\Pi\tilde{\rho}\Pi}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\tilde{\rho}})}\right\|_{1}

(181)

\displaystyle=\|\Pi\rho\Pi\|_{1}\frac{|\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\rho})-\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\tilde{\rho}})|}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\rho})\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\tilde{\rho}})}+\frac{\left\|\Pi(\rho-\tilde{\rho})\Pi\right\|_{1}}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\tilde{\rho}})}

(182)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\tilde{\rho}})}+\frac{\epsilon}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\tilde{\rho}})}\leq\frac{2\epsilon}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi\rho})-\epsilon}.

(183)

∎

Applied to our setting this implies the following result:

Lemma 13
.

Assume we run a QLSA algorithm returning
\rho
with
\|\rho-\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny LS}}
, with
\ket{y}
the normalized solution of
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{c}
. Then denote by

\sigma_{H}:=\frac{\Pi_{H}\rho\Pi_{H}}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi_{H}\rho\Pi_{H}})},\quad\ket{x_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}\!\!\bra{x_{H}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}:=\frac{\Pi_{H}\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}\Pi_{H}}{\mathrm{tr}({\Pi_{H}\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}\Pi_{H}})}.

Proof.

The statement for the history state follows from Lemma

∎

Now we analyze the total error in terms of the

Theorem 4

(Total error bound)
.

With the notation of Lemma

Proof.

To show the claimed error bound, we first bound the vector norm error between the discrete and continuous dynamics. Recall that from Corollary
7
, with the notation
\ket{x_{F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}:=\ket{x^{M}}

\|\ket{x^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}_{H,F}}-\ket{x_{H,F}}\|\leq 2\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}.

(188)

Next we have that

\|\sigma_{H,F}-\ket{x_{H,F}}\!\!\bra{x_{H,F}}\|_{1}\leq\|\sigma_{H,F}-\ket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}\!\!\bra{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}\|_{1}+\|\ket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}\!\!\bra{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}-\ket{x_{H,F}}\!\!\bra{x_{H,F}}\|_{1}.

(189)

We can bound the first term using Eq. (
186

For the second term, we first note that

\|\ket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}\!\!\bra{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}-\ket{x_{H,F}}\!\!\bra{x_{H,F}}\|_{1}=2\sqrt{1-\left|\braket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}|x_{H,F}}\right|^{2}}.

\|\ket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}}-\ket{x_{H,F}}\|^{2}=2(1-\textrm{Re}\braket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}|x_{H,F}})\geq 2\left(1-\left|\braket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}|x_{H,F}}\right|\right)

Hence,

\displaystyle\left(1-\left|\braket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}|x_{H,F}}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\left|\braket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}|x_{H,F}}\right|\right)\left(1+\left|\braket{x_{H,F}^{\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}}|x_{H,F}}\right|\right)

Appendix G
Fast-Forwarding, No Fast-Forwarding, and relation to linear solvers

We now discuss our results in relation to asymptotic complexities and the relation between our ODE solver and both the problem of Hamiltonian simulation and quantum linear solvers.

G.1
Fast-Forwarding of stable systems

The ODE solver presented in this work relies upon a quantum linear solver algorithm (QLSA). The best query complexity is
O(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
, which we use to establish the claimed fast-forwarding result. For the explicit application to a concrete system we will however use the recent explicit query counts given in
Jennings et al. 2023
, which are better than the best analytical or numerical upper bounds provided in the optimal asymptotic result provided in
Costa et al. 2022
for all condition numbers up to at least
\kappa\sim 10^{32}

Jennings et al. 2023
.

We now establish the asymptotic fast-forwarding claimed in the main text. Recall that the notation

,
\boldsymbol{b}h

\boldsymbol{x}^{0}
,
otherwise.

G.2
The No Fast-Forwarding theorem and quantum linear solvers

It should be noted that we may recast any linear ODE into another that is stable with the solutions of each simply related. More precisely, given
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b}
we may choose
z\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mu(A-zI)=\mu(A)-z<0
. In particular
z=\|A\|+\delta
suffices for any
\delta>0
. Then defining
A^{\prime}=A-zI
we have that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{zt}e^{A^{\prime}t}\boldsymbol{x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!ds\,\,e^{A^{\prime}s}e^{zs}\boldsymbol{b}

\displaystyle=e^{zt}\left(e^{A^{\prime}t}\boldsymbol{x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!ds\,\,e^{A^{\prime}s}e^{z(s-t)}\boldsymbol{b}\right).

(202)

In the homogeneous case the solution to the original ODE can be obtained from
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x}
by rescaling by a scalar, while in the inhomogeneous case
\boldsymbol{b}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
this generates an effective time-dependent driving term, and to account for this we can realize the integrated scaling
e^{-zt}
term if in the algorithm we increment
\boldsymbol{b}
by a factor
e^{-zh}
at each time-step. In particular, we can consider
A=-iH
, for Hermitian
H
, and transform into an ODE with negative log-norm. Given negative log-norm systems have
O(\sqrt{T})
complexity, this would seem to conflict with No Fast-Forwarding, which requires that simulation of general quantum dynamics has complexity
\Omega(T)
. However, the error constraint on the
\dot{x}=A^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x}
system is exponential in
T
since we require
\epsilon^{\prime}_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}=e^{-zT}\epsilon_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
, where
\epsilon^{\prime}_{\mbox{\tiny TD}}
is the error in the system with negative log-norm. This restores the
\Omega(T)
complexity.

Finally, we note that since stable systems have
\|e^{At}\|
upper bounded by an exponentially decreasing function in
t
we can extend the results of
An et al. 2022b
in a straightforward manner to obtain a query complexity lower bound for the dynamics in terms of the associated complexity of inverting the stable matrix
A
. More, precisely, as observed in
An et al. 2022b
the asymptotic dynamics of the inhomogeneous system
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}
tends to an equilibrium for which
A\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\approx\boldsymbol{0}
. This implies that
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\approx A^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
for
t
large and therefore constitutes a linear solver algorithm. This leads to the following complexity lower bound for simulating the dynamics of stable systems.

Theorem 15

(Extended from
An et al. 2022b
, Proposition 19.)
.

Consider
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}
, and assume that
A
is stable. Any quantum algorithm outputting the normalized final state
\ket{x_{F}}
up to error
\epsilon
must have worst-case complexity
\Omega(\min(\log^{\gamma}(1/\epsilon),T^{\gamma}))
assuming the worst-case query complexity for a linear-solver for the equation
A\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}
has complexity
\Omega(\log^{\gamma}(1/\epsilon))
.

The proof of this is identical to the one presented in
An et al. 2022b
with the Euclidean log-norm replaced with the more general log-norm that arises from the Lyapunov analysis.
