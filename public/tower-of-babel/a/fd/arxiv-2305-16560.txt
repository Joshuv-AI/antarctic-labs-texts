Energetic cost for speedy synchronization in non-Hermitian quantum dynamics
Maxwell Aifer, Juzar Thingna, Sebastian Deffner

Abstract
Quantum synchronization is crucial for understanding complex dynamics and holds potential applications in quantum computing and communication. Therefore, assessing the thermodynamic resources required for finite-time synchronization in continuous-variable systems is a critical challenge. In the present work, we find these resources to be extensive for large systems. We also bound the speed of quantum and classical synchronization in coupled damped oscillators with non-Hermitian anti- 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric interactions, and show that the speed of synchronization is limited by the interaction strength relative to the damping. Compared to the classical limit, we find that quantum synchronization is slowed by the non-commutativity of the Hermitian and anti-Hermitian terms. Our general results could be tested experimentally and we suggest an implementation in photonic systems.

In this letter, we apply quantum speed limits and quantum thermodynamics to a general model of continuous-variable quantum systems. A measure of complete synchronization in continuous-variable systems is defined, which is scale-invariant and is sufficient for phase synchronization. We obtain bounds which relate the degree of synchronization to the distance from thermodynamic equilibrium, resulting in an extensive expression for the minimal work necessary to achieve synchronization.

Specifically, we study a quantum master equation that includes both non-Hermitian dynamics and a dissipative term in the Gorini-Kassakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) form, resulting in a non-linear dynamical semigroup. We find that the rate of synchronization is determined by a competition between the irreversible entropy production caused by damping, which slows synchronization, and the strength of the anti-Hermitian coupling, which speeds up synchronization. The resulting upper bound on the synchronization rate has terms of the form of the Mandelstam-Tamm inequality
Mandelstam and Tamm 1991
, where speed scales with the uncertainty of the energy, except in this case even the uncertainties of the Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts of the Hamiltonian are crucial. As an example, we consider a dissipatively coupled photonic dimer. The classical counterpart of all our results are obtained and in case of the dimer we find that the model reduces to the celebrated coupled Stuart-Landau oscillators
Landau 1944
;
Stuart 1960
. For the dimer model we find that the quantum system synchronizes in a parameter regime wherein it is impossible for the classical model to synchronize, thereby displaying a quantum advantage.

Measure of synchronization

We consider
N
quantum oscillators with annihilation operators
\hat{a}_{1}\dots\hat{a}_{N}
. The corresponding dimensionless quadrature operators
\hat{\mathbb{r}}=(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{p}_{1},\dots\hat{x}_{N},\hat{p}_{N})^{T}
read
Serafini 2023
;
Serafini et al. 2004
;
Adesso et al. 2014

\hat{x}_{j}=\frac{\hat{a}_{j}+\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}},\>\>\hat{p}_{j}=\frac{\hat{a}_{j}-\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}}{i\sqrt{2}}.

(1)

For the system to synchronize, the phase space coordinates of the different oscillators need to converge. There can be two distinct types of synchronization: (i) complete synchronization (amplitude and phase synchronization), where the phase space trajectories of multiple subsystems converge, and (ii) phase synchronization, for which tshe phase angles of multiple subsystems converge
Ott 2002
. An intuitive measure to characterize complete synchronization, but not phase synchronization, of a quantum bipartite system is
\mathcal{S}_{c}=2\braket{(\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{2}-\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{1})^{2}}^{-1}

Mari et al. 2013
. The growth of
\mathcal{S}_{c}
does not require phase synchronization,e.g., in case of amplitude death
\mathcal{S}_{c}
always grows but there is no phase synchronization
Giorgi et al. 2019
. Therefore, we define a new measure of
complete
synchronization by looking at the distance between the oscillators
relative
to the total radius in phase space. For a bipartite system, we have

D^{2}\equiv\frac{\braket{(\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{2}-\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{1})^{2}}}{\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}^{2}}},

(2)

and we note that the so-defined
D
is scale-invariant with respect to
\hat{\mathbb{r}}
. Hence, the degree of synchronization does not change when the system is viewed at different magnifications. The bipartite distance measure can be expressed in terms of angular and radial measures of similarity,

D^{2}=1-\mathcal{S}_{r}\mathcal{S}_{\theta},

(3)

where

\mathcal{S}_{\theta}=\frac{\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{1}\cdot\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{2}}}{\left|\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{1}}\right|\left|\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{2}}\right|}\equiv\cos\theta,

(4)

and

\mathcal{S}_{r}=2\sqrt{\frac{\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{1}^{2}}}{\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}^{2}}}\left(1-\frac{\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{1}^{2}}}{\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}^{2}}}\right)}.

(5)

The quantity
\mathcal{S}_{r}
defined above is similar to the binary entropy function
MacKay 2003
, and is maximized when
\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{1}^{2}}=\braket{\hat{\mathbb{r}}_{2}^{2}}
where
\mathcal{S}_{r}=1
. Equations (
4
) and (
5
) reveal that
D^{2}
is between
0
and
2
, with values less than
1
indicating synchronization and values greater than
1
indicating anti-synchronization. It is also clear from the form of Eq. (
3
) that for
D^{2}
to become small, both
\mathcal{S}_{r}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
must approach their maximal values of
1
, implying that we capture complete and phase synchronization by requiring a decay of
D^{2}
in time. For a system of
N
oscillators, Eq. (
2
) can be generalized as

D^{2}\equiv 2\left(1-\frac{\braket{\bar{\mathbb{r}}^{2}}}{\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}}\right),

(6)

where
\bar{\mathbb{r}}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{j},\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{p}_{j}\right)^{T}/N
and
\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}=\braket{\mathbb{r}^{2}}/N
. Equation (
6
) reduces to Eq. (
2
) for two oscillators, and intuitively captures the notion of synchronization in phase space. Moreover,
D^{2}
is non-negative, which follows from Jensen’s inequality.

Throughout this work, we consider scenarios in which the
N
oscillators are initially uncoupled and separately in contact with a thermal bath at inverse temperature
\beta
. Initially, the oscillators are allowed to come to their respective equilibrium states
\hat{\rho}_{j}^{\text{eq}}
, and then a coupling between them is turned on. Hence,
\hat{\rho}_{0}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\rho}_{j}^{\text{eq}}
is our initial state. To quantify if our system has synchronized we will require that the distance
D
becomes small and then
stays
small. In other words, given a
D_{s}
, the system synchronizes to within the distance
D_{s}
if there exists a time
\tau
such that for all
t\geq\tau
,
D\leq D_{s}
. The smallest
\tau
for which this holds will be called the synchronization time
\tau_{s}
.

Dynamics

Further, the considered
N
oscillators have natural frequencies
\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{N}
and an arbitrary anti-Hermitian coupling. The corresponding Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+i\hat{H}_{c}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\left(\hat{n}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+i\hat{H}_{c},

(7)

where
\hat{H}_{c}
is Hermitian,
\hat{n}_{j}
is the number operator
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}
, and we set
\hbar=1
. Non-Hermitian Hamiltonians such as the one in Eq. (
7
) are effective descriptions of
controlled
dissipation in open quantum systems
Ashida et al. 2020
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Bender and Boettcher 1998
;
Song et al. 2019
;
Rotter 2009
;
Ju et al. 2019
;
Moiseyev 2011
;
Gündoğdu et al. 2020
. In addition, our system interacts with a thermal environment
Thingna et al. 2012
;
Becker et al. 2022
, yielding the following quantum master equation (QME)

\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}=-i[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}]+\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}+\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}],

(8)

where
\braket{\hat{O}}=\text{tr}\{\hat{O}\hat{\rho}\}
, and the term
-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}
is included to preserve normalization. This
nonlinear
equation satisfies the convex quasi-linearity condition and the semigroup property making it a valid quantum evolution
Rembieliński and Caban 2021
. The dissipator
\mathcal{D}
takes the GKSL form
\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]=\sum_{i}\hat{F}_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{F}_{i}^{\dagger}-\left\{\hat{F}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{F}_{i},\hat{\rho}\right\}/2
. We also split the Liouvillian into non-interacting
\mathcal{L}_{0}[\hat{\rho}]
and interacting
\mathcal{L}_{c}[\hat{\rho}]
parts given by,

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{0}[\hat{\rho}]&=-i[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}]+\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]\\
\mathcal{L}_{c}[\hat{\rho}]&=\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho},\end{split}

(9)

such that
d{\hat{\rho}}/dt=\mathcal{L}(\hat{\rho})=\mathcal{L}_{0}(\hat{\rho})+\mathcal{L}_{c}(\hat{\rho})
. The state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
is a stationary state of the Hermitian
\hat{H}_{0}
and Lindblad terms,i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{0}(\hat{\rho}_{0})=0
. Note that Eq. (
8
) is nonlinear in
\hat{\rho}
only because of the term
-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}
, as
\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}
itself depends linearly on
\hat{\rho}
. We will first examine the case of general
\hat{H}_{c}
, and later specialize to a specific dimer model which results in a
quantum
Stuart-Landau equation.

Quantum synchronization far from equilibrium

As the system evolves, it will depart from the initial state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
due to the anti-Hermitian coupling. Let
\hat{\rho}_{G;E}
denote a Gibbs state of the uncoupled system with energy
E
,
\hat{\rho}_{G;E}\propto\exp(-\beta_{E}\hat{H}_{0})
, and
\text{tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{G;E}\hat{H}_{0}\}=E
. We introduce a measure
\chi
, an
ergotropy

Allahverdyan et al. 2004
;
Sone and Deffner 2021

of synchronization
, to quantify the departure of the reduced state
\hat{\rho}
from the set of Gibbs states of the uncoupled system,

\chi\equiv\min_{U}S(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\rho}_{G;U}),

(10)

in terms of the quantum relative entropy
S(\hat{\rho}_{1}\|\hat{\rho}_{2})=\text{tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{1}(\ln\hat{\rho}_{1}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{2})\}

Vedral 2002
. The minimization over
U
in Eq. (
10
) means that
\chi
is a property of the state
\hat{\rho}
and does not depend on the environment parameters (i.e., the bath temperature). This is preferable as the degree of synchronization itself should be independent of the bath parameters, so
\chi
should be as well to obtain a meaningful relation between them. In the supplemental material
noa
, we show that for the distance measure
D
to become small,
\chi
must become large, and we generally have

\chi\geq-2(N-1)\ln\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{1/(N-1)}\sqrt{\frac{(N/\kappa)^{N/(N-1)}}{2(N-1)}}D\right),

(11)

where
\kappa=\omega_{\text{min}}/\omega_{\text{max}}
, and for
N=2
the above general expression reduces to

\chi\geq-2\ln\left(\frac{eD}{\sqrt{2}\kappa}\right).

(12)

The analogous bound for classical bipartite systems (see supplemental material
noa
) reads

\chi^{\text{(cl)}}\geq-2\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}D}{\kappa}\right),

(13)

which is defined in terms of the classical relative entropy
Kullback and Leibler 1951
;
Cover and Thomas 2006
. We note that the results (
12
) and (
13
) have been proved for the case of coupled oscillators, but the proof only relies on certain mild assumptions including the positivity of heat capacity, which is generally valid with some exceptions

As stated earlier, we assume the system to begin in the uncoupled equilibrium state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
, and we quantify the departure from this

L=S(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\rho}_{0}).

(14)

Unlike

L\geq\chi\geq\Lambda.

(15)

Above

By assumption,

\tau_{s}
to distance

L\geq\chi_{\text{min}}(N,\kappa,D_{s}).

(16)

Also note that

\dot{L}=\beta\dot{E}-\dot{S}

\dot{E}=\dot{W}-\dot{Q}
, and the second law as
\dot{S}+\beta\dot{Q}\geq 0

Deffner and Lutz 2010

\dot{W}\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\dot{L}.

Above

Rate of quantum synchronization

The rate of evolution of
L
can be found directly from the master equation (
8
)
noa
, and is given by

\dot{L}=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}+2\beta_{0}\mathcal{C}_{CE}-\sigma_{0}.

(22)

where
\mathcal{C}_{CE}=\frac{1}{2}\braket{\hat{H}_{c}\hat{H}_{0}}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\braket{\hat{H}_{0}}
is the covariance of
\hat{H}_{0}
and
\hat{H}_{c}
, and
\sigma_{0}=-\,\text{tr}\left\{\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}](\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\right\}
is the irreversible entropy production
Thingna et al. 2017
of the uncoupled system, which is always non-negative
Spohn 1978
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
. In the case that
\hat{H}_{c}
is a unbounded operator,
\dot{L}
can be bounded from above,

\begin{split}\dot{L}&\leq 2\Delta_{C}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}+S_{G;E}^{2}}\\
&\quad+2\beta_{0}\sqrt{\Delta_{E}^{2}\Delta_{C}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left|\braket{[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{c}]}\right|^{2}}-\sigma_{0}.\end{split}

(23)

Here, we use the von Neumann entropy of the Gibbs state,
S_{G;E}=-\text{tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{G;E}\ln\hat{\rho}_{G;E}\}
, as well as the capacity of entanglement
\mathcal{E}=\,\text{tr}\left\{\hat{\rho}(\ln\hat{\rho})^{2}\right\}-S^{2}
, which is the second moment of surprisal
Shrimali et al. 2022
.

In the special case where
\hat{H}_{c}
is a bounded operator, we may instead use the alternative bound given in the supplemental material
noa
where the capacity of entanglement does not appear. A classical limit of the master equation (
8
) can be derived by identifying a quantum phase space distribution with a classical probability density
Schleich 2001
;
Louisell 1990
;
Graefe et al. 2010
, and a corresponding classical bound (see supplemental material
noa
) reads

\begin{split}\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}&\leq\beta_{0}\braket{\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}}-\braket{\nabla^{2}H_{c}}\\
&\quad+2\Delta_{C}\braket{\ln(f)^{2}}+2\beta_{0}\Delta_{C}\Delta_{E}-\sigma_{0}.\end{split}

(24)

Equation (
24
) differs from Eq. (
23
) because of the presence of the geometric terms associated with phase space flow
Daems and Nicolis 1999
, as well as the absence of the commutator. The interpretation of Eq. (
23
) is as follows: the final term
\sigma_{0}
is the rate of irreversible entropy production
Spohn 1978
which one would obtain in the absence of coupling, and this term will always be negative. Therefore the other terms must have a net positive effect larger than this entropy production rate in order for synchronization to occur. The first term is of the form of a quantum speed limit with the additional factor of entropy. This term unsurprisingly implies that stronger non-Hermitian coupling leads to faster synchronization. The second term is reminiscent of the Mandelstam-Tamm quantum speed limit
Mandelstam and Tamm 1991
, and involves the second moments of both the Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts of the Hamiltonian, however there is a penalty that scales with the square of their commutator. This term arises from the uncertainty relation
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
, and can be explained by the fact that synchronization is most effective when there is a large correlation between the observables corresponding to the Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts of the Hamiltonian.

k\geq|\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}|
(see Fig.
3
and supplemental material
noa
). The corresponding quantum system is also synchronous for
k\geq|\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}|
, and synchronizes for smaller values of
k
than the classical system
noa
. This presents clear evidence of a quantum advantage that leads to a wider regime of synchronization well beyond the classical case.

Concluding remarks

In this work is that we have found the minimal amount of work required for synchronization of an arbitrary number of oscillators, as well as bounded the speed at which synchronization may occur. Our numerical results for the model of a anti-
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian serve to illustrate an experimentally realizable system where this process may occur. Our analysis is formulated mostly in terms of informational quantities, and therefore allows for an information-theoretic interpretation of the synchronization process as a form of communication. This connection could be made more explicit in future work by relating our measure of synchronization to mutual information. It remains to be seen whether there are states that display a quantum advantage, in the sense of Fig.
1
, and a related goal for future work is to understand the apparent quantum advantage displayed in synchronization of the dimer model (see Fig.
3
).

Classical Synchronization Measure

We set

The classical Gibbs distribution is

(37)

II
Quantum synchronization measure

Let
\hat{\mathbb{r}}=(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{p}_{1},\dots\hat{x}_{N},\hat{p}_{N})^{T}
be the dimensionless quadrature operators satisfying the canonical commutation relations

[\hat{x}_{j},\hat{p}_{k}]=i\delta_{jk},

(62)

and
\hat{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}
are the corresponding annihilation and creation operators. The Hamiltonian of the uncoupled system is

\hat{H}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}(\hat{x}_{j}^{2}+\hat{p}_{j}^{2})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\left(\hat{n}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(63)

The Gibbs state is
Deffner and Campbell 2019

\hat{\rho}_{G;U}=\frac{\exp(-\beta_{E}\hat{H}_{0})}{\text{tr}\{\exp(-\beta_{E}\hat{H}_{0})\}}=\frac{1}{Z_{U}}\exp(-\beta_{E}\hat{H}_{0}),

(64)

where
Z_{E}=\text{tr}\{\exp(-\beta_{E}\hat{H}_{0})\}
is the canonical partition function. In the quantum case,
\chi

\chi=\min_{U}S(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\rho}_{G;U}),

(65)

where
S(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\sigma})=\text{tr}\{\hat{\rho}(\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\sigma})\}

Vedral 2002
. We proceed in a similar way to the classical case, and write the relative entropy to the Gibbs state as

\displaystyle S(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\rho}_{G;U})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\{\hat{\rho}(\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{G;U})\}

(66)

\displaystyle=-S+\beta_{U}E+\ln Z_{U}

(67)

\displaystyle=-S+\beta_{U}E+S_{G;U}-\beta_{U}U

(68)

\displaystyle=-S+\beta_{U}(E-U)+S_{G;U}.

(69)

Again, only the last two terms depend on
U
, and we minimize these terms. As in the classical case, temperature is monotonically increasing with energy for the quantum harmonic oscillator. By the same reasoning as in the classical case, we have for all
U
,
E

S_{G;E}\leq\beta_{U}(E-U)+S_{G;U},

(70)

so for fixed
E
and arbitrary
U
,

S(f\|f_{G;E})\leq S(f\|f_{G;U}),

(71)

which completes the proof of Eq. (
65

\chi
can be expressed as

\chi=S_{G;E}-S,

(72)

and we will proceed by finding a lower bound for

S_{1}=\frac{1-\mu}{2\mu}\ln\left(\frac{1+\mu}{1-\mu}\right)-\ln\left(\frac{2\mu}{1+\mu}\right),

(73)

where

\mu=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\left|\Sigma\right|}}.

(74)

Useful upper and lower bounds on this quantity are

\ln(2\sqrt{\left|\Sigma\right|})\leq S_{1}\leq\ln(e\sqrt{\left|\Sigma\right|}).

(75)

The Gibbs state of the uncoupled Hamiltonian is a tensor product state, so its von Neumann entropy is additive, meaning

S_{G;E}\geq\sum_{j}\ln\left(2\sqrt{\left|\Sigma_{j}\right|}\right)=\ln\left(2^{N}\sqrt{\left|\Sigma_{G;E}\right|}\right),

(76)

where we have also used the fact that the covariance matrix is diagonal in the Gibbs state
Serafini 2023
. By the same steps as we used in the classical case, we have

\sqrt{\left|\Sigma_{G;E}\right|}\geq\left(\frac{\kappa\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}}{2}\right)^{N},

(77)

so

S_{G;E}\geq N\ln\left(\kappa\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}\right).

(78)

We also require an upper bound on
S
. This can be obtained by noting that the von Neumann entropy is always sub-additive, so

S\leq\sum_{j}\ln\left(e\sqrt{\left|\Sigma_{j}\right|}\right)=\ln\left(e^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{\left|\Sigma_{j}\right|}\right).

(79)

Again we may make a change of basis to the operators
\hat{c}_{1}\dots\hat{c}_{2N}
, which are defined in the same way as in the classical case, but have been promoted to operators. By Fischer’s inequality,

S\leq\ln\left(e^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}\braket{c_{j}^{2}}\right),

(80)

and so the same steps in the classical case give the result

S\leq\ln\left(e^{N}\frac{N^{N}(N-1)}{(2N-2)^{N}}\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}\left(\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}-\braket{\bar{\mathbb{r}}^{2}}\right)^{N-1}\right)

(81)

We then have the lower bound for
\chi
,

\chi\geq\ln\left(\frac{(2N-2)^{N}}{e^{N}N^{N}(N-1)}\frac{\left(\kappa\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}\right)^{N}}{\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}\left(\braket{\overline{\mathbb{r}^{2}}}-\braket{\bar{\mathbb{r}}^{2}}\right)^{N-1}}\right)\\
,

(82)

and we see that the argument of the log differs from the classical case by a factor of
(2/e)^{N}
. Therefore we have the lower bound

\chi_{\text{min}}=\chi_{\text{min}}^{\text{(cl)}}-N(1-\ln 2).

(83)

Explicitly,

\chi\geq-2(N-1)\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{(eN/2\kappa)^{N/(N-1)}}{2(N-1)}}D\right).

(84)

For
N=2
this reduces to

\chi\geq-2\ln\left(\frac{eD}{\sqrt{2}\kappa}\right).

(85)

In the limit of large

, the lower bound in Eq. (

\chi_{\text{min:as}}=-2\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{e}D}{2\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)N,

(86)

And again, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\chi_{\text{min}}}{\chi_{\text{min:as}}}=1.

(87)

III
Quantum synchronization speed limit

We assume that the generator of dynamics has the form

\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}]=-i[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}]+\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}+\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}],

(88)

where

\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]=\sum_{i}\hat{F}_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{F}_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{F}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{F}_{i},\hat{\rho}\right\}.

(89)

We also define

\mathcal{L}_{0}[\hat{\rho}]=-i[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}]+\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}],

(90)

\mathcal{L}_{c}[\hat{\rho}]=\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}.

(91)

Here we derive a bound on the rate at which the quantity
L
can change, which can then be used to determine the minimal time for quantum synchronization. Recall that
L
is defined as

L=\text{tr}\{\hat{\rho}(\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\},

(92)

and its time-derivative can be expressed as
Breuer and Petruccione 2002

\dot{L}=\text{tr}\{\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\}.

(93)

Using the definition
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\mathcal{L}_{c}
, we have

\dot{L}=\dot{L}_{0}+\dot{L}_{c},

(94)

where

\dot{L}_{0}=\text{tr}\{\mathcal{L}_{0}[\hat{\rho}](\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\},

(95)

and

\dot{L}_{c}=\text{tr}\{\mathcal{L}_{c}[\hat{\rho}](\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\}.

(96)

Evaluating
\dot{L}_{0}
, we find

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{0}

\displaystyle=-i\text{tr}\{[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}](\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\}+\text{tr}\{\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}](\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\}

(97)

\displaystyle=-i\text{tr}\{\hat{H}_{0}[\hat{\rho},\ln\hat{\rho}]-[\ln\hat{\rho}_{0},\hat{H}_{0}]\hat{\rho}_{0}]\}+\text{tr}\{\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}](\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\}

(98)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\{\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}](\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\},

(99)

where we used the fact that
[\hat{\rho},\ln\hat{\rho}]=0
and
[\hat{H}_{0},\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}]=0
. Due to a result by Spohn
Spohn 1978
, the above can be equated to the negative irreversible entropy production rate of the uncoupled system, which we call
-\sigma_{0}

\dot{L}_{0}=-\sigma_{0}.

(100)

We next evaluate
\dot{L}_{c}
,

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{c}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left\{\left(\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}\right)(\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\right\}

(101)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left\{\left(\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}\right)\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}-\text{tr}\left\{\left(\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}\right)\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}\right\}

(102)

\displaystyle=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}-\text{tr}\left\{\left(\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}\right)\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}\right\}

(103)

\displaystyle=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}-\text{tr}\left\{\left(\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}\right)(-\beta_{0}\hat{H}_{0}-\ln Z_{0})\right\}

(104)

\displaystyle=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}+\ln Z_{0}\>\text{tr}\left\{\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}\right\}+\beta_{0}\>\text{tr}\left\{\left(\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}\right)\hat{H}_{0}\right\}

(105)

\displaystyle=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}+\beta_{0}\>\text{tr}\left\{\left(\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}\right)\hat{H}_{0}\right\}

(106)

\displaystyle=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}+\beta_{0}\>\text{tr}\left\{\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}\hat{H}_{0}\right\}-2\beta_{0}\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\braket{\hat{H}_{0}}

(107)

\displaystyle=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}+\beta_{0}\>\text{tr}\left\{\{\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{c}\}\hat{\rho}\right\}-2\beta_{0}\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\braket{\hat{H}_{0}}

(108)

\displaystyle=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}+2\beta_{0}\mathcal{C}_{CE},

(109)

where the covariance
\mathcal{C}_{CE}
is defined as
Serafini 2023

\mathcal{C}_{CE}=\frac{1}{2}\braket{\{\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{c}\}}-\braket{\hat{H}_{0}}\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}.

(111)

Putting together the expressions for
\dot{L}_{0}
and
\dot{L}_{c}
, we get

\dot{L}=2\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}+2\beta_{0}\mathcal{C}_{CE}-\sigma_{0}.

(112)

It follows from the above analysis that this can be written as

\dot{L}=\beta_{0}\dot{E}_{c}-\dot{S}_{c}-\sigma_{0},

(113)

with

\dot{E}_{c}=\text{tr}\{\mathcal{L}_{c}[\hat{\rho}]\hat{H}_{0}\}=2\mathcal{C}_{CE},

(114)

and

\dot{S}_{c}=-\text{tr}\{\mathcal{L}_{c}[\hat{\rho}]\ln\hat{\rho}\}=-2\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}.

(115)

In order to bound the first term in Eq. (
112
), we expand the density operator in its eigenbasis,

\hat{\rho}=\sum_{j}p_{j}\ket{\phi_{j}}\bra{\phi_{j}},

(116)

so this term takes the form

-\dot{S}_{c}=2\sum_{j}(\braket{\phi_{j}|\hat{H}_{c}|\phi_{j}}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})p_{j}\ln p_{j}.

(117)

In the case that
\hat{H}_{c}
is a bounded operator, we have in terms of the operator norm

\left|\braket{\phi_{j}|\hat{H}_{c}|\phi_{j}}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\right|\leq 2\|\hat{H}_{c}\|,

(118)

leading to the bound

\left|\dot{S}_{c}\right|\leq 4\|\hat{H}_{c}\|S.

(119)

Finally, we can bound the entropy by the Gibbs entropy of the uncoupled equilibrium state with the same value of
\braket{H_{0}}
, to give

\left|\dot{S}_{c}\right|\leq 4\|\hat{H}_{c}\|S_{G;E}.

(120)

If
\hat{H}_{c}
is not bounded, as is often the case in the continuous variable setting, we may bound
\dot{S}_{c}
using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality as follows

\displaystyle\left|\dot{S}_{c}\right|

\displaystyle=2\left|\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}\right|

(121)

\displaystyle=2\left|\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})\sqrt{\hat{\rho}}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}}\ln\hat{\rho}\right\}\right|

(122)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\,\text{tr}\left\{(\hat{H}_{c}-\braket{\hat{H}_{c}})^{2}\hat{\rho}\right\}}\sqrt{\,\text{tr}\left\{\hat{\rho}(\ln\hat{\rho})^{2}\right\}}

(123)

\displaystyle=2\Delta_{C}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}+S^{2}},

(124)

where
\mathcal{E}
is known as the capacity for entanglement, and is defined as
Shrimali et al. 2022

\mathcal{E}=\,\text{tr}\left\{\hat{\rho}(\ln\hat{\rho})^{2}\right\}-S^{2}.

(125)

We again use the Gibbs entropy of the uncoupled equilibrium state to upper bound the entropy, giving

\left|\dot{S}_{c}\right|\leq 2\Delta_{C}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}+S_{G;E}^{2}}.

(126)

Using the Schrodinger uncertainty relation
Nielsen and Chuang 2000

\Delta_{E}^{2}\Delta_{C}^{2}\geq|\mathcal{C}_{CE}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left|\braket{[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{c}]}\right|^{2},

(127)

so

|\mathcal{C}_{CE}|\leq\sqrt{\Delta_{E}^{2}\Delta_{C}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left|\braket{[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{c}]}\right|^{2}}.

(128)

Using the bounds obtained above, in the case that
\hat{H}_{c}
is bounded we find

\dot{L}\leq 4\|\hat{H}_{c}\|S_{G;E}+2\beta_{0}\sqrt{\Delta_{E}^{2}\Delta_{C}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left|\braket{[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{c}]}\right|^{2}}-\sigma_{0}.

(129)

In the case where
\hat{H}_{c}
is unbounded, instead we have

\dot{L}\leq 2\Delta_{C}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}+S_{G;E}^{2}}+2\beta_{0}\sqrt{\Delta_{E}^{2}\Delta_{C}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left|\braket{[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{c}]}\right|^{2}}-\sigma_{0}.

(130)

IV
Classical Master Equation

Graefe et al
Graefe et al. 2010
give a recipe for obtaining a classical limit of a non-Hermitian von Neumann equation (without a Lindblad form dissipator). The equation thus obtained is deterministic, and describes the evolution of a system whose initial distribution is a delta function in the phase space, and which remains a delta function over time. In order to find the evolution of a distribution, it is not permissible to simply use the Liouville equation with the drift velocity obtained by Graefe et al, as the total quantum master equation is nonlinear, and therefore we cannot take a linear superposition of the delta function solutions. Here we generalize the arguments from
Graefe et al. 2010
to the case of classical probability distributions, and also allow for a Fokker-Planck dissipative term
Zwanzig 2001
, resulting in a classical master equation analogous to Eq. (
88
).

We first write the Hamiltonian part of the quantum master equation as

\mathcal{L}_{H}[\hat{\rho}]=-i[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}]+\{\hat{H}_{c},\hat{\rho}\}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}=-i[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}]+[\hat{H}_{c},\rho]+2\hat{\rho}\hat{H}_{c}-2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\hat{\rho}.

(131)

We consider the evolution of the quantities
\braket{\hat{a}_{j}}
due to the Hamiltonian part only,

\,\text{tr}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{H}[\hat{\rho}]\hat{a}_{j}\right\}=-i\braket{[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{H}_{0}]}+\braket{[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{H}_{c}]}+2\braket{\hat{H}_{c}\hat{a}_{j}}-2\braket{\hat{a}_{j}}\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}.

(132)

Whereas Graefe et al assume the system is in a coherent state
\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}
, we allow for a general state which can be expressed as a sum of coherent states in the P representation
Schleich 2001
,

\hat{\rho}=\int d^{2N}\boldsymbol{\alpha}P(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\bra{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},

(133)

where
P(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
is known as the P function of the state
\hat{\rho}
. So we have

\displaystyle\,\text{tr}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{H}[\hat{\rho}]\hat{a}_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int d^{2N}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\,P(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\left(-i\braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{H}_{0}]|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+\braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{H}_{c}]|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+2\braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\hat{H}_{c}\hat{a}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-2\braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\hat{a}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\right)

(134)

\displaystyle=\int d^{2N}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\,P(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\left(-i\braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{H}_{0}]|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+\braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{H}_{c}]|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+2\alpha_{j}\braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\hat{H}_{c}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-2\alpha_{j}\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\right).

(135)

Making use of the formal representation
Louisell 1990
;
Graefe et al. 2010

[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{X}]=\frac{\partial\hat{X}}{\partial\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}},\>\>\>\>[\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger},\hat{X}]=-\frac{\partial\hat{X}}{\partial\hat{a}_{j}}

(136)

we have

\displaystyle\,\text{tr}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{H}[\hat{\rho}]\hat{a}_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int d^{2N}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\,P(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\left(-i\Braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\frac{\partial\hat{H}_{0}}{\partial\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+\Braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\frac{\partial\hat{H}_{c}}{\partial\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+2\alpha_{j}\braket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\hat{H}_{c}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-2\alpha_{j}\braket{\hat{H}_{c}}\right)

(137)

We now introduce the classical phase space coordinates
\mathbb{r}=(q_{1},p_{1},q_{2},p_{2})^{T}
, as well as complex coordinates
z_{j}=(q_{j}+ip_{j})/\sqrt{2}
. The Hamiltonian can be viewed as a function of the quadrature variables
H(\mathbb{r})
or of the complex coordinates
H(\mathbb{z},\mathbb{z}^{*})
. Because the quantum Hamiltonian is generally represented in terms of the operators
\hat{a}_{j}
and
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}
, we will treat it here as a function of
z_{j}
and
z^{*}_{j}
, which respectively take the place of
\hat{a}_{j}
and
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}
. To obtain a classical limit, we will replace averages as
\braket{\hat{a}_{j}}\to\braket{z_{j}}
,
\braket{\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}}\to\braket{z_{j}^{*}}
. The classical probability density function
f
will be treated as the limiting form of the P function. Finally, an operator which can be expressed as an analytic function
\hat{X}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})
can be cast as a function of complex variable
z
as
X(z,z)=\braket{z|\hat{X}|z}
, where
\ket{z}
is the coherent state. With these substitutions, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\braket{z_{j}^{H}}

\displaystyle=\int d^{2N}\boldsymbol{z}\,f(\boldsymbol{z})\left(-i\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial z_{j}^{*}}+\frac{\partial H_{c}}{\partial z_{j}^{*}}+2z_{j}H_{c}-2z_{j}\braket{H_{c}}\right).

(138)

Note that in the above, the average
\braket{H_{c}}
is interpreted as

\braket{H_{c}}=\int d^{2N}\boldsymbol{z}f(\boldsymbol{z})H_{c}(\boldsymbol{z}),

(139)

and so the last term makes the equation nonlinear in
f
. Taking a delta function
f(\boldsymbol{z})=\delta(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{Z})
, we see that the last two terms in Eq. (
138
) cancel, and that we are left with

\dot{Z_{j}}=-i\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial z_{j}^{*}}+\frac{\partial H_{c}}{\partial z_{j}^{*}}.

(140)

Looking at the first term, we see that

\displaystyle-i\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial z_{j}^{*}}

\displaystyle=-i\left(\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial q_{j}}\frac{\partial q_{j}}{\partial z_{j}^{*}}+\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial z_{j}^{*}}\right)

(141)

\displaystyle=-i\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial q_{j}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}i}\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial p_{j}}\right)

(142)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(-i\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial q_{j}}+\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial q_{j}}\right).

(143)

Noting that
\dot{q}_{j}=\sqrt{2}\Re[\dot{z}_{j}]
and
\dot{p}_{j}=\sqrt{2}\Im[\dot{z}_{j}]
, we see that Hamilton’s equations are satisfied for this term. We may write this succinctly using the symplectic form
\Omega

Serafini 2023
as

\dot{\mathbb{r}}_{0}=\Omega\nabla H_{0}.

(144)

Looking at the
H_{c}
term, we find by the same process

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H_{c}}{\partial z_{j}^{*}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{\partial H_{c}}{\partial q_{j}}+i\frac{\partial H_{c}}{\partial q_{j}}\right),

(145)

so instead of Hamilton’s equations this term gives

\dot{\mathbb{r}}_{c}=\nabla H_{c}.

(146)

Therefore the classical evolution due to the Hamiltonian part for a delta function distribution is

\mathcal{L}_{H}^{\text{(cl)}}[f]=-\nabla\cdot\left(\left[\Omega\nabla H_{0}+\nabla H_{c}\right]f\right).

(147)

Note that the first term can be written using the Poisson bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\text{P}}
, as

-\nabla\cdot([\Omega\nabla H_{0}]f)=\{H_{0},f\}_{\text{P}}.

(148)

However, when the distribution
f
is not a delta function the last two terms of Eq. (
138
) in general do not cancel. It is easily seen that Eq. (
138
) is satisfied if we include the additional term
2(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f
in the generator, giving

\mathcal{L}_{H}^{\text{(cl)}}[f]=-\nabla\cdot\left(\left[\Omega\nabla H_{0}+\nabla H_{c}\right]f\right)+2(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f.

(149)

Equation (
149
) generalizes the result from
Graefe et al. 2010
by including the nonlinear term corresponding to that in Eq. (
88
). Finally, we generalize further by allowing for a Fokker-Planck form dissipator
\mathcal{D}^{\text{(cl)}}
, which plays a similar role to the Lindblad dissipator
\mathcal{D}^{\text{(q)}}
in Eq. (
88
)

\mathcal{L}^{\text{(cl)}}[f]=-\nabla\cdot\left(\left[\Omega\nabla H_{0}+\nabla H_{c}\right]f\right)+2(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f+\mathcal{L}^{\text{(cl)}}[f],

(150)

where

\mathcal{D}^{\text{(cl)}}[f]=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbb{v}_{\text{d}}f)+\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}(\Gamma_{ij}f),

(151)

with drift velocity
\mathbb{v}_{\text{d}}
and a
4\times 4
diffusion tensor
\Gamma
.

V
Classical synchronization rate

In the classical case, we again have

\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}=\beta_{0}\dot{E}-\dot{S}^{\text{(cl)}}.

(152)

As in the quantum case, the first Hamiltonian term (with
H_{0}
) in Eq (
150
) contributes no change to the energy or the entropy, and therefore can be ignored. We first look at the effects of the second Hamiltonian term (with
H_{c}
). The change in energy due to this term can be expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{c}

\displaystyle=\int d^{2N}\mathbb{r}f(\mathbb{r})\dot{E}_{c}(\mathbb{r})

(153)

\displaystyle=\int d^{2N}\mathbb{r}f(\mathbb{r})\dot{\mathbb{r}}_{c}(\mathbb{r})\cdot\nabla H_{0}

(154)

\displaystyle=\int d^{2N}\mathbb{r}f(\mathbb{r})\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}

(155)

\displaystyle=\Braket{\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}}.

(156)

It has been shown
Daems and Nicolis 1999
that in the absence of diffusion, a Liouville-type equation has entropy generation simply given by the average divergence of the drift velocity, and so

\dot{S}^{\text{(cl)}}_{c}=\braket{\nabla^{2}H_{c}}.

(157)

Therefore the contribution of the second Hamiltonian term to
\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}
is

\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}_{c}=\beta_{0}\braket{\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}}-\braket{\nabla^{2}H_{c}}.

(158)

Next we consider the effects of the nonlinear term in Eq. (
150
)

\displaystyle\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}_{\text{NL}}

\displaystyle=2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f(\ln f-\ln f_{0})

(159)

\displaystyle=2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f-2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f_{0}

(160)

\displaystyle=2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f-2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f(-\beta_{0}H_{0}-\ln Z_{0})

(161)

\displaystyle=2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f+2\ln Z_{0}\left(\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\right)+2\beta_{0}\left(\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}H_{0}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\right)

(162)

\displaystyle=2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f+2\beta_{0}\left(\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}H_{0}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\right)

(163)

\displaystyle=2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f+2\beta_{0}\left(\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}H_{0}H_{c}f-\braket{H_{c}}\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}H_{0}f\right)

(164)

\displaystyle=2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f+2\beta_{0}(\braket{H_{0}H_{c}}-\braket{H_{0}}\braket{H_{c}})

(165)

\displaystyle=2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f+2\beta_{0}\mathcal{C}_{CE}.

(166)

Now we have the dynamical equation for
L^{\text{(cl)}}

\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}=\beta_{0}\braket{\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}}-\braket{\nabla^{2}H_{c}}+2\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f+2\beta_{0}\mathcal{C}_{CE}-\sigma_{0}.

(167)

First, suppose that the function
H_{c}
is bounded, in which case we define the infinity norm

\|H_{c}\|_{\infty}=\max_{\mathbb{r}}\left|H_{c}(\mathbb{r})\right|.

(168)

We then have the absolute value of the third term bounded by
4\|H_{c}\|_{\infty}S^{\text{(cl)}}
, so

\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}\leq\beta_{0}\braket{\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}}-\braket{\nabla^{2}H_{c}}+4\|H_{c}\|_{\infty}S^{\text{(cl)}}_{G;E}+2\beta_{0}\mathcal{C}_{CE}-\sigma_{0}.

(169)

And because
\mathcal{C}_{CE}\leq\Delta_{C}\Delta_{E}
,

\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}\leq\beta_{0}\braket{\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}}-\braket{\nabla^{2}H_{c}}+4\|H_{c}\|_{\infty}S^{\text{(cl)}}_{G;E}+2\beta_{0}\Delta_{C}\Delta_{E}-\sigma_{0}.

(170)

If, on the other hand,
H_{c}
is not bounded, then we may use the Cauchy Schwartz inequality to write

\displaystyle\left|\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})f\ln f\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})\sqrt{f}\sqrt{f}\ln f\right|

(171)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}(H_{c}-\braket{H_{c}})^{2}f}\sqrt{\int d^{4}\mathbb{r}f(\ln f)^{2}}

(172)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{C}\braket{\ln(f)^{2}},

(173)

so

\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}\leq\beta_{0}\braket{\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}}-\braket{\nabla^{2}H_{c}}+2\Delta_{C}\braket{\ln(f)^{2}}+2\beta_{0}\mathcal{C}_{CE}-\sigma_{0}.

(174)

Again because
\mathcal{C}_{CE}\leq\Delta_{C}\Delta_{E}
,

\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}\leq\beta_{0}\braket{\nabla H_{c}\cdot\nabla H_{0}}-\braket{\nabla^{2}H_{c}}+2\Delta_{C}\braket{\ln(f)^{2}}+2\beta_{0}\Delta_{C}\Delta_{E}-\sigma_{0}.

(175)

VI
Quantum dimer model

We consider the following coupling Hamiltonian

H_{c}=i\frac{k}{2}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{a}_{2}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{a}_{1}).

(176)

For Lindblad operators we use

\hat{F}_{i-}=\hat{a}_{i}^{2}\>\>\>\hat{F}_{i+}=(\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger})^{2}.

(177)

We first find the stationary state of a single mode under these dynamics when the anti-Hermitian coupling is turned off. The single-mode dissipator is

\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]=\gamma_{+}\mathcal{D}_{+}[\hat{\rho}]+\gamma_{-}\mathcal{D}_{-}[\hat{\rho}],

(178)

where

\mathcal{D}_{-}[\hat{\rho}]=\hat{a}^{2}\hat{\rho}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\{(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\hat{a}^{2},\hat{\rho}\}

(179)

and

\mathcal{D}_{+}[\hat{\rho}]=(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}^{2}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2},\hat{\rho}\}.

(180)

We compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{-}[\ket{n}\bra{n}]

\displaystyle=n(n-1)\ket{n-2}\bra{n-2}-n(n-1)\ket{n}\bra{n}

(181)

\displaystyle=n(n-1)(\ket{n-2}\bra{n-2}-\ket{n}\bra{n}),

(182)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{+}[\ket{n}\bra{n}]

\displaystyle=(n+1)(n+2)\ket{n+2}\bra{n+2}-(n+1)(n+2)\ket{n}\bra{n}

(183)

\displaystyle=(n+1)(n+2)(\ket{n+2}\bra{n+2}-\ket{n}\bra{n}).

(184)

We assume the stationary state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
is diagonal in the number basis, which ensures that the Hamiltonian term is eliminated

\hat{\rho}_{0}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\ket{n}\bra{n}.

(185)

We then have

\braket{m|\mathcal{D}_{-}[\hat{\rho}]|m}=-m(m-1)p_{m}+(m+2)(m+1)p_{m+2}

(186)

and

\braket{m|\mathcal{D}_{+}[\hat{\rho}]|m}=-(m+1)(m+2)p_{m}+(m-1)mp_{m-2},

(187)

so

\braket{m|\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]|m}=\gamma_{+}\left[m(m-1)p_{m-2}-(m+1)(m+2)p_{m}\right]+\gamma_{-}\left[(m+1)(m+2)p_{m+2}-m(m-2)p_{m}\right]

(188)

Assuming
p_{m}=C\exp(-\xi m)
, we then have

\displaystyle\braket{m|\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]|m}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{+}m(m-1)\exp(2\xi)p_{m}-\left[\gamma_{+}(m+1)(m+2)+\gamma_{-}m(m-1)\right]p_{m}+\gamma_{-}(m+1)(m+2)\exp(-2\xi)

(189)

\displaystyle=\left[\gamma_{+}\exp(2\xi)-\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}+\gamma_{-}\exp(-2\xi)\right]p_{m}m^{2}+\left[-\gamma_{+}\exp(2\xi)-3\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-}+3\gamma_{-}\exp(-2\xi)\right]p_{m}m

(190)

\displaystyle+\left[-2\gamma_{+}+2\gamma_{-}\exp(-2\xi)\right].

(191)

We set
\braket{m|\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]|m}=0
for all
m
, and therefore the terms of order zero, one, and two in
m
must be identically zero. We find

2\gamma_{-}\exp(-2\xi)=2\gamma_{+}

(192)

\xi=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\gamma_{-}}{\gamma_{+}}\right).

(193)

This is simply the Gibbs state of the harmonic oscillator with inverse temperature

\beta=\frac{\xi}{\omega}=\frac{1}{2\omega}\ln\left(\frac{\gamma_{-}}{\gamma_{+}}\right).

(194)

Because there is no interaction by assumption, the Gibbs state of the combined system is the tensor product

\hat{\rho}_{0}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\rho}_{G;\beta_{j}},

(195)

where

\beta_{j}=\frac{1}{2\omega_{j}}\ln\left(\frac{\gamma_{j-}}{\gamma_{j+}}\right).

(196)

If we fix
\gamma_{j+}=\gamma_{+}
and treat the frequencies
\omega_{j}
as free parameters, may then choose

\gamma_{j-}=\exp(2\beta\omega_{j})\gamma_{+},

(197)

which is the form of the fluctuation dissipation theorem for this system. The commutator which appears in Eq. (
130
) is

\displaystyle[H_{0},H_{c}]

\displaystyle=i\frac{k}{2}[\omega_{1}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{a}_{1}+\omega_{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{a}_{2},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{a}_{2}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{a}_{1}]

(198)

\displaystyle=i\frac{k}{2}(\omega_{1}a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}+\omega_{2}a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{1}-\omega_{1}a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{1}-\omega_{2}a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2})

(199)

\displaystyle=i\frac{k}{2}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})(a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}-a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{1}),

(200)

so

\dot{L}\leq 2\Delta_{C}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}+S_{G;E}^{2}}+2\beta_{0}\sqrt{\Delta_{E}^{2}\Delta_{C}^{2}-\frac{1}{8}k^{2}|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|^{2}\left|\braket{a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}-a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{1}}\right|^{2}}-\sigma_{0}.

(201)

VII
Classical dimer model

In the classical limit of the dimer model, the function
H_{c}
is

H_{c}=\frac{k}{2}(x_{1}x_{2}+p_{1}p_{2}),

(202)

and

H_{0}=\frac{\omega_{1}}{2}(x_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2})+\frac{\omega_{2}}{2}(x_{2}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}).

(203)

Therefore

\nabla H_{0}=(\omega_{1}x_{1},\omega_{1}p_{1},\omega_{2}x_{2},\omega_{2}p_{2})^{T},

(204)

while

\nabla H_{c}=\left(\frac{k}{2}x_{2},\frac{k}{2}p_{2},\frac{k}{2}x_{1},\frac{k}{2}p_{1}\right)^{T},

(205)

We then find

\nabla H_{0}\cdot\nabla H_{c}=\frac{k}{2}(\omega_{1}x_{1}x_{2}+\omega_{2}p_{2}p_{2}+\omega_{2}x_{1}x_{2}+\omega_{2}p_{1}p_{2})=\frac{k(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}{2}\mathbb{r}_{1}\cdot\mathbb{r}_{2},

(206)

and

\nabla^{2}H_{c}=0,

(207)

so

\dot{L}^{\text{(cl)}}\leq\beta_{0}\frac{k(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}{2}\braket{\mathbb{r}_{1}\cdot\mathbb{r}_{2}}+2\Delta_{C}\sqrt{\braket{\ln(f)^{2}}}+2\beta_{0}\mathcal{C}_{CE}-\sigma_{0}.

(208)
