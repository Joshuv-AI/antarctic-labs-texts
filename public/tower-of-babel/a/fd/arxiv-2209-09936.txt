Solving A Class of Fredholm Integral Equations of the First Kind via Wasserstein Gradient Flows
Francesca R. Crucinio, Valentin De Bortoli, Arnaud Doucet, Adam M. Johansen

Abstract
Solving Fredholm equations of the first kind is crucial in many areas of the applied sciences. In this work we consider integral equations featuring kernels which may be expressed as scalar multiples of conservative (i.e. Markov) kernels and we adopt a variational point of view by considering a minimization problem in the space of probability measures with an entropic regularization. Contrary to classical approaches which discretize the domain of the solutions, we introduce an algorithm to asymptotically sample from the unique solution of the regularized minimization problem. As a result our estimators do not depend on any underlying grid and have better scalability properties than most existing methods. Our algorithm is based on a particle approximation of the solution of a McKean–Vlasov stochastic differential equation associated with the Wasserstein gradient flow of our variational formulation. We prove the convergence towards a minimizer and provide practical guidelines for its numerical implementation. Finally, our method is compared with other approaches on several examples including density deconvolution and epidemiology.

Fredholm integral equations of the first kind are
typically defined by

g(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\mathrm{k}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x

(2)

where the kernel
\mathrm{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is known, and
g:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is known but indirectly observed, and the objective is to identify the unknown function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. If we interpret this as a Lebesgue integral with
\mathrm{d}x
denoting the Lebesgue measure then we may identify the functions with measures
\mu(\mathrm{d}y):=g(y)\mathrm{d}y,\pi(\mathrm{d}x):=f(x)\mathrm{d}x
and
\mathrm{k}
with an integral operator
\mathrm{K}(x,\mathrm{d}y):=\mathrm{k}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y
which leads to the representation that we will consider throughout this paper:

\textstyle{\mu(\mathsf{A})=\pi\mathrm{K}(\mathsf{A}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{K}(x,\mathsf{A})\mathrm{d}\pi(x),}

(3)

for any Borel set
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
, with
\pi
an unknown measure on

\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mu
an observed measure on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and

\mathrm{K}:\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\to\mathbb{R}
an integral operator. These equations
model the task of reconstructing the signal
\pi
from a distorted observed
version
\mu

concerned with the reconstruction of signals that are
a priori
known to
be non-negative
[
28
]

\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
are probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p}

\mathrm{K}
is a Markov kernel. A great many Fredholm integral
equations, particularly those for which the kernel is conservative up to a scalar multiplier in the sense that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathrm{K}(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathrm{K}(x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}y)
for every
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

normalization
[
25

In a probabilistic and variational approach, we seek a solution of (
3
)
by minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence between
\mu
and
\pi\mathrm{K}

[
81

\displaystyle\textstyle{\pi^{\star}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}\{\textup{KL}\left(\mu|\pi\mathrm{K}\right)\,:\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\},}

(4)

\textup{KL}\left(\mu|\pi\mathrm{K}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\log((\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\pi\mathrm{K})(y))\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\mu
admits a density w.r.t.
\pi\mathrm{K}

\pi^{\star}
corresponds to the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for
\pi
. Using this formulation, it is possible to define approximate
solutions of Fredholm integral equations of the first kind as solutions of a minimization
problem on the space of measures. However, in most interesting cases, the integral equation (
3
)
is ill-posed,
i.e.
there might exists more than one solution
\pi

to (
3

respect to changes in
\mu
. This lack of stability is the primary
concern when attempting to solve Fredholm integral equations of the first kind: in practical applications we often only have access to (noisy) observations from
\mu
and not to its analytic form, and the instability of (
3
) means that small errors in
\mu
do not necessarily correspond to small errors in the recovered solution
\pi

[
65
, Chapter 15]
. This issue
reflects onto the minimization problem (
4
), which often does not admit a unique
minimizer
[
66
]
.

In order to circumvent this issue, a regularization term is often added to the
variational formulation (
4
)
[
19
,
48
]

[

57

\pi^{\star}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}\{\mathscr{F}_{\alpha}(\pi)\,:\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\},\qquad\mathscr{F}_{\alpha}(\pi)=\textup{KL}\left(\mu|\pi\mathrm{K}\right)+\alpha\textup{KL}\left(\pi|\pi_{0}\right),

The parameter

[

1

Recently,

introduced a sequential Monte Carlo method (SMC-EMS) to approximately solve (

The functional (

In this work, we
adopt a probabilistic approach and present a novel particle algorithm to solve Fredholm integral equations of the first kind. More precisely, we derive a particle approximation to the Stochastic
Differential Equation (SDE) associated with the Wasserstein gradient flow

[
2
,
91
]

minimizing surrogates of (
5
).
Wasserstein gradient flows have been widely used to solve a variety of optimization problems in the space of probability measures
[
5
,
60
,
68
,
41
]
, including finding solutions to finite dimensional inverse problems
[
44
]
, but, to the best of our knowledge, not for infinite dimensional inverse problems like the integral equation (
3
).
The SDE associated with this
gradient flow is non-linear in the sense that it depends not only upon the
position of the solution but also on its marginal distribution. Such SDEs are
called McKean–Vlasov SDEs (MKVSDE), and have been actively studied during the past decades

[
97
,
96
,
74
,
75
,
84
,
72
,
22
,
47
]
. In
this work, we derive a particular MKVSDE whose solution converges towards a minimizer of (
5
).
However, this MKVSDE cannot be solved analytically, and we introduce a numerical approximation using an Euler–Maruyama discretization and a particle
system.

We perform an extensive simulation study in which we compare our method with SMC-EMS and problem-specific algorithms and show that it achieves comparable performances. The results obtained with our method and SMC-EMS are particularly similar, since both methods aim at minimizing a regularized Kullback–Leibler divergence. However, while in this work we explicitly have a minimization problem for the regularized functional (
5
), the SMC-EMS algorithm does not minimize a particular
functional but corresponds to a smoothed version of the EM algorithm minimizing
the Kullback–Leibler divergence (
4
).
When
\alpha
and the smoothness parameter controlling the amount of regularization in SMC-EMS are both small, the two algorithms will therefore give very similar reconstructions.
Nevertheless, the method proposed in this work comes with a number of additional theoretical guarantees and practical improvements. From the theoretical point of view, we show that (
5
) admits a unique minimizer for any
\alpha
and that this minimizer can be seen as the probabilistic counterpart of the classical solution to the Tikhonov regularized least-squares problem. On the other hand, uniqueness of the fixed point of the EMS recursion approximated by SMC-EMS has not been established.
From the practical point of view, minimizing (
5
) allows us to introduce additional information about the solution through
\pi_{0}
, this proves particularly beneficial when the kernel
\mathrm{K}
leads to model misspecification (see Section
5.2
).
When compared on higher dimensional problems, the proposed method outperforms both standard discretization-based methods and SMC-EMS (Section
5.4
), opening the door to solving Fredholm integral equations for high-dimensional problems.

In addition, the value of the regularization parameter
\alpha
can be chosen through common
approaches for selecting the regularization parameter in Tikhonov regularization settings (e.g.
cross–validation
[
102
,
1
]
), while in the case of SMC-EMS the link between the particle system and the functional which is minimized is less clear, and cannot be exploited as straightforwardly.

To summarize, our main
contributions are as follows:

(a)

First, we establish that the functional in (
5
) is continuous and
admits a unique minimizer under mild conditions on
\mu,\mathrm{K}
. In addition, we show that a modification of (
5
) is stable with respect to perturbations of the observation
\mu
and study the limit behaviour of
\pi^{\star}
as
\alpha\to 0
.

(b)

Second, we derive the Wasserstein gradient flow used to minimize a
surrogate of (
5
) and show that the associated MKVSDE
converges towards a unique minimizer using recent results from optimal
transport
[
53
]
.

(c)

Third, we present and study a discrete-time particle approximation of
this MKVSDE. In particular, we establish geometric ergodicity of the particle system under regularity conditions.

(d)

Finally, we illustrate the efficiency of our method to approximately solve
Fredholm integral equations of the first kind on a range of different
numerical examples and show that it achieves superior or comparable performances to those of problem-specific algorithms and SMC-EMS, outperforming the latter for high dimensional problems. In addition, we show on a task from epidemiology that, when the kernel
\mathrm{K}
does not correctly model the problem at hand, the presence of a reference measure
\pi_{0}
allows us to outperform even problem-specific estimators.

2.2
Variants and connections with other methods

2.2.1
Maximum entropy methods

The functional
\mathscr{F}_{\alpha}
can be seen as the Lagrangian
associated with the following primal problem

\operatorname*{arg\,min}\{\textup{KL}\left(\pi|\pi_{0}\right)\,:\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \textup{KL}\left(\mu|\pi\mathrm{K}\right)=0\}.

(13)

The latter problem is a maximum entropy problem in the sense of

[
32
]
(where the entropy functional is replaced by the
Kullback–Leibler divergence). Closely related to (
5
) is
the functional
\tilde{\mathscr{F}}_{\alpha}
given (when it is defined) by

\tilde{\mathscr{F}}_{\alpha}(\pi)=\textup{KL}\left(\mu|\pi\mathrm{K}\right)-\alpha\mathrm{H}(\pi).

(14)

The choice of an entropic penalty does not require specifying a
reference measure
\pi_{0}
and connects (
5
) with the
classical maximum entropy methods
[
58
]
. Indeed,
(
14
) is the Lagrangian associated with the following primal
problem

\operatorname*{arg\,max}\{\mathrm{H}(\pi)\,:\pi\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \textup{KL}\left(\mu|\pi\mathrm{K}\right)=0\},

(15)

where
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the set of probability distributions with
finite entropy. However, the functional
\tilde{\mathscr{F}}_{\alpha}
is not lower
bounded and the corresponding minimization problem is not well-defined and
therefore is not considered here. On the contrary,
\mathscr{F}_{\alpha}
is always
non-negative making the optimization problem well-defined. A number of maximum
entropy approximations of solutions of Fredholm integral equations have been
proposed in the literature, most of which maximize the entropy subject to moment
constraints obtained by integrating
\mu
and
\pi\mathrm{K}
w.r.t. a (possibly
infinite) set of basis functions
[
64
,
56
,
59
,
73
]
.
These approaches work particularly well in the one-dimensional case, as in this case the maximum entropy solution can be written analytically (see, e.g.,
[
56
, Proposition 3.1]
). For higher dimensional problems, the maximum entropy solution is usually approximated by discretizing the support of
\pi
(see, e.g.,
[
78
]
).

2.2.2
Generalized Bayesian inference

As mentioned in the introduction, minimizing
\mathscr{F}
results in the maximum
likelihood estimator (
4
). Given a set of observations

y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}
from
\mu
, the log-likelihood for the infinite-dimensional
parameter
\pi
is
[
24
]

\textstyle{(1/M)\sum_{j=1}^{M}\log\pi[\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y_{j})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\log\pi[\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)]\mathrm{d}\mu^{M}(y),}

(16)

with
\mu^{M}=(1/M)\sum_{j=1}^{M}\updelta_{y_{j}}
.
For any
\alpha>0
, minimizing the functional

\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
in (
11
) is equivalent to minimizing

\displaystyle\textstyle{-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\log(\pi[\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)])\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+\textup{KL}\left(\pi|\pi_{0}\right),}

(17)

which can be interpreted as generalized Bayesian inference
in the sense of adjusting the weight (or learning rate) of the likelihood (
16
) w.r.t. the prior
\pi_{0}
(see, e.g.,
[
12
, Section 3]
and
[
50
]
).
In particular, if we assume that the
target measure
\pi
is a discrete distribution with a fixed and finite number of
components (a straightforward case which is not considered here),
i.e.

\pi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\updelta_{x^{i}}p_{i}
, with
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=1
and

\{x^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, then (
3
) is a finite mixture model
and minimizing (
11
) corresponds to posterior inference for the locations

\{x^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the weights
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with prior
\pi_{0}
and learning rate
\alpha^{-1}
.

Choosing the appropriate
\alpha
then amounts to the calibration problem in generalized Bayesian inference with standard Bayesian inference obtained when
\alpha=1

[
50
,
12
]
. Contrary to the standard setting, in which values of
0<\alpha^{-1}<1
are preferred since the model is believed to be misspecified, we are in the setting in which we want to reduce the influence of the prior
\pi_{0}
which is used as a regularizer in (
11
) and favour values of
\alpha^{-1}>1
.

2.2.3
Tikhonov regularization

We now move onto considering the link between
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
and Tikhonov
regularization. In fact, minimising
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
can be seen as the
probabilistic counterpart to the classical Tikhonov regularization setting

[
49
]
which we recall briefly.

Let
\mathrm{A}\in\ \mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
(note that this discussion
is still valid in more general Hilbert spaces but we restrict ourselves to the
finite-dimensional case for the sake of clarity). Define

g:\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\to\left[0,+\infty\right)
for any

by

g(x)=\|\mathrm{A}x-y\|^{2}
. Denote by
\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}
the Moore-Penrose
inverse of
\mathrm{A}
, see
[
80
]
for a definition.

\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}y\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g
and moreover

\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}y\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}\{x\mapsto\left\|x\right\|\,:x\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\}
,
i.e.

\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}y
is the solution of the
minimization of
g
with least norm. For any
\alpha\geq 0
, the Tikhonov regularization with level
\alpha
of
g
is given by
g_{\alpha}:\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\to\left[0,+\infty\right)
such that
g_{\alpha}(x)=g(x)+\alpha\left\|x\right\|^{2}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. There exists a unique
x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

x_{\alpha}\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{\alpha}
. It is known that

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}x_{\alpha}=\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}y
,
i.e.
the solution of the
Tikhonov regularization converges towards the solution of
g
with minimal norm

[
49
]
.

In
Proposition

4
, we show a similar property in the probabilistic
setting. In this case the norm function is replaced by the Kullback–Leibler
divergence with respect to some reference measure
\pi\mapsto\textup{KL}\left(\pi|\pi_{0}\right)

\mathscr{G}:\ \mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\left(-\infty,\infty\right]
in (

), we
obtain for any
\alpha,\eta\geq 0
a regularized functional
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}(\pi)=\mathscr{G}^{\eta}(\pi)+\alpha\textup{KL}\left(\pi|\pi_{0}\right)

Tikhonov regularization, there exists a unique
minimizer to
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
,

, see
Proposition

2

limiting point of the sequences
(\pi_{\alpha_{n},\eta_{n}}^{\star})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\alpha_{n}=0
and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\eta_{n}=0
, is a minimizer of
\mathscr{G}
with least
Kullback–Leibler divergence with respect to
\pi_{0}
.

To control the behaviour of the family of minima

\{\inf_{\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}(\pi)\,:\alpha,\eta>0\}

when

controls the tail behaviour of

and the tail behaviour of the density

3
McKean-Vlasov Stochastic Differential Equation and minimization of functionals

In most interesting cases the direct minimization of (
12
) is not
tractable. In this work, we propose to follow a Wasserstein gradient flow
approach in order to approximate these minimizers. Our methodology stems from
the connection between minimization of functionals in the space of probability
measures and partial differential equations (PDEs) pointed out in

[
60
,
85
]
. In particular, we draw links
between the minimization of
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
and a SDE whose invariant measure is the minimizer of

\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
. An informal presentation of the bridges between Wasserstein
gradient flows and a certain class of SDEs, namely McKean-Vlasov SDEs (MKVSDEs), is
presented in
Section

3.1
. We investigate the long-time behaviour of
these MKVSDEs in the context of the minimization of
\mathscr{G}^{\eta}_{\alpha}
in

Section

3.2
. Finally, we show that there exists a system of
interacting SDEs which approximates the obtained MKVSDE in
Section

3.3
.

3.1
Minimization and gradient flows

In this section, we set
\alpha,\eta>0
and informally derive the
continuous-time dynamics we study in the rest of this paper by drawing a link
with gradient flows; formal results are given in
Section

B.1
. We assume that all the probability measures we consider admit
densities w.r.t the Lebesgue measure and do not distinguish between the
distribution and its density. Let
(\pi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a family of
probability measures satisfying (in a weak sense)

\partial_{t}\pi_{t}=-\mathrm{div}(\pi_{t}\mathbf{v}_{t}),

(21)

where
\mathbf{v}_{t}
belongs to the Wasserstein subdifferential of a functional

\mathscr{F}:\ \mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\left[0,+\infty\right]
, see
[
2
, Definition
10.1.1]
for a definition.
Then
(\pi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is called a Wasserstein gradient flow associated
with
\mathscr{F}

[
2
, Definition 11.1.1]
and it has
been shown in numerous settings that such gradient flows converge towards the
minimizers of
\mathscr{F}
when they exist

[
2
,
91
]
. In

Proposition

19
in
Section

B.1
we show that

\displaystyle\textstyle{x\mapsto-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}(\eta+\pi[\mathrm{k}(x,y)])^{-1}\nabla_{1}\mathrm{k}(x,y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+\alpha\{\nabla U(x)+\nabla\log\pi(x)\},}

(22)

belongs to the subdifferential of
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
at
\pi
under mild
conditions and assuming that
\pi_{0}(x)\propto\exp[-U(x)]
. We also derive the corresponding gradient flow equation

\textstyle{\partial_{t}\pi_{t}=-\mathrm{div}\left(\pi_{t}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}(\pi_{t}[\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)]+\eta)^{-1}\nabla_{1}\mathrm{k}(x,y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-\alpha\nabla U(x)\right]\right)+\alpha\Delta\pi_{t}.}

(23)

For strongly geodesically convex (i.e. convex along geodesics) functionals the
gradient flow (
21
) converges geometrically towards the unique
minimizer. However, in our setting
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
is not geodesically
convex but only convex. Using recent results from
[
53
]
we establish the convergence of the Wasserstein gradient flow in

Section

3.2
but without quantitative convergence rates.

We use the gradient flow equation (
23
) to draw a link between the
minimization of
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
and MKVSDEs
[
72
]
, a class of SDEs in which the drift
and diffusion coefficients depend not only on the current position of the
solution but also on its distribution. Since (
23
) is a
Fokker-Plank equation, we informally derive the corresponding SDE

\textstyle{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\star}=\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\tilde{b}_{\eta}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\star},\bm{\lambda}_{t}^{\star},y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-\alpha\nabla U(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\star})\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\alpha}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}\qquad\mathbf{X}_{0}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},}

where

is a

\bm{\lambda}_{t}^{\star}
is
the distribution of

and for any

and

\tilde{b}_{\eta}(x,\nu,y)=\nabla_{1}\mathrm{k}(x,y)/(\nu[\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)]+\eta).

(25)

In particular, note that
\tilde{b}_{\eta}
is always defined since
\eta>0
.
In the following section, we establish various theoretical properties of (
24

unique and converges towards the minimizer of
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}

3.3
Particle system

The MKVSDE (
24
) has several shortcomings from a
methodological point of view. First, since the drift of the SDE depends not only
on the current position of the solution but also on its distribution, it is not
possible to sample from (
24
) even in simplistic
settings. Second, as is the case for classical SDEs, it is usually difficult to sample
from continuous-time processes. We circumvent the first issue by introducing a
particle system
[
72
,
14

(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{1:N})_{t\geq 0}=\{(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{k,N})_{t\geq 0}\}_{k=1}^{N}
for any

which satisfies a classical SDE. In the limit

we have that
(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{1,N})_{t\geq 0}
approximates

(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\star})_{t\geq 0}
. In
Section

4
we will investigate
a time discretization scheme for this particle system SDE in order to obtain a
particle system Markov chain which can be implemented.

We introduce the particle system
(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{1:N})_{t\geq 0}
which satisfies the
following SDE: for any

,
\mathbf{X}_{0}^{k,N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\textstyle{\mathbf{X}_{t}^{k,N}=\mathbf{X}_{0}^{k,N}+\int_{0}^{t}b(\mathbf{X}_{s}^{k,N},\bm{\lambda}_{s}^{N}(\mathbf{X}_{0}^{1:N}))\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{t}^{k},}

(26)

where for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\nu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\textstyle{b(x,\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\tilde{b}_{\eta}(x,\nu,y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-\alpha\nabla U(x),}

(27)

\{(\mathbf{B}_{t}^{k})_{t\geq 0}\,:k\in\mathbb{N}\}

Brownian motions and
\bm{\lambda}_{t}^{N}(\mathbf{X}_{0}^{1:N})

associated with
\{(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{k,N})_{t\geq 0}\}_{k=1}^{N}
with starting point

\mathbf{X}_{0}^{1:N}

i.e.
we have for any

\textstyle{\bm{\lambda}^{N}_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{0}^{1:N})=(1/N)\sum_{k=1}^{N}\updelta_{(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{k,N})}.}

(28)

that there exists a unique strong solution to (

Proposition 7

4.3
Implementation guidelines

Obtaining the estimator (
38
) of the minimizer of

\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
requires specification of a number of parameters: the
reference measure
\pi_{0}
and the regularization parameter
\alpha
control the
properties of the measure
\pi
we want to reconstruct (e.g. its smoothness),
while the parameter
\eta
, the number of particles
N,M
and the time
discretization step
\gamma
control accuracy and stability of the numerical
implementation.

Choice of
\pi_{0}

There are of course, many possible choices for the
reference measure
\pi_{0}
to which we want the regularized solution to be close
depending on the constraints we want to impose on the reconstructed solution.
The choice of an improper reference measure
\pi_{0}\propto C
with
C>0
results
in a McKean-Vlasov SDE in which there is no dependence on
\pi_{0}

\textstyle{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\star}=\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\tilde{b}_{\eta}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\star},\bm{\lambda}_{t}^{\star},y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\alpha}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t},\qquad\mathbf{X}_{0}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},}

(39)

where
(\mathbf{B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and

\bm{\lambda}_{t}^{\star}
is the distribution of
(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\star})_{t\geq 0}
. This
scheme corresponds to what we would obtain by applying the gradient flow
procedure to the functional (
14
).
Under additional assumptions on the tail behaviour of the kernel
\mathrm{k}
, it is possible to show that this functional is proper, lower semi-continuous and coercive in an appropriate sense;
[
30
, Chapter 7]
shows empirically that the corresponding particle system is stable.
Nevertheless, in many scenarios some knowledge of the target will be embedded in the problem at hand. In deconvolution problems,
\mu
is the distribution of noisy samples from
the target distribution
\pi
; in this case it is sensible to expect that
\pi

itself will be close to
\mu
and therefore choose
\pi_{0}
to be reasonably
close to
\mu
. More generally, for problems in which

\mathrm{k}(x,y)=\mathrm{k}(y-x)
is a deconvolution kernel, the distribution
\mu

will be the convolution of
\pi
and
\mathrm{k}
and therefore encapsulates
information on
\pi
. In these problems we choose
\pi_{0}
to be a Gaussian
distribution with mean and variance given by the empirical mean and variance of
the sample from
\mu
. For the epidemiology examples in
Section

5.2
, we set
\pi_{0}
to be
\mu
shifted
back by a number of days equal to the mean
[
45
]
or
the mode
[
103
]
of the infection-to-confirmed delay
distribution
\mathrm{k}
. For image reconstruction,
\pi_{0}
could be selected to
enforce particular characteristics in the reconstructed image, e.g. smoothness

[
79
]
or sparsity
[
108
,
67
]
.
Alternatively, if a large data set of previously reconstructed images is
available one could use score matching priors
[
62
]
to
obtain a reference measure
\pi_{0}
encapsulating all the relevant features of
the image to reconstruct. We study the influence of the reference measure

\pi_{0}
on a toy example in
Section

D.1
and find that
\pi_{0}

influences the shape of the minimizer of
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
and its minimum
but not the speed at which convergence to the minimum occurs.

Choice of
\alpha

As the parameter
\alpha>0
controls the amount
of regularization introduced by the cross-entropy penalty, its value should be
chosen to give a good trade-off between the distance from the data distribution

\mu
,
\textup{KL}\left(\mu|\pi\mathrm{K}\right)
, and that from
\pi_{0}
,
\textup{KL}\left(\pi|\pi_{0}\right)
. A common
approach for selecting the regularization parameter in Tikhonov regularization
is cross-validation
[
102
,
1
]
. Since the case
in which
\mu
is not known but a sample drawn from it is available is the most
likely in applications, we propose the following approach to cross-validation:
to estimate the value of
\alpha
we divide the sample from
\mu
into
L

subsets and find the value of
\alpha
which minimizes

\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathrm{CV}(\alpha)=(1/L)\sum_{j=1}^{L}\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\alpha}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{j}),}

(40)

where
\hat{\pi}^{j}
is obtained using
Algorithm

1
with the samples
from
\mu
which are not in group
j\in\{1,\dots,L\}
and
\widehat{\mathscr{G}}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
is an approximation of
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\alpha}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{j}):=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\log\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathrm{k}(X_{n}^{k,N,M},y_{j})\right)+\frac{\alpha}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\log\left(\frac{\hat{\pi}^{j}(X_{n}^{k,N,M})}{\pi_{0}(X_{n}^{k,N,M})}\right).

(41)

If some prior information on the smoothness of the solution
\pi
is known (e.g. its variance), one could chose
\alpha
so that the smoothness of (
38
), matches the expected smoothness of
\pi
.
We show how the value of
\alpha
influences the reconstructions of
\pi

on simple problems in
Section

D.2
.

Choice of
\eta

The parameter
\eta
has been introduced
in (
12
) to deal with the possible instability of the functional

\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
; we did not find performances to be significantly
influenced by this parameter as long as its value is sufficiently small.
In practice, in the
experiments in Section
5
we set
\eta\equiv 0
.
This choice might seem counter-intuitive, since most of our theoretical results are obtained with
\eta>0
,
On one hand, this suggests that for regular enough
\mathrm{k}
, the functional
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
is well-behaved; on the other hand, we observe that the introduction of the tamed Euler–Maruyama scheme (
32
) prevents most of the numerical instability issues arising when discretizing (
26
).

Choice of
N,\gamma
and

The values of the number of particles

N
, the time discretization step
\gamma
and the number of samples
m
from

\mu
to use at each iteration control the quality of the numerical approximation of (
24
),
their choice is therefore largely application dependent.
However, the
results in
Proposition

7
,
10
and
12
give the following global error estimate: for any
T\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathbb{E}[\sup_{n\in\{0,\dots,n_{T}\}}\|\mathbf{X}_{n\gamma}^{\star}-X_{n}^{\ell,N,m}\|]\leq C_{T}(N^{-1/2}+\gamma+\gamma m^{-1/2}).}

(42)

Choosing
N
amounts to the classical task of selecting an appropriate sample size for Monte Carlo approximations, while the choice of
\gamma
corresponds to the specification of a timescale on which to discretize a continuous time (stochastic) process; hence, one can exploit the vast literature on Monte Carlo methods and discrete time approximations of SDEs to select these values
[
63
]
.
In practice, we found that the choice of
N
largely depends on the dimensionality of the problem; for
one-dimensional examples values between
N=200
and
N=1000

achieve high enough accuracy to compete with specialized algorithms (see
Sections
5.1
,
5.2
); as the dimension

increases larger

should be considered (see Section
5.3
).
Similar
considerations apply to the choice of the time discretization step
\gamma
. In
particular, this parameter should be chosen taking into account the order of
magnitude of the gradient, to give a good trade-off between the Monte Carlo
error and the time discretization error. In practice, we observed good results
with
\gamma
between
10^{-1}
and
10^{-3}
.

The number of samples
m
from
\mu
to employ at each iteration is again problem dependent. In many cases, we are given a number
M
of observations from
\mu
, if
M
is very large, the resulting algorithm might become computationally too expensive.
In this setting, we propose the following batch strategy to approximate
\mu
: fix
N
and at each time step approximate the integral with respect to
\mu

using
m
samples where
m
is the smallest between the number of particles
N

chosen for the particle system (
26
) and the total number of
samples available from
\mu
; if
N
is larger than the total number of observed samples from
\mu
then the whole
sample is used at each iteration, if
N
is smaller we resample without replacement
m
times from the empirical measure of the sample.
This amounts to a maximum cost of
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
per time step.

In cases in which we have access to a sampling mechanism from
\mu
, and we can therefore draw
m
samples at each iteration, we simply set
m=N
.

Choice of

It is straightforward to choose the number of time steps

, adaptively. The number of steps necessary to give
convergence of (
26
) to its stationary distribution is estimated
by approximating the value of
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
through numerical

We test FE-WGF on a number of examples of Fredholm integral

5.2
Epidemiology

In epidemiology, Fredholm integral equations link the unknown infection

\pi
to the number of reported cases, deaths or
hospitalizations. This is a particular instance of the deconvolution problem, in

techniques have been used to infer

in the case of HIV

9
]

[
45

and

[

26

As an example, we take the spread of the pandemic influenza in the city of Philadelphia between the end of September and the beginning of October 1918
[
45

package

[
77

To obtain a parametric form for

[

\displaystyle\mathrm{k}(x,y)=0.595\mathcal{N}(y-x;8.63,2.56^{2})+0.405\mathcal{N}(y-x;15.24,5.39^{2}).

(48)

Although this choice assigns
\approx 10^{-3}
mass to the negative reals, we
found that a mixture of Gaussians fits the observed delay distribution better
than other commonly used distributions (e.g. Gamma, log-normal, see

[
83
]

1

We compare the reconstructions obtained using FE-WGF with the

[

[

[

6
Discussion

We consider a probabilistic approach to the solution of Fredholm integral equations of the first kind (
3
) by introducing the minimization problem (
5
) and its surrogate (
12
).
Under mild smoothness assumptions on the kernel
\mathrm{K}
and the reference measure
\pi_{0}
, we show that the surrogate functional admits a unique minimizer which is stable w.r.t.
\mu
.
Leveraging a Wasserstein gradient flow construction, we introduce an interacting particle system (
26
) which approximates the minimizer of the surrogate functional and is ergodic for any finite
N
.
Combining standard propagation of chaos results and strong convergence of tamed Euler–Maruyama schemes we obtain the bound (
42
) which provides a practical guideline for the selection of the time discretization step
\gamma
, the number of particles
N
and the number of samples
m
from
\mu
to use at each iteration.

In Section
5
we compare FE-WGF with both problem specific and state-of-the-art methods to solve (
3
); specifically, we compare our algorithm with SMC-EMS, a particle method to solve (
3
) which also aims at minimizing a regularized Kullback–Leibler divergence. This particle method has been shown to achieve state-of-the-art performances in a number of settings and to scale with the dimension of the state space
d
better than standard discretization based algorithms
[
31
]
.
Our results show that FE-WGF has always comparable or superior performances to problem specific algorithms and outperforms SMC-EMS in high dimensional setting.
Moreover, FE-WGF allows the introduction of a reference measure
\pi_{0}
, which can be particularly beneficial when some features of the measure
\pi
to be reconstructed are known (as, for instance, in epidemiology applications as that in Section
5.2
).

From a theoretical perspective,
Proposition

2
and the convergence properties in
Section

3
guarantee that the particle system (
26
) approximates the unique minimizer of (
12
); an equivalent uniqueness property has not yet been established for SMC-EMS.
However, the results presented in Section
5
also provide some additional
confidence in the use of SMC-EMS, demonstrating empirically that it recovers
comparable solutions to the provably convergent method developed in this paper
in a range of settings.

Moreover, while both algorithms minimize a regularized Kullback–Leibler divergence, and for small values of the regularization parameters the reconstruction they provide are similar, the methods through which they approximate the minimizer present some notable differences. FE-WGF is based on a numerical approximation of the MKVSDE (
24
) and therefore the associated particle system has a diffusive behaviour, while SMC-EMS uses a resampling mechanism to eliminate the particles which are not in the support of
\pi
and replicate those close to high-probability regions. The effect of this different algorithmic approach can be observed by considering different initial distributions for
\{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{k,N}\}_{k=1}^{N}
: SMC-EMS benefits from overly diffused initial distributions, since the resampling mechanism allows to quickly move the particles towards high probability regions, for concentrated distributions (e.g. Dirac delta) convergence still occurs but in a larger number of iterations
[
31
, Appendix E.1]
. The convergence speed of FE-WGF is less influenced by the initial state
\{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{k,N}\}_{k=1}^{N}
as shown in
Appendix

D
.
In contrast with SMC-EMS, FE-WGF requires specification of a reference measure
\pi_{0}
. While this might seem a limitation of our method, we found that in practice we often have sufficient knowledge about the problem at hand to build
\pi_{0}
. For instance, in image processing applications usually a large number of images needs to be processed and it is possible to build reference measures using previously processed images (e.g.
[
62
]
), while in epidemiology, one can exploit previous studies on similar diseases, since they often present incidence curves with the same characteristics (e.g.
[
45
]
).
In fact, it is in this latter setting that we find that FE-WGF outperforms SMC-EMS in terms of accuracy (Section
5.2
).

FE-WGF describes one possible numerical approximation of the particle system (
26
), however, we anticipate that more sophisticated time discretization schemes will prove beneficial in some settings. For instance, in our experiments we observed that in the first few iterations the drift component in (
26
) has larger values, and pushes the particles towards high
\pi
-probability regions; after the first iterations, the magnitude of the drift component decreases and the shape of the approximated solution is refined. In terms of the functional
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
, the first few iterations correspond to a steepest decrease in
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
, which then stabilizes around the minimum (see e.g.,
Appendix

D
). To speed up convergence to the minimizer, one could consider having a larger time step
\gamma
for earlier iterations, and then gradually reduce
\gamma
to refine the approximation. More sophisticated adaptive strategies for MKVSDE have been recently studied in
[
88
]
.

In conclusion, FE-WGF offers a general recipe to obtain regularized solutions to Fredholm integral equations of the first kind, which can be refined exploiting specific knowledge of the problem at hand; for example, choosing informative reference measures
\pi_{0}
or adapting the time discretization step
\Delta t
to the magnitude of the drift (
27
).

B.1

In this section we recall basic results on gradient flows in Wasserstein spaces
and provide a slight extension of the classical variational formula. We refer to
[
2
]
for further details. We denote by
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\nu)

the set of square integrable functions w.r.t. a measure
\nu

many times differentiable functions with compact support and by
\mathrm{W}^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of functions which are in
\mathrm{W}^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathsf{X})
for all compact sets
\mathsf{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathrm{W}^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathsf{X})
denotes the Sobolev space of functions whose zero-th and first derivative are bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{1}
. For any

\nu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
t:\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
measurable, we denote by

t_{\#}\nu
the push-forward measure,
t_{\#}\nu(A)=\nu(t^{-1}(A))
for all

A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We start by recalling the definition of the
subdifferentiability in Wasserstein spaces, see
[
2
, Definition
10.1.1]
. We denote
\mathscr{P}_{2}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of
probability distributions in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which are absolutely continuous
w.r.t the Lebesgue measure.

Definition 15

(Fréchet subdifferential)
.

Let
\Phi:\ \mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\left(-\infty,+\infty\right)
. Let

\pi\in\mathscr{P}_{2}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

\xi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
belongs to the strong Fréchet
subdifferential
\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\Phi(\pi)
of
\Phi
at
\pi
if for any
t\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)

\textstyle{\liminf_{t\to\operatorname{Id}}\left.\left\{\Phi(t_{\#}\pi)-\Phi(\pi)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi(x),t(x)-x\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\right\}\middle/\left\|t-\operatorname{Id}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\right.=0.}

(99)

The following lemma is a specific case of the chain-rule formula.

Lemma 16
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
be Lipschitz, convex and lower-bounded. For any

y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, let
\mathscr{L}:\ \mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\pi\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and

\xi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p},\pi\otimes\mu)
be such that for any

y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\pi\in\mathscr{P}_{2}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\xi(\pi,y)\in\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\mathscr{L}(\pi,y)
and

-\xi(\pi,y)\in\partial_{\mathrm{s}}(-\mathscr{L})(\pi,y)
.
In addition, assume
that there exists
h\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p},\mu)
such that for any

\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

we have

\left|\mathscr{L}(\pi_{1},y)-\mathscr{L}(\pi_{2},y)\right|\leq h(y)\mathbf{W}_{2}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}).

(100)

Let
\mathscr{M}
be given for any
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\textstyle{\mathscr{M}(\pi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}g(\mathscr{L}(\pi,y))\mathrm{d}\mu(y).}

(101)

Then, we have that
\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\mathscr{M}(\pi)\neq\emptyset
and

\{x\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}g^{\prime}(\mathscr{L}(\pi,y))\xi(\cdot,y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\}\in\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\mathscr{M}(\pi)
.

Proof.

Let
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
t\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
g
is convex we have

g(\mathscr{L}(t_{\#}\pi,y))-g(\mathscr{L}(\pi,y))\geq g^{\prime}(\mathscr{L}(\pi,y))(\mathscr{L}(t_{\#}\pi,y)-\mathscr{L}(\pi,y)).

(102)

We have

\displaystyle\textstyle{g(\mathscr{L}(t_{\#}\pi,y))-g(\mathscr{L}(\pi,y))-g^{\prime}(\mathscr{L}(\pi,y))\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi(x,y),t(x)-x\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(x)}

(103)

\displaystyle\qquad\textstyle{\geq g^{\prime}(\mathscr{L}(\pi,y))\left(\mathscr{L}(t_{\#}\pi,y)-\mathscr{L}(\pi,y)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi(x,y),t(x)-x\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\right).}

(104)

In addition, using that
\xi(\pi,y)\in\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\mathscr{L}(\pi,y)
and

-\xi(\pi,y)\in\partial_{\mathrm{s}}(-\mathscr{L})(\pi,y)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\liminf_{t\to\operatorname{Id}}\left(\mathscr{L}(t_{\#}\pi,y)-\mathscr{L}(\pi,y)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi(x,y),t(x)-x\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\right)/\|t-\operatorname{Id}\|_{2}\geq 0,}

(105)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\limsup_{t\to\operatorname{Id}}\left(\mathscr{L}(t_{\#}\pi,y)-\mathscr{L}(\pi,y)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi(x,y),t(x)-x\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\right)/\|t-\operatorname{Id}\|_{2}\leq 0.}

(106)

Hence, we have

\textstyle{\lim_{t\to\operatorname{Id}}\left(\mathscr{L}(t_{\#}\pi,y)-\mathscr{L}(\pi,y)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi(x,y),t(x)-x\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\right)/\|t-\operatorname{Id}\|_{2}=0.}

(107)

We also have that

\displaystyle\textstyle{\left|\mathscr{L}(t_{\#}\pi,y)-\mathscr{L}(\pi,y)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi(x,y),t(x)-x\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\right|/\|t-\operatorname{Id}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\textstyle{\leq h(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\xi(x,y)\right\|\mathrm{d}\pi(x).}

(109)

We conclude upon using this result, the fact that

∎
