Abstract
. In this paper we present a complete proof of a conjecture due to V. V. Prelov in 2010 about an information inequality for the binary entropy function.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 26D15, 94A15.

1.
Introduction

Consider the binary entropy function

h(x)=-x\log x-(1-x)\log(1-x),\quad x\in[0,1].

(1.1)

In computing the maximum of mutual information of several random variables via the variational distance
(see Pinsker
[
Pin05
]
and Prelov
[
Pre09
]
),
Prelov established in
[
Pre10
]
some new inequalities for the binary entropy function.

Theorem 1.1

(Prelov, 2010)
.

For
p>q
, we have

p^{n}(1+q^{n}-p^{n})h(q^{n})>q^{n}(1+p^{n}-q^{n})h(p^{n}),

(1.2)

if

0<q<q_{0},\quad\text{or}\,\,\,\,q_{1}<q<1/2,\quad\text{or}\,\,\,\,n>n_{0},

where
q_{0}
,
q_{1}
and
n_{0}
are some positive numbers.

For
n\in\{1,\infty\}
, or for
(p,q)\in\{(1,0),(1/2,1/2)\}
, (
1.2
) is an equality.
Prelov conjectures that (
1.2
) is valid for all
q\in(0,1/2)
,
p=1-q
and all integers
n\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The above conjecture of Prelov is true.

For the impact of this result to information theory, see
[
Pre10
, Proposition 1]
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
in quadratic case

Using
p=1-q
we see that (
1.2
) for
n=2
is equivalent to

ph(q^{2})>qh(p^{2}).

(2.1)

Using the definition (
1.1
) and
p+q=1
, we have further equivalences

\displaystyle\eqref{eqn:Prelov'}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow-2pq^{2}\log q-p^{2}(1+q)\log(1+q)-p^{2}(1+q)\log p

(2.2)

\displaystyle>-2qp^{2}\log p-q^{2}(1+p)\log(1+p)-q^{2}(1+p)\log q

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow q^{2}\left[(1-p)\log(1-p)+(1+p)\log(1+p)\right]

\displaystyle>p^{2}\left[(1-q)\log(1-q)+(1+q)\log(1+q)\right].

To this end, we introduce

\lambda(x)=\frac{\left[(1-x)\log(1-x)+(1+x)\log(1+x)\right]}{x^{2}}

and

\lambda^{\prime}(x)=\frac{(x-2)\log(1-x)-(x+2)\log(1+x)}{x^{3}}=:\frac{\beta(x)}{x^{3}}.

Note that

\gamma(x):=\beta^{\prime}(x)=\log(1-x)+\frac{x-2}{x-1}-\log(1+x)-\frac{x+2}{x+1}

and

\gamma^{\prime}(x)=\frac{x}{(x-1)^{2}}-\frac{x}{(x+1)^{2}}=\frac{4x^{2}}{(1-x^{2})^{2}}>0,\quad x\in(0,1).

Now, since
\gamma(0)=0
, so
\gamma>0
on
(0,1)
and
\beta
is strictly increasing.
Similarly, since
\beta(0)=0
, so
\beta>0
on
(0,1)
and
\lambda
is strictly increasing.
In particular,

p>\frac{1}{2}>q\Longrightarrow\lambda(p)>\lambda(q).

This together with the equivalence (
2.2
) proves the theorem.

Remark 2.1
.

Observe that we have the simple inequality

h(p^{2})<2ph(p),\quad 0<p<1.

(2.3)

Indeed, by the definition of
h
, (
2.3
) is equivalent to

g(p)=(1+p)\log(1+p)+(1-p)\log(1-p)>0,\quad 0<p<1,

which is obvious by
g(0)=0
and
g^{\prime}(p)>0
for
0<p<1
.
Now, using (
2.1
) and (
2.3
),

h(q^{2})+h(p^{2})<\frac{h(q^{2})}{q}<2h(q)=2h(p),\quad 0<p=1-q<1.

This gives an alternative (non-information-theoretic) proof of
[
Pre10
, (10)]
for
n=2
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
in general case

Given Theorem
1.2
in quadratic case (already verified in previous section),
the idea is then to tailor for (
1.2
) the following symmetric function

F(x,y)=\frac{1}{x-y}\left(\frac{1+y-x}{y}h(y)-\frac{1+x-y}{x}h(x)\right)

defined on the off-diagonal square
S^{\prime}:=\{x\neq y:0<x,y<1\}
.
So we have verified

F(x,y)>0\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{2}:=\left\{(x,y)\in S^{\prime}:x^{\frac{1}{2}}+y^{\frac{1}{2}}=1\right\},

and we want to show, for any
3\leq n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

F(x,y)>0\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{n}:=\left\{(x,y)\in S^{\prime}:x^{\frac{1}{n}}+y^{\frac{1}{n}}=1\right\}.

Note that
\Gamma_{n}
lies below
\Gamma_{2}
.
Now, the trick is to show
F(x,y)
is decreasing along the diagonal direction
(1,1)
.
By direct computations this directional derivative is

\displaystyle G(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{d}{dt}F(x+t,y+t)\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x-y}\left(\frac{1+y-x}{y}h^{\prime}(y)-\frac{1+y-x}{y^{2}}h(y)-\frac{1+x-y}{x}h^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1+x-y}{x^{2}}h(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{g(x)-g(y)}{x-y}+g(x)+g(y),

where

g(x)=\frac{h(x)-xh^{\prime}(x)}{x^{2}}=\frac{2x-1}{x^{2}}\log(1-x).

Note that
g(1/2)=0
, and by routine computations, we have

g^{\prime}<0\quad\text{on}\quad(0,1);\quad g^{\prime\prime}>0\quad\text{on}\quad(0,1/2).

(3.1)

By symmetry we can consider
G
only for
x<y
. We then argue in two cases.

Case I.
If
x<y
and
y>1/2
, by monotonicity and
1+\frac{1}{x-y}<0
,

\displaystyle G(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{x-y}\right)g(x)+\left(1-\frac{1}{x-y}\right)g(y)

\displaystyle<\left(1+\frac{1}{x-y}\right)g(y)+\left(1-\frac{1}{x-y}\right)g(y)=2g(y)<0.

Case II.
If
x<y
and
y\leq 1/2
, by convexity and monotonicity,

G(x,y)<\frac{g(x)-g(1/2)}{x-1/2}+g(x)+g(x)=\frac{4}{x}\ln(1-x)<0.

Theorem
1.2
in general case is proved.

Remark 3.1
.

For completeness, we also justify (
3.1
) with details. Indeed,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left(1-x\right)^{2}\ln\left(1-x\right)-2x^{2}+x}{x^{3}\left(1-x\right)}

\displaystyle<\frac{-2(1-x)^{2}x-2x^{2}+x}{x^{3}(1-x)}=-\frac{1-2x+2x^{2}}{x^{2}(1-x)}<0.

g^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{x(6x^{2}-9x+4)+(6-4x)(1-x)^{2}\ln(1-x)}{x^{4}(1-x)^{2}}=:-\frac{a(x)}{x^{4}(1-x)^{2}}.

To see
a(x)<0
when
x\in(0,1/2)
, note that
a(0)=0
,
a(1/2)<0
, and

a^{\prime\prime}(x)=4(2+4x+(7-6x)\ln(1-x))>4(2+4x-(7-6x)\times 1.4x)>0.
