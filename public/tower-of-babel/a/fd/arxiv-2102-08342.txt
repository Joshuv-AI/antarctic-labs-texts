On the sampling Lovász Local Lemma for atomic constraint satisfaction problems
Vishesh Jain, Huy Tuan Pham, Thuy Duong Vuong

Abstract
. We study the problem of sampling an approximately uniformly random satisfying assignment for atomic constraint satisfaction problems i.e. where each constraint is violated by only one assignment to its variables. Let p p denote the maximum probability of violation of any constraint and let Δ \Delta denote the maximum degree of the line graph of the constraints. Our main result is a nearly-linear (in the number of variables) time algorithm for this problem, which is valid in a Lovász local lemma type regime that is considerably less restrictive compared to previous works. In particular, we provide sampling algorithms for the uniform distribution on: • q q -colorings of k k -uniform hypergraphs with Δ ≲ q ( k − 4 ) / 3 + o q ​ ( 1 ) . \Delta\lesssim q^{(k-4)/3+o_{q}(1)}. The exponent 1 / 3 1/3 improves the previously best-known 1 / 7 1/7 in the case q , Δ = O ⁡ ( 1 ) q,\Delta=O(1) [Jain, Pham, Vuong; arXiv, 2020] and 1 / 9 1/9 in the general case [Feng, He, Yin; STOC 2021]. • Satisfying assignments of Boolean k k -CNF formulas with Δ ≲ 2 k / 5.741 . \Delta\lesssim 2^{k/5.741}. The constant 5.741 5.741 in the exponent improves the previously best-known 7 7 in the case k = O ⁡ ( 1 ) k=O(1) [Jain, Pham, Vuong; arXiv, 2020] and 13 13 in the general case [Feng, He, Yin; STOC 2021]. • Satisfying assignments of general atomic constraint satisfaction problems with p ⋅ Δ 7.043 ≲ 1 . p\cdot\Delta^{7.043}\lesssim 1. The constant 7.043 7.043 improves upon the previously best-known constant of 350 350 [Feng, He, Yin; STOC 2021]. At the heart of our analysis is a novel information-percolation type argument for showing the rapid mixing of the Glauber dynamics for a carefully constructed projection of the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments. Notably, there is no natural partial order on the space, and we believe that the techniques developed for the analysis may be of independent interest.



1.
Introduction

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
denote a collection of independent random variables and let
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\}
denote a collection of events depending on
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
(here, the letter
C
is chosen to represent a “constraint”). For
C\in\mathcal{C}
, let
\operatorname{vbl}(C)\subseteq\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
be such that
C
depends only on
X_{i}\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)
. The celebrated Lovász Local Lemma (LLL)
[
EL73
]
(stated here in its variable version, symmetric form) asserts that

(1.1)

e\cdot p\cdot\Delta\leq 1\implies\mathbb{P}[\wedge_{i\in[m]}\overline{C_{i}}]\geq(1-e\cdot p)^{|\cal{C}|}>0,

where
\overline{C}
denotes the complement of the event
C
,
e
is the base of the natural logarithm,

(1.2)

p=\max_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{P}[C_{i}],

and
\Delta\geq 1
satisfies

(1.3)

\#\{j\in[m]:\operatorname{vbl}(C_{j})\cap\operatorname{vbl}(C_{i})\neq\emptyset\}\leq\Delta\quad\text{for all }i\in[m].

The original proof of
Equation

1.1
is non-constructive and does not provide an efficient algorithm to find a point in
\wedge_{i\in[m]}\overline{C_{i}}
(such a point is called a satisfying assignment). After much work over a period of two decades (cf.
[
Bec91
,
Alo91
,
MR98
,
CS00
,
Sri08
,
Mos08
,
Mos09
]
), the landmark work of Moser and Tardos
[
MT10
]
provided an efficient (randomized) algorithm to find a satisfying assignment whenever the LLL condition (i.e. the condition on the left hand side of
Equation

1.1
) is satisfied, provided that one is able to efficiently sample from the distribution of
X_{i}
, and efficiently able to determine the set of constraints that are violated by a given realization of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.

In recent years, much attention (cf.
[
HSZ19
,
Moi19
,
GLLZ19
,
GJL19
,
FGYZ20
,
FHY21
,
JPV20
]
) has been devoted to approximate counting and sampling variants of the algorithmic LLL: under conditions similar to the LLL condition, can we efficiently approximately count the total number of satisfying assignments? Can we efficiently sample from approximately the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments?

This problem turns out to be computationally harder than the problem of efficiently finding one satisfying assignment. Indeed, consider the Boolean
k
-
\operatorname{CNF-SAT}
problem, in which we are given
n
Boolean variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
m
constraints
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
such that each constraint depends on exactly
k
variables, and each constraint is violated by exactly one assignment (out of the
2^{k}
possible assignments) to its variables. A direct application of the LLL shows that if each constraint shares variables with at most (approximately)
2^{k}/e
other constraints, then the formula has a satisfying assignment, in which case, the algorithm of
[
MT10
]
efficiently finds such a satisfying assignment. However, it was shown by Bezáková et al.
[
BGG+19
]
that it is
\boldsymbol{\operatorname{NP}}
-hard to approximately count the number of satisfying assignments for a Boolean
k
-CNF formula in which every variable is allowed to be present in
\geq 5\cdot 2^{k/2}
constraints, even when the formula is monotone.

On the algorithmic side, both deterministic and randomized algorithms have been devised for approximate counting under LLL-like conditions. On the deterministic side, Moitra
[
Moi19
]
provided a deterministic algorithm to approximately count the number of satisfying assignments of a Boolean
k
-CNF formula in which each constraint shares variables with at most
\Delta\lesssim 2^{k/60}
other constraints (the
\lesssim
hides polynomial factors in
k
), provided that
k=O(1)
. Moitra’s method was extended by Guo, Liao, Lu, and Zhang
[
GLLZ19
]
to provide an efficient deterministic algorithm for approximately counting proper
q
-colorings of
k
-uniform hypergraphs with maximum degree
d
, provided that
q\gtrsim d^{14/(k-14)}
and
q,k=O(1)
. Recently
[
JPV20
]
, the authors of this paper showed that for
any
instance of the variable version, symmetric form of the LLL, if each constraint depends on at most
k
variables and if each variable takes on at most
q
values, then there is an efficient deterministic algorithm to approximately count the number of satisfying assignments provided that
q,k=O(1)
and
p\Delta^{7}\lesssim 1
, where
\lesssim
hides polynomial factors in
q,k
. Here,
p
and
\Delta
are as in
Equations

1.2
and
1.3
. In particular, this subsumes and improves upon
[
Moi19
,
GLLZ19
]
. We note that these approximate counting algorithms also lead to efficient algorithms for sampling from approximately the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments.

On the randomized side, algorithms have been devised for instances of the variable version, symmetric LLL with
atomic
constraints. Here, an atomic constraint refers to a constraint which is violated by exactly one assignment to its variables. For the special case of
monotone
Boolean
k
-CNF formulas, Hermon, Sly, and Zhang
[
HSZ19
]
showed that the Glauber dynamics mix rapidly provided that each variable is present in at most
c2^{k/2}
constraints, for some absolute constant
c
; note that this matches the hardness regime from
[
BGG+19
]
up to a constant factor. For
extremal
Boolean
k
-CNF formulas (see
[
GJL19
]
for the definition) and for
d
in the entire LLL regime, the method of partial rejection sampling due to Guo, Jerrum, and Liu
[
GJL19
]
allows efficient
perfect
sampling from the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments. For general Boolean
k
-CNF formulas, Feng, Guo, Yin, and Zhang
[
FGYZ20
]
analyzed the Glauber dynamics on a certain “projected space” inspired by Moitra’s method, and obtained a near-linear time algorithm for sampling from approximately the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments provided that
\Delta\lesssim 2^{k/20}
(
\lesssim
hides polynomial factors in
k
) – the motivation for constructing the projected space is that, while the original space of satisfying assignments might not even be connected, by passing to an appropriate projection, not only do we have connectivity, but no bottlenecks for the Glauber dynamics. At the same time, since we want to be able to sample from the original distribution conditioned on the realisation of the projected assignment, the projection should be relatively ‘mild’ so as not to lose too much information.

Most relevant to this paper is the recent work of Feng, He, and Yin
[
FHY21
]
, which introduced the idea of ‘states compression’, thereby considerably expanding the applicability of the method used in
[
FGYZ20
]
. We will survey their results in the next subsection when we compare them with our own. Here, we only note that compared to the deterministic algorithms for approximate counting, the randomized algorithms discussed here have two advantages: the running time is much faster (in fact, nearly linear in
n
), and they are efficient even when parameters such as
k,q
grow with
n
. On the other hand, the disadvantage is that so far, these methods are limited to atomic constraints, whereas the algorithm of
[
JPV20
]
is applicable to general instances of the symmetric LLL.

1.1.
Our results

We provide randomized algorithms for approximately counting the number of satisfying assignments and sampling from approximately the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments for LLL instances with atomic constraints.

We begin with our result for the following class of instances, which capture many interesting problems such as Boolean
k
-
\operatorname{CNF-SAT}
and
k
-hypergraph
q
-coloring. Later, in
Theorem

1.5
, we discuss a result for general atomic constraints.

Definition 1.1
.

A
(k,\Delta,q)
-CSP (constraint satisfaction problem) is an instance of the variable version, symmetric LLL in which each variable
X_{i}
is uniformly distributed on an alphabet
\Omega_{i}
of size
q
, each constraint depends on exactly
k
variables, and each constraint shares variables with at most
\Delta
other constraints.

As before, we say that a
(k,\Delta,q)
-CSP is atomic if every constraint is violated by exactly one assignment to its variables. Note that for an atomic
(k,\Delta,q)
-CSP, the LLL asserts that if
\Delta\leq cq^{k}
, for an absolute constant
c
, then there exists a satisfying assignment.

Theorem 1.2
.

Given an atomic
(k,\Delta,q)
-CSP on the variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, an error parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, and a parameter
\eta\in(0,1)
, suppose that one of the following conditions holds.

(T1)

k\geq 3
,
q\geq q_{0}(\eta)
, and
\Delta\leq c(\eta)\cdot q^{(k-1)/3+o_{q}(1)}
.

(T2)

k=2
,
q\geq q_{0}(\eta)
, and
\Delta\leq c(\eta)\cdot q^{4/9+o_{q}(1)}
.

(T3)

k\geq 2
,
q\geq 4
,
\Delta\leq c(\eta)\cdot q^{0.221k}/(k^{2}\cdot q\log{q})
.

(T4)

k\geq 2
,
q=3
,
\Delta\leq c(\eta)\cdot 3^{0.2k}/k^{2}
.

(T5)

k\geq 2
,
q=2
.
\Delta\leq c(\eta)\cdot 2^{0.1742k}/k^{2}
.

Here
q_{0}(\eta)
and
c(\eta)
are constants depending only on
\eta
.
Then, there is a randomized algorithm which runs in time

\tilde{O}(n\cdot((n/\epsilon)^{\eta}+\Delta)\cdot\Delta\cdot k),

where
\tilde{O}
hides polylogarithmic factors in
n,\Delta,1/\epsilon
,
k
,

\mu_{\operatorname{alg}}
of

where
\operatorname{uniform-satisfying}
denotes the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments and
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{TV}}
denotes the total variation distance between probability measures.

Remark
.

In
[
FHY21
]
, analogs of (T1) and (T5) are considered. In these cases, they obtain an algorithm for sampling from approximately the distribution
\operatorname{uniform-satisfying}
, and with a similar running time, under the more restrictive conditions:

(T’1)
[
FHY21
, Theorem 5,4]

k\geq 13
,

, and

.

(T’5)
[

k\geq 2
,

,
\Delta\leq c(\eta)\cdot 2^{k/13}
.

Remark
.

We find case (T1) of

remarkable since, prior to the work of Moser
[

]
, the best-known version of the

required the condition

1.2.
Approximate counting

Theorems

1.2
and
1.5
also imply efficient algorithms for approximately counting the number of satisfying assignments in the same regime. Indeed, by using the simulated annealing reduction in
[
FGYZ20
]
, one can easily show that if
T(\epsilon)
is the time to obtain one sample (from a distribution which is
\epsilon
-close in total variation distance to the uniform distribution), then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there is a randomized algorithm for approximately counting the number of satisfying assignments within a multiplicative factor of
(1+\delta)
, which runs in time

\tilde{O}\left(\frac{m}{\delta^{2}}T(\epsilon_{m,\delta})\right),

where

1.3.
Techniques

In
[
HSZ19
]
, the authors showed that for a Boolean
k
-CNF formula which is
monotone
, the Glauber dynamics on the space of satisfying assignments mix rapidly outside (a constant factor of) the hardness regime identified in
[
BGG+19
]
. For not necessarily monotone
k
-CNF Boolean formulas, the space of satisfying assignments may not even be connected. To overcome this barrier, and inspired by Moitra’s approach
[
Moi19
]
of ‘marking’ variables, the work
[
FGYZ20
]
introduced the following two step procedure for sampling a uniformly random satisfying assignment: first, sample from the induced distribution on the so-called unmarked variables, and then, given such a sample
Y
, sample from the uniform distribution on the satisfying assignments conditioned on the assignment to the unmarked variables being
Y
. The reason the last step is easy is that, given a typical assignment to the unmarked variables, the remainder of the formula factors into logarithmic-sized connected components, so that ordinary rejection sampling succeeds with high probability. The key, therefore, is to sample from the induced distribution on unmarked variables.

The recent work
[
FHY21
]
introduced the idea of ‘states-compression’, which generalizes the marking procedure of Moitra. Now, for each variable
v
with domain
\Omega_{v}
, one constructs a suitable map
\pi_{v}:\Omega_{v}\to Q_{v}
, and the assignment
Y
now lives in
\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}
. Once again, the projection is to be chosen so that given a typical realisation of
Y
, the remainder of the formula factors into logarithmic-sized connected components on which ordinary rejection sampling succeeds with high probability, and the main part is showing that one can efficiently sample
Y
from the corresponding distribution.

In order to sample
Y
, both
[
FGYZ20
,
FHY21
]
show that the Glauber dynamics for the distribution on the space
\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}
, induced by the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments, mix rapidly. For this, both works employ a one-step path coupling argument based on, and extending, the argument from
[
Moi19
]
. However, showing that the one-step path coupling is contracting requires additional assumptions on the relationship between
p
and
\Delta
, and indeed, this is the main reason for the degradation of the dependence between
p
and
\Delta
in the final results of
[
FGYZ20
,
FHY21
]
(and also in
[
Moi19
,
GLLZ19
]
). Furthermore, establishing contraction of the one-step path coupling requires considerable case analysis for different ranges of the parameters – for instance, the mixing for the regimes corresponding to
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.5
are analyzed separately in
[
FHY21
]
.

The main contribution of our work is to completely dispense with the path coupling analysis of this ‘projected Glauber dynamics’ and instead, to devise a novel information-percolation based argument which avoids the need to consider worst-case neighborhoods of a vertex. Such an argument is also the crux of
[
HSZ19
]
(which, in turn, is inspired by the argument in
[
LS16
]
). However, compared to
[
HSZ19
]
, we critically do
not
have monotonicity at our disposal. This makes certain ‘sandwiching’ arguments inaccessible, and consequently, necessitate developing a careful and somewhat elaborate notion of combinatorial structures, which we call minimal discrepancy checks (
Definition

5.6
). In a nutshell, the information-percolation argument is based on the fact that if the maximal one-step coupling of the Glauber dynamics fails to couple at some time, then there must already be another discrepancy between the configurations at that time. By tracking the evolution of these discrepancies back in time, we show, in fact, that the origin of the failure of the one-step maximal coupling can be attributed to the appearance of a minimal discrepancy check. By analyzing these minimal discrepancy checks with considerable care, we then show that they occur with probability which is essentially just low enough (
Proposition

5.9
) so as to overcome the union bound on the number of possible minimal discrepancy checks. We expect this part of our argument to also be useful in other contexts.

Introducing minimal discrepancy checks allows us to handle the projected Glauber dynamics in all regimes in a unified manner. Another component which facilitates this, and also contributes to our improved quantitative estimates, is the notion of admissible projection schemes (
Definition

3.2
) – in contrast to
[
FHY21
]
, the conditions we demand of the projections
\pi_{v}:\Omega_{v}\to Q_{v}
are seemingly more complicated, but these are exactly the conditions which show up in the analysis of the algorithm, and therefore, avoid unnecessary degradation of the parameters. Once the correct conditions for the admissible projection schemes are identified (and this is the non-trivial part), the argument for the existence itself is a standard (although a necessarily quite careful) probabilistic argument.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Lovász Local Lemma

The LLL provides a sufficient condition guaranteeing that the probability of avoiding a collection
\mathcal{C}
of “bad events” in a probability space is positive. When the LLL condition
Equation

1.1
is satisfied, the so-called LLL distribution,

\mu_{S}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{P}[\cdot\mid\wedge_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\overline{C}]

is well-defined (here, the subscript
S
is chosen to represent “satisfying”). For later use, we record a standard comparison between the LLL distribution
\mu_{S}[\cdot]
and the original distribution
\mathbb{P}[\cdot]
on the probability space i.e. the product distribution on
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. For any event
B
in the probability space, let

\Gamma(B)=\{C\in\mathcal{C}:\operatorname{vbl}(B)\cap\operatorname{vbl}(C)\neq\emptyset\}.

Theorem 2.1

(cf.
[
HSS11
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Under
Equation

1.1
, for any event
B
in the probability space,

\mu_{S}[B]\leq\mathbb{P}[B]\prod_{C\in\Gamma(B)}(1-e\cdot\mathbb{P}[C])^{-1}.

We also record here the following algorithmic version of the Lovász Local Lemma, which follows directly from Moser-Tardos algorithm
[
MT10
]
and is also used in
[
Moi19
,
FGYZ20
,
FHY21
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
MT10
]
)
.

Under
Equation

1.1
, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists a randomized algorithm which outputs, with probability at least
1-\delta
, a satisfying assignment in time
O(n\Delta k\log(1/\delta))
, where
k=\max_{C\in\cal{C}}|\operatorname{vbl}(C)|
.

Proof.

By
[
MT10
]
, under
Equation

1.1
, there exists a randomized algorithm which finds a satisfying assignment in at most
\frac{|\cal{C}|}{\Delta}\leq n
steps in expectation, where each step has time complexity
O(\Delta k)
. By Markov’s inequality, if we run this algorithm for
2n
steps, then with probability at least
1/2
, the algorithm returns a satisfying assignment. The desired conclusion now follows by running
\log(1/\delta)
independent copies of this algorithm for
2n
steps each. ∎

2.2.
Constraint satisfaction problems

Let
V
denote a collection of variables with finite domains
(\Omega_{v})_{v\in V}
satisfying
|\Omega_{v}|\geq 2
for all
v\in V
. A constraint on
V
is a map

C:\prod_{v\in V}\Omega_{v}\to\{\operatorname{True},\operatorname{False}\}.

We say that
C
depends on a variable
v\in V
if there exist
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\prod_{v\in V}\Omega_{v}
differing only in
v
such that
C(\sigma_{1})\neq C(\sigma_{2})
. For every constraint
C
, we fix
\operatorname{vbl}(C)\subseteq V
containing all variables that
C
depends on.
A constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) is specified by
\Phi=(V,(\Omega_{v})_{v\in V},\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of constraints. Given a constraint satisfaction problem, we say that
\sigma\in\prod_{v}\Omega_{v}
satisfies
\Phi
if and only if

C(\sigma)=\operatorname{True}\quad\text{for all }C\in\mathcal{C}.

We define the degree
\Delta
of a CSP to be

\Delta=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}}|\{C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}:\operatorname{vbl}(C)\cap\operatorname{vbl}(C^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\}|.

We say that
C
is an atomic constraint if

|C^{-1}(\operatorname{False})|=1.

A CSP
\Phi
is said to be atomic if every
C\in\mathcal{C}
is an atomic constraint. Popular examples of atomic constraint satisfaction problems are:

•

k
-CNF-SAT. Here,
\Omega_{v}=\{0,1\}
for all
v\in V
and
|\operatorname{vbl}(C)|=k
for all
C\in\mathcal{C}
.

•

k
-Hypergraph
q
-coloring. Let
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
denote a
k
-uniform hypergraph. To each vertex
v\in V
, we assign a color in
[q]
such that no hyperedge is monochromatic. This corresponds naturally to an atomic CSP
\Phi=(V,(\Omega_{v})_{v\in V},\mathcal{C})
with
\Omega_{v}=[q]
for all
v\in V
and
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{e,i}:e\in\mathcal{E},i\in[q]\}
where for
\sigma\in[q]^{V}
,

C_{e,i}(\sigma)=\operatorname{False}\iff\sigma(w)=i\quad\forall w\in e.

To every CSP, we associate an instance of the LLL as follows: the random variables are
X_{1},\dots,X_{v}
, where each
X_{i}
is uniformly distributed on
\Omega_{i}
. To each constraint
C\in\mathcal{C}
, we associate the event

\left\{\sigma\in\prod_{v\in V}\Omega_{v}:C(\sigma)=\operatorname{False}\right\}.

We will abuse notation and denote this event by
C
and the collection of all such events by
\mathcal{C}
.

Finally, for a CSP
\Phi
, we let
\mu_{\Phi}
denote the LLL distribution of the associated LLL instance i.e.
\mu_{\Phi}
is the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments of
\Phi
. When the underlying CSP is clear from context, we will omit the subscript and denote
\mu_{\Phi}
simply by
\mu
.

3.
Projection schemes

3.1.
Preliminaries

Given a CSP
\Phi=(V,(\Omega_{v})_{v\in V},\mathcal{C})
, a projection scheme is a collections of maps

\pi_{v}:\Omega_{v}\to Q_{v},

where
Q_{v}
is a finite alphabet with
|Q_{v}|\geq 1
. We will frequently denote the collection
(\pi_{v})_{v\in V}
simply by
\pi
. We let
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
denote the product distribution on
\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}
induced via
\pi
by the uniform distribution on
\prod_{v\in V}\Omega_{v}
. We also let
\mu_{\pi}
denote the distribution on
\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}
induced via
\pi
by
\mu=\mu_{\Phi}
.

Let
\Phi
be an atomic CSP. Recall that this means that for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists some
\boldsymbol{C}\in\prod_{v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)}\Omega_{v}
such that
X\in\prod_{v\in V}\Omega_{v}
does not satisfy
C
if and only if

X(v)=\boldsymbol{C}(v)\quad\forall v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C).

Given an atomic CSP
\Phi
and a projection scheme
\pi
, for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
, we define
\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}\in\prod_{v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)}Q_{v}
by

\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)=\pi_{v}(\boldsymbol{C}(v))\quad\forall v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C).

This naturally leads to a CSP
\Phi_{\pi}=(V,(Q_{v})_{v\in V},\mathcal{C}_{\pi}),

where for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
, there is a constraint
C_{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
such that for
Y\in\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}
,

C_{\pi}(Y)=\operatorname{False}\iff Y(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)\quad\forall v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C).

Motivated by this, for a constraint
C_{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
,
v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C_{\pi}):=\operatorname{vbl}(C)
and
Y\in\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}
, we say that
Y(v)
does not satisfy
C_{\pi}
if and only if
Y(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)
.

For a constraint
C\in\cal{C}
, let

b(C):=\max_{Y\in\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}}\prod_{u\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)}\mathbb{P}[X(u)=\boldsymbol{C}(u)|Y],

where
Y=\pi(X)
. Equivalently,

b(C)=\prod_{u\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)}|\pi_{u}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(u))|^{-1}.

Let

b:=\max_{C\in\cal{C}}b(C).

Also, let

q:=\max_{v\in V,Y\in\prod_{u\in V}Q_{u}}d_{\operatorname{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot],\mu_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot\mid Y^{-v}]),

where
Y^{-v}
denotes the
|V|-1
dimensional vector obtained by removing
Y(v)
from
Y
.

The following useful bound on the conditional marginals of
\mu_{\pi}
follows from

.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Phi
be an atomic CSP and let

Suppose that

Proof.

Consider the product distribution

on

and the

. The

\displaystyle\mu_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot\mid Z]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot](1-3b)^{-\Delta}.\qed

3.2.
Admissible projection schemes

The next definition isolates the class of projection schemes we will be interested in.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\Phi
be an atomic CSP and let
\pi
be a projection scheme. Let
\eta\in(0,1/2)
. We say that
\pi
is
admissible
if

(A1)

b\leq\eta/(300\Delta)
.

(A2)

There exists
\kappa\geq 4\log(3000\Delta)
such that for any
C\in\cal{C}
,

\displaystyle|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|^{2}\cdot\kappa^{2}\cdot\zeta(C)\cdot\prod_{v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)}\left((1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]+e^{-\kappa/3}\right)\leq(60000\Delta)^{-2}.

Furthermore,
\kappa\leq K(\log\Delta+\log{q}+\log{k})
for a universal constant
K
, for
q=\max_{v\in V}|Q_{v}|
, and for
k=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}}|\operatorname{vbl}(C)|
.

(A3)

For any
v\in V
and
C,C^{\prime}\in\cal{C}
with
v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)\cap\operatorname{vbl}(C^{\prime})
,

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]\leq\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}^{\prime}_{\pi}(v)]\leq 2\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)].

(A4)

For
v\in V
,
\pi(v)
can be computed in time
K\log|\Omega_{v}|
, and for any
q\in Q_{v}
a uniform value in
\pi_{v}^{-1}(q)
can be sampled in time

, where

is a universal constant.

Remark
.

Note that the condition

for

in (A1) guarantees that

(1-3b)^{-\Delta}\leq 1+6b\Delta\leq 1+\eta/50.

The following is the main result of this section.

Let

with

.
Moreover, for any

, this projection scheme can be constructed, with probability at least

Proof.

We give here the complete proof of

Let

. For each

, we let

and define the projection
\pi_{v}
arbitrarily so that the preimage of each element in

has size either

or

. Clearly this projection scheme can be constructed in time

,
\pi(v)
can be computed in time

, and for any

. This confirms (A4).

Assuming that

is a sufficiently large constant depending on

and that

which confirms (A1).
Moreover, for

and for any

.

(3.1)

\frac{1}{1.5A^{2/3}}\leq\frac{\lfloor A/R\rfloor}{A}\leq\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]\leq\frac{\lceil A/R\rceil}{A}\leq\frac{1.5}{A^{2/3}},

assuming again that

is a sufficiently large constant. This confirms (A3).

It remains to verify (A2). Let
\kappa=12\log(3000(\Delta+A))
. Note that
\kappa\geq 4\log(3000\Delta)
.
For all

, we have using

•

•

provided that

. ∎

4.2.
Connected components of the projected CSP

To control failures due to (S1) and (I1), we will show that, with high probability, the projected chain
Y_{t}
satisfies the property that for every connected component
H^{\prime}
of
H(Y_{t})
, we have
|\cal{C}(H^{\prime})|\leq 20\Delta\log(n\kappa/\epsilon)
. As in
[
Moi19
,
FGYZ20
,
FHY21
,
JPV20
]
, our analysis uses
2
-trees, which were first used in a similar context by Alon
[
Alo91
]
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
d_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the graph metric. A set of vertices
\cal{T}\subseteq V
is called a
2
-tree if for every
u\neq v\in\cal{T}
,
d_{G}(u,v)\geq 2
, and such that if we add an edge between all pairs of vertices
u,v\in\cal{T}
with
d_{G}(u,v)\leq 2
, then the resulting graph on
\cal{T}
is connected.

We will need the following result on the number of
2
-trees of a prescribed size which contain a given vertex.

Lemma 4.4

(cf.
[
FGYZ20
, Corollary 5.7]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with maximum degree
\Delta
. For any
v\in V
, the number of
2
-trees in
G
which contain
v
and have size
\ell
is at most
\frac{(e\Delta^{2})^{\ell-1}}{2}
.

Here,
e
is the base of the natural logarithm.

The next (standard) lemma shows that large
2
-trees exist in graphs of bounded maximum degree. We include the proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with maximum degree
\Delta
. Let
H=(V(H),E^{\prime})
be a connected subgraph of
G
and let
v\in V(H)
. Then, there exists a
2
-tree
\cal{T}
with
v\in\mathcal{T}\subseteq V(H)
such that
|\mathcal{T}|\geq|V(H)|/(\Delta+1)
.

Proof.

We construct such a
2
-tree greedily. Let
\cal{T}_{0}=\{v\}
,
H_{0}=V(H)
, and
v_{0}=v
. In the
i^{th}
-step, for
i\geq 1
, let
H_{i}=H_{i-1}\setminus(\{v_{i-1}\}\cup N_{G}(v_{i-1}))
, and choose (if possible)
v_{i}
to be some vertex in
H_{i}
such that
d_{G}(\cal{T}_{i-1},v_{i})=2
. We then let
\cal{T}_{i}=\cal{T}_{i-1}\cup\{v_{i}\}
. Observe that
H_{i}=V(H)\setminus(\cal{T}_{i-1}\cup N_{G}(\cal{T}_{i-1}))
.

We claim that if we cannot find
v_{i}
satisfying
d_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{i-1},v_{i})=2
, then it must be the case that
H_{i}=\emptyset
. Indeed, assume that
H_{i}
is nonempty. Since
H_{i}=V(H)\setminus(\cal{T}_{i-1}\cup N_{G}(\cal{T}_{i-1}))
is not empty, and since
H
is connected, there must exist some
u\in H_{i}
for which
d_{G}(\cal{T}_{i-1},u)>1
and some
u^{\prime}\in\cal{T}_{i-1}\cup N_{G}(\cal{T}_{i-1})
such that there is an edge between
u
and
u^{\prime}
. Note that
u^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{T}_{i-1}
, since
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{i-1}
means that
u\in N_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{i-1})
, a contradiction. Hence,
d_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{i-1},u^{\prime})=1
so that
d_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{i-1},u)\leq 2
, which combined with the previous lower bound gives
d_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{i-1},u)=2
.

Thus, when our construction terminates, we have a
2
-tree
\mathcal{T}
satisfying
\cal{T}\cup N_{G}(\cal{T})\supseteq V(H)
. Since the maximum degree in
G
is
\Delta
, it follows in particular that
|\cal{T}|\geq|V(H)|/(\Delta+1)
, as desired.
∎

With this preparation, we are ready to bound the probability of failure due to (S1) or (I1).

Proposition 4.6
.

Fix
0\leq t\leq T
. Then, with probability at least
1-(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{10}
, for every connected component
H^{\prime}
of
H(Y_{t})
, we have
|\cal{C}(H^{\prime})|<20\Delta\log(n\kappa/\epsilon)
.

Proof.

By the law of total probability, it suffices to prove the result after conditioning on the choice of variables
v_{1},\dots,v_{t}
chosen to be updated in the first
t
steps.
Suppose for contradiction that there exists a connected component
H^{\prime}
of
H(Y_{t})
with
|\mathcal{C}(H^{\prime})|\geq 20\Delta\log(n\kappa/\epsilon)=:\alpha
. Then, by definition, there must exist a connected component of
G(Y_{t})
of size at least
\alpha
containing a constraint
C_{*}\in\mathcal{C}(H^{\prime})
and a variable
v_{*}
with
v_{*}\in\operatorname{vbl}(C_{*})
(in particular,
v_{*}\in H^{\prime}
). Since the maximum degree of
G(Y_{t})
is
\Delta
, it follows from
Lemma

4.5
that there exists a
2
-tree
\cal{T}_{*}
of constraints in
\cal{C}(Y_{t})
such that
C_{*}\in\mathcal{T}_{*}
and
|\mathcal{T}_{*}|\geq\alpha/(\Delta+1)
. We now proceed to bound the probability of appearance of such a
2
-tree.

By
Lemma

4.4
, the number of
2
-trees in
G(Y_{t})
which are rooted at
C_{*}
and which have size
\ell
is at most
(e\Delta^{2})^{\ell-1}
. The main observation is the following: fix any such
2
-tree
\mathcal{T}
. Then, by definition, for every constraint
C\in\mathcal{T}
and for every
v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)
, we must have
Y_{t}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)
. Moreover, for any variable
v
, letting
t_{v}
denotes the last time before (and including)
t
that the value of
v
was updated (note that
t_{v}
is determined by our conditioning), we have (by the assumed distribution of
Y_{0}
and the description of the subroutine
\operatorname{Sample}(Y,u)
) that the value of
v
at
t_{v}
is chosen from one of two distributions:

•

The uniform distribution on
Q_{v}
, or

•

The distribution
\mu_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot\mid Y_{t_{v}-1}^{-v}]
.

In either case, the probability that
Y_{t_{v}}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)
is at most
(1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]
– indeed, this is true for the uniform distribution even without the
(1-3b)^{-\Delta}
factor, whereas for the other case, this follows immediately from
Lemma

3.1
. Since the last time before (and including)
t
that each variable is updated is determined by our conditioning, and since different constraints in
\mathcal{T}
are disjoint, it follows that the probability that none of the constraints in
\mathcal{T}
are satisfied is at most

\prod_{C\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)}((1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}[Y(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)])\leq\prod_{C\in\mathcal{T}}(3000\Delta)^{-2}\leq(3000\Delta)^{-2\ell},

where the first inequality uses condition (A2) of
Definition

3.2
. Therefore, by the union bound, it follows that the probability that there exists some
C_{*}\in\mathcal{C}(Y_{t})
and a
2
-tree
\mathcal{T}_{*}
rooted at
C_{*}
such that
|\mathcal{T}_{*}|\geq\alpha/(\Delta+1):=\ell
, and such that none of the constraints in
\mathcal{T}_{*}
are satisfied, is at most

n\Delta\cdot(e\Delta^{2})^{\ell-1}\cdot(3000\Delta)^{-2\ell},

where the first factor accounts for the number of choices for
\mathcal{C}_{*}
and the second factor is from
Lemma

4.4
.
Since by our choice of
\ell
,

n\Delta\cdot(e\Delta^{2})^{\ell-1}\cdot(3000\Delta)^{-2\ell}\leq(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{10},

we have the required assertion. ∎

4.3.
Rejection sampling

Having seen that the probability of failure due to (S1) or (I1) is very low, we now show that the probability of failure due to (S2) or (I2) is also very low.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
and let
Y\in\prod_{v\in V^{\prime}}Q_{v}
be a partial assignment. Let
H^{\prime}
be a connected component of
H(Y)
and suppose that the size of

is at most

. Let

be obtained by sampling each

independently and uniformly from

, and from

for each

. Then, the probability that

satisfies all constraints

is at least
(n\kappa/\epsilon)^{-\eta}
.

Here,
\eta
is the parameter appearing in
Definition

3.2
.

Proof.

By definition of
X
, the probability that a constraint

is not satisfied by

is at most

b(C)=\prod_{v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)}\frac{1}{|\pi_{v}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v))|}.

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the event that

satisfies all constraints

. Since

for all

and since

by (A1) of

, it follows from
Theorem

and (A1) that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}]\geq(1-3b)^{|\cal{C}(H^{\prime})|}\geq(1-3b)^{20\Delta\log(n\kappa/\epsilon)}\geq(n\kappa/\epsilon)^{-\eta}.\qed

We immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.8
.

Fix
1\leq t\leq T
. The probability that
\operatorname{Sample}
fails due to (S2) at time

is at most
(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{10}
. Moreover, the probability that
\operatorname{InvSample}
fails due to (I2) is at most
(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{9}
.

Recall that
S=10(n\kappa/\epsilon)^{\eta}\log(n\kappa/\epsilon)
.
By

4.7
, the probability that
\operatorname{Sample}
fails due to (S2) at time

is at most

(1-(\kappa n/\epsilon)^{-\eta})^{S}\leq\exp\left(-10(\kappa n/\epsilon)^{-\eta}(\kappa n/\epsilon)^{\eta}\log(n\kappa/\epsilon)\right)=(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{10}.

Moreover, again by
Proposition

4.7
, the probability that
\operatorname{InvSample}
fails due to any connected component

with

Therefore, by the union bound over all (at most

.
∎

Analysis of the main algorithm

The proof of

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Let
\mu_{\operatorname{alg}}
be the distribution on

\{\text{satisfying assignments of }\Phi\}\cup\{\operatorname{ERROR}\}

given by the output of the algorithm
\operatorname{Main}(\Phi,\pi,\epsilon)
. Also, let
\mu_{\operatorname{alg}^{\prime}}
be the distribution on

\{\text{satisfying assignments of }\Phi\}\subseteq\prod_{v\in V}\Omega_{v}

where

for

(where

is as in (M1)).

The relation between the distributions
\mu_{\operatorname{alg}}
and

is as follows: let
\mathcal{G}_{T}
denote the event that none of the calls to
\operatorname{Sample}
fail (either due to (S1) or (S2)) and that the call to

also does not fail (either due to (I1) or (I2)). Observe that

\mu_{\operatorname{alg}}\mid\mathcal{G}_{T}=\mu_{\operatorname{alg}^{\prime}}.

Therefore, by the characterization of the total variation distance in terms of coupling (cf.
[

, Proposition 4.7]
), we have

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{TV}}(\mu_{\operatorname{alg}},\mu_{\operatorname{alg}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[\mathcal{G}_{T}]

\displaystyle\leq T(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{10}+(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{9}+(T+1)(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{10}

\displaystyle\leq(\epsilon/\kappa n)^{5},

where the second line follows from

Proposition

4.6
and
Corollary

4.8
, and the third line follows from the value of
T
and since
\kappa\geq\log(\Delta)
((A2) of
Definition

3.2
).

Moreover, by

4.2
, we know that

from which it immediately follows (again by

Therefore, by the triangle inequality we have that

It remains to analyze the running time of the algorithm. Let

Each call to

takes time

so that the running time of the algorithm is

5.
Glauber dynamics for the projected distribution: Proof of
Proposition

4.2

5.1.
Preliminaries

Throughout this section, we fix an atomic CSP
\Phi=(V,(\Omega_{v})_{v\in V},\mathcal{C})
and an admissible projection scheme
\pi=(\pi_{v})_{v\in V}
with
\pi_{v}:\Omega_{v}\to Q_{v}
. Recall that
\mu_{\pi}
is the distribution on
\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}
induced via
\pi
by the uniform distribution on satisfying assignments,
\mu=\mu_{\Phi}
on
\prod_{v\in V}\Omega_{v}
. In this section, which is the main innovation of our work, we study the mixing of the Glauber dynamics for the distribution
\mu_{\pi}
. Recall that the Glauber dynamics is a discrete time Markov chain on the state space
\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}
whose transitions are as follows: given the current state
Y
, choose a uniformly random vertex
v\in V
and move to the state
Y^{\prime}
where

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}(w)

\displaystyle=Y(w)\quad\forall w\neq v

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}(v)

\displaystyle\sim\mu_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot\mid Y^{-v}].

It is standard that this chain is aperiodic and reversible with respect to
\mu_{\pi}
and by using the condition
e\cdot b\cdot\Delta<1
along with the LLL, it is also easily seen (cf.
[
FHY21
, Proposition 8.1]
) that this chain is irreducible. Therefore (cf.
[
LP17
, Corollary 1.17]
),
\mu_{\pi}
is the unique stationary distribution of this chain.

We denote the Glauber dynamics for
\mu_{\pi}
by
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Given
X_{0},Y_{0}
, let
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote a coupling of two copies of
Z_{t}
starting from
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
. For this coupling, let

\tau_{\operatorname{couple}}=\min\{t\geq 0:X_{t}=Y_{t}\}.

It is well known (cf.
[
LP17
, Theorem 5.4]
) that

(5.1)

\max_{Z_{0}}d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Z_{t},\mu_{\pi})\leq\max_{X_{0},Y_{0}}\inf_{\operatorname{couplings}}\mathbb{P}[\tau_{\operatorname{couple}}\geq t],

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of two copies of the Glauber dynamics with initial states
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
respectively. Thus, our goal in this section is to show that for any
X_{0},Y_{0}
, there is a coupling
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
which coalesces quickly with high probability.

In fact, the coupling that we will analyze is the optimal one-step coupling of the chains. Recall that this coupling is constructed as follows: given the current state
(X_{t-1},Y_{t-1})
, we choose a uniformly random vertex
v\in V
(in this case, we say that
v
is updated at time
t
) and move to the state
(X_{t},Y_{t})
where

\displaystyle X_{t}(w)=X_{t-1}(w)\quad\forall w\neq v

\displaystyle Y_{t}(w)=Y_{t-1}(w)\quad\forall w\neq v

\displaystyle(X_{t}(v),Y_{t}(v))\text{ is sampled from the optimal coupling of }(\mu_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot\mid X_{t-1}^{-v}],\mu_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot\mid Y_{t-1}^{-v}]).

Hence, throughout the remainder of this section,
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
will always denote the optimal one-step coupling of two copies of
Z_{t}
starting at
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
respectively.

We partition time into blocks of size

H=100\kappa\cdot n,

where
\kappa\geq 2
is the parameter appearing in (A2) of
Definition

3.2
.
By time block
K
, we mean the time interval
[HK,H(K+1))
. We will also need the following notation. Let
\mathfrak{C}=\cal{C}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
. Recall that

denotes the graph whose vertex set consists of constraints
C\in\mathcal{C}
and there is an edge between

if and only if
\operatorname{vbl}(C)\cap\operatorname{vbl}(C^{\prime})\neq\emptyset

Let

U=\{(v,t)\in V\times\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}:v\textrm{ is updated at time }t\}.

Recall that this means that
v
is the vertex chosen by the Glauber dynamics when the current states are
X_{t-1}
and
Y_{t-1}
. Recall also that by the definition of the optimal one-step coupling, the same vertex is chosen to be updated at a given time

in both chains.
Let

D=\{(v,t)\in V\times\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}:X_{t}(v)\neq Y_{t}(v)\}.

We call

the set of
discrepancies
.

Given a time interval

, we denote by

the set of variables
v\in V
which are not updated in

the set of variables
v\in V
which are updated at least

times in

.

Finally, for

does not satisfy

in

and that a partial assignment

denote the first (according to the orientation) constraint in

for which

. Let
\mathsf{P_{i}}
denote the part of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
from the starting point until and including

. We claim that the paths
\mathsf{P}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{P}_{K-1}
and the Boolean variables

from
\mathcal{D}
constitute a minimal discrepancy check starting at

, where we use the variables

from
\mathcal{D}
for each

in order to check condition (M3).

Indeed, (M1) and (M2) follow from (D1). (M3) follows from (D3) and

.
The first bullet point of (M4) follows from the the second bullet point of (D4), the construction of

s, and the fact that the

∎

Combining this lemma with

, we have the following.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
(v_{0},T_{0})\in U\cap D
. Then there exists a minimal discrepancy check
\cal{M}
starting at
(v_{0},T_{0})
.

To prepare for the next subsection, we introduce some more notation. To every minimal discrepancy check
\mathcal{M}
, we associate a graph
G(\mathcal{M})=(V(\mathcal{M}),E(\mathcal{M}))
defined as follows. The vertex set
V(\mathcal{M})
consists of pairs
(C,i)
where
1\leq i\leq K-1
and
C\in\mathsf{P}_{i}
. The vertices
(C,i)
and
(C^{\prime},i)
are connected to each other if and only if
C
and
C^{\prime}
are adjacent in
\mathsf{P}_{i}
. Moreover, if
C
is the last constraint in
\mathsf{P}_{i}
and
C^{\prime}
is the first constraint in
\mathsf{P}_{i+1}
, then
(C,i)
and
(C^{\prime},i+1)
are adjacent. Given the Boolean variables
f_{1},\dots,f_{K-1}
of
\mathcal{M}
, we can find some
1\leq\ell\leq K-1
and disjoint intervals
L_{1},\dots,L_{\ell}\subset\{1,\dots,K-1\}
with the following properties.

•

For all
a<b
,
\max{L_{a}}<\min{L_{b}}
.

•

For every
1\leq s\leq\ell
and for every

,
f_{i}=1
.

•

For every
i
such that
f_{i}=1
, there exists some
1\leq s\leq\ell
such that
i\in L_{s}
.

Given
L_{1},\dots,L_{\ell}
, we define oriented induced paths
\widehat{P}_{1},\dots,\widehat{P}_{\ell}
in

where
\widehat{P}_{1}
contains all the points

and for

\widehat{P}_{j}
contains all the points

. For
1\leq j\leq\ell
, let

We say that

is the

of the minimal discrepancy check and
\widehat{r}_{1},\dots,\widehat{r}_{\ell}
are its

.

Finally, for each

, by taking every other point

starting with the very last point, we obtain an independent set

in

such that

We have two cases.

(Ind1)

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\widehat{r}_{j}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_{i}/7
. In this case, we define

and

.

(Ind2)

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\widehat{r}_{j}<\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_{i}/7
. In this case, observe that we can find an independent set

in

such that every element

satisfies

,

, and

is also an independent set in

.

5.5.
Probability of a minimal discrepancy check

In the previous subsection, we showed that if
(v_{0},T_{0})\in U\cap D
, then there must exist a minimal discrepancy check starting at
(v_{0},T_{0})
. In this subsection, we will bound the probability (under the randomness driving the Glauber dynamics) of seeing a minimal discrepancy check with given leg lengths. This will then be combined with a union bound argument in the next subsection.

It will be convenient to use the following description of the Glauber dynamics for
\mu_{\pi}
. To each vertex
v
and each time
t
, we associate an independent uniform random variable
U(v,t)\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]
. At time
t
(recall that this means that the current states are
X_{t-1},Y_{t-1}
), we choose a variable
v
to update from the uniform distribution on
V
and choose
X_{t}(v)
and
Y_{t}(v)
according to the one-step maximal coupling of the corresponding conditional marginal distributions, with the realisation of
(X_{t}(v),Y_{t}(v))
determined using
U(v,t)
in the natural manner. With this notation, observe the following.

(O1)

For each
t,X_{t-1},Y_{t-1},v
, there exist subsets
I_{d}(v,X_{t-1},Y_{t-1})\subseteq[0,1]
of measure at most
2q
such that if
(v,t)\in U\cap D
, then
U(v,t)\in I_{d}(v)
. Here, as was defined in
Section

3
,

q=\max_{v\in V,Y\in\prod_{v\in V}Q_{v}}d_{\operatorname{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot],\mu_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\cdot\mid Y^{-v}]).

(O2)

Suppose
e\cdot b\cdot\Delta\leq 1
. Then, by
Lemma

3.1
, for each
v,C
, there exist subsets
I_{s}(v,C)\subseteq[0,1]
of measure at most

(1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]

such that if
v
does not satisfy
C
in at least one of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
and if
t^{\prime}
is the last update of
v
before time
t
, then
U(v,t^{\prime})\in I_{s}(v,C)
. Here, recall that

b=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}}b(C),

where

b(C)=\prod_{u\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)}|\pi_{u}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(u))|^{-1}.

Let
\mathbf{P}
denote the probability measure corresponding to the randomness of the Glauber dynamics i.e. the choice of vertex to update at time
t
and the i.i.d. random variables
U(v,t)\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]
. The main result of this subsection is the following.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
\Phi
be an atomic CSP and let
\pi
be an admissible projection scheme.
Let
\cal{M}
be a minimal discrepancy check starting from
(v_{0},T_{0})
, where
T_{0}\geq HK
(
H=100\kappa n
), and with leg lengths
(r_{1},\dots,r_{K-1})
. Further, let
\ell
be the effective parameter of
\mathcal{M}
and let its effective leg lengths be
(\widehat{r}_{1},\dots,\widehat{r}_{\ell})
. Then,

\mathbf{P}(\cal{M})\leq(3000\Delta)^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\hat{r}_{i}}\cdot(3000\Delta)^{-2|I_{0}(\mathcal{M})|}

Proof.

Recall the definition of the graph
G(\mathcal{M})
associated to
\mathcal{M}
and the independent set
I(\mathcal{M})=I_{0}(\mathcal{M})\cup I_{1}(\mathcal{M})
in this graph. Recall also that
I(\mathcal{M})
does not contain
(C_{0},1)
.
For
1\leq i\leq K-1
, let

\mathfrak{C}_{i}=\{(C,i)\in I(\mathcal{M})\}.

Also, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{0}(C,i)

\displaystyle=V^{0}((T_{i+1},T_{i}))\cap\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{+}(C,i)

\displaystyle=V^{+}((T_{i},T_{i-1}))\cap\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C),

and let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}^{0}_{i}

\displaystyle=\cup_{(C,i)\in\mathfrak{C}_{i}}\mathcal{V}^{0}(C,i),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}^{+}_{i}

\displaystyle=\cup_{(C,i)\in\mathfrak{C}_{i}}\mathcal{V}^{+}(C,i).

Note that for
1\leq i\leq K-1
, the sets
\mathfrak{V}^{+}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{V}^{0}_{i-1}
are completely determined by the choice of vertices selected to be updated between times
(T_{i},T_{i-1})=:\operatorname{Int}_{i-1}
. We say that
\operatorname{Int}_{i-1}
is
exceptional
if

|\mathfrak{V}^{+}_{i}|+|\mathfrak{V}^{0}_{i-1}|\geq\frac{n}{2}.

It follows from a straightforward application of the Chernoff bound that for any

,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\operatorname{Int}_{i-1}\text{ is exceptional}]\leq 2\exp(-H/12)\leq\exp(-8\kappa n).

Moreover, for disjoint subsets
V^{0}
and
V^{+}
of
V
such that
|V^{0}|+|V^{+}|\leq n/2
, we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\mathfrak{V}^{0}_{i-1}=V^{0},\mathfrak{V}^{+}_{i}=V^{+}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}[\mathfrak{V}^{+}_{i}=V^{+}\mid\mathfrak{V}^{0}_{i-1}=V^{0}]\mathbf{P}[\mathfrak{V}^{0}_{i-1}=V^{0}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{H|V^{+}|}{6n}\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{|V^{0}|}{n}\right)^{H}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-10\kappa\cdot(|V^{0}|+|V^{+}|)},

where in the second line, we have used that conditioned on
\mathfrak{V}_{i-1}^{0}=V^{0}
, the vertex to be updated at each step is chosen uniformly from a set of size at least
n/2
, and further, membership in
\mathfrak{V}_{i}^{+}
for different vertices is negatively dependent.

Let
\mathfrak{I}\subset\{1,\dots,K-1\}
denote the (random) set of
i
such that
\operatorname{Int}_{i-1}
is exceptional. By the law of total probability, it suffices to show that

\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{M}\cap\{\mathfrak{I}=\hat{J}\}]\leq(3000\Delta)^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{r}_{i}}\cdot(3000\Delta)^{-2|I_{0}(\mathcal{M})|}.

for every subset
\hat{J}
of
\{1,\dots,K-1\}
. Therefore, for the remainder of the proof, we fix such a choice of
\hat{J}
and let

\hat{I}=\{i\in\{1,\dots,K-1\}:\{i-1,i,i+1\}\notin\hat{J}\}.

Also, for each
i\in\hat{I}
, let
\mathfrak{T}_{i-1}
denote the sigma-algebra generated by the random variables which record the choice of vertex to update for times between
[T_{i},T_{i-1})
. Note that
\mathfrak{V}^{0}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{V}^{+}_{i}
are measurable with respect to
\mathfrak{T}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{T}_{i-1}
respectively. We denote the realizations of these random sets, given the relevant sigma algebras, by
V^{0}_{i}(\mathfrak{T}_{i})
and
V^{+}_{i}(\mathfrak{T}_{i-1})
respectively. Note that, by the definition of
\hat{I}
, it is necessarily the case that
|V^{+}_{i}(\mathfrak{T}_{i-1})|+|V^{0}_{i-1}(\mathfrak{T}_{i-1})|\leq n/2
. We also let

\hat{I}_{1}:=\{i\in\hat{I}:f_{i}=1\},

and

\hat{I}_{0}:=\{i\in\hat{I}:f_{i}=0,\exists C\in\mathcal{C}\text{ such that }(C,i)\in I_{0}(\mathcal{M})\}.

Observe that, by construction of the set

, we have that for any

and any
j\in\hat{I}_{1}
,

(5.4)

\operatorname{Int}_{i-1}\cap(\operatorname{Int}_{j-1}\cup\operatorname{Int}_{j})=\emptyset.

Now, consider

and
V^{0}_{i}=V^{0}_{i}(\mathfrak{T}_{i})
. Moreover, conditioning on

fixes, for each

and all the update times of each variable

. Observe that

|\mathcal{T}(\widehat{C})|\leq 2\kappa H/n\cdot|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|

and that the following holds.

•

If there exists
T^{\prime}\in[T_{i},T_{i-1})
such that
C
is not satisfied in at least one of

and
Y_{T^{\prime}}
by
\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)\setminus({V}^{0}_{i}\cup{V}^{+}_{i})
, then there exists some

v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)\setminus(V^{0}_{i}\cup V^{+}_{i})

. Indeed,
t
can be taken to simply be the maximum of
T_{i}
and the last time before (and including)
T^{\prime}
that any variable in
\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)\setminus(V^{0}_{i}\cup V^{+}_{i})
is updated. Call this event

.

Since conditioned on
\mathfrak{T}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{T}_{i-1}

is determined by

for each

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{0}(\widehat{C})\mid\mathfrak{T}_{i},\mathfrak{T}_{i-1}]

\displaystyle\leq|\cal{T}(\widehat{C})|\prod_{v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)\setminus({V}^{0}_{i}\cup{V}^{+}_{i})}\mathbf{P}[U(v,t_{v})\in I_{s}(v,C)\mid\mathfrak{T}_{i},\mathfrak{T}_{i-1},t]

•

If there exists
T^{\prime}\in[T_{i},T_{i-1})
and

–

, and

–

, and

–

U(v^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in I_{d}(v^{\prime})\cap I_{s}(v^{\prime},C^{\prime})

Call this event

.

Let
\mathfrak{T}_{\leq i}
denote the sigma algebra generated by

Note that, conditioned on

,

(for all

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1}(\widehat{C})\mid\mathfrak{T}_{\leq i}]

\displaystyle\leq|\cal{T}(\widehat{C})|\cdot\mathbf{P}[U(v^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in I_{d}(v^{\prime})\cap I_{s}(v^{\prime},C^{\prime})\mid\mathfrak{T}_{\leq i},t]\cdot\prod_{v\in V^{\prime}(\widehat{C})}\mathbf{P}[U(v,t_{v})\in I_{s}(v,C)\mid\mathfrak{T}_{\leq i},t]

\displaystyle\leq 200|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|\kappa^{2}\cdot\min\left(q,\Delta(1-3b)^{-\Delta}\max_{C^{\prime}:v^{\prime}\in\operatorname{vbl}(C^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{C^{\prime}}_{\pi}(v^{\prime})]\right)

3.2

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}(\widehat{C})\mid\mathfrak{T}_{\leq i}]

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{M}\cap\{\mathfrak{I}=\hat{J}\}]

5.2
,

5.3
,

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
K\geq 1
and let
\cal{B}_{K}
be the event that there exists a minimal discrepancy check starting from
(v_{0},T_{0})
with
T_{0}
in time block
K
. Then,

Proof.

This follows from a union bound. First, note that the graph
G(\mathfrak{C})
whose vertices are
\mathfrak{C}=\mathcal{C}\times\{0,1,\dots,K\}
and which has an edge between
(C,i)
and
(C^{\prime},j)
if and only if
|j-i|\leq 1
and
\operatorname{vbl}(C)\cap\operatorname{vbl}(C^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
has maximum degree at most
3\Delta
. From this, it readily follows that the number of minimal discrepancy checks starting from time block
K
, with effective parameter
\ell
, and with effective leg lengths
(\widehat{r}_{1},\dots,\widehat{r}_{\ell})
is at most

(3\Delta)\cdot(3\Delta)^{\widehat{r}_{1}+\dots+\widehat{r}_{\ell}}\cdot 2^{K-1},

where the factor
2^{K-1}
accounts for the choice of
f_{i}
and the first factor of
(3\Delta)
accounts for the choice of the vertex in
G(\mathfrak{C})
adjacent to
(C_{0},1)
. By
Proposition

5.9
, we know that for a minimal discrepancy check with effective parameter
\ell
and effective leg lengths
(\widehat{r}_{1},\dots,\widehat{r}_{\ell})
,

\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{M}]\leq(3000\Delta)^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{r}_{i}}\cdot(3000\Delta)^{-2|I_{0}(\mathcal{M})|}.

Therefore, by the union bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{B}_{K}]

\displaystyle\leq(3\Delta)\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{K-1}\sum_{\widehat{r}_{1},\dots,\widehat{r}_{\ell}}(3000\Delta)^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{r}_{i}}\cdot(3\Delta)^{\widehat{r}_{1}+\dots+\widehat{r}_{\ell}}\cdot(3000\Delta)^{-2|I_{0}(\mathcal{M})|}\cdot 2^{K-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{K-1}\cdot(3\Delta)\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{K-1}\sum_{\widehat{r}_{1},\dots,\widehat{r}_{\ell}}2^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{r}_{i}}\cdot 500^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{r}_{i}}\cdot(3000\Delta)^{-2|I_{0}(\mathcal{M})|}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{K-1}\cdot(3\Delta)\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{K-1}\sum_{\widehat{r}_{1},\dots,\widehat{r}_{\ell}}2^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{r}_{i}}\cdot 500^{-2|I(\mathcal{M})|}

Here, the third line follows by the construction of
I(\mathcal{M})
and the fourth line follows from (Ind1), (Ind2), and
\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_{i}\geq K-1
. ∎

v\in\operatorname{vbl}(C)\cap\cal{V}_{S}
. Note that
t_{S}(C)b_{S}(C)=p_{S}(C)
.

Let
\gamma>0
be a parameter to be chosen later from an optimization problem. As in previous cases, we can use Markov’s inequality and obtain

\displaystyle\Pr[b(C)^{-1}<p^{-\gamma}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[b_{S}(C)^{-1}<p_{L}(C)^{\alpha_{*}-\gamma}p_{S}(C)^{-\gamma}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[b_{S}(C)/p_{S}(C)>p_{L}(C)^{\gamma-\alpha_{*}}p_{S}(C)^{\gamma-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\theta>0}\left(\left(p_{L}(C)^{\alpha_{*}-\gamma}p_{S}(C)^{1-\gamma}\right)^{\theta}\mathbb{E}[(b_{S}(C)/p_{S}(C))^{\theta}]\right).

Let
\mathfrak{b}_{A}
be the random variable
1/|\pi_{v}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v))|
for (some)
v\in\operatorname{vbl}_{A}(C)
; note that the distribution of this random variable is the same for all
v\in\operatorname{vbl}_{A}(C)
so that
\mathfrak{b}_{A}
is indeed well defined.
Let

\theta_{b}=\arg\min_{\theta>0}\max_{A\in\{2,3,5,7\}}\log_{A}\left(A^{-(1-\gamma)\theta}\mathbb{E}[(A\mathfrak{b}_{A})^{\theta}]\right),

and let

m_{b}=-\max_{A\in\{2,3,5,7\}}\log_{A}\left(A^{-(1-\gamma)\theta_{b}}\mathbb{E}[(A\mathfrak{b}_{A})^{\theta_{b}}]\right).

Then

\Pr[b(C)^{-1}<p^{-\gamma}]\leq p_{L}^{(\alpha_{*}-\gamma)\theta_{b}}p_{S}^{m_{b}}\leq p^{\min(m_{b},(\alpha_{*}-\gamma)\theta_{b})}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[t(C)^{-1}<p^{-3\gamma}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[t_{S}(C)^{-1}<p_{L}(C)^{3(\alpha_{*}-\gamma)}p_{S}(C)^{-3\gamma}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[b_{S}(C)^{-1}>p_{S}(C)^{-1+3\gamma}p_{L}(C)^{3(\gamma-\alpha_{*})}]

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\theta>0}\left(\left(p_{L}(C)^{3(\alpha_{*}-\gamma)}p_{S}(C)^{1-3\gamma}\right)^{\theta}\mathbb{E}[(b_{S}(C)^{-\theta}]\right).

Let

\theta_{t}=\arg\min_{\theta>0}\max_{A\in\{2,3,5,7\}}\log_{A}\left(A^{-(1-3\gamma)\theta}\mathbb{E}[(\mathfrak{b}_{A})^{-\theta}]\right),

and let

m_{t}=-\max_{A\in\{2,3,5,7\}}\log_{A}\left(A^{-(1-3\gamma)\theta_{t}}\mathbb{E}[(\mathfrak{b}_{A})^{-\theta_{t}}]\right).

Then

\Pr[t(C)^{-1}<p^{-3\gamma}]\leq p_{L}^{3(\alpha_{*}-\gamma)\theta_{t}}p_{S}^{m_{t}}\leq p^{\min(m_{t},3(\alpha_{*}-\gamma)\theta_{t})}.

We will maximize
\gamma
subject to the constraints that

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{p}}\left(\min(m_{b},(\alpha_{*}-\gamma)\theta_{b})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma,

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{p}}\left(\min(m_{t},3(\alpha_{*}-\gamma)\theta_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma.

Numerical optimization shows that one can take
\gamma=0.142
.

For such
\gamma
and for sufficiently small
\eta>0
, assuming further that
\Delta\leq p^{-\gamma-o_{p}(1)}
, it follows from the LLL that there exists a choice of projections
\pi_{v}
for
v\in\mathcal{V}_{S}
so that for all
C\in\cal{C}
,

b(C)\leq(600\Delta/\eta)^{-1},

and

(6.1)

t(C)\leq p^{3\gamma}.

Furthermore, by
Theorem

2.2
, such projection maps can be constructed in time
O(n\Delta k\log(1/\delta))
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

For such a projection scheme, the properties (A1), (A3) and (A4) are easily verified. We now show that (A2) is also satisfied. Let
\kappa=12\log(3000(\Delta+100))
. Since
\zeta(C)\leq 2\Delta
, we have

\displaystyle|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|^{2}\kappa^{2}\cdot\zeta(C)\cdot\prod_{v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)}\left((1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]+e^{-\kappa/3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(12\log(3000(\Delta+100)))^{2}\cdot 2\Delta\cdot|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|^{2}\prod_{v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)}\left((1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]+e^{-\kappa/3}\right).

By (A1) and our choice of
\kappa
, we have

(1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]+e^{-\kappa/3}\leq\frac{3}{4}
for all
C\in\mathcal{C}
and
v\in\operatorname{\overline{vbl}}(C)
. We have two cases.

(1) If there exists some
v\in\operatorname{\overline{vbl}}(C)
for which
e^{-\kappa/3}>\frac{1}{|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]
, then

\displaystyle(12\log(3000(\Delta+100)))^{2}\cdot 2\Delta\cdot|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|^{2}\prod_{v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)}\left((1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]+e^{-\kappa/3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(12\log(3000(\Delta+100)))^{2}\cdot 2\Delta\cdot 2|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|^{2}\cdot(3/4)^{\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)-1}\cdot e^{-\kappa/3}

\displaystyle\leq(60000\Delta)^{-2},

where the last inequality follows by our choice of
\kappa
.

(2) On the other hand, if for all
v\in\operatorname{\overline{vbl}}(C)
,
e^{-\kappa/3}\leq\frac{1}{|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]
, and further since

(6.2)

\displaystyle 6b\Delta\leq\eta/100,

we have

\displaystyle(12\log(3000(\Delta+100)))^{2}\cdot 2\Delta\cdot|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|^{2}\prod_{v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)}\left((1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]+e^{-\kappa/3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(12\log(3000(\Delta+100)))^{2}\cdot 6\Delta\cdot|\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)|^{2}\prod_{v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)}\left((1-3b)^{-\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]\right)

(6.3)

\displaystyle\leq(12\log(3000(\Delta+100)))^{2}\cdot\Delta\cdot C_{\eta}\prod_{v\in\overline{\operatorname{vbl}}(C)}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]^{1-\eta}.

Here we have used the inequality
x^{2}(1-\delta)^{x}<C_{\delta}
, which holds for all
\delta\in(0,1)
,
x\geq 1
, and for a sufficiently large
C_{\delta}
depending only on
\delta
.
Now, since
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\operatorname{value}(v)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v)]=\frac{|\pi_{v}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\pi}(v))|}{|\Omega_{v}|}
, it follows from
Equation

6.1
that
Section

6
is at most
(60000\Delta)^{-2}
, which verifies (A2). ∎
