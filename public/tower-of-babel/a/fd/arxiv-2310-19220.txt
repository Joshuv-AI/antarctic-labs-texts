From Stream to Pool: Pricing Under the Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility Authors are alphabetically ordered

Abstract
Dynamic pricing models often posit that a stream of customer interactions occur sequentially, where customers’ valuations are drawn independently. However, this model is not entirely reflective of the real world, as it overlooks a critical aspect, the law of diminishing marginal utility, which states that a customer’s marginal utility from each additional unit declines. This causes the valuation distribution to shift towards the lower end, which is not captured by the stream model. This motivates us to study a pool-based model, where a pool of customers repeatedly interacts with a monopolist seller, each of whose valuation diminishes in the number of purchases made according to a discount function. In particular, when the discount function is constant, our pool model recovers the stream model. We focus on the most fundamental special case, where a customer’s valuation becomes zero once a purchase is made. Given k k prices, we present a non-adaptive, detail-free (i.e., does not “know” the valuations) policy that achieves a 1 / k 1/k competitive ratio, which is optimal among non-adaptive policies. Furthermore, based on a novel debiasing technique, we propose an adaptive learn-then-earn policy with a O ~ ​ ( k 2 / 3 ​ n 2 / 3 ) \tilde{O}(k^{2/3}n^{2/3}) regret.

Introduction

Pricing with an unknown demand function is a fundamental challenge in revenue management.
Originating from the seminal work of
Gallego and Van Ryzin 1994
, many existing works employ what we call the
stream
model:
a stream of customers arrives sequentially, wherein each customer makes a purchase if the current price is lower than their valuation, which is an i.i.d. draw from a fixed distribution.
The seller aims to maximize the total revenue by controlling the price.

However, the stream model does not adequately model many real-world scenarios.
Consider the sale of new clothing items.
Customers sporadically monitor the price (by visiting the store in person or checking the price online) based on their availability.
A customer usually needs at most one unit of the clothing, so they exit the market immediately after making a purchase.
In other words, their valuation has
diminished
to zero.
Moreover, due to the product’s affordability, customers do not consider it worthwhile to strategically time their purchases, even if they are aware that the price might decrease.

In this example, the demands are
neither
identically nor independently distributed.
On the one hand, high-valuation customers tend to purchase early and subsequently leave the market, resulting in a
shift
in the valuation distribution towards the lower end.
Although non-stationary demand models have been studied (see, e.g.,
Besbes and Zeevi 2011
,
Besbes and Sauré 2014
,
Den Boer 2015
), the non-stationarity is often
exogenous
, incorporating external factors such as seasonality instead of the seller’s actions.
In contrast, the non-stationarity in the clothing example is
endogenous
, governed by the seller’s decisions.
Specifically, the price sequence affects how the remaining customers’ valuations are distributed.

Orthogonal to non-stationarity, the independence assumption is also questionable in the above scenario.
Consider a two-period
markdown
policy that reduces the price from high to low, and a
markup
policy that chooses these two prices in reverse order.
In the stream model, these two policies have the same expected revenue.
However, in the above example the markup policy yields less revenue than the markdown policy because high-valuation customers might take advantage of the low initial price, and thus the seller misses out on higher revenue opportunities.

1.1
The Pool Model: An Informal Description

The above discussion motivates us to introduce the following
pool model
(PM).
A
pool
of myopic customers is present at the beginning of the time horizon.
Each customer
interacts
with the seller by peeking at the price according to an independent Poisson process (whose parameter we refer to as the
interaction rate
).
When such an interaction occurs, the customer purchases one unit if her valuation is higher than the price.

The valuation of each customer is proportional to a
discount function

\psi(m)
where
m
is the number of purchases she has made.
This function encompasses a fundamental aspect often overlooked in the study of dynamic pricing: The marginal utility of each additional purchase decreases.
This phenomenon is commonly known as the
law of diminishing marginal utility

(
Gossen 1854
)
and is well studied in economics; see, e.g.,
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
,
Arrow et al. 1974
, and
Rabin 2000
.

One facet of this problem, where
\psi(m)
is constant, has been well understood.
In fact, the pool model is
equivalent
to the stream model (see Theorem
2.1
) in this case, which has been extensively studied

(
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
,
Besbes and Zeevi 2009
,
Babaioff et al. 2015
)
.
For example, if the unknown valuation distribution is supported on a known set of cardinality
k
, the stream model is equivalent to
k
-armed bandits, which has an
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{kn})
minimax regret
(
Auer et al. 2002
)
.
(Here, the notation
\tilde{O}
suppresses poly-logarithmic terms in
n,k
.)

However, little is known about the opposite (albeit equally fundamental) extreme where
\psi(m)=\mathbbm{1}(m=0)
, which we call as the
unit-demand pool model
(UDPM).
In this setting, each customer exits the market permanently once a purchase is made.
This scenario has only been previously analyzed in the
immediate-buy
setting where the interaction rate is infinite
(
Talluri and Van Ryzin 2006
)
, which is a somewhat straightforward scenario; see our discussion in
Section

3
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of the UDPM: Red dots represent customer interactions, where they peek at the price.
A purchase is made at the first interaction event (
T_{3}
) where the price is lower than the valuation.

We emphasize that in our model, customers make myopic decisions
based only on the current price.
The myopic model provides a reasonable approximation of customer behavior in many scenarios, particularly where the unit price is relatively low, making strategic behavior unappealing to most customers.
As
Li et al. 2014
observed from air travel data sets, (only) “5.2% to 19.2% of the population are strategic”.

Against this backdrop, we study the pool model, with a focus on the most basic setting - the UDPM.
We first show that when the valuations are known,
we can handle the problem using fairly standard techniques.
Then, we present non-adaptive and adaptive policies that do not require knowledge of the valuations and yet exhibit robust performance.

We initiate the study of the pool model, focusing on the simplest yet fundamental setting where
\psi(m)=\mathbbm{1}(m=0)
.
We contribute to the study of dynamic pricing in the following ways.

1. A Novel Model: Unification of Two Worlds.

Our primary contribution is the introduction of a novel pool-based pricing model, which better aligns with reality in many scenarios compared to the extensively studied stream model.
Our pool model also encompasses the stream model as a special case (see
Theorem

2.1
), thereby broadening the scope of dynamic pricing and providing new avenues for further research.

2. Known Valuations: Computing Optimal Policies.

We provide two algorithms for computing a near-optimal policy, assuming that the valuations are known.
As the structure of the optimal adaptive policy is intricate, we will focus on non-adaptive policies (i.e., with predetermined price sequences).
Specifically, we show the following:

(a)

Price Monotonicity.
We show that there exists an optimal non-adaptive policy whose price sequence is non-increasing; see Proposition
3.2
.
These policies are usually called
markdown policies
.

(b)

Concavity of the Revenue Function.
(a) suggests that we may restrict our attention to markdown policies, which can be represented as
k
-dimension vectors.
We show that the expected revenue is a concave function in the markdown policy; see
Proposition

3.5
.

(c)

Computing an Optimal Non-Adaptive Policy.
Building upon (a) and (b), we present two efficient algorithms for computing an optimal non-adaptive policy, based on dynamic programming and concave maximization.

3. Unknown Valuations: Competitive Non-Adaptive Policies.

We present detail-free (i.e., do not “know” the valuations) policies that enjoy robust performance against any policy, possibly adaptive or detail-dependent (i.e., knows the valuations).
We provide a complete settlement by showing the following.

(a)

Competitive Policy for Finite Price Set.

Given any set of
k
prices, we present a non-adaptive, detail-free policy that guarantees a
(1/k)
fraction of the revenue achievable by any policy; see
Theorem

4.8
.

(b)

Competitive Policy for Infinite Price Set.
Given any (continuous) price interval
[p_{\rm min},p_{\rm max}]
, we construct a detail-free, non-adaptive policy which guarantees a
1/(1+\ln\rho)
fraction of the optimum, where
\rho=p_{\rm min}/p_{\rm max}
; see
Theorem

4.12
.

(c)

Optimality.
Our algorithm achieves the optimal competitive ratio.
Specifically, we show that for any
\varepsilon>0
and any non-adaptive policy, there is a
k
-price instance on which this policy yields no more than
1/k+\varepsilon
fraction of the optimal revenue; see
Theorem

4.2
.
A similar result can be shown for price intervals.

4. Unknown Valuations: Adaptive Policy with Sublinear Regret.

Despite their simplicity, non-adaptive policies have two major drawbacks: Their performance (i) does not improve as
n
increases, and (ii) deteriorates
linearly
in
k
.
Thus, we study adaptive policies, presenting one with a
\tilde{O}(k^{2/3}n^{2/3})
regret against the optimal non-adaptive policy; see
Theorem

5.2
.
This is achieved by combining the following components.

(a)

Debiasing the Demand.
Unlike the stream model, which is equivalent to
multi-armed bandits
(MAB), we face an additional challenge of
confounding
observations.
Specifically, when we select a price
p
, customers with valuations greater than
p
can also buy, but we do not observe the customers’ valuations.
This makes it difficult to estimate the valuations.
To address this, we propose a novel unbiased estimator for the valuations.

(b)

Achieving
o(n)
Regret.

We propose a simple
learn-then-earn
policy based on the above estimator.
When
k=2
, this policy achieves a
\tilde{O}(n^{2/3})
regret as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
A straightforward extension of the analysis leads to a
\tilde{O}(kn^{2/3})
regret bound for arbitrary
k<\infty
.

(c)

Achieving
o(kn)
Regret.

Unlike in MAB, it is not straightforward to obtain regret bounds that are sublinear in
k
.

We improve the dependence on
k
via a more refined analysis.
We show that the cumulative estimation error prices form a
self-neutralizing
process, which, loosely, means that the estimation error at the
j
-th price level has the tendency to cancel the cumulative (signed) errors over the previous
(j-1)
price levels.
Furthermore, we show that the sequence of estimation errors satisfies
conditional sub-gaussianity
.
These two properties enable us to obtain the claimed regret bound.

Related Work

Our work is related to the following lines of research.

1.3.1
Dynamic Pricing in the Stream Model

The stream model has been extensively studied since the seminal work of
Gallego and Van Ryzin 1994
.
The problem is particularly intriguing when the demand model is unknown, where the seller must balance learning and earning

(
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
)
.
Various fundamental aspects have also been investigated, including finite inventory
(
Besbes and Zeevi 2009
,
Wang et al. 2014
,
Babaioff et al. 2015
,
den Boer and Zwart 2015
)
, parametric demand functions
(
den Boer and Zwart 2013
)
,
joint inventory-pricing control
(
Chen and Simchi-Levi 2004
,
Chen et al. 2019a
)
, customer choice model
(
Broder and Rusmevichientong 2012
)
, personalization
(
Cohen et al. 2020
,
Ban and Keskin 2021
)
, non-stationarity
(
Besbes and Zeevi 2011
,
Chen et al. 2023
)
, ranking
(
Gao et al. 2022
)
and discontinuity in the demand function
(
den Boer and Keskin 2020
)
, among others.
Although the stream model is broadly applicable, in this work we aim to understand the pricing problem from a new perspective through the pool-based model.

We will soon see that every UDPM instance admits and optimal non-adaptive policy which is non-increasing; see
Proposition

3.2
.
In revenue management, these policies are often referred to as
markdown
policies
(
Smith and Achabal 1998
,
Heching et al. 2002
,
Caro and Gallien 2012
)
.
A recent line of work studies markdown policies under the stream model, with unknown demand

(
Chen 2021
,
Jia et al. 2021
,
Jia et al. 2022
,
Salem et al. 2021
)
.
Unlike our work, they view monotonicity as a
constraint
rather than as a property derived from the model’s intrinsic properties.

1.3.2
Intertemporal Pricing.

Intertemporal price discrimination has been studied since the seminal work of
Coase 1972
, who postulates that a monopolist selling a durable good would have to sell the product at its marginal cost unless it commits to a pricing policy.
The idea behind what is known as the
Coase conjecture
is that rational consumers know that the monopolist will reduce the price and therefore would wait until the price reaches the marginal cost.
Henceforth, many works in economics focused on
characterizing optimal pricing policies, given that consumers strategically time their purchases (
Coase 1972
,
Stokey 1979
,
Conlisk et al. 1984
, and
Sobel 1991
).
This problem has also received significant attention in the revenue management community; see, e.g.,
Su 2007
,
Aviv and Pazgal 2008
,
Ahn et al. 2007
,
Correa et al. 2016
,
Wang 2016
.

However, the strategic behavior assumption is no longer reasonable in many applications.
For example, when the supply is finite or the good is perishable, consumers may not have the incentive to wait for a lower price
(
Correa et al. 2016
)
.
This motivates some work to incorporate myopic customers

(
Besanko and Winston 1990
,
Besbes and Lobel 2015
,
Lobel 2020
,
Caldentey et al. 2017
)
.
These works assumed
continuous
monitoring of prices.
In contrast, in our model, customers monitor the price stochastically according to independent Poisson processes.

Talluri and Van Ryzin 2006
presented an LP formulation for computing an optimal policy for the pool-based model with
\lambda=\infty
when given a limit on the number of price changes.

Other work has considered price-monitoring with finite frequency, usually by assuming a monitoring cost

(
Su and Zhang 2009
,
Aviv and Pazgal 2008
,
Cachon and Feldman 2015
)
and
Chen and Farias 2018
.
Empirically,
(
Moon et al. 2018
)
estimated that consumers’ opportunity costs for an online visit range from $2 to $25.
These works either focus on characterizing the equilibrium or assume a known model of the dynamics, whereas our work focuses on decision making under unknown demand.

Finally, there is a line of work which studies time-evolution of customer valuations.

Garrett 2011
and
Deb 2014
considered the impact on pricing strategies when customers’ valuation evolves stochastically.

Kakade et al. 2013
and
Pavan et al. 2014
studied dynamic mechanism design where the buyer valuation changes over time, based on the private signals received.

Chawla et al. 2016
investigated a pricing problem where a consumer’s valuation evolves according to her previous experience as a martingale.

Chen et al. 2019c
studied a dynamic pricing problem in which the demand for a single product type in multiple local markets evolves linearly between two consecutive change points.

Leme et al. 2021
considered a dynamic pricing problem in which the buyer’s valuation can change adversarially, but not too quickly.

1.3.3
Robust Optimization in Pricing.

In the non-adaptive setting, our problem (of optimizing the competitive ratio) boils down to a
robust optimization
problem, a framework commonly used in the revenue management literature; see, e.g.,
Ma et al. 2021
,
Besbes et al. 2022
,
Wang 2023
.
Our non-adaptive setting may be most similar to the myopic customer setting in Section 4 of
Caldentey et al. 2017
.
They also assume that the seller only knows the support of the valuations, but not the actual valuations.
However, they assume that a customer makes a purchase
immediately
when the price is below the valuation, which is essentially a special case of our problem where the interaction rate approaches
\infty
.

In contrast, our work focuses on general
\lambda
, which is substantially more challenging than the
\lambda=\infty
case.
In fact, with
\lambda=\infty
,
any
policy that selects the prices in descending order is optimal.
(Note, however, that this does not trivialize
Caldentey et al. 2017
’s results, as they consider customer arrivals.)
However, if
\lambda<\infty
, the seller should make judicious decisions about how much time to spend at each price:
If the price drops too quickly, a high-valuation customer may not have any interactions before the price reduces, incurring a revenue loss.
If the price decreases too slowly, the seller has less time to attract customers from the lower segments of the market.

1.3.4
Partially Observable Reinforcement Learning.

Our problem can be reformulated as a
Markovian Decision Process
(MDP).
In fact, we can characterize the state by (i) the remaining customers in each valuation group and (ii) the remaining time.
A key challenge is that the seller only observes the total demand, but not the demand from each valuation group.
To address this, it is natural to introduce a prior distribution and reformulate this problem as a
partially observable MDP
(POMDP).
However, classical hardness results suggest that learning in POMDPs can be (both computationally and statistically) intractable even in simple settings
(
Krishnamurthy et al. 2016
)
.
Recent results for learning POMDPs are not applicable to our problem for multiple reasons.
First, they are restricted to MDPs with special properties, such as
block MDPs

(
Krishnamurthy et al. 2016
,
Du et al. 2019
)
and
decodable MDPs

(
Efroni et al. 2022
)
, but these do not hold in our setting.
Second, these methods typically depend on revisiting states, which is not feasible in our problem: state evolution is unidirectional, since the number of customers is non-increasing.

A related class of POMDP is the
restless bandits
problem
(
Whittle 1988
)
, where the state of each arm evolves regardless of whether it is played.
This resembles the property of our problem that the remaining number of customers in every valuation group (“state”) may change even if the corresponding price (“arm”) is not selected.
However, in our problem, the transition probabilities depend on the arm selected, which is not true in restless bandits, rendering the existing results
(
Guha et al. 2010
,
Ortner et al. 2012
)
inapplicable.

2
Formulation

We formally define our pool model and explain how it captures the stream model as a special case.

2.1
The General Pool Model

The seller is given a set
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1}>\dots>p_{k}\}
of prices to select from.
A pool of
n
customers is present from the beginning.
Customer
i\in[n]
has an
initial valuation

v_{i}>0
.
After making
m
purchases, her valuation becomes
\psi(m)v_{i}
where the
discount function

\psi:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow[0,1]
is non-increasing.

Each customer interacts with the seller according to an independent Poisson process over a finite time horizon
[0,1]
, (this does not lose generality since we can rescale
\lambda
by the length of the horizon) with an identical rate
\lambda>0
for all customers.
We call each event an
interaction
and
\lambda
the
interaction rate
.
When an interaction occurs, the customer observes the price and makes a purchase if the price is less than or equal to her valuation.
The seller has unlimited inventory and knows both
\lambda
and
\psi
.
We denote this instance by
{\rm Pool}(\lambda,\mathcal{P},{\bf v},\psi)
where
{\bf v}=(v_{i})_{i\in[n]}
.

The seller aims to choose a sequence of prices to maximize the expected revenue.
The process of choosing prices is called a
policy
, which can be formally defined as a stochastic process
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
on
{\cal P}
.
We refer to a policy as
detail-free
or
detail-dependent
, depending on whether it relies on the knowledge of
\bf v
.
The
revenue
of a policy
X
is
R_{X}:=\int_{0}^{1}X_{t}\ dS_{t}
where
S_{t}
is the (random) sales up to time
t
.

2.2
Unifying the Pool and Stream Models

To see the connection between the pool and stream models, let us rephrase the stream model
(
Gallego and Van Ryzin 1994
)
.
Customer interactions occur according to a Poisson process with a
base rate

\lambda_{\rm base}
.
In each interaction, the customer’s valuation is drawn i.i.d., where we denote by
d(p)
the Phys. Rev. B that the valuation is greater than or equal to
p\in\mathcal{P}
.
For any policy
(X_{t})
, the demand rate at time
t
is
d(X_{t})\cdot\lambda_{\rm base}
.
We denote this instance by
{\rm GvR}(\lambda_{\rm base},\mathcal{P},d)
.

We show that the stream model is a special case of the pool model.
Suppose the valuations are given by
{\bf v}=(v_{i})_{i\in[n]}
.
The
demand function

d_{\bf v}:\cal P\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is then given by
d_{\bf v}(p)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(v_{i}\geq p)
.
Next, we show that
{\rm Pool}(\lambda,{\cal P},{\bf v},\psi\equiv 1)
is equivalent to
{\rm GvR}(n\lambda,{\cal P},d_{\bf v})
in terms of expected revenue.

Theorem 2.1

(Unifying Pool and Stream)

Let
X
be any policy. For any
s,t\in[0,1]
, denote by
D_{s,t}
the (random) demand in the time interval
[s,t]
.
Denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\rm GvR}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\rm Pool}
the expectation in instances
{\rm GvR}(\mathcal{P},n\lambda,d_{\bf v})
and
{\rm Pool}(\mathcal{P},\lambda,{\bf v},\psi\equiv 1)
respectively.
Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\rm GvR}\left[D_{s,t}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rm Pool}\left[D_{s,t}\right].

To see this, suppose the price is
X_{t}
in a small interval
[t,t+\delta]\subseteq[0,1]
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rm GvR}[D_{t,t+\delta}]\approx d(X_{t})\cdot\lambda_{\rm base}\delta=d(X_{t})\cdot n\lambda\delta.

(1)

On the other hand, in the pool model, each customer monitors the price with Phys. Rev. B
\approx\lambda\delta
.
Thus,

\mathbb{E}_{\rm Pool}[D_{t,t+\delta}]\approx\lambda\delta\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbbm{1}(v_{i}\geq X_{t})=\lambda\delta\cdot d(X_{t})n,

which matches
Eq.

1
.
We finally note that this equivalence holds in expectation, but the revenue distributions may differ.

2.3
Our Focus: The Unit-Demand Pool Model (UDPM)

In this work, we focus on the important special case where
\psi(m)=\mathbbm{1}(m=0)
, referred to as the
unit-demand pool model
(UDPM).
In this setting, each customer
i
’s valuation at any time is either
0
or the initial valuation
v_{i}
.
Therefore, it loses no generality to assume that
v_{i}\in\cal P=\{p_{j}\}_{j\in[k]}
.
We denote by
n_{j}
the number of customers with initial valuation
p_{j}
, which we refer to as
type-
j
customers.
We denote by
{\rm UDPM}(\lambda,\mathcal{P},(n_{j})_{j\in[k]})
the corresponding UDPM instance.

The problem is easy when the interaction rate is
\lambda=\infty
.
If the price is allowed to change arbitrarily many times, the problem is trivial, since any policy that selects all prices in
\cal P
in decreasing order is optimal.
If the number of price changes is limited by a budget, the problem is non-trivial but straightforward via a simple linear program (LP); see Section 5.5.1 in
Talluri and Van Ryzin 2006
.
Therefore, we focus on the scenario where
\lambda<\infty
.

3
Detail-Dependent Policy

We start with the scenario where the valuation distribution is known.
Unfortunately, unlike the stream model, where the optimal detail-dependent policy selects a fixed price, it is substantially more challenging to characterize the optimal policy in the pool model.
To see this, recall that in the MDP formulation, the “state” (i.e., number of remaining customers of each type) is not observable.
We therefore restrict ourselves to a simple yet compelling policy class where prices are determined in advance, regardless of the observations.

Definition 3.1

(
Non-adaptive Policy)

A policy
(X_{s})_{s\in[0,1]}
is non-adaptive if for any
s\in[0,1]
, the random variable
X_{s}
is a constant.

Non-adaptive policies are widely applied in practice due to their simplicity

(
Aviv and Pazgal 2008
,
Yin et al. 2009
,
Chen et al. 2016
,
Caldentey et al. 2017
,
Chen et al. 2019b
,
Ma et al. 2021
)
.
We show how to efficiently find an optimal non-adaptive policy for any UDPM instance when the valuations are known.

3.1
Monotonicity of the Optimal Non-Adaptive Policy

We first introduce a key structural result:
we show that an optimal non-adaptive policy can be found within the class of markdown policies.
Similar monotonicity results have been established in, for example,
Gallego et al. 2008
,
Nasiry and Popescu 2011
,
Adida and Özer 2018
.
This monotonicity property is obvious when
\lambda=\infty
;
Here, we establish this result for arbitrary
\lambda
.

Proposition 3.2

(Price Monotonicity)

There exists an optimal non-adaptive policy
(X_{s})_{s\in[0,1]}
such that
X_{s}\geq X_{t}
a.s. whenever
0\leq s<t\leq 1
.

To see this, we apply a swapping argument.
Suppose that the price is
p_{L}
(“low”) in some time interval
[t-\varepsilon,t]
and increases to
p_{H}
(“high”) in
[t,t+\varepsilon]
.
We claim that the expected revenue does not decrease if we swap
p_{H},p_{L}
.
To see this, consider a customer with valuation
v
.
If
v<p_{L}
, then the customer will not buy in
[t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon]
regardless of the swap.
If
p_{L}\leq v<p_{H}
, then the purchase Phys. Rev. B remains the same.
If
v\geq p_{H}
, then our revenue can only increase (or remain the same) if we offer
p_{H}
earlier than
p_{L}
.

Remark 3.3

This monotonicity may not hold for optimal adaptive policies.
In fact, suppose that at some time, the market no longer contains low-valuation customers, yet high-valuation customers still remain.
Then, an optimal adaptive policy should increase the price.
This suggests that adaptive policies are substantially more intricate than non-adaptive ones.
\square

We present a closed-form revenue function for any non-adaptive markdown policy, represented by a sequence
{\bf t}=(t_{j})_{j\in[k]}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}=1
.
Here,
t_{j}
represents the amount of time the policy selects
p_{j}
.

Proposition 3.4

(Revenues of Non-adaptive Markdown Policies)

Consider a UDPM instance
\mathcal{I}={\rm UDPM}(\lambda,\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k},\{n_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
.
For any non-adaptive markdown policy
{\bf t}=(t_{j})_{j\in[k]}
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\mathcal{I}):=\mathbb{E}[R_{X}]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}n_{\ell}\sum_{j:\ell\leq j}p_{j}e^{-\lambda\sum_{i=\ell}^{j-1}t_{i}}\left(1-e^{-\lambda t_{j}}\right).

(2)

Each term in the inner summation of
Eq.

2
is the expected revenue from type-
\ell
customers when we select
p_{j}
.
The term
e^{-\lambda\sum_{i=\ell}^{j-1}t_{i}}
is the probability that a type-
\ell
customer is still in the market (i.e., not made a purchase yet) when the policy switches to
p_{j}
.
The term
1-e^{-\lambda t_{j}}
is the probability that a type-
\ell
customer makes a purchase when the price is
p_{j}
, conditional on the event that she is still in the market.

3.2
Two Algorithms

By
Propositions

3.2
and
3.4
, finding an optimal non-adaptive policy reduces to

\displaystyle\max_{{\bf t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})}\quad\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}n_{\ell}\sum_{j:\ell\leq j}p_{j}e^{-\lambda\sum_{i=\ell}^{j-1}t_{i}}\left(1-e^{-\lambda t_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}=1,\quad t_{j}\geq 0,\quad\forall j\in[k].

(3)

Our first approach is based on dynamic programming (DP). We encode the
state
1
1

1

We note this is distinct from
the “state” in the MDP formulation described earlier.
by (i) remaining time
t
, (ii) price
p_{j}
under consideration, and (iii) the number
m
of customers remaining in the market whose valuations are
strictly
higher than
p_{j}
.
The remaining time can continuously vary in
[0,1]
.
Formally, we define the
value function
as

\Phi(k,m,t)=p_{k}(m+n_{k})\cdot(1-e^{-\lambda t})

(4)

for any
m>0
and
t\in[0,1]
.
Then for any
j\leq k-1
, we recursively define

\Phi(j,m,t)=\max_{0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\left\{p_{j}\left(m+n_{j}\right)\left(1-e^{-\lambda t^{\prime}}\right)+\Phi\left(j+1,\left(m+n_{j}\right)e^{-\lambda t^{\prime}},t-t^{\prime}\right)\right\}.

(5)

Here, the function
\Phi(j,m,t)
can be interpreted as the maximum expected revenue that a non-adaptive policy earns by marking down the price from
p_{j}
, with
m
additional customers whose valuations are higher than
p_{j}
.
Specifically, we can view
t^{\prime}
as the amount of time that the policy selects
p_{j}
.
The first term is the “immediate reward” (i.e., expected revenue at
p_{j}
) and the second is the maximum expected future revenue.

Our goal is to compute
\Phi(1,0,1)
.
This can be done via a DP-based

Algorithm 1

using the recursion in
Eq.

4
and
Eq.

5
.
For efficient computation, we discretize the time horizon into small intervals of length
\varepsilon
.
Since the function
1-e^{-\lambda t}
is
\lambda

O(\lambda\varepsilon)
discretization error in the value function.

Our second approach is based on continuous optimization.
The global convergence of this approach is ensured by the concavity of the revenue function.

Proposition 3.5

(Concavity of Revenue Function)

The function
{\rm Rev}({\bf t},{\cal I})
is concave in
\bf t
for any fixed UDPM instance
{\cal I}
.

In the case of either approach, we conclude the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6

(Computing Optimal Non-Adaptive Policy)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, given any UDPM instance
{\cal I}
we can compute a non-adaptive policy
{\bf t}
such that
|{\rm OPT}_{\rm NA}(\mathcal{I})-{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\mathcal{I})|=O(\lambda\varepsilon)
in time polynomial in
k
and
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
.

So far, we have shown that the UDPM is tractable if the
(n_{j})

In
Sections

4
and
5
, we consider the case where the
(n_{j})
’s are unknown.

4

Non-Adaptive Detail-Free Policy

One of the central challenges in dynamic pricing is demand uncertainty.
From now on, we focus on detail-free policies, i.e., those that do not require knowledge of
(n_{j})_{j\in[k]}
.
We identify non-adaptive policies with robust performance, quantified by the competitive ratio.

Definition 4.1

(
Competitive ratio)

Let
\Pi
be the class of all (possibly adaptive) policies and
\mathcal{F}
be a family of UDPM instances.
The competitive ratio of a policy
X
is

{\rm CR}(X,\mathcal{F}):=\inf_{\mathcal{I}\in\cal F}\frac{{\rm Rev}(X,\mathcal{I})}{{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I})}\quad\text{where}\quad{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I})=\sup_{X^{\prime}\in\Pi}{\rm Rev}(X^{\prime},\mathcal{I}).

When the family
{\cal F}
of instances is clear, we will say a policy
X
is
c
-competitive if
{\rm CR}(X,{\cal F})\geq c
.
Note that the competitive ratio is defined for any policy
X
, regardless of its adaptivity, and measures the performance of
X
relative to that of an optimal (possibly adaptive and detail-dependent) policy.

We first state a negative result.
Considering detail-free non-adaptive policies, we first show that the best-possible competitive ratio one could hope for is
1/k
.

Theorem 4.2

(Upper Bound on the Competitive Ratio)

Let
{\cal F}_{k}
be the family of UDPM instances with
k
prices.
For any
\varepsilon>0,k\geq 1
and detail-free non-adaptive policy
{\bf t}
, there is a UDPM instance
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
s.t.

{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon})\leq\left(\frac{1}{k}+\varepsilon\right){\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}).

Essentially, this upper bound is proved by considering a Dirac distribution of valuations on a price that depends on
\cal P
.

We defer the proof to Appendix
B.1
.

4.1

Two Simple (But Impractical) Policies

The upper bound in
Theorem

4.2
can be matched with a simple detail-free policy, called the naive randomized (NR) policy: Select a price uniformly at random and commit to it in the entire time horizon.

Proposition 4.3

The NR policy is
1/k
-competitive.

However, this policy is highly impractical since it behaves in a pathological manner.

Suppose all customer valuations are at the lowest price.
In this case, the NR policy chooses the optimal price w.p.
1/k
but otherwise generates
0
revenue (w.p.
1-1/k
).

A natural fix is to consider a deterministic analogue of the NR policy. Consider the naive deterministic (ND) policy that allocates
1/k
time to each price.

Using Jensen’s inequality, it
is not hard to show:

Proposition 4.4

The ND policy is
1/k
-competitive.

Although this policy also meets the optimal
1/k
-bound, there are many cases
where it performs poorly, since it does
not
take into account the price set.
Consider a set
{\cal P}
of
k\gg 2
prices evenly spaced in the interval
[1,2]
.
A simple policy that commits to the lowest price
p_{k}=1
is
1/2
-competitive, but the ND policy is only
1/k
-competitive (attained when all valuations are
1
).

4.2

A Robust Non-adaptive Policy

In the rest of this section, we propose a policy with a simple closed-form formula that not only matches the upper bound on the competitive ratio, but also behaves more robustly compared to the NR and ND policy.

4.2.1

Max-Min Optimization.

To proceed, we introduce an intuitive upper bound on the revenue of
any
detail-dependent policy.
By Proposition
3.2
, we lose no generality by restricting ourselves to markdown policies.
For any instance
\mathcal{I}=(\lambda,\mathcal{P},(n_{j})_{j\in[k]})
and non-adaptive markdown policy
{\bf t}=(t_{j})_{j\in[k]}
, consider
f({\bf t};n_{1},\dots,n_{k}):={\rm Rev}(X,\mathcal{I})/{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I}).

Then, the problem of maximizing the CR is equivalent to

(P0)

\displaystyle\max_{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}}\min_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}\left\{f(t_{1},\dots,t_{k};n_{1},\dots,n_{k}):\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}=n\right\},

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}=1,

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad t_{j}\geq 0,\quad\forall j\in[k].

Unfortunately, it is challenging to directly solve (P0), since most results on max-min optimization rely on special structures such as concavity-convexity.
(To clarify, the function
f
is not concave or convex. Please do not confuse this with the concavity of the revenue function in
Proposition

3.5
, since the revenue function is only the numerator part of
f
.)
Furthermore, nonconvex-nonconcave min-max optimization is computationally hard
(
Daskalakis et al. 2021
)
.
An alternative idea is to find a closed-form formula for the “min” part given any
\bf t
, thus reducing (P0) to a single-level problem.
However, finding a closed-form solution for
{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I})
is considerably formidable.

4.2.2

Upper Bound on the Optimum

We address the above challenge from a different perspective:
We will approximate the numerator and denominator of
f
, and transform (P0) into a tractable problem.
Then we argue that this transformation preserves the CR up to a factor of
k
.
We start by introducing an upper bound on
{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I})
.

Proposition 4.5

(Upper Bound on the Optimum)

For any (possibly adaptive or detail-dependent) policy
X
and instance
\mathcal{I}={\rm UDPM}(\lambda,\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k},\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k})
, we have

{\rm Rev}(X,\mathcal{I})\leq{\rm UB}(\mathcal{I}):=\sum_{i\in[k]}n_{i}p_{i}(1-e^{-\lambda}).

This inequality is self-evident by observing that the expected revenue of a customer with a valuation
v
is at most
v(1-e^{-\lambda})
.
For readers familiar with economics,
{\rm UB}(\mathcal{I})
is
the revenue achieved by
the first-degree price discrimination, modulo a factor of
1-e^{-\lambda}
.

Remark 4.6

This upper bound becomes tighter as
\lambda
increases.
To see this, observe that when
\lambda=\infty
, we can achieve (perfect) first-degree price discrimination by moving from the highest to the lowest price, hence the identity holds.
On the other extreme, UB can be very loose when
\lambda
is close to
0
, essentially because
{\rm UB}(\mathcal{I})
is achieved by a personalized policy while
{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I})
is defined over non-personalized policies.
Next, we construct a non-adaptive policy whose revenue is
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\cdot\mathrm{UB}(\mathcal{I})
, which implies that UB approximates the optimum within a factor of
k
.
\square

Next, we derive a robust policy based on this upper bound.

4.2.3

A Robust Non-adaptive Policy

The above upper bound motivates us to consider a simpler function
\tilde{f}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k};n_{1},\dots,n_{k}):={\rm Rev}(\mathcal{I})/{\rm UB}(\mathcal{I})
as a surrogate for the original objective
f
.
Unfortunately,
\tilde{f}
is the ratio of two non-linear functions, and so the corresponding max-min problem is still challenging to solve.

We address this by linearizing both the numerator and denominator, thereby converting the max-min optimization to an LP.
The above sequence of reductions loses only a factor of
k
(see Appendix
B.5
).
We then derive the following robust non-adaptive policy by finding a closed-form solution to the resulting LP.

Definition 4.7

(
Robust Policy)

Given any price set
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}
, the robust policy
{\bf t}^{\star}_{\mathcal{P}}=(t^{\star}_{j})_{j\in[k]}
is

\displaystyle t_{k}^{\star}=\frac{1}{k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j+1}}{p_{j}}}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad t_{j}^{\star}=\left(1-\frac{p_{j+1}}{p_{j}}\right)t_{k}^{\star},\quad\forall j=1,\dots,k-1.

(6)

For example, for
\mathcal{P}=\left\{1,2^{-1},\dots,2^{-(k-1)}\right\}
, we have
t_{k}^{\star}=2/(k+1)
and
t^{\star}_{j}=1/(k+1)
for
j=1,\dots,k-1
, i.e., the policy spends
1/(k+1)
time on each of the first
(k-1)
prices and
2/(k+1)
time on the last.

This robust policy also matches the upper bound on the CR.

Theorem 4.8

(Competitive Non-adaptive Policy)

Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}
the family of UDPM instances with price set
\mathcal{P}
.
Then,

\displaystyle{\rm CR}({\bf t}^{\star}_{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}})\geq\frac{1}{k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j+1}}{p_{j}}}\geq\frac{1}{k}.

(7)

Remark 4.9

The second inequality can be very loose.
Consider again the set
{\cal P}
of
k
prices evenly spaced in
[1,2]
.
The ND policy has a CR of
\frac{1-e^{-\lambda/k}}{1-e^{-\lambda}}\approx\frac{1}{k}
when
\lambda
is small.
In contrast, our robust policy has a CR of

\frac{1}{k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j+1}}{p_{j}}}=\frac{1}{k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{2-(j+1)/k}{2-j/k}\right)}=\frac{1}{k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{k-j}\right)}\approx\frac{1}{1+\log k}

for
\cal P
when
k
is large, which is much better than the ND policy.
\square

As a by-product,
Theorem

4.8
implies a bound on the adaptivity gap.
Let
\Pi_{\rm NA}
be the class of non-adaptive detail-free policies and
\Pi
the class of (any) detail-free policies.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
be the set of UDPM instances with
k
prices.
For any instance
\cal I
, denote
{\rm OPT}_{\rm NA}(\mathcal{I})=\sup_{X\in\Pi_{\rm NA}}{\rm Rev}(X,\mathcal{I})
.
Then,

{\rm Rev}({\bf t}^{\star}_{\cal P},\cal I)\leq{\rm OPT}_{\rm NA}(\cal I)\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm UB}(\cal I)\geq{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I}),

and therefore Theorem
4.8
immediately implies the following.

Corollary 4.10

(Adaptivity Gap)

For any
k\geq 1
and
\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
, we have

{{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I})}\leq k\cdot{\rm OPT}_{\rm NA}(\mathcal{I}).

4.3

Extending to Continuous Price Intervals

Suppose
\cal P=[a,b]
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we define the
\varepsilon
-geometric grid as
\overline{\cal P}_{\varepsilon}=\{a_{\ell}:=(1+\varepsilon)^{\ell}\mid\ell=1,\dots,\lceil\log_{1+\varepsilon}(b/a)\rceil\}.

We now analyze the CR of the robust policy (given in
Definition

4.7
) on
\overline{\cal P}_{\varepsilon}
.

By restricting to the geometric grid, we lose a (multiplicative) factor of
(1+\varepsilon)
in the revenue. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Rev}({\bf t}^{\star}_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}},\mathcal{I})}{{\rm UB}(\mathcal{I})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(1+\epsilon)\left(k-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{i+1}}{p_{i}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)(k-\frac{k-1}{1+\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon\log_{1+\varepsilon}\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{1+\ln\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}},

(8)

where the last step is because
\varepsilon/\ln(1+\varepsilon)
increases in
\varepsilon
.
We summarize the above as follows.

Definition 4.11

(
Robust Policy for Continuous Price Space)

Given the price interval
[p_{\rm min},p_{\rm max}]
, we define the robust non-adaptive policy
X=(X_{t})
as

\displaystyle X_{t}=\begin{cases}p_{\max}\cdot e^{-t\left(1+\ln\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}\right)},&\text{if }0\leq t\leq 1-\frac{1}{1+\ln\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}}\\
p_{\min},&\text{if }1-\frac{1}{1+\ln\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}}<t\leq 1.\end{cases}

(9)

Theorem 4.12

(Competitive Ratio for Price Intervals)

For any UDPM instance
\cal I
with price set
\cal P=[p_{\rm min},p_{\rm max}]
, the robust policy
X
satisfies

\frac{{\rm Rev}(X,\mathcal{I})}{{\rm UB}(\mathcal{I})}\geq\frac{1}{1+\ln\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}}.

We visualize the robust non-adaptive policies in
Fig.

2
.
The price curves become steeper as
p_{\min}
decreases and when
t
approaches 1, the price reaches
p_{\min}
and remains constant at
p_{\min}
until the end of the horizon.
This is consistent with our intuition.
In fact, when
p_{\min}
is small, the “opportunity cost” to choose the lowest price is relatively small.

5

Adaptive Detail-Free Policy

Now we consider adaptive policies in the presence of unknown demand.
Specifically, the seller only knows that the underlying instance
\cal I
comes from the family
{\cal F}(\lambda,{\cal P},n)
of instances with interaction rate
\lambda
, price set
\cal P
and a total number
n
of customers.
In particular, the seller does not know
(n_{j})_{j\in[k]}
.

As discussed in
Section

3
, the structure of the optimal adaptive policy is substantially more delicate (in particular, it may not be markdown). We therefore compare
our policy against an optimal non-adaptive policy.
By abuse of notation, we denote by
\opt(\mathcal{I})
instead of
{\rm OPT}_{\rm NA}({\cal I})
.

Definition 5.1

(
Regret)

For any UDPM instance
{\cal I}={\rm UDPM}(\lambda,{\cal P},(n_{j})_{j\in[k]})
, we define the regret of a policy
X
as

\textrm{Regret}(X,{\cal I}):={\rm OPT}({\cal I})-\mathbb{E}[R_{X}]
where
R_{X}
is the revenue of
X
.

5.1

The Learn-then-Earn Policy

We present a learn-then-earn (LTE) policy (which is adaptive and detail-free) with sublinear regret in
both

n
and
k
.
The policy operates in two phases: It first learns the
(n_{j})
’s by exploring each price, then commits to an optimal non-adaptive policy for the learned instance.
More precisely,

•

In the learning phase, we explore each price
p_{j}
for a fixed interval of length
s_{j}
in decreasing order.
Then, we construct estimates
(\widehat{n}_{j})_{j\in[k]}
of
(n_{j})_{j\in[k]}
using the realized demands
D_{j}
based on a novel debiasing approach (see
Section

5.2
).

•

In the earning phase, we compute a (nearly) optimal non-adaptive policy
\widehat{\bf t}
for the estimated instance
\widehat{\cal I}=(\lambda,{\cal P},\{\widehat{n}_{j}\}_{j\in[k]})
, which can be done efficiently per our discussion in
Section

3.2
.
We note that since we used
\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}<1
time to learn the instance, we will shrink every interval in
\widehat{\bf t}
by a
(1-\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j})
-factor.

We formally state the policy in
Algorithm

1
.
As the main result of this section, we show that this policy achieves sublinear regret in both
n,k
.

Theorem 5.2

(Adaptive Policy with Sublinear Regret)

For any
\lambda,{\cal P},n
, there exist parameters for
Algorithm

1
such that for any

{\cal I}\in{\cal F}(\lambda,{\cal P},n)
, we have

{\rm Regret}(X^{\rm LTE},{\cal I})=\tilde{O}(p_{1}(kn)^{2/3}).

Remark 5.3

Our regret bound is higher than the minimax optimal
\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{kn})

(
Auer et al. 2002

for the stream model (which is equivalent to

Intuitively, this is because in the stream model, the effect of exploration is
local

In contrast, in our model, a price decision has a
global

\square

Although this is similar to the Explore-Then-Commit (ETC) policy in MAB, there are several new challenges in the analysis, as we will explain in the rest of this section.

5.2

Key Component: Unbiased Estimator for the Valuations

As is standard in MAB, the key is to construct
unbiased
estimates of
(n_{j})
’s.
This is easy for
n_{1}
. Recall that in Algorithm
1
, we selected
p_{1}
in the time interval
[0,s_{1}]
and observed a (random) demand of
D_{1}
.
Note that
D_{1}\sim{\rm Bin}(n_{1},q(s_{1}))
where
q(x):=1-\exp(-\lambda x)
, so
D_{1}/q(s_{1})
is an unbiased estimate of
n_{1}
.

However, the situation becomes more complicated when we explore lower prices due to
confounding
.
In fact, when we select
p_{2}
, there may still be type-
1
customers who have not yet exited the market.
Therefore, both type-
1
and type-
2
customers contribute to the demand
D_{2}
observed at the price
p_{2}
.

We derive a novel unbiased estimator to overcome this.
Starting with
\widehat{n}_{1}=D_{1}/q(s_{1})
, for each
j=2,3,\dots,k
, we recursively define

\widehat{n}_{j}=\frac{D_{j}}{q(s_{j})}-\sum_{i\leq j-1}\left(\widehat{n}_{i}-D_{i}\right),

(10)

The first part is similar to the naive estimator for
\widehat{n}_{1}
, while the second part aims to “deconfound” the signals from customers with higher valuations.
We show that this estimator is unbiased.

Lemma 5.4

(Unbiasedness)

For any UDPM instance
{\cal I}
and policy parameters
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{n}_{j}]=n_{j}
for any
j=1,\dots,k
.

Proof 5.5

Proof Sketch.
For any
j\in[k]
, we have
D_{j}\sim{\rm Bin}(\sum_{i\leq j}n_{i}-\sum_{i\leq j-1}D_{i},\ q(s_{j}))
, so

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{n}_{j}\mid D_{1},\ldots,D_{j-1}]=\sum_{i\leq j}n_{j}-\sum_{i\leq j-1}D_{i}-\sum_{i\leq j-1}(\widehat{n}_{i}-D_{i})=n_{j}+\sum_{i\leq j-1}\left(n_{i}-\widehat{n}_{i}\right),

Therefore, by the law of total
Phys. Rev. B
,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{n}_{j}]=n_{j}+\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i\leq j-1}(n_{i}-\widehat{n}_{i})]
.
The second term is
0
by induction

Hypothesis 1

.
\square

\widehat{n}_{1}=D_{1}/q(s)
and
\widehat{n}_{2}=n-\widehat{n}_{1}
are unbiased estimates of
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
.
Using these, we compute an optimal non-adaptive policy
\widehat{\bf t}=(\widehat{t}_{1},\widehat{t}_{2})
for the estimated instance
\widehat{\cal I}=(\lambda,{\cal P},(\widehat{n}_{1},\widehat{n}_{2}))
.
Finally, since the remaining time is
1-s
, we implement the policy
(1-s)\bf\widehat{t}
, in which each price
p_{i}
is selected for
(1-s)\widehat{t}_{i}
time.

To analyze the regret, we first decompose it into two parts.

Definition 5.6

(
Regret Due to Learning and Estimation)

The remaining instance is defined as
{\cal I}_{\rm rem}=(\lambda,{\cal P},\{N_{1},N_{2}\})
where
N_{j}
(
j=1,2
) is the (random) number of type-
j
customers remaining after the learning phase.
Let
{\bf t^{\star}}
be an optimal non-adaptive policy for
\mathcal{I}
.
We define

\eta_{1}=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Rev}((1-s)\widehat{\bf t},\ {\cal I}_{\rm rem})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Rev}\left(\widehat{\bf t},\mathcal{I}\right)\right]\right|

and

\eta_{2}=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Rev}\left(\widehat{\bf t},\mathcal{I}\right)\right]-\opt(\mathcal{I})\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Rev}\left(\widehat{\bf t},\mathcal{I}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Rev}\left({\bf t}^{\star},\mathcal{I}\right)\right]\right|.

We can view
\eta_{2}
as the regret due to estimation error.
The interpretation of
\eta_{1}
may be less obvious.
It is the regret due to learning - the difference in the revenue if we apply
{\bf\widehat{t}}
on
\mathcal{I}_{\rm rem}
versus on
\mathcal{I}
.
Using the above, we can isolate these two sources of regret:

Lemma 5.7

(Regret Decomposition)

For any UDPM instance
\mathcal{I}
, we have
{\rm Regret}(X^{\rm LTE},\mathcal{I})\leq\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}.

Unsurprisingly,
\eta_{1}
is at most linear in
s
.

Lemma 5.8

(Bounding
\eta_{1}
)

It holds that
\eta_{1}=O(\lambda np_{1}s)
.

Proof 5.9

Proof Sketch.
The number of customers who made a purchase during the learning phase is
O(n\lambda s)
.
Therefore, the number of customers in
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\rm rem}
differ by
O(n\lambda s)
.
Next, we show that
\bf\widehat{t}
and
(1-s)\bf\widehat{t}
differ by
O(n\lambda s)
in their expected revenue.
In fact, in the
j
-th time interval of the earning phase earned, the revenues of these two policies differ by at most
|e^{-\lambda\widehat{t}_{j}}-e^{-(1-s)\lambda\widehat{t}_{j}}|=e^{-\lambda\widehat{t}_{j}}(e^{\lambda s\widehat{t}_{j}}-1)=O(\lambda s\widehat{t}_{j}).

\square

We next bound
\eta_{2}
. Note that
D_{1}
is an i.i.d. sum of
n_{1}
Bernoulli variables with mean
q(s)
, we can derive high-
Phys. Rev. B
bounds on
|n_{1}-\widehat{n}_{1}|
using concentration bounds.
A naive application of Hoeffding’s inequality implies that
\eta_{2}=O(\frac{\sqrt{n\log n}}{\lambda s})
, which leads to a subpar regret bound of
\tilde{O}(n^{3/4})
.
However, this bound can be substantially improved.
In fact, Hoeffding’s inequality neglects the fact that
q(s)=o(1)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
To utilize this, we apply Bernstein’s inequality and derive the following stronger bound:

Lemma 5.10

(Bounding
\eta_{2}
)

We have
\eta_{2}=O\left(p_{1}\sqrt{\frac{n\log n}{\lambda s}}\right)
.

Combining
Lemma

5.7
,
Lemma

5.8
and
Lemma

5.10
, we obtain

{\rm Regret}(X^{\rm LTE},{\cal I})\leq O\left(\lambda np_{1}s+p_{1}\sqrt{\frac{n\log n}{\lambda s}}\right).

Taking
s=n^{-1/3}/\lambda
, we immediately obtain the
\tilde{O}(n^{2/3})
regret bound.

5.4

Extending to
k>2

The analysis for general
k
is more involved, since we need to carefully analyze how the estimation errors accumulate.
Rather than bounding the estimation errors
\widehat{n}_{j}-n_{j}
individually, we prove a novel concentration inequality for the total estimation error
\sum_{j\in[k]}(\widehat{n}_{j}-n_{j})
.
The first challenge is dependence: The estimator
\widehat{n}_{j}
depends on
\widehat{n}_{1},\dots,\widehat{n}_{j-1}
.
Additionally, we will see that the process
Y_{j}=\sum_{i\leq j}(\widehat{n}_{i}-n_{i})
does not form a martingale, so standard tools such as Azuma-Hoeffding’s inequality do not apply either.
We address this by showing that the process admits a self-neutralizing property that allows us to apply the Cramér-Chernoff method to obtain a concentration bound.

As in the
k=2
setting, we start by decomposing the regret.
Let
\widehat{\cal I}
be the estimated instance given in
Algorithm

1
.
Analogously to the
k=2
setting, we define the remaining instance as
{\cal I}_{\rm rem}={\rm UDPM}(\lambda,{\cal P},(N_{j})_{j\in[k]})
where
N_{j}
is the (random) number of type-
j
customers remaining after the learning phase.
Then, the regret can be decomposed as
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}
where

\eta_{1}=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Rev}\left(\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}\right)\widehat{\bf t},\ {\cal I}_{\rm rem}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Rev}\left(\widehat{\bf t},\mathcal{I}\right)\right]\right|\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{2}=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Rev}\left(\widehat{\bf t},\mathcal{I}\right)\right]-\opt(\mathcal{I})\right|.

As in the
k=2
setting, it is straightforward to show that
\eta_{1}
is linear in the length of the learning phase.

Lemma 5.11

(Bounding
\eta_{1}
)

We have
\eta_{1}=O(\lambda np_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j})
.

The main
challenge
is to bound
\eta_{2}
.
We need to ensure that the estimation errors accumulate slowly.
We devote the remainder of this section to showing a bound on
\eta_{2}
that is sublinear in
both

n
and
k
.

5.4.1

Self-Neutralizing Process and Conditional Subgaussianity

Our analysis of
\eta_{2}
is based on two key properties of the error process.
Consider a stochastic process
{\bf\Delta}=(\Delta_{j})_{j\in[k]}
(which will be chosen as
\Delta_{j}=\widehat{n}_{j}-n_{j}
) adapted to the filtration
({\cal F}_{j})_{j\in[k]}
where
{\cal F}_{j}=\sigma(D_{1},\dots,D_{j-1})
.
The first property states that this process has a tendency to move towards the origin.

Definition 5.12

(
Self-Neutralizing Property)

The process
{\bf\Delta}
is self-neutralizing (SN) if for all
j\in[k]
we have

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{j}\mid{\cal F}_{j-1}]=-\sum_{i\leq j-1}\Delta_{i}.

To control the deviation, we also need to bound the moment generating function (mgf).

Definition 5.13

(
Conditional Sub-gaussianity)

A process
{\bf\Delta}
is conditionally sub-gaussian if there exist real numbers
(c_{j})_{j\in[k]}
such that for all
j\in[k]
and
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\Delta_{j}}\mid{\cal F}_{j-1}\right]\leq\exp\left(\gamma\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{j}\mid{\cal F}_{j-1}]+\frac{\gamma^{2}c_{j}^{2}}{2}\right).

(11)

In particular, if the process is also self-neutralizing, then
Eq.

11
is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{j}\Delta_{i}}\mid{\cal F}_{j-1}\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}c_{j}^{2}}{2}\right).

As we will explain in
Section

5.4.2
, the estimation error can be viewed as a weighted sum of a conditionally sub-gaussian SN process. This motivates the following concentration bound for such processes.

Theorem 5.14

(MGF Bound)

Let
{\bf\Delta}=(\Delta_{j})_{j\in[k]}
be a self-neutralizing and conditionally sub-gaussian process with constants
(c_{j})_{j\in[k]}
.
Then, for any
\gamma>0
and non-increasing sequence
{\bf r}=(r_{j})_{j\in[k]}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\langle{\bf r},{\bf\Delta}\rangle}\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(r_{j}c_{j})^{2}\right).

(12)

Remark 5.15

The above bound resembles the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality for subgaussian martingale difference sequences; see e.g., Theorem 2.19 of
Wainwright 2019
.
However, we reiterate that
\Delta_{j}
is not a super/sub-martingale, so the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality does not apply.
Instead, we prove the above by a careful induction in Appendix
E.1
.
We also emphasize that the monotonicity in
\bf r
is necessary, since our induction step considers a new non-increasing sequence
{\bf r}^{\prime}=(r_{j}^{\prime})
where
r_{j}^{\prime}=r_{j}-r_{k}\geq 0
.
\square

5.4.2

Bounding the Deviation of Estimation Error

We next use
Theorem

5.14
to control the estimation error in the expected revenue for a fixed non-adaptive policy.
To see this connection, we write the revenue function as the inner product of a non-increasing vector and a conditionally sub-gaussian SN process as follows.
Observe that for any UDPM instance
\mathcal{I}
and non-adaptive policy
{\bf t}
, we have

{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\mathcal{I})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}({\bf t})\cdot n_{j},

where
r_{j}({\bf t})
is the expected revenue from type-
j
customers, formally given by

r_{j}({\bf t})=\sum_{i=j}^{k}e^{-\lambda\sum_{\ell=j}^{i-1}t_{\ell}}\left(1-e^{\lambda t_{i}}\right)p_{i}.

Thus, we can write the estimation error as

{\rm Rev}\left({\bf t},\ \widehat{\mathcal{I}}\right)-{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\ \mathcal{I})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}({\bf t})(\widehat{n}_{j}-n_{j})=\langle{\bf r,\ \widehat{n}-n}\rangle.

(13)

which matches the exponent in
Eq.

12
.

This enables us to apply the mgf bound in
Theorem

5.14
on
Eq.

13
and hence control the estimation error of
{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\ \mathcal{I})
.
To this end, we verify that the process
\{\widehat{n}_{j}-n_{j}\}_{j\in[k]}
is self-neutralizing and conditional sub-gaussian with
c_{j}^{2}=2\sum_{i\leq j}n_{i}/q(s_{j})
; see
Proposition

E.1
and
Proposition

E.3
in Appendix
E.2
.
Moreover,
{\bf r}=(r_{j}({\bf t}))_{j\in[k]}
is non-increasing for any fixed
\bf t
.
Thus, by
Theorem

5.14
and Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\langle{\bf r,\ \widehat{n}-n}\rangle>\tau\right]\leq e^{-\gamma\tau}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\langle{\bf r,\ \widehat{n}-n}\rangle}\right]\leq e^{-\gamma\tau}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}^{2}c_{j}^{2}}\right).

(14)

Taking
\gamma=\frac{\tau}{2\sum_{j=1}c_{j}^{2}r_{j}^{2}}
and recalling that
c_{j}^{2}=2\sum_{i\leq j}n_{i}/q(s_{j})
, we have

\displaystyle\eqref{eqn:022824f}=\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}^{2}\sum_{i\leq j}\frac{n_{i}}{q(s_{j})}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{4r_{1}^{2}n\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{q(s_{j})}}\right),

where the inequality follows since
\sum_{i\leq j}n_{i}\leq n
.
By symmetry, we can obtain a similar upper bound on
\mathbb{P}\left[\langle{\bf r,\ \widehat{n}-n}\rangle<-\tau\right]
.
The above can be summarized as follows.

Proposition 5.16

(Concentration Bound for the Error Process)

Let
\bf t
be any non-adaptive policy and write
{\bf r}=(r_{j}({\bf t}))_{j\in[k]}
.
Then, for any
\tau>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|{\rm Rev}\left({\bf t},\ \widehat{\mathcal{I}}\right)-{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\ \mathcal{I})\right|>\tau\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\langle{\bf r,\ \widehat{n}-n}\rangle\right|>\tau\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{4r_{1}^{2}n\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{q(s_{j})}}\right).

Next, we explain how to use the above to bound the regret in the earning phase.

5.4.3

From Pointwise to Uniform Concentration

So far, we have bounded the estimation error for each non-adaptive policy individually (“pointwise”).
However, this is not sufficient to conclude the analysis.
To see this, let us recall the regret analysis of the LTE policy in the stream model (which is equivalent to MAB).
In the learning phase, we obtain an estimate
\hat{\mu}_{a}
of the mean reward
\mu_{a}
for each arm
a\in[k]
.
In the earning phase, by committing to the empirically optimal arm, we can bound the regret using
\max_{a\in[k]}|\mu_{a}-\widehat{\mu}_{a}|
, which is controlled by a standard combination of union bound and concentration bounds.

The above suggests that the key is to control is the maximum estimation error of a set of “arms” (policies, in our context).
Unfortunately, in our pool model, there are infinitely many “arms”.
One is tempted to discretize the policy space (which is a
(k-1)
-dimensional simplex), but this involves
m=e^{\Omega(\sqrt{k})}
terms, which leads to an extra
\log m=\Omega(\sqrt{k})
regret term.
Another idea is to extend the above analysis to random sequences of
r_{j}
’s that depends on
all

\widehat{n}_{j}
’s.
However,
Theorem

5.14
requires that
{\bf r}
be independent of the error sequence
{\bf\Delta}
.
We address this using the following structure of the policy space.

Lemma 5.17

(Basis of the Policy Space)

Denote by
(e_{j})_{j\in[k]}
the natural basis for
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
For each
j\in\{0\}\cup[k]
, define
\phi_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}e_{i}
.
Then, any non-increasing sequence
{\bf r}\in[0,1]^{k}
is a convex combination of
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{k}
.

Taking the union bound over
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{k}
, we immediately obtain a uniform concentration bound.

Proposition 5.18

(Uniform Concentration for the Estimation Error)

Let
{\cal P}
be a set of
k
prices and
\Pi(\cal P)
be the family of all non-adaptive policies for price set
\cal P
.
Then, for any
\tau>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf t}\in\Pi({\cal P})}\left|{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\ \widehat{\mathcal{I}})-{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\ \mathcal{I})\right|>\tau\right]\leq 2(k+1)\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{4p_{1}^{2}n\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{q(s_{j})})}\right).

(15)

5.4.4

Concluding the Proof

We combine all the above components to prove
Theorem

5.2
, the main regret bound.
Intuitively,
\eta_{2}=|\mathbb{E}[{\rm Rev}(\widehat{\bf t},\ \mathcal{I})]-\mathbb{E}[{\rm Rev}({\bf t}^{\star},\ \mathcal{I})]|
is not too large because (i) the functions
{\rm Rev}(\cdot,\mathcal{I})
and
{\rm Rev}(\cdot,\hat{\mathcal{I}})
are uniformly close due to
Proposition

5.18
, and (ii)
\widehat{\bf t}
and
{\bf t^{\star}}
maximize these two functions respectively.
We formalize this idea as follows.

Proposition 5.19

(Bounding
\eta_{2}
, the
k
-price setting)

For all
\tau>0
, we have

\eta_{2}\leq 4\left(\tau+p_{1}n\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf t}\in\Pi(\cal P)}\left|{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\widehat{\mathcal{I}})-{\rm Rev}({\bf t},\mathcal{I})\right|>\tau\right]\right).

In particular, if
s_{j}=s
for all
j\in[k]
and
\tau=p_{1}\sqrt{8kn\log(nk)/q(s)}
, then the right-hand side of
Eq.

15
becomes
O(n^{-2}k^{-1})
.
Therefore, by
Proposition

5.19
,

\eta_{2}=O(\tau+n\cdot n^{-2}k^{-1})=O\left(p_{1}\sqrt{\frac{kn}{q(s)}}\right).

Also, recall that
q(s)=O(\lambda s)
and from

Lemma

5.11
that
\eta_{1}=O(\lambda p_{1}kns)
, so we conclude that

{\rm Regret}\left(X^{\rm LTE},\mathcal{I}\right)=\widetilde{O}\left(\lambda p_{1}kns+p_{1}\sqrt{\frac{kn}{\lambda s}}\right).

In particular, for
s=\tilde{\Theta}(\lambda^{-1}(kn)^{-1/3})
, the above becomes
\tilde{O}\left(p_{1}(kn)^{2/3}\right)
.

\square

Experiments

We now numerically demonstrate the efficacy of our non-adaptive and adaptive policies.
Specifically, we investigate (i) the empirical performance of our policies against policies designed for the stream model, and (ii) the impact of model misspecification, i.e., the loss due to mistakenly making decisions based on the stream model when the actual dynamics follow a pool model.

6.1

Non-adaptive Setting

6.1.1

The Robust Stream Policy.

In
Section

4
we constructed a
1/k
-competitive non-adaptive policy
{\bf t}^{*}_{\cal P}
(see Theorem
4.8
) for any price set
{\cal P}
.
We will refer to this policy as the Robust Pool (RP) policy.
The same approach can also be used to find a robust policy for the stream model.

Suppose the seller believes that the true model is a stream model and has unlimited inventory.
In this setting, we can represent a policy as a distribution over
\cal P
.
Formally, we denote by
t_{i}\in[0,1]
the probability of choosing
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
in each round.
The expected revenue per round is
\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}t_{i}(\sum_{j:1\leq j\leq i}n_{j})
, and the optimal detail-dependent policy has expected revenue
\max_{i\in[k]}\{v_{i}\sum_{j:1\leq j\leq i}n_{j}\}
.
A robust stream (RS) policy aims to find the
t_{i}
’s that maximizes the competitive ratio, i.e.,

\min_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}\frac{\sum_{i}v_{i}t_{i}\left(\sum_{j\geq i}n_{j}\right)}{\max_{i}\{v_{i}\cdot\sum_{j\geq i}n_{j}\}},

subject to
\sum_{i\in[k]}t_{i}=1
and
t_{i}\geq 0
.
We derive a closed-form formula for the optimal stream policy, which we call the robust stream (RS) policy, in
Appendix

C
.
In the following simulation, we will use this RP policy as a benchmark.

Conclusion and Future Directions

We introduce a novel single-item revenue maximization problem involving a
pool
of unit-demand, non-strategic customers.
These customers interact repeatedly with a single seller, following independent Poisson processes, and purchase if the observed price is lower than their private valuation, after which they leave the market permanently.
We propose an algorithm that generates a non-adaptive, detail-free policy ensuring a
1/k
fraction of the expected revenue compared to any policy.
We also show that the policy is
(1+\ln\rho)
-competitive, where
\rho
is the ratio between the highest and lowest prices.
Furthermore, we present an adaptive LTE policy with s
\tilde{O}((kn)^{2/3})
regret based on a novel debiasing technique.
Additionally, we provide numerical simulations to demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed non-adaptive and adaptive policies.
We believe that this work paves the way for future research on pricing under the law of diminishing utility.
The following open questions may be of interest:

•

General Discount Functions.
We established the first results dynamic pricing for the UDPM, i.e.,
\psi\equiv 1
, which is the most basic special case of the pool model.
A natural question is, to what extent can we extend our results to more general discount functions
\psi(m)
?

•

Stronger Regret Guarantees.
In
Section

5
we gave a simple adaptive detail-free policy with a
\tilde{O}((kn)^{2/3})
regret against an optimal non-adaptive policy.
Is there a policy with improved regret (in either
k
or
n
)?

•

Regret Lower Bounds.

Section

5
gives upper bounds on the regret for our LTE policy.
Can we establish minimax lower bounds on the regret for detail-free adaptive policies?
Standard lower bound approaches for MAB do not directly apply to our setting.

•

Understanding Adaptivity.
Finally, we remark that this paper has used non-adaptive policies as a benchmark class of policies.
Naturally, one might ask if we can develop a better understanding of adaptive policies, even for the case of the UDPM?

Appendix C

Robust Non-adaptive Policy for the Stream Model

Theorem C.1

(Competitive Ratio, Stream Model)

For any
k\geq 1
and detail-free non-adaptive policy
X
,
there is a stream instance
\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
such that

\frac{{\rm Rev}(X,\mathcal{I})}{{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I})}\leq\frac{1}{k}.

Proof C.2

Proof.
For the stream model, we assume at each round, one customer arrives with the valuation randomly drawn from
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1}>\dots>p_{k}\}
with probability
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}=1
. We define a non-adaptive policy for the stream model is
X=\{t_{1},\dots t_{k}\}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}=1
where
t_{i}
denotes the probability the seller chooses price
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
in each round. Therefore, the competitive ratio of a non-adaptive policy
X
for the stream model is defined as

{\rm CR}(X,\mathcal{F}):=\inf_{\mathcal{I}\in\cal F}\frac{{\rm Rev}(X,\mathcal{I})}{{\rm OPT}(\mathcal{I})}=\inf_{\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\}\in\cal F}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}p_{i}\left(\sum_{j\leq i}q_{j}\right)}{\max_{i}p_{i}\left(\sum_{j\leq i}q_{j}\right)}.

Note
\sum_{j\leq i}q_{j}
is the probability that customers arrive with a valuation larger than the price
p_{i}
.

To show
\max_{X}{\rm CR}(X,\mathcal{F})=\frac{1}{k}
, we first show that for any
X
, the competitive ratio
{\rm CR}(X,\mathcal{F})
is minimized at
q_{i}=1
for some
i\in[k]
. If not, suppose the denominator
p_{i}\left(\sum_{j\leq i}q_{j}\right)
is maximized at
i^{*}
, and there exist
i<i^{*}
such that
q_{i}>0
.
We can construct a new instance
\{q^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
which will give us a smaller ratio.
Specifically, let
q^{\prime}_{i^{*}}=q_{i}^{*}+q_{i}
,
q^{\prime}_{i}=0
and
q^{\prime}_{j}=q_{j}
for all
j\in[k]\backslash\{i,i^{*}\}
.
Now we have
p_{i^{*}}\sum_{j=1}^{i^{*}}q^{\prime}_{j}=p_{i^{*}}\sum_{j=1}^{i^{*}}q_{j}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}p_{i}\left(\sum_{j\leq i}q^{\prime}_{j}\right)<\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}p_{i}\left(\sum_{j\leq i}q_{j}\right)
since customer’s valuation is more likely to be lower than before. Then, the ratio for instance
\{q_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{k}
will be smaller. Therefore, the optimal
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\}
satisfies
q_{i}=0
for any
i<i^{*}
.

Moreover, if there exists
i>i^{*}
such that
q_{i}>0
, let
q^{\prime}_{i^{*}}=q_{i}^{*}+q_{i}
,
q^{\prime}_{i}=0
and
q^{\prime}_{j}=q_{j}
for all
j\in[k]\backslash\{i,i^{*}\}
. Then, the competitive ratio is

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}\right)}{p_{i^{*}}\left(q_{i^{*}}+q_{i}\right)}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right)+\sum_{j=i^{*}}^{i-1}t_{j}p_{j}q_{i}}{p_{i^{*}}\left(q_{i^{*}}+q_{i}\right)},

where
\sum_{j=i^{*}}^{i-1}t_{j}p_{j}q_{j}
is the extra revenue since we set
q^{\prime}_{i^{*}}=q_{i^{*}}+q_{i}
. Now to show the competitive ratio will decrease, i.e,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right)+\sum_{j=i^{*}}^{i-1}t_{j}p_{j}q_{i}}{p_{i^{*}}\left(q_{i^{*}}+q_{i}\right)}\leq\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right)}{p_{i^{*}}q_{i^{*}}},

we only need to show that

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right)+\sum_{j=i^{*}}^{i-1}t_{j}p_{j}q_{i}\right)\left(p_{i^{*}}q_{i^{*}}\right)\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right)\right)p_{i^{*}}\left(q_{i^{*}}+q_{i}\right).

Equivalently, we only need to show

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=i^{*}}^{i-1}t_{j}p_{j}q_{i}\right)q_{i^{*}}\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right)\right)q_{i},

where the equivalence follows from canceling the common term
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right)\left(p_{i^{*}}q_{i^{*}}\right)
in the sum.
We note that
q_{i*}\leq\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}
for any
j\geq i^{*}
,
the inequality above is always true since

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=i^{*}}^{i-1}t_{j}p_{j}\right)q_{i^{*}}\leq\left(\sum_{j=i^{*}}^{i-1}t_{j}p_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}p_{j}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\leq j}q_{j^{\prime}}\right).

Therefore, we conclude that the inner min is always obtained at some
q_{i^{*}}=1
.

Since the inner min is always obtained at some
q_{i^{*}}=1
, the maximization of
{\rm CR}(X,\mathcal{F})
will be

\displaystyle\max_{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}}\min_{i\in[k]}\quad\left\{\frac{\sum_{j\in[k]:j\geq i}t_{j}p_{j}}{p_{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\,\quad\sum_{j}t_{j}=1,\quad t_{j}\geq 0\quad\forall j\in[k].

which is equivalent to
Section

B.5.2
(P2).
By the result of
Section

B.5.2
(P2), we know the
{\rm CR}(X,\mathcal{F})
is at most
1/k
. Further, the ratio
1/k
is tight, when
X
is the optimal solution of
Section

B.5.2
(P2), the competitive ratio is exactly
1/k
.

\square
