Oscillation of the remainder term in the prime number theorem of Beurling, ”caused by a given ζ \zeta -zero”
Szilárd Gy. Révész

Abstract
Continuing previous studies of the Beurling zeta function, here we prove two results, generalizing long existing knowledge regarding the classical case of the Riemann zeta function and some of its generalizations. First, we address the question of Littlewood, who asked for explicit oscillation results provided a zeta-zero is known. We prove that given a zero ρ 0 \rho_{0} of the Beurling zeta function ζ 𝒫 \zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}} for a given number system generated by the primes 𝒫 {\mathcal{P}} , the corresponding error term Δ ⁡ ( x ) := ψ 𝒫 ​ ( x ) − x \Delta(x):=\psi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x)-x , where ψ 𝒫 ​ ( x ) \psi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x) is the von Mangoldt summatory function shows oscillation in any large enough interval, as large as π / 2 − ε | ρ 0 | ​ x ℜ ⁡ ρ 0 \frac{\pi/2-{\varepsilon}}{|\rho_{0}|}x^{\Re\rho_{0}} . The somewhat mysterious appearance of the constant π / 2 \pi/2 is explained in the study. Finally, we prove as the next main result of the paper the following: given ε > 0 {\varepsilon}>0 , there exists a Beurling number system with primes 𝒫 {\mathcal{P}} , such that | Δ ⁡ ( x ) | ≤ π / 2 + ε | ρ 0 | ​ x ℜ ⁡ ρ 0 |\Delta(x)|\leq\frac{\pi/2+{\varepsilon}}{|\rho_{0}|}x^{\Re\rho_{0}} . In this second part a nontrivial construction of a low norm sine polynomial is coupled by the application of the wonderful recent prime random approximation result of Broucke and Vindas, who sharpened the breakthrough probabilistic construction due to Diamond, Montgomery and Vorhauer.

In

we used two additional assumptions to prove a density theorem on the zeroes of the Beurling zeta function. One is that the norm would actually map to the natural integers.

Definition 2

(Condition B)
.

We say that

Condition B
is satisfied, if
|\cdot|:{\mathcal{G}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
, that is,
the norm
|g|
of any element

is a natural number.

As is natural, we will write

arithmetical function on

. The next condition is a kind of

Definition 3

(Condition G)
.

We say that

is satisfied, if with a certain

we have
for the function

F_{p}(X):=\frac{1}{X}\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}};|g|\leq X}G(|g|)^{p}=\frac{1}{X}\sum_{\nu\in|{\mathcal{G}}|;\nu\leq X}G(\nu)^{1+p}=\frac{1}{X}\int_{1}^{X}G^{p}(x)d{\mathcal{N}}(x)

the property that

(21)

that is, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0

.

Note that in case
\log G(\nu)=o(\log\nu)
, i.e. when for all

{\varepsilon}>0
we have
G(\nu)=O(\nu^{{\varepsilon}})
, then Condition G is
automatically satisfied. Even this stronger

order
estimate is proved for many important cases, see e.g. 2.4. Theorem
and 2.5. Corollary of
[
32
]
.

There are many natural examples of the above condition. For a discussion see the original book of Knopfmacher or Section 3.1 of
[
50
]
. The main result of
[
50
]
was the following Carlson-type density theorem, proved following the methods in
[
40
]
and
[
42
]
.

.

{\mathcal{G}}

6
General remarks on the sharpness of Theorem
2
and a special sine polynomial

In the following our goal will be to show–by giving appropriate examples of Beurling prime number and integer systems–that Theorem
5
is optimal, i.e, there exist systems which satisfy all assumptions, yet an oscillation of the size
(\pi/2+{\varepsilon})x^{\beta_{0}}/|\rho_{0}|
fails. In other words, we seek systems where
|\Delta_{{\mathcal{G}}}(x)|\leq(\pi/2+{\varepsilon})x^{\beta_{0}}/|\rho_{0}|~(x\geq x_{0})
with a certain
\zeta
-zero
\rho_{0}
of
\zeta
.

It is easy to see that such a Beurling zeta function must have
\beta_{0}=\theta_{0}
where

\theta_{0}:=\max(\theta,\sup\{\Re\rho~:~\zeta(\rho)=0\}).

Indeed, if otherwise then with any other zero
\rho_{1}=\beta_{1}+\gamma_{1}
with
\beta_{1}>\beta_{0}
already Theorem
5
, when applied to this new zero, provides essentially larger oscillation (of the order of
x^{\beta_{1}}
). In particular, it follows that
\theta_{0}<1
, as it is well-known that the line
\Re s=1
does not contain a zero of the Beurling zeta function under much weaker hypothesis than Axiom A.

Moreover, the only reasonable choice is
\gamma_{0}=\min\{\gamma>0~:~\zeta(\beta_{0}+i\gamma)=0\}
: for picking other zeroes for
\rho_{0}
would simply decrease the constant
(\pi/2+{\varepsilon})/|\rho_{0}|
, making our task more difficult, and, in view of Theorem
5
, even impossible.

Given that we are talking about a Beurling zeta function, arising from a number system, which belongs to a real valued
{\mathcal{N}}(x)
, the Beurling zeta function is also real valued for real variables
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence by the reflection principle together with any zeta-zero
\rho
also the conjugate zero
\overline{\rho}
occurs. Now let us take a look at the terms, ”caused by a given zeta zero
\rho
”, as they occur in the Riemann-von Mangoldt type formula of Proposition
1
. They provide

\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho}+\frac{x^{\overline{\rho}}}{\overline{\rho}}=2x^{\beta}\frac{\cos(\gamma\log x-\alpha)}{|\rho|}\qquad\left(\alpha:=\arg(\rho)=\arctan(\gamma/\beta)\right),

or, if we have a series
\rho_{k}
of known zeroes with
\beta_{k}=\beta_{0}(=\theta)
, then

\sum_{k}\frac{x^{\rho_{k}}}{\rho_{k}}+\frac{x^{\overline{\rho_{k}}}}{\overline{\rho_{k}}}=2x^{\beta}\sum_{k}\frac{\cos(\gamma_{k}\log x-\alpha_{k})}{|\rho_{k}|}\qquad\left(\alpha_{k}:=\arg(\rho_{k})=\arctan(\gamma_{k}/\beta)\right).

To handle these terms easier, let us assume that
\gamma_{0}
is chosen very large; then
\alpha_{k}\approx\pi/2
and
|\rho_{k}|\approx\gamma_{k}
, so that the above sum is approximately

-2x^{\beta}\sum_{k}\frac{\sin(\gamma_{k}\log x)}{\gamma_{k}}.

In principle, the sum here can contain infinitely many elements as well, but then the delicate issue of convergence arises. In any case, let us see what we may expect from such a sum. The well-known Fourier series

\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sign}\,(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2\frac{\sin((2k+1)y)}{2k+1}

(47)

suggests that we should strive for getting
\Delta(x)\approx\frac{\pi}{2}x^{\beta_{0}}{\rm sign}\,(\gamma_{0}\log x)/\gamma_{0}
, that is
\psi(x)\approx x+\frac{\pi}{2}x^{\beta_{0}}{\rm sign}\,(\gamma_{0}\log x)/\gamma_{0}
.

There is only one obstacle here, but a serious one. As said, the two conjugate terms belonging to
\rho
and its conjugate together rise to a size of
2x^{\beta_{0}}/|\rho_{0}|
time to time. Therefore, to uniformly push down the oscillation, caused by them to only
(\pi/2)x^{\beta_{0}}/|\rho_{0}|
, we heavily rely on the interference of other terms of similar size. That happens in the slowly and non-uniformly convergent series of
(\pi/2){\rm sign}\,(y)
, but to construct a
\Delta(x)
of that same terms would require a sequence of
\zeta
-zeroes at
\rho_{k}=\beta_{0}+(2k+1)\gamma_{0}
, i.e. a constant times
T
zeroes on the
\Re s=\theta_{0}=\beta_{0}
line. However, that is impossible for
\beta_{0}>\theta+\frac{5}{6}(1-\theta)
, as was recently demonstrated–upon the additional assumptions of Conditions B and G–by the new density result in Theorem
4
. On such a line, and in general in a rectangular domain
[\alpha,2]\times[-iT,iT]
with any
\alpha>\theta+\frac{5}{6}(1-\theta)
, only
o(T)
zeros can occur. Then, similarly to the analysis in
[
47
]
, it even follows that we cannot get better uniform bounds for a properly rare sequence of zeros (which at least meet the criteria, posed by the density theorem), then for simply assuming only finitely many zeros. However, from (
47
) there is not so easy to come to a finite sum of the same low maximum norm (so the same level of interference extinguishing a
(1-\pi/4)
portion of the magnitude of the first term). This is known as Gibbs phenomenon or overshooting convergence: see, e.g., page 61 of
[
72
]
. According to this phenomenon, partial sums
S_{n}(\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sign}\,)
have definitely larger maximum norm than
\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sign}\,
itself. More precisely,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|S_{n}(\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sign}\,)\|=\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin x/x)dx\approx 1.8519\ldots>\pi/2=\|\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sign}\,\|
, although already less than 2, the maximal size of the first summand.

This is a point when construction of a finite sum
S
of terms from the series (
47
), with about the same low maximum norm
\pi/2+{\varepsilon}
as the total sum itself, becomes of some challenge. That was first solved in §6 of
[
47
]
by a probabilistic construction. Later Régis de la Bretèche and Gérald Tenenbaum furnished a deterministic construction, too, through so-called ”entieres friables”, see in particular the explanation following Théorème 2.2 of
[
4
]
. Both the original probabilistic construction and the later arithmetical construction (so-called P-summability) applies to a wide class of Fourier series, borrowing some importance to the otherwise seemingly rather special question here, see
[
48
]
and
[
5
]
.

The existence of such a special sine polynomial will be one starting point for our construction, so that we formulate it here as a lemma.

Lemma 9
.

For any
{\varepsilon}>0
there exists a natural number
N
and a finite sequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset{\mathbb{N}}
, such that with
n_{0}:=0
the sine polynomial
S(y):=2\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\sin((2n_{k}+1)y)}{2n_{k}+1}
has maximum norm
\|S\|\leq\pi/2+{\varepsilon}
.

7
A construction of a Beurling number system with given zeroes of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}

The above suggests that we will need a system of Beurling primes
{\mathcal{P}}
such that the corresponding Beurling zeta function will have a special configuration of zeroes while satisfy certain analytic and order estimate conditions as well. In this section we will present such a construction in a greater generality with possible further applications in mind.

So we set to the following task. Let
r
be a real number parameter satisfying
1/2\leq r<1
. Also, let a finite multiset
{\mathcal{S}}
of would-be zeta-zeroes be given. We assume that each element
\rho=\beta+i\gamma\in{\mathcal{S}}
satisfies
\Re\rho=\beta\in(r,1)
, always, and is listed according to multiplicity, moreover,
{\mathcal{S}}
is symmetric with respect to the real axis (so that
\rho
is listed with the same multiplicity as
\overline{\rho}
, the condition being self-evident for real zeroes with
\gamma=0
). Then, the task is to construct a Beurling system of primes
{\mathcal{P}}
and corresponding set of Beurling generalized integers
{\mathcal{N}}
subject to Axiom A, and such that
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
has zeta-zeroes in the halfplane
\Re s>r
–or even in
\Re s>1/2
–exactly as prescribed by
{\mathcal{S}}
. Moreover, we want, roughly speaking, that the system satisfy Axiom A with ”the best value” of
\theta
to be
r
. More precisely,
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
be analytic in
\Re s>1/2
except for a simple pole at
s=1
, and possibly another one at
s=r
if
r
was exceeding
1/2
; and Axiom A is to be satisfied with
\theta
as either the given value
r>1/2
(and then, according to the assumed singularity at
s=r
, with no smaller value than
r
), if
r
exceeded
1/2
, or with all
1/2+{\varepsilon}
with any
{\varepsilon}>0
, if
r=1/2
.

In the course of our work we will establish a number of other useful properties, too, and at the end of the section we will summarize our findings in Theorem
8
. That will be combined with Section
6
in the next section to prove Theorem
3
, too.

Our Beurling number system
{\mathcal{N}}
will arise as a result of a prime selection procedure, designed to approximate a pre-set distribution function as well as possible. This part is far from trivial, and the method of doing so involves probabilistic considerations. The approach was introduced into the study of Beurling number systems by the breakthrough work of Diamond, Montgomery and Vorhauer
[
15
]
, and then refined by Zhang
[
70
]
as follows.

Theorem 6

(Diamond-Montgomery-Vorhauer-Zhang)
.

Let
f:(1,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a non-negative, locally integrable function with
\int_{1}^{\infty}f(u)du=\infty
, and satisfying the ”pointwise Chebyshev bound”
f(x)\ll 1/\log x
. Write
F(x):=\int_{1}^{x}f(y)dy
.

Then there exists a set of generalized primes
{\mathcal{P}}=\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that for all
x\geq 1
it holds
|\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x)-F(x)|\leq\sqrt{x}
, and, moreover, we have for all
x\geq 1
and all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
the estimate

\left|\sum_{p_{j}\leq x}p_{j}^{-it}-\int_{1}^{x}u^{-it}f(u)du\right|\ll\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{\frac{x\log(|t|+1)}{\log(x+1)}}.

(48)

Recently a nice sharpening of the method appeared in
[
7
]
. This latter result–Theorem 1.2 in
[
7
]
–will not be indispensable for us, but we will take some slight advantage of it, too.

Theorem 7

(Broucke-Vindas)
.

Let
F
be a non-decreasing right-continuous function tending to
\infty
, with
F(1)=0
and satisfying the ”global Chebyshev bound”
F(x)\ll x/\log x
.

Then there exists a set of generalized primes
{\mathcal{P}}=\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that for all
x\geq 1
it holds
|\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x)-F(x)|\leq 2
, and, moreover, we have for all
x\geq 1
and all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
the estimate

\left|\sum_{p_{j}\leq x}p_{j}^{-it}-\int_{1}^{x}u^{-it}dF(u)\right|\ll\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{\frac{x\log(|t|+1)}{\log(x+1)}}.

(49)

We will exploit the full strength of the above marvelous results. Moreover, our proof of Theorem
8
will draw much from the proof of Theorem 3.1 of
[
7
]
.

Denote
B:=\max\{\beta=\Re\rho~:~\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
and
N:=\#{\mathcal{S}}
(counted according to multiplicity). Take a further constant
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
and consider the function

f_{0}(x):=x+\frac{1}{r}x^{r}+2M\sqrt{x}-\sum_{\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho}\qquad(x\geq 1),

(50)

where here and everywhere else we will mean summation–or taking product–over
{\mathcal{S}}
according to multiplicity. Direct differentiation yields

f_{0}^{\prime}(x)=1+x^{r-1}+Mx^{-1/2}-\sum_{\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}}x^{\rho-1}\geq 1+Mx^{-1/2}-Nx^{B-1}.

The right hand side will be nonnegative if the constant
M
has been chosen large enough. A little calculus gives that
M\geq M_{0}:=2N^{\frac{1}{2(1-B)}}
suffices. It is clear, too, that
f_{0}(x)=O(x)
, and
f_{0}(x)\sim x
as
x\to\infty
. Therefore, the function

F(x):=\int_{1}^{x}df(y)\quad\textrm{where}\quad f^{\prime}(y):=\frac{1-1/y}{\log y}f^{\prime}_{0}(y),\quad\textrm{i.e.}\quad df(y):=\frac{1-1/y}{\log y}df_{0}(y)

(51)

is a well-defined, continuously differentiable, nondecreasing function, and it admits a Chebyshev bound
F(x)\ll x/\log x
, too, whence it is subject to all the requirements of Theorem
7
of Broucke and Vindas above. As a result, there exists a prime number system
{\mathcal{P}}
with
|\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x)-F(x)|\leq 2
and satisfying (
49
), too. Referring to Theorem
6
only would give here
|\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x)-F(x)|\ll\sqrt{x}
, still well sufficient for us, as will be seen below.

For this prime number system
{\mathcal{P}}
the corresponding
\vartheta
function is defined as
\vartheta(x):=\int_{1}^{x}\log yd\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(y)
. Let us see that it will satisfy
\vartheta(x)=f_{0}(x)+O(\log x)
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\vartheta(x)

\displaystyle:=\left[\log y\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(y)\right]_{1}^{x}-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(y)}{y}dy=\log x~\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x)-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{F(y)}{y}dy+O(\log x)

\displaystyle=\log x(F(x)+O(1))-\left\{\int_{1}^{x}\left(\int_{1}^{y}\frac{1-1/z}{\log z}df_{0}(z)\right)\frac{dy}{y}\right\}+O(\log x)

\displaystyle=F(x)\log x-\left\{\int_{1}^{x}\left(\int_{z}^{x}\frac{dy}{y}\right)\frac{1-1/z}{\log z}df_{0}(z)\right\}+O(\log x)

\displaystyle=F(x)\log x-\left\{\int_{1}^{x}(\log x-\log z)\frac{1-1/z}{\log z}df_{0}(z)\right\}+O(\log x)

\displaystyle=F(x)\log x-\left\{\log xF(x)-f_{0}(x)+\int_{1}^{x}\frac{df_{0}(z)}{z}\right\}+O(\log x)=f_{0}(x)+O(\log x).

As a direct consequence, for the respective von Mangoldt summatory function
\psi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{[\log x]}\vartheta(x^{1/n})
we necessarily have
\psi_{{\mathcal{P}}}(x)=f_{0}(x)+g(x)
with
g(x)=O(\theta(\sqrt{x}))=O(\sqrt{x})
. Using Theorem
6
, the same argument would furnish the weaker
\vartheta(x)=x+O(\sqrt{x}\log x)
only–due to the change of
O(1)
to
O(\sqrt{x})
in the second line–but afterwards for
\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
we are to get the only slightly weaker result
g(x)=O(\sqrt{x}\log x)
. However, for this the dominant error comes from the error of the applied theorem, whence cannot be ”tricked out” by a modification of the prescribed distribution for
\vartheta
, i.e., for
\pi_{\mathcal{P}}
, while using the strong
O(1)
result of Broucke and Vindas, we can even achieve
g(x)=O(x^{\varepsilon})
for
\psi_{\mathcal{P}}
. For more about this exploitation of the full strength of Theorem
7
see Remark
1
below.

Next, we are to compute the respective Beurling zeta function
\zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}}
from the Mellin transform of
\psi_{{\mathcal{P}}}
. Recall that in our terminology the Mellin transform is defined as
{\mathcal{M}}(\phi)(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}d\phi(x)=-\phi(1)-\int_{1}^{\infty}\phi(x)dx^{-s}
, and the basic connection between the Beurling zeta function
\zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}}
and the von Mangoldt summatory function
\psi_{\mathcal{P}}
is that
{\mathcal{M}}(\psi_{{\mathcal{P}}})(s)=-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}{\zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(s)
.

Denote the power function
x\to x^{z}
as
p_{z}
. Its Mellin transform is
{\mathcal{M}}(p_{z})(s)=\frac{z}{s-z}
; whence using
{\mathcal{M}}(\psi_{\mathcal{P}})={\mathcal{M}}(p_{1})+\frac{1}{r}{\mathcal{M}}(p_{r})+2M{\mathcal{M}}(p_{1/2})-\sum_{\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{1}{\rho}{\mathcal{M}}(p_{\rho})+{\mathcal{M}}(g)
we are led to

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}{\zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{s-1}-\frac{1}{s-r}-\frac{M}{(s-1/2)}+\sum_{\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{1}{s-\rho}-G(s),

(52)

where
G(s)
is the Mellin transform of
g
, and as such, is analytic for
\Re s>1/2
.

Consider the product

Q(s):=\frac{1}{s-1}\frac{1}{s-r}\frac{1}{(s-1/2)^{M}}\prod_{\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left({s}-{\rho}\right).

(53)

It is clear that
\frac{\zeta^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}{\zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(s)=\frac{Q^{\prime}}{Q}(s)-G(s)
. Therefore

\zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}}(s)=c~Q(s)H(s)\qquad\textrm{with}\quad H(s):=e^{-\int_{1}^{s}G(z)dz}\quad\textrm{and}\quad c\neq 0\quad\textrm{a constant}.

(54)

By construction,
H(s)
is analytic and nonvanishing for
\Re s>1/2
and so is
\zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}}
except for a simple pole at
s=1
and possibly another simple pole at
s=r
(if
r
exceeded
1/2
), and zeroes
\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}
with exactly the multiplicity given in
{\mathcal{S}}
. Let us underline that
\zeta_{{\mathcal{P}}}
vanishes nowhere else in the halfplane
\Re s>1/2
of meromorphic continuation.

Next, we are to show that the integer counting function
{\mathcal{N}}(x)
, generated by our set
{\mathcal{P}}
of primes, will satisfy Axiom A. To succeed, we will need the full strength of (
48
) or (
49
), which we formulate with the use of the function

J(x,t):=\sum_{p_{j}\leq x}p_{j}^{-it}-\int_{1}^{x}y^{-it}dF(y)=\int_{1}^{x}y^{-it}d(\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(y)-F(y)).

With this, the above theorems say that we have

|J(x,t)|\ll\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{x\frac{\log(|t|+1)}{\log(x+1)}}.

(55)

So far the analytic characteristics of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
were found, but we also need good order estimates. Note that for
\Re s\geq 3/2
we definitely have
|\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)|\leq\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(3/2)<\infty
, whence the order of magnitude of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
is under some control. However, to proceed we need a different, more precise control on the size of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
, valid also in the critical strip. Equivalently, we estimate

\displaystyle\log\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}\right)=\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}d\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)={\mathcal{M}}(\Pi_{\mathcal{P}})(s),

(56)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
is the Riemann modified prime counting function–coming to picture in view of the Euler product formula (

0\qquad&\textrm{if}\quad|t|>T,\end{cases}

that is, assuming RH for
|t|\leq T
, and nothing for
|t|>T
. Although very special as for
\eta
, it still has importance, because numerical information on the validity of RH over a finite range already yield effective error estimates for PNT. Even if Johnston presents his work for the Riemann zeta only, the proofs are general and can as well be applied for the Beurling case. Similarly, an explicit error estimate of
\Delta(x)
can always be obtained from knowledge of a de la Vallée Poussin-type zero-free region (
9
), which, in turn, can be obtained by century old methods of de la Vallée Poussin and Landau even in the Beurling case–unlike more sophisticated results for zero-free regions, which do not extend to the Beurling setup. Our point is the precise connection of constants: if (
9
) is known with a concrete
c
, then we also want a concrete constant in (
10
). (E.g. in
[
36
]
the constants obtained for the Riemann case are
1/5.573412
and
1/\sqrt{6.315}
.) Our goal is to extend such special results to general ones, which tell, in the generality of the Beurling setup and in an essentially optimal way in each direction, how the information on zero-free regions is connected to error bounds for PNT.
