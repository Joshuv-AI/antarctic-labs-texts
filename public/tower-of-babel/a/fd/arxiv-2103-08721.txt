A Central Limit Theorem for Differentially Private Query Answering
Jinshuo Dong, Weijie J. Su, Linjun Zhang

Abstract
Perhaps the single most important use case for differential privacy is to privately answer numerical queries, which is usually achieved by adding noise to the answer vector. The central question, therefore, is to understand which noise distribution optimizes the privacy-accuracy trade-off, especially when the dimension of the answer vector is high. Accordingly, extensive literature has been dedicated to the question and the upper and lower bounds have been matched up to constant factors [ BUV18 , SU17 ] . In this paper, we take a novel approach to address this important optimality question. We first demonstrate an intriguing central limit theorem phenomenon in the high-dimensional regime. More precisely, we prove that a mechanism is approximately Gaussian Differentially Private [ DRS21 ] if the added noise satisfies certain conditions. In particular, densities proportional to e − ‖ x ‖ p α \mathrm{e}^{-\|x\|_{p}^{\alpha}} , where ‖ x ‖ p \|x\|_{p} is the standard ℓ p \ell_{p} -norm, satisfies the conditions. Taking this perspective, we make use of the Cramer–Rao inequality and show an “uncertainty principle”-style result: the product of the privacy parameter and the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -loss of the mechanism is lower bounded by the dimension. Furthermore, the Gaussian mechanism achieves the constant-sharp optimal privacy-accuracy trade-off among all such noises. Our findings are corroborated by numerical experiments.

GDP and the ROC Functions

The decision theoretic interpretation of DP was first proposed in

Suppose each individual’s sensitive information is an element in the abstract set

Let a randomized algorithm

ROC function of the Gaussian mechanism

For
\mu\geqslant 0

G_{\mu}:=T[\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)]

\displaystyle T[M(D),M(D^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=T[N(f(D),\mu^{-2}),N(f(D^{\prime}),\mu^{-2})]

\displaystyle=T[N(0,1),N(\mu,1)]=G_{\mu}.

(GDP)

. This inequality says

offers more privacy than the corresponding Gaussian mechanism.
If it holds with equality, i.e.,

\inf_{D,D^{\prime}}T[M(D),M(D^{\prime})]=G_{\mu},

(2)

then it means the mechanism

Consider an

5
Conclusions and Future Work

In this work, we study constant-sharp optimality of noise-addition algorithms for high-dimensional query answering with differential privacy. We demonstrate that the ROC function offers good insight in the design of such algorithms when the dimension
n=1
. However, when
n
is large, the CLT shows that a large class of noise-addition mechanisms behaves like a Gaussian mechanism through the lens of the ROC function. On the one hand, this suggests that
(\varepsilon,\delta)
privacy budget cannot be fully utilized, while on the other hand, if we adopt a GDP budget, then an “uncertainty principle” style of result yields an elegant characterization of the precise privacy-accuracy trade-off, and justifies the constant-sharp optimality of the Gaussian mechanism. We believe the insights offer a novel perspective to the long-lived privacy-accuracy trade-off question.

Various extensions are possible. An immediate one is to extend the CLT to a broader class of noise distributions, such as log-concave distributions as specified in Conjecture
3.4
. This may require significant machinary from convex geometry. For non-log-concave noise,
Lemma

3.5
suggests that a corresponding log-concave noise with no less privacy and accuracy may exist. For algorithms beyond noise-addition,
[
BDKT12
]
shows that they can be reduced to a noise-addition mechanism with better accuracy and slightly worse privacy.

Another exciting question is to search for distributions such that (
8
) is tight, where
[
DK20
,
GKM20
]
can serve as good hints. Since
[
DK20
]
uses bounded noise, a CLT compatible with bounded log-concave noise is also desirable.

\displaystyle=\tfrac{n}{\alpha}\cdot\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{\alpha})\cdot\mathrm{vol}(K_{\varphi})

where

\displaystyle K_{\varphi}=\{(r,\theta):r\leqslant\varphi(\theta)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\}=\{(r,\theta):r^{\alpha}\varphi(\theta)\leqslant 1\}=\{(r,\theta):\varphi(r\theta)\leqslant 1\}=\{x:\varphi(x)\leqslant 1\}.

Noticing
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
, we have

Z=\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{\alpha}+1)\cdot\mathrm{vol}(K_{\varphi})

∎

Lemma C.5
.

Let

. Let

has uniform distribution over

independently from

has density

Proof of
Lemma

C.5
.

We use a more principled way: assume
r
has density
p(r)
over
(0,+\infty)
and
rV
has density
\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\varphi}
, find
p(r)
. Let
B
be a small ball.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(rV\in x+B)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(V\in\frac{x+B}{r})\cdot p(r)\,\mathrm{d}r

\mathbb{P}(V\in\frac{x+B}{r})=\begin{cases}0,&if $\frac{x}{r}\notin K_{\varphi}$\\
\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\frac{B}{r})}{\mathrm{vol}(K_{\varphi})},&if $\frac{x}{r}\in K_{\varphi}$.\end{cases}

\frac{x}{r}\in K_{\varphi}\Leftrightarrow\varphi(\frac{x}{r})\leqslant 1\Leftrightarrow\varphi(x)\leqslant r^{\alpha}\Leftrightarrow r\geqslant\varphi(x)^{1/\alpha}
.

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(V\in\frac{x+B}{r})\cdot p(r)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{\varphi(x)^{1/\alpha}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\frac{B}{r})}{\mathrm{vol}(K_{\varphi})}\cdot p(r)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vol}(B)\cdot\int_{\varphi(x)^{1/\alpha}}^{\infty}\frac{p(r)r^{-n}}{\mathrm{vol}(K_{\varphi})}\,\mathrm{d}r.

at

is

\displaystyle\int_{\varphi(x)^{1/\alpha}}^{\infty}p(r)r^{-n}\,\mathrm{d}r

Lemma C.8
.

If
\varphi
is invariant under the action of
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and cyclic group of size
n
, i.e.

1.

\varphi(\pm x_{1},\pm x_{2},\ldots,\pm x_{n})=\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

2.

\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n})=\varphi(x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n},x_{1})

then
\mathcal{I}_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{2}^{2}\cdot I
.

Proof of
Lemma

C.8
.

First we use the symmetry to show
\mathcal{I}_{\varphi}=cI
for some
c
.

\displaystyle\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\varphi(-x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{1}\varphi(-x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}\varphi(-x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

This shows
\partial_{1}\varphi\cdot\partial_{2}\varphi
is an odd function of
x_{1}
. On the other hand, we know the density
\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi}
is an even function of
x_{1}
. So we conclude that
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{1}\varphi\cdot\partial_{2}\varphi]=0
. Similarly, we can show that for any
i\neq j,\mathbb{E}[\partial_{i}\varphi\cdot\partial_{j}\varphi]=0
. This shows that
\mathcal{I}_{\varphi}
is a diagonal matrix.

By cyclic symmetry, we have

\displaystyle\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n})

\displaystyle=\varphi(x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n},x_{1})

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n})

\displaystyle=\partial_{n}\varphi(x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n},x_{1})

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n})}

\displaystyle=\partial_{n}\varphi(x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n},x_{1})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n})}

\displaystyle=\partial_{n}\varphi(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n-1},y_{n})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi(y_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1})}

\displaystyle=\partial_{n}\varphi(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n-1},y_{n})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})}

This shows
\mathbb{E}\partial_{1}\varphi^{2}=\cdots=\mathbb{E}\partial_{n}\varphi^{2}
. Hence
\mathcal{I}_{\varphi}=cI
for some
c
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathcal{I}_{\varphi}=\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbb{E}[\nabla\varphi\nabla\varphi^{T}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}\,\nabla\varphi\nabla\varphi^{T}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}\,\nabla\varphi^{T}\nabla\varphi]=\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{2}^{2}.

On the other hand,
\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathcal{I}_{\varphi}=\mathrm{Tr}\,cI=cn
, so
c=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{2}^{2}
.
∎

Since
X
is symmetric, the above expression implies the
Condition D3
, that is,
\|\nabla\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(X)\|_{2}\sim\sqrt{n}
and therefore
v^{\top}\nabla\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(X)\to N(0,1)
.

To prove
Condition D1
, since
\frac{1}{2}vI_{\varphi}v=\frac{1}{2}
, it suffices to show when
\|v\|_{2}=1,\alpha=1
,

\|X+v\|_{1}-\|X\|_{1}\to N(\frac{1}{2},1).

The case where
\alpha>1
can be reduced to this setting by using the following technique. Let us write

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}t^{1/\alpha}\cdot r\cdot U,

where
t\sim\Gamma(\frac{n}{\alpha}+1,1)
,
U
has uniform distribution over
\partial K_{\varphi}=\{x:\|x\|_{p}=n^{1-1/\alpha}\}
,
r
has density
nx^{n-1}
over
[0,1]
, and
U,r,t
are independent.

\tilde{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{t}\cdot r\cdot\tilde{U},

where
\tilde{t}\sim\Gamma({n}+1,1)
and
\tilde{U}=n^{1/\alpha-1}U
.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(|X_{i}+v_{i}|-|X_{i}|)\sim

\displaystyle t^{1/\alpha}\cdot r\cdot\sum_{i}{(|U_{i}+(\frac{n}{\alpha})^{-1/\alpha}v_{i}|-|U_{i}|)}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle t^{1/\alpha}\cdot r\cdot n^{1-1/\alpha}\sum_{i}{(|\tilde{U}_{i}+n^{-1}\cdot\alpha^{1/\alpha}v_{i}|-|\tilde{U}_{i}|)}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{\alpha})^{1/\alpha}\cdot\tilde{t}^{1/\alpha}\cdot r\cdot n^{1-1/\alpha}\sum_{i}{(|\tilde{U}_{i}+n^{-1}\cdot\alpha^{1/\alpha}v_{i}|-|\tilde{U}_{i}|)}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{\alpha})^{1/\alpha}\cdot n^{1-1/\alpha}\sum_{i}{(|\tilde{X}_{i}+n^{1/\alpha-1}\cdot\alpha^{1/\alpha}v_{i}|-|\tilde{X}_{i}|)},

which reduces to the
\alpha=1
setting up to some scaling.

Therefore, it suffices to show the asymptotic normality of
\|X+v\|_{1}-\|X\|_{1}
when
X
has density
\propto e^{-\|X\|_{1}}
. We are going to use the Berry-Esseen theorem. Suppose we have
n
independent random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
with
\mathbb{E}X_{i}=\mu_{i},\mathrm{Var}X_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2},\mathbb{E}|X_{i}-\mu_{i}|^{3}=\rho_{i}^{3}
. Consider the normalized random variable

S_{n}:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{2}_{i}}}.

Denote its cdf by
F_{n}
. Then

Theorem D.3

(Berry-Esseen)
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F_{n}(x)-\Phi(x)|\leqslant C\cdot\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}^{3}}{\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\big)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

In the following, we proceed to calculating these three moments for the random variable
X_{1},...,X_{n}\sim p
with density
p(x)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

D.5.1
Expectation

Without loss of generality we assume
v>0
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X+v|]-\mathbb{E}[|X|]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x+v|e^{-|x|}\;dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\int_{-\infty}^{-v}|x+v|e^{-|x|}\;dx+\int_{-v}^{0}|x+v|e^{-|x|}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}|x+v|e^{-|x|}\;dx-\int_{-\infty}^{0}|x|e^{-|x|}\;dx-\int_{0}^{\infty}|x|e^{-|x|}\;dx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\int_{-\infty}^{-v}-(x+v)e^{x}\;dx+\int_{-v}^{0}(x+v)e^{x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+v)e^{-x}\;dx+\int_{-\infty}^{0}xe^{x}\;dx-\int_{0}^{\infty}xe^{-x}\;dx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\int_{v}^{\infty}xe^{-x}\;dx-\int_{0}^{v}xe^{-x}\;dx-\int_{0}^{\infty}xe^{-x}\;dx+v(-\int_{v}^{\infty}e^{-x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{v}e^{-x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}\;dx))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{v}xe^{-x}\;dx+v\int_{0}^{v}e^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(1-e^{-v}-ve^{-v})+v(1-e^{-v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(v-1+e^{-v})=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+o(v^{2})

D.5.2
Variance

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[|X+v|\cdot|X|]=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x+v|\cdot|x|e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-v}|x+v|\cdot|x|e^{-|x|}\;dx+\int_{-v}^{0}|x+v|\cdot|x|e^{-|x|}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}|x+v|\cdot|x|e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-v}(x+v)\cdot xe^{x}\;dx-\int_{-v}^{0}(x+v)xe^{x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+v)\cdot xe^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-v}x^{2}e^{x}\;dx-\int_{-v}^{0}x^{2}e^{x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{-x}\;dx+v(\int_{-\infty}^{-v}xe^{x}\;dx-\int_{-v}^{0}xe^{x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}xe^{-x}\;dx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{v}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{-x}\;dx+2v\cdot\int_{0}^{v}xe^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2v^{2}e^{-v}+4ve^{-v}+4e^{-v}+2v\cdot(1-ve^{-v}-e^{-v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2v+2ve^{-v}+4e^{-v}\sim 4+o(v^{2})

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[|X+v|^{2}]=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x+v)^{2}e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{-|x|}\;dx+v^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{-x}\;dx+2v^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4+2v^{2}

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2}]=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|^{2}e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[(|X+v|-|X|)^{2}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X+v|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2}]-2\mathbb{E}[|X+v|\cdot|X|]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(8+2v^{2}-2(4+o(v^{2})))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}+o(v^{2})

D.5.3
Third moment

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X|^{3}]=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|^{3}e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{3}e^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 12

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[|X+v|^{3}]=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x+v|^{3}e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-v}|x+v|^{3}e^{-|x|}\;dx+\int_{-v}^{0}|x+v|^{3}e^{-|x|}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}|x+v|^{3}e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-v}-(x+v)^{3}e^{x}\;dx+\int_{-v}^{0}(x+v)^{3}e^{x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+v)^{3}e^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6e^{-v}+(-6+6e^{-v}+6v-3v^{2}+v^{3})+(v^{3}+3v^{2}+6v+6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 12e^{-v}+12v+2v^{3}

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[|X+v|^{2}\cdot|X|]=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x+v)^{2}\cdot|x|e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{0}(x+v)^{2}xe^{x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+v)^{2}\cdot xe^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(v^{2}-4v+6)+(v^{2}+4v+6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2v^{2}+12

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[|X+v|\cdot|X|^{2}]=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x+v|\cdot x^{2}e^{-|x|}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{-v}(x+v)\cdot x^{2}e^{x}\;dx+\int_{-v}^{0}(x+v)x^{2}e^{x}\;dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+v)x^{2}e^{-x}\;dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-v}(v^{2}+4v+6)+[2v-6+e^{-v}(v^{2}+4v+6)]+2(v+3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4v+2e^{-v}(v^{2}+4v+6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(|X+v|-|X|)^{3}]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(-12+12e^{-v}+12v+2v^{3}-3(2v^{2}+12)+3(4v+2e^{-v}(v^{2}+4v+6)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(-48+48e^{-v}+24v+2v^{3}-6v^{2}+6v^{2}e^{-v}+24ve^{-v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(48(-v+v^{2}/2+o(v^{2}))+24v+2v^{3}-6v^{2}+6v^{2}(1-v+o(v))+24v(1-v+o(v)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o(v^{2})

Combining the pieces, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}+v_{i}|-|X_{i}|]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2}+o(1)=\frac{1}{2}+o(2)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}Var[|X_{i}+v_{i}|-|X_{i}|]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2}+o(1)=1+o(1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[(|X_{i}+v_{i}|-|X_{i}|-\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}+v_{i}|-|X_{i}|])^{3}]=o(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2})=o(1).

Therefore, by using Theorem
D.3
, we get the desired result.
