Extension of a method of Gilmer
Luke Pebody

Abstract
It is a well-known conjecture, sometimes attributed to Frankl, that for any family of sets which is closed under the union operation, there is some element which is contained in at least half of the sets. Gilmer [ 1 ] was the first to prove a constant bound, showing that there is some element contained in at least 1% of the sets. They state in their paper that the best possible bound achievable by the same method is 3 − 5 2 ≈ 38.1 % \frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 38.1\% . This note achieves that bound by finding the optimum value, given a binary variable X X potentially depending on some other variable S S with a given expected value E ⁡ ( X ) E(X) and conditional entropy H ⁡ ( X | S ) H(X|S) of the conditional entropy of H ⁡ ( X 1 ∪ X 2 | S 1 , S 2 ) H(X_{1}\cup X_{2}|S_{1},S_{2}) for independent readings X 1 , S 1 X_{1},S_{1} and X 2 , S 2 X_{2},S_{2} .

Theorem 1
.

Let
A
and
B
denote independent samples from a distribution over subsets of
[n]
. Assume that for
all
i\in[n]
,
\Pr[i\in A]\leq\alpha
for some value
0<\alpha\leq\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}
. Then

H(A\cup B)\geq\frac{H(\alpha^{2})}{H(\alpha)}H(A).

This will imply the following.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a union-closed family,
\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset
. Then there exists

i\in[n]
that is contained in at least a
\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 38.1\%
fraction of the sets
in
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Suppose not. Let
A
and
B
be drawn independently and uniformly from
\mathcal{F}
. Then, since

\Pr[i\in A]
is rational for each
i
, there exists some
\alpha<\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}
for which

\Pr[i\in A]\leq\alpha
for all
i\in[n]
. Then from Theorem
1
it follows that

H(A\cup B)\geq\frac{H(\alpha^{2})}{H(\alpha)}H(A)>H(A)
. But this causes a contradiction, as

A
is drawn from the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{F}
and
A\cup B
is drawn from some other
distribution on
\mathcal{F}
and the uniform distribution has the maximum entropy.
∎

4
Properties of
H(x)/x

Our proof depends on various properties of
\frac{H(x)}{x}
(and its inverse). For ease of notation,
let us write
f(x)=\frac{H(x)}{x}
.

Lemma 7
.

The function
f
is continuous, onto and decreasing from
(0,1]
to
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

The derivative of
f
is
f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\log(1-x)}{x^{2}}
, which is negative on the region.
Clearly
\frac{H(1)}{1}=0
and as
x\to 0
,
\frac{H(x)}{x}>-\log x
tends to infinity.
∎

It follows that for all non-negative real
y
, there is a unique value
g(y)
for which
f(g(y))=y
.
By standard properties of derivatives, if
g(y)=x
,
g
is differentiable at
y
, with derivative

1/f^{\prime}(x).

Lemma 8
.

For all
0<\alpha<1
, the function
f(\alpha g(x))
is convex.

Proof.

The derivative of
f(\alpha g(x))
with respect to
x
is

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\alpha g(x))\alpha g^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1-\alpha g(x))}{\alpha^{2}g(x)^{2}}\frac{\alpha}{f^{\prime}(g(x))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1-\alpha g(x))}{\alpha^{2}g(x)^{2}}\frac{\alpha g(x)^{2}}{\log(1-g(x))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1-\alpha g(x))}{\alpha\log(1-g(x))}.

We will show this is increasing in
x
. Since
g(x)
is decreasing, this is the same as showing
that
\frac{\log(1-\alpha y)}{\alpha\log(1-y)}
is decreasing on the range
(0,1]
.

The derivative of this with respect to
y
is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dy}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\alpha/(1-\alpha y)\alpha\log(1-y)+\alpha/(1-y)\log(1-\alpha y)}{\alpha^{2}\log(1-y)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\alpha(1-y)\log(1-y)+(1-\alpha y)\log(1-\alpha y)}{\alpha\log(1-y)^{2}(1-y)(1-\alpha y)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(1-y)\log(1-y)/y+(1-\alpha y)\log(1-\alpha y)/(\alpha y)}{\log(1-y)^{2}(1-y)(1-\alpha y)^{2}/y}.

So showing that
f(\alpha g(x))
is convex for all
0<\alpha<1
is the same as showing that

-(1-\alpha y)\log(1-\alpha y)/(\alpha y)>-(1-y)\log(1-y)/y
for all
0<\alpha<1
, which is the
same as showing that
-(1-z)\log(1-z)/z
is decreasing on
[0,1]
.

An elementary property of the derivative of quotients is that if
g^{\prime}(x)>0
and
g(x)>0
,

f(x)/g(x)
is decreasing whenever
f(x)/g(x)\geq f^{\prime}(x)/g^{\prime}(x)
. Thus
-(1-z)\log(1-z)/z
is
decreasing if
-(1-z)\log(1-z)/z\geq 1+\log(1-z)
, which can be rewritten as

\log(1-z)\leq-z
, or
1-z\leq\exp(-z)
. Since this is true for all
z
, we are done.
∎

5
Optimising Joint Entropy

In this section we will show that for any distribution
p
on
[0,1]
, there exists a
distribution
q
on
[0,1]
with at most one non-zero point in its support, for which

\mathbb{E}_{q}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{p}(X)
,
\mathbb{E}_{q}(H(X))=\mathbb{E}_{p}(H(X))
and
\mathbb{E}_{q,q}(H(X_{1}X_{2}))\leq\mathbb{E}_{p,p}(H(X_{1}X_{2})
.

We will do this by proving first for distributions with finite support, inductively by reducing
the size of the non-zero support by one at a time.

This will be achieved by the following technical lemma.

Lemma 9
.

Given numbers
p_{1},p_{2},x_{1},x_{2}\in(0,1]
, there exists numbers
q
and
y
such that

p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}=qy
and
p_{1}H(x_{1})+p_{2}H(x_{2})=qH(y)
.

For such
q
and
y
, the following properties hold:

1.

q\leq p_{1}+p_{2}
,

2.

For all
0\leq z\leq 1
,
p_{1}H(zx_{1})+p_{2}H(zx_{2})\geq qH(zy)
and

3.

p_{1}^{2}H(x_{1}^{2})+2p_{1}p_{2}H(x_{1}x_{2})+p_{2}^{2}H(x_{2}^{2})\geq q^{2}H(y)^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
f:t\to H(t)/t
is onto, there exists
y
such that

\displaystyle\frac{H(y)}{y}=f(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}x_{1}}{p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}}f(x_{1})+\frac{p_{2}x_{2}}{p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}}f(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}H(x_{1})+p_{2}h(x_{2})}{p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}}.

Thus if we set
q=\frac{p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}}{y}
, the required equalities clearly hold.

By construction,
\frac{H(y)}{y}
is contained in the interval spanned by

\frac{H(x_{1})}{x_{1}}
and
\frac{H(x_{2})}{x_{2}}
. Since
f
is monotonic, it follows that

y
is contained in the interval spanned by
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. Hence it follows that
q\leq p_{1}+p_{2}
.

Fix
0\leq z\leq 1
. By Lemma
8
,
f(zg(x))
is convex. If we write

p=\frac{p_{1}x_{1}}{p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}}
then since
f(y)=pf(x_{1})+(1-p)f(x_{2})
, it follows that

f(zg(f(y)))\leq pf(zg(f(x_{1})))+(1-p)f(\alpha g(f(x_{2})))
. Since
g
and
f
are inverse
functions, this can be rewritten as
f(zy)\leq pf(zx_{1})+(1-p)f(zx_{2})
, which gives

\displaystyle\frac{H(zy)}{y}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p_{1}x_{1}}{p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}}\frac{H(zx_{1})}{x_{1}}+\frac{p_{2}x_{2}}{p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}}\frac{H(zx_{2})}{x_{2}}

\displaystyle H(zy)

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}x_{1}\frac{H(zx_{1})}{x_{1}}+p_{2}x_{2}\frac{H(zx_{2})}{x_{2}}

\displaystyle=p_{1}H(zx_{1})+p_{2}H(zx_{2}).

Finally the last inequality comes from the second inequality applied at
z=y,x_{1}
and
x_{2}
:

\displaystyle q^{2}H(y^{2})

\displaystyle\leq q(p_{1}H(yx_{1})+p_{2}H(yx_{2}))

\displaystyle=p_{1}(qH(yx_{1}))+p_{2}(qH(yx_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}(p_{1}H(x_{1})^{2}+p_{2}H(x_{1}x_{2}))+p_{2}(p_{1}H(x_{1}x_{2})+p_{2}H(x_{2})^{2}).

∎

This solves the above optimisation problem.

Theorem 10
.

For any distribution
p
on
[0,1]
, there exists a
distribution
q
on
[0,1]
with at most one non-zero point in its support, for which

\mathbb{E}_{q}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{p}(X)
,
\mathbb{E}_{q}(H(X))=\mathbb{E}_{p}(H(X))
and
\mathbb{E}_{q,q}(H(X_{1}X_{2}))\leq\mathbb{E}_{p,p}(H(X_{1}X_{2})
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
p
is of finite support, but has more than one non-zero point in its support.
Let
x_{1},x_{2}
be distinct non-zero points in the support and let
p_{1}=\Pr(X=x_{1})
and
p_{2}=\Pr(X=x_{2})
.

If we let
q
and
y
be the numbers described in Lemma
9
, and make
p^{\prime}
be a distribution
which is identical to
p
but replaces hitting the elements
x_{1},x_{2}
with probability
p_{1},p_{2}
by
hitting the elements
y,0
with probability
q,p_{1}+p_{2}-q
, we still have a probability
distribution (as
0\leq q\leq p_{1}+p_{2}
), but with smaller support. Further, by the definition of
q
and

y
,
\mathbb{E}_{p}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{p^{\prime}}(X)
and
\mathbb{E}_{p}(H(X))=\mathbb{E}_{p^{\prime}}(H(X))
. Further, from the inequalities in
Lemma
9
,
\mathbb{E}_{p^{\prime},p^{\prime}}(H(X_{1}X_{2}))\leq\mathbb{E}_{p,p}(H(X_{1}X_{2})
.

Inductively, for any distribution of finite support we can replace two non-zero elements with one
repeatedly, maintaining
\mathbb{E}_{p}(X)
and
\mathbb{E}_{p}(H(X))
but never increasing
\mathbb{E}_{p,p}(H(X_{1}X_{2}))
.
Eventually we will reach such a distribution
q
. This proves the Theorem for all distributions
of finite support.

The distributions of finite support are dense in the set of all distributions and these expectations
are continuous. As such, we can generalise to all distributions.
∎

We will rewrite this Theorem using the function
g
which we defined as the inverse of

Corollary 11
.

For real numbers
0<t,u<1
with
u\leq H(t)
, the minimum possible value of

\mathbb{E}_{p,p}(H(X_{1}X_{2}))
with
\mathbb{E}_{p}(X)=t
and
\mathbb{E}_{p}(H(x))=u
is

t^{2}H(v^{2})/v^{2}
where
v=g(u/t)
.

Proof.

This is achievable by the distribution which takes
v
with probability
\frac{t}{v}
and
0 with probability
1-\frac{t}{v}
, note that
f(v)=u/t\leq H(t)/t=f(t)
, so
t<v
and this is
a proper distribution.

To show this is optimal, take any distribution
p
. Then we know by Theorem
10

there is a distribution
q
on
[0,1]
with at most one non-zero point in its support for which

\mathbb{E}_{q}(X)=t
,
\mathbb{E}_{q}(H(X))=u
and
\mathbb{E}_{q,q}(H(X_{1})X_{2}))\leq\mathbb{E}_{p,p}(H(X_{1}X_{2}))
.

Since the expectation of
X

q
is non-zero, there is a non-zero point in the support of

q

and the probability be
p

Then
t=pv^{\prime}
and

f(v^{\prime})=u/t=f(v)
and hence
v=v^{\prime}
and
p=\frac{t}{v}

the distribution

∎
