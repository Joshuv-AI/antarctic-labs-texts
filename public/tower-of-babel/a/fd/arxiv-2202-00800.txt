1.
L_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-best Rational Approximants

1.1.
Meromorphic Approximation Problem

Let
\mathbb{D}
be the unit disk and
\mathbb{T}
be the unit circle. Denote by
L^{2}
the space of square-summable functions on
\mathbb{T}
and by
H^{2}
the Hardy space of functions
f(z)
that are holomorphic in
\mathbb{D}
and satisfy

\displaystyle\|f\|_{2}^{2}:=\sup_{0<r<1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f(r\tau)|^{2}|\mathrm{d}\tau|<\infty.

Every function in
H^{2}
is uniquely determined by its non-tangential limit on
\mathbb{T}
, which necessarily belongs to
L^{2}
and the
L^{2}
-norm of this trace is equal to the
H^{2}
-norm of the function. Thus,
H^{2}
can be regarded as a closed subspace of
L^{2}
. We shall further denote by
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
H^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
the subspaces of
L^{2}
and
H^{2}
, respectively, consisting of functions with real Fourier coefficients, that is, functions satisfying
f(\bar{\tau})=\overline{f(\tau)}
for
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
.

Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
the space of algebraic polynomials of degree at most
n
with real coefficients and by
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
its subset consisting of monic polynomials with exactly
n
zeroes in
\mathbb{D}
. Put

\mathcal{R}_{n}:=\left\{\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}=\frac{p_{n-1}z^{n-1}+p_{n-2}z^{n-2}+\cdots+p_{0}}{z^{n}+q_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+q_{0}}:~p\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1},~q\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}\right\}

and consider the following meromorphic approximation problem:
given
f\in L_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, find
g_{n}\in H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}+\mathcal{R}_{n}
such that
\|f-g_{n}\|_{2}=\inf_{g\in H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}+\mathcal{R}_{n}}\|f-g\|_{2}
.

1.2.
Rational Approximation Problem

The above problem can be reduced to a rational approximation problem. To this end, denote by
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
the orthogonal complement of
H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
in
L_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
L_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\oplus\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, with respect to the standard scalar product. From the viewpoint of analytic function theory,
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
can be regarded as a space of traces of functions holomorphic in
\{|z|>1\}
, having real Fourier coefficients and vanishing at infinity, and whose square-means on the concentric circles centered at zero (this time with radii greater then 1) are uniformly bounded above. The orthogonal decomposition
L_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\oplus\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
yields that

\|f-g_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\|f^{+}-g_{n}^{+}\|_{2}^{2}+\|f^{-}-g_{n}^{-}\|_{2}^{2},

where
f=f^{+}+f^{-}
,
g_{n}=g_{n}^{+}+g_{n}^{-}
, and
f^{+},g_{n}^{+}\in H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
f^{-},g_{n}^{-}\in\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. One can see that in order for
g_{n}
to be a best approximant it is necessary that
g_{n}^{+}=f^{+}
. Moreover, one can readily check that
g_{n}^{-}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
. Thus, an equivalent rational approximation problem can be stated as follows:
given
f\in\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, find
r_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
such that
\|f-r_{n}\|_{2}=\inf_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}\|f-r\|_{2}
.

1.3.
Irreducible Critical Points

L_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-best rational approximants are a part of a larger class of
critical points
in rational
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-approximation. From the computational viewpoint, critical points are as important as best approximants since a numerical search is more likely to yield a locally best rather than a best approximant. For a fixed
f\in\bar{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
, critical points can be defined as follows. Set

\begin{array}[]{rll}\Sigma_{f,n}:\mathcal{P}_{n-1}\times\mathcal{Q}_{n}&\to&[0,\infty)\\
(p,q)&\mapsto&\|f-p/q\|_{2}^{2}.\end{array}

In other words,
\Sigma_{f,n}
is the squared error of approximation of
f
by
r=p/q
in
\mathcal{R}_{n}
. The cross-product
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}\times\mathcal{Q}_{n}
is topologically identified with an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with coordinates
p_{j}
and
q_{k}
,
j,k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
. Then a pair of polynomials
(p_{c},q_{c})\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}\times\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, identified with a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, is said to be a
critical pair of order

n
, if all the partial derivatives of
\Sigma_{f,n}
do vanish at
(p_{c},q_{c})
. Respectively, a rational function
r_{c}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
is a
critical point of order

n
if it can be written as the ratio
r_{c}=p_{c}/q_{c}
of a critical pair
(p_{c},q_{c})
in
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}\times\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. A particular example of a critical point is a
locally best approximant
. That is, a rational function
r_{l}=p_{l}/q_{l}
associated with a pair
(p_{l},q_{l})\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}\times\mathcal{Q}_{n}
such that
\Sigma_{f,n}(p_{l},q_{l})\leq\Sigma_{f,n}(p,q)
for all pairs
(p,q)
in some neighborhood of
(p_{l},q_{l})
in
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}\times\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. We call a critical point of order
n

irreducible
if it belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{n-1}
. Best approximants, as well as local minima, are always irreducible critical points unless
f\in\mathcal{R}_{n-1}
. In general, there may be other critical points, reducible or irreducible, which are saddles or maxima.

One of the most crucial features of the critical points is the fact that they are “maximal” rational interpolants. More precisely, if
f\in\bar{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
r_{n}(z)
is an irreducible critical point of order
n
, then
r_{n}(z)
interpolates
f(z)
at the reflection (
z\mapsto 1/\bar{z}
) of each pole of
r_{n}(z)
with order twice the multiplicity of the pole

[
6
]
, which is the maximal number of interpolation conditions (i.e.,
2n
) that can be imposed in general on a rational function of type
(n-1,n)
.

1.4.
Multipoint Padé Approximants

The just described property of irreducible critical points can also be expressed by saying that they are multipoint Padé approximants. The latter are defined as follows. Let
f(z)
be a function holomorphic and vanishing at infinity (the second condition is there for convenience only as functions in
\bar{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
vanish at infinity by definition) and
D
be an unbounded domain in which
f(z)
is analytic or to which it possesses an analytic continuation, again, denoted by
f(z)
. Let
\{E_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a triangular scheme of points in
D
, i.e., each
E_{n}
consists of
2n
not necessarily distinct nor necessarily finite points. Further, let
v_{n}(z)
be the monic polynomial with zeroes at the finite points of
E_{n}
(multiplicity of a zero is equal to the number of its occurrences in
E_{n}
). The
n
-th diagonal Padé approximant of
f(z)
associated with
E_{n}
is a rational function
(p_{n}/q_{n})(z)
such that
\deg p_{n}\leq n
,
\deg q_{n}\leq n
, and
q_{n}\not\equiv 0
, while

(1.1)

R_{n}(z):=\frac{q_{n}(z)f(z)-p_{n}(z)}{v_{n}(z)}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/z^{n+1}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty,

and is analytic in
D
. Multipoint Padé approximant always exists since the conditions for
p_{n}(z)
and
q_{n}(z)
amount to solving a system of
2n+1
homogeneous linear equations with
2n+2
unknown coefficients, no solution of which can be such that
q_{n}\equiv 0
(thus, we may assume that
q_{n}(z)
is monic). Observe that given
q_{n}(z)
,
p_{n}(z)
is uniquely defined. Indeed, if
p(z)
and
p_{*}(z)
were to correspond to the same
q_{n}(z)
, the expression
(p-p_{*})(z)/v_{n}(z)
would vanish at infinity with order at least
n+1
while being finite at every zero of
v_{n}(z)
, which is clearly impossible. Moreover, if the pairs
p(z),q(z)
and
p_{*}(z),q_{*}(z)
are solutions, then so is any linear combination
(c_{1}p+c_{2}p_{*})(z),(c_{1}q+c_{2}q_{*})(z)
. Therefore, the solution corresponding to the monic denominator of the smallest degree is unique. In what follows, we understand that
p_{n}(z),q_{n}(z),R_{n}(z)
come from this unique solution and write
[n/n;E_{n}]_{f}(z):=(p_{n}/q_{n})(z)
.

As mentioned above, irreducible critical points
r_{n}(z)=(p_{n}/q_{n})(z)
turn out to be multipoint Padé approximants for which
v_{n}(z):=\varkappa_{n}\tilde{q}_{n}^{2}(z)
, where
\tilde{q}_{n}(z):=z^{n}q_{n}(1/z)
is the reciprocal polynomial of
q_{n}(z)
and
\varkappa_{n}
is the reciprocal of the leading coefficient of
\tilde{q}_{n}^{2}(z)
.

2.
Markov Functions on One Interval

2.1.
Markov Functions and Szegő Measures

Assume that

(2.1)

f(z)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu(x)}{z-x}

for some finite positive compactly supported Borel measure
\mu
on the real line. Such functions, known as Markov functions, are well suited for asymptotic analysis of the behavior of their multipoint Padé approximants because the denominator polynomials
q_{n}(z)
turn out to be orthogonal polynomials. More precisely, it holds that

(2.2)

\int x^{m}q_{n}(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu(x)}{v_{n}(x)}=0,\quad m\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\},

and the error of approximation can be represented as

f(z)-\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}=\frac{v_{n}(z)}{q_{n}^{2}(z)}\int\frac{q_{n}^{2}(x)}{v_{n}(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu(x)}{z-x}.

In this section we shall assume that
\mu
is a Szegő measure, that is,
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=[a,b]
and

(2.3)

\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\frac{\dot{\mu}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{\pi\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}}+\mathrm{d}\mu_{s}(x),

where
\mu_{s}
is singular to Lebesgue measure and
\dot{\mu}(x)
satisfies Szegő condition
\int\log\dot{\mu}(x)\mathrm{d}x>-\infty
.

2.2.
Multipoint Padé Approximants

Set
w(z):=w(z;a,b)=\sqrt{(z-a)(z-b)}
to be holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[a,b]
and normalized so that
w(z)=z+\mathcal{O}(1)
as
z\to\infty
. Given a Szegő measure, one can define its Szegő function by

S_{\dot{\mu}}(z):=\exp\left\{\frac{w(z)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\log\dot{\mu}(x)}{x-z}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{w_{+}(x)}\right\},\quad z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a,b],

where
F_{\pm}(x):=\lim_{y\to 0}F(x\pm\mathrm{i}y)
,
x\in(a,b)
, for any function
F(z)
holomorphic off
[a,b]
. This is a holomorphic and non-vanishing function in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a,b]
whose traces exist almost everywhere on
[a,b]
and satisfy
|S_{\dot{\mu}\pm}(x)|^{2}=\dot{\mu}(x)
. Now, let
\psi(z)
be the conformal map of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a,b]
onto
\mathbb{D}
with
\psi(\infty)=0
and
\psi^{\prime}(\infty)>0
. It is given by

\psi(z)=\frac{2}{b-a}\left(z-\frac{b+a}{2}-w(z)\right).

We shall assume that the interpolation sets
E_{n}
are conjugate-symmetric (i.e., if
e\in E_{n}
, then
\bar{e}\in E_{n}
) and lie sufficiently far away from
[a,b]
in the sense that

(2.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{e\in E_{n}}\big(1-|\psi(e)|\big)=\infty.

The above condition is always satisfied if there exists a neighborhood of
[a,b]
devoid of elements of all
E_{n}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f(z)
be given by (
2.1
) for a Szegő measure
\mu
, supported on
[a,b]
, and
p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
be the multipoint Padé approximant of
f(z)
associated with
E_{n}
, where the interpolation sets
E_{n}
are conjugate-symmetric and satisfy (
2.4
). Then it holds that

f(z)-\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}=(2+o(1))\frac{S_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}(z)}{w(z)}\prod_{e\in E_{n}}\frac{\psi(z)-\psi(e)}{1-\psi(z)\overline{\psi(e)}}

as
n\to\infty
locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a,b]
(condition (
2.4
) ensures that the products above converge to zero locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a,b]
).

As stated this theorem is taken from
[
9
, Theorem 2]
, with an additional admissibility condition it was proven in
[
3
]
, and the case of absolutely continuous measures and the interpolation points separated from
[a,b]
was considered in
[
10
]
.

2.3.
Irreducible Critical Points

Let now
[a,b]\subset(-1,1)
, in which case
f\in\bar{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
r_{n}(z)=p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
be an irreducible critical point of order
n
in rational
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-approximation of
f(z)
. Since the denominator polynomials satisfy (
2.2
) with
v_{n}(z)=\varkappa_{n}\tilde{q}_{n}^{2}(z)
, all their zeroes belong to
[a,b]
and therefore the zeroes of
\tilde{q}_{n}(z)
belong to
[a,b]^{-1}:=\{x:x^{-1}\in[a,b]\}
. Then one can reformulate Theorem
2.1
in a way more suitable for
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-approximants.

To this end, let
w(z)=w(z;a,b)
be as above and set
\tilde{w}(z):=zw(1/z)
. Observe that
\tilde{w}(z)
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[a,b]^{-1}
and normalized so that
w(0)=1
. Put

\mathrm{d}\omega_{[a,b],\mathbb{T}}(x):=\frac{1-ab}{2K}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{|(w\tilde{w})(x)|},\quad x\in[a,b],

where the constant
K
turns
\omega_{[a,b],\mathbb{T}}
into a probability measure and is, in fact, the complete elliptic integral of the first kind with modulus
(b-a)/(1-ab)
. The measure
\omega_{[a,b],\mathbb{T}}
can be interpreted from the point of view of potential theory as the condenser equilibrium distribution on
[a,b]
of the condenser
([a,b],\mathbb{T})
, see
[
2
]
. Define

G_{\dot{\mu}}:=\exp\left\{\int\log\dot{\mu}(x)\mathrm{d}\omega_{[a,b],\mathbb{T}}(x)\right\}.

It holds that
G_{\dot{\mu}}>0
if and only if
\mu
is a Szegő measure on
[a,b]
, see
[
2
, Lemma 2.37]
. Put

D_{\dot{\mu}}(z):=\exp\left\{\frac{(w\tilde{w})(z)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1-2xz+x^{2}}{(x-z)(1-xz)}\log\left(\frac{\dot{\mu}(x)}{G_{\dot{\mu}}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{(w_{+}\tilde{w})(x)}\right\}

for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a,b]\cup[a,b]^{-1}\big)
. The function
D_{\dot{\mu}}(z)
is non-vanishing and holomorphic in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a,b]\cup[a,b]^{-1}\big)
, its argument has zero increment along
\mathbb{T}
and
|D_{\dot{\mu}}(\tau)|\equiv 1
for
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
. Moreover, its traces exist almost everywhere on
[a,b]\cup[a,b]^{-1}
and satisfy
G_{\dot{\mu}}|D_{\dot{\mu}\pm}(x)|^{2}=\dot{\mu}(x)
,
x\in[a,b]
, and
G_{\dot{\mu}}|D_{\dot{\mu}\pm}(x)|^{-2}=\dot{\mu}(1/x)
,
x\in[a,b]^{-1}
, see
[
2
, Lemma 2.40]
. Further, let

\varphi(z):=\exp\left\{\pi\frac{1-ab}{2K}\int_{1}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(w\tilde{w})(s)}\right\},

where the path of integration belongs to
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a,b]\cup[a,b]^{-1}\big)
. This function is in fact the conformal map of the ring domain
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a,b]\cup[a,b]^{-1}\big)
onto the annulus
\{z:~\rho<|z|<1/\rho\}
, where
\rho:=\varphi(b)
. The following theorem was proven in
[
2
, Theorem 8]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
f(z)
be given by (
2.1
), for a Szegő measure
\mu
on
[a,b]\subset(-1,1)
. Further, let
\{r_{n}(z)\}
be a sequence of irreducible critical points in rational
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-approximation of
f(z)
. Then it holds that

f(z)-r_{n}(z)=\big(2G_{\dot{\mu}}+o(1)\big)\frac{D_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}(z)}{w(z)}\left(\frac{\rho}{\varphi(z)}\right)^{2n}

as
n\to\infty
locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a,b]\cup[a,b]^{-1}\big)
.

3.
Padé Approximation of Markov Functions On Several Intervals

In this section we shall explain how results from
[
11
]
specialize to the case of Markov functions on several intervals. Part of the goal of this section is to provide as many explicit formulae as possible (most of them were not presented in
[
11
]
).

3.1.
Measures of Orthogonality

Assume now that
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=\cup_{i=1}^{g+1}[a_{i},b_{i}]=:\Delta
, where
g\geq 1
and, for convenience, we assume that
b_{i}<a_{i+1}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
. Let

(3.1)

w(z):=\sqrt{(z-a_{1})(z-b_{1})\cdots(z-a_{g+1})(z-b_{g+1})}

be holomorphic in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
and normalized so that
w(z)=z^{g+1}+\mathcal{O}(z^{g})
as
z\to\infty
. In this section we shall suppose that (
2.3
) is replaced by more stringent assumption

(3.2)

\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=-\frac{1}{\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x}{w_{+}(x)},\quad x\in\Delta,

where
\rho(x)
is a real-valued, smooth, see (
3.4
) further below, and non-vanishing function on
\Delta
whose sign distribution is such that
\mu
is a positive measure. To capture the positivity, choose
m(x)
to be a monic polynomial of degree
g
with exactly one zero in each gap
(b_{k},a_{k+1})
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
. Observe also that
-\mathrm{i}w_{+}(x)=(-1)^{g+1-k}|w(x)|
for
x\in[a_{k},b_{k}]
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,g+1\}
. Hence, if we write

(3.3)

\rho(x)=:\dot{\mu}(x)m(x),\quad x\in\Delta,

then
\dot{\mu}(x)
is a positive function on
\Delta
from which we require that its logarithm belongs to a fractional Sobolev space:

(3.4)

\iint_{\Delta\times\Delta}\left|\frac{\log\dot{\mu}(x)-\log\dot{\mu}(y)}{x-y}\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y<\infty

for some
p>4
(then
\log\dot{\mu}(x)
is Hölder continuous with exponent at least
1-2/p>1/2
). Notice that
\rho(x)
also admits a continuous branch of the logarithm on
\Delta
that satisfies (
3.4
) (this is the condition used in
[
11
]
). A particular choice of
m(x)
, on which
\dot{\mu}(x)
does depend, is not important to us. In fact, we could have chosen
m(x)
simply to be
1
or
-1
on the intervals comprising
\Delta
. However, in the author’s opinion, some of the formulae further below would have been less elegant in this case.

3.2.
Riemann Surface and its Holomorphic Differentials

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
be a Riemann surface realized as two copies of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
, say
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
glued crosswise along
\Delta
. Formally, it can be defined as

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z,w):w^{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{g+1}(z-a_{i})(z-b_{i})\right\}.

Of course, it holds that
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}=\boldsymbol{D}\cup\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cup\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}:=\pi^{-1}(\Delta)
and
\pi({\boldsymbol{z}})=z
is the natural projection. We denote by
\cdot^{*}
a conformal involution defined by
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}=(z,-w)
for
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z,w)
. We choose a homology basis
\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{g}
on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
in the following way:
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}:=\pi^{-1}([b_{i},a_{i+1}])
and is oriented away from
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}
on
\boldsymbol{D}
while
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}\subset\boldsymbol{D}\cup\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is such that
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i})
is a convex Jordan curve that passes through
a_{1}
and
b_{i}
and is oriented counter-clockwise (we also assume that
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i})\setminus\{a_{1}\}
is contained in the interior of
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i+1})
).

Let
\mathsf{V}
be the following matrix:

\mathsf{V}:=\left[\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{x^{l}\mathrm{d}x}{w(x)}\right]_{i=1,l=0}^{g,g-1},

where
i
is the row index and
l
is the column one. It is straightforward to see that

\mathrm{det}(\mathsf{V})=\int_{b_{1}}^{a_{2}}\cdots\int_{b_{g}}^{a_{g+1}}\frac{V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{g})}{w(x_{1})\cdots w(x_{g})}\mathrm{d}x_{g}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{1}\neq 0,

where
V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{g})
is the Vandermonde determinant and the inequality
\mathrm{det}(\mathsf{V})\neq 0
follows from the obvious fact that all the gaps
(b_{k},a_{k+1})
are disjoint. Hence, there exist polynomials
l_{i}(x)
,
\deg(l_{i})=g-1
, such that

(3.5)

\int_{b_{k}}^{a_{k+1}}\frac{l_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{w(x)}=\delta_{ki},\quad i,k\in\{1,\ldots,g\},

where
\delta_{ki}
is the usual Kronecker symbol. Indeed, the coefficients of these polynomials are the columns of
\mathsf{V}^{-1}
. Moreover, since these polynomials have degree
g-1
and real coefficients, (
3.5
) yields that each
l_{i}(x)
has exactly one zero in each gap
(b_{k},a_{k+1})
for
k\neq i
.

Observe also that the differentials

(3.6)

\mathcal{H}_{i}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}(l_{i}(z)\mathrm{d}z)/(2w(z)),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D},\\
-\mathcal{H}_{i}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{*},\end{array}\right.

are holomorphic on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
and normalized so that
\oint_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{i}=\delta_{ki}
(since
w_{+}(x)=-w_{-}(x)
on
\Delta
, these differentials do holomorphically extend across
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
). It is a well-known fact of the theory of compact Riemann surfaces, see
[
4
, Section III.2.7]
, that the integrals of the normalized holomorphic differentials over the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-cycles of the chosen homology basis form a symmetric matrix with positive definite imaginary part:

(3.7)

\mathsf{B}:=\big[\mathsf{B}_{kj}\big]_{k,j=1}^{g},\quad\mathsf{B}_{kj}:=\oint_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{j}=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}\frac{l_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{w_{+}(x)},

where to get the second representation for
\mathsf{B}_{kj}
we used holomorphy of the integrands to deform
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
to
\pi^{-1}([a_{1},b_{k}])\cap(\boldsymbol{D}\cup\boldsymbol{\Delta})
and the fact that the integrals over the gaps cancel each other out. This representation shows that all the entries of
\mathsf{B}
are purely imaginary.

3.3.
Third Kind Differentials

Let
m_{\infty}(z)
be a polynomial of degree
g
such that

(3.8)

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{|z|=\rho}\frac{m_{\infty}(s)\mathrm{d}s}{w(s)}=-1\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{m_{\infty}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{w(x)}=0,\;\;i\in\{1,\ldots,g\},

where the circle
|z|=\rho
is positively oriented and contains
\Delta
in its interior. Using polynomials
l_{i}(z)
from (
3.5
), it can be readily checked that

m_{\infty}(x)=-x^{g}+\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left(\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{y^{g}\mathrm{d}y}{w(y)}\right)l_{i}(x).

Clearly, the polynomial
m_{\infty}(x)
has real coefficients and therefore has exactly one simple zero in each gap
(b_{i},a_{i+1})
. Thus, the measure
\mathrm{d}\omega_{\Delta}(x)=(m_{\infty}(x)\mathrm{d}x)/(\pi\mathrm{i}w_{+}(x))
is a positive probability measure on
\Delta
. In fact, from the point of view of potential theory,
\omega_{\Delta}
can be interpreted as the logarithmic equilibrium distribution on
\Delta
(in particular, if one chooses
m(x)=m_{\infty}(x)
in (
3.3
), then
\dot{\mu}(x)
again can be interpreted as the Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to the logarithmic equilibrium distribution).

More generally, given
e\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
, let
m_{e}(z)
be a polynomial of degree
g
such that

(3.9)

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{|z-e|=\rho}\frac{m_{e}(s)}{s-e}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{w(s)}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{m_{e}(x)}{x-e}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{w(x)}=0,\;\;i\in\{1,\ldots,g\},

where the circle
|z-e|=\rho
is positively oriented and contains
\Delta
in its exterior. Similarly to
m_{\infty}(x)
, one can check that

m_{e}(x)=c_{e}\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left(\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{1}{y-e}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{w(y)}\right)(x-e)l_{i}(x)\right),

where the normalizing constant
c_{e}
is chosen so that the first condition in (
3.9
) is fulfilled. Notice that
m_{e}(x)
is a polynomial with real coefficients when
e
is real (in particular, it has exactly one zero in each gap
(b_{i},a_{i+1})
). In this situation the corresponding measure
(m_{e}(x)\mathrm{d}x)/(\pi\mathrm{i}(x-e)w_{+}(x))
is a positive probability measure on
\Delta
and can be interpreted as the weighted equilibrium distribution in the field generated by a single unit charge placed at
e
, or equivalently as the balayage of the Dirac mass at
e
to
\Delta
. This is no longer the case when
\Im e\neq 0
. However, since
m_{\bar{e}}(z)=\overline{m_{e}(\bar{z})}
, it holds that
(z-\bar{e})m_{e}(z)+(z-e)m_{\bar{e}}(z)
is a polynomial with real coefficients as well (again, it must have exactly one zero in each gap). In this case the average of densities corresponding to
e
and
\bar{e}
produces a positive probability measure with a similar potential-theoretic interpretation.

Let
\Omega_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})
be the normalized (that is, having zero periods on the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-cycles) third kind differential on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
with two simple poles at
\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{e}^{*}
,
\boldsymbol{e}\in\boldsymbol{D}
, with respective residues
1
and
-1
, which is otherwise holomorphic. It can be readily checked that

\Omega_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}(m_{e}(z)\mathrm{d}z)/((z-e)w(z)),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D},\\
-\Omega_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{*}.\end{array}\right.

When
e
is real (including
e=\infty
, in which case
m_{e}(z)/(z-e)
above is replaced by
m_{\infty}(z)
),
\Omega_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})
can be considered as normalized to have purely imaginary periods on all of the cycles of the homology basis due to the second requirements in (
3.8
) and (
3.9
). This is no longer true when
\Im e\neq 0
, however, the differential
\Omega_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})+\Omega_{\bar{e}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
can also be seen as normalized to have purely imaginary periods.

3.4.
Blaschke-type Functions

Let
E_{n}
be a conjugate-symmetric interpolation set in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
(as usual, it consists of
2n
not necessarily finite nor distinct elements). Let

(3.10)

\psi_{n}(z):=\exp\left\{\int_{b_{g+1}}^{z}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{n},|e|<\infty}\frac{m_{e}(s)}{s-e}+\sum_{e\in E_{n},|e|=\infty}m_{\infty}(s)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{w(s)}\right\},

where the path of integration lies entirely in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,b_{g+1})
. Furthermore, set

(3.11)

\omega_{n,k}:=\mathrm{fr}\left\{-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{n},|e|<\infty}\frac{m_{e}(x)}{x-e}+\sum_{e\in E_{n},|e|=\infty}m_{\infty}(x)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{w_{+}(x)}\right\}

k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
, where
\mathrm{fr}\{x\}\in[0,1)
is such that
x-\mathrm{fr}\{x\}\in\mathbb{Z}
. It follows from the conjugate-symmetry of
E_{n}
and the discussion after (
3.9
) that these constants are real.

Proposition 3.1
.

The function
\psi_{n}(z)
is analytic in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
and has a zero at each
e\in E_{n}
of order equal to the multiplicity of
e
in
E_{n}
. It holds that
|\psi_{n}(z)|<1
in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
and

(3.12)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi_{n+}(x)=\psi_{n-}(x)e^{-4\pi\mathrm{i}\omega_{n,k}},&x\in(b_{k},a_{k+1}),\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,g\},\\
|\psi_{n\pm}(x)|\equiv 1,&x\in\Delta.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

The integrand in (
3.10
) behaves like
-ks^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(s^{-2})
as
s\to\infty
, where
k
is the multiplicity of
\infty
in
E_{n}
. Therefore, the integral of the integrand is equal to an integer multiple of
2\pi\mathrm{i}
on any closed curve encircling
[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
, which implies analyticity of
\psi_{n}(z)
in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
. Vanishing of
\psi_{n}(z)
at
e\in E_{n}
follows from the first requirements in (
3.8
) and (
3.9
). Since the integrand in (
3.10
) is real on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\Delta
, (
3.12
) follows from conjugate symmetry of
E_{n}
and the second requirements in (
3.8
) and (
3.9
). As the function
\log|\psi_{n}(z)|
is subharmonic in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
and is identically zero on
\Delta
, the conclusion
|\psi_{n}(z)|<1
follows from the maximum principle for subharmonic functions
[
8
, Theorem 2.3.1]
.
∎

For the future comparison with
[
11
]
, let us point out that a function

(3.13)

S_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\exp\left\{\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{g+1}}^{\boldsymbol{z}}G_{n}\right\}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D},\\
v_{n}^{-1}(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{*},\end{array}\right.

was defined there, where, as before,
v_{n}(z)=\prod_{e\in E_{n},|e|<\infty}(z-e)
,
\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{b}_{i}
are the ramification point of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
with the respective natural projections
a_{i},b_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,g+1\}
, and
G_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is a meromorphic differential on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
given by

G_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in E_{n},|e|<\infty}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z-e}-\Omega_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in E_{n},|e|=\infty}\Omega_{\infty}({\boldsymbol{z}}).

This function is holomorphic and non-vanishing in
\pi^{-1}(\mathbb{C}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}])
with a pole and a zero of order
n
lying on top of infinity in
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
, respectively. It then follows that

(3.14)

\psi_{n}(z)=v_{n}(z)S_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})S_{n}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\quad{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}.

As far as the boundary values of
S_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
are concerned, it holds that

(3.15)

S_{n+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=S_{n-}({\boldsymbol{s}})\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}v_{n}(s),&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta},\\
e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\omega_{n,k}},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k},\;\;k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}.\end{array}\right.

There exists an alternative construction of the functions
S_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
. It will be presented further below in Section
3.8
for the sake of completeness of the exposition. There, rather than using third kind differentials, we shall use Riemann theta functions.

3.5.
Szegő Functions

Let
p(z)
be a monic polynomial of degree
d\leq g
with simple zeroes, say
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}
(of course, when
d=0
, there are no zeroes). Assume that the zeroes of
p(z)
do not belong to
\Delta
. Given
\mu
as in (
3.2
)–(
3.3
), let

(3.16)

S_{\dot{\mu}}(z):=\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{w(z)}{p(z)}\left[\int_{\Delta}\frac{\log\dot{\mu}(x)}{x-z}\frac{p(x)\mathrm{d}x}{w_{+}(x)}-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}c_{\dot{\mu},i}}{y-z}\frac{p(y)\mathrm{d}y}{w(y)}\right]\right\},

z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
, where the constants
c_{\dot{\mu},i}
are defined by

(3.17)

c_{\dot{\mu},i}:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Delta}\log\dot{\mu}(x)\frac{l_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{w_{+}(x)}.

Proposition 3.2
.

The Szegő function
S_{\dot{\mu}}(z)
is analytic and non-vanishing in its domain of definition. Moreover, it holds that

(3.18)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S_{\dot{\mu}+}(x)=S_{\dot{\mu}-}(x)e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}c_{\dot{\mu},k}},&x\in(b_{k},a_{k+1}),\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,g\},\\
|S_{\dot{\mu}\pm}(x)|^{2}=\dot{\mu}(x),&x\in\cup_{i=1}^{g}(a_{i},b_{i}).\end{array}\right.

The function
S_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}(z)
does not depend on the choice of a polynomial
p(z)
.

Proof.

Observe that

(3.19)

x^{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{g}l_{i}(x)\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{y^{j}\mathrm{d}y}{w(y)}\equiv 0

for any
j\in\{0,\ldots,g-1\}
. Indeed, (
3.19
) holds with
x^{j},y^{j}
replaced by
l_{k}(x),l_{k}(y)
for any
k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
by the very definition of the polynomials
l_{k}(x)
in (
3.5
). Since these polynomials are linearly independent, (
3.19
) follows. Given
p(z)
as above, (
3.19
) yields that

(3.20)

\frac{p(x)}{x-z_{l}}-\sum_{i=1}^{g}l_{i}(x)\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{p(y)}{y-z_{l}}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{w(y)}\equiv 0

for each
l\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, and that

(3.21)

\frac{p(x)}{x-z}-\sum_{i=1}^{g}l_{i}(x)\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{p(y)}{y-z}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{w(y)}=\mathcal{O}\left(z^{d-g-1}\right),\quad z\to\infty.

The first claim of the proposition holds because the term in square brackets in (
3.16
) vanishes at every zero
z_{l}
of
p(z)
by (
3.20
) and at infinity with order
g+1-d
by (
3.21
) (notice that
(w/p)(z)=z^{g+1-d}+\mathcal{O}(z^{g-d})
as
z\to\infty
).

The well-known boundary behavior of Cauchy integrals, see
[
5
, Section I.4]
, yields the first relation in (
3.18
). It also follows from
[
5
, Section I.4]
that
S_{\dot{\mu}+}(x)S_{\dot{\mu}-}(x)=\dot{\mu}(x)
. If the polynomial
p(z)
has real coefficients, then the obvious conjugate-symmetry implies the second relation in (
3.18
). To prove it in general, take a ratio of Szegő functions corresponding to two different polynomials (one having real coefficients). Call it
S(z)
and lift it to
\boldsymbol{D}
. Lift
S^{-1}(z)
to
\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
. Since
S_{+}S_{-}\equiv 1
on
\Delta
, the lifted function is in fact holomorphic on the whole surface
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
. That is, it is a constant. Because both,
S(z)
and
S^{-1}(z)
, are equal to this constant, the constant is either
1
or
-1
. Hence,
S_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}(z)
is independent of
p(z)
and, as relations (
3.18
) do not change if
S_{\dot{\mu}}(z)
is multiplied by
-1
, the second relation in (
3.18
) follows.
∎

In
[
11
, Section 5.2]
, the above construction was applied directly to the function
\rho(x)
, see (
3.2
)–(
3.3
). However, we use it only for
\dot{\mu}(x)
since we can then leverage the positivity of
\dot{\mu}(x)
, it makes the comparison with the case of classical Szegő functions (
g=0
) clearer, and because a Szegő function of a polynomial can be constructed differently using Riemann theta functions, see Section
3.8
. There it will become clear that (
3.16
) is not the only function which is non-vanishing and has boundary behavior as in (
3.18
). However, the ratio of any two such functions is equal to the exponential of a linear combination of holomorphic differentials with coefficients that are integer multiples of
2\pi\mathrm{i}
.

3.6.
Jacobi Inversion Problem

Recall that a divisor on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
is a formal linear combination of points from
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
with integer coefficients. Denote by
\mathrm{Jac}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}})
the Jacobi variety of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
, that is, the set of equivalence classes
[\vec{u}]
,
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{g}
, where
[\vec{u}]=[\vec{v}]
if and only if
\vec{u}-\vec{v}=\vec{j}+\mathsf{B}\vec{m}
for some
\vec{j},\vec{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}
and
\mathsf{B}
was defined in (
3.7
). Abel’s map from the divisors of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
onto
\mathrm{Jac}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}})
is defined by

(3.22)

\sum n_{j}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\mapsto\left[\sum\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{g+1}}^{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\right],

where
\vec{\mathcal{H}}:=(\mathcal{H}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{g})^{\mathsf{T}}
is the column vector of the holomorphic differentials on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
, see (
3.6
) (since the difference of two paths with the same endpoints is homologous to a linear combination of the cycles of the homology basis with integer coefficients, this map is indeed well-defined).

Let
\vec{\omega}_{n}:=(\omega_{n,1},\ldots,\omega_{n,g})^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\vec{c}_{\dot{\mu}}:=(c_{\dot{\mu},1},\ldots,c_{\dot{\mu},g})^{\mathsf{T}}
be the column vectors of real constants defined in (
3.11
) and (
3.17
), respectively. Further, let
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
be such that
\pi({\boldsymbol{s}}_{i})\in(b_{i},a_{i+1})
and is a zero of the polynomial
m(x)
from (
3.3
),
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
. We are interested in the solutions of the following Jacobi inversion problem:
find a divisor
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
such that

(3.23)

\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g}\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{g+1}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\right]=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g}\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{g+1}}^{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}+\vec{c}_{\dot{\mu}}+\vec{\omega}_{n}\right].

It is known that (
3.23
) is always solvable and the solution is unique up to a principal divisor (divisor of a rational function on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
). That is, if
\mathcal{D}_{n}-\big\{\mbox{ principal divisor }\big\}
is an effective divisor (all the coefficients are positive), then it also solves (
3.23
). Immediately one can see that the subtracted principal divisor should have a positive part of degree at most
g
. As
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
is hyperelliptic, such divisors come solely from rational functions on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
lifted to
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}

[
7
, Appendix 1]
. In particular, such principal divisors are involution-symmetric. Hence, if a solution of (
3.23
) contains at least one involution-symmetric pair of points, then replacing this pair by another such pair produces a different solution of (
3.23
). However, if a solution does not contain such a pair, then it solves (
3.23
) uniquely. Hence, in the case where (
3.23
) has multiple solutions, we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{n}
the one whose involution-symmetric pairs are all of the form
\boldsymbol{\infty}+\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}
, where
\boldsymbol{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}
are the points on top of infinity in
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
, respectively. In particular, if
k_{n}
is the number of involution-symmetric pairs within a given solution of (
3.23
), then
\mathcal{D}_{n}-k_{n}\boldsymbol{\infty}-k_{n}\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}
is a common part of every solution of (
3.23
).

Proposition 3.3
.

With an appropriate labeling, solution
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
of (
3.23
) is such that
\pi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i})\in[b_{i},a_{i+1}]
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
(in particular, there are no other solutions).

Proof.

Recall that
\pi({\boldsymbol{s}}_{i})\in(b_{i},a_{i+1})
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
, and observe that (
3.23
) can be rewritten as

(3.24)

\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g}\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\right]=\left[\vec{V}_{n}\right],\quad\vec{V}_{n}:=\vec{V}+\vec{c}_{\dot{\mu}}+\vec{\omega}_{n},\quad\vec{V}:=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\int_{[\boldsymbol{b}_{i},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}]}\vec{\mathcal{H}},

where the path of integration
[\boldsymbol{b}_{i},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}]
is a “segment” on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
, that is,
\pi:[\boldsymbol{b}_{i},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}]\to[b_{i},s_{i}]
is a bijection. In this case (
3.6
) immediately shows that the vector
\vec{V}
and therefore the vectors
\vec{V}_{n}
have real entries. Denote by
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}
the point on the same sheet of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
as
{\boldsymbol{z}}
with
\pi(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})=\bar{z}
if
{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
if
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}
. Since the polynomials
l_{i}(x)
have real coefficients, it holds that

\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}=\overline{\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}}

by (
3.6
), where the paths of integration are reflections of each other under the map
{\boldsymbol{s}}\mapsto\bar{\boldsymbol{s}}
. Thus, since
\vec{V}_{n}
is a real vector, if
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\sum{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
solves (
3.24
), so does
\sum\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
. As explained just before the proposition, it must holds that
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\sum\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
. Now, let us partially fix the labeling of the points of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Namely, if a cycle
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}
contains at least one point of the divisor
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, we label one of these points as
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,k}
and distribute indices
i
arbitrarily to the rest of elements of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Further, let
I_{1}\cup I_{2}\cup I_{3}\cup I_{4}:=\{1,\ldots,g\}
be disjoint sets such that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
for
i\in I_{1}
,
\pi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i})\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
for
i\in I_{2}
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
for some
j\neq i
when
i\in I_{3}
(notice that necessarily
j\in I_{1}
in this case), and for each
i\in I_{4}
there exists
j\in I_{4}
such that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,j}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
. Then
\vec{V}_{n,i}:=\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{g}
when
i\in I_{1}
. Moreover,

\left[\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\right]=\left[\vec{V}_{n,i}+\frac{1}{2}\big[\mathsf{B}\big]_{i}\right],\quad\vec{V}_{n,i}:=\int_{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{g},

for
i\in I_{2}
, where
\big[\mathsf{B}\big]_{i}
is the
i
-th column of
\mathsf{B}
and the path of integration in the last integral has a natural projection that belongs to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
(it is allowed to pass through points on top of infinity). Further,

\left[\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\right]=\left[\vec{V}_{n,i}+\frac{1}{2}\big[\mathsf{B}\big]_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\big[\mathsf{B}\big]_{j}\right],\quad\vec{V}_{n,i}:=\int_{[\boldsymbol{b}_{j},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}]}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{g},

for
i\in I_{3}
, where
j
is such that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
and one needs to notice that adding rather than subtracting half a column of
\mathsf{B}
does not change the point on the Jacobi variety as vectors that differ by a linear combination of the columns of
\mathsf{B}
with integer coefficients define the same element of
\mathrm{Jac}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}})
. Next, let
i,j\in I_{4}
be such that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,j}
. Then

\left[\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}+\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,j}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\right]=\left[\vec{V}_{n,i}+\frac{1}{2}\big[\mathsf{B}\big]_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\big[\mathsf{B}\big]_{j}\right],\quad\vec{V}_{n,i}=2\Re\left(\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}}\vec{\mathcal{H}}\right).

Denote by
d_{i}+1
the number of elements of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
that belong to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
for
i\in I_{1}
, and let
I_{5}
be the largest subset of
I_{4}
such that for any
i\in I_{5}
there exists
j\in I_{4}\setminus I_{5}
for which
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,j}
and
j<i
. Then it follows from (
3.24
) that

\left[\vec{V}_{n}\right]=\left[\sum_{i\in I_{1}\cup I_{2}\cup I_{3}\cup I_{5}}\vec{V}_{n,i}+\vec{U}\right],\quad\vec{U}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{1}}d_{i}\big[\mathsf{B}\big]_{i}+\sum_{i\in I_{2}\cup I_{3}\cup I_{4}}\big[\mathsf{B}\big]_{i}\right).

Recall that the entries of
\mathsf{B}
are purely imaginary and that the columns of
\mathsf{B}
are linearly independent. Thus,
\vec{U}\in(\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R})^{g}
and
[\vec{U}]=[\vec{0}]
only if the sets
I_{2}=I_{3}=I_{4}=\varnothing
, which then yields that each
d_{i}=0
. This observation finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Proposition
3.3
did not appear in
[
11
]
since there are no reasons to believe that solutions of (
3.23
) are confined to a certain subset of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}^{g}
for more general geometries.

3.7.
Riemann Theta Function

Let
\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})
be a function defined by

(3.25)

\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\int_{\boldsymbol{b}_{g+1}}^{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\vec{\mathcal{H}},\quad{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{g}\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\cup\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}\},

where the path of integration can be any as the domain of definition is simply connected. This function has continuous traces on the cycles of the homology basis (away from the points of intersection of different cycles) that satisfy

(3.26)

\mathfrak{a}_{+}({\boldsymbol{s}})-\mathfrak{a}_{-}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\mathsf{B}\vec{e}_{k},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k},\\
\vec{e}_{k},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k},\end{array}\right.\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,g\},

by (
3.7
) and the normalization of
\vec{\mathcal{H}}
.

The theta function associated with
\mathsf{B}
is an entire transcendental function of
g
complex variables defined by

\theta\left(\vec{u}\right):=\sum_{\vec{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}}\exp\bigg\{\pi\mathrm{i}\vec{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{B}\vec{n}+2\pi\mathrm{i}\vec{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{u}\bigg\},\quad\vec{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{g}.

As shown by Riemann, the symmetry of
\mathsf{B}
and positive definiteness of its imaginary part ensures the convergence of the series for any
\vec{u}
. It can be directly checked that
\theta(-\vec{u})=\theta(\vec{u})
and it enjoys the following periodicity property:

(3.27)

\theta\left(\vec{u}+\vec{j}+\mathsf{B}\vec{m}\right)=\exp\bigg\{-\pi\mathrm{i}\vec{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{B}\vec{m}-2\pi\mathrm{i}\vec{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{u}\bigg\}\theta\big(\vec{u}\big),\quad\vec{j},\vec{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}.

It is also known that
\theta\left(\vec{u}\right)=0
if and only if
\big[\vec{u}-\vec{K}\big]
is the image of some effective divisor of degree
g-1
under Abel’s map (
3.22
), where
\vec{K}
is a fixed vector known as the vector of Riemann constants (it can be explicitly defined via
\vec{\mathcal{H}}
).

Let
\vec{V}=:(V_{1},\ldots,V_{g})^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\vec{V}_{n}=:(V_{n,1},\ldots,V_{n,g})^{\mathsf{T}}
be given by (
3.24
). Set

(3.28)

\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\frac{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\vec{V}_{n}-\vec{K}\right)}{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\vec{V}-\vec{K}\right)},

which is a multiplicatively multi-valued meromorphic function on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
with a simple zero at each
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
, a simple pole at each
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
, and otherwise non-vanishing and finite. In fact, it follows from (
3.26
) and (
3.27
) that it is holomorphic, non-vanishing, and single-valued in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{g}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
and for
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}
it holds that

(3.29)

\Theta_{n+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\Theta_{n-}({\boldsymbol{s}})\exp\left\{2\pi\mathrm{i}\big(V_{k}-V_{n,k}\big)\right\}=\Theta_{n-}({\boldsymbol{s}})\exp\left\{-2\pi\mathrm{i}(c_{\dot{\mu},k}+\omega_{n,k})\right\}

(these traces do vanish at
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
’s and blow up at
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}
’s).

The functions
\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
form a normal family in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. Indeed, for any subsequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k}
there always exists a subsequence
\{n_{k_{i}}\}_{i}
along which the vectors
\vec{V}_{n_{k_{i}}}
converge to some vector
\vec{V}_{*}
since these vectors form a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{g}
by (
3.11
) and (
3.24
). Then the corresponding limit point is obtained be replacing
\vec{V}_{n}
with
\vec{V}_{*}
in (
3.28
) and is holomorphic and non-vanishing in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
(its zeroes must belong to the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-cycles). Hence, according to Montel’s theorem, the functions
\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
are uniformly bounded on closed subsets of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. Moreover, since their zeroes belong to the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-cycles, the above argument also shows that the reciprocals of the functions
\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
also form a normal family in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and therefore the functions themselves are uniformly bounded away from zero on closed subsets of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Recall the notation
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}
introduced after (
3.24
). There exists a constant
c_{n}
such that

(3.30)

\overline{\Theta_{n}(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})}=c_{n}\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\quad{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}.

Indeed, functions
\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
and
\overline{\Theta_{n}(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})}
are both holomorphic in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, have the same jumps across the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-cycles (the fact that
\vec{c}_{\dot{\mu}}+\vec{\omega}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{g}
is important here), and have the same poles and zeroes (points on the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-cycles remain unchanged under the map
{\boldsymbol{s}}\mapsto\bar{\boldsymbol{s}}
). Thus, their ratio is holomorphic on the whole surface
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
and therefore is a constant.

Proposition 3.4
.

For each natural number
n
,
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
, and
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}
, let

(3.31)

T_{n}(z):=\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})\Theta_{n}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{z}}).

Then
T_{n}(z)
is holomorphic and non-vanishing in its domain of definition and

(3.32)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T_{n+}(x)=T_{n-}(x)e^{4\pi\mathrm{i}(c_{\dot{\mu},k}+\omega_{n,k})},&x\in(b_{k},a_{k+1}),\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,g\},\\
|T_{n\pm}(x)|\equiv 1,&x\in\Delta.\end{array}\right.

Hence,
T_{n}(z)
can be analytically continued through each gap
(b_{i},a_{i+1})
and any such continuation has a simple pole at
s_{i}
, a simple pole at
x_{n,i}
if
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}\in\boldsymbol{D}
, and a simple zero at
x_{n,i}
if
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
(if
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
is a ramification point of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
then
T_{n}(z)
has a non-zero finite limit at
x_{n,i}
). Moreover, the families
\big\{T_{n}(z)\big\}_{n}
and
\big\{T_{n}(z)^{-1}\big\}_{n}
are normal in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
.

Proof.

The properties of the functions
T_{n}(z)
follow immediately from the corresponding properties of the functions
\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
except for the second identity of (
3.32
). To derive it, notice that on the one hand
T_{n}(z)=\overline{T_{n}(\bar{z})}
by (
3.30
) and therefore
T_{n\pm}(x)=\overline{T_{n\mp}(x)}
for
x\in\Delta
, and on the other,
T_{n\pm}(x)=1/T_{n\mp}(x)
,
x\in\Delta
, by the very definition (
3.31
). These two equalities together do yield the desired claim. ∎

3.8.
Szegő Functions of Polynomials

Take
q(z)=z-e
,
e\not\in\Delta
. As usual, let
{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
be such that
\pi({\boldsymbol{e}})=e
and let
\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{e}})
be given by (
3.25
), where with a slight abuse of notation we set
\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{e}}):=\mathfrak{a}_{+}({\boldsymbol{e}})
if
{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\cup_{i=1}^{g}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
. Define

S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\frac{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*})-\vec{K}\right)}{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{e}})-\vec{K}\right)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1,&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D},\\
q(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{*},\end{array}\right.

where, as before,
\boldsymbol{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}
are the points on top infinity that belong to
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{D}^{*}
, respectively. This function is holomorphic and non-vanishing on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Delta}
(notice that both the numerator and the denominator of the fraction above have a zero of order
g-1
at
\boldsymbol{b}_{g+1}
as can be easily seen from (
3.22
) and (
3.25
)), whose traces on
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
and the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-cycles satisfy

S_{e+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=S_{e-}({\boldsymbol{s}})\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}q(s),&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta},\\
e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{k}({\boldsymbol{e}})-\mathfrak{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}))},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k},\;\;k\in\{1,\ldots,g\},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathfrak{a}_{k}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is the
k
-th component of the vector
\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})
and we exclude the points of intersection of different cycles. It is quite straightforward to see that the product
S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})
is continuous across both
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
and the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-cycles and therefore is an entire function on the whole surface. Thus,
S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})\equiv S_{e}^{2}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1})
on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
. Hence, if we put
S_{q}(z):=S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})/S_{e}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1})
,
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}
, then
S_{q}(z)
is holomorphic and non-vanishing in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
and satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S_{q+}(x)S_{q-}(x)=q(x),&x\in\Delta,\\
S_{q+}(x)=S_{q-}(x)e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{k}({\boldsymbol{e}})-\mathfrak{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}))},&x\in(b_{k},a_{k+1}),\;\;k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}.\end{array}\right.

Now, if
e\in(-\infty,\infty)\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
, one can see from (
3.6
) and (
3.25
) that the constant
\mathfrak{a}_{k}({\boldsymbol{e}})-\mathfrak{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*})
is real. In this case, similarly to (
3.30
), one can check that
\overline{S_{e}(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})}=c_{e}S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})
and therefore
S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})/S_{e}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1})=\overline{S_{e}(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})}/\overline{S_{e}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1})}
. Hence, it additionally holds that
|S_{q\pm}(x)|^{2}=q(x)
for
x\in\Delta
. In the same way one can also check that the last equality also holds when
q(z)=(z-e)(z-\bar{e})
(in this case we define
S_{q}(z)
as a product of
S_{\cdot-e}(z)
and
S_{\cdot-\bar{e}}(z)
).

The non-uniqueness of the above construction stems from the vectors
\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*})
and
\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{e}})
as they can be replaced by any pair
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
such that
[\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*})]=[\vec{u}]
and
[\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{e}})]=[\vec{v}]
. Such a substitution will lead to a different function
S_{q}(z)
with the same jump relation on
\Delta
, but different constant jumps in the gaps. Clearly, the ratio of two such Szegő functions is equal to a constant multiple of
\exp\{2\pi\mathrm{i}{\vec{m}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})\}
for some
\vec{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}
as can be seen from (
3.27
).

The above construction also leads to a formula for
S_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
different from (
3.13
). Indeed, up to multiplication by a constant multiple of
\exp\{2\pi\mathrm{i}{\vec{m}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})\}
,
\vec{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}
,
S_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is equal to

\left(\prod_{e\in E_{n},~|e|<\infty}S_{e}({\boldsymbol{z}})\right)\left(\frac{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*})-\vec{K}\right)}{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty})-\vec{K}\right)}\right)^{n}.

3.9.
Main Theorem

Recall (
3.1
) and Propositions
3.1
–
3.4
. An analog of Theorem
2.1
in the multi-cut case can be stated as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f(z)
be given by (
2.1
) and (
3.2
)–(
3.4
). Further, let
p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
be the multipoint Padé approximant of
f(z)
associated with
E_{n}
, where the interpolation sets
E_{n}
are conjugate-symmetric and there exists a neighborhood of
[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
disjoint from all the multisets
E_{n}
. Then it holds that

f(z)-\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}=(2+o(1))(T_{n}\psi_{n})(z)\frac{\big(mS_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}\big)(z)}{w(z)},

as
n\to\infty
locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
.

Recall that
\psi_{n}(z)
satisfies
|\psi_{n}(z)|<1
,
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
, and that it has
2n
zeroes there. Since these zeroes are separated from
\Delta
for all
n
, the functions
\psi_{n}(z)
converge to zero geometrically fast in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
. Recall also that the functions
T_{n}(z)
are uniformly bounded away from infinity and zero on closed subsets of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
.

Proof.

Let
S_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
,
S_{\dot{\mu}}(z)
, and
\Theta_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
be given by (
3.13
), (
3.16
), and (
3.28
), respectively. Define

(3.33)

\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=(S_{n}\Theta_{n})({\boldsymbol{z}})\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1/S_{\dot{\mu}}(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D},\\
(mS_{\dot{\mu}})(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{*}.\end{array}\right.

It holds that
\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is a meromorphic function in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Delta}
with a pole of order
n
at
\boldsymbol{\infty}
, a zero or order
n-g
at
\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}
, a simple zero at each
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
, and otherwise non-vanishing and finite. Moreover, it has continuous traces on
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
that satisfy

(3.34)

\Psi_{n+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\Psi_{n-}({\boldsymbol{s}})(v_{n}/\rho)(x),\quad{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta},

by (
3.3
), (
3.15
), (
3.18
), and (
3.29
). Hence,
\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is exactly the function from
[
11
, Proposition 3.3]
. Further, let
\vec{W}_{n}:=\vec{V}_{n}+\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*})-\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty})
, see (
3.24
). Define

(3.35)

\Upsilon_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\frac{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty})-\vec{K}\right)}{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*})-\vec{K}\right)}\frac{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\vec{W}_{n}-\vec{K}\right)}{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\vec{V}_{n}-\vec{K}\right)}.

Then it follows from (
3.24
) and (
3.27
) that
\Upsilon_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is a rational function on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
with poles only at
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,g},\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}
, which are simple, and a zero at
\boldsymbol{\infty}
(the rest of
g
zeroes are uniquely determined by the corresponding Jacobi inversion problem). This is exactly the function from
[
11
, Proposition 3.4]
. Observe also that an argument virtually similar to the one after (
3.29
) shows that the functions
\Upsilon_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
form a normal family in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}\}
.

Let
\gamma_{n}
be a constant such that
\lim_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\to\boldsymbol{\infty}}\gamma_{n}z^{-n}\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})=1
. Recall also the definition of
R_{n}(z)
in (
1.1
). It was shown in
[
11
, Theorem 3.7]
that

(3.36)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}q_{n}(z)&=&\gamma_{n}\big(1+o(1)+o(1)\Upsilon_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})\big)\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\\
(wR_{n})(z)&=&\gamma_{n}\big(2+o(1)+o(1)\Upsilon_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})\big)\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}),\end{array}\right.

locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
, where
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{D}
and the error terms
o(1)
vanish at infinity. The normality of
\Upsilon_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
and the fact that each of them has a simple pole at
\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}
while the error functions are holomorphic and vanish at infinity coupled with the maximum modulus principle show that we can write

(3.37)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}q_{n}(z)&=&\gamma_{n}\big(1+o(1)\big)\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\\
(wR_{n})(z)&=&\gamma_{n}\big(2+o(1)\big)\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}),\end{array}\right.

locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
. The desired claim now follows from the very definitions of
R_{n}(z)
and
\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
, (
3.14
), and (
3.31
).
∎

4.
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-approximation of Markov Functions On Several Intervals

Assume now in addition to (
3.2
)–(
3.4
) that
\Delta\subset(-1,1)
.

4.1.
Auxiliary Polynomials

Given
w(z)
as in (
3.1
), set

(4.1)

\tilde{w}(z):=z^{g+1}w(1/z),

which is holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta^{-1}
and normalized so that
\tilde{w}(0)=1
(in particular,
\tilde{w}(x)>0
for
x\in(-1,1)
). Let
\mathsf{W}
be the following matrix:

(4.2)

\mathsf{W}:=\left[\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\big(x+x^{-1}\big)^{l}\frac{x^{g}\mathrm{d}x}{(w\tilde{w})(x)}\right]_{i=1,l=1}^{g,g},

where
i
is the row index and
l
is the column one (powers
l
do go up to
g
but exclude
0
). Let us abbreviate
y_{l}=x_{l}+x_{l}^{-1}
. Then

\mathrm{det}(\mathsf{W})=\int_{b_{1}}^{a_{2}}\cdots\int_{b_{g}}^{a_{g+1}}V\big(y_{1},\ldots,y_{g}\big)\frac{(y_{g}\cdots y_{1})(x_{g}^{g}\cdots x_{1}^{g})}{(w\tilde{w})(x_{g})\cdots(w\tilde{w})(x_{1})}\mathrm{d}x_{g}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{1},

where, as before,
V(y_{1},\ldots,y_{g})
is the Vandermonde determinant.

If
0\in\Delta
, then it is clear that
\mathrm{det}(\mathsf{W})\neq 0
since all the gaps
(b_{k},a_{k+1})
are disjoint and the Jukovski map
x+x^{-1}
preserves this property. In this case we can set

(4.3)

\ell_{j}(x):=x^{g}\sum_{i=1}^{g}\ell_{ij}\big(x+x^{-1}\big)^{i}=h_{j}\prod_{i=1}^{g}(x-x_{ij})(1-xx_{ij}),

where
[\ell_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{g}=\mathsf{W}^{-1}
and we agree that
|x_{ij}|\leq 1
. Then these polynomials satisfy

(4.4)

\int_{b_{k}}^{a_{k+1}}\frac{\ell_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{(w\tilde{w})(x)}=\delta_{kj},\quad k,j\in\{1,\ldots,g\}.

Since each
\ell_{j}(x)
has real coefficients, it has at least one zero in each gap
(b_{k},a_{k+1})
,
k\neq j
.

To see that polynomials satisfying (
4.4
) exist even if
0\not\in\Delta
, observe that they can be carried forward and backward by Möbius transformations. Indeed, let
\ell(t)=h\prod_{i=1}^{g}(t-t_{i})(1-tt_{i})
be a symmetric polynomial of degree
2g
and
t(x)=(x-x_{0})/(1-xx_{0})
be a Möbius transformation that carries a system of intervals
\Delta_{1}
into another system
\Delta_{2}
. Then

\ell(t)=h\prod_{i=1}^{g}\left(\frac{1-x_{0}^{2}}{1-x_{i}x_{0}}\right)^{2}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{g}(x-x_{i})(1-xx_{i})}{(1-xx_{0})^{2g}},

where
t_{i}=t(x_{i})
. Let
w_{\Delta_{i}}(x)
and
\tilde{w}_{\Delta_{i}}(x)
be defined via (
3.1
) and (
4.1
), respectively, with respect to the system of intervals
\Delta_{i}
. Then

(w_{\Delta_{2}}\tilde{w}_{\Delta_{2}})(t)=\prod_{k=1}^{g+1}\frac{(1-x_{0}^{2})^{2}}{(1-a_{k}x_{0})(1-b_{k}x_{0})}\frac{(w_{\Delta_{1}}\tilde{w}_{\Delta_{1}})(x)}{(1-xx_{0})^{2g+2}}.

Since
\mathrm{d}t=(1-x_{0}^{2})(1-xx_{0})^{-2}\mathrm{d}x
, it holds that

\int_{t(c)}^{t(d)}\frac{\ell(t)\mathrm{d}t}{(w_{\Delta_{2}}\tilde{w}_{\Delta_{2}})(t)}=\int_{c}^{d}\frac{\ell_{*}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{(w_{\Delta_{1}}\tilde{w}_{\Delta_{1}})(x)},

where
\ell_{*}(x):=h_{*}\prod_{i=1}^{g}(x-x_{i})(1-xx_{i})
and
h_{*}:=h(1-x_{0}^{2})\prod_{k=1}^{g+1}\frac{(1-a_{k}x_{0})(1-b_{k}x_{0})}{(1-x_{k-1}x_{0})^{2}}
. Then, by applying to
\Delta
a Möbius transformation that sends a point from
\Delta
to the origin, constructing polynomials satisfying (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) for the image of
\Delta
, and carrying them back by the inverse Möbius transformation, we shall construct the desired polynomials
\ell_{i}(x)
for
\Delta
.

4.2.
Condenser Map

Let
u(x)
be a symmetric polynomial of degree
2g
such that

(4.5)

\int_{\Delta}\frac{u(x)\mathrm{d}x}{(w_{+}\tilde{w})(x)}=-\mathrm{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{b_{k}}^{a_{k+1}}\frac{u(x)\mathrm{d}x}{(w\tilde{w})(x)}=0,\;\;k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}.

It can be readily checked that

(4.6)

u(x)=u_{0}\left(x^{g}-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left(\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{y^{g}\mathrm{d}y}{(w\tilde{w})(y)}\right)\ell_{i}(x)\right)=c\prod_{i=1}^{g}(x-x_{i})(1-x_{i}x),

where the constant
c
(and therefore
u_{0}
) is chosen so that the first condition in (
4.5
) is fulfilled and we agree that
|x_{i}|\leq 1
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
. Observe that each gap
(b_{k},a_{k+1})
must contain exactly one zero of of
u(x)
, say
x_{k}
. In particular, since
w_{+}(x)=(-1)^{g+1-k}\mathrm{i}|w(x)|
for
x\in(a_{k},b_{k})
, it holds that
(-1)^{g+1-k}u(x)>0
for
x\in(a_{k},b_{k})
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,g+1\}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Define

(4.7)

\varphi(z):=\exp\left\{\pi\int_{1}^{z}\frac{u(s)\mathrm{d}s}{(w\tilde{w})(s)}\right\},

where the path of integration lies entirely in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a_{1},b_{g+1}]\cup[a_{1},b_{g+1}]^{-1}\big)
. The function
\varphi(z)
is well-defined and holomorphic in its domain of definition and satisfies

(4.8)

\varphi(\bar{z})=\overline{\varphi(z)}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(1/z)=1/\varphi(z).

Moreover, the increment of the argument of
\varphi(z)
along the unit circle is equal to
2\pi
and it holds that

(4.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\varphi(\tau)|\equiv 1,&\tau\in\mathbb{T},\\
|\varphi(x)|\equiv\rho^{\pm 1},&x\in\Delta^{\pm 1},\;\;\rho:=\varphi(b_{g+1})<1.\end{array}\right.

Furthermore,
\varphi(z)
has continuous traces in the gaps
\cup_{i=1}^{g}(b_{i},a_{i+1})
that satisfy

(4.10)

\varphi_{+}(x)=\varphi_{-}(x)e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}},\quad\omega_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}\mathrm{d}\omega_{\Delta,\mathbb{T}},

for
x\in(b_{k},a_{k+1})
and each
k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
, where the probability measure
\omega_{\Delta,\mathbb{T}}
is given by

(4.11)

\mathrm{d}\omega_{\Delta,\mathbb{T}}(x):=\frac{|u(x)|\mathrm{d}x}{|(w\tilde{w})(x)|}=\frac{\mathrm{i}u(x)\mathrm{d}x}{(w_{+}\tilde{w})(x)},\quad x\in\Delta.

Proof.

The function
\varphi(z)
is well-defined due to the first requirement in (
4.5
). Relations (
4.8
) follow from the conjugate-symmetry of the integrand as well as the symmetry with respect to
s\mapsto 1/s
(here, one needs to include the differential
\mathrm{d}s
as well). The first relation in (
4.9
) is a direct consequence of (
4.8
) and to get the second relation one needs to use the second requirement in (
4.5
) and the observation that the integrand in (
4.7
) is purely imaginary on each side of
\Delta
. The claim about the increment of the argument follows directly from (
4.5
). Relations (
4.10
) can be verified via a direct computation.
∎

The probability measure
\omega_{\Delta,\mathbb{T}}
can be interpreted from the point of view of potential theory as the condenser equilibrium distribution on
\Delta
of the condenser
(\Delta,\mathbb{T})
. Moreover, since the traces
\varphi_{\pm}(x)
have constant argument in each gap
(b_{i},a_{i+1})
,
\varphi(z)
is a conformal map of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a_{1},b_{g+1}]\cup[a_{1},b_{g+1}]^{-1}\big)
into an annulus
\{\rho<|z|<\rho^{-1}\}
with “internal spikes”.

4.3.
Condenser Szegő Function

Set

(4.12)

K(z;x):=\frac{1}{x-z}+\frac{x}{1-xz}=\frac{1-2xz+x^{2}}{(x-z)(1-xz)}

to be the condenser version of the Cauchy kernel. Given a Hölder-smooth function
\lambda(x)
on
\Delta
that is positive away from the endpoints
\{a_{i},b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{g+1}
and has integrable logarithm, let

(4.13)

D_{\lambda}(z):=\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{(w\tilde{w})(z)}{u(z)}\left[\int_{\Delta}K(z;x)\log\left(\frac{\lambda(x)}{G_{\lambda}}\right)\frac{u(x)\mathrm{d}x}{(w_{+}\tilde{w})(x)}-\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.\sum_{i=1}^{g}\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}2\pi\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\lambda,i}K(z;y)\frac{u(y)\mathrm{d}y}{(w\tilde{w})(y)}\right]\right\}

for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a_{1},b_{g+1}]\cup[a_{1},b_{g+1}]^{-1}\big)
, where the constants
G_{\lambda}
and
k_{\lambda,i}
are given by

(4.14)

G_{\lambda}:=\exp\left\{\int\log\lambda(x)\mathrm{d}\omega_{\Delta,\mathbb{T}}(x)\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa_{\lambda,i}:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Delta}\log\left(\frac{\lambda(x)}{G_{\lambda}}\right)\frac{\ell_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{(w_{+}\tilde{w})(x)}.

Proposition 4.2
.

It holds that
D_{\lambda}(z)
is a well-defined non-vanishing holomorphic function in its domain of definition. It has continuous traces on each open subinterval of
\Delta
and in each gap that satisfy

(4.15)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}G_{\lambda}|D_{\lambda\pm}(x)|^{2}=\lambda(x),&x\in\cup_{i=1}^{g}(a_{i},b_{i}),\\
D_{\lambda+}(x)=D_{\lambda-}(x)e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\lambda,k}},&x\in(b_{k},a_{k+1}),\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}.\end{array}\right.

Furthermore, the increment of the argument of
D_{\lambda}(z)
along the unit circle is equal to zero and it holds that

(4.16)

D_{\lambda}(1/z)=1/D_{\lambda}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad|D_{\lambda}(\tau)|\equiv 1,\;\;\tau\in\mathbb{T}.

Moreover, if
\lambda(x)=|x-e|^{\alpha}\lambda_{*}(x)
, where
e\in\{a_{i},b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{g+1}
and
\lambda_{*}(x)
is Hölder-smooth, then

(4.17)

|D_{\lambda}(z)|^{2}=|z-e|^{\alpha}|D_{*}(z)|^{2},

where the function
D_{*}(z)
is bounded and non-vanishing in some neighborhood of
e
(including the traces on
\Delta
).

Proof.

To prove the first claim, it is enough to investigate what happens in the unit disk only as
D_{\lambda}(1/z)=1/D_{\lambda}(z)
. Indeed, we have that
(w\tilde{w}/u)(1/z)=(1/z^{2})(w\tilde{w}/u)(z)
and
(1/z^{2})K(1/z;x)=(2/z)-K(z;x)
. Since the integrals in (
4.13
) with
K(z;x)
replaced by
1/z
vanish due to (
4.5
), (
4.11
), and the choice of
G_{\lambda}
in (
4.14
), the claim follows.

To continue, let us make the following observation: it follows from (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) that

\ell_{k}(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\ell_{i}(x)\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{\ell_{k}(y)\mathrm{d}y}{(w\tilde{w})(y)}\equiv 0

for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
. Further, let
p(x)
be any symmetric polynomial of degree
2g
, which we can write as,
p(x)=p_{0}x^{g}+\sum_{k=1}^{g}c_{k}(p)\ell_{k}(x)
for some constants
c_{k}(p)
. The previous identity then yields that

(4.18)

p(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\ell_{i}(x)\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{p(y)\mathrm{d}y}{(w\tilde{w})(y)}=p_{0}\left(x^{g}-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\ell_{i}(x)\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{y^{g}\mathrm{d}y}{(w\tilde{w})(y)}\right)=\frac{p_{0}}{u_{0}}u(x),

where
u(x)
was defined in (
4.6
).

Going back to the proof of the first claim, observe that
K(z;x)-(z-x)^{-1}
is an analytic function of
z\in\mathbb{D}
for each
x\in\Delta
, see (
4.12
). Therefore,
D_{\lambda}(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
away from the zeroes of
u(x)
. However, it is analytic at those zeroes as well since the term in square brackets vanishes at them. Indeed, let
x_{j}
be such a zero. Observe that
u(x)K(x_{j};x)
is a symmetric polynomial of degree
2g
according to (
4.12
). Therefore, the term in square brackets is equal to

\int_{\Delta}\left(u(x)K(x_{j};x)-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\ell_{i}(x)\int_{b_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{u(y)K(x_{j};y)\mathrm{d}y}{(w\tilde{w})(y)}\right)\log\left(\frac{\lambda(x)}{G_{\lambda}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{(w_{+}\tilde{w})(x)}=\\
\frac{h_{j}}{u_{0}}\int_{\Delta}u(x)\log\left(\frac{\lambda(x)}{G_{\lambda}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{(w_{+}\tilde{w})(x)}=0

by (
4.18
) and the choice of
G_{\lambda}
in (
4.14
), where
h_{j}
is the coefficient next to
x^{g}
of
u(x)K(x_{j};x)
. Hence,
D_{\lambda}(z)
is indeed holomorphic and non-vanishing in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a_{1},b_{g+1}]\cup[a_{1},b_{g+1}]^{-1}\big)
.

Relations (
4.15
) and (
4.17
) hold due to known behavior of Cauchy integrals and the fact that
K(z;x)
is “essentially” a Cauchy kernel,
[
5
, Sections 4.2 and 8.6]
.

The first identity in (
4.16
) has already been explained and the second one follows from the first and the fact that
D_{\lambda}(\bar{z})=\overline{D_{\lambda}(z)}
.

It only remains to prove the claim about the increment of the argument of
D_{\lambda}(z)
, say
I
, along
\mathbb{T}
(the proof is an adjustment of the one in
[
2
, Lemmas 2.40 and 5.2]
).
I
is equal to the integral of the tangential derivative of the argument of
D_{\lambda}(z)
over the unit circle (oriented counter-clockwise). Due to Cauchy-Riemann relations we have that
I=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\mathbb{T}}(\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}h)(s)|\mathrm{d}s|
, where
\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}
is the partial derivative on
\partial U
with respect to the inner normal and
h(z):=\log|D_{\lambda}(z)|
is harmonic in
U:=\mathbb{D}\setminus\Delta
(harmonicity across the gaps follows from the second relation in (
4.15
) and the fact that constants
\kappa_{\lambda,i}
are real). Then it follows from Green’s formula that

\oint_{\partial U}g(s)(\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}h)(s)|\mathrm{d}s|=\oint_{\partial U}h(s)(\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}g)(s)|\mathrm{d}s|,

where
g(z):=\log|\varphi(z)/\rho|
is also a harmonic function in
U
, see (
4.7
) and (
4.9
). Since
h(x)=(1/2)\log(\lambda/G_{\lambda})(x)
for
x\in\Delta
and
h(z)\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{T}
by (
4.15
) and (
4.16
), and
g(z)\equiv 0
on
\Delta
and
g(z)\equiv-\log\rho
on
\mathbb{T}
by (
4.9
), it holds that

I=\frac{-1}{4\pi\log\rho}\int_{\Delta}\log\left(\frac{\lambda(x)}{G_{\lambda}}\right)\big(\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}+}g+\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}-}g)(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Now, it readily follows from (
4.7
) that

(\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}\pm}g)(x)=\left.\pm\pi\partial_{y}\Re\left(\int_{1}^{z}\frac{u(s)\mathrm{d}s}{(w\tilde{w})(s)}\right)\right|_{z=x}=\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}u(x)}{(w_{+}\tilde{w})(x)}.

Plugging the above expression in the last formula for
I
and recalling (
4.11
) gives us

I=\frac{-1}{2\log\rho}\int_{\Delta}\log\left(\frac{\lambda(x)}{G_{\lambda}}\right)\mathrm{d}\omega_{\Delta,\mathbb{T}}(x)=0,

where we used (
4.14
) for the last equality.
∎

4.4.
Main Theorem

Let
f(z)
be given by (
2.1
) and (
3.2
)–(
3.4
) and
\{r_{n}(z)\}
be a sequence of irreducible critical points in rational
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-approximation of
f(z)
. Write
r_{n}(z)=(p_{n}/q_{n})(z)
. Recall that
q_{n}(z)
satisfies (
2.2
) with

(4.19)

v_{n}(z)=\varkappa_{n}\tilde{q}_{n}^{2}(z),

where
\tilde{q}_{n}(z)=z^{n}q_{n}(1/z)
and
\varkappa_{n}
is the reciprocal of the square of the product of non-zero zeroes of
q_{n}(z)
, which turns
v_{n}(z)
into a monic polynomial. Let divisors
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
be the solutions of Jacobi inversion problem (
3.23
) with
v_{n}(z)
given by (
4.19
). Recall that the points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}
could be labeled so that
x_{n,k}\in[b_{k},a_{k+1}]
for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
. Set

(4.20)

m_{n}(z):=(z-x_{n,1})\cdots(z-x_{n,g}).

Denote further by
B_{n}(z)
the Blaschke product that vanishes exactly at those points
x_{n,i}
that correspond to the elements of the divisor
\mathcal{D}_{n}
that belong to
\boldsymbol{D}
, that is,

(4.21)

B_{n}(z):=\prod_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,i}\in\boldsymbol{D}}\frac{z-x_{n,i}}{1-x_{n,i}z}.

We shall denote by
d_{n}
the number of zeroes of
B_{n}(z)
, i.e., the number of elements of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
that belong to
\boldsymbol{D}
. Finally, put

(4.22)

\lambda_{n}(x):=\rho(x)B_{n}^{2}(x)/m_{n}(x),\quad x\in\Delta,

which is a positive and continuous function (with the exception of those
x_{n,i}
that belong to
\{a_{i},b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{g+1}
). Recall the definition of the function
w(z)
in (
3.1
) as well as Propositions
4.1
–
4.2
. An analog of Theorem
2.2
in the multi-cut case can be stated as follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f(z)
be given by (
2.1
) and (
3.2
)–(
3.4
). Further, let
\{r_{n}(z)\}
be a sequence of irreducible critical points in rational
\bar{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-approximation of
f(z)
. Then it holds that

f(z)-r_{n}(z)=\big(2G_{\lambda_{n}}+o(1)\big)\frac{m_{n}(z)}{B_{n}^{2}(z)}\frac{D_{\lambda_{n}}^{2}(z)}{w(z)}\left(\frac{\rho}{\varphi(z)}\right)^{2(n-d_{n})}

as
n\to\infty
locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\big([a_{1},b_{g+1}]\cup[a_{1},b_{g+1}]^{-1}\big)
.

It is tempting to surmise that
D_{\lambda_{n}}(z)/\varphi^{n-d_{n}}(z)
is analytic across the gaps the way the product
(T_{n}\psi_{n}S_{\dot{\mu}}^{2})(z)
from Theorem
3.1
is. Unfortunately, while the choice of
T_{n}(z)
was driven exactly by the need to cancel the jumps of
(\psi_{n}S_{\dot{\mu}}^{2})(z)
across the gaps, the function
\lambda_{n}(x)
was devised out of necessities of the forthcoming proof. Observe also that its definition requires the knowledge of the divisors
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and therefore of the exact locations of the zeroes of the polynomials
q_{n}(z)
. Of course, it would be much preferable to be able to argue that points
x_{n,i}
need to be selected so that
D_{\lambda_{n}}(z)/\varphi^{n-d_{n}}(z)
has no jumps across the gaps and then to prove Theorem
4.1
with this knowledge only.

Below, we follow the approach developed in
[
2
]
for the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

As above, write
r_{n}(z)=p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
. Since
q_{n}(z)
has real coefficients and its zeroes belong to the unit disk,
v_{n}(z)
has real coefficients and is non-zero in
\mathbb{D}
. Thus, Theorem
3.1
is applicable to
(p_{n}/q_{n})(z)
and we have

(4.23)

(f-r_{n})(z)=(2+o(1))(T_{n}\psi_{n})(z)\frac{\big(mS_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}\big)(z)}{w(z)}

locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
. Define
h_{n}(z):=\log|H_{n}(z)|
, where

H_{n}(z):=(T_{n}\psi_{n})(z)\frac{m(z)}{m_{n}(z)}\frac{B_{n}^{2}(z)S_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}(z)}{G_{\lambda_{n}}D_{\lambda_{n}}^{2}(z)}\left(\frac{\varphi(z)}{\rho}\right)^{2(n-d_{n})}.

It follows from (
4.10
) and (
4.15
) that
|H_{n+}(x)|=|H_{n-}(x)|
for
x\in\cup_{k=1}^{g}(b_{k},a_{k+1})
and the jump is constant in each gap. Moreover, according to Proposition
3.4
and equations (
4.17
), (
4.21
), the function
|(mT_{n}B_{n}^{2}/m_{n}D_{\lambda_{n}}^{2})(z)|
is non-vanishing in the gaps of
\Delta
(observe that if
x_{n,i}
is equal to either
b_{i}
or
a_{i+1}
, then it is of course a simple zero of
m_{n}(z)
, it is essentially a ”simple pole” of
D_{\lambda_{n}}^{2}(z)
, while all other functions are non-vanishing around it). Since the zeroes of the polynomials
q_{n}(z)
belong to
\Delta
, the zeroes of
\tilde{q}_{n}(z)
and therefore of
\psi_{n}(z)
belong to
\Delta^{-1}
. Thus,
h_{n}(z)
is harmonic in
\mathbb{D}\setminus\Delta
as well as across
\mathbb{T}
(recall that
\Delta\subset(-1,1)
). Furthermore, since

G_{\lambda_{n}}|D_{\lambda_{n}\pm}^{2}(x)|=\lambda_{n}(x)=B_{n}^{2}(x)\dot{\mu}(x)m(x)/m_{n}(x)

by (
4.15
), it holds by (
3.12
), (
3.18
), (
3.32
), and (
4.9
) that

(4.24)

h_{n}(x)\equiv 0,\quad x\in\Delta.

To describe the behavior of
h_{n}(z)
on the unit circle, set
b_{n}(z):=q_{n}(z)/\tilde{q}_{n}(z)
. Then it follows from (
1.1
) that

(4.25)

(f-r_{n})(z)=\varkappa_{n}\big(b_{n}^{-2}q_{n}R_{n}\big)(z).

Let now
\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
and
\gamma_{n}
be as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, see (
3.33
) and (
3.36
). Then we get from (
3.37
) that

(f-r_{n})(z)=\big(\varkappa_{n}\gamma_{n}^{2}\big)\frac{2+o(1)}{w(z)}\frac{\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})}{b_{n}^{2}(z)}

locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[a_{1},b_{g+1}]
. It is quite easy to see from (
3.34
) that the product
\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})
has no jump on
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
and therefore is a rational function on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
. Since it is symmetric with respect to the involution
{\boldsymbol{z}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
, it must be a lift of a rational function on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
to
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}
. The form of its zero/pole divisor then yields that

(4.26)

\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})=(\gamma_{n}^{*}/\gamma_{n})m_{n}(z),

where
\gamma_{n}^{*}:=\lim_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\to\boldsymbol{\infty}^{*}}\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})z^{n-g}
. Since Blaschke products
b_{n}(z)
are unimodular on the unit circle, we can write

(4.27)

|(f-r_{n})(\tau)|=(2+o(1))|\varkappa_{n}\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n}^{*}||(m_{n}/w)(\tau)|

uniformly for
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
. Comparing (

) and (

\varkappa_{n}\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n}^{*}=(1+o(1))|(T_{n}\psi_{n})(\tau)||(m/m_{n})(\tau)||S_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}(\tau)|

uniformly for
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
. We do not need an absolute value around the constant
\varkappa_{n}\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n}^{*}
since

\varkappa_{n}\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n}^{*}=\varkappa_{n}\lim_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\to\boldsymbol{\infty}}z^{2n-g}\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})/\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\lim_{0<x\to\infty}x^{2n-g}\frac{\psi_{n}(x)}{\tilde{q}_{n}^{2}(x)}\frac{T_{n}(x)m(x)}{S_{\dot{\mu}}^{2}(x)}>0,

where the first equality follows straight from the definitions of these constants, see (

) and (

), the second one is a consequence of (

), (

), (
3.31
), (

), as well as the meromorphy of
\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})/\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
around
\boldsymbol{\infty}
, and the last inequality holds because

is real for

,

is positive there (exponential of a real-valued function), and so is

(it is real and non-vanishing there and

). Because Blaschke products

are unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
, it follows from (
4.9
) and (
4.16
) that

(4.28)

h_{n}(\tau)=\log\left(\frac{\varkappa_{n}\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n}^{*}}{G_{\lambda_{n}}\rho^{2(n-d_{n})}}\right)+o(1)

uniformly on
\mathbb{T}
. Let us now recall
[
2
, Lemma 4.7]
. It states that if
U\subset\overline{\mathbb{C}}
is a domain such that
\partial U=K_{1}\cup K_{2}
, where

are two compact disjoint sets and if

such that

(4.29)

\oint_{C}\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}h(s)|\mathrm{d}s|=0,

where

from
K_{2}
and has winding number

with respect to
K_{1}
and

with respect to
K_{2}
, then for both

it holds that

\sup_{z^{\prime}\in K_{l}}\limsup_{U\ni z\to z^{\prime}}h(z)\geq\inf_{z^{\prime}\in K_{3-l}}\liminf_{U\ni z\to z^{\prime}}h(z).

We would like to apply this lemma with
