Expressiveness of Commutative Quantum Circuits: A Probabilistic Approach
JORGE M. RAMIREZ 1, ELAINE WONG 1 , CAIO ALVES 1 , SARAH CHEHADE 1 AND RYAN BENNINK. 1

Abstract
This study investigates the frame potential and expressiveness of commutative quantum circuits. Based on the Fourier series representation of these circuits, we express quantum expectation and pairwise fidelity as characteristic functions of random variables, and expressiveness as the recurrence probability of a random walk on a lattice. A central outcome of our work includes formulas to approximate the frame potential and expressiveness for any commutative quantum circuit, underpinned by convergence theorems in probability theory. We identify the lattice volume of the random walk as means to approximate expressiveness based on circuit architecture. In the specific case of commutative circuits involving Pauli- Z Z rotations, we provide theoretical results relating expressiveness and circuit structure. Our probabilistic representation also provide means for bounding and approximately calculating the frame potential of a circuit through sampling methods.

I
Introduction

Quantum computing carries the potential to facilitate accelerated computations, enhanced computational efficiency, and the resolution of hitherto intractable problems
Abhijith et al. (2022)
.
However, near-term quantum devices are small (10-100 qubits) and prone to noise and other hardware errors, which limits their utility.
This has motivated the development of hybrid algorithms in which a quantum computer and a digital computer work together, each performing the tasks for which they are most suited.
This approach has the potential to facilitate new computational capabilities as qubit number and quality increase
(
Preskill, 2018
)
.

The vast majority of hybrid algorithms developed to date employ variational quantum circuits
(
Cerezo et al., 2021
)
.
In these algorithms, a quantum circuit applies parameterized operations or “gates” to a set of qubits to prepare a candidate solution to a problem.
The qubits are then measured and the digital computer uses the measurement results to adjust the circuit parameters in order to improve the state produced by the quantum circuit.
Variational quantum algorithms have been developed and demonstrated for various problem domains such as optimization, quantum simulation, machine learning
(
Grimsley et al., 2019
;
Chowdhury et al., 2020
;
Skolik et al., 2022
)
.

A challenge in variational quantum algorithms is designing circuit forms that can produce a wide range of candidate solutions for a particular problem class
(
Du et al., 2020
)
.
The range of quantum states or unitaries a variational circuit can produce is known as its
expressiveness

(
Du et al., 2020
)
,
expressibility

(
Sim et al., 2019
;
Holmes et al., 2022
)
, or capacity
(
Haug et al., 2021
)
.
The greater the expressiveness, the more likely the circuit is able to produce a good solution to an arbitrary given problem.
Expressiveness is related to computational complexity
(
Morales et al., 2020
)
as well as trainability
(
Holmes et al., 2022
)
.
Expressiveness has been quantified in various ways, including the rank of the Jacobian matrix
(
Haghshenas et al., 2022
)
or quantum Fisher information matrix
(
Larocca et al., 2023
)
, distance to a quantum
t
-design
(
Sim et al., 2019
;
Holmes et al., 2022
)
, or closeness of a pairwise fidelity distribution to an ideal distribution
(
Sim et al., 2019
, Section 3.1.1)
. In this paper we conceptualize expressiveness in terms of the
frame potential
of the circuit. This approach has been used in
Sim et al. (2019)
, and is based on the observation that frame potentials can be used to estimate the non-uniformity of the set of states generated by a parametric quantum circuit.

We compute and analyze the frame potential and expressiveness of a particular family of quantum circuits consisting of mutually commuting operators. These
commutative quantum circuits
are of fundamental interest as they are one of the simplest classes of quantum circuits that cannot be efficiently simulated with digital computers
(
Shepherd and Bremner, 2009
;
Bremner et al., 2011
;
Bremner et al., 2016
)
.
They also have shown significant potential as variational ansatze for applications including machine learning
(
Havlíček et al., 2019
)
.

Our approach is probabilistic and leverages the Fourier series representation of commutative quantum circuits, as discussed in
Vidal and Theis (2018)
and
Casas and Cervera-Lierta (2023)
. We demonstrate that the quantum expectation and pairwise fidelity of these circuits can be understood as characteristic functions of distinct random variables whose values are related to the spectrum of the associated Hamiltonians. Consequently, we are able to compute expressiveness as the recurrence probability of a random walk on a discrete lattice defined by the circuit form.
This novel probabilistic representation brings to bear an additional set of tools for understanding and computing expressiveness of these circuits.
In contrast to the prevalent numerical methods used to characterize expressiveness in existing literature, our findings are anchored in rigorous mathematical proofs that apply to all instances of commutative quantum circuits. We further undertake a detailed analysis of commutative quantum circuits defined through Pauli-
Z
rotations.

Our most significant contribution are formulas to approximate the frame potential and the expressiveness of any commutative quantum circuit. These formulas are rooted in convergence theorems from the realm of probability theory. Our analysis unveils that the relationship between expressiveness and circuit architecture is mostly mediated by the volume of the lattice of the aforementioned random walk. Specializing to commutative quantum circuits whose Hamiltonians are Pauli-
Z
rotations, we derive results and provide illustrative examples that show how this volume depends on the circuit, providing insights about how to design circuits of high expressiveness.

We will be considering quantum operators acting on
n
qubits, and specifically a family of unitaries
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
parameterized by a vector of circuit parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
uniformly distributed over
[-\pi,\pi]^{N}
for some positive integer
N
, with
n\leq N\leq 2^{n}
. The initial quantum state is
|\psi_{0}\rangle
. The following definition introduces the main objects of our study
(
Sim et al., 2019
)
.

Definition 1
.

Let
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
be the unitary operator of an
n
-qubit quantum circuit parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in[-\pi,\pi]^{N}
where
n\leq N\leq 2^{n}
, and has a base quantum state
\psi_{0}
. The ‘fidelity’ of
U
is

F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}):=\left|\langle\psi_{0}|U(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\dagger}U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})|\,\psi_{0}\rangle\right|^{2}.

(1)

The ‘frame potential’ of
U
is

\mathcal{F}_{U}(t):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2N}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{2N}}F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})^{t}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}.

(2)

The ‘expressiveness’ of
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is

\mathcal{E}_{U}(t):=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\text{Haar}}(t)}{\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)},

(3)

where

\mathcal{F}_{\text{Haar}}(t):=\binom{2^{n}+t-1}{2^{n}-1}^{-1}.

(4)

The fidelity
F_{U}\in[0,1]
in
Eq.

2
quantifies the similarity between states produced by circuits with two different parameter settings. Thus for fixed
t>0
, the frame potential quantifies the average similarity between all pairs of states the circuit can produce. A relatively small frame potential indicates that most producible states are dissimilar to each other, which intuitively corresponds to large expressiveness. To obtain a more meaningful measure of expressiveness, we invert the frame potential and normalize it by
\mathcal{F}_{\text{Haar}}(t)
, the minimum possible frame potential
(
Sim et al., 2019
)
. This is achieved when the distribution of output states is uniform (Haar invariant) over the entire Hilbert space. With this,
\mathcal{E}_{U}(t)\in[0,1]
with larger values corresponding to greater expressiveness. The order
t
controls the sensitivity, with greater
t
corresponding to greater sensitivity.

In this paper we specialize on commutative quantum circuits. Namely, on unitary operators of the form

U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=e^{i\theta_{1}H_{1}}\cdots e^{i\theta_{N}H_{N}},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N})\in[-\pi,\pi]^{N}
and the Hamiltonians
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are assumed to be commutative and diagonalizable with integer eigenvalues. In this case, the fidelity can be expressed as

F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})=F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left|\langle\psi_{0}|U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta})|\psi_{0}\rangle\right|^{2}.

(6)

We thus focus on analyzing the quantum expectation

f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\langle\psi_{0}|U(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\psi_{0}\rangle,

(7)

from which the fidelity can be computed simply as
F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=|f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}
. The function
f_{U}
can be written as a Fourier sum

\displaystyle f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\hat{f}_{U}(\boldsymbol{k})e^{i\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}},

(8)

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{U}(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{N}}f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})e^{-i\boldsymbol{\theta}.\boldsymbol{k}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is the ‘spectrum’ and the ‘wave vectors’
\boldsymbol{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
are determined by the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonians
(
Vidal and Theis, 2018
;
Schuld et al., 2021
)
. The coefficients
\hat{f}_{U}(\boldsymbol{k})
have been characterized for very general circuits in
Casas and Cervera-Lierta (2023)
, but here we will show that they can be understood as probabilities. Namely, that for commutative circuits, they satisfy
\hat{f}_{U}(\boldsymbol{k})>0
for all
\boldsymbol{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and
\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\hat{f}_{U}(\boldsymbol{k})=1
. Thus, we can write

f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}e^{i\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot K},

(10)

where the random variable
K
has probability mass function
\mathbb{P}(K=\boldsymbol{k})=\hat{f}_{U}(\boldsymbol{k})
, for all
\boldsymbol{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
.

The expectation operation
\mathbb{E}
in
Eq.

10
is not a quantum expectation but rather a mathematical construct that writes
f_{U}
as the
characteristic function
of
K
, a random variable taking values on the spectrum
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
of
U
. Our approach is based on this observation and uses tools of probability theory to analyze the fidelity, frame potential, and expressiveness of
U
.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In
Section

II
we derive the representation
Eq.

10
for general commutative circuits. The properties of characteristic functions allow us to obtain in
Section

II-B
a representation for the frame potential as
\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=0)
where
W=\{W_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
is a random walk on a lattice
\lat(W)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. In
Section

II-C
we apply the central limit theorem to obtain an approximate formula for
\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)
in terms of the variance of
K
and the volume
V_{U}
of
\lat(W)
.
Sections

III
and
IV
specialize to commutative quantum circuits where each Hamiltonian
H_{j}
is a Pauli-
Z
rotation on an arbitrary subset of qubits (see
Eq.

31
below). In this case, the circuit architecture is encoded in a binary matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
specifying which qubits are included on each rotation. In
Section

III-A
we characterize the probabilistic description of
U
in terms of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Sections

III-B
and
III-C
include the derivation of formulas for the volume
V_{U}
and some of its properties, along with means for computing bounds for the frame potential of any circuit with
n
qubits.
Section

IV
includes illustrative examples and applications of our results to various quantum circuits. It highlights different ways of computing expressiveness, and its behavior as a function of the number of rotations. Lastly, in
Section

V
we discuss our results, and provide interesting observations about the possibility of extending our probabilistic framework to non-commutative circuits.

II
General Commutative Circuits

We consider an operator
U
of the form
Eq.

5
acting on
n
qubits, defined by
N\geq n
Hamiltonians
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, and parameterized by an
N
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in[-\pi,\pi]^{N}
. Under the assumption that the Hamiltonians in
Eq.

5
are commutative and diagonalizable, it follows that they are simultaneously diagonalizable. Therefore, there exists an operator
Q
such that
H_{j}=Q\Lambda_{j}Q^{{\dagger}}
for each
j
, where
\Lambda_{j}
is a diagonal operator. Moreover, the quantum expectation
f_{U}
in
Eq.

7
can be written as
f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\langle\psi_{0}|Q^{\dagger}e^{i\theta_{1}\Lambda_{1}}\dots e^{i\theta_{N}\Lambda_{N}}Q|\psi_{0}\rangle
. We thus assume, without loss of generality, that the operator has the form

U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=e^{i(\theta_{1}H_{1}+\dots+\theta_{N}H_{N})},

(11)

where each Hamiltonian
H_{j}
is a diagonal operator with integer eigenvalues,

H_{j}=\diag(k^{(j)}_{1},\dots,k^{(j)}_{2^{n}}),\quad k_{x}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{Z},

(12)

j=1,\dots,N
,
x=1,\dots,2^{n}.

We further assume that
n\leq N
and that the set
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
is linearly independent.

II-A

Probabilistic Representation

Establishing that
f_{U}
in
Eq.

7
can be represented as the characteristic function of a random variable is achieved through various methods. First,
f_{U}
is a positive-definite function: Let
\{z_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be finite sequences in
\mathbb{C}
and
[-\pi,\pi]^{N}
respectively, then

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}z_{k}z_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}z_{k}z_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k^{\prime}})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k}z_{k}f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})\right|^{2}\geq 0.

Bochner’s theorem then implies that
f_{U}
is a characteristic function
(
Bochner, 2005
)
. Namely, there exists a probability space with a random variable
K
defined on it, such that
Eq.

10
holds. We can also directly construct
K
. For this, denote

\boldsymbol{k}_{x}:=(k_{x}^{(1)},\dots,k_{x}^{(N)}),\quad x=1,\dots,2^{n},

(13)

and write
(\theta_{1}H_{1}+\dots+\theta_{N}H_{N})=\diag(\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}_{2^{n}})
. Thus,

\displaystyle f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|e^{i\diag(\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}_{2^{n}})}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{2^{n}}|\langle\psi_{0}|x\rangle|^{2}e^{i\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}_{x}}

(15)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{i\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}_{X}}\right),

(16)

where
X
is a random variable taking values on
\{1,\dots,2^{n}\}
with
\mathbb{P}(X=x)=|\langle\psi_{0}|x\rangle|^{2}
. It will be useful to think of
\boldsymbol{k}_{X}
as a row chosen randomly from the matrix

\boldsymbol{K}:=\begin{pmatrix}-&\boldsymbol{k}_{1}&-\\
&\vdots&\\
-&\boldsymbol{k}_{2^{n}}&-\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{n}\times N}.

(17)

Lastly, let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
be the set of rows of
\boldsymbol{K}
. In other words,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\{\boldsymbol{k}_{x}\}_{x=1}^{2^{n}}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, or equivalently the matrix where each row of
\boldsymbol{K}
appears only once, and by our hypothesis having rank equal to
N
. If we define the random variable
K=\boldsymbol{k}_{X}
and take into account the possible repetitions of each wave vector, we have

\mathbb{P}(K=\boldsymbol{k})=\sum_{x:\boldsymbol{k}_{x}=\boldsymbol{k}}|\langle\psi_{0}|x\rangle|^{2},\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega},

(18)

and the probabilistic representation in
Eq.

10
holds.

The moments of
K
are determined by the Hamiltonians and the base state. The expected value of the
j
-th entry of the vector
K
is

\mathbb{E}K^{(j)}=\sum_{x=1}^{2^{n}}k_{x}^{(j)}|\langle\psi_{0}|x\rangle|^{2}=\langle\psi_{0}|H_{j}|\psi_{0}\rangle,

(19)

namely, the quantum expected value of
H_{j}
in the state
\psi_{0}
. Similarly, its covariance matrix is

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(K)_{i,j}=\langle\psi_{0}|H_{i}H_{j}|\psi_{0}\rangle-\langle\psi_{0}|H_{j}|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|H_{i}|\psi_{0}\rangle,

(20)

for
i,j=1,\dots,N
.

A corresponding probabilistic representation for the fidelity
F_{U}
and its powers follows from the properties of characteristic functions. Note that

F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\dagger}=\mathbb{E}e^{i\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot D},

(21)

where
D=K-K^{\prime}
and
K,K^{\prime}
are assumed to be independent and identically distributed as
Eq.

18
. We denote the sample space of
D
as

\boldsymbol{\Delta}:=\{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}:\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{N},

(22)

its cardinality as
L
, and write
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\{\boldsymbol{d}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. Note that the zero vector
\boldsymbol{0}
is always an element of
\boldsymbol{D}
. Also, the distribution of
D
is symmetric, namely for all
\boldsymbol{d}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}
, one has
-\boldsymbol{d}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}
and
\mathbb{P}(D=\boldsymbol{d})=\mathbb{P}(D=-\boldsymbol{d})
. It follows in particular that
D
has mean
\mathbb{E}D=0
and covariance matrix
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(D)=2\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(K)
.

For powers of the fidelity, we can write

F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{t}=\mathbb{E}e^{i\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot(D_{1}+\dots+D_{t})},\quad t=0,1,\dots,

(23)

where
\{D_{1},\dots,D_{t}\}
are independent and distributed as
D
. We define the random walk

W_{t}=D_{1}+\dots+D_{t},\quad t=1,2,\dots

(24)

with independent increments distributed as
D
. The paths of
W
start at the origin and lie on the lattice
\lat(W)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{N}
generated by
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
,

\lat(W)=\sspan_{\mathbb{N}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=\sspan_{\mathbb{Z}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}),

(25)

namely the set of linear combinations of elements of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{Z}
, respectively. The fact that both representations hold for
\lat(W)
follows from the symmetry of the distribution of
D
. An important quantity associated with
W
is the volume
V_{U}
of its lattice, which can be computed as

V_{U}=|\det(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*})|,

(26)

where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}
is a matrix whose rows are selected from
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
and has row space over the integers equal to
\lat(W)
. See
Fig.

1
.

Fig. 1:
Schematic representation of the lattice
\lat(W)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{N}
in (
25
), its generator
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}
and volume
V_{U}
in
Eq.

26
, and a path of the random walk
W
in (
24
).

II-B

The Frame Potential as a Probability

Our first result establishes a representation for the frame potential
\mathcal{F}_{U}
in terms of the random walk
W
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
U
is given by
Eq.

11
and let
W
be the random walk in
Eq.

24
. The frame potential of
U
is

\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=0),\quad t=1,2,\dots

(27)

Proof.

By virtue of (
6
), under the change of variables
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
, the frame potential is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)=\frac{1}{2^{N}(2\pi)^{2N}}\int\int_{R}F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{t}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}

where the region of integration is

R=\{(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime})\in[-2\pi,2\pi]^{2N}:|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|+|\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}|\leq 2\pi\}.

The following symmetries of
F_{U}
,

F(\boldsymbol{\varphi})=F(-\boldsymbol{\varphi})=F(\pi+\boldsymbol{\varphi})=F(\pi-\boldsymbol{\varphi}),

imply that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{2N}}{(2\pi)^{2N}}\int\int_{[0,\pi]^{2N}}F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{t}\,d\boldsymbol{\varphi}\,d\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{N}}\int_{[0,\pi]^{N}}F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{t}\,d\boldsymbol{\varphi}.

To compute the resulting integral, note that for
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\displaystyle\int_{[0,\pi]^{N}}

\displaystyle e^{i\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{y}}\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\pi^{N},&\boldsymbol{y}=0,\\
\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2\sin(\pi y^{(j)})}{y^{(j)}},&y^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }j,\\
0,&y^{(j)}\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for some }j.\end{cases}

But since
W_{t}
takes only values with integer entries,

\displaystyle\int_{[0,\pi]^{N}}F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{t}\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{[0,\pi]^{N}}e^{i\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot W_{t}}\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\pi^{N}\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=0)

from which the result follows.
∎

As an initial application of Theorem
1
, we prove a useful corollary regarding the monotonicity of the frame potential with respect to the set of rotations. It confirms that augmenting the number of rotations of a circuit decreases the frame potential, which by virtue of
Definition

1
, increases the expressiveness; an intuitive result which has been numerically verified (see Section
Section

IV
).

Corollary 1
.

Let the number
n
of qubits be fixed, and
U
be the operator in
Eq.

11
. Consider another circuit
U^{\prime}
with
N^{\prime}>N
but sharing the first
N
factors with
U
, namely

U^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=U(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N})e^{i\theta_{N+1}H_{N+1}}\cdots e^{i\theta_{N^{\prime}}H_{N^{\prime}}},

for some Hamiltonians
H_{N+1},\dots,H_{N^{\prime}}
that commute, are diagonalizable, and have integer eigenvalues. Then
\mathcal{F}_{U^{\prime}}(t)\leq\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)
for all
t
.

Proof.

We denote with prime the corresponding quantities associated with the operator
U^{\prime}
. By construction, the first
N
components of each
\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}_{x}
agree with those of
\boldsymbol{k}_{x}
in
Eq.

16
for all
x=1,\dots,2^{n}
. Namely, the matrix
\boldsymbol{K}^{\prime}
shares the same first
N
columns with
\boldsymbol{K}
in
Eq.

17
. Since
K^{\prime}
is obtained by randomly selecting rows from
\boldsymbol{K}^{\prime}
, its first
N
components will have the same distribution as
K
. It follows that the first
N
components of
W_{t}^{\prime}
have the same distribution as
W_{t}
, for all
t
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{U^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{\prime(1)}=0,\dots,W_{t}^{\prime(N)}=0,\dots W_{t}^{\prime(N^{\prime})}=0),

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{\prime(1)}=0,\dots,W_{t}^{\prime(N)}=0),

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(1)}=0,\dots,W_{t}^{(N)}=0)=\mathcal{F}_{U}(t).

∎

II-C

Asymptotic Behavior of the Frame Potential and Expressiveness

The probabilistic interpretation of
F_{U}
, along with the Central Limit Theorem, imply convergence in distribution of
W_{t}/\sqrt{t}
to a multivariate Gaussian vector. Equivalently, by Lévy’s continuity theorem
(
Williams, 1991
, Section 18.1)
, one has point-wise convergence of the fidelity
F_{U}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}/\sqrt{t})^{t}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(D)\boldsymbol{\theta})},\;\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

(28)

where
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(D)=2\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(K)
. The asymptotic behavior of the frame potential
\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=0)
follows from an application of the corresponding Local Limit Theorem, for which we follow the theory outlined in
Gikhman and Skorokhod (2004, Ch. II.7)
and
Lawler (2018, Ch. 2.3)
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
U
is given by
Eq.

11
. Let
K
be the random variable in the probabilistic representation
Eq.

10
of
f_{U}
, and
V_{U}
the volume of
\lat(W)
. Define the approximate frame potential by

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{U}(t)=\frac{V_{U}}{(4\pi t)^{N/2}\sqrt{\det(\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(K))}}.

(29)

Then there exists
c>0
such that
|\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{U}(t)|<c~t^{-(N/2+1)}
for all

Proof.

If the Hamiltonians form a linearly independent set of operators, then both
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
have rank

and the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N\times N}

\boldsymbol{\Psi}=(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*})^{-1}
and consider the transformed random walk
\tilde{W}_{t}:=W_{t}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
with increments in the set
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}:=\{\boldsymbol{d}\boldsymbol{\Psi},\boldsymbol{d}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\}=:\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. We will show that the transformed random walk
\tilde{W}
is ‘completely irreducible’. First note that by construction, each
\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}_{l}
contains the coefficients to express
\boldsymbol{d}_{l}
as a linear combination of elements of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
, and therefore
\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. Also,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
is symmetric and contains the canonical basis for
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. Therefore
\lat(\tilde{W})=\mathbb{Z}^{N}
and
\tilde{W}
is an ‘irreducible’ random walk. To prove complete irreducibility, we must prove reducibility for a random walk with increments on
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}
where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}
is an arbitrary vector of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
. This reduces to showing that
\sspan_{\mathbb{Z}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}})=\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. Clearly,
\sspan_{\mathbb{Z}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}})\subset\sspan_{\mathbb{Z}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}})=\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. Let
\boldsymbol{k}\in\sspan_{\mathbb{Z}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}})
, then we can write for some

,

\boldsymbol{k}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}_{l}}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}_{l}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}})-m_{l}(\boldsymbol{0}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}})

which is a linear combination of vectors in
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}})
because
\boldsymbol{0}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}
. Then
\boldsymbol{k}\in\sspan_{\mathbb{Z}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}})
. Since
\tilde{W}
is completely irreducible, the Local Limit Theorem 7.2 in
Gikhman and Skorokhod (2004)
and Theorem 2.3.5 of
Lawler (2018)
apply. Specifically, there exists a constant

such that

\left|\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W_{t}}=0)-\frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{N/2}\sqrt{\det(\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\tilde{D}))}}\right|\leq\frac{c}{t^{\frac{N}{2}+1}}.

Note that
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W_{t}}=0)=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=0)
. Also, from
Eq.

40
and the definition of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
follows that

\displaystyle\det(\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\tilde{D}))=\det(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\dagger}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(D)\boldsymbol{\Psi})=\frac{2^{N}\det(\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(K))}{V_{U}^{2}},

which completes the proof.
∎

The following asymptotic result for the expressiveness follows from combining

\mathcal{F}_{\text{Haar}}(t)
in

.

Corollary 2
.

Define the approximate expressiveness by

(30)

Then, under the assumptions of Theorem

there exists a positive constant

for all

.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose

where

n

Proof.

Each column
\boldsymbol{k}^{(j)}
of
\boldsymbol{K}
can be explicitly written as a Kronecker product of vectors corresponding to the diagonals

of

in

\boldsymbol{k}^{(j)}=\bigotimes_{m=1}^{n}o^{(j)}_{m},

where for each

o^{(j)}_{m}=\begin{cases}\diag{(Z)},&m\in Q_{j}\\
\diag{(I_{2})},&m\notin Q_{j}\end{cases}.

(37)

It follows from

\boldsymbol{K}
is a column from the Hadamard matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
of order
n
obtained by Sylvester’s construction. See

for reference. In particular, the
N

\boldsymbol{K}
form an orthogonal set of

and are therefore linearly independent. To establish

35

\diag{(Z)}=\begin{pmatrix}\hphantom{-}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{0}\\

1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{0}\\
(-1)^{0}\end{pmatrix}

in

to

(-1)^{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}0\\
a_{m}^{(j)}\end{pmatrix}},\quad m=1,\dots,n,\quad j=1,\dots,N,

\boldsymbol{A}
as in

(-1)

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}

. In other words, for the

\boldsymbol{k}^{(j)}=-1^{\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{a}^{(j)}\right)_{\text{mod}\,2}}=\boldsymbol{1}-2(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{a}^{(j)})_{\text{mod}\,2},\quad j=1,\dots,N,

The random index

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}

Eq.

31

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{1}-2\mathrm{row}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}}(\boldsymbol{A}),

(38)

where

\boldsymbol{A}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
. The distribution of

\mathbb{P}\left(K^{(j)}=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(K^{(j)}=-1\right)=\frac{1}{2},

(39)

for

\mathbb{E}K=0,\quad\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}K=I_{N}.

(40)

Proof.

By
Eq.

35

, each row
\boldsymbol{k}_{x}
of
\boldsymbol{K}
corresponds to the linear combination
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{x}\boldsymbol{A})_{\text{mod}\,2}

\boldsymbol{A}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}

n

and

. Each row

The number of such solutions equals two raised to the nullity of

.

Proposition 3

are given by

, then the approximate frame potential and expressiveness of

are, respectively,

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{U}(t)=\frac{V_{U}}{(4\pi t)^{N/2}},\quad\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{U}(t)=\frac{(4\pi t)^{N/2}\mathcal{F}_{\text{Haar}}(t)}{V_{U}}.

(42)

Example 2
.

For the circuit shown in

with

.

compares the exact frame potential and expressiveness with their corresponding approximate formulas in

In fact, the lattice volume
V_{U}
completely determines the behavior of the frame potential for large values of
t
. We aim at characterizing
V_{U}
as a function of the circuit’s architecture. The ultimate goal is to understand which operators give rise to small frame potentials (as a function of
t
) and therefore to large expressiveness for a given amount of resources. First we prove a lemma with two distinct representations of the lattice volume.

Lemma 1
.

Let
U
be an operator on
n
qubits with
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
given by
Eq.

31
with
n\leq N\leq 2^{n}-1
. The lattice volume is given by

V_{U}=2^{N}|\det((\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})_{\text{mod}\,2}^{*})|,

(43)

where
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})_{\text{mod}\,2}^{*}
is an
N\times N
matrix whose rows are selected from
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})_{\text{mod}\,2}
and span
\lat(W)
. Also, there exists an
N\times N
minor, denoted
\mathrm{Min}^{*}(\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}})
, of the Hadamard matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
such that

V_{U}=|\mathrm{Min}^{*}(\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}})|.

(44)

Proof.

Eq.

43
follows from
Eq.

41
in
Proposition

2
because
\mathrm{row}_{\text{mod}\,2}(\boldsymbol{A})
is the set of rows of
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})_{\text{mod}\,2}
. For
Eq.

44
, recall that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is the matrix obtained by selecting
N
columns from of
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
and deleting the repeating rows. Consider the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime}:=(\boldsymbol{\Omega}-\boldsymbol{1})^{*}+\boldsymbol{1}
, where the operation
*
selects
N
rows of
(\boldsymbol{\Omega}-\boldsymbol{1})
generating the lattice. Then
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime}
is also a sub-matrix of
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
. Also,
Eq.

41
implies
V_{U}=|\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{1})|
. Consider the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}
obtained by appending a row vector of
1
s to the top of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime}
and a column vector of
1
s to the left of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime}
. Then
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}
is also a sub-matrix of
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
because the first row and column of
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
have all elements equal to one. If for all
i=2,\dots,N+1
we replace the
i
-th row of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}
by itself minus the constant vector of
1
s, the resulting matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime\prime}
has the same determinant as
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}
with entries
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{1,1}=1
,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{j,1}=0
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i,j}=(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{1})_{i-1,j-1}
for
i,j=2,\dots,N+1
. Taking cofactors with respect to the first column in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime\prime}
, we obtain

\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime})=\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime\prime})=\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{1})=\det((\boldsymbol{\Omega}-\boldsymbol{1})^{*}).

But
\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime})
is a minor of
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
and therefore
V_{U}=|\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime})|
satisfies
Eq.

44
as desired.
∎

The minors of any Hadamard matrix satisfy the famous ‘Hadamard bound’ which states that any
N\times N
matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
with entries in the complex unit disk, satisfies
|\det(\boldsymbol{M})|\leq N^{N/2}
. Hadamard matrices are precisely those for which the bound is realized, in particular, for Sylvester’s construction,
|\det(\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}})|=(2^{n})^{2^{n-1}}
. See
Kravvaritis (2016)
and references therein. For example, it follows directly from
Eq.

43
and the Hadamard bound, that
V_{U}\leq 2^{N}N^{N/2}
for all
N
. The following result refines this bound and provides a more comprehensive picture of the volume.

Theorem 4
.

Let
U
be an operator on
n
qubits with
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
given by
Eq.

31
with
n\leq N\leq 2^{n}-1
. Then the lattice volume,
V_{U}
, satisfies the following bound

\displaystyle V_{\min}\leq 2^{N}\leq V_{U}\leq(N+1)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}\leq V_{\max},

(45)

where
V_{\min}:=2^{n}
and
V_{\max}:=(2^{n})^{2^{n-1}}
. The maximum possible value of the volume
V_{\max}
is attained by a circuit with all rotations,
N=2^{n}-1
.
The minimum possible value of the volume
V_{\min}
is attained by a circuit with
N=n
which occurs if and only if
\boldsymbol{A}
is a square matrix of full rank in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
.

Proof.

The left most inequality is trivial since by hypothesis
n\leq N
, which implies
2^{n}\leq 2^{N}
. For the next inequality, since
|\det((\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})^{*}_{\text{mod}\,2})|>1
, it follows that
2^{N}\leq 2^{N}|\det((\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})^{*}_{\text{mod}\,2})|=V_{U}
, by
Eq.

43
. The third inequality follows directly from the Hadamard bound applied to the
V_{U}=|\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime})|
where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}
is the the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix constructed in
Lemma

1
. The last inequality follows from monotonicity of the function
N\mapsto N^{N/2}
.

For the case of minimum possible volume, recall that
\boldsymbol{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times N}
with
N\geq n
. If
N=\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}}(\boldsymbol{A})
, then
N=n
and
\mathrm{row}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}}(\boldsymbol{A})=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
.
Eq.

38
implies that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}-\boldsymbol{1}
contains all elements of
\{-2,0\}^{N}
and therefore by
Eq.

41
,
V_{U}=2^{N}
.
Conversely, if
N>n
the left null space of
\boldsymbol{A}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
is not trivial, and
\mathrm{row}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}}(\boldsymbol{A})\subset\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
. But the set of rows of
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})_{\text{mod}\,2}
is exactly
\mathrm{row}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}}(\boldsymbol{A})
. Therefore, by selecting rows that generate
\lat(W)
, one obtains a matrix
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})^{*}_{\text{mod}\,2}
that is row deficient in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
, namely
\det((\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})^{*}_{\text{mod}\,2})=0~\text{mod}\,2
. But since
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})^{*}_{\text{mod}\,2}
is invertible in
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, then
\det((\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{A})^{*}_{\text{mod}\,2})
is a multiple of two, and
V_{U}>2^{N}
. For the case of maximum volume, note that by Hadamard’s theorem
|\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime})|=V_{\max}
only if
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}
is a
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
Hadamard matrix. The construction in the proof of
Lemma

1
thus implies that the number of rotations is the maximum possible
N=2^{n}-1
.
∎

Note that the smallest value of the volume
V_{U}
does not correspond necessarily with the smallest possible frame potential because of the presence of the factor
t^{-N/2}
in (
42
). In
Section

IV
we will explore the numerical behavior of the frame potential and the expressiveness as a function of
t,n
and
N
, and its relationship with the lattice volume.

t
with an alphabet of size
2^{n}
. We include the proof here for completeness and because our approach is different from the one taken in
Bennink (2023)
.

Theorem 5
.

If the circuit includes all possible rotations,
N=2^{n}-1
, the frame potential can be computed as the expected value of a multinomial coefficient,

\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)=\frac{1}{2^{nt}}\mathbb{E}\left[\binom{t}{B^{(1)}_{t}\,\cdots B^{(2^{n})}_{t}}\right],

(46)

where
B_{t}=(B_{t}^{(1)},\dots,B_{t}^{(2^{n})})
is a random walk with increments uniformly distributed on the canonical basis for
\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
.

Proof.

Let
S_{t}
and
S^{\prime}_{t}
be independent random variables distributed as
K_{1}+\dots+K_{t}
. Then we can write
\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)=\mathbb{P}(S_{t}=S_{t}^{\prime})
. Further,
S_{t}=B_{t}\boldsymbol{K}
, where
B_{t}
is a row vector of
2^{n}
non-negative integers counting how many times in
S_{t}
has each
\boldsymbol{k}\in\boldsymbol{K}
appeared,

B_{t}^{(x)}=\#\{s\leq t:K_{s}=\boldsymbol{k}_{x}\},\quad x=1,\dots,2^{n}.

At each time-step
t
, the row vector
B_{t}
increases by one at a single entry uniformly selected from
2^{n}
choices, which is equivalent to a random walk with increments on the canonical basis for
\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
. Also, each
B_{t}
is distributed as a multinomial with
t
trials and
2^{n}
equally likely outcomes,

\mathbb{P}(B_{t}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{2^{n}}))=\frac{1}{2^{nt}}\binom{t}{b_{1}\cdots b_{2^{n}}},

with
b_{1},\dots,b_{2^{n}}\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
. Defining
B^{\prime}_{t}
analogously for
S_{t}^{\prime}
, we can write,

\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)=\mathbb{P}((B_{t}-B^{\prime}_{t})\in\text{null}(\boldsymbol{K}^{\top})).

Recall from
Proposition

1
that
\boldsymbol{K}
has rank equal to
N
, so the dimension of
\text{null}(\boldsymbol{K}^{\top})
equals
2^{n}-N
. In the special case
N=2^{n}-1
,
\boldsymbol{K}
exhausts every column of
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
except for the column containing only ones (which would correspond to a rotation gate where the identity operator is applied to each qubit). As a consequence,
\text{null}(\boldsymbol{K}^{\top})
has dimension one and contains only constant vectors. Further,
\sum_{x=1}^{2^{n}}B_{t}^{(x)}=\sum_{x=1}^{2^{n}}{B^{\prime}}_{t}^{(x)}=t
, so
(B_{t}-B^{\prime}_{t})\in\text{null}(\boldsymbol{K}^{\top})
is equivalent to
B_{t}=B^{\prime}_{t}
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(B_{t}=B_{t}^{\prime})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{b}}\mathbb{P}(B_{t}=\boldsymbol{b})^{2}

(47)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{nt}}\mathbb{E}\left[\binom{t}{B^{(1)}_{t}\,\cdots B^{(2^{n})}_{t}}\right]

(48)

where the sum is taken over all possible values
\boldsymbol{b}
of
B_{t}
.
∎

In the case of the minimum possible number of rotations, the frame potential can be explicitly computed. Note that by the monotonicity property established in
Corollary

1
, the frame potential in this case is an upper bound for the frame potential of any circuit with the same number of qubits.

Theorem 6
.

If the circuit has the minimum number of rotations
N=n
, then the frame potential is explicitly given for all
t
by

\mathcal{F}(t)=\left(\frac{\Gamma(t+\tfrac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(t+1)}\right)^{n}

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Gamma function.

Proof.

Suppose
r=n=N
. Then
K=(K^{(1)},\dots,K^{(N)})
is a vector of independent and identically distributed random variables. To see that, note that if
r=N
, by
Eq.

39
in the proof of Theorem
1
,

2^{-N}=\mathbb{P}(K^{(1)}=k^{(1)})\cdots\mathbb{P}(K^{(N)}=k^{(N)})=\mathbb{P}(K=\boldsymbol{k})

for any
\boldsymbol{k}=(k^{(1)},\dots,k^{(N)})\in\boldsymbol{K}
. Likewise, the vectors
D_{t}
and
W_{t}
in
Eq.

24
have independent and identically distributed components. In this case we can compute

\mathcal{F}(t)=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=0)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(j)}=0)=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(1)}=0)^{N},

where
W_{t}^{(1)}=D_{1}^{(1)}+\dots+D_{t}^{(1)}
is a random walk with increments distributed as

\mathbb{P}(D_{1}^{(1)}=\pm 1)=\frac{1}{4},\quad\mathbb{P}(D_{1}^{(1)}=0)=\frac{1}{2}.

We can compute
\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(1)}=0)
by counting how many elements of
\boldsymbol{D}_{t}^{(1)}=\{D_{1}^{(1)},\dots,D_{t}^{(1)}\}
fall on each of
\{-1,0,1\}
, namely,
C_{-1}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t}^{(1)})
,
C_{0}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t}^{(1)})
,
C_{1}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t}^{(1)})
. Consider the case when
t
is even. Then the event
[W_{t}^{(1)}=0]
implies that
C_{0}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t}^{(1)})
is even, say
C_{0}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t}^{(1)})=2c_{0}
, and that
C_{-1}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t}^{(1)})=C_{1}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t}^{(1)})=(t-2c_{0})/2
. Thus we can write the probability in terms of multinomial coefficients as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W^{(1)}_{t}=0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{0}=0}^{t/2}\frac{t!}{(2c_{0})!(\tfrac{t-2c_{0}}{2})!(\tfrac{t-2c_{0}}{2})!}\frac{1}{2^{2c_{0}}}\frac{1}{4^{t-2c_{0}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(t+\tfrac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(t+1)}

which yields the exact formula for
\mathcal{F}(t)
.
∎

V
Discussion and Outlook

In this study, we conducted a detailed analysis of the frame potential and expressiveness in parametric commutative circuits with integer eigenvalues. Our techniques are probabilistic, based on the observation that for the corresponding operator
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the Fourier expansion of the function
f_{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\langle\psi_{0}|U(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\psi_{0}\rangle
in
Eq.

7
can be written as the characteristic function of a random vector
K
with integer entries. As a consequence,
Theorem

1
shows that the frame potential can be expressed as
\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)=\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=0)
where
W
is a random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, with
N
being the number of Hamiltonian operators in
U
. Our key result,
Theorem

2
, provides an approximate formula for the frame potential that is useful for estimating the expressiveness. It also reveals the existence of a quantity
V_{U}
that greatly influences the order of magnitude of expressiveness. In our formulation,
V_{U}
is the volume of the sub-lattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
where the random walk
W
takes values.

Focusing on commutative circuits composed of Pauli-
Z
rotations,
Section

III
uses the probabilistic representation to understand the relationship between the architecture of the circuit, its frame potential, and expressiveness.
Theorem

3
and
Proposition

2
explicitly characterize the distribution of
K
and the lattice of
W
in terms of the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
which explicitly encodes the circuit’s architecture. As a result, in
Lemma

1
and
Theorem

4
we obtain expressions to compute and bound
V_{U}
. In addition, for circuits that include all rotations with
n
, we derive a further probabilistic representation for
\mathcal{F}_{U}
that generalizes the combinatorial approach of
Bennink (2023)
, and provides a useful sampling method for the calculation of expressiveness.

The numerical examples presented in
Section

IV
illustrate that with Pauli-
Z
rotations, some of the practical insights that can be obtained using our results. We provide different approaches to compute or estimate the frame potential to different levels of accuracy, and we show how our approximate formula
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{U}(t)
allows for accurate calculation of the expressiveness for
t
-values that were previously unfeasible.

The volume
V_{U}
, or its associated
v_{U}
in
Eq.

53
, appear to be natural indicators of expressiveness for commutative quantum circuits. Its computation is generally straightforward given the spectrum
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and, as shown in
Section

IV
, its value can vary over several orders of magnitude. The results shown in
Fig.

5
, along with others not included here, allow us to make some observations and conjectures. First, we observe that
v_{U}
seems to always be a power of two, growing to very large numbers as
N
increases. However,
v_{U}
does not take all possible powers of two, and for a given
N
, multiple values of
v_{U}
can be possible, spanning various orders of magnitude. This is significant because it means that given
n
and
N
, the choice of rotations can directly affect the expressiveness, and one would like criteria to always select an architecture with minimum volume. Along these lines, we observe that for any given number of resources
n,N
, it is sufficient to design the circuit to have all rotations up to some number of qubits, which in practice is the easiest architecture to implement.

A rigorous characterization of the possible values of
V_{U}
is, by
Lemma

1
, equivalent to characterizing the minors of the Hadamard matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
that arise from arbitrarily selecting columns, and rows generating a given lattice. Calculating minors from general Hadamard matrices is a problem of great interest dating back to 1907. In fact, the possible values of minors of order
n-4
of a Hadamard matrix can be computed
(see
Kravvaritis, 2016
, Theorem 5)
, and theoretically generalized to
n-j
minors, for any
j<n
. However, this generalization is currently impossible to implement due to the complexity of the computation. For
N\in\{2^{n}-4,\dots,2^{n}-1\}
, existing characterizations of the minors of
\boldsymbol{H}_{2^{n}}
yield the specific possible values that
V_{U}
can take. Further explorations of the behavior of the volume
V_{U}
will reveal interesting and informative patterns on how expressiveness can be managed through careful selection of circuit architecture.

The results in
Section

II
apply to any commutative quantum circuit of the form (
5
). However, their applications to obtain estimates to the frame potential via
Theorem

2
require the characterization of the distribution of
K
, which in
Section

III
is carried out for circuits comprising only of Pauli rotations. The analysis for the case when
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are commuting Hamiltonians in the Pauli group
\mathbb{S}_{n}
is the subject of a forthcoming note.
(
Yu et al., 2024
, See)
.

A natural question that arises is whether the probabilistic framework presented here can be extended to the case of parametric quantum circuits built with Hamiltonian operators that are not simultaneously diagonalizable, and hence non-commutative. This extension would allow, as was done here, for the use of techniques in probability theory to analyze a wider class of parametric quantum circuits. This is the subject of future research but, to close, we now present some interesting preliminary observations regarding this matter.

These examples highlight the need for additional investigation to determine which (sub)-classes of non-commutative circuits naturally lend itself to a probabilistic interpretation, as in the commutative case. In particular, we seek to understand under what circumstances or algebraic structures does a non-commuting circuit of Pauli rotations results in a Fourier series with a probability mass function as its weights.
