Anomalous relaxation and hyperuniform fluctuations in center-of-mass conserving systems with broken time-reversal symmetry
Anirban Mukherjee, Dhiraj Tapader, Animesh Hazra, Punyabrata Pradhan

Abstract

I
Introduction

Recently there has been a surge in interest in characterizing transport in quantum many-body systems, such as fractonic fluids
Prem et al. 2018
;
Gromov et al. 2020
;
Feldmeier et al. 2020
;
Morningstar et al. 2020
;
Sala et al. 2020
;
Pai et al. 2019
;
Glorioso et al. 2022
;
Scherg et al. 2021
;
Shenoy and Moessner 2020
, which have more than one conservation law and consequently possess an interesting dynamical structure. Indeed, by using phenomenological hydrodynamic theory, it was claimed that the time evolution of initially localized charge density in dipole-moment conserving systems, with short-ranged interaction, can only have subdiffusive temporal growth
Feldmeier et al. 2020
;
Morningstar et al. 2020
;
Han et al. 2023
;
Glorioso et al. 2022
. The class of models studied in these works typically have time-reversal symmetry present in the systems.
Of course, broken time-reversal symmetry allows for more choices in microscopic dynamics
Bertrand et al. 2019
and its role on the large-scale properties of dipole-moment conserving systems is examined by us in this study. We provide a counter-example of a paradigmatic model system - the celebrated Oslo model
Christensen et al. 1996
;
Grassberger et al. 2016
, which has both mass and center of mass (CoM) conservation (analogous to dipole-moment conservation), but for which subdiffusive time-evolution equation, such as Eq. (1) of Ref.
Han et al. 2023
, does not hold.
Our findings show that the dynamic properties of CoM conserving systems depend on microscopic details and need not always be subdiffusive.

The Oslo model, also known as the Oslo “ricepile”
Christensen et al. 1996
, is an example of threshold-activated systems, collectively called “sandpiles”
Bak 1997
.
Not only does the model serve as a prototype for many-body systems with CoM conservation and broken time-reversal symmetry (thus no detailed balance), but it also constitutes a paradigm for absorbing-phase transition (APT) with multiple conservation laws.
Dynamical activities in these highly nonlinear systems propagate in space and time via intermittent burst-like “avalanches”, resulting in long-ranged spatio-temporal correlations.
Sandpiles have several variants, depending on whether mass variable being discrete or continuous, particle-transfer rules being deterministic or stochastic
Dhar 1990
;
Dhar 1999
;
Manna 1991
;
Dickman et al. 2001
, randomness in threshold mass
Christensen et al. 1996
, “stickiness”
Mohanty and Dhar 2002
;
Mohanty and Dhar 2007
and various other local and nonlocal variations of microscopic dynamics
Kadanoff et al. 1989
.
The Oslo model is a stochastic variant of the BTW sandpile
Bak 1997
in that the threshold mass can take random values; the model should be contrasted with the stochastic Manna sandpile
Manna 1991
, which has a fixed threshold value (like the BTW sandpile) and the stochastic particle-transfer rule (unlike the Oslo model).
Because of the deterministic mass transfer, the CoM remains conserved in both the Oslo and BTW models.

Various conserved-mass transport processes, such as random-organization model
Corté et al. 2008
, chiral active matter
Lei et al. 2019
;
Kuroda and Miyazaki 2023
and CoM conserving Manna sandpile
Hexner and Levine 2017
;
Bertrand et al. 2019
, among others
Ikeda 2023
, have been studied in the past to better understand the role of CoM conservation on their large-scale spatio-temporal properties in an out-of-equilibrium setting.
For concreteness, in this work we focus on the Oslo model, which, like other conserved-mass (“fixed-energy”) sandpiles, exhibits an absorbing phase transition
Lübeck 2004
;
Dickman et al. 2000
, where the fraction
a(\rho)
of
active
sites (those with mass greater than or equal to the threshold value) vanishes when density
\rho
falls below a critical density
\rho_{c}
.
One of the most striking characteristics of such a transition is that, unlike in equilibrium, where fluctuations diverge for a continuous phase transition, mass fluctuations are suppressed further upon approaching criticality (from above) and, in the thermodynamic limit, eventually vanishes at the critical point. Such a phenomenon is known as
hyperuniformity

Torquato and Stillinger 2003
;
Torquato 2016
;
Torquato 2018
;
Torquato 2021
and has been receiving a lot of attention
Pine et al. 2005
;
Corté et al. 2008
;
Jeanneret and Bartolo 2014
;
Hexner and Levine 2015
;
Weijs et al. 2015
;
Grassberger et al. 2016
;
Hexner and Levine 2017
;
Garcia-Millan et al. 2018a
.
While time-series of avalanches in sandpiles have been extensively investigated in the past
Kertesz and Kiss 1990
;
Manna and Kertész 1991
;
Laurson et al. 2005
, the time-dependent properties related to large-scale transport and relaxation have received much less attention
Carlson et al. 1990
;
da Cunha et al. 2009
;
Yadav et al. 2012
;
Garcia-Millan et al. 2018b
. Only recently, it has been shown that the relaxation of long-wavelength density perturbations in the celebrated Manna sandpile, which has only one conserved quantity (mass), is diffusive far from criticality, but exhibits transport instabilities near criticality
Chatterjee et al. 2018
;
Tapader et al. 2021
;
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
. That is, the density-dependent transport coefficients - the self- and bulk-diffusion coefficients and the particle mobility - are all singular. Near criticality, while the mobility
\chi(\rho)\sim\Delta^{\beta}
and the self-diffusion coefficient
{\cal D}_{s}(\rho)\sim\Delta^{\beta}
vanish as a function of the relative density
\Delta=\rho-\rho_{c}
, the bulk-diffusion coefficient diverges
D(\rho)\sim\Delta^{-(1-\beta)}
, resulting in anomalous (superdiffusive) transport where the dynamic exponent
z=2-(1-\beta)/\nu_{\perp}
was found to be related to the (static) order parameter exponent
\beta
and correlation length exponent
\nu_{\perp}

Chatterjee et al. 2018
;
Tapader et al. 2021
.
A detailed study of the near-critical properties of the Oslo model was carried out in Ref.
Grassberger et al. 2016
and the standard critical exponents were numerically found to be close to the rational values with
\beta\approx 5/21
,
\nu_{\perp}\approx 4/3
and
z\approx 10/7
. However, the question of how multiple conservation laws affect the dynamic properties and the universality class
Le Doussal and Wiese 2015
;
Shapira and Wiese 2023
is not fully understood yet.

In this paper, by using a microscopic approach, we provide a large-scale characterization of various static and dynamic properties of mass and current in the Oslo model far from as well as near criticality; we consider the system strictly above criticality. We begin with long-wavelength density relaxations, demonstrating that, despite having highly constrained microscopic dynamics due to center-of-mass conservation, the Oslo model exhibits diffusive relaxation away from criticality and super-diffusive relaxation near criticality.
Next we study dynamic fluctuations by analytically calculating unequal-time (two-point) correlation functions, and associated power spectra, involving current and mass.
We find a mass-conservation principle, which connects (dynamic) current and (static) mass fluctuations and exactly determines the decay exponents of the near-critical dynamic correlation functions in terms of the standard static exponents. Away from criticality, we exactly calculate within the closure scheme the decay exponent.
Notably, the dynamic correlation functions are qualitatively different from that observed in diffusive systems with a single conservation law, and thus from that seen in the conserved Manna sandpiles.
The findings are summarized below.

(I)

Density relaxation.–
Far from criticality, by performing a diffusive scaling of space
X\rightarrow x=X/L
, time
t\rightarrow\tau=t/L^{2}
and local density
\rho(X,t)=g(X/L^{2},t/L^{2})
with
L
being system size, we show that the space and time dependent coarse-grained density
g(x,\tau)
satisfies a nonlinear diffusion equation
\partial_{\tau}g(x,\tau)=\partial_{x}[D(g)\partial_{x}g(x,\tau)]
, where
D(g)=da(g)/dg
and
a(g)
are the density-dependent bulk-diffusion coefficient and the steady-state activity, respectively.
However, near criticality, the system becomes super-diffusive, with diverging
D\sim\Delta^{-(1-\beta)}
, thus implying relaxation time
\tau_{r}\sim L^{2}/D\sim L^{z}
with the dynamic exponent
z=2-(1-\beta)/\nu_{\perp}
, a relation which is, quite remarkably, exactly satisfied by the critical exponents conjectured in Ref.
Grassberger et al. 2016
.

(II)

A mass-conservation principle and its consequences.–
We obtain a mass-conservation principle, which connects the time-integrated bond-current fluctuation
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle
and the static mass fluctuation
\left\langle\Delta M_{l}^{2}\right\rangle
where
\Delta M_{l}=\left(M_{l}-\langle M_{l}\rangle\right)
, with
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(T)
and
M_{l}
being the cumulative current across
i
th bond in a time interval
[0,T]
and mass in a subsystem of size
l
, respectively. Indeed, for any fixed
\rho>\rho_{c}
, the asymptotic values of current and mass fluctuations
\Sigma^{2}_{Q}(\rho)
=
\lim_{L\to\infty}\left[\lim_{T\to\infty}\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle\right]
and
\Sigma^{2}_{M}(\rho)=\lim_{l\to\infty}\left[\lim_{L\to\infty}\left\langle\Delta M_{l}^{2}\right\rangle\right]
, respectively, converge to a finite value. By using a mass-conservation principle, they are related through an exact equality,

\Sigma^{2}_{Q}(\rho)=\Sigma^{2}_{M}(\rho).

(1)

Consequently, the particle mobility
\chi(\rho)\equiv\lim_{L\to\infty}[\lim_{T\to\infty}L\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T,L)\right\rangle/2T]
, which is defined by taking the infinite-time limit first and then the infinite-volume limit, vanishes identically for any density
\rho>\rho_{c}
, as opposed to the conserved Manna sandpile, or any other diffusive systems with a single conservation law, for which the mobility is finite (except at critical point).
Interestingly, both the asymptotic current and mass fluctuations,
\Sigma^{2}_{Q}(\rho)
and
\Sigma^{2}_{M}(\rho)
, respectively, are a nonmonotonic function of density; as a function of
\Delta=\rho-\rho_{c}
, they diverge as
\Delta^{2}
for
\Delta\gg\rho_{c}
and as
\Delta^{-\delta}
, with
\delta>0
, for
\Delta\to 0^{+}
.

(III)

Finite-time characteristics of time-integrated bond current.–
Temporal growth of time-integrated current fluctuation is qualitatively different from that in diffusive systems with a single conservation law. In the regime
1\ll T\ll L^{2}
, we show that, far from criticality, the time-integrated bond-current fluctuation saturates as
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle\simeq\Sigma^{2}_{Q}(\rho)-{\rm const.}T^{-1/2}
.
However, near criticality, the current fluctuation grows as
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle\sim T^{\alpha}
, but eventually saturates to a large
\Sigma^{2}_{Q}(\rho)\sim\Delta^{-\delta}
. By using eq.(
1
), we determine
\alpha=\delta/\nu_{\perp}z
and
\delta=2\zeta/\nu_{\perp}=2(1-1/\nu_{\perp})/\nu_{\perp}
in terms of the standard critical exponents - hyperuniformity, correlation-length and dynamic exponents
\zeta
,
\nu_{\perp}
and
z
, respectively.

(IV)

Statistics of instantaneous current.–
By decomposing instantaneous current
\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\equiv\\
d\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)/dt
as a sum of diffusive part
\mathcal{J}^{(d)}
and a fluctuating (or “noise”) part
\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{J}^{(d)}(t)+\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}(t)
, we calculate respective dynamic correlation functions. We show that, far from criticality and for long times
t
, the current correlation function is long-ranged and decays as
\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\mathcal{J}_{i}(0)\right\rangle\simeq-{\rm const.}t^{-5/2}
, which is faster than that in the Manna sandpile, having a
t^{-3/2}
power-law decay.
However, the correlation function for the fluctuating current is delta correlated in time, i.e.,
\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{r}(t)\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{0}(0)\right\rangle=\Gamma_{r}\delta(t)
, with space-integrated strength
\sum\Gamma_{r}=0
, implying greatly suppressed current fluctuations, and thus resulting in the temporal and spatial hyperuniformity, in the system due to the CoM conservation.

(V)

Power spectrum of current and mass.–
Likewise, as
f\to 0
, we find that the power-spectrum of the instantaneous bond current
S_{\mathcal{J}}(f)\sim f^{\psi_{\mathcal{J}}}
with
\psi_{\mathcal{J}}=1/2+\mu
, where
\mu=1
away from criticality and
\mu=1/2-\delta/\nu_{\perp}z
near criticality; the decay is much faster compared to diffusive systems with a single conservation law, where
\mu=0
.
Notably the above results imply that the current fluctuation far from criticality is much more suppressed than that near criticality.
On the other hand, for small frequency, the power spectrum for subsystem mass
S_{M}(f)\sim f^{-\psi_{M}}
grows with decreasing frequency, where the corresponding exponent is
\psi_{M}=2-\psi_{\mathcal{J}}
; similar to the current fluctuations, the mass fluctuations far from criticality are much more suppressed than that near criticality.

(VI)

Static structure factor.–
We find that the static subsystem mass fluctuations are always hyperuniform, both far from and near criticality (strictly, from above), where the structure factor
S(q)\sim q^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>2
. Away from criticality, we analytically calculate, within our closure scheme, the structure factor and show that, for small wavenumber
q\to 0
,
S(q)
is characterized by exponent
\gamma=2
, thus exhibiting an extreme form of (“class I”) hyperuniformity
Torquato 2016
in the active phase and thus providing a microscopic explanation for previous numerical observations in a CoM conserving system
Hexner and Levine 2017
. Notably, the mass fluctuation is more suppressed far from criticality than that near criticality, consistent with the dynamic behavior of current and mass fluctuations [as described in points (II) - (V)].

(VII)

Self-diffusion coefficient of tagged particles.–
Finally, we show that the density-dependent self-diffusion coefficient
\mathcal{D}_{s}
exactly equals to the ratio of density-dependent activity to the density itself, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{s}(\bar{\rho})=a(\bar{\rho})/\bar{\rho}
. The result implies that, like conserved Manna sandpile
da Cunha et al. 2009
;
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
, the behavior of the self-diffusion coefficient near criticality is exactly same as that of the order parameter - the activity. That is, like the activity,
\mathcal{D}_{s}(\Delta)
decays as
\Delta^{\beta}
, where
\beta
is the order-parameter exponent.

II
Model

We consider the conserved-mass Oslo model - a prototype of conserved stochastic sandpiles
Dickman et al. 2001
, on a one-dimensional periodic lattice of size
L
. The continuous-time microscopic dynamical rule for a site
i\in[0
,
1
,
2
,
\ldots
,
L-1]
is specified in terms of two local dynamical variables - the mass and the threshold mass for toppling. Mass (also called “height”) or number of particles
m_{i}\geq 0
at site
i
takes integer values with total mass
N=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}m_{i}
conserved; the global density is denoted by
\rho=N/L
. The threshold value at site
i
is denoted as
m_{c,i}
, with
m_{c,i}=2
or
3
,reset to a new randomly chosen value after
a toppling occurs at the site. An
active
site
i
, with
m_{i}\geq m_{c,i}
, topples with unit rate by
deterministically
transferring two particles, one to its right-nearest neighbor and another to its left-nearest neighbor, followed by a random resetting of the threshold mass at site
i
.
The continuous-time update rules of local mass
m_{i}(t)
at a site
i
in an infinitesimal time interval
(t,t+dt)
can be wriiten as

\displaystyle m_{i}(t+dt)=\begin{cases}\textbf{{events}}&\textbf{{probabilities}}\\
m_{i}(t)+1&\hat{a}_{i+1}dt\\
m_{i}(t)+1&\hat{a}_{i-1}dt\\
m_{i}(t)-2&\hat{a}_{i}dt\\
m_{i}(t)&\left(1-\Sigma dt\right),\end{cases}

(2)

where the sum of (exit) rates
\Sigma=\left(\hat{a}_{i+1}+\hat{a}_{i-1}+\hat{a}_{i}\right)
and we denote
\hat{a}_{i}
as an indicator function,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{{\rm for} }m_{i}\geq m_{c,i},\\
0&\text{{\rm otherwise}}.\end{cases}

(3)

The conserved Oslo model violates detailed balance (or, the microcopic time reversibility) in the bulk as the reverse transition corresponding to any allowed transition is forbidden. The system exhibits a continuous absorbing-phase transition (APT) below a critical density
\rho_{c}\approx 1.732594

Grassberger et al. 2016
; that is, for global density
\rho<\rho_{c}
, dynamical activities (topplings) cease to exist and the system settles into one of the many absorbing states, frozen in time and with the number of active sites being zero. On the other hand, for
\rho\geq\rho_{c}
, the system remains in an active state, where the dynamical activities go on for ever.
The absorbing phase transition is characterized through the steady-state density of active sites, or simply activity
a(\rho)
- the “order parameter”, which is computed as the steady-state average
a(\rho)=\sum_{i}\langle\hat{a}_{i}\rangle/L
and depends on the global density
\rho
.
Interestingly, the standard critical exponents for the Oslo model are known and, through large-scale simulations, they were found to be close to rational fractions, i.e., the order-parameter, correlation length and dynamic exponents are given by
\beta\approx 5/21
,
\nu_{\perp}\approx 4/3
, and
z\approx 10/7
, respectively
Grassberger et al. 2016
.

III
Theory of Density relaxation

III.1
Hydrodynamics

In this section, we derive an exact hydrodynamic structure of the Oslo model. In contrast to the conserved Manna sandpile studied in Refs.
Chatterjee et al. 2018
;
Tapader et al. 2021
, where particle transfer is stochastic, particle transfer rules in the Oslo model are deterministic with one particle moving to the right and the other to the left during a toppling event, thus conserving the CoM. While, for the Manna sandpile, the stochastic nature of particle transfer ensures diffusive relaxation (away from criticality), it is not evident that the density relaxation in the Oslo model will be diffusive as well. Indeed, for relaxation processes in the far-from-critical regime, we provide here a precise theoretical argument for why the relaxation process in the latter case is in fact diffusive. We also argue why a suitably coarse-grained density field must satisfy a nonlinear diffusion equation, quite similar to that arises in the case of the Manna sandpile
Chatterjee et al. 2018
;
Tapader et al. 2021
.

We consider the Oslo model on a one dimensional periodic lattice having
L
sites. We specify the system through a local density
\rho_{X}(t)=\langle m_{X}(t)\rangle
- the average of the particle number
m_{X}(t)
at site
X
and time
t
; or, equivalently, we can define a local excess density
\Delta(X,t)=(\rho_{X}(t)-\rho_{c})
.
By using the microscopic rules as in (
2
), we can derive the following density-evolution equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{X}(t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[a_{X-1}(t)-2a_{X}(t)+a_{X+1}(t)]\equiv\nabla^{2}[a_{X}(t)],~~~

(4)

where
a_{X}(t)
is the space and time dependent average local activity; here we denote
\nabla^{2}
as the discrete Laplacian. It is worth noting that the density-evolution equation for the Oslo model is exactly the same in terms of appropriately defined corresponding local activities
Chatterjee et al. 2018
.
Now we immediately find that the above equation for locally conserved density field can be expressed as a (discrete) continuity equation
{\partial\rho_{X}(t)}/{\partial t}=J(X,t)-J(X+1,t),
where we define a local current

J(X,t)=a_{X-1}(t)-a_{X}(t)\equiv-\nabla a_{X}(t);

(5)

note that here we have the local current manifestly expressed as a discrete gradient of activity
a_{X}(t)
and is readily recognized as diffusive current, which satisfies the Fick’s law.
Importantly, we now invoke a local-equilibrium-like property of the inhomogeneous state so that the (“locally steady”) activity at long times can be essentially determined by the “local steady-state” activity, which depends on the local coarse-grained density - the slow variable in the system. In other words, we write

a_{X}(t)=\langle\hat{a}_{X}\rangle_{\rho(X,t)}^{st}=a[\rho_{X}(t)],

where
\langle.\rangle_{\rho_{X}(t)}^{st}
represents the steady-state average conditioned on the fact that the local density is
\rho_{X}(t)
.
As a result, the density evolution can be written in terms of a
nonlinear
diffusion equation,

\frac{\partial\rho_{X}(t)}{\partial t}\simeq\frac{\partial^{2}a[\rho_{X}(t)]}{\partial X^{2}}.

(6)

In the above equation,
a(\rho)
is the activity calculated, in the steady state, as a function of density
\rho
.
Furthermore, the density-dependent bulk-diffusion coefficient can now be written as the derivative of (density-dependent) steady-state activity w.r.t. density, i.e.,

D(\rho)=\frac{da(\rho)}{d\rho}.

(7)

The diffusive structure of equation (
6
) allows us to write the space and time dependent density in the scaling form,

\rho_{X}(t)=g\left(\frac{X}{L},\frac{t}{L^{2}}\right),

(8)

which, as one can immediately show, satisfies a nonlinear diffusion equation for coarse-grained density field
g(x,\tau)
,

\frac{\partial g(x,\tau)}{\partial\tau}=\frac{\partial^{2}a(g)}{\partial x^{2}}.

(9)

Note that, in the limit of
L
large, the rescaled space
x
and time
\tau
in the above equation can be considered a continuous variable and the local coarse-grained activity
a(g)
is a nonlinear function of
g
. In other words, the local-equilibrium hypothesis used to derive Eq. (
9
) translates into the assertion that the local activity is indeed slave to local density.
Furthermore, on a large (coarse-grained) spatial scale, we can represent the initial density as a function of scaled position
x=X/L
, i.e.,
\rho(X,t=0)\equiv\rho_{in}(X)=g_{in}\left({X}/{L}\right).
Clearly, the above equation must be solved given the initial condition
g(x,\tau=0)=g_{in}(x)
and has a unique solution. Now, with a given density field, the CoM is also fixed locally (as well as globally) and therefore does not constitute an independent time-evolution equation for the other locally conserved quantity, i.e., the local CoM field.

It is quite expected that, away from criticality, correlation length
\xi
in the system should be finite and hence the bulk-diffusion coefficient, as we find in this study, is nonzero and finite. In such scenario, the density perturbations, characterized by wave numbers
k\rightarrow 0
being small, relax over a time scale
1/k^{2}D(\rho)
; equivalently, for a system with size
L
, we have the relaxation time
\tau_{r}\sim L^{2}/D(\rho)
.
The above mentioned argument for diffusive scaling, on the other hand, does not work in the near-critical regime. In fact, for near-critical density
\rho_{X}(t)\sim\rho_{c}^{+}
, the bulk-diffusion coefficient diverges as
D(\rho)\sim(\rho-\rho_{c})^{\beta-1}
because, in this scaling regime, the activity
a(\rho)\sim(\rho-\rho_{c})^{\beta}
has a form of a power-law with order-parameter exponent
\beta
being model-dependent, but usually less than one
Grassberger et al. 2016
;
Dickman et al. 2001
; in other words, the particle transport should be anomalous (superdiffusive) near criticality.

Therefore we conclude that the hydrodynamic structure for density relaxation in the conserved-mass Oslo model is indeed quite similar to that in the case of the conserved Manna sandpile. This finding establishes one of the main results of this paper that, despite constrained dynamics
Han et al. 2023
;
Bertrand et al. 2019
, the density relaxation is
not always subdiffusive
in systems with CoM conservation. However, we must mention here that, due to the CoM conservation, the dynamic correlation functions for density and current fluctuations, on the contrary, are drastically different from that observed in the Manna sandpile
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
.

In the subsequent section, we obtain explicit theoretical solutions of Eq. (
9
) (mostly obtained by a numerical integration scheme) for various initial conditions and compare them with direct Monte Carlo simulations. For the purpose of comparing the results for the Oslo model with that in the Manna sandpile, we consider similar parameter regimes (e.g., densities and system sizes, etc.) and the initial conditions for density field (e.g., step-like, wedge-like and Gaussian density profiles) as considered previously
Tapader et al. 2021
.

III.2
Comparison: Theory and simulations

In this section, we explicitly obtain the solutions of the nonlinear diffusion equation (
9
), corresponding to various initial conditions
\rho(x,0)\equiv g_{in}(x)
in the domain
x\in[0,1]
; we consider throughout a periodic boundary condition
\rho(0)=\rho(1)
.
Starting from an initial density profile, we study the relaxation of density profiles on macroscopically large spatio-temporal scales. These initial density profiles, in most cases, are taken as a step, or box, density profile; though, in a few cases, wedge and Gaussian initial density profiles are also considered.
In this way, we verify the nonlinear diffusion equation (
9
), where we perform microscopic simulations in various density regimes (far from and near criticality) and compare the space and time dependent density with that obtained by theoretically solving equation (
9
).

To investigate the hydrodynamic theory, we numerically integrate eq.(
9
) by using the Euler method of numerical integration by discretizing space
x
as
\delta x=10^{-3}
and time
\tau
as
\delta\tau=10^{-7}
, respectively. We integrate the diffusion equation by using the explicit density dependence of the nonlinear function
a(\rho)
- the steady-state activity. We generate the steady-state activity

vs.
\rho
from direct microscopic simulations of the Oslo model and measure the activity in steps of density
\delta\rho=10^{-2}
. In the numerical integration scheme, we calculate

III.2.1
Relaxation of density profile far from criticality

In this section, we study the density relaxation when the system is far from the criticality. In simulation, to produce the initial density profile, we have generated numerous random initial configurations and done an ensemble average over them. Now, to observe the density profile at subsequent times, we evolve in time the initial density profile by obeying the microscopic dynamics up to that time and then averaging over initial (random) configurations and also over stochastic trajectories.

III.2.2
Near-critical density relaxation

The near-critical regime corresponds to local excess density
\Delta(X,t)\sim L^{-1/\nu_{\perp}}
very small; so the correlation length is large and it is of the order of the system size. As the correlation length is large, the transport is
not
diffusive any more. This is because the activity field
a(\rho)\sim\Delta^{\beta}
has a form of a power law where we have exponent
\beta<1
and thus has a singularity at the critical point
\rho=\rho_{c}
. As a result, the bulk-diffusion coefficient, which is simply the derivative of the activity with respect to density, varies as
D(\Delta)\sim\Delta^{\beta 1}
, which diverges at the critical point.
In the near-critical scaling regime, we use a finite-size scaling of the activity
A(\Delta,L)=L^{-\beta/\nu_{\perp}}{\cal A}(L^{1/\nu_{\perp}}\Delta)
, with
{\cal A}
being a function of the scaling variable
L^{1/\nu_{\perp}}\Delta

Dickman et al. 2001
;
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
. Consequently, we obtain a time-evolution equation of the scaled excess density
G(x,\tau)=L^{1/\nu_{\perp}}\Delta(X,t)
, satisfying a non-linear diffusion equation
\partial_{\tau}G(x,\tau)=\partial_{x}^{2}{\cal A}(G)
,
where space and time are scaled as following -
x={X}/{L}
and
\tau={t}/{L^{z}}
, respectively; here
z
is the dynamical exponent being determined via the two static exponents
\beta
and
\nu_{\perp}
, where we use the following scaling relation,

z=2-\frac{(1-\beta)}{\nu_{\perp}}.

(22)

The arguments given above can be verified in simulations.
To this end, we study rescaled excess density field
\mathcal{G}(x,\tau)
starting from a step initial density profile
\mathcal{G}_{in}(x)\equiv\mathcal{G}(x,\tau=0)
, which is given by

\displaystyle G_{in}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho_{1}&\mbox{for}~x_{1}\leq x\leq x_{2},\cr 0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We consider here the width of the initial profile
w=1/4
and height
\rho_{1}\simeq 5.0
from
x_{1}=1/2-w/2
to
x_{2}=1/2+w/2
. We generate the initial density profile over a uniform critical background density
\rho_{c}
.
Now, we verify the aforementioned ‘superdiffusive’ scaling of the time-dependent density profiles. From this verification, we proceed to test the scaling relation as in (
22
) as follows: We consider four systems with sizes
L_{1}=1500
,
L_{2}=2000
,
L_{3}=2500
, and
L_{4}=5000
. These systems evolve from an initial step profile given by (
III.2.2
) up to times
t_{1}=\tau L_{1}^{z}
,
t_{2}=\tau L_{2}^{z}
,
t_{3}=\tau L_{3}^{z}
, and
t_{4}=\tau L_{4}^{z}
, with
\tau
held fixed.
In this context, we determine the dynamic exponent
z
by employing the scaling relation (
22
), by incorporating the previously conjectured static exponents
\beta\simeq 5/21
and
\nu_{\perp}\simeq 4/3
for the conserved Oslo model
Grassberger et al. 2016
. As part of our finite-size-scaling argument regarding the bulk-diffusion coefficient, we expect the density profiles, evolved up to the above mentioned times, to exhibit a collapse onto each other.

III.2.3
Density relaxation on critical background

In this section, we study density relaxation on an infinite critical background having density
\rho_{c}
.
The excess density
\Delta
over the critical background is not taken too far away from criticality but much larger than
{\cal O}(L^{-1/\nu_{\perp}})
i.e., we have excess density of order
{\cal O}(L^{-1/\nu_{\perp}})\ll\Delta\raisebox{-3.22916pt}[-6.45831pt]{$\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\;$}1
. So, the correlation length is still large
\xi\gg 1
but
k\xi\ll 1
. In this regime, we exactly calculate, within our theory, the asymptotic scaling function for density profiles, which have evolved upto a long time. Near criticality, the activity
a(\Delta)
as a function of excess density
\Delta
is a power law, i.e., we can write
a(\Delta)\simeq C\Delta^{\beta},

where
C
is the constant of proportionality and
\beta
is the order parameter exponent. Now, we use the power-law scaling of activity in eq. (
6
), and, in that case, the time-evolution equation for excess density can be written as following,

\frac{\partial\Delta(X,t)}{\partial t}=C\frac{\partial^{2}[\Delta(X,t)]^{\beta}}{\partial X^{2}}.

(26)

To study the density relaxation in this regime, we take initial condition having a form of delta function
\Delta(X,t=0)=N_{1}\delta(X),

with
N_{1}
number of particles added at the midpoint, to create the initial perturbation over the critical background. The reason for taking such initial condition is that here we are interested to study evolution of a localized initial density profile on an infinite critical background on a large space and time scales. In that case, it is interesting that, for the following boundary condition
\Delta(x=\pm\infty,t)=0
, the nonlinear diffusion equation (
26
) can exactly be solved. We consider a scaling ansatz of the excess density as

\Delta(X,t)=\frac{1}{(Ct)^{\omega}}{\cal G}\left[\frac{X}{(Ct)^{\omega}}\right],

(27)

where the scaling function is
{\cal G}(y)
satisfies the following differential equation,

\frac{d^{2}{\cal G}^{\beta}}{dy^{2}}=\omega\left[{\cal G}+y\frac{d{\cal G}}{dy}\right],

(28)

where the growth exponent is given by
\omega={1}/(1+\beta)
; for the Oslo model,
\beta<1
and therefore
\omega>1/2
.
By solving eq. (
28
), we exactly have

{\cal G}(y)=\frac{1}{\left[g_{0}^{\beta-1}+\frac{\omega(1-\beta)}{2\beta}y^{2}\right]^{1/(1-\beta)}},

(29)

with the suitable choice of boundary conditions,
{\cal G}(y=0)=g_{0}
and
\left[{d{\cal G}}/{dy}\right]_{y=0}=0,

where
g_{0}
is the normalization constant, being proportional to the number of particles added initially in the system.

IV
Theory of Dynamic fluctuation

IV.1
Current fluctuation

IV.1.1
Definitions and notations

Due to the conserved dynamics of the Oslo model, the microscopic time-evolution equation of local density, as provided in Eq. (
4
), can be expressed in a form of a microscopic continuity equation. This equation involves the local difference of the microscopic instantaneous particle current operator, denoted as
\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)
, across the bond
(i,i+1)
in the temporal interval
(t,t+dt)
and can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho_{i}(t)}{dt}=\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i-1}(t)-\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\right\rangle,

(31)

where we write the average of instantaneous current as

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{a}_{i}(t)-\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\right\rangle.

(32)

We decompose the instantaneous current as a sum of diffusive current
\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)
and fluctuating current
\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}(t)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)+\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}(t),

(33)

where we identify the stochastic variable
\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)=\hat{a}_{i}(t)-\hat{a}_{i+1}(t),

(34)

with the following statistical properties of
\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)
and
\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}(t)
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)\right\rangle\equiv\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{a}_{i}(t)-\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\right\rangle,

(35)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}(t)\right\rangle=0,

(36)

so that Eq. (
33
) is consistent with Eq. (
31
). In this work, our main goal is to calculate dynamic correlation function
\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\mathcal{J}_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle
and the fluctuation properties of
\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}(t)
. To this end, we define time-integrated bond current
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)
, which represents cumulative (time-integrated) particle current across bond
(i,i+1)
in a time interval
\left[0,t\right]
and is related to instantaneous current
\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)=\left.\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t^{\prime})\right\rvert_{t^{\prime}=t}.

(37)

From the dynamic correlations of time-integrated current
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle
, we can find the dynamic correlation of instantaneous current as

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\mathcal{J}_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle,

(38)

for any arbitrary values of
t
and
t^{\prime}
. We introduce the following notation for correlation function,

\displaystyle C^{AB}_{r}(t,t^{\prime})=\left\langle A_{r}(t)B_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle-\left\langle A_{r}(t)\right\rangle\left\langle B_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle,

(39)

whereas the stationary correlation functions are defined as
C^{AB}_{r}(t)=C^{AB}_{r}(t,0)
. Also, here we define the Fourier transform of the correlation functions
C_{r}^{AB}(t,t^{\prime})
in the spatial domain of
r
as

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{q}^{AB}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum\limits_{r=0}^{L-1}C_{r}^{AB}(t,t^{\prime})e^{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}qr},

(40)

where we have
q=2\pi k/L
with
k=0
,
1
,
\ldots
,
L-1
; then the inverse Fourier transform can be written as

\displaystyle C_{r}^{AB}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{q}\tilde{C}_{q}^{AB}(t,t^{\prime})e^{-\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}qr},

(41)

with
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}^{2}=-1
.

IV.1.2
Correlation of integrated current and the truncation scheme

In this section, we study the unequal-space-time correlation of the integrated bond current
\mathcal{Q}
starting from writing the evolution equations, which can be obtained from the following infinitesimal-time update rules for the quantity
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t+dt)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t+dt)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\textbf{{events}}&\textbf{{probabilities}}\\
\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)+1\right)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&\hat{a}_{i}(t)dt\\
\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)-1\right)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)dt\\
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&1-\Sigma dt,\end{cases}

(42)

for
t>t^{\prime}
, where
\Sigma=\left(\hat{a}_{i}(t)+\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\right)
. From the above update rules, we obtain the time-evolution equation for the two-point correlation function involving integrated bond current,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}C_{r}^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}(t,t^{\prime})=\left\langle\left\{\hat{a}_{i}(t)-\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\right\}\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle,

(43)

solving which, we obtain the exact expression for the unequal-time unequal-space correlation function of the integrated current,
C^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(t,t^{\prime})
, as

\displaystyle C_{r}^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}(t,t^{\prime})=\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t^{\prime\prime})\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle+C_{r}^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

(44)

where
\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t^{\prime\prime})
on the right-hand side of the above equation is given by Eq. (
34
). Note that in Eqs. (
43
) and (
44
), we omit all terms containing the average of the integrated current,
\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\rangle
, since we are only concerned with the steady state where
\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\rangle=0
due to the periodic boundary condition and the absence of any biasing force.

To further simplify the Eq. (
44
), we need to calculate the unequal-time unequal-space correlation function for the diffusive current and the time-integrated current, which involves the dynamic correlation function of activity and integrated current. One could start by writing down the corresponding update rules for the function
\hat{a}_{i}(t+dt)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})
within an infinitesimal-time interval
(t,t+dt)
, and try to obtain the corresponding evolution equation,

d\left\langle\hat{a}_{i}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle/dt
, which generates more higher-order correlation functions and thus we obtain an infinite hierarchy of correlation functions.
Now we employ an approximate closure scheme along the lines of what was introduced in the context of the conserved Manna model
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
. The closure scheme is incorporated by expressing the microscopic diffusive current across a bond in terms of the difference in local mass between two neighboring sites connected by that specific bond as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)\simeq D(\bar{\rho})\left(m_{i}(t)-m_{i+1}(t)\right),

(45)

where
D(\bar{\rho})=a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})
is the bulk-diffusion coefficient as defined in Eq. (
7
).
In Eq. (
45
), we assume that any difference in local activity should follow the difference in local mass. This implies that the diffusive current is proportional to the local mass difference, with an additional assumption that fluctuations are quite small, allowing us to express the bulk diffusion coefficient obtained for the corresponding global density as the proportionality constant. The approximation in Eq. (
45
) essentially implies that, involving any correlation function involving diffusive current and another dynamic quantity, we should substitute the diffusive current with Eq. (
45
); for example, consider the following correlation function,

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)A_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left\{\hat{a}_{i}(t)-\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\right\}A_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq D(\bar{\rho})\left\langle\left\{m_{i}(t)-m_{i+1}(t)\right\}A_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle,

(46)

where
A
could be any dynamic observable.
Now, by substituting Eq.(
45
) in Eq.(
44
) we obtain,

\displaystyle C_{r}^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\left\langle\left\{m_{i}(t^{\prime\prime})-m_{i+1}(t^{\prime\prime})\right\}\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+C_{r}^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

(47)

which requires the correlation function
C_{r}^{m\mathcal{Q}}(t,t^{\prime})
instead of the correlation involving activity variable and integrated current. By using the following update rules of the function
m_{i}(t+dt)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})
for
t>t^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle m_{i}(t+dt)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\textbf{{events}}&\textbf{{probabilities}}\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&\left(\hat{a}_{i+1}+\hat{a}_{i-1}\right)dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)-2\right)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&\hat{a}_{i}dt\\
m_{i}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&\left(1-\Sigma dt\right),\end{cases}

(48)

where the probability of happening nothing is
\Sigma dt
, we obtain the corresponding evolution equation for
C_{r}^{m\mathcal{Q}}(t,t^{\prime})
as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}C^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{i,k}\left\langle a_{k}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left\{\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i-1}(t)-\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)\right\}\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(49)

where
\Delta_{i,k}=(\delta_{i+1,k}+\delta_{i-1,k}-2\delta_{i,k})
is the discrete Laplacian.
We further simplify the above equation by approximating the diffusive current by Eq. (
45
) and obtain the following equation,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}C^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(t,t^{\prime})\simeq a^{\prime}({\bar{\rho}})\sum_{k}\Delta_{i,k}\left\langle m_{k}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(50)

We can write the solution of the above equation using the discrete Fourier transformation (defined in Eq.(
40
)) as

\displaystyle\tilde{C}^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{q}(t,t^{\prime})=\exp\left[-a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\lambda_{q}(t-t^{\prime})\right]\tilde{C}^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{q}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

(51)

where
\tilde{C}^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{q}(t,t^{\prime})
is the Fourier transformed
C^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(t,t^{\prime})
and
\lambda_{q}=2[1-\cos{q}]
are the eigenvalues of the discrete Laplacian.
Although Eq. (
IV.1.2
) along with Eq. (
51
) completely describe the dynamical correlations of the integrated current, we need to calculate the equal-time correlation of current and mass to obtain the complete solution.

By using the dynamical update rules of
m_{i}(t+dt)

\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t+dt)
, we readily write the infinitesimal time-evolution equation for the equal-time two-point spatial correlation of mass and current, denoted as
C^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(t,t)
, as following:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}C^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(t,t)\simeq a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\sum_{k}\Delta_{i,k}\left\langle m_{k}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)\right\rangle+f_{r}(t),

(52)

where
f_{r}(t)
is the source of the corresponding correlation function and is given as

\displaystyle f_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=C^{m\hat{a}}_{r}(t,t)-C^{m\hat{a}}_{r+1}(t,t)

\displaystyle+a\left\{3\left(\delta_{0,r+1}-\delta_{0,r}\right)+\left(\delta_{0,r-1}-\delta_{0,r+2}\right)\right\};

(53)

for details, see appendix (
A
).
The solution of Eq. (
52
), obtained through the Fourier transformation, is given by,

\displaystyle\tilde{C}^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{q}(t,t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\exp\left[-a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\lambda_{q}(t-t^{\prime})\right]\tilde{f}_{q}(t^{\prime}),

(54)

where we write the Fourier transform of the source term as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{q}=ae^{-\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}2q}\left(e^{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}q}-1\right)^{3}+\tilde{C}_{q}^{m\hat{a}}\left(1-e^{-\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}q}\right).

(55)

We further calculate the correlation function
C^{m\hat{a}}_{r}(t,t)
by using the steady-state condition of equal-time two-point spatial mass correlation function,
dC_{r}^{mm}(t,t)/dt=0
, from which we obtain

\displaystyle C^{m\hat{a}}_{r}(t,t)=a\delta_{0,r}-\frac{a}{2}\left(\delta_{0,r-1}-\delta_{0,r+1}\right);

(56)

for details see appendix (
B
). Correspondingly, the Fourier transform of
C^{m\hat{a}}_{r}(t,t)
,
\tilde{C}_{q}^{m\hat{a}}
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{C}^{m\hat{a}}_{q}(t,t)=a\frac{\lambda_{q}}{2},

(57)

and finally putting Eq. (
57
) in Eq. (
55
) we write

\tilde{f}_{q}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{q}=-a\frac{\lambda_{q}}{2}\left(1-e^{-\textbf{{i}}q}\right).

(58)

By substituting, Eq.(
58
) in Eq.(
54
) and then putting Eq.(
54
) in Eq.(
51
), we finally obtain the Fourier transform of unequal-time mass and integrated current as

\displaystyle\tilde{C}^{m\mathcal{Q}}_{q}(t,t^{\prime})=-a(\bar{\rho})\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}e^{-a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\lambda_{q}(t-t^{\prime\prime})}\frac{\lambda_{q}}{2}\left(1-e^{-\textbf{{i}}q}\right).

(59)

To obtain the complete expression of Eq. (
IV.1.2
), we still need to calculate the equal-time correlation of the integrated current, which is given on the right-hand side of the corresponding equation. On the other hand, the first integral on the right-hand side is explicitly computed by using the inverse Fourier transform of Eq. (
59
).
It can be shown from the microscopic update rules of the function
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t+dt)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t+dt)
, the equal-time integrated current correlation satisfies the following evolution equation,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}C^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(t,t)=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{r}(t)+C_{r}^{\mathcal{J}^{(d)}\mathcal{Q}}(t,t)+C_{L-r}^{\mathcal{J}^{(d)}\mathcal{Q}}(t,t),

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\Gamma_{r}(t)+a^{\prime}\left\{C_{r}^{m\mathcal{Q}}(t,t)-C_{r-1}^{m\mathcal{Q}}(t,t)\right\}

\displaystyle+a^{\prime}\left\{C_{L-r}^{m\mathcal{Q}}(t,t)-C_{L-r-1}^{m\mathcal{Q}}(t,t)\right\},

(60)

where we denote
\Gamma_{r}
as the strength of the fluctuating current, or
\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}(t)\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i+r}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\Gamma_{r}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
; for a detailed proof of this relation see Ref.
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
. Using microscopic dynamics, as demonstrated in appendix
C
, the expression for
\Gamma_{i,j}

\Gamma_{r}

r=j-i

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i,j}=u_{i,j}(t)-u_{i+1,j}(t)

(61)

where
u_{i,j}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle u_{i,j}(t)=\hat{a}_{i}(t)\left(\delta_{i,j}-\delta_{i-1,j}\right).

(62)

In the steady state, we can write
\Gamma_{r}
simply as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{r}=2a\delta_{0,r}-a\delta_{0,r+1}-a\delta_{0,r-1},

(63)

which is derived in Eq. (
146
).
We obtain the full solution of Eq. (
IV.1.2
), which constitutes the second part of Eq. (
IV.1.2
), by substituting the inverse Fourier transform of Eq. (
54
) for equal-time mass and integrated current correlation. Finally, using the inverse Fourier transform of Eq.(
59
) in Eq.(
IV.1.2
) and the solution of Eq.(
IV.1.2
), we get the unequal-time unequal-space correlation function of the integrated current for
t\geq t^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle C_{r}^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}(t,t^{\prime})=\int\limits_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\Gamma_{r}(t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle-\frac{a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})a(\bar{\rho})}{L}\sum\limits_{q}\int\limits_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{t^{\prime\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime\prime}e^{-a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\lambda_{q}(t^{\prime\prime}-t^{\prime\prime\prime})}\frac{\lambda_{q}^{2}}{2}\left[2-\lambda_{qr}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})a(\bar{\rho})}{L}\sum\limits_{q}\int\limits_{t}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime\prime}e^{-a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\lambda_{q}(t^{\prime\prime}-t^{\prime\prime\prime})}\frac{\lambda_{q}^{2}}{2}e^{-\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}qr}.

(64)

The above expression of the dynamic current correlation function is subtly different from that in the Manna sandpile studied in Ref.
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
and has significant ramifications for the decay exponents of unequal-time current correlations and particle mobility.
To precisely understand the physical consequences of the above formula, and the corresponding fluctuating hydrodynamics of the Oslo model, we now study the detailed statistical properties of the space- and time-integrated current in the system.

IV.1.3
Fluctuating current and its relation to total current

Now we study the dynamic (two-point) correlation function for the fluctuating part
\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}(t)
of the instantaneous bond current, as defined in Eq.(
33
), and we have

\displaystyle C^{\mathcal{J}^{\left(fl\right)}\mathcal{J}^{\left(fl\right)}}_{r}(t,t^{\prime}=0)\equiv C^{\mathcal{J}^{\left(fl\right)}\mathcal{J}^{\left(fl\right)}}_{r}(t)=\delta(t)\Gamma_{r}(\bar{\rho}),

(65)

where
\Gamma_{r}
is given in Eq. (
61
). The above equation implies that the variance of the fluctuating current across a single bond is obtained by setting
r=0
in Eq. (
61
) as given below:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C^{\mathcal{J}^{\left(fl\right)}\mathcal{J}^{\left(fl\right)}}_{0}(t)dt=\Gamma_{0}=2a(\bar{\rho}).

(66)

However, the variance of the total integrated fluctuating current is identically
zero
,

\displaystyle\sum_{r}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C^{\mathcal{J}^{\left(fl\right)}\mathcal{J}^{\left(fl\right)}}_{r}(t)dt=\sum_{r}\Gamma_{r}=0.

(67)

In a diffusive system, the variance
\left\langle\bar{Q}^{2}(L,T)\right\rangle

-

\left\langle\bar{Q}(L,T)\right\rangle^{2}
of the space-time-integrated particle current
\bar{Q}(L,T)=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(T)
in a system of size
L
and up to time
T
in steady state is directly related to the density-dependent particle mobility, or, equivalently conductivity in a charged system,
\chi(\rho)=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}

\left\langle\bar{Q}^{2}(L,T)\right\rangle/2LT

Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
. Alternatively, it can be shown that the actual current fluctuation is also related to the variance of fluctuating current as

\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\left\langle\bar{Q}^{2}(L,T)\right\rangle}{LT}=\sum_{r}\Gamma_{r},

(68)

which, by using Eq.(
67
), immediately leads to the particle mobility to exactly vanish, i.e.,
\chi=0
in the Oslo model. This result is consistent with our later finding that the current fluctuation
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle
saturates in the time domain
T\gg L^{2}
[see Eq.(
75
)]. That is, for
T\gg L^{2}
, the time-derivative of the bond current, which also equals to
2\chi/L
with
\chi
being the mobility [see Eq.(
76
)], is identically zero.

To substantiate Eq.(
66
) in simulations, we define the following quantity - the time-integrated fluctuating bond current up to time
T
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{(fl)}(T)=\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{J}_{i}^{(fl)}(t).

(69)

Then, by using Eq. (
61
), we obtain a fluctuation relation, which readily relates the scaled current fluctuation to the density-dependent activity,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left\langle\left[\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{(fl)}(T)\right]^{2}\right\rangle=2a(\bar{\rho}).

(70)

In Fig.
5
, we plotted the simulation data of
\left\langle\left[\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{(fl)}(T)\right]^{2}\right\rangle/T
across a bond and up to time
T=100
as a function of the relative density
\Delta=\rho-\rho_{c}
, shown as a solid blue line. We observe an excellent agreement with twice the activity, represented by the black dotted line, as given in Eq. (
70
).

Figure 5:
Intensive fluctuation of the excess bond current is plotted vs. relative density
\Delta=\bar{\rho}-\rho_{c}
(
\rho_{c}\approx 1.732
) for a system size of
L=1000
and
T=100
, represented by the solid blue line. Additionally, we plot twice the activity
2a
as a function of
\Delta
in the same plot, represented by the dotted black line, which exhibits an excellent match with the intensive excess current fluctuation as described by Eq. (

).

IV.1.4
Bond current fluctuation: Away from criticiality

The steady-state fluctuation of the time-integrated bond current,
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle
, can be obtained from Eq. (
IV.1.2
), which by setting
r=0
and
t=t^{\prime}=T
, results in,

\displaystyle C_{0}^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}(T,T)=\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{i}(T,L,\bar{\rho})\right\rangle=\frac{a(\bar{\rho})}{a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{q}\left(1-e^{-a^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\lambda_{q}T}\right).

(71)

Now we perform the asymptotic analysis of the above equation. By rearranging the sum, we rewrite the above equation as

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle=\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}-\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{q}e^{-a^{\prime}\lambda_{q}T}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{L}\right),

(72)

where we have used
\sum_{q}1/L\simeq 1
.
Now going to the continuum limit, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle\simeq\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}-2\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}\int_{1/L}^{1/2}e^{-a^{\prime}\lambda(x)T}dx,

(73)

where
\lambda(x)\simeq 4\pi^{2}x^{2}
. Furthermore, using the variable transformation
z=4\pi^{2}x^{2}a^{\prime}T
, the above expression can be simplified as

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle\simeq\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}-\frac{aT^{-1/2}}{2\pi{a^{\prime}}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{-z}}{\sqrt{z}}=\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}-\frac{aT^{-1/2}}{2\sqrt{\pi}{a^{\prime}}^{\frac{3}{2}}},

(74)

in the limit
T\gg 1
being large.
Note that, unlike the conserved Manna sandpile
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
or the symmetric simple exclusion processes
Sadhu and Derrida 2016
, where there is only a single conserved quantity, the bond-current fluctuation
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{i}(T,L,\bar{\rho})\right\rangle
in the Oslo model, for any fixed
\bar{\rho}
and in the limit of large time
T\gg L^{2}\gg 1
, saturates to a finite value
\Sigma^{2}_{Q}(\bar{\rho})
. That is, we have the existence of the following limit,

\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\left[\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{i}(T,L,\bar{\rho})\right\rangle\right]\equiv\Sigma^{2}_{Q}(\bar{\rho})=\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}.

(75)

The specific order of limits mentioned above means that we first take the infinite-time limit and then the infinite-system-size limit. For diffusive systems, the transport coefficient, which characterizes current fluctuation in the system, is the conductivity
\chi(\bar{\rho})
(or equivalently, mobility) and is defined as

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{i}(T,L,\bar{\rho})\right\rangle}{T}\equiv 2\frac{\chi(\bar{\rho})}{L}.

(76)

It should be noted that, in the long-time limit (
T\gg L^{2}
), the vanishing temporal growth of fluctuations of the time-integrated bond current in the Oslo model renders the conductivity
\chi(\bar{\rho})=0
- a direct consequence of the deterministic particle transfer during a toppling event.

IV.2
Mass fluctuation

The dynamic fluctuation properties of mass in a subsystem is another important quantity, that we study in this section.
To calculate the fluctuation of the mass of a subsystem of size
l
,
M_{l}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{l=1}m_{i}(t)
, we begin with the steady-state correlation function of the unequal time and unequal space of the single-site mass for
t\geq 0
,
C_{r}^{mm}(t,0)=

\left\langle m_{i}(t)m_{i+r}(0)\right\rangle-\left\langle m_{i}(t)\right\rangle\left\langle m_{i+r}(0)\right\rangle

\equiv

C_{r}^{mm}(t)
. It is evident that, due to the microscopic dynamics that permit simultaneous mass changes in three sites, we can anticipate the presence of spatial correlations involving more than just individual sites in the steady state.
The corresponding evolution equation of
C_{r}^{mm}(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}C_{r}^{mm}(t)=\sum\limits_{k}\Delta_{i,k}\left\langle\hat{a}_{k}(t)m_{i+r}(0)\right\rangle,

(77)

using the following update rules for
t>t^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle m_{i}(t+dt)m_{i+r}(t^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\textbf{{events}}&\textbf{{probabilities}}\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)m_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&\hat{a}_{i+1}dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)m_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&\hat{a}_{i-1}dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)-2\right)m_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&\hat{a}_{i}dt\\
m_{i}(t)m_{i+r}(t^{\prime})&1-\Sigma dt,\end{cases}

(78)

where
\Sigma dt=\left(\hat{a}_{i+1}+\hat{a}_{i-1}+\hat{a}_{i}\right)dt
is the probability of nothing happening.
Eq.(
77
) can be simplified further using the approximation scheme given in Eq.(
45
) as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}C_{r}^{mm}(t)\simeq{a^{\prime}}\sum\limits_{k}\Delta_{r,k}C^{mm}_{k}(t).

(79)

Solving the above equation in the Fourier modes, we obtain the following solution

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{q}^{mm}(t)\simeq e^{-{a^{\prime}}(\bar{\rho})\lambda_{q}t}\tilde{C}_{q}^{mm}(0),

(80)

where the factor
\tilde{C}_{q}^{mm}(0)
represents the Fourier transform of the (equal-time) two-point spatial correlation function. In the steady state, the corresponding equation involving this correlation function
C_{r}^{mm}(0)
, obtained by using the condition
dC_{r}^{mm}(t,t)/dt=0
, can be written as

\displaystyle 2{a^{\prime}}\sum\limits_{k}\Delta_{0,k}\left\langle m_{k}m_{r}\right\rangle+B_{r}=0,

(81)

where the source term
B_{r}
is given by

\displaystyle B_{r}

\displaystyle=6a(\bar{\rho})\delta_{0,r}-4a(\bar{\rho})\left(\delta_{0,r+1}+\delta_{0,r-1}\right)

\displaystyle+a(\bar{\rho})\left(\delta_{0,r+2}+\delta_{0,r-2}\right);

(82)

for details, see appendix
B
. The corresponding generating function
\tilde{G}(z)=\sum_{z=0}^{\infty}C_{r}^{mm}(t,t)z^{r}
, to solve Eq.(
IV.2
), can be derived in a similar way to Eq. (
141
), by multiplying the above equation by
z^{r}
and taking the sum over
r
from
0
to
\infty
, which finally leads to

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(z)=\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}-\frac{a}{2a^{\prime}}z.

(83)

The generating function leads to the steady-state spatial correlation function for mass,

\displaystyle C^{mm}_{r}(0)=\frac{a}{a^{\prime}}\delta_{0,r}-\frac{a}{2a^{\prime}}\left(\delta_{0,r-1}+\delta_{0,r+1}\right).

(84)

The Fourier transform of the above correlation function can immediately be found as

\displaystyle\tilde{C}^{mm}_{q}(t,t)=\frac{a}{2a^{\prime}}\lambda_{q},

(85)

which we put in Eq.(
80
) to obtain,

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{q}^{mm}(t)\simeq e^{-a^{\prime}\lambda_{q}t}\frac{a}{2a^{\prime}}\lambda_{q}.

(86)

Finally, using the inverse Fourier transform of
\tilde{C}_{q}^{mm}(t)
, we get our desired correlation function
C_{r}^{mm}(t)
as

C_{r}^{mm}(t)\simeq\frac{1}{L}\frac{a}{2a^{\prime}}\sum_{q}e^{-\textbf{{i}}qr}e^{-a^{\prime}\lambda_{q}t}\lambda_{q}.

(87)

We can now compute the time-dependent correlation function for mass
M_{l}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}m_{r}(t)
in a subsystem of size
l
(where we take
l<L
) by using the following standard identity,

\displaystyle C^{M_{l}M_{l}}(t)=lC_{0}^{mm}(t)+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{l-1}(l-r)\left(C_{r}^{mm}(t)+C_{-r}^{mm}(t)\right).

(88)

Then by substituting Eq.(
87
) into the above equation, we get the dynamic correlation function for mass of the subsystem as

C^{M_{l}M_{l}}(t)\simeq\frac{1}{L}\frac{a}{2{a^{\prime}}}\sum_{q}e^{-{a^{\prime}}\lambda_{q}t}\lambda_{ql}.

(89)

Using
\lambda_{ql}\simeq 2
for all values of
q
for
l\gg 1
,
L\gg 1
, and
l/L\ll 1
, and replacing the sum as an integral, followed by using the transformation
z=4\pi^{2}a^{\prime}x^{2}t
, we obtain the dynamical correlation function for the subsystem mass,

\displaystyle C^{M_{l}M_{l}}(t)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2a}{a^{\prime}}\int_{1/L}^{1/2}e^{-a^{\prime}\lambda(x)t}dx

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{a}{2\pi{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}t^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-z}}{\sqrt{z}}dz=\frac{a}{2\sqrt{\pi}{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}t^{-1/2},~~~~~~

(90)

which decays with time as
t^{-1/2}
.
Now, by taking the infinite time limit
t\to\infty
, the above equal-time correlation gives us the fluctuation of the subsystem mass,

C^{M_{l}M_{l}}(0)=\left\langle M_{l}^{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle M_{l}\right\rangle^{2}\equiv\Sigma^{2}_{M}(\bar{\rho},l),

(91)

which, in the limit of subsystem size
l\rightarrow\infty
, converges to a finite value and can be written as a function of global density
\bar{\rho}
only,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}_{M}(\bar{\rho})\equiv\lim_{l\to\infty}\Sigma^{2}_{M}(\bar{\rho},l)=\frac{a}{{a^{\prime}}};

(92)

here we have already taken the thermodynamic limit
L\to\infty
with
l/L\to 0
. In diffusive systems with a single conservation law (such as symmetric exclusion process or the Manna sandpile away from criticality), the subsystem mass fluctuation as in Eq.(
91
) grows as
l
, which is not the case here. As we discuss later in the context of static structure factor, this particular observation is related to an extreme form of hyperuniformity (class I).
Moreover, by comparing Eq.(
75
) and Eq.(
92
), we immediately obtain the following identity,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}_{Q}(\bar{\rho})=\Sigma^{2}_{M}(\bar{\rho}),

(93)

which relates asymptotic (dynamic) time-integrated bond current fluctuation and the asymptotic (static) subsystem mass fluctuation.
We mention here that, while it captures the dynamic properties of the system remarkably well, the closure scheme, perhaps not surprisingly, is not strictly applicable in the near-critical regime since the spatial correlations in that case become long-ranged and are not captured in the calculations. However, the fluctuation relation as in eq. (
93
), which is a direct consequence of a mass-conservation principle (discussed below), is found to be valid both near and far from criticality [see Fig.
6
]. Importantly, as argued later, eq. (
93
) can be used to determine the exponent governing the near-critical temporal growth of current in terms of the standard static exponents.
Indeed the relation in eq. (
93
) could be understood physically from a mass-conservation principle as following. Let us consider a spatial domain
[0,l-1]
of size
l
and having mass
M_{l}(t)
at time
t
. Now the time-integrated boundary current
{\cal Q}^{B}(t)={\cal Q}_{-1}(t)-{\cal Q}_{l-1}(t)
flown into the subsystem in a time interval
[0,t]
is identically equal to the difference in the subsystem mass
\Delta M_{l}(t)=M_{l}(t)-M_{l}(0)
at two times
t=0
and
t
. Therefore, the corresponding fluctuations are also equal, i.e.,
\langle[{\cal Q}^{B}(t)]^{2}\rangle=\langle[\Delta M_{l}(t)]^{2}\rangle=\langle[M_{l}(0)]^{2}\rangle+\langle[M_{l}(t)]^{2}\rangle-2\langle M_{l}(0)M_{l}(t)\rangle
; the equality is valid for any time
t
and can be suitably generalized to higher dimensions. Now, by first taking the infinite-time limit and then the infinite-subsystem (and infinite-system) limit (i.e.,
t\gg L^{2}
and
L\gg l\gg 1
), and then assuming complete decorrelation between
M_{l}(t)
and
M_{l}(0)
[as shown in eq. (
IV.2
)] as well as that between
{\cal Q}_{-1}(t)
and
{\cal Q}_{l-1}(t)
, we recover the relation as given in eq. (
93
). It should be noted here that the above scaling limit does not exist in the case of the conserved-mass Manna sandpile, and also other diffusive systems with a single conservation law, for which the time-integrated boundary current fluctuations
\langle{\cal Q}^{2}_{l}(t)\rangle
or
\langle[{\cal Q}^{B}(t)]^{2}\rangle
, and therefore
\langle[\Delta M_{l}(t)]^{2}\rangle
, grow with time.

IV.3.4
Instantaneous current correlation: Away from criticality

In this section, we demonstrate that the instantaneous current correlation decays faster than other diffusive systems, such as the Manna model.
The instantaneous bond-current correlation,
C_{r}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)

=

\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\mathcal{J}_{i+r}(0)\right\rangle

-

\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\right\rangle\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{i+r}(0)\right\rangle
for
t\geq t^{\prime}
, can be obtained by differentiating Eq.(
IV.1.2
) as

\displaystyle C_{r}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)=\left[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}C_{r}^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}(t,t^{\prime})\right]_{t^{\prime}=0,t\geq 0},

(102)

which gives us,

\displaystyle C_{r}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)=\Gamma_{r}\delta(t)-aa^{\prime}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{q}e^{-\lambda_{q}a^{\prime}t}\frac{\lambda_{q}^{2}}{2}e^{-\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}qr}.

(103)

The decay of the current correlation over time is governed by the second part of the right-hand side of Eq. (
103
). Its asymptotics for
t\gg 1
and for a single bond
r=0
can be understood by converting the sum into an integral in the continuum limit using
i\rightarrow x=i/L
, where
\lambda_{q}
can be approximated as
\lambda_{q}\rightarrow\lambda(x)\simeq 4\pi^{2}x^{2}
. Then, using the variable transformation
z=4\pi^{2}x^{2}a^{\prime}t
, we can write Eq. (
103
) for
t\gg 1
as follows:

\displaystyle C_{0}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)\simeq-\frac{at^{-5/2}}{4\pi{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z}z^{3/2}dz=-\frac{3at^{-5/2}}{16\sqrt{\pi}{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}.

(104)

10
, the negative instantaneous bond-current correlation

is plotted as a function of time for different away from criticality densities. Simulation data are plotted as solid lines for
\bar{\rho}=2
(red colored line),

(blue colored line) and
\bar{\rho}=4
(green colored line) for
L=1000
. The corresponding theoretical dotted line is plotted for
\bar{\rho}=4.0
using Eq.(

), which indicates the
t^{-5/2}
decay of correlation function in time.
The corresponding decay of the correlation function for other diffusive models with a single conserved quantity is contrastingly different from what we derived here and is given by
-t^{-3/2}
, which is much slower compared to the Oslo ricepile. We also determine the asymptotic behavior of
C^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}_{r}(t)
using the dimensional analysis of
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}(T)\right\rangle
and express it in terms of
\alpha
as

\displaystyle C_{0}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)\sim-t^{-(2-\alpha)}.

(105)

Furthermore, the asymptotic behaviors near and away from criticality can be written using the
\alpha
values from Eq. (
101
) as

\displaystyle C_{0}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)\sim\begin{cases}-t^{-\left(2-{\delta}/{\nu_{\perp}z}\right)}&{\rm for}\hskip 6.0pt\bar{\rho}\simeq\rho_{c},\\
-t^{-5/2}&\hskip 1.0pt{\rm for}\hskip 6.0pt\bar{\rho}\gg\rho_{c},\end{cases}

(106)

where the asymptotic behavior in the far from critical (from above) regime is the same as that obtained in Eq.(
104
).
Note that, for the conserved Manna model
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
, the decay of the near-critical current correlation function,
C_{0}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)\sim-t^{-(3/2+\mu)}
with
\mu>0
, is indeed faster compared to the far-from-critical one, where
\mu=0
. This result implies a faster suppression of the dynamical fluctuation
\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}\rangle
of bond current; in Ref.
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
, we identified the phenomenon of a faster near-critical decay of the instantaneous current correlation function with
\mu>0
as “dynamical hyperuniformity”.
However, for the Oslo model, the situation is qualitatively quite different. From Eq. (
106
), we observe that the corresponding values of the
dynamical hyperuniformity
exponents for near and away from criticality (from above) are
\mu=\left(1/2-\delta/\nu_{\perp}z\right)\simeq 0.303
and
\mu=1
, respectively.

The above results indicate that the decay of the instantaneous bond-current correlation away from criticality is faster than that near criticality. In other words, compared to the diffusive systems with a single conserved quantity, the Oslo model is dynamically more hyperuniform far from criticality than near criticality. The result underscores the significance of conserved quantities in determining large-scale fluctuations in these systems. As a consequence of the maximal (static as well as dynamic) hyperuniformity, which arises away from criticality, we also observe that the time-integrated correlation function for bond current away from criticality decays, in the thermodynamic limit
L\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle\int_{-T}^{T}C_{0}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)dt=\frac{a(\rho)}{L}\sum_{q}\lambda_{q}e^{-a^{\prime}(\rho)T\lambda_{q}}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{a}{2\pi{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}T^{-3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z}\sqrt{z}dz=\frac{a}{4\sqrt{\pi}{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}T^{-3/2}.

(107)

Perhaps not surprisingly, this particular decay behavior is considerably faster compared to the behavior of the same dynamic quantity for the conserved Manna sandpile
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023

T^{-1/2}
.

IV.3.5
Power spectrum: Bond current

Another way to characterize the dynamic fluctuation is to calculate the power spectrum. Theoretically, the power spectrum of the bond current in a steady state, denoted as
S_{\mathcal{J}}(f)
, can be obtained for a particular frequency
f
by Fourier transforming the two-point correlation function
C^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}_{r}(t)
given in Eq. (
103
) with
r=0
as follows,

\displaystyle S_{\mathcal{J}}(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dtC_{0}^{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}(t)e^{2\pi\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}ft}=\frac{a(\bar{\rho})}{L}\sum_{q}\frac{4f^{2}\pi^{2}\lambda_{q}}{{a^{\prime}}^{2}\lambda_{q}^{2}+4f^{2}\pi^{2}}.

(108)

In the limit of the large system size,
L\gg 1
, the asymptotic behaviour of the above sum in the frequency domain
1/L^{2}\ll f\ll 1
, can be obtained by approximating the above sum by the following integral,

\displaystyle S_{\mathcal{J}}(f)\simeq 2a\int_{\frac{1}{L}}^{\frac{1}{2}}dx\lambda(x)\frac{4\pi^{2}f^{2}}{\lambda(x)^{2}{a^{\prime}}^{2}+4\pi^{2}f^{2}},

(109)

where in the continuum limit, we perform the substitution
q=2\pi x
, which leads to
\lambda(x)\approx 4\pi^{2}x^{2}
. Then by using the variable transformation,
x=\sqrt{f}y^{1/4}/\sqrt{2\pi{a^{\prime}}}
, we convert the above integral into the following in the limit
L\rightarrow\infty
and obtain

\displaystyle S_{\mathcal{J}}(f)=f^{3/2}\frac{a\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dy}{y^{1/4}(1+y)}=a\frac{\pi^{3/2}}{{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}f^{3/2}.

(110)

By employing a straightforward dimensional analysis, one can obtain the exponent
\psi_{\mathcal{J}}
, governing the asymptotics
S_{\mathcal{J}}(f)\sim f^{\psi_{\mathcal{J}}}
, in terms of the growth exponent
\alpha
of the time-integrated bond current, as shown below,

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{J}}=1-\alpha.

(111)

Using the values of
\alpha
, given in Eq.(
101
), for near and away from criticality, we can write
\psi_{\mathcal{J}}
as

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{J}}\sim\begin{cases}(1-{\delta}/{\nu_{\perp}z})\simeq 0.803&{\rm for}\hskip 6.0pt\bar{\rho}\simeq\rho_{c},\\
{3}/{2}&{\rm for}\hskip 6.0pt\bar{\rho}\gg\rho_{c},\\
\end{cases}

(112)

where away from criticality it is governed by the same exponent that appeared in Eq.(
110
).
Compared to diffusive systems with a single conserved quantity, where we have found
\psi_{\mathcal{J}}=1/2+\mu

Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
, and where
\mu>0
suggests dynamical hyperuniformity, the Oslo model exhibits different behavior. Near criticality,
\mu=1/2-\delta/\nu_{\perp}z\simeq 0.303
indicates dynamic hyperuniformity. On the other hand, away from criticality,
\mu=1
, indicates the presence of maximal dynamic hyperuniformity in current fluctuation. This is because the steeper decay of the power spectrum with decreasing frequencies characterizes a faster vanishing rate of the current fluctuation.

In simulations, we compute the power spectrum by discretizing the instantaneous current into small temporal intervals, typically denoted as
\delta t

Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
. The Fourier transform is then calculated as follows,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{n;T}=\delta(t)\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{J}_{i}(k)e^{2\pi\textbf{{i}}f_{n}k},

(113)

where we denote
f_{n}=n/T
for
T\gg 1
. Thus the power spectrum for bond current can be defined as

\displaystyle S_{\mathcal{J}}(f_{n})=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\langle\lvert\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{n;T}\rvert^{2}\rangle,

(114)

where
f_{n}=n/T
for
T\gg 1
. The limit as
T
tends to infinity represents an average over an infinitely long time period. In the limit os system size
L\rightarrow\infty
large, the convergence of the discrete sum as given in Eq.(
114
) to its continuum limit is expected, as expressed in Eq.(
108
).

11
, we plot far-from-critical power spectrum
S_{\mathcal{J}}(f)
vs. frequency
f
for densities
\bar{\rho}=

2
(red),
3
(blue), and

(green) in solid lines for
L=1000
. The corresponding theoretical result, obtained by using Eq. (
110
) for
\bar{\rho}=4.0
, is shown in the plot as a black dashed line. This asymptotic theoretical result quite nicely captures the
f^{3/2}
decay of the power spectrum as
f\to 0
.

, we present the simulation data of the power spectrum for near-critical densities
\bar{\rho}=1.7344
(green line),
1.736

IV.3.6
Power spectrum: Subsystem mass

In this section, we compute the power spectrum of subsystem mass, given in Eq.(
89
) by taking the Fourier transform of
C^{M_{l}M_{l}}(t)
, which can be expressed as given below,

\displaystyle S_{M}(f)=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}C^{M_{l}M_{l}}(t)e^{2\pi\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}ft}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\frac{a(\rho)}{2a^{\prime}(\rho)}\sum\limits_{q}\frac{2\lambda_{q}a^{\prime}(\rho)}{\lambda_{q}^{2}{a^{\prime}}^{2}(\rho)+4\pi^{2}f^{2}}\lambda_{lq}.

(115)

Similarly, like the current power spectrum, this expression can also be written as an integral where we take the continuum limit
i\rightarrow x=i/L
and the large system size limit
L\gg 1
. In the limit of the size of the large subsystem
l\gg 1
and
l/L\ll 1
, we can also approximate
\lambda_{lq}\simeq 2
.
Furthermore, using the variable transformation,
x=\sqrt{f}y^{1/4}/\sqrt{2\pi a^{\prime}}
, the mass power spectrum can finally be written as

\displaystyle S_{M}(f)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2a}{a^{\prime}}\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}\pi^{3/2}\sqrt{{a^{\prime}}}\sqrt{f}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dy}{y^{1/4}(1+y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{2\sqrt{\pi}{a^{\prime}}^{3/2}}f^{-1/2}.

(116)

The asymptotic behavior,
S_{M}(f)\sim f^{-\psi_{M}}
, can also be obtained from the current power spectrum exponent
\psi_{\mathcal{J}}
, as we noted earlier in
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
, the exponents
\psi_{\mathcal{J}}
and
\psi_{M}
follow the relation
\psi_{M}=2-\psi_{\mathcal{J}}

(117)

Immediately, using the Eq.(

\displaystyle\psi_{M}=\begin{cases}(1+{\delta}/{\nu_{\perp}z})\simeq 1.197&{\rm for}\hskip 6.0pt\bar{\rho}\simeq\rho_{c},\\
{1}/{2}&{\rm for}\hskip 6.0pt\bar{\rho}\gg\rho_{c},\end{cases}

(118)

where the value of the away from criticality exponent is the same as that we obtained in Eq.(
IV.3.6

That is, the power spectrum for subsystem mass in one dimension can be written as

Structure factor

The static density fluctuation can also be quantified by the structure factor
S(q)=\left\langle\lvert\tilde{\delta m}_{q}(t)\rvert^{2}\right\rangle/N

Torquato 2018
, where
\tilde{\delta m}_{q}(t)
is the Fourier transform of the excess mass
\delta m_{i}(t)=m_{i}(t)-\bar{\rho}
at a site
i
. For hyperuniform states,
S(q)\to 0
as
q\to 0
, particularly, for the maximal hyperuniform states
S(q)\sim q^{2}
in the small
q
limit
Hexner and Levine 2017
. In the following, we calculate
S(q)
using the microscopic evolution equation for
\delta m_{i}(t)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta m_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i-1}-\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}\right)+\left(\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i-1}-\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}\right),

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle D(\bar{\rho})\Delta_{i,k}\delta m_{k}(t)+\left(\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i-1}-\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}\right).

(119)

We express
\delta m_{i}(t)
and
\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}_{i}(t)
in the Fourier modes in the above equation and obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{\delta m}_{q}(t)\simeq-D\lambda_{q}\tilde{\delta m}_{q}(t)+\left(e^{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}q}-1\right)\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(fl)}_{q}(t),

(120)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(fl)}_{q}(t)
is the Fourier transform of fluctuating current
\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}
. Solving the above equation, we write
\tilde{m}_{q}(t)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta m}_{q}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{-\lambda_{q}D(t-t^{\prime})}\left(e^{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}q}-1\right)\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(fl)}_{q}(t^{\prime}).

(121)

Now, we calculate the structure factor
S(q)
, using the
\tilde{\delta m}_{q}(t)
as

\displaystyle S(q)=\frac{1}{L\bar{\rho}}\left\langle\lvert\tilde{\delta m}_{q}(t)\rvert^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L\bar{\rho}}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}e^{-\lambda_{q}D(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}\lambda_{q}\left\langle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(fl)}_{q}(t^{\prime})\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(fl)}_{-q}(t^{\prime\prime})\right\rangle.

(122)

Using the correlation of function of
\mathcal{J}^{(fl)}
, given in Eq.(
65
), we can write,

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(fl)}_{q}(t^{\prime})\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(fl)}_{q^{\prime}}(t^{\prime\prime})\right\rangle=La(\bar{\rho})\lambda_{q}\delta_{q,-q^{\prime}}\delta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}),

(123)

which by putting in Eq.(
V
), we obtain,

\displaystyle S(q)=\left(1-e^{-2\lambda_{q}Dt}\right)\frac{\lambda_{q}}{2D\bar{\rho}}a(\bar{\rho}).

(124)

Finally, in the limit
t\to\infty
and approximating
\lambda(q)\simeq q^{2}
for
q\to 0
, we obtain the steady-state structure factor

\displaystyle S(q)\simeq\frac{a(\bar{\rho})}{2D\bar{\rho}}q^{2},

(125)

the expression, which is valid far from criticality.

Tagged particle diffusion

In this section, we examine the variance of the displacements
X_{\alpha}(T)
of a tagged particle
\alpha
in a temporal domain
[0,T]
. Since a particle can only hop a distance of
+1
or
-1
from the toppled site, the variance of the total hop length
\langle X^{2}_{\alpha}(T)\rangle
depends solely on the number of topplings experienced by the tagged particle within this time interval, denoted as
N_{\alpha}^{(h)}(T)
, as shown in the following equation,

\displaystyle\langle X_{\alpha}^{2}(T)\rangle=N_{\alpha}^{(h)}(T).

(126)

By summing over all the tagged particles of the system, the total variance can be written in terms of twice of the total topplings
N^{(tp)}(T)
during that interval as

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\langle X_{\alpha}^{2}(T)\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}^{(h)}(T)=2N^{(tp)}(T),

(127)

as at each toppling two particle jumps out of the site.
The number of total toppling in the system,
N^{(tp)}(T)
, on average, is equal to the number of active site density
a(\bar{\rho})
times corresponding spacetime volume,

\displaystyle N^{(tp)}(T)=a(\bar{\rho})LT.

(128)

Due to the homogeneity of the system, we can express the left-hand side of Equation (
127
) as the total number of particles multiplied by the variance of a particle tagged
\left\langle X^{2}(T)\right\rangle
. Using Equation (
128
), we can rewrite Equation (
127
) as follows,

\displaystyle N\langle X^{2}(T)\rangle=2a(\bar{\rho})LT\hskip 6.0pt\textit{or}\hskip 6.0pt\langle X^{2}(T)\rangle=2\frac{a(\bar{\rho})}{\bar{\rho}}T.

(129)

The self-diffusion coefficient
\mathcal{D}_{s}(\bar{\rho})
in the steady state is defined via the time-dependent (steady-state) mean-squared displacement
\left\langle X^{2}(T)\right\rangle
as

\displaystyle\left\langle X^{2}(T)\right\rangle=2\mathcal{D}_{s}(\bar{\rho})T,

(130)

comparing this with Eq.(
129
), we obtain the exact expression of self-diffusivity in terms of the activity and the corresponding global density
\bar{\rho}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{s}(\bar{\rho})=\frac{a(\bar{\rho})}{\bar{\rho}}.

(131)

The mean square fluctuation of the cumulative displacement of the tagged particles up to time
T
(represented by the solid red line) is plotted in Fig.

\Delta
.

VII
Summary and conclusions

In this paper, we use a microscopic approach for investigating the one-dimensional Oslo model on a ring geometry having
L
discrete sites. We provide a large-scale characterization of various static and dynamic properties of mass and current in the system near as well as far from (above) criticality. The model is a paradigm for out-of-equilibrium systems, that conserve both mass and center-of-mass (CoM), but lack time-reversal symmetry (i.e., detailed balance is violated). We show that, despite having highly constrained microscopic dynamics due to CoM conservation, the Oslo model in fact exhibits
diffusive
relaxation far from criticality and
superdiffusive
relaxation near criticality.
In the near-critical scaling regime, the relaxation time has the following algebraic dependence on system size
L
, i.e.,
\tau_{r}\sim L^{z}
, where the dynamical exponent
z=2-(1-\beta)/\nu_{\perp}<2
is determined by the two static exponents
\beta
and
\nu_{\perp}
. Indeed the above scaling relation satisfied by the dynamic exponent is in perfect agreement with the near-rational values of the exponents, which were obtained through large-scale simulations in Ref.
Grassberger et al. 2016
.

Quite interestingly, in the long-time limit, the additional CoM conservation manifests itself in making the temporal growth rate of time-integrated current fluctuation, and consequently,
the particle mobility, to vanish

In other words, current fluctuations become anomalously suppressed as compared to that observed in diffusive systems with only mass conservation, leading to hyperuniformity in spatial as well as in temporal domains in the system.
To gain a better understanding of dynamic fluctuations, we implement a closure scheme, which helps one to calculate unequal-time (two-point) correlation functions, and the associated power spectra, involving current and mass. We obtain a mass-conservation principle as encoded in eq. (
93
), which connects (dynamic) current and (static) mass fluctuations and exactly determines the decay exponents of the respective dynamic correlation functions near criticality in terms of the standard static exponents. We calculate the decay exponent exactly within the closure scheme, away from criticality. In the far-from-critical regime, we also analytically calculate the static structure factor, which has the following behaviour
S(q)\sim q^{2}
in the limit of small wave number
q\to 0
[see eq. (
125
)]. Quite strikingly, the static structure factor far from criticality exhibits class I hyperuniformity
Torquato 2021
, whereas it exhibits class III hyperuniformity near criticality.

We believe they would elucidate the dynamical origin of anomalous relaxation and hyperuniform fluctuations in systems with multiple conservation laws, and provide a fresh perspective on the general theoretical understanding of the problem.

\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(m_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{i-1}\delta_{i,i+r}dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(m_{i+r}(t)-2\right)&\hat{a}_{i-1}\delta_{i-1,i+r}dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(m_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{i-1}\delta_{i-2,i+r}dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(m_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{i+1}\delta_{i,i+r}dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(m_{i+r}(t)-2\right)&\hat{a}_{i+1}\delta_{i+1,i+r}dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(m_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{i+1}\delta_{i+2,i+r}dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)-2\right)m_{i+r}(t)&\hat{a}_{i}\left(1-\delta_{i+1,i+r}-\delta_{i,i+r}-\delta_{i-1,i+r}\right)dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)m_{i+r}(t)&\hat{a}_{i-1}\left(1-\delta_{i-1,i+r}-\delta_{i,i+r}-\delta_{i-2,i+r}\right)dt\\
\left(m_{i}(t)+1\right)m_{i+r}(t)&\hat{a}_{i+1}\left(1-\delta_{i+1,i+r}-\delta_{i,i+r}-\delta_{i+2,i+r}\right)dt\\
m_{i}(t)\left(m_{i+r}(t)-2\right)&\hat{a}_{i+r}\left(1-\delta_{i,i+r+1}-\delta_{i,i+r}-\delta_{i,i+r-1}\right)dt\\
m_{i}(t)\left(m_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{i+r+1}\left(1-\delta_{i,i+r+1}-\delta_{i,i+r+2}-\delta_{i,i+r}\right)dt\\
m_{i}(t)\left(m_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{i+r-1}\left(1-\delta_{i,i+r-1}-\delta_{i,i+r}-\delta_{i,i+r-2}\right)dt\\
m_{i}(t)m_{i+r}(t)&1-\Sigma dt,\end{cases}

(136)

where
\Sigma dt
is the sum of probabilities of all previous events. The corresponding evolution equation of
C^{mm}_{r}(t,t)
can be written using the above update rules as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}C^{mm}_{r}(t,t)=\sum_{k}\Delta_{i,k}\left\langle\hat{a}_{k}m_{i+r}\right\rangle+\sum_{k}\Delta_{i+r,k}\left\langle m_{i}\hat{a}_{k}\right\rangle+B_{i,i+r},

(137)

where
B_{i,i+r}
is the source part of this correlation, given as

\displaystyle B_{i,i+r}=

\displaystyle\delta_{i,i+r}\left(4\hat{a}_{i}+\hat{a}_{i-1}+\hat{a}_{i+1}\right)-2\delta_{i-1,i+r}\left(\hat{a}_{i}+\hat{a}_{i-1}\right)-2\delta_{i+1,i+r}\left(\hat{a}_{i}+\hat{a}_{i+1}\right)+

\displaystyle\delta_{i-2,i+r}\hat{a}_{i-1}+\delta_{i+2,i+r}\hat{a}_{i+1}.

(138)

In the steady state we must have
\frac{d}{dt}C^{mm}_{r}(t,t)=0
and using the translation symmetry, Eq.(
137
) can be written as

\displaystyle 2\left(C^{\hat{a}m}_{r-1}-C^{\hat{a}m}_{r}+C^{\hat{a}m}_{r+1}\right)+B_{r}=0,

(139)

and the source term
B_{r}
is given by,

\displaystyle B_{r}=6a(\bar{\rho})\delta_{0,r}-4a(\bar{\rho})\left(\delta_{0,r+1}+\delta_{0,r-1}\right)+a(\bar{\rho})\left(\delta_{0,r+2}+\delta_{0,r-2}\right).

(140)

Note that we can also straightforwardly derive Eq. (
81
) in the main text simply by inserting our truncation relation, as given in Eq. (
45
), into Eq. (
139
).
We can solve Eq.(
139
) by multiplying both sides by
z^{r}
and defining the generating function
G(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}z^{r}C^{\hat{a}m}_{r}
. Imposing the convergence of
G(z)<\infty
when
z<\infty
, we can write the generating function as

\displaystyle G(z)=a(\bar{\rho})-\frac{a(\bar{\rho})}{2}z.

(141)

From the generating function of above, we write the correlation function
C^{\hat{a}m}_{r}
as

\displaystyle C^{\hat{a}m}_{r}=a(\bar{\rho})\delta_{0,r}-\frac{a}{2}\left(\delta_{r+1}+\delta_{r-1}\right),

(142)

and thus we prove Eq. (
56
).

Appendix C
Equal-time current-current correlation

To derive Eq. (
IV.1.2
), we write the
evolution equation of the equal-time unequal-space correlation of integrated current using the following update equation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t+dt)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t+dt)=\begin{cases}\textbf{{events}}&\textbf{{probabilities}}\\
\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{i}(t)\delta_{i,{i+r}}dt\\
\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)-1\right)&\hat{a}_{i}(t)\delta_{i-1,{i+r}}dt\\
\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)-1\right)\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)-1\right)&\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\delta_{i,{i+r}}dt\\
\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)-1\right)\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\delta_{i+1,{i+r}}dt\\
\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)+1\right)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)&\hat{a}_{i}(t)\left(1-\delta_{i,{i+r}}-\delta_{i-1,{i+r}}\right)dt\\
\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)-1\right)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)&\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\left(1-\delta_{i,{i+r}}-\delta_{i+1,{i+r}}\right)dt\\
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)+1\right)&\hat{a}_{{i+r}}(t)\left(1-\delta_{i,{i+r}}-\delta_{i,{i+r-1}}\right)dt\\
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\left(\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)-1\right)&\hat{a}_{{i+r}+1}(t)\left(1-\delta_{i,{i+r}}-\delta_{i,{i+r+1}}\right)dt\\
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)&1-\Sigma dt,\end{cases}

(143)

where
\Sigma dt
is the probability of happening nothing in the time interval
dt
. The corresponding dynamical equation can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}C^{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(t,t)=\Gamma_{i,i+r}(t)+\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i}(t)\mathcal{Q}_{i+r}(t)\right\rangle_{c}+\left\langle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(t)\mathcal{J}^{(d)}_{i+r})(t)\right\rangle_{c},

(144)

where
\Gamma_{i,i+r}
is the source of the equation or correlation functions can be written as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i,i+r}(t)=\hat{a}_{i}(t)\left(\delta_{i,{i+r}}-\delta_{i-1,{i+r}}\right)-\hat{a}_{i+1}(t)\left(\delta_{i+1,{i+r}}-\delta_{i,{i+r}}\right).

(145)

In the steady state, this source function can be written as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{r}(t)=a(\bar{\rho})\left(2\delta_{0,{r}}-\delta_{0,{r+1}}-\delta_{0,{r-1}}\right),

(146)

which is the derivation of Eq. (
63
) in the main text.
