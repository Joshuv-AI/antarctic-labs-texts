Faster quantum-inspired algorithms for solving linear systems
Changpeng Shao, Ashley Montanaro

Abstract
We establish an improved classical algorithm for solving linear systems in a model analogous to the QRAM that is used by quantum linear solvers. Precisely, for the linear system A ​ 𝐱 = 𝐛 A{\bf x}={\bf b} , we show that there is a classical algorithm that outputs a data structure for 𝐱 {\bf x} allowing sampling and querying to the entries, where 𝐱 {\bf x} is such that ‖ 𝐱 − A + ​ 𝐛 ‖ ≤ ϵ ​ ‖ A + ​ 𝐛 ‖ \|{\bf x}-A^{+}{\bf b}\|\leq\epsilon\|A^{+}{\bf b}\| . This output can be viewed as a classical analogue to the output of quantum linear solvers. The complexity of our algorithm is O ~ ​ ( κ F 4 ​ κ 2 / ϵ 2 ) \widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}^{4}\kappa^{2}/\epsilon^{2}) , where κ F = ‖ A ‖ F ​ ‖ A + ‖ \kappa_{F}=\|A\|_{F}\|A^{+}\| and κ = ‖ A ‖ ​ ‖ A + ‖ \kappa=\|A\|\|A^{+}\| . This improves the previous best algorithm [Gilyén, Song and Tang, arXiv:2009.07268] of complexity O ~ ​ ( κ F 6 ​ κ 6 / ϵ 4 ) \widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}^{6}\kappa^{6}/\epsilon^{4}) . Our algorithm is based on the randomized Kaczmarz method, which is a particular case of stochastic gradient descent. We also find that when A A is row sparse, this method already returns an approximate solution 𝐱 {\bf x} in time O ~ ​ ( κ F 2 ) \widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}^{2}) , while the best quantum algorithm known returns | 𝐱 ⟩ |{\bf x}\rangle in time O ~ ​ ( κ F ) \widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}) when A A is stored in the QRAM data structure. As a result, assuming access to QRAM and if A A is row sparse, the speedup based on current quantum algorithms is quadratic.

Randomized iterative methods have received a lot of attention recently in solving linear systems because of their connection to the stochastic gradient descent method.
The randomized Kaczmarz method
[
35

Geometrically, the next point is the orthogonal projection of the previous one onto the hyperplane defined by the chosen linear constraint. The simple iterative structure and clear geometric meaning greatly simplifies the analysis of this method and makes it popular in applications like computed tomography
[
3

Usually, the randomized iterative methods need to do some importance sampling according to the norms of the rows. Sampling access is made available by the data structure for quantum-inspired algorithms and the QRAM for quantum linear solvers. So in this paper, for the fairness of comparison we also assume a similar data structure for the randomized iterative methods.

1.2
Summary of the techniques

Our main algorithm is based on a generalization of Kaczmarz method. As briefly introduced above (also see Section
2
for more details), the Kaczmarz method aims to solve one linear constraint at each step of the iteration, which can be viewed as a particular case of the stochastic gradient method. To design an efficient quantum-inspired algorithm, we concentrate on the dual form of the Kaczmarz method. Namely, introducing
{\bf y}
such that
{\bf x}=A^{\dagger}{\bf y}
, then focusing on the iteration on
{\bf y}
. The linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
can be reformulated as the least squares problem (LSP)
{\bf x}=\arg\min_{{\bf x}}\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
. It turns out that
{\bf y}
converges to the optimal solution of the dual problem of the LSP. Our idea is to apply the iterative method to find a sparse approximate solution
{\bf y}
of the dual problem. The sparsity is guaranteed by the iterative method. Now
{\bf x}=A^{\dagger}{\bf y}
gives an approximate solution of the LSP. Since
{\bf y}
is sparse, it is easy to query an entry of
{\bf x}
. To sample an entry of
{\bf x}
, we can use the rejection sampling idea.

The technical part of this paper is the complexity analysis of the rejection sampling method. However, it turns out that the main cost of the above algorithm comes from the computation of
{\bf y}
, whose complexity analysis is straightforward. It is time-consuming if we directly use the iterative method because it relates to the calculation of inner products. To alleviate this, we modify the iteration by using the sampling idea. We prove that this modified method has the same convergence rate as the original one by choosing some parameters appropriately.

Randomized Kaczmarz algorithm

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix,
{\bf b}
be an
m\times 1
vector.
Solving the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
can be described as a quadratic optimization problem, i.e., the so-called least squares problem (LSP)

\arg\min_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\quad\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|^{2}.

(1)

Classically, there are many algorithms for solving the LSP
[
14
]
. The best known classical algorithm might be the conjugate-gradient (CG) method. Compared to CG, the Kaczmarz algorithm has received much attention recently due to its simplicity. For instance, at each step of iteration, CG uses the whole information of the matrix
A
and
O(mn)
operations, while Kaczmarz method only uses one row of
A
and
O(n)
operations. So Kaczmarz method becomes effective when the entire matrix (data set) cannot be loaded into memory.

Definition 1

(Randomized Kaczmarz algorithm
[
35
]
)
.

Let
{\bf x}_{0}
be an arbitrary initial approximation to the solution of (
1
). For
k=0,1,\ldots
, compute

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+\frac{b_{r_{k}}-\langle A_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle}{\|A_{r_{k}*}\|^{2}}A_{r_{k}*},

(2)

where
A_{r_{k}*}
is the
r_{k}
-th row of
A
, and
r_{k}
is chosen from
[m]
randomly with probability proportional to
\|A_{r_{k}*}\|^{2}
.

The Kaczmarz algorithm has a clear geometric meaning, see Figure
1
. Geometrically,
{\bf x}_{k+1}
is the orthogonal projection of
{\bf x}_{k}
onto the hyperplane
A_{r_{k}*}\cdot{\bf x}=b_{r_{k}}
. So at each step of iteration, this method finds the closest vector that satisfies the chosen linear constraint.

From the definition, we can simplify the notation by defining

\tilde{{\bf b}}={\rm diag}(\|A_{i*}\|^{-1}:i\in[m]){\bf b},\quad\widetilde{A}={\rm diag}(\|A_{i*}\|^{-1}:i\in[m])A,

(3)

so that

\tilde{b}_{i}=b_{i}/\|A_{i*}\|
and

\widetilde{A}_{i*}=A_{i*}/\|A_{i*}\|
.
Then we can rewrite

2

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+\left(\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}-\langle\widetilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle\right)\widetilde{A}_{r_{k}*}=\left(I-|\widetilde{A}_{r_{k}*}\rangle\langle\widetilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|\right){\bf x}_{k}+\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}\widetilde{A}_{r_{k}*}.

(4)

In
[
34
]

to a block of a unitary

This unitary is closely related to the Grover diffusion operator when we apply it to
|-\rangle|{\bf x}\rangle

Lemma 2

(Theorem 2 of
[
35
]
)
.

Assume that
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent.
Let
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
be a solution of (
1
). Then the randomized Kaczmarz algorithm converges to
{\bf x}_{*}
in expectation, with the average error

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\leq(1-\kappa_{F}^{-2})^{T}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2},

(5)

where
\kappa_{F}=\|A\|_{F}\|A^{+}\|
.

The above result is stated for consistent linear systems, while the Kaczmarz algorithm also works for inconsistent linear systems, for example, see
[
27
]
. The convergence rate does not change, the error bound is affected by an extra term depending on
\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
. Our results (especially the main Theorem
13
) stated below are indeed for general cases – consistent and inconsistent.

The above result holds for all
{\bf x}_{0}
, so throughout this paper, we shall assume
{\bf x}_{0}=0
for simplicity.
In the above lemma, by Markov’s inequality, with high probability 0.99, we have
\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}\leq 100(1-\kappa_{F}^{-2})^{T}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}
.
To make sure the error is bounded by
\epsilon^{2}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}
with high probability,
it suffices to choose

T=O(\kappa_{F}^{2}\log(1/\epsilon)).

(6)

From (
2
), we know that there exist
y_{k,0},\ldots,y_{k,k-1}
such that

{\bf x}_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}y_{k,j}A_{r_{j}*},

that is
{\bf x}_{k}=A^{\dagger}{\bf y}_{k}
for some vector
{\bf y}_{k}
. One obvious fact is that
{\bf y}_{k}
is at most
k
-sparse. In
[
12
]
,
{\bf y}_{k}
is called the sparse description of
{\bf x}_{k}
. So this description comes easily in the Kaczmarz method. Actually,
{\bf y}_{k}
converges to the optimal solution of the dual problem of the LSP (
1
).
More precisely, an alternative formulation of LSP is to find the least-norm solution of the linear system

\min_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf x}\|^{2},\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad A{\bf x}={\bf b}.

The dual problem takes the form:

\min_{{\bf y}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}}\quad g({\bf y}):=\frac{1}{2}\|A^{\dagger}{\bf y}\|^{2}-{\bf b}\cdot{\bf y}.

(7)

The randomized Kaczmarz algorithm (
2
) is equivalent to one step of the stochastic gradient descent method
[
29
]
applied to
\frac{1}{2}(A_{r_{k}*}\cdot{\bf x}-b_{r_{k}})^{2}
with stepsize
1/\|A_{r_{k}*}\|^{2}
. It is also equivalent to one step of the randomized coordinate descent method
[
30
]
applied to the dual problem (
7
). Namely, when we apply this method to the
r_{k}
-th component, the gradient is
\nabla_{r_{k}}g=\langle A_{r_{k}*}|A^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle-b_{r_{k}}
. Setting the stepsize as
1/\|A_{r_{k}*}\|^{2}
leads to the updating

{\bf y}_{k+1}={\bf y}_{k}+\frac{b_{r_{k}}-\langle A_{r_{k}*}|A^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle}{\|A_{r_{k}*}\|^{2}}{\bf e}_{r_{k}},

(8)

where
\{{\bf e}_{1},\ldots,{\bf e}_{m}\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
We can recover the original Kaczmarz iteration (
2
) by multiplying
A^{\dagger}
on both sides of (
8
).

The Kaczmarz method can be generalized to use multiple rows at each step of iteration
[
16
]
. Instead of orthogonally projecting to a hyperplane determined by one linear constraint, we can orthogonally project to a vector space defined by several linear constraints. This idea is similar to the minibatch stochastic gradient descent. However, this generalization needs to compute the pseudoinverse of certain matrices. A simple pseudoinverse-free variant is as follows
[
27
]
:

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+\frac{\alpha}{2q}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\frac{b_{i}-\langle A_{i*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle}{\|A_{i*}\|^{2}}A_{i*},

(9)

where
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
is a random set of
q
row indices sampled with replacement,
\alpha
is known as the relaxation parameter.
Each index
i\in[m]
is put into
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
with probability proportional to
\|A_{i*}\|^{2}
. For instance, let
\alpha=2
, then (
9
) can be rewritten in a parallel form

{\bf x}_{k+1}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\left({\bf x}_{k}+\frac{b_{i}-\langle A_{i*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle}{\|A_{i*}\|^{2}}A_{i*}\right).

It was proved in
[
27
, Corollary 3]
that if
\alpha=q=\|A\|_{F}^{2}/\|A\|^{2}
(also see a related proof in Appendix
B
),

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\leq\left(1-\kappa^{-2}\right)^{T}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}.

(10)

So the number of iterations required to achieve error
\epsilon
via (
9
) is
O(\kappa^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))
.

{\bf v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have
SQ({\bf v})
, sampling and query access to
{\bf v}
, if we can:

1.

Sample: obtain independent samples
i\in[n]
following the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{{\bf v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{{\bf v}}(i)=|v_{i}|^{2}/\|{\bf v}\|^{2}
.

2.

Query: query entries of
{\bf v}
as in
Q({\bf v})
;

3.

Norm: query
\|{\bf v}\|
.

Let
{\bf q}({\bf v}),{\bf s}({\bf v})
, and
{\bf n}({\bf v})
denote the cost of querying entries, sampling indices, and querying the norm respectively. Further define
{\bf s}{\bf q}({\bf v}):={\bf q}({\bf v})+{\bf s}({\bf v})+{\bf n}({\bf v})
.

Definition 5

(Sampling and query access to a matrix)
.

For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
, we have
SQ(A)
if we have
SQ(A_{i*})
for all
i\in[m]
and
SQ({\bf a})
, where
{\bf a}
is the vector of row norms. We define
{\bf s}{\bf q}(A)
as the cost to obtain
SQ(A)
.

The sampling and query access defined above can be viewed as a classical analogue to the QRAM data structure, which is widely used in many quantum machine learning (QML) algorithms. In QML, this data structure can be used to efficiently prepare the quantum states of vectors, which are usually the inputs of the QML algorithms. For matrices, it allows us to find an efficient block-encoding so that many quantum linear algebraic techniques become effective. However, the QRAM (and Definition
5
) is a quite strong data structure because it contains a lot of information (e.g.
\|A_{i*}\|
and
\|A\|_{F}
) about
A
, which is usually not easy to obtain. For instance, it usually needs
\Theta(\|A\|_{0})
operations to build this data structure, which
\|A\|_{0}
is the number of nonzero entries of
A
. For quantum-inspired algorithms, we need to use certain sampling techniques to make sure that the complexity has a polylog dependence on the dimension, so it seems quite natural to use the above data structure. In comparison, there are also some other efficient quantum models, e.g., sparse-access model, in which the QML algorithms are still efficient.

The sampling operation on
{\bf v}
can be viewed as a classical analogue of the measurement of the quantum state
|{\bf v}\rangle=\|{\bf v}\|^{-1}\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}|i\rangle
in the computational basis. For some problems, we do not have the state
|{\bf v}\rangle
exactly. Instead, what we have is a state of the form
\sin(\theta)|{\bf v}\rangle|0\rangle+\cos(\theta)|{\bf w}\rangle|1\rangle
. Then the probability of obtaining
|i,0\rangle
is
\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{\|{\bf v}\|^{2}}\sin^{2}(\theta)
.
This leads to the definition of oversampling for quantum-inspired algorithms.

Definition 6

(Oversampling and query access)
.

For
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\phi\geq 1
, we have
SQ_{\phi}({\bf v})
,
\phi
-oversampling and query access to
{\bf v}
, if we have
Q({\bf v})
and
SQ(\tilde{{\bf v}})
for a vector
\tilde{{\bf v}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying
\|\tilde{{\bf v}}\|^{2}=\phi\|{\bf v}\|^{2}
and
|\tilde{v}_{i}|^{2}\geq|v_{i}|^{2}
for all
i\in[n]
.
We denote
{\bf s}_{\phi}(v)={\bf s}(\tilde{{\bf v}}),{\bf q}_{\phi}(v)={\bf q}(\tilde{{\bf v}}),{\bf n}_{\phi}(v)={\bf n}(\tilde{{\bf v}})
, and
{\bf s}{\bf q}_{\phi}({\bf v})={\bf s}_{\phi}(v)+{\bf q}_{\phi}(v)+{\bf q}(v)+{\bf n}_{\phi}(v)
.

For example, if we view
{\bf v}
as the vector
({\bf v},0,\ldots,0)
and
\tilde{{\bf v}}
as the vector
({\bf v},\|{\bf v}\|\cot(\theta){\bf w})
so that they have the same dimension, then it is easy to see that
\|\tilde{{\bf v}}\|^{2}=\|{\bf v}\|^{2}/\sin^{2}(\theta)
and
\phi=1/\sin^{2}(\theta)
. The condition
|\tilde{v}_{i}|^{2}\geq|v_{i}|^{2}
is also satisfied. But the above definition is stronger than this intuition.

Lemma 7

(Lemma 2.9 of
[
6
]
)
.

Given
SQ_{\phi}({\bf v})
and
\delta\in(0,1]
we can sample from
\mathcal{D}_{{\bf v}}
with success probability at least
1-\delta
and cost
O(\phi{\bf s}{\bf q}_{\phi}({\bf v})\log(1/\delta))
. We can also estimate
\|{\bf v}\|
up to relative error

\epsilon
in time
O(\epsilon^{-2}\phi{\bf s}{\bf q}_{\phi}({\bf v})\log(1/\delta))
.

Lemma 8

(Lemma 2.10 of
[
6
]
)
.

Given
SQ_{\varphi_{i}}({\bf v}_{i})
and
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
for
i\in[k]
, denote
{\bf v}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}{\bf v}_{i}
, then we have

SQ_{\phi}({\bf v})
for

\phi=k\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}\|\lambda_{i}{\bf v}_{i}\|^{2}/\|{\bf v}\|^{2}
and
{\bf s}{\bf q}_{\phi}({\bf v})=\max_{i}{\bf s}_{\varphi_{i}}({\bf v}_{i})+\sum_{i}{\bf q}({\bf v}_{i})
.

The first lemma tells us how to construct the original sampling access from an over-sampled one.
By Definition
6
, given
SQ_{\phi}({\bf v})
, we have
Q({\bf v})
. Lemma
7
tells us how to obtain
S({\bf v})
from
SQ_{\phi}({\bf v})
. So this lemma actually tells us how to obtain
SQ({\bf v})
from
SQ_{\phi}({\bf v})
.
The second lemma is about how to construct a linear combination of the data structures. Equivalently, it is about constructing the data structure for
M{\bf c}
when we have the data structures of
M
and
{\bf c}
, where
M
is a matrix and
{\bf c}
is a vector.

In our algorithms below,
{\bf v}_{i}
will be a row of
A
,
k
will refer to the sparsity of a certain known vector and
{\bf v}
will be the approximate solution to the linear system. So in Lemma

,
{\bf s}{\bf q}_{\phi}({\bf v})=O(k{\bf s}{\bf q}(A))
. Combing the above two lemmas, given
SQ({\bf v}_{i})
and
\lambda_{i}
, the cost to sample and query
{\bf v}
is
\widetilde{O}(k\phi{\bf s}{\bf q}(A))
, and the cost to estimate its norm is
\widetilde{O}(k\phi{\bf s}{\bf q}(A)/\epsilon^{2})
. So a main difficulty in the the complexity analysis is the estimation of

.

4
The algorithm for solving linear systems

4.1
Solving general linear systems

In this section, based on the Kaczmarz method, we give a quantum-inspired classical algorithm for solving linear systems. The basic idea is similar to
[
12
]
. However, our result has a lower complexity and the analysis is much simpler.
The main idea is as follows: we first use the iteration (
8
) to compute
{\bf y}_{T}
, then output
SQ({\bf x}_{T})
with
{\bf x}_{T}=A^{\dagger}{\bf y}_{T}
by Lemmas
7
and
8
.

When
A
is dense, the calculation of
\langle A_{r_{k}*}|A^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle
in (
8
) is expensive.
Note that
\langle A_{r_{k}*}|A^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{r_{k},j}\langle A_{*j}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle
. As an inner product, we can use the sampling idea (e.g. Monte Carlo) to approximate it.
Now let
j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}
be drawn randomly from
[n]
under the distribution that
{\rm Pr}[j]=\|A_{*j}\|^{2}/\|A\|_{F}^{2}
. This distribution is designed to put more weight on the most important rows
[
10
,
35
]
.
Let
S=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}\}
be the sequence of the chosen indices.
We modify the updating of
{\bf y}
to:

{\bf y}_{k+1}={\bf y}_{k}+\frac{1}{\|A_{r_{k}*}\|}\left(\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j\in S}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}\langle A_{*j}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\right){\bf e}_{r_{k}}.

(11)

For conciseness, define the matrix
D
as

D=\sum_{j\in S}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{d\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}|j\rangle\langle j|.

(12)

Then

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{k}+\frac{\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}-\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|DA^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle}{\|A_{r_{k}*}\|}{\bf e}_{r_{k}}

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{k}+\frac{\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}-\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|A^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle}{\|A_{r_{k}*}\|}{\bf e}_{r_{k}}+\frac{\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|(I-D)A^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle}{\|A_{r_{k}*}\|}{\bf e}_{r_{k}}.

(14)

Compared to the original iteration (
8
), the procedure (
14
) contains a perturbation term.
To analyze its convergence rate, we need to determine
d
.
Before that, we first analyze the properties of the following random variable:

\mu_{k}:=\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|(I-D)A^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle=\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|I-D|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle=\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j\in S}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}x_{k,j}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}.

(15)

Lemma 9
.

The mean and the variance of
\mu_{k}
satisfy

{\mathbb{E}}_{D}[\mu_{k}]=0,{\rm Var}_{D}[\mu_{k}]\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}^{2}x_{k,j}^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}.

Proof.

The results follow from the standard calculation.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{D}[\mu_{k}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle-{\mathbb{E}}_{D}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j\in S}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}x_{k,j}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle-{\mathbb{E}}_{D}\left[\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}x_{k,j}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}x_{k,j}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\frac{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

\displaystyle{\rm Var}_{D}[\mu_{k}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Var}_{D}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j\in S}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}x_{k,j}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S}{\rm Var}_{D}\left[\frac{1}{d}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}x_{k,j}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}{\mathbb{E}}_{D}\left[\left(\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}x_{k,j}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}x_{k,j}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}^{2}x_{k,j}^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 10
.

In an early version, we further bounded
{\rm Var}_{D}[\mu_{k}]
by
\frac{1}{d}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\min_{j\in[n]}\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\|{\bf x}_{k}\|^{2}
. This leads to
d=\widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}^{4}/\epsilon^{2})
. In comparison, the current bound leads to
d=\widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
. So, the overall complexity of the algorithms presented below can be reduced by a factor of
\kappa_{F}^{2}
.

From (
14
), the updating of
{\bf x}_{k}=A^{\dagger}{\bf y}_{k}
then becomes

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+(\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}-\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle)\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}+\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|I-D|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}.

(16)

Next, we prove a similar result to Lemma
2
. The idea of our proof is similar to that of Lemma
2
from
[
35
]
, which nicely utilizes the geometrical structure of the Kaczmarz method.

Proposition 11
.

Choose
d=4(\kappa_{F}^{2}/\epsilon^{2})(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})
, then after
T=\kappa_{F}^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})
steps of iteration of the procedure (
16
), we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}
.

Proof.

For any vector
{\bf v}
such that
A{\bf v}\neq 0
we have
\|{\bf v}\|^{2}\leq\|A^{+}\|^{2}\|A{\bf v}\|^{2}
. This implies

\frac{\|{\bf v}\|^{2}}{\kappa_{F}^{2}}=\frac{\|{\bf v}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}\|A^{+}\|^{2}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|{\bf v}\rangle^{2}\frac{\|A_{i*}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}={\mathbb{E}}[\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|{\bf v}\rangle^{2}].

(17)

Here the random variable is defined by

{\rm Pr}[i]=\frac{\|A_{i*}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}.

For simplicity, we set the initial vector
{\bf x}_{0}=0
.
In procedure (
16
), we denote

\tilde{{\bf x}}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+(\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}-\langle\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle)\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}
, it is the orthogonal projection of
{\bf x}_{k}
onto the hyperplane
\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}\cdot{\bf x}=\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}
. Thus we can rewrite the iteration as

{\bf x}_{k+1}=\tilde{{\bf x}}_{k+1}+\mu_{k}\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}.

The orthogonality implies (see Figure
2
)

\displaystyle\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\tilde{{\bf x}}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}-\|{\bf x}_{k}-\tilde{{\bf x}}_{k+1}\|^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}-\langle{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}|\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}\rangle^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\langle\frac{{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}}{\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|}|\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}\rangle^{2}\right)\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}.

{\bf x}_{*}
\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}\cdot{\bf x}=\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}
{\bf x}_{k}
\tilde{{\bf x}}_{k+1}
{\bf x}_{k+1}
\mu_{k}

Taking the expectation yields

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]=\left(1-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\frac{{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}}{\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|}|\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}\rangle^{2}\right]\right){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}[\mu_{k}^{2}].

In the above, the expectation is taken first on
D
by fixing
k,r_{k}
, then on
r_{k}
by fixing
k
, and finally on
k
. Also if
A({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*})=0
, then
{\bf x}_{k}
is already the optimal solution, so we assume that this does not happen before convergence.

By equation (
17
),

1-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\frac{{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}}{\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|}|\tilde{A}_{r_{k}*}\rangle^{2}\right]\leq 1-\kappa_{F}^{-2}.

By Lemma
9
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{r_{k}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{D}[\mu_{k}^{2}]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{r_{k}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}^{2}x_{k,j}^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{r_{k}=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{A}_{r_{k},j}^{2}x_{k,j}^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\frac{\|A_{r_{k}*}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|{\bf x}_{k}\|^{2}}{d}.

Hence,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\kappa_{F}^{-2}\right){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{1}{d}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\kappa_{F}^{-2}\right){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{2}{d}(\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\kappa_{F}^{-2}+\frac{2}{d}){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{2}{d}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}.

Now we choose
d=\frac{4\kappa_{F}^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})}{\epsilon^{2}}
, then the above estimation leads to

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\leq\left(1-\kappa_{F}^{-2}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{F}^{-2}\epsilon^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})^{-1}\right){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\kappa_{F}^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}.

Setting
\rho:=1-\kappa_{F}^{-2}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{F}^{-2}\epsilon^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})^{-1}
which is less than 1, we finally obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho^{T}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\kappa_{F}^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\rho^{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho^{T}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}+\frac{T\epsilon^{2}}{2\kappa_{F}^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}.

Let
T=\kappa_{F}^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})
, then

\displaystyle\rho^{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(\kappa_{F}^{2}(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})\log(1-\kappa_{F}^{-2}+\kappa_{F}^{-2}\epsilon^{2}(\log 1/\epsilon)^{-1}))

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\exp(-(\log 2/\epsilon^{2})(1-\epsilon^{2}(\log 1/\epsilon)^{-1})))

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}/2.

Therefore,
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}
as claimed.

Theorem 12
.

Assume that
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
.
Given
SQ(A),Q({\bf b})
, there is an algorithm that returns
SQ({\bf x})
such that
\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq\epsilon\|{\bf x}_{*}\|
with probability at least 0.99 and

{\bf s}{\bf q}({\bf x})=\widetilde{O}((\frac{\kappa_{F}^{6}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\alpha\kappa_{F}^{4}\kappa^{4})({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b}))),

(18)

where
\alpha=\eta/(1-\eta^{2})
and
\eta=\|A{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf b}\|/\|{\bf b}\|
.

Proof.

Let
d=\widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
.
By the updating formula (
11
), we know that
{\bf y}_{k}
is at most
k
-sparse. So in the
k
-th step of the iteration, the calculation of the summation in (
11
) costs
O(kd)
. At each step of the iteration, we sample a row of
A
and query the corresponding entry of
{\bf b}
. So the overall cost of
k
-th step is
O(kd({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b})))
. After
T=O(\kappa_{F}^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))
steps, it converges and the cost is
O(T^{2}d({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b})))=\widetilde{O}((\kappa_{F}^{6}/\varepsilon^{2})({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b})))
.

Note that
{\bf x}_{T}=A^{\dagger}{\bf y}_{T}
and
{\bf y}_{T}
is
T
-sparse. By Lemma
8
(taking
{\bf v}_{i}
to be rows of
A
), we can obtain
SQ_{\phi}({\bf x}_{T})
in time
O(T{\bf s}{\bf q}(A))
, where

\phi=T\frac{\sum_{i}y_{T,i}^{2}\|A_{i*}\|^{2}}{\|{\bf x}_{T}\|^{2}}.

In Appendix
A
, we shall show that
\phi=O(\kappa_{F}^{4}+\alpha\kappa_{F}^{2}\kappa^{4})
.
By Lemma
7
, we can obtain
SQ({\bf x}_{T})
in time

O(\phi T{\bf s}{\bf q}(A))=O((\kappa_{F}^{6}+\alpha\kappa_{F}^{4}\kappa^{4}){\bf s}{\bf q}(A)).

∎

In the above, the parameter
\eta
describes the overlap of
{\bf b}
in the column space of
A
. If
{\bf b}
almost lies in the column space of
A
, then
\eta
is close to 0. In this case, the complexity is basically dominated by the first term.

We next use the Kaczmarz method with averaging (
9
) to reduce the dependence on
\kappa_{F}
in the above theorem.
Similar to (

), we change the updating rule into

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}(\tilde{b}_{i}-\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle)\tilde{A}_{i*}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|I-D_{i}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle\tilde{A}_{i*},

(19)

The definition of
D_{i}
is similar to (
12
), which depends on

now.
In Appendix
B
, we shall prove that if

d=\widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
,
then after

steps of iteration, we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}
. Together with the estimation of
\phi
in Appendix

, we have the following improved result.

Theorem 13
.

Assume that
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
.
Given
SQ(A),Q({\bf b})
, there is an algorithm that returns
SQ({\bf x})
such that
\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq\epsilon\|{\bf x}_{*}\|
with probability at least 0.99 and

{\bf s}{\bf q}({\bf x})=\widetilde{O}\left((\frac{\kappa_{F}^{4}\kappa^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\alpha\kappa_{F}^{2}\kappa^{6})({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b}))\right),

(20)

where
\alpha=\eta/(1-\eta^{2})
and
\eta=\|A{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf b}\|/\|{\bf b}\|
.

Proof.

From the updating formula (
19
), the updating of
{\bf y}_{k}
is

{\bf y}_{k+1}={\bf y}_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\frac{\tilde{b}_{i}-\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|D_{i}A^{\dagger}|{\bf y}_{k}\rangle}{\|A_{i*}\|}{\bf e}_{i}.

So
{\bf y}_{k}
is at most
(k\kappa_{F}^{2}/\kappa^{2})
-sparse. Thus the cost in step

is
O(kd\kappa_{F}^{2}({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b}))/\kappa^{2})=\widetilde{O}(k\kappa_{F}^{4}({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b}))/\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{2})
. After convergence, the total cost is

\widetilde{O}(T^{2}\kappa_{F}^{4}({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b}))/\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{2})=\widetilde{O}(\kappa_{F}^{4}\kappa^{2}({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b}))/\epsilon^{2})
.
By the estimation in Appendix

, we have
\phi=O(\kappa_{F}^{2}\kappa^{2}+\alpha\kappa^{6})
, so the cost to obtain
SQ({\bf x}_{T})
is
O(\phi T\kappa_{F}^{2}{\bf s}{\bf q}(A)/\kappa^{2})=\widetilde{O}((\kappa_{F}^{4}\kappa^{2}+\alpha\kappa_{F}^{2}\kappa^{6}){\bf s}{\bf q}(A))
.
∎

4.2
Solving symmetric positive definite linear systems

Now suppose
A
is a symmetric positive definite matrix. Then a simple approach to solve
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is the randomized coordinate descent iteration
[
16
]

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-\frac{\langle A_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle-b_{r_{k}}}{A_{r_{k},r_{k}}}{\bf e}_{r_{k}},

(21)

where the probability of choosing
r_{k}
is
A_{r_{k},r_{k}}/{\rm Tr}(A)
.
If we apply the above iteration to the normal equation
A^{\dagger}A{\bf x}=A^{\dagger}{\bf b}
, we will obtain the Kaczmarz method.
As for the iteration (
21
), it is not hard to show that

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{\|A^{+}\|{\rm Tr(A)}}\right)^{k}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}.

In fact, assume
A{\bf x}_{*}={\bf b}
, then (
21
) implies that

{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}=\left(I-\frac{|r_{k}\rangle\langle r_{k}|A}{A_{r_{k},r_{k}}}\right)({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}).

Thus

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}\left[I-\frac{|r_{k}\rangle\langle r_{k}|A}{A_{r_{k},r_{k}}}\right]\right\|{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|I-\frac{A}{{\rm Tr}(A)}\right\|{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{\|A^{+}\|{\rm Tr(A)}}\right){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}].

Similar to the idea introduced above to handle Kaczmarz method, we can use the randomized coordinate descent method to solve dense linear systems
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
such that
A
is symmetric positive definite. The only difference is that
\|A\|_{F}^{2}
becomes
{\rm Tr}(A)
, and
\|A^{+}\|^{2}
becomes
\|A^{+}\|
. Moreover, the randomized coordinate descent (
21
) can also be parallelized into

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{2q}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\frac{\langle A_{i*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle-b_{i}}{A_{i,i}}{\bf e}_{i}.

(22)

If we take
\alpha=q={\rm Tr}(A)/\|A\|
, then it converges after
O(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations. The proof is the same as that of (
9
). Therefore, by a similar argument to the proof of Theorem
13
, we have

Theorem 14
.

Let
A
be symmetric positive definite.
Assume that
{\bf x}_{*}=\arg\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
.
Given
SQ(A),Q({\bf b})
, there is an algorithm that returns
SQ({\bf x})
such that
\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq\epsilon\|{\bf x}_{*}\|
with probability at least 0.99 and

{\bf s}{\bf q}({\bf x})=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{{\rm Tr}(A)^{2}\|A^{+}\|^{2}\kappa({\bf s}{\bf q}(A)+{\bf q}({\bf b}))}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(23)

The above result is actually more general than Theorem
13
. If we consider the normal equation
A^{\dagger}A{\bf x}=A^{\dagger}{\bf b}
, Theorem
14
implies Theorem
13
.

5
Discussion

5.1
Solving row sparse linear systems

When
A
is row sparse, the inner product in the Kaczmarz method (
2
) is not expensive to calculate. As a result, we can directly apply the Kaczmarz method to solve the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
. The result is summarized below, and the proof is straightforward.

Theorem 15
.

Assume that
A
has row sparsity
s
. Let
{\bf x}_{*}=\arg\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
.
Given
SQ(A),SQ({\bf b})
, then there is an algorithm that returns an
O(s\kappa_{F}^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))
-sparse vector
{\bf x}
such that
\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq\epsilon\|{\bf x}_{*}\|
with probability at least 0.99 in time

O(s\kappa_{F}^{2}\log(1/\epsilon)).

(24)

Proof.

In the Kaczmarz iteration (
2
), since
A
has row sparsity
s
, it follows that the calculation of the inner product
\langle A_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle
costs
O(s)
operations. This is also the cost of the
k
-th step of iteration. After convergence, the total cost is
O(Ts)
, where
T=O(\kappa_{F}^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))
.
As for the sparsity of
{\bf x}_{k}
, we can choose the initial approximation
{\bf x}_{0}=0
, then
{\bf x}_{k}
is at most
sk
-sparse.
∎

Similarly, based on the randomized coordinate descent method (
21
), we have

Theorem 16
.

Suppose that
A
is symmetric positive definite and
s
-sparse. Let
{\bf x}_{*}=\arg\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
.
Given
SQ(A),SQ({\bf b})
, then there is an algorithm that returns an
O({\rm Tr}(A)\|A^{+}\|\log(1/\epsilon))
-sparse vector
{\bf x}
such that
\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq\epsilon\|{\bf x}_{*}\|
with probability at least 0.99 in time

O(s{\rm Tr}(A)\|A^{+}\|\log(1/\epsilon)).

(25)

Differently from quantum and quantum-inspired algorithms, the outputs of the above two randomized algorithms are vectors.
It is known that if
A
has row and column sparsity
s
and given in the sparse access input model, the currently best known quantum algorithm for matrix inversion costs
O(s\|A\|_{\max}\|A^{+}\|\log^{2}(1/\epsilon))
, where
\|A\|_{\max}
refers to the maximal entry in the sense of absolute value
[
4
]
.
The comparison between this quantum algorithm and the randomized algorithm stated in Theorem
15
may be not fair enough because they are building on different models – the quantum algorithm uses the sparse access model, while the later one assumes access to
SQ(A)
. However, if we only concentrate on the final complexity, the quantum linear solver achieves large speedups if
\|A\|_{F}\gg\|A\|_{\max}
. This can often happen, especially when the rank of
A
is large (e.g.
A
is a sparse unitary).
However, when the rank of
A
is polylog in the dimension, then
\|A\|_{F}=\widetilde{O}(s\|A\|_{\max})
if
A
is row and column sparse. In this case, the quantum computer only achieves a quadratic speedup.
For a row sparse but column dense matrix
A
, if it is stored in the QRAM data structure, the complexity to solve
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
using a quantum computer is
O(\kappa_{F}\log^{2}(1/\epsilon))

[
4
]
. This only gives a quadratic speedup in terms of
\kappa_{F}
over Theorem
15
.

By Theorem
16
, when
A
is symmetric positive definite, the randomized coordinate descent method finds an approximate solution in time
O({\rm Tr}(A)\|A^{+}\|\log(1/\epsilon))
. For simplicity, let us consider the special case that
A
is a sparse density operator, then
{\rm Tr(A)}=1
. Under certain conditions
[
13
]
, we can efficiently find a unitary such that
A
is a sub-block. This means that the complexity of the quantum linear solver for this particular linear system is
O(\|A\|\|A^{+}\|\log^{2}(1/\epsilon))
, while the algorithm based on Theorem
16
costs
O(\|A^{+}\|\log(1/\epsilon))
. So if
\|A\|=\Theta(1)
, it is possible that there is no quantum speedup based on the current quantum linear solvers.

In conclusion, quantum linear solvers can build on many different models, e.g. sparse-access input model, QRAM model, etc. Whenever we have an efficient block-encoding, quantum computers can solve the corresponding linear system efficiently. In comparison, so far there is only one model for quantum-inspired algorithms. The above two theorems indicate that to explore large quantum speedups for sparse problems, it is better to focus on other models rather than the QRAM model. This also leads to the problem of exploring other models for quantum-inspired algorithms.

5.2
Reducing the dependence on
\|A\|_{F}

The Frobenius norm can be large for high-rank matrices. For example, when we use the discretization method (e.g. Euler method) to solve a linear system of ordinary differential equations, the obtained linear system contains the identity as a sub-matrix. So the Frobenius norm can be as large as the dimension of the problem. The performance of quantum-inspired algorithms is highly affected by the Frobenius norm, so currently they are mainly effective for low-rank systems. To enlarge the application scope, it is necessary to consider the problem of how to reduce or even remove the the dependence on the Frobenius norm in the complexity.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no generalizations of the Kaczmarz method such that the total cost is independent of
\|A\|_{F}
. However, it can be generalized such that
\|{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}]\|\leq(1-\kappa^{-r})^{k}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|
and each step of iteration is not expensive, where
r\in\{1,2\}
. For example, a simple generalization is

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}}(\tilde{b}_{r_{k}}-\langle\widetilde{A}_{r_{k}*}|{\bf x}_{k}\rangle)\widetilde{A}_{r_{k}*}.

Generally, the relaxation parameter
{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}/{\|A\|^{2}}\gg 2
, so
this iterative scheme is not convergent,
4
4

4

To make sure that it is convergent, the relaxation parameter should be chosen from
(0,2)
. Usually, the optimal choice is 1.
that is we usually do not have
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\|\leq\rho^{k}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}
for some
\rho<1
. However, we do have
\|{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}]\|\leq(1-\kappa^{-2})^{k}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|
. The proof is straightforward. If we set
T=O(\kappa^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))
, then
\|{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}]\|\leq\epsilon\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|
. There are also some other generalizations
[
28
,
33
,
26
]
such that
T=O(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
, which is optimal for this kind of iterative schemes. One idea to apply this result is to generate
L
samples
{\bf x}_{T1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{TL}
such that
\|{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf x}_{T}]-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\bf x}_{Tl}\|\leq\epsilon\|{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf x}_{T}]\|
. The matrix Bernstein inequality might be helpful to determine
L
. However, to apply this method, we have to find tight bounds for
\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|
and
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]
, which seems not that obvious.

6
Outlook

In this paper, for solving linear systems, we reduced the separation between quantum and quantum-inspired algorithms from
\kappa_{F}:\kappa_{F}^{6}\kappa^{6}/\epsilon^{4}
to
\kappa_{F}:\kappa_{F}^{4}\kappa^{2}/\epsilon^{2}
. It is interesting to consider whether the parameters of our algorithm can be improved further.
For the class of iterative schemes including the Kaczmarz iteration, the optimal convergence rate equals the condition number
\kappa
(as opposed to
\kappa_{F}^{2}
as in the Kaczmarz method), which is achieved by the conjugate gradient method. However, there is currently no such result for the Kaczmarz iterative schemes themselves.
As discussed in Section
5.2
, in
[
28
,
33
,
26
]
, it was proved that there are generalizations of the Kaczmarz method so that
\|{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}]\|\leq(1-\kappa^{-1})^{T}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|
. This convergence result is a little weak but may be helpful to reduce the separation in terms of
\kappa_{F}
further.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{D}\left[\left\langle\left(I-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|\right)({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*})\Bigg|\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\mu_{ik}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(I-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|\right)({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*})\Bigg|\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}{\mathbb{E}}_{D}[\mu_{ik}]|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\right\rangle=0.

Hence, after computing the mean value over
D
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}\leq\left\|\left(I-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|\right)({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*})\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4d}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{A}_{i,j}^{2}x_{k,j}^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}.

For the first term, we now compute its mean value over the random variable
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\left(I-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|\right)({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(I-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|\right)^{2}\right]|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\rangle.

And it can be shown that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(I-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[I-\frac{3}{4}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k},i\neq j}|\tilde{A}_{i*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{i*}|\tilde{A}_{j*}\rangle\langle\tilde{A}_{j*}|\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I-\frac{3q}{4}\frac{A^{\dagger}A}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}(q^{2}-q)\left(\frac{A^{\dagger}A}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\preceq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{3q}{4}\frac{\sigma_{\min}(A^{\dagger}A)}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}(q^{2}-q)\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}(A^{\dagger}A)}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)I

\displaystyle\preceq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\right)I.

In the last step, we used the result
q=\|A\|_{F}^{2}/\|A\|^{2}
so that the second term is
3/4\kappa^{2}
and the third term is less than
1/4\kappa^{4}\leq 1/4\kappa^{2}
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{1}{4d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{A}_{i,j}^{2}x_{k,j}^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A_{*j}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{4d\|A\|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}\|^{2}].

Now set
T=O(\kappa^{2}\log(2/\epsilon^{2}))
, and

d=\frac{T\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}\epsilon^{2}}=\frac{\kappa_{F}^{2}\log(2/\epsilon^{2})}{\epsilon^{2}}.

(28)

Then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4T}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2T}){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2T}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}.

Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{T}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}]\lesssim(1-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2T})^{T}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}\|{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}.

Appendix C
Estimation of
\phi
for Kaczmarz method with averaging

The calculation here is similar to that in Appendix

.
For simplicity, denote

d

28
).

A
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{D}[\langle{\bf w}_{k}|{\bf w}_{k}^{\prime}\rangle_{\Lambda}]={\mathbb{E}}_{D}[\langle{\bf y}_{k}|{\bf w}_{k}^{\prime}\rangle_{\Lambda}]=0
