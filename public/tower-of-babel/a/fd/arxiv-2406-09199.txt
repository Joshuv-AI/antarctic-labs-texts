Precise analysis of ridge interpolators under heavy correlations – a Random Duality Theory view
Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract




Abstract

We consider fully row/column-correlated linear regression models and study several classical estimators (including minimum norm interpolators (GLS), ordinary least squares (LS), and ridge regressors). We show that
Random Duality Theory
(RDT) can be utilized to obtain precise closed form characterizations of all estimators related optimizing quantities of interest, including the
prediction risk
(testing or generalization error). On a qualitative level out results recover the risk’s well known non-monotonic (so-called double-descent) behavior as the number of features/sample size ratio increases. On a quantitative level, our closed form results show how the risk explicitly depends on all key model parameters, including the problem dimensions and covariance matrices. Moreover, a special case of our results, obtained when intra-sample (or time-series) correlations are not present, precisely match the corresponding ones obtained via spectral methods in
[
24
,
16
,
17
,
6
]
.

Index Terms: Random linear regression; Interpolators. Ridge estimators; Correlations
.

2
Mathematical model and definitions

We start with the standard linear model

\displaystyle{\bf y}=X\bar{\beta}+{\bf e},

(1)

where
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
is a matrix of covariates or feature vectors,
{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a vector of responses, and
{\bf e}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a noise vector (unless stated otherwise, throughout the paper all vectors are assumed to be
column vectors
). The model is beyond well known and needs no further or extensively elaborate motivation. As such it has been utilized in a variety of scientific and engineering fields over the last at least a couple of centuries. For the easiness of exposition and the concreteness of presentation, we will connect it to the process of traning/testing data relations. Within such a context, we will view the rows of
X
(
X_{i,:},i=1,\dots,m
) as vectors of features that are presumed to create the responses
{\bf y}_{i}
through a (potentially imperfect or noisy) linear combination

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{i}=X_{i,:}\bar{\beta}+{\bf e}_{i},

(2)

where
\bar{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
contains coefficients of the desired linearity. Given the access to
m
data pairs
(X_{i,:},{\bf y}_{i})
. one would want to recover
\bar{\beta}
as accurately as possible. As hinted in say
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
, various measures of accuracy are of interest. We here consider the so-called
prediction risk
or as it is often called (particularly within the machine learning and neural networks communities), the
genratlization/testing error
. It will be formally defined in the following way. Assuming a statistical context, where
X
and
{\bf e}
are random, for an estimator of
\bar{\beta}
, say
\hat{\beta}
, we have the prediction risk

\displaystyle R(\bar{\beta},\hat{\beta})\triangleq{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf x}^{(t)}}\left(\left(\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\hat{\beta}-\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\bar{\beta}\right)^{2}|X,{\bf y}\right),

(3)

where,
{\bf x}^{(t)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the so-called
testing
vector of features completely independent of the given
training
data pairs
(X_{i,:},{\bf y}_{i})
. While
R(\bar{\beta},\hat{\beta})
is clearly deterministic when viewed through the prism of
{\bf x}^{(t)}
, it remains random when viewed through the prism of randomness od
X
and
{\bf y}
. Along the same lines, throughout the paper, we adopt the convention that the subscripts next to
{\mathbb{E}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}
denote the randomness with respect to which the evaluation is taken (when it is clear from the context, the subscript(s) will be omitted). It should also be noted that the above prediction risk can alternatively be viewed as the so-called
excess risk
. In such a context the prediction risk itself (given in (
3
)) could be redefined slightly differently with one option, for example, being replacement of
\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\bar{\beta}
by
{\bf y}^{(t)}
, where
{\bf y}^{(t)}
would be the testing response. Switching between these alternative definitions is analytically relatively easy and, as it brings no conceptual novelty, we, throughout the paper, utilize the definition given in (
3
). One should also note that the prediction risk is defined as a function of both the “true” (
\bar{\beta}
) and the estimated (
\hat{\beta}
) linear coefficients. Mathematically speaking, its studying, therefore, falls into the category of – what is in
[
44
]
termed as – the problem dependent analysis.

The prediction risk defined in (
3
) will be the central focus of our study below. Before we start the presentation of the technical analysis, we will need to introduce the types of scenarios that we consider. Three things are of critical importance to properly set the analytical infrastructure:
(i)
The problem dimensions;
(ii)
The problem statistics; and
(iii)
The relevant estimators. We briefly discuss each of them in the following three subsections.

2.1
Dimensions

We consider large dimensional scenario, which will mean that
all
key underlying dimensions go to infinity. Under such a context, we are interested in mathematically the hardest, so-called large
n

linear
(proportional) regime, where

\displaystyle\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n},

(4)

and
\alpha
clearly remains constant as
n
grows. It is not that difficult to see that
\alpha
is the under-parametrization ratio (or the inverse of the over-parametrization ratio). We generically assume that all underlying matrices are of full rank. On occasion however, the context may allow considerations of lower ranks. While the appearance of any such instance will be followed by a specifically tailored discussion, we here emphasize that throughout the paper all matrix ranks always remain
linearly
proportional to
n
. Also, to simplify the writing and to make the overall presentation look neater, we often avoid repeatedly using the
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}
notation throughout derivations. It will be clear from the context that any expression involving
n
is assumed to be taken in the
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}
sense.

2.2
Statistics

To further emphasize the overall presentation neatness, we assume a Gaussian (albeit a very general one) statistical scenario. We assume that
\bar{\beta}
in (
1
) is deterministic and fixed and that the only source of randomness are
X
and
{\bf e}
. In particular, we take both
X
and
{\bf e}
to be comprised of Gaussian entries and, for two full rank (non-necessarily symmetric) matrices
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and
\overline{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
, given in the following way

\displaystyle X=ZA,\quad\quad{\bf e}=\sigma\overline{A}{\bf v},

(5)

where
Z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
and
{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
have iid standard normal entries and
\sigma
is a fixed constant. One can then rewrite (
1
) as

\displaystyle{\bf y}=X\bar{\beta}+{\bf e}=ZA\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{A}{\bf v}.

(6)

It is then not that difficult to see that the rows of feature matrix
X
,
X_{i:,}
, are independent of each other but the elements within each row are correlated among themselves with the correlation matrix given as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}X_{i,:}^{T}X_{i,:}=A^{T}A.

(7)

Analogously one also has for the noise correlations

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}{\bf e}{\bf e}^{T}=\overline{A}\overline{A}^{T}.

(8)

Writing the SVDs of both
A
and
\overline{A}
, we also have

\displaystyle A=U\Sigma V^{T},\quad\overline{A}=\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}\overline{V}^{T}.

(9)

Combining (
6
) and (
9
), we easily find

\displaystyle{\bf y}=ZA\bar{\beta}+\overline{A}{\bf v}=ZU\Sigma V^{T}\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}\overline{V}^{T}{\bf v}.

(10)

Due to rotational symmetry of the standard normal entries of
Z
and
{\bf v}
, (
10
) is statistically equivalent to the following

\displaystyle{\bf y}=Z\Sigma V^{T}\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}.

(11)

In other words, one can basically view
X
and
{\bf e}
as

\displaystyle X=Z\Sigma V^{T},\quad{\bf e}=\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}.

(12)

The above assumed statistics will be sufficient to present the key ideas. It essentially allows for the existence of the cross-sectional (or inter-features) correlations and intra-sample (or time-series) noise correlations. Later on (see, Section
4
), we will complete the model by allowing for intra-sample (time-series) type of correlations among the rows of
X
.

Unless otherwise stated, we will also assume that the testing features vector
{\bf x}^{(t)}
has the same statistics as any of the rows of the training features matrix
X
. That basically means that we will assume that

\displaystyle\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}=\left({\bf z}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\Sigma V^{T},

(13)

where
{\bf z}^{(t)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is comprised of iid standard normals independent of
X
and
{\bf y}
(basically,
X
and
{\bf e}
, or
Z
and
{\bf v}
). It is useful to note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}{\bf x}^{(t)}\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}={\mathbb{E}}V\Sigma{\bf z}^{(t)}\left({\bf z}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\Sigma V^{T}=V\Sigma\Sigma V^{T}.

(14)

Also, as there will typically be not much of a point in having both
\sigma
and magnitude of
\bar{\beta}
vary, we will (unless otherwise stated) scale one of them to pone. In other words, we will take
\|\bar{\beta}\|_{2}=1
.

2.3
Estimators

Depending on the system dimensions (regimes of operation), we will consider three different estimators: 1) Minimum
\ell_{2}
norm interpolator (also, often called the generalized least squares (GLS) estimator); 2) Ridge estimator; and 3) Plain least squares. As is well known, they are all directly related to each other. We will recall on some of these relations throughout the presentation as well. Here we formally introduce the estimators.

1) Minimum
\ell_{2}
norm interpolator (over-parameterized regime,
n>m
):

\displaystyle\beta_{gls}\triangleq\mbox{arg}\min_{\beta}

\displaystyle\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle X\beta={\bf y}.

(15)

It is not that difficult to see that the above optimization admits a closed form solution

\displaystyle\beta_{gls}=X^{T}(XX^{T})^{-1}{\bf y},

(16)

or, if written in an alternative form,

\displaystyle\beta_{gls}=(X^{T}X)^{-1}X^{T}{\bf y},

(17)

where
(X^{T}X)^{-1}
is the pseudo-inverse of
X^{T}X
(only the positive eigenvalues are inverted).

2) Ridge estimator (any
n
and
m
):
For a given fixed
\lambda>0
, we define the so-called ridge regression estimator as

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}(\lambda)\triangleq\mbox{arg}\min_{\beta}\lambda\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{m}\|{\bf y}-X\beta\|_{2}^{2}.

(18)

After trivially finding the derivatives, it is again not that difficult to see that the above optimization admits the following closed form solution

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{m}\left(\lambda I+\frac{1}{m}X^{T}X\right)^{-1}X^{T}{\bf y}=\left(\lambda mI+X^{T}X\right)^{-1}X^{T}{\bf y}.

(19)

Moreover, comparing (
17
) and (
19
), one easily recognizes that the GLS estimator is basically a special case of the ridge regression one, obtained for a particular value of ridge parameter
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. In other words, one has

\displaystyle\beta_{gls}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\beta_{rr}(\lambda).

(20)

One should note that
\lambda
is not allowed to take value zero in this regime.

3) Plain least squares (under-parameterized regime,
n<m
):

\displaystyle\beta_{ls}\triangleq\mbox{arg}\min_{\beta}\frac{1}{m}\|{\bf y}-X\beta\|_{2}^{2}.

(21)

After taking the derivative one again simply finds the closed form solution

\displaystyle\beta_{ls}=(X^{T}X)^{-1}X^{T}{\bf y}.

(22)

In this regime the definition of the ridge estimator can be extended to
\lambda\geq 0
and one would then have

\displaystyle\beta_{ls}=\beta_{rr}(0).

(23)

All three estimators, (
17
), (
19
), and (
22
) are clearly functions of
X
and
{\bf y}
. In other words, they are functions of the available training data. Given the above assumed statistics of
X
,
{\bf y}
, and
{\bf x}^{(t)}
, we will in the rest of the paper be interested in proving a precise statistical characterization of the (random) prediction risk

\displaystyle R(\bar{\beta},\hat{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf x}^{(t)}}\left(\left(\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\hat{\beta}-\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\bar{\beta}\right)^{2}|X,{\bf y}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf x}^{(t)}}\left(\bar{\beta}-\hat{\beta}\right)^{T}{{\bf x}^{(t)}}\left({{\bf x}^{(t)}}\right)^{T}\left(\bar{\beta}-\hat{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\bar{\beta}-\hat{\beta}\right)^{T}V\Sigma\Sigma V^{T}\left(\bar{\beta}-\hat{\beta}\right),

where, depending on the regime of interest,
\hat{\beta}
will be one of
\beta_{gls}
,
\beta_{rr}
, and
\beta_{ls}
. Our main concern however will be the over-parameterized regime and consequently the GLS estimator.

3
Precise analysis of all three estimators

We below provide a precise performance analysis of all the three above introduced classical estimators. Each of the estimators is discussed in a separate subsection. As mentioned earlier when discussing underlying statistics, we in this section consider scenarios with the cross-sectional (or inter-features) correlations and intra-sample (or time-series) noise correlations. The intra-sample (time-series) correlations among the rows of
X
will be discussed in Section
4
. Separating these discussions allows to emphasize two key points: (i) there is a substantial analytical difference needed to complete the fully correlated models of Section
4
; and (ii) despite such a difference the analysis of the row-uncorrelated
X
can still be utilized for the fully correlated scenario.

We start with the GLS interpolator as it relates to the over-parameterized regime that has gained a lot popularity in recent years due to its utilization in machine learning and the architecture design of neural networks.

3.1
GLS interpolator

The analysis that we provide below will do slightly more than just providing the characterization of the GLS estimator. Namely, we will actually provide a precise statistical characterization of all the key quantiti4es associated with the optimization program (
15
). It will turn out that the prediction risk from (
24
) is among them as well. We start by defining the objective of the program (
15
)

\displaystyle\xi_{gls}\triangleq\min_{\beta}

\displaystyle\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle X\beta={\bf y}.

(25)

To ensure that the presentation is not overloaded with a tone of special cases and unnecessary tiny details that bring no conceptual insights, we assume that all the key quantities appearing in the derivations below are bounded (deterministically or randomly). Recalling on (
11
) and (
12
), we proceed by writing a statistical equivalent to (
25
)

\displaystyle\xi_{gls}\triangleq\min_{\beta}

\displaystyle\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle Z\Sigma V^{T}\beta=Z\Sigma V^{T}\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}.

(26)

After writing the Lagrangian we further have

\displaystyle\xi_{gls}=\min_{\beta}\max_{\nu}

\displaystyle\left(\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}+\nu^{T}\left(Z\Sigma V^{T}\beta-Z\Sigma V^{T}\bar{\beta}-\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}\right)\right).

(27)

One should now note that the above actually holds generically, i.e., it holds for any
Z
,
{\bf v}
,
V
,
\Sigma
,
\overline{U}
,
\overline{\Sigma}
,
\sigma
, and
\bar{\beta}
. It then automatically applies to the random instances as well. As precisely such instance are of our particular interest here, the following would be a simple summary of the probabilistic characterization of
\xi_{gls}
that we are ultimately looking for.

Ultimate goal:

Given:

\displaystyle\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}\in(1,\infty)\quad\mbox{and}\quad V,\Sigma,\overline{U},\overline{\Sigma},\sigma,\bar{\beta}

find

\displaystyle\xi_{gls}^{(opt)}

such that

\displaystyle\forall\epsilon>0,\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{Z,{\bf v}}\left((1-\epsilon)\xi_{gls}^{(opt)}\leq\xi_{gls}\leq(1+\epsilon)\xi_{gls}^{(opt)}\right)\longrightarrow 1.

3.1.1
Handling GLS estimator via RDT

We find it useful to first briefly recall on the main RDT principles. After that we continue by showing, step-by-step, how each of them relates to the problems of our interest here.

Summary of the RDT’s main principles

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip-13.73148pt\mbox{1) \emph{Finding underlying optimization algebraic representation}}&\hskip 0.0pt\mbox{2) \emph{Determining the random dual}}\\
\hskip-13.73148pt\mbox{3) \emph{Handling the random dual}}&\hskip 0.0pt\mbox{4) \emph{Double-checking strong random duality.}}\end{array}

1)
Algebraic characterization:

Lemma 1

(Algebraic optimization representation) Let

\displaystyle\xi_{rp}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{Z,{\bf v}}f_{rp}(Z,{\bf v}).

Then

\xi_{gls}=f_{rp}(Z,{\bf v})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{Z,{\bf v}}\xi_{gls}=\xi_{rp}.

Proof.

Follows automatically via the Lagrangian from (

Determining the random dual:

Theorem 1

(Objective characterization via random dual) Assume the setup of Lemma

\displaystyle\|\Sigma V^{T}\left(\beta-\bar{\beta}\right)\|_{2}

\displaystyle f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h},{\bf v})

\displaystyle\min_{\beta}\max_{\nu}\left(\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}+c_{2}\nu^{T}{\bf g}+\|\nu\|_{2}{\bf h}^{T}\Sigma V^{T}\left(\beta-\bar{\beta}\right)-\sigma\nu^{T}\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}\right)\hskip 50.58878pt({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\textbf{random dual}})

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bf g},{\bf h},{\bf v}}\left(f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h},{\bf v})\geq(1-\epsilon)\xi_{rd}\right)\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{Z,{\bf v}}\left(f_{rp}(Z,{\bf v})\geq(1-\epsilon)\xi_{rd}\right).

Proof.

Follows as an immediate application of the Gordon’s probabilistic comparison theorem (see, e.g., Theorem B in

]

∎

3)
Handling the random dual:
We start by solving the inner maximization over

\displaystyle f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h},{\bf v})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\min_{\beta}\max_{\nu}\left(\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}+c_{2}\nu^{T}{\bf g}+\|\nu\|_{2}{\bf h}^{T}\Sigma V^{T}\left(\beta-\bar{\beta}\right)-\sigma\nu^{T}\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}\right)

\displaystyle\min_{\beta}\max_{\|\nu\|_{2}}\left(\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nu\|_{2}{\bf h}^{T}\Sigma V^{T}\left(\beta-\bar{\beta}\right)+\|\nu\|_{2}\|c_{2}{\bf g}-\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\min_{\beta}\max_{\|\nu\|_{2}}\left(\|V^{T}\beta\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nu\|_{2}{\bf h}^{T}\Sigma\left(V^{T}\beta-V^{T}\bar{\beta}\right)+\|\nu\|_{2}\|c_{2}{\bf g}-\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x},c_{2}\geq 0}\max_{\nu_{s}\geq 0}\left(\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}+\nu_{s}{\bf h}^{T}\Sigma\left({\bf x}-{\bf c}\right)+\nu_{s}\|c_{2}{\bf g}-\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}\|_{2}\right),

\displaystyle{\bf x}=V^{T}\beta,\quad{\bf c}\triangleq V^{T}\bar{\beta},\quad\nu_{s}=\|\nu\|_{2},

\displaystyle c_{2}=\|\Sigma\left({\bf x}-{\bf c}\right)\|_{2}.

(38)

\nu_{s}

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x}}\max_{\gamma}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}+\nu_{s}{\bf h}^{T}\Sigma\left({\bf x}-{\bf c}\right)+\nu_{s}\|c_{2}{\bf g}-\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}\|_{2}+\gamma\|\Sigma\left({\bf x}-{\bf c}\right)\|_{2}^{2}-\gamma c_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\max_{\gamma}\min_{{\bf x}}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}+\nu_{s}{\bf h}^{T}\Sigma\left({\bf x}-{\bf c}\right)+\nu_{s}\|c_{2}{\bf g}-\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v}\|_{2}+\gamma\|\Sigma\left({\bf x}-{\bf c}\right)\|_{2}^{2}-\gamma c_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\max_{\gamma}\min_{{\bf x}}{\mathcal{L}}_{2}\left({\bf x},\gamma;c_{2},\nu_{s}\right),

5
Conclusion

We studied classical linear regression models and three of the most well known estimators associated with them: (i) minimum norm interpolators (generalized least squares (GLS)); (ii) ordinary least squares (LS); and (ii) ridge estimators. A statistical context with Gaussian feature matrix and noise is considered. In addition to inter-feature (cross-sectional) correlations, the intra-sample ones for both the features and noise are considered as well. The (random) optimization programs that produce the above three estimators are statistically analyzed through a utilization of a powerful mathematical engine called
Random Duality Theory
(RDT). Precise closed form characterizations of all optimizing quantities associated with all three optimization programs are obtained. Among these quantities, we particularly focused on the
prediction risk
(generalization/testing error) and observed that it exhibits the well known non-monotonic (so-called double-descent) behavior as the over-parametrization increases. As our results are relatively simple closed forms, they explicitly show how the risk depends on all key model parameters, including the problem dimensions ratios and covariance matrices. When the intra-sample (or time-series) correlations are absent, our results precisely match the corresponding ones obtained via spectral methods in
[
24
,
16
,
17
,
6
]
.

The presented methodology is fairly generic and allows for a variety of extensions and/or generalizations. For example all those mentioned in companion paper
[
54
]
apply here as well. Imperfections in observable features, their potential absence, and adversarial responses are only a few among many popular (yet practically very relevant) scenarios that received a strong attention in recent years in both machine learning and statistics communities. For each of these scenarios,various different types of estimators could be of interest as well. All these extensions can be handled through the program presented here without any further conceptually novel insights. As is usually the case within the context of RDT, the concrete technical realizations of such extensions are problem specific and as such need to be tailored for each of them separately. We will present these adjustments together with concrete results that they produce in separate papers.

As pointed out in
[
52
]
, the RDT considerations do not require the standard Gaussianity. We have utilized it, however, as it allowed for the presentation to look neater. The needed conceptual adjustments, as one deviates from the Gaussianity, are minimal (the underlying writing, however, becomes a bit lengthy and cumbersome). An application of the Lindeberg central limit theorem variant (see, e.g.,
[
33
]
) is in our view the most elegant way to extend the RDT results to various different statistics and the approach of
[
13
]
is particularly convenient in that regard.
