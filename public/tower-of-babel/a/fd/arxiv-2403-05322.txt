Direct-search methods in the year 2025: Theoretical guarantees and algorithmic paradigms
K. J. Dzahini, F. Rinaldi, C. W. Royer, D. Zeffiro

Abstract
Optimizing a function without using derivatives is a challenging paradigm, that precludes from using classical algorithms from nonlinear optimization, and may thus seem intractable other than by using heuristics. Nevertheless, the field of derivative-free optimization has succeeded in producing algorithms that do not rely on derivatives and yet are endowed with convergence guarantees. One class of such methods, called direct-search methods , is particularly popular thanks to its simplicity of implementation, even though its theoretical underpinnings are not always easy to grasp. In this work, we survey contemporary direct-search algorithms from a theoretical viewpoint, with the aim of highlighting the key theoretical features of these methods. We provide a basic introduction to the main classes of direct-search methods, including line-search techniques that have received little attention in earlier surveys. We also put a particular emphasis on probabilistic direct-search techniques and their application to noisy problems, a topic that has undergone significant algorithmic development in recent years. Finally, we complement existing surveys by reviewing the main theoretical advances for solving constrained and multiobjective optimization using direct-search algorithms.



1
Introduction

Direct-search methods, whose name was coined by Hooke and Jeeves
[
61
]
,
form one of the main classes of derivative-free optimization (DFO) algorithms.
Those methods work in a very easy and intuitive way: they sample the objective
function at a finite number of points at each iteration and decide which actions to
take next solely based on those function values, either by function value
comparison through ranking or based on numerical values, and without
fundamentally relying upon
any explicit
or implicit derivative approximation or model building. This simple principle partly explains why direct-search approaches
have been
highly popular
since
the early days of numerical optimization. Despite the existence of tools that
allow to
easily
use first- or second-order derivative information in an algorithm (i.e., tools that
compute derivatives automatically), there is still
a need for methods that
rely solely on function values in simulation-based problems where derivative
information can hardly be obtained
[
9
]
. As a result, direct-search
methods remain a popular choice of practitioners. Although classical techniques
such as Nelder–Mead’s simplex method
[
81
]
or evolutionary
algorithms
[
16
]
are not built on strong theoretical
foundations, modern direct-search algorithms are typically equipped with
convergence guarantees, that certify their ability to converge to a
(possibly approximate) solution of the problem at hand asymptotically (global
convergence) or in finite time (complexity bounds). Still, those guarantees are
somewhat scattered in the literature, in research papers as well as surveys and
books on derivative-free optimization.

Indeed, direct-search methods have been the subject of several dedicated
surveys
[
4
,
65
,
73
,
91
]
.
The 1996 survey of Wright
[
91
]
was arguably the first on
direct-search schemes, with an emphasis on simplicial methods that may lack
convergence guarantees. The later survey of Lewis et al.
[
73
]

discussed in particular pattern search methods along with the convergence results
that relied on simple decrease. The landmark survey of Kolda et
al.
[
65
]
followed and provided a thorough investigation of convergence
results on
direct-search schemes
based on simple and sufficient decrease,
highlighting developments in the (linearly) constrained setting but focusing mainly
on smooth objectives. To the best of our knowledge, the most recent survey article
is due to Audet
[
4
]
, who described the developments of

direct-search methods
with an emphasis on mesh adaptive
direct-search

(MADS)
variants and their
use in applications.
More broadly, direct-search methods have been featured in textbooks and reviews on
derivative-free optimization. Conn et al.
[
32
]
covered the basics of
direct-search methods with convergence results. Audet and Hare
[
9
]

focused on direct-search schemes based on simple decrease, and provided a thorough
introduction to pattern search and MADS. Finally, in the recent overview of
derivative-free algorithms, Larson et al.
[
70
]
provided
complexity
results for direct-search methods in comparison with zeroth-order algorithms,
especially in the convex setting.

This survey aims at complementing existing work by reviewing the main
direct-search techniques proposed in the literature and their theoretical
guarantees. First, more emphasis is put on line-search techniques, that
have been somewhat overlooked in previous surveys. Secondly, connections are drawn
between the directional and the mesh-based direct-search techniques, two classes
of methods that tend to be discussed separately. We believe that readers should be
aware of the two classes of algorithms, along with their specifics, that are
highlighted throughout our work. Third, this survey begins with a thorough
treatment of direct-search methods in the unconstrained setting, in both a smooth
and a nonsmooth setting (note that we do not leverage convexity in this survey’s
results). The underlying goal is to ease the transition from this setting to more
complicated setups, such as noisy or constrained optimization. We believe that a
reader interested in using direct-search methods would likely face such challenges,
and that a good understanding of an algorithm’s behavior in the simplest possible
setting provides valuable insight regarding its extension to other settings.

The rest of the survey is structured as follows. Section
2

provides a generic direct-search template for unconstrained problems, along with
the key mathematical concepts used to establish convergence results.
Section
3
reviews the main classes of algorithms in the unconstrained setting and represents a starting point for any of the subsequent sections.
Section
4
describes algorithms that tolerate noise in the function
values. Section
5
is concerned with handling constrained formulations.
Section
6
presents the main results in a multiobjective setting.
The final comments are given in Section
7
.

2.1
The direct-search framework

In this section, we introduce the main ideas behind
direct-search schemes

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\bm{x}),

where

In this survey, we describe theoretical guarantees that require some regularity

.

Practical direct-search methods typically include an optional search step,
that allows for using problem-specific heuristics as well as additional strategies
for optimization
[
9
, Section 7.2]
. Nevertheless, theoretical
guarantees of direct-search techniques do not rely on the properties of this search
step. Since our survey focuses on these guarantees, we present direct-search
methods without a search step, but emphasize that search steps are often crucial
to the practical performance of these algorithms.

2.2
Main mathematical concepts

In this section, we gather key algebraic definitions associated with the choice
of directions in a direct-search method, for future reference in the rest of the
survey. We also provide standard analytical definitions associated with the
objective function (and the constraint functions when applicable).

2.2.1
Geometry of polling sets

One of the main features of a direct-search method is its strategy to choose the
poll directions. Those directions play a key role in guaranteeing
convergence, provided they possess favorable geometric properties. The first
property below is an intrinsic property of a direction set.

Definition 2.1
.

(
[
57
, Section 2.1]
)

For a vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and a set of directions
\mathbb{D}
,
the cosine measure of
\mathbb{D}
with respect to
\bm{v}
is defined as

\textnormal{cm}(\mathbb{D},\bm{v}):=\max_{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}}\frac{\left\langle\bm{d},\bm{v}\right\rangle}{\|\bm{d}\|\|\bm{v}\|}\,.

The
cosine measure
of
\mathbb{D}
is then

\textnormal{cm}(\mathbb{D}):=\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\textnormal{cm}(\mathbb{D},\bm{v}).

The cosine measure of a set
\mathbb{D}
captures how much of the space is covered by the
cone spanned using the vectors in
\mathbb{D}
. The best scenario corresponds to spanning
the entire space, and corresponds to the following notion.

Definition 2.2
.

A set
\mathbb{D}
of
n_{D}
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
positive spanning set
if its
cosine measure is positive, or equivalently, if every vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be
written as a nonnegative linear combination of vectors in
\mathbb{D}
.

Positive spanning sets are the typical choice of poll directions for direct-search
methods. In addition to relying on positive spanning sets, certain direct-search
methods ensure that the points considered for evaluation lie on carefully designed
grids. We adopt here the terminology of meshes and frames
[
9
]
, and
provide the definitions independently of any algorithm.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\bm{D}:=\bm{G}\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{D}}
be the matrix representation of a set
\mathbb{D}
, where
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is invertible and the columns of
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n_{D}}
form a positive spanning set for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For any vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any

\alpha^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}>0
, the
mesh
of coarseness
\alpha^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}
corresponding to
\bm{D}

and centered at
\bm{x}
, is defined by

\mathcal{M}=\left\{\bm{x}+\alpha^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}\bm{D}\bm{z}:\ \bm{z}\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{D}}\right\}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

(3)

T
he frame
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
of
extent

\alpha^{p}\geq\alpha^{m}
generated by
\bm{D}
, centered at
\bm{x}
,
is defined by

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{M}:{\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha^{p}d_{\max}}\right\}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},}

(4)

where
d_{\max}:=\max\left\{{\left\lVert\bm{d}^{\prime}\right\rVert}_{\infty}:\bm{d}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}\right\}
.

One interesting feature of meshes and frames is that they lead to asymptotic
density, a concept that has proven fundamental in analyzing direct-search methods,
especially in the nonsmooth setting
[
7
,
9
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

An infinite sequence
\{\mathbb{D}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
of vector sets
defines an asymptotically dense set of directions if the set

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{D}_{\infty}:=}\left\{\bm{d}/{\left\lVert\bm{d}\right\rVert}_{\infty}:\ \bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}

is dense in the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.
\forall\bm{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\forall\epsilon>0,\exists\bm{d}_{\infty}\in\mathbb{D}_{\infty}
such that
{\left\lVert\bm{u}-\bm{d}_{\infty}\right\rVert}_{2}<\epsilon
.

2.2.2
Function classes and stationary points

By design, a direct-search method can be applied to any function for which the
objective value (or approximations thereof, see Section
4
) can be
queried. Still, in order to establish convergence
and convergence rates
, one
typically assumes a certain level of regularity in the objective function. For this
reason, we
assume
continuity of the objective function, though we note that

direct-search methods have

also
been applied to extended-value and
discontinuous problems
[
14
,
24
,
35
]
.

In this survey, we consider both Lipschitz continuous functions or functions
with Lipschitz continuous derivative, in the sense of the two
definitions below.

Definition 2.5
.

A function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}
with
n^{\prime}\in\{1,n\}
is said to be
L
-Lipschitz continuous

for
L>0
if

\forall(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\|F(\bm{x})-F(\bm{y})\|\leq L\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|.

Definition 2.6
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be
L
-smooth
for
L>0
if

f
is continuously differentiable and
\nabla f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

L
-Lipschitz continuous.

When optimizing a smooth function without constraints, direct-search methods
generally aim at converging towards a point with zero gradient, called a

stationary point
. We
present
results of that form in the upcoming
sections. Existing analyzes of direct-search methods also provide guarantees on
nonsmooth functions using generalized derivatives. The next two definitions

from Clarke differential calculus
[
31
]
correspond to the most
classical notions of generalized derivatives
used in the direct-search literature.

Definition 2.7
.

The
Clarke directional derivative
of
f
at
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the
direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

f^{\circ}(\bm{x};\,\bm{v})=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{x}\\
t\searrow 0\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\bm{y}+t\bm{v})-f(\bm{y})}{t}\,.

(5)

The
Clarke subdifferential
of
f
at
\bm{x}
is then defined as

\partial f(\bm{x})=\left\{\bm{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \middle|\ \bm{g}^{\top}\bm{v}\leq f^{\circ}(\bm{x};\,\bm{v})\quad\forall\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

(6)

Definition 2.8
.

The point
\bm{x}^{*}
is
Clarke stationary
for
f
if

f^{\circ}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{*}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0};}\,\bm{v})\geq 0
for every
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

or, equivalently, if
\mathbf{0}\in\partial f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{*}})
.

The notion of Clarke stationarity is weaker than many others used in variational
analysis.
As a result, it has been used extensively to derive guarantees for
direct-search schemes
[
5
]
. More recently, an alternate notion
of stationarity due to Goldstein
[
55
]
has been employed
to obtain complexity results for direct-search methods.

Definition 2.9
.

Given
\delta>0
, the point
\bm{x}^{*}
is called
\delta
-Goldstein
stationary
for
f
if

\inf\left\{\|\bm{g}\|\ \middle|\ \bm{g}\in\mathrm{conv}\left(\cup_{\bm{y}\in B_{\delta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{*}})}\partial f(\bm{y})\right)\right\}=0

where
\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
denotes the set of convex
combinations of vectors in
\mathcal{S}
.

Note that this survey
also considers
approximate definitions of stationary
points in order to derive complexity results. For instance, in the smooth setting,
rather than a point with zero gradient, we might be interested in converging
towards a point with small enough gradient norm. In the nonsmooth setting, the
notion of an approximate stationary point is more intricate, and we provide the
definition below.

Definition 2.10
.

Given
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, a vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a

(\delta,\varepsilon)
-Goldstein stationary
point for a function
f
if

\inf\left\{\|\bm{g}\|\ \middle|\ \bm{g}\in\text{conv}\cup_{\bm{y}\in B_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\partial f(\bm{y})\right\}\leq\varepsilon.

(7)

The concept of approximate stationary points
are also

discussed in Section
3.1
.

2.3
Basic convergence ingredients

In this section, we provide the main assumptions and technical lemmas
that are used while analyzing a direct-search method. As in the rest
of Section
2
, we focus on the unconstrained,
deterministic setting, and provide results for both smooth and
nonsmooth objectives.

2.3.1
Main assumptions

We begin by listing the key assumptions that are made on the objective
function.

Assumption 2.1
.

The objective
f
is lower bounded, i.e., there exists

f^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f(\bm{x})\geq f^{*}

\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Assumption
2.1
is typically used together with a guarantee
of decrease at every iteration of the algorithm to establish convergence.

Assumption 2.2
.

The sublevel set

L_{0}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ f(\bm{x})\leq f(\bm{x}_{0})\}
is
bounded, where
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
initial
point of
Algorithm
1
.

Assumption
2.2
certifies that the iterates of
Algorithm
1
remain in a bounded set, which is particularly
helpful to guarantee convergence of a subsequence of the iterates.

In addition to assumptions on the objective function, an analysis of
Algorithm
1
typically requires conditions on the polling
directions, that are stated below.

Assumption 2.3
.

For
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
we have

\|\bm{d}\|\in[d_{\min},d_{\max}]
where
d_{\min}
and
d_{\max}
are positive
constants.

The upper bound ensures that the information obtained in unsuccessful steps is
relevant for the directional derivative, while the lower bound ensures that the
sufficient decrease condition can indeed be satisfied for good descent directions.

The last classical assumption concerns the forcing function.

Assumption 2.4
.

The forcing function
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, is continuous,

nondecreasing and

\rho(\alpha)=o(\alpha)
as
\alpha\rightarrow 0
.

Note that using simple decrease (i.e.,
\rho\equiv 0
) leads to immediate
satisfaction of Assumption
2.4
. Popular choices for
\rho
in direct
search based on sufficient decrease include
\rho(\alpha)=c\,\alpha^{q}
with
c>0

and
q>1
.

2.3.2
Key lemmas

A remarkable feature of direct-search schemes is that their analysis
relies upon
unsuccessful iterations
, at which the iterate does
not change. On those iterations, one can relate a stationarity
criterion of interest to the stepsize parameter. In a smooth setting,
the following lemma is the first step towards establishing theoretical
guarantees of a direct-search technique.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f
be an
L
-smooth function, and consider that
Algorithm
1
is applied to the minimization of
f
.
Suppose that the
k
th iteration of Algorithm
1
is
unsuccessful. Then,
for any
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}
,

-\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k}),\bm{d}\right\rangle\leq\alpha_{k}\frac{L\|\bm{d}\|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\rho(\alpha_{k})}{\alpha_{k}}\,.

(8)

Proof.

Since iteration
k
is unsuccessful, for any
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}
, the decrease
condition is not satisfied, and thus

f(\bm{x}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bm{d})-f(\bm{x}_{k})\geq-\rho(\alpha_{k}).

(9)

Since
f
is
L
-smooth by assumption, using for example
[
9
, Lemma 9.4]
yields

f(\bm{x}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bm{d})\leq f(\bm{x}_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k}),\bm{d}\right\rangle+\frac{L\alpha_{k}^{2}}{2}\|\bm{d}\|^{2}.

Combining this inequality with (
9
) and dividing by
\alpha_{k}>0

yields the desired result.

∎

Lemma
2.1
highlights the importance of choosing a good set of polling
directions, so as to relate the left-hand side of (
8
) with
a convergence criterion. The use of positive spanning sets is particularly
suited in that regard, as it leads to the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
hold
, in particular that the
k
th iteration of Algorithm
1
is
unsuccessful
.
Suppose further that Assumption
2.3
holds, and that

\mathbb{D}_{k}
is a positive spanning set with cosine measure
\tau>0
. Then,

\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\left[\alpha_{k}\frac{Ld_{\max}}{2}+\frac{\rho(\alpha_{k})}{\alpha_{k}\,d_{\min}}\right]\,.

(10)

The result of Proposition
2.1
can be used to
guarantee convergence of the gradient norm to zero for a subsequence of
unsuccessful iterates. We
illustrate
such a result in
Section
3.1
.

In absence of smoothness, the result of Lemma
2.1
no longer holds. However,
one can still guarantee that

\frac{f(\bm{x}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bm{d})-f(\bm{x}_{k})}{\alpha_{k}}\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{\rho(\alpha_{k})}{\alpha_{k}}.

(11)

for any
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}
.

The left-hand side of (
11
) resembles the formula of the Clarke
derivative, and is used together with the notion of refining subsequence to yield
asymptotic guarantees on the Clarke directional derivatives. We define the former
concept below.

Definition 2.11
.

Consider a sequence of iterates
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, a sequence of stepsize
parameters
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence of polling sets

\{\mathbb{D}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
associated with a direct-search algorithm. Let

\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{N}
be an
infinite
index subsequence such that the
k
th iteration
is unsuccessful for every
k\in\mathcal{K}
and the subsequence

\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
converges towards
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

The subsequence
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
is called a

refining subsequence
if
\lim_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\alpha_{k}=0
, in
which case
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\hat{\bm{x}}}
is called a refined point.

•

If
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
is a refining subsequence, a
direction
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
refining direction
if
there exists an infinite subset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}\subset\mathcal{K}
and a sequence

\{\bm{d}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}}\}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}\in\mathcal{L}}
such that

\bm{d}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}}\in\mathbb{D}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}}
for any

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}\in\mathcal{L}
and

\lim_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}\tfrac{\bm{d}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}}}{\|\bm{d}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}}\|}=\tfrac{\bm{d}}{\|\bm{d}\|}
.

By reasoning on refining subsequences, one can directly establish asymptotic results on (Clarke) directional derivatives. This property is crucial in analyzing algorithms based on simple decrease such as those described in
Section
3.2
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
f
is Lipschitz continuous, and consider that
Algorithm
1
is applied to the minimization of
f
with
a forcing function that satisfies Assumption
2.4
.
Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{N}
be an
infinite
index subsequence such that

\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
is a refining subsequence with refined point

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
. Then, for any refining direction
\bm{d}
, it holds that

f^{\circ}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}};\bm{d})\geq 0\,.

(12)

Proof.

Let
\{\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}
be a sequence of directions such that

\bm{d}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{D}_{\ell}
for every
\ell\in\mathcal{L}

and
\lim_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\frac{\bm{d}_{\ell}}{\|\bm{d}_{\ell}\|}=\frac{\bm{d}}{\|\bm{d}\|}
.
Without loss of generality, suppose that all iterations in
\mathcal{L}
denote
unsuccessful iterations. Applying (
11
) at iteration

\ell\in\mathcal{L}
to the direction
\bm{d}_{\ell}
gives

\frac{f(\bm{x}_{\ell}+\alpha_{\ell}\bm{d}_{\ell})-f(\bm{x}_{\ell})}{\alpha_{\ell}}\geq-\frac{\rho(\alpha_{\ell})}{\alpha_{\ell}}.

Taking the limit on both sides as
\ell
goes to infinity, we have

\bm{d}_{\ell}\rightarrow\bm{d}^{*}
,

\alpha_{\ell}\rightarrow 0
as well as
\bm{x}_{\ell}\rightarrow{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}}
.
It follows that

f^{\circ}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}};}\,\bm{d})\geq\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(\bm{x}_{\ell}+\alpha_{\ell}\bm{d}_{\ell})-f(\bm{x}_{\ell})}{\alpha_{\ell}}\geq 0.

∎

This lemma
is
used in the subsequent analyzes of

direct-search algorithms
in the nonsmooth setting.

3
Algorithms with guarantees for unconstrained optimization

In this section, we review the main instances of Algorithm
1

that possess theoretical guarantees. We begin by reviewing directional algorithms,
arguably the simplest class of such instances. We then present a simple mesh-based
algorithm, and present its convergence guarantees in the unconstrained setting,
that follow from standard analyzes of MADS-type algorithms. Finally, we review the
theory behind line-search algorithms.

3.1
Directional algorithms

Directional direct-search methods directly follow the template of
Algorithm
1
. Those schemes differ in their choice of polling
directions, polling strategy, and forcing function. Under standard
assumptions, a directional method generally comes with global convergence
guarantees
[
65
]
. Variants with sufficient decrease are also amenable
to a complexity analysis
[
89
]
, and we stress out that sufficient
decrease is currently the only paradigm in which complexity guarantees have been
obtained.

We first review the main results for deterministic directional
direct-search methods on smooth problems in
Section
3.1.1
. We then describe the corresponding
results for probabilistic directional direct-search schemes in
Section
3.1.2
.
Finally, we cover existing guarantees for deterministic methods on nonsmooth
problems in Section
3.1.3
.

3.1.1
Deterministic convergence results in the smooth case

We begin the analysis of Algorithm
1
by focusing on the smooth
setting. In that context, the polling sets are typically required to be positive
spanning sets with positive cosine measure. We formalize this requirement through
the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1
.

The polling sets
\{\mathbb{D}_{k}\}
used in Algorithm
1
satisfy

\mathrm{cm}(\mathbb{D}_{k})\geq\tau
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\tau>0
.

A direct application of Proposition
2.1
under this
assumption yields the following convergence result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that Algorithm
1
is applied to an
L
-smooth function

f
under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.4
.
Suppose also
that the sequence
\{\mathbb{D}_{k}\}
satisfies Assumptions
2.3

and
3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a index set of unsuccessful iterations such that

\lim_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\alpha_{k}\rightarrow 0
. Then,

\lim_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|=0.

(13)

Proof.

All the assumptions made in Proposition
2.1
hold whence taking the limit when
k\to\infty,k\in\mathcal{K}
in (
10
) yields (
13
) since
\rho(\alpha_{k})/\alpha_{k}\to 0
per Assumption
2.4
.
∎

Theorem
3.1
is a weak convergence result, in that it only
guarantees that a subsequence of gradient norms is going to zero provided there
exists a subsequence of stepsizes that converges to
0
. Note that neither this
result nor that of Lemma
2.1
do leverage the use of sufficient decrease.
A first step towards improving the results of Theorem
3.1

consists
of
exploiting sufficient decrease to guarantee that the entire sequence
of stepsizes converges to

.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that Algorithm
1
is applied to an
L
-smooth function

f
under
Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
and
2.4
.
Suppose further that the algorithm uses sufficient decrease. Then,

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{k}=0
.

Combining Lemma
3.1
with Theorem
3.1
does not
guarantee that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|=0
, since the
result of Theorem
3.1
only concerns subsequences of unsuccessful
iterations.
A popular approach to obtain these results requires complete
polling to be performed
[
65
,
32
]
, which corresponds to our
assumptions in Theorem
3.2
below. Note that similar results can
also be obtained in the context of locally strongly convex
objectives
[
65
,
43
]
, or in the context of line-search
methods
[

]
, to be discussed in Section

.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let the assumptions of Lemma
3.1
hold. In addition,
suppose that Algorithm
1
is applied using
complete
polling
and a sequence of polling sets satisfying Assumption
3.1
.
Then,

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|=0.

Complexity guarantees

In addition to global convergence results, the
sufficient decrease condition also endows direct-search schemes with complexity
bounds, or non-asymptotic results, that quantify how many iterations or function
evaluations are needed in the worst case to reach an approximate optimality
criterion, such as
\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|\leq\epsilon
(for nonconvex objectives) or

f(\bm{x}_{k})-\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\bm{x})\leq\epsilon
(for convex and strongly convex
objectives).
The first bounds of this nature for direct-search schemes
were
established by Vicente
[
89
]
in the nonconvex setting, and later
extended to the convex and strongly convex setting by Dodangeh and
Vicente
[
41
]
. A unified and simplified argument was presented
in
[
68
]
, assuming however no expansion in successful steps and
a suitable initialization of the algorithm.

One thing
these results
have in common
is that they exhibit additional dependencies on
the problem dimension compared to those for derivative-based algorithms, a common
trait among derivative-free algorithms
[
28
]
.

In this survey, we focus on analyzing the nonconvex setting, and give the
corresponding result below.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
hold.
Suppose further that
\rho(\alpha)=c\,\alpha^{2}
for some
c>0
,
and that
|\mathbb{D}_{k}|\leq m
for
every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for any

\epsilon>0
,
Algorithm
1
reaches an iterate

\bm{x}_{k}
such that
\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|\leq\epsilon
in at most

\mathcal{O}\left(\tau^{-2}\epsilon^{-2}\right)\ \mbox{iterations}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{O}\left(m\tau^{-2}\epsilon^{-2}\right)\ \mbox{function evaluations},

(14)

where
\tau
is the bound on the cosine measures given by
Assumption
3.1
.

As stated, the result of Theorem
3.3
does not exhibit explicit
dependencies on the problem dimension. However, if one aims at minimizing the
complexity bounds as functions of
m
and
\tau
, it has been shown that the best
choice consists
of
selecting
\mathbb{D}_{k}
as an orthonormal basis and its negative, in
which case the results hold with
m=2\,n
and

\tau=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
(see, e.g.,
[
42
,
90
]
). As a result, the iteration
complexity bounds exhibit a dependency in
n
, while the evaluation complexity
bounds have a dependency in
n^{2}
. In the next section, we
describe

variants of directional
direct-search algorithms
that
attempt
to
reduce that dependency through randomization.

Remark 3.1
.

Under convexity assumptions on
f
, it is possible to improve the
dependency on
\epsilon
from
\epsilon^{-2}
to
\epsilon^{-1}
in
the convex case and
\log(\epsilon^{-1})
in the strongly convex
case, thereby following similar improvements in the complexity of
gradient descent techniques
[
41
]
. Note however
that the dependencies on
m
and
\tau
, which are intrinsic to the
use of a direct-search scheme, are identical to that in the
nonconvex setting.

3.1.2
Probabilistic convergence results in the smooth case

An important subclass of directional
direct-search algorithms
, that has
grown in importance over the past decade,
relies on assuming
probabilistic
properties
of the polling sets rather than relying on deterministic properties
such as Assumption
3.1
. The associated schemes
employ a
small number of
randomly chosen
directions to effectively reduce the
number of points considered at every iteration
compared to a deterministic
approach from
\mathcal{O}(1)
to
\mathcal{O}(n)
. We note that such randomized
strategies have been applied to zeroth-order algorithms that attempt to build a
gradient approximation, yielding improved theoretical guarantees compared to
deterministic variants
[
82
,
19
]
.

In the context of directional
direct-search methods
,
Gratton et al
.

[
57
]

established theoretical guarantees for randomized approaches thanks to the
notion of
probabilistic descent
, that considers the polling sets as
random objects.

Assumption 3.2
.

The random polling set sequence
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}}_{k}\}

satisfies

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{cm}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}}_{k},-\nabla f(\bm{{\hbox{\hskip 2.85764pt\hskip-2.85764pt\hbox{$x$}\hskip-2.85764pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 2.85764pt}}}_{k}))\geq\tau\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{k-1})\geq\beta\qquad\mbox{for all}\ k,}

(15)

where
\tau>0
,
\beta\in(0,1)
,
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
is the
\sigma
-algebra
generated by
\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{0},\dots,\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{k-1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{-1}=\emptyset
.

Note that Assumption
3.2
is clearly satisfied when
Assumption
3.1
holds, i.e., when the polling sets are positive
spanning sets.
We note that since the objective function is deterministic and
\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{0},\dots,\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{k-1}
are
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
-measurable by construction, then
\nabla f(\bm{{\hbox{\hskip 2.85764pt\hskip-2.85764pt\hbox{$x$}\hskip-2.85764pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 2.85764pt}}}_{k})
is also
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
-measurable and hence deterministic with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
since its distribution before the construction of
\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{k}
is only determined by
\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{0},\dots,\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{k-1}
. When the
\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{i},i=0,1,\dots,k

\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{k}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
so that for all

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{cm}(\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{k},\bm{v})\geq\tau\right)\geq\beta

is enough to satisfy (

\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{i}

) is provided by
[

, Proposition B.3]
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, one can derive a high-probability function
evaluation complexity
for Algorithm

, that implies
almost-sure global convergence of the algorithmic process. Note that expected
value results can also be obtained by that process
[

]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that Algorithm
1
is applied to an
L
-smooth
function
f
with
\rho(\alpha)=c\,\alpha^{2}
for some

and

\gamma=\theta^{-1}
. Suppose that Assumption

holds
for all realizations of
\{\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{k}\}

3.2

holds with

and
|\hbox{\hskip 4.27847pt\hskip-4.27847pt\hbox{$\mathbb{D}$}\hskip-4.27847pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.27847pt}_{k}|\leq m

.

\epsilon>0

reaches an

.

When every

and

. The complexity bounds, that are
probabilistic, improve over the deterministic result of

by a factor of

.

Similar
results were also established for random search schemes in Bergou
et al.
[
19
]
, in which a stepsize dependent on the
Lipschitz constant of the gradient
was
used. Direct-search methods
employing a random direction and its negative at every iteration can be viewed
as adaptive versions of those schemes, that maintain an estimate of the
Lipschitz constant of the gradient through the stepsize parameter. The analysis
of
direct-search methods
using probabilistic descent directions was recently extended by
Roberts and Royer
[
87
]
to allow for unbounded sets of
descent directions, highlighting the connection with the theory of random
matrices. In that work, each descent direction is defined through combinations
of columns of a random matrix
{\hbox{\hskip 4.41405pt\hskip-4.41405pt\hbox{$\bm{P}$}\hskip-4.41405pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.41405pt}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, that
represents a subspace of possible directions at iteration

.
The complexity result from Gratton et al.
[
57
]
is recovered, with an additional dependence on the probabilistic bounds for the extreme singular values of
{\hbox{\hskip 4.41405pt\hskip-4.41405pt\hbox{$\bm{P}$}\hskip-4.41405pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{4.30554pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.41405pt}}_{k}
.

Such probabilistic bounds are non trivial to compute and have been
the subject of recent studies
[
48
]
.

3.1.3
Deterministic convergence results in the nonsmooth case

We now turn to the nonsmooth setting, where we merely assume that
f
is
Lipschitz continuous. Convergence results for
deterministic
methods based on
sufficient decrease require asymptotic density of the set of directions.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose that Algorithm
1
is applied to a Lipschitz
continuous function
f
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be a sequence of unsuccessful steps such
that
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
is a refining subsequence converging to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
.
If the corresponding set of refining directions is dense in the unit sphere,
then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
is a Clarke stationary point.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
, for any refining direction
\bm{d}
associated
with
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
, we have
f^{\circ}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}};\bm{d})\geq 0
. Since the
set of refining directions is dense in the unit sphere, for any nonzero
vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a sequence of refining directions

\{\bm{d}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\bm{d}_{i}\rightarrow\tfrac{\bm{u}}{\|\bm{u}\|}

as
i\rightarrow\infty
. As a result, we obtain in the limit that

f^{\circ}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}};\tfrac{\bm{u}}{\|\bm{u}\|}\right)\geq 0
. Since the Clarke
directional derivative is positively homogeneous

[
31
, Proposition 2.1.1]
, we also have

f^{\circ}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}};\bm{u})\geq 0
, from which the desired conclusion follows.
∎

Under additional assumptions, one can obtain a stronger result akin to
Theorem
3.2
for nonsmooth objectives. While this result follows
from well established techniques, we are not aware of any existing reference in
the literature.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
3.5
hold. Suppose further that
Assumptions
2.1
,
2.4
and
2.3
hold.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\hat{\mathbb{D}}_{k}={\left\{{\bm{d}}/{\|\bm{d}\|}\ |\ \bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}\right\}}
.
If
\cup_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{\mathbb{D}}_{k}
is dense in the unit sphere for every
infinite subsequence
\mathcal{K}
, then every limit point of

\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is Clarke stationary.

Unlike in the smooth setting, complexity bounds for
direct-search algorithms

remain rather unexplored in the nonsmooth setting. Garmanjani and
Vicente
[
53
]
provided complexity results for a
special class of nonsmooth
objectives that can be approximated by a family
of smooth functions

By design, a mesh-based algorithm aims at generating an asymptotically dense

Non-asymptotic results, such as complexity results or convergence rates, have

4
Handling noisy and stochastic values

This section focuses on direct-search methods for unconstrained optimization
problems where the objective function cannot be evaluated exactly due to noise in
the calculation. Standard direct-search methods such as those described in

certain magnitude associated with the stepsize
[

modifying the basic direct-search framework to establish
(probabilistic) convergence guarantees.
Our goal in this section is to provide the reader with key approaches
in this very active area of research.

\|\nabla f(\hbox{\hskip 3.29514pt\hskip-3.29514pt\hbox{$\bm{x}$}\hskip-3.29514pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.29514pt}_{k})\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}}{\alpha_{\min}}+\alpha_{\min}\right)

after at most
\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{\min}^{-2})
iterations.
By setting

\alpha_{\min}=\mathcal{O}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}^{1/2})
, one guarantees a gradient accuracy of
order
\mathcal{O}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}^{1/2})
in
\mathcal{O}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}^{-1})
iterations. In the nonsmooth
case, the method converges asymptotically to a

(\alpha_{\min},\mathcal{O}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}/\alpha_{\min}+\alpha_{\min}))
-Goldstein
stationary point in the sense of Definition
2.10
.
Setting again
\alpha_{\min}=\mathcal{O}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}^{1/2})
guarantees convergence to

({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}}))
-Goldstein stationary points.

Note that the noise is stochastic in nature, but the uniform bound is deterministic. As a result, the aforementioned complexity results hold for every realization of the method.

Finally, we mention another approach that has been used to deal with
controlled noise in stochastic optimization. Berahas et
al.
[
17
,
18
]
proposed line-search
methods based on a relaxed Armijo condition that explicitly involves the noise
level value. In a smooth setting, these methods can converge to an approximate
stationary point with gradient norm of order
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}})
where

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}>0
is the noise level. Although these techniques typically employ
a gradient approximation, a similar approach could be applied in direct
search by using a forcing function of the form

\rho(\underline{\alpha}-\mathcal{O}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{f}}))
.

4.2
Direct-search methods
for general random noise

In this section, we consider more general
forms of noise on function values
that are not necessarily bounded. A typical formulation of the problem in that
setting is

\underset{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\min}f(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hbox{\hskip 1.81944pt\hskip-1.81944pt\hbox{$\zeta$}\hskip-1.81944pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.3611pt}{\hbox{\rule{2.41112pt}{0.22603pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 1.81944pt}}}\left[f(\bm{x},\hbox{\hskip 2.1875pt\hskip-2.1875pt\hbox{$\zeta$}\hskip-2.1875pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.94444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 2.1875pt})\right],

(20)

and we again assume that the algorithm can only evaluate noisy
function values of the form
f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},\hbox{\hskip 2.1875pt\hskip-2.1875pt\hbox{$\zeta$}\hskip-2.1875pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.94444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 2.1875pt})

Problem (

derivative-free optimization community beyond the direct-search paradigm.
Under smoothness assumptions on

and

line-search
techniques that employ a stochastic estimation of the gradient

been investigated

“step search”, modify the search direction along with the stepsize, thus
bringing them closer to direct-search methods in spirit

]
.
Extensions of the direct-search schemes reviewed in Section

Audet et al.

At every iteration, this method uses estimates

One drawback of using such estimates is that the simple decrease condition

Sample complexity

Sample complexity refers to bounding the number of evaluations of the noisy
function estimates (also called samples) required to achieve a desired convergence
level. To the best of our knowledge, there are no bounds available on the number
of samples required to achieve a given accuracy for direct-search methods with
adaptive sampling, despite recent developments in the general stochastic
optimization setting
[
63
]
. On the other hand, bounds on the
number of samples per iteration have been obtained in the direct-search
literature. A first bound of that nature was given by Audet et
al.
[
13
]
, where estimates (
23
) required

\mathcal{O}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underline{\alpha}_{k}}}^{-4})
samples in order to obtain convergence
guarantees. A similar bound in
\mathcal{O}(\underline{\alpha}_{k}^{-4})
can be showed
for SDDS
[
46
]
. An improvement to
\mathcal{O}(\underline{\alpha}_{k}^{-2q})

samples per iteration was proved by Rinaldi et al.
[
86
]

based on using a forcing function of the form
\rho(\alpha)=c\,\alpha^{q}
with

c>0
. Assuming a common random number generator can be used to sample the
function estimates, and that

Another notable result is the

[

fewer samples per iteration in cases where the sufficient decrease condition is
satisfied or violated with a certain margin.

5
Handling constraints

In this section, we consider direct-search methods designed for
constrained

optimization problems of the form

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\bm{x})\qquad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega,

(25)

where
the feasible set
\Omega
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that encodes constraints
on the problem variables
.
As in the rest of the paper, information about the objective function is only available through function evaluations. As for the constraint set, access to derivative information is common for simple constraints, but is not necessary to design a
direct-search scheme. In fact, constraints in derivative-free information are subject to a careful
classification
, according to the information they
provide regarding feasibility
or lack thereof

[
71
]
. In the
context of this survey, we
classify a constraint
as
relaxable
or

unrelaxable
depending on whether
the objective function and all other constraints can be queried at points that do not satisfy the first constraint.
We
also

distinguish

quantifiable
and
non-quantifiable
constraints: the former
corresponds to constraints that produce a numerical value, while the latter

can be viewed as a binary certificate of constraint satisfaction.

The first part of this section is concerned with feasible direct-search
methods, that aim at preserving feasibility of the iterates throughout the
optimization process. Section
5.1
surveys theoretical guarantees
that have been obtained in the presence of linear constraints, manifold
constraints or non-relaxable constraints. We then move to infeasible
direct-search methods, for which feasibility can only be guaranteed
asymptotically, in Section
5.2
.

5.1.1
Linear constraints

Linear constraints, and in particular bound constraints, are the simplest kind
of constraints in optimization. In direct-search techniques, linear
constraints can be easily handled by a careful choice of polling
directions
[
65
]
. We present below the main tools and results for
this purpose in the context of directional direct-search schemes, then
discuss related results for mesh-based and line-search techniques.

Suppose that the feasible set
\Omega
of Problem (
25
) is
polyhedral and described by linear inequalities, i.e.
,

\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \bm{A}\bm{x}\leq\bm{b}\}

(26)

for
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times n}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
that the rows of

are
unit vectors. Convergence results for Algorithm

in that
setting aim at proving that the quantity
\chi(\bm{x}_{k})
goes to zero, where

\forall\bm{x}\in\Omega,\chi(\bm{x})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}+\bm{d}\in\Omega\\
\|\bm{d}\|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left\langle\bm{d},[-\nabla f(\bm{x})]\right\rangle.

(27)

The measure
\chi(\bm{x})
is always nonnegative, and equals zero if and only if

\bm{x}
is a first-order KKT stationary point of Problem
[
65
]
.

The analysis of
direct-search methods
in that setting relies heavily
on the notion of approximate tangent and normal cones
,that involve
both a feasible point and a tentative stepsize
.

Definition 5.1
.

Given
\bm{x}\in\Omega
and
\alpha>0
, the
approximate normal cone

is defined by

N_{\Omega}(\bm{x},\alpha)=\mbox{pspan}\left(\left\{\bm{a}_{i}\ |\ b_{i}-\left\langle\bm{a}_{i},\bm{x}\right\rangle\leq\alpha\right\}\right),

where
\mbox{pspan}(\mathcal{A})
denotes the set of nonnegative
linear combinations of vectors in
\mathcal{A}
.
The
approximate normal cone
is then defined as

T_{\Omega}(\bm{x},\alpha)=N_{\Omega}(\bm{x},\alpha)^{\circ}
.

It follows from
Definition
5.1
that a displacement of length
\alpha
along any direction in the
approximate tangent cone preserves feasibility, while the normal cone is generated by normals corresponding to constraints that can be violated with such a displacement.
A natural strategy for defining
polling directions in Algorithm
6
then consists
of
using
generators of the approximate tangent cone at the current
iterate
[
66
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Indeed, the cosine measure defined in Section
2
can be
generalized to the linearly constrained setting
[
65
,
58
]
as follows.

Definition 5.2
.

Given
\bm{x}\in\Omega
and
\alpha>0
, let

denote the orthogonal projection onto
the approximate tangent cone

. Given
\bm{x}\in\Omega
,
\alpha>0
, and a direction set
\mathbb{D}

\textnormal{cm}_{T_{\Omega}(\bm{x},\alpha)}(\mathbb{D})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\|\pi_{T_{\Omega}(\bm{x},\alpha)}[\bm{v}]\|\neq 0\end{subarray}}\max_{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}}\frac{\left\langle\bm{d},\bm{v}\right\rangle}{\|\bm{d}\|\|\bm{v}\|}.

(28)

When
\mathbb{D}
contains generators of

T(\bm{x},\alpha)
, it can be shown that this quantity is
positive
[
66
]
, and thus such directions
guarantee feasible descent, i.e. preserve feasibility and yield descent from

\bm{x}
with a stepsize
\alpha
as long as

\pi_{T_{\Omega}(\bm{x},\alpha)}\left[-\nabla f(\bm{x})\right]>0
. Although the latter
quantity is not a good measure of stationarity, it bears a natural connection
with the measure

Lemma 5.1
.

(

Note that

Theorem 5.1
.

(

Suppose that

Although the bounds can be shown to hold for finite values of

and positive values of

Although the random polling sets to be used must be restricted to
preserve feasibility, it is not clear that the complexity improves

linear equality constraints. Nevertheless, by decomposing all tangent cones as the
sum of pointed cones and subspaces, random directions can be drawn in the former,
leading to a possible improvement of the number of directions used at every
iteration.

We note that linear constraints have also been investigated in the context of
mesh-based algorithms, with more emphasis on handling linear inequality constraints
and bounds to obtain convergence results

,
85
]
.
Although those result apply in both a smooth and a nonsmooth setting, no
complexity guarantees are available.

Furthermore, in a line-search setting, we note that the feasible direction
framework based on approximate tangent cones was used in the context of bound
constraints in Lucidi and Sciandrone
[

to establish convergence
guarantees. The idea was then extended to general algebraic unrelaxable smooth
constraints through the use of projections, leading again to global convergence
guarantees
[

]
.

Finally, Cristofari and Rinaldi

feasible direct-search methods for problems with structured feasible set (i.e.,

with

that use directions pointing towards/away from the vertices of the feasible set, and are somehow related to
zeroth-order
Frank–Wolfe methods ( see, e.g.,
[

and references therein for further details on the Frank–Wolfe method).

5.1.2
Riemannian
direct-search
schemes

In this section, we discuss an emerging trend in feasible methods
related to Riemannian optimization, a well-established subfield of nonlinear
optimization
that is concerned with finding the best value of a function over a smooth manifold

[
25
]
.
Interestingly,
the idea of extending direct-search methods to the Riemannian setting started
early in the development of numerical Riemannian optimization.
The first
direct-search proposal for Riemannian optimization problems is due to
Dreisigmeyer
[
44
]
, and consists
of
an extension of

mesh-based algorithm
to Riemannian manifolds.

The special case of reductive homogeneous spaces, that includes several
matrix manifolds, is discussed in a follow-up work, where both deterministic
and probabilistic directional methods are
proposed
[
45
]
. Those references focus on properly
defining the algorithms rather than on establishing convergence guarantees.

More recently, Kungurtsev et al.
[
69
]
describe
basic direct-search schemes of directional and line-search types for minimizing
smooth and nonsmooth objectives on Riemannian manifolds. Global convergence
properties are established, that match those of the unconstrained setting.

More precisely, counterparts to
Theorem
3.2
and
3.5
are established for
directional variants on smooth and Lipschitz continuous objectives,
respectively, while counterparts to Theorem
3.11
and

3.12
are obtained for variants using line-search extrapolation.
We note, however, that complexity results have yet to be derived
for Riemannian direct-search schemes.

5.1.3
Extreme barrier

The extreme barrier approach is
arguably
the most general way to handle
constraints in direct-search methods so as to enforce feasibility. It consists
of

setting the objective equal to
+\infty
outside the feasible set. The first proof
of convergence (to Clarke stationary points) of an extreme barrier approach was
done for mesh adaptive
direct-search algorithms

[
7
]
.
Consequently, most of
the theory in Section

extends to the case of a constrained
set handled through extreme barrier. We present below the main ingredients to
derive convergence results in the context of Algorithm

below, but
note that similar arguments can be applied to any direct-search method with a
dense set of poll directions.

Akin to Section

cones that characterize stationarity.

5.2
Infeasible methods

This section is concerned with direct-search schemes that allow for evaluating the
objective outside of the feasible set. Such methods are termed
infeasible
,
and apply to problems with quantitative relaxable constraints of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\bm{x})\qquad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\bm{g}(\bm{x})\leq\mathbf{0}\,,

(32)

where
\bm{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{l}
. Thanks to the quantitative
nature of the constraints, it is possible to define an infeasibility measure

h(\cdot)
that quantifies the distance to infeasibility. Typical choices for
such measures are

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\max\{0,g_{i}(\bm{x})\},

(33)

which is typically nonsmooth,
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\max\{0,g_{i}(\bm{x})^{2}\}

(34)

which is a smooth penalty function
for smooth functions
g_{i}
.

The theory for such
progressive barrier
functions was developed
in Audet and Dennis
[
8
]
. A two-phase approach for
solving Problem (
32
) using
h
consists of applying a
suitable optimization method to the problem

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(\bm{x})

(35)

in order to find a feasible point. Once such a point is obtained, the original Problem (
32
) can be tackled using the extreme barrier approach of Section
5.1.3
.

When the derivatives of
\bm{g}
are not available, one may consider
using Algorithm
1
or any of the methods discussed in
Section
3
in the first phase. To this end, the sufficient
decrease condition (
2
) must be modified so as to accept
any feasible point that is encountered by the algorithm. However, note that the
local nature of direct-search methods only guarantees convergence to a
stationary point of the barrier function
h
. Still, provided the iterates
converge towards the feasible set, a feasible point can be found in
finitely many iterations. In that case, convergence results follow from that
of the extreme barrier approach, and can be obtained for both progressive
barrier functions (
33
) and (
34
).

Since the two-phase approach ultimately reduces to an extreme barrier
approach, it suffers from the same shortcoming in that information at
infeasible points eventually stops being used. The next three sections describe
algorithms that exploit infeasibility information.

Note that these variants are inspired by derivative-based constrained optimization techniques and, as such, possess the same advantages and drawbacks that their derivative-based counterparts. Thus, rather than formally stating their convergence results, we provide a summary of the key guarantees achievable by each algorithmic strategy, drawing from the main definitions and vocabulary of Section
5.1
.

5.2.1
Filter-based methods

A filter approach consists of a biobjective view of the constrained
optimization Problem (
32

original function
f
and the progressive barrier
h

a set of “incumbent” points

With this definition, the set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{S}}_{k}
may consist of both feasible and infeasible

. Otherwise, one checks whether an infeasible
point with improved barrier value can be found, even if it increases
the objective value, and such a point is added to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{S}}_{k}
if it exists. As
a result, the method tries to improve both objectives or puts the
emphasis on constraint violation.

In a mesh-based setting, Audet and Dennis
[
8
]
show
convergence guarantees for refining sequences of the algorithm. If such a sequence
consists of feasible points, convergence to a (Clarke) stationary point can be
established under density of the refining directions. If the sequence consists of
infeasible points, only convergence to a stationary point for
Problem (
35
) is guaranteed.

5.2.2
Restoration-based
methods

Restoration-based approaches rely on alternating steps that improve the
objective with so-called restoration steps, which focus on improving the measure
of infeasibility
h
. The approach of Bueno et al.
[
27
]
handles
differentiable constraints, and alternates a gradient-based restoration phase with
direct-search steps on a problem with linearized versions of the constraints. In
this smooth setting, convergence to a KKT point for the original
Problem (
32
) can be established.

The method of Gratton and Vicente
[
56
]
considers two
conditions to accept a point at iteration
k
, namely

h(\bm{x}_{k})-h(\bm{x}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bm{d}_{k})>\rho(\alpha_{k})\quad\text{and}\quad h(\bm{x}_{k})>C\rho(\alpha_{k})

(36)

for some
C>1
, and

M(\bm{x}_{k},\mu)-M(\bm{x}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bm{d}_{k},\mu)>\rho(\alpha_{k})

(37)

for some
\mu>0
,
where
M(\bm{x},\mu)=f(\bm{x})+\mu h(\bm{x})
is referred to
as a merit function
. Within the restoration phase, only (
36
) is
considered
to accept a new point
.
Convergence results for this
algorithm are established using the nonsmooth barrier (
33
). For
nonsmooth objective, stationarity of refining points is established with a similar
distinction than in the filter-based setting of Audet and
Dennis
[
8
]
. In addition, results are specialized to the
case of smooth objectives, thereby avoiding to require density of the refining
directions.

5.2.3
Penalty approaches

In penalty based approaches, a direct-search scheme is applied to an auxiliary
objective, that combines a measure of infeasibility with the objective function.

The augmented Lagrangian technique proposed by Lewis and
Torczon
[
72
]
handles
nonlinear relaxable equality constraints
as well as bounds on the objective. This technique was later extended to handle
linear constraints in addition to bound constraints
[
67
]
.

In both cases, a directional method is used to solve the augmented Lagrangian
subproblems.
The underlying theoretical guarantees rely on using the stepsize as a
stopping criterion for solving the subproblems.

In another line of work, Liuzzi et al.
[
75
]

proposes

an exact penalty approach to reformulate problem constrained with nonlinear,
relaxable inequalities. The resulting penalized objective is defined on an open
set, and tackled with a line-search method akin to those described in
Section
3.3
. Liuzzi et al.
[
76
]

considers
a
sequential penalty approach for problems with smooth objective, inequality
constraints and bound constraints. In this approach, the inequality constraints
other than bounds are relaxed and a line-search method is applied to minimize

f(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}(\bm{x})
under bound constraints, where

h
is the smooth infeasibility measure (
34
)
and

\varepsilon>0

is decreased based on a condition involving an upper bound
on the stepsizes
. The case of nonsmooth relaxable constraints combined with bounds
was addressed by Fasano et al.
[
50
]
. Under constraint
qualification and for small enough
\varepsilon
, it was then shown that all the
stationary points corresponding to minimizing

f(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}(\bm{x})

with
h
defined by (
33
)
are also (Clarke)
stationary for the original problem.

6
Multiobjective direct-search methods

In this section, we survey the main developments in extending
direct-search schemes
to Multiobjective optimization (MOO) problems.
The theory of multiobjective continuous
optimization has undergone significant development since the 2000s, allowing
for further development in the context of direct-search methods. Moreover,
multiobjective problems are ubiquitous in engineering problems, a primary
source of applications for direct-search methods.

Directional methods were extended to the multiobjective setting by
Custódio et al.
[
36
]
, along with convergence to a
stationary point in the sense of Pareto. More recently, complexity bounds
were derived for a directional direct-search
scheme
[
37
]
. Mesh-based algorithms were considered
by Audet et al.
[
10
,
12
]
, the
bi-objective version being successfully implemented in the NOMAD
software
[
15
]
. Finally, line-search techniques are amenable to
multiobjective extension. Liuzzi et al.
[
77
]
propose
an analysis based on new optimality conditions.

We consider MOO problems of the form

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(\bm{x})\equiv(f_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,f_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}}(\bm{x}))^{\top},\

(38)

where
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},
with
i=1,\dots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}
. In this context the concept of Pareto dominance is used to compare two points.

Definition 6.1

(Pareto dominance)
.

Given two points
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
\bm{x}
dominates
\bm{y}
, when
F(\bm{x})\leq F(\bm{y})
, i.e.,
f_{i}(\bm{x})\leq f_{i}(\bm{y})
for all
i=1,\dots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F(\bm{x})}\neq F(\bm{y})
.

A point
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
is then a local Pareto minimizer if there exists no point
\bm{y}\in{\cal B}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}},\delta)
s.t.
F(\bm{y})\leq F({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}})
, for some
\delta>0
. In both the nonsmooth and the smooth settings, one can define necessary optimality conditions as follows.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
F
be Lipschitz continuous near
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
. We say that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
is a Pareto-Clarke stationary point if

\forall\ \bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\exists\ i_{d}\in[1:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}]:\ f_{i_{d}}^{\circ}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}};\bm{d})\geq 0.

(39)

Definition 6.3
.

Let
F
be continuously differentiable at
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
. We say that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
is Pareto stationary, if

\forall\ \bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\exists\ i_{d}\in[1:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}]:\ \left\langle\nabla f_{i_{d}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}),\bm{d}\right\rangle\geq 0.

(40)

It follows that the quantity

\mu(\bm{x}):=-\min_{\|\bm{d}\|\leq 1}\max_{i\in[1:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}]}\left\langle\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}),\bm{d}\right\rangle.

(41)

can be used to characterize Pareto stationarity.
In our context, a finite set of polling directions is however used at each iteration. An approximation of
\mu(\bm{x})
is hence often used in the analysis to take into account this aspect
[
37
]
:

\mu_{\mathbb{D}_{k}}(\bm{x}):=-\min_{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{k},\|\bm{d}\|\leq 1}\max_{i\in[1:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}]}\left\langle\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}),\bm{d}\right\rangle.

(42)

We here focus on the Direct Multisearch (DMS) algorithm proposed in
[
36
]
. This approach,
inspired by the search/poll paradigm of direct-search methods of directional type, does not aggregate any of the objective functions but rather handles a list
L_{k}
of nondominated points (from which the new iterates are
chosen). The aim of DMS is to generate as many points in the Pareto front as possible from the polling
procedure itself, while keeping the whole framework general enough to accommodate other
strategies from the literature. DMS hence generalizes to multiobjective optimization (MOO) all direct-search methods of directional type. We report a simplified version of DMS in Algorithm
8
. It uses the strict partial order induced by the cone
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
. Following
[
37
]
, we indicate with
D(L)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
the image of the set points dominated by a list of points
L
and
D(L;a)
the image related to those set of points with an
\ell_{\infty}
-norm distance to
D(L)
no larger than
a
. An iteration is successful in Algorithm
8
when there are modifications in the list of nondominated points, that is a new point was accepted, such that
F(\bm{x})\notin D(L,\rho(\alpha))
.
It is crucial to emphasize that, when dealing with MOO problems, the cone of descent directions related to all components of the objective
F
can become arbitrarily narrow. We hence always need to consider density of the search directions at a given limit point
[
36
,

]
to guarantee convergence.

Convergence results can be established under the following assumptions, that generalize those made in single-objective optimization.

Assumption 6.1
.

For all
i\in[1:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}]
, the function
f_{i}
is continuously differentiable with Lipschitz continuous gradient with constant
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{i}}
. Set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{\text{max}}}=\max_{i\in[1:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}]}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{i}}
.

Assumption 6.2
.

For all
i\in[1:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}]
, the function
f_{i}
is lower and upper bounded in
\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:F(\bm{x})\notin D(\{\bm{x}_{0}\})\}
, with lower bound
f_{i}^{\min}
and upper bound
f_{i}^{\max}
. Let

F^{\min}:=\min\{f_{1}^{\min},\dots,f_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}}^{\min}\}\quad\text{and}\quad F^{\max}:=\max\{f_{1}^{\max},\dots,f_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}}^{\max}\}.

Assumption 6.3
.

The set
\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:F(\bm{x})\notin D(\{\bm{x}_{0}\})\}
is compact.

We further assume, without any loss of generality that all the positive spanning sets considered have normalized directions. Similarly to the analysis carried out in the single-objective case, we now report a result that connects at each iteration
k
the approximate stationarity measure
\mu_{\mathbb{D}_{k}}(\bm{x}_{k})
and the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
, whose proof is given in
[
37
]
:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let Assumption
6.1
hold. Consider that
Algorithm
8
is applied to the minimization of
F
, with
\mathbb{D}_{k}
positive spanning set.
Suppose that the
k
th iteration of Algorithm
8
is
unsuccessful. Then,

\mu_{\mathbb{D}_{k}}(\bm{x}_{k})\leq\alpha_{k}\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{\text{max}}}}{2}+\frac{\rho(\alpha_{k})}{\alpha_{k}}\,.

(43)

The following assumption, which connects the exact and approximate stationarity measure, is also needed in the analysis.

Assumption 6.4
.

There exists
C_{1}>0
such that

|\mu_{\mathbb{D}_{k}}(\bm{x}_{k})-\mu(\bm{x}_{k})|\leq C_{1}\mu_{\mathbb{D}_{k}}(\bm{x}_{k}),\quad\forall\ k\geq 0.

A direct application of Proposition
6.1
under this assumption yields the following convergence result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose that Algorithm
8
is applied to
F
under Assumptions
6.1
,
6.2
and
6.4
. Suppose also
that the sequence
\{\mathbb{D}_{k}\}
is such that
\mathbb{D}_{k}
is a positive spanning set for all
k\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be a index set of unsuccessful iterations such that

\lim_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\alpha_{k}\rightarrow 0
. Then,

\lim_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\mu(\bm{x}_{k})=0.

(44)

As noticed in
[
37
]
, Assumption
6.3
and the use of sufficient decrease to accept new nondominated points guarantee the existence of a refining subsequence
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{\mathcal{K}}
such that
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{\mathcal{K}}
converges to zero (see Definition
2.11
). We would also like to highlight that Assumption
6.4
basically requires nonnegativity of the approximate measure
\mu_{\mathbb{D}_{k}}(\bm{x}_{k})
at each
k
. This can only be obtained if a rich enough set
\mathbb{D}_{k}
of poll directions is available.

We now state the main convergence result for the basic DMS algorithmic scheme we reported above. Similarly to what we have seen in the single-objective case, we consider refining subsequences and assume density in the unit sphere for the related refining directions. The proof is directly derived from
[
36
, Theorem 4.9]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Suppose that Algorithm
8
is applied to a Lipschitz
continuous function
F
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be a sequence of unsuccessful steps such
that
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
is a refining subsequence converging to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
.
If the corresponding set of refining directions is dense in the unit sphere,
then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
is a Pareto-Clarke stationary point. If in addition
F
is continuously differentiable, then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\bm{x}}}
is a Pareto stationary point.

It is important to note that the density of the refining directions is also needed in the smooth case. As mentioned before, this is obviously due to the fact that the cone of descent directions for the components of the function
F
can become arbitrarily narrow.

In addition to guaranteeing global convergence, the
sufficient decrease condition also provides DMS with complexity
bounds that quantify how many iterations or function evaluations are needed in the worst case scenario to reach an approximate optimality
criterion, such as
\mu(\bm{x}_{k})\leq\epsilon
. This is once again comparable to what we observed in the single-objective case, though we note that the analysis is somewhat more involved. Indeed, it is based on
linked sequences, i.e., sequences of pairs
(\bm{x}_{j_{k}},\alpha_{j_{k}})
, such that for any
k=1,2,\dots
the pair
(\bm{x}_{j_{k}},\alpha_{j_{k}})\in L_{k}
is generated at iteration
k-1
of Algorithm
8
by the pair

(\bm{x}_{j_{k-1}},\alpha_{j_{k-1}})\in L_{k-1}
.

Theorem 6.3

(
[
37
]
)
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
6.1
hold.
Suppose further that
\rho(\alpha)=c\,\alpha^{2}
for some
c>0
,
and that
|\mathbb{D}_{k}|\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}
for
every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for any

\epsilon>0
, Algorithm
8
reaches an iterate

\bm{x}_{k}
such that
\mu(\bm{x}_{k})\leq\epsilon
in at most

\mathcal{O}\left(|L(\epsilon)|\epsilon^{-2m}\right)\ \mbox{iterations}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{O}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}|L(\epsilon)|\epsilon^{-2m}\right)\ \mbox{function evaluations},

(45)

where
L(\epsilon)
is the set of linked sequences between the first unsuccessful iteration and the iteration before the one that satisfies the criticality condition.

The reported bounds do not match the
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-2}\right)
complexity bound that the gradient method for MOO problems has (see
[
51
]
for further details). The main reason behind this difference is that an iteration is declared successful in DMS when a sufficient decrease is guaranteed for at least one component of
F
, while in the gradient method the iterate is updated when a suitable decrease is obtained with respect to all components. The dependence on the term
|L(\epsilon)|
is due instead to the fact that DMS tries to approximate the whole Pareto front using the list
L_{k}
, while the gradient method in
[
51
]
only finds one Pareto stationary point.

As a final remark, we would like to highlight that for the case of (strongly) convex optimization problems, i.e., where all components of
F
are (strongly) convex, a complexity analysis should ideally expand upon the findings presented in
[
41
]
, which focus on the single-objective scenario. However, it still remains as an open question whether similar complexity bounds to those derived in
[
41
]
could be established for the multiobjective case.

The handling of constraints in a MOO problem was already addressed in
[
10
,
12
,
36
]
, where an extreme barrier approach was used for this purpose. An exact penalty approach was analyzed in
[
77
]
for handling general constraints in a linesearch-based algorithmic framework. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no existing work provides a detailed account of a Direct-Search algorithm for stochastic MOO problems.

7
Conclusion

Since their formal introduction more than 60 years ago, direct-search
algorithms have been endowed with a rich convergence theory.
In addition
to possessing convergence and complexity guarantees in the smooth,
unconstrained setting,
direct-search methods can also be designed to cope
with nonsmoothness,
noisy function values, constraints of various types
as well as multiple objectives.
In this survey, we provided key results

as well as pointers to the literature
that we hope

have convinced
the readers of the strong theoretical foundations upon
which modern direct-search algorithms are built.

Our presentation has highlighted several
lines of research that will in
our opinion shape
the future of direct-search techniques. Recent advances in
analyzing randomized direct-search techniques pave the way for scaling up
those algorithms, possibly through combination with other tools from
high-dimensional statistics.
Modern constrained optimization techniques
such as manifold theory provide principled ways of handling common
constraints, and may lead to novel analyzes departing from the use of
standard nonlinear programming tools.

Tackling noise in function values is likely to keep growing
in importance to address problems involving massive amounts of data samples
such as hyperparameter tuning, while much remains to be done in
obtaining a comprehensive, probabilistic analysis of direct-search algorithms in that setting.

Overall, we believe that these three
research directions are
likely to form the basis of next-generation direct-search methods.
