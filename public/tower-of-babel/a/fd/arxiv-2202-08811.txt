1.
Introduction

Let
\mathrm{O}(V)
be the full orthogonal group corresponding to a non-degenerate symmetric form on a finite-dimensional vector space
V
over a field of characteristic which is not 2. It is a result of M. Wonenburger
[
22
]
that every element of
\mathrm{O}(V)
is the product of two involutions from
\mathrm{O}(V)
. This is equivalent to the fact that every element of
\mathrm{O}(V)
may be conjugated to its inverse by an involution in
\mathrm{O}(V)
. That is, every conjugacy class of the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(V)
is
strongly real
. For certain subgroups of orthogonal groups, this property is further explored by F. Knüppel and G. Thomsen
[
13
]
, who classify when the commutator subgroup
\Omega(V)
of
\mathrm{O}(V)
, and the kernel of the spinor norm on
\mathrm{O}(V)
, are strongly real.

If
\mathrm{O}(V)
is now the orthogonal group corresponding to a non-defective quadratic form on
V
over a field which is characteristic 2, it was shown by E. Ellers and W. Nolte
[
3
]
and R. Gow
[
6
]
that
\mathrm{O}(V)
is also strongly real. In the case that
V
has dimension divisible by
4
and
V
is defined over a finite field of characteristic
2
, it is proven by J. Rämö
[
14
]
that the commutator subgroup
\Omega(V)
of
\mathrm{O}(V)
is strongly real. This is not the case if the dimension of
V
is 2 modulo
4
, by the work of P. H. Tiep and A. E. Zalesski
[
17
]
.

For an element (or conjugacy class) of a group to be strongly real, it is necessary that it is
real
, or conjugate to its inverse within the group. In the representation theory of finite groups, it is of interest to understand which conjugacy classes of a group are real, because the number of real conjugacy classes of a finite group is equal to the number of real-valued irreducible complex characters of that group. The classification of finite simple groups with the property that all elements are real is completed by P. H. Tiep and A. E. Zalesski
[
17
]
. The classification of finite simple groups such that all elements are strongly real is completed by J. Rämö
[
14
]
, and E. P. Vdovin and A. A. Gal
′
t
[
18
]
, with the somewhat surprising result that all elements of a finite simple group are real if and only if they are all strongly real.

In this paper, we focus on the finite simple orthogonal groups, and the question of when all real elements are strongly real. By the results mentioned above, the answer to this question is understood in the case when all elements are real, and so we study the remaining cases. We give a complete answer to the question in the case that the underlying vector space has dimension which is 2 modulo 4.

The connection between the strongly real classes of a finite group and the irreducible complex characters of that group is still mysterious in the general case, and one motivation for our study is to further understand this connection in the case of finite simple orthogonal groups. In particular, in some classes of finite groups there is a connection between strongly real classes and the irreducible complex characters which are afforded by a real representation. For example, it is proven in
[
20
]
that for a finite simple group, all conjugacy classes are strongly real if and only if all irreducible complex characters can be afforded by a real representation. In this paper we gather evidence that for finite simple orthogonal groups, all real classes are strongly real if and only if all real-valued irreducible complex characters can be afforded by real representations.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give some standard information on orthogonal groups over finite fields and their important subgroups. In Section
3
, after giving some previous results, we prove our main results in the case of odd characteristic, which are reality results for
\Omega(V)
and
\mathrm{P}\Omega(V)
in the remaining cases when
\dim(V)\equiv 2(
mod
4)
. In particular, in the cases when these groups are known to have classes which are not real, we show that all real elements are strongly real, in Proposition
3.1
and Theorem
3.5
, except in the case of
\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
and
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 3(
mod
4)
. In that exceptional case, we construct elements which are real but not strongly real in Theorem
3.6
(with technical base case constructions given in the Appendix). In Section
4
, we prove that all real elements are strongly real in
\Omega^{\pm}(4m+2,q)
when
q
is a power of
2
, and we classify all real elements, in Theorem
4.1
. In Section
5
, we prove in Theorem
5.1
that all real-valued irreducible characters of
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
and
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
can be afforded by real representations, except possibly in the case
\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
and
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 3(
mod
4)
, and we give some concluding remarks.

2.
Finite orthogonal groups

In this section we state some well-known facts about finite orthogonal groups and some of their subgroups. All of these results may be found in
[
8
]
or
[
1
, Chapter 16]
.

Let
q
be the power of a prime, and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
a finite field with
q
elements. Fix a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space
V
, say
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(V)=n
, and we assume
V
carries a quadratic form
Q
. In the case that
q
is odd, we assume that the bilinear form
B
associated with
Q
is non-degenerate, and in the case that
q
is even we assume that
n
is even and
Q
is non-defective. That is, when
q
is odd we have, for all
u,v\in V
,

Q(u+v)=Q(u)+2B(u,v)+Q(v),

for a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
B
, while if
q
is even we have

Q(u+v)=Q(u)+B(u,v)+Q(v),

for a non-degenerate symplectic form
B
. We may then consider the group
\mathrm{O}(V)
of isometries of the quadratic form
Q
, which is the
orthogonal group
. When
q
is odd, this is the same as the group of isometries of the associated symmetric bilinear form
B
.

For all
n
and
q
considered above, there are precisely two equivalence classes of quadratic forms
Q
. For
v\in V
, we take
v
to have coordinate representation
v=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
with respect to some basis. When
n=2m
is even, one form may be taken to be

Q(v)=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}+\cdots+x_{2m-1}x_{2m},

which we say is a
split
form, or
+
-type
. When
q
is odd and
n=2m+1
is odd, the split, or
+
-type, form is given by

Q(v)=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}+\cdots+x_{2m-1}x_{2m}+x_{2m+1}^{2}.

The second equivalence class of quadratic forms (which we do not formulate explicitly here) in the cases under consideration will be called
non-split
forms, or
-
-type
. We will denote by
\mathrm{O}^{+}(n,q)
or
\mathrm{O}^{-}(n,q)
the orthogonal groups corresponding to a quadratic form of split or non-split type, respectively, and we will write
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
for the generic case. In the case that
n
and
q
are odd, we in fact have
\mathrm{O}^{+}(n,q)\cong\mathrm{O}^{-}(n,q)
, but these groups are not isomorphic in the general case.

One group of primary interest in this paper is the derived (or commutator) subgroup of the orthogonal group. Letting
X^{\prime}
denote the derived subgroup of a group
X
, we may define

\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)=\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)^{\prime}.

When
q
is even,
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
is an index two subgroup of
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
and is a simple group. When
q
is odd,
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
is an index
4
subgroup of
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
, and may or may not contain the central involution of
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
. Whether the central involution is contained in
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
may be determined by
n
,
q
, and the type
\pm
of the underlying form, as described in
[
1
, pg. 230]
. If
Z
is the center of
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
, then
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)=\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)/Z
is a finite simple group (with a finite number of exceptions when
n
is small).

We now assume that
q
is the power of an odd prime until Section
4
. In this case, we may define the special orthogonal groups
\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(n,q)
to be the elements of
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
with determinant
1
. Then
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
is an index
2
subgroup of
\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(n,q)
, and
\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(n,q)
is an index
2
subgroup of
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
. One may also define the
spinor norm
on
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
, which is a multiplicative homomorphism

\theta:\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}/(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2},

and the explicit definition can be found in
[
8
, Chapter 9]
. Then
\ker(\theta)
is an index
2
subgroup of
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
, and we write
\ker(\theta)=\mathrm{K}^{\pm}(n,q)
. Also
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
is an index 2 subgroup of
\mathrm{K}^{\pm}(n,q)
, and in fact we have

\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)=\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(n,q)\cap\mathrm{K}^{\pm}(n,q).

There is a third index two subgroup of
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
containing
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
, consisting of
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
together with the coset in
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
disjoint from both
\mathrm{K}^{\pm}(n,q)
and
\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(n,q)
. We define this subgroup to be
\mathrm{T}^{\pm}(n,q)
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}^{\pm}(n,q)

\displaystyle=\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)\cup\{g\in\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)\,\mid\,g\not\in\mathrm{K}^{\pm}(n,q)\cup\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(n,q)\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ker}(\theta\times\det).

We denote the
discriminant
of the symmetric form
B
carried by
V
, defined in
[
8
, Chapter 2]
, by
dV\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}/(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
.

The discriminant, spinor norm, and determinant each behave as a direct product map over an orthogonal direct sum of the underlying space
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. That is, suppose
V=\oplus_{i}V_{i}
is an orthogonal direct sum with respect to
B
, so that we may consider the non-degenerate symmetric form
B
restricted to the subspace
V_{i}
, denoted
B_{V_{i}}
. Then we have
dV=\sum_{i}dV_{i}
. If
g\in\mathrm{O}(V)
and each
V_{i}
is
g
-invariant, then we may consider
g
with restricted action on each subspace
V_{i}
, and denote this by
g_{i}=g_{V_{i}}\in\mathrm{O}(V_{i})
. Then we have that the spinor norm and determinant maps satisfy

\theta(g)=\sum_{i}\theta(g_{i})\quad\text{ and }\quad\det(g)=\prod_{i}\det(g_{i}).

When restricting the symmetric form
B
to a subspace
V_{i}
, the
\pm
type of the corresponding quadratic form may be determined by the values of
q
,
\dim(V_{i})
, and the discriminant
dV_{i}
, as described in
[
1
, pgs. 221-222]
.

3.
Odd characteristic

We take
q
to be the power of an odd prime,
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
endowed with a quadratic form
Q
, and
B
the associated non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on
V
. Let
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
be the corresponding orthogonal group.

3.1.
Preliminary Results

In this section we give previous results which will be repeatedly used in what follows. If
G
is a finite group, then we say
g\in G
is
real in
G
if there is an element
h\in G
such that
hgh^{-1}=g^{-1}
. If all elements of
G
satisfy this property, then we say
G
is a
real group
. We say
g\in G
is
strongly real in
G
if there is an element
s\in G
such that
s^{2}=1
and
sgs^{-1}=g^{-1}
, or equivalently, if there are elements
s_{1},s_{2}\in G
such that
s_{1}^{2}=s_{2}^{2}=1
and
g=s_{1}s_{2}
. If all elements of
G
are strongly real in
G
, then we say
G
is a
strongly real group
.

We have the following result of Wonenburger
[
22
]
mentioned in the introduction (see also
[
13
, Proposition 4.5]
).

Theorem 3.1
.

If
q
is the power of an odd prime and
n\geq 2
, then
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
is strongly real.

The following essentially comes from
[
22
]
, but also follows from
[
15
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

If
q
is the power of an odd prime and
n\geq 3
, then
\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(n,q)
is strongly real if and only if it is real, and this group is real if and only if
n\not\equiv 2(
mod
4)
.

The following is the result
[
13
, Theorem 8.6]
of Knüppel and Thomsen, specialized to the case of finite fields.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
q
is the power of an odd prime, and
n\geq 3
(and
q>3
when
n<6
), then
\mathrm{K}^{\pm}(n,q)
is strongly real if and only if:

(i)

q\equiv 1(\mathrm{mod}\;4)
, or

(ii)

q\equiv 3(
mod
4)
and is one of
\mathrm{K}^{+}(4m+2,q)
,
\mathrm{K}^{+}(4m+3,q)
,
\mathrm{K}^{-}(4m,q)
, or
\mathrm{K}^{-}(4m+1,q)
, or

(iii)

n=8
or
n=9
.

Knüppel and Thomsen also prove the following in
[
13
, Theorem 8.5]
(see also
[
5
]
).

Theorem 3.4
.

If
q
is the power of an odd prime, and

(and

), then
\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
is strongly real if and only if:

(i)

and

, or

(ii)

, or

.

Note that for

is isomorphic to other well-understood finite groups, by

.

Recall that if
F
is a field with fixed algebraic closure
\overline{F}
, given a monic non-constant polynomial with nonzero constant
f(t)\in F[t]
, the
reciprocal polynomial
of
f(t)
, denoted

\alpha\in\overline{F}^{\times}
of

,
\alpha^{-1}
is a zero of

with the same multiplicity. Then

is

We next describe an important orthogonal decomposition of the underlying space

. Given any

, then as in

3.2.
Reality results

We begin with the following observation.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G
be one of the finite simple groups
G=\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 1(\mathrm{mod}\;4)
or
G=\Omega^{+}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 3(\mathrm{mod}\;4)
, where
m\geq 1
. Then all real classes of
G
are strongly real.

Proof.

We have
G
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(4m+2,q)
, and all real classes of this group are strongly real by Theorem
3.2
. Let
g\in G
be real in
G
, which is then real in
\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(4m+2,q)
. So we may write
g=s_{1}s_{2}
, where
s_{1}^{2}=s_{2}^{2}=1
, and we may assume
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(4m+2,q)\setminus G
. We have
-I\not\in G
, and so the involutions
-s_{1},-s_{2}\in G
with
g=(-s_{1})(-s_{2})
. Thus
g
is strongly real in
G
.
∎

The goal for this section is to prove that the same statement holds true in the case
G=\Omega^{+}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 1(
mod
4)
and for the simple group
G/Z=\mathrm{P}\Omega^{+}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 1(
mod
4)
, but in contrast we also show that the groups
\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
and
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 3(
mod
4)
always have weakly real classes.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{T}^{\pm}(n,q)
, with
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. Let
V=\oplus V_{i}
be the orthogonal decomposition corresponding to
g
. Then
g
is strongly real in
\mathrm{T}^{\pm}(n,q)
if:

(1)

there exists a subspace
V_{i}
such that
\dim(V_{i})\equiv 2(\mathrm{mod}\;4)
which is type
2^{*}
or
3
, or

(2)

there exists a subspace
V_{i}
which is type
2^{\pm}
and
dV_{i}=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
.

Proof.

First, since
g\in\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
, then
g
is the product of two involutions in
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
by Theorem
3.1
. Let us take
g=sh
with
s^{2}=h^{2}=1
, and we assume
s,h\in\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)\setminus\mathrm{T}^{\pm}(n,q)
, otherwise
g
is already strongly real in
\mathrm{T}^{\pm}(n,q)
. Write
s_{i}=s_{V_{i}}
for each subspace
V_{i}
, and by the proof of
[
13
, Proposition 4.5]
, we may assume
s_{i}\in\mathrm{O}(V_{i})
. First suppose that there is a subspace
V_{i}
with
\dim(V_{i})\equiv 2(
mod
4)
which is of type
2^{*}
or type
3
. We consider the spinor norm on
\mathrm{O}(V_{i})
, and by
[
13
, Lemma 5.8]
and its proof, for any non-square
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\setminus(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
, we may replace
s_{i}
with some involution
s_{i}^{\prime}
such that
\theta(s_{i}^{\prime})=\alpha\theta(s_{i})
, where
s_{i}^{\prime}
is a
\mathrm{GL}(V_{i})
-conjugate of
s_{i}
so that
\det(s_{i})=\det(s_{i}^{\prime})
. Then we define

s^{\prime}=s_{i}^{\prime}\oplus\bigoplus_{j\neq i}s_{j},

and by construction

, and

is an inverting involution for

.

Next assume that there is a subspace
V_{i}
which is type
2^{\pm}
and has discriminant

. Then we define

s^{\prime}=(-s_{i})\oplus\bigoplus_{j\neq i}s_{j},

so that
s^{\prime}
is an involution in
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
. Since

, then
-I_{V_{i}}\in\mathrm{ker}(\theta_{i})
(by

), and so

. Since

is type

, then

is odd, and so

. So by construction we now have

and

is an inverting involution for

.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let

and
q\equiv 1(\mathrm{mod}\;4)
. Then
\mathrm{T}^{\pm}(n,q)
is strongly real.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathrm{T}^{\pm}(n,q)
with

the orthogonal decomposition corresponding to

. By Lemma
3.1
, we may assume that any

of type

or

of type

has discriminant

For any subspace

, it follows from

. Since

Consider a subspace

of type

. Then

\theta(g_{i})=dV_{i}\neq(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
, and it follows that we have

.

Now let

be the orthogonal direct sum of all subspaces of type

or type

, and so

, and let

. If

, then the number of subspaces of type

Theorem 3.5
.

Let

and

. In either of the groups

Proof.

From Theorem

Let

\Omega^{+}(4m+2,q)

To prove the statement in the group

Characteristic two

We now assume that
q

has no

can be orthogonally decomposed, as

is a (non-orthogonal) direct sum,

and

has a single elementary divisor

is irreducible and self-reciprocal, and if

(3)

a (non-orthogonal) direct sum

of totally isotropic
g
-invariant subspaces (with

is any other inverting involution for

where

and

. Now write

, where

is the direct sum of all

on which

has eigenvalues different from

, and

is the direct sum of those on which

has eigenvalues only equal to

. Write

and
g_{u}=g_{V_{u}}
. From the description of centralizers given by Wall

, it follows that we have the direct product

(4.1)

C_{G}(g)=C_{\mathrm{O}(V_{y})}(g_{y})\times C_{\mathrm{O}(V_{u})}(g_{u}).

Since

does not satisfy condition (ii), then neither does

is an

Since

∎

5.
Real-valued characters

In this section, we give some results on real-valued irreducible complex characters of finite simple orthogonal groups, and compare them to the known reality results of their conjugacy classes.

Given a finite group
G
, we let
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
denote the collection of complex irreducible characters of
G
, so that each
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
is afforded by a complex representation
(\pi,W)
of
G
. Given a basis
\mathcal{B}
of
W
, let
[\pi]=[\pi]_{\mathcal{B}}
denote the associated matrix representation. We say that
(\pi,W)
is a
real representation
if there is a basis
\mathcal{B}
of
W
such that
[\pi(g)]
has all real entries for all
g\in G
. The
Frobenius-Schur indicator
of
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, which we denote by
\varepsilon(\chi)
, is given by the formula

\varepsilon(\chi)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g^{2}),

and takes the values
\varepsilon(\chi)=0
if
\chi
is not real-valued,
\varepsilon(\chi)=-1
if
\chi
is real-valued but
(\pi,W)
is not a real representation, and
\varepsilon(\chi)=1
if
(\pi,W)
is a real representation (see
[
10
, Chapter 4]
). In particular, because the number of real conjugacy classes of
G
is equal to the number of real-valued irreducible complex characters of
G
, then
\varepsilon(\chi)=\pm 1
for all
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
if and only if
G
is a real group.

There is a twisted variation of the Frobenius-Schur indicator, due to N. Kawanaka and H. Matsuyama
[
11
]
, which we will need here. Let
\iota
be an automorphism of the group
G
, such that
\iota^{2}=1
, and let
{{}^{\iota}\chi}
denote the character defined by
{{}^{\iota}\chi}(g)=\chi(\iota(g))
. If we define
\varepsilon_{\iota}(\chi)
by

\varepsilon_{\iota}(\chi)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g\cdot\iota(g)),

then
\varepsilon_{\iota}(\chi)
takes only the values
1,-1,0
for
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, and
\varepsilon_{\iota}(\chi)=\pm 1
if and only if
{{}^{\iota}\chi}=\overline{\chi}
. This twisted variant generalizes the Frobenius-Schur indicator, and has many other interesting properties, but we do not need them here. We have the following, which is only a slight variation of
[
16
, Lemma 2.3(i)]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
H
and
G
are finite groups with
H
an index two subgroup of
G
such that
G=\langle H,s\rangle
with
s^{2}=1
. If
\varepsilon(\chi)=1
for all
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, then
\varepsilon(\psi)\geq 0
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
. If
H
is a real group, then
\varepsilon(\psi)=1
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
.

Proof.

We define
\iota
on
H
by
{{}^{\iota}g}=sgs^{-1}
. Given any
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
, the induced character
\psi^{G}
is either irreducible or the sum of two distinct irreducibles of
G
. As in the proof of
[
16
, Lemma 2.3]
, if
\psi^{H}=\chi
irreducible, we have
\varepsilon(\chi)=\varepsilon(\psi)+\varepsilon_{\iota}(\psi)
, while if
\psi^{H}=\chi_{1}+\chi_{2}
with
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, we have

\varepsilon(\chi_{1})+\varepsilon(\chi_{2})=\varepsilon(\psi)+\varepsilon_{\iota}(\psi)\;\text{ with }\;\varepsilon(\chi_{1})=\varepsilon(\chi_{2}).

Since
\varepsilon(\chi)=1
for all
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, and
\varepsilon(\psi)
,
\varepsilon_{\iota}(\psi)
can only take the values
0
or
\pm 1
, then in the first case we must either have
\varepsilon(\psi)=1
and
\varepsilon_{\iota}(\psi)=0
, or
\varepsilon(\psi)=0
and
\varepsilon_{\iota}(\psi)=1
. In the second case, we must have
\varepsilon(\psi)=\varepsilon_{\iota}(\psi)=1
. Thus
\varepsilon(\psi)\geq 0
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
as claimed. Further, if
H
is a real group, then
\varepsilon(\psi)=\pm 1
for all
\psi
, and so we must have
\varepsilon(\psi)=1
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
.
∎

We may now obtain the following result on the Frobenius-Schur indicators of the finite simple orthogonal groups.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
q
be any prime power,
n\geq 1
, and
H=\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
, other than the case
H=\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 3(
mod
4)
. Then for any
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
, we have
\varepsilon(\psi)\geq 0
. That is, if
\psi
is a real-valued irreducible character of
H
, then
\psi
is afforded by a real representation. The same statement holds for the groups
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
, other than
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 3(
mod
4)
.

Proof.

If
q
is a power of
2
, then this statement is proven in
[
20
, Theorems 8.3 and 8.6]
and in
[
19
, Theorem 4.2]
(noting that
\mathrm{SO}(2m+1,q)\cong\mathrm{Sp}(2m,q)
when
q
is a power of
2
). Thus we now assume that
q
is the power of an odd prime.

For any
\theta\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q))
, we have
\varepsilon(\theta)=1
by a result of Gow
[
7
, Theorem 1]
. For each case, we consider an index 2 subgroup
G
of
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)
such that
G
is strongly real. If
n\neq 4m+2
, then we can take
G=\mathrm{SO}^{\pm}(n,q)
by Threorem
3.2
. When
n=4m+2
, then we may take
G=K^{\pm}(4m+2,q)
by parts (i) and (ii) of Theorem
3.3
. In particular, for each of these cases
G
is a real group and
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)=\langle G,t\rangle
with
t
any involution in
\mathrm{O}^{\pm}(n,q)\setminus G
. It follows from the second statement in Lemma
5.1
that
\varepsilon(\chi)=1
for all
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. Now
[G:H]=2
, and
G=\langle H,s\rangle
with
s
any involution in
G\setminus H
. It now follows from the first statement in Lemma
5.1
that
\varepsilon(\psi)\geq 0
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
. Note that in the case that
H=\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)
is itself a real group, then we have
\varepsilon(\psi)=\pm 1
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
, and so it follows that
\varepsilon(\psi)=1
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
. This is the case for the groups given in Theorem
3.4
.

Now consider the group
\tilde{H}=P\Omega^{\pm}(n,q)=H/Z
, where
Z=Z(H)
is the center of
H
. Given any
\omega\in\mathrm{Irr}(\tilde{H})
, we may define
\psi
of
H
by
\psi(h)=\omega(hZ)
. It follows from a direct computation that
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=\langle\omega,\omega\rangle=1
, and so
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(H)
. Then we may compute

\varepsilon(\omega)=\frac{|Z|}{|H|}\sum_{hZ\in\tilde{H}}\omega(h^{2}Z)=\frac{|Z|}{|H|}\sum_{hZ\in\tilde{H}}\psi(h^{2})=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\psi(h^{2})=\varepsilon(\psi).

The result now follows, since
\varepsilon(\psi)\geq 0
.
∎

We cannot make the same conclusion for the group
H=\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
with
q\equiv 3(
mod
4)
, where to do so it would be enough to show that
H
is contained in an index 2 subgroup of
\mathrm{O}^{-}(4m+2,q)
which is real (but not necessarily strongly real). By Theorem
3.2
, such an index 2 subgroup cannot be
\mathrm{SO}^{-}(4m+2,q)
. In fact, one can show that both of the groups
\mathrm{K}^{-}(4m+2,q)
and
\mathrm{T}^{-}(4m+2,q)
, with

mod

, contain elements which are not real. That is, in the exceptional case

with
q\equiv 3(
mod

,

is not contained in any real index two subgroup of
\mathrm{O}^{-}(4m+2,q)
. We could also make this conclusion from the above if we construct some
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q))
,

mod
4)
, such that
\varepsilon(\chi)=-1
. By
[
12
, Proposition 2.9.1(vii)]
, we have
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(6,q)\cong\mathrm{PSU}(4,q)
, and the character table for
\mathrm{PSU}(4,3)
reveals the existence of characters of
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(6,3)
which indeed have Frobenius-Schur indicator
-1

[

, pg. 54]
.

Given the results obtained here, we expect that for any values of

and

, all real classes of

or

are strongly real if and only if all real-valued irreducible complex characters have Frobenius-Schur indicator equal to 1. We hope to complete the proof of this statement in the sequel, by constructing irreducible complex characters with Frobenius-Schur indicator
-1
of
\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
and
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(4m+2,q)
with

mod
4)
, and showing all real classes are strongly real in the groups
\Omega(2n+1,q)
,
\Omega^{+}(4m,q)
, and
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{+}(4m,q)
with

mod

.

1&(a_{1}^{2}+c_{1}^{2})/4&0&c_{1}(a_{1}+b_{1})/4\\
0&0&0&1\\
0&c_{1}(a_{1}+b_{1})/4&1&(b_{1}^{2}+c_{1}^{2})/4\end{array}\right),

which has determinant 1. It follows this is a split form by
[
1
, pg. 222]
, and the involution is again an element of
\Omega^{-}(6,q)
by
[
1
, Proposition 16.30]
, giving the result.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

The element
h_{0}\in\Omega^{-}(10,q)
is such that
h_{0}Z
is weakly real in
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(10,q)
when
q\equiv 3(\mathrm{mod}\;4)
.

Proof.

It was shown above that the element
u
is conjugated to its inverse by elements in
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(4,q)
which are not elements of
\Omega^{+}(4,q)
, and that
u
is not conjugated to its inverse by any element in
\mathrm{O}^{+}(4,q)
which squares to
-I
. Thus both of these statements hold for
-u
as well.

As mentioned above, the centralizer of
u_{1}
contains elements in
\mathrm{SO}^{-}(6,q)
which are not in
\Omega^{-}(6,q)
, which follows from
[
1
, Proposition 16.34]
. Taking any element of
\Omega^{-}(6,q)
which conjugates
u_{1}
to its inverse (for example, any of the involutions described above), and multiplying it by an element of the centralizer of
u_{1}
from
\mathrm{SO}^{-}(6,q)\setminus\Omega^{-}(6,q)
, yields an element
y_{1}
from that outer coset which conjugates
u_{1}
to its inverse. We have already found an involution
s_{0}
from
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(4,q)\setminus\Omega^{+}(4,q)
which conjugates
-u
to its inverse, and so now the block diagonal element
(y_{1},s_{0})\in\Omega^{-}(10,q)
conjugates
h_{0}=(u_{1},-u)
to its inverse. Thus
h_{0}Z
is real in
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(10,q)
. Next we show that
h_{0}Z
is not strongly real in
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(10,q)
, and note that we cannot have
h_{0}
conjugate to
-h_{0}^{-1}
since these elements have different sets of elementary divisors. So we must show there is no
s\in\Omega^{-}(10,q)
such that
sh_{0}s^{-1}=h^{-1}
and
s^{2}=\pm I
.

Since
u_{1}
and
-u
have distinct eigenvalues, and since the centralizer of
-u
in
\mathrm{O}^{+}(4,q)
is completely contained in
\Omega^{+}(4,q)
by
[
1
, Proposition 16.34]
, then it follows from
[
21
, Case (C), (iv) of 2.6]
that we have

C_{\Omega^{-}(10,q)}(h_{0})=C_{\Omega^{-}(6,q)}(u_{1})\times C_{\Omega^{+}(4,q)}(-u).

Since there is an element

(y_{1},s_{0})\in(\mathrm{SO}^{-}(6,q)\setminus\Omega^{-}(6,q))\times(\mathrm{SO}^{+}(4,q)\setminus\Omega^{+}(4,q)),

such that
y_{1}
conjugates
u_{1}
to its inverse and
s_{0}
conjugates
-u
to its inverse, then from the structure of the centralizer of
h_{0}
, any element of
\Omega^{-}(10,q)
which conjugates
h_{0}
to its inverse must take this form. However, there is no element in
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(4,q)\setminus\Omega^{+}(4,q)
which conjugates
-u
to its inverse and which squares to
-I
, so there is no such conjugating element for
h_{0}
in
\Omega^{-}(10,q)
. Since there is no involution in
\mathrm{SO}^{-}(6,q)\setminus\Omega^{-}(6,q)
which conjugates
u_{1}
to its inverse by Lemma
A.2
, then there can be no involution in
\Omega^{-}(10,q)
which conjugates
h_{0}
to its inverse. Thus
h_{0}Z
is not strongly real in
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{-}(10,q)
.
∎
