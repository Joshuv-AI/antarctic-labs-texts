Epidemics on Hypergraphs: Spectral Thresholds for Extinction
Desmond John Higham, Henry-Louis de Kergorlay

Abstract
Epidemic spreading is well understood when a disease propagates around a contact graph. In a stochastic susceptible-infected-susceptible setting, spectral conditions characterise whether the disease vanishes. However, modelling human interactions using a graph is a simplification which only considers pairwise relationships. This does not fully represent the more realistic case where people meet in groups. Hyperedges can be used to record such group interactions, yielding more faithful and flexible models, allowing for the rate of infection of a node to vary as a nonlinear function of the number of infectious neighbors. We discuss different types of contagion models in this hypergraph setting, and derive spectral conditions that characterize whether the disease vanishes. We study both the exact individual-level stochastic model and a deterministic mean field ODE approximation. Numerical simulations are provided to illustrate the analysis. We also interpret our results and show how the hypergraph model allows us to distinguish between contributions to infectiousness that (a) are inherent in the nature of the pathogen and (b) arise from behavioural choices (such as social distancing, increased hygiene and use of masks). This raises the possibility of more accurately quantifying the effect of interventions that are designed to contain the spread of a virus.

2
Stochastic SIS models

2.1
Stochastic SIS model on a graph

Classical ODE compartmental models are based on the assumption that
any pair of individuals is equally likely to interact—this
is the homogeneous mixing case
[
2
]
.
If, instead, we have knowledge of all
possible pairwise interactions between individuals,
then this information may be
incorporated via a contact graph and used in a stochastic model.
Here, each node represents an individual, and
an edge between nodes
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
indicates that
individuals
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
interact.
For a population with
\displaystyle n
individuals,
we may let

\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denote the
corresponding symmetric adjacency matrix, so nodes
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
interact if and only

\displaystyle A_{ij}=1
.
In this setting, a stochastic SIS model
uses the two-state random variable

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)

to represent the §
status of node
\displaystyle i
at time
\displaystyle t
, with
\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=0

for a susceptible node and

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=1

for an infected node. Each

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)

then
follows
a continuous time Markov process where
the infection rate is given by

\beta\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}X_{j}(t)

(1)

and the recovery rate is
\displaystyle\delta
. Here,

\displaystyle\beta>0
and
\displaystyle\delta>0
are parameters governing the strength of the two effects.
In this model, we see from (
1
) that the
current
chance of infection increases linearly in proportion to the
current number of infected neighbors.

This model was studied in

[
21
, Theorem 1]
, where it was argued that the
condition

\lambda(A)\frac{\beta}{\delta}<1

(2)

guarantees the disease will die out.
Here,
\displaystyle\lambda(A)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric matrix
\displaystyle A
.
Further justification for this result may be found, for example,
in

[
11
,
18
]
.
We note that (
2
) gives an elegant generalization of the homogeneous mixing case (where
\displaystyle A
corresponds to the complete graph).

2.2
Why use a hypergraph?

It has been argued

[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
10
]

that
in many network science applications
we lose information by
recording only pairwise interactions.
For example, emails can be sent to groups of recipients,
scholarly articles may have multiple coauthors,
and
many proteins may interact to form a complex.
In such cases, recording the relevant lists of interacting
nodes gives a more informative picture than reducing
these down to a collection of edges.

In the setting of an SIS model, we may argue that
individuals typically come together in
well-defined groups, for example, in a household,
a workplace or a social setting.
Such groups may be handled by the use of hyperedges,
leading to a hypergraph; these
concepts are formalized in the next section.

With a classic graph model, as described in section
2.1
,
the rate of infection of a node is linearly proportional to the number of infectious neighbors.
With a hypergraph we may consider more intricate contagion mechanisms.
For example,
using the terminology of

[
17
]
,
the
collective contagion
model
is used in
[
8
,
15
,
17
]
). Here, infection only starts spreading within a hyperedge after a certain threshold number of infectious neighbors has been reached.
This type of behaviour is relevant, for example, in an office environment. A small number of workers may be able to
socially distance in way that effectively eliminates the
risk of infection.
However,
if the number of
individuals (size of the hyperedge) is too large, then
the disease may spread.

We mention here that an alternative type of mechanism may also
operate, which we call
collective suppression
.
Imagine that a disease may be contracted through
contact with a surface that was previously touched by an infected individual.
Now suppose that
a group of individuals is likely to use the
same physical object, such as a door handle, hand rail,
cash machine, or water cooler.
If an infected individual contaminates the object, then further
contamination by other individuals is less relevant.
In this case,
doubling the number of common users will increase the
risk of infection by a factor less than two; generally
risk grows sublinearly as a function of the size of the hyperedge.

These arguments motivate us to study the case where
the rate of infection of a node within a hyperdege is dependent on a generic function
\displaystyle f
of the number of infectious neighbors in a hyperedge; this approach was also taken in
[
6
]
.
We will be particularly concerned with the
case where
\displaystyle f
is concave, since this
is tractable for analysis and allows us to
draw conclusions about the
collective contagion model.

We note that if
\displaystyle f
is the identity, then we recover linear dependence on the number of infectious neighbors and the hypergraph model is equivalent to a virus spreading on the clique graph of the hypergraph.

3
SIS on a Hypergraph

3.1
Background

We continue with some standard definitions

[
7
]
.

Definition 1
.

A
hypergraph
is a tuple
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(V,E)
of
nodes

\displaystyle V
and
hyperedges

\displaystyle E
such that
\displaystyle E\subset{\mathcal{P}}(V)
.
Here,
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}(V)
denotes the power set of
\displaystyle V
.

We will let
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle m
denote the number of nodes
and hyperedges, respectively; that is,

\displaystyle|V|=n
and
\displaystyle|E|=m
. Loosely, a hypergraph generalizes
the concept of a graph by allowing an “edge” to be a list of more than two nodes.

Definition 2
.

Consider a hypergraph
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(V,E)
.
The
incidence matrix
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}
,
is the
\displaystyle n\times m
matrix such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{ih}=1
if node
\displaystyle i
belongs to hyperedge
\displaystyle h
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{ih}=0
otherwise.

It is also useful to introduce

\displaystyle W\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{I}}^{T}
.
This
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix has the property that

\displaystyle W_{ij}
records the number of hyperedges containing both nodes
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
.
In particular, if
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}
is a graph then
\displaystyle W
is the affinity matrix of the graph.

3.2
General infection model

In our context, the nodes represent individuals and
a
hyperedge records a collection of individuals who
are known to interact as a group.
As in the graph case introduced in subsection
2.1
,
we use a state vector
\displaystyle X(t)

which follows a continuous time Markov process,
where, for each
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n
,

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=1
if node
\displaystyle i
is infected at time

\displaystyle t
and

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=0
otherwise.
We continue to assume that
an infectious node becomes susceptible with
constant recovery rate
\displaystyle\delta>0
.
However,
generalizing
(
1
), we now assume
that a susceptible node
\displaystyle i
becomes infectious with
rate

\beta\sum_{h\in E}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f\big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}(t)\mathcal{I}_{jh}\big),

(3)

where
\displaystyle\beta>0
is a constant.
In (
3
),

\displaystyle f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

specifies the
manner in which
the contribution to the overall level of
infectiousness
from each hyperedge involving node
\displaystyle i

is assumed to increase
in proportion to the number of
infected nodes in that hyperedge.
Throughout our analysis we will always assume that

\displaystyle f(0)=0
and

\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle C^{1}
in a neighborhood of
\displaystyle 0
.

If
\displaystyle f
is the identity, the rate of infection reduces to
\displaystyle\beta\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}X_{j}(t)
. This gives a weighted version of the infection rate of an SIS model on a graph.
As discussed in subsection
2.2
,
it may be appropriate to choose
nonlinear
\displaystyle f
in certain circumstances.
We note that in
[
6
]
the authors have in mind functions which behave like the identity near the origin and have a horizontal asymptote. Instances of such functions are

\displaystyle x\mapsto\mathrm{arctan}(x)
and
\displaystyle x\mapsto\min\{x,c\}
for some
\displaystyle c>0
. Relaxing these conditions, we may ask more generally in such a
setting that the function be concave. On the other hand, the authors in
[
8
,
15
]
consider a
collective contagion model
, where infection spreads within a hyperdge only if a certain threshold of infectious vertices is reached in that hyperedge.
A collective contagion model may be represented via the function
\displaystyle x\mapsto c_{2}\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1})
for some
\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2}>0
, or
\displaystyle x\mapsto\max\{0,x-c\}
for some
\displaystyle c>0
.

3.3
Partitioned hypergraph model

We also introduce a more general case where we partition the hyperedges into
\displaystyle K
disjoint categories with each category
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq K
having its own distinct rate of infection in response to the number infected nodes in a hyperedge, represented by a function
\displaystyle f_{k}
.
For example, the categories may correspond to different types of housing, workplaces, hospitality venues or sports facilities.
We may then represent the infection rate of node
\displaystyle i
as

\beta\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{h\in E}{\mathcal{I}}^{(k)}_{ih}f_{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}(t){\mathcal{I}}^{(k)}_{jh}\right),

(4)

where we let
\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}^{(k)}_{ih}=1
if
\displaystyle i
belongs to hyperedge
\displaystyle h
in the category
\displaystyle k
and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}^{(k)}_{ih}=0
otherwise; so

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}^{(k)}
is the incidence matrix of the subhypergraph consisting of only the hyperedges from category
\displaystyle k
.
We will refer to this as a

partitioned hypergraph model
.

In this generalized case, a collective contagion model could be defined by first organizing the hyperedges into categories depending on their size, so that category
\displaystyle k
is the set of hyperedges of size
\displaystyle k+1
. A collective contagion model may then represented, for example, via the functions
\displaystyle f_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\mapsto x
, and
\displaystyle f_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\mapsto c_{2,k}\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1,k})
,
\displaystyle k\in\{2,\dots,K\}
.

4
Mean Field Approximation

A classic approach to studying processes such as
(
3
), where
infection rates are random,
is to develop
a mean field approximation for the expected process

\big(\mathbb{E}[X_{i}(t)]\big)_{t\geq 0}=\big(\mathbb{P}(X_{i}(t)=1)\big)_{t\geq 0}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(p_{i}(t))_{t\geq 0},

with
deterministic rates.
In our case,
the rate of recovery
\displaystyle\delta
is constant, so can remain unchanged. Let us express the rate of infection (
3
) of a node solely in terms of the expected processes

\displaystyle\{p_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{n}
. To do this we can substitute the
\displaystyle X_{j}(t)
appearing in (
3
) by their expected values
\displaystyle p_{j}(t)
. The approximate rate of infection for node
\displaystyle i
then becomes

\beta\sum_{h\in E}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f\big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}(t)\mathcal{I}_{jh}\big).

(5)

We arrive at the deterministic mean field ODE

\frac{dP(t)}{dt}=g(P(t)),

(6)

where
\displaystyle g_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

g_{i}(P(t))\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta\sum_{h\in E}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}(t)\mathcal{I}_{jh}\right)(1-p_{i}(t))-\delta p_{i}(t).

(7)

5
Simulations and Comparison between Exact and Mean Field Models

Let us emphasize that the approximate infection rates in (
5
) differ
in general from the expectation of the random rates in (
3
). When the function
\displaystyle f
is concave, however, Jensen’s reverse inequality indicates that the rates in (
5
) are greater than the expectation of the rates in (
3
). Hence, in this case the expected quantities
\displaystyle p_{i}(t)
are overestimated by
(
6
)–(
7
).
This is fine since we are looking for conditions for the disease to vanish. If
\displaystyle f
is not concave (e.g., for a collective contagion model), these expected quantities are underestimated and it is not clear a priori whether the exact model is well approximated by the mean field ODE.

In this section we therefore present
results of computational simulations in order to gain insight into the accuracy of our mean field
approximation.

5.1
Simulation algorithm

Before presenting numerical results, we
summarize our approach for simulating the

see, for example,
[

Using a small fixed time step
\displaystyle\Delta t

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle t+\Delta t

First, let
\displaystyle r\in[0,1]^{n}
be a random vector
of i.i.d. values uniformly sampled from
\displaystyle[0,1]
. For every node
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n
,

•

when
\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=0
, set
\displaystyle X_{i}(t+\Delta t)=1
if

r_{i}<1-\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f(\sum_{j}X_{j}(t)\mathcal{I}_{jh})\Delta t\right),

and set
\displaystyle X_{i}(t+\Delta t)=0
otherwise;

•

when
\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=1
, set
\displaystyle X_{i}(t+\Delta t)=0
if

r_{i}<1-\exp\left(-\delta\Delta t\right),

and set
\displaystyle X_{i}(t+\Delta t)=1
otherwise.

6
Stability Analysis

We provide below spectral conditions which imply that
the infection-free solution
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a locally or globally asymptotically stable equilibrium of
(
6
)–(
7
).
We will find that local asymptotic stability can be shown with no structural assumptions on
\displaystyle f
.
We will also find that
global asymptotic stability
follows
under the same conditions
when
\displaystyle f
is concave.
Our conclusions fit into a framework that generalizes
the graph case (
2
):
the spectral threshold takes the form

\lambda(W)\frac{c_{f}\beta}{\delta}<1

for some constant
\displaystyle c_{f}>0
depending only on the choice of
\displaystyle f
.

Throughout this work, to be concrete we
let
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|
denote the Euclidean norm.

6.1
Local asymptotic stability

Theorem 6.1
.

If

\lambda(W)\frac{f^{\prime}(0)\beta}{\delta}<1

(8)

then

\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of (
6
)–(
7
); that is,
there exists a positive
\displaystyle\gamma
such that

\displaystyle||P(0)||<\gamma\Rightarrow\lim_{t\to\infty}||P(t)||=0
.

Proof.

We see that
\displaystyle g(0)=0
, so
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an equilibrium for (
6
). It remains to show that this solution is locally asymptotically stable.
Appealing to a standard linearization
result
[
20
]
, it suffices to show that every eigenvalue of the Jacobian matrix
\displaystyle\nabla g(0)
has a negative real part.
We compute

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial p_{j_{0}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{j_{0}h}f^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})(1-p_{i}),&\text{ }j_{0}\neq i,\\
\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{j_{0}h}f^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})(1-p_{i})-\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})-\delta,&\text{ }j_{0}=i.\end{cases}

We see that
\displaystyle\nabla g(0)=\beta f^{\prime}(0)W-\delta I
.
This matrix is symmetric and therefore has real eigenvalues.
Hence,
it suffices that the
largest eigenvalue of

\displaystyle\beta f^{\prime}(0)W
does not exceed

\displaystyle\delta
, and the result follows.

(9)

and we let

\displaystyle W^{(k)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\mathcal{I}}^{(k)}{{\mathcal{I}}^{(k)}}^{T}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

If

\lambda\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}^{\prime}(0)W^{(k)}\right)\frac{\beta}{\delta}<1

(10)

then

\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium
of (
6
),
with
\displaystyle g_{i}
defined in (
9
).

Proof.

The proof of
Theorem
6.1

extends straightforwardly.
We compute

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial p_{j_{0}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}_{ih}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}_{j_{0}h}f_{k}^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}_{jh})(1-p_{i}),&\text{ }j_{0}\neq i,\\
\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}_{ih}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}_{j_{0}h}f_{k}^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}_{jh})(1-p_{i})-\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}_{ih}f_{k}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}_{jh})-\delta,&\text{ }j_{0}=i,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial p_{j_{0}}}|_{P=0}=\begin{cases}\beta\sum_{k}W^{(k)}_{ij}f_{k}^{\prime}(0),&\text{ }j_{0}\neq i,\\
\beta\sum_{k}W^{(k)}_{ij}f_{k}^{\prime}(0)-\delta,&\text{ }j_{0}=i,\end{cases}

and note that

\lambda(\beta\sum_{k}f^{\prime}_{k}(0)W^{(k)}-\delta I)<0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\lambda(\sum_{k}f^{\prime}_{k}(0)W^{(k)})<\frac{\delta}{\beta}.

∎

6.2
Global asymptotic stability for the concave infection model

We now show that
when
\displaystyle f
is concave
the
condition in
Theorem
6.1

ensures global stability of the zero equilibrium, and hence guarantees that the disease dies out
according to the mean field approximation.

Definition 3
.

Given a matrix
\displaystyle A
, define its symmetric version to be

A^{(S)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(A+A^{T})/2.

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
are
\displaystyle n\times n
real matrices, and suppose that there exists a diagonal matrix
\displaystyle\Lambda
such that for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\},\ \Lambda_{ii}\geq 0
, and

A=B-\Lambda.

Then the largest eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
satisfy
\displaystyle\lambda(A)\leq\lambda(B)
, and the largest eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A^{(S)}
and
\displaystyle B^{(S)}
also satisfy
\displaystyle\lambda(A^{(S)})\leq\lambda(B^{(S)})
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x
be a unit eigenvector associated with
\displaystyle\lambda(A^{(S)})
. We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\lambda(A^{(S)})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=x^{T}Ax+x^{T}A^{T}x

\displaystyle\displaystyle=x^{T}Bx+x^{T}B^{T}x-2x^{T}\Lambda x

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(b_{ij}+b_{ji})x_{i}x_{j}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda_{ii}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq x^{T}(B+B^{T})x

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\max\{x^{T}(B+B^{T})x\ |\ x^{T}x=1\}=2\lambda(B^{(S)}).

The inequality
\displaystyle\lambda(A)\leq\lambda(B)
may be shown similarly.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

Suppose
\displaystyle f
is concave.
If

\lambda(W)\frac{f^{\prime}(0)\beta}{\delta}<1,

then
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium of
(
6
)–(
7
); so

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}||P(t)||=0

for any valid initial condition (that is, with

\displaystyle 0\leq p(0)_{i}\leq 1
).

Proof.

From the global asymptotic stability result in
[
14
, Lemma
\displaystyle 1^{\prime}
]

it is sufficient to show that
all eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix
\displaystyle(\nabla g(P))^{(S)}

are strictly less than
\displaystyle 0
, for all
\displaystyle P\neq 0
.
We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial p_{j_{0}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{j_{0}h}f^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})(1-p_{i}),&\ j_{0}\neq i,\\
\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{j_{0}h}f^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})(1-p_{i})-\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})-\delta,&\ j_{0}=i.\\
\end{cases}

Letting
\displaystyle B
denote the
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle B_{ij_{0}}=\begin{cases}\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{j_{0}h}f^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})(1-p_{i}),&\ j_{0}\neq i,\\
\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{j_{0}h}f^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})(1-p_{i})-\delta,&\ j_{0}=i,\\
\end{cases}

we have
\displaystyle\nabla g(P)=B-\Lambda
, where
\displaystyle\Lambda
is the
\displaystyle n\times n
diagonal matrix where for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots\}
,
\displaystyle\Lambda_{ii}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})\geq 0
. On the one hand Lemma
6.3
now yields
\displaystyle\lambda((\nabla g(P))^{(S)})\leq\lambda(B^{(S)})
; on the other hand, note that
\displaystyle B+\delta I\leq\nabla g(0)+\delta I
, where we interpret the inequality in a componentwise sense, and where we use
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})\leq f^{\prime}(0)
,
since
\displaystyle f
is concave. Hence
\displaystyle B^{(S)}+\delta I\leq\nabla g(0)+\delta I
, and since
\displaystyle B^{(S)}+\delta I
has only non-negative entries, appealing to the Perron-Frobenius theorem, we have
\displaystyle\lambda(B^{(S)})\leq\lambda(\nabla g(0))
.

Combining these inequalities and using the spectral condition in the statement of the theorem, we deduce that

\lambda\left((\nabla g(P))^{(S)}\right)\leq\lambda(\nabla g(0))=\lambda\left(\beta f^{\prime}(0)W-\delta I\right)<0,

as required.
∎

To proceed, we recall our assumption that at time zero each node has

\displaystyle i_{0}

for

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq n

This implies that

\displaystyle n\,i_{0}
is the expected number of infectious individuals at time zero.

We are interested in the stochastic process

\displaystyle\sum_{i}X_{i}(t)

Our analysis generalizes arguments in

[

\displaystyle f

8.1
Extinction

Our first result shows that the spectral condition
arising from the mean field analysis in Theorems
6.1

and
6.4
is
also
relevant
to the probability of extinction in the
individual-level model.

Theorem 8.1
.

Suppose
\displaystyle f
is concave in the hypergraph infection model (
3
). Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}X_{i}(t)>0\right)\leq n\,i_{0}\,\exp\left((\beta f^{\prime}(0)\lambda(W)-\delta)t\right).

Hence, if

\displaystyle\lambda(W)f^{\prime}(0)\beta/\delta<1
then the disease vanishes at an exponential rate.

Proof.

Consider the continuous time Markov process
\displaystyle\{(Y_{i}(t))_{t\geq 0}\}_{i=1}^{n}

taking values in
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{n}
, with transition of states defined for every
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n
and
\displaystyle\ t\geq 0
by

\begin{cases}k&\rightarrow\ k+1\text{, with rate }\beta f^{\prime}(0)\sum_{j}W_{ij}Y_{j}(t),\\
k&\rightarrow\ k-1\text{, with rate }\delta.\end{cases}

This new process is introduced here purely for the purpose of analysis. However, it may be interpreted
as a disease model where the state of each individual is represented by a non-negative integer that indicates severity of infection. Here, exposure to
highly infected individuals raises the chance
of an increase in infection severity.

Suppose also that
\displaystyle X_{i}(0)=Y_{i}(0)

for all
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n
. Since
\displaystyle f
is concave,

\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f(\sum_{j}X_{j}(t)\mathcal{I}_{jh})\leq\beta\sum_{h}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f^{\prime}(0)\sum_{j}X_{j}(t)\mathcal{I}_{jh}=\beta f^{\prime}(0)\sum_{ij}W_{ij}X_{j}(t),

from which we see that
\displaystyle Y_{i}
stochastically dominates
\displaystyle X_{i}
. Hence

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}X_{i}(t)>0\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}Y_{i}(t)>0\right)\leq\sum_{i}q_{i}(t),

where
\displaystyle q_{i}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(t)]
.
In terms of the
Chapman–Kolmogorov Equation,
or Chemical Master Equation,

[
12
]
,
we have

\frac{dq_{i}(t)}{dt}=\beta f^{\prime}(0)\sum_{j}W_{ij}q_{j}(t)-\delta q_{i}(t).

Letting
\displaystyle Q(t)=[q_{1}(t),q_{2}(t),\ldots,q_{n}(t)]^{T}
,
this linear ODE system solves to give

Q(t)=\exp\left(t(\beta f^{\prime}(0)W-\delta I)\right)Q(0).

The matrix
\displaystyle\exp\left(t(\beta f^{\prime}(0)W-\delta I)\right)
is symmetric and has spectral radius

\displaystyle\exp\left((\beta f^{\prime}(0)\lambda(W)-\delta I)t\right)
.
Hence, in Euclidean norm,

||Q(t)||\leq\exp((\beta f^{\prime}(0)\lambda(W)-I)t)||Q(0)||.

Since
\displaystyle||Q(0)||=\sqrt{n}\,i_{0}
and,
by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\sum_{i}q_{i}(t)\leq\sqrt{n}||Q(t)||,

the proof is complete.
∎

We deduce, analogously to
[
11
]
, the following corollary.

Corollary 8.2
.

Suppose
\displaystyle f
is concave in the hypergraph infection model (
3
).
Let
\displaystyle\tau
denote the time of extinction of the disease and suppose

\displaystyle\lambda(W)f^{\prime}(0)\beta<\delta
, then

\mathbb{E}[\tau]\leq\frac{\log n+1}{\delta-f^{\prime}(0)\beta\lambda(W)}.

Proof.

Using Theorem
8.1
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)dt

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i}X_{i}(t)>0)dt

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log n}{\delta-f^{\prime}(0)\beta\lambda(W)}+\int_{(\log n)/(\delta-f^{\prime}(0)\beta\lambda(W))}^{\infty}n\exp((\beta f^{\prime}(0)\lambda(W)-\delta)t)dt

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log n+1}{\delta-f^{\prime}(0)\beta\lambda(W)}.

∎

Likewise the partitioned case yields the following result.

Theorem 8.3
.

Suppose every
\displaystyle f_{k}
is concave in the partitioned
hypergraph model with infection rate
(

Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}X_{i}(t)>0\right)\leq n\,i_{0}\,\exp\left(\beta\lambda(\sum_{k=1}^{K}f^{\prime}_{k}(0)W^{(k)})-\delta\right).

Hence, if

\displaystyle\lambda(\sum_{k=1}^{K}f^{\prime}_{k}(0)W^{(k)})\beta/\delta<1
then the disease vanishes at an exponential rate.

We also have the following analogue of Corollary 7.2 on the expected time to extinction for the partitioned case.

Corollary 8.4
.

Suppose every
\displaystyle f_{k}
is concave in the partitioned hypergraph model with infection rate (
4
).
Let
\displaystyle\tau
denote the time of extinction of the disease and suppose

\displaystyle\lambda(\sum_{k=1}^{K}f^{\prime}_{k}(0)W^{(k)})\beta/\delta<1

8.2
Conditions that preclude extinction

So far, we have focused on deriving thresholds
that imply extinction.
In this subsection, following
ideas from
[
11
]
, we
derive a condition under which
the disease will
persist.

Note that our analysis does not require
the graph
associated with
\displaystyle W

to be connected.
The disconnected setting
is relevant, for example, when interventions have been imposed in order to limit interactions.
We let

\displaystyle\Delta\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=D-W
denote the Laplacian,
and let
\displaystyle\lambda_{c}(\Delta)>0
denote the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
\displaystyle\Delta
.
We also let
\displaystyle e_{\max}
denote the size of the largest hyperedge.

Definition 4
.

Given a hypergraph
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}
,
a function
\displaystyle f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
a subset of the nodes
\displaystyle S\subset V
, let

E(S,f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{h\in E}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f(\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\mathcal{I}_{jh}),

where
\displaystyle S^{c}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=V\backslash S
is the complement of
\displaystyle S
.
Also define for integer
\displaystyle 1\leq m\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor

\eta(\mathcal{H},m,f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf\left\{\frac{E(S,f)}{|S|}\ |\ \ 1\leq|S|\leq m\right\},

and let
\displaystyle\eta(\mathcal{H},m)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\eta(\mathcal{H},m,Id)
.

Notice that when
\displaystyle S
consists of those nodes for which

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=0
,
we can write the infection transition rate of
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(t)
as
\displaystyle\beta E(S,f)
.
More generally,

\displaystyle\beta E(S,f)
may be regarded as the rate at which
nodes in the set
\displaystyle S
may be infected by nodes in
the remainder of the network. When
\displaystyle f=Id
and
\displaystyle m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
,
\displaystyle\eta(\mathcal{H},m)
is the Cheeger constant, or isoperimetric number, associated with the weighted graph induced by
\displaystyle W=\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}^{T}
.
We may also regard
\displaystyle\eta(\mathcal{H},m,f)

as the smallest
average infection rate over all subsets consisting of no more than half of the network.

The next theorem gives a probabilistic lower bound on the time to extinction.

Theorem 8.5
.

Recall that
\displaystyle\tau
denotes the hitting time of the state
\displaystyle 0
for the process
\displaystyle(\sum_{j}X_{j}(t))_{t\geq 0}

in the hypergraph model (
3
).
If
\displaystyle f
is concave and
\displaystyle\lambda_{c}(\Delta)>\left(2\frac{e_{\max}-1}{f(e_{\max}-1)}\right)\frac{\delta}{\beta}
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau>\frac{\lfloor r^{-m+1}\rfloor}{2m}\right)\geq\frac{1-r}{e}(1+O(r^{m})),

where
\displaystyle r\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{(e_{\max}-1)\delta}{f(e_{\max}-1)\,\beta\,\eta(\mathcal{H},m)}<1
and
\displaystyle m\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
.

From standard Cheeger inequalities
[
19
]
, we know that the Cheeger constant of the graph induced by
\displaystyle W
satisfies
\displaystyle 2\eta(\mathcal{H},m)\geq\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
, hence using the assumptions on
\displaystyle\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
in the theorem, we see that
\displaystyle r<1
indeed.

In order to prove this result, we introduce the following lemma.

Lemma 8.6
.

If
\displaystyle f
is concave and non-decreasing, then

\frac{f(e_{\max}-1)}{e_{\max}-1}\eta(\mathcal{H},m)\leq\eta(\mathcal{H},m,f).

Proof.

The proof is immediate once we see that by concavity of
\displaystyle f
, for all
\displaystyle x\in\{0,\dots,e_{\max}-1\}

\frac{f(e_{\max}-1)}{e_{\max}-1}x\leq f(x).

∎

Now, to prove Theorem
8.5
consider the Markov process
\displaystyle(Z(t))_{t\geq 0}
valued in
\displaystyle\{0,\dots,m\}
, with transition of states given by

\begin{cases}k\ \rightarrow\ k+1\text{ with transition rate }k\beta\frac{f(e_{\max}-1)}{e_{\max}-1}\eta(\mathcal{H},m),\\
k\ \rightarrow\ k-1\text{ with transition rate }k\delta.\end{cases}

This Markov process is stochastically dominated by
\displaystyle((\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(t))_{t\geq 0})
, which has
the same downward transition rate, and an upward transition rate that is at least as large:

\displaystyle\displaystyle k\beta\frac{f(e_{\max}-1)}{e_{\max}-1}\eta(\mathcal{H},m)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq k\beta\eta(\mathcal{H},m,f)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\beta E(S,f),

where
\displaystyle S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\ |\ X_{i}(t)=0\}
. Thus, to show Theorem
8.5
it suffices to find a suitable lower bound for

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\tau}>\frac{\lfloor r^{-m+1}\rfloor}{2m}\right)
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}
is the hitting time of
\displaystyle 0
for the process
\displaystyle(Z(t))_{t\geq 0}
. This follows by applying Theorem
\displaystyle 4.1
of
[
11
]
to the process
\displaystyle(Z(t))_{t\geq 0}
.

Collective Contagion Models

We now consider the collective contagion models from

[
8
,
15
,
17
]
, where infection only starts spreading within a hyperedge once a threshold number of infectious nodes in that hyperedge has been reached.
As discussed in subsection
3.2
, collective contagion models can be represented by nonlinear functions of the form
\displaystyle f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max\{0,x-c\}
for some
\displaystyle c>0
, or
\displaystyle f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=c_{2}\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1})
for some
\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2}>0
.
In these cases it is obvious that the zero-infection state for the mean field approximation is locally asymptotically stable (and, indeed,
Theorem
6.1
applies).
However,
because the functions
are not concave, the theory found in
Section
8

for the exact model does not directly apply.
Nonetheless, we can still derive similar spectral conditions for the vanishing of the disease by finding concave functions which serve as upper bounds for
\displaystyle f
. For instance using
\displaystyle c_{2}\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1})\leq\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}x\mathbbm{1}(x\leq c_{1})+c_{2}\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1})
,
the bounds in
Theorem
8.1

and Corollary
8.2

lead to the
the following result.

Theorem 9.1
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=c_{2}\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1})
for some
\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2}>0
.
Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}X_{i}(t)>0\right)\leq n\,i_{0}\,\exp\left(\beta\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\lambda(W)-\delta\right).

In particular if
\displaystyle\lambda(W)<\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\frac{\delta}{\beta}
, then the disease asymptotically vanishes with exponential decay and the extinction time
\displaystyle\tau
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\tau]\leq\frac{\log n+1}{\delta-\beta\lambda(W)c_{2}/c_{1}}.

Likewise note that

\displaystyle\max\{0,x-c\}\leq\frac{e_{\max}-1-c}{e_{\max}-1}x,

where we recall that
\displaystyle e_{\max}
is the largest size of a hyperedge of
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}
.
Hence we deduce the following result.

Theorem 9.2
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max\{0,x-c\}
for some
\displaystyle c>0
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}X_{i}(t)>0\right)\leq n\,i_{0}\,\exp\left(\beta\frac{e_{\max}-1-c}{e_{\max}-1}\lambda(W)-\delta\right).

In particular if

\lambda(W)<\left(\frac{e_{\max}-1}{e_{\max}-1-c}\right)\frac{\delta}{\beta},

then the disease asymptotically vanishes with exponential decay and the extinction time
\displaystyle\tau
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\tau]\leq\frac{\log n+1}{\delta-\beta\frac{e_{\max}-1-c}{e_{\max}-1}\lambda(W)}.

10
Discussion

In this work we derived several
spectral conditions that control the
spread of disease in an SIS model on a hypergraph.
The conditions have the general form

\beta\,\lambda(W)\,c_{f}/\delta<1,

(11)

where
\displaystyle c_{f}>0
is a constant depending on the
function
\displaystyle f

that determines the nonlinear infection rate within a hyperedge.

We note that in the special case where
(i) the hypergraph
is an undirected graph and hence
\displaystyle W
becomes the binary adjacency matrix,
and
(ii) we have linear dependence on the number of infectious neighbors for the infection rate of a node, so
\displaystyle f
is the identity function,
the condition
(
11
)
reduces to the
well-known vanishing spectral condition
studied in, for example,
(
[
11
,
18
,
21
]
).

There are two important points to be made about the
general form of
(
11
).
First, the hypergraph structure appears only via the presence of the symmetric matrix

\displaystyle W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Recall that

\displaystyle W_{ij}
records
the number of times that

\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
both appear in the same
hyperedge.
Such weighted but
pairwise

information is all that feeds into this
spectral
threshold.
On a positive note, this implies that useful
predictions can be made about
disease spread on a hypergraph without
full knowledge of the types of hyperedge
present and the distribution of nodes within them.
(For example, when collecting human interaction data it is more reasonable to ask an individual to list each
neighbour and state how many different ways they
interact with that neighbour than to ask
an individual to list all hyperedges they take part in.)
However,
this observation also raises the possibility that
more refined analysis might lead to
sharper
bounds, perhaps at the expense of simplicity and interpretability.

Our second point is that
the new vanishing condition
(
11
)
neatly separates three aspects:

(a)

The biologically-motivated infection parameter,
\displaystyle\beta
.

(b)

The interaction
structure, captured in
\displaystyle\lambda(W)
.

(c)

The
coefficient
\displaystyle c_{f}
that arises from
modelling the nonlinear infection process.
For instance, Theorem
6.1
and Theorem
6.4
have
\displaystyle c_{f}=f^{\prime}(0)
. In the collective contagion model case
\displaystyle f(x)=c_{2}\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1})
, Theorem
9.1
indicates that we can take
\displaystyle c_{f}=c_{2}/c_{1}
.

We may view
\displaystyle\beta
as an invariant biological constant
that reflects the underlying virulence of the disease
and is not affected by human behaviour.
The factor
\displaystyle\lambda(W)
, which arises from the interaction structure, will be determined by
regional and cultural issues, including
population density,
age demographics,
typical household sizes, and the
nature of prevalent commercial and manufacturing activities.
Interventions, including full or partial lockdowns,
could be modeled through a change in
\displaystyle\lambda(W)
.
The third factor,
\displaystyle c_{f}
,
is strongly dependent upon
human behaviour and may be adjusted to reflect
individual-based containment strategies such as social distancing, mask wearing or more frequent hand washing.

This work has focused on modelling, analysis and interpretation at the abstract level, concentrating on the
fundamental question of disease extinction.
Having developed this theory,
it would, of course, now be of great interest to
perform practical experiments using realistic interaction and infection data, with the aim of

•

calibrating model parameters,

•

testing hypotheses about the appropriate functional form of the infection rate,

•

testing the predictive power of the modeling framework, especially in comparison with
simpler homogeneous mixing and pairwise interaction versions,

•

quantifying the effect of different interventions.
