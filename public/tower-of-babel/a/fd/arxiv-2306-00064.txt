Multiphoton Spectroscopy of a Dynamical Axion Insulator Preprint: APS/123-QED
Olivia Liebman, Jonathan B. Curtis, Ioannis Petrides, Prineha Narang

Abstract
The unusual magnetoelectric transport present in Weyl semimetals and 3D topological insula- tors can be compactly understood as manifestations of a background axion field, which itself is determined by the microscopic band structure. In the presence of correlations, an additional axion quasiparticle may emerge as the collective excitations on top of the mean background field. Such modes couple nonlinearly to electric and magnetic fields, giving rise to a dynamical magnetoelectric response. However, unambiguous identification of this collective axion mode is challenging due to its inherent nonlinear dynamics. Here, we propose an all-optical protocol that utilizes a pump-probe setup for verifying and characterizing the transient dynamics of axion fields in three-dimensional insulator systems. In particular, we show that nonlinear Raman processes induce dynamical oscillations of the axion field that depend on the geometry of the incident electromagnetic fields. These oscillations manifest in the polarization and magnetization of the material, hence, can be subsequently measured using time-resolved Kerr rotation spectroscopy. Our results open a pathway towards using multi-photon and quantum pair spectroscopies to identify new correlated phases of quantum matter.

Introduction

Axionic particles were originally proposed in high-energy physics in order to solve the charge-parity problem as they were associated to the Nambu-Goldstone bosons of a new global axial U(1) symmetry
Peccei and Quinn 1977a
;
Peccei and Quinn 1977b

Today, axions are a leading dark matter candidate and could help explain the matter-antimatter asymmetry present in the universe
Chadha-Day et al. 2022
.
While the axion has yet to be discovered in particle physics, its condensed matter counterpart has been theorized to exist in certain three-dimensional materials
Nenno et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2010
;
Wang and Zhang 2013
;
Roy and Sau 2015
;
Wan et al. 2011
;
Pace et al. 2021
;
Petrides and Zilberberg 2022

A characteristic identifier of the presence of an axion is a signature magnetoelectric response, leading to a modified form of Maxwell’s equations known as axion electrodynamics
Sekine and Nomura 2020
;
Zyuzin et al. 2012
;
Essin et al. 2009
;
these give rise to physical phenomena which can broadly be classified as either transport or density responses.
Axion-induced transport includes anomalous Hall currents which are related to the gradient of the axion, and a magnetic conductivity that relates to the axion’s first time derivative.
Density responses have to do with the topological magnetoelectric effect where the axion acts as the coupling constant between magnetic field and polarization; and similarly between electric field and magnetization.

In correlated systems, the axion field has an associated collective excitation which couples
nonlinearly
to the electromagnetic field; this induces a dynamical magnetoelectric effect on top of the mean field response due to the background axion field

;
Wieder et al. 2020

Sehayek et al. 2020

McKay and Bradlyn 2021

A variety of candidate materials have been put forward where axion field effects manifest in the electronic band structure.
For example, axion fields naturally appear in correlated Weyl semimetals that undergo lattice translation symmetry breaking due to the emergence of charge density wave (CDW) order.
In this case, the mean background axion field is determined by the separation of Weyl points in momentum space and in energy; while its dynamical contribution is associated with collective excitations of the phase of the CDW, i.e., the Nambu-Goldstone bosons
Zyuzin and Burkov 2012
;
Shi et al. 2021

In the presence of uncompensated carrier densities the softening of the axionic collective mode may even lead to spatially inhomogeneous correlated phases

3D topological insulators (TI) can also support a static, background axion field which, due to time-reversal symmetry, acts as a

When interactions are turned on that spontaneously break time-reversal symmetry, as in the case of antiferromagnetic (AFM) order,

Here, the fluctuations of the axion field are induced by the spin-wave excitations of the AFM order parameter

The emergence of axion physics in condensed matter systems has ignited hope of studying such elusive physics in a tangible, tabletop experimental setting.
While the high energy axion continues to go undetected,
measuring axion quasiparticles in condensed matter systems have posed its own theoretical and experimental challenges due its inherent nonlinear coupling.
Prominent techniques rely on studying linear perturbations in the presence of a strong magnetic field, for example in measuring the attenuated total reflection
Li et al. 2010

Piezomagnetic probes have also been used to identify the dynamical bulk orbital magnetization induced by strain

Li et al. 2010

These approaches have proven valuable for detecting equilibrium responses, however, collective dynamics often rely on nonlinearities that induce a more complex evolution.

Here, we define an all-optical, contactless protocol for the identification of nonlinear signatures of dynamical axion excitations in a three-dimensional insulating system, termed
dynamical axion insulator

We keep the approach general such that the results can be applied to any system which hosts axionic excitations.
This protocol breaks down into two parts; first a dynamical axion mode is resonantly excited from a background axion field through two-photon Raman processes.
Specifically, by solving the dynamics of electromagnetic fields coupled nonlinearly to collective axion modes we show that the transient evolution of the latter carries a distinct dependence on the incident angle, polarization, and frequencies of the two photons.
Following excitation, the induced axion electrodynamics are then detected through a third probe beam, which monitors the oscillations of the Kerr angle and acts as a faithful proxy for tracking the dynamical evolution of the axion field in the material

;
Ahn et al. 2022

Otey et al. 2010

In addition, our work demonstrates the potential of exploiting entanglement pairs to probe correlated phases and engineer strong nonlinear optical responses in quantum materials.

Axion electrodynamics

We start by considering the axion-modified electromagnetic Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon\bm{E}^{2}-\frac{1}{\mu}\bm{B}^{2}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\theta\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}.

(1)

The first two terms are the sourceless Maxwell Lagrangian in terms of the electric (magnetic) fields
\bm{E}
(
\bm{B}
), and
\epsilon
(
\mu
) is the permittivity (permeability) tensor.
The last term proportional to
\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}
is the so-called axion response that leads to a magnetoelectric coupling mediated by the
\theta
term, and
\alpha\approx\frac{1}{137}
is the fine-structure constant. Note that natural units
c=\hbar=1
are used throughout unless otherwise specified.

From the axion-modified electromagnetic Lagrangian (
1
) the current density is given by

\bm{J}=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\nabla\theta\times\bm{E}+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\partial_{t}\theta\bm{B},

(2)

which quantifies the electromagnetic response of the system due to the axion term.
The first term in the above equation is equivalent to an anomalous Hall response proportional to the spatial gradient of the axion field, while the second term is known as the chiral magnetic effect and is only relevant in the presence of time-dependent axion fields.

In a dynamical axion insulator,
\theta
is a degree of freedom which has its own equations of motion and may depend on space and time,
\theta(\bm{r},t)=\theta_{0}(\bm{r},t)+\delta\theta(\bm{r},t)
,
where the first term is the background axion, while the second term
\delta\theta(\bm{r},t)
is the
dynamical axion mode
.

\theta_{0}
is determined from the microscopic band structure alone, whereas
\delta\theta
is a dynamical field with distinct equations of motion that couple nonlinearly to the electromagnetic fields.

When this is the case, the electromagnetic Lagrangian of Eq. (
1
) is supplemented by the equations of motion of a spinless bosonic field that characterizes the dynamical axion mode, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm NG}=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left[\left(\partial_{t}\delta\theta\right)^{2}-\Omega_{0}^{2}\delta\theta^{2}\right],

(3)

where
\kappa
is a materials-dependent parameter, to be given a physical interpretation as “compressibility” later on when we consider the specific case of an axion insulator.
We have also included a finite gap for the collective mode
\sim\Omega_{0}
, which reflects the possibility of a resonance at finite frequency, e.g., due to collective mode pinning from disorder.
As we are dealing with electrodynamics, we assume that the speed at which the axion propagates is orders of magnitude slower than the speed of light; as such the axion is effectively dispersionless provided it has a finite gap
\Omega_{0}
.
Only in the case of the gap
\Omega_{0}
going to zero would the axion dispersion become important.
This can easily be seen from the axion dispersion, given as
\omega^{2}=(\nu k)^{2}+\Omega_{0}^{2}
for frequency
\omega
, speed
\nu
, and momentum
k
of the axion.
Comparing to the speed of light,
c\gg\nu
so that
all terms relating to first gradients of the dynamical axion field,
\nabla\delta\theta
, can be safely ignored; these describe effects from axion dispersion through the medium.

The equation of motion for the dynamical axion mode
\delta\theta
are given by

\kappa\left(\partial_{t}^{2}+\gamma\partial_{t}+\Omega_{0}^{2}\right)\delta\theta=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B},

(4)

where we have included a phenomenologically relevant dissipation term
\kappa\gamma\partial_{t}\delta\theta
, with damping rate
\gamma
, which represents the coupling of the order parameter to other degrees of freedom in the bulk.
The above equation describes the dynamics of a massive spinless bosonic field, driven by an effective force
\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}
.
Thus as long as the electric and magnetic fields have some non-orthogonal component the dynamical axion mode can be excited.
In the following, we show how the solutions of these nonlinear coupled equations depend on the different configurations of the driving term
\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}
and how can this be utilized in selectively exciting the dynamical axion.

II
Results

II.1
Dynamical axion generation

We now describe the protocol to excite a dynamical axion mode, where the approach is kept general and may apply to any axionic, correlated system which may endow the axion field with intrinsic dynamics.
For ease of calculation we switch to the electromagnetic gauge field notation which relates the electric and magnetic fields
\bm{E}=-\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A}
to the scalar potential
\phi
and vector potential
\bm{A}
.
For simplicity, we take the Weyl gauge where
\phi=0
.
Perturbatively expanding
\delta\theta
and
\bm{A}
in the small parameter
\alpha
, i.e., the fine structure constant,

\displaystyle\bm{A}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{A}^{(0)}+\alpha\bm{A}^{(1)}+\alpha^{2}\bm{A}^{(2)}+\alpha^{3}\bm{A}^{(3)}+...,

(5)

\displaystyle\delta\theta(\bm{x},t)=\delta\theta^{(0)}+\alpha\delta\theta^{(1)}+\alpha^{2}\delta\theta^{(2)}+\alpha^{3}\delta\theta^{(3)}+...,

(6)

plugging these expansions into Eq. (
4
) and collecting terms via their order in
\alpha
, the lowest surviving order to excite a dynamical axion is
\delta\theta^{(1)}

\kappa(\partial_{t}^{2}+\gamma\partial_{t}+\Omega_{0}^{2})\delta\theta^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{t}\bm{A}^{(0)}(\nabla\times\bm{A}^{(0)}).

(7)

Performing a Fourier transform of the above expression and simplifying (see Methods section
IV.1
for detailed derivation)

\displaystyle\delta\theta^{(1)}(q)=\frac{1}{\pi\kappa}\left[-\Omega^{2}+i\gamma\Omega+\Omega_{0}^{2}\right]^{-1}\times

\displaystyle\int_{q_{1},q_{2}}\delta_{q_{1}+q_{2},q}\omega_{1}\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{1})\cdot(\bm{q_{2}}\times\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{2})).

(8)

Here
\delta\theta^{(1)}(q)
is the first order dynamical axion response, where
q=(\Omega,\bm{q})
is the four-momenta, with frequency
\Omega
and momentum
\bm{q}
.
The zeroth order gauge field
\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{i})
for
i=1,2
describes an electromagnetic field with four-momentum
q_{i}=(\omega_{i},\bm{q}_{i})
for frequency
\omega_{i}
and momentum
\bm{q}_{i}
.

Exciting a dynamical axion mode in a material is a nonlinear optical process that minimally requires interactions with two electromagnetic fields, see Eq. (
4
).
As such we consider the following plane-wave ansatz
\bm{A}^{(0)}(q)=\mathbfcal{A}_{1}\delta_{q,q_{1}}+\mathbfcal{A}_{2}\delta_{q,q_{2}}
, composed of two waves with distinct frequency and momentum, whose field amplitudes
\mathbfcal{A}_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{i}\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{i}
are related to the polarizations
\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{i}
which obey
\bm{q}_{i}\cdot\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{i}=0
.
Using the above ansatz we arrive at our first key result: the response of the dynamical axion at first order
\delta\theta=\alpha\delta\theta^{(1)}+O(\alpha^{2})
to the application of external electromagnetic fields
\bm{A}^{(0)}(q)

\displaystyle\delta\theta(q)=\frac{(\alpha/\pi\kappa)\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{A}_{2}}{\Omega^{2}+i\Omega\gamma-\Omega_{0}^{2}}\left(\frac{\bm{q}_{1}}{\omega_{1}}-\frac{\bm{q}_{2}}{\omega_{2}}\right)\cdot\left(\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}_{1}}\times\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}_{2}}\right),

(9)

where axion frequency is
\Omega=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
and axion momentum
\bm{q}=\bm{q}_{1}+\bm{q}_{2}
due to energy-momentum conservation.
In general, the intensity of the axion response depends on the frequency, momenta, polarization, and angle of the incident beams, see Fig.
1
(a).

Importantly, the amplitude of oscillations given in Eq. (
9
) depend on: (i) a geometric volume part defined by the two polarization vectors
\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{i}
and the three-momentum
q_{i}/\omega_{i}
of the incident electromagnetic fields, and (ii) a frequency-dependent Lorentzian-type response.
As the former is a second order nonlinear optical process, there are essentially two channels for three-wave mixing: two-photon absorption (understood as second harmonic generation or sum frequency generation depending on if the two incident frequencies are equal or not), as shown in a Feynman-like diagram Fig.
2
(a); and stimulated Raman scattering (understood as difference frequency generation), as shown in Fig.
2
(b).
The dependence on the excitation channel can be understood geometrically as the parallelpiped volume made by the polarization unit vectors,
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}
, and the vector composed of the three-momenta
\bm{q}_{1}/\omega_{1}-\bm{q}_{2}/\omega_{2}
, see Fig.
2
(c).
Its contribution to the amplitude response of the axion is thus constrained by the allowed geometry of the excitation beams.
The excitation scheme that maximizes this volume corresponds to beams with orthogonal polarizations and which are also orthogonal with respect to their relative three-momentum
\bm{q}_{1}/\omega_{1}-\bm{q}_{2}/\omega_{2}
.

We explore the efficacy of this two-photon excitation protocol for a variety of different incident beam angles
\varphi
and frequencies
\omega_{i}
in the maximally-crossed polarization geometry, i.e., we keep the polarizations of the excitation beams orthogonal
\hat{\varepsilon}_{1}\cdot\hat{\varepsilon}_{2}=0
, c.f., Fig.
1
.
In Fig.
3
, we show the induced dynamical axion amplitude
|\delta\theta|^{2}
as a function of incident photon frequencies
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
for different incident angles,
\varphi=0,\pi/4
, and
\pi/2
.
Depending on the incident angle
\varphi
made by the beams, we find two distinct mechanisms that can be used to excite the dynamical axion mode.
First, when the excitation beams are close to head-on, i.e.,
\varphi=0
, the two-photon absorption channel appears in the upper half-plane, as shown in Fig.
3
(a); when the excitation frequencies match the axion’s frequency, i.e.,
\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}=\Omega_{0}
, the amplitude of oscillations is maximized for this case.

On the other hand, for parallel-propagating collinear beams, i.e.,
\varphi=\pi/2
, this channel is entirely suppressed and instead the stimulated (inelastic) Raman excitation channel emerges in the lower half-plane, see Fig.
3
(b).
Note in this case the relative momentum of the two beams is zero and therefore there must be a finite relative-frequency. As indicated by the color bar, this beam configuration achieves the strongest axion response. This can be understood by again referring to Eq. (
9
) as well as Fig.
2
(c). For configurations where
\omega_{1}>0
and
\omega_{2}<0
, this will maximize the vector
\bm{q}_{1}/\omega_{1}-\bm{q}_{2}/\omega_{2}
which will become a sum instead of a difference. In turn this will maximize the parallelpiped volume of Fig.
2
(c), as the polarization vectors
\hat{\varepsilon_{i}}
are kept fixed.

At intermediate incident angles, e.g.,
\varphi=\pi/4
, both channels are active, see Fig.
3
(c).
Due to the dependence of the response to the geometric volume given in Eq. (
9
), the two-photon absorption channel is significantly weaker as compared to the stimulated Raman scattering channel.
We, hence, separate the two half-planes at
y=0
to resolve these responses.
Fig.
3
clearly shows how varying degrees of freedom of the incident beam can excite the axion field, with the inelastic stimulated Raman channel for collinear beams resulting in the strongest response.

II.2
Axion detection

Now that the protocol for exciting an axion collective mode has been established, we move on to demonstrate how it can be detected via an optical signature.
The key idea is to use a linearly polarized detection beam of known frequency at normal incidence, to allow for optical detection of the axion field by its effect on the Kerr angle of the reflected light; a technique well established for the spectroscopy of static axion responses
Wu et al. 2016
.

The proposed pump-probe style protocol is laid out in Fig.
1
, where the pump beam excites the dynamical axion mode in the bulk and is subsequently probed through the vacuum by the reflected field.
The background axion in the material is assumed to break both inversion and time-reversal symmetry, such that it gives rise to a bulk Hall conductivity
\nabla\theta_{0}
, and bulk magnetic conductivity
\partial_{t}{\theta}_{0}
, respectively [c.f. Eq. (
2
)].
We note that in the presence of time-reversal symmetry the Hall effect, related to
\nabla\theta_{0}
, will vanish; correspondingly, in the presence of inversion symmetry the chiral magnetic effect, related to
\partial_{t}\theta_{0}
, will vanish.

The dispersion relation of electromagnetic fields in the material is obtained by solving the equations of motion of the electromagnetic field

\epsilon\partial^{2}_{t}{\bm{A}}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla^{2}\bm{A}=\partial_{t}\bm{J},

(10)

where
\epsilon
is the relative permittivity,
\mu
is the relative permeability, and
\bm{J}
is the current given in Eq. (
2
).
Without loss of generality we assume a non-magnetic, spatially isotropic medium such that the permeability and permittivity tensors are simplified to
\mu^{-1}=\mathds{1}
and
\epsilon=\epsilon\mathds{1}
, where
\epsilon
is a constant characterizing the material.
Furthermore, we consider propagation along the z-axis and solve the equations of motion in the plane orthogonal to the direction of propagation.
In this geometry, the natural basis is given by
\bm{A}_{\pm}=A_{\pm}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1,\pm i\right)^{T}
where
A_{\pm}
is an amplitude prefactor, with corresponding dispersion
k=\omega n_{\pm}
.
The refractive index given by (see Methods section
IV.2
)

\displaystyle n_{\pm}=\left(\frac{\epsilon\pm\epsilon^{\prime}}{1\pm\mu^{\prime}}\right)^{1/2}

(11)

where
\epsilon^{\prime}=\omega^{-1}{\partial_{z}\theta}
is related to the electric conductivity and
\mu^{\prime}=k^{-1}{\partial_{t}\theta}
is related to the magnetic conductivity.

The reflectance and transmittance amplitudes can be obtained via the continuity of electric and total magnetic fields at the interface, given as
\bm{E}^{I}+\bm{E}^{R}=\bm{E}^{T}
, and
\bm{B}^{I}+\bm{B}^{R}=\bm{B}^{T}+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\delta\theta\bm{E}^{T}
(see Methods section
IV.2
).
Without loss of generality, here we only include the dynamical axion
\delta\theta
contributions to the Fresnel equations, and set the background axion at the boundary to zero.
Any such contributions, as in
\mathds{Z}_{2}
topological insulators where the axion takes quantized values, can be incorporated in the equilibrium position of the dynamical axion mode
\delta\theta
.
Using the matching conditions, the coefficients of reflection are computed as

R_{\pm}=\pm\frac{1-(n_{\pm}\mp i\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\delta\theta)}{1+(n_{\pm}\mp i\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\delta\theta)},

(12)

where
R_{\pm}=\frac{A_{\pm}^{R}}{A_{\pm}^{I}}
is the ratio between the reflected and initial amplitudes of the fields.
The reflection coefficients are, in general, different depending on the refractive index of left- and right-circularly polarized light, and the dynamical axion angle.
From this, the complex Kerr rotation angle
\Phi_{K}=\Theta_{K}+i\eta_{K}
may be computed, where the real part
\Theta_{K}
is termed the polar Kerr rotation, while the imaginary part
\eta_{K}
is the polar Kerr ellipticity. The polar Kerr rotation relates to the argument of the reflection coefficients,
R_{\pm}=|R_{\pm}|e^{i\Delta_{\pm}}
, as
\Theta_{K}=-\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{+}-\Delta_{-})
, where
\Delta_{\pm}
are the phases of the respective reflection coefficient.
Meanwhile the polar Kerr ellipticity is related to the amplitudes as
\eta_{K}=(R_{+}-R_{-})/(R_{+}+R_{-})

Sato and Ishibashi 2022
.

The polar Kerr ellipticity
\eta_{K}
arises solely from the bulk background axion field and the associated change in the refractive index
n_{\pm}
, see Eq. (
11
).
On the other hand, the polar Kerr rotation
\Theta_{K}
presented here is entirely distinct from this.
It is related to the collective fluctuations of the DAI, therefore, generating a dynamical response of the polar Kerr rotation angle.
We note that an additional nontrivial background axion field at the boundary can even lead to a quantized Kerr response
Wu et al. 2016
;
Ahn et al. 2022
.

We linearize Eq. (
12
) for small
\delta\theta
and focus on the real part of the complex Kerr rotation,
\Theta_{K}
, given by

\displaystyle\Theta_{K}=-\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{+}-\Delta_{-})\approx\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\frac{(-2+n_{+}^{2}+n_{-}^{2})}{(n_{+}^{2}-1)(n_{-}^{2}-1)}\delta\theta.

(13)

The final result of this work is contained within the above equation, which shows the modulation of the reflected beam’s polar Kerr rotation angle and its dependence on the axion field fluctuations, c.f., Eq. (
9
). This serves as our smoking gun optical signature to unambiguously deduce the presence of a dynamical axion field in the material.

The transient dynamics of the polar Kerr rotation
\Theta_{K}
approximately tracking the dynamical axion field oscillations is depicted in Fig.
4
.
We emphasize that the amplitudes shown are not to scale, rather this figure is meant to illustrate the coherence between
\Theta_{K}
and
\delta\theta
.
The axion equation of motion Eq. (
4
) can be understood as a driven, damped harmonic oscillator, whereby following excitation the damping term
\gamma
causes a ring down of the axion field.
To simplify the dynamics, we take an effective force given by a Gaussian wavepacket
\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}\propto e^{(t/\sigma)^{2}}cos(\Omega_{D}t)
, where
\sigma
is the spread, and
\Omega_{D}
is the driving frequency.
As a result, the dynamical axion mode and polar Kerr rotation angle oscillate in-phase at the frequency of the drive, with the amplitude maximized when on-resonance, i.e., when
\Omega_{D}=\Omega_{0}
.

Manifestations of excited axion collective modes in transient Kerr angle rotation.
Using the protocol established here, the axion is excited by a pump profile taken as
\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}\propto e^{(t/\sigma)^{2}}cos(\Omega_{D}t)
, which is tuned to be in two-photon resonance with the dynamical axion, i.e.,
\Omega_{D}=\Omega_{0}
.
This induces out-of-phase collective oscillations of the axion angle, shown above as
\delta\theta(t)
, with the relative phase determined by the resonant driving condition and the axion dumping parameter
\gamma
; for on-resonance driving it takes the value of
\pi/2
.
These oscillations then manifest in the time-dependence of the Kerr-angle
\Theta_{K}(t)
which approximately track the axion field. We note that the amplitudes depicted here are not to scale.

III
Discussion

Up to now the treatment has been generic for any three-dimensional axionic material in the presence of strong interactions where the axion becomes a fluctuating field.
The minimal model of such a dynamical axion insulator is defined by a set of four, anti-commuting elements, that form the basis of the four-dimensional Clifford algebra
Cl_{4}
, and a chiral operator
Qi et al. 2008
;
Petrides and Zilberberg 2022
.
The phenomenology of this model is equivalent to a correlated Weyl fermion, where the low-energy Hamiltonian is determined by a three-dimensional linear dispersion and a mass with two degrees of freedom, e.g., amplitude and phase.
The topology of the ground state at half-filling is characterized by an axion field, which can even become quantized when chiral symmetry is preserved.
Within this model, the axion field is intimately related to strong interactions which can endow a dynamical behavior; specific material realizations are given by the corresponding irreducible representations.

For example, in the case of a Weyl semimetal with charge density wave order the form of the Hamiltonian expanded near the degeneracy points is

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{k})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}v_{F}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{k}&\Delta\\
\bar{\Delta}&-v_{F}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{k}\end{array}\right),

where
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity,
\bm{\sigma}
is the triplet of Pauli matrices,
\bm{k}
is the crystal momentum, and
\Delta=|\Delta|e^{i\theta}
is the CDW term and is analogous to a complex mass term, also represented in terms of the gap amplitude
|\Delta|
and phase
\theta
.
In an uncorrelated Weyl semimetal with no CDW the nodes are gapless and the chirality of the Weyl nodes is given by the topological charge associated to each band crossing, which is identical to the Berry flux
C=+1
(
C=-1
) for the right- (left-) handed Weyl particles
Yan and Felser 2017
for a spinful system. This chiral charges can be understood as magnetic monopoles, or sources and sinks, of the Berry curvature which become singular at the nodes and act as an effective magnetic field in reciprocal space
Burkov 2018
.

In the presence of sufficiently strong interactions, a transition to a CDW ordered phase with complex order parameter
\Delta
can occur.
If the order is incommensurate, i.e., the wave vector of the CDW is not a rational multiple of the reciprocal lattice vector, by Goldstone’s theorem this will have a soft sliding mode associated with the phase of the CDW order, i.e.,
\theta
.
We note that for the case of commensurate CDWs the equations of motion for the dynamical axion field (
4
) are supplemented by a confining potential that modifies the spectrum of possible excitations.
In this regime, the collective modes are equivalent to a massive Goldstone boson with a finite energy at zero momentum due to the commensuration potential.
As the excitation mechanism given by the right-hand side of Eq. (
4
), i.e.,
\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}
, is independent of the particular form of the homogeneous solutions, the dynamical axion can still be excited and probed, given that its zero-momentum energy is with reach of the excitation beams’ frequencies.

Due to the chiral anomaly, it is known that the phase of the complex CDW can be identified with the axion response
Wang and Zhang 2013
of the material. The total CDW phase
\theta
is given by

\displaystyle\theta(\bm{r},t)=\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{r}+Q_{0}t+\delta\theta(\bm{r},t)\,,

(17)

where the first two terms are dubbed the background axion field
\theta_{0}=\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{r}+Q_{0}t
and are determined by the band structure of the material: the separation in momentum-space
\bm{Q}
, and energy
Q_{0}
, between the Weyl nodes.
The former is non-zero only in the case of time-reversal symmetry breaking, while the latter is non-zero for inversion-symmetry breaking systems.
The final term
\delta\theta(\bm{r},t)
is the dynamical axion mode, which can be understood as the collective mode associated to the sliding phase mode of the CDW.
In this case, the parameter
\kappa
in Eq. (
3

To conclude, we have shown that, for a generic, three-dimensional, axion insulating system with strong interactions, a two-step excitation-detection protocol may be used to unambiguously detect the presence of a dynamical axion mode in the bulk via its effects on the polar Kerr rotation angle.
To model the dynamical axion and determine a novel optical signature we perturbatively solved the nonlinear partial differential equations of Eq. (
4
) describing its dynamics.
We established a two-step, pump-probe style protocol which excites this mode via a two-photon excitation scheme, and subsequently detects it with a third probe beam.
We showed that the intensity of the axion excitation is stronger for parallel collinear beams relative to other beam configurations, understood as the stimulated Raman scattering pathway.
Our key result is the proposed smoking-gun optical signature, manifested in the modulation of the polar Kerr rotation which approximately tracks the oscillations in the underlying axion field.

From a fundamental physics perspective, intuition gleaned from detecting the condensed matter axion may offer insight when mapped back to its counterpart in quantum chromodynamics.
The condensed matter axion
arising in topological materials allows for a more accessible, tabletop setting for studying the more inaccessible world of high energy particle physics.
The QCD axion, possibly within reach of experimental verification by current experiments, represents one of the most compelling pathways beyond the Standard Model, by providing at the same time an elegant solution to the strong CP problem and an excellent dark matter candidate

Our work also has implications for condensed matter axion systems that are being used to detect their high energy counterparts in direct detection dark matter experiments

From an applications perspective, the magnetoelectric coupling mediated by the axion response and its inherent nonlinear dynamics may be of practical value in optical technologies.

IV.1
Derivation of axion response function

Here we provide more detailed derivations of the results presented in the main work. We begin by outlining the steps to excite the axion mode via two externally applied light beams, and arrive at the axion response function. To start, we show how the gauge field and axion equations of motion may be derived from the full axion-modified electrodynamic Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}(\epsilon\bm{E}^{2}-\frac{1}{\mu}\bm{B}^{2})+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\theta\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}+

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}[(\partial_{t}\delta\theta)^{2}-\Omega_{0}^{2}\delta\theta^{2}].

(18)

by utilizing the Euler-Lagrange formalism. To simplify things, we switch to writing in terms of potentials
\bm{E}=-\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A}
where
\phi
is scalar potential and
\bm{A}
is vector potential.

\mathcal{L}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}(-\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}\bm{A})^{2}-\frac{1}{2\mu}(\nabla\times\bm{A})^{2}+\\
\frac{\kappa}{2}\left[(\partial_{t}\delta\theta)^{2}-\Omega_{0}^{2}\delta\theta^{2})\right]+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\theta\left[-\nabla\phi(\nabla\times\bm{A})-(\partial_{t}\bm{A})(\nabla\times\bm{A})\right].

(19)

We choose the Weyl gauge such that
\phi=0
.
The Euler-Lagrange equation for
\bm{A}
is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial(\partial_{t}\bm{A})}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial(\nabla\bm{A})}\right)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{A}}=0.

(20)

Varying with respect to
\bm{A}
yields its equation of motion

\epsilon\partial^{2}_{t}\bm{A}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\nabla\times\bm{A}=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\partial_{t}\theta\nabla\times\bm{A}.

(21)

Similarly for the axion field

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial(\partial_{t}\theta)}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial(\nabla\theta)}\right)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}=0.

(22)

and the corresponding equation of motion

\kappa(\partial_{t}^{2}+\gamma\partial_{t}+\Omega_{0}^{2})\delta\theta=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}[\partial_{t}\bm{A}(\nabla\times\bm{A})].

(23)

As stated in the main text, terms proportional to
\nabla\delta\theta
may be dropped by observing the speed of the axion (i.e. speed of sound) is much slower as compared to the speed of light in the medium, as such we assume the axion is dispersionless. This assumption is relaxed only when we consider the discontinuity that occurs in
\nabla\delta\theta
at the boundary between medium and vacuum. This is discussed in greater detail when we consider boundary conditions in Methods section
IV.3
.

The goal is to solve for the axion response to an externally applied electromagnetic field. This is first carried out by perturbatively expanding for
\delta\theta
and
\bm{A}
with the fine structure constant,
\alpha
, as the small parameter

\displaystyle\bm{A}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{A}^{(0)}+\alpha\bm{A}^{(1)}+\alpha^{2}\bm{A}^{(2)}+\alpha^{3}\bm{A}^{(3)}+...

(24)

\displaystyle\delta\theta(\bm{x},t)=\delta\theta^{(0)}+\alpha\delta\theta^{(1)}+\alpha^{2}\delta\theta^{(2)}+\alpha^{3}\delta\theta^{(3)}+...

(25)

We are chiefly concerned with how the axion responds to interactions with incident light. As such, we include only relevant equations to this end.
Plugging in the above expansions into the Eq. (
23
) axion equation of motion, and collecting terms according to their order in
\alpha
, we can determine the lowest surviving order. We take the constant term
\delta\theta^{(0)}=0
as there are no dynamical axion excitations to start:

\kappa(\partial_{t}^{2}+\gamma\partial_{t}+\Omega_{0}^{2})\delta\theta^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\pi}(\partial_{t}\bm{A}^{(0)}))(\nabla\times\bm{A}^{(0)})).

(26)

The lowest order in
\delta\theta
is second order in the fields. Thus the nonlinear coupling of
\delta\theta
to
\bm{A}
is a second order process, and minimally requires two interactions with electromagnetic fields to excite.

With this in mind, we solve for
\delta\theta^{(1)}
by Fourier transforming Eq. (
26
). Note the vector potential Fourier transforms as
\bm{A}(x_{i})=\int_{q}e^{iq_{i}\cdot x}\bm{A}(q_{i})
; and we denote
\int_{q}=\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}
, and
q\cdot x=\bm{q}\cdot\bm{x}-\Omega t
in the exponential. The left-hand side is

\displaystyle\kappa\int d^{4}xe^{-iq\cdot x}(\partial_{t}^{2}+\gamma\partial_{t}+\Omega_{0}^{2})\delta\theta^{(1)}(x)=

\displaystyle\kappa(-\Omega^{2}+i\gamma\Omega+\Omega_{0}^{2})\int d^{4}xe^{-iq\cdot x}\delta\theta^{(1)}(x)=

\displaystyle\kappa(-\Omega^{2}+i\gamma\Omega+\Omega_{0}^{2})\delta\theta^{(1)}(q),

(27)

where
\Omega
is the frequency of the axion, and
\Omega_{0}
is its resonance.
Inverting the matrix
(-\Omega^{2}+i\Omega+\Omega_{0}^{2})\mathbb{I}
to isolate
\delta\theta^{(1)}(q)
on the left-hand side:

\displaystyle\delta\theta^{(1)}(q,\omega)=[-\Omega^{2}+i\gamma\Omega+\Omega_{0}^{2}]^{-1}(\frac{1}{\pi\kappa})\times

\displaystyle\int d^{4}xe^{-iq\cdot x}[\partial_{t}\bm{A}^{(0)}(x_{1})\cdot\nabla\times\bm{A}^{(0)}(x_{2})]

\displaystyle=[-\Omega^{2}+i\gamma\Omega+\Omega_{0}^{2}]^{-1}(\frac{1}{\pi\kappa})\times

\displaystyle\int d^{4}xe^{-iq\cdot x}[\partial_{t}\int e^{iq_{1}\cdot x}\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{1})\cdot\nabla\times\int e^{iq_{2}\cdot x}\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{2})]

\displaystyle=[-\Omega^{2}+i\gamma\Omega+\Omega_{0}^{2}]^{-1}(\frac{1}{\pi\kappa})\int_{q_{1},q_{2}}\int_{q}d^{4}x\times

\displaystyle e^{-i(q\cdot x-q_{1}\cdot x-q_{2}\cdot x)}(-i\Omega_{1}\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{1}))(i\bm{q_{2}}\times\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{2})).

(28)

Making use of the exponential representation as the Dirac delta function
\int d^{4}xe^{-i(q\cdot x-q_{1}\cdot x-q_{2}\cdot x)}=\delta_{q_{1}+q_{2},q}
simplifies this to

\displaystyle\delta\theta^{(1)}(q)=\frac{1}{\pi\kappa}\left[-\Omega^{2}+i\gamma\Omega+\Omega_{0}^{2}\right]^{-1}\times

\displaystyle\int_{q_{1},q_{2}}\delta_{q_{1}+q_{2},q}\omega_{1}\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{1})\cdot(\bm{q_{2}}\times\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{2})).

(29)

Here
q=(\Omega,\bm{q})
is the axion four-momenta, with axion frequency
\Omega
and momentum
\bm{q}
; with resonant frequency
\Omega_{0}
.
The zeroth order gauge field
\bm{A}^{(0)}(q_{i})
for
i=1,2
describes an incident electromagnetic field with four-momentum
q_{i}=(\omega_{i},\bm{q}_{i})
for frequency
\omega_{i}
and momentum
\bm{q}_{i}
.

Recall the field is minimally composed of two waves whose frequencies must add up to axion frequency
\Omega=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
.
We consider the following plane-wave ansatz
\bm{A}^{(0)}(q)=\mathbfcal{A}_{1}\delta_{q,q_{1}}+\mathbfcal{A}_{2}\delta_{q,q_{2}}
whose field amplitudes
\mathbfcal{A}_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{i}\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{i}
are related to the polarizations
\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{i}
which obey
\bm{q}_{i}\cdot\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{i}=0
. We further assume that the momenta satisfy the free-space dispersion relations so that
|\bm{q}_{i}|=|\omega_{i}|

Using this ansatz with Eq. (
29
) we can write down the response of the dynamical axion to the application of externally applied fields
\bm{A}^{(1)}(q_{1})
and
\bm{A}^{(1)}(q_{2})

\displaystyle\delta\theta(q)=\frac{(\alpha/\pi\kappa)\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{A}_{2}}{\Omega^{2}+i\Omega\gamma-\Omega_{0}^{2}}\left(\frac{\bm{q}_{1}}{\omega_{1}}-\frac{\bm{q}_{2}}{\omega_{2}}\right)\cdot\left(\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}_{1}}\times\bm{\hat{\varepsilon}_{2}}\right),

(30)

where
\Omega=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
since the two incident beams’ frequencies will add up to axion frequency
\Omega
.

IV.2
Index of refraction in the axion insulator

The fluctuations of the axion field are incorporated in the displacement field
\bm{D}=\epsilon\bm{E}-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\theta\bm{B}
and magnetic field strength
\bm{H}=\frac{1}{\mu}\bm{B}+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\theta\bm{E}
, where
\epsilon
(
\mu
) is the relative permittivity (permeability). In addition, we assume that the probe frequency is much higher than the pinning frequency
\Omega_{0}
such that the dynamical axion contributions to the magnetic conductivity can be neglected.

In this regime, the equations of motion for the electromagnetic vector field inside the material can be written in frequency and momentum space as

\omega^{2}\left(\epsilon+i\epsilon^{\prime}\right){\bm{A}}=k^{2}\mu^{-1}\left(1-i\mu^{\prime}\right){\bm{A}},

(31)

where
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\sigma^{E}}{\omega}
is the contribution due to the electric conductivity tensor, and
\mu^{\prime}/\mu=\frac{\sigma^{B}}{k}
is the contribution due to the magnetic conductivity tensor, c.f., Eq. (
2
).
Without loss of generality we assume a non-magnetic, spatially isotropic medium such that the permittivity and permeability tensors are simplified to
\epsilon=\epsilon\mathds{1}
and
\mu^{-1}=\mathds{1}
.
To obtain the dispersion relation of electromagnetic fields it is sufficient to consider propagation along the z-axis and solve the equations of motion in the plane orthogonal to the direction of propagation.
In this geometry, the electric and magnetic conductivity tensors are reduced to
\sigma^{E}_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ij}\partial_{z}\theta
and
\sigma^{B}_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ij}\partial_{t}\theta
, respectively, with
\varepsilon_{ij}
the two-dimensional Levi-Civita tensor.
For simplicity, the contributions of such terms to the equations of motion are written as
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon^{\prime}\sigma_{y}
and
\mu^{\prime}=\mu^{\prime}\sigma_{y}
, where
\epsilon^{\prime}=\partial_{z}\theta/\omega
,
\mu^{\prime}=\partial_{t}\theta/k
, and
\sigma_{y}
is the second Pauli matrix.
Solving Eq. (
31
) naturally yields the chiral basis
\bm{A}_{\pm}=A_{\pm}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1,\pm i\right)^{T}
where
A_{\pm}
is an amplitude prefactor, with corresponding dispersion
k_{\pm}=\omega n_{\pm}
where the refractive index is given by

\displaystyle n_{\pm}^{2}=\frac{\epsilon\pm\epsilon^{\prime}}{1\pm\mu^{\prime}}

(32)

We note that for a time-reversal and inversion symmetry preserving system, the eigenvalues are degenerate
n_{+}=n_{-}
.

IV.3
Boundary conditions and Kerr rotation angle

We start by considering the interface between the vacuum and material as a region that separates a trivial insulator with zero total axion field, from a nontrivial dynamical axion insulator.
We take the axion field to be a continuous function that smoothly goes from zero to its bulk value across the boundary and integrate Maxwell’s equations to find the Frensel equations for the electric and magnetic fields

\displaystyle\bm{E}^{I}+\bm{E}^{R}=\bm{E}^{T},

(33)

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{I}+\bm{B}^{R}=\bm{B}^{T}+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\delta\theta\bm{E}^{T}.

(34)

Due to the onset of interactions, the dynamical axion mode
\delta\theta
experiences a jump at the boundary and, hence, has a non-zero contribution to the Fresnel equations.
We note that any additional contributions to the matching conditions from a nontrivial background axion field at the boundary are neglected; these can be incorporated in the current description by redefining the equilibrium position of the dynamical axion mode
\delta\theta
.

We consider light propagating in the
z
-direction with dispersion in the vacuum given by
\bm{k}=\omega\hat{z}
and in the DAI by
\bm{k}=\omega n_{\pm}\hat{z}
, as above.
We use the notation
\bm{A}_{\pm}=A_{\pm}\hat{e}_{\pm}
where
A_{\pm}
is the amplitude and
\hat{e}_{\pm}
is the circularly polarized basis vectors.
The boundary condition for the electric field gives the simple condition
1+R_{\pm}=T_{\pm}
, where
R_{\pm}={A_{\pm}^{R}}/{A_{\pm}^{I}}
are the reflection coefficients, and
T_{\pm}={A_{\pm}^{T}}/{A_{\pm}^{I}}
are the transmission coefficients.
The boundary condition for magnetic field in the chiral basis is given by

\displaystyle-\hat{z}\times\bm{A}^{I}_{\pm}+\hat{z}\times\bm{A}^{R}_{\pm}=-n_{\pm}\hat{z}\times\bm{A}^{T}_{\pm}-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\delta\theta\bm{A}^{T}_{\pm}.

(35)

Plugging in for the vector potentials and using
\hat{z}\times\hat{e}_{\pm}=\pm i\hat{e}_{\pm}
we obtain

\displaystyle 1-R_{\pm}=\left(n_{\pm}+i\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\delta\theta\right)T_{\pm}.

(36)

Using
1+R_{\pm}=T_{\pm}
and solving for the reflection coefficients gives Eq. (
12
).
