On the Convergence of Non-Integer Linear Hopf Flow
Brendan Guilfoyle, Morgan Robson

Abstract
. The evolution of a rotationally symmetric surface by a linear combination of its radii of curvature equation is considered. It is known that if the coefficients form certain integer ratios the flow is smooth and can be integrated explicitly. In this paper the non-integer case is considered for certain values of the coefficients and with mild analytic restrictions on the initial surface. We prove that if the focal points at the north and south poles on the initial surface coincide, the flow converges to a round sphere. Otherwise the flow converges to a non-round Hopf sphere. Conditions on the fall-off of the astigmatism at the poles of the initial surface are also given that ensure the convergence of the flow. The proof uses the spectral theory of singular Sturm-Liouville operators to construct an eigenbasis for an appropriate space in which the evolution is shown to converge.

Our method of proof involves a spectral expansion in an eigenbasis of the non-integer Legendre operator, defined on the weighted Hilbert space

Section

2.
Rotationally Symmetric Surfaces

The class of surfaces we concern ourselves within this work are elements of the set

\mathscr{W}=\left\{S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}:\parbox{230.00035pt}{$S$ is an embedded $C^{2}$-smooth topological 2-sphere which is rotationally symmetric and strictly convex.}\right\}.

The terminology “topological sphere” which we will abbreviate as just “sphere” is taken to mean a closed surface of genus 0.

A given
\mathcal{S}\in\mathscr{W}
will be orientated with outwards pointing normal
\hat{n}
in a right handed coordinate system
\vec{X}=(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
. Align the
x^{3}
-axis with the axis of rotational symmetry of
\mathcal{S}
.
\mathcal{S}
is parametrized by pushing forward the standard polar coordinates
(\theta,\phi)
of
S^{2}
onto
\mathcal{S}
by the inverse of the Guass map
\mathcal{N}^{-1}:S^{2}\to\mathcal{S}
. Hence
\theta\in[0,\pi]
measures the angle made between the normal vector
\hat{n}
of
\mathcal{S}
, and the axis of rotational symmetry whereas
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
measures the angle made by a clockwise rotation from the
x^{2}x^{3}
-plane.

As a consequence of rotational symmetry many quantities

are independent of
\phi
, in which case we write for short-hand
f(\theta)
instead of
f(\vec{X}(\theta,\phi))
. In particular the radii of curvature of
\mathcal{S}
,
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, associated to the meridian and profile principal foliations respectively, are functions of
\theta
only. Of critical importance to our study is the astigmatism of
\mathcal{S}
given by

s=r_{2}-r_{1}.

If
\mathcal{S}
is assumed strictly convex then the radii are
C^{m}
-smooth functions of the parameter
\theta
whenever
\mathcal{S}
is
C^{m+2}
-smooth. Furthermore
s
vanishes at, and only at, the umbilic points of
\mathcal{S}
. In particular
s(0)=s(\pi)=0
and
s\equiv 0
if and only if
\mathcal{S}
is a round sphere.

Our description of
\mathcal{S}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
will be facilitated by the support function
r:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
r=\vec{X}\cdot\hat{n}
. In the present setting
r
is a function of
\theta
only and
\vec{X}(\theta,\phi)
can be recovered from
r
and its derivatives via:

\displaystyle x^{1}+ix^{2}=(\sin\phi+i\cos\phi)\left(r\sin\theta+\derivative{r}{\theta}\cos\theta\right),

\displaystyle x^{3}=r\cos\theta-\derivative{r}{\theta}\sin\theta.

Remark 2.1
.

For later convenience, note that the focal points
[

]
of
\mathcal{S}
at

and

\displaystyle f_{0}=r(0)-r_{1}(0),

\displaystyle f_{\pi}=-r(\pi)+r_{1}(\pi).

This can be deduced by the above equation for

We collect together some useful relationships between the above quantities on rotationally symmetric surfaces.

Proposition 2.2
.

The following relationships hold

(2.1)

\displaystyle r_{1}=\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\sin\theta}\derivative[]{\theta}\left(\frac{r}{\cos\theta}\right),

\displaystyle r_{2}=\derivative[2]{r}{\theta}+r,

(2.2)

r=C_{2}\cos\theta+C_{1}+\int\sin\theta\left[\int\frac{s}{\sin\theta}d\theta\right]d\theta,

for constants

,

. If in addition surface is

\displaystyle\derivative{r_1}{\theta}=(r_{2}-r_{1})\cot\theta.

Proof.

The derivation of equation (

) can be found in
[

or derived from the definition of the support function. Equations (

∎

Remark 2.3

Given

with support function

at each point along its normal line by a distance

2.1.
The Slope at an Isolated Umbilic

If
\mathcal{S}
possesses isolated umbilic points at its north and south poles, i.e. at
\theta=0,\pi
, then we define the

of

as

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\lim_{\theta\to 0}\left(\frac{r_{2}(\theta)-r_{2}(0)}{r_{1}(\theta)-r_{1}(0)}\right),

\displaystyle\mu_{\pi}=\lim_{\theta\to\pi}\left(\frac{r_{2}(\theta)-r_{2}(\pi)}{r_{1}(\theta)-r_{1}(\pi)}\right).

If
\mathcal{S}
is
C^{3}
-smooth, the umbilic slopes are just
\derivative{r_2}{r_1}
evaluated at
\theta=0
and
\pi
respectively.

We remark that coordinate substitution

corresponds to reversing the direction of the

\mu_{0}
into

and vice-versa. Hence arguments that are made concerning the umbilic at one pole will often hold at the other pole also. When this is the case we will simply say the argument follows by reflection.

The question of what values of umbilic slope are possible on various spheres is an area of active research
[
12
]

[
5
]
. The derived Codazzi-Mainardi equation (

-smooth surface to be rotationally symmetric and has some striking consequences in this direction, two of which are the following

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\in\mathscr{W}
be a
C^{3}
sphere with an isolated umbilic at
\theta=0
. For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

(1)

\mu_{0}>\alpha+1\implies\lim\limits_{\theta\to 0}\left(\frac{\displaystyle{s}}{\displaystyle{\sin^{\alpha}\theta}}\right)=0

(2)

\mu_{0}<\alpha+1\implies\lim\limits_{\theta\to 0}\left(\frac{\displaystyle{s}}{\displaystyle{\sin^{\alpha}\theta}}\right)=\pm\infty

If
\mathcal{S}
has an isolated umbilic at

dictates the behaviour of

as

Proof.

We prove the result for

such that

-smooth,
\derivative{r_2}{\theta}
is continuous and bounded. Therefore
\derivative{r_2}{r_1}
is continuous on
(0,\delta]

From the Codazzi-Mainardi equation and the definition of

\derivative{r_2}{r_1}=1+\frac{\tan\theta}{s}\derivative{s}{\theta}(\theta).

Integrating from
\theta

\displaystyle\left|s\right|

\displaystyle=|s(\delta)|\exp\left\{\int^{\delta}_{\theta}\cot\theta\left(1-\derivative{r_2}{r_1}\right)d\theta\right\}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{s(\delta)}{\sin^{\alpha}\delta}\right|\sin^{\alpha}\theta\cdot\exp\left\{\int^{\delta}_{\theta}\cot\theta\left(\alpha+1-\derivative{r_2}{r_1}\right)d\theta\right\},

we derive the relationship

\displaystyle\left|\frac{s(\theta)}{\sin^{\alpha}\theta}\right|=\left|\frac{s(\delta)}{\sin^{\alpha}\delta}\right|\exp\left\{\int^{\delta}_{\theta}\cot\theta\left(\alpha+1-\derivative{r_2}{r_1}\right)d\theta\right\}

\displaystyle\forall\theta\in(0,\delta].

Now let
\theta\to 0
. If
\mu_{0}>\alpha+1
the quantity in parenthesis diverges to

, implying

. On the other hand if
\mu_{0}<\alpha+1
the quantity in parenthesis diverges to

which implies
|s/\sin^{\alpha}\theta|\to\infty
.
∎

Remark 2.5

Proposition

Theorem 2.6
.

If
\mathcal{S}\in\mathscr{W}
is

and has an isolated umbilic at the north or south pole, then the umbilic slope at that pole when it exists & is finite, takes a value of an odd integer greater than or equal to

.

If furthermore

Let

is strictly convex, rotationally symmetric and smooth,

Now assume for contradiction that all even derivatives also vanish. Then

for all

. In particular for any

\lim_{\theta\to 0}\left(\frac{s}{\sin^{\beta}\theta}\right)=\ldots=\frac{1}{\beta!}s^{(\beta)}(0)=0.

Using the contrapositive of (2) in Proposition

, it follows that

such that

Hence by the converse of (2) in Proposition (

3.
Linear Hopf Flow

3.1.
Stationary Solutions

The stationary solutions of the linear Hopf flow (

(3.1)

0=ar_{1}+br_{2}+c.

The surfaces in
\mathscr{W}
for which a general linear curvature relationship such as equation (

.

Proposition 3.1
.

The linear Hopf spheres which solve equation (

) for given parameter values

,

and

have astigmatisms of the form

and support functions of the form

where

,

and

are constants and

.

Combing the derived Codazzi-Mainardi equation (

) with the curvature relationship (

) results in a separable ODE which is solved to give equation (
3.2
). The support function (

) follows by integrating equation (

) by quadrature as in equation (

).

Any surface with isolated umbilic points satisfying equation (

) necessarily has umbilic slopes taking the common value

Furthermore, if this surface is in

and smooth, Theorem

For this reason the work undertaken in

3.2.

The curvature flow equation (

\displaystyle\partialderivative[]{r}{t}=b\frac{\partial^{2}r}{\partial\theta^{2}}+a\cot\theta\frac{\partial r}{\partial\theta}+(a+b)r+c,

The solution

Under the linear Hopf flow the astigmatism evolves as

Differentiating equation (

Remark 3.4

As expected the astigmatism of the appropriate linear Hopf sphere is stationary under the flow since

A crucial ingredient for solving equation (
3.5
) in the case
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is that the eigenfunctions of
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
are the associated Legendre polynomials. The associated Legendre polynomials,
\text{P}^{n}_{m}(\cos\theta)
, are polynomials in sine and cosine and form an orthogonal basis of
C^{0}[0,\pi]
. In particular they span the higher index terms (
l\geq n
) of the following astigmatism decomposition for smooth surfaces:

(3.7)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\sum\limits^{\infty}_{l=0}(a_{l}+b_{l}\cos\theta)\sin^{2l+2}\theta.

In
[
7
]
this decomposition enabled the flow to be solved analytically when
n\in\mathbb{N}
by projecting the flow equation (
3.5
) into each eigenbasis of
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
and solving for the time dependency of the coefficients
a_{l}(t)
,
b_{l}(t)
.

The term with coefficient
a_{n}
is the astigmatism of the linear Hopf sphere with umbilic slope
2n+3
, therefore in the case of convergence of
s
to a linear Hopf sphere,
b_{l}\to 0
for all
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
a_{l}\to 0
for all
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\backslash\{n\}
as
t\to\infty
, leaving only the linear Hopf term. In the case of convergence to a round sphere,
a_{n}=0
.

We try to emulate the above argument in this paper for
n\notin\mathbb{N}
. In the non-integer case, the eigenfunctions of
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
are no longer trigonometric polynomials and are not terms in the series expansion (
3.7
). Furthermore it is no longer clear if the non-integer Legendre functions are orthogonal or form a basis. To address this we will write the Legendre operator in its Sturm-Liouville form

(3.8)

\mathscr{L}^{\mu}_{\nu}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right)+\nu(\nu+1)\sin\theta-\frac{\mu^{2}}{\sin\theta}\right]

and show using Singular Sturm-Liouville theory that we can find an orthogonal basis in which the surfaces astigmatism can be decomposed.

4.
Singular Sturm-Liouville Operators

In this section we summarize the theory of singular Sturm-Liouville problems following
[
21
]
. In Section
5
the theory will be used to deduce the existence of an orthogonal eigenbasis for the non-integer Legendre operator associated with the linear Hopf flow.

Consider the general Sturm-Liouville operator

(4.1)

T=-\frac{1}{w(x)}\left[\derivative{x}\left(p(x)\derivative{x}\right)+q(x)\right],

where
1/p,q,w\in L_{loc}\big((a,b);\mathbb{R}\big)
are locally Lebesgue integrable real-valued functions on the interval
(a,b)
and
w>0
. Operators of this form are called
singular Sturm-Liouville operators
. The Legendre operator discussed in Section
3
is an example of such.

View
T
as a linear operator on
L^{2}\big((a,b),w(x)dx;\mathbb{C}\big)
, the Hilbert space of complex-valued square integrable functions with weight
w
, denoted simply by
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
. Under the standard inner-product of the
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
spaces

\langle f,g\rangle_{w}=\int^{b}_{a}f(x)\cdot\overline{g(x)}\cdot w(x)dx,

the operator
T
satisfies the so-called Greens formula

(4.2)

\langle Tf,g\rangle_{w}=p(x)\left(f(x)\overline{g^{\prime}(x)}-f^{\prime}(x)\overline{g(x)}\right)\bigg|^{x=b^{-}}_{x=a^{+}}+\langle f,Tg\rangle_{w},

where the evaluation of the boundary term is to be understood as a limit. Greens formula allows us to investigate the symmetry of
T
in
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
so long as the functions
f
and
g
are chosen to be such that the terms in (
4.2
) are well defined. For this purpose the
maximal domain
is introduced:

D_{\text{max}}=\big\{f\in L^{2}_{w}(a,b):f,pf^{\prime}\in\text{AC}_{\text{loc}}(a,b),\enskip Tf\in L^{2}_{w}(a,b)\big\},

where
\text{AC}_{\text{loc}}(a,b)
is the space of functions which are absolutely continuous on all compact intervals of
(a,b)
.

The requirement that
f,pf^{\prime}\in\text{AC}_{\text{loc}}(a,b)
, is enough to ensure that
f
,
pf^{\prime}
are differentiable almost everywhere and their derivatives are Lebesgue integrable, which gives meaning to equation (
4.2
).

In the case that the coefficient
p
satisfies
1/p\in\text{AC}_{\text{loc}}(a,b)
, the description of
D_{\text{max}}
simplifies to

(4.3)

D_{\text{max}}=\big\{f\in L^{2}_{w}(a,b):f^{\prime}\in\text{AC}_{\text{loc}}(a,b),\enskip Tf\in L^{2}_{w}(a,b)\big\},

in particular the elements of
D_{\text{max}}
must be
C^{1}(a,b)
and have second derivative a.e. Such is the case with the Legendre operator (
3.8
).

Sturm-Liouville operators often come supplied with boundary conditions as to make the boundary term in equation (
4.2
) vanish, i.e.

(4.4)

\displaystyle\langle Tf,g\rangle_{w}=\langle f,Tg\rangle_{w}.

These boundary conditions constitute part of
T
’s domain of definition, which naturally must be a subset of
D_{\text{max}}
.

In non-singular Sturm-Liouville theory
1
1

1

which requires the stronger condition
1/p,q,w
are Lebesgue integrable over
(a,b)
the well-known boundary conditions

(4.5)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}f(a)+\alpha_{2}p(a)f^{\prime}(a)=0,

\displaystyle\beta_{1}f(b)+\beta_{2}p(b)f^{\prime}(b)=0,

\displaystyle\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{C},

define a domain
D_{\text{S.A.}}
for
T
which make
T|_{D_{\text{S.A.}}}
self adjoint
[
15
]
. It is for this reason
D_{\text{S.A.}}
will be referred to as a self adjoint domain for
T
.

In singular problems however, the quantities
f(x)
and
p(x)f^{\prime}(x)
may not exist as
x\to a
or
b
, even if
f\in D_{\text{max}}
. Therefore boundary conditions such as (
4.5
) are not appropriate. To facilitate the description of boundary conditions for singular problems the
Lagrange bracket

(4.6)

[f,g]_{p}(x)=p(x)\left(f(x)\overline{g^{\prime}(x)}-f^{\prime}(x)\overline{g(x)}\right),

is introduced. Unlike the terms in boundary condition (
4.5
), the Lagrange bracket
[f,g]_{p}(x)

is
finite in the limits
x\to a,b
so long as
f,g\in D_{\text{max}}
.

In order to give appropriate boundary conditions in the singular case, we first give a definition.

Definition 4.1
.

Given a singular Sturm-Liouville operator
T
, we say
T
is
limit-circle
(LC) at
x=a
if for a given
\chi\in\mathbb{C}
all solutions of the eigenvalue equation

Ty=\chi y,

are in
L^{2}_{w}(a,c)
for some
c\in(a,b)
. Otherwise we say
T
is
limit-point
(LP) at
a
.

Similarly we say
T
is LC at
x=b
if correspondingly
y\in L^{2}_{w}(c,b)
and LP at
b
otherwise.

T
is said to be LC(LP) if it is LC(LP) at both
a
and
b
.

Remark 4.2
.

T
being LC or LP is independent of
\chi\in\mathbb{C}

[
21
]
.

The next theorem states the parallel of boundary condition (
4.5
) for LC Sturm-Liouville operators.

Theorem 4.3
.

[
21
]

Let
T
be a LC Sturm-Liouville operator and
\eta,\psi
be real valued functions in
D_{\text{max}}
such that
[\eta,\psi]_{p}(a)=1
and
[\eta,\psi]_{p}(b)=1
. Consider the separated boundary condition

(4.7)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}[u,\eta]_{p}(a)-\alpha_{2}[u,\psi]_{p}(a)=0

\displaystyle(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\{0\}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\beta_{1}[u,\eta]_{p}(b)-\beta_{2}[u,\psi]_{p}(b)=0

\displaystyle(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\{0\},

where

\displaystyle[u,f]_{p}(a)=\lim_{x\to a^{+}}[u,f]_{p}(x)

\displaystyle[u,f]_{p}(b)=\lim_{x\to b^{-}}[u,f]_{p}(x).

Then given
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
,
i=1,2
as above, the domain

D_{\text{S.A.}}=\{y\in D_{\text{max}}:\text{equations(\ref{eq: genral seperated S.A. B.C. at a })-(\ref{eq: genral seperated S.A. B.C. at b }) hold}\},

is a self adjoint-domain for
T
, i.e.
T|_{D_{\text{S.A.}}}
is a self-adjoint operator.

In addition

Proposition 4.4
.

[
20
]

If
T
is a LC Sturm-Liouville operator then
T
has a compact resolvent.

Together Theorem
4.3
and Proposition
4.4
are enough to give us an orthonormal basis due to the following spectral theorem:

Theorem 4.5

(Spectral Theorem)
.

[
19
]

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable complex Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and let
T:\text{Dom}(T)\subseteq\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be a linear, self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
with compact resolvent. Then, there exists a sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
and a complete orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n}
of
\mathcal{H}
with
e_{n}\in\text{Dom}(T)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(1)

Te_{n}=\lambda_{n}e_{n}
,

(2)

\text{Dom}(T)=\{x\in\mathcal{H}|(\lambda_{n}\langle x,e_{n}\rangle)_{n}\in\ell^{2}\}
,

(3)

Tx=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\langle x,e_{n}\rangle e_{n}
for all
x\in\text{Dom}(T).

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=b\cdot\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}u,&[0,\infty)\times[0,\pi]\\

The function space

is a self adjoint domain for

and plays the role of an effective a parabolic boundary condition in (

\displaystyle s(t,\theta)\sim\widetilde{\gamma}\cdot s_{\text{\tiny{Hopf}}},

\displaystyle r(t,\theta)\sim r_{\text{\tiny{Sphere}}}+\widetilde{\gamma}\cdot r_{\text{\tiny{Hopf}}},

where

is the support function of a sphere with radius

5.1.
Existence of the Eigenbasis and The Self-Adjoint Domain

First we remark for which values of
n
that
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
is LC.

Proposition 5.1
.

\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
is LC if and only if
n\in(-1,1)
.

Proof.

To check if
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
is LC, solve the eigenvalue problem
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}u=\chi u
. Recall when checking LC/LP, we are free to choose
\chi
as we please. If we set
\chi:=-n(n+1)
we must solve the problem

\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\derivative{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\derivative{u}{\theta}\right)-\frac{n^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}u=0.

If we can show that any two linearly independent solutions are square integrable (with weight
w=\sin\theta
), it follows that every solution is square integrable by the triangle inequality.
First assume
n=0
. Then the eigenvalue problem is simply

\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\derivative{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\derivative{u}{\theta}\right)=0,

with two linearly independent solutions
\ln\cot\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
and a constant function. These are square integrable. Now assume
n\neq 0
, then two linearly independent solutions are

u_{\pm}=\cot^{\pm n}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right).

We have then,

||u_{\pm}||^{2}_{L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)}=\int^{\pi}_{0}\cot^{\pm 2n}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\cdot\sin\theta d\theta=2\int^{\pi}_{0}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right]^{1\pm 2n}\cdot\left[\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right]^{1\mp 2n}d\theta,

which is convergent if and only if
-1<n<1
and therefore
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
is LC if and only if
n\in(-1,1)
.
∎

For such values of
n
we may now use Theorem
4.3
to find self-adjoint domains for
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
. Furthermore, as to make the convergence obvious, we’d like the eigenbasis of
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
to contain explicitly the stationary solution to equation (
3.5
):

\frac{s_{\text{\tiny{Hopf}}}}{\sin^{n+2}\theta},

it is therefore necessary that
\frac{s_{\text{\tiny{Hopf}}}}{\sin^{n+2}\theta}\in D_{\text{S.A.}}
.
Finding such a self-adjoint domain will be the content of the next proposition.

Proposition 5.2
.

The separated boundary condition

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\theta\to 0}\left[\sin^{2n+1}\theta\derivative[]{\theta}\left(\frac{u}{\sin^{n}\theta}\right)\right]=0,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\theta\to\pi}\left[\sin^{2n+1}\theta\derivative[]{\theta}\left(\frac{u}{\sin^{n}\theta}\right)\right]=0,

generates a self-adjoint domain for the Legendre operator

(5.4)

D_{\text{S.A.}}=D_{\text{max}}\cap\left\{u\in L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi):\text{ boundary condition }(\ref{eq: seperated boundary conditions for dsa.})\text{ holds.}\right\},

where
D_{\text{max}}
is given by equation (
4.3
) for
(a,b)=(0,\pi)
and
T=\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
. Furthermore
s_{\text{\tiny{Hopf}}}/\sin^{n+2}\theta\in D_{\text{S.A.}}
.

Proof.

The expression for
D_{\text{max}}
for the Legendre operator takes the form

D_{\text{max}}=\big\{u\in L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi):{\Large\textstyle\derivative{u}{\theta}}\in\text{AC}_{\text{loc}}(0,\pi),\enskip\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}u\in L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)\big\}.

Theorem
4.3
tells us that all self adjoint domains of the Legendre operator generated by separated boundary conditions are given by functions

satisfying

\alpha_{1}[u,\eta]_{\sin\theta}(0)+\alpha_{2}[u,\psi]_{\sin\theta}(\pi)=0\enskip\&\enskip\beta_{1}[u,\eta]_{\sin\theta}(0)+\beta_{2}[u,\psi]_{\sin\theta}(\pi)=0,

where
\left\{\eta,\psi\right\}\subset D_{\text{max}}
and
[\eta,\psi]_{\sin\theta}(0)=[\eta,\psi]_{\sin\theta}(\pi)=1
, with
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\backslash\{0\}
.

Take

(\eta,\psi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sin^{n}\theta,\sin^{-n}\theta\right)
.
It is straightforward to check that this choice of
\eta
and
\psi
satisfy the above requirements. From Theorem
4.3
, the boundary conditions with this choice of

and

are

(5.5)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\theta\to 0}\left[\alpha_{1}\sin^{2n+1}\theta\derivative[]{\theta}\left(\frac{u}{\sin^{n}\theta}\right)+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\sin^{2n-1}\theta}\derivative[]{\theta}\left(u\cdot\sin^{n}\theta\right)\right]=0,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\theta\to\pi}\left[\beta_{1}\sin^{2n+1}\theta\derivative[]{\theta}\left(\frac{u}{\sin^{n}\theta}\right)+\frac{\beta_{2}}{\sin^{2n-1}\theta}\derivative[]{\theta}\left(u\cdot\sin^{n}\theta\right)\right]=0.

If we let

Theorem 5.8
.

Let

Starting with the expansion formula for

where the higher order terms in the expansion vanish via orthogonality. The integral on the left hand side can be evaluated by using sequentially, equation (

5.3.
Time Evolution of the Eigenbasis Expansion

In this section we give a solution for each
n\in(-1,1)
to the time evolution problem (
5.1
) for all
t\geq 0
and for almost every
\theta\in[0,\pi]
. Furthermore this solution is unique in the sense that it is equal point-wise to a strong solution of (
5.1
) whenever a strong solution exists, for all most all
\theta\in[0,\pi]
, for every
t\geq 0
.

Proposition 5.10
.

Fix
n\in(-1,1)
and let
\{e_{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
be the Legendre functions
\{\text{P}^{-n}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)\}^{\infty}_{m=0}
, normalised with respect to the
L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)
inner product, i.e.

e_{m}=\frac{\text{P}^{-n}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)}{||\text{P}^{-n}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)||}.

Let
u_{0}\in D_{\text{S.A.}}
have the eigenbasis decomposition

u_{0}=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}a_{m}e_{m}.

and
u:[0,\infty)\to L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)
be the mapping defined by the series

\displaystyle u(t)=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}A_{m}(t)e_{m},

\displaystyle A_{m}(t)=a_{m}\exp\{-(2n+1+m)mb\;t\}.

Then, for each
t\geq 0
,
u(t)
converges in
L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)
, is an element of
D_{\text{S.A.}}
and

(5.12)

\displaystyle[u(t)](\theta)=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}A_{m}(t)e_{m}(\theta),

point-wise for almost all
\theta\in(0,\pi)
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure).

Furthermore,
u(t)
is the unique classical solution to equation (
5.1
) for almost all
\theta\in(0,\pi)
,
t\in(0,\infty)
with initial data
u_{0}
.

Proof.

First we remark that
u(0)=u_{0}
. Take
t\in[0,\infty)
and let
{\lambda_{m}=-(2n+1+m)m}
, which is the eigenvalue of
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}
associated to
e_{m}
. Since
\lambda_{m}\leq 0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\in(-1,1)
and
b>0
, we have that
A_{m}(t)
is decreasing in
t
, and in particular
|A_{m}(t)|\leq|a_{m}|
. It follows that since
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)
, by comparison
u(t)\in L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)
.

Furthermore, by the Spectral Theorem
4.5
,
D_{\text{S.A.}}
is characterised by those elements
x\in L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)
such that
(\lambda_{n}\langle x,e_{n}\rangle)_{n}\in\ell^{2}
, therefore since
u_{0}\in D_{\text{S.A.}}
,

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}|\lambda_{m}A_{m}(t)|^{2}\leq\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}|\lambda_{m}a_{m}|^{2}<\infty,

and
u(t)\in D_{\text{S.A.}}
. Therefore
u(t)
satisfies the boundary conditions of equation (
5.1
).

We now show
u(t)
is a classical solution to equation (
5.1
) a.e. First we show (
5.12
) holds, to do so we invoke the following theorem.

Theorem 5.11

(Rademacher-Menchov)
.

[
14
, p251]

Let
\{e_{n}\}_{n}
be an orthonormal series with respect to
L^{2}_{\sin\theta}(0,\pi)
. The series

\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}b_{n}e_{n}(\theta),

converges for almost all
\theta\in(0,\pi)
if the sequence
(b_{n})_{n}
satisfies

\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}(\log_{2}(n+1)b_{n})^{2}<\infty.

∎

To see that the sequence
(a_{m})_{m}
satisfies the requirement of Rademacher-Menchov, it is enough to notice that the sequence
(\lambda_{m})_{m}
grows quadratically with
m
, hence

\sum^{\infty}_{m=M}(\log_{2}(m+1)a_{m})^{2}<\sum^{\infty}_{m=M}|\lambda_{m}a_{m}|^{2}<\infty

for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore by Rademacher-Menchov
\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}a_{m}e_{m}(\theta)
converges a.e., and so must
\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}A_{m}(t)e_{m}(\theta)
for all
t\geq 0
. Now we have proved convergence, the equality in (
5.12
) follows by uniqueness of limits, since the partial sums of
u(t)
converge a.e. along some sub-sequence to
u(t)
.

Fix
\theta\in[0,\pi]
such that the
u_{0}(\theta)
is equal to the series given in equation (
5.12
). By Fubini’s Theorem on differentiation
[
13
, p527]
, since
A_{m}(t)
is monotonic in
t
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have from term by term differentiation

\partial_{t}\big([u(t)](\theta)\big)=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\lambda_{m}A_{m}(t)e_{m}(\theta)

a.e.
t\in[0,\infty)
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure). However this means we can find arbitrarily small
t^{\prime}
such that the above series converges and therefore converges for all
t>t^{\prime}
, i.e. we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big([u(t)](\theta)\big)=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\lambda_{m}A_{m}(t)e_{m}(\theta)

\displaystyle\forall t\in(0,\infty),\text{ a.e. }\theta\in(0,\pi).

However since
u(t)\in D_{\text{S.A.}}
, we have
\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}u(t)=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\lambda_{m}A_{m}(t)e_{n}
, and because the series
\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\lambda_{m}A_{m}(t)e_{m}(\theta)
converges a.e. with respect to
\theta
, we have

\displaystyle\left[\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}u(t)\right](\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\lambda_{m}A_{m}(t)e_{m}(\theta)

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,\infty),\text{ a.e. }\theta\in(0,\pi),

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\big([u(t)](\theta)\big)

\displaystyle\forall t\in(0,\infty),\text{ a.e. }\theta\in(0,\pi).

Hence
u(t)
satisfies the desired time evolution equation.

Uniqueness follows from the theory of ODEs by projecting the time evolution equation into each eigenbasis.

∎

Making the substitution
u=s/\sin^{n+2}\theta
turns the time evolution problem (
5.1
) into

(5.13)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\partialderivative[]{t}\left(\frac{s}{\sin^{n+2}\theta}\right)=b\cdot\mathscr{L}^{n}_{n}\left(\frac{s}{\sin^{n+2}\theta}\right)},&[0,\infty)\times[0,\pi]\\
\displaystyle{\frac{s(t,\cdot)}{\sin^{n+2}(\cdot)}\in D_{\text{S.A.}}}&t\in[0,\infty)\\
s=s_{0}&\{t=0\}\times[0,\pi],\end{cases}

which describes the evolution of a surfaces astigmatism under the linear Hopf flow (
3.5
).

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}\in\mathscr{W}
be an initial surface with an astigmatism
s_{0}
satisfying
s_{0}/\sin^{n+2}\theta\in D_{\text{S.A.}}
. Under the linear Hopf flow (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) with
n\in(-1,1)
, for all
\theta\in[0,\pi]
,
t\geq 0
, the following quantities evolve as

\displaystyle s(t,\theta)=\gamma_{0,n}(t)\sin^{2n+2}\theta+\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n,m}(t)\sin^{n+2}\theta\text{P}^{-n}_{n+m}(\cos\theta),

\displaystyle\big(\text{a.e. }\theta\in[0,\pi]\big),

r_{1}(t,\theta)=C_{1}(t)+\frac{\gamma_{0,n}\sin^{2n+2}\theta}{2n+2}+\sin^{n+2}\theta\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n,m}(t)\left[\text{P}^{-(n+2)}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)+\cot\theta\text{P}^{-(n+1)}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)\right],

+\sin^{n+2}\theta\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n,m}(t)\text{P}^{-(n+2)}_{n+m}(\cos\theta),

for all
\theta\in[0,\pi]
, where
\Gamma_{m,n}(t)=\gamma_{m,n}\exp\{-(2n+1+m)mb\cdot t\big\}

and
\gamma_{m,n}
are the decomposition coefficients of the initial surface
\mathcal{S}_{0}
in the basis
\{\text{P}^{-n}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)\}^{\infty}_{m=0}
. Furthermore the constants
C_{1}(t)
and
C_{2}(t)
evolve as

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)=C_{1}(0)e^{-2(n+1)bt}+\frac{c\left(1-e^{-2(n+1)bt}\right)}{2(n+1)b},

\displaystyle C_{2}=\text{ constant }.

Proof.

Fix
n\in(-1,1)
and let
s=\sin^{n+2}\theta\cdot u
. Expand
u
as in Proposition
5.10
. The coefficients
\Gamma_{m,n}(t)
are related to
A_{m}(t)
by
A_{m}(t)=\Gamma_{n,m}(t)||\text{P}^{-n}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)||
. This derives the expression for

.
The expressions for
r_{1}(t,\theta)
and
r(t,\theta)
follow by integration as in the proof of Theorem
5.8
.

To derive the behaviour of
C_{1}(t)
and
C_{2}(t)
, we ensure they satisfy equation (

) for the linear Hopf flow. i.e.

\displaystyle\partialderivative[]{r(t,\theta)}{t}

\displaystyle=ar_{1}(t,\theta)+br_{2}(t,\theta)+c

\displaystyle=c+b\left[s(t,\theta)-2(n+1)r_{1}(t,\theta)\right].

where we have first written the right hand side of (
3.4

r_{1}
&

-a/b=2n+3

and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}C_{2}(t)\cos\theta+\partial_{t}C_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=c-2(n+1)bC_{1}(t)+b\sin^{n+2}\theta\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n,m}(t)\Delta_{m,n},

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{m,n}=\text{P}^{-n}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)-2(n+1)\cot\theta\text{P}^{-(n+1)}_{n+m}(\cos\theta)-(2n+2+m)(m-1)\text{P}^{-(n+2)}_{n+m}(\cos\theta).

and

[

\mathrm{P}_{\nu}^{\mu+2}(x)+2(\mu+1)x\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\mathrm{P}_{\nu}^{\mu+1}(x)+(\nu-\mu)(\nu+\mu+1)\mathrm{P}_{\nu}^{\mu}(x)=0,

we can see that

is identically

\partial_{t}C_{2}(t)\cos\theta+\partial_{t}C_{1}(t)=c-2(n+1)bC_{1}(t).

It is easy to see that
\partial_{t}C_{2}(t)=0
. Solving the remaining ODE gives the stated time evolution of

∎

Remark 5.13
.

We have the following asymptotic behaviour as
t\to\infty
,

r(t,\theta)\sim C_{2}\cos\theta+\frac{c}{2(n+1)b}+\gamma_{0,n}\left[\frac{\sin^{2n+2}\theta}{2n+2}-\cos\theta\int^{\theta}_{0}\sin^{2n+1}\theta d\theta\right].

This is the astigmatism of a linear Hopf sphere. If
\gamma_{0,n}=0
, i.e. if the focal points of the initial surface coincide, then the astigmatism is that of a sphere with radius

\frac{c}{2(n+1)b}=-\frac{c}{a+b},

as claimed.
