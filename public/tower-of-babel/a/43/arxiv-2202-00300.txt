Higher order Goh conditions for singular extremals of corank 1 1
Francesco Boarotto 1, Roberto Monti 2, Alessandro Socionovo 3

Abstract
. We prove Goh conditions of order n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 for strictly singular length-minimizing curves of corank 1 1 , under the assumption that the domain of the n n th instrinsic differential is of finite codimension. This result relies upon the proof of an open mapping theorem for maps with regular n n th differential.

Main: 46A30, 53C17. Secondary: 49K15, 93B17.

1.
Introduction

One of the main open problems in sub-Riemannian geometry is the regularity of length-minimizing curves.
Its difficulty is due to the singularities of the end-point map, i.e., to the presence of points where its differential is not surjective.
In this paper, we study the end-point map up to order
n
obtaining necessary conditions of Goh-type for optimal trajectories.
The fine understanding of sub-Riemannian geodesics with their best regularity is of great importance in several fields ranging from
Nonholonomic Mechanics to Geometric Control Theory.

A sub-Riemannian manifold is a triplet
(M,\Delta,g)
, where
M
is a smooth, i.e.,
C^{\infty}
manifold,
\Delta\subset TM
is a distribution of rank
2\leq d<\dim(M)
, and
g
is a metric on
\Delta
. In a neighborhood
U\subset M
of any point
q\in M
, there exist vector-fields
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\in\mathrm{Vec}(U)
such that
\Delta=\mathrm{span}\{f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\}
on
U
. Since our considerations are local, we can assume in the sequel that
U=M
.
We also assume that
\Delta
satisfies Hörmander’s condition

(1.1)

\mathrm{Lie}\{f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\}(p)=T_{p}M,\quad p\in M,

i.e., that
\Delta
is completely non-integrable. For an exhaustive introduction to sub-Riemannian geometry, we refer the reader to
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
12
,
23
,
27
]
.

Let
I=[0,1]
be the unit interval. A curve
\gamma\in AC(I;M)
is
horizontal
if
\dot{\gamma}\in\Delta_{\gamma}
a.e. on
I
, that is

(1.2)

\dot{\gamma}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}(t)f_{i}(\gamma(t)),\quad\text{for a.e.\penalty\ $t\in I$},

for some unique
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})\in L^{1}(I;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, called control of
\gamma
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that

g
makes
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}
orthonormal, in which case
the length of
\gamma
is the
L^{1}
-norm of its control. We can also replace the Banach space
L^{1}(I;\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the smaller Hilbert space
X=L^{2}(I;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The end-point map
F_{q}:X\to M
with base-point
q\in M
is defined letting
F_{q}(u)=\gamma_{u}(1)
, where
\gamma_{u}
is the unique solution to (
1.2
) with
\gamma_{u}(0)=q
. The point
\bar{q}=F_{q}(u)
is the end-point of the curve
\gamma_{u}
.
Since
q\in M
is fixed, we shall simply write
F=F_{q}
.

Controls
u\in X
where the differential
d_{u}F
is not
surjective are called
singular
. Now consider the extended end-point map
F_{J}:X\to M\times\mathbb{R}
,

F_{J}(u)=(F(u),\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2})
.
If for every
(\lambda,\lambda_{0})\in\mathrm{Im}(d_{u}F_{J})^{\perp}
we have
\lambda_{0}=0
, the singular control
u
is called

strictly singular
, see Definition
6.1
.
If
u
is not strictly singular (namely, if it is
normal
) then a length-minimizing
curve
\gamma_{u}
is smooth.
For this reason the regularity problem of sub-Riemannian geodesics reduces to the regularity of strictly singular minimizers.

In his ground-breaking work
[
22
]
,
Montgomery first proved that
strictly singular
curves can be as a matter of fact length-minimizing.
His example was discovered studying a
charged particle traveling in the plane under
the influence of a magnetic field.
Also
nice abnormal extremals
, see
[
19
]
, are locally length-minimizing.
Examples of purely Lipschitz and spiral-like abnormal curves in Carnot groups are presented in
[
16
,
17
]
, and an algorithm for producing many new examples is proposed in
[
9
]
. The length-minimality property of all these examples is not yet well-understood.

A recent approach to the regularity problem of length-minimizing curves is based on the analysis of specific singularities such as corners, spiral-like curves or
curves with no straight tangent line. This approach does not use open mapping theorems but it rather relies on the ad hoc construction of shorter competitors, see

[
4
,
10
,
11
,
18
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.

Another new and interesting approach to the problem is proposed in
[
20
]
, where the authors prove that the controls of strictly singular length-minimizers are
L^{p}
-Hölder continuous.

On the other hand, necessary conditions for the minimality of singular extremals can be obtained from the differential study of the end-point map. The theory is well-known till the second order and was initiated by Goh
[
8
]
and developed by Agrachev and Sachkov in
[
3
]
. Using second order open mapping theorems (index theory),
for a strictly singular length-minimizing curve
\gamma
and for any adjoint curve
\lambda
they prove the validity of the following Goh conditions:

(1.3)

\langle\lambda,[f_{i},f_{j}](\gamma)\rangle=0,\quad\textrm{ $i,j=1,\ldots,d$.}

The first order conditions
\langle\lambda,f_{i}(\gamma)\rangle=0
are ensured by
Pontryagin Maximum Principle. Partial necessary conditions of the third order are obtained in
[
7
]
.

Generalized second order Goh conditions have been recently obtained in
[
14
]
.

Our goal is to extend the second order theory of
[
1
]
to any order
n\geq 3
and to get necessary conditions as in (
1.3
) involving brackets of
n
vector fields.

There is a clear connection between the geometry of
\Delta
and the expansion of the end-point map
F
. In particular, the commutators of length
n
should appear in the
n
th order term of the expansion of
F
. In Section
5
, we provide a first positive answer to this idea.

In order to develop the theory, we need a suitable definition of
n
th differential.
For
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in X
and
u\in X
, we first define

D_{u}^{n}F(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}F\Big(u+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{t^{i}v_{i}}{i!}\Big)\bigg|_{t=0}.

Then, we restrict
D_{u}^{n}F
to a suitable domain
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F)\subset X^{n-1}
that, roughly speaking, consists of points where the lower order differentials

d_{u}F,D_{u}^{2}F,\dots,D_{u}^{n-1}F
vanish. Finally, we define

\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F:\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F)\to\mathrm{coker}(d_{u}F)
letting

\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F=\mathrm{pr}(D_{u}^{n}F)
, where
\mathrm{pr}
is the projection onto
\mathrm{coker}(d_{u}F)
, see Definition
2.2
.
A motivation for this definition is the fact that

\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F
behaves covariantly, in the sense that, for a given diffeomorphism
P\in C^{\infty}(M;M)
,

\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}(P\circ F)
depends only on the first order derivatives of
P
.

If the set of all
v_{1}\in\ker(d_{u}F)
that can be extended to some
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1})\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F)

contains a linear space of finite codimension in
X
, we say that
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F)
has finite codimension, see Definition
2.5
.
This property is in general difficult to check. However,
if the lower intrinsic differentials vanish

(1.4)

\mathscr{D}^{h}_{u}F=0,\ \ h=2,\dots,n-1,

then
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F)
automatically has finite codimension. This is a corollary of Proposition
2.6
.

Our main result consists of necessary conditions of Goh-type for length-minimizing strictly singular curves

\gamma_{u}
of corank-one, i.e., such that
\mathrm{im}(d_{u}F)
has codimension
1
in
T_{\gamma_{u}(1)}M
.
For the definition of adjoint curve, see Section
9

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M,\Delta,g)
be a sub-Riemannian manifold and
\gamma=\gamma_{u}\in AC(I;M)
be a strictly singular length-minimizing curve of corank
1
.

If
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F)
,
n\geq 3
, has finite codimension
then any adjoint curve
\lambda\in AC(I;T^{*}M)
satisfies

(1.5)

\langle\lambda(t),[f_{j_{n}},[\dots[f_{j_{2}},f_{j_{1}}]\dots]](\gamma(t))\rangle=0,

for all
t\in I
and for all
j_{1},\dots,j_{n}=1,\dots,d
.

In general, necessary conditions as in (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) are not enough to prove the non-minimality of corners or spirals (see for instance the example on page 17 in
[
14
]
).

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on an open mapping argument applied to the extended end-point map
F_{J}=(F,J):X\to M\times\mathbb{R}
, where
J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I;\mathbb{R}^{d})}
is the energy of
\gamma=\gamma_{u}
. Minimizing the energy is in fact equivalent to minimizing the length, because for horizontal curves parameterized by arc-length the
L^{2}
-norm of the control coincides with its
L^{1}
-norm.

Motivated by this application, in Section
3
we develop a theory about open mapping theorems of order
n
for functions
F:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
between Banach spaces. In our opinion, this preliminary study is worth of interest on its own. It adapts in a geometrical perspective some ideas presented in
[
28
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and let
F\in C^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, be a smooth mapping.

If the intrinsic differential
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F:\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\to\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)
,
n\geq 2
,
is regular at the critical point
0\in X
then
F
is open at
0
.

The notion of
“regularity”
used in Theorem
1.2
is delicate because
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F

is a non-linear mapping defined on a domain without linear structure. Denoting by
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
the corank of the critical point
0
,
Definition

2.8
of regular differential depends on
the existence of some homogeneous map from
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
into
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
inverting
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F
in a bounded way.
Under the assumption of vanishing lower differentials (
1.4
) this homogeneous map can be constructed explicitly, see Proposition
2.10
.
When
0\in X
is a critical point of corank
1
the definition of regular differential is effective, see Corollary
3.2
.

The rest of the paper is devoted to the study of the open mapping property for the extended end-point map
F_{J}
around a singular control
u
.
In fact, we will study the auxiliary map
G
, called
variation map
,
defined by
G(v)=F(u+v)
,
in order to move the base-point from
u
to
0
.

A crucial ingredient in our analysis is the definition of non-linear sets
V_{h}\subset X
,
h\in\mathbb{N}
,
consisting of controls with vanishing iterated integrals for any order
h\leq n-1
, see (
4.4
). Using such controls we are able to catch the geometric structure of the
n
th differential
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G
in terms of Lie brackets.

The algebraic properties of the sets
V_{h}
appear in the theory of rough paths (see for instance
[
21
]
) and
are studied in
Section
4
.

In
Sections
5
and
6
,
we use the formalism of chronological calculus
[
3
, Chapter 2]
to compute the
n
th differential
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G
of the variation map
and the final outcome is formula (
5.13
). This formula contains a localization parameter
s>0
that can be used to shrink the support of the control in a neighborhood of some point
t_{0}\in[0,1)
. Passing to the limit as
s\to 0^{+}
, we obtain a new map

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}:X\to\mathbb{R}
:

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)=\int_{\Sigma_{n}}\langle\lambda,[g_{v(t_{n})}^{t_{0}},[\dots,[g_{v(t_{2})}^{t_{0}},g_{v(t_{1})}^{t_{0}}]]\ldots](\bar{q})\rangle dt_{1}\dots dt_{n},

where
\Sigma_{n}=\{0\leq t_{n}\leq t_{n-1}\leq\ldots\leq t_{1}\leq 1\}
is the standard simplex,

\lambda\in T^{*}_{\bar{q}}M
is a fixed covector orthogonal to
\mathrm{coker}(d_{u}F)
,

\bar{q}=F(q)\in M
is the end-point,
and

g_{v(t_{i})}^{t_{0}}
is the pull-back of the time-dependent vector field
f_{v}=v^{1}f_{1}+\ldots+v^{d}f_{d}
along the flow of
u
.
In the corank
1
case, we show that if there exists
v\in V_{n-1}
with

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)\neq 0

then the extended map
G_{J}
is open at
0
, see our Theorems
6.3
and

6.4
where the hypothesis on
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G)
to have finite codimension is crucial. So
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}=0
on
V_{n-1}
becomes a necessary condition for the length-minimality of singular extremals.

In Sections
7
and
8
, we study the geometric implications of equation
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}=0
.
First, we explore the symmetries of
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}
, showing how the shuffle algebra of iterated integrals interacts with generalized Jacobi identities of order
n
, see Theorem
7.1
. In spite of the non-linear structure of
V_{n-1}
, we are able to polarize the equation
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}=0
on linear subspaces of
V_{n-1}

of arbitrarily large dimension, thus de facto bypassing the non-linearity of the problem.

At this point, we regard
the quantities in (
1.5
) as unknowns of a nonsingular system of linear equations, thus proving their vanishing.
To get this nonsingularity, we work with families of trigonometric functions having sparse and high frequences, see Theorems
8.1
and
8.2
.

Our argument leading to the final proof of Theorem
1.1
is summarized in Section
9
.
In Section 10 we complete the study of a well-known example of
a strictly singular
curve.
In
M=\mathbb{R}^{3}
we consider the distribution spanned by the vector-fields

(1.6)

\displaystyle f_{1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad f_{2}=(1-x_{1})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}+x_{1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is a parameter. Using the theory developed in Sections 2-6, we
show that for odd
n
the curve
\gamma(t)=(0,t,0)
,
t\in[0,1]
, is not length-minimizing.
This is interesting because, for even
n
, this singular curve is on the contrary locally length-minimizing.
In fact, for even
n
the
n
th differential of the end-point map does not satisfy assumption i) of Proposition
2.9
and our open mapping theorem does not apply. See our discussion in Remark
10.4
.

We conclude this introductory part commenting on the assumptions made in Theorem
1.1
.

The assumption on
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{u}^{n}F)
to have finite codimension cannot be easily dropped:
it is used in the key Theorem
6.3
.
The corank
1
assumption on the length-minimizing curve is used when Theorem
1.2

is applied to the end-point map. We think that it should be possible to drop the corank
1
assumption,
but this certainly requires some new deep idea.

2.
Intrinsic differentials

Let
X
be a Banach space and
F\in C^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, be a smooth map.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define the
n
th differential of
F
at
0\in X
as the map
d_{0}^{n}F:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

d_{0}^{n}F(v)=\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}F(tv)\big|_{t=0},\quad v\in X.

With abuse of notation, the associated
n
th multilinear differential is the map
d_{0}^{n}F:X^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined in one of the following two equivalent ways,
for
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in X
,

(2.1)

\begin{split}d_{0}^{n}F(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=&\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial t_{1}\dots\partial t_{n}}F\Big(\sum_{h=1}^{n}t_{h}v_{h}\Big)\bigg|_{t_{1}=\dots=t_{n}=0}\\
=&\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial t_{1}\dots\partial t_{n}}d_{0}^{n}F\Big(\sum_{h=1}^{n}t_{h}v_{h}\Big)\bigg|_{t_{1}=\dots=t_{n}=0}.\end{split}

We have the identity
d_{0}^{n}F(v)=d_{0}^{n}F(v,\dots,v)
. The differential
d_{0}^{n}F
is
symmetric, in the sense that
d_{0}^{n}F(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=d_{0}^{n}F(v_{\sigma_{1}},\dots,v_{\sigma_{n}})

for any permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Here and hereafter, we use the notation
\sigma_{i}=\sigma(i)
for a permutation
\sigma

and for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

A different
n
th multilinear differential for
F
at
0
is the map
D_{0}^{n}F:X^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by the formula

(2.2)

D_{0}^{n}F(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}F\Big(\sum_{h=1}^{n}\frac{t^{h}v_{h}}{h!}\Big)\bigg|_{t=0},\quad v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in X.

The multilinear differential
D_{0}^{n}F
is not symmetric.

The
n
th multilinear differentials
d_{0}^{n}F
and
D_{0}^{n}F
are related via the Faà di Bruno formula
[
13
]
.
We denote by
\mathscr{I}_{n}
the set of
n
-multi-indices, i.e.,

\mathscr{I}_{n}=\left\{\alpha\mid\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\right\},

where
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
starts from
1
. When the naturals start from
0
we use the notation
\mathscr{I}_{n}^{0}
. Also, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
we use the notation
\mathscr{I}_{n,d}
for the sets of
n
-multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathscr{I}_{n}
with values in
\{1,\dots,d\}^{n}
. For
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n}
, we use the standard notation
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}
for the length (or weight) of
\alpha
and
\alpha!=\alpha_{1}!\dots\alpha_{n}!
for its factorial.

Proposition 2.1

(Faà di Bruno)
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in X
we have

(2.3)

D_{0}^{n}F(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\sum_{h=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h},|\alpha|=n}\frac{n!}{h!\alpha!}d_{0}^{h}F(v_{\alpha}),

where, for
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h}
, we set
v_{\alpha}=(v_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,v_{\alpha_{h}})\in X^{h}
.

The
n
th differential
D^{n}_{0}F
,
n\geq 2
, does not transform covariantly, in the sense that, for a generic diffeomorphism
P\in\ C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R}^{m})
the differential
D^{n}_{0}(P\circ F)
depends also on the derivatives of
P
of order
2
and higher. In order to have an “intrinsic”
n
th differential, we need to restrict
D_{0}^{n}F
to a suitable domain and project it onto
\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)
.
We denote by
\mathrm{pr}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)
the standard projection (
i.e., the quotient map modulo
\ker(d_{0}F)
).
We fix coordinates in
X
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in such a way that
\mathrm{coim}(d_{0}F)=\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}
and
\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)=\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
.
So we have
the splittings

(2.4)

X=\ker(d_{0}F)\oplus\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell},\qquad\mathbb{R}^{m}=\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\oplus\mathrm{im}(d_{0}F),

where
\ell=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F))
. The differential
d_{0}F:\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}\to\mathrm{im}(d_{0}F)
is a linear isomorphism.

Definition 2.2

(Intrinsic
n
th differential)
.

Let
F\in C^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. By induction on
n\geq 2
,
we define a domain
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\subset X^{n-1}
and a map
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F:\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\to\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)
, called

intrinsic
n
th differential
of
F
at
0
, in the following way.

When
n=2
we let
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F)=\left\{v\in X\mid D_{0}F(v)=0\right\}=\ker(d_{0}F)\subset X
and we define

\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F:\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F)\to\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)

(2.5)

\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F(v)=\mathrm{pr}(D_{0}^{2}F(v,*)),\quad v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F).

Inductively, for
n>2
we set

\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)=\left\{v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n-1}F)\times\mathrm{coim}(d_{0}F)\mid D_{0}^{n-1}F(v)=0\right\}\subset X^{n-1},

and we define
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F:\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\to\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)
as

(2.6)

\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F(v)=\mathrm{pr}(D_{0}^{n}F(v,*)),\quad v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F).

Remark 2.3
.

The definition of
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F
in (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) does not depend on the last argument
*\in X
of
D_{0}^{n}F
.
Indeed, by formula (
2.3
) with
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
, so with
*=v_{n}
in the notation above, we have

(2.7)

D_{0}^{n}F(v)=d_{0}F(v_{n})+\sum_{h=2}^{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h},|\alpha|=n}\frac{n!}{h!\alpha!}d_{0}^{h}F(v_{\alpha}),

where
v_{\alpha}
does not contain
v_{n}
when
|\alpha|=n
and
h\geq 2
, and
\mathrm{pr}(d_{0}F(v_{n}))=0
.

Remark 2.4
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce on
X^{n}
the dilations
\delta_{\lambda}:X^{n}\to X^{n}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\delta_{\lambda}(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})=(\lambda v_{1},\lambda^{2}v_{2},\ldots,\lambda^{n}v_{n}).

From formulas (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) it follows that for any
v\in X^{n}
we have the
\delta
-homogeneity property

D_{0}^{n}F(\delta_{\lambda}(v))=\lambda^{n}D_{0}^{n}F(v).

Then the domain
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
is also
\delta
-homogeneous as a subset of
X^{n-1}
:

(2.8)

\delta_{\lambda}(\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F))=\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\quad\textrm{for any nonzero }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

While
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F)=\ker(d_{0}F)
has finite codimension in
X
, this might not be the case when
n>2
.
In order to develop the theory within a consistent setting we need some additional assumption on
F
.

Definition 2.5

(Domain with finite codimension)
.

We say that
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
,
n\geq 3
, has finite codimension if the set

\{v_{1}\in\ker(d_{0}F)\mid\textrm{there exists }v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1})\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\}

contains a linear space of finite codimension in
X
. We define the codimension of
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
as the smallest of these codimensions.

The vanishing of lower order differentials (
1.4
) ensures that
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
has finite codimension.
This is a corollary of the following proposition.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let

F\in C^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}^{m})
be a smooth map.
If
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}F=0
for all
2\leq h<n
, with
n\geq 3
, then

\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\subset X^{n-1}
is a nonempty affine bundle over
\ker(d_{0}F)
that is diffeomorphic to

\ker(d_{0}F)^{n-1}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n\geq 3
. When
n=3
the domain of
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{3}F

is

\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{3}F)=\big\{(v_{1},v_{2})\in\ker(d_{0}F)\times\mathrm{coim}(d_{0}F)\mid D_{0}^{2}F(v_{1},v_{2})=0\big\},

where, as in (
2.7
),

D_{0}^{2}F(v_{1},v_{2})=d_{0}F(v_{2})+d^{2}_{0}F(v_{1},v_{1}).

We use the splittings (
2.4
).
Since the map
d_{0}F:\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}\to\mathrm{im}(d_{0}F)
is invertible, we can define

\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\ker(d_{0}F),\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell})
letting

\varphi(v_{1})=-(d_{0}F)^{-1}(d^{2}_{0}F(v_{1},v_{1})).

This is well-defined
because,
by assumption, we have
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F=0
and this implies
\mathrm{pr}(d^{2}_{0}F(v_{1},v_{1}))=0
.
Now, letting

\Phi(v_{1},v_{2})=(v_{1},v_{2}+\varphi(v_{1})),

we obtain a diffeomorphism
\Phi:\ker(d_{0}F)^{2}\to\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{3}F)
.

Suppose the theorem is true for
n
and let us prove it for
n+1
.
Our inductive assumption is the existence of a diffeomorphism
\Phi\in C^{\infty}(\ker(d_{0}F)^{n-1},\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F))
.
Now we have

\begin{split}\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n+1}F)&=\left\{(v,w)\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\times\mathrm{coim}(d_{0}F)\mid D_{0}^{n}F(v,w)=0\right\}\\
&=\left\{(\Phi(u),w)\mid u\in\ker(d_{0}F)^{n-1},\,w\in\mathrm{coim}(d_{0}F),\,D_{0}^{n}F(\Phi(u),w)=0\right\},\end{split}

and, by (
2.7
),
equation
D_{0}^{n}F(\Phi(u),w)=0
reads

w=\psi(u)=-(d_{0}F)^{-1}\sum_{h=2}^{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h},|\alpha|=n}\frac{n!}{h!\alpha!}d_{0}^{h}F(\Phi(u)_{\alpha}).

The function
\psi
is well-defined because
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F=0
.
Now
\Psi(u,z)=(u,z+\psi(u))
is a diffeomorphism from
\ker(d_{0}F)^{n}
to
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n+1}F)
.

∎

Our next goal is to introduce the notion of regular
n
th differential. Recall that

0\in X
is a critical point of
F
with corank
\ell\in\{1,\dots,m\}
if

\dim(\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F))=\ell
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\ell\geq 1
and
n\geq 2
.
We say that a continuous map
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1}):\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to X^{n-1}
is separately
\delta
-homogeneous if there exist vectors

v_{j}^{\beta,\alpha}\in X
, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
,
\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0}
with
1\leq|\beta|\leq n-1
, and
\alpha\in\{-1,1\}^{\ell}
, such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
we have

(2.9)

w_{j}(t)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0},|\beta|=j}t^{\beta}v_{j}^{\beta,\mathrm{sgn}(t)},\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1,

where
\mathrm{sgn}(t)=(\mathrm{sgn}(t_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{sgn}(t_{1}))
.

For constant
\mathrm{sgn}(t)
, the coordinate
w_{j}
is a homogeneous polynomial in
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
with degree
j
and coefficients in
X
.
If in (
2.9
) the coefficients

do not depend on

we say the

is
\delta
-homogeneous.

Definition 2.8

(Regular
n
th differential)
.

Let

0\in X
be a critical point of corank
\ell\in\{1,\dots,m\}
of a smooth map

F\in C^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
We say that the differential
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F:\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)\to\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)
,
n\geq 2
, is regular if there exists a separately
\delta
-homogeneous map
w:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
such that
the function

f:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)

(2.10)

f(t)=\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F(w(\varrho(t))),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell},

is a homeomorphism with bounded inverse at zero, i.e., there exists
0<L<\infty
such that

(2.11)

|f^{-1}(\tau)|\leq L|\tau|,\quad\tau\in\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F).

Above we let
\varrho(t)=\left(\mathrm{sgn}(t_{1})|t_{1}|^{1/n},\dots,\mathrm{sgn}(t_{\ell})|t_{\ell}|^{1/n}\right)

When the corank is
\ell=1
the notion of regular
n
th differential is effective.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
F\in C^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}^{m})
be a smooth map such that

0\in X
is a critical point of corank
\ell=1
. Assume that:

i)

n\geq 2
is even and there exist
2
elements
v^{\pm}\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
such that
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F(v^{+})>0
and
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F(v^{-})<0
; or,

ii)

n\geq 3
is odd and there exists
v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
such that
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F(v)\neq 0
.

Then
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F
is regular.

Proof.

We prove the claim in the case i). Let
v^{\pm}=(v_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,v_{n-1}^{\pm})\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
and define the separately
\delta
-homogeneous function
w:\mathbb{R}\to X^{n-1}

w(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(tv_{1}^{+},t^{2}v_{2}^{+},\ldots,t^{n-1}v_{n-1}^{+}),\quad t>0,\\
(tv_{1}^{-},t^{2}v_{2}^{-},\ldots,t^{n-1}v_{n-1}^{-}),\quad t<0.\end{array}\right.

By (
2.8
) we have
w(t)\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The function
f(t)=\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F(w(\varrho(t)))
is separately linear for
t>0
and
t<0
, with
f(1)>0
and
f(-1)<0
. Then it is a homeomorphism from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
with bounded inverse at zero, in the sense (
2.11
).

When
n
is odd the proof is analogous.

∎

When the corank is larger,
\ell>1
, the existence of (separately)
\delta
-homogeneous maps from

into
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
is non-trivial.
Under assumption (

),
the following theorem guaranties that for any linear map into

there exists a
\delta
-homogeneous extension into

.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let

F\in C^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}^{m})
be a smooth map such that
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}F=0
for all
2\leq h<n
, for some
n\geq 2
.
For any
v_{1}^{1},\ldots,v_{1}^{\ell}\in\ker(d_{0}F)\subset X
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist vectors
v_{j}^{\beta}\in X
,
j=1,\dots,n-1
and
\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0}
with
|\beta|=j
,
such that the function
w\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\ell};X^{n-1})
with coordinates

(2.12)

w_{j}(t)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0},|\beta|=j}t^{\beta}v_{j}^{\beta},\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1,

satisfies
w(t)\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. In particular, when
\mathrm{e}^{i}
is the
i
th vector of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}

we have
v_{1}^{\mathrm{e}^{i}}=v_{1}^{i}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n\geq 2
.

For
n=2
the statement follows from the fact that
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F)=\ker(d_{0}F)
is a vector space.
In this case, we have
j=1
and
\beta=\mathrm{e}^{i}
for some
i
. Fixing
v_{1}^{\beta}=v_{1}^{i}
with
v_{1}=(v_{1}^{1},\ldots,v_{1}^{\ell})
,
formula (
2.12
) gives a function
w_{1}
with values in
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{2}F)
.

We assume the claim for
n-1
and we prove it for
n
. In particular, for
j\leq n-2
,
the vectors
v_{j}^{\beta}\in X
are already fixed so that the functions defined in
(
2.12
) with
j\leq n-2
satisfy

(w_{1}(t),\dots,w_{n-2}(t))\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n-1}F)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
.
Our goal is to find
v_{n-1}^{\beta}
, for
\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0}
with
|\beta|=n-1
, such that
w(t)=(w_{1}(t),\dots,w_{n-1}(t))\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
.

The condition
w(t)\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
is equivalent to

1)

(w_{1}(t),\dots,w_{n-2}(t))\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n-1}F)
;

2)

D_{0}^{n-1}F(w(t))=0
.

The first condition is true by induction. By formula (
2.7
), the latter
is equivalent to

(2.13)

d_{0}F(w_{n-1}(t))+\sum_{h=2}^{n-1}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h},|\alpha|=n-1}\frac{(n-1)!}{h!\alpha!}d_{0}^{h}F(w_{\alpha}(t))=0.

We solve this equation to determine the vectors
v_{n-1}^{\beta}\in X
. By linearity, we have

d_{0}F(w_{n-1}(t))=\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0},|\beta|=n-1}t^{\beta}d_{0}F(v_{n-1}^{\beta}),

and

\displaystyle d_{0}^{h}F(w_{\alpha}(t))

\displaystyle=d_{0}^{h}F(w_{\alpha_{1}}(t),\dots,w_{\alpha_{1}}(t))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta^{1}\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0},|\beta^{1}|=\alpha_{1}}\dots\sum_{\beta^{h}\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0},|\beta^{h}|=\alpha_{h}}t^{\beta^{1}+\dots+\beta^{h}}d_{0}^{h}F(v_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta^{1}},\dots,v_{\alpha_{h}}^{\beta^{h}}).

By the identity principle of polynomials, solving equation (
2.13
) is equivalent to solving the set of equations

(2.14)

d_{0}F(v_{n-1}^{\beta})+\sum_{h=2}^{n-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h},|\alpha|=n-1\\
\beta^{1}+\dots+\beta^{h}=\beta\end{subarray}}\frac{(n-1)!}{h!\alpha!}d_{0}^{h}F(v_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta^{1}},\dots,v_{\alpha_{h}}^{\beta^{h}})=0,

for
\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0}
with
|\beta|=n-1
. This is possible since, by assumption, we have
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}F=0
for
1\leq h\leq n-1
. This implies that
\mathrm{pr}(d_{0}^{h}F(v_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta^{1}},\dots,v_{\alpha_{h}}^{\beta^{h}}))=0
, i.e.,
d_{0}^{h}F(v_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta^{1}},\dots,v_{\alpha_{h}}^{\beta^{h}})\in\mathrm{im}(d_{0}F)
and thus we can find

v_{n-1}^{\beta}\in X
solving equation (
2.14
).
∎

Proposition

can be improved making the construction separately

-homogeneous. We omit the details.

3.
Open mapping theorem of order
n

In this section, we prove our main open mapping theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
F\in C^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}^{m})
be a smooth map with regular differential
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F
,
n\geq 2
, at the critical point
0\in X
.
Then
F
is open at
0
.

Proof.

Let
0\in X
be a critical point for
F
of corank
\ell\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.
We identify
\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}F)=\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and we split
X=\mathbb{R}^{\ell-m}\oplus\ker(d_{0}F)
.

The regularity of
\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F
means that there exists a separately
\delta
-homogeneous map
w:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)

such that the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
in (
2.10
) is a homeomorphsim and satisfies (
2.11
).
By formula (
2.9
), the map
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1})
is of the form

w_{j}(t)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{\ell}^{0},|\beta|=j}t^{\beta}v_{j}^{\beta,\mathrm{sgn}(t)},\quad j=1,\dots,n-1,

for some
v_{j}^{\beta,\mathrm{sgn}(t)}\in X
.

We define the map
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to X
letting

\Phi(r,t)=r+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{w_{j}(t)}{j!},\quad(r,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}.

Above and hereafter, we identify
r\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell-m}
with
(r,0)\in X
, so that the sum
r+v
with
v\in X
is well defined.
We claim that we have the expansion

(3.1)

F\left(\Phi(r,t)\right)=d_{0}F(r)+D_{0}^{n}F(w(t),0)+R(r,t),

where the remainder satisfies

(3.2)

\lim_{(r,t)\to 0}\frac{R(r,t)}{|r|+|t|^{n}}=0.

For any positive scalar
s\geq 0
, using the homogeneity
w_{j}(st)=s^{j}w(t)
we obtain the formula

\Phi(0,st)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{s^{j}}{j!}w_{j}(t),

and, for fixed
t
, the function
\varphi(s)=F(\Phi(0,st))
has the Taylor expansion

(3.3)

\varphi(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\varphi^{(j)}(0)}{j!}s^{j}+\frac{\varphi^{(n+1)}(\bar{s})}{(n+1)!}s^{n+1},\quad s\in[0,1],

for some
\bar{s}\in[0,s]
.

By definition (
2.2
), we have
\varphi^{(j)}(0)=D^{j}_{0}F(w_{1}(t),\dots,w_{j-1}(t))
and since
w(t)\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F)
, we deduce that

\varphi^{(j)}(0)=0
for
j=1,\dots,n-1
, while for
j=n
we have

\varphi^{(n)}(0)=D^{n}_{0}F(w_{1}(t),\dots,w_{n-1}(t),0)=D^{n}_{0}F(w(t),0).

From the Taylor expansion (
3.3
) with
s=1
, we obtain

(3.4)

F(\Phi(0,t))=D^{n}_{0}F(w(t),0)+E(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell},

where the error satisfies the estimate

(3.5)

|E(t)|=\Big|\frac{\varphi^{(n+1)}(\bar{s})}{(n+1)!}\Big|\leq C|t|^{n+1},

for some constant
C>0
.

Now, we obtain the expansion (
3.1
) adding a development in
r
of the first order.
We are left with the proof of (
3.2
). Also by (
3.5
), the error
R(r,t)
is estimated by a sum of the form

|R(r,t)|\leq\sum_{0\leq i\leq 2,0\leq j\leq n+1}c_{ij}|r|^{i}|t|^{j},

with constants
c_{ij}
such that
c_{0j}=0
if
j\leq n
and
c_{10}=0
. So we have
|R(r,t)|\leq C(|r|^{2}+|r||t|+|t|^{n+1})
and the mixed term is estimated by Young inequality:

|r||t|\leq\frac{n}{n+1}|r|^{(n+1)/n}+\frac{1}{n+1}|t|^{n+1}.

This finishes the proof of (
3.2
).

The map
F
is open at
0
if the map
F\circ\Phi
is open at
0
. And
F\circ\Phi
is open at
0

if and only if the map

(r,t)\mapsto\Psi(r,t)=F(\Phi(r,\varrho(t)))=d_{0}F(r)+D_{0}^{n}F(w(\varrho(t)),0)+R(r,\varrho(t))

is open at
(r,t)=0
. We will show that
\Psi
is open at zero by a fixed point argument.

With respect to the factorization
(r,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, we introduce the norm
\|(r,t)\|=\max\{|r|,\lambda_{0}|t|\}
and the balls

B_{\delta}=\{(r,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:\|(r,t)\|\leq\delta\}

for positive
\delta>0
. The balls
B_{\delta}
are compact and convex.
The parameter
\lambda_{0}>0
will be fixed later.

The map
\Psi
is open at
0
if for any (small)
\varepsilon>0

there exists
\delta>0
such that
B_{\delta}\subset\Psi(B_{\varepsilon})
.
We pick
(\xi,\tau)\in B_{\delta}
and we look for
(r,t)\in B_{\varepsilon}
such that
\Psi(r,t)=(\xi,\tau)
.
We factorize

D_{0}^{n}F(w(\varrho(t)),0)=(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}F(w(\varrho(t))),g(t))=(f(t),g(t)),

and
R(r,\varrho(t))=(R_{1}(r,t),R_{2}(r,t))\in\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. Here, with a slight abuse of notation, we are incorporating
\varrho
into
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
.

Since
g
is continuous and homogeneous of degree
1
, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

(3.6)

|g(t)|\leq C_{1}|t|.

By (
3.2
), for any
0<\sigma\leq 1
there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that for
|r|+|t|\leq\varepsilon
(in particular for
(r,t)\in B_{\varepsilon}
)
we have

(3.7)

|R_{1}(r,t)|+|R_{2}(r,t)|\leq\sigma(|r|+|t|).

We will fix
\sigma
in a while.

Equation

\Psi(r,t)=(\xi,\tau)
is then equivalent to the system

(3.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d_{0}F(r)+g(t)+R_{1}(r,t)=\xi\\
f(t)+R_{2}(r,t)=\tau,\end{array}\right.

that reads as the following fixed-point system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r=d_{0}F^{-1}\big(\xi-g(t)-R_{1}(r,t)\big)=h_{1}(r,t)\\
t=f^{-1}\big(\tau-R_{2}(r,t)\big)=h_{2}(r,t).\end{array}\right.

We claim that the map
h=(h_{1},h_{2})
maps
B_{\varepsilon}
into
itself, provided that
\delta>0
,
\sigma>0
, and
\lambda_{0}>0
are small enough.
Indeed, by (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) we have

\begin{split}|h_{1}(r,t)|&\leq\|d_{0}F^{-1}\|(|\xi|+|g(t))|+|R_{1}(r,t)|)\\
&\leq C_{2}(|\xi|+|t|+\sigma(|r|+|t|))\\
&\leq C_{2}(\delta+2\lambda_{0}\varepsilon+\sigma\varepsilon).\end{split}

Choosing
\delta\leq\varepsilon/3C_{2}
,
\lambda_{0}=1/6C_{2}
, and
\sigma\leq 1/3C_{2}
we obtain
|h_{1}(r,t)|\leq\varepsilon
.

On the other hand, by (
2.11
) and (
3.7
)

\begin{split}|h_{2}(r,t)|&\leq L(|\tau|+|R_{2}(r,t)|)\\
&\leq L(|\tau|+\sigma(|r|+|t|))\\
&\leq L(\lambda_{0}\delta+2\sigma\varepsilon).\end{split}

Choosing
\delta\leq\varepsilon/2L
and
\sigma\leq\lambda_{0}/4L
we obtain
|h_{2}(r,t)|\leq\lambda_{0}\varepsilon
.
This finishes the proof that for each
(\xi,\tau)\in B_{\delta}
there exists
(r,t)\in B_{\varepsilon}
solving the system (
3.8
).

∎

When the corank is
\ell=1
, by Proposition
2.8
we have the following:

4.
Integrals on simplexes

In this section, we prove some elementary properties of integrals on simplexes that will be used in the analysis of the end-point map.
Here and hereafter,
I=[0,1]
denotes the unit interval. We fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
(it will be the rank of the distribution of vector fields on the manifold) and in the rest of the paper we let

X=L^{2}(I;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The tensor product
\otimes:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell m}
is defined by

(v\otimes w)^{k}=v^{i}w^{j},\quad k=m(i-1)+j,

where
1\leq i\leq\ell
and
1\leq j\leq m
. Above, we are using the notation
v=(v^{1},\dots,v^{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, etc.
The map
\otimes
is associative but not commutative.

Definition 4.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t,s\in I
such that
t+s\leq 1
, we define the
n
-dimensional simplex

(4.1)

\Sigma_{n}(t,s)=\big\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in I^{n}\mid t<t_{n}<\dots<t_{1}<t+s\big\}.

When
t=0
and
s=1
we use the short notation
\Sigma_{n}=\Sigma_{n}(0,1)
.
We also let

(4.2)

\Sigma_{n}^{\flat}(t,s)=\big\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in I^{n}\mid t<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n}<t+s\big\},

and
\Sigma_{n}^{\flat}=\Sigma_{n}^{\flat}(0,1).

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define the subset of
X

(4.3)

U_{n}=\left\{v\in X\mid\int_{\Sigma_{n}}v(t_{n})\otimes\dots\otimes v(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}=0\right\},

Here and in the following, we denote by
\mathscr{L}^{n}
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We also set

(4.4)

V_{n}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}.

For any multi-index
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n,d}=\big\{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n}\mid\alpha_{i}\in\{1,\dots,d\}\big\}
and
v\in X
, we define the integral

I_{n}^{\alpha}(v)=\int_{\Sigma_{n}}v^{\alpha_{n}}(t_{n})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}.

Then
v\in U_{n}
if and only if
I_{n}^{\alpha}(v)=0
for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n,d}
.

For
v\in X
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t,s\in I
such that
t+s\leq 1
, we let

\begin{split}I_{n}(t,s;v)&=\int_{\Sigma_{n}(t,s)}v(t_{n})\otimes\dots\otimes v(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n},\\
I_{n}^{\flat}(t,s;v)&=\int_{\Sigma_{n}^{\flat}(t,s)}v(t_{n})\otimes\dots\otimes v(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}.\end{split}

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
v\in V_{n}
and
t\in I
we have

(4.5)

I_{n}^{\flat}(0,t;v)=(-1)^{n}I_{n}(t,1-t;v).

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
. When
n=1
the claim reads

\int_{0}^{t}v(s)ds=-\int_{t}^{1}v(s)ds,\quad t\in I,

that holds true because
v\in V_{1}
means
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}v(s)ds=0
.

We assume that formula (
4.5
) holds for
n-1
and we prove it for
n
. Indeed, using first
v\in U_{n}
and then
v\in V_{n-1}
we get

\begin{split}I_{n}^{\flat}(0,t;v)&=\int_{0}^{t}v(t_{n})\otimes I_{n-1}^{\flat}(0,t_{n};v)dt_{n}\\
&=-\int_{t}^{1}v(t_{n})\otimes I_{n-1}^{\flat}(0,t_{n};v)dt_{n}\\
&=(-1)^{n}\int_{t}^{1}v(t_{n})\otimes I_{n-1}(t_{n},1-t_{n};v)dt_{n}\\
&=(-1)^{n}I_{n}(t,1-t;v).\end{split}

∎

The reverse parametrization of a function
v\in X

is the function
v^{\flat}\in X
defined by the formula

v^{\flat}(t)=v(1-t),\quad t\in I.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let

v\in X
. Then
v\in V_{n}
if and only if
v^{\flat}\in V_{n}
.

Proof.

If
v\in V_{n}
, by Lemma
4.2
with
t=0
it follows that
v^{\flat}\in V_{n}
.
The opposite implication follows from
v^{\flat\flat}=v
.
∎

The set
V_{n}
is stable with respect to localization.
Given
v\in X
,
s>0
and
t_{0}\in I
such that
t_{0}+s\leq 1
, we define

(4.6)

v_{t_{0},s}(t)=v\left(\frac{t-t_{0}}{s}\right)\chi_{[t_{0},t_{0}+s]}(t),\quad t\in I.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
v\in V_{n}
then
v_{t_{0},s}\in V_{n}
for all
s>0
and
t\in I
such that
t_{0}+s\leq 1
.

Proof.

The claim
v_{t_{0},s}\in X
is clear. We prove that, for every
1\leq i\leq n
,

(4.7)

I_{i}(t_{0},s;v_{t_{0},s})=\int_{\Sigma_{i}(t_{0},s)}v_{t_{0},s}(t_{i})\otimes\dots\otimes v_{t_{0},s}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}=0.

Indeed, by the change of variable
(t_{1},\dots,t_{i})=(s\tau_{1}+t_{0},\dots,s\tau_{i}+t_{0})
, we get

I_{i}(t_{0},s;v_{t_{0},s})=s^{i}I_{i}(0,1;v)=0.

∎

The set
V_{n-1}\subset X
is not a linear space and the map
v\mapsto I_{n}(v)=I_{n}(0,1;v)
is not additive.
However, we can construct linear subsets of
V_{n-1}
of any finite dimension starting from one function.
Given
v\in X
, we define
v_{1},v_{2}\in X
letting

v_{1}=v_{0,1/2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad v_{2}=v_{1/2,1/2}.

These are the localization of
v
with
t_{0}=0,1/2
and
s=1/2
.

Proposition 4.5
.

If
v\in V_{n-1}
then
v_{1},v_{2},v_{1}+v_{2}\in V_{n-1}
and

I_{n}(v_{1}+v_{2})=I_{n}(v_{1})+I_{n}(v_{2}).

Moreover, we have
I_{n}(v_{1})=I_{n}(v_{2})=\frac{1}{2^{n}}I_{n}(v)
.

Proof.

The fact that
v_{1},v_{2}\in V_{n-1}
is proved in Lemma
4.4
.
We show the remaining claims. For any multi-index
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h,2}
,
1\leq h\leq n-1
, consider the integral

(4.8)

I^{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{2})=\int_{\Sigma_{h}}v^{1}_{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})\dots v^{h}_{\alpha_{h}}(t_{h})d\mathscr{L}^{h}.

Letting
I_{1}=[0,1/2]
and
I_{2}=[1/2,1]
, the support of the function
v^{1}_{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})\dots v^{h}_{\alpha_{h}}(t_{h})
is contained in the product

I_{\alpha_{1}}\times\dots\times I_{\alpha_{h}}
. If there exist
i<j
such that
\alpha_{i}=1<\alpha_{j}=2
, then
\Sigma_{h}\cap I_{\alpha_{1}}\times\dots\times I_{\alpha_{h}}=\emptyset
, and then
I^{\alpha}(v_{1}+v_{2})=0
.

The complementary case is when
\alpha_{1}=\dots=\alpha_{k}=2
and
\alpha_{k+1}=\dots=\alpha_{h}=1
for some
k=0,1,\dots,h
.
In this case,
the integral in (
4.8
) splits
into the product of two integrals:

I^{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{2})=\Big(\int_{\Sigma_{k}}v^{1}_{2}(t_{1})\dots v^{k}_{2}(t_{k})d\mathscr{L}^{k}\Big)\Big(\int_{\Sigma_{h-k}}v^{k+1}_{1}(t_{k+1})\dots v^{h}_{1}(t_{h})d\mathscr{L}^{h-k}\Big).

If
v_{1},v_{2}\in V_{n-1}
this shows that
I^{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{2})=0
for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h,2}
and for all
h\leq n-1
.
This proves that
v_{1}+v_{2}\in V_{n-1}
.

When
h=n
the argument above shows that for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n,2}
such that
\alpha\neq(1,\dots,1)
and
\alpha\neq(2,\dots,2)
we have

I^{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{2})=0
. We conclude that

I_{n}(v_{1}+v_{2})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n,2}}I^{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{2})=I_{n}(v_{1})+I_{n}(v_{2}).

∎

5.
Expansion of the end-point map

In this section we expand the end-point map and we compute its
n
th order term. The computations use the language of chronological calculus for non-autonomous vector fields. A detailed introduction to this formalism can be found in
[
3
,
Chapter 2]
.

A different approach to the Taylor expansion of the end-point map in Lie groups using adapted coordinates is given in

[
15
]
.

Let
M
be a manifold with dimension
m=\mathrm{dim}(M)
. Since our analysis is local, we shall without loss of generality identify
M
with
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
So
M
-valued maps will be in fact
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued, fitting the framework of Section
2
.

For vector fields
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\in
Vec
(M)
and
u\in X
, we define the time-dependent vector field
f_{u(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}(t)f_{i}
. For a fixed initial point
q\in M
, the end-point map
F_{q}:X\to M
is defined as

F_{q}(u)=q\circ\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{0}^{1}f_{u(t)}dt,\quad u\in X.

Here,
\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{0}^{1}f_{u(t)}dt
denotes the right exponential of a time-dependent vector field. As explained in
[
3
, Chapter 20]
,
points of
M
, vector fields, and diffeomorphisms of
M
are identified with operators on
C^{\infty}(M)
. In this formalism,

\circ
stands for a composition of operators.

We denote by
\bar{q}=F_{q}(u)
the end-point and we define the map

G^{u}_{\bar{q}}:X\to M
letting

G^{u}_{\bar{q}}(v)=\bar{q}\circ\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{0}^{1}g_{v(t)}^{u,t}dt,\quad v\in X,

where
g_{v(t)}^{u,t}
is the time-dependent vector field

(5.1)

g_{v(t)}^{u,t}=\mathrm{Ad}\left(\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{1}^{t}f_{u(\tau)}d\tau\right)f_{v(t)}.

The maps
F_{q}
and
G^{u}_{\bar{q}}
are related by the variation formula, see
[
3
, Formula
(2.28)
]
.
For

u,v\in X
we have

\displaystyle F_{q}(u+v)

\displaystyle=q\circ\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{0}^{1}f_{u(t)+v(t)}dt

\displaystyle=q\circ\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{0}^{1}f_{u(t)}dt\circ\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Ad}\left(\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{1}^{t}f_{u(\tau)}d\tau\right)f_{v(t)}dt

\displaystyle=G^{u}_{\bar{q}}(v).

For the definition of the
\mathrm{Ad}
operator in chronological calculus see
[
3
, Chapter 2]
.

The control
u
is a critical point of corank
\ell

for
F
if and only if 0 is a critical point of
G^{u}_{\bar{q}}
of corank

\ell
. We shall omit the subscript
\bar{q}
and the superscript
u
and write
G=G^{u}_{\bar{q}}
. We call
G

variation map
.

Our next goal is to compute the Taylor’s expansion of the variation map. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
v\in X
, we define the vector field
W_{k}(v)
as

(5.2)

\begin{split}W_{k}(v)&=\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\mathrm{ad}g_{v(\tau_{k})}^{u,\tau_{k}}\circ\dots\circ\mathrm{ad}g_{v(\tau_{2})}^{u,\tau_{2}}\big(g_{v(\tau_{1})}^{u,\tau_{1}}\big)d\mathscr{L}^{k}\\
&=\int_{\Sigma_{k}}[g_{v(\tau_{k})}^{u,\tau_{k}},\dots,g_{v(\tau_{1})}^{u,\tau_{1}}]d\mathscr{L}^{k}.\end{split}

Here and hereafter, we use the following notation for the iterated bracket of vector fields
g_{k},\dots,g_{1}
:

[g_{k},\dots,g_{1}]=[g_{k},[\cdots,[g_{2},g_{1}]\cdots]]=\mathrm{ad}g_{k}\circ\cdots\circ\mathrm{ad}g_{2}(g_{1}).

For a multi-index
\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{k}
we define the operator (composition of vector fields)

(5.3)

W_{\beta}(v)=W_{\beta_{1}}(v)\circ\dots\circ W_{\beta_{k}}(v).

The operator-valued map
v\mapsto W_{\beta}(v)
introduced in (
5.3
)
is polynomial in
v
with homogeneous degree
p=|\beta|
. Its
p
-polarization is defined via the formula

(5.4)

W_{\beta}(v_{1},\dots,v_{p})=\frac{1}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p}}{\partial t_{1}\dots\partial t_{p}}W_{\beta}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{p}t_{i}v_{i}\Big)\bigg|_{t_{1}=\dots=t_{p}=0},

where
v_{1},\dots,v_{p}\in X
.
This definition is consistent with (
2.1
).

By the argument of Lemma 3.3 and Remark 3.4 in
[
7
]
,
for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
and for any
v\in X
the
p
-differential of
G
has the representation

(5.5)

d_{0}^{p}G(v)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{k},|\beta|=p}c_{\beta}W_{\beta}(v),

where, for any
\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{k}
, we set

c_{\beta}=|\beta|!\prod_{s=1}^{k}(\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{s})^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}.

Using these formulas we obtain a representation for the differentials
D^{h}_{0}G
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
h\in\mathbb{N}
and for all

v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{h})\in X^{h}
we have

(5.6)

D_{0}^{h}G(v)=\sum_{p=1}^{h}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{p},|\alpha|=h}\frac{h!}{\alpha!p!}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{k},|\beta|=p}c_{\beta}W_{\beta}(v_{\alpha}),

where
v_{\alpha}=(v_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,v_{\alpha_{p}})
for
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{p}
.

Proof.

Formula (
2.3
) reads

(5.7)

D_{0}^{h}G(v)=\sum_{p=1}^{h}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{p},|\alpha|=h}\frac{h!}{\alpha!p!}d_{0}^{p}G(v_{\alpha}),

and by (
2.1
), (
5.5
), and (
5.4
) we deduce that, for
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{p})\in X^{p}
,

(5.8)

\begin{split}d_{0}^{p}G(w)&=\frac{1}{p!}\frac{\partial^{p}}{\partial t_{1}\dots\partial t_{p}}d_{0}^{p}G\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{p}t_{i}w_{i}\Big)\bigg|_{t_{1}=\dots=t_{p}=0}\\
&=\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{k},|\beta|=p}c_{\beta}\frac{\partial^{p}}{\partial t_{1}\dots\partial t_{p}}W_{\beta}\Big(\sum_{h=1}^{p}t_{h}w_{h}\Big)\bigg|_{t_{1}=\dots=t_{p}=0}\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{k},|\beta|=p}c_{\beta}W_{\beta}(w).\end{split}

∎

For a given
v\in X
let us consider the localization
v_{t_{0},s}
for some
t_{0}\in[0,1)
and
small
s>0
, as in (
4.6
).

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{N}
,
v\in X
, and
t_{0}\in(0,1)
. For any
s\in(0,1-t_{0})
we have

(5.9)

W_{h}(v_{t_{0},s})=s^{h}\int_{\Sigma_{h}}[g_{v(t_{h})}^{t_{0}},\dots,g_{v(t_{1})}^{t_{0}}]d\mathscr{L}^{h}+O(s^{h+1}).

Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that
|O(s^{h+1})|\leq Cs^{h+1}
for all
v\in X
with
\|v\|_{X}\leq 1
.

Proof.

With the notation introduced in (
5.1
) and omitting the superscript
u
, we have

\begin{split}g_{v_{t_{0},s}(t)}^{\tau}&=\mathrm{Ad}\left(\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{1}^{\tau}f_{u(\sigma)}d\sigma\right)f_{v_{t_{0},s}(t)}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{t_{0},s}^{i}(t)\mathrm{Ad}\left(\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{1}^{\tau}f_{u(\sigma)}d\sigma\right)f_{i}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{t_{0},s}^{i}(t)g^{\tau}_{i},\end{split}

where
g^{\tau}_{i}
is defined via the last identity. Letting, for
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h,d}
,

(5.10)

J_{t_{0},s}^{\alpha}=\int_{\Sigma_{h}(t_{0},s)}v_{t_{0},s}^{\alpha_{h}}(\tau_{h})\dots v_{t_{0},s}^{\alpha_{1}}(\tau_{1})[g^{\tau_{h}}_{\alpha_{h}},\dots,g^{\tau_{1}}_{\alpha_{1}}]d\mathscr{L}^{h},

formula (
5.2
) reads

W_{h}(v_{t_{0},s})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h,d}}J_{t_{0},s}^{\alpha}.

With the change of variable

\vartheta_{i}=\frac{\tau_{i}-t_{0}}{s}
, for
i=1,\dots,n
, the integral in (
5.10
) becomes

(5.11)

J_{t_{0},s}^{\alpha}=s^{h}\int_{\Sigma_{h}}v^{\alpha_{h}}(\vartheta_{h})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(\vartheta_{1})[g^{s\vartheta_{h}+t_{0}}_{\alpha_{h}},\dots,g^{s\vartheta_{1}+t_{0}}_{\alpha_{1}}]d\mathscr{L}^{h}.

Since the maps

t\mapsto g^{t}_{i}=\mathrm{Ad}\left(\overrightarrow{\exp}\int_{1}^{t}f_{u(\sigma)}d\sigma\right)f_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,d,

are Lipschitz continuous, for every
i=1,\dots,d
and
j=1,\dots,h
we have the expansion

g_{i}^{s\vartheta_{j}+t_{0}}=g_{i}^{t_{0}}+O(s),

with a uniform error
O(s)
for
\vartheta_{j}\in I
. So we conclude that

\displaystyle J_{t_{0},s}^{\alpha}=s^{h}[g_{\alpha_{h}}^{t_{0}},\dots,g_{\alpha_{1}}^{t_{0}}]\int_{\Sigma_{h}}v^{\alpha_{h}}(\vartheta_{h})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(\vartheta_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{h}+O(s^{h+1}).

The claim (
5.9
) follows by summing over
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h,d}
.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
v\in V_{h}
for some
h\in\mathbb{N}
and
t_{0}\in(0,1)
. For any
s\in(0,1-t_{0})
we have

d_{0}^{h}G(v_{t_{0},s},\dots,v_{t_{0},s})=O(s^{h+1})
.

Proof.

By formula (
5.8
), the
h
-differential of
G
has the representation

(5.12)

d_{0}^{h}G(w)=\sum_{k=1}^{h}\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{k},|\beta|=h}c_{\beta}W_{\beta}(v_{t_{0},s},\dots,v_{t_{0},s}).

Let
\beta\in\mathscr{I}_{k}
with
|\beta|=h
.
We claim that the coefficient of
s^{h}
in the expansion of
s\mapsto W_{\beta}(v_{t_{0},s},\dots,v_{t_{0},s})
vanishes.
Indeed, consider the coordinate
j=\beta_{i}
. By
Proposition
5.2
we have

\begin{split}W_{j}(v_{t_{0},s})&=s^{j}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{j,d}}[g_{\alpha_{j}}^{t_{0}},\dots,g_{\alpha_{1}}^{t_{0}}]\int_{\Sigma_{j}}v^{\alpha_{j}}(\vartheta_{j})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(\vartheta_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{j}+O(s^{j+1})\\
&=O(s^{j+1}),\end{split}

because for
j\leq h
we have

\int_{\Sigma_{j}}v^{\alpha_{j}}(\vartheta_{h})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(\vartheta_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{j}=0,

by our assumption
v\in V_{h}
and by Lemma
4.2
.
The claim follows.

∎

Assume that for small
s>0
we have
w^{s}=(w_{1}^{s},\ldots,w_{n-1}^{s})\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G)
where
w_{1}^{s}=v_{t_{0},s}
for some
v\in X
and
t_{0}\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
v\in V_{n-1}
then
\|w_{j}^{s}\|_{X}=O(s^{j+1})
,
s\to 0^{+}
, for all
j=2,\dots,n-1
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
j=2,\dots,n-1
. We start with
j=2
.
Since
(w_{1}^{s},w_{2}^{s})\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{3}G)
we have
D_{0}^{2}G(w_{1}^{s},w_{2}^{s})=0
, and by (
2.3
) this equation
reads

d_{0}G(w_{2}^{s})=-d_{0}^{2}G(v_{t_{0},s},v_{t_{0},s})=O(s^{3}),

by Corollary
5.3
. The claim follows composing with the inverse of
d_{0}G
.

Now we assume that the claim holds for
j\leq n-2
and we prove it for
j=n-1
.
Since
w^{s}\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G)
we have
D_{0}^{n-1}G(w^{s})=0
and, by (
2.3
), this equation
reads

d_{0}G(w_{n-1}^{s})=-d_{0}^{n-1}G(v_{t_{0},s},\dots,v_{t_{0},s})-\sum_{h=2}^{n-2}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h},|\alpha|=n-1}\frac{(n-1)!}{\alpha!h!}d_{0}^{h}G\big(w^{s}_{\alpha}\big).

We have
d_{0}^{n-1}G(v_{t_{0},s},\dots,v_{t_{0},s})=O(s^{n})
, by Corollary
5.3
.

We estimate the terms in the sum.
When
2\leq h\leq n-2
and
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{h}
with
|\alpha|=n-1
, the multi-index
\alpha
contains at least one coordinate different from
1
and does not contain the coordinate
n-1
, and so

\mathrm{Card}\{j\mid\alpha_{j}=1\}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(i+1)\,\mathrm{Card}\{j\mid\alpha_{j}=i\}>|\alpha|=n-1.

Then, from our inductive assumption it follows that
d_{0}^{h}G\big(w^{s}_{\alpha}\big)=O(s^{n})
.

∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Assume that for small
s>0
we have
w^{s}=(w_{1}^{s},\ldots,w_{n-1}^{s})\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G)
, with
w_{1}^{s}=v_{t_{0},s}
for some
v\in X
and
t_{0}\in(0,1)
.
If
v\in V_{n-1}
then we have for
s\to 0^{+}

(5.13)

D_{0}^{n}G(w^{s})=c_{n}s^{n}\int_{\Sigma_{n}}[g_{v(t_{n})}^{t_{0}},\dots,g_{v(t_{1})}^{t_{0}}]d\mathscr{L}^{n}+O(s^{n+1}).

Proof.

By formula (
5.7
),

D_{0}^{n}G(w^{s})=\sum_{p=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{p},|\alpha|=n}\frac{n!}{\alpha!p!}d_{0}^{p}G(w^{s}_{\alpha}).

If
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{p}
has one entry different from 1, then
d_{0}^{p}G(w^{s}_{\alpha})=O(s^{n+1})
by Lemma
5.4
.
The leading term is given by
p=n
and
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n}
with
\alpha=(1,\dots,1)
.
The expansion of this term is given by
formula (
5.9
) with
h=n
and this yields formula (
5.13
).

∎

6.
Open mapping property for the extended end-point map

In this section, we study the open mapping property for the extended end-point map at critical points of corank
1
.
As in Section
5
, we denote by
\bar{q}=F_{q}(u)
the end-point and we consider the variation map
G=G_{\bar{q}}^{u}
.
The cokernel
\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}G)
is a subset of the tangent space
T_{\bar{q}}M
. We identify
M
and
T_{\bar{q}}M
with
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\in\mathrm{Vec}(M)
be smooth vector fields on the manifold
M
spanning the distribution
\Delta
and satisfying the Hörmander condition (
1.1
). If
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}
are declared orthonormal, the length of a horizontal curve
\gamma\in AC(I;M)
,
\dot{\gamma}=f_{u}(\gamma)
, is
L(\gamma)=\|u\|_{L^{1}(I;\mathbb{R}^{d})}
while its energy is given by the functional
J:X\to[0,\infty)

(6.1)

J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I;\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

The minimizers of
J
coincide with minimizers of the length by standard arguments.
The extended end-point map is the map
F_{J}:X\to M\times\mathbb{R}

given by
F_{J}(u)=(F(u),J(u))
.

Definition 6.1

(Regular, singular, strictly singular)
.

A critical point
u\in X
of
F_{J}
is
regular
(
resp.,

singular
) if there exists a nonzero
(\lambda,\lambda_{0})\in\mathrm{Im}(d_{u}F_{J})^{\perp}\subset T_{F(u)}^{*}M\times\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
(resp.,
\lambda_{0}=0
).
A critical point
u\in X
is
strictly singular
if for every
(\lambda,\lambda_{0})\in\mathrm{Im}(d_{u}F_{J})^{\perp}
we have
\lambda_{0}=0
.

We define the extended variation map
G_{J}(v)=(F(u+v),J(u+v))
. Then,
0\in X
is a regular, singular, strictly singular critical point of
G_{J}
if and only if
u
is a regular, singular, strictly singular critical point of
F_{J}
.

We are interested in strictly singular critical points of
F_{J}
. In this case,
\mathrm{im}(d_{u}F_{J})=\mathrm{im}(d_{u}F)\oplus\mathbb{R}
, that is,

\mathrm{coker}(d_{u}F_{J})
and
\mathrm{coker}(d_{u}F)
are isomorphic and can be identified.
The differential analysis of the extended map
F_{J}
can be consequently reduced to the analysis of the end-point map
F
.
In fact, for any
h\geq 2
we have

\mathscr{D}_{u}^{h}F_{J}=\mathscr{D}_{u}^{h}F\big|_{\ker(d_{u}F_{J})}
,
where the kernel
\ker(d_{u}F_{J})=\ker(d_{u}F)\cap\ker(d_{u}J)
is finitely complemented in
X
, and the restriction to
\ker(d_{u}F_{J})
means
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}F_{J})=\{v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}F)\,|\,v_{1}\in\ker(d_{u}J)\}
.
Similarly, we have

(6.2)

\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}G_{J}=\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}G\big|_{\ker(d_{0}G_{J})},\qquad h\geq 2,

with
\ker(d_{0}G_{J})
finitely complemented in
X
, and

(6.3)

\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}G_{J})=\{v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{h}G)\,|\,v_{1}\in\ker(d_{u}J)\}.

Finally,
0\in X
is a critical point for
G_{J}
of corank
\ell=1
if and only if
u
is a critical point for
G
of corank
\ell=1
.

Thanks to the previous remarks, we can without loss of generality consider the situation where

0
is a corank-one critical point for
G
. This means that
\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}G)
has dimension
1
.
We fix a nonzero dual vector
\lambda\in\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}G)^{*}
such that

\langle\lambda,w\rangle=\mathrm{pr}(w)
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\mathrm{pr}
is the projection onto
\mathrm{coker}(d_{0}G)
.

For
n\geq 2
and
t_{0}\in[0,1)
, we consider the function
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}:X\to\mathbb{R}

(6.4)

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)=\int_{\Sigma_{n}}\langle\lambda,[g_{v(t_{n})}^{t_{0}},\dots,g_{v(t_{1})}^{t_{0}}]\rangle d\mathscr{L}^{n},\quad v\in X.

This is the coefficient of the leading term in the expansion of
D_{0}^{n}G(w_{t_{0},s})
in (
5.13
) scalarized with
\lambda
, up to the constant
c_{n}
.
Here and hereafter, vector fields are evaluated at the end-point
\bar{q}
, with notation as in the previous section.

For a multi-index
\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n,d}
let us introduce the short notation

(6.5)

[g_{\alpha}^{t_{0}}]=[g_{\alpha_{n}}^{t_{0}},\dots,g_{\alpha_{1}}^{t_{0}}],

where the entries
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
appear in the bracket with reversed order, and

(6.6)

I^{\alpha}(v)=\int_{\Sigma_{n}}v^{\alpha_{n}}(t_{n})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}.

Then formula (
6.4
)
reads

(6.7)

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n,d}}\langle\lambda,[g_{\alpha}^{t_{0}}]\rangle I^{\alpha}(v).

We remind that the space
V_{n}
is defined as

V_{n}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}U_{i},

where

U_{n}=\left\{v\in X\mid\int_{\Sigma_{n}}v(t_{n})\otimes\dots\otimes v(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}=0\right\},

and
\Sigma_{n}
is the standard
n
-dimensional simplex (see equations (
4.1
)-(
4.4
) in Section 4).

Lemma 6.2
.

If
v\in V_{n-1}
then
v^{\flat}\in V_{n-1}
and
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v^{\flat})=(-1)^{n-1}\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)
.

Proof.

We have
v^{\flat}\in V_{n-1}
by Corollary
4.3
. By Lemma
4.2
– here we use the assumption
v\in V_{n-1}
, – the integrals
I^{\alpha}(v)
can be transformed in the following way:

\begin{split}I^{\alpha}(v)&=\int_{0}^{1}v^{\alpha_{n}}(t_{n})\Big(\int_{\Sigma_{n-1}(t_{n},1-t_{n})}v^{\alpha_{n-1}}(t_{n-1})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n-1}\Big)dt_{n}\\
&=(-1)^{n-1}\int_{0}^{1}v^{\alpha_{n}}(t_{n})\Big(\int_{\Sigma_{n-1}^{\flat}(0,t_{n})}v^{\alpha_{n-1}}(t_{n-1})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n-1}\Big)dt_{n}\\
&=(-1)^{n-1}\int_{\Sigma_{n}^{\flat}}v^{\alpha_{n}}(t_{n})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}\\
&=(-1)^{n-1}I^{\alpha}(v^{\flat}).\end{split}

The last identity follows by the change of variable
t_{i}=1-s_{i}
.
This proves our claim
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v^{\flat})=(-1)^{n-1}\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)
.

∎

In the next step, we show that if
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}
is positive and additive on a suitable subspace of
V_{n-1}

then the extended map
G_{J}
is open at zero.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
0
be a strictly singular critical point of
G_{J}

with corank
1
and with
\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G_{J})
of finite codimension
h\in\mathbb{N}
.
Assume that there exist
t_{0}\in[0,1)
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in V_{n-1}
such that:

i)

k=h+1
when
n
is even and
k=2(h+1)
when
n
is odd;

ii)

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v_{i})=1
for
i=1,\dots,k
;

iii)

v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
span a vector space
Y\subset V_{n-1}
of dimension
k
;

iv)

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}
is additive on
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
, in the sense that

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}v_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(\tau_{i}v_{i})

for any
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Then the extended map

G_{J}
is open at
0
.

Proof.

We denote by
W\subset X
a
h
-codimensional linear set of elements
w_{1}\in X
such that there exist
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1})\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}^{n}_{0}G)
.
For
s>0
, let
L_{s}:X=L^{1}(I;\mathbb{R}^{d})\to X_{s}{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,0}:=\,}L^{1}([t_{0},t_{0}+s];\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the linear isomorphism

L_{s}(v)=v_{t_{0},s}
and define

W_{s}=L_{s}^{-1}(W\cap X_{s}).

Since
W\cap X_{s}

has codimension at most
h
in
X_{s}
, then
L_{s}^{-1}(W\cap X_{s})\subset X
has codimension at most
h

and thus

(6.8)

\displaystyle\dim(Y\cap W_{s})\geq k-h=1

when
n
is even,

(6.9)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dim(Y\cap W_{s})\geq k-h=k/2+1}

when
n
is odd.

We discuss the case when
n
is even. By iv),
n
-homogeneity and ii),
for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\tau\neq 0
, we have

(6.10)

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}v_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}^{n}>0.

Thus, the function
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}
attains a positive minimum on the sphere
K=\{v\in Y:\|v\|_{X}=1\}
: there exists
\delta>0
such that

(6.11)

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)\geq\delta>0\quad\textrm{for all }v\in K.

By (
6.8
), for any
s>0
there exists
v^{s}\in K
such that

v^{s}_{t_{0},s}=L_{s}(v^{s})\in W
.
Then there exist
w^{s}_{2},\ldots,w_{n-2}^{s}\in X
such that
w^{s}=(v^{s}_{t_{0},s},w_{2}^{s},\ldots,w_{n-1}^{s})\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G_{J})
.
By (
6.2
) and by formula (
5.13
)

\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G_{J}(w^{s})=\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G(w^{s})=s^{n}c_{n}\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v^{s})+O(s^{n+1}),

where
|O(s^{n+1})|\leq C_{1}s^{s+1}
for a constant
C_{1}>0
independent of
v^{s}
with
\|v^{s}\|_{X}\leq 1
.
Choosing
0<s<\frac{1}{2}\delta/C_{1}c_{n}
, from (
6.11
) we deduce that

\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G_{J}(w^{s})\geq s^{n}\big(c_{n}\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v^{s})-C_{1}s\big)\geq s^{n}\frac{\delta}{2}>0.

By Lemma
6.2
, for
n
even we have
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v^{\flat})=-\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)
.
Repeating the above argument starting from
v_{1}^{\flat},\dots,v_{k}^{\flat}
,
we conclude that for all
s>0
small enough there exists

w^{\flat,s}\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G_{J})
such that

\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G_{J}(w^{\flat,s})<0
.
By Corollary
3.2
part i), we conclude that
G_{J}
is open at
0
.

We pass to the case when
n
is odd. For small
s>0
let

m(s)=\sup_{v\in K\cap W_{s}}|\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)|.

We claim that there exists
\delta>0
and an infinitesimal sequence of
s_{j}>0
such that
m(s_{j})\geq\delta
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
If the claim is true, the proof can be concluded as in the even case.

By contradiction, assume that
m(s)\to 0
as
s\to 0^{+}
.
By compactness, there exists an infinitesimal sequence of
s_{j}
such that
Y\cap W_{s_{j}}
is converging
to a subspace of
Y
that, by (
6.9
), has
dimension at least
k/2+1
. Since
m(s)\to 0
, we have
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}=0
on this subspace.
Identifying
Y
with
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, by (
6.10
)
this means that there exists
a
(k/2+1)
-dimensional subspace of
(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

(6.12)

\tau_{1}^{n}+\ldots+\tau_{k}^{n}=0.

But this is not possible because the maximal dimension of linear spaces contained in the homogeneous variety defined by (
6.12
) is
k/2
.

∎

In fact, in order
G_{J}
to be open it is sufficient that
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}
is positive at one element of
V_{n-1}
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
0
be a strictly singular critical point of
G_{J}
with corank
1
and assume that

\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{n}G)
,

, has finite codimension.

If there exist

and

such that

is open at

.

Proof.

For any

, we apply iteratively Proposition
4.5
to find
2^{k}
functions

with mutually disjoint support, spanning a linear space in

and such that

\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{k}}v_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{k}}\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v_{i}),

and
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v_{i})=\frac{1}{2^{kn}}\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)
. The claim follows from Theorem
6.3
.
∎

If
G_{J}
is not open at

we have
\mathscr{G}^{n}_{t_{0}}(v)=0
for all

t_{0}\in[0,1)
and

.
Even though

is not a linear space, we polarize the map

.

The polarization of

is the multilinear map

defined in one of the two equivalent ways

(6.13)

\begin{split}\mathscr{T}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})&=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial t_{1}\dots\partial t_{n}}T\Big(\sum_{h=1}^{n}t_{h}v_{h}\Big)\bigg|_{t_{1}=\dots=t_{n}=0}\\
&=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{I}_{n,d}}\langle\lambda,[g^{t_{0}}_{\alpha}]\rangle\int_{\Sigma_{n}}v^{\alpha_{n}}_{\sigma_{n}}(t_{n})\dots v^{\alpha_{1}}_{\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}.\end{split}

If

is a linear subspace such that

on

then
\mathscr{T}=0
on

.
This follows easily from the differential definition of polarization.
Linear spaces

where

can be obtained with the following construction.

Fix
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in X
, with coordinates
w_{i}=(w_{i}^{1},\dots,w_{i}^{d})
,
and fix a selection function

,

.
This function will be used to select (with multiplicity) which vector-fields from

appear in the bracket
[g_{\alpha}^{t_{0}}]
.

We can define
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in X
setting, for

,

(6.14)

v_{i}=(0,\dots,0,u^{i},0,\dots,0),\quad\textrm{with }u^{i}=w_{i}^{s_{i}},

where
u^{i}
is the
i
th coordinate. Then we define

as
u=(u^{1},\dots,u^{n})
.
The function
u
depends on the selection function
s
, but we do not keep track of this dependence in our notation.
As in (
6.6
), for a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
we let

I^{\sigma}(u)=\int_{\Sigma_{n}}u^{\sigma_{n}}(t_{n})\cdots u^{\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}.

When
u\in V_{n-1}
, where now

is defined as in (

)
and (

) but with

, the polarization

takes the following form.

Lemma 6.5
.

For any selection function
s
, if

then

span a linear subspace of

and

(6.15)

\begin{split}\mathscr{T}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})&=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\langle\lambda,[g^{t_{0}}_{s\sigma}]\rangle I^{\sigma}(u).\end{split}

Proof.

Given

and

, by the structure (

) of

, there holds

∎

7.
Generalized Jacobi identities and integrals on simplexes

In this section we fix a selection function
s:\{1,\dots,n\}\to\{1,\dots,d\}
and
u=(u^{1},\dots,u^{n})
, as in (
6.14
).
For varying
\sigma\in S_{n}
, the brackets
[g_{\sigma}^{t_{0}}]=[g_{\sigma_{n}}^{t_{0}},\dots,g_{\sigma_{1}}^{t_{0}}]
satisfy several linear relations.
Using generalized Jacobi identities, we clean up formula (
6.15
) getting rid of these relations.

Our goal is to prove Theorem
7.1
below. We denote by

S_{n}^{1}:=\{\sigma\in S_{n}\mid\sigma_{1}=1\},

the set of permutations
\sigma\in S_{n}
fixing
1
.

Theorem 7.1
.

For any selection function
s
and for any
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
as in (
6.14
), we have the identity

(7.1)

\mathscr{T}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g^{t_{0}}_{s\sigma}]\rangle I^{\sigma}(u).

The fact that in (
7.1
) the sum is restricted to permutations fixing
1
will be important in the next section.
The proof relies upon the generalized Jacobi identities proved in
[
6
]
.
For
n,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq j\leq n
, let us consider the sets of permutations

(7.2)

X_{nj}=\{\xi\in S_{n}\,|\,\xi_{1}>\xi_{2}>\dots>\xi_{j}=1\textrm{ and }\,\xi_{j}<\xi_{j+1}<\dots<\xi_{n}\},

and

(7.3)

X_{n}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}X_{nj}.

The set
X_{n1}
contains only the identity permutation, while
X_{nn}
contains only the order reversing permutation.
We are denoting elements of
X_{n}
by
\xi
, while in
[
6
]
they are denoted by
\pi
.

Let
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
be elements of a Lie algebra. The action of a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
on the iterated bracket

[g_{n},\dots,g_{1}]=[g_{n},[\dots,[g_{2},g_{1}]\dots]]
is denoted by

\sigma[g_{n},\dots,g_{1}]=[g_{\sigma_{n}},\dots,g_{\sigma_{1}}].

The selection function
s
acts similarly,
s[g_{n},\dots,g_{1}]=[g_{s_{n}},\dots,g_{s_{1}}]
, and so in the notation used above we have

[g^{t_{0}}_{s\sigma}]=s[g^{t_{0}}_{\sigma}]
.

The generalized Jacobi identities of order
n
that we need are
described in the next theorem.

Theorem 7.2
.

For any Lie elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
and for any permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
such that
\sigma_{1}\neq 1
,

(7.4)

\Big(\sigma+\sum_{\xi\in X_{n},\,\sigma\xi(1)=1}(-1)^{\xi^{-1}(1)}\sigma\xi\Big)[g_{n},\dots,g_{1}]=0,

where
X_{n}
is the set of permutations introduced in (

).

Proof.

The proof of formula (
7.4
)
is contained in
[
6
]
on pages 117 and 119.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

For any selection function
s
and
for any
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
as in (
6.14
), we have the identity

(7.5)

\mathscr{T}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g^{t_{0}}_{s\sigma}]\rangle\sum_{\xi\in X_{n}}c_{\xi}I^{\sigma\xi^{-1}}(u),

where
c_{\xi}=(-1)^{1+\xi^{-1}(1)}
.

Proof.

Starting from (
6.15
) and using (
7.4
), we get

(7.6)

\begin{split}n!\,\mathscr{T}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})&=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g_{s\sigma}^{t_{0}}]\rangle I^{\sigma}(u)+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n},\,\sigma_{1}\neq 1}\langle\lambda,[g_{s\sigma}^{t_{0}}]\rangle I^{\sigma}(u)\\
&=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g_{s\sigma}^{t_{0}}]\rangle I^{\sigma}(u)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in X_{n},\,\sigma\in S_{n}\\
\sigma_{1}\neq 1,\,\sigma\xi(1)=1\end{subarray}}c_{\xi}\langle\lambda,[g_{s\sigma\xi}^{t_{0}}]\rangle I^{\sigma}(u)\\
&=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g_{s\sigma}^{t_{0}}]\rangle\Big(I^{\sigma}(u)+\sum_{{\xi\in X_{n},\,\sigma\xi^{-1}(1)\neq 1}}c_{\xi}I^{\sigma\xi^{-1}}(u)\Big)\\
&=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g_{s\sigma}^{t_{0}}]\rangle\sum_{\xi\in X_{n}}c_{\xi}I^{\sigma\xi^{-1}}(u).\end{split}

In the last line, we used the fact that, when
\sigma_{1}=1
, we have
\sigma\xi^{-1}(1)=1
if and only if
\xi
is the identity.

∎

A permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
acts on the integrals
I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)
as
\sigma I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)=I^{\sigma\xi^{-1}}(u)
. So,
the sum over
\xi\in X_{n}
appearing in (

) reads

(7.7)

\sum_{\xi\in X_{n}}c_{\xi}I^{\sigma\xi^{-1}}(u)=\sigma\Big(\sum_{\xi\in X_{n}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)\Big),

where the action is extended linearly.
Our next task is to compute the sum in the round brackets.

A permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
acts on the simplexes
\Sigma_{n}(t,s)
, with
0\leq t<t+s\leq 1
, as

(7.8)

\sigma\Sigma_{n}(t,s)=\big\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid t<t_{\sigma_{n}}<\ldots<t_{\sigma_{1}}<t+s\big\}.

In particular,
if
\bar{\sigma}\in S_{n}
is the reversing order permutation,
\bar{\sigma}(i)=n-i+1
, then

\Sigma_{n}^{\flat}(t,s)=\bar{\sigma}\Sigma_{n}(t,s)
.
We also let

\Sigma_{n}^{\sigma}(t,s)=\sigma\Sigma_{n}(t,s)
and

\Sigma_{n}^{\sigma}=\Sigma_{n}^{\sigma}(0,1)
.

Finally, for
k=1,\ldots,n
we let

\begin{split}I_{k}^{\flat}(u)&=\int_{\Sigma_{k}^{\flat}}u^{1}(s_{1})\dots u^{k}(s_{k})d\mathscr{L}^{k},\\
I_{n-k}(u)&=\int_{\Sigma_{n-k}}u^{k+1}(s_{k+1})\dots u^{n}(s_{n})d\mathscr{L}^{n-k}.\end{split}

Lemma 7.4
.

For any
j=2,\dots,n
we have the identity

(7.9)

\sum_{\xi\in X_{nj}}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)=I_{j-1}^{\flat}(u)I_{n-j+1}(u)-\sum_{\xi\in X_{n,j-1}}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u).

Proof.

Fix a permutation
\xi\in X_{nj}
, so that
\xi_{j}=1
. In the integral

I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)
we perform the change of variable
t_{\xi_{k}}=s_{k}
. The integration domain

\Sigma_{n}
is transformed into the new domain
\Sigma_{n}^{\xi^{-1}}=\{0<s_{\xi^{-1}_{n}}<\dots<s_{\xi^{-1}_{1}}<1\}
:

(7.10)

\begin{split}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)&=\int_{\Sigma_{n}}u^{\xi^{-1}_{n}}(t_{n})\dots u^{\xi^{-1}_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}\\
&=\int_{\Sigma_{n}}u^{n}(t_{\xi_{n}})\dots u^{1}(t_{\xi_{1}})d\mathscr{L}^{n}\\
&=\int_{\Sigma_{n}^{\xi^{-1}}}u^{n}(s_{n})\dots u^{1}(s_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}.\end{split}

We denote by
\widehat{s}_{j}
the variables
(s_{1},\dots,s_{j-1},s_{j+1},\dots,s_{n})
.
Since
s_{\xi^{-1}1}=s_{j}
, the set
\Sigma_{n}^{\xi^{-1}}
is

\Sigma_{n}^{\xi^{-1}}=I\times\Sigma_{n-1;j}^{\xi^{-1}}(0,s_{j}),

where
s_{j}\in I
and

\Sigma_{n-1;j}^{\xi^{-1}}(0,s_{j})=\big\{\widehat{s}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\mid 0<s_{\xi^{-1}_{n}}<\dots<s_{\xi^{-1}_{2}}<s_{j}\big\}.

Since
\xi\in X_{nj}
, here we have
s_{n}<\dots<s_{j+1}<s_{j}
and
s_{1}<\dots<s_{j-1}<s_{j}
. For varying
\xi\in X_{nj}
, we obtain all the shuffles of
s_{1}<\dots<s_{j-1}
into
s_{n}<\dots<s_{j+1}
and thus we have

\bigcup_{\xi\in X_{nj}}\Sigma_{n-1;j}^{\xi^{-1}}(0,s_{j})=A_{j-1}(s_{j})\times B_{n-j}(s_{j}),

where
A_{j-1}(s_{j})=\Sigma_{j-1}^{\flat}(0,s_{j})
and
B_{n-j}(s_{j})=\Sigma_{n-j}(0,s_{j})
.

Summing (
7.10
) over
\xi\in X_{nj}
we get

(7.11)

\begin{split}\sum_{\xi\in X_{nj}}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)&=\sum_{\xi\in X_{nj}}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\int_{\Sigma_{n-1}^{\xi^{-1}}(0,s_{j})}\prod_{k\neq j}u^{k}(s_{k})d\mathscr{L}^{n-1}(\widehat{s}_{j})\Big)u^{j}(s_{j})ds_{j}\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\int_{A_{j-1}(s_{j})\times B_{n-j}(s_{j})}\prod_{k\neq j}u^{k}(s_{k})d\mathscr{L}^{n-1}(\widehat{s}_{j})\Big)u^{j}(s_{j})ds_{j}.\end{split}

The inner integral is the product of two integrals. Namely, letting

(7.12)

\begin{split}f(s_{j})&=u^{j}(s_{j})\int_{B_{n-j}(s_{j})}u^{j+1}(s_{j+1})\dots u^{n}(s_{n})d\mathscr{L}^{n-j},\\
g(s_{j})&=\int_{A_{j-1}(s_{j})}u^{j-1}(s_{j-1})\dots u^{1}(s_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{j-1},\end{split}

formula (
7.11
)
becomes

(7.13)

\begin{split}\sum_{\xi\in X_{nj}}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)=\int_{0}^{1}f(s_{j})g(s_{j})ds_{j}.\end{split}

A primitive for
f
is the function
h(s_{j})=\int_{0}^{s_{j}}f(\sigma)d\sigma
, and an integration by parts gives

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}f(s_{j})g(s_{j})ds_{j}&=h(1)g(1)-\int_{0}^{1}h(s_{j})g^{\prime}(s_{j})ds_{j},\end{split}

where the boundary term is easily computed:

\begin{split}h(1)&=\int_{B_{n-j+1}(1)}u^{j}(s_{j})\dots u^{n}(s_{n})d\mathscr{L}^{n-j+1}=I_{n-j+1}(u),\\
g(1)&=\int_{A_{j-1}(1)}u^{j-1}(s_{j-1})\dots u^{1}(s_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{j-1}=I^{\flat}_{j-1}(u).\end{split}

In order to compute the integral, notice that

g^{\prime}(s_{j})=u^{j-1}(s_{j})\int_{A_{j-2}(s_{j})}u^{j-2}(s_{j-2})\dots u^{1}(s_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{j-2},

and thus, by (
7.11
) but for
j-1
, we have

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}h(s_{j})g^{\prime}(s_{j})ds_{j}&=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\int_{A_{j-2}(\sigma)\times B_{n-j+1}(\sigma)}\prod_{k\neq j-1}u^{k}(s_{k})d\mathscr{L}^{n-1}(\widehat{s}_{j-1})\Big)u^{j-1}(\sigma)d\sigma\\
&=\sum_{\xi\in X_{n,j-1}}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u).\end{split}

∎

Corollary 7.5
.

For any
u\in V_{n-1}
there holds

\sum_{\xi\in X_{n}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)=n\int_{\Sigma_{n}}u^{1}(t_{1})\dots u^{n}(t_{n})d\mathscr{L}^{n}.

Proof.

When
u\in V_{n-1}
we have

I^{\flat}_{j-1}(u)=I_{n-j+1}(u)=0
for
j=2,\dots,n
. Taking into account the constants
c_{\xi}=(-1)^{1+\xi^{-1}_{1}}
, formula (
7.9
) reads

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi\in X_{nj}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in X_{n,j-1}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u).

Applying iteratively this identity, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi\in X_{n}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in X_{nn}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)+\sum_{\xi\in X_{n,n-1}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)+\dots+\sum_{\xi\in X_{n1}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\xi\in X_{n,n-1}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)+\sum_{\xi\in X_{n,n-2}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)+\dots+\sum_{\xi\in X_{n1}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)

\displaystyle=3\sum_{\xi\in X_{n,n-2}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)+\sum_{\xi\in X_{n,n-3}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)+\dots+\sum_{\xi\in X_{n1}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)

\displaystyle=\dots

\displaystyle=n\sum_{\xi\in X_{n1}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u).

Since
X_{n1}
contains only the identity permutation, the claim follows.
∎

With this corollary, we can conclude the proof of Theorem
7.1
. By (
7.7
), identity (
7.5
) of Lemma
7.3
reads

(7.14)

\mathscr{T}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g^{t_{0}}_{s\sigma}]\rangle\sigma\left(\sum_{\xi\in X_{n}}c_{\xi}I^{\xi^{-1}}(u)\right).

Then, applying Corollary
7.5
to (
7.14
) we get

(7.15)

\begin{split}\mathscr{T}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})&=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g^{t_{0}}_{s\sigma}]\rangle\sigma(nI(u))\\
&=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}\langle\lambda,[g^{t_{0}}_{s\sigma}]\rangle I^{\sigma}(u),\end{split}

completing the proof.

8.
Non-singularity via trigonometric functions

We start the study of equation
\mathscr{T}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=0
for the polarization map
\mathscr{T}
in (
7.1
). We will work with functions
v_{i}
as in (
6.14
) of
trigonometric-type.

For each permutation fixing 1,
\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}
, we introduce a real unknow
x_{\sigma}
. There are
(n-1)!=\mathrm{Card}(S_{n}^{1})
unknowns.
We are interested
in the linear system of equations

(8.1)

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}}I^{\sigma}(u_{\tau})x_{\sigma}=0,\quad\tau\in S_{n}^{1},

where

I^{\sigma}(u_{\tau})
are regarded as coefficients depending on
u_{\tau}\in V_{n-1}
. In this section, we prove the following preparatory result.

Theorem 8.1
.

There exist
u_{\tau}\in V_{n-1}
,
\tau\in S_{n}^{1}
, such that
\det(I^{\sigma}(u_{\tau}))_{\sigma,\tau\in S_{n}^{1}}\neq 0
.

With a choice of coefficients as in Theorem
8.1
, the linear system (
8.1
) has only the zero solution, implying
x_{\sigma}=0
for all
\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}
. This fact will be used in Section
9
.

For
z=a+ib\in\mathbb{C}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
we let

w_{z;k}(t)=a\cos(2k\pi t)+b\sin(2k\pi t),\quad t\in I.

We call
w_{z;k}
a
w
-type function of parameters
z
and
k
, and we call
k
frequence of
w_{z;k}
.
We need exact computations for iterated integrals on
n
-simplexes of
w
-type functions.
In particular, we are interested in the case when every linear combination with coefficients
\pm 1
of at most
n-1
frequences out of a set of
n
frequences is not zero, see (
8.4
) below. This condition will ensure assumption
u\in V_{n-1}
in Lemma
6.5
.

Any
w
-type function satisfies the integration formula

(8.2)

\int_{t}^{1}w_{z;k}(s)ds=\frac{1}{2k\pi}\big(w_{iz;k}(1)-w_{iz;k}(t)\big),\quad k\neq 0,

and a pair of
w
-type functions satisfies the multiplication formula (Werner’s identities)

(8.3)

w_{z;k}w_{\zeta;h}=\frac{1}{2}\big(w_{z\zeta;k+h}+w_{\bar{z}\zeta;h-k}\big).

For
h\in\mathbb{N}
, we let

\mathscr{J}_{h}=\{1,\dots,h\}
and

\mathscr{A}_{h}=\big\{\alpha:\mathscr{J}_{h}\to\{1,-1\}\mid\alpha_{1}=1\big\}.

Here, we are denoting
\alpha(j)=\alpha_{j}
and, with a slight abuse of notation, we identify
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h}
with
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{h})\in\{1,-1\}^{h}
.
Letting
\mathrm{z}_{h}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{h})\in\mathbb{C}^{h}
,
for each
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h}
we define the multiplicative function
p_{\alpha}:\mathbb{C}^{h}\to\mathbb{C}

p_{\alpha}(\mathrm{z}_{h})=\prod_{\ell\in\mathscr{J}_{h},\alpha_{\ell}=1}z_{\ell}\prod_{j\in\mathscr{J}_{h},\alpha_{j}=-1}\bar{z}_{j}.

Also, letting
\mathrm{k}_{h}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{h})\in\mathbb{N}^{h}
, we define the additive function
s_{\alpha}:\mathbb{N}^{h}\to\mathbb{N}

s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h})=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\alpha_{j}k_{j}.

Notice that, since
\alpha_{1}=1
,
\bar{z}_{1}
never appears in
p_{\alpha}(\mathrm{z}_{h})
and
k_{1}
has always positive sign in
s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h})
.

Finally, for
\ell,h\in\mathbb{N}
with
\ell\leq h
we let
\mathscr{B}_{\ell}^{h}=\{\beta:\mathscr{J}_{\ell}\to\mathscr{J}_{h}\mid\beta\textrm{ injective}\}
and for
\mathrm{k}_{h}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{h})\in\mathbb{N}^{h}

and
\beta\in\mathscr{B}_{\ell}^{h}
we set
\mathrm{k}_{h}^{\beta}=(k_{\beta_{1}},\dots,k_{\beta_{\ell}})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
.
Here, we are using the math-roman font for vectors and italics for coordinates.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
\mathrm{k}_{n}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be a vector of frequences such that

(8.4)

s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{n}^{\beta})\neq 0\quad\textrm{for all }\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h}\textrm{ and }\,\beta\in\mathscr{B}_{h}^{n},\,\,\ 1\leq h\leq n-1.

Then for any
1\leq h\leq n-1
, for all
\mathrm{z}_{h}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{h})\in\mathbb{C}^{h}
and for all
t\in I
we have

(8.5)

\int_{\Sigma_{h}(t,1-t)}w_{z_{h};k_{h}}(t_{h})\dots w_{z_{1};k_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{h}=g_{\mathrm{z}_{h};\mathrm{k}_{h}}^{h}(t)-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h}}c_{\alpha}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})w_{p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{h});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}(t),

where
c_{\alpha}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})\neq 0
and the function
g_{\mathrm{z}_{h};\mathrm{k}_{h}}^{h}
satisfies

(8.6)

g_{\mathrm{z}_{h};\mathrm{k}_{h}}^{h}(0)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h}}c_{\alpha}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})w_{p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{h});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}(0),

and

(8.7)

\int_{\Sigma_{n-h}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}(t_{n})\dots w_{z_{h+1};k_{h+1}}(t_{h+1})g_{\mathrm{z}_{h};\mathrm{k}_{h}}^{h}(t_{h+1})d\mathscr{L}^{n-h}=0.

The constants
c_{\alpha}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})
in (
8.6
) are given by the formula

(8.8)

c_{\alpha}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})=\frac{2}{4^{h}\pi^{h}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{h}\frac{\alpha_{\ell}}{s_{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell})}(\mathrm{k}_{\ell})}.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 2
.
The formula
(
8.8
)
is well-defined because
s_{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell})}(\mathrm{k}_{\ell})\neq 0

by assumption (
8.4
).

When
n=2
we only have
h=1
and
\alpha=1
, so that
c_{1}^{1}(\mathrm{k}_{1})=1/2\pi k_{1}
.
The integration formula in (
8.2
) gives (
8.5
) with
g_{\mathrm{z}_{1};\mathrm{k}_{1}}^{1}=c_{1}^{1}(\mathrm{k}_{1})w_{iz_{1};k_{1}}(1)
, a constant.
Identity (
8.6
) is satisfied and also identity (
8.7
):

\int_{0}^{1}w_{z_{2};k_{2}}(t_{2})g_{\mathrm{z}_{1};\mathrm{k}_{1}}^{1}dt_{2}=g_{\mathrm{z}_{1};\mathrm{k}_{1}}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}w_{z_{2};k_{2}}(t_{2})dt_{2}=0,

because
k_{2}\neq 0
, again by (
8.4
).

Now we assume the theorem holds for
n-1
and we prove it for
n
.
In particular, from (
8.5
) with
t=0
and (
8.6
) we have the inductive assumption

(8.9)

\int_{\Sigma_{h}}w_{z_{h};k_{h}}(t_{h})\dots w_{z_{1};k_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{h}=0,\quad h=1,\dots,n-2.

We distinguish the cases
h=1
and
2\leq h\leq n-1
. When
h=1
, (
8.5
) is exactly the integration formula
(
8.2
) with

g^{1}_{z_{1},k_{1}}=\frac{1}{2k_{1}\pi}w_{z_{1};k_{1}}(1).

The 1-periodicity of
w
-type functions also proves (
8.6
). In order to prove (
8.7
), we claim that

\int_{\Sigma_{n-1}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}(t_{n})\dots w_{z_{2};k_{2}}(t_{2})d\mathscr{L}^{n-1}=0.

In fact,

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{n-1}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}(t_{n})\dots w_{z_{2};k_{2}}(t_{2})d\mathscr{L}^{n-1}=\;g^{1}_{z_{2},k_{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{n-2}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}(t_{n})\dots w_{z_{3};k_{3}}(t_{3})

\displaystyle d\mathscr{L}^{n-2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2k_{2}\pi}\int_{\Sigma_{n-2}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}(t_{n})\dots w_{z_{3};k_{3}}(t_{3})w_{z_{2};k_{2}}(t_{3})

\displaystyle d\mathscr{L}^{n-2}.

By the multiplication formula (
8.3
), in the second integral are involved the vectors of frequences
(k_{2}\pm k_{3},k_{4},\dots,k_{n})
. Both of them satisfy (
8.4
), by assumption (
8.4
) itself.
Then both the summands vanish thanks to the inductive assumption (
8.9
), proving our claim.

For
2\leq h\leq n-1
, set

D_{h}(t)=\int_{\Sigma_{h}(t,1-t)}w_{z_{h};k_{h}}(t_{h})\dots w_{z_{1};k_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{h}.

When
h\geq 2
, we use the inductive assumption (
8.5
) for
h-1
and the multiplication formula (
8.3
)
to obtain

\displaystyle D_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{1}w_{z_{h};k_{h}}(t_{h})\Big(\int_{\Sigma_{h-1}(t_{h},1-t_{h})}w_{z_{h-1};k_{h-1}}(t_{h-1})\dots w_{z_{1};k_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{h-1}\Big)dt_{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{1}w_{z_{h};k_{h}}(t_{h})\Big(g^{h-1}_{\mathrm{z}_{h-1};\mathrm{k}_{h-1}}-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h-1}}c_{\alpha}^{h-1}(\mathrm{k}_{h-1})w_{p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{h-1});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h-1})}\Big)dt_{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{1}w_{z_{h};k_{h}}g^{h-1}_{\mathrm{z}_{h-1};\mathrm{k}_{h-1}}dt_{h}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h-1}}c_{\alpha}^{h-1}(\mathrm{k}_{h-1})\int_{t}^{1}\big(w_{**}+w_{\dagger\dagger}\big)dt_{h},

where
**=z_{h}p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{h-1});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h-1})+k_{h}
and
\dagger\dagger=\bar{z}_{h}p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{h-1});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h-1})-k_{h}
satisfy

\begin{split}w_{**}&=-w_{ip_{(\alpha,1)}(i\mathrm{z}_{h});s_{(\alpha,1)}(\mathrm{k}_{h})},\\
w_{\dagger\dagger}&=w_{ip_{(\alpha,-1)}(i\mathrm{z}_{h});s_{(\alpha,-1)}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}.\end{split}

By the integration formula (
8.2
), the function
D_{h}
equals

\begin{split}D_{h}&=g^{h}_{\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h}}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h-1}}c_{\alpha}^{h-1}(\mathrm{k}_{h-1})\Big(\frac{w_{p_{(\alpha,1)}(i\mathrm{z}_{h});s_{(\alpha,1)}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}}{s_{(\alpha,1)}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}-\frac{w_{p_{(\alpha,-1)}(i\mathrm{z}_{h});s_{(\alpha,-1)}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}}{s_{(\alpha,-1)}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}\Big),\end{split}

where

(8.10)

g^{h}_{\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h}}(t)=\int_{t}^{1}w_{z_{h};k_{h}}g^{h-1}_{\mathrm{z}_{h-1};\mathrm{k}_{h-1}}dt_{h}+c(\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h}),

with
c(\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h})
a constant that we are going to fix in a moment.
Using
\mathscr{A}_{h}=\{(\alpha,1)\mid\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h-1}\}\cup\{(\alpha,-1)\mid\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h-1}\}
, and
the relations

\frac{1}{4\pi}c_{\alpha}^{h-1}(\mathrm{k}_{h-1})\frac{1}{s_{(\alpha,1)}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}=c_{(\alpha,1)}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{4\pi}c_{\alpha}^{h-1}(\mathrm{k}_{h-1})\frac{1}{s_{(\alpha,-1)}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}=-c_{(\alpha,-1)}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h}),

we conclude that

D_{h}(t)=g^{h}_{\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h}}(t)-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h}}c_{\alpha}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})w_{p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{h});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}(t).

This proves (
8.5
).

We are left with the proof of (
8.6
) and (
8.7
).
The constant above is

c(\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{h}}c_{\alpha}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})w_{p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{h});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{h})}(1).

By the
1
-periodicity of
t\mapsto g^{h}_{\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h}}(t)
, we have

g^{h}_{\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h}}(0)=g^{h}_{\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h}}(1)=c(\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h}),

and this shows (
8.6
).

Finally, we check (
8.7
). By (
8.10
) it is sufficient to show that

(8.11)

\int_{\Sigma_{n-h}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}(t_{n})\dots w_{z_{h+1};k_{h+1}}(t_{h+1})\Big(\int_{t_{h+1}}^{1}w_{z_{h};k_{h}}g^{h-1}_{\mathrm{z}_{h-1};\mathrm{k}_{h-1}}dt_{h}\Big)d\mathscr{L}^{n-h}=0

and

(8.12)

c(\mathrm{z}_{h},\mathrm{k}_{h})\int_{\Sigma_{n-h}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}(t_{n})\dots w_{z_{h+1};k_{h+1}}(t_{h+1})d\mathscr{L}^{n-h}=0.

Identity (
8.12
) holds by (
8.9
) and identity (
8.11
) holds by the inductive validity of (
8.7
).

∎

The explicit formula (
8.8
) for the constants
c_{\alpha}^{h}(\mathrm{k}_{h})
will be crucial in Lemma
8.5
.

Corollary 8.3
.

Let
\mathrm{k}_{n}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be a vector of frequences satisfying (
8.4
)
and assume there exists a unique
\bar{\alpha}\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
of the form
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha,-1)
with
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{n-1}
such that
s_{\bar{\alpha}}(\mathrm{k}_{n})=0
. Then we have

(8.13)

\int_{\Sigma_{n}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}(t_{n})\dots w_{z_{1};k_{1}}(t_{1})d\mathscr{L}^{n}=-\frac{1}{2}c_{\alpha}^{n-1}(\mathrm{k}_{n-1})\mathrm{Re}\big(\bar{z}_{n}p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{n-1})\big).

Proof.

Using formulas (
8.5
) and (
8.7
)
we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\Sigma_{n}}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}\dots w_{z_{1};k_{1}}&d\mathscr{L}^{n}=\int_{0}^{1}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}\Big(\int_{\Sigma(t_{n},1-t_{n})}w_{z_{n-1};k_{n-1}}\dots w_{z_{1};k_{1}}d\mathscr{L}^{n-1}\Big)dt_{n}\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}\Big(g_{\mathrm{z}_{n-1};\mathrm{k}_{n-1}}^{n-1}-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{n-1}}c_{\alpha}^{n-1}(\mathrm{k}_{n-1})w_{p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{n-1});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{n-1})}\Big)dt_{n}\\
&=-c_{\alpha}^{n-1}(\mathrm{k}_{n-1})\int_{0}^{1}w_{z_{n};k_{n}}w_{p_{\alpha}(i\mathrm{z}_{n-1});s_{\alpha}(\mathrm{k}_{n-1})}dt_{n}.\\
\end{split}

Now we use the multiplication formula (
8.3
