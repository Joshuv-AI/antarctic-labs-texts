Generalized Regret Analysis of Thompson Sampling using Fractional Posteriors
Prateek Jaiswal, Debdeep Pati, Anirban Bhattacharya, Bani K. Mallick

Abstract
Thompson sampling (TS) is one of the most popular and earliest algorithms to solve stochastic multi-armed bandit problems. We consider a variant of TS, named α \alpha -TS, where we use a fractional or α \alpha -posterior ( α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) ) instead of the standard posterior distribution. To compute an α \alpha -posterior, the likelihood in the definition of the standard posterior is tempered with a factor α \alpha . For α \alpha -TS we obtain both instance-dependent 𝒪 ⁡ ( ∑ k ≠ i ∗ Δ k ​ ( log ⁡ ( T ) C ⁡ ( α ) ​ Δ k 2 + 1 2 ) ) \mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{k\neq i^{*}}\Delta_{k}\left(\frac{\log(T)}{C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right) and instance-independent 𝒪 ⁡ ( K ​ T ​ log ⁡ K ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT\log K}) frequentist regret bounds under very mild conditions on the prior and reward distributions, where Δ k \Delta_{k} is the gap between the true mean rewards of the k t ​ h k^{th} and the best arms, and C ⁡ ( α ) C(\alpha) is a known constant. Both the sub-Gaussian and exponential family models satisfy our general conditions on the reward distribution. Our conditions on the prior distribution just require its density to be positive, continuous, and bounded. We also establish another instance-dependent regret upper bound that matches (up to constants) to that of improved UCB ( Auer and Ortner 2010 ) . Our regret analysis carefully combines recent theoretical developments in the non-asymptotic concentration analysis and Bernstein-von Mises type results for the α \alpha -posterior distribution. Moreover, our analysis does not require additional structural properties such as closed-form posteriors or conjugate priors.

Introduction

The stochastic multiarmed bandit (MAB) problem is one of the most popular and oldest frameworks for sequential decision-making. It is widely used to model a variety of sequential decision-making problems such as add placement, website optimization, mobile health, packet routing, clinical trials, revenue management, etc.
(
Bubeck 2012
, Chapter 1)
. The objective of this problem is to maximize the total reward collected by trying out different arms sequentially. In each trial, the bandit (or the agent) chooses an arm based on the information collected from past trials/experiences to receive a random reward from the chosen arm. While choosing an arm, the agent can either use the observed information to try the arm with the highest chances of generating maximum immediate reward (exploitation) or take a risk and try less explored arms to expect that it may produce a higher reward (exploration). MAB models this exploration/exploitation trade-off intrinsic to many sequential decision-making problem. Algorithms for solving the MAB problem are designed to address this exploration/exploitation dilemma.

Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)
(
Lai et al. 1985
;
Auer et al. 2002
)
and Thompson Sampling (TS) are two popular classes of algorithms to solve stochastic MAB problems. In this work, we study Thompson sampling (TS)
(
Thompson 1933
)
that uses a Bayesian heuristic to address the exploration/exploitation trade-off in MAB. In practice, TS is more straightforward to implement than UCB algorithms and empirically demonstrated to outperform the alternative UCB algorithms on many sequential decision-making problems
(
Chapelle and Li 2011
)
. In TS, we first posit a prior distribution on the parameters of the reward distribution for each arm and then update the prior belief by computing a posterior distribution using sequentially observed rewards from the previous trials. TS models the exploration/exploitation trade-off by capturing the uncertainty in mean rewards through the posterior distribution. More specifically, TS samples a reward distribution
for each arm
from the posterior distribution at each step and picks an arm with the best mean reward. In essence, TS randomly selects an arm according to the probability of it being optimal. Therefore, unlike UCB algorithms, TS computes a randomized arm selection policy. Intuitively, TS encourages exploration because there is always a positive posterior probability at each step of TS to sample a set of reward distributions for which a less explored arm would have the highest mean reward. However, this probability decreases as more rewards are generated from the optimal arm.

The theoretical performance of these algorithms is typically studied by computing an upper bound on the cumulative regret, where the regret is defined as the expected loss in reward when playing the arm selected by the algorithm instead of the best oracle arm. The regret bounds are further classified as instance-dependent and independent. A regret bound is called instance-dependent when it depends on the difference between the true mean rewards for each arm pair, and this difference is considered to be a constant. In contrast, the instance-independent bound has no such dependence; therefore, the true mean rewards for two arms can be arbitrarily close.
In this work, we will focus on computing both types of regret bounds for TS-type algorithms.

The germinal work by
Agarwal et al. 2017
derived both the instance-independent and dependent regret bounds for TS that closely match the respective lower bounds for such problems. However, their analysis exploited a specific analytic form of the sampling (posterior) distribution. In a similar vein,
Korda et al. 2013
derived instance-dependent bound for exponential family models with Jeffrey’s prior — that also have an analytic form of the posterior. Recall that the TS algorithm is more versatile and can be used with any prior and reward combinations (
Li and Chapelle 2012
;
Chapelle and Li 2011
;
Urteaga and Wiggins 2018
;
Hong et al. 2022
). Therefore,
Gopalan et al. 2013
analyzes TS with no closed-form posterior distribution but under the assumption that the prior and reward distributions have discrete support.
Mazumdar et al. 2020
further generalizes the analysis of TS and derives instance-dependent regret bound for log-concave and Lipschitz smooth prior and reward distributions. Their proof technique uses specific properties of log-concave and Lipschitz smooth density functions (e.g., see the proof of Lemma 15 in
Mazumdar et al. 2020
), which we find challenging to generalize to other classes of prior and reward distributions.

This work contributes to this growing body of work towards generalizing the analysis of the TS algorithm. Our analysis of TS identifies regularity conditions on the reward and prior distribution to derive both instance-independent and dependent regret bounds without assuming any analytical form of the posterior distribution. Our general condition on the reward distribution includes both sub-Gaussian and exponential family reward distributions. Moreover, our theory works for a general class of prior distributions under very mild assumptions, and in particular, one does not need to fix it to an exponential family or a log-concave distribution. Our condition on the prior distribution requires the prior measure of a decreasing Rényi-neighborhood of the true reward distribution to be bounded away from a term that decays exponentially at a certain rate. Such assumptions are common in the Bayesian posterior concentration literature (
Ghosal et al. 2000
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
) and are satisfied by a large class of distributions. The class of priors spanned by this condition is general enough to include Gaussian mixtures or other complex priors as considered in a few recent works (
Hong et al. 2022
;
Urteaga and Wiggins 2018
). Notably, our work also addresses an open problem of generalizing the regret analysis of TS for a broader class of priors, as noted by
(
Russo et al. 2017
, page 69, line 2)
.

Our general analysis leverages recent developments in the theory of posterior concentration
(
Ghosal et al. 2000
;
Kleijn and van der Vaart 2006
;
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2007
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
;
Yang et al. 2020
;
Zhang and Gao 2020
;
Alquier and Ridgway 2020
)
and their finite sample Bernstein von-Mises type analysis(
Spokoiny 2012
;
Panov and Spokoiny 2015
)
in the Bayesian statistics community. Nonetheless, our analysis also borrows essential ideas from the work on regret bounds for TS in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
, which introduced a novel and simplified way to quantify the sufficiency of the (expected) number of times the best arm is sampled.

We derive regret bounds for the TS algorithm with an
\alpha
-posterior (or fractional posterior) distribution
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
)
. We denote this algorithm as
\alpha
-TS. To compute an
\alpha
-posterior distribution, the likelihood in the Bayes formula is tempered with a factor
\alpha\in(0,1)
, unlike the standard posterior where
\alpha=1
. Nonetheless, we would also like to note that we use
\alpha
-posteriors as a convenient technical device
to obtain regret bounds under a minimal prior mass condition. Extension to the case of
\alpha=1
is possible along the lines of Section 3.2 of
Yang et al. 2020
with a few additional assumptions on identifiability of the model and the complexity of the parameter space.

Problem setup

We consider a stochastic MAB problem in which an agent is given a slot machine with
K
arms. At each time step
t\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, the agent pulls an arm
k\in[K]
and receives a random reward, where for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
[n]
denotes the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Let
X_{k,t}=\{x_{k,1},x_{k,2},\ldots,x_{k,n_{k}(t)}\}
denote the rewards observed from the arm
k
till time step
t
, where
n_{k}(t)
is the number of times arm
k
has been pulled till (and including) time step
t
. Let
X_{t}:=\{X_{1,t},X_{2,t},\ldots,X_{K,t}\}
. At each time step
t
, a stochastic MAB algorithm determines the arm
i_{t}\in[K]
using the observations
X_{t-1}
. We formally define
n_{k}(t):=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)
, where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is an indicator function. For each arm
k\in[K]
, we assume that
x_{k}
is a random variable defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{S},P_{\theta_{k}})
, where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K}\}\in\Theta^{K}
, where
\Theta
is an arbitrary set of parameters. We assume that given
i_{t}=k
(for any
k\in[K]
) the reward generated from the
k^{th}
arm is independent of any of the previous rewards. For any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, we denote
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\prod_{k=1}^{K}P_{\theta_{k}}
. We assume that there exists (but unknown) a true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}=\{\theta_{0,1},\ldots,\theta_{0,K}\}\in\Theta^{K}
. For any
k\in[K]
, we denote
\mu_{k}
and
\mu_{0,k}
as the mean of the reward distributions
P_{\theta_{k}}
and
P_{\theta_{0,k}}
respectively. We also assume that for each distribution
P_{\theta_{k}}
there exist a density function
p(\cdot|\theta_{k})
. For any
\alpha>0
, we denote the
\alpha-
Rényi divergence between a reward density
p(\cdot|\theta_{k})
and the true reward density
p(\cdot|\theta_{0,k})
for the
i^{th}
arm as

D_{\alpha}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k}):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\int p(x|\theta_{k})^{\alpha}p(x|\theta_{0,k})^{1-\alpha}dx.

The (pseudo-)regret after
T
time steps is defined as

\displaystyle\text{Regret}(T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mu_{0,i^{*}}-\mu_{0,i_{t}}\right),

(1)

where
i^{*}=\arg\max_{k\in[K]}\mu_{0,k}
.

Typically, the objective of any stochastic MAB algorithm is to minimize
\mathbb{E}[\text{Regret}(T)]
, where expectation is taken with respect to (w.r.t) the randomness in choosing
i_{t}
. Since,
i_{t}
is typically a mapping from the generated rewards (data) to an arm in
[K]
, this expectation is w.r.t the true reward generating distribution.
In next few lines, we describe the true reward generating process in the stochastic MAB setting.

Fix an algorithm that computes
i_{t}:X_{t-1}\to[K]
for any
t\geq 1
. Let us assume that we first collect reward from each arm and then start using an MAB algorithm.
So at time
t=1
, we have a set of
K
rewards (denoted as
X_{0}
) sampled independently from
P_{\theta_{0,k}}
for each
k\in[K]
.
Thereafter, the algorithm uses this information
X_{0}
to compute (or sample)
i_{1}\in[K]
and sample a reward from
P_{\theta_{0,i_{1}}}
and update
X_{1}
. Similarly, a sequence of rewards are generated from
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
using
X_{t}
at each time step
t+1
and then updating
X_{t+1}
. So, in essence, the true reward generation process depends on
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
and the algorithm. Following the standard in the bandit literature
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
, throughout, we say that
f(T)=\mathcal{O}(g(T))
or
g(T)=\Omega(f(T))
, if
\left|\frac{f(T)}{g(T)}\right|<\text{C}
, where
C
is some constant.
Note that the original definition of Bachmann-Landau
(
Bachmann 1923
;
Landau 1909
)
requires
\limsup_{T\to\infty}\left|\frac{f(T)}{g(T)}\right|<\text{C}
.

2.1
\alpha
-Thompson Sampling

Thompson sampling (TS) was proposed by
Thompson 1933
to solve a MAB problem, where exploration/exploitation dilemma is addressed using a Bayesian heuristic. It is also referred as
probability matching
or
posterior sampling
in some of the literature
(
Russo and Van Roy 2014
)
. Unlike standard TS algorithm, we use an
\alpha
-posterior instead of the standard posterior distribution (
\alpha=1
), and call the resulting algorithm as
\alpha
-TS.
The pseudo code of
\alpha
-TS is provided in Algorithm
1
. In
\alpha
-TS, we first assume a prior distribution
\pi_{k}
on
\theta_{k}
. For each
k\in[K]
,
t\geq 1
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we define the
\alpha
-posterior distribution as

\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha}(\theta_{k}|X_{t},\alpha-\text{TS})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}(\theta_{k})\prod_{s=1}^{t}\left[p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{i_{s}})p_{s}^{k}\right]^{\alpha\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)}}{\int\pi_{k}(\theta_{k})\prod_{s=1}^{t}\left[p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{i_{s}})p_{s}^{k}\right]^{\alpha\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)}d\theta_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}(\theta_{k})\prod_{s=1}^{t}\left[p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{i_{s}})\right]^{\alpha\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)}}{\int\pi_{k}(\theta_{k})\prod_{s=1}^{t}\left[p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{i_{s}})\right]^{\alpha\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)}d\theta_{k}},

where
p_{s}^{k}:=\pi_{\alpha}(\mu_{k}=\max_{j}\mu_{j}|X_{s-1},\alpha-\text{TS})
is the probability of choosing arm
k\in[K]
at time
s
using
X_{s-1}
.
In particular at each step
t\geq 1
, the
\alpha
-TS algorithm samples the optimal arm from the categorical distribution
\text{Cat}\left(\left\{p_{t}^{k}\right\}_{k\in[K]}\right)
.
Observe that the definition of the
\alpha
-posterior distribution is unaffected by the probability of choosing the best arm as
k
at each time step
t\geq 1
.

2.2
Related Works

The first theoretical result for MAB was derived in
Lai et al. 1985
, where the authors establish a generic (for any algorithm) asymptotic lower bound for one-parameter reward models. They also introduce the popular UCB methodology to achieve the derived asymptotic lower bound for Laplace and Gaussian (with known variance) reward models.
Burnetas and Katehakis 1996
generalizes the asymptotic lower bound to handle multi-parameter and nonparametric settings. Under mild regularity condition they show that

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{Regret}(T)]}{\log(T)}\geq\sum_{k:\mu^{0}_{k}<\mu_{0,i^{*}}}\frac{\Delta_{k}}{\inf_{\theta:\mu>\mu_{0,i^{*}}}\text{KL}(P_{\theta_{k}}^{0}\|P_{\theta})},

(4)

where
\text{KL}(p\|q):=\int p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}dx
is the Kullback-Liebler divergence between distributions
p
and
q
, and
\Delta_{k}=\mu_{0,i^{*}}-\mu_{0,k}
. There are a number of works both for UCB
(
Auer et al. 2002
;
Honda and Takemura 2010
)
and TS
(
Kaufmann et al. 2012
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2012
;
Honda and Takemura 2014
;
Korda et al. 2013
)

that establish asymptotically optimal regret bounds that are instance dependent in the sense of (
4
).

Moreover,
Li and Chapelle 2012
posed establishing problem-independent (that is independent of
\mu_{0,i^{*}}-\mu_{0,k}
) regret bound for TS that is close to non-asymptotic lower bound
\Omega(\sqrt{KT})
as an open problem. Subsequently,
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
, establish problem independent regret bounds of the TS algorithm for two popular MAB settings with Beta and Gaussian priors, which are near optimal. The result in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
assumes apriori that the sampling distribution at each stage of their TS algorithm is Gaussian (or Beta) with mean and variance updated in a non-Bayesian fashion. This enables them to claim their regret bound to hold for any reward distribution with bounded support and where they use the well-defined Gaussian structure in their proof. Nonetheless, many ideas developed in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
are general enough to include Bayesian posterior with Gaussian (or Bernoulli) reward. However, to the best of our knowledge, no result establishes frequentist regret bound for TS algorithm for general prior and reward distributions and without leveraging conjugacy or specific distributional assumption on the sampling distribution. The closest are the works by
Mazumdar et al. 2020
and
Gopalan et al. 2013
.

Mazumdar et al. 2020
provide two efficient Langevin MCMC algorithms for TS with provable and (optimal) instance-dependent regret bound under restrictive structural conditions on the prior and the reward models.
In
Gopalan et al. 2013
, the authors assume that the prior and reward distributions are discretely supported and compute an asymptotically optimal instance-dependent regret bound. In addition, frequentist regret bounds with general prior and reward distributions for various complex extensions of MAB such as MAB with finite but dependent arms, linear Bandit with infinite arms and linear reward, and Gaussian process bandit are still open. Another work by
Honda and Takemura 2014
establishes finite time regret bounds for TS with Gaussian rewards with unknown mean and variances and show that their bounds are not asymptotically optimal in the sense of (
4
) for some class of non-informative priors. Table
1
summarizes the existing non-asymptotic bounds on the frequentist regret of TS.
Interestingly, there is a recent work by
Jin et al. 2021
where they propose a minimax optimal version of Gaussian TS algorithm that clips the samples from the Gaussian posterior distribution, however, their approach require the knowledge of time horizon in-advance in addition to a closed form sampling distribution.
There is a parallel line of work that considers Bayesian regret for TS and compute non-asymptotic bounds
(
Russo and Van Roy 2014
)
. Our bounds are not comparable to this work as it is computed for a different definition of the regret.

Furthermore, there is a recent interest in studying limiting distributional properties of the random regret (and arm sampling distribution) instead of just computing a finite time bound on the expected regret (which summarizes all the information in the regret distribution to a single performance measure). In particular,
Fan and Glynn 2021
;
Wager and Xu 2021
show that
\text{Regret}(T,\text{TS})
converge weakly to a diffusion limit, which is characterized as the solution to a stochastic differential equation. The authors establish these results in a specific diffusion-asymptotic regime where
\Delta_{k}
depends on
T
and is of the order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}
. However, the results in
Wager and Xu 2021
also assume that the prior variance scales with the time horizon and asymptotically becomes non-informative, which is uncommon in TS literature where fixed priors are used.
Wager and Xu 2021
also note that the existing state-of-the-art instance-dependent regret bound derived in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
diverges in the diffusion-asymptotic regime. We also address this gap by deriving a new instance-dependent bound (see Theorem
4.1
), under our general setting.
Also,
Kalvit and Zeevi 2021
studies the behaviour of sampling distribution of the arms for the Beta-Bernoulli TS, when both the arms have reward distribution Bernoulli(
\theta
). They observe that the sampling distribution in this case either converges to the uniform distribution (
\text{Beta}(1,1)
) for
\theta=1
or a Dirac measure at
1/2
for
\theta=0
and also provide theoretical justification for this phenomenon. We believe that the theoretical development in this paper can be used to generalize the result in
Kalvit and Zeevi 2021
for TS to general prior and reward distribution.

3
Regret Bounds

In this section, we identify regularity conditions on the prior and reward distributions to compute a non-asymptotic bound on the
\alpha
-TS regret as defined in (
3
). We carefully adapt the first and second order finite sample analysis of
\alpha
-posterior to compute the required regret bounds.
To study first order properties of
\alpha
-posterior, we essentially use a variant of prior-mass condition (Assumption
3.4
) specified in
Zhang and Gao 2020
and derive an
\alpha
-posterior concentration bound in expectation instead of high-probability (as derived in
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
).
Then, we combine our
\alpha
-posterior concentration bounds to derive regret bounds for
\alpha
-TS, by generalizing the arguments in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
.

A critical step in the proof technique developed in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
, requires the posterior probability of over estimating the mean of the best arm (by sampling from the posterior distribution) to be bounded away from zero (in expectation). Establishing such lower bounds requires studying the second order properties of the
\alpha-
posterior. While it is convenient to establish such lower bounds when the posterior is Gaussian as assumed in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
or under some restrictive conditions as stated in
Mazumdar et al. 2020
, it is challenging to establish it for more general posteriors. In our setting with more general conditions on the prior and the likelihood, we appropriately adapt the finite sample Bernstein-von Mises results developed in
Spokoiny 2012
;
Panov and Spokoiny 2015
to compute a lower bound on such posterior probability.
We briefly discuss the relevant results from
Spokoiny 2012
;
Panov and Spokoiny 2015
in Section
3.1
and Lemma
3.1
.
We first discuss regularity conditions on the reward and prior distributions.

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume that there is a unique optimal arm
i^{*}\in[K]
.

The assumption above is standard in the TS literature. It implies that for each
k\in[K]\setminus\{i^{*}\}
, there exist
x_{k},y_{k}
such that
\mu_{0,k}<x_{k}<y_{k}<\mu_{0,i^{*}}
. To be precise, we define
y_{k}:=\mu_{0,i^{*}}-{\Delta_{k}/3}
and
x_{k}:=\mu_{0,k}+{\Delta_{k}/3}
, that we use later in our proofs. Moreover,
Agrawal and Goyal 2012
show that if there are more than one optimal arms then the expected regret will further decrease. Therefore, we can assume uniqueness of the best arm without loss of generality.

We next specify the assumptions on the reward distributions. In addition to the assumptions on the reward distributions listed in
3.1
, our theory holds for any reward distribution for which we can bound the squared difference in respective means (evaluated at two of its parameters) by the corresponding
\alpha
-Rényi divergence between them, that is for any
k\in[K]
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\alpha|\mu_{k}-\mu_{k}^{0}|^{2}\leq CD_{\alpha}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})
, for a constant
C>0
. Below we specify two families of distribution that satisfies this condition (the proofs are provided in the Appendix and may be of independent interest). The first one specifies the regularity conditions for a sub-Gaussian reward.

Assumption 3.2

(Sub-Gaussian)
.

For any arbitrary
\Theta
and for each arm
k
, we assume that
the reward distribution
P_{\theta_{k}}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma=1
, that is
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\theta_{k}}}[e^{sx_{k}}]\leq e^{s\mu_{k}^{t}+s^{2}/2}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Next, we specify the conditions for exponential family reward models.

Assumption 3.3

(1-d Exponential)
.

We assume for

that

i.

p(x|\theta_{k})=e^{x\theta_{k}-A(\theta)+C(x)}

\displaystyle\gamma A(x)+(1-\gamma)A(y)-A(\gamma x+(1-\gamma)y)\geq\frac{1}{2}\gamma(1-\gamma)m|x-y|^{2}.

3.1
Finite Sample Bernstein-von Mises

In this section, we specify the regularity conditions developed in
Spokoiny 2012
;
Panov and Spokoiny 2015
for finite sample Bernstein-von Mises (BvM) theorem to hold for parametric models. First, we specify some of the simplifying notations for this section. Let
X_{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
be an i.i.d. sample from a measure
P_{\theta_{0}}\equiv P_{0}
. We assume that the data generating distributional family
P_{\theta}
is spanned by
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Denote the log-likelihood of generating
X_{n}
as
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\log dP_{\theta}(X_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(X_{i},\theta)
, where
\ell(X_{i},\theta)=\log dP_{\theta}(X_{i})
.
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)
denotes gradient of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
evaluated at
\theta
and
\nabla^{2}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
stands for the Hessian of the expected log likelihood. Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}^{2}:=

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0}).

(5)

Note that
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{2}=nI(\theta_{0})\equiv I_{0}:=\mathcal{V}_{0}^{2}
, where
I(\theta_{0})
is the Fisher information of
P_{\theta}
at
\theta_{0}
.
Also denote the maximum likelihood estimator as
\hat{\theta}_{n}:=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
and
\theta_{0}=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]
. The stochastic part of the log-likelihood is denoted as
\zeta_{i}(\theta):=\ell(X_{i},\theta)-\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta)]
.

The regularity conditions required for the finite sample BvM to hold are split into local and global. The local conditions only describe the properties of the process
\ell(X_{1},\theta)

for
\theta\in\Theta(\mathtt{u}_{0})
with some fixed value
\mathtt{u}_{0}
, where

\displaystyle\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u}_{0}):=\big\{\theta\in\Theta\colon\|\mathcal{V}_{0}(\theta-\theta_{0})\|\leq\mathtt{u}_{0}\big\}.

(6)

The global conditions have to be fulfilled on the whole
\Theta
.

Assumption 3.5
.

We start with some exponential moments conditions.

{(e\!d_{0})}

There exists constants
\nu>0

and
\mathtt{g}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\mathtt{m}\frac{\langle\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0}),\gamma\rangle}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}\leq\frac{\nu^{2}\mathtt{m}^{2}}{2},~|\mathtt{m}|\leq\mathtt{g}.

(7)

{(e\!d_{1})}

There are constants
\omega>0
and
\mathtt{g}_{1}>0
such that for each
\mathtt{u}\leq\mathtt{u}_{0}
and
\mathtt{m}\leq\mathtt{g}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u})}\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma^{\top}[\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta)-\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})]}{\mathtt{u}\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}\leq\frac{\nu^{2}\mathtt{m}^{2}}{2},~|\mathtt{m}|\leq\mathtt{g}_{1}.

(8)

{(\ell_{0})}

There exists a constant
\delta^{*}
such that it holds for all
\mathtt{u}\leq\mathtt{u}_{0}

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\mathtt{u})}\bigg|\frac{2\text{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)}{(\theta-\theta_{0})^{\top}I(\theta_{0})(\theta-\theta_{0})}-1\bigg|\leq\delta^{*}\mathtt{u},

(9)

where
\text{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)
is the KL divergence between model
P_{\theta_{0}}
and
P_{\theta}
.

The following two are the required global conditions.

{(\ell{\mathtt{u}})}

For any
\mathtt{u}>0
there exists a value
\mathtt{b}(\mathtt{u})>0
,
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta:\|\mathcal{V}_{0}(\theta-\theta_{0})\|=u}\frac{\text{KL}(\theta,\theta_{0})}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}(\theta-\theta_{0})\|^{2}}\geq\mathtt{b}(\mathtt{u}).

(10)

{(e\mathtt{u})}

For each
\mathtt{u}\geq 0
, there exists
\mathtt{g}_{1}(\mathtt{u})>0
, such that for all
|\mathtt{m}|\leq\mathtt{g}_{1}(\mathtt{u})
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u})}\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\mathtt{m}\frac{\gamma^{\top}\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta)}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}\leq\frac{\nu^{2}\mathtt{m}^{2}}{2}.

(11)

The discussion regarding these assumptions can be found in Section 5.1 of
Spokoiny 2012
and partly in
Panov and Spokoiny 2015
. Recall from Assumption
3.4
that (effectively) no assumptions are imposed on the reward distribution in order to derive first-order concentration properties of the
\alpha
-posterior distribution.
However, when aiming to derive a more-refined second-order BvM type result, a more comprehensive set of local and global moment conditions is required, as specified in Assumption
3.5
. These conditions impose additional regularity requirements on the structural properties of the reward distributions. In Appendix

, we show that the exponential (Assumption

Spokoiny 2012
;

.

To extend the lower bound computed in

\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha}(\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u}_{0})|X_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\Theta}\exp\big\{{\alpha}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\theta_{0})\big\}\pi(\theta)\mathbb{I}\big\{\theta\in\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u}_{0})\big\}d\theta}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\big\{{\alpha}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\theta_{0})\big\}\big\}\pi(\theta)d\theta}.

Lai et al. 1985
;
Burnetas and Katehakis 1996
for general reward distributions. Nonetheless,
Agrawal and Goyal 2012
and
Auer et al. 2002
computes instance-dependent regret bounds that depend on
\Delta_{k}
instead of
\textsc{KL}(\theta_{i^{*}}^{0},\theta_{k})
for TS and UCB1 respectively.

4.1
Proof sketch

First, we present our main lemma, where we compute finite sample sub-exponential bound on the expected
\alpha-
posterior measure of a complement of a ball centered at the true parameter
\theta_{0,k}
, where the ball is defined using the difference in the observed and the true mean reward.

Lemma 4.1

(
\alpha
-posterior concentration)
.

Fix
k\in[K]
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C(\alpha)\nabla_{k}^{2}\geq\epsilon_{n_{k}(t)}^{2}
. Under
Assumption
3.2
for sub-Gaussian rewards or Assumption
3.3
for exponential family rewards and Assumption
3.4
, for
\epsilon^{2}=C(\alpha)\nabla_{k}^{2}
and all
n_{k}(t)\geq n_{k}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[E\left[\mathbb{I}\{|\mu_{k}^{t}-\mu_{0,k}|^{2}\geq\nabla_{k}^{2}\}|X_{t}\right]\big|n_{k}(t)\right]\leq 2^{-1}e^{-C(\alpha)n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}},

(16)

where
C(\alpha)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}(1-\alpha)\min\left(2\alpha,1-\alpha\right)/16

and
D=1
for sub-Gaussian rewards and
D=\frac{m}{C_{g}^{2}}
for exponential family rewards
.

The results in Lemma
4.1
differs from the result in
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
, Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.2)
due to
n_{k}(t)
, which is stochastic and also due to the bounds, which are on expectation with respect to the data generating distribution instead of high-probability bounds in
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
)
. The proof of Lemma
4.1
leverages the conditional independence of the observed reward from arm
k
, that is
X_{k,t}
given
n_{k}(t)
and the observation that the inner expectation in the RHS of (
16
) is bounded above by
E\left[\mathbb{I}\{D_{\alpha}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{\alpha}{2}\nabla_{k}^{2}\}|X_{t}\right]

due to the two technical lemmas stated in the Appendix. These lemmas bounds the difference in mean by the respective
\alpha
-Rényi divergence for sub-Gaussian and exponential family rewards
. We then define a set of rewards

A_{n_{k}(t)}:=\left\{X_{k,t}:\int_{\Theta}\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\theta_{k})e^{-\alpha\ell_{n_{k}(t)}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})}d\theta_{k}\leq 4^{-\alpha}e^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{(\alpha-\alpha^{2})}{8}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}}\right\},

where for any
B\subset\Theta
,
\tilde{\pi}_{k}(B)=\pi_{k}(B\cap\theta)/\pi_{k}(B\left(\theta_{0,k},\nabla_{k}\right))
and
\ell_{n_{k}(t)}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})=\log\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}(t)}p(x_{i,k}|\theta_{0,k})}{\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}(t)}p(x_{i,k}|\theta_{k})}
. We decompose the expected
\alpha
-posterior probability of
\{D_{\alpha}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{\alpha}{2}\nabla_{k}^{2}\}
by dividing it on
A_{n_{k}(t)}
and its complement and bound each term separately using Assumption
3.4
and the Markov inequality.

Now recall
\Delta_{k}=\mu_{0,i^{*}}-\mu_{0,k}
and note that the expected regret

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}E[\mu_{0,i^{*}}-\mu_{0,i_{t}}|X_{t-1}]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mu_{0,i^{*}}-\mu_{0,i_{t}})\right]=\sum_{k\neq i^{*}}\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}(T)]=\sum_{k\neq i^{*}}\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\right].

Essentially, in our proofs, we decompose the expectation above on the set
\{\mu_{k}^{t}\leq y_{k}\}
and its complement and bound them separately. Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}^{t}>y_{k}\}\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}^{t}\leq y_{k}\}\right].

(17)

In essence, the first and the second term accounts for over-estimation of the sub-optimal arms and under-estimation of the best arm respectively.
The bound on the first term in (
17
) is a direct consequence of Lemma
4.1
as it is easier to control the over-estimation of samples from the posterior distribution using its concentration properties. We present below the result that bounds the first term.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For any
k\neq i^{*}
, fix
x_{k}=\mu_{0,k}+\delta_{k}
, where
\delta_{k}=\Delta_{k}/3
. Assume that (B1) is satisfied for any
k\neq i^{*}
for
\epsilon^{2}=C(\alpha)\nabla_{k}^{2}
with
\nabla_{k}=\delta_{k}
. Then, under Assumption
3.1
, for
L_{k}(T)=\frac{\log(TM)}{C(\alpha)\delta_{k}^{2}}
, we have for each
k\neq i^{*}
and
t\in[T]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}^{t}>y_{k}\}\right]\leq L_{k}(T)+\left(2\right)^{-1}.

(18)

Observant readers would have noted that
L_{k}(T)
is the term that defines the appropriate bound on the expected regret. We anticipate to have a similar bound on the second term in (
17
). To analyse the second term in (
17
), we define
p_{k,t}:=\pi_{\alpha}(\mu_{i^{*}}^{t}\geq y_{k}|X_{t-1})
for any
k\neq i^{*}
and bound it using the following lemma. This result is similar to
(
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
, Lemma 2.8)
, which significantly simplifies the analysis of TS than the prior techniques.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
k\neq i^{*}
and
t\in[T]

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}^{t}\leq y_{k}\}|X_{t-1}\right]\leq\frac{1-p_{k,t}}{p_{k,t}}P(i_{t}=i^{*},\mu_{k}^{t}\leq y_{k}|X_{t-1}).

(19)

In particular, the result above bounds the posterior probability of playing the sub-optimal arm (when the best arm is under-estimated) by a linear function of the posterior probability of playing the best arm times the odds ratio of under-estimating the best arm to over-estimating it.
Thereafter, we use the above lemma to show that
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}^{t}\leq y_{k}\}\right]\leq\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right]
, where

\tau_{j}
is the time-step at which the best arm
i^{*}
is pulled for the
j^{th}
time.

To bound
\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right]
, for
j>L_{k}(T)
, observe that
\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}
is the mean of a geometric random variable denoting the number of consecutive independent trials until
\mu_{i^{*}}^{t}>y_{k}
given
j
observations from the best arm and then expressing this mean as an infinite sum of the tail probabilities of this geometric random variable. The upper bound for
j>L_{k}(T)
follows by using Lemma
4.1
in bounding the above described geometric tail probability and using the definition of
L_{k}(T)
. However, note that we also need to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right]
for
j\leq L_{k}(T)
. Since, the concentration results does not hold in this regime, we have to resort to some finite sample analysis of posterior distribution that can upper bound
\mathbb{E}\left[p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}^{-1}\right]
for all
j
. This is the most critical part of the TS analysis that evidently requires posterior anti-concentration bounds.

The work in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
assumes Gaussian posterior, that enables them to conveniently compute this bound and on the other hand
Mazumdar et al. 2020
assumes sufficient structural assumption on the prior and the likelihood model so that posterior can be lower bounded by a Gaussian distribution and thus enabling them too to compute the required upper bound. However, in general, it is hard to have this nice Gaussian structure. One way to generalise the analysis is to use finite sample Bernstein-von Mises theorem to compute a Gaussian approximation of the posterior with the aim to compute a finite sample lower bound on the probability of over-estimating the best arm. We leverage such result derived in
Spokoiny 2012
;
Panov and Spokoiny 2015
(and reproduced in Lemma
3.1
]) to compute the required bound.

In the next lemma, we bound the term
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right]
. The proof technique for the second result in the following lemma is adapted from
(
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
, Lemma 2.13)
and uses Lemma
4.1
. The first result requires developing new techniques using Lemma
3.1
and is a generalization to the similar results established in
(
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
, Lemma 2.13)
and
(
Mazumdar et al. 2020
, Lemma 15)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For any
k\neq i^{*}
,
y_{k}=\mu_{0,i^{*}}-\delta_{k}
and
\delta_{k}={\Delta_{k}/3}
. Assume that (B1) is satisfied for
k=i^{*}
with
\nabla_{i^{*}}=\delta_{k}
for all
k\neq i^{*}
and
\epsilon=C(\alpha)\nabla_{k}^{2}
with
\nabla_{k}=\delta_{k}
. Then, under Assumptions
3.1
,
3.5
,and
3.6
, for
L_{k}(T)=\frac{\log(MT)}{C(\alpha)\delta_{k}^{2}}
, defined for any
M\in(0,1]
, we have for each
k\neq i^{*}
and
\tau_{j}\leq T
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right]\leq\begin{cases}(r_{0}(C,\alpha,n_{0},\mathbb{C})+2)&\forall j\geq 1\\
\left(MT\right)^{-1}&\forall j>L_{k}(T).\end{cases}

(20)

Consequently, using the results above, we bound (
17
) to first derive the instance-dependent regret bound of Theorem
4.1
. In its derivation, we fix
M=C(\alpha)\delta_{k}^{2}
in Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.2
. We compute instance-independent bound from the instance-dependent regret bound of Theorem

by decomposing the expected regret on the set of arms that satisfies

and its complement. To derive our final

5
Concluding remarks and open problems

We believe that this work establishes a connection between the rich literature on posterior concentration in the Bayesian statistics
(
Ghosal et al. 2000
;
Kleijn and van der Vaart 2006
;
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2007
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
;
Zhang and Gao 2020
;
Spokoiny 2012
;
Panov and Spokoiny 2015
)

and the existing theory for TS to generalize the theoretical analysis of TS. The ideas developed in this work can be developed further by leveraging the generality of the existing posterior concentration results (and also of its variational approximations)
(
Ghosal et al. 2000
;
Zhang and Gao 2020
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
)
to analyze other versions of TS that are designed to solve more complex sequential decision-making problems efficiently
(
Russo et al. 2017
)
.
We also note that
\alpha
-posteriors are used here as a convenient technical device to obtain regret bounds under a minimal prior mass condition. However, with additional work, we expect the results to extend to
\alpha=1
. It requires the development of some additional regularity conditions on the prior and the reward distribution that is in line with the theory for standard posterior distribution in
Ghosal et al. 2000
. In particular, we need the existence of non-trivial testing/ entropy conditions [Theorem 2.1 and 7.1]
(
Ghosal et al. 2000
)
. This point has also been discussed in
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
arguing the simplified concentration analysis of
\alpha
-posterior compared to that of the standard posterior.

Furthermore, we conjecture that for any prior distribution, the problem-independent regret lower-bound for
\alpha
-TS (and TS) will also be of the order
\Omega(\sqrt{KT\log K})
as derived for the Gaussian priors in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
. The analysis would require establishing a lower bound for the posterior concentration, a complementary result to Lemma
4.1
, which is an interesting problem by itself.

We first prove two technical lemmas that establish the relation between the absolute difference in true and observed means and the
\alpha-
Rényi divergence for sub-Gaussian and Exponential family reward distributions. These results could be of independent interest too.

Lemma A.1
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For any two sub-Gaussian measures
\mu
and
\nu
with sub-Gaussian parameter
\sigma_{\mu}
and
\sigma_{\nu}
respectively, such that
\nu
is absolutely continuous wrt
\mu
, that is
\nu<<\mu
, the
\alpha-
Rényi divergence can be bounded below by the absolute difference between the respective means. In particular, for any random variable
X
having measure
\mu
and
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[X]|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{(\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\alpha+\sigma_{\nu}^{2}(1-\alpha))}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}D_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)}.

(21)

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, recall the definition of
\alpha-
Rényi divergence
D_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)=\log\int g^{\alpha}d\mu=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}{\log\int\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\right)^{\alpha}d\mu}
, where
g\equiv\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}
. Now observe that

\displaystyle(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[g^{\alpha-1}e^{-(\alpha-1)X}e^{(\alpha-1)X}]=\log\int(ge^{-X})^{\alpha-1}e^{(\alpha-1)X}d\nu

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\int e^{(\alpha-1)X}d\nu\right)^{\alpha}\left(\int(ge^{-X})^{-1}e^{(\alpha-1)X}d\nu\right)^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[e^{(\alpha-1)X}]^{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[g^{-1}e^{\alpha X}]^{1-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[e^{(\alpha-1)X}]^{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[e^{\alpha X}]^{1-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\log\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[e^{(\alpha-1)X}]+(1-\alpha)\log\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[e^{\alpha X}],

(22)

where the first inequality follows from the Hölder’s inequality wrt measure
e^{(\alpha-1)X}d\nu
.

Now fix
X=sX
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
(without loss of generality). Since,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it follows from the inequality above that

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)

\displaystyle\geq\left[\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[e^{(\alpha-1)sX}]-\log\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\int e^{\alpha sX}]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left[\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}[s(\alpha-1)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X]+\sigma_{\nu}^{2}s^{2}(\alpha-1)^{2}/2]-[s\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[X]+\sigma_{\mu}^{2}s^{2}\alpha^{2}/2]\right]

\displaystyle=\left[s\alpha[\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[X]]-\frac{s^{2}\alpha}{2}[\sigma_{\nu}^{2}(1-\alpha)+\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\alpha]\right],

(23)

where the second inequality uses the fact that
\mu
and
\nu
are sub-Gaussian measures.
Recall if
X\sim\mu
, is a sub-Gaussian random variable, then
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[e^{sX}]\leq e^{s\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[X]+\sigma_{\mu}^{2}s^{2}/2}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Now it follows from above that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[X]|

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{|s|}\frac{D_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)}{|s|\alpha}+\frac{|s|}{2}(\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\alpha+\sigma_{\nu}^{2}(1-\alpha))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\alpha+\sigma_{\nu}^{2}(1-\alpha))}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}D_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)}.

(24)

∎

\displaystyle=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{0,i_{s}})}{p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{i_{s}})}\right)^{-\alpha\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)}\big|n_{k}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}}\left[\left(\frac{p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{0,i_{s}})}{p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{i_{s}})}\right)^{-\alpha\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)}\big|i_{s}\right]\big|n_{k}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}}\left[\left(\frac{p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{0,i_{s}})}{p(x_{i_{s}}|\theta_{i_{s}})}\right)^{-\alpha}\big|i_{s}\right]^{\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)}\big|n_{k}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-(1-\alpha)\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)D_{\alpha}(\theta_{i_{s}},\theta_{0,i_{s}})}\big|n_{k}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-(1-\alpha)\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{I}(i_{s}=k)D_{\alpha}(\theta_{i_{s}},\theta_{0,i_{s}})}\big|n_{k}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-n_{k}(t)(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})}.

Hence, using Fubini’s theorem and the observation above, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{F_{k}}\pi_{k}(\theta_{k})e^{-\alpha\ell_{n_{k}(t)}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})}d\theta_{k}\Big|n_{k}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{(\alpha-\alpha^{2})}{2}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}},

(34)

where the last inequality is due to the definition of
F_{k}
.
Substituting (
34
) into (
33
) yields,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\int_{F_{k}}\pi_{k}(\theta_{k})e^{-\alpha\ell_{n_{k}(t)}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})}d\theta_{k}}{\int\pi_{k}(\theta_{k})e^{-\alpha\ell_{n_{k}(t)}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})}d\theta_{k}}\mathbb{I}(A_{n_{k}(t)}^{c})\Big|n_{k}(t)\right]\leq 4^{-1}e^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{(\alpha-\alpha^{2})}{4}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}}.

(35)

Next we analyse the first term in (

, it follows from the Markov inequality that

\displaystyle\left(\left[\int\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\theta_{k})e^{-\alpha\ell_{n_{k}(t)}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})}d\theta_{k}\right]^{-1/\alpha}\geq 4e^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{1-\alpha}{8}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}}\Big|n_{k}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4^{-1}{e^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{1-\alpha}{8}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}}}\left[\int\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\theta_{k})\mathbb{E}\left(e^{\ell_{n_{k}(t)}(\theta_{k},\theta_{0,k})}\Big|n_{k}(t)\right)d\theta_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=4^{-1}{e^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{1-\alpha}{8}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}}}\left[\int\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\theta_{k})e^{n_{k}(t)D_{2}\left({\theta_{0,k}}\|{\theta_{k}}\right)}d\theta_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4^{-1}{e^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{1-\alpha}{8}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}}e^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{(\alpha-\alpha^{2})}{8}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}}}\leq 4^{-1}e^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D}\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{8}n_{k}(t)\nabla_{k}^{2}},

where second inequality is due to Jensen’s and last inequality uses the definition of the set

Combining (

Proof of Lemma

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}(T)]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(r_{0}+1)\log(T/9C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}^{2})}{C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}}+r_{0}\Delta_{k}+\frac{27}{2C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}}\right).

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

First, using the assumption that
\Delta_{k}\leq 1
, observe that the instance dependent upper-bound in Theorem
4.2

\displaystyle\left(\frac{9(r_{0}+1)\log(\frac{T}{9}C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}^{2})}{C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}}+r_{0}\Delta_{k}+\frac{27}{2C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{9(r_{0}+1)\log(TC(\alpha)\Delta_{k}^{2})}{C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}}+r_{0}+\frac{27}{2C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}}\right).

(60)

The upper bound above decreases for
\Delta_{k}>\frac{e}{\sqrt{TC(\alpha)}}
, therefore, for
\Delta_{k}>\frac{e\sqrt{K\log(K)}}{\sqrt{TC(\alpha)}}
, the upper bound above is bounded by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{9(r_{0}+1)\log(TC(\alpha)\Delta_{k}^{2})}{C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}}+r_{0}+\frac{27}{2C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{9(r_{0}+1)\log(e^{2}K\log(K))}{C(\alpha)}\frac{\sqrt{TC(\alpha)}}{e\sqrt{K\log(K)}}+r_{0}+\frac{27}{2C(\alpha)}\frac{\sqrt{TC(\alpha)}}{e\sqrt{K\log(K)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{18(r_{0}+1)}{e}+\frac{18(r_{0}+1)+27/2}{e}\right)\frac{\sqrt{T\log(K)}}{\sqrt{C(\alpha)K}}+r_{0}

\displaystyle\leq 19(r_{0}+1)\frac{\sqrt{T\log(K)}}{\sqrt{C(\alpha)K}}+r_{0}.

(61)

Moreover, for
\Delta_{k}\leq\frac{e\sqrt{K\log(K)}}{\sqrt{TC(\alpha)}}

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}(T)]\leq\frac{e\sqrt{TK\log(K)}}{\sqrt{C(\alpha)}}.

(62)

Now observe that for
M(T)=\frac{e\sqrt{K\log(K)}}{\sqrt{TC(\alpha)}}
, it follows from (
61
), (
62
), and (
54
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mu^{0}_{i^{*}}-\mu^{0}_{i_{t}})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\forall k\neq i^{*}}\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}(T)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]\setminus\{i^{*}\}}\left[\mathbb{I}\left(\Delta_{k}\leq M(T)\right)\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}(T)]+\mathbb{I}\left(\Delta_{k}>M(T)\right)\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}(T)]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in[K]\setminus\{i^{*}\}}\left[\mathbb{I}\left(\Delta_{k}\leq M(T)\right)\frac{e\sqrt{TK\log(K)}}{\sqrt{C(\alpha)}}+19(r_{0}+1)\frac{\sqrt{T\log(K)}}{\sqrt{C(\alpha)K}}+r_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(K+\frac{\sqrt{KT\log(K)}}{\sqrt{C(\alpha)}}\right).

(63)

Also, for
\frac{K}{\log(K)}\leq\frac{T}{C(\alpha)}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mu^{0}_{i^{*}}-\mu^{0}_{i_{t}})\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{KT\log(K)}}{\sqrt{C(\alpha)}}\right)
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

For
\Delta_{k}=\mu^{0}_{i^{*}}-\mu^{0}_{k}
, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu^{0}_{i^{*}}-\mu^{0}_{i_{t}}\right]=\sum_{\forall k\neq i^{*}}\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}(T)]=\sum_{\forall k\neq i^{*}}\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\right]=\sum_{\forall k\neq i^{*}}\Delta_{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[i_{t}=k\right]

(64)

In what follows, we analyze
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[i_{t}=k\right]
. Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}\leq y_{k}\}\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}>y_{k}\}\right],

(65)

where for any
k\neq i^{*}
, we assume that there exists
\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
such that
\mu^{0}_{k}<x_{k}<y_{k}<\mu^{0}_{i^{*}}
. Now for any
k\neq i^{*}
define
p_{k,t}:=\pi_{\alpha}(\mu_{i^{*}}\geq y_{k}|X_{t-1})
.

Therefore, using Lemma
4.3
, the first term in (
65
) can be bounded as

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[E\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}\leq y_{k}\}|X_{t-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[E\left[\frac{1-p_{k,t}}{p_{k,t}}\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=i^{*})\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}\leq y_{k}\}|X_{t-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,t}}{p_{k,t}}\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=i^{*})\right].

(66)

Recall
\tau_{j}
is the time-step at which the best arm
i^{*}
is pulled for the
j^{th}
time. Note that
\tau_{T}\geq T
. Therefore, it follows from the inequality above that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[E\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}\leq y_{k}\}|X_{t-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{T}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,t}}{p_{k,t}}\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=i^{*})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\sum_{t=\tau_{j}+1}^{\tau_{j+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,t}}{p_{k,t}}\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=i^{*})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\sum_{t=\tau_{j}+1}^{\tau_{j+1}}\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=i^{*})\right]=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right],

(67)

where the second equality follows since
p_{k,t}
does not change (and is equals to
p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}
) in the time interval
[\tau_{j}+1,\tau_{j+1}]
.

Consequently, it follows from (
67
) and Lemma
4.4
that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[E\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}\leq y_{k}\}|X_{t-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor L_{k}(T)\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right]+\sum_{j=\lfloor L_{k}(T)\rfloor+1}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1-p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}{p_{k,\tau_{j}+1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}(L_{k}(T)+1)+1.

(68)

Now consider the second term in (
55
). It follows from Lemma
4.2
(for
M=1
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}\geq y_{k}\}\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(i_{t}=k)\mathbb{I}\{\mu_{k}>x_{k}\}\right]\leq\frac{\log(T)}{C(\alpha)\delta_{k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}.

(69)

Consequently, the result follows from substituting (
68
) and (
69
) into (

) and then into (

) and noting that

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}(T)]

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{k}\left(9(r_{0}+1)\frac{\log(T)}{C(\alpha)\Delta_{k}^{2}}+\frac{2r_{0}+3}{2}\right).

(70)

∎

Proposition A.1
.

The exponential family of reward distributions satisfying Assumption
3.3
with priors having strictly positive and continuous density on
\Theta
satisfy Assumption
3.4
.

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

First observe that

\displaystyle D_{2}(\theta_{0},\theta)

\displaystyle=\log\int p_{\theta}(r)^{2}p_{0}(r)^{-1}d\mu

\displaystyle=\left[A(2\theta-\theta_{0})-2A(\theta)+A(\theta_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{g}\left|\theta-\theta_{0}\right|^{2}

(71)

where the third equality uses the definition of
\alpha
-Rényi divergence for exponential family of distributions and the first inequality follows due to Assumption
3.3
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\Pi\left(D_{2}(\theta_{0},\theta)\leq\epsilon_{n}^{2}\right)\geq\Pi\left(|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{n}^{2}}{C_{g}}\right).

(72)

Now observe that for any prior
\Pi(\cdot)
with density
\phi(\cdot)
and for
C=\sqrt{\frac{1}{C_{g}}}
that

\displaystyle\Pi\left(|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{n}^{2}}{C_{g}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pi\left(\left\{\theta_{0}-C\epsilon_{n}\leq\theta\leq\theta_{0}+C\epsilon_{n}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{x\in B(\theta_{0},C\epsilon_{n})}\phi(x){2C\epsilon_{n}}.

(73)

For any
M\leq 1
, fix
\epsilon_{n}^{2}=\frac{\log(nM)}{C^{2}nC_{\phi}^{2}}
and observe that there exist a
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
C\epsilon_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{\log(Mn)}{nC_{\phi}^{2}}}\leq 1
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Now it follows that
\min_{x\in B(\theta_{0},C\epsilon_{n})}\phi(x)\geq\min_{x\in B(\theta_{0},1)}\phi(x)\geq C_{\phi}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Consequently, for all
n\geq n_{0}
, it follows from our choice of
\epsilon_{n}
that

\displaystyle\Pi\left(|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{2}\leq\frac{D\alpha}{4}\epsilon_{n}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq C_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{4\log(Mn)}{nC_{\phi}^{2}}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\log(Mn)}{n}}.

(74)

Now the assertion of the lemma follows using the fact that
\sqrt{\frac{\log Mn}{n}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\log M^{2}n}{n}}\geq 4^{1+\alpha}\frac{1}{Mn}=4^{1+\alpha}e^{-\frac{C^{2}C_{\phi}^{2}}{1}n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}=4^{1+\alpha}e^{-\frac{C^{2}_{\phi}}{C_{g}}n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
for any
n\geq n_{0}(\alpha)
. In particular, we have from (
72
), (
73
), (
74
) and the arguments above that

\displaystyle\Pi\left(D_{2}(\theta_{0},\theta)\leq\epsilon_{n}^{2}\right)\geq 4^{1+\alpha}e^{-\frac{C_{\phi}^{2}}{C_{g}}n\epsilon_{n}^{2}},

(75)

and the result follows for any prior for which
C_{\phi}^{2}\leq C_{g}
.
∎

A.2
Exponential and sub-Gaussian families satisfy Assumption
3.5

Recall from the definition of the exponential family of distribution that:
p(x_{i}|\theta)=e^{x_{i}\theta-A(\theta)+C(x_{i})}
. Therefore,
\ell(x_{i},\theta)=x_{i}\theta-A(\theta)+C(x_{i})
. Now note that
\nabla\zeta_{i}(\theta)=\nabla\ell(x_{i},\theta)-\mathbb{E}[\nabla\ell(x_{i},\theta)]=x_{i}-A^{\prime}(\theta)-\mathbb{E}[x_{i}-A^{\prime}(\theta)]=x_{i}-E[x_{i}]
. Thus
\nabla\zeta_{i}(\theta)
does not depend on
\theta
. Consequently,
(ed_{0})
,
(ed_{1})
, and
(eu)
are satisfied for exponential family models with finite variance using
[
Spokoiny 2012
, Lemma 2.14]
. The assumptions
(\ell\mathtt{u})
and
(\ell_{0})
are satisfied by the observation that for 1-d exponential family models,
-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\theta,\theta_{0})]=\textsc{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)=A(\theta)-A(\theta_{0})-A^{\prime}(\theta_{0})(\theta-\theta_{0})=(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}I(\theta^{*})/2
, where last inequality uses the second order Taylor expansion of
-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\theta,\theta_{0})]
and the fact that
\theta_{0}
is the extreme point of
-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0},\theta)]
, and
\theta^{*}
is a point between
\theta
and
\theta_{0}
. To observe
(\ell_{0})
, note that
\left|\frac{\textsc{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)}{(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}I(\theta_{0})}-1\right|=\left|\frac{I(\theta^{*})}{I(\theta_{0})}-1\right|
and
I(\cdot)
is continuous, therefore there must exists a
\delta^{*}
such that
\left|\frac{I(\theta^{*})}{I(\theta_{0})}-1\right|<\delta^{*}\mathtt{u}
.

For sub-Gaussian models, note that

\ell(x_{i},\theta)=\log dP_{\theta}(x_{i})=\log p(x_{i}-\theta)
, where
p(\cdot)
is the density of the sub-Gaussian error with mean zero and variance parameter
1
and denote
h(\cdot)=\log p(\cdot)
. Also, recall

\zeta_{1}(\theta)=\ell(x_{i},\theta)-\mathbb{E}[\ell(x_{i},\theta)]=\log p(x_{i}-\theta)-\mathbb{E}[\log p(x_{i}-\theta)].

Note that,
\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})=\nabla\ell(x_{i},\theta_{0})=-h^{\prime}(x-\theta_{0})
, because
\nabla\mathbb{E}[\ell(x_{i},\theta_{0})]=0
. Now observe that

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\mathtt{m}\frac{-h^{\prime}(\epsilon)\cdot\gamma}{|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma|}\right\}\leq\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\mathtt{m}\frac{|h^{\prime}(\epsilon)|}{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\right\},

(76)

where last inequality follows because
\mathcal{V}_{0}=I_{0}^{1/2}
is positive.
One can observe that the assumption
{(ed_{0})}
is satisfied of all sub-exponential family models (which includes sub-Gaussians).

{(ed_{1})}
can be satisfied if we assume the gradient of log error density is Lipschitz, that is

\displaystyle|\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta)-\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})|

\displaystyle=|\nabla\log p(x_{i}-\theta)-\nabla\log p(x_{i}-\theta_{0})|+\mathbb{E}[|\nabla\log p(x_{i}-\theta)-\nabla\log p(x_{i}-\theta_{0})|]

\displaystyle\leq 2L|\theta-\theta_{0}|.

In particular, note that

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u})}\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma^{\top}[\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta)-\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})]}{\mathtt{u}\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}\leq\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u})}\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma 2L|\theta-\theta_{0}|}{\mathtt{u}|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma|}\right\}=\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{2L}{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\right\}.

(77)

\displaystyle\log

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma^{\top}[\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta)]}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}=\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma^{\top}[\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta)-\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})+\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})]}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma^{\top}[|\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta)-\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})|+\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})]}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma^{\top}[2L|\theta-\theta_{0}|+\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})]}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma^{\top}[2L|\theta-\theta_{0}|]}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}+\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\mathtt{m}}{\omega}\frac{\gamma^{\top}[\nabla\zeta_{1}(\theta_{0})]}{\|\mathcal{V}_{0}\gamma\|}\right\}

To satisfy
\ell\mathtt{u}
, observe that for any model if
\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta)]
is locally strongly concave, that is there exists
m(\mathtt{u})>0
for all
\theta\in\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u})
and
\mathtt{u}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta)]\leq\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta_{0})]+\nabla\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta_{0})](\theta-\theta_{0})-m(\mathtt{u})/2\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|^{2}.

(78)

Since
\nabla\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta_{0})]=0
, under the assumption above note that

\displaystyle\textsc{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta_{0})]-\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta)]\geq\frac{m(\mathtt{u})}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|^{2}.

(79)

Now it is straight forward to observe that for 1-d parametric models

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)}{I_{0}(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}}\geq\frac{m(\mathtt{u})}{I_{0}}>0.

(80)

Now we specify condition on the error model to satisfy
\ell_{0}
. In addition local strong convexity, we need
\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta)]
to be locally smooth too, that is there exists
L(\mathtt{u})>0
for all
\theta\in\Theta_{0}(\mathtt{u})
and
\mathtt{u}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta)]\geq\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta_{0})]+\nabla\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta_{0})](\theta-\theta_{0})-L(\mathtt{u})/2\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|^{2}.

(81)

Now combining locally smooth an local strong convexity conditions with the fact that
\nabla\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta_{0})]=0
, observe,

\displaystyle\frac{m(\mathtt{u})}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|^{2}\leq\textsc{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta_{0})]-\mathbb{E}[\ell(X_{i},\theta)]\leq\frac{L(\mathtt{u})}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|^{2}.

(82)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{m(\mathtt{u})}{I_{0}}\leq\frac{2\textsc{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)}{I_{0}(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}}\leq\frac{L(\mathtt{u})}{I_{0}}\implies\left|\frac{2\textsc{KL}(\theta_{0},\theta)}{I_{0}(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}}-1\right|\leq\max\left(\left|\frac{m(\mathtt{u})}{I_{0}}-1\right|,\left|\frac{L(\mathtt{u})}{I_{0}}-1\right|\right).

(83)

Note that the RHS above goes to
0
as
u\to 0
, since
m(\mathtt{u})
and
L(\mathtt{u})
will both converge to
I_{0}
.
