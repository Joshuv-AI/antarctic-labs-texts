LLMs as for Doctors: Leveraging Medical LLMs to Assist Doctors , Not Replace Them Thanks: Feng Jiang and Benyou Wang are the corresponding authors.
Wenya Xie, Qingying Xiao, Yu Zheng, Xidong Wang, Junying Chen, Ke Ji

Abstract
The recent success of Large Language Models (LLMs) has had a significant impact on the healthcare field, providing patients with medical advice, diagnostic information, and more. However, due to a lack of professional medical knowledge, patients are easily misled by generated erroneous information from LLMs, which may result in serious medical problems. To address this issue, we focus on tuning the LLMs to be medical assistants who collaborate with more experienced doctors. We first conduct a two-stage survey by inspiration-feedback to gain a broad understanding of the real needs of doctors for medical assistants. Based on this, we construct a Chinese medical dataset called DoctorFLAN to support the entire workflow of doctors, which includes 92K Q&A samples from 22 tasks and 27 specialists. Moreover, we evaluate LLMs in doctor-oriented scenarios by constructing the DoctorFLAN- test containing 550 single-turn Q&A and DotaBench containing 74 multi-turn conversations. The evaluation results indicate that being a medical assistant still poses challenges for existing open-source models, but DoctorFLAN can help them significantly. It demonstrates that the doctor-oriented dataset and benchmarks we construct can complement existing patient-oriented work and better promote medical LLMs research.

Introduction

Current large language models for healthcare applications

;
Singhal et al. (2023b)
;

;

;
Wang et al. (2023b)
;

However, several challenges hinder their deployment in real-world scenarios
Umapathi et al. (2023)
;
Wang et al. (2023c)
. Firstly, patients often lack the professional knowledge necessary to verify the accuracy of the model’s outputs. This can pose significant risks when these models provide incorrect or misleading advice.
Furthermore, issues such as model hallucination currently impede their practical application
Fan et al. (2023)

Recognizing these challenges, we advocate for repositioning LLMs in healthcare as supportive collaborators alongside experienced doctors who can identify and correct inaccuracies in the model’s outputs, ensuring safer implementation.
However, effectively integrating LLMs into medical practice necessitates the development of new tasks that go beyond the online consultation LLMs for patients.
For example, doctors may need assistance with differential diagnosis, which is not required for patients in online consultation scenarios.

To achieve this goal, we conduct a two-stage survey using an inspiration-feedback method to gain a comprehensive understanding of the real needs of doctors for medical assistants.
From the survey, we identify four tasks that doctors consider most effective under LLM’s assistance, positioning them as ideal starting points in practice.

Building upon these insights, we develop DoctorFLAN, a comprehensive Chinese medical dataset that captures the full spectrum of the diagnostic process.
Additionally, to assess the real-world clinical capabilities of these assistant LLM models, we designate DoctorFLAN-
test
for single-turn evaluations and introduce DotaBench, a three-turn evaluation that can mimic actual medical interactions.

After that, we benchmark the existing medical LLMs and our model DotaGPT trained on DoctorFLAN automatically and manually. Our evaluation results indicate that existing online consultations medical models, struggle with the diverse and complex tasks of real-world assistant roles.
Conversely, models trained on DoctorFLAN exhibit robust performance across tasks in DoctorFLAN-
test
and DotaBench.
Moreover, based on our findings, some of the tasks prioritized by doctors (e.g.,
Preoperative Education
) in the previous survey are promising candidates for initial deployment due to the models’ impressive performance in these areas.

The contributions of this work are manifold. 1) We first propose
LLMs for Doctors
, a paradigm shift in the application of LLMs in healthcare, and conduct a two-stage survey to identify the real needs of clinical doctors in their daily practice.
2) We build the DoctorFLAN, containing about 92K samples covering 22 tasks in the entire workflow of doctors, to fill the gap for training LLMs’ assistance to doctors with professional responses.
3) We develop the first expert-involved benchmark to evaluate LLMs in doctor-oriented scenarios with the single-turn DoctorFLAN-test and the multi-turn DotaBench, complementing the existing evaluation designed for patient-oriented scenarios.

LLMs as Doctors or for Doctors?

LLMs in healthcare applications fulfill two crucial roles:
LLMs as Doctors
that autonomously provide medical consultants for patients and
LLMs for Doctors
, which assist doctors in their daily work
(
Knoll et al., 2022
;
Cai et al., 2022
;
Krishna et al., 2020
)
.
In this section, we first discuss the challenges with
LLMs as Doctors
and advocate for a strategic shift toward safer and more effective applications.

2.1
Challenges with LLMs as Doctors

Popular Medical LLMs such as ChatDoctor
Yunxiang et al. (2023)
and HuatuoGPT
Zhang et al. (2023)
are designed to autonomously provide medical consultations. These models are trained on extensive patient-doctor dialogues to simulate medical advice.
However, current implementations of such medical models exhibit relatively low accuracy rates
(
Fan et al., 2023
)
.
Exposing them to patients who don’t have professional medical knowledge directly may pose significant risks as they could receive misleading responses.
For example, a patient consulting an LLM about severe stomachache and fever might receive incorrect advice to simply rest, potentially overlooking critical conditions like appendicitis that require immediate medical intervention.

2.2

LLMs for Doctors

Doctors can quickly identify and correct errors in diagnosis made by
LLMs for Doctors
, thereby safeguarding patient health through critical intervention.

2.3
Identifying Doctors’ Needs for LLMs

To accurately capture the medical scenarios where LLMs can be most beneficial serving as
LLMs for Doctors
, we design a two-stage heuristic-feedback task collection method to ensure the tasks involved in the medical workflow are professional and practical. Additionally, we conduct further analysis to determine which tasks are considered most effective for doctors when assisted by LLMs, aiming to prioritize these tasks for early model deployment.

2.3.1
Task Collection

Our initial step is to collaborate with experienced doctors to identify essential tasks in their daily workflows using a heuristic top-down method. Subsequently, we validate the universality of these tasks through an extensive user survey across multiple medical institutions with feedback.

Heuristic Task Definition.

We organize a symposium with 16 healthcare professionals
3
3

3

Details of the healthcare professionals are provided in Appendix
C
.
from four distinct specialties to establish task definitions based on heuristics, ensuring the tasks we identify are closely aligned with the practical needs of doctors. During the discussion, experts systematically categorize the medical workflow into 4 stages (pre-diagnosis, diagnosis, treatment, and post-treatment) with 22 tasks. It comprehensively covers all scenarios for the doctor’s workflow, from the triage to the creation of follow-up plans, as shown in Figure
1
. Unlike typical online consultations, which primarily address pre-treatment issues, such as initial diagnoses, and disease prevention, our approach extensively covers a wide range of tasks that doctors might encounter in a hospital setting, encompassing both inpatient and outpatient tasks.

Task Confirmation with Feedback.

Then, we survey doctors from over a dozen top-tier hospitals with their feedback to validate the universality of these tasks. In the survey

4

D
.
, doctors are asked to rate the helpfulness of each task with LLMs’ assistance, where a rating scale from 1 to 5 is given, with scores of 4 and 5 indicating high and critical applicability, respectively. In over 86% (71/82) of the valid questionnaires collected, the higher scores achieved in all tasks demonstrate their effectiveness and importance as recognized by the surveyed doctors, as shown in Figure
2
.

2.3.2
Analysis of Tasks Prioritized for Doctors

To identify which tasks should be prioritized for initial deployment of
LLMs for Doctors
, we analyze the efficiency scores that doctors assign to each task in the survey.
It is noticed that tasks such as
Triage
,

,
Medication Inquiry

3
Dataset Construction

To address these needs, we develop the DoctorFLAN and DotaBench for single-turn Q&A and multi-turn conversation scenarios separately.

3.1
DoctorFLAN

To develop a dataset that comprehensively covers the entire medical workflow, we construct DoctorFLAN based on the tasks previously defined.
We collect source data from a wide range of medical data and complete the construction of the dataset through two steps: instruction normalization and response polishing. Additionally, we also ensure the high quality of the data by manually verifying some of the sampled data.

Data Collection.

Our dataset primarily comprises multiple-choice questions sourced from Medtiku
5
5

5

https://www.medtiku.com/
, a repository of expert-level medical examination questions, and various online resources. These questions are selected due to their extensive coverage of professional medical knowledge and their relevance to real-world clinical scenarios. In addition, we supplement our dataset with encyclopedia entries
6
6

6

Hosted by 120 Ask, https://m.120ask.com/
for constructing inquiry tasks such as
Medicine Inquiry
. We also incorporat a subset of the PromptCBLUE dataset
(
Zhu et al., 2023
)
, selected for its diverse tasks and high quality.
After collecting all the original data, we perform deduplication and categorization using crafted regular expressions as part of the preprocessing stage.

Refinement of Original Data.

Given the lack of practicality of the original data, we adopt a refinement process consisting of two key components: refinement of instructions and enhancement of responses.
First, We ask doctors to manually draft the instructions for the 22 task categories to ensure that these instructions can reflect actual use scenarios.
Second, in response to the newly refined instructions, we employ GPT-4
7
7

7

GPT-4 is chosen for its superior performance among various models we tested.
to generate updated answers.
Specifically, we provide GPT-4 with reference materials, such as the original multiple-choice question answers, to serve as a basis for generating responses. It enables GPT-4 to produce responses that are not only accurate but also tailored to the required context and depth, aligning with the practical demands and professional standards expected in the medical field.

Data Verification.

To ensure that the responses generated by the GPT-4 model are factually accurate and realistic, we use a structured review process in which a sample of 1050 responses (50 data points per item across 22 items) are reviewed by three medical professionals, each reviewing 350 items.

The verification criteria include
Correctness
, where a response is considered correct if it contains no factual errors, and
Practicality
, where a response is deemed practical if it is more effective than the original answer. Our results demonstrate a high level of correctness (100%) and practicality (99.9%), underscoring the robustness of the DoctorFLAN. Further details (e.g., the graphical user interface used for verification and the detailed analysis of failure cases) are available in Appendix
F
.

3.2
DotaBench

Extending the single-turn dataset DoctorFLAN, we introduce DotaBench to evaluate multi-turn dialogues among medical assistants. This enhancement reflects real-world needs where medical assistants must handle multiple rounds of Q&A to thoroughly assess a condition, requiring continuous and consistent responses across interactions.

Data Collection.
To ensure its high professionalism, we select CMB-Clin
(
Wang et al., 2023c
)
, a multi-round question-answering dataset derived from real, complex medical records, as the data source for DotaBench.

Refinement of Original Data.

However, the samples from CMB-Clin, sourced from medical exams, exhibit a formal written style and often lack continuity between rounds of questioning.

To better simulate the multi-turn interactions typical between medical assistants and doctors, we engage medical professionals to rephrase these questions in a manner consistent with their routine inquiry processes. In handling multiple rounds of questioning, they are instructed to maintain logical sequence and coherence across three question items, thereby creating a more realistic simulation of conversational turns.

We employ the original Q&A sets as a reference for evaluation. Unlike DoctorFLAN, which directly involves LLMs in data generation, DotaBench is crafted without LLM intervention, thereby eliminating the need for subsequent data verification and ensuring more controlled evaluation conditions.

Evaluation Method

Considering both accuracy, reliability, and cost, our evaluation methodology incorporates both automatic and human evaluations.

For model evaluation, the decoding hyperparameters used across all models are max_new_tokens = 1024, top_p = 0.7, temperature = 0.5, and repetition_penalty = 1.1.

Automatic Evaluation.

Conclusion

In this paper, we emphasize a paradigm shift from developing
LLMs as Doctors
to
LLMs for Doctors
. We conduct a two-stage survey to accurately determine the real-world needs of doctors and create the DoctorFLAN dataset to address the training deficiencies of previous models. Additionally, we introduce DotaBench as a complementary evaluation framework to evaluate the ability of popular medical LLM as the doctor assistant. The benchmarking results show that most existing LLM models still face difficulties in being competent as medical assistants, but the superiority of DotaGPT demonstrates that the dataset we construct can help improve this ability. It proves that the dataset and benchmark we construct are effective and a beneficial supplement to current medical LLM research.

Limitations

The DoctorFLAN is currently only available in Chinese and may require supplementation in other languages. Consequently, it cannot be guaranteed that DotaGPT trained on DoctorFLAN will perform well in languages other than the one it has been tested. However, the methodology employed to create DoctorFLAN can be applied universally across different languages. Additionally, although DotaGPT has demonstrated impressive performance on the benchmarks, it is important to exercise caution when using its outputs, particularly in real-world doctor-assistant interactions.

Ethics Statement

Ensuring the privacy and security of data is paramount in the development of medical applications. The datasets used in this study, primarily derived from Medtiku, an open-source repository of medical examination questions, and PromptCBLUE
Zhu et al. (2023)

Given the potential issues with the credibility of content generated by DotaGPT, we are committed to strictly regulating the model’s use to prevent misuse. Our datasets, DoctorFLAN and DotaBench, will be released under terms that uphold the highest ethical standards. This commitment ensures that while advancing the capabilities of large language models in healthcare, we also safeguard sensitive medical data.

Appendix D
Details of the Survey Process

In this section, we present the distribution process of the questionnaire, our method for selecting valid responses, and the full text of the questionnaire itself.

D.1
Design of the Survey

The questionnaire is divided into two main sections. The first section aims to gauge doctors’ evaluations of the usefulness of 22 specific tasks to their work efficiency, rated on a scale of 1 to 5. A score of 4 signifies that the LLM assistance is helpful but not essential, while a score of 5 indicates its crucial role in significantly enhancing efficiency. The second section seeks to understand doctors’ preferences regarding the LLMs.

D.2
Survey Target Respondents

Doctors are sampled from over 12 Grade A tertiary care hospitals to ensure the universality of the tasks we developed. Feedback and suggestions are gathered from these doctors through the questionnaires.

D.3
Survey Collection

We collect a total of 82 responses, which are then refined to 71 valid responses through a detailed review. The validity of the responses is determined based on two stringent criteria: 1) the completion time is less than one-third of the average duration observed across all submissions, indicating a potential lack of due consideration; and 2) a marked uniformity in responses (e.g., repetitive selection of the same response option), suggesting insufficient engagement with the content of the questionnaire. This dual-condition criterion ensures that respondents have engaged with the material thoughtfully, recognizing and reflecting on the varied tasks the survey examined. On average, respondents spend 191.82 seconds on the questionnaires deemed valid. Importantly, the survey is designed to protect participant anonymity by not collecting any personal information.

F.1
Data Verification Setting

Due to the extensive size of the dataset and budget constraints, full verification is not feasible; instead, we sample 50 data points per task, resulting in a total of 1050 samples for doctors to review. A senior professional with a high-level title conducted the verification, dedicating 10 hours to the task. This approach balances thoroughness with practical limitations, ensuring credible verification within the available resources.

To ensure the reliability of our dataset, we establish specific criteria for data verification focusing on the following aspects:

Correctness:
This criterion evaluates the accuracy of the "GPT-4 Answer". An answer is deemed correct if it is factually accurate within the medical context. If the "GPT-4 Answer" is entirely correct and free of medical factual errors, please select "Yes"; otherwise, select "No".

Practicality:
This criterion assesses the usefulness of the "GPT-4 Answer" compared to the "Original Answer". If the "GPT-4 Answer" is deemed more practical, select "Yes"; if not, select "No".
