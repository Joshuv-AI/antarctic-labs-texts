An Infinite Dimensional Virtual Cohomology Group of SL 3 ⁡ ( ℤ ⁡ [ t ] ) \SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
Matthew Goroff

Abstract
. We prove that SL 3 ⁡ ( ℤ ⁡ [ t ] ) \SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t]) has a finite index subgroup Γ \Gamma such that H 2 ​ ( Γ , ℚ ) H^{2}(\Gamma;{\mathbb{Q}}) is infinite dimensional. The proof uses the geometry of the Euclidean building for SL 3 ⁡ ( ℚ ⁡ ( ( t − 1 ) ) ) \SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1}))) .



1.
Introduction

In
[
10
]
, Suslin proves that
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
is finitely generated by elementary matrices. In
[
7
]
, Kristic and McCool prove that
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
is not finitely presented. In
[
5
]
, Bux, Mohammadi, and Wortman prove that
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
is not
FP_{2}
.

In this paper, we use and add to these results by showing that
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
has a finite index subgroup with an infinite dimensional second cohomology group with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Specifically, consider the homomorphism
\iota\colon{\mathbb{Z}}[t]\to{\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}}
where
t\mapsto 0
and integers are reduced mod 3. This induces a homomorphism
\hat{\iota}\colon\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\to\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}})
. Let
\Gamma=\ker(\hat{\iota})
. We will prove the following:

Theorem 1.

H^{2}(\Gamma;{\mathbb{Q}})
is infinite dimensional.

The structure of this paper borrows from
[
6
]
, where Cesa and Kelly attempt to prove that
H^{2}(\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t]);{\mathbb{Q}})
is infinite dimensional. In
[
6
]
, they define an infinite family of cocycles, but they do not prove that these cocycles are non-zero. For an explanation of why this is not done in
[
6
]
, see remark
9.7
. We manage to avoid this by restricting to the finite index subgroup
\Gamma
. We also take a closer look at the spherical building for
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
as a way to construct cycles which are evaluated nontrivially. In
[
6
]
, they borrow a Morse function defined in
[
4
]
, but it is used on a subspace of the building which does not necessarily have connected descending links. We avoid this problem by not using a Morse function, and instead find an alternate way to extend local disks to global disks in the building. This process is done in Section
9
and takes up a substantial portion of the paper.

1.1.
Outline of Paper

The structure of this paper is modeled on
[
11
]
, where Wortman proves a similar result for arithmetic groups over function fields. The main difference is that much effort is spent in this paper on extending local disks about vertices to global disks whose boundaries are contained in a neighbordhood of a
\Gamma
-orbit in a Euclidean building, whereas Wortman utilizes a Morse function developed in
[
4
]
. We also face the challenge that the Euclidean building we are working with is not locally compact.

In Section
2
, we review some key notions about the Euclidean building for
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
and the spherical building for
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
that will be used in this paper. We briefly explore the connection between the two, as well as the spherical building which is the visual boundary of the Euclidean building. In addition, we establish notation that will be used to denote upper triangular subgroups throughout the paper. Then we illustrate, via a lemma, why we chose
\Gamma
in such a way.

In Section
3
, we define several key maps which allow us to translate the action of a stabilizer of a vertex on that vertex’s star to the action of an upper triangular subgroup on a spherical building. Then in Section
4
, we choose a specific sequence of vertices which will be used throughout the paper.

In Section
5
, we construct a 2-connected space for
\Gamma
to act on, and show that it has a large contractible subspace.

In Section
6
, we shift our attention to the spherical building for
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
and define a nontrivial cocycle on a quotient space of it. Later in the paper, we extend this cocycle, and the cycle which it evaluates to be non-zero, to a cocycle and cycle on the larger building.

In Sections
7
and
8
, we define a series of unipotent subgroups in
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])
and show that after modding out by them, we can bound the height of certain disks in the building.

In Section
9
, we define an infinite sequence of cocycles modeled on the local cocycles found in Section
6
. Then we extend the local cycles found in Section
6
to global disks in the Euclidean building and cycles in the quotient space.

Finally, in Section
10
, we prove Theorem 1 using a spectral sequence argument.

2.
Preliminaries

We will make heavy use of Euclidean and spherical buildings in this paper. We will state some key notions below, but for reference, see
[
2
]
.

Let
X
be the Euclidean building for the group
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
. We let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the apartment in
X
corresponding to the diagonal subgroup of
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
and
x_{0}
be the vertex fixed by
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[[t^{-1}]])
. Recall that there is an action of
\GL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
on
X
which is transitive on vertices. For
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\in{\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1}))^{\times}
, we let
D(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
be the diagonal matrix with the
s_{i}
along the diagonal. Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be the chamber with vertices
x_{0}
,
D(t,1,1)x_{0}
, and
D(t,t,1)x_{0}
. Let
\mathfrak{S}
be the sector in
{\mathcal{A}}
with vertex
x_{0}
and containing
{\mathcal{C}}
. We recall that
\mathfrak{S}
is a fundamental domain for the action of
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])
on
X

[
9
]
. The vertices of the sector
\mathfrak{S}
can be given uniquely by diagonal matrices of the form
D(t^{m_{1}},t^{m_{2}},1)
where
m_{1}\geq m_{2}\geq 0
. That is, each vertex of
\mathfrak{S}
is a translate of
x_{0}
by a unique such diagonal matrix.

We will call
x_{0}
the standard vertex in
X
,
{\mathcal{C}}
the standard chamber in
X
, and
{\mathcal{A}}
the standard apartment in
X
.

Notice that
\Gamma<\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])<\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])<\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
, so each has a well-defined action on
X
. For a cell
\sigma<X
, we write
K_{\sigma}=\Stab_{K}(\sigma)
where
K
is any group which acts on
X
.

Let
\Xi
be the spherical building for
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}})
.
\Xi
can be realized as the flag complex of subspaces of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
where a basis for
{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
will give an apartment in
\Xi
. Let
e_{i}
be the
i
th standard coordinate vector in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
, then the basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
gives an apartment in
\Xi
which we will call
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Xi}
. Equivalently, we can describe simplices in
\Xi
by their stabilizers in
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}})
, which will be parabolic subgroups. We will call
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Xi}
the standard apartment in
\Xi
, and the chamber which corresponds to the complete flag
\langle e_{1}\rangle<\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle<{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
the standard chamber.

As
{\mathbb{Q}}
is the residue field for
{\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1}))
, the link about any vertex in
X
is naturally identified with
\Xi
. In Section
3
, we will detail this relationship more closely.

We let
X(\infty)
be the visual boundary of
X
. This can be naturally identified with the spherical building for
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
, so we can describe simplices in
X(\infty)
by partial flags in
({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))^{3}
. We will write
{\mathcal{A}}(\infty)
to denote the visual boundary of the apartment
{\mathcal{A}}<X
. This is the apartment of
X(\infty)
given by the basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
of
({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))^{3}
.

We will reference upper triangular subgroups frequently in this paper, so adopt the following notation.

U(K)\text{ is the subgroup of upper triangular matrices in $K$ with 1's on the diagonal}

B(K)\text{ is the subgroup of upper triangular matrices in $K$}

where
K
can be any subgroup of
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
which we reference in the paper. It is always the case that
U(K)\subseteq B(K)
, but when
K=\Gamma
, they are equal, which we prove below and is the motivating reason we chose
\Gamma
.

Lemma 2.1
.

B(\Gamma)=U(\Gamma)
.

Proof.
Notice that if
u\in B(\Gamma)
, then the product along the diagonal must be 1, so each element must be either 1 or -1 (the only units of
{\mathbb{Z}})
. But each must be taken to 1 under
\iota
, that is, each must be 1 and
u\in U(\Gamma)
.∎

3.
Stars about Vertices in
\mathfrak{S}

In this section , we give an explicit map from
\St(y)
to
\Xi
for any vertex
y
in the interior of
\mathfrak{S}
, which we denote
\mathring{\mathfrak{S}}
. In addition, we provide an equivariant map from
\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y)
to
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
. Throughout this paper, we use open cells and open stars.

First we note that there is a well-defined map from the chambers in
\St(y)
to
\Lk(y)
:

\displaystyle\downarrow_{y}\colon Ch(\St(y))

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\Lk(y)

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\widebar{\tau}\cap\Lk(y)

So in order to define a map from the chambers of
\St(y)
to the spherical building, it is sufficient to define a map from
\Lk(y)
.

Recall that each vertex in the standard sector can be given uniquely by a certain diagonal matrix in
\GL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
. For each
y\in\mathfrak{S}
, let
D_{y}
be this diagonal matrix, that is,
y=D_{y}\cdot x_{0}
.

A simple valuation argument gives us that
\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y)\subseteq\hat{U}(\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t]))
. Let
u\in\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y)
, then
D_{y}^{-1}uD_{y}\in\Stab(x_{0})=\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[[t^{-1}]])
. Consider the homomorphism from
{\mathbb{Q}}[[t^{-1}]]\to{\mathbb{Q}}
which is the identity on
{\mathbb{Q}}
and takes
t^{-1}
to 0; this induces a homomorphism from
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[[t^{-1}]])\to\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
, let’s call this homomorphism restriction to
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
(so we write
|_{\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}}
). This restriction homomorphism takes upper triangular matrices to upper triangular matrices. So we can define

\displaystyle\rho_{y}\colon\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle B(\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}})

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle(D_{y}^{-1}uD_{y})|_{\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}}

As we stated in the prior section , the link of
x_{0}
is exactly
\Xi
. If we identify
\Lk(x_{0})\cap{\mathcal{C}}
with the standard chamber in
\Xi
, then we can push around this identification with
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
. As these two spaces are identified and have an identical action by
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
, we will refer to them interchangeably.

Now we define a map from the chambers of
\St(y)
to the spherical building:

\displaystyle\eta_{y}\colon Ch(\St(y))

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\Xi

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle D_{y}^{-1}(\downarrow_{y}(\tau))

Lemma 3.1
.

For each
y\in\mathring{\mathfrak{S}}
,
\rho_{y}
and
\eta_{y}
are equivariant. That is, for
E\in Ch(\St(y))
and
u\in\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y)
:

\eta_{y}(u\cdot E)=\rho_{y}(u)\cdot\eta_{y}(E)

Proof.
First note that for any
q\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[[t^{-1}]])
and
x\in\Lk(x_{0})
,
q\cdot x=q|_{\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}}\cdot x
. So we can manipulate:

\eta_{y}(u\cdot E)=D_{y}^{-1}(\downarrow_{y}(u\cdot E)))=D_{y}^{-1}uD_{y}D_{y}^{-1}(\downarrow_{y}(E))=(D_{y}^{-1}uD_{y})\cdot\eta_{y}(E)=\rho_{y}(u)\cdot\eta_{y}(E)

∎

In addition, we prove the following two lemmas, which will be useful later:

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
y\in\mathring{\mathfrak{S}}
,
\rho_{y}(U(\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y)))
surjects onto
U(\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}})
. In addition,
\rho_{y}([U(\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y)),U(\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y))])
surjects onto
[U(\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}),U(\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}})]
.

Proof.
The first claim is a consequence of the restriction map
|_{\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}}
being the identity on
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
. So for any
h\in U(\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}})
, we have that
D_{y}hD_{y}^{-1}\in\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])}(y)
and
\rho(D_{y}hD_{y}^{-1})=h
. The same logic gives us the second claim.∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
y\in\mathring{\mathfrak{S}}
,
\rho_{y}(U(\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])}(y)))
surjects onto
U(\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Proof.
Let
u\in U(\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Z}})
. Then
D_{y}uD_{y}^{-1}\in U(\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])}(y))
, and
\rho_{y}(D_{y}uD_{y}^{-1})=u
.∎

4.
A Sequence of Vertices in
\mathfrak{S}

In this section , we describe a sequence of vertices in the sector
\mathfrak{S}
which will be used throughout the paper.

In
[
8
]
, Schulz gives a description of open hemisphere complexes in spherical buildings which we will describe here and use for this paper. Any spherical building is endowed with the angular metric
d_{\Xi}
. For any vertex
x\in\Xi
, the open hemisphere complex opposite
x
is the full subcomplex spanned by
\{y\in\Xi^{(0)}\ |\ d_{\Xi}(x,y)>\pi/2\}
. In
[
8
]
, it is proved that such open hemisphere complexes are spherical of dimension
1
. Although the name is confusing, these open hemisphere complexes are in fact closed simplicial complexes.

Let
P\in\Xi
be the parabolic subgroup of
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Q}}
which stabilizes the subspace
\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle<{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
. We will use
P
to denote both the subgroup and the corresponding vertex in
\Xi
. Let
\Omega<\Xi
be the open hemisphere complex opposite
P
. For any vertex
y\in\mathfrak{S}
, we let
\Omega(y)=\eta_{y}^{-1}(\Omega)<\St(y)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

\Omega=P\cdot\widebar{\St}(\langle e_{3}\rangle)
, where we are taking the closed star about the vertex
\langle e_{3}\rangle\in\Xi
.

Proof.
First we note that
P\cdot{\mathcal{A}}_{\Xi}=\Xi
because
\Xi
is a building. If we focus our attention on the apartment
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Xi}
, we can see that each vertex is connected by an edge to either
\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle=P
or
\langle e_{3}\rangle
. As
\Xi
is irreducible, each chamber has diameter less than
\pi/2
. So the vertices in
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Xi}
which are in
\Omega
are exactly those that are connected to
\langle e_{3}\rangle
by an edge. The span of these is precisely the closed star of
\langle e_{3}\rangle
in
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Xi}
. As
P
acts by isometries on
\Xi
, we can push this closed star around by
P
to obtain all of
\Omega
while also showing that every other vertex is within
\pi/2
of
P
.∎

For any
y\in\mathfrak{S}
, the chambers of
\Omega(y)\cap{\mathcal{A}}
can be extended to sectors in the apartment
{\mathcal{A}}
of
X
. For any such chamber
C
, let
\widehat{C}
denote this sector intersected with
\mathfrak{S}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
C<\Omega(y)\cap{\mathcal{A}}
,
\widehat{C}
is compact.

Proof.
The sector of
{\mathcal{A}}
associated to
C
is the union of every ray in
{\mathcal{A}}
starting at
y
and going through
C
. Chambers in irreducible buildings have radius at most
\pi/2
, so the half-space of
{\mathcal{A}}
composed of every ray starting at
y
with an angle greater than
\pi/2
from
P
intersects
\mathfrak{S}
in a compact set. Each
\widehat{C}
is contained in here, and hence is also compact.∎

Now let

\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}(y)=\bigcup_{C<\Omega(y)\cap{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{C}

which by construction is a compact subset of
\mathfrak{S}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists
y_{1},y_{2},\dots\in\mathfrak{S}
such that for all
k\geq 1
:

i)

y_{k}
is not in the closed star of
\partial\mathfrak{S}
, and

ii)

for all
i<k
,
y_{k}\notin\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}(P)(y_{i})

Proof.
Let
y_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}
be a vertex which is not in the closed star of the boundary of
\mathfrak{S}
. Then we define
y_{k}
recursively for
k>1
to be a vertex which is neither in the closed star of the boundary of
\mathfrak{S}
nor in
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}(P)(y_{i})
for all
i<k
. As each
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}(P)(y_{i})
is compact, and
\mathfrak{S}
minus the closed star of the boundary is an unbounded space, we can always pick another
y_{k}
.∎

5.
Construction of a
2
-connected
\Gamma
-complex

In this section , we construct a 2-connected
\Gamma
-complex
Y
, as well as a
\Gamma
-equivariant map
\psi\colon Y\to X
.

Let
X_{0}=\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot{\mathcal{C}}
, and
\psi\colon X_{0}\to X
be the inclusion map. We will add to
X_{0}
in order to construct
Y
, and extend
\psi
at the same time.

A result of Suslin
[
10
]
tells us that
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
is generated by elementary matrices which each fix a point in the closed star about
x_{0}
in
{\mathcal{A}}
, a subspace of
X_{0}
, hence
X_{0}
is connected.

Now, for any cellular map
\sigma\colon S_{1}\to X_{0}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
, glue a
2
-disk to
X_{0}
along the attaching map
\gamma\cdot\sigma
. And have
\psi
take this disk to the unique filling disk of
\psi(\gamma\cdot\sigma(S_{1}))
in
X
. Call the resulting space
X_{1}
.

Now repeat the process an additional time, glueing in
3
-disks and call the resulting space
Y
. Let
\psi
take each 3-disk to
X
in a continuous fashion that is
\Gamma
-invariant.

By construction, this space has to be 2-connected, as we have glued in any required filling disks and filling spheres. Importantly, notice that the
1
-skeleton of
Y
is the 1-skeleton of
X_{0}=\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot{\mathcal{C}}
.

5.1.
\Gamma\setminus X_{0}
is Contractible

Ultimately, we will be working with the quotient space
\Gamma\setminus Y
, but in this section, we prove the following convenient fact:

Proposition 5.1
.

The quotient space
\Gamma\setminus X_{0}
is contractible.

We do this with the several following lemmas. First we remark that the action of
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}((t^{-1})))
is type preserving on simplices of
X
. So when we write that two simplices are of the same type in
X_{0}
, we mean that there exists some
h\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
which takes one to the other. For any simplex
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq X_{0}
and vertex
x\in{\mathcal{S}}
, let

{\mathcal{S}}\setminus x=\Span\{v\in{\mathcal{S}}^{(0)}\ |\ v\neq x\}

that is, the span of the rest of the vertices of
{\mathcal{S}}
. If
{\mathcal{S}}=x
, then this is the empty set.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
be a simplex which includes
x_{0}
. Let
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\subseteq X_{0}
be a simplex of the same type such that
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\cap{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}
. Then there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.
First we address the case where
{\mathcal{S}}=x_{0}
. Thus
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}=hx_{0}
for some
h\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
. Let
h|_{\mathbb{Z}}
be the element in
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Z}}
where we restrict each entry to the constant term. This gives an element of
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Z}}
, and furthermore,
\hat{\iota}(h)=\hat{\iota}(h|_{\mathbb{Z}})
. So consider the element
h(h|_{\mathbb{Z}})^{-1}\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
. We have that
h(h|_{\mathbb{Z}})^{-1}\cdot x_{0}=h\cdot x_{0}
as
\SL_{3}{\mathbb{Z}}
fixes
x_{0}
. Also,
h(h|_{\mathbb{Z}})^{-1}
is in the kernel of
\hat{\iota}
and thus is an element of
\Gamma
. So we have provided an element of
\Gamma
which takes
{\mathcal{S}}
to
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
.

Now we address the case where
{\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}
is a vertex. There are only two such choices for
{\mathcal{S}}
: when
{\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}=D(t,1,1)x_{0}
and
{\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}=D(t,t,1)x_{0}
. Either way, there exists
h\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
such that
h{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
, and
h\in\Stab_{\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])}({\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0})
. If we examine
h|_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we notice that in the first case,

h|_{\mathbb{Z}}=\begin{pmatrix}\pm 1&*&*\\
0&*&*\\
0&*&*\end{pmatrix}\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})

which also fixes
D(t,1,1)x_{0}={\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}
. In the second case,

h|_{\mathbb{Z}}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*&*\\
*&*&*\\
0&0&\pm 1\end{pmatrix}\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})

which fixes
D(t,t,1)x_{0}={\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}
. So we can run a similar argument as in the above paragraph and see that
h(h|_{\mathbb{Z}})^{-1}({\mathcal{S}})={\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
and
h(h|_{\mathbb{Z}})^{-1}
is in
\Gamma
as it is in the kernel of
\hat{\iota}
.

If
{\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}
is an edge, then it is the edge that spans the two vertices in the paragraph above. We can run the same argument, and see that

h|_{\mathbb{Z}}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*&*\\
0&*&*\\
0&0&*\end{pmatrix}\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})

which fixes the edge and
x_{0}
. So
h(h|_{\mathbb{Z}})^{-1}\in\Gamma
is our desired element.∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq X_{0}
be a simplex which contains
x_{0}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\subseteq X_{0}
be a simplex of the same type such that
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}
. Then
\Gamma{\mathcal{S}}=\Gamma{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.
There must exist some
r\in\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
such that
r{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
. Then
r{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\cap r{\mathcal{S}}=r{\mathcal{S}}\setminus x_{0}
. So by lemma
5.2
, there exists a
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma r{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}=r{\mathcal{S}}
. Thus
r^{-1}\gamma r{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{S}}
.
\Gamma
is normal in
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
and hence we have shown that
\Gamma{\mathcal{S}}=\Gamma{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
.∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
be simplices in
X_{0}
such that
\Gamma(\partial{\mathcal{S}}_{1})=\Gamma(\partial{\mathcal{S}}_{2})
and
\Gamma x_{0}\in\Gamma(\partial{\mathcal{S}}_{1})
. Then
\Gamma{\mathcal{S}}_{1}=\Gamma{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
.

Proof.
If
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
is a translate of
x_{0}

which contains
R_{\alpha}
. This space in turn is in the orbit of the closed star about
\partial\mathfrak{S}
. To see this, recall that
\mathfrak{S}
is a strict fundamental domain for the action of
\SL_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}[t])
on all of
X
. Now we will show that the space
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot g^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
has some nice properties:

Lemma 9.15
.

U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot A_{\alpha}
is connected.

Proof.
The vertices
\{a_{\alpha}^{k}\cdot x_{0}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
are contained in
A_{\alpha}
. If we can show that the
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
-stabilizers of these vertices generate all of
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
, then we have the claim.

First note that
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cong({\mathbb{Z}}[t])^{2}
by the map which we will call
F
. For each
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the set
a_{\alpha}^{k}U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}})a_{\alpha}^{-k}
is contained in the
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
-stabilizer, so if we can show that these sets generate, then we are done. Well,
F(a_{\alpha}^{k}U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}})a_{\alpha}^{-k})=a^{\pm k}\cdot{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. We showed above that these sets generate all of
({\mathbb{Z}}[t])^{2}
, and as
F
is an isomorphism, we have that their preimages under

must generate all of
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
.∎

There is a natural continuous map
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}\colon g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}\times[0,\infty)\to g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
which moves any point
p
along
R_{\alpha}(p)
at unit-speed. To see this, remember that any apartment in
X
is a Euclidean plane and each
R_{\alpha}(p)
is parallel. We can extend this map to a continuous map on all of
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}\times[0,\infty)
by taking:

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(u\cdot p,s)=u\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(p,s)

where
u\in U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
and
p\in g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
. It remains to show that this map is well-defined.

Lemma 9.16
.

The map
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
is well-defined.

Proof.
First we show that
g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
is a strict fundamental domain for the action of
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
on
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
. That is, that if
p,p^{\prime}\in g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
and

such that

, then
p=p^{\prime}
. In this case,
u^{-1}u^{\prime}p^{\prime}=p
. As
g_{\alpha}
normalizes
U_{\alpha}
, we have that
g_{\alpha}u^{-1}u^{\prime}g_{\alpha}^{-1}\in U_{\alpha}
. Furthermore,
g_{\alpha}p,g_{\alpha}p^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
g_{\alpha}u^{-1}u^{\prime}g_{\alpha}^{-1}(g_{\alpha}p^{\prime})=g_{\alpha}p
.
{\mathcal{A}}
is contained in the fundamental domain for any unipotent acton on
X
, thus
g_{\alpha}p^{\prime}=g_{\alpha}p
, which implies that
p=p^{\prime}
.

Now we assume that
u,u^{\prime}\in U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
and
p\in g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
such that
up=u^{\prime}p
. Then we have to show that for all
s\in[0,\infty)
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(up,s)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(u^{\prime}p,s)
. That is, that
u\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(p,s)=u^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(p,s)
, or equivalently that
u^{-1}u^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(p,s)=\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(p,s)
.

Notice that
\{\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(p,s)\}_{s\in[0,\infty)}=R_{\alpha}(p)
. So it is sufficient to show that
u^{-1}u^{\prime}
fixes
R_{\alpha}(p)
.

Again, we translate to the standard apartment of
X
. As
u^{-1}u^{\prime}
fixes
p
, then
g_{\alpha}u^{-1}u^{\prime}g_{\alpha}^{-1}
fixes
g_{\alpha}p\in{\mathcal{A}}
. And again, we know that
g_{\alpha}u^{-1}u^{\prime}g_{\alpha}^{-1}\in U_{\alpha}
. In the lemma above, we showed that such unipotent elements fix a convex set in
{\mathcal{A}}
which includes
P_{\alpha}
in its visual boundary. Thus, as
g_{\alpha}u^{-1}u^{\prime}g_{\alpha}^{-1}
fixes
g_{\alpha}p
, it also fixes the entire ray
R_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p)
. This ray is exactly
g_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(p)
, so we have:

\displaystyle u^{-1}u^{\prime}\cdot R_{\alpha}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(g_{\alpha}^{-1}g_{\alpha})u^{-1}u^{\prime}(g_{\alpha}^{-1}g_{\alpha})\cdot R_{\alpha}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{-1}(g_{\alpha}u^{-1}u^{\prime}g_{\alpha}^{-1})g_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{-1}g_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(p)=R_{\alpha}(p)

∎

Eventually, we want to use our map
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
to construct homotopies which push loops in
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot A_{\alpha}
to loops in
g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
. To do this, we need the following:

Lemma 9.17
.

Let
p\in g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
and
u\in U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])
. Then there exists
S>0
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(up,s)\in g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
for
s\geq S
.

Proof.
\{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(up,s)\}_{s\geq 0}
is exactly the ray
R_{\alpha}(up)=uR_{\alpha}(p)
. As
g_{\alpha}
normalizes
U_{\alpha}
, there is some
u^{\prime}\in U_{\alpha}
such that
g_{\alpha}u=u^{\prime}g_{\alpha}
. So:

g_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(up)=g_{\alpha}uR_{\alpha}(p)=u^{\prime}g_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(p)=u^{\prime}R_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p)

R_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p)
is a ray contained in
{\mathcal{A}}
, and so we know that all but a compact portion is fixed by
u^{\prime}
. That is, there exists some
S>0
such that for all
s\geq S
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p,s)
is fixed by
u^{\prime}
. That is,
u^{\prime}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p,s)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p,s)
. Now we can apply
g_{\alpha}^{-1}
to both sides and find that for all
s\geq S
:

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{-1}u^{\prime}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p,s)

\displaystyle ug_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}p,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(p,s)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(up,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(p,s)\in g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}

∎

Lemma 9.18
.

Let
\ell
be a loop in
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
, then there exists an
S>0
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\ell,s)\in g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}

Proof.
For each point

, the above lemma gives such an

Lemma 9.19

Let
\ell
be a loop in

\ell

U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}}[t])\cdot g_{\alpha}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}

By the above lemma, we have an
