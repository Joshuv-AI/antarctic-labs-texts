Most integers are not a sum of two palindromes Thanks: Zakharov’s research was supported by the Jane Street Graduate Fellowship.
Dmitrii Zakharov

Abstract
. For g ⩾ 2 g\geqslant 2 , we show that the number of positive integers at most X X which can be written as sum of two base g g palindromes is at most X log c ⁡ X \frac{X}{\log^{c}X} . This answers a question of Baxter, Cilleruelo and Luca.



Fix an integer
g\geqslant 2
. Every positive integer
a\in\mathbb{N}
has a base
g
representation, i.e. it can be uniquely written as

(1)

a=\overline{a_{n}a_{n-1}\ldots a_{0}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}g^{i}a_{i},~\text{ where }a_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,g-1\}\text{ and }a_{n}\neq 0.

A number
a\in\mathbb{N}
with representation (
1
) is called
a base
g
palindrome
if
a_{i}=a_{n-i}
holds for all
i=0,\ldots,n
. Baxter, Cilleruelo, and Luca
[
2
]
studied additive properties of the set of base
g
palindromes. Improving on a result of Banks
[
1
]
, they showed that every positive integer can be written as a sum of three palindromes (provided that
g\geqslant 5
). They also showed that the number of integers at most
X
which are sums of two palindromes is at least
Xe^{-c_{1}\sqrt{\log X}}
and at most
c_{2}X
, for some constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}<1
depending on
g
, and asked whether a positive fraction of integers can be written as a sum of two base
g
palindromes. This was later reiterated by Green in his list of open problems as Problem 95. We answer this question negatively:

Theorem 1
.

For any integer
g\geqslant 2
there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\#\{n<X:~n\text{ is a sum of two base }g\text{ palindromes}\}\leqslant\frac{X}{\log^{c}X},

for all large enough
X
.

It is an interesting open problem to close the gap between this result and the lower bound of Baxter, Cilleruelo and Luca
[
2
]
. We now proceed to the proof.

For
n\geqslant 1
, let
P_{n}
be the set of base
g
palindromes with exactly
n
digits and
P=\bigcup_{n\geqslant 1}P_{n}
be the set of all base
g
palindromes. Note that

|P_{n}|=\begin{cases}g^{n/2}-g^{n/2-1},~n\text{ is even,}\\
g^{(n+1)/2}-g^{(n-1)/2},~n\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

For an integer
N\geqslant 1
, we write
[N]=\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
. For
A,B\subset\mathbb{Z}
we let
A+B=\{a+b,~a\in A,~b\in B\}
denote the sumset of
A
and
B
.
Let
k\geqslant 1
be sufficiently large and let
X=g^{k}
, it is enough to consider numbers
X
of this form only. With this notation, our goal is to upper bound the size of the intersection
(P+P)\cap[X]
.
We have

(P+P)\cap[X]=\bigcup_{k\geqslant n\geqslant m\geqslant 1}(P_{n}+P_{m})\cap[X]

and so we can estimate

(2)

|(P+P)\cap[X]|\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant n\geqslant m\geqslant 1}|P_{n}+P_{m}|.

We have
|P_{n}|\leqslant g^{(n+1)/2}
,
|P_{m}|\leqslant g^{(m+1)/2}
so using the trivial bound
|P_{n}+P_{m}|\leqslant|P_{n}||P_{m}|
we can immediately get rid of the terms where
m
is small:

(3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geqslant n\geqslant m\geqslant 1\\
m\leqslant k-\gamma\log k\end{subarray}}|P_{n}+P_{m}|\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant n\geqslant 1}|P_{n}|\cdot\sum_{m\leqslant k-\gamma\log k}|P_{m}|\leqslant

\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant n\geqslant 1}|P_{n}|\cdot 4g^{(k+1)/2-\gamma\log k/2}\leqslant 16g^{k+1-\gamma\log k/2}\lesssim\frac{X}{k^{\gamma\log g/2}}\sim\frac{X}{(\log X)^{\gamma\log g/2}},

where
\gamma>0
is a small constant which we will choose.
Now we focus on a particular sumset
P_{n}+P_{m}
from the remaining range. Write
m=n-d
for some
d\geqslant 0
.

For an integer
a=\overline{a_{n}\ldots a_{0}}
let
r(a)=\overline{a_{0}\ldots a_{n}}
be the integer with the reversed digit order in base
g
(we allow some leading zeros here). For
d\geqslant 0
define

a=\overline{1\underbrace{0\ldots 0}_{d}1},~b=\overline{0\underbrace{0\ldots 0}_{d}0},~a^{\prime}=\overline{0\underbrace{\ell\ldots\ell}_{d}0},~b^{\prime}=\overline{\underbrace{0\ldots 0}_{d}11},

where we denoted
\ell=g-1
. These strings are designed to satisfy the following:

(4)

a+b=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}~\text{ and }~g^{d}r(a)+r(b)=g^{d}r(a^{\prime})+r(b^{\prime}).

Indeed, note that

a^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}g^{i}\ell=g^{d+1}-g=(g^{d+1}+1)+0-(g+1)=a+b-b^{\prime}

and

g^{d}r(a^{\prime})=g^{d}a^{\prime}=g^{2d+1}-g^{d+1}=g^{d}(g^{d+1}+1)+0-(g^{d+1}+g^{d})=g^{d}r(a)+r(b)-r(b^{\prime}).

We claim that the fact that (
4
) holds for some
a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
forces the sumset
P_{n}+P_{n-d}
to be small. Roughly speaking, whenever palindromes
p\in P_{n}
and
q\in P_{n-d}
contain strings
a
and
b
on the corresponding positions, we can swap
a
with
a^{\prime}
and
b
with
b^{\prime}
to obtain a new pair of palindromes
p^{\prime}\in P_{n}
and
q^{\prime}\in P_{n-d}
with the same sum
p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}=p+q
. A typical pair
(p,q)
will have
\gtrsim C^{-d}n
disjoint substrings
(a,b)
and so we can do the swapping in
\gtrsim\exp(C^{-d}n)
different ways. So a typical sum
p+q\in P_{n}+P_{n-d}
has lots of representations and this means that the sumset has to be small.

Denote
t=[\frac{n}{3(d+2)}]
. For
p=\overline{p_{0}p_{1}\ldots p_{1}p_{0}}\in P_{n}
and
q=\overline{q_{0}q_{1}\ldots q_{1}q_{0}}\in P_{n-d}
let
S(p,q)
denote the number of indices
1\leqslant j\leqslant t
such that

(5)

\overline{p_{(d+2)j+d+1}p_{(d+2)j+d}\ldots p_{(d+2)j+1}p_{(d+2)j}}=a,

(6)

\overline{q_{(d+2)j+d+1}q_{(d+2)j+d}\ldots q_{(d+2)j+1}q_{(d+2)j}}=b,

i.e. the segments of digits of
p
and
q
in the interval
[(d+2)j,(d+2)j+d+1]
are precisely
a
and
b
.

Proposition 1
.

The number of pairs
(p,q)\in P_{n}\times P_{n-d}
such that
S(p,q)\leqslant\frac{t}{2g^{2d+4}}
is at most
\exp\left(-\frac{t}{8g^{2d+4}}\right)|P_{n}||P_{n-d}|
.

Proof.

Draw
(p,q)
uniformly at random from
P_{n}\times P_{n-d}
. Then
S(p,q)
is a sum of
t
i.i.d Bernoulli random variables with mean
g^{-2(d+2)}
. So the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{p,q}S(p,q)
is given by
\mu=tg^{-2(d+2)}
and by Chernoff bound,

\Pr[S(p,q)\leqslant\mu/2]\leqslant\exp\left(-\mu/8\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{t}{8g^{2d+4}}\right).

\Box

Now we observe that for any
p=\overline{p_{0}p_{1}\ldots p_{1}p_{0}}\in P_{n}
,
q=\overline{q_{0}q_{1}\ldots q_{1}q_{0}}\in P_{n-d}
, the sum
s=p+q
has at least
2^{S(p,q)}
distinct representations
s=p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}
for
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in P_{n}\times P_{n-d}
. Indeed, let
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{u}
be an arbitrary collection of indices such that (
5
) and (
6
) hold for
j=j_{1},\ldots,j_{u}
. Let
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
be obtained from
p
and
q
by replacing the
a
and
b
-segments on positions
j_{1},\ldots,j_{u}
by
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
and replacing
r(a)
and
r(b)
-segments on the symmetric positions by
r(a^{\prime})
and
r(b^{\prime})
, respectively. Then we claim that
p^{\prime}\in P_{n}
,
q^{\prime}\in P_{n-d}
and
p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}=p+q
. Indeed, more formally, we can write

p^{\prime}=p+\sum_{i=1}^{u}g^{(d+2)j_{i}}(a^{\prime}-a)+g^{n-(d+2)j_{i}-d-1}(r(a^{\prime})-r(a)),

q^{\prime}=q+\sum_{i=1}^{u}g^{(d+2)j_{i}}(b^{\prime}-b)+g^{(n-d)-(d+2)j_{i}-d-1}(r(b^{\prime})-r(b)),

and so (
4
) implies that
p+q=p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}
. Since we can choose
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{u}
to be an arbitrary subset of
S(p,q)
indices, we get
2^{S(p,q)}
different representations
p+q=p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}
.

Using this and Proposition
1
we get

|P_{n}+P_{n-d}|\leqslant\#\left\{p+q~|~S(p,q)\geqslant\frac{t}{2g^{2d+4}}\right\}+\#\left\{p+q~|~S(p,q)\leqslant\frac{t}{2g^{2d+4}}\right\}\leqslant

\leqslant 2^{-\frac{t}{2g^{2d+4}}}|P_{n}||P_{n-d}|+\exp\left(-\frac{t}{8g^{2d+4}}\right)|P_{n}||P_{n-d}|\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{n}{30(d+2)g^{2d+4}}\right)|P_{n}||P_{n-d}|.

Using this bound we can estimate the part of (
2
) which was not covered by (
3
):

\sum_{k\geqslant n\geqslant m\geqslant k-\gamma\log k}|P_{n}+P_{m}|\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant n\geqslant k-\gamma\log k}\sum_{d=0}^{\gamma\log k}|P_{n}+P_{n-d}|\leqslant

\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant n\geqslant k-\gamma\log k}\sum_{d=0}^{\gamma\log k}2\exp\left(-\frac{n}{30(d+2)g^{2d+4}}\right)|P_{n}||P_{n-d}|\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant n\geqslant k-\gamma\log k}2\exp\left(-\frac{n}{k^{3\gamma\log g}}\right)g^{k+1}

so if we take, say,
\gamma=\frac{1}{4\log g}
then this expression is less than, say,
k^{-1}g^{k}\lesssim\frac{X}{\log X}
provided that
k
is large enough. Combining this with (
3
) gives
|(P+P)\cap[X]|\leqslant\frac{X}{(\log X)^{0.1}}
for large enough
X
(the proof actually gives
1/4-\varepsilon
instead of
0.1
here).
