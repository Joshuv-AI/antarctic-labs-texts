Jesse Franklin, Sheng-Yang Kevin Ho, Mihran Papikian

Abstract
. We study the Drinfeld modular curves arising from the Hecke congruence subgroups of SL 2 ⁡ ( 𝔽 q ​ [ T ] ) \SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]) . Using a combinatorial method of Gekeler and Nonnengardt, we obtain a genus formula for these curves. In cases when the genus is one, we compute the Weierstrass equation of the corresponding curve.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field with
q
elements and let
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
be the
ring of polynomials in indeterminate
T
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Let
F=\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)
be the fraction field of
A
.
The degree function
\deg=\deg_{T}\colon A\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{-\infty\}
, which assigns to
0\neq a\in A
its degree
as a polynomial in
T
and
\deg_{T}(0)=-\infty
, extends to
F
by
\deg(a/b)=\deg(a)-\deg(b)
.
The map
-\deg
is a valuation on
F
; the corresponding place of
F
is usually denoted by
\infty
.
Let
\left|\cdot\right|
denote the corresponding absolute value on
F
normalized by
\left|T\right|=q
.
The completion
F_{\infty}
of
F
with respect to this absolute value is isomorphic to field
\mathbb{F}_{q}\!\left(\mkern-4.0mu\left(1/T\right)\mkern-4.0mu\right)

of Laurent series in
1/T
. Finally, let
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
be the completion of an algebraic closure of
F_{\infty}
. The absolute value

\left|\cdot\right|
has a unique extension, also denoted by
\left|\cdot\right|
, to
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
.

Through the well-known relation between
A
-lattices of rank
2
in
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
and Drinfeld
A
-modules of rank
2
over
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
, cf.
[
Dri74
]
,
the group
\GL_{2}(A)
comes to play a role in the theory of Drinfeld modules and Drinfeld modular forms similar
to the group
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
in the theory of elliptic curves over
\mathbb{C}
and classical modular forms.
In particular, the orbits of the action of
\GL_{2}(A)
on the Drinfeld upper half-plane
\Omega\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}-F_{\infty}

are in bijection with the isomorphism classes of rank
2
Drinfeld modules over
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
.

By considering congrunce subgroups of
\GL_{2}(A)
, one obtains Drinfeld modular curves of higher genus.
These modular curves possess rich arithmetic theory. An important example of a congruence group is
the Hecke congruence group
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
of level
\mathfrak{n}\in A
defined by

\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\GL_{2}(A)\ :\ \mathfrak{n}\mid c\right\}.

The quotient
Y_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\Omega
is an affine
curve with a canonical model over
F
; let
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
be the projective completion of
Y_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
. The analogue of the modularity
theorem is known in this context by the results of Drinfeld and Deligne (cf.
[
GR96
]
): If
E
is an elliptic curve over
F

with split multiplicative reduction at
\infty
and conductor
\mathfrak{n}\cdot\infty
, then there is a non-constant morphism
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\to E
.

In this article, we are interested in the congruence groups

\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\cap\SL_{2}(A)

and the corresponding Drinfeld modular curves
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
. One reason why congruence subgroups of
\SL_{2}(A)
are natural and interesting
is given in Lemma
2.2
, where it is shown that the direct sum of modular forms on
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
of fixed weight but varying type is isomorphic
to the space of modular forms on
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
. The group
\SL_{2}(A)
is also studied in Serre’s book
[
Ser80
,
\lx@sectionsign
II.2]

and in Gerritzen and van der Put’s book
[
GvdP80
, Ch. X]
. We also note that
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})=X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
when
q
is even, and there
is a natural morphism
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\to X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
of degree
2
when
q
is odd.

The main results of this article are the following:

•

In Section
3
, we compute the genus of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
following the combinatorial algorithm of Gekeler and Nonnengardt
[
GN95
]
.
The resulting formula depends on the degrees and multiplicities of prime factors in the prime decomposition of
\mathfrak{n}
.
When
\mathfrak{n}
is prime of odd degree, this formula appears in
[
GvdP80
, 10.13.3]
, where it is deduced by geometric methods.

•

Assume
q
is odd. From the genus formula, we observe that
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
has genus
1
if and only if
\deg(\mathfrak{n})=2
and
\mathfrak{n}
is square-free;
more explicitly, either
\mathfrak{n}=T(T+1)
(up to an automorphism of
F
) or
\mathfrak{n}
is irreducible of degree
2
. In these cases, in Section
4
, we deduce the
Weierstrass equation of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
.

The fact that
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
is an elliptic curve when
q
is odd and
\mathfrak{n}
is square-free of degree
2
is somewhat remarkable.
Indeed,
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
is an elliptic curve only in two cases, namely when
q=2
and
\mathfrak{n}
is either
T^{2}(T+1)
or
T^{3}
(up to an automorphism of
F
);
cf.
[
GN95
, Cor. 2.20]
. The corresponding Weierstrass equations in these cases are given in (9.7.2) and (9.7.3) of
[
GR96
]
.
In addition, among the other modular curves
X_{1}^{*}(\mathfrak{n})
,
X_{1}(\mathfrak{n})
,
X(\mathfrak{n})
considered in
[
GN95
]
, the only elliptic curve is
X_{1}(\mathfrak{n})
for

q=3
and
\mathfrak{n}=T(T+1)
.

2.
Preliminaries

In addition to the notation in the introduction, we will use the following notation and conventions.
Each nonzero ideal
\mathfrak{n}\lhd A
has a unique monic generator, which, by abuse of notation, we will also denote by
\mathfrak{n}
.
It will always be clear from the context whether
\mathfrak{n}
denotes an ideal or its monic generator. We call a nonzero prime
ideal
\mathfrak{p}\lhd A
a
prime
of
A
; the primes of
A
are in bijection with the set of monic irreducible polynomials
of
A
of positive degree.

2.1.
Drinfeld modules

An
A
-lattice

\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
of rank
r\geq 1
is an
A
-submodule of the form

\Lambda=A\omega_{1}+\cdots+A\omega_{r}
, where
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{r}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
are linearly independent over
F_{\infty}
. To such a
lattice we associate its
exponential function

e_{\Lambda}(x)=x\prod_{0\neq\lambda\in\Lambda}\left(1-\frac{x}{\lambda}\right).

The function
e_{\Lambda}(x)
is everywhere convergent on
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
and gives a surjective
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear map
e_{\Lambda}\colon\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\to\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
. Let

\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\langle x\rangle=\{a_{0}x+a_{1}x^{q}+\cdots+a_{n}x^{q^{n}}\mid n\geq 0,a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\}
be the noncommutative ring
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear polynomials with usual addition of polynomials but where multiplication is defined via the composition of polynomials. For each

a\in A
, there is
\phi_{a}^{\Lambda}(x)\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\langle x\rangle
such that
\deg_{x}\phi_{a}^{\Lambda}(x)=q^{r\deg_{T}(a)}
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\phi_{a}^{\Lambda}(x)=a
, and

e_{\Lambda}(ax)=\phi_{a}^{\Lambda}(e_{\Lambda}(x)).

The map
\phi^{\Lambda}\colon A\to\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\langle x\rangle
,
a\mapsto\phi_{a}^{\Lambda}(x)
, is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra homomorphism called the
Drinfeld module

of rank
r
associated to
\Lambda
. Conversely, if
\phi\colon A\to\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\langle x\rangle
,
a\mapsto\phi_{a}(x)
, is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra homomorphism defined by

\phi_{T}(x)=Tx+g_{1}x^{q}+\cdots+g_{r}x^{q^{r}}
with
g_{r}\neq 0
, then there is a unique
A
-lattice
\Lambda
of rank
r
such that
\phi=\phi^{\Lambda}
.
Via
\phi^{\Lambda}
,
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
acquires a new
A
-module structure
a\circ z=\phi^{\Lambda}_{a}(z)
denoted
{{}^{\phi^{\Lambda}}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
. Thus, there is an exact sequence of
A
-modules

(2.1)

0\longrightarrow\Lambda\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\overset{e_{\Lambda}}{\longrightarrow}{{}^{\phi^{\Lambda}}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\longrightarrow 0.

Two Drinfeld modules
\phi
and
\psi
are
isomorphic
if there is
c\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}

such that
\phi_{a}(cx)=c\psi_{a}(x)
for all
a\in A
. One shows that
\phi^{\Lambda_{1}}
and
\phi^{\Lambda_{2}}
are isomorphic if and only if
\Lambda_{1}=c\Lambda_{2}
for some

c\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}
, i.e., if and only if the corresponding lattices are homothetic.
We will be primarily interested in Drinfeld modules of rank
2
. In this case, every lattice is homothetic to a lattice of the form
Az+A
for some
z\in\Omega
.
It is not hard to show that the isomorphism classes of Drinfeld modules of rank
2
are in bijection with the orbits of
\GL_{2}(A)
acting on
\Omega=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}-F_{\infty}

via linear fractional transformations

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

Detailed proofs of the above statements can be found in
[
Pap23
, Ch. 5]
.

2.2.
Modular curves

The center
Z(\GL_{2}(A))\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
acts trivially on
\Omega
, so
the action of
\GL_{2}(A)
on
\Omega
factors through
\PGL_{2}(A)=\GL_{2}(A)/\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Similarly, the action of

\SL_{2}(A)
factors through
\PSL_{2}(A)=\SL_{2}(A)/\{\pm 1\}
. Let
\overline{\Gamma}_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
(resp.
\overline{\Gamma}_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
)
denote the image of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
in
\PGL_{2}(A)
(resp. the image of
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
in
\PSL_{2}(A)
).
There is a commutative diagram with exact rows

\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mod\pm 1}
\textstyle{\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mod\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}
\scriptstyle{\det}
\textstyle{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mod(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}}
\textstyle{1}
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\overline{\Gamma}_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\overline{\Gamma}_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}/(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{1.}

Hence

(2.2)

[\overline{\Gamma}_{0}(\mathfrak{n}):\overline{\Gamma}_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})]=\begin{cases}2&\text{if $q$ is odd};\\
1&\text{if $q$ is even}\end{cases}

The set
\Omega=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}-F_{\infty}
has a natural structure of a smooth rigid-analytic space; cf.
[
GR96
,
\lx@sectionsign
1]
.
The quotients
Y_{0}(\mathfrak{n})=\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\Omega
and
Y_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})=\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\Omega
are the underlying
analytic spaces of smooth affine algebraic curves defined over
F_{\infty}
; cf.
[
GR96
,
\lx@sectionsign
2]
. Denote by
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
(resp.
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
)
the projective closure of
Y_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
(resp.
Y_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
). From (
2.2
) it follows that
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})=X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
if
q
is even,
and there is a natural morphism
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\to X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
of degree
2
if
q
is odd. The set of points
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})-Y_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
are
the
cusps
of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
; the cusps of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
are defined similarly.

For
0\neq\mathfrak{n}\in A
and a Drinfeld module
\phi
of rank
2
over
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
, let
\phi[\mathfrak{n}]
be the set of zeros of
\phi_{\mathfrak{n}}(x)
. This set

\phi[\mathfrak{n}]
is an
A
-submodule of
{{}^{\phi}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
and it follows from (
2.1
) that
\phi[\mathfrak{n}]\cong\Lambda/\mathfrak{n}\Lambda\cong(A/\mathfrak{n})^{2}
; note that

\phi[\mathfrak{n}]
depends only on the ideal generated by
\mathfrak{n}
. A cyclic
\mathfrak{n}
-submodule of
\phi
is an
A
-submodule of
\phi[\mathfrak{n}]
isomorphic to
A/\mathfrak{n}
.
The curve
Y_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
classifies the isomorphism classes of pairs
(\phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{n}})
, where
\phi
is a Drinfeld module of rank
2
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{n}}

is a cyclic
\mathfrak{n}
-submodule. A moduli interpretation for
Y_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
can be deduced from
[
Bre16
, Thm. 5.2]
. If
q
is odd, then

Y_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
parametrizes isomorphism classes of Drinfeld modules with cyclic
\mathfrak{n}
-submodules along with
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
-classes of

T
-torsion points on their determinant modules. We refer to
[
Bre16
,
\lx@sectionsign
5]
for the definition of this latter concept.

Remark 2.1
.

In
[
Bre16
]
, Breuer
considers the group

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(\mathfrak{n})=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\mid\det(\gamma)\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}\}

and the quotient
Y_{0}^{+}(\mathfrak{n})=\Gamma_{0}^{+}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\Omega
. It is easy to check that the image of
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(\mathfrak{n})
in
\PGL_{2}(A)
coincides with
\overline{\Gamma}_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
, so

Y_{0}^{+}(\mathfrak{n})=Y_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
.

2.3.
Modular forms

Our main references for this subsection are
[
Gos80
]
and
[
Gek88
]
.
From now
on, unless indicated otherwise,
we assume that
q
is odd
.

Let
\Gamma
be a congruence subgroup of
\GL_{2}(A)
, i.e.,
\Gamma(\mathfrak{n})\subseteq\Gamma\subseteq\GL_{2}(A)
for some
\mathfrak{n}\neq 0
, where
\Gamma(\mathfrak{n})

is the (normal) subgroup of
\GL_{2}(A)
consisting of matrices congruent to the identity matrix modulo
\mathfrak{n}
.
A
Drinfeld modular form
for
\Gamma
of weight
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and type
m\in\mathbb{Z}/(q-1)\mathbb{Z}
is a holomorphic function

f\colon\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}

such that

(i)

f(\gamma z)=(\det\gamma)^{-m}(cz+d)^{k}f(z)
for all
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma
, and

(ii)

f(z)
is holomorphic at the cusps of
\Gamma
.

Denote the space of such functions by
M_{k,m}(\Gamma)
.

We explain condition (ii). Because
\Gamma
is a congruence group, it contains the subgroup
U_{b}=\begin{pmatrix}1&bA\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
for
some nonzero
b\in A
. Condition (i) implies that
f(z+b)=f(z)
, which itself implies that
f(z)
can be expanded as

f(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}(1/e_{bA}(z))^{n},\qquad a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty},

assuming
\Im(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\inf_{\alpha\in F_{\infty}}\left|z-\alpha\right|\gg 0
. We say that
f(z)
is
holomorphic at the cusp
\infty
if in the above expansion
a_{n}=0
for all
n<0
(this vanishing of coefficients with negative indices does not depend on the choice of
b
).
Next, for
g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\GL_{2}(A)
, put
f|_{g}(z)=(\det(g))^{m}(cz+d)^{-k}f(gz)
. This

f|_{g}
satisfies (i) for
\gamma\in g^{-1}\Gamma g
, which is again a congruence group. Condition (ii) means that
f|_{g}
is holomorphic at
\infty
for all
g\in\GL_{2}(A)
.
(Note that
f|_{g}=f
for
g\in\Gamma
, so for this last condition to hold it suffices that
f|_{g}
is holomorphic at
\infty
for left coset representatives of
\Gamma
in
\GL_{2}(A)
.)

Now we specialize to
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
. Since
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})

contains the group of scalar matrices
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, applying
condition (i) to such matrices one concludes that if
M_{k,m}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))\neq 0
, then
k\equiv 2m\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)
. Hence, if
M_{k,m}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))\neq 0
, then
k
is necessarily even and
m=k/2
or
m=k/2+(q-1)/2
modulo
q-1
. Next, a simple calculation shows that the differential
\mathrm{d}z
on
\Omega
satisfies

\mathrm{d}(\gamma z)=\frac{\det(\gamma)}{(cz+d)^{2}}\mathrm{d}z\quad\text{for all }\gamma\in\GL_{2}(F_{\infty}).

Hence, if
f(z)\in M_{2k,k}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))
, then
f(z)(\mathrm{d}z)^{k}
can be identified with a
k
-fold differential form on
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
.
These differential forms are holomorphic up to a divisor that accounts for the ramification in the covering
\Omega\to Y_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
,
and the ramification at the cusps; cf.
[
Gek86a
, pp. 51-53]
. Thus, knowing the genus of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
and the aformentioned divisor, one easily computes the dimension
of
M_{2k,k}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))
for arbitrary
k
using the Riemann-Roch theorem. On the other hand, modular forms in
M_{2k,k+\frac{q-1}{2}}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))
do
not correspond to differential forms on
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
, so the previous strategy does not quite work for computing the dimension of
M_{2k,k+\frac{q-1}{2}}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))
.
To circumvent this problem, observe that the modular forms for
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
do not have type, or rather the type is always
0
, so
every modular form in
M_{2k,0}(\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n}))
corresponds to a
k
-fold differential form on
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
. Now one can compute the
dimension of
M_{2k,k+\frac{q-1}{2}}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))
by applying the Riemann-Roch theorem to
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
, thanks to the following lemma:

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
k\geq 0
,

M_{2k,0}(\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n}))=M_{2k,k}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))\oplus M_{2k,k+\frac{q-1}{2}}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})).

Proof.

The argument here is similar to the proof of Lemma 4.3.1 in
[
Miy06
]
.
Since
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
is normal in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
,
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
acts on
M_{2k,0}(\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n}))
by

f\mapsto f|_{\gamma}
,
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
. This action induces a representation of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})/\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})

on
M_{2k,0}(\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n}))
. Since
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})/\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, all irreducible representations of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})/\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
are
induced by powers of the determinant. Therefore, we obtain

M_{2k,0}(\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n}))=\bigoplus_{m\in\mathbb{Z}/(q-1)\mathbb{Z}}M_{2k,m}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))=M_{2k,k}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}))\oplus M_{2k,k+\frac{q-1}{2}}(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})).

∎

Important examples of modular forms arise as “coefficient forms”. For
z\in\Omega
, we define the rank-
2
lattice

\Lambda_{z}=A+Az\subset\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
.
Denote the Drinfeld module of rank
2
associated to
\Lambda_{z}
by
\phi^{z}
. It is determined by

(2.3)

\phi^{z}_{T}=Tx+g(z)x^{q}+\Delta(z)x^{q^{2}}.

Then the functions
g(z)
and
\Delta(z)
are modular forms for
\GL_{2}(A)
of type
0
and of weights
q-1
and
q^{2}-1
, respectively.
Goss proved in
[
Gos80
]
that
g(z)
and
\Delta(z)
are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
, and

\bigoplus_{k\geq 1}M_{k,0}(\GL_{2}(A))=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}[g,\Delta].

It turns out that
\Delta(z)
possesses a
(q-1)
-th root in the ring of modular forms for
\GL_{2}(A)
. More precisely, Gekeler proved in
[
Gek88
]
that there is
h(z)\in M_{q+1,1}
such that
h(z)^{q-1}=\Delta(z)
, and

\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
m\in\mathbb{Z}/(q-1)\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}M_{k,m}(\GL_{2}(A))=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}[g,h].

The
j
-function
is
j(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g(z)^{q+1}/\Delta(z)
. This function is holomorphic on
\Omega
but has a pole at the cusp
\infty
.
Since
j(\gamma z)=j(z)
for all
\gamma\in\GL_{2}(A)
, it defines a rational function on
X_{0}(1)
.
In fact,
j(z)
generates the field of rational functions on
X_{0}(1)\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
. Put

\sqrt{j}(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g^{(q+1)/2}/h^{(q-1)/2}.

It is easy to check that

\sqrt{j}(\gamma z)=(\det(\gamma))^{(q-1)/2}\sqrt{j}(z)\quad\text{for all }\gamma\in\GL_{2}(A).

Thus,
\sqrt{j}(z)
is a modular function for
\SL_{2}(A)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}^{1}
be the fields of rational functions on
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
and
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
, respectively.
Then
\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}^{1}=\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}(\sqrt{j})
.

Proof.

Since
\sqrt{j}
is not a modular function for
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
but its square is, we have

[\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}(\sqrt{j}):\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}]=2.

On the other hand,
[\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}^{1}:\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}]=2
because there is a morphism
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\to X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
of degree
2
.
Since
\sqrt{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}^{1}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}^{1}=\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}(\sqrt{j})
.
∎

3.
Genus formula for
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})

In this section we compute a genus formula for
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
following
the combinatorial algorithm of Gekeler and Nonnengardt
[
GN95
]
. The algorithm itself actually computes the
quotient of the Bruhat-Tits tree
\mathcal{T}
of
\PGL_{2}(F_{\infty})
under the action of a congruence subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\GL_{2}(A)
.
The fact that the genus of the projective compactification of
\Gamma\setminus\Omega
is equal to the dimension of
the homology group
H_{1}(\Gamma\setminus\mathcal{T},\mathbb{C})
is essentially due to Mumford
[
Mum72
]
(see also Reversat
[
Rev96
]
).

A genus formula for
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
can also be deduced from the known genus formula for
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
(see
[
GN95
, Thm. 2.17]
)
by analyzing the double cover
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\to X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
and applying the Riemann-Hurwitz formula. In this approach, one
needs to compute the number of cusps and elliptic points that ramify in the covering. This calculation does not seem to be much easier
than the combinatorial and group-theoretic calculations in this section. In addition, the graph
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\mathcal{T}
contains
other interesting arithmetic information about
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
, such as the structure of
the group of connected components of the Néron model of the Jacobian variety of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
over
F_{\infty}
, or
the space of
\mathbb{C}
-valued automorphic forms for
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

(u,v)
modulo
(A/\mathfrak{n})^{\times}
. There is an isomorphism

\displaystyle\SL_{2}(A)/\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})

\displaystyle\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{P}^{1}(A/\mathfrak{n})

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\longmapsto(a:c)\mod\mathfrak{n}

as
\SL_{2}(A)
-sets, where the action of
\SL_{2}(A)
on

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}(u:v)=(au+bv:cu+dv).

Proof.

This map is surjective since given

and an arbitrary representative of it

\begin{pmatrix}b\\

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\

\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=1

Under the given map, the preimage of
(1:0)
in

\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
. Since

acts transitively on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(A/\mathfrak{n})

\mathbb{P}^{1}(A/\mathfrak{n})
is a coset of
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
.
∎

Thus, one can compute

acting on

3.2.
Prime power case

The calculation of various orbits and stabilizers in the primary case is a crucial preliminary step

To determine

First, we make some observations about

.

\#\mathbb{P}^{1}(A/\mathfrak{p}^{r})=\sum_{h=0}^{r}\#\mathbb{P}^{1}(A/\mathfrak{p}^{r})(h)=\left|\mathfrak{p}\right|^{r-1}(\left|\mathfrak{p}\right|+1)

Suppose

and the equation resulting from

, Thm. 2.17]
, gives the simple relationship

(3.7)

g\left(\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\mathcal{T}\right)=2\cdot g\left(\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\mathcal{T}\right)-1+2^{s-1}(1+\mathrm{e}(\mathfrak{n})).

Remark 3.13
.

Comparing (
3.7
) with the Riemann-Hurwitz formula, we recognize the term
2^{s}(1+\mathrm{e}(\mathfrak{n}))
as the ramification
of the covering
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\to X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
. We know that the regular cusps contribute
2^{s}
to the ramification, and the other cusps do not ramify.
Hence
2^{s}
elliptic points of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
ramify in
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\to X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
if
\mathrm{e}(\mathfrak{n})=1
, and otherwise only the regular cusps ramify.
The ramified elliptic points of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
are the ones that are unramified over
X_{0}(1)
.

Corollary 3.14
.

We have

•

g\left(\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\mathcal{T}\right)=0
if and only if either
\mathfrak{n}
is a prime of degree
1
or
\mathfrak{n}
is a square of such prime.

•

g\left(\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})\setminus\mathcal{T}\right)=1
if and only if either
\mathfrak{n}
is a product of two distinct primes of degree
1
, or
\mathfrak{n}
is a prime of degree
2
.

See the appendix for pictures of the quotient graphs in the corollary.

4.
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
of genus
1
: equations

In this section, for the cases when
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
has genus
1
(see Corollary
3.14
), we deduce
the defining Weierstrass equations. The idea of our approach is fairly simple:

(1)

In the two indicated cases,
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
has genus
0
. First, we find its “Hauptmodul”, i.e., a generator
z
of the function field
\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}
of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
.

(2)

We express the
j
-function as a rational function of
z
,
j=a(z)/b(z)
, where
a(z),b(z)\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}[z]
.

(3)

Finally, by Lemma
2.3
, the function field
\mathcal{F}_{1,\mathfrak{n}}
of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
is obtained from
\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}
by adjoining the square root of
j
.
If we identity
\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{n}}=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}(z)
, then

\mathcal{F}_{1,\mathfrak{n}}=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}(z)[\sqrt{a(z)/b(z)}]=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}(x)[\sqrt{a(z)\cdot b(z)}].

Because
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
has genus
1
, the polynomial
a(z)b(z)
, up to a square, is a monic square-free polynomial
f(z)
of degree
3
. The Weierstrass
equation of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{n})
is
y^{2}=f(z)
.

Note that in the case when
\mathfrak{n}
is a product of two distinct primes of degree
1
, by applying an automorphism of
F
, we may assume that
\mathfrak{n}=T(T+1)
.
We treat the case when
\mathfrak{n}=T(T+1)
and
\mathfrak{n}
is irreducible of degree
2
separately since the actual calculations in steps (1) and (2) above
are quite different.

4.1.
Equation of
X_{0}^{1}(T(T+1))

Let

(4.1)

\phi_{T}(x)=Tx+x^{q}+j^{-1}x^{q^{2}},

be the “universal” Drinfeld module of rank
2
with
j
-invariant
j
, where we consider
j
as a variable.
A cyclic
T
-submodule
\mathcal{C}
of
\phi
is the set of roots of an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear polynomial of the form
f_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=x+\alpha x^{q}
, where

\alpha
is another variable
(we may assume that the coefficient of
x
in
f_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
is
1
because the polynomial is separable and the set of zeros does not change
if we multiply
f_{\mathcal{C}}
by a nonzero constant). Since
\mathcal{C}\subset\phi[T]
, we have

\phi_{T}(x)=(Tx+\tilde{\alpha}x^{q})\circ(x+\alpha x^{q}).

Thus,
j^{-1}=\tilde{\alpha}\alpha^{q}
and
\alpha T+\tilde{\alpha}=1
. This leads to
j^{-1}=(1-\alpha T)\alpha^{q}
. Substituting
\alpha\mapsto-1/\alpha
, we obtain

T+\alpha+j^{-1}\alpha^{q+1}=0.

Note that
\mathcal{C}
is automatically a
\phi(A)
-module, i.e.,
f_{\mathcal{C}}\circ\phi_{T}=g\circ f_{\mathcal{C}}
for some
g\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\langle x\rangle
, since any root of
f_{\mathcal{C}}(x)

maps to
0
under the action of
\phi_{T}
. Thus,
X_{0}(T)
is defined by

j=-\frac{\alpha^{q+1}}{\alpha+T}.

Similarly,
X_{0}(T+1)
is defined by

j=-\beta^{q+1}/(\beta+(T+1))
for another variable
\beta
. Since a cyclic
T(T+1)
-submodule of
\phi_{T}
decomposes into a direct product of cyclic
T
and
T+1
submodules, we
conclude that
\mathcal{F}_{0,T(T+1)}=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}(j,\alpha,\beta)=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}(\alpha,\beta)
. Changing the notation for the variables
\alpha
and
\beta
to the
more conventional
x
and
y
, we obtain the following as an equation of the curve
X_{0}(T(T+1))
in the affine plane
\Spec(\mathbb{C}_{\infty}[x,y])
:

(4.2)

\frac{x^{q+1}}{T+x}=\frac{y^{q+1}}{(T+1)+y}.

Let
z
be a generator of the function field of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
. Put
S\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}1/(T^{q}+1)=1/(T+1)^{q}
. Then, as one checks by a straightforward calculation,

z\longmapsto(x(z),y(z))=\left(\frac{-T(z^{q+1}+z^{q}+S^{q-1}z+S^{q})}{z^{q+1}+Sz^{q}+S^{q-1}z+S^{q}},\frac{-(T+1)(z^{q+1}+z^{q}+S^{q-1}z+S^{q})}{z^{q+1}+S^{q-1}z}\right),

defines a morphism
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}\to X_{0}(T(T+1))
, i.e.,
x(z)
and
y(z)
satisfy (
4.2
). Since this morphism
has degree
1
, it is an isomorphism. Write

j(z)=-\frac{x(z)^{q+1}}{T+x(z)}=\frac{a(z)}{b(z)},

where
a(z),b(z)\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}[z]
. One checks by a tedious calculation that

(4.3)

\displaystyle a(z)b(z)=

\displaystyle(T^{q}+1)z^{q}\left(z+\frac{1}{(T+1)^{q}}\right)^{q}\left(z+\frac{1}{(T+1)^{q-1}}\right)^{q^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(z^{q+1}+z^{q}+S^{q-1}z+S^{q}\right)^{q+1}.

As was explained at the beginning of this section, extracting the square-free part from the above polynomial,
we obtain a Weierstrass equation for
X_{0}^{1}(T(T+1))
:

(4.4)

y^{2}=x\left(x+\frac{1}{(T+1)^{q}}\right)\left(x+\frac{1}{(T+1)^{q-1}}\right).

(Here we again changed the notation for the variable to
z
to the more conventional
x
.)

The elliptic curve given by (
4.4
) is visibly defined over
F
. Thus, the modular curve
X_{0}^{1}(T(T+1))

has a model over
F
, but the model (
4.4
) is not “canonical” in a modular sense.
In fact, throughout this subsection we could have worked over
F
, since (
4.2
) and the explicit parametrization
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{F}\to X_{0}(T(T+1))

are defined over
F
. Extracting the square-free part of (
4.3
) over
F
, we end up with

y^{2}=(T+1)x(x+1/(T+1)^{q})(x+1/(T+1)^{q-1})
. So, substituting
y\mapsto y/(T+1)
and
x\mapsto x/(T+1)
, we get the following Weierstrass
equation for
X_{0}^{1}(T(T+1))
:

(4.5)

\boxed{y^{2}=x\left(x+\frac{1}{(T+1)^{q-1}}\right)\left(x+\frac{1}{(T+1)^{q-2}}\right)}

There are some differences between the models (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) of
X_{0}^{1}(T(T+1))
. The elliptic
curve
E_{1}
defined by (
4.4
) has conductor
\mathfrak{n}_{E_{1}}=T\cdot(T+1)^{2}\cdot\infty
,
and the elliptic curve
E_{2}
defined by (
4.5
) has conductor
\mathfrak{n}_{E_{2}}=T\cdot(T+1)\cdot\infty^{2}
. In particular, these
curves are not isomorphic over
F
.
It is known that the conductor of a non-isotrivial elliptic curve over
F
must have degree
\geq 4
, so both
\mathfrak{n}_{E_{i}}
are minimal in that sense.
Elliptic curves over
F
with conductor of degree
4
are called
extremal
; such curves have special geometric properties; cf.
[
Ito98
]
.

4.2.
Equation of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})

In this subsection, we assume that
\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+aT+b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
is irreducible of degree
2
.
One can pursue the same strategy as in
\lx@sectionsign
4.1
to obtain an equation for
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
, but this results in a system of
polynomial equations rather than a convenient plane model. Instead we use modular forms to construct an explicit generator of
\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{p}}
;
this is partly motivated by
[
Yan06
]
.

Let
\Delta(z)
be the Drinfeld discriminant function defined in
\lx@sectionsign
2.3
and let
\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Delta(\mathfrak{p}z)
.
The quotient
\Delta/\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}
is invariant under the action of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
. It was shown by Gekeler (see
[
Gek97
, (3.18), (3.20), (3.22)]
) that there is a
modular form
\eta(z)
for
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
of weight
0
and type
0
such that
\eta^{q^{2}-1}=\Delta/\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Moreover, from the proof of Corollary 3.23 in
[
Gek97
]
,
the divisor of
\eta
on
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
is
[0]-[\infty]
, where
[0]
and
[\infty]
are the cusps of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
. Thus,
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}(\eta)=\mathcal{F}_{0,\mathfrak{p}}
. The
problem now becomes expressing
j(z)
as a rational function in
\eta(z)
.

Remark 4.1
.

For general irreducible
\mathfrak{p}
, the cuspidal divisor group
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{p})
of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
is cyclic of order
(\left|\mathfrak{p}\right|-1)/(q^{2}-1)
if
\deg(\mathfrak{p})
is even
and
(\left|\mathfrak{p}\right|-1)/(q-1)
if
\deg(\mathfrak{p})
is odd; see
[
Gek97
, Cor. 3.23]
. The cuspidal divisor group of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
is the subgroup
of its Jacobian generated by the differences of cusps. Since there are only two cusps in this case, the cuspidal divisor group is clearly cyclic.
The calculation of its order is closely related to the calculation of the largest root of
\Delta/\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}
in the group of modular units on
\Omega
.
Gekeler’s result can be extended to
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
as follows: The cuspidal divisor group
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
is cyclic of order

2(\left|\mathfrak{p}\right|-1)/(q^{2}-1)
if
\deg(\mathfrak{p})
is even
and
(\left|\mathfrak{p}\right|-1)/(q-1)
if
\deg(\mathfrak{p})
is odd. The proof is similar to
loc. cit.

Again,
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
has only two cusps, which, by abuse of notation,
we denote
[0]
and
[\infty]
, depending on which cusp of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
each maps to under
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})\to X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
.
The divisor of
\Delta/\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}
on
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
is
2(\left|\mathfrak{p}\right|-1)\cdot([0]-[\infty])
. It follows from (3.21) and (3.22) in
[
Gek97
]

that the largest
n
such that there is a holomorphic nonvanishing function
D_{\mathfrak{p}}
on
\Omega
such that
D_{\mathfrak{p}}^{n}=\Delta/\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}

and
D_{\mathfrak{p}}
is invariant under the action of
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
is
q^{2}-1
if
\deg(\mathfrak{p})
is even and
2(q-1)
if
\deg(\mathfrak{p})
is odd.
This implies that the order of
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
is given by the formula above.

As a function on
X(1)
,
j(z)
has a simple pole at the unique cusp
[\infty^{\prime}]
of
X(1)
. In the natural covering
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})\to X(1)
, which has degree
q^{2}+1
,
the cusp
[\infty]
does not ramify over
[\infty^{\prime}]
but the cusp
[0]
ramifies with index
q^{2}
. Thus, as a function on
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
,
j(z)

has a pole of order
q^{2}
at
[0]
, a simple pole at
[\infty]
, and no other poles. Since
\eta
has a simple zero at
[0]
, and no other zeros, there
is a polynomial
f_{1}(x)\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}[x]
of degree
\leq q^{2}
such that
j-f_{1}(\eta)/\eta^{q^{2}}
has a pole only at
[\infty]
and a zero at
[0]
. Moreover, because

\eta
has a simple pole at
[\infty]
, the pole of
j-f_{1}(\eta)/\eta^{q^{2}}
at
[\infty]
is the pole of
j
, so it is simple. Since
\eta

also has a simple pole at
[\infty]
, there is a nonzero constant
c\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
such that
j-f_{1}(\eta)/\eta^{q^{2}}-c\eta
has no poles, so it is a constant.
Because
\eta
and
j-f_{1}(\eta)/\eta^{q^{2}}
have zeros at
[0]
, the constant above is zero. We conclude that there is a polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}[x]

of degree
q^{2}+1
such that

j=\frac{f(\eta)}{\eta^{q^{2}}}.

Once we find
f(x)
, this can serve as a defining equation of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
if
j
and
\eta
are treated as indeterminates. Moreover,
the square-free part of
f(x)
must be a quadratic polynomial
f_{2}(x)
with distinct roots and the equation of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
will be
y^{2}=x\cdot f_{2}(x)

by the argument outlined at the beginning of this section. To find
f
we use the
t
-expansions of
j(z)
and
\eta(z)
.

Let
\pi_{C}
be the Carlitz period, i.e., a generator of the lattice corresponding to the Carlitz module defined by
\rho_{T}=Tx+x^{q}
. Let

t(z)=\frac{1}{\pi_{C}e_{A}(z)}=\pi_{C}^{-1}\sum_{a\in A}\frac{1}{z+a}.

This function is the parameter at infinity of
\Omega
analogous to the classical
\exp(2\pi iz)
. Define the
a
-th inverse cyclotomic polynomial

\theta_{a}(x)=\rho_{a}(x^{-1})x^{\left|a\right|}.

Gekeler proved that (cf.
[
Gek88
, Thm. 6.1]
)

(4.6)

\pi_{C}^{1-q^{2}}\Delta(z)=-t^{q-1}\prod_{a\in A\text{ monic}}\theta_{a}(t)^{(q^{2}-1)(q-1)}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\delta_{i}t^{(q-1)i},

where the product converges for
\Im(z)
sufficiently large. (Since
\GL_{2}(A)
contains
matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, modular transformation rule for
\Delta(z)

implies that
\Delta(\alpha z)=\Delta(z)
, so the coefficient of
t^{i}
in the
t
-expansion of
\Delta
is zero if
(q-1)\nmid i
.)
Using (
4.6
), it is possible to compute the coefficients
\delta_{i}
,
i\leq N
, for any desired finite
N
. Already in
[
Gek88
, p. 692]
,
one finds (nonzero)
\delta_{i}
for
i\leq 2q^{2}
:

\delta_{1}=-1,\quad\delta_{q}=1,\quad\delta_{q+1}=-(T^{q}-T),\quad\delta_{q^{2}-q+1}=-1,\quad\dots

Next, by
[
Gek88
, (6.2)]
,

\displaystyle t_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}t(\mathfrak{p}z)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{\left|\mathfrak{p}\right|}}{\theta_{\mathfrak{p}}(t)}=\frac{t^{q^{2}}}{\mathfrak{p}t^{q^{2}-1}+(T^{q}+T+a)t^{q^{2}-q}+1}

\displaystyle=t^{q^{2}}-(T^{q}+T+a)t^{2q^{2}-q}+\cdots

Substituting
t_{\mathfrak{p}}
into
\Delta(t)
, one obtains the
t
-expansion of

\pi_{C}^{1-q^{2}}\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)=\pi_{C}^{1-q^{2}}\Delta(t_{\mathfrak{p}})=-t^{(q-1)q^{2}}+\cdots

Thus, we can compute (using a computer) the
t
-expansion of

\Delta/\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}=t^{-(q^{2}-1)(q-1)}+\cdots

and also find a
(q^{2}-1)
th root
\eta(s):=\sqrt[q^{2}-1]{\Delta/\Delta_{\mathfrak{p}}}:=s^{-1}+\cdots\in\frac{1}{s}\cdot\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)[[s]]
with leading coefficient
1
, where
s=t^{q-1}
(cf.
[
Gek88
, p. 683]
). Next,

[
Gek88
, Cor. 10.11]
gives the
s
-expansion of
g(z)
defined in (
2.3
):

\pi_{C}^{1-q}g(s)=1-(T^{q}-T)\left(s+s^{q^{2}-q+1}(1-s^{q-1}+(T^{q}-T)s^{q})^{(1-q)}\right)+\cdots

and thus also of
j(s)=g^{q+1}/\Delta=-s^{-1}+\cdots

Substituting the
s
-expansions of
\eta
and
j
into
j\eta^{q^{2}}=f(\eta)
and treating the coefficients of
f(x)
as variables, one obtains
a system of linear equations in those variables that can be uniquely solved (the number of coefficients in the
s
-expansions of
\eta
and
j

that need to be computed to solve this system depends on
q
).

Example 4.2
.

The computations in this and the other examples in this section were performed using the computer program
Magma
.

Let
q=3
and
\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+T+2
. We have

\displaystyle\eta(s)

\displaystyle=s^{-1}+s+(2T+1)s^{2}+T(T+1)s^{3}-(T-1)^{3}s^{4}+\cdots

\displaystyle j(s)

\displaystyle=-s^{-1}+(T^{3}-T)-s+(T^{9}+T^{3}+T)s^{2}+\cdots

In this case, we find that

f(x)=-(x^{2}+(2T+1)\mathfrak{p}x-\mathfrak{p}^{2})^{4}(x^{2}+(2T+1)x+\mathfrak{p}).

Thus,
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
can be defined by the equation

y^{2}=x(x^{2}+(2T+1)x+\mathfrak{p}).

For the other two irreducibles of degree
2
in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[x]
we get:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+2T+2

\displaystyle\leadsto

\displaystyle f(x)=-(x^{2}+(2T+2)\mathfrak{p}x-\mathfrak{p}^{2})^{4}\cdot(x^{2}+(2T+2)x+\mathfrak{p})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+1

\displaystyle\leadsto

\displaystyle f(x)=-(x^{2}+(2T)\mathfrak{p}x-\mathfrak{p}^{2})^{4}\cdot(x^{2}+(2T)x+\mathfrak{p}).

Thus, writing
\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+aT+b
we conclude that for
q=3
the equations of modular curves are

\displaystyle X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})\colon\quad j\cdot x^{q^{2}}=-(x^{2}+(2T+a)\mathfrak{p}x-\mathfrak{p}^{2})^{(q+1)}(x^{2}+(2T+a)x+\mathfrak{p})

\displaystyle X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})\colon\quad y^{2}=x(x^{2}+(2T+a)x+\mathfrak{p}).

Remark 4.3
.

The computations leading to an equation for
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
are valid also in even characteristic. For example, for
q=2
and
\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+T+1

one obtains

X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})\colon\quad j\cdot x^{q^{2}}=(x-\mathfrak{p})^{(q+1)}(x^{2}+x+\mathfrak{p}).

Example 4.4
.

Now let
q=5
and
\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+T+2
. One computes that

\displaystyle\eta(s)=s^{-1}+s^{3}+(2T+1)s^{4}+\mathfrak{p}s^{5}+\cdots

\displaystyle j(s)=-s^{-1}+(T^{5}-T)-s^{3}+(T^{25}+T^{5}+3T)s^{4}+(4T^{30}+T^{26}+T^{6}+4T^{2})s^{5}+\cdots.

With the help of these expansions, one computes the
defining equation of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
:

j=-\frac{(\eta^{4}-(T+3)(T^{2}+T+1)\mathfrak{p}\eta^{3}+2(T+2)(T+4)\mathfrak{p}^{2}\eta^{2}+(2T+1)\mathfrak{p}^{3}\eta-\mathfrak{p}^{4})^{6}(\eta^{2}+(2T+1)\eta+\mathfrak{p})}{\eta^{q^{2}}}.

Similar computations for
\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+T+1
lead to

j=-\frac{(\eta^{4}-(T+3)(T^{2}+T+2)\mathfrak{p}\eta^{3}+2T(T+1)\mathfrak{p}^{2}\eta^{2}+(2T+1)\mathfrak{p}^{3}\eta-\mathfrak{p}^{4})^{6}(\eta^{2}+(2T+1)\eta+\mathfrak{p})}{\eta^{q^{2}}}.

Thus, we conclude that in both cases

X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
is defined by
y^{2}=x(x^{2}+(2T+a)x+\mathfrak{p})
.

Based on the above examples, we propose that for odd
q
and
\mathfrak{p}=T^{2}+aT+b
, the elliptic curve
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
is defined by

(4.7)

\boxed{y^{2}=x(x^{2}+(2T+a)x+\mathfrak{p})}

Remark 4.5
.

It is easy to see certain patterns in
-f(x)/(x^{2}+(2T+a)x+\mathfrak{p})
, namely this is a polynomial of the form

\left(x^{q-1}+b_{q-2}\mathfrak{p}x^{q-2}+b_{q-3}\mathfrak{p}^{2}x^{q-3}+\cdots+b_{0}\mathfrak{p}^{q-1}\right)^{q+1},

where
b_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
,
\deg_{T}(b_{i})=i
for
0\leq i\leq q-2
,
b_{0}=-1
,
b_{1}=2T+a
. But what is the general formula for these polynomials is not clear to us.

The elliptic curve (
4.7
) is visibly defined over
F
. Moreover, as one verifies using Tate’s algorithm, its
conductor is
\mathfrak{p}\cdot\infty^{2}
and its fibres at
\mathfrak{p}
and
\infty
are of types
I_{2}
and
I_{2}^{*}
, respectively, in Kodaira’s classification;
cf.
[
Sil94
, p. 354]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve over
F
with multiplicative reduction at
\mathfrak{p}
, bad reduction at
\infty
of type
I_{2}^{*}
, and good reduction
at all other places of
F
. Such an
E
is isomorphic over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(T)
to the elliptic curve defined by (
4.7
).

Proof.

By assumption, the conductor of
E
is
\mathfrak{p}\cdot\infty^{2}
, so it has degree
4
. Thus,
E
is extremal over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(T)
; see
[
Ito98
, Prop. 4.2]
.
Such curves have been classified in characteristic
p\geq 5
by W. Lang
[
Lan94
]
and
p=2,3
by Schweizer
[
Sch00
]
.
A convenient summary of Lang’s result can be found in
[
Ito98
, p. 720]
.

Now note that over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(T)
the curve
E
will have bad reduction at three places, two of which lie over
\mathfrak{p}
and one lies over
\infty
.
The reduction at the places over
\mathfrak{p}
will be multiplicative and the reduction at the place over
\infty
must still
be additive as the conductor cannot have degree
<4
. Thus, the type of
E
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(T)
is
(I_{n},I_{n},I_{2}^{*})
.
Comparing with types of extremal elliptic curves in Lang’s classification (see
[
Ito98
, p. 720]
and also
[
Sch00
, Prop. 4.2]
),
we see that only the type
(I_{2},I_{2},I_{2}^{*})
is possible, and there is a unique curve of this type, up to an isomorphism. Because the
curve defined by (
4.7
) has this type, the lemma follows.
∎

By adapting an argument of Deligne and Rapoport for classical modular curves
to Drinfeld modular curves, Gekeler showed in
[
Gek86b
]
that
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
has a model over
\Spec(A)
which has semistable fibre
at
\mathfrak{p}
and smooth fibres at all other primes of
A
; here
\mathfrak{p}
is an arbitrary prime of
A
.
This heavily relies on the moduli interpretation of
X_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
and the work of Drinfeld
[
Dri74
]
.
Using similar methods, it should be possible to prove the same result also for
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
, although the arguments will be quite technical.
Assuming this, namely that
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
has a model over
\Spec(A)
with semistable fibre
at
\mathfrak{p}
and smooth fibres at all other primes of
A
, we have the following:

Proposition 4.7
.

Under the above assumption,
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
is isomorphic over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(T)
to the elliptic curve defined by (
4.7
)
when
\mathfrak{p}
has degree
2
.

Proof.

Thanks to Lemma
4.6
, it is enough to show that the reduction type of the given model of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
at
\infty
is
I_{2}^{*}
.
Because the conductor of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
must have degree at least
4
, the reduction at
\infty
is additive. On the other hand,

X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
has rigid analytic uniformization over
F_{\infty}
. Thus, it has a model over
\mathcal{O}_{\infty}
whose closed fibre
is the dual graph of
\Gamma_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})\setminus\mathcal{T}
without cusps. Since this graph consists of two edges
joined at their terminal vertices and the stabilizers of the edges are
\pm 1
(see Figure
3
), one concludes that
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
over
F_{\infty}
has
multiplicative reduction and its component group has order
2
. (This is mostly a consequence of well-known facts about Mumford curves;
cf.
[
Pap04
,
\lx@sectionsign
4.2]
.) Therefore, the fibre at
\infty
of the given model of
X_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{p})
over
F
is of type
I_{2}^{*}
.
∎
