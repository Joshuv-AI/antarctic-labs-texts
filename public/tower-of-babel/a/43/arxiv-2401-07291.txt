Abstract
. In recent years, SPDEs have become a well-studied field in mathematics. With their increase in popularity, it becomes important to efficiently approximate their solutions. Thus, our goal is a contribution towards the development of efficient and practical time-stepping methods for SPDEs. Operator splitting schemes are a powerful tool for deterministic and stochastic differential equations. An example is given by domain decomposition schemes, where we split the domain into sub-domains. Instead of solving one expensive problem on the entire domain, we deal with cheaper problems on the sub-domains. This is particularly useful in modern computer architectures, as the sub-problems may often be solved in parallel. While splitting methods have already been used to study domain decomposition methods for deterministic PDEs, this is a new approach for SPDEs. We provide an abstract convergence analysis of a splitting scheme for stochastic evolution equations and state a domain decomposition scheme as an application of the setting. The theoretical results are verified through numerical experiments.

1.
Introduction

Modeling with partial differential equations (PDEs) is ubiquitous in many areas of natural sciences and engineering. Phenomena described by PDEs include models of vibrations or waves, flows of liquids or the distribution of heat. In all such models, one may take uncertainty of parameters, natural variability of evolution or extrinsic noise into account, arriving at random PDEs (RPDEs) or stochastic PDEs (SPDEs). In the former case, only parameters or initial or boundary data are described by random variables or processes, whereas in the latter case the new type of PDEs involves a driving noise, often an infinite dimensional Wiener process. Applications employing SPDEs include neuroscience
[
11
,
34
]
, micro-ferro-magnetism
[
4
,
6
]
, reservoir engineering and geo-statistical climate models
[
17
,
28
]
.
Design and analysis of numerical methods to approximate solutions of SPDEs or quantities of interest constitute an active area of research, we refer to early articles
[
20
,
22
,
35
]
, and the book
[
30
]
. However, compared to the level of theoretical and practical development of numerical methods for PDEs, this area is still in its infancy. In this article, we are in particular interested in the topic of domain decomposition, which is a standard tool for the parallelisation of numerical algorithms and thus speed-up, as well as treatment of multi-physics description systems.
To the best of our knowledge, for SPDEs this topic is only addressed in
[
8
,
23
,
39
]
. While in
[
39
]
the problem considered differs from our work as it is only concerned with stationary models, the papers
[
8
,
23
]
also consider time-dependent problems. However, their underlying equations are either less general, in
[
8
]
the stochastic heat equation is considered, or in
[
23
]
they consider the nonlinear Schrödinger equation which is of a different kind.
In contrast to the very few works on domain decomposition approaches for SPDEs, there exist several articles treating RPDEs, e.g.,
[
10
,
24
]
.

As stated in
[
31
]
, domain decomposition methods can be described as a class of divide-and-conquer methods for the numerical approximation of solutions of PDEs. A standard approach for a decomposition framework, called hybrid formulation in
[
31
]
, consists of the following steps. First the domain of the PDE is decomposed into (overlapping or non-overlapping) sub-domains, then, on each sub-domain, a local PDE problem is formulated and neighbouring local problems are coupled by matching conditions. Each local problem must be well-defined and uniquely solvable, as well as consistent with the global PDE problem. Overall, the coupled system of PDEs is required to be equivalent to the original problem and the global solution can be found by summing up the local solutions using an appropriate partition of unity. Variations of this setting include Schwarz iterative methods or Lagrange Multiplier methods in the context of optimization problems. This approach works directly for time-independent PDEs, while in the case of parabolic PDEs, usually the first step is to apply an implicit time discretization method. For each time-step one obtains a time-independent problem, to which a domain decomposition method can be applied.

Another divide-and-conquer approach to solving differential equations (usually time-dependent problems) is a splitting method, we refer to
[
19
,
32
]
for overviews in the deterministic case and very exemplary to
[
1
,
3
,
7
,
36
]
for the case of stochastic differential equations.

Domain decomposition operator splittings, which were introduced already in 1989 by Vabishchevich
[
37
]
and called regionally additive schemes there, have been discussed recently in various contexts, see
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
18
,
21
]
. The main idea, in contrast to the approach to domain decomposition described above, is to first decompose or split the differential operators and right-hand side of the PDE into a sum of component terms, where each one is defined on a sub-domain. Therefore, each sub-operator represents the vector field of the global PDE on its corresponding sub-domain.
These split operators can then directly be incorporated in the time-integration through an operator splitting. Thus, an advantage of this formulation is that the domain decomposition and its corresponding error is directly combined in the time-integration strategy. In contrast to this, the previous approach only uses the domain decomposition as a black box method to solve elliptic equations that arise in every time step after the application of a time-stepping method. In this process of solving the arising elliptic equations, a domain decomposition method is usually used as an iterative method that can require additional computational costs due to the iterations. These additional iterations do not appear in our approach.

The purpose of this article is to extend this approach to certain class of stochastic evolution equations for the first time. In particular, we provide the theoretical background and analysis for a domain decomposition operator splitting applied to a stochastic evolution equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\mathrm{d}u(t)=\tilde{A}(t,u(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+B(t,u(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad t\in(0,T],\\
u(0)=u_{0}.\end{cases}

More precisely, we are considering an abstract Cauchy problem with nonlinear monotone and coercive drift term, additional Lipschitz nonlinear term and multiplicative
Q
-Wiener noise. The equation is studied in the variational context which is well-suited for our numerical analysis, in particular for deriving a priori bounds. In order to obtain the final error bound for the method, we require additional regularity of the solution. More precisely, we need the solution to be Hölder continuous in time. While this cannot be guaranteed for every admissible solution in our framework, we can state one slightly more restrictive setting where the needed regularity is available in the existing literature. Note that these additional assumptions are not needed, if the Hölder regularity of the solution can be obtained by other means.
The considered semi-discrete operator splitting scheme involves a decomposition of the space into sub-spaces and splitting all the operators on these sub-spaces. On every sub-space, the obtained split Cauchy problem is discretized in time using an implicit-explicit (IMEX) Euler–Maruyama method.

For the method, we prove well-posedness of the stated implicit equations. The path-wise well-posedness follows directly from standard PDE theory and we also provide the measurability of the obtained solution. As a particular example of our scheme, we consider a domain decomposition method. Based on the derived bound of the semi-discrete solution, we provide mean-square convergence of our numerical method.
In the last part of the paper, we illustrate the obtained convergence with numerical experiments for a two-dimensional stochastic heat equation. Here, we consider both a linear equation with a multiplicative noise term and a semi-linear equation with additive noise.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we state the stochastic evolution equation considered in this paper with all the needed assumptions. Moreover, we specify the solution concept and state a restricted setting where we can provide addtional regualrity of the solution. With this at hand we can then state the semi-discretized scheme in Section
3
. More precisely, we give a detailed explanation about the setup of the scheme in Section
3.1
, prove the well-posedness of the implicit equation in Section
3.2
and conclude the section with a our main application for the splitting which is a domain decomposition of an SPDE. The main theoretical result of this paper can be found in Section

4.3

5

A

2.
Analytical background

In this section, we formally state the equation and gather the main assumptions concerning the setting we are interested in.
In the following, let
T\in(0,\infty)
be a given final time point. We then denote the underlying filtered probability space by
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},\mathcal{P})
, which satisfies the usual conditions, and we assume that the
Q
-Wiener process
W
is adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. We then consider the stochastic abstract Cauchy problem:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\mathrm{d}u(t)=\tilde{A}(t,u(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+B(t,u(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad t\in(0,T],\\
u(0)=u_{0},\end{cases}

where the operator
\tilde{A}(t,\cdot)
consits of three different parts

\displaystyle\tilde{A}(t,\cdot)=F(t,\cdot)+G(t)-A(t,\cdot)

that we will explain in more detail in the following subsection. Decomposing the operator
\tilde{A}(t,\cdot)
into these three parts provides a large degree of freedom for the numerical scheme explained in Section
3.1
. We will use the different parts of the operator to propose an implicit-explicit (IMEX) time-stepping method in combination with an operator splitting that can be used to describe a domain decomposition method.

2.1.
Assumptions

Let
(H,(\cdot,\cdot)_{H},\|\cdot\|_{H})
be a real, separable Hilbert space and let
(V,\|\cdot\|_{V})
be a real, separable, reflexive Banach space such that
V
is continuously and densely embedded into
H
. In a standard way, we obtain the Gelfand triple
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\hookrightarrow}}H\cong H^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\hookrightarrow}}V^{*}
.
For
f\in V^{*}
and
v\in V
we denote the dual paring
\langle f,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=f(v)
, which extends the
H
-inner product.
In the following, we state the exact assumptions needed on the coefficients in (
2.1
).

Assumption 1
.

The initial data
u_{0}\colon\Omega\to H
is
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable and belongs to
L^{2}(\Omega;H)
.

Assumption 2
.

Let the nonlinear operator
A\colon[0,T]\times V\times\Omega\to V^{*}
be progressively measurable. By writing
A(t,v)
the map
\omega\mapsto A(t,v,\omega)
is meant. Moreover, the following conditions hold:

(i)

The operator
A(t,\cdot)
is monotone and coercive uniformly in
t
, i.e. there exist constants
\eta,\mu\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\langle A(t,v)-A(t,w),v-w\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\geq\eta\|v-w\|_{V}^{2},\quad\langle A(t,v),v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\geq\mu\|v\|_{V}^{2}

almost surely for all
t\in[0,T]
,
v,w\in V
.

(ii)

The operator
A(t,\cdot)
is bounded uniformly at
0\in V
,
\nu_{A}
-Hölder continuous with respect to the first argument and Lipschitz continuous with respect to the second argument, i.e. there exist constants
\nu_{A}\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
K_{A},L_{A}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\|A(t,0)\|_{V^{*}}\leq K_{A},\quad\|A(t,v)-A(\tau,w)\|_{V^{*}}\leq L_{A}\big(|t-\tau|^{\nu_{A}}+\|v-w\|_{V}\big)

almost surely for
t,\tau\in[0,T]
and
v,w\in V
.

Assumption 3
.

Let the nonlinear operator
F\colon[0,T]\times H\times\Omega\to H
and the function
G\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to V^{*}
be progressively measurable. Again, by writing
F(t,v)
and
G(t)
the maps
\omega\mapsto F(t,v,\omega)
and
\omega\mapsto G(t,\omega)
are meant. Moreover, the operator
F(t,\cdot)
is bounded uniformly at
0\in H
,
\nu_{F}
-Hölder continuous with respect to the first variable and Lipschitz continuous with respect to the second variable, i.e. there exist constants
\nu_{F}\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
K_{F},L_{F}\in[0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\|F(t,0)\|_{H}\leq K_{F},\quad\|F(t,v)-F(\tau,w)\|_{H}\leq L_{F}\big(|t-\tau|^{\nu_{F}}+\|v-w\|_{H}\big)

almost surely for
t,\tau\in[0,T]
and
v,w\in H
. Moreover, there exists
\nu_{G}\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
such that the function
G
is an element of
C^{\nu_{G}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;V^{*}))
.

In the following, we denote the space of linearly bounded operators from a Hilbert space
(H_{1},(\cdot,\cdot)_{H_{1}},\|\cdot\|_{H_{1}})
into a second Hilbert space
(H_{2},(\cdot,\cdot)_{H_{2}},\|\cdot\|_{H_{2}})
by
\mathcal{L}(H_{1},H_{2})
. The space is equipped with the norm
\|S\|_{\mathcal{L}(H_{1},H_{2})}=\sup_{\|v\|_{H_{1}}=1}\|Sv\|_{H_{2}}
.
Moreover, by
\mathcal{HS}(H_{1},H_{2})
we denote the Hilbert space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators equipped with the norm
\|S\|_{\mathcal{HS}(H_{1},H_{2})}=\sqrt{\text{tr}(S^{*}S)}
, where

is the trace operator.

Assumption 4
.

Let
Q\in\mathcal{L}(H)
be a self-adjoint, positive operator with finite trace whose eigenfunctions
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
form an orthonormal basis of
H
. The corresponding eigenvalues of
Q
are denoted by
\{q_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. With a family
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of independent scalar Brownian motions on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},\mathcal{P})
, the
Q
-Wiener noise can be stated through

\displaystyle W(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k}\beta_{k}(t),

almost surely for
t\in[0,T]
.

Assumption 5
.

For
L_{2}^{0}:=\mathcal{HS}(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(H),H)
, let the
B\colon[0,T]\times H\times\Omega\to L_{2}^{0}
be progressively measurable. Again, by writing
B(t,v)
the map
\omega\mapsto B(t,v,\omega)
is meant. Moreover, the operator
B(t,\cdot)
is bounded uniformly at
0\in H
,
\nu_{B}
-Hölder continuous with respect to the first variable and Lipschitz continuous with respect to the second variable, i.e. there exist constants
\nu_{B}\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
,
K_{B},L_{B}\in[0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\|B(t,0)\|_{L_{2}^{0}}\leq K_{B},\quad\|B(t,v)-B(\tau,w)\|_{L_{2}^{0}}\leq L_{B}\big(|t-\tau|^{\nu_{B}}+\|v-w\|_{H}\big),

2.2.
Solution concept and additional regularity

With the exact assumptions at hand, we can state that the stochastic evolution equation (
2.1
) has a unique variational solution. Moreover, to prove our error bounds, we also need additional Hölder regularity of the solution. While the numerical scheme is well-defined in the broader framework explained above, this setting does not guarantee the needed regularity for the error bounds. We can provide the additional regularity requirements for a subclass which we explain later in this subsection.

Let us first recall what is meant by a variational solution, as stated for example in
[
29
]
. An
H
-valued adapted continuous process
\{u(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a variational solution to Equation (
2.1
) on
[0,T]
if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{V}\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty

and

(u(t),v)_{H}=(u_{0},v)_{H}+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\tilde{A}(\tau,u(\tau)),v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}(B(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau),v)_{H}

holds almost surely for all
t\in[0,T]
and
v\in V
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
5
be fulfilled. Then the stochastic evolution equation (
2.1
) has a unique (up to
\mathcal{P}
-indistinguishability) variational solution that fulfills

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\Big].

The proof is a standard result of well-posedness of variational solution of stochastic evolution equation, that even holds with weaker assumptions on
\tilde{A}
and
B
, and can be found for example in
[
29
, Theorem 4.2.4]
.

Given the next assumption, we can provide additional regularity on the solution. This regularity is needed in our error bound in Theorem
4.4
.

Assumption 6
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
5
be fulfilled. Additionally, the following stronger conditions hold:

(i)

Let
A\colon V\to V^{*}
be a linear operator. Moreover, let
H^{2}:=\{v\in V:A(v)\in H\}
be a dense subset of
H
such that it is a complete space with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{2}}
and let
A\colon H^{2}\subset H\to H
be a self-adjoint and positive definite operator. The inverse of
A
in
H
is assumed to be compact.

(ii)

The initial value
u_{0}\colon\Omega\to H^{2}
is
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable with
\mathbb{E}[\|u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}]<\infty
.

(iii)

There exists
\nu_{r}\in[0,1)
such that the mapping
B(t,\cdot)
preserves additional regularity in the sense that

\displaystyle\|A^{\frac{\nu_{r}}{2}}B(t,v)\|_{L_{2}^{0}}\leq C(1+\|A^{\frac{\nu_{r}}{2}}v\|_{H})

for every
v\in H
with
\|A^{\frac{\nu_{r}}{2}}v\|_{H}<\infty
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
6
be fulfilled. Then the solution of Equation (
2.1
) is Hölder continuous in time with values in
L^{2}(\Omega;V)
. More precisely, for
\nu_{r}\in[0,1)
from Assumption
6
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{t,\tau\in[0,T],t\neq\tau}\frac{(\mathbb{E}[\|u(t)-u(\tau)\|_{V}^{2}])^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|t-\tau|^{\min(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\nu_{r}}{2})}}<\infty.

A proof for autonomous coefficients can be found in
[
26
, Theorem 4.1]
and for non-autonomous coefficients in
[
25
, Theorem 2.31]
. Note that in the cited papers mild solutions are considered. However, in our setting, results regarding equivalence of mild and variational solutions hold, see for example
[
38
, Section 7.5]
.

3.
A semi-discrete operator splitting scheme

In the following subsections, we will explain the scheme we choose for the approximation of the solution of (
2.1
). We begin with a derivation and exact statement of the method in Section
3.1
. Here, we will use an IMEX scheme with respect to the different parts of
\tilde{A}(t,\cdot)
and decompose both
\tilde{A}(t,\cdot)
and
B(t,\cdot)
further with a domain decomposition in mind. The derivation of the scheme is followed by a justification of why the appearing implicit equations are well-posed. In Section
3.3
, we exemplify the splitting with our main application for the method and explain how a domain decomposition approach fits into the setting.

3.1.
Derivation of method

In the following, we describe the strategy of the method that we will analyze in this paper to approximate the solution of (
2.1
). The main idea behind the method can be summarized into three steps that we will explain in more detail throughout this subsection.

Step 1:

We split the data. More precisely, we decompose the space int sub-spaces and all operators into sub-operators on these sub-spaces.

Step 2:

We introduce a time discretization for every split abstract Cauchy problem. As a method, we choose an IMEX-Euler–Maruyama method.

Step 3:

We combine the numerical solutions to the sub-problems using the Lie-splitting scheme and obtain a an approximation of the original problem.

Combining these steps, we can define an operator splitting method for stochastic evolution equations (
2.1
). In the following, let us explain
Step 1
–
3
in more detail.

Step 1:

We split the equation (
2.1
) into
s\in\mathbb{N}
sub-equations. To this end, we begin by splitting the space
V
into a sequence of sub-spaces. More precisely, for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, let
(V_{\ell},\|\cdot\|_{V_{\ell}})
be real, reflexive, seperable Banach spaces, which are continuously and densely embedded into
H
, such that
\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{s}V_{\ell}=V
and
\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|\cdot\|_{V_{s}}
is equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{V}
.
On these spaces we can now define split versions of the operators
A
,
F
,
B
and the function
G
.

Assumption 7
.

Let the operators
A\colon[0,T]\times V\times\Omega\to V^{*}
,
F\colon[0,T]\times H\times\Omega\to H
,
G\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to V^{*}
and
B\colon[0,T]\times H\times\Omega\to L_{2}^{0}
be given as in Assumptions
2
,
3
and
5
, respectively.
For
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, let
A_{\ell}\colon[0,T]\times V_{\ell}\times\Omega\to V_{\ell}^{*}
also fulfill Assumption
2
, with
V
replaced by
V_{\ell}
and
F_{\ell}\colon[0,T]\times H\times\Omega\to H
,
G_{\ell}\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to V_{\ell}^{*}
and
B_{\ell}\colon[0,T]\times H\times\Omega\to L_{2}^{0}
fulfill Assumptions
3
and
5
, respectively.
For simplicity, assume that all the constants from Assumptions
2
,
3
and
5
are the same for both the unsplit and the split operators.

Moreover, the split operators fulfill the sum property

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}A_{\ell}(t,v,\omega)

\displaystyle=A(t,v,\omega)\quad\text{in }V^{*},\quad\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}F_{\ell}(t,w,\omega)=F(t,w,\omega)\quad\text{in }H,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}G_{\ell}(t,\omega)

\displaystyle=G(t,\omega)\quad\text{in }V^{*},\quad\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}B_{\ell}(t,w,\omega)=B(t,w,\omega)\quad\text{in }L_{2}^{0}

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
v\in V
,
w\in H
and almost all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Using the split data, we can define
s
separate stochastic evolution equations of the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\mathrm{d}u_{\ell}(t)=\tilde{A}_{\ell}(t,u_{\ell}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+B_{\ell}(t,u_{\ell}(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad t\in(0,T],\\
u_{\ell}(0)=u_{0},\end{cases}

with
\tilde{A}_{\ell}=F_{\ell}+G_{\ell}-A_{\ell}
.

Note that in the following, we do not split the noise
W
into
s
parts as this is not necessary for the domain decomposition application that we have in mind. Here, every split operator only acts on a subset of the spatial domain
\mathcal{D}
of the given SPDE. Thus, the split diffusion operator
B_{\ell}
is only non-zero on one part of the spatial domain and the product
B_{\ell}(t,u(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t)
therefore only acts on this part of the domain even if the noise still acts on the entire domain. We allow that the noise truncation parameter
K_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
used in
Step 2
is different on every part of the domain. This means that we may approximate the noise more or less accurately on the different domains.

Step 2:
We propose a time-discretization of Equation (
2.1
) using the IMEX-Euler–Maruyama method, i.e. the scheme is implicit with respect to
A_{\ell}

and explicit with respect to
F_{\ell}
and
B_{\ell}
.
We define the time grid
0=t_{0}<\dots<t_{N}=T
with
h_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
such that
h_{n}\leq h
for all
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, where
h\in(0,T)
is the maximal step size. To obtain an approximation of the Wiener process, we consider the truncation of the series representation of the form

\displaystyle W^{K_{\ell}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k}\beta_{k}(t)

for
t\in[0,T]
with
K_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
. Further, we denote the increments by
\Delta W^{n,K_{\ell}}=W^{K_{\ell}}(t_{n})-W^{K_{\ell}}(t_{n-1})
.
We apply the IMEX-Euler–Maruyama scheme for each
\ell
-th sub-problem as

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}U_{\ell}^{n}-U_{\ell}^{n-1}+h_{n}A_{\ell}(t_{n},U_{\ell}^{n})=h_{n}F_{\ell}(t_{n-1},U_{\ell}^{n-1})+h_{n}G_{\ell}(t_{n})\\
\hskip 142.26378pt+B_{\ell}(t_{n-1},U^{n-1})\Delta W^{n,K_{\ell}}&\text{in }V_{\ell}^{*},\\
U_{\ell}^{0}=u_{0}&\text{in }H\end{cases}

for
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

Step 3:

The last step is to find an approximation
U^{n}
of the original problem (
2.1
). In (
3.2
), we so far only approximated the split problems (
3.1
) individually but there is no interaction between the sub-problems. This interaction we will now introduce using the Lie-splitting method. The main difference compared to (
3.2
) becomes that we solve the sub-problems after one another and use the solution of one as the initial value for the next problem. This can be seen in the second line of the scheme

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}U_{\ell}^{n}-U_{\ell-1}^{n}+h_{n}A_{\ell}(t_{n},U_{\ell}^{n})=h_{n}F_{\ell}(t_{n-1},U_{\ell-1}^{n})+h_{n}G_{\ell}(t_{n})\\
\hskip 142.26378pt+B_{\ell}(t_{n-1},U^{n-1})\Delta W^{n,K_{\ell}},&\text{in }V_{\ell}^{*},\\
U^{n}=U_{s}^{n}=U_{0}^{n+1}&\text{in }H,\\
U_{0}^{1}=u_{0}&\text{in }H\end{cases}

for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
and
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
In (
3.3
), we solve
s
sub-problems after each other within one time step and find an approximation

\displaystyle U^{n}=U_{s}^{n}\approx u(t_{n})

for every
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

3.2.
Well-posedness of the implicit equation

Our numerical scheme defined in (
3.3
) is an implicit method in
U_{\ell}^{n}
. For such a method, it remains to verify that it admits a unique solution. In our case, we need to prove that there exists a unique solution
U_{\ell}^{n}\colon\Omega\to H
that is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
-measurable.

The first step is to show that for a fixed
\omega\in\Omega
, there exists a unique element
U_{\ell}^{n}(\omega)\in V_{\ell}
that solves the path of (
3.3
). For this we can apply a standard tool from deterministic PDE theory. Since
I+h_{n}A_{\ell}(t_{n},\cdot,\omega)
is bounded, Lipschitz continuous, strictly monotone and coercive, we can apply a variant of the Browder–Minty theorem from
[
33
, Theorem 2.14]
and obtain a unique solution
U_{\ell}^{n}(\omega)\in V_{\ell}
.

However, the Browder-Minty theorem does not provide any measurability results for the random variable
U_{\ell}^{n}
. Thus, we provide Lemma
3.1
below for this purpose.
Rearranging the first equation in the scheme (
3.3
), its solution
U_{\ell}^{n}(\omega)
is given by the root
U
of the equation
\gamma(U,\omega)=0
, where the function
\gamma
is defined by

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma(U,\omega)&:=U-U_{\ell-1}^{n}(\omega)+h_{n}A_{\ell}(t_{n},U,\omega)-h_{n}F_{\ell}(t_{n-1},U_{\ell-1}^{n}(\omega),\omega)\\
&\quad-h_{n}G_{\ell}(t_{n},\omega)-B_{\ell}(t_{n-1},U^{n-1}(\omega),\omega)\Delta W^{n,K_{\ell}}(\omega).\end{split}

The result in the following lemma ensures the measurability of the function that maps
\omega
onto the root of
\gamma(\cdot,\omega)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{P})
and a reflexive, real, seperable Banach space
\Xi
, let
\gamma\colon\Omega\times\Xi\to\Xi^{*}
fulfill the following conditions, where
\mathcal{N}\subset\Omega
is a set with
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{N})=0
:

(1)

The mapping
u\mapsto\langle\gamma(u,\omega),v\rangle_{\Xi^{*}\times\Xi}
from
\Xi
into
\mathbb{R}
is continuous for every
v\in\Xi
and
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{N}
.

(2)

The mapping
\omega\mapsto\gamma(u,\omega)
from
\Omega
into
\Xi
is
\mathcal{F}
-measurable for every
u\in\Xi
.

(3)

For every
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{N}
, there exists a unique element
\mathbf{U}(\omega)\in\Xi
such that
\gamma(\mathbf{U}(\omega),\omega)=0
.

Consider the mapping
\mathbf{U}\colon\Omega\to\Xi
,
\omega\mapsto\mathbf{U}(\omega)
, where
\mathbf{U}(\omega)
is the unique element in
\Xi
described in (3) for
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{N}
and
\mathbf{U}(\omega)=0
for
\omega\in\mathcal{N}
. Then
\mathbf{U}
is
\mathcal{F}
-measurable.

A proof of the previous lemma can be found in
[
12
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
5
and Assumption
7
be fulfilled. Then the scheme (
3.3
) has a unique solution
U_{\ell}^{n}
that is in
V_{\ell}
almost surely, such that
\omega\mapsto U_{\ell}^{n}(\omega)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
-measurable.

Proof.

For
n=1
, we begin to verify that for every
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
the mapping
\omega\mapsto U_{\ell}^{1}(\omega)
is measurable. This can be done inductively over
\ell
. First, by Assumption
1
, the initial value
\omega\mapsto u_{0}(\omega)=U_{0}^{1}(\omega)
is a
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable function and therefore in particular
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
-measurable.

Assuming that for a fixed
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, the function
\omega\mapsto U_{\ell-1}^{1}(\omega)\in H
exists and is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
-measurable, the idea in the following is to apply the Browder-Minty theorem to prove the existence of
U_{\ell}^{1}(\omega)
for fixed
\omega\in\Omega
. With this at hand, the measurablility of
\omega\mapsto U_{\ell}^{1}(\omega)
will be shown using Lemma
3.1
.

In order to apply the variant of the Browder–Minty theorem, we notice that for
\omega\in\Omega
, the value
U_{\ell}^{n}(\omega)
is given implicitly through the equation

\displaystyle(I+h_{1}A_{\ell}(t_{1},\cdot,\omega))U_{\ell}^{1}(\omega)=U_{\ell-1}^{1}(\omega)+h_{1}F_{\ell}(t_{0},U_{\ell-1}^{1}(\omega),\omega)+h_{1}G_{\ell}(t_{1},\omega)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+B_{\ell}(t_{0},u_{0}(\omega),\omega)\Delta W^{1,K_{\ell}}(\omega)\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }V_{1}^{*}.

Since
I+h_{1}A_{\ell}(t_{1},\cdot,\omega)
is bounded, Lipschitz continuous, strictly monotone and coercive, we can apply
[
33
, Theorem 2.14]
to ensure the existence of a unique element
U_{\ell}^{1}(\omega)\in V_{\ell}\subset H
that fulfills the equation.
We now consider the mapping
\gamma\colon V_{\ell}\times\Omega\to V_{\ell}^{*}
as defined in (
3.4
) for
n=1
. Due to the Browder–Minty argument above, we have found the unique element
U_{\ell}^{1}(\omega)\in V_{\ell}
such that
\gamma(U_{\ell}^{1}(\omega),\omega)=0
.
Since

\displaystyle\big|\langle\gamma(v_{1},\omega),w\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}-\langle\gamma(v_{2},\omega),w\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|\gamma(v_{1},\omega)-\gamma(v_{2},\omega)\|_{V_{\ell}^{*}}\|w\|_{V_{\ell}}\leq\big(\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{V_{\ell}^{*}}+h_{n}L_{A}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{V_{\ell}}\big)\|w\|_{V_{\ell}}

shows the mapping
v\mapsto\langle\gamma(v,\omega),w\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}
is Lipschitz continuous for every

and
\omega\in\Omega
, it is in particular continuous.
The mapping

\displaystyle\omega\mapsto\gamma(v,\omega)

\displaystyle=v-U_{\ell-1}^{1}(\omega)+h_{1}A(t_{1},v,\omega)-h_{1}F(t_{0},U_{\ell-1}^{1}(\omega),\omega)

\displaystyle\quad-h_{1}G(t_{1},\omega)-B(t_{0},u_{0}(\omega),\omega)\Delta W^{1,K_{\ell}}(\omega)

is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
-measurable as a composition of
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
-measurable functions. Thus, we can now apply Lemma

and find that the mapping

is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
-measurable.

Altogether this shows that the function
\omega\mapsto U_{0}^{2}(\omega)=U_{s}^{1}(\omega)\in H
exists and is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
-measurable. For the following time steps, we can argue by marching through the

and using the existence and

as well as the same argumentation as above for the
s

∎

3.3.
Application: Domain decomposition

The abstract framework from the previous section can be applied to allow for a domain decomposition setting similar to
[
5
,
13
,
15
,
21
]
for deterministic nonlinear evolution equations. In the following, we will summarize this setting for the convenience of the reader.

To provide an example for our abstract equation (
2.1
), we consider the setting where the operator
A
is of Laplace type. Then, let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, be a bounded domain with a Lipschitz boundary
\partial\mathcal{D}
. We consider a stochastic semi-linear heat equation with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\mathrm{d}u-\alpha(t)\Delta u\,\mathrm{d}t=(\tilde{F}(t,x,u)+\tilde{G}(t,x))\,\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{B}(t,x,u)\,\mathrm{d}W(t,x),\\
\hskip 227.62204ptt\in[0,T],x\in\mathcal{D},\\
u(t,x)=0,\hskip 177.82971ptt\in[0,T],x\in\partial\mathcal{D},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\hskip 157.91287ptx\in\mathcal{D},\end{cases}

where
\alpha\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
u_{0}\colon\mathcal{D}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, the notation
\tilde{F}
,
\tilde{G}
and
\tilde{B}
are used to differentiate between the functions
\tilde{F}\colon[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and the abstract function
F
on
[0,T]\times L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\times\Omega
that it gives rise to through
[F(t,u,\omega)](x)=\tilde{F}(t,x,u,\omega)
and analogously
\tilde{G}
and
\tilde{B}
.

In the following, we will use a variational formulation of (
3.5
) in order to introduce the domain decomposition. For this formulation, we use the concept of Sobolev spaces. The Sobolev space of all functions
v\in L^{p}(\mathcal{D})
such that every weak partial derivative
\partial_{i}v
,
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, exists and is an element of
L^{p}(\mathcal{D})
is denoted by
W^{1,p}(\mathcal{D})
.
Additionally, for
p\in[1,\infty]
we consider the space
W_{0}^{1,p}(\mathcal{D})
,
which is the closure of the infinitely many times differentiable functions with a compact support
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
with respect to the norm of
W^{1,p}(\mathcal{D})
.
For an introduction and more details, we refer the reader to
[
2
]
.

In order to split the domain, we decompose
\mathcal{D}
into
s\in\mathbb{N}
overlapping sub-domains
\{\mathcal{D}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
. We assume that each of these subsets
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}
has a Lipschitz boundary and that their union is
\mathcal{D}
, i.e.
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathcal{D}_{\ell}=\mathcal{D}
.
Further, let the partition of unity
\{\chi_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}\subset W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{D})
be given such that for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}

\displaystyle\chi_{\ell}(x)>0\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\quad\chi_{\ell}(x)=0\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\chi_{\ell}=1.

Together with the weight functions
\{\chi_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}}
, we can now define suitable function spaces and operators. First, let the pivot space
\left(H,(\cdot,\cdot)_{H},\|\cdot\|_{H}\right)
be the space
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
of square integrable functions on
\mathcal{D}
with the usual norm and inner product. The space
V
is given by

\displaystyle V=\text{clos}_{\|\cdot\|_{V}}\big(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})\big)=W_{0}^{1,2}(\mathcal{D}),\quad\text{with}\quad\|\cdot\|_{V}^{2}=\|\cdot\|_{H}^{2}+\|\nabla\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})^{d}}^{2},

where
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})^{d}
is the space of all measurable functions
v\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the components are in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.

In order to state the spaces
V_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, we use the weighted Lebesgue space
L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\chi_{\ell})^{d}
that consists of all measurable functions
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\|(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\chi_{\ell})^{d}}=\Big(\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\ell}}\chi_{\ell}|(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

is finite. Then
V_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, is given by

\displaystyle V_{\ell}=\text{clos}_{\|\cdot\|_{V_{\ell}}}\big(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})\big),\quad\text{with}\quad\|\cdot\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}=\|\cdot\|_{H}^{2}+\|\nabla\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\chi_{\ell})^{d}}^{2}.

The spaces
V_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, are densely embedded into
H
and their intersection
\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{s}V_{\ell}
is the original space
V
.
The operators
A(t,\cdot,\omega)\colon V\to V^{*}
,
F(t,\cdot,\omega)\colon H\to H
,
G(t,\omega)\in V^{*}
,
B(t,\cdot,\omega)\colon H\to H
, and the split operators
A_{\ell}(t,\cdot,\omega)\colon V_{\ell}\to V^{*}_{\ell}
,
F_{\ell}(t,\cdot,\omega)\colon H\to H
,
G_{\ell}(t,\omega)\in V_{\ell}^{*}
,
B_{\ell}(t,\cdot,\omega)\colon H\to H
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
,
t\in[0,T]
, are given by

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle A(t,v,\omega),w\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}&=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\alpha(t,\omega)\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}x,\\
\langle A_{\ell}(t,v_{\ell},\omega),w_{\ell}\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}&=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\ell}}\chi_{\ell}\alpha(t,\omega)\nabla v_{\ell}\cdot\nabla w_{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\langle G(t,\omega),v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\tilde{G}(t,\cdot,\omega)

\displaystyle v\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\langle G_{\ell}(t,\omega),v_{\ell}\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\ell}}\chi_{\ell}\tilde{G}(t,\cdot,\omega)v_{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}x

for
v,w\in V
,
v_{\ell},w_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}
and
\omega\in\Omega
as well as

(3.8)

\displaystyle(F(t,v,\omega),w)_{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\tilde{F}(t,\cdot,v,\omega)w\,\mathrm{d}x,\ (F_{\ell}(t,v,\omega),w)_{H}=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\ell}}\chi_{\ell}\tilde{F}(t,\cdot,v,\omega)w\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.9)

\displaystyle(B(t,v,\omega),w)_{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\tilde{B}(t,\cdot,v,\omega)v\,\mathrm{d}x,\ (B_{\ell}(t,v,\omega),w)_{H}=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\ell}}\chi_{\ell}\tilde{B}(t,\cdot,v,\omega)w\,\mathrm{d}x

for
v,w\in H
and
\omega\in\Omega
. In order to fulfill the rest of the assumptions of the previous section, we further assume that
\alpha\in C^{\nu_{A}}([0,T];L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}))
,
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D};L^{2}(\Omega))
,
\tilde{F}(\cdot,\cdot,v,\cdot)\in C^{\nu_{F}}([0,T];L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D}\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}))
,
\tilde{G}\in C^{\nu_{G}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;(W_{0}^{1,2}(\mathcal{D}))^{*}))
as well as
\tilde{B}(\cdot,\cdot,v,\cdot)\in C^{\nu_{B}}([0,T];L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D}\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}))
for
v\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\tilde{F}
and
\tilde{B}
are Lipschitz continuous in the third argument.
A proof that the setting stated in this section fulfills the Assumptions
1
–
5
and
7
can be carried through in an analogous manner to
[
15
, Lemma 3.1]
but it remains to show that the operator
B(t,v)
is an element of
L_{2}^{0}
almost surely. To this extend, we choose the orthonormal basis
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
H
from Assumption
4
. We can then write

\displaystyle\|B(t,v)\|_{L_{2}^{0}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|B(t,v)(q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k})\|_{H}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\int_{\mathcal{D}}|\tilde{B}(t,\cdot,v)\psi_{k}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

which is bounded if
\tilde{B}(t,\cdot,v)\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
for every
v\in H
. Alternatively, if the orthonormal basis
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is even bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
, it is instead sufficient if
\tilde{B}(t,\cdot,v)\in H
for
v\in H
since

\displaystyle\|B(t,v)\|_{L_{2}^{0}}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\|\psi_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{D}}|\tilde{B}(t,\cdot,v)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\|\tilde{B}(t,\cdot,v)\|_{H}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\|\psi_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}

is then bounded.

In order to obtain the additional regularity from Theorem
2.2
, we need the setting to fit into Assumption
6
. To this end, we assume additionally that
\alpha\equiv 1
,
H^{2}=H^{2}(\mathcal{D})\cap H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{D})
,
u_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathcal{D};L^{2}(\Omega))\cap H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{D};L^{2}(\Omega))
and the operator
B(t,\cdot)
preserves the regularity of an element
v
as stated in Assumption
6

(iii)
. This last condition is fulfilled for additive noise
B(t,v)=I_{H}
(where
I_{H}
is the identity operator in
H
) if the basis
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
fulfills that they are eigenfunctions of
A
to the corresponding eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{q_{k}\lambda_{k}^{\nu}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\|A^{\frac{\nu}{2}}B(t,v)\|_{L_{2}^{0}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|A^{\frac{\nu}{2}}I(q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k})\|_{H}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\int_{\mathcal{D}}|A^{\frac{\nu}{2}}\psi_{k}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\lambda_{k}^{\nu}

is finite. This condition is more difficult to prove for multiplicative noise.

4.
Error analysis

In this section, we state a formal analysis for our method (
3.3
). This analysis contains several steps. We begin to show that the solution to our time-discrtization has bounded moments. This we will state in Lemma
4.1
. Moreover, we will present a result for the regularity of an auxiliary function that we will need for the following analysis. We conclude this section with our main result Theorem
4.4
, where we provide an explicit error bound of the scheme.

4.1.
Boundedness of the numerical solution

As stated in (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) the variational solution to the problem (
2.1
) fulfills certain moment bounds. More precisely, there exists
C_{\textup{bound, cont.}}\in(0,\infty)
such that the exact solution
u
fulfills

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t)\|_{V}^{2}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{\textup{bound, cont.}}.

A first step is to replicate this bound for the numerical solution. In the following lemma, we provide an a priori bound for our numerical approximation. In particular, this lemma ensures that the numerical solution fulfills the analogous moment bounds as the exact solution.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
5
and Assumption
7
be fulfilled and
let
U_{\ell}^{n}
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
,
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, be the unique solution of the scheme (
3.3
). Then there exists a constant

C_{\textup{bound}}\in(0,\infty)
such that for every maximal step size
h\in(0,T)
the following a priori bound holds

\displaystyle\max_{n\in\{1,\dots,N\}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]\leq C_{\textup{bound}}.

The proof follows with standard arguments used for a priori bounds for evolution equation as for example in
[
33
, Lemma 8.6]
. Moreover, the structure is similar to the proof of our main result in Theorem
4.4
. For the sake completeness, the proof of the Lemma
4.1
can be found in Appendix
B
.

4.2.
Regularity of the domain decomposition

From our numerical scheme, we obtain an approximation
U^{n}
of
u(t_{n})
after every time step. Every time step consists of
s
sub-steps that take the different operators
\tilde{A}_{\ell}
and
B_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, into account. This means that for a sub-solution
U_{\ell}^{n}
we consider the operators
\tilde{A}_{j}
and
B_{j}
for
j
between
1
and
\ell
.
In the following we introduce functions
u_{\ell}
to compare these sub-solutions to. These functions are defined as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{\ell}(t)&=u(t_{n-1})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big)\\
&=u_{\ell-1}(t_{n})+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t}\tilde{A}_{\ell}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t}B_{\ell}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\end{split}

for
t\in(t_{n-1},t_{n}]
,
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and with
\tilde{A}_{\ell}=F_{\ell}+G_{\ell}-A_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
.
In order to prove that these auxiliary functions do not differ too much from the exact solution
u
, we additionally need the following assumption.

Assumption 8
.

Let Assumptions
2
–
5
and
7
be fulfilled. There exists
C_{\textup{split}}\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
t,\tau\in[0,T]
it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{s}\Big(\int_{\tau}^{t}\tilde{A}_{j}(\sigma,v(\sigma))\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{\tau}^{t}B_{j}(\sigma,v(\sigma))\,\mathrm{d}W(\sigma)\Big)\Big\|_{V}\\
&\geq C_{\textup{split}}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\Big(\int_{\tau}^{t}\tilde{A}_{j}(\sigma,v(\sigma))\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{\tau}^{t}B_{j}(\sigma,v(\sigma))\,\mathrm{d}W(\sigma)\Big)\Big\|_{V_{\ell}}\\
&\quad+C_{\textup{split}}\Big\|\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{s}\Big(\int_{\tau}^{t}\tilde{A}_{j}(\sigma,v(\sigma))\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{\tau}^{t}B_{j}(\sigma,v(\sigma))\,\mathrm{d}W(\sigma)\Big)\Big\|_{V_{\ell}}\end{split}

almost surely for every
v\in L^{2}(0,T;V)
and
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
.

Note that in the case of the orthogonality of the integral operators of Lie splitting operators
\tilde{A}_{\ell}
and
B_{\ell}
, the previous inequality becomes equality and
C_{\textup{split}}=1
. Similar types of orthogonality conditions are common in multi-grid methods.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let Assumptions
2
–
5
,
7
and
8
be fulfilled. Then it follows that

\displaystyle C_{\textup{split}}\|u(t_{n})-u_{\ell}(t_{n})\|_{V_{\ell}}+C_{\textup{split}}\|u_{\ell}(t_{n})-u(t_{n-1})\|_{V_{\ell}}\leq\|u(t_{i})-u(t_{i-1})\|_{V}.

Proof.

The difference of the function
u
at two grid points
t_{n}
and
t_{n-1}
can be split up into two parts

\displaystyle u(t_{n})-u(t_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{s}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big).

Using this decomposition and the definition of the function
u_{\ell}(t_{n})
, we find that

\displaystyle u_{\ell}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=u(t_{n})-\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big)

\displaystyle=u(t_{n-1})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big).

This then enables us to find a representation of
u(t_{n})-u_{\ell}(t_{n})
and
u_{\ell}(t_{n})-u(t_{n-1})
given by

\displaystyle u(t_{n})-u_{\ell}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big)

and

\displaystyle u_{\ell}(t_{n})-u(t_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big).

Applying Assumption
8
, it then follows that

\displaystyle C_{\textup{split}}\|u(t_{n})-u_{\ell}(t_{n})\|_{V_{\ell}}+C_{\textup{split}}\|u_{\ell}(t_{n})-u(t_{n-1})\|_{V_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=C_{\textup{split}}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big)\Big\|_{V_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\textup{split}}\Big\|\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big)\Big\|_{V_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\tilde{A}_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}B_{j}(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}W(\tau)\Big)\Big\|_{V}

\displaystyle=\|u(t_{n})-u(t_{n-1})\|_{V}.

∎

4.3.
Error bound

In this section, we provide a result on the mean-square convergence of the scheme (
3.3
) which is a combination of an IMEX-Euler–Maruyama method and a Lie splitting scheme for the stochastic evolution equation (
2.1
).

In the following section, we define the full error
e^{n}=u(t_{n})-U^{n}
as well as as the sub-step error
e_{\ell}^{n}=u_{\ell}(t_{n})-U_{\ell}^{n}
for
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
. We then prove that
\mathbb{E}[\|e^{n}\|_{H}^{2}]
tends to zero as the maximal time step size
h\to 0
. In order to prove this error bound, we summarize the necessary assumption on the setting:

(H1)

Let Assumptions
1
–
5
be fulfilled which ensures that the stochastic evolution equation (
2.1
) has a unique variational solution
u
.

(H2)

For
\nu_{u}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, let the exact solution
u
of the stochastic evolution equation (
2.1
) be an element of
C^{\nu_{u}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;V))
. The Hölder exponent
\nu_{u}
determines the order of the error.

(H3)

Let Assumption
7
be fulfilled to state the decomposed problem. Further, let Assumption
8
be fulfilled to ensure that the auxiliary function
u_{\ell}
defined in (
4.1
) stays sufficiently close to the solution
u
of (
2.1
).

(H4)

Assume that for every
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
there exists
\varepsilon_{\ell}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\sum_{k>K_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|B_{\ell}(t,v)(q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k})\|_{H}^{2}\big]\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}\big(1+\mathbb{E}\big[\|v\|_{H}^{2}\big]\big)
for every
v\in L^{2}(\Omega;H)
. This ensures that the truncation error
\varepsilon=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{\ell}
is sufficiently small.

Remark 4.3
.

The assumption
\sum_{k>K_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|B_{\ell}(t,v)(q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k})\|_{H}^{2}\big]\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}\big(1+\mathbb{E}\big[\|v\|_{H}^{2}\big]\big)
on the truncation is fulfilled, if the eigenvalues
\{q_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
Q
are summable and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},v)\big(\psi_{k}\big)\big\|_{H}^{2}\big]\leq C_{\textup{truncation}}\big(1+\mathbb{E}\big[\|v\|_{H}^{2}\big]\big)

is fulfilled. The value
\varepsilon_{\ell}
is then given by
\varepsilon_{\ell}=C_{\textup{truncation}}\sum_{k>K_{\ell}}q_{k}
.
An example for this setting is if
B_{\ell}(t,v)\in\mathcal{L}(H)
,
t\in[0,T]
. But also weaker assumptions can be made if the eigenfunctions
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are more regular.

Theorem 4.4

(Bound for the error)
.

Let
U^{n}
and
U_{\ell}^{n}
for
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
be given by the split IMEX-Euler–Maruyama method (
3.3
).
Under the assumptions stated in (H1)–(H4), there exists a constant
C_{\textup{bound}}\in(0,\infty)
such that the errors
e^{n}=u(t_{n})-U^{n}
and
e_{\ell}^{n}=u_{\ell}(t_{n})-U_{\ell}^{n}
of the scheme (
3.3
) can be bounded by

\displaystyle\max_{n\in\{1,\dots,N\}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\textup{bound}}\big(h^{2\nu}+\varepsilon\big),

where
\nu=\min\{\nu_{A},\nu_{F},\nu_{B},\nu_{G},\nu_{u}\}
.

Proof.

The proof is based on techniques from PDE analysis in a variational framework. In the following, we will state an equation for the error, which we will test with the error itself. The remaining terms can either be compensated by, for example, the monotonicty of
A_{\ell}(t_{i},\cdot)
or are small due to the regularity assumptions.

We begin to subtract the numerical approximation
U_{\ell}^{i}
from
u_{\ell}(t_{i})
. Applying the abbreviation
e_{\ell}^{i}=u_{\ell}(t_{i})-U_{\ell}^{i}
, we then obtain

\displaystyle e_{\ell}^{i}

\displaystyle=e_{\ell-1}^{i}+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\big(F_{\ell}(t,u(t))-F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\big(G_{\ell}(t)-G_{\ell}(t_{i-1})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\big(A_{\ell}(t,u(t))-A_{\ell}(t_{i},U_{\ell}^{i})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\big(B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)

\displaystyle\quad+B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(\Delta W^{i}-\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}}\big).

Next, we test the equation with
e_{\ell}^{i}
and use the identity
(a-b,a)_{H}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\|a\|_{H}^{2}-\|b\|_{H}^{2}+\|a-b\|_{H}^{2}\big)
for
a,b\in H
. After summing up the equation from
\ell=1
to
s
and using the telescopic sum argument, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(\|e^{i}\|_{H}^{2}-\|e^{i-1}\|_{H}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(F_{\ell}(t,u(t))-F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i}),e_{\ell}^{i}\big)_{H}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\langle G_{\ell}(t)-G_{\ell}(t_{i-1}),e_{\ell}^{i}\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\langle A_{\ell}(t,u(t))-A_{\ell}(t_{i},U_{\ell}^{i}),e_{\ell}^{i}\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),e_{\ell}^{i}\Big)_{H}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(\Delta W^{i}-\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}}\big),e_{\ell}^{i}\big)_{H}=\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}+\Gamma_{3}+\Gamma_{4}+\Gamma_{5}.

The main idea will be to split the terms such that they can be compensated by
\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}
, the monotoicity condition on
A_{\ell}(t_{i},\cdot)
or Grönwall’s inequality. The remaining terms will be bounded using the regularity assumptions stated on the solution and Assumption
8
.
First note that by assumption the exact solution
u
is in
C^{\nu}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;V))
. This implies, in particular, that it lies in
C^{\nu}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))
and
C^{\nu}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;V_{\ell}))
for every
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
. Moreover, by Lemma
4.2
and the fact that
V_{\ell}
is continuously embedded into
H
, the distance of
u(t)
and
u_{\ell}(t)
is also bounded in the
V_{\ell}
and
H
-norm. For simplicity of notation, we will use in the following that there exits a constant
C_{\textup{reg}}\in(0,\infty)
such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t)-u(\tau)\|_{H}^{2}\big]\leq C_{\textup{reg}}|t-\tau|^{2\nu},\quad\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t)-u(\tau)\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]\leq C_{\textup{reg}}|t-\tau|^{2\nu},\\
\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t)-u_{\ell}(\tau)\|_{H}^{2}\big]\leq C_{\textup{reg}}|t-\tau|^{2\nu},\quad\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t)-u_{\ell}(\tau)\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]\leq C_{\textup{reg}}|t-\tau|^{2\nu}\end{split}

for all
t,\tau\in[0,T]
.
We now bound each term
\Gamma_{j}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,5\}
, separately.
For
\Gamma_{1}
, we begin by adding and subtracting
e_{\ell-1}^{i}
to the right side of the inner product. We the find that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(F_{\ell}(t,u(t))-F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i}),e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\big)_{H}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(F_{\ell}(t,u(t))-F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i}),e_{\ell-1}^{i}\big)_{H}\,\mathrm{d}t=\Gamma_{1,1}+\Gamma_{1,2}.

We will now bound
\Gamma_{1,1}
by applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Lemma
A.1
with
\xi=h_{i}9
on the following three summands and inserting Assumption
3
. We then obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(F_{\ell}(t,u(t))-F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},u(t_{i-1})),e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\big)_{H}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},u(t_{i-1}))-F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},u_{\ell-1}(t_{i})),e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\big)_{H}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(F_{\ell}(t,u_{\ell-1}(t_{i}))-F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i}),e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\big)_{H}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}9L_{F}^{2}\Big(s\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\big(|t-t_{i-1}|^{2\nu}+\|u(t)-u(t_{i-1})\|_{H}^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|u(t_{i-1})-u_{\ell-1}(t_{i})\|_{H}^{2}+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\Big)+\frac{1}{12}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}.

Similarly for the next term
\Gamma_{1,2}
, we apply Lemma
A.1
with
\xi=\frac{3}{4}
and obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1,2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{4}L_{F}^{2}\Big(s\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\big(|t-t_{i-1}|^{2\nu}+\|u(t)-u(t_{i-1})\|_{H}^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|u(t_{i-1})-u_{\ell-1}(t_{i})\|_{H}^{2}\Big)+h_{i}\Big(\frac{3}{4}L_{F}^{2}+1\Big)\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}.

Due to the regularity condition from (
4.2
), it then follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{1}\big]

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}^{1+2\nu}\Big(h\frac{9(1+C_{\textup{reg}})L_{F}^{2}s}{1+2\nu}+h9C_{\textup{reg}}L_{F}^{2}s+\frac{3(1+C_{\textup{reg}})L_{F}^{2}s}{4+8\nu}+\frac{3}{4}C_{\textup{reg}}L_{F}^{2}s\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{12}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+h_{i}\Big(h_{i}9L_{F}^{2}+\frac{3}{4}L_{F}^{2}+1\Big)\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=:h_{i}^{1+2\nu}C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+\frac{1}{12}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+h_{i}C_{\Gamma_{1},2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big].

Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the dual pairing and Lemma
A.1
with
\xi=\frac{3}{2\eta}
, where
\eta
comes from the monotonicity condition on
A_{\ell}(t_{i},\cdot)
, we find for
\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{2}\big]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|G_{\ell}(t)-G_{\ell}(t_{i})\|_{V_{\ell}^{*}}\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2\eta}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|G_{\ell}(t)-G_{\ell}(t_{i})\|_{V_{\ell}^{*}}^{2}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t+h_{i}\frac{\eta}{6}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}^{1+2\nu}C_{\Gamma_{2}}+h_{i}\frac{\eta}{6}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big],

since
G_{\ell}\in C^{\nu}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;V_{\ell}^{*}))
.
For the third term
\Gamma_{3}
, we extend it by adding and subtracting both
A_{\ell}(t_{i},u(t_{i}))
and
A_{\ell}(t_{i},u_{\ell}(t_{i}))
to the left side of the dual pairing. The resulting terms can be bounded using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the monotonicity condition and the Lipschitz condition from Assumption
2
, as well as Lemma
A.1
with
\xi=\frac{3}{2\eta}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{3}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|A_{\ell}(t,u(t))-A_{\ell}(t_{i},u(t_{i}))\|_{V_{\ell}^{*}}\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|A_{\ell}(t_{i},u(t_{i}))-A_{\ell}(t_{i},u_{\ell}(t_{i}))\|_{V_{\ell}^{*}}\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}\big]-h_{i}\eta\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3L_{A}^{2}}{2\eta}\Big(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(|t-t_{i}|^{2\nu}+\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t)-u(t_{i})\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t_{i})-u_{\ell}(t_{i})\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]\Big)-h_{i}\frac{2\eta}{3}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}^{1+2\nu}\frac{3L_{A}^{2}s}{2\eta}\Big(\frac{1+C_{\textup{reg}}}{1+2\nu}+C_{\textup{reg}}\Big)-h_{i}\frac{2\eta}{3}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=:h_{i}^{1+2\nu}C_{\Gamma_{3}}-h_{i}\frac{2\eta}{3}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big],

where we again used the regularity condition from (
4.2
).
For the term
\Gamma_{4}
, we add and subtract
e^{i-1}
to the right side of the inner product. Using Lemma
A.1
with
\xi=3s^{2}
, we find that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{4}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}(B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1}))\,\mathrm{d}W(t),e_{\ell}^{i}-e^{i-1}\Big)_{H}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),e^{i-1}\Big)_{H}

\displaystyle\leq 3s^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big\|\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\big(B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)\Big\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{12s^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e^{i-1}\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),e^{i-1}\Big)_{H}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{4,1}+\Gamma_{4,2}+\Gamma_{4,3}.

In expectation, the term
\Gamma_{4,1}
can be bounded by first using the Itô-isometry and then Assumption
5
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{4,1}\big]

\displaystyle=3s^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big\|_{L_{2}^{0}}^{2}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq 6s^{2}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},u(t_{i-1}))\big\|_{L_{2}^{0}}^{2}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},u(t_{i-1})-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big\|_{L_{2}^{0}}^{2}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 6L_{B}^{2}s^{2}\Big(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\big(|t-t_{i-1}|^{2\nu}+\mathbb{E}\big[\|u(t)-u(t_{i-1})\|_{H}^{2}\big]\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+h_{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|e^{i-1}\big\|_{H}^{2}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 6L_{B}^{2}s^{2}\Big(h_{i}^{1+2\nu}\frac{1+C_{\textup{reg}}}{1+2\nu}+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle=:h_{i}^{1+2\nu}C_{\Gamma_{4},1}+h_{i}C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}\big],

where we used the regularity condition from (
4.2
) and the fact that
\|e^{i-1}\|_{H}^{2}=\|e_{0}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}
. For the second term
\Gamma_{4,2}
, we apply a telescopic sum argument and Hölder’s inequality for sums. Then we find that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{4,2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12s^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\big(e_{j}^{i}-e_{j-1}^{i}\big)\Big\|_{H}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{12s^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\ell\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\big\|e_{j}^{i}-e_{j-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{12s^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}s\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big\|e_{j}^{i}-e_{j-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big\|e_{j}^{i}-e_{j-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}.

The last term
\Gamma_{4,3}
disappears in expectation. This can be seen by the stochastic independence and properties of the Itô integral,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{4,3}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),e^{i-1}\Big)_{H}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}B_{\ell}(t,u(t))-B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\,\mathrm{d}W(t)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}\Big],e^{i-1}\Big)_{H}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}\Big]\Big]=0.

It remains to bound
\Gamma_{5}
. We begin to add and subtract
e^{i-1}
in the right side of the inner product, insert the expansion for the
Q
-Wiener process and apply Lemma
A.1
for
\xi=3s^{2}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{5}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(\Delta W^{i}-\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}}\big),e_{\ell}^{i}-e^{i-1}\big)_{H}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(\Delta W^{i}-\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}}\big),e^{i-1}\big)_{H}

\displaystyle\leq 3s^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big\|B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(\Delta W^{i}-\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}}\big)\big\|_{H}^{2}+\frac{1}{12s^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e^{i-1}\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(\Delta W^{i}-\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}}\big),e^{i-1}\big)_{H}=\Gamma_{5,1}+\Gamma_{5,2}+\Gamma_{5,3}.

For the first term
\Gamma_{5,1}
, we note that we can exchange
\Delta W^{i}-\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}}
by the sum
\sum_{k>K_{\ell}}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}(t)
of scalar Wiener processes and recal that
\{q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(H)
. Further, we recall that all these summands are stochastically independent, apply Itô’s isometry, the bound on the truncation error and the a prioiri from Lemma
4.1
to find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{5,1}\big]

\displaystyle=3s^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\sum_{k>K_{\ell}}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}(t)\Big\|_{H}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=3s^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\sum_{k>K_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}(t)\Big\|_{H}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=h_{i}3s^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\sum_{k>K_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\big(q_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{k}\big)\big\|_{H}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}3s^{2}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{\ell}\Big)\big(1+\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{i-1}\|_{H}^{2}\big]\big)\leq h_{i}3s^{2}(1+C_{\textup{bound}})\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{\ell}=:h_{i}\varepsilon C_{\Gamma_{5}}.

Analogously to
\Gamma_{4,2}
and
\Gamma_{4,3}
, it follows that
\Gamma_{5,2}\leq\frac{1}{12}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{5,3}\big]=0
. We can now combine the previous bounds to the following,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|e^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\|e^{i-1}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}^{1+2\nu}\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+C_{\Gamma_{2}}+C_{\Gamma_{3}}+C_{\Gamma_{4},1}\big)+h_{i}\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},2}+C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\big)\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\quad-h_{i}\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]+h_{i}\varepsilon C_{\Gamma_{5}}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|e_{j}^{i}-e_{j-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}\big].

After multiplying by
2
, rearranging some terms and summing up from
i=1
to
n
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|e^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\|e^{0}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\eta\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq h^{2\nu}2T\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+C_{\Gamma_{2}}+C_{\Gamma_{3}}+C_{\Gamma_{4},1}\big)+2\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},2}+C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle+\varepsilon 2TC_{\Gamma_{5}}.

In the last step, we use that the initial error
\mathbb{E}\big[\|e^{0}\|_{H}^{2}\big]
is zero and it then remains to apply Grönwall’s inequality for sums. We abbreviate the terms as follows

\displaystyle u_{n,\ell}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{j}^{i}-e_{j-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\eta\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{j}^{i}\|_{V_{j}}^{2}\big],

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=h^{2\nu}2T\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+C_{\Gamma_{2}}+C_{\Gamma_{3}}+C_{\Gamma_{4},1}\big)+\varepsilon 2TC_{\Gamma_{5}},\quad b_{i,\ell}=h_{i}2\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},2}+C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\big).

Recall that
\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{s}^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\|e^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]
.
Applying Lemma
A.2
then shows that
u_{n,s}\leq a\exp\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}b_{i,\ell}\big)
or equivalently

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|e^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}-e_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\eta\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|e_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big(2sT\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},2}+C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\big)\big)\big(h^{2\nu}2T\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+C_{\Gamma_{2}}+C_{\Gamma_{3}}+C_{\Gamma_{4},1}\big)+\varepsilon 2TC_{\Gamma_{5}}\big),

which is our desired bound.
∎

Appendix A
Basic inequalities

Lemma A.1
.

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
\xi>0
it follows that
|xy|\leq\xi|x|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\xi}|y|^{2}
.

A proof can be found
[
16
, B.2. Elementary inequalities]
.
Further, we need a form of Grönwall’s inequality in this paper. A proof for the standard statement can be found in
[
9
]
. We use a variant that is based on a rearrangement of the terms, where the summands are subdivided into two sums.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
(u_{n,\ell})_{n\in\mathbb{N},\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}}
and
(b_{n,\ell})_{n\in\mathbb{N},\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}}
be two nonnegative sequences
that satisfy, for given
a\in[0,\infty)
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, that

\displaystyle u_{n,s}\leq a+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}b_{i,\ell}u_{i,\ell},\quad n\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

Then it follows that

\displaystyle u_{n,s}\leq a\exp\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}b_{i,\ell}\Big),\quad n\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

Appendix B
Proof of moment bound

For the sake of completeness, we state the proof of Lemma
4.1
in the following.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

The idea of this proof follows the idea of an a priori bound that is commonly used for the Rothe methode for deterministic (nonlinear) evolution equations. A common step for variational solutions, we start by testing the equation given by the scheme with the solution and use the coercivity and Lipschitz continuity of the operators. A similar proof structure can be found in
[
33
, Lemma 8.6]
. First, we consider a sub-step of the numerical scheme

\displaystyle U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}=h_{i}G_{\ell}(t_{i})+h_{i}F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i})-h_{i}A_{\ell}(t_{i},U_{\ell}^{i})+B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}},

which we test with the solution
U_{\ell}^{i}
. Then we use the identity
(a-b,a)_{H}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\|a\|_{H}^{2}-\|b\|_{H}^{2}+\|a-b\|_{H}^{2}\big)
for
a,b\in H
and sum up the equation from
\ell=1
to
s
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\big(\|U_{\ell}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}-\|U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}+\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i},U_{\ell}^{i})_{H}

\displaystyle=h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i}),U_{\ell}^{i})_{H}+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\langle G_{\ell}(t_{i}),U_{\ell}^{i}\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\quad-h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\langle A_{\ell}(t_{i},U_{\ell}^{i}),U_{\ell}^{i}\rangle_{V_{\ell}^{*}\times V_{\ell}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(B_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}},U_{\ell}^{i})_{H}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}+\Gamma_{3}+\Gamma_{4}.

In the following, we bound the single terms separately. By adding and subtracting the term
U_{\ell-1}^{i}
in the right side of the inner product and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the first term
\Gamma_{1}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i})\|_{H}\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i})\|_{H}\|U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}.

Further applying Lemma
A.1
with
\xi=2h
and
\xi=\frac{1}{4}
respectively, as well as the Lipschitz continuity of
F_{\ell}
and boundedness of
F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},0)

-norm, we can bound
\Gamma_{1}
by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big(2h\|F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i})\|_{H}^{2}+\frac{1}{8h}\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\Big(\frac{1}{4}\|F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i})\|_{H}^{2}+\|U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}\Big(4h+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},U_{\ell-1}^{i})-F_{\ell}(t_{i-1},0)\|_{H}^{2}+h_{i}\Big(4h+\frac{1}{2}\Big)sK_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}+h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}\Big(4hL_{F}^{2}+\frac{L_{F}^{2}}{2}+1\Big)\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}+h_{i}\Big(4h+\frac{1}{2}\Big)sK_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2},

where we abbreviate
C_{\Gamma_{1},1}:=4hL_{F}^{2}+\frac{L_{F}^{2}}{2}+1
and
C_{\Gamma_{1},2}:=(4h+\frac{1}{2})sK_{F}^{2}
.
Furthermore, for the second summand
\Gamma_{2}

A.1

\Gamma_{4,2}
. This can be done by inserting a telescopic sum, which we then bound by an application of Hölder’s inequality for sums. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{4,2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8s^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\big(U_{j}^{i}-U_{j-1}^{i}\big)\Big\|_{H}^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{8s^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\Big[\ell\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\big\|U_{j}^{i}-U_{j-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{8s^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\Big[s\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big\|U_{j}^{i}-U_{j-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}\Big]=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|U_{j}^{i}-U_{j-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}\big].

Due to the stochastic independence of
B(t_{i-1},U^{i-1})
and
\Delta W^{i,K_{\ell}}
, we observe that the last term
\Gamma_{4,3}
disappears in expectation. Thus, combining all the previous bounds, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{i-1}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+C_{\Gamma_{4},1}\big)\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}+h_{i}\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},2}+C_{\Gamma_{2}}+C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\big)

\displaystyle-h_{i}\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\|U_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|U_{j}^{i}-U_{j-1}^{i}\big\|_{H}^{2}\big].

Rearranging the terms and multiplying the result by
2
, shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{i-1}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+h_{i}\mu\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq h_{i}2\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+C_{\Gamma_{4},1}\big)\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+h_{i}2\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},2}+C_{\Gamma_{2}}+C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\big).

We sum up the inequality from
i=1
to
n
and use a telescoping sum argument to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{0}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+C_{\Gamma_{4},1}\big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+2T\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},2}+C_{\Gamma_{2}}+C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\big).

For the next step, we will apply Grönwall’s inequality analogously to the proof of Theorem
4.4
. Then we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{n}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}-U_{\ell-1}^{i}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}\big[\|U_{\ell}^{i}\|_{V_{\ell}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big(2sT\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},1}+C_{\Gamma_{4},1}\big)\big)\big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|U^{0}\|_{H}^{2}\big]+2T\big(C_{\Gamma_{1},2}+C_{\Gamma_{2}}+C_{\Gamma_{4},2}\big)\big),

which is finite.
∎
