Algebraic approach to the completeness problem for ( k , n ) (k,n) -arcs in planes over finite fields
Gábor Korchmáros, Gábor P. Nagy, Tamás Szőnyi

Abstract
. In a projective plane over a finite field, complete ( k , n ) (k,n) -arcs with few characters are rare but interesting objects with several applications to finite geometry and coding theory. Since almost all known examples are large, the construction of small ones, with k k close to the order of the plane, is considered a hard problem. A natural candidate to be a small ( k , n ) (k,n) -arc with few characters is the set Ω ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \Omega(\mathcal{C}) of the points of a plane curve 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of degree n n (containing no linear components) such that some line meets 𝒞 \mathcal{C} transversally in the plane, i.e. in n n pairwise distinct points. Let 𝒞 \mathcal{C} be either the Hermitian curve of degree q + 1 q+1 in PG ⁡ ( 2 , q 2 ​ r ) {\rm{PG}}(2,q^{2r}) with r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 , or the rational BKS curve of degree q + 1 q+1 in PG ⁡ ( 2 , q r ) {\rm{PG}}(2,q^{r}) with q q odd and r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 . Then Ω ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \Omega(\mathcal{C}) has four and seven characters, respectively. Furthermore, Ω ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \Omega(\mathcal{C}) is small as both curves are either maximal or minimal. The completeness problem is investigated by an algebraic approach based on Galois theory and on the Hasse-Weil lower bound. Our main result for the Hermitian case is that Ω ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \Omega(\mathcal{C}) is complete for r ≥ 4 r\geq 4 . For the rational BKS curve, Ω ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \Omega(\mathcal{C}) is complete if and only if r r is even. If r r is odd then the uncovered points by the ( q + 1 ) (q+1) -secants to Ω ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \Omega(\mathcal{C}) are exactly the points in PG ⁡ ( 2 , q ) {\rm{PG}}(2,q) not lying in Ω ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \Omega(\mathcal{C}) . Adding those points to Ω ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \Omega(\mathcal{C}) produces a complete ( k , q + 1 ) (k,q+1) -arc in PG ⁡ ( 2 , q r ) {\rm{PG}}(2,q^{r}) , with k = q r + q k=q^{r}+q . The above results do not hold true for r = 2 r=2 and there remain open the case r = 3 r=3 for the Hermitian curve, and the cases r = 3 , 4 r=3,4 for the rational BKS curve. As a by product we also obtain two results of interest in the study of the Galois inverse problem for PGL ​ ( 2 , q ) \mbox{\rm PGL}(2,q) .

1.
Introduction

Let
k,n
with
2\leq n<k
be two positive integers. In the projective plane
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of order
q
, a
(k,n)
-arc is point-set
\mathcal{K}
of size
k
such that
n
is the maximum number of collinear points in
\mathcal{K}
. The concept of a
(k,n)
-arc has a useful interpretation in coding theory as the incidence matrix of a
(k,n)
-arc of
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
is the parity check matrix of a
[k,3,k-n]_{q}
almost MDS code which is non-extendible if and only if the corresponding
(k,n)
-arc is complete. Here, completeness means that the
(k,n)
-arc is maximal (not contained properly in a larger
(k^{\prime},n)
-arc), in other words each point off the
(k,n)
-arc in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
is incident to some
n
-secant i.e. lines meeting the
(k,n)
-arc in exactly
n
points. An (incidence) character
n_{i}
of a
(k,n)
-arc
\mathcal{K}
is any integer
0\leq n_{i}\leq n
such that some line meets
\mathcal{K}
in exactly
n_{i}
points. The foundation of the theory of
(k,n)
-arcs with special attention on their characters was laid down in the 1950s by B. Segre and A. Barlotti.

In the smallest case
n=2
, plenty of results and constructions are known, especially for larger complete
(k,2)
-arcs; see
[
13
]
. In particular, the maximum size of a
(k,2)
-arc is
q+1
or
q+2
according as
q
odd or even. In the odd order case, the
(q+1,2)
-arcs are exactly the sets consisting of all points of an irreducible conic, whereas the classification of
(q+2,2)
-arcs is still open. The second and third largest
(k,2)
-arcs have also been studied intensively.
On the other end, there is an elementary lower bound for the size of a complete arc, due to Lunelli and Sce, see
[
13
]
, Thm. 9.12 and a slight improvement in Thm. 9.13. Probabilistic methods show the existence of complete
(k,2)
-arcs for
k=O(\sqrt{q}\log^{c}q)
with a positive constant
c
. Several effective constructions for small
(k,2)
-arcs are available in the literature (see e.g.
[
20
]
), the best explicit example so far is for
k=O(q^{3/4})
.

Our current knowledge of
(k,n)
-arcs with
n\geq 3
is much less although well known combinatorial structures embedded in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
, such as unitals, nets and other designs, provide inspiring examples of
(k,n)
-arcs with few characters.The upper bound on
k
for
(k,n)
-arcs is due to Barlotti (see
[
13
]
, Corollary 12.5), and Ball, Blokhuis and Mazzocca
[
4
]
showed that it cannot be achieved in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
,
q
odd. More details can be found in Chapter 12 of
[
13
]
. There is also a combinatorial lower bound, due to Alabdullah and Hirschfeld
[
2
]
, analogous to the Lunelli-Sce bound. Also in this case one can apply probabilistic methods, and also effective constructions, in particular when
n
is reasonably large compared to
q
.
Since
(k,n)
-arcs are truly combinatorial objects, counting arguments and incidence geometry are prevalent in their studies. Algebraic and geometric methods combined with combinatorics have also been successfully developed to construct and investigate new and interesting families of
(k,n)
-arcs, the principal tools being polarities, geometric transformations, groups of symmetries, and algebraic curves. Nevertheless, the study of
(k,n)
-arcs is still hard whenever it requires that the
(k,n)
-arc be complete with only few characters and small size
k\approx q
.

A natural candidate to be such a complete
(k,n)
-arc is the set of the points of a plane curve
\mathcal{C}
of degree
n
(containing no linear components over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
) such that some line of
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
meets
\mathcal{C}
transversally in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
, i.e. in
n
pairwise distinct points in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
. The most interesting families of complete
(k,n)
-arcs with very few characters are of this kind including Baer subplanes, ovals, classical unitals and Denniston type maximal arcs; see Section
3
. In particular, the classical unital in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q^{2})
consists of all points of the Hermitian curve
\mathcal{H}_{q}
which is also well known in number theory as being the most important
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
-maximal curve. Further examples of
(k,n)
-arcs consisting of the points of (Frobenius non-classical) curves are found in
[
11
]
and
[
5
,
7
]
. The work by J.W.P. Hirschfeld and F.J. Voloch for cubic curves, and by M. Giulietti and F. Torres for
n\geq 4
, was the first important step towards a kind of sophisticated algebraic theory of
(k,n)
-arcs arising from plane algebraic curves.
A main result in this theory concerns plane curves of degree
n
defined over a subfield
\mathbb{F}_{\bar{q}}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and viewed as a curve of
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
: If
q
is large enough compared to the parameters of
\mathcal{C}
, namely

and
\bar{q}
, then the set of the points of any absolutely irreducible curve
\mathcal{C}
of degree

in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
is a
(k,n)
-arc of small size
k\approx q
.

The algebraic theory approach is also adequate for the completeness problem for such
(k,n)
-arcs even if it needs Galois theory in positive characteristic together with some Dirichlet or
\rm{\check{C}}
ebotarev type density theorem; see paper
[
3
]
that finds its origin in previous work by Guralnick, Tucker, Zieve
[
12
]
and others on permutation polynomials. The essential idea is to express the condition that a point
P\in{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
is incident with a line intersecting transversally

in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
in terms of the Galois closure of the algebraic extension

where

is the function field of
\mathcal{C}
and

is the rational subfield of

arising from the projection of
\mathcal{C}
from

. The favorable situation occurs when the (geometric) Galois group

is the symmetric group
\rm{\mbox{\rm Sym}}_{n}
on the roots of the polynomial associated with

. In fact, for this case, a variant of the classical
\rm{\check{C}}
ebotarev density theorem
[

works well and ensures the existence of a line

through

meeting transversally
\mathcal{C}
in
{\rm{PG}}(2,q)
provided that

is large enough compared to the two parameters of
\mathcal{C}
, namely the degree of
\mathcal{C}
and the order of the plane
{\rm{PG}}(2,\bar{q})
where
\mathcal{C}
is defined. For

, the
(k,n)
-arc may not be complete; nevertheless completeness can still be achieved by adding some (at most

) points; see
[
3
]
. As a corollary, see
[
3
, Theorem 5.3]
, for all but finitely many

is large enough, there are complete
(k,n)
-arcs of small size

.

The question arises whether complete

-arcs of small size

can be obtained in this way for (almost) every

. As it appears plausible, at least intuitively, the choice of the curve is critical. We thoroughly work out two cases investigating the Hermitian curve

defined over

and the rational BKS curve

defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, respectively. Our main result for the Hermitian case is that for every

is a complete

. For the rational BKS case, the set

of the points in

The above results do not hold true for

The result

An important tool for the study of the action of the Galois group on the roots of its defining polynomial, given by explicit equation, is Abhyankar’s skew derivative introduced in

Gal(f,K)
of

(over
K
). The group

is transitive on

if and only if
f(T)
is irreducible (over
K
).

From now on assume that
F(T)
is irreducible. Following
[
1
, Section 4]
, we “throw away” a root of

Then

is

-transitive on
\Delta
. As Abhyankar stressed in

, it does not matter which root of

are independent of which root we call

. Likewise, by throwing away

f_{s}=f_{s}(T)=\frac{f(T)}{(T-\alpha_{1})\cdots(T-\alpha_{s})}=T^{m-s}+d_{1}T^{m-s-1}+\ldots+d_{m-s}\in K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s})[T],

.

For any polynomial
