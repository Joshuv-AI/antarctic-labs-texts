Abstract
. We study a random matching problem on closed compact 2 2 -dimensional Riemannian manifolds (with respect to the squared Riemannian distance), with samples of random points whose common law is absolutely continuous with respect to the volume measure with strictly positive and bounded density. We show that given two sequences of numbers n n and m = m ⁡ ( n ) m=m(n) of points, asymptotically equivalent as n n goes to infinity, the optimal transport plan between the two empirical measures μ n \mu^{n} and ν m \nu^{m} is quantitatively well-approximated by ( Id , exp ⁡ ( ∇ h n ) ) # ​ μ n \big(\mathrm{Id},\exp(\nabla h^{n})\big)_{\#}\mu^{n} where h n h^{n} solves a linear elliptic PDE obtained by a regularized first-order linearization of the Monge-Ampère equation. This is obtained in the case of samples of correlated random points for which a stretched exponential decay of the α \alpha -mixing coefficient holds and for a class of discrete-time Markov chains having a unique absolutely continuous invariant measure with respect to the volume measure.

1.1.
The random matching problem and its asymptotic

The random matching problem is a popular optimization problem at the interface between analysis and probability with applications in many different fields such as statistical physics
[
16
,
44
]
, computer science
[
40
]
and economics
[
20
,
24
]
. Within the mathematical literature, it has been subject of intense studies due to its interactions with many areas, including for instance graph theory
[
38
]
and geometric probability
[
50
]
. In this paper we focus on one of its simple versions. Let
\{X_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n}
and
\{Y_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq m}
(with possibly
m>n
) be two families of random points on a compact Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
(endowed with the Riemannian distance
\mathrm{d}
). We are interested in the quadratic matching problem

(1.1)

\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{nm}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}(X_{i},Y_{j})

where

\Pi_{nm}:=\bigg\{\pi\in[0,1]^{n\times m}\ \Big|\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{n}\bigg\}.

Classically, (
1.1
) can be phrased in terms of a transport problem. Indeed, letting

(1.2)

\mu^{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\nu^{m}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{Y_{j}},

be the empirical measures associated to the two point clouds, the linear programming problem (
1.1
) amounts to determine the quadratic Wasserstein distance
\ W_{2}^{2}(\mu^{n},\nu^{m})
.

In the special case
n=m
the Birkhoff-von Neumann’s Theorem provides a correspondence between (
1.1
) and the usual bipartite matching

(1.3)

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}^{2}(X_{i},Y_{\sigma(i)}),

where
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denotes the set of injective maps
\sigma:\{1,\dots,n\}\rightarrow\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Indeed, since
\Pi_{nn}
is a convex polytope, minimizers in (
1.1
) have to be searched among extremal points. By the Birkhoff-von Neumann Theorem
[
8
, Lemma 2.1.3]
, the latter are nothing but permutation matrices (up to a factor
\tfrac{1}{n}
).

A first natural question is to understand the asymptotic of (
1.1
) as
n,m\uparrow\infty
. For the same number of samples
n=m
and independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) on the unit square
[0,1]^{d}
, the scaling of the cost (
1.1
) has been well understood in the mathematical and statistical physics literature. A simple heuristic argument, see for instance
[
44
]
, suggests that given a point
X_{i}
, we can find a point
Y_{j}
within a volume of order
O(n^{-1})
with high probability. For this reason, the typical inter-point distance is of order
O(n^{-\frac{1}{d}})
suggesting that the scaling of (
1.1
) is of order
O(n^{-\frac{2}{d}})
. Although attractive, this heuristic turns out to be unfortunately false in low dimension showing a critical behavior when
d=2
. This critical case is the one on which we focus in this paper. Ajtai, Komlós and Tusnády in
[
1
]
were the first to show that, for i.i.d. uniform samples, a logarithmic correction is needed, deriving

\mathbb{E}\big[W_{2}^{2}(\mu^{n},\nu^{n})\big]\sim\frac{\log(n)}{n},

extended later by Talagrand in

1.2.
Linearization ansatz

We now briefly reproduce the linearization ansatz introduced in
[
16
]
. For simplicity, we consider the case
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
n=m
and i.i.d. samples with common distribution
\rho\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}
. Let
T^{n}
be an optimal transport map (whose existence is ensured by Brenier’s Theorem
[
14
]
) between
\mu^{n}
and
\nu^{n}
. Based on the transport relation
T^{n}_{\#}\mu^{n}=\nu^{n}
and a change of variables,
T^{n}
solves (formally) the Monge-Ampère equation

(1.7)

(\mu^{n}\circ T^{n})\det(\nabla T^{n})=\nu^{n}.

Since the cost is quadratic, by
[
48
, Theorem 1.25]
, there exists a function
h^{n}
such that
T^{n}=\mathrm{Id}+\nabla h^{n}
. Applying the law of large numbers, we further have the weak convergences
\mu^{n},\nu^{n}{\rightharpoonup}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}
as
n\uparrow\infty
so that we expect
T^{n}\approx\mathrm{Id}
as
n\uparrow\infty
. Thus, this suggests that the correction
\nabla h^{n}
is small as
n\uparrow\infty
, allowing to perform (formally) the Taylor expansions

(1.8)

\mu^{n}\circ T^{n}\approx\mu^{n}+\nabla\mu^{n}\cdot\nabla h^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\det(\nabla T^{n})\approx 1+\Delta h^{n}.

Plugging (
1.8
) into (
1.7
), neglecting the higher order terms and replacing
\mu^{n}
by
\rho
yields

(1.9)

-\nabla\cdot\rho\nabla h^{n}=\mu^{n}-\nu^{n}.

This formal linearization suggests the following two conjectures

(1.10)

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\big[W_{2}^{2}(\mu^{n},\nu^{n})\big]-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\nabla h^{n}|^{2}\bigg]\bigg|=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|T^{n}-(\text{Id}+\nabla h^{n})|^{2}=0\text{ a.s.}

Unfortunately, (
1.10
) cannot hold as it is, since the solution of (
1.9
) does not belong to
\mathrm{H}^{1}
due to the roughness of the source term. To overcome this, following the strategy in
[
6
]
, a regularization using the heat-semigroup at time
\sim\frac{1}{n}
is made. Doing so, the first item of (
1.10
) turns out to be true, leading to the result (
1.5
) (see for instance
[
3
]
for a convergence rate).

1.4.
Extension to a class of Markov chains

We first recall some basic facts on discrete-time Markov chains on
\mathcal{M}
. Such a Markov process is described by its initial distribution
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
and its transition kernel
K
, that is a measurable map from
\mathcal{M}
to the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
. We recall that
K
acts on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
in form of

(1.26)

(K\mu)(A)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}K(\cdot,A)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\quad\text{for every Borel set $A\subset\mathcal{M}$ and $\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})$,}

and likewise on bounded measurable function
\psi
in form of

(1.27)

(K\psi)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\psi(y)\,K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\quad\text{for any $x\in\mathcal{M}$.}

Given an initial distribution
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, we recall that the law of a Markov chain
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
can be expressed with the help of the transition kernel, namely

(1.28)

X_{n}\sim K^{n}\mu_{0}\quad\text{for any $n\geq 0$,}

where
K^{n}
stands for the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
-iteration of the kernel
K
.

We now introduce the class of discrete-time Markov chains we consider.

Assumption 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
2
-dimensional compact Riemannian manifold. Let
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
and
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{M}
be a Markov chain with initial law
\mu_{0}
. We assume that the chain satisfies the two following conditions:

(i)

We assume that there exists a measurable function
k:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\rightarrow[0,1]
and
\lambda,\Lambda>0
such that for any Borel set
A\subset\mathcal{M}

(1.29)

K(x,A)=\int_{A}k(x,\cdot)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\quad\text{with $\lambda\leq k\leq\Lambda$}.

(ii)

We assume that there exists an unique invariant measure
\mu_{\infty}
, i.e.
K\mu_{\infty}=\mu_{\infty}
, and
V:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, such that
\int V\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}<\infty
, as well as
\sigma\in(0,1)
for which

(1.30)

\|K^{\ell}\phi-\mu_{\infty}(\phi)\|_{V}\leq\sigma^{\ell}\|\phi-\mu_{\infty}(\phi)\|_{V}\quad\text{for any $\phi$ such that $\|\phi\|_{V}<\infty$,}

with

\|\phi\|_{V}:=\sup\frac{|\phi|}{1+V}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{\infty}(\phi):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}.

We now comment on the consequences of the above assumptions. First, the condition (
1.29
) ensures that the invariant measure
\mu_{\infty}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the volume measure with bounded density, namely

(1.31)

\mu_{\infty}=\rho\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\quad\text{with $\lambda\leq\rho\leq\Lambda$.}

We briefly give the argument for (
1.31
). Using the second item of (
1.29
), we have that the operator

\mathcal{I}:f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{M}}k(\cdot,y)f(y)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}(y)\quad\text{is compact.}

Furthermore, we note that the closed convex set
\mathcal{C}:=\{f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}\,|\,\lambda\leq f\leq\Lambda\text{ and }\int_{\mathcal{M}}f=1\}
is invariant under the action of
\mathcal{I}
. Therefore Schauder’s fixed point theorem implies that
\mathcal{I}
admits a fixed point in
\mathcal{C}
. Given such a fixed point
\rho
, it is clear that
\mu_{\infty}
defined in (
1.31
) is an invariant measure according to (
1.29
).

Second, we note that by Harris’ theorem
[
31
, Theorem 3.7]
, the condition (
1.30
) is satisfied when the kernel satisfies the following geometric drift condition: There exist a function
V:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and constants
\tau\in(0,1)
and
C\geq 0
such that

KV\leq\tau V+C,

and the kernel
K
is bounded on the sublevel sets of
V
, i.e. for all
R\geq 0
, there exists a constant
0<a<1
for which

\|k(x,\cdot)-k(y,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}}\leq 2(1-a)\quad\text{for every $x,y$ such that $V(x)+V(y)\leq R$.}

Moreover, the assumption (
1.30
) implies the geometric decay of the
\beta
-mixing coefficient (
1.12
), namely there exists a constant
C
depending on
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and
\mathrm{m}(\mathcal{M})
such that

(1.32)

\beta_{ij}\leq C\sigma^{\min\{i,j\}}\quad\text{for any $i,j$,}

which ensures that (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) (with
\eta=1
and
b=-\log(\sigma)
) hold and ensures good concentration property of the Markov chain, see Proposition
A.1
. The estimates (
1.32
) can be seen as a direct consequence of the combination of the representation for the
\beta
-mixing in
[
21
, Proposition 1]
and the assumption (
1.29
).

Finally, the condition (
1.30
) quantifies the weak convergence of the law of the Markov chain to its stationary distribution, namely there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
f\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})

(1.33)

|\mathbb{E}[f(X_{n})]-\mu_{\infty}(f)|\leq C\sigma^{n}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}.

We shortly give the argument. We first notice that a direct inductive argument together with the semigroup property
K^{n_{1}+n_{2}}=K^{n_{1}}K^{n_{2}}
for every
n_{1},n_{2}>0
and Fubini’s theorem gives

(1.34)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\,\mathrm{d}K^{n}\mu_{0}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}K^{n}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}\quad\text{for any $n\geq 1$.}

The combination of (
1.28
), (
1.34
) and (
1.30
) gives

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[f(X_{n})]-\mu_{\infty}(f)|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{LawOfTheChain},\eqref{eq:swapkern}}}{{=}}\Big|\int_{\mathcal{M}}(K^{n}f-\mu_{\infty}(f))\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}\Big|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ConvergenceRate}}}{{\leq}}\sigma^{n}\|f-\mu_{\infty}(f)\|_{V}\int_{\mathcal{M}}(1+V(x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0},

which leads to (
1.33
) using that
\|f-\mu_{\infty}(f)\|_{V}\leq\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}
and by Assumption
1.5

\int_{\mathcal{M}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}<\infty
.

A classical example of a Markov chain satisfying Assumption
1.5
is given by iterated function systems with additive noise. For simplicity, let
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Let
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be i.i.d. random variables with common law
h\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}
for some
h
satisfying
\lambda\leq h\leq\Lambda
. Let
F:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
be a contraction, i.e. there exists a constant
L<1
such that

(1.35)

|F(x)-F(y)|\leq L|x-y|\quad\text{for any $x,y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}$.}

We define the iterated function system
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
according to the induction

X_{n+1}=F(X_{n})+\theta_{n}\quad\text{for any $n\geq 1$}.

The kernel is given by

K(x,A)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{A}(F(x)+\theta)h(\theta)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}(\theta),

so that
K
satisfies (
1.29
) with

k(x,\cdot)=h(\cdot-F(x)).

Moreover, the condition (
1.35
) ensures the validity of (
1.30
), see for instance
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
. Thus the Markov process
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfies Assumption
1.5
.

We show in Appendix
C
that the conclusions of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.2
hold true for Markov chains satisfying Assumption
1.5
.

1.5.
Open problems

We conclude this section with open questions that arise in view of our results:

(1)

Sharpness of the rates.
The convergence rate in (
1.20
) and (
1.23
) match with the one obtained for the case of uniformly distributed samples in
[
4
]
. However, even in the latter case, it has not been shown whether the rate is optimal and we suspect the opposite. A possible way to track the optimal rate could be to perform a second-order linearization of the Monge-Ampère equation (
1.7
). Following the same type of computations leading to (
1.9
) in the case
\rho=1
, a second-order linearization
q^{n}
should solve

-\Delta q^{n}=\text{det}(\nabla^{2}h^{n}),

where we recall that
h^{n}
solves (
1.9
), providing the conjecture

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|T^{n}-(\text{Id}+\nabla h^{n})|^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\nabla q^{n}|^{2}\bigg|=0.

This direction of research is left for future investigations.

(2)

Extension to
p
-costs.
A natural question is to investigate if our results hold for different cost functions as
p
-cost functions for
p>1
. The behavior of the cost has been optimally quantified in
[
1
,
13
,
9
,
23
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, quantitative estimates on the transport plan in the setting of general
p
-costs are not known, even for uniformly distributed samples.
A possible approach would be to revise the linearization ansatz of
[
16
]
for general
p
-costs. As observed in
[
34
]
, if the transport cost between two points
x,y
is given by
\frac{1}{p}|x-y|^{p}
, Gangbo-McCann’s theorem
[
25
, Theorem 1.2]
ensures that there exists a map
h^{n}
such that the optimal transport map
T^{n}
takes the form
T^{n}=\text{Id}+|\nabla h^{n}|^{p^{\prime}-2}\nabla h^{n}
, where
p^{\prime}
denotes the conjugate exponent. Therefore, following the same type of computations leading to (
1.9
), a first-order linearization should solve the following degenerate
p^{\prime}
-Laplace equation

-\nabla\cdot\rho|\nabla h^{n}|^{p^{\prime}-2}\nabla h^{n}=\mu^{n}-\nu^{m},

and we may expect

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\int_{\mathcal{M}}|T^{n}-(\text{Id}+|\nabla h^{n}|^{p^{\prime}-2}\nabla h^{n})|^{p}=0.

2.
Structure of the proof

This section is devoted to describe the main ideas and how are organized the proofs of Theorem
1.2
and
1.3
. We mainly focus on the proof of Theorem
1.2
since the proof of Theorem
1.3
follows by the same strategy.

General strategy.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
follows the strategy employed in
[
4
]
to deal with independent and uniformly distributed random points.
The main idea is to use the quantitative stability result for transport maps in
[
4
, Theorem 3.2]
, stating that two transports map are close in the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-topology if the target measures are close in the
W_{2}
-topology. We restate below the result for reader’s convenience.

Theorem 2.1

(Stability of transport maps)
.

Let
\nu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
such that
\nu\ll\mathrm{m}
and let
S,T:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be the optimal transport maps respectively for the pairs of measures
(\nu,\mu_{1})
and
(\nu,\mu_{2})
. We assume that
S=\exp(\nabla f)
for some
f:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\mathrm{C}^{1,1}
-regularity.

There exists a constant
c>0
depending on
\mathcal{M}
such that, provided

(2.1)

\|\nabla f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\leq c,

we have

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{2}(S,T)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\lesssim W^{2}_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+W_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})W_{2}(\nu,\mu_{1}).

The first step consists of using Theorem
2.1
to deduce a stability estimate (in terms of the quadratic Wasserstein distance) of transport plans in the special case where
\mu_{1}=\nu^{m}
,
\mu_{2}=\exp(\nabla h^{n,t})_{\#}\mu^{n}
and
\nu=\mu^{n}
. In this step, we immediately face the issue of the lack of regularity of
h^{n,t}
necessary to ensure the condition (
2.1
): Indeed, recalling that
h^{n,t}
solves (
1.18
), it does not have the
\mathrm{C}^{1,1}
-regularity condition for non-smooth densities
\rho
that we consider here. We overcome this issue introducing an additional regularization step: We smooth the operator
-\nabla\cdot\rho\nabla
and implicitly
\gamma^{n,t}
in form of

(2.2)

\gamma^{n,t}_{\delta}:=\big(\text{Id},\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})\big)_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\quad\text{with }-\nabla\cdot\rho_{\delta}\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta}=\mu^{n,t}-\nu^{m,t},

for a regularization parameter
\delta
to be optimized and
\rho_{\delta}:=\text{P}_{\delta}\rho
. Classical Schauder’s theory ensures that
h^{n,t}_{\delta}
owns
\text{C}^{\infty}
-regularity.
Doing so, we can use Theorem
2.1
to deduce, provided that

(2.3)

\|\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}h^{n,t}_{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\ll 1,

a stability error estimate which reads

(2.4)

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi}W^{2}_{2}(\pi,\gamma^{n,t}_{\delta})\lesssim

\displaystyle W^{2}_{2}\big(\nu^{m,t},\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\big)+W_{2}\big(\nu^{m,t},\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\big)W_{2}(\mu^{n},\nu^{m})

\displaystyle+W^{2}_{2}(\nu^{m,t},\nu^{m})+W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})+\big(W_{2}(\nu^{m,t},\nu^{m})+W_{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})\big)W_{2}(\mu^{n},\nu^{m}),

where we refer to (
3.107
) for further details. Our argument then differs from
[
4
]
by splitting the error in two parts

(2.5)

\inf_{\pi}W^{2}_{2}(\pi,\gamma^{n,t})\leq\underbrace{W^{2}_{2}(\gamma^{n,t},\gamma^{n,t}_{\delta})}_{\text{regularization error}}+\underbrace{\inf_{\pi}W^{2}_{2}(\pi,\gamma^{n,t}_{\delta})}_{\text{stability error}}.

Our proof then proceeds in two steps, controlling separately the two terms in (
2.5
).

Control of the regularization error

To deal with the regularization error, we look at the difference
e^{n,t}_{\delta}:=h^{n,t}_{\delta}-h^{n,t}
which solves, according to (
1.18
) and (
2.2
),

(2.6)

-\nabla\cdot\rho_{\delta}\nabla e^{n,t}_{\delta}=\nabla\cdot(\rho_{\delta}-\rho)\nabla h^{n,t}.

Using an energy estimate, we get

(2.7)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla e^{n,t}_{\delta}|^{2}\lesssim\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\rho-\rho_{\delta}|^{2}|\nabla h^{n,t}|^{2}.

On the one hand, since
\rho\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
, we have
\rho_{\delta}\rightarrow\rho
as
\delta\downarrow 0
in every
\mathrm{L}^{q}
with
q<\infty
. On the other hand, we learn from Meyers’ estimate (recalled in Proposition
A.3
) that there exists
\bar{q}>2
such that
\nabla h^{n,t}\in\mathrm{L}^{\bar{q}}
. Consequently, we can treat (
2.7
) using Hölder’s inequality which provides

(2.8)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla e^{n,t}_{\delta}|^{2}\lesssim\|\rho_{\delta}-\rho\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2(\frac{\bar{q}}{2})^{\prime}}}\|\nabla h^{n,t}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\bar{q}}}.

The next step is to control the averaged Meyers’ norm
\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla h^{n,t}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\bar{q}}}\big]
, that we show in Proposition
3.3
to be of order of

(2.9)

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla h^{n,t}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\bar{q}}}\big]\lesssim\frac{|\log(t)|+\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)}{n},

where we recall that
\eta
denotes the correlation length, see Assumption
1.1
. The combination of (
2.8
), (
2.9
) and local Lipschitzianity of the exponential map yields

(2.10)

\mathbb{E}\big[W^{2}_{2}(\gamma^{n,t}_{\delta},\gamma^{n,t})\big]\lesssim\|\rho_{\delta}-\rho\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2(\frac{\bar{q}}{2})^{\prime}}}\frac{|\log(t)|+\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)}{n},

where we refer to (
3.104
) for further details.

Control of the stability error

For the stability error, we first need to ensure (
2.3
). Our strategy follows the idea in
[
3
]
which consists of showing that (
2.3
) is satisfied with very high probability. In our case, a new difficulty comes from our regularization of
\rho
and the regularization parameter
\delta
has to be carefully optimized. We show that if
\delta
is taken as an inverse power of
\log(n)
, (
2.3
) becomes very likely as
n\uparrow\infty
. More precisely, we show in Proposition
3.4
that given two exponents
\kappa_{1}
and
\upsilon\gg\kappa_{1}
, there exists
\kappa_{2}
such that given the choices

(2.11)

\delta=\frac{1}{\log^{\kappa_{1}}(n)}\quad\text{and}\quad t=\frac{\log^{\kappa_{2}}(n)}{n},

we have

(2.12)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\|\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}h^{n,t}_{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}>\tfrac{1}{\log^{\upsilon}(n)}\Big)=o\Big(\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\Big)\quad\text{for any $\ell\in\mathbb{N}$}.

This result is in the spirit of
[
3
, Theorem 3.3]
but, in our setting, the proof relies on Schauder’s theory rather than an explicit formula for
h^{n,t}_{\delta}
as well as concentration inequalities in form of Proposition
A.1
to treat the correlations. For further details on the strategy, we refer to Section
3.3
. With (
2.12
) in hands, we can restrict the analysis to realizations satisfying
\|\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}h^{n,t}_{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\leq\tfrac{1}{\log^{\upsilon}(n)}
where, for
n\gg 1
, (
2.3
) is satisfied which puts us in the validity of (
2.4
). We then treat each terms appearing in (
2.4
) separately:

•

The optimal control of the cost

W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n},\nu^{m})
has been already well studied and is optimally estimated by

(2.13)

\mathbb{E}\big[W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n},\nu^{m})\big]\lesssim\frac{\log(n)}{n}.

We refer to Appendix
B
for a detailed statement, references and extensions to the cases of Assumption
1.1
and Assumption
1.5
.

•

The smoothing errors

W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n},\mu^{n,t})
and
W^{2}_{2}(\nu^{m},\nu^{m,t})
. Classical contractivity estimates are known and are usually applied to deal with these errors, see for instance
[
22
, Theorem 3]
, which bound the errors by
t
. However, due to the choice of
t
in (
2.11
), this result is of no use since
t
is much larger than the magnitude of the cost, namely
t\gg\frac{\log(n)}{n}
. Instead, we follow the approach in
[
3
]
, where the authors showed that in the particular case of empirical measures in dimension
2
, we can improve the rate and obtain the bound
\frac{\log\log(n)}{n}\ll\frac{\log(n)}{n}
. We extend this result to our setting of non-constant densities and correlated points. In Proposition
3.5
, we derive

(2.14)

\mathbb{E}\big[W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n},\mu^{n,t})\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[W^{2}_{2}(\nu^{m},\nu^{m,t})\big]\lesssim\frac{\log\log(n)}{n}+t\|\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}}.

As opposed to
[
3
]
, our approach uses Fourier analysis together with additional cares to handle the correlations and non-constant densities.

•

The error in the Moser coupling

W^{2}_{2}\big(\nu^{m,t},\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\big)
. We follow the strategy in
[
3
]
. This error can be related to a Moser coupling between
\mu^{n,t}
and
\nu^{m,t}
(see for instance
[
56
, Appendix p. 16]
): The equation (
1.18
) gives a natural coupling between
\mu^{n,t}
and
\nu^{m,t}
which can be formulated using Benamou-Brenier’s theorem
[
10
]
,

\nu^{m,t}=\phi(1,\cdot)_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\quad\text{with $\phi$ being the flow induced by $s\mapsto\frac{\rho_{\delta}\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta}}{s\mu^{n,t}+(1-s)\nu^{m,t}}$}.

Then, using the transport plan
\big(\phi(1,\cdot),\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})\big)_{\#}\mu^{n,t}
as a competitor, we get

\displaystyle W^{2}_{2}\big(\nu^{m,t},\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\big)

\displaystyle=W^{2}_{2}\big(\phi(1,\cdot)_{\#}\mu^{n,t},\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\big(\phi(1,\cdot),\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})\big),

that we combine with a quantitative stability result for flows of vector fields, proved in
[
3
, Proposition A.1]
, leading to

W^{2}_{2}\big(\nu^{m,t},\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\big)\lesssim\big(\|\rho_{\delta}-\rho\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2(\frac{\bar{q}}{2})^{\prime}}}+\|\rho_{t}-\rho\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2(\frac{\bar{q}}{2})^{\prime}}}+\tfrac{1}{\log^{\upsilon}(n)}\big)\|\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\bar{q}}},

where we recall that
\bar{q}
denotes the Meyers’ exponent introduced in (
2.7
). For further details, we refer to (
3.115
). It then remains to appeal to Meyers’ estimate, see Proposition
A.3
, to (
2.6
) together with (
2.9
) in form of

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\bar{q}}}\big]\lesssim\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla h^{n,t}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\bar{q}}}\big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{LqEstimatesIntro}}}{{\lesssim}}\frac{|\log(t)|+\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)}{n},

which finally yields

(2.15)

\mathbb{E}\big[W^{2}_{2}\big(\nu^{m,t},\exp(\nabla h^{n,t}_{\delta})_{\#}\mu^{n,t}\big)\big]\lesssim\big(\|\rho_{\delta}-\rho\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2(\frac{\bar{q}}{2})^{\prime}}}+\|\rho_{t}-\rho\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2(\frac{\bar{q}}{2})^{\prime}}}+\tfrac{1}{\log^{\upsilon}(n)}\big)\frac{|\log(t)|+\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)}{n}.

To conclude, we see that all bounds involve errors in terms of the approximation of
\rho
using the heat-semigroup, that we need to quantify. This is where the assumption
\rho\in\mathrm{H}^{\varepsilon}
plays a role, in form of the quantitative estimate

(2.16)

\|\rho_{s}-\rho\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{\mathrm{H}^{\varepsilon}}\,s^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{for any $s>0$},

see (
3.94
) for a proof. Combining (
2.4
), (
2.5
), (
2.10
), (
2.13
), (
2.14
), (
2.15
), (
2.16
) with the choices of
\delta
and
t
in (
2.11
), we obtain Theorem
1.2
. The proof of Theorem
1.3
is obtained using the same strategy where the first step is simpler, since we apply directly Theorem
2.1
with
\mu_{1}=\rho
,
\nu=\mu^{n}
and
\mu_{2}=\exp(\nabla f^{n,t}_{\delta})_{\#}\mu^{n}
where
f^{n,t}
solves
-\nabla\cdot\rho_{\delta}\nabla f^{n,t}=\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t}
.

Proofs

3.1.
Notations and preliminary results

We provide in this section some notations and preliminary results needed in the proofs of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
. We recall that throughout the paper, we denote by
\mathcal{M}
a
2
-dimensional compact connected Riemannian manifold (or the square
[0,1]^{2}
) endowed with the Riemannian distance
\mathrm{d}
.

Wasserstein distance.
Given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, we define the quadratic Wasserstein distance as

(3.1)

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu):=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{2}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
, that is the set of
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M})
having
\mu
and
\nu
as first and second marginal, respectively. We refer the reader to the monographs
[
55
,
56
]
for a detailed overview on the subject of optimal transport. We recall the following simple, but useful Lipschitz contraction property of the Wasserstein distance.

Lemma 3.1

(Lipschitz property of the Wasserstein metric)
.

Let
(D,\mathrm{d}_{D})
be a complete and separable metric space, let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
and let
T:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow D
be a
L
-Lipschitz map. It holds

(3.2)

W_{2}^{2}(T_{\#}\mu,T_{\#}\nu)\leq L^{2}W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu).

Proof.

For any coupling
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, the push-forward
(T,T)_{\#}\pi
is a coupling between
T_{\#}\mu
and
T_{\#}\nu
. Moreover, it holds

\begin{split}\int_{D\times D}\mathrm{d}^{2}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}((T,T)_{\#}\pi)(x,y)&=\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{2}(T(x),T(y))\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)\\
&\leq L^{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{2}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y).\end{split}

Taking the infimum among all possible couplings
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
leads to (
3.2
).
∎

Heat semigroup and heat kernel.
We recall some basic facts on the heat semigroup and its generator, we refer the reader to
[
18
, Chapter VI]
for a more detailed overview. For
t>0
, we denote by
p_{t}
the fundamental solution of the heat operator
\partial_{t}-\Delta
on
\mathcal{M}
, where
\Delta
denotes the Beltrami-Laplace operator. Classical Schauder’s theory ensures that
p_{t}
is smooth and it is known, see for instance
[
52
]
and
[
3
, Appendix B]
, that
p_{t}
and its derivatives satisfy for some
C>0
depending on
\mathcal{M}

(3.3)

|\nabla^{N}p_{t}(x,y)|\lesssim t^{-1-\frac{N}{2}}\exp\Big(-\tfrac{1}{C}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}(x,y)}{t}\Big)\quad\text{for any $t>0$, $N\geq 1$ and $x,y\in\mathcal{M}$.}

The kernel
p_{t}
admits the spectral decomposition

(3.4)

p_{t}(x,y):=\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{-t\lambda_{k}}\phi_{k}(x)\phi_{k}(y)\quad\text{for any $t>0$ and $x,y\in\mathcal{M}$},

converging in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M})
, where we recall that
\{\lambda_{k},\phi_{k}\}
denotes the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
-\Delta
on
\mathcal{M}
. Specifying (
3.4
) on the diagonal and using
\|\phi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}=1
, we obtain the trace formula

(3.5)

\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{-t\lambda_{k}}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{t}(x,x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}(x)\quad\text{for any $t>0$.}

We recall that
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
can be estimated in terms of the eigenvalues,

(3.6)

\|\phi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}_{k}.

We briefly recall the argument. Applying the Gagliardo-Nirenberg’s interpolation inequality
[
7
, Theorem 3.70]
, it holds

\|\phi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{k}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\|\phi_{k}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}=\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{k}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}.

In combination with
-\Delta\phi_{k}=\lambda_{k}\phi_{k}
and elliptic regularity, in form of

\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\lesssim\lambda_{k}\|\phi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}=\lambda_{k},

we obtain (
3.6
).

We recall that
(\text{P}_{t})_{t>0}
admits the spectral gap property, that is there exists a constant
C_{\text{sg}}>0
such that

(3.7)

\|\mathrm{P}_{t}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\leq e^{-C_{\text{sg}}t}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\quad\text{for any $f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}$ with $\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}=0$.}

Note that equivalently, (
3.7
) can be formulated in terms of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
in form of

(3.8)

\inf_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}\geq C_{\text{sg}},

simply by specifying (
3.7
) on
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
. Finally, we recall the Riesz-transform bound

(3.9)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\lesssim\int_{\mathcal{M}}\big|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}f\big|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\quad\text{for any $f$ such that $\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}<\infty$},

where
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
can be defined via its spectral representation

(3.10)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}f=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\,\big(\phi_{k},f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\phi_{k}\quad\text{for any $f\in\mathrm{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}$,}

with
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}
the inner product in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
. We refer to the monograph
[
57
]
for a discussion of the inequalities (
3.7
) and (
3.9
), see Chapter 1 for the case of a Riemannian manifold without boundary and Chapter 2 for the case of a Riemannian manifold with (convex) boundary. In connection with the Wasserstein metric, the heat semigroup satisfies the following classical contraction property.

Lemma 3.2

(Semigroup contraction for absolutely continuous measures)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
satisfy (
1.6
). Given
\rho_{t}:=\mathrm{P}_{t}\rho
, it holds

(3.11)

W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m},\rho\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m})\lesssim t\|\rho_{t}-\rho\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}}\quad\text{for any $t>0$.}

Proof.

Using
g
defined via
-\Delta g=\rho_{t}-\rho
together with (
1.6
),
[
3
, Corollary 4.4]
yields

W^{2}_{2}(\rho_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m},\rho\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m})\lesssim\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}(\rho_{t}-\rho)g\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}.

Writing
g=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{P}_{\tau}(\rho_{t}-\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\tau=-\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau
together with
|\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau|\leq\|\rho\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}t
gives (
3.11
).
∎

3.2.
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-type estimates

As we have seen in (
2.8
), we need a sharp control of the averaged Meyers’ norm
\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla h^{n,t}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\bar{q}}}\big]
. This will be obtained as an immediate corollary of the following proposition, for more details see (
3.101
).

Proposition 3.3

(
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-estimates)
.

Let
\{\mu^{n}\}_{n}
be defined in (
1.2
) with point clouds satisfying Assumptions
1.1
. Let
\bar{q}
be the Meyers exponent given in Theorem
A.3
for the operator
-\nabla\cdot\rho\nabla
. The solution
4
4

4

with Neumann boundary conditions in case
\mathcal{M}
has a boundary

f^{n,t}\in{\rm\dot{H}^{1}}
of

(3.12)

-\nabla\cdot\rho\nabla f^{n,t}=\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t},

satisfies:

(3.13)

\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f^{n,t}|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{2}{q}}\leq\mathcal{C}_{n,t}\frac{|\log(t)|+\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)}{n}\quad\text{for any $q\in[2,\min\{\bar{q},4\}]$},

and a random variable
\mathcal{C}_{n,t}
satisfying for some generic constant
C_{q}
depending on
q
,

(3.14)

\sup_{n,t}\mathbb{E}[\tfrac{1}{C_{q}}\mathcal{C}_{n,t}]\leq 1.

Furthermore, if (
1.14
) holds with
\eta\geq 1
then the assumption (
1.13
) can be dropped and the stochastic integrability can be improved up to losing a
\log(n)
factor, namely

(3.15)

\begin{split}\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f^{n,t}|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{2}{q}}$\mbox{}\hfil\\
&\leq\mathcal{D}_{n,t}\Big(\frac{\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)|\log(t)|}{n}+\frac{t^{-1}(1+\log^{2}(n)\mathds{1}_{\eta\neq\infty})}{n^{2}}\Big)\quad\text{for any $q\in[2,\min\{\bar{q},4\}]$},\end{split}

and a random variable
\mathcal{D}_{n,t}
satisfying for some generic constant
D_{q}
depending on
q
,

\sup_{n,t}\mathbb{E}[\exp(\tfrac{1}{D_{q}}\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{n,t})]\leq 2.

Proof.

We proceed in four steps. In the first step, we prove a representation formula for
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
that we will use as the core tool in the next steps. In the second step, we compare the two operators
-\nabla\cdot\rho\nabla
and
-\Delta
, with help of Meyers’ estimate recalled in Theorem
A.3
. Doing so, we then have to bound the
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-norms (
2\leq q\leq\bar{q}
) of the gradient of the solution
5
5

5

which belongs to any
\mathrm{L}^{q}
for any
q<\infty
from Calderón-Zygmund’ theory, see for instance
[
26
]
to the Poisson equation with r.h.s.
\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t}
and Neumann boundary conditions. We control all the norms by the
\mathrm{L}^{4}
-norm that, in turn, we estimate using the Riesz-transform bound (
3.9
) and following some ideas from
[
6
, Lemma 3.17]
. In the third and fourth steps, we control the bound previously obtained in expectation where our main tool is Assumption
1.1
and the concentration inequalities in Proposition
A.1
.

Step 1. A representation formula for
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

We show that given
f\in\text{C}^{2}
such that
n_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\nabla f=0
on
\partial\mathcal{M}
we have

(3.16)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta\text{P}_{\tau}f\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Note that
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}f
, defined in (
3.10
), is well defined in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
. Indeed, using the fact that
(\phi_{k},f)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}(\phi_{k},\Delta f)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}
and (
3.8
), we have for any
N\leq M<\infty

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{N\leq n\leq M}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}\,\big(\phi_{n},f)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\phi_{n}\bigg\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}=\sum_{N\leq n\leq M}\lambda_{n}|(\phi_{n},f)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq n\leq M}\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{n}}|(\phi_{n},\Delta f)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{C_{\text{sg}}}\sum_{n\geq N}|(\phi_{n},\Delta f)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}|^{2},

which vanishes as
N\uparrow\infty
uniformly in
M
.

We now justify (
3.16
). Observe that since
n_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\nabla f=0
on
\partial\mathcal{M}
,

(3.17)

\Delta\text{P}_{s}f=\text{P}_{s}\Delta f\quad\text{for any $s\in(0,\infty)$,}

which is a direct consequence of two integration by parts using the heat-kernel representation
\text{P}_{s}f=\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{s}(\cdot,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}(y)
. Therefore

(3.18)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta{\rm P}_{\tau}f\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm P}_{\tau}\Delta f\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where the last integral is well-defined in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
since from (
3.7
)

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathrm{P}_{\tau}\Delta f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-C_{sg}\tau}\|\Delta f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau<\infty.

We then use the spectral decomposition of the heat semigroup (
3.4
) to get

(3.19)

\mathrm{P}_{\tau}\Delta f=\sum_{n}e^{-\lambda_{n}\tau}\big(\phi_{n},\Delta f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\phi_{n}\quad\text{in $\mathrm{L}^{2}$.}

The combination of (
3.18
) and (
3.19
) yields for any
\eta\in\mathrm{L}^{2}

(3.20)

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta{\rm P}_{\tau}f,\eta\bigg)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\mathrm{P}_{\tau}\Delta f,\eta\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Laplacian12:Eq2}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n}e^{-\lambda_{n}\tau}\big(\phi_{n},\Delta f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\big(\phi_{n},\eta\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Using (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n}e^{-\lambda_{n}\tau}|\big(\phi_{n},\Delta f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}||\big(\phi_{n},\eta\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}|\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-C_{sg}\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\Big)\sum_{n}|\big(\phi_{n},\Delta f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}||\big(\phi_{n},\eta\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}|<\infty,

so that we can exchange integration and summation in (
3.20
) to obtain

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta{\rm P}_{\tau}f,\eta\bigg)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\lambda_{n}\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\Big)\big(\phi_{n},\Delta f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\big(\phi_{n},\eta\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}\sum_{n}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}\big(\phi_{n},\Delta f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\big(\phi_{n},\eta\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}\sum_{n}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}\,\big(\phi_{n},f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\big(\phi_{n},\eta\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Laplacian12}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\pi}\big((-\Delta f)^{\frac{1}{2}},\eta\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}},

which gives (
3.16
) by arbitrariness of
\eta
. Finally, note that the r. h. s. integral in (
3.16
) is absolutely convergent thanks to the integration by parts
\Delta\mathrm{P}_{\tau}f=\mathrm{P}_{\tau}\Delta f
and the bounds on the heat kernel (
3.3
), so that it defines a function in
\text{C}^{0}
.

Step 2. Comparison with the solution of the Poisson equation.
We claim that for any
q\in[2,\min\{\bar{q},4\}]
and
p<\infty

(3.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f^{n,t}|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{2p}{q}}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim_{q}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big((-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\mu^{n}-\rho)\Big)^{2p}\Big]^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Let
g^{n,t}\in{\rm\dot{H}^{1}}
be the solution of the following Poisson equation

(3.22)

-\Delta g^{n,t}=\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t}.

Re-expressing the right-hand side of (
3.12
) as
\nabla\cdot\nabla g^{n,t}
, we apply Meyers’ estimate recalled in Theorem
A.3
and Hölder’s inequality to obtain:

(3.23)

\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f^{n,t}|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{2}{q}}\lesssim\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g^{n,t}|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{2}{q}}\lesssim_{q}\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g^{n,t}|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{for any $q\in[2,\min\{\bar{q},4\}]$,}

We now introduce the Paley-Littlewood functional

\mathcal{L}(g):=\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}s(\partial_{s}\text{P}_{s}g)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{for any $g\in\mathrm{L}^{4}$ and $\int_{\mathcal{M}}g=0$.}

We recall that the inverse of
\mathcal{L}
is continuous, see
[
51
]
, namely

(3.24)

\|g\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}}\lesssim\|\mathcal{L}(g)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}}.

Combining the Riesz transform bound (
3.9
) with (
3.24
) yields

(3.25)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g^{n,t}|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\lesssim\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Big(\mathcal{L}\Big((-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{n,t}\Big)\Big)^{4}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,s(\partial_{s}\text{P}_{s}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{n,t})^{2}\Big)^{2}.

We now claim that

(3.26)

\partial_{s}\text{P}_{s}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{n,t}=(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\mu^{n}-\rho)\quad\text{for any $s\geq 0$,}

which requires a special care when
\mathcal{M}
has a boundary. We use the definition of
\text{P}_{s}
in form of
\partial_{s}\text{P}_{s}=\Delta\text{P}_{s}
to get

(3.27)

\partial_{s}\text{P}_{s}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{n,t}=\Delta\text{P}_{s}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{n,t}.

Recalling that
n_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\nabla g^{n,t}=0
, (
3.17
) implies that
\Delta\text{P}_{\tau}g^{n,t}=\text{P}_{\tau}\Delta g^{n,t}
which, combined with (
3.22
) and (
3.16
), gives

(3.28)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{n,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{\tau}(\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

In particular, it implies that
n_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\nabla(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{n,t}=0
. Therefore, one can use once more (
3.17
) and, together with (
3.28
), (
3.27
) turns into

(3.29)

\partial_{s}\text{P}_{s}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{n,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s}\Delta\text{P}_{\tau}(\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Using a last time (
3.17
) in form of
\text{P}_{s}\Delta\text{P}_{\tau}(\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t})=\Delta\text{P}_{s}\text{P}_{\tau}(\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t})
that we combine with the semigroup property
\text{P}_{t}\text{P}_{t^{\prime}}=\text{P}_{t+t^{\prime}}
yields

\text{P}_{s}\Delta\text{P}_{\tau}(\mu^{n,t}-\rho_{t})=\Delta\text{P}_{\tau}\text{P}_{s+t}(\mu^{n}-\rho),

which, together with (
3.29
) and (
3.16
) leads to (
3.26
).

The combination of (
3.23
), (
3.25
) and (
3.26
) together with Minkowski’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f^{n,t}|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{2p}{q}}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Eq41}}}{{\lesssim}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\Big((-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\mu^{n}-\rho)\Big)^{2}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{2}}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big((-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\mu^{n}-\rho)\Big)^{2p}\Big]^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},

which is (
3.21
).

Step 3. Proof of (
3.13
).
The estimate (
3.13
) is a consequence of (
3.21
) applied with
p=1
and

(3.30)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big((-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\mu^{n}-\rho)(x)\Big)^{2}\Big]\lesssim\frac{\zeta(s,t)}{n}(1+\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)s^{\frac{1}{2\log(n)}}\zeta(s,t)^{1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}})\quad\text{for any $s\in(0,\infty)$,}

with

(3.31)

\zeta(s,t):=\min\big\{(s+t)^{-1},(s+t)^{-2}\big\}.

Indeed, plugging (
3.30
) in (
3.21
) gives

(3.32)

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{2}{q}}\bigg]\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta(s,t)(1+\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)s^{\frac{1}{2\log(n)}}\zeta(s,t)^{1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}})\,\mathrm{d}s.

Using that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta(s,t)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t}^{1}s^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{1}^{\infty}s^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}s\lesssim|\log(t)|,

and analogously

\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\frac{1}{2\log(n)}}\zeta(s,t)^{1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}\,\mathrm{d}s\lesssim 1,

(
3.32
) implies (
3.13
).

We now show (
3.30
). First, using the definition (
1.2
) of
\mu^{n}
, we have

(3.33)

(-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\mu^{n}-\rho)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,X_{k})\quad\text{with}\quad\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,y):=(-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho),

such that expanding the square gives

\displaystyle\Big((-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\mu^{n}-\rho)(x)\Big)^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(\omega_{s+t}(x,X_{k})\big)^{2}+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq n}\omega_{s+t}(x,X_{\ell})\omega_{s+t}(x,X_{\ell^{\prime}}).

The estimate (
3.30
) is then a consequence of

(3.34)

\mathbb{E}[|\omega_{s+t}(x,X_{1})|^{2}]\lesssim\min\Big\{(s+t)^{-1},(s+t)^{-2}\Big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{y\in\mathcal{M}}|\omega_{s+t}(x,y)|\lesssim\sqrt{s}\min\Big\{(s+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}},(s+t)^{-2}\Big\}.

Indeed, the first item of (
3.34
) immediately implies

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,X_{k})\big)^{2}\bigg]\lesssim n\zeta(s,t),

while, using the assumption (
1.14
) and that
t^{\frac{2}{\log(n)}}\lesssim 1
,

(3.35)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq n}\mathbb{E}[\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,X_{\ell})\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,X_{\ell^{\prime}})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq n}\mathbb{E}[\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,X_{\ell})\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,X_{\ell^{\prime}})]^{1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}\mathbb{E}[\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,X_{\ell})\omega_{s+t}(\cdot,X_{\ell^{\prime}})]^{\frac{1}{\log(n)}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{1\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq n}\alpha^{\frac{1}{\log(n)}}_{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[|\omega_{s+t}(x,X_{1})|^{2}]^{1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}\big(\sup_{y\in\mathcal{M}}|\omega_{s+t}(x,y)|\big)^{\frac{2}{\log(n)}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Eq53},\eqref{DecayAlphaMixing}}}{{\lesssim}}n\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)s^{\frac{1}{2\log(n)}}\zeta(s,t)^{1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}.

It remains to prove (
3.34
) and we start with the first item. Here, we use the fact that

(3.36)

\|(-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\quad\text{for all $f\in\text{C}^{2}$ and uniformly in $s$.}

This can be seen from (
3.10
): Using that
\mathrm{P}_{\frac{s}{2}}
is an auto-adjoint operator in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
and that from (
3.19
) we learn
\mathrm{P}_{\frac{s}{2}}\phi_{n}=e^{-\lambda_{n}\frac{s}{2}}\phi_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\|(-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}}f\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}=\sum_{n}(s\lambda_{n})|\big(\phi_{n},\mathrm{P}_{\frac{s}{2}}f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}|^{2}=\sum_{n}(s\lambda_{n})e^{-s\lambda_{n}}|\big(\phi_{n},f\big)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}|^{2}\lesssim\|f\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}.

Hence, using the semi-group property
\text{P}_{s+t}=\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}}\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}+t}
, we deduce

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\omega_{s+t}(x,X_{1})|^{2}]\lesssim\|(-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{s+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|(-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}}\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{UniformContinuityL2}}}{{\lesssim}}\|\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}.

We now bound
\|\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}
in two different ways. First, using the bounds (
3.3
) of the heat-kernel, we have by the triangle inequality

\|\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\lesssim\|p_{\frac{s}{2}+t}(\cdot,y)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Eq28}}}{{\lesssim}}(s+t)^{-1},

yielding to the first alternative in the first item of (
3.34
). Second, applying Poincaré’s inequality yields

\|\text{P}_{\frac{s}{2}+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\lesssim\|\nabla p_{\frac{s}{2}+t}(\cdot,y)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Eq28}}}{{\lesssim}}(s+t)^{-2},

yielding to the second alternative in the first item of (
3.34
).

We now turn to the second item of (
3.34
). Here, we make use of the representation formula (
3.16
) applied to
f=\mathrm{P}_{s+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho)
. Using the semi-group property
\text{P}_{\tau}\text{P}_{s+t}=\text{P}_{\tau+s+t}
, this takes the form

\omega_{s+t}(x,y)=\sqrt{\frac{s}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta\text{P}_{\tau+s+t}(\delta_{y}-\rho)(x)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

A direct application of the heat-kernel bounds (
3.3
) leads to

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\mathcal{M}}|\omega_{s+t}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\tau+s+t)^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\lesssim\sqrt{s}\,(s+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}},

which is the first alternative in the second item of (
3.34
). For the second alternative, we write

) of
v^{2}
. We use the assumption (
1.14
) together with the two first items of (
3.59
) in form of

\displaystyle\sum_{k>\ell}|\mathbb{E}[\partial^{2}_{ij}\omega(x,X_{i})\partial^{2}_{ij}\omega(x,X_{j})]|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>\ell}|\mathbb{E}[\partial^{2}_{ij}\omega(x,X_{\ell})\partial^{2}_{ij}\omega(x,X_{k})]|^{\frac{1}{\log(n)}}|\mathbb{E}[\partial^{2}_{ij}\omega(x,X_{\ell})\partial^{2}_{ij}\omega(x,X_{k})]|^{1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k>\ell}\alpha^{\frac{1}{\log(n)}}_{k-\ell}(\sup_{y\in\mathcal{M}}|\partial^{2}_{ij}\omega(x,y)|)^{\frac{2}{\log(n)}}(\mathbb{E}[|\partial^{2}_{ij}\omega(x,X_{1})|^{2}])^{1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Eq58},\eqref{DecayAlphaMixing}}}{{\lesssim}}\delta^{-4(1-\frac{1}{\log(n)})}t^{-1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\exp(-b\tfrac{k^{\eta}}{\log(n)})

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{\frac{4}{\log(n)}}t^{-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)\delta^{-4}t^{-1},

which concludes since
\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}\delta^{\frac{4}{\log(n)}}t^{-\frac{1}{\log(n)}}\lesssim 1
.

3.4.
Contractivity estimates

This section is devoted to the control of the smoothing errors
W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})
and
W^{2}_{2}(\nu^{m,t},\nu^{m})
for the particular choice of
t
given in Proposition

. The first result is in the spirit of
[

, Theorem 5.2]
that we extend in the case of non-uniformly distributed and correlated points. This extension requires a finer analysis of the error and the proof relies on Berry-Esseen type inequalities in the spirit of
[
12
, Theorem 5]
.

Proposition 3.5

(Semigroup contraction for empirical measures)
.

Let
\{\mu^{n}\}_{n}
be defined in (
1.2
) with point clouds satisfying Assumption
1.1
. Given
t
such that Proposition
3.4
holds, we have

(3.72)

W_{2}^{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})\leq\mathcal{C}_{n}\frac{\log\log(n)}{n}+t\big\|\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}},

for some random variable
\mathcal{C}_{n}
satisfying for
C<\infty

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}[\tfrac{1}{C}\mathcal{C}_{n}]\leq 1.

Furthermore, if (
1.14
) holds with
\eta\geq 1
then the assumption (
1.13
) can be dropped and the stochastic integrability can be improved up to losing a
\log(n)
factor, namely

(3.73)

W_{2}^{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})\leq\mathcal{D}_{n}\frac{\log^{\frac{1}{\eta}}(n)\log\log(n)}{n}+t\big\|\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}},

for some random variable
\mathcal{D}_{n}
satisfying for
D<\infty

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\tfrac{1}{D}\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{n})\big]\leq 2.

Proof.

According to the fluctuation estimates in Proposition
3.4
together with
W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})\leq(\text{diam}(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, we can restrict the analysis in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
defined in (
3.43
). Note that for
n
large enough, (
1.6
) yields

(3.74)

\frac{\lambda}{2}\leq\mu^{n,t}\leq\Lambda+1\quad\text{in $\mathcal{A}_{n}$.}

We split the proof into three steps. In the first step, we prove a Berry–Esseen type smoothing inequality for
W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})
which decomposes the error in a deterministic part involving
\rho
and a random part involving the Fourier coefficients
\{\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)\}_{k}
of
\mu^{n}
. In the second step, we prove (
3.72
). In the third step, we control the fluctuations of
\{\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)\}_{k}
using the concentration inequalities in Proposition

and deduce (
3.73
).

Step 1. Berry-Esseen type inequality.
Recalling that we denote by
\{\lambda_{k},\phi_{k}\}_{k}
the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
respectively, we prove that

(3.75)

W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})\lesssim\frac{1}{n}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e^{-\frac{2}{n}\lambda_{k}}}{\lambda_{k}}\big(e^{-t\lambda_{k}}-1\big)^{2}|\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)-\widehat{\rho}(k)|^{2}+t\|\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}},

where

\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\rho}(k):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho\,\phi_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}.

We first apply the triangle inequality and use the classical contractivity estimate in
[
22
, Theorem 3]
to get

(3.76)

W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t},\mu^{n})\lesssim W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}},\mu^{n})+W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}},\mu^{n,t})+W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}},\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}})\lesssim\frac{1}{n}+W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}},\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}}).

We then apply Peyre’s estimate
[
46
]
to the second r. h. s. , which takes the form

(3.77)

W^{2}_{2}(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}},\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}})\leq 4\sup\bigg\{\Big|\int_{\mathcal{M}}(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}-\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}})\,f\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big|^{2}\quad\text{with}\quad\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}\,|\nabla f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\leq 1\bigg\}.

Now, given an arbitrary
f
such that

(3.78)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}\,|\nabla f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\leq 1,

we split

(3.79)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}-\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}})\,f\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\big(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}-\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}}-(\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}})\big)\,f\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}.

For the first r. h. s. term of (
3.79
), we expand the integral using (
3.4
). Thus, together with the semigroup property of
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t>0}
and Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}\big(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}-\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}}-(\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}})\big)\,f\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\big(\mu^{n,t}-\mu^{n}-(\rho_{t}-\rho)\big)(y)\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}(x)\,f(x)p_{\frac{1}{n}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{-\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\big(\mu^{n,t}-\mu^{n}-(\rho_{t}-\rho)\big)(y)\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}(x)\,f(x)\phi_{k}(x)\phi_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{-\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}}\widehat{f}(k)\big(\widehat{\mu^{n,t}}(k)-\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)-(\widehat{\rho_{t}}(k)-\widehat{\rho}(k))\big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e^{-\frac{2}{n}\lambda_{k}}}{\lambda_{k}}|\widehat{\mu^{n,t}}(k)-\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)-(\widehat{\rho_{t}}(k)-\widehat{\rho}(k))|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

Using that from (
3.74
) we have
\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}
and recalling (
3.78
), we get

\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}\,|\nabla f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}.

Furthermore, noticing that
\mathrm{P}_{t}\phi_{k}=e^{-t\lambda_{k}}\phi_{k}
which implies that, since
\mathrm{P}_{t}
is self-adjoint

\widehat{\mu^{n,t}}(k)=e^{-t\lambda_{k}}\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\rho_{t}}(k)=e^{-t\lambda_{k}}\widehat{\rho}(k),

we obtain

\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e^{-\frac{2}{n}\lambda_{k}}}{\lambda_{k}}|\widehat{\mu^{n,t}}(k)-\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)-(\widehat{\rho_{t}}(k)-\widehat{\rho}(k))|^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e^{-\frac{2}{n}\lambda_{k}}}{\lambda_{k}}\big(e^{-t\lambda_{k}}-1\big)^{2}|\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)-\widehat{\rho}(k)|^{2}.

This leads to

(3.80)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\big(\mu^{n,t+\frac{1}{n}}-\mu^{n,\frac{1}{n}}-(\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}})\big)\,f\lesssim\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e^{-\frac{2}{n}\lambda_{k}}}{\lambda_{k}}\big(e^{-t\lambda_{k}}-1\big)^{2}|\widehat{\mu^{n}}(k)-\widehat{\rho}(k)|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For the second r. h. s. of (
3.79
), we introduce
w\in\dot{{\rm H}}^{1}
satisfying

-\Delta w=\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}},

so that from an integration by parts, Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality and the combination of (
3.74
) and (
3.78
), we obtain

\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\nabla f\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\lesssim\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla w|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Using then the explicit formula
w=-\int_{\frac{1}{n}}^{t+\frac{1}{n}}\rho_{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau
together with
|\int_{\frac{1}{n}}^{t+\frac{1}{n}}\rho_{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau|\leq\Lambda t
, we get

\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla w|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}=\int(\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}})w\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\lesssim t\|\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}},

so that

(3.81)

\Big|\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\Big|\lesssim\sqrt{t}\|\rho_{t+\frac{1}{n}}-\rho_{\frac{1}{n}}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\mathrm{L}^{1}}.

The combination of (
3.79
), (
3.80
), (
3.81
) and (

) leads to (

).

Step 1. Preliminary estimates.

Heat kernel regularization.
The assumption

Step 1. Markov chains case.

where we recall that

. Thus, using in addition (

\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}}}{\lambda_{k}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(\phi_{k}(X_{\ell})-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{k}(X_{\ell})])\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{-\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:traceformula}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{m}\,p_{\frac{1}{n}}(x,x)\lesssim\frac{1}{n}.

Appendix C

We provide in this Section the arguments for extending Theorem
1.2

1.4

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(\mathbb{E}[(-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}p_{s+t}(x,X_{\ell})]-\mu_{\infty}((-s\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}p_{s+t}(x,\cdot))
