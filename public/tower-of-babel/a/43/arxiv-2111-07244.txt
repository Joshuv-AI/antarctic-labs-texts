Abstract
We consider the Vector Scheduling problem on identical machines: we have m m machines, and a set J J of n n jobs, where each job j j has a processing-time vector p j ∈ ℝ ≥ 0 d p_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d} . The goal is to find an assignment σ : J → [ m ] {\sigma}:J\to[m] of jobs to machines so as to minimize the makespan max i ∈ [ m ] max r ∈ [ d ] ( ∑ j : σ ⁡ ( j ) = i p j , r ) \max_{i\in[m]}\max_{r\in[d]}\bigl(\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}p_{j,r}\bigr) . A natural lower bound on the optimal makespan is 𝑙𝑏 := max ⁡ { max j ∈ J , r ∈ [ d ] ⁡ p j , r , max r ∈ [ d ] ⁡ ( ∑ j ∈ J p j , r / m ) } \mathit{lb}:=\max\{\max_{j\in J,r\in[d]}p_{j,r},\max_{r\in[d]}(\sum_{j\in J}p_{j,r}/m)\} . Our main result is a very simple O ⁡ ( log ⁡ d ) O(\log d) -approximation algorithm for vector scheduling with respect to the lower bound 𝑙𝑏 \mathit{lb} : we devise an algorithm that returns an assignment whose makespan is at most O ⁡ ( log ⁡ d ) ⋅ 𝑙𝑏 O(\log d)\cdot\mathit{lb} . As an application, we show that the above guarantee leads to an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ m ) O(\log\log m) -approximation for stochastic minimum-norm load balancing ( 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝖼𝗁𝖭𝗈𝗋𝗆𝖫𝖡 \mathsf{StochNormLB} ). In 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝖼𝗁𝖭𝗈𝗋𝗆𝖫𝖡 \mathsf{StochNormLB} , we have m m identical machines, a set J J of n n independent stochastic jobs whose processing times are nonnegative random variables, and a monotone, symmetric norm f : ℝ m → ℝ ≥ 0 f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} . The goal is to find an assignment σ : J → [ m ] {\sigma}:J\to[m] that minimizes the expected f f -norm of the machine-load vector 𝗅𝗈𝖺𝖽 → σ \overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}} , where the i th i^{\mathrm{th}} coordinate of 𝗅𝗈𝖺𝖽 → σ \overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}} is the (random) total processing time assigned to machine i i . Our O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ m ) O(\log\log m) -approximation guarantee is in fact much stronger: we obtain an assignment that is simultaneously an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ m ) O(\log\log m) -approximation for 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝖼𝗁𝖭𝗈𝗋𝗆𝖫𝖡 \mathsf{StochNormLB} with all monotone, symmetric norms. Next, this approximation factor significantly improves upon the O ⁡ ( log ⁡ m / log ⁡ log ​ m ) O(\log m/\log\log m) -approximation in (Ibrahimpur and Swamy, FOCS 2020) for 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝖼𝗁𝖭𝗈𝗋𝗆𝖫𝖡 \mathsf{StochNormLB} , and is a consequence of a more-general black-box reduction that we present, showing that a γ ⁡ ( d ) \gamma(d) -approximation for d d -dimensional vector scheduling with respect to the lower bound 𝑙𝑏 \mathit{lb} yields a simultaneous γ ⁡ ( log ⁡ m ) \gamma(\log m) -approximation for 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝖼𝗁𝖭𝗈𝗋𝗆𝖫𝖡 \mathsf{StochNormLB} with all monotone, symmetric norms. We emphasize that it is crucial for this reduction that the approximation guarantee for vector scheduling is with respect to the lower bound 𝑙𝑏 \mathit{lb} .

Introduction

We consider the well-studied
Vector Scheduling
problem on identical machines, a natural generalization of the (scalar) makespan minimization problem to a setting with
d\geq 1
dimensions.
We have a set
J
of
n
jobs that are to be processed on exactly one of
m
identical machines.
We use
[N]
to denote
\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Each job
j\in J
has a processing-time (or size)
vector

p_{j}=(p_{j,1},\dots,p_{j,d})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
, where
p_{j,r}
denotes the size of job
j
in dimension
r\in[d]
; for example, dimensions could correspond to processor cycles, storage space, or network bandwidth needed to complete the job.
An assignment
{\sigma}:J\to[m]
of jobs to machines induces an
(m\times d)
-dimensional load vector
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}
(one entry per machine-dimension pair): for machine
i\in[m]
and dimension
r\in[d]
, the load in the
r^{\mathrm{th}}
dimension of machine
i
is
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}_{i,r}:=\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}p_{j,r}
.
The
makespan objective
of an assignment
{\sigma}
is defined as
\max_{i\in[m],r\in[d]}\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}_{i,r}
i.e., the maximum load across all machines and all dimensions.
The goal in vector scheduling is to find an assignment
{\sigma}
that minimizes the makespan.

Vector scheduling (a.k.a. vector load balancing) was first considered by Chekuri and Khanna
[
4
]
who gave an
O(\log^{2}d)
-approximation algorithm for the problem.
Meyerson, Roytman and Tagiku in
[
16
]
improved the approximation guarantee to a factor
O(\log d)
; in fact, they gave an
O(\log d)
-competitive algorithm for the online version of the problem where jobs arrive one at a time and have to be assigned irrevocably to a machine on arrival.
The current best approximation for the problem is an
O(\log d/\log\log d)
-competitive algorithm by Im, Kell, Kulkarni and Panigrahi
[
14
]
.
In terms of hardness, very recently, Sai Sandeep
[
18
]
showed that, for any
\epsilon>0
, (offline) vector scheduling is hard to approximate to a factor
O((\log d)^{1-\epsilon})
under some complexity theoretic assumptions.

A natural lower bound on the optimal makespan in vector scheduling is given by:

\mathit{lb}:=\max\left\{\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{job }j\in J\\
\text{dimension }r\in[d]\end{subarray}}p_{j,r},\,\quad\frac{1}{m}\cdot\max_{\text{dimension }r\in[d]}\sum_{\text{job }j\in J}p_{j,r}\right\}.

(1)

The first term above arises because each job has to assigned to some machine, and the second term arises because in each dimension
r
, a total load of
\sum_{j}p_{j,r}
is distributed across
m
machines.

Motivated by applications in stochastic load balancing,
the main question that initiated this work is: how well can we approximate vector
scheduling with respect to the natural lower bound
\mathit{lb}
?

We devise a simple
\mathit{lb}
-relative
randomized
O(\log d)
-approximation algorithm
(see Theorem
1.1
).

We make a few remarks comparing this result with some of of the (previously mentioned)
prior work on vector scheduling. The
O(\log d)
approximation guarantee of Meyerson et
al.
[
16
]
is also with respect to
\mathit{lb}
, although it is not explicitly
stated in this form.
While their guarantee is deterministic and holds also in the online setting,
our chief notable feature is the simplicity of our algorithm and analysis.

The algorithm of Meyerson et al. is also fairly simple—it assigns the
recently-arrived job to the machine that leads to the least increase in an exponential
potential function—but the analysis is slightly involved.

From a pure approximation-standpoint, the
O(\log d/\log\log d)
-approximation of

[
14
]
is of course better, but from the description of their algorithm it is unclear if their guarantee holds
with respect to the lower bound
\mathit{lb}
; also, their algorithm and analysis are
significantly more involved.
Finally, we note that the hardness result in
[
18
]
essentially shows that our approximation guarantee is tight up to
\operatorname{poly}(\log\log d)
factors.

While the simplicity and rather evident nature of the lower bound
\mathit{lb}
make it appealing
to design
\mathit{lb}
-relative approximation algorithms for vector scheduling,
there are also other benefits of working with this lower bound.
As an application, we show how our results on vector scheduling lead to useful approximation algorithms for a problem in stochastic load balancing that has recently received renewed interest.
In the stochastic load balancing model that we consider, job sizes are (scalar) random variables with known distributions, and we have
m
identical machines.
That is, the size of a job
j\in J
is distributed as a nonnegative random variable
X_{j}
.
Throughout this paper, we assume that the job random variables are independent of each other.
An assignment
{\sigma}:J\to[m]
induces a random
m
-dimensional load vector
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}
, where the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
coordinate of
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}
is the (random) total processing time assigned to machine
i\in[m]
i.e.,
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}_{i}:=\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}X_{j}
.
In the
stochastic minimum norm load balancing
(
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
) problem, we have
n
stochastic jobs
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in J}
,
m
identical machines, and a monotone symmetric norm
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Recall that
f
being a norm means that: (i)
f(x)=0
if and only if
x=0
; (ii)
f(\theta x)=|\theta|f(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\theta\in\mathbb{R}
; and (iii)
f(x+y)\leq f(x)+f(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
A
monotone
norm
f
satisfies
f(x)\leq f(y)
for all
0\leq x\leq y
(coordinate-wise inequality), and
symmetry
is the property that permuting the coordinates of
x
does not change its norm.
The goal in
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
is to find an assignment
{\sigma}
that minimizes
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[f(\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}})]
— the expected
f
-norm of the induced load vector — where the expectation is over the randomness in the job-size distributions.
We emphasize that all jobs are assigned to machines up front without the knowledge of the job-size realizations.
We sometimes use
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
f
-
\mathsf{LB}
to explicitly indicate the norm
f
that we are considering in an instance of
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
.

Kleinberg, Rabani and Tardos
[
15
]
were the first to consider the
stochastic makespan minimization
problem (
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
when
f
is the
\ell_{\infty}
norm) and gave an
O(1)
-approximation algorithm.
Subsequent work by
[
6
,
8
,
17
,
2
,
3
,
11
,
5
,
13
]
have focused on obtaining approximation algorithms for various
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
settings: (i) unrelated-machines setting where job-size distributions can depend on the machine; (ii) the norm
f
is an
\ell_{p}
norm or a
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norm (where
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(x)
is defined as the sum of
\ell
largest coordinates of
x
); and (iii) job-sizes distributions are Bernoulli, Exponential, Poisson random variables, or even deterministic (
X_{j}=p_{j}
with probability
1
).
We elaborate on related work in Section
1.2
.
The result most relevant to this work is the
O(\log m/\log\log m)
-approximation algorithm of
[
11
]
for
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
when
f
is an arbitrary monotone symmetric norm and jobs are arbitrarily distributed.
In this work, we give an exponential improvement in the approximation guarantee for
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
when machines are identical, and to do this we crucially use the
\mathit{lb}
-relative approximation algorithm for vector scheduling.

1.1
Our Results and Techniques

We formally state our contributions in this work with a brief overview of the techniques used.
Our first main result is a simple
O(\log d)
-approximation algorithm for vector scheduling where the approximation guarantee is with respect to the natural lower bound.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is a randomized algorithm for the
d
-dimensional vector scheduling problem that computes an assignment
{\sigma}
with makespan at most
O(\log d)\cdot\mathit{lb}
.
The algorithm runs in time polynomial in the input size, and succeeds in finding an approximate assignment with probability at least
2/3
.

Our vector-scheduling algorithm in Theorem
1.1
is extremely simple to implement since it is based on randomly sampling jobs to be processed on a machine. Its analysis is equally simple because it only uses the well-known Chernoff tail bounds.
By scaling job-size vectors, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathit{lb}=1
.
In particular, this implies
p_{j,r}\in[0,1]
for any job
j\in J
and dimension
r\in[d]
, and
\sum_{j}p_{j,r}\leq m
for any
r
.
For simplicity, further assume that
\sum_{j}p_{j,r}=m
for all
r
.
Consider a random job-set
S\subseteq J
where job
j
is independently included in
S
with probability
c\log d/m
for a sufficiently large constant
c\geq 1
.
By our choice of
S
, for any dimension
r\in[d]
, the expected total size of jobs in
S
in that dimension is
c\log d
.
By Chernoff tail bounds, in any dimension
r
, we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Pr}}}[1\leq\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}\leq O(\log d)]\leq 1/\operatorname{poly}(d)
.
By union bound, with some positive probability,
\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}
is in
[1,O(\log d)]
simultaneously for all dimensions
r\in[d]
.
We assign the job-set
S
to one of the
m
machines, and recurse on the residual instance with job-set
J\setminus S
and
m-1
identical machines; note that
\mathit{lb}
for the residual instance is at most
1
.
We formalize the argument in Section
2
.

Our second main result is an
O(\log\log m)
-approximation for
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

We can compute (in polynomial time) an assignment
{\sigma}:J\to[m]
such that, with probability at least
2/3
,
{\sigma}
is an
O(\log\log m)
-approximation to the given instance of
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
.

In fact, we prove a stronger result: we return an assignment that
simultaneously
achieves an
O(\log\log m)
-approximation for
all
monotone, symmetric norms.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider
n
stochastic jobs
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in J}
and
m
identical machines.
We can compute (in polynomial time) an assignment
{\sigma}:J\to[m]
such that, with
probability at least
2/3
,
{\sigma}
is simultaneously an
O(\log\log m)
-approximation to all
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
g
-
\mathsf{LB}
instances where
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a monotone, symmetric norm.
Moreover, given an
\mathit{lb}
-relative
\gamma(d)
-approximation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
d
-dimensional vector scheduling, we can use one call of
\mathcal{A}
to obtain an
O(\gamma(\log m))
simultaneous-approximation for
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
g
-
\mathsf{LB}
for all monotone, symmetric norms
g
.

The randomization above stems from our randomized guarantee for vector scheduling.
We remark that the probability of success in Theorem
1.3
is of a detectable (polytime-verifiable) event (see Remark
1
).
Therefore, we can lower the failure probability to
\delta
, for any
\delta>0
, by repeating the algorithm in Theorem
1.3

O(\log(1/\delta))
times.

At a high level, to prove Theorem
1.3
we utilize two key ideas from
[
11
]
.
First, a technical result from
[
11
]
called as
approximate stochastic majorization
(Theorem
3.2
) gives a reduction from the problem of approximating all monotone symmetric norms to that of approximating only a small collection of
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norms.
Formally, a simultaneous
\alpha
-approximation to all
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
instances with
\ell\in\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{\lfloor\log_{2}m\rfloor}\}
implies a simultaneous
O(\alpha)
-approximation for all
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
instances (see Theorem
3.3
).
Second, we specialize the
O(1)
-approximation algorithm for
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
found in
[
11
]
to the identical-machines setting.
We first note that, modulo some technical assumptions,
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
problem on identical machines is essentially an instance of
1
-dimensional vector scheduling where the (scalar) size of a job

is a certain sophisticated function of its job-size distribution

.
More crucially, we show that an
\mathit{lb}
-relative

-approximate assignment for the
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}

Since the expression of

\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}

\mathsf{LB}

As Theorem

-approximation in Theorems

and

.
We remark that our algorithms for stochastic load balancing applications only require black-box access to

If the vector-scheduling algorithm is deterministic (randomized), then our algorithm for Theorem

is also deterministic (randomized).

Vector Scheduling

Recall that in
d
-dimensional vector scheduling, we have a set
J
of vector jobs and
m
identical machines.
The size-vector of job
j
is
p_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
.
To keep the notation simple, we reserve
i\in[m]
to index the machine-set,
j\in J
to index the job-set, and
r\in[d]
to index the dimensions.
We seek an assignment
{\sigma}:J\to[m]
that minimizes the makespan
\max_{i,r}\bigl(\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}p_{j,r}\bigr)
.
The natural lower bound
\mathit{lb}
on the optimal makespan is
\max\{\max_{j,r}p_{j,r},\max_{r}\sum_{j}p_{j,r}/m\}
(see (
1
)).
By scaling, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathit{lb}=1
; this implies

p_{j,r}\in[0,1]
for all
j,r
, and
\sum_{j}p_{j,r}\leq m
for all
r
.

We prove our main result on vector scheduling (Theorem
1.1
) by proving a slightly stronger result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider an instance of vector scheduling with
p_{j,r}\in[0,1]
for all
j\in J,r\in[d]
, and
\sum_{j\in J}p_{j,r}\leq m\log d
for all
r\in[d]
.
There is a randomized algorithm that produces an assignment
{\sigma}
whose makespan is
O(\log d)
.
The algorithm succeeds with probability at least
2/3
.

The only tool that we use in the proof of Theorem
2.1
is Chernoff tail bounds, which we state below.

Lemma 2.2

(Chernoff Bounds)
.

Let
\{Z_{j}\}
be a finite collection of independent
[0,1]
-bounded random variables, and let
S=\sum_{j}Z_{j}
denote their sum.
Then,

1.

for any
\mu\geq{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[S]
and
\delta\geq 0
, we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Pr}}}[S\geq(1+\delta)\mu]\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{2+\delta}\bigr)
.

2.

for any
\mu\leq{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[S]
and
\delta\in[0,1]
, we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Pr}}}[S\leq(1-\delta)\mu]\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{2}\bigr)
.

As mentioned in Section
1.1
, our algorithm for vector scheduling is based on finding a subset of jobs
S\subseteq J
that can all be processed on a single machine by incurring a load of at most
O(\log d)
in each dimension
r\in[d]
, while ensuring that the residual problem is also a valid sub-instance of the problem with
m-1
machines.
We formalize the argument below.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
m\geq 7
and
d\geq 2
.
Consider a random job-set
S\subseteq J
where job
j\in J
is independently included in
S
with probability
q:=7/m
.
With probability at least
1/2
, for all
r\in[d]
we have
\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}\leq 14\log d
and
\sum_{j\in J\setminus S}p_{j,r}\leq(m-1)\log d
.

Proof.

The proof is based on a straightforward application of Chernoff bounds.
By our choice of
q
, for any
r
we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}]\leq 7\log d
.
Using Lemma
2.2
(a) with
\delta=1
, for any
r
we have:

{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Pr}}}\bigl[\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}\geq 14\log d\bigr]\leq\exp\bigl(\frac{-7\log d}{3}\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{d^{2}}.

Next, we use Chernoff bounds for the lower tail to prove the remaining size-bound on jobs in
J\setminus S
.
Let
B:=\{r\in[d]:\sum_{j\in J}p_{j,r}>(m-1)\log d\}
.
Since
m\geq 7
, for any
r\in B
we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}]\geq(1-1/m)\cdot 7\log d\geq 6\log d
.
Using Lemma
2.2
(b) with
\delta=5/6
we get:

{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Pr}}}[\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}\leq\log d]\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{25}{36}\cdot\frac{6\log d}{2}\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{d^{2}}.

By union-bound, with probability at least
1/2
, we have
\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}\leq 14\log d
for all
r\in[d]
, and
\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}\geq\log d
for all
r\in B
.
The desired bounds follow.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We call an instance of vector scheduling as a
valid instance with
m
machines
if
p_{j,r}\in[0,1]
for all
j,r
, and
\sum_{j}p_{j,r}\leq m\log d
for all
r
.
Note that in Theorem
2.1
we are given a valid instance with
m
machines.
We will assume that
d\geq 2
, since otherwise a greedy list-scheduling algorithm gives
an easy
2
-approximation.

Let
N:=\lceil\log_{2}(3m)\rceil=O(\log m)
.
We now describe a randomized algorithm for vector scheduling.
The overall procedure has at most
m
iterations, and if the algorithm is successful, it produces an assignment with makespan
O(\log d)
.
Consider the start of an iteration
t
for some
t\geq 1
.
We have a valid instance with
m-t+1
identical machines.
If
m-t+1=6
, then we simply assign all remaining jobs to a single machine; note that
\sum_{j}p_{j,r}\leq 6\log d
for all dimensions
r
, so we are not assigning too much load to this machine in any dimension.
Otherwise,
m-t+1\geq 7
.
Consider random subsets
S_{1},\dots,S_{N}
where each
S_{\ell}\subseteq J,\ell\in[N]
is obtained by independently including job
j
in
S_{\ell}
with probability
q:=7/m
.
By Lemma
2.3
, with probability at most
2^{-N}\leq\frac{1}{3m}
,
none of the
S_{\ell}
sets satisfy the conclusion of the lemma; in this case we terminate
the algorithm with a failure.
Otherwise, for some
\ell\in[N]
, we have for all
r
,
\sum_{j\in S_{\ell}}p_{j,r}\leq 14\log d
and
\sum_{j\in J\setminus S_{\ell}}p_{j,r}\leq(m-t)\log d
.
In this case, we assign all jobs in
S_{\ell}
to one machine and end the iteration with a valid instance with
m-t
machines and residual job-set
J\setminus S_{\ell}
.
The probability that the algorithm terminates with a failure in any given iteration is at most
1/3m
, so the overall failure probability of the algorithm is at most
1/3
.
In other words, with probability at least
2/3
, we obtain an assignment whose makespan is
at most
14\log d
.
∎

We remark that if we assume
\sum_{j}p_{j,r}\leq m\cdot U
for all dimensions
r
in the
statement of Theorem
2.1
(where
U
does not depend on
m
), then we can
easily adapt the above arguments to obtain makespan
O(\max(\log d,U))
.
Suppose
U>\log d
.
Sampling jobs as before with probability
7/m
now ensures that the random subset

S
satisfies

{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Pr}}}\bigl[\sum_{j\in S}p_{j,r}\leq 14U,\ \sum_{j\in J\setminus S}p_{j,r}\leq(m-1)U\bigr]\geq 1-\frac{2}{d^{2}}
,
for
m\geq 7
.
Given this, we proceed exactly as in Theorem
2.1
, where a valid instance
with
m
machines now means that the total load in each dimension
r
is at most
m\cdot U
.

3
Stochastic Minimum-Norm Load Balancing

We now utilize our results on vector scheduling to derive approximation algorithms for
stochastic minimum norm load balancing
(
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
), crucially leveraging the fact that our approximation guarantee for vector scheduling is with respect to the natural lower bound
\mathit{lb}
(see (
1
)).

Recall that in
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
we have a set
J
of
n
independent stochastic jobs and
m
identical machines.
The processing time of a job
j
is a nonnegative random variable
X_{j}
whose distribution is given to us. (We will only however need a certain kind of access to these distributions.)
We are also given a monotone symmetric norm
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Our goal in
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
is to find an assignment
{\sigma}
that minimizes
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[f(\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}})]
, where
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}_{i}:=\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}X_{j}
for
i\in[m]
.
Also recall that we use
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
g
-
\mathsf{LB}
to explicitly indicate the norm
g
that we are considering in an instance of
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
.

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
by giving a simultaneous
O(\log\log m)
-approximation for all
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
g
-
\mathsf{LB}
instances where
g
is a monotone, symmetric norm.
Theorem
1.2
immediately follows from Theorem
1.3
.
The proof of Theorem
1.3
is based on a reduction to vector
scheduling with
O(\log m)
-dimensional vectors, by utilizing suitable tools from prior work
[
15
,
11
]
.
We first discuss these relevant tools, and show in Section
3.2
how they lead to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
We first introduce some notation.
For a nonnegative random variable
Z
and a scalar
\theta
, we define the
truncated random variable

Z^{<\theta}:=Z\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{Z<\theta}
as the random variable that takes size
Z
when the event
\{Z<\theta\}
happens, and size
0
otherwise.
We also define the
exceptional random variable

Z^{\geq\theta}:=Z\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{Z\geq\theta}
as the random variable that takes size
Z
when the event
\{Z\geq\theta\}
happens, and size
0
otherwise.
Note that
Z=Z^{<\theta}+Z^{\geq\theta}
.
We reserve
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
to denote an arbitrary monotone symmetric norm and
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})
to denote an arbitrary
m
-dimensional random vector whose coordinates
\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
form an independent collection of nonnegative random variables.
For brevity, we will say that
Y
follows a product distribution on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
.
Observe that load vectors
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}
that arise in
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
instances with
m
machines follow a product distribution on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
.

Let
\mathsf{POS}=\mathsf{POS}(m):=\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{\lfloor\log_{2}m\rfloor}\}
.
The first tool that we utilize shows importantly that we can control
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[g(Y)]
by controlling a collection of simpler norms called
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norms (Theorem
3.2
).

Definition 3.1

(
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norm)
.

For any
\ell\in[m]
, the
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norm
is defined as follows: for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
,
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(x)
is the sum of the
\ell
largest coordinates of
x
, i.e.,
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}x^{\downarrow}_{i}
, where
x^{\downarrow}
denotes the vector
x
with its coordinates sorted in non-increasing order.

Like
\ell_{p}
norms,
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norms give us a way to interpolate between the
\mathrm{max}
-norm (
\mathsf{Top}_{1}
) and the
\mathrm{sum}
-norm (
\mathsf{Top}_{m}
).
A well-known mathematical result called the
majorization inequality

[
9
]
(see also
[
2
]
) shows that controlling all
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norms gives us a handle on all monotone symmetric norms: for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, if
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(x)\leq\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(y)
for all
\ell\in[m]
, then
g(x)\leq g(y)
for every monotone, symmetric norm
g
.
Recently, in
[
11
]
, we proved a substantial stochastic generalization
(qualitatively speaking) of this result.

Theorem 3.2

(Approximate Stochastic Majorization, Theorem 5.2 in
[
12
]
)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})
and
W=(W_{1},\dots,W_{m})
follow product distributions on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, and let
\alpha
be a positive scalar.

1.

If
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]\leq\alpha{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(W)]
for all
\ell\in[m]
, then
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[g(Y)]\leq 28\cdot\alpha{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[g(W)]
.

2.

If
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]\leq\alpha{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(W)]
for all
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}(m)
, then

{{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[g(Y)]\leq 56\cdot\alpha{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[g(W)]}
.

By Theorem
3.2
(ii), the task of proving Theorem
1.3
reduces to proving the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider
n
stochastic jobs
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in J}
and
m
identical machines.
With probability at least
2/3
, we can obtain an assignment
{\sigma}:J\to[m]
that is simultaneously an
O(\log\log m)
-approximation to all
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
instances with
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
.

We prove Theorem
3.3
using an approximation algorithm for

|\mathsf{POS}|
-dimensional vector scheduling
with respect to the natural lower bound
\mathit{lb}
.
Since
|\mathsf{POS}|=O(\log m)
, and Section
2
presents such an

O(\log d)
-approximation algorithm for
d
-dimensional vector scheduling, we obtain the desired result.

3.1
Handling a single
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norm

We next discuss how to tackle stochastic load balancing with a single
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norm.
Combining the ingredients here for all
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
will yield our reduction to
|\mathsf{POS}|
-dimensional vector scheduling (see Section
3.2
).

We fix
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
throughout this subsection.
In
[
11
]
, the authors give an
O(1)
-approximation for
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
, even in the more-general setting of unrelated machines via an LP-based algorithm, but for identical machines, the underlying arguments can be substantially simplified.
In particular,
[
11
]
show that,
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]
can be approximated via a separable expression (in the
Y_{i}
s) when
Y
follows a product distribution
(Theorem
3.4
), and show how one can deal with this proxy expression by
generalizing certain arguments from
[
15
]
.
Mimicking the proof strategy in
[
15
]
, which gives an
O(1)
-approximation for
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{1}
-
\mathsf{LB}
, then shows that
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
can
be reduced to (scalar) makespan minimization (i.e.,
1
-dimensional vector scheduling).
The following technical theorem gives a handle on
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]
when
Y
follows a product distribution on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\theta
be a positive scalar.

1.

(Lemma 4.1 in
[
12
]
)
If
\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]\leq\ell\theta
, then
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]\leq 2\ell\theta
.

2.

(Lemma 4.2 in
[
12
]
)
If
\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]>\ell\theta
, then
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]>\ell\theta/2
.

The upshot of Theorem
3.4
is that if
\theta
is such that
\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]
is roughly
\ell\theta
, then the
separable expression

\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]
is a constant-factor proxy for

{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]
.
In
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
, we have
Y_{i}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}_{i}
(where
{\sigma}:J\mapsto[m]
is the job-assignment),
which is a sum of independent random variables, and we next need to address how to obtain
a handle on
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]
.
Similar to
[
15
,
8
,
11
]
, given the “scale”
\theta
, we
can decompose any instance of
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
into an “easy” sub-instance, for which
it is trivial to bound
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]
and any assignment yields an

O(1)
-approximation, and a (more) “difficult” sub-instance, where we need some
technical results to control
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]
and obtain an
O(1)
-approximation.
Lemmas
3.5
and
3.7
indicate how one may define these two subinstances.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\theta
be a positive scalar.
Consider an instance of
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
where for each job
j
, the distribution of
X_{j}
is supported on
\{0\}\cup[\theta,\infty)
i.e.,
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Pr}}}[0<X_{j}<\theta]=0
.

1.

If
\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}]\leq\ell\theta
, then for any assignment
{\sigma}
, we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}})]\leq 2\ell\theta
.

2.

If
\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}]>\ell\theta
, then for any assignment
{\sigma}
, we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}})]>\ell\theta/2
.

Proof.

Fix an assignment
{\sigma}
and let
Y:=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}
denote the induced random load vector.
By Theorem
3.4
, it suffices to argue that
\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]=\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}]
.
Since the distribution of each
X_{j}
is supported on
\{0\}\cup[\theta,\infty)
, the distribution of the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
machine’s load
Y_{i}=\sum_{j\in J:{\sigma}(j)=i}X_{j}
is also supported on
\{0\}\cup[\theta,\infty)
.
Thus,

\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]=\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}]=\sum_{i\in[m]}\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}]=\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}],

and so both parts follow.
∎

Thus, the difficulty in bounding
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\theta}]
(where

Y_{i}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}_{i}
) arises when the
X_{j}
job random variables are supported on
[0,\theta)
.
We need the notion of
effective size
to deal with such difficult
instances.

Definition 3.6

(Effective Size)
.

For a nonnegative random variable
Z
and a scalar parameter
\lambda>1
, the
\lambda
-effective size
\beta_{\lambda}(Z)
of
Z
is defined as
\log_{\lambda}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\lambda^{Z}]
.
We define
\beta_{1}(Z)
to be
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Z]
.

The benefit of effective sizes follows because Lemmas
A.1

and
A.2
(from
[
11
]
) show that, for
independent random variables
\{Z_{j}\}
, we can control

{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\bigl(\sum_{j}Z_{j}^{<\theta}\bigr)^{\geq\theta}]
by bounding the
effective sizes of certain random variables related to the
Z_{j}^{<\theta}
random
variables.
We leverage this to obtain the following result, which is implicit in
[
11
]

and is a generalization of Lemma 3.4 from
[
15
]
;
for completeness, we present the proof in Appendix
A
,
since this statement does not explicitly appear in prior work.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\theta
be a positive scalar and let
\lambda:=\lfloor\frac{2m}{\ell}\rfloor
.
Consider an instance of stochastic
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-norm load balancing where for each job
j\in J
, the distribution of
X_{j}
is supported on
[0,\theta)
i.e.,
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Pr}}}[X_{j}\geq\theta]=0
.

1.

If
\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda}(X_{j}/4\theta)\leq 8m
, then for any assignment
{\sigma}
we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}})]\leq 32\cdot\alpha\cdot\ell\theta
, where
\alpha:=\max\{1,\max_{i\in[m]}\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}\beta_{\lambda}(X_{j}/4\theta)\}
.

2.

If
\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda}(X_{j}/4\theta)>8m
, then for any assignment
{\sigma}
we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}})]>\ell\theta/2
.

Lemmas
3.5
and
3.7
yield the following approach for
obtaining a constant-factor approximation for
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
.
Let
\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}
denote the optimal value.
For a given scalar
\theta
, “split” each job
j
as
X_{j}=X_{j}^{<\theta}+X_{j}^{\geq\theta}
.
This yields the
exceptional sub-instance
with the exceptional job-variables

\{X_{j}^{\geq\theta}\}_{j}
, and the
truncated sub-instance
with the truncated
job-variables
\{X_{j}^{<\theta}\}_{j}
.
Clearly, for any assignment
{\sigma}
, if
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
denote the respective load vectors in these two sub-instances, we have
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}=Y+Y^{\prime}
.

Let
\lambda=\lfloor\frac{2m}{\ell}\rfloor
.
Now, we can use binary search to find the
right
threshold
t_{\ell}
, such that for

\theta=t_{\ell}
, the conditions in part (i) of Lemmas
3.5

and
3.7
hold, and for some
\theta\geq t_{\ell}/(1+\epsilon)
, the opposite is
true.
This is because Lemmas
3.5
and
3.7
imply that for
\theta

large enough, the conditions in part (i) of these lemmas hold; we flesh out the
binary-search interval at the end of Section
3.2
.
So we have:
(1)
\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}^{\geq t_{\ell}}]\leq\ell t_{\ell}
and

\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda}(X_{j}^{<t_{\ell}}/4t_{\ell})\leq 8m
; and
(2) for some
\theta\geq t_{\ell}/(1+\epsilon)
,
we have
\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}^{\geq\theta}]>\ell\theta
or

\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda}(X_{j}^{<\theta}/4\theta)>8m
.
Due to (2), we obtain that the optimum value for the exceptional or truncated sub-instance
is
\Omega(\ell t_{\ell})
, and hence
\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}=\Omega(\ell t_{\ell})
.
Due to (1), by part (i) of Lemma
3.5
, the expected
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-load of the
assignment
{\sigma}
in the exceptional sub-instance is
O(\ell t_{\ell})
.
Also, considering
deterministic
scheduling with job sizes

p_{j}:=\beta_{\lambda}(X_{j}^{<t_{\ell}}/4t_{\ell})
(which is at most
1
), we can easily compute an
assignment
{\sigma}
that assigns total
p_{j}
-load at most
\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}/m+1\leq 9
on any
machine; part (i) of Lemma
3.7
then shows that the expected
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-load
of
{\sigma}
in the truncated sub-instance is also
O(\ell t_{\ell})
(at most

32\cdot 9\cdot\ell t_{\ell}
).
Therefore, we have
{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}})]=O(\ell t_{\ell})
, showing that
{\sigma}
is an

O(1)
-approximate solution to the given instance of
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
.

3.2
Proof of Theorem
3.3
: simultaneous
approximation for
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
via vector scheduling

We now prove Theorem
3.3
and, as a consequence, Theorem
1.3
as well.
Recall that we seek an assignment
{\sigma}
that is
simultaneously a
O(\log\log m)
-approximation to
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
for all

\ell\in\mathsf{POS}=\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{\lfloor\log_{2}m\rfloor}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\gamma(d)
-approximation algorithm for the
d
-dimensional vector scheduling
problem with respect to the natural lower bound
\mathit{lb}
.
As we show below, we use
\mathcal{A}
in a black-box fashion, so if
\mathcal{A}
is deterministic
(respectively randomized), then our simultaneous-approximation for
\mathsf{StochNormLB}
is also deterministic (respectively randomized).

The idea is simple: we essentially follow the approach used to obtain an

O(1)
-approximation for
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
(outlined above) for each
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}

separately.
More precisely, we first find the right
t_{\ell}
, for each
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
separately, via
binary search. That is, for each
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
, letting

\lambda_{\ell}=\lfloor\frac{2m}{\ell}\rfloor
,
we have that: (i)
\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}^{\geq t_{\ell}}]\leq\ell t_{\ell}
,
(ii)
\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda_{\ell}}(X_{j}^{<t_{\ell}}/4t_{\ell})\leq 8m
, and (iii)

\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}=\Omega(\ell t_{\ell})
.
We defer the details of this binary search to the end of the proof.
If we consider the exceptional and truncated sub-instances for an index
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
,
then, as before, the expected
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-load for the exceptional sub-instance is

O(\ell t_{\ell})
, under any assignment. So we only need to consider the truncated
sub-instances for
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
(with the truncated random variables

\{X_{j}^{<t_{\ell}}\}_{j}
).

Define
p_{j,\ell}:=\beta_{\lambda_{\ell}}(X_{j}^{<t_{\ell}}/4t_{\ell})

for each job
j\in J
and index
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
.
By Lemma
3.7
(i),
it order to obtain expected
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-load of
O(\alpha)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}
for each

\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
, it suffices to find an assignment
{\sigma}
such that, for each
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
,
the total
p_{j,\ell}
-load on each machine
i
is at most
\alpha
. In other words,
considering the
job vectors

p_{j}=(p_{j,\ell})_{\ell\in\mathsf{POS}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathsf{POS}}
for each job

j\in J
, we seek a solution to this
vector-scheduling instance
with makespan
\alpha
.

When we have only one index
\ell
(i.e.,
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
), it is easy to
argue (e.g., using Graham’s list scheduling) that
\sum_{j\in J}p_{j,\ell}=O(m)

and
p_{j,\ell}\leq 1
for all
j,\ell
, implies that we can assign the jobs so that the
total
p_{j,\ell}
-load on each machine is
O(1)
.
In our vector-scheduling instance, we have a bound on the total-load
p_{j,\ell}
load on
each coordinate
\ell
(due to (ii)), and we know that
p_{j,\ell}\leq 1
for all
j,\ell
.
Thus, the natural lower bound
\mathit{lb}
for this vector-scheduling instance is at most
8
, and what we now need is an
\mathit{lb}
-relative approximation algorithm for vector scheduling
.
We can therefore utilize
\mathcal{A}
to obtain makespan
\gamma(\log m)
for our vector-scheduling
instance, which thus yields an
O(\gamma(\log m))
approximation to
\mathsf{Stoch}
-
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
-
\mathsf{LB}
for all

\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
. Theorem
2.1
yields
\gamma=O(\log d)
, which yields a
simultaneous
O(\log\log m)
-approximation.

Finally, we furnish the details of the binary-search procedure to find the
t_{\ell}
s.
Fix an index
\ell\in\mathsf{POS}
.
We first establish suitable upper and lower bounds for
t_{\ell}
.
Define
\kappa:=\max_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}]
(which can be easily computed from input data).
Let
W
denote the random load vector induced by an optimal solution and

\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}:={\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(W)]
denote the optimal value.
By the contrapositive of part (ii) of Lemmas
3.5
and
3.7
,
for any
\theta\geq 2\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}/\ell
the following inequalities hold:
\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}^{\geq\theta}]\leq\ell\theta
and
\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lfloor\frac{2m}{\ell}\rfloor}(X_{j}^{<\theta}/4\theta)\leq 8m
.
In particular, since
\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}\leq{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\sum_{i\in[m]}W_{i}]=\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}]\leq n\kappa
,
these two inequalities hold for
\theta=\mathsf{hi}:=2n\kappa\geq 2\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}/\ell
.
Next, observe that for any job
k
and
\theta={\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{k}]/(m+2)
, we have

\sum_{j}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}^{\geq\theta}]\geq{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{k}^{\geq\theta}]\geq{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{k}]-\theta=(m+1)\theta>\ell\theta.

Thus, for any
\theta\leq\mathsf{low}:=\kappa/(m+2)
, we have
\sum_{j}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}^{\geq\theta}]>\ell\theta
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be a small constant (say,
1/1000
).
Then, by doing a binary search in the interval
[\mathsf{low},\mathsf{hi}]
,
we can find scalars
t_{\ell}
and
t^{\prime}_{\ell}
satisfying:

1.

t^{\prime}_{\ell}<t_{\ell}\leq t^{\prime}_{\ell}(1+\epsilon)
.

2.

\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}^{\geq t_{\ell}}]\leq\ell t_{\ell}
and

\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda_{\ell}}(X_{j}^{<t_{\ell}}/4t_{\ell})\leq 8m
.

3.

\sum_{j\in J}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[X_{j}^{\geq t^{\prime}_{\ell}}]>\ell t^{\prime}_{\ell}
or

\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda_{\ell}}(X_{j}^{<t^{\prime}_{\ell}}/4t^{\prime}_{\ell})>8m
.

Properties (i) and (iii) imply that
\mathsf{OPT}_{\ell}>\ell t^{\prime}_{\ell}/2\geq\ell t_{\ell}/2(1+\epsilon)
.

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

Recall that
\ell\in[m]
is a positive integer,
\theta
is a positive scalar, and
\lambda:=\lfloor\frac{2m}{\ell}\rfloor
.
Fix an assignment
{\sigma}
of jobs to machines, and let
Y:=\frac{1}{4\theta}\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}}
denote the induced load vector scaled down by a factor
4\theta
.
For the first part, suppose that the total effective size of all jobs is at most
8m
:
\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda}(\frac{X_{j}}{4\theta})\leq 8m
.
We want to argue that the expected
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}
norm of the induced load vector is bounded from above.
Recall that
\alpha:=\max\{1,\max_{i\in[m]}\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}\beta_{\lambda}(X_{j}/4\theta)\}
is the maximum of
1
and the makespan of
{\sigma}
w.r.t.
\beta_{\lambda}(\cdot)

Since

\beta_{\lambda}(Y_{i})=\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}\beta_{\lambda}(\frac{X_{j}}{4\theta})\leq\alpha
.
By Lemma
A.1
:

{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\alpha+1}]\leq{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\beta_{\lambda}(Y_{i})+1}]\leq\frac{\beta_{\lambda}(Y_{i})+3}{\lambda}\leq\frac{\alpha+3}{\lambda}.

Summing over all

\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq\alpha+1}]\leq(\alpha+3)\frac{m}{\lambda}\leq(\alpha+3)\ell

Using Theorem
3.4

\alpha+3

{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]\leq 2(\alpha+3)\ell

{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(\overrightarrow{\mathsf{load}}^{{{\sigma}}})]=4\theta{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}(Y)]\leq 8(\alpha+3)\ell\theta\leq 32\cdot\alpha\cdot\ell\theta.

For the second part, suppose that the total effective size of all jobs is larger than
8m

\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda}(\frac{X_{j}}{4\theta})>8m

We want to argue that the expected
\mathsf{Top}_{\ell}

For any
i\in[m]
,

[0,1]

By Lemma

\sum_{i\in[m]}{\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Exp}}}[Y_{i}^{\geq 1}]\geq\frac{1}{4\lambda}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\left(\sum_{j:{\sigma}(j)=i}\beta_{\lambda}(\frac{X_{j}}{4\theta})-6\right)=\frac{1}{4\lambda}\cdot\left(\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{\lambda}(\frac{X_{j}}{4\theta})-6m\right)>\frac{(8m-6m)}{4\cdot\frac{2m}{\ell}}=\ell/4,
