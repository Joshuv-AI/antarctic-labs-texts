Fast Multiobjective Gradient Methods with Nesterov Acceleration via Inertial Gradient-like Systems
Konstantin Sonntag, Sebastian Peitz

Abstract
We derive efficient algorithms to compute weakly Pareto optimal solutions for smooth, convex and unconstrained multiobjective optimization problems in general Hilbert spaces. To this end, we define a novel inertial gradient-like dynamical system in the multiobjective setting, whose trajectories converge weakly to Pareto optimal solutions. Discretization of this system yields an inertial multiobjective algorithm which generates sequences that converge weakly to Pareto optimal solutions. We employ Nesterov acceleration to define an algorithm with an improved convergence rate compared to the plain multiobjective steepest descent method (Algorithm 1 ). A further improvement in terms of efficiency is achieved by avoiding the solution of a quadratic subproblem to compute a common step direction for all objective functions, which is usually required in first order methods. Using a different discretization of our inertial gradient-like dynamical system, we obtain an accelerated multiobjective gradient method that does not require the solution of a subproblem in each step (Algorithm 2 ). While this algorithm does not converge in general, it yields good results on test problems while being faster than standard steepest descent by two to three orders of magnitude.



1
Introduction

In many applications in industry, economics, medicine or transport, optimizing several criteria is of interest. In the latter, one wants to reach a destination as fast as possible with minimal power consumption. Drug development aims for maximizing efficacy while minimizing side effects. Even these elementary examples share an inherent feature. The different criteria one seeks to optimize are in general contradictory. There is no design choice that is best for all criteria simultaneously. This insight shifts the focus from finding a single optimal solution to a set of optimal compromises – the Pareto set. Given the Pareto set, a decision maker can select an optimal compromise according to their preferences. In this paper, we derive efficient gradient-based algorithms to compute elements of the Pareto set. Formally, a problem involving multiple criteria can be formalized as an unconstrained multiobjective optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}(x)\end{array}\right],

where
f_{i}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\dots,m
are the objective functions describing the different criteria. Popular approaches to tackle this problem in the differentiable case are first order methods which exploit the smooth structure of the problem while not being computationally demanding compared to higher order methods involving exact or approximated Hessians.

While in single objective optimization,
accelerated
first order methods are very popular, these methods are not studied sufficiently from a theoretical point of view in the multiobjective setting. A fruitful approach to analyze accelerated gradient methods is to interpret them as discretizations of suitable gradient-like dynamical systems
[
1
]
. The analysis of the continuous dynamics is often easier and can later on be transferred to the discrete setting. This perspective is until now not fully taken advantage of in the area of multiobjective optimization. In this paper, we utilize this approach to derive accelerated gradient methods for multiobjective optimization.
To this end, we define and analyze the following novel dynamical gradient-like system

\displaystyle\ddot{x}(t)+\alpha\dot{x}(t)+\proj_{C(x(t))}{(-\ddot{x}(t))}=0,

(IMOG’)

with
\alpha>0
,
C(x)\coloneqq\co\left(\{\nabla f_{i}(x)\,:\,i=1,\dots,m\}\right)
, where
\co(\cdot)
denotes the convex hull and
\proj_{C(x(t))}{(-\ddot{x}(t))}
is the projection of
-\ddot{x}(t)
onto the convex set
C(x(t))
. The system (
IMOG’
) is an
inertial multiobjective gradient-like system
. We choose the designation (
IMOG’
) to emphasize its relation to the system

\displaystyle m\ddot{x}(t)+\gamma\dot{x}(t)+\proj_{C(x(t))}{(0)}=0,

(IMOG)

with
m,\gamma>0
, which was discussed in
[
2
]
. In the single objective setting (
m=1
), both (
IMOG’
) and (
IMOG
) reduce to the
heavy ball with friction system

\displaystyle m\ddot{x}(t)+\gamma\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0,

(HBF)

which is well studied for different types of objective functions
f
, see e.g.
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

We discretize (
IMOG’
) to obtain an iterative scheme of the form

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=x^{k}+a(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-s\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}^{k}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),

with appropriately chosen coefficients
a,s>0
and
\theta^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. This scheme can be interpreted as an inertial gradient method for (
1
). We show that it shares many properties with its continuous counterpart and that iterates defined by this algorithm converge weakly to Pareto critical points. To the best of our knowledge this is the first multiobjective method involving a constant momentum term with guaranteed convergence to Pareto critical solutions.

In a further step, we introduce time-dependent friction and informally define the following
multiobjective gradient-like system with asymptotically vanishing damping

\displaystyle\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{t}\dot{x}(t)+\proj_{C(x(t))}{(-\ddot{x}(t))}=0.

(MAVD)

In the single objective setting this system simplifies to the following inertial system with
asymptotically vanishing damping

\displaystyle\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{t}\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0.

(AVD)

It is well-known that (
AVD
) is naturally linked with Nesterov’s accelerted gradient method
[
1
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Discretizing the dynamical system (
MAVD
), and using our knowledge about (
IMOG’
), we derive an accelerated gradient method for multiobjective optimization that takes the form

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\frac{k-1}{k+2}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-s\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}^{k}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),

with appropriately chosen coefficients
s>0
and
\theta^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In a recent preprint, Tanabe, Fukuda and Yamashita derive an accelerated proximal gradient method for multiobjective optimization using the concept of merit functions
[
9
]
. We show that the method we derive from the differential equation (
MAVD
) achieves the same convergence rate of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
for the function values, measured with a so-called merit function.

This remainder of the paper is organized according to the novelties described above. After introducing some basic definitions and notation in Section
2
, we prove that solutions to the system (
IMOG’
) exist in finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces in Section
3
, and that they converge to Pareto critical points in Section
4
. Based on that, we derive a discrete optimization algorithm from an explicit discretization on (
IMOG’
) and show that the iterates defined by this method converge weakly to Pareto critical points, in Section
5
. We then introduce Nesterov Acceleration and prove an improved convergence result in Section
6
, and a further improvement in numerical efficiency is discussed in Section
7
. These two central algorithms are summarized in Algorithms
1
and
2
in the respective sections. We compare these on convex and non-convex example problems in Section
8
, and conclude our findings and list future research projects in Section
9
.

Background

2.1
Notations

Throughout this paper,
\mathcal{H}
is a real Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and induced norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
. The set
\Delta_{m}\coloneqq\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\,\,:\,\,\alpha\geq 0,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=1\right\}
is the unit simplex. We denote the open ball with radius
\delta>0
and center
x
by
B_{\delta}(x)\coloneqq\left\{y\in\mathcal{H}\,\,:\,\,\lVert y-x\rVert<\delta\right\}
. The closed ball with radius
\delta>0
and center
x
is denoted by
\overline{B_{\delta}(x)}
. For a set of vectors
\left\{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{k}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
we denote the convex hull of these vectors by
\co(\left\{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{k}\right\})\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\xi_{i}\,\,:\,\,\alpha\in\Delta_{m}\right\}
. For a closed convex set
C\subseteq\mathcal{H}
the projection of a vector
x\in\mathcal{H}
onto
C
is
\proj_{C}(x)\coloneqq\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{H}}\lVert y-x\rVert^{2}
. For two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we define the partial order
x\leq y:\Leftrightarrow x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,k
. We define
\geq
,
<
,
>
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
analogously. When we treat dynamical systems,
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathcal{H}
are the time and state variable, respectively. We denote trajectories in
\mathcal{H}
with
t\mapsto x(t)
with first derivative
\dot{x}(t)
and second derivative
\ddot{x}(t)
.

2.2
Multiobjective Optimization

Consider the unconstrained multiobjective optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}(x)\end{array}\right],

with at least once differentiable objective functions
f_{i}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\dots,m
. The definitions in this subsection are aligned with
[
10
]
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
10
]
, pp.10-20)
.

Consider the optimization problem (
2.2
).

i)

A point
x^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
is Pareto optimal if there does not exist another point
x\in\mathcal{H}
such that
f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(x^{*})
for all
i=1,\dots,m,
and
f_{j}(x)<f_{j}(x^{*})
for at least one index
j
. The set of all Pareto optimal points is the Pareto set, which we denote with
P
.

ii)

A point
x^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
is locally Pareto optimal if there exists
\delta>0
such that
x^{*}
is Pareto optimal in
B_{\delta}(x^{*})
.

iii)

A point
x^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
is weakly Pareto optimal if there does not exist another vector
x\in\mathcal{H}
such that
f_{i}(x)<f_{i}(x^{*})
for all
i=1,\dots,m
.

iv)

A point
x^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
is locally weakly Pareto optimal if there exists
\delta>0
such that
x^{*}
is weakly Pareto optimal in
B_{\delta}(x^{*})
.

In this paper, we treat convex MOPs. Therefore, the objective functions
f_{i}
are convex for all
i=1,\dots,m
. In this setting, every locally (weakly) Pareto optimal point is also (weakly) Pareto optimal. For unconstrained MOPs, the so-called Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions can be written as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

A point
x^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfies the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions if there exists
\alpha\in\Delta_{m}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x^{*})=0
. If
x^{*}
satisfies the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions, we call it Pareto critical.

The condition
0\in\co\left(\left\{\nabla f_{i}(x^{*})\,\,:\,\,i=1,\dots,m\right\}\right)
is equivalent to the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions. Analogously to the single objective setting, criticality of a point is a necessary condition for optimality. In the convex setting, the KKT conditions are also sufficient conditions for weak Pareto optimality. If we denote the Pareto set by
P
, the weak Pareto set by
P_{w}
and the Pareto critical set by
P_{c}
in the setting of smooth and convex multiobjective optimization, we observe the relation

\displaystyle P\subset P_{w}=P_{c}.

2.3
Accelerated Methods for Multiobjective Optimization

Accelerated methods for multiobjective optimization are not sufficiently discussed from a theoretical point of view in the literature yet. In
[
11
]
El Moudden and El Moutasim propose an accelerated method for multiobjective optimization which incorporates the multiobjective descent direction by Fliege
[
12
]
and the same acceleration scheme as in Nesterov’s accelerated method
[
13
]
. El Moudden and El Moutasim prove a convergence rate of the function values with rate
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
. Their proof relies on the restrictive assumption that the Lagrange multipliers of the quadratic subproblem, that is used to compute the step direction in every iteration, remain fixed from a certain point on. Under this assumption, the method simplifies to Nesterov’s method for single objective optimization problems applied to a weighted sum of the objective functions with fixed weights. Only recently, Tanabe, Fukuda and Yamashita derived an accelerated proximal gradient method for multiobjective optimization problems in
[
9
]
. They developed their method using the concept of merit functions (see Subsection
2.5
) and show that the function values converge with rate
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
without additional assumptions on the Lagrange multipliers.

2.4
Dynamical Systems linked to Multiobjective Optimization

In
[
14
]
Smale presents the idea of treating multiobjective optimization problem with a continuous time perspective that is motivated from an economical point of view using utility functions in a multi-agent framework. The simplest dynamical system for multiobjective optimization problems is the
multiobjective gradient system

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)+\proj_{C(x(t))}(0)=0,

(MOG)

where
C(x(t))=\co\left(\{\nabla f_{i}(x(t))\,:\,i=1,\dots,m\}\right)
. This system is already treated in
[
15
]
and in addition by Cornet in
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
as a dynamical system for resource allocation from an economical point of view. In
[
19
,
20
]
the system (
MOG
) gets introduced as a tool for multiobjective optimization. The system (
MOG
) can also be seen as a continuous version of the multiobjective steepest descent method by Fliege
[
12
]
. In the single objective setting (
m=1
), the system (
MOG
) simplifies to the
steepest descent dynamical system

\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0
. Generalizations of (
MOG
) are treated in
[
20
,
21
]
. In
[
20
]
Attouch and Goudou discuss a dynamical system for constrained minimization and in
[
21
]
Attouch, Garrigos and Goudou present a differential inclusion for constrained nonsmooth optimization.

In
[
2
]
, Attouch and Garrigos introduce inertia in the system (
MOG
) and define the following
inertial multiobjective gradient-like dynamical system

\displaystyle m\ddot{x}(t)+\gamma\dot{x}(t)+\proj_{C(x(t))}(0)=0.

(IMOG)

Trajectories of (
IMOG
) converge weakly to Pareto optimal solutions given
\gamma^{2}>mL
, where
L
is a common Lipschitz constant for the gradients of the objective functions.

2.5
Merit Functions

A merit function associated with an optimization problem is a function that returns zero at an optimal solution and which is strictly positive otherwise. An overview on merit functions used in multiobjective optimization is given in
[
22
]
. In our proofs we use the merit function

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)\coloneqq\sup_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\min_{i=1,\dots,m}f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z),

(7)

which satisfies the following statement.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
22
]
Theorem 3.1)
.

It holds that
u_{0}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
. Moreover,
x\in\mathcal{H}
is weakly Pareto optimal for (
2.2
), if and only if
u_{0}(x)=0
.

Additionally,
u_{0}(x)
is lower semicontinuous. Therefore, if
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
is a sequence with
u_{0}(x^{k})\to 0
, every cluster point of
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
is weakly Pareto optimal. This motivates the usage of
u_{0}(x)
as a measure of complexity for multiobjective optimization methods. The function
u_{0}(x)
is not the only merit function for multiobjective optimization problems, see also
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
and further references in
[
22
]
.

3
Global Existence in Finite Dimensions

In this section, we show that solutions exist for the Cauchy problem related to
(
IMOG’
), i.e

\displaystyle\left\lvert\begin{array}[]{l}\ddot{x}(t)+\alpha\dot{x}(t)+\proj_{C(x(t))}{(-\ddot{x}(t))}=0,\\
\\
x(0)=x_{0},\quad\dot{x}(0)=v_{0},\end{array}\right.

with initial data
x_{0},v_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
.
To this end, we show that for this system solutions exist if there exists a solution for a first order differential inclusion

\displaystyle(\dot{u},\dot{v})\in F(u,v),

with a set-valued mapping
F:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
. Then, we use an existence theorem for differential inclusions from
[
26
]
. Our argument works only in finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Thus, we assume
\dim(\mathcal{H})<+\infty
from here on. In our context, the following set-valued mapping is of interest:

\displaystyle F:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H},\quad(u,v)\mapsto\{v\}\times\left(\big(-\argmin_{z\in C(u)}\langle z,v\rangle\big)-\alpha v\right)

(11)

As stated above,
C(u)\coloneqq\co\left(\{\nabla f_{i}(u)\,:\,i=1,\dots,m\}\right)
. We can show that (
3
) has a solution if the differential inclusion

\displaystyle\left\lvert\begin{array}[]{l}(\dot{u}(t),\dot{v}(t))\in F(u,v),\\
\\
(u(0),v(0))=(u_{0},v_{0}),\end{array}\right.

with appropriate initial data
u_{0},v_{0}
has a solution.

3.1
Existence of Solutions to (DI)

To show that there exist solutions to (
3
), we investigate the set-valued mapping
(u,v)\rightrightarrows F(u,v)
defined in (
11
). The basic definitions for set-valued mappings used in this subsection can be found in
[
26
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
,
F(u,v)\subset\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
is convex and compact.

Proof.

The statement follows directly from the definition.
∎

To use an existence theorem from
[
26
]
, we need to show that
(u,v)\rightrightarrows F(u,v)
is upper semicontinuous. Showing this is elementary. We omit the full proof here but sketch a possible way to prove this result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
C(u)\coloneqq\co(\{c_{i}(u)\,:\,i=1,\dots,m\})
with
c_{i}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H},\,u\to c_{i}(u)
continuous for
i=1,\dots,m
. Let
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
be fixed.

Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
\delta>0
such that for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
with
\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert<\delta
and
\lVert v-v_{0}\rVert<\delta
and for all
z\in\argmin_{z\in C(u)}\langle z,v\rangle
there exists
z_{0}\in\argmin_{z_{0}\in C(u_{0})}\langle z_{0},v_{0}\rangle
with
\lVert z-z_{0}\rVert<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

The proof follows straightforward.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

The set-valued mapping
(u,v)\rightrightarrows F(u,v)
is upper semicontinuous.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.2
we can show in a straightforward manner

\displaystyle F((u_{0},v_{0})+B_{\delta}(0))\subset F(u_{0},v_{0})+B_{\varepsilon}(0)

using only continuity arguments. Then, the statement follows by the fact that
(u,v)\rightrightarrows F(u,v)
is locally compact.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
have finite dimension. Then, the mapping

\displaystyle\phi:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H},\quad(u,v)\mapsto\left(v,\,\proj_{F(u,v)}(0)\right),

is locally compact.

Proof.

If
\dim(\mathcal{H})<+\infty
the proof follows easily since all images
F(u,v)
are compact and depend on
(u,v)
in a well behaved manner. On the other hand, from
\phi
being locally compact, we get that
v\mapsto v
is locally compact which is equivalent to
\mathcal{H}
being finite-dimensional.
∎

The following existence theorem from
[
26
]
is applicable in our setting.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
26
]
, p. 98, Theorem 3)
.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\times X
be an open subset containing
(0,x_{0})
. Let
F
be an upper semicontinuous map from
\Omega
into the non-empty closed convex subsets of
X
. We assume that
(t,x)\mapsto\proj_{F(t,x)}(0)
is locally compact. Then, there exists
T>0
and an absolutely continuous function
x(\cdot)
defined on
[0,T]
which is a solution to the differential inclusion

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)\in F(t,x(t)),\quad x(0)=x_{0}.

We are finally in the position to state an existence theorem for (
3
).

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional and that the gradients of the objective function
\nabla f_{i}
are globally Lipschitz continuous. Then, for all
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
there exists
T>0
and an absolutely continuous function
(u(\cdot),v(\cdot))
defined on
[0,T]
which is a solution to the differential inclusion (
3
).

Proof.

The proof follows immediately from Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
which show that the set-valued mapping satisfies all conditions required for Theorem
3.5
.
∎

In the following, we show that under additional conditions on the objective functions
f_{i}
there exist solutions defined on
[0,+\infty)
. The extension of the solution works using a standard argument. We show that the solutions to (
3
) remain bounded. Then, we use Zorn’s Lemma to retrieve a contradiction if there is a maximal solution that is not defined on
[0,+\infty)
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Assume
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional and that the gradients of the objective function
\nabla f_{i}
are globally Lipschitz continuous. Then, for all
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
there exists an absolutely continuous function
(u(\cdot),v(\cdot))
defined on
[0,+\infty)
which is a solution to the differential inclusion (
3
).

Proof.

Theorem
3.6
guarantees the existence of solutions defined on
[0,T)
for some
T\geq 0
. Using the domain of definition, we can define a partial order on the set of solutions to the problem (
3
). Assuming there is no solution defined on
[0,+\infty)
, Zorn’s Lemma guarantees the existence of a solution
(u(\cdot),v(\cdot)):[0,T)\to\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
with
T<+\infty
which can not be extended. We will show that
(u(t),v(t))
does not blow up in finite time and therefore can be extended which contradicts the claimed maximality.

Define

\displaystyle h(t)\coloneqq\lVert(u(t),v(t))-(u(0),v(0))\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}},

where
\lVert(x,y)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}}=\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert^{2}+\lVert y\rVert^{2}}
. We show that
h(t)
can be bounded by a real-valued function. Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we directly get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}h^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle(\dot{u}(t),\dot{v}(t)),(u(t),v(t))-(u(0),v(0))\rangle

(15)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert F(u(t),v(t))\rVert h(t).

(16)

We next derive a bound on
\lVert F(u,v)\rVert
. The basic inequalities between the
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
norm applied to
\left(\lVert x\rVert,\lVert y\rVert\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
yield

\displaystyle\lVert(x,y)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}}\leq\lVert x\rVert+\lVert y\rVert\leq\sqrt{2}\lVert(x,y)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\lVert F(u,v)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}}\leq\lVert v\rVert+\lVert\big(-\argmin_{z\in C(u)}\langle z,v\rangle\big)-\alpha v\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)\lVert v\rVert+\max_{\theta\in\Delta_{m}}\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(u)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)\lVert v\rVert+\max_{\theta\in\Delta_{m}}\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\left(\nabla f_{i}(u)-\nabla f_{i}(0)\right)\rVert+\max_{\theta\in\Delta_{m}}\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(0)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)\lVert v\rVert+L\lVert u\rVert+\max_{i=1,\dots,m}\lVert\nabla f_{i}(0)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c(1+\lVert(u,v)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}}),

where we have chosen
c=\sqrt{2}\max\left\{1+\alpha,L,\max_{i=1,\dots,m}\lVert\nabla f_{i}(0)\rVert\right\}
.
Combining the last inequality with (
15
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}h^{2}(t)\leq c(1+h(t))h(t),\,\,\text{for all}\,\,t\in[0,T).

Using a Gronwall-type argument (see Lemma A.4 and Lemma A.5 in
[
27
]
) just as in Theorem 3.5 in
[
2
]
, we know that there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that for an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle h(t)\leq cT\exp(cT),\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,t\in[0,T-\varepsilon].

Since this upper bound is independent of
t
and
\varepsilon
, it follows that
h\in L^{\infty}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, solutions to (
3
) do not blow up in finite time and can be extended. This is a contradiction to the maximality of the solution
(u(t),v(t))
.
∎

3.2
Existence of Solutions to (CP)

Using the findings of the previous subsection, we can proceed with the discussion of the Cauchy problem (
3
). In this subsection, we show that solutions to the differential inclusion (
3
) immediately give solutions to the Cauchy problem (
3
).

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
x_{0},v_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
. Assume that
(u(t),v(t))
for
t\in[0,+\infty)
is a solution to (
3
) with
(u(0),v(0))=(x_{0},v_{0})
. Then, it follows that
x(t)\coloneqq u(t)
satisfies the differential equation

\displaystyle\ddot{x}(t)+\alpha\dot{x}(t)+\proj_{C(x(t))}(-\ddot{x}(t))=0,\,\,\text{for all}\,\,t\in(0,+\infty),

and
x(0)=x_{0}
,
\dot{x}(0)=v_{0}
.

Proof.

Since
(u(t),v(t))
is a solution to (
3
), it holds for all
t\in(0,+\infty)
that

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t)

\displaystyle=v(t),

\displaystyle\dot{v}(t)

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{z\in C(u(t))}\langle z,v(t)\rangle-\alpha v(t).

Using Lemma
A.1
, the second line gives
-\alpha v(t)=\proj_{C(u(t))+\dot{v}(t)}(0)
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\dot{v}(t)+\alpha v(t)+\proj_{C(u(t))}(-\dot{v}(t))=0.

Rewriting this system using
x(t)=u(t)
,
\dot{x}(t)=\dot{u}(t)=v(t)
and
\ddot{x}=\dot{v}(t)
and verifying the initial conditions
x(0)=u(0)=x_{0}
and
\dot{x}(0)=v(0)=v_{0}
yields the desired result.
∎

Finally, we can state the full existence theorem for the Cauchy problem (
3
).

Theorem 3.9
.

Assume
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional and that the gradients of the objective function
\nabla f_{i}
are globally Lipschitz continuous. Then, for all
x_{0},v_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists a twice continuously differentiable function
x(t)
defined on
[0,+\infty)
which is absolutely continuous with absolutely continuous first derivative
\dot{x}(t)
, and which is a solution to the Cauchy problem (
3
) with initial values
(x_{0},v_{0})
.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately combining Theorem
3.7
and Theorem
3.8
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

Throughout this section, we have assumed that the gradients
\nabla f_{i}
of the objective functions are globally Lipschitz continuous. One can relax this condition and only require the gradients to be Lipschitz continuous on bounded sets if we can guarantee that the solutions remain bounded. This holds for example if one of the objective functions
f_{i}
is coercive.

4
Asymptotic Analysis of Trajectories of (IMOG’)

In this section, we omit the assumption
\dim(\mathcal{H})<+\infty
. We show that trajectories of the differential equation (
IMOG’
) converge weakly to Pareto critical points of the optimization problem (
2.2
). This follows from a dissipative property of the system and an argument that relies on Opial’s Lemma. We first define a so-called energy function for the system (
IMOG’
) that has Lyapunov-type properties.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
x:[0,+\infty)\to\mathcal{H}
be a solution to (
3
). For
i=1,\dots,m
define the global energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{R},\quad t\mapsto f_{i}(x(t))+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}.

Then, for all
t\in(0,+\infty)
it holds that
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{i}(t)\leq-\alpha\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}
.

Proof.

From the definition of the differential equation (
IMOG’
), it follows that
-\alpha\dot{x}(t)=\proj_{\co\left(\nabla f_{i}(x(t))+\ddot{x}(t)\right)}(0)
. By the variational characterization of the convex projection, we get for all
i=1,\dots,m

\displaystyle\langle\alpha\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f_{i}(x(t))+\ddot{x}(t),\alpha\dot{x}(t)\rangle\leq 0,

which immediately gives

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f_{i}(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle+\langle\dot{x}(t),\ddot{x}(t)\rangle\leq-\alpha\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}.

Applying the chain rule to
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{i}(t)
yields the desired result.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
x:[0,+\infty)\to H
be a bounded solution of (
3
) and let further
\nabla f_{i}
be Lipschitz continuous on bounded sets. Then, for all
i=1,\dots,m

i)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathcal{E}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}>-\infty

ii)

\dot{x}\in L^{2}([0,+\infty))\cap L^{\infty}([0,+\infty))

iii)

\ddot{x}\in L^{\infty}([0,+\infty))
,
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert=0
and
\lim_{t\to+\infty}f_{i}(x(t))=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}

iv)

There exists a monotonically increasing unbounded sequence
(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}
with

\proj_{C(x(t_{k}))}(0)\to 0
for
k\to+\infty
.

Proof.

i) From Proposition
4.1
, we immediately get that
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is monotonically decreasing and therefore
\mathcal{E}_{i}(t)\to\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}
as
t\to+\infty
. We have to show that in fact
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}>-\infty
. Since
\nabla f_{i}
is bounded on bounded sets, we can conclude by the mean value theorem that
f_{i}
is bounded on bounded sets. Since
x(t)
remains bounded by assumption, we conclude that
f_{i}(x(t))
is bounded from below, and hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}\geq\inf_{t\geq 0}f_{i}(x(t))>-\infty.

ii) We know that
f_{i}(x(t))
is bounded. Then, by the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{i}
and the fact that
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is monotonically decreasing, we immediately get that
\dot{x}
is bounded for all
t\geq 0
. Since it is of class
C^{1}
, it follows that
\dot{x}\in L^{\infty}([0,+\infty))
. Using Proposition
4.1
it follows that

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{0}^{+\infty}\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}\,dt\leq-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{i}(s)\,ds=\mathcal{E}_{i}(0)-\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}<+\infty,

and therefore
\dot{x}\in L^{2}([0,+\infty))
.

iii) Since
\dot{x}(t)
and
\nabla f_{i}(x(t))
remain bounded for all
t\geq 0
it follows that
\ddot{x}(t)=-\alpha\dot{x}(t)-\proj_{C(x(t))}(-\ddot{x}(t))
remains bounded for all
t\geq 0
. By the fact that
x
is twice continuously differentiable, it follows that
\ddot{x}
is continuous and hence
\ddot{x}\in L^{\infty}([0,+\infty))
. Then, from
\dot{x}\in C^{1}([0,+\infty))\cap L^{2}([0,+\infty))
and
\ddot{x}\in C([0,+\infty))\cap L^{\infty}([0,+\infty))
it follows that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert=0
. From
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert=0
and part i) we can immediately conclude
\lim_{t\to+\infty}f_{i}(x(t))=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}
.

iv)  Assume that the negation of statement iv) holds, namely

\displaystyle\exists M>0\,\,\exists T\geq 0\,\,\forall t\geq T\,\,:\,\,\lVert\proj_{C(x(t))}(0)\rVert\geq M.

(17)

Fix an arbitrary
\delta>0
. Since
\dot{x}(t)\to 0
and
\nabla f_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous on a set containing
x(t)
it follows that there exists
T_{\delta}>T
such that for all
t>T_{\delta}
and
s\in[t,t+\delta)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\nabla f_{i}(x(s))-\nabla f_{i}(x(t))\rVert<\frac{M}{8}\,\,\text{and}\,\,\lVert\alpha\dot{x}(s)\rVert<\frac{M}{8}.\end{split}

(18)

Define
v\coloneqq\proj_{C(x(t))}/\lVert\proj_{C(x(t))}\rVert
. From (
17
) it follows that

\displaystyle\forall\xi\in C(x(t))\,\,:\,\,\langle\xi,v\rangle\geq M.

Combining the last statement with (
18
) and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get

\displaystyle\forall s\in[t,t+\delta)\,\,\forall\xi\in C(x(s))\,\,:\,\,\langle\xi+\alpha\dot{x}(s),v\rangle\geq\frac{M}{4}.

And hence

\displaystyle\forall s\in[t,t+\delta)\,\,:\,\,\langle-\ddot{x}(s),v\rangle\geq\frac{M}{4}.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality again, we get

\displaystyle\lVert\dot{x}(t)-\dot{x}(t+\delta)\rVert\geq\langle\dot{x}(t)-\dot{x}(t+\delta),v\rangle=\langle\int_{t}^{t+\delta}-\ddot{x}(s)\,ds,v\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+\delta}\langle-\ddot{x}(s),v\rangle\,ds\geq\int_{t}^{t+\delta}\frac{M}{4}\,ds=\frac{M\delta}{4}.

Since we can choose
\delta
arbitrarily large and independently from
M
, this contradicts
\dot{x}(t)\to 0
.
∎

We will use part iv) of Proposition
4.2
to show that a weak limit point of the trajectory
x(t)
is Pareto critical. To this end we introduce the following lemma that states a demiclosedness property of the set-valued mapping
C:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H},\,x\mapsto C(x)
.

Lemma 4.3

(
[
6
]
Lemma 2.4,
[
2
]
Lemma 4.10)
.

Assume that the objective functions
f_{i}
are continuously differentiable. Let
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{H}
that converges weakly to
x^{\infty}
, and assume there exists a sequence
(g^{k})_{k\geq 0}
with
g^{k}\in C(x^{k})
that converges strongly to zero. Then,
0\in C(x^{\infty})
and hence
x^{\infty}
is Pareto critical.

If we can show that the trajectories of (
IMOG’
) converge weakly, Proposition
4.2
together with Lemma
4.3
guarantees that the limit points are Pareto critical. To show that the trajectories are in fact converging, we require Opial’s Lemma.

Lemma 4.4

(Opial’s Lemma
[
28
]
)
.

Let
S\subset\mathcal{H}
be a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{H}
and

x:[0,+\infty)\to\mathcal{H}
. Assume that
x(t)
satisfies the following conditions.

i)

Every weak sequential cluster point of
x(t)
belongs to
S
.

ii)

For every
z\in S
,
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\lVert x(t)-z\rVert
exists.

Then,
x(t)
converges weakly to an element
x^{\infty}\in S
.

To use Opial’s Lemma, we need a suitable nonempty set
S\subset\mathcal{H}
that we define in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
x(t)
be a bounded solution to (
3
). Then, the set

\displaystyle S\coloneqq\left\{z\in\mathcal{H}\,:\,f_{i}(z)\leq\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}\,\,\text{for all }i=1,\dots,m,\right\},

(19)

is nonempty.

Proof.

Part iii) of Proposition
4.2
states that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}f_{i}(x(t))=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}
for all
i=1,\dots,m
. Since
x(t)
is bounded, it possesses at least one weak sequential cluster point
x^{\infty}
. The objective functions
f_{i}
are convex and continuous and therefore weakly lower semicontinuous. From this we conclude
x^{\infty}\in S
.
∎

For the set
S
defined in (
19
) and a bounded solution
x(t)
of (
3
), the first part of Opial’s Lemma is easy to obtain. It follows analogously to the proof of Proposition
4.5
where it is shown that
S
is nonempty. To show the second part of Opial’s Lemma, we verify that
h_{z}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lVert x(t)-z\rVert^{2}
satisfies a differential inequality. Then, the convergence can be deduced from the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6

(
[
5
]
Lemma 4.2)
.

Let
h\in C^{1}([0,+\infty),\mathbb{R})
be a positive function satisfying
\alpha\dot{h}(t)+\ddot{h}(t)\leq g(t)
for all
t\geq 0
, with
g\in L^{1}([0,+\infty),\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha>0
. Then,
\lim_{t\to+\infty}h(t)
exists.

With these ingredients, we can formulate the main convergence theorem of this subsection.

Theorem 4.7
.

Assume that the objective functions
f_{i}
are convex with gradients
\nabla f_{i}
that are Lipschitz continuous on bounded sets. Then, solution
x:[0,+\infty)\to\mathcal{H}
of (
3
) with arbitrary initial conditions
x^{0},v^{0}\in\mathcal{H}
that remains bounded converges weakly to a Pareto critical point of (
2.2
).

Proof.

For
z\in S
define

\displaystyle h_{z}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lVert x(t)-z\rVert^{2}.

Using the chain rule, we compute the first and the second derivative of
h_{z}(t)
as

\displaystyle\dot{h}_{z}(t)=\langle x(t)-z,\dot{x}(t)\rangle,\quad\ddot{h}_{z}(t)=\langle x(t)-z,\ddot{x}(t)\rangle+\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}.

For a fixed
t\in(0,+\infty)
, write

\displaystyle\alpha\dot{h}_{z}(t)+\ddot{h}_{z}(t)=\langle\ddot{x}(t)+\alpha\dot{x}(t),x(t)-z\rangle+\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}.

Using the definition of (
IMOG’
), we can write
\ddot{x}(t)+\alpha\dot{x}(t)=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x(t))
for some weights
\theta\in\Delta_{m}
. Then, we write

\displaystyle\alpha\dot{h}_{z}(t)+\ddot{h}_{z}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(x(t)),z-x(t)\rangle+\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}.

(20)

Proposition
4.1
gives for all
i=1,\dots,m

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}(t)=f_{i}(x(t))+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}\geq\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}\geq f_{i}(z)\geq f_{i}(x(t))+\langle\nabla f_{i}(x(t)),z-x(t)\rangle,

and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(x(t)),z-x(t)\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}.

(21)

Combining inequalities (
20
) and (
21
) we get

\displaystyle\alpha\dot{h}_{z}(t)+\ddot{h}_{z}(t)\leq\frac{3}{2}\lVert\dot{x}(t)\rVert^{2}.

By Proposition
4.2
, we know
\lVert\dot{x}(\cdot)\rVert^{2}\in L^{1}([0,+\infty))
. Then, Lemma
4.6
states that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}h_{z}(t)
exists. In addition, we know that every weak sequential cluster point of
x(t)
belongs to
S
by the weak lower semicontinuity of the objective functions
f_{i}
. Then, we can use Opial’s Lemma
5.5
to prove that
x(t)
converges weakly to an element in
S
. Let
x^{\infty}
be the weak limit of
x(t)
. Then, by Proposition
4.2
, there exists a monotonically increasing unbounded sequence
(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}
with
\proj_{C(x(t_{k}))}(0)\to 0
for
k\to+\infty
. Since
x(t_{k})
converges weakly to
x^{\infty}
, Lemma
4.3
states that
x^{\infty}
is Pareto critical.
∎

5
An Inertial Multiobjective Gradient Algorithm

In this section, we derive an inertial first order method for multiobjective optimization problems from an explicit discretization of the differential equation (
IMOG’
). We write the system (
IMOG’
) in the equivalent form

\displaystyle\alpha\dot{x}(t)+\proj_{C(x(t))+\ddot{x}(t)}(0),

and use the following discretization of the differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha\frac{x^{k+1}-x^{k}}{h}+\proj_{C(x^{k})+\frac{x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+x^{k-1}}{h^{2}}}(0)=0,\\
\alpha h(x^{k+1}-x^{k})+\proj_{h^{2}C(x^{k})+x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+x^{k-1}}(0)=0.\end{split}

Lemma
A.2
states that
x^{k+1}
is uniquely defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}x^{k+1}&=-\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha h}\proj_{h^{2}C(x^{k})-2x^{k}+x^{k-1}}(-x^{k})-\frac{\alpha h}{1+\alpha h}x^{k}\right)\\
&=-\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha h}\left[-x^{k}+\proj_{h^{2}C(x^{k})-x^{k}+x^{k-1}}(0)\right]-\frac{\alpha h}{1+\alpha h}x^{k}\right)\\
&=x^{k}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha h}\proj_{h^{2}C(x^{k})-x^{k}+x^{k-1}}(0).\end{split}

(22)

Therefore,
x^{k+1}
can be written as

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\frac{1}{1+\alpha h}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-\frac{h^{2}}{1+\alpha h}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}^{k}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),

(23)

where
\theta^{k}\in\Delta_{m}
is the solution to the quadratic optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\,\left\lVert h^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k})\right)-(x^{k}-x^{k-1})\right\rVert^{2}\,\,\st\,\theta\geq 0\,\,\text{and}\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}=1.\end{split}

(24)

In the following subsection, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of the sequence
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
that is defined by equations (
23
) and (
24
).

5.1
Asymptotic Analysis

The asymptotic analysis of the sequence
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
defined by (
23
) and (
24
) works surprisingly similar to the asymptotic analysis of the trajectories
x(t)
of the differential equation (
IMOG’
). We start by proving that the sequence
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
satisfies a dissipative property. To this end, we introduce the following preparatory lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
be defined by (
23
) and (
24
) with
x^{0}=x^{1}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\alpha,h>0
. Then, for all
i=1,\dots,m
it holds that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle\leq-\frac{\alpha}{h}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\left[\lVert x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rVert^{2}-\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}\right].

Proof.

Using the variational characterization of the convex projection in the identity (
5
), we get for all
i=1,\dots,m
,

\displaystyle\langle\alpha h(x^{k+1}-x^{k})+h^{2}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k})+(x^{k+1}-x^{k})-(x^{k}-x^{k-1}),\alpha h(x^{k+1}-x^{k})\rangle\leq 0,

which can be rearranged into

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle\\
\leq&-\left(\frac{1}{h^{2}}+\frac{\alpha}{h}\right)\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert+\frac{1}{h^{2}}\langle x^{k+1}-x^{k},x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rangle\\
\leq&-\frac{\alpha}{h}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\left[\lVert x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rVert^{2}-\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}\right].\end{split}

(25)

∎

Using Lemma
5.1
, we show that there exists an energy sequence which can be seen as a discretization of the energy function defined in Proposition
4.2
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume that the gradients
\nabla f_{i}
of the objective functions are globally
L
-Lipschitz continuous for all
i=1,\dots,m
and further assume
Lh<2\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i,k}\coloneqq f_{i}(x^{k})+\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\lVert x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rVert^{2},

(26)

is monotonically decreasing.

Proof.

We start with investigating the difference

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i,k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{i,k}=f_{i}(x^{k+1})-f_{i}(x^{k})+\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\left[\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert-\lVert x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rVert\right].

Using that
f_{i}
is convex with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient, we estimate the expression above by

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\left[\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert-\lVert x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rVert\right].

Using Lemma
5.1
, we estimate this term by

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{h}\right)\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\lVert x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+x^{k-1}\rVert^{2}.

For
hL<2\alpha
it holds that
\left(\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{h}\right)<0
and we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i,k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{i,k}\leq\left(\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{h}\right)\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\lVert x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+x^{k-1}\rVert^{2},

(27)

which completes the proof.
∎

The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Proposition
5.2
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Assume all conditions of Proposition
5.2
are met. Then, for all
i=1,\dots,m
and all
k\geq 1
it holds that
f_{i}(x^{k})\leq f_{i}(x^{0})
.

Corollary
5.3
hints at a condition that guarantees that the sequence
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
remains bounded. If the level set
\mathcal{L}_{i}(f_{i}(x^{0}))\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{H}\,:\,f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(x^{0})\}
of one objective function
f_{i}
is bounded, the sequence
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
remains bounded. In the following proposition we collect some immediate consequences of Proposition
5.2
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Assume the gradients
\nabla f_{i}
of the objective functions are
L
-Lipschitz continuous on a bounded set containing the sequence
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
, that is defined by equations (
23
) and (
24
). Assume
Lh<2\alpha
, then for all
i=1,\dots,m
the following statements hold.

i)

\mathcal{E}_{i,k}\to\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}>-\infty
as
k\to+\infty

ii)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}<+\infty

iii)

f_{i}(x^{k})\to\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}
as
k\to+\infty

Proof.

i)  Proposition
5.2
states that
\mathcal{E}_{i,k}
is monotonically decreasing. Therefore,
\mathcal{E}_{i,k}\to\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}
holds. We have to show that
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}>-\infty
. Since the objective functions
f_{i}
have Lipschitz continuous gradients on a bounded set containing
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
, it follows by the mean value theorem that
f_{i}
is bounded on this sets and in particular on
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
. Therefore, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}=\lim_{k\to+\infty}f_{i}(x^{k})+\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\lVert x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rVert^{2}\geq\liminf_{k\to+\infty}f_{i}(x^{k})>-\infty.

ii)  From the inequality (
27
) we immediately follow

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{E}_{i,K+1}-\mathcal{E}_{i,1}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i,k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{i,k}\right)\\
\leq&\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{h}\right)\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{h^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lVert x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+x^{k-1}\rVert^{2}.\end{split}

(28)

Since
Lh<2\alpha
, it holds that
\left(\frac{\alpha}{h}-\frac{L}{2}\right)>0
and therefore we get for all
K\geq 1

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\alpha}{h}-\frac{L}{2}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}_{i,1}-\mathcal{E}_{i,K+1}.

From part i), we know that the right hand side converges which completes the proof of ii).

iii) Since
\mathcal{E}_{i,k}\to\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}
and
\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}\to 0
, it follows that
f_{i}(x^{k})\to\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\infty}
.
∎

We use the following discrete version of Opial’s Lemma to prove that
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
converges weakly to a Pareto critical point of (
2.2
).

Lemma 5.5

Proof.

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\alpha h}\langle x^{k-1}-x^{k},x^{k}-z\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha h)}\lVert x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rVert^{2}

Lemma 6.4

Lemma 6.5

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(z)-\sigma_{k}(z)

sL\leq 1
, we can bound this expression by

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i=1,\dots,m}\left(\langle\nabla f_{i}(y^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}\right).

Now we use Lemma
6.4
and get the equality

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}^{k}\langle\nabla f_{i}(y^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}.

From here, we continue by using the definitions of
x^{k}
and
y^{k}
from (
41
) to get

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\langle y^{k}-x^{k+1},x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{s}\langle x^{k+1}-y^{k},y^{k}-x^{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}.

∎

Theorem 6.6
.

The sequence
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
defined by (
41
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(z)\leq\frac{2\left(\lVert x^{1}-z\rVert^{2}+\lVert x^{2}-z\rVert^{2}\right)}{s(k+1)^{2}}.

Proof.

Lemma
6.3
and Lemma
6.5
state

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(z)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{s}\langle x^{k+1}-y^{k},y^{k}-z\rangle-\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}\,\,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(z)-\sigma_{k}(z)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{s}\langle x^{k+1}-y^{k},y^{k}-x^{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}.

Taking a convex combination of the last inequalities with weights
\frac{2}{k+2}
and
\frac{k}{k+2}
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma_{k+1}(z)-\frac{k}{k+2}\sigma_{k}(z)\\
\leq&-\frac{1}{s}\langle x^{k+1}-y^{k},y^{k}-\frac{k}{k+2}x^{k}-\frac{2}{k+2}z\rangle-\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}\\
=&\frac{1}{s}\langle x^{k+1}-y^{k},\frac{k}{k+2}(x^{k}-y^{k})+\frac{2}{k+2}(z-y^{k})\rangle-\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}.\end{split}

(46)

Define

\displaystyle z^{k}\coloneqq\frac{k+2}{2}y^{k}-\frac{k}{2}x^{k}=x^{k}+\frac{k-1}{2}(x^{k}-x^{k-1}),

(47)

and notice that

\displaystyle\frac{k}{k+2}(y^{k}-x^{k})+\frac{2}{k+2}(y^{k}-z)=\frac{2}{k+2}(z^{k}-z).

(48)

Using the identity (
48
) in (
46
) we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(z)\leq\frac{k}{k+2}\sigma_{k}(z)-\frac{2}{s(k+2)}\langle x^{k+1}-y^{k},z^{k}-z\rangle-\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}.

(49)

From the definition of
z^{k}
in (
47
) one can see that

\displaystyle z^{k+1}=z^{k}+\frac{k+2}{2}(x^{k+1}-y^{k}).

Using this identity we can simply compute the squared norm of
\lVert z^{k+1}-z\rVert^{2}
as

\displaystyle\lVert z^{k+1}-z\rVert^{2}=\lVert z^{k}-z\rVert^{2}+(k+2)\langle z^{k}-z,x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rangle+\left(\frac{k+2}{2}\right)^{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}.

Rearranging this identity and multiplying with
\frac{2}{s(k+2)^{2}}
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{2}{s(k+2)^{2}}\left(\lVert z^{k}-z\rVert^{2}-\lVert z^{k+1}-z\rVert^{2}\right)\\
=&-\frac{2}{s(k+2)}\langle z^{k}-z,x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{k+1}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}.\end{split}

(50)

Combining (
49
) and (
50
), in total we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(z)\leq\frac{k}{k+2}\sigma_{k}(z)+\frac{4}{2s(k+2)^{2}}\left(\lVert z^{k}-z\rVert^{2}-\lVert z^{k+1}-z\rVert^{2}\right).

Multiplying both sides with
(k+2)^{2}
then yields

\displaystyle(k+2)^{2}\sigma_{k+1}(z)\leq k(k+2)\sigma_{k}(z)+\frac{2}{s}\left(\lVert z^{k}-z\rVert^{2}-\lVert z^{k+1}-z\rVert^{2}\right).

Using
k(k+2)\leq(k+1)^{2}
we get

\displaystyle(k+2)^{2}\sigma_{k+1}(z)-(k+1)^{2}\sigma_{k}(z)\leq\frac{2}{s}\left(\lVert z^{k}-z\rVert^{2}-\lVert z^{k+1}-z\rVert^{2}\right).

Summing this inequality from
k=1,\dots,K
, we get for all
z\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle(K+2)^{2}\sigma_{K+1}(z)\leq\frac{2}{s}\lVert x^{1}-z\rVert^{2}+4\sigma_{1}(z).

Similar computations to Lemma
6.3
yield

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(z)\leq\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{2}-z\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{2s}\lVert x^{2}-x^{1}\rVert^{2}.

Then, for all
k\geq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(z)\leq\frac{2\left(\lVert x^{1}-z\rVert^{2}+\lVert x^{2}-z\rVert^{2}\right)}{s(k+1)^{2}}.

∎

The theorem above is not straight forward to interpret since we only get convergence of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
for
\min_{i=1,\dots,m}f_{i}(x^{k})-f_{i}(z)
. This on it’s own does not state that the vector
f(x^{k})=\left(f_{1}(x^{k}),\dots,f_{m}(x^{k})\right)
converges to an element of the Pareto front. However we can refine the statement of Theorem
6.6
in the following way to get a stronger convergence statement under a weak additional assumption.

Theorem 6.7
.

Assume in addition to the assumption in Theorem
6.6
that for all
x\in\mathcal{L}(f(x_{0}))
there exists an
x^{*}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}\coloneqq P_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}(f(x_{0}))
with
f(x^{*})\leq f(x)
and

\displaystyle\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L}^{*})}\inf_{x\in f^{-1}(\{f^{*}\})}\lVert x-x^{0}\rVert<+\infty.

(51)

Then, there exists
R\geq 0
with

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\sigma_{k}(z)\leq\frac{R}{(k+1)^{2}}.

Proof.

Theorem
6.6
gives for all
z\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(z)\leq\frac{2\left(\lVert x^{1}-z\rVert^{2}+\lVert x^{2}-z\rVert^{2}\right)}{s(k+1)^{2}}.

Taking a supremum over this inequality we get

\displaystyle\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L^{*}})}\inf_{z\in f^{-1}(f^{*})}\sigma_{k}(z)\leq\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L^{*}})}\inf_{z\in f^{-1}(f^{*})}\frac{2\left(\lVert x^{1}-z\rVert^{2}+\lVert x^{2}-z\rVert^{2}\right)}{s(k+1)^{2}}.

Since
x^{1},x^{2}\in\mathcal{L}(f(x^{0}))
assumption (
51
) yields

\displaystyle\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L^{*}})}\inf_{z\in f^{-1}(f^{*})}\frac{2\left(\lVert x^{1}-z\rVert^{2}+\lVert x^{2}-z\rVert^{2}\right)}{s(k+1)^{2}}\leq\frac{4R}{s(k+1)^{2}},

with

\displaystyle R=\max_{j=1,2}\left\{\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L^{*}})}\inf_{z\in f^{-1}(f^{*})}\lVert x^{j}-z\rVert^{2}\right\}.

(52)

It remains to show that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\sigma_{k}(z)=\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L^{*}})}\inf_{z\in f^{-1}(f^{*})}\sigma_{k}(z).

Writing out the definition of
\sigma_{k}(z)
, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L^{*}})}\inf_{z\in f^{-1}(f^{*})}\sigma_{k}(z)=\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L^{*}})}\inf_{z\in f^{-1}(f^{*})}\min_{i=1,\dots,m}\left(f_{i}(x^{k})-f_{i}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f^{*}\in f(\mathcal{L^{*}})}\min_{i=1,\dots,m}\left(f_{i}(x^{k})-f_{i}^{*}\right)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{L}^{*}}\min_{i=1,\dots,m}\left(f_{i}(x^{k})-f_{i}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\min_{i=1,\dots,m}\left(f_{i}(x^{k})-f_{i}(z)\right).

∎

The function
u_{0}(x)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\min_{i=1,\dots,m}f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)
attains the value zero if and only if
x
is weakly Pareto optimal. Theorem
6.7
shows that
u_{0}(x^{k})=\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
.

6.5
Relation to Tanabe’s Accelerated Multiobjective Gradient Method

In the recent preprint
[
9
]
, Tanabe, Fukuda and Yamashita define an accelerated proximal gradient method for MOPs with objective functions that have a separable structure of the form
f_{i}(x)=g_{i}(x)+h_{i}(x)
, where
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex, continuously differentiable with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient and
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex, lower semicontinuous and proper for all
i=1,\dots,m
. Since we only treat the case of smooth objective functions
f_{i}
, we set from here on
h_{i}\equiv 0
. Tanabe et al. discovered their method using techniques different from the ones used throughout this paper, using the concept of merit functions. We will not recite their method here but refer the reader to
[
9
]
. To understand the similarity between their method and Algorithm
1
, we investigate the quadratic optimization problems that have to be solved in each iteration of the methods, respectively. In the method from
[
9
]
, the step direction is computed by solving a quadratic optimization problem with the following objective function
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\Psi(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{s}{2}\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(y^{k})\rVert^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\left(f_{i}(x^{k})-f_{i}(y^{k})\right).

Using the first order approximation
f_{i}(y^{k})-f_{i}(x^{k})\approx\langle\nabla f_{i}(y^{k}),y^{k}-x^{k}\rangle
, we get

\displaystyle\Psi(\theta)\approx\frac{s}{2}\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(y^{k})\rVert^{2}+\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(y^{k}),x^{k}-y^{k}\rangle.

Minimizing
\Psi(\theta)
is equivalent to minimizing the function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\Phi(\theta)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{s^{2}}{2}\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(y^{k})\rVert^{2}+\langle s\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(y^{k}),x^{k}-y^{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\lVert x^{k}-y^{k}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert s\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla f_{i}(y^{k})+(x^{k}-y^{k})\rVert^{2}.

Using
x^{k}-y^{k}=-\frac{k-1}{k+2}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})
we note that
\Phi(\theta)
is in fact the objective function of the quadratic optimization problem (
42
). After this observation, it is not surprising that the method by Tanabe et al. shows covergence behavior similar to Algorithm
1
.

First order methods for multiobjective optimization that are based on the steepest descent method by Fliege and Svaiter
[
12

7.2
Backtracking for unknown Lipschitz Constants

In all presented algorithms, we can include backtracking if the Lipschitz constants of the gradients
\nabla f_{i}
of the objective functions are unknown. We can do this as stated in
[
33
,
9
]
. To include backtracking, we choose an initial step size
s_{0}>0
and a parameter
\sigma\in(0,1)
. In all discussed algorithms there is a step
x^{k+1}=w^{k}-sd^{k}
, with
d^{k}\in\mathcal{H}
and
w^{k}=x^{k}
or
w^{k}=y^{k}
. One can replace this step with
x^{k+1}=w^{k}-s_{k}d^{k}
, with a step size
s_{k}
that is determined using backtracking. We choose in every step
s_{k}=\sigma^{l_{k}}s_{k-1}
where
l_{k}\geq 0
is the smallest nonnegative integer satisfying for all
i=1,\dots,m

\displaystyle f_{i}(w^{k}-\sigma^{l_{k}}s_{k-1}d^{k})\leq f_{i}(w^{k})-\sigma^{l_{k}}s_{k-1}\langle\nabla f_{i}(w^{k}),d^{k}\rangle+\frac{\sigma^{l_{k}}s_{k-1}}{2}\lVert d^{k}\rVert^{2}.

The sequence
(s_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is monotonically decreasing by definition. Under the condition that the objective functions posses
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradients, it is guaranteed that the sequence
(s_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is constant from same
k
on. This is true since
s_{k}
can only decrease as long as
s_{k}>\frac{1}{L}
. Therefore,
s_{k}
can only decrease finitely many times until it reaches a point where
s_{k}\leq\frac{1}{L}
. Using this observation, we can include backtracking in Algorithm
1
and still use the proofs of Theorem
6.6
and Theorem
6.7
to show that the same convergence results can be achieved.

9
Conclusion and Open Questions

We present the novel inertial gradient-like dynamical system (
IMOG’
) for Pareto optimization. We show that trajectories of this system converge weakly to Pareto critical points of (
2.2
). Based on this, we define a novel inertial gradient method for multiobjective optimization and show weak convergence to Pareto critical points. We derive an accelerated gradient method from the informally introduced internal gradient-like system (
MAVD
) which incorporates asymptotically vanishing damping. Using the concept of merit functions, we show that our method possesses an improved convergence rate. Using a different discretization of the system (
MAVD
), we define a further accelerated gradient method which does not require the solution to a quadratic optimization problem in every step. A comparison on selected test problems shows that the accelerated methods are in fact superior to the plain multiobjective steepest descent method.

There are a lot of open questions arising from the presented work. The gradient system (
IMOG’
) can be analyzed for different problem classes. Also, we have not formally discussed the system (
MAVD
). It would be interesting to see if we can derive solutions from this system for
\alpha>3
and obtain stronger convergence results similar to the single objective setting. In addition, we can adapt our gradient systems and algorithms to treat problems with a separable smooth and nonsmooth structure using proximal methods. It would also be interesting to investigate the behavior of our algorithms for high-dimensional and nonconvex problems. In addition, one could apply the presented algorithms in the area of machine learning, e.g., for multitask learning problems
[
35
]
.

Appendix A
Two Lemmas on Convex Projections

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space,
C\subset\mathcal{H}
a convex and compact set and
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
a fixed vector. Then,
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
is a solution to the problem

\displaystyle\text{Find $\xi\in\mathcal{H}$ such that : }\,\eta=\proj_{C+\xi}(0),

(58)

if and only if it has the form
\xi=\eta-\mu
, where
\mu
is a solution to the constrained optimization problem
\min_{\mu\in C}\,\langle\mu,\eta\rangle
.

Proof.

First, we show that an element of the form
\xi=\eta-\mu
, with
\mu
a solution to
\min_{\mu\in C}\,\langle\mu,\eta\rangle
is a solution to problem (
58
). The set of minimizers of the problem
\min_{\mu\in C}\,\langle\mu,\eta\rangle
is nonempty, since
C
is compact. Let
\mu\in\argmin_{\mu\in C}\langle\mu,\eta\rangle
. Since
C
is convex, the first order optimality condition for this problem gives that for all
x\in C
it holds that
\langle x-\mu,\eta\rangle\geq 0
and hence

\displaystyle\langle x+\xi-(\mu+\xi),\eta\rangle\geq 0.

Since we have chosen
\xi=\eta-\mu
the equation above reads as

\displaystyle\langle x+\xi-\eta,\eta\rangle\geq 0,

which is equivalent to
\eta=\proj_{C+\xi}(0)
. The other direction works analogously. If the vector
\xi
is a solution to problem (
58
) this guarantees that
\mu=\xi-\eta
satisfies the first order optimality condition for problem
\min_{\mu\in C}\,\langle\mu,\eta\rangle
. Since problem
\min_{\mu\in C}\,\langle\mu,\eta\rangle
is convex and defined over a convex set, this is equivalent to
\mu
being an optimal solution to
\min_{\mu\in C}\,\langle\mu,\eta\rangle
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space,
C\subset\mathcal{H}
a convex and closed set and
a>0,\nu\in\mathcal{H}
fixed, with
a\neq-1
. Then, the problem

\displaystyle\text{Find $\xi\in\mathcal{H}$ such that : }\,-a(\xi+\nu)=\proj_{C+\xi}(0),

(59)

has the unique solution
\xi=-\left(\frac{1}{1+a}\proj_{C}\left(\nu\right)+\frac{a}{1+a}\nu\right)
.

Proof.

First, we show that
\xi=-\left(\frac{1}{1+a}\proj_{C}\left(\nu\right)+\frac{a}{1+a}\nu\right)
is a solution to (
59
). It is easy to check that
-a(\xi+\nu)\in C+\xi
. Define
p\coloneqq\proj_{C}\left(\nu\right)
. For all
x\in C
it holds that
\langle x-p,p-\nu\rangle\geq 0
and hence for all
x\in C
we get

\displaystyle\langle x+\xi+a(\xi+\nu),a(\xi+\nu)\rangle\leq 0,

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle-a(\xi+\nu)=\proj_{C+\xi}(0).

The uniqueness follows the same way. Assume we have a solution
\tilde{\xi}
to (
59
). By the same computations as above it holds that for all
x\in C

\displaystyle\langle x+(1+a)\tilde{\xi}+a\nu,\tilde{\xi}+\nu\rangle\leq 0.

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle-((1+a)\tilde{\xi}+a\nu)=\proj_{C}(\nu),

from which follows that
\xi=\tilde{\xi}
is the unique solution.
∎
