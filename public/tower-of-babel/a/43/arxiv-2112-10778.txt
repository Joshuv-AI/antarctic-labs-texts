Wannier Function Perturbation Theory: Localized Representation and Interpolation of Wavefunction Perturbation
Jae-Mo Lihm, Cheol-Hwan Park

Abstract
Thanks to the nearsightedness principle, the low-energy electronic structure of solids can be represented by localized states such as the Wannier functions. Wannier functions are actively being applied to a wide range of phenomena in condensed matter systems. However, the Wannier-function-based representation is limited to a small number of bands and thus cannot describe the change of wavefunctions due to various kinds of perturbations, which require sums over an infinite number of bands. Here, we introduce the concept of the Wannier function perturbation, which provides a localized representation of wavefunction perturbations. Wannier function perturbation theory allows efficient calculation of numerous quantities involving wavefunction perturbation, among which we provide three applications. First, we calculate the temperature-dependent indirect optical absorption spectra of silicon near the absorption edge nonadiabatically, i.e., differentiating phonon-absorption and phonon-emission processes, and without arbitrary temperature-dependent shifts in energy. Second, we establish a theory to calculate the shift spin conductivity without any band-truncation error. Unlike the shift charge conductivity, an exact calculation of the shift spin conductivity is not possible within the conventional Wannier function methods because it cannot be obtained from geometric quantities for low-energy bands. We apply the theory to monolayer WTe 2 . Third, we calculate the spin Hall conductivity of the same material again without any band-truncation error. Wannier function perturbation theory is a versatile method that can be readily applied to calculate a wide range of quantities related to various kinds of perturbations.

I
Introduction

Most physical and chemical systems are “nearsighted”
Kohn 1996
;
Prodan and Kohn 2005
: Their properties can be studied by inspecting one local region at a time and the long-range effects can be easily approximated.
A localized representation of a system is highly desirable because it enables one to exploit the nearsightedness to its full extent.
Wannier functions (WFs) provide a localized representation of wavefunctions
Wannier 1937
;
Kohn 1959
.
The invention of the maximally localized Wannier function (MLWF) method
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
;
Souza et al. 2001
enabled the calculation of the localized WFs for electronic structures computed using density functional theory (DFT).
Using the MLWFs, the low-energy eigenvalues and eigenstates can be interpolated very efficiently.

Applications of MLWFs are stretched over all fields of condensed matter physics and materials science
Marzari et al. 2012
.
MLWFs are used to efficiently compute electron-phonon coupling (EPC) at a dense grid of electron and phonon wavevectors
Giustino et al. 2007
.
Wannier interpolation of EPC is actively being applied to study transport
Zhou and Bernardi 2016
;
Poncé et al. 2018
, phonon-assisted optical absorption
Noffsinger et al. 2012
, superconductivity
Margine and Giustino 2013
, and ultrafast carrier dynamics
Jhalani et al. 2017
.
Various linear and nonlinear responses of solids to dc and ac electric fields are also routinely calculated using Wannier interpolation
Wang et al. 2006
;
Yates et al. 2007
;
Ibañez-Azpiroz et al. 2018
;
Tsirkin et al. 2018
;
Qiao et al. 2018
;
Ryoo et al. 2019
.

However, one of the most severe limitations of WF interpolation is the limited number of bands being interpolated.
Consequently, WFs cannot represent the wavefunction perturbation, which is the sum of contributions from an infinite number of bands.
Obtaining MLWFs for the high-energy, plane-wave-like bands is a highly nontrivial problem.
Even assuming that the MLWFs are obtained, calculations in the MLWF basis will be much more costly, negating the efficiency of Wannier interpolation.
In DFT calculations, the wavefunction perturbation can be calculated without referring to the high-energy states by solving a linear equation known as the Sternheimer equation
Sternheimer 1954
.
This method forms the basis of the calculation of the linear response properties of solids using density functional perturbation theory (DFPT)
Baroni et al. 2001
.
However, the Sternheimer equation is not directly applicable to WFs since the WFs do not accurately represent the wavefunction perturbation.
Therefore, WF methods cannot deal with properties that require the calculation of wavefunction perturbations.
Ge
et al.

Ge and Lu 2015
developed a local representation of wavefunction perturbation for the dielectric response of insulators.
However, this formalism cannot be applied to WFs for entangled bands
Souza et al. 2001
, while such generalization is necessary for many applications.

A representative example where the interpolation of wavefunction perturbations is needed is the real part of the phonon-induced electron self-energy.
This self-energy causes the temperature dependence and zero-point renormalization of electron band structures
Giustino et al. 2010a
;
Gonze et al. 2011
.
The state-of-the-art method for the perturbative calculation of the real part of the self-energy is to solve the Sternheimer equation using a complete basis set such as the plane waves
Gonze et al. 2011
.
However, such a calculation is too costly to be done on a large number of
k
points.
The high computational cost prohibits the consideration of phonon-induced renormalization at a dense
k
-point mesh in the study of optical and transport properties of materials.
Also, band disentanglement
Souza et al. 2001
is necessary even for the valence band of insulators because the wavefunctions and energy eigenvalues of the low-lying conduction bands are required.

Another example of a quantity involving the wavefunction perturbation is the shift current
Sipe and Shkrebtii 2000
.
For the shift charge current, the wavefunction perturbation can alternatively be expressed as a derivative with respect to the crystal momentum
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
, allowing an exact WF-based calculation
Ibañez-Azpiroz et al. 2018
.
However, such an alternative expression cannot be made for the shift spin current in spin-orbit coupled systems
Lihm and Park
.
Previous calculations of shift spin current in spin-orbit coupled systems truncated the infinite sum over bands
Xu et al. 2021
;
Mu et al. 2021
or used the tight-binding approximation
Lihm and Park
.
Similarly, conventional Wannier interpolation schemes for the spin Hall conductivity
Qiao et al. 2018
;
Ryoo et al. 2019
resorts to the band truncation.
To our knowledge, the effect of band truncation on the shift spin or spin Hall conductivity has not been investigated.

In this work, we develop Wannier function perturbation theory (WFPT), which enables a localized representation of wavefunction perturbation, alleviating the conceptual and practical limitations of the WF method.
We define the Wannier function perturbation (WFP) as the change of the WF due to a perturbation.
The WFPs are spatially localized and thus can be used to efficiently interpolate the perturbation of eigenstate wavefunctions and the related matrix elements.
We provide three applications of WFPT.
The first application is the temperature-dependent indirect optical absorption of silicon.
We calculate the phonon renormalization of the electron band structure using WFPT.
Using the renormalized band structures, we perform a predictive calculation of the temperature dependence of the phonon-assisted optical absorption spectra near the absorption edge.
Our calculation takes into account nonadiabatic effects and does not require arbitrary shifts in energy depending on the temperature.
The second and third applications are the shift spin and spin Hall conductivity of monolayer WTe
2
, respectively.
In these applications, we show that WFPT is necessary to use an ultradense
k
-point grid and eliminate the band-truncation error at the same time.

II
Formalism of Wannier function perturbation theory

Before discussing the formalism of WFPT in detail, let us briefly summarize the key ideas.
First, we Wannierize both the unperturbed and perturbed systems.
We call the corresponding WFs the unperturbed WFs and the perturbed WFs, respectively.
To study the perturbative properties of the WFs, the perturbed WFs must converge smoothly to the unperturbed WFs in the limit of a small perturbation.
To achieve this property, we use the unperturbed WFs as the initial guesses and construct projection-only WFs
Souza et al. 2001
of the perturbed system.
The resulting first-order derivatives of the WFs with respect to the perturbation strength are the WFPs.
Next, we show that the perturbation of the eigenstate wavefunction can be efficiently interpolated using WFPs.
Finally, we study the interpolation of matrix elements that involve wavefunction perturbations.

II.1
Wannier function perturbation

In this subsection, we first introduce the setting for our theory.
Then, we derive the expression for the WFs of the perturbed system and the WFPs.
Finally, we argue that the WFPs are spatially localized.

II.1.1
Setting

The WFs of a perfect crystal can be written as

\ket{w\oord{0}_\iR}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{n,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\ket{\psi\oord{0}_\nk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{ni;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}{e^{-i{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\cdot{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}}}.

(1)

Here,
\ket{\psi\oord{0}_\nk}
is the unperturbed electron eigenstate for band
n
and crystal momentum
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
,
i=1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{W}}}
the WF index,
{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}
the unit cell position, and

N_{k}
the size of the Born-von Karman supercell.
The semi-unitary gauge transformation matrix
U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{ni;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}
is chosen to make
\ket{w\oord{0}_\iR}
spatially localized.
The unperturbed eigenstates satisfy

\innerproduct{\psi\oord{0}_\mk}{\psi\oord{0}_\nkp}=\delta_{mn}\delta_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}.

(2)

We apply a perturbation
\lambda\hat{V}
, where
\lambda
is a small perturbation parameter.
The perturbation
\hat{V}
may or may not break the periodicity of the lattice but should respect the Born-von Karman periodic boundary condition.
The only additional condition we impose on the perturbation
\hat{V}
is that
\matrixelement{\mb{r}}{\opV}{\mb{r'}}
should be small if
\absolutevalue{\mb{r} - \mb{r'}}
is large.
This condition is necessary for the perturbed system to be nearsighted.
Any local potentials, phonon perturbation potentials that contain contributions from nonlocal pseudopotentials, velocity operator
\hat{v}^{a}=[\hat{r}^{a},\hat{H}^{(0)}]/i\hbar
, where
\hat{H}^{(0)}
is the unperturbed Hamiltonian, and spin-velocity operator
\hat{j}^{s;a}=\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{\hat{S}_s}{\hat{v}^a}
all fulfill this condition.
Here,
\hat{S}_{s}
is the spin operator for spin polarization along
s
.

Eigenstates of the perturbed system can be expanded as

\ket{\psi_\nk}=\ket{\psi\oord{0}_\nk}+\lambda\ket{\psi\oord{1}_\nk}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2}),

(3)

where the wavefunction perturbation is

\ket{\psi\oord{1}_\nk}=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}\ket{\psizero_\npkp}\frac{\matrixelement{\psizero_\npkp}{\opV}{\psizero_\nk}}{\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}.

(4)

Hereafter, the primed sum denotes that the terms making the denominator zero are excluded.
For degenerate states, the true perturbed eigenstates in the
\lambda\rightarrow 0
limit will, in general, converge to the unperturbed eigenstates that diagonalize
\hat{V}
in the degenerate subspace, not to
\ket{\psizero_\nk}
.
In this case, we linearly combine the perturbed eigenstates so that they are no longer eigenstates of the perturbed Hamiltonian but satisfy Eqs. (
3
,
4
).
This operation is allowed because our goal is to construct WFs of the perturbed system, and there is gauge freedom in constructing the WFs that allows such a linear combination.
We note that this treatment is needed only to make the derivation more concise, while, in practice, one does not have any issue with degeneracy because of the careful use of energy windows that will be described in the following.

II.1.2
WFs of the perturbed system and the WFPs

We aim to accurately describe the wavefunction perturbation of the low-energy states whose eigenvalues are inside the “perturbation window”
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
.
We denote the inner (frozen) and outer (disentanglement) windows for the unperturbed system by
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}
and
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
, respectively
Souza et al. 2001
.
The following inclusion relation should hold:

{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}

(5)

This relation is illustrated in Fig.
1
.
In principle,
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
can be chosen to be identical to
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}
.
Using
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
slightly narrower than
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}
is useful when the perturbation mixes the states right above and below the boundary of the inner window for the unperturbed system.
For later use, we define the projection operators onto the subspaces spanned by the unperturbed eigenstates inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
and
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
as
{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{P}}}
and
{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}
, respectively, and we also define
{\hat{Q}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}=\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}
.
Additionally,
N_{X}
and
N_{X,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}
denote the number of unperturbed states inside
{\mathcal{W}}_{X}
, and those with crystal momentum
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
, respectively, for
X=\mathrm{P},\mathrm{F},\mathrm{D}
.
We only consider small
\lambda
such that no eigenvalues cross the boundary of the windows as
\lambda
increases from 0.
Another important subspace is the unperturbed Wannier subspace, of which the projection operator is

{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}=\sum_{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{W}}}}\ket{w_\iR\oord{0}}\bra{w_\iR\oord{0}}.

(6)

By construction,
{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{P}}}\subseteq{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\subseteq{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}
holds, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
We define “subspaces
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
” as shown in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:

Schematic illustration of the subspaces and energy windows.
The vertical lines indicate the separation of the full Hilbert space into subspaces.
Solid vertical lines separating different regions indicate the separation by energy eigenvalues.
The dashed vertical line separates the subspace spanned by the unperturbed WFs from its complement and does not indicate an energetic separation.
The “high-energy” states may also include deep semicore states which are excluded in the Wannierization process.

In general, the perturbed WFs need not make any reference to the unperturbed WFs.
However, to study the perturbative property of the WFs, the WFs must change smoothly around
\lambda=0
.
Our key idea to make the WFs smooth functions of
\lambda
is to use the unperturbed WFs as the initial guesses for the projection-only WFs of the perturbed system.
For Wannierization of the perturbed system, the inner and outer windows are set to
{\mathcal{W}}_{\rm P}
and
{\mathcal{W}}_{\rm D}
, respectively.
Using the perturbation window as the inner window is natural because only the states inside the perturbation window need to be accurately represented.
The outer window is set to make the conditions for Wannierization of the perturbed system as similar as possible to that of the unperturbed system.
This condition guarantees that the perturbed WFs are close to the unperturbed WFs; hence, it makes the WFPs well localized.
See Appendix
B
for the numerical verification that this choice is indeed beneficial.

Now, we explicitly construct the perturbed WFs.
First, we select the subspace to be Wannierized following the initialization recipe described in Sec. III.G of Ref.
Souza et al. 2001
.
We take the perturbed eigenstates inside the inner window
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
directly.
For the states not included in
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
, we project
{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}
, the projection operator for the unperturbed Wannier subspace, onto the subspace
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
and diagonalize it.
Since we are Wannierizing a supercell, we need
N_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}{N_{\mathrm{W}}}
WFs.
Hence, we select
N_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}{N_{\mathrm{W}}}-N_{\rm P}
states inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
with the largest eigenvalues
Souza et al. 2001
together with
N_{\rm P}
states inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
and use them as the basis states for the perturbed WFs.

After we obtain the disentangled subspace, we compute the perturbed WFs.
Following the scheme of Ref.
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
, we project the initial guess functions, the unperturbed WFs, onto the disentangled subspace and perform Löwdin orthonormalization.
The resulting WFs of the perturbed system are (see Appendix
A
for the detailed derivation)

\displaystyle\ket{w_\iR(\lambda)}=\ket{w^{(0)}_\iR}+\lambda\ket{w^{(1)}_\iR}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2}),

(7)

where

\displaystyle\ket{w^{(1)}_\iR}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}{\hat{Q}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mi,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}

(8)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\\
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}\end{subarray}}\left({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mi;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}.

We call
\ket{w^{(1)}_\iR}
, the first-order derivative of a WF at
\lambda=0
, the “Wannier function perturbation.”
The concept of WFP and its explicit form [Eq. (
8
)] is the first main theoretical result of this paper.

The WFPs can be computed without explicitly referring to the states outside the outer window.
The first term of Eq. (
8
) can be evaluated by solving the Sternheimer equation to obtain the wavefunction perturbation for states
{m\mathbf{k}}
inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
.
The second term of Eq. (
8
) can be rewritten using

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\\
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\left({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\\
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{m^{\prime}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{n^{\prime},{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}}\ket{\psizero_\mpkp}

\displaystyle\times\left(P^{(\mathrm{0})}_{\rm D}-P^{(\mathrm{0})}_{\rm W}\right)_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime};{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}\frac{\matrixelement{\psizero_\npkp}{\opV}{\psizero_\mk}}{\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}.

(9)

Evaluating this expression requires only the eigenenergies and matrix elements of
\hat{V}
and
{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}
for states inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
.

The formula for the WFPs [Eq. (
8
)] can be intuitively understood from the partition of subspaces shown in Fig.
1
.
First, consider subspaces
1
and
2
, whose union is the unperturbed Wannier subspace. The wavefunction perturbation in this subspace is not included in the WFPs because it can be reconstructed from the perturbation theory of the WF-based tight-binding Hamiltonian.
Second, consider subspace
3
, which includes the states inside the outer window but not selected for Wannierization. To accurately describe the wavefunction perturbation, these states should be included in the WFPs.
However, the contribution of these states to the perturbation of eigenstates in subspace
2
might diverge because the separation of subspaces
2
and
3
is done by Wannierization not by the energy eigenvalues.
In Eq. (
8
), this problem is absent as the contribution of subspace
3
is included only if
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
.
From Eq. (
II.1.2
), one can find that this condition ensures that the denominator
\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}
is not zero because states
{m\mathbf{k}}
and
{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}
are inside subspaces
1
and
3
, respectively.
The last subspace, subspace
4
, is energetically separated from other states by the outer window
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
.
Its contribution to the wavefunction perturbation is included for all states as shown in the first term of Eq. (
8
).
The separation of energy ranges by the windows is the key idea in WFPT which makes the WFPs spatially localized.

II.1.3
Spatial localization of the WFPs

The most important property of the WFPs is their spatial localization.
We provide two arguments for the localization of the WFPs.
First, the localization of WFPs follows from the localization of the perturbed WFs.
There exists much empirical evidence that the WFs are well localized in real space if a proper initial guess is used
He and Vanderbilt 2001
;
Yates et al. 2007
;
Marzari et al. 2012
.
The initial guesses we used for the WFs of the perturbed system are the MLWFs of the unperturbed system, which is an ideal guess in the
\lambda\rightarrow 0
limit.
Thus, it is very likely that the perturbed WFs are also localized, at least for small
\lambda
.
Since the WFPs are the derivative of the WFs at
\lambda=0
,
the localization of the WFs for a finite range of
\lambda
including
\lambda=0
implies the localization of the WFPs.

The second argument is that the summands of Eq. (
8
) are almost smooth functions of
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
.
The summand being smooth in reciprocal space implies localization in the real space.
We emphasize that the condition
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
in the second term of Eq. (
8
) plays a crucial role.
If this condition is absent, the denominator
\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}
in Eq. (
II.1.2
) can be zero, leading to a divergence in the summand and rendering the WFPs delocalized.
In our construction, the denominator never becomes zero because
\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}
is forced to be inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
(subspace
1
), while
\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}
is forced to be in subspace
3
.

Although these two arguments are not rigorous in a mathematical sense, they provide a solid justification that the WFPs are spatially localized.
We note that the localization of disentangled MLWFs is also an empirical fact without a mathematical proof
Marzari et al. 2012
;
Cornean et al. 2019
.
Later, we numerically demonstrate that the WFPs and the matrix elements involving WFPs are localized.

One source of singularity in Eq. (
8
) is the discontinuity that occurs when
\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}
crosses
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
. In this case, the summand may change discontinuously because of the condition
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
, although it does not diverge.
This discontinuity can be removed by smoothing the inner window, as described in Appendix
C
.
However, for the systems studied in this work, we find that the WFPs are already sufficiently localized without this additional step.
Hence, we use the WFPs as defined in Eq. (
8
).

We also note that if two bands lie close to the boundary of the outer window from inside and outside, respectively, at some
k
point, the first term of Eq. (
8
) can give a large contribution rapidly changing with
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
.
In such a rare case, one can locally, i.e., at that particular
k
point, extend the outer window to include the band outside the original outer window to make the summand change slowly.
The local extension of the outer window can be done just for the construction of WFPs without affecting the unperturbed WFs.
In the applications studied in this paper, we did not perform this modification as the WFPs were already sufficiently localized.

II.2
Interpolation of wavefunction perturbation

Next, we study how to interpolate the wavefunction perturbation of Bloch eigenstates using the WFPs.
In this subsection, we first construct the perturbed tight-binding model using the matrix elements of the perturbed Hamiltonian in the perturbed WF basis.
Then, we use first-order perturbation theory to obtain the eigenvector of this tight-binding model.
Finally, we calculate the wavefunction perturbation.

II.2.1
Perturbed tight-binding Hamiltonian

Let us calculate the Hamiltonian matrix elements of the perturbed system in the perturbed WF basis.
The Hamiltonian matrix element is

\displaystyle H_{{i^{\prime}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}},{i\mathbf{R}}}=

\displaystyle\matrixelement{w_\ipRp}{(\opH\oord{0} + \lambda\opV)}{w_\iR}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{(\mathrm{0})}_{{i^{\prime}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}},{i\mathbf{R}}}+\lambda H^{(\mathrm{1})}_{{i^{\prime}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}},{i\mathbf{R}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2}),

(10)

where

H^{(\mathrm{0})}_{{i^{\prime}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}},{i\mathbf{R}}}=\matrixelement{w^\ord{0}_\ipRp}{\opH^\ord{0}}{w^\ord{0}_{\iR}}

(11)

is the unperturbed Hamiltonian matrix element and

\displaystyle H^{(\mathrm{1})}_{{i^{\prime}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}},{i\mathbf{R}}}=

\displaystyle\matrixelement{w^\ord{0}_\ipRp}{\opV}{w^\ord{0}_{\iR}}

(12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\matrixelement{w^\ord{1}_\ipRp}{\opH^\ord{0}}{w^\ord{0}_{\iR}}+\matrixelement{w^\ord{0}_\ipRp}{\opH^\ord{0}}{w^\ord{1}_{\iR}}

is the perturbative correction.
Note that
H^{(\mathrm{1})}_{{i^{\prime}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}},{i\mathbf{R}}}
contains a direct contribution from
\hat{V}
as well as indirect contributions from the change of the WFs.

We write the perturbation
\hat{V}
as a sum of monochromatic perturbations

\hat{V}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}\hat{V}_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}},

(13)

where each monochromatic perturbation satisfies

\matrixelement{\mb{r+R}}{\opV_\mb{q}}{\mb{r'+R}}={e^{i{{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}\cdot{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}}}\matrixelement{\mb{r}}{\opV_\mb{q}}{\mb{r'}}

(14)

for every lattice vector
{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}
.
Correspondingly, the WFPs can be written as

\ket{w\oord{1}_\iR}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}\ket{w\oord{1}_{\iR;\mb{q}}},

(15)

where

\innerproduct{\mb{r+R'}}{w\oord{1}_{i\mb{R+R'};\mb{q}}}={e^{i{{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}\cdot{{\bm{\mathbf{R^{\prime}}}}}}}\innerproduct{\mb{r}}{w\oord{1}_{i\mb{R};\mb{q}}}.

(16)

We also define real-space matrix elements

g_{ij{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}\equiv\matrixelement{w\oord{0}_{i\mb{0}}}{\opV_\mb{q}}{w\oord{0}_{j\mb{R}}},

(17)

\delta g_{ij{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}\equiv\matrixelement{w^\ord{0}_{i\mb{0}}}{\opH^\ord{0}}{w^\ord{1}_{\jR;\mb{q}}}+\matrixelement{w^\ord{1}_{i\mb{0};\mb{-q}}}{\opH^\ord{0}}{w^\ord{0}_\jR},

(18)

and

\widetilde{g}_{ij{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}\equiv g_{ij{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}+\delta g_{ij{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}.

(19)

Here,
g_{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}
is the usual WF-based matrix element of
\hat{V}_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}
, and
\delta g_{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}
is a correction originating from the change of the WFs.
This correction term is not needed for calculating the scattering matrix elements, but it is necessary when calculating the perturbed wavefunctions.
We define the Fourier transformation of
g_{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}
as

g^{\mathrm{(W)}}_{ij{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}=\sum_{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}g_{ij{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}},

(20)

and analogously, we define
\widetilde{g}^{\mathrm{(W)}}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}
.

II.2.2
Perturbed tight-binding eigenvector

The Hamiltonian of the perturbed system in the Bloch representation is

H^{(\mathrm{\rm W})}_{i^{\prime}{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}},i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}},{\bm{\mathbf{R^{\prime}}}}}H_{{i^{\prime}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}},{i\mathbf{R}}}{e^{-i{{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}\cdot{{\bm{\mathbf{R^{\prime}}}}}}}{e^{i{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\cdot{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}}}.

(21)

Since the unperturbed system has lattice translation symmetry, the zeroth-order part of
H^{(\mathrm{\rm W})}
is block diagonal:

H^{(\mathrm{\rm W0})}_{i^{\prime}{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}},i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}=\delta_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}},{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}H^{(\mathrm{\rm W0})}_{i^{\prime}i;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}=\delta_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}},{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}H^{(\mathrm{0})}_{i^{\prime}{\bm{\mathbf{0}}},i{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}.

(22)

The unperturbed Hamiltonian
H^{(\mathrm{\rm W0})}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}
can be diagonalized as

(V^{(0)\dagger}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}H^{(\mathrm{\rm W0})}_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}})_{mn}=\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{\rm H0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}\delta_{m,n},

(23)

where
V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
is an
{N_{\mathrm{W}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{W}}}
unitary matrix.
In the Born-von Karman supercell, the unperturbed eigenvector is

V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{{i\mathbf{R}},{n\mathbf{k}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{in;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}.

(24)

Next, we consider the perturbative correction.
Using Eqs. (
14
,
16
), one can show that the Fourier transformation of
H^{(\mathrm{1})}_{{i^{\prime}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}},{i\mathbf{R}}}
is

\displaystyle H^{(\mathrm{\rm W1})}_{i^{\prime}{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}},i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}=

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}^{\mathrm{(W)}}_{i^{\prime}i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}-k}}}}

(25)

(see Appendix
D
for a proof).
The matrix element of
H^{(\mathrm{\rm W1})}
in the unperturbed eigenstate basis is

H^{(\mathrm{H1})}_{{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}},{n\mathbf{k}}}=\left(V^{{(\mathrm{0})}\dagger}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}\widetilde{g}^{\mathrm{(W)}}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}-k}}}}V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\right)_{n^{\prime}n}\equiv\widetilde{g}^{(\mathrm{H})}_{n^{\prime}n{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}-k}}}}.

(26)

We also define
g^{(\mathrm{H})}
analogously.

We now apply first-order perturbation theory to the tight-binding Hamiltonian.
The perturbed eigenvector in the WF basis is

\displaystyle V_{{i\mathbf{R}},{n\mathbf{k}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{in;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}

(27)

\displaystyle+\lambda\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{n^{\prime}=1}}^{{N_{\mathrm{W}}}}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{in^{\prime};{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}}\frac{\widetilde{g}^{(\mathrm{H})}_{n^{\prime}n{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{k^{\prime}-k}}}}}{\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2}).

II.2.3
Calculation of wavefunction perturbation

We can now calculate the wavefunction perturbation as we know the basis functions (the perturbed WF
\ket{w_\iR}
) as well as the coefficients (the perturbed eigenvector
V_{{i\mathbf{R}},{n\mathbf{k}}}
) for the perturbed wavefunction.
The perturbed wavefunction reads

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^\ord{\rm H}_\nk}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}\ket{w_\iR}V_{{i\mathbf{R}},{n\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^\ord{\rm H0}_\nk}+\lambda\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}\ket{\psi^\ord{\rm H1}_{\nk;\mb{q}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2}),

(28)

where

\ket{\psi^\mathrm{(H0)}_\nk}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{i,{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}\ket{w^\mathrm{(0)}_\iR}V^{(0)}_{in,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}

(29)

and

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^\ord{\rm H1}_{\nk;\mb{q}}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{i,{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}\ket{w^\ord{1}_{\iR;\mb{q}}}V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{in;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{m=1}}^{{N_{\mathrm{W}}}}\ket{\psi^\ord{H0}_{m\mb{k+q}}}\frac{\widetilde{g}^{(\mathrm{H})}_{mn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}}{\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{m{\bm{\mathbf{k+q}}}}}.

(30)

Equation (
II.2.3
) contains contributions from both the WFP and the perturbation of the tight-binding eigenvector
V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{in;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}
.

Equation (
II.2.3
) is the interpolation equation for the first-order wavefunction perturbation.
This equation is the second main theoretical result of this paper.
The contribution of the states outside
{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}
, the unperturbed Wannier subspace, to the wavefunction perturbation is taken into account by the WFPs and is the first term of Eq. (
II.2.3
).
The contribution of the states inside
{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}
is the second term, which is derived by the perturbation theory in the tight-binding model.
Since the WFPs are spatially localized, the wavefunction perturbation converges quickly with the number of lattice vectors used in the sum over
{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}
.

First, we consider quantities of the form

\displaystyle S_{mn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}=

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\psi^\ord{H1}_{\mk;\mb{q}}}{\opV'_\mb{q}}{\psi^\ord{H0}_{\nk}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime},{\bm{\mathbf{R}}},{\bm{\mathbf{R^{\prime}}}}}V^{(0)\dagger}_{mi^{\prime},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\matrixelement{w^\ord{1}_{\ipRp;\mb{q}}}{\opV'_\mb{q}}{w^\ord{0}_\iR}V^{(\mathrm{0})}_{in;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot({\bm{\mathbf{R-R^{\prime}}}})}+\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{p=1}}^{{N_{\mathrm{W}}}}\frac{\left(\widetilde{g}^{(\mathrm{H})}_{pm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}\right)^{*}\matrixelement{\psi^\ord{H0}_{p\mb{k+q}}}{\opV'_\mb{q}}{\psi^\ord{H0}_\nk}}{\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{p{\bm{\mathbf{k+q}}}}}

and the WFPs [Eq. (

II.3.2
Interpolation of

Next, we show that the matrix element

\displaystyle K_{mn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}=

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\psi^\ord{1}_{\mk;\mb{q}}}{\psi'^{(1)}_{\nk;\mb{q}}}

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{p}}\frac{\matrixelement{\psi^\ord{0}_\mk}{\opV_\mb{q}^\dagger}{\psi^\ord{0}_{p\mb{k+q}}}\matrixelement{\psi^\ord{0}_{p\mb{k+q}}}{\opV'_\mb{q}}{\psi^\ord{0}_\nk}}{(\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{p{\bm{\mathbf{k+q}}}})(\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{0})}_{p{\bm{\mathbf{k+q}}}})}.

can similarly be interpolated using WFPT.
This matrix element is relevant to the evaluation of the Kubo formula for noninteracting quasiparticles.
When
{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}=0
and
\hat{V}_{\bm{\mathbf{0}}}
and
\hat{V}^{\prime}_{\bm{\mathbf{0}}}
are the velocity and spin-velocity operators, respectively,
K_{nn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}
is directly related to the spin Berry curvature and the spin Hall conductivity.

Defining the real-space matrix element

k_{ij{\bm{\mathbf{R}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}\equiv\innerproduct{w^{(1)}_{i\mb{0};\mb{q}}}{w^{(1)}_{i\mb{0};\mb{q}}}{w^{\prime(1)}_{{j\mathbf{R}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}},

(38)

can be interpolated as

(39)

k^{(\mathrm{H})}_{mn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}\equiv\left(V^{(0)\dagger}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}k^{(\mathrm{W})}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}V^{(0)}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\right)_{mn}

(40)

and

II.4
Proof-of-principles example: Berry curvature

Figure 3:

Band-resolved Berry curvature of monolayer WTe
2
. VBM stands for the highest-energy valence band, or valence band maximum.
The plane-wave calculation is done by solving the Sternheimer equation, Eq. (
II.4
), to directly evaluate Eq. (
42
).

Now, let us present a proof-of-principles example of WFPT interpolation by interpolating the band-resolved Berry curvature.
Since the Berry curvature strongly oscillates in momentum space due to the contribution from nearby bands, we exclude the intra-valence-band contribution as follows:

\widetilde{\Omega}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{ab}=-2\imaginary\matrixelement{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^a}}{\opQ_{\mb{k},{\rm valence}}}{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^b}}\,.

(42)

Here,
\ket{u_\mk}=e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}}}\ket{\psi_\mk}
and
{\hat{Q}}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}},{\rm valence}}=\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}},{\rm valence}}
, with
{\hat{P}}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}},{\rm valence}}=\sum_{n\in{\rm valence}}\ket{u_\nk}\bra{u_\nk}
the projection operator to the valence bands at
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
.
By “valence bands,” we mean only the occupied bands inside the outer window for Wannierization,
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
: The low-lying bands that are outside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
are not included in
{\hat{P}}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}},{\rm valence}}
.

The Berry curvature can be calculated using WFPT interpolation.
Since the velocity operator is the derivative of the Hamiltonian with respect to
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
, the derivative of the wavefunction is equivalent to the wavefunction perturbation due to a velocity operator perturbation.
Hence, by choosing the velocity operators as the perturbations for the WFPs,
\hat{V}_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}=\hat{v}^{a}\delta_{{\bm{\mathbf{q}}},{\bm{\mathbf{0}}}}
and
\hat{V}^{\prime}_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}=\hat{v}^{b}\delta_{{\bm{\mathbf{q}}},{\bm{\mathbf{0}}}}
, we have

\ket{\psi^{(1)}_\mk}=e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}}\ket{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^a}}\text{ and }\ket{\psi^{'(1)}_\mk}=e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}}\ket{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^b}}.

(43)

Using Eq. (
37
) and Eq. (
39
), we find

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Omega}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{ab}=

\displaystyle-2\imaginary\left(\innerproduct{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^a}}{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^b}}-\sum_{n\in{\rm valence}}\innerproduct{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^a}}{u_\nk}\innerproduct{u_\nk}{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^b}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\imaginary\left(\innerproduct{\psi^{(1)}_\mk}{\psi^{'(1)}_\mk}-\sum_{n\in{\rm valence}}\innerproduct{\psi^{(1)}_\mk}{\psi^{(0)}_\nk}\innerproduct{\psi^{(0)}_\nk}{\psi^{'(1)}_\mk}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\imaginary\left(K_{mm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}-\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
n\in{\rm valence}\end{subarray}}}}^{{N_{\mathrm{W}}}}\frac{\left(g^{\rm(H)}_{nm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\right)^{*}g^{\prime{\rm(H)}}_{nm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}{(\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{n\mathbf{k}})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\imaginary\left(k^{\mathrm{(H)}}_{mm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
n\notin{\rm valence}\end{subarray}}}}^{{N_{\mathrm{W}}}}\frac{\left(\widetilde{g}^{{\rm(H)}}_{nm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\right)^{*}\widetilde{g}^{\prime{\rm(H)}}_{nm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}{(\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{n\mathbf{k}})^{2}}\right).

(44)

This equation is the WFPT interpolation formula for
\widetilde{\Omega}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{ab}
.
In the last equality, we used the fact that in the limit of exact interpolation,
g^{\rm(H)}_{nm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}=\widetilde{g}^{\rm(H)}_{nm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}
holds for
{m\mathbf{k}},{n\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
, which can easily be shown to follow from the
P^{(\mathrm{0})}_{\mathrm{D}}-P^{(\mathrm{0})}_{\mathrm{W}}
terms in Eq. (
F.1
).
Because of the interpolation error,
\delta g^{(\mathrm{H})}_{nm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}=0
holds exactly only if
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
belongs to the coarse
k
-point grid for Wannier interpolation.
But, the deviation at a generic
{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
is small and can be diminished by using a denser coarse
k
-point grid for Wannierization, as with all other Wannier-interpolated quantities.

We also calculate the Berry curvature directly in the plane-wave basis by solving the following Sternheimer equation to calculate
\hat{Q}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}},\rm{valence}}\ket{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^a}}
:

\displaystyle\left(\hat{H}({\bm{\mathbf{k}}})-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}}\right)\hat{Q}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}},\rm{valence}}\ket{\frac{\partial u_\mk}{\partial k^a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
n\notin{\rm valence}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\ket{u_{n\mb{k}}}\bra{u_{n\mb{k}}}\right)\frac{\partial\hat{H}({\bm{\mathbf{k}}})}{\partial k^{a}}\ket{u_\mk},

(45)

where
\hat{H}({\bm{\mathbf{k}}})=e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}}}\hat{H}e^{i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}}}
.
The conventional Wannier interpolation method is also exact for the calculation of Berry curvature
Wang et al. 2006
.
There,
\widetilde{\Omega}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{ab}
is calculated by first calculating the full Berry curvature and then subtracting the intra-valence-band contribution.

Figure
3
shows the Berry curvature of four valence bands of monolayer WTe
2
calculated using WFPT interpolation, direct plane-wave calculations, and exact conventional Wannier interpolation.
The WFPT result agrees perfectly with the plane-wave and Wannier interpolation results, demonstrating the validity of the WFPT interpolation method.

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm Fan}_{nn^{\prime}{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\mathbf{q}\nu}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}g_{mn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}\nu}^{*}\,g_{mn^{\prime}{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}\nu}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\pm}\frac{n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+[1\pm(2f_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}-1)]/2}{\omega-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\pm\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+i\eta},

(47)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm DW}_{nn^{\prime}{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\mathbf{q}\nu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+\frac{1}{2}}{2\omega_{{\mathbf{q}\nu}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,\kappa,a\\
\kappa^{\prime},a^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}_{nn^{\prime}}^{\kappa a\kappa^{\prime}a^{\prime}}({\bm{\mathbf{k}}},{\bm{\mathbf{q}}})U_{\kappa a,\nu}^{*}({\bm{\mathbf{q}}})U_{\kappa^{\prime}a^{\prime},\nu}({\bm{\mathbf{q}}}).

Here,

{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}
in units of inverse square root of mass,

the Fermi-Dirac occupation factor, and

In practice, the DW matrix element is calculated within the rigid-ion approximation

;
Poncé et al. 2014

Under the on-the-mass-shell approximation

;
Miglio et al. 2020
, the diagonal part of the self-energy of the state

is
\Sigma_{n\mathbf{k}}=\Sigma_{nn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}})
.
In this work, we only consider the diagonal part of the self-energy because the off-diagonal part has little effect on silicon, which has a band gap much larger than the band energy renormalization

.

In practical calculations, the Fan term is separated into the lower Fan and the upper Fan terms, which are the contributions from the low-energy and high-energy bands, respectively.
We write the set of low-energy states as
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lower}}
.
The upper bound of the energy window for
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lower}}
is chosen to be a few eV above

We set
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lower}}
to be inside the perturbation window, which is necessary because the lower Fan term [Eq. (
III.1
)] requires an explicit Wannier interpolation of the EPC matrix elements for bands inside
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lower}}
.

In the upper Fan term, one neglects the phonon frequency
\omega_{{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}\nu}
and
\eta

is always much larger than

and
\eta

The lower and upper Fan terms read

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm lower\ Fan}_{n{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\mathbf{q}\nu}}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\\
m{\bm{\mathbf{k+q}}}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lower}}\end{subarray}}\absolutevalue{g_{mn\mb{k};\mb{q}\nu}}^{2}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\pm}\frac{n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+[1\pm(2f_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}-1)]/2}{\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\pm\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+i\eta}\,,

(49)

and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm upper\ Fan}_{n{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\mathbf{q}\nu}}=(2n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\\

The DW self-energy under the rigid-ion approximation
Allen and Heine 1976
;
Poncé et al. 2014

.
The lower Fan term [Eq. (

Calculation of the upper Fan term [Eq. (
50

The reason is that the upper Fan term involves a sum over an infinite number of bands.
Typically, a few hundred bands are needed to reach convergence

;
Gonze et al. 2011

Wannier interpolation of such a large number of bands is impractical, if not impossible.

Using WFPT, the upper Fan term can be easily interpolated without explicitly performing the sum over bands.
By choosing
\hat{V}_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}
and
\hat{V}^{\prime}_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}

in the definition of

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{nn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};\nu{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\

\displaystyle s^{(\mathrm{H})}_{nn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};\nu{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\

An alternative method to study EPC is the adiabatic supercell method, where supercells with distorted lattice structures are used

;

The major speedup of the interpolation scheme comes from the calculation of the lower Fan term, where both the

Although this speedup comes from the conventional WF interpolation, WFPT interpolation enables one to consistently calculate the self-energy in the WF basis.

Assigning band indices to those two calculations when the bands are

The WFPT interpolation of the upper Fan self-energy for the fine

The speedup of the WFPT interpolation will be much greater if a wider energy window is considered, or if the valence and conduction bands have a larger effective mass.
Even for silicon, the speedup will reach the above-mentioned factor of over 50 as the number of points in the fine
k

We note that the WFP interpolation of EPC-induced electron self-energy is conceptually completely different from the Wannier interpolation of the
GW
quasiparticle band structure
Hamann and Vanderbilt 2009
.
In the latter, the
GW
quasiparticle energy is first calculated for all bands to be Wannierized and then Wannier interpolated.
However, in the WFP interpolation, the matrix elements are Wannier interpolated, and then the self-energy is calculated.
We briefly discuss why one should not directly interpolate the phonon-induced electron self-energy.
First, one may try to calculate the self-energy on the coarse
k
grid before Wannierization.
However, doing so is very inefficient because one needs to sample
q
points on a very fine grid to converge the self-energy.
Second, one may try to interpolate only the sum of the upper Fan and DW terms, as it converges at a relatively coarse
q
grid.
However, to do so, one must set
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lower}}
to be outside the
outer
window
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
because the upper Fan term should be calculated for all states inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
for Wannierization.
Then, the lower Fan terms cannot be calculated using Wannier interpolation because the contribution from the states that are excluded from Wannierization, which are outside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}
but inside
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lower}}
, is not captured.
In our method, we get around all these problems by using WFPT to interpolate the upper Fan self-energy and by using electron and phonon WFs to efficiently calculate the EPC on a fine
k
and
q
grid.

the electron charge, and

the Fermi-Dirac occupation factor.
Contrary to the Berry curvature, the spin Berry curvature is not a geometric quantity.
Thus, in principle, an infinite number of bands should be included to evaluate Eq. (

For example, Ref.

Moreover, a very fine

However, conventional Wannier interpolation methods for computing spin Hall conductivity

;
Ryoo et al. 2019
truncate the sum over band
n
in Eq. (
61
) to include only the Wannier-interpolated bands.

By solving the Sternheimer equation, one can accurately calculate the spin Berry curvature and the spin Hall conductivity without explicitly including the high-energy bands.
To our knowledge, the spin Hall conductivity has not been studied using the Sternheimer-equation-based method.
However, the computational cost of the Sternheimer approach is much higher than Wannier interpolation for the same
k
-point grid.
WFPT enables an efficient Wannier-interpolation calculation of the spin Hall conductivity without any band-truncation error, taking the advantages of both the Sternheimer method and the WF method.

The spin Berry curvature can be calculated by WFPT interpolation similarly to the case of the spin-velocity derivative.
We set
\hat{V}_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}=\hat{j}^{s,a}\delta_{{\bm{\mathbf{q}}},{\bm{\mathbf{0}}}}
and
\hat{V}^{\prime}_{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}=\hat{v}^{b}\delta_{{\bm{\mathbf{q}}},{\bm{\mathbf{0}}}}
and use Eq. (

) to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{s,ab}_{{m\mathbf{k}}}\approx

\displaystyle K_{mm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}-\beta_{{m\mathbf{k}}}(0)+\beta_{{m\mathbf{k}}}(\eta)=k^{(\mathrm{H})}_{mm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+\beta_{{m\mathbf{k}}}(\eta)

(63)

where

\beta_{{m\mathbf{k}}}(\eta)=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{{n=1}}^{{N_{\mathrm{W}}}}\frac{\imaginary\left[\widetilde{j}^{s,a\rm(H)}_{mn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\widetilde{v}^{b\rm(H)}_{nm{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\right]}{\left(\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon^{(\mathrm{H0})}_{n\mathbf{k}}\right)^{2}+\eta^{2}}.

(64)

Here, we approximate that for

and
{n\mathbf{k}}\notin{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}
,
\absolutevalue{\veps_\mk- \veps_\nk}\gg\eta

In practice, we avoid the sums over pairs of fully occupied states

and

IV
Discussion

If one is only interested in the valence bands of an insulator, one may choose all the windows,
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
,
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}
, and
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
, to include only the occupied bands.
If WFPT is applied to the electronic dielectric response of such insulators, the WFP derived in this work simplifies to the result of Ref.
Ge and Lu 2015
, i.e., Eq. (
8
) without the second term on the right-hand side.
We note, however, that even the Wannierization of only the valence bands may not be possible in the case of narrow-gap semiconductors or topological insulators where the orbital characters of the valence bands change rapidly with
k
.
In the case of such semiconductors, where the low-energy conduction bands need to be interpolated and thus disentanglement is required, or that of metals, the simplified results do not apply.
Our formulation of WFPT is applicable to both isolated and entangled bands and provides a clear physical understanding of why the WFPs are spatially localized.
The generalization of WFPT from isolated bands to entangled bands requires the deliberate use of energy windows and subspaces to separate the energy ranges.
In addition, the changes in the Wannier basis functions and the resulting changes in the Hamiltonian matrix [Eq. (
12
)] must be addressed to correctly interpolate matrix elements involving the sum over an infinite number of bands as done in Eqs. (
II.3.1
,
39
).
For WFs constructed without disentanglement, the Hamiltonian matrix elements do not change because the WFPs are spanned by eigenstates outside the outer window so that the Hamiltonian matrix elements between the WFPs and the unperturbed WFs [Eq. (
18
)] are zero.
Only as these highly nontrivial problems are solved, the methods for accurately calculating the proper sum over an infinite number of bands can be developed (as summarized in Table
1
), and WFPT enables several new kinds of calculations that were beyond the computational reach.

WFPT could find wide applications.
For EPC, WFPT opens up a way to systematically study the effect of phonon-induced band renormalization to quantities that require integration over fine
k
points.
Phonon-assisted optical properties are one example.
We emphasize that the predictive calculation of the indirect absorption onset (Fig.
4
(d)) is currently possible only with the WFPT interpolation.
Another example is electronic transport.
In transport calculations, the EPC-induced band renormalization is ignored or included only partially as a correction to the effective mass
Schlipf et al. 2018
;
D’Souza et al. 2020
.
WFPT interpolation will allow the complete consideration of the renormalization by calculating the band renormalization at every
k
point in the fine grid for transport calculations.
Also, a self-consistent calculation of the phonon-induced electron self-energy
Brown-Altvater et al. 2020
without neglecting the
k
dependence of the self-energy may become feasible.

Zero-point renormalization and temperature dependence of band structures are also interesting by themselves.
Calculation of the zero-point renormalization for a large set of materials began only very recently
Miglio et al. 2020
.
Also, most calculations are limited to the correction of the valence band maximum and conduction band minimum, partly due to the large computational cost of calculations for a large number of
k
points.
WFPT will considerably reduce the barrier of the calculation of the zero-point and temperature-dependent renormalization for a large number of materials or for many
k
points.
Combined with the automatic generation of Wannier functions
Vitale et al. 2020
, an efficient, automatized high-throughput calculation will also be possible.

Similar developments can also be made for the shift spin current.
The interpolation scheme based on WFPs is necessary to calculate converged shift spin current spectra at a reasonable computational cost.
Considering the non-geometric property of the spin-transport-related matrix elements, WFPT will be useful for studying a wide range of spin transport phenomena.

Another promising application of WFPT is the calculation of the nonadiabatic Born effective charge, a concept recently introduced in Refs.
Bistoni et al. 2019
;
Binci et al. 2021
;
Dreyer et al. 2021
.
Calculation of the nonadiabatic Born effective charge of metals and doped semiconductors requires a fine
k
-point sampling of wavefunction perturbations for phonon perturbations
Dreyer et al. 2021
.
WFPT interpolation allows such a calculation for all kinds of materials, not just for the special case where the low-energy conduction bands are isolated from the high-energy bands.

Finally, we discuss two ways the WFPT formalism itself can be expanded.
First, in this work, we did not consider the localized representation of the perturbations.
The concept of WFPs may be generalized to localized perturbations, for example by using phonon Wannier functions
Giustino et al. 2007
;
Eiguren and Ambrosch-Draxl 2008
.
Such a generalization may enable the study of phonon-induced band renormalization in complex heterostructures by stitching WF- and WFP-based real-space matrix elements.
Second, considering time-periodic perturbations will allow the localized representation of time-dependent wavefunction perturbations.
This generalization will be useful for the study of dynamic properties such as the dielectric function and the higher-harmonic generation.
Also, these two generalizations may enable the application of WFPT to
GW
calculations, especially in the calculation of frequency- and momentum-dependent screened Coulomb interaction using the Sternheimer equation
Giustino et al. 2010b
or the Lanczos method
Umari et al. 2010
.

V
Conclusion

In conclusion, we introduced the concept of WFPT, which is the extension of the nearsightedness principle to wavefunction perturbations.
We defined the WFP and showed that it gives an accurate localized representation of wavefunction perturbations.
Using WFPs, various matrix elements involving the wavefunction perturbation can be efficiently interpolated.
We applied WFPT to the calculation of the temperature-dependent electron band structures, the shift spin conductivity, and the spin Hall conductivity.
The significant speedup gained thanks to WFPT enabled the study of phenomena such as the temperature-dependent onset of the indirect optical absorption and the shift spin currents, which were beyond the reach of previous methods.
As density-functional perturbation theory has significantly expanded the applications of density functional theory, WFPT will also widen the scope of Wannier function methods for the theoretical and computational study of condensed matter.

\ket{\phi_{p\mb{k}}}
becomes

\ket{\phi_{p\mb{k}}}=\sum_{n}\ket{\psizero_\nk}\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{np;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+\lambda\sum_{m}\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}})\right)\ket{\psi_\mk\oord{1}}\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mp;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})

(71)

for
p={N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}+1,...,{N_{\mathrm{W}}}
.

Combining results in Eq. (
67
) and Eq. (
71
), the final disentangled subspace for the perturbed system is spanned by

\bigcup_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\left(\left\{\ket{\phi\oord{0}_{p\mb{k}}}:p=1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}\right\}\cup\left\{\ket{\phi_{p\mb{k}}}:p=N_{{\rm P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{W}}}\right\}\right).

(72)

The dimension of the disentangled subspace is
N_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}{N_{\mathrm{W}}}
, as desired.

Next, to make computation simpler, we linearly combine the basis states and write them in a simpler form.
For
\lambda=0
, the disentangled subspace [Eq. (
72
)] is identical to the unperturbed Wannier subspace.
Hence, we can linearly combine the basis states to make their first-order terms orthogonal to the unperturbed Wannier subspace.
Mathematically, this orthogonalization is equivalent to applying
\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)
to the first-order terms:

\displaystyle\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_\pk}=\begin{cases}\sum_{m}\left[\ket{\psi_\mk\oord{0}}+\lambda\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)\ket{\psi_\mk^\ord{1}}\right]\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mp;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})&\text{for }p=1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}},\\
\sum_{m}\left[\ket{\psi_\mk\oord{0}}+\lambda\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}\right)\ket{\psi_\mk^\ord{1}}\right]\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mp;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})&\text{for }p={N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}+1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{W}}}.\end{cases}

(73)

This application of
\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)
guarantees that the basis states for the disentangled subspace,
\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_\pk}
’s, are orthonormal to each other with errors of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})
. Although the final form of the WFs of the perturbed system is not affected by this orthonormalization, it makes the following analysis simpler.

Using Eq. (
67
) and the fact that
1\leq m\leq{N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}
is equivalent to
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
, we find

\displaystyle\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_\pk}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\Big[\ket{\psi_\mk\oord{0}}+\lambda{\hat{Q}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}\Big]\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mp;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+\lambda\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\\
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}\end{subarray}}\Big[\left({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}\Big]\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mp;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})\,.

(74)

Finally, we compute the perturbed WFs.
We follow the scheme of Ref.
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
, where the initial guess functions are projected onto the disentangled subspace and then orthonormalized by Löwdin orthogonalization.
This procedure does not change the subspace but fixes the unitary transformation within the subspace to give localized WFs.
The overlap matrix
A
between the initial guesses and the disentangled basis states is

\displaystyle A_{{n\mathbf{k}},{i\mathbf{R}}}\equiv\innerproduct{\widetilde{\phi}_{n\mb{k}}}{w_\iR\oord{0}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}(\widetilde{U}^{(0)\dagger}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}})_{ni}{e^{-i{{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\cdot{{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2}).

(75)

The first-order term of
A_{{n\mathbf{k}},{i\mathbf{R}}}
vanishes because we linearly combined the disentangled basis functions so that their first-order term is orthogonal to the unperturbed Wannier subspace [Eq. (
73
)].
We perform Löwdin orthogonalization to find the gauge matrix and multiply it by
\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_\nk}
to obtain the WFs for the perturbed system:

\displaystyle\ket{w_\iR(\lambda)}=\sum_{{n\mathbf{k}}}\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_\nk}\left[A(A^{\dagger}A)^{-1/2}\right]_{{n\mathbf{k}},{i\mathbf{R}}}.

(76)

From Eq. (
75
), one can show that the gauge matrix satisfies

\displaystyle\left[A(A^{\dagger}A)^{-1/2}\right]_{{n\mathbf{k}},{i\mathbf{R}}}=A_{{n\mathbf{k}},{i\mathbf{R}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2}).

(77)

Using Eqs. (
74
-
77
), we obtain Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
8
).

Appendix B
Necessity of all terms in the WFP

In this section, we discuss four possible modifications to the WFP formula [Eq. (
8
)] that might seem reasonable but turn out to be undesirable.
The two most important properties of the WFPs are that they accurately represent the wavefunction perturbations and that they are spatially localized.
We demonstrate that the modifications make the WFPs lose either one of these two properties.

Let us consider the following cases.

Case 0
: The true WFPs as defined in Eq. (
8
).

Case 1
: Keep only the first term in Eq. (
8
). This case is equivalent to using no inner window for the Wannierization of the perturbed system, or, in other words,
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}=\emptyset
.

\ket{w^{(1),\ \mathrm{Case 1}}_\iR}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}{\hat{Q}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mi,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}

(78)

Case 2
: Keep only the second term in Eq. (
8
). In other words, ignore the contribution from states in subspace
4
, which are outside the outer window
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}
.

\ket{w^{(1),\ \mathrm{Case 2}}_\iR}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\\
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}\end{subarray}}\left({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mi;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}

(79)

Case 3
: Do not impose the condition
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
in the second term of Eq. (
8
).

\displaystyle\ket{w^{(1),\ \mathrm{Case 3}}_\iR}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}{\hat{Q}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mi,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\left({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mi;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mi;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\\

(81)

To study whether the modified WFPs can accurately and efficiently interpolate the wavefunction perturbation, we calculate the following matrix element which we call the “smoothed Sternheimer matrix element”:

\displaystyle X_{mn{\bm{\mathbf{k}}};{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=1\\

In the following, we set

Lihm and Park 2020

To summarize, all four modifications to the definition of the WFPs considered in this section either fail to produce WFPs that accurately represent the wavefunction perturbations or generate delocalized WFPs.

Appendix C
Smoothed inner window for WFPT

In this section, we discuss how to remove the discontinuity due to the
{m\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
condition in Eq. (
8
) by smoothing the inner window in the disentanglement step for constructing the projection-only WFs.
The issue we aim to solve is that the inner window is applied in an “all-or-nothing” manner.
So, the solution is to assign weights to the eigenstates that are 1 for states inside the inner window and continuously decay to 0 outside the inner window.

Concretely, we define a coefficient
l_{n\mathbf{k}}
for each state
{n\mathbf{k}}
inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
.
The coefficient should decay from 1 to 0 as the unperturbed eigenvalue moves away from the boundary energy for
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
.
In Appendix
A
, in the disentanglement step, we projected
{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}
to the perturbed eigenstates inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
and diagonalized the resulting matrix
P_{{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k^{\prime}}},{n\mathbf{k}}}
[Eq. (
A
)].
Here, instead of projecting to
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
, we use the following operator:

\displaystyle{\hat{Q}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{{n\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}}\ket{\psi_\nk}\bra{\psi_\nk}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{{n\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}}\frac{1}{1-l_{n\mathbf{k}}}\ket{\psi_\nk}\bra{\psi_\nk}.

(89)

For each state
{n\mathbf{k}}
inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{F}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
, the weight of the projector
\ket{\psi_\nk}\bra{\psi_\nk}
in
{\hat{Q}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}
increases as
\varepsilon^{\rm(0)}_{n\mathbf{k}}
approaches the boundary energy of
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
and
l_{n\mathbf{k}}
approaches 1.
Then, we diagonalize

{\hat{P}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}={\hat{Q}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}{\hat{Q}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}

(90)

and take the
N_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}{N_{\mathrm{W}}}-N_{\rm P}
states with the largest eigenvalues.
If all
l_{n\mathbf{k}}=0
,

{\hat{Q}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}=\sum_{{n\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}}\ket{\psi_\nk}\bra{\psi_\nk}

(91)

holds and the original scheme of Ref.
Souza et al. 2001
is obtained.

Now, we calculate the eigenstates of
{\hat{P}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}
using perturbation theory.
Writing the zeroth-order eigenstates as
\ket{\phi\oord{0}_\pk}
for
p={N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}+1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{D},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}
, and using Eq. (
67
), we have

{\hat{P}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\lambda=0)\ket{\phi\oord{0}_\pk}=\begin{cases}\frac{\ket{\phi\oord{0}_\pk}}{(1-l_{p\mathbf{k}})^{2}}&:\ {N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}<p\leq{N_{\mathrm{F},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}\\
\ket{\phi\oord{0}_\pk}&:\ {N_{\mathrm{F},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}<p\leq{N_{\mathrm{W}}}\\
0&:\ {N_{\mathrm{W}}}<p\leq{N_{\mathrm{D},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}.\end{cases}

(92)

The total number of states with nonzero unperturbed eigenvalues of
{\hat{P}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}
is
N_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}{N_{\mathrm{W}}}-N_{\rm P}
, and the perturbed version of these states will be selected for Wannierization.
Since the mixing between the selected states is not important because our goal here is to determine the subspace spanned by them, we only consider the mixing between the eigenstates of
{\hat{P}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\lambda=0)
with nonzero eigenvalues and those with zero eigenvalues.

Using first-order perturbation theory in a similar manner to Eq. (
A
), the perturbed eigenstates of
{\hat{P}}_{\mathrm{smooth}}
are

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_\pk}-\ket{\phi\oord{0}_\pk}=\begin{cases}-\lambda(1-l_{p\mathbf{k}})\sum_{m}({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}})\ket{\psi_\mk^\ord{1}}\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mp;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})&\text{for }p={N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}+1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{F},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}\\
-\lambda\sum_{m}({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}})\ket{\psi_\mk^\ord{1}}\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mp;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})&\text{for }p={N_{\mathrm{F},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}+1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{W}}}\end{cases}

(93)

The only difference with Eq. (
A
) is the
\left(1-l_{p\mathbf{k}}\right)
factor for
p={N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}+1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{F},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}
.

Finally, we include the states inside
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}
(
\ket{\phi\oord{0}_\pk}
for
p=1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}
) and orthogonalize them to
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
as done in Eq. (
73

With Eq. (
67
), the resulting basis states for the disentangled subspace are

\displaystyle\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_\pk}=\begin{cases}\ket{\psi_\pk\oord{0}}+\lambda\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}}\right)\ket{\psi_\pk^\ord{1}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})&\text{for }p=1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}},\\
\ket{\psi_\pk\oord{0}}+\lambda\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}+l_{p\mathbf{k}}({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}})\right)\ket{\psi_\pk^\ord{1}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})&\text{for }p={N_{\mathrm{P},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}+1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{F},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}\\
\sum_{m}\left[\ket{\psi_\mk\oord{0}}+\lambda\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}\right)\ket{\psi_\mk^\ord{1}}\right]\widetilde{U}^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mp;{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})&\text{for }p={N_{\mathrm{F},{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}}+1,\cdots,{N_{\mathrm{W}}}.\end{cases}

(94)

Note the additional
l_{p\mathbf{k}}({\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}-{\hat{P}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{W}}})

73

\displaystyle\ket{w^{(1)}_\iR}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}{\hat{Q}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{D}}}\ket{\psi^\ord{1}_\mk}U^{(\mathrm{0})}_{mi,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}}e^{-i{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{R}}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}\\

(95)

The only difference between Eq. (

8
)] is that Eq. (

Appendix G

In this section, we discuss the improved double-grid method for accelerating the convergence of the phonon-induced electron self-energy with respect to the coarse
k

Here, we focus on the diagonal part of the self-energy, but the results can also be applied to the off-diagonal one

Our aim is to calculate

{n\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{P}}}

The convergence of the self-energy with respect to the
q

The convergence of the upper Fan term with respect to the

We find that the contribution of modes with small
\absolutevalue{\mathbf{q}}
is indeed suppressed by adding the lower Fan term.

The cancellation becomes exact as
\eta_{\mathrm{large}}
is decreased.
However, we need to choose
\eta_{\mathrm{large}}
to be large enough so that the denominator in the lower Fan term [Eq. (
III.1
)] is always far from zero and does not create additional convergence issues.
For example, Fig.
13
(c) shows a slow convergence of the self-energy for the lowest conduction band at
\mathrm{\Gamma}
for
\eta_{\mathrm{large}}=0.5
eV.
The slow convergence is more pronounced for states far from band extrema because there are more resonant couplings mediated by phonons to other electron states, leading to a rapid variation in the phonon-mode-resolved lower Fan self-energy.
Practically, we find that
\eta_{\mathrm{large}}
in [1 eV, 2 eV] is a good choice for silicon.

Figure
13
shows that the total self-energy converges much faster with respect to the coarse
q
grid when using the improved double-grid method.
By using the improved double-grid method with
\eta_{\mathrm{\large}}=1~\mathrm{eV}
, the absolute (relative) error in the band-gap renormalization at 400 K is only 2.4 meV (2%) with a
6\times 6\times 6
coarse
q
grid, in contrast to 14 meV (13%) for the conventional double-grid method.
Also, the choice of
\eta_{\mathrm{\large}}
gives only a minor effect on the convergence rate, and more importantly, the convergence is consistently faster than the conventional double-grid method for a wide range of
\eta_{\mathrm{\large}}
in [1 eV, 2 eV].
