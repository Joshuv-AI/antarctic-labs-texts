Adjoint-Free 4D-Var Methods Via Line Search Optimization For Non-Linear Data Assimilation
Elias D. Nino-Ruiz, J. Diaz-Rodriguez

Abstract
This paper proposes two practical implementations of Four-Dimensional Variational (4D-Var) Ensemble Kalman Filter (4D-EnKF) methods for non-linear data assimilation. Our formulations’ main idea is to avoid the intrinsic need for adjoint models in the context of 4D-Var optimization and, even more, to handle non-linear observation operators during the assimilation of observations. The proposed methods work as follows: snapshots of an ensemble of model realizations are taken at observation times, these snapshots are employed to build control spaces onto which analysis increments can be estimated. Via the linearization of observation operators at observation times, a line-search based optimization method is proposed to estimate optimal analysis increments. The convergence of this method is theoretically proven as long as the dimension of control-spaces equals model one. In the first formulation, control spaces are given by full-rank square root approximations of background error covariance matrices via the Bickel and Levina precision matrix estimator. In this context, we propose an iterative Woodbury matrix formula to perform the optimization steps efficiently. The last formulation can be considered as an extension of the Maximum Likelihood Ensemble Filter to the 4D-Var context. This employs pseudo-square root approximations of prior error covariance matrices to build control spaces. Experimental tests are performed by using the Lorenz 96 model. The results reveal that, in terms of Root-Mean-Square-Error values, both methods can obtain reasonable estimates of posterior error modes in the 4D-Var optimization problem. Moreover, the accuracies of the proposed filter implementations can be improved as the ensemble sizes are increased.

\left\{{\bf x}^{b}_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{M}

\left\{{\bf y}_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{M}

M

is a vector of observations, and
m

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{t_{k-1}\rightarrow t_{k}}\left({\bf x}_{k-1}\right)\,\,\text{ for $0\leq k-1\leq M$, and ${\bf x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}$}\,,

(1)

where
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}

(2)

are considered to estimate the initial state
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
which best fit a given assimilation window

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}^{a}_{0}=\arg\underset{{\bf x}_{0}}{\min}\,\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}_{0}\right)\,,\text{subject to ${\bf x}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{t_{k-1}\rightarrow t_{k}}\left({\bf x}_{k-1}\right)$}\,.

(3)

A potential drawback of 4D-Var based methods is the use of adjoint models. In practice, these models can be labor-intensive to develop and computationally expensive to run

The Ensemble Kalman Filter

The Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF)
[
29
,
9
]
is a sequential ensemble based method which employes an ensemble of model realizations to estimate moments of prior error distributions
[
19
,
47
]
:

\displaystyle{\bf X}_{k}^{b}=\left[{\bf x}^{b[1]}_{k},\,{\bf x}^{b[2]}_{k},\,\ldots,\,{\bf x}^{b[N]}_{k}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}\,,

(4)

where
{\bf x}^{b[e]}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
denotes the
e
-th ensemble member, for
1\leq e\leq N
, at time
k
, for
0\leq k\leq M
. The empirical moments of (
4
) are employed to estimate the forecast state
{\bf x}^{b}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\cdot\sum_{e=1}^{N}{\bf x}^{b[e]}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}\,,

(5)

and the backgrund error covariance matrix
{\bf B}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf P}^{b}_{k}=\frac{1}{N-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}^{b}_{k}\cdot\left[\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}^{b}_{k}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\,,

(6)

where the matrix of member deviations reads:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}^{b}_{k}={\bf X}^{b}_{k}-\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}\cdot{\bf 1}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}\,.

(7)

The posterior ensemble can then be built by using synthetic observations:

\displaystyle{\bf X}^{a}_{k}={\bf X}^{b}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}^{a}_{k}\,,

(8)

where the analysis updates can be obtained by solving the following linear system:

\displaystyle\left[\left[{\bf P}^{b}_{k}\right]^{-1}+{\bf H}_{k}^{T}\cdot{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}\cdot{\bf H}_{k}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}^{a}_{k}={\bf H}_{k}^{T}\cdot{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}\cdot{\bf D}^{s}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}\,,

(9)

the innovation matrix reads
{\bf D}_{k}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
whose
e
-th column
{\bf y}_{k}-{\bf H}_{k}\cdot{\bf x}^{b[e]}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k}^{[e]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
is a synthetic observation with
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k}^{[e]}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0}_{m},\,{\bf R}_{k}\right)
. In operational DA scenarios, the ensemble size can be lesser than model dimensions by order of magnitudes and as a direct consequence, sampling errors impact the quality of analysis increments. To counteract the effects of sampling noise, localizations methods are commonly employed
[
13
,
5
]
. In the context of covariance tappering, the use of the
spatial-predecessors
concept can be employed to obtain sparse estimators of precision matrices
[
25
]
. The predecessors of model component
i
, from now on
P(i,\,r)
, for
1\leq i\leq n
and a radius of influence
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, are given by the set of components whose labels are lesser than that of the
i
-th one.

In the EnKF based on a modified Cholesky decomposition (EnKF-MC)
[
37
,
38
]
the following estimator is employed to approximate the precision covariance matrix of the background error distribution
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bf B}^{-1}_{k}=\widehat{\bf L}_{k}^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf D}^{-1}_{k}\cdot\widehat{\bf L}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\,,

(10)

where the Cholesky factor
{\bf L}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a lower triangular matrix,

\displaystyle\left\{\widehat{\bf L}_{k}\right\}_{i,v}=\begin{cases}-\beta_{i,v,k}&\,,\,v\in P(i,r)\\
1&\,,\,i=v\\
0&\,,\,otherwise\end{cases}\,,

(11)

whose non-zero sub-diagonal elements
\beta_{i,v,k}
are obtained by fitting models of the form,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{{\bf x}_{[i]}^{T}}_{k}=\sum_{v\in P(i,\,r)}\beta_{i,v,k}\cdot{{\bf x}_{[v]}^{T}}_{k}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}\,,\text{ $1\leq i\leq n$}\,,

(12)

where
{{\bf x}_{[i]}^{T}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
denotes the
i
-th row (model component) of the ensemble (
4
), components of vector
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
are samples from a zero-mean Normal distribution with unknown variance
\sigma^{2}_{k}
, and
{\bf D}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements read,

\displaystyle\left\{{\bf D}_{k}\right\}_{i,i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\bf var}\left({{\bf x}_{[i]}^{T}}_{k}-\sum_{v\in P(i,\,r)}\beta_{i,v,k}\cdot{{\bf x}_{[j]}^{T}}_{k}\right)^{-1}

(13)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle{\bf var}\left({\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}}_{k}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}>0\,,\text{ with $\left\{{\bf D}_{k}\right\}_{1,1}=\widehat{\bf var}\left({{\bf x}_{[1]}^{T}}_{k}\right)^{-1}$}\,,

(14)

where
{\bf var}(\bullet)
and
\widehat{\bf var}(\bullet)
denote the actual and the empirical variances, respectively. The analysis equations can then be written as discussed in
[
38
]
.

3.1
Maximum Likelihood Ensemble Filter

The filter derivation in the EnKF context is based on the assumptions that prior and observational errors are Gaussian. However, for non-linear observation operators (i.e., information brought by satellite radiances), observational errors are not Gaussian anymore and as a direct consequence, EnKF based methods can fail to obtain reasonable estimates of posterior error distributions. The Maximum Likelihood Ensemble Filter (MLEF)
[
56
]
is a numerical method that minimizes the Three-Dimensional Variational (3D-Var) cost function:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}({\bf x}_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|{\bf x}_{k}-\overline{\bf x}_{k}^{b}\right\|^{2}_{{\bf B}^{-1}_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|{\bf y}_{k}-\mathcal{H}_{k}\left({\bf x}_{k}\right)\right\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}}\,,

(15)

onto the ensemble space. For this purpose, analysis increments are constrained to the space spanned by the ensemble of anomalies (
7
), this is:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}_{k}=\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}\cdot{\bf w}\,,

(16)

which results in a cost function of the form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}\cdot{\bf w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N-1}{2}\cdot\left\|{\bf w}\right\|^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|{\bf y}_{k}-\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}\cdot{\bf w}\right)\right\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}}\,,

and corresponding optimization problem:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf w}^{*}=\arg\underset{{\bf w}}{\min}\,\mathcal{J}(\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}\cdot{\bf w})\,.

(18)

Note that, since all computations are performed onto the ensemble space, the computational effort of the MLEF becomes:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(N^{3}+n\cdot m\cdot N\right)\,,

(19)

once all analysis increments are mapped back onto the model space (and the posterior ensemble is built about the estimated posterior mode), which is linear with regard to the model dimension.

3.2
Four Dimensional Variational Ensemble Kalman Filter

In Four Dimensional Variational Ensemble Kalman Filter (4D-EnKF) methods
[
27
,
30
]
, the model trajectory in (
3
) is constrained to the space spanned by the background ensemble members, this is:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}_{k}=\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}\cdot{\bf w}\,,

(20)

where
{\bf w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
is a vector in redundant coordinates to be determined later. Making the assumption that all sources of errors are Gaussian (i.e., the observation operator is linear), and by replacing (
20
) into the equation (
2
) one obtains:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}({\bf x}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{0}\cdot{\bf w}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{J}}({\bf w})=\frac{(N-1)}{2}\cdot\left\|{\bf w}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{M}\left\|{\bf d}_{k}-{\bf Q}_{k}\cdot{\bf w}\right\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}}\,,

where
{\bf d}_{k}={\bf y}_{k}-{\bf H}_{k}\cdot\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
is the innovation vector and
{\bf Q}_{k}={\bf H}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}^{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
. The optimal values of the control variable
{\bf w}
is then sought in order to estimate the initial analysis state:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf w}^{*}=\arg\,\underset{{\bf w}}{\min}\,\widehat{\mathcal{J}}({\bf w})\,.

(22)

The gradient of (
21
) equals:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf w}}\widehat{\mathcal{J}}({\bf w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(N-1)\cdot{\bf w}-\sum_{k=0}^{M}{\bf Q}_{k}^{T}\cdot{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}\cdot\left[{\bf d}_{k}-{\bf Q}_{k}\cdot{\bf w}\right]

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[(N-1)\cdot{\bf I}+\sum_{k=0}^{M}{\bf Q}_{k}^{T}\cdot{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}\cdot{\bf Q}_{k}\right]\cdot{\bf w}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{M}{\bf Q}_{k}^{T}\cdot{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}\cdot{\bf d}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}\,,

and by setting this gradient to zero, the optimal weights (
22
) reads:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf w}^{*}=\left[(N-1)\cdot{\bf I}+\sum_{k=0}^{M}{\bf Q}_{k}^{T}\cdot{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}\cdot{\bf Q}_{k}\right]^{-1}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{M}{\bf Q}_{k}^{T}\cdot{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}\cdot{\bf d}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}\,,

(24)

from which the initial analysis state can be estimated:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\bf x}_{0}^{a}=\overline{\bf x}_{0}^{b}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{0}^{b}\cdot{\bf w}^{*}\,.

(25)

Similar to the MLEF method, the computational effort of 4D-EnKF based methods are linearly bounded by the model dimension; it can be easily seen that all computations are performed onto the ensemble space. Note that, in this formulation, posterior estimates can be highly biased regarding the actual state when the observation operator is non-linear.

wherein

]

,

]
,
or quasi-Newton based method

.
A concise survey of Newton based methods can be consulted in

. In the context of line search, the following assumptions are commonly done:

The function

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f({\bf x})-\nabla f({\bf z})\right\|\leq L\cdot\left\|{\bf x}-{\bf z}\right\|,\,\text{ for ${\bf x},\,{\bf z}\in B$, and $L>0$}.

These together with iterates of the form,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}^{(u+1)}={\bf x}^{(u)}+\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf x}^{(u)}\,,

ensure global convergence

as long as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\arg\,\underset{\alpha\geq 0}{\min}\,f\left({\bf x}^{(u)}+\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf x}^{(u)}\right)\,.

(28)

4
Proposed Method

In this section, we develop an efficient and practical implementation of a 4D-EnKF method via a modified Cholesky decomposition for non-linear observation operator.

4.1
Filter Derivation

Our main goal is to find an initial analysis ensemble
{\bf X}^{a}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
whose model trajectories account for the information brought by observations within a given assimilation window. To accomplish this, we proceed as follows: given an assimilation window of
M
observations (with no loss of generality, evenly distributed in time), by using the numerical model (
1
), the initial background ensemble
{\bf X}_{0}^{b}
is propagated in time and snapshots of its time evolution are taken at observation times. Denote by
k
, the
k
-th step where an observation is available, for
0\leq k\leq M
, at each observation time, the background ensemble (
4
) can be employed to estimate a full-rank square-root approximation of the precision covariance matrix
{\bf B}_{k}^{-1}
via a modified Cholesky decomposition (
10
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\bf B}^{-1/2}_{k}=\widehat{\bf L}^{T}_{k}\cdot\widehat{\bf D}^{-1/2}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\,.

(29)

This square-root estimation can then be employed to build a control space onto which analysis increments can be estimated:

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{k}-\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}\in{\bf range}\left\{\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\right\}\,,

or equivalently:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}_{k}=\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}\,,

(30)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
is a vector in redundant coordinates to be determined later. Since the background error covariance matrix onto the control space (
30
) is nothing but the identity matrix, the following error statistics hold for the prior weights
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{b}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{b}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\,{\bf I}\right)\,.

Due to this, the 4D-Var cost function (
2
) onto the space (
30
) can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{0}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\|^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{M}\left\|{\bf y}_{k}-\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)\right\|_{{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}}^{2}\,,

The adjoint-free optimization problem to solve reads:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{a}=\arg\,\underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\min}\,\mathcal{J}\left(\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{0}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)\,.

(32)

The next section develops a Line-Search method for solving the optimization problem (
32
).

4.2
An Adjoint-Free 4D-Var Method Via Modified Cholesky Decomposition (4D-Var-MC)

To approximate a solution of (
32
), we propose iterates of the form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}^{(u+1)}_{k}={\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\,,\text{ for $0\leq u\leq U$}\,,

(33)

where
u
denotes iteration step,
U
stands for the number of iterations, and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
is a vector in redundant coordinates to be determinated later. The background trajectory is set as the initial solution
{\bf x}^{(0)}_{k}=\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}
, for
0\leq k\leq M-1
, and therefore
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0}={\bf 0}
. Note that, at any iteration
u
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}_{k}^{(u+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{(0)}_{k}+\sum_{v=0}^{u}\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{v}=\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\sum_{v=0}^{u-1}\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{v}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\,

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\left[\sum_{v=0}^{u-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{v}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right]=\overline{\bf x}^{b}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\left[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right]\,

where we have let
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}=\sum_{v=0}^{u-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{v}
. By replacing (
33
) and (
34
) in (
2
) one obtains:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{J}}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right)=\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\left\|{\bf y}_{k}-\mathcal{H}_{k}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right)\right\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}}\,.

At iteration
u
, we choose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}
in (
33
) as the value that solves the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}^{*}=\arg\,\underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}}{\min}\,\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right)\,.

(35)

A solution of this problem can be approximated as follows: consider the linearization of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
about
{\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}
, at iteration
u

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}\left({\bf x}_{k}\right)\approx\widehat{\mathcal{H}}^{(u)}_{k}\left({\bf x}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}\right)+{\bf H}^{(u)}_{k}\cdot\left[{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}\right)+{\bf H}^{(u)}_{k}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\,,

where
{\bf H}^{(u)}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the Jacobian of
\mathcal{H}_{k}({\bf x}_{k})
at
{\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}
,
then, at iteration
u
, we make use of the next quadratic approximation:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{J}}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\left\|{\bf y}_{k}-\widehat{\mathcal{H}}^{(u)}_{k}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right)\right\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\left\|{\bf d}^{(u)}_{k}-{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}}

where
{\bf d}^{(u)}_{k}={\bf y}_{k}-\mathcal{H}_{k}\left({\bf x}_{k}^{(u)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
, and
{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}={\bf H}^{(u)}_{k}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}-\sum_{k=0}^{M}\left[{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\right]^{T}\cdot{\bf R}^{-1}_{k}\cdot\left[{\bf d}^{(u)}_{k}-{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[{\bf I}+\sum_{k=0}^{M}\left[{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\right]^{T}\cdot{\bf R}^{-1}_{k}\cdot{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left[-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}+\sum_{k=0}^{M}\left[{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\right]^{T}\cdot{\bf R}^{-1}_{k}\cdot{\bf d}^{(u)}_{k}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}\,,

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf I}+\sum_{k=0}^{M}\left[{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\right]^{T}\cdot{\bf R}^{-1}_{k}\cdot{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\,.

We consider the search direction as the solution of
\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{u})={\boldsymbol{0}}
, for computing partial analysis increments onto the control space, this is

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{u}=\left[\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{u})\right]^{-1}\cdot\left[-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}+\sum_{k=0}^{M}\left[{\bf Q}_{k}^{(u)}\right]^{T}\cdot{\bf R}^{-1}_{k}\cdot{\bf d}^{(u)}_{k}\right]\,,

but, since we employ a linear approximation of the observation operator to approximate a descent direction of (
3
) , we perform a line search to estimate the partial analysis increments:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{u}=\arg\,\underset{\rho_{u}\in[0,\,1]}{\min}\,\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}+\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\left[\rho_{u}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{u}\right]\right)\,,

(36)

and therefore, the next iterates reads:

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{(u+1)}_{k}={\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}+{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}\cdot\left[\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{u}\right]

(37)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}^{*}\approx\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{u}
, this yields to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}^{*}\approx\sum_{v=1}^{u-1}\,\rho^{*}_{v}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{v}
. This process is repeated until some stopping criteria is satisfied (i.e., a maximum number of iterations
U
is reached). The resulting analysis state reads:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\bf x}_{0}^{a}=\overline{\bf x}_{0}^{b}+{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\sum_{u=1}^{U}\,\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{u}\,,

which is nothing but a linear combination of the approximated search directions among iterations. Thus, we can approximate
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{a}
in (
32
) as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{a}\approx\sum_{u=1}^{U}\,\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{u}\,.

We can employ any line-search rule to solve the optimization problem (
36
). Putting it all together, the optimization steps are detailed in the Algorithm
1
. The posterior ensemble onto the control space can then be built by using a square root approximation of the information matrix, its can be easily shown that the posterior error statistics read:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{a[e]}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{a},\,\left[{\bf I}+\sum_{k=0}^{M}\left[{\bf Q}_{k}^{(U)}\right]^{T}\cdot{\bf R}_{k}^{-1}\cdot{\bf Q}^{(U)}_{k}\right]^{-1}\right)\,,\text{ for $1\leq e\leq N$}\,,

(38)

with corresponding analysis members in the model space:

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{a[e]}_{0}=\overline{\bf x}^{a}_{0}+\widehat{\bf B}_{0}^{1/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{a[e]}\,.

The initial ensemble members are then propagated in time from which the optimal (ensemble) trajectory is recovered.

An Adjoint-Free 4D-Var Method Via Modified Cholesky Decomposition (4D-Var-MC)

Matrix inversions in eht 4D-Var-MC can be avoided by considering at each optimization step the matrix-free 4D-Var formulation
[
36
]
.

4.3
An Adjoint-Free 4D-Var Method Via MLEF (4D-Var-MLEF)

The proposed 4D-Var-MC can be similarly derived onto the ensemble space by employing the matrix of anomalies (
7
) as our set of basis vectors at observation times. We then can propose iterates of the form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}^{(u+1)}_{k}={\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{u}\,,\text{ for $0\leq u\leq U$}\,,

(39)

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
. We perform analogous derivations as in (
34
)–(
4.2
), replacing the control space
\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{k}
by the ensemble space
\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}
. Search directions are then approximated by considering linearizations of the form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}\left({\bf x}_{k}\right)\approx\widehat{\mathcal{H}}^{(u)}_{k}\left({\bf x}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{k}\right)+{\bf H}^{(u)}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Delta}{\bf X}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\,,

and similar to (
36
), a line-search method can be employed to control step lengths among iterations. This algorithm becomes an adjoint-free method based on the MLEF method discussed in section
3.1
; we name it the 4D-Var-MLEF. Note that, since all computations are performed onto the ensemble space, the computational cost of estimating analysis increments together with analysis updates reads:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(N^{3}+N^{2}\cdot m+N\cdot m\cdot n\right)\,.

As a drawback, the 4D-Var-MLEF suffers from the MLEF limitations regarding global convergence.

4.4
Global Convergence of the 4D-Var-MC

For proving the convergence of the line-search method during the assimilation of observations in the 4D-Var-MC, we consider the model (
1
), (
2
), and we assume

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\left[\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right]<0,\,\text{ for $1\leq k\leq K$}\,.

(40)

The next Theorem states the necessary conditions to ensure global convergence for the line search method during the assimilation of observations in the 4D-Var-MC method.

Theorem 4.1
.

If (
1
), (
2
), and (
40
) hold,
then the line search in the 4D-Var-MC generates an infinite sequence
\left\{{\bf x}^{(u)}\right\}_{u=0}^{\infty}
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underset{u\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\left[\frac{-\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}}{\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|}\right]^{2}=0

(41)

holds.

Proof.

By Taylor series and the Mean Value Theorem we know that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{0}+\rho_{u}^{*}\cdot{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\rho^{*}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\,\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{0}+\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot t\cdot{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\cdot dt\,,

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}
is given by (
35
), and therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{0}\right)-\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u+1)}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\,\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}_{0}+\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot t\cdot{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle{\bf Q}^{(u)}\cdot{\bf w}^{*}\cdot dt

for any
{\bf x}^{(u+1)}
on the ray
{\bf x}^{(u)}+\rho_{u}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}
, with
\rho_{u}\in[0,\,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)-\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u+1)}\right)\geq\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)-\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}+\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right)\,,

hence:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u+1)}\right)\geq-\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}^{*}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\,\left[\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}+\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot t\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right)-\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)\right]^{T}

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\cdot dt\,,

by the Cauchy Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u+1)}\right)\geq-\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\,\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}+\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot t\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right)-\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|\cdot dt

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\,L\cdot\left\|\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot t\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|\cdot\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}^{*}\right\|\cdot dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot L\cdot\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\left\|t\cdot\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|\cdot dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\rho^{*}_{u}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot{\rho^{*}_{u}}^{2}\cdot L\cdot\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|^{2}\,,

since the gradient is Lipschitz continuous, we can choose

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{u}=-\frac{\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}}{L\cdot\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|^{2}}\,,

therefore,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u+1)}\right)\geq\frac{\left[\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right]^{2}}{L\cdot\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\left[-\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right]^{2}}{L\cdot\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\cdot L}\cdot\left[-\frac{\nabla\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)^{T}\cdot\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}}{\left\|\widehat{\bf B}^{1/2}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{u}\right\|}\right]^{2}\,.

By (
1
), and (
40
), it follows that
\left\{\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)\right\}_{u=0}^{\infty}
is a monotone decreasing number sequence and it has a bound below, therefore
\left\{\mathcal{J}\left({\bf x}^{(u)}\right)\right\}_{u=0}^{\infty}
has a limit, and consequently (
41
) holds.

Experimental Settings

We make use of the Lorenz-96 model
[
31
]
as our surrogate model in order to tests the compared EnKF implementations. The Lorenz-96 model is described by the following set of ordinary differential equations
[
10
]
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dx_{j}}{dt}=\begin{cases}\left(x_{2}-x_{n-1}\right)\cdot x_{n}-x_{1}+F&\text{ for $j=1$},\\
\left(x_{j+1}-x_{j-2}\right)\cdot x_{j-1}-x_{j}+F&\text{ for $2\leq j\leq n-1$},\\
\left(x_{1}-x_{n-2}\right)\cdot x_{n-1}-x_{n}+F&\text{ for $j=n$},\end{cases}

(42)

where
F
is an external force and
n=40
is the number of model components. Periodic boundary conditions are assumed. When
F=8
units, the model exhibits chaotic behavior, which makes it a relevant surrogate problem for atmospheric dynamics
[
22
,
12
]
. A time unit in the Lorenz-96 represents 120 hours (5 days) in the atmosphere.

The experimental settings are described below, they are similar to those presented in
[
35
]
:

•

An initial random solution is integrated over a long time period in order to obtain an initial condition
{\bf x}^{*}_{-2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
dynamically consistent with the model (
42
).

•

A perturbed background solution
{\bf x}^{b}_{-2}

by drawing a sample from the Normal distribution,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}^{b}_{-2}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf x}^{*}_{-2},\,0.05^{2}\cdot{\bf I}\right)\,,

this solution is then integrated for 10 time units (equivalent to 50 days in the atmosphere) in order to obtain a background solution
{\bf x}^{b}_{-1}
consistent with the numerical model.

•

An initial perturbed ensemble is built about the background state by taking samples from the distribution,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf x}^{b[i]}_{-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf x}^{b}_{-1},\,0.05^{2}\cdot{\bf I}\right)\,,\text{ for $1\leq i\leq N$}\,.

These samples are propagated over a time period of 10 units in order to make them consistent with the model dynamics (
42
), after which the initial ensemble members

Conclusions

We propose two adjoint free 4D-Var methods for non-linear data assimilation. In both methods, control spaces are built to avoid the intrinsic need for adjoint models in the 4D-Var context. The techniques work as follows: based on an ensemble of model realizations, control spaces are built at observation times, then model states are constrained to the space spanned by those control spaces. The analysis increments are computed onto the proposed spaces by linearizing observation operators together with employing line-search optimization methods. The global convergence of the method can be proven as long as the control-space dimension equals that of the model one. We also propose a matrix-free method to compute search directions whose computational effort is linearly bounded by the model dimension; this feature is attractive under data assimilation operational settings. Experimental tests are performed by using the Lorenz-96 model and by employing a non-linear observation operator. The results reveal that both filters can compute initial posterior modes of the error distribution with reasonable accuracy in terms of RMSE values. Even more, the 4D-EnKF-MC can be employed to mitigate the impact of sampling noise as well as to guarantee the convergence of line-search methods during optimization steps.
