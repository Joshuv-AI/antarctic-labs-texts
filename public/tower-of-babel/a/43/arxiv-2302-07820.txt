Equilibria of large random Lotka-Volterra systems with vanishing species: a mathematical approach
Imane Akjouj (1) , Walid Hachem (2) , Mylène Maïda (1),(3) , Jamal Najim (2)

Abstract
. Ecosystems with a large number of species are often modelled as Lotka-Volterra dynamical systems built around a large random interaction matrix. Under some known conditions, a global equilibrium exists and is unique. In this article, we rigorously study its statistical properties in the large dimensional regime. Such an equilibrium vector is known to be the solution of a so-called Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP). We describe its statistical properties by designing an Approximate Message Passing (AMP) algorithm, a technique that has recently aroused an intense research effort in the fields of statistical physics, Machine Learning, or communication theory. Interaction matrices taken from the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble, or following a Wishart distribution are considered. Beyond these models, the AMP approach developed in this article has the potential to describe the statistical properties of equilibria associated to more involved interaction matrix models.



1.
Introduction

Equilibrium of a large Lotka-Volterra system

In the field of mathematical ecology, Lotka-Volterra (LV) systems of coupled
differential equations are widely used to model the time evolution of the
abundances of
N
interacting species within an ecosystem
[
35
]
.
Such systems take the form

(1)

\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}_{N}}{dt}(t)=\boldsymbol{x}_{N}(t)\odot\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{N}-\left(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N}\right)\boldsymbol{x}_{N}(t)\right),\quad\boldsymbol{x}_{N}(0)\in(0,\infty)^{N},

where the vector function
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}:[0,\infty)\to{{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}=[0,\infty)^{N}

represents the abundances of the
N
species,
\odot
is the componentwise
product,
\boldsymbol{r}_{N}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
is the so-called vector of intrinsic growth rates of the species, and

\Sigma_{N}=(\Sigma_{ij})\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N\times N}
represents the interaction matrix. More precisely
\Sigma_{ij}
represents the effect of species
j
on the growth of species
i
. Equivalently, (
1
) can be written as a series of coupled ordinary differential equations:

\frac{dx_{i}}{dt}(t)=x_{i}(t)\left(r_{i}-x_{i}(t)+\left(\Sigma_{N}\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\right)_{i}(t)\right)\ ,\quad x_{i}(0)>0\ ,\quad 1\leq i\leq N\,,

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}=(x_{i})
and
\boldsymbol{r}_{N}=(r_{i})
.

In theoretical ecology, the interaction matrix
\Sigma_{N}
and the vector
\boldsymbol{r}_{N}
are often modelled as random when the number
N
of species is large, turning
the ecological system into a large disordered system.
Such systems have aroused an important
amount of research in the fields of mathematical ecology, borrowing
tools from statistical physics, high dimensional probability, or random matrix
theory
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we shall be interested in the situation where the LV dynamical
system is well-defined for all
t\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}
and
possesses an unique globally stable equilibrium vector:

\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}=\left(x^{\star}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{N}\qquad\textrm{with}\qquad\boldsymbol{x}_{N}(t)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}\,.

It is well-known that the property
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}(0)\in(0,\infty)^{N}
is maintained for all
t>0
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}(t)\in(0,\infty)^{N}
. However, in general, the
equilibrium vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}
may lie at the boundary of
{{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
, i.e. may have vanishing components.
Moreover, assuming that
\Sigma_{N}
and
\boldsymbol{r}_{N}
are random, the vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}
is random as well.

When
N
becomes large, it is of interest to understand the statistical properties of
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}
such as for example its proportion of non-zero components, or the distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}
’s components, encoded in the empirical probability measure

\mu^{\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{\star}_{i}}\ ,

where
\delta_{a}
stands for the Dirac measure at
a
.
Measure
\mu^{\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}}
is a random measure defined on the
probability space of
\boldsymbol{r}_{N}
and
\Sigma_{N}
.

Among the classical interaction matrix models considered in the literature
devoted to large LV systems are the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE) model,
the real Ginibre model (i.i.d. centered Gaussian entries for
\Sigma_{N}
), or
the so-called elliptical model, that can be seen as an interpolation between
the GOE and the real Ginibre models
[
4
]
. For these models,
feasible equilibria where
x_{i}^{\star}>0
for
1\leq i\leq N
are
studied in
[
10
,
16
,
3
,
17
]
.

The large-
N
properties of
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}
were
recently considered in the theoretical ecology literature. In
[
12
]
, Bunin considered
a non-centered elliptical model with the help of the dynamical cavity method. A similar result
was obtained by Galla in
[
24
]
by means of generating functionals
techniques, see also
[
31
,
36
]
. Many insights are provided by these techniques from a physicist point of view.
However, up to our knowledge, no rigorous method to describe the asymptotic properties of
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}
can be found in the literature so far.

The purpose of this paper is to address this question in the case where the interaction matrix is either taken from the GOE or follows a Wishart distribution. Our results on the asymptotics of
\mu^{\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}}
mathematically confirm Bunin and Galla’s works.

Linear Complementarity Problem

When it exists, the globally stable equilibrium

\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}=(x_{i}^{\star})
of the LV equation above is known to be the solution of a so-called
Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP), see for instance
[
35
, Chap. 3]
, which consists in
finding a vector with real entries that satisfies a system of inequalities involving
matrix
\Sigma_{N}
and vector
\boldsymbol{r}_{N}
:

(2)

\begin{cases}x^{\star}_{i}&\geq 0,\\
x^{\star}_{i}\left(r_{i}-\left[(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N})\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}\right]_{i}\right)&=0,\\
r_{i}-\left[(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N})\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}\right]_{i}&\leq 0,\end{cases}\qquad\textrm{for all}\ i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}\,.

The two first conditions are natural for an equilibrium to system (
1
). The third one is necessary for its stability and also admits an ecological interpretation related to the notion of non-invasibility. Sufficient conditions on
\Sigma_{N}
to ensure existence and uniqueness of the solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}
are known. The problem boils down to the following question: how can we asymptotically extract statistical information on
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}
, solution to the highly non-linear problem (
2
), given that
\Sigma_{N}
and
\boldsymbol{r}_{N}
are random?

The reader is referred to
Section
3.3
below for a quick overview of the LCP theory, and to

[
18
,
30
]
for complete and comprehensive expositions.

Approximate Message Passing

The idea we develop in this paper is that the distribution

\mu^{\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}}
can be estimated for large
N
by designing a proper Approximate
Message Passing (AMP) algorithm.

Approximate Message Passing (AMP) is a technique that has
recently aroused an intense research effort in the fields of statistical
physics, machine learning, high-dimensional statistics and communication theory.
Among the many landmark articles, we can cite

[
20
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
11
]
. More references can be found in the recent tutorial
[
23
]
.

An AMP algorithm produces a sequence of
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
–valued
random vectors, say
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}=(\xi^{k}_{i})
, which are iteratively built around a
N\times N
random matrix, sometimes called the measurement matrix. This algorithm is conceived in such a way that for any finite collection
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}
of these vectors, the following joint empirical
distribution:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\xi^{1}_{i},\cdots,\xi^{k}_{i}}

converges as
N\to\infty
to a Gaussian distribution on
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{k}
whose parameters can be fully characterized by the
so-called Density Evolution (DE) equations. In the context of our LV
equilibrium problem, it turns out that an AMP algorithm can be designed in such
a way that the AMP iterates approximate our LCP solution after an adequate
transformation. Thanks to this approximation, the asymptotic properties of

\mu^{\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}}
can be deduced from the DE equations.

Random matrix model and perspectives

Regarding the statistical model for
\Sigma_{N}
, we shall consider in this paper
the GOE model
[
4
]
, and the so-called Wishart model. The latter
is a particular case of a kernel matrix, which is considered when the
interaction between two species depends on a distance between the values of
some functional traits attached to these species, see
[
2
, §4.6]
and the references therein, or the recent paper

[
33
]
. Both models rely on Gaussian random variables, see Assumptions
2
-
4
, but we also provide results beyond the Gaussian case, see Assumptions
8
-
9
.

We believe that this LCP/AMP approach for studying
\mu^{\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}}
can
be generalized and applied to more complex models for the interaction matrix

\Sigma_{N}
.
For instance, the recent results of Fan
[
22
]
might be used to cover
the general rotationally invariant case; more general models are also considered in

[
6
,
38
]
. Matrices with a variance profile,
possibly sparse
[
25
]
, and non-symmetric matrix models
(i.i.d. elements, elliptical models) could also be considered as well. Some of
these generalizations are currently under investigation.

2.1.
Equilibria, Wasserstein space and pseudo-Lipschitz functions

Independently of the struture of
\Sigma_{N}

\|\Sigma_{N}\|<1

\boldsymbol{x}_{N}(0)\succ 0

Given

, the

{\mathcal{P}}_{k}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d})

is defined as the set of probability measures
\mu
over
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
with finite

k^{\text{th}}

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{k}\mu(d\boldsymbol{x})<\infty

Given

{\mathcal{M}}_{k}(\mu,\nu)

the set of probability measures in
{\mathcal{P}}_{k}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d})
with
marginals
\mu

\nu
, i.e.

\eta\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k}(\mu,\nu)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\begin{cases}\eta(A\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d})&=\mu(A)\,,\\
\eta({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\times B)&=\nu(B)\,,\end{cases}

for all
A,B
Borel sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can endow the space

{\mathcal{P}}_{k}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d})
with the distance:

d_{k}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\eta\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k}(\mu,\nu)}\left\{\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{k}\eta(d\boldsymbol{x}d\boldsymbol{y})\right\}^{1/k}\,.

A function
\varphi:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is

with constant

and degree

if for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}

|\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})-\varphi(\boldsymbol{y})|\quad\leq\quad L\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|\left(1+\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{k-1}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{k-1}\right)\,.

We denote by

this set of functions. We will rely on the following classical lemma in the sequel, see for instance
[
23
, Section 1.1 and 7.4]
and
[

]

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mu_{N},\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{k}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d})
for
k\geq 2
. The following
conditions are equivalent:

(i)

d_{k}(\mu_{N},\mu)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0

(ii)

For all
\varphi\in PL_{k}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d})

\int\varphi d\mu_{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\int\varphi d\mu
,

(iii)

\mu_{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{w}\mu

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{k}\mu_{N}(d\boldsymbol{x})\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{k}\mu(d\boldsymbol{x})
.

If one of the equivalent conditions of Lemma

(\mu_{N})
converges in

\mu_{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathcal{P}}_{k}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d})}\mu\,.

If not misleading, we will occasionally drop

{{\mathbb{E}}}(A^{(N)}_{ij})^{2}=1\quad(i<j)\,,\quad\sup_{N}\max_{i}{{\mathbb{E}}}(A^{(N)}_{ii})^{2}<C\,,

(Wigner matrices)

[

Remark 6

(Sparse models)

Sparsity of the food interactions is often justified from an ecological point of view, see

Beyond the model described in Remark

Outline of the proof

Step 1

Step 2

4

Step 3

In Section

is given in (

Step 4

The strength of the AMP procedure is that we can track down via the Density Evolution (DE) equations the asymptotic distribution of
(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k})_{+}
’s empirical measure for any

. We can then transfer if to
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}
by using a perturbation result by Chen and Xiang in

3.2.
Existence and uniqueness of the solution of system (
5
)

We begin with the following technical lemma, the third part of which will be used in Section
3.4
. To avoid any ambiguity, we shall always refer to
\sigma
as the unique positive root of
\sigma^{2}>0
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\bar{r}
be a non negative r.v. with
{\mathcal{L}}(\bar{r})\neq\delta_{0}
.

(i)

For a given
\delta>0
, Equation (
5b
) admits a solution

\sigma^{2}
if and only if
\delta>1/\sqrt{2}
. In this case, this
solution is unique, and is denoted by
\sigma^{2}(\delta)
.

(ii)

Let
\delta>1/\sqrt{2}
then

\mathbb{P}\{\sigma(\delta){\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}\geq 0\}<\delta^{2}\,.

(iii)

Assume
\delta>1/\sqrt{2}
. Starting with an arbitrary
\sigma_{0}\geq 0
,
consider the iterative scheme:

\sigma^{2}_{t+1}=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}}\left(\sigma_{t}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}\right)_{+}^{2}\,,\qquad\textrm{then}\quad\sigma^{2}_{t}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\sigma^{2}(\delta)\,.

Proof of Lemma
2
is postponed to Appendix
A.1
.

We now establish that system (
5
) has a unique solution

(\delta,\sigma,\gamma)\in(1/\sqrt{2},\infty)\times(0,\infty)\times(0,1)\,.

Let
\delta>1/\sqrt{2}
,
\sigma^{2}(\delta)
be defined by (
5b
), and
\gamma(\delta)
by (
5c
). Setting
f(\sigma^{2})=\mathbb{E}(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}^{2}
, we have established in the proof of Lemma
2
-(i) that

\gamma(\delta)=\frac{df}{d\sigma^{2}}\bigg|_{\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}(\delta)}\,.

Moreover
\gamma(\delta)<\delta^{2}
by Lemma
2
-(ii). All what remains to show is that the equation

(12)

\kappa=\delta+\frac{\gamma(\delta)}{\delta}

has a unique solution
\delta>1/\sqrt{2}
. We thus need to study the
behavior of
\gamma(\delta)
. In all the remainder, differentiability issues
can be easily checked and are skipped.

Recall that
df(\sigma^{2})/d\sigma^{2}
decreases asymptotically to

1/2
as
\sigma^{2}
increases from
0
to
\infty
, from which we can deduce that
\sigma^{2}(\delta)\to\infty
as

\delta\downarrow 1/\sqrt{2}
by Lemma
2
-(ii). Using the fact that

\sigma^{2}(\delta)=\frac{f(\sigma^{2}(\delta))}{\delta^{2}}

and taking the derivatives with respect to
\delta
, we get that

\frac{d\sigma^{2}(\delta)}{d\delta}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\left.\frac{df(\sigma^{2})}{d\sigma^{2}}\right|_{\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}(\delta)}\Bigr)=-\frac{2f(\sigma^{2}(\delta))}{\delta^{3}},

which shows that
\sigma^{2}(\delta)
is a decreasing function. Hence
\gamma(\delta)
is increasing since
\sigma\mapsto\mathbb{P}\{\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}\geq 0)
is decreasing (cf. proof of Lemma
2
).

We can now conclude. For
\delta\downarrow 1/\sqrt{2}
,
\sigma^{2}(\delta)\to\infty
by what
precedes, thus,
\gamma(\delta)\downarrow 1/2
, and

\delta+\gamma(\delta)/\delta\to\sqrt{2}<\kappa
.
Near infinity,
\delta+\gamma(\delta)/\delta\sim\delta>\kappa
. Consequently, Eq. (
12
) has a solution by
continuity. To establish uniqueness, we prove that the function
\delta\mapsto\delta+\gamma(\delta)/\delta
is increasing. Indeed,

\frac{d}{d\delta}\left(\delta+\frac{\gamma(\delta)}{\delta}\right)=1+\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(\delta)}{\delta}-\frac{\gamma(\delta)}{\delta^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{\gamma(\delta)}{\delta^{2}}>0

as shown by Lemma
2
-(ii), and we are done. Proof of Theorem
1
-(i) is completed.

3.3.
Characterization of
x^{\star}_{N}
through a LCP

In this section, we recall the connection between the possible stable equilibrium of the ODE (
1
) and the solution of an underlying LCP in the theory of mathematical programming. We mainly rely on chapter 3 of Takeuchi’s book
[
35
]
.

Given a matrix
M\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N\times N}
and a vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
, the LCP
problem, denoted as
\LCP(M,\boldsymbol{c})
, consists in finding couples of vectors

(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w})\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
satisfying

(13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\boldsymbol{w}&=&M\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{c}\succcurlyeq 0\,,\\
\boldsymbol{y}&\succcurlyeq&0\,,\\
\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}&=&0\,.\end{array}\right.

Notice that the last condition can be written equivalently either
w_{i}y_{i}=0
for all
i\in[N]
or

\support(\boldsymbol{w})\cap\support(\boldsymbol{y})=\emptyset
. When a solution
(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w})
exists we write
\boldsymbol{y}\in\LCP(M,\boldsymbol{c})
. If a solution exists and is unique, we write

\boldsymbol{y}=\LCP(M,\boldsymbol{c})\,.

A necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a unique solution to the LCP problem has been given by Murty
[
29
]
, see also
[
18
]
. For a symmetric matrix, this condition is simply to be positive definite.

The following proposition establishes a connection between the solution of an LCP problem and globally stable equilibrium for a LV system .

Proposition 3

(Lemma 3.2.2 and Theorem 3.2.1 of
[
35
]
)
.

Given a symmetric matrix
B\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N\times N}
and a vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
,
consider the following LV system of ODE:

(14)

\frac{d\boldsymbol{y}}{dt}(t)=\boldsymbol{y}(t)\odot\left(\boldsymbol{c}+B\boldsymbol{y}(t)\right)\,,\quad\boldsymbol{y}(0)\succ 0\,.

for all
t\geq 0
. Then, the LCP problem
\LCP(-B,-\boldsymbol{c})
has an unique solution for each
\boldsymbol{c}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
if and only if
B<0
, i.e.
B
is negative definite.
On the
domain where
B<0,c\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
, the function
x=\LCP(-B,-c)
is
measurable. Moreover, if
B<0
, then for every
\boldsymbol{c}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
, the ODE (
14
) has a
globally stable equilibrium
\boldsymbol{y}^{\star}
given by
\boldsymbol{y}^{\star}=\LCP(-B,-\boldsymbol{c})
.

Indeed, the equilibrium is characterized by the conditions
\boldsymbol{y}^{\star}\succcurlyeq 0
and for all
i\in[N],y_{i}^{\star}(c+(By^{\star})_{i})=0
whereas the condition
-c-By^{\star}\preccurlyeq 0
(with the obvious meaning of
\preccurlyeq
) turns out to be a necessary
condition for the equilibrium
y^{\star}
to be stable in the classical
sense of Lyapounov theory (see
[
35
, Chapter 3]
to recall the
different notions of stability, and
[
35
, Theorem 3.2.5]
for this
result).

Going back to system (
1
), a potential equilibrium
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}
should satisfy

\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}\succcurlyeq 0\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad x^{\star}_{i}\left(r_{i}-\left[(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N})\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}\right]_{i}\right)=0\quad\textrm{for all}\quad i\in[N]\,

and

\boldsymbol{r}_{N}+\left(\Sigma_{N}-I_{N}\right)\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}\preccurlyeq 0,

which means that the couple
(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star},\boldsymbol{w}_{N}^{\star})
solves the problem
\LCP(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N},-\boldsymbol{r}_{N})
.

Applying the reminder (
4
) and Assumption
3
, matrix
I_{N}-\Sigma_{N}
is eventually positive definite with probability one. Define now the vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}
by

(15)

\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\LCP(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N},-\boldsymbol{r}_{N})&\text{if }\|\Sigma_{N}\|<1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{array}\right.

Then, from Proposition
3
, we get that vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}
satisfies the statement of Theorem
1
-(ii).

We end this section by providing an alternative expression of the LCP problem as the solution of a fixed point equation.

Alternative expression for the LCP solution

This fact will be useful in the next section.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{i})\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
and consider the fixed-point equation:

(16)

\boldsymbol{z}=\Upsilon_{N}\boldsymbol{z}_{+}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}

where
\boldsymbol{z}_{+}=\left((z_{i})_{+}\right)
. Then
\boldsymbol{z}
is a solution of (
16
) iff
\boldsymbol{z}_{+}\in\LCP(I_{N}-\Upsilon_{N},-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{z}
is a solution of (
16
) and write
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{z}_{+}-\boldsymbol{z}_{-}
. Then

\boldsymbol{z}_{+},\boldsymbol{z}_{-}\succcurlyeq 0\,,\quad(\boldsymbol{z}_{+})^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{-}=0\quad\textrm{and}\quad\boldsymbol{z}_{-}=(I_{N}-\Upsilon_{N})\boldsymbol{z}_{+}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}\,.

Hence
\boldsymbol{z}_{+}\in\LCP(I_{N}-\Upsilon_{N},-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N})
.

To establish the converse, let
(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w})
a solution of
\LCP(I_{N}-\Upsilon_{N},-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N})
. Define
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{w}
then

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{z}_{+}=\boldsymbol{y}\\
\boldsymbol{z}_{-}=\boldsymbol{w}\end{cases}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{w}=(I_{N}-\Upsilon_{N})\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{z}=\Upsilon_{N}\boldsymbol{z}_{+}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}\,.

∎

3.4.
Design of an AMP algorithm to approximate the LCP solution

The AMP principles in a nutshell

We begin with some of the fundamental results of the AMP theory.
The now classical form of an
AMP iterative algorithm, as formalized in the article
[
7
]
of
Bayati and Montanari based in part on a result of Bolthausen
[
11
]
,
can be presented as follows. Let

(h^{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence of Lipschitz
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
functions. By the Lipschitz assumption, the derivative

\frac{\partial h^{k}(u,a)}{\partial u}

is defined almost everywhere and the function

\partial_{1}h^{k}(u,a)
is any function that coincides with this derivative
where it is defined. For
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{i})_{i\in[N]}
, define by
\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{N}
the scalar quantity:

\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}x_{i}\,.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
be a random vector of so-called auxiliary information. Recall that

A_{N}
is the GOE matrix introduced in Assumption
2
. Starting with a
vector
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{N}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
, the AMP recursion is written

(17)

\boldsymbol{u}^{k+1}_{N}=\frac{A_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}h^{k}(\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N},\boldsymbol{a}_{N})\ -\ \langle\partial_{1}h^{k}(\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N},\boldsymbol{a}_{N})\rangle_{N}\ h^{k-1}(\boldsymbol{u}^{k-1}_{N},\boldsymbol{a}_{N})\,,

where
h^{k}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a})=\left(h^{k}(u_{i},a_{i})\right)_{i\in[N]}
.

From this recursion, it is possible to precisely evaluate the
asymptotic behavior of the empirical measures

\mu^{\boldsymbol{a}_{N},\boldsymbol{u}^{1}_{N},\cdots,\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N}}

as
N\to\infty
for any
k
, and to prove that
\mu^{\boldsymbol{a}_{N},\boldsymbol{u}^{1}_{N},\cdots,\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N}}
converges toward a centered Gaussian vector whose covariance structure is defined by the so-called Density Evolution (DE). The term

\langle\partial_{1}h^{k}(\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N},\boldsymbol{a}_{N})\rangle_{N}\ h^{k-1}(\boldsymbol{u}^{k-1}_{N},\boldsymbol{a}_{N})

(equal to zero for
k=0
) is referred to as the Onsager term and plays a crucial role in making possible this convergence. For a detailed exposition of the AMP theory,
along with the description of many of its applications, the reader is referred
to the recent tutorial
[
23
]
.

A specific AMP algorithm for the LCP

To establish Theorem
1
, we design the following
AMP algorithm and study its properties. For each
N
, let
(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{N},\boldsymbol{a}_{N})\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
be a
couple of random vectors independent of
A_{N}
, with

\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\succcurlyeq 0
. Assume that there exists a couple of
L^{2}
random
variables
(\bar{u},\bar{a})
such that

(18)

(a.s.)\qquad\mu^{\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{N},\boldsymbol{a}_{N}}\quad\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})}\quad{\mathscr{L}}((\bar{u},\bar{a}))\ ,\quad\bar{a}\neq 0\,.

Vectors
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{N}
and

\boldsymbol{a}_{N}
will be specified later, see (
23
). Notice that
\bar{a}\geq 0
. By Assumption
3
,
\kappa
is larger than
\sqrt{2}
hence (

) admits an unique solution
(\delta,\sigma^{2},\gamma)
by the first part of the theorem. Let

h^{t}\equiv h

for all

h(u,a)=\frac{(u+a)_{+}}{\delta}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\partial_{1}h(u,a)=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{u+a>0\}}}{\delta}\,.

The AMP iteration

(19)

\boldsymbol{u}^{k+1}_{N}=\frac{A_{N}}{\delta\sqrt{N}}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{N}^{k}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\right)_{+}-\frac{\langle\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}>0\}}\rangle_{N}\,\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{N}^{k-1}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\right)_{+}}{\delta^{2}}.

The DE equations for this algorithm are provided by the following

(see also

):

Proposition 5
.

For
N\geq 1
, Let
A_{N}
be a GOE matrix and let
(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{N},\boldsymbol{a}_{N})\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
be a
couple of random vectors independent of
A_{N}
, with

\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\succcurlyeq 0
.
Assume (
18
) and consider the recursion (
19
). Then, for every
k\geq 1
,

(a.s.)\qquad\mu^{\boldsymbol{a}_{N},\boldsymbol{u}^{1}_{N},\cdots,\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N}}\quad\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{k+1})}\quad{\mathscr{L}}((\bar{a},Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{k}))\,,

where
\left(Z^{1},\cdots,Z^{k}\right)
is a centered Gaussian vector,
independent of
(\bar{u},\bar{a})
. The
k\times k
covariance matrix
R^{k}
of the random vector
\left(Z^{1},\cdots,Z^{k}\right)
is
defined recursively in
k
as follows:

R^{1}={{\mathbb{E}}}(Z^{1})^{2}=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}}(\bar{u}+\bar{a})_{+}^{2}\,,

and given
R^{k}
, matrix
R^{k+1}
’s first principal submatrix is
R^{k}
,

\left[R^{k+1}\right]_{ij}=\left[R^{k}\right]_{ij}\qquad\textrm{for}\quad i,j\in[k]\,,

whereas the last row and column of
R^{k+1}
are defined via the equations:

\left[R^{k+1}\right]_{k+1,\ell}={{\mathbb{E}}}Z^{k+1}Z^{\ell}=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}{{\mathbb{E}}}(Z^{k}+\bar{a})_{+}(Z^{\ell-1}+\bar{a})_{+}&\text{if}&\ell\in\{2,\ldots,k+1\}\,,\\
{{\mathbb{E}}}(Z^{k}+\bar{a})_{+}(\bar{u}+\bar{a})_{+}&\text{if}&\ell=1\,.\end{array}\right.

Notice that by writing
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{k+1}=\left((\bar{u}+\bar{a})_{+},(Z^{1}+\bar{a})_{+},\cdots,(Z^{k}+\bar{a})_{+}\right)^{\top}
, we see
that
R^{k+1}={{\mathbb{E}}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{k+1})^{\top}
, which immediately shows that

R^{k+1}
is a positive semidefinite matrix (actually, one can prove that it is definite, see

[
23
]
).

Denote by

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}=\boldsymbol{u}_{N}^{k}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\,.

What is going to drive the following computations
is the fact that the vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k+1}
will tend to be aligned as
N\to\infty
then
k\to\infty
. This will be formalized and proved in Lemma
6
.
Denote by
\gamma^{k}_{N}=\big\langle\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}>0\right\}}\big\rangle_{N}

and recall the expression of
\gamma
given
in (
5c
). With these notations at hand, the AMP recursion (

) reads:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A_{N}}{\delta\sqrt{N}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\frac{\gamma^{k}_{N}}{\delta^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}\big)_{+}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\ ,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A_{N}}{\delta\sqrt{N}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\frac{\gamma}{\delta^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}\big)_{+}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}+\frac{\gamma-\gamma^{k}_{N}}{\delta^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}\big)_{+}\ ,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A_{N}}{\delta\sqrt{N}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\frac{\gamma}{\delta^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}+\frac{\gamma-\gamma^{k}_{N}}{\delta^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}\big)_{+}+\frac{\gamma}{\delta^{2}}\left(\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}\big)_{+}\right)\ .

Replacing now
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k+1}
by
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}

(20)

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N}=\frac{A_{N}}{\delta\sqrt{N}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\frac{\gamma}{\delta^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N},

where

Massaging (

(22)

\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\frac{\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{-}}{1+\gamma/\delta^{2}}=\frac{A_{N}}{\kappa\sqrt{N}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}+\frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{a}_{N}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N})}{\kappa}\,.

Denote by

\boldsymbol{z}=\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\frac{\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{-}}{1+\gamma/\delta^{2}}\,.

Notice that
\boldsymbol{z}_{+}=\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}
and set finally

(23)

\boldsymbol{u}_{N}^{0}=\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{a}_{N}=\frac{\kappa}{\delta}\boldsymbol{r}_{N}\,.

With these notations, (

(24)

\boldsymbol{z}=\Sigma_{N}\boldsymbol{z}_{+}+\boldsymbol{r}_{N}+\frac{\delta}{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{N}^{k}\,.

Relying on Proposition
4
and on the fact that
\|\Sigma_{N}\|<1
eventually, we conclude that
\boldsymbol{z}_{+}=\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}
is the unique solution of

\LCP\left(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N},-\boldsymbol{r}_{N}-\frac{\delta}{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{N}^{k}\right)

for

\frac{\delta}{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{N}^{k}
- see Eq. (

Remark 8
.

Retrospectively, notice that with the choice (
23
), assumptions of Proposition
5
are satisfied:
(\boldsymbol{u}_{N}^{0},\boldsymbol{a}_{N})
is independent of
A_{N}
and (
18
) holds thanks to Assumption
1
with
\bar{a}=\frac{\kappa}{\delta}\bar{r}
.

Before bounding
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{N}^{k}
, let us first study the behavior of
\mu^{\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}}
. Applying Proposition
5
, we get that for all
k\geq 2
:

\mu^{\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N}}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({{\mathbb{R}}})}{\mathscr{L}}(Z^{k})\,,

where
Z^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathcal{L}}}}{{=}}\theta_{k}\bar{Z}
with
\bar{Z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathcal{L}}}}{{=}}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
and
\theta_{k}
satisfying the following DE equation:

\theta^{2}_{k+1}=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}}(\theta_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{a})_{+}^{2}\,.

Since function
\varphi(u,a)=(u+a)_{+}
is Lipschitz, it is clear that

(26)

\mu^{(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N})_{+}}\quad\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({{\mathbb{R}}})}\quad{\mathscr{L}}\left((\theta_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{a})_{+}\right)\,.

Furthermore, since the distribution function of

\theta_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{a}
has no discontinuity, the following convergence holds:

(a.s.)\qquad\gamma^{k}_{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\theta_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{a}>0\right)\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad\gamma^{k}_{N}=\big\langle\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}>0\right\}}\big\rangle_{N}\,.

Introduce the quantity:

(27)

\sigma_{k}=\frac{\delta}{\kappa}\theta_{k}\,.

Following (
25
), the recursive equation satisfied by
\sigma_{k}
is

\sigma_{k+1}^{2}=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sigma_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}\right)_{+}^{2}

which is precisely the equation appearing in Lemma
2
-(ii). As a conclusion,
\sigma_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\sigma
, where
\sigma
satisfies (
5b
). This convergence has two interesting consequences:

{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\theta_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{a}>0\right)={{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\sigma_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}>0\right)\quad\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\quad{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}>0\right)=\gamma\,,

where
\gamma
satisfies (

), and

{\mathcal{L}}\left((\theta_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{a})_{+}\right)={\mathcal{L}}\left(\left(1+\gamma/\delta^{2}\right)(\sigma_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}\right)\quad\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({{\mathbb{R}}})}\quad{\mathcal{L}}\left(\left(1+\gamma/\delta^{2}\right)(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}\right)\ ,

the latter being the distribution appearing in Theorem

-(iii).

Control of the error term
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}

Recall the expression of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}
given in (
21
):

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}=\frac{\gamma-\gamma^{k}_{N}}{\delta^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}\big)_{+}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k+1}+\frac{\gamma}{\delta^{2}}\left(\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}\big)_{+}\right)\,.

A direct consequence of (
26
) yields that

\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1})_{+}\|^{2}}{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.}{{\mathbb{E}}}\left(\theta_{k-1}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{a}\right)_{+}^{2}=\theta_{k}^{2}\delta^{2}\,.

In particular, the sequence
\left(\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1})_{+}\|^{2}}{N}\right)_{N}
is
bounded. Furthermore,

\lim_{k}(a.s.)\lim_{N}(\gamma-\gamma^{k}_{N})=0
. We thus have

(28)

\lim_{k\to\infty}(a.s.)\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{(\gamma-\gamma^{k}_{N})^{2}}{\delta^{4}}\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1})_{+}\|^{2}}{N}=0\,.

The main idea to control the two remaining terms
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k+1}
and
\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}\big)_{+}
is to establish that the correlation coefficient

(29)

Q_{k}:=\frac{{{\mathbb{E}}}Z^{k-1}Z^{k}}{\theta_{k-1}\theta_{k}}

converges to
1
as
k\to\infty
. This can be interpreted as an alignement of vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1}
. This argument was developed in a similar context in
[
28
]
, see also

[
19
]
. For self-containedness, we state and prove the following lemma:

Lemma 6
.

The sequence
(Q_{k})_{k\geq 2}
defined in (
29
)
satisfies
Q_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}1
.

Proof of Lemma
6
is postponed to Appendix
A.2
.

We now conclude the proof of Theorem
1
.
Consider
\varphi(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}\in\PL_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. By Proposition
5
, we have

(a.s.)\quad\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k+1}_{N}\|^{2}}{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(u^{k}_{i},u^{k+1}_{i})\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}{{\mathbb{E}}}\left(Z^{k+1}-Z^{k}\right)^{2}=\theta_{k+1}^{2}+\theta_{k}^{2}-2\theta_{k+1}\theta_{k}Q_{k+1}\,.

Applying Lemma
6
, we get that:

(30)

\lim_{k\to\infty}(a.s.)\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k+1}_{N}\|^{2}}{N}=0\,.

A similar argument applies to the last term.

\frac{1}{N}\|\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N}\big)_{+}-\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k-1}_{N}\big)_{+}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\|\big(\boldsymbol{u}^{k}_{N}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\big)_{+}-\big(\boldsymbol{u}^{k-1}_{N}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\big)_{+}\|^{2}\\
\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.}\quad\mathbb{E}\left((Z^{k}+\bar{a})_{+}-(Z^{k-1}+\bar{a})_{+}\right)^{2}={{\mathbb{E}}}\left(Z^{k+1}-Z^{k}\right)^{2}\,.

Finally, using that

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}\|^{2}}{N}\quad\leq\quad\frac{3}{N}\left(\frac{(\gamma-\gamma^{k}_{N})^{2}}{\delta^{4}}\|(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1})_{+}\|^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\delta^{4}}\|\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}-(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k-1})_{+}\|^{2}\right),

we conclude that

(31)

\lim_{k\to\infty}(a.s.)\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}\|^{2}}{N}=0\,.

Notice that the fact that the a.s.
\lim_{N}
at the left hand side exists can be deduced again from
Proposition
5
.

From the approximated LCP to the genuine LCP

Recall that whenever
\|\Sigma_{N}\|<1
, which happens eventually,

\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}=\LCP(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N},-\boldsymbol{r}_{N})\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}=\LCP\left(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N},-\boldsymbol{r}_{N}-\frac{\delta}{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{N}^{k}\right)\,.

Statistical properties have been established for
\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}
via the AMP procedure, see
for instance (
26
). Using LCP perturbation results, we shall identify the limiting empirical distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{\star}
. Let us introduce:

\mu^{\star}={\mathcal{L}}\left((1+\gamma/\delta^{2})(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}\right)={\mathcal{L}}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\delta}(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}\right)

In
[
15
, Th. 2.7, Th. 2.8]
, Chen and Xiang provide the following bound:

(32)

\|\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}-(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N})_{+}\|\ \leq\ \left\|\left(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N}\right)^{-1}\right\|\times\frac{\kappa}{\delta}\left\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}\right\|\ =\ b_{N}\left\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}\right\|\\
\textrm{where}\quad b_{N}:=\left\|\left(I_{N}-\Sigma_{N}\right)^{-1}\right\|\times\frac{\kappa}{\delta}\,.

Let
\varphi:{{\mathbb{R}}}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
be an arbitrary function in
\PL(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with Lipschitz constant
L_{\varphi}
.
For a given positive integer
k
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(x^{\star}_{i})-\int\varphi d\mu^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\varphi(x^{\star}_{i})-\varphi((\xi^{k}_{i})_{+})\right)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi((\xi^{k}_{i})_{+})-\int\varphi d\mu^{\star}

\displaystyle:=\epsilon^{1}_{N}(k)+\epsilon^{2}_{N}(k).

We first handle
\epsilon^{2}_{N}(k)
. By Proposition
5
, we have:

\epsilon_{N}^{2}(k)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}{{\mathbb{E}}}\,\varphi\left(\frac{\kappa}{\delta}(\sigma_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}\right)-{{\mathbb{E}}}\,\varphi\left(\frac{\kappa}{\delta}(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}\right)\,.

The r.h.s. is easily bounded by a constant
C(k)
which converges to zero as
k\to\infty
, using the fact that
\lim_{k}\sigma_{k}=\sigma
.

We now turn to
\epsilon^{1}_{N}(k)
. By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\varphi(x^{\star}_{i})-\varphi((\xi^{k}_{i})_{+})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{L_{\varphi}}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left|x^{\star}_{i}-(\xi^{k}_{i})_{+}\right|\left(1+|x^{\star}_{i}|+|(\xi^{k}_{i})_{+}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{L_{\varphi}}{N}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}-(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N})_{+}\right\|\left(\sum_{i\in[N]}(1+|x^{\star}_{i}|+|\xi^{k}_{i})_{+}|)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3L_{\varphi}\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}-(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N})_{+}\right\|}{\sqrt{N}}\left(1+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{N}\|}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N})_{+}\|}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

Recall the bound (
32
) and the definition of
b_{N}
, then

|\epsilon^{1}_{N}(k)|\leq 3L_{\varphi}b_{N}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}\|}{\sqrt{N}}\left(1+2\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N})_{+}\|}{\sqrt{N}}+b_{N}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{k}_{N}\|}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

By Assumption
3
,
b_{N}
a.s. converges
to a positive constant. By Proposition
5
, we furthermore have

\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{N})_{+}\|}{\sqrt{N}}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}\left({{\mathbb{E}}}(\theta_{k}{\bar{Z}}+\bar{a})_{+}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

which is bounded in
k
. Using (
31
), we obtain that

\limsup_{N}|\epsilon^{1}_{N}(k)|
is bounded with probability one by a constant

C_{1}(k)
which converges to zero as
k\to\infty
. Finally,

(a.s.)\qquad\limsup_{N}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\varphi(x^{\star}_{i})-\int\varphi d\mu^{\star}\right|\leq C(k)+C_{1}(k)\,.

Since
C(k)+C_{1}(k)
can be made arbitrarily small, we have

(a.s.)\qquad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(x^{\star}_{i})\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\int\varphi d\mu^{\star},

which ends the proof of Theorem
1
.

From AMP recursions to an approximate LCP solution

We introduce the following notations:

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}=\boldsymbol{u}_{N}^{k}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\ ,\qquad\gamma_{N}^{k}=\langle 1_{\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}>0\}}\rangle_{N}\,.

\gamma

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}+\frac{\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k}\big)_{+}}{\delta}=\frac{B_{N}^{\top}B_{N}}{\left(1+\frac{c\gamma}{\delta}\right)\delta P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}^{k})_{+}+\boldsymbol{a}_{N}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{N}^{k}

A.1.
Proof of Lemma
2

Consider the function

f(\sigma^{2})={{\mathbb{E}}}(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}^{2}
. Then,
Equation (
5b
) is equivalent to the fixed-point equation:

(35)

\frac{f(\sigma^{2})}{\delta^{2}}=\sigma^{2}.

We can prove by elementary means that

\frac{df}{d\sigma^{2}}(\sigma^{2})=\frac{1}{2\sigma}\frac{df}{d\sigma}(\sigma^{2})=\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathbb{E}{\bar{Z}}(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}\ .

Moreover, conditioning on
\bar{r}
and applying the integration by parts formula for the Gaussian r.v.
{\bar{Z}}
we get

\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathbb{E}\left({\bar{Z}}(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})_{+}\mid\bar{r}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}\geq 0\}}\mid\bar{r}\right)\,.

Hence

\frac{df}{d\sigma^{2}}(\sigma^{2})=\mathbb{P}\{\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}\geq 0\}=\mathbb{P}\{{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}/\sigma\geq 0\}\,.

Notice that
\frac{df}{d\sigma^{2}}
is a decreasing function since

\sigma<\sigma^{\prime}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\{{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}/\sigma^{\prime}\geq 0\}\subset\{{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}/\sigma\geq 0\}\,,

with

\lim_{\sigma^{2}\to\infty}\frac{df}{d\sigma^{2}}(\sigma^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\,.

We now introduce function
g(\sigma^{2})=\frac{f(\sigma^{2})}{\delta^{2}}-\sigma^{2}
. Notice that
g(0)=\mathbb{E}\bar{r}^{2}/\delta^{2}>0
and that

(36)

\frac{dg}{d\sigma^{2}}(\sigma^{2})=\frac{\mathbb{P}\{{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}/\sigma\geq 0\}}{\delta^{2}}-1>\frac{1}{2\delta^{2}}-1\,.

If
\frac{1}{2\delta^{2}}-1\geq 0
which is equivalent to the condition
\delta<(\sqrt{2})^{-1}
then
g
’s derivative is positive hence
g
is increasing with a positive starting point and never vanishes.

Suppose now that
\delta>1/\sqrt{2}
. We shall prove that
g
vanishes at a unique point
\sigma^{2}(\delta)
:

(37)

g(\sigma^{2}(\delta))=0\qquad\textrm{for}\qquad\sigma^{2}(\delta)>0\,.

Notice that the derivative
dg/d\sigma^{2}
is decreasing with a negative limit at infinity

\lim_{\sigma^{2}\to\infty}\frac{dg}{d\sigma^{2}}(\sigma^{2})=\frac{1}{2\delta^{2}}-1<0\,.

Depending on the sign of the value of
dg/d\sigma^{2}
at zero, either
g
is constantly decreasing from the positive value
g(0)
or
g
is first increasing then eventually decreasing. We now prove that

(38)

\lim_{\sigma^{2}\to\infty}g(\sigma^{2})<0\,.

This will yield (
37
).

\frac{g(\sigma^{2})}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}(\sigma{\bar{Z}}+\bar{r})^{2}_{+}}{\delta^{2}\sigma^{2}}-1=\frac{\mathbb{E}({\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}/\sigma)^{2}_{+}}{\delta^{2}}-1\xrightarrow[\sigma^{2}\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{2\delta^{2}}-1<0\,.

Hence
g
’s limit is
-\infty
at infinity. Eq. (
38
) is proved, so is (
37
). The first statement of the lemma is proved.

We now address the second point of the lemma. Let
\delta>1/\sqrt{2}
be fixed.
From the previous analysis, we know that

\frac{dg}{d\sigma^{2}}\bigg|_{\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}(\delta)}<0.

From (
36
), one can compute

\frac{dg}{d\sigma^{2}}\bigg|_{\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}(\delta)}=\frac{\mathbb{P}\{\sigma(\delta){\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}\geq 0\}}{\delta^{2}}-1,

and this gives the second point :

\mathbb{P}\{\sigma(\delta){\bar{Z}}+\bar{r}\geq 0\}<\delta^{2}\,.

We now address the third point of the lemma. Consider a sequence
(\sigma_{t})
such that

\sigma_{0}^{2}>0\textrm{ and }\sigma_{p+1}^{2}=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}f(\sigma_{p}^{2})\,.

One can easily prove that
\sigma_{p}^{2}\uparrow_{p}\sigma^{2}(\delta)
(resp.
\sigma_{p}^{2}\downarrow\sigma^{2}(\delta)
) if
\sigma_{0}^{2}<\sigma^{2}(\delta)
(resp.
\sigma_{0}^{2}>\sigma^{2}(\delta)
). The sequence remains constant if
\sigma^{2}_{0}=\sigma^{2}(\delta)
. Lemma
2
is proved.

Proof.

\theta_{k}
in (

in (

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}

\displaystyle\frac{{{\mathbb{E}}}Z^{k}Z^{k+1}}{\theta_{k}\theta_{k+1}}=\frac{{{\mathbb{E}}}(\theta_{k-1}\bar{Z}^{k-1}+\bar{a})_{+}(\theta_{k}\bar{Z}^{k}+\bar{a})_{+}}{\sqrt{{{\mathbb{E}}}(\theta_{k-1}\bar{Z}^{k-1}+\bar{a})_{+}^{2}{{\mathbb{E}}}(\theta_{k}\bar{Z}^{k}+\bar{a})_{+}^{2}}}\ ,
