Towards coherence theorems for equational extensions of type theories Thanks: The author was supported by the European Union, co-financed by the European Social Fund (EFOP-3.6.3-VEKOP-16-2017-00002).
Rafaël Bocquet

Abstract
. We study the conservativity of extensions by additional strict equalities of dependent type theories (and more general second-order generalized algebraic theories). The conservativity of Extensional Type Theory over Intensional Type Theory was proven by Hofmann. Our goal is to generalize such results to type theories without the Uniqueness of Identity Proofs principles, such as variants of Homotopy Type Theory. For this purpose, we construct what is essentially the ∞ \infty -congruence on the base theory that is freely generated by the considered equations. This induces a factorization of any equational extension, whose two factors can be studied independently. We conjecture that the first factor is always an equivalence when the base theory is well-behaved. We prove that the second factor is an equivalence when the ∞ \infty -congruence is 0 0 -truncated.



1.
Introduction

Equality and computation are central components of type theories.
The computational content of a type theory is presented by computation rules (often called
\beta
-rules, or
\iota
-rules for inductive types), and perhaps uniqueness rules (usually called
\eta
-rules) or more exotic rules (such as the
\nu
-rules considered in
[
AMB13
]
).
This computational content is typically explained by the means of a normalization algorithm.
In presence of identity types, there is a distinction between two notions of equality or identification between terms of a type theory.
Internally, the identity types provide the notion of
internal identification
, also called
propositional equality
or
typal equality
.
Externally, we can also compare terms up to
strict equality
, which is the proof-irrelevant equality of our metatheory.
Strict equality is also often called
definitional
or
judgemental
equality.

When working internally to a type theory, it is desirable to have as many strict equalities as possible.
Indeed, strict equality can be implicitly and silently coerced over.
On the other hand, internal identifications require explicit transports and coercions, which quickly clutter the terms of the theory.
Conversely, the trade-off is that type theories with additional strict equalities have fewer models, and their semantics are therefore more complicated.

Hofmann proved in
[
Hof95
]
a conservativity theorem, showing that all internal identifications in a type theory can conservatively be turned into strict equalities, when the base type theory satisfies some principles.
The most important of these principles is the Uniqueness of Identity Proofs (UIP) principle, which states that there is an identification between any two elements of identity types.
A more syntactic proof was later given by Oury
[
Our05
]
for the calculus of constructions.
Oury’s proof had some issues, mainly due to a presentation of the syntax of type theory with too few annotations.
An improvement of Oury’s proof and a presentation of this result as a constructive and effective syntactic translation has been given recently by Winterhalter
et al.

[
WST19
]
.

Since Hofmann’s proof of conservativity, there has been a lot of interest going into the study of type theories with non-trivial higher dimensional content inconsistent with UIP
[
HS94
]
, and their semantics in homotopy theoretic
[
AW09
,
KL21a
]
and
\infty
-categorical structures
[
KS17
]
.
For type theories without UIP, strict equalities are even more important, because they are automatically coherent.
Thus having more strict equalities means that we escape not only “transport hell”, but also “higher-dimensional transport and coherence hell”.
Conversely, it is in practice much harder to justify strict equalities in many homotopy theoretic models.
Some authors have even considered weak variants of the basic computation rules of type theories.
For instance, weak identity types, whose computation rule only holds up to internal paths, have been introduced by
[
vdB18
]
, under the name of propositional identity types.
The path types of cubical type theories
[
CCHM17
]
also only satisfy weakly the computation rule of identity types.
Other type structures can be weakened similarly, and we can even consider type theories whose computation rules are all expressed by internal identifications instead of strict equalities.
At the level of types and universes, instead of assuming that each type former is strictly classified by some code in the universe, we can ask for them to be classified only up to type equivalence.
These weak Tarski universes have been introduced in
[
Gal14
]
.
The fact that homotopy type theory with strict univalent universes, rather than weak universes, can be interpreted in every
(\infty,1)
-topos has only been established recently
[
Shu19
]
.

In this setting, we can wonder how type theories with varying amounts of strict equalities can be compared.
More precisely, we wish to know how to establish coherence and strictification theorems, that would allow us, when working internally to a model of a weak type theory, to pretend that it satisfies more strict equalities than it actually does, by replacing it by an equivalent stricter model.
The question of the conservativity of strict identity types over weak identity types has been asked at the TYPES 2017 conference
[
ACC+17
]
, motivated by the fact that the path types in cubical type theory only satisfy the elimination principle of weak identity types.
This was also the original motivation for the present paper.

We give some examples of weakenings and extensions of homotopy type theory that ought to be equivalent to standard homotopy type theory.

Examples 1.1
.

•

Weakening the
\beta
and
\eta
computation rules of identity types,
\Sigma
-types, inductive types, etc, gives a weaker variant of HoTT.

•

We can add strict equalities that make the addition on natural numbers into a strictly associative and commutative operation.
That is, while the inductive definition of
(-+-):\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
only satisfies the strict equalities
0+y=y
and
(\mathsf{suc}\ x)+y=\mathsf{suc}\ (x+y)
, we would add the strict equalities
x+0=0
,
x+(\mathsf{suc}\ y)=\mathsf{suc}\ (x+y)
,
x+y=y+x
,
(x+y)+z=x+(y+z)
, etc.

•

Similarly, we could make the composition of paths into a strictly associative operation, optionally with strict inverses.

•

We can extend the theory with a universe of strict proposition
[
GCST19
]
that is equivalent to the universe of homotopy propositions.

•

Similarly, we can extend the theory with universes of definitional categories, definitional rings, etc, that satisfy strictly the equations of the theories of categories, rings, etc.

•

We can extend the theory with a universe of “definitionally” pointed types
\mathsf{DPtType}
, equivalent to the universe of pointed types
\mathsf{PtType}\triangleq(A:\mathcal{U})\times A
, with a smash product operation
(-\wedge-):\mathsf{DPtType}\to\mathsf{DPtType}\to\mathsf{DPtType}
with more strict equalities than the smash product of
\mathsf{PtType}
.
This would provide an alternative interpretation of Brunerie’s rewriting based method to prove that the smash product is a symmetric monoidal product on pointed types
[
Bru18
]
.

Some progress has been made by Isaev in
[
Isa18b
]
.
In that paper, Isaev defines the notion of Morita equivalence between type theories, and gives some characterizations of that notion.
A first conservativity result in the absence of UIP is also proven, showing that type theories with weak or strict unit types are Morita equivalent.

The constructions by Isaev
[
Isa18a
]
and Kapulkin and Lumsdaine
[
KL16
,
KL21b
]
, of Quillen model or semi-model structures over the categories of models of type theories, are also extremely relevant for our work.
In particular, as remarked in
[
KL16
]
, Hofmann’s conservativity theorem proves exactly that the morphism
\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ITT}}\to\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ETT}}
between the initial models of intensional type theory and extensional type theory is a trivial fibration of their semi-model structure.
The weak equivalences of the same semi-model structure correspond to a weaker notion of conservativity than trivial fibrations.
Isaev’s definition of Morita equivalence relies on that notion of weak equivalence.

This paper builds on top of the aforementioned work.
While Isaev considers the notion of Morita equivalence for arbitrary morphisms between type theories, we restrict our attention to the equational extensions
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}_{E}
of a weak type theory to a strict type theory
\mathcal{T}_{E}
, by a family of equations
E
, which should hold weakly in
\mathcal{T}
and strictly in
\mathcal{T}_{E}
.
We then establish sufficient conditions for the theories
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}_{E}
to be Morita equivalent.

The situation can be compared to other well-known coherence theorems, such as Mac Lane’s coherence theorem for monoidal categories
[
Lan63
]
.
They can often be stated in multiple different ways.
For example, here are two related ways to state the coherence theorem for monoidal categories.

(1)

Every (weak) monoidal category is monoidally equivalent to a strict monoidal category.

(2)

In a freely generated monoidal category, every diagram made up of associators and unitors commutes.

The statement (
1
) is generally the one that we want to use: it allows us to work with any weak monoidal category as if it was strict.
The statement (
2
) is however perhaps easier to prove, because free monoidal categories can be seen as syntactic objects, that are relatively easy to describe explicitly and understand.
See
[
JS91
]
for a proof of the statement (
1
) that relies on the statement (
2
).
In the case of monoidal category, it is actually possible to prove the statement (
1
) more directly using representation theorems similar to the Yoneda lemma.
This kind of approach does not seem suitable for the coherence theorems that we are interested in.

The main result of this paper is a coherence theorem for type theories that is analogous to the fact the statement (
1
) can be deduced from the statement (
2
).
It states that to establish the conservativity of the equational extension
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}_{E}
, it suffices to check that the
\infty
-congruence over
\mathcal{T}
freely generated by the equations of
E
exists and is
0
-truncated.
This
0
-truncatedness condition encodes the same idea as the fact that every diagram made up of associators and unitors commutes in a freely generated monoidal category.

In general, we work at the level of
second-order generalized algebraic theories
(SOGATs) and their classifying
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwFs.
SOGATs correspond to the class of theories classified by representable map categories, which were introduced by Uemura
[
Uem19
]
.
First-order generalized algebraic theories are also SOGATs, so our results also apply to theories that are not type theories, such as monoidal categories, etc. A SOGAT
\mathcal{T}
is specified by its classifying
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF, also denoted by
\mathcal{T}
, which can be seen as a syntactic model of some type theory.
A SOGAT has functorial semantics in
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwFs.
Even if one is only interested in a specific theory, working with SOGATs explicitly is advantageous.
Indeed, many semantic conditions can instead be stated more concisely and syntactically directly at the level of the
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF
\mathcal{T}
.

The notion of
\infty
-congruence over a SOGAT
\mathcal{T}
is specified at the level of the classifying
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF.
Informally, a
1
-congruence over
\mathcal{T}
is an extension of the set-valued
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF
\mathcal{T}
to a setoid-valued
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF; this equips the sets of contexts, substitutions, types and terms of
\mathcal{T}
with equivalence relations satisfying some conditions.
Instead, an
\infty
-congruence should be an extension of
\mathcal{T}
to a space-valued
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF; this equips all components of
\mathcal{T}
with
\infty
-groupoid structures.

Defining
\infty
-congruences require choosing a model of
\infty
-groupoids among many.
Instead of working with e.g. Kan simplicial sets, we rely on the fact that
\infty
-groupoids are closely related to models of type theory with identity types.
Instead of working with space valued
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwFs, with work with
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwFs with identity types, i.e.
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs.
In the original presentation of this work, this was inspired by Brunerie’s type-theoretic definition of
\infty
-groupoid
[
Bru16
]
.
Another point of view is that models of type theory with identity types correspond to structured
\infty
-categories.
When working with
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs, we morally work with representable map
\infty
-categories.
Representable map
\infty
-categories have recently been used by Nguyen and Uemura
[
NU22
]
to give functorial semantics to
\infty
-type theories, generalizing the functorial semantics of
1
-type theories in representable map categories.

We don’t give any application of our coherence theorem in this paper, which is instead focused on proving general results that hold for any SOGAT.
Two elements are still missing for applications, discussed in more details in
section

9
.
First, we need a proof of
6.3
, which essentially states that our construction of the
\infty
-congruence generated by
E
is meaningful when
\mathcal{T}
satisfies external univalence
[
Boc22
]
.
Second, we need ways to prove the
0
-truncatedness conditions for concrete theories.
We believe that in most cases, this
0
-truncatedness can be obtained as a consequence of a homotopy normalization proof.
A strict normalization states that every term has a unique normal form; homotopy normalization states instead that every term has a contractible space of normal forms.
Uemura has recently given a general proof scheme for normalization of
\infty
-type theories
[
Uem22
]
, which can be used to prove such homotopy normalization results.
Because we work with
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs rather than
\infty
-type theories, the proof does not directly carry over to our setting, but we believe that the results we need can be proven using similar methods.

2.
Background

We recall in this section the semantics of type theories in categories with families (CwFs), and introduce the tools and notations that we will use in this paper.
We will make use in particular of the internal type-theoretic language of the presheaf category
\mathbf{Psh}(\mathcal{C})
as a tool to define and work with type-theoretic structures over a CwF
\mathcal{C}
.

2.1.
Metatheory and basic notations

We work in a constructive metatheory, say extensional type theory with enough universes and all QIITs.
In some cases we will need distinguish split and non-split surjective functions.
We adopt terminology from the HoTT book; we use the adverb
merely
to indicate that we have an element of the propositional truncation of some set.
For example, a function
f:A\to B
is a split surjection when for every
b:B
there is some element
a:A
such that
f(a)=b
.
A function
f:A\to B
is a non-split surjection when f or every
b:B
there merely exists some
a:A
such that
f(a)=b
.

The sets of dependent function are written
(a:A)\to B(a)
, and dependent functions are introduced by
(a:A)\mapsto b(a)
.
We sometimes omit function arguments when they can be inferred from the context.
For instance, given a function
f:(a:A)\to B(a)\to C(a)
, and elements
a:A
and
b:B(a)
, we may just write
f(b)
or
f_{a}(b)
instead of
f(a,b)
.

The sets of dependent pairs are written
(a:A)\times B(a)
, and dependent pairs are introduced by
(a,b)
.

We make use of the interpretation of extensional type theory into presheaf toposes.
Because our metatheory is constructive, any of our external constructions can also be interpreted in presheaf toposes.

2.4.
Categories with families

We review the notion of category with families (CwF), which is used to model type theory.

We first recall the definition of locally representable presheaf, which is used to encode the context extensions in models of type theory.
This definition is a reformulation of the notion of representable natural transformation, which has been used by Awodey
[
Awo18
]
to define natural models, which are equivalent to categories with families.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
Y

We say that
Y

The conservativity of Extensional Type Theory over Intensional Type Theory can be stated using the notion of trivial fibration:
the unique morphism
\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ITT}}\to\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ETT}}
between the initial models of the two type theories is a non-split trivial fibration in
\mathbf{CwF}
, i.e. its actions on types and terms are surjective.

The global picture of Hofmann’s proof of conservativity
[
Hof95
]
is the following.
We construct a quotient
Q
of the syntactic model
\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ITT}}
.
By the universal properties of the quotient
Q
and of the model
\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ETT}}
, we obtain a section-retraction pair
(\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ETT}}\to Q,Q\to\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ETT}})
.
This exhibits the map
\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ITT}}\to\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ETT}}
as a retract of the quotient inclusion
\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ITT}}\to Q
.
Since the quotient inclusion is a non-split trivial fibration and non-split trivial fibration are closed under retracts, we obtain the desired conservativity result.
Most of the complexity of the proof lies in the construction of the quotient
Q
.

It is not possible to prove constructively that
\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ITT}}\to\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ETT}}
is a split trivial fibration.
Indeed, if it were possible, it would be possible to decide equality of terms of
\mathbf{0}_{\mathsf{ETT}}
by deciding the equality of their lifts in
\mathsf{ITT}
.

2.5.
Contextual CwFs

An important class of CwFs are the
contextual
CwFs, whose objects and morphisms are really given by lists of types and terms.
Indeed, from some point of view, in the language of type theory, we never explicitly talk about the objects and morphisms of a model, but only about types and terms that live in the same contextual slice of a given model.
From this perspective, only the contextual models matter.
However, a direct definition of contextual models is complicated (their generalized algebraic presentation is infinite, as it quantifies over all possible context lengths), and many intermediate constructions go through non-contextual models.
It is thus more convenient to define contextuality as a property of general models.
Fortunately, they can be described by the means of an orthogonal factorization system on
\mathbf{CwF}

3.
Weak identity types

In this section we define the weak and strict variants of identity types.
Generally, the computation rules of a weak type structure are expressed by internal identifications, whereas the computation rules of strict type structures are expressed by strict equalities.
These notions should not be confused with weakly stable and strictly stable type-structures.
Since type theories with weak computation rules are the main focus of this paper, we reserve the short naming for this notion.
In ambiguous cases, we could talk of weakly computational and strictly computational type-structures.
In the literature, strict identity types sometimes refer to
(-1)
-truncated identity types, which satisfy the Uniqueness of Identity Proofs principle.

3.1.
Definition

In presence of (strict)
\Sigma
-type and
\Pi
-types, there are several equivalent ways to define the eliminator for identity types.
The Martin-Löf eliminator is given by the following rule.

\frac{A\ \mathsf{type}\quad x:A,y:A,p:\mathsf{Id}_{A}(x,y)\vdash D(x,y,p)\ \mathsf{type}\quad x:A\vdash d(x):D(x,x,\mathsf{refl})\quad x:A\quad y:A\quad p:\mathsf{Id}_{A}(x,y)}{\mathsf{J}(D,d,y,p):D(x,y,p)}\quad\textsc{Martin-L\"{o}f eliminator}

In absence of
\Pi
-types, the Martin-Löf eliminator does not seem to be strong enough to even define transport.

In
[
GG08
]
, a variant of the Martin-Löf eliminator, now called the Frobenius variant of the Martin-Löf eliminator, is introduced.
The idea is to circumvent the absence of
\Pi
-types by allowing the target type family
D
of the elimination to depend on any telescope
\Delta
of parameters.

\frac{A\ \mathsf{type}\quad x:A,y:A,p:\mathsf{Id}_{A}(x,y)\vdash\Delta(x,y,p)\ \mathsf{type}^{\star}\quad\quad x:A,y:A,p:\mathsf{Id}_{A}(x,y),\delta:\Delta(x,y,p)\vdash D(x,y,p,\delta)\ \mathsf{type}\quad x:A,\delta:\Delta(x,x,\mathsf{refl})\vdash d(x,\delta):D(x,x,\mathsf{refl},\delta)\quad\quad x:A\quad y:A\quad p:\mathsf{Id}_{A}(x,y)\quad\delta:\Delta(x,y,p)}{\mathsf{J}(D,d,x,y,p,\delta):D(x,y,p,\delta)}\quad\textsc{Frobenius Martin-L\"{o}f eliminator}

Another alternative is the Paulin-Mohring eliminator, also known as based path induction, or one-sided eliminator.

\frac{A\ \mathsf{type}\quad x:A\quad y:A,p:\mathsf{Id}_{A}(x,y)\vdash D(y,p)\ \mathsf{type}\quad\quad d(x):D(x,\mathsf{refl})\quad y:A\quad p:\mathsf{Id}_{A}(x,y)}{\mathsf{J}(D,d,y,p):D(y,p)}\quad\textsc{Paulin-Mohring eliminator}

North
[
Nor19
]
and Isaev
[
Isa21
]
have independently given proofs of the fact that the Paulin-Mohring eliminator is equivalent to the Frobenius variant of the Martin-Löf eliminator in the presence of
\Sigma
-types.

We use the weak variant of the Paulin-Mohring eliminator, with the computation rule weakened to a weak equality.
We will show that the other eliminators can also be derived, even in the absence of
\Sigma
-types.
In fact, we will prove that weak identity types can be lifted from a family
\mathbb{C}
to its telescope family
\mathbb{C}^{\star}
, i.e. the family whose types are list of types of
\mathbb{C}
; the derivation of the Frobenius eliminator can be seen as a consequence of this fact.
The main step of this derivation is originally due to András Kovács.
The proof has been simplified using the notion of join of families by Christian Sattler.

4.
Second-order generalized algebraic theories

In this section we recall the theory of second-order generalized algebraic theories.
Generalized algebraic theories (GATs) are theories specified by a collection of dependent sorts, operations and equations between them.
Second-order generalized algebraic theories (SOGATs) generalize GATs by allowing second-order operations, meaning that the arguments of an operation can bind some variables.
Most type theories can be encoded as SOGATs.
Any (first-order) GAT can also be seen as a SOGAT.
Among other examples, we will consider the SOGAT
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Id}}
of weak identity types, the GAT
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{MonCat}}
of monoidal categories, and their extensions to strict identity types and strict monoidal categories.

It is possible to develop the functorial semantics SOGATs in
\mathbf{CwF}_{\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}}}
.
Given a SOGAT
\mathcal{T}
, there is a notion of model of
\mathcal{T}
in any
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF
\mathcal{C}
, corresponding to an interpretation of the sorts and operations of
\mathcal{T}
as types and terms of
\mathcal{C}
.
The SOGAT
\mathcal{T}
can be identified with its classifying
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF, also written
\mathcal{T}
.
A model of
\mathcal{T}
in
\mathcal{C}
is then identified with a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF morphism
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{C}
.

By working with SOGATs we know that our results apply to a large class of type theories.
More importantly, many semantic conditions can be expressed more naturally at the level of the classifying
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF
\mathcal{T}
.
Instead of considering arbitrary models of
\mathcal{T}
, it is often sufficient to consider
\mathcal{T}
instead.
The reason combines two ideas.
First, any object of
\mathcal{T}
can be seen as a signature presenting a finitely generated model; this should induce a fully faithful functor
\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{T}}[-]:\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}}
whose essential image consists of the finitely generated models of
\mathcal{T}
, see
4.16
.
Second, any model can be approximated by finitely generated models; more precisely any model admits a cofibrant replacement that is freely generated, and any freely generated model is a filtered colimit of finitely generated models.

For this reason, we focus on the classifying
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF of a SOGAT.
Our main results are stated at this level and do not depend on the semantics of SOGATs.
We still develop some of the semantics in order to provide some additional intuition for our definitions.
However, semantic considerations that would require rather technical proofs are omitted or left to future work.

We also review the notion of external univalence for a SOGATs equipped with homotopy relations.
This notion was introduced by the author in
[
Boc22
]
.
Given a choice of a homotopy relation for every sort of a SOGAT, external univalence expresses the well-behavedness of the homotopy relations; in particular the fact that they are preserved by all operations.
External univalence is also stated at the level of the classifying
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF
\mathcal{T}
, as the existence of some identity type structure (unfortunately both weakly computational and weakly stable) on
\mathcal{T}
.
These identity types have to satisfy a univalence condition, also called saturation condition.
An analogous partial saturation condition will be introduced in
section

6
.

More semantically, external univalence is closely related to left semi-model structures on the category of models, as considered by Kapulkin and Lumsdaine
[
KL16
]
and Isaev
[
Isa18a
]
.
External univalence is also a way to morally view a SOGAT as an
\infty
-type theory (in the sense of Nguyen and Uemura
[
NU22
]
), without using quasicategories.

4.2.
Structure of the types of a SOGAT

Because SOGATs are
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwFs that are presented without equations between types or representable types, their types admit simple normal forms: they are the free closure of the generating types by the operations of a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF.

There is a set
\mathsf{GenTy}_{\mathcal{T}}
of generating types, which can be read from the cellular presentation of
\mathcal{T}
.
For example, the generating types of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Cat}}
are
(\vdash{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{Ob}}}\ \mathsf{type})
,
(x,y:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{Ob}}}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{Hom}}}(x,y)\ \mathsf{type})
and
(x,y:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{Ob}}},f,g:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{Hom}}}(x,y)\vdash{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{EqHom}}}(f,g)\ \mathsf{type})
.

Similarly, there is a set
\mathsf{GenRepTy}_{\mathcal{T}}
of generating representable types, with an inclusion
\mathsf{GenRepTy}_{\mathcal{T}}\hookrightarrow\mathsf{GenTy}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

A
basic type
of
\mathcal{T}
is a type of the form
(\Gamma\vdash{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)\ \mathsf{type})
, where
\sigma:\Gamma\to\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}
for a generating type
(\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}})\in\mathsf{GenTy}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
The
basic representable types
are defined similarly.
A basic non-representable type is a basic type whose generating type is in
(\mathsf{GenTy}_{\mathcal{T}}\backslash\mathsf{GenRepTy}_{\mathcal{T}})
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Any type or representable type in
\mathcal{T}
can be uniquely obtained from the basic non-representable types and basic representable types, and from the operations
\mathbf{1}
-,
\Sigma
-,
\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}}
and the inclusion from representable types into types.
∎

4.5.
Homotopy relations

The data of homotopy relations on a SOGAT equips every sort of the SOGAT with a notion of identification or sameness that is weaker than definitional equality.
It does not presuppose that these relations are well-behaved, for example they could fail to even be symmetric and transitive.
The well-behavedness of homotopy relations is encoded in the notion of external univalence.

Definition 4.17
.

The data of
homotopy relations
on a SOGAT
\mathcal{T}
consists, for every generating sort
{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}

\displaystyle\_\sim_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\_)}\_:(\sigma:\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}})(x,y:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma))\to\mathbb{T},

4.6.
External univalence

The notion of external univalence then captures the well-behavedness of homotopy relations on a SOGAT.

Here a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},\mathsf{Id}_{\mathsf{ws}})
-CwF is a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF equipped with weakly stable identity types satisfying function extensionality.
We have not defined weakly stable identity types, but they are only needed for this definition, which is only used to define the notion of external univalence.

Definition 4.27
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},\mathsf{Id}_{\mathsf{ws}})
-CwF equipped with an internal model of
\mathcal{T}
, i.e. a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF morphism
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{C}
.
We say that
\mathcal{C}
is
saturated
(or that the internal model is
univalent
) if for every generating sort
{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}
, the dependent type

(y:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma))\times(p:x\sim_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)}y)

is contractible over the context
(\sigma:\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}},x:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma))
.

\lrcorner

Note that the above definition also makes sense for
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs.

Definition 4.28
.

We say that
\mathcal{T}
satisfies
external univalence
if
\mathcal{T}
can be equipped with weakly stable identity types that satisfy function extensionality and saturation.

\lrcorner

The following conjecture was made in
[
Boc22
]
.

Conjecture 4.29
.

A SOGAT
\mathcal{T}
satisfies external univalence if and only if the category
\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathsf{cxl}}
, equipped with the previously defined classes of cofibrations, weak equivalences and fibrations, is a left-semi model category.

\lrcorner

Example 4.30
.

The theories
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Cat}}
,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{MonCat}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{StrMonCat}}
of categories, monoidal categories and strict monoidal categories all satisfy external univalence with respect to the homotopy relations defined in
4.18
.

Indeed, the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Cat}}

and

and

This only needs to be checked for

and
\mathbin{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\bm{\otimes}}}}

5.
Equational extensions and conservativity

5.1.
Equational extensions

We now define the notion of equational extension of a SOGAT.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a SOGAT equipped with homotopy relations.

An equational extension of
\mathcal{T}
is an extension
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}_{E}
of SOGATs, where
E
is a family of homotopies in
\mathcal{T}
.
In
\mathcal{T}_{E}
, all the homotopies in
E
become definitional equalities.
Here, a homotopy in
\mathcal{T}
is a tuple
({\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}},\Gamma,\sigma,x,y,p)
, where
{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}\in\mathsf{GenTy}_{\mathcal{T}}
is a generating sort and
(\gamma:\Gamma\vdash p(\gamma):x(\gamma)\sim_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma(\gamma))}y(\gamma))\in\mathcal{T}
is a homotopy between
x
and
y
over
\Gamma
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
E
be a family of homotopies of
\mathcal{T}
; elements of
E
are said to be
marked
.

The
equational extension
of
\mathcal{T}
by
E
is the SOGAT
\mathcal{T}_{E}
defined by extending
\mathcal{T}
by equations
(\gamma:\Gamma\vdash x=y)
and

(\gamma:\Gamma\vdash p(\gamma)=\mathsf{hrefl}_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma(\gamma))}(x(\gamma)))

for every marked homotopy
(\gamma:\Gamma\vdash p(\gamma):x(\gamma)\sim_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma(\gamma))}y(\gamma))\in E
.

\lrcorner

The following are examples of equational extensions of first-order GATs.

Examples 5.2
.

(1)

The extension
\mathcal{T}_{{E\text{-}\mathbf{Cat}}}\to\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Cat}}
is the equational extension obtained by marking
p:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{EqHom}}}(f,g)
over the context
(x,y:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{Ob}}},f,g:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{Hom}}}(x,y),p:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{EqHom}}}(f,g))
.

(2)

The extension
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{MonCat}}\to\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{StrMonCat}}
is the equational extension obtained by marking the associator and unitors in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{MonCat}}
.

(3)

We can also consider the extension
\mathcal{T}_{{E\text{-}\mathbf{Cat}}}\to{(\mathcal{T}_{{E\text{-}\mathbf{Cat}}})}_{E}
obtained by only marking the unitality and associativity laws.
This extension can be better behaved than
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Cat}}
for some applications.
Indeed,
{(\mathcal{T}_{{E\text{-}\mathbf{Cat}}})}_{E}
has decidable equality, while
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Cat}}
does not (e.g. by reduction to the undecidability of the word problem for groups).

\lrcorner

We give some examples of equational extensions of type theories, obtained by marking a family of type equivalences and identifications between terms.

Examples 5.3
.

(1)

For the extensions from weak computation rules to strict computation rules, we mark the computation rules that should be made strict.
For example, in the case of identity types, we mark the family of identifications
\mathsf{J}_{\beta}(P,d):\mathsf{Id}(\mathsf{J}_{A,x}(P,d,\mathsf{refl}),d)
over the context
(A,x,P,d)
.

(2)

When considering the extension from inductive natural numbers to natural numbers with a strictly associative addition, we proceed in in two steps.
First we extend the base theory
\mathcal{T}_{0}
by adding

\mathsf{plus}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbf{N}\to\mathbf{N}

as a new primitive operation, along with some of the equalities that it satisfies, such as

\displaystyle\mathsf{plus}_{0}:\forall x\to\mathsf{plus}(0,x)\simeq x,

\displaystyle\mathsf{plus}_{1}:\forall x\to\mathsf{plus}(x,0)\simeq x,

\displaystyle\mathsf{plus}_{2}:\forall x\ y\ z\to\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y)),z)\simeq\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{plus}(y,z)).

The operation
\mathsf{plus}
is homotopic to the usual inductively defined addition, but not strictly equal to it.
The weak type theory
\mathcal{T}
is then this extended theory.
Because
\mathcal{T}
can be seen as the extension of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
by new elements of contractible sorts, it should be a conservative extension of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
(see
5.6
).

As a second step, we consider the equational extension of
\mathcal{T}
obtained by marking the identifications
\mathsf{plus}_{0}
,
\mathsf{plus}_{1}
,
\mathsf{plus}_{2}
, etc.
Thus the extended theory
\mathcal{T}_{E}
includes the strict equalities
\mathsf{plus}(0,x)=x
,
\mathsf{plus}(x,0)=x
,
\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y),z)=\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{plus}(y,z))
, etc.
It also includes the strict equalities
\mathsf{plus}_{0}=\mathsf{refl}
,
\mathsf{plus}_{1}=\mathsf{refl}
,
\mathsf{plus}_{2}=\mathsf{refl}
, etc.

(3)

Similarly, we can consider the extension of type theory by a composition operation on paths satisfying strictly the laws of a
1
-groupoid.
We start by extending the base theory
\mathcal{T}_{0}
by new elements

\displaystyle\mathsf{trans}:(x\simeq y)\to(y\simeq z)\to(x\simeq z),

\displaystyle\mathsf{inv}:(x\simeq y)\to(y\simeq x),

\displaystyle\mathsf{idl}:\mathsf{trans}(\mathsf{refl},p)\simeq p,

\displaystyle\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{refl}:\mathsf{inv}(\mathsf{refl})\simeq\mathsf{refl},

\displaystyle\mathsf{idr}:\mathsf{trans}(p,\mathsf{refl})\simeq p,

\displaystyle\mathsf{assoc}:\mathsf{trans}(\mathsf{trans}(p,q),r)\simeq\mathsf{trans}(p,\mathsf{trans}(q,r)),

\displaystyle\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{l}:\mathsf{trans}(\mathsf{inv}(p),p)\simeq\mathsf{refl},

\displaystyle\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{r}:\mathsf{trans}(p,\mathsf{inv}(p))\simeq\mathsf{refl},

along with some higher-dimensional identifications that are needed to ensure that this is an extension by new elements of
contractible
sorts.
We can see
\mathsf{idl}
and
\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{refl}
as identifications specifying
\mathsf{trans}
and
\mathsf{inv}
using the universal property of
\mathsf{Id}
-types, so we need additional identifications specifying
\mathsf{idr}
,
\mathsf{assoc}
,
\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{l}
and
\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{r}
:

\displaystyle-:\mathsf{idr}(\mathsf{refl})\simeq\mathsf{idl}(\mathsf{refl}),

\displaystyle-:\mathsf{assoc}(p,q,\mathsf{refl})\simeq\dots,

\displaystyle-:\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{l}(\mathsf{refl})\simeq\dots,

\displaystyle-:\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{r}(\mathsf{refl})\simeq\dots,

where the right-hand sides should be the usual proofs of these laws.

We obtain a theory
\mathcal{T}
that should be a conservative extension of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
.

Secondly, we consider the equational extension of
\mathcal{T}
obtained by marking the identifications
\mathsf{idl}
,
\mathsf{inv}\text{-}\mathsf{refl}
, etc., as well as the additional higher-dimensional identifications.

(4)

To consider the extension of a type theory with a new universe of strict propositions, we would also perform two steps.
As a first step, we introduce a new constant type
\mathsf{SProp}
, along with an equivalence
\mathsf{SProp}\simeq\mathsf{HProp}
with the type
\mathsf{HProp}
of propositions.
We write
F:\mathsf{SProp}\to\mathsf{HProp}
for the associated transport function.

Secondly, we mark the family of identifications

(A:\mathsf{SProp})(a,b:F(A))\to\mathsf{Id}(a,b).

In the resulting strict type theory, the only way to obtain closed elements of
\mathsf{SProp}
is to use the inverse of the equivalence
F
to replace elements of
\mathsf{HProp}
by elements in
\mathsf{SProp}
.

Note that the equational extension that marks instead the family of identifications

(A:\mathsf{HProp})(a,b:A)\to\mathsf{Id}(a,b)

is not a conservative extension in the absence of UIP.
Indeed, as remarked in
[
GCST19
]
, if all propositions are strict propositions, then UIP holds.

(5)

So far we have only marked identification between terms.
We can also mark equivalences between types.

For example, let
\mathcal{T}
be a type theory with a weak Tarski universe closed under identity types.
This means that
\mathcal{T}
extends
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Id}}
with:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:\mathsf{Ty},

\displaystyle\mathsf{El}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathsf{Ty},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{Id}}:(A:\mathcal{U})\to\mathsf{El}(A)\to\mathsf{El}(A)\to\mathcal{U},

\displaystyle\alpha:\forall A\ x\ y\to\mathsf{El}(\widehat{\mathsf{Id}}(A,x,y))\simeq\mathsf{Id}(\mathsf{El}(A),x,y).

We then mark the equivalence
\alpha
over the context
(A,x,y)
.
The resulting theory
\mathcal{T}_{E}
then has a strict Tarski universe.

Note that we have assumed that the same definition of equivalence (say relational equivalences) is used in the specification of
\alpha
and in the specification of the homotopy relations of
\mathcal{T}
in
4.19
.

If
\alpha
were instead a family of say half-adjoint equivalences, we would need to construct the extension in two steps similarly to the previous examples.
In the first step, we add a new family
\alpha^{\prime}
of relational equivalences, along with homotopies between
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
.
In the second step, we consider the equational extension obtained by marking the equivalences
\alpha^{\prime}
.

(6)

As a last example, we can also mark the universal identification
p
over the generic context
(A:\mathsf{Ty},x:A,y:A,p:\mathsf{Id}(x,y))
.
The corresponding strict type theory then includes the equality reflection rule.
Equivalently, one might mark all identifications over arbitrary contexts.

\lrcorner

5.2.
Morita equivalences

We now define the notion of Morita equivalence between SOGATs.
In general, two theories are Morita equivalent when they have equivalent categories of models.
Accordingly, two SOGATs equipped with homotopy relations should be Morita equivalent when they have equivalent
\infty
-categories of models (provided that these
\infty
-categories exist).
Isaev
[
Isa18b
]
has defined a notion of Morita equivalence between type theories and shown that two theories are Morita equivalent if and only if their categories of models are Quillen equivalent (provided that the model or semi-model structures on the categories of models exist).
We give a simpler definition at the level of the classifying
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwFs.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
be a morphism of SOGATs, where
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is equipped with homotopy relations.

Definition 5.4
.

We say that the morphism
\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
is a (split/non-split) Morita equivalence if the map
\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
is a (split/non-split) weak equivalence in
\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}
.

\lrcorner

We have an adjunction between the categories
\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}
and
\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}
of models of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\top}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}

When
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is an equational extension of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, the right adjoint
R
is the forgetful functor that forgets that a model satisfies the additional equations of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.

We have the following characterizations of Morita equivalences.
Condition (
1
) is Isaev’s definition of Morita equivalence
[
Isa18b
]
.
The full proof is left to future work, but it ought to follow from the same ideas and methods as the proof of
4.29
.

Conjecture 5.5
.

Assume that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
satisfies external univalence.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

For every cofibrant model
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, the unit
\eta_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{C}\to R(L(\mathcal{C}))
is a weak equivalence in
\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}
.

(2)

For every freely generated model
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, the unit
\eta_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{C}\to R(L(\mathcal{C}))
is a weak equivalence in
\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}
.

(3)

For every finitely generated model
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, the unit
\eta_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{C}\to R(L(\mathcal{C}))
is a weak equivalence in
\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}
.

(4)

The SOGAT morphism
\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
is a Morita equivalence.

Proof sketch.

The fact that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
satisfies external univalence is needed to ensure that weak equivalences in
\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}
satisfy some closure conditions (some directions of the
2
-out-of-
3
property and closure under retracts).

The equivalence between (
1
) and (
2
) follows from the fact that every cofibrant model is a retract of a freely generated model.

The equivalence between (
2
) and (
3
) follows from the fact that every
I
-cellular model is a filtered colimit of finitely generated models (because
\mathcal{T}
is a finitary theory).

The equivalence between (
3
) and (
4
) follows from
4.16
.
∎

5.3.
Extensions by contractible sorts

In our examples of equational extensions, we have often considered compositions
\mathcal{T}_{0}\to\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}_{E}
of extensions, where
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}_{E}
is an equational extension and
\mathcal{T}_{0}\to\mathcal{T}
is the extension of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
by new elements of contractible sorts.

The main focus of this paper is the conservativity of the extension
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}_{E}
, but we also need a way to prove that
\mathcal{T}_{0}\to\mathcal{T}
is a conservative extension.
In general, we would need a proof of the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a cofibrant SOGAT that satisfies external univalence.
Let
(\Gamma\vdash Y\ \mathsf{type})\in\mathcal{T}
be a sort of
\mathcal{T}
that is contractible (with respect to the weakly stable identity types arising from external univalence).
Then the extension
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}[\gamma:\Gamma\vdash\bm{y}(\gamma):Y(\gamma)]
is a Morita equivalence, and
\mathcal{T}[\gamma:\Gamma\vdash\bm{y}(\gamma):Y(\gamma)]
also satifies external univalence (with the same homotopy relations as
\mathcal{T}
).

\lrcorner

Proving
5.6
or any special case would require some more tools (including
4.29
), so we leave any proof to future work.

It should not too difficult to prove the special case of
5.6
for
closed
contractible sorts, using the fact that
\mathcal{T}[\bm{y}:Y]
is isomorphic to the contextual slice
(\mathcal{T}\sslash 1_{\mathcal{T}}.Y)
, that is the contextual core (in
\mathbf{CwF}_{\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}}}
) of the slice model
(\mathcal{T}/1_{\mathcal{T}}.Y)
.

The general case is more complicated.
However, if we consider
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs instead of SOGATs with external univalence (which are
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},\mathsf{Id}_{ws})
-CwFs), then the analogous result follows from
4.29
, applied to the SOGAT
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}}}
that classifies
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs.
This means that
5.6
should follow from a coherence theorem providing a way to strictify
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},\mathsf{Id}_{ws})
-CwFs into
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs.
This strictification is likely only possible for
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},\mathsf{Id}_{ws})
-CwFs which satisfy some cofibrancy condition, e.g. which are cofibrant in
\mathbf{CwF}_{\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}}}
.

6.
Partial saturation

This section introduces partially saturated
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs with respect to a family
E
of homotopies, and the initial such CwF
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}
.
The main theorem of the paper reduces the conservativity of the equational extension
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}_{E}
to properties of
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}
.

6.1.
Definitions

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwF equipped with an internal model of
\mathcal{T}
.
For every generating sort
{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}\in\mathsf{GenTy}_{\mathcal{T}}
, we have a comparison map going from outer identifications (
\simeq
) to homotopies (
\sim
), defined by sending the outer reflexivity
\mathsf{refl}
to the constant homotopy
\mathsf{hrefl}
.

\displaystyle{\mathsf{id}\text{-}\mathsf{to}\text{-}\mathsf{hpty}}_{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}:(x\simeq_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)}y)\to(x\sim_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)}y),

\displaystyle{\mathsf{id}\text{-}\mathsf{to}\text{-}\mathsf{hpty}}_{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\mathsf{refl})\simeq\mathsf{hrefl}_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}}.

The CwF
\mathcal{C}
is
saturated
in the sense of
4.27
exactly when these maps are equivalences for every generating sort.
Accordingly, we will say that
\mathcal{C}
is partially saturated with respect to a family of homotopies when the restriction of
{\mathsf{id}\text{-}\mathsf{to}\text{-}\mathsf{hpty}}_{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}
to these homotopies are all equivalences.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwF equipped with an internal model of
\mathcal{T}
, i.e. a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF morphism
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{C}
.
We say that
\mathcal{C}
is
partially saturated
with respect to
E
if for every homotopy
(\gamma:\Gamma\vdash p(\gamma):x(\gamma)\sim_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)}y(\gamma))\in E
, the following contractibility condition holds:

\gamma:\Gamma\vdash\mathrm{isContr}((q:x(\gamma)\simeq_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)}y(\gamma))\times({\mathsf{id}\text{-}\mathsf{to}\text{-}\mathsf{hpty}}_{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(q(\gamma))\simeq p(\gamma))).

In that case, we have the following center of contraction:

\displaystyle\gamma:\Gamma\vdash\widehat{p}(\gamma):x(\gamma)\simeq_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)}y(\gamma),

\displaystyle\gamma:\Gamma\vdash\widetilde{p}(\gamma):{\mathsf{id}\text{-}\mathsf{to}\text{-}\mathsf{hpty}}_{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\widehat{p}(\gamma))\simeq p(\gamma).

\lrcorner

In particular,
\mathcal{C}
is saturated exactly when it is partially saturated with respect to all generic homotopies:

\{(\sigma:\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}},x:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma),y:{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma),p:x\sim_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)}y\vdash p:x\sim_{{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}(\sigma)}y)\mid{\color[rgb]{0.9492,0.375,0.207}{\mathbf{S}}}\in\mathsf{GenTy}_{\mathcal{T}}\},

or equivalently with respect to all homotopies.

We write
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}
for the initial object among
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs equipped with an internal model of
\mathcal{T}
that is partially saturated with respect to
E
.
The superscript
-^{\infty}
indicates that
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}
is morally an
\infty
-category, up to the conjectured correspondence between
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwF and representable map
\infty
-categories.
The subscript
-_{{\widehat{E}}}
indicates that
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}
includes lifts of every marked homotopy in
E
.

We write
\mathcal{T}^{1}_{E}
for the initial object among
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},\mathsf{Eq})
-CwFs equipped with an internal model of
\mathcal{T}_{E}
.
The superscript
-^{1}
now indicates that
\mathcal{T}^{1}_{E}
is morally a representable map
1
-category.
Note that
\mathcal{T}^{1}_{E}
can be seen as a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs equipped with an internal model of
\mathcal{T}
.
It is then saturated with respect to
E
, because the equations of
E
hold definitionally in
\mathcal{T}_{E}
.
By the universal property of
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}
, we therefore have a morphism
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}\to\mathcal{T}^{1}_{E}
, such that the following square commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}_{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}}

7.
Quotients of models of first-order GATs

In this section we discuss congruences over models of (first-order) GATs and quotients of such congruences.
In this paper, we only need applications to the GAT of
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwFs, but it is easier to present them in full generality.

For ordinary algebraic theories, congruences and quotients are very well-behaved.
A congruence simply consists of equivalence relations for every sort, preserving all operations in the theory.
The quotient of a model is computed by taking the quotient of every sort by the specified equivalence relation.

For generalized algebraic theories, matters are more complicated, because of the presence of dependent sorts.
We still have a general notion of congruence, but quotients can no longer be computed sortwise.
In other words, the quotient inclusion is not necessarily a
trivial fibration
.

For example, we can consider the quotient of the category

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

by the congruence that identifies
b
and
c
.
In the quotient, it is possible to compose
f
and
g
, but there is no morphism corresponding to their composition in the original category.
The quotient inclusion functor is not full.

However, by adding additional fibration condition to the definition of congruence, it becomes possible to compute the quotient pointwise.
The quotient inclusion of any “fibrant” congruence is then a non-split trivial fibration.
Furthermore, every non-split trivial fibration arises (up to isomorphism) as the quotient of a fibrant congruence.

Q(B)\to Q(A)\times_{Q(X)}Q(Y)
is an isomorphism.

We define the inverse

using the universal properties of the quotients.
Take a pair

in

.
We have
[f(a)]=[p(y)]
, i.e.
f(a)\sim p(y)
in the setoid
X
.
Since

is a fibration, we can find

such that
p(y)=f(a)
and
y^{\prime}\sim y
. We send the pair
([a],[y])
to the element
[(a,y^{\prime})]
of
Q(B)
.
It can be shown that element does not depend on the choices of
a
,
y
and
y^{\prime}
, thus the candidate inverse is well-defined.

It is then straightforward to show that the defined map is an inverse.
∎

8.1.
Equivalences and dependent equivalences

For this proof we need to consider some equivalences over the contexts and types of
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
.
It is most convenient to work with relational equivalences, also known as one-to-one correspondences.
A relational equivalence between two types
A
and
B
is a type-valued relation that is functional in both directions.
The advantage of this definition is that is easy to show that the standard type structures (
\mathbf{1}
,
\Sigma
and
\Pi
) preserve equivalences.

Let
\mathbb{C}
be a family equipped with
\Sigma
-types and weak identity types.

Definition 8.2
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}:\mathbb{C}
be two types.
An
equivalence

\alpha:A_{1}\simeq A_{2}
is a type-valued relation

\alpha:A_{1}\to A_{2}\to\mathbb{C}

satisfying the following two contractibility conditions

\displaystyle(a_{1}:A_{1})\to\mathrm{isContr}((a_{2}:A_{2})\times\alpha(a_{1},a_{2})),

\displaystyle(a_{2}:A_{2})\to\mathrm{isContr}((a_{1}:A_{1})\times\alpha(a_{1},a_{2})).

\lrcorner

The elements of
\alpha(a_{1},a_{2})
will be called identifications between
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.

Definition 8.3
.

Let
B_{1}:A_{1}\to\mathbb{C}
and
B_{2}:A_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be two dependent types.
A
dependent equivalence

\beta:B_{1}\simeq_{\alpha}B_{2}
over an equivalence
\alpha:A_{1}\simeq A_{2}
is a family

\beta:\forall a_{1}\ a_{2}\to\alpha(a_{1},a_{2})\to B_{1}(a_{1})\simeq B_{2}(a_{2})

of equivalences.

\lrcorner

Definition 8.4
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}:A_{1}\simeq A_{2}
be two parallel equivalences.
A
homotopy

H:\alpha_{1}\simeq\alpha_{2}
between
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
is a family

H:\forall a_{1}\ a_{2}\to\alpha_{1}(a_{1},a_{2})\simeq\alpha_{2}(a_{1},a_{2})

of equivalences.

\lrcorner

Definition 8.5
.

Let
\beta_{1}:B_{1}\simeq_{\alpha_{1}}B_{2}
and
\beta_{2}:B_{1}\simeq_{\alpha_{2}}B_{2}
be two parallel dependent equivalences, and
H:\alpha_{1}\simeq\alpha_{2}
be a homotopy between
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
.
A
dependent homotopy

G
between
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
is a family

\displaystyle G:\forall a_{1}\ a_{2}\ (a_{e1}:\alpha_{1}(a_{1},a_{2}))\ (a_{e2}:\alpha_{2}(a_{1},a_{2}))\ (a_{ee}:H(a_{e1},a_{e2}))

\displaystyle\quad\to\forall b_{1}\ b_{2}\to\beta_{1}(a_{e1},b_{1},b_{2})\simeq\beta_{2}(a_{e2},b_{1},b_{2})

of equivalences.

\lrcorner

These notions come with various composition operations, including identity equivalences, inverse equivalences, etc.

8.2.
Construction of the fibrant congruence

We now assume that
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}
is merely
0
-truncated relatively to
K:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{\infty}_{{\widehat{E}}}
.

We want to construct a quotient of
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}
by a fibrant congruence.
This congruence should identify terms of
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}
when there is an identification between them in
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
, and it should identify types of
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}
when they are equivalent in
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
.
Since
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
is
0
-truncated relatively to
K
, the choice of identifications won’t matter.
However, there may be multiple parallel equivalences in
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
.
Because of that, we need to consider a restricted class of equivalences, built from transport over identifications.
This is analogous to the partial families
\mathsf{co}_{\Gamma,\Delta}
and
\mathsf{ty}_{\Gamma,\Delta,\sigma,\tau}
of propositional isomorphisms that are used in Hofmann’s conservativity proof
[
Hof95
]
.

Construction 8.6
.

We define the following set-valued relations and families of equivalences by induction over the structure of contexts and types of
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}
.

\displaystyle({\approx}):\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}\to\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}\to\mathrm{Set},

\displaystyle({\approx}):(\Gamma_{1}\approx\Gamma_{2})\to\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}.\mathsf{Ty}\to\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}.\mathsf{Ty}\to\mathrm{Set},

\displaystyle({\approx})^{\mathsf{rep}}:(\Gamma_{1}\approx\Gamma_{2})\to\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}.\mathsf{Ty}_{\mathsf{rep}}\to\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}.\mathsf{Ty}_{\mathsf{rep}}\to\mathrm{Set},

\displaystyle\beta:(p:\Gamma_{1}\approx\Gamma_{2})\to(A\approx_{p}B)\to(K(A)\simeq_{\alpha_{p}}K(B)),

\displaystyle\beta^{\mathsf{rep}}:(p:\Gamma_{1}\approx\Gamma_{2})\to(A\approx^{\mathsf{rep}}_{p}B)\to(K(A)\simeq_{\alpha_{p}}K(B)).

Given

9.
Future directions

9.1.
Alternative proofs of theorem 8.1

The proof of
8.1
is very close to Hofmann’s original proof of the conservativity of extensional type theory over intensional type theory.
We discuss potential ways to obtain simpler proofs of
8.1
.

Local universes

In presence of enough universes in
\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
, there would be a simpler way to define the relations of the congruence
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}}
.
Indeed, we could say that
(\Gamma\sim\Delta)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}}
when there merely exists
p:\mathsf{code}(K(\Gamma))\simeq_{\mathcal{U}}\mathsf{code}(K(\Delta))
, where
\mathsf{code}(-)
gives the code of a context or type in the universe.

In the absence of universe, we can try to use local universes.
A local universe in
\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
is a type
(V\vdash E\ \mathsf{type})\in\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
.
By induction on the shape of contexts and types of
\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}
, we would define for every
\Gamma\in\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}
a local universe
(V_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma})
and an element
(\chi_{\Gamma}:V_{\Gamma})\in\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
such that
K(\Gamma)=E_{\Gamma}[\chi_{\Gamma}]
.
We could then say that
(\Gamma\sim\Delta)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}}
when
(V_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma})=(V_{\Delta},E_{\Delta})
and there merely exists
p:\chi_{\Gamma}\simeq_{V_{\Gamma}}\chi_{\Delta}
.

Unfortunately, it is not possible to define these local universes: we would need arbitrary
\Pi
-types in
\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
, but we only have
\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}}
-types.
If we could conservatively embed
\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
into a
(\Sigma,\Pi,{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwF, this method would be applicable.

Higher-order congruences

Another possible simplification would be obtained by working internally to
\mathbf{Psh}(\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}})
instead of externally.

Consider the following external diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}_{E}^{1}\hbox to0.0pt{\ .\hss}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}

Following ideas from
[
BKS23
]
, it is possible to internalize this diagram internally to
\mathbf{Psh}(\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}})
as follows:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Tele}_{\mathbb{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}}}

Here

The internal versions of
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}_{E}
should be constant over , i.e. defined by restriction of their external counterparts over the unique functor
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}\to 1_{\mathbf{Cat}}

It should then be possible to define a “higher-order congruence” over
\mathbb{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}
.
The advantage of this approach is that higher-order congruences only consist of equivalence relations on types and terms, not on contexts and substitutions;
we would avoid the parts of the current proof that have to deal with contexts and substitutions.

This higher-order congruence should induce a fibrant “first-order” congruence over the

This proof strategy requires some additional tools for axiomatizing models, morphisms of models and their properties in the internal language of presheaf toposes.

\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{T}}[-]:\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathsf{cxl}}

The theory

suffices.

9.3.
Coherence problems and the Yoneda embedding

Coherence problems arise from the use of
(\Sigma,{\mathsf{Id}})
- or
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
- CwFs to morally represent structured
\infty
-categories.
In particular, the notion of external univalence is expressed using weakly stable identity types.
Thus, in order to rely on external univalence, the two choices are either to only use weakly stable identity types, or to prove coherence theorem that allow for the strictification of the weakly stable identity types.

The Yoneda embedding is one of the most important constructions of category theory.
For applications, it seems very important to access the
\infty
-categorical Yoneda embedding at the level of
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs.
If
\mathcal{C}
is a cofibrant
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwF, it should be possible to find a model
\mathcal{D}
of HoTT (corresponding to
\infty
-presheaves over
\mathcal{C}
) and a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwF morphism
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
that is essentially surjective on terms (corresponding to the fully faithful Yoneda embedding).

We hope that both of these issues will be solved in the future using cubical methods, thanks to the fact that the cubical model of HoTT is constructive (using a suitable cube category).
If
\mathcal{C}
is any
1
-category with a terminal object, then the category
[\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{op}},\mathbf{cSet}]
of cubical presheaves is equipped with a model of HoTT.
Indeed, we can construct the cubical set model internally to
\mathbf{Psh}(\mathcal{C})
.
This internal construction corresponds externally to a functor
\mathcal{C}\to\mathbf{Mod}_{\mathsf{HoTT}}^{\mathsf{op}}
.
By composing this functor with
1_{\mathcal{C}}:1_{\mathbf{Cat}}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{op}}
, we obtain an external model of HoTT; it can be shown that its underlying category is the category
[\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{op}},\mathbf{cSet}]
of cubical presheaves over
\mathcal{C}
.
When
\mathcal{C}
is a
(\Sigma,{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwF or a
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}})
-CwF, we then want to compare
\mathcal{C}
with this model
[\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{op}},\mathbf{cSet}]
.
It should then suffice to compare, internally to
\mathbf{Psh}(\mathcal{C})
, the CwF
\mathrm{Set}_{\mathsf{rf}}
of sets and Reedy-fibrant types with the cubical set model
\mathsf{cSet}
.
This problem seems tractable (using, for example, ideas from two-level type theory (2LTT) and the development of Reedy-fibrant diagrams in 2LTT
[
ACKS17
]
).

9.4.
Proving
0
-truncatedness from normalization

In order to apply our methods, we would need to be able to prove the (relative)
0
-truncatedness of
\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
in concrete examples.
We believe that such results can be obtained by lifting normalization proofs from
\mathcal{T}_{E}
to
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
.
These normalization results should even provide direct proofs of the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}\to\mathcal{T}_{E}
is a
split
trivial fibration, without going through any quotient.

Strict normalization for
\mathcal{T}_{E}
states that every term of
\mathcal{T}_{E}
admits a unique normal form.
Normalization is usually proven for the syntax of type theory, that is for the initial model
\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{T}_{E}}
.
Proving normalization for
\mathcal{T}_{E}
instead is akin to proving normalization for all freely generated models of
\mathcal{T}_{E}
at once.
For most theories, proving normalization for
\mathcal{T}_{E}
is not more complicated than proving normalization for
\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{T}_{E}}
.

Homotopy normalization for
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
should then state that every term of
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
admits a contractible space of normal forms (over contexts in the image of
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
).
Stated appropriately, this is sufficient to obtain the
0
-truncatedness of
\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{E}}^{\infty}
.
Proving homotopy normalization for
\mathcal{T}_{{\widehat{E}}}^{\infty}
is however much more complicated than proving strict normalization.
Indeed, where strict normalization proofs for type theory make use of set-valued logical relations, homotopy normalization proofs should use space-valued logical relations.

For this we essentially need to use
\infty
-presheaves over some
\infty
-categories, and the interpretation of HoTT into
\infty
-toposes.
But because we have used
(\Sigma,{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs or
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs instead of other models of
\infty
-categories, we cannot directly use the known interpretation of HoTT into
\infty
-toposes.
Hopefully, the cubical methods mentioned in
section

9.3
can be used instead.

We note that Uemura has recently written a proof of normalization and
0
-truncatedness for the initial models of
\infty
-type theories
[
Uem22
]
.
Only a small
\infty
-type theory with only
\Pi
-types has been considered so far, but the methods should also work for other
\infty
-type theories.
It does not seem realistic to directly use these results for our purposes (although it would be possible in principle, given a precise comparison between
(\Sigma,\Pi_{\mathsf{rep}},{\mathsf{Id}})
-CwFs and
\infty
-type theories (representable map
\infty
-categories)).
But the fact that
0
-truncatedness results can be obtained for
\infty
-type theories indicates that similar methods should also yield the
0
-truncatedness results we need.
