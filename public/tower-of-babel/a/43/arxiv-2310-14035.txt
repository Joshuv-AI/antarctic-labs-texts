Topological phases induced by charge fluctuations in Majorana wires Preprint: APS/123-QED
M. S. Shustin, S. V. Aksenov, I. S. Burmistrov

Abstract
One of the problems concerning topological phases in solid-state systems which still remains urgent is an issue of many-body effects. In this study we address it within perturbative theory framework by considering topological phase transitions related to charge correlations in the extended Kitaev chain model that belongs to the BDI symmetry class. Obtained corrections to a zero-frequency quasiparticle Green’s function allow to separate the mean-field and fluctuation contributions to a total winding number. As a result, the phase transitions caused solely by the latter are unveiled. We thoroughly analyze the mechanism of such transitions in terms of fluctuation-induced nodal points and spectrum renormalization. Additionally, features of other quasiparticle properties such as effective mass and damping are discussed in the context of topological phase transitions.



I
Introduction

In the last decade, hybrid superconducting (SC) nanowires have been intensively studied. One of the driving forces here is an idea to utilize these structures as a platform for topological quantum computations based on the manipulation of Majorana modes. It was predicted that the nonlocal Majorana states being a hallmark of topologically nontrivial phase might emerge in wires with s-wave SC pairing, spin-orbit coupling and the Zeeman splitting
Lutchyn et al. 2010
;
Oreg et al. 2010
. To detect Majoranas in practice, a semiconducting InSb or InAs wire is used as a core that is covered by a parent SC layer, for example, Al. Such a shell becomes a donor of Cooper pairs in the semiconducting core inducing superconductivity there due to the proximity effect
Chang et al. 2015
. The latter can be also responsible for the Zeeman splitting if the core is additionally partly coated by a ferromagnetic EuS layer (see, for example
Vaitiekenas et al. 2021
;
Maiani et al. 2021
;
Vaitiekenas et al. 2022
). However, an external magnetic field is an alternative option for this purpose
Mourik et al. 2012
;
Nichele et al. 2017
;
Aghaee et al. 2022
.

As a result, the electronic subsystem of the hybrid structure has properties of a spin-polarized superconducting nanowire (SW) with spin-orbit interaction. From the symmetry point of view
Zirnbauer 1997
;
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
;
Heinzner et al. 2005
, the Hamiltonian modeling such a system belongs to the BDI class, which makes it possible to implement Majorana modes and even many pairs of them
Schnyder et al. 2008
;
Kitaev 2009
;
Turner et al. 2011
. An experimental study of such a system led to the discovery of a stable zero-biased peak, which indirectly indicates the Majorana mode presence. However, the obtained experimental data are interpreted ambiguously and are the subject of discussions (see, for example,
Moore et al. 2018
;
Reeg et al. 2018
;
Prada et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2021
).

There are experimental data indicating that Coulomb interaction in InAs can be controlled by gate electrodes and becomes significant
Sato et al. 2019
. In particular, for an ensemble of InAs nanowires, the electron transport demonstrated features which can be described in terms of the Luttinger liquid with a constant
K
corresponding to strong electron correlation regime.

Noticeable progress has been made in the study of charge-charge interaction in Majorana wires. In specific cases of the Kitaev model
Kitaev 2001
with the local repulsion,
U
, the twofold degenerate ground states with opposite parity and many-body generalization of the Majorana operators were obtained analytically
Katsura et al. 2015
;
Miao et al. 2017
.
Interestingly, although the interaction
suppresses the bulk gap in both SW and Kitaev models, the nontrivial phase can be achieved at the lower Zeeman energies and wider range of the chemical potential values (the
U
-proportional renormalizations leading to such effects can be easily seen already in the mean-field approximation)
Gangadharaiah et al. 2011
;
Stoudenmire et al. 2011
;
Thomale et al. 2013
. It was shown that in some models the on-site repulsion itself can be vital for the Majorana modes to emerge
Haim et al. 2014
;
Thakurathi et al. 2018
.

When the many-body effects are important, Bloch wave functions cannot be used to characterize the spectral and topological characteristics of the system. A natural solution is to apply a Green’s function approach
Volovik 1991
. In particular, topological invariant can be expressed via the Green’s function matrix at zero frequency,
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}(\omega=0)
, with the transitions between phases defined by its zeros and poles
Volovik 2010
;
Silaev and Volovik 2010
;
Gurarie 2011
;
Wang and Zhang 2012
;
Manmana et al. 2012
;
Yoshida et al. 2014
;
Blason and Fabrizio 2023
. Furthermore, one can interpret the topological invariant as a winding number of a complex function around the origin (an analogue of the Anderson pseudo vector
Anderson 1958
) which real and imaginary parts are the off-diagonal and diagonal components of
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}(\omega=0)
, respectively
Li and Han 2018
;
Sugimoto 2023
.

As demonstrated in recently studies
Choi and Trauzettel 2023
;
Gavensky et al. 2023
, it is possible to decompose the winding number into some bare term corresponding to noninteracting quasiparticles and a part related to quasiparticle’s hybridization or interactions.
Then, one can observe topological phase transitions governed solely by the quasiparticle’s interplay.
In this work we extract the contribution to the topological invariant related to the nearest-neighbour Coulomb repulsion in the extended Kitaev model
Vodola et al. 2014
;
Patrick et al. 2017
;
Alecce and Dell’Anna 2017
beyond the mean-field regime. The corrections to the self-energy obtained up to the second order in perturbation theory make it possible to analyze thoroughly the quasiparticle properties and the mechanism behind the fluctuation-induced phase transitions.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
the extended Kitaev model is derived from an SW model in a highly spin-polarized+strongly-correlated regime and its topological phases are considered in the mean-field approximation. In Sec.
III
we develop the perturbation theory for the Matsubara normal and anomalous Green’s functions up to second order in the intersite repulsive interaction and describe the corresponding effects on quasiparticle properties. In Section
IV
we show that the topological invariant can be represented as a sum of the mean-field and fluctuation terms. Next, we elucidate in detail how this residual interaction gives rise to the topological phase transitions.
We end the paper with conclusions (Sec.
V
). Some details of computations are delegated to Appendices. Throughout the paper we use unites with
k_{B}{=}\hbar{=}1
.

II
Model and Mean-Field Treatment

II.1
Formulation of the model

We write the SW Hamiltonian describing the electronic system of the hybrid structure in the following form
Aksenov et al. 2020
;
Shustin and Aksenov 2021

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{W}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{f\sigma}\Big(\xi_{\sigma}a_{f\sigma}^{{\dagger}}a_{f\sigma}+Un_{f\sigma}n_{f\bar{\sigma}}-t_{0}a_{f\sigma}^{{\dagger}}a_{f+1\sigma}

(1)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\alpha\eta_{\sigma}a_{f\sigma}^{{\dagger}}a_{f+1\bar{\sigma}}+2\Delta_{s}\eta_{\sigma}a_{f\sigma}a_{f\bar{\sigma}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\Delta_{1s}\eta_{\sigma}\left(a_{f\sigma}a_{f+1\bar{\sigma}}+a_{f+1\sigma}a_{f\bar{\sigma}}\right)+\mathrm{h.c.}\Big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle V_{0}\sum_{f\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}n_{f\sigma}\,n_{f+1,\sigma^{\prime}}.

Here the terms in the first line represent the 1D Hubbard model with a hopping amplitude
t_{0}/2
, a spin-dependent single-site energy
\xi_{\sigma}=-\mu+\eta_{\sigma}h
and a Hubbard repulsive interaction
U
. The Zeeman energy related to the external magnetic field or induced magnetism from the EuS layer is denoted by
h
. The second line of the Hamiltonian contains the terms associated with the spin-orbit coupling with the magnitude
\alpha/2
. The proximity-induced
s
-wave superconducting pairing has amplitudes
\Delta_{s}
and
\Delta_{1s}
. The last term in Eq. (
1
) describes the intersite,
V_{0}
, Coulomb repulsion of electrons.
a_{f\sigma}(a^{+}_{f\sigma})
is an annihilation (creation) operator of a fermion at a site
f
and spin projection
\sigma={\uparrow,\downarrow}
,
\eta_{\uparrow,\downarrow}=\pm 1
. The electron number operator at the
f
-th site is
{n}_{f\sigma}=a^{+}_{f\sigma}a_{f\sigma}
. Bearing in mind
large
g
-factors in InAs, InSb (
g\simeq 50
)
Mourik et al. 2012
;
Lutchyn et al. 2018
and, consequently, strong Zeeman splitting, in the following it is reasonable to assume that
2|h|\gg|t_{0}|,\,|\alpha|,\,|\Delta_{s}|,\,|\Delta_{1s}|
.

We will focus on the regime of low electron densities and high spin polarization motivated by the available experimental results
Vaitiekenas et al. 2022
;
Sato et al. 2019
. Hence, let us project the Hamiltonian (
1
) onto the spin-polarized lower Hubbard band by an operator

P=\prod_{f}\left(X^{00}_{f}+X^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{f}\right).

The above expression is written using the Hubbard operators acting on single-site states,
X_{f}^{nm}=|f,\,n\rangle\langle f,\,m|
. In our case there are four states on the site
f
: a state
|f,0\rangle
with no electrons, two states
|f,\sigma\rangle
describing one electron with the spin
\sigma
and a state
|f,2\rangle
with two electrons possessing opposite spin projections.

The restriction of Hubbard operators to the low-energy subspace allows to express
\mathcal{H}_{W}
in terms of spinless fermion operators:

\displaystyle PX_{f}^{0\uparrow}P=c_{f},~~PX_{f}^{\uparrow\uparrow}P=c^{+}_{f}c_{f}=n_{f}.

(2)

As a result, the desired Hamiltonian in terms of the spinless fermions is given as (see
Appendix
A
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=

\displaystyle\sum_{f=1}^{N}\left(\,\epsilon-\mu\,\right)\,n_{f}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{f=1}^{N-1}\Big(t_{1}\,c^{+}_{f}c_{f+1}+\Delta_{1}\,c^{+}_{f}c^{+}_{f+1}-{V}\,n_{f}n_{f+1}+\textrm{h.c.}\,\Big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{f=2}^{N-1}\Big(\,c^{+}_{f+1}\,\hat{t}_{2}\,c_{f-1}+c^{+}_{f-1}\,\hat{\Delta}_{2}\,c^{+}_{f+1}+\textrm{h.c.}\,\Big),

(3)

where renormalized hoppings and SC pairings are defined as

\hat{t}_{2}=t_{2}+F\,n_{f},~~\hat{\Delta}_{2}=\Delta_{2}-G\,n_{f}.

(4)

The Hamiltonian (
3
) is a generalization of the Kitaev chain Hamiltonian
Kitaev 2001
;
Lutchyn et al. 2010
for the case of long-range hoppings and p-type pairings. The parameters of low-energy model (
3
) depend on the parameters of the original one (
1
), see Eqs. (
78
)-(
81
). In the regime
1/|\mu|\sim|1/h|\sim 1/\left(U+2|\mu|\right)\ll\max|\{t_{0}\,,\,\alpha\,,\,\Delta_{s}\,,\,\Delta_{1s}\}|
the effective model (
3
) describes the low-energy properties of (
1
) with a good accuracy. However, in this study we will consider the parameters of (
3
) as independent ones.
This is useful since the effective Hamiltonian similar to (
3
) can be derived from other microscopic models such as the
XY
spin-
1/2
chain with next-nearest-neighbour frustration
Bahovadinov et al. 2022
.

The three-center interactions,
\mathcal{H}_{3}
,
last line in Eq. (
3
), with amplitudes
F
and
G
describe charge-correlated hoppings and SC pairings in the second coordination sphere. The physical meaning of these terms is discussed in Appendix
A
, see Eq. (
82
).
Note that the effective interactions are weak,
F,~G\ll 1
, and vanish in the absence of Hubbard repulsion,
U=0
. In that follows, we will treat these interactions at the mean-field level, and the charge fluctuations will be described only by the term
\sim V
. Thus, the Hamiltonian studied further belongs to the BDI symmetry class
Karcher et al. 2019
and is described by the
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
topological classification
Fidkowski and Kitaev 2011
.

II.2
Mean-field approximation

At first let us consider the model (
3
) in the mean-field approximation. Using the Hartree-Fock decoupling

\displaystyle c^{+}_{n}c^{+}_{m}c_{f}c_{g}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\langle c^{+}_{n}c^{+}_{m}\rangle c_{f}c_{g}+\langle c_{f}c_{g}\rangle c^{+}_{n}c^{+}_{m}-\langle c^{+}_{n}c_{f}\rangle c^{+}_{m}c_{g}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\langle c^{+}_{m}c_{g}\rangle c^{+}_{n}c_{f}+\langle c^{+}_{n}c_{g}\rangle c^{+}_{m}c_{f}+\langle c^{+}_{m}c_{f}\rangle c^{+}_{n}c_{g},

and introducing the Fermi operators in the momentum space

c_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{f}c_{f}\,e^{-ikf},

(5)

one can obtain the following mean-field Hamiltonian in
k
-space from the expression (
3
)
Vodola et al. 2014
;
Patrick et al. 2017
;
Alecce and Dell’Anna 2017
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{mf}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left[\tilde{\xi}_{k}\,c^{+}_{k}\,c_{k}+\left(\frac{i}{2}\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\,c_{k}^{+}\,c_{-k}^{+}-\frac{i}{2}\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\,c_{-k}\,c_{k}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\tilde{\xi}_{k}

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\mu}-\tilde{t}_{1}\cos k-\tilde{t}_{2}\cos 2k,

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Delta}_{1}\sin k+\tilde{\Delta}_{2}\sin 2k.

(6)

Here the renormalized parameters are given as follows

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}=\mu-2V\,n-2F\,\textrm{Re}(\langle c^{+}_{f}c_{f+2}\rangle)-2G\,\textrm{Re}\langle c_{f}c_{f+2}\rangle,

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{1}=t_{1}+2V\,\langle c^{+}_{f}c_{f+1}\rangle+4F\,\langle c^{+}_{f}c_{f+1}\rangle+4G\,\textrm{Re}\langle c_{f}c_{f+1}\rangle,

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{1}=\Delta_{1}+2V\,\langle c_{f}c_{f+1}\rangle-4F\,\langle c_{f}c_{f+1}\rangle-4G\,\textrm{Re}\langle c^{+}_{f}c_{f+1}\rangle,

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{2}=t_{2}-2F\,n,~~\tilde{\Delta}_{2}=\Delta_{2}-G\,n,

(7)

and depend on a fermion concentration
n
as well as on normal and anomalous correlation functions,

\displaystyle n=\langle{n}_{f}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\xi_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\,dk\;,

\displaystyle N_{l}=\langle c^{+}_{f}c_{f+l}\rangle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\textrm{Re}{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\xi_{k}\,e^{ikl}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\,dk}\;,

\displaystyle A_{l}=\langle c_{f}c_{f+l}\rangle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\textrm{Im}{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\Delta_{k}\,e^{ikl}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\,dk}.

(8)

We emphasize that they should be calculated self-consistently.

The diagonalization of the Hamiltonian can be carried out using the unitary transformation in the Fock space
Haken 1976
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}
the Fock space in which the operators
c_{k}
act. Consider the unitary rotation to the space
\mathcal{A}
,

\mathcal{U}\,:\,\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}\,,~~~~~\mathcal{U}^{+}\,:\,\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{C},

(9)

in such a way that the Hamiltonian (
6
) has a diagonal form in the basis
\mathcal{A}
. For this purpose, we define the annihilation operators
\alpha_{k}
in the space
\mathcal{A}
using the relation

\alpha_{k}\,\mathcal{U}\,|\,\Phi\,\rangle=\mathcal{U}\,c_{k}\,|\,\Phi\,\rangle,~~\mathcal{U}^{+}\,\alpha_{k}\,|\,\Psi\,\rangle=c_{k}\,\mathcal{U}^{+}\,|\,\Psi\,\rangle,

(10)

where
|\,\Phi\,\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
and
|\,\Psi\,\rangle\in\mathcal{A}
. As a result, it becomes possible to define the structure of Fock space in
\mathcal{A}
.

We set unitary operators
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{U}^{+}
as follows

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=

\displaystyle\prod_{0\leq k\leq\pi}\exp\left(p_{k}\,\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\left(e^{i\phi_{k}}\,d_{k}^{+}d_{-k}^{+}-e^{-i\phi_{k}}d_{-k}d_{k}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{0\leq k\leq\pi}\Bigg[1+p_{k}\,\left(e^{i\phi_{k}}\,d_{k}^{+}d_{-k}^{+}-e^{-i\phi_{k}}d_{-k}d_{k}\right)\,\sin\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(1-\left(n_{k}-n_{-k}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\cos\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}-1\right)\Bigg],

(11)

\displaystyle~~~\mathcal{U}=\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\,|_{d_{k}\to\alpha_{k}},~~~\mathcal{U}^{+}=\tilde{\mathcal{U}}^{+}\,|_{d_{k}\to c_{k}},

(12)

where
n_{k}=d^{+}_{k}d_{k}
,
\beta_{k}\,,\,\phi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
p_{k}=\sign(\tilde{\Delta}_{k})
. If we would like to find the result of the action of the operator
\mathcal{U}
(
\mathcal{U}^{+}
) on the many-body state
|\,\Phi\,\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
(
|\,\Psi\,\rangle\in\mathcal{A}
), then we should act as if the occupation numbers are defined in
\mathcal{A}
(
\mathcal{C}
), respectively. It is easy to obtain that

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}\,c_{k}\,\mathcal{U}^{+}=\cos\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\,c_{k}-e^{i\phi_{k}}\,p_{k}\,\sin\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\,c^{+}_{-k}\,,

(13)

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{+}\,\alpha_{k}\,\mathcal{U}=\cos\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\,\alpha_{k}+e^{i\phi_{k}}\,p_{k}\,\sin\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\,\alpha^{+}_{-k}\,.

(14)

Then, substituting Eq.(
14
) into the Hamiltonian (
6
), the requirement for its diagonalization reduces to

\tan\beta_{k}={\tilde{\Delta}_{k}}/{\tilde{\xi}_{k}},~~\phi_{k}=\pm\pi/2.

(15)

Due to periodicity of the function
\tan\beta_{k}

\tilde{\Delta}_{k}/\tilde{\xi}_{k}
can correspond to a set of
\beta_{k}
values. A natural way to uniquely determine this angle is to fix

\displaystyle\cos\,\beta_{k}=\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}},~~\sin\,\beta_{k}=\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}},~~\varepsilon_{k}=\sqrt{\,\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{k}+\tilde{\Delta}^{2}_{k}\,}.

In this case, the
\beta_{k}
angle determines the Berry phase of the Bloch states of the mean-field Hamiltonian (
6
). However, for certain problems, other special choices of
\beta_{k}
values that do not violate Eq. (

in such a way that the unitary operator

does not perform rotations in the subspaces corresponding to the nodal points of
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}

\displaystyle c_{k^{\prime}}\to\alpha_{k^{\prime}}=c_{k^{\prime}},~~\tilde{\Delta}_{k^{\prime}}=0,~~\beta_{k^{\prime}}=0.

(17)

Consider the action of unitary operator
\mathcal{U}
on subspace corresponding to the nodal points
k^{\prime}
. As can be seen from Eq. (

), the system has symmetric nodal points

For these points

\displaystyle c_{k}=u_{k}\,\alpha_{k}-i\,v_{k}\,\alpha^{+}_{-k},~~l_{k}=\sign\left(\tilde{\xi}_{k}\right)^{|\sign(\tilde{\Delta}_{k})|+1},

(19)

\displaystyle u_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1+l_{k}\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\right)},~v_{k}=\sign(\tilde{\Delta}_{k})\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-l_{k}\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\right)}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{mf}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\xi}_{0}\,\alpha_{0}^{+}\alpha_{0}+\tilde{\xi}_{\pi}\,\alpha_{\pi}^{+}\alpha_{\pi}+\tilde{\xi}_{k_{*}}\left(\,\alpha_{k_{*}}^{+}\alpha_{k_{*}}+\alpha_{-k_{*}}^{+}\alpha_{-k_{*}}\,\right)

(20)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq 0,\pi,k_{*}}\sqrt{\,\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{k}+\tilde{\Delta}^{2}_{k}\,}\,\alpha_{k}^{+}\alpha_{k}+E_{0},

where the terms for which

(mod
2\pi
): their implementation leads to the appearance of an additional loop on the curve
\{\,\tilde{\xi}_{k}\,,\,\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\,\}
, which, if it encircles the origin, leads to a change of
N_{BDI}^{mf}
. It can also be seen from Fig.
2
that the conditions for the phases with different
|N_{BDI}^{mf}|
are determined by the relations:

\displaystyle|N_{BDI}^{mf}|=0:\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{0})=\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{\pi})=\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{k_{*}}),

\displaystyle|N_{BDI}^{mf}|=1:\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{0})=-\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{\pi})=\pm\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{k_{*}}),

(23)

\displaystyle|N_{BDI}^{mf}|=2:\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{0})=\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{\pi})=-\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{k_{*}}).

Note that, in the first two lines (
23
), the existence of
k_{*}
is not necessary, while for the conditions of the third line it is needed. The boundaries of the topological phases can be carried out by searching the gapless excitations,
\varepsilon_{k}=0
. They can be obtained from the conditions
\tilde{\xi}_{0,\pi}=0
and
\tilde{\xi}_{k_{*}}=0
:

\tilde{\mu}_{1,2}=2\left(\tilde{t}_{2}\pm\tilde{t}_{1}\right),~~~~~~\tilde{\mu}_{*}=\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{1}}{\tilde{\Delta}_{2}}\left(\tilde{t}_{1}-\tilde{t}_{2}\,\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{1}}{\tilde{\Delta}_{2}}\right).

(24)

For
V\,,\,F\,,\,G=0
these relations explicitly determine the boundaries of the topological phases. If
V\,,\,F\,,\,G\neq 0
the above relations turn to nonlinear equations against to
\mu
.

As follows from Fig.
2
the realization of quantum phase transitions is not always accompanied by topological quantum transitions. The same topological phases can correspond to different GSs. To clarify this statement let us consider two pair of points on the topological phase diagram Fig.
1
. In the first case of ‘c’ and ‘g’ points, we have different types of GSs (with the same fermion parity) as the states with
k=0,~\pi
either occupied or not. However, the loops in the corresponding Fig.
2
c and Fig.
2
g are homeomorphic. In the second case of ‘a’ and ‘b’ points, the wave function structure is equivalent (that is
|\,\Phi_{0}\,\rangle
), but the loops in Figs.
2
a and Figs.
2
b are already not homeomorphic. Thus, in general situation the topology of the set of wave functions grouped according to the filling of symmetric points (
k=0
and
k=\pi
) is not equivalent to the topology of the loops group, or mapping
\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and the index
N^{mf}_{BDI}

describing the degree of the last mapping.
There is still isomorphism between the Majorana number
\mathcal{M}=(-1)^{N_{BDI}^{mf}}
and the GS fermion parity
Kitaev 2001
.

III
Interaction effects beyond the mean-field approximation

III.1
Self-energy corrections

Here we consider the effect of quasiparticle interaction. For simplicity, we will further assume that the three-center interactions with amplitudes
F
and
G
, renormalize the system parameters only at the mean-field level in accordance with Eq. (
II.2
). To justify this approximation, let us remind that the three-center interactions arise as effective ones for the spin-polarised nanowire in the second order of the operator perturbation theory, see Appendix
A
and Eqs. (
80
)-(
81
). At the same time, the effective intersite repulsion of fermions,
V
, see Eq.(
79
), results from both renormalizations caused by the virtual transitions to the upper Hubbard subband and the original intersite Coulomb repulsion,
V_{0}
, which can be significant in the strongly-correlated regime. Then the Hamiltonian can be divided into two parts, the mean-field term and residual ones,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{mf}+\mathcal{H}_{int}

Coffey 1993
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{mf}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{k}\,\alpha_{k}^{+}\alpha_{k}\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{int}

\displaystyle=\frac{V}{N}\sum_{kpq}\,\Bigr[\Bigr(A_{k,p,-q;k+p-q}\,\alpha^{+}_{k}\,\alpha^{+}_{p}\,\alpha^{+}_{-q}\,\alpha_{k+p-q}

\displaystyle+\,B_{k,p,-q,-k-p+q}\,\alpha^{+}_{k}\,\alpha^{+}_{p}\,\alpha^{+}_{-q}\,\alpha^{+}_{-k-p+q}\,+\textrm{h.c.}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+C_{k,p;q,k+p-q}\,\alpha^{+}_{k}\,\alpha^{+}_{p}\,\alpha_{q}\,\alpha_{k+p-q}\Bigl],

(25)

where the amplitudes in
\mathcal{H}_{int}
have the following structure

\displaystyle A_{k,p,-q;k+p-q}=iu_{k}v_{-q}\Bigl\{v_{p}v_{k+p-q}\Bigl[e^{i\left(k-q\right)}-e^{-i\left(k+p\right)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+u_{p}u_{k+p-q}\left[e^{-i\left(p-q\right)}-e^{i\left(k-q\right)}\right]\Bigr\},\,

\displaystyle B_{k,p,-q,-k-p+q}=-u_{k}u_{p}v_{-q}v_{-k-p+q}e^{-i\left(p-q\right)},

(26)

\displaystyle C_{k,p;q,k+p-q}{=}v_{k}v_{p}v_{q}v_{k{+}p{-}q}e^{i\left(p{-}q\right)}{+}u_{k}u_{p}u_{q}u_{k{+}p{-}q}e^{i\left(q{-}p\right)}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+2u_{k}v_{p}v_{q}u_{k+p-q}\left[\cos\left(k+p\right)-\cos\left(p-q\right)\right].\,

From the perturbation theory point of view, the
\mathcal{H}_{int}
is treated as an interaction operator leading to fluctuation corrections to the mean-field approximation.

Let’s introduce the quasiparticle Matsubara Green’s function,

\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\\
=-\left(\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}\langle\,T_{\tau}\,\alpha_{k}(\tau)\,\alpha_{k}^{+}(\tau^{\prime})\,\rangle&\langle\,T_{\tau}\,\alpha_{k}(\tau)\,\alpha_{-k}(\tau^{\prime})\,\rangle\\
\langle\,T_{\tau}\,\alpha^{+}_{-k}(\tau)\,\alpha^{+}_{k}(\tau^{\prime})\,\rangle&\langle\,T_{\tau}\,\alpha^{+}_{-k}(\tau)\,\alpha_{-k}(\tau^{\prime})\,\rangle\end{array}\right)\\
=T\sum_{\omega}e^{-i(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\omega}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(i\omega),\\
\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(i\omega)=\left[\,\hat{g}^{-1}_{k}(i\omega)-\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(i\omega)\,\right]^{-1},\,

(27)

where
\hat{g}_{k}(i\omega)=i\omega\,\tau_{0}-\varepsilon_{k}\,\tau_{z}
is bare propagator of the system depending on
n
th Matsubara frequency,
\omega=\left(2n+1\right)\pi T
. Hereinafter
\tau_{0,x,y,z}
denote standard Pauli matrices. By definition, the elements of the Fourier transformed matrix are

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(i\omega)\right]_{2,2}=-\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{-k}(-i\omega)\right]_{1,1}^{*},

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(i\omega)\right]_{1,2}=-\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{-k}(-i\omega)\right]_{2,1}^{*}.

(28)

The nonzero anomalous components of
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}(i\omega)
and the
self-energy
\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(i\omega)
arise from the presence of the anomalous terms with the amplitudes
A_{k,p,-q;k+p-q}
and
B_{k,p,-q,-k-p+q}
in
\mathcal{H}_{int}
. However, such anomalous quasiparticle Green’s functions have lower order compared to the ones built on the original fermion operators
c_{k}
. This feature will be used below when calculating the quasiparticle spectrum.

In general, the diagrammatic series generated by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{A}_{k;p,-q,k-p+q}=\sum_{P_{3}}\left(-1\right)^{P_{3}}\,A_{k;P_{3}\{p,-q,k-p+q\}}\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{B}_{k,p,q,-k-p-q}=\sum_{P_{4}}\left(-1\right)^{P_{4}}\,B_{P_{4}\{k,p,q,-k-p-q\}}\,,

(29)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{C}_{k,p;q,k+p-q}=\sum_{P_{2},\,P^{\prime}_{2}}(-1)^{P_{2}+P^{\prime}_{2}}\,C_{P_{2}\{k,\,p\}P_{2}^{\prime}\{q,\,k+p-q\}}\,.

In the above expressions all possible permutations in a set of two, three and four quasimomenta,
\{k,p\}
(

\{p,-q,k-p+q\}
and

(

),

and

, respectively. In turn,

provides the parity of such permutations. The quasimomentum variables under symmetrization should correspond to propagators that have the same direction with respect to the vertices, i.e. either entering or leaving them (see Fig.

).

Expanding the scattering matrix up to the second order in
\mathcal{H}_{int}
and averaging according to Wick’s theorem, we obtain 15 irreducible diagrams describing the corrections for the normal and anomalous Green’s functions. The explicit form of such diagrams and analytical expressions for the self-energies are given in Appendix
B
. The diagrams can be divided into four classes as it is depicted in Fig.

. Then, the corresponding analytical expressions for the self-energies of each class are

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(1)}_{(i)}=-\frac{V}{N}\sum_{p}\Gamma^{\mu}\cdot f_{p}\,,

(30)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(2)}_{(ii)}=\left(\frac{V}{N}\right)^{2}\sum_{pq}\frac{\Gamma^{\mu}\,\Gamma^{\nu}}{2\varepsilon_{p}}\left(2f_{p}-1\right)\,f_{q}\,,

(31)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(2)}_{(iii)}=\left(\frac{V}{N}\right)^{2}\left(\sum_{p}\Gamma^{\nu}\partial_{\omega}f|_{w=\varepsilon_{p}}\right)\left(\Gamma^{\mu}f_{q}\right)\,,

(32)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(2)}_{(iv)}=C_{\mu\nu}\left(\frac{V}{N}\right)^{2}\sum_{pq}\frac{s\,s^{\prime}\,s^{\prime\prime}\,\Gamma^{\mu}\,\Gamma^{\nu}}{i\omega-s^{\prime}\varepsilon_{p}-s^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon_{q}-s\varepsilon_{sr}}

\displaystyle\times\left[f_{s^{\prime}p}\,f_{s^{\prime\prime}q}\,f_{sr}+(1-f_{s^{\prime}p})(1-f_{s^{\prime\prime}q})(1-f_{sr})\right]\,,

(33)

where
\mu,\,\nu=A,\,B,\,C
;
s,\,s^{\prime},\,s^{\prime\prime}=\pm 1
;
sr=sk-s^{\prime}p-s^{\prime\prime}q
;
f_{s^{\prime}p}\equiv f(s^{\prime}\varepsilon_{p})
stands for the Fermi-Dirac distribution function.

C_{\mu\nu}
are coefficients depending on the types of vertices in the diagram (see Appendix
B
for details). The explicit dependencies of
\Gamma^{\mu}
on the
k
,
p
and
q
are skipped but can be easily restored. It is important to note that in the above expressions, the excitation energies at the nodal points coincide with
\tilde{\xi}_{p}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}=\left\{{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\sqrt{\tilde{\xi}_{p}^{2}+\tilde{\Delta}_{p}^{2}}>0\,,~~~\tilde{\Delta}_{p}\neq 0};\\
{~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\tilde{\xi}_{p}\,,~~~\tilde{\Delta}_{p}=0,}\end{array}}\right.

and can be negative.
At low temperatures we can assume
\partial_{w}f(w)|_{w=\varepsilon_{p}}\sim\delta(\varepsilon_{p})
. The case of
\varepsilon_{p}=0
corresponds to the bulk gap closing and is beyond the scope of our study. Hence, in what follows we will set
\partial_{w}f(w)|_{w=\varepsilon_{p}}=0

doesn’t depend on frequency and can lead to a shift of the excitation spectrum. However, due to the presence of the Fermi functions, such corrections can contribute to the self-energy at low temperatures only if

\Sigma^{(1)}_{(i)}\sim V/N

The second-order corrections

depend on the external Matsubara frequency and contribute in both spectrum renormalization and damping. By definition given in Appendix

,
\Gamma<1
for majority points of the Brillouin zone (for which

In what follows, we will investigate chains of finite length, large enough for the formation of the Majorana zero modes,

. In this case the spectrum shifts,
\delta\varepsilon^{\left(1,2\right)}
, found numerically from the terms

and

Corrections to the self-energy of the second type
\Sigma^{(2)}_{(ii)}
also contribute at the nodal points of the SC order parameter only. Therefore, they can be estimated as
\Sigma^{(2)}_{(ii)}\sim V^{2}\Gamma\,/\,N\sim\left(V\Gamma\right)^{3}
that is beyond the accuracy of our consideration. Similarly, the contributions from the type iii) diagrams are also negligible due to the factor

. Thus, only the corrections (
30

Since the renormalized excitation spectrum,
\bar{\varepsilon}_{k}
, can be determined from the equation

\det\left[\,\hat{g}^{-1}_{k}(i\omega)-\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(i\omega)\,\right]=0,

(37)

it is obvious that the anomalous (off-diagonal) elements of the self-energy matrix result in the negligible fourth-order contributions. Then, to obtain
\bar{\varepsilon}_{k}
, it is sufficient to consider only normal electron-like matrix element due to the symmetry properties of
\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})

\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(i\omega)\right]_{1,1}=\frac{1}{i\omega-\varepsilon_{k}-\Sigma_{k}(i\omega)}\,,

(38)

where
\left[\,\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(i\omega)\,\right]_{1,1}=\Sigma_{k}(i\omega)
. According to the above arguments, the actual self-energy
\Sigma_{k}(i\omega)
has the following form:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}(\,i\omega\,)=\Sigma^{n(1)}_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Sigma^{n(2)}_{k;\,j}(i\omega),

(39)

where the explicit expressions of the right-side terms are given in the Appendix
B
.

III.2
Modification of the excitation spectrum

The first-order perturbation correction has only real part that is nonzero exceptionally in the nodal points with the negative excitation energy,

\Sigma^{n(1)}_{k}=\delta\varepsilon^{(1)}_{k}\\
=-2\frac{V}{N}\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\left\{\left(1-\cos k\right)f(\tilde{\xi}_{0})+\left(1+\cos k\right)f(\tilde{\xi}_{\pi})\right.\\
\left.+2\left(1+\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{1}}{2\tilde{\Delta}_{2}}\cos k\right)f({\tilde{\xi}_{k_{*}}})\right\}.

(40)

Such corrections lead to the fluctuation shift in the single-particle excitation energy
\delta\varepsilon^{(1)}
. The physical reason for this shift is as follows: when nodal modes with negative energies
\tilde{\xi}_{0}
,
\tilde{\xi}_{\pi}
and
\tilde{\xi}_{k_{*}}
appear a quantum transition to the many-body GS occurs in the system. This added fermions renormalize the many-body spectrum due to the Coulomb interaction. Accordingly, the single-particle excitation spectrum is modified as well. This effect should be enhanced under the
V
increase and vanish at
N\to\infty
, when the partial weights of single fermions in the many-body spectrum vanish.

Thus, the excitation spectrum is renormalized due to the coexistence of the Coulomb interaction, finite-size effects, and nodal points,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}\to\bar{\varepsilon}_{k}=\varepsilon_{k}+\re\Sigma^{n\left(1\right)}_{k}.

(41)

As shown in Sec.
II
, quantum transitions with the filling of the nodal modes do not necessarily cause topological phase change. However, their experimental identification can be a precursor of the latter.

The spectrum
\bar{\varepsilon}_{k}
has extrema at the points
\bar{k}=0,~\pi
with an effective mass

m_{\bar{k}}=\sign(\tilde{\xi}_{\bar{k}})\left[\,\ddot{\tilde{\xi}}_{\bar{k}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\xi}_{\bar{k}}}\left(\dot{\tilde{\Delta}}_{\bar{k}}\right)^{2}\,\right]^{-1}.

(42)

In turn, its change caused by the many-body effects at these points has the form

\displaystyle\delta m^{(1)}_{\bar{k}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{V}{N}\Big[\,\bigl((1-L_{\bar{k}})\cos\bar{k}+L_{\bar{k}}\bigr)f(\varepsilon_{0})

\displaystyle-\bigl((1-L_{\bar{k}})\cos\bar{k}-L_{\bar{k}}\bigr)f(\varepsilon_{\pi})

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{1}}{\tilde{\Delta}_{2}}(1-L_{\bar{k}})\cos\bar{k}-2L_{\bar{k}}\right)f(\varepsilon_{k_{*}})\,\Big]^{-1},

(43)

where
L_{\bar{k}}=\dot{\tilde{\Delta}}_{\bar{k}}\,/\,\tilde{\xi}_{\bar{k}}
.

It can be seen from Eq. (
43
) that the filling of each nodal mode with
k=0,\,\pi,\,k_{*}
leads to a unique change in the effective mass at
\bar{k}
. Based on the expression (
43
), one can distinguish between different topological phases. For example, in the limit of weak superconductivity,
|\tilde{\Delta}_{1,2}|\ll|\tilde{t}_{1,2}|
,
L_{\bar{k}}\ll 1
, and
|\tilde{\Delta}_{1}|>2|\tilde{\Delta}_{2}|
(i.e.
k_{*}
doesn’t exist), in the topologically trivial phases with the GSs
|\,\Phi_{0}\,\rangle
or
|\,\Phi_{3}\,\rangle
the effective mass change at
\bar{k}
is absent
\delta m^{(1)}_{\bar{k}}\approx 0
. For the nontrivial phase with the GSs
|\,\Phi_{1,2}\,\rangle=c_{0,\pi}^{+}|\,\Phi_{0}\,\rangle
, the mass modification is
\delta m^{(1)}_{\bar{k}}\approx\mp(V/N)\cos\bar{k}
.

According to (
39
), the spectrum changes in the second-order perturbation theory are given by the four self-energy terms. Taking into account their analytical form (
B
) and
making analytic continuation from
i\omega
to
\omega+i0^{+}

it becomes obvious that for

the main contribution is determined by
\Sigma_{1}^{n(2)}

\displaystyle\im\Sigma_{1}^{n(2)}(k,\omega)=-\frac{V^{2}}{24\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p\sum_{i=1}^{N_{q}}\frac{|\Gamma^{A}_{k;p,-q_{0i},k-p+q_{0i}}|^{2}}{|\varepsilon^{\prime}_{q_{0i}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{k-p+q_{0i}}|}\,F_{1},

\displaystyle\re\Sigma_{1}^{n(2)}(k,\omega)=\frac{1}{\pi}{\textrm{p}.v.}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\im\Sigma_{1}^{n(2)}(k,\omega^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\,\omega^{\prime}}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}.

(44)

The quasimomenta
q_{0}
are the roots of

(45)

It is seen from Eqs. (

Let us denote the denominator of the Green’s function by

at the wave vector
{k_{0}}
where the mean-field spectrum has minimum (a) and
{k_{0}}
values themselves (b). Parameters are identical to the ones of Fig.

Summation over the Matsubara frequencies yields the sought first-order fluctuation corrections to the anomalous pairing amplitude,

\displaystyle\delta\Delta^{(1)}_{k}=T\sum_{\omega}e^{i\omega 0^{+}}\,\left[\,{\delta^{(1)}\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(i\omega)}\,\right]^{(1)}_{1,2}=i\,\frac{V}{N}\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}_{k}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{p}\left[\,{\tilde{\xi}_{p}}\,\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\,\left(1-c_{k-p}\right)-\tilde{\xi}_{k}\,\tilde{\Delta}_{p}\,c_{k-p}\,\right]\frac{f(\varepsilon_{p})}{\varepsilon_{p}},

(51)

where
c_{k-p}=\cos(k-p)
. Note that, by analogy with the energy shift discussed in Sec.
III.2
, the presence of nodal points of
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}
leads to the nonzero
\delta\Delta^{(1)}_{k}\sim V\,/\,N
in the finite-size chains.

Let us focus on the
k
-dependence of
\delta\Delta_{k}^{(1)}
in the case of the Kitaev chain, for which

\tilde{\xi}_{k}=-\tilde{\mu}-\tilde{t}_{1}\,\cos k\,,~~\tilde{\Delta}_{k}=\tilde{\Delta}_{1}\,\sin k.

(52)

In this case, the antisymmetric dependence of
\delta\Delta^{(1)}_{k}
on
k
is formed by two harmonics:

\displaystyle\delta\Delta^{(1)}_{k}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle a_{1}\,\sin k+a_{2}\,\sin 2k,

So, one can conclude that the interaction effects induce the additional harmonic in
p
-wave superconductivity. In turn, the new harmonic can provide additional nodal points of SC order parameter. As shown in Sec.
IV.2
and Appendix
C
, the similar nodal points, induced by charge fluctuations, occur in the second-order perturbation theory as well.
Moreover, Sec.
IV.2
and Appendix
C
shows that these nodal points lie nearby the extreme points of the mean-field spectrum,

\displaystyle k_{0}=\arccos\left(\,\frac{-{\tilde{\mu}}\,/\,{\tilde{t}_{1}}}{1-\left(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}\,/\,\tilde{t}_{1}\right)^{2}}\,\right):~\left.\dot{\varepsilon}\,\right|_{k=k_{0}}=0.

(53)

The noted features underlie the mechanism of the fluctuation topological transitions according to the scenario similar to that discussed in Sec.
II.2
.

IV
Topological phase transitions

IV.1
Fluctuation contribution to winding number

To calculate the fluctuation contributions to the topological invariant, we use the well-known expression in terms of the Green’s functions
Volovik 2009
;
Volovik 2010
;
Gurarie 2011

N_{BDI}\\
=\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Tr\left[\,\tau_{x}\,\partial_{k}\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}\,\partial_{\omega}\left(\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}^{(c)}\right)^{-1}\,\right.\hphantom{aaaaaa}\\
\left.-\tau_{x}\,\partial_{\omega}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}\,\partial_{k}\left(\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}^{(c)}\right)^{-1}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}k.

(54)

Here
\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}(i\omega)
is the matrix Green’s function built on the bare operators
c_{k}
:

\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\\
=-\left(\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}\langle\,T_{\tau}\,c_{k}(\tau)\,c_{k}^{+}(\tau^{\prime})\,\rangle&\langle\,T_{\tau}\,c_{k}(\tau)\,c_{-k}(\tau^{\prime})\,\rangle\\
\langle\,T_{\tau}\,c^{+}_{-k}(\tau)\,c^{+}_{k}(\tau^{\prime})\,\rangle&\langle\,T_{\tau}\,c^{+}_{-k}(\tau)\,c_{-k}(\tau^{\prime})\,\rangle\end{array}\right)\\
=T\sum_{\omega}e^{-i(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\omega}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}(i\omega).

(55)

It can be obtained by the
SU(2)
rotation of the matrix
\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(i\omega)
that is valid for the arbitrary
n
-order correction,

\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}=U_{mf}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}\,U_{mf}^{+}\,;~~~U_{mf}=\left(\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{u_{k}}&{-iv_{k}}\\
-iv_{k}&{u_{k}}\end{array}\right).

(56)

Note that, when calculating the topological invariant, we put
l_{k}\equiv 1
in Eq. (
19
) and the Green’s functions
\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}
in Eq. (
54
) depend on the real frequency
i\omega\to\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using its asymptotic behavior

\left.\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}(\omega)\right|_{\omega\to\infty}\to\left(\,1\,/\,\omega\,\right)\tau_{0},

that does not depend on the quasimomentum, the expression for the topological invariant can be rewritten as:

N_{BDI}=\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}k\,\Tr\left.\left(\,\tau_{x}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}\cdot\partial_{k}\left(\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}^{(c)}\right)^{-1}\,\right)\right|_{\omega=0}.

(57)

This expression involves the matrix Green’s functions at zero frequency
\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}_{k}(\omega=0)
.
Let us transfer to a basis in which such a matrix is diagonal. It can be seen from Eqs. (
56
)-(
57
) that for this goal it is sufficient to diagonalize the matrix

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(\omega=0)\right]^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{X_{k}}&{iY_{k}}\\
-iY_{k}&{-X_{k}}\end{array}\right),

where

X_{k}=-\varepsilon_{k}-\left[\,\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(\omega=0)\,\right]_{1,1},~Y_{k}=i\left[\,\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(\omega=0)\,\right]_{1,2},

(61)

and
X_{k}=X_{-k}
,
Y_{k}=-Y_{-k}
, with
X_{k},\,Y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. The latter can be diagonalized by the unitary rotation

\displaystyle{U}_{fl}^{+}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{(\alpha)}_{k}(\omega=0)\,{U}_{fl}=\lambda_{k}\tau_{z}\,,~~{U}_{fl}=x_{k}\tau_{0}-iy_{k}\tau_{x},

\displaystyle x_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{X_{k}}{R_{k}}\right)}\,,~y_{k}=\sign(Y_{k})\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{X_{k}}{R_{k}}\right)},

\displaystyle R_{k}=\sqrt{X_{k}^{2}+Y_{k}^{2}}.

(62)

Then, by combining the Eqs. (
55
)-(
62
) it is easy to get an expression for the topological invariant

\displaystyle N_{BDI}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Tr\tau_{x}\left(U^{+}_{mf}\,\partial_{k}{U}_{mf}+U^{+}_{fl}\,\partial_{k}{U}_{fl}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\left(\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\beta}_{k}\,\right)\equiv N_{BDI}^{mf}+N_{BDI}^{fl},

(63)

where the angle
\beta_{k}
is defined in Eq.(
16
), and the angle
\bar{\beta}_{k}
is defined similarly,

\bar{\beta}_{k}=\arctan\left(\frac{Y_{k}}{X_{k}}\right),~~\cos\frac{\bar{\beta}_{k}}{2}=x_{k},~~\sin\frac{\bar{\beta}_{k}}{2}=y_{k}.

(64)

Thus, contribution to the winding number due to the Coulomb fluctuations can be written by analogy with the mean-field term
N_{BDI}^{mf}
(
22
) as follows

N_{BDI}^{fl}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{X_{k}\dot{Y}_{k}-Y_{k}\dot{X}_{k}}{X^{2}_{k}+Y^{2}_{k}}dk.

(65)

V
Conclusions

In this paper we have developed the theory of fluctuation topological transitions in Majorana nanowires of the BDI class with Coulomb interaction. In particular, the Kitaev chain model with long-range hopping and superconducting pairings, as well as Coulomb repulsion between neighboring fermions of the strength
V
was considered. It was demonstrated that the topological invariant,
N_{BDI}
, built on the Green’s functions, for such a system decomposes into the sum of the mean-field and fluctuation contributions:
N_{BDI}=N_{BDI}^{mf}+N_{BDI}^{fl}
. The existence of the latter is due to residual interaction between the Bogoliubov quasiparticles beyond the mean-field treatment. In the second order of perturbation theory in
V
, corrections to the Matsubara quasiparticle Green’s function were calculated in the Gorkov-Nambu formalism. It turned out that the fluctuations lead to the nonzero
N_{BDI}^{fl}
values only near the lines in parametric space where
N_{BDI}^{mf}
changes. The maximum width of the fluctuation-modified topological diagram regions near these lines scales as
\sim V^{2}
.

The FTT physics is closely related to the properties of the anomalous Green’s function at zero frequency –
Y_{k}
. Its quasimomentum dependence is determined by several Fourier harmonics. Therefore, in addition to the symmetrical nodal points
k=0,\pi
, native to the system, the function
Y_{k}
has additional ones,
k_{*}^{(2)}
. It is important that some of them appear near the minima of the mean-field spectrum
\varepsilon_{k}
. On the other hand, in such
k_{*}^{(2)}
the spectral shift is negative,
\delta\varepsilon_{k_{*}^{(2)}}<0
. If it is sufficient to close the gap, see Eq.(
66
), then the nodal points induced by the fluctuations are effectively filled leading to the topological transitions. This mechanism is similar to what happens at the mean-field level, when the nodal points
k_{*}
of the proximity-induced order parameter are filled. Moreover, if
k_{*}^{(2)}\approx k_{*}
, a cascade of topological transitions can occur with a slight change of the system parameters.
Note that an open issue in the study of the fluctuation topological phases is features of bulk-boundary correspondence. Recent works have shown that it can be violated in strongly correlated matter (see, e.g.
Zhao et al. 2023
).

Considering the original Kitaev chain model
Kitaev 2001
, we showed that the quasiparticle states with the energies near
\min(\varepsilon_{k})
, which mostly contribute to the nonzero
N_{BDI}^{fl}
, have a finite lifetime,
\tau_{k}=1/\gamma_{k}
.
This time is much less than the one of the states for which
N_{BDI}^{mf}\neq 0
and
N_{BDI}^{fl}=0
. However, it is still much longer than the lifetime of high-energy excitations near
\max(\varepsilon_{k})
which is minimal if
\Delta_{1}\ll t_{1}
and near the borders of the mean-field nontrivial phase.
Analysis of the mean-field-spectrum shift features
\delta\varepsilon_{k}
made it possible to find the effect potentially suitable for detecting topological phase transitions in the long but finite nanowires. Since the implementation of topological phase is accompanied by the filling of “nodal modes” with quasimomenta
k=0,\pi,\pm k_{*}
, such “additional” fermions renormalize the excitation spectrum due to the interaction leading to the observable modification of the effective mass in the finite system, cf. Eq. (
43
).

Finally, the fluctuation topological phases found in this work can be interpreted as a topological analogue of the so-called, vestigial order, implemented in quantum systems with composite order parameters
Fernandes et al. 2023
. In our case, the residual interactions between quasiparticles
\mathcal{H}_{int}
, cf. Eq. (
25
), can be considered as corrections that describe fluctuations of the charge (the amplitude
C
) and superconducting (the amplitudes
A
and
B
) orders. When
\varepsilon_{k_{0}}\ll 1
, such fluctuations begin to play an important role, and ultimately lead to the destruction of topological order at
\varepsilon_{k_{0}}=0
. However, those fluctuations near the transition boundaries give rise to the fluctuation-induced phase that can be
thought as a topological vestigial phase.

To summarize, we developed the analytical theory of fluctuation topological transitions in Majorana wires of the BDI symmetry class. The key point of the theory is an idea to go beyond mean-field approximation and to treat the residual interactions of the Bogoliubov quasiparticles. The residual interactions were considered as perturbations when calculating the diagram series for quasiparticle Green’s functions. This made it possible to explicitly extract the contribution to the topological invariant due to charge correlations and to calculate a topological phase diagram taking the latter into account. The effective filling of the nodal points in the superconducting order parameter
induced by the residual interactions was proposed as a mechanism of the fluctuation topological transitions. In addition, we studied the interaction effects on the quasiparticle spectrum.

A further extension of the work would be to study a disorder effect on the fluctuation topological transitions. Taking into account the results of recent studies
McGinley et al. 2017
;
Kells et al. 2018
;
Karcher et al. 2019
;
Francica et al. 2023
, one can expect even richer picture of the topological transitions in the model (
3
) due to, e.g. interplay of disorder and nonlocal interactions
\sim F,\,G
.

Appendix A
Derivation of the effective model

Let us rewrite the original Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{W}
as a sum of terms of zero, first and second order of smallness,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{W}=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{V}_{1}+\mathcal{V}_{2}.

(67)

Here
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is an unperturbed Hamiltonian and
\mathcal{V}_{j}

(j=1,2)
– operators describing weak interactions.

It is convenient to choose many-body eigenstates
|\,m\,\rangle
of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{0}
as a basis in the Hilbert space of the operator
\mathcal{H}
:
\mathcal{H}_{0}|\,m\,\rangle=E_{m}|\,m\,\rangle
.
An essential assumption allowing us to develop the perturbation theory is an existence of a large energy gap in the
E_{m}
spectrum
Bir and Pikus 1974
.
The subspace below the gap (the, so called, “low-energy” sector of the Hilbert space) is denoted as
\mathcal{M}
. The corresponding eigenstates and eigenvalues are
enumerated by
m
.
To count the eigenstates above the gap in the “high-energy” sector
\mathcal{L}
, the symbol
l
is used.
Note that both
|m\rangle\in\mathcal{M}
and
|l\rangle\in\mathcal{L}
can be degenerate but not all the eigenvalues
E_{m}
(and as well
E_{l}
) must necessarily be equal to each other.

Using the many-body states
|m\rangle
we can define projection operator
P
onto the low-energy sector
\mathcal{M}
as:

P=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}X^{mm}
,
with
X^{mm}=|m\rangle\langle m|
being the Hubbard operators.
The projection operator allows to divide interactions
\mathcal{V}_{j}
(
j=1,2
) in the Hamiltonian (
67
) into two parts:
\mathcal{V}_{j}=\mathcal{\bar{V}}_{j}+\mathcal{\bar{\bar{V}}}_{j}
. The first part
{\mathcal{\bar{V}}_{j}}
consisting of two terms,

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bar{V}}_{j}=P\,\mathcal{V}_{j}\,P+(1-P)\,\mathcal{V}_{j}\,(1-P),

\displaystyle P\,\mathcal{V}_{j}\,P=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\mathcal{V}_{j}\right)_{m,m^{\prime}}\hphantom{l}X^{mm^{\prime}},

\displaystyle(1-P)\,\mathcal{V}_{j}\,(1-P)=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathcal{V}_{j}\right)_{l,l^{\prime}}\hphantom{l}X^{ll^{\prime}},

(68)

does not mix the low- and high-energy sectors of the Hilbert space and, hence, is called a diagonal part.
The second part
\mathcal{\bar{\bar{V}}}_{j}
also consisting of two terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bar{\bar{V}}}_{j}=(1-P)\,\mathcal{V}_{j}\,P+P\,\mathcal{V}_{j}\,(1-P),

\displaystyle(1-P)\,\mathcal{V}_{j}\,P=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathcal{M}\\
l\in\mathcal{L}\end{subarray}}\left(\mathcal{V}_{j}\right)_{l,m}\hphantom{l}X^{lm},

\displaystyle P\,\mathcal{V}_{j}\,(1-P)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathcal{M}\\
l\in\mathcal{L}\end{subarray}}\left(\mathcal{V}_{j}\right)_{m,l}\hphantom{l}X^{ml},

(69)

is nondiagonal because of mixing the sectors
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{L}
. In Eqs. (
A
) and (
A
) the matrix elements
\langle m|\mathcal{V}_{j}|l\rangle
are denoted as
\left(\mathcal{V}_{j}\right)_{m,l}
.

Consider the following unitary transformation of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\to\tilde{\mathcal{H}}=e^{-S}\,\mathcal{H}\,e^{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}+[\,\mathcal{H},S\,]+\frac{1}{2}[\,[\,\mathcal{H},\,S\,],\,S\,]

(70)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}[\,[\,[\,\mathcal{H},\,S\,],\,S\,],S\,]+\ldots

We will assume that the operator
S
in (
70
) is nondiagonal

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathcal{M}\\
l\in\mathcal{L}\end{subarray}}\left[\left(S\right)_{m,l}\hphantom{l}X^{ml}+\left(S\right)_{l,m}\hphantom{l}X^{lm}\right],

(71)

and its decomposition starts with terms of the first order of smallness:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}+\ldots.

(72)

Substituting the expressions (
67
) and (
72
) into the series (
70
), we retain only the terms whose order of smallness is not higher than three.
Next, to get rid of the nondiagonal terms in
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
, the following conditions on the operators
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
have to be imposed:

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bar{\bar{V}}}_{1}+[\,\mathcal{H}_{0}\,,\,S_{1}\,]=0,

(73)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bar{\bar{V}}}_{2}+[\,\mathcal{H}_{0}\,,\,S_{2}\,]+[\,\mathcal{\bar{V}}_{1},\,S_{1}\,]=0.

(74)

Then, the matrix elements of
S_{1}
can be found from the operator equation (
73
),

\displaystyle\left(S_{1}\right)_{m,l}=\frac{\left(\mathcal{V}_{1}\right)_{m,l}}{E_{l}-E_{m}}\,,~\left(S_{1}\right)_{l,m}=-\frac{\left(\mathcal{V}_{1}\right)_{l,m}}{E_{l}-E_{m}}\,.

(75)

Here we took advantage of the equalities

\mathcal{H}_{0}|\,m\,\rangle=E_{m}|\,m\,\rangle
and

\mathcal{H}_{0}|\,l\,\rangle=E_{l}|\,l\,\rangle
.

Projecting out the high-energy processes the operators acting within the low-energy sector
\mathcal{M}
of the Hilbert space are only left. Thus, the general form of the required effective Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{eff}=P\,\mathcal{H}\,P~~~~~~~~~~~

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\,P\left(\big[\,\mathcal{\bar{\bar{V}}}_{1}\,,\,S_{1}+S_{2}\,\big]+\big[\,\mathcal{\bar{\bar{V}}}_{2}\,,\,S_{1}\big]\right)P.

(76)

If we consider the spin-polarized SW the projection operator on the sector
\mathcal{M}
can be written in the form:

\displaystyle P=\prod_{f}\left(\,|\,f\,,0\,\rangle\langle\,f,0\,|+|\,f,\,\uparrow\,\rangle\langle\,f,\,\uparrow\,|\,\right).

(77)

The performed calculations for the original model (
1
) allow us to find the effective Hamiltonian (
3
) with the following parameters of effective interactions:

\displaystyle\frac{E_{0}}{N}=\frac{\Delta_{1s}^{2}}{2\mu}-\frac{\Delta_{s}^{2}}{U-2\mu},

(78)

\displaystyle\mu=\mu+h+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4h}+\frac{2\Delta_{1s}^{2}}{\mu}+\frac{2\Delta_{1s}^{2}-\Delta^{2}}{U-2\mu},

\displaystyle t=t_{0}-\frac{4\Delta_{s}\Delta_{1s}}{U-2\mu};~t_{1}=\frac{\Delta^{2}_{1s}}{\mu}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4h},

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{\alpha\Delta_{s}}{U-2\mu}+\frac{\alpha\Delta_{s}}{U+2h};~~\Delta_{1}=\frac{\alpha\Delta_{1s}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{h}-\frac{1}{\mu}\right),

where
E_{0}
gives corrections to the ground state energy due to the virtual creations and annihilations of Cooper pairs. This term is omitted in (
3
). Taking into account the Hubbard repulsion
U
leads to the effective interactions between spinless fermions. On the one hand, a repulsive interaction between fermions with amplitude
V
is induced. On the other hand, nonzero
U
leads to three-centered charge-correlated hoppings and SC pairings of fermions belonging to the secondary coordination sphere (parameters
F
and
G
, respectively). The dependence of such parameters on the SW parameters reads

\displaystyle{V}=V_{0}{+}\frac{(\alpha/2)^{2}}{2h}-\frac{(\alpha/2)^{2}}{U{+}2h}+\frac{\Delta_{1s}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{\Delta_{1s}^{2}}{U{-}2\mu},

(79)

\displaystyle F=\frac{(\alpha/2)^{2}}{U+2h}-\frac{(\alpha/2)^{2}}{2h}+\frac{\Delta_{1s}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{\Delta_{1s}^{2}}{U-2\mu},

(80)

\displaystyle G=\frac{\alpha\Delta_{1s}}{2}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2h}-\frac{1}{U{+}2h}-\frac{1}{2\mu}-\frac{1}{U{-}2\mu}\Bigg).

(81)

Note, that the three-center interactions can be considered as the operators of the number of high-energy states deformed when projected onto the low-energy sector

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{3}=P\,e^{-S}\,\left(\,X^{\downarrow\downarrow}+X^{22}\,\right)\,e^{S}\,P.

(82)

Appendix C
Vicinity of mean-field spectrum minima and fluctuation-induced nodal points

Real and imaginary parts of anomalous self-energy
\left[\,\Sigma^{(2)}_{k_{0}}\,\right]_{12}
as functions of frequency
\omega
at the wave vector
k_{0}
where the mean-field spectrum has minimum for parameters corresponding to the central point (a) and asterisk (b) on the Fig.

a. The dots mark the functions at the mass shell,
\omega=\omega_{k}
. It can be seen that
\im\left[\,\Sigma^{(2)}_{k_{0}}(0)\,\right]_{12}\cong\im\left[\,\Sigma^{(2)}_{k_{0}}(\omega_{k})\,\right]_{12}
and
\re\left[\,\Sigma^{(2)}_{k_{0}}(\omega_{k})\,\right]_{12}\cong 0
justifying the relations (
94
) and (

).

Here we analyze the qualitative reasons why the nodal points of
Y_{k}
can appear near the points of mean-field-spectrum minimum
k_{0}
.
Let us start with the introduction of functions

\displaystyle X_{k}\to\bar{X}_{k}=\omega_{k}-\varepsilon_{k}-\re\left[\,\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(\omega_{k}+i0^{+})\,\right]_{11},

\displaystyle Y_{k}\to\bar{Y}_{k}=\im\left[\,\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(\omega_{k}+i0^{+})\,\right]_{12},

(92)

that, in fact, define the real parts of the normal and anomalous components of the inverse Green’s function at the frequency
\omega=\omega_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. The frequency
\omega_{k}
is determined from the equation

\re\left(\det\left[\,\hat{g}^{-1}_{k}(\omega_{k})-\hat{\Sigma}_{k}(\omega_{k})\,\right]\right)=0,

which is similar to Eq. (
48
), but takes into account the anomalous self-energy components. Bearing in mind that
|\gamma_{k_{0}}|\ll|\gamma_{\tilde{k}}|
,
|\delta\varepsilon_{k_{0}}|\ll|\delta\varepsilon_{k_{\tilde{k}}}|
, where
\tilde{k}
:
|\gamma_{\tilde{k}}|=\max\left(|\gamma_{k}|\right)
(see Figs.
5
c and
6
a in Sec.
III.2
), and the behavior of the anomalous self-energy shown in Fig.
15
, the two branches of the renormalized spectrum can be approximately defined as

\displaystyle\bar{\varepsilon}_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle\approx\pm\sqrt{\bar{X}_{k_{0}}^{2}+\bar{Y}_{k_{0}}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{\varepsilon}}_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\,\bar{X}_{k_{0}}\,\dot{\bar{X}}_{k_{0}}+\bar{Y}_{k_{0}}\,\dot{\bar{Y}}_{k_{0}}\,\right)=0

(93)

and

\displaystyle X_{k_{0}}\approx\,\bar{X}_{k_{0}}\,,~~Y_{k_{0}}\approx\,\bar{Y}_{k_{0}}.\,

(94)

Since there is the proximity-induced superconductivity in the system, it can be assumed that the functions
\bar{X}_{k}
and
\bar{Y}_{k}
are independent from each other. Therefore, to satisfy Eq. (
93
) one has to require that

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{k_{0}}\,\dot{\bar{X}}_{k_{0}}=0\,,~~\bar{Y}_{k_{0}}\,\dot{\bar{Y}}_{k_{0}}=0.

(95)

As noted in Sec.
III.1
, the renormalized energy of the particle-like excitations is
\bar{\varepsilon}_{k_{0}}\cong-\bar{X}_{k_{0}}\neq 0
, since the contributions from
\bar{Y}_{k_{0}}^{2}
give the corrections
\sim\left(V\cdot\Gamma\right)^{4}
.
Therefore,
\dot{\bar{X}}_{k_{0}}\cong 0
at the extreme point of the mean-filed spectrum.

On the other hand, the derivatives
\dot{\bar{X}}_{k_{0}}
and
\dot{\bar{Y}}_{k_{0}}
cannot simultaneously become zero due to the condition (
94
) and the fact that the vector
\left(\,\dot{X}_{k}\,,\,\dot{Y}_{k}\,\right)
is a tangent to the fluctuation loop. Then, in order to satisfy Eq. (
93
) and Eq. (
95
), we should expect that

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{X}}_{k_{0}}\cong 0\,,~~\bar{Y}_{k_{0}}\cong 0,~~\dot{\bar{Y}}_{k_{0}}\neq 0,

(96)

Comparing expressions (
94
) and (
96
), we see that the static part of the anomalous self-energy component
Y_{k}
, see Eq. (
61

\varepsilon_{k}
. This is confirmed by the numerical calculations of the
k

\varepsilon_{k}
and
Y_{k}
for a wide range of the system parameters including the system with the long-range hoppings and superconducting pairings.
