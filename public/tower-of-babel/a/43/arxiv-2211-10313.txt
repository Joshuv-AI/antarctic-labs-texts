A new infinite family of σ \sigma -elementary rings
Eric Swartz, Nicholas J. Werner

Abstract
. A cover of an associative (not necessarily commutative nor unital) ring R R is a collection of proper subrings of R R whose set-theoretic union equals R R . If such a cover exists, then the covering number σ ⁡ ( R ) \sigma(R) of R R is the cardinality of a minimal cover, and a ring R R is called σ \sigma -elementary if σ ⁡ ( R ) < σ ⁡ ( R / I ) \sigma(R)<\sigma(R/I) for every nonzero two-sided ideal I I of R R . In this paper, we provide the first examples of σ \sigma -elementary rings R R that have nontrivial Jacobson radical J J with R / J R/J noncommutative, and we determine the covering numbers of these rings.



1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, all rings are associative, but do not necessarily contain a multiplicative identity. A
cover
of a ring
R
is a collection of proper subrings of
R
whose set-theoretic union is all of
R
, where
S\subseteq R
is a subring if
S
is a group under addition and closed under multiplication; that is, a subring need not contain a multiplicative identity. A ring need not have a cover—consider, for example, a finite field—but, assuming one exists, the ring is said to be
coverable
, and we define the
covering number

\sigma(R)
to be the cardinality of a minimal cover. If no such cover exists, we say that
\sigma(R)=\infty
.

Analogous problems have been studied extensively for groups (see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
17
,
22
,
23
,
25
]
). In recent years, covering numbers of rings have begun to receive considerable attention (see
[
5
,
6
,
8
,
16
,
20
,
24
,
26
]
). In the case of groups, it is a famous theorem of Tomkinson
[
25
]
that no group has covering number 7. While many other examples of positive integers that are not the covering number of any group are known (see
[
11
]
), it is an open problem to determine whether or not there are infinitely many positive integers that do not occur as the covering number of a group. To date, no similar theorems have been proved for rings, although it is conjectured that there is no ring with covering number 13. The purpose of the present article is to study a class of finite rings that we believe will help resolve that conjecture, and will be useful in determining covering numbers of other rings.

As noted in
[
24
, Proof of Proposition 3.1]
, results by Neumann
[
19
, Lemma 4.1, 4.4]
and Lewin
[
14
, Lemma 1]
together show that, if a ring
R
has finite covering number, then there exists a finite homomorphic image of
R
with the same covering number; in other words, to determine which integers are covering numbers of rings, it suffices to consider finite rings. Moreover, by
[
24
, Theorem 3.12]
, if
R
is a unital ring such that its covering number
\sigma(R)
is finite, then there exists a two-sided ideal
I
of
R
such that
R/I
is finite,
R/I
has prime characteristic, the Jacobson radical
J
of
R/I
satisfies
J^{2}=\{0\}
, and
\sigma(R/I)=\sigma(R)
.

The reductions mentioned above hint at a strategy, which was originally employed for the study of covers of groups in
[
9
]
. It is not difficult to see that, if
I
is a two-sided ideal of
R
and
R/I
has a cover, then the inverse images of the subrings in the cover under the natural epimorphism form a cover for
R
; that is,
\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(R/I)
. It follows that the rings of interest for determining covering numbers are those rings
R
whose covering number is strictly less than the covering number of all proper quotients. (For this reason, we use the convention that a ring without a proper cover has infinite covering number.)

Definition 1.1
.

A ring
R
is said to be
\sigma
-elementary
if
\sigma(R)<\sigma(R/I)
for every nonzero two-sided ideal
I
of
R
. Note that a
\sigma
-elementary ring
R
must be coverable, since
\sigma(R)<\sigma(\{0\})=\infty
.

Evidently, determining which integers are covering numbers of rings amounts to classifying all
\sigma
-elementary rings and finding their covering numbers. In some sense, there are four cases for
\sigma
-elementary rings
R
with Jacobson radical
J
: (1)
R
is semisimple (i.e.,
J=\{0\}
) and
R/J
is commutative, (2)
R
is not semisimple and
R/J
is commutative, (3)
R
is semisimple and
R/J
is not commutative, or (4)
R
is not semisimple and
R/J
is not commutative.
Examples have been found for each of the first three cases (see
[
26
,
24
,
16
]
), but, up to this point, it is an open question whether examples exist in the fourth case.

The main result of this paper is the identification of an infinite family of
\sigma
-elementary rings with nontrivial Jacobson radical and noncommutative semisimple quotient. In order to define these rings, we must first introduce some notation. We will assume that the reader is familiar with the basic theory of finite rings (e.g., the Artin-Wedderburn Theorem and the structure of finite semisimple rings) and their modules and bimodules. Given a prime power
q
, let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field with
q
elements, and let
M_{n}(q)
be the ring of
n\times n
matrices with entries from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For a ring
R
, the Jacobson radical of
R
is
\mathscr{J}(R)
, and if
R
is unital, then
R^{\times}
denotes the unit group of
R
.

Next, let
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
be powers of
p
. We define
q_{1}\otimes q_{2}
to be the order of the field compositum of
\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}
, which is
\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}
. Note that if
q_{1}=p^{d_{1}}
and
q_{2}=p^{d_{2}}
, then
q_{1}\otimes q_{2}=p^{\text{lcm}(d_{1},d_{2})}
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
, let
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
be powers of the same prime
p
, and let
q=q_{1}\otimes q_{2}
. We define
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
to be the following subring of
M_{n+1}(q)
:

A(n,q_{1},q_{2}):=\left(\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$M_{n}(q_{1})$&$M_{n\times 1}(q)$\\
\hline\cr$0$&$\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}$\end{tabular} }\right).

The rings
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
were inspired by representations of the affine general linear group AGL
(n,q)
, which is isomorphic to a subgroup of the unit group of
A(n,q,q)
. For this reason, we refer to
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
as a ring of
AGL-type
. Note that by construction,
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
is noncommutative and has a nonzero radical.

Next, let
\omega
denote the prime omega function, which counts the number of distinct prime divisors of a natural number. Note that
\omega(1)=0
. Finally, let
\binom{n}{k}_{q}
denote the
q
-binomial coefficient, which counts the number of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
:

\binom{n}{k}_{q}=\frac{(q^{n}-1)(q^{n-1}-1)\cdots(q^{n-(k-1)}-1)}{(q^{k}-1)(q^{k-1}-1)\cdots(q-1)}.

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
R\cong A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
, where
n\geqslant 1
, and let
q:=q_{1}\otimes q_{2}=q_{1}^{d}
. If
n\geqslant 2
, then let
a
be the smallest prime divisor of
n
. Then,
R
is
\sigma
-elementary if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

n=1
and
(q_{1},q_{2})\neq(2,2)
or
(4,4)
. In this case,
\sigma(R)=q+1
.

(2)

n\geqslant 3
,
d<n-(n/a)
, and
(n,q_{1})\neq(3,2)
. In this case,

\sigma(R)=q^{n}+\binom{n}{d}_{q_{1}}+\omega(d).

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we collect a number of background results that will be used in the rest of the article. Section
3
is devoted to an analysis of the rings
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
when
n=1,2
, Section
4
sets up the machinery for case when
n\geqslant 3
, and Section
5
is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Frequently used results

Here, we collect some previously established results that will be used often in later sections. Each part of our first lemma is straightforward to prove, and we will apply these properties without attribution going forward.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
R
be a ring with unity.

(1)

R
is coverable if and only if
R
cannot be generated (as a ring) by a single element.

(2)

If
R
is noncommutative, then
R
is coverable.

(3)

For any two-sided ideal
I
of
R
, a cover of
R/I
can be lifted to a cover of
R
. Hence,
\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(R/I)
.

(4)

If each proper subring of
R
is contained in a maximal subring, then we may assume that any minimal cover of
R
consists of maximal subrings.

Slightly more substantial is the next lemma, which can ensure that a maximal subring of
R
is included in every cover of
R
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
R
be a coverable ring, and let
\mathcal{C}
be a minimal cover of
R
.

(1)

[
26
, Lemma 2.1]
If
M
is a maximal subring of
R
and
M\notin\mathcal{C}
, then
\sigma(M)\leq\sigma(R)
.

(2)

[
24
, Lemma 2.2]
Let
S
be a proper subring of
R
such that
R=S\oplus I
for some two-sided ideal
I
of
R
. If
\sigma(R)<\sigma(R/I)
, then
S\subseteq T
for some
T\in\mathcal{C}
. If, in addition,
S
is a maximal subring of
R
, then
S\in\mathcal{C}
.

Any ring of AGL-type is a finite ring of characteristic
p
. The Wedderburn-Malcev Theorem
[
21
, Sec. 11.6, Cor. p. 211]
,
[
18
, Thm. VIII.28]
(also known as the Wedderburn Principal Theorem) describes the basic structure of such rings.

Theorem 2.3
.

(Wedderburn-Malcev Theorem)
Let
R
be a finite ring with unity of characteristic
p
. Then, there exists an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-subalgebra
S
of
R
such that
R=S\oplus\mathscr{J}(R)
, and
S\cong R/\mathscr{J}(R)
as
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebras. Moreover,
S
is unique up to conjugation by elements of
1+\mathscr{J}(R)
.

By Theorem
2.3
, all semisimple complements to
\mathscr{J}(R)
in
R
are conjugate. We let
\mathscr{S}(R)
be the set of all such complements. Thus, for any
S\in\mathscr{S}(R)
, we have
R=S\oplus\mathscr{J}(R)
, and
S\cong R/\mathscr{J}(R)
.

Aside from providing a valuable decomposition for rings of characteristic
p
, the Wedderburn-Malcev Theorem affords a way to classify maximal subrings of
R
.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
24
, Theorem 3.10]

Let
R
be a finite ring with unity of characteristic
p
, let
M
be a maximal subring of
R
, and let
J=\mathscr{J}(R)
.

(1)

J\subseteq M
if and only if
M
is the inverse image of a maximal subring of
R/J
.

(2)

J\not\subseteq M
if and only if
M=S\oplus\mathscr{J}(M)
, where
S\in\mathscr{S}(R)
and
\mathscr{J}(M)=M\cap J
is an ideal of
R
that is maximal among the subideals of
R
contained in
J
.

3.
Basic properties and small cases

Recall from the introduction that when
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are powers of the same prime
p
, we define
q_{1}\otimes q_{2}
to be the order of
\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}
. For
n\geqslant 1
, we let
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
be the following subring of
M_{n+1}(q)
:

A(n,q_{1},q_{2}):=\left(\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$M_{n}(q_{1})$&$M_{n\times 1}(q)$\\
\hline\cr$0$&$\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}$\end{tabular} }\right),

where
q=q_{1}\otimes q_{2}
. The definition of these rings is motivated by the matrix representation of the affine general linear group
\textnormal{AGL}(n,q)
, which is isomorphic to the subgroup

\left(\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$\textnormal{GL}(n,q)$&$M_{n\times 1}(q)$\\
\hline\cr$0$&$1$\end{tabular} }\right)

of the unit group of
A(n,q,q)
.

The goal of this section is describe basic properties of
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
and, for
n\leqslant 2
, to calculate its covering number and determine when it is
\sigma
-elementary (Theorem
3.6
). The corresponding results for
n\geqslant 3
are given later in Corollary
5.13
and Theorem
5.16
. Our strategy is based on the approach used to prove
[
11
, Theorem 1.7]
, where it is shown that for
n\neq 2
, the group
\textnormal{AGL}(n,q)
has covering number
(q^{n+1}-1)/(q-1)
. However, our formulas for
\sigma(A(n,q_{1},q_{2}))
—and the methods used to establish them—are more complicated than in the group case.

Throughout this section,
R=A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
, and
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are the following subrings of
R
:

S_{1}:=\left(\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$M_{n}(q_{1})$&$0$\\
\hline\cr$0$&$0$\end{tabular} }\right)\cong M_{n}(q_{1}),\quad\text{ and }\quad S_{2}:=\left(\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$0$&$0$\\
\hline\cr$0$&$\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}$\end{tabular} }\right)\cong\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}.

The Jacobson radical of
R
is

J:=\left(\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$0$&$M_{n\times 1}(q)$\\
\hline\cr$0$&$0$\end{tabular} }\right),

which is a simple
(S_{1},S_{2})
-bimodule of order
q^{n}
. We take
S:=S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
to be a fixed semisimple complement to
J
in
R
. Note that
S\cong R/J
.

The set of all semisimple complements to
J
in
R
is denoted by
\mathscr{S}(R)
. By Theorem
2.3
,
S
is unique up to conjugation by elements of
1+J
, so
\mathscr{S}(R)=\{S^{1+x}:x\in J\}
. It is clear that
J^{2}=\{0\}
, so
(1+x)^{-1}=1-x
for all
x\in J
. Hence,
S^{1+x}=\{s+sx-xs:x\in J\}
for each
x\in J
.

Lemma 3.1
.

J\cap Z(R)=\{0\}
, and consequently
|\mathscr{S}(R)|=|J|
.

Proof.

Let
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
be the unit elements of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, respectively. Then,
e_{1}x=x=xe_{2}
for any
x\in J
. However,
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
annihilate one another, so if
x\in J\cap Z(R)
, then

x=e_{1}x=xe_{1}=(xe_{2})e_{1}=0.

Now, for all
s\in S
and
x\in J
, we have
s^{1+x}=s+sx-xs
. So, having
S^{1+x}=S
is equivalent to having
sx-xs\in S
. But,
sx-xs\in J
, and
S\cap J=\{0\}
, so
S^{1+x}=S
if and only if
x\in J\cap Z(R)
. Thus,
|\mathscr{S}(R)|=|J:(J\cap Z(R))|=|J|
.
∎

We wish to determine the covering number of
R
, and to classify exactly when
R
is a
\sigma
-elementary ring. It is easy to check that the latter condition holds exactly when
\sigma(R)<\sigma(S)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The ring
R
is
\sigma
-elementary if and only if
\sigma(R)<\sigma(S)
.

Proof.

Because
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are simple rings and
J^{2}=\{0\}
, the only nonzero, proper two-sided ideals of
R
are
J
,
S_{1}\oplus J
, and
S_{2}\oplus J
. The corresponding residue rings of
R
are isomorphic to
S
,
S_{2}
, and
S_{1}
, respectively. The result follows after noting that
\sigma(S)\leqslant\min\{\sigma(S_{1}),\sigma(S_{2})\}
.
∎

Next, we give a proposition that fundamentally determines the covering number of
R
. For
x\in J
, let
C_{S}(x):=\{s\in S:sx=xs\}
be the centralizer of
x
in
S
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a minimal set of maximal subrings of
S
such that

\bigcup_{x\in J\backslash\{0\}}C_{S}(x)\subseteq\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M,

and define

\mathscr{Z}:=\{M\oplus J:M\in\mathcal{M}\}.

Then,

(1)

\mathscr{S}(R)\cup\mathscr{Z}
is a cover of
R
.

(2)

If
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, then
\mathscr{S}(R)\cup\mathscr{Z}
is a minimal cover of
R
.

(3)

|\mathscr{Z}|\leqslant(|J|-1)/(q-1)=(q^{n}-1)/(q-1)
.

(4)

If
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, then
q^{n}+1\leqslant\sigma(R)\leqslant(q^{n+1}-1)/(q-1)
.

Proof.

(1) We first show that adding in the elements of
\mathscr{Z}
to those of
\mathscr{S}(R)
constitutes a cover. We know that
\mathscr{S}(R)=\{S^{1+x}:x\in J\}
,
and so

\bigcup_{S_{0}\in\mathscr{S}(R)}S_{0}=S^{1+J}.

Moreover, since
s^{1+x}=s+(sx-xs)\subseteq s+J
, it suffices to cover the elements
s
for which
s^{1+J}\subsetneq s+J
by maximal subrings of
S
. Note that since
0_{S},1_{S}\in C_{S}(x)
for any
x\in J
,
S^{1+J}\subsetneq R
, so the maximal subrings in
\mathscr{S}(R)
alone do not constitute a cover.

Suppose
s^{1+J}\subsetneq s+J
for a fixed
s\in S
. Since
|s+J|=|J|
, this implies that
s^{1+x}=s^{1+y}
for some
x\neq y\in J
. However, this means
s^{1+(x-y)}=s
and
s\in C_{S}(x-y)
. Conversely, if
s\notin\bigcup_{x\in J\backslash\{0\}}C_{S}(x)
, then
|s^{1+J}|=|J|=|s+J|
, which implies that
s+J=s^{1+J}\subseteq S^{1+J}
. By Lemma
2.4
, every maximal subring of
R
not contained in
\mathscr{S}(R)
is of the form
T\oplus J
, where
T
is a maximal subring of
S
. Thus, extending
\mathscr{S}(R)
to a cover of
R
amounts precisely to finding a set
\mathcal{M}
of maximal subrings of
S
such that

\bigcup_{x\in J\backslash\{0\}}C_{S}(x)\subseteq\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M.

That
\mathscr{S}(R)\cup\mathscr{Z}
is a cover follows.

(2) Assume that
R
is
\sigma
-elementary. Then,
\sigma(R)<\sigma(S)
. By Lemma
2.2
(2), every complement to
J
must be contained in any minimal cover of
R
. Thus, by the arguments above, extending
\mathscr{S}(R)
to a minimal cover of
R
amounts to finding a minimal cover of
\bigcup_{x\in J\backslash\{0\}}C_{S}(x)
.

(3) Note that
C_{S}(cx)=C_{S}(x)
whenever
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. This means that there are at most
(|J|-1)/(q-1)
subrings
C_{S}(x)
of
S
. Hence,
|\mathscr{Z}|\leqslant(|J|-1)/(q-1)
.

(4) By Lemma
3.1
,
|\mathscr{S}(R)|=|J|=q^{n}
. As in part (2), each subring in
\mathscr{S}(R)
is contained in every minimal cover of
R
because
R
is
\sigma
-elementary. But, every conjugate of
S
contains
0
, so the union of all the subrings in
\mathscr{S}(R)
has cardinality at most

|\mathscr{S}(R)|(|S|-1)+1=|J|(|S|-1)+1<|R|.

Thus, using part (1) and part (3),

|\mathscr{S}(R)|+1=q^{n}+1\leqslant\sigma(R)\leqslant q^{n}+\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1},

which completes the proof.
∎

When
n=1
or
n=2
, we can determine the covering number of
R
and describe when
R
is
\sigma
-elementary by comparing the bound for
\sigma(R)
in Proposition
3.3
(4) with the value of
\sigma(S)
. For larger values of
n
, we will construct a cover of the union of all the centralizers
C_{S}(x)
(see Proposition
4.4
), which will allow us to find
\sigma(R)
.

Since
R/(S_{2}\oplus J)\cong S_{1}\cong M_{n}(q_{1})
,
\sigma(M_{n}(q_{1}))
provides an upper bound for
\sigma(R)
. When
n\geqslant 2
,
M_{n}(q_{1})
is noncommutative, and hence is coverable (in fact, being a simple ring,
M_{n}(q_{1})
is
\sigma
-elementary), and
\sigma(M_{n}(q_{1}))
was determined in
[
15
,
8
]
. The formula for
\sigma(M_{n}(q_{1}))
is somewhat complicated and will be cited below. For our purposes it will be enough to have an upper bound on this covering number.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
, let
a
be the smallest prime divisor of
n
, and let
d\geqslant n-(n/a)
. Then, for every prime power
q
,
\sigma(M_{n}(q))\leqslant q^{nd}
, with equality only when
n=q=2
and
d=1
.

Proof.

By
[
15
, Section 7]
and
[
8
, Theorem A]
,

\sigma(M_{n}(q))=\frac{1}{a}\prod_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{k})+\sum_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{n\choose k}_{q}.

It suffices to prove the lemma for
d=n-n/a
. When
2\leqslant n\leqslant 4
, the inequality can be verified by inspection using the above formula. Note that when
n=2
, we have
\sigma(M_{2}(q))=\tfrac{1}{2}(q^{2}+q+2)
, which is less than or equal to
q^{2}
for all
q\geqslant 2
, and is exactly
q^{2}
when
q=2
. So, assume that
n\geqslant 5
.

Now, since
d
is the number of factors in
\prod_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{k})
, we certainly have

\frac{1}{a}\prod_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{k})<\frac{1}{2}(q^{n})^{d}.

So, it suffices to bound
\sum_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{n\choose k}_{q}
by
\tfrac{1}{2}q^{nd}
. Note that since
a\geqslant 2
,
nd\geqslant(n^{2})/2
. We will show that the aforementioned sum is bounded above by
\tfrac{1}{2}q^{(n^{2})/2}
.

Assume first that
q>2
. One form of the
q
-Binomial Theorem (also known as Cauchy’s Binomial Theorem) states that

\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+q^{k})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{k(k-1)/2}\binom{n}{k}_{q}.

Using this and recalling that
n\geqslant 5
,

\sum_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{n\choose k}_{q}<\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{k(k-1)/2}\binom{n}{k}_{q}=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+q^{k}).

Let

. For all
1\leqslant k\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1
, we see that

(3.5)

(1+q^{k})(1+q^{n-1-k})\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^{k}<q^{n}.

Also, when
k=0
,
(1+q^{k})(1+q^{n-1})=2(1+q^{n-1})
, which is less than
q^{n}
because
q\neq 2
. Thus, if

is even then
P
consists of
n/2
product pairs
(1+q^{k})(1+q^{n-1-k})
, each of which is less than

. If
n
is odd, then there are
\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1
such pairs, along with one factor

, which is less than
q^{n/2}
because

. Hence,

, and we are done.

In the case where
q=2
, (
3.5
) still holds, but

. To correct for this, we group together the first two and last two factors of

and note that

\displaystyle(1+1)(1+2)(1+2^{n-2})(1+2^{n-1})

\displaystyle=6(1+2^{n-2}+2^{n-1}+2^{2n-3})

\displaystyle\leqslant 6(1+\tfrac{1}{16}\cdot 2^{2n-3}+\tfrac{1}{8}\cdot 2^{2n-3}+2^{2n-3})

\displaystyle<6(\tfrac{5}{4}\cdot 2^{2n-3})<2^{2n}.

The argument then proceeds as before, and we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let

Lemma 4.5

Let
\Pi

R

(i)

, where

is an index set for collections of maximal subrings of

(ii)

is a cover of
\Pi
.

(iii)

\Pi

contains elements of
\Pi

\Pi_{i}

(iv)

For any

, then

For a maximal subring

If

for all maximal subrings

\mathcal{D}
is a minimal cover of the elements of
\Pi
. Moreover, if
c(M)<1
for all maximal subrings

is the unique minimal cover of the elements of
\Pi
that uses only maximal subrings.

Proof.

The proof is essentially identical to that of
[
11
, Lemma 5.1]
and
[
23
, Lemma 3.1]
with the word “group” changed to the word “ring,” but it is included for the sake of completeness. Let
\mathcal{D}
and
\Pi
be as in the statement of the lemma, and assume that

for all maximal subrings not in
\mathcal{D}
; the proof with strict inequalities is analogous. Suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is another cover of the elements of
\Pi
, and let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}\backslash(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{B})
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\mathcal{B}\backslash(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{B})
. The collection
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
consists only of subrings from classes
\mathcal{M}_{i}
, where

, and we let

be the number of subrings from
\mathcal{M}_{i}
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Similarly, the collection
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
consists only of subrings from classes
\mathcal{M}_{j}
, where

, and we let

be the number of subrings from
\mathcal{M}_{j}
in
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Note that since

is a different cover than

, we must have

By removing

must cover the elements of

that were in these subrings. Hence, for all

, if

denotes a subring in class

for each

which in turn implies that, for all

This means that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\in I}a_{i}\leqslant\sum\limits_{i\in I}\sum\limits_{j\not\in I}b_{j}\frac{|M_{j}\cap\Pi_{i}|}{|M_{i}\cap\Pi_{i}|}=\sum\limits_{j\not\in I}\sum\limits_{i\in I}b_{j}\frac{|M_{j}\cap\Pi_{i}|}{|M_{i}\cap\Pi_{i}|}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j\not\in I}\left(\sum\limits_{i\in I}\frac{|M_{j}\cap\Pi_{i}|}{|M_{i}\cap\Pi_{i}|}\right)b_{j}=\sum\limits_{j\not\in I}c(M_{j})b_{j}\leqslant\sum\limits_{j\not\in I}b_{j}=|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|,

which shows that

Hence, any other cover of the elements of

is a minimal cover of
\Pi
.

5.
The Covering Number of
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})

We continue to use the notations defined in Section
4

below, we will assemble all of the disparate pieces constructed in Section
4
and apply Lemma
4.5
to conclude that
\mathscr{C}
is a minimal cover of

. Before doing so, we need some preparatory lemmas. First, we consider which maximal subrings of

might contain elements of type

(cf.
[

,

).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
q
be any prime power, let
k
be an integer such that
1\leqslant k<n/2
, let
U
be a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and let
t\in M_{n}(q)
be an element of type
T_{k}
stabilizing
U
. If
T
is a maximal subring of
M_{n}(q)
containing
t
, then either

•

T=T_{U}
or
T=T_{\phi_{k}(U)}
, where
T_{U}
and
T_{\phi_{k}(U)}
are the Type I-
k
and Type I-
(n-k)
subspaces leaving
U
and
\phi_{k}(U)
invariant, respectively; or

•

T
is a Type II-
\ell
maximal subring, where
\ell
is a common prime divisor of
k
and
n
.

Proof.

The stated classification of Type I or Type II maximal subrings containing
t
is shown in
[
15
, Proposition 7.3]
and
[
15
, Lemma 7.4]
. We briefly summarize these arguments for the sake of completeness.

A Singer cycle on an
m
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has minimal polynomial of degree
m
that is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Consequently, the minimal polynomial of
t
is a product of two irreducible polynomials, one of degree
k
and the other of degree
n-k
. It follows that if
t
stabilizes a nonzero proper subspace
W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, then either
\dim W=k
or
\dim W=n-k
, and therefore either
W=U
or
W=\phi_{k}(U)
. We conclude that
T_{U}
and
T_{\phi_{k}(U)}
are the only Type I maximal subrings of
M_{n}(q)
containing
t
.

Now, assume that
\ell
is a common prime divisor of both
n
and
k
. The cyclic subgroup generated by a Singer cycle in
{\rm GL}(k,q)
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
{\rm GL}(k/\ell,q^{\ell})
, so
t
is contained in some Type II-
\ell
maximal subring of
M_{n}(q)
. On the other hand, suppose that
\ell
is a prime divisor of
n
but
\ell\nmid k
. The centralizer
C
of
t
in
{\rm GL}(n,q)
has order
(q^{k}-1)(q^{n-k}-1)
. If
t
is contained in a Type II-
\ell
maximal subring, then
t
is contained in a copy of
{\rm GL}(n/\ell,q^{\ell})
, and
C
contains the group of scalar matrices of this group. Hence,
q^{\ell}-1
divides
(q^{k}-1)(q^{n-k}-1)
. However, since
\ell\nmid k
,
\gcd(q^{\ell}-1,q^{k}-1)=q-1=\gcd(q^{\ell}-1,q^{n-k}-1)
. It follows that
q^{\ell}-1
divides
(q-1)^{2}
, which is impossible since
\ell\geqslant 2
.

It remains to show that no Type III maximal subring of
M_{n}(q)
contains
t
. Suppose that
\mathbb{F}_{r}
is a maximal subfield of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and let
b
be the prime such that
q=r^{b}
. Then, the order of
t
is at least
r^{b(n-k)}-1
. Since
b\geqslant 2
and
k<n/2
, we have
b(n-k)\geq 2((n/2)+1)>n
. As noted in
[
2
, Lemma 4.5]
, the maximum order of an element of
{\rm GL}(n,r)
is
r^{n}-1
, which is less than the order of
t
. Thus,
t
cannot lie in any Type III-
r
maximal subring of
M_{n}(q)
.
∎

Next, we derive some numerical bounds that will be needed when we examine
|M\cap\Pi_{i}|
and
|M_{i}\cap\Pi_{i}|
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
, let
1\leqslant d\leqslant n
, and let
m\geqslant 1
be a common divisor of both
n
and
d
. Then,

\binom{n/m}{d/m}_{q}\leqslant q^{d/m}\cdot q^{d(n-d)/(m^{2})},

and

\dfrac{|{\rm GL}(d,q)|}{|{\rm GL}(d/m,q^{m})|}\geqslant q^{d(d-1)(m-1)/m}.

Proof.

For each
0\leqslant k\leqslant(d/m)-1
,

\dfrac{q^{(n/m)-k}-1}{q^{(d/m)-k}-1}\leqslant q^{(n/m)-(d/m)+1},

which gives

\binom{n/m}{d/m}_{q}=\prod_{k=0}^{(d/m)-1}\dfrac{q^{(n/m)-k}-1}{q^{(d/m)-k}-1}\leqslant(q^{(n/m)-(d/m)+1})^{d/m}=q^{d/m}\cdot q^{d(n-d)/(m^{2})}.

Next, since
|{\rm GL}(n,q)|=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{k})
, we get

(5.3)

\dfrac{|{\rm GL}(d,q)|}{|{\rm GL}(d/m,q^{m})|}=\prod_{k=0,m\nmid k}^{d-1}(q^{d}-q^{k}).

Now,
q^{d}-q^{k}\geqslant q^{d-1}
for each
k
, and the product in (
5.3
) has
d-(d/m)
factors, so

\dfrac{|{\rm GL}(d,q)|}{|{\rm GL}(d/m,q^{m})|}\geqslant(q^{d-1})^{d-(d/m)}=q^{d(d-1)(m-1)/m},

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
n\geqslant 5
, let
a
be the smallest prime divisor of
n
, let
2\leqslant d<n-(n/a)
, and let
\ell
be a common prime divisor of both
n
and
d
. Then,

\binom{n/\ell}{d/\ell}_{q}\cdot\dfrac{|{\rm GL}(d/\ell,q^{\ell})|}{|{\rm GL}(d,q)|}\cdot\dfrac{|{\rm GL}(n/\ell-d/\ell,q^{\ell})|}{|{\rm GL}(n-d,q)|}\leqslant q^{-(\ell-1)^{2}-3}.

Proof.

Let

B:=\binom{n/\ell}{d/\ell}_{q}\cdot\dfrac{|{\rm GL}(d/\ell,q^{\ell})|}{|{\rm GL}(d,q)|}\cdot\dfrac{|{\rm GL}(n/\ell-d/\ell,q^{\ell})|}{|{\rm GL}(n-d,q)|}.

Note that
d\neq n/2
, and that the expression for
B
is symmetric with respect to
d
and
n-d
. So, we may assume without loss of generality that
d<n/2
. Using Lemma
5.2
,

B\leqslant q^{d/\ell}\cdot q^{d(n-d)/(\ell^{2})}\cdot q^{-d(d-1)(\ell-1)/\ell}\cdot q^{-(n-d)(n-d-1)(\ell-1)/\ell}.

Using the facts that
\frac{d}{\ell}\geqslant 1
and
d-1\geqslant\ell-1
, we have

(5.5)

\dfrac{d}{\ell}-\dfrac{d(d-1)(\ell-1)}{\ell}=-\dfrac{d}{\ell}((d-1)(\ell-1)-1)\leqslant-(\ell-1)^{2}+1.

Next,

(5.6)

\dfrac{d(n-d)}{\ell^{2}}-\dfrac{(n-d)(n-d-1)(\ell-1)}{\ell}=\dfrac{n-d}{\ell^{2}}\Big(d-(n-d-1)(\ell-1)\ell\Big).

Since
d<n/2
,
n-d-1\geqslant n/2
. (Since
\ell>1
by assumption, we may exclude
n=2k+1
and
d=k
.) Hence,

(5.7)

d-(n-d-1)(\ell-1)\ell\leqslant d-(n/2)(1)(2)=d-n.

Combining (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) yields

\dfrac{d(n-d)}{\ell^{2}}-\dfrac{(n-d)(n-d-1)(\ell-1)}{\ell}\leqslant\dfrac{(n-d)(d-n)}{\ell^{2}}=-\Big(\dfrac{n-d}{\ell}\Big)^{2}.

Now,
\ell\leqslant d<n/2
, so
n-d>\ell
. Thus,
(n-d)/\ell
is an integer greater than 1. Consequently,
-((n-d)/\ell)^{2}\leqslant-4
. Using this and (
5.5
), we obtain

B\leqslant q^{-(\ell-1)^{2}+1}\cdot q^{-4}=q^{-(\ell-1)^{2}-3},

which is the desired result.
∎

Theorem 5.8
.

The collection
\mathscr{C}

.

Proof.

Next, consider condition (iv) of Lemma

is invariant under conjugation by elements of

. As described in

, for a fixed

From here, we must calculate

), and there are

Next, assuming

Lemma 5.14

Let

, let

be the smallest prime divisor of

Proof.

Let
P:=(1/a)\prod_{k=1,a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{k})
. We will verify the result directly for
2\leqslant n\leqslant 4
. When
n=2
, we have
P=\tfrac{1}{2}(q^{2}-q)\geqslant 1
, with equality only when
q=2
. Similarly, when
n=3
,
P=\tfrac{1}{3}(q^{3}-q)(q^{3}-q^{2})\geqslant q^{3}
, with equality only when
q=2
. Next, when
n=4
,
P=\tfrac{1}{2}(q^{4}-q)(q^{4}-q^{3})>q^{4}
.

From here, assume that
n\geqslant 5
. Let
\delta=n-(n/a)
, which is the number of factors in the product in
P
. Since
a\nmid(n-1)
,
q^{n}-q^{n-1}
always occurs as a factor in
P
, and the other
\delta-1
factor are bounded below by
q^{n}-q^{n-2}
. So,

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\geqslant\dfrac{1}{a}(q^{n}-q^{n-2})^{\delta-1}(q^{n}-q^{n-1})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{a}\Big[q^{n-1}\cdot\Big(\dfrac{q^{2}-1}{q}\Big)\Big]^{\delta-1}(q^{n-1})(q-1)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{a}(q^{n-1})^{\delta}(q-1)\Big(\dfrac{q^{2}-1}{q}\Big)^{\delta-1}.

Now,
(n-1)\delta-n(\delta-1)=n-\delta=n/a
. So, to prove that
P>q^{n(\delta-1)}
, it will be enough to show that

(5.15)

\dfrac{1}{a}(q^{n/a})(q-1)\Big(\dfrac{q^{2}-1}{q}\Big)^{\delta-1}>1.

Suppose first that
a=n
. Then,
\delta=n-1
, and the left-hand side of (
5.15
) is bounded below by

\dfrac{1}{n}(2)(1)\Big(\dfrac{3}{2}\Big)^{n-2}.

This quantity is greater than 1 since
n\geqslant 5
, so the result holds when
a=n
. So, assume that
a<n
. Since
n\neq 4
, we have
2\leqslant a\leqslant(n/3)
. This gives us the following lower bounds for the terms in (
5.15
):
(1/a)\geqslant(3/n)
,
q\geqslant 2
,
(n/a)\geqslant 3
, and
\delta\geqslant(n/2)
. Applying these bounds to the left-hand side of (
5.15
) yields

\dfrac{3}{n}(2^{3})(1)\Big(\dfrac{3}{2}\Big)^{(n/2)-1},

which is greater than 1 for
n\geqslant 5
, as desired.
∎

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
n\geqslant 3
, let
a
be the smallest prime divisor of
n
, and let
q=q_{1}\otimes q_{2}=q_{1}^{d}
. If
d<n-(n/a)
, then
\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(M_{n}(q_{1}))
, with equality only when
n=3
and
q_{1}=2
. Thus,
R
is
\sigma
-elementary if
(n,q_{1})\neq(3,2)
.

Proof.

Recall that

\sigma(M_{n}(q_{1}))=\frac{1}{a}\prod_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q_{1}^{n}-q_{1}^{k})+\sum_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{n\choose k}_{q_{1}}.

By Corollary
5.13
,
\sigma(R)
is bounded above by

B:=q^{n}+\binom{n}{d}_{q_{1}}+\omega(d)

Hence, it suffices to show that
B<\sigma(M_{n}(q_{1}))
except when
n=3
and
q_{1}=2
. Assume that
d<n-(n/a)
. To ease the notation, let
f=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.

The cases
n=3
and
n=4
(both of which force
d=1
) can be verified by inspection. Note that if
n=3
and
q=2
, then
B=15=\sigma(M_{3}(2))
. So, assume that
n\geqslant 5
. By Lemma
5.14
,

q^{n}=q_{1}^{nd}\leqslant q_{1}^{n(n-(n/a)-1)}<\frac{1}{a}\prod_{k=1,a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q_{1}^{n}-q_{1}^{k}).

Next, the sum

(5.17)

\sum_{k=1,a\nmid k}^{f}\binom{n}{k}_{q_{1}}

has at least two summands, one of which is
\binom{n}{1}_{q_{1}}
. Certainly,
\omega(d)<n<\binom{n}{1}_{q_{1}}
. If
n\not\equiv 0
mod
4
, then
\binom{n}{f}_{q_{1}}
occurs as a summand of (
5.17
), and
\binom{n}{d}_{q_{1}}\leqslant\binom{n}{f}_{q_{1}}
. If
n\equiv 0
mod
4
, then
d\leqslant f-1
, and
\binom{n}{f-1}_{q_{1}}
occurs in (
5.17
), so
\binom{n}{d}_{q_{1}}\leqslant\binom{n}{f-1}_{q_{1}}
. Thus, in all cases,

q^{n}+\binom{n}{d}_{q_{1}}+\omega(d)\leqslant\frac{1}{a}\prod_{k=1,a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q_{1}^{n}-q_{1}^{k})+\sum_{k=1,a\nmid k}^{f}\binom{n}{k}_{q_{1}},

as desired.
∎

Finally, we are able to prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem

.

The cases where

d\geqslant n-(n/a)

3.6
