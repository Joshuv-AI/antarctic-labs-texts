Commitments are equivalent to statistically-verifiable one-way state generators
Rishabh Batra, Rahul Jain

Abstract
One-way state generators ( OWSG \mathrm{OWSG} ) [ MY22a ] are natural quantum analogs to classical one-way functions. We consider statistically-verifiable OWSG \mathrm{OWSG} s ( sv-OWSG ), which are potentially weaker objects than OWSG \mathrm{OWSG} s. We show that O ⁡ ( n log ⁡ ( n ) ) O\left(\frac{n}{\log(n)}\right) -copy sv-OWSG s ( n n represents the input length) are equivalent to poly ⁡ ( n ) \mathrm{poly}(n) -copy sv-OWSG s and to quantum commitments. Since known results show that o ⁡ ( n log ⁡ ( n ) ) o\left(\frac{n}{\log(n)}\right) -copy OWSG \mathrm{OWSG} s cannot imply commitments [ CGG+23 ] , this shows that O ⁡ ( n log ⁡ ( n ) ) O\left(\frac{n}{\log(n)}\right) -copy sv-OWSG s are the weakest OWSG \mathrm{OWSG} s from which we can get commitments (and hence much of quantum cryptography). Our construction follows along the lines of Håstad, Impagliazzo, Levin and Luby [ HILL99 ] , who obtained classical pseudorandom generators ( PRG \mathrm{PRG} ) from classical one-way functions ( OWF \mathrm{OWF} ), however with crucial modifications. Our construction, when applied to the classical case, provides an alternative to the construction provided by [ HILL99 ] to obtain a classical mildly non-uniform PRG \mathrm{PRG} from any classical OWF \mathrm{OWF} . Since we do not argue conditioned on the output f ⁡ ( x ) f(x) , our construction and analysis is arguably simpler and may be of independent interest. For converting a mildly non-uniform PRG \mathrm{PRG} to a uniform PRG \mathrm{PRG} , we can use the same construction as [ HILL99 ] .

Introduction

Commitments are one of the most fundamental primitives in cryptography. A commitment scheme allows a sender to commit some message
m
(in an encrypted form) to a receiver such that the following properties hold:

Hiding
: the receiver should not be able to learn the message
m
before the sender decides to reveal it.

Binding
: the sender should not be able to change the message to some
m^{\prime}\neq m
once it has committed to
m
.

An intuitive way to think about commitments is as follows: the sender Alice locks the message to be sent in a physical box and sends it across to the receiver Bob and keeps the key with herself.
When Alice wants to reveal the message, she just sends the key across to Bob. Hiding follows from the fact that Bob cannot open the box unless Alice sends the key. Binding follows from the fact that Alice can’t change the message once she has sent the box across.

There are two types of securities considered in cryptography: statistical security (against computationally unbounded adversaries) and computational security (against computationally bounded adversaries). Most of the results in classical cryptography are based on some computational assumptions.

It is known that both statistical hiding and statistical binding cannot be achieved using classical protocols. There are some tasks e.g. statistically secure key distribution that are not possible using classical protocols, however are possible using quantum protocols
[
BB84
,
Eke91
]
. This leads to the question if it is possible to have both statistical hiding and statistical binding using quantum protocols for commitments. It is known that
this is also not possible
[
May97
,
Lo97
,
LC97
]
. Thus in the quantum regime also, computational assumptions are necessary for the existence of many cryptographic primitives.

A pseudorandom generator (
\mathrm{PRG}
) is an efficiently computable function,
\mathrm{PRG}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{l(n)}
such that
l(n)>n
and
\mathrm{PRG}(U_{n})
(
U_{n}
represents the uniform distribution on
n
bits) is computationally indistinguishable from
U_{l(n)}
. A one-way function (
\mathrm{OWF}
) is a function that is efficiently computable and hard to invert.
The existence of one-way functions is a widely used computational assumption in classical cryptography and many of the classical cryptographic primitives are known to imply the existence of one-way functions. An EFID pair
[
Gol90
]
is a pair of efficiently samplable distributions that are statistically far and computationally indistinguishable. It is known that one-way functions, EFID and
\mathrm{PRG}
are equivalent to each other
[
Gol01
]
.

In the quantum world, however, the story is not as straightforward. Morimae and Yamakawa
[
MY22b
]
showed that quantum commitments can be based on pseudorandom state generators (
\mathrm{PRSG}
)
[
JLS18
,
AQY22
]
, which can be thought of as a generalization of
\mathrm{PRG}
s with quantum outputs. It is known that
\mathrm{PRSG}
s can exist even if BQP = QMA (relative to a quantum oracle)
[
Kre21
]
or if P = NP (relative to a classical oracle)
[
KQST23
]
, which does not allow for the existence of one-way functions (relative to these oracles).

An
\mathrm{EFI}
(see Definition
17
) is a quantum polynomial time (
\mathrm{QPT}
) algorithm that generates a pair of quantum states that is statistically far and computationally indistinguishable
[
BCQ23
]
. Quantum commitments are known to be equivalent to EFI
[
BCQ23
,
Yan22
]
. A
k^{*}
-imbalanced
\mathrm{EFI}
is a
\mathrm{QPT}
algorithm that takes as input a parameter
k
and generates a pair of states, say
\rho_{0}(k)
and
\rho_{1}(k)
, that is computationally indistinguishable for
k\leq k^{*}
and is statistically far for
k\geq k^{*}
. It is easily seen that an
\mathrm{EFI}
implies an imbalanced-
\mathrm{EFI}
. Khurana and Tomer
[
KT24
]
show that the converse is also true (below
\lambda
represents the security parameter).

Fact 1

(
[
KT24
]
)
.

Let
k^{*}(\lambda)\in\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
. Then a
k^{*}
-imbalanced
\mathrm{EFI}
implies
\mathrm{EFI}
.

One-way state generators (
\mathrm{OWSG}
)
[
MY22a
]
are quantum generalizations of one-way functions in which the output
\phi(x)
(for input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
) is a quantum state and the function is difficult to invert even when multiple copies of the output state, that is
\phi(x)^{\otimes m}
, is given. Note that for classical one-way functions, providing many copies of
f(x)
to the adversary was not changing anything since the adversary could have made the copies itself. However, the quantum no-cloning theorem does not allow the adversary to copy quantum states and hence providing many copies may help the adversary.

We use a definition of statistically-verifiable
\mathrm{OWSG}
(
sv-OWSG
, see Definition
15
) that differs from the definitions used in
[
MY22a
]
and
[
KT24
]
. In our definition, the verifier
Ver
is an unbounded algorithm and the adversary
A
is a non-uniform
\mathrm{QPT}
algorithm, that is a
\mathrm{QPT}
algorithm that is provided a polynomial-size classical advice string. Providing polynomial-size advice string to adversaries is quite common in classical cryptography
[
Gol01
]
and allows for several reductions to go through. In the definition by
[
MY22a
]
, both
Ver
and
A
are uniform
\mathrm{QPT}
algorithms. In the definition by
[
KT24
]
,
Ver
is uniform
\mathrm{QPT}
and
A
is a
\mathrm{QPT}
algorithm that is provided polynomial-size quantum states as advice. Note that increasing the power of
Ver
allows for a larger class of
\mathrm{OWSG}
s and increasing the power of
A
allows for a smaller class of
\mathrm{OWSG}
s. In that sense, our definition is incomparable to that of
[
MY22a
]
. An
\mathrm{OWSG}
according to
[
KT24
]
is also an
sv-OWSG
.

[
KT24
]
show that
\mathrm{OWSG}
s with pure-state output imply
\mathrm{EFI}
. Construction of an
\mathrm{EFI}
from a mixed-state output
\mathrm{OWSG}
was left open. We resolve this question by constructing EFI from
sv-OWSG
(which is implied by
\mathrm{OWSG}
). Our main result is (below
n
represents the key-length of the
sv-OWSG
):

Theorem 1
.

Let
m=\frac{cn}{\log(n)}
for some constant
c>0
. An
m
-copy
sv-OWSG
implies a
k^{*}
-imbalanced
\mathrm{EFI}
for some
k^{*}(\lambda)\in\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
.

From Theorem
1
and Fact
1
, we get:

Corollary 2
.

Let
m=\frac{cn}{\log(n)}
for some constant
c>0
. An
m
-copy
sv-OWSG
implies an
\mathrm{EFI}
.

Combining this with previous works that show oblivious transfer and secure multi-party computation from quantum commitments
[
GLSV21
,
BCKM21
,
AQY22
,
BCQ23
]
(which are in turn equivalent to EFI), we get:

Corollary 3
.

Let
m=\frac{cn}{\log(n)}
for some constant
c>0
. An
m
-copy
sv-OWSG
implies oblivious transfer and secure multi-party computation for all functionalities.

We show the converse to Corollary
2
is also true.

Theorem 4
.

An
\mathrm{EFI}
implies a
\mathrm{poly}(n)
-copy
sv-OWSG
.

Combining the above results we get:

Corollary 5
.

The following cryptographic primitives are equivalent:

•

\mathrm{poly}(n)
-copy
sv-OWSG
,

•

O\left(\frac{n}{\log(n)}\right)
-copy
sv-OWSG
,

•

\mathrm{EFI}
,

•

quantum commitments.

A natural question that can be asked at this point is whether an
o\left(\frac{n}{\log(n)}\right)
-copy

implies a
\mathrm{poly}(n)
-copy
sv-OWSG
(equivalently
\mathrm{EFI}
)? Cavalar et al.
[

]
show that
o\left(\frac{n}{\log(n)}\right)
-copy pure-state output

s (that are potentially stronger than

s) with statistical security exist unconditionally. Hence they cannot imply an
\mathrm{EFI}
unless
\mathrm{EFI}
s also exist unconditionally. This gives the following corollary.

Corollary 6
.

Unless
\mathrm{EFI}
s exist unconditionally,
O\left(\frac{n}{\log(n)}\right)
-copy
sv-OWSG
s are the weakest
\mathrm{OWSG}
s from which we can get an

, equivalently, commitment schemes.

Proof idea:

Let
f(\cdot)
be an
\mathrm{OWF}
that is hard to invert given
f(X)
(
X
represents the input random variable). A hardcore function
g(\cdot)
for
f(\cdot)
is an efficiently computable function such that
f(X)g(X)
and
f(X)\otimes U_{|g(X)|}
are computationally indistinguishable. However, we do not have the guarantee that
f(X)g(X)
and
f(X)\otimes U_{|g(X)|}
are statistically far apart which is necessary for them to be an
\mathrm{EFI}
pair.

Håstad, Impagliazzo, Levin and Luby
[
HILL99
]
presented a construction of a classical
\mathrm{PRG}
from a classical
\mathrm{OWF}
. The idea in
[
HILL99
]
is to first append
HH(X)
(where
H,H(X)
are the seed and the output of a
2
-universal hash function based extractor) to
f(X)
to increase the amount of information about
X
in
f(X)HH(X)
. This (sort of) makes
X\rightarrow f(X)HH(X)
an injective function. On appending
HH(X)
, one needs to ensure that the resultant function remains one-way. For this to happen, one can take
|H(X)|
to be around
S_{2}(X|f(X))
which ensures that
HH(X)
is nearly independent of
f(X)
. Here
S_{\alpha}(\cdot)
represents
\alpha
-Rényi entropy (see Definition
5
). In
[
HILL99
]
,
|H(X)|
depended on the number of preimages of
f(X)
, and hence they needed to condition on the outcome
f(X)
. They then appended a hardcore function
g(X)
to
f(X)HH(X)
. Doing this maintains computational indistinguishability of
f(X)HH(X)g(X)
from

f(X)HH(X)\otimes U_{|g(X)|}
. Since
f(X)HH(X)
carries most information about
X
(injectivity), they argued that
f(X)HH(X)g(X)
and

f(X)HH(X)\otimes U_{|g(X)|}
are statistically far apart, thus producing an
\mathrm{EFI}
pair.

In the quantum regime, this approach runs into an obvious issue that one cannot condition on a quantum state.
[
KT24
]
circumvent this by measuring the output state using efficient shadow tomography (which preserves expectation values for exponentially many observables). They then get a classical output which allows them to proceed with arguments along the lines of
[
HILL99
]
. However they face an additional issue: after conditioning on the measurement outcome, the probability distribution on the preimages may not be uniform. They overcome this using novel arguments. Finally, they show that a pure-state
\mathrm{OWSG}
implies commitments. Due to the properties needed for known methods for efficient shadow tomography (requiring some norm bounds on states), their argument could only go through for pure-state
\mathrm{OWSG}
s.

Unlike
[
HILL99
]
and
[
KT24
]
, our proof does not go via measuring the output state of the
\mathrm{OWSG}
and works for mixed-state
\mathrm{OWSG}
s. Following
[
HILL99
]
, we wish to append
HH^{l}(X)
to the combined input-output state
\tau^{XQ^{i}}
of an
m
-copy
\mathrm{OWSG}
for some
i\in[m]
(here
i
represents the number of copies of the output state and
l
represents the output length of the seeded extractor). Consider the states

\tau_{0}(i,l)=Q^{i}HH^{l}(X)Rg(X,R)_{\tau},~~\tau_{1}(i,l)=Q^{i}HH^{l}(X)_{\tau}\otimes U_{|R|}\otimes U_{|g(X,R)|}.

To get an EFI pair, we want to argue the following:

\tau_{0}(i,l)\approx_{\mathsf{negl}_{C}}\tau_{1}(i,l);~~S(\tau_{1}(i,l))-S(\tau_{0}(i,l))\geq\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}.

The simplified idea for this is as follows. We first identify an
i^{*}\in[m]
where

\mathrm{D}(\tau^{XQ^{i^{*}+1}}\|\mathbb{1}^{X}\otimes\tau^{Q^{i^{*}+1}})\approx\mathrm{D}(\tau^{XQ^{i^{*}}}\|\mathbb{1}^{X}\otimes\tau^{Q^{i^{*}}}).

Here
\approx
means the quantities have a difference at most
O(\log(n))
.
In order to argue the entropy difference between
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{0}
, we want
S(X|Q^{i^{*}}HH^{l}(X))_{\tau}\approx 0,

which requires
l\approx S(X|Q^{i^{*}})_{\tau}
. However, due to the properties of the extractor, we can only extract up to
l=S_{2}(X|Q^{i^{*}})_{\tau}
. Therefore, we want
l\approx S(X|Q^{i^{*}})_{\tau}\approx S_{2}(X|Q^{i^{*}})_{\tau}
. From the properties of the quantum hardcore function, we can argue that

Q^{i^{*}}HH^{l}(X)Rg(X,R)_{\tau}\approx_{\mathsf{negl}_{C}}Q^{i^{*}}HH^{l}(X)_{\tau}\otimes U_{|R|}\otimes U_{|g(X,R)|},

if we can ensure that
XQ^{i^{*}}HH^{l}(X)_{\tau}
is a one-way state (see Definition
15
). For this to hold, we require

l\approx S_{2}(X|Q^{i^{*}+1})_{\tau}\approx S_{2}(X|Q^{i^{*}})_{\tau}.

We identify a substate
\gamma
of
{\tau}
(see Definition
10
) with weight at least
\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
for which

\displaystyle l_{i^{*}}\approx S_{2}(X|Q^{i^{*}+1})_{\gamma}\approx S_{2}(X|Q^{i^{*}})_{\gamma}\approx S(X|Q^{i^{*}})_{\gamma}.

We then take a convex mixture of
\tau_{0}(i,l)
over
i
and
l
to remove the non-uniformity in the construction due to the choice of
i^{*}
and
l_{i^{*}}
.
This enables us to create a pair of states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
such that they are computationally indistinguishable and have
1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
difference in their von Neumann entropies. At this point,
\rho_{0}
is uniformly generated, however,
\rho_{1}
is still non-uniformly generated. We then take multiple copies of them to amplify their entropy difference and apply a quantum extractor on them to get an imbalanced
\mathrm{EFI}
pair with one of the outputs being the maximally-mixed state. We then use Fact
1

[
KT24
]
that gives an
\mathrm{EFI}
from an imbalanced
\mathrm{EFI}
, thus completing our proof.

Note that our construction, when applied to the classical case, provides an alternate to the construction provided by
[
HILL99
]
to obtain a classical mildly non-uniform
\mathrm{PRG}
from any classical
\mathrm{OWF}
. Since we do not argue conditioned on the output
f(x)
, our construction and analysis is arguably simpler and may be of independent interest. For converting a mildly non-uniform
\mathrm{PRG}
to a uniform
\mathrm{PRG}
, we can use the same construction as
[
HILL99
]
.

Definition 5

(Rényi entropy)
.

For a state
\rho

S_{\alpha}(\rho){\text{}:=}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{\alpha}).

S_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\alpha}\right).
