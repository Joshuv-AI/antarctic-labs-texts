𝒅 \boldsymbol{d} -Orthogonal Analogs of Classical Orthogonal Polynomials This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Orthogonal Polynomials, Special Functions and Applications (OPSFA14). The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/OPSFA2017.html
Emil HOROZOV †‡

Abstract
Classical orthogonal polynomial systems of Jacobi, Hermite and Laguerre have the property that the polynomials of each system are eigenfunctions of a second order ordinary differential operator. According to a famous theorem by Bochner they are the only systems on the real line with this property. Similar results hold for the discrete orthogonal polynomials. In a recent paper we introduced a natural class of polynomial systems whose members are the eigenfunctions of a differential operator of higher order and which are orthogonal with respect to d d measures, rather than one. These polynomial systems, enjoy a number of properties which make them a natural analog of the classical orthogonal polynomials. In the present paper we continue their study. The most important new properties are their hypergeometric representations which allow us to derive their generating functions and in some cases also Mehler–Heine type formulas.

The terminology “Bochner’s property” derives from the Bochner’s theorem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{j}(P_{n}P_{m})=0,&m>nd+j,\ n\geq 0,\\

\displaystyle xP_{n}(x)=P_{n+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{d}\gamma_{j}(n)P_{n-j}(x).

Here and later we use mostly monic polynomials, i.e., whose coefficient at the highest degree is 1.

The

[

The main tools we used in
[
38
]
are the automorphisms of non-commutative algebras. We explain the construction for the case of the first Weyl algebra
W_{1}
. It can be realized as the algebra of differential operators with polynomial coefficients in one variable.
W_{1}
acts on the space of polynomials
{\mathbb{C}}[x]
. We consider the simplest polynomial system
\{\psi_{n}(x)=x^{n},\,n=0,1,\ldots\}
, and the differential operator
H=x\partial
which satisfies

\displaystyle H\psi_{n}(x)=n\psi_{n}(x),\qquad\partial\psi_{n}(x)=n\psi_{n-1}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad x\psi_{n}(x)=\psi_{n+1}(x).

Take any polynomial
q(\partial)
in
\partial
, where
\partial:=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}
. Below for simplicity we take
q(\partial)=-\partial^{l}/l
,
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It defines an automorphism
\sigma=e^{\operatorname{ad}_{q(\partial)}}
of
W_{1}
, acting on
A\in W_{1}
as

\displaystyle\sigma(A)=e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{\partial^{l}/l}}A=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{ad}^{j}_{-\partial^{l}/l}(A)}{j!},

where
\operatorname{ad}_{A}(B)=[A,B]
. It is easy to see that the sum is finite. The images of the above operators are

\displaystyle\sigma(H)=H-\partial^{l},\qquad\sigma(\partial)=\partial,\qquad\sigma(x)=x-\partial^{l-1}.

If we define the polynomials

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=e^{-\partial^{l}/l}\psi_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\partial^{l}/l)^{j}x^{n}}{j!},

and put
L=\sigma(H)
we easily see that

\displaystyle LP_{n}(x)=nP_{n}(x),

\displaystyle xP_{n}(x)=P_{n+1}(x)+n(n-1)\cdots(n-l+2)P_{n-l}(x).

The main point is that we constructed simultaneously the polynomial system
\{P_{n}(x)\}
, the differential operator
L
and the finite-term recurrence.
Notice that for
l=2
these are the Hermite polynomials. For arbitrary

these are the Gould–Hopper polynomials
[
33
]
. See also the examples.

The same procedure can be repeated with other algebras. We can take instead of
\partial

G=R(H)\partial
, where
R(H)
is any polynomial in

W_{1}
we can take the algebra spanned by
G
,

,

The case of discrete orthogonal polynomials can be treated exactly in the same manner, realizing
W_{1}
by difference operators, and starting with
\psi(x,n)=x(x-1)\cdots(x-n+1)

[

In the present paper we study further the
d
-orthogonal polynomials with the Bochner property adding to the tools some new arguments which are not present in
[
38
]
. Let us first list the new properties which we discuss below.

The most fundamental one, of which the rest are consequences, is
the hypergeometric representation
of the class of
d
-orthogonal polynomials with the Bochner property corresponding to the special case
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
,
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\rho\in{\mathbb{C}}
, which we obtain both in the continuous and the discrete cases. Moreover, in both cases we provide two different representations in terms of hypergeometric functions. One of these representations has the advantage that the corresponding formula has the same values of hypergeometric parameters for all positive integers
n
. However, the other formula, in which the corresponding parameters depend on
n

({\rm mod}~l)
seems to be more useful in applications.

The hypergeometric representations show that the class of
d
-orthogonal polynomials under consideration is very similar to the Gould–Hopper polynomials
[
33
]
, which correspond to
G=\partial_{x}
and can also be included as a special case of our construction. For this reason, we call the
d
-orthogonal polynomials introduced above the
generalized Gould–Hopper
(
GGH
)
polynomials
.

One immediate consequence of the above hypergeometric representations is that these special polynomial systems naturally split in
l
families, each originating from the initial differential (difference) operator
G
, exactly as in the case of the Hermite polynomials, which also is a particular case of our general construction. Recall that the latter are naturally subdivided into two families: the even-indexed
H_{2n}(x)
,
n=0,1,2\ldots
and the odd-indexed ones
H_{2n+1}(x)
,
n=0,1,2\ldots
. The corresponding representations are
H_{2n}(x)=L_{n}^{(-1/2)}\big(x^{2}\big)
and
H_{2n}(x)=xL_{n}^{(1/2)}\big(x^{2}\big)
(up to multiplicative constants), where
L^{(\alpha)}_{n}
are the generalized Laguerre polynomials.

It is worth to point out that the above mentioned hypergeometric representations also use our main construction of the
d
-orthogonal polynomials in
[
38
]
, but they require some additional transformations. The point is that in these representations of the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
each summand naturally corresponds to a summand in a generalized hypergeometric series. In the differential case when one uses
q(G)=G
, the hypergeometric representations were known before, see
[
15
]
. Also the case of the Gould–Hopper polynomials is known
[
46
]
. However, for
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
,
l>1,
our formulas are new. In the discrete case, the formulas for the families corresponding to

,

and
q(G)=G^{l}
,

can be found in
[
16
]
. All other formulas, except for the Charlier and Meixner polynomials are new.

Our next goal is to find the
generating functions
for the GGH polynomials. We present two such formulas, both based on the second hypergeometric representation. For the first family of generating functions, we use the well-known method to obtain the generating functions for the classical orthogonal polynomials. For the second family, we apply a formula due to Srivastava
[
55
]
. Some of the
d
-orthogonal polynomials are known to have a generating function. These are exactly the ones mentioned above and which can be found in
[

Finally we find Mehler–Heine type asymptotic formulas

,

]
, which are again based on the second hypergeometric representation. In case of the multiple orthogonal polynomials, such formulas can be found in
[

,

[

It is essential to mention that the constructions of all systems of
d
-orthogonal polynomials in
[
38

are based on the so-called

, see
[

,

. Namely, these polynomials are the eigenfunctions for two linear operators – the first one is differential (difference)

in the variable

in the variable

, corresponding to the finite recurence relation:

\displaystyle L(x)P_{n}(x)=nP_{n}(x),\qquad xP_{n}(x)=\Lambda(n)P_{n}(x).

It turned out that both the generating functions and the Mehler–Heine asymptotics in the continuous case are expressed in terms of hypergeometric functions of the form

\displaystyle{}_{0}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{q}\end{matrix};xt\right].

The latter functions in the form of Meijer’s
G
-functions appear in an entirely different bispectral problem – for which both variables

and

are continuous, see
[

, where they are called generalized Bessel functions. It seems that this is not a mere coincidence but could be exploited further. In particular, the already known results for the Bessel bispectral functions and their Darboux transformations could be used as a model in the study of our
d
-orthogonal polynomials with the Bochner property.

Apart from Darboux transformations, some other possible directions of continuations of the present studies might include the so-called linearization problem (Clebsch–Gordan coefficients) and the problem of “connection coefficients” for the GGH polynomials. The asymptotic formulas from the present paper seem promising in the study of the zeros of the
d
-orthogonal polynomials with the Bochner property.

Some other classical issues could be studied as well, such as finding the measures and the corresponding Pearson equations for them. In another project we intend to pursue the connections of the polynomial systems and the corresponding measures with integrable systems such as bi-graded Toda hierarchy and KP-hierarchy. Some of the well known matrix models originate from the special solutions of these hierarchies, corresponding to these polynomial systems or their measures, e.g. the generalized Kontsevich–Penner model, Brezin–Gross–Witten model, etc., see
[

Apart from the Gould–Hopper polynomials we can mention the Konhauser–Toscano polynomials, whose special cases have been studied and used as early as in 1951 in connection with the penetration and diffusion of the

In this section which is central for the present paper, we derive formulas for some of the families of

This property is very important and, in particular, it makes our polynomial systems similar to the classical orthogonal polynomials. Notice that the system of Laguerre polynomials

is the Pochhammer symbol (
2.2
) (also the symbol of the rising factorial), is called a
generalized hypergeometric series
. When it is convergent in some open set its analytic continuation is called generalized hypergeometric function.

This function satisfies the differential equation

\displaystyle\left\{\partial_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{q}(x\partial_{x}+\beta_{j}-1)-\prod_{k=1}^{p}(x\partial_{x}+\alpha_{k})\right\}{}_{p}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p}\\
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q}\end{matrix};x\right]=0.

We have to point out that the above function does not always exist. However, when one of the parameters
\alpha_{i}
is a non-positive integer, the series terminates and the function becomes a polynomial. In this paper we mainly deal with generalized hypergeometric polynomials. In the remaining cases the series will be obviously convergent, due to the fact that
p<q
in the generating functions and in the Mehler–Heine asymptotic formulas. (For more details consult
[
51
]
.) From now on we will omit the word “generalized” since there will be no danger of confusion. Notice however that the expression “the hypergeometric function” usually refers to
{}_{2}F_{1}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a,b\\
c\end{smallmatrix};x\right]
.

3.1
Notation

In what follows we will introduce some notation that will be used to formulate and prove the results. First we will use the shorthand notation

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}(\alpha_{p})\\
(\beta_{q})\end{matrix};x\right]

for the hypergeometric series (
3.1
). Let us introduce
\alpha_{d+1}=0
in an effort to make the notation less clumsy. By
(\alpha_{d+1})
we denote the vector of parameters
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d+1})
. Recall that the notation
\Delta(l;\lambda)
abbreviates the vector

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lambda}{l},\frac{\lambda+1}{l},\ldots,\frac{\lambda+l-1}{l}\right),\qquad l\in{\mathbb{N}},

of
l
parameters.

We will also combine the latter symbol with
(\alpha_{d+1})
to write
(\Delta(l;\alpha_{d+1}))
for

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{1}}{l},\ldots,\frac{\alpha_{1}+l-1}{l},\ldots,\frac{\alpha_{d+1}}{l},\ldots,\frac{\alpha_{d+1}+l-1}{l},

in the expressions for the hypergeometric functions.

We assume that
\rho\neq 0
. Let us put
\eta=l^{d+1}\rho
.

Let us fix
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,l-1\}
and consider the polynomials with
n=ml+i
,
m=0,1,\ldots
. Then the parameters
\Delta(l;-n-\alpha_{d+1})
can be presented as follows

\displaystyle\Delta(l;-n-\alpha_{d+1})=\left(-m-\frac{i+\alpha_{1}}{l},-m-\frac{i-1+\alpha_{1}}{l},\ldots,-m-\frac{i+\alpha_{1}-l+1}{l},\ldots,\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hphantom{\Delta(l;-n-\alpha_{d+1})=}{}-m-\frac{i+\alpha_{d+1}}{l},-m-\frac{i-1+\alpha_{d+1}}{l},\ldots,-m-\frac{i+\alpha_{d+1}-l+1}{l}\right).

Let us introduce the sets

\displaystyle S_{k}(i)=\left\{\frac{\alpha_{k}+i-r}{l}+1,\ r=0,\ldots,l-1\right\},\qquad k=1,\ldots,d+1

and
S(i)=\cup_{k=1}^{d+1}S_{k}(i)
. By
\hat{S}(i)
we denote the set
S(i)\setminus\{1\}
, 1 is the element, corresponding to
k=d+1
,
r=i
. The elements of
S(i)
will be denoted by
S_{\beta}(i)
,
\beta=1,\ldots,(d+1)l
. By
I
we denote the set of indexes
\beta
, which correspond to
\hat{S}(i)
. Notice that
I
depends on
i
.

We also introduce a notation for the product of Pochhammer symbols:

\displaystyle[\alpha_{p}]_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\prod_{k=1}^{p}(\alpha_{k})_{n}.

Most of the notation is taken from
[
40
,
41
,
46
,
56
]
.

3.2
Continuous GGH polynomials

Some of the polynomial systems of the previous section have well known representations in terms of generalized hypergeometric functions
[
15
]
, where the authors take the formulas as their definition. Below we will derive the representation from
[
15
]
on the basis of the constructions from
[
38
]
. Our proof will help us to find hypergeometric representations for the other systems studied here, that are not treated elsewhere. The discrete
d
-orthogonal polynomials will be treated in the same manner.

We start with the case when the automorphism
\sigma
is defined by
G=R(H)\partial_{x}
where
R(H)
is a polynomial. Before that let us factor the polynomial
R(H)
into
R(H)=\rho\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m}(H+\alpha_{j}+1)
,
\rho\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
\rho\neq 0
. Here some of the numbers
\alpha_{j}
can be equal. In what follows we will drop the dependence of the
d
-orthogonal polynomials on
R
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The polynomials
P_{n}(x)
have the following hypergeometric representations:

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=x^{n}\,{}_{d+1}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}-n,(-n-\alpha_{d})\\
-\end{matrix};(-1)^{d+1}\rho x^{-1}\right],

(3.2)

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\rho^{n}[\alpha_{k}+1]_{n}\,{}_{1}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-n\\
(\alpha_{d}+1)\end{matrix};-\frac{x}{\rho}\right].

(3.3)

For the second formula the coefficients
\alpha_{j}
have to be different from negative integers or zero.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle Gx^{n}=nR(H)x^{n-1}=nR(n-1)x^{n-1}.

By induction we find that for any
j\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle G^{j}x^{n}=\prod_{s=0}^{j-1}(n-s)R(n-1-s)x^{n-j}.

In terms of the Pochhammer symbol we can write the last formula as

\displaystyle G^{j}x^{n}=(-1)^{j(d+1)}(-n)_{j}\rho^{j}[-n-\alpha_{d}]_{j}x^{n-j}.

The last formula allows to express the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
as

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=x^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{j(d+1)}(-n)_{j}[-n-\alpha_{d}]_{j}\rho^{j}x^{-j}}{j!}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P_n(x)}{}=x^{n}{}_{d+1}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}-n,(-n-\alpha_{d})\\
-\end{matrix};(-1)^{d+1}\rho x^{-1}\right].

(3.4)

To obtain the second expression for the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
, we transform the coefficients in the first line of (
3.4
) using the formula

\displaystyle(-n-\mu)_{j}=(-1)^{j}\frac{(\mu+1)_{n}}{(\mu+1)_{n-j}}.

(3.5)

Substitute this expression for the parameter values
\mu=0
,
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
into the sum (
3.4
), defining
P_{n}(x)
. As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\rho^{n}[\alpha_{d}+1]_{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{j!}\frac{1}{[\alpha_{d}+1]_{n-j}}\frac{\rho^{-n+j}x^{n-j}}{(n-j)!}.

Write the expression
n!/j!
as
(-1)^{n-j}(-n)_{n-j}
. After changing the summation index
j\rightarrow s=n-j
, we find

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\rho^{n}[\alpha_{d}+1]_{n}\,{}_{1}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-n\\
(\alpha_{d}+1)\end{matrix};-\frac{x}{\rho}\right].

Of course we need to impose the condition
\alpha_{k}\neq-1,-2,\ldots
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

(1)
This expression coincides with the corresponding one in
[
15
]
. The form of the roots of
R
was chosen for this purpose as well as to obtain the hypergeometric formula of the generalized Laguerre polynomials
L^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x)
.

(2) Formula (
3.4
) is valid for all values of the coefficients
\alpha_{j}
. However if some of the coefficients
\alpha_{j}
is are negative integers, the polynomial system becomes
x^{n}
for
n\geq-\alpha_{j}
and therefore in such a case it forms a finite system of
d
-orthogonal polynomials.

Let us consider the polynomials obtained by the automorphisms
\sigma_{q}
, where
q(G)
is some polynomial without constant term. We recall that they are given by

\displaystyle P_{n}^{q}(x):=e^{q(G)}x^{n}=x^{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k}(G)x^{n}}{k!}.

I don’t know if all these
d
-orthogonal polynomials have representations in terms of generalized hypergeometric functions or other special functions. However in the case when
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
they have. We are going to present the formula. Let us fix
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
. Finally we will drop the dependence on
q(G)
as there is no danger of confusion.

Theorem 3.3
.

For
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
have the following representation in hypergeometric functions:

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=x^{n}\,{}_{dl+l}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}(\Delta(l;-n-\alpha_{d}))\\
-\end{matrix};\left(\frac{(-1)^{d+1}\eta}{x}\right)^{l}\right].

(3.6)

Proof.

Again we use the formula for
P_{n}(x)
:

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{G^{lj}x^{n}}{j!}.

We know that

\displaystyle G^{lj}x^{n}=\prod_{s=0}^{lj-1}(n-s)R(n-1-s)x^{n-lj}.

Using that
\alpha_{d+1}=0
we present the coefficient at
x^{n-lj}
of
G^{lj}x^{n}
in the form

\displaystyle\eta^{lj}(-1)^{lj(d+1)}\prod_{r=0}^{l-1}\left[\frac{-n-\alpha_{d+1}+r}{l}\right]_{j}.

(3.7)

Then the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
are given by

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=x^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(G^{l})^{j}x^{n}}{j!}=x^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta^{lj}(-1)^{lj(d+1)}\prod_{r=0}^{l-1}\left[\frac{-n-\alpha_{d+1}+r}{l}\right]_{j}\frac{x^{-lj}}{j!}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P_n(x)}{}=x^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\prod_{r=0}^{l-1}\left[\frac{-n-\alpha_{d+1}+r}{l}\right]_{j}\left(\frac{(-1)^{d+1}\eta}{x}\right)^{lj}\frac{1}{j!}.

This proves (
3.6
).
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The formula for the
d
-orthogonal polynomials, corresponding to
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
, in the above theorem resembles the representation of Hermite polynomials in hypergeometric functions, see, e.g.,
[
51
]
. Notice that we don’t need to sum up to
n
but only up to
\big\lceil{\frac{n}{l}}\big\rceil
as the next terms are 0.

As in the case
l=1
we are going to find a second representation.

Theorem 3.5
.

The polynomials
P_{n}(x)
, where
n=ml+i
, have the following hypergeometric representation

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\eta^{ml}x^{i}\prod_{\beta\in I}\big(\hat{S}_{\beta}(i)\big)_{m}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l}\left[\begin{matrix}-m\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};-\left[\frac{x}{\eta}\right]^{l}\right].

(3.8)

Proof.

Notice that (
3.6
) can be written in the form

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=x^{ml+i}\,{}_{dl+l}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}(-S(i)+1-m)\\
-\end{matrix};\left(\frac{(-1)^{d+1}\eta}{x}\right)^{l}\right].

Let us put
y=\big(\frac{x}{\eta}\big)^{l}
and consider the polynomials
P_{m}^{(i)}(y)
defined by
P_{n}(x)=x^{i}\eta^{ml}P_{m}^{(i)}(y)
. We use that one of the elements of the set
S(i)
is
1
, which shows that they have the representation (
3.3
) from Theorem
3.1

\displaystyle P_{m}^{(i)}(y)=y^{m}\,{}_{dl+l}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}-m,(-\hat{S}(i)+1-m)\\
-\end{matrix};\frac{(-1)^{ld+l}}{y}\right].

They are exactly in the form of (
3.2
). This means that they can be written as

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(i)}(y)=\eta^{ml}\prod_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{m}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};-y\right].

Returning to the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
we obtain the formula (
3.8
).
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

The polynomials
P_{n}(x)
have also the representation

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\eta^{ml}\prod_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{m}\,{}_{2}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m,1\\
(S(i))\end{matrix};-\left[\frac{x}{\eta}\right]^{l}\right].

Proof.

Use that
S(i)=\hat{S}(i)\cup\{1\}
. Then the general formula

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}(\alpha_{p})\\
(\beta_{q})\end{matrix};x\right]={}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left[\begin{matrix}(\alpha_{p}),1\\
(\beta_{q}),1\end{matrix};x\right]

applied to (
3.8
) gives the result.
∎

We see that the GGH polynomial system
P_{n}(x)
can be split into
l
families of
d
-orthogonal polynomials with Bochner’s property exactly as Hermite polynomials split into 2 families in terms of Laguerre polynomials. More precisely

Corollary 3.7
.

The polynomial system
P_{n}(x)
consists of
l
families
P_{n}(x)=C_{i}x^{i}P_{m}^{(i)}(y)
,
i=0,\ldots,l-1
, where

\displaystyle y=\left(\frac{x}{\eta}\right)^{l},\qquad C_{i}=\eta^{ml}\prod_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{m}.

The family
P_{m}^{(i)}(x)
is the system of
d
-orthogonal polynomials with Bochner’s property corresponding to
G=\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(H+S_{\beta})\partial
.

While the above statement does not need a proof it is worth to point out that it is a precise formulation of Remark 7.4. in
[
38
]
. In
[
38
, Example 7.3]
we demonstrated that such a splitting of the family exists for the simplest case of
q(G)=G^{2}
, where
G=(x\partial_{x}+\alpha+1)\partial_{x}
without using hypergeometric functions.

3.3
Discrete GGH Polynomials

The discrete polynomial systems also have representation in terms of generalized hypergeometric functions. Below we will derive them using the approach that was exploited for continuous
d
-orthogonal polynomials. These representations are new except for the cases of Charlier and Meixner polynomials.

We again factor the polynomial
R(H)
into
R(H)=\rho\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m}(H+\alpha_{j}+1)
.

Theorem 3.8
.

The discrete
d
-orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}(x)
have the following hypergeometric representations:

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}(-x)_{n}\,{}_{d+1}F_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}-n,(-n-\alpha_{d})\\
x-n+1\end{matrix};(-1)^{d+1}\rho\right],

(3.9)

and

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=[\alpha_{d}+1]_{n}\,{}_{2}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-n,-x\\
(\alpha_{d}+1)\end{matrix};\rho\right].

(3.10)

Proof.

First we transform the formula for
P_{n}(x)
to get rid of the difference operators. We are going to exploit again the formula

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{G^{j}\psi(x,n)}{j!}\qquad\text{with}\quad\psi(x,n)=(-1)^{n}(-x)_{n}.

Using that

\displaystyle G\psi(x,n)=nR(n-1)\psi(x,n-1),

we obtain

\displaystyle G^{j}\psi(x,n)=\rho^{j}(-1)^{n-j}(-x)_{n-j}(-1)^{j}(-n)_{j}(-1)^{j}[-\alpha_{d}-n]_{j}.

Let us use the formula

\displaystyle(-1)^{n-j}(-x)_{n-j}=\frac{(-x)_{n}(-1)^{n}}{(x-n+1)_{j}}.

(3.11)

We obtain that

\displaystyle G^{j}\psi(x,n)=(-1)^{n}(-x)_{n}\frac{\rho^{j}(-1)^{dj+j}(-n)_{j}[-\alpha_{d}-n]_{j}}{(x-n+1)_{j}}.

For the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
this gives the expression

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}(-x)_{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{\rho^{j}(-1)^{(d+1)j}(-n)_{j}[-\alpha_{d}-n]_{j}}{(x-n+1)_{j}}{(x-n+1)_{j}j!},

which is (
3.9
).

The second hypergeometric expression can be obtained as in the continuous case. Using again (
3.5
) we find

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{m}(\alpha_{k}+1)_{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}\frac{(-1)^{n-j}\rho^{j}(-x)_{n-j}}{j![-\alpha_{d}-n]_{n-j}}{(x-n+1)_{j}}.

After standard manipulations that we used for the continuous
d
-orthogonal polynomials we come to the second formula (
3.10
).
∎

We can obtain hypergeometric representations for the class of discrete
d
-orthogonal polynomials, constructed via
q(G)
, where
G=R(H)\Delta
. Again we will treat the case when
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
. Let us put

\displaystyle\eta_{1}=\frac{(-1)^{(d+1)l}\eta}{l}.

Theorem 3.9
.

In the case when
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
have the following representation in hypergeometric functions

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}(-x)_{n}\,{}_{dl+l}F_{l}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(l;-n),(\Delta(l;-n-\alpha))\\
\Delta(l;x-n+1)\end{matrix};\eta_{1}^{l}\right].

(3.12)

Proof.

The proof needs a few changes in comparison with the continuous case but otherwise is straightforward. We are going to use again the series defining the polynomials

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{G^{jl}\psi(x,n)}{j!}.

A formula, similar to the one in the continuous case holds

\displaystyle G^{lj}(-1)^{n}(-x)_{n}=\prod_{s=0}^{lj-1}(n-s)R(n-1-s)(-x)_{n-lj}(-1)^{n-lj}.

For the term
(-x)_{n-lj}
we use (
3.11
) with
lj
instead of
j

\displaystyle(-1)^{n-lj}(-x)_{n-lj}=\frac{(-1)^{n}(-x)_{n}}{(x-n+1)_{lj}}.

The factor
(x-n+1)_{lj}
can be presented as indicated in Section
3.2

\displaystyle(x-n+1)_{lj}=l^{lj}\prod_{r=0}^{l-1}\left(\frac{x-n+r+1}{l}\right)_{j}.

This gives

\displaystyle(-1)^{n-lj}(-x)_{n-lj}=\frac{(-1)^{n}(-x)_{n}}{l^{lj}\prod\limits_{r=0}^{l-1}\left(\frac{x-n+r+1}{l}\right)_{j}}.

(3.13)

Using (
3.7
) for
\prod\limits_{s=0}^{lj-1}(n-s)R(n-1-s)
and expressing the last factor by (
3.13
) in the sum for the polynomials we obtain the desired formula (
3.12
).
∎

Again we can find a second formula for the
d
-orthogonal polynomials, corresponding to
q(G)=\rho G^{l}
. We use the notations (
3.2
) from Section
3.1
. With this notation we have

Theorem 3.10
.

The polynomials
P_{n}(x)
, where
n=ml+i
have the following hypergeometric representation

\displaystyle P_{ml+i}(x)=(-1)^{mdl+1}C(i)(-x)_{i}\prod_{k=1}^{ld+l-1}\,{}_{l}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m,\Delta(l;-x+i-1)\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};-\eta_{1}^{-l}\right],

(3.14)

where
C(i)
was defined in Corollary
3.7
.

Proof.

We start with the formula

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod\limits_{s=0}^{lj-1}(n-s)R(n-1-s)(-1)^{n-lj}(-x)_{n-lj}}{j!}.

We transform the expression

\displaystyle\prod_{s=0}^{lj-1}(n-s)R(n-1-s)

using (
3.7
) to obtain

\displaystyle(-1)^{lj}\eta^{lj}(-m)_{j}\prod_{\beta\in I}(-m-S_{\beta}(i))_{j}.

We further transform the last expression using (
3.5
) into

\displaystyle(-1)^{l(d+1)j}\eta^{lj}\frac{(-1)^{j}m!}{(m-j)!}\frac{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{m}}{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{m-j}}.

(3.15)

Next we transform the factor
(-x)_{n-lj}
as follows. First

\displaystyle(-1)^{n-jl}(-x)_{n-lj}=(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}(-1)^{ml-jl}(-x+i)_{lm-lj}.

Then we present the last factor in the right-hand side of the last formula as

\displaystyle(-x+i)_{lm-lj}=l^{(m-j)l}\prod_{r=0}^{l-1}\left(\frac{-x+i-r}{l}\right)_{m-j}.

At the end plugging the last formula and (
3.15
) into the sum for
P_{n}(x)
and changing the summation index

we obtain

\displaystyle P_{ml+i}=C(i)(-x)_{i}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{(-m)_{j}\prod\limits_{r=0}^{l-1}\left(\frac{-x+i-r}{l}\right)_{j}}{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta})_{j}}\frac{\big[{-}\eta_{1}^{-l}\big]^{j}}{j!},

which is (
3.14
).
∎

4.1
Continuous GGH Polynomials

In what follows we use a different normalization of the
d
-orthogonal polynomials. Namely, we introduce the polynomials

\displaystyle Q_{n}(x):=x^{i}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\left[\frac{x}{(-1)^{d+1}\eta\rho}\right]^{l}\right].

(4.1)

They differ from the polynomials
P_{n}(x)
, given by (
3.8
) by a multiplicative constant. We also assume that
((-1)^{d+1}\eta)^{l}=-1
which can be achieved by rescaling of
x
together with multiplication by a suitable constant. Our first result is as follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

For a given positive integer
i
, the function
\Phi_{i}(x,t)
defined as

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(x,t):=(tx)^{i}e^{t^{l}}\,{}_{0}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};(xt)^{l}\right],

(4.2)

generates the polynomial system
\{Q_{lm+i}(x)\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
by means of the formula

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(x,t)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{ml+i}}{m!}Q_{ml+i}(x).

Proof.

Consider the series

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{ml+i}}{m!}Q_{ml+i}(x)=t^{i}x^{i}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{ml}}{m!}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};-x^{l}\right].

One can present the sum in the right-hand side of the latter equation in the form of a double series

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{ml+i}}{m!}Q_{ml+i}(x)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{ml}}{m!}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}\frac{(-m)_{j}}{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{j}}\frac{\big({-}x^{l}\big)^{j}}{j!}.

Changing the order of summation in the double series, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big({-}x^{l}\big)^{j}}{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{j}j!}\sum\limits_{m=j}^{\infty}\frac{(-m)_{j}t^{ml}}{m!}.

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\frac{(-m)_{j}}{m!}=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(m-j)!}.

Hence after introducing a new index
s=m-j
of summation, the double series becomes

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(xt)^{lj}}{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{j}j!}\sum\limits_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{sl}}{s!}.

This gives for the double sum

\displaystyle{}_{0}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};(xt)^{l}\right]e^{t^{l}},

which implies (
4.2
) for the function
\Phi_{i}(x,t)
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We notice that the hypergeometric functions without upper parameters appear in a quite different bispectral problem. In
[
11
]
we defined the functions
\Psi_{\beta}(x,z)
, which are solutions of an equation of the form

\displaystyle x^{-N}(\theta-\beta_{1})\cdots(\theta-\beta_{N})\Psi_{\beta}(x,z)=z^{N}\Psi_{\beta}(x,z),

where

and
\beta_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
. We called them generalized Bessel functions. As one of the referees kindly informed me these functions have been studied by P. Delerue in
[

]
, and are called hyper-Bessel functions. They are expressed in terms of the hypergeometric functions without upper parameters (in
[
11
]
we used Meijer’s
G

I hope that such a connection exists and in this case it would be useful in the studies of Darboux transformation of the generalized Gould–Hopper polynomials. Even in the case of

From the above formulas we can write a generating function for the entire family

\displaystyle\Phi(x,t)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{l-1}\Phi_{i}(x,t)=e^{t^{l}}\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}(xt)^{i}\cdot{}_{0}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\

The function

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{\lceil{n/l}\rceil!}Q_{n}(x)=\Phi(x,t).

The proof is obvious and we omit it.

Remark 4.4
.

Polynomial systems that have a generating function of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(x)t^{n}=A(t)B(xt).

are called Brenke polynomials, see, e.g.,
[

It deserves to write separately the formula for the case

Corollary 4.5

(

When

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}Q_{n}(x)={}_{0}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\

We are going to obtain a second formula based on a theorem from

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
-a\notin{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p}
,
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q}
be complex numbers such that the hypergeometric function

\displaystyle{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left[\begin{matrix}-n,(\alpha_{p})\\
a+1,(\beta_{q})\end{matrix};x\right]

be well defined. Then the following formula holds

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{a+n}{n}\,{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left[\begin{matrix}-n,(\alpha_{p})\\
a+1,(\beta_{q})\end{matrix};x\right]t^{n}=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{a+1}}\,{}_{p}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}(\alpha_{p})\\
(\beta_{q})\end{matrix};\frac{xt}{1-t}\right].

(4.3)

See
[
55
]
for a simple proof.

We again use the
d
-orthogonal polynomials
Q_{n}(x)
from (

) as well as the convention
\eta=1
.

Theorem 4.7
.

The function
G_{i}
given by

\displaystyle G_{i}(x,t)=\frac{(tx)^{i}}{1-t^{l}}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\frac{(xt)^{l}}{1-t^{l}}\right],

generates the polynomials
Q_{lm+i}(x)
,
m=0,1,\ldots
in the following way

\displaystyle G_{i}(x,t)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}t^{ml+i}Q_{ml+i}(x).

Proof.

Let us multiply the polynomials
Q_{lm+i}
by
t^{ml+i}
and sum. Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}t^{ml+i}Q_{ml+i}(x)=t^{i}x^{i}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}t^{ml}\,{}_{2}F_{ld+l}\left[\begin{matrix}-m,1\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big),1\end{matrix};x^{l}\right].

Here we have used that

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}(\alpha_{p})\\
(\beta_{q})\end{matrix};x\right]={}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left[\begin{matrix}(\alpha_{p}),\mu\\
(\beta_{q}),\mu\end{matrix};x\right],\qquad\mu\neq 0.

We apply the above cited formula (
4.3
) from
[

]
with

to obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}t^{ml+i}Q_{ml+i}(x)=\frac{(tx)^{i}}{1-t^{l}}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\frac{(xt)^{l}}{1-t^{l}}\right].

∎

From this theorem we can write a generating function for all polynomials

Just sum up the generating functions

for

.
∎

4.2
Discrete GGH polynomials

In the discrete case there is nothing special. We follow the arguments for the continuous case. However we will keep the coefficient
\rho
. We again put
n=ml+i
and fix
i
. We use the following modification of the polynomials (

)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(x)=(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}\,{}_{1+l}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m,\Delta(l;-x+i-1)\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\eta_{1}^{l}\right],

which differ from

only by a multiplicative constant.

Theorem 4.10
.

The function
\Phi_{i}
given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(x,t)=(-1)^{j}(-x)_{i}t^{i}e^{t^{l}}\,{}_{l}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(l;-x+i)\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};-(\eta_{1}t)^{l}\right].

is a generating function for the polynomials
Q_{lm+i}(y)
in the sense that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{ml+i}}{m!}Q_{ml+i}(x)=\Phi_{i}(x,t).

Proof.

We write the defining series as

\displaystyle(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{ml+i}}{m!}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}\frac{(-m)_{j}(\Delta(l;-x+i))_{j}\eta_{1}^{jl}}{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{j}j!}.

In the right-hand side we make the following transformations. We first change the order of the summation and then introduce a new summation variable
m\to s=m-j
. We obtain

\displaystyle(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}t^{i}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}\frac{(\Delta(l;-x+i))_{j}\big[\eta_{1}^{l}]^{j}}{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{j}j!}\sum\limits_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-s-j)_{j}t^{(s+j)l}}{(s+j)!}.

Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{(-s-j)_{j}}{(s+j)!}=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{s!}.

This gives

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(x,t)=(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}t^{i}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}\frac{\eta_{1}^{lj}(-1)^{j}t^{lj}}{\prod\limits_{\beta\in I}(S_{\beta}(i))_{j}j!}\sum\limits_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{sl}}{s!},

which is exactly the desired formula.
∎

As in the continuous case we are going to derive a second formula.

Theorem 4.11
.

The function
G_{i}
given by

\displaystyle G_{i}(x,t)=\frac{t^{i}(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}}{1-t}\,{}_{1+l}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(l;-x+i),1\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};-\left[\frac{(-1)^{d}}{\rho}\right]^{l}\right]

generates the polynomials
Q_{lm+i}(y)
in the sense that

\displaystyle G_{i}(x,t)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}t^{m}Q_{ml+i}(x).

Proof.

We are going to use again Proposition
4.6
. It is obvious that we need to put

. We have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}t^{m}Q_{ml+i}(x)=(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}t^{m}\;{}_{2+l}F_{ld+l}\left[\begin{matrix}-m,\Delta(l;-x+i-1),1\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big),1\end{matrix};\eta_{1}^{l}\right].

Then (
4.3
) gives

\displaystyle(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}t^{ml+i}\;{}_{2+l}F_{ld+l}\left[\begin{matrix}-m,\Delta(l;-x+i-1),1\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big),1\end{matrix};\eta_{1}^{l}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{(-1)^{i}(-x)_{i}t^{i}}{1-t^{l}}\,{}_{1+l}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(l;-x+i-1),1\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\frac{\eta_{1}^{l}}{1-t^{l}}\right].

∎

As a trivial corollary

Finally for

5
Mehler–Heine type formulas

In this section, we are going to consider only the continuous
d
-orthogonal polynomials and, without loss of generality, we will assume that
\rho l^{d+1}=1
. (This assumption will make the formulas simpler.)
There are many ways to write down the Mehler–Heine type formulas depending on the normalization of the polynomials of which we choose only the simplest one.

Let us start with the case of continuous
d
-orthogonal polynomials corresponding to
q(G)=G
and use the polynomials
Q_{n}(x)
given by (
4.1
).

Theorem 5.1
.

For the
d
-orthogonal polynomials obtained from
q(G)=G
, the following Mehler–Heine type formula holds

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}(x/n)={}_{0}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
(\alpha_{d}+1)\end{matrix};x\right].

(5.1)

Proof.

Notice that in the case
l=1
, equation (
4.3
) can be written as

\displaystyle Q_{n}(x)={}_{1}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-n\\
(\alpha_{d}+1)\end{matrix};-x\right].

Then we use the formula

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}{}_{p+1}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}(\alpha_{p}),a\lambda\\
(\beta_{q})\end{matrix};\frac{x}{\lambda}\right]={}_{p}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}(\alpha_{p})\\
(\beta_{q})\end{matrix};ax\right],

with
\lambda=n
,
a=-1
, see, e.g.,
[
41
, p. 5]
. This immediately gives (
5.1
).
∎

Remark 5.2
.

This theorem is proved in
[
59
]
. We present it here as an illustration of the results of the present paper. Also the Mehler–Heine formula for general GGP follows from it.

As one of the referees kindly pointed to me, the polynomials
Q_{n}(x)
considered here all are Jensen polynomials. This means that they are associated to an
entire function
\varphi(x)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}
in the following way

\displaystyle Q_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\gamma_{j}x^{j}.

Also the function
e^{t}\varphi(xt)
is their generating function:

\displaystyle e^{t}\varphi(xt)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!},

see, e.g.,
[
23
]
for a contemporary reference to properties of Jensen polynomials that we refer to here. In our case

\displaystyle\varphi(x)={}_{0}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
(\alpha_{d}+1)\end{matrix};x\right].

By the properties of the Jensen polynomials we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}(x/n)=\varphi(x)={}_{0}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
(\alpha_{d}+1)\end{matrix};x\right]

locally uniformly as was found by Jensen himself in 1913.

Observe that the hypergeometric function

\displaystyle\varphi(x)={}_{0}F_{d}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
(\alpha_{d}+1)\end{matrix};x\right]

appears once again naturally as in the formulas for the generating functions for the polynomial system
\{Q_{n}(x)\}
. In both cases this is connected to the fact that they are Jensen polynomials.

Now consider the polynomials obtained by the automorphisms
\sigma_{q}
, where
q(G)
is some polynomial. We recall that they are given by

\displaystyle P_{n}(x):=e^{q(G)}x^{n}=x^{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k}(G)x^{n}}{k!}.

We will restrict ourselves to the case
q(G)=G^{l}
for which the corresponding hypergeometric representation was obtained in Section
3
. Presenting
n
as
n=ml+i
and using (
4.1
) for the polynomials
Q_{n}(x)
, we get

\displaystyle Q_{n}(x)=x^{i}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\big[(-1)^{d+1}x\big]^{l}\right].

Theorem 5.3
.

For the
d
-orthogonal polynomials obtained via the automorphisms
\sigma_{q}
with
q(G)=G^{l}
, the following Mehler–Heine type formula holds

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{m\to\infty}m^{i/l}Q_{n}\big(x/m^{1/l}\big)=x^{i}\,{}_{0}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\big[(-1)^{d+1}x\big]^{l}\right].

(5.2)

Proof.

We will use the hypergeometric representation (
3.8
). Consider the expression
Q_{n}\big(x/m^{1/l}\big)
which gives

\displaystyle Q_{ml+i}\big(x/m^{1/l}\big)=\frac{x^{i}}{m^{i/l}}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\frac{\big[(-1)^{d+1}x\big]^{l}}{m}\right].

The latter formula can be rewritten in the form

\displaystyle m^{i/l}Q_{ml+i}\big(x/m^{1/l}\big)=x^{i}\,{}_{1}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\frac{\big[(-1)^{d+1}x\big]^{l}}{m}\right].

Then formula (
5.2
) follows from (
5.1
).
∎

It is worth noticing that the asymptotics depends on the remainder
n

({\rm mod}~l)
which indicates that there is probably no general asymptotic formula, but it might exist for the subsequence with the same value of the remainder
n

({\rm mod}~l)
. This phenomenon is well-known in the case of Hermite polynomials, where the even-indexed and the odd-indexed polynomials have different asymptotics, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
.

Notice that as we explained in Remark
4.2
the function

\displaystyle{}_{0}F_{ld+l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};\big[(-1)^{d+1}x\big]^{l}\right]

is also a generalized Bessel function in the sense of
[
11
]
.

6
Examples

Example 6.1

(Gould–Hopper polynomials)
.

Consider the simplest case
R(H)\in{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.,
G=\partial
. Then for
q=\tau G^{l}
, using equation (
3.6
) we obtain the polynomial system

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=x^{n}\,{}_{l}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}\dfrac{-n}{l},\ldots,\dfrac{-n+l-1}{l}\\
-\end{matrix};\tau\left(\frac{-l}{x}\right)^{l}\right].

These polynomials coincide with the well-known Gould–Hopper polynomials
g^{l}_{n}(x,\tau)
, cf.
[
33
,
46
]
.

According to our scheme they are the eigenfunctions of the differential operator

\displaystyle L=l\tau\partial^{l}+x\partial

and satisfy the recurrence relation

\displaystyle xg^{l}_{n}(x,\tau)=g^{l}_{n+1}(x,\tau)-\tau ln(n-1)\cdots(n-l+2)g^{l}_{n-l+1}(x,\tau).

Using the second form of hypergeometric representation (
3.8
) we can also express them as

\displaystyle g_{ml+i}^{l}(x)=x^{i}{}_{1}F_{l-1}\left[\begin{matrix}-m\\
\big(\hat{S}(i)\big)\end{matrix};-\tau\left(\frac{x}{l}\right)^{l}\right].

Observe that for
l=2
, these polynomials coincide (up to rescaling) with the classical Hermite polynomials. Eventually the Gould–Hopper polynomials turned out to be quite useful in quantum mechanics, integrable systems (Novikov–Vesselov equation), combinatorics, etc., see, e.g.,
[
21
,
24
,
62
]
.

The cases with
G=R(H)\partial
with arbitrary
R
can be considered as generalizations of the Gould–Hopper polynomials. In this situation we use
q(G)=G^{l}
, where
G=R(H)\partial
,
R
being a polynomial of an arbitrary degree. The corresponding expression for these polynomials provided by (
3.6
) is as follows

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=x^{n}\,{}_{dl+l}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(l;-n),(\Delta(l;-n-\alpha))\\
-\end{matrix};\left(\frac{(-l)^{d+1}\rho}{x}\right)^{l}\right].

We can explicitly write discrete analogs of the (generalized) Gould–Hopper polynomials. Namely,

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=(x)_{n}\,{}_{dl+l}F_{l}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(l;-n),(\Delta(l;-n-\alpha))\\
\Delta(l;x-n)\end{matrix};\big((-1)^{(d+1)}l^{d}\rho\big)^{l}\right].

The most straightforward analog, which one might call the
discrete Gould–Hopper polynomials
, corresponds to
G=\tau\Delta^{l}
(
d=0
). These polynomials have the following hypergeometric representation

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)=(x)_{n}\,{}_{l}F_{l}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(l;-n)\\
\Delta(l;x-n)\end{matrix};(-l\rho)^{l}\right].

Having in mind the existing applications of the Gould–Hopper polynomials it is worth checking whether their generalized versions have similar or other applications.

Example 6.2

(Konhauser–Toscano polynomials)
.

In
[
42
]
Konhauser has defined two families of polynomials denoted by
Y^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)
and
Z^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)
,
n=0,\ldots
, where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The polynomials
Z^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)
are in fact polynomials in
x^{l}
. The polynomials
Y^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)
are polynomials in the original variable
x
.

These two families are biorthogonal with respect to the weight function corresponding to the Laguerre polynomials, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha}e^{-x}Y^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)Z^{\alpha}_{m}(x;l){\rm d}x=h_{m}\delta_{n,m}

with
h_{m}\neq 0
. The polynomials
Z^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)
were introduced earlier by Toscano, see
[
58
]
. Their hypergeometric representation

\displaystyle Z^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)=\dbinom{\alpha+ln}{ln}\frac{(ln)!}{n!}\,{}_{1}F_{l}\left[\begin{matrix}-n\\
\Delta(l;\alpha+1)\end{matrix};(x/l)^{l}\right],

found in
[
46
]
shows that they can be constructed using the methods of the present paper. Let us consider
G=R(H)\partial
, withl
R(H)=\prod\limits^{l}_{s=1}\big(H+\frac{\alpha+s}{l}\big)
. Then

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\alpha}(x;l)=P_{n}\big((x/l)^{l}\big).

In the case
l=2
the polynomials were discovered much earlier by L.V. Spencer and U. Fano
[
54
]
in their studies of the
X
-rays diffusion.

Remark 6.3
.

In
[
42
]
Konhauser has proven that the polynomials
Y^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)
are the eigenfunctions of a differential operator of order
l+1
and that they satisfy a
(l+2)
-recurrence relation of the form

\displaystyle x^{l}Y^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)=\sum\limits_{j=-1}^{l}\gamma_{j}(n)Y^{\alpha}_{n+j}(x;l).

The polynomials
Z^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)
satisfy a relation of the form

\displaystyle x^{l}Z^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)=\sum\limits_{j=-1}^{l}\beta_{j}(n)Z^{\alpha}_{n-j}(x;l).

(This relation follows from the definition
Z_{n}(x;l):=P^{R}_{n}\big((x/l)^{l}\big)
and the properties of
P^{R}_{n}\big((x/l)^{l}\big)
.)

As mentioned earlier the Konhauser–Toscano polynomials have applications to random matrix theory. The so-called Borodin–Muttalib ensembles
[
20
,
50
]
are based on their biorthogonality.

In fact the polynomials
Y^{\alpha}_{n}(x;l)
,
n=0,1,\ldots,l-1
are closely related with the functionals, which define the
d
-orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}(x)
.

It is quite interesting to define discrete analogs for the above Konhauser polynomials.

Example 6.4

(matching polynomials of graphs)
.

Polynomial systems considered in this example are taken from
[
9
]
and they are relevant for the so-called chemical graph theory.

We need some notions from the graph theory, see, e.g.,
[
9
,
27
,
30
]
and the references therein. Let
K
be a connected graph with
n
vertices. Following
[
52
]
we define the higher Hosoya number
p_{r}(K,j)
as the number of ways one can select
j
non-incident paths of length
r
in
K
. Using the Hosoya numbers, the higher-order matching polynomial
M_{r}(K)
of
K
is defined by the relation

\displaystyle M_{r}(K):=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\lceil{\frac{n}{r+1}}\rceil}(-1)^{j}p_{r}(K,j)x^{n-(r+1)j},

see
[
9
,
30
]
. When
K=K_{n}
is the complete graph on
n
vertices (i.e., each pair of vertices is connected by an edge) the corresponding polynomials were explicitly computed in
[
9
,
30
]
. Using combinatorial arguments it was shown that these polynomials are given by

\displaystyle M_{r}(K_{n})=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\frac{n!x^{n-(r+1)j}}{(n-(r+1)j)!j!2^{j}}.

If we compare this expression with

\displaystyle P_{n}=e^{-G^{r+1}/2}x^{n},

where
G=\partial_{x}
, we see that they coincide. Hence we obtain a hypergeometric representation

\displaystyle M_{r}(K_{n})=x^{n}{}_{r+1}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(r+1;-n)\\
-\end{matrix};\frac{(-1)^{r}(r+1)^{r+1}}{2x^{r+1}}\right].

(This representation was earlier found in
[
9
]
.) We see that the matching polynomials of complete graphs are the eigenfunctions of a linear differential operator and that they satisfy an
(r+2)
-term recurrence relation which can be useful in the studies of these polynomials.

The case
r=1
deserves a special attention since it corresponds to the Hermite polynomials as was observed long ago, e.g., in
[
9
,
34
]
. The corresponding coefficients
p_{1}(K,j)
are the original Hosoya numbers
[
39
]
.

Let us consider another example of matching polynomials, this time of complete bipartite graphs
K_{n,m}
,
n\geq m
with
n+m
vertices. (Recall that
K_{n,m}
is a graph whose vertices are split into two nonintersecting sets
V_{n}
and
V_{m}
with
n
and
m
elements resp. and every vertex in
V_{n}
is connected to every vertex in
V_{m}
. We consider the case when
r
is odd. Then
[
9
,
30
]
contain the formula

\displaystyle M_{r}(K_{n,m})=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{jr}\binom{m}{jr}\frac{(-1)^{j}((jr)!)^{2}}{j!}x^{n-2rj},

which can easily be transformed into

\displaystyle M_{r}(K_{n,m})=x^{n+m}\,{}_{r+1}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(r+1;-n),\Delta(r+1;-m)\\
-\end{matrix};\frac{-(r+1)^{r+1}}{(2x)^{r+1}}\right].

\displaystyle M_{r}(K_{n,m})=x^{2n-M}\,{}_{r+1}F_{0}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta(r+1;-n),\Delta(r+1;-n+M)\\
-\end{matrix};\frac{-(r+1)^{r+1}}{(2x)^{r+1}}\right].

While the hypergeometric representation is known, the properties of these polynomials listed in Theorem
2.3
are new. In particular, the differential equation and the recurrence relations they satisfy seem to be new.

Notice that for
