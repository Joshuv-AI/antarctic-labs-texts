Near-optimal Closed-loop Method via Lyapunov Damping for Convex Optimization
Severin Maier Camille Castera Department of MathematicsUniversity of TübingenGermany Peter Ochs Department of Mathematics and Computer ScienceSaarland UniversityGermany

Abstract
We introduce an autonomous system with closed-loop damping for first-order convex optimization. While, to this day, optimal rates of convergence are almost exclusively achieved by non-autonomous methods via open-loop damping ( e.g. , Nesterov’s algorithm), we show that our system, featuring a closed-loop damping, exhibits a rate arbitrarily close to the optimal one. We do so by coupling the damping and the speed of convergence of the system via a well-chosen Lyapunov function. By discretizing our system we then derive an algorithm and present numerical experiments supporting our theoretical findings.

Introduction

We consider the unconstrained minimization of a smooth convex real-valued and lower-bounded function
f
over a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{H}}f(x).

One of the most famous algorithms for such optimization problems is Nesterov’s Accelerated Gradient method (NAG)
[
27
]
, which is known to achieve the optimal rate of convergence for first-order methods on convex functions with Lipschitz-continuous gradient. Among several ways to explain the efficiency of NAG,
Su et al. 2014
studied the algorithm through the lens of Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs) and proposed the following model:

\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{a}{t}\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0,\qquad\forall t\geq t_{0},

(
\text{AVD}_{a}
)

where
a>0
,
t_{0}\geq 0
and
\nabla f
denotes the gradient of the smooth real-valued convex function
f
.
Here,
\dot{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}
, respectively
\ddot{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}x}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}
, denotes the first, resp. second, time-derivative (or velocity, resp. acceleration) of the solution
x
of the ODE.
AVD stands for Asymptotically Vanishing Damping and relates to the coefficient
a/t
.
NAG is obtained by (non-straightforward) discretization of (

AVD
a

). Conversely (

AVD
a

) can be seen as NAG with infinitesimal step-sizes. Following
[
33
]
, many works studied the system (

AVD
a

), and notably proved that when
a>3
, the function values along a solution (or trajectory) of (

AVD
a

) converge with the asymptotic rate
o\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right)
to the optimal value as
t\to+\infty

[
33
,
6
,
26
]
. This matches the rate of NAG in the case of discrete (or iterative) algorithms
[
27
]
. We further discuss additional results for (

AVD
a

) and other possible choices of
a
later in Section
2
.

The interest in the connection between ODEs (or dynamical systems) and algorithms comes from the abundance of theory and tools for analyzing ODEs and the insights that they provide for understanding algorithms.
In particular, continuous-time analyses relying on Lyapunov functions can often be adapted to the discrete setting, see
e.g.
,
[
6
]
.
In this work, we are interested in a special case of the Inertial Damped Gradient (IDG) system:

\ddot{x}(t)+\gamma(t)\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0,\qquad\forall t\geq t_{0},

(
\text{IDG}_{\gamma}
)

where
\gamma
is a positive function and is called “damping” by analogy with mechanics.
There are two inherently different ways of designing the damping coefficient
\gamma
: the so-called open- and closed-loop manners.
Optimal convergence rates are usually obtained via open-loop damping, for example, when
\gamma(t)=\frac{a}{t}
with
a>3
in (

AVD
a

). Achieving the same with a closed-loop damping is significantly more challenging as explained hereafter.
We propose a closed-loop damping that provides (

IDG
γ

) with near-optimal rate of convergence. Our special instance of (

IDG
γ

) is called (
LD
) and is introduced below.
We first discuss the problem setup in more details.

1.1
Problem setting

The damping
\frac{a}{t}
in (

AVD
a

) depends explicitly on the time variable
t
, making the system non-autonomous.
We say that a damping with such explicit dependence on the time
t
is
open-loop
.
In contrast, a damping
\gamma
that does not explicitly depend on
t
is called
closed-loop
and makes (

IDG
γ

) an autonomous ODE (since the ODE is then independent of
t_{0}
):
the feedback for the system, in terms of
\gamma
, may only depend on the state
x
(and its derivatives) but
not explicitly
on the time
t
.
For optimization, autonomous ODEs are often preferable over non-autonomous ones, since the dependence on the time
t
, and hence on the initial time
t_{0}
, is removed. For example, even though the asymptotic rate of convergence of (

AVD
a

) does not depend on
t_{0}
, the trajectory does depend on the choice of
t_{0}
: when choosing a large
t_{0}
, the damping
a/t
is very small at all time and the “damping effect” is almost completely lost. We illustrate such a pathological behavior in Figure
1
.
Additionally some important tools for analyzing ODEs only hold (or are simpler to use) for autonomous ODEs, see
e.g.
, the Hartman–Grobman Theorem
[
21
,
20
]
or the so-called ODE method
[
24
,
14
]
, which formally states that algorithms with vanishing step-sizes asymptotically behave like solutions of corresponding ODEs.

In this work we therefore tackle the following question:

Can one design the damping
\gamma
in (

IDG
γ

) in a closed-loop manner (so as to make the ODE autonomous) while still achieving the optimal convergence rate of (

AVD
a

)?

Figure 1:
Comparison of our autonomous system (
LD
) and the non-autonomous one (

AVD
a

) for different initial times
t_{0}
on the 2D function
f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{4}+0.1x_{2}^{4}
. The left plot shows the evolution of the function values over time. The right plot shows the trajectories in the space
(x_{1},x_{2})
. Different initial times heavily affect the solution of (

AVD
a

), but not (
LD
). We approximated the solutions using NAG for (

AVD
a

) and LYDIA (see Algorithm
1
) for (
LD
), both with very small step-sizes.

This question hides a circular argument that makes it hard to solve. Indeed, according to
Cabot et al. 2009
, a sufficient condition on
\gamma
for the convergence of the function values is that
\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\gamma(t)\mathrm{d}t=+\infty
.
The straightforward choice is then the constant damping
\gamma(t)=a>0
, which in (

IDG
γ

) yields the Heavy Ball with Friction (HBF) algorithm
[
30
]
, known to be sub-optimal for convex functions.
We therefore seek a closed-loop damping that converges to zero, but not “too fast”.
In the open-loop setting, the natural choice is
\gamma(t)=\frac{a}{t},\ a>0
because this is the fastest polynomial decay that converges to zero while being non-integrable.
Further
[
4
]
showed that any damping of the form
\frac{a}{t^{\beta}}
with
\beta\in(0,1)
yields a sub-optimal rate.
Therefore we formally seek to design a closed-loop damping
\gamma
that behaves like
\frac{1}{t}
.
Because it cannot depend directly on the variable
t
, our closed-loop damping
\gamma
must be built from other quantities of the system that may converge to zero, for example
\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert
.
However, the rate at which such quantities converge to zero depend themselves on the choice of
\gamma
.
To escape this loop of thought, we design
\gamma
using quantities that behave asymptotically like
\frac{1}{t}
in the case of (

AVD
a

) and investigate whether this choice of
\gamma
still gives fast convergence rates. This idea takes inspiration from
[
10
]
which used
\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert
as damping, with a key difference that we now explain.

1.2
Our contribution

In the case of (

AVD
a

) with
a>3
, it is known that under standard assumptions the quantity
E(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(x(t))-f^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}
(where
f^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\min_{\mathcal{H}}f
) is such that
E(t)=o\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right)

[
26
]
. Therefore, following the aforementioned intuition, we propose a closed-loop damping defined for all
t\geq t_{0}
by
\gamma(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{E(t)}
.
Our version of (

IDG
γ

) then reads:

\ddot{x}(t)+\sqrt{E(t)}\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0,\qquad\forall t\geq t_{0},

(LD)

and is called (
LD
) for
Lyapunov Damping
. It will indeed turn out that
E
is non-increasing, making it a “Lyapunov function” for (
LD
).
Note that (
LD
) assumes the availability of the optimal value
f^{\star}
, we further discuss this later in Remark
4.10
.
Our main result is that our closed-loop damping system (
LD
) yields the following rate of convergence.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
f
is a continuously differentiable convex function and that
\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f\neq\emptyset
. Then, for any bounded solution
x
of (
LD
) and for any
\delta>0
,

f(x(t))-f^{\star}=o\left(\frac{1}{t^{2-\delta}}\right).

This means that the rate of our method is arbitrarily close to
o\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right)
, the optimal one achieved by (

AVD
a

) with
a>3
.
Also, note that (
LD
) does not make use of the hyper-parameter
a
, whose choice is crucial in (

AVD
a

).

Organization

In Section
2
we review related work regarding ODEs for optimization, closed-loop damping, and known results in the open-loop case. In Section
3

). Section
4
is devoted to showing our main result. In Section

) and use it to perform numerical experiments. We finish by drawing conclusions and further discussing our results.

2
Related Work

ODEs for optimization

There is a long line of work in exploiting the interplay between ODEs and optimization algorithms, going back, at least, to the work of Polyak’s
[
30
]
Heavy Ball with Friction (HBF) method for acceleration. As previously stated,
Su et al. 2014
linked NAG
[
27
]
to the differential equation (

AVD
a

), hence providing a new view on the heavily used, yet not perfectly understood algorithm. NAG is however obtained via a non-straightforward discretization of (

AVD
a

) since the gradient of
f
is evaluated at an “extrapolated point”. Recently,
Alecsa et al. 2021
proposed a model with “implicit Hessian”, whose Euler explicit discretization yields NAG. Higher-order ODEs have also been introduced to better understand NAG:
Attouch et al. 2016b
proposed a model with “Hessian damping” based on
[
3
]
, while
Shi et al. 2021
similarly considered higher-resolution ODEs allowing to better distinguish NAG from other (

IDG
γ

) systems.

Closed-loop damping

The closest work to ours is
[
10
]
, which proposed a closed-loop damping of the form
\gamma=\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}\right\rVert^{p}
for several values of
p\in\mathbb{R}
.
Our work builds on
[
10
]
since our damping
\sqrt{E}
also involves
\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}\right\rVert
. Yet,
Attouch et al. 2022
could provide counterexamples for which their systems do not achieve near-optimal rates, unlike ours. A key difference is that our damping
\sqrt{E}
is non-increasing, while
\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}\right\rVert
may oscillate heavily.
They nonetheless derived rates under additional assumptions (
e.g.
strong convexity or the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property).

Another line of work regarding closed-loop damping consists in deriving them from a feedback loop that follows an algebraic equation. This was initiated by
[
7
]
for proximal Newton’s method and then used for first-order optimization by
[
23
]
then generalized by
[
11
]
. These works rely on a so-called “time-rescaling” technique, which formally amounts to accelerating convergence by making time go faster. This allows obtaining optimal convergence rates in the ODE setting, with the drawback that this acceleration is
artificial
: it cannot be transferred to algorithms via explicit discretization.
1
1

1

Discretizing time-rescaling amounts to use larger step-sizes which is not possible without causing numerical instability issues.
By combining this approach with “time-averaging”,
[
23
,
11
]
could further obtain “non-artificial” acceleration and optimal rates from the ODE perspective. They then derived practical algorithms that are faster than the sub-optimal
O(\frac{1}{k})
of gradient descent but still sub-optimal compared to NAG. Furthermore
[
11
]
must resort to using implicit discretization (via proximal operators) which is expensive in general. In contrast our approach does not involve time-rescaling, which makes it possible to use explicit Euler discretization ensuring numerical stability under standard assumptions (see Theorem
5.1
).

Regarding other work involving closed-loop damping,

Adly et al. 2006
proposed to replace the term
\gamma(t)\dot{x}(t)
in (

IDG
γ

) by a “non-smooth” potential. Their non-smooth potential provides the solutions of their differential inclusion with finite length and convergence to a point very close to a minimizer of
f
(if such a point exists). However, since the solution does not converge to the minimal value, it does not have the optimal rate that we seek.

Our damping makes use of the optimality gap
f(x(t))-f^{\star}
(see Remark
4.10
for further discussion). This idea is not new and is used, for example, in the Polyak step-size
\frac{f(x_{k})-f^{\star}}{\mathop{}\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}

[
29
]
. In our system (
LD
) the optimality gap is rather used for designing the damping
\gamma
rather than the step-size.

Open-loop damping and proof techniques

The convergence properties of (

AVD
a

) (and more generally (

IDG
γ

) in the open-loop setting) have been intensively studied. First, for (

AVD
a

) with
a>3
, following
[
33
]
, the convergence rate
o\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right)
for function values and the convergence of the trajectories were proved in
[
26
,
6
,
18
]
. In the setting
a<3
,
[
12
,
9
]
derived the sub-optimal rate
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t^{\frac{2a}{3}}}\right)
. For the critical value
a=3
only the rate
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right)
is known and whether this rate can be improved to
o\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right)
and whether the trajectories converge remains open.
Rates have also been derived under additional assumptions such as the KL property
[
13
]
.

Regarding (

IDG
γ

),
Attouch and Cabot 2017
developed general conditions for the convergence of the values and of the trajectories, which unify several results mentioned above. All these results have in common that they rely on the analysis of Lyapunov functions, like
E
previously introduced. We refer to
[
34
,
35
]
for more details on Lyapunov analyses.
The proof of our main result takes inspiration from those in
[
16
]
where sufficient conditions to derive optimal rates are provided. They must nonetheless be significantly adapted since some of the conditions in
[
16
]
do not hold generally for closed-loop dampings like ours. We replace them by using a specific property of our system (see Lemma
4.1
hereafter). A more detailed comparison follows our main analysis in Remark
4.9
.

3
Preliminaries and Existence of Solutions

Throughout the paper we fix a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with inner product
\mathop{}\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
and induced norm
\mathop{}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
.

We make the following assumptions on the function
f
.

Assumption 1
.

The function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is

(i)

convex and continuously differentiable with locally Lipschitz-continuous gradient
\nabla f
;

(ii)

bounded from below by
f^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\mathcal{H}}f.

We also fix an initial time
t_{0}\geq 0
and initial conditions
x(t_{0})=x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, and
\dot{x}(t_{0})=\dot{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 1
.

A function
x:[t_{0},+\infty[\to\mathcal{H}
, which is twice continuously differentiable on
]t_{0},+\infty[
and continuously differentiable on
[t_{0},+\infty[
, is called a (global)
solution
or
trajectory
to (

IDG
γ

), resp. (
LD
), if it satisfies (

IDG
γ

), resp. (
LD
), for all
t>t_{0}
and satisfies the initial conditions previously mentioned.

Given the setting above, we can ensure the existence and uniqueness of the solutions of (
LD
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumption
1
, there exists a unique solution
x
to (
LD
) with initial conditions
\left(x_{0},\dot{x}_{0}\right)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
and initial time
t_{0}\geq 0
.

The proof relies on the Picard–Lindelöf Theorem and is postponed to Appendix
A
.

Remark 3.2
.

The local Lipschitz-continuity of
\nabla f
is only required to guarantee the existence and uniqueness of the solutions on
[t_{0},+\infty[
(see below), but is not used elsewhere in our analysis.

We finally recall a special case of the Landau notation for asymptotic comparison that we use heavily in the sequel.

Definition 2
.

For any non-negative function
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\forall\alpha\geq 0
,

\displaystyle g(t)=o\left(\frac{1}{t^{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\iff\lim_{t\to+\infty}t^{\alpha}g(t)=0.

4
Main Result: Convergence Rates for (
LD
)

This section is devoted to proving our main result Theorem
1.1
, which states that the solution of (
LD
) achieves a convergence rate that is arbitrarily close to the optimal one for convex functions. First we show that
E
is indeed a Lyapunov function for the system (
LD
).

4.1
The Lyapunov function of (
LD
)

Throughout what follows, let
x
be the solution of (
LD
) with the initial conditions stated in Section
3
.
Recall that the damping coefficient in (
LD
) is
\sqrt{E(t)}
where for all
t\geq t_{0}
,
E(t)=f(x(t))-f^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}
. We first show identities that are specific to (
LD
) and that play a crucial role in what follows.

Lemma 4.1
.

Under Assumption
1
the solution
x
of (
LD
) is such that
E
is continuously differentiable for all
t\geq t_{0}
and

\frac{\mathrm{d}E(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\sqrt{E(t)}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2},

(1)

so in particular
E
is non-increasing. Furthermore, for all
t\geq t_{0}
it holds that

\frac{\mathrm{d}\sqrt{E(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2},\quad\text{or equivalently,}\quad\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(s)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s=2\sqrt{E(t_{0})}-2\sqrt{E(t)}.

(2)

Proof.

We apply the chain rule and use the fact that
x
solves (
LD
) to obtain:

\frac{\mathrm{d}E(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathop{}\left\langle\nabla f(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle+\mathop{}\left\langle\ddot{x}(t),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle\\
=\mathop{}\left\langle\nabla f(x(t))-\sqrt{E(t)}\dot{x}(t)-\nabla f(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle=-\sqrt{E(t)}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2},

(3)

which proves (
1
).
As for the second part, the chain rule and (
1
) imply

\frac{\mathrm{d}\sqrt{E(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{E(t)}}\frac{\mathrm{d}E(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{E(t)}}\sqrt{E(t)}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}.

Finally, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and the continuity of
\dot{x}
this is equivalent to

\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(s)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s=2\sqrt{E(t_{0})}-2\sqrt{E(t)}.

∎

The function
E
describes a quantity which is non-increasing along the trajectory
x
, and note that
E(t)=0
if, and only if,
f(x(t))=f^{\star}
and
\dot{x}(t)=0
. Such a function is called
a Lyapunov function
for the system (
LD
).

4.2
Preliminary convergence results

We make the following assumptions, which are consistent with those in Theorem
1.1
.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that

(i)

\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f\neq\emptyset
;

(ii)

the solution
x
of (
LD
) is uniformly bounded on
[t_{0},+\infty[
.

Remark 4.2
.

Assumption
2
-
(ii)
holds, for example, when
f
is coercive.

We begin our analysis by showing that the trajectory
x
minimizes the function
f
. We do so by showing that
E(t)
tends to zero as
t\to+\infty
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
,
E(t)
converges to zero as
t\to+\infty
. This implies in particular that
\displaystyle f(x(t))\xrightarrow[t\to+\infty]{}f^{\star}
and
\displaystyle\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert\xrightarrow[t\to+\infty]{}0
.

We make use of the following classical result to prove Theorem
4.3
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
g
be a non-negative continuous function on
[t_{0},+\infty[
such that
\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}g(t)\mathrm{d}t
is finite, then either
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}g(t)
does not exist or
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}g(t)=0
.

The proof of Lemma
4.4
is postponed to Appendix
B
. We now prove Theorem
4.3
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Let
z\in\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f
, and for all
t\geq t_{0}
define the so-called “anchor function”
h_{z}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert x(t)-z\right\rVert^{2}
. Then
h_{z}
is twice differentiable for all
t\geq t_{0}
and we have:

\displaystyle\dot{h_{z}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\left\langle x(t)-z,\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle.

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ddot{h_{z}}(t)&=\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}+\mathop{}\left\langle x(t)-z,\ddot{x}(t)\right\rangle\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{LD}}}{{=}}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}+\mathop{}\left\langle x(t)-z,-\sqrt{E(t)}\dot{x}(t)-\nabla f(x(t))\right\rangle\\
&=\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}+\mathop{}\left\langle x(t)-z,-\sqrt{E(t)}\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle-\mathop{}\left\langle x(t)-z,\nabla f(x(t))\right\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq::hztdot}}{\leq}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}-\sqrt{E(t)}\dot{h_{z}}(t)+f^{\star}-f(x(t)),\end{split}

(5)

where we used the first-order characterization of the convexity of
f
in the last step. So
h_{z}(t)
fulfills the following differential inequality:

\ddot{h_{z}}(t)+\underset{=E(t)}{\underbrace{f(x(t))-f^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}}}\leq\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}-\sqrt{E(t)}\dot{h_{z}}(t),

or equivalently,

E(t)\leq\frac{3}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}-\ddot{h_{z}}(t)-\sqrt{E(t)}\dot{h_{z}}(t).

(6)

We integrate (
6
) from
t_{0}
to
T>t_{0}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}E(t)\mathrm{d}t&\leq\frac{3}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\ddot{h_{z}}(t)\mathrm{d}t-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\sqrt{E(t)}\dot{h_{z}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\\
&=3\left(\sqrt{E(t_{0})}-\sqrt{E(T)}\right)-\dot{h_{z}}(T)+\dot{h_{z}}(t_{0})-\sqrt{E(t)}h_{z}(T)+\sqrt{E(0)}h_{z}(t_{0})\\
&\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}h_{z}(t)\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(7)

where we performed integration by parts on the last integral and used Lemma
4.1
. Note that by the boundedness of
E
and the continuity of
f
,
f(x)-f^{\star}
and
\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}\right\rVert
are uniformly bounded on
[t_{0},+\infty[
. This implies together with Assumption
2
that
h_{z}
and
\dot{h_{z}}

Remark 4.9
.

The induction step performed in Theorem

resembles the proof of
[

(then generalized in
[

]
). Yet, the result therein is not directly applicable to (

Cabot and Frankel 2012
showed how to iteratively repeat the application of this result but their proof relies on a strong integrability statement on

4.4
Drawbacks of the approach

Now that we presented the main benefits of using a Lyapunov function as damping in (
LD
), we discuss some drawbacks of our approach, starting with the following remark.

Remark 4.10
.

Our system (
LD
) makes use of the optimal value
f^{\star}
. The latter might be unknown in general but is known in several practical cases such as over-determined regression with squared loss, or empirical risk minimization of some over-parameterized machine learning problems
[
19
]
. We refer to
[
15
]
for more examples and further discussion.
All the results above do not require the knowledge of
f^{\star}
, only its existence. However Algorithm
1
presented in Section
5
is only practical when
f^{\star}
is known.

The second drawback of our approach is that we can lower bound the convergence rate of our damping
\sqrt{E(t)}
. This not surprising as the bound actually matches the sufficient condition from
[
17
]
mentioned in the introduction.

Proposition 4.11
.

The convergence rate of the damping
\sqrt{E}
of the dynamical system (
LD
)
cannot
be asymptotically faster than
\frac{1}{t}
,
i.e.
, there exists no
\alpha>1
such that
\sqrt{E(t)}=o\left(\frac{1}{t^{\alpha}}\right)
for a non-constant bounded solution of (
LD
).

Remark 4.12
.

Proposition
4.11
expresses that
E
can never vanish faster than
o(\frac{1}{t^{2}})
.
However, this does not mean that the convergence rate of
f(x(t))-f^{\star}
can never be faster than the worst-case
o(\frac{1}{t^{2}})
; one can sometimes obtain faster convergence of function values, as we will observe in the numerical experiments.

Proof of Proposition
4.11
.

Assume that
\sqrt{E(t)}=o\left(\frac{1}{t^{\alpha}}\right)
for some
\alpha>1
, then by Lemma
4.8
,

\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\sqrt{E(t)}\mathrm{d}t<+\infty.

(19)

Let us make a similar computation as in

, Prop. 3.5]
:

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}E(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+2\sqrt{E(t)}E(t)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq::derE}}}{{=}}-\sqrt{E(t)}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}+2\sqrt{E(t)}\left(f(x(t))-f^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\right)\\
&=2\sqrt{E(t)}(f(x(t))-f^{\star})\geq 0.\end{split}

(20)

Multiply (
20
) by
e^{2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{E(s)}\mathrm{d}s}>0
to obtain

\begin{split}0\leq e^{2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{E(s)}\mathrm{d}s}\frac{\mathrm{d}E(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+2\sqrt{E(t)}e^{2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{E(s)}\mathrm{d}s}E(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[e^{2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{E(s)}\mathrm{d}s}E(t)\right].\end{split}

\begin{split}0&\leq e^{2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{E(s)}\mathrm{d}s}E(t)-E(t_{0})\\

Therefore, we conclude that

∎

Conclusions and Perspectives

We introduced a new system (

, unlike (

We showed that it is possible to design a closed-loop damping achieving near-optimal rates of convergence for convex optimization. The key ingredient is the coupling between the damping and the speed of convergence of the system. This yielded in particular the identity (

) and (

AVD
a

).
While our work focuses on the ODE setting, we provided numerical experiments corroborating our theoretical results as well as a new practical first-order algorithm LYDIA, derived from (

As for future work, it remains open to know whether we can improve the rate from “arbitrarily close to optimal” to optimal (

We also suspect that one can drop Assumption

On the algorithmic side, our experiments suggest that the rate of (

Finally, it is worth investigating whether one can find systems and algorithms with the same properties that do not require the optimal value

Appendix A
Proof of Existence and Uniqueness

This section is devoted to proving Theorem
3.1
. We recall some result from the theory of ODEs.

Theorem A.1
.

[
31
, Theorem 4.2.6]
(Classical Version of Picard–Lindelöf)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space, let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{H}
be open. Let
G:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
be continuous and let
\left(t_{0},x_{0}\right)\in\Omega
. Assume that there exists
L\geq 0
such that for all
(t,x),(t,y)\in\Omega
we have

\mathop{}\left\lVert G(t,x)-G(t,y)\right\rVert\leq L\mathop{}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert.

Then, there exists
\delta>0
such that the initial value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}u^{\prime}(t)=G(t,u(t))\quad\left(t\in\left(t_{0},t_{0}+\delta\right)\right),\\
u\left(t_{0}\right)=x_{0},\end{array}\right.

admits a unique continuously differentiable solution
u:\left[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta\right[\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
, which satisfies
(t,u(t))\in\Omega
for all
t\in\left[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta\right[
.

Definition 3
.

[
28
, Definition 3.20]

Let
\phi:I\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be a solution to an ODE defined on some open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
.

(a)

We say
\phi
admits an extension or continuation to the right (resp. to the left) if, and only if, there exists a solution
\psi:J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the same ODE, defined on some open interval
J\supset I
such that
\psi|_{I}=\phi
and

\sup J>\sup I\quad(\text{resp.\ }\inf J<\inf I)

An extension or continuation of
\phi
is a function that is an extension to the right or an extension to the left (or both).

(b)

The solution
\phi
is called a
maximal
solution if, and only if, it does not admit any extension in the sense defined in
(a)
.

Theorem A.2
.

[
28
, Theorem 3.22]

Every solution
\phi_{0}:I_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
to some ODE, defined on an open interval
I_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}
, can be extended to a maximal solution of this ODE.

Remark A.3
.

Definition
3
is easily generalized to Hilbert spaces and Theorem
A.2
is valid for an ODE in an infinite dimensional space as well, since the proof relies on Zorn’s Lemma.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We first reduce the system (
LD
) to an equivalent first-order system:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}x(t)\\
\dot{x}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}(t)\\
-\sqrt{f(x(t))-f^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t)\right\rVert^{2}}\dot{x}(t)-\nabla f(x(t))\end{pmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}G(t,(x(t),\dot{x}(t))).

(33)

We first show the claim in the case
\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f\neq\emptyset
and later for the case
\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f=\emptyset
.

First, if
\left(x_{0},\dot{x}_{0}\right)\in\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f\times\left\{0\right\}
, then
(x(t),\dot{x}(t))\equiv(x_{0},0)
is the unique global solution, since
\ddot{x}(t)=0
for all
t\geq t_{0}
.
Now assume that
\left(x_{0},\dot{x}_{0}\right)\not\in\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f\times\left\{0\right\}
and let us check that for all
t\geq t_{0}
, the function

(u,v)\mapsto G(t,(u,v))=\begin{pmatrix}v\\
-\sqrt{f(u)-f^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}}v(t)-\nabla f(u)\end{pmatrix}

is Lipschitz continuous on the open set

\mathcal{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H})\setminus(\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f\times\left\{0\right\}).

The first coordinate of
G
is clearly locally Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
(u,v)
and therefore it suffices to check that the second coordinate of
G
is also locally Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
(u,v)
on
\mathcal{A}
.

The square root function is locally Lipschitz continuous everywhere except at 0 and
f(u)-f^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}\neq 0
for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{A}
. Therefore
(u,v)\in\mathcal{A}\mapsto\sqrt{f(u)-f^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{}\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}}v
is locally Lipschitz continuous as a product of locally Lipschitz-continuous functions. Finally
\nabla f
is locally Lipschitz continuous by Assumption
1
-(i) , hence
G
is locally Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{A}
.
We can now apply the Picard–Lindelöf Theorem
A.1
to any open and bounded neighborhood of the initial conditions
(t_{0},(x_{0},\dot{x}_{0}))
to find some
\delta>0
and a unique solution
(x,\dot{x})
to (
33
) on
[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta[
.

It now remains to show that the solution exists on
[t_{0},+\infty[
. Let
T\in\,]t_{0},+\infty]
be the maximal time of existence (as stated in Theorem
A.2
) of the solution
(x,\dot{x})
of (
33
). Assume that
T<+\infty
, then the limits of
x
and
\dot{x}(t)
exist as
t\to T
. Indeed, first note that the uniform boundedness of
E
implies that
\dot{x}
is bounded on
[t_{0},T[
and by (
LD
)
\ddot{x}
is bounded on
[t_{0},T[
as well. Now consider a Cauchy sequence
\left(t_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
T
and let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then by the mean value Theorem we have:

\begin{split}\mathop{}\left\lVert x(t_{n})-x(t_{m})\right\rVert&\leq\sup_{s\in]t_{0},T[}\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(s)\right\rVert\left|t_{n}-t_{m}\right|,\text{ and}\\
\mathop{}\left\lVert\dot{x}(t_{n})-\dot{x}(t_{m})\right\rVert&\leq\sup_{s\in]t_{0},T[}\mathop{}\left\lVert\ddot{x}(s)\right\rVert\left|t_{n}-t_{m}\right|.\end{split}

Therefore
\left(x(t_{n})\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\left(\dot{x}(t_{n})\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to T}x(t)\text{ and }\lim_{t\to T}\dot{x}(t)

Then, if
(x(T),\dot{x}(T))\in\mathcal{A}
then we can extend the solution by applying Picard–Lindelöf to an open and bounded neighborhood of
\left((T,\left(x(T),\dot{x}(T)\right)\right)
which contradicts the fact that

was the maximal time of existence.
If
(x(T),\dot{x}(T))\in\partial\mathcal{A}\subset\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f\times\left\{0\right\}
, where
\partial\mathcal{A}
denotes the boundary of
\mathcal{A}
, then
\ddot{x}(T)=0
and we can extend the solution on
[T,T+\delta[
, for some
\delta>0
, by taking
(x(t),\dot{x}(t))=(x(T),0)
. This is again a contradiction, therefore we necessarily have

.

Finally, in the case where
\argmin_{\mathcal{H}}f=\emptyset
, we see similarly to before that
G(t,(u,v))
is locally Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
(u,v)
everywhere on
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
. So the Picard–Lindelöf Theorem implies the existence of a unique local solution for any initial value, which we can again extend to a global solution on
[t_{0},+\infty[
.
∎
