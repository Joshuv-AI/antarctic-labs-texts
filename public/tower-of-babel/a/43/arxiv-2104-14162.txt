The Weighted Bergman spaces and Pseudoreflection groups
Gargi Ghosh

Abstract
. We consider a bounded domain Ω ⊆ ℂ d \Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d} which is a G G -space for a finite pseudoreflection group G G . For each one-dimensional representation of the group G , G, the relative invariant subspace of the weighted Bergman space on Ω \Omega is isometrically isomorphic to a weighted Bergman space on the quotient domain Ω / G . \Omega/G. Consequently, formulae involving the weighted Bergman kernels and projections of Ω \Omega and Ω / G \Omega/G are established. As a result, a transformation rule for the weighted Bergman kernels under a proper holomorphic mapping with G G as its group of deck transformations is obtained in terms of the character of the sign representation of G G . Explicit expressions for the weighted Bergman kernels of several quotient domains (of the form Ω / G \Omega/G ) have been deduced to demonstrate the merit of the described formulae.

1.1.
On the weighted Bergman kernels

Given a continuous weight function
\omega:\Omega\to(0,\infty),

L_{\omega}^{2}(\Omega)
denotes the Hilbert space of Lebesgue measurable functions (equivalence classes of functions) on
\Omega
which are square integrable with respect to the measure
\omega(\boldsymbol{z})dV(\boldsymbol{z}),
where
dV
is the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\Omega.
The weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
is the closed subspace consisting of holomorphic functions in
L_{\omega}^{2}(\Omega)
. For
\omega\equiv 1,

\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
reduces to the Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\Omega).
We consider a weight function of the form
\omega=\widetilde{\omega}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
for a continuous map
\widetilde{\omega}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to(0,\infty).
For each one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
we set
\omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}))=\frac{|\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}}{|J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}}\widetilde{\omega}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z})),
where
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the determinant of the complex jacobian matrix of the basic polynomial map
\boldsymbol{\theta}.
The following theorem is the main result of this article.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a finite pseudoreflection group and a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a
G
-space. Then for each one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
the relative invariant subspace
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega))
is isometrically isomorphic to the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),
where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a basic polynomial map associated to the group
G.

Moreover, the reproducing kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}}
of the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})\text{~~for ~~}\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega,

( 1.2)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
is the reproducing kernel of the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
,
\chi_{\varrho}
denotes the character of the representation
\varrho
and
\ell_{\varrho}
is as described in Lemma
2.5
.

We provide a number applications of Theorem
1.1
in Section
5
to determine explicit formulae for the weighted Bergman kernels of various quotient domains. For example, the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
with the
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-invariant weight function
\omega(\boldsymbol{z})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-|z_{j}|^{2})^{\lambda-2},\lambda>1,
has two relative invariant subspaces

\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\rm anti}(\mathbb{D}^{d})=\{f\in\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{D}^{d}):\sigma(f)={\rm sign}(\sigma)f\text{~for~}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}\}
and
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\rm sym}(\mathbb{D}^{d})=\{f\in\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{D}^{d}):\sigma(f)=f\text{~for~}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}\}
associated to only one-dimensional representations of

The subspace

\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}\big(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{\det\big(\!\!\big((1-z_{i}\bar{w_{j}})^{-\lambda}\big)\!\!\big)_{i,j=1}^{d}}{\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{j}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)},\,\,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{D}^{d},

and
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\rm sym}(\mathbb{D}^{d})

where

The case of abelian groups. If

is a finite abelian group, each irreducible representation of

\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\cong\displaystyle\oplus_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),

The reproducing kernel of each weighted Bergman space

can be obtained from Equation (

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}.

Recently, in

1.2.
On the weighted Bergman projections

The weighted Bergman projection
P_{\Omega}^{\omega}:L^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
is defined by

(P_{\Omega}^{\omega}\phi)(\boldsymbol{z})=\langle\phi,\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{z})\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\phi(\boldsymbol{w})\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})\omega(\boldsymbol{w})dV(\boldsymbol{w}),\,\,\phi\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega),

where
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
is the reproducing kernel of the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega).
For a holomorphic function
\phi
in
L^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega),
we have
(P_{\Omega}^{\omega}\phi)(\boldsymbol{z})=\langle\phi,\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{z})\rangle=\phi(\boldsymbol{z}).

Theorem 1.2
.

For each one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
the weighted Bergman projections
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)}^{\omega_{\varrho}}
and
P_{\Omega}^{\omega}
are related by the following formula:

\displaystyle P_{\Omega}^{\omega}\big(\ell_{\varrho}~(\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)=\ell_{\varrho}\big((P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)}^{\omega_{\varrho}}\phi)\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\big),\,\,\phi\in L^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big),

( 1.6)

where
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)}^{\omega_{\varrho}}:L^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
is the weighted Bergman projection.

That is, we get
k
formulae involving weighted Bergman projections, where

the number of one-dimensional representations in
\widehat{G}.
For a fixed one-dimensional representation
\varrho\in\widehat{G},
Equation (
1.6
) is true for the weight tuple
(\omega,\omega_{\varrho})
whenever
\omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}))=\frac{|\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}}{|J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}}\omega(\boldsymbol{z})
for
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega

[

2.
Pseudoreflection groups and analytic Hilbert modules

We begin by recalling a number of useful definitions and standard results about pseudoreflection groups.

2.1.
Chevalley-Shephard-Todd Theorem

Let
G
be a finite pseudoreflection group. Recall that
G
acts on the set of functions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
by
\sigma(f)(\boldsymbol{z})=f({\sigma}^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}).
A function is said to be
G
-invariant if
\sigma(f)=f,
for all
\sigma\in G.
The ring of all complex polynomials in
d
variables is denoted by
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
. Moreover, the set of all
G
-invariant polynomials, denoted by
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]^{G}
, forms a subring and coincides with the relative invariant subspace
R^{G}_{\rm trivial}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}])
associated to the trivial representation of
G
. Chevalley, Shephard and Todd characterize finite pseudoreflection groups in the following theorem.

Theorem

(CST Theorem)
.

[
19
, p. 112, Theorem 3]

The invariant ring
{\mathbb{C}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]^{G}
is equal to
{\mathbb{C}}[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}]
, where
\theta_{i}
’s are algebraically independent
homogeneous polynomials if and only if
G
is a finite pseudoreflection group.

The collection of homogeneous polynomials
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
is called a homogeneous system of parameters (hsop) or basic polynomials associated to the pseudoreflection group
G
. Although a hsop is not unique but the degrees of
\theta_{i}
’s are unique for
G
up to order.
The map
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, defined by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})=\big(\theta_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}),\ldots,\theta_{d}(\boldsymbol{z})\big),\,\,\boldsymbol{z}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}

( 2.1)

is called a basic polynomial map associated to the group
G.
The following proposition shows that a pseudoreflection group
G
always induces a canonical polynomial proper mapping.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
14
, p. 16, Proposition 5.4]

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
be a
G
-invariant domain. Then

(i)

\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
is a domain, and

(ii)

\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
is a proper holomorphic map with
G
as the group of deck transformations.

Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
is another basic polynomial map associated to the group
G,
then the domain
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is biholomorphically equivalent to
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)

[
14
, p. 12]
. The following proposition illustrates a significance of a basic polynomial map.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is a proper holomorphic map with the finite pseudorflection group
G
as the group of deck transformations. Then there exists a unique biholomorphic map
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})\to\Omega_{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{f}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega_{1}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})
is a basic polynomial map associated to the pseudoreflection group
G.

We use the analytic version of Chevalley-Shephard-Todd theorem to prove this proposition. Let
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
denote the ring of all holomorphic functions on
\Omega.

Theorem 2.3

(Analytic CST Theorem)
.

[
14
, p. 12]

Let
G
be a finite pseudoreflection group and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a
G
-invariant domain. For any
G
-invariant holomorphic function
f
on
\Omega,
there exists a unique
\widehat{f}\in\mathcal{O}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)
such that
f=\widehat{f}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}.

A function
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is said to be
G
-invariant if
\boldsymbol{f}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{z})
for all
\sigma\in G,\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega_{1}
. Clearly, if
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}):\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is a
G
-invariant holomorphic map, then each
f_{i}
is a
G
-invariant holomorphic map on
\Omega_{1}
. By analytic CST theorem, we get
f_{i}=\widehat{f}_{i}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},
where
\widehat{f}_{i}\in\mathcal{O}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})\big).
That is,
\boldsymbol{f}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},
where the function
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}=(\widehat{f_{1}},\ldots,\widehat{f_{d}}):\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})\to\Omega_{2}
is uniquely determined.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Since the group of deck transformations of
\boldsymbol{f}
is
G,

\boldsymbol{f}
is
G
-invariant. So there exists a unique holomorphic map
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}
such that
\boldsymbol{f}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
. In order to show that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})\to\Omega_{2}
is biholomorphic, it is enough to prove that the map is bijective. Then from
[
35
, p. 303, Theorem 15.1.8]
, it follows that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}
is biholomorphic.

Being a proper holomorphic map,
\boldsymbol{f}
is surjective. Since
\boldsymbol{f}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
, the map
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}
is also surjective. Arguing by contradiction, suppose that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}
is not injective, then there exist two distinct
\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2}\in\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})
such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{2}).
Suppose that for
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\in\Omega_{1},
we have
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i})=\boldsymbol{z}_{i},
for
i=1,2.
That implies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{2})=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}).

Since
G
is the group of deck transformations of
\boldsymbol{f}
, there is an element
\sigma\in G
such that
\sigma(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})=\boldsymbol{w}_{2}.
Consequently,
G
-invariance of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
implies that
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\sigma(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}))=\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})
, that is,
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})=\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}),
that is,
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\boldsymbol{z}_{2}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Moreover, the complex jacobian
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\det\big(\!\!\!\big(\frac{\partial\theta_{i}}{\partial z_{j}}\big)\!\!\!\big)_{i,j=1}^{d}
has an association with the sign representation of

A detailed discussion regarding one-dimensional representations of pseudoreflection groups is provided which consequently establishes the association.

2.2.
one-dimensional representations of pseudoreflection groups

Definition 2.4
.

A hyperplane
H
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is called reflecting if there exists a pseudoreflection in
G
acting trivially
on
H
.

For a pseudoreflection
\sigma\in G,
define
H_{\sigma}:=\ker({\rm id}-\sigma).
By definition, the subspace
H_{\sigma}
has dimension
d-1
. Clearly,
\sigma
fixes the hyperplane
H_{\sigma}
pointwise. Hence each
H_{\sigma}
is a reflecting hyperplane. By definition,
H_{\sigma}
is the zero set of a non-zero homogeneous linear polynomial
L_{\sigma}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, determined up to a non-zero constant multiplier, that is,
H_{\sigma}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}:L_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z})=0\}
. Moreover, the elements of
G
acting trivially on a reflecting hyperplane forms a cyclic subgroup of
G
.

Let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{t}
denote the distinct reflecting hyperplanes associated to the group
G
and the corresponding cyclic subgroups are
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t},
respectively. Suppose
G_{i}=\langle a_{i}\rangle
and the order of each
a_{i}
is
m_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,t.
For every one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
there exists a unique
t
-tuple of non-negative integers
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}),
where
c_{i}
’s are the least non-negative integers that satisfy the following:

\displaystyle\varrho(a_{i})=\big(\det(a_{i})\big)^{c_{i}},\,\,i=1,\ldots,t.

( 2.2)

The
t
-tuple
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})
solely depends on the representation
\varrho.
The character of the one-dimensional representation
\varrho,

\chi_{\varrho}:G\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
coincides with the representation
\varrho.
The set of polynomials relative to the representation
\varrho
is given by

R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}])=\{f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]:\sigma(f)=\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)f,~{\rm for~~all~}\sigma\in G\}.

The elements of the subspace
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}])
are said to be
\varrho
-invariant polynomials. Stanley proves a typical property of the elements of
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}])
in
[
36
, p. 139, Theorem 3.1]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
36
, p. 139, Theorem 3.1]

Suppose that the linear polynomial
\ell_{i}
is a defining function of
H_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,t.
The homogeneous polynomial
\ell_{\varrho}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\ell_{i}^{c_{i}}
is a generator of the module
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}])
over the ring
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]^{G},
where
c_{i}
’s are unique non-negative integers as described in Equation (
2.2
).

In particular, the sign representation of a finite pseudoreflection group
G
, described in Equation (
1.3
), is given by
{\rm sgn}(a_{i})=\big(\det(a_{i})\big)^{m_{i}-1}
,
i=1,\ldots,t,

[
36
, p. 139, Remark (1)]
and it has the following property.

Corollary 2.6
.

[
38
, p. 616, Lemma]

Let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{t}
denote the distinct reflecting hyperplanes associated to the group
G
and let
m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}
be the orders of the corresponding cyclic subgroups
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t},
respectively. Suppose that the linear polynomial
\ell_{i}
is a defining function of
H_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,t.
Then for a non-zero constant
c
,

\displaystyle J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})=c\prod_{i=1}^{t}\ell_{i}^{m_{i}-1}(\boldsymbol{z})=\ell_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{z}).

Consequently,
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a basis of the module
R^{G}_{{\rm sgn}}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}])
over the ring
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]^{G}
.

Clearly, the character of the sign representation is given by
\chi_{{\rm sgn}}:G\to{\mathbb{C}}^{*}

\displaystyle\chi_{\rm sgn}(\sigma)=\big(\det(\sigma)\big)^{-1},\,\,\sigma\in G.

( 2.3)

Now, for any one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
we generalize the notion of
\varrho
-invariance for analytic Hilbert modules.

Definition 2.7
.

We recall the following definitions from
[
21
]
and
[
24
]
.

1.

A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is said to be a
Hilbert module
over an algebra
\mathcal{A}
if the map
(f,h)\mapsto T_{f}(h):=f\cdot h,

f\in\mathcal{A},h\in\mathcal{H},
defines an algebra homomorphism
f\mapsto T_{f}
of
\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}).

2.

A Hilbert module
\mathcal{H}
over
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
is said to be an
analytic Hilbert module
if

(a)

the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
consists of holomorphic functions on some bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{d},

(b)

\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
and

(c)

\mathcal{H}
possesses a reproducing kernel on
\Omega.

The module action in an analytic Hilbert module is given by pointwise multiplication, that is,
{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}(h)(\boldsymbol{z})=p(\boldsymbol{z}){h}(\boldsymbol{z}),\,h\in\mathcal{H},\,\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega.

For example, a weighted Bergman space on some bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
is an analytic Hilbert module over
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
on
\Omega.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an analytic Hilbert module consisting holomorphic functions on
\Omega
, where
\Omega
is a
G
-invariant domain. We also assume that the reproducing kernel
K
of
\mathcal{H}
is
G
-invariant, that is,

\displaystyle K(\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol{w})=K(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\sigma\in G.

For a one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
recall that the relative invariant subspace of
\mathcal{H},
defined by

\displaystyle R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H})=\{f\in\mathcal{H}:\sigma(f)=\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)f~{\rm for~~all~}\sigma\in G\}.

( 2.4)

The elements of the subspace
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H})
are said to be
\varrho
-invariant. A result analogous to Lemma
2.5
is proved for
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H})
in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
be an analytic Hilbert module and
f\in R_{\varrho}^{G}(\mathcal{H}).
Then
\ell_{\varrho}
divides
f
and the quotient
\frac{f}{\ell_{\varrho}}
is a
G
-invariant holomorphic function on
\Omega,
where
\ell_{\varrho}
is as in Lemma
2.5
.

Proof.

For a fixed
1\leq i\leq t,
suppose that the reflecting hyperplane
H_{i}
is fixed by the cyclic subgroup
G_{i}
and the pseudoreflection
a_{i}
generates
G_{i}.
Using a linear change of coordinates in
\Omega,
we consider a new coordinate system
y_{1}=\ell_{i},y_{2}=x_{2},\ldots,y_{d}=x_{d}.
In this new coordinate system, we have
a_{i}={\rm diag}(\omega_{i},1,\ldots,1),
where
\omega_{i}
is a primitive
m_{i}
-th root of unity. Then,

\displaystyle f(a_{i}^{-1}\cdot(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{\varrho}(a_{i})f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})

\displaystyle f(\omega_{i}y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big({\det}(a_{i})\big)^{c_{i}}f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{i}^{c_{i}}f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}).

Therefore,
f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
is divisible by
y_{1}^{c_{i}}.
Now changing the coordinates, we conclude that
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
is divisible by
\ell_{i}^{c_{i}}.

If
\boldsymbol{z}\notin H_{i}\cap\Omega
, then
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})\neq 0
and this is true in a neighbourhood of
\boldsymbol{z}.
On the other hand, if
H_{i}\cap\Omega
is non-empty and
\boldsymbol{z}\in H_{i}\cap\Omega,
then it follows from Weierstrass Division Theorem
[
28
, p. 11]
that
\ell_{i}
divides
f
in a neighbourhood of
\boldsymbol{z}
since
\ell_{i}
is irreducible. As the holomorphic functions which are obtained locally, patch to give a global holomorphic function, since both
f
and
\ell_{i}
are defined on all of
\Omega
, it follows that
\ell_{i}
divides
f
in
\mathcal{O}(\Omega).
Repetitive application of this argument gives us that the quotient function
\frac{f}{\ell_{i}^{c_{i}}}
is in
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
. These steps can be repeated for all distinct hyperplanes associated to the group
G
. Thus one gets that
\ell_{\varrho}
divides
f
and the quotient function is holomorphic. Moreover,
\ell_{\varrho}
is
\varrho
-invariant from
[
36
, p. 138, Theorem 2.3]
. The quotient of a
\varrho
-invariant function by a
\varrho
-invariant function is clearly
G
-invariant. Hence
\frac{f}{\ell_{\varrho}}
is
G
-invariant.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Let
f\in R_{\varrho}^{G}(\mathcal{H}).
Combining Theorem
2.3
and Lemma
2.8
, we write
\frac{f}{\ell_{\varrho}}=\widehat{f}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
for a unique
\widehat{f}\in\mathcal{O}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big).

That is,
f=(\widehat{f}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\ell_{\varrho}.

In other words,
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\{(\widehat{f}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\ell_{\varrho}\mid\widehat{f}\in\mathcal{O}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)\}.

2.3.
Orthogonal projections

Each one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G
induces the unique linear operator
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\phi=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})~\phi\circ\sigma^{-1},\,\,\,\phi\in\mathcal{H},

( 2.5)

where
\chi_{\varrho}
is the character of the representation
\varrho
and
|G|
denotes the order of the group
G
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
G
be a finite pseudoreflecion group and
\Omega
be a
G
-invariant domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}.
If
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
is an analytic Hilbert module with the
G
-invariant reproducing kernel, the operator
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H}).

Proof.

The reproducing kernel of the analytic Hilbert module
\mathcal{H}
is
G
-invariant. So
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}
is an orthogonal projection on
\mathcal{H}
by
[
14
, p. 15]
. It remains to show that
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\mathcal{H}=R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H}).

For any
\tau\in G,

\phi\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\mathcal{H},

\displaystyle\tau(\phi)=\tau(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})~\phi\circ\sigma^{-1}\tau^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\eta\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\eta^{-1}\tau)~\phi\circ\eta^{-1}\,\,(\text{~taking~}\eta=\tau\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{\varrho}(\tau)~\phi.

Hence
\phi
is in
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H}).

To prove
R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\mathcal{H},
consider
\phi\in R^{G}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H}).
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(\phi)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})~\phi\circ\sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)~\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\phi=\phi.

This completes the proof.
∎

The group action in Equation (
1.1
) is known as the left regular representation. The left regular representation of
G
admits the isotypic decomposition
\oplus_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}n_{\varrho}\varrho,
where the positive integer
n_{\varrho}=\chi_{\varrho}({\rm id})
is the multiplicity of
\varrho
in the left regular representation of
G
and
\widehat{G}
is the set of equivalence classes of irreducible representations of
G.
The linear map
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}
is the orthogonal projection onto the isotypic component associated to the irreducible representation
\varrho
in the decomposition of the left regular representation on
\mathcal{H}.

Theorem 2.11
.

[
14
]

An analytic Hilbert module
\mathcal{H}
on a
G
-space
\Omega
with
G
-invariant kernel admits an orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\oplus_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\mathcal{H}
and each
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\mathcal{H}
is isometrically isomorphic to a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\varrho}\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{\varrho}^{2}},
where
n_{\varrho}=\chi_{\varrho}({\rm id}).

3.
On the weighted Bergman modules

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. We start by recalling weighted Bergman spaces. Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{d}.
For a continuous weight function
\omega:\Omega\to(0,\infty),
the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
is defined by

\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)=\{f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega):\int_{\Omega}|f(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}\omega(\boldsymbol{z})dV(\boldsymbol{z})<\infty\},

where
dV
is the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\Omega.
Clearly, the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
is an analytic Hilbert module.

Let
G
be a finite pseudoreflection group and
\Omega
be a
G
-space. We consider a weight function of the form
\omega=\widetilde{\omega}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
for a continuous map
\widetilde{\omega}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to(0,\infty),
where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a basic polynomial map associated to the group
G.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{d}
denote the group of unitary operators on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}.
Since any finite group
G
generated by pseudoreflections on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{U}_{d}
, it can be shown that the reproducing kernel of the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
is
G
-invariant as the weight function
\omega
is
G
-invariant. Specializing Lemma
2.10
to the weighted Bergman module
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega),
we obtain the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite pseudoreflection group and

\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a
G
-invariant domain.
Then
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}:\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
is an orthogonal projection and
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big)=R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big).

For each one-dimensional representation
\varrho\in\widehat{G},
note that the function
\frac{|\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}}{|J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}}
is a positive-valued continuous function on
\Omega\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{t}H_{i},
where the hyperplanes
H_{i}
’s are as in Corollary
2.6
. Since
\ell_{\varrho}
is
\varrho
-invariant, there exists a unique positive integer, say
m_{\varrho},
such that
\ell_{\varrho}^{m_{\varrho}}
is
G
-invariant. Then
\ell_{\varrho}^{m_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{z})=f_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}))
for a unique polynomial
f_{\varrho}
. The function
\omega_{\varrho}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\setminus\boldsymbol{\theta}(\cup_{i=1}^{t}H_{i})\to(0,\infty)
defined by

\displaystyle\omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{|f_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u})|^{\frac{2}{m_{\varrho}}}}{|f_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{u})|^{\frac{2}{m_{\rm sgn}}}}\widetilde{\omega}(\boldsymbol{u}),\,\,\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\setminus\boldsymbol{\theta}(\cup_{i=1}^{t}H_{i})

( 3.1)

is continuous.
With each one-dimensional representation
\varrho\in\widehat{G},
we associate the linear map
\Gamma_{\varrho}:\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varrho}\psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}(\psi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\ell_{\varrho}.

Note that

\displaystyle\left\|{\Gamma_{\varrho}\psi}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\int_{\Omega}|\psi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}|\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}\omega(\boldsymbol{z})dV(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\int_{\Omega\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{t}H_{i}}|\psi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}\frac{|f_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}))|^{\frac{2}{m_{\varrho}}}}{|f_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}))|^{\frac{2}{m_{\rm sgn}}}}\widetilde{\omega}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}))|J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}dV(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\setminus\boldsymbol{\theta}(\cup_{i=1}^{t}H_{i})}|\psi(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}\omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})dV(\boldsymbol{z})=\left\|{\psi}\right\|^{2},

the last equality follows since the set
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\setminus\boldsymbol{\theta}(\cup_{i=1}^{t}H_{i})
is of Lebesgue measure
0.
So
\Gamma_{\varrho}
is an isometry. Therefore, the range of
\Gamma_{\varrho}
is closed and
\Gamma_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\big)
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Moreover, we show in the next lemma that it coincides with the relative invariant subspace associated to the representation
\varrho.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a finite pseudoreflecion group and
\Omega
be a
G
-invariant domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}.
Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
is a basic polynomial map associated to the group
G
. Then
\Gamma_{\varrho}:\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\to R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big)
is unitary.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma_{\varrho}
is an isometry, it is enough to prove that
\Gamma_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\big)=R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big).

Suppose that
h\in\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).
Then for every
\sigma\in G
,

\displaystyle\sigma(\Gamma_{\varrho}h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sigma\big((h\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\ell_{\varrho}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sigma(\ell_{\varrho})\sigma(h\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)\ell_{\rho}(h\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})=\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)\Gamma_{\varrho}h.

Hence
\Gamma_{\varrho}h
is a
\varrho
-invariant element.

On the other hand, consider
h\in R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big).
By Remark
2.9
, we write
h=\ell_{\varrho}(\widehat{h}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}),
for
\widehat{h}\in\mathcal{O}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big).
Clearly,

\displaystyle\left\|{h}\right\|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|h(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}\omega(\boldsymbol{z})dV(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}|\widehat{h}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}\omega(\boldsymbol{z})dV(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|G|\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)}|\widehat{h}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}\omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})dV(\boldsymbol{z}).

This shows that
\widehat{h}\in\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)
and
\Gamma_{\varrho}(\sqrt{|G|}~\widehat{h})=h.
Therefore,
h\in\Gamma_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)\big).

∎

Remark 3.3
.

From Remark
2.9
, we have
R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big)\subseteq\{\ell_{\varrho}~(\widehat{f}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\widehat{f}\in\mathcal{O}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)\}.
Whereas Lemma
3.2
asserts that

R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big)=\{\ell_{\varrho}~(\widehat{f}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\widehat{f}\in\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)\}.

We refer
\ell_{\varrho}
by
generating polynomial
of
R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big).

Now we have all the ingredients to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The first part follows from Lemma
3.2
.

In Lemma
2.10
, we show that
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}
is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big)=\Gamma_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)\big).
So the reproducing kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\varrho}
of
\Gamma_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\big)\big)
is given by
\mathcal{B}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\langle\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{w}},(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\big(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{w}}\big)(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}).

( 3.2)

An analogous calculation as in
[
27
, p. 6]
yields the following formula for the reproducing kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\varrho}
of
\Gamma_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\big)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{|G|}\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}\mbox{~for~}\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega,

( 3.3)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}}
is the kernel of
\mathbb{A}_{\omega_{\varrho}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
. Combining Equation (
3.2
) and Equation (
3.3
), we have the desired result.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

For a fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega
, as a function of
\boldsymbol{z},
we write
\mathcal{B}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\big(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{w}}\big)(\boldsymbol{z})=\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})\big((\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\varrho}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)
for some
(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\varrho}\in\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
. Similarly,
\mathcal{B}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\big(\overline{\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\big)(\boldsymbol{w})=\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}\big(\overline{(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\varrho}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)}
for some
(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\varrho}\in\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),
when
\boldsymbol{z}
is fixed but arbitrary. Since the variables
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
are independent of each other,
\mathcal{B}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})
divisible by
\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}
for every
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega.
Therefore, the right side of Equation (
1.2
) is well-defined, even if
\boldsymbol{z}
or
\boldsymbol{w}
belongs to
N_{\varrho}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega:\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})=0\}.

Additionally, if
G
is abelian, we have the following result.

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose that
G
is an abelian group, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\cong\displaystyle\oplus_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}))\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})},\,\,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega,

where
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}}
are the reproducing kernels of
\mathbb{A}_{\omega}(\Omega)
and
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),
respectively, and the polynomial
\ell_{\varrho}
is as described in Lemma
2.5
for
\varrho\in\widehat{G}.

Proof.

Since
G
is a finite abelian group, each irreducible representation of
G
is one-dimensional. The isotypic decomposition of the left regular representation of
G
on
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
yields the orthogonal decomposition
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)=\oplus_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big)
(cf. Theorem
2.11
). Moreover,
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}
is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
R^{G}_{\varrho}\big(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)\big)
(cf. Lemma
3.1
) which is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
(cf. Lemma
3.2
). Thus, the first part follows.

For every fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega,
the function
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
and it admits the decomposition
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}).
Then Equation (
3.3
) proves the second part.
∎

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

First note that for a fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega,

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{\varrho}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}})_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})})(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\frac{1}{\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}).

Moreover, the
\Gamma_{\varrho}
map can be extended to
L^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
isometrically. Therefore, for a fixed but arbitrary
\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega
we have the following:
\phi\in L^{2}_{\omega_{\varrho}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)}^{\omega_{\varrho}}\phi)(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}))=\langle\phi,(\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}})_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{\varrho}\phi,\Gamma_{\varrho}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega_{\varrho}})_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\langle\ell_{\varrho}(\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}),\frac{1}{\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\frac{1}{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)\langle\ell_{\varrho}(\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\frac{1}{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}\langle\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)\sigma^{-1}(\ell_{\varrho})(\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}\langle\ell_{\varrho}(\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\mathcal{B}_{\omega})_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{w})}P_{\Omega}^{\omega}(\ell_{\varrho}(\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}))(\boldsymbol{w}).

Hence the result follows.
∎

Sign representation.

G

where
\omega=\widetilde{\omega}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}.
In particular, for the weight function
\omega\equiv 1,

R_{\rm sgn}^{G}(\mathbb{A}^{2}(\Omega))
of
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\Omega).
Subsequently, we get the following results.

We recall from Equation (

) that the character of the sign representation is given by

for

. Also from Corollary
2.6

\ell_{\rm sgn}=J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.
Therefore, the following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem

.

Corollary 3.6
.

The weighted Bergman kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}
of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\widetilde{\omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
is given by the following formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\det(\sigma)\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega,

( 3.4)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
is the reproducing kernel of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega)
and
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the determinant of the complex jacobian matrix of the basic polynomial map

Now we state a formula involving the weighted Bergman projections

The weighted Bergman projections

where

4.
Proper Holomorphic maps and Bergman Kernels

In this section, we prove a transformation formula for weighted Bergman kernels under a proper holomorphic map whose group of deck transformations is either a finite pseudoreflection of group or a conjugate to a finite pseudoreflection group.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is a proper holomorphic map with the finite pseudorflection group
G
as the group of deck transformations and
\omega:\Omega_{1}\to(0,\infty)
is a continuous function of the form
\omega=\widetilde{\omega}\circ\boldsymbol{f}
for a continuous function
\widetilde{\omega}:\Omega_{2}\to(0,\infty)
. A transformation rule for the weighted Bergman kernels of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega_{1})
and
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\widetilde{\omega}}(\Omega_{2})
under the proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{f}
is established in next theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

The reproducing kernels
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\Omega_{1})
and
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}
of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\widetilde{\omega}}(\Omega_{2})
transform according to

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}\big(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\det(\sigma)~\mathcal{B}_{\omega}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega_{1}.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
2.2
that there exists a unique biholomorphic map
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})\to\Omega_{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{f}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}.
We write
\omega^{\prime}=\widetilde{\omega}\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}
and thus
\omega=\omega^{\prime}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}.

Under the biholomorphic map
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})\to\Omega_{2},
the weighted Bergman kernels
\mathcal{B}_{\omega^{\prime}}
of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1}))
and
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}
of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\widetilde{\omega}}(\Omega_{2})
are related as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z})),\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}))\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}))\overline{J_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}))}}\mathcal{B}_{\omega^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})),

( 4.1)

see
[
29
]
. Hence we get the result combining Equation (
4.1
) and Corollary
3.6
.
∎

In particular, for
\omega\equiv 1
we get the following the transformation formula for the Bergman kernels of
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
which overlaps with the Bell’s transformation formula described in
[
11
, p. 687, Theorem 1]
. We emphasize that our transformation formula works for the critical points of
\boldsymbol{f}
as well.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
be a proper holomorphic map with a finite pseudoreflection group
G
as the group of deck transformations. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}\big(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\det(\sigma)\mathcal{B}_{1}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}),\,\,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega_{1},

( 4.2)

where
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
denote the Bergman kernels of
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2},
respectively.

The Bergman kernel of a domain is
rational
if it is a rational function of the coordinates. If the Bergman kernel
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and the proper map
\boldsymbol{f}
are rational, then from Equation (
4.2
) it is clear that
\mathcal{B}_{2}
is also rational.

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose that the Bergman kernel of
\Omega_{1}
is rational. If there exists a proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
which is rational and the group of deck transformations of
\boldsymbol{f}
is a finite pseudoreflection group
G
, then the Bergman kernel of
\Omega_{2}
is also rational.

4.1.
Groups Conjugate to Pseudoreflection groups

Suppose that
\Omega_{1}\text{~and~}\Omega_{2}
are two domains in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
G\subseteq{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
is a finite pseudoreflection group. The group

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}G\boldsymbol{\Psi}

( 4.3)

is said to be a conjugate to the pseudoreflection group
G
by an automorphism
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\in{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1}).
Let
\boldsymbol{F}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
be a proper holomorphic map with the group of deck transformations
\widetilde{G}.
Equivalently,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{z})=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\widetilde{G}}\{\sigma(\boldsymbol{z})\}\,\,\text{~for~}\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega_{1}.

( 4.4)

The proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{F}
satisfying Equation (
4.4
) is referred as
factored by automorphisms

\widetilde{G}
in
[
9
,
23
]
. We obtain a characterization for such proper holomorphic maps in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{F}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is a proper holomorphic map between two bounded domains in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\widetilde{G}\subseteq{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
is a conjugate to a pseudoreflection group
G
by the automorphism
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\in{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
. Then
\boldsymbol{F}
is factored by
\widetilde{G}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a basic polynomial map associated to the group
G
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is a biholomorphic map from
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})
to
\Omega_{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{F}
is factored by the group
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}G\boldsymbol{\Psi}
. Then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{z})=\bigcup_{\sigma\in G}\{(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\circ\sigma\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi})(\boldsymbol{z})\}\text{~for all~}\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega_{1}.

Consider the map
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{F}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}.
For every
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega_{1},

\boldsymbol{f}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{z})=\bigcup_{\sigma\in G}\{\sigma(\boldsymbol{z})\},
that is, the proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is factored by the finite pseudoreflection group
G.
From Proposition
2.2
, we get that
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is a biholomorphic map from
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})
to
\Omega_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a basic polynomial map associated to the group
G.
Therefore,
\boldsymbol{F}
can be written in the desired way.

Conversely, assume that we can express
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is a biholomorphic map from
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})
to
\Omega_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a basic polynomial map associated to
G
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\in{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
. Note that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
is factored by the group
G.
Since
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{F}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z})
for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega_{1},
the result follows.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose that
\Omega_{i},
for
i=1,2
are two bounded domains in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{F}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is a proper holomorphic map which is factored by
\widetilde{G}\subseteq{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
, where
\widetilde{G}
is as in Equation (
4.3
). Then
\mathcal{B}_{2}
can be expressed in terms of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
by the following formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}\big(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{F}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{F}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathcal{B}_{1}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z}),{\boldsymbol{w}}\big),

( 4.5)

where
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is the Bergman kernel of the domain
\Omega_{i},\,\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}\in{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\circ\sigma^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}
for
\sigma\in G.

Proof.

The Bergman kernel
\mathcal{B}_{1}
transforms under the automorphism
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\in{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
following
[
30
, p. 419, Proposition 12.1.10]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\mathcal{B}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})\mbox{~for~}\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega_{1}.

Therefore, for a fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega_{1},

\displaystyle{(\mathcal{B}_{1})}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w})}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{w})}J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)}{(\mathcal{B}_{1})}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)

\displaystyle{(\mathcal{B}_{1})}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w})}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{w})}J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})\big)}{(\mathcal{B}_{1})}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})\big)

\displaystyle{(\mathcal{B}_{1})}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w})}\big(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{w})}J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\circ\sigma^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)}{(\mathcal{B}_{1})}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\circ\sigma^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big).

Let
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}:=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\circ\sigma^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}.
Then
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}=\sigma^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}.
Application of the chain rule on the both sides yields
J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{z})=J_{\sigma^{-1}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{z}).
Hence we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}\big(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{z})}{\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{w})}J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{z})J_{\sigma^{-1}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)}\mathcal{B}_{1}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z}),{\boldsymbol{w}}\big).

( 4.6)

Suppose that
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denotes the Bergman kernel of the domain
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1}).
From Equation (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w}))\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\det(\sigma)\mathcal{B}_{1}\big(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w})\big),

for
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega_{1}
. Equation (
4.6
) implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})),\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w}))\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z}))\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\det(\sigma)\frac{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{z})}{\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{w})}J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{z})J_{\sigma^{-1}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)}\mathcal{B}_{1}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z}),{\boldsymbol{w}}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\det(\sigma)\frac{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{z})}{J_{\sigma^{-1}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)}\mathcal{B}_{1}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z}),{\boldsymbol{w}}\big).

Since
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi},
where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega_{1})\to\Omega_{2}
is a biholomorphism, we have the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}\big(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{F}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{F}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\det(\sigma)\frac{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{z})}{J_{\sigma^{-1}}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z}))}\mathcal{B}_{1}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z}),{\boldsymbol{w}}\big).

Note that for every
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega_{1},

J_{\sigma^{-1}}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z}))=\det(\sigma)
, so
\frac{\det(\sigma)}{J_{\sigma^{-1}}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{z}))}=1.
This completes the proof.
∎

\boldsymbol{\iota}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{1}

5.
Applications

The Bergman kernel on the domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}.
We fix this notation for the rest of our discussion. Let
\mathbb{B}_{d}
be the unit ball with respect to the
\ell^{2}
-norm induced by the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}.
The Bergman kernel of
\mathbb{B}_{d}
is given by
[
7
, p. 172]

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{B}_{d}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\big)^{-(d+1)}.

( 5.1)

Let
\mathbb{D}^{d}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:|z_{1}|,\ldots,|z_{d}|<1\}
, the unit ball with respect to
\ell^{\infty}
-norm, be the polydisc in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The Bergman kernel of
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-z_{j}w_{j})^{-2}.

( 5.2)

In this section, we obtain formulae for the weighted Bergman kernels of several domains which are biholomorphically equivalent to some quotient domains of the form
\Omega/G,
where
\Omega=\mathbb{B}_{d}
or
\mathbb{D}^{d}
and
G
is a finite pseudoreflection group. This demonstrates an useful application of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
4.5
.

Suppose that for a finite pseudoreflection group
G,
a basic polynomial map associated to the group
G
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\theta}.
Clearly,
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is again a polynomial. If
\mathbb{B}_{d}
(or
\mathbb{D}^{d}
) is
G
-invariant, then the kernel function
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{B}_{d})}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})
(or
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{d})}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})
) is rational from Corollary
4.3
. In this section, we consider domains (except Rudin’s domains) which can be realized as
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
for a basic polynomial map
\boldsymbol{\theta}
associated to some finite pseudoreflection group
G.
This provides classes of domains with rational Bergman kernels.

5.1.
Rudin’s domain

Rudin characterizes proper holomorphic mappings from
\mathbb{B}_{d}
onto a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{d},d>1,
in
[
34
, p. 704, Theorem 1.6]
, see also
[
8
, p. 506]
. The result is stated as:

Theorem
.

[
34
, p. 704, Theorem 1.6]

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{F}:\mathbb{B}_{d}\to\Omega
is a proper holomorphic mapping from the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}(d>1)
onto a domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with multiplicity
m>1.
Then there exists a unique finite pseudoreflection group
G
of order
m
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\circ\boldsymbol{\Psi},

( 5.3)

where
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{B}_{d},

\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a basic polynomial mapping associated to
G
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{B}_{d})\to\Omega
is biholomorphic.

In other words, any proper holomorphic mapping from the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
onto the domain
\Omega
is factored by automorphisms
\widetilde{G},
where
\widetilde{G}
is a conjugate to the finite pseudoreflection group
G
by the automorphism
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\in{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{B}_{d}).
Such a domain
\Omega
is referred as Rudin’s domain in
[
12
, p. 427]
. Here, we include formulae for the Bergman kernels of Rudin’s domains to exhibit a direct application of Theorem
4.5
. Moreover, we show that the Bergman kernels of a subclass of Rudin’s domains are rational.

The group of unitary operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d},

\mathcal{U}_{d},
leaves the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
invariant. Since any pseudoreflection group
G
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{U}_{d},

\mathbb{B}_{d}
is
G
-invariant. Now we use Theorem
4.5
to get the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{F}:\mathbb{B}_{d}\to\Omega
is a proper holomorphic mapping. Then the Bergman kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}
is given by the following formula :

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}\big(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{F}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{F}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in G}\frac{J_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{z})}{\big(1-\langle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\big)^{d+1}},\qquad

( 5.4)

where
\Psi\in{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{B}_{d})
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
for
\sigma\in G.

The automorphisms of the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}

are given by certain rational functions
[
35
, p. 28, Theorem 2.2.5]
. So the functions
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z})
and

are rational in

A proper holomorphic map from
\mathbb{B}_{d}

.

[

5.2.
Symmetrized Polydisc

The permutation group on
d
symbols is denoted by
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
. The group
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by permuting its coordinates, that is,

\displaystyle\sigma\cdot(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=(z_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\ldots,z_{\sigma^{-1}(d)})\,\,\text{for}\,\,\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}\,\,\text{and}\,\,(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Clearly, the open unit polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is invariant under the action of the group
\mathfrak{S}_{d}.

Let
s_{k}
denote the elementary symmetric polynomials of degree
k
in
d
variables, for
k=1,\ldots,d.
The symmetrization map
\boldsymbol{s}:=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d}):\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a basic polynomial map associated to the pseudoreflection group
\mathfrak{S}_{d}.
The domain
\mathbb{G}_{d}:=\boldsymbol{s}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
is known as the symmetrized polydisc.

The weight function
\omega(\boldsymbol{z})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-|z_{j}|^{2})^{\lambda-2}
is
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-invariant, so there exists continuous function
\widetilde{\omega}:\mathbb{G}_{d}\to(0,\infty)
such that
\omega=\widetilde{\omega}\circ\boldsymbol{s}.
For
\lambda>1,
the reproducing kernel of the weighted Bergman space
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
is
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}^{(\lambda)}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-z_{j}w_{j})^{-\lambda}.

The symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
has only two one-dimensional representations in
\widehat{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}.
Those are the sign representation and the trivial representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
.

For the sign representation. The relative invariant subspace subspace
R^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}}_{\rm sgn}(\mathbb{A}_{\omega}^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{d}))
is isometrically isomorphic to the Bergman space
\mathbb{A}_{\widetilde{\omega}}^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{d})
(cf. Theorem
1.1
). Therefore, the weighted Bergman kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}
of
\mathbb{A}_{\widetilde{\omega}}^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{d})
can be derived in terms of the weighted Bergman kernel of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}^{(\lambda)}.
From Equation (
1.4
), the weighted Bergman kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}
is given by the formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}\big(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}\det(\sigma)\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}^{(\lambda)}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}).

Note that
\det(\sigma)={\rm sgn}(\sigma^{-1}),
for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}\big(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}{\rm sgn}(\sigma^{-1})\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}^{(\lambda)}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}{\rm sgn}(\sigma^{-1})\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-z_{i}\bar{w}_{\sigma^{-1}(i)})^{-\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\det\big(\!\!\big((1-z_{i}\bar{w_{j}})^{-\lambda}\big)\!\!\big)_{i,j=1}^{d}.

Note that
J_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{z})=\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})

[
25
, p. 370, Lemma 10]
.

Proposition 5.3
.

The weighted Bergman kernel of
\mathbb{A}_{\widetilde{\omega}}^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{d})
is given by

\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\omega}}\big(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{\det\big(\!\!\big((1-z_{i}\bar{w_{j}})^{-\lambda}\big)\!\!\big)_{i,j=1}^{d}}{\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{j}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)},\,\,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{D}^{d}.

In particular, for
\lambda=2
the weight function
\omega\equiv 1
(consequently,
\widetilde{\omega}\equiv 1
) and the Bergman kernel of the symmetrized polydisc
\mathbb{G}_{d}
can be deduced from the above result which is

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{G}_{d}}(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{w}))=\frac{\det\big(\!\!\big((1-z_{i}\bar{w_{j}})^{-2}\big)\!\!\big)_{i,j=1}^{d}}{\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{j}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)}.

[

using Bell’s transformation rule on the set of regular values of the symmetrization map
\boldsymbol{s}.
A different approach is followed to derive this formula in
[
32

.

For the trivial representation. The trivial representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
is given by
{\rm tr}:\mathfrak{S}_{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that
{\rm tr}(\sigma)=1
for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}.
The generating polynomial
\ell_{\rm tr}

Then from Theorem

( 5.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}\mathcal{B}^{(\lambda)}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-z_{i}\bar{w}_{\sigma^{-1}(i)})^{-\lambda}

5.4.
Monomial Polyhedron

For
d\geq 2,
a
d
-tuple
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
of rational numbers and a
d
-tuple of complex numbers
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we denote
\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{d}z_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}.

Consider a matrix
B\in M_{d}(\mathbb{Q}).
We enumerate the row vectors of
B
by
\mathcal{F}=\{\boldsymbol{b}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}^{d}\},
where
\boldsymbol{b}^{k}=(b_{1}^{k},\ldots,b_{d}^{k}).
The monomial polyhedron associated to
B
is defined by

\mathscr{U}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\boldsymbol{b}^{k}}|<1\text{~for all ~}1\leq k\leq d\},

unless for some
1\leq k,j\leq d
, the quantity
z_{j}^{b_{j}^{k}}
is not defined due to the division of zero, see
[
13
, Equation 1.1]
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
B\in M_{d}(\mathbb{Z}),~\det(B)>0
and
B^{-1}\succeq 0

[
13
, Equation 3.3]
.

Set
A=\adj B.
The rows of
A
are enumerated by
\{\boldsymbol{a}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}^{d}\}
. We borrow
the proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}:\mathbb{D}^{d}_{L(B)}\to\mathscr{U}
defined by

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}(\boldsymbol{z})=(\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{a}^{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{a}^{d}}),\,\,\text{~for~}\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{D}^{d}_{L(B)}

from
[
13
, Theorem 3.12]
, where
\mathbb{D}^{d}_{L(B)}
is the product of some copies of the unit disc with some copies of the
punctured unit disc. The proper map
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}
is of quotient type with pseudoreflection group
G
and
G
is isomorphic to the direct product of cyclic groups
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}/{\delta i}\mathbb{Z},
where each
\delta_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
is coming from the Smith Normal form of the matrix
A,
that says,
A=PDQ,
where
P,Q\in GL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
and
D={\rm diag}(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{d})\in M_{d}(\mathbb{Z}).
Let
\wp:\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}/{\delta i}\mathbb{Z}\to G
be a group isomorphism. Set
S_{G}=\{\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:1\leq n_{i}\leq|\delta_{i}|\}.
We write

\displaystyle G=\{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{n}}:\wp\big(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{|\delta_{i}|}^{n_{i}}\big)=\sigma_{\boldsymbol{n}}\text{~for~}\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})\in S_{G}\},

where
\omega_{|\delta_{i}|}
is a primitive
|\delta_{i}|
-th root of unity for
i=1,\ldots,d
.

Proposition 5.7
.

The Bergman kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\mathscr{U}}
of the monomial polyhedron
\mathscr{U}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathscr{U}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{(\det A)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}\overline{w_{i}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{a}^{i}}\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\boldsymbol{a}^{i}}}\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in S_{G}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{|\delta_{i}|}^{n_{i}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}),

( 5.7)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}
is the Bergman kernel of
\mathbb{D}^{d}.

Proof.

We use Corollary
4.2
to get the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathscr{U}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\frac{1}{J_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}}(\boldsymbol{z})\overline{J_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}}(\boldsymbol{w})}}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{n}}\in G}\det(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{n}})\widehat{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}}(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}),

where the Bergman kernel of
\mathbb{D}^{d}_{L(B)}
is denoted by
\widehat{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}}
. Note that
\widehat{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}),
whenever
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{D}^{d}_{L(B)}.

Since the character remains unchanged under group isomorphism, the representation
\mu
of
G
, as described in Equation (
2.3
), gives
\chi_{\mu}(\sigma^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{n}})=\det(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{n}})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{|\delta_{i}|}^{n_{i}}
for every
\boldsymbol{n}\in S_{G}.

Moreover, from
[
13
, Lemma 3.8]

\displaystyle J_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}}(\boldsymbol{z})=\det A\cdot\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{a}^{i}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}}.

( 5.8)

Thus the result follows.
∎

In
[
13
, Proposition 3.22]
, Chakrabarti et al. proved rationality of the Bergman kernel of the monomial polyhedron using Bell’s transformation rule for the Bergman kernels under a proper holomorphic mapping. Since
J_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{A}}(\cdot)
is rational, we conclude the following from Equation (
5.7
).

Corollary 5.8
.

The Bergman kernel of monomial polyhedron
\mathscr{U}
is rational.

5.4.1.
Fat Hartogs Triangle.

Let
\gamma
be a positive integer. For the matrix
B=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&-1\\

, the domain
\mathscr{U}
can be written as
\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|z_{1}|^{\gamma}<|z_{2}|<1\}.
This turns out to be a subclass of ‘fat Hartogs triangle’
[

. We denote such domains by

The map

From Equation (
5.7
), we write the Bergman kernel for the domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\Omega_{\gamma}}\big(\Phi_{A}(\boldsymbol{z}),\Phi_{A}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}(z_{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{2}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)^{\gamma}}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\gamma}\omega_{\gamma}^{k}~\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}\big((\omega_{\gamma}^{k}z_{1},\omega_{\gamma}^{-k}z_{2}),(w_{1},w_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}(z_{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{2}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)^{\gamma}}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\gamma}\frac{\omega_{\gamma}^{k}}{(1-\omega_{\gamma}^{k}z_{1}\overline{w_{1}})^{2}(1-\omega_{\gamma}^{-k}z_{2}\overline{w_{2}})^{2}},

where
\omega_{\gamma}
is a primitive
\gamma
-th root of unity.

5.5.
Complex Ellipsoid

A subclass of complex ellipsoids is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{p,q}:=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|z_{1}|^{2p^{\prime}}+|z_{2}|^{2q^{\prime}}<1\}

for
p=\frac{1}{p^{\prime}},q=\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}
. The holomorphic map
\Phi:\mathbb{B}_{2}\to\Omega_{p,q}
defined by
(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(z_{1}^{p},z_{2}^{q})
is proper. The map
\Phi
is factored by automorphisms
G\subseteq{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{B}_{2}),
where
G
is isomorphic to the pseudoreflection group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

We use Corollary
4.2
to determine a formula for the Bergman kernel of
\Omega_{p,q},
denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{\Omega_{p,q}}
. Therefore, the kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\Omega_{p,q}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\Omega_{p,q}}\big(\Phi(z_{1},z_{2}),\Phi(w_{1},w_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(pq)^{2}(z_{1}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)^{p-1}(z_{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{2}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)^{q-1}}\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{p}\sum_{\alpha_{2}=1}^{q}\omega_{p}^{\alpha_{1}}\omega_{q}^{\alpha_{2}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{B}_{2}}\big((\omega_{p}^{\alpha_{1}}z_{1},\omega_{q}^{\alpha_{2}}z_{2}),(w_{1},w_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(pq)^{2}(z_{1}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)^{p-1}(z_{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw_{2}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)^{q-1}}\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{p}\sum_{\alpha_{2}=1}^{q}\frac{\omega_{p}^{\alpha_{1}}\omega_{q}^{\alpha_{2}}}{\big(1-(\omega_{p}^{\alpha_{1}}z_{1}\overline{w_{1}}+\omega_{q}^{\alpha_{2}}z_{2}\overline{w_{2}})\big)^{3}},

where
(z_{1},z_{2}),(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{B}_{2},
and
\omega_{k}
denotes a primitive
k
-th root of unity.
