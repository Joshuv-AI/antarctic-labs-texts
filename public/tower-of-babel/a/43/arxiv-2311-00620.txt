Sigma invariants for partial orders on nilpotent groups
Kevin Klinge

Abstract
. We prove that a map onto a nilpotent group Q Q has finitely generated kernel if and only if the preimage of the positive cone is coarsely connected as a subset of the Cayley graph for every full archimedean partial order on Q Q . In case Q Q is abelian, we recover the classical theorem that N N is finitely generated if and only if S ⁡ ( G , N ) ⊆ Σ 1 ​ ( G ) S(G,N)\subseteq\Sigma^{1}(G) . Furthermore, we provide a way to construct all such orders on nilpotent groups. A key step is to translate the classical setting based on characters into a language of orders on G G .

2.
Partially ordered groups

We start by having a look at partially ordered groups in general.
A more thorough introduction may be found for example in
[
7
]
.
It will be of special interest how we can translate between orders and maps.
As far as I am aware, there is no source where this relation is made explicit.
Some of it may be found in sources like
[
7
]
or
[
4
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group.
A
partial order
on
G
is a relation
\mathrel{\prec}
on the set
G
such that for all
f,g,h\in G

•

g\mathrel{\nprec}g
(antireflexive)

•

g\mathrel{\prec}h\Rightarrow h\mathrel{\nprec}g
(antisymmetric)

•

f\mathrel{\prec}g~\mathrm{and}~g\mathrel{\prec}h\Rightarrow f\mathrel{\prec}h
(transitive)

Additionally, a partial order may have the following properties

•

f\mathrel{\prec}g\Rightarrow hf\mathrel{\prec}hg
(left invariant)

•

f\mathrel{\prec}g\Rightarrow fh\mathrel{\prec}gh
(right invariant)

and
\mathrel{\prec}
is said to be
bi-invariant
if it satisfies both.

Remark 2.2
.

((1))

The symbol
=
always refers to honest equality as elements of
G
.

((2))

For an order
\mathrel{\prec}
, we set

g\mathrel{\preccurlyeq}h\iff g\mathrel{\prec}h~\mathrm{or}~g=h.

The symbols
\mathrel{\succ}
and
\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}
are defined as the respective opposite orders.

((3))

In the literature one often finds the definition of
(G,\mathrel{\preccurlyeq})
as definition of a partially ordered group and the symbols
\mathrel{\prec},\mathrel{\succcurlyeq},\mathrel{\succ}
are then derived.
But I find it often makes notation easier to view
\mathrel{\prec}
as “the order” so that is the convention we use here.

((4))

When we use
<,\leqslant,>
and
\geqslant
, we always mean them in their well established, canonical meanings, such as the standard order on the real numbers.

Let us have a look at some important examples.
We will review them in more detail in
2.7
and
2.13
.

Example 2.3
.

((1))

The
trivial order

g\mathrel{\nprec}h
for each pair
g,h\in G
is a partial order for any group
G
.

((2))

For any group homomorphism
\Phi\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
, we can define a bi-invariant partial order
\mathrel{\prec}
on
G
by letting

g\mathrel{\prec}h\iff\Phi(g)<\Phi(h).

For
\Phi=0
, we obtain the trivial order on
G
.

((3))

For
G=\mathbb{Z}^{2}={\langle a,b\mid[a,b]\rangle}
, there is the
lexicographic order

a^{i}b^{j}\mathrel{\prec}a^{k}b^{l}\iff i\mathop{\leqslant}k~\mathrm{and}~i=k\Rightarrow j<l.

We say
(G,\mathrel{\prec})
or sometimes just

is an
ordered group
as a shortcut for

being a group and
\mathrel{\prec}
a bi-invariant partial order on
G
.
In this article, any order will be partial and bi-invariant unless noted otherwise.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
G
be an ordered group.

((1))

Two elements

are called

g\mathrel{\prec}h~\mathrm{or}~h\mathrel{\prec}g
and

otherwise.

((2))

An order is called

((3))

g\in G

and negative if

.

((4))

{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}\coloneqq\{g\in G\mid g\mathrel{\succ}1\}

of
(G,\mathrel{\prec})
.

((5))

For the inclusion of a subset

the

is the partial order
\mathrel{{\mathrel{\prec}}_{|{S}}}
on
S
such that for any

s\mathrel{{\mathrel{\prec}}_{|{S}}}t\iff\iota(s)\mathrel{\prec}\iota(t).

((6))

A subset

is called an

if
\mathrel{{\mathrel{\prec}}_{|{S}}}
is the trivial order.

((7))

An antichain

is

We say that

if it is an antichain and a subgroup and it is maximal amongst subgroups that are antichains.

((8))

For a subset

, set

S_{*}\coloneqq\{g\in G\mid\exists s\in S\colon g*s\}.

Remark 2.5
.

((1))

Being incomparable is in general not transitive.

((2))

A subgroup
H\leqslant G
is an antichain if and only if
H\cap{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}
is the empty set.
The same cannot be said if
H
is just any subset.

((3))

For any subset
S\subseteq G
we have
S_{\mathrel{\prec}}\cap S_{\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}}=\emptyset
if and only if
S
is an antichain.
Additionally,
S_{\mathrel{\prec}}\cup S_{\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}}=G
if and only if
S
is a maximal antichain.
The latter is not always true if
S
is a maximal antichain subgroup as we will see for instance in

.

((4))

If
g
is a torsion element of

, then

and
g
are necessarily incomparable since if

is positive, then

1\mathrel{\prec}g\mathrel{\prec}g^{2}\mathrel{\prec}\dots\mathrel{\prec}g^{n}=1

If the set of all torsion elements

is induced by an order on

.
Since this will almost always be the case in this work, it suffices for us to think of

as torsion free.

It is often useful and allows for more ergonomic notation to think of the positive cone instead of the order itself.
The following lemma tells us that an order is uniquely determined by its positive cone.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
G
be a group.

((1))

If
G
is ordered, the positive cone
{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}
is closed under multiplication and under conjugation with elements of
G
and if
g\in{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}
then
{g}^{-1}\notin{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}
.

((2))

For any subset
S\subseteq G
that is closed under multiplication and under conjugation with elements of
G
and such that
S\cap{S}^{-1}=\emptyset
,
there is a unique order on
G
such that
{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}=S
.

Proof.

((1))

This is immediate from the definition of a bi-invariant partial order.

((2))

The postulated order is

g\mathrel{\prec}h\iff{g}^{-1}h\in S.

Checking that this is indeed an order is again essentially just applying the definition.
Transitivity follows from the assumption that
S
is closed under multiplication.
For anti-symmetry, we use that
S\cap{S}^{-1}=\emptyset
.
In particular,
1\notin S
, so
\mathrel{\prec}
is anti-reflexive.
Left invariance is straightforward and for right invariance we need
S
closed under conjugation.

As for uniqueness, note that

g\mathrel{\prec}h\iff 1\mathrel{\prec}{g}^{-1}h\iff{g}^{-1}h\in{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}=S

is necessarily true for any bi-invariant relation with
{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}=S
.

∎

Our goal later on will be to translate properties of group homomorphisms into a language based on partial orders.
To see how the two concepts relate, we introduce the following notions.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
(G,\mathrel{\prec})
be an ordered group.

((1))

Let

\varphi\colon G\to Q

Then

g\mathrel{\prec}h\Rightarrow\varphi(g)\mathrel{\preccurlyeq}\varphi(h).

((2))

Let
\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}
be another order on
G
.
We say that
\mathrel{\prec}
is a
suborder
of
\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}
if

{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}\subseteq{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}^{\prime}}.

((3))

Let
(Q,\mathrel{\prec}_{Q})
be an ordered group and
\Phi\colon G\to Q
order preserving.
We say that
\mathrel{\prec}

is induced
by
(\Phi,\mathrel{\prec}_{Q})
if the following condition holds:
For every order
\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}
on
G
such that
\Phi\colon(G,\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime})\to(Q,\mathrel{\prec}_{Q})
is order preserving,
\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}
is a suborder of
\mathrel{\prec}
.

We also say that
\mathrel{\prec}
is induced by
\Phi
or by
\mathrel{\prec}_{Q}
if the other object is clear from the context.

((4))

Let
(H,\mathrel{\prec}_{H})
be an ordered group and
\iota\colon H\to G
order preserving.
We say that
\mathrel{\prec}

is induced
by
(\iota,\mathrel{\prec}_{H})
if the following condition holds:
For every order
\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}
on
G
such that
\iota\colon(H,\mathrel{\prec}_{H})\to(G,\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime})
is order preserving,
\mathrel{\prec}
is a suborder of
\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}
.

Again, we say that
\mathrel{\prec}
is induced by
\iota
or by
\mathrel{\prec}_{H}
if the other is clear.
We denote the induced orders by
\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}_{Q}^{{\Phi}^{-1}}}
and
\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}_{H}^{\iota}}
respectively or just by
\mathrel{\prec}
if there is no chance of confusion.

((5))

If
(Q,\mathrel{\prec}_{Q})
is an ordered group and
\Phi\colon G\to Q
such that
\mathrel{\prec}
is induced by
(\Phi,\mathrel{\prec}_{Q})
, then
\Phi
is
order inducing on the domain
.
An
order inducing map on the codomain

\iota\colon H\to G
is defined analogously.
We will omit the (co)domain part if it is clear on which side a map is order inducing.

Remark 2.8
.

The map
\iota
is not necessarily injective.
However, if

The projection

Thus

can be made injective without losing any information whenever
\iota
is order inducing.

In many cases, if
\mathrel{\prec}
is an induced order, it is possible to make its positive cone explicit.
Most of the time I find it easier to think about induced orders using the following description.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
G
be a group and
Q,H
be ordered groups.
Let
\Phi\colon G\to Q
and
\iota\colon H\to G
.

Then

((1))

\Phi
induces the following order on
G
:

1\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}^{{\Phi}^{-1}}}g\iff 1\mathrel{\prec}\Phi(g)

((2))

Consider the relation

1\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}g\iff g\in G\cdot\iota({H}^{\mathrel{\succ}})

Here,
G\cdot\iota({H}^{\mathrel{\succ}})
denotes the image of
\iota({H}^{\mathrel{\succ}})
under conjugation with elements in
G
.
If
\iota
induces an order on
G
, then
\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}
is an order and it is the order induced by
\iota
.

Proof.

((1))

This is the order such that
{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}={\Phi}^{-1}({Q}^{\mathrel{\succ}})
.
Checking that
{\Phi}^{-1}({Q}^{\mathrel{\succ}})
is closed under multiplication, conjugation with
g\in G
and does not contain two elements inverse to each other is straightforward since we know that
{Q}^{\mathrel{\succ}}
has all these properties.
The order is then well-defined by
2.6
.

As any order on
G
such that
\Phi
is order preserving requires
\Phi({G}^{\mathrel{\succ}})\subseteq{Q}^{\mathrel{\succ}}
, all such orders are suborders of
\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}^{{\Phi}^{-1}}}
.

((2))

If
\iota
is order preserving, then every element of
\iota({H}^{\mathrel{\succ}})
has to be positive.
Because the positive cone of the induced order has to be closed under conjugation, this extends to
G\cdot\iota({H}^{\mathrel{\succ}})
.
Hence if the relation
\mathrel{\prec}^{\prime}
defined in the statement is actually an order, then it is a suborder of every order such that
\iota
is order preserving.

Checking that
G\cdot\iota({H}^{\mathrel{\succ}})
is closed under multiplication and conjugation is straightforward.
It may however contain two elements inverse to each other.
But in this case, the above argument shows that
\iota
cannot be order preserving for any order on
G
and hence
\iota
does not induce any order.

∎

Remark 2.10
.

In particular, any map
\Phi
with an ordered codomain induces an order on the domain.
But not every map
\iota
with an ordered domain induces an order on the codomain.
However, it is easy to tell if a given order on the codomain is induced by
\iota
.
Especially if
\iota({H}^{\mathrel{\succ}})
is already closed under conjugation with
G
, then this is the positive cone of the order induced by
\iota
.
Also, if
G
admits some order such that

, then that order is induced by the inclusion of

.

We may chain order-inducing maps as expected:

Let there be three ordered groups

The kernel of a map will be of particular interest to us later on.
Given a map that is order inducing on the domain, we would like to know if we can recover the kernel of that map just by looking at the induced order.
This is not always possible.

But in most other cases, we get some restrictions on what the kernel might have been.

Remark 2.14

For a lexicographic order with respect to some sequence

In case

The lexicographic order has both

\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}_{H}^{\iota}}

So one might think about it as “induced by

Definition 2.15

Let

.
In this case, we write

An order is called

Remark 2.16
.

((1))

The way we worded the definition,

is infinitesimal with respect to any positive element.

((2))

If
\mathrel{\prec}_{G}
is a lexicographic order with respect to some orders

\mathrel{\prec}_{H}

\mathrel{\prec}_{Q}
and
\mathrel{\prec}_{H}

In particular, an order induced by another order is archimedean if and only if the inducing order is.

((3))

It is a fact due to Hölder

An account in English may be found for example in

For partially ordered groups, this is not the case as we will see in

Let

((1))

((2))

Example 2.18
.

((1))

On
\mathbb{Z}={\langle X\rangle}
, there are exactly two non-trivial full orders:
The standard order where
X\mathrel{\succ}1
and the opposite order, where we interchange the meanings of “positive” and “negative”.
That is the unique order where
X\mathrel{\prec}1
.
The non-trivial isomorphism
(\mathbb{Z},\mathrel{\prec})\to(\mathbb{Z},\mathrel{\succ})
is order inducing in either direction.

If we don’t require our order to be full, any submonoid that does not contain any two elements inverse to each other defines an order on
\mathbb{Z}
.
This includes for example the monoids
2\mathbb{N}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
and
\{1,X^{k}\mid k\mathop{\geqslant}2\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
.

((2))

On
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, any primitive order is total or lexicographic.
Thus we may construct all primitive orders on a finitely generated abelian group by decomposing it into totally or trivially ordered factors.
Then we order their product stepwise by the lexicographic order of these factors.

The resulting order is archimedean if at most one factor was non-trivially ordered.
It is factorizing and hence full if at every step of constructing a lexicographic order, if the left factor is trivially ordered, then so is the right factor.

((3))

To make the previous example more concrete, consider the case

and recall

.
When writing

as a product of abelian groups, we can either use just one factor

and order that totally.
In this case we obtain an order like

.
Or we decompose it into two factors

.
Then we can order

,
the other way round as in

,
or if we order both factors totally we get

.

Note that an order on

is induced by a map to

if and only if it is full and archimedean.
Also note that totality is not a useful criterion in this situation and that is why we consider partial orders in the first place.

The fact that every order is full or archimedean is due to

\mathbb{Z}^{3}

Namely the lexicographic order with respect to

((1))

Definition 3.1

In order to generalise to maps onto non-abelian groups, we need to find an analogue of “maps to

We would like to use this as a definition in cases where the codomain is any partially ordered group.

if there exist a finitely generated subgroup
H\subseteq G
containing
K
and a finite generating set
S\subseteq H
such that the full subgraph of
\operatorname{Cay}(H,S)
spanned by
K
is connected.

Remark 3.5
.

((1))

If
G
itself is finitely generated, then there is no need to pass to a subgroup
H
as every finite generating set of a subgroup is contained in a finite generating set of
G
.

((2))

If
G
is contained in a finitely generated group
G^{\prime}
such that
K
is coarsely connected as a subset of
G^{\prime}
, then
K
is also coarsely connected as a subset of
G
.
If
K
is coarsely connected as a subset of
G
, then it is also coarsely connected as a subset of
G^{\prime}
.

((3))

Suppose
G
is finitely generated and
S
is a finite generating set.
Then
K\subseteq G
is coarsely connected if and only if there exists some constant
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
g,h\in K
there exists an
n
-path in
\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)
from
g
to
h
supported on
K
.
An
n
-path supported on
K
is a path in
\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)
such that any segment of
n
consecutive vertices on the path contains at least one point in
K
.
To see that this is equivalent to the definition of coarse connectedness, note that if
K
is connected by
n
-paths with respect to
S
, then it is connected by
1
-paths with respect to the finite generating set that consists of words in
S
of length at most
n
.

Example 3.6
.

In the group of integers, the subset of even numbers is coarsely connected as it is connected with respect to the generating set
\{X,X^{2}\}
.
The set of powers of
2
is not coarsely connected because the distance between two adjacent powers of
2
is unbounded.
Similarly, for any surjective map
\Phi\colon F_{2}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, the preimage of the positive numbers is not coarsely connected.
Recall
Figure

2
for an illustration of the latter.

In case
G
is not finitely generated, it might not be apparent why this is the correct definition.
But we will see in
Section

5
how this definition ties in nicely with the finitely generated case.

Now we can define the
\Sigma
-invariant for maps to potentially non-abelian groups:

Definition 3.7
.

Let
G
be a group.
Then the
order
\Sigma
-invariant

\Sigma^{1}_{\mathrm{ord}}(G)
is the subset of the set of non-trivial full archimedean orders on
G
defined as follows.

Let
\mathrel{\prec}
be a non-trivial full archimedean order on
G
.
Then
\mathrel{\prec}\in\Sigma^{1}_{\mathrm{ord}}(G)
if and only if for every antichain normal subgroup
K\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 9.02773pt\hskip-9.02773pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{4.75223pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{3.31525pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 8.19444pt\hskip-8.19444pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{3.53351pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{2.36806pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}G
that is maximal among antichain normal subgroups,
K_{\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}}
is coarsely connected.

To understand how this definition generalises
3.1
, we note that they align if we identify a character with the order it induces on
G
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
G
be a group and
\Phi\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
a character.
Then

\Phi\in\Sigma^{1}(G)\iff\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}^{{\Phi}^{-1}}}\in\Sigma^{1}_{\mathrm{ord}}(G),

where
\mathrel{\prec}
is the standard order on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Take
G
to be ordered by
\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}^{{\Phi}^{-1}}}
.
Then
\ker\Phi
is the only maximal antichain subgroup of
G
by
2.12
.
Hence
\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}^{{\Phi}^{-1}}}\in\Sigma^{1}_{\mathrm{ord}}(G)
if and only if
(\ker\Phi)_{\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}}
is coarsely connected.
The latter is equivalent to
(\ker\Phi)_{\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}}
being connected for some finite generating set, which is the definition of
\Phi\in\Sigma^{1}(G)
.
∎

Now that we have an analogue of
\Sigma^{1}(G)
, we are missing just one ingredient to state a generalised version of
3.3
:
The relative character sphere
S(G,N)
.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
G
be a group,
N
a normal subgroup and
\pi\colon G\twoheadrightarrow G/N
the projection map.
Then define the
relative order sphere
as

S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)\coloneqq\{\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}^{{\pi}^{-1}}}~\mid~\mathrel{\prec}~\text{a non-trivial full archimedean order on}~G/N\}.

Recall that in case
G/N
is abelian,
2.20
tells us that every full archimedean order on
G/N
is induced by a map to
\mathbb{R}
.

S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)
contains all orders induced by maps to
G/N
.
As
G/N
is abelian, every order on
G/N
is induced by a map to
\mathbb{R}
.
That is
S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)
contains precisely those orders induced by maps
G\to G/N\to\mathbb{R}
, which are by definition exactly the maps in
S(G,N)
.
So this is actually a generalisation of
S(G,N)
for
G/N
abelian in the same sense as
\Sigma^{1}_{\mathrm{ord}}(G)
is for
\Sigma^{1}(G)
.

The following is an often useful description of the relative order sphere.

Lemma 3.10
.

For a group
G
and
N\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 9.02773pt\hskip-9.02773pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{4.75223pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{3.31525pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 8.19444pt\hskip-8.19444pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{3.53351pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{2.36806pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}G
a normal subgroup,
S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)
is the set

\{\mathrel{\prec}\mid\mathrel{\prec}\text{a non-trivial full archimedean order on}~G~\text{such that}~N~\text{is an antichain}\}.

Proof.

By definition of
\mathrel{\mathrel{\prec}^{{\pi}^{-1}}}
, for every order in
S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)
,
N
is an antichain.

Now let
\mathrel{\prec}
be a full order such that
N
is an antichain.
Then it is induced by the projection
G\twoheadrightarrow G/N
and hence
\mathrel{\prec}\in S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)
.
∎

4.
Classification of partial orders on nilpotent groups

By replacing
\Sigma^{1}(G)
and
S(G,N)
in
3.3
by
\Sigma^{1}_{\mathrm{ord}}(G)
and
S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)
, we obtain a criterion for finite generatedness of
N
.
We will make this precise in
Section

5
for the case
G/N
nilpotent.
But before we do that, we want to study what
S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)
looks like in this case.
By definition,
S_{\mathrm{ord}}(G,N)
is in 1-to-1 correspondence with the non-trivial full archimedean orders on
G/N
.
So our actual goal is to understand orders on nilpotent groups.
To begin, we recall the definition and some basic facts about nilpotent groups.
A comprehensive introduction may be found for example in
[
2
]
.

The trivial group is the only
nilpotent group of class
0
.
A
nilpotent group of class
n+1
is a group
G
such that
G/Z(G)
is nilpotent of class
n
but
G
itself is not.
Here and from now on,
Z(G)
denotes the
center
of
G
.
That is the subgroup of
G
containing all
g
such that
g
commutes with every other element of
G
.
For example, if
G
is abelian, then
Z(G)=G
so
G
is nilpotent of class at most
1
.

From a nilpotent group
G
, we can derive what is called the
lower central series

G_{0}\geqslant G_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant G_{n}.

Its terms are
G_{0}=G
and

G_{i+1}=[G,G_{i}]={\Bigl\langle[g,h]\bigm|g\in G,h\in G_{i}\Bigr\rangle}\leqslant G.

By
[g,h]
we mean the commutator of
g
and
h
and we adopt the convention

[g,h]={g}^{-1}{h}^{-1}gh.

Nilpotency of
G
guarantees that after finitely many steps, the trivial group appears as a term in the lower central series.
If
n
is the nilpotency class of
G
, then
G_{n}
is the first trivial term.

We call
G

free-nilpotent of class
n
and rank
k
if it has a
k
-element generating set and has no more relations than those absolutely necessary to make the group nilpotent.
To be precise, we get a presentation

G={\langle a_{1}\dots a_{k}\mid G_{n}=1\rangle}.

Every nilpotent group of class no larger than
n
on at most
k
generators is a quotient of the respective free-nilpotent group.
This is analogue to free groups or free-abelian groups in the categories of groups or abelian groups.

Note that if
S
is a generating set of
G
and
T
is a generating set of
G_{i}
, then
G_{i+1}
is generated by
\{[g,h]\mid g\in S,h\in T\}
.
In particular, from a generating set
S
of
G
, we may produce in a canonical way generating sets of every subgroup in the lower central series.

For any subgroup
H\leqslant G
we have that
H
is also nilpotent and
H_{i}\leqslant G_{i}
.
In particular, the nilpotency class of
H
is no larger than the nilpotency class of
G
.
If
G
is finitely generated, then so is
H
.

We want to study the relation between
\Sigma^{1}_{\mathrm{ord}}(G)
and finiteness properties of the kernels of maps onto nilpotent groups.
In order to do that, we are first going to understand partial orders on nilpotent groups.

As we will see later, it will at least in this work be enough to consider cases where
G
is finitely generated and the order is full and archimedean.
We make these assumptions whenever they are convenient.
Let us start by looking at some examples.

Example 4.1
.

((1))

Recall
2.18
constructing all full orders on finitely generated free-abelian groups.

((2))

Let
H
be the free-nilpotent group of class
2
and rank
2
.
It is also known as the
(discrete) Heisenberg group
.
To be specific, we have

H={\Bigl\langle a,b\bigm|1=[a,[a,b]]=[b,[a,b]]\Bigr\rangle}

Any element
g\in H
can be written uniquely as
g=a^{\alpha}b^{\beta}{{[{a},{b}]}}^{\gamma}
for some
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Any order on

\mathbb{Z}^{2}=H_{\mathrm{ab}}=H/{\langle{[{a},{b}]}\rangle}

induces an order on
H
via the projection map.
Even more, for any order on
H
the projection modulo
{[{a},{b}]}
is order preserving.
Thus any order is lexicographic with respect to the projection.

In particular, if the order we pick on
H_{\mathrm{ab}}
is non-trivial, then the only way to complete this to an archimedean order on
H
is the order induced by the projection.

So any archimedean order on
H
is either induced by the projection
H\twoheadrightarrow H_{\mathrm{ab}}
or it is one of the two orders

a^{\alpha}b^{\beta}{{[{a},{b}]}}^{\gamma}\mathrel{\succ}1\iff\alpha=\beta=0~\mathrm{and}~\gamma>0

and

a^{\alpha}b^{\beta}{{[{a},{b}]}}^{\gamma}\mathrel{\succ}1\iff\alpha=\beta=0~\mathrm{and}~\gamma<0

That is, any order is lexicographic with respect to the exact sequence

{\langle{[{a},{b}]}\rangle}\hookrightarrow H\twoheadrightarrow H_{\mathrm{ab}}.

To obtain an archimedean order, at least one of the two factors has to be ordered trivially by
2.16
.

((3))

Now let
G
be the free-nilpotent group of class
2
and rank
3
. That is

G={\Bigl\langle a,b,c\bigm|{[{x},{{[{y},{z}]}}]}=1~\forall x,y,z\in\{a,b,c\}\Bigr\rangle}

It contains the Heisenberg group
H
as the subgroup generated by
\{a,b\}
.
Any order on
G
therefore restricts to an order on
H
.
If
\mathrel{{\mathrel{\prec}}_{|{H}}}
is induced by an order on
H_{\mathrm{ab}}
, then
\mathrel{\prec}
is induced by an order on
G_{\mathrm{ab}}
.
If
\mathrel{{\mathrel{\prec}}_{|{H}}}
is one of the two archimedean orders such that
{[{a},{b}]}
and
1
are comparable, then
\mathrel{\prec}
is induced by one of the inclusions

\mathbb{Z}^{2}={\langle{[{a},{b}]},c\rangle}_{\mathrm{ab}}\hookrightarrow G

or

\mathbb{Z}^{3}\cong{\langle{[{a},{b}]},{[{a},{c}]},{[{b},{c}]}\rangle}_{\mathrm{ab}}=G_{1}\hookrightarrow G.

Up to choice of embedding of
H
and hence isomorphism of
G
, these are all archimedean orders.

For now, we omit the proof. At the end of this section we will have the tools to verify that these examples are indeed correct.

In the examples we see that the orders are largely determined by orders on
G_{\mathrm{ab}}
.
For instance in the third example if there is more than a single generator comparable to
1
, all of
G_{1}
is necessarily trivially ordered.
This leads to an intuition saying the more elements of
G\setminus G_{1}
are comparable to
1
, the fewer possibilities there are to extend an order on
G\setminus G_{1}
to an order on
G
.
With these ideas in mind, our goal is to make precise what orders on nilpotent groups look like.
The case of nilpotent groups of class
1
, that is, abelian groups, has already been dealt with in
2.20
.

Remark 4.2
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated free-abelian group and
\Phi\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then
\ker\Phi
is also finitely generated free-abelian.
And so is
G/\ker\Phi
, as any torsion element would have to be mapped by
\Phi
to some torsion element of
\mathbb{R}
, but
\mathbb{R}
is torsion-free.

Hence
\ker\Phi\oplus G/\ker\Phi
is also finitely generated free-abelian.
By counting dimensions we see that it is even isomorphic to
G
.

Take
\mathbb{R}
to be ordered by the standard order.
As
\Phi
factors through
G/\ker\Phi
,
\Phi
induces a total order on
G/\ker\Phi
and the projection map then induces an order on
G
.
This is the same order that
\Phi
induces on
G
.

Any isomorphism
G\cong\ker\Phi\oplus G/\ker\Phi
produces a free-abelian generating set of
G
from such sets for
\ker\Phi
and
G/\ker\Phi
.
That is, for any full archimedean order on
G
, if we make the right choice of free generators, every generator is either positive or incomparable to
1

and the positive cone is a subset of the subgroup spanned by the positive generators.
This is not true for arbitrary nilpotent groups.
For example in the Heisenberg group, it is possible that none of the generators is positive but their commutator is, as we have seen in
4.1
.

Now let us consider the case where
G
is a non-abelian nilpotent group.
Any order on the abelianisation
G_{\mathrm{ab}}
induces an order on
G
and these orders we already understand by
2.20
.
They are the ones such that
G_{1}
is an antichain.
For all other orders, the following lemma is a restriction on how the order on
G_{1}
may look like.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated ordered nilpotent group and
g,h\in G
such that
{[{g},{h}]}\mathrel{\prec}g\mathrel{\succ}1
.

Then
{[{g},{h}]}\ordll g
.

Proof.

Let
N={\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}g\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 9.02773pt\hskip-9.02773pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{4.75223pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{3.31525pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 8.19444pt\hskip-8.19444pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{3.53351pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{2.36806pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}G
be the normal subgroup generated by
g
and
n
the nilpotency class of
N
.
Note that
{[{g},{h}]}={g}^{-1}({h}^{-1}gh)\in N
.

Suppose that every element of
N_{i+1}
is infinitesimal with respect to
g
and that
{[{g},{h}]}\in N_{i}
.
Then for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
there is some
c\in N_{i+1}
(more precisely, it is some product of elements of the form
{[{x},{{[{g},{h}]}}]}
, where
x
is again of this form or
x=h
) such that

g{{[{g},{h}]}}^{-2k}=h^{2k}gh^{-2k}c\mathrel{\succ}h^{2k}h^{-2k}c=c

so

g^{2}\mathrel{\succ}{c}^{-1}g\mathrel{\succ}{{[{g},{h}]}}^{2k}

for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
As
\mathrel{\prec}
is full, we get
g\mathrel{\succ}{{[{g},{h}]}}^{k}
and hence
g\ordgg{[{g},{h}]}
.
The claim follows by repeated application of this argument as
N_{n}
is trivial and therefore in particular infinitesimal with respect to
g
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated partially ordered nilpotent group with a full archimedean order
\mathrel{\prec}
.
Then there is a normal subgroup
H\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 9.02773pt\hskip-9.02773pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{4.75223pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{3.31525pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 8.19444pt\hskip-8.19444pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{3.53351pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{2.36806pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}G
such that
\mathrel{\prec}
is induced by a total order on
Z(G/H)
.

That is, we may obtain any full archimedean order on
G
from the standard order on
\mathbb{R}
via the following chain of maps.

\mathbb{R}\hookleftarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cong Z(G/H)\hookrightarrow G/H\twoheadleftarrow G

Proof.

Suppose that
Z(G)
is totally ordered.
If
1\mathrel{\prec}g
for some
g\notin Z(G)
, we may choose some
h\in G
such that
{[{g},{h}]}\in Z(G)
.
By
4.3
we get
{[{g},{h}]}\ordll g
contradicting
\mathrel{\prec}
being archimedean.
Thus
{G}^{\mathrel{\succ}}\subseteq Z(G)
.
That is,
\mathrel{\prec}
is induced by the inclusion
Z(G)\hookrightarrow G
. So by letting
H=1
we see that the claim is true.

Otherwise, we do an induction. For this, we order finitely generated nilpotent groups as follows:
Let
A,B
be finitely generated nilpotent groups.
For any
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
A_{i}/A_{i+1}
is a finitely generated free-abelian group.
Set
\operatorname{rk}_{i}A\coloneqq\operatorname{rk}A_{i}/A_{i+1}
.
We say that
A
comes before
B
if and only if
\operatorname{rk}_{i}A<\operatorname{rk}_{i}B
for the largest
i
such that these ranks are not equal.

If
Z(G)
is not totally ordered, pick a maximal cyclic subgroup
C\leqslant Z(G)
that is an antichain.
As a subgroup of the center,
C
is automatically normal in
G
.
Since
\mathrel{\prec}
is full, it is induced by the projection
G\twoheadrightarrow G/C
.

Let
k
be the largest number such that
C\leqslant G_{k}
. Then we have

\operatorname{rk}_{i}G/C=\begin{cases}\operatorname{rk}_{i}G&\text{if}~i\neq k\\
\operatorname{rk}_{i}G-1&\text{if}~i=k\end{cases}

Note that
\operatorname{rk}_{i}G/C\leqslant\operatorname{rk}_{i}G
for any
i
and
rk_{k}G/C<\operatorname{rk}_{k}G
.
Hence by induction as explained above, we find that there is some
H^{\prime}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.55551pt\hskip-10.55551pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{6.26665pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{4.73611pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 9.02773pt\hskip-9.02773pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{4.75223pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{3.31525pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 8.19444pt\hskip-8.19444pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\leqslant$}$}\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{3.53351pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\rule{0.3pt}{2.36806pt}$}\rule{0.75pt}{0.0pt}$}}}}}G/C
, such that
\mathrel{\prec}
is induced by the projection
G\twoheadrightarrow G/C\twoheadrightarrow(G/C)/H^{\prime}
and the order on
(G/C)/H^{\prime}
is induced by a total order on its center.

By
2.11
,
\mathrel{\prec}
is then induced by the projection
G\twoheadrightarrow(G/C)/H^{\prime}
and setting
H
to be the kernel of this projection finishes the proof.
∎

Conversely, every choice of
H
such that
G/H
is torsion free and
\iota\colon Z(G/H)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}
induces a unique order on
G
.
Two choices
H,\iota
and
H^{\prime},\iota^{\prime}
yield the same order if and only if
H=H^{\prime}
and
\iota=\lambda\iota^{\prime}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Thus we get a full characterisation of all full archimedean orders on finitely generated nilpotent groups.

Remark 4.5
.

Note that if

is abelian,

, so we get that

is induced by

Hence we recover that every full archimedean order on

is induced by a map to
\mathbb{R}
.

To conclude the section, now is a good time to revisit

The center of the Heisenberg group is

Let

By

Let

We would like to know if

is also finitely generated.

For

Q

It states that

By

As we know from

Theorem 5.3

((2))

((3))
