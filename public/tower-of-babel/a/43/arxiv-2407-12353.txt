Non-equilibrium phase coexistence in boundary-driven diffusive systems
Shin-ichi Sasa, Naoko Nakagawa

Abstract
Liquid-gas phase coexistence in a boundary-driven diffusive system is studied by analyzing fluctuating hydrodynamics of a density field defined on a one-dimensional lattice with a space interval Λ \Lambda . When an interface width ℓ \ell is much larger than Λ \Lambda , the discrete model becomes the standard fluctuating hydrodynamics, where the phase coexistence condition is given by the local equilibrium thermodynamics. In contrast, when ℓ < Λ \ell<\Lambda , the most probable density profile is determined by a new variational principle, where the chemical potential at the interface is found to deviate from the equilibrium coexistence chemical potential. This means that metastable states at equilibrium stably appear near the interface as the influence of the particle current. The variational function derived in the theoretical analysis is also found to be equivalent to the variational function formulated in an extended framework of thermodynamics called global thermodynamics. Finally, the validity of the theoretical result is confirmed by numerical simulations.

Introduction

A rich variety of phenomena exhibit non-equilibrium phase coexistence, such as boiling heat transfer, pattern formation in crystal growth, and motility-induced phase separation
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Although many such impressive phenomena are dynamic and complex, a non-trivial and surprising phenomenon has been predicted in calm and simple phase coexistence out of equilibrium. One example is the quantitative prediction that, in liquid-gas coexistence under heat conduction, the temperature of the liquid-gas interface is lower than the equilibrium coexistence temperature for the pressure
[
9
,
10
]
, where the equilibrium phase coexistence occurs at the
first-order phase transition point far from the critical point. This phenomenon
means that metastable states at equilibrium are stabilized by a steady
current even in the linear response regime.

The prediction was presented in an extended framework of thermodynamics that we call
global thermodynamics
. This framework was first proposed as a natural extension of the minimum principle of free energy with the key concept of global temperature
[
9
]
. Applying the framework to a van-der Waals fluid revealed that the temperature of the liquid-gas interface is different from the first-order transition temperature at equilibrium. Then, the formulation was carefully arranged so that quantitative predictions could be made for real materials
[
10
]
. The equivalence among different ensembles was discussed, and finally, the maximum entropy principle was formulated for enthalpy-conserving heat conduction systems
[
11
]
. The entropy defined in the formulation is found to possess a non-additive term in addition to the space integral of the local entropy density field. This formulation enables us to apply global thermodynamics to various systems.

There have been no experimental reports on the predictions of global thermodynamics. Nevertheless, it is worth mentioning that numerical simulation of the Hamiltonian Potts model in heat conduction shows results consistent with the quantitative prediction of global thermodynamics for large enough systems
[
12
]
. The singular nature of the phase coexistence has also been discussed by analyzing a mesoscopic model describing the order parameter dynamics in heat conduction
[
13
]
. However, this analysis involves some phenomenological assumptions in calculating the singular part. Furthermore, the model is too complicated to extract the microscopic mechanism of the deviation in interface temperature from the equilibrium phase coexistence temperature.

On the basis of this background, we study a simple system that exhibits non-equilibrium phase coexistence. We consider a system in which the number density field is a single dynamical variable and the density field is directly driven by a boundary condition of the chemical potential, where the temperature is given as a constant parameter of the system. The stochastic time evolution of the density field is described in terms of a discrete form of fluctuating hydrodynamics with
a space interval
\Lambda
. When the width of an interface in phase coexistence
\ell
is much larger than
\Lambda
, the model is equivalent to the standard fluctuating hydrodynamics
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. Then, local fluctuation properties of thermodynamic quantities are described by local equilibrium distribution
[
18
]
. In contrast, when
\ell\ll\Lambda
, the local distribution may be out of equilibrium
[
19
]
. For this case,
we derive the variational function that determines the most probable density profile. We then find that the chemical potential at the interface
of the most probable profile deviates from the equilibrium coexistence
value. This means that metastable states at equilibrium stably appear
near the interface of the driven system.
The formula describing the deviation takes the same
form as those phenomenologically predicted by global thermodynamics.
Indeed, we can derive the variational function
for this system by using the method of global thermodynamics.
We also confirm the validity of the theoretical calculation by numerical
simulations.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
2
, we introduce a stochastic
model we study in this paper.
We then review phase coexistence conditions for equilibrium systems,

In Sec.
3

the Zubarev-McLennan representation of the stationary distribution.
In Sec.
4
, we rewrite the variational equation as the form
giving the chemical potential at the interface.
In Sec.
5
, we derive the variational function
using the method of global thermodynamics.
In Sec.
6
, we show results of
numerical simulations. Section
7
provides some
concluding remarks.

Setup

2.1
Model

Figure 1:

Schematics of a system we study. A one-dimensional density field is
defined on a ring with a battery at
x=0
.

We consider a collection of stochastic and diffusive particles in a closed tube
which are driven by an external battery at one surface
x=0
. See
Fig.
1
for the illustration of the setup.
We describe
the system by an averaged particle density
\rho(x)
defined in a
one-dimensional region
[0,L]
. More precisely, we define

\rho(x)\equiv\frac{1}{A}\int dydz~\rho(x,y,z)

(2.1)

for the three-dimensional particle density
\rho(x,y,z)
,
where the area of the cross section of the tube is denoted by
A
.
We then assume a standard continuum description of fluctuating hydrodynamics
of
\rho(x)
. The density field
\rho(x,t)
satisfies the continuity
equation

\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}j=0.

(2.2)

Based on the mean field picture in the cross section,
we assume the current as

j(x,t)=-\sigma(\rho(x))\left[\partial_{x}\frac{\delta{\cal F}}{\delta\rho(x)}-\phi\delta(x)\right]+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma(\rho(x))T}{A}}\cdot\xi(x,t).

(2.3)

T
is the temperature,
\sigma(\rho)
is a conductivity
as a smooth function of
\rho
, and

\phi
represents the voltage of a battery located at
x=0
.

\xi
is Gaussian-white noise satisfying
\left\langle\xi\right\rangle=0
and

\left\langle\xi(x,t)\xi(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(2.4)

The symbol “
\cdot
” in front of
\xi
in (
2.3
)
represents the Stratonovich product in space and the Ito product in time.
Here, because the variance of the surface average

\Xi(x,t)\equiv\int dydz\xi(x,y,z,t)/A

of the three-dimensional Gaussian-white noise
\xi(x,y,z,t)

with unit variance
is
1/A
, we set
\Xi(x,t)=\xi(x,t)/\sqrt{A}
, which determines
the
A
dependence of (
2.3
).
The free energy functional
{\cal F}
of the density profile

{\bm{\rho}}=(\rho(x))_{0\leq x\leq L}

is expressed as

{\cal F}({\bm{\rho}})=\int_{0}^{L}dx\left[f(\rho(x))+\frac{\kappa}{2}(\partial_{x}\rho)^{2}\right].

(2.5)

As a specific example, we consider the case that

f(\rho)=-\frac{1}{2}(\rho-1.5)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(\rho-1.5)^{4}

(2.6)

by introducing dimensionless length and energy in this form.
Note that our argument below is independent of
the specific form if
f(\rho)
contains two local minima.
The length unit is assumed to be the order of particle
distances.
\kappa
characterizes the interface free energy, which
is relevant when
\partial_{x}\rho
is large. In particular,
when
0.5<\bar{\rho}<2.5
in noiseless equilibrium
systems with
T=0
and
\phi=0
, phase coexistence occurs
with two interfaces. Then,
\sqrt{\kappa}
determines
the interface width in the phase coexistence.

Now, we notice that there
is a cutoff length
\Lambda
of the continuum description.
Because the noise is assumed to be white in space,

\Lambda
should be larger than the microscopic length,
which is set to the order of unity.
On the other hand, it is obvious that
\Lambda
should be
much smaller than the system size
L
. In many cases, the calculation
result of fluctuating hydrodynamics is independent of the cutoff

\Lambda
even in the limit
\Lambda\to 0
, while there is a case
where a singular cutoff dependence is observed
[
20
]
.
Here, let us recall that the width of interfaces in phase coexistence
is estimated as a microscopic length. Thus, this may be smaller than
the cutoff length of fluctuating hydrodynamics. Such a case cannot
be studied by the continuum model. We thus need to propose and analyze
a discrete model in which the microscopic cutoff
\Lambda
is
explicitly introduced.

With this background, we consider a sequence of
N
-boxes
in a one-dimensional ring.
Let
\rho_{i}
be the density of particles at the
i
-th box,
where
1\leq i\leq N
. Mathematically,
\rho_{i}
is defined
on the
i
-th site in the one-dimensional lattice

\{i|1\leq i\leq N,\ i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with
the periodic boundary condition
\rho_{0}\equiv\rho_{N}

and
\rho_{N+1}=\rho_{1}
. The horizontal size of the box is denoted
by
\Lambda
and the cross section area of the box is
A
.
The system size
L
is given by
L=\Lambda N
.
The free energy functional given in (
2.5
)
is then replaced with

{\cal F}({\bm{\rho}})=\Lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[f(\rho_{i})+\frac{\kappa}{2\Lambda^{2}}(\rho_{i+1}-\rho_{i})^{2}\right]

(2.7)

for
{\bm{\rho}}=(\rho_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
.
The density
\rho_{i}
satisfies the continuity equation

\frac{d\rho_{i}}{dt}+\frac{j_{i}-j_{i-1}}{\Lambda}=0

(2.8)

with
j_{0}=j_{N}
. The current
j_{i}
is defined on the

i
-th bond connecting the
i
-th site and the
(i+1)
-th site.
Using the generalized
chemical potential
\tilde{\mu}_{i}
defined by

\tilde{\mu}_{i}\equiv\frac{1}{\Lambda}\frac{\partial{\cal F}}{\partial\rho_{i}},

(2.9)

we replace the current given in (
2.3
) by

j_{i}(t)=-\frac{\sigma(\rho_{i}^{\rm m})}{\Lambda}(\tilde{\mu}_{i+1}-\tilde{\mu}_{i}-\phi\delta_{i,N})+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma(\rho_{i}^{\rm m})T}{A\Lambda}}\cdot\xi_{i}(t),

(2.10)

where we set
\rho^{\rm m}_{i}=(\rho_{i}+\rho_{i+1})/2
to satisfy the
detailed balance condition.
\xi_{i}(t)
is
Gaussian-white noise satisfying
\left\langle\xi_{i}\right\rangle=0
and

\left\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(2.11)

The
\Lambda
dependence of the noise intensity in
(
2.10
) is understood from the replacement of

\delta(x-x^{\prime})
with
\delta_{ij}/\Lambda
.
Explicitly,
\tilde{\mu}_{i}
is written as

\tilde{\mu}_{i}=\mu(\rho_{i})-\frac{\kappa}{\Lambda^{2}}(\rho_{i+1}+\rho_{i-1}-2\rho_{i})

(2.12)

with
\mu(\rho_{i})=\partial f(\rho_{i})/\partial\rho_{i}
.
The total number of particles
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}=\bar{\rho}N

is conserved in the time evolution. The average density
\bar{\rho}

is a parameter of the system.

The steady state of this system is characterized by five parameters

(\kappa_{\Lambda},T_{\rm eff},\phi,\bar{\rho},N)
, where

\kappa_{\Lambda}\equiv\frac{\kappa}{\Lambda^{2}},\qquad T_{\rm eff}\equiv\frac{T}{A}.

(2.13)

That is, systems with the same values of
(\kappa_{\Lambda},T_{\rm eff},\phi,\bar{\rho},N)

exhibit the same steady state. In the argument below, the

\Lambda
dependence appear only through
\kappa_{\Lambda}
dependence.
When
\kappa_{\Lambda}\gg 1
and
N\to\infty
,
the system behavior of (
2.8
) and (
2.10
)
is understood by analyzing (
2.2
) and (
2.3
)
because (
2.8
) and (
2.10
) correspond to
an accurate approximation of (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).
In contrast,
the system behavior for the case
\kappa_{\Lambda}<1
cannot be
described by (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).
For such cases, we have to analyze the discrete model with focusing
on the limiting case that
\kappa_{\Lambda}\ll 1
. See Fig.
2

for the illustration of two limiting cases.
Finally, we note that

A\gg 1
because
A
is the square of
a macroscopic length. We thus consider the weak noise limit

T_{\rm eff}\to 0
for the steady state realized in the limit
t\to\infty
.

\kappa_{\Lambda}\ll 1
and (b)
\kappa_{\Lambda}\gg 1
.

2.2
Review of equilibrium phase coexistence

We review phase coexistence states for the equilibrium system
with
\phi=0
.
The stationary distribution of
{\bm{\rho}}
is given by

{\cal P}_{{\rm eq}}({\bm{\rho}};\bar{\rho})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{T_{\rm eff}}{\cal F}({\bm{\rho}})\right]\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}-\bar{\rho}N\right).

(2.14)

See Appendix
A
for the derivation.
The most probable profile in the weak noise limit
T_{\rm eff}\to 0
is
determined as the density profile that minimizes
{\cal F}({\bm{\rho}})

defined by (
2.7
).
The variational equation is obtained as

\tilde{\mu}_{i}=\tilde{\mu}_{i+1},

(2.15)

which means that
\tilde{\mu}_{i}
is a constant independent of
i
.

We study two limiting cases
\kappa_{\Lambda}\gg 1
and
\kappa_{\Lambda}\ll 1
.
First, for the case
\kappa_{\Lambda}\gg 1
,
we can derive the phase coexistence condition from the analysis
of the continuum limit of (
2.15
) with
N\to\infty
,
which is given in (
B.2
). Importantly, the solution of the
variational equation is unique under the boundary condition that

\partial_{x}\rho(0)=\partial_{x}\rho(L)=0
with
\rho(0)>\rho(L)
.
As shown in Appendix
B
, phase coexistence occurs when

\bar{\rho}
satisfies
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}\leq\bar{\rho}\leq\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
,
where
\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
and
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
are determined by

\mu(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c})=\mu(\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}),\qquad p(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c})=p(\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c})

(2.16)

with the thermodynamic pressure
p(\rho)
defined by

p(\rho)\equiv\rho\mu(\rho)-f(\rho).

(2.17)

Hereafter, the equilibrium value of the coexistence chemical potential
is denoted by
\mu_{c}\equiv\mu(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c})=\mu(\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c})
.
We assume
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}<\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
without loss of generality.
Then,
\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
and
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
represent the densities of the liquid
and gas in the phase coexistence, respectively.
Furthermore, once
\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
and
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}

are obtained, the fraction of the liquid region
X^{\rm eq}
is uniquely
determined from
\Lambda\sum_{i}\rho_{i}=\bar{\rho}L
,
which is expressed by

\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}X^{\rm eq}+\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}(1-X^{\rm eq})=\bar{\rho}.

(2.18)

See Fig.
3
(c) for the profile.

Figure 3:

Illustration of metastable profile and equilibrium profile.

For the other case
\kappa_{\Lambda}\ll 1
, which we mainly study in this paper,
the derivation method in the continuum limit cannot be applied. However,
even in this case, the variational principle determines the most probable
profile.
The variational equation (
2.15
) is rewritten as
\mu_{i}=\mu_{i+1}

in the limit
\kappa_{\Lambda}\to 0
so that the chemical potential is uniform.
In this case, there is a one-parameter family of solutions

\rho_{i;X}^{\phi=0}
characterized
by
X\in[0.1]
, where
NX
is an half integer.
Let us
write the solution
\rho_{i;X}^{\phi=0}
explicitly.
Referring to Fig.
3
(a),
for a given
X
,
we set
{\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}
and
{\rho}^{\rm G}_{X}

as those satisfying

\mu({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X})=\mu({\rho}^{\rm G}_{X}),\qquad X{\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}+(1-X){\rho}^{\rm G}_{X}=\bar{\rho}

(2.19)

with
{\rho}^{\rm G}_{X}<{\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}
.
We can then express the solution as

\rho_{i;X}^{\phi=0}={\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}\chi(i\in[1,NX])+{\rho}^{\rm G}_{X}\chi(i\in[NX,N]),

(2.20)

where
\chi(P)
represents the
characteristic function that takes
1
if
P
is true
and
0
otherwise. From Fig.
3
(b),
it is found that
X
corresponds to the interface position
of
\rho_{i;X}^{\phi=0}
.
Note that the free energy of the solutions is smaller
than the other solutions with more interfaces as far as
\kappa_{\Lambda}>0
.
The uniform value of the chemical potential for the
solution
\rho_{i;X}^{\phi=0}
is denoted by
\mu_{X}^{\phi=0}
,
which is equal to
\mu({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X})
and
\mu({\rho}^{\rm G}_{X})
.

Note that the value of
X
is not determined
from the variational equation (
2.15
) with
\kappa_{\Lambda}\to 0
.
Physically, the solutions
{\bm{\rho}}^{\phi=0}_{X}
form a
family of metastable states characterized by
X
.
The most probable value of
X
, which is denoted by
X_{*}
,
is derived from the minimum free energy principle formulated
as follows. We first define the variational function

{\cal F}_{\rm eq}(X;\bar{\rho})\equiv{\cal F}({\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi=0}).

(2.21)

Then, the minimum free energy principle means

{\cal F}_{\rm eq}(X_{*};\bar{\rho})=\min_{X}{\cal F}_{\rm eq}(X;\bar{\rho}),

(2.22)

where, in the present case,
X_{*}
satisfies

\left.\frac{d{\cal F}_{\rm eq}(X;\bar{\rho})}{dX}\right|_{X=X_{*}}=0.

(2.23)

Substituting the expression

{\cal F}({\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi=0})=LXf({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X})+L(1-X)f({\rho}^{\rm G}_{X})

(2.24)

into (
2.23
), we obtain

f({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}})-f({\rho}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})=\mu_{X_{*}}^{\phi=0}({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-{\rho}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})

(2.25)

using (
2.19
) and (
2.19
).
Comparing (
2.19
) and (
2.25
) with (
2.16
), we find

\mu_{c}=\mu_{X_{*}}^{\phi=0},\quad\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}={\rho}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}},\qquad\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}={\rho}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}.

(2.26)

Moreover,
X_{*}
is equal to
X^{\rm eq}
.
The result means that the equilibrium states for the two
limiting cases,
\kappa_{\Lambda}\gg 1
and
\kappa_{\Lambda}\ll 1
, are equivalent.

2.3
Preliminaries for non-equilibrium phase coexistence

We concentrate on the case that
\bar{\rho}
satisfies

\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}<\bar{\rho}<\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
where liquid and gas coexist.
When the voltage of the battery
\phi
is positive, the stationary
distribution is not written as the canonical form (
2.14
).
Therefore, we do not have a general variational principle for determining
the most probable profile for
\phi>0
. Nevertheless, we divide the problem for determining the most probable
profile into two steps. As the first step, we consider stationary solutions
of the deterministic equations without noise. If the stationary solution is
unique, this is the most probable profile in the weak noise limit. In contrast,
if stationary solutions form a one parameter family, we proceed to the second
step, where we will formulate a variational principle for selecting the most
probable profile among the stationary solutions as we have examined
in (
2.22
) for the equilibrium system. In this section, we focus
on the first step.

We consider stationary solutions of the deterministic
equation given in (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) with

T_{\rm eff}=0
. Let
J
be the steady particle current induced by
the battery. Setting
j_{i}(t)=J
for all
i
in (
2.3
),
we obtain the conduction equation

\frac{1}{\Lambda}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{i+1}-\tilde{\mu}_{i}+\phi\delta_{i,N}\right)=-\frac{J}{\sigma(\rho_{i}^{\rm m})}.

(2.27)

The uniformity of
J
yields

J=\phi\left(\Lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sigma(\rho_{i}^{\rm m})}\right)^{-1}.

(2.28)

When
\kappa_{\Lambda}\gg 1
and
N\to\infty
with
L=N\Lambda
and

\kappa=\kappa_{\Lambda}\Lambda^{2}
fixed, we can analyze the continuum
limit of (
2.27
). As shown in Appendix
B
,
we find that the solution of the deterministic equation is
unique and that the chemical potential and pressure are
continuous at the interface in the space
\hat{x}=x/L
with
L\to\infty
. We can then conclude that the chemical potential
at the interface is
\mu_{\rm c}
. See Appendix
B
for the derivation
of this result.

In contrast, when we first fix
\kappa_{\Lambda}\ll 1
and take the
limit
N\to\infty
,
there is a family of stationary solutions of the conduction
equation (
2.27
).
In the equilibrium system, they correspond to metastable states
characterized by (
2.19
), as shown in Fig.
3
(b),
and the metastable states were candidates for the most probable
profile. We thus expect that a family of stationary solutions

\rho_{i;X}^{\phi}
for
\phi>0
correspond to metastable states
among which the most probable profile is selected.
In the remainder of this section, we express the metastable
states explicitly as a preliminary for the analysis in the next section.

We concentrate on small
\phi
and ignore the contribution of
O(\phi^{2})
.
The solution
\rho_{i;X}^{\phi}
should be given as a perturbation of
the equilibrium solution
\rho_{i;X}^{\phi=0}
given in (
2.20
).
We thus assume that

|\rho_{i;X}^{\phi}-\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}|<|\rho_{i;X}^{\phi}-\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}|
for
1\leq i<NX
,
and
|\rho_{i;X}^{\phi}-\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}|<|\rho_{i;X}^{\phi}-\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}|
for
NX<i\leq N
.
Letting

\sigma^{L}=\sigma(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}),\qquad\sigma^{G}=\sigma(\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}),

(2.29)

the conduction equation (
2.27
) with
\kappa_{\Lambda}\to 0
results in

\displaystyle\mu(\rho_{i;X}^{\phi})=-\frac{1}{\sigma^{L}}JL\frac{i-1}{N}+\mu_{1:X}^{\phi}+O(\phi^{2}),\qquad(1\leq i\leq NX),

(2.30)

\displaystyle\mu(\rho_{i;X}^{\phi})=-\frac{1}{\sigma^{G}}JL\frac{i-N}{N}+\mu_{N;X}^{\phi}+O(\phi^{2}),\qquad(NX<i\leq N),

(2.31)

where

\mu_{1;X}^{\phi}=\mu_{N;X}^{\phi}+\phi.

(2.32)

From the direct calculation, we also obtain the
difference between the chemical potentials of two adjacent sites
over the interface as

\mu(\rho_{X-1/(2N);X}^{\phi})-\mu(\rho_{X+1/(2N);X}^{\phi})=O(\phi/N).

(2.33)

Finally, the condition

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i;X}^{\phi}=\bar{\rho}

(2.34)

uniquely determines
\mu_{1;X}^{\phi}
and
\rho_{1;X}^{\phi}

for a given
X
. We can easily confirm that

\lim_{\phi\to 0}\rho_{i;X}^{\phi}
is equivalent to (
2.20
).

2.4
Singular continuum description

We introduce a continuum description
using a real variable
\hat{x}=x/L
defined on the interval
[0,1]
by taking
the limit
N\to\infty
. The mathematical formulation
of this limit
is beyond the scope of the present paper. However, to simplify the
calculation in the subsequent sections, we naively introduce the continuum
description based on expected behaviors of
\rho_{i}(t)
for large
N
.
For a discrete variable

{\bm{\rho}}=(\rho_{i}(t))_{i=1}^{N}
at
time
t
, we first define
\rho_{N}(\hat{x},\hat{t})
as the piece-wise
linear function
obtained by connecting two consecutive points
(i/N,\rho_{i}(t))

and
((i+1)/N,\rho_{i+1}(t))
for
0\leq i\leq N

in the
(\hat{x},\rho)

space, where we set
\hat{t}=t/L^{2}
for later convenience.
For sufficiently large
N
, we expect that there exists an
almost continuous function
\rho(\hat{x},\hat{t})
such that

|\rho(\hat{x},\hat{t})-\rho_{N}(\hat{x},\hat{t})|=O(1/N)
. The chemical potential

\mu(\hat{x},\hat{t})
in the continuum description is defined from

\mu_{i}(t)
by the same procedure and it is expected that

\mu(\hat{x},\hat{t})=\mu(\rho(\hat{x},\hat{t}))
. The definition of

j(\hat{x},\hat{t})

is slightly different from
\rho(\hat{x},\hat{t})
and

To simplify the notation, we use
x

and

In the previous section, we determined the candidates of steady density

\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x)
characterized by

limit

among them for given system parameters
(\bar{\rho},\phi)
,
in Sec.

we derive a variational function using the Zubarev-McLennan

3.1
Stationary distribution

We consider the stationary distribution

{\cal P}_{\rm ss}({\bm{\rho}};\bar{\rho},\phi)

of density field
{\bm{\rho}}
.
When
\phi=0
, the stationary distribution is given by
(
2.14
). However, the stationary distribution
for the system with
\phi>0
is not generally obtained.
Nevertheless,
in the linear response regime out of equilibrium,
there is a useful expression
called the Zubarev-McLennan representation

{\cal P}_{{\rm ss}}({\bm{\rho}};\bar{\rho},\phi)={\cal P}_{{\rm eq}}({\bm{\rho}};\bar{\rho})\exp\left[-\frac{\phi\left\langle Q\right\rangle^{{\rm eq}}_{{\bm{\rho}}}+O(\phi^{2})}{T_{\rm eff}}\right]

(3.1)

with

Q=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~j_{N}(t),

(3.2)

where
j_{N}(t)
is a fluctuating current at the
N
-th bond,
which is defined in (
2.10
). The
N
-th bond is
the only bond on which the driving
\phi
is imposed.

\left\langle\ \right\rangle^{{\rm eq}}_{{\bm{\rho}}}
denotes the conditioned expectation for
the equilibrium path ensemble provided that the initial density profile is
given by the specified
{\bm{\rho}}
as the
argument of the stationary distribution

[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.
See Appendix
C
for the derivation of (
3.1
).

For the general expression (
3.1
) with (
3.2
),
we take the limit
\kappa_{\Lambda}\to 0
and consider the singular continuum
description introduced in the previous section.
From (
2.14
) and (
3.1
), we have

{\cal P}_{{\rm ss}}({\bm{\rho}};\bar{\rho},\phi)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{T_{\rm eff}}({\cal F}({\bm{\rho}})+\phi\left\langle Q\right\rangle^{{\rm eq}}_{{\bm{\rho}}}+O(\phi^{2})+{\rm const})\right]\delta\left(\int_{0}^{1}dx\rho(x)-\bar{\rho}\right)

(3.3)

with

Q=L\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~j(0,t).

(3.4)

Note that
j_{N}(t)
becomes
j(0,t)=j(1,t)
in the singular
continuum description. The most probable density profile is identified
as
\rho_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(x)
that minimizes

-\log{\cal P}_{\rm ss}({\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi};\bar{\rho},\phi)
.
This is the variational principle to determine
X_{*}
.
From (
3.3
), we obtain the variational function as

{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)={\cal F}({\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi})+\phi\left\langle Q\right\rangle_{{\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi}}^{\rm eq}+O(\phi^{2}).

(3.5)

We then find
X_{*}
as the special value of
X

that minimizes the variational function

{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)
with
(\bar{\rho},\phi)
fixed.
That is,
X_{*}
is determined as

{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X_{*};\bar{\rho},\phi)=\min_{X}{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi).

(3.6)

We thus need to calculate
\left\langle Q\right\rangle_{{\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi}}^{\rm eq}
.

we have to know the typical time evolution of

starting from

\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x)
at

under the equilibrium condition
\phi=0

in the weak noise
limit
T_{\rm eff}\to 0
, where

is taken after

considered. Here, from (

only

are necessary for the calculation because
\phi

absorbed into

\mu_{X}^{\phi}(x)=\mu_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)+O(\phi),\qquad\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x)=\rho_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)+O(\phi).

(3.7)

See Fig.
5

\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x)
and

\rho_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)

for

Using these relations, we find

\left\langle Q\right\rangle_{{\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi}}^{\rm eq}=\left\langle Q\right\rangle_{{\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi=0}}^{\rm eq}+O(\phi).

(3.8)

That is, we study the typical time evolution of the density field starting from

\rho_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)
. Since we study the case
\kappa_{\Lambda}\to 0
,

\rho_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)
represents a metastable profile that does not evolve
in time without noises.
Nevertheless, weak noise slowly drives the metastable profile

\rho_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)
to the equilibrium state characterized by
(
2.14
). One may conjecture that the
most probable time evolution from
\rho_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)

in the weak noise limit is described by the relaxation process
to the equilibrium profile
\rho_{X_{*}}^{\phi=0}(x)

in Fig.
3
(c)
from the metastable profile in Fig.
3
(b).
However, this is not correct because of the space translational symmetry
of equilibrium systems. Note that metastable profiles for a given width

X
of the liquid region form a one-parameter
family of profiles obtained by any space translation
of
\rho_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)
, and
similarly equilibrium profiles also form a one-parameter family.
Thus, stochastic dynamics in this neutral direction are equally probable,
which are represented by Brownian motion of the profile with the width
of the liquid region fixed.
In other words, most probable process is not uniquely determined
in the weak noise limit. We have to
consider a collection of trajectories that are equally probable
and dominantly contribute to
\left\langle Q\right\rangle_{{\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi=0}}^{\rm eq}
.
We call this collection
the highly probable path ensemble

to distinguish it with the most probable process.

Here, we concretely describe the highly probable path ensemble
starting from the initial condition
\rho_{X}^{\phi=0}(x)
.
We assume that each trajectory in the highly probable path ensemble
satisfies the following two conditions. First, the liquid or gas region
is not separated into smaller pieces of liquid or gas at any time
t
.
That is, the number of interfaces in the system is always two.
Second, slow dynamics
occurs along a continuous family of metastable states by the
influence of weak noise, where the metastable states are characterized
by the position of two interfaces.
We then consider the time evolution
\rho(x,t)
as
follows.
Let
D^{\rm L}(t)
and
D^{\rm G}(t)
denote the liquid region and gas region at
any time
t
. Recalling the relation (
2.20
) with (
2.19
),
we express
\rho(x,t)
in the highly probable
path ensemble as

\rho(x,t)=\rho^{\rm L}(t)\chi(x\in D^{\rm L}(t))+\rho^{\rm G}(t)\chi(x\in D^{\rm G}(t)),

(3.9)

where

\rho^{\rm L}(t)
and
\rho^{\rm G}(t)

are determined from

\mu(\rho^{\rm L}(t))=\mu(\rho^{\rm G}(t))

(3.10)

and

\rho^{\rm L}(t)|D^{\rm L}(t)|+\rho^{\rm G}(t)|D^{\rm G}(t)|=\bar{\rho}.

(3.11)

It should be noted that we adopt the singular continuum description introduced
in the previous section. Using this form
of the time evolution, we estimate (
3.2
).

Because
\rho(x,t)
in (
3.9
) is described by
D^{\rm L}(t)
and

D^{\rm G}(t)
, we explicitly express them in terms of
the interface positions

X_{\uparrow}(t)
and
X_{\downarrow}(t)
at time
t
, where they satisfy

\rho(X_{\uparrow}(t)+\epsilon,t)-\rho(X_{\uparrow}(t)-\epsilon,t)>0,\quad\rho(X_{\downarrow}(t)+\epsilon,t)-\rho(X_{\downarrow}(t)-\epsilon,t)<0

for small positive

. See Fig.
6

Then,
the liquid region

and gas region
D^{\rm G}(t)

are expressed as

D^{\rm L}(t)=[X_{\uparrow}(t),X_{\downarrow}(t)]
and
D^{\rm G}(t)=[0,1]\backslash D^{\rm L}(t)

, or
D^{\rm G}(t)=[X_{\downarrow}(t),X_{\uparrow}(t)]

and
D^{\rm L}(t)=[0,1]\backslash D^{\rm G}(t)

.

Now, we consider the time evolution of interface positions,

X_{\uparrow}(t)
and
X_{\downarrow}(t)
, starting from

and
X_{\downarrow}(0)=X
.
However, because

{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)\in\mathbb{R}

{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)\in\mathbb{R}

to

{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)-{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(0)

That is,
{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)
and
{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)

of the left and right interfaces of the liquid region

\mathbb{R}
.
The interface positions

X_{\downarrow}(t)

\displaystyle X_{\uparrow}(t)={\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)-\lfloor{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)\rfloor,\qquad X_{\downarrow}(t)={\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)-\lfloor{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)\rfloor,

where

\displaystyle|D^{\rm L}(t)|={\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)-{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),\quad|D^{\rm G}(t)|=1-|D^{\rm L}(t)|={\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)-{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)+1

(3.15)

Using the width
|D^{\rm L}(t)|

\displaystyle{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)=\hat{Y}(t)+\frac{|D^{\rm L}(t)|}{2},\qquad{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)=\hat{Y}(t)-\frac{|D^{\rm L}(t)|}{2}.

(3.16)

while

\displaystyle q(\tau)=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{|D^{\rm L}(\tau)|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}(\tau)|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{D^{\rm L}(\tau)}dx~\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{D^{\rm G}(\tau)}dx~\psi\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\left[\frac{X}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{1-X}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{0}^{X}dx~\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{X}^{1}dx~\psi\right].

(3.28)

From the piece-wise linear nature of
\psi(x,t)
, the integrals are calculated by the trapezoidal rule as

\displaystyle\int_{D^{\rm L}}dx~\psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}|D^{\rm L}|\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)+\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)\right),

(3.29)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{\rm G}}dx~\psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}|D^{\rm G}|\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)+\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)\right),

(3.30)

where
\psi(X_{\uparrow}(t)+1)=\psi(X_{\uparrow}(t))
is applied.
Using these relations, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{D^{\rm L}}dx~\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{D^{\rm G}}dx~\psi=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{|D^{\rm L}|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right)\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)+\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)\right).

(3.31)

Substituting this into (
3.28
), we have

\displaystyle q(\tau)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(\tau),\tau)+\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(\tau),\tau)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi(0,0)+\psi(X,0)\right).

(3.32)

Here,

\displaystyle\psi(0,0)=0,\qquad\psi(X,0)=({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}-\bar{\rho})X,

(3.33)

and

\displaystyle\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(\tau),\tau)=\psi(X_{\uparrow}(\tau),\tau),\qquad\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(\tau),\tau)=\psi(X_{\downarrow}(\tau),\tau).

(3.34)

We thus obtain

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\left\langle q(\tau)\right\rangle^{{\rm eq}}_{{\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi=0}\to*}=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\left(\left\langle{\psi(X_{\uparrow}(\tau),\tau)}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\psi(X_{\downarrow}(\tau),\tau)}\right\rangle\right)-\frac{1}{2}({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}-\bar{\rho})X.

(3.35)

Note that the positions of the interfaces,
X_{\uparrow}
and
X_{\downarrow}
,
are uniformly distributed in the interval
x\in[0,1]

as
\tau\to\infty
.
We thus conclude that

\left\langle Q\right\rangle^{{\rm eq}}_{{\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi=0}}=L\left[C-\frac{1}{2}({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}-\bar{\rho})X\right],

(3.36)

where
C
is a constant independent of
X
.
Using the relation

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}=X{\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}+(1-X){\rho}^{\rm G}_{X},

(3.37)

which comes from (
2.43
),
we can also express (
3.36
) as

\left\langle Q\right\rangle^{{{\rm eq}}}_{{\bm{\rho}}_{X}^{\phi=0}}=L\left[C-\frac{1}{2}({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}-{\rho}^{\rm G}_{X})X(1-X)\right].

(3.38)

Substituting (
3.38
) into (
3.5
), we have

{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)=L\left[\int_{0}^{1}dx~f(\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x))-\frac{\phi}{2}({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}-{\rho}^{\rm G}_{X})X(1-X)+\phi C\right].

(3.39)

We note that
\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x)
is uniquely determined
for given
(X,\phi,\bar{\rho})
by
\mu(\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x))=\mu_{X}^{\phi}(x)

with (
2.39
) and (
2.40
) and that

{\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}
and
{\rho}^{\rm G}_{X}
are functions of
(X,\bar{\rho})
.
Thus,
{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)
is the variational function
for determining the most probable interface position
X_{*}

for a given
(\bar{\rho},\phi)
. The most probable density
profile is expressed as
\rho_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(x)
.

4
Variational equation

In this section, using the variational function (
3.39
),
we determine the most probable value of
X
.
This is regarded as an extension of the argument for determining the
equilibrium profile in the paragraph containing (
2.23
).
Thus, similarly to (
2.23
), we analyze
the variational equation

\left.\frac{d{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)}{dX}\right|_{X=X_{*}}=0

(4.1)

with (
3.39
). The difference from (
2.23
)
is the
O(\phi)
terms in (
3.39
).
As shown in Fig.
4
,
one of the interfaces of
\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x)
is located at
x=0

for any
X
, both the liquid and gas density profiles
are sloped, and the chemical potential profile
\mu_{X}^{\phi}(x)

is piece-wise linear. Then, as did for the equilibrium system,
we first derive the chemical potential at the interface

\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}
defined by
(
2.38
) instead of directly calculating
X_{*}
.
Once we have
\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}
,
we obtain the most probable profiles
\mu_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(x)
and

\rho_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(x)
, and the value of
X_{*}
. The determination
of
\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}
is also physically important because
if
\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}\neq\mu_{\rm c}
,
metastable states at equilibrium stably appear in the
non-equilibrium phase coexistence.

4.1
Preliminaries for the calculation

In the argument below, we ignore the contribution of
O(\phi^{2})
.
We first define

{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}\equiv\frac{1}{X}\int_{0}^{X}dx\rho^{\phi}_{X}(x),\quad{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}\equiv\frac{1}{1-X}\int_{X}^{1}dx\rho^{\phi}_{X}(x),

(4.2)

and

{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}\equiv\frac{1}{X}\int_{0}^{X}dx\mu^{\phi}_{X}(x),\quad{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}\equiv\frac{1}{1-X}\int_{X}^{1}dx\mu^{\phi}_{X}(x).

(4.3)

Because the density profile
\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x)
and the chemical potential
profile
\mu_{X}^{\phi}(x)
are linear in the respective regions,

[0,X]
and
[X,1]
, we obtain

f\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}\right)=\frac{1}{X}\int_{0}^{X}dxf(\rho^{\phi}_{X}(x)),\qquad f\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}\right)=\frac{1}{1-X}\int_{X}^{1}dxf(\rho^{\phi}_{X}(x)),

(4.4)

and

{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}=\mu({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}),\qquad{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}=\mu({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}).

(4.5)

The pressures in the liquid and gas regions are characterized
by
p^{\rm L}_{X}\equiv p({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X})
and
p^{\rm G}_{X}\equiv p({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X})
, which are
expressed by

\displaystyle p^{\rm L}_{X}=\mu^{\rm L}_{X}{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}-f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}),\qquad p^{\rm G}_{X}=\mu^{\rm G}_{X}{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}-f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}).

(4.6)

The first term on the right side of (
3.39
) is written as

\int_{0}^{1}dx~f(\rho_{X}^{\phi}(x))=Xf\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}\right)+(1-X)f\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}\right)+O(\phi^{2}),

(4.7)

and the variational function (
3.5
) as

\displaystyle{{\cal F}_{\rm ss}}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)=L\left[Xf\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}\right)+(1-X)f\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}\right)-\frac{\phi}{2}\left({\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}-{\rho}^{\rm G}_{X}\right)X(1-X)+\phi C\right].

(4.8)

Since
{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}={\rho}^{\rm L}_{X}+O(\phi)
and

{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}={\rho}^{\rm G}_{X}+O(\phi)
, we can rewrite (
4.8
) as

\displaystyle{{\cal F}_{\rm ss}}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)=L\left[Xf\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}\right)+(1-X)f\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}\right)-\frac{\phi}{2}\left({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}\right)X(1-X)+\phi C\right].

(4.9)

We emphasize that (
4.9
) is explicitly expressed as a function of
X
.

Noting that the chemical potential profile is piece-wise linear as shown
in Fig.
4
,
we estimate

\displaystyle{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}=\frac{\mu_{X}^{\phi}(0)+\mu_{X}^{\rm I}}{2},\qquad{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}=\frac{\mu_{X}^{\phi}(1)+\mu_{X}^{\rm I}}{2}.

(4.10)

From (
4.10
),
\phi=\mu_{X}^{\phi}(1)-\mu_{X}^{\phi}(0)
is rewritten as

{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}-{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}=\frac{\phi}{2}.

(4.11)

Furthermore, using (
2.39
) and (
2.40
),
we can express
{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}
and
{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}
in terms
of
\mu_{X}^{\rm I}
as

\displaystyle{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}=\mu_{X}^{\rm I}+\frac{JL}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\frac{X}{2},\quad{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}=\mu_{X}^{\rm I}-\frac{JL}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\frac{1-X}{2}.

(4.12)

4.2
Steady state

We consider the variational equation

\frac{d{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)}{dX}=0.

(4.13)

Substituting (
4.9
) into (
4.13
), we have

\displaystyle f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X})-f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X})-\frac{\phi}{2}({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X})(1-2X)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}\frac{d{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}}{dX}X+{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}\frac{d{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}}{dX}(1-X)-\frac{\phi}{2}\left(\frac{d{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}}{dX}-\frac{d{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}}{dX}\right)X(1-X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(4.14)

Using (
4.11
), we rewrite the second line as

\displaystyle[X{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}+(1-X){\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}]\left[X\frac{d{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}}{dX}+(1-X)\frac{d{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}}{dX}\right].

(4.15)

Here, taking the derivative of
\bar{\rho}=X{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}+(1-X){\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}
in
X
,
we obtain

\displaystyle X\frac{d{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}}{dX}+(1-X)\frac{d{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}}{dX}=-({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X}).

(4.16)

We substitute this into (
4.15
) and combine it with
(
4.14
). Then, the variational equation (
4.14
) becomes

\displaystyle f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X})-f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X})-({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X})[(1-X){\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X}+X{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X}]=0.

(4.17)

The solution of (
4.17
) provides the most probable value

X_{*}
of the interface position. Therefore, we express

\displaystyle f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}})-f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})-({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})[(1-X_{*}){\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}+X{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}]=0.

(4.18)

Now, we rewrite (
4.18
) as a different form using the chemical
potential at the interface position
X_{*}
.
We subtract the equilibrium version of (
4.18
),
i.e.,
f(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c})-f(\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c})-(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}-\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c})\mu_{\rm c}=0
, from (
4.18
).
Noting
f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}})-f(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c})=({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c})\mu_{\rm c}+O(\phi^{2})
,
we obtain

\displaystyle({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})\left\{\mu_{\rm c}-[(1-X_{*}){\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}+X_{*}{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}]\right\}=0.

(4.19)

Substituting (
4.12
) into this equation and
using
{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}\neq{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}
, we have

\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}=\mu_{\rm c}-\frac{JLX_{*}(1-X_{*})}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right).

(4.20)

Because
X_{*}=X^{\rm eq}+O(\phi)
, we can rewrite it as

\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}=\mu_{\rm c}-\frac{JLX^{\rm eq}(1-X^{\rm eq})}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right).

(4.21)

Furthermore, combining (
2.42
) with
(
4.21
), we finally obtain

\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}=\mu_{\rm c}+\frac{\phi}{2}\frac{(\sigma^{\rm L}-\sigma^{\rm G})X^{\rm eq}(1-X^{\rm eq})}{\sigma^{\rm G}X^{\rm eq}+\sigma^{\rm L}(1-X^{\rm eq})}.

(4.22)

Recalling that
X^{\rm eq}
is uniquely determined by
\bar{\rho}

from (
2.18
), we conclude that
\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}
is
expressed in terms of the system parameters.
Thus, the chemical potential at the interface deviates
from
\mu_{\rm c}
linearly with the voltage
\phi
. This means
that metastable states at equilibrium stably appear
around the interface. The relation (
4.22
)
is the most important achievement of our theory.

Next, from (
4.11
) and (
4.19
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}=\mu_{\rm c}+X^{\rm eq}\frac{\phi}{2},\quad{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}=\mu_{\rm c}-(1-X^{\rm eq})\frac{\phi}{2},

(4.23)

which also gives
\mu_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(0)
and
\mu_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(1)

using (
4.10
).
The result yields
\mu_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(x)
. Using

\mu_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(x)=\mu(\rho_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(x))
, we have
\rho_{X_{*}}^{\phi}(x)
.
Finally, from
X_{*}{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}+(1-X_{*}){\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}=\bar{\rho}
, we can
express
X_{*}-X^{\rm eq}
in terms of system parameters. In this manner, all
thermodynamic quantities are determined by the variational principle.
As one example,
we discuss the pressure in the steady state.
Using (
4.6
), we can express (
4.18
) as

\displaystyle p^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-p^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}=\frac{\phi\bar{\rho}}{2}.

(4.24)

Recalling that the local pressure is given by
p(x)=p(\rho(x))
,
we define the pressures at the left and right sides of the
interface as

\displaystyle p_{-}\equiv\lim_{x\to X_{*}^{-}}p(\rho(x)),\qquad p_{+}\equiv\lim_{x\to X_{*}^{+}}p(\rho(x)).

(4.25)

Then, using

\rho^{\rm L/G}_{X_{*}}(\mu^{\rm L/G}_{X_{*}}-\mu^{\rm I}_{X_{*}})=p^{\rm L/G}_{X_{*}}-p_{-/+},

(4.26)

we can derive

\displaystyle p_{-}=p_{\rm c}+(\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}-\mu_{\rm c})\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c},\qquad p_{+}=p_{\rm c}+(\mu_{X_{*}}^{\rm I}-\mu_{\rm c})\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c},

(4.27)

where
p_{\rm c}
is the equilibrium coexistence pressure.
This result indicates that the pressure is not continuous
at the interface. It should be noted that this discontinuity
occurs only in the singular continuum description for
\kappa_{\Lambda}\ll 1
but
never occur in the continuum description for
\kappa_{\Lambda}\gg 1
as shown
in Appendix
B
.

5
Global thermodynamics

The extension of the variational function
from
{\cal F}_{\rm eq}(X;\bar{\rho})
to
{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)
is closely related to the extension of thermodynamic functions.
Without analyzing specific
stochastic models, one can construct such an extended thermodynamic
framework relying on the consistency, uniqueness, and predictability.
This phenomenological argument, which is called
global thermodynamics
,
was developed for heat conduction systems exhibiting phase coexistence

[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Furthermore, global
thermodynamics was applied to the order-disorder transition in heat
conduction, and the prediction by global thermodynamics
was confirmed by numerical simulations
[
12
]
.
Similarly, in the present setup,
we can determine the variational function (
4.9
)
following the method in Refs.

[
11
,
27
]
.

We set
M\equiv\bar{\rho}L
, where
MA
represents
the total number of particles in the tube.
In equilibrium thermodynamics,
the free energy function
F_{\rm eq}(L,M)
is determined as

F_{\rm eq}(L,M)={\cal F}_{\rm eq}(X_{*};\bar{\rho})

(5.1)

using the variational function
{\cal F}_{\rm eq}(X;\bar{\rho})

given by (
2.21
).
We then have the fundamental relation of thermodynamics

dF_{\rm eq}=-p_{\rm c}dL+\mu_{\rm c}dM,

(5.2)

where
p_{\rm c}
and
\mu_{\rm c}
are the equilibrium values of pressure
and chemical potential in phase coexistence states.
Note that
p_{\rm c}
and
\mu_{\rm c}
are constants in
(L,M)
.
Extending the relations (
5.1
) and (
5.2
)
to non-equilibrium systems, we attempt to determine the thermodynamic
function
F_{\rm ss}(L,M,\phi)
and the variational function

{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(X;\bar{\rho},\phi)
.

5.1
Thermodynamic function

For the system under consideration, we have the equilibrium free
energy
F_{\rm eq}(L,M)
. In the phase coexistence state,
it takes the simple form

F_{\rm eq}(L,M)=-p_{\rm c}L+\mu_{\rm c}M.

(5.3)

To derive
F_{\rm ss}(L,M,\phi)
,
we first assume

\displaystyle dF_{\rm ss}=-\bar{p}dL+\bar{\mu}dM-\Psi d\phi.

(5.4)

without an explicit form of
\Psi
,
where
\bar{p}
and
\bar{\mu}
are the average
pressure and chemical potential defined by

\displaystyle\bar{p}=X_{*}p^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}+(1-X_{*})p^{\rm G}_{X_{*}},\qquad\bar{\mu}=X_{*}{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}+(1-X_{*}){\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}.

(5.5)

Note that
\bar{p}
and
\bar{\mu}
are
functions of
(\bar{\rho},\phi)
and that
the steady state value
X_{*}
of the interface position
is also given as a function of
(\bar{\rho},\phi)
.

\Psi
is a conjugate
variable of the battery voltage
\phi
. From the reflection
symmetry,
\Psi
is an odd function of
\phi
and thus
\Psi=0

for
\phi=0
. On the other hand, from the singular nature of the
phase coexistence, we assume that
\Psi\not=0
in the limit

\phi\to 0+
. For simplicity, we consider only the case
\phi>0

as in the previous sections and we can set
\Psi=\Psi(L,M)
by considering
the limit
\phi\to 0+
to study the linear response regime.
Furthermore, from the extensivity in the equilibrium state,
we have

\displaystyle\Psi(L,M)=M\psi(v)

(5.6)

with
v=L/M=1/\bar{\rho}
.

Next, we set

F_{\rm ss}(L,M,\phi)=F_{\rm eq}(L,M)-M\psi(v)\phi

(5.7)

in the linear response regime. By substituting this
expression into (
5.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{p}=p_{\rm c}+\psi^{\prime}(v)\phi,\qquad\bar{\mu}=\mu_{\rm c}+(v\psi^{\prime}(v)-\psi(v))\phi.

(5.8)

We solve these differential equations of
\psi(v)
.
Eliminating
\psi^{\prime}(v)
from these two
equations, we obtain a necessary condition for
\psi(v)

as

\displaystyle\psi(v)\phi=v(\bar{p}-p_{\rm c})-(\bar{\mu}-\mu_{\rm c}).

(5.9)

Here, we simplify the right-hand side of (
5.9
)
using the expressions given in (
5.5
). We first
note the relations

\displaystyle p^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-p_{\rm c}={\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}({\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-\mu_{\rm c}),\qquad p^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}-p_{\rm c}={\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}({\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}-\mu_{\rm c}).

(5.10)

Using the relations,
we express (
5.9
) as

\displaystyle\psi(v)\phi=(v{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-1)X_{*}{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}+(v{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}-1)(1-X_{*}){\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}.

(5.11)

We next notice that the relation (
3.37
) yields useful expressions

\displaystyle(v{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-1)X_{*}+(v{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}-1)(1-X_{*}){\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}=0,

(5.12)

and

\displaystyle{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-\bar{\rho}=(1-X_{*})({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}).

(5.13)

Using (
4.11
) and (
5.12
), we rewrite (
5.11
) as

\displaystyle\psi(v)\phi=(v{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-1)X_{*}\frac{\phi}{2}.

(5.14)

By substituting (
5.13
) into (
5.14
), we obtain

\displaystyle\phi\Psi(L,M)=\phi M\psi(v)=\frac{\phi}{2}L({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})X_{*}(1-X_{*}).

(5.15)

Thus, if a solution
exists for (
5.8
), this should be
(
5.15
).

Conversely, suppose that (
5.15
) holds.
Noting the relation

\displaystyle F_{\rm eq}(L,M)=LX_{*}f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}})+L(1-X_{*})f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})+O(\phi^{2}),

(5.16)

we find that

F_{\rm ss}
assumed in (
5.7
)
is expressed as

\displaystyle F_{{\rm ss}}(L,M,\phi)

\displaystyle=LX_{*}f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}})+L(1-X_{*})f({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})-\frac{\phi}{2}L({\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})X_{*}(1-X_{*}).

(5.17)

Then, using (
4.6
) and (
4.11
),
we rewrite (
5.17
) as

\displaystyle F_{{\rm ss}}(L,M,\phi)

\displaystyle=-LX_{*}p^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}-L(1-X_{*})p^{\rm G}_{X_{*}}+(X_{*}{\bar{\mu}}^{\rm L}_{X_{*}}+(1-X){\bar{\mu}}^{\rm G}_{X_{*}})M.

(5.18)

Furthermore, using (
5.5
),
we further rewrite
F_{\rm ss}
as a suggestive form

\displaystyle F_{{\rm ss}}(L,M,\phi)

\displaystyle=-\bar{p}L+\bar{\mu}M.

(5.19)

Now, taking the derivative of
F_{\rm ss}
in
L
, we have

\frac{\partial F_{\rm ss}(L,M,\phi)}{\partial L}=-\bar{p}-\frac{\partial\bar{p}(L,M,\phi)}{\partial L}L+\frac{\partial\bar{\mu}(L,M,\phi)}{\partial L}M.

(5.20)

Here, using (
4.5
), (
4.6
), (
4.11
), and
(
4.24
), we can confirm

\frac{\partial\bar{p}(L,M,\phi)}{\partial L}=\bar{\rho}\frac{\partial\bar{\mu}(L,M,\phi)}{\partial L}.

(5.21)

Substituting this result into (
5.20
), we obtain

\frac{\partial F_{\rm ss}(L,M,\phi)}{\partial L}=-\bar{p}.

(5.22)

By repeating the similar calculation, we also have

\frac{\partial F_{\rm ss}(L,M,\phi)}{\partial M}=\bar{\mu}.

(5.23)

Finally, (
5.15
) leads to

\Psi=-\frac{\partial F_{\rm ss}(L,M,\phi)}{\partial\phi}.

(5.24)

These three relations (
5.22
), (
5.23
), and (
5.24
) are
summarized as an extended form of the fundamental relation of
thermodynamics (
5.4
).
This means that
\psi(v)
given by (
5.15
)
satisfies (
5.8
).
We then conclude that (
5.17
)
is the free energy extended to the non-equilibrium steady
state.

5.2
Variational function

We formulate a variational principle for
determining the steady state. In the phase coexistence state,
unconstrained thermodynamic variables are the length of the liquid region

LX
and the particle number of the liquid
M^{\rm L}\equiv L\rho^{L}

per unit area. Let
{\cal F}_{{\rm ss}}(LX,M^{\rm L};L,M,\phi)
be
the variational function for
(LX,M^{\rm L})
with
(L,M,\phi)
fixed.
Following the standard method of thermodynamics, we assume

\displaystyle F_{{\rm ss}}(L,M,\phi)=\min_{X,M^{\rm L}}{\cal F}_{{\rm ss}}(LX,M^{\rm L};L,M,\phi).

(5.25)

With (
5.17
), (
5.25
) naturally leads to

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{{\rm ss}}(LX,M^{\rm L};L,M,\phi)=F_{\rm eq}(LX,M^{\rm L})+F_{\rm eq}(L(1-X),M^{\rm L})-\frac{\phi}{2}(M^{\rm L}-MX),

(5.26)

where we have used (
5.14
).
The variational equations are then expressed as

\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(LX,M^{\rm L};L,M,\phi)}{\partial X}=0,

(5.27)

and

\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(LX,M^{\rm L};L,M,\phi)}{\partial M^{\rm L}}=0.

(5.28)

The solution
(X_{*},M^{\rm L}_{*})
of the equations is the
steady state value.

Now, we construct a variational function for determining
X_{*}

from the variational function (
5.26
) for determining

X_{*}
and
M^{\rm L}_{*}
. For a given
X
, we write the
solution of (
5.28
) as
M^{\rm L}_{X}
. We can then confirm

\left.\frac{d{\cal F}_{\rm ss}(LX,M^{\rm L}_{X};L,M,\phi)}{dX}\right|_{X=X_{*}}=0

(5.29)

using (
5.27
) and (
5.28
). The equation
(
5.29
) means that
{\cal F}_{{\rm ss}}(LX,M^{\rm L}_{X};L,M,\phi)

is the variational function for determining
X_{*}
.
We note that
M^{\rm L}_{X}=L{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm L}_{X}
because (
5.28
)
leads to (
4.11
). Thus, the variational function

{\cal F}_{{\rm ss}}(LX,M^{\rm L}_{X};L,M,\phi)

takes the same form as (
3.39
) expect for the constant term.
Namely, the steady state determined by the phenomenological argument is equivalent to that calculated in the previous section.
Therefore, all the
prediction made by global thermodynamics for the present setup are the
same as the theoretical result for the stochastic
model we study. In our research history, the
variational function (
4.9
) was first derived using
global thermodynamics, and after that it was re-derived by analyzing
the stochastic model.

7
Concluding remarks

We have derived the variational function determining the steady state for a boundary-driven diffusive system with
\kappa_{\Lambda}\ll 1
.
The result is consistent with global thermodynamics, which is an extended framework of thermodynamics. Before ending the paper, we present a few remarks.

The first remark is on the boundary conditions. It is natural
to study a system with different boundary conditions leading to the same most
probable profile. As more familiar boundary conditions,
one considers the case that chemical potentials at boundaries are fixed.
However, as far as we attempted, we could not evaluate the
Zubarev-McLennan representation for this case. To study the boundary condition
dependence of the system is a future problem.

Second, in general, fluctuating hydrodynamics is regarded as a mesoscopic
model obtained by coarse-graining microscopic dynamics. Thus,
it is a significant problem to find relationship between microscopic
dynamics and the discrete fluctuating dynamics. As the first step
of such studies, parameter values of the model should be determined
from the observation of microscopic dynamics. In particular, it
seems highly challenging to identify the value of
\kappa_{\Lambda}
from
microscopic models.

Third, as a generalization of the present model, one may consider
a discrete fluctuating hydrodynamics describing liquid-gas
phase coexistence in heat conduction systems. One can numerically
study the model by changing
\kappa_{\Lambda}
. It is interesting
to observe the deviation of the interface temperature from the equilibrium
coexistence temperature. Furthermore, following the theoretical method
presented in this paper, we may develop a theory for calculating the
deviation. We conjecture that the deviation formula is equivalent to
that predicted by global thermodynamics.

The most important future work is an experimental observation of non-equilibrium phase coexistence in which metastable states are stable as the influence of a non-equilibrium current. As shown in this paper, the phenomenon is expected to occur in systems described by a discrete fluctuating hydrodynamics. However, it is not obvious whether experimental systems are described by a discrete fluctuating hydrodynamics. It would be interesting to clarify an experimental condition for realizing a discrete fluctuating hydrodynamics.

C

Appendix B
Analysis of the Continuum model

In this section, we analyze the continuum model
(
2.2
) and (
2.3
) which corresponds to
the discrete model (
2.8
) and (
2.10
)
in the limit
\kappa_{\Lambda}\to\infty
and
N\to\infty
with
L
and
\kappa

fixed. Explicitly, we derive the phase
coexistence condition (
2.16
) for the equilibrium
system with
\phi=0
and the chemical potential at the
interface for the case
\phi>0
.

B.1
Equilibrium phase coexistence

Stationary solutions of (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) with

\phi=0
and
T=0
satisfy

\partial_{x}\frac{\delta{\cal F}}{\delta\rho(x)}=0.

(B.1)

Using (
2.5
), (
B.1
)
is explicitly written as

f^{\prime}(\rho)-\kappa\partial_{x}^{2}\rho=\mu_{\rm c},

(B.2)

where
\mu_{\rm c}
is a constant in
x
.
Furthermore, multiplying

(\partial_{x}\rho)
with (
B.2
) and integrating
in
x
, we obtain

f(\rho)-\frac{\kappa}{2}(\partial_{x}\rho)^{2}-\mu_{\rm c}\rho=p_{\rm c},

(B.3)

where
p_{\rm c}
is also a constant in
x
.

We first consider necessary conditions
under which there is a stationary and spatially inhomogeneous
solution, which we call the phase coexistence solution, because it
connects two stationary and spatially homogeneous solutions that
represent a liquid phase and a gas phase, respectively.
To make the argument clear, we take the limit
L\to\infty

and we assume
\rho(0)>\rho(\infty)
for the phase coexistence
solution. Because the phase coexistence solution approaches to the stationary
and spatially homogeneous solutions
\rho(0)
and
\rho(\infty)

as
x\to 0
and
x\to\infty
, we have

\partial_{x}\rho(0)=0,\qquad\partial_{x}^{2}\rho(0)=0,\qquad\partial_{x}\rho(\infty)=0,\qquad\partial_{x}^{2}\rho(\infty)=0.

(B.4)

Therefore, (
B.2
) and (
B.3
) lead to
necessary conditions as

\mu(\rho(0))=\mu(\rho(\infty))=\mu_{\rm c},

(B.5)

and

f(\rho(0))-\mu_{\rm c}\rho(0)=f(\rho(\infty))-\mu_{\rm c}\rho(\infty)=p_{\rm c},

(B.6)

which is further rewritten as

\mu_{\rm c}=\frac{f(\rho(0))-f(\rho(\infty))}{\rho(0)-\rho(\infty)}.

(B.7)

For the function
f(\rho)
with two local minima, the
conditions (
B.5
) and (
B.7
) represents
the common tangent line at the special values
\rho=\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
and

\rho=\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
. We set
\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}>\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
without loss of generality.
We thus identify
\rho(0)=\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
and
\rho(\infty)=\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
,
and the values of the constants
\mu_{\rm c}
and
p_{\rm c}
are also determined.
Note that (
B.5
) and (
B.6
) are regarded as the
conditions giving
\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
,
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
,
\mu_{\rm c}
and
p_{\rm c}
by the form

\mu(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c})=\mu(\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c})=\mu_{\rm c},\qquad p(\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c})=p(\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c})=p_{\rm c}.

(B.8)

Now, suppose that
\bar{\rho}
satisfies
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}<\bar{\rho}<\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
,
where
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
and
\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
are determined by
(
B.8
). By setting
\rho(0)=\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
and
\rho(\infty)=\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
,
we solve (
B.2
) with the determined value of
\mu_{\rm c}
.
We here notice that (
B.2
) is interpreted as Newton’s equation
describing the motion of a point particle under a potential field,
where
\rho
and
x
correspond to position and time, respectively.

\kappa
is interpreted as the mass, and the potential function
V(\rho)

is given by
V(\rho)\equiv\mu_{\rm c}\rho-f(\rho)
. (
B.3
) represents
the energy conservation for the equation of motion. From
(
B.8
), we find that
\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
and
\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
are local maximal
points with the same potential value. Therefore, the phase coexistence
solution
\rho(x)
with
\rho(0)=\rho^{\rm L}_{\rm c}
and
\rho(\infty)=\rho^{\rm G}_{\rm c}
is
given by the heteroclinic orbit connecting the two maximal points with
the same potential value. This result corresponds to the statement
involving (
2.16
) in the main text.

B.2
Non-equilibrium phase coexistence

Stationary solutions of (
2.2
) and (
2.3
)
with
\phi>0
and
T=0

\partial_{x}\frac{\delta{\cal F}}{\delta\rho(x)}=-\frac{J}{\sigma(\rho(x))},

(B.9)

which corresponds to (
2.27
) in the continuum limit

\kappa_{\Lambda}\to\infty
and

\kappa_{\Lambda}\to\infty

By analyzing (

First, to uniquely identify the interface position, we introduce a
scaled coordinate
\hat{x}=x/L

\tilde{\mu}(x)
given

\tilde{\mu}(x)=\mu(\rho(x))-\kappa\partial_{x}^{2}\rho,

Finally, we present a remark on the singular continuum description

Appendix C
Zubarev-McLennan representation

In this section, we derive the Zubarev-McLennan representation
(
3.1
) in Sec.
3.1
.

We study stochastic processes of
{\bm{\psi}}=(\psi_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}

defined by (
A.1
).
The time evolution of
{\bm{\psi}}
is
described by (
A.4
).
Let
\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}=({\bm{\psi}}_{t})_{t=0}^{\tau}
be a trajectory
in the time interval
[0,\tau]
.
The path probability
density
{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})
in the system with
\phi>0

starting from a density profile sampled from an equilibrium
distribution
{\cal P}_{\rm eq}({\bm{\psi}}_{0})
is expressed as

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})

\displaystyle={\cal P}_{\rm eq}({\bm{\psi}}_{0})

\displaystyle\times{\rm const}\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4T_{\rm eff}}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Lambda}{\sigma(\rho_{i}^{\rm m})}\left[\frac{d\psi_{i}}{dt}+\frac{\sigma(\rho_{i}^{\rm m})}{\Lambda}\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}}{\partial\psi_{i}}+\phi\delta_{i,N}\right)\right]^{2}\right).

(C.1)

For the time-reversed trajectory
\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger}=({\bm{\psi}}_{\tau-t})_{t=0}^{\tau}
of
\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle={\cal P}_{\rm eq}({\bm{\psi}}_{\tau})

\displaystyle\times{\rm const}\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4T_{\rm eff}}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Lambda}{\sigma(\rho_{i}^{\rm m})}\left[-\frac{d\psi_{i}}{dt}+\frac{\sigma(\rho_{i}^{\rm m})}{\Lambda}\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}}{\partial\psi_{i}}+\phi\delta_{i,N}\right)\right]^{2}\right).

(C.2)

The ratio of the two yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})}{{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger})}=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{T_{\rm eff}}\phi\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\frac{d\psi_{N}(t)}{dt}\right],

(C.3)

where (
A.6
) has been substituted into

{\cal P}_{\rm eq}({\bm{\psi}}_{0})
and
{\cal P}_{\rm eq}({\bm{\psi}}_{\tau})
.

To simplify the notation, we introduce the accumulated current

Q_{\tau}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}dt~j_{N}(t).

(C.4)

Using (
A.1
) and (
C.4
), we rewrite
(
C.3
) as

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})}{{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger})}=\exp\left[\frac{\phi Q_{\tau}}{T_{\rm eff}}\right].

(C.5)

The distribution of
{\bm{\psi}}
at time
t
is expressed as

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\tau}({\bm{\psi}})

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}~{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})\delta({\bm{\psi}}_{\tau}-{\bm{\psi}})

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger}\exp\left[\frac{\phi Q_{\tau}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})}{T_{\rm eff}}\right]{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger})\delta({\bm{\psi}}_{\tau}-{\bm{\psi}}),

(C.6)

where we have used
{\cal D}\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}={\cal D}\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger}

and (
C.5
). When the path integration variable is transformed,
the right side of (
C.6
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}~\exp\left[\frac{\phi Q_{\tau}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger})}{T_{\rm eff}}\right]{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})\delta({\bm{\psi}}_{0}-{\bm{\psi}}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}~\exp\left[-\frac{\phi Q_{\tau}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})}{T_{\rm eff}}\right]{\cal P}_{\rm path}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})\delta({\bm{\psi}}_{0}-{\bm{\psi}}),

(C.7)

where we have used
Q_{\tau}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\dagger})=-Q_{\tau}(\hat{{\bm{\psi}}})
.
We thus have the relation

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\tau}({\bm{\psi}})={\cal P}_{\rm eq}({\bm{\psi}})\left\langle\exp\left[-\frac{\phi Q_{\tau}}{T_{\rm eff}}\right]\right\rangle_{{\bm{\psi}}}.

(C.8)

Taking the limit
\tau\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\rm ss}({\bm{\psi}})={\cal P}_{\rm eq}({\bm{\psi}})\left\langle\exp\left[-\frac{\phi Q}{T_{\rm eff}}\right]\right\rangle_{{\bm{\psi}}}

(C.9)

with

\displaystyle Q=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~j_{N}(t).

(C.10)

In the limit
T_{\rm eff}\to 0
, we estimate

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left[-\frac{\phi Q}{T_{\rm eff}}\right]\right\rangle_{{\bm{\psi}}}\simeq\exp\left[-\frac{\phi\left\langle Q\right\rangle_{{\bm{\psi}}}}{T_{\rm eff}}\right].

(C.11)

We then expand
\left\langle Q_{\infty}\right\rangle_{{\bm{\psi}}}
in
\phi
,
we obtain

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\rm ss}({\bm{\psi}})={\cal P}_{\rm eq}({\bm{\psi}})\exp\left[-\frac{\phi\left\langle Q\right\rangle_{{\bm{\psi}}}^{\rm eq}+O(\phi^{2})}{T_{\rm eff}}\right].

(C.12)

This is the Zubarev-McLennan representation of the steady state
distribution. By using (
A.2
), we obtain the stationary
distribution of
{\bm{\rho}}
as the form (
3.1
).

Appendix D
Derivation of (
3.23
)

In (
3.21
), we consider the decomposition
of
j(0,t)
into
\Phi(t)
and
\Phi_{0}(t)
. In this
section, we calculate
\Phi_{0}(t)
.

D.1
Preliminaries for the calculation

We first note that (
3.14
) and (
3.16
) yield

\displaystyle\frac{d{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}}{dt}=\frac{d\hat{Y}}{dt}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d|D^{\rm L}|}{dt},\qquad\frac{d{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}}{dt}=\frac{d\hat{Y}}{dt}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d|D^{\rm L}|}{dt},\qquad\frac{d|D^{\rm G}|}{dt}=-\frac{d|D^{\rm L}|}{dt}.

(D.1)

From the chain rule of the derivative, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{|D^{\rm L}|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{|D^{\rm L}|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]^{-2}\circ\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\frac{d|D^{\rm L}|}{dt}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\frac{d|D^{\rm G}|}{dt}\right]

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right)\left[\frac{|D^{\rm L}|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]^{-2}\circ\frac{d|D^{\rm L}|}{dt},

(D.2)

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{D^{\rm L}}dx~\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{D^{\rm G}}dx~\psi\right]=\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)}^{{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)}dx~\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t)}^{{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t)+1}dx~\psi\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\left[\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)\circ\frac{d{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}}{dt}-\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)\circ\frac{d{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}}{dt}\right]+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\left[\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)\circ\frac{d{\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}}{dt}-\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)\circ\frac{d{\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}}{dt}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{D^{\rm L}}dx~\partial_{t}\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{D^{\rm G}}dx~\partial_{t}\psi

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right)\left[\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)-\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)\right)\circ\frac{d\hat{Y}}{dt}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)+\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)\right)\circ\frac{d|D^{\rm L}|}{dt}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{D^{\rm L}}dx~\partial_{t}\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{D^{\rm G}}dx~\partial_{t}\psi.

(D.3)

D.2
Relation between
\Phi(t)
and
j(0,t)

Noting the two derivatives (
D.2
) and (
D.3
), we define

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv-\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right)\left[\frac{|D^{\rm L}|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]^{-2}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{D^{\rm L}(t)}dx~\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{D^{\rm G}(t)}dx~\psi\right]\circ\frac{d|D^{\rm L}|}{dt},

(D.4)

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right)\left[\frac{|D^{\rm L}|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\left[\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)-\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)\right)\circ\frac{d\hat{Y}}{dt}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)+\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)\right)\circ\frac{d|D^{\rm L}|}{dt}.\right]

(D.5)

Then, the definition of
\Phi(t)
in (
3.22
) leads to

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\Phi_{1}(t)+\Phi_{2}(t)+j(0,t)

(D.6)

by using (
3.20
). Here, from the piece-wise linear nature of

\psi(x,t)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}\int_{D^{\rm L}(t)}dx~\psi+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\int_{D^{\rm G}(t)}dx~\psi=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{|D^{\rm L}|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)+\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)\right).

(D.7)

See also (
3.31
) for the same equation.
Using this relation, we obtain

\Phi_{1}+\Phi_{2}=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm L}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right)\left[\frac{|D^{\rm L}(t)|}{\sigma^{\rm L}}+\frac{|D^{\rm G}(t)|}{\sigma^{\rm G}}\right]^{-1}\left(\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)-\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)\right)\circ\frac{d\hat{Y}}{dt}.

(D.8)

Finally, we note that

\displaystyle\psi({\hat{X}}_{\downarrow}(t),t)-\psi({\hat{X}}_{\uparrow}(t),t)

\displaystyle=\int_{D^{\rm L}}dx~\partial_{x}\psi,

\displaystyle=|D^{\rm L}(t)|(\rho^{\rm L}(t)-\bar{\rho}).

(D.9)

Substituting this into (
D.8
), we obtain
(
3.23
) where
\Phi_{0}\equiv\Phi_{1}+\Phi_{2}
.
