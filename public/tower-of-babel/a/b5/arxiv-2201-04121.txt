Computing Conjugate Barrier Information for Nonsymmetric Cones
Lea Kapelevich, Erling D. Andersen, Juan Pablo Vielma

Abstract
The recent interior point algorithm by Dahl and Andersen dahl2021primal for nonsymmetric cones as well as earlier works nesterov2012towards ; nesterov1999infeasible require derivative information from the conjugate of the barrier function of the cones in the problem. Besides a few special cases, there is no indication of when this information is efficient to evaluate. We show how to compute the gradient of the conjugate barrier function for seven useful nonsymmetric cones. In some cases this is helpful for deriving closed-form expressions for the inverse Hessian operator for the primal barrier.



∎

Introduction

Many convex optimization problems can be expressed as the minimization of a linear function over an intersection of
symmetric
cones.
These include the nonnegative, (rotated) second order, and positive semidefinite cones.
Many popular solvers for programming with symmetric cones implement primal-dual interior point methods (PDIPMs) specialized for these cones only (e.g.
borchers1999csdp
;
yamashita2003implementation
;
vandenberghe2010cvxopt
).
These specialized PDIPMs enjoy properties such as efficiently computable
symmetric
search directions (that ensure the algorithm is invariant to exchanging of roles of the primal and dual programs), which usually rely on forming so-called
scaling matrices
for symmetric cones, first suggested by
nesterov1997self
.
In addition, the symmetric cones are self-dual and admit barrier functions with highly efficient oracles for PDIPMs.
Unfortunately, many properties of symmetric cones are not straightforward to generalize for other conic sets.
In spite of the fact that many useful sets can only be modeled directly or can be modeled more efficiently with cones that are not symmetric, there are significantly fewer implementations of algorithms for
nonsymmetric
cones.

Early algorithmic frameworks for nonsymmetric cones have been suggested by
nesterov1999infeasible
and
nesterov2012towards
, but have not been implemented by any solver we are aware of.
The high-level algorithms in both papers require information relating to the conjugates of the barrier functions of the cones in the problem, and it is not clear when this can be evaluated efficiently.
Specifically, the only nonsymmetric cones with known, efficient procedures for evaluating the conjugate of the barrier function (or its gradient) are the three-dimensional exponential cone
serrano2015algorithms
, the three-dimensional power cone
nesterov2012towards
, and the cone of sparse positive semidefinite matrices with chordal sparsity
andersen2013logarithmic
.
Additionally,
nesterov1999infeasible
requires solving a linear system in each iteration that is twice the size of the linear systems that arise in symmetric algorithms.

More recent nonsymmetric algorithms bypass some of the aforementioned issues.
These include the algorithm by Skajaa and Ye
skajaa2015homogeneous
, which has been implemented in the solvers Alfonso
papp2021alfonso
and Hypatia
coey2020solving
.
A key advantage of this algorithm is that it requires very few oracles for each primal cone in the problem, and doesn’t require oracles relating to the conjugate of the primal barrier.

In this paper we are primarily motivated by the alternative algorithm of Dahl and Andersen
dahl2021primal
, which is implemented by the MOSEK solver
mosek2020modeling
.
The algorithm is based on a technique by
tunccel2001generalization
;
myklebust2014interior
which generalizes the concept of scaling matrices from symmetric cones.
Like the algorithm of
skajaa2015homogeneous
, the linear systems solved in each iteration are equal in size to those arising in symmetric algorithms.
Unlike the algorithm of
skajaa2015homogeneous
, the gradient of the conjugate barrier is required to compute search directions.
However, the search directions satisfy a number of desirable properties such as ensuring that the violations of residuals and complementarity conditions decrease at the same rate.
The authors also propose a neighborhood that allows stepping further away from the central path compared to
skajaa2015homogeneous
, although there is no proof of polynomial time convergence.
One might expect, owing to the use of conjugate barrier information, that the search directions of
dahl2021primal
would allow convergence in fewer iterations than the search directions of
skajaa2015homogeneous
.

The search directions in the algorithm by
dahl2021primal
relate to those from symmetric algorithms as follows.
Given a primal-dual pair of points
(w,r)
for a symmetric cone, there exists a unique scaling matrix
T
satisfying the secant equations
r=Tw
and
g(w)=Tg^{\ast}(r)
, where
g
and
g^{\ast}
denote the gradients of the primal barrier and its conjugate
nesterov1997self
;
nesterov1998primal
.
A key idea of
myklebust2014interior
;
dahl2021primal
is to construct a general positive definite matrix for any cone, satisfying the two secant equations.
The authors choose a specific formula for this scaling matrix that requires calculating the Hessian of a primal barrier function, and adding BFGS updates to the Hessian.
These low rank updates include adding multiples of the conjugate gradient.

The conjugate gradient can always be evaluated via a numerical procedure (e.g. applying Newton’s method to an optimization problem), but this generic approach is computationally slow and can be numerically challenging.
In particular, it requires applying the inverse Hessian of the primal barrier in each iteration (which can become a new bottleneck in a PDIPM).
A large number of damped Newton iterations may be necessary to get near the region of quadratic convergence towards the end of the PDIPM, when the distance to the cone boundary is small.
Our aim is to show efficient methods of calculating conjugate gradients for seven useful nonsymmetric cones, which an interior point solver could support.
Aside from their use in the algorithm by
dahl2021primal
, procedures for evaluating conjugate gradients are useful due to their applications in the frameworks by
nesterov1999infeasible
;
nesterov2012towards
.
The cones for which we are able to demonstrate efficient procedures for evaluating conjugate gradients are:

Logarithm cone,

the hypograph of the perspective of the sum of logarithms.

Log-determinant cone,

the hypograph of the perspective of the log-deter-minant function.

Hypograph power cone,

the hypograph of the power mean function.

Root-determinant cone,

the hypograph of the
d
th-root-determinant function.

Radial power cone,

the generalized power cone by
(
chares2009cones
, Section 3.1.2)
.

Infinity norm cone,

the epigraph of the
\ell_{\infty}
norm function.

Spectral norm cone,

the epigraph of the spectral norm function.

In all cases, computing conjugate gradients can be reduced to simple numerical procedures (e.g. univariate root-finding).
For some special cases (e.g. where the hypograph or radial power cones are parametrized by equal powers), the conjugate gradients can be written as closed-form expressions.

Our conjugate gradient evaluations lead to a second result.
By differentiating the procedure to calculate the conjugate gradients we are able to derive closed-form expressions for the inverse Hessian of the primal barrier.
This is useful for measuring central path proximity and some of the linear system methods suggested by
coey2021performance
.
The inverse Hessian is already known for five of the cones above
coey2021conic
;
coey2022thesis
, so we only show it for the hypograph and radial power cones.

For a natural number

.

1_{P}
is the indicator function, that is equal to one if statement
P
is true and zero otherwise.
For a set
C
,

and

denote the closure and interior of

We use round parentheses, e.g.

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

\Diag
maps a vector to a matrix with the vector on the diagonal.

2.2
Cones and Barrier Functions

A proper cone
\mathcal{K}
is closed, convex, pointed, and full-dimensional.
The
dual cone
of
\mathcal{K}
is
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}\coloneqq\{r\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle w,r\rangle\geq 0,\forall w\in\mathcal{K}\}
.

\mathcal{K}^{\ast}
is a proper cone if and only if
\mathcal{K}
is a proper cone.

Analysis of conic interior point methods relies on associating with each cone a
logarithmically-homogeneous, self-concordant barrier
(LHSCB).
Following
(
nesterov1994interior
, Sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.3)
,
f:\intr(\mathcal{K})\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\nu
-LHSCB for
\mathcal{K}
, where
\nu\geq 1
is the
LHSCB parameter
, if it satisfies
f(w_{i})\to\infty
along every sequence
w_{i}\in\intr(\mathcal{K})
converging to the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
, and:

\displaystyle\big\lvert\nabla^{3}f(w)[r,r,r]\big\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\bigl(\nabla^{2}f(w)[r,r]\bigr)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\forall w\in\intr(\mathcal{K}),r\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1a)

\displaystyle f(\theta w)

\displaystyle=f(w)-\nu\log(\theta)

\displaystyle\forall w\in\intr(\mathcal{K}),\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

(1b)

As a consequence of (
1b
), the gradient
g
of
f
satisfies
(
nesterov1997self
, Equation (2.5))
:

\langle-g(w),w\rangle=\nu\quad\forall w\in\mathcal{K}.

(2)

For the LHSCB
f
, we define the convex conjugate,
f^{\ast}
, as the function:

f^{\ast}(r)\coloneqq\textstyle\sup_{w\in\intr(\mathcal{K})}\{-\langle r,w\rangle-f(w)\}.

(3)

In fact,
f^{\ast}
is an LHSCB for
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}

(
nesterov1997self
, Equation (2.6))
and we refer to it as the
conjugate barrier
.
The gradient
g^{\ast}
of
f^{\ast}
may be defined from the unique solutions to the optimization problem in
Equation

3
:

g^{\ast}(r)=-\argsup_{w\in\intr(\mathcal{K})}\{-\langle r,w\rangle-f(w)\}.

(4)

From
Equation

3
,
Equation

4
and
Equation

2
:

f^{\ast}(r)=-\langle r,-g^{\ast}(r)\rangle-f(-g^{\ast}(r))=-\nu-f(-g^{\ast}(r)).

(5)

We refer to
g^{\ast}
as the
conjugate gradient
(which has no relation to the method of conjugate gradients).
The negative gradients of LHSCBs
f
and
f^{\ast}
are bijective linear maps between
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}
.
In particular
(
myklebust2014interior
, Theorem 2.5)
:

-g^{\ast}(-g(w))=w\quad\forall w\in\mathcal{K},\quad-g(-g^{\ast}(r))=r\quad\forall r\in\mathcal{K}^{\ast}.

(6)

Note that
Equation

6
characterizes the gradient and conjugate gradient maps as negative inverses of each other.

Some of the LHSCBs we use are related to
unitarily invariant
functions
lewis1995convex
.
Let
W=U_{W}\Diag(\sigma_{W})V_{W}^{\top}
be the singular value decomposition of
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
, such that
\sigma_{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
.
If
W
is symmetric, then
\sigma_{W}
are the eigenvalues of
W
and
U_{W}=V_{W}
.
Suppose
F:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function given by
F(W)=f(\sigma_{W})
, where
f\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}
is some symmetric function (invariant to the order of its inputs).
Then
F
is unitarily invariant.
Let
G
and
g
denote the gradients of
F
and
f
.
In
Sections

3.1
,
3.2
and
3.4
, we use
(
lewis1995convex
, Theorem 3.1)
:

G(W)=U_{W}\Diag(g(\sigma_{W}))V_{W}^{\top}.

(7)

3.1
Logarithm Cone and Log-determinant Cone

Let

\mathcal{K}_{\log}^{\ast}\coloneqq\cl\bigl\{(p,q,r)\in\mathbb{R}_{--}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{++}:q\geq p\textstyle\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\log\bigl(-\tfrac{r_{i}}{p}\bigr)+pd\bigr\}.

\omega(\beta)+\log(\omega(\beta))=\beta.

(8)

The Wright omega function is used in

Proposition 1

The conjugate gradient at

has components:

\displaystyle g^{\ast}_{p}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{-d-2+\nicefrac{{q}}{{p}}+2\bar{\omega}}{p(1-\bar{\omega})},

(9a)

\displaystyle g^{\ast}_{q}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{p(1-\bar{\omega})},

(9b)

\displaystyle g^{\ast}_{r_{i}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\bar{\omega}}{r_{i}(1-\bar{\omega})}

\displaystyle\forall i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,

(9c)

\bar{\omega}\coloneqq d\cdot\omega\bigl(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}\bigl(1+d-\nicefrac{{q}}{{p}}+\textstyle\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\log(-\nicefrac{{r_{i}}}{{p}})\bigr)-\log(d)\bigr)

The proof is given in
Section

5.1
.
By substituting

9

5
, we obtain the conjugate barrier:

f^{\ast}(p,q,r)=-2-d-2\log(-p)-\log\Bigl(\tfrac{(\bar{\omega}-1)^{d+1}}{\bar{\omega}^{d}}\Bigr)-\textstyle\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\log(r_{i}).

The conjugate gradient for

can be easily modified to obtain a conjugate gradient for the

coey2020solving
, Section 5.6)

\mathcal{K}_{\logdet}\coloneqq\cl\bigl\{(u,v,W)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}\times\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++}:u\leq v\logdet(\nicefrac{{W}}{{v}})\bigr\},

which has the dual:

\mathcal{K}_{\logdet}^{\ast}\coloneqq\cl\bigl\{(p,q,R)\in\mathbb{R}_{--}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++}:q\geq p\logdet(-\nicefrac{{R}}{{p}})+pd\bigr\}.

-LHSCB

Proposition 2

Let
(p,q,R)\in\intr(\mathcal{K}_{\logdet}^{\ast})
, let
R=U_{R}\Diag(\lambda_{R})U_{R}^{\top}
be the eigendecomposition of

, and let

has components:

\displaystyle G^{\ast}_{p}

\displaystyle=g^{\ast}_{p}(p,q,\lambda_{R})=\tfrac{-d-2+\nicefrac{{q}}{{p}}+2\bar{\Omega}}{p(1-\bar{\Omega})},

\displaystyle G^{\ast}_{q}

\displaystyle=g^{\ast}_{q}(p,q,\lambda_{R})=-p^{-1}(1-\bar{\Omega})^{-1},

\displaystyle G^{\ast}_{R}

\displaystyle=U_{R}\Diag(g^{\ast}_{r}(p,q,\lambda_{R}))U_{R}^{\top}=\tfrac{\bar{\Omega}}{(1-\bar{\Omega})}R^{-1}.

Proof

For fixed
u
and
v
,

is a unitarily invariant function of
W
.
Due to
Equation

7

is

, and

The result can be verified from this and

3.3
Radial Power Cone

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

\varphi(w)\coloneqq\textstyle\prod_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}w_{i}^{2\alpha_{i}}
, parametrized by

;

4
Inverse Hessians

In

the authors derive efficient inverse Hessian operators for a number of cones, including
\mathcal{K}_{\log}
,
\mathcal{K}_{\logdet}
,
\mathcal{K}_{\hgeom}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{\rtdet}
.
Inverse Hessians for
\mathcal{K}_{\ell_{\infty}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\ell_{\spec}}
are described in
coey2022thesis
.
The authors are motivated by the need to apply inverse Hessians in several parts of the algorithm implemented by the Hypatia solver
coey2021performance
.
The inverse Hessian is used to measure proximity to the central path as well as some of the linear system solving methods described in
(
coey2021performance
, Appendix A)
.
Our derivations of the conjugate gradients offer an alternative method for deriving inverse Hessian operators.
Since these have not been previously written for
\mathcal{K}_{\hpower}
or
\mathcal{K}_{\rpower}
, we derive those inverse Hessian operators here.

A general technique for obtaining an inverse Hessian operator using an oracle for the conjugate gradient may be described as follows.
For
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
, where
\mathcal{K}
is either
\mathcal{K}_{\hpower}
or
\mathcal{K}_{\rpower}
parametrized by
\alpha
, let
\tilde{u}=(u,w)\in\intr(\mathcal{K})
, and
\tilde{x}=(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
be an arbitrary direction.
Due to
(
nesterov1997self
, Equation (2.11))
:

H(\tilde{u})^{-1}\cdot\tilde{x}=H^{\ast}(-g(\tilde{u}))\cdot\tilde{x}.

(16)

Therefore to derive the inverse Hessian operator
H(\tilde{u})^{-1}\cdot\tilde{x}
, we may derive an expression for
H^{\ast}(\tilde{p})\cdot\tilde{x}
, for arbitrary
\tilde{p}=(p,r)\in\intr(\mathcal{K}^{\ast})
, and substitute
-g(\tilde{u})
for
\tilde{p}
.
In a practical implementation of a PDIPM,
g(\tilde{u})
is usually already available at the time when
H(\tilde{u})^{-1}
is needed.
Note that:

H^{\ast}(\tilde{p})\cdot\tilde{x}=\tfrac{\diff}{\diff t}g^{\ast}(\tilde{p}+t\tilde{x})\big\rvert_{t=0}.

The right hand side can be deduced from our calculations for
g^{\ast}
.
