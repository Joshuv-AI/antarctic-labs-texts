General position problems in strong and lexicographic products of graphs
Pakanun Dokyeesun, Sandi Klavžar, Dorota Kuziak, Jing Tian

Abstract
Outer, dual, and total general position sets are studied on strong and lexicographic products of graphs. Sharp lower and upper bounds are proved for the outer and the dual general position number of strong products and several exact values are obtained. For the lexicographic product, the outer general position number is determined in all the cases, and the dual general position number in many cases. The total general position number is determined for both products. Along the way some results on outer general position sets are also derived.

Introduction

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a graph and
X\subseteq V(G)
. Vertices
u,v\in V(G)
are
X
-positionable
if for any shortest
u,v
-path
P
we have
V(P)\cap X=\{u,v\}
. Clearly, adjacent vertices are
X
-positionable no matter whether they belong to
X
. Setting
\overline{X}=V(G)\setminus X
, the set
X
is

•

a
general position set
, if every
u,v\in X
are
X
-positionable;

•

a
total general position set
, if every
u,v\in V(G)
are
X
-positionable;

•

an
outer general position set
, if every
u,v\in X
are
X
-positionable, and every
u\in X
,
v\in\overline{X}
are
X
-positionable; and

•

a
dual general position set
, if every
u,v\in X
are
X
-positionable, and every
u,v\in\overline{X}
are
X
-positionable.

The cardinality of a largest general position set, a largest total general position set, a largest outer general position set, and a largest dual general position set of a graph
G
will be respectively
denoted by
{\rm gp}(G)
,
{\rm gp}_{\rm t}(G)
,
{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(G)
, and
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G)
. These graph invariants will be respectively called the
general position number
, the
total general position number
, the
outer general position number
, and the
dual general position number
of
G
. Moreover, for any
\tau(G)\in\{{\rm gp}(G),{\rm gp}_{\rm t}(G),{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(G),{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G)\}
, by a
\tau
-set
we mean a set of cardinality
\tau(G)
having the respective property.

The above described variety of general position sets was introduced in
[
21
]
, while (standard) general position sets have already been investigated a lot after their independent introduction in
[
6
,
16
]
. In particular, a lot of attention has been given to general position sets of graph operations
[
8
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
22
,
23
]
. Among other contributions on the topic we highlight the papers
[
18
,
20
,
24
]
. With respect to graph operations, general position sets were also investigated in
[
12
]
on strong products. Among other matters covered in
[
12
]
we point to the problem whether the general position number of the strong product of two graphs equals the product of the general position numbers of the factors, which is one of the main outstanding problems in the area.

In this paper we focus on the recently introduced three general position numbers of strong and lexicographic products. In the next section we give necessary definitions and call up the known results that we need later. In Section
3
we prove some results on outer general position sets that are on one hand interesting in their own right and are on the other hand applied later on. The strong product is the topic of Section
4
. We prove general lower and upper bounds on the outer and the dual general position number, demonstrate their sharpness, and give exact values in some specific cases. Then, in Section
5
, we turn our attention to lexicographic products. We first determine the outer general position number in all the cases, and complete the paper by establishing the dual general position number in many cases. Along the way we also determine the total general position number of both products.

Preliminaries

Unless stated otherwise, graphs
G=(V(G),E(G))

The

On outer general position sets

In this section we prove some results on outer general position sets that are interesting in their own right and that we will need later in the paper. The first of them holds for all types of general position sets.

Lemma 3.1

Let
H
be an isometric subgraph of a graph
G
. If
X
is (dual, outer, total) general position set of
G
, then
X\cap V(H)
is a (dual, outer, total) general position set of
H
.

Proof.
Set
X_{H}=X\cap V(H)
.

Assume that
X
is a general position set of
G
and suppose on the contrary that
X_{H}
is not a general position set of
H
. Then there exist vertices
h,h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}
of
X_{H}
such that
h^{\prime\prime}
lies on a shortest
h,h^{\prime}
-path
P
in
H
. Since
H
is isometric in
G
, the path
P
is also a shortest path in
G
, but then
X
is not a general position set of
G
. This contradiction shows that
X_{H}
is a general position set of
H
.

Assume now that
X
is a dual general position set of
G
. We already know that
X_{H}
is a general position set of
H
, hence in view of Theorem
2.1
(iii) we claim that
V(H)\setminus X_{H}
is convex in
H
. Assume on the contrary that this is not the case. Then there exist two vertices
u,v\in V(H)\setminus X_{H}
and a shortest
u,v
-path
Q
in
H
which contains a vertex of
X_{H}
. As
H
is isometric,
Q
is also a shortest path in
G
. But since
u,v\in V(G)\setminus X
, this implies that
V(G)\setminus X
is not convex in
G
, a contradiction with Theorem
2.1
(iii). It follows that
X_{H}
is a dual general position set of
H
.

Assume next that
X
is an outer general position set of
G
. Then
X_{H}
is a general position set of
H
and similarly as above we infer that also no shortest path between a vertex from
X_{H}
and a vertex from
V(H)\setminus X_{H}
contains a vertex of
X_{H}
. It follows that
X_{H}
is an outer general position set of
H
.

Finally, if
X
is a total general position set of
G
, then the above arguments imply that
X_{H}
is a total general position set of
H
.

\square

The case of Lemma
3.1
for general position sets was earlier implicitly proved within the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
.

By
G{}_{\rm tt}^{-}
we represent the graph obtained from
G
by removing the edge between each pair of true twins. In particular, if
G
has no true twins, then
G{}_{\rm tt}^{-}\cong G
, and
(K_{n}){}_{\rm tt}^{-}
is the edgeless graph of order

Proposition 3.2

If
G
is a graph with
{\rm diam}(G)=2
, then
{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(G)=\omega(G{}_{\rm tt}^{-})
. In particular, if
G
has no true twins, then
{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(G)=\alpha(G)
.

Proof.
We have already observed that every pair of true twins are MMD in a graph.
Since
{\rm diam}(G)=2
, vertices
u
and
v
of
G
are MMD if and only if either
d_{G}(u,v)=2
, or
u
and
v
are true twins. Then for every
u,v\in V(G)
, vertices
u,v
are MMD in
G
if and only if they are MMD in
G{}_{\rm tt}^{-}
which means that
G{}_{\rm SR}\cong(G{}_{\rm tt}^{-}){}_{\rm SR}
. By Theorem

(ii) and
{\rm diam}(G)=2
, we have
{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(G)=\omega(G{}_{\rm SR})=\omega((G{}_{\rm tt}^{-}){}_{\rm SR})=\omega(\overline{G{}_{\rm tt}^{-}})=\alpha(G{}_{\rm tt}^{-})
.

Note that any two non-adjacent vertices are MMD as
{\rm diam}(G)=2
. If
G
has no true twins, we have
\omega(G{}_{\rm SR})=\omega(\overline{G})=\alpha(G)
and hence
{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(G)=\alpha(G)
.

\square

A parallel result to Proposition
3.2
for the case of general position sets was proved in

, where it is established that if

{\rm diam}(G)=2
, then
{\rm gp}(G)=\omega(G{}_{\rm SR})
if and only if
{\rm gp}(G)=\alpha(G)
.

If
k\geq 1
, then a set

is

-independent
if

for each pair of vertices

. The
k

of

is the cardinality of a largest

. Note that

.

Proposition 3.3

If
G
is a graph with
{\rm diam}(G)=k\geq 2
, then
{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(G)\geq\alpha_{k-1}(G)
.

Proof.
Let
X
be a
(k-1)
-independent set of
G

|X|=\alpha_{k-1}(G)
. Let
u,v\in X
, where
u\neq v
. As
{\rm diam}(G)=k
and
d_{G}(u,v)>k-1
, we necessarily have

. Using again the fact that

we see that

are MMD. It follows that

, so that
\omega(G{}_{\rm SR})\geq|X|=\alpha_{k-1}(G)

\square

The bound of Proposition
3.3
is sharp for all
k\geq 2
. To see it, we give two constructions depending on the parity of

.

First, let
k\geq 2
be even and let
r=\frac{k-2}{2}
. Then the graph
K_{1,s}^{r}
,

, is obtained from

by subdividing all edges

{\rm diam}(K_{1,s}^{r})=k=2r+2
and all leaves of this graph form the largest

-independence set. Hence
\alpha_{k-1}(K_{1,s}^{r})=n_{1}(K_{1,s}^{r})
. Moreover, by Theorem

(ii) we have that
{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(K_{1,s}^{r})=\omega((K_{1,s}^{r}){}_{\rm SR})=n_{1}(K_{1,s}^{r})
.

For
k\geq 3
odd, let

. Then the graph
K_{n}^{t}
,

, is obtained by attaching a path

to every vertex of

. By construction,
{\rm diam}(K_{n}^{t})=k=2t+1
and
\alpha_{k-1}(K_{n}^{t})=n_{1}(K_{n}^{t})
. Using Theorem

(ii) again we can conclude that
{\rm gp}_{\rm o}(K_{n}^{t})=\omega((K_{n}^{t}){}_{\rm SR})=n_{1}(K_{n}^{t})=\alpha_{k-1}(K_{n}^{t})
.

Strong products

The
strong product
of two graphs
G
and
H
is the graph
G\boxtimes H
such that
V(G\boxtimes H)=V(G)\times V(H)
, and two vertices
(g,h),(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in V(G\boxtimes H)
are adjacent if either
g=g^{\prime}
and
hh^{\prime}\in E(H)
, or
gg^{\prime}\in E(G)
and
h=h^{\prime}
, or
gg^{\prime}\in E(G)
and
hh^{\prime}\in E(H)
.
For a vertex
h\in V(H)
, set
G^{h}=\{(g,h)\in V(G\boxtimes H):\ g\in V(G)\}
. The set
G^{h}
is called a
G
-layer
of
G\boxtimes H
and induces a subgraph of
G\boxtimes H
isomorphic to
G
. For
g\in V(G)
,
H
-layer

{}^{g}H
is defined as
{}^{g}H=\{(g,h)\in V(G\boxtimes H):\ h\in V(H)\}
.
If
X\subseteq V(G\boxtimes H)
, the
projection

p_{G}(X)
of
X
to
G
is the set
\{g\in V(G):\ (g,h)\in X~{\rm for~some}~h\in V(H)\}
. The projection
p_{H}(X)
of
X
to
H
is defined analogously.

The following formulas are well-known, cf.
[
9
]
:

\displaystyle d_{G\boxtimes H}((g,h),(g^{\prime},h^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\max\{d_{G}(g,g^{\prime}),d_{H}(h,h^{\prime})\}\quad{\rm and}

(2)

\displaystyle N_{G\boxtimes H}[(g,h)]

\displaystyle=N_{G}[g]\times N_{H}[h]\,.

(3)

[

]
, several exact results and bounds were proved on the general position number of strong products. In addition, the problem was posed whether
{\rm gp}(G\boxtimes H)={\rm gp}(G){\rm gp}(H)
holds for any graphs

and

[

contains a vertex
(g,h)
such that

By the commutativity of the strong product we can use parallel arguments as in Case 2 to derive the conclusion

.

Case 4
:

contains a vertex

such that

Lemma

implies that one of

and

must actually respectively lie in
\partial(G)
and

, hence this case is not possible.

We have exhausted all the possibilities for

, hence the claimed upper bound is proved.

Assume now that

and

are block graphs. Then we know from

]
that

and

and

. Hence the bounds of the theorem coincide if

\square

The bounds of Theorem

are thus sharp on block graphs. On the other hand, recall from

that

Proposition 4.5

If

, then

.

Proof.
If

. Since

\square

4.2
Dual general position sets

For the dual general position number of strong products we have the following general bounds.

Theorem 4.6

If
G
and
H
are connected graphs, then

\displaystyle s(G)s(H)\leq{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G\boxtimes H)\leq\min\{

\displaystyle s(G)n(H)+s(H)n(G)-s(G)s(H),

\displaystyle n(G){\rm gp}_{\rm d}(H),n(H){\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G)\}\,.

Proof.
In
[
21
, Corollary 3.2]
it was proved that if
G
is a graph and
X\subseteq S(G)
, then
X
is a dual general position set of
G
. Hence
S(G\boxtimes H)
is a dual general position set of
G\boxtimes H
. Lemma
4.1
then yields the lower bound.

Lemma
3.1
applied to dual general position sets implies that
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G\boxtimes H)\leq n(G){\rm gp}_{\rm d}(H)
and
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G\boxtimes H)\leq n(H){\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G)
.

To prove that
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G\boxtimes H)\leq s(G)n(H)+s(H)n(G)-s(G)s(H)
, suppose on the contrary that there exists a dual general position set
R
of
G\boxtimes H
such that
|R|>s(G)n(H)+s(H)n(G)-s(G)s(H).
Then there exists a vertex
(x,y)\in R
such that
x\notin S(G)
and
y\notin S(H)
, that is,
x
and
y
are not simplicial vertices of
G
and
H
, respectively. Let
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in N_{G}(x)
, and let
y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\in N_{H}(y)
, where
x^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime}\not\in E(G)
and
y^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}\not\in E(H)
. Then the subgraph induced by
\{x^{\prime},x,x^{\prime\prime}\}\times\{y^{\prime},y,y^{\prime\prime}\}
is isomorphic to
P_{3}\boxtimes P_{3}
with the universal vertex
(x,y)
.

Since
R
is a dual general position set of
G\boxtimes H
and because
(x,y)\in R
, Theorem
2.1
(iii) implies that one of the vertices
(x^{\prime},y)
and
(x^{\prime\prime},y)
must be in
R
, we may without loss of generality assume that
(x^{\prime},y)\in R
. Using again that
R
is a general position set of
G\boxtimes H
, we get
(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime})\notin R
and
(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\notin R
. Since the complement of
R
is convex, we then infer that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})
must be in
R
. But then the vertices
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
,
(x^{\prime},y)
, and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})
induce a shortest
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})
-path, a contradiction with the assumption that
R
is a general position set.

\square

To demonstrate that the three upper bounds of Theorem
4.6
are pairwise incomparable, consider the following example. Let
C_{n}^{+}
be the graph obtained from
C_{n}
by attaching a new vertex to an arbitrary vertex of
C_{n}
. Now we consider
C_{2k+1}^{+}\boxtimes C_{2\ell+1}^{+}
,
k,\ell\geq 2
, and compute the values of the upper bounds of Theorem
4.6
. Since
s(C_{2k+1}^{+})=s(C_{2\ell+1}^{+})=1
, the first bound is

\displaystyle\quad\ s(C_{2k+1}^{+})n(C_{2\ell+1}^{+})+s(C_{2\ell+1}^{+})n(C_{2k+1}^{+})-s(C_{2k+1}^{+})s(C_{2\ell+1}^{+})

\displaystyle=(2\ell+2)+(2k+2)-1

\displaystyle=2\ell+2k+3\,.

For the other two bounds, we first infer that
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(C_{2k+1}^{+})=3
and
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(C_{2\ell+1}^{+})=3
. Assume without loss of generality that
k\leq\ell
. Then we have

\min\{{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(C_{2k+1}^{+})n(C_{2\ell+1}^{+}),{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(C_{2\ell+1}^{+})n(C_{2k+1}^{+})\}=\min\{6\ell+6,6k+6\}=6k+6\,.

Theorem
4.6
thus gives

{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(C_{2k+1}^{+}\boxtimes C_{2\ell+1}^{+})\leq\begin{cases}2\ell+2k+3;&k\leq\ell\leq 2k+\frac{3}{2},\\
6k+6;&\ell>2k+\frac{3}{2}\,.\end{cases}

We conclude this section with the following exact result on the dual general position number of strong products.

Theorem 4.7

If
H
is a connected graph, then
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(K_{n}\boxtimes H)=n\cdot{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(H)\,.

Proof.
By Theorem
4.6
,
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(K_{n}\boxtimes H)\leq n\cdot{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(H)
.

To prove the lower bound, let
V(K_{n})=[n]
and let
X
be a
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}
-set of
H
. We claim that
X^{\prime}=[n]\times X
is a dual general position set of
K_{n}\boxtimes H
. We first need to show that
X^{\prime}
is a general position set of
K_{n}\boxtimes H
. For the sake of contradiction suppose this is not the case and let
(i,h)
,
(i^{\prime},h^{\prime})
,
(i^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})
be three vertices of
X^{\prime}
such that
(i^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})
lies on a shortest
(i,h),(i^{\prime},h^{\prime})
-path
P
in
K_{n}\boxtimes H
. Then
p_{H}(P)
induces a shortest
h,h^{\prime}
-path in
H
which contains
h^{\prime\prime}
, a contradiction with the assumption that
X
is a general position set of
H
.

We second need to verify that
V(K_{n}\boxtimes H)\setminus X^{\prime}
is convex in
K_{n}\boxtimes H
. For this sake consider arbitrary two vertices
(i,h)
and
(i^{\prime},h^{\prime})
from
V(K_{n}\boxtimes H)\setminus X^{\prime}
. Suppose that there exists a shortest
(i,h),(i^{\prime},h^{\prime})
-path
P
containing a vertex from
X^{\prime}
, say
(j,k)
, such that
k\in X
. Then it follows that there exists a shortest
h,h^{\prime}
-path containing the vertex
k
in
H
. Since
V(H)\setminus X
is convex,
d_{H}(h,h^{\prime})<d_{H}(h,k)+d_{H}(k,h^{\prime})
. By (
2
), we have

\displaystyle d_{K_{n}\boxtimes H}((i,h),(i^{\prime},h^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=d_{H}(h,h^{\prime}),

\displaystyle d_{K_{n}\boxtimes H}((i,h),(j,k))

\displaystyle=d_{H}(h,k),

\displaystyle d_{K_{n}\boxtimes H}((j,k),(i^{\prime},h^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=d_{H}(k,h^{\prime}).

Then

d_{K_{n}\boxtimes H}((i,h),(i^{\prime},h^{\prime}))<d_{K_{n}\boxtimes H}((i,h),(j,k))+d_{K_{n}\boxtimes H}((j,k),(i^{\prime},h^{\prime}))\,,

which implies that
P
is not a shortest
(i,h),(i^{\prime},h^{\prime})
-path in
K_{n}\boxtimes H
. This contradiction yields that
V(K_{n}\boxtimes H)\setminus X^{\prime}
is convex.
By Theorem
2.1
(iii) we can conclude that
X^{\prime}
is a dual general position set of
K_{n}\boxtimes H
and hence
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(K_{n}\boxtimes H)\geq|X^{\prime}|=n\cdot{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(H)
, and we are done.

\square

Let
H
be an arbitrary graph with
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(H)=s(H)
, for instance, an arbitrary block graph. Then Theorem
4.7
yields

{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(K_{n}\boxtimes H)=n\cdot{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(H)=s(K_{n})s(H)\,,

which demonstrates sharpness of the lower bound of Theorem
4.6
.

5
Lexicographic products

The
lexicographic product

G\circ H
of two graphs
G
and
H
has
V(G\circ H)=V(G)\times V(H)
. Two vertices
(g,h),(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in V(G\circ H)
are adjacent if either
g=g^{\prime}
and
hh^{\prime}\in E(H)
, or
gg^{\prime}\in E(G)
. The projections
p_{G}(X)
and
p_{H}(X)
of
X\subseteq V(G\circ H)
on
G
and
H
are defined just as for the strong product, and
G
-layers and
H
-layers are also defined analogously.

The following result is parallel to Lemma
4.1
. Since we did not find it in the literature, its proof is provided.

Lemma 5.1

If
G
and
H
are connected graphs of order at least
2
, then

S(G\circ H)=\begin{cases}S(G)\times V(H);&H\text{ is complete},\\
\emptyset;&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Proof.
We first assume that
H
is a non-complete graph and suppose on the contrary that
S(G\circ H)\neq\emptyset
. Let
(x,y)\in S(G\circ H)
. As
G
is a connected graph with at least two vertices, there exists
x^{\prime}\in V(G)
such that
xx^{\prime}\in E(G)
. Since
H
is non-complete, there exist two non-adjacent vertices
y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\in V(H)
. Then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\in N_{G\circ H}((x,y))
but they are not adjacent, a contradiction.

Assume second that
H
is complete. Consider a vertex
(x,y)\in S(G)\times V(H)
and let
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\in N_{G\circ H}((x,y))
. If
x^{\prime}=x^{\prime\prime}
, then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and
(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})
are adjacent since
H
is complete. And if
x^{\prime}\neq x^{\prime\prime}
, then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and
(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})
are adjacent because
x\in S(G)
and hence
y^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}\in E(H)
. This proves that
S(G)\times V(H)\subseteq S(G\circ H)
. To see that also
S(G\circ H)\subseteq S(G)\times V(H)
holds, we can use the argument from the first paragraph of the proof.

\square

Lemma
5.1
together with Theorem
2.1
(i) yield:

Corollary 5.2

If
G
and
H
are graphs of order at least
2
, then

{\rm gp}_{\rm t}(G\circ H)=\begin{cases}s(G)n(H);&H\text{ is complete},\\
0;&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

5.2
Dual general position sets

We finally investigate the dual general position number. For this sake we recall that in
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
, non-complete convex sets of lexicographic products

are characterized with three conditions. One of them asserts that
G\circ H
can contain a non-complete convex subgraph only if

is a complete graph.

Theorem 5.9

If
G
is a connected graph, then the following hold.

(i)

If
G
has no simplicial vertex and
H
is a graph with no simplicial vertex, then
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G\circ H)=0
.

(ii)

If
n\geq 1
, then
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G\circ K_{n})={\rm gp}_{\rm d}(G)\cdot n
.

Proof.
(i) Let
X
be a dual general position set of
G\circ H
and let
(g,h)\in X
. Since
g\notin S(G)
, it has neighbors
g^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime\prime}
such that
g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}\notin E(G)
. Consider now the vertices
(g^{\prime},h)
and
(g^{\prime\prime},h)
and note that
d_{G\circ H}((g^{\prime},h),(g^{\prime\prime},h))=2
. Thus we cannot have
(g^{\prime},h)\in X
and
(g^{\prime\prime},h)\in X
. Moreover, we also cannot have
(g^{\prime},h)\notin X
and
(g^{\prime\prime},h)\notin X
because
(G\circ H)\setminus X
is convex. Hence, assume without loss of generality that
(g^{\prime},h)\in X
and
(g^{\prime\prime},h)\notin X
. Let now
(g^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime})
be a vertex with
d_{H}(h,h^{\prime})=2
. Such a vertex exists since
h
is not simplicial. Then
(g^{\prime},h)\in X
and
(g,h)\in X
imply that
(g^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime})\notin X
. But then
(g^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime})-(g,h)-(g^{\prime\prime},h)
is a shortest path with
(g,h)\in X
, a contradiction with the convexity of
(G\circ H)\setminus X
.

(ii) Let
X
be a
{\rm gp}_{\rm d}
-set of
G
and let
V(K_{n})=[n]
.
Set
Y=X\times[n]
and
\overline{Y}=(V(G)-X)\times[n]
.
We first claim that
Y
is a general position set of
G\circ H
.
Suppose this is not the case and let
(g,h)
,
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
,
(g^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})
be three vertices of
Y
such that
(g^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})
lies on a shortest
(g,h),(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
-path
P
in
G\circ K_{n}
. Then
p_{G}(P)
induces a shortest
g,g^{\prime}
-path in
G
which contains
g^{\prime\prime}
, a contradiction with the assumption that
X
is a general position set of
G
.

Next, we prove that
\overline{Y}
is convex. Suppose on the contrary that there exist two vertices
(g,h)
and
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
from
\overline{Y}
such that some shortest
(g,h),(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
-path in
G\circ K_{n}
contains a vertex
(g^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})\in Y
, where
g^{\prime\prime}\in X
. By the definition of
G\circ K_{n}
, since
\{h,h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\}
