Computing the L 1 L^{1} optimal transport density: a FEM approach
Federico Piazzon, Enrico Facca, Mario Putti

Abstract
. The L 1 L^{1} optimal transport density μ ∗ \mu^{*} is the unique L ∞ L^{\infty} solution of the Monge-Kantorovich equations. It has been recently characterized also as the unique minimizer of the L 1 L^{1} -transport energy functional ℰ \mathcal{E} . In the present work we develop and we prove convergence of a numerical approximation scheme for μ ∗ . \mu^{*}. Our approach relies upon the combination of a FEM-inspired variational approximation of ℰ \mathcal{E} with a minimization algorithm based on a gradient flow method.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
L^{1}
optimal transport

Optimal transport is the mathematical problem modeling the quest for an optimal strategy for moving a mass displacement of a material to a target distribution. Apart from the original motivation of soil dragging
[
27
]
, optimal transport finds a large variety of applications, e.g, metrics in probability theory, smoothing in image processing, baricentric interpolation and many others (see for instance
[
30
,
31
]
and references therein). The modern formulation of optimal transport is due to Kantorovich, see
[
23
]
, and can be stated as follows. Let
\nu^{+},\nu^{-}
be two Borel measures defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite equal masses. Let
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a Borel function.
Find a non-negative Borel Measure
\gamma
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
(termed
optimal transport plan
) realizing the following infimum

(1.1)

\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}c(x,y)d\gamma(x,y)\right\},

under the constraints

\displaystyle\gamma(A,\mathbb{R}^{d})=\nu^{+}(A)\ \forall A\mbox{ Borel set in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathbb{R}^{d},B)=\nu^{-}(B)\ \forall B\mbox{ Borel set in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Existence of such an optimal transport plan is guaranteed by mild assumptions on
c
, e.g., lower semicontinuity and boundedness from below.

In this paper we focus on the
L^{1}
case, i.e., we assume
c(x,y):=\|x-y\|
. Also, we will work under the (classical) further assumption of
\nu^{+}=f^{+}dx
,
\nu^{-}=f^{-}dx
for a given zero-mean function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
In this setting not only the problem (
1.1
) admits a unique optimal transport plan, also such a plan is defined by an
optimal transport map
, i.e., a Borel function
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
realizing the minimum in the Monge formulation of optimal transport, i.e.

\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}c(x,T(x))d\nu^{+},T_{\#}\nu^{+}=\nu^{-}\right\},

where
T_{\#}\nu^{+}
is the push-forward measure.

The aim of the present work is to study an approximation algorithm for the solution of the
L^{1}
-optimal transport.
While the numerical solution of
L^{2}
optimal transport has been tackled in several previous works (see e.g.
[
3
,
5
,
11
]
for recent contributions), schemes for the
L^{1}
case are sparser.
Typical methods for the numerical solution of this latter problem are based on the entropic regularization of the linear programming problem associated to the Kantorovich formulation and augmented Lagrangian methods, or on the reformulation of the problem in Beckmann form
[
13
,
4
,
24
,
28
]
.

In our work, we take inspiration from the work of
[
16
,
17
]
, where a numerical solver for the PDE based formulation of this problem was proposed. The PDE formulation of the
L^{1}
-optimal transport originated in
[
15
]
who showed that the
L^{1}
-optimal transport map
T
can be constructed starting from the
optimal transport density
, i.e., the unique
L^{\infty}
solution
\mu^{*}
of the so-called
Monge-Kantorovich equations

(1.2)

\begin{cases}-\divergence{(\mu^{*}\nabla u^{*})}=f^{+}-f^{-},&\text{ in
}\Omega\\
|\nabla u^{*}|\leq 1,&\text{ in }\Omega\\
|\nabla u^{*}|=1\,&\mu^{*}\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\end{cases},

where
\Omega
is a bounded convex domain compactly containing
\support f
.
The approximation of the solution of (
1.2
) is a very difficult task. Indeed, the coupling of the
linear
PDE with the constraints on the gradient of
u
give rise to a highly
nonlinear
problem with inequality constraints in the variable
\mu
. Also, due to the nonlinearity of (
1.2
), it is not
a priori
clear weather (and in which sense) the solution
\mu_{n}
of certain discretized version of (
1.2
) would converge to the solution of (
1.2
).

The fundamental advance introduced in
[
16
,
17
]
is to address the numerical solution of the variational reformulation of the Monge-Kantorovich equation presented by
[
9
]
and solve the related optimization problem by a gradient method.
In these works, the authors introduce a Lyapunov-candidate functional and conjecture the existence of a continuous time gradient flow whose asymptotic solution coincides with the optimal transport density. Relying on this conjecture, the authors propose an efficient numerical scheme for the integration of the flow based on the explicit Euler discretization of the gradient flow and the finite element approximation of the relevant integrals. We would like to note that this approach is based on the formulation of a minimization problem and its subsequent discretization. The resolution of the conjecture has been hampered by the lack of results related to existence and uniqueness of the gradient flow and its trajectories, an outcome of the setup of the minimization problem in non-separable infinite dimensional spaces. As a consequence, no theoretical convergence of the above numerical scheme have been obtained and only experimental results are available to justify this approach.

The aim of the present work is to fill this gap and develop the complete theoretical convergence analysis of the FEM-based numerical method for the approximation of the
L^{1}
optimal transport density. The properties of the studied discretization naturally suggest that, in contrast with the explicit nature of the method proposed in
[
16
,
17
]
, implicit Euler and Newton schemes must combine effectively to provide a super-linearly convergent method for the solution of the
L^{1}
-optimal transport problem.

The strategy that allows the completion of our goal can be summarized as follows. Rather than tackling the proof of the conjecture, we reformulate the problem by first discretizing the relevant functional and then defining a gradient flow in a finite-dimensional setting. This discretize-minimize approach, opposite to the minimize-discretize suggested in
[
17
]
, defines a sequence of finite-dimensional approximations of the above mentioned Lyapunov-candidate functional that are designed to provide a sequence of minimizers that converge towards the optimal transport density. The minimization step of the proposed strategy exploits the real analyticity of these approximations to derive a well-posed and smooth gradient flow that converges exponentially towards the sought solution under mild hypotheses. Within this proposed framework, we are able to prove the convergence of the proposed algorithm towards the optimal transport density, to provide a sharp convergence rate estimate, and to design robust and efficient stopping criteria. The final scheme includes an adaptive time-stepping strategy that allows the use of a geometrically increasing sequence of time-step sizes leading to super-exponential convergence towards the minimizer, thus drastically improving the computational performance with respect to previous work.

1.2.
Our study

Our starting point is the variational characterization of the transport density proposed in
[
18
]
and the following definition of a
L^{1}
-
transport energy
:

Definition 1.1

(
L^{1}
-Transport energy
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let

f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
be such that

\co(\support f)\subset\subset\Omega
and
\int_{\Omega}fdx=0
. We
denote by
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)\rightarrow[0,+\infty]

the transport energy functional defined by

(1.3)

\mathcal{E}(\mu):=\sup_{u\in\mathscr{C}^{1}(\overline{\Omega}),\int_{\Omega}udx=0}\left(2\int_{\Omega}fu\,dx-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}d\mu\right)+\int_{\Omega}d\mu.

and we consider the following variational problem:

Problem 1.2

(Minimization of the transport energy)
.

Given
f,\Omega
as above, find
\mu_{\mathcal{E}}
such that

\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\mathcal{E}})=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}(\nu).

Here and throughout the paper we denote by
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
the
space of Borel signed measures on
\Omega),
by
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)

the non-negative Borel measures, and by
\mathcal{M}^{1}(\Omega)
the
space of Borel probability measures.
In addition, our work relies on the following fundamental property, see
[
18
, Prop. 2.1]
.

Proposition 1.3
.

Problem
1.2
has an unique solution
\mu_{\mathcal{E}}
which is an absolutely continuous measure with

d\mu_{\mathcal{E}}=\mu^{*}dx.

We start by introducing a double indexed sequence of functionals
\mathcal{E}_{n,m}
corresponding to the restriction of
\mathcal{E}
to certain finite element spaces
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}
and
\mathcal{W}_{m}
on which we approximate
\mu
and
u
, respectively (Section
2
). We consider the diagonal sequence
\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\mathcal{E}_{n,n}
and we show that its
\Gamma
-limit is
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
, the lower semicontinuous regularization of the restriction of
\mathcal{E}
to a dense subspace. Nevertheless, we prove in Theorem
2.6
that

\argmin_{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\subseteq\argmin_{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E},

moreover
any sequence
\{\mu_{n}^{*}\}
of minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
converges to the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
in the weak
∗
topology of measures, i.e.,

\mu_{n}^{*}\rightharpoonup\mu^{*}.

This result motivates the study of the numerical solution of the problems

\mu_{n}^{*}\in\argmin_{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}}\mathcal{E}_{n}.

Next (Section
3
), we study conditions that ensure well-posedness and well-conditioning of these discrete minimum problems. In particular we show in Proposition
3.2
that under the Hypothesis
3.1
there exists a unique minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e., the discretized minimum problems are well-posed. Under a slightly different assumption, namely Hypothesis
3.3
, we prove that such problems are also well-conditioned, see Proposition
3.4
.

The following step (Section
4
) is to prove global existence, uniqueness, and regularity of the following two gradient flows

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\mu}=-\partial^{\circ}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}),&\forall t>0\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}(0)=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}&\end{cases}\;\;,\;\;\;\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=-\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),&\forall t>0\\
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(0)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}\end{cases}\;.

Namely the gradient flow equation (
4.1
) for the function
E_{n}
which is obtained from
\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\mathcal{E}_{n,n}
with the use of canonical coordinates on
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}\cong\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
, and the gradient flow equation (
4.8
) for the function
F_{n}
which is obtained from
E_{n}
by composition with the coordinate square map (i.e.,
\mathbb{R}^{N}\ni\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mapsto(\sigma_{1}^{2},\sigma_{2}^{2},\dots\sigma_{N}^{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
). In particular, in Theorem
4.1
, we prove that any trajectory of the first flow is converging to a global minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, while we prove in Theorem
4.3
that any trajectory of the latter is converging to a local minimizer of
F_{n}
: applying the coordinate square map we recover a global minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{n}.

The definition and study of the optimization algorithms for
E_{n}
and
F_{n}
derived from the time discretization of the two flows above conclude our theoretical contributions (Section
5
). In the first case one needs to take into account the non-negativity constraint on
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, so an explicit time discretization seems to be necessary. Thus the gradient flow (
4.1
) of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
can be integrated by the so-called
projected forward Euler scheme
, which can be solved by Algorithm
1
. On the contrary, the gradient flow of
F_{n}
is defined on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and this makes feasible the use of an implicit scheme: our choice is indeed the
backward Euler scheme
coupled with the (zero finding) Newton’s Method with a prescribed initial guess. We focus on the latter scheme, both because it is more promising from a numerical point of view, e.g., there are no inequality constraints, and because -roughly speaking- the higher smoothness of the continuous time trajectories should provide faster convergence. We prove in Theorem
5.1
the consistency of this scheme, i.e., the Newton’s Method converges at each time step and the sequence of the backward Euler iterates converges to a local minimizer of
F_{n}.
Then we define Algorithm
2
by choosing a particular
a posteriori
stopping criterion that allows us to prove a stability estimate (see Proposition
5.2
). We finally prove in Theorem
5.3
the convergence of Algorithm
2
and give in Proposition
5.5
a sharp estimate of the rate of such convergence. The combination of Theorem
2.6
, Theorem
5.1
, and Theorem
5.3
leads to (see Corollary
5.4
)

{\lim_{n}}^{*}\mathcal{I}_{n}\big(\lim_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{\ell}\big)=\mu^{*}dx,

where we denoted by
\lim^{*}
the limit in the weak
∗
topology of measures, and by
\mathcal{I}_{n}
the embedding
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}^{+}
deriving from the FEM coordinates.

Finally (Section
2
), we perform some numerical tests of Algorithm
2
in few cases where the optimal transport density is known. Also we check numerically the assumptions we made for the well-posedness and well-conditioning of the discrete variational problems.

2.
\Gamma
-convergence of finite elements discretization

2.1.
Finite elements discretization of
\mathcal{E}

We want to introduce and study a sequence of functionals that
provides a well-designed sequence of approximations of
\mathcal{E}

for minimization purposes. In order to be termed ”well designed” the
sequence needs to fulfill certain desirable properties as preserving
the convexity of
\mathcal{E}
, having (at each stage) finite
dimensional domain, possibly being smooth, and being relatively easy
to be evaluated at any given point. In addition, since
we are interested in the minimization of
\mathcal{E}
, we need to be
able to prove that any sequence of minimizers converges to the
minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
. We introduce a suitable FEM-based
sequence of approximations of
\mathcal{E}
in the present subsection
and we will prove its convergence in the next subsection.

Let us remark that, as pointed out in
[
15
]
and in
[
18
]
,
the role of
\Omega
is not relevant in our work as long as it
is an open bounded convex set compactly containing the set

\co\left(\support f^{+}\cup\support f^{-}\right),
where
\co
stands for the convex envelope operator and
\support
for the support. Therefore, possibly
up to translations an dilations, we can assume
\Omega:=(0,1)^{d},
a very convenient choice for our purposes. This choice prevents the need of the analysis of
the so-called geometric error that occurs when considering
approximations of
\Omega
by unions of simplices which are the elements of choice in our approach.

First, we pick a sequence of nested triangulations

\mathcal{T}^{n}=\{T_{j}^{n}\}_{j=1}^{N}
of
\Omega
indexed over
n\in\mathbb{N}
and obtained by uniform refinements. Let us denote by
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{T}^{n})
the space of continuous real
functions
v
such that
v|_{T_{j}^{n}}
is an affine function for any

j=1,2,\dots,N.
Similarly,
\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathcal{T}^{n})
is the
space of functions
v\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
v|_{T_{j}^{n}}
is equivalent to a constant function for any
j=1,2,\dots,N.
Then we can consider the finite dimensional function spaces

\mathcal{M}_{n}:=\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathcal{T}^{n}),\;\;\mathcal{W}_{m}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{T}^{m+1}):\int_{\Omega}udx=0\right\}\;,

and denote by
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}
the non-negative cone in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}:=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathcal{T}^{n}):\mu|_{T_{j}^{n}}\geq 0,\,\forall T_{j}^{n}\in\mathcal{T}^{n}\right\}.

Second, we pick a monotone sequence
\{\delta_{m}\}
such that
\delta_{m}\downarrow 0
as
m\to+\infty
.

We can now introduce a double sequence
\{\mathcal{E}_{n,m}\}_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
of approximations of the functional
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
that
is a good candidate for the variational approximation of
\mathcal{E}.
Notice that we can re-write
\mathcal{E}
, for notational convenience,
as

\mathcal{E}(\mu):=\mathcal{L}(\mu)\;+\;\int_{\Omega}d\mu,

where

\mathcal{L}(\mu):=\sup_{u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\int_{\Omega}udx=0}\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)
and

\mathcal{D}(\mu,u):=\begin{cases}2\int_{\Omega}fu\,dx-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}d\mu&\text{ if }u\in\mathscr{C}^{1}(\overline{\Omega}),\;\int_{\Omega}udx=0\\
+\infty&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

Then we define

(2.1)

\mathcal{E}_{n,m}(\mu):=\begin{cases}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{W}_{m}}\mathcal{D}_{m}(\mu,u)+\int_{\Omega}d\mu&\text{ if }\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}\\
+\infty&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

where, for any
\mu\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega):=\{\mu\in L^{\infty}(\Omega):\,\mu\geq 0\text{ a.e.}\}
, we set

(2.2)

\mathcal{D}_{m}(\mu,u):=\begin{cases}2\int_{\Omega}fu\,dx-\int_{\Omega}(\mu+\delta_{m})|\nabla u|^{2}dx&\text{ if }u\in\mathcal{W}_{m}\\
+\infty&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

Remark 2.1
.

We would like to stress here that the use of the two indices
n
and
m
is dictated by notational convenience. Indeed, as we will see in the next sections, the principal object of our study will be the diagonal sequence
\mathcal{E}_{n,n}
, and in this case it produces the wanted combination of meshes and spaces
\mathcal{T}^{n}
for
\mathcal{M}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}^{n+1}
for
\mathcal{W}_{n}
.

In our work we will use the functionals

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\infty,m}(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{m}(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\scm\mathcal{E}_{\infty,m}(\mu),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{m}(\mu),

where
\scm
stands for the lower semicontinuous envelope with
respect to the weak
∗
topology of measures, i.e.,

\scm\mathcal{F}(\mu):=\sup\left\{\mathcal{G}(\mu),\;\mathcal{G}\leq\mathcal{F},\;\mathcal{G}\text{ is l.s.c. in the weak${}^{*}$ topology}\right\}.

Here and throughout the paper, when we consider measures that are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we use (by a slight abuse of notation) the same symbol both for the density of a measure and the measure itself. Accordingly,
\mu+\delta
denotes the measure
\mu+\delta dx.

Note that the definition of the functionals
\mathcal{E}_{n,m}
is taylored to the need of treating the convergence of minimizers. A more explicit representation is obtained by means of the linear isomorphism
\mathcal{I}_{n}
mapping
\mathbb{R}^{N}
onto
\mathcal{M}_{n}
. Precisely, for any
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{1\}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{2\}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{N\}}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we can define

\mathcal{I}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}\chi_{T_{i}^{n}}(x),

and, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
, we denote by
\boldsymbol{\mu}
the vector

(2.6)

\boldsymbol{\mu}:=\mathcal{I}_{n}^{-1}(\mu)=\left(\frac{\int_{T_{1}^{n}}\mu dx}{|T_{1}^{n}|},\frac{\int_{T_{2}^{n}}\mu dx}{|T_{2}^{n}|},\dots,\frac{\int_{T_{N}^{n}}\mu dx}{|T_{N}^{n}|}\right)^{t}.

Analogously, given a basis
\{\phi_{m,1},\phi_{m,2},\dots,\phi_{m,M}\}
of
\mathcal{W}_{m}
(where
M:=\dimension\mathcal{W}_{m}
), we can identify any function
u\in\mathcal{W}_{m}
with its coordinates
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, where
u(x)=\sum_{p=1}^{M}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\{p\}}\phi_{m,p}(x).

Given
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, we us introduce the matrices

(2.7)

A^{(n,m,i)}:=\left(\int_{T_{i}^{n}}\nabla\phi_{m,p}\cdot\nabla\phi_{m,q}dx\right)_{p,q=1,\dots,M}

and, for any
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
, the matrix-valued function (the stiffness matrix)

(2.8)

A^{(n,m)}(\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}+\delta_{m})A^{(n,m,i)}.

We extend such a definition to each
\mu\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
\mu\geq 0
a.e., by setting

(2.9)

A^{(n,m)}(\mu):=\left(\int_{\Omega}(\mu+\delta_{m})\nabla\phi_{m,p}\cdot\nabla\phi_{m,q}dx\right)_{p,q=1,\dots,M}.

We also denote by
\boldsymbol{f}_{m}
the load vector
({\boldsymbol{f}_{m}}_{\{1\}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{f}_{m}}_{\{M\}})^{t}
, where
{\boldsymbol{f}_{m}}_{\{p\}}=\int_{\Omega}f\phi_{m,p}dx
,
p=1,2,\dots,M.

Remark 2.2
.

We remark that, in order to distinguish indeces denoting an element of a sequence from indeces refering to a component of a vector, we use the following convention: in the latter instance we add parentheses to the index, e.g., given the sequence of vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we denote by
{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}}_{\{i\}}
the
i
-th component of the vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
.

It is worth pointing out that the operation of taking the supremum of
\mathcal{D}_{m}
among
u\in\mathcal{W}_{m}
(used in the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{n,m}
given in eq. (
2.1
)) corresponds to calculating the
\mathcal{P}_{1}
-finite element approximation of the solution of

(2.10)

\begin{cases}-\divergence((\mu+\delta_{m})\nabla u)=f&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\partial_{n}u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\\
\int udx=0\end{cases}.

More precisely, given
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}
, we have

(2.11)

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{W}_{m}}\mathcal{D}_{m}(\mu,u)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{W}_{m}}\mathcal{D}_{m}(\mu,u)=\int fu[\mu]dx=\int(\mu+\delta_{m})|\nabla u[\mu]|^{2}dx,

where
u[\mu]
is the unique element of
\mathcal{W}_{m}
such that

(2.12)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u[\mu]\cdot\nabla v(\mu+\delta_{m})dx=\int_{\Omega}fvdx,\;\forall v\in\mathcal{W}_{m},

in other words,

(2.13)

A^{(n,m)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\boldsymbol{u}[\boldsymbol{\mu}]=\boldsymbol{f}_{m}.

In view of this we set
E_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\mathcal{E}_{n,m}(\mu)
, that is

(2.14)

E_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\begin{cases}(\boldsymbol{f}_{m})^{t}[A^{(n,m)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})]^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}_{m}+\langle\boldsymbol{\mu};\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\rangle&\text{ if }\ {\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}\geq 0,\,i=1,2,\dots,N\\
+\infty&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

where we denoted by
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}
the area vector
\left(\int_{T_{1}^{n}}dx,\int_{T_{2}^{n}}dx,\dots,\int_{T_{N}^{N}}dx\right)^{t}.

Let us consider the coordinate square map
\cdot^{\boldsymbol{2}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined by setting

(2.15)

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\boldsymbol{2}}:=({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\{1\}}^{2},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\{2\}}^{2},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\{N\}}^{2}).

We can introduce the functional

F_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=E_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{\sigma^{2}}).

Notice that the image under the map
\cdot^{\boldsymbol{2}}
of any local minimizer
\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}
of
F_{n,m}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a local minimizer of
E_{n,m}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}.
Moreover
{\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}}^{\boldsymbol{2}}
is a global minimizer of
E_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
due to the convexity of the objective.

The next proposition collects some properties of
E_{n,m}
and
F_{n,m}
that will be useful later on.

Proposition 2.3

(Differential properties of
E_{n,m}
and
F_{n,m}
)
.

For any
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
the functional
E_{n,m}
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and real analytic on

\mathbb{R}_{\delta_{m}}^{N}:=\{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}>-\delta_{m},\,i=1,2,\dots,N\}\supset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}.

The functional
F_{n,m}
is real analytic on
\mathbb{R}^{N}.
In particular,
\forall\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
and any
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\boldsymbol{2}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
, we have

(2.16)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E_{n,m}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=1-(\boldsymbol{u}[\boldsymbol{\mu}])^{t}A^{(n,m,i)}\boldsymbol{u}[\boldsymbol{\mu}]

(2.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{n,m}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\{i\}}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=2{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\{i\}}\frac{\partial E_{n,m}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\boldsymbol{2}})

the optimal transport density of the Monge-Kantorovich equations.

Remark 2.7
.

We choose to focus our study on the ”diagonal sequence” of functionals
\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\mathcal{E}_{n,n}
to simplify our notation and avoid few technicalities in our proofs. However, the reader can easily check that our results still hold for any subsequence
\{\mathcal{E}_{n_{j},m_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\{n_{j}\}
and
\{m_{j}\}
are non-decreasing diverging sequences.

\Gamma
-convergence is the main tool that we are to going to use in the proof of Theorem
2.6
, and, to fix our notation, we recall in the following paragraphs the needed definitions and properties. Let
(X,\tau)
be a topological space and, for any
x\in X
, let us denote by
\mathcal{N}(x)
the filter of the neighborhoods of
x.
Let
f_{n}:X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}},

n\in\mathbb{N}.
We define

\displaystyle\left(\gammaliminf_{n\to+\infty}f_{n}\right)(x)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{U\in\mathcal{N}(x)}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\inf_{y\in U}f_{n}(y),

\displaystyle\left(\gammalimsup_{n\to+\infty}f_{n}\right)(x)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{U\in\mathcal{N}(x)}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\inf_{y\in U}f_{n}(y).

If there exists a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
such that

(2.24)

\left(\gammalimsup_{n\to+\infty}f_{n}\right)(x)\leq f(x)\leq\left(\gammaliminf_{n\to+\infty}f_{n}\right)(x),\;\;\forall x\in X,

then we say that
f_{n}

\Gamma
-converges to
f
with respect to the topology
\tau
and we write
f_{n}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}f
or
\gammalim f_{n}=f.

Our main interest on this notion of convergence is given by the following property (cfr. for instance
[
14
, Cor. 7.20]
). Assume that
f_{n}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}f
and
x_{n}
is a minimizer of
f_{n}
. Then any cluster point
x
of
\{x_{n}\}
is a minimizer of
f
and
f(x)=\limsup_{n}f_{n}(x_{n}).
If moreover
x_{n}
converges to
x
in the topology
\tau,
then
f(x)=\lim_{n}f_{n}(x_{n}).

A useful property of
\Gamma
-convergence is that it is well-behaving under monotone limits of lower semicontinuous functionals,
[
14
, Prop. 5.4, Rem. 5.5, Prop. 5.7]
. Indeed we can prove a preliminary result exploiting the two monotonicities of the double sequence
\mathcal{E}_{n,m}.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let the functionals
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{E}_{n,m}

\gammalim_{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{m}=\mathcal{E}_{\infty}

Proof.

Equation

\{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{m}\}
is an increasing sequence of lower semicontinuous functionals and hence its
\Gamma
-limit coincides with its point-wise limit.

To prove the second statement, we first notice that

(2.27)

\sup_{m}\mathcal{E}_{\infty,m}=\sup_{m}\inf_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}=\inf_{n}\sup_{m}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}.

The first equality follows by definition. For the second equality we proceed as follows. We first notice that, if

then there exists

such that, for any

and

Then we can write

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\inf_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}(\mu)=

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\begin{cases}\mathcal{E}_{\bar{n},m}(\mu)&\text{ if }\mu\in\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}\\
+\infty&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\sup_{m}\mathcal{E}_{\bar{n},m}(\mu)&\text{ if }\mu\in\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{M}_{n}^{+}\\

If we pick the lower semicontinuous regularization of

\scm\sup_{m}\mathcal{E}_{\infty,m}=\scm\sup_{m}\inf_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}=\scm\inf_{n}\sup_{m}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}.

Note that, since iterated

\scm\sup_{m}\mathcal{E}_{\infty,m}=\sup_{m}\scm\mathcal{E}_{\infty,m}=\gammalim_{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{m}=\mathcal{E}_{\infty}.

On the other hand, using again the monotonicity we have

\displaystyle\scm\sup_{m}\inf_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}=

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\scm\inf_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}=\sup_{m}\gammalim_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}

(2.30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gammalim_{m}\gammalim_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n,m},

(2.31)

\displaystyle\scm\inf_{n}\sup_{m}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}=

\displaystyle\scm\inf_{n}\gammalim_{m}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}=\gammalim_{n}\gammalim_{m}\mathcal{E}_{n,m}

The combination of equations (

Proposition 2.9

Let us define

We have seen that the sequence of minimum problems

Hypothesis 3.1

There exists

If Hypothesis

Now we want to conclude that

5.
Minimization algorithms from numerical integration of the gradient flows

Since the gradient flow of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
converges to
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*}\in\argmin\mathcal{E}_{n}
as
t\to+\infty
, we may try to numerically integrate the flow, in order to design an approximation algorithm for
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*}
. The most straighforward idea is to adapt the forward Euler scheme to our constrained setting by enforcing the positivity constraint at each iteration. When the feasible set is a convex subset of an Hilbert space, one can modify such a scheme including an orthogonal projection of the Euler update on the feasible set. This algorithm (and its many variants) can be casted within the framework of
proximal algorithms
(see
[
29
]
) and is termed
projected gradient descent
or
projected forward Euler scheme
, and its application to our case is described in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Projected forward Euler Scheme

Input
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
,
\tau>0
,
n_{step}\in\mathbb{N}
,
toll>0

while

s<n_{step}
and
err>toll

do

s=s+1

Compute
\boldsymbol{u}=A^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\delta_{n})\boldsymbol{f}

Compute
\boldsymbol{v}=(\boldsymbol{u}^{t}A^{(j)}\boldsymbol{u}^{t})_{j}-1

Set
v^{(j)}=(v^{(j)})^{+}
if
\mu^{(j)}=0

Set
\boldsymbol{\mu}:=(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\tau\boldsymbol{v})^{+}

err=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}

end

while

return

\boldsymbol{\mu}

The convergence analysis of Algorithm
1
is rather standard. It can be proven that, for sufficiently small and constant time steps, any cluster point of the sequence computed by Algorithm
1
is a stationary point of the objective functional, see for instance
[
7
]
. Unfortunately, the use of the orthogonal projection in the definition of the iterates implies a non-monotone convergence and may cause few stability issues, expecially if the objective tends to be non-strongly convex.

For these reasons we pursue a different strategy by working with
F_{n}
, thus avoiding the explicit implementation of the non-negativity constraint. This entails the derivation of a numerical algorithm that mimics De Giorgi’s
minimizing movements
which have been introduced for the construction of gradient flows in very general contexts, such as Banach or metric spaces,
[
2
]
. This approach applied to the construction of the gradient flow of the functional
F_{n}
consists in picking an initial point
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0}
and a time step
\tau
(or a time step sequence
\{\tau^{\ell}\}
), then computing iteratively
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}
such that

(5.1)

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau):=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}}{2\tau}.

Then, one shrinks the size of the time step (or the size of the partition in the case of variable time stepping) to zero and tries to prove that the linear interpolation of the discrete time trajectories is converging to a solution of the gradient flow equation.

Here we are interested in approximating a minimizer of
F_{n}
rather than computing the trajectory of the flow. Therefore we are not going to shrink the time step to zero, instead we keep iterating eq. (
5.1
) and consider
\lim_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}.
The derived optimization technique is called
variational backward Euler method

[
26
]
and is still casted by some authors (see e.g.,
[
20
]
) in the framework of proximal algorithms.

Note that equation (
5.1
) does not define yet a numerical algorithm, since one needs to choose a method for solving the optimization problem and a stopping criterion for
\ell
, possibly with error bounds. Starting from (
5.1
), the proposed numerical algorithm is based on the following choices:

•

the variational problem (
5.1
) is replaced by the first-order optimality condition

(5.2)

\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1};\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)=\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1})+\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}}{\tau}=0,

which in principle may be not equivalent to eq. (
5.1
).

•

the solution of the non-linear problem (
5.2
) is approximated by Newton method starting at
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}
,

•

Newton method is stopped with a specific criterion based on the relative residual and on the sign of the new iterate, quantities that are both easily computed
a posteriori
,

•

the algorithm stops when
\|\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\|
is smaller than a prescribed tollerance, as suggested by Proposition
4.5
.

The final scheme is reported in Algorithm
2
.

Passing from the study of the iterated solution of (

some issues come into play. First, since the function

we will prove that, for a sufficiently small

, we can iterativelly compute

Note that the use of the Newton method and the prescribed choice of the initial data provide a natural well-defined sequence

even when the solution of (

Theorem

refers to an exact solution of (

along the computed discrete trajectory, see Proposition

the following sequences are well defined

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,0}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r+1}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}-\left[\Hess G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell+1,r};\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)\right]^{-1}\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell+1,r};\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}:=\lim_{r}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}.

For any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(5.3)

\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1})=-\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}}{\tau}.

Moreover there exists
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*}\in\argmin E_{n}
such that

(5.4)

\lim_{\ell}{(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})}^{\boldsymbol{2}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*}.

Proof.

The first part of the proof is a sharp version of the classical proof of the local convergence of Newton method.
Let
\Omega_{\ell}
be the connected component of the interior of the set
\{F_{n}\leq F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\}
containing
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}
. Let us set

(5.5)

\displaystyle d_{\ell}:=\diam(\Omega_{\ell}),

(5.6)

\displaystyle U_{\ell}:=\cup_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Omega_{\ell}}B(\boldsymbol{\sigma},d_{\ell}),

(5.7)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\ell}:=\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Omega_{\ell}}\|\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma};\boldsymbol{\sigma},\tau)\|,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}:=\min_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\lambda_{min}(\Hess F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma})),

(5.9)

\displaystyle\Lambda:=\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\overline{\Omega}}\lambda_{max}(\Hess F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma})),

(5.10)

\displaystyle P_{i}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma}):=\Hess\partial_{i}F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma})),\;i=1,2,\dots,N,

(5.11)

\displaystyle R_{\ell}:=\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\rho(P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma})),\dots,\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\rho(P_{N}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma}))\right).

Notice that the function
G_{n}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma},\tau)
has Hessian matrix independent by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
:

\Hess G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi};\boldsymbol{\sigma},\tau)=\Hess F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi})+\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{I}

and it is strongly convex on
U_{\ell}
for any
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Omega_{\ell}
, provided

(5.12)

\tau<\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ell}^{-}}=:\tau_{1,\ell}.

Assuming (
5.12
), we can denote by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}
the unique minimizer of
G_{n}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)
in
U_{\ell}.
Note that, since

F_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1})+\frac{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}}{2\tau}=G_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1};\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)\leq G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell};\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)=F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}),

then

(5.13)

F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})-F_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1})\geq\frac{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}}{2\tau}.

This in particular implies that
F_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1})\leq F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})
, but
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}
may lie in a component of
\{F_{n}<F(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\}
different from
\Omega_{\ell}.
This cannot occour because
G_{n}(\cdot):=G_{n}(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)
is
(\lambda_{\ell}+1/\tau)
-convex in
U_{\ell}
, for any
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in U_{\ell}
, i.e.,

G_{n}((1-t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}+t\boldsymbol{\varsigma})\leq(1-t)G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+tG_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma})-t(1-t)\frac{\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1}{2\tau}|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\varsigma}|^{2},\;\;\forall t\in(0,1)\;.

Indeed, if we assume that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}
lies in the disjoint component
\tilde{\Omega}_{\ell}
, take
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in[\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}]\cap\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{\ell}
so that
F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})
, and set
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{\varsigma}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}
to yeld

\displaystyle G(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi})+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}}{2\tau}=F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}}{2\tau}

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})+tF_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+t\frac{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}}{2\tau}-t(1-t)\frac{\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1}{2\tau}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}\,,

where
t=\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|/\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|
.
From the previous equation we can write:

\displaystyle t(F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})-F_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|}{2\tau}+t\frac{\|{\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|}{2\tau}-t(1-t)(\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1)\frac{\|{\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|}{2\tau}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-t(1-t)\lambda_{\ell}\tau\frac{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}}{2\tau}\leq 0

which is in contrast with (
5.13
). As a consequence
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}\in\Omega_{\ell}.

We want to show that using Newton method with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}
as initial guess we compute
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}
(possibly with an infinite number of steps).
It is convenient to introduce the following notations in order to simplify the computations:

\displaystyle e^{\ell+1,r}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle s^{\ell+1,r}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1,r-1}\,.

Then, we can rewrite the Newton step as

s^{\ell+1,r+1}=-[\Hess G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r})]^{-1}\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}).

We now assume that, for a given
r\in\mathbb{N}
and for
s=0,1,\ldots,r
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,s}\in U_{\ell}.
Writing the second order Taylor expansion for
0=\nabla G_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1})
centered at
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}
, multipling both sides of the equation by
[\Hess G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r})]^{-1}
and using Newton equation, we obtain

\displaystyle\|e^{\ell+1,r+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|[\Hess G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r})]^{-1}((e^{\ell+1,r})^{t}P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})e^{\ell+1,r},\dots,(e^{\ell+1,r})^{t}P_{N}(\boldsymbol{\xi})e^{\ell+1,r})^{t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{2(\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1)}\|R\|\|e^{\ell+1,r}\|^{2}.

Thus

(5.14)

\|e^{\ell+1,r+1}\|\leq\frac{\|R\|\tau}{2(\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1)}\|e^{\ell+1,r}\|^{2}.

Using the first order Taylor expansion of
0=\nabla G_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1})
centered at
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,0}
we obtain

\|e^{\ell+1,0}\|\leq\frac{\tau}{\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1}\|\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\|\leq\frac{\tau\gamma}{\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1}.

It is not difficult to see that, if

(5.15)

\tau<\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\|R_{\ell}\|\gamma_{\ell}}-\lambda_{\ell})^{+}}=:\tau_{2,\ell},

then

\frac{\|R\|\tau}{2(\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1)}\|e^{\ell+1,0}\|<1/2
. Thus, due to (
5.14
), we have

(5.16)

\|e^{\ell+1,1}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|e^{\ell+1,0}\|\,.

In particular,

\frac{\|R\|\tau}{2(\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1)}\|e^{\ell+1,1}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|R\|\tau}{2(\lambda_{\ell}\tau+1)}\|e^{\ell+1,0}\|

and, by finite induction,
\|e^{\ell+1,r+1}\|\leq\frac{1}{2^{r+1}}\|e^{\ell+1,0}\|
.
Therefore, we can conclude that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r+1}\in B(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1},d_{\ell})\subseteq U_{\ell}
and that we can iterate the above reasoning to get

\|e^{\ell+1,r+1}\|\leq\frac{1}{2^{r+1}}d_{\ell}\qquad\mbox{for all }r\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Using in addition (
5.14
), we can conclude that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}
is converging quadratically to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}
and (
5.3
) holds true.

Note that the bounds (
5.12
) and (
5.15
) on the time-step size can be uniformly satisfied by setting

(5.17)

\tau^{*}(c):=\min\{\tau^{*}_{1}(c),\tau^{*}_{2}(c)\}\leq\min\{\inf_{\ell}\tau_{1,\ell},\inf_{\ell}\tau_{2,\ell}\}\,,

where

\tau^{*}_{1}(c):=\frac{1}{\lambda^{-}_{c}}\qquad\tau^{*}_{2}(c):=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\|R_{c}\|\gamma_{c}}-\lambda_{c})^{+}}\,,

and

\displaystyle d:=\diam(\Omega_{0})\,,

\displaystyle U:=\cup_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Omega_{0}}B(\boldsymbol{\sigma},d)\,,

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}:=\min_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U}\lambda_{min}(\Hess F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma}))\,,

\displaystyle R_{c}:=\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U}\rho(P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma})),\dots,\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U}\rho(P_{N}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma}))\right)\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{c}:=\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U}\max_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Omega_{0}}\|\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma};\boldsymbol{\sigma},\tau)\|\,.

Now we show that the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is convergent to a point
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}
, that needs to be a critical point for
F_{n}.
To this aim, since
0=\tau\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell};\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell-1},\tau)=\tau\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell-1}
, if
\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})=0
for some
\ell
, then
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\equiv\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell-1}\equiv\dots\equiv\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}
. In such a case
\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0})=\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})=0
but this violates the hypothesis Theorem
5.1
. Thus we can assume
\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\neq 0

\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\in\Omega_{\ell}

\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, using (
5.3
) and the definintion of
\lambda_{c}
, we can write the second order Taylor expansion of
F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{j})
centered at
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{j+1}
and get the estimate

F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{j})-F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{j+1})\geq\frac{\lambda_{c}\tau+2}{2\tau}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{j+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{j}\|^{2}=\tau\frac{\lambda_{c}\tau+2}{2}\|\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{j+1})\|^{2}.

Summing up over
j
ranging from
0
to
\ell-1
we obtain

(5.18)

F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0})-F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\geq\frac{\lambda_{c}\tau+2}{2\tau}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{j+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{j}\|^{2}=\tau\frac{\lambda_{c}\tau+2}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\|\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{j+1})\|^{2}.

Since
F_{n}
is bounded from below, then the sequences
\{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\|\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\|\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
are square-summable, and we can conclude that
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy. Denoting by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}
the limit of
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}},
the continuity of
\nabla F_{n}
allows us to state that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}
is critical for
F_{n}.

We are still left to prove that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*}:={\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}}^{\boldsymbol{2}}
is a minimizer of
E_{n}
. To this aim we introduce the set
I_{0}:=\{i\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}:\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{*}=0\}
and we distinguish the two cases
I_{0}=\emptyset
and
I_{n}\neq\emptyset.
In the first case, if for any
i=1,2,\dots,N

{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{*}\neq 0
and
0=\partial_{i}F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*})=2{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{*}\partial_{i}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*})
, then we have
\nabla E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*})=0
. The convexity of
E_{n}
allows to conclude.

In the second case we first notice that, for any
i=1,2,\dots,N
, the sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
has constant sign, being either constantly non-negative or constantly non-positive. FMoreover we recall that,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}
is characterized as the unique minimizer of
G_{n}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)
in
\Omega_{\ell}
. If for one
i\in\{1,2,\dots N\}
we have
\sign{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell}\neq\sign({\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell+1})
, then the point

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}:=(\sign{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}}_{\{1\}}|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}}_{\{1\}}|,\dots,\sign{(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}}_{\{N\}}|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}}_{\{N\}}|)

is such that
F_{n}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1})=F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1})
and
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|^{2}
. Thus
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{\ell+1}
minimizes
G_{n}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell},\tau)
in
\Omega_{\ell}
as well, leading to a contradiction. Thus
\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
needs to be either non-negative for any
\ell
or non-positive for any
\ell
.

Then, we can pick a subsequence
j\mapsto\ell_{j}
such that
\sign{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell_{j}}=\sign\partial_{i}F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell_{j}}),
for any
i\in I_{0}.
This last claim can be proven again by contradiction. Indeed, writing
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell-1}-\tau\partial_{i}F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})
, and using the fact that the sign of
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell}
is constant, shows that
\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
must be a non-increasing sequence for
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0})_{i}<0
or a non-decreasing sequence for
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0})_{i}>0
, and cannot converge to
0
.

From the fact that

\sign{(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell_{j}})}_{\{i\}}=\sign\partial_{i}F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell_{j}})=\sign\left(2{(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell_{j}})}_{\{i\}}\partial_{i}E_{n}({(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell_{j}})}^{\boldsymbol{2}})\right),

we easily obtain

\partial_{i}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*})=\lim_{j}\partial_{i}E_{n}({(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell_{j}})}^{\boldsymbol{2}})\geq 0,\;\;\forall i\in I.

On the other hand,
\partial_{i}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*})=0
for any
i\notin I_{0}
due to (
5.18
) and the definition of
I_{0}
. Thus we have

(5.19)

\begin{cases}\partial_{i}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*})\geq 0,&\forall i\\
\partial_{i}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*})=0,&\forall i:{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}}_{\{i\}}^{*}>0\end{cases}\;\;.

These are precisely the KKT necessary optimality conditions for
E_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}.
Since the function
E_{n}
is convex, such conditions are also sufficient, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*}\in\argmin E_{n}.

∎

In order to continue our study of Algorithm
2
, it is convenient to introduce some notations. Let us denote by

\Phi(\cdot;\tau):\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geq}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geq}

the map that, for any
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, returns the exact solution of (
5.2
) provided by the Newton method with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}
as initial guess. As a biproduct of Theorem
5.1
this map is well defined, provided
\tau>0
is sufficiently small. We also define the map
\Phi_{\epsilon}(\cdot;\tau):\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
as

\Phi_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell};\tau):=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell+1,\hat{r}},

where

(5.20)

\hat{r}:=\min\left\{r:|\partial_{i}G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r})|\leq\epsilon|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}}_{\{i\}}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}}_{\{i\}}|,\;\sign{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell+1,r}}_{\{i\}}=\sign{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}}_{\{i\}}\,\forall i\right\}.

We remark that, given an intial point
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0}
and suitable
\epsilon,\tau>0
, the Algorithm
2
computes the finite sequence

(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{2},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{3},\dots):=\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0},\Phi_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau),\Phi^{2}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau),\Phi^{3}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau),\dots\right)

of length at most
n_{step}+1
.

Proposition 5.2

(Stability estimate for Algorithm
2
)
.

Let
\min F_{n}<c<+\infty
and let
\tau<\tau^{*}(c)
be as above. There exists
\epsilon^{*}>0
, depending only on
c
and
\tau
, such that

(5.21)

F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})-F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau))\geq\frac{F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})-F_{n}(\Phi(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau))}{2},\;\;\forall\boldsymbol{\sigma}:F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})<c,

for any
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon^{*}
.

Proof.

It is convenient to introduce the notation

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle:=F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})-F_{n}(\Phi(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau))\;,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle:=F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})-F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau))\;,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}

\displaystyle:=\Phi(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau)-\boldsymbol{\sigma}\;,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle:=\Phi_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau)-\boldsymbol{\sigma}\;.

Let us pick
0<\epsilon<\lambda_{c}+1/\tau
.
Using the standard error bound for the Newton method we can write

\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\epsilon}\|:=\|\Phi_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau)-\Phi(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau)\|\leq\max_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Omega_{0}}\|[\Hess G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi})]^{-1}\|\|\nabla G_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau))\|.

Then, by the
(\lambda_{c}+1/\tau)
-convexity of
G_{n}
and the stopping criterion (
5.20
), we get

(5.22)

\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\epsilon}\|\leq\frac{\tau\epsilon}{\lambda_{c}\tau+1}.

By the triangular inequality we have

\|\boldsymbol{s}_{\epsilon}\|\leq\frac{\lambda_{c}\tau+1}{(\lambda_{c}-\epsilon)\tau+1}\|\boldsymbol{s}\|:=C_{\epsilon}\|\boldsymbol{s}\|\,,

which yields:

(5.23)

\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\epsilon}\|\leq\frac{C_{\epsilon}\epsilon\tau}{\lambda_{c}\tau+1}\|\boldsymbol{s}\|\,.

Using the second order Taylor expansion of
G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
centered at

\Phi(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau)
we can obtain

(5.24)

\Delta\geq\frac{\lambda_{c}\tau+2}{2\tau}\|\boldsymbol{s}\|^{2}.

On the other hand, due to (
5.23
) and (
5.24
), we can write

\displaystyle\Delta_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\Delta-(F_{n}(\Phi(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau))-F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\tau)))=\Delta+\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{\epsilon},\frac{\boldsymbol{s}}{\tau}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{\epsilon}^{T}\Hess F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{e}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\Delta-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{s}\|\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\epsilon}\|}{\tau}-\frac{\Lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\geq\Delta-\|\boldsymbol{s}\|^{2}\left(C_{\epsilon}\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda_{c}\tau+1}+\frac{\Lambda C_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2}\frac{(\epsilon\tau)^{2}}{(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Delta\left[1-\frac{2\tau C_{\epsilon}\epsilon}{(\lambda_{c}\tau+2)(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)}\left(1+\frac{\Lambda\tau^{2}C_{\epsilon}\epsilon}{\lambda_{c}\tau+1}\right)\right]\;.

In order to conclude the proof, we are left to verify that for small

\epsilon>0
we have

1-\frac{2\tau C_{\epsilon}\epsilon}{(\lambda_{c}\tau+2)(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)}\left(1+\frac{\Lambda\tau^{2}C_{\epsilon}\epsilon}{\lambda_{c}\tau+1}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Thus

(5.25)

C_{\epsilon}\epsilon\leq\left(\sqrt{1+\Lambda\tau(\lambda_{c}\tau+2)}-1\right)\frac{\lambda_{c}\tau+1}{2\Lambda\tau^{2}}\,.

Note that
C_{\epsilon}\epsilon=\epsilon+o(\epsilon)
as

\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, so (
5.25
) is certainly satisfied for
\epsilon>0
small enough.
∎

Theorem 5.3

(Convergence of Algorithm
2
)
.

Let
\min F_{n}<c<+\infty
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0}
such that
F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0})<c
and
\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0})\neq 0
. Let
\tau>0
and
\epsilon
satisfy the hypothesis of Proposition
5.2
. Then the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}:=\{\Phi_{\epsilon}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
admits a limit
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}
. Moreover

(5.26)

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*}:={(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*})}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\in\argmin E_{n}.

Proof.

If
\nabla F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))=0
for certain
\ell
, then the use of
\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)
as initial guess in the Newton method for the computation of
[\Phi_{\epsilon}]^{(\ell+1)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)
guarantees that
\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell+j)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)=\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)
for any
j\in\mathbb{N}.
More importantly, using (
5.20
), we can repeat the argument of the proof of Theorem
5.1
, to show that
\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)\equiv\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0}
, which is not possible since we are assuming
\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0})\neq 0.
Therefore we can assume without lost of generality that
\nabla F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))\neq 0

\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

From Proposition
5.2
and the optimality of the exact step, i.e.,
\Phi^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)-\Phi^{(\ell-1)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)=-\tau\nabla F_{n}(\Phi^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))
, to get

\displaystyle F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))-F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell+1)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))-F_{n}(\Phi(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau));\tau))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{c}\tau+1}{4\tau}\left\|\Phi(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau));\tau)-\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)\right\|^{2}.

Notice that, using the notation introduced in the proof of Proposition
5.2

On the other hand, using (

\displaystyle F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))-F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell+1)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))\geq\frac{[(\lambda_{c}-\epsilon)\tau+1]^{2}}{4\tau(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)}\|\boldsymbol{s}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{[(\lambda_{c}-\epsilon)\tau+1]^{2}}{4\tau(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)}\|\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell+1)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)-\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)\|^{2}.

It is clear that, for any

we have

. Thus we can write

\displaystyle+\infty>c-\min F_{n}\geq\lim_{\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\left(F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))-F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(j+1)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{[(\lambda_{c}-\epsilon)\tau+1]^{2}}{2\tau(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\|\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(j+1)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)-\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)\|^{2}

Thus in particular
F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))-F_{n}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell+1)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau))\to 0
as

and
\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau)
is a Cauchy sequence, whose limit we denote by

.

By a similar reasoning, starting from (
5.27
) we can show that

thus
\Phi(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{0};\tau);\tau)\to\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}.
It follows, by the definition of the map

i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}
is critical for

We are left to show that

is actually a local minimizer of

and thus
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{*}
is a global minimizer for

We reason as in the final step of the proof of Theorem

. If none of the components of

Proof.

Let us introduce the following auxiliary functions and notations. We denote by

Therefore we can compute, for

(5.33)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell-1}}{\tau}=\frac{\boldsymbol{s}_{\epsilon}}{\tau}

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})-F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell-1})}{\tau}=-\frac{\Delta_{\epsilon}}{\tau}\;,

raised

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\prime}(t)\|
and
\int_{t}^{+\infty}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\prime}(s)\|ds

Let us start from the lower bound. We pick

The lower bound is obtained by means of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Let us start noticing that, due to the chain rule and (

\frac{d}{dt}H^{\vartheta}(t)=\vartheta H^{\frac{\vartheta}{1-\vartheta}}(t)H^{\prime}(t)=-\vartheta\frac{\Delta_{\epsilon}}{\tau}H^{\frac{\vartheta}{1-\vartheta}}(t)\leq 0.

Then, using the estimates (
5.27

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}
such that

Combining this last equation with (
5.35

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H^{\vartheta}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\vartheta\frac{((\lambda_{c}-\epsilon)\tau+1)^{2}}{2(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell-1}\|}{\tau}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell-1}\|}{\tau L\|\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})\|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\vartheta\frac{((\lambda_{c}-\epsilon)\tau+1)^{2}}{2(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell-1}\|}{\tau}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell-1}\|}{\tau L\|\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})+\Hess F_{n}(\eta)(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\vartheta\frac{((\lambda_{c}-\epsilon)\tau+1)^{2}}{2(\lambda_{c}\tau+1)}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell-1}\|}{\tau}

6.
An improved algorithm with adaptive time-stepping

In the present section we introduce a variant of Algorithm
2
that fully exploits the local convexity of the objective functional
F_{n}
around its minimizers achieving an improved global convergence.
Indeed, under the conditions stated in Hypothesis

The development of this enhanced algorithm takes inspiration from a closer examination of the first part of the proof of Theorem
5.1

), which derives from the uniformization of the sharper requirements (
5.12
) and (
5.15
) with respect to the time-step
\ell
. Note that this uniformization procedure is required only when working with fixed time-step size.
On the other hand, when we allow for a variable time-step size
\tau_{\ell}
, we can repeat the argument of the first part of the proof of Theorem
5.1
directly using (
5.12
) and (
5.15
) instead of (
5.17
). The rest of the proof works also in this variable time-step size framework with only minor modifications. The same holds true for Proposition
5.2
and Theorem
5.3
.

It is very natural to wonder which sequences
\{\tau_{\ell}\}
can be used to define our modified algorithm. Notice that, under Hypothesis
3.3
, the sequences of the sets
\Omega_{\ell}
and
U_{\ell}
shrink to the point
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}
, a minimizer of
F_{n}
that possesses a neighbourhood
U
such that
F_{n}
is strongly convex on
U
. Thus

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell}\min_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\lambda_{min}(\Hess F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{min}(\Hess F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}))>0,

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell}\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Omega_{\ell}}\|\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma};\boldsymbol{\sigma},\tau_{\ell})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell}\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Omega_{\ell}}\|\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\|+\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{\ell}}+\Lambda_{\ell}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}\|+\frac{\diam\Omega_{\ell}}{\tau_{\ell}}=0

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell}R_{\ell}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell}\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\rho(P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma})),\dots,\max_{\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\in\co U_{\ell}}\rho(P_{N}(\boldsymbol{\varsigma}))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\rho(P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*})),\dots,\rho(P_{N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}))\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Hence both the upper bounds for
\tau_{\ell}
are diverging as
\ell\to+\infty
:

(6.1)

\lim_{\ell}\min\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ell}^{-}},\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\|R_{\ell}\|\gamma_{\ell}}-\lambda_{\ell})^{+}}\right\}=+\infty.

This provides a justification for the use of an increasing sequence
\{\tau_{\ell}\}
of time-step sizes. However a trade-off is clearly needed in picking
\{\tau_{\ell}\}
: on one hand a faster diverging sequence would lead to a faster converging algorithm, but on the other hand, in order to ensure the Newton method convergence,
\tau_{\ell}
needs to satisfy the upper bounds (
5.12
) and (
5.15
) at any finite stage
\ell
.

Our strategy is to choose the classical geometric sequence whereby the new tentative time-step size for time level
\ell+1
is set to

(6.2)

\tau_{\ell+1}=\alpha\tau_{\ell}\,,

with
\alpha>1
.
If the subsequent Newton method does not achieve the desired tolerance within the given maximum number of iterations, then the time-step is discarded and restarted with a smaller
\tau_{\ell+1}
. This procedure is summarized in Algorithm
3
. We note that, in our experiments, even if
\alpha
is chosen considerably larger than
1
, typically
1.1\div 2
, only few Newton iterations are needed to achieve the desired tolerance and very few restarts occur.

We now show that, for a finite number of restarts, our method achieves super-exponential convergence towards the optimum. Indeed, repeating the argument of the proof of Proposition (
5.5
) taking into account (
6.2
), we can write:

(6.3)

\frac{v^{\prime}}{v}(t)\leq-\frac{A}{B},\;\;\mbox{ for a.e. }t>\alpha^{\bar{\ell}}\tau,

where
v
is an upper bound of the error, i.e.
v(\alpha^{\ell}\tau)\geq\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{*}\|
holds for
\ell>\bar{\ell}
. Then, integrating over
[\alpha^{\bar{\ell}}\tau,\alpha^{\ell}\tau]
, we obtain

\displaystyle v(\alpha^{\ell}\tau)\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{A\tau}{B}(\alpha^{\ell}-\alpha^{\bar{\ell}})\right)v(\alpha^{\bar{\ell}}\tau)=\exp\left(\frac{A\tau}{B}\alpha^{\bar{\ell}}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{A\tau}{B}\alpha^{\ell}\right)v(\alpha^{\bar{\ell}}\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\exp\left(-\frac{A}{B}\alpha^{\ell}\right)v(\alpha^{\bar{\ell}}\tau)\,.

Note that the sequence
\ell\mapsto\exp(-A/B\tau\alpha^{\ell})
converges to
0
with order exactly equal to
\alpha
and unit asymptotic constant.

7.
Numerical experiments

In this section we test our theoretical results by looking at: i) verification of Hypothesis
3.3
; ii) convergence of our discrete gradient flow (Algorithms
2
and
3
), i.e., convergence towards the minimum of
E_{n}
; and iii) convergence of our numerical solution towards the real optimal transport density
\mu^{*}
, as given in Theorem
2.6
. To this aim, in i) we use as optimal point the converged numerical solution for a given tolerance as obtained by our algorithm; in ii) we look at quantities such as differences in the numerical solution at consecutive time steps and residuals of approximate KKT conditions; in iii) we make use of an explicit solution to the OT problem considered in
[
10
]
, which constitutes our test case and is described in Section
7.1
.

We recall that Hypothesis
3.3
implies the uniqueness of
\mu_{n}^{*}
(see Proposition
3.4
) and the exponential convergence of Algorithm
2
(see Proposition
5.5
), and guarantees the well-conditioning of the optimization problem (see Proposition
4.5
). Note that the last proposition provides an a-posteriori error estimate in terms of the gradient of the functional
F_{n}
. Thus the following exit criterion for the gradient flow based on
\|\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{\ell})\|
can be used:

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell-1}\|\leq C\|\nabla F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\ell})\|\leq\tau_{\ell}\mbox{Toll}\,.

Verification of Hypothesis
3.3
is done for different
\delta_{n}
sequences, namely
\delta_{n}\in\{h_{n},h^{2}_{n}\}
.
Convergence of the gradient flow is verified by measuring the
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-increment between time-steps

(7.1)

\Delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ell}:=\|\sigma_{n}^{\ell}-\sigma_{n}^{\ell-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

which, as seen above, is a local estimator of the error. In addition, we control the approximate discrete KKT conditions in (
5.19
) by making sure that they satisfy:

(7.2)

\mbox{KKT}_{\mbox{toll}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\max_{i}\left(\,\left\{\begin{aligned} &\max\left\{0,-\partial_{i}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\right\}&\mbox{ if }{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}<\mbox{toll}\\
&\partial_{i}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}))&\mbox{ if }{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}\geq\mbox{toll}\end{aligned}\right.\;\right),

where
toll
is the tolerance used to distinguish whether
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\{i\}}>0
or not.
Verification of the convergence of the gradient flow and the convergence towards
\mu^{*}
is again performed for the different sequences of the relaxation parameter
\delta_{n}
defined above.
