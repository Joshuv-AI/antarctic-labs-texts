Multilevel Stein variational gradient descent with applications to Bayesian inverse problems
Terrence Alsup, Luca Venturi, Benjamin Peherstorfer

Abstract
This work presents a multilevel variant of Stein variational gradient descent to more efficiently sample from target distributions. The key ingredient is a sequence of distributions with growing fidelity and costs that converges to the target distribution of interest. For example, such a sequence of distributions is given by a hierarchy of ever finer discretization levels of the forward model in Bayesian inverse problems. The proposed multilevel Stein variational gradient descent moves most of the iterations to lower, cheaper levels with the aim of requiring only a few iterations on the higher, more expensive levels when compared to the traditional, single-level Stein variational gradient descent variant that uses the highest-level distribution only. Under certain assumptions, in the mean-field limit, the error of the proposed multilevel Stein method decays by a log factor faster than the error of the single-level counterpart with respect to computational costs. Numerical experiments with Bayesian inverse problems show speedups of more than one order of magnitude of the proposed multilevel Stein method compared to the single-level variant that uses the highest level only.

Monte Carlo, multilevel and multifidelity, particle methods, Bayesian inference

Introduction

Sampling from a target distribution
\pi
is a common task in Bayesian inference. Typically, in machine learning, the (unnormalized) density of the target distribution can be evaluated to approximately sample from it with Monte Carlo, variational, and particle methods
[
39
,
37
,
38
,
43
]
.
We look at a setup that is more common in scientific machine learning and scientific computing, where a sequence of distributions
(\pi^{(\ell)})
is given that converges weakly to a computationally intractable target
\pi
for increasing level
\ell\to\infty
. Here, intractable means that one cannot numerically evaluate the (unnormalized) density of
\pi
.
For example, one finds such a setup in Bayesian inverse problems
[
40
,
24
,
30
]
, where the target
\pi
corresponds to a posterior distribution that depends on a forward model through the likelihood.
The forward model is typically a system of partial differential equations (PDEs)
for which only numerical solutions can be computed; increasingly more accurate, more expensive discretizations (e.g., mesh width going to 0) of the forward-model PDEs then give rise to a sequence of distributions
(\pi^{(\ell)})
that converges to
\pi
.

To approximately sample from the target
\pi
, one then selects a level
L
such that
\pi^{(L)}
is a sufficiently accurate approximation of
\pi
and then applies Monte Carlo or particle methods to
\pi^{(L)}
; see, e.g.,
[
40
,
24
,
30
]
.
One challenge of such an approach is that the density of
\pi^{(L)}
can be computationally expensive to evaluate, because each evaluation of the density entails at least one numerical solve of the PDEs underlying the forward model, which can quickly make sampling from it prohibitively expensive.

Our contributions

We propose to extend Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD)
[
29
]
to a multilevel SVGD (MLSVGD) that leverages the distributions from all levels
\ell=1,\dots,L
to more efficiently approximately sample from
\pi
than traditional, single-level SVGD that uses the distribution
\pi^{(L)}
on the highest level
L
only; thus, the proposed MLSVGD builds on the long history of exploiting hierarchies of discretizations in scientific computing (see below for literature review).
Our contributions are as follows:
(1)
an analysis that shows the cost complexity of the proposed MLSVGD is lower than the cost complexity of single-level SVGD;
(2)
a numerical algorithm that builds on an adaptive stopping criterion that can be applied in a black-box way;
(3)
numerical experiments with Bayesian inverse problems involving nonlinear diffusion-reaction and Euler-Bernoulli beam models that demonstrate that taking into account all levels
\ell=1,\dots,L
can lead to more than one order of magnitude speedup compared to single-level SVGD.

Preliminaries: Approximating measures with SVGD

Let
\Theta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
(\Theta,\mathcal{B}(\Theta))
be a measurable space with
\mathcal{B}(\Theta)
denoting the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\Theta
. Consider approximating some target measure
\eta
on
\Theta
via an empirical measure, i.e., an ensemble of samples (particles); in the following, the distribution
\eta
, and all other distributions that will be considered, admit a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure over
\Theta
. Moreover, the target distribution has the form
\eta\propto e^{-V}
with the potential
V
.

2.1
Approximating measures with SVGD

The SVGD method
[
29
]
iteratively moves forward an empirical distribution given by an ensemble
\{{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from time
t
to time
t+\delta
via a map
{\bm{\phi}}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{\theta}}-\delta{\bm{g}}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})
, where
\delta
is a step size and
{\bm{g}}_{t}:\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a vector field. SVGD chooses
{\bm{g}}_{t}
from a vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}^{d}
with kernel
K:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
via a functional gradient descent step on the KL divergence (cf. (
47
) in Appendix
A
). Denote the distribution of the particles at time
t
as
\mu_{t}
and define the functional
J_{t}({\bm{g}})=\operatorname{KL}((I-{\bm{g}})_{\#}\mu_{t}||\eta)
, where
(I-{\bm{g}})_{\#}\mu_{t}
denotes the pushfoward measure. Then, SVGD chooses the gradient by setting
{\bm{g}}_{t}=\nabla J_{t}(\bm{0})
, where
\bm{0}
is the zero function. Using the RKHS formulation, there is a closed form expression for
\nabla J_{t}({\bm{0}})
, so that during the gradient descent the particles evolve according to the ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\dot{{\bm{\theta}}}^{[i]}_{t}=-\nabla J_{t}({\bm{0}})\left({\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{t}}\left[K({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t})\nabla\log\eta({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})+\nabla_{1}K({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t})\right]\,,

(1)

where
\nabla_{1}
denotes the gradient with respect to the first argument. In practice, the expectation is approximated using the empirical distribution of the ensemble of particles
\{{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the ODE is integrated using the forward Euler method. Thus, the SVGD update becomes

{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t+\delta}={\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t}+\frac{\delta}{N}\left(\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{1}K({\bm{\theta}}^{[j]}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{[i]})+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N}K({\bm{\theta}}^{[j]}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t})\nabla\log\eta({\bm{\theta}}^{[j]}_{t})\right)\,.

(2)

In
[
28
]
, the distribution of the particles
\{{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in the limit as
N\to\infty
is given by the mean-field PDE

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=-\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{t}}\left[K({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}})\nabla\log\eta({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})+\nabla_{1}K({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}})\right]\right)\,,

(3)

with an initial measure
\mu_{0}
; see also
[
7
,
19
]
.
[
28
]
shows that a steady state is reached in the limit
t\to\infty
and the empirical distribution converges weakly (i.e. in distribution) to the target
\eta
.

2.2
Approximating intractable target measures with SVGD

Consider now an intractable target distribution
\pi
; in contrast to the measure
\eta
in Section
2.1
, we can neither evaluate the (unnormalized) density of
\pi
nor sample from
\pi
directly. Thus, the SVGD algorithm cannot directly be applied to
\pi
. Instead, suppose we have a sequence of distributions
(\pi^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 1}
that converges weakly to
\pi
for
\ell\to\infty
(note that weak convergence is implied by convergence in the KL divergence) and call
\ell
the level. Moreover, we can evaluate the unnormalized density of each
\pi^{(\ell)}
with computational costs
c_{\ell}
. Such a setup is common in Bayesian inverse problems; cf. Section
1
.

Single-level approximation with SVGD

The aim is deriving a distribution
\mu
that approximates
\pi
with accuracy
\epsilon
. To quantify how close the approximation
\mu
is to the target distribution, we consider the Hellinger distance
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\cdot,\cdot)
in the following:
First, select a level
L\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\pi^{(L)},\pi)\leq\epsilon/2
. Then, to approximate
\pi^{(L)}
with SVGD, derive
\mu
with accuracy
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu,\pi^{(L)})\leq\epsilon/2
from an initial distribution
\mu_{0}
; the triangle inequality leads to
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu,\pi)\leq\epsilon
. The fact that the Hellinger distance is a metric is important because it allows us to separate the error due to truncating at level
L
and the error due to the SVGD approximation of
\pi^{(L)}
; see Appendix
A
for the definition of the Hellinger distance.

Computational costs

The costs of such an approach depend on two factors: (1) the costs
c_{L}
of evaluating the density
\pi^{(L)}
on level
L
, which is independent of SVGD, and (2) the costs of SVGD to find
\mu
from
\pi^{(L)}
with initial distribution
\mu_{0}
to achieve
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu,\pi^{(L)})\leq\epsilon/2
. In the continuous SVGD given by Equation (
1
), we identify the costs of the approximation
\mu_{T}
after integrating up to end time
T
as

c_{\mathrm{SL}}(T)=c_{L}T\,.

(4)

We will see that the integration time
T
depends on the divergence between
\mu_{0}
and
\pi^{(L)}
. For the discrete SVGD given by Equation (
2
), time is replaced with number of iterations and the costs must be multiplied by the number of particles
N
.

Remark 1
.

Although we use the Hellinger distance
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}
in the following, the proposed analysis is also applicable if a different metric is used as long as it can be upper bounded by the KL divergence; see Section
3.3
for more details. Indeed, we make frequent use of the fact that the Hellinger distance can be bounded as

2\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})^{2}\leq\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{1}\ ||\ \rho_{2})\,

(5)

for two distributions
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
; see Lemma 2.4 of
[
41
]
(note that the definition of Hellinger distance there is scaled by a constant factor
\sqrt{2}
). The Hellinger distance is also useful because it can be used to bound the bias of a Monte Carlo estimator as shown in
[
40
]
.

3.1
Continuous MLSVGD

To describe the proposed MLSVGD, consider the levels
\ell=1,\dots,L
and let
\mu_{0}
be an initial distribution. At level
\ell=1
, we define
\mu_{T_{1}}^{(1)}
as the distribution of the continuous SVGD (
3
) at time
T_{1}
with target
\pi^{(1)}
and initial
\mu_{0}
. At level
\ell=2
, we obtain
\mu_{T_{2}}^{(2)}
at time
T_{2}
with the target
\pi^{(2)}
and initial distribution
\mu_{T_{1}}^{(1)}
. In general, at level
\ell
, we obtain
\mu^{(\ell)}_{T_{\ell}}
at time
T_{\ell}
with target
\pi^{(\ell)}
and initial distribution
\mu_{T_{\ell-1}}^{(\ell-1)}
. Thus, deriving
\mu^{(\ell)}_{T_{\ell}}
is an iterative process over the levels
1,\dots,\ell-1
, depicted in Figure
1
, of first computing
\mu_{T_{1}}^{(1)},\dots,\mu_{T_{\ell-1}}^{(\ell-1)}
. The costs of MLSVGD are given by

c_{\mathrm{ML}}(T_{1},\dots,T_{L})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}c_{\ell}T_{\ell}\,,

(6)

cf. the costs
c_{\mathrm{SL}}(T)=c_{L}T
of the single-level SVGD as defined in (
4
).

3.2
Assumptions for cost complexity analysis of single-level SVGD and MLSVGD

We build on the following three assumptions to derive the cost complexity of both traditional single-level SVGD as well as the proposed MLSVGD. The first assumption is a standard assumption in scientific computing on the cost of evaluating the densities, while the second and third are needed to certify that
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu,\pi)\leq\epsilon
.

Assumption 1
.

The costs
c_{\ell}
of evaluating the (unnormalized) density
\pi^{(\ell)}
are bounded as

c_{\ell}\leq c_{0}s^{\gamma\ell}\,,\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}\,,

with constants
c_{0},\gamma>0
independent of
\ell
and
s>1
.

Assumption 2
.

There exists
\alpha,k_{0},k_{1}>0
independent of
\ell
such that
\operatorname{KL}(\mu_{0}||\pi^{(\ell)})\leq k_{0}
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and

\operatorname{KL}(\pi^{(\ell)}||\pi)\leq k_{1}s^{-\alpha\ell}\,,\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}\,,

where
s
is the same constant independent of
\ell
as in Assumption
1
and
\mu_{0}
is the initial distribution.

Assumption 3
.

There exists a rate
\lambda>0
such that for any initial distribution
\nu_{0}

\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{t}||\pi^{(\ell)})\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{0}||\pi^{(\ell)})\,,\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}\,,

holds, where
\nu_{t}
solves the mean-field SVGD equation (
3
) at time
t
.

[
25
]
show that Assumption
3
is satisfied if the measures
\pi^{(\ell)}
satisfy a Stein log-Sobolev inequality.
[
7
]
also show that Assumption
3
is satisfied for a specific choice of the kernel
K
. We also note that the exponential convergence rate for the KL divergence appears in the theory for the convergence of Markov processes when the target measure satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality
[
2
, Theorem 5.2.1]
; however, SVGD approximates the gradient in an RKHS and thus
[
2
, Theorem 5.2.1]
is not directly applicable.

3.3
Cost complexity of continuous single-level SVGD

Consider the single-level SVGD that selects
L
such that
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\pi^{(L)},\pi)\leq\epsilon/2
and then starts with a
\mu_{0}
to find
\mu^{\text{SL}}_{T}
that satisfies
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu_{T}^{\text{SL}},\pi^{(L)})\leq\epsilon/2
. For brevity, we write
\mu^{\text{SL}}=\mu^{\text{SL}}_{T}
. The following proposition bounds the costs of this single-level SVGD with respect to the tolerance
\epsilon
.

Proposition 1
.

If Assumptions
1
–
3
hold, then the costs of continuous single-level SVGD to obtain
\mu^{\text{SL}}
with

\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu^{\mathrm{SL}},\pi)\leq\epsilon

is bounded as

c_{\mathrm{SL}}^{*}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{2c_{0}s^{\gamma}}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2k_{1}}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\gamma/\alpha}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{KL}(\mu_{0}\ ||\ \pi^{(L)})}}{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}\right)\,.

(7)

Proof.

By the triangle inequality for the Hellinger distance we have that

\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu^{\text{SL}},\pi)\leq\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu^{\text{SL}},\pi^{(L)})+\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\pi^{(L)},\pi),

so we will bound both of these terms independently by
\epsilon/2
. By inequality (
5
), it is sufficient to bound the KL divergence because

\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu^{\text{SL}},\pi^{(L)})\leq\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\mu^{\text{SL}}\ ||\ \pi^{(L)})}{2}},

(8)

and similarly for
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\pi^{(L)},\pi)
. By Assumption
2
choose
L
to be

L=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\alpha}\log_{s}\left(\frac{2k_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\right\rceil\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\log_{s}\left(\frac{2k_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)+1,

(9)

so that

\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\pi^{(L)},\pi)\leq\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\pi^{(L)}\ ||\ \pi)}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{k_{1}s^{-\alpha L}}{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

(10)

Now by Assumptions
3
the time needed to integrate with SVGD to achieve
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu^{\text{SL}},\pi^{(L)})\leq\epsilon/2
is

T^{*}_{\text{SL}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left(\frac{\text{KL}(\mu_{0}\ ||\ \pi^{(L)})}{2\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(11)

The total cost to integrate until time
T^{*}_{\text{SL}}
at level
L
is thus

c_{\mathrm{SL}}^{*}(\epsilon)=c_{0}s^{\gamma L}T_{\mathrm{SL}}^{*}\leq\frac{2c_{0}s^{\gamma}}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2k_{1}}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\gamma/\alpha}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\text{KL}(\mu_{0}\ ||\ \pi^{(L)})}}{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}\right)\,.

∎

Discussion of cost complexity of single-level SVGD

The bound (
7
) in Proposition
7
shows that if we start with an initial distribution
\mu_{0}
that has a large
\operatorname{KL}
divergence
\operatorname{KL}(\mu_{0}||\pi^{(L)})
with respect to
\pi^{(L)}
, then we will need to integrate for a long time with SVGD to reach our tolerance. The proposed MLSVGD is aiming to avoid the long time integration by starting the integration at the highest level
L
with good initial distributions found on the cheaper, lower levels
\ell=1,\dots,L-1
that are closer to
\pi^{(L)}
in the KL divergence than
\mu_{0}
.

3.4
Cost complexity of continuous MLSVGD

Consider now the MLSVGD approach of Section
3.1
. We need to make one additional assumption compared to the single-level SVGD regarding the KL divergence between consecutive measures
\pi^{(\ell)}
and
\pi^{(\ell-1)}
that will allow us to chain them together as in Figure
1
.

Assumption 4
.

There exists a constant
k_{2}>0
independent of
\ell
such that
\operatorname{KL}(\pi^{(\ell-1)}||\pi^{(\ell)})\leq k_{2}s^{-\alpha\ell}
, where
\alpha
is the same rate as in Assumption
2
.

The key result is to use a triangle-
like
inequality as in Appendix
D
to decompose the KL divergence. In particular,

\operatorname{KL}\left(\mu^{(\ell-1)}_{T_{\ell-1}}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)}\right)=\operatorname{KL}\left(\mu^{(\ell-1)}_{T_{\ell-1}}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell-1)}\right)+\operatorname{KL}\left(\pi^{(\ell-1)}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)}\right)+R_{\ell}

(12)

with the remainder
R_{\ell}
given by

R_{\ell}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\mu^{(\ell-1)}_{T_{\ell-1}}({\bm{\theta}})-\pi^{(\ell-1)}({\bm{\theta}})\right)\log\left(\frac{\pi^{(\ell-1)}({\bm{\theta}})}{\pi^{(\ell)}({\bm{\theta}})}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}.

(13)

Because
\pi^{(\ell)}
converges to
\pi
, we have that
\pi^{(\ell-1)}/\pi^{(\ell)}\to 1
pointwise and hence
\log\left(\pi^{(\ell-1)}/{\pi^{(\ell)}}\right)\to 0
. Moreover,
\mu^{(\ell)}_{T_{\ell}}\to\pi^{(\ell)}
as
T_{\ell}\to\infty
. Thus,
R_{\ell}\to 0
. In particular
R_{\ell}
is a bounded sequence meaning that there is some constant
R\geq R_{\ell}
for all
\ell
.
The following proposition give bounds on the costs of MLSVGD. The later Proposition
24
will give a faster decaying bound on the costs if
R_{\ell}
goes to zero with a known rate, as in our Bayesian inverse problems in Section
4
.

Proposition 2
.

If Assumptions
1
–
4
hold, then continuous MLSVGD gives
\mu^{\text{ML}}
with
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu^{\text{ML}},\pi)\leq\epsilon
with costs bounded as

c_{\mathrm{ML}}^{*}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{2c_{0}s^{2\gamma}}{\lambda\gamma\log(s)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2k_{1}}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\gamma/\alpha}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}+2(k_{2}+R)}}{\epsilon}\right)\,,

where
R
bounds (
13
).

Proof.

As in Equation (
9
) in the proof of Proposition
7
we select the level
L
as

L=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\alpha}\log_{s}\left(\frac{2k_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\right\rceil\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\log_{s}\left(\frac{2k_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)+1,

(14)

so that
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\pi^{(L)},\pi)\leq\epsilon/2
. The total cost for the continuous MLSVGD is

c_{\mathrm{ML}}^{*}(\epsilon)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}c_{0}s^{\gamma\ell}T_{\ell}\,,

(15)

where it remains to choose the integration times
T_{\ell}
at each level. To do this we balance the KL divergence of the SVGD approximation with the KL divergence due to the fidelity. By Equation (
12
), we have

\operatorname{KL}(\mu^{(\ell)}_{T_{\ell}}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)})\leq e^{-\lambda T_{\ell}}\left(\operatorname{KL}\left(\mu^{(\ell-1)}_{T_{\ell-1}}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell-1)}\right)+\operatorname{KL}\left(\pi^{(\ell-1)}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)}\right)+R_{\ell}\right)\,,

(16)

giving a recursive bound on the KL divergence in terms of the KL divergence at the previous level. At each level
\ell
choose the integration time
T_{\ell}
so that

\operatorname{KL}\left(\mu^{(\ell)}_{T_{\ell}}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)}\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}

(17)

is satisfied. In particular, at the final level
L
we will have that
\operatorname{KL}(\mu^{\text{ML}}\ ||\ \pi^{(L)})\leq\epsilon^{2}/2
and hence
\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu^{\text{ML}}\ ||\ \pi^{(L)})\leq\epsilon/2
as desired. By choosing
T_{\ell}
so that this is satisfied at every level we have from Equation (
16
) that

\operatorname{KL}\left(\mu^{(\ell)}_{T_{\ell}}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)}\right)\leq e^{-\lambda T_{\ell}}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}+\operatorname{KL}(\pi^{(\ell-1)}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)})+R_{\ell}\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\,.

(18)

Thus, we choose
T_{\ell}
sequentially so that Equation (
17
) is always satisfied. As a result, the integration time
T_{\ell}
needed at each level
\ell
is bounded by

T_{\ell}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left(1+\frac{2(\operatorname{KL}(\pi^{(\ell-1)}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)})+R_{\ell})}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\,.

(19)

Finally, the total cost can be bounded by

c_{\mathrm{ML}}^{*}(\epsilon)\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda}s^{\gamma\ell}\log\left(1+\frac{2(\operatorname{KL}(\pi^{(\ell-1)}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)})+R_{\ell})}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\,.

(20)

We now use the fact that
R_{\ell}\leq R
and
\operatorname{KL}(\pi^{(\ell-1)}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)})\leq k_{2}
to obtain

c_{\mathrm{ML}}^{*}(\epsilon)\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda}s^{\gamma\ell}\log\left(1+\frac{2(k_{2}+R)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\,.

(21)

Since the terms in this sum are increasing, we can upper bound the cost further by switching to an integral

\begin{split}c_{\mathrm{ML}}^{*}(\epsilon)&\leq\int_{0}^{L+1}\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda}s^{\gamma x}\log\left(1+\frac{2(k_{2}+R)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\ \mathrm{d}x\\
&=\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda\gamma\log(s)}\log\left(1+\frac{2(k_{2}+R)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)s^{\gamma(L+1)}\\
&\leq\frac{2c_{0}s^{2\gamma}}{\lambda\gamma\log(s)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2k_{1}}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\gamma/\alpha}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}+2(k_{2}+R)}}{\epsilon}\right)\,.\end{split}

(22)

∎

We now consider the case where the remainder term behaves as
R_{\ell}\lesssim s^{-\alpha\ell}
, which allows us to make a more efficient choice when selecting the integration time
T_{\ell}
at each level. In particular, it allows us to set
T_{\ell}
such that

\operatorname{KL}(\mu^{(\ell)}_{T_{\ell}}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)})\sim s^{-\alpha\ell}\,,

(23)

which leads to the following proposition that shows an improved cost complexity compared to Proposition
2
.

Proposition 3
.

If Assumptions
1
–
4
hold and
R_{\ell}\leq k_{3}s^{-\alpha\ell}
, then the costs of continuous MLSVGD to have

\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu^{\mathrm{ML}},\ \pi)\leq\epsilon
can be bounded as

c_{\mathrm{ML}}^{*}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{c_{0}s^{2\gamma}}{\lambda\gamma\log(s)}\log\left(s^{\alpha}+\frac{k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{2k_{1}}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\gamma/\alpha}\,.

(24)

Proof.

Starting from Equation (
16
) in the proof of Proposition
2
change
\epsilon
to instead be

\epsilon_{\ell}=\sqrt{2k_{1}}s^{-\alpha\ell/2}\,

(25)

at each level
\ell
. By Assumption
2
we know that
L
is chosen so that

\epsilon_{L}^{2}=2k_{1}s^{-\alpha L}\leq\epsilon^{2}\,,

(26)

so that
\epsilon_{L}\leq\epsilon
. Plugging in this choice gives that the integration times needed are

T_{\ell}^{*}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left(s^{\alpha}+\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\pi^{(\ell-1)}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell)})+R_{\ell}}{k_{1}s^{-\alpha\ell}}\right)\,.

(27)

By Assumption
4
and the assumption in the proposition, we have that

T_{\ell}^{*}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left(s^{\alpha}+\frac{k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1}}\right)\,,

(28)

so that the integration time is fixed at each level. The cost is now bounded by

(29)

Since the terms in the sum are increasing, we can further bound this with an integral :

c_{\mathrm{ML}}^{*}(\epsilon)\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda}s^{\gamma\ell}\log\left(s^{\alpha}+\frac{k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1}}\right)\leq\int_{1}^{L+1}\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda}s^{\gamma x}\log\left(s^{\alpha}+\frac{k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1}}\right)\ \mathrm{d}x\,.

(31)

Finally, by plugging in

we obtain

c_{\mathrm{ML}}^{*}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{c_{0}s^{2\gamma}}{\lambda\gamma\log(s)}\log\left(s^{\alpha}+\frac{k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{2k_{1}}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\gamma/\alpha}\,.

(32)

∎

Discussion of cost complexity of MLSVGD

Looking at the single-level SVGD and MLSVGD cost bounds from Propositions
7
and
24
, respectively, we note two major differences. The first is that there is no
\log\epsilon^{-1}
term in the cost bound (
24
) of Proposition
24
and thus MLSVGD achieves a cost complexity that grows by
\log\epsilon^{-1}
slower than the cost complexity of single-level SVGD as
\epsilon\to 0
. Moreover, whenever
\epsilon\to 0
, we now have a fixed integration time at each level
\ell
as opposed to requiring an increasing number of iterations as the level goes to infinity as in the single-level case. The second notable difference is that the constant
k_{0}
, which depends on the KL divergence from the initial distribution
\mu_{0}
and the target
\pi
, does not appear in (
24
). Instead the bound (
24
) depends on the constant
k_{2}
from Assumption
4
, which depends only on the KL divergence between two consecutive levels. Thus, if the KL divergence between consecutive levels is low, then the previous level serves as a good preconditioner for the next level leading to reduced costs.

Remark 2
.

The order
\log\epsilon^{-1}
comes from the exponential decay rate of the KL divergence for SVGD in Assumption
3
. If the assumption is violated and, for example, the KL divergence decays only algebraically, then we expect the speedup to be on the order of
\epsilon^{-\beta}
for some constant
\beta>0
. This is further supported by our numerical results that indicate that MLSVGD obtains speedups even if SVGD converges slower than in Assumption
3
. We leave the detailed analysis of this to future work.

MLSVGD for Bayesian inverse problems

Typically, in Bayesian inverse problems in scientific computing, one is interested in inferring an unknown quantity
{\bm{\theta}}
from some noisy observed data
{\mathbf{y}}=G({\bm{\theta}}^{*})+{\bm{e}}
with

denoting the parameter-to-observable map and
{\bm{e}}
being the noise; see, e.g.,
[

be the prior and consider zero-mean Gaussian noise with covariance
{\bm{\Gamma}}

(33)

with the normalizing constant

(34)

where
\|{\bm{u}}\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}=\langle{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\bm{u}},{\bm{u}}\rangle
. Now let
(G_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
denote a sequence of approximations to the parameter-to-observable map

, and define

by replacing

with

and define

similarly. The next two assumptions will be sufficient to apply our results from Section

.

Assumption 5

(Model error)
.

There is a function

\psi(\ell)\to 0
as

where the

is the
L^{2}
norm over
\pi_{0}
; cf. (
49
) in Appendix

.

Assumption 6
.

There exists a constant
b_{3}>0
independent of
\ell
such that

(36)

for all

The next theorem shows that if Assumptions

,
3
,
5
, and
6
are satisfied, then our cost complexity results derived for MLSVGD in Section

hold in the Bayesian inverse problem setting. These assumptions can be interpreted in the context of Bayesian inverse problems as follows: Assumption

and Assumption

are related to the forward model. Together they state that the approximation

converges in an

as the level

is increased and

gets closer to

. This is typical behavior in, e.g., finite-element forward models where refining the mesh (increasing the level) leads to more accurate approximations and at the same time the computational costs of computing the finite-element solution increase with the number of mesh points. Furthermore, Assumption

Inputs:
(unnormalized) densities
\pi^{(1)},\dots,\pi^{(L)}
, initial particles
\{{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{0}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, step size
\delta
, tolerance
\epsilon
;

\{{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t}\}_{i=1}^{N}

for

;

Algorithm 1

Discrete MLSVGD with adaptive stopping criterion

In this section, we propose a discrete, heuristic MLSVGD method given in Algorithm
1
that uses an adaptive stopping criterion to decide when to switch to the next higher level. The proposed Algorithm
1
uses the estimates of the gradient norms to decide when to switch to the next higher level.
Thus, the algorithm avoids requiring any constants that are not readily available in practice. In particular, the algorithm is independent of the constants and rates used in the MLSVGD cost complexity analysis to derive the optimal choice of times
T_{1}^{*},\dots,T_{L}^{*}
.

Let
{\bm{g}}^{(\ell)}_{t}
denote the functional gradient of the KL divergence, as discussed in Section
2.1
, at
\mu^{(\ell)}_{t}
with target measure
\pi^{(\ell)}
. We approximate the expected norm of the gradient
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim\mu^{(\ell)}_{t}}\left\|{\bm{g}}^{(\ell)}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\right\|
with the estimator

\hat{{\bm{g}}}^{(\ell)}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{1}K({\bm{\theta}}^{[j]}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{[i]})+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N}K({\bm{\theta}}^{[j]}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}^{[i]}_{t})\nabla\log\pi^{(\ell)}({\bm{\theta}}^{[j]}_{t})\right\|\,,

(43)

where we note that each term in the sum is computed during the update (
2
). The adaptive stopping criteria used in Algorithm
1
is to terminate the iterations at level
\ell
whenever
\hat{{\bm{g}}}^{(\ell)}_{t}\leq\epsilon
.
Ideally, one would want to track the KL divergence between the SVGD approximation and the target distribution and switch to the following level once the KL divergence is below some specified threshold. However, because the normalized target density as well as the density of the SVGD approximation itself are unknown, attempting to monitor the KL divergence at each iteration is impractical.
The adaptive stopping criteria based on the gradient norm, which we use, is motivated by
[
12
, Equation 61]
. It states that for small perturbations from the target density, the KL divergence between the perturbed distribution and the target distribution is asymptotically the same as the norm of the gradient squared.

7
Conclusions

The proposed MLSVGD shows that speedups compared to single-level SVGD can be achieved by balancing the SVGD error with the discretization error given by a hierarchy of ever more accurate and ever more expensive-to-sample distributions. The analysis is conducted in the mean-field limit and shows a cost complexity reduction of MLSVGD compared to single-level SVGD. The numerical experiments demonstrate empirically that MLSVGD achieves up to one order of magnitude speedup compared to single-level SVGD in the discrete-time and finite-particle regime in the applications considered in this work. A cost analysis in discrete time and with finite particles remains future work for MLSVGD especially because there are only limited convergence results available even for single-level SVGD for discrete-time and finite-particle regimes.

Appendix A
Metrics and divergences and other definitions

The Hellinger distance between two probability distributions
\mu
and
\eta
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is defined as

\operatorname{d}_{\mathrm{Hell}}(\mu,\eta)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\sqrt{\mu({\bm{\theta}})}-\sqrt{\eta({\bm{\theta}})}\right)^{2}\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}}\,.

(46)

The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence from
\mu
to
\eta
is defined as

\operatorname{KL}(\mu||\eta)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mu({\bm{\theta}})\log\left(\frac{\mu({\bm{\theta}})}{\eta({\bm{\theta}})}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\,.

(47)

Define the
L^{2}(\mu)
space for a distribution
\mu
and vector-valued functions as

L^{2}(\mu)=\left\{f:\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|f({\bm{\theta}})\|^{2}\mu({\bm{\theta}})\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}<\infty\right\}\,,

(48)

and the
L^{2}(\mu)
norm of a vector-valued function as

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|f({\bm{\theta}})\|^{2}\mu({\bm{\theta}})\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\,.

(49)

Appendix B
Lemma
1
and proof

Lemma 1
.

If Assumption
5
holds, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
1\leq\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\leq\infty
sufficiently large

\text{\emph{KL}}(\pi^{(\ell_{1})}\ ||\ \pi^{(\ell_{2})})\leq C\|G_{\ell_{1}}-G_{\ell_{2}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\,.

(50)

Note that for
\ell=\infty
we say
G_{\ell}=G
.

We note that this proof closely mirrors the proofs of Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3 in
[
31
]
, but is slightly more general.

Proof.

For brevity write
G_{i}=G_{\ell_{i}}
,
Z_{i}=Z_{\ell_{i}}
, and
\pi_{i}=\pi^{(\ell_{i})}
for
i=1,2
. Consider that for any vectors
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and symmetric positive definite matrix
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
we have

\begin{split}\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{w}}\|_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{2}-\|{\bm{v}}-{\bm{w}}\|_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{2}&=\|({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}})+({\bm{v}}-{\bm{w}})\|_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{2}-\|{\bm{v}}-{\bm{w}}\|_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{2}\\
&=\langle({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}),\ {\mathbf{A}}({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}})\rangle+2\langle({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}),\ {\mathbf{A}}({\bm{v}}-{\bm{w}})\rangle\\
&=\langle({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}),\ {\mathbf{A}}({\bm{u}}+{\bm{v}}-2{\bm{w}})\rangle\\
&\leq\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}\|\cdot\|{\mathbf{A}}({\bm{u}}+{\bm{v}}-2{\bm{w}})\|\,,\end{split}

(51)

with the last line following from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Applying this bound with
{\bm{u}}=G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})
,
{\bm{v}}=G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})
,
{\bm{w}}={\mathbf{y}}
, and
{\mathbf{A}}={\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}
gives

\begin{split}&\int_{\Theta}\left|\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}-\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right|\pi_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&\leq\int_{\Theta}\|G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})\|\cdot\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}(2{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}}))\|\pi_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&\leq\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\cdot\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}(2{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}-G_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\,,\end{split}

(52)

where the last line again follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the inner-product space
L^{2}(\pi_{0})
. The KL divergence can now be bounded using Equation (
52
)

\begin{split}\text{KL}(\pi_{1}\ ||\ \pi_{2})&=\int_{\Theta}\pi_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\log\left(\frac{\pi_{1}({\bm{\theta}})}{\pi_{2}({\bm{\theta}})}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&=\int_{\Theta}\pi_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\log\left(\frac{Z_{2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)}{Z_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&=\log\left(\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\right)+\int_{\Theta}\pi_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\log\left(\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&\leq\log\left(\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\right)+\frac{1}{2Z_{1}}\int_{\Theta}\left|\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}-\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right|\pi_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&\leq\left|\log\left(\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\right)\right|+\frac{1}{2Z_{1}}\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\cdot\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}(2{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}-G_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\,,\end{split}

(53)

where in the second-to-last line we used the fact that
\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\geq 0
and hence

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)\leq 1\,.

(54)

We bound the logarithm of the ratio of the normalizing constants by first bounding the difference of the normalizing constants using the bound in Equation (
52
)

\begin{split}|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|&=\left|\int_{\Theta}\left\{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)\right\}\pi_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\right|\\
&\leq\int_{\Theta}\left|\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)\right|\pi_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Theta}\left|\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}-\|{\mathbf{y}}-G_{2}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}}^{2}\right|\pi_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\cdot\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}(2{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}-G_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\,.\end{split}

(55)

The third line follows from the fact that
|e^{-x}-e^{-y}|\leq|x-y|
for all
x,y\geq 0
. Let
\gamma_{\min}>0
denote the smallest eigenvalue of the noise covariance matrix
{\bm{\Gamma}}
. By the triangle inequality

\begin{split}\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}(2{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}-G_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}&\leq 2\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}+\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}(G_{1}+G_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\\
&\leq 2\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}+2\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}G\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}+\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}(G_{1}+G_{2}-2G)\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\\
&\leq 2\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}+2\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}G\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}}\|G_{1}-G\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}}\|G_{2}-G\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\,.\end{split}

(56)

Since
\|G_{\ell}-G\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\to 0
by Assumption
5
, we can bound
\|G_{1}-G\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}
and
\|G_{2}-G\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}
independently of
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
. Therefore, there exists a constant
b_{1}>0
independent of
\ell
such that

\|{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}(2{\mathbf{y}}-G_{1}-G_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\leq b_{1}.

(57)

Combining Equations (
55
) and (
57
) yields

|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|\leq\frac{b_{1}}{2}\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\,.

(58)

The ratio of the normalizing constants can be written

\left|\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}-1\right|=\frac{1}{Z_{1}}\left|Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right|\,,

(59)

so the logarithm can be bounded as

\left|\log\left(\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\right)\right|\leq\max\left\{\left|\log\left(1-\frac{|Z_{2}-Z_{1}|}{Z_{1}}\right)\right|,\ \log\left(1+\frac{|Z_{2}-Z_{1}|}{Z_{1}}\right)\right\}

(60)

since
x\mapsto|\log x|
is decreasing on
(0,1]
and increasing on
[1,\infty)
. Combining this with the inequality that
\frac{x}{1+x}\leq\log(1+x)\leq x
for all
x>-1
gives

\begin{split}\left|\log\left(\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\right)\right|&\leq\max\left\{\frac{\frac{|Z_{2}-Z_{1}|}{Z_{1}}}{1-\frac{|Z_{2}-Z_{1}|}{Z_{1}}},\ \frac{|Z_{2}-Z_{1}|}{Z_{1}}\right\}\leq\frac{|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|}{Z_{1}-|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|}\,.\end{split}

(61)

Since
Z_{\ell}\to Z\in(0,\infty)
is a convergent sequence, there exists a constant
b_{2}>0
such that

Z_{1}^{-1}\leq\sup_{\ell\geq 1}Z_{\ell}^{-1}\leq b_{2}\,.

(62)

Moreover, for all
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
sufficiently large
|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|\leq b_{2}^{-1}/2
. Using the bound gives

\left|\log\left(\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|}{b_{2}^{-1}-|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|}\leq 2b_{2}|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|\,.

(63)

Combining Equations (
53
), (
57
), (
58
), (
62
), and (
63
) gives

\text{KL}(\pi_{1}\ ||\ \pi_{2})\leq\frac{3}{2}b_{1}b_{2}\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\,.

(64)

Now set
C=\frac{3}{2}b_{1}b_{2}
to obtain the result.
∎

Appendix D
A triangle-like inequality for the KL divergence

Let
\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
be three probability distributions on
\Theta
. We have that

\begin{split}\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{0}\ ||\ \rho_{2})&=\int_{\Theta}\rho_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\log\left(\frac{\rho_{0}({\bm{\theta}})}{\rho_{2}({\bm{\theta}})}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&=\int_{\Theta}\rho_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\log\left(\frac{\rho_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\rho_{1}({\bm{\theta}})}{\rho_{1}({\bm{\theta}})\rho_{2}({\bm{\theta}})}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&=\int_{\Theta}\rho_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\log\left(\frac{\rho_{0}({\bm{\theta}})}{\rho_{1}({\bm{\theta}})}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}+\int_{\Theta}\rho_{0}({\bm{\theta}})\log\left(\frac{\rho_{1}({\bm{\theta}})}{\rho_{2}({\bm{\theta}})}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\\
&=\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{0}\ ||\ \rho_{1})+\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{1}\ ||\ \rho_{2})+\int_{\Theta}(\rho_{0}({\bm{\theta}})-\rho_{1}({\bm{\theta}}))\log\left(\frac{\rho_{1}({\bm{\theta}})}{\rho_{2}({\bm{\theta}})}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}\,,\end{split}

(68)

cf. the inequality given in
[
31
]
. We refer to this third term in the last line as the remainder term.
