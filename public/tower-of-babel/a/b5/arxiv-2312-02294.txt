Boundary Exponential Stabilization for the Linear KP-II equation without Critical Size Restrictions
F.A Gallego, J.R Muñoz

Abstract
In this paper, we delve into the intricacies of boundary stabilization for the linearized KP-II equation within the constraints of a bounded domain, a phenomenon known as “critical length.” Our primary aim is to design a feedback law that ensures the existence and exponential stabilization of solutions in the energy space, without length restrictions on the domain Ω = ( 0 , L ) × ( 0 , L ) \Omega=(0,L)\times(0,L) , L > 0 L>0 . Furthermore, we examine the interaction between the drift term u x u_{x} under these constraints.

Introduction

The KP equation, short for the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili equation, holds great significance in the realm of partial differential equations. It emerges prominently when investigating nonlinear waves and solitons, with applications spanning water wave dynamics, plasma physics, and various theoretical physics and mathematical disciplines. This equation, independently discovered by B.B. Kadomtsev and V.I. Petviashvili in 1970 (see
kp70
), takes center stage in the study of weakly nonlinear, long waves within dispersive media. Its general form is typically expressed as follows:

u_{t}+u_{x}+uu_{x}+u_{xxx}+\gamma\partial_{x}^{-1}(u_{yy})=0,\quad\gamma=\pm 1.

When
\gamma=-1
, we denote it as KP-I, and when
\gamma=1
, it’s KP-II, inspired by the KP equation’s convention. KP-II describes long gravity waves with subtle surface tension, while KP-I models capillary gravity waves, especially with strong surface tension. Notably, the KP-I equation also arises in the transonic limit (under appropriate scaling) of the defocusing nonlinear Schrodinger equation, as documented in
jones
.

In this paper, we explore boundary stabilization of the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equation, focusing on control strategies and dissipative boundary conditions to ensure stability near domain boundaries. This is crucial in applications like fluid dynamics to prevent wave or soliton instability. Researchers use energy methods, feedback control, and simulations to achieve global solution existence and decay. The integrable nature of the KP equation adds to its intrigue, making it a challenging problem in partial differential equations and mathematical physics.

1.2
State of the Art

Significant advancements in control theory have focused on understanding how damping mechanisms impact energy dynamics in systems governed by partial differential equations, particularly in dispersive equations related to water waves in bounded domains. Well-established results demonstrate the exponential stability of equations like KdV
Zuazua2002
, Boussinesq-KdV
Pazoto2008
, and Kawahara
Araruna2012

Lions1988

Cerpa2021

employ Urquiza and Backstepping approaches. These results utilize damping mechanisms either within the equations or at the boundaries to achieve control.

In two noteworthy studies,
ailton2021
and
Panthee2011
, exponential decay was observed in the KP-II and K-KP-II equations. These investigations established equation regularity and well-posedness while showing that introducing a suitable damping term (
a(x,y)u(x,y,t)
) leads to exponential energy decay. Notably, the first exploration of stabilization techniques for two-dimensional nonlinear dispersive equations in bounded domains, following the methodology presented here, is found in
Panthee2011
, which specifically examines the KP-II equation. In
Panthee2011
, external damping defined by a non-negative function
a(x,y)
results in exponential energy decay, even when
a
vanishes on a compact set
\Theta\subset(\delta,L-\delta)\times(\delta,L-\delta)
for some
\delta>0
. The proof relies on an observation inequality for the KP-II equation, established through the unique continuation principle in
panthee
and energy estimates using multiplier techniques. In contrast,
ailton2021
employs the classical dissipation-observability method and Bourgain’s unique continuation principle
bourgain

Capistrano2023
, the authors investigate the K-KP-II system with both a damping mechanism

acting as a feedback-damping mechanism, as seen in

To the best of our knowledge, this current study represents the inaugural achievement in boundary feedback stabilization pertaining to the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili II equation within a rectangular domain

, with any restriction in

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(T-t)u(t)u_{t}(t)\,dx\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle=-\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}\,dx\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(t)\,dx\,dy\,dt,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(T-t)u(t)u_{x}(t)\,dx\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle=0

and the another terms are given by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(T-t)u(t)u_{xxx}(t)\,dx\,dy\,dt=\frac{(1-\alpha^{2})}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(T-t)u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt

On the other hand, note that calling
f_{x}(x,y)=u_{y}(x,y)
with
f(L,y)=0
and from (
6
), it follows that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(T-t)u(t){\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{yy}(t)\,dx\,dydt=\beta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(T-t)u^{2}(x,L,t)dxdt\\
+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(T-t)({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t))^{2}dydt.

Now, adding the above estimates, we deduce (
10
).
∎

3
Exponential Stabilization - Compactness Uniqueness approach

In this section, we study the long-term behavior of solutions to (
1
). Our goal is to demonstrate that the energy associated with the linear KP equation decays exponentially.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By leveraging energy dissipation, specifically the estimate (
8
), in conjunction with a classical argument, our task simplifies to proving the
observability inequality
, that is, there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u^{2}(x,L,t)\,dx\,dt\right.

(11)

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t))^{2}\,dy\,dt\right]

where
u
denotes the solution of (
1
). Indeed, suppose that (
11
) is verified and, as the energy is non-increasing, we have, thanks to (
8
), that

\displaystyle E(T)-E(0)

\displaystyle\leq-C\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u^{2}(x,L)\,dx\,dt\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t))^{2}\,dy\,dt\right]\leq-CE(0)

\displaystyle\leq-CE(T)

which implies that

E(T)\leq\gamma E(0),\text{ with }\gamma=\frac{C}{1+C}<1

The same argument used on the interval
[(m-1)T,mT]
for
m=1,2,\ldots
, yields that

E(mT)\leq\gamma E((m-1)T)\leq\cdots\leq\gamma^{m}E(0)

Thus, we have

E(mT)\leq e^{-\theta mT}E(0)\quad\text{{ with}}\quad\theta=\frac{1}{T}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{C}\right)>0.

For an arbitrary positive
t
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
(m-1)T<t\leq mT
, and by the non-increasing property of the energy, we conclude that

E(t)\leq E((m-1)T)\leq e^{-\theta(m-1)T}E(0)\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}e^{-\theta t}E(0).

By the density of
D(A)
in
\mathcal{H}
, we deduce that the exponential decay of the energy
E
holds for any initial data in
\mathcal{H}
, showing so Theorem (
1.2
).

∎

Let us now prove the inequality (
11
).

Proof of Observability Inequality (
11
).

To establish the observability inequality (
11
), we approach it in three distinct steps:

STEP 1. (Compactness-Uniqueness Argument)

We argue by contradiction, applying the compactness uniqueness
argument due to E. Zuazua (see
Lions1
). If (
11
) is false, then we may find a sequence
\{u^{n}_{0}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

in
\mathcal{H}
such that
\|u_{0}^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
and

\displaystyle\|u_{0}^{n}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\geq n\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(u^{n}_{x})^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(u^{n})^{2}(x,L,t)\,dx\,dt\right.

(12)

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u^{n}_{y}(0,y,t))^{2}\,dy\,dt\right].

On the other hand, it follows from (
9
) that
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=\{{S}(\cdot)u_{0}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})
. By (
1
),
\{u^{n}_{t}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-2}(\Omega))
, Indeed, by (
1
),

u_{t}^{n}=-u_{x}^{n}-u_{xxx}^{n}-\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}^{n}.

As
\left\{u^{n}\right\}_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega))
implies that
\left\{u_{xxx}^{n}\right\}_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-2}(\Omega))
.
Define
f
such that

u_{y}^{n}=f_{x}^{n}
which implies
\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}^{n}=f_{y}^{n}.

Therefore, by using the anisotropic Gagliardo-Niremberg inequality (Theorem
1.1
), it yields that

\lVert\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}^{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=\lVert f_{y}^{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C\lVert f_{x}^{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=C\lVert u_{y}^{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C^{2}\|u_{x}^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty.

Recognizing
\mathcal{H}
as the pivot space such that
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{H}\subset H^{-1}(\Omega)
, we select
\xi
with sufficient regularity to ensure that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\left\langle\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}^{n},\xi\right\rangle_{H^{-1},H_{0}^{1}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left\langle f_{y}^{n},\xi\right\rangle_{H^{-1},H_{0}^{1}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\left\langle\xi,f_{y}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\xi\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\lVert f_{y}^{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C\lVert\xi\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\lVert f_{x}^{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=C\lVert\xi\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\lVert u_{y}^{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}.

Then,

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}^{n}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\,dt\leq C^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert u_{y}^{n}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\lVert u^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega))}.

Hence, applying Aubin’s lemma, we see that a subsequence of
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, again denoted by
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, converges strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})
towards some
u
. Moreover, using (
10
) and (
12
), we see that
\{u_{0}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{H}
, hence converges for some
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
. It means that

u_{0}^{n}\rightarrow u_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}
.
Clearly,
u={S}(\cdot)u_{0}
, and we infer from (
12
) that

u_{x}(0,y,t)=u(x,L,t)=\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(0,y,t)=0\quad\text{for all $x,y\in[0,L]$}

(13)

and that
\|u_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
.

STEP 2. (Reduction to a Spectral Problem)

We reduce the proof of the observability inequality into a spectral problem as done in
Rosier
for the Korteweg-de
Vries equation

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
T>0
, let
N_{T}
denote the space of all the initial states,
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
for which the solution
u(t)=S(t)u_{0}
of (
1
) satisfies (
13
). Then
N_{T}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

The proof uses the same arguments as those given in
(
Rosier
, Lemma 3.4)
, Therefore, if
N_{T}\neq\emptyset
, the map
u_{0}\in N_{T}\mapsto{A}(N_{T})\subset\mathbb{C}N_{T}
(where
\mathbb{C}N_{T}
denote the complexification of
N_{T}
) has (at least) one eigenvalue; hence, there exist
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
u_{0}\in H^{3}_{x}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}_{y}(\Omega)
such that

\begin{cases}\lambda u_{0}(x,y)+u_{x,0}(x,y)+u_{xxx,0}(x,y)+\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy,0}(x,y)=0,\\
u_{0}(0,y)=u_{0}(L,y)=0,\quad u_{y,0}(x,L)=\beta u_{x,0}(x,L),\\
u_{x,0}(L,y)=\alpha u_{x,0}(0,y)=0,\quad u_{y,0}(x,0)=0\\

(14)

with

. To conclude the proof of the Lemma, we prove that this does not hold.
∎

In order to obtain the contradiction, it remains to prove that a duple

as above does not exist.

Step 3. (No Nontrivial Solution for the Spectral Problem)

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
u_{0}\in H^{3}_{x}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}_{y}(\Omega)
fulfilling (
14
). Then
u_{0}=0
.

Proof.

Let us introduce the notation
u(x,y)=u_{0}(x,y)
. Then we can rewrite our spectral problem (
14
) as

\begin{cases}\lambda u(x,y)+u_{x}(x,y)+u_{xxx}(x,y)+\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}(x,y)=0,\\
u(0,y)=u(L,y)=0,\quad u_{y}(x,L)=\beta u_{x}(x,L)=0,\\
u_{x}(L,y)=\alpha u_{x}(0,y)=0,\\
u_{y}(x,0)=0,\quad u_{x}(0,y)=u(x,L)=\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(0,y)=0.\end{cases}

(15)

Assume that
u
is a non-trivial solution that satisfies (
15
). Then, by multiplying by
\bar{\lambda}u(x,y)
and integrating over
\Omega
, it follows that

0\leqslant\int_{\Omega}|\lambda|^{2}u^{2}dxdy=\bar{\lambda}\left(-\int_{\Omega}uu_{x}dxdy-\int_{\Omega}uu_{xxx}dxdy-\int_{\Omega}u\partial^{-1}_{x}u_{yy}dxdy\right).

(16)

Note that, from the boundary conditions in (
15
), it yields

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}uu_{x}dxdy=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left(u^{2}(L,y)-u^{2}(0,y)\right)dy=0

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}uu_{xxx}dxdy=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left(u_{x}^{2}(L,y)-u^{2}_{x}(0,y)\right)dy=0

On the other hand, let us define (as before)
f_{x}(x,y)=u_{y}(x,y)
. Thus,
f_{xy}(x,y)=u_{yy}(x,y)
,
f_{y}(x,y)=\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}(x,y)
and
f(x,y)=\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(x,y)
. It is important to recognize that the definition of the non-local operator
\partial_{x}^{-1}
suggests that
f_{y}(L,y)=f(L,y)=0
. Then,

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u\partial^{-1}_{x}u_{yy}dxdy=\int_{\Omega}u_{y}fdxdy-\int_{0}^{L}\left(u(x,L)f(x,L)-u(x,0)f(x,0)\right)dx

By using the boundary conditions and (

), we get

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u\partial^{-1}_{x}u_{yy}dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u_{y}fdxdy=\int_{\Omega}f_{x}fdxdy=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left(f(L,y)-f(0,y)\right)dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}^{2}(0,y)dy=0

Therefore, (

) indicates that
\int_{\Omega}|\lambda|^{2}u^{2}dxdy=0
. This result implies
u=0
, contradicting our assumption that

was a non-trivial solution.
∎

With these Lemmas, we find

leading to a contradiction as
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
. Thus, we have successfully proven exponential stabilization.

Remark 2

(
The model without drift term).

The drift term
u_{x}
naturally arises from the derivation of the equation in fluid mechanics. On the entire domain, this drift term can be eliminated by a Galilean transform. However, this is not feasible on the half-line or bounded domains due to boundary conditions. In control theory, it is known that by combining some appropriate boundary conditions and incorporating the drift term into the model, the phenomenon of the critical set appears. This imposes certain restrictions on the length size of the domain. This represents a novel observation for the KdV equation. Following this direction, it is possible to consider the KP equation without the presence of the drift term:

\begin{cases}u_{t}(x,y,t)+u_{xxx}(x,y,t)+\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}(x,y,t)=0,\\
u(x,y,0)=u_{0}(x,y)\end{cases}

(17)

with the same boundary conditions:

\begin{cases}u(0,y,t)=u(L,y,t)=0\,\,\ u_{y}(x,L,t)=\beta u_{x}(x,L,t),\\
u_{x}(L,y,t)=\alpha u_{x}(0,y,t),\,\,u_{y}(x,0,t)=0.\end{cases}

(18)

All the calculations work for the system (
17
)-(
18
) except for Step 3, which is related to the nontrivial solution for the spectral problem when proving the observability inequality, specifically Lemma
3.2
. In this case, we need to slightly modify the argument in the following way:

Let us introduce the notation
u(x,y)=u_{0}(x,y)
and define
v_{x}(x,y)=u_{y,0}(x,y)
such that
v(L,y)=0
. Thus, we can rewrite our system (
14
) (without
u_{x}
) as:

\begin{cases}-u_{y}(x,y)+v_{x}(x,y)=0\\
v_{y}(x,y)+u_{xxx}(x,y)=-\lambda u(x,y)\end{cases}

(19)

with boundary conditions,

\begin{cases}u(0,y)=u(L,y)=0,\\
u(x,L)=0,\,u_{y}(x,L)=\beta u_{x}(x,L)=0,\\
u_{x}(L,y)=\alpha u_{x}(0,y)=0,\,u_{y}(x,0)=0,\\
v(0,y)=0.\end{cases}

The existence of a solution is guaranteed by Lemma
3.1
. Now, we focus on establishing uniqueness for system (
19
). We assume that both
(u_{1},v_{1})
and
(u_{2},v_{2})
are solutions and define
u:=u_{1}-u_{2}
and
v:=v_{1}-v_{2}
. We analyze two cases: one where
\lambda=0
and another where
\lambda\neq 0
. When
\lambda=0
, we have that
-u_{y}(x,y)+v_{x}(x,y)=0
and
v_{y}(x,y)+u_{xxx}(x,y)=0.
We use an approach by defining an energy functional. Let us define

\tilde{E}(u,v)(y):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left(v^{2}(x,y)+u_{x}^{2}(x,y)\right)dx,\quad y\in(0,L),

the energy associated to the system (
19
). Note that, by multiplying the first and second equations of (

) by

and by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}u_{xx}(x,y)\left(-u_{y}(x,y)+v_{x}(x,y)\right)+v(x,y)\left(v_{y}(x,y)+u_{xxx}(x,y)\right)\,dx=0.

Straightforward calculations and the boundary conditions imply that

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\tilde{E}(u,v)=\left[u_{x}(x,y)u_{y}(x,y)-u_{xx}(x,y)v(x,y)\right]_{x=0}^{x=L}=0.

Then,

\tilde{E}(u,v)(y)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}v^{2}(x,y)+u_{x}^{2}(x,y)\,dx=C,\quad y\in(0,L).

Note that, by definition

Thus, by the boundary condition

Since

and

In particular, it follows that

Then, we have that

Consolidating all information, we deduce that

Futher Comments

In this work, we studied the exponential stabilization of the KP-II equation. Our main result, Theorem
1.2
, ensures the exponential stability of the linearized KP-II under a feedback mechanism. However, extending this result to the nonlinear KP-II with the term

uu_{x}
(as in KdV) is challenging due to the lack of
a priori

L^{2}
-estimates, mainly because of the domain’s dimension. Additionally, the compactness-uniqueness argument focuses on existence and general stability, but issues like critical lengths may arise, requiring additional feedback inputs (see
Rosier
;
capistrano2024
). Furtermore, the exponential stabilization with a variation of a feedback-law can be considered. For example, considering an anti-damping mechanism as the boundary time-delay, we believe that a variation of the approach introduced in
Baudouin
;
Capistrano2023
can give positive answers to the long time behavior of the KP-II equation under damping and anti-damping mechanism, at least for the linear system.
