Voting with Partial Orders: The Plurality and Anti-Plurality Classes
Federico Fioravanti, Ulle Endriss

Abstract
The Plurality rule for linear orders selects the alternatives most frequently appearing in the first position of those orders, while the Anti-Plurality rule selects the alternatives least often occurring in the final position. We explore extensions of these rules to partial orders, offering axiomatic characterisations for these extensions. Keywords: Plurality, Anti-Plurality, Strict Partial Orders, Voting.



1
Introduction

We consider the problem of a society choosing alternatives from a fixed set of finitely many alternatives.
In a deviation from the model most commonly studied in social choice theory
(
Arrow et al., 2002
;
Zwicker, 2016
)
, we focus on situations in which each individual’s preference is a
strict partial order
, allowing any given individual, for each pair of alternatives, to report either that she strictly prefers one over the other or that she does not wish to compare the two.
The study of collective decision making on the basis of partial-order preferences deserves attention, as requiring voters to fully rank a (possibly) large number of
alternatives may be overly demanding in many circumstances.
Partial orders offer a less restrictive setting, giving voters more flexibility for expressing their true beliefs, while still allowing for the option to express preferences as linear orders for those who wish to do so.

Example 1
.

Consider the following situation, inspired by an example first discussed by
Terzopoulou and Endriss, 2019
.
Suppose we want to run a competition to look for the best mobile app. We ask users to rank different mobile apps, namely
Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, Uber, Gmail
, and
Yahoo
.
A particular user might be sure that she prefers
Instagram
to
Facebook
, and
Facebook
to
TikTok
, as she uses the three of them for posting videos; she also is sure that she prefers
Gmail
to
Yahoo
; but she might not be able to compare
Uber
to the rest of the apps, or
Gmail
to
Instagram
.
Instagram
Facebook
TikTok
Gmail
Yahoo
Uber

So it is clear that
Instagram
and
Gmail
are among the most preferred apps, and that
TikTok
and
Yahoo
are the least preferred ones.
But it is less clear whether
Uber
should be considered a good or a bad app, as it is not preferred to other alternatives, but there also are no alternatives that are preferred to it.
Overall, it is not obvious what app should be considered
the
best for this user, as the set of most preferred alternatives has a particular structure (e.g.,
Instagram
beats two other apps, while
Uber
has not been compared to any other app).
What is even less clear is how the preferences of multiple users should be aggregated in a situation like this.

\vartriangle

For the standard model of social choice, in which voters report strict linear orders, the most widely used rule in practice is the Plurality rule, which selects the alternative(s) most frequently ranked at the top of an individual preference order.
While the Plurality rule has been rightfully criticised in the literature for its shortcomings
(
Laslier, 2011
)
, it does have the significant advantage of being particularly simple: every voter simply awards a point to her most preferred alternative.
Can we preserve some of this simplicity when moving from linear to partial orders? The answer is not obvious, as an individual who only reports a partial order might not have a uniquely identified most preferred alternative.

If we stick to the idea that an individual should contribute more to her most preferred alternatives, we arrive at what we shall call the
Plurality Class
of voting rules for partial orders.
For instance, in the mobile apps example,
Instagram, Gmail
, and
Uber
are all top alternatives (in the sense that there are no alternatives more preferred to them), but other preferences might have a different structure.
One natural rule might assign 1 point to each of them (and 0 to all others), while another might assign each top alternative as many points as dominated alternatives are in that preference (and still 0 to all others).
Both of these rules belong to the Plurality Class, and both reduce to the standard Plurality rule for the special case of linear orders.

Our core contribution in this paper is to identify and axiomatically characterise the Plurality Class, as well as the closely related Anti-Plurality Class, which generalises the well-known Anti-Plurality rule (also known as the Veto rule) for linear orders.
In the standard setting, this is the rule that selects the alternative(s) least frequently ranked in the last position.

To characterise these two classes, we adapt many previously introduced axioms to this setting, while also proposing new ones.
The standard Plurality and Anti-Plurality rules belong to the family of positional scoring rules, which are rules that assign scores to alternatives based on their positions in the linear orders, and then choose the alternative(s) that maximise the sum of the scores across all voters.
Since
Young, 1975
characterised the scoring rules as the only ones satisfying Anonymity, Neutrality, Reinforcement, and Continuity, many different characterisations of the Plurality rule and the Anti-Plurality rule for linear orders have been obtained.
Let us briefly mention some of the characterisations of the Plurality rule that can be found in the literature:

2
The Model

In this section we present the model of voting with partial orders we study.
It is a model of
variable electorates
, meaning that any finite subset of an infinite universe of voters might cast a ballot in any given election.
Our model is one of the standard models of voting commonly studied in the literature, except that individual preferences are assumed to be strict partial orders rather than strict linear orders.

We also recall the definition of the family of positional scoring rules for this model and then introduce the Plurality Class and the Anti-Plurality Class of voting rules belonging to this family.

2.1
Voting with Partial Orders

Let
A
be a finite set of
alternatives
, with
|A|=m\geqslant 3
.
A
preference
is a strict partial order
\succ
over the set of alternatives, i.e., an irreflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive (but not necessarily complete) binary relation on
A
. We use
\mathcal{D}
to denote the
domain
of all such preferences.
For
a,b\in A
, we say that
a
is (strictly)
preferred
to
b
in case
a\succ b
.
If neither
a\succ b
nor
b\succ a
holds, we say that
a
is
incomparable
to
b
.
We use
\succ^{ab}
to denote the preference
\succ
with alternatives
a
and
b
swapped, while everything else remains the same.
The
top
of a preference
\succ
is the set of alternatives that are not dispreferred to any other alternative:

\displaystyle T(\succ)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\,a\in A\mid x\nsucc a\text{ for all }x\in A\,\}

The
non-top
of a preference
\succ
is the set
\mathit{NT}(\succ)=A\setminus T(\succ)
.
Analogously, the
bottom
of
\succ
is the set of alternatives that are not preferred to any other alternatives:

\displaystyle B(\succ)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\,b\in A\mid b\nsucc x\text{ for all }x\in A\,\}

The
non-bottom
of
\succ
is the set
\mathit{NB}(\succ)=A\setminus B(\succ)
.
Note that it is not necessarily the case that
T(\succ)\cap B(\succ)=\emptyset
.
For instance, in Example
1
, alternative
Uber
belongs to both the top and the bottom.
3
3

3

Although it is possible for an alternative to belong to the intersection of
T(\succ)
and
B(\succ)
, each voting rule considered in this paper will focus on either the top alternatives or the bottom alternatives, but never both.

We consider elections in which a finite society
N\subset\mathbb{N}
of
agents
(or
voters
) participate.
That is, we are working in a model of voting with variable electorates. Each agent
i\in N
casts her vote by reporting a preference
{\succ_{i}}\in\mathcal{D}
, giving rise to a
profile
of individual preferences. Formally, a profile for society
N
is a function from
N
to
\mathcal{D}
, but for notational convenience we will denote profiles as vectors
{\bm{\succ}}=(\succ_{i})_{i\in N}
. We write
\mathcal{D}^{N}
for the set of all such profiles.
We use
\bm{\succ}_{-i}
to denote the profile where the preference of agent
i
in profile
\bm{\succ}
has been omitted;
and we use
({\succ^{\prime}}_{i},\bm{\succ}_{-i})
to denote the profile we obtain when we replace the preference of the agents
i
in
\bm{\succ}
with
{\succ^{\prime}}_{i}
.
Given two profiles
{\bm{\succ}}\in\mathcal{D}^{N}
and
{\bm{\succ}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{D}^{N^{\prime}}
, with
N\cap N^{\prime}=\emptyset
, we write
(\bm{\succ},\bm{\succ}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}^{N\cup N^{\prime}}
for their concatenation, i.e., the profile in which both groups report preferences.

Finally, a
voting rule
is a function
F:\bigcup_{N\subset\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{D}^{N}\to 2^{A}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
, mapping any given profile of preferences (for any finite society
N\subset\mathbb{N}
) to a non-empty set of alternatives, the
winners
of the election in question. That is, we are working with voting rules that might return several alternatives that are tied for winning the election.
Next, we introduce some concrete examples for natural definitions of voting rules in this setting.

2.2
Specific Classes of Voting Rules

For the standard model of voting with strict linear orders, the class of
positional scoring rules
includes some of the best-known and most widely used rules, such as the Borda rule and the Plurality rule. Following
Kruger and Terzopoulou, 2020
, we now generalise the definition of this class to our model of voting with partial orders.

A
scoring function

s:A\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
maps any given alternative
a\in A
in a given preference
{\succ}\in\mathcal{D}
to a real number
s_{\succ}(a)
, which we call the
score
of
a
. We are specifically interested in scoring functions that are positional, meaning that the score of an alternative only depends on its position in the given preference.
Formally, a scoring function
s
is
positional
if, for all permutations
\sigma:A\to A
, all preferences
{\succ}\in\mathcal{D}
, and all alternatives
a\in A
, it is the case that
s_{\succ}(a)=s_{\sigma(\succ)}(\sigma(a))
, where
\sigma(\succ)=\{\,(\sigma(a),\sigma(b))\mid a\succ b\,\}
.

Intuitively, a positional scoring function assigns scores to positions in a graph.
Three well-known positional scoring functions for linear orders are the Borda scoring function, giving
m-1
points to the first alternative,
m-2
points to the second alternative, and so forth; the Plurality scoring function, assigning one point to the first alternative and zero to the rest; and the Anti-Plurality scoring function, assigning one point to all the alternatives except for the last-ranked one, which gets zero points.

For every positional scoring function
s
there is an associated
positional scoring rule

F_{s}
, which is the voting rule defined as follows:

\displaystyle F_{s}(\bm{\succ})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{a\in A}\sum_{i\in N}s_{\succ_{i}}(a)\quad\text{for any profile}\ {\bm{\succ}}\in\mathcal{D}^{N}.

We now propose a class of positional scoring rules for strict partial orders that, we believe, captures one of the options available for generalising the Plurality rule for linear orders.

Definition 1
.

A positional scoring rule
F_{s}
belongs to the
Plurality Class
if the associated scoring function
s
satisfies the following property: Given a preference
{\succ}\in\mathcal{D}
, for all
a\in T(\succ)
and all

, it is the case that
s_{\succ}(a)=k
and
s_{\succ}(b)=k^{\prime}
, with
k>k^{\prime}
, where
k
and
k^{\prime}
depend only on the number of alternatives that are in
T(\succ)
.

Thus, we require that all alternatives that are in the top of a preference receive the same score, all the alternatives not in the top also receive the same score and that score must be lower, and that both of these scores depend only on the number of alternatives in the top of the preference.
This way, we are able to exploit the preference’s structure and, for example, award alternatives that are unique at the top more points than those given to alternatives that share the top with others.
Observe that on the domain of strict linear orders, every voting rule in the Plurality Class reduces to the familiar Plurality rule for linear orders.

Note that applying an affine transformation to a scoring function does not change the voting rule that is being induced.

Applying an affine transformation
x\mapsto\alpha x+\beta

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

\beta\in\mathbb{R}
to a scoring function
s
means replacing, for every preference
{\succ}\in\mathcal{D}
and every alternative

s_{\succ}(a)
with the new score
\alpha\cdot s_{\succ}(a)+\beta
.

Indeed, while an affine transformation will change the scores each alternative receives, it will not change whether or not a given alternative receives the highest score and thus wins the election.
So if
s^{\prime}
is an affine transformation of

, then
F_{s}=F_{s^{\prime}}
, meaning that for every given positional scoring rule
F_{s}
there are infinitely many ways of representing that one rule.

Let us now focus on the positional scoring rules in the Plurality Class that assign the same score to all the top alternatives and the same (lower) score to all the alternatives not in the top.
Such a Plurality rule is
uniform
in the sense that the score assigned to a top alternative does not depend on the structure of the preference but is uniform across all possible preferences, and the same is true for the alternatives that are not in the top.
Note that, in view of what we said earlier about positional scoring rules being invariant under affine transformations, there in fact is only a single rule in the Plurality Class that is uniform—but it can be represented by infinitely many different scoring functions. We call this unique rule the
Uniform Plurality
rule.
The canonical way of defining it would be as the rule that assigns 1 point to every alternative in the top and 0 points to all other alternatives.

Definition 2
.

The
Uniform Plurality
rule
F_{s}
is the rule belonging to the Plurality Class that is induced by the scoring function
s
with
s_{\succ}(a)=1
and
s_{\succ}(b)=0
for every preference
{\succ}\in\mathcal{D}
, every alternative
a\in T(\succ)
, and every alternative
b\in NT(\succ)
.

This rule might be regarded as the most natural extension of the Plurality rule for linear orders to our model of voting with partial orders, and is highly appealing due to its simplicity.
It captures the most basic intuition about the Plurality rule for linear orders, selecting the alternatives that most frequently appear in the top positions of the ballots.

We also propose a class of positional scoring rules for strict partial orders that generalises the Anti-Plurality rule for linear orders.

s_{\succ}(a)=k
and
s_{\succ}(b)=k^{\prime}
, with

, where
k
and

depend only on the number of alternatives that are in

Also here it is easy to see that, indeed, when applied to a profile of strict linear orders, any rule in the Anti-Plurality Class reduces to the standard Anti-Plurality rule.

3
Axioms

In this section, we introduce a number of axioms, i.e., fundamental normative requirements for voting rules, which later will turn out to be useful for our characterisation results.
Every axiom postulates a property that, one might argue, any reasonable voting rules
F
should satisfy.

The first four axioms, Anonymity, Neutrality, Reinforcement, and Continuity, are very common in the literature on scoring rules and are used by
Young, 1975
to characterise them.
The first two are symmetry requirements that essentially state that the names of the agents and alternatives should not be considered important for choosing a winner.
We formulate Anonymity in terms of a bijection
\mu:N\rightarrow N^{\prime}
for any two societies
N,N^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{N}
with the same number of agents. Similarly, we formulate Neutrality in terms of a permutation
\sigma:A\rightarrow A
on the set of alternatives, which extends to both sets
S\subseteq A
of alternatives and preferences
{\succ}\in\mathcal{D}
in a natural manner:

\sigma(S)=\{\,\sigma(x)\mid x\in S\,\}
and
\sigma(\succ)=\{\,(\sigma(x),\sigma(y))\mid x\succ y\,\}
.

Axiom 1

(Anonymity)
.

For any two societies
N,N^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{N}
with the same number of agents and any bijection
\mu:N\rightarrow N^{\prime}
, it should be the case that
F((\succ_{i})_{i\in N})
=
F((\succ_{\mu(i)})_{i\in N})
.

Axiom 2

(Neutrality)
.

For any profile
{\bm{\succ}}=(\succ_{i})_{i\in N}
and any permutation
\sigma:A\rightarrow A
, it should be the case that
\sigma(F((\succ_{i})_{i\in N}))=\mbox{$F((\sigma(\succ_{i}))_{i\in N})$}
.

Thus, Anonymity says that changing the names of the voters should not change the winners, while Neutrality says that any change in the names of the alternatives in the profile should be simply be applied to the original winners to obtain the new winners.

The next axiom, which is also known as Consistency, states that, if two disjoint electorates agree on some winning alternatives, then those alternatives must be selected when we consider a profile where the members of both electorates cast a vote.

Axiom 3

(Reinforcement)
.

For any two profiles
{\bm{\succ}}\in\mathcal{D}^{N}
and
{\bm{\succ^{\prime}}}\in\mathcal{D}^{N^{\prime}}
with
N\cap N^{\prime}=\emptyset
such that

F(\bm{\succ})\cap F(\bm{\succ^{\prime}})\neq\emptyset
, it should be the case that
F(\bm{\succ},\bm{\succ^{\prime}})=F(\bm{\succ})\cap F(\bm{\succ^{\prime}})
.

The next axiom, called the Archimedean property by
Smith, 1973
and
Young, 1975
, and Overwhelming Majority by
Myerson, 1995
, stipulates that, although a small number of agents cannot completely over-ride the decision of a large majority, they might still break ties in favour of some of the alternatives selected by the majority.

Axiom 4

(Continuity)
.

For any two profiles
\bm{\succ}
and
\bm{\succ^{\prime}}
there should exist a bound
K
such that, for every natural number
k>K
, the following inclusion holds:

\displaystyle F(\underbrace{\bm{\succ},\cdots,\bm{\succ}}_{k\ \text{times}},\bm{\succ^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle F(\bm{\succ})

In the sequel, we will write
k{\bm{\succ}}
as a shorthand for the profile
(\bm{\succ},\cdots,\bm{\succ})
consisting of a concatenation of
k
copies of the basic profile
\bm{\succ}
.

The next two axioms are minimal responsiveness and efficiency requirements for a voting rule.
The first one is a version of the Faithfulness axiom due to
Young, 1974
, and the second one is a version of Averseness introduced by
Kurihara, 2018
.
The former states that, for the special case of single-voter profiles, the voting rule must select alternatives that are in the top of that agent.
Thus, dominated alternatives are not selected.
The latter states that for such profiles it should not select
all
of the bottom alternatives. So, not all dominated alternatives are selected.

Axiom 5

(Faithfulness)
.

For any single-voter profile
\succ\in\mathcal{D}
, it should be the case that
F(\succ)\subseteq T(\succ)
.

Axiom 6

(Averseness)
.

For any single-voter profile
\succ\in\mathcal{D}
with
B(\succ)\neq A
, it should be the case that
B(\succ)\nsubseteq F(\succ)
.

Note that Averseness is a weaker requirement than Faithfulness, in the formal sense of being implied by the latter.

The remaining axioms we propose all demand, in one way or another, that a voting rule should limit the amount of information it takes into account when producing a winner.
This is motivated by the idea that a voter’s attention is a scarce resource and a voting rule that might suggest a change in winners every time a voter reports a change in her preference that only concerns less “salient” alternatives, might be judged to be too brittle for use in practice.

The first two axioms are instances of the idea that the outcome should depend only on the most relevant information.
In one case, the set of top alternatives of each agent is the only information required to determine the outcome, while in the other case, the set of bottom alternatives of each agent is the only information required.
5
5

5

For further motivation regarding these two axioms, we refer to
Sekiguchi, 2012
and
Kurihara, 2018
.

Axiom 7

(Tops-Only)
.

For any two profiles
{\bm{\succ}},{\bm{\succ}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{D}^{N}
with
T(\succ_{i})=T(\succ_{i}^{\prime})
for all
i\in N
, it should be the case that
F(\bm{\succ})=F(\bm{\succ}^{\prime})
.

Axiom 8

(Bottoms-Only)
.

For any two profiles
{\bm{\succ}},{\bm{\succ}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{D}^{N}
with
B(\succ_{i})=B(\succ_{i}^{\prime})
for all
i\in N
, it should be the case that
F(\bm{\succ})=F(\bm{\succ}^{\prime})
.

Observe that if a voting rule satisfies Tops-Only, not only are the alternatives that are not in the top not relevant, but also the arrangement of top alternatives becomes irrelevant.
An analogous property holds for Bottoms-Only.

For the final two axioms, we require some further notation.
For a given profile
{\bm{\succ}}\in\mathcal{D}^{N}
, the number of times the alternative
x
appears in a voter’s top is denoted by
t_{x}(\bm{\succ})
:

\displaystyle t_{x}(\bm{\succ})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{\,i\in N\mid x\in T(\succ_{i})\,\}|.

Analogously,
b_{x}(\bm{\succ})
is the number of times alternative
x
appears in a voter’s bottom set:

\displaystyle b_{x}(\bm{\succ})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{\,i\in N\mid x\in B(\succ_{i})\,\}|.

The following axioms are stronger versions of Tops-Only and Bottoms-Only. They apply to a wider range of pairs of profiles. For instance, while Tops-Only applies to all profiles (of the same society) inducing the same profile of tops, Strong Tops-Only applies to all pairs of profiles (regardless of the society) that agree on the number of times any one alternative appears in a top set.

Axiom 9

(Strong Tops-Only)

For any two profiles
{\bm{\succ}}
and
{\bm{\succ}^{\prime}}
with
\mbox{$t_{x}(\bm{\succ})$}=t_{x}(\bm{\succ}^{\prime})

{\bm{\succ}}

Note that, for the particular case where preferences are linear orders, Tops-Only, and Strong Tops-Only both reduce to the same property, and the same is true for Bottoms-Only, and Strong Bottoms-Only.

5
Conclusion

We have provided axiomatic characterisations for generalised versions of both the Plurality and Anti-Plurality rules to scenarios in which preferences take the form of partial orders.
In the standard scenario with preferences that are linear orders, the Plurality (Anti-Plurality) rule gives one point (zero points) only to the most (least) preferred alternative and zero points (one point) to the rest.
We generalised this defining feature to the case of partial orders in a manner that accounts for the fact that there is more than one way in which an alternative might be most (least) preferred in a partial order, given that any top alternative might be either the unique one in the top or one amongst several such alternatives.

When considering linear orders, the characterisations we have presented all converge to familiar standards established in the existing literature.

An intriguing path for future investigation lies in exploring similar generalisations of other scoring rules to the domain of partial orders, including but not limited to the Borda, Cumulative, and Stepwise scoring rules.
