Achieving Exponential Asymptotic Optimality in Average-Reward Restless Bandits without Global Attractor Assumption
Yige Hong, Qiaomin Xie, Yudong Chen, Weina Wang

Abstract
We consider the infinite-horizon average-reward restless bandit problem. We propose a novel two-set policy that maintains two dynamic subsets of arms: one subset of arms has a nearly optimal state distribution and takes actions according to an Optimal Local Control routine; the other subset of arms is driven towards the optimal state distribution and gradually merged into the first subset. We show that our two-set policy is asymptotically optimal with an O ⁡ ( exp ⁡ ( − C ​ N ) ) O(\exp(-CN)) optimality gap for an N N -armed problem, under the mild assumptions of aperiodic-unichain, non-degeneracy, and local stability. Our policy is the first to achieve exponential asymptotic optimality under the above set of easy-to-verify assumptions, whereas prior work either requires a strong global attractor assumption or only achieves an O ⁡ ( 1 / N ) O(1/\sqrt{N}) optimality gap. We further discuss obstacles in weakening the assumptions by demonstrating examples where exponential asymptotic optimality is not achievable when any of the three assumptions is violated. Notably, we prove a lower bound for a large class of locally unstable restless bandits, showing that local stability is particularly fundamental for exponential asymptotic optimality. Finally, we use simulations to demonstrate that the two-set policy outperforms previous policies on certain RB problems and performs competitively overall.

Introduction

The restless bandit (RB)
[
18
]
problem is a stochastic sequential decision-making problem consisting of multiple arms coupled by a constraint.
Each arm operates as a Markov decision process (MDP) with two possible actions: activating or keeping passive.
At each time step, the decision maker observes the states of the arms and chooses a fixed fraction of arms to activate, aiming to maximize the expected reward collected from the arms.
The RB problem has a long history and diverse applications. We refer the readers to the recent survey paper
[
14
]
for a comprehensive review of the literature.

In this paper, we study the infinite-horizon RBs in discrete time, with the long-run average-reward objective.
Since computing the exact optimal policy for an RB problem is intractable when the number of arms
N
is large
[
15
]
, we focus on developing asymptotically optimal policies that can be efficiently computed.
Specifically, the optimality gap of a policy is defined as the difference between the average reward per arm and that of an optimal policy.
A policy is
asymptotically optimal
if the optimality gap is
o(1)
in the asymptotic limit
N\to\infty
.

In this paper, we use standard Bachmann–Landau notation and focus on the asymptotics with respect to
N
.

The asymptotic optimality of average-reward RB in the large
N
regime has been studied for several decades, starting with the seminal papers on the renowned Whittle index policy
[
18
,
19
]
.
Since then, researchers have been weakening the assumptions for asymptotic optimality
[
17
,
12
,
13
,
20
]
or improving the order of the optimality gaps
[
7
,
6
]
.
A recent milestone is the
exponential asymptotic optimality
established in
[
7
,
6
]
, where a class of policies called LP-Priority is proved to achieve an optimality gap
O(\exp(-CN))
for a constant
C
, under some assumptions.
This exponential gap is particularly noteworthy because it “beats” the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), an intriguing theoretical property that is not a priori obvious to be possible.
In particular, on a high-level, all asymptotic optimality results in the RB literature are based on the concentration of the empirical distribution of the arms’ states around a certain optimal distribution. Therefore, an
O(1/\sqrt{N})
bound owing to CLT was believed to be fundamental.
An important observation made in
[
7
,
6
]
is that if the system has a certain notion of
local linearity
, the optimality gap only depends on the distance between the optimal distribution and the
expected
empirical state distribution, allowing one to go beyond CLT.

Despite the above intuition,
it remains unclear what is the fundamental mechanism leading to exponential asymptotic optimality. Although a simple assumption called non-degeneracy (or non-singularity) suffices to ensure the local linearity property described above, non-degeneracy alone is not enough to guarantee exponential optimality of the LP-Priority policies considered in
[
7
,
6
]
.
In particular, another crucial assumption is needed, namely the Uniform Global Attractor Property (UGAP).
UGAP is a stronger version of the global attractor property (GAP) — the latter is assumed in all asymptotic analyses of LP-Priority policies, without which an LP-Priority may even have a constant optimality gap
[see
19
,
5
,
12
, for concrete examples]
.
Unfortunately, UGAP is hard to interpret and verify, as it concerns the global convergence of a certain non-linear difference equation.

In light of the difficulty caused by UGAP, very recent work has studied new policies that are asymptotically optimal without assuming UGAP
[
12
,
13
,
20
]
.
Different from LP-Priority and Whittle index policies, these new policies actively control the empirical distribution of the states of the arms, driving the distribution to
globally converge
towards a certain optimal distribution, without relying on extraneous assumptions.
However, while these results conclude that UGAP is not needed for asymptotic optimality, it remains unclear whether UGAP is fundamental for exponential asymptotic optimality — the best optimality gap proved in this line of work is
O(1/\sqrt{N})
.

Given these developments, it is natural to ask if removing UGAP necessarily comes at the cost of degrading the optimality gap.
Is it possible to efficiently find policies that achieve
exponential asymptotic optimality
without assuming UGAP?

Our contributions.

The primary contribution of the paper is to design the first policy, the
two-set policy
, that achieves exponential asymptotic optimality without UGAP.
The two-set policy maintains two dynamic subsets of arms and applies two different subroutines to the subsets:
one subset of arms has a nearly optimal state distribution and takes actions according to a certain Optimal Local Control; the other subset of arms is driven towards the optimal state distribution and gradually merged into the first subset.
Intuitively, the Optimal Local Control in the first subset induces local linearity, while the second subset enables global convergence.
While the ideas in each subroutine are not completely new, the two-set policy leverages them in a novel way to achieve the best of both worlds.
The theoretical guarantee of the two-set policy is informally summarized in the theorem below.

Theorem

(Informal version of Theorem
1
)
.

Suppose each arm is unichain, aperiodic, non-degenerate, and locally stable.
Then the two-set policy
\pi
satisfies

\displaystyle R^{\textup{rel}}-R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})=O(\exp(-CN)),

(1)

where
R^{\textup{rel}}
is an upper bound on the optimal long-run average reward, and
R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})
is the long-run average reward under the policy
\pi
given the vector of initial states
\bm{S}_{0}
.

We will make precise the definitions of non-degeneracy and local stability. We emphasize that we only need aperiodic unichain under a particular policy rather than all policies.
The assumptions of Theorem
1
are a subset of, and hence strictly weaker than, the assumptions made in prior work
[
7
,
6
]
for exponential asymptotic optimality. Moreover, all our assumptions pertain to a linear program defined by problem primitives and are thus much easier to verify than UGAP.

The algorithmic ideas of the two-set policy share some common traits with those in the “set-expansion policy” in
[
13
]
and “align and steer” policy in
[
20
]
.
However, these two policies have only been shown to have
O(1/\sqrt{N})
and
o(1)
optimality gaps, respectively.

The proof of Theorem
1
employs a novel multivariate Lyapunov function technique, which generalizes the focus-set approach in
[
13
]
. This multivariate Lyapunov function enables us to decouple the complex dynamics under the two-set policy, where the states of the arms and the two dynamic subsets are coupled and change simultaneously.

We next elucidate the roles of our assumptions in achieving exponential optimality.
The importance of unichain, aperiodicity and non-degeneracy is well recognized in the literature; we will complement the discussion with concrete examples.
We further establish the fundamental importance of local stability by proving the following theorem.

Theorem

(Informal version of Theorem
2
)
.

For regular unstable RBs, every policy
\pi
satisfies

\displaystyle R^{\textup{rel}}-R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})=\Omega(1/\sqrt{N}).

(2)

We will formally define the class of regular (locally) unstable RB and provide concrete examples. Note that all known efficient policies rely on the upper bound
R^{\textup{rel}}
.
For these policies,
Theorem

2
shows that local stability is crucial for achieving exponential asymptotic optimality for a natural class of RB instances.
The proof of
Theorem

2
uses Lyapunov analysis to reveal an interesting trade-off between two competing needs when local stability fails: to keep the states of the arms near the optimal distribution, and to take the optimal actions when the states are near the optimal distribution.

Problem setup

Consider the discrete-time average-reward restless bandit problem with
N
homogeneous arms, referred to as the
N
-armed problem. Index the arms by
[N]\triangleq\{1,2,\dots,N\}.

To avoid confusion with the Whittle index, we refer to
i
as the
ID
of Arm
i
. Each arm is associated with a Markov decision process (MDP) defined by
(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{A},P,r),
which is called the single-armed MDP.
Here
\mathbb{S}
is a finite state space;

\mathbb{A}=\{0,1\}
is the action space, where action
1
is activating/pulling the arm;

P:\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{S}\to[0,1]
is the transition kernel;

r:\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the reward function.
Let
r_{\max}=\max_{s\in\mathbb{S},a\in\mathbb{A}}\left\lvert r(s,a)\right\rvert
.
The RB problem has a
budget constraint
, given by a constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
, which requires that
\alpha
fraction of arms must be pulled at every time step. We assume that
\alpha N
is an integer.
We focus on the setting where all the model parameters,
\mathbb{S},\mathbb{A},P,r,\alpha
, are known.

A policy
\pi
for the
N
-armed problem chooses the action for each of the
N
arms in each time step.
The policy can be randomized and history-dependent.
Under
\pi
, the
state vector

\bm{S}_{t}^{\pi}\triangleq(S_{t}^{\pi}(i))_{i\in[N]}\in\mathbb{S}^{N}
represents the states of all arms at time
t
, and the
action vector

\bm{A}_{t}^{\pi}\triangleq(A_{t}^{\pi}(i))_{i\in[N]}\in\mathbb{A}^{N}
denotes the actions applied to each arm.
For each policy
\pi,
consider the following two quantities: the limsup average reward

R^{+}(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})\triangleq\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\left[r(S_{t}^{\pi}(i),A_{t}^{\pi}(i))\right];
and the liminf average reward
R^{-}(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})\triangleq\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\left[r(S_{t}^{\pi}(i),A_{t}^{\pi}(i))\right]
.
When the limsup and te liminf average rewards are equal, the
long-run average reward
is defined as:

R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})\triangleq\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\left[r(S_{t}^{\pi}(i),A_{t}^{\pi}(i))\right].

Our goal is solving the optimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{\text{policy }\pi}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\quad R^{-}(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})\qquad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{i\in[N]}A_{t}^{\pi}(i)=\alpha N,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(RB)

Let
R^{*}(N,\bm{S}_{0})
be the optimal value of (
RB
), termed as the
optimal reward
.
Note that
R^{*}(N,\bm{S}_{0})=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}}R^{-}(\pi^{\prime},\bm{S}_{0})=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}}R^{+}(\pi^{\prime},\bm{S}_{0})
because (
RB
) is an MDP with finite state and action spaces
[
16
, Theorem 9.1.6]
.
For any policy
\pi
, we define its optimality gap as
R^{*}(N,\bm{S}_{0})-R^{-}(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})
. Consistent with the literature, we call a policy
\pi

asymptotically optimal
if its optimality gap vanishes as
N\to\infty
, i.e.,
R^{*}(N,\bm{S}_{0})-R^{-}(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})=o(1)

[
17
, Definition 4.11]
.

In the rest of the paper, we restrict ourselves to policies whose long-run average reward
R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})
is well-defined. Such policies include all stationary Markovian policies
[
16
, Proposition 8.1.1]
, as well as any stationary policy that makes decisions based on an augmented system state with a finite state space, because they all induce finite-state Markov chains.
Note that focusing on such policies is sufficient because there always exists a stationary Markovian policy that achieves the optimal reward
[
16
, Theorem 9.1.8]
.
Consequently, we can refer to
R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})
as the objective function of (
RB
) and write the optimality gap as
R^{*}(N,\bm{S}_{0})-R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})
.

Scaled state-count vector.

We introduce an alternative representation of the

-armed system state,

For each subset

, where

We consider the linear programming (LP) relaxation of the

Optimal single-armed policy.
For a fixed optimal solution to (

{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}=({{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}(a|s))_{s\in\mathbb{S},a\in\mathbb{A}}

{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}(a|s)=\begin{cases}y^{*}(s,a)/(y^{*}(s,0)+y^{*}(s,1)),&\text{if }y^{*}(s,0)+y^{*}(s,1)>0,\\

(5)

We call
{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}

3.2
Overview of our main policy

Before starting to introduce elements for constructing our main policy, the two-set policy (
Algorithm

1
), we first give an overview.
The two-set policy utilizes two subroutines, Unconstrained Optimal Control (
Subroutine

1
), and a local version of LP-Priority named Optimal Local Control (
Subroutine

2
).
At each time step, the two-set policy partitions
N
arms into three subsets: the first subset
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
has nearly optimal state distribution and takes actions according to the Optimal Local Control; the second subset
D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}
is driven towards the optimal state distribution by the Unconstrained Optimal Control and is gradually merged into
D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}
; the third subset
(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\cup D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})^{c}
chooses actions to meet the budget constraint
\sum_{i\in[N]}A_{t}(i)=\alpha N
, and will be gradually merged into
D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}
.
In the long run,
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
will contain all arms in the system and remain so for the majority of the time; the Optimal Local Control guarantees that the average reward is close to
R^{\textup{rel}}
.

3.3
Subroutine 1: Unconstrained Optimal Control

We now describe the
Unconstrained Optimal Control
, as presented in
Subroutine

1
. The subroutine takes the optimal single-armed policy
{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}
defined in (
5
) and a subset of arms
D
from the RB system as the input. The subroutine decides the actions for all arms in
D
to match with
{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}.
Specifically, let the number of arms from
D
in state
s\in\mathbb{S}
be
z(s).
The subroutine aims to activate
{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}(1|s)
fraction of the
z(s)

randomized rounding
: it activates
\left\lfloor{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}(1|s)z(s)\right\rfloor
or
\left\lceil{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}(1|s)z(s)\right\rceil

{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}(1|s)
.

As shown in
Lemma

3
, if all
N

,
the expected scaled state-count vector
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}([N])\right]
converges to
\mu^{*}
. As a result, the expected fraction of arms in state

converges to
y^{*}(s,a)
;
the
expected budget usage
of the arms converges to
\alpha N
;
the long-run average reward achieves the optimum of (
LP

Our first main theorem states an upper bound on the optimality gap of the two-set policy.

Remark 2
.

We discuss the relationship between the two-set policy in this paper and a policy from prior work named the set-expansion policy
[

The two-set policy in this paper refines this approach by maintaining two judiciously chosen sets instead of one set.
Furthermore, we argue that the two-set policy can be implemented in a way such that when Assumption

Using similar arguments as those in
[

The two-set policy maintains two dynamics subsets, each running a different subroutine. The main challenge of the proof lies in analyzing the complex dynamics of the two subsets.
To tackle this challenge, we use a novel
multivariate Lyapunov function
that takes not only the system state
X_{t}
but also the subsets
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
,
D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}
as variables.

Step 1.
We first show that the optimality gap can be bounded as follows:

R^{\textup{rel}}-R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})\leq O(1)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big[D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty}\neq[N]\big],

where the subscript
\infty
indicates the steady-state distribution, which we assume to exist in this proof sketch. The proof of this bound is similar to those of Theorems 3 and 8 of
[
6
]
and is provided in
Lemma

7
.

Step 2.
Next, by the definition of
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
, it is not hard to see that if
\left\lVert X_{t}([N])-\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{U}\leq\eta
, then
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}=[N]
. Therefore, it suffices to show that
\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert X_{\infty}([N])-\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{U}>\eta\right]
is exponentially small.
To bound the distance
\lVert X_{\infty}([N])-\mu^{*}\rVert_{U}
, an important step is to construct proper bivariate functions that capture the dynamics of the arms in the subsets
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
and
D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}
. We define

\displaystyle h_{W}(x,D)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert x(D)-m(D)\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{W},\quad h_{U}(x,D)=\left\lVert x(D)-m(D)\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{U},

where
W
is a
|\mathbb{S}|
-by-
|\mathbb{S}|
weight matrix and
{v}_{W}=\sqrt{vWv^{\top}}
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{S}|}
.
To understand
h_{W}
and
h_{U}
, note that the matrix
W
is designed such that
P_{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}
is a pseudo-contraction on
\Delta(\mathbb{S})
with the fixed point
\mu^{*}
under the
W
-weighted norm (
Lemma

5
). The bivariate function
h_{W}
thus witnesses the convergence of state distributions on a fixed subset of arms when that subset continues to follow the Unconstrained Optimal Control. Similarly,
\Phi
is a pseudo-contraction under the
U
-weighted norm, so
h_{U}
witnesses the convergence under the Optimal Local Control (
Lemma

9
).

Step 3.
We use subset Lyapunov functions
h_{W}
and
h_{U}
as building block for constructing our global Lyapunov function
V(x,D^{\textup{OL}},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})
, defined as

V(x,D^{\textup{OL}},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})=h_{U}(x,D^{\textup{OL}})+h_{W}(x,D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})+L(1-m(D^{\textup{OL}})),

where
L
is a constant ensuring that
V(x,D^{\textup{OL}},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})
bounds
h_{U}(x,[N])=\lVert X_{\infty}([N])-\mu^{*}\rVert_{U}
.
We show that
V(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})
decreases by a large amount every time step with high probability, unless
V(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})=O(1/\sqrt{N})
, which implies that the Lyapunov function is small in steady-state.

A key idea in analyzing the changes of
V(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})
is to analyze those changes caused by state transitions and those caused by set-updates separately, leading to the key inequality below:

\displaystyle V(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1})-V(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\big(h_{U}(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})-h_{U}(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})\big)+\big(h_{W}(X_{t+1},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})-h_{W}(X_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})\big)

\displaystyle\mspace{20mu}+4\big(\lambda_{U}^{1/2}+\lambda_{W}^{1/2}\big)m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\backslash D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1})+O\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big),

where
\lambda_{U}
and
\lambda_{W}
are the spectral norms of
U
and
W
.
To upper bound the RHS of this inequality, we prove two lemmas to show that
m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\backslash D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1})
is small (
Lemma

10
) and that
\big(h_{U}(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})-h_{U}(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})\big)+\big(h_{W}(X_{t+1},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})-h_{W}(X_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})\big)
is sufficiently negative (
Lemma

11
).
This gives us a negative-drift inequality that allows us to bound the tail probabilities of
V(X_{\infty},D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{\infty})
. In particular, we can show that
P(V(X_{\infty},D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{\infty})\geq\eta-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{fe}})=O(\exp(-CN))
for some constant
C>0
. Because
V(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})
upper bounds
h_{U}(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})
, and
h_{U}(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})\leq\eta-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{fe}}
implies
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}=[N]
, we finish the proof.
See
Appendix

B
for full details of the proof.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the conditions for achieving exponentially asymptotic optimality in discrete-time RBs with the infinite-horizon average-reward criterion. We propose a novel two-set policy that achieves exponential asymptotic optimality under three assumptions: aperiodic unichain (
Assumption

1
), non-degeneracy (
Assumption

2
), and local stability (
Assumption

3
). Our assumptions are strictly weaker than those in the prior work, where a global attractor assumption is also required for their policies to be exponentially asymptotic optimal.

To complement our positive result, we also discuss the necessities of our three assumptions. In particular, we prove a lower bound of
R^{\textup{rel}}-R^{*}(N,\bm{S}_{0})
for a large class of RB instances that do not satisfy local stability, illustrating the fundamental role of the local stability for exponential asymptotic optimality.

Finally, we numerically evaluate the performance of the two-set policies. Our numerical results show that the two-set policy achieves the best performance among the policies simulated here when our three assumptions are satisfied but UGAP is violated; the two-set policy also performs reasonably well on RB instances generated uniformly at random.

For the future work, one interesting direction is to give a more precise characterization of the constant
C
in the
O(\exp(-CN))
optimality gap bound of the two-set policy. Another possible direction is extending the lower bound in
Theorem

2
to RB instances that are not regular unstable.

We first introduce a few notational conventions.
Note that the two-set policy in
Algorithm

1
induces a finite-state Markov chain whose state at time
t
is
(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})
. We use
\Sigma_{t}
as a shorthand for
(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})
.
We use
X_{\infty},D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{\infty},\Sigma_{\infty}
to represent variables following long-run average distribution: For example, for any function
f
of
\Sigma_{t}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\Sigma_{\infty})\right]\triangleq\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[f(\Sigma_{t})\right],

where the limit always exists because
\Sigma_{t}
is a finite-state Markov chain, though it could depend on initial state
(X_{0},D^{\textup{OL}}_{0},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{0})
.

Our first lemma,
Lemma

6

D^{\textup{OL}}
and
D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}
can indeed follow the intended subroutine when
\eta
is small enough. This lemma is proved in
Section

B.3
.

We first define two classes of functions,

For any system state

\displaystyle h_{W}(x,D)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert x(D)-m(D)\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{W}

\displaystyle h_{U}(x,D)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert x(D)-m(D)\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{U}.

(34)

These two classes of functions quantify the convergence of state distributions for subsets of arms that follow the Unconstrained Optimal Control or the Optimal Local Control. Such functions are referred to as
subset Lyapunov functions
and are used as building blocks in the Lyapunov analysis from prior work
[
13

Proof.

Define the Lyapunov function
V(x,D^{\textup{OL}},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})
as

V(x,D^{\textup{OL}},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})=h_{U}(x,D^{\textup{OL}})+h_{W}(x,D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})+L(1-m(D^{\textup{OL}})),

(43)

where
L=2\lambda_{U}^{1/2}+4\lambda_{W}^{1/2}-2\lambda_{W}^{1/2}\beta
.
We claim that

\mathbb{P}\big[V(X_{\infty},D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{\infty})>\eta-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{fe}}\big]=O(\exp(-CN)),

(44)

for some positive constant
C
.
To see why (
44
) helps, note that by
Lemma

9
,

V(x,D^{\textup{OL}},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})\geq h_{U}(x,D^{\textup{OL}})+2\lambda_{U}^{1/2}(1-m(D^{\textup{OL}}))\geq h_{U}(x,[N]),

so
V(X_{\infty},D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{\infty})\leq\eta-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{fe}}
implies
h_{U}(X_{\infty},[N])\leq\eta-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{fe}}
, which further implies
D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty}=[N]
by
Lemma

11
. Therefore, suppose we get (
44
), with
Lemma

7
and the above argument, we have

\displaystyle R^{\textup{rel}}-R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})

\displaystyle\leq O(1)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big[D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty}\neq[N]\big]

\displaystyle\leq O(1)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big[h_{U}(X_{\infty},[N])>\eta-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{fe}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq O(1)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big[V(X_{\infty},D^{\textup{OL}}_{\infty},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{\infty})>\eta-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{fe}}\big]

\displaystyle=O(\exp(-CN)),

which will finish the proof.

In the rest of the proof, we will show (
44
) by invoking
Lemma

8
with
\overline{\eta}=\eta-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{fe}}
.
First,
V(x,D^{\textup{OL}},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}})
is non-negative and upper bounded by
\lambda_{U}^{1/2}+\lambda_{W}^{1/2}+L
, so the condition (
27
) holds. To show the other conditions, we first examine the difference
V(\Sigma_{t+1})-V(\Sigma_{t})
.
Consider the decomposition

\displaystyle V(\Sigma_{t+1})-V(\Sigma_{t})

\displaystyle=\big(V(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1})-V(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}))

(45)

\displaystyle\mspace{20mu}+\big(V(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})-V(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})\big).

(46)

We call the difference term in RHS of (
45
) the
set-update term
, and the difference term in (
46
) the
state-transition term
.
We bound them separately.

For the set-update term in (
45
), we can derive a bound fully in terms of the sizes of sets, using the Lipschitz continuity of
h_{W}(x,D)
and
h_{U}(x,D)
with respect to
D
given in (
38
) of
Lemma

9
:

\displaystyle\mspace{20mu}V(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1})-V(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=h_{U}(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1})-h_{U}(X_{t+1},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})+h_{W}(X_{t+1},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1})-h_{W}(X_{t+1},D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})

\displaystyle\mspace{20mu}-L(m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1})-m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{U}^{1/2}\big(m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1}\backslash D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})+m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\backslash D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1})\big)+2\lambda_{W}^{1/2}\big(m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1}\backslash D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})+m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}\backslash D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1})\big)

(47)

\displaystyle\mspace{20mu}-L(m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1}\backslash D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})-m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\backslash D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1})).

(48)

We can bound
m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1}\backslash D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})
and
m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}\backslash D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1})
in the last expression in terms of
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1}
and
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
.
By definition,

is disjoint from

, so it is not hard to see that

(49)

Moreover, by definition
m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})\geq\beta(1-m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}))-1/N

\displaystyle m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1}\backslash D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t}\backslash D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1})+m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t+1})-m(D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1}\backslash D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})-\beta(m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t+1})-m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}))+\frac{1}{N}.

such that

\displaystyle r_{\lambda^{*}}(s,0)-R^{\textup{rel}}+\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s,0,s^{\prime})f^{*}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq f^{*}(s)-\epsilon_{0}\quad\forall s\in S^{+}

(102)

\displaystyle r_{\lambda^{*}}(s,1)-R^{\textup{rel}}+\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s,1,s^{\prime})f^{*}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq f^{*}(s)-\epsilon_{0}\quad\forall s\in S^{-}.

(103)

Define the function

as

Q^{*}(s,a)=r_{\lambda^{*}}(s,a)-R^{\textup{rel}}+\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s,a,s^{\prime})f^{*}(s^{\prime}).

Then (
100
) implies that
f^{*}(s)\geq Q^{*}(s,a)
for any
s\in\mathbb{S},a\in\mathbb{A}
;
(
102
) and (
103
) imply that

is at least
\epsilon_{0}
for
s\in S^{+}
and at most
-\epsilon_{0}
for
s\in S^{-}
.

Now, we are ready to bound

: for any policy
\pi
and initial state vector
\bm{S}_{0}
,

\displaystyle R^{\textup{rel}}-R(\pi,\bm{S}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[R^{\textup{rel}}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}r_{\lambda^{*}}(S_{\infty}(i),A_{\infty}(i))\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\Big[R^{\textup{rel}}-r_{\lambda^{*}}(S_{\infty}(i),A_{\infty}(i))\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\Big[R^{\textup{rel}}-r_{\lambda^{*}}(S_{\infty}(i),A_{\infty}(i))-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}}P(S_{\infty}(i),A_{\infty}(i),s^{\prime})f^{*}(s^{\prime})+f^{*}(S_{\infty}(i))\Big]

(104)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\Big[-Q^{*}(S_{\infty}(i),A_{\infty}(i))+f^{*}(S_{\infty}(i))\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\epsilon_{0}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{S_{\infty}(i)\in S^{+},A_{\infty}(i)=0\right\}+\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{S_{\infty}(i)\in S^{-},A_{\infty}(i)=1\right\}\Big]

(105)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{0}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{s\in S^{+}}Y_{\infty}(s,0)+\sum_{s\in S^{-}}Y_{\infty}(s,1)\Big].

(106)

where to get (
104
), we use the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}}P(S_{\infty}(i),A_{\infty}(i),s^{\prime})f(s^{\prime})]=\mathbb{E}[f(S_{\infty}(i))]
for any function
f\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Next, we show

Lemma 13

•

•

We construct the subset

by removing a suitable subset of arms from

.

Next, we conduct Lyapunov analysis with the help of

\displaystyle U_{-}

Lemma 14
.

For any regular unstable RBs (

Proof.

To show the well-definedness

and

, note that the moduli of all eigenvalues of

Next, we show (
112
). Observe that

\displaystyle\Phi U_{+}\Phi^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big(VJJ_{+}^{\dagger k}V^{-1}\big)\big(VJJ_{+}^{\dagger k}V^{-1}\big)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\big(VJJ_{+}^{\dagger}V^{-1}\big)\big(VJJ_{+}^{\dagger}V^{-1}\big)^{\top}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\big(VJJ_{+}^{\dagger k}V^{-1}\big)\big(VJJ_{+}^{\dagger k}V^{-1}\big)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\big(VI_{+}V^{-1}\big)\big(VI_{+}V^{-1}\big)^{\top}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\big(VJ_{+}^{\dagger(k-1)}V^{-1}\big)\big(VJ_{+}^{\dagger(k-1)}V^{-1}\big)^{\top}

We claim that there exists a positive constant
\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}
such that for any

,

(116)

Without loss of generality, we only need to show (
116
) for

not being the zero vector. By normalization, it suffices to show that the function

\{v\colon\left\lVert v\big(VI_{+}V^{-1}\big)\right\rVert_{2}=1\}

Let

be the linear subspace spanned by rows of

’s diagonal.

and

.

Observe that

Next, we prove (

We claim that there exists a positive constant

it suffices to show that the function

Consider the decomposition

\displaystyle\Phi U_{-}\Phi^{\top}

C.3
Remarks on Property
(c)
of regular unstable RBs (

)

In this section, we prove an intuitive sufficient condition for Property
(c)
of regular unstable RBs and also provide a weaker version of this property.

We first state and prove the intuitive sufficient condition for Property
(c)
:

Lemma 17
.

Consider an RB instance with the single-armed MDP
(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{A},P,r)
, where
P(s,a,s^{\prime})>0
for all
s\in\mathbb{S},a\in\mathbb{A},s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
.
Then this RB instance satisfies Property
(c)
in
Definition

2
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, we fix
X_{t}
and
A_{t}
and omit them in the conditioning when writing expectations or probabilities.

Recalling the statement of Property
(c)
, it suffices to show that for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{S}|}
with
\lVert\xi\rVert_{2}=1
and
\mu^{*}\xi^{\top}=0
,
there exists a constant
K_{\textup{noise}}>0
such that
for any
N
larger than a constant and any time step
t
, if the local optimal policy is applicable, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\big\lvert\big(X_{t+1}([N])-\mu^{*}-(X_{t}([N])-\mu^{*})\Phi\big)\xi^{\top}\big\rvert\,\big|\,X_{t},\bm{A}_{t}\big]\geq\frac{K_{\textup{noise}}}{\sqrt{N}},

(139)

where
\bm{A}_{t}
follows the Optimal Local Control.
Note that in this case, the parameter
\eta^{\prime}
in Property
(c)
of
Definition

2
could be any positive number such that
\left\lVert X_{t}([N])-\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\eta^{\prime}
guarantees the condition (
7
) for applying the Optimal Local Control.

We first show that for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{S}|}
such that
\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{2}=1
and
\mu^{*}\xi^{\top}=0
,

\xi_{\max}-\xi_{\min}\geq 1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}.

(140)

where
\xi_{\max}=\max_{s\in\mathbb{S}}\xi(s)
and
\xi_{\min}=\min_{s\in\mathbb{S}}\xi(s)
.
Observe that we must have either
\xi_{\max}\geq 1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}
or
\xi_{\min}\geq-1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}
, because otherwise
\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{2}<1
.
We first show (
140
) for the case with
\xi_{\max}\geq 1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}
. In this case, we must have
\xi_{\min}\leq 1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}
, because otherwise
\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{2}>1
.
Let
\mathbbm{1}_{\textup{scaled}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{S}|}
be the whose entries are all
1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}
. Then

\left\lVert\xi-\mathbbm{1}_{\textup{scaled}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\max\big(\xi_{\max}-1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|},1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}-\xi_{\min}\big)\leq\xi_{\max}-\xi_{\min}.

On the other hand, because
\left\lVert\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{1}=1
and
\mu^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{\textup{scaled}}^{\top}=1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}
,

\left\lVert\xi-\mathbbm{1}_{\textup{scaled}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq\mu^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{\textup{scaled}}^{\top}-\mu^{*}\xi^{\top}=1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}.

Combining the last two inequalities yields (
140
). For the case with
\xi_{\min}\leq-1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}
, (
140
) can be proved in an analogous way.

To show (
139
), we invoke the Paley-Zygmund inequality to get

\displaystyle\mspace{23mu}\mathbb{P}\left[\big(X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}]\big)^{2}>\frac{1}{2}\text{Var}{\left[X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}\right]}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\text{Var}{\left[X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}\right]}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\big(X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}]\big)^{4}\right]}.

(141)

Next, we lower bound the second moment of
X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}]
in (
141
).
For any timestep
t
, given
X_{t},\bm{A}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{S}}X_{t+1}([N],s)\xi(s)

(142)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{S}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{\bm{S}_{t+1}(i)=s\right\}\xi(s)

(143)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\xi(\bm{S}_{t+1}(i)).

(144)

Note that conditioned on
X_{t},\bm{A}_{t}
,
\xi(\bm{S}_{t+1}(i))
are independent across
i
. Therefore, the variance of
X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}
is given by

\text{Var}{\left[X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}\right]}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\text{Var}{\left[\xi(\bm{S}_{t+1}(i))\right]}.

Because
P(s,a,s^{\prime})>0
for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S},a\in\mathbb{A}
, the support of
\bm{S}_{t+1}(i)
is the whole state space
\mathbb{S}
. Moreover, because
\xi_{\max}-\xi_{\min}>1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{S}|}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}{\left[\xi(\bm{S}_{t+1}(i))\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{s,a,s^{\prime}}P(s,a,s^{\prime})\Big((\xi_{\max}-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi(\bm{S}_{t+1}(i))\right])^{2}+(\xi_{\min}-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi(\bm{S}_{t+1}(i))\right])^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\min_{s,a,s^{\prime}}P(s,a,s^{\prime})(\xi_{\max}-\xi_{\min})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\min_{s,a,s^{\prime}}P(s,a,s^{\prime})}{2|\mathbb{S}|},

where
\min_{s,a,s^{\prime}}
is a shorthand for
\min_{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S},a\in\mathbb{A}}
.
Therefore,

\text{Var}{\left[X_{t+1}([N])\xi^{\top}\right]}\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\text{Var}{\left[\xi(\bm{S}_{t+1}(i))\right]}\geq\frac{\min_{s,a,s^{\prime}}P(s,a,s^{\prime})}{2|\mathbb{S}|N}.

(145)

Next, we upper bound the fourth moment of

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{z}-m\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{U}\leq\eta m-\frac{|S^{\emptyset}|+1}{N}-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{rd}}

(159b)

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathbb{S}}z(s)=m

(159c)

\displaystyle 0\leq z(s)\leq X_{t}(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t-1},s)\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{S}.

(159d)

We define an intermediate set
D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}
based on the outcome of solving this SOCP. If this SOCP is infeasible, we let
D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}=\emptyset
.
If the SOCP is feasible, let the optimal solution be
(\bm{z}^{*},m^{*})
. We let the set
D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}
consist of
\left\lfloor Nz^{*}(s)\right\rfloor
arms with state
s
, for each
s\in\mathbb{S}
.
One can verify that the set can be chosen as a subset of
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t-1}
because
z^{*}(s)\leq X_{t}(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t-1},s)
.
Also, the constraint (
159b
) ensures that
D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}
satisfies
\delta(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}})\geq 0
, following a similar argument as before.

After obtaining
D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}
, we solve the second SOCP given by

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{z},\,m}{\text{maximize}}\mspace{12mu}

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathbb{S}}z(s)

(160a)

subject to

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{z}-m\mu^{*}\right\rVert_{U}\leq\eta m-\frac{|S^{\emptyset}|+1}{N}-\epsilon_{N}^{\text{rd}}

(160b)

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathbb{S}}z(s)=m

(160c)

\displaystyle X_{t}(D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}},s)\leq z(s)\leq X_{t}([N],s)\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{S}.

(160d)

If this SOCP is infeasible, we let
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}=D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}
.
We argue that
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
is an
\epsilon_{N}^{\text{rd}}
-maximal feasible set:
first, because
\delta(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}})\geq 0
or
D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}=\emptyset
, the same holds for
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
;
then observe that for any
D^{\prime}
such that
D^{\prime}\supseteq D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}=D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}
and
\delta(X_{t},D^{\prime})\geq\epsilon_{N}^{\text{rd}}
,
(X_{t}(D^{\prime}),m(D^{\prime}))
satisfies the constraint of the SOCP, so such
D^{\prime}
does not exist by the infeasibility, which implies that
D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}
is
\epsilon_{N}^{\text{rd}}
-maximal feasible.

If this SOCP is feasible, we let its optimal solution be
(\bm{z}^{*},m^{*})
. We let the set
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
consist of
\left\lfloor Nz^{*}(s)\right\rfloor
arms with state
s
, for each
s\in\mathbb{S}
.
The constraint (
160b
) ensures that
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
satisfies
\delta(X_{t},D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})\geq 0
, following a similar argument as before.
Moreover, for any set
D^{\prime}\supseteq D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\supseteq D^{\textup{OL}}_{\textup{temp}}
and such that
\delta(X_{t},D_{t})\geq\epsilon_{N}^{\text{rd}}
,
(X_{t}(D^{\prime}),m(D^{\prime}))
satisfies the constraints of this SOCP, so
m(D^{\prime})\leq m^{*}\leq m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})+|\mathbb{S}|/N\leq m(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t})+\epsilon_{N}^{\text{rd}}
. Therefore,
D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}
is
\epsilon_{N}^{\text{rd}}
-maximal feasible.

D.1.3
Deciding actions for the arms in
(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\cup D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})^{c}

In our implementation of the two-set policy, we choose the actions for the arms in
(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\cup D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})^{c}
following similar ideas as the ID policy in
[
13
]
.
Specifically, we generate a random ideal action
\widehat{A}_{t}(i)
and temporarily set
A_{t}(i)=\widehat{A}_{t}(i)
for each
i\in(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\cup D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})^{c}
. If

\sum_{i\in[N]}A_{t}(i)>\alpha N,

we change
A_{t}(i)
to
0
for
i\in(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\cup D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})^{c}
starting from large
i
’s to small
i
’s, until
\sum_{i\in[N]}A_{t}(i)=\alpha N
.
Similarly, if

\sum_{i\in[N]}A_{t}(i)<\alpha N,

we change
A_{t}(i)
to
1
for
i\in(D^{\textup{OL}}_{t}\cup D^{{{\bar{\pi}}^{*}}}_{t})^{c}
starting from large
i
’s to small
i
’s, until
\sum_{i\in[N]}A_{t}(i)=\alpha N
. As guaranteed by
Lemma

6
, we can always achieve
\sum_{i\in[N]}A_{t}(i)=\alpha N
through this process.

D.2.1
Definitions of the instances in
Figure

1

Here, we provide some details on the RB instances simulated in
Figure

1(a)
and
Figure

1(b)
.

The RB instance corresponding to
Figure

1(a)
is defined in
[
13
]
. We repeat its definition below for completeness.
This RB isntance is defined by a single-armed MDP with state space
\mathbb{S}=\{0,1,\ldots,7\}
.
Each state in
\mathbb{S}
is labelled by a
preferred action
, which is chosen to be action
1
for states
\{0,1,2,3\}
and action
0
for states
\{4,5,6,7\}
.
If an arm is in state
s
and takes the preferred action, it moves to state
(s+1)\bmod 8
with probability
p_{s,\text{R}}
, and stays in state
s
otherwise; if it does not take the preferred action, it moves to state
(s-1)^{+}
with probability
p_{s,\text{L}}
;
here the probabilities
p_{s,\text{R}}=0.1
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
, and
p_{s,\text{L}}
is defined as

\displaystyle p_{s,\text{L}}=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{ if }s=0,1\\
0.5-0.1s/8\quad&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

The reward function is defined as
r(7,0)=0.1
,
r(0,1)=1/300
, and
r(s,a)=0
for all the other
s\in\mathbb{S},a\in\mathbb{A}
.
The budget parameter
\alpha=0.45
.
We initialize the states to be uniformly distributed over
\{4,5,6,7\}
.

Here we briefly comment on the rationale of constructing this example. The main purpose of this transition structure is to let the LP index policy use a suboptimal priority order.
Specifically, the LP index policy in this example prioritizes state
3
over state
5
, and state
5
over state
4
.
Thus, if at a certain time step, all arms are in states
\{3,4,5\}
with a suitable distribution, all arms in state
3
and
5
will be activated. Consequently, the arms in
3
or
5
will return to state
4
, so all arms are stuck in these three states without getting any reward.

Apart from the transition structure, the value of the reward function
r(0,1)
is also specially chosen to make the problem non-indexable. The budget
\alpha
is chosen to be less than
0.5
, so that state
0
is the neutral state.

The RB instance in
Figure

1(b)
is constructed with similar ideas. The single-armed MDP of this instance has the state space
\mathbb{S}=\{0,1,2,\dots,12\}
; the preferred actions are
1
for states
\{0,1,\dots,5\}
, and are
0
for the rest of the states. The transition rules after taking the preferred (non-preferred) action are the same as the last example, with
p_{\cdot,L}
adjusted to

p_{s,\text{L}}=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{ if }s=0,1\\
0.5-0.1s/12\quad&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

We let the reward function be defined as
r(7,0)=0.1
and
r(0,1)=1/300
, and
r(s,a)=0
for all the other
s\in\mathbb{S},a\in\mathbb{A}
.
We let the budget parameter
\alpha=0.4
.
We initialize the states to be uniformly distributed over
\{6,7,8,9,10,11\}
.
In this example, the LP index policy prioritizes state
5
over state
7
, and state
7
over state
6
, so intuitively, the arms could be stuck on these three states.

One can potentially generalize the above two examples, and construct more RB instances that satisfy Assumptions
1
–
3
, but violate UGAP under the Whittle index policy and the LP index policy.
