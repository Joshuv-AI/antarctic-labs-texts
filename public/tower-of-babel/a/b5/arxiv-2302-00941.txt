Robust Multi-Item Auction Design Using Statistical Learning: Overcoming Uncertainty in Bidders’ Types Distributions
Jiale Han jialehan@ucla.eduDepartment of Statistics, University of California, Los Angeles, Xiaowu Dai dai@stat.ucla.eduDepartment of Statistics, University of California, Los Angeles

Abstract
This paper presents a novel mechanism design for multi-item auction settings with uncertain bidders’ type distributions. Our proposed approach utilizes nonparametric density estimation to accurately estimate bidders’ types from historical bids, and is built upon the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism, ensuring satisfaction of Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC) and δ \delta -individual rationality (IR). To further enhance the efficiency of our mechanism, we introduce two novel strategies for query reduction: a filtering method that screens potential winners’ value regions within the confidence intervals generated by our estimated distribution, and a classification strategy that designates the lower bound of an interval as the estimated type when the length is below a threshold value. Simulation experiments conducted on both small-scale and large-scale data demonstrate that our mechanism consistently outperforms existing methods in terms of revenue maximization and query reduction, particularly in large-scale scenarios. This makes our proposed mechanism a highly desirable and effective option for sellers in the realm of multi-item auctions.

Introduction

Designing optimal-revenue auctions is a fundamental problem in economics and computer science, with applications in a wide range of fields such as e-commerce, online advertising, and spectrum auctions. While Myerson’s celebrated work
(
Myerson 1981
)
provided optimal design for single-item auctions, assuming knowledge of bidders’ type distributions, the problem becomes increasingly complex in the multi-item case. The optimal mechanism may involve bundling items and providing a menu of options for the buyer
(
Daskalakis et al. 2015
)
, leading to a menu-size complexity that scales exponentially with the number of items
(
Hart et al. 2013
;
Dughmi et al. 2014
;
Daskalakis 2015
)
. Additionally, the information available to sellers is limited as bidders’ type distributions are private, and the only information that bidders can provide is public historical data of their bids.
To complicate matters further, the process of communicating or querying bidders before an auction can be time-consuming and expensive when the number of bidders and items is large.

To address these challenges, there are
various methods proposed to alleviate the analytical and computational intractability of multi-item mechanism design, including
Alaei 2014
,
Cai and Daskalakis 2011
,
Alaei et al. 2012
, and
Cai and Huang 2013
. However, these methods typically assume knowledge of the bidders’ types distribution, which may not be available in practice. Recently, there has been some progress in the development of mechanisms that are robust to errors in the distribution of bidders’ types, such as
Cai and Daskalakis 2017
,
Brustle et al. 2020
, and
Cai and Daskalakis 2022
. However, there are currently no practical algorithms that utilize historical data to estimate bidders’ types. In this paper, we propose a novel learning method for multi-item optimal auction design that utilizes historical data to estimate bidders’ types while making fewer queries than existing methods, resulting in a more efficient and practical solution for multi-item auctions.

Our proposed method leverages nonparametric density estimation to estimate bidders’ types from their historical bids’ data. This approach can take into account the possibility of bids that have not been made before.
This contrasts with previous approaches that either rely on a pre-specified distribution. One of the key contributions of our work is the introduction of two new strategies for reducing the number of queries. First, we propose a novel filtering method for selecting potential winners by screening their value regions within the confidence intervals generated by our estimated distribution, while ensuring fairness in the auction. Second, we classify intervals based on their length and designate the lower bound of an interval as the estimated type when the length is below a threshold value. This strategy effectively reduces the number of potential winners, resulting in a more efficient algorithm.
We also provide a new mechanism design that is based on the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism and is able to satisfy Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC) and
\delta
-individual rationality (IR). Furthermore, we prove that the revenue obtained from our mechanism is only slightly lower than the true revenue, with a high probability.
Overall, our proposed mechanism for multi-item auctions offers a significant improvement over existing methods in terms of revenue maximization and query reduction, making it a highly effective and desirable option for sellers.

To demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed mechanism, we conducted simulations utilizing both small-scale and large-scale data. The results of our simulations reveal that our proposed mechanism consistently outperforms existing methods.
Specifically, we compared our mechanism to two other mechanisms: one that did not utilize a density function and another that did not filter the data. Our mechanism achieved a higher expected revenue compared to the method that did not utilize a density function and made half the number of queries with a high confidence rate when compared to the method that did not filter the data.
Furthermore, our mechanism demonstrated particularly strong performance in large-scale data scenarios, particularly when the number of bidders was greater than the number of items or vice versa. The final reduced revenue was also found to be minimal, indicating that our method effectively queried the true values of most winners, which is desirable for sellers.

1.1
Related Work

We review some of the related work from multiple literatures, including optimal auction, multi-item auction, robust mechanism design, and learning in mechanism design.

Optimal Auction.

The seminal work of Myerson
(
Myerson 1981
)
laid the foundation for optimal auction design in single-item settings, assuming knowledge of bidders’ type distributions. However, the extension of this framework to multi-item scenarios poses significant challenges, as counterintuitive properties may arise that do not present in single-item settings
(
Daskalakis 2015
)
. There are studies showing that the use of Myerson’s optimal auction or VCG with entry fees, as proposed in
Yao 2014
;
Cai et al. 2016
, can achieve revenue at least
1/8
of the optimal revenue when bidders have additive valuations. In this paper, we adopt the VCG mechanism and selling items separately, while providing a lower bound on the revenue. The revelation principle, as stated in
Myerson 1981
, suggests that achieving optimal design requires a round of direct communication in which bidders simultaneously and confidentially announce their value estimates to the seller. Therefore, to design a mechanism that maximizes revenue, the seller must make some queries to the bidders.

Multi-item Auction

In the realm of multi-item auctions, the optimal mechanism for selling items can vary greatly depending on the distribution of bidders’ types. Grand-bundling mechanisms, characterized in
Daskalakis et al. 2015
, have been shown to be optimal in certain situations, while a menu of options may be more suitable in cases where bidders’ types are not independently and identically distributed. However, the complexity of such mechanisms can become intractable as the number of items increases, as demonstrated in
Dughmi et al. 2014
and
Babaioff et al. 2017
. In recent years, a plethora of strategies have been proposed to alleviate these analytical and computational difficulties, including
Alaei 2014
;
Cai and Daskalakis 2011
;
Alaei et al. 2012
;
Cai and Huang 2013
;
Cai et al. 2012a
;
Cai et al. 2012b
;
Chawla et al. 2007
;
Chawla et al. 2010
;
Cai et al. 2013
;
Morgenstern and Roughgarden 2016
;
Goldner and Karlin 2016
. However, the majority of these methods require knowledge of the true distribution of bidders’ types, which may not be available in practice. Instead, we may only have access to an estimated distribution that is close to the true distribution.

Robust Mechanism Design.

The presence of private and unknown bidder types has motivated a substantial body of research on robust mechanism design. Studies in this area have sought to robustify mechanisms to errors in the distribution of bidder valuations, without relying on Bayesian assumptions. Some notable examples include
Cai and Daskalakis 2017
,
Cai and Daskalakis 2022
,
Brustle et al. 2020
and
Bergemann and Schlag 2011
, which have all aimed to design mechanisms that achieve optimal revenue with only approximate distributions of bidder types.
In our work, we propose a novel approach to learning approximate distributions of bidder types based on historical data, and design a mechanism that is able to achieve high revenue with minimal communication between the seller and bidders. Our method is both efficient and easy to implement, making it a promising avenue for future research in this area.

Learning in Mechanism Design.

The application of machine learning techniques to the automated design of mechanisms has been a key area of research at the intersection of economics and computing
(
Conitzer and Sandholm 2002
)
. Recent advancements in the field have seen the use of neural networks to optimize revenue in multi-item settings. For example,
Feng et al. 2018
employs deep learning to discover novel, high-revenue auctions for multi-unit settings with private budgets, while
Shen et al. 2019
presents a framework that combines a mechanism network and a buyer network.
Dütting et al. 2019
also provides an end-to-end approach for solving the multi-item auction design problem. However, these approaches often require prior knowledge of the bidders’ type distribution or rely on inputting individual bidder type profiles
(
Shen et al. 2019
;
Dütting et al. 2019
;
Feng et al. 2018
)
.
Besides, there is a growing body of works discussing machine learning in market designs
(
Dai and Jordan 2021a
;
Dai and Jordan 2021b
;
Dai et al. 2022
)
.
Our work, on the other hand, utilizes historical data to learn the bidders’ type distribution without the need for explicit queries to individual bidders for their type profiles.

Contributions and Outline

In this work, we present a novel mechanism for achieving high revenue through selective querying of a limited number of bidders’ types, utilizing historical data. Our primary methodological and theoretical contributions are as follows.

•

We construct an estimated distribution
\widehat{D}
from historical data through the utilization of kernel density estimation, as detailed in Section
3.1
. We generate high-confidence intervals for bidders’ types

through rejection sampling from the estimated distribution

•

Our approach reduces the number of queries by introducing two new strategies based on the confidence intervals obtained from historical data. First, we propose a novel filtering method for selecting potential winners by screening their value regions within the confidence intervals, while ensuring fairness in the auction (Section

•

We provide three algorithms for obtaining the concrete formula of the estimated bidders’ types

),

).

•

In order to demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed mechanism, we conduct comprehensive simulation studies (Section

m
, is either larger or smaller than the number of queries,
N
. Notably, our mechanism is able to achieve a high revenue while reducing the number of queries by nearly half.

Problem Formulation

In this section, we introduce key definitions and notations. Specifically, we aim to formulate a mechanism design strategy for multi-item auctions with additive valuations that maximizes the expected revenue while ensuring incentive compatibility.

2.1
Preliminaries

We consider a scenario in which a seller aims to auction off
N\geq 1
items to a set of
m>1
bidders, denoted by
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{m}\}
. The items can be sold separately or in bundles
Cai and Daskalakis 2017
.
Each bidder
B_{i}
is characterized by a type
t_{i}=(t_{i1},t_{i2},\dots,t_{iN})
, which represents the bidder’s private valuations for the
N
items. We assume that each
t_{i}
is drawn from a private distribution
D_{i}
, with the set of distributions being represented by
D=(D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{m})
.

Valuation.
For a given bundle
S\subset[N]={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\{}1,2,\dots,N{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\}}
, a bidder
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B_{i}}
’s value for
S
is given by the additive valuation function as
Goldner and Karlin 2016
,

v_{i}(t_{i},S)=\sum_{j\in S}t_{ij}.

Auction.

An auction mechanism
M
is a mapping from the space of bidders’ types
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{m})
to the space of allocations
A(t)
and prices
P(t)=(P_{1}(t),P_{2}(t),\dots,P_{N}(t))
, where
P_{i}(t)
denotes the price of item
i
given bidders’ types
t
.
Since the bidders’ types follow the distribution
D
, the goal is to design a mechanism for the seller that maximum her revenue,

\mathbb{E}_{t\sim D}\left[\sum_{j\in[N]}P_{j}(t)\right].

Given a multi-item auction mechanism
M
, the utility of a bidder
B_{i}
with type
t_{i}
upon winning a bundle of items
S
at a price
P(t,S){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\sum_{j\in S}P_{j}(t)}
is formulated as

u_{i}(t_{i},M(t))=v_{i}(t_{i},S)-P(t,S).

Bayesian Incentive Compatibility.

To ensure truthfulness, we must impose incentive compatibility constraints. We adopt the definition of Bayesian Incentive Compatibility (BIC)
(
Cai and Daskalakis 2022
, e.g.,)
.

Definition 1

(BIC)
.

Consider a mechanism
M
and a distribution
D=(D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{m})
of bidders’ types. We say that
M
is BIC with respect to
D
if, for each bidder
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B_{i}}}
,
i\in[m]
, the expected utility of truthfully reporting one’s type,
\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t_{-i}}\sim D_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-i}}}[u_{i}(t_{i},M(t_{i},t_{-i}))]
, is no less than the expected utility of reporting any alternative type
t^{\prime}_{i}
. That is,

\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t_{-i}}\sim D_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-i}}}[u_{i}(t_{i},M(t_{i},t_{-i}))]\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t_{-i}}\sim D_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-i}}}[u_{i}(t_{i},M(t^{\prime}_{i},t_{-i}))],

where the expectation is taken over other bidders’ types
t_{-i}
.

Individual Rationality.

Next, we introduce a novel generalization of the concept of individual rationality (IR), denoted as
\delta
-IR, which is different from the traditional definitions in the literature. The introduction of
\delta
-IR is motivated by our proposed mechanism in Section
3
, which utilizes a method of estimating bidders’ types based on the lower bound of their corresponding confidence intervals.

Definition 2

(
\delta
-IR)
.

A mechanism
M
is
\delta
-individual rationality (
\delta
-IR) if for all types
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{m})
and for all bidder
i\in[m]
,

\Pr\left\{u_{i}(t_{i},M({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{t}}))\geq 0\right\}\geq 1-\delta.

A mechanism is IR if
\delta=0
, which means for all types
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{m})
and for all bidder
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B_{i}}}
,
i\in[m]
, we have

u_{i}(t_{i},M({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{t}}))\geq 0
with probability
1
.

VCG Mechanism.

In this paper, we propose a novel mechanism that builds upon the foundation of the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism. Given an allocation method
A(t)
, the VCG mechanism operates by allocating the bundle of items in accordance with
A(t)
to the bidder who expresses the highest valuation for the bundle and charges them the value of the second highest bid. Mathematically, the value of a bundle
S
for a given bidder
B_{i}
is represented by
v_{i}(t_{i},S)=\sum_{j\in[S]}\hat{t}_{ij}
, where

\hat{t}_{ij}
is the bid of bidder
B_{j}
to item
j
.
The winner of the bundle
S
is determined as

i_{*}:=\arg\max\limits_{i}\sum_{j\in[S]}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{t}}}_{ij},

(1)

and the winner is subsequently charged with

\max_{i,i\neq i_{*}}\sum_{j\in[S]}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{t}}}_{ij}
.
It is well-known that truthful reporting is a dominant strategy for each bidder in a VCG mechanism
(
Ausubel et al. 2006
, e.g.,)
. This implies that, when using a VCG mechanism and soliciting bids from bidders, it can be expected that they will rationally provide truthful valuations, i.e.
\hat{t}_{ij}=t_{ij}
for any bidder
B_{i}
and item
j
.

This feature greatly simplifies the estimation of bidders’ types, as any queries made to bidders will yield accurate responses, leaving the need for estimation solely on the types that were not queried.
The VCG mechanism has also been widely recognized for its desirable properties of IR and BIC
(
Clarke 1971
;
Che and Yoo 2001
;
Vickrey 1961
)
.

2.2
Goal

Obtaining information from bidders is a fundamental element in the design of mechanisms, as it has been established that without it, meaningful design is unattainable
(
Cai and Daskalakis 2022
)
. The seller can acquire such information through two means: either by analyzing historical data or by directly querying the bidders.

First, the utilization of historical data presents a cost-effective approach to obtaining information on bidders, despite the potential limitations of the data. Specifically, prior to the auction, we gather historical bids for all items made by each bidder from publicly available market data and use this information to perform estimations.
For a given item
j
, let
n_{ij}
represent the number of times bidder
B_{i}
has bid on that item. This results in a dataset
\Gamma_{ij}
containing
n_{ij}
data points.
When all
m
bidders’ historical bid data for the
N
items is obtained, we can estimate each bidder
B_{i}
’s private type distribution
D_{i}
to inform the mechanism design. The estimated distribution for bidder
B_{i}
is represented as
\widehat{D}_{i}
, and the set of all estimated distributions is denoted as
\widehat{D}=\{\widehat{D}_{1},\widehat{D}_{2},\dots,\widehat{D}_{m}\}
.
Since the bidders’ types are private, obtaining the true set of distributions
D
is not possible. The goal is to ensure that the distance between the estimated distribution
\widehat{D}
and the true distribution
{D}
is minimized. Mathematically, as motivated by Prokhorov Metric
(
Strassen 1965
, e.g.,)
, we want to ensure that

\Pr_{(\hat{t},t)\sim(\widehat{D},D)}[\|\hat{t}-t\|_{\infty}>\epsilon]\leq\gamma,

(2)

for some small values of
\epsilon,\gamma>0
. To achieve this, appropriate sampling methods must be employed to generate accurate estimates
\hat{t}
, and strategies must be devised to control the distance between the estimated and true distributions while also maximizing revenue. This requires the mechanism to be robust to estimation errors.

Second, we can implement a query protocol to obtain bidders’ information, which involves the exchange of information with bidders over possibly several rounds. While this method may provide more precise information, the cost may be relatively high. One common query protocol is to ask bidders if they are willing to pay at least a certain value for winning an item
(
Cai and Daskalakis 2022
)
. However, this method may be susceptible to truth-telling issues, which can negatively impact the seller’s revenue. To mitigate this, it is crucial to implement a mechanism with properties such as BIC and IR, which encourage truthful reporting and thus, maximize revenue.
The use of query protocols presents a trade-off between the number of queries and the amount of information obtained. In cases where the number of items is large, the number of queries required may be prohibitively high.
Conversely, a reduction in the number of queries may result in a decrease in the revenue obtained.

In this paper, we propose a multi-item mechanism that utilizes historical data to reduce the number of queries while also maximizing the revenue for the seller.

Main Results

In this section, we present the main results of this paper.
We begin by introducing a method for estimating the distribution and constructing confidence intervals for bidders’ types in Section
3.1
. Next, we propose a novel strategy for reducing the number of queries in Section
3.2
. We then provide a mechanism that concretely embodies the estimated bidders’ types in Section
3.3
and demonstrate a lower bound for the revenue of this mechanism in Section
3.4
. Finally, in Section
3.5
, we discuss the selection of a key parameter in our mechanism.

3.1
Estimation of Distribution and Confidence Intervals for Bidders’ Types

In order to estimate each individual bidder’s private valuations, we collect historical bid data for each item from each bidder
B_{i}
. Let the dataset of previous bids for bidder
B_{i}
on item
j
be denoted as
\Gamma_{ij}
, which consists of
n_{ij}
independent and identically distributed samples,
\Gamma_{ij}\equiv\{x_{ij1},x_{ij2},\ldots,x_{ijn_{ij}}\}
.

Kernel Density Estimate.
With the data
\Gamma_{ij}
, we can perform kernel density estimation of
D_{ij}
using the function
\widehat{D}_{ij}
, as defined by:

\widehat{D}_{ij}(x)=\frac{1}{{n_{ij}}h}\sum^{{n_{ij}}}_{s=1}K\left(\frac{x-x_{ijs}}{h}\right).

Here,
K
represents a density function known as the kernel, and
h
is the bandwidth. This method, which dates back to the works of Rosenblatt (1956) and Parzen (1962), commonly utilizes a Gaussian kernel,
K(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}
. A choice of the bandwidth is
h=1.06\cdot\min{\bigl\{\hat{\sigma},\frac{IQR}{1.34}}\bigr\}\cdot{n_{ij}^{-1/5}}
, where
\hat{\sigma}
is the sample standard deviation,
n_{ij}
denotes the sample size, and
IQR
represents the sample interquartile range. This choice of
h
is known to be robust against outliers
(
Scott 1992
)
, and is minimax optimal for estimation
(
Zambom and Ronaldo 2013
)
with the mean integrated square error (MISE) of
\mathbb{E}\int[\widehat{D}_{ij}(x)-D_{ij}(x)]^{2}dx=O(n_{ij}^{-4/5})
.

Rejection Sampling
:
In order to sample from the estimated density, we can utilize the rejection sampling method. This involves selecting random samples from a given distribution,
g(x)
, and choosing a constant
c
such that

cg(x)>\widehat{D}_{ij}(x),\quad\forall x.

Then, we accept the sample
x
with a probability of
\widehat{D}_{ij}(x)/cg(x)
, resulting in a sampling process equivalent to that from
\widehat{D}_{ij}(x)
as described in
Casella et al. 2004
. In our scenario, for each item
j\in[N]
, we can define the support of the density
\widehat{D}_{ij}
to be within the known minimum and maximum values,
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
, respectively, which may be set by the seller or obtained from the market. Thus, we can set
g(x)
as a uniform distribution over the interval
[a_{j},b_{j}]
.

We repeat this sampling process
\mathcal{N}
times, and compute the
(\alpha/2)
th
and (
1-\alpha/2
)th
quantiles of the samples as the lower and upper bounds of the
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval of
t_{ij}
. As the MISE of
\hat{D}_{ij}
is
O(n_{ij}^{-4/5})
, we can assert that when
n_{ij}
is large enough, the difference between
\widehat{D}_{ij}
and
D
is negligible, resulting in high precision of the confidence interval. We provide a detailed outline of this procedure in Algorithm
1
, which is deferred to Section
4.1
.

3.2
Winnow Down Potential Winners

We now introduce a novel strategy that significantly reduces the number of queries while ensuring fairness in the game by narrowing down the potential winners.
We begin by defining the fairness of the mechanism.

Definition 3

(
\alpha
-fairness)
.

Given an item
j\in[N]
and the winner of that item,
B_{i*}
, a mechanism is
\alpha
-fair if, with probability at least
1-\alpha
, for all
i\in[m],i\neq i_{*}
, it holds true that
t_{ij}<t_{{i_{*}}j}
.

This definition draws inspiration from
Joseph et al. 2016
, in which fairness is defined through pairwise comparison. In other words, a fair mechanism assigns higher probabilities of winning to bidders with higher values. In our scenario, we only need to consider the winner, as the outcome is unique.
Based on the definition of
\alpha
-fairness, it is clear that a smaller
\alpha
value corresponds to a fairer game.

From Section
3.1
, we assume that we have already obtained
\widehat{D}_{ij},\forall i\in[m],j\in[N],
and the
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval
T_{ij}=[\hat{t}_{ijL},\hat{t}_{ijU}]
for each bidder
B_{i}
’s type for item
j
, based on historical data. This means that with probability more than
(1-\alpha)
, the true value
t_{ij}
is within the interval
[\hat{t}_{ijL},\hat{t}_{ijU}]
.
For each item
j
, we identify the bidder
B_{i_{*}^{j}}
with the largest
upper confidence interval
among all bidders,

i_{*}^{j}=\arg\max\limits_{i\in[m]}\hat{t}_{ijU}.

(3)

This bidder has the potential to have the highest type, and is therefore more likely to win the item. However, other bidders whose estimated intervals overlap with
B_{i^{j}}
’s may also have a chance to win. To account for this scenerio, we define a new interval relationship. For each item
j
, we consider bidders
B_{i}
and
B_{i^{{}^{\prime}}}
to be
linked
if

[\hat{t}_{ijL},\hat{t}_{ijU})\cap(\hat{t}_{i^{{}^{\prime}}jL},\hat{t}_{i^{{}^{\prime}}jU}]\neq\emptyset.

(4)

In order to mitigate the computational complexity associated with determining the winner of a multi-item auction, we propose a mechanism that selectively considers bids from a subset of bidders for each item. Specifically, for each item
j\in[N]
, we only consider the bids from bidders that are linked to the bidder with the highest upper confidence bound for item
j
, as defined by
\mathcal{B}_{j}=\{B_{i}|i\in[m],B_{i}\text{ is linked to }B_{i_{*}^{j}}\text{ for item }j\}
. Here
i_{*}^{j}
and the linked relation are defined in Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
), respectively. The algorithm of constructing the set
\mathcal{B}_{j}
is given in Section
4.2
.
We show that this mechanism is
\alpha
-fair for each item
j\in[N]
when
\alpha
is small enough.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{j}
denote the set of bidders linked to the bidder with the highest upper confidence bound for item
j
, as defined by
\mathcal{B}_{j}=\{B_{i}|i\in[m],B_{i}\text{ is linked to }B_{i_{*}^{j}}\text{ for item }j\}
. Then for each item
j\in[N]
, considering only bidders in the set
\mathcal{B}_{j}
results in a mechanism that is
\alpha
-fair.

The proof of this proposition is given in Appendix
A.3
. Proposition
4
shows that for each item
j\in[N]
, if we restrict our attention to the subset of bidders
\mathcal{B}_{j}
, the mechanism is
\alpha
-fair. This implies that by limiting our queries to the bidders in
\mathcal{B}_{j}
, we can achieve a reduction in the number of required queries while maintaining a desired level of fairness.

Note that it becomes difficult to sell items in a bundle, as the total value of a bundle would be the sum of the bidders’ types for each item, which cannot be calculated with ignored bids.
Moreover, the allocation space
A(t)
that the mechanism needs to search for becomes increasingly large as the number of items increases. As an example, we calculate the lower bound of the complexity of allocation when each bundle has at most 2 items.

Proposition 5
.

When each bundle has at most 2 items, the complexity of the allocation grows exponentially as a function of the number of items. Specifically, the number of potential allocations exceeds
(2N/3)^{\lfloor N/3\rfloor}
.

The proof of Proposition
5
is provided in Appendix
A.4
. Hence the number of allocations increases exponentially as a function of the number of items.
In this paper, we consider that the seller sells each item separately, which gives a practical solution to alleviate the computational complexity associated with determining the allocation of items in a bundle.

3.3
Lower Confidence Bound Method

In this section, we present a mechanism for obtaining a concrete form of the estimated bidders’ type
\hat{t}
. As previously established in Section
2.1
, the use of the VCG mechanism guarantees truthful reporting of bidders’ values for each item. However, in practice, obtaining these values through a large number of queries can be costly. The strategy outlined in Section
3.2
provides a solution to this problem. We now propose an additional approach to further reduce the number of queries.

We note in Section
2.2
that our aim is to ensure Eq. (
2
) for small values of
\epsilon
and
\gamma
. In order to maintain
\delta
-IR, we want to minimize the estimated types.
To achieve this, we utilize the lower bound of the confidence interval as a replacement for queries. Specifically, for any bidder
B_{i},i\in[m]
and item
j\in[N]
, we let
\hat{t}_{ijL}
be the designated estimated type, such that

\Pr(\hat{t}_{ijL}-t_{ij}>0)\leq\alpha/2,

which satisfies Eq. (
2
) with
\epsilon=0
and
\gamma=\alpha/2
. Additionally, we define
d_{ij}=\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}
as the length of the interval. Then, we have

\Pr(t_{ij}-\hat{t}_{ijL}>d_{ij})\leq\alpha/2.

Thus, the length of the intervals
d_{ij}
becomes a crucial factor in determining the accuracy of the estimated type
\hat{t}_{ijL}
.

Using the lower bound of the confidence interval balances the trade-off between seller revenue and estimation precision.
To implement this approach, we first specify a threshold value
d>0
and consider the number of intervals,
(\hat{t}_{ijL},\hat{t}_{ijU})
, for each bidder
B_{i}
, whose bandwidth is less than
d
. Specifically, we define

L_{i}=\{j|\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}\leq d,j\in[N]\},

(5)

with
n_{i}=|L_{i}|
representing the number of such intervals for bidder
B_{i}
. Additionally, we also define the set of items for which the estimated types are not well-controlled, denoted as
Q_{i}=\{j|\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}>d,j\in[N]\}
, with
|Q_{i}|=N-n_{i}
.

For each element in
Q_{i}
, we solicit the bidder’s true type through a query under the VCG mechanism, which guarantees truthful reporting. These types are designated as
type I
estimated types.
On the other hand, for the items in the set
L_{i}
, we have a well-controlled estimate of the bidder’s type, and therefore we use the lower bound
\hat{t}_{ijL}
as the estimate, i.e.
\hat{t}_{ij}=\hat{t}_{ijL}
. This way, we have

\Pr(\hat{t}_{ij}-t_{ij}<0)=1-\alpha/2\ \text{ and }\ \Pr(|\hat{t}_{ij}-t_{ij}|>d)\leq\alpha/2.

Hence with probability more than
1-\alpha/2
, we have
\hat{t}_{ij}<t_{ij}
, and with probability
1-\alpha/2
, the distance between
t_{ij}
and
\hat{t}_{ij}
is not larger than
d
. With probability
(1-\alpha/2)^{n_{i}}
, for every
j\in[N]
, we have
\hat{t}_{ij}<t_{ij}
. We refer to this type of estimated type as
type II
estimated types.

In summary, we have the set of type I estimated types,
Q=\{\hat{t}_{i,j}|i\in[m],j\in Q_{i}\}
, and the set of type II estimated type,

L=\{\hat{t}_{i,j}|i\in[m],j\in L_{i}\}.

(6)

We define the parameter
n
as,

n=\sum_{i\in[m]}n_{i}.

(7)

Then
|Q|=mN-n
and
|L|=n
.
We demonstrate the validity of our proposed mechanism by showing that it satisfies two important properties: BIC and
\delta
-IR.

Theorem 6
.

The mechanism obtained by applying VCG to the estimated bidders’ types
\hat{t}
is BIC and
\delta
-IR with
\delta=\alpha n/2
.

The proof of Theorem
6
is presented in Appendix
A.1
. It relies on the well-established BIC and IR properties of the VCG mechanism, and the uncertainty in IR is introduced by our use of type II estimated types, where we designate types without making inquiries to the bidders.
Theorem
6
implies that our proposed mechanism is practically viable. The only challenge is to keep
\alpha n
as small as possible to ensure that bidders are willing to participate in the auction. To make
\alpha
small, we can use historical data or sample more extensively from the estimated distribution to obtain a more precise confidence interval for
t
.

3.4
Analysis of Revenue

For any bidder
B_{i}
, we have the set of type II estimated types
L_{i}
, which is defined in Eq. (
5
). Let

L_{i}^{*}:=\{j|\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}>0,j\in[N]\}
,
and

(8)

Let

be the number of items in set

Only the items in

may have reduced revenue, as the type I estimated types are equal to true types and will not impact the final revenue. Additionally, for any
i\in[m]
and
j\in[N]
, when
\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}=0
, with probability
(1-\alpha/2)
, the true bid is smaller than the estimated bid.

Theorem 7
.

With probability
(1-\alpha/2)^{n}
, the revenue based on the estimated type
\hat{t}
we get from Section
3.3
is less than the expected revenue of true type

by at most

The proof of this theorem is given in Appendix

.
We make three remarks on Theorem
7
. First, when the number of items,
N
, is limited, it is likely that the number of items,
k
, in the set
K
in Eq. (
8
) will also be limited because of
k<N
. As a result,
kd
will also be relatively small, which in turn indicates that the proposed mechanism is likely to perform effectively in this scenario.

Second, in comparison to the findings in
Cai and Daskalakis 2022
, our proposed mechanism demonstrates a distinct advantage in scenarios where the number of bidders,
m
, is large and the number of items,
N
, is small. Specifically, while their revenue lower bound exhibits a negative correlation with the number of bidders,
m
, our revenue lower bound exhibits a negative correlation with the number of items,
N
, and is independent of
m
. Thus, our mechanism is particularly well-suited for environments with a high number of bidders and a limited number of items.

Third, Theorem
7
shows that the performance of our proposed mechanism depends on
kd
. The value of
d
is a hyper-parameter that is chosen based on the specific characteristics of the data set at hand. The density function, and subsequently the length of the confidence intervals, can vary from dataset to dataset, thus requiring the selection of an appropriate value of
d
. In Section
3.5
, we will further explore the appropriate range of values for

.

3.5
Tuning Parameters

Theorems
6
and
7
provide a theoretical foundation for our mechanism.
We consider two key tuning parameters,
n
in Eq. (
7
) and
d
in Eq. (
5
).

First, we consider the tuning of
n
. In order to achieve the desired IR and expected revenue in high confidence, the value of
(1-\alpha/2)^{n}
needs to be as large as possible. For any confidence rate
\eta\in[0,1]
, let

(1-\alpha/2)^{n}\geq\eta.

This yields the inequality

n\leq\log(\eta)/\log(1-\alpha/2)
.
Additionally, as defined in Section
3.3
,
n
must be less than
mN
. Therefore, we can set

n\leq n^{*}\equiv\min\left\{\frac{\log(\eta)}{\log(1-\alpha/2)},mN\right\}.

Second, we consider the tuning of
d
. We desire to control
d
such that

\left|\{(i,j)|\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}\leq d,i\in[m],j\in[N]\}\right|\leq n\leq n^{*}.

Formally, this can be achieved by selecting
d
as the maximum value that satisfies

\left|\{(i,j)|\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}\leq d,i\in[m],j\in[N]\}\right|\leq n^{*}.

(9)

In practice, we begin by selecting
d
as the theoretical maximum value given by Eq. (
9
). We then calculate the corresponding value of
kd
. If this value is too large, we reduce the value of
d
, whereas if the seller is willing to tolerate a reduced revenue and take on a small risk, we may increase the value of
d
to reduce the number of queries. This process of adjusting
d
can be performed by sorting the length of intervals in increasing order and selecting the
n^{*}
th length as the initial value of
d
, and iteratively adjusting until a satisfactory value is found.
However, when the product
mN
is large, this method may encounter limitations. In such cases, we can use the strategy outlined in Section
3.2
to filter out unnecessary intervals, thus reducing the number of intervals considered and allowing us to utilize the mechanism proposed in Section
3.3
more effectively. In practice, we recommend starting with the value of
d
given by Eq. (
9
) for a specified value of
\eta
, and adjusting as necessary.

5.3
Discussion of the Results

Our proposed mechanism, Method 1, demonstrates outstanding performance across a range of experiments, particularly when applied to large-scale datasets. By selectively filtering and ignoring unnecessary bids, Method 1 enables the seller to reduce the number of queries required to achieve a given level of revenue, without sacrificing significant revenue. This is made possible by the precision with which we identify potential winners, ensuring that queries are targeted to those individuals whose true values are most likely to contribute to the estimated revenue. This is evident in the results of our three experiments, where we set the confidence rate to 0.9 and observed negligible regret, less than 3, in all cases. This not only confirms the efficiency of our mechanism, but also shows the fairness in Proposition
4
.

The accuracy of our density estimation method is validated by the fact that all true values fall within the constructed confidence intervals, further supporting the IR of our mechanism because the true values are all bigger than the lower bound of their confidence intervals. Theorem
6
holds strong in practice, and the potential for larger
n
values is suggested by the minimal impact of reducing
\alpha
from
0.01
to
0.001
on the confidence interval.

In all three experiments, it was found that the theoretical regret was consistently larger than the practical regret, which corroborates Theorem
7
.
Further refinement is possible. Currently, when calculating regret, the revenue for items in set
K
defined in Eq. (
8
) is reduced. In practice, a more precise method would be to sort the intervals’ bandwidths and deduce the maximum bandwidth for each item. Specifically, we can define

d_{j}=\max\limits_{i}\{\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}|i\in[m],0<\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}\leq d\}.

With this approach, the revenue based on the estimated type
\hat{t}
would be less than the expected revenue of the true type
t
by at most
\sum_{j\in K}d_{j}
, which is smaller than
kd
. This would yield a more accurate result than Theorem
7
.

Finally, we have demonstrated in Section
3.5
that, when the confidence rate
\eta=0.9
and
\alpha=0.01
, at most
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{n^{*}=}}21
intervals are required to achieve a high level of precision. However, our experimental results indicate that, in practice, the number of intervals required can be greater without significantly impacting revenue. This supports the efficacy of our proposed method, which uses the lower bound as the estimated types, and further highlights the robustness of our approach to variations in the confidence rate
\eta
.

6
Conclusion

This paper has presented a novel mechanism design for multi-item auction settings with uncertain bidders’ type distributions. Our proposed approach, which leverages nonparametric density estimation to accurately estimate bidders’ types from historical bids’ data and is built upon the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism, not only satisfies the important criteria of Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC) and
\delta
-individual rationality (IR), but also introduces two novel strategies for query reduction that effectively enhance the efficiency of the mechanism. The results of our simulation experiments demonstrate the superiority of our mechanism over existing methods in terms of revenue maximization and query reduction, especially in large-scale scenarios. Thus, our proposed mechanism is a highly desirable and effective option for sellers in the realm of multi-item auctions.
Future research directions include exploring alternative methods for efficient density estimation and evaluating the applicability of our mechanism to other auction mechanisms. Real-world implementation of our proposed mechanism is also an area for future investigation.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
6

Proof.

First, we prove the BIC property.
For each
i\in[m]
, we need to demonstrate that
the change of the bid
t_{i}
will reduce the bidder
B_{i}
’s utility. Our mechanism only elicits queries from bidders with estimated type I, with the type II values determined by the seller. Hence, the only types that bidders may alter are the type I values. The VCG mechanism, which we employ in our design, ensures truth-telling from bidders in order to maximize their utility, thereby ensuring satisfaction of the BIC criterion.

Second, we prove the
\delta
-IR property.
If a bidder
B_{i}
,
i\in[m]
wins a bundle
S\subset[N]
, then her payment is

\max\limits_{k\neq i,k\in[m]}\sum_{j\in[S]}\hat{t}_{kj},

and

\max\limits_{k\neq i,k\in[m]}\sum_{j\in[S]}\hat{t}_{kj}\leq\sum_{j\in[S]}\hat{t}_{ij}.

In accordance with the methodology presented in Section
3.3
for computing
\hat{t}
, we have that for each item
j\in L_{i}
, there exists a probability of
(1-\alpha/2)
that the actual bid
t_{ij}
exceeds the estimated bid
\hat{t}_{ij}
. For items
j
not in
L_{i}
,
t_{ij}=\hat{t}_{ij}
. The size of
L_{i}
is
n_{i}
. Therefore, with a probability of at least
(1-\alpha/2)^{n_{i}}
, the actual bid
t_{ij}
exceeds the estimated bid
\hat{t}_{ij}
for all
j\in S
. Thus, we can conclude that with a probability of at least
(1-\alpha/2)^{n_{i}}
,

\begin{split}u_{i}(t_{i},M(\hat{t}))&=v_{i}(t_{i},S)-P(\hat{t},S)\\
&=\sum_{j\in S}t_{ij}-\max\limits_{k\neq i,k\in[m]}\sum_{j\in S}\hat{t}_{kj}\\
&\geq\sum_{j\in S}t_{ij}-\sum_{j\in S}\hat{t}_{ij}\\
&=\sum_{j\in S}(t_{ij}-\hat{t}_{ij})\\
&\geq 0\end{split}

Then for every
i
,

\Pr\left(u_{i}(t_{i},M(\hat{t}))\geq 0\right)\geq\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}}=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{n}\geq 1-\frac{\alpha n}{2}.

So our mechanism is
\delta
-IR with
\delta=\frac{\alpha n}{2}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7

Proof.

The estimation of the type
\hat{t}
is made possible by leveraging historical data and the confidence intervals derived from Section
3.1
. We note that
\hat{t}
is unique under these conditions. Meanwhile, the bids estimated in type I are determined by the actual values given by the bidders. The only source of uncertainty lies in the type II estimated bids, specifically within the set
\{\hat{t}_{ij}|i\in[m],j\in L_{i}\}
. It is within this subset that the true value may differ from the estimated data.
Given a fixed
t
, the following relationship holds for every
i\in[m]
with a probability of
(1-\alpha/2)
,

\hat{t}_{ij}>t_{ij}-d,\text{ where }j\in K\text{ as defined in Eq.~\eqref{K}}.

(C.1)

Let

n_{j1}=|\{i|0<\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}\leq d,i\in[m]\}|.

Then with probability

(1-\alpha/2)^{n_{j1}}
, the inequality (
C.1
) holds for all of
i\in[m]
.
Consequently, the price of item
j
, represented by
P_{j}(t)
, which is the second highest bid among
t_{ij}
for all
i\in[m]
, satisfies the inequality:

P_{j}(\hat{t})>P_{j}(t)-d.

When
j\notin K

i\in[m]

\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}=0

(1-\alpha)

\hat{t}_{ij}>t_{ij}

\hat{t}_{ijU}-\hat{t}_{ijL}\neq 0

\hat{t}_{ij}=t_{ij}

Then with probability
(1-\alpha/2)^{n_{j2}}

Altogether, we have that with probability

\begin{split}\sum_{j\in[N]}P_{j}(\hat{t})&\geq\sum_{j\in[N]}P_{j}(t)-\sum_{j\in K}d\\

(C.2)

By taking expectations on both sides of Eq. (

\sum_{i\in[m]}P_{j}(\hat{t})\geq\mathbb{E}_{t\sim D}\left[\sum_{i\in[m]}P_{j}(t)\right]-kd,

(C.3)

where we used the fact that only the term
\sum_{i\in[m]}P_{j}(t)
is random over the change of
t
.

Proof of Proposition
4

Proof.

For each item
j\in[N]
and every bidder
B_{k}
that is not in
\mathcal{B}_{j}
, with probability
1-\alpha/2
,
t_{kj}
is smaller than

1-\alpha/2

t_{i_{*}^{j}j}

A.4
Proof of Proposition
5

Proof.

When each bundle just has one item, We only have 1 allocation rule:

\bigl\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\dots,\{N\}\bigr\}.

When at least a bundle has 2 items, We use
\lfloor N/2\rfloor
to denote the maximum integer which less than or equal to N/2. Then if the allocation has
i\ (i\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor)
2-item bundle, the number of allocation is

\frac{\tbinom{N}{2i}\tbinom{2i}{2}\tbinom{2i-2}{2}\cdots\tbinom{4}{2}}{i!}=\frac{N!}{(N-2i)!i!2^{i}}

When
N\geq 3i
,
N-i\geq 2i
, we have
(N-2i+1)(N-2i)\dots(N-i)\geq 1\times 2\times\dots\times 2i
, and
N-i\leq\frac{2N}{3}
, so

\begin{split}\frac{N!}{(N-2i)!i!2^{i}}&=\frac{(N-2i+1)(N-2i)\cdots(N)}{1\times 2\times\cdots\times i\times 2^{i}}\\
&=\frac{(N-2i+1)(N-2i)\cdots(N)}{2\times 4\times\dots\times 2i}\\
&\geq(N-i+1)(N-i+2)\cdots N\\
&\geq(N-i)^{i}\\
&\geq\big(\frac{2N}{3}\big)^{i}\end{split}

(C.4)

The number of allocations is the sum of the number of locations for
i\ (i\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor)
, which is

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\frac{N!}{(N-2i)!i!2^{i}}&\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/3\rfloor}\frac{N!}{(N-2i)!i!2^{i}}\\
&\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/3\rfloor}\big(\frac{2N}{3}\big)^{i}\\
&=\frac{2N}{2N-1}\Big(\big(\frac{2N}{3}\big)^{\lfloor N/3\rfloor}-1\Big)\\
&\geq\big(\frac{2N}{3}\big)^{\lfloor N/3\rfloor}-1\end{split}

where the first inequality uses
N/2>N/3
, the second inequality uses inequality (
C.4
), and the third inequality uses
\frac{2N}{2N-1}<1
.
Hence the total number of allocations is

1+\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\frac{N!}{(N-2i)!i!2^{i}}\geq\left(\frac{2N}{3}\right)^{\lfloor N/3\rfloor}

This completes the proof.
∎
