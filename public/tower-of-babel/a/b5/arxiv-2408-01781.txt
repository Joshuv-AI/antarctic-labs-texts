Abstract
. We prove extreme value laws for cusp excursions of the horocycle flow in the case of surfaces of constant negative curvature. The key idea of our approach is to study the hitting time distribution for shrinking Poincaré sections that have a particularly simple scaling property under the action of the geodesic flow. This extends the extreme value law of Kirsebom and Mallahi-Karai [arXiv:2209.07283] for cusp excursions for the modular surface. Here we show that the limit law can be expressed in terms of Hall’s formula for the gap distribution of the Farey sequence.



1.
Introduction

Important examples in the history of ergodic theory are the geodesic and horocycle flows on finite area hyperbolic surfaces, which were the basis for the pioneering works of
Hedlund and Hopf in the 1930s on minimality and ergodicity.
Such geodesic flows were shown to be strong mixing by Hedlund, using a geometric argument
[
17
]
, and
in 1952 by Gelfand and Fomin, using unitary representation theory
[
14
]
.
The following year Parasjuk used this approach to show that horocycle flows were also strong mixing
[
34
]
.
Later Ratner
[
36
]
and Moore
[
33
]
refined these results to study the speeds of mixing of both flows.
Other important progress came with the classification of horocycle invariant measures and equidistribution for horocycle flows, which was established by Furstenberg
[
15
]
(for compact surfaces) and Dani and Smillie
[
10
]
(for non-compact surfaces).

Horocycle flows are of particular interest since they enjoy statistical features that are reminiscent of those seen in “chaotic” dynamics, despite the lack of sensitive dependence on initial conditions. These include the above strong mixing property, non-standard limit theorems for ergodic averages
[
9
]
and temporal limit theorems
[
13
]
. Particularly remarkable is the logarithm law for cusp excursions for typical horocycles due to Athreya and Margulis
[
5
]
(see also
[
6
,
21
,
22
,
45
]
for unipotent flows in more general settings), by analogy with results for geodesic flows by Sullivan
[
39
]
.

Kirsebom and Mallahi-Karai
[
23
]
refined the logarithm law by proving an extreme value theorem for cusp excursions on the modular surface, again by analogy with results for geodesic flows
[
35
]
. One objective of the present paper is to extend their extreme value theorem to general hyperbolic surfaces and more general initial data. It should also be possible to extend the approach developed here to cusp excursions in infinite volume surfaces, using the techniques of
[
26
]
.

To state our first main result, let
{\mathcal{X}}=\operatorname{T{}}^{1}{\mathcal{Y}}
be the unit tangent bundle of a non-compact hyperbolic surface
{\mathcal{Y}}
with finite area. Let

and

be the geodesic flow, unstable horocycle flow, and stable horocycle flow on
{\mathcal{X}}
at time

(1.1)

\varphi_{t}\circ h_{s}^{+}=h_{s\exp t}^{+}\circ\varphi_{t},\qquad\varphi_{t}\circ h_{s}^{-}=h_{s\exp(-t)}^{-}\circ\varphi_{t}.

Denote by
\mu
the invariant Liouville measure on
{\mathcal{X}}
, which we assume is normalised so that

. We denote by

the Riemannian distance on

, which is normalised so that the geodesic flow moves points with speed one. That is, if

is the natural projection, then

for every

and every sufficiently small

The plan for the remainder of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
we define a shrinking family of Poincaré sections for the horocycle flow, and show that the hitting time process, for random initial data, converges to a limit process. The key input in the proof is the scaling property of the section and the mixing property of the geodesic flow. The limit law is then extended to cuspidal neighbourhoods by establishing their proximity to the section. This argument will require us to also track the location of the hit, and we will therefore prove limit theorems for the joint process of hitting times and impact parameters. In Section
3
we express the hitting time/impact parameter process in terms of a natural lattice point counting problem in
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})

(0,1)
under
\Gamma

4

\overline{\eta}_{1}

6
considers the special example of the modular group
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
, where we show that the limit laws can be expressed via the Hall distribution, and provide explicit formulas for the first hitting time. Section
7
extends the setting to the joint distribution of hitting times in shrinking sections in all
K
cusps of the surface. Section
8
shows that Theorem
1
is now a consequence of the results obtained for hitting times, and provides a formula for the limit distribution.

Hitting time statistics

Throughout this paper we assume the surface
{\mathcal{Y}}
has
K

{\mathcal{C}}_{\kappa}
is a closed stable horocycle of length one around the
\kappa
th cusp, for
\kappa=1,\ldots,K
. This means the unit tangent vectors on the horocycle point towards the cusp; in other words, for
x\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\kappa}
we have
h_{s}^{-}(x)=h_{s+1}^{-}(x)
and
\varphi_{t}(x)
escapes into the cusp as
t\to\infty
.

For
R\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define

(2.1)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(R)=\{\varphi_{t}({\mathcal{C}}_{\kappa}):t\geq R\},

which has the obvious scaling property

\varphi_{t}{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(R)={\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(R+t)
. By construction, the set
{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(R)
is an embedded closed submanifold of
{\mathcal{X}}
of co-dimension one, which (by the Anosov property of
\varphi_{t}
) is transversal to the unstable horocycle flow and therefore defines a Poincaré section. We furthermore note that
{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}=\emptyset
for
\kappa\neq\ell
. We will in the following focus on the hitting times for the cusp
\kappa
.

Note that due to the ergodicity of the horocycle flow
\mu
-almost every point will eventually hit the section
{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(R)
. A natural parametrisation of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(R)
is given by the embedding

(2.2)

\iota_{R}:\Sigma=[0,1)\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(R),\qquad(s,t)\mapsto\varphi_{t+R}\circ h_{s}^{-}(x_{\kappa})

for some fixed but arbitrary choice of
x_{\kappa}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\kappa}
, and
\Sigma=[0,1)\times{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, where
[0,1)
parametrises
{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
with
0
and
1
identified.

The map
(r,s,t)\mapsto h_{r}^{+}\circ\varphi_{t}\circ h_{s}^{-}(x_{\kappa})
provides a parametrisation of
{\mathcal{X}}
(up to a set of
\mu
-measure zero), where
(s,t)\in\Sigma
and
0\leq r<\eta_{1}(s,t)
, and
\eta_{1}(s,t)
is the first return time to the section
{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(0)=\iota_{0}(\Sigma)
. In these local coordinates, the Liouville measure reads

(2.3)

d\mu(r,s,t)=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;dr\,d\nu(s,t)

(2.4)

d\nu(s,t)=\mathrm{e}^{-t}dt\,ds,\qquad\overline{\eta}_{1}=\int_{\Sigma}\eta_{1}(s,t)\,d\nu(s,t).

This choice of normalisation ensures, in view of Kac’s formula for the average return time, that
\mu({\mathcal{X}})=1
. For more background on the connection of hitting and return times, see

Theorem 2
.

Assume
\lambda

X>0

{\mathcal{D}}\subset\Sigma

\lim_{R\to\infty}\lambda\{x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}:\mathrm{e}^{-R}\xi_{1}(x_{0},R)>X,\;w_{1}(x_{0},R)\in{\mathcal{D}}\}\\
=\int_{X}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\Psi(r,w)d\nu(w)dr

with

(2.8)

\Psi(r,w)=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;{\mathbbm{1}}\left(0\leq r<\eta_{-1}(w)\right).

Proof.

By the scaling property (
2.5
), we have

(2.9)

\begin{split}&\lambda\{x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}:\mathrm{e}^{-R}\xi_{1}(x_{0},R)>X,\;w_{1}(x_{0},R)\in{\mathcal{D}}\}\\
&=\lambda\{x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}:\xi_{1}(\varphi_{-R}x_{0},0)>X,\;w_{1}(\varphi_{-R}x_{0},0)\in{\mathcal{D}}\}\\
&=\lambda(\varphi_{R}{\mathcal{E}}),\end{split}

where

(2.10)

\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}&=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\xi_{1}(x,0)>X,\;w_{1}(x,0)\in{\mathcal{D}}\}\\
&=\iota_{0}\{(r,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\times\Sigma:X<r\leq\eta_{-1}(w),\;w\in{\mathcal{D}}\},\end{split}

and hence

(2.11)

\mu({\mathcal{E}})=\int_{X}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\Psi(r,w)d\nu(w)dr.

The right hand side of (
2.9

\mu({\mathcal{E}})

2.6

follows from the assumption that

has boundary of Lebesgue measure zero, see Proposition

below. This in turn implies that
\Psi\in\operatorname{L{}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\times\Sigma,\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{R}}\otimes\nu)
and hence, by our normalisation, a probability density.

3.
From hitting times to hyperbolic lattice points

Let
\{\pm 1\}\subset\Gamma\subset\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
be a discrete subgroup so that
{\mathcal{Y}}=\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}}
, where
{\mathbb{H}}
is the complex upper half plane on which
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
acts by Möbius transformations. We can further identify
{\mathcal{X}}=\Gamma\backslash\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
by setting
x=\Gamma g
, and note that the choices

(3.1)

\varphi_{t}(x)=x\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix},\quad h^{+}_{s}(x)=x\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-s&1\end{pmatrix},\quad h^{-}_{s}(x)=x\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

satisfy the commutation relations (
1.1
). By abuse of notation, we also denote by
\mu
the Haar measure on
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, normalised so that
\mu({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})=1
where
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}
is a fundamental domain of
\Gamma
in
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
.
The injection

(3.2)

A:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}}),\qquad(r,s,t)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}

provides a parametrisation of
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
up to a null set, and we have in these coordinates

(3.3)

d\mu(r,s,t)=\overline{\eta}_{1}^{-1}\;dr\,d\nu(s,t)

with

(3.4)

d\nu(s,t)=\mathrm{e}^{-t}dt\,ds

and the same

\overline{\eta}_{1}

as in (
2.4
).

We may assume without loss of generality that the cusp
\kappa
is presented in
{\mathbb{H}}
as a cusp at infinity of width one. This implies that
\Gamma
contains the subgroup

(3.5)

\Gamma_{\infty}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&m\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:m\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}.

With this choice of coordinates, the section is represented as

(3.6)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(R)=\Gamma\backslash\left\{\Gamma\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}:s\in[0,1),t\geq R\right\},

and the sequence of hitting times
(\xi_{j}(x_{0},R))_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is precisely given by the times the lifted horocycle

(3.7)

\left\{g_{0}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-r&1\end{pmatrix}:r>0\right\}

intersects the disjoint union

(3.8)

\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left\{\gamma\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}:s\in[0,1),t\geq R\right\}

where
g_{0}\in\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is any representative such that
x_{0}=\Gamma g_{0}
. Since the above union is disjoint, the hitting time
\xi_{j}
determines a unique
\gamma_{j}\in\Gamma
so that

(3.9)

g_{0}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-\xi_{j}&1\end{pmatrix}=\gamma_{j}\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}

with
(s+{\mathbb{Z}},t-R)=w_{j}(x_{0},R)
. On the other hand, every
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that

(3.10)

\gamma g_{0}\in\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}:r\in(0,T],s\in[0,1),t\geq R\right\}

yields a unique
(r,s,t)
and
j
such that
(\xi_{j}(x_{0},R),w_{j}(x_{0},R))=(r,s+{\mathbb{Z}},t-R)
.

We conclude that, for
{\mathcal{B}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\Sigma
, we have

(3.11)

\#\{j:(\xi_{j}(x_{0},R),w_{j}(x_{0},R))\in{\mathcal{B}}\}\\
=\#\left(\Gamma g_{0}\cap\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}:(r,s,t-R)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\right),

which we restate in the following propositon.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
R\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\times\Sigma
,

(3.12)

\#\{j:(\mathrm{e}^{-R}\xi_{j}(x_{0},R),w_{j}(x_{0},R))\in{\mathcal{B}}\}=\#\left(\Gamma g_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-R/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{R/2}\end{pmatrix}\cap A({\mathcal{B}})\right).

Proof.

Using (
3.11
), we have

(3.13)

\begin{split}&\#\{j:(\mathrm{e}^{-R}\xi_{j}(x_{0},R),w_{j}(x_{0},R))\in{\mathcal{B}}\}\\
&=\#\left(\Gamma g_{0}\cap\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}:(r\mathrm{e}^{-R},s,t-R)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\right)\\
&=\#\left(\Gamma g_{0}\cap\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{(t+R)/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-(t+R)/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r\mathrm{e}^{R}&1\end{pmatrix}:(r,s,t)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\right)\\
&=\#\left(\Gamma g_{0}\cap\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{R/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-R/2}\end{pmatrix}:(r,s,t)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\right)\\
&=\#\left(\Gamma g_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-R/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{R/2}\end{pmatrix}\cap\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}:(r,s,t)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\right).\end{split}

∎

Proposition 3.2
.

For any Borel set
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\times\Sigma
,

(3.14)

\mu\left\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\#\left\{j:(\xi_{j}(x,0),w_{j}(x,0))\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\geq 1\right\}\leq\mu\left(A({\mathcal{B}})\right).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
and Markov’s (or Chebyshev’s) inequality

(3.15)

\begin{split}&\mu\left\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\#\left\{j:\left(\xi_{j}(x,R),w_{j}(x,R)\right)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\geq 1\right\}\\
&=\mu\left\{g\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}:\#\left(\Gamma g\cap A({\mathcal{B}})\right)\geq 1\right\}\\
&\leq\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\chi_{A({\mathcal{B}})}(\gamma g)d\mu(g),\end{split}

where
\chi_{A({\mathcal{B}})}
is the characteristic function of the set
A({\mathcal{B}})
. Sum and integral can be combined to an integral over all of
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, and the result follows.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k_{1},\ldots,k_{N}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
0<T_{1}\leq\ldots\leq T_{N}
, and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{D}}_{N}\subset\Sigma
Borel sets with boundary of Lebesgue measure zero. Then

(3.16)

\mu\left\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\#\left\{j:\left(\xi_{j}(x,0),w_{j}(x,0)\right)\in(0,T_{i}]\times{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\right\}=k_{i}\forall i\leq N\right\}\\
=\mu\left\{g\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}:\#\left\{\Gamma g\cap A\left((0,T_{i}]\times{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\right)\right\}=k_{i}\forall i\leq N\right\}.

We will now explore the case when the test set
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\Sigma
only depends on the second variable, i.e., the height of the section
\Sigma
.

4.
From hyperbolic lattice points to Euclidean point sets

Let
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
act on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
on the right, in the standard fashion.
The orbit
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}=(0,1)\Gamma
of the point
(0,1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
under the action of the lattice
\Gamma
is a locally finite set.
It is in one-to-one correspondence with the left coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma
via the isomorphism

(4.1)

\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma\to{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma},\qquad\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\mapsto(0,1)\gamma.

This bijection extends to the deformed set
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g
via

(4.2)

\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma g\to{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g,\qquad\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma g\mapsto(0,1)\gamma g.

It is a result of Veech that for any
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with boundary of Lebesgue measure zero, we have

(4.3)

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T^{2}}\#(\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma:(0,1)\gamma g\cap T{\mathcal{A}})=\frac{2\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}({\mathcal{A}})}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})},

where
\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}
and
\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}
are the standard Riemannian volumes of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{H}}
, respectively.
This observation, which was key in Veech’s work
[
43
, Section 3]
, follows from the theory of Eisenstein series of even integral weight
[
24
]
, which are the generating functions for the above counting problem. (The relevant pole is a simple pole at
s=1
for the weight zero Eisenstein series, with residue
1/\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})
. The factor
2
reflects the fact that we are both counting
\gamma
and
-\gamma
, whereas the Eisenstein series takes only one representative of the two.)

We furthermore have the following well known Siegel-type identity.
Let
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}
be any measurable fundamental domain of
\Gamma
in
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, and denote by
\mu
the Haar measure of
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
normalised such that
\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})=1
; it represents the normalised Liouville measure on
{\mathcal{X}}
, hence the same notation.

Proposition 4.1
.

For every measurable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
,

(4.4)

\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}}\bigg(\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g}f(p)\bigg)d\mu(g)=\frac{2}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}f(a)da.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
f
has compact support. Then

(4.5)

\begin{split}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}}\bigg(\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g}f(p)\bigg)d\mu(g)&=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}}\bigg(\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma}f((0,1)\gamma g)\bigg)d\mu(g)\\
&=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma_{\infty}}}f((0,1)g)d\mu(g).\end{split}

We can use the Iwasawa decomposition

(4.6)

g=\begin{pmatrix}1&u\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v^{1/2}&0\\
0&v^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

In these coordiates

(4.7)

d\mu(g)=\frac{1}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})}\frac{du\,dv\,d\theta}{v^{2}},

where the normalisation ensures
\mu
is a probability measure on the fundamental domain
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}={\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}\times[0,\pi)
(it is
\pi
not
2\pi
since
-1\in\Gamma
).
A fundamental domain for
\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is, on the other hand given by
u\in[0,1)
,
v>0
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
(now
2\pi
because
\Gamma_{\infty}
does not contain
-1
), and so (
4.5
) evaluates to

(4.8)

\frac{1}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(v^{-1/2}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta))\frac{dv\,d\theta}{v^{2}},

which yields the right hand side of (
4.4
) after the variable substitution
r=v^{-1/2}
and going from polar to cartesian coordinates.
∎

If we apply Proposition
4.1
with
f=\chi_{\mathcal{A}}
, the characteristic function of a bounded measurable set
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, then Markov’s inequality yields the useful bound

(4.9)

\mu\left\{g\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}:\#\left({\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g\cap{\mathcal{A}}\right)\geq 1\right\}\leq\frac{2\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}({\mathcal{A}})}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})},

which shows in particular that it is very unlikely to find an element of
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g
in a small set.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
R\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
,

(4.10)

\#\left\{j:\left(\mathrm{e}^{-R}\xi_{j}(x_{0},R),t_{j}(x_{0},R)\right)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}=\#\left({\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-R/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{R/2}\end{pmatrix}\cap\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}(1,r):(r,t)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\right).

Proof.

In view of the bijection (
4.1
) and Proposition
3.1
,

(4.11)

\begin{split}&\#\left\{j:\left(\mathrm{e}^{-R}\xi_{j}(x_{0},R),t_{j}(x_{0},R)\right)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\\
&=\#\left({\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-R/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{R/2}\end{pmatrix}\cap\left\{(0,1)\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}:(r,t)\in{\mathcal{B}},\;s\in[0,1)\right\}\right)\\
&=\#\left({\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-R/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{R/2}\end{pmatrix}\cap\left\{(0,1)\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}:(r,t)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}\right),\end{split}

which yields (
4.10
).
∎

For
X>0
, define the triangle
\Delta_{X}=\left\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:0<u_{1}\leq Xu_{2},\;0<u_{2}\leq 1\right\}
.
Note that
\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}(\Delta_{X})=\frac{1}{2}X
.

Corollary 4.1
.

For
X>0
,

(4.12)

\#\left\{j:\mathrm{e}^{-R}\xi_{j}(x_{0},R)\leq X\right\}=\#\left({\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}g_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-R/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{R/2}\end{pmatrix}\cap\Delta_{X}\right).

Proof.

In Proposition
4.2
take
{\mathcal{B}}=(0,X]\times{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
. Then

(4.13)

\left\{(0,1)\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
r&1\end{pmatrix}:(r,t)\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}=\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}(r,1):0<r\leq X,\;t\geq 0\right\}.

∎

The past hitting times
\xi_{j}
with
j
negative satisfy an analogue of (
4.12
) with the triangle
\Delta_{-X}=\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:-Xu_{2}\leq u_{1}<0,\;0<u_{2}\leq 1\}
.
Again,
\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}(\Delta_{-X})=\frac{1}{2}X
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For
r>0
,
(s,t)\in\Sigma
,

(4.14)

\Psi(r,s,t)=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;{\mathbbm{1}}\left({\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\cap\Delta_{-r}=\emptyset\right)

with

(4.15)

\overline{\eta}_{1}=\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}).

Proof.

Relation (
4.14
) follows from the backward version of Corollary
4.1
, since the first return time is the first hitting time for initial data on the Poincaré section. Proposition
4.1
and Corollary
4.1
imply that

(4.16)

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\#\left\{j:\xi_{j}(x,0)\leq X\right\}d\mu(x)=\frac{2\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}(\Delta_{X})}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})}=\frac{X}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})},

which means that the stationary point process
\left(\xi_{j}(x,0)\right)_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
(with
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
random according to
\mu
) has intensity
(\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma}))^{-1}
.
∎

5.
Tail bounds

In this section we will provide uniform upper and lower bounds for the density
\Psi(r)
defined in equation (
2.14
), for all
r\geq 0
. We will assume there is only one cusp, i.e.,
K=1
. The multiple cusp case is analogous and discussed in Section
7
.

We first show that
\Psi(r)
is constant for sufficiently small
r
. By construction, the return time of the horocycle flow to the section
{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(0)
has a strictly positive lower bound, i.e.,

(5.1)

\eta_{1}^{*}=\inf_{(s,t)\in\Sigma}\eta_{1}(s,t)=\inf_{(s,t)\in\Sigma}\eta_{-1}(s,t)>0.

Hence, for
0<r<\eta_{1}^{*}
we have, in view of (
2.8
),

(5.2)

\Psi(r,s,t)=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}=\frac{1}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})}

and therefore also

(5.3)

\Psi(r)=\frac{1}{\pi\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}({\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma})}.

To understand the distribution of hitting times also for larger values of
r
, note that the formula for the half-séjour time (
2.12
) yields the lower bound

(5.4)

\eta_{-1}(s,t)\geq\left(\mathrm{e}^{t-\sigma_{1}}-1\right)^{1/2}

for all
t\geq\sigma_{1}
, where
\sigma_{1}
is chosen sufficiently large so that
\pi{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(\sigma_{1})
is a proper cuspidal neighbourhood on the surface
{\mathcal{Y}}
. Hence we have that

(5.5)

\Psi(r,s,t)\geq\frac{1}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;{\mathbbm{1}}\left(0\leq r<\left(\mathrm{e}^{t-\sigma_{1}}-1\right)^{1/2}\right)\;{\mathbbm{1}}\left(t\geq\sigma_{1}\right),

and therefore, by (
2.14
),

(5.6)

\Psi(r)\geq\frac{1}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;\int_{\sigma_{1}}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}\left(0\leq r<(\mathrm{e}^{t-\sigma_{1}}-1)^{1/2}\right)\;\mathrm{e}^{-t}dt=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{1}}}{r^{2}+1}.

Let us turn to the upper bound. We define
t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}(s,t)
as the height of the pre-image under the return map on the section
\Sigma
, and
s^{\prime}=s^{\prime}(s,t)
as the corresponding horizontal coordinate. Then
\eta_{-1}(s,t)=\eta_{1}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
and, due to the invariance of
\nu
under the return map, we have under the variable substitution
(s,t)\mapsto(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(5.7)

\Psi(r)=\int_{\Sigma}\Psi(r,s,t)\,d\nu(s,t)=\int_{\Sigma}\Psi_{+}(r,s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,d\nu(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})

where

(5.8)

\Psi_{+}(r,s^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;{\mathbbm{1}}\left(0\leq r<\eta_{1}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right).

Now choose
\tilde{\sigma}_{1}
sufficiently large so that

\pi\left({\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(-\tilde{\sigma}_{1})\right)={\mathcal{Y}}
. Then

(5.9)

\eta_{-1}(s,t)\leq\left(\mathrm{e}^{t+\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}-1\right)^{1/2}+\left(\mathrm{e}^{t^{\prime}+\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}-1\right)^{1/2}

for all
(s,t)\in\Sigma
and
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
the image of
(s,t)
under the first return map as above. We divide the integration in (
5.7
) into the regions
t^{\prime}\leq t
and
t\leq t^{\prime}
, where

(5.10)

\eta_{-1}(s,t)\leq 2\left(\mathrm{e}^{t+\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}-1\right)^{1/2}\qquad(t^{\prime}\leq t),

(5.11)

\eta_{1}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\leq 2\left(\mathrm{e}^{t^{\prime}+\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}-1\right)^{1/2}\qquad(t\leq t^{\prime}),

respectively. Hence,

(5.12)

\begin{split}\Psi(r)&=\int_{t^{\prime}<t}\Psi(r,s,t)\,d\nu(s,t)+\int_{t<t^{\prime}}\Psi(r,s,t)\,d\nu(s,t)\\
&=\int_{t^{\prime}<t}\Psi(r,s,t)\,d\nu(s,t)+\int_{t<t^{\prime}}\Psi_{+}(r,s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,d\nu(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}),\end{split}

where in the second integration
t
is viewed as a function of
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
.
Now, using (
5.10
), we have for the first integral

(5.13)

\int_{t^{\prime}<t}\Psi(r,s,t)\,d\nu(s,t)\leq\frac{2}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}\left(0\leq r<(\mathrm{e}^{t+\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}-1)^{1/2}\right)\;\mathrm{e}^{-t}dt\leq\frac{2}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}}{r^{2}+1},

and the same for the second integral, using (
5.8
) and (
5.11
). This yields

(5.14)

\Psi(r)\leq\frac{4}{\overline{\eta}_{1}}\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}}{r^{2}+1}.

Combining both upper and lower bounds, we conclude there exist constants
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
such that for all
r\geq 0
we have

(5.15)

\frac{c_{1}}{r^{2}+1}\leq\Psi(r)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{r^{2}+1}.

In this general setting, the quadratic tail bound (
5.15
) was first observed in
[
25
]
.

6.
Example: the modular surface

In the case when
\Gamma
is the modular group
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
, the set
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}
is the set of primitive lattice points
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}=\{(p,q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:\gcd(p,q)=1\}
, and the intersection of
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}g
for random
x=\Gamma g
has been studied in the context of statistics of directions of lattice points, Farey fractions and smallest denominators, see
[
1
,
4
,
28
,
30
,
31
]
and references therein. The volume of the modular surface is
\pi/3
and hence, by (
4.15
), we have

\overline{\eta}_{1}=\pi^{2}/3
. Furthermore note that (
4.3
) yields the well known value
6/\pi^{2}
for the density of primitive lattice points.

Next, note that

(6.1)

{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{t/2}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\cap\Delta_{-\sigma}=\emptyset

is equivalent to

(6.2)

{\mathcal{P}}_{\Gamma}\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\cap\Delta_{-\sigma,t}=\emptyset

with the triangle

\Delta_{r,t}=\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:0<u_{1}\leq r^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{t}u_{2},\;u_{2}\leq r\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\}
.
A comparison with
[
28
, Eq. (30)]
shows that

(6.3)

\Psi(r,t):=\int_{0}^{1}\Psi(r,s,t)ds=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\times\begin{cases}1&\text{if $r\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\leq 1$}\\
1-\mathrm{e}^{t/2}+r^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{t}&\text{if $1<r\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}\leq(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/2})^{-1}$}\\
0&\text{if $r\mathrm{e}^{-t/2}>(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/2})^{-1}$.}\\
\end{cases}

Integrating this against
\mathrm{e}^{-t}dt
yields, via (
2.14
),

(6.4)

\Psi(r)=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\times\begin{cases}1&\text{if $r\in[0,1)$}\\
-1+2r^{-1}+2r^{-1}\log r&\text{if $r\in[1,4]$}\\
-1+2r^{-1}+2\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-r^{-1}}-4r^{-1}\log\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-r^{-1}}\right)&\text{if $r\in[4,\infty]$,}\end{cases}

which was found by Hall
[
16
]
to describe the distribution of gaps in the Farey sequence. For
r\to\infty
we have the asymptotics

(6.5)

\Psi(r)\sim\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}r^{-2},

which sharpens the general estimate (
5.15
).

We note that there are formulas analogous to the Hall distribution for more general Fuchsian groups
\Gamma
, which describe the statistics of slopes or gaps of saddle connections in flat surfaces
[
3
,
7
,
25
,
37
,
40
,
44
,
42
]
. By following the same steps as above, the formulas in these papers can be turned into explicit limit densities for the extreme value laws for cusp excursions on
\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}}
.

8.
Extreme value laws

We start by discussing a variant of Theorem
1
in a setting closer to our previous discussion of hitting times.

In each cusp the height of the horocycle excursion is relative to a closed horocycle of unit length.
Thus with surfaces having cusps of different widths the definition of the height of horocycle excursion changes accordingly.
Using the standard parametrisation, via (
7.3
), of the section
{\mathcal{H}}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0)
by
(s,t,\kappa)\in\Sigma^{K}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}})
, we define the marked height of a point
x\in{\mathcal{H}}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0)
by
(\tau(x),\kappa(x))
as the second (vertical) coordinate
t
and cusp
\kappa
. Note that the marked height is independent of the choice of
\sigma
, as long as
x
is in the section. We denote the relative height by
\widetilde{\tau}(x,{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}})=\tau(x)-\sigma_{\kappa(x)}
. We do the same for the projected section

(8.1)

\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0)=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{K}\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\kappa}(\sigma_{\kappa}),\qquad\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\kappa}(\sigma_{\kappa})=\pi{\mathcal{H}}_{\kappa}(\sigma_{\kappa}),

assuming here that
\sigma_{\kappa}
are sufficiently large so that the map

\pi\circ\iota_{{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0}:\Sigma^{K}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}})\to{\mathcal{Y}}
defines an embedding with image
\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0)
, with
\iota_{{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0}
as defined in (
7.3
).

Every
y\in\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0)
then has a unique pre-image
\{x\}=\pi^{-1}(y)\cap{\mathcal{H}}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0)
, and we define the marked height
(\tau(y),\kappa(y))
and relative height
\widetilde{\tau}(y,{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}})=\tau(y)-\sigma_{\kappa(y)}
of such a
y
as the height and relative height of
x
, respectively.
Geometrically,
\widetilde{\tau}(y,{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}})
is the Riemannian distance from the horocycle
\varphi_{\sigma_{\kappa}}({\mathcal{C}}_{\kappa})
(the boundary of
\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\kappa}(\sigma_{\kappa})
) to
y\in\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\kappa}(\sigma_{\kappa})
.

The following limit law provides the distribution of extremal heights of cusp excursions of the horocyclic orbit
\{h_{s}^{+}(x_{0}):0\leq s\leq T\}
with random initial data
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}
. We will consider both extreme excursions on the surface and extreme locally maximal excursions, which occur at time
\xi_{j}=\xi_{j}(x_{0},{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0)
when the trajectory hits the section
{\mathcal{H}}({\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},0)
.
As we will see below, Theorem
1
is a consequence of the following statement.

Theorem 6
.

Assume
\lambda
is admissible. Then, for
H\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
,

(8.2)

\lim_{T\to\infty}\lambda\left\{x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}:\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\widetilde{\tau}(\pi\circ h_{s}^{+}(x_{0}),{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}})>H+\log T\right\}=\int_{H}^{\infty}\rho_{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}}(s)ds

and

(8.3)

\lim_{T\to\infty}\lambda\left\{x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}:\sup_{0\leq\xi_{j}\leq T}\widetilde{\tau}(h_{\xi_{j}}^{+}(x_{0}),{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}})>H+\log T\right\}=\int_{H}^{\infty}\rho_{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}}(s)ds

with the
\operatorname{L{}}^{1}
probability density

(8.4)

\rho_{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}}(s)=\mathrm{e}^{-s}\Psi_{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}}(\mathrm{e}^{-s}).

Proof.

The critical observation is that

(8.5)

\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\widetilde{\tau}(\pi\circ h_{s}^{+}(x_{0}),{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}})\leq R

if and only if

(8.6)

\xi^{\pi}_{1}(x_{0},{\text{\boldmath$\sigma$}},R)>T.

Hence Eq. (
7.13
) of Corollary
7.1
yields, for
T=\mathrm{e}^{R}X
and
X=\mathrm{e}^{-H}

\ell_{y}=d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\kappa}(\sigma_{\kappa})).

Here the upper bound follows from the observation that the point
y^{\prime}
is at height
\tau(y^{\prime})
and therefore contained in a closed horocycle of length
\mathrm{e}^{-\tau(y^{\prime})}
