Adaptive and Implicit Regularization for Matrix Completion
Zhemin Li, Tao Sun, Hongxia Wang, Bao Wang

Abstract
The explicit low-rank regularization, e.g., nuclear norm regularization, has been widely used in imaging sciences. However, it has been found that implicit regularization outperforms explicit ones in various image processing tasks. Another issue is that the fixed explicit regularization limits the applicability to broad images since different images favor different features captured by different explicit regularizations. As such, this paper proposes a new adaptive and implicit low-rank regularization that captures the low-rank prior dynamically from the training data. The core of our new adaptive and implicit low-rank regularization is parameterizing the Laplacian matrix in the Dirichlet energy-based regularization, which we call the regularization AIR . Theoretically, we show that the adaptive regularization of AIR enhances the implicit regularization and vanishes at the end of training. We validate AIR’s effectiveness on various benchmark tasks, indicating that the AIR is particularly favorable for the scenarios when the missing entries are non-uniform. The code can be found at https://github.com/lizhemin15/AIR-Net .

Introduction

Natural images usually lie in a high-dimensional ambient space, but with a much lower intrinsic dimension
[
1
]
, which has motivated many statistical priors for image processing, including sparsity
[
2
]
and low-rank
[

. Other priors, such as Total Variation (TV) and Dirichlet Energy (DE), also play vital roles in image processing. There is no standard way to choose a proper prior to a particular task, and people always depend on empirical experiences.

This paper is devoted to establishing a new adaptive regularization. The core of our proposed adaptive regularization is parameterizing the Laplacian matrix in the conventional DE regularization, and this regularization changes on the fly as the underlying matrix gets updated. In particular, the proposed adaptive regularizer estimates the prior matrix based on the historical information during learning iterations for matrix completion. Compared to the existing regularization schemes for matrix completion, the proposed regularizer is adaptable to matrix completion problems from different applications with different missing patterns. Furthermore, our adaptive regularizer learns hyperparameters from data rather than manually tuning, saving tremendous computational costs. Theoretically, we prove that solving our proposed model with gradient descent can enhance the low-rank property of the recovered matrices, c.f. Theorem

2

2.1.3
Total variation and Dirichlet energy regularization

Compared to solving exact low-rank matrix completion problems, directly applying DMF for solving low-rank-related practical problems, including image inpainting, often gives opposite results. As shown in Figure
1
(b), the deep matrix factorization model performs inferior to the shallow models. By investigating the optimization trajectory during the gradient descent procedure, we notice that the major issue that causes deeper models to perform worse than the shallow ones is because the underlying image inpainting problem cannot be precisely formulated as a low-rank matrix completion problem. Indeed, some detailed structures of the matrix correspond to small singular values of the matrix, which the low-rank prior cannot entirely model. To resolve the above issue, we need to enforce other priors to compensate for subtleties in solving image inpainting problems and beyond.

A simple but valuable prior for natural image processing is piece-wise smoothness, which can be induced using the Total Variation (TV) regularization
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
. TV has been integrated with DMF, achieving promising recovery results for matrix completion problems, especially when the rate of missing entries is high
[
34
]
. As illustrated in Figure
1
(c), deep DMF models outperform shallow DMF models when TV regularization is enforced. Though TV is a natural prior for natural images, it is suboptimal or inappropriate for many other practical applications, including recommendation systems through matrix completion. Besides the TV regularization, the Dirichlet Energy (DE)-based regularization, defined through the Laplacian matrix
[
11
]
, has also been employed for low-rank matrix completion. The DE regularization can describe the similarity between rows and columns of a given matrix. Moreover, we can view the DE regularization as a nonlocal extension of the TV regularization.

2.2
Do we need a new regularization: Fixed or adaptive

Given a matrix completion problem, we can integrate several existing explicit regularizers with DMF or other matrix completion models to obtain some successful results. However, the design or selection of explicit regularizers is often task- and missing pattern-dependent. As shown in Figure
1
(d), (e), and (f), although TV regularization performs quite well for random missing patterns and natural images, the performance of using TV regularizers dramatically degrades when they are applied for other missing patterns and data. As such, we need to develop a new regularizer adaptable to different missing patterns and different types of data.

3
Adaptive Laplacian Regularization

Besides low-rank, self-similarity is another widely used prior for solving inverse problems, and the self-similarity is usually measured by the DE. A particular type of DE is the TV
[
36
]
, which describes the piece-wise self-similarity within a given image. To adapt the DE regularization to different missing patterns and different types of data, we raise its degree of freedom by introducing a learnable DE regularization. In particular, we propose the following model

\textrm{minimize}_{{\bm{X}},{\bm{W}}_{i}}\left\{{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{all}}:=\Bigg({\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{Y}}}\left({\bm{Y}},{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{X}})\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\cdot{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{i}}\left({\mathcal{T}}_{i}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\right)\Bigg)\right\},

(1)

where the linear operator
{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{o}
,
{\bm{Y}}\in{\mathbb{Y}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{o}
is a measure vector and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{Y}}
is a distance metric on
{\mathbb{Y}}
. Different from other regularization models for matrix completion, here
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is parameterized by products of matrix which tends to be low-rank implicitly
, and
{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{i}}
is an adaptive regularizer and
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
is a constant.
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}(\cdot)
is a linear or nonlinear transformation. See Section
3.2
for details. A particular case of (
1
) for matrix completion is given in Section

below.

3.1
Self-similarity and Dirichlet energy

Given a matrix
{\bm{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
, DE associated with the weighted adjacency matrix
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
, along rows of
{\bm{X}}
, is a classical approach to encode the self-similarity prior of the matrix
{\bm{X}}
. The adjacency matrix
{\bm{A}}
measures the similarity between rows of
{\bm{X}}
, and
{\bm{A}}_{ij}
is bigger if rows
i
and
j
of
{\bm{X}}
are more similar. The corresponding Laplacian matrix of
{\bm{A}}
is
{\bm{L}}:={\bm{D}}-{\bm{A}}
, where
{\bm{D}}
is the degree matrix with
{\bm{D}}_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bm{A}}_{ij}
and
{\bm{D}}_{ij}=0
if
i\neq j
. Then we can mathematically formulate the DE as follows

\text{tr}({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{X}})=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq m}{\bm{A}}_{ij}\left\|{\bm{X}}_{i,:}-{\bm{X}}_{j,:}\right\|^{2}.

It is evident that when DE is minimized, rows
{\bm{X}}_{i,:}
and
{\bm{X}}_{j,:}
become closer with bigger
{\bm{A}}_{ij}
.

There are two major obstacles to using DE in applications: (i)
{\bm{L}}
is unknown for an incomplete matrix, and (ii) DE only encodes the similarity between rows of
{\bm{X}}
; other similarities such as block similarity cannot be captured. To resolve the above two issues, we parameterize
{\bm{L}}
as a function of
{\bm{X}}
and learn it during completing
{\bm{X}}
.

3.2
Learned Dirichlet energy as an adaptive regularizer

Directly parameterizing
{\bm{L}}
without enforcing any particular structure and then optimizing it by minimizing
\text{tr}({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{X}})
might result in all entries of
{\bm{L}}
being very negative. To remedy this problem, we first recall a few essential properties of
{\bm{L}}
as follows:

1.

The Laplacian matrix
{\bm{L}}
can be generated from the corresponding adjacency matrix
{\bm{A}}
, that is
{\bm{L}}={\bm{D}}-{\bm{A}}
, where
{\bm{D}}
is the degree matrix.

2.

{\bm{A}}
is a symmetric matrix and
{\bm{A}}_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j
.

One of the most straightforward approaches is to model
{\bm{A}}
as the covariance matrix, e.g.,
{\bm{A}}:={\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}
, of the rows of
{\bm{X}}
. However, this model may not capture sufficient similarities among rows of
{\bm{A}}
. To this end, the self-attention mechanism
[
39
]
has been proposed to model similarities among different rows. However, the learning self-attention mechanism usually requires a large amount of training data, which is not the case for the matrix completion problem. Therefore, we cannot leverage the off-the-shelf self-attention mechanism for matrix completion.

We propose a new parameterization of the adjacency matrix that is similar to but simpler than the self-attention mechanism. Our new parametrization can capture row similarities of a given matrix beyond the covariance. In particular, we parameterize
{\bm{A}}
as follows

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\bm{A}}=\frac{\exp({\bm{W}}+{\bm{W}}^{\top})}{\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}\exp({\bm{W}})\mathbf{1}_{m}}\\
{\bm{L}}=\left({\bm{A}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}\right)\odot\mathbf{I}_{m}-{\bm{A}}\end{array}\right.,

(2)

where
\mathbf{1}_{m}
is an
m
-dimensional vector whose entries are all 1s,
\mathbf{I}_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix,
\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}
is the
m\times m
matrix whose entries are all 1s and
\odot
is Hadamard product.
{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
has the same size as
{\bm{A}}
and
\exp(\cdot)
denotes the element-wise exponential. Note that the above parameterization of
{\bm{A}}
guarantees
{\bm{A}}
to be symmetric, and all its entries are positive. To search for the optimal
{\bm{A}}
, we need to update
{\bm{W}}
start from a certain initialization. It is clear that the minimum value of DE is 0, i.e.,
\text{tr}({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{X}})=0
, which is obtained when
{\bm{A}}={\bm{L}}={\bf 0}
and this is known as the trivial solution. Our parameterization (
2
) directly avoids the above trivial solution since
{\bm{A}}_{ij}>0
. In Theorem
2
, we will show that
{\bm{L}}
will converge to a non-trivial minimum under the gradient descent dynamics using the parameterization in (
2

To capture more types of self-similarity, we further replace
{\bm{X}}
with
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}\left({\bm{X}}\right)
, where
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}(\cdot)
is a linear or nonlinear transformation that will be specified in particular applications. And we can generalize the above adaptive regularizer as follows

{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{i}}\left({\mathcal{T}}_{i}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left[{\mathcal{T}}_{i}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\right]^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{i}\left({\bm{W}}_{i}\right){\mathcal{T}}_{i}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\right),i=1,2,\ldots,N,

(3)

where
{\bm{L}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}\times m_{i}}
is parameterized by
{\bm{W}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}\times m_{i}}
following (
2

{\mathcal{T}}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
transforms

into another domain, making the adaptive regularization able to capture different similarities in data.
The common choice of
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}(\cdot)
can be
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}\left({\bm{X}}\right)={\bm{X}}
and

Another particularly interesting case is

Proof.

By direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{X}}}\left(\lambda_{r}{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{r}}+\lambda_{c}{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathrm{tr}\left(\lambda_{r}{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{r}{\bm{X}}+\lambda_{c}{\bm{X}}{\bm{L}}_{c}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\right)}{\partial{\bm{X}}}=2\lambda_{r}{\bm{L}}_{r}{\bm{X}}+2\lambda_{c}{\bm{X}}{\bm{L}}_{c}

\displaystyle=2\lambda_{r}{\bm{L}}_{r}\sum\limits_{s}\sigma_{s}{\bm{U}}_{s,:}{\bm{V}}_{s,:}^{\top}+2\lambda_{c}\sum\limits_{s}\sigma_{s}{\bm{U}}_{s,:}{\bm{V}}_{s,:}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{c}.

Note that

\langle{\bm{V}}_{s,:},{\bm{V}}_{s^{\prime},:}\rangle=\langle{\bm{U}}_{s,:},{\bm{U}}_{s^{\prime},:}\rangle=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&s=s^{\prime}\\
0,&s\neq s^{\prime}\\
\end{array}\right.,

Therefore

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}_{k,:}^{\top}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{X}}}\left(\lambda_{r}{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{r}}+\lambda_{c}{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{c}}\right)\right){\bm{V}}_{k,:}

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{k}(\lambda_{r}{\bm{U}}_{k,:}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{r}{\bm{U}}_{k,:}+\lambda_{c}{\bm{V}}_{k,:}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{c}{\bm{V}}_{k,:})=2\sigma_{k}\gamma_{k}(t),

where the term
\gamma_{k}(t)=2\sigma_{k}(\lambda_{r}{\bm{U}}_{k,:}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{r}{\bm{U}}_{k,:}+\lambda_{c}{\bm{V}}_{k,:}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{c}{\bm{V}}_{k,:})\geq 0
.
Furthermore, according to (
7
) in the appendix, we have

{\bm{U}}_{k,:}^{\top}\nabla_{{\bm{X}}}{\mathcal{L}}{\bm{V}}_{k,:}=-L\left(\sigma_{k}^{2}(t)\right)^{1-\frac{1}{L}}\left\langle\nabla_{{\bm{X}}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{X}}(t)),{\bm{U}}_{k,:}(t)\left[{\bm{V}}_{k,:}(t)\right]^{\top}\right\rangle.

Combined the above results with
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{X}})={\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{Y}}}({\bm{X}})+\lambda_{r}{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{r}}+\lambda_{c}{\mathcal{R}}_{{\bm{W}}_{c}}
, we have

\dot{\sigma}_{k}(t)=-L\left(\sigma_{k}^{2}(t)\right)^{1-\frac{1}{L}}\left\langle\nabla_{{\bm{X}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{Y}}}({\bm{X}}(t)),{\bm{U}}_{k,:}(t)\left[{\bm{V}}_{k,:}(t)\right]^{\top}\right\rangle-2L\left(\sigma_{k}^{2}(t)\right)^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{L}}\gamma_{k}(t),

which completes the proof.
∎

Compared with the result of the vanilla DMF whose order of
\sigma_{k}(t)
is
2-\frac{2}{L}
, Theorem
1
demonstrates that AIR’s
\sigma_{k}(t)
has a higher dynamics order
3-\frac{2}{L}
. Note that the adaptive regularizer keeps
\gamma_{k}(t)\geq 0
. This way, a faster convergence rate gap appears between different singular values
\sigma_{r}
than the vanilla DMF. Therefore, AIR enhances the implicit low-rank regularization over DMF.

and

{\bm{L}}_{kl}^{*}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0,&(k,l)\in{\mathbb{S}}_{1}\\
\gamma,&(k,l)\in{\mathbb{S}}_{2}\\
-\sum_{l^{\prime}=1,l^{\prime}\neq l}^{m}{\bm{L}}_{kl^{\prime}}^{*},&k=l\end{array}\right..

is the
(k,l)
-th element of
{\bm{L}}(t)

\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}
is a matrix whose entries are all 1s.

and

.

5.2
AIR behaves like a momentum

This subsection makes a heuristic and direct connection between AIR and the momentum method. AIR converges to the vanilla DMF model when the adaptive regularization vanishes, and Corollary
1
shows that the adaptive regularization vanishes exponentially fast. We need to distinguish AIR and DMF according to the optimization trajectory.
We compare three models (DMF, DMF+TV, and DMF+AIR, i.e., AIR) in Figure
6
, and we see that the three models perform dramatically different near the convergence. At the beginning of training, the observed and unobserved MSEs of the three models drop similarly.
When the observed MSE becomes smaller, the model learns
details in observed elements. The unobserved MSE increased during the observed MSE decrease in the vanilla DMF and DMF+TV cases. Our proposed AIR keeps the decaying trend for both observed and unobserved MSEs.

Looking back into the training of AIR, we see that the update of
{\bm{X}}(t+1)
involves both
{\bm{X}}(t)
and
{\bm{L}}(t)
, and the update of
{\bm{L}}(t)
depends on
{\bm{X}}(t-1)
.
To understand the training dynamics, we consider the following simplified model

\textrm{minimize}_{{\bm{X}},{\bm{L}}}\left\{{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{Y}}+\text{tr}\left({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{X}}\right)\right\},

and we have

\nabla_{{\bm{X}}(t)}{\mathcal{L}}=\nabla_{{\bm{X}}(t)}{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{Y}}+2{\bm{L}}(t){\bm{X}}(t),

where
{\bm{L}}(t)
is the function of
\left\{{\bm{X}}(t_{0})\mid t_{0}<t\right\}
. Therefore, every iteration step of AIR leverages all the learned previous information
\left\{{\bm{X}}(t_{0})\mid t_{0}<t\right\}
. While the update of both vanilla DMF and DMF+TV only depends on
{\bm{X}}(t)
.
From this viewpoint, AIR shares a similar spirit as the momentum method, which leverages history to improve performance.

5.4
Contrasting computational time of AIR with DMF

The proposed AIR has several advantages, including avoiding over-fitting and adapting to data. However,
AIR has more parameters than vanilla DMF, requiring
more computational time in each iteration. In this subsection, we compare the complexity of AIR with two benchmark algorithms, namely, DMF and RDMF.

We focus on square matrices of the size
m\times m
.
The loss function of
L
-layer DMF is given by
\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{A}}\left(\prod_{l=0}^{L-1}{\bm{W}}^{[l]}\right)-{\bm{Y}}^{*}\right\|_{F}^{2}
, where
{\bm{W}}^{[l]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
for
l=0,\ldots,L-1
and
{\bm{X}}=\prod_{l=0}^{L-1}{\bm{W}}^{[l]}
is the recovered matrix. Therefore the computational complexity of computing the loss function of DMF is
{\mathcal{O}}((L-1)m^{3})
. As for RDMF, its loss function involves the calculation of an additional TV term whose computational complexity is
{\mathcal{O}}(m^{2})
. Therefore, RDMF is only slightly more expensive than DMF.
The AIR’s loss function calculates two additional adaptive regularization terms
tr({\bm{X}}{\bm{L}}_{r}{\bm{X}}^{T})
and
tr({\bm{X}}^{T}{\bm{L}}_{c}{\bm{X}})
, with computational complexity being
{\mathcal{O}}(2m^{3})
.

The previous analysis shows that the computational complexity of each model is dominated by the size of
{\bm{X}}
(
m
) and the depth of the model (
L
). We numerically verify the previous computational complexity analysis on the Barbara image with different
m
and
L
. In particular, we iterate each model for
10000
iterations and report the computational time in Table
2
. We conduct our experiments on Tesla V100 GPUs and report the GPU time consumption of three kinds of DMF-based methods, where the reported GPU times are averaged over ten independent runs. The numerical results show that:

1.

For any fixed
m
and
L
, AIR takes more computational time than RDMF, and DMF is computationally the cheapest among the three models.

2.

For any fixed
m
, as
L
increases, the ratio between the computational time of AIR and DMF decays. This is because the computational complexity of DMF increases as DMF becomes deeper. Nevertheless, the computational complexity of both TV and adaptive regularization is independent of the depth
L
.

3.

For any fixed depth
L
, as
m
increases, the ratio between the computational time of RDMF and DMF decays, while the ratio between the computational time of AIR and DMF keeps near a constant. This result echoes our computational complexity analysis. In particular, the computational complexity ratio between RDMF and DMF is
{\mathcal{O}}(1+\frac{1}{(L-1)m})
and the ratio between AIR and DMF is
{\mathcal{O}}(1+\frac{2}{(L-1)})
.

Although AIR takes more computational time than the baseline algorithms, it achieves significantly better accuracy than the baselines, which is valuable in many applications.

Conclusion

This paper proposes that AIR solve matrix completion problems without knowing the prior in advance. AIR parameterizes the Laplacian matrix in DE and can adaptively learn the regularization according to different data at different training steps.
In addition, we demonstrate that AIR can avoid the over-fitting issue.
AIR is a generic framework for solving inverse problems that simultaneously encode the self-similarity and low-rank prior. Some interesting properties of the proposed AIR deserve further theoretical analysis. Such as the momentum phenomenon, which calls for a more detailed dynamic analysis.

is
diagonal. We denote the diagonal entries of
{\bm{S}}(t)

\sigma_{1}(t),\ldots,

{\bm{U}}(t)
and
{\bm{V}}(t)

denoted by

Proof.

We denote
{\bm{X}}={\mathcal{T}}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
, then we consider the differential of
\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{X}}\right)

d\left[\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{X}}\right)\right]
, note that

\displaystyle d\left[\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{X}}\right)\right]=\mathrm{tr}\left[d\left({\bm{L}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\left(d{\bm{A}}\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}\right)\odot I_{m}{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}-d{\bm{A}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}_{m}\odot\left(d{\bm{A}}\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}\right)\right)-{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}d{\bm{A}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{I}}_{m}\right)^{\top}d{\bm{A}}\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}d{\bm{A}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\left(\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{I}}_{m}\right)\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}\right)^{\top}d{\bm{A}}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}d{\bm{A}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{I}}_{m}\right)-{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\right)d{\bm{A}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\left(\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{I}}_{m}\right)\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\right)d{\bm{A}}\right].

We denote
{\bm{C}}=\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{I}}_{m}\right)\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top},S=\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}\exp\left({\bm{W}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{m}
, then

\displaystyle d\left[\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{X}}\right)\right]=\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{C}}d{\bm{A}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S^{2}}\mathrm{tr}\left[{\bm{C}}\left(S\exp({\bm{W}}+{\bm{W}}^{\top})\odot d({\bm{W}}+{\bm{W}}^{\top})\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-{\bm{C}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}\left(\exp({\bm{W}})\odot d{\bm{W}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{m}\right)\exp({\bm{W}}+{\bm{W}}^{\top})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[{\bm{C}}\left({\bm{A}}\odot d\left({\bm{W}}+{\bm{W}}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{S^{2}}\mathrm{tr}\left[\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}\left(\exp({\bm{W}})\odot d{\bm{W}}\right)\right]\mathrm{tr}\left[{\bm{C}}\exp\left({\bm{W}}+{\bm{W}}^{\top}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[{\bm{C}}\left({\bm{A}}\odot d\left({\bm{W}}+{\bm{W}}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]-\frac{1}{S}\mathrm{tr}\left(\exp({\bm{W}}^{T})d{\bm{W}}\right)\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{C}}{\bm{A}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[{\bm{C}}\left({\bm{A}}\odot d\left({\bm{W}}+{\bm{W}}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]-\mathrm{tr}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{C}}{\bm{A}}\right){\bm{A}}^{\prime}d{\bm{W}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\left(\left({\bm{C}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{A}}\right)^{\top}+{\bm{C}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{A}}-\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{C}}{\bm{A}}\right){\bm{A}}^{\prime}\right)d{\bm{W}}\right].

Therefore,

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{i}{\bm{X}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\bm{C}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{A}}\right)^{\top}+{\bm{C}}^{\top}\odot{\bm{A}}-\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{C}}{\bm{A}}\right){\bm{A}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2{\bm{C}}\odot{\bm{A}}-2\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{C}}{\bm{A}}^{\prime}\right){\bm{A}}^{\prime}.

Notice that
{\bm{X}}={\mathcal{T}}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
, the proposition is proved.
