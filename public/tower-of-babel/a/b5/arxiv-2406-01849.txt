Conditional uncorrelation equals independence
Dawid Tarłowski

Abstract
We show that the stochastic independence of real-valued random variables is equivalent to the conditional uncorrelation, where the conditioning takes place over the Cartesian products of intervals. Next, we express the mutual independence in terms of the conditional correlation matrix. Our results extend the results of Jaworski et al. (Electron. J. Stat., 18(1), 653-673, 2024), which are based on the copula functions and assume the existence of the joint density of the variables. We relax this assumption and show that the independence characterization via conditional uncorrelation is valid in full generality - that is, for all kinds of random variables and any dependencies between them. Additionally, we analyse the assumptions under which the independence is determined by the local uncorrelation. The measure-theoretic methodology we present uses the Radon-Nikodym derivative to reduce the multidimensional characterization problem to the simple one-dimensional conditioning. To demonstrate the potential usefulness of the presented results, various numerical examples are presented.



keywords
Radon-Nikodym derivative, independence, correlation, linear regression

Preliminaries

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. Given

will denote the characteristic function of

1_{A}(x)=1

1_{A}(x)=0

\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and we will say that

is a random variable (r.v.) iff
Z\colon(\Omega,\Sigma)\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

Z^{-1}(C)\in\Sigma

. The probability distribution of r.v.
Z\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{Z}
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{Z}
is a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
\mathbb{P}_{Z}(D)=\mathbb{P}[Z^{-1}(D)]
. We will often use notation
\{Z\in D\}:=Z^{-1}(D)
and
\mathbb{P}[Z\in D]:=\mathbb{P}_{Z}(D)
. If we shortly say that
Z
is a random variable without specifying the set of values then we mean that
Z
is one-dimensional, i.e.
Z\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. In this paper all random variables are defined on
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
. The mathematical expectation of r.v.
Z
is defined by
E[Z]=\int\limits_{\Omega}Zd\mathbb{P}
. If
U\in\Sigma
satisfies
\mathbb{P}[U]>0
then
\mathbb{P}[\cdot|U]
will denote the conditional probability measure given by
\mathbb{P}[C|U]=\frac{\mathbb{P}[C\cap U]}{\mathbb{P}[U]}
,
C\in\Sigma
, and

will denote the conditional expectation
E[Z|U]=\int\limits_{\Omega}Zd\mathbb{P}[\cdot|U]
. Naturally,
E[Z|U]=\frac{E[Z\cdot 1_{U}]}{\mathbb{P}[U]}
. If
U\in\Sigma
is of the form
U=\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}
then we will write

E[Z|X_{1}\in A_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in A_{n}]:=E[Z|U].

If
X
and
Y
are non-constant random variables with
E[X^{2}]<\infty
and
E[Y^{2}]<\infty
then both the covariance and the (Pearson‘s) correlation are well defined:

\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]\cdot E[Y]\mbox{ \ and \ }\operatorname{cor}(X,Y)=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_{X}\cdot\sigma_{Y}},

where
\sigma_{Z}=\sqrt{E[Z^{2}]-(E[Z])^{2}}
denotes the standard deviation of r.v.
Z
.

If
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
are Borel sets with
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times B)>0
then one may consider the corresponding conditional covariance and the conditional correlation, i.e. for
U=\{(X,Y)\in A\times B\}
one may calculate those coefficients with respect to the conditional probability measure
\mathbb{P}[\cdot|U]
. The conditional covariance
\operatorname{cov}_{(A,B)}(X,Y)
is defined by

\operatorname{cov}_{(A,B)}(X,Y)=E[XY|(X,Y)\in A\times B]-E[X|(X,Y)\in A\times B]\cdot E[Y|(X,Y)\in A\times B],

and the conditional correlation
\operatorname{cor}_{(A,B)}(X,Y)
is defined by :

\operatorname{cor}_{(A,B)}(X,Y)=\frac{\operatorname{cov}_{(A,B)}(X,Y)}{\sigma_{(A,B)}(X)\cdot\sigma_{(A,B)}(Y)},

where
\sigma_{(A,B)}(\cdot)
is the standard deviation calculated with respect to the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}[\cdot|U]=\mathbb{P}[\cdot|X\in A,Y\in B]
. Note that the conditional covariance is well defined for any bounded rectangle
A\times B
regardless of whether the
X
and
Y
are square-integrable.

Now, we will shortly introduce some notation and basic facts from measure theory,
[
17
]
,
[
24
]
. Let
\mathcal{M}^{1}(\Omega,\Sigma)
denote the set of probability measures on the measurable space
(\Omega,\Sigma)
. We will say that
Q
is a nonnegative measure on
(\Omega,\Sigma)
if
Q
is of the form
Q=c\cdot P
, where
c\geq 0
and
P\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(\Omega,\Sigma)
. We will say that
Q
is a signed measure if
Q=c_{1}\cdot P_{1}-c_{2}\cdot P_{2}
, where
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 0
and
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(\Omega,\Sigma)
. A signed measure

is absolutely continuous with respect to

, denoted by

, if for any
A\in\Sigma
with
P(A)=0
we have
Q(A)=0
. If
Q<P
, then by Radon-Nikodym theorem there exists a measurable function
h\colon(\Omega,\Sigma)\to(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
satisfying:

Q(A)=\int\limits_{A}h(x)P(dx),\ A\in\Sigma.

(2)

Any such function
h
is called the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
Q
with respect to
P
, denoted by
\frac{dQ}{dP}
. Naturally, if
h_{1}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and

satisfy (

h_{1}=h_{2}

P
- almost surely, and formally, the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{dQ}{dP}
is an element of
L^{1}(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
, the space of
P-
integrable functions, where the functions that agree
P-
almost everywhere are identified. If we shortly write
\frac{dQ}{dP}(x)=h(x)
or
\frac{dQ}{dP}=h
, then we just mean that function
h
satisfies
(
2
)
, and we do remember about the almost sure nature of such equalities. A useful property is that if we have three measures with
Q_{2}<Q_{1}
and
Q_{1}<P
, and
\frac{dQ_{2}}{dP}(x)=h_{2}(x)
,
\frac{dQ_{1}}{dP}(x)=h_{1}(x)
, then

\frac{dQ_{2}}{dQ_{1}}(x)=\frac{h_{2}(x)}{h_{1}(x)}\cdot 1_{\{z\colon h_{1}(z)>0\}}(x)\ \ \mbox{ for }Q_{1}-\mbox{ almost any }x\in\Omega.

(3)

In case of Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there is a beautiful characterization of
\frac{dQ}{dP}
credited to Besicovitch, namely, given
Q
and
P
, two finite Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the limit

h(x)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{Q(B(x,\varepsilon))}{P(B(x,\varepsilon))}

is well-defined for
P-\mbox{ almost any }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and defines a Borel measurable function
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
B(x,\varepsilon)
denotes the Euclidean ball centered at
x
with radius
\varepsilon
. Most importantly, if
Q<P
then we have

\frac{dQ}{dP}(x)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{Q(B(x,\varepsilon))}{P(B(x,\varepsilon))}\mbox{ \ \ \ \ \ for\ \ }P\mbox{\ \ - almost any }\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(4)

see Theorem 2.12 and Chapter 2 in
[
17
]
for more details.

Similarly, by Fubini‘s theorem, for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
,

Q_{2}(A)=E[1_{A}(X)XZ]=\int\limits_{A\times\mathbb{R}}xzf(x,z)dxdz=\int\limits_{A}x\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}zf(x,z)dzdx=\int\limits_{A}xh(x)dx.

As
Q_{1}(A)=\int\limits_{A}h(x)dx
and
Q_{2}(A)=\int\limits_{A}xh(x)dx
, we indeed have
\frac{dQ_{2}}{dQ_{1}}(x)=x
.

4
Main results

It is a simple consequence of Fubini‘s theorem that if random variables
X
and
Y
are independent (in the sense
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}=\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y}
) then
cov(X,Y)=0
, and it is easy to show that the independence of
X
and
Y
implies
cov_{(A,B)}(X,Y)=0
for any admissible test set
A\times B
. Theorem
4
states that this implication may be reversed if one considers the family of bounded intervals or half-bounded intervals as test sets. In particular, the square-integrable random variables
X
and
Y
are independent iff they are uncorrelated conditioning on the tails
\{X\geq t\}
and
\{Y\geq s\}
, i.e. when for any
t,s
with
\mathbb{P}[X\geq t,Y\geq s]>0
we have:
\operatorname{cov}_{([t,\infty),[s,\infty))}(X,Y)=0
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}=\{[a,b]\colon a<b\}
. For any random variables
X
and
Y
, the following conditions are equivalent:

1)

X
and
Y
are independent,

2)

for any
U=\{X\in A,Y\in B\}
with
A,B\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times B)>0
,

E[XY|U]=E[X|U]\cdot E[Y|U].

Under additional assumption:
E|X|<\infty
and
E|Y|<\infty
, the bounded intervals may be replaced with the half-bounded intervals
\{[t,\infty)\colon\ t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

Proof.

The implication
"1)\Rightarrow 2)"
is a rather straigthforward calculation. We will prove the second implication
"2)\Rightarrow 1)"
.

Step 1.
Assume that
E|X|<\infty
and
E|Y|<\infty
, and
\mathcal{K}=\{[a,b]\}_{a<b}
or
\mathcal{K}=\{[t,\infty)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.

1a.
First we consider the case
\mathbb{P}[Y>0]=1
. Fix
B\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbb{P}[Y\in B]>0
which implies that
E[Y1_{B}(Y)]>0
. More, for any
A\in\mathcal{K}
we have

E[1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]>0\Leftrightarrow E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]>0.

From condition 2) of the theorem, for any
A\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times B)>0
we have

\frac{E[XY1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}{\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times B)}=\frac{E[X1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}{\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times B)}\cdot\frac{E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}{\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times B)}

and thus

\frac{E[XY1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}{E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}=\frac{E[X1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}{E[1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}.

(13)

Note that

\hat{P_{1}}(A)=E[1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]\mbox{ and }\hat{Q_{1}}(A)=E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)],\ A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),

are nonnegative Borel measures with the same class of null sets
\hat{P_{1}}(A)=0\Leftrightarrow\hat{Q_{1}}(A)=0
. We now normalize those measures to obtain the corresponding probability measures:

Q_{1}(A):=\frac{1}{E[Y1_{B}(Y)]}\cdot E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)],\ A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})

and

P_{1}(A):=\\
P[X\in A|Y\in B]=\frac{E[1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}{E[1_{B}(Y)]},\ A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}).

We want to reformulate equation (
13
) in a way which will allow us to use Lemma
3
. In this purpose define the following signed measures

Q_{2}(A):=\frac{1}{E[Y1_{B}(Y)]}\cdot{E[XY1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]},\ \ A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),

P_{2}(A):=\frac{1}{E[1_{B}(Y)]}\cdot E[X1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)],\ \ A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),

so, for any
A\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbb{P}[X\in A,Y\in B]>0
, equation (
13
) takes the form

\frac{Q_{2}(A)}{Q_{1}(A)}=\frac{P_{2}(A)}{P_{1}(A)}.

(14)

Now, we apply Lemma
3
to the pair
Q_{2}
and
Q_{1}
(with
Z:=Y1_{B}(Y)
) which provides the Radon Nikodym-derivative
\frac{dQ_{2}}{dQ_{1}}(x)=x
. In case
\mathcal{K}=\{[a,b]\colon a<b\}
, Equation
(
14
)
forces
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}=\frac{dQ_{2}}{dQ_{1}}
. Indeed, by (
4
), for
(P_{1}+Q_{1})
- almost any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}(x)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{P_{2}([x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon])}{P_{1}([x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon])}\mbox{ and }\frac{dQ_{2}}{dQ_{1}}(x)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{Q_{2}([x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon])}{Q_{1}([x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon])},

and the above limits are equal to each other by (
14
). However, in case
\mathcal{K}=\{[t,\infty)\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
we need to use Lemma
3
again: the pair
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, by Lemma
3
with
Z:=1_{B}(Y)
, satisfies
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}(x)=x
. Hence, in both cases, the equation (
14
) takes the form

\frac{\int\limits_{A}xQ_{1}(dx)}{Q_{1}(A)}=\frac{\int\limits_{A}xP_{1}(dx)}{P_{1}(A)},\ \mbox{ for any }A\in\mathcal{K}\mbox{ with }P_{1}(A)>0.

By Lemma
1
, we have
Q_{1}(A)=P_{1}(A)
,
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. As
B\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbb{P}_{Y}(B)>0
was fixed arbitrarily, by definitions of
Q_{1}
and
P_{1}
, we have obtained that for any
B\in\mathcal{K}
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times B)>0
, we have

\frac{E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}{E[1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}=\frac{E[Y1_{B}(Y)]}{E[1_{B}(Y)]}.

In other words, for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),B\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbb{P}[X\in A,Y\in B]>0
,

E[Y|X\in A,\ Y\in B]=E[Y|Y\in B].

(15)

As the conditioning
E[Y|X\in A,Y\in B]
does not depend on
X
, it is natural that
X
and
Y
are independent. To show this, we will use Lemma
3
again: Equation (
15
) may be rewritten as:

\frac{\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}[X\in A]}\cdot E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)]}{\mathbb{P}[Y\in B|X\in A]}=\frac{E[Y1_{B}(Y)]}{\mathbb{P}[Y\in B]},

and hence, by Lemma
3
,

\frac{\int\limits_{B}y\mathbb{P}_{Y}[dy|X\in A]}{\mathbb{P}[Y\in B|X\in A]}=\frac{\int\limits_{B}y\mathbb{P}_{Y}(dy)}{\mathbb{P}_{Y}[B]},

where
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),\ B\in\mathcal{K}
are arbitrary with
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times B)>0
.
By Observation
2
, we have
\mathbb{P}_{Y}(\ \cdot\ |X\in A)=\mathbb{P}_{Y}(\cdot)
, where
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
is arbitrary with
\mathbb{P}[X\in A]>0
. This implies that
X
and
Y
are independent.

1b.
Now consider the case
Y>-c
, where
c>0
. By the assumption,
X
and
Y
are conditionally uncorrelated in sense
cov_{(A,B)}(X,Y)=0
for any admissible
A
,
B\in\mathcal{K}
. By
1a.
, it is enough to show that
X
and

are conditionally uncorrelated too. Note that for any
A,B\in\mathcal{K}
we have
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y+c)}(A\times B)>0\Leftrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(A\times(B-c))>0
, where
B-c=\{b-c\colon b\in B\}
. Furthermore, the covariance is translation-invariant and the class
\mathcal{K}
is translation invariant in sense
B\in\mathcal{K}\Rightarrow B-c\in\mathcal{K}.
Thus, for any
A,B\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y+c)}(A\times B)>0
, we have:

cov_{(A,B)}(X,Y+c)=cov(X,Y+c|X\in A,Y+c\in B)=

=cov(X,Y|X\in A,Y+c\in B)=cov(X,Y|X\in A,Y\in B-c)=0.

Now, as we see that
Y+c
and
X
are conditionally uncorrelated, by point
1a.

X
and
Y+c
are independent. Hence,
X
and
Y
are independent.

1c.
Now we do not assume that
Y
is bounded from below. The random variables
X
and
Y
are independent if and only if for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{P}[Y>c]>0

the

and

are independent with respect to the conditional probability

, i.e.

\mathbb{P}[X\in A,Y\in B|Y>c]=\mathbb{P}[X\in A|Y>c]\cdot\mathbb{P}[Y\in B|Y>c],\ A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}).

For any
c
small enough let
cov_{\mathbb{P}_{c}}
denote the covariance calculated on the probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P}_{c})
. Note that for
A,B\in\mathcal{K}
, if

then

. Furthermore,

cov_{\mathbb{P}_{c}}(X,Y|X\in A,Y\in B)=cov(X,Y|X\in A,Y\in B\cap[c,\infty)).

Hence,
X
and
Y
are conditionally uncorrelated with respect to the conditional probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{c}=\mathbb{P}[\cdot|Y\geq c]
which, by
1b.
, implies that
X
and
Y
are independent with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{c}
. As

may be arbitrarily small,
X
and

are independent.

Step 2.
It remains to consider the case
\mathcal{K}=\{[a,b]\colon a<b\}
without assuming the existence of the first moments of

and

. To prove that

and

are independent it is sufficient to prove that they are independent with respect to the conditional probability

\mathbb{P}_{c}=\mathbb{P}[\ \cdot\ |(X,Y)\in[-c,c]^{2}]

for any

large enough to have
\mathbb{P}[(X,Y)\in[-c,c]^{2}]>0
. On the probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P}_{c})
the

and

are integrable and conditionally uncorrelated by the assumptions (note that if
A\in\mathcal{K}
and

then

), which by
Step 1
implies that

and

are
\mathbb{P}_{c}
- independent for any large

. ∎

Remark 1
.

One has to be careful while dealing with the quotient of measures. For instance, in case of the half-intervals
\mathcal{K}=\{(-\infty,t]\colon\ t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, the quotient of the form

\frac{P_{2}(A)}{P_{1}(A)},\ A\in\mathcal{K}^{+}_{P_{1}},

(16)

does not determine neither the ratio
\frac{P_{2}(D)}{P_{1}(D)}
,
D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, nor even the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}
. To illustrate that, it is enough to consider a simple two-state probability space: let
\Omega=\{0,1\}
and let
\Sigma=2^{\Omega}
be the family of all subsets of
\Omega
. Fix
a\in(0,1)
and define two measures on
(\Omega,\Sigma)
:

P_{1}(\{0\})=a,P_{1}(\{1\})=1-a,\mbox{ and }P_{2}(\{0\})=\frac{a}{2},P_{2}(\{1\})=1-\frac{a}{2}.

It is easy to notice that the ratio
(
16
)
does not depend on
a\in(0,1)
, namely, it is determined by the following:

\frac{P_{2}(\{0\})}{P_{1}(\{0\})}=\frac{1}{2}\mbox{ and }\frac{P_{2}(\{0,1\})}{P_{1}(\{0,1\})}=1.

At the same time, the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}
is uniquely defined by:

\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}(0)=\frac{1}{2}\mbox{ and }\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}(1)=\frac{1-\frac{a}{2}}{1-a},

and does depend on
a\in(0,1)
. Naturally, similar subtleties arise in the multidimensional settings. Replacing the family
\{[a,b]\colon a<b\}
with the family
\{(-\infty,t]\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is not always straightforward in characterisation problems.

Now, we will prove the multivariate generalisation of Theorem
4
. If
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are random variables then for
U=\{X_{1}\in A_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in A_{n}\}
with
\mathbb{P}[U]>0
we define

\operatorname{cov}_{U}(X_{i},X_{j})
,
\operatorname{cor}_{U}(X_{i},X_{j})
and
sd_{U}(\cdot)
as the covariance, correlation and the standard deviation calculated with respect to the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}[\cdot|U]
. Let
\Sigma_{U}
denote the conditional
correlation matrix, i.e.

\Sigma_{U}[i,j]=cor_{U}(X_{i},X_{j}),\ \ i,j=1,\dots,n.

Above, if for some
i
we have
sd_{U}(X_{i})=0
then we shortly write
cor_{U}(X_{i},X_{j}):=\delta_{ij}
(Kronecker delta) which will keep the notation compact. The following theorem generalizes Theorem
4
and will be proved by mathematical induction with use of the same techniques.

Theorem 5
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be random variables and let
\mathcal{K}=\{[a,b]\colon a<b\}
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are mutually independent (i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})}=\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{P}_{X_{n}}
),

2.

the conditional correlation matrix equals to the identity matrix, i.e.

\Sigma_{U}=I_{n},

for any
U=\{X_{1}\in A_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in A_{n}\}
such that
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbb{P}[U]>0
.

If
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
have finite second moments then the bounded intervals may be replaced with
\mathcal{K}=\{[t,\infty)\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

Proof.

The implication
"1.\Rightarrow 2."
is straightforward and we will focus on
"2.\Rightarrow 1."
The proof is based on the induction. The case
n=2
is a direct conclusion from Theorem
4
. For the induction step assume that Theorem
5
holds true for some natural
n\geq 2
and assume that we are given the random vector
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n+1})
which satisfies condition 2., i.e.
\Sigma_{U}=I_{n+1}
for any admissible
U
. We may apply Theorem
5
to any
n
-dimensional vector of the form
(X_{i})_{i\neq j}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{j-1},X_{j+1},\dots,X_{n})
which implies the mutual independence of
X_{1},\dots,X_{j-1},X_{j+1},\dots,X_{n}
. Thus, to show the induction step it is enough to show that some marginal variable
X_{j}
is independent of the
n
-dimensional vector
(X_{i})_{i\neq j}
. The general induction step from
"n"
to
"n+1"
is the same as in the case from
n=2
to
n=3
but the notation is slightly more complex. We will show the implication from
"n=2"
to
"n=3"
. Let
(X,Y,Z):=(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
. We will show that
Y
is independent of the vector
(X,Z)
. The proof is similar the reasoning from the proof of Theorem 1 and we will be a little more concise.

1.
Assume that
X,Y,Z
are square-integrable and
K=\{[a,b]\colon a<b\}
or
K=\{[t,\infty)\}
.

1a)
Assume that
Y>0
. If
(A,B,C)\in\mathcal{K}^{3}

, then for

which may be rewritten as

\frac{E[XY1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]}{E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]}=\frac{E[X1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]}{E[1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]}.

(17)

We fix
B,C\in\mathcal{K}
with

and we normalize the nonnegative measures
A\to E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]
and
A\to E[1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]]
to obtain the following two probability Borel measures

Q_{1}(A)=\frac{E[Y1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]}{E[Y1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]}\mbox{ and }P_{1}(A)=\frac{E[1_{A}(X)1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]}{E[1_{B}(Y)1_{C}(Z)]}.

Now we rewrite (
17

(18)

for any

and
B,C\in\mathcal{K}
with

\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y,Z)}[A\times B\times C]>0

By the assumptions,

and

1b)
If there is

.

Remark 2

.

and Chapter 6 in
[
7
]
.

In practice, given the sample of pairs of observations
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and a rectangle
A\times B
, the conditional versions of covariance and correlation coeficients are easy to estimate: one simply calculates the standard empirical covariance and correlation coefficients on the subsample of
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
determined by the set of indices:

I=\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\colon(x_{i},y_{i})\in A\times B\}.

The mathematical justification of this procedure follows directly from the properties of the conditional probability: if the random vectors
Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
i=1,2,\dots
, are i.i.d. and
U=\{(X_{1},Y_{1})\in A\times B\}
satisfies
\mathbb{P}[U]>0
then for

N_{1}:=\min\{n\colon Z_{n}\in A\times B\}\mbox{ and }N_{k+1}:=\min\{n>N_{k}\colon Z_{n}\in A\times B\}

the random variables
Z_{N_{1}},Z_{N_{2}},\dots
are independent and have probability distribution equal to the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{Z_{1}}[\cdot|U]
, i.e.

\mathbb{P}[Z_{N_{k}}\in D]=\mathbb{P}[Z_{1}\in D|U],\ D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),k\in\mathbb{N}.

In practice, set
A\times B
may be determined by the quantiles of the sample, see
[
12
]
for the theoretical properties of such estimators, or one can choose the conditioning set based on the specific features of the
sample. Now, we will discuss a few examples to better illustrate the general concept and its potential usefulness, without aiming to develop a rigorous statistical framework. We will start with the simple numerical illustration of the the
textbook example
[
3
]
, where
X
and
Y
are uncorrelated but the conditional correlation varies between
0
and
1
, depending on the choice of conditioning set. In this example the analytic formula for quantile conditioning can be derived explicitely, see equation (20) in
[
10
]
.

Example 3

(Illustrative example.)
.

Let
X
and
Z
be independent and such that
\mathbb{P}_{X}=N(0,1)
and
\mathbb{P}[Z=1]=\mathbb{P}[Z=-1]=\frac{1}{2}
. Define
Y=Z\cdot X
. It is easy to see that
\mathbb{P}_{Y}=N(0,1)
,
X
and
Y
are not independent, and at the same time they are uncorrelated:
\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=E[YX]-E[X]E[Y]=E[ZX^{2}]=E[Z]\cdot E[X^{2}]=0.
Also, it is easy to see that the vector
(X,Y)
is supported on the set
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon|x|=|y|\}
, and that for any
t>0
and
U=\{|X|\leq t,|Y|\leq t\}
we will have

\operatorname{cor}_{U}(X,Y)=0.

The above follows directly from the geometric interpreation of the Pearson‘s correlation coefficient: given any square
\{|X|\leq t,|Y|\leq t\}
, the line of best fit between
Y
(independent variable) and
X
(dependent variable) is horizontal. At the same time, on the set
V=\{X\geq 0,Y\geq 0\}
we have full linear dependence:
Y=X
and
\operatorname{cor}_{V}(X,Y)=1
. Figure 1 presents a simple numerical illustration.

200
points are sampled from the probability distribution of the vector
(X,Y)
. The red dashed line is the contour of the square
U=[-1,1]^{2}
and the green dashed line is the contour of the set
V=\{(x,y)\colon x\geq 0,y\geq 0\}

Example 4

(Discrete variables)
.

Assume that
X\in\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
and
Y\in\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathbb{P}[X=a_{i}]>0
,
\mathbb{P}[Y=b_{j}]>0
, for all
i
,
j
. Assume additionally that
a_{i}<a_{i+1}
and
b_{j}<b_{j+1}
for all
i<n
,
j<n
. From Theorem
4
it follows that
X
and
Y
are independent if and only if for any
i\leq n,j\leq m
.

E[XY|X\leq a_{i},Y\leq b_{j}]=E[X|X\leq a_{i},Y\leq b_{j}]\cdot E[Y|X\leq a_{i},Y\leq b_{j}].

(22)

If
i=1
or
j=1
, equation
(
22
)
is satisfied trivially. Hence, in the simplest scenario:
X\in\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
and
Y\in\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
, we have direct conclusion that
X
and
Y
are independent if and only if

E[XY]=E[X]\cdot E[Y].

(23)

In particular, if
X\in\{0,1\}
and
Y\in\{0,1\}
, it is easy to see that condition
E[XY]=E[X]\cdot E[Y]
transforms directly into
\mathbb{P}[X=1,Y=1]=\mathbb{P}[X=1]\cdot\mathbb{P}[Y=1],
which implies the independence of
X
and
Y
.

If
X
and
Y
are categorical variables then one can apply equation (
22
) to any transformation of the variables, namely, one can apply equation (
22
) to
\hat{X}
and
\hat{Y}
, where
\hat{X}=\varphi_{1}(X)
and
\hat{Y}=\varphi_{2}(Y)
, and
\varphi_{1}\colon\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi_{2}\colon\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\}\to\mathbb{R}
are injective mappings chosen arbitrarilly. In practice, however, the choice of transformations
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
may influence the robustness of the corresponding statistical tests, especially when the values of
X
and
Y
do not have a natural order. An exception is the case of two-points distributions, in which the Pearson‘s coefficient will be transformations‘ invariant - this follows directly from its invariance under the linear transformations.

Now we will discuss the concept of local uncorrelation,
[
7
]
. The authors of
[
10
]
pointed out an ellegant property of local uncorrelation: if the random variables
X
and
Y
are locally linearly independent, then they are stochastically independent. More precisely,
X
and
Y
are independent if and only if they are uncorrelated conditioning on rectangles of arbitrarily small size. While this observation holds true under the assumptions of
[
10
]
, it cannot be extended to full generality. We will start the discussion with the simple example.

Example 7
.

Assume that
X
is supported on the discrete set
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
, and
Y\in\mathbb{R}
is arbitrary. If
\varepsilon>0
is small enough then any rectangle
D=[a,b]\times[c,d]
with
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(D)>0
and
\operatorname{diam}(D)<\varepsilon
satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{X}([a,b])=\mathbb{P}_{X}[\{a_{j}\}]
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
and thus for
U=\{(X,Y)\in[a,b]\times[c,d]\}
we have:

E[XY|U]=E[XY|X=a_{j},Y\in[c,d]]=a_{j}\cdot E[Y|X=a_{j},Y\in[c,d]]=

=E[X|X=a_{j},Y\in[c,d]]\cdot E[Y|X=a_{j},Y\in[c,d]]=E[X|U]\cdot E[Y|U],

regardless of whether
X
and
Y
are dependent or not.

Now, we will address the question of when the local uncorrelation determines independence. Recall that the support of a measure
P
, denoted by
\operatorname{supp}(P)
, is the smallest closed set
D
with
P(D)=1
. In case of Example
7
, the support of
\mathbb{P}_{X}
is not an interval, and in such case the local conditioning
E[X|X\in A]
,
A\in K_{X}^{+}(\varepsilon)
, does not determine the measure
\mathbb{P}_{X}
, see Example
1
from Section
3
. Given an arbitrary
P\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\varepsilon>0
, recall the notation:
K^{+}_{P}(\varepsilon)=\{[a,b]\colon P([a,b])>0\mbox{ and }b-a\leq\varepsilon\}.
We will prove the following lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Assume that
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are random variables such that both supports
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}})
and
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}})
are non-degenerate intervals (bounded or unbounded). Let
P_{1}:=\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}
and
P_{2}:=\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and assume that
\mathcal{K}_{P_{2}}^{+}(\varepsilon)\subset\mathcal{K}_{P_{1}}^{+}(\varepsilon)
. If

E[X_{1}|X_{1}\in[a,b]]=E[X_{2}|X_{2}\in[a,b]]\mbox{\ \ for any \ }[a,b]\in\mathcal{K}_{P_{2}}^{+}(\varepsilon),

(24)

then
P_{1}=P_{2}
.

Proof.

First we will prove that equation (
24
) forces
\mathcal{K}_{P_{1}}^{+}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{K}_{P_{2}}^{+}(\varepsilon)
. For a contradiction, assume that for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<b-a\leq\varepsilon
, we have
P_{1}([a,b])>0
and
P_{2}([a,b])=0
. As the support of
P_{2}
is an interval, this implies that
P_{2}([b,+\infty])=1
oraz
P_{2}(-\infty,a])=1
. Without loss of generality assume that
P_{2}([b,+\infty])=1
.
Let

x:=\inf\operatorname{supp}(P_{2}).

As
x\geq b
, and
\operatorname{supp}(P_{2})
is an interval with
\operatorname{supp}(P_{2})\subset\operatorname{supp}(P_{1})
, we have that
x
belongs to the interior of
\operatorname{supp}(P_{1})
. Hence,
P_{1}\left([x-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x)\right)>0
and
P_{1}\left((x,x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]\right)>0
, which forces

E[X_{1}|X_{1}\in[x-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]]<E[X_{1}|X_{1}\in[x,x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]].

(25)

At the same time, by the definition of
x
we have
P_{2}\left([x-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x)\right)=0
, and hence

E[X_{2}|X_{2}\in[x-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]]=E[X_{2}|X_{2}\in[x,x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]].

(26)

By the assumptions:
E[X_{1}|X_{1}\in[x,x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]]=E[X_{2}|X_{2}\in[x,x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]]
, and thus (
25
) and (
26
) lead to:

E[X_{1}|X_{1}\in[x-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]]<E[X_{2}|X_{2}\in[x-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]],

a contradiction with (
24
).

From now we assume that
\mathcal{K}_{P_{2}}^{+}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{K}_{P_{1}}^{+}(\varepsilon)
. Fix
A\in\mathcal{K}_{P_{1}}^{+}(\varepsilon)
and note that both conditional measures

P_{1}^{A}:=P_{1}(\ \cdot\ |A)\mbox{ and }P_{2}^{A}=P_{2}(\ \cdot\ |A),

satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
1
. Namely, by (
24
), for any interval
[a,b]\subset A
,

\frac{\int\limits_{[a,b]}xP_{1}^{A}(dx)}{P_{1}^{A}([a,b])}=\frac{\int\limits_{[a,b]}xP_{2}^{A}(dx)}{P_{2}^{A}([a,b])},

and hence, by Lemma
1
,
P_{1}^{A}=P_{2}^{A}
. Thus, by the definition of
P_{i}^{A}
,
i=1,2
,

\frac{P_{1}([c,d])}{P_{1}(A)}=\frac{P_{2}([c,d])}{P_{2}(A)},\mbox{ for any }[c,d]\subset A,

and hence

\frac{P_{2}([c,d])}{P_{1}([c,d])}=\frac{P_{2}(A)}{P_{1}(A)},\mbox{ for any }[c,d]\subset A.

As the above quotient
\frac{P_{2}([c,d])}{P_{1}([c,d])}
is constant for any
[c,d]\subset A
, by (
4
) we obtain that the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}
is constant
P_{1}
- almost surely on the interior of
A
. It is easy to conclude that the R-N derivative
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}
is constant
P_{1}
-almost surely on the whole interval
A
: write
A=[s,t]
and note that if, for instance,
P_{1}(\{t\})>0
, then the value of
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}
at point
t
is defined uniquely by

\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}(t)=\frac{P_{2}(\{t\})}{P_{1}(\{t\})}=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{P_{2}([t-\varepsilon,t])}{P_{1}([t-\varepsilon,t])}=\frac{P_{2}(A)}{P_{1}(A)}.

Now, let
x_{0}=0
and
x_{k}:=k\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let

A_{k}:=[x_{k}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x_{k}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}],\ k\in\mathbb{Z},

so the family
\{A_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is the family of overlapping intervals which cover the whole real line. Let

\mathbb{I}=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}\colon P_{1}(A_{k})>0\}.

Naturally:

\operatorname{supp}(P_{1})\subset\bigcup\limits_{i\in\mathbb{I}}A_{i}.

As
\operatorname{supp}(P_{1})
is an interval, for any
k\in\mathbb{I}
such that
k+1\in\mathbb{I}
we have

P_{1}(A_{k}\cap A_{k+1})>0.

(27)

Additionally, as
A_{k}
and
A_{k+1}
belong to
\mathcal{K}_{P_{1}}(\varepsilon)
, the derivative
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}
is constant
P_{1}
- almost surely on
A_{k}
and is constant
P_{1}
-almost surely on
A_{k+1}
. As we have
(
27
)
, by simple induction we obtain that
\frac{dP_{2}}{dP_{1}}
is constant
P_{1}
-almost surely on the whole support of
P_{1}
. This implies that for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
we have
P_{2}=c\cdot P_{1}
. As both
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are probability measures, we have
P_{2}=P_{1}
.

∎

Remark 3
.

A probability measure with jumps (i.e. a measure with nontrivial discrete part) still may satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
6
as far as the jump points are not isolated points of the support.

Now, we are back to the question of when the local conditioning determines the independence. We will say that
X
and
Y
are locally uncorrelated if and only if there is
\varepsilon>0
such that
\operatorname{cov}_{U}(X,Y)=0
for any
U=\{X\in[a,b],\ Y\in[c,d]\}
with
\mathbb{P}[U]>0
and
\operatorname{diam}([a,b]\times[c,d])=\max(b-a,d-c)\leq\varepsilon
.

At first, note that if
P\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
has marginals
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, i.e.:

P_{1}(A)=P(A\times\mathbb{R})\mbox{ and }P_{2}(A)=P(\mathbb{R}\times A),\ A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),

then

\operatorname{supp}(P)\subset\operatorname{supp}(P_{1})\times\operatorname{supp}(P_{2}).

Additionally, we always have:

\operatorname{supp}(P_{1}\otimes P_{2})=\operatorname{supp}(P_{1})\times\operatorname{supp}(P_{2}).

We are ready to state the following.

Theorem 7
.

Assume that
X
and
Y
are such that both supports
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
and
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{Y})
are arbitrary intervals (bounded or unbounded), and that

\operatorname{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)})=\operatorname{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X})\times\operatorname{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{Y}).

(28)

Under these assumptions,
X
and
Y
are independent if and only if they are locally uncorrelated.

To prove the above it is enough to repeat the steps of the proof of Theorem
4
with the exception that instead of Lemma
1
and Observation
2
from Section
3
we use Lemma
6
. The assumptions of Theorem
7
guarantee that all the conditional measures from the proof of Theorem
4
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
6
.

Remark 4
.

Now we comment on the assumptions of
[
10
]
: the assumption that the joint density of
(X,Y)
is positive on the cube guarantees that assumption (
28
) is satisfied, and, in particular, the assumption that
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
are invertible guarantee that the supports of
X
and
Y
are intervals.

At the end we present a numerical example in which
X
and
Y
are locally uncorrelated but not independent. In the example the assumption (
28
) is satisfied but the supports of
X
and
Y
are not intervals.

Example 8

(Unconnected support)
.

Let us consider the sample of observation pairs
(X,Y)
, for which:
X=X_{1}+2\cdot Z_{1}
, and
Y=X_{2}+2\cdot Z_{2}
, where
(X_{1},X_{2}))
is uniformly distributed on the cube
[0,1]^{2}
, and
Z=(Z_{1},Z_{2})\in\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}
is not distributed uniformly:
\mathbb{P}_{Z}(\{(0,1)\})=\mathbb{P}_{Z}(\{(1,0)\})=\frac{1}{6}
,
\mathbb{P}_{Z}(\{(0,0)\})=\mathbb{P}_{Z}(\{(1,1)\})=\frac{1}{3}
. Additionally, the variables
(X_{1},X_{2},Z_{1},Z_{2})
are assumed to be mutually independent. In this toy example we treat
Z
as a hidden variable
that controls the location of
X
and
Y
. Here, the local uncorrelation determines the independence with respect to the conditional measure
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}(\ \cdot\ |Z=(a,b))
for any
a,b\in\{0,1\}
, whereas full independence
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}=\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y}
is not satisfied.
