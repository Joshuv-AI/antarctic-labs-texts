Rotators-Translators to Mean Curvature Flow in ℍ 2 × ℝ \mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R} Thanks: The second and third authors were partially supported by the National Council for Scientific and Technological Development – CNPq
R. F. de Lima and A. K. Ramos and J. P. dos Santos

Abstract
. We establish the existence of one-parameter families of helicoidal surfaces of ℍ 2 × ℝ \mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R} which, under mean curvature flow, simultaneously rotate about a vertical axis and translate vertically. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 53E10 (primary), 53E99 (secondary). Key words and phrases: rotator – translator – mean curvature flow.



1.
Introduction

Given an orientable Riemannian
3
-manifold
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, let

\mathcal{G}:=\{\Gamma_{t}\,;\,t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}

be a one-parameter subgroup of its group of isometries, and denote by
\xi
the Killing field determined by
\mathcal{G}
.
In this setting, a surface

\varSigma
of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
with unit normal
\eta
and mean curvature
H

is called a
\mathcal{G}
-
soliton
to mean curvature flow
if the equality

(1)

H=\langle\xi,\eta\rangle

holds everywhere on
\varSigma
. It is well known that,
under mean curvature flow (MCF), a
\mathcal{G}
-soliton
\varSigma

moves by the actions of the isometries of
\mathcal{G}
(see, e.g.,
[
3
]
).
More precisely, if
X_{t}\colon M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
,
t\in[0,T)
, is
the MCF in
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
whose initial condition
\varSigma:=X_{0}(M)

is a
\mathcal{G}
-soliton, then
X_{t}(M)=\Gamma_{t}(\varSigma)
for all
t\in[0,T)
.

The most considered
\mathcal{G}
-solitons in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, called

translators
, are those whose associated group
\mathcal{G}
of isometries
consists of translations in a fixed direction.
There are also the
rotators
, which are those whose
associated group
\mathcal{G}
consists of rotations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
about a fixed axis.
In
[
2
]
, Halldorsson obtained one-parameter families of helicoidal rotators in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which are also
translators.

In this note, inspired by
Halldorsson’s work, for each
h>0
, we establish the existence of a one-parameter family of
helicoidal rotators-translators to MCF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
of pitch
h

(see Definition
3
in Section
2
), where

\mathbb{H}^{2}
is the hyperbolic plane.
The results read as follows.

Theorem 1
.

For any
h>0,
there exists a one-parameter family of complete
rotators to
MCF
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
which are helicoidal surfaces of pitch
h.
For each such surface,
the trace of the generating curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
consists of two unbounded properly embedded arms
centered at a point
o\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
, with
each arm spiraling around
o
(Fig.
1
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
\varSigma=X(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a helicoidal surface of pitch
h>0
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}.

Then,
\varSigma
is a rotator to
MCF

if and only if
\varSigma
is a translator to
MCF
with respect to the Killing field
\xi=-h\partial_{t}
, where

\partial_{t}
is the gradient of the height function of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
.

.

1
-pitched helicoidal rotator-translator in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
(right) and its
generating curve in the Poincaré disk
\mathbb{H}^{2}
(left).

It should be mentioned that helicoidal rotators-translators to MCF have been considered in other ambient
3
-spaces,
such as the Heisenberg space
{\rm Nil}_{3}
(cf.
[
4
]
), and the hyperbolic
space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
(cf.
[
1
]
).

2.
Proof of Theorems
1
and
2

Let
\mathbb{L}^{3}=(\mathbb{R}^{3},\langle\,,\rangle)
be the Lorentzian space,
where

\langle\,,\,\rangle=ds^{2}:=dx_{1}^{2}+ds_{2}^{2}-dx_{3}^{2},

and consider the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}\subset\mathbb{L}^{3}
as

\mathbb{H}^{2}=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{3}\,;\,\langle p,p\rangle=-1,\,\,\langle p,e_{3}\rangle<0\}

with the induced Lorentzian metric, where
e_{3}=(0,0,1)\in\mathbb{L}^{3}.

In this model, the tangent plane of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
at
p\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
is the subspace

\{p\}^{\perp}
of
\mathbb{L}^{3},
that is,

T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{2}=\{w\in\mathbb{L}^{3}\,;\,\langle w,p\rangle=0\}.

Consider the product
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
endowed with its standard metric

\langle\,,\,\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}+dt^{2},

together with its standard embedding in
\mathbb{L}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}.

The rotators to MCF we shall consider are those defined by
the one-parameter group
\mathcal{G}=\{\Gamma_{t}\,;\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}

of rotations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
about
the axis
\ell:={\{(0,0,1)\}}\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R},
that is,

\Gamma_{t}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}e^{tJ}&&\\
&1&\\
&&1\end{array}\right],

where
J(x_{1},x_{2})=(-x_{2},x_{1})
,
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The Killing field associated to
\mathcal{G}
is

\xi(p):=\frac{\partial\Gamma_{t}}{\partial t}|_{t=0}(p)=J\pi(p),\,\,\,p\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R},

where
\pi(p)
denotes the projection of
p
over
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times{\{(0,0,0)\}}\subset\mathbb{L}^{3}.

Therefore, considering equality (
1
), we have that an oriented surface
\varSigma
of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

with unit normal
\eta
is a rotator to MCF (with rotation axis
\ell
) if and
only if its mean curvature function
H
satisfies

(2)

H(p)=\langle J\pi(p),\eta(p)\rangle\,\,\,\forall p\in\varSigma.

In order to
introduce the helicoidal surfaces of

\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
with axis
\ell
,
consider a differentiable curve
\sigma:I\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}
,
written as

(3)

\sigma=(\alpha,\phi),

where
\alpha\colon I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a regular curve parameterized by
arc length, and
\phi
is a differentiable function on the open interval
I.
Notice that we are identifying
the plane
\{e_{3}\}^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{L}^{3}
with the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
so that the local theory of plane curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
applies to
\alpha.

Keeping that in mind, set

T=\alpha^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad N=JT,

and recall that the curvature of
\alpha
is the function

k:=\langle\alpha^{\prime\prime},N\rangle,

which satisfies the
Frenet equations

T^{\prime}=kN
and
N^{\prime}=-kT
.
We use
T
and
N
to define the following functions,
which will play a fundamental role in the sequel.

(4)

\uptau:=\langle\alpha,T\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\mu:=\langle\alpha,N\rangle.

From the Frenet equations, the derivatives of
\uptau
and
\mu
satisfy:

(5)

\uptau^{\prime}=1+k\mu\quad\text{and}\quad\mu^{\prime}=-k\uptau.

Definition 3
.

Given
h>0
and a differentiable curve

\sigma=(\alpha,\phi)
in
\mathbb{H}^{2},
we call a parameterized surface

\varSigma=X(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
a
helicoidal
surface generated by
\sigma
(as in (
3
)) with
pitch

h,

if the parameterization
X:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
writes as

(6)

X(u,v)=(e^{vJ}\alpha(u),\phi(u),hv),\,\,\,(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

We proceed now by determining the mean curvature function of
a helicoidal surface in terms of its pitch
h
and the functions

\uptau
and
\mu
defined in (
4
). For that, we will use
the well-known formula that expresses
H
with respect to the coefficients of
the first and second fundamental forms; namely

H=\frac{Eg-2fF+Ge}{2(EG-F^{2})}\cdot

For a parameterization
X
as in (
6
), we have

(7)

\frac{\partial X}{\partial u}(u,v)=(e^{vJ}T(u),\phi^{\prime}(u),0)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial X}{\partial v}(u,v)=(e^{vJ}J\alpha(u),0,h).

Besides, since
\sigma
satisfies
\langle\sigma,\sigma\rangle=-1
, we
have that
\langle\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\rangle=0
. Thus,
setting
r^{2}:=\uptau^{2}+\mu^{2}
, the following equalities hold true.

(8)

\phi^{2}=1+r^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\phi\phi^{\prime}=\uptau.

Therefore, the coefficients of the first fundamental form of
X
are

E=\frac{1+\mu^{2}}{\phi^{2}},\quad F=-\mu,\quad\text{and}\quad G=r^{2}+h^{2}.

Also, it is easily seen that a unit normal to
\varSigma=X(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is

(9)

\eta:=\varrho(e^{vJ}(aT+bN),\mu,c),\quad\varrho=(a^{2}+b^{2}-\mu^{2}+c^{2})^{-1/2},

where
a,b,
and
c
are the following functions of
u,

(10)

a:=\mu\phi^{\prime},\quad b:=\frac{1+\mu^{2}}{\phi},\quad\text{and}\quad c:=-\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{h}\cdot

Regarding the second derivatives of
X,
we have

(11)

\displaystyle X_{uu}(u,v)

\displaystyle=(e^{vJ}k(u)N(u),\phi^{\prime\prime}(u),0),

\displaystyle X_{uv}(u,v)

\displaystyle=(e^{vJ}N(u),0,0),

\displaystyle X_{vv}(u,v)

\displaystyle=(-e^{vJ}\alpha(u),0,0),

where
k
is the curvature function of
\alpha.

Therefore, the coefficients of the second fundamental form of
X
are

e=\varrho(bk-\phi^{\prime\prime}\mu),\quad f=\varrho b,\quad\text{and}\quad g=-\varrho(a\uptau+b\mu),

which implies that the mean curvature
H
of
\varSigma
at
X(u,v)
is

(12)

H=\varrho\frac{\phi^{2}(bk-\phi^{\prime\prime}\mu)(h^{2}+r^{2})+2b\mu\phi^{2}-(a\uptau+b\mu)(1+\mu^{2})}{{2}(\uptau^{2}+h^{2}(1+\mu^{2}))}\cdot

By differentiating the second equality in (
8
), one gets

\phi^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{\phi}\left(1+k\mu-\frac{\uptau^{2}}{\phi^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{\phi}\left(k\mu+\frac{1+\mu^{2}}{\phi^{2}}\right).

Hence, from (
10
), we have

(13)

\displaystyle bk-\phi^{\prime\prime}\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\mu^{2})}{\phi}k-\frac{\mu}{\phi}\left(k\mu+\frac{1+\mu^{2}}{\phi^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{\phi}-\frac{\mu(1+\mu^{2})}{\phi^{3}}\cdot

In addition, a direct computation gives

(14)

2b\mu\phi^{2}-(a\uptau+b\mu)(1+\mu^{2})=\phi\mu(1+\mu^{2}).

From (
12
), (
13
), and (
14
), we obtain

(15)

H=\frac{\varrho}{\phi}\frac{(\phi^{2}k-\mu(1+\mu^{2}))(h^{2}+r^{2})+\mu(1+\mu^{2})\phi^{2}}{2(\uptau^{2}+h^{2}(1+\mu^{2}))}\cdot

In the next result, we use the above expression of
H
to ensure the existence of
one-parameter families of complete
h
-pitched helicoidal surfaces with
prescribed mean curvature functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proposition 4
.

For any smooth function
\Psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and any
constant
h>0,
there exists a
one-parameter family of complete
helicoidal surfaces of pitch
h
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
,
each of them with mean curvature function
H
satisfying

H(X(u,v))=\Psi(\uptau(u),\mu(u)),

where
X
is the parameterization given
in (
6
) and
\uptau
and
\mu

are as in (
4
).

Proof.

From equalities (
8
)–(
10
),

\phi
and
\varrho
are differentiable functions of
\uptau
and
\mu.

Hence, considering equality (
15
) for the given function
H=H(\uptau,\mu)
and solving for

k
(notice that the coefficient of
k
in (
15
) is positive), we have that

k=k(\uptau,\mu)
is a smooth function of
(\uptau,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Thus, we can apply
[
2
, Lemma 3.2]
to conclude that there exists a
one-parameter family
\mathscr{F}

of plane curves defined on the whole line
\mathbb{R}
, each of them
with curvature function
k.

Therefore, given
h>0
, for any curve
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
\mathscr{F}
,
the
h
-pitched helicoidal surface of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
which is determined by the curve

\sigma=(\alpha,\phi),\,\,\,\phi=\sqrt{1+\|\alpha\|^{2}},

has mean curvature function
H=H(\uptau,\mu)
, as we wished to prove.
∎

Suppose that
\varSigma=X(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a helicoidal surface of pitch
h>0

as in (
6
).
Then,
J\pi(X)=e^{vJ}J\alpha
and
\eta
is given as in (
9
), so that
equality (
2
) takes the form:

(16)

H=\varrho(b\uptau-a\mu)=\frac{\varrho\uptau}{\phi}\cdot

Therefore, since
\varrho
and
\phi
are functions of
\uptau
and
\mu
, we have

Lemma 5
.

A helicoidal surface
\varSigma=X(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of pitch
h>0
parameterized as in (
6
)
is a rotator to
MCF
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
if and only if its mean curvature function

H=H(\uptau,\mu)
satisfies (
16
).

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The existence part of the statement follows directly from
Proposition
4
and Lemma
5
.
To prove the asserted properties of the generating curve
\sigma=(\alpha,\phi),

let us first observe that, by (
15
) and (
16
),
the curvature of
\alpha
is the function

(17)

k=\frac{2(\uptau^{2}+h^{2}(1+\mu^{2}))\uptau+(h^{2}-1)(1+\mu^{2})\mu}{(1+r^{2})(h^{2}+r^{2})}\cdot

Then, by combining equalities (
5
) and (
17
), one concludes
that the functions
\uptau
and
\mu
are solutions of the following ODE system:

(18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \uptau^{\prime}&=1+\frac{2(\uptau^{2}+h^{2}(1+\mu^{2}))\uptau\mu+(h^{2}-1)(1+\mu^{2})\mu^{2}}{(1+r^{2})(h^{2}+r^{2})},\\
\mu^{\prime}&=-\frac{2(\uptau^{2}+h^{2}(1+\mu^{2}))\uptau^{2}+(h^{2}-1)(1+\mu^{2})\uptau\mu}{(1+r^{2})(h^{2}+r^{2})}\cdot\end{aligned}\right.

We shall verify the properties of
\sigma
by establishing the
asymptotic behavior of the solutions

(\uptau,\mu)
of (

) as suggested in

. This will
be done through the following chain of claims. We note that some of the arguments to prove such claims

Claim 6
.

The
ODE
system (
18
) has no constant solutions,
and all solutions are defined on
\mathbb{R}.

Proof of Claim
6
.

Let us assume, by contradiction, that there exists a constant solution

\psi(s)=(\uptau_{0},\mu_{0}),\,s\in\mathbb{R}.
Since the derivatives of
\uptau
and

\mu
vanish identically, we have
from (
5
) that
k_{0}:=k(\uptau_{0},\mu_{0})
satisfies

k_{0}\mu_{0}=-1
and
k_{0}\uptau_{0}=0,
which yields
\uptau_{0}=0

and
\mu_{0}\neq 0.
But, from the first equation in (
18
),
one has

(19)

\uptau^{\prime}=1+\frac{(h^{2}-1)\mu_{0}^{2}}{h^{2}+\mu_{0}^{2}}=\frac{h^{2}(1+\mu_{0}^{2})}{h^{2}+\mu_{0}^{2}}>0,

which is a contradiction. Hence, the system (
18
) has no constant solutions.
From this fact, and since
k=k(\uptau,\mu)
is defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have that any solution
of (
18
) is defined on
\mathbb{R}.

∎

Claim 7
.

Suppose that for any solution
\psi(s):=(\uptau(s),\mu(s))
of the system (
18
), the limit

\lim_{s\to+\infty}\uptau(s)
(resp.
\lim_{s\to+\infty}\mu(s)
) exists and satisfies:

\lim_{s\to+\infty}\uptau(s)=L\,\,\,(\text{resp.}\,\,\lim_{s\to+\infty}\mu(s)=L),

where
L
is independent of
\psi
and
-\infty\leq L\leq+\infty.
Then,

\lim_{s\to-\infty}\uptau(s)
(resp.
\lim_{s\to-\infty}\mu(s)
) exists and satisfies:

\lim_{s\to-\infty}\uptau(s)=-L\,\,\,(\text{resp.}\,\,\lim_{s\to-\infty}\mu(s)=-L).

Proof of Claim
7
.

Let
\psi(s):=(\uptau(s),\mu(s))
be a solution
of the system (
18
). Then,
it is easily checked that
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\psi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(s):=-\psi(-s)
is also
a solution of that system.
Setting
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\psi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\uptau\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),

we have that
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\uptau\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(s)=-\uptau(-s)

and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(s)=-\mu(-s).
By hypothesis,

\lim_{s\to+\infty}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\uptau\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(s)
exists and the first
part of the claim follows from noticing that

\lim_{s\to-\infty}\mu(s)=-\lim_{s\to+\infty}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(s)
.
The remainder of the proof is analogous and will
be omitted.
∎

Claim 8
.

The function
\uptau
has precisely one zero
s_{0},

being negative in
(-\infty,s_{0})
and positive in

(s_{0},+\infty).
As a consequence, the function

r^{2}=\uptau^{2}+\mu^{2}
has a global minimum and satisfies

\lim_{s\rightarrow\pm\infty}r^{2}=+\infty.

Proof of Claim
8
.

Firstly, observe that the equalities (
5
) give

(r^{2})^{\prime}=2(\uptau\uptau^{\prime}+\mu\mu^{\prime})=2(\uptau(1+k\mu)+\mu(-k\uptau))=2\uptau,

which implies that the zeroes of
\uptau
are the critical points
of
r^{2}
. Also, as seen in the first part of the proof of
Claim
6
,
if
\uptau(s_{0})=0
for some
s_{0},
then
\uptau^{\prime}(s_{0})>0,

which implies that
\uptau
has at most one zero
s_{0},

in which case
\uptau
is
negative in
(-\infty,s_{0}),
and positive in

(s_{0},+\infty).

Now, arguing by contradiction,
we assume that
\uptau
has no zeroes.
We will also assume that
\uptau>0
on
\mathbb{R},
since
the complementary case
\uptau<0
can be treated analogously.
Under this assumption, the function

r^{2}
is strictly increasing. So, there exists
\delta\geq 0
such that

\lim_{s\to-\infty}r^{2}(s)=\delta.

In particular, since
\uptau=\frac{(r^{2})^{\prime}}{2}
, we also have that

(20)

\lim_{s\to-\infty}\uptau(s)=0,

which yields
\mu^{2}\rightarrow\delta
as
s\rightarrow-\infty.

However, the first equality in (
18
) yields

\lim_{s\rightarrow-\infty}\uptau^{\prime}(s)>0,

which contradicts (
20
), proving
that
\uptau
has exactly one
zero and that
r^{2}
has only one critical point. Consequently,
both the limits of

r^{2}
as
s\rightarrow\pm\infty
exist in
[0,+\infty]
.

To finish the proof of the claim, we have just to observe that
if either
\lim_{s\to-\infty}r^{2}=\delta
or

\lim_{s\to+\infty}r^{2}=\delta
for some

\delta>0
, the same arguments as before
lead to a contradiction. Hence,

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}r^{2}(s)=+\infty
.
∎

Claim 9
.

The curvature

has at most one zero

is negative in

.

Claim 10
.

The limits of
\uptau

10
.

It follows from Claims

Concerning
\uptau

In this case, for some
\uptau_{0}>0,

strictly increasing

\uptau(s_{n})=\uptau_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\uptau^{\prime}(s_{n})\uptau^{\prime}(s_{n+1})<0\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Claim
8
implies that

\lim r^{2}(s_{n})=+\infty

\lim\mu^{2}(s_{n})=+\infty

In this case, our previous
