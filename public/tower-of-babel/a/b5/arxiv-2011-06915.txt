Translating Solitons in a Lorentzian Setting, Submersions and Cohomogeneity One Actions
Marie-Amélie Lawn

Abstract
We study new examples of translating solitons of the mean curvature flow, especially in Minkowski space. We consider for this purpose manifolds admitting submersions and cohomegeneity one actions by isometries on suitable open subsets. This general setting also covers the classical Euclidean examples. As an application, we completely classify timelike, invariant translating solitons by rotations and boosts in Minkowski space.



Keywords:
Timelike Translating soliton, submersions, pseudo-Riemannian manifolds, Lie group, Cohomogeneity one action.

MSC[2010] Classification:
53C44, 53C21, 53C42, 53C50.

1
Introduction

The evolution by mean curvature flow is classicaly studied for hypersurfaces in the Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. One of the approaches is to consider particular solutions, the so-called translating solitons or translators, which are invariant by a subgroup of translations of the ambient space. It is well-known that they admit a forever flow, characterized by a constant (unit) vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
n\geq 1
, in such a way that the mean curvature flow equation simplifies to

H=v^{\perp}.

Translators have been widely studied in literature (
[
2
,
5
,
12
,
10
]
,…, and references therein). For instance, they naturally appear in the study of solutions of the mean curvature flow with a certain type of singularities (see for example
[
6
]
) and are equivalent to minimal surfaces for a conformally modified metric
[
7
]
. There are other studies for translators in other ambients spaces, such as
\mathbb{R}\times M

[
11
]
, in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

[
4
]
, a solvable group
[
14
]
, the Heisenberg 3-group
[
15
]
, etc.

One of the first analytical point of view was to see which of them were rotationally invariant
[
5
]
. This approach can be revisited from a geometrical point of view, in the sense that they are invariant by the Lie group
SO(n-1)
. Thus, the PDE is rapidly reduced to an ODE, which is simpler to study. Translating solitons were studied from an analytical point of view in the Lorentz-Minkowski space,
[
9
]
, focusing on the rotationally invariant case and only for spacelike hypersurfaces. Again, the underlying geometrical structure is the use of the Lie group
SO(n)
as a subgroup of isometries.

In this paper, we are interested in studying graphical translating solitons in Minkowski space by the action of some subgroups of isometries. From a pure analytical point of view, we seek to simplify the PDE to an ODE by a group of transformations. However, adding a geometrical sauce provides an extra layer of flavour. To do so, we wished to use quotients, although we realized that suitable tools are submersions, for almost the same effort. Those who wish to stick to the analytical taste can jump directly to Sections
5
and
6
.

Typical examples are cohomogeneity one actions by Lie groups. If the action of a group
\Sigma
is proper and free, the orbit space becomes a smooth manifold (with boundary), and the natural projection is a submersion. This holds when
M
is Riemannian and
\Sigma
is compact, acting by isometries.
However, the situation becomes much more complicated when the action is not proper.
Fortunately, it is still possible to work on some good open subsets
\Omega
of
M
quite often, because the orbit space
\Omega/\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to an open interval, even when
M/\Sigma
is not Hausdorff.

Since our starting point was to study translators in Lorentz-Minkowski space, the base manifolds of the quotients have to be either Riemannian or Lorentzian. To unify the notations, we are forced to consider pseudo-Riemannian submersions. The advantage is that we can make a quite more general study, although we will return to our origins in the last sections.

We will mainly focus on
graphical
translating solitons. Namely, given
u\in C^{2}(M)
, we construct its graph map
F:M\rightarrow M\times\mathbb{R}
,
F(x)=(x,u(x))
, and we assume that its induced metric is not degenerate. Let
\nu
be the upward normal vector along
F
with
\varepsilon=\left\langle\nu,\nu\right\rangle=\pm 1
. We recall that
\varepsilon=-1
when
F(M)
is spacelike, and
\varepsilon=+1
if timelike. In Section
2
, we show that the mean curvature flow is characterized by the following partial differential equation, which is similar to the one obtained in the classical case (see
[
5
]
)

\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\big(\varepsilon^{\prime}+|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\big)}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\big(\varepsilon^{\prime}+|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\big)}}=H.

As in
[
4
,
5
,
11
]
, we show in Section
3
that it is possible to simplify this PDE to an ODE under reasonable assumptions on the geometry of the manifold
M
, namely when
M
admits a submersion
\pi:(M,g)\rightarrow(I,\tilde{\varepsilon}ds^{2})
whose fibers
\pi^{-1}(s)
have constant mean curvature
h(s)
, where
I
is an interval,
\tilde{\varepsilon}=\pm 1
. In Theorem
3.1
, we prove that a function
f\in C^{2}(M)
is solution to the ODE

f^{\prime\prime}(s)=(\tilde{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{\prime}f^{\prime}(s)^{2})(1-f^{\prime}(s)h(s)),

if, and only if, the graph map
F:M\to M\times\mathbb{R}
,
F(x)=(x,(f\circ\pi)(x))
, is a translating soliton.
Next, we will give some results about the behavior of translating solitons within this context. Corollary
3.1
generalizes the well-known Wing-like translating soliton in
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
,
[
5
]
, whose tangent planes at some points are vertical, and thus, they are not graphical at such points. The standard idea is to rewrite the ODE by putting
f
as the inverse function of a given
\alpha
. Corollary
3.2
is an existence result for globally defined graphical solutions.

Section
4
is devoted to analytical computations, which are necessary for the final sections.

Section
5
is devoted to applying all these ideas to rotationally invariant translating solitons in Minkowski space
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
,
n\geq 2
, that is to say, those invariant by the action of
SO(n)
as a subgroup of isometries of
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
. Also, in Section
6
, we consider the Lie group
SO^{\uparrow}(n-1,1)
acting on
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
,
n\geq 2
. We focus on the timelike cases, because the spacelike cases were already considered in
[
9
]
(although without the Geometrical topping). Probably, the most surprising result is the fact that there is an example which crosses more than one fundamental region. Or in other words, we need more than one profile curve to obtain the whole surface, by gluing up four pieces lying in continuous open subsets. Probably, this unusual and slippery technique seems to prevent many people from studying timelike examples, but makes the Lorentzian setting surprisingly different from the classical Euclidean case.

2
Setup

Let
(M,g)
be a connected  pseudo-Riemannian manifold. Consider
u\in C^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})
, and let
F:M\rightarrow M\times\mathbb{R}=:\bar{M}
,
F(p)=(p,u(p))
be its graph map. We denote
(p,t)\in M\times\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
|w|_{g}^{2}={g(w,w)}
the squared
g
-norm of any tangent vector
w
to
M
. Assume that
F:(M,\gamma=F^{*}\left\langle,\right\rangle)\rightarrow(\bar{M},\left\langle,\right\rangle=g+\varepsilon^{\prime}dt^{2})
is a non-degenerate hypersurface, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\pm 1
. Under the usual identifications, for each
X\in TM
, we have

dF(X)=(X,du(X))=(X,g(\nabla u,X)),

where
\nabla u
is the
g
-gradient of
u
. We consider the metric
\gamma=F^{*}\left\langle,\right\rangle
on
M
. By assumption,
\gamma
is non-degenerate. The upward normal vector field is

\nu=\frac{1}{W}(-\varepsilon^{\prime}\nabla u,1),\quad W=+\sqrt{\varepsilon\big(\varepsilon^{\prime}+|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\big)},

(1)

where we are assuming that
\varepsilon:=\mathrm{sign}\big(\varepsilon^{\prime}+|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\big)=\pm 1
is a constant function on the whole
M
.
Note that
\left\langle\nu,\nu\right\rangle=\varepsilon
.
It is important to remark our definitions of the mean curvature vector and function. In our setting, if
II_{F}
is the second fundamental form, the mean curvature vector is

\vec{H}_{F}=\mathrm{trace}_{g_{M}}(II_{F})=\varepsilon H\nu_{F},

where
H
is the mean curvature function. Similar notations will be used for other hypersurfaces along the paper.

The following proposition is well-known in the Euclidean setting (see for example
[
10
]
). The proof is similar in the pseudo-Riemannian context, but we include it for completeness and discuss the necessary modifications.

Proposition 2.1
.

Under the previous setting,
F
is a (vertical) translating soliton if, and only if, function
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\big(\varepsilon^{\prime}+|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\big)}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\big(\varepsilon^{\prime}+|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\big)}}=H.

(2)

Proof.

Take a local
g
-orthonormal frame
B=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
on
M
, such that
g(e_{i},e_{j})=\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{ij}
for any
i,j=1,\dots n
, with
\varepsilon_{i}=\pm 1
depending on the signature of
g
. We denote
u_{i}=du(e_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. For this frame, we compute the induced metric
\gamma=F^{*}\left\langle,\right\rangle
, so that the coefficients of the Gram matrix and of its inverse are

\gamma_{ij}=\gamma(e_{i},e_{j})=\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{ij}+\varepsilon^{\prime}u_{i}u_{j},\quad\gamma^{ij}=\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{ij}-\frac{\varepsilon}{W^{2}}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}u_{i}u_{j}.

Let
\bar{\nabla}
the Levi-Civita connection of
(\bar{M},\left\langle,\right\rangle)
. We recall O’Neill’s book
[
13
]
, and its equations for the Levi-Civita connection of a (warped) product. Then,

\displaystyle H=\left\langle\vec{H}_{F},\nu\right\rangle=\left\langle\partial_{t}^{\perp},\nu\right\rangle=\left\langle\partial_{t},\nu\right\rangle=\left\langle\nu,\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}(II_{F})\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\gamma^{ij}\left\langle\nu,\bar{\nabla}_{dF(e_{i})}dF(e_{j})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\gamma^{ij}\left\langle\nu,\big(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},0\big)+e_{i}(u_{j})\partial_{t}\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\gamma^{ij}e_{i}(u_{j})\left\langle\nu,\partial_{t}\right\rangle,

so that

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{i,j}\gamma^{ij}e_{i}(u_{j})=\sum_{i,j}\left(\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{ij}-\frac{\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}u_{i}u_{j}}{\varepsilon W^{2}}\right)e_{i}(u_{j})=\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)-\frac{\varepsilon}{W^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}u_{i}u_{j}e_{i}(u_{j}).

By recalling that
W^{2}=\varepsilon(\varepsilon^{\prime}+g(\nabla u,\nabla u))
, we compute

\displaystyle 2W\left\langle\nabla W,\nabla u\right\rangle=\left\langle\nabla(W^{2}),\nabla u\right\rangle=\varepsilon\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}u_{i}e_{i}\left(g(\nabla u,\nabla u)\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}u_{i}\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}e_{i}(u_{j}^{2})=2\varepsilon\sum_{i,j}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}u_{i}u_{j}e_{i}(u_{j}).

Coming back

\displaystyle 1=\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)-\frac{1}{W}\left\langle\nabla W,\nabla u\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W}=\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}-\frac{1}{W^{2}}\left\langle\nabla W,\nabla u\right\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}+\left\langle\nabla\big(1/W\big),\nabla u\right\rangle=\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right).

∎

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact orientable manifold without boundary. Then,
M
does not admit any globally defined, non-degenerate, graphical, spacelike Translating Soliton
F:M\rightarrow(M\times\mathbb{R},g+\varepsilon^{\prime}dt^{2})
.

Proof.

Assume that there exists a globally defined graphical Translating Soliton on
M
. Then, for some function
u\in C^{2}(M)
, equation (
2
) holds true. By using the volume form
d\mu_{g}
, we obtain

0=\int_{M}\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{\varepsilon(\varepsilon^{\prime}+|\nabla u|^{2}_{g})}}\right)d\mu_{g}=\int_{M}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon(\varepsilon^{\prime}+|\nabla u|^{2}_{g})}}d\mu_{g}>0.

This is a contradiction.
∎

3
Submersions and Lie Groups

Our next target consists of studying when the equation of the mean curvature flow can be reduced to a particular ODE. The main geometrical technique consists of using a Lie group acting by isomorphisms whose orbits are codimension one sumbanifolds, namely cohomogenity one actions. We choose the case when the image of the natural projection is going to be identified with an open interval. This is not a big deal, since one can remove the non-regular orbits, but later it might be necessary to see the possible extension. Before starting, we need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Take
\varepsilon^{\prime},\tilde{\varepsilon}\in\{\pm 1\}
,
I
an open interval and
\pi:(M,g_{{}_{M}})\rightarrow(I,\tilde{\varepsilon}ds^{2})
a pseudo-Riemannian submersion such that each fiber
\pi^{-1}(s)
has constant mean curvature
h(s)
w.r.t.
-\nabla\pi
. Given
f\in C^{2}(I)
, define
u=f\circ\pi\in C^{2}(M)
.
Assume that
\varepsilon=\mathrm{sign}(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\tilde{\varepsilon}(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)^{2})=\pm 1
is a constant function.

1.

h:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function, and such that
\mathrm{div}(\nabla\pi)=h\circ\pi
.

2.

\nabla u=(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)\nabla\pi
,
|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}=\tilde{\varepsilon}(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)^{2}
,
\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)=\tilde{\varepsilon}(f^{\prime\prime}\circ\pi)+(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)(h\circ\pi)
.

3.

If
W=+\sqrt{\varepsilon\big(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\tilde{\varepsilon}(f^{\prime}(\pi))^{2}\big)}\,>0
, then
\nabla W=\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon\tilde{\varepsilon}f^{\prime}(\pi)f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)}{W}\nabla\pi.

Proof.

First, given a local orthonormal frame
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
such that
e_{n}=\nabla\pi
and the vector fields
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
are tangent to the fibers, with
\varepsilon_{i}=g(e_{i},e_{i})
, then

\mathrm{div}(\nabla\pi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}g(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla\pi,e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\varepsilon_{i}g(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla\pi,e_{i})=h\circ\pi.

For simpleness, we write
f\circ\pi=f(\pi)
, etc. For the second statement, given
p\in M
and
X\in T_{p}M
, we compute

g_{p}(\nabla(f\circ\pi),X)=d(f\circ\pi)_{p}X=df_{\pi(p)}(d\pi_{p}(X))=f^{\prime}(\pi(p))g_{p}(\nabla\pi,X),

and we point out that
|\nabla\pi|_{g}^{2}=\tilde{\varepsilon}
. Next,

\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)=\mathrm{div}(f^{\prime}(\pi)\nabla\pi)=g(\nabla(f^{\prime}\circ\pi),\nabla\pi)+f^{\prime}(\pi)\mathrm{div}(\nabla\pi)=\tilde{\varepsilon}f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)+f^{\prime}(\pi)h(\pi).

Finally,
2W\nabla W=\nabla(W^{2})=2\tilde{\varepsilon}\varepsilon f^{\prime}(\pi)f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)\nabla\pi.

∎

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a connected pseudo-Riemannian manifold and
I
an open interval. Let
\Sigma
be a Lie group acting by isometries on
M
and
\pi:M\rightarrow I
be a submersion such that the fibers of
\pi
are orbits of the action, and
\nabla\pi
is never zero or lightlike. Then, there exist a constant
\tilde{\varepsilon}=\pm 1
and a smooth bijective map
v:I\rightarrow J\subset\mathbb{R}
,
J
an interval, such that
v\circ\pi:(M,g)\rightarrow(J,\tilde{\varepsilon}ds^{2})
is a pseudo-Riemannian submersion with constant mean curvature fibers.

Proof.

Since
\pi
is a submersion, and
|\nabla\pi|_{g}^{2}\neq 0
, there exists a smooth function
z:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
such that
(z\circ\pi)^{2}=\tilde{\varepsilon}|\nabla\pi|_{g}^{2}
, for a constant
\tilde{\varepsilon}=\pm 1
. Next, we consider a function
v:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
v^{\prime}=1/z
. As
v^{\prime}
has constant sign,
v
is injective, and we restrict it to its image,
v:I\rightarrow J
. By Lemma
3.1
,
|\nabla(v\circ\pi)|^{2}_{g}=\tilde{\varepsilon}
. In particular,
v\circ\pi:(M,g)\rightarrow(J,\tilde{\varepsilon}ds^{2})
is a pseudo-Riemannian submersion. Note that the fibers of
v\circ\pi
are the same as the fibers of
\pi
, which are the orbits of
\Sigma
. As it acts by isometries, they have constant mean curvature w.r.t.
-\nabla(v\circ\pi)
.
∎

From now on, we assume that the action of the Lie group
\Sigma
on
(M,g)
by isometries is
proper
, and that at least one of the orbits is of codimension one. These are the well-known
cohomogeneity one
\Sigma
-manifolds
. According to
[
1
]
(and references therein), there is another Riemannian metric
g_{R}
such that
\Sigma
acts on
(M,g_{R})
by isometries, and the quotient
M/\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to an interval or to
\mathbb{S}^{1}\equiv\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. By removing the possible singular orbits (if any, up to two), we can assume that
M/\Sigma=:I
is (diffeomorphic to) an open interval. This works at differentiable level, so we can return to our original metric
g
, and the quotient is still an open interval. Let
\pi:M\to I\equiv M/\Sigma
be the projection. We need
\nabla\pi
not to be zero or lightlike. By Lemma
3.1
, we can assume that
g_{{}_{M}}(\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi)=\pm 1=:\tilde{\varepsilon}
, making
\pi
a pseudo-Riemannian submersion. Each fiber of
\pi
will be an orbit of
\Sigma
. And because it acts by isometries, then each orbit
\pi^{-1}\{s\}
will be of constant mean curvature
h(s)
w.r.t
-\nabla\pi
. Thus, we have an associated smooth function
h:I\to\mathbb{R}
. We need to bear in mind all this setting, so that we introduce the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Under the previous setting, let
\Sigma_{o}
one of the stabilizers of
\Sigma
. Let
\beta:I\to M
be a unit curve orthogonal to all orbits of
\Sigma
, such that the map

\phi:I\times(\Sigma/\Sigma_{o})\to M,\quad\phi(s,[\sigma])=\sigma\cdot\beta(s),

(3)

is a diffeomorphism and
\pi\circ\phi(s,[\sigma])=s
. We will say that (
3
) is a decomposition of
M
by
\Sigma
, or that
M
is decomposable by
\Sigma
, with associated function
h:I\to\mathbb{R}
. We cannot forget the submersion
\pi:(M,g)\to(I,\tilde{\varepsilon}ds^{2})
.

Remark 3.1
.

For the sake of simpleness, sometimes is it better to use
\phi:I\times\Sigma\to M
. But then,
\phi(s,-):\Sigma\to M
might not be an immersion, although the image is again an orbit.
\Box

We recall that a function
u:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
invariant
by the Lie group
\Sigma
, or also
\Sigma
-invariant, if it satisfies

u:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad u(x)=u(\sigma\cdot x),\ \forall x\in M,\ \forall\sigma\in\Sigma.

(4)

Accordingly, we say that a graphical translating soliton is
invariant
by the Lie group
\Sigma
, or also
\Sigma
-invariant
, when its graph map is invariant by
\Sigma
. Similarly, a translating soliton
F:\Omega\subset M\to M\times\mathbb{R}
is called
\Sigma
-invariant when
\sigma\cdot F(x)\in F(\Omega)
for each
x\in\Omega
and each
\sigma\in\Sigma
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a pseudo-Riemannian manifold, decomposable by
\Sigma
, as in Definition
3.1
. Given
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\pm 1
,
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth function, construct the graph map
F:M\rightarrow(M\times\mathbb{R},\left\langle,\right\rangle=g_{{}_{M}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}dt^{2})
,
F(x)=(x,u(x))
,
u=f\circ\pi
. Then,
F
is a
\Sigma
-invariant translating soliton if, and only if,
f
is a solution to

f^{\prime\prime}(s)=\big(\tilde{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{\prime}(f^{\prime}(s))^{2}\big)\big(1-f^{\prime}(s)h(s)\big).

(5)

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We denote
u=f\circ\pi
.
Using Lemma
3.1
we get directly from equation (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W}=\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}-\frac{g(\nabla W,\nabla u)}{W^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)+f^{\prime}(\pi)h(\pi)}{W}-\frac{1}{W^{2}}g\left(\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\varepsilon f^{\prime}(\pi)f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)}{W}\nabla\pi,f^{\prime}(\pi)\nabla\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)+f^{\prime}(\pi)h(\pi)}{W}-\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\varepsilon f^{\prime}(\pi)^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)}{W^{3}}g\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W}\left(f^{\prime}(\pi)h(\pi)+f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}\tilde{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{\prime}+\tilde{\varepsilon}f^{\prime}(\pi)^{2}}\right).

Therefore, we conclude that (
2
) reduces to

f^{\prime}(\pi)h(\pi)+\frac{1}{\tilde{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{\prime}f^{\prime}(\pi)^{2}}f^{\prime\prime}(\pi)=1.

In other words, function
f
is a solution to (
5
).
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Another approach is to start with a
\Sigma
-invariant function
u:M\to\mathbb{R}
. But then, we immediately obtain another function
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u=f\circ\pi
.

On the other hand, we can extend the action of
\Sigma
to
M\times\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{M}=M\times\mathbb{R},\left\langle\,,\right\rangle=g+\varepsilon\mathrm{d}t^{2},\ \varepsilon=\pm 1,\quad\Sigma\times\bar{M}\to\bar{M},\ (\sigma,(x,t))\mapsto(\sigma\cdot x,t).

In fact, given any curve
\delta:J\subset I\to I\times\mathbb{R}
,
\delta=(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, we can construct a map whose image is
\Sigma
-invariant, namely

F:J\times(\Sigma/\Sigma_{o})\to M\times\mathbb{R},\quad F(s,[\sigma])=\big(\phi(\delta_{1}(s),[\sigma]),\delta_{2}(s)\big).

(6)

We will now give some general results about the behavior of solutions. This next result states the existence of the so-called
wing-like solutions
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Assume there
M
is decomposable as in Definition
3.1
, where
h
is the mean curvature of the orbits of the action. Consider
s_{o}\in I
such that either
h(s_{o})\neq 0
, or
s_{o}
is an isolated zero of
h
. Then, for each
y_{o}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist a real number
\rho>0
and a translating soliton
F:(y_{o}-\rho,y_{o}+\rho)\times(\Sigma/\Sigma_{o})\rightarrow\bar{M}
such that it is the union of two graphical translating solitons.

Proof.

Choose
y_{o}\in\mathbb{R}
. We consider the following IVP:

\alpha^{\prime\prime}(y)=\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\tilde{\varepsilon}\alpha^{\prime}(y)^{2}\right)\left(h(\alpha(y))-\alpha^{\prime}(y)\right),\quad\alpha^{\prime}(y_{o})=0,\ \alpha(y_{o})=s_{o}\in I.

As usual, there exists a smooth solution
\alpha:(y_{o}-\rho,y_{o}+\rho)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Note that
y_{o}
is a critical point of
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime\prime}(y_{o})=\varepsilon^{\prime}h(s_{o}).

Case
h(s_{o})\neq 0
:
Then,
y_{o}
is an extremum of
\alpha
. The restrictions
\alpha_{+}=\alpha|_{(y_{o},y_{o}+\rho)}
and
\alpha_{-}=\alpha|_{(y_{o}-\rho,y_{o})}
will be injective, by reducing
\rho
if necessary. Construct their inverse functions
f_{+}=\alpha_{+}^{-1}
and
f_{-}=\alpha_{-}^{-1}
. We just have to show that
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
satisfy (
5
). To do so, we put
f_{+}(\alpha(y))=y
, and therefore

\displaystyle 1=f_{+}^{\prime}(\alpha(y))\,\alpha^{\prime}(y),\quad 0=f_{+}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha(y))\,\alpha^{\prime}(y)^{2}+f_{+}^{\prime}(\alpha(y))\,\alpha^{\prime\prime}(y),

\displaystyle f_{+}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha(y))\,\alpha^{\prime}(y)^{2}=-f_{+}^{\prime}(\alpha(y))\big(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\tilde{\varepsilon}\alpha^{\prime}(y)^{2}\big)\big(h(\alpha(y)-\alpha^{\prime}(y)\big).

Next, we change
s=\alpha(y)
, and then
\alpha^{\prime}(y)=1/f^{\prime}_{+}(s)
, so that

Case

Remark 3.3

By (

Corollary 3.2
.

Proof.

By Theorem

, any

-invariant translating soliton will be constructed by a solution

to (

, so it reduces to

Remark 3.4
.

This section generalizes several classical results in a general geometric context. A well-known example is the case of a rotationally invariant translating soliton in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We consider the Lie group
SO(n)
acting by isometries on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which gives rise to the Riemannian submersion
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\,\pi(x)=\|x\|
, and the Riemannian product
\bar{M}=(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R},g^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}+dt^{2})
. An easy computation shows that the mean curvature of the fiber

is

. Using Theorem
3.1
, we recover the well-known ODE for rotationally invariant translating solitons

, studied in
[
5
]
, where such solitons are classified. In that paper, it is shown that there exist only two types: a globally defined graphical soliton, the so-called bowl soliton or translating paraboloid, which can be seen as a particular application of our Corollary
3.2
, and a family of non-graphical wing-like translators or translating catenoids, obtained by gluing two graphical solutions, which are a special case of Corollary
3.1
.

Case I:

Case II:
