The Rank of the Odd Normal Out
Philip T. Labo

Abstract
A century ago Thurstone wrote about using 𝐗 ∼ 𝒩 n ​ ( 𝝁 , 𝚺 ) \mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right) to model our preferences ( Thurstone 1927a ; Thurstone 1927b ; Thurstone 1927c ). Today latent normals frequently model random rankings. Despite this, for R i ≔ # ⁡ { 1 ≤ j ≤ n : X j ≤ X i } R_{i}\coloneqq\#\left\{1\leq j\leq n:X_{j}\leq X_{i}\right\} , little is known about ( R i 0 , … , R i m ) \left(R_{i_{0}},\ldots,R_{i_{m}}\right) ’s distribution in non-i.i.d. settings. We consider the simplest of such settings, namely that with n + 1 n+1 independent normals, where X 0 ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( μ 0 , σ 0 2 ) X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2}\right) and X 1 , X 2 , … , X n ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( μ , σ 2 ) X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right) . In this setting ( R 0 | X 0 ) ∼ 1 + Binomial ( n , Φ ( ( X 0 − μ ) / σ ) ) \left(\left.R_{0}\right|X_{0}\right)\sim 1+\mathrm{Binomial}\left(n,\Phi\left(\nicefrac{{\left(X_{0}-\mu\right)}}{{\sigma}}\right)\right) , and Φ ⁡ ( ( X 0 − μ ) / σ ) \Phi\left(\nicefrac{{\left(X_{0}-\mu\right)}}{{\sigma}}\right) is approximately beta-distributed. The beta distribution’s conjugacy for the binomial implies that R 0 − 1 R_{0}-1 is roughly beta-binomial. We approximate the distribution of ( R i 0 , … , R i m ) \left(R_{i_{0}},\ldots,R_{i_{m}}\right) , deriving 𝔼 ​ R i \mathbb{E}R_{i} , Var ⁡ ( R i ) \mathrm{Var}\left(R_{i}\right) , Cov ⁡ ( R i , R j ) \mathrm{Cov}\left(R_{i},R_{j}\right) , and its limiting distributions as key parameters grow large or small.

1.1
Rank Distributions from 1927 to Present

In the i.i.d. setting with
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)

and
R_{i}
the rank of
X_{i}
(as in (
1
)), the rank
vector
\mathbf{R}
distributes uniformly over
\Pi_{n}
, the set
of permutations of
\left[n\right]\coloneqq\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
:
for
\mathbf{r}\in\Pi_{n}
,
\Pr\left(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{r}\right)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n!}}
.
This holds for any continuous distribution
F
.

The literature describes four basic approaches to defining non-uniform
probability distributions over
\Pi_{n}
(
Critchlow et al 1991
;
Alvo & Yu 2014
). If
we assume continuous
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
and let
o_{j}

be the value of
i
such that
r_{i}=j
, then the order statistics
models put
\Pr\left(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{r}\right)=\Pr\left(X_{o_{1}}<X_{o_{2}}<\cdots<X_{o_{n}}\right)
.
4
4

4

In defining order statistics models we do not require the independence
of
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
.
The probability of ranking
\mathbf{r}
is the probability that

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
fall in the order
\mathbf{r}
defines.
Ours is an order statistics model. Other approaches for defining probability
distributions over
\Pi_{n}
include paired comparison models, distance-based
models, and multi-stage models (
e.g.
,
Bradley & Terry 1952
;
Diaconis 1988
;
Harville 1973
).

Order statistics models at times give closed-form expressions for

\Pr\left(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{r}\right)
. For example, in the setting
with independent
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)
,

1\leq i\leq n
, we have

\Pr\left(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{r}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\lambda_{o_{j}}}{\sum_{k=j}^{n}\lambda_{o_{k}}}\right)

(9)

(
Dansie 1983
). Here we see
\min_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{i}\right)
,
for
\mathcal{S}\subset\left[n\right]
; the memoryless property of
the exponential distribution; and a joint order statistic/multi-stage
model. The
j=1
factor in (
9
) gives the probability
that the
X_{i}
with rank one is smallest. Conditional on this,
the
j=2
factor gives the probability that the
X_{i}
with rank
two is next,
etc
. An identical thing happens in the setting
with independent
Y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Gumbel}\left(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}\right)
,
for
1\leq i\leq n
, because
e^{\nicefrac{{-Y_{i}}}{{\sigma_{i}}}}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left(e^{\nicefrac{{\mu_{i}}}{{\sigma_{i}}}}\right)
.
In this setting we have

\Pr\left(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{r}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\exp\left(\left.\mu_{o_{n-j+1}}\right/\sigma_{i}\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^{n-j+1}\exp\left(\left.\mu_{o_{k}}\right/\sigma_{i}\right)}\right)

(10)

(
Luce 1959
;
Yellott 1977
). The minus in
e^{\nicefrac{{-Y_{i}}}{{\sigma_{i}}}}

reverses the order of traversal. The
j=1
factor in (
10
)
gives the probability that the
Y_{i}
with rank
n
is largest.
Conditional on this, the
j=2
factor gives the probability that
the
Y_{i}
with rank
n-1
is next,
etc
. Note that, for

f
monotonically-increasing,
\left\{Z_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}

and
\left\{f\left(Z_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
have identical
rank distributions.

While the
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\right)
computation in (
9
)
uses independent
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\left(1,\lambda_{i}\right)
,
we now generalize this to independent
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\left(s,\lambda_{i}\right)
,
for
s\in\left\{1,2,\ldots\right\}
.
Henery 1983
and
Stern 1990

derive expressions for
\Pr\left(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{r}\right)
in
this setting, noting that the sum of i.i.d.
\xi_{i,1},\ldots,\xi_{i,s}\sim\textrm{Exp}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)

is
\mathrm{Gamma}\left(s,\lambda_{i}\right)
. They imagine a game
with
n
independent Poisson processes, with
N_{i,t}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}\left(t\lambda_{i}\right)
.
Each
N_{i,t}
races to reach
s
and leaves the race after reaching
it. The products of negative binomial probabilities in
Henery 1983
’s
expression below, with
i_{n}\equiv 0
and
\Lambda_{j}\coloneqq\sum_{k=j}^{n}\lambda_{o_{k}}
,

\Pr\left(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{r}\right)=\sum_{i_{n-1}=0}^{s-1+i_{n}}\cdots\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{s-1+i_{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{s-1+i_{j}}{i_{j}}\left(\frac{\Lambda_{j+1}}{\Lambda_{j}}\right)^{i_{j}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{o_{j}}}{\Lambda_{j}}\right)^{s},

(11)

give the probability of
i_{j}
failures before object
o_{j}
reaches

s
successes,
1\leq j<n

ordering in
\mathbf{r}
. The sum, like the one in
Stern 1990

has
\mathcal{O}\left(s^{n-1}\right)

hard to use in practice (
Stern 1990

Stern 1990

have exactly one differently-distributed
\mathrm{Gamma}\left(s,\lambda_{i}\right)\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\nicefrac{{s}}{{\lambda_{i}}},\nicefrac{{s}}{{\lambda_{i}^{2}}}\right)

4

Owing to the ubiquity of the normal distribution, in nature and in

\Pi_{n}

(

12

4
Discussion and Conclusions

We consider the setting with
n+1
independent, normal random variables,
where
X_{0}
has one distribution and
\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}

have another, and ask: what distribution do the ranks
\mathbf{R}\coloneqq\left(R_{0},R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}\right)

have? To date little is known about the the distributions of normal
ranks in non-i.i.d. settings (§
1.1
).
In this simplest of non-i.i.d. settings,
R_{0}-1
is approximately

\mathrm{BetaBinomial}\left(n,a_{\rho,\delta},b_{\rho,\delta}\right)
,
for
a_{\rho,\delta}
and
b_{\rho,\delta}
in (
25
)
and (
26
). With
R_{0}
fixed,
\left(R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{n}\right)

distributes uniformly over the permutations of
\left.\left\{1,2,\ldots,n+1\right\}\right\backslash\left\{R_{0}\right\}
.

Our argument rests on two key observations. The first is straightforward;
the second takes work. In (
2
) we note that
\left(\left.R_{0}\right|X_{0}\right)-1

is
\mathrm{Binomial}\left(n,\Phi\left(\nicefrac{{\left(X_{0}-\mu\right)}}{{\sigma}}\right)\right)
.
In section
2
we then show that
\Phi\left(\nicefrac{{\left(X_{0}-\mu\right)}}{{\sigma}}\right)

is roughly
\mathrm{Beta}\left(a_{\rho,\delta},b_{\rho,\delta}\right)
,
for
a_{\rho,\delta}
and
b_{\rho,\delta}
in (
25
)
and (
26
). We, in effect and in Bayesian terms, approximate
the prior in order to approximate the marginal. The beta distribution’s
conjugacy for the binomial then provides for a relatively simple expression—one
that relies on beta functions. While our work considers the simplest
of non-i.i.d. settings, it may inspire and inform future excursions
into more complex normal settings. Perhaps, in time,
Thurstone 1927a
;
Thurstone 1927b
;
Thurstone 1927c
’s
vision will be fully unpacked.

\alpha,\beta,\epsilon

(164)

where
\sim
assumes
\alpha,\beta,\epsilon
small and
\rightarrow

sends
\alpha,\beta,\epsilon
