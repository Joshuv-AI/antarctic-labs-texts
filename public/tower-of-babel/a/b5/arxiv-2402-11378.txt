A bivariational, stable and convergent hierarchy for time-dependent coupled cluster with adaptive basis sets
Mads Greisen Højlund, Ove Christiansen

Abstract
We propose a new formulation of time-dependent coupled cluster with adaptive basis functions and division of the one-particle space into active and secondary subspaces. The formalism is fully bivariational in the sense of a real-valued time-dependent bivariational principle and converges to the complete-active-space solution, a property that is obtained by the use of biorthogonal basis functions. A key and distinguishing feature of the theory is that the active bra and ket functions span the same space by construction. This ensures numerical stability and is achieved by employing a split unitary/non-unitary basis set transformation: The unitary part changes the active space itself, while the non-unitary part transforms the active basis. The formulation covers vibrational as well as electron dynamics. Detailed equations of motion are derived and implemented in the context of vibrational dynamics, and the numerical behavior is studied and compared to related methods.



I
INTRODUCTION

Computing the nuclear quantum dynamics of molecules presents a tremendous
challenge for systems with more than a few degrees of freedom.
One of the most versatile methods that has been developed for this purpose is the multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree method,
Meyer, Manthe, and Cederbaum 1990
;
Beck et al. 2000

which employs a complete wave function expansion inside an active space spanned by a limited
number of adaptive basis functions. The division of the one-particle space into
active and secondary parts allows a compact and highly flexible active space,
something that is a key factor in the success of the method.
An analogous scheme for computing the electron dynamics of molecules
is called multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree–Fock.
Zanghellini et al. 2003
;
Kato and Kono 2004
;
Nest, Klamroth, and Saalfrank 2005
;
Caillat et al. 2005

While multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree and multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree–Fock constitute important reference approaches in their respective fields, they
involve a computational cost that scales exponentially,
thus limiting their application to comparatively small systems.
It is therefore highly pertinent to investigate and develop alternative methods that are accurate and achieve polynomial scaling,
a goal that has motivated an immense amount of research in different directions that we will not attempt to survey here.
Instead, we will focus on the coupled cluster
Ansatz
and, specifically, on the idea
of combining the time-dependent coupled cluster
Ansatz
with adaptive basis functions.
Adaptive or optimized basis functions have a long history in ground-state electronic coupled cluster theory,
starting with the Brueckner coupled cluster method,
Chiles and Dykstra 1981
;
Handy et al. 1989
;
Raghavachari et al. 1990
;
Hampel, Peterson, and Werner 1992

where the orbitals are determined in such a way that the singles projections vanish.
A closely related approach known as orbital-optimized coupled cluster
Purvis and Bartlett 1982
;
Scuseria and Schaefer 1987
;
Sherrill et al. 1998
;
Krylov et al. 1998
;
Pedersen, Koch, and Hättig 1999

instead minimizes the coupled cluster energy by using unitary (orthogonal)
orbital transformations. It turns out, however, that orbital-optimized coupled cluster does not reproduce the full configuration interaction limit,
Köhn and Olsen 2005

which is of course a disadvantage.
Replacing the unitary (orthogonal) orbital transformation with a non-unitary (non-orthogonal) one
defines the non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster method,
Pedersen, Fernández, and Koch 2001

which does in fact recover the full configuration interaction limit, as shown by Myhre.
Myhre 2018

Time-dependent orbital-optimized coupled cluster and non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster (TDOCC and TDNOCC) were introduced by Pedersen
et al.
Pedersen, Koch, and Hättig 1999
;
Pedersen, Fernández, and Koch 2001

for the purpose of deriving gauge invariant response functions. Concrete working equations for
actually propagating the time-dependent orbital-optimized coupled cluster and time-dependent non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster wave functions in time were, however, not provided.
The first example of an explicitly time-dependent coupled cluster method with dynamical orbitals is thus
Kvaal’s orbital-adaptive time-dependent coupled cluster
Kvaal 2012
method from 2012,
which employs biorthogonal (i.e. non-orthogonal) orbitals. Importantly, this method allows
division of the orbital basis and reproduces the multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree–Fock limit correctly.
The orbital-adaptive time-dependent coupled cluster and time-dependent non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster
Ansätze
are equivalent if the orbital-adaptive time-dependent coupled cluster orbital space
is not divided into active and secondary parts.

Just as orbital-adaptive time-dependent coupled cluster can be viewed as a combination of the coupled cluster approach with the basic
idea of multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree–Fock, the time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster
Madsen et al. 2020a
method
developed in our group combines vibrational coupled cluster with adaptive basis functions
in the spirit of multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree. We have, however, discovered certain stability issues with the time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster approach
that occur specifically when the time-dependent biorthogonal basis set is divided.
Højlund et al. 2022

Our investigation of the problem showed that it is associated with the following fact:
If a biorthogonal basis is divided into active and secondary parts, then there is no guarantee
that the active bra functions and the active ket functions span the same space. In itself, this
is not necessarily a cause for concern, but we have observed that the two spaces tend to drift dangerously far
apart, resulting in serious numerical trouble. In Ref.
21
,
we proposed a so-called restricted polar version of time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster (rpTDMVCC) that forces
the active bra and ket spaces to be the same, while allowing non-orthogonality within the active space.
The restricted polar time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster model was shown to solve the stability issue without compromising accuracy,
but it should be stressed that the restricted polar time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster equations are not fully bivariational.
Apart from being somewhat unappealing from a formal standpoint,
this fact also leads to minor energy non-conservation beyond ordinary numerical noise and integration error.
Even when energy conservation is not of major interest in itself, it serves
as a convenient tool for gauging the integration error (at least for some types of integration schemes)
and is therefore a desirable property in practical computations.

A different approach that cannot suffer from the stability issue described above is to simply
use an orthogonal basis.
Real-time time-dependent orbital-optimized coupled cluster has indeed been introduced by Sato
et al.
,
Sato et al. 2018

and we have recently considered an orthogonal version of time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster (oTDMVCC)
Højlund, Zoccante, and Christiansen 2024

analogous to time-dependent orbital-optimized coupled cluster. Our detailed benchmark calculations confirmed numerically that orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster
does not converge to multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree,
which is certainly a disadvantage.
In concrete terms, the non-convergence means that one cannot generally expect the quality of oTDMVCC
to improve systematically towards the appropriate limit. Although we did not usually observe this deficiency at low
excitation levels,
Højlund, Zoccante, and Christiansen 2024
it is surely a problem when high accuracy is required.
Another problem of significant practical
importance is that the linear equations determining the basis set time evolution
(the so-called constraint equations)
are more involved in
orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster than in time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster and restricted polar time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster.
Højlund, Zoccante, and Christiansen 2024

In this paper, we propose a new and fully bivariational formulation of time-dependent coupled cluster with adaptive basis functions
that combines the following attractive properties: (i) Convergence to the MCTDH/MCTDHF limit, (ii) numerical stability, and (iii)
simple basis set equations. Although we are mainly concerned with the vibrational structure problem, we stress that the formalism
is also applicable to electron dynamics after removing (summations over) mode indices.
The key idea is to separate the basis set transformation into
two parts: An interspace (active–secondary) transformation
and an intraspace (active–active) transformation. Choosing the former to be unitary and the latter to be non-unitary
results in biorthogonal bra and ket basis functions that span the same space by construction.
We derive fully bivariational equations of motion by applying a real-valued
time-dependent bivariational principle
Arponen 1983
;
Kvaal 2012

and specialize the formalism to the vibrational dynamics case.
The resulting method is called split TDMVCC, or sTDMVCC
for short, since it uses a basis set transformation that is split into
unitary and non-unitary parts.

The paper is organized as follows: Section
II
starts with a rather general
explanation of some essential concepts (including adaptive basis functions, biorthogonal bases, and basis set division),
which in turn motivates the introduction of a new
Ansatz
for the basis set transformation.
Section
II
then continues with the details of the theory, including
an outline of the time-dependent bivariational principle and derivations of the equations of motion. This is followed by a brief description of
our implementation in Sec.
III
and a few numerical examples in Sec.
IV
.
Section
V
concludes the paper with a summary of our findings.

II
THEORY

II.1
Orthogonal and biorthogonal adaptive basis sets

Before deriving any detailed equations of motion, we make some general remarks on basis set parameterization with emphasis
on the distinction between orthogonal and biorthogonal bases and on the role of basis set division.
For simplicity, we will refer to an underlying or primitive basis, which is taken to be orthonormal.
The time-dependent basis functions are then expressed (with time-dependent coefficients)
in terms of the primitive functions. This representation
has been convenient in our previous work, but it is not an integral part of the formalism as such. It is thus fully
possible to derive all equations without reference to an underlying basis, similarly to
the works of Kvaal
Kvaal 2012
and Sato et al.
Sato et al. 2018

Such an approach may be more useful if the time-dependent basis is represented on a grid
(Appendix
B
gives some indications on the connection between
the two approaches).

We start by considering separate bra and ket states,
\bra{\psi^{\prime}}
and
\ket{\psi}
, that are expressed in the primitive basis.
Any basis set transformation that conserves (bi)orthogonality can now be stated as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle\rightarrow e^{\hat{\kappa}}\ket{\psi},

(1a)

\displaystyle\bra{\psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bra{\psi^{\prime}}e^{-\hat{\kappa}},

(1b)

where
\hat{\kappa}
is a one-particle operator:

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}=\sum_{m}{\sum_{pq}}^{(m)}\kappa^{m}_{{p}{q}}\,E^{m}_{{p}{q}},\quad E^{m}_{{p}{q}}=a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}.

(2)

The operators
a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}
and
a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}
create and annihilate, respectively, the primitive basis, and we
have used a superscript
(m)

p,q
summation for each vibrational mode
(in particular, the
m
summation and the superscripted
p,q
summation do not commute,
which should be kept in mind when reading the paper).
Anti-Hermitian
\hat{\kappa}
generate unitary transformations that conserve orthogonality, whereas
generic
\hat{\kappa}
generate invertible transformations that conserve biorthogonality.
It is a well-known fact

that the
creators and annihilators transform as

\displaystyle a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\kappa}}\,a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{p}}^{(m)}a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}\exp(\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq},

(3a)

\displaystyle a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\tilde{b}^{m}_{\mspace{-1mu}p}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\kappa}}\,a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{q}}^{(m)}\exp(-\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq}\,a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q},

(3b)

where
\mathbf{K}^{m}
is the matrix containing the
\kappa^{m}_{{p}{q}}
parameters.
We note that the transformed annihilators are
not the adjoint of the transformed creators, except for the special case where
\mathbf{K}^{m}
is anti-Hermitian.
The corresponding transformation
for the ‘first-quantized’ basis functions reads as

\displaystyle\chi^{m}_{q}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\tilde{\varphi}^{m}_{q}

\displaystyle={\sum_{p}}^{(m)}\chi^{m}_{p}\exp(\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq},

(4a)

\displaystyle\chi^{m*}_{p}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime m}_{p}

\displaystyle={\sum_{q}}^{(m)}\exp(-\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq}\,\chi^{m*}_{q}.

(4b)

The transformed functions are generally not each other’s complex conjugate
unless
\mathbf{K}^{m}
is anti-Hermitian,
and one should therefore think of the primal (or ket) basis [Eq. (
4a
)]
as being separate from the dual (or bra) basis [Eq. (
4b
)].
In the case where all basis functions are active (no basis set division), the difference between
an orthogonal and a biorthogonal basis is easily illustrated by using ordinary vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; see Fig.
1
.
When the basis is real (as in Fig.
1
), the dual basis spans the same space as the primal basis, i.e.
the dual space is identical to the primal space.
In the complex case, the dual space is exactly the complex conjugate of the primal space.

We now introduce a division of the time-dependent basis into active and secondary parts (see Fig.
2
for a schematic).
The idea is to use only the active functions for constructing the wave function,
while the secondary functions have exactly zero occupation. Such a division enables a
compact and highly flexible active basis, something that has been used to great effect within the
multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree
Meyer, Manthe, and Cederbaum 1990
;
Beck et al. 2000

framework. In the context of multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree (and similar ‘variational’
Ansätze
) one uses an orthogonal active basis,
so that the active bra space is automatically the conjugate of the active ket space (informally, we will say
that the spaces are the same). This is not so when
\hat{\kappa}
is a generic one-particle operator,
as illustrated in Fig.
3

1(c)

In particular, we note that the dual basis is not uniquely determined by the primal basis when unit overlap is
the only constraint.

We would like an alternative parameterization where the active dual/bra and primal/ket spaces are
the same, but without restricting the active basis to be orthogonal.
The first step towards such a parameterization is to understand that the exponential function
e^{\hat{\kappa}}

has two roles: (i) choosing an active
space
and (ii) choosing a
basis
within that space.
These roles are mixed together in the single exponential formulation,
but they can be separated by introducing a double or split exponential parameterization:

\displaystyle e^{\hat{\kappa}}\rightarrow e^{\hat{\sigma}}e^{\hat{\kappa}},

(5a)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}=\sum_{m}{\sum_{pq}}^{(m)}\sigma^{m}_{{p}{q}}\,E^{m}_{{p}{q}},\quad\hat{\kappa}=\sum_{m}{\sum_{pq}}^{(m)}\kappa^{m}_{{p}{q}}\,E^{m}_{{p}{q}}.

(5b)

Here, the first factor (
e^{\hat{\sigma}}
) is an operator that
changes the active
space
, while the second factor (
e^{\hat{\kappa}}
)
changes the active
basis
(the order of the factors will be explained shortly).
Equivalently, one can think of the factors as generating

interspace
and
intraspace
transformations, i.e. mixing
between
the active and secondary spaces
and mixing
within
the active space.
Such a separation can be realized by requiring a certain type
of structure in the time derivative of the operators
\hat{\sigma}
and
\hat{\kappa}
.
The details for the general case (i.e.
\hat{\sigma}\neq 0
and
\hat{\kappa}\neq 0
)
can be worked out by using the Lie algebraic techniques of Ref.
26
,
but for now we will focus on the simple case where
\hat{\sigma}=0
and
\hat{\kappa}=0
.
At this point, the time derivatives are required to assume a particularly transparent structure, namely

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mspace{2mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{3mu}&{}\mspace{3mu}\bm{*}\\
\hline\cr\mspace{2mu}\bm{*}\mspace{3mu}&{}\mspace{3mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{2mu}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mspace{2mu}\bm{*}\mspace{3mu}&{}\mspace{3mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{2mu}\\
\hline\cr\mspace{2mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{3mu}&{}\mspace{3mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{2mu}\end{array}\right],

where the block structure refers to the
active–secondary division.
We have introduced the matrices
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{m}
and
\mathbf{K}^{m}
containing
the
\sigma^{m}_{{p}{q}}
and
\kappa^{m}_{{p}{q}}
parameters, respectively,
and used asterisks to indicate non-zero blocks.
Regardless of the detailed structure, the basic operators transform as

\displaystyle a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\breve{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\sigma}}e^{\hat{\kappa}}\,a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}}e^{-\hat{\sigma}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{\bar{p}p}}^{(m)}a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\bar{p}}\exp(+\mathbf{\Sigma}^m)_{\bar{p}p}\exp(+\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq}

\displaystyle={\sum_{\bar{p}}}^{(m)}a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\bar{p}}\,\mathcal{U}_{\bar{p}q}^{m},

(7a)

\displaystyle a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\breve{b}^{m}_{\mspace{-1mu}p}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\sigma}}e^{\hat{\kappa}}\,a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}}e^{-\hat{\sigma}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{\bar{q}q}}^{(m)}\exp(-\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq}\exp(-\mathbf{\Sigma}^m)_{q\bar{q}}\,a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}\bar{q}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{\bar{q}}}^{(m)}\mathcal{W}_{p\bar{q}}^{m}\,a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}\bar{q}},

(7b)

where we have defined matrices
\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{m}
and
\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{m}
holding
the coefficients of the total transformation for mode
m
(these matrices are of course the inverse of each other).
It is important to note that the transformations by
\exp(+\mathbf{\Sigma}^m)

and
\exp(-\mathbf{\Sigma}^m)
are carried out first, contrary
to what one might expect from the ordering of the product
e^{\hat{\sigma}}e^{\hat{\kappa}}

The transformation that defines the active space thus precedes the one that defines
a suitable basis within that space.

If we take
\hat{\sigma}
and
\hat{\kappa}
to be both anti-Hermitian (or both generic)
we get an overall transformation that is unitary (or non-unitary) and
completely equivalent to a single exponential transformation.
We are, however, free to choose an anti-Hermitian
\hat{\sigma}
and
a generic
\hat{\kappa}
. This allows unitary transformations of the active space,
and non-unitary transformations within the active space. In particular, the
active bra and ket bases are not necessarily orthogonal, but they span the same space.
The double exponential format is useable as it stands (i.e. one can
propagate the matrices
\mathbf{K}^{m}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{m}
as the time-dependent basis set

We are free to absorb the unitary transformation into

\displaystyle{\sum_{\bar{p}p}}^{(m)}a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\bar{p}}\exp(+\mathbf{\Sigma}^m)_{\bar{p}p}\exp(+\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq}

\displaystyle={\sum_{p}}^{(m)}\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}\exp(+\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\kappa}}\,\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}},

(8a)

\displaystyle{\sum_{\bar{q}q}}^{(m)}\exp(-\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq}\exp(-\mathbf{\Sigma}^m)_{q\bar{q}}\,a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}\bar{q}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{q}}^{(m)}\exp(-\mathbf{K}^m)_{pq}\,\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\kappa}}\,\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}}.

(8b)

Although we have not changed the notation, the
\hat{\kappa}
operator
is now defined with respect to the moving creators
\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}=\sum_{m}{\sum_{pq}}^{(m)}\kappa^{m}_{{p}{q}}\tilde{E}^{m}_{{p}{q}},\quad\tilde{E}^{m}_{{p}{q}}=\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}.

(9)

\displaystyle\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}={\sum_{\bar{p}}}^{(m)}a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\bar{p}}\exp(\mathbf{\Sigma}^m)_{\bar{p}p}={\sum_{\bar{p}}}^{(m)}a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\bar{p}}V^{m}_{\mspace{-2mu}\bar{p}p},

Returning to the double exponential format at the point

we could also require

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mspace{2mu}\bm{*}\mspace{3mu}&{}\mspace{3mu}\bm{*}\\
\hline\cr\mspace{2mu}\bm{*}\mspace{3mu}&{}\mspace{3mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{2mu}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mspace{2mu}\bm{*}\mspace{3mu}&{}\mspace{3mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{2mu}\\
\hline\cr\mspace{2mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{3mu}&{}\mspace{3mu}\mathbf{0}\mspace{2mu}\end{array}\right],

with
\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{m}
anti-Hermitian and
\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{m}
Hermitian.
This leads to a polar-type decomposition
Hall 2015
of the overall basis set transformation
with
e^{\hat{\sigma}}
unitary and
e^{\hat{\kappa}}
positive definite,
and where the latter factor is restricted to acting within the active space.
We arrived at such a ‘restricted polar’ parameterization from a different starting point in Ref.
21
,
but the equations of motion obtained were not fully bivariational. Although we did not observe
any deterioration of accuracy, we did see some energy non-conversation as a symptom
of the non-bivariational nature of the theory. It should be possible to derive
a fully bivariational version of this theory, but we believe it would be
rather complicated compared to the approach of separating interspace and intraspace transformations.

The idea of a double exponential basis set transformation has in fact been considered
in ground state electronic structure theory. Köhn and Olsen
Köhn and Olsen 2005

considered a ‘modified OCC’ method defined by a (bi)variationally optimized, unitary interspace transformation
and a Brueckner-type intraspace transformation determined by projection. This method recovers the full configuration interaction
limit for ground state energies, but inherits the unphysical second-order poles in the
Brueckner coupled cluster response function.
Aiga, Sasagane, and Itoh 1994
;
Koch, Kobayashi, and Jørgensen 1994

It is also not clear to us how one would actually propagate a Brueckner-type
transformation in time. Consequently, we do not consider the ‘modified orbital-optimized coupled cluster’
Ansatz

as an attractive choice for our purposes. We also mention the work of Olsen
Olsen 2015
on CI-type
expansions with non-orthogonal orbitals that are optimized using a polar-type product
e^{\hat{\sigma}}e^{\hat{\kappa}}
, where

\hat{\sigma}
is anti-Hermitian (anti-symmetric) and
\hat{\kappa}
is Hermitian (symmetric). Although this approach is highly
relevant for the present discussion, it is not directly applicable in the context of wave functions that
are explicitly time-dependent, complex-valued, and bivariational.

II.2
The Hamiltonian in second quantization

The first-quantized Hamiltonian can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\hat{H}^{\mathbf{m}},

(15)

where
\mathbf{m}
is a mode combination, i.e. a set of modes.
The notation means that a given term
\hat{H}^{\mathbf{m}}
acts
only on the modes
\mathbf{m}
. In the second-quantized formalism,
Christiansen 2004

the Hamiltonian with respect to the primitive basis reads

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}{\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}}^{(\mathbf{m})}H^{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}E^{\mathbf{m}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}{\bm{\beta}}},\quad E^{\mathbf{m}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}{\bm{\beta}}}=\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}E^{m}_{{\alpha^{m}}{\beta^{m}}},

(16)

with integrals

\displaystyle H^{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}=\Big\langle\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\chi^{m}_{\alpha^{m}}\Big|\hat{H}^{\mathbf{m}}\Big|\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\chi^{m}_{\beta^{m}}\Big\rangle.

(17)

Here,
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
are vectors of primitive indices, which are henceforth represented by lowercase Greek letters.
Each operator
\hat{H}^{\mathbf{m}}
is typically represented as a sum of products of one-mode operators, in which case the integrals factor into products of one-mode integrals.
In the basis defined by the moving creators of Eq. (
9
), the operator looks like

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}{\sum_{\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}}}^{(\mathbf{m})}\tilde{H}^{\mathbf{m}}_{\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}}\tilde{E}^{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{q}}},

(18)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}^{\mathbf{m}}_{\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}}={\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}}^{(\mathbf{m})}\left(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}V^{m*}_{\mspace{-2mu}\alpha^{m}p^{m}}\right)H^{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}\left(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}V^{m}_{\mspace{-2mu}\beta^{m}q^{m}}\right),

(19)

but we remark that Eqs. (
16
) and (
18
) are strictly equal. We will also encounter
the similarity transformed Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\bar{H}=e^{-\hat{\kappa}}He^{\hat{\kappa}}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}{\sum_{\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}}}^{(\mathbf{m})}\breve{H}^{\mathbf{m}}_{\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}}\tilde{E}^{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{q}}}.

(20)

The creators and annihilators are still in the basis of Eq. (
9
), but the integrals have been transformed once more:

\displaystyle\breve{H}^{\mathbf{m}}_{\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{\bar{\mathbf{p}}\bar{\mathbf{q}}}}^{(\mathbf{m})}\left(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\exp(-\mathbf{K}^m)_{p^{m}\bar{p}^{m}}\right)\tilde{H}^{\mathbf{m}}_{\bar{\mathbf{p}}\bar{\mathbf{q}}}\left(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\exp(\mathbf{K})_{\bar{q}^{m}q^{m}}\right)

\displaystyle={\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}}^{(\mathbf{m})}\left(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\mathcal{W}^{m}_{p^{m}\alpha^{m}}\right)H^{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}\left(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\mathcal{U}^{m}_{\beta^{m}q^{m}}\right).

(21)

II.3
The time-dependent bivariational principle

The time-dependent bivariational principle in its original form
Arponen 1983
;
Kvaal 2012

considers a complex-valued Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{L}=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|(i\partial_{t}-H)|\Psi\rangle,

(22)

and the corresponding complex-valued action,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\mathcal{L}\differential{t}.

(23)

The bra and ket states are taken to be independent.
Requiring the action to be stationary (
\delta\mathcal{S}=0
) with respect to arbitrary
and independent variations that vanish at the end points is equivalent
to the Schrödinger equation and its dual:

\displaystyle i\ket{\dot{\Psi}}=H\ket{\Psi},

(24a)

\displaystyle-i\bra{\dot{\Psi}^{\prime}}=\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}H.

(24b)

In practice, the bra and ket states are parameterized in terms of a set of complex
parameters
z_{i}
and the stationary condition is then equivalent to a set
of Euler–Lagrange equations,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{z_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{z}_i}.

(25)

These equations are consistent as long as the Lagrangian is holomorphic (complex analytic) in
the parameters (in particular, complex conjugation must not appear in
\mathcal{L}
).
This subtle point is mentioned by Kvaal
Kvaal 2012
and discussed
in some detail in Ref.
23
and its Appendix A. Although it may seem like a technicality, it
has some important consequences for the kinds of parameterizations that can be permitted.
As an example, the complex-valued Lagrangian rules out the use of an orthogonal basis
(in that case the bra basis is simply the complex conjugate of the ket basis).

Instead, we may consider a manifestly real Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}=\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{L})=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{L}^{*}),

(26)

where we allow the parameterization of the bra and ket states to be non-holomorphic,
i.e. we allow the states to depend on a set of parameters
\mathbf{z}
as well
as the complex conjugate parameters
\mathbf{z}^{*}
.
The total set of independent variational parameters is thus
\{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*}\}

(it is also possible to use
\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{z})
and
\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{z})
as the independent parameters,
but this is somewhat inconvenient in practical derivations).
The evolution of the parameters is determined by making the real action functional

\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{S})
stationary, which
is equivalent to a set of Euler–Lagrange equation for the parameters
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{z}^{*}
separately, i.e.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{z_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\dot{z}_i},

(27a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{z_i^*}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\dot{z}_i^*}.

(27b)

These equations are each other’s complex conjugate, since the Lagrangian is real. It is thus sufficient to solve
one of them.

We note that if
\mathcal{L}
depends on a certain parameter
z_{k}
, but not on the complex conjugate
z_{k}^{*}
, then
the real-valued time-dependent bivariational principle reduces to the complex-valued
time-dependent bivariational principle for that particular parameter:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{z_k}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\dot{z}_k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{z_k}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{z}_k}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{z_k^*}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{z}_k^*}\right)^{\!*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{z_k}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{z}_k}\right).

(28)

II.4
Parameterization and constraints

As explained in Sec.
II.1
, we consider a moving orthonormal
basis or, equivalently, a set of moving creators

\displaystyle\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}={\sum_{\alpha}}^{(m)}a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\alpha}V^{m}_{\mspace{-2mu}\alpha p}.

(29)

At this point we consider all basis functions (active and secondary) explicitly, so that
the matrix
\mathbf{V}^{m}
containing the expansion coefficients is a square, unitary matrix.

The time-dependent basis must be orthonormal at any given time,
which is a constraint on the basis set. In addition, the
time derivative of this constraint must vanish at any given time
(this is a so-called consistency condition
Dirac 1950
;
Ohta 2000
;
Ohta 2004
).
Using matrix notation, the constraints and consistency conditions read

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{m\dagger}\,\mathbf{V}^{m}=\mathbf{1}^{m},

(30a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m\dagger}\,\mathbf{V}^{m}+\mathbf{V}^{m\dagger}\,\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m}.

(30b)

The latter equation holds trivially if

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m\dagger}\,\mathbf{V}^{m}

\displaystyle=+i\mathbf{G}^{m},

(31a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{m\dagger}\,\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m}

\displaystyle=-i\mathbf{G}^{m},

(31b)

where
\mathbf{G}^{m}
is a Hermitian but otherwise arbitrary matrix, which
we call the constraint matrix. Using Eq. (
31b
)
and the fact that
\mathbf{V}^{m}
is unitary we see that

\displaystyle i\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m}=\mathbf{V}^{m}\mathbf{G}^{m},

(32)

which means that the constraint matrix acts as a generator for the time evolution of
\mathbf{V}^{m}
.

It is convenient at this point to divide the time-dependent basis
into an active basis indexed by
t
,
u
,
v
,
w

and a secondary basis indexed by
x
,
y
.
We use
\alpha
,
\beta
to denote primitive indices, while
p
,
q
,
r
,
s

denote generic time-dependent indices. The division of the basis induces
a block structure in
\mathbf{V}^{m}
and
\mathbf{G}^{m}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{m}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}{\mathbf{V}^{m}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}\,}&\,\mathbf{V}^{m}_{\!\textsc{s}}\rule[-4.30554pt]{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{m}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}{\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}}&{\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{s}}}\rule{0.0pt}{10.33327pt}\\
\hline\cr{\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{a}}}&{\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{s}}}\rule{0.0pt}{10.33327pt}\end{array}\right].

Right-hand subscripts
a
and
s
are used to indicate the active and secondary
blocks of matrices that have one primitive and one time-dependent index.
Matrices with two time-dependent indices have four blocks:
(i) active–active (
a
a
); (ii) active–secondary (
a
s
); (iii)
secondary–active (
s
a
); and (iv) secondary–secondary (
s
s
).

In order to ensure that
\mathbf{G}^{m}
generates only interspace transformations,
we enforce the block structure of Eq. (
6
), i.e. we set

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{m}\equiv\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mathbf{0}&{\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{s}}}\rule{0.0pt}{10.33327pt}\\
\hline\cr{\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{a}}}&\mathbf{0}\rule{0.0pt}{10.33327pt}\end{array}\right].

The
\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{s}}
block is always redundant and can safely be set to zero,
whereas the
\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}
block would ordinarily not be redundant.
It is, however, not needed in our case since we have explicitly introduced a separate parameterization
for the intraspace transformations. In conclusion, we only retain the

\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{a}}
and
\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{s}}=(\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{a}})^{\dagger}
blocks, which
mix the active and secondary spaces.
It is important to note that this particular
Ansatz
for
\mathbf{G}^{m}
generates unitary transformations
that change the active space without determining an appropriate
active basis. That is to say that the columns of
\mathbf{V}^{m}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}
span
the active space, but they are generally not good basis vectors in themselves.

To summarize: We will consider rather general bra and ket parameterizations of the form

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\kappa},\mathbf{V})}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\kappa}}\ket{\psi(\bm{\alpha},\mathbf{V})},

(36a)

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\kappa},\mathbf{V}^{*})}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi^{\prime}(\bm{\alpha},\mathbf{V}^{*})}e^{-\hat{\kappa}},

(36b)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}=\sum_{m}{\sum_{tu}}^{(m)}\kappa^{m}_{{t}{u}}\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}},\quad\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}}=\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}t}\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}u}.

(37)

The
\kappa^{m}_{{t}{u}}
parameters are collected in vectors
\bm{\kappa}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}
or matrices
\mathbf{K}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}
depending
on context (the vector
\bm{\kappa}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}
contains all
\kappa^{m}_{{t}{u}}
for all modes).

\bm{\alpha}
is a vector of complex configurational or correlation parameters (e.g. coupled cluster amplitudes) and

\mathbf{V}=\{\mathbf{V}^{m}\}
denotes the basis set coefficients. It is important to note
that the ket state depends on the coefficients themselves, while the bra state depends
on the complex conjugate coefficients,
\mathbf{V}^{*}
.

II.5
Equations of motion

II.5.1
Lagrangian

In order to compute the Lagrangian, Eq. (
26
),
we will start by considering the action of the time-derivative on
a single Hartree product:

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}s^{m}}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

(38)

It is easy to see by applying the product rule that the time derivative
acts as a one-particle operator on a single Hartree product (the same holds
for a single Slater determinant). In fact, one can show (see Ref.
21
)
that

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{\mathbf{r}}|\partial_{t}|\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}\rangle=\langle\Phi_{\mathbf{r}}|(-iG)|\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}\rangle

(39)

where the so-called constraint operator,

\displaystyle G=\sum_{m}{\sum_{pq}}^{(m)}g^{m}_{{p}{q}}\,\tilde{E}^{m}_{{p}{q}},

(40)

is constructed from the constraint matrix elements. Next, we introduce an active-space
resolution of identity and write the bra and ket as

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\ket{\mu}\mspace{-2mu}\mspace{-2mu}\braket{\mu|\Psi}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\kappa})\ket{\mu(\mathbf{V})},

(41a)

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\braket{\Psi^{\prime}|\mu}\mspace{-2mu}\mspace{-2mu}\bra{\mu}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mu}{c^{\prime}}_{\mspace{-6mu}\mu}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\kappa})\bra{\mu(\mathbf{V}^{*})}.

(41b)

The summations run over all Hartree products (or Slater determinants) within the active space.
Defining
\mathbf{y}=(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\kappa})
, we find that

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\partial_{t}|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}\sum_{\mu}\big(\dot{c}_{\mu}\ket{\mu}+c_{\mu}\ket{\dot{\mu}}\big)

\displaystyle=\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}\sum_{\mu j}\dot{y}_{j}\partialderivative{c_\mu}{y_{j}}\ket{\mu}+\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}(-iG)\ket{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\dot{y}_{j}\Big\langle\Psi^{\prime}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\Psi}{y_{j}}\Big\rangle-i\langle\Psi^{\prime}|G|\Psi\rangle

(42)

and, consequently, that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|(i\partial_{t}-H)|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=i\sum_{j}\dot{y}_{j}\Big\langle\Psi^{\prime}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\Psi}{y_{j}}\Big\rangle-\langle\Psi^{\prime}|(H-G)|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{H}^{\prime}.

(43)

The first term depends only on
\mathbf{y}
and
\dot{\mathbf{y}}
, while the second term depends
on
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{V}
,
\dot{\mathbf{V}}
and
\mathbf{V}^{*}
.

II.5.2
Basis set coefficients

Equation (
II.5.1
) has exactly the same form as the Lagrangian in Ref.
23
, so we simply
restate the result from that paper: The Euler–Lagrange equations

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\vplain{m}{\alpha}{q}}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\vplaindot{m}{\alpha}{q}}

(44)

lead to the following equations for
{\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{a}}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{a}}}\;\mathbb{H}[\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\displaystyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\rho$}}}}^{m}_{\!\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}({\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{m}_{\!\textsc{s}\textsc{a}}}+{\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime m\dagger}_{\!\!\textsc{a}\textsc{s}}})

(45a)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{V}^{m\dagger}_{\!\textsc{s}}(\check{\mathbf{F}}^{m}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}+\check{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime m\dagger}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}).

(45b)

Here,
\mathbb{H}[\,\cdot\,]
denotes the Hermitian part of a square matrix.
We have defined one-particle density matrices,

\displaystyle\rho^{m}_{pq}=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\tilde{E}^{m}_{{q}{p}}|\Psi\rangle={\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}}^{(m)}\exp(\mathbf{K}^m)_{p\bar{p}}\,\langle\psi^{\prime}|\tilde{E}^{m}_{{\bar{q}}{\bar{p}}}|\psi\rangle\exp(-\mathbf{K}^m)_{\bar{q}q},

(46)

generalized mean-field or Fock matrices,

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{\prime m}_{{q}{p}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|[H,\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}]\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}|\Psi\rangle,

(47a)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{m}_{{q}{p}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p}[\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q},H]|\Psi\rangle,

(47b)

and partially transformed mean-field matrices,

\displaystyle\check{F}^{\prime m}_{{q}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{H}}{\vplain{m}{\alpha}{q}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|[H,a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\alpha}]\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}|\Psi\rangle,

(48a)

\displaystyle\check{F}^{m}_{{\alpha}{q}}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{H}}{\vplainconj{m}{\alpha}{q}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}q}[a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}\alpha},H]|\Psi\rangle.

(48b)

These matrices are partially transformed in the sense that one index refers to the primitive basis, while
the other refers to the orthogonal tilde basis.
Equation (
46
) relates the density matrix of the
\hat{\kappa}
-transformed states
to that of the untransformed states in a simple way, which is useful for practical implementations.
One also needs to address the
\hat{\kappa}
-transformation inside the mean-field
matrix elements, and some care is needed in the partially transformed case
since it involves creators and annihilators in different bases.
It is always possible to write the (partially transformed) mean fields in
terms of contractions of (partially transformed) Hamiltonian integrals and density matrices.
The
\hat{\kappa}
-transformation adds extra one-index contractions that can be absorbed
into either the integrals or the density matrices, depending on implementation details.
Our approach is outlined in Appendix
A
.

Unlike Ref.
23
, we dot not solve any equations for
\mathbf{G}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}
, since
these constraint elements are simply not part of our parameterization.
Dropping the mode index for clarity,
Eq. (
32
) now reads

\displaystyle i\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\dot{\mathbf{V}}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}&{\,}\dot{\mathbf{V}}_{\!\textsc{s}}\rule{0.0pt}{10.33327pt}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}&{\,}\mathbf{V}_{\!\textsc{s}}\rule{0.0pt}{10.33327pt}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}{\mathbf{0}}&{\,}{\mathbf{G}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{s}}}\rule{0.0pt}{10.33327pt}\\
\hline\cr{\mathbf{G}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{a}}}&{\,}{\mathbf{0}}\rule{0.0pt}{10.33327pt}\end{array}\right],

or, focusing on the active block,

\displaystyle i\dot{\mathbf{V}}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{\!\textsc{s}}\,{\mathbf{G}_{\textsc{s}\textsc{a}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{\!\textsc{s}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\mspace{-2mu}\mathbf{V}_{\!\textsc{s}}^{\dagger}\tfrac{1}{2}(\check{\mathbf{F}}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}+\check{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime\dagger}_{\!\!\textsc{a}})\,\mathbb{H}[\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\displaystyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\rho$}}}}_{\!\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Q}(\check{\mathbf{F}}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}+\check{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime\dagger}_{\!\!\textsc{a}})\,\mathbb{H}[\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\displaystyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\rho$}}}}_{\!\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}]^{-1}

(53)

The secondary-space projector
\mathbf{Q}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{V}_{\!\textsc{s}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\mspace{-2mu}\mathbf{V}_{\!\textsc{s}}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\mspace{-2mu}\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}^{\dagger}

(54)

and allows us to propagate
\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}
without reference to secondary-space quantities.

II.5.3
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\kappa}
parameters

The complex Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
is an analytic function of

\mathbf{y}
and
\dot{\mathbf{y}}

(

and
\dot{\mathbf{y}}^{*}
never appear),
so the Euler–Lagrange equations

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{y_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\dot{y}_i}

(55)

are equivalent to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{y_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{y}_i}.

(56)

This set of equations is identical to that studied in Ref.
26
,
except for the fact that the Hamiltonian appearing in
\mathcal{L}

is modified by

; see Eq. (
II.5.1
). The operator

does not depend on
\mathbf{y}
or

,

In fact, the

) and the usual killer conditions. We will thus proceed with
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
.
It is not difficult to show
Højlund, Zoccante, and Christiansen 2023

that Eq. (
56
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle i\sum_{j}\mathcal{M}_{ij}\dot{y}_{j}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{H}}{y_i},

(57)

where

\displaystyle i\dot{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{h}^{\prime},

(59a)

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\big)\tilde{\mathbf{g}}

(59b)

(60a)

\displaystyle A_{{i}({m^{\prime}}{v}{w})}

(60b)

\displaystyle C_{({m}{t}{u})({m^{\prime}}{v}{w})}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\tilde{E}^{m^{\prime}}_{{v}{w}},\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}}]|\psi\rangle,

(60c)

\displaystyle f_{(m{t}{u})}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\bar{H},\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}}]|\psi\rangle,

(60d)

\displaystyle h_{i}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\alpha_i}\langle\psi^{\prime}|\bar{H}|\psi\rangle,

(60e)

\displaystyle h_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\alpha_i}\langle\psi^{\prime}|(\bar{H}-\tilde{G})|\psi\rangle,

(60f)

\displaystyle\bar{H}

\displaystyle=e^{-\hat{\kappa}}He^{\hat{\kappa}}.

(60g)

The operator
\tilde{G}
, which is determined by Eq. (

),
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{G}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}{\sum_{vw}}^{(m)}\tilde{g}^{m}_{vw}\,\tilde{E}^{m}_{{v}{w}}

\displaystyle=ie^{-\hat{\kappa}}\derivative{e^{\hat{\kappa}}}{t}

\displaystyle=i\sum_{m}{\sum_{tu}}^{(m)}\dot{\kappa}^{m}_{{t}{u}}\,e^{-\hat{\kappa}}\partialderivative{e^{\hat{\kappa}}}{\kappaplain{m}{t}{u}}

\displaystyle\equiv i\sum_{m}{\sum_{tu}}^{(m)}\dot{\kappa}^{m}_{{t}{u}}\,D^{m}_{tu}

\displaystyle=i\sum_{m}{\sum_{tuvw}}^{(m)}\dot{\kappa}^{m}_{{t}{u}}\,d^{m}_{({t}{u})({v}{w})}\tilde{E}^{m}_{{v}{w}},

(61)

or, in matrix-vector notation,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{m}=i(\mathbf{D}^{m})^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\dot{\bm{\kappa}}^{m}_{\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}.

(62)

\displaystyle=\exp(\mbf{K}^m_{\subA\subA}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{G}}^{m}=i\mathbf{W}^{m}_{\!\!\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}\dot{\mathbf{U}}^{m}_{\!\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}=-i\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\!\!\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}\mathbf{U}^{m}_{\!\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}

II.6
Working equations for the CC
Ansatz

We now state the working equations in greater detail with emphasis on the coupled cluster
Ansatz
:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}=e^{\hat{\kappa}}\ket{\psi}=e^{\hat{\kappa}}e^{T}\ket{\Phi},

(66a)

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}=\bra{\psi^{\prime}}e^{-\hat{\kappa}}=\bra{\Phi}Le^{-T}e^{-\hat{\kappa}}.

(66b)

The operators
T
and
L
are given by

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}t_{\mu}\tilde{\tau}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=t_{0}+T_{2}+T_{3}+\ldots

(67a)

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}l_{\mu}\tilde{\tau}_{\mu}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=l_{0}+L_{2}+L_{3}+\ldots

(67b)

where it should be noted that the single excitations are omitted since they are redundant
with the basis set transformation generated by
\hat{\kappa}
.
Kvaal 2012
;
Sato et al. 2018
;
Madsen et al. 2020a
;
Pedersen, Koch, and Hättig 1999
;
Pedersen, Fernández, and Koch 2001

The excitation operators
\tilde{\tau}_{\mu}
and the reference
\ket{\Phi}

are defined in terms of the moving creators
\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}v}
,
and we can thus consider Eqs. (
66
) as an non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster-type expansion inside an evolving active space.
This
Ansatz
is able to reproduce the complete wave function expansion
within the active space,
Myhre 2018

which means that exact active-space densities and mean fields are obtained when
T
and
L
are not truncated.
Exact densities and mean fields in turn generate the exact evolution of the active space itself
according to Eq. (
II.5.2
). The
Ansatz
thus reproduces the multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree limit in the
vibrational case and the multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree–Fock limit in the electronic structure case.

The amplitude equations of motion can of course be derived by applying the time-dependent bivariational principle
directly to the coupled cluster
Ansatz
, but in our general framework we order the
amplitudes like
\bm{\alpha}=(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{l})
and apply Eq. (
60a
) to find that

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mathbf{0}&-\mathbf{1}\\
\hline\cr+\mathbf{1}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right],\quad\mathbf{M}^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mathbf{0}&+\mathbf{1}\\
\hline\cr-\mathbf{1}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right].

Having obtained
\mathbf{M}^{-1}
, Eq. (
59a
) readily yields the result:

\displaystyle i\dot{t}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=+\langle\mu|e^{-T}(\bar{H}-\tilde{G})|\psi\rangle,

(72a)

\displaystyle i\dot{l}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\bar{H}-\tilde{G},\tilde{\tau}_{\mu}]|\psi\rangle.

(72b)

The non-unitary basis set transformation is determined by Eq. (
59b
),

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\big)\tilde{\mathbf{g}}=\big(\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{h}\big),

(73)

which we rewrite simply as

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}=\mathbf{f}^{\prime}.

(74)

The primes indicate the inclusion of the terms depending on
\mathbf{A}
.
Referring to Eqs. (
60
), one easily computes
the elements of
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}
:

C^{\prime}_{({m}{t}{u})({m^{\prime}}{v}{w})}=\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\tilde{E}^{m^{\prime}}_{{v}{w}},\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}}]|\psi\rangle\\
+\sum_{\mu}\Big(\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}},\tilde{\tau}_{\mu}]|\psi\rangle\langle\mu|e^{-T}\tilde{E}^{m^{\prime}}_{{v}{w}}|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\tilde{E}^{m^{\prime}}_{{v}{w}},\tilde{\tau}_{\mu}]|\psi\rangle\langle\mu|e^{-T}\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}}|\psi\rangle\Big)

(75)

and

f^{\prime}_{(m{t}{u})}=\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\bar{H},\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}}]|\psi\rangle\\
+\sum_{\mu}\Big(\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}},\tilde{\tau}_{\mu}]|\psi\rangle\langle\mu|e^{-T}\bar{H}|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\bar{H},\tilde{\tau}_{\mu}]|\psi\rangle\langle\mu|e^{-T}\tilde{E}^{m}_{{t}{u}}|\psi\rangle\Big).

(76)

The active space is divided into occupied (
i,j,\ldots
) and virtual (
a,b,\ldots
) subspaces,
which means that index pairs
tu
can be divided into four types:

ai
(up),
ia
(down),
ab
(forward) and
ij
(passive). A particular feature
of vibrational structure theory is that each mode has only a single occupied index,
something that is useful in concrete implementations. Using
u
,
d
,
f
and
p

to label the four types of index pairs induces a block structure in the linear equations.
Many of these blocks can be shown to vanish,
Madsen et al. 2020a

so that we are left with

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c | c | c}\mathbf{0}&{{}^{ud}\mathbf{C}}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr{{}^{du}\mathbf{C}}&{{}^{dd}\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&{\hskip 7.3194pt}\mathbf{0}&\hskip 7.3194pt\mathbf{0}{\hskip 6.45831pt}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{{}^{u}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr{{}^{d}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr{{}^{f\!}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr{{}^{p}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{{}^{u}\mathbf{f}}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr{{}^{d}\mathbf{f}^{\prime}}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\end{array}\right].

Evidently, the forward and passive blocks of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
are redundant. This is not
surprising since these blocks generate transformations within the virtual and occupied
spaces, respectively. Such intraspace transformations are redundant for a wide
range of time-dependent and time-independent wave function
Ansätze
, including coupled cluster.
One can in fact determine the redundancies from the outset by geometric arguments
(see e.g. Refs.
19
and
36
),
but we find it satisfying and reassuring to see that they also show up in a concrete derivation.

The non-redundant equations now read

\displaystyle{{}^{ud}\mathbf{C}}\,{{}^{d}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}={{}^{u}\mathbf{f}},

(89a)

\displaystyle{{}^{du}\mathbf{C}}\,{{}^{u}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}+{{}^{dd}\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}\,{{}^{d}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}={{}^{d}\mathbf{f}^{\prime}},

(89b)

and can be solved as

\displaystyle{{}^{d}\mathbf{g}}=({{}^{ud}\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\,{{}^{u}\mathbf{f}},

(90a)

\displaystyle{{}^{u}\mathbf{g}}=({{}^{du}\mathbf{C}})^{-1}({{}^{d}\mathbf{f}^{\prime}}-{{}^{dd}\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}\,{{}^{d}\mathbf{g}}).

(90b)

We observe the following: (i) the
up
and
down
equations
can be solved sequentially and (ii) the matrices
{{}^{ud}\mathbf{C}}
and
{{}^{du}\mathbf{C}}

are diagonal in the mode index [see Eq. (
60c
)], meaning they can be
inverted one mode at a time. Equations (
90
)
thus consist of
2M
linear systems of dimension
N_{\textsc{v}}N_{\textsc{o}}\times N_{\textsc{v}}N_{\textsc{o}}=N_{\textsc{v}}\times N_{\textsc{v}}
,
where
M
is the number of vibrational modes. The symbols
N_{\textsc{v}}
and
N_{\textsc{o}}=1
denote the
number of virtual and occupied functions per mode, and we assume, for simplicity,
that
N_{\textsc{v}}
is the same for all modes (this is often not the case in practical calculations).
The fact that the linear equations are up-down and mode-mode
decoupled constitutes a large reduction in the computational effort compared
to the orthogonal case,
Højlund, Zoccante, and Christiansen 2024

where one generally (except at the doubles level) has to solve a single linear system of dimension

2MN_{\textsc{v}}N_{\textsc{o}}\times 2MN_{\textsc{v}}N_{\textsc{o}}=2MN_{\textsc{v}}\times 2MN_{\textsc{v}}
.
In electronic structure theory, only the up-down decoupling applies, and
the reduction from one system of dimension
2N_{\textsc{v}}N_{\textsc{o}}\times 2N_{\textsc{v}}N_{\textsc{o}}
(orthogonal orbitals) to
two systems of dimension
N_{\textsc{v}}N_{\textsc{o}}\times N_{\textsc{v}}N_{\textsc{o}}
(biorthogonal orbitals) is less striking, although still significant.

III
IMPLEMENTATION

The sTDMVCC method has been implemented in the Molecular Interactions, Dynamics and Simulations Chemistry Program Package,
Christiansen et al. 2023

which includes an array of methods for solving the time-independent and
time-dependent vibrational Schrödinger equations and for constructing potential energy surfaces.
At the two-mode coupling level (sTDMVCC
[2]
/H2, i.e.
T=T_{2}
and
H=H_{1}+H_{2}
),
we have simply adapted the efficient TDMVCC
[2]
/H2 implementation of
Ref.
38
, which
offers a cubic-scaling computational effort. For general coupling levels in
T
and

H
we adapt the original time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster code,
Madsen et al. 2020a

which is based on the full-space matrix representation implementation of Ref.
39
.
This is a FCI-type code, so the computational cost scales exponentially with respect to system size.
Such a scaling is of course prohibitive for practical usage, but the full-space matrix representation code
has nevertheless been instrumental for testing and benchmarking new coupled cluster variants
with limited programming effort.

To avoid possible (near-)singularities in the one-mode density matrices [see Eq.(
II.5.2
)],
we use a regularization scheme based on singular value decomposition:

\displaystyle\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\displaystyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\rho$}}{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\rho$}}}}_{\!\textsc{a}\textsc{a}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{Y}^{\dagger}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}+\epsilon\exp(-\mathbf{\Sigma}/\epsilon)\big)\mathbf{Y}^{\dagger}.

(91)

Here,
\epsilon
is a small regularization parameter (typically,
\epsilon=10^{-8}
).
We rarely observe singularities in the main body of a simulation,
but they do occur close to
t=0
when using a vibrational self-consistent field initial state or some other initial
state that has active basis functions with exactly zero occupation.
The same kind of regularization is used for inverting the individual blocks of

{{}^{ud}\mathbf{C}}
and
{{}^{du}\mathbf{C}}
in Eqs. (
90
).
Finally, we modify the secondary-space projector of Eq. (
54
)
according to

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\mspace{-2mu}\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}^{\dagger}\rightarrow\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}^{\phantom{\dagger}})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}^{\dagger}.

(92)

This guarantees that
\mathbf{Q}
remains a proper projector even if the columns of
\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\textsc{a}}
are not strictly orthonormal.

IV.2
6D salicylaldimine

Our second example is the intra-molecular proton transfer in salicylaldimine.
This is an asymmetric double-well system with the lower well corresponding to the enol
tautomer, which is more stable than the keto tautomer.
We use the six-dimensional model potential energy surface from Ref.
40
,
which includes the modes labelled as

The initial state is a Gaussian wave packet placed just on the enol side of the barrier,
but with an energy lower than the barrier height
(see Refs.
40
and
44

Crossing the barrier is thus classically forbidden and any flux over the barrier is solely due to tunneling.

The primitive basis for the calculation is obtained in the following way:
Initially, a large B-spline basis is used for the double-well mode,
while the remaining modes are described by 21 harmonic oscillator functions each.
This basis is then transformed to a set of orthonormal vibrational self-consistent field
modals. For the double-well mode, we use the lowest 30 vibrational self-consistent field modals
as the primitive basis, while the remaining modes use all 21 vibrational self-consistent field modals.
The initial active modals are simply the
N_{\!\textsc{a}}=3
lowest vibrational self-consistent field modals
for each mode. We do not expect the calculations to be fully converged with respect
to
N_{\!\textsc{a}}
, but we note that our results are very similar
to Ref.
44
. Even if
N_{\!\textsc{a}}

is not quite converged, this has no effect on the comparison between methods.

20\,000\text{\,}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}
(

IV.2.3
Energy conservation

The physical energy
E=\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{Re}\langle\Psi^{\prime}|H|\Psi\rangle
[Fig.
10(a)
] is
always conserved for the fully bivariational methods, i.e. TDMVCC, oTDMVCC, and sTDMVCC,
which is of course reassuring.
In contrast, rpTDMVCC does not conserve energy, except when the cluster expansion becomes complete.
We take this as an unfortunate symptom of the non-bivariational nature of this method.

The imaginary part of the Hamiltonian function,
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{H})
, has
no experimental significance, but it reveals some interesting and fundamental differences
between methods that are
complex
bivariational (based on the complex-valued Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
)
and methods that are
real
bivariational (based on the real-valued Lagrangian
\bar{\mathcal{L}}
).
For a complex bivariational method (such as TDMVCC), the dynamics is generated by the complex-valued
Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}
. This entails conservation of
\mathcal{H}
and thus of
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{H})
,
which is confirmed by Fig.
10(b)
.
The dynamics of real bivariational methods (such as oTDMVCC and sTDMVCC) is generated by
the real-valued Hamiltonian function
\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{H})
, which implies
conservation of
\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{H})
. We cannot expect
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{H})
to be conserved,
unless the wave function reproduces the complete-active-space solution exactly. Indeed, Fig.
10(b)
shows that oTDMVCC
never conserves
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{H})
, while sTDMVCC conserves
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{H})
in the
exact limit. We remark that rpTDMVCC behaves much like sTDMVCC, but in this case the analysis is less clear
since rpTDMVCC is not fully bivariational in either the real or the complex sense.

V
SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK

We have introduced a new formulation of time-dependent coupled cluster with adaptive basis functions and division of the
one-particle space into active and secondary parts.
The formalism
is fully bivariational and converges to the appropriate limit (i.e. MCTDH/MCTDHF) in a numerically stable manner.
The key idea of the theory is to parameterize the time-dependent basis in terms of strictly separate interspace and intraspace
transformations that change the active space itself and the active basis within that space, respectively.
Choosing a unitary interspace transformations and a non-unitary intraspace transformation
guarantees that the active bra and ket functions are biorthogonal while spanning the same space.
The former ensures convergence to the correct limit, while the latter ensures numerical stability.
The theory covers electron as well as vibrational dynamics, but our main interest lies
with the vibrational problem to which we specialize the theory. The resulting method, which is called split time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster,
is implemented, benchmarked, and compared to three similar coupled cluster methods that have previously
been considered by our group. Table
1
summarizes our findings,
and it is clear that only split time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster succeeds in combining the
four attractive properties we have listed. In addition, we find that the split unitary/non-unitary basis
set parameterization allows a transparent and conceptually appealing understanding of adaptive basis sets in coupled cluster theory.
Although we have not presented numerical results for the electronic structure problem,
we expect that the theory will also prove useful in this context.

We currently have
an efficient implementation of split time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster that covers the two-mode coupling case (
T=T_{2}
and
H=H_{1}+H_{2}
),
and although this is likely sufficient in many cases, we believe that a
polynomial-scaling implementation that allows higher-order couplings
is required for broader applicability of the method. Such an implementation would involve
terms (such as mean fields) that do not occur in coupled cluster ground and response theory and
are therefore not efficiently computable in our program at the present time.
Although the techniques of Refs.
45
and
46
can likely be used,
we expect this will be a significant undertaking.
With an efficient and general implementation at hand, we believe that the attractive combination
of a compact, evolving basis and a correlated coupled cluster description offered by the
sTDMVCC approach will allow dynamics studies that were previously out of reach,
e.g. in the context of photochemistry. Extending the theory to also cover non-adiabatic dynamics
is an important topic for future research.

In this paper, we have exclusively considered real-time time-dependent wave functions, but it is of course
possible to employ an split time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster-like
Ansatz
in ground state or response theory, which are possible subjects
for future development. An split time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster-type ground state may be of interest in its own right, and
it is certainly attractive as an initial state for time-dependent simulations.
