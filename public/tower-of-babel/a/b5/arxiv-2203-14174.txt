Almost-periodic ground state of the non-self-adjoint Jacobi operator and its applications
Xing Liang, Hongze Wang, Qi Zhou

Abstract
. We study the ground states of the one-dimensional non-self-adjoint Jacobi operators in the almost periodic media by using the method of dynamical systems. We show the existence of the ground state. Particularly, in the quasi-periodic media, we show that the lower regularity of coefficients can guarantee the existence of ground states. Besides that, we give two applications: the first application is to show the existence and uniqueness of the positive steady state of the discrete Fisher-KPP type equation; the second application is to investigate the asymptotic behavior of the discrete stationary parabolic equation with large lower order terms.

1.1.
Applications

Now we give some applications of our main results.
Before that, to state our application in a broader way, we notice that our main results actually state the existence of the positive almost periodic eigenfunction of the difference operator

Indeed, under the assumption

1.1.1.
Steady states of the KPP-type discrete diffusion equation

First, we consider the positive steady state of the following KPP type equation in discrete media

This equation is usually used to model the growth and discrete migration of species in mathematical biology. Its dynamics and propagation phenomena are investigated in many papers, e.g. see

A steady state is a function

\|W-\hat{W}(0)\|_{1,\gamma}\leq\epsilon_{0},

(
1.13
) has a unique bounded steady state
u(n)=U(n\omega)
with
U\in C({\mathbb{T}},{\mathbb{R}})
and
U>0
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
, and suppose that
B_{0}\neq 0
,
W^{\prime}\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}})
,
\hat{W}^{\prime}(0)\geq 0
. There exists
\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon_{0}(A_{0},B_{0})>0
, and if

\|W^{\prime}-\hat{W}^{\prime}(0)\|_{0}<\epsilon_{0},

then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
W\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}})
which satisfies
\|W-W^{\prime}\|_{0}<\epsilon
and
\hat{W}(0)=\hat{W}^{\prime}(0)
such that
(
1.13
) has a unique bounded steady state
u(n)=U(n\omega)
with
U\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}})
and
U>0
.

Remark 1.1
.

Notice that
\hat{W}(0)\geq 0,W\not=0
is a weaker assumption than
\inf\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}W(n\omega)>0
. Actually, if a potential
W
satisfies
\hat{W}(0)=0
and
W\not=0
, then
\inf\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}W(n\omega)<0
.

Remark 1.2
.

The assumption of
\hat{W}(0)\geq 0,W\not=0
guarantees
E_{0}>0
in Proposition
1.1
, and it is a necessary condition as we could check from the case

. In fact, Theorem
1.4
(Theorem
1.5
) is also valid for a more general equation:

u_{t}=D^{*}(A_{1}(n\omega)Du)+B_{0}D^{*}u+W(n\omega)u-u^{2},\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{Z}},

if we impose on an additional assumption
\|A_{1}-\hat{A}_{1}(0)\|_{1,\gamma}\leq\epsilon_{0}
(
\|A_{1}-\hat{A}_{1}(0)\|_{0}\leq\epsilon_{0}
) with
\epsilon_{0}
depending on
\hat{A}_{1}(0),B_{0}
.

1.1.2.
Averaging problem for the discrete parabolic equation with large lower term

The averaging problem for the parabolic equations is to investigate the asymptotic behavior of the solution for the parabolic equations with rapidly oscillating coefficients (such as periodic, almost periodic, random).

Let
\Omega
be a Lipschitz domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
, and
u_{\epsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be the weak solution of the Dirichlet problem

(1.14)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \rho_{\epsilon}(x)\partial_{t}u_{\epsilon}-div(\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}(x)\nabla u_{\epsilon})&=0&&\text{ in }[0,T]\times\Omega,\\
u_{\epsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}&=\varphi&&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{ij}\},\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}(x)=\mathcal{A}(\epsilon^{-1}x),\rho_{\epsilon}(x)=\rho(\epsilon^{-1}x)
are periodic, almost periodic or random. Moreover,
\mathcal{A}(x)
satisfies the ellipticity condition

a_{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\lambda|\xi|^{2},\quad\forall\xi,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\lambda>0.

In the periodic case, the qualitative homogenization theory for (
1.14
) has been known since 1970s
[
14
]
. Indeed, as
\epsilon\to 0
, the solution
u_{\epsilon}
converges to the solution
u_{0}
of a parabolic equation with constant coefficients, and the equation with constant coefficients is called averaged (homogenized) equation.

In recent years, there have been a great many works in the quantitative homogenization theory for partial differential equations. Some progress has been made for elliptic equations and systems of the divergence type in the periodic and non-periodic settings. Besides that, the convergence rate of
u_{\epsilon}
was also obtained, e.g. see
[
10
,
46
,
47
,
69
,
63
,
39
,
44
]
.

In 1984, Kozlov
[
48
]
studied the averaging problem for a singularly perturbed parabolic equation with large lower order term:

(1.15)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (u_{\epsilon})_{t}&=\bigr(a(\frac{x}{\epsilon})(u_{\epsilon})_{x}\bigr)_{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}b(\frac{x}{\epsilon})(u_{\epsilon})_{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}c(\frac{x}{\epsilon})u_{\epsilon}&&\text{ in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}},\\
u_{\epsilon}|_{t=0}&=\psi&&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}},\end{aligned}\right.

where
a,b,c
are sufficiently smooth quasi-periodic functions, and
\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. He proved that if the frequencies of
a,b,c
satisfy some kind of arithmetic condition, and if there exist quasi-periodic functions
p,p_{*}
and
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}

that satisfy

(1.16)

(a(x)p_{x})_{x}+b(x)p_{x}+c(x)p=\lambda_{0}p,\quad(a(x)(p_{*})_{x})_{x}-(b(x)p_{*})_{x}+c(x)p_{*}=\lambda_{0}p_{*},

and their means
\langle p\rangle=\langle p^{*}\rangle=1,

then we have

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{u_{\epsilon}(t,x-lt/\epsilon)}{p(x/\epsilon-lt/\epsilon^{2})}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{0}t/\epsilon^{2}}=v_{0}(t,x),

where the convergence is uniform in any compact set in
{\mathbb{R}}
for any
t\in(0,T]
, and
v_{0}
is the solution of the averaged problem

(1.17)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (v_{0})_{t}&=\hat{a}(v_{0})_{xx}&&\text{ in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}},\\
v_{0}|_{t=0}&=\frac{1}{\langle pp_{*}\rangle}\psi(x)&&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}},\end{aligned}\right.

with the constant
\hat{a}>0
only depending on
a,b,c
.

Besides that, equation (
1.15
) was also studied in the periodic case
[
23
,
11
]
. However, as for the almost periodic case, we still don’t know anything about the averaging.

Now we consider the averaging problem for the discrete version of (
1.15
). First we introduce the difference operator on
L^{2}(\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}})
:
D_{\epsilon}u(x)=\frac{u(x+\epsilon)-u(x)}{\epsilon},

D^{*}_{\epsilon}u(x)=\frac{u(x)-u(x-\epsilon)}{\epsilon}
, and define the following function space on
\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}}
:

L^{2}(\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}}):=\biggr\{u:\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}|\ \|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}})}:=\epsilon^{2}\sum\limits_{x\in\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}}}|u(x)|^{2}<\infty\biggr\}.

Let
\omega\in\mathrm{DC}_{\infty}(\gamma,\tau)
,
0<\gamma<1
,
\tau>1
. Consider the discrete parabolic equation with almost periodic coefficients in
[0,T]\times\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
T>0
as follows:

(1.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u_{\epsilon}=D_{\epsilon}^{*}(A_{1}(\frac{\omega x}{\epsilon})D_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon})+\frac{1}{\epsilon}A_{2}(\frac{\omega x}{\epsilon})D_{\epsilon}^{*}u_{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}W(\frac{\omega x}{\epsilon})u_{\epsilon},\\
&u_{\epsilon}(0,x)=\varphi(x)\text{ \ in }\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}},\end{aligned}\right.

where
A_{1},A_{2},W\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{\infty},{\mathbb{R}})
with
\inf A_{1}(\theta),\inf(A_{1}(\theta)-A_{2}(\cdot+\omega))>0
,
\varphi\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
and satisfies
|\varphi(z)|\leq C|z|^{-k}
with
k\geq 2,\ z\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Note that in (
1.18
),
\varphi
can be restricted as a function in
\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The averaging result involves, as in quasi-periodic media
[
48
]
, the principal eigenvalue problem of the operator

(1.19)

\mathcal{L}p=D^{*}(A_{1}(n\omega)Dp)+A_{2}(n\omega)D^{*}p+W(n\omega)p=E_{0}p.

As the application of Theorem
1.3
, the existence of the almost periodic ground state of (
1.19
) is given by:

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
\omega\in DC_{\infty}(\gamma,\tau)
,
0<\gamma<1,\tau>1,\ r>0
,
A_{1},A_{2},W\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{\infty},{\mathbb{R}})
. Suppose that
\hat{A}_{2}(0)\neq 0.
Then there
exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
, depending only on
\hat{A}_{1}(0),\hat{A}_{2}(0)
, such that if

\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{*}^{\infty}}(|\hat{A_{1}}(k)|+|\hat{A_{2}}(k)|+|\hat{W}(k)|)\mathrm{e}^{r|k|_{1}}-|\hat{A}_{1}(0)|-|\hat{A}_{2}(0)|-|\hat{W}(0)|\leq\epsilon_{0},

then (
1.19
) admits a simple ground state energy and an almost-periodic ground state
p(n)=P(n\omega)
with
P\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{\infty},{\mathbb{R}}),P>0
. Moreover,
\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{*}^{\infty}}|\hat{P}(k)|\mathrm{e}^{r^{\prime}|k|_{1}}<\infty
for any
r^{\prime}\in(0,r)
.

Denote
\lfloor z\rfloor
the floor of
z
. Now we state the result for the discrete parabolic equation:

Theorem 1.6
.

Under the assumption of Proposition
1.2
, let
p
be the almost periodic ground state of (
1.19
). Then the solution
u_{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\epsilon{\mathbb{Z}}))
of the equation (
1.18
)
satisfies the relation

(1.20)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{u_{\epsilon}(t,\lfloor z/\epsilon-lt/\epsilon^{2}\rfloor\epsilon)}{p(\lfloor z/\epsilon-lt/\epsilon^{2}\rfloor)}\mathrm{e}^{-E_{0}t/\epsilon^{2}}=u_{0}(t,z),\ z\in{\mathbb{R}},

where the convergence is uniform in any compact set in
{\mathbb{R}}
for any
t\in(0,T]
, and
u_{0}
is the solution of
the averaged equation

(1.21)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (u_{0})_{t}&=\bar{a}\partial_{zz}u_{0}&&\text{ in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}},\\
u_{0}|_{t=0}&=c_{0}\varphi&&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}},\end{aligned}\right.

for
\bar{a},c_{0}>0,l\in{\mathbb{R}}
being constants which only depend on
A_{1},A_{2},W
.

Although the result is very similar to Kozlov’s, our discrete model is different from the Kozlov’s continuous setting in some ways. First, as the application of Theorem
1.3
, we discuss the averaging result for discrete parabolic equation in the almost periodic media, while in
[
48
]
, Kozlov only established the averaging result in quasi-periodic media. Secondly, Kozlov’s work can not be directly applied to the discrete model due to the loss of continuity. In fact, under the assumption of (
1.16
), Kozlov observed that
v_{\epsilon}(t,x):=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda_{0}t}{\epsilon^{2}}}\frac{u_{\epsilon}(t,x-\frac{lt}{\epsilon})}{p(\frac{x}{\epsilon}-\frac{lt}{\epsilon^{2}})}
satisfies the equation defined in
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}
,

(1.22)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (pp_{*})(x-\frac{lt}{\epsilon})(v_{\epsilon})_{t}&=\bigr(\tilde{a}(x-\frac{lt}{\epsilon})(v_{\epsilon})_{x}\bigr)_{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigr(\bar{c}-l(pp_{*})(x-\frac{lt}{\epsilon})\bigr)(v_{\epsilon})_{x},&&\\
v_{\epsilon}|_{t=0}&=\frac{\psi(x)}{p(x/\epsilon)},&&x\in{\mathbb{R}},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\tilde{a}>0,\bar{c}\equiv\mathrm{const}
which only depend on
a,b,c
, and
l\in{\mathbb{R}}
to be specified. After solving the cell problem of (
1.22
):

(1.23)

(\tilde{a}(x)u_{x})_{x}-\bar{c}u_{x}=\tilde{a}_{x}+\bar{c}-lpp_{*},

the averaging result can be obtained by the multiscale expansion method.
Note that (
1.23
) is solvable for
l=\frac{\bar{c}}{\langle pp_{*}\rangle}
and the additional arithmetic condition on frequencies.
Unfortunately, the transformation
v_{\epsilon}(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda_{0}t}{\epsilon^{2}}}\frac{u_{\epsilon}(t,x-\frac{lt}{\epsilon})}{p(\frac{x}{\epsilon}-\frac{lt}{\epsilon^{2}})}
seems to lose its effect in our discrete setting.
To overcome this difficulty, we will extend (
1.18
) continuously in
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}
by lifting it in the infinite dimensional torus and restricting it in the line
\{\omega z|z\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
, and then the resulting equation can be analyzed in Kozlov’s method.

In section 4, we prove the positive almost periodic eigenfunction is exactly the almost periodic ground state and the associated eigenvalue is the simple ground state energy. Then we discuss the fundamental properties of the ground state. This finishes the proof of Theorem

1.6

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
cocycle and Jacobi operator

Let
X
be a compact metric space,
(X,\nu,T)
be ergodic, and
A:X\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
be a continuous map.
A
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
cocycle over
(T,X)
is an action defined on
X\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

(T,A):(x,v)\in X\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto(Tx,A(x)\cdot v)\in X\times\mathbb{R}^{2}.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
A_{n}
is defined by
(T,A)^{n}=(T^{n},A_{n})
, where
A_{0}(x)=\operatorname{id}
,

\quad A_{n}(x)=\prod_{j=n-1}^{0}A(T^{j}x)=A(T^{n-1}x)\cdots A(Tx)A(x),\ n\geq 1,

and
A_{-n}(x)=A_{n}(T^{-n}x)^{-1}
.

Let
W_{1},W_{2},V
be a continuous function defined on torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
(
d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
or
d=\infty
). For
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
,
we define the non-self-adjoint Jacobi operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}):

(\mathcal{L}_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{g}u)(n)=\bigr(\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(n\omega)\bigr)u(n+1)+\bigr(\mathrm{e}^{g}+W_{2}(n\omega)\bigr)u(n-1)+V(n\omega)u(n).

Note that the eigenfunction problem of
\mathcal{L}_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{g}
can be represented as:

\begin{pmatrix}u(n+1)\\
u(n)\end{pmatrix}=S^{E,g}_{W_{1},W_{2},V}(\theta+n\omega)\begin{pmatrix}u(n)\\
u(n-1)\end{pmatrix},

where we denote

S^{E,g}_{W_{1},W_{2},V}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{E-V(\theta)}{\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(\theta)}&-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{g}+W_{2}(\theta)}{\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(\theta)}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then
(\omega,S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g})
defines a
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
cocycle on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2},
and we will call this non-self-adjoint Jacobi cocycle.

Remark 2.1
.

If
g=0
,
W_{1}=W_{2}=0
, then it reduces to the classical (self-adjoint) Schrödinger cocycle
[
55
]
.

2.2.
Maximum principle, existence and uniqueness for the Cauchy Problem

The maximum principle on the whole space can be stated as follows:

Proposition 2.1

(Maximum principle
[
19
]
)
.

Let
v\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
. Assume that for any bounded interval
I=[0,t_{0}]\subset[0,\infty)
,
u
is bounded in
I\times\mathbb{Z}
. If
u
satisfies

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{t}-D^{*}\bigr(a(n)Du\bigr)-b(n)D^{*}u+c(n)u&\geq 0&\text{ in }I\times{\mathbb{Z}},\\
u(0,n)&\geq 0&\text{ in }{\mathbb{Z}},\end{aligned}\right.

then
u\geq 0
in
I\times\mathbb{Z}.

The following Harnack inequality is a very useful technique when we study the properties of the solution of (
2.1
). We will present it here for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 2.2

(Harnack inequality
[
53
]
)
.

Assume that
u
is bounded on
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z}
and solves (
2.1
). Then for any
(t,n)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z},\ T>0
, there exists a positive constant
C=C(T)
such that

u(t,n)\leq C(T)u(t+T,m),\ m\in\{n\pm 1,n\}.

Remark 2.2
.

The proof of the Harnack inequality could be found in
[
53
]
with the initial value
u(0,n)
having a finite support. However, we should notice that the argument can be applied to (
2.1
) similarly with minor modification.

Combining the Harnack inequality with the maximum principle, we can deduce the strong maximum principle as follows:

Corollary 2.1

(Strong maximum principle
[
53
]
)
.

Under the assumption of Proposition
2.1
, either
u\equiv 0
or
u>0
in
I\times\mathbb{Z}
.

The comparison principle is a consequence of the strong maximum principle, and it is useful for us to construct the almost periodic steady state. To state it, we first define super-sub solutions of the discrete Fisher-KPP type equation:

Let
\bar{u},\underline{u}\in C(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z})
be two bounded functions.
We say that
\bar{u}
is a supersolution of

(2.2)

\bar{u}_{t}-D^{*}\bigr(a(n)D\bar{u}\bigr)-b(n)D^{*}\bar{u}-c(n)\bar{u}+\bar{u}^{2}=0

if for any given
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\bar{u}
is absolutely continuous in
t
and satisfies

\bar{u}_{t}-D^{*}(a(n)D\bar{u})-b(n)D^{*}\bar{u}-c(n)\bar{u}+\bar{u}^{2}\geq 0\text{ for }t\in(0,\infty),

and
\underline{u}
is a subsolution if for any given
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\underline{u}
is absolutely continuous in
t
and satisfies

\underline{u}_{t}-D^{*}(a(n)D\underline{u})-b(n)D^{*}\underline{u}-c(n)\underline{u}+\underline{u}^{2}\leq 0\text{ for }t\in(0,\infty).

The strong comparison principle is given by

Proposition 2.3

(Strong comparison principle)
.

Let
\overline{u}
and
\underline{u}
be a supersolution and a subsolution of (
2.2
)
repectively. If
\underline{u}(0,n)\leq\overline{u}(0,n)
in
\mathbb{Z}
, then
\underline{u}(t,n)<\overline{u}(t,n)
or
\underline{u}\equiv\overline{u}
in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z}
.

Usually, well-behaved Cauchy Problem possesses the property that it admits a unique global solution. For the convenience of our applications, we give the following existence and uniqueness theorems for discrete Fisher-KPP type equations and linear parabolic equations.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
62
]
)
.

For any initial value
\varphi\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
, there exists a unique
u\in C([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z})
with
u(t,\cdot)\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
for any
t\in[0,\infty)
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{t}-D^{*}(a(n)Du)-b(n)D^{*}u&-c(n)u+u^{2}=0&&\text{ in }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z},\\
u(0,n)&=\varphi(n)&&\text{ in }{\mathbb{Z}}.\end{aligned}\right.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
62
]
)
.

For any initial value
\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, there exists a unique
u\in C([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z})
with
u[t,\cdot)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
for any
t\in(0,\infty)
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{t}-D^{*}(a(n)Du)-b(n)D^{*}u&-c(n)u=0&&\text{ in }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z},\\
u(0,n)&=\varphi(n)&&\text{ in }{\mathbb{Z}}.\end{aligned}\right.

3.
Construction of positive almost-periodic eigenfunction

In this section, we develop a new method to construct the positive almost periodic eigenfunction of the non-self-adjoint operator (
1.2
). The method is from the side of dynamical systems.

3.1.
Criterion of the existence of positive almost periodic eigenfunction

First we give a criterion, which says that if the non-self-adjoint Jacobi cocycle
(\omega,S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g})
is reducible to a constant cocycle with eigenvalue 1, and the conjugacy has special form, then the corresponding non-self-adjoint Jacobi operator admits a positive almost-periodic eigenfunction. Recall that a
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
cocycle
(\omega,A)
is said to be reducible if there exist
B(\cdot)\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d},\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
and constant
\tilde{A}\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
such that

B(\theta+\omega)^{-1}A(\theta)B(\theta)=\tilde{A}.

Then we have the following:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
. Suppose that there exists
B\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d},\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
such that

(3.1)

B(\theta+\omega)^{-1}S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g}(\theta)B(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&s\end{pmatrix},

where
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover,
B
takes the form

B(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}B_{11}(\theta)&B_{12}(\theta)\\
B_{21}(\theta)&B_{22}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}:=P\mathrm{e}^{Y(\theta)}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{y_{1}(\theta)}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{y_{2}(\theta)}\end{pmatrix}\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d},\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R}))

where
P\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, and

(3.2)

\|Y\|_{0}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}\leq\frac{|P_{11}|}{4(|P_{11}|+|P_{12}|)}.

Then
u(n)=U(n\omega)
is an almost periodic solution of

(3.3)

\bigr(\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(n\omega)\bigr)u(n+1)+\bigr(\mathrm{e}^{g}+W_{2}(n\omega)\bigr)u(n-1)+V(n\omega)u(n)=Eu(n),

where
U\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}),\ U>0.

Remark 3.1
.

Note here we don’t need any control on
y_{i}(\theta)
, that’s the main reason why we obtain semi-local results (c.f. Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.3
).

Remark 3.2
.

One should compare Proposition
3.1
with the criterion in the self-adjoint case (Lemma 5.1 of
[
55
]
), where the conjugacy
B
is only allowed to be close to the identity.

Proof.

Denote
B(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}B_{11}(\theta)&B_{12}(\theta)\\
B_{21}(\theta)&B_{22}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}
, then (
3.1
) gives us

\displaystyle\frac{E-V(\theta)}{\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(\theta)}B_{11}(\theta)-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{g}+W_{2}(\theta)}{\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(\theta)}B_{21}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{11}(\theta+\omega),

\displaystyle B_{11}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{21}(\theta+\omega),

that is

\bigr(\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(\theta)\bigr)B_{11}(\theta+\omega)+\bigr(\mathrm{e}^{g}+W_{2}(\theta)\bigr)B_{11}(\theta-\omega)+V(\theta)B_{11}(\theta)=EB_{11}(\theta).

Hence
u(n)=B_{11}(n\omega)
is the almost periodic solution of (
3.3
).

Denote that
\mathrm{e}^{Y(\theta)}=\begin{pmatrix}Y_{11}(\theta)&Y_{12}(\theta)\\
Y_{21}(\theta)&Y_{22}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}.
Direct computation shows that

B_{11}(\theta)=\mathrm{e}^{y_{1}(\theta)}\bigr(P_{11}Y_{11}(\theta)+P_{12}Y_{21}(\theta)\bigr).

Without loss of generality, assume
P_{11}>0
(otherwise consider
-B_{11}
instead). By our assumption,

\|Y_{11}-1\|_{0}\leq 2\tilde{\epsilon},\|Y_{12}\|_{0}\leq 2\|Y\|_{0}\leq 2\tilde{\epsilon},

thus by (
3.2
), we have

B_{11}(\theta)\geq\frac{P_{11}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\min_{\theta}y_{1}(\theta)}>0.

This yields that

B_{11}(n\omega)
is a positive almost periodic solution.
∎

3.2.
Reducibility of the non-self-adjoint Jacobi cocycle

If we denote

A_{g}(E):=\begin{pmatrix}E\mathrm{e}^{g}&-\mathrm{e}^{2g}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\tilde{F}_{E,g}(\theta):=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2g}V(\theta)+E\mathrm{e}^{-g}W_{1}(\theta)}{\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(\theta)}&\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2g}W_{2}(\theta)-W_{1}(\theta)}{\mathrm{e}^{-g}+W_{1}(\theta)}+1\end{pmatrix},

then
S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g}(\theta)=A_{g}(E)\tilde{F}_{E,g}(\theta)
. Then basic observation is that if
W_{1},W_{2},V
are small enough, then
\tilde{F}_{E,g}(\theta)
is close to identity, i.e.
S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g}
is close to constant. Hence one can try to use Newton’s iteration to conjugate the cocycle
(\omega,S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g})
to constant. We will distinguish the proof into three steps:

3.2.1.
Diagonalization using Implicit Function Theorem

The first step is to diagonalize the cocycle
(\omega,S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g})
using Implicit Function Theorem. There are two main observations of this step. The first observation (indeed the key observation of the whole proof) is that in the non-self-adjoint case
g\neq 0
(without loss of generality, assume
g>0
), the constant cocycle
(\omega,A_{g}(E))
is strongly hyperbolic. To see this, since we are looking for the almost periodic ground state of the operator (
1.6
), the energy should lie in the upper bound of the spectrum. Thus without loss of generality, we just consider the case

(3.4)

E\geq 2+\delta

for
\delta>0
to be specified.
Notice that the characteristic equation of
A_{g}(E)
reads as

(3.5)

\lambda^{2}-E\mathrm{e}^{g}\lambda+\mathrm{e}^{2g}=0.

If we denote its eigenvalues as
\lambda(E)
and
\mu(E)
, direct computation shows that

\lambda(E)=\mathrm{e}^{g}\frac{E-\sqrt{E^{2}-4}}{2},\qquad\mu(E)=\mathrm{e}^{g}\frac{E+\sqrt{E^{2}-4}}{2}.

Then under the assumption (
3.4
),
\lambda(E)
,
\mu(E)
are both real, and furthermore

\mu(E)-\lambda(E)\geq e^{g}\delta.

The second observation is that as a consequence of strong hyperbolicity of
(\omega,A_{g}(E))
, and if
V,W_{1},W_{2}
are small enough, then one can diagonalize
(\omega,S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g})
. Just note in this step, we don’t need to assume any arithmetic condition on
\omega
, since one will not meet small divisor problem.

To prove this, we need a non-resonance cancelation lemma which will be the basis of our proof. Now we first introduce some settings of it.
Denote
\mathcal{B}(*)
as an
*
-valued Banach space with the norm
|\cdot|_{\mathcal{B}}
, where
*
will usually denote
{\mathbb{R}}
,
\mathrm{gl}(2,\mathbb{R})
or
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, we assume that
\mathcal{B}(*)
admits a crucial property:

Definition 3.1
.

A Banach space
\mathcal{B}
admits condition
(H)
if
\|fg\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{B}}
for any
f,g\in\mathcal{B}.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be any closed interval in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Then we denote the function space

\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}(*):=\{f\in C(\mathcal{I}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d},*)|f(\xi,\cdot)\in\mathcal{B}(*)\text{ for any given }\xi\in\mathcal{I}\}

with the norm
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}:=\sup\limits_{\xi\in\mathcal{I}}|f(\xi)|_{\mathcal{B}}
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
. For simplicity of the notations, in the following we just denote
\mathcal{B}(\mathrm{gl}(2,\mathbb{R}))
as
\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{gl}(2,\mathbb{R}))
as
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}
.

Assume that for any given
\eta>0,\omega\in\mathbb{T}^{d},A\in C(\mathcal{I},\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
, and
we have a decomposition of the Banach space
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}
into non-resonant spaces and resonant spaces, i.e.

\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}=\mathscr{B}^{nre}_{\mathcal{I}}(\eta)\oplus\mathscr{B}^{re}_{\mathcal{I}}(\eta)
. Here
\mathscr{B}^{nre}_{\mathcal{I}}(\eta)
is defined in the following way: for any
Y\in\mathscr{B}^{nre}_{\mathcal{I}}(\eta),
we have

\displaystyle A(\xi)^{-1}Y(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi)\in\mathscr{B}^{nre}_{\mathcal{I}}(\eta)\text{ for any }\xi\in\mathcal{I},

\displaystyle|A(\xi)^{-1}Y(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi)-Y(\xi,\theta)|_{\mathcal{B}}\geq\eta|Y(\xi,\theta)|_{\mathcal{B}}\text{ for any }\xi\in\mathcal{I}.

For any
f\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}},
denote
\widehat{f(\xi)}(k)
the Fourier coefficient of
f(\xi,\cdot)
with respect to the second variable
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}.

Once we have this, then the following holds:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a closed interval in
{\mathbb{R}}
, and
assume that
A\in C\bigr(\mathcal{I},\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})\bigr)
.
Then there exists an absolute constant
c_{1}>0
such that for any
F\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}
with
\|F\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\epsilon\leq\frac{(\inf|\det A|)^{2}\min\{\eta^{2},1\}}{c_{1}(\|A\|^{3}+1)^{2}},
there exist
Y\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}
and
F^{re}\in\mathscr{B}^{re}_{\mathcal{I}}(\eta)
such that

e^{-Y(\xi,\theta+\omega)}A(\xi)e^{F(\xi,\theta)}e^{Y(\xi,\theta)}=A(\xi)e^{F^{re}(\xi,\theta)},

where
\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\|F^{re}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq 2\epsilon.

Remark 3.3
.

This lemma generalizes the previous result of
[
18
]
where
A
is a constant
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
matrix.
The proof is based on the quantitative Implicit Function Theorem, and it will be given in Appendix A.

In the following, we will always fix
\mathcal{I}:=[2+\frac{1}{9}\min\{g^{2},1\},2(\mathrm{e}^{g}+\mathrm{e}^{-g})]
. The key step for us is the following:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}\cup\{\infty\},

g>0
.
Then there exists an absolute constant
C_{0}>0
such that if

\|W_{1}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}+\|W_{2}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}+\|V\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\epsilon\leq C_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-12g}g^{6},

then there exist
Y,f_{i}\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}
such that

\mathrm{e}^{-Y(E,\theta+\omega)}P(E)^{-1}S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g}(\theta)P(E)\mathrm{e}^{Y(E,\theta)}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda(E)e^{f_{1}(E,\theta)}&0\\
0&\mu(E)e^{f_{2}(E,\theta)}\end{pmatrix},

where
P(E)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda(E)&\mu(E)\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}
and

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq\frac{\lambda(E)}{4\lambda(E)+4\mu(E)},\ \|f_{i}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that for any
E\in\mathcal{I}
, one has

(3.7)

\displaystyle|\lambda(E)/\mu(E)-1|

\displaystyle=\biggr|\frac{E+\sqrt{E^{2}-4}}{E-\sqrt{E^{2}-4}}-1\biggr|\geq\frac{\min\{g^{2},1\}}{9},

\displaystyle|\mu(E)/\lambda(E)-1|

\displaystyle=\biggr|\frac{E-\sqrt{E^{2}-4}}{E+\sqrt{E^{2}-4}}-1\biggr|\geq\frac{\min\{g^{2},1\}}{27},

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(E)}{\lambda(E)+\mu(E)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{E-\sqrt{E^{2}-4}}{2E}\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2g}}{16}.

Denote
P(E):=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda(E)&\mu(E)\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, then we can diagonalize
A_{g}(E)
as

A^{\prime}_{g}(E):=P(E)^{-1}A_{g}(E)P(E)=diag(\lambda(E),\mu(E)).

On the other hand, note that
\|\tilde{F}_{E,g}(\theta)-\operatorname{id}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{g}\epsilon
. Thus by Implicit Function Theorem, there exists

F_{g}\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}
, such that
\tilde{F}_{E,g}(\theta)=\mathrm{e}^{F_{g}(E,\theta)}
with the estimate
\|F_{g}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{g}\epsilon
.
Denote

F^{\prime}_{g}(E,\theta)=P(E)^{-1}F_{g}(E,\theta)P(E),

then there holds the estimate

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}_{g}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\limits_{E\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{2\|P(E)\|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{g}\epsilon}{|\det P(E)|}\leq\sup\limits_{E\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{2(E+2)^{2}\epsilon}{\sqrt{E^{2}-4}}\leq C^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{2g}\max\{\frac{1}{g^{2}},1\}\epsilon,

where
C^{\prime}>0
is an absolute constant.

Now we are ready to apply the non-resonance cancelation lemma (Lemma
3.1
) to the cocycle
(\omega,A^{\prime}_{g}(E)e^{F^{\prime}_{g}(E,\theta)}).

Define

(3.9)

\Lambda=\biggr\{f\in C(\mathcal{I}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\mathrm{gl}(2,\mathbb{R}))|f=\begin{pmatrix}0&f_{1}\\
f_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}\biggr\}.

Then we prove that for any
Y\in\Lambda
, the operator

Y\rightarrow A^{\prime}_{g}(E)^{-1}Y(E,\theta+\omega)A^{\prime}_{g}(E)-Y(E,\theta)

has a bounded inverse. To prove this, we only need to consider the equation

(3.10)

A^{\prime}_{g}(E)^{-1}Y(E,\theta+\omega)A^{\prime}_{g}(E)-Y(E,\theta)=G(\theta).

Write that

Y(E,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}0&Y_{1}(E,\theta)\\
Y_{2}(E,\theta)&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad G(E,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}0&G_{1}(E,\theta)\\
G_{2}(E,\theta)&0\end{pmatrix}.

Comparing the Fourier coefficients of the equation (
3.10
), we arrive at

\left\{\begin{aligned} \widehat{Y_{1}(E)}(k)&=\frac{\widehat{G_{1}(E)}(k)}{\frac{\mu}{\lambda}(E)e^{\mathrm{i}\langle k,\omega\rangle}-1},\\
\widehat{Y_{2}(E)}(k)&=\frac{\widehat{G_{2}(E)}(k)}{\frac{\lambda}{\mu}(E)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\langle k,\omega\rangle}-1}.\end{aligned}\right.

Notice (
3.7
) implies that

\displaystyle|\lambda(E)/\mu(E)-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\langle k,\omega\rangle}|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle|1-\lambda(E)/\mu(E)|\geq\frac{\min\{g^{2},1\}}{27},

\displaystyle|\mu(E)/\lambda(E)-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\langle k,\omega\rangle}|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle|\mu(E)/\lambda(E)-1|\geq\frac{\min\{g^{2},1\}}{27}.

Thus for any
Y\in\Lambda
, we have

(3.11)

\|A^{\prime}_{g}(E)^{-1}Y(E,\theta+\omega)A^{\prime}_{g}(E)-Y(E,\theta)\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\geq\frac{\min\{g^{2},1\}}{27}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}},

which just means
\Lambda\subset\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}^{nre}(\frac{\min\{g^{2},1\}}{27})
.

Just note
\det A^{\prime}_{g}(E)=\lambda(E)\mu(E)=e^{2g}
, and then

C^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{3g}\epsilon\max\{\frac{1}{g^{2}},1\}\leq\frac{e^{4g}\min\{g^{4},1\}}{c_{2}\sup\limits_{E\in\mathcal{I}}|\mu(E)|^{6}}.,

where
c_{2}
is an absolute constant.
It follows from Lemma
3.1
that there exist
Y,f_{i}\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}}
such that

\displaystyle e^{-Y(E)(\theta+\omega)}P(E)^{-1}S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g}(\theta)P(E)\mathrm{e}^{Y(E)(\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-Y(E)(\theta+\omega)}A^{\prime}_{g}(E)e^{F^{\prime}_{g}(E,\theta)}\mathrm{e}^{Y(E)(\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda(E)e^{f_{1}(E,\theta)}&0\\
0&\mu(E)e^{f_{2}(E,\theta)}\end{pmatrix},

Moreover, there hold the estimates

\displaystyle\|f_{i}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2C^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{2g}\max\{\frac{1}{g^{2}},1\}\epsilon\leq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{\prime\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{g}\max\{\frac{1}{g},1\}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}.

∎

3.2.2.
Solving the cohomological equation

Note Proposition
3.2
says that one can diagonalize the cocycle, thus to make the cocycle reducible, one only need to solve the typical cohomological equation

(3.12)

y(\theta+\omega)-y(\theta)=f(\theta)-\widehat{f}(0).

To solve this equation, there arises the small divisor problem, and then one has to assume some regularity condition on
f
, and some arithmetic property on
\omega
. It is well-known that if
\omega
is Diophantine, say
\omega\in DC_{d}(\gamma,\tau),
where
\tau>d+1
,
f\in C^{k}({\mathbb{T}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}})
then (
3.12
) has a solution if
k>\tau+1
.

Finitely differentiable function

However, if
f
is a function with lower regularity, this result can be improved in the following:

Proposition 3.3

(
[
38
]
)
.

Assume that
f\in C^{k,\gamma}({\mathbb{T}})
for some
k\geq 1
and
0<\gamma<1.

Then for any
\omega\in\mathcal{P}
, the equation (
3.12
) possesses a solution
y\in C^{k-1,\gamma^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}})
for any
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
.

Denote
\mathcal{B}_{k,\gamma}:=C^{k,\gamma}({\mathbb{T}},\mathrm{gl}(2,\mathbb{R}))
, and the function space

\mathscr{B}_{k,\gamma,\mathcal{I}}:=\{f\in C(\mathcal{I}\times{\mathbb{T}},\mathrm{gl}(2,\mathbb{R}))|f(E,\cdot)\in\mathcal{B}_{k,\gamma}\text{ for any given }E\in\mathcal{I}\}

which is equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{k,\gamma,\mathcal{I}}:=\sup\limits_{E\in\mathcal{I}}\|f(E)\|_{k,\gamma},
and it is straightforward to check that
C^{1,\gamma}({\mathbb{T}},\mathrm{gl}(2,\mathbb{R}))
admits condition
(H).

Then as a result of Proposition
3.2
and Proposition
3.3
, we have the following:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
g>0,

\omega\in\mathcal{P},\gamma\in(0,1)
, and suppose that
W_{1},W_{2},V\in C^{1,\gamma}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathbb{R}})
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C_{0}>0
such that if

\|W_{1}\|_{1,\gamma}+\|W_{2}\|_{1,\gamma}+\|V\|_{1,\gamma}\leq\epsilon\leq C_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-12g}g^{6},

then for any

there exists

such that

where

takes the form

\|Y\|_{0,\gamma,\mathcal{I}}\leq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq\frac{\lambda(E)}{4(\lambda(E)+\mu(E))}.

Proof.

Denote
P(E)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda(E)&\mu(E)\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}
.
By Proposition
3.2
, there exist

such that

\mathrm{e}^{-Y(E,\theta+\omega)}P(E)^{-1}S_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{E,g}(\theta)P(E)\mathrm{e}^{Y(E,\theta)}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda(E)e^{f_{1}(E,\theta)}&0\\

By Proposition
3.3
, the equation

(3.13)

\displaystyle y_{i}(E,\theta+\omega)-y_{i}(E,\theta)=f_{i}(E,\theta)-\widehat{f_{i}(E)}(0),i=1,2.

has a solution

.

then the result follows.
∎

Continuous function without any frequency restriction

If we don’t add any arithmetic assumption on the frequency
\omega
, or if we just assume
f
to be a continuous function, then (
3.12
) might have no continuous solution, even no

solution
[

]
. However, if
f
is trigonometric polynomial, then (
3.12
) always have a solution

. Indeed, the price is the norm of

might lose uniform control.
This motivates us to prove that if we perturb the potential
V
, then the resulting operator has a positive almost periodic eigenfunction. The key step is Lemma
3.3
, which says a perturbation of the (non-self-adjoint) Jacobi cocycle can be converted into a (non-self-adjoint) Jacobi cocycle.

Define
A^{V,W}=\begin{pmatrix}V&W\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, and denote the function space

\mathscr{B}_{0,\mathcal{I}}(*)=C(\mathcal{I}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d},*)

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
be rationally independent, and let
\tilde{V},\frac{1}{\tilde{V}},W,\frac{1}{W}\in\mathscr{B}_{0,\mathcal{I}}({\mathbb{R}})
. There exists
\epsilon>0
such that if

satisfies

satisfying

\mathrm{e}^{-Y(\theta+\omega)}A(\theta)\mathrm{e}^{Y(\theta)}=A^{V^{\prime},W}.

Moreover,
\|Y\|_{0}\leq C\epsilon
, and
\|\tilde{V}-V^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq C\epsilon
, where
C
depends on
\tilde{V},W
.

Remark 3.4
.

If
W\equiv-1,
i.e.,

is a Schrödinger cocycle, it was first proved in analytic topology by Avila-Jitomirskaya (Lemma 2.3 in
[

Proof.

Denote
A_{0}=A^{\tilde{V},W}
, and
A=A_{0}\mathrm{e}^{X_{0}}
,

f_{3}&f_{4}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathscr{B}_{0,\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{gl}(2,\mathbb{R}))

Then

and
\mathrm{e}^{y(E,\theta+\omega)}A(E,\theta)\mathrm{e}^{-y(E,\theta)}
is for the form

where

,

∎

4.
Ground state of the non-self-adjoint Jacobi operator

Define the ground state energy set of a linear bounded operator
\mathcal{L}

(4.1)

E_{\max}(\mathcal{L}):=\{E\in\Sigma(\mathcal{L})|\Re E^{\prime}\leq\Re E,\text{ for any }E^{\prime}\in\Sigma(\mathcal{L})\},

where
\Sigma(\mathcal{L})
denotes the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
.
Since
\mathcal{L}
is a linear bounded operator, it is well known that
\Sigma(\mathcal{L})
is a compact, non-empty set, and for any
E\in\Sigma(\mathcal{L})
,
|E|\leq\|\mathcal{L}\|
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator norm. Hence
E_{\max}(\mathcal{L})
is not an empty set.

Last section, we have constructed a positive almost-periodic eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{g}
with energy
E_{0}
. In this section, we will prove actually
E_{0}\in E_{\max}(\mathcal{L}_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{g})
, and further show it is simple. It is direct to see the Jacobi operator
\mathcal{L}_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{g}
can be rewritten as
the discrete elliptic operator:

(4.2)

\mathcal{L}u=D^{*}\bigr(a(n)Du\bigr)+b(n)D^{*}u+c(n)u.

where

\inf\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a(n)>0,\inf\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(a(n-1)-b(n))>0.

As we have explained in the introduction, the results for (
4.2
) will be given first for applications. We should notice the results for (
4.2
) still hold for
\mathcal{L}_{W_{1},W_{2},V}^{g}
.

4.1.
Ground state of the discrete elliptic operator

We introduce the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{L}
in
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
:

\mathcal{L}^{*}u=D^{*}(a(n)Du)-D(b(n)u)+c(n)u.

Now we will show if there exist
E_{0},E_{0}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and two almost-periodic functions
p,p_{*}
with
\inf p,\inf p_{*}>0
satisfying

(4.3)

\mathcal{L}p=E_{0}p,\ \mathcal{L}^{*}p_{*}=E^{\prime}_{0}p_{*},

then
E_{0}=E_{0}^{\prime}\in E_{\max}(\mathcal{L})
, and thus
E_{0}
is the ground state energy.

The first observation is the following:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p
and
p_{*}
be two bounded solutions of
\mathcal{L}p=E_{0}p
and
\mathcal{L}^{*}p_{*}=E^{\prime}_{0}p_{*}
. If
\inf p,\inf p_{*}>0
, then
E_{0}=E_{0}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{\chi}_{k}
be the characteristic function such that

\chi_{k}(n)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&\text{if }|n|\leq k\\
&0&\text{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

Denote
\tilde{b}(n)=a(n-1)-b(n)
. Since for any
k\in{\mathbb{Z}},\chi_{k}p,\chi_{k}p_{*}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
, one has

(4.4)

\langle\chi_{k}\mathcal{L}(\chi_{k}p),\chi_{k}p_{*}\rangle=\sum\limits_{n=-k}^{k}E_{0}(pp_{*})(n)-(ap_{*})(k)p(k+1)-(\tilde{b}p_{*})(-k)p(-k-1),

and similarly,

(4.5)

\langle\chi_{k}p,\chi_{k}\mathcal{L}^{*}(\chi_{k}p_{*})\rangle=\sum\limits_{n=-k}^{k}E^{\prime}_{0}(pp_{*})(n)-(ap_{*})(-k-1)p(-k)-(\tilde{b}p_{*})(k+1)p(k).

Note that
\langle\chi_{k}\mathcal{L}(\chi_{k}p),\chi_{k}p_{*}\rangle=\langle\chi_{k}p,\chi_{k}\mathcal{L}^{*}(\chi_{k}p_{*})\rangle
. Subtracting (
4.5
) from (
4.4
), one has

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=-k}^{k}(E_{0}-E_{0}^{\prime})(pp_{*})(n)=

\displaystyle(ap_{*})(k)p(k+1)+(\tilde{b}p_{*})(-k)p(-k-1)

\displaystyle-(\tilde{b}p_{*})(k+1)p(k)-(ap_{*})(-k-1)p(-k).

Since
p,p_{*}
are two bounded solutions with positive infimums, the right-hand side is bounded. However, the left-hand side tends to infinite if
E_{0}\neq E_{0}^{\prime}.
Then the result follows.
∎

This lemma also shows such
E_{0}
, with the property that the corresponding eigenfunction is positive almost periodic, is unique.
Once we have this, we shall show a crucial observation that is needed in the rest of the paper.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
p,p_{*}\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}})
be two bounded solutions of
\mathcal{L}p=E_{0}p,

\mathcal{L}^{*}p_{*}=E_{0}p_{*}
. If
\inf p,\inf p_{*}>0,
then we have

p_{*}(\mathcal{L}-E_{0})(pu)=D^{*}\bigr(\tilde{a}(n)Du\bigr)+\bar{c}(D^{*}u+Du),

where
\bar{c}\equiv\mathrm{const}
and
\inf\tilde{a}>|\bar{c}|
.

Proof.

Denote
c^{\prime}=c-E_{0}
.
For any
u\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}),
since
\mathcal{L}p=E_{0}p
, one has

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u

\displaystyle:=p_{*}(\mathcal{L}-E_{0})(pu)

\displaystyle=p_{*}(n)D^{*}\biggr(a(n)D\bigr(p(n)u\bigr)\biggr)+(bp_{*})(n)D^{*}\bigr(p(n)u\bigr)+(p_{*}c^{\prime}p)(n)u

\displaystyle=p_{*}(n)D^{*}\biggr(a(n)D\bigr(p(n)u\bigr)\biggr)+(bp_{*})(n)D^{*}\bigr(p(n)u\bigr)

\displaystyle-p_{*}(n)\biggr[\biggr(D^{*}\bigr(a(n)Dp(n)\bigr)\biggr)+b(n)D^{*}p(n)\biggr]u

\displaystyle=D^{*}\bigr(a(n)p_{*}(n)p(n+1)Du\bigr)+\tilde{c}(n)D^{*}u=(I),

where
\tilde{c}(n)=(ap_{*})(n-1)p(n)-(ap)(n-1)p_{*}(n)+(bp_{*})(n)p(n-1)
.

Meanwhile, let
\tilde{b}(n)=a(n-1)-b(n)
. One can rewrite that

(\mathcal{L}-E_{0})u=D^{*}(a(n)Du)+b(n)D^{*}u+c^{\prime}(n)u=D(\tilde{b}(n)D^{*}u)+b(n+1)Du+c^{\prime}(n)u.

Then we have,

\displaystyle(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u)=

\displaystyle p_{*}(n)D\biggr(\tilde{b}(n)D^{*}\bigr(p(n)u\bigr)\biggr)+b(n+1)p_{*}(n)D\bigr(p(n)u\bigr)+(c^{\prime}pp_{*})(n)u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{*}(n)D\biggr(\tilde{b}(n)D^{*}\bigr(p(n)u\bigr)\biggr)+b(n+1)p_{*}(n)D\bigr(p(n)u\bigr)

\displaystyle-p_{*}(n)\biggr[D\bigr(\tilde{b}(n)D^{*}p(n)\bigr)+b(n+1)Dp(n)\biggr]u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\bigr(\tilde{b}(n)p_{*}(n)p(n-1)D^{*}u\bigr)+\tilde{c}(n+1)Du=(II).

It is straightforward to check from
\mathcal{L}pp_{*}=\mathcal{L}^{*}p_{*}p=E_{0}pp_{*}
that
\tilde{c}(n)=\tilde{c}(n+1).

Hence

(4.6)

\tilde{c}(n)=a(n-1)p_{*}(n-1)p(n)-\tilde{b}(n)p(n-1)p_{*}(n)\equiv const:=2\bar{c}.

Adding
(I)
and
(II)
, we have

\displaystyle 2\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u

\displaystyle=D\bigr(\tilde{b}(n)p(n-1)p_{*}(n)D^{*}u\bigr)+2\bar{c}Du+D^{*}\bigr(a(n)p_{*}(n)p(n+1)Du\bigr)+2\bar{c}D^{*}u,

\displaystyle=2D^{*}\bigr(\tilde{a}(n)Du\bigr)+2\bar{c}\bigr(Du+D^{*}u\bigr),

where
\tilde{a}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\bigr[(ap)(n)p_{*}(n+1)-(bp_{*})(n+1)p(n)+(ap_{*})(n)p(n+1)\bigr].
One can check that

.
Then we finish the proof.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
p,p_{*}\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}})
be two bounded solutions of
\mathcal{L}p=E_{0}p,

\mathcal{L}^{*}p_{*}=E_{0}p_{*}
. If
\inf p,\inf p_{*}>0,
then
E_{0}\in E_{\max}
.

Proof.

For
\Re(E-E_{0})>0
, consider the equation

(4.7)

(E_{0}-\mathcal{L})v=f,\ f\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}).

Since
\mathcal{L}p=E_{0}p,\mathcal{L}^{*}p_{*}=E_{0}p_{*}
, it follows from Lemma
4.2
that one can transform
\mathcal{L}-E_{0}
into

p_{*}(n)(E_{0}-\mathcal{L})\bigr(p(n)v\bigr)=-D^{*}(\tilde{a}(n)Dv)-\bar{c}(Dv+D^{*}v),

where
\inf\tilde{a}>0
,
\bar{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Denote that
v=u/p\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. For
\Re(E-E_{0})>0
, equation (
4.7
) becomes

(4.8)

-D^{*}(\tilde{a}(n)Dv)-\bar{c}\bigr(D^{*}v+Dv\bigr)+(E-E_{0})(pp_{*})(n)v=(p_{*}f)(n).

Since
pp_{*}\geq\inf p\inf p_{*}:=\delta
, multiplying both sides of (
4.8
) by
v
, and taking their sums, one could notice from its real part that for any

\displaystyle\delta\Re(E-E_{0})\|v\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\tilde{a}(n)|Dv(n)|^{2}+\Re(E-E_{0})\langle pp_{*}v,v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|p_{*}f\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}}{\delta\Re(E-E_{0})}+\frac{\delta\Re(E-E_{0})}{2}\|v\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}\|f\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}}{\Re(E-E_{0})}+\frac{\delta\Re(E-E_{0})}{2}\|v\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})},

where
C_{1}
depends only on
p,p_{*}
.
Hence

\|u\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}\leq C_{2}\|v\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{\|f\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}}{\Re(E-E_{0})},

where
C_{2}
only depends only on
p,p_{*}
. Then (

) can be solved by Lax-Milgram theorem, and this yields that
\mathcal{L}-E
is invertible for

lies in the resolvent set of
\mathcal{L}
if

belongs to the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}

Proposition 4.2
.

5.
Steady state of the discrete Fisher-KPP equation

As our first application, we establish the existence of the positive almost periodic steady state of the Fisher-KPP type equation in discrete media

(5.1)

u_{t}=D^{*}(a(n)Du)+b(n)D^{*}u+c(n)u-u^{2}=0,\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}.

where
a,b,c
are almost periodic and
\inf\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a(n),\inf\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigr(a(n)-b(n-1)\bigr)>0,

\langle c\rangle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c(i)\geq 0
and
c\not=0
.
Here, we will always assume that the linearized operator

(5.2)

\mathcal{L}p=D^{*}(a(n)Dp)+b(n)D^{*}p+c(n)p=E_{0}p

E_{0}>0
and a bounded function

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
p
be a bounded solution of (
5.2
) with positive infimum. Then (
5.1
) has a unique steady state with positive infimum.

Proof.

Now we prove the result in the following steps:

Step 1: First we claim there exists a solution
u
of the equation (
5.1
). Moreover,
u(t,n)
is nondecreasing in
t
.

Since
-c(n)M+M^{2}>0
for
M>\sup c
, then
\overline{u}:=M
is a supersolution of (
5.1
).
Denote
\underline{u}:=\epsilon p
with
\epsilon
to be chosen.
Then for
\epsilon<\frac{\min\{M,E_{0}\}}{\sup p}

\underline{u}_{t}-\mathcal{L}\underline{u}+\underline{u}^{2}=-\epsilon(\mathcal{L}p)+\epsilon^{2}p^{2}=-\epsilon E_{0}p+\epsilon^{2}p^{2}<0.

This yields that
\underline{u}
is a subsolution of (
5.1
). Moreover,
\underline{u}=\epsilon p<M=\bar{u}.

Define the sequence
\{u_{i}\}
as follows:
u_{i}
is the solution of (
5.1
) for
t>-i
with initial condition
u_{i}(-i,n)=\overline{u}(-i,n)
, and the existence and uniqueness of
u_{i}
is due to Theorem
2.1
. By Proposition
2.3

\forall t>-i,\ n\in\mathbb{Z},\ \underline{u}(t,n)\leq u_{i}(t,n)\leq\overline{u}(t,n).

Thus, for

j<i

\overline{u}

u_{j}(-j,n)=\overline{u}(-j,n)\geq\overline{u}(-j-h,n)\geq u_{i}(-j-h,n).

Note that
u_{i}(\cdot-h,\cdot)

5.1

2.3

\forall j<i,\ 0<h<1,t>-j,\ \ u_{j}(t,n)\geq u_{i}(t-h,n).

Now by the arguments before, we can prove

\underline{u}\leq u\leq\overline{u}
of (
5.1

u(t,n)\geq u(t-h,n)
for all

is nondecreasing in

Step 2: Then we get the steady state of the equation (
5.1

with

and
u
is nondecreasing in

\mathcal{L}u_{0}-u_{0}^{2}=D^{*}(a(n)Du_{0})+b(n)D^{*}u_{0}+c(n)u_{0}-u_{0}^{2}=0.

Moreover,
\epsilon p\leq u_{0}\leq M.

Assume by contradiction that
v_{0}
is a steady state with positive infimum which is different from
u_{0}
. Without loss of generality we assume that
u_{0}(0)>v_{0}(0)
. Note that
\inf v_{0}>0
and
u_{0},v_{0}
are both bounded, then as we have explained in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, there exists
\epsilon\in(0,1)

such that

By the direct calculation,

\displaystyle w_{t}-\mathcal{L}w+w^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\mathrm{e}^{\delta t}(\delta u_{0}-\mathcal{L}u_{0}+\epsilon\mathrm{e}^{\delta t}u_{0}^{2})\leq\epsilon\mathrm{e}^{\delta t}u_{0}(\delta+\epsilon\mathrm{e}^{\delta t}u_{0}-u_{0}).

This implies that

and thus

in

. This contradicts with the assumption

such that
a(\cdot+n_{i}),b(\cdot+n_{i}),c(\cdot+n_{i})
converge uniformly, so does
u_{0}(\cdot+n_{i})
.

Proof.

Now we divide the proof in the following steps:

Step 1: First we claim that there exists a unique bounded solution with positive infimum of the following equation

(5.3)

D^{*}(\tilde{a}(n)Du)+\tilde{b}(n)D^{*}u+\tilde{c}(n)u-u^{2}=0.

where
\tilde{a}(n)=\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}a(n+n_{i}),\tilde{b}(n)=\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}b(n+n_{i}),\tilde{c}(n)=\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}c(n+n_{i})
for some sequence
\{n_{i}\}_{i}
.

From
\mathcal{L}p=E_{0}p
, we have
|p(n)|\leq C(n)
, and then
p(\cdot+n_{i})
converges locally uniformly to a positive bounded solution
\tilde{p}
of

D^{*}(\tilde{a}(n)Dp)+\tilde{b}(n)D^{*}p+\tilde{c}(n)p=E_{0}p.

Meanwhile,
\inf p(\cdot+n_{i})\geq\inf p
implies that
\inf\tilde{p}>\inf p.
Now by the arguments in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, the claim is proved.

Step 2: Now we prove the steady state is almost periodic. Let
u_{0}
be the steady state of (
5.1
).
It is sufficient to prove for any pair of sequences
\{n_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\ \{m_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{Z}
, we can extract a common subsequences
\{n^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\ \{m^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\lim\limits_{i\rightarrow\infty}u_{0}(n+m^{\prime}_{i}+n^{\prime}_{i})=\lim\limits_{i\rightarrow\infty}\left(\lim\limits_{j\rightarrow\infty}u_{0}(n+n^{\prime}_{i}+m^{\prime}_{j})\right).

Now consider two sequences
\{n_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\ \{m_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
\tilde{a}_{*}(n):=\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}a(n+n_{i}+m_{i}),\tilde{b}_{*}(n):=\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}b(n+n_{i}+m_{i})
,
\tilde{c}_{*}(n):=\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}c(n+n_{i}+m_{i})
exist. Since the limits

\quad\quad\quad\tilde{u}_{0}:=\lim\limits_{i\rightarrow\infty}u_{0}(\cdot+n_{i}),\ \tilde{u}_{*}:=\lim\limits_{i\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{u}_{0}(\cdot+m_{i}),\ u_{*}:=\lim\limits_{i\rightarrow\infty}u_{0}(\cdot+n_{i}+m_{i}),

exist (up to subsequences) locally uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and from the almost periodicity of
a,b,c
, we have
\tilde{u}_{*}
and
u_{*}
are both solutions of (
5.3
) with the coefficients of
\tilde{a}_{*},\tilde{b}_{*},\tilde{c}_{*}
. Moreover,

\sup\tilde{u}_{*}\leq\sup u_{0},\sup u_{*}\leq\sup u_{0},\inf u_{0}\leq\inf\tilde{u}_{*},\inf u_{0}\leq\inf u_{*}.

By step 1,
u_{*}=\tilde{u}_{*}
, and then the result is proved.
∎

Combing Proposition

Proposition 6.1

Let

\inf A_{1},\inf\bigr(A_{1}(\theta-\omega)-A_{2}(\theta)\bigr)

Recall that
z^{\prime}=\omega\bigr(\frac{z}{\epsilon}-\frac{lt}{\epsilon^{2}}\bigr)

\sigma(z)=Q(z^{\prime}),\tilde{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\bigr[(A_{1}P)(z^{\prime})P_{*}(z^{\prime}+\omega)-(A_{2}P_{*})(z^{\prime}+\omega)P(z^{\prime})+(A_{1}P_{*})(z^{\prime})P(z^{\prime}+\omega)\bigr]

Lemma 6.1

\displaystyle\tilde{D}^{*}\bigr((A(\theta)+\bar{c})\tilde{D}N\bigr)+2\bar{c}\tilde{D}^{*}N=F.

Proposition 6.2

Proof.

Now we approximate the solution

Q(z^{\prime})(u_{0})_{t}(t,z)-\bigr[\tilde{A}_{1}(z^{\prime}-\omega)+\bigr(\epsilon\tilde{A}_{1}D_{\epsilon}N+\epsilon D_{\epsilon}^{*}(\tilde{A}_{1}N)+2\bar{c}N-lQN\bigr)(z^{\prime})\bigr]\partial_{zz}u_{0}(t,z).

\|(D_{Y}\Psi(\xi,0,0))^{-1}\|\leq\eta^{-1}.

Fix
\xi_{0}\in\mathcal{I},

since
A\in C(\mathcal{I},\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
, there exists
\rho=\rho(\xi_{0})>0
with
|\xi_{0}-\xi|\leq\rho
such that
\|A(\xi_{0})-A(\xi)\|+\|A(\xi_{0})^{-1}-A(\xi)^{-1}\|\leq\epsilon
. Setting
s=\min\{\rho,\epsilon\},\delta=\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\eta\geq\frac{60(\|A\|^{3}+1)}{\inf|\det A|}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
, there holds the estimate

\quad 2\sup\limits_{\overline{B_{s}((\xi_{0},0))}}\|\Psi(\xi,0,f)\|\times\|(D_{Y}\Psi(\xi_{0},0,0))^{-1}\|\leq 2\epsilon\frac{\inf|\det A|}{60(\|A\|^{3}+1)}\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\delta,

and then (
A.1
) is satisfied.

On the other hand, since
Y,f
are small
\mathrm{gl}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
matrices, by Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula, one has

\displaystyle D_{Y}\Psi(\xi,Y,f)(Y^{\prime})-D_{Y}\Psi(\xi_{0},0,0)(Y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{nre}((A(\xi_{0})^{-1}-A(\xi)^{-1})Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi_{0}))-A(\xi)^{-1}Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta+\omega)(A(\xi)-A(\xi_{0}))

\displaystyle-\mathbb{P}_{nre}(O(A(\xi)^{-1}Y(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi))A(\xi)^{-1}Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi)+\frac{1}{2}[Y^{\prime\prime\prime},M+H]+\cdots)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}_{nre}(O(Y(\xi,\theta))Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta)+\frac{1}{2}[D+G,-Y^{\prime\prime}]+\cdots).

where
Y^{\prime\prime}(\xi,\theta)=Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta)+O(Y(\xi,\theta))Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta)
,
Y^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi,\theta)=-A(\xi)^{-1}Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi)-O(A(\xi)^{-1}Y(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi))A(\xi)^{-1}Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi)
,
D(\xi,\theta)=-A(\xi)^{-1}(Y(\xi,\theta+\omega)+Y^{\prime}(\xi,\theta+\omega))A(\xi)+f(\xi,\theta)+Y(\xi,\theta),

M(\xi,\theta)=-A(\xi)^{-1}Y(\xi,\theta+\omega)A(\xi)+f(\xi,\theta)+Y(\xi,\theta)
and
G,H
are higher order terms.

Therefore, since
\mathcal{B}
admits property
(H)
, for
\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\delta,\|f\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq s,

\displaystyle\|D_{Y}\Psi(\xi,Y,f)(Y^{\prime})-D_{Y}\Psi(\xi_{0},0,0)(Y^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6|Y^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{B}}(\|A\|^{3}+1)}{|\det A(\xi_{0})|}\bigr(2\|A(\xi)-A(\xi_{0})\|+2\|A(\xi_{0})^{-1}-A(\xi)^{-1}\|+|Y|_{\mathcal{B}}+|f|_{\mathcal{B}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6(\|A\|^{3}+1)|Y^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{B}}}{\inf|\det A(\xi)|}\bigr(3\epsilon+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr).

which implies

\sup\limits_{\overline{B_{s}((\xi_{0},0))}\times\overline{B_{\delta}(0)}}\|D_{Y}\Psi(\xi_{0},0,0)-D_{Y}\Psi(\xi,Y,f)\|\leq\frac{6(\|A\|^{3}+1)(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}+3\epsilon)}{\inf|\det A(\xi)|}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\overline{B_{s}((\xi_{0},0))}\times\overline{B_{\delta}(0)}}\|\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal{B}_{\xi_{0}}^{nre}(\eta)}-(D_{Y}\Psi(\xi_{0},0,0))^{-1}\times D_{Y}\Psi(\xi,Y,f)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup\limits_{\overline{B_{s}((\xi_{0},0))}\times\overline{B_{\delta}(0)}}\|D_{Y}\Psi(\xi_{0},0,0)-D_{Y}\Psi(\xi,Y,f)\|\|(D_{Y}\Psi(\xi_{0},0,0))^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6(\|A\|^{3}+1)(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}+3\epsilon)}{\inf|\det A(\xi)|}\frac{\inf|\det A(\xi)|}{60(\|A\|^{3}+1)}\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}

which implies that (
A.2
) is satisfied. By Theorem
A.1
, for
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\epsilon
and
\eta\geq\frac{60(1+\|A\|^{3})\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\inf|\det A|}
, there
exist
Y=Y(\xi,f),\ F^{re}=F^{re}(\xi,f)\in C(\mathcal{I}\times\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{I}},\mathscr{B}^{re}_{\mathcal{I}})
with the estimates
\|Y(\xi,f)\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
such that

\Psi(A(\xi),Y(\xi,f),f)=0,

i.e.,

\mathrm{e}^{-A(\xi)^{-1}Y(\xi,f)(\theta+\omega)A(\xi)}\mathrm{e}^{f(\theta)}\mathrm{e}^{Y(\xi,f)(\theta)}=\mathrm{e}^{F^{re}(\xi,f)(\theta)}.

It is equivalent to

\mathrm{e}^{-Y(\xi,\theta+\omega)}A(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{F(\xi,\theta)}\mathrm{e}^{Y(\xi,\theta)}=A(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{F^{re}(\xi,\theta)}.

It is clear to check that
\|F^{re}\|_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}}\leq 2\epsilon
.
The proof is complete.
∎
