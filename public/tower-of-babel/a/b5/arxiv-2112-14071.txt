Determining kernels in linear viscoelasticity
Barbara Kaltenbacher, Ustim Khristenko, Vanja Nikolić, Mabel Lizzy Rajendran, and Barbara Wohlmuth

Abstract
. In this work, we investigate the inverse problem of determining the kernel functions that best describe the mechanical behavior of a complex medium modeled by a general nonlocal viscoelastic wave equation. To this end, we minimize a tracking-type data misfit function under this PDE constraint. We perform the well-posedness analysis of the state and adjoint problems and, using these results, rigorously derive the first-order sensitivities. Numerical experiments in a three-dimensional setting illustrate the method.

1.
Introduction

When elastic waves propagate through complex media such as biological tissues, their amplitude is attenuated according to a frequency-dependent law. In recent years, it has become evident that these attenuation laws are more complicated than initially thought and that nonlocal wave equations are needed to model such behavior. Indeed, elastic wave equations with weakly singular kernels arise in various important medical and industrial applications of wave propagation in complex media. For example, frequency power laws with powers between zero and two are often encountered in shear-wave elastography; see
[
40
,
18
,
23
]
.

In this work, we tackle the inverse problem of determining the kernel functions that best describe the mechanical behavior of a complex medium from over-specified data. To this end, we first derive and analyze a general nonlocal elastic wave model containing three kernels, cf. (
2.2
) below.
By taking the Laplace transform, it can be seen that, in general, not all three kernels can be uniquely determined independently of each other. Therefore, we consider the inverse problem of determining two of these kernels (cf. (
2.2
) below) from over-specified data and study the PDE-constrained optimization problem resulting from the (optionally regularized) minimization of the data misfit. Using an adjoint-based calculation, we then rigorously derive the first-order sensitivities and develop a numerical kernel recovery method.

We note that some work in the direction of kernel identification can be found in the literature; in particular, we refer to
[
6
,
8
,
19
,
41
,
45
]
and the references contained therein. For results with Abel integral kernels, that is, models involving time-fractional derivatives, we refer to, e.g.,
[
17
,
20
,
27
,
26
,
34
]
.

The remaining of our exposition is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we derive a general nonlocal elastodynamic wave equation and discuss the setup of the inverse problem. Section
3
is concerned with the well-posedness analysis of the state problem. In Section
4
, we derive and analyze the corresponding adjoint problem. Section
5
is focused on the estimation of the kernels from additional observations. Finally, in Section
6
, we present numerical experiments that illustrate our theoretical results.

2.
Modeling and problem setup

In this section, we provide the setup for the problem and derive the viscoelastic equation from the conservation law and the constitutive relation of stress and strain.

Consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, with Lipschitz smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
acted upon by force. Let
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}(x,t)
be the displacement field and
\rho=\rho(x)
the density. The linearized strain due to the deformation is defined by

\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T}.

The balance of momentum in the body is given by

(2.1)

\rho\mathbf{u}_{tt}=\textup{div}\bm{\sigma}+\mathbf{f},

where
\bm{\sigma}
denotes the stress tensor.

In this paper we consider the following three- and two-kernel constitutive relations involving hydrostatic and deviatoric parts of the strain
\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})_{ii}
,
\mathbf{\varepsilon}_{d}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})-\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{I}\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})
, separately

(2.2)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}+(k_{\sigma}*\bm{\sigma})_{t}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{A}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})+k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{I}\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),

and

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{A}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})+k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{I}\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}).

We point to Remark
1
for their relation to existing models in the literature.
The operator
*
denotes the convolution on the positive half-line with respect to the time variable.

2.1.
The viscoelastic wave model

The model for the wave propagation in complex media considered in this work is derived by coupling the conservation law with more appropriate constitutive relation, as described above. The two hyperbolic models obtained by coupling (
2.1
) with two forms of constitutive relations, (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), are as follows.

•

Model 1:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\rho\mathbf{u}_{tt}+\rho(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{u}_{tt})_{t}-\textup{div}\left[\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{A}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})+k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{I}\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{f})_{t};

•

Model 2:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\rho\mathbf{u}_{tt}-\textup{div}[\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{A}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})+k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{I}\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})]=\mathbf{f}.

We can rewrite both of these equations as

(2.6)

\displaystyle\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt}-\textup{div}\left[\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})+\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})\right]=\mathbf{g},

by introducing the auxiliary variable
\mathbf{z}
, tensor
\mathbb{D}
, and the forcing function
\mathbf{g}
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}+k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{u}_{t},\quad\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{A}k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}-\mathbb{C}k_{\sigma}{+\mathbb{I}k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\textup{tr}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{f}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{f})_{t}+\rho{k_{\sigma}}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{t}(0),

where
k_{\sigma}=0
for Model 2. We note that the scalar counterpart of this model has been considered in the literature with fractional kernels; see, for example,
[
21
]
. We mention in passing that third-order in time models of viscoelasticity were introduced in
[
14
]
and have been recently a topic of extensive research, often referred to as the Moore–Gibson–Thompson viscoelasticity; see, for example,
[
32
,
7
]
and the references therein.

Assumptions on the medium parameters, kernels, and tensors

To allow for heterogeneous viscoelastic materials, we assume that
\rho\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and that there exists
\underline{\rho}>0
, such that

(2.8)

\rho(x)\geq\underline{\rho}>0\qquad\text{a.e.\ in}\ \Omega.

The fourth-order tensors
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{A}
are assumed to be constant and symmetric,

(2.9)

\mathbb{C},\ \mathbb{A}\,\in\mbox{Sym}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mbox{ and $\mathbb{C}$ is positive definite.}

Moreover,
we assume that the kernel
k_{\sigma}
and tensor
\mathbb{D}

according to (
2.7
)
are such that the following non-negativity conditions hold:

(2.10)

\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{y})(s):(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{y})_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s\geq 0;}

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{y})(s):\mathbf{y}(s)\,\textup{d}s\geq\underline{\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}_{H^{-\delta}(0,t)}

for some
\underline{\gamma}>0
,
\delta>0

and all
\mathbf{y}\in W^{1,1}(0,T,\mbox{Sym}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
Here

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mbox{Sym}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle=\{\mathbb{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}\,:\mathbb{C}_{ijk\ell}=\mathbb{C}_{ij\ell k}=\mathbb{C}_{jik\ell}=\mathbb{C}_{k\ell ij}\}\,,

\displaystyle\mbox{Sym}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\,:\mathbf{y}_{ij}=\mathbf{y}_{ji}\}\,,

and
H^{-\delta}(0,t)
is the dual of the Sobolev space
H_{0}^{\delta}(0,t)
.

In the scalar case

(2.13)

\mathbb{D}(t)=\mathbb{M}m(t)

with
\mathbb{M}\in\mbox{Sym}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
positive semidefinite and using the
\mathbb{M}
bilinear form

(2.14)

\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\rangle=\mathbb{M}\mathbf{w}:\mathbf{v}

as well as seminorm, condition (
2.10
) follows from the integrated version of Lemma
B.1
, provided
k_{\sigma}=k*m
with
k\geq 0
and
k^{\prime}\leq 0
, using
\mathbf{w}=m*\mathbf{y}
, which satisfies
\mathbf{w}(0)=0
and assuming
\mathbf{w}\in H^{1}(0,T)
.
In the same setting, assuming strict positivity of
\mathbb{M}
and
\Re(\mathcal{F}m)(\omega)\geq\gamma(1+\omega^{2})^{-\delta/2}
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
(where
\Re
denotes the real part and
\mathcal{F}
the Fourier transform), we can conclude
from Lemma
B.2

that
(
2.11
) holds.

&\hskip 17.07164pt-\textup{div}\left[\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{A}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})+k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})\mathbb{I}\right]=\mathbf{g}&&\text{in }\ \Omega\times(0,T),\\

with

defined in (

Proposition 3.1
.

Let assumptions

Proof.

The proof follows by employing a Galerkin discretization in space based on the eigenvectors

with the bilinear form

(3.9)

a(\bm{\phi}_{j},\mathbf{v})=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\bm{\phi}_{j}):\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v})\,\textup{d}x;

cf.
[
30
]
. The basis can be chosen as orthonormal in
the weighted Lebesgue space

L^{2}_{\rho}(\Omega)^{d}
, as proven in
[
30
, Lemma 4.1]
. Denote
V_{n}=\textup{span}\{\bm{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\phi}_{n}\}
. We seek an approximate solution in the form

(3.10)

\mathbf{u}^{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}(t)\bm{\phi}_{j}(x)

with
\mathbf{u}^{n}(0)
,
\mathbf{u}_{t}^{n}(0)

\mathbf{u}_{0}
,
\mathbf{u}_{1}

onto

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\xi_{i,tt}(t)+(k_{\sigma}*\xi_{i,tt})_{t}(t)+\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\xi_{i}(t)\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{g}(t),\bm{\phi}_{i})_{\Omega}+((\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t})(t),\bm{\phi}_{i})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}}

for

and
t\in[0,T]
. In vector notation
\bm{\xi}=[\xi_{1}\ \ldots\ \xi_{n}]^{T}

(3.12)

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}_{tt}+(k_{\sigma}*\bm{\xi}_{tt})_{t}+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}\bm{\xi}+\mathbf{A}k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\bm{\xi}_{t}+\bm{\mu}k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\bm{\xi}_{t}=\bm{b},

with the right-hand side vector given by

(3.13)

\displaystyle\bm{b}(t)=(\mathbf{g}(t),\bm{\phi}_{i})_{\Omega}+((\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t})(t),\bm{\phi}_{i})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}}.

Further, we have introduced
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}=[\tilde{{\lambda}}_{1}\ \ldots\ \tilde{{\lambda}}_{n}]
,
\mathbf{A}=[\mathbf{A}_{ij}]_{n\times n}
, and
\bm{\mu}=[\bm{\mu}_{ij}]_{n\times n}
with

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{ij}=(\mathbb{A}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\bm{\phi}_{j}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\bm{\phi}_{i})),\quad\bm{\mu}_{ij}=(\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\bm{\phi}_{j})\mathbb{I},\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\bm{\phi}_{i})).

Note that we have
k_{\sigma}\in W^{1,1}(0,T)\hookrightarrow C[0,T]
. Denote
\bm{\chi}=\bm{\xi}_{tt}
. Then

(3.15)

(k_{\sigma}*\bm{\xi}_{tt})_{t}=k_{\sigma t}*\bm{\xi}_{tt}+k_{\sigma}(0)\bm{\xi}_{tt}

with
k_{\sigma t}\in L^{1}(0,T)
. We have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\bm{\chi}_{t}=1*\bm{\chi}+\bm{\xi}_{1},\quad\bm{\chi}=1*1*\bm{\chi}+t\bm{\xi}_{1}+\bm{\xi}_{0}.

Then since we have assumed that
k_{\sigma}(0)>-1
, we can rewrite the equation as

(3.17)

\displaystyle(1+k_{\sigma}(0))\bm{\chi}+k_{\sigma t}*\bm{\chi}+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}(1*1)*\bm{\chi}+\mathbf{A}(k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*1)*\bm{\chi}+\bm{\mu}(k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*1)*\bm{\chi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{b}+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}(t\bm{\xi}_{1}+\bm{\xi}_{0})+\mathbf{A}k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(t)\bm{\xi}_{1}+\bm{\mu}k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(t)\bm{\xi}_{1}.

We can then see this problem as a system Volterra integral equations of the second kind

(3.18)

\displaystyle(1+k_{\sigma}(0))\bm{\chi}+\bm{K}*\bm{\chi}=\tilde{\bm{b}}(t)

with the kernel

(3.19)

\bm{K}=k_{\sigma t}+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}(1*1)+\mathbf{A}(k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*1)+\bm{\mu}(k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*1)

According to existence theory for the Volterra integral equations of the second kind (see, e.g.
[
16
, Ch. 2, Theorem 3.5]
), there exists a unique

, which solves (

. Note that then the combined function
\mathbf{z}=k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{u}
due to (

) and the identities

(3.21)

\mathbf{z}_{t}=k_{\sigma t}*\mathbf{u}_{t}+(1+k_{\sigma}(0))\mathbf{u}_{t},

(3.22)

\mathbf{z}_{tt}=k_{\sigma t}*\mathbf{u}_{tt}+k_{\sigma t}*\mathbf{u}_{t}(0)+(1+k_{\sigma}(0))\mathbf{u}_{tt}

Energy analysis.
Testing the semi-discrete problem given in terms of
\mathbf{z}=k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{u}
:

(3.23)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}(\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt},\mathbf{v})_{\Omega}+(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}))_{\Omega}+(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}))_{\Omega}\\
=(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{v})_{\Omega}+(\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t},\mathbf{v})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}},\end{multlined}

with
\mathbf{z}_{t}(t)=(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{u})_{t}(t)\in V_{n}
and integrating in space and time leads at first to the following identity:

(3.24)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{2}\|\rho^{1/2}\mathbf{z}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbb{C}^{1/2}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}_{t}))_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}s\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{2}\|\rho^{1/2}\mathbf{z}_{t}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbb{C}^{1/2}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}(0))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{z}_{t})_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}s\\
+\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t},\mathbf{z}_{t})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}}\,\textup{d}s.\end{multlined}

By Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, we have

(3.25)

\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{z}_{t})_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}s\leq\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\rho^{-1/2}\mathbf{g}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\,\textup{d}s\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sup_{s\in(0,t)}\|\rho^{1/2}\mathbf{z}_{t}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

Employing integration by parts with respect to time yields

(3.26)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t},\mathbf{z}_{t})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}}\,\textup{d}s=\,(\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t},\mathbf{z})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}}\Big|_{0}^{t}-\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbf{h}_{t}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{tt},\mathbf{z})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}}\,\textup{d}s.

Thus,

(3.27)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t},\mathbf{z}_{t})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}}\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|(\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t})(t)\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\textup{N}})^{d}}\|\mathbf{z}(t)\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\textup{N}})^{d}}+\|\mathbf{h}(0)\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\textup{N}})^{d}}\|\mathbf{z}(0)\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\textup{N}})^{d}}\\
-\|\mathbf{h}_{t}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{tt}\|_{L^{1}(0,t;H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\textup{N}})^{d})}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t;H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\textup{N}})^{d})}.\end{multlined}

By recalling the definition of
\mathbf{z}
, we further have

(3.28)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}_{t}))_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}s=\,\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}))_{t}+\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}))_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}s.

We can employ our assumption (
2.11
) on the kernels, which guarantees that

(3.29)

\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}))_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}s\geq\underline{\gamma}\|\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})\|^{2}_{H^{-\delta}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}.

We can further use assumption (
2.10
) to conclude that

(3.30)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}))_{t})_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}s\geq 0.

Thus, from (
3.24
), by relying on the assumptions on
\rho
and
\mathbb{C}
, we have the estimate

(3.31)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{z}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\underline{\gamma}\|\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})\|^{2}_{H^{-\delta}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega)^{2d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\mathbf{z}_{t}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{z}(0)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{g}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}^{2}\\
+\|\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t}\|^{2}_{W^{1,1}(0,t;H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\textup{N}})^{d})}\end{multlined}

at first in a discrete setting.

Applying the
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
norm to (
3.21
) with monotonically decreasing kernel
k_{\sigma}
and using the estimate

(3.32)

\displaystyle\|k_{\sigma t}*\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|k_{\sigma t}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}=(k_{\sigma}(0)-k_{\sigma}(t))\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}

under the assumption
k_{\sigma}(t)\geq\underline{k}>-1
we get

(3.33)

(1+\underline{k})\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|\mathbf{z}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

Likewise, taking the
L^{\infty}(0,T;\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)^{d})
norm of
\mathbf{z}=k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{u}=k_{\sigma\,t}*\mathbf{u}+k_{\sigma}(0)\mathbf{u}-k_{\sigma}\mathbf{u}(0)
, we conclude

(3.34)

(1+\underline{k})\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)^{d})}\leq\|\mathbf{z}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)^{d})}+\|k_{\sigma}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)^{d})}.

Since
\mathbb{D}\in W^{1,1}(0,T;{\mbox{Sym}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})})
, we can obtain an additional estimate on
\mathbf{z}_{tt}
as follows. For
\mathbf{v}\in\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)
with
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)}\leq 1
, we decompose it into
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}^{1}+\mathbf{v}^{2}
, where
\mathbf{v}^{1}\in\textup{span}\{\bm{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\phi}_{n}\}
and
\mathbf{v}^{2}\in\textup{span}\{\bm{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\phi}_{n}\}^{\bot}

[
12

for similar arguments. Then
\|\mathbf{v}^{1}\|_{\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)}\leq 1

and

(3.35)

\displaystyle(\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt},\mathbf{v})_{\Omega}=

\displaystyle(\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt},\mathbf{v}^{1})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}^{1}))_{\Omega}-(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}^{1}))_{\Omega}+(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{v}^{1})_{\Omega}+(\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t},\mathbf{v}^{1})_{\Gamma_{\textup{N}}}.

From here, using the identity

(3.36)

(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}))(t)=(\mathbb{D}_{t}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}))(t)+\mathbb{D}(0)\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}(t))-\mathbb{D}(t)\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}(0))

(3.37)

\displaystyle\|\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt}\|_{L^{{p}}(0,T;(\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega))^{*})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}+\|\mathbb{D}_{t}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbb{D}(0)\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}\\
+\|\mathbf{g}\|_{L^{p}(0,T;\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega))^{*}}+\|\mathbf{h}+(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{h})_{t}\|_{{L^{p}}(0,T;H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\textup{N}})^{d})}\\
+\|\mathbb{D}\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}\,\|\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}(0))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.\end{multlined}

With
p=1
we get
\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt}\in L^{1}(0,T;(\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega))^{*})
. If additionally
\mathbf{g}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega))^{*}
and
\mathbf{u}_{t}(t=0)=0
, then we can also use

.

Similarly to (
3.32
), by taking the
L^{{p}}(0,T;\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)^{*})

\rho\mathbf{u}_{tt}
as for
\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt}
.

Usual compactness arguments then lead to a solution of the state problem. Uniqueness follows by proving that the only solution of the homogeneous problem

(3.38)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\langle\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt},\mathbf{v}\rangle+(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}))_{\Omega}+(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}))_{\Omega}=0\end{multlined}

is
\mathbf{u}=0
. To prove uniqueness, we cannot test the above weak form with
\mathbf{z}_{t}
as in the semi-discrete setting due to its insufficient spatial regularity. Instead, following, e.g.,
[
12
, Ch. 7.2]
, we take

(3.39)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{t}^{s}\mathbf{z}(\tau)\,\textup{d}\tau\ &\text{if}\ 0\leq t\leq s,\\
0\ &\text{if}\ s\leq t\leq T,\end{cases}

which satisfies
\mathbf{v}(t)\in\bm{H}^{1}_{\textup{D}}(\Omega)
for each
0\leq t\leq T
. Furthermore,

(3.40)

\mathbf{v}_{t}=-\mathbf{z},\quad 0\leq t\leq s.

Since
\mathbf{v}(s)=0
and (with zero initial data)
\mathbf{z}_{t}(0)=0
, taking this test function in (
3.38
) yields

(3.41)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\left(\langle\rho\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{z}\rangle-(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}_{t}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}))_{\Omega}+((\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}))_{t},\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}))_{\Omega}\right)\,\textup{d}t=0,

where we have also used that
\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})=(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}))_{t}-\mathbb{D}(t)\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}(0))=(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}))_{t}
. Integration by parts with respect to time yields

(3.42)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\rho^{1/2}\mathbf{z}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbb{C}^{1/2}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}(0))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{s}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}))_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}t=0.

Due to our assumptions on
\mathbb{D}
, we know that

(3.43)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}))_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}t=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}),k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})+\mathbf{u})_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}(\mathbb{D}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}),(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}))_{t}+\mathbf{u})_{\Omega}\,\textup{d}t\geq 0.

Thus, from (
3.42
), we conclude that
\mathbf{z}=0
and thus

(3.44)

k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{u}=k_{\sigma t}*\mathbf{u}+k_{\sigma}(0)\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}=0.

Together with
\mathbf{u}|_{t=0}=0
, this implies that
\mathbf{u}=0
.
∎

We next consider the well-posedness of the state problem for a singular
k_{\sigma}
. Note that the analysis will lead to the same estimate (
3.31
) of the combined quantity
\mathbf{z}
, but the resulting estimate for
\mathbf{u}
crucially depends on whether

is singular or not.
We also point out that even for a singular kernel

as

, where
(k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{u}_{tt})_{t}
behaves similary to a Riemann–Liouville derivative of order

\gamma
of
\mathbf{u}_{tt}
, no initial condition on
\mathbf{u}_{tt}
is imposed, which is in line with the theory of Riemann–Liouville fractional ODEs; see, e.g.,
[
22
,
33
]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Given
T>0
, let assumptions (
2.8
), (

) and

2.10

Furthermore, assume that
k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}

3.1

(3.46)

(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t})|_{t=0}=(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1})\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\{0\}.

Further, assume that the source term and boundary data satisfy the assumptions of Proposition

5.
Estimation of the kernels from additional observations

By taking Laplace transforms, we have shown in Remark
1

Therefore, without loss of generality, concerning parameter identification we will focus on the two-kernel model (formally setting

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\rho\mathbf{u}_{tt}-\textup{div}[\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{A}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})+k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\textup{tr}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})\mathbb{I}]=\mathbf{f},\quad&&\text{in }\ \Omega\times(0,T),\\

To keep notation simple, we will restrict ourselves to

In order to define a gradient, we need a Hilbert space setting and thus consider

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}}(\vec{k},\mathbf{u}(\vec{k}),\mathbf{p}(\vec{k}))h=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{A}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\overline{\mathbf{p}}))(T-t)\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}t+\gamma_{\epsilon}\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})h

(6.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\rho\mathbf{u}_{tt}=\textup{div}\bm{\sigma},\\
&\bm{\sigma}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}_{d}(\mathbf{u}_{t})+k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}*\mathbb{I}\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t}),\\
&\mathbf{u}\bigr|_{x_{1}=0}=0,\qquad\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}\bigr|_{x_{1}=1}=\bm{B}\text{ or }\bm{T},\\
&(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}))|_{t=0}=(0,0,0).\end{aligned}\right.

We employ the Newmark method (
\beta=0.25
,
\gamma=0.5
) for time-stepping in combination with a sum-of-exponentials kernel representation; see, e.g.,
[
24
,
42
]
. More precisely, we are looking for the kernels
k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
,
k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
in the form

(6.4)

k_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}}w_{\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}e^{-\lambda_{\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}t},\qquad k_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}}w_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}e^{-\lambda_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}t},

with unknown weights
w_{\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}
,
w_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}
and exponents
\lambda_{\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}
,
\lambda_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}
.
For simplicity, we have fixed
m_{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}=m_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
.
Let us denote by
\theta:=\{w_{\mathbf{\varepsilon},k},w_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon},k},\lambda_{\mathbf{\varepsilon},k},\lambda_{\textup{tr}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon},k}\}
the vector of all learnable parameters.
We assume that the measurements for the tip displacement (more precisely, the mean displacement over the right end face) are available at some discrete time points in the interval
[0,t_{meas}]
with
t_{meas}=T/2
. We minimize the objective function

(6.5)

J(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\|u_{tip}(\theta)-u_{tip}^{meas}\|^{2}_{[0,t_{meas}]},

with respect to the parameters
\theta
, using the
\ell_{2}
-norm on the discretized interval
[0,t_{meas}]
.

We use the FEniCS platform
[
2
]
for the finite elements implementation of the forward problem and the dolfin-adjoint package
[
28
]
for the adjoint problem in combination with PyTorch
[
31
]
through the Torch-FEniCS interface
[
4
]
.
The minimization is performed using the LBFGS method
[
46
]
with the strong Wolfe line search.
The beam is discretized using
60\times 10\times 5
linear tetrahedral elements (
Figure

).
The time interval is discretized uniformly with the step size
\Delta t=T/100
.

Here, we have been assuming that the measurement data are given for all discrete time points of the time discretization scheme in the interval
[0,t_{meas}]
.
Moreover, in our synthetic setting, we assume that for a given
\theta_{*}
, it holds that

(6.6)

u_{tip}^{meas}=u_{tip}(\theta_{*})+\eta,

where
\eta

Appendix A
Appendix: Regularity

We briefly discuss here the higher regularity of the displacement
\mathbf{u}
in a setting where
\Gamma_{\textup{N}}=\emptyset
and
\partial\Omega
sufficiently smooth. More precisely, we present the formal derivation of a higher-order estimate in terms of
\mathbf{z}
and note that additional bounds on
\mathbf{u}
can be obtained by distinguishing the smooth and singular cases for
k_{\sigma}
, as in Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
.

Abbreviating
\bm{w}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})
,
\bm{v}={\mathbb{D}}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})
and testing

(A.1)

\displaystyle\rho\mathbf{z}_{tt}-\textup{div}\left[\bm{w}+\bm{v}\right]=\mathbf{g}

with
-\textup{div}(\bm{w}_{t}+\bm{v}_{t})
after integration over
\Omega\times(0,t)
yields

(A.2)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{2}\|\varrho^{1/2}\mathbb{C}^{1/2}\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}_{t}(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\textup{div}(\bm{w}+\bm{v})(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left((k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})_{tt}+\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{tt})\right){\mathbb{D}}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{tt})\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}t\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{g}_{t}(s)\textup{div}(\bm{w}+\bm{v})(s)\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s+\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{g}(t)\textup{div}(w+v)(t)\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{L^{1}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}+\frac{1}{8}\|\textup{div}(\bm{w}+\bm{v})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}^{2}+2\|\mathbf{g}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\\
+\frac{1}{8}\|\textup{div}(\bm{w}+\bm{v})(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2},\end{multlined}

where we have used
\bm{w}(t=0)=\bm{v}(t=0)=0
. Note that the second and fourth term on the right-hand side can be absorbed into the second term on the left-hand side. Due to the assumptions on
\mathbb{D}
, we have

(A.3)

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left((k_{\sigma}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{t})_{tt}+\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{tt})\right){\mathbb{D}}*\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{tt})\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}t\geq 0.

Altogether, we obtain the estimate

(A.4)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z}_{t}(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}(\bm{w}+\bm{v})(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\lesssim\,\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{L^{1}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}+\|\mathbf{g}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}.

a.e. in time.

Appendix B
Appendix: Auxiliary inequalities

Analogously to
[
1
, Lemma 1]
, one can prove the following lower bound.

Lemma B.1
.

Given Hilbert space
X
and any
w\in H^{1}(0,T;H)
,
k\in W^{1,1}(0,T)
with
k\geq 0
,
k^{\prime}\leq 0
a.e.,

(B.1)

\int_{0}^{T}\langle w(t),k*w_{t}(t)\rangle\,\textup{d}t\geq\frac{1}{2}(k*\|w\|^{2})(T)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}k(t)\,\textup{d}t\,\|w(0)\|^{2}\,.

Proof.

The statement follows from

(B.2)

\displaystyle\langle w(t),(k*w_{t})(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}(k*\|w\|^{2}_{t})(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle w(t)-w(s),k(t-s)w_{t}(s)\right\rangle\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle\int_{s}^{t}w_{t}(r)\,\textup{d}r,k(x-s)w_{t}(s)\rangle\,\textup{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{r}\langle w_{t}(r),k(t-s)w_{t}(s)\rangle\,\textup{d}s\textup{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{k(t-r)}k(t-r)\langle w^{\prime}(r),\int_{0}^{r}k(t-s)w_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s\rangle\,\textup{d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{k(t-r)}\frac{d}{dr}\Bigl\|\int_{0}^{r}k(t-s)w_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s\Bigr\|^{2}\,\textup{d}r

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{k^{\prime}(x-r)}{k^{2}(x-r)}\Bigl\|\int_{0}^{r}k(t-s)w_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s\Bigr\|^{2}\,\textup{d}r+\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{k(t-r)}\Bigl\|\int_{0}^{r}k(t-s)w^{\prime}(s)\,\textup{d}s\Bigr\|^{2}\right]_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

∎

Similarly to
[
9
, Lemma 2.3]
(see also
[
44
, Theorem 1]
) one obtains the following coercivity estimate.

Lemma B.2
.

For some Hilbert space
X
and any
w\in H^{-\delta/2}(0,T;H)
,
k\in L^{1}(0,T)
with
\Re(\mathcal{F}k)(\omega)\geq\gamma(1+\omega^{2})^{-\delta/2}
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,

(B.3)

\int_{0}^{T}\langle w(s),k*w(s)\rangle\,\textup{d}s\geq\gamma\|w\|_{H^{-\delta/2}(0,T;X)}^{2},

Proof.

We first assume
w\in L^{2}(0,T;X)
and approximate

k*w
by
k_{\epsilon}*w
with
k_{\epsilon}(t)=k_{\epsilon}(t)e^{-\epsilon t}

for
\epsilon>0
and extend
w\in L^{2}(0,T)
to all of
\mathbb{R}
by zero.
Plancherel’s Theorem and the Convolution Theorem then yield

(B.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\langle w(s),(k_{\epsilon}*w)(s)\rangle\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\mathcal{F}w(\omega),\overline{\mathcal{F}[k_{\epsilon}*w]}(\omega)\rangle\,\textup{d}\omega

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\mathcal{F}w(\omega)\|^{2}\overline{\mathcal{F}k_{\epsilon}}(\omega)\,\textup{d}\omega\,.

Here both sides have to be real valued, because obviously the left hand side is, and therefore

(B.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\langle w(s),(k_{\epsilon}*w)(s)\rangle\,\textup{d}s=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\mathcal{F}w(\omega)\|^{2}\Re(\mathcal{F}k_{\epsilon})(\omega)\,\textup{d}\omega

Letting
\epsilon
tend to zero and recalling that

\|w\|_{H^{-\delta/2}(0,x)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}w(\omega)|^{2}(1+\omega^{2})^{-\delta/2}\,\textup{d}\omega
, we can conclude the inequality (
B.3
) for
w\in L^{2}(0,T;X)
.
Now the assertion follows for general
w\in H^{-\delta/2}(0,T;X)
by density.
∎
