Simple and Rigorous Proof Method for the Security of Practical Quantum Key Distribution in the Single-Qubit Regime Using Mismatched Basis Measurements
Michel Boyer 1 , Gilles Brassard 1,2 , Nicolas Godbout 3 , Rotem Liss 1,4,∗ , and Stéphane Virally 3

Abstract
Quantum key distribution (QKD) protocols aim at allowing two parties to generate a secret shared key. While many QKD protocols have been proven unconditionally secure in theory, practical security analyses of experimental QKD implementations typically do not take into account all possible loopholes, and practical devices are still not fully characterized for obtaining tight and realistic key rates. We present a simple method of computing secure key rates for any practical implementation of discrete-variable QKD (which can also apply to measurement-device-independent QKD), initially in the single-qubit lossless regime, and we rigorously prove its unconditional security against any possible attack. We hope our method becomes one of the standard tools used for analysing, benchmarking, and standardizing all practical realizations of QKD.

Introduction

The purpose of quantum key distribution (QKD) is to allow two legitimate
parties, typically named Alice and Bob, to generate
an information-theoretically secure key
Bennett and Brassard 1984
.
Most QKD protocols have been proven secure
even if the adversary Eve is allowed to apply any theoretical attack
allowed by the laws of quantum theory.
However, despite enormous progress in recent years, unconditional
security of practical implementations of QKD has remained elusive.

The difficulty of achieving practical security stems from the fact
that practical implementations deviate from the theoretical protocols
in many important aspects. The theoretical models of the preparation
devices, the transmitted quantum systems, the quantum channels,
and the measurement devices differ enormously
from any experimental realization, and these differences open up loopholes
and weaknesses that Eve may be able to exploit
(see, e.g.,
Brassard et al. 2000
;
Lydersen et al. 2010
).

Most security weaknesses of the measurement devices can be closed
using
measurement-device-independent
(MDI)
QKD
Biham et al. 1996
;
Inamori 2002
;
Lo et al. 2012
;
Braunstein and Pirandola 2012
.
However, MDI QKD still requires us to trust the preparation devices of
Alice and Bob, and deviations of the actually prepared quantum states
from the theoretical states still pose a significant security threat.
Alternatively, in
(fully) device-independent
(DI)
QKD
Mayers and Yao 1998
;
Masanes et al. 2011
;
Vazirani and Vidick 2014
,
Alice’s and Bob’s devices are completely uncharacterized,
and violations of Bell’s inequality prove the secrecy of the final key.
This method, while promising and theoretically solid, still achieves
far worse secret key rates than standard QKD (including MDI QKD) in realistic
experimental settings
Lo et al. 2014
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Pirandola et al. 2020
;
in addition, it still requires assumptions, including the assumption
that Alice’s and Bob’s uncharacterized devices are never allowed to communicate
with each other or with Eve.
Therefore, while we believe that both DI QKD
and standard (especially MDI) QKD are important directions that can
lead to practical security (perhaps in different levels of security),
in this paper, we focus on standard and MDI QKD
protocols, where the most pressing practical security problem that has
no fully available solution is imperfectly generated quantum states.

We suggest a simple and systematic method for analysing source imperfections
and proving unconditional security of a large variety of QKD protocols.
Our method (similarly to the “loss tolerant” QKD
protocol
Tamaki et al. 2014
;
Xu et al. 2015
;
Mizutani et al. 2015
;
Pereira et al. 2019
) assumes
that the quantum source can only emit three possible quantum states
(instead of the four states used by BB84), and it uses a mismatched-basis
analysis (see, e.g.,
Krawec 2016
) for deriving the key rate
in the finite-key regime.
Our analysis method is vastly simplified and rigorous, takes into account
many subtle points that are often omitted in other security proofs,
and gives an explicit key rate formula in the finite-key regime.
We further suggest a practical step-by-step process
for analysing experimental implementations of QKD, and we verify that
the restriction to three states is indeed essential for practical security.

Our method currently applies only to the qubit regime (in
the generalized sense: namely, we require the three emitted quantum states
to be linearly dependent and, therefore, lie inside a two-dimensional
Hilbert subspace), it does not support losses, and it does not support
decoy states
Hwang 2003
;
Lo et al. 2005
;
Wang 2005
.
We believe that the analysis of losses and decoy states will work
within our framework (see, e.g.,
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
;
Hasegawa et al. 2007
;
Lim et al. 2014
),
but we leave their rigorous and precise analysis for future research.
We also believe that our analysis can prove security for practical implementations
of MDI QKD using the reduction techniques introduced by
Biham et al. 1996
;
Lo et al. 2012
,
but we leave a detailed analysis of this direction for future research.

In Section
2
, we explain how the security of practical
implementations of QKD should be rigorously analysed and proved.
In Section
3
, we fully define the analysed QKD protocol,
and in Section
4
, we prove its security;
our final security result (the key rate) is presented
as Corollary
4.3
.
In Section
5
, we explain why four source states are likely
too many (in the qubit and two-basis regime) and why we must restrict our protocol
to three states.

2
Step-by-Step Analysis of Practical Implementations of QKD

Nowadays, despite the enormous progress made on practical security analysis,
a comprehensive method for proving security is still lacking. In many descriptions
of practical implementations of QKD,
while the theoretical model suggested for analysis is close to the practical
implementation, it is naturally not identical, and the reduction from
the practical implementation to the theoretical protocol sometimes uses
hand wavy arguments instead of fully rigorous mathematical modelling and analysis.

For rigorously proving the security of a practical implementation
in the case of a measurement-device-independent (MDI) protocol,
we suggest the following way:

1.

The implementation should be evaluated and tested. In particular,
the emitted quantum states must be repeatedly measured in all aspects,
including determining the modes—frequency spectrum (which includes wavelengths and their relative phases),
polarization, timing and location of emission,
direction of propagation (wave vector), and their degrees of mutual coherence—and performing a full tomography for each mode, thereby
discovering the resulting quantum states. Each resulting quantum
state (for each basis choice and data choice) must be reconstructed
and explicitly written;
this reconstruction is essential for the security analysis.

2.

The quantum states must be given as inputs to the security proof.
The security proof then gives us a key rate formula and security parameters.

3.

The key rate and security parameters can now be compared to the security
definition. The result of this comparison decides whether security
of the practical implementation has been proved.

In particular, if this process requires any reduction between
the practical implementation and the theoretical model,
the reduction must be rigorous and precise, it must be included as a part of
the proof, and it must be verified to work against any possible attack.

Definition of the QKD Protocol

The QKD protocol we analyse in this paper is a prepare-and-measure protocol,
which is defined as follows:

1.

Alice and Bob publicly agree on the parameters
of the protocol:

•

Three normalized quantum states

\{\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}\}

(identical between all rounds
i
)

(and, therefore, must span a two-dimensional Hilbert subspace).
Specifically, we denote

\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}=a\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}+b\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},

(1)

where
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
. We require
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}>\frac{1}{2}

or, equivalently,

\Re(a^{\star}b\langle{\gamma_{0}}|{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}})<\frac{1}{4}
.

•

Bob’s generalized measurement operators for each round
i
:

(a)

\{M^{\mathrm{Z},t}_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}\}_{t\in\{0,1\}}
,
which we name “measurement in the standard basis”
or “measurement in the
z
basis”, and

(b)

\{M^{\mathrm{X},t}_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}\}_{t\in\{0,1\}}
,
which we name “measurement in the conjugate basis”
or “measurement in the
x
basis”.

which are defined similarly to
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
.
We note that Bob’s measurement operators can be arbitrary and are not required
to be perfectly implemented or perfectly known. However, they influence
the measurement results and the error rate, which influence the protocol’s
success probability and key rate.

•

The number
m
of all rounds (all quantum states sent by Alice to Bob).

•

The probabilities that Alice chooses each “preparation basis”:

p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}
represents the probability that Alice prepares either

\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}
or
\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}

(each of which she chooses with an equal probability,
\frac{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{2}
),
and
p^{\mathrm{A}}_{x}
represents the probability that Alice prepares

\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}
. We require
p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}+p^{\mathrm{A}}_{x}=1
.

•

The probabilities that Bob chooses to measure in each measurement basis:

p^{\mathrm{B}}_{z}
(for choosing to measure in the “
z
basis”)
and
p^{\mathrm{B}}_{x}
(for choosing to measure in the “
x

p^{\mathrm{B}}_{z}+p^{\mathrm{B}}_{x}=1
.

•

The numbers
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}

and the second letter represents Bob’s basis choice)

and
the number
n_{1}
of required INFO bits

.

•

The error rate threshold
\delta
(maximal allowed noise
in TEST-Z-Z and TEST-X-X bits).

•

The zero rate threshold
\delta_{\mathrm{mismatch}}

(maximal allowed rate of “
+
” or “
0
” results measured by Bob
in TEST-Z-X and TEST-X-Z bits, respectively).

•

The error correction and privacy amplification parameters
described in
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
, including, in particular, the final key length
\ell
.

2.

Alice randomly chooses
a string
\Phi_{\mathrm{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
of basis choices:
she chooses each bit independently
to have value

In addition, Alice chooses a uniformly random string

3.

For each round

and

—namely:

•

Alice prepares

Alice randomly chooses four subsets

Each one of Alice and Bob produces five substrings

•

•

(the TEST-X-X rounds) of Alice and Bob, respectively;

•

X^{1}
and

(the INFO rounds) of Alice and Bob, respectively.

8.

Alice sends
V^{1},V^{4}
to Bob, and Bob compares

W^{1},W^{4}
and computes the error rates.
If the error rate in either the TEST-Z-Z rounds

or

\delta
,
Bob sets
F^{\mathrm{pe}}=\emptyset
and aborts the protocol.

In addition, Bob evaluates his bit strings

and computes their zero rates
(namely, the percentages of his “

” or “

” measurement results,

If both tests pass, Bob sets

,
and the protocol proceeds.

9.

Alice and Bob perform error correction
and privacy amplification to their secret INFO bits
X^{1},Y^{1}

in the standard way for BB84 protocols
(described, e.g., in

) to obtain their final secret keys.
We note that Alice and Bob generate another flag,
F^{\mathrm{ec}}
,
and they abort the protocol if
F^{\mathrm{ec}}=\emptyset

(see details in

);
however, if
F^{\mathrm{ec}}=\checkmark
, the protocol succeeds,
and Alice’s and Bob’s final secret keys are denoted by

K_{\mathrm{A}},K_{\mathrm{B}}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, respectively.

We point out that this is a very general protocol in the lossless qubit regime
because Alice’s emitted states

\{\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}\}

can be
any
states (assuming they are linearly dependent
and satisfy
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}>\frac{1}{2}
), even if they lie
inside a very general Hilbert space (which may be infinite-dimensional
or even continuous). Thus, for this security proof to apply, Alice and Bob must
first test their devices, perform a full quantum tomography of their emitted
states, and input the resulting states

\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}

to the security proof, as described in Section
2
.

Using pure states
\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}
does not hurt generality
because if Alice sends a mixed state, we can always assume that she also
sends the purifying system (which Eve intercepts and uses): this assumption
is only beneficial to Eve, so it makes our security proof stronger.

4
Security Proof

Our security proof is a generalized version of the rigorous, mostly self-contained
security proof presented by
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
for BB84-based protocols.
That security proof uses entropic uncertainty relations to derive
a key rate formula in the finite-key regime, showing a reduction
from the prepare-and-measure protocol to an entanglement-based protocol.
Here, we generalize this approach to apply to our practical protocol
(in the qubit regime) described in Section
3
.

4.1
Equivalent Modified Entanglement-Based Protocol

We begin our security proof by performing a reduction
to the following modified entanglement-based protocol.
We point out that this protocol does not strictly adhere to standard definitions
of “entanglement-based” protocols because it requires Alice to prepare
a specific entangled state, measure some portions of it, and send other portions
to Bob (which Eve can attack). Therefore, it is similar to prepare-and-measure
protocols. Nevertheless, this protocol is entanglement-based
in the narrowest sense because it allows Alice
to delay her measurements (on some portions of her state)
and relies on the resulting entanglement for proving security.

Therefore, we call it a “modified entanglement-based protocol”,
and it is defined as follows:

1.

Alice and Bob publicly agree on the parameters
of the protocol:

•

\{\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}\}

(identical between all rounds
i
)
that can reside in any arbitrary Hilbert space but must be linearly dependent
(and, therefore, must span a two-dimensional Hilbert subspace).
Specifically, we denote

\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}=a\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}+b\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}},

(2)

where
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
. We require
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}>\frac{1}{2}

4.2
Security Proof for the Modified
Entanglement-Based Protocol

Our security proof is a generalization of Section 6 of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017

(which proves security of an entanglement-based version
of BB84
Bennett and Brassard 1984
;
Ekert 1991
;
Bennett et al. 1992
),
requiring a few modifications of their proof.

The proof of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
is based on an
entropic uncertainty relation

which, roughly speaking, links two quantities:
the smooth min-entropy of Alice’s data conditioned on Eve’s data
(denoted
H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{E})
)
and the smooth max-entropy of Alice’s data conditioned on Bob’s data
(denoted
H_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{B})
).
Generally speaking, these entropies are measures of
uncertainty
:
they capture the number of bits in Alice’s system
\mathrm{A}

that are unknown to either Eve or Bob, respectively.
Intuitively (and imprecisely),
the smooth min-entropy
H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{E})
describes
the number of secret bits that can be
extracted
from Alice’s system
\mathrm{A}

and will be completely secret even from Eve
(or from anyone that has Eve’s system
\mathrm{E}
),
while the smooth max-entropy
H_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{B})
describes
the number of
extra
information bits that Bob will have to receive
from Alice if he wants to have full information on her system
\mathrm{A}

(which is roughly equivalent to asking how much information Alice would need
to send to Bob during the error correction procedure).

Roughly speaking, the entropic uncertainty relation used by
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017

shows a lower bound on

H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{E})+H_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{B})
.
Then, their proof upper-bounds
H_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{B})

by bounding the error rate between Alice and Bob using a law of large numbers,
which shows it is unlikely that the error rate on TEST bits is less than
\delta

while the error rate on INFO bits
is more than
\delta+\nu
. (Intuitively,
the smoothness parameter
\epsilon
means that we do not necessarily use
the original quantum state given as an input to the entropy,
but we may use any quantum state up to distance
\epsilon
from it.
In our case, for example,
\epsilon^{2}
represents the maximal probability
that the law of large numbers is violated—namely, the maximal probability
that the true error rate in the INFO bits is much higher
than the error rate observed in the TEST bits.
Using the smooth min- and max-entropy allows us to upper-bound the impact
of this unwanted possibility.)
The combination of these two results implies
a lower bound on
H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{E})
,
and this lower bound immediately gives us the protocol’s key rate using
the Leftover Hashing Lemma
Renner 2008

(which intuitively says that roughly
H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{E})
bits,
known to Alice and completely secret from Eve, can be extracted from Alice’s system
using a standard procedure of privacy amplification).

We mainly modify the first two parts of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
’s proof:
the entropic uncertainty relation
and the use of the law of large numbers.
In addition, we need to justify security of our sifting step.

4.2.1
The Sifting Step

The sifting step of our modified entanglement-based protocol
does not appear in the entanglement-based protocol
of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
. Therefore, we must prove that it does not hurt security
by showing that it keeps Alice’s and Bob’s basis choice strings

\Phi_{\mathrm{A}},\Phi_{\mathrm{B}}
independent of the other systems.

Each bit of
\Phi_{\mathrm{A}},\Phi_{\mathrm{B}}
is chosen independently
(with probabilities
p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}
,
p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}
,
p^{\prime\mathrm{B}}_{z}
, and
p^{\prime\mathrm{B}}_{x}
,
respectively). Moreover, the state that Alice generates for each round is as follows:

\lvert{\Psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}\mathrm{B}_{i}}\triangleq\frac{\lvert{0}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}+\lvert{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\lvert{\xi_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}+\sqrt{2T-1}\lvert{\xi_{-}}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}}{\sqrt{2T}},

(12)

so we observe that if
(\Phi_{\mathrm{A}})_{i}=0

(namely, if Alice chooses the “
z
basis”),
Alice obtains the “
0
” and “
1
” results
with equal conditional probabilities (
\frac{1}{2}
);
and if
(\Phi_{\mathrm{A}})_{i}=1
(namely, if Alice chooses the “
x

Alice obtains the “
\xi_{+}
” result with conditional probability
\frac{1}{2T}

and obtains the “
\xi_{-}
” result with conditional probability
\frac{2T-1}{2T}

We conclude the following:

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(0)={\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{2},

(13)

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(\xi_{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}\cdot\frac{1}{2T}=\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}}{2T},

(14)

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(\xi_{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}\cdot\frac{2T-1}{2T}=\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}(2T-1)}{2T}.

(15)

To justify the sifting step, we notice that this probabilistic process
can equivalently be described as the following two-stage process:

1.

First, for each round

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(D_{i}=1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(\xi_{-})=\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}(2T-1)}{2T},

(16)

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(D_{i}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(0)+{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(1)+{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(\xi_{+})=2\cdot\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{2}+\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}}{2T}=p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}+\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}}{2T}.

(17)

Remember that we define
\Omega\triangleq\{1\leq i\leq M^{\prime}\mid D_{i}=0\}

as the set of non-discarded rounds.

2.

Then, for each round

in
\Omega

(each non-discarded round), Alice determines the basis:

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\left[(\Phi_{\mathrm{A}})_{i}=0\mid D_{i}=0\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(0)+{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(1)}{{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(D_{i}=0)}=\frac{2\cdot\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{2}}{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}+\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}}{2T}}=\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}+\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}}{2T}},

(18)

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\left[(\Phi_{\mathrm{A}})_{i}=1\mid D_{i}=0\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(\xi_{+})}{{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(D_{i}=0)}=\frac{\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}}{2T}}{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{z}+\frac{p^{\prime\mathrm{A}}_{x}}{2T}}.

(19)

These probabilities are
independent
between the rounds:
namely, the basis is determined independently for each non-discarded round.

Note that this equivalence is only correct with respect to the

and

4.2.2
Entropic Uncertainty Relation

The security proof in
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
uses the uncertainty relation described
in Proposition 4 of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
, which is equivalent to Corollary 7.4
of
Tomamichel 2012
. This uncertainty relation
uses the symmetry of the BB84 protocol with respect to the chosen basis
of the INFO bits: namely, it uses the property that each INFO bit in BB84
is measured in a uniformly random basis, independently of the TEST bits’ results
and bases. Since a similar property does not apply to our protocol
(because all our INFO bits are measured in the “
z
basis”),
we must use a different uncertainty relation.

Adopting an approach similar to
Tomamichel et al. 2012
,
we use Theorem 7.2 of
Tomamichel 2012
as
the generalized entropic uncertainty relation
(using slightly different notations compared to
Tomamichel 2012
):

{Theorem}

(Theorem 7.2 of
Tomamichel 2012
)
Given
\epsilon\geq 0
and a non-normalized state
\rho_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{S}}

over the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{S}}
,
and given two generalized measurement operators

M_{1}=\{M_{1}^{x}\}_{x}~,~M_{2}=\{M_{2}^{x^{\prime}}\}_{x^{\prime}}

on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}
and a projective measurement
\{P^{p}\}_{p}

on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}
, the two post-measurement states

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{X}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x,p}\lvert{x}\rangle_{\mathrm{X}}{\langle{x}\rvert}_{\mathrm{X}}\otimes\lvert{p}\rangle_{\mathrm{P}}{\langle{p}\rvert}_{\mathrm{P}}\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{S}}\left(M_{1}^{x}P^{p}\rho_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{S}}P^{p}(M_{1}^{x})^{\dagger}\right),

(20)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{S}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime},p}\lvert{x^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathrm{X}^{\prime}}{\langle{x^{\prime}}\rvert}_{\mathrm{X}^{\prime}}\otimes\lvert{p}\rangle_{\mathrm{P}}{\langle{p}\rvert}_{\mathrm{P}}\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{R}}\left(M_{2}^{x^{\prime}}P^{p}\rho_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{S}}P^{p}(M_{2}^{x^{\prime}})^{\dagger}\right)

(21)

satisfy the following inequality (uncertainty relation):

H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{X}|\mathrm{P}\mathrm{R})_{\sigma}+H_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{X}^{\prime}|\mathrm{P}\mathrm{S})_{\sigma^{\prime}}\geq\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{c_{\mathrm{P}}}\right),

(22)

where:

c_{\mathrm{P}}\triangleq\max_{p,x,x^{\prime}}\left|\left|M_{1}^{x}P^{p}(M_{2}^{x^{\prime}})^{\dagger}\right|\right|_{\infty}^{2}.

(23)

{proof}

Proved in Subsection 7.3.2 of
Tomamichel 2012
as Theorem 7.2.

The next proposition will give us a similar result to Corollary 5 of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017

(namely, roughly speaking, a lower bound on the sum

H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{E})+H_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{B})
),
with one important difference:
the first term
H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{A}|\mathrm{E})
will still refer
to the real QKD protocol (the modified entanglement-based protocol
described in Subsection
4.1
),
but the second term

4.2.3
The Law of Large Numbers

Intuitively, the proof of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
uses a law of large numbers
(Lemma 6 of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
) to upper-bound the max-entropy

H_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{X}|\mathrm{Y})
—namely, the max-entropy of Alice’s raw key
conditioned on Bob’s raw key in the
real
protocol.
However, in our proof, we need
to bound
H_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{X}^{\prime 1}|\mathrm{Y}^{\prime 1})
—namely,
the max-entropy of Alice’s raw key conditioned on Bob’s raw key
in a
hypothetical
protocol where Alice and Bob measure the INFO bits
in the conjugate (“
x
”) basis.
For obtaining this bound, we need to apply the law of large numbers
twice

to two different hypothetical protocols:

1.

In the
X-X hypothetical protocol
(where both Alice and Bob measure
the INFO bits in the “
x
basis”), we can compare the TEST-X-X bits
(where the only non-discarded rounds are those where Alice measured
“
\xi_{+}
”) to the INFO bits. This way, we can discover the error rate
on the “
\xi_{+}
” bits.

2.

For finding the error rate on the “
\xi_{-}
” bits,
we use the
Z-X hypothetical protocol
,
where Alice measures the INFO bits in the “
z
basis”
while Bob measures them in the “
x
basis”.
The following intuitive formula will give us the needed bound:

{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{A}_{i}=-~,~\mathrm{B}_{i}=+)~=~{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{A}_{i}=0~,~\mathrm{B}_{i}=+)+{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{A}_{i}=1~,~\mathrm{B}_{i}=+)-{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{A}_{i}=+~,~\mathrm{B}_{i}=+).

(29)

This formula is intuitively trivial because it follows from the following formula:

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{A}_{i}=-~,~\mathrm{B}_{i}=+)+{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{A}_{i}=+~,~\mathrm{B}_{i}=+)~~=~~{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{B}_{i}=+)

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{A}_{i}=0~,~\mathrm{B}_{i}=+)+{\operatorname{Pr}}(\mathrm{A}_{i}=1~,~\mathrm{B}_{i}=+).

Formally, it follows from the independence of Alice’s and Bob’s operations,
as elaborated in the “bounding the fourth probability” portion of the proof of
Proposition
4.2.3
below.

This idea can be compared with
Pereira et al. 2020
’s analysis
of the “loss tolerant” protocol (improving on the usual analysis which involves
matrix computations
Tamaki et al. 2014
;
Xu et al. 2015
;
Mizutani et al. 2015
;
Pereira et al. 2019
),
but their analysis is more complicated and has several free parameters.
Here, we present a full and precise analysis, leading to an explicit key rate formula
in the single-qubit regime.

Formally, we use the following law of large numbers (Lemma 6 of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
):

Lemma
\thetheorem

(Lemma 6 of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
)
Given a set of
N
random variables
Z=(Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{N})
, where
each
Z_{i}
takes values in
\{0,1\}
and

,
and given an
independent
, a uniformly distributed subset

{\operatorname{Pr}}\left[\sum_{i\in\Pi}Z_{i}\leq a\delta~\wedge~\sum_{i\in\overline{\Pi}}Z_{i}\geq b\cdot(\delta+\nu)\right]\leq e^{-\frac{2ba^{2}\nu^{2}}{(a+b)(a+1)}}.

(31)

{proof}

Proved in Subsection 6.3 of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
as Lemma 6.

We also use another law of large numbers,
proved in Section 2 of
Hoeffding 1963
as Theorem 1:

Lemma
\thetheorem

Let

be independent random variables
with finite first and second moments,
such that

for all
1\leq i\leq N

If

is the expected value
of
\overline{Z}
, then for any
\nu>0
,

{\operatorname{Pr}}\left[\overline{Z}-\mu\geq\nu\right]\leq e^{-2N\nu^{2}}.

(32)

4.3

to the Modified Entanglement-Based Protocol

Intuitively, to prove security of our original prepare-and-measure protocol

where the equality between the two expressions in Equation (

can be shown algebraically using Equations (

Then, Alice either measures her subsystem

in the standard (“

Formally, we use an adapted version of the reduction in Section 9 of

First, given the parameters of the original prepare-and-measure protocol

The parameters

Given the parameters

Given the parameters

Given the parameters

Formally, the differences between the protocols are settled as follows:

•

The modified entanglement-based protocol includes the possibility of

\xi_{-}

For this, we use the explanation in Subsubsection

to divide the process into two stages (again, this division works with respect
to the probability distribution,

to the actual quantum operations):
stage
1
, where Alice determines which rounds are

2
, where Alice determines the basis

As explained in Subsubsection
4.2.1
,
stage

is in fact independent of the bases
used for the non-discarded rounds; furthermore, the results
of stage

are promptly communicated to Eve,
who can devise her attack accordingly.
Meanwhile, stage
2
is completely identical between
the two protocols, as shown in the next item.

•

Alice’s preparation
is different between the two protocols:

In the prepare-and-measure protocol,
Alice randomly chooses
\Phi_{\mathrm{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}

(where each bit, independently, is

,

\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}
, or
\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}
with probabilities

\frac{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{2}
,
\frac{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{2}

In the modified entanglement-based protocol, Alice generates the following

\lvert{\Psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}\mathrm{B}_{i}}
for each round

\lvert{\Psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}\mathrm{B}_{i}}\triangleq\frac{\lvert{0}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}+\lvert{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\lvert{\xi_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}+\sqrt{2T-1}\lvert{\xi_{-}}\rangle_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}_{i}}}{\sqrt{2T}},

randomly chooses the measurement bases
\Phi_{\mathrm{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{M^{\prime}}

1

(In fact, Alice’s measurement is delayed to Step

As explained in Subsubsection

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(0)={\operatorname{Pr}}_{\mathrm{A}_{i}}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\frac{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}+2Tp^{\mathrm{A}}_{x}}}{\frac{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}+2Tp^{\mathrm{A}}_{x}}+\frac{\frac{2Tp^{\mathrm{A}}_{x}}{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}+2Tp^{\mathrm{A}}_{x}}}{2T}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}+p^{\mathrm{A}}_{x}}=\frac{p^{\mathrm{A}}_{z}}{2},

•

Eve’s attack
is slightly different between the two protocols:
on the prepare-and-measure protocol, it is applied to the

(including the discarded rounds) when Eve knows

We need to prove that any attack that Eve applies
to the
m

(namely, to the
m

This is indeed true because in the modified entanglement-based protocol,

and therefore, she knows exactly which rounds are included in

This means that any attack by Eve on the
m
rounds of the prepare-and-measure
protocol is a completely legitimate and valid attack on the
m

\Sigma

of the modified entanglement-based protocol, and it gives the same outputs
in both protocols.

•

The rest of the steps in the prepare-and-measure protocol
(Steps

From the above, we can deduce that any attack by Eve on the prepare-and-measure

This conclusion only applies when we condition on

(for the prepare-and-measure protocol)

Corollary
\thetheorem

If the modified entanglement-based protocol is secure with

the prepare-and-measure protocol is secure with the same security parameter.

5
Necessity of the Restriction to Three Source States

In our protocol, similarly to the “loss tolerant”
protocol
Tamaki et al. 2014
;
Xu et al. 2015
;
Mizutani et al. 2015
;
Pereira et al. 2019
,
only three source states are used.
This restriction is necessary in the imperfect-generation regime,
as we briefly explain below.

Let us assume that our protocol emits
four
input states (similarly to BB84),
denoted
\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle
.
For standard security analysis to work, the following conditions is required
for some
0<p<1
and
0<q<1
:

p\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{0}}\rvert}+(1-p)\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{1}}\rvert}=q\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{+}}\rvert}+(1-q)\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{-}}\rvert},

(83)

which means that Alice sends to Bob
identical
mixed states
in each round of the protocol, independently of the chosen basis.
(Otherwise, Eve may gain information on the basis and attack differently
on each basis, which refutes the crucial possibility of comparing her attack’s
influence on different bases.)

For meeting the above condition,
we obviously need
\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle
and
\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle
to be in the
two-dimensional Hilbert subspace spanned by
\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle
and
\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle
.
Therefore, we require (for some
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
):

\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle=a\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle+b\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle~~~,~~~\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle=c\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle+d\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle.

(84)

Substituting this into Equation (
83
), we obtain the following:

\displaystyle q\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{+}}\rvert}+(1-q)\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{-}}\rvert}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q\cdot[a\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle+b\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle]\cdot[a^{\star}{\langle{\gamma_{0}}\rvert}+b^{\star}{\langle{\gamma_{1}}\rvert}]

(85)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(1-q)\cdot[c\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle+d\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle]\cdot[c^{\star}{\langle{\gamma_{0}}\rvert}+d^{\star}{\langle{\gamma_{1}}\rvert}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[q\cdot|a|^{2}+(1-q)\cdot|c|^{2}]\cdot\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{0}}\rvert}+[q\cdot ab^{\star}+(1-q)\cdot cd^{\star}]\cdot\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{1}}\rvert}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle[q\cdot a^{\star}b+(1-q)\cdot c^{\star}d]\cdot\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{0}}\rvert}+[q\cdot|b|^{2}+(1-q)\cdot|d|^{2}]\cdot\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle{\langle{\gamma_{1}}\rvert}.~~~~~~~

We thus obtain the following conditions for equality between
Equations (
83
) and (
85
):

\displaystyle q\cdot|a|^{2}+(1-q)\cdot|c|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p,

(86)

\displaystyle q\cdot|b|^{2}+(1-q)\cdot|d|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-p,

(87)

\displaystyle q\cdot ab^{\star}+(1-q)\cdot cd^{\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(88)

\displaystyle q\cdot a^{\star}b+(1-q)\cdot c^{\star}d

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(89)

The two last equations are the complex conjugates of one another,
so one of them is sufficient.

Therefore, for standard security proofs to work, we require very stringent
conditions on
a,b,c,d
. In particular,
according to Equation (
89
), we require

q=-\frac{c^{\star}d}{a^{\star}b-c^{\star}d}=\frac{c^{\star}d}{c^{\star}d-a^{\star}b},

(90)

and for
q
to be real (and satisfy
0<q<1
), the complex phases of

c^{\star}d
and
a^{\star}b
must be
opposite

(namely, they must differ by
\pm\pi
,
which is equivalent to having opposite signs).

This requirement seriously restricts the possible values on

\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle
.
In particular, if we assume (without loss of generality) that
a
and
c

are real and non-negative,
it requires
b
and
d
to have opposite phases. Namely,

\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle=|a|\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle+|b|e^{i\phi}\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle~~~,~~~\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle=|c|\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle-|d|e^{i\phi}\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle,

(91)

where
|c|
and
|d|
are dictated by
|a|
and
|b|
, respectively
(see Equations (
86
) and (
87
)).

The above analysis means that
\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle
is, in fact, completely determined
by the choice of
\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle

(because
|c|
,
|d|
, and
\phi
can all be inferred from
\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle
).
From a realistic point of view, this means that a four-state protocol
measured with two bases could be
practically insecure
whenever
a slight deviation of
\lvert{\gamma_{-}}\rangle
(or of the states

\lvert{\gamma_{0}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{1}}\rangle,\lvert{\gamma_{+}}\rangle
which determine it)
causes the protocol to violate the conditions
of Equations (
86
)–(
89
).
Essentially, this means that in the presence of source imperfections,
the use of at most three states (or, alternatively,
measurements in three or more bases, which we do not explore here)
is
required
for practical security,
and the use of four states could lead to practical security issues.

6
Conclusions

To sum up, we have found a new way to analyse
the security of practical QKD protocols
by generalizing the results of
Tomamichel and Leverrier 2017
to more practical protocols
(using a modified entropic uncertainty relation and
a refined analysis of finite-key statistics).
Our proof, compared with other proofs, is rigorous, careful,
and simple, aiming to make it easy-to-use in the lossless qubit regime
(its extension to losses and decoy states is left for future research
because they present specific hurdles in this analysis regime:
in particular, losses would need to be declared by Eve
in the modified entanglement-based protocol, which could complicate the analysis).
We believe that our suggested tools can contribute to benchmarking and certifying
the security of practical implementations of QKD.
