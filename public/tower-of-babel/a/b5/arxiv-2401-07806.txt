Optimal Design for Linear Models via Gradient Flow
Ruhui Jin, Martin Guerra, Qin Li, Stephen Wright

Abstract
Optimal experimental design (OED) aims to choose the observations in an experiment to be as informative as possible, according to certain statistical criteria. In the linear case (when the observations depend linearly on the unknown parameters), it seeks the optimal weights over rows of the design matrix 𝐀 \mathbf{A} under certain criteria. Classical OED assumes a discrete design space and thus a design matrix with finite dimensions. In many practical situations, however, the design space is continuous-valued, so that the OED problem is one of optimizing over a continuous-valued design space. The objective becomes a functional over the probability measure, instead of over a finite dimensional vector. This change of perspective requires a new set of techniques that can handle optimizing over probability measures, and Wasserstein gradient flow becomes a natural candidate. Both the first-order criticality and the convexity properties of the OED objective are presented. Computationally Monte Carlo particle simulation is deployed to formulate the main algorithm. This algorithm is applied to two elliptic inverse problems.

The problem of inferring unknown parameters from measurements is ubiquitous in real-world engineering contexts, such as biological chemistry

The need to collect informative data economically gives rise to the area of optimal experimental design (OED)

We denote by

\theta\in\Omega

\Omega

A naïve strategy to handle the continuous-valued design space is to discretize
\Omega

m

\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, defined a priori, thus reducing the infinite-in-size problem to the finite classical setting. In
[
11

1.2
Our contributions

The main contribution of this paper is a computational framework for solving OED over a continuous design space. Inspired by recent developments in optimal transport, we define a gradient flow scheme for optimizing a smooth probability distribution driven by the OED objective on the Wasserstein metric. We use Monte-Carlo particle approximation to translate the continuum flow of probability measure into gradient-descent flow for the finite set of sample particles, whose evolution captures the dynamics of the underlying infinite-dimensional flow. This evolution can be characterized by a coupled system of ordinary differential equations (ODE). We investigate theoretical aspects of the proposed technique, including convexity, criticality conditions, computation of Fréchet derivatives, and convergence error with respect to key hyperparameters in the particle gradient flow algorithm. Finally, we apply our approach to two problems, with one from medical imaging: the linearized electrical impedance tomography (EIT) and the other related to inverse Darcy flow. The experimental design produced by our algorithm provides informative guidance for sensor placement.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We prepare for the technical background on the OED problem and the gradient flow technique in

Section

5
and
Section

6
, respectively. The second test on 1D Darcy flow is described in
Section

7
.

2.1
Optimal experimental design

To introduce the classical OED setup, we consider the linear regression model:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}.

(1)

The number of measurements is
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, with observations collected in the vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

The (linear) forward observation map is encoded in the matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}~(m\gg d)
, with random noise contributions in the vector
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We wish to infer the parameters
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For the vastly overdetermined system (
1
), an estimate of
\mathbf{x}^{*}
can be obtained without requiring access to the full map
\mathbf{A}
. The aim of OED is to identify a combination of measurements that enables accurate yet economical recovery. Specifically, since each row of the system (
1
) represents an
experiment
, we seek a vector
w=(w_{1},w_{2},\dotsc,w_{m})^{\top}
whose components represent the
weights
that we assign to each experiment, that solves the following problem:

\min_{w}\,F[w]\;\;\mbox{subject to}\;\;w\geq 0,\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}=1.

(2)

The function
F:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
represents certain design criterion, with smaller objective values of
F
implying better design.

Many statistical criteria have been proposed for OED.
We present the two most commonly used standards
[
47
]
, denote by the letters “A” (for “average”) and “D” (for “determinant”).
They follow from an explanation in terms of Bayesian inference
[
43
,
2
]
.

It is well known that the optimal inference result for (
1
) (under the
\ell_{2}
metric) that makes use of all data is

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{y}.

When the noise vector is assumed to follow an i.i.d. Gaussian distribution, that is,

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m})
, the variance matrix of the solution
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
is

\displaystyle\text{var}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\big)^{-1}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}]\mathbf{A}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}.

The above calculation shows that the inference “uncertainty” depends on the property of the data matrix through the term
(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
. Smaller variances indicate lower levels of uncertainty and a more accurate reconstruction. We can modify this variance matrix by weighting the experiments using the weights
w
, leading to the following weighted inverse variance
[
16
, Section 7.5]
:

\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[w]:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}\,\mathbf{A}(k,:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(k,:).

(3)

The OED problem chooses
w
to minimize a scalar function of the inverse of this weighted variance matrix. The A- and D- design criteria are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\text{A-optimal:}\quad\min F^{A}[w]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[w]\big)^{-1},

(4a)

\displaystyle\text{D-optimal:}\quad\max F^{D}[w]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\log\big(\det\big( \mA^\top\mA[w ]\big)\big).

(4b)

Remark 1
.

Note that the design criteria (
4a
) and (
4b
) solely follow the Bayesian inference strategy. Note that the problem of selecting the optimal
w
depends only on the map
\mathbf{A}
, and is independent of the true underlying parameters
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, the measurements
\mathbf{y}
, and the errors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
.

Remark 2
.

In some scenarios, one would further like the weight vector
w
to be sparse, so that only a small number of experiments are chosen. One well studied approach deploys the classical concept of leverage score
[
30
,
51
]
.

2.2
Wasserstein gradient flow

We describe here the basics of gradient flow
[
6
]
and related methods.
Analogous to gradient descent in Euclidean space, gradient flow optimizes a probability measure objective by defining a flow in the variable space based on a gradient of the objective function. Proper metricization of the space is a critical issue. In this regard, we leverage significant advances in optimal transport
[
63
,
33
]
and Wasserstein gradient flow
[
45
,
6
]
, reviewed below.

We require the class of probability measures
\rho
to have bounded second moments, that is,

\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)=\left\{\rho~~\big|~~\rho(\theta)\geq 0~~\forall\theta\in\Omega,~~\int_{\Omega}\differential\rho(\theta)=1,~~\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}\differential\rho(\theta)<\infty\right\}\,.

(5)

Note that the probability distribution
\rho
is not necessarily absolute continuous.
Dirac delta functions can be used, enabling practical computations. It is natural to equip the
\mathbf{Pr}_{2}
space (
5
) with the Wasserstein-2 metric to measure the distances between probability distributions.
For this purpose, we define the joint probability measure
\gamma:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and the set
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
to be the space of joint probability measures whose first and second marginals are
\mu\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
and
\nu\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
, respectively.

Definition 1
.

Given the domain
\Omega,
the Wasserstein-
2
distance between two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
is defined as

W_{2}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\left(\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\|x-y\|^{2}\differential\gamma(x,y)\right)^{1/2},

(6)

where the norm in the integrand is the Euclidean
\ell_{2}
-distance in
\Omega
.

For a given objective functional
F:\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, we pose the optimization problem

\min_{\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F[\rho]\,.

From an initial guess for
\rho
, we seek a path in this variable along which
F
decreases, by making use of the gradient of
F
.
The special structure of
\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
manifold requires care in the definition of the “gradient”.
Under the Wassertein-
2
metric, this gradient is

\nabla_{W_{2}}F[\rho]=-\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\rho\,\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\right),

(7)

where
\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
is the Fréchet derivative derived on the
L_{2}
function space and the operation
\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\rho\nabla_{\theta}\right)
defines a “projection” of the
L_{2}
motion onto the probability measure space.
By descending along the negative of this gradient, we obtain the Wasserstein gradient flow of
F
:

\partial_{t}\rho=-\nabla_{W_{2}}F[\rho]=\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\rho\,\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\right).

(8)

3.1
Continuous optimal design

In the continuous setting, the
m\times d
matrix
\mathbf{A}
of (
1
) is replaced by an object that we call the “continuous experiment/data matrix” with
d
columns but “row” space indexed by
\theta\in\Omega
, so that
\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)
is a real row vector with
d
elements. We also define a “continuous” counterpart of the weighted matrix product of (
3
):

\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\differential\rho(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

(9)

Accordingly, following the A- and D-optimal discrete design in (
4a
) and (
4b
), we arrive at the corresponding criteria in the continuous context:

\displaystyle\text{A-optimal:}\quad\rho^{*}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F^{A}[\rho]\equiv\text{Tr}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1},

(10)

\displaystyle\text{D-optimal:}\quad\rho^{*}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F^{D}[\rho]\equiv\log\big(\det\big( \mA^\top\mA[\rho]\big)\big).

(11)

These definitions require the continuous experiment/data matrix
\mathbf{A}
to have full column rank
d
. This is a weak assumption because the continuous design space
\Omega
would generally not have uniform dependencies among the components of
\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)
for all
\theta\in\Omega
.

To apply Wasserstein gradient flow (
8
)
1
1

1

Note that D-optimal design formulates a maximization problem (
11
). Contrary to A-optimal design, the associated gradient-flow follows in the ascending direction of the gradient. Consequently, the sign in (
8
) should be flipped.
, we need to prepare Fréchet derivatives of the OED objectives.
These are defined in the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that the continuous experiment/data matrix
\mathbf{A}
defined in this section is full rank. The Fréchet derivatives for (
10
) and (
11
) are

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)=-\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F^{D}[\rho]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)=\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\,.

(13)

Proof.

Given a probability measure
\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
and a perturbation
\delta\rho:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. To get the Fréchet derivative
\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F(\theta)}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta=\text{the~first-order~approximation~to~}F[\rho+\delta\rho]-F[\rho].

(14)

For the A-optimal criterion (
10
), we have

\displaystyle F^{A}[\rho+\delta\rho]-F^{A}[\rho]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho+\delta\rho]\big)^{-1}-\text{Tr}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]+\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\big)^{-1}-\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(-\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\big)\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1}\Big).

Following more derivations, we obtain

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(-\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\big)\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1}\Big)

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(-\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-2}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(-\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\text{Tr}\Big(-\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\Big)\,\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}-\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\,\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F^{A}(\theta)}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta.

The above results make use of the fact that the trace of matrix products is commutative (lines 2 and 5) and also that the trace and integration operations are interchangeable (line 4).
From the last two equations above, we obtain the derivative of A-design:

\frac{\delta F^{A}(\theta)}{\delta\rho}=-\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}.

A similar derivation holds for the D-optimal objective (
11
). We consider

F^{D}[\rho+\delta\rho]-F^{D}[\rho]=\log\Big(\det\big( \mA^\top\mA[\rho+ \delta\rho]\big)\big)-\log\big(\det\big( \mA^\top\mA[\rho]\big)\Big).

The linear approximation to the first term on RHS is

\displaystyle\log\Big(\det\big( \mA^\top\mA[\rho+ \delta\rho]\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\log\Big(\det\big( \mA^\top\mA[\rho]\big) + \det\big( \mA^\top\mA[\rho]\Big) \, \tr\Big((\mA^\top\mA[\rho])^{-1}\mA^\top\mA[\delta\rho]\big) \Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\Big(\det\big( \mA^\top\mA[\rho]\big)\Big)+\frac{1}{\text{Det}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)}\text{Det}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)\,\text{Tr}\Big((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\Big(\det\big( \mA^\top\mA[\rho]\big)\Big)+\text{Tr}\big((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\big).

(The second line is due to the Jacobi’s formula for derivative of matrix determinant, while the third line is by applying the derivative of log function.)
We then have the linear difference term for
F^{D}
:

\displaystyle F^{D}[\rho+\delta\rho]-F^{D}[\rho]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-1}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\,\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F^{D}(\theta)}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta.

(The second step above is similar to the penultimate equality in (
15
).)
We finally obtain the D design derivative:

\frac{\delta F^{D}(\theta)}{\delta\rho}=\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\big)^{-1}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}.

∎

3.2
Particle gradient flow

Proposition

1
in combination with (
8
) defines the gradient flow for finding the OED probability measure over the design space
\Omega
.
Classical techniques for solving this PDE formulation involve discretizing
\Omega
and tracing the evolution of
\rho
on the resulting mesh.
This strategy presents a computational challenge: The size of the mesh (or equivalently, the degrees of freedom required to represent
\rho
in the discrete setting) grows exponentially with the dimension of the design space.
The computational complexity required to implement this strategy would exceed the experiment budget, in terms of the optimized weighting object size and total measurements.

One advantage of employing the Wasserstein gradient flow is its close relationship to a particle ODE interpretation
[
21
,
14
,
25
]
.
We can use Monte Carlo to represent the probability measure
\rho
by a particle samples on the design space
\Omega
.
This simulation translates the PDE into a coupled ODE system on the sample vector
\theta\in\Omega
.
Following the descending trajectory of (
8
), when
\rho
is known, the characteristic of this PDE is

\frac{\differential\theta}{\differential t}=-\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}(\theta).

(16)

In computation, the distribution
\rho
is unknown,
but we can use an empirical measure for the approximation to
\rho
.
Given a fixed number of particles
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},
we consider a set of particles
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\Omega.
The estimated probability
\rho
is the average of Dirac-delta measures at selected particles
\rho_{N}
, that is,

\rho\approx\rho_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\theta_{i}}\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega).

(17)

The distribution
\rho_{N}
approximates the continuous range of
\Omega
better than a costly setup in which discretization is predefined in
Section

3.2
. By inserting the empirical measure (
17
) into (
16
), and employing forward-Euler time integration, we arrive at the following particle gradient flow method.

Remark 4

\displaystyle-\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\Big)^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top},

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F^{D}[\rho_{N}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)

4.1
First-order critical condition

We first specify the stationary point condition under the
W_{2}
metric.

Lemma 1
.

The distribution
\rho^{\ast}\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
is a stationary solution to
\min_{\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}
or
\max_{\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F[\rho]
— that is,
\partial_{t}\rho^{\ast}=0
— if it satisfies the following first-order critical condition:

\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho^{*}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)=0,\quad\forall~\theta\in\text{\rm supp}(\rho^{*}).

(20)

Proof.

We present the proof for the minimization problem. (The proof for maximization is similar.)
In the Wasserstein flow of
\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
, the differential of the objective
F
is

\frac{\differential F[\rho]}{\differential t}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)\,\partial_{t}\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta.

(21)

When we substitute for
\partial_{t}\rho
from (
8
), we obtain

\frac{\differential F[\rho]}{\differential t}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)\,\nabla_{\theta}\,\cdot\left(\rho\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\right)\differential\theta=-\int_{\Omega}\rho(\theta)\left|\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\right|^{2}\,\differential\theta\leq 0.

(22)

The last derivation is from the Green’s identity and the assumption that the velocity term
\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}
vanishes on the boundary
\partial\Omega.

First-order criticality conditions for
\rho^{*}
are
\frac{\differential F[\rho^{*}]}{\differential t}=0
. Hence equation (
22
) implies that the critical condition (
20
) is required for the integrand in (
22
) to be zero everywhere in
\Omega.

∎

In the
W_{2}
descent flow (
8
), the objective
F
keeps decreasing until
\rho
achieves stationarity.
(A similar claim applies to ascent in the maximization case.) The critical condition (
20
) does not give the explicit stationary measure
\rho^{*}
except in special cases, one of which we present now.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that in the continuous experiment/data matrix
\mathbf{A}
there are a set of values
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}\subset\Omega
such that the vectors
\{\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\}_{i=1}^{d}
are orthogonal, and in addition that

\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\perp\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\,,\quad\forall i\in[d].

(23)

Then the following form satisfies the A- (
10
) and D-optimal (
11
) design criteria:

\rho^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\delta_{\theta_{i}},\quad\text{s.t.}~\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}=1,~~\alpha_{i}>0,~\forall i\in[d].

(24)

Proof.

By rescaling the orthogonal rows
\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)
for
i\in[d]
we can define an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with rows
\mathbf{U}(i,:)
defined by

\mathbf{U}(i,:)=\frac{\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)}{\|\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\|},\quad\forall i\in[d].

(25)

For the sampled target by
\rho^{*}
, we obtain from this formula and (
3
) that

\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho^{*}]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)=\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U},

where
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a diagonal matrix with
i
th diagonal
\mathbf{C}(i,i)=\alpha_{i}\|\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\|^{2}
.
We thus have by orthogonality of
\mathbf{U}
that

(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho^{*}])^{-1}=\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{U},\quad(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho^{*}])^{-2}=\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-2}\mathbf{U}.

For
\theta_{i}\in\text{supp}(\rho^{*}),
the A-optimal derivative is

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho^{*}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho^{*}])^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)^{\top}

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-2}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)^{\top}.

Since
\{\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\}_{i=1}^{d}
have orthogonal rows, we have from (
25
) that

\mathbf{C}^{-2}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)^{\top}=\|\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\|\mathbf{C}^{-2}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}(i,:)^{\top}=\frac{\|\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\|}{\mathbf{C}(i,i)^{2}}\mathbf{e}_{i},

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the
i
-the unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By substituting into (
26
) and using (
25
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho^{*}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{i}\frac{\|\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\|}{\mathbf{C}(i,i)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\mathbf{U}(i,:)^{\top}\frac{\|\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\|}{\mathbf{C}(i,i)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)\mathbf{A}(\theta_{i},:)^{\top}\frac{1}{\mathbf{C}(i,i)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

where the final equality follows from (
23
).
A similar argument shows that
\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F^{D}[\rho^{*}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta_{i})=0
.

Since the gradient of the Fréchet derivative is
0
for all support points
\theta_{i}
,
\rho^{*}
satisfies the first-order criticality condition (
20
).
∎

4.2
Convexity of design objectives

Another feature of the OED problems (
10
)-(
11
) is that they are convex optimization problems in the
L_{2}
sense, as we explain next.

Proposition 4
.

For any probability measure
\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
that ensures invertibility of the matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]
defined in (
9
), the objective functionals for both A-optimal and D-optimal defined in (
10
) and (
11
) are second order differentiable. Moreover, the Hessian functionals
\text{Hess}~F^{A}[\rho]
and
\text{Hess}~F^{D}[\rho]
are semidefinite operators (positive and negative, respectively), so both problems are convex optimization problems in the
L_{2}
sense.

Proof.

Fix a probability distribution
\rho\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega),
we will explicitly compute the two Hessian terms. For any given two perturbation measures
\delta\rho_{1},\delta\rho_{2}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. The bilinear Hessian operator is computed by:

\displaystyle\text{Hess}~F[\rho](\delta\rho_{1},\delta\rho_{2})

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{(F[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho_{1}+\varepsilon\delta\rho_{2}]-F[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho_{2}])-(F[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho_{1}]-F[\rho])}{\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho_{2}]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho_{1}(\theta)\,\differential\theta-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho_{1}(\theta)\,\differential\theta\right)\,.

The last derivation is from the definition of Fréchet derivative (
14
).

For positive definiteness, we need
\text{Hess}~F[\rho](\delta\rho,\delta\rho)\geq 0
for all
\delta\rho
. Since the sign is retained when passing to the limit, we will study the first-order expansion of

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta\geq 0,\quad\forall~\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}.

(28)

Regarding (
9
), we define the following shorthand notation:

\mathbf{M}:=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\quad\mathbf{D}:=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

(29)

(Note that the theorem assumes positive definiteness of
\mathbf{M}
.)

We first study the A-optimal design objective (
10
). For the first term in (
28
), we deploy (
12
) from
Proposition

1
to obtain

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho])^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta.

Since the integrand is a scalar, it is equivalent to its trace value.
Thus the expression above is equal to

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\text{Tr}\big(\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho])^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\big)\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\text{Tr}\big((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho])^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\big)\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{Tr}\Big(\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho])^{-2}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{Tr}\Big((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho])^{-2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\delta\rho(\theta)\differential\theta\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]+\varepsilon\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho])^{-2}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\big).

The second line follows from the cyclic property of matrix product in the trace operation.
The order of trace and integration can be switched as both are linear operators (third line).
The last line follows from linear expansion of the
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
operator.

Using the matrix notation in (
29
), we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta=-\text{Tr}\left((\mathbf{M}+\varepsilon\mathbf{D})^{-2}\mathbf{D}\right).

From the first-order approximation

(\mathbf{M}+\varepsilon\mathbf{D})^{-2}\approx\mathbf{M}^{-2}-\varepsilon\mathbf{M}^{-2}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\varepsilon\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-2},

we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta

(30)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\text{Tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{M}^{-2}-\varepsilon\mathbf{M}^{-2}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\varepsilon\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-2}\right)\,\mathbf{D}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-2}\mathbf{D}-2\varepsilon\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\right),

which gives the first term of (
28
).
For the second term in (
28
), we have

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta=-\text{Tr}\big((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-2}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\delta\rho]\big)=-\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-2}\mathbf{D}\right).

(31)

By combining (
30
) and (
31
) into (
27
), we obtain the A-optimal Hessian formula:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Hess}~F^{A}[\rho](\delta\rho,\delta\rho)&=\displaystyle 2\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\right).\end{array}

(32)

We now prove this Hessian operator is positive semidefinite by proving nonnegativity of (
32
).
By definition, the matrix

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\rho(\theta)\differential\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

is positive definite, so
\mathbf{M}^{-1}
is also positive definite. It follows that for any vector
\mathbf{z}
, we have

\mathbf{z}^{T}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{z})^{\top}\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{z})\geq 0,

so
\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}
in (
32
) is positive semidefinite, as required.
The Hessian value (
32
) is non-negative, so
F^{A}[\cdot]
is a convex functional.

For
F^{D}[\cdot]
defined in (
11
), we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F^{D}[\rho+\varepsilon\delta\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta&\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left((\mathbf{M}+\varepsilon\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}\right)\\
&\displaystyle\approx\text{Tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\varepsilon\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{D}\right),\end{array}

(33)

and

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F^{D}[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\delta\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta=\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\right).

(34)

By combining (
33
) and (
34
), we obtain

\text{Hess}~F^{D}[\rho](\delta\rho,\delta\rho)=-\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\right).

Using an argument similar to the one for
F^{A}
, we can show that the D-optimal Hessian value

-\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\right)=-\text{Tr}\big((\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1/2})(\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1/2})\big)\leq 0,

for all
\delta\rho
, where the nonnegativity of the trace follows from symmetry of
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{M}
and thus of
\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}
.
Therefore, the D-optimal objective Hessian operator is negative semidefinite for measure
\rho
.
∎

We comment that the convexity shown above is presented in
L_{2}
metric: Both
\rho
and
\rho+\delta\rho
are elements in
L_{2}
function space.
Convexity in
L_{2}
does not imply convexity in
W_{2}
.
2
2

2

For two measures
u,v\in\mathbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega),
the
W_{2}
space considers the displacement convexity notion (Section 3 of
[

]
), i.e.:

F(T_{t}(\mu,\nu))\leq tF(\mu)+(1-t)F(\nu),

where the Wasserstein geodesic
T_{t}(\mu,\nu)
replaces the classical convex interpolation:
T_{t}(\mu,\nu)=t\mu+(1-t)\nu
.
Thus

cannot be applied directly to show that the Wasserstein gradient flow (

) drives
\rho
to its global minimum of OED objectives (

4.3
Particle gradient flow simulation error

We turn now from examining the properties of the continuous OED formulation to the performance of
Algorithm

1
. Multiple layers of numerical approximations are deployed in the algorithm, and rigorous convergence analysis is somewhat convoluted. Rather than present such an analysis fully, we identify the main source for the numerical error and provide a possible roadmap for a convergence analysis. We propose a conjecture about the convergence behavior, leaving detailed analysis to future research.

The error we aim to control is the difference between the global optimizer
\rho^{*}
, defined in (
10
) or (
11
), and the output of
Algorithm

1

\rho^{T}_{N,\differential t}
.
(We have added subscript
\differential t
to stress the dependence on time-discretization in the algorithm.)
Since Wasserstein provides a metric that honors the triangle inequality, we deduce that

W_{2}(\rho^{*},\rho^{T}_{N,\differential t})\leq W_{2}(\rho^{*},\rho^{T})+W_{2}(\rho^{T},\rho^{T}_{N})+W_{2}(\rho^{T}_{N},\rho^{T}_{N,\differential t}),

(35)

where
\rho^{T}
is the solution to the Wasserstein gradient flow (
8
) at time
T
and
\rho^{T}_{N}
denotes the particle approximation of
\rho^{T}
using (
17
). We expect all three terms are controllable under certain scenarios.

1.

When the problem is geodesically convex, we expect
\rho^{T}\to\rho^{*}
as
T\to\infty
. The nature of this convergence will be problem-dependent.

2.

Replacing
\rho
by
\rho_{N}
amounts to replacing the continuous-in-space PDE by a finite number of samples.
Intuitively, the more samples one pays to simulate the underlying flow, the more accurate the PDE solution becomes.
Rigorously evaluating the difference is the main theme of mean-field analysis
[
34
]
.
When the gradient flow is Lipschitz-smooth, it is expected that
\rho^{T}_{N}\xrightarrow{W_{2}}\rho^{T}
as
N\to\infty
, with a potential rate of

\mathbb{E}\,\left[W_{2}(\rho^{T},\rho^{T}_{N})\right]\sim O\left(\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\right)\,,

(36)

for
\alpha=\min\{2/\text{dim}(\Omega),1/2\}
. This convergence rate may be pessimistic in the sense that the rate is slow when
\text{dim}(\Omega)
is high.

3.

The discrepancy between
\rho^{T}_{N,\differential t}
and
\rho^{T}_{N}
is due to the discrete time stepping scheme.
Following standard analysis of Euler’s method
[
9
]
, the convergence is
\rho_{N,\differential t}^{T}\xrightarrow{W_{2}}\rho_{N}^{T},
as
\differential t\to 0
, with rate

W_{2}(\rho^{T}_{N},\rho^{T}_{N,\differential t})\sim O(\differential t)

(see
[
19
, p. 69]
).

A similar analysis could be conducted for metrics other than the
W_{2}
distance, such as TV norm or
\phi
-divergence (such as the KL divergence). We can also measure the weak convergence on a test function, possibly tightening the convergence in (
36
). For any given
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}
,
\langle\rho^{T}-\rho^{T}_{N},\psi\rangle\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
. We refer to
[
58
]
for relations between different metrics.

5
Optimal design model problem

We demonstrate the optimal design setup for the case of of electrical impedance tomography (EIT)
[
15
,
69
]
, a well-studied application from medical imaging.

5.1
EIT inverse problem and its linearization

The EIT experiment considers injection of a voltage into biological tissue and measurement of the electrical intensity on the surface (skin).
The problem is to infer the coefficient
\sigma
in an inhomogenous elliptic equation from boundary measurements (Dirichlet and Neumann).
It is typically assumed that the biological tissue is close to a ground-truth medium, so linearization
[
20
]
can be performed to recover the deviation from this ground-truth.
The linearized problem solves the following equation for
\sigma
:

\int_{\mathcal{D}}r_{\theta}(y)\,\sigma(y)\differential y=\text{data}_{\theta}\,,

(37)

where
r_{\theta}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is a representative function.
That is, when
r_{\theta}(y)
is tested on
\sigma(y)
, it produces the data on the right hand side.
The hope is that as one exhausts values of
\theta
, the testing function
r_{\theta}(y)
spans the entire space
L_{2}(\mathcal{D})
, and the Fredholm first-kind integral problem (
37
) yields a unique reconstruction of
\sigma(y)
in its dual space, which is also
L_{2}(\mathcal{D})
.

For this particular problem, the representative function
r_{\theta}(y)
can be written explicitly as

r_{\theta}(y)=\nabla_{y}u(\theta_{1},y)\cdot\nabla_{y}v(\theta_{2},y),

where
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
represents the design point and
u
and
v
solve the following forward and adjoint equations, respectively:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\text{forward~model~(voltage)}:~\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla_{y}\cdot(\sigma\nabla_{y}u)=0,~~y\in\mathcal{D}\\
u|_{\partial\mathcal{D}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\theta_{1}},\end{array}\right.\\
\\
\displaystyle\text{adjoint~model~(intensity)}:~\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla_{y}\cdot(\sigma\nabla_{y}v)=0,~~y\in\mathcal{D}\\
v|_{\partial\mathcal{D}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\theta_{2}}\end{array}\right..\end{array}

(38)

Physically, this equation describes a voltage being applied at
\theta_{1}\in\partial\mathcal{D}
with electrical intensity collected at point
\theta_{2}\in\partial\mathcal{D}
. The data on the right hand side of (
37
) is the recording of this electrical intensity.
The design space is therefore

(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in\partial\mathcal{D}^{2}=:\Omega.

We set the computational domain
\mathcal{D}
to be a unit disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2},
so the boundary
\partial\mathcal{D}
is a unit circle. We parameterize
\partial\mathcal{D}
using
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,2\pi]
, so that
\Omega=\partial\mathcal{D}^{2}=[0,2\pi]^{2}
.
We discretize the integration domain
\mathcal{D}
and represent it using a mesh
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{d}\}
.
Using numerical quadrature, we reduce (
37
) to a linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
, where the vector
\mathbf{b}
takes on the value of
\text{data}_{\theta}
from (
37
).
The continuous experiment/data matrix
\mathbf{A}
has
d
columns, with rows indexed by a particle pair
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in\partial\mathcal{D}^{2}
, that is,

\underbrace{[\dots\nabla_{y}u(\theta_{1},y_{j})\cdot\nabla_{y}v(\theta_{2},y_{j})\dots]}_{\mathbf{A}\left(\left(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\right),:\right)}\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
\sigma(y_{j})\,\Delta y_{j}\\
\vdots\end{array}\right]}_{\mathbf{x}}.

(39)

Note that
u
and
v
are solutions to equations parameterized by
\sigma
, so different values of the ground truth
\sigma
would lead to different matrices
\mathbf{A}
.

Remark 6
.

To prepare the continuously indexed matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we discretize the boundary domain
\partial\mathcal{D}
into a finite collection of nodes and simulate the forward and adjoint models (
38
) with these discretized boundary conditions. For particles
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
between the nodes, we use linear interpolation to approximate the solutions
u(\theta_{1},:)
and
v(\theta_{2},:).
For example, when
\theta_{1}\in[\theta_{1}^{L},\theta_{1}^{R}]
, where
\theta_{1}^{L}
and
\theta_{1}^{R}
represent the nearest nodes from the discretization that are left and right of
\theta_{1}
, respectively, we approximate the forward model solution by

u(\theta_{1},:)=\frac{\theta_{1}^{R}-\theta_{1}}{\theta_{1}^{R}-\theta_{1}^{L}}u\left(\theta_{1}^{L},:\right)+\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{1}^{L}}{\theta_{1}^{R}-\theta_{1}^{L}}u(\theta_{1}^{R},:)\,.

8
Conclusions

As computational techniques involving optimal transport and Wasserstein gradient flow become more mature, they offer the opportunity to deal with infinite-dimensional probability measure space, enabling a new and wider range of applications.
The optimal experimental design (OED) problem in continuous design space is one such example, offering an important generalization over more traditional discrete experimental design.

The move from finite-dimensional Euclidean space to the infinite-dimensional probability manifold results in a more challenging optimization problem.
We use newly available Wasserstein gradient flow techniques to recast the continuous OED problem.
In particular, the gradient flow on measure space is mapped to gradient descent on a discrete set of particles representing the distribution in the Euclidean space.

Algorithm

1
can be applied to solve the continuous OED.
Moreover, we have provided the first criticality condition and basic convexity analysis under the A- and D-optimal design criteria. As a proof of concept, we assessed the algorithm’s performance on the EIT problem, observing convergence of
Algorithm

1
to distributions that reveal interesting design knowledge on specific EIT media examples.

The present work opens the door to many additional questions, including the following.

1.

Tensor structure in particle gradient flow
Algorithm

1
. If the design space
\Omega
is high dimensional, it might be possible to decompose the particle update in
Algorithm

1
and follow a multi-modal scheme. How to take advantage of such tensor structure to improve efficiency of the optimization process is one interesting direction to pursue.

2.

Sensitivity to noise. It would be interesting to study sensitivity of the continuous OED optimizer to the noise encoded in the objective function and data.

3.

Explicit error bound in the simulation.
The results available currently do not constitute a rigorous numerical analysis, though we provided a roadmap
Section

4.3
.
It would be interesting to fill in the missing technical arguments.

4.

Nonlinear optimal design.
This paper solely addresses the optimal design problem for only the linear inverse problem.
Since most physical models do not have a linear relationship between the inferred quantity and data observations, study of OED in the convoluted nonlinear setting is a useful question for future study.
