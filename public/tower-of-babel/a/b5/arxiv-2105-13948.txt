Positroid Links and Braid varieties
Roger Casals, Eugene Gorsky, Mikhail Gorsky, José Simental

Abstract
. We study braid varieties and their relation to open positroid varieties. We discuss four different types of braids associated to open positroid strata and show that their associated Legendrian links are all Legendrian isotopic. In particular, we prove that each open positroid stratum can be presented as the augmentation variety for four different Legendrian fronts described in terms of either permutations, juggling patterns, cyclic rank matrices or Le diagrams. We also relate braid varieties to open Richardson varieties and brick manifolds, showing that the latter provide projective compactifications of braid varieties, with normal crossing divisors at infinity.

1.
Introduction

This article studies braid varieties
[
8
,
51
]
and their relation to open positroid varieties
[
42
]
. In a nutshell, we study four braids associated to any open positroid variety, and develop new techniques to algebraically study their braid varieties. In addition, this paper brings to bear insight from contact and symplectic topology to explicitly study these braid varieties, with a focus on Legendrian links and their relation to open positroid varieties in Grassmannians.

An open positroid variety
\Pi
of the Grassmannian
\Gr(k,n)
can be indexed by either of the following four pieces of data. First, a pair of permutations
u,w\in S_{n}
such that
u\leq w
in the Bruhat order and
w
is a
k
-Grassmannian permutation. Second, a
k
-bounded affine permutation
f:\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
of size
n
. Third, a cyclic rank matrix
r
and, fourth, a Le diagram. The bijections between these objects and the description of their associated positroid varieties are provided in
[
42
,
57
]
. In this article, we study four braids, one associated to each of these four pieces of data, and introduce and study their associated Legendrian links.

The first result of this manuscript, within the realm of algebraic combinatorics, is showing that these four braids close up to links in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that are smoothly isotopic, up to trivially adding unlinks. For that, we develop new results using the positroid data above:
k
-Grassmannian permutations,
k
-bounded affine permutations, cyclic rank matrices and Le diagrams. In particular, this requires addressing the dissonance in the number of strands between these braids, which we address by introducing a Markov-type destabilization move that suits the algebraic combinatorics associated to positroids.

The main contact geometric result of this manuscript is then showing that the four associated Legendrian links are all Legendrian isotopic, up to trivially unlinked unknots. In particular, we show that our results and constructions in the smooth case, related to the algebraic combinatorics of positroids, can all be realized by contact isotopies. To our knowledge, the conceptual insight that certain Legendrian links, not just smooth links, underlie each of these four presentations of a positroid variety is also new. It has the important consequence of allowing the description and study of positroid strata in terms of contact topology, which has already been initiated in other works with fruitful consequences, cf.
[
1
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
11
]
. In particular,
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
established the relationship between braid varieties and augmentation varieties. The contact isotopies between the four Legendrian links above imply that the four braid varieties associated to these Legendrians are isomorphic to the corresponding positroid variety, up to trivially adding frozens.

Finally, the article includes new results relating braid varieties to projective brick manifolds and open Richardson varieties. In particular, we show that brick varieties are good projective compactifications of our affine braid varieties. This also allows us to relate their homology to the top
a
-degree Khovanov-Rozansky homology of the underlying smooth link and establish the curious Lefschetz property for open Richardson varieties. The article concludes with a brief discussion on conjectural matters regarding cluster structures and Legendrian links.

Theorem 1.2

2.1.1.

By definition, a permutation

is said to be

w(1)<w(2)<\cdots<w(k),\mbox{ and }w(k+1)<\cdots<w(n).

Similarly,

is said to be a

\Sh^{-1}(1)<\Sh^{-1}(2)<\cdots<\Sh^{-1}(k),\mbox{ and }\Sh^{-1}(k+1)<\cdots<\Sh^{-1}(n).

The set of

-Grassmannian permutations in

(equivalently, the set of

-shuffles) is in bijection with the set of partitions
\lambda
whose Young diagram fits inside a

-rectangle, cf.
[

and its Young diagram; we draw the latter in French notation. Thus, if the Young diagram of
\lambda
fits inside a
k\times(n-k)

\lambda\subseteq(n-k)^{k}
. Such
\lambda
can be written as

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})

where
n-k\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{k}\geq 0
. Note that it is possible that
\lambda
has a zero part, e.g.
\lambda_{k}=0
is allowed. Similarly, the transposed partition can be written as

\lambda^{t}=(\lambda_{1}^{t},\dots,\lambda_{n-k}^{t})

where
k\geq\lambda_{1}^{t}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n-k}^{t}\geq 0
. Again, it is allowed that
\lambda_{n-k}^{t}=0
; this happens if and only if

.

For
\lambda\subseteq(n-k)^{k}
, we denote by

its associated

(2.1)

Note that the length of

is

. In fact, we can obtain reduced decompositions for

as follows:

(2.2)

\begin{array}[]{rl}w_{\lambda}=&(s_{\lambda_{k}}\cdots s_{1})\cdots(s_{k+\lambda_{2}-2}\cdots s_{k-1})(s_{k+\lambda_{1}-1}s_{k+\lambda_{1}-2}\cdots s_{k})\\

2.2.
Richardson Braids

Given a permutation
v\in S_{n}
, we will denote by
\beta(v)\in\mathrm{Br}_{n}^{+}
its reduced positive braid lift to the
n
-stranded braid group. A positive braid word for the braid
\beta(v)\in\mathrm{Br}_{n}^{+}
, which we also denote by
\beta(v)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{+}
, is obtained by considering a reduced expression
v=s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{\ell(v)}}
for
v\in S_{n}
in terms of a product of the simple transpositions
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\in S_{n}
generating the symmetric group
S_{n}
and substituting each
s_{i}
by the Artin braid generator
\sigma_{i}
,
i\in[1,n]
, i.e.
\beta(v)=\sigma_{i_{1}}\ldots\sigma_{i_{\ell(v)}}
. The braid word
\beta(v)
depends on the choice of a reduced expression of
v
, but all such words for a given
v
are related by braid relations
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}
, a.k.a. Reidemester III moves, and commutation relations, and thus represent the same braid. Recall the notation
v^{*}:=w_{0}vw_{0}
.

Definition 2.7

(Richardson braid)
.

Let
u,w\in S_{n}
be two permutations such that
u\leq w
in the Bruhat order. The Richardson braid word
R_{n}(u,w)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
associated to the pair
(u,w)
is

R_{n}(u,w):=\beta(u^{*})^{-1}\beta(w^{*}).

By definition, the smooth Richardson link is the smooth link
\Lambda(u,w)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by the
0
-framed closure of the
n
-stranded braid word
R_{n}(u,w)
.
\Box

Definition
2.7
is inspired by
[
29
, Section 1.5]
. The reason we have to conjugate by
w_{0}
is Corollary
4.6
. See also Remark
4.7
. Let us also define a version of the Richardson braid with only positive Artin generators, together with its associated smooth link:

Definition 2.8

(Positive Richardson braid)
.

Let
u,w\in S_{n}
be two permutations such that
u\leq w
in the Bruhat order. The positive Richardson braid word
R^{+}_{n}(u,w)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
associated to the pair
(u,w)
is

R^{+}_{n}(u,w):=\beta(u^{-1}w_{0})\beta(w^{*}).

By definition, the positive Richardson link is the smooth link
\Lambda^{+}(u,w)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
associated to the
0
-framed closure of the
n
-stranded braid word
\Delta^{-1}_{n}R^{+}_{n}(u,w)
.
\Box

Remark 2.9
.

We emphasize that the braid words in Definitions
2.7
and
2.8
depend on the choice of reduced expressions of
u
and
w
, but the braids
R_{n}(u,w),R^{+}_{n}(u,w)
, and
\Delta^{-1}_{n}R^{+}_{n}(u,w)
depend only on the pair
(u,w)
. The smooth links
\Lambda(u,w)
and
\Lambda^{+}(u,w)
thus also depend only on the pair
(u,w)
.

By construction, the smooth links

are smoothly isotopic. Indeed, note that we can find a reduced expression

\Delta_{n}^{-1}=\beta(u^{*})^{-1}\beta(u^{-1}w_{0})^{-1}

and it follows that, in the braid group

\Box

Lemma 2.33
.

Let

Let us work by induction on the number of columns of

\mathrm{L}

\mathrm{L}

has no columns, i.e. if it is associated to the empty Young diagram, then

is the trivial braid. In that case, all the arcs in the juggling diagram for
f
are special, which implies that
J^{(1)}(f)
is the trivial braid as well. Let us now assume the result for the Le diagram

\mathrm{L}

, with associated bounded affine permutation
f
, and let
\reflectbox{$\mathrm{L}$}^{\prime}
be a Le diagram (with permutation
f^{\prime}
) obtained from

\mathrm{L}

by adjoining an extra column to the left. We let
n
be the width of the Le diagram

\mathrm{L}

, so that
n+1
is the width of
\reflectbox{$\mathrm{L}$}^{\prime}
.

If this added column is empty then
D^{(1)}_{k}(\reflectbox{$\mathrm{L}$})=D^{(1)}_{k}(\reflectbox{$\mathrm{L}$}^{\prime})
by definition. Since
f^{\prime}(1)=1
and
f^{\prime}(i)=f(i-1)+1
for

, the juggling braids for
f
and
f^{\prime}
coincide, and thus
J^{(1)}(f)=J^{(1)}(f^{\prime})
.

If this added column is non-empty, let us study first how the bounded affine permutation
f^{\prime}
is obtained from the bounded affine permutation
f
. For this, let

be the height of the added column. Note that, if

then

. On the other hand, if

then

i
and
i+1

f(i)=i

(i,f(i))

(i,i)
(or to
(i,1)

to
i
.

(i,n)
to
(i,i)
. This horizontal segment will intersect exactly once with any strand in the braid that contains a vertical segment separating
j
and
j+1

. Thus, up to braid moves pulling the strands to the right, the braid

is of the form

, as needed.

3.

The goal of this section is to associate Legendrian links to positroid data and show that equivalent positroid data yield Legendrian isotopic links, up to trivially adding unlinked unknots. These Legendrian links are introduced in Subsection

. Theorem
3.6
establishes the necessary Legendrian isotopies. It is proven in Subsection

, after the main technical lemma is proven in Subsection

(B_{i}(z))_{jk}:=\begin{cases}1&j=k\text{ and }j\neq i,i+1\\
1&(j,k)=(i,i+1)\text{ or }(i+1,i)\\
z&j=k=i+1\\
0&\text{otherwise;}\end{cases},\quad\mbox{i.e. }B_{i}(z):=\left(\begin{matrix}1&\cdots&&&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&&&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&1&\cdots&0\\
0&\cdots&1&z&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&&&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}\right).

Here the only non-trivial
(2\times 2)
-block of
B_{i}(z)
is at the

th and
(i+1)
st rows.

4.2.
Richardson and Positroid varieties

We introduce Richardson and positroid varieties, following
[
42
]
, see also
[
62
]
. Let us consider the flag variety
\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}
associated to the group
G:=\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
. That is,
\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}=G/B
where
B
is the Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices. We denote by
\mathscr{F}^{A}
the flag associated to a matrix
A\in G
. Namely, the
i
-th space in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
for the flag
\mathscr{F}^{A}
is spanned by the first
i
columns of the matrix
A
. Let us denote by
\mathscr{F}^{\text{st}}:=(0\subseteq\langle e_{1}\rangle\subseteq\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n})
the standard flag, and by
\mathscr{F}^{\ant}:=(0\subseteq\langle e_{n}\rangle\subseteq\langle e_{n},e_{n-1}\rangle\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\langle e_{n},\dots,e_{2}\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n})
the anti-standard flag. Note that we have an action of
G
on
G/B=\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}
by left multiplication. By restricting, we also have an action of
B
on
\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}
. Similarly, if
B_{-}
is the opposite Borel subgroup of lower triangular matrices, we also have an action of
B_{-}
on
\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}
by left multiplication.

Let
w\in S_{n}
be a permutation. The
Schubert cell

X^{\circ}_{w}
associated to
w\in S_{n}
is defined to be the
B
-orbit of the flag
wB/B
, where
w
is understood as a permutation matrix:

\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}_{w}:=BwB/B=\{\mathscr{F}\in\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}\mid\dim(F^{\text{st}}_{p}\cap F_{q})=\#\{i\leq q\mid w(i)\leq p\}\;\text{for all}\;p,q\in[1,n]\}.

The
opposite Schubert cell
of
w\in S_{n}
is defined to be the
B_{-}
-orbit of the flag
wB/B
:

\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}\mskip-3.0mu^{w}:=B_{-}wB/B.

Similarly to
\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}_{w}
, the opposite Schubert cell
\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}\mskip-3.0mu^{w}
admits a explicit description as follows. Let
w_{0}
be the longest element of
S_{n}
. Then:

\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}\mskip-3.0mu^{w}=\{\mathscr{F}\in\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}\mid\dim(F_{p}\cap F_{q}^{\ant})=\#\{i\leq q\mid w_{0}w(i)\leq p\}\;\text{for all}\;p,q=1,\dots,n\}.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
u,w\in S_{n}
be two permutations. The
open Richardson variety

\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w)\subseteq\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}
is the intersection:

\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w):=\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}_{w}\cap\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}\mskip-3.0mu^{u}.

It is known that
\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w)
is nonempty if and only if
u\leq w
in the Bruhat order, cf.
[
62
]
, in which case it is a smooth affine variety of dimension
\ell(w)-\ell(u)
. In particular,
\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(w,w)
is a single point.

Now fix
k\leq n
and consider the Grassmannian
\Gr(k,n)
of
k
-planes in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. In fact,
\Gr(k,n)=G/P
, where
P
is the subgroup of
G=\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
consisting of block-upper-triangular matrices with blocks of sizes
k
and
n-k
. We have the associated subgroup
W_{P}=S_{k}\times S_{n-k}\subseteq S_{n}
. Again, we have an action of
B
on
\Gr(n,k)
and, given a permutation
w\in S_{n}
, we have an associated Schubert cell in the Grassmannian:

C_{w}:=BwP/P\subseteq\Gr(k,n).

In fact,
C_{w}=C_{w^{\prime}}
if the cosets
wW_{P}
and
w^{\prime}W_{P}
coincide.

Note that we have a natural projection
\pi:\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}\to\Gr(k,n)
. In terms of flags,
\pi(\mathscr{F})=\mathscr{F}_{k}
is the
k
-th subspace of the flag. This map is
B
-equivariant and thus
\pi(\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}_{w})\subseteq C_{w}
. The following result underscores the importance of
k
-Grassmannian permutations.

Lemma 4.3

(Section 5,
[
42
]
)
.

The map
\pi|_{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}_{w}}:\,\,\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}_{w}\to C_{w}
is an isomorphism of algebraic varieties if and only if
w
is a
k
-Grassmannian permutation.

In particular, if
w
is a
k
-Grassmannian permutation and
u\leq w
then we have:

\mathscr{F}\ell_{n}\supseteq\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w)\cong\pi(\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w))\subseteq\Gr(k,n).

Definition 4.4
.

Let
u,w\in S_{n}
, with
w
a
k
-Grassmannian permutation and
u\leq w
. The
open positroid variety

\Pi_{u,w}\subseteq\Gr(k,n)
is:

\Pi_{u,w}:=\pi(\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w)).

By definition, the positroid variety
\Pi_{u,w}
is isomorphic to the Richardson variety
\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w)
. Since positroids can be defined inside the Grassmannian, they are specially nice amongst other Richardson varieties, see e.g.
[

,
42

,

4.3.
Open Richardson varieties as braid varieties

The following result is the main theorem of this subsection. It states that open Richardson varieties can be described as braid varieties.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{n}
be two reduced positive braid words, and
w_{1},w_{2}\in S_{n}
be their Coxeter projections.

(a)

The map

\iota:\mathbb{C}^{\ell(\beta_{1})}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ell(\beta_{2})}\longrightarrow\mathscr{F}\ell_{n},\qquad(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto\mathscr{F}^{B_{\beta_{1}}^{-1}(z_{1})},\quad(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell(\beta_{1})}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ell(\beta_{2})},

restricts to an isomorphism

X(\beta_{1}\beta_{2};w_{0})\longrightarrow\iota(X(\beta_{1}\beta_{2};w_{0}))\cong\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}_{w_{1}^{-1}}\cap\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}\mskip-3.0mu^{w_{2}w_{0}}

of affine algebraic varieties.

(b)

The map

\jmath:\mathbb{C}^{\ell(\beta_{1})}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ell(\beta_{2})}\longrightarrow\mathscr{F}\ell_{n},\qquad(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto\mathscr{F}^{w_{0}B_{\beta_{1}}^{-1}(z_{1})},\quad(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell(\beta_{1})}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ell(\beta_{2})}

restricts to an isomorphism

X(\beta_{1}\beta_{2};w_{0})\longrightarrow\jmath(X(\beta_{1}\beta_{2};w_{0}))\cong\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}\mskip-3.0mu^{w_{0}w_{1}^{-1}}\cap\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0ptX}\limits^{\circ}}_{w_{2}^{*}}

of affine algebraic varieties.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
u,w\in S_{n}
be such that
u\leq w
in Bruhat order, and
\beta(w),\beta(u^{-1}w_{0}),\beta(w^{*})\in\mathrm{Br}_{n}
positive lifts of
w,u^{-1}w_{0},w^{*}
. Then restrictions give the following isomorphisms of affine algebraic varieties

\iota:X(\beta(w)\beta(u^{-1}w_{0});w_{0})\overset{\sim}{\to}\;\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u^{-1},w^{-1}),\qquad\jmath:X(\beta(u^{-1}w_{0})\beta(w^{*});w_{0})\overset{\sim}{\to}\;\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w).

Theorem
4.5
, through Corollary
4.6
, implies Theorem
1.3
, as discussed in Subsection
4.3.3
below. Subsections
4.3.1
and
4.3.2
now prove Theorem
4.5
.

Remark 4.7
.

We note that interpreting open Richardson varieties as braid varieties allows for a clear description of certain isomorphisms between them.
In particular,
the composition
\iota\circ\jmath^{-1}
associated with
\beta_{1}=\beta(u^{-1}w_{0}),\beta_{2}=\beta(w^{*})
gives an isomorphism

\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w)\overset{\sim}{\to}\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(w_{0}w,w_{0}u).

Also, we have an explicit isomorphism

\psi:X(\beta(w)\beta(u^{-1}w_{0});w_{0})\cong X(\beta(u^{-1}w_{0})\beta(w^{*});w_{0})

given by a “cyclic rotation” of the braid word. The description of
\psi
and the proof that it is an isomorphism can be found in
[
7
, Lemma 3.10]
.
5
5

5

The paper
[
7
]
uses different conventions to define braid varieties, but the same arguments apply in our setting.

The composition

\jmath\circ\psi\circ\iota^{-1}
, with
\iota,\jmath
as in Corollary

, gives an isomorphism

\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u^{-1},w^{-1})\overset{\sim}{\to}\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w).

In fact, the existence of these isomorphisms implies that we could have chosen slightly different braids throughout the paper so that their varieties would be isomorphic to open Richardson and positroid varieties, at the cost of changing such isomorphisms. Namely, our positive Richardson braid is

\bigoplus_{I}\mathrm{Hom}(T_{w_{0}},\mathcal{B}_{\beta_{I}})=\mathrm{Hom}\left(T_{w_{0}},[\mathcal{B}_{i_{1}}\xrightarrow{b_{i_{1}}}R]\otimes_{R}\cdots\otimes_{R}[\mathcal{B}_{i_{r}}\xrightarrow{b_{i_{r}}}R]\right)=

\mathrm{Hom}(T_{w_{0}},T_{i_{1}}\bigotimes_{R}\cdots\bigotimes_{R}T_{i_{r}})=\mathrm{Hom}(T_{w_{0}},T_{\beta})=\mathrm{Hom}(R,T_{\beta}T_{w_{0}}^{-1})=\mathrm{HHH}^{a=0}(\beta\Delta^{-1}).

Finally, by the main result of
[
34
]
we conclude
\mathrm{HHH}^{a=0}(\beta\Delta^{-1})\simeq\mathrm{HHH}^{a=n}(\beta\Delta)
.
∎

Finally,
[

]
studied the
curious Lefschetz property
, as defined by

, for cohomology rings of character varieties. In

]
proves the curious Lefschetz property for each braid variety. Therefore, Corollary
4.6
implies:

Corollary 4.21
.

Let

be such that
u\leq w
in Bruhat order.
Then the open Richardson variety
\mathcal{R}^{\circ}\mskip-3.0mu(u,w)
for

satisfies the curious Lefschetz property.
\Box

Let
\Lambda\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{3},\xi_{st})
be a Legendrian link and
T\subseteq\Lambda
a set of marked points, with at least one marked point per component. From now onward, unless otherwise specified, we assume that
\Lambda
is isotopic to the Legendrian lift
\Lambda_{\beta}
of a
(-1)
-closure of a positive braid
\beta
. Let us denote by
\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda,T)
either of the two smooth affine varieties:

(1)

\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda,T)=\mbox{Spec}(H^{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda,T)))
, where
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda,T)
denotes the (commutative) Legendrian contact dg-algebra associated to
(\Lambda,T)
. This dg-algebra was defined in
[
12
]
, see
[
20
]
for a survey.

(2)

\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda,T)
is the moduli stack of pseudo-perfect objects of microlocal rank one in
\mbox{Sh}_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{2},T)
with a trivialization of the microlocal functor on
\Lambda\setminus T
. See
[

for more details.

These varieties are isomorphic, cf.
[
11
, Section 4]
and
[

, or
[

]
. If
T
is chosen so that every strand of the positive braid word
\beta
associated to
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\beta}
has exactly one marked point, then
\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda_{\beta},T)
is isomorphic to the braid variety of
\beta
, cf.
[
8
, Section 2]
. In
[
7
]
we proved that the algebra of regular functions
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda,T)]

. This fact was conjectured in the initial version of this manuscript, prior to ibid.

Let us consider a contact isotopy
\{\phi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
, so that each
\phi_{t}\in\mbox{Cont}^{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\xi_{st})
is a compactly supported contactomorphism and
\phi_{0}=\mbox{Id}
. Note that any Legendrian isotopy extends to a contact isotopy
[
33
]
. Then the contact isotopy
\{\phi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
induces an algebraic isomorphism of affine varieties

\Phi_{t}:\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda,T)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{M}(\phi_{t}(\Lambda),\phi_{t}(T)).

Indeed, this is proved in
[
12
]
for the Floer-theoretic approach with the dg-algebra, and holds by
[
36
]
in the sheaf-theoretic approach. We conjecture that these are not just isomorphisms of affine varieties, but also quasi-cluster isomorphisms. See
[
27
]
for details on quasi-cluster structures and their morphisms. The precise conjecture reads as follows:

Conjecture 5.1

(Legendrian invariance of cluster structures)
.

Let
\Lambda_{0}
and
\Lambda_{1}
be two Legendrian links in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\xi_{st})
and let
\{\phi_{t}\}
be a contact isotopy
\phi_{t}\in\operatorname{Cont}^{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\xi_{st})
,
{t\in[0,1]}
, such that
\phi_{1}(\Lambda_{0})=\Lambda_{1}
. Consider a set of marked points
T_{0}\subseteq\Lambda_{0}
, with at least one marked point per component and write
T_{1}:=\phi_{1}(T)
. Then the induced algebraic morphism

\Phi_{t}^{*}:\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda_{1},T_{1})]\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda_{0},T_{0})]

is a quasi-cluster isomorphism, where both target and domain are endowed with the cluster structures constructed in
[
7
]
. That is, the quasi-cluster isomorphism type of the cluster algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda,T)]
is a Legendrian isotopy invariant of a Legendrian link
\Lambda\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{3},\xi_{st})
with a set of marked points
T\subseteq\Lambda
.

Following the symplectic geometry behind the construction of the cluster structures in
[
7
,
11
]
, it is natural to expect a generalization of our previous work and the above conjecture to any Legendrian link
\Lambda\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{3},\xi_{st})
, even if it is not the
(-1)
-closure of a positive braid. A first challenge is structural: the spaces
\mathfrak{M}(\Lambda,T)
are in general just
D^{-}
-stacks and there is no definition known to us of what it means for a derived stack to admit a cluster structure. More generally, both the dg-category
\mbox{Sh}_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{2},T)
of sheaves above and the stable tame isomorphism type of
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda,T)
are Legendrian invariant. The geometry indicates that they should admit a sort of ‘‘cluster structure’’; a challenge is that we do not know what it means for a (finite type) dg-category to admit a cluster structure
8
8

8

A desirable feature would be that the “ring of regular functions” of its derived stack of pseudo-perfect objects – e.g. supposing it is isomorphic to an affine scheme – is a cluster algebra, again in the appropriate sense.
or for a dg-algebra to be a “cluster” dg-algebra. The characteristic algebra, as introduced in
[
56
, Section 3]
, or the graded augmentation variety, modulo dg-homotopies, might be reasonable objects to study from this viewpoint. In either case, based on the geometry, we conjecture that such generalizations of cluster structures exist and are Legendrian invariant, up to quasi-cluster isomorphisms.
