An algebraic approach towards a conjecture on the Davenport constant
Naveen K. Godara, Renu Joshi, Eshita Mazumdar

Abstract
For a finite group G , G, 𝖣 ⁡ ( G ) \mathsf{D}(G) is defined as the least positive integer k k such that for every sequence S = g 1 ⋅ g 2 ⋅ … ⋅ g k S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{2}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dotsc\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{k} of length k k over G G , there exist 1 ≤ i 1 < i 2 < ⋯ < i m ≤ k 1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{m}\leq k such that g i 1 g i 2 ⋯ g i m = 1 , g_{i_{1}}g_{i_{2}}\cdots g_{i_{m}}=1, where 1 1 is the identity element of G . G. The small Davenport constant 𝖽 ⁡ ( G ) \mathsf{d}(G) is the maximal positive integer k k such that there is a sequence of length k k over G G which has no non-trivial product-one subsequence. In 2004, Dimitrov proved that 𝖣 ⁡ ( G ) ≤ 𝖫 ⁡ ( G ) \mathsf{D}(G)\leq\mathsf{L}(G) for a finite p p -group G G , where p p is a prime and 𝖫 ⁡ ( G ) \mathsf{L}(G) is the Loewy length of 𝔽 p ​ [ G ] . \mathbb{F}_{p}[G]. In this article, we compute 𝖣 ⁡ ( G ) \mathsf{D}(G) for certain classes of finite non-abelian p p -groups, including metacyclic groups, and show that the conjecture is true by determining the precise value of 𝖫 ⁡ ( G ) \mathsf{L}(G) . 1
Introduction and statements of main results

The concept of the Davenport constant arose in the study of class groups of algebraic number fields. Specifically, if
K
is an algebraic number field and
G
is its class group,
the Davenport constant
\mathds{D}(G)
represents the maximal number of
prime ideals (counting multiplicity) that can occur in the prime decomposition of the principal ideal generated by an irreducible integer in
K
(see
[
19
]
for more details). For a finite group
G
(written multiplicatively), the
Davenport constant

\mathds{D}(G)
is defined as the least positive integer
k
such that any sequence of
k
elements from
G
(repetition allowed) contains a subsequence of
S
whose product of terms equals the identity element when multiplied in some order.

For a finite abelian group
G
,
\mathds{D}(G)
represents the well-known Davenport constant. The question of determining
\mathds{D}(G)
was proposed by H. Davenport in 1966. The study of the Davenport constant is a classical and challenging problem in the fields of additive and combinatorial number theory. The exact value of
\mathds{D}(G)
is known only for certain types of finite abelian groups, including all finite abelian groups of rank at most two
[
18
]
,
p
-groups of any rank
[
17
]
, where
p
is a prime, and for specific classes of abelian groups.

To better understand this concept, we define some essential terms. Let
G
be a finite multiplicative group with the identity element
1
, and let
S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{2}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dotsc\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}
be a sequence of length
\ell
over
G
. A sequence

T=g_{i_{1}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{i_{2}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dotsc\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{i_{m}}
is said to be a
subsequence
of
S

if
1\leq{i_{1}},\dotsc,{i_{m}}\leq{\ell}
and
{i_{1}},\dotsc,{i_{m}}
are pairwise distinct. A sequence
S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{2}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dotsc\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}
is said to be a
product-one sequence
if
\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{\sigma(i)}=1
for some
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell},
where
\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}
is the symmetric group of
\ell
symbols.

We remark that for a finite group
G
,
\mathds{D}(G)=\mathsf{d}(G)+1
, where
\mathsf{d}(G)
is the
small Davenport constant
, which is the maximal positive integer
k
such that there is a sequence of length
k
over
G
that does not contain any non-trivial product-one subsequence.

For non-abelian groups, there are several natural extensions of the Davenport constant.
Over the past few decades, mathematicians have explored various versions of the Davenport constant for finite non-abelian groups and established their connections to distinct fields. In 1977, Olson and White
[
16
]
introduced the ordered version of the Davenport constant. The
ordered Davenport constant

\mathsf{D}(G)
is defined as the least positive integer
k
such that for every sequence
S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{2}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dotsc\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{k}
of length
k
over
G
there exist
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{m}\leq k
such that
g_{i_{1}}g_{i_{2}}\cdots g_{i_{m}}=1.
We want to point out that in
[
8
]
,
\mathsf{D}(G)
is referred to as the strong Davenport constant, but in the literature, the strong Davenport constant
[
2
]
is meant for something else. So, to avoid confusion, we call it the ordered Davenport constant. By the definitions of
\mathsf{d}(G)
and
\mathsf{D}(G)
, it is evident that
\mathsf{d}(G)+1\leq\mathsf{D}(G)
for any finite group
G
. In the same paper, Olson and White gave a general upper bound
\mathsf{D}(G)\leq\big\lceil\frac{|G|+1}{2}\big\rceil
for any finite non-cyclic group
G
. Therefore, it follows that
\mathsf{d}(G)\leq\big\lfloor\frac{|G|}{2}\big\rfloor
for any finite non-cyclic group
G
. Another version was introduced by Geroldinger and Grynkiewicz (see
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
for more details), known as the large Davenport constant. The
large
Davenport constant

\mathcal{D}(G)
is defined as the maximal length of a product-one sequence that cannot be partitioned into two non-trivial product-one subsequences. It was also shown that
\mathsf{d}(G)+1\leq\mathcal{D}(G)
, with equality holds when
G
is abelian.

From the above discussion, it is clear that
\mathsf{d}(G)+1\leq\min\{\mathsf{D}(G),\mathcal{D}(G)\}
for any finite group
G
. However, there is no concrete relationship between
\mathsf{D}(G)
and
\mathcal{D}(G).
For example, it has been observed that if
G
is the
\text{ non-abelian group of order }27\text{ of exponent }3
, then
\mathsf{d}(G)=6
,
\mathcal{D}(G)=8
, and
\mathsf{D}(G)=9
. On the other hand, when
G
is the non-abelian group of order
27
of exponent
9
, then
\mathsf{d}(G)=10
,
\mathcal{D}(G)=12
and
\mathsf{D}(G)=11
(we refer to
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
, and
[
8
]
for details).

If
G
is abelian, we have
\mathsf{d}(G)+1=\mathsf{D}(G)=\mathcal{D}(G).
Once the question shifts to determining the precise value of these invariants for finite non-abelian groups, the problem becomes more complicated than finding the same for finite abelian groups. Recently, Qu, Li and Teeuwsen proved the following upper bound on
\mathsf{d}(G)
:

Theorem 1.1
.

[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
Let
G
be a finite non-cyclic group, and
p
be the smallest prime divisor of
|G|
. Then

\mathsf{d}(G)\leq|G|/p+p-2

with equality if
G
contains a cyclic subgroup of index
p
.

The invariants
\mathsf{d}(G)
(hence
\mathds{D}(G)
) and
\mathcal{D}(G)
have been studied extensively, but not much is known about
\mathsf{D}(G)
, for more details, we refer
[
6
,
12
]
. So, throughout the paper, we mainly focus on

.

Building upon the bound for
\mathsf{D}(G)
provided by Olson and White, we can derive our first result, which is as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Then

(i)
For the dicyclic group
Q_{4n}=\langle x,y\mid x^{2}=y^{n},y^{2n}=1,x^{-1}yx=y^{-1}\rangle,
we have

\mathsf{D}(Q_{4n})=2n+1.

(ii)
For the semi-dihedral group
SD_{8n}=\langle x,y\mid x^{2}=y^{4n}=1,x^{-1}yx=y^{2n-1}\rangle,
we have

\mathsf{D}(SD_{8n})=4n+1.

After Olson and White, in 2004, Dimitrov
[

]
dealt with the ordered Davenport constant and provided an upper bound on

for any finite
p
-group

Let

be a finite

For finite abelian

, and

Proposition 3.1

Let

M_{i}=\begin{cases}{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s}}G^{p^{s}}&\mbox{if }~2p^{s-1}+1\leq i\leq p^{s},\\

Proof.

From the definition of the

M_{2}=\gamma_{2}(G)G^{p}
. Assume that

\displaystyle P(s)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle M_{2p^{s-1}+1}=\cdots=M_{p^{s}}={\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s}}G^{p^{s}},

\displaystyle Q(s)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle M_{p^{s}+1}=\cdots=M_{2p^{s}}={\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s}}G^{p^{s+1}},

We will use simultaneous induction on

\displaystyle=a^{ip^{s}}(b^{j}[a,b]^{t})^{p^{s}}[b^{j}[a,b]^{t},a^{i}]^{p^{s}\choose 2}

To prove Theorem
1.5
, we use the following crucial results:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
G
be a finite non-abelian
p
-group such that

G=(\langle c\rangle\times\langle a\rangle)\rtimes\langle b\rangle,

where
[a,b]=c,[a,c]=[b,c]=1,

o(a)=p^{\alpha},o(b)=p^{\beta},o(c)=p^{\gamma},\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}\;\text{with}\;\alpha\geq\beta\geq\gamma\geq 1.
Then
\mathsf{L}(G)=p^{\alpha}+p^{\beta}+2p^{\gamma}-3
.

Proof.

Observe that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}=\langle[a,b]\rangle
. Therefore,
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s}}=\langle[a,b]^{p^{s}}\rangle=\langle c^{p^{s}}\rangle,
and

{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s}}\subseteq G^{p^{s}}\;\text{for all}\;s\geq 1.
We now show that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\cap G^{p^{s}}=\langle c^{p^{s}}\rangle\;\text{for all}\;1\leq s\leq\gamma,
and hence

{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\nsubseteq G^{p^{s}}\;\text{for all}\;1\leq s\leq\gamma
.

Note that
\langle c^{p^{s}}\rangle\subseteq{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\cap G^{p^{s}}
.
Let
g\in{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\cap G^{p^{s}}
then by Proposition
3.2
, we can write
g=a^{ip^{s}}b^{jp^{s}}[a,b]^{tp^{s}}
for some non-negative integers
i,j,t
. Also, we have
g=[a,b]^{kp^{s-1}}
for some non-negative integer
k
. Thus,
a^{ip^{s}}b^{jp^{s}}[a,b]^{tp^{s}}=[a,b]^{kp^{s-1}}
, and consequently

a^{ip^{s}}b^{jp^{s}}[a,b]^{p^{s-1}(pt-k)}=1.

Let us define
\bar{G}=\frac{G}{\gamma_{2}({G})}.

Set
\bar{a}=a\gamma_{2}({G})
and
\bar{b}=b\gamma_{2}({G})
. Hence,
\bar{G}\cong C_{p^{\alpha}}\times C_{p^{\beta}}
. So, we have
\bar{a}^{ip^{s}}\bar{b}^{jp^{s}}=\bar{1}
. Therefore,
ip^{s}\equiv 0\ (\textrm{mod}\ p^{\alpha})\;\text{and}\;jp^{s}\equiv 0\ (\textrm{mod}\ p^{\beta})
. Thus
g=[a,b]^{tp^{s}}\subseteq\langle c^{p^{s}}\rangle
. This shows
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\cap G^{p^{s}}=\langle c^{p^{s}}\rangle
.

Also, notice that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}=1\;\text{for all}\;\gamma+1\leq s\leq\alpha,\;\text{which implies}\;{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\subseteq G^{p^{s}}\;\text{for all}\;\gamma+1\leq s\leq\alpha
. Therefore, we have the following using Proposition
3.1
:

Case 1:
If
\alpha=\gamma\geq 1
, then

M_{k}=\begin{cases}G&\text{if}\;\;k=1\\
\gamma_{2}(G)G^{p}&\text{if}\;\;k=2\\
G^{p}&\text{if}\;\;3\leq k\leq p\\
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{i}}G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;p^{i}+1\leq k\leq 2p^{i},\text{ for all }i\in[\gamma-2]\\
G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;2p^{i}+1\leq k\leq p^{i+1},\text{ for all }i\in[\gamma-2]\\
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{\gamma-1}}&\text{if}\;\;p^{\gamma-1}+1\leq k\leq 2p^{\gamma-1}\\
1&\text{if}\;\;2p^{\gamma-1}+1\leq k\leq p^{\gamma}.\end{cases}

Case 2:
For the cases
\alpha\geq\beta>\gamma\geq 1
and
\alpha>\beta=\gamma\geq 1
, we obtain

M_{k}=\begin{cases}G&\text{if}\;\;k=1\\
\gamma_{2}(G)G^{p}&\text{if}\;\;k=2\\
G^{p}&\text{if}\;\;3\leq k\leq p\\
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{i}}G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;p^{i}+1\leq k\leq 2p^{i},\text{ for all }i\in[\gamma-1]\\
G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;2p^{i}+1\leq k\leq p^{i+1},\text{ for all }i\in[\gamma-1]\\
G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;p^{i}+1\leq k\leq p^{i+1},\text{ for all }\gamma\leq i\leq\alpha-2\\
1&\text{if}\;\;p^{\alpha-1}+1\leq k\leq p^{\alpha}.\end{cases}

Using Proposition
3.2
, we have

|G^{p^{i}}:G^{p^{i+1}}|=p^{3}\text{ for all }i\in[\gamma-1],

|G^{p^{i}}:G^{p^{i+1}}|=p^{2}\text{ for all }\gamma\leq i\leq\beta-1,
and
|G^{p^{i}}:G^{p^{i+1}}|=p\text{ for all }\beta\leq i\leq\alpha-1.
Hence
|M_{1}:M_{2}|=p^{2},|M_{2}:M_{3}|=p,|M_{p^{i}}:M_{p^{i}+1}|=p^{2}\text{ for all }i\in[\beta-1],|M_{p^{i}}:M_{{p^{i}}+1}|=p\text{ for all }\beta\leq i\leq\alpha-1,\text{ and }|M_{2p^{i}}:M_{2p^{i}+1}|=p
for all
i\in[\gamma-1].
Therefore, by Lemma
2.1
with
d=\begin{cases}2p^{\gamma-1}&\text{ if }\alpha=\beta=\gamma,\\
p^{\alpha-1}&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}
, we have

\mathsf{L}(G)=1+(p-1)\sum_{i=1}^{p^{\alpha-1}}ie_{i}=p^{\alpha}+p^{\beta}+2p^{\gamma}-3
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
G
be a finite non-abelian
p
-group such that

G=\langle a\rangle\rtimes\langle b\rangle,

where
[a,b]=a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}},o(a)=p^{\alpha},o(b)=p^{\beta},o([a,b])=p^{\gamma},

\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}\;\text{with}\;\alpha\geq 2\gamma,\beta\geq\gamma\geq 1.
Then
\mathsf{L}(G)=p^{\alpha}+p^{\beta}-1.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\alpha\geq\beta.

As
[a,b]=a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}},
one can easily verifies that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}=\langle a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma+(s-1)}}\rangle\subseteq\langle a^{p^{s}}\rangle\subseteq G^{p^{s}}
for all
s\geq 1
. We have
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s}}=\langle[a,b]^{p^{s}}\rangle\subseteq G^{p^{s}},\;\text{for all}\;s\geq 0
. We have the following using Proposition
3.1
:

M_{k}=\begin{cases}G&\text{if}\;\;k=1\\
G^{p}&\text{if}\;\;2\leq k\leq p\\
G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;p^{i}+1\leq k\leq p^{i+1},\text{ for all }i\in[\alpha-2]\\
1&\text{if}\;\;p^{\alpha-1}+1\leq k\leq p^{\alpha}.\end{cases}

Using the relation,
[a,b]=a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}},
Proposition
3.2
reduces to
G^{p^{s}}=\langle a^{p^{s}},b^{p^{s}}\rangle
for all
s\geq 1
. So,
|G^{p^{s}}|=p^{\alpha+\beta-2s}
for all
s\geq 1.
Now, we have

|M_{p^{i}}:M_{p^{i}+1}|=|G^{p^{i}}:G^{p^{i+1}}|
for all
0\leq i\leq\alpha-1.
Therefore,
|M_{p^{i}}:M_{{p^{i}}+1}|=p^{2}
for all
0\leq i\leq\beta-1
and
|M_{p^{i}}:M_{{p^{i}}+1}|=p
for all
\beta\leq i\leq\alpha-1.
Then, by Lemma
2.1
with
d=p^{\alpha-1}
, we have

\mathsf{L}(G)=1+(p-1)\sum_{i=1}^{p^{\alpha-1}}ie_{i}=p^{\alpha}+p^{\beta}-1.
For the case
\beta>\alpha,
the result follows in a similar way.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
G
be a finite non-abelian
p
-group such that

G=(\langle c\rangle\times\langle a\rangle)\rtimes\langle b\rangle,

where
[a,b]=a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}}c,[c,b]=a^{-p^{2(\alpha-\gamma)}}c^{-p^{\alpha-\gamma}},

o(a)=p^{\alpha},o(b)=p^{\beta},o(c)=p^{\sigma},\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\sigma\in\mathbb{N}\;\text{with}\;\beta\geq\gamma>\sigma\geq 1,\alpha+\sigma\geq 2\gamma.
Then
\mathsf{L}(G)=p^{\alpha}+p^{\beta}+2p^{\sigma}-3.

Proof.

Consider,
\alpha\geq\beta.

Note that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s}}=\langle[a,b]^{p^{s}}\rangle=\langle(a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}}c)^{p^{s}}\rangle\subseteq G^{p^{s}}
for all
s\geq 1
. We first prove that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\cap G^{p^{s}}=\langle[a,b]^{p^{s}}\rangle\;\text{for all}\;1\leq s\leq\sigma
, then it follows that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\nsubseteq G^{p^{s}}
for all
1\leq s\leq\sigma
.

Clearly,
\langle[a,b]^{p^{s}}\rangle\subseteq{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\cap\;G^{p^{s}}.
On the other hand let us assume
g\in{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\cap\;G^{p^{s}}.
Then
g=a^{ip^{s}}b^{jp^{s}}[a,b]^{tp^{s}}

=[a,b]^{kp^{s-1}},
for some non-negative integers
i,j,t,k
. So,
a^{ip^{s}}b^{jp^{s}}[a,b]^{p^{s-1}(pt-k)}=1
. Let us define

\bar{G}=\frac{G}{\langle a,c\rangle}.
Set
\bar{b}=b\langle a,c\rangle.
Therefore,
\bar{G}=\langle\bar{b}\rangle\cong C_{p^{\beta}}
. Thus, we have
\bar{b}^{jp^{s}}=\bar{1},
where
\bar{1}
is the identity element of
\bar{G}.
Hence,
jp^{s}\equiv 0\ (\textrm{mod}\ p^{\beta})
.
So, the equation
a^{ip^{s}}b^{jp^{s}}[a,b]^{p^{s-1}(pt-k)}=1
reduces to
a^{ip^{s}}[a,b]^{p^{s-1}(pt-k)}=1
.
As
[a,b]=a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}}c,
we obtain
a^{ip^{s}+p^{\alpha-\gamma+s-1}(pt-k)}c^{p^{s-1}(pt-k)}=1
.
Since
\langle a,c\rangle\cong C_{p^{\alpha}}\times C_{p^{\sigma}}
, it follows that
ip^{s}+p^{\alpha-\gamma+s-1}(pt-k)\equiv 0\ (\textrm{mod}\ p^{\alpha})\;\text{ and }\;p^{s-1}(pt-k)\equiv 0\ (\textrm{mod}\ p^{\sigma}).

The condition
p^{s-1}(pt-k)\equiv 0\ (\textrm{mod}\ p^{\sigma})
, implies that
p^{\sigma-s+1}|(pt-k)
(as
\sigma-s+1\geq 1\;\text{ for }\;1\leq s\leq\sigma
). Thus,
p|(pt-k)
and hence
p|k
. As a consequence,
g=[a,b]^{kp^{s-1}}\in\langle[a,b]^{p^{s}}\rangle
. This shows that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\cap G^{p^{s}}=\langle[a,b]^{p^{s}}\rangle
.

Now, we prove that
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\subseteq G^{p^{s}}\;\text{for all}\;\sigma+1\leq s\leq\alpha
. From the relation
[a,b]=a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}}c
and using that fact that

o(c)=p^{\sigma}
, we have
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}=\langle[a,b]^{p^{s-1}}\rangle=\langle(a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}}c)^{p^{s-1}}\rangle=\langle a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma+s-1}}\rangle\subseteq G^{p^{s}}\;\text{for all}\;\sigma+1\leq s\leq\gamma.

Note that
|\gamma_{2}(G)|=p^{\gamma}
which implies
1={\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\subseteq G^{p^{s}}\;\text{for all}\;\gamma+1\leq s\leq\alpha
. Therefore,
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{s-1}}\subseteq G^{p^{s}}\;\text{for all}\;\sigma+1\leq s\leq\alpha
.
Using Proposition
3.1
, we arrive at the following computations:

M_{k}=\begin{cases}G&\text{if}\;\;k=1\\
\gamma_{2}(G)G^{p}&\text{if}\;\;k=2\\
G^{p}&\text{if}\;\;3\leq k\leq p\\
{\gamma_{2}(G)}^{p^{i}}G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;p^{i}+1\leq k\leq 2p^{i},\text{ for all }i\in[\sigma-1]\\
G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;2p^{i}+1\leq k\leq p^{i+1},\text{ for all }i\in[\sigma-1]\\
G^{p^{i+1}}&\text{if}\;\;p^{i}+1\leq k\leq p^{i+1},\text{ for all }\sigma\leq i\leq\alpha-2\\
1&\text{if}\;\;p^{\alpha-1}+1\leq k\leq p^{\alpha}.\end{cases}

For
s\geq 1
, let
x=a^{p^{s}},y=b^{p^{s}}
, and
z=c^{p^{s}}
, then
[x,y]=[a,b]^{p^{2s}}=a^{p^{\alpha-\gamma+2s}}c^{p^{2s}}=x^{p^{\alpha-\gamma+s}}z^{p^{s}}
,
[y,z]=[x,y]^{p^{\alpha-\gamma}}=x^{p^{2(\alpha-\gamma)+s}}z^{p^{\alpha-\gamma+s}}
, and
[x,z]=1
. Therefore, one can deduce that

G^{p^{s}}\cong(\langle x\rangle\times\langle z\rangle)\rtimes\langle y\rangle,\;\text{where}\;[x,y]=x^{p^{\alpha-\gamma+s}}z^{p^{s}},[y,z]=x^{p^{2(\alpha-\gamma)+s}}z^{p^{\alpha-\gamma+s}},o(x)=p^{\alpha-s},o(y)=p^{\beta-s},o(z)=p^{\sigma-s}
, and hence
|G^{p^{s}}|=p^{\alpha+\beta+\sigma-3s}
for all

.
Thus, we obtain

|G^{p^{i}}:G^{p^{i+1}}|=p^{3}\text{ for all }i\in[\sigma-1],

|G^{p^{i}}:G^{p^{i+1}}|=p^{2}\text{ for all }\sigma\leq i\leq\beta-1,

|G^{p^{i}}:G^{p^{i+1}}|=p\text{ for all }\beta\leq i\leq\alpha-1.
Therefore,
|M_{1}:M_{2}|=p^{2},|M_{2}:M_{3}|=p,

|M_{p^{i}}:M_{p^{i}+1}|=p^{2}\text{ for all }i\in[\beta-1],

|M_{p^{i}}:M_{p^{i}+1}|=p\text{ for all }\beta\leq i\leq\alpha-1,
and
|M_{2p^{i}}:M_{2p^{i}+1}|=p\text{ for all }i\in[\sigma-1].

Then, by Lemma
2.1
with

, we have
\mathsf{L}(G)=1+(p-1)\sum_{i=1}^{p^{\alpha-1}}ie_{i}=p^{\alpha}+p^{\beta}+2p^{\sigma}-3
.
For the case
\beta>\alpha,
, the result follows similarly.
