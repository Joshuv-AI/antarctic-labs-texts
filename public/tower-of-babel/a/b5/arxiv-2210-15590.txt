Abstract
. This paper provides the first variational proof of the existence of periodic nonlocal-CMC surfaces. These are nonlocal analogues of the classical Delaunay cylinders. More precisely, we show the existence of a set in ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} which is periodic in one direction, has a prescribed (but arbitrary) volume within a slab orthogonal to that direction, has constant nonlocal mean curvature, and minimizes an appropriate periodic version of the fractional perimeter functional under the volume constraint. We show, in addition, that the set is cylindrically symmetric and, more significantly, that it is even as well as nonincreasing on half its period. This monotonicity property solves an open problem and an obstruction which arose in an earlier attempt, by other authors, to show the existence of minimizers.

Introduction

For a general set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the nonlocal (or
s
-fractional,
0<s<1
) mean curvature of

\partial E
(or of
E
) at a boundary point
x\in\partial E
is defined by

(1.1)

H_{s}[E](x):=PV\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}dy\,\frac{\chi_{E^{c}}(y)-\chi_{E}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+s}}=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\{|x-y|>\epsilon\}´}dy\,\frac{\chi_{E^{c}}(y)-\chi_{E}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+s}}.

Here
PV
denotes the principal value sense,
\chi_{E}
is the characteristic function of the set
E,
and
E^{c}
is the complement in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of the set
E
. The nonlocal mean curvature arises when computing the first variation of the fractional perimeter, an extension of the classical perimeter that was introduced, in its localized or Dirichlet version, in the pioneering article of Caffarelli, Roquejoffre, and Savin
[
13
]
. Their work has allowed for a natural extension of the theory of minimal surfaces to the nonlocal framework.

The current paper concerns the nonlocal analogue of the classical result of Delaunay
[
17
]
on the existence of periodic surfaces of revolution with constant mean curvature (CMC).
We provide the first variational proof of the existence of periodic surfaces of revolution having constant nonlocal mean curvature and a prescribed (but arbitrary) volume within one period. Throughout the paper, surfaces with constant nonlocal mean curvature will be called nonlocal-CMC surfaces.
Before our work, and up to our knowledge, the only papers which had established the existence of some periodic nonlocal-CMC surfaces are
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
, by Cabré, Fall, Solà-Morales, and Weth. These works
did not use variational methods,
but perturbative techniques (the implicit function theorem, essentially). Thus, they only obtained nonlocal-CMC surfaces which are very close to certain explicit configurations, as explained next.

In arbitrary dimension,
[
11
]
showed the existence of a one parameter family of periodic nonlocal-CMC surfaces bifurcating from a straight cylinder. The paper extended a previous analogous result in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
from
[
10
]
.
1
1

1

Such nonlocal-CMC curves in the plane have no analogue, obviously, for the classical mean curvature.
Later, also by perturbation techniques,

[
12
]
proved the existence of a periodic array of disjoint near-balls, the full array having constant nonlocal mean curvature. They bifurcate from infinity, starting from a single round ball of radius
1
—in particular, the near-balls have all the same shape (which is very close to a unit ball) and are very distant from each other. Thus, after rescaling them to have their consecutive centers at distance

2\pi
-periodic near-cylinders from
[

have a certain positive, bounded below, width. Prior to the current paper, no result connecting both configurations, through other periodic nonlocal-CMC surfaces, was available.

Recall that, in the local case, the classical Delaunay surfaces vary continuously from a cylinder to an infinite compound of tangent spheres.

This paper establishes for the first time the existence of a periodic nonlocal-CMC surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for every given volume within a slab.
By a variational method
we show the existence of a minimizer of the functional

(introduced for the first time in

]

under a volume constraint within the slab

\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\,-\pi<x_{1}<\pi\}
, among subsets
E
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=\{x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\}
which are
2\pi
-periodic in the coordinate
x_{1}

Notice that the functional

differs from the fractional
s
-perimeter of

\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\,-\pi<x_{1}<\pi\}

{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]+\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus E)\cap\{-\pi<x_{1}<\pi\}}\!dx\int_{E\cap\{|y_{1}|>\pi\}}dy\,\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+s}}.

Still, and somehow surprisingly,
we establish that the first variation of these two functionals —the periodic first variation for (
1.2

1.3

.

Given

In addition, up to sets of measure zero:

1.1.
Symmetry and monotonicity properties of minimizers

Theorem
1.1
contains two results on the symmetry and monotonicity properties of minimizing sets: their cylindrical symmetry, (
1.5
), and the fact that
u
is an even function which is nonincreasing in
(0,\pi).
These properties will follow from two rearrangement inequalities.

The first one concerns the
cylindrical rearrangement
, defined as follows. For a measurable set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, consider its sections
E_{x_{1}}=\{x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}:\,(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in E\}
, where
x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Define
E^{*\!\operatorname{cyl}}
as the set whose sections
(E^{*\!\operatorname{cyl}})_{x_{1}}
for every
x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
are concentric open balls of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
centered at
0
and of the same
(n-1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure as
E_{x_{1}}
. The fact that the cylindrical rearrangement does not increase the functional
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}
follows from the Riesz rearrangement inequality, and it was already used in
[
16
, Proposition 13]
. It will lead to the existence of a cylindrical symmetric minimizer. A stronger fact is that
every minimizer is cylindrically symmetric, as stated in
Theorem

1.1
. This will follow from a strict version of the Riesz rearrangement inequality which analyzes the case of equality; see Lemma
3.1
.

The second rearrangement that we need to consider is the
symmetric decreasing periodic rearrangement
, which is defined in the following way. First, for a measurable set
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
consider

A^{*}:=\text{Steiner symmetrization of $A$ with respect to }\{x_{1}=0\}.

This means that
A^{*}
is symmetric with respect to the hyperplane
\{x_{1}=0\}
and that
A^{*}\cap\{x^{\prime}=c\}
is an open interval of the same
1
-dimensional Lebesgue measure as
A\cap\{x^{\prime}=c\}
for every
c\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}.
Now, given a set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
which is
2\pi
-periodic in the
x_{1}
-variable we define its symmetric decreasing periodic rearrangement by

E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}:=\text{$2\pi$-periodic extension of }\left(E\cap\{-\pi<x_{1}<\pi\}\right)^{*},

where the periodic extension is meant in the coordinate
x_{1}
.
It is called “symmetric decreasing” since the characteristic function
\chi_{E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}}
is nonincreasing in
x_{1}
within half of the period,
(0,\pi)
, for each given
x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
. Note also that if
E
is cylindrically symmetric, i.e., as in (
1.5
), then

(1.6)

E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,|x^{\prime}|<u^{*\!\operatorname{per}}(x_{1})\},

where
u^{*\!\operatorname{per}}
is the
2\pi
-periodic function which is even, nonincreasing in
(0,\pi)
, and equimeasurable with
u
(see
Section

3
for the precise definition).

Our second main result establishes that the periodic fractional perimeter
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}
in (
1.2
) does not increase under symmetric decreasing periodic rearrangement. This was left as an open problem in the last paragraph of
[
16
]
. Our result also characterizes the case of equality.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a measurable set which is
2\pi
-periodic in the coordinate
x_{1}
and satisfies
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]<+\infty.

Then,
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}]\leq{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E].
Moreover, if

{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}]={\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]
then, up to a set of measure zero,
E=E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}+ce_{1}
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where
e_{1}:=(1,0,\ldots,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

A key ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
is a Riesz rearrangement inequality on the circle
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
, a result which is not as well-known as the classical Riesz rearrangement inequality on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Such periodic rearrangement inequality is originally due to Baernstein and Taylor
[
7
]
and to Friedberg and Luttinger
[
19
]
, who proved independently different versions of the inequality around the same time; see more details in the comments following Theorem
3.2
. The case of equality in the Riesz rearrangement inequality on the circle (and more generally on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
) was first treated by Burchard and Hajaiej in
[
8
]
.

1.2.
On the shape of minimizers: stability of straight cylinders

Let us first recall what is known about the shape of minimizers in the local case, that is, for the classical isoperimetric problem relative to a slab.

6

In the local case, the isoperimetric problem in a slab is commonly formulated without a periodicity condition. To relate this setting with the periodic one, one considers an even reflection of the slab minimizer. In this way, a period corresponds to twice the width of the slab.

It turns out that, for

, the minimizers are either spheres or straight cylinders, depending on whether the volume constraint is smaller or greater, respectively, than a critical volume; see
[

When

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

and

-periodic functions in

with values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

Then, this last function belongs also to
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}\times(-\pi,\pi))

(2.1)

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!dx\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\!dy\;\Phi\big(u(x),v(y),|x-y|\big)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\!dx\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!dy\;\Phi\big(u(x),v(y),|x-y|\big).

Proof.

In the integral on the left-hand side of (

|\Phi|
and for

, we can write the domain of integration

as the union over all
\big((2k-1)\pi,(2k+1)\pi\big),

(x,y)\in(-\pi,\pi)\times\big((2k-1)\pi,(2k+1)\pi\big)
. For each

we carry out the change of variables

and
y=\overline{y}+2k\pi,
where
(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in\big((-2k-1)\pi,(-2k+1)\pi\big)\times(-\pi,\pi).
Now, the periodicity of

and

, and the identity
|x-y|=|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|
, yield the claim of the lemma.

Let

(0,\pi)

and the left-hand side of (

then

) if and only if at least one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

either

(ii)

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let us first show that
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}]\leq{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E].

In contrast to the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we now write
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]
as

{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}dx^{\prime}\int_{E_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}dx_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}dy^{\prime}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus E_{y^{\prime}}}dy_{1}\,\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+s}},

where we have set
E_{x^{\prime}}:=\{x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}:\,(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in E\}
(in analogy with the sections
E_{x_{1}}
defined above).
Writing
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}]
in the same way (simply replacing
E
by
E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}
) and interchanging the order of integration
dx_{1}\,dy^{\prime}
, we see that it is sufficient to prove that

(3.13)

\int_{E_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}dx_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus E_{y^{\prime}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}\geq\int_{(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}dx_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}

for almost every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
.

For this, set

a=|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma=\frac{n+s}{2}\quad\text{ (and thus }|x-y|^{n+s}=\big((x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+a^{2}\big)^{\gamma}\text{)},

observe that the following integrals are all finite when
a\neq 0
, and that the condition
a\neq 0
holds for almost every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
. We have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus E_{y^{\prime}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}-\int_{E_{y^{\prime}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}

and

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}-\int_{(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}.

From the equality
|E_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)|=|(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)|,
we infer that

\int_{E_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}dx_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}=\int_{(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}dx_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{dy_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+s}}.

Therefore, to prove (
3.13
) it is enough to show that

(3.14)

\int_{E_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}dx_{1}\!\int_{E_{y^{\prime}}}\!\frac{dy_{1}}{((x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+a^{2})^{\gamma}}\leq\int_{(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}dx_{1}\!\int_{(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}}\!\frac{dy_{1}}{((x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+a^{2})^{\gamma}}

for all
a\neq 0
.

In order to do this, recall that
(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}=(E_{y^{\prime}})^{*\!\operatorname{per}}
and that both
E_{y^{\prime}}
and
(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}
are
2\pi
-periodic. Taking these facts in consideration, (
3.14
) will follow if we show that, for every two measurable subsets
A
and
B
of
(-\pi,\pi)
, it holds

\begin{split}\int_{A}dx_{1}\int_{\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(B+2k\pi)}&\frac{dy_{1}}{((x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+a^{2})^{\gamma}}\leq\int_{A^{*}}dx_{1}\int_{\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(B^{*}+2k\pi)}\frac{dy_{1}}{((x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+a^{2})^{\gamma}}.\end{split}

By the change of variables
\overline{y}_{1}=y_{1}-2k\pi
, this can be equivalently written as

(3.15)

\int_{A}dx_{1}\int_{B}d\overline{y}_{1}\,g(x_{1}-\overline{y}_{1})\leq\int_{A^{*}}dx_{1}\int_{B^{*}}d\overline{y}_{1}\,g(x_{1}-\overline{y}_{1})\text{ for all subsets $A$ and $B$ of $(-\pi,\pi)$},

where we have set

(3.16)

g(z):=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{1}{((z+2k\pi)^{2}+a^{2})^{\gamma}}.

Clearly, the function
g
is positive,
2\pi
-periodic, and even. We will next show that
g
is decreasing in
(0,\pi)
, and hence (
3.15
) will follow from Theorem
3.2
applied to the characteristic functions
f=\chi_{A}
and
h=\chi_{B}
.

In order to show that
g
is decreasing in
(0,\pi)
, we introduce the Laplace transform of the function
t\mapsto t^{\gamma-1}
, which amounts to the equality

(3.17)

w^{-\gamma}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,t^{\gamma-1}e^{-wt}\quad\text{for all $w>0$,}

where

\Gamma(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dr\,{r}^{\gamma-1}e^{-r}

is the Gamma function.
The identity (
3.17
) follows simply by a change of variables
r=wt
in the definition of
\Gamma(\gamma)
.
Using (
3.17
) in the definition of
g
yields

(3.18)

g(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,t^{\gamma-1}e^{-((z+2k\pi)^{2}+a^{2})t}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,t^{\gamma-1}e^{-a^{2}t}G_{t}(z),

where

G_{t}(z):=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-(z+2k\pi)^{2}t}.

We will now show that
G_{t}
is decreasing in
(0,\pi)
for every
t>0
, yielding, in view of (
3.18
), that
g
is also decreasing in
(0,\pi).
The claim on the strict monotonicity of
G_{t}
follows directly from the fact that the fundamental solution of the heat equation in
(-\pi,\pi)
with periodic boundary conditions (or, in other words, of the heat equation on the circle), which is

\Lambda_{t}(z):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\frac{(z+2k\pi)^{2}}{4t}},

is decreasing in
(0,\pi)
for all
t>0
. For the sake of completeness, in
Theorem

B.1
we give a simple proof of this well-known fact, based solely on maximum principles for the heat equation.

We now prove that if
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}]={\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]<+\infty
, then
E=E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}+ce_{1}
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, up to a set of measure zero. Indeed, in view of (
3.13
) and the equalities following it, if
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}]={\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]
then there must be equality in (
3.14
) for almost every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
. Equivalently, using the formulation of (
3.15
),

\begin{split}\int_{E_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}\!\!\!dx_{1}\int_{E_{y^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}\!\!\!dy_{1}\,g(x_{1}-y_{1})=\int_{(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}\!\!\!dx_{1}\int_{(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)}\!\!\!dy_{1}\,g(x_{1}-y_{1})\end{split}

for almost every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
.

Now, let
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
be the set of points
x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
for which
E_{x^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)
is neither
(-\pi,\pi)
nor
\varnothing
(up to zero measure sets). We may assume that
J
has positive
(n-1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure —if
J
has measure zero then
E=E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}
up to a set of measure zero in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and we would be done.
Then,
J\times J
has positive measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
, and (
3
) holds for almost every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in J\times J
. Note that, by definition of
J
, we don’t fall in case
(i)
of Theorem
3.2
when using (
3
) on points
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
in
J
. Hence, by (
3
) and
Theorem

3.2

(ii)
we deduce that, for almost every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in J\times J
, we have

E_{x^{\prime}}=(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{x^{\prime}}+c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\quad\text{ and }\quad E_{y^{\prime}}=(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}+c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

for some constant
c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}
depending, a priori, on
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
.
This easily yields that
c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=c
is actually independent of
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
. Indeed, considering two different values
x^{\prime}\in J
and
\overline{x}^{\prime}\in J
for the same
y^{\prime}
, we have

(3.19)

(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}+c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=E_{y^{\prime}}=(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}+c(\overline{x}^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Since
y^{\prime}\in J
,
(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}
is a
2\pi
-periodic set for which
(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}\cap(-\pi,\pi)
is a nonempty open
interval different from
(-\pi,\pi)
. Therefore (
3.19
) yields that
c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=c(\overline{x}^{\prime},y^{\prime})
modulo
2\pi
. By symmetry,
c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is also independent of
y^{\prime}
. Therefore,

E_{y^{\prime}}=(E^{*\!\operatorname{per}})_{y^{\prime}}+c
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
and all
y^{\prime}\in J
. By the definition of
J
, we conclude that
E=E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}+ce_{1}
up to a set of measure zero.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

From the last part of the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, in particular in what regards (
3.17
), we see that
Theorem

1.2
easily extends to any kernel which is the Laplace transform of a nonnegative function. More precisely, consider a periodic nonlocal perimeter functional of the form

{\mathcal{P}}_{K}[E]:=\int_{E\cap\{-\pi<x_{1}<\pi\}}dx\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus E}dy\,K(|x-y|^{2}),

where
K:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
is a kernel given by

K(w):=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,\kappa(t)e^{-wt},\quad w>0,

for some measurable function
\kappa:(0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
which is not identically zero. Then, Theorem
1.2
holds also for
{\mathcal{P}}_{K}
.

Indeed, arguing as in the proof of Theorem
1.2
up to (
3.15
), one sees that it is enough to show that

(3.20)

\int_{A}dx_{1}\int_{B}dy_{1}\,g_{K}(x_{1}-y_{1})\leq\int_{A^{*}}dx_{1}\int_{B^{*}}dy_{1}\,g_{K}(x_{1}-y_{1})\quad\text{for all subsets $A$ and $B$ of $(-\pi,\pi)$},

where

(3.21)

g_{K}(z):=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}K((z+2k\pi)^{2}+a^{2}),\quad z\in{\mathbb{R}}.

But

(3.22)

g_{K}(z)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,\kappa(t)e^{-((z+2k\pi)^{2}+a^{2})t}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,\kappa(t)e^{-a^{2}t}G_{t}(z)

is a decreasing function in
(0,\pi)
by
Theorem

B.1
and by the fact that
\kappa\geq 0
is not identically zero in

(0,+\infty)
. Hence, Theorem
3.2
still applies in this framework and, thus, we deduce that
{\mathcal{P}}_{K}
is nonincreasing under periodic decreasing rearrangement. Moreover, as before, if

{\mathcal{P}}_{K}[E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}]={\mathcal{P}}_{K}[E]
then
E=E^{*\!\operatorname{per}}+ce_{1}
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, up to a set of measure zero.

Appendix A
Periodic minimizers in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

In this appendix we present, when
n=2
, an expression for the functional
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}
which is simpler than that of
Lemma

2.2
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. As mentioned in the Introduction, this expression allowed us to prove the existence of minimizer when
n=2
in a simple way. We later found the existence proof of Theorem
1.1
in all dimensions.

The formula for the periodic version of the fractional perimeter when
n=2
reads as follows.
If
E=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\,|x_{2}|<u(x_{1})\}
, for some nonnegative
2\pi
-periodic function
u
, then
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]
can be written in terms of
u
as

(A.1)

{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx_{1}\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy_{1}\,\frac{2}{|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{s}}\Big\{G\Big(\frac{u(x_{1})-u(y_{1})}{|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\Big)+H\Big(\frac{u(x_{1})+u(y_{1})}{|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\Big)\Big\},

where

(A.2)

G(t):=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,(t-\tau)(1+\tau^{2})^{-\frac{2+s}{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad H(t):=G^{\prime}(+\infty)t-G(t)\quad\text{for $t\in{\mathbb{R}}$.}

Note that
G(0)=0
,

(A.3)

0<G^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,(1+\tau^{2})^{-\frac{2+s}{2}}<G^{\prime}(+\infty)<+\infty,

and

G^{\prime\prime}(t)=(1+t^{2})^{-\frac{2+s}{2}}>0
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Therefore,
G
is a nonnegative even function which is increasing in

(0,+\infty)
and strictly convex on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Furthermore, it behaves as a quadratic function near the origin and approaches a linear one as
t\to\pm\infty
.
On the other hand,
H(0)=0
,
H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}(+\infty)-G^{\prime}>0
, and
H^{\prime\prime}=-G^{\prime\prime}<0
in
[0,+\infty).

Thus,
H
is positive, increasing, and strictly concave in
[0,+\infty)
. Finally, by Fubini’s theorem,

\begin{split}H(+\infty)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,H^{\prime}(t)&=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,(G^{\prime}(+\infty)-G^{\prime}(t))=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\int_{t}^{+\infty}d\tau\,(1+\tau^{2})^{-\frac{2+s}{2}}\\
&=\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\tau\,\tau(1+\tau^{2})^{-\frac{2+s}{2}}<+\infty.\end{split}

For the sake of completeness we will give the proof of (
A.1
), even though this expression was not used to prove the results of this paper. But before, let us explain how to use (
A.1
) to give a simple proof of the existence of a constrained minimizer of
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}
when
n=2
. The argument goes as follows. Since
G(t)+C_{1}\geq C_{2}|t|
for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, the term in (
A.1
) involving

G
bounds the
W^{s,1}(-\pi,\pi)
norm of
u
up to an additive constant.
In this way, since
H\geq 0
, a minimizing sequence for
{\mathcal{P}}_{s}
is a bounded sequence in
W^{s,1}(-\pi,\pi)
. By compactness, this yields the existence of the desired constrained minimizer. We refer to
[
2
]
for the details on this argument; see also the end of Part 1 in our proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
u:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be nonnegative,
2\pi
-periodic, and such that
u\in W^{s,1}(-\pi-\epsilon,\pi+\epsilon)
for some (and hence for all)

\epsilon>0
. Let

E=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\,|x_{2}|<u(x_{1})\}
. Then,
(
A.1
) holds.

Proof.

We prove (
A.1
) using (
2.3
). Let us first rewrite
\phi
in (
2.4
) for
n=2
in a more convenient way by introducing the function
G^{\prime}
. For
p\geq 0
and
q\geq 0
we have (using the change of variables
z=\tau+w
twice)

\begin{split}\phi(p,q)&=\int_{-p}^{p}dw\,\Big\{\int_{-\infty}^{-q}dz\,(1+|z-w|^{2})^{-\frac{2+s}{2}}+\int^{+\infty}_{q}dz\,(1+|z-w|^{2})^{-\frac{2+s}{2}}\Big\}\\
&=\int_{-p}^{p}dw\,\Big\{G^{\prime}(-q-w)-G^{\prime}(-\infty)+G^{\prime}(+\infty)-G^{\prime}(q-w)\Big\}.\end{split}

Note that
G^{\prime}
is odd,
0<-G^{\prime}(-\infty)=G^{\prime}(+\infty)<+\infty
, and
G^{\prime}(-q-\cdot)-G^{\prime}(q-\cdot)
is an even function. Therefore,

\begin{split}\phi(p,q)&=2\int_{0}^{p}dw\,\Big\{G^{\prime}(-q-w)-G^{\prime}(q-w)+2G^{\prime}(+\infty)\Big\}\\
&=4G^{\prime}(+\infty)p+2\int_{0}^{p}dw\,G^{\prime}(-q-w)-2\int_{0}^{p}dw\,G^{\prime}(q-w)\\
&=4G^{\prime}(+\infty)p-2\int_{-q}^{-q-p}dt\,G^{\prime}(t)+2\int_{q}^{q-p}dt\,G^{\prime}(t)\\
&=2\big(2G^{\prime}(+\infty)p+G(p-q)-G(p+q)\big),\end{split}

where we also used in the last equality that
G
is even. Now, from (
2.3
) and (
A
) we deduce that

\begin{split}{\mathcal{P}}_{s}[E]&=2\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy_{1}\,|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{-s}\\
&\phantom{aaaaaaaa}\times\Big\{G\Big(\frac{u(x_{1})-u(y_{1})}{|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\Big)-G\Big(\frac{u(x_{1})+u(y_{1})}{|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\Big)+2G^{\prime}(+\infty)\,\frac{u(x_{1})}{|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\Big\}.\end{split}

Our goal is to show that in the term involving

2G^{\prime}(+\infty)u(x_{1})|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{-1}
we can replace
u(x_{1})
by
u(y_{1})
and the identity (
A
) remains unchanged. Once this is proved, we get that in this last term one can replace
2u(x_{1})
by
u(x_{1})+u(y_{1})
. Hence, using that
H(t)=G^{\prime}(+\infty)t-G(t)
, this gives (
A.1
).

Therefore, it only remains to show that we can replace
u(x_{1})
by
u(y_{1})
in the last term on the right-hand side of (
A
).
This follows immediately from
Lemma

2.1
(used with
m=l=1
) applied to the integral

(A.4)

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy\,\frac{f(x,y)}{|x-y|^{s}},

where

f(x,y):=-G\Big(\frac{u(x)+u(y)}{|x-y|}\Big)+2G^{\prime}(+\infty)\,\frac{u(x)}{|x-y|}.

To check the hypothesis of the lemma, we need to verify that

(A.5)

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy\,\frac{|f(x,y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}<+\infty.

For this purpose we write
f(x,y)=A+B,
where

A:=-G\Big(\frac{u(x)+u(y)}{|x-y|}\Big)+G\Big(\frac{2u(x)}{|x-y|}\Big)

and

B:=-G\Big(\frac{2u(x)}{|x-y|}\Big)+2G^{\prime}(+\infty)\,\frac{u(x)}{|x-y|}=H\Big(\frac{2u(x)}{|x-y|}\Big).

Using that
u
is nonnegative,
H
is nonnegative and increasing in
(0,+\infty)
, and
H(+\infty)<+\infty
, we have

(A.6)

0\leq B\leq H(+\infty)<+\infty.

Concerning the quantity
A
, observe that

(A.7)

|A|=\left|\int_{\frac{u(x)+u(y)}{|x-y|}}^{\frac{2u(x)}{|x-y|}}dt\,G^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}|G^{\prime}(t)|\,\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|}=G^{\prime}(+\infty)\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|}.

Recall that we are assuming
u\in W^{s,1}(-\pi-\epsilon,\pi+\epsilon)
for some
\epsilon>0
. According to this, to prove (
A.5
) we split the integral and we use (
A.6
) and (
A.7
) to get

\begin{split}&\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy\,\frac{|f(x,y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{|x-y|\leq\epsilon}dy\,\frac{|A|+|B|}{|x-y|^{s}}+\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{|x-y|>\epsilon}dy\,\frac{|f(x,y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}\\
&\leq G^{\prime}(+\infty)\|u\|_{W^{s,1}(-\pi-\epsilon,\pi+\epsilon)}+H(+\infty)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{-\pi-\epsilon}^{\pi+\epsilon}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{s}}+\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{|x-y|>\epsilon}dy\,\frac{|f(x,y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}.\end{split}

The first two terms on the right-hand side of (
A
) are finite.
To estimate the third one, we use that
0\leq G(t)\leq G^{\prime}(+\infty)t
for all
t\geq 0,
and hence

\begin{split}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{|x-y|>\epsilon}dy\,\frac{|f(x,y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}\leq&\,G^{\prime}(+\infty)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{|x-y|>\epsilon}dy\,\frac{3u(x)}{|x-y|^{1+s}}\\
&+G^{\prime}(+\infty)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{|x-y|>\epsilon}dy\,\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{1+s}}.\end{split}

The first double integral in the right-hand side of (
A
) is finite, since
s>0
and
u\in W^{s,1}(-\pi-\epsilon,\pi+\epsilon)\subset L^{1}(-\pi,\pi)
.
For the second double integral, since
u
is
2\pi
-periodic and
nonnegative, using
Lemma

2.1
we deduce

(A.8)

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{|x-y|>\epsilon}dy\,\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{1+s}}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}dy\,\frac{\chi_{\{|x-y|>\epsilon\}}}{|x-y|^{1+s}}\,u(y)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\overline{y}\,u(\overline{y})\int_{|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|>\epsilon}\frac{d\overline{x}}{|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|^{1+s}}.

As before, this is finite since
s>0
and

u\in L^{1}(-\pi,\pi)
.
Hence (
A.5
) follows from (
A
) and the finiteness of the integrals in (
A
).
∎

Appendix B
Strict monotonicity of the periodic heat kernel

In this section we address the proof of an important ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, namely, that the function defined in (
3.16
) is decreasing in
(0,\pi)
. As shown there, this property follows from the fact that the periodic heat kernel is decreasing with respect to the spatial variable in
(0,\pi)
. This is a well-known fact
[
1
,
3
,
14
,
15
,
24
]
, whose proofs are commented next. Here, for the sake of completeness, we provide a simple proof based on maximum principles for the heat equation.

Prior to this, let us briefly comment on the proofs given in the above-mentioned references. The one in
[
1
]
follows exactly the same lines as the one presented here, but it is carried out within the context of hypersurfaces of revolution. The one in
[
15
]
is also based on the strong maximum principle for the heat equation, but it is more technically involved than
[
1
]
, since a more general class of manifolds is considered there. The proof in

[
3
]
is based on an explicit computation. The one in
[
14
]
follows by expressing the derivative of the kernel in terms of the heat kernel in smaller dimensions and using an induction argument; see
[
14
, §6.3]
. Finally, the one in
[
24
]
is a straightforward application of the sharp estimates for the heat kernel on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
found there.

The fundamental solution of the heat equation in
(-\pi,\pi)
with periodic boundary conditions is given by

(B.1)

\Gamma(z,t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{(z+2k\pi)^{2}}{4t}}\quad\text{for $z\in{\mathbb{R}}$ and $t>0$}.

Given a
2\pi
-periodic initial data
g,
the function

(B.2)

u(x,t)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dy\,\Gamma(x-y,t)g(y)

satisfies

\partial_{t}u-\partial_{xx}u=0
in
{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,+\infty)
, that
u(\cdot,t)
is
2\pi
-periodic for all
t>0
, and that

u(\cdot,0)=g
in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
This follows immediately from the properties of the standard (nonperiodic) heat kernel and the fact that

\begin{split}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dy\,e^{-\frac{(x-y+2k\pi)^{2}}{4t}}g(y)&=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dy\,e^{-\frac{(x-y+2k\pi)^{2}}{4t}}g(y-2k\pi)\\
&=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{-\pi-2k\pi}^{\pi-2k\pi}d\overline{y}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\overline{y})^{2}}{4t}}g(\overline{y})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}d\overline{y}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\overline{y})^{2}}{4t}}g(\overline{y}).\end{split}

We now state and prove the result on the periodic heat kernel that we used in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Theorem B.1
.

For every
t>0
, the function

z\mapsto\Gamma(z,t)\quad\text{is decreasing in }(0,\pi).

Proof.

Let us first show that
\Gamma(\cdot,t)
is nonincreasing in
(0,\pi)
. For this, we take functions
g_{\epsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
, where
0<\epsilon<\pi/2
, approximating the Dirac delta as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, and we consider their
2\pi
-periodic extensions from
[-\pi,\pi]
to
{\mathbb{R}}
(which we also denote by
g_{\epsilon}
). In particular, we assume that

\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}g_{\epsilon}=1\quad\text{for all $0<\epsilon<\pi/2$}.

We additionally assume that the
g_{\epsilon}
are nonnegative, even, and nonincreasing in
(0,\pi)
.

Let
u_{\epsilon}
be the solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u_{\epsilon}-\partial_{xx}u_{\epsilon}=0&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,+\infty),\\
u_{\epsilon}(\cdot,0)=g_{\epsilon}&\text{ in ${\mathbb{R}}$}.\end{array}\right.

Thus,
u_{\epsilon}(x,t)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dy\,\Gamma(x-y,t)g_{\epsilon}(y)
and
u_{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)
is
2\pi
-periodic for all
t>0
. Notice that
u_{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)
is even with respect to
x=0
and
x=\pi
(by uniqueness, since so is
g_{\epsilon}
). We deduce that the derivative
v_{\epsilon}:=\partial_{x}u_{\epsilon}
solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v_{\epsilon}-\partial_{xx}v_{\epsilon}=0&\text{ in }(0,\pi)\times(0,+\infty),\\
v_{\epsilon}(0,t)=v_{\epsilon}(\pi,t)=0&\text{ for all $t>0$}.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, by the assumptions on
g_{\epsilon}
, we have that

v_{\epsilon}(x,0)=\partial_{x}u_{\epsilon}(x,0)=g_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for all
x\in[0,\pi]
.
Hence, the maximum principle yields

(B.3)

v^{\epsilon}\leq 0\quad\text{ in }[0,\pi]\times[0,+\infty).

We now claim that, for every given
t>0
and
m=0,1,2,\ldots
,

(B.4)

u_{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)\text{ converges to }\Gamma(\cdot,t)\text{ in $C^{m}([0,\pi])$ as $\epsilon\downarrow 0$}.

This follows by combining the uniform continuity in

[0,\pi]
of
\Gamma(\cdot,t)
and of all its derivatives with the fact that, for every
\delta>0
, we have

\big|\partial^{m}_{x}u_{\epsilon}(x,t)-\partial^{m}_{x}\Gamma(x,t)\big|\leq\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}dy\,\big|\partial^{m}_{x}\Gamma(x-y,t)-\partial^{m}_{x}\Gamma(x,t)\big||g_{\epsilon}(y)|\leq\delta

if
\epsilon
is chosen small enough.

Now, from (
B.3
) and (
B.4
) we conclude that

\partial_{x}\Gamma(x,t)=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\partial_{x}u_{\epsilon}(x,t)=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}v_{\epsilon}(x,t)\leq 0

for all
x\in[0,\pi]
and
t>0
.

Finally, we show that
\Gamma(\cdot,t)
is decreasing in
(0,\pi)
. For this, notice that we already proved that, for all
t>0
,
\Gamma(\cdot,t)
is even with respect to
x=0
and
x=\pi
. Therefore, given any
t_{0}>0
, we see that
v:=\partial_{x}\Gamma
solves

(B.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v-\partial_{xx}v=0&\text{ in }(0,\pi)\times(t_{0},+\infty),\\
v(0,t)=v(\pi,t)=0&\text{ for all $t>t_{0}$},\\
v(\cdot,t_{0})=\partial_{x}\Gamma(\cdot,t_{0})\leq 0&\text{ in $[0,\pi]$}.\end{array}\right.

Now, the strong maximum principle yields that either
v<0
in
(0,\pi)\times(t_{0},+\infty)
or
v\equiv 0
in

[0,\pi]\times[t_{0},+\infty)
. The proof is now complete, since
v\equiv 0
is absurd —it would give that
\Gamma(\cdot,t)
is constant in
x
, clearly contradicting the fact that
\Gamma
is the fundamental solution of the heat equation with periodic boundary conditions, as shown in the beginning of this appendix.
∎
