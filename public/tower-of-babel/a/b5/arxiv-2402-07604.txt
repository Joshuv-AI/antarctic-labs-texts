THE SIEGEL MODULAR GROUP IS THE LATTICE OF
MINIMAL COVOLUME IN THE SYMPLECTIC GROUP

Abstract




Abstract.
Let
n\geqslant 2
. We prove that, up to conjugation,
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z})
is the unique lattice in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
of the smallest covolume.

Keywords:
Symplectic group, arithmetic group, lattice, covolume, Prasad’s volume formula

MSC:
22E40 (Primary), 11E57, 20G30, 51M25 (Secondary)

1
Introduction

A lattice in a semisimple Lie group
G
is a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
such that the
G
-invariant measure on the quotient
G/\Gamma
is finite. For example,
\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\bf Z})
is a lattice in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\bf R})
, and
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z})
is a lattice in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
, for any
n\geqslant 1
. In fact, by the work of Siegel, the covolumes of these lattices are known exactly: When the measures
\mu
on
G=\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\bf R}),\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
are suitably normalized, one has the beautiful formulas

\displaystyle\mu(G/\Gamma)=\begin{cases}\zeta(2)\zeta(3)\cdots\zeta(n),&\text{if }G=\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\bf R}),\\[3.00003pt]
\zeta(2)\zeta(4)\cdots\zeta(2n),&\text{if }G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R}),\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\Gamma=G\cap\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\bf Z})
in either case, and
\mu(G/\Gamma)
denotes the volume of a fundamental domain of
\Gamma
in
G
. However, both the special linear and the symplectic group contain many more lattices than these standard examples.

In this paper, we investigate lattices of
minimal
covolume; that is, lattices which are as dense as possible in their ambient groups. Moreover, we focus our attention on
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
. An obvious question is whether such a lattice necessarily exists — for example, in the case of the group
{\bf R}^{n}
, one may find lattices of arbitrarily large or small covolume. The setting when one considers a semisimple Lie group such as
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
, however, is more rigid, as the Kazdan–Margulis Theorem
[
14
]
shows that lattices of minimal covolume always exist. Clearly, such a lattice has to be maximal, i.e. not properly contained in any other lattice. Thus, we let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
be a lattice with minimal covolume.

By the celebrated arithmeticity theorem of Margulis (see e.g.
[
21
, Thm. 16.3.1]
), any irreducible lattice
\Lambda
in a semisimple Lie group
G
of real rank greater than
1
is also an arithmetic subgroup of
G
. In particular, if such a Lie group
G
is a simple, any lattice is arithmetic. That is, there exists a
{\bf Q}
-embedding
\iota:G\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\bf R})
such that
\Lambda
is commensurable to the group
G({\bf Z})=\iota^{-1}(\iota(G)\cap\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\bf Z}))
of integer points of
G
. For our purposes, the utility of this identification of lattices with arithmetic subgroups is that arithmetic subgroups are, in some sense, easier to describe. However, as any individual arithmetic subgroup of
G
is associated to a corresponding embedding of
G
into
\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\bf R})
, investigating all arithmetic subgroups involves the abstraction of treating all
{\bf Q}
-isomorphic copies of
G
in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\bf R})
equally.

We therefore let
\mathbb{G}
be an algebraic group over a number field
K
, for which there exists an embedding
\upsilon_{0}:K\rightarrow{\bf R}
which induces an
{\bf R}
-isomorphism from
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
(the standard split form of the symplectic group) to
\mathbb{G}
. This is enough to capture any candidate for
\Gamma
that we will want to consider. Moreover, it leads to a useful alternative characterization of
\Gamma
: Rohlfs gave a cohomological criterion
[
28
, Satz 3.5]
(see also
[
6
, Prop. 1.4]
) for maximality of arithmetic subgroups, according to which the lattice
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
must be conjugate to the normalizer of a so-called
principal arithmetic subgroup

\Lambda
of
\mathbb{G}(K)
. Such subgroups are distinguished by being topologically well-behaved in relation to the group
\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{A}_{K})
of adelic points of
\mathbb{G}
. In particular,
\Lambda
is given by the intersection
\mathbb{G}(K)\cap\Pi_{\upsilon<\infty}P_{\upsilon}
for a certain (topologically coherent) family of compact-open (more precisely,
parahoric
) subgroups
P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
, where
\upsilon
ranges over all finite places of
K
. These facts make the problem of computing the covolume of
\Lambda
, and thereby identifying a lattice
\Gamma
of minimal covolume, amenable to a wide range of analytic and algebraic tools.

The main tool for studying the covolume of principal arithmetic subgroups of certain well-behaved algebraic groups
\mathbb{G}
is
Prasad’s volume formula
, which expresses the covolume of
\Lambda
(identified with its diagonal embedding) in

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{\infty}:=\prod_{\upsilon\mid\infty}\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})

in terms of a variety of arithmetic invariants of the number field
K
and, additionally, in terms of the product of certain measures of the parahoric subgroups
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{P_{\upsilon}:\upsilon<\infty}}\right\}
(see Section
2.3
for more details). As such, it has been used in several works to identify lattices of minimal covolume in different Lie groups. Many concrete instances of this problem have been studied before: The case
\mathrm{SL}_{2}({\bf C})
was studied from different perspectives by Meyerhoff
[
20
]
, Gehring–Martin
[
10
]
, and Marshall–Martin
[
19
]
. Lubotzky
[
17
]
considered this group over function fields and studied minimal covolume lattices of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}((t^{-1})))
. For certain indefinite orthogonal groups, lattices of minimal covolume were identified by Belolipetsky
[
4
]
(in the case of
\mathrm{SO}(n,1)
) and Belolipetsky–Emery
[
5
]
(for
\mathrm{PO}(n,1)^{\circ}
). Also, Emery–Kim
[
8
]
considered the analogous question for the indefinite symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(1,n)
. Most recently, Thilmany
[
31
]
proved that in the special linear groups
\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\bf R})
with
n\geqslant 3
, the lattices of minimal covolume are precisely the conjugates of the standard lattice
\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\bf Z})
. By contrast, for the case
n=2
it was already known to Siegel
[
30
]
that the
(2,3,7)
triangle group
\langle s,t\mid s^{2}=t^{3}=(st)^{7}=1\rangle
has minimal covolume in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}({\bf R})
. In light of Thilmany’s result about
\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\bf R})
and the fact that
\mathrm{SL}_{2}({\bf R})=\mathrm{Sp}_{2}({\bf R})
, it is natural to ask about the corresponding minimal covolume lattices in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
. The purpose of this paper is to answer this question by proving the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geqslant 2
, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
be a lattice of minimal covolume. Then for some
g\in\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
,
\Gamma=g^{-1}\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z})g
.

1.1
Outline of the argument

We now describe the method of proof, which is based on
[
31
]
and other works in which questions of arithmetic lattices of minimal covolume are investigated.

By the symplectic part of the formula (
1.1
), we know the covolume of
\Gamma_{0}:=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z})
in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
explicitly. The idea is then to use this number as a reference, which is to be compared to the covolume
\mu(\mathbb{G}_{\infty}/\Lambda)
assigned by Prasad’s volume formula to an arbitrary principal arithmetic subgroup
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{G}(K)
, and thus, with the help of the relation

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbb{G}_{\infty}/\Gamma)=\frac{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma:\Lambda}}\right]}\mu(\mathbb{G}_{\infty}/\Lambda),

to the covolume of
\Gamma
. However, a subtle point here is that the parametrization of the Haar measure on the maximal compact subgroup
\mathrm{SO}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
implicit in the equality (
1.1
) is, in fact, different from the parametrization implicit in the volume formula; hence our reference covolume will have to be corrected with the appropriate factor, which
turns out to be
\Pi(n):=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(2\pi)^{-2j}(2j-1)!
. Thus, we will instead be comparing
\mu(\mathbb{G}_{\infty}/\Gamma)
to

\displaystyle\Psi(n):=\mu(\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})/\Gamma_{0})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta(2j)\frac{(2j-1)!}{(2\pi)^{2j}}=\Pi(n)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta(2j).

(1.2)

This comparison involves both global and local aspects, for which all relevant parameters are introduced and discussed in Section
2
.

As far as our global considerations are concerned, Prasad’s Volume Formula involves certain arithmetic invariants of the number field
K
, such as the absolute value
D_{K}
of the discriminant of
K
and the degree
d_{K}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[K:{\bf Q}}}\right]
. As we are interested in
\Gamma
of
minimal
covolume, the condition
\mu(\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})/\Gamma)\leqslant\Psi(n)
, which results from the minimality we demand of
\mu(\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})/\Gamma)
, will eventually put a number of restrictions on these arithmetic invariants, effectively narrowing down the list of possible number fields
K
that can realize
\Gamma
(in the sense of the discussion above).

The restrictions mentioned above are described explicitly in Section
3
, following a series of lower bounds on the covolume
\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma)
, where
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})
denotes the image of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
under the isomorphism induced by the embedding
\upsilon_{0}
discussed above. These bounds are the result of a form of compromise: on the one hand, it is difficult to keep track of too many parameters (e.g. the regulator
R_{K}
, the class number
h_{K}
, etc.) at the same time, so it is convenient to eliminate as many of these as we can by estimating them in terms of
d_{K}
; on the other hand, we also want the precision of the lower bounds that
do
involve these invariants. As such, we will initially use our crudest bounds to obtain upper bounds on
d_{K}
and
D_{K}
depending on the rank
n
of our group, and then subsequently apply detailed estimates for each single case where the arithmetic invariants are no longer abstract parameters, but concrete numbers that we can either compute or look up.

Once the number of admissible number fields
K
is sufficiently small, we can take the relevant local aspects into account and analyze them on a case-by-case basis to determine if they can give rise to a lattice of minimal covolume. After identifying
{\bf Q}
as the only possibility, we conclude that
\mathbb{G}
must be the split form of the symplectic group, and that
\Gamma
must be a conjugate of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z})
. This is accomplished in Section
4
.

1.2
Notation and conventions

We will use the following notation throughout:

•

{\bf Q}
,
{\bf R}
, and
{\bf C}
will denote the fields of rational, real, and complex numbers (respectively). For a prime number
p
,
{\bf Q}_{p}
will denote the field of
p
-adic numbers, and
{\bf Z}_{p}
its ring of integers.
\mathbb{F}_{q}
will denote the finite field with
q
elements.

•

K
will denote a number field, and
\mathbb{A}_{K}
will denote the corresponding ring of adeles. The degree of
K
(over
{\bf Q}
) will be denoted by
d_{K}
, and the absolute value of its discriminant (over
{\bf Q}
) will be denoted by
D_{K}
. The notation
\overline{K}
will be used to denote an algebraic closure of
K
.

•

\mathbb{G}
will denote a linear algebraic group defined over
K
, which is a real form of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{G}
will denote the unique quasisplit inner
K
-form of
\mathbb{G}
.

Without necessarily mentioning it explicitly, we will occasionally consider
\mathbb{G}(K)
and its subgroups as embedded diagonally into
\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{A}_{K})
whenever this is appropriate.

•

The symbol
\upsilon
will be used to denote a place of
K
, either infinite (in which case we write
\upsilon\mid\infty
) or finite (in which case we write
\upsilon<\infty
). Moreover,
\upsilon_{0}
will denote a distinguished real place of
K
, over which our algebraic group
\mathbb{G}
splits, i.e.
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})\simeq\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
.

•

P_{\upsilon}
(where
\upsilon
denotes a finite place of

) will denote a parahoric subgroup of
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
.

•

will denote a lattice of minimal covolume defined by the
K
-form
\mathbb{G}
of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
. (We will also consider the gamma function and denote it by
\Gamma
, but this will cause no ambiguity.)
\Gamma_{0}
will denote the Siegel modular group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z})
.

•

will denote the principal arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{\infty}
defined by the collection of parahorics
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{P_{\upsilon}:\upsilon<\infty}}\right\}
, for which

is its normalizer in the group
\mathbb{G}({\bf R})
(cf.
[
6
, Prop. 1.4.iv)]
).

•

\zeta
will denote the Riemann zeta function, and
\zeta_{K}
will denote the Dedekind zeta function of the number field

.

2
Preliminaries

We will now recall a number of definitions and results from algebraic number theory and the theory of algebraic and arithmetic groups. We end this section by describing the main technical tool needed in this paper, namely the volume formula due to G. Prasad.

2.1
Number fields

Let
K
be an algebraic number field, i.e. an extension of the rational numbers of degree
d_{K}<\infty
, and let
D_{K}
be the absolute value of its discriminant.

The discriminant is of course unbounded as a function of the degree, but it is possible to give lower bounds for
D_{K}
in terms of
d_{K}
. For our purposes, the series of bounds provided by Odlyzko for totally real
K
will suffice: These bounds have the form

\displaystyle D_{K}>A^{d_{K}}e^{-E},

(2.1)

where the pair
(A,E)
can be chosen from the list
[
23
]
.

In the remainder of this section, we will assume that
K
is totally real. This means that
K
has precisely
r=d_{K}
distinct embeddings
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{r}
into
{\bf C}
with
\sigma_{i}(K)\subseteq{\bf R}
for all
i
. If
\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq K
is the ring of algebraic integers of
K
, the multiplicative group
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
of units is a finitely generated abelian group and can therefore be decomposed as
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\simeq\mu(K)\times U_{K}
, where
\mu(K)
denotes the torsion subgroup, consisting of units of finite order, and
U_{K}
denotes the free part. Then the statement of Dirichlet’s unit theorem
[
22
, Thm. I.7.3]
is that
U_{K}
has rank
r-1=d_{K}-1
. Let us suppose that
K\neq{\bf Q}
so that this rank is non-zero. Then, any set of
r-1
units
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r-1}}}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
that generate the free group
U_{K}
is called a
fundamental system of units
or a collection of
fundamental units
. For example, if
K
is a quadratic number field, then a fundamental unit is given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\frac{a+b\sqrt{D_{K}}}{2},

(2.2)

where
(a,b)\in{\bf Z}_{+}^{2}
is the smallest pair of integers satisfying Pell’s equation
a^{2}-D_{K}b^{2}=\pm 4
.

Two particular subgroups of
U_{K}
will be of special interest to us, namely the group of
totally positive
units

\displaystyle U_{K}^{+}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}:\sigma_{i}(u)>0\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,d_{K}}}\right\},

and the group
U_{K}^{2}
of squares of units. If
u^{2}\in U_{K}^{2}
is any such square, then naturally
u^{2}
is totally positive as
\sigma_{i}(u)\in{\bf R}
for each
i
; that is,
U_{K}^{2}\subseteq U_{K}^{+}
is a subgroup. For its index we will need the following facts, which are straightforward consequences of Dirichlet’s unit theorem:

•

With no assumptions on the totally real number field
K
, one always has

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{K}^{+}:U_{K}^{2}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|U_{K}^{+}/U_{K}^{2}}}\right|\leqslant 2^{d_{K}+r_{K}-1}=2^{2d_{K}-1}.

(2.3)

•

If
K
is a real quadratic number field with fundamental unit
\varepsilon
, it is known that if
\varepsilon
is not totally positive, then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{K}^{+}:U_{K}^{2}}}\right]=1.

(2.4)

Under the Minkowski embedding

\displaystyle\log^{+}\!\circ\,\bm{\sigma}=(\log^{+}\!\circ\,\sigma_{1},\ldots,\log^{+}\!\circ\,\sigma_{r}):K^{\times}\longrightarrow{\bf R}^{r},\quad\quad\hskip-10.00002pt\alpha\longmapsto(\log|\sigma_{1}(\alpha)|,\ldots,\log|\sigma_{r}(\alpha)|),

the image
\bm{\sigma}(U_{K})\subseteq{\bf R}^{r}
of the multiplicative group
U_{K}
is a free additive group of rank
r-1
, or in other words, an
(r-1)
-dimensional lattice in its
(r-1)
-dimensional ambient space
\bm{\sigma}(U_{K})\otimes{\bf R}\subseteq{\bf R}^{r}
. (A collection of fundamental units could then also be defined as the preimage of any basis for
\bm{\sigma}(U_{K})
under
\log^{+}\!\circ\,\bm{\sigma}
.) Denoting the (finite) covolume of this lattice in
\bm{\sigma}(U_{K})\otimes{\bf R}
by

\displaystyle V_{K}:=\mathrm{vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\bm{\sigma}(U_{K})\otimes{\bf R})/\bm{\sigma}(U_{K})}}\right),

we obtain the
regulator
of
K
as the scaled covolume
R_{K}=V_{K}/\sqrt{r}=V_{K}/\sqrt{d_{K}}
. (In case
K={\bf Q}
, one defines
R_{K}:=1
.) It is a useful fact that this quantity can be bounded from below in terms of the degree
d_{K}
of
K
. Indeed, Zimmert proved
[
34
]
that for any totally real number field
K
,

\displaystyle R_{K}\geqslant 0.04e^{0.46\cdot d_{K}}.

(2.5)

Although the regulator of
K
does not appear in relation to Prasad’s volume formula, it is closely related to an invariant of
K
that appears in the crucial estimate (
3.1
) of the index
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma:\Lambda}}\right]
(see Section
2.3
). This invariant is the
class number

h_{K}
of
K
(see
[
22
, §I.6]
). The relation between
R_{K}
and
h_{K}
is given by the following version of the Brauer–Siegel formula, which appears at various places in the literature concerning the covolumes of arithmetic groups (e.g.
[
6
, eq. (6.1)]
).

Proposition 2.1

(Corollary to
[
29
]
)
.

Let
K
be a totally real number field of degree
d_{K}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[K:{\bf Q}}}\right]
, and let
\zeta_{K}
denote the Dedekind zeta function of
K
. Let
D_{K}
denote the absolute value of the discriminant of
K
, and let
h_{K}
and
R_{K}
denote the class number and the regulator of
K
, respectively. Then for any
t>0
, one has the estimate

\displaystyle R_{K}h_{k}\leqslant t(t+1)2^{1-d_{K}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{-d_{K}}D_{K}}}\right)^{(1+t)/2}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1+t}{2}}}\right)^{d_{K}}\zeta_{K}(1+t).

Proof.

We initially define the notation

\displaystyle N(\textbf{x})=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{d_{K}},\quad\quad\mathrm{Tr}(\textbf{x})=x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{d_{K}},\quad\quad\textbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d_{K}})\in{\bf R}^{d_{K}}.

If we let
\textbf{H}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{x}\in{\bf R}^{d_{K}}:N(\textbf{x})\geqslant 1}}\right\}
and
\lambda=\sqrt{D_{K}}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{res}}_{s=1}\zeta_{K}(s)
, we obtain from
[
29
, Lemma 1]
that, for any
s\in{\bf C}
,

\displaystyle\pi^{-{d_{K}}s/2}D_{K}^{s/2}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{s}{2}}}\right)^{d_{K}}\zeta_{K}(s)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\frac{\lambda}{s(s-1)}+\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}}\int_{\textbf{H}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N(\textbf{x})^{s/2}+N(\textbf{x})^{(1-s)/2}}}\right)e^{-\pi N(\mathfrak{m})^{2/{d_{K}}}D_{K}^{-1/{d_{K}}}\mathrm{Tr}(\textbf{x})}\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}}{x_{1}}\cdots\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{d}}{x_{d}},

since
K
is assumed to be totally real.

The definition of
H
implies that the sum over ideals
\mathfrak{m}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
is positive. If we suppose that
s
is real and satisfies
s>1
, then we have

\displaystyle\pi^{-{d_{K}}s/2}D_{K}^{s/2}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{s}{2}}}\right)^{d_{K}}\zeta_{K}(s)\geqslant\frac{\lambda}{s(s-1)},\quad\quad s>1.

(2.6)

By the class number formula
[
22
, Corollary 5.11]
, we have
\lambda=2^{{d_{K}}-1}R_{K}h_{K}
. The claim now follows from this by substituting
s=t+1
in (
2.6
), where
t>0
is arbitrary.
∎

In order to handle the gamma function appearing in the statement of Proposition
2.1
, we record the following “pointwise” version of Stirling’s formula due to Robbins
[
27
]
. Thus, if
n\geqslant 1
is arbitrary, one has the estimates

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{e}}}\right)^{n}e^{1/(12n+1)}<n!<\sqrt{2\pi n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{e}}}\right)^{n}e^{1/12n}.

(2.7)

We will also need to consider the adele ring
\mathbb{A}_{K}
of
K
. To describe this ring, we recall that a
place
of
K
is an equivalence class of valuations on
K
, where two valuations are deemed equivalent if they induce the same topology on
K
. Suppressing the formality of equivalence classes, we say that a
finite
place
\upsilon
of
K
is such a valuation
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\,\cdot\,}}\right|_{\upsilon}
which extends a
p
-adic valuation on
{\bf Q}
for some rational prime
p
. Equivalently, the completion
K_{\upsilon}
of
K
with respect to
\upsilon
is a finite extension of the field
{\bf Q}_{p}
of
p
-adic numbers. On the other hand, an embedding of
K
into the field
{\bf C}
of complex numbers is an
infinite
place of
K
. If the image of
K
under this embedding is real, the corresponding place is dubbed a
real
place; and if not, it is
complex
. The image of
K
is then
{\bf R}
or
{\bf C}
, respectively.

The
adele ring

\mathbb{A}_{K}
of
K
is the locally compact topological ring given by the restricted direct product

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{K}=\prod_{\upsilon\mid\infty}K_{\upsilon}\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{\upsilon<\infty}K_{\upsilon}=\lim_{\rightarrow}\mathbb{A}_{S},\quad\quad\mathbb{A}_{S}=\prod_{\upsilon\in S}K_{\upsilon}\times\prod_{\upsilon\not\in S}\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon},

where
S
runs over all finite subsets of places containing all the infinite places. We recall that the restricted direct product defining
\mathbb{A}_{K}
is characterized by the fact that, as far as the coordinates belonging to the finite places are concerned, at most finitely many coordinates lie outside of the ring
\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon}:=\mathcal{O}_{K_{\upsilon}}
of integers in
K_{\upsilon}
. In particular, the number field
K
embeds diagonally into
\mathbb{A}_{K}
. We refer to
[
24
, Chapter 1]
for more information.

2.2
Algebraic groups

We will now recall some standard definitions and results from the theory of linear algebraic groups (cf. also
[
24
, Chapters 2 and 3]
). Throughout this section,
K
will denote a number field, and
\mathbb{G}
will denote a linear algebraic group.

A
(linear) algebraic group
is a Zariski-closed subgroup
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\bf C})
of some special linear group over the complex numbers. In other words,
\mathbb{G}
can be identified with the vanishing locus of an ideal of polynomials. We say that
\mathbb{G}
is
defined over

K
, or that
\mathbb{G}
is an
K
-group
, if
\mathbb{G}
can be defined by a set of polynomials over
K
.

We say that an algebraic group
\mathbb{G}
is
simply connected
if any isogeny from a connected algebraic group to
\mathbb{G}
is trivial. Moreover, we will call
\mathbb{G}

K
-simple
if it does not contain any non-trivial connected, closed, normal subgroups defined over
K
. In case
\mathbb{G}
is defined over
K
and simple over an algebraic closure
\overline{K}
of
K
, we will call
\mathbb{G}

absolutely simple
.

For
K
-groups
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
, we say that
\mathbb{H}
is a
K
-form
of
\mathbb{G}
if there exists an isomorphism from
\mathbb{G}
to
\mathbb{H}
defined over
\overline{K}
. Two forms are said to be
equivalent
if they are isomorphic over
K
. Let
E(K,\mathbb{G})
be the set of all equivalence classes of
K
-forms of
\mathbb{G}
. The Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{K}/K)
acts on the set of all isomorphisms from
\mathbb{G}
to
\mathbb{H}
: If
\varphi:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
is an isomorphism and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{K}/K)
, then
\sigma.\varphi:=\varphi^{\sigma}
is the isomorphism obtained by applying
\sigma
to all the coefficients of the rational functions defining
\varphi
. Let now
\varphi
be any fixed isomorphism from
\mathbb{G}
to
\mathbb{H}
. Then we obtain a map
\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{K}/K)\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}_{\overline{K}}(\mathbb{G})
to the group of all
\overline{K}
-automorphisms of
\mathbb{G}
, which is given by
\sigma\mapsto\varphi^{-1}\circ\varphi^{\sigma}
. In the case when the image of this map in
\mathrm{Aut}_{\overline{K}}(\mathbb{G})
consists only of inner automorphisms of
\mathbb{G}
(considered as an algebraic group over
\overline{K}
), we say that
\mathbb{H}
is an
inner form
of
\mathbb{G}
. If not,
\mathbb{H}
is called an
outer form
of
\mathbb{G}
.

The group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
has no outer forms. Indeed, from
[
24
, Sect. 2.1.13]
it follows in particular that the number of isomorphism classes of outer forms of a group is equal to the number of graph automorphisms of its Dynkin diagram. Since the diagram
C_{n}
has no non-trivial automorphisms, our claim follows.

On the other hand, the (simply connected) inner forms of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
can be described in the following straightforward manner: By
[
24
, Prop. 2.19]
every simply connected inner form of a group of type
C_{n}
is of the form
\mathrm{SU}_{m}(D,f)
where
D
is a central division algebra of index
2n/m\in{\bf Z}
over
K
, equipped with an involutive antiautomorphism
\tau:D\rightarrow D
, and
f
is a nondegenerate sesquilinear form, which is either Hermitian or skew-Hermitian (depending on the
type
of
\tau
— see
[
24
, Sect. 2]
).

A subgroup
B\subseteq\mathbb{G}
is called a
Borel subgroup
if it is both connected and solvable and not properly contained in any other connected, solvable subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
.
\mathbb{G}
itself is called
quasisplit
over
K
if it contains a Borel subgroup which is defined over
K
. An important fact is that over any field
K
, an algebraic group has a unique quasisplit inner form (cf.
[
7
, Prop. 7.2.12]
).

Another distinguished type of subgroup
T\subseteq\mathbb{G}
is called a
torus
if it is a connected and closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
which is diagonalizable over an algebraic closure of
K
. We say a torus is
K
-split
if is diagonalizable over
K
, and the group
\mathbb{G}
is called
K
-split
if it contains a maximal
K
-split torus which is defined over
K
.

It is well-known that if a group
\mathbb{G}
is
K
-split, it is also quasisplit over
K
. In particular, for an algebraic group without outer forms, the notions of a quasisplit form and a split form coincide.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an algebraic group defined over
K
. Then there is a canonical way to turn
\mathbb{G}
into an algebraic group defined over
{\bf Q}
, namely
restriction of scalars
or
Weil restriction
.

Suppose that the
K
-group
\mathbb{G}
equals the vanishing locus of the ideal
\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
, where each
f_{j}
is a polynomial with coefficients in
K
. Let
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
be the respective number of real and complex embeddings of
K
. For any such embedding
\sigma:K\hookrightarrow{\bf C}
, we then let
\mathbb{G}^{\sigma}
be the group whose corresponding ideal is
I(\mathbb{G}^{\sigma})=\langle\sigma\circ f_{1},\ldots,\sigma\circ f_{m}\rangle
. Then the
restriction of scalars

\mathrm{Res}_{K/{\bf Q}}(\mathbb{G})
of
\mathbb{G}
is the algebraic
{\bf Q}
-group defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}_{K/{\bf Q}}(\mathbb{G})=\prod_{i=1}^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\mathbb{G}^{\sigma_{i}}.

(Strictly speaking,
\mathrm{Res}_{K/{\bf Q}}(\mathbb{G})
is an algebraic group which is isomorphic over
\overline{K}
to the product on the right-hand side above. More specifically, the group of
{\bf Q}
-rational points of
\mathrm{Res}_{K/{\bf Q}}(\mathbb{G})
is isomorphic to the group of
K
-rational points of
\mathbb{G}
.) In particular, we take note of the fact that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}_{K/{\bf Q}}(\mathbb{G})({\bf R})\simeq\prod_{\upsilon\mid\infty}\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon}),

where
K_{\upsilon}
is the completion of
K
at the infinite place
\upsilon
. For more details, we refer to
[
24
, Sect. 2.1.2]
.

Rather than looking at rational points over a smaller field, we can also consider the group of points of
\mathbb{G}
over the adele ring
\mathbb{A}_{K}
. Since the ring operations work coordinatewise, one has the explicit description

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{A}_{K})=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{\upsilon}\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})=\mathbb{G}_{\infty}\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{\upsilon<\infty}\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon}).

Furthermore, as a consequence of the fact that
K
embeds diagonally into
\mathbb{A}_{K}
, the same is true for the group
\mathbb{G}(K)
of
K
-rational points, which in fact embeds as a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{A}_{K})
. We refer to
[
18
, Sect. I.(0.33)]
for more details.

As mentioned above, we know that our lattice
\Gamma
is a maximal arithmetic subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
. In consequence,
K
must be a totally real number field:

First of all, we see from
[
21
, §18.5]
that at least
K\subseteq{\bf R}
. Next,
[
21
, Corollary 5.5.16]
shows that
\mathbb{G}({\bf R})
is simple, and that the diagonal embedding
\Delta(\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{K}))
in
\mathrm{Res}_{K/{\bf Q}}(\mathbb{G})({\bf R})
is therefore an
irreducible
lattice due to
[
21
, Prop. 5.5.8 and Remark 5.5.9]
.

If we consider the projection of
\mathrm{Res}_{K/{\bf Q}}(\mathbb{G})({\bf R})
onto the factor corresponding to the identity embedding, we have
\pi(\Delta(\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{K})))=\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{K})=\Gamma
. Accordingly, if the product defining the restriction were to contain two or more non-compact factors, the projection of an irreducible lattice would (by definition) be dense in the first factor. Since
\Gamma
is discrete, it therefore follows that
\mathbb{G}(\overline{\sigma(K)})
is compact for any embedding
\sigma\neq\mathrm{id}
.

This means, in turn, that any such
\sigma
must be a real embedding of
K
since, in the alternative case where
\sigma(K)\not\subseteq{\bf R}
, the group
\mathbb{G}(\overline{\sigma(K)})\simeq\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf C})
is not compact. (By contrast, the explicit description of
\mathbb{G}
as a special unitary group shows that for a real embedding
\sigma
, the corresponding group of points can be compact.)

2.3
Prasad’s volume formula

One of the most subtle parts of Prasad’s volume formula has to do with a certain set of compact open subgroups which are intrinsically linked to the principal arithmetic subgroup under consideration. Before discussing the volume formula in detail, we will describe some generalities about these groups.

2.3.1
Parahoric subgroups

Just as one has the notions of
Borel
and
parabolic
subgroups of a reductive algebraic group, one has the notions of
Iwahori
and
parahoric
subgroups of an algebraic group over non-archimedean local fields.

As a matter of fact, the following picture provides a useful intuition: if
K_{\upsilon}
is the completion of a number field
K
at a finite place
\upsilon
, and
\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon}\subseteq K_{\upsilon}
is the valuation ring of
K_{\upsilon}
containing the unique maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{\upsilon}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon}:\upsilon(x)>0}}\right\}
, one has the usual projection map
\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon}/\mathfrak{m}_{\upsilon}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}}
to the finite field with
q_{\upsilon}
elements. An Iwahori subgroup
I_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is then essentially the preimage
\pi^{-1}(B)
of a Borel subgroup
B
of
\overline{\mathbb{G}}
, the group
\mathbb{G}
“considered” as a group over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}}
. (This will be made precise below.) Furthermore, a parabolic subgroup of
\overline{\mathbb{G}}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})
is, by definition, any group containing (a conjugate of)
B
, and a parahoric subgroup
P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is then essentially the preimage
\pi^{-1}(P)
of a parabolic subgroup.

We now proceed to more precise definitions. A subgroup
B_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is an
Iwahori subgroup
if it is the normalizer of a maximal pro-
p
-subgroup of
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
(that is, the inverse limit of a coherent sequence of finite
p
-groups, which is not properly contained in any other such group). A subgroup
P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is called
parahoric
if it contains an Iwahori subgroup.

We recall the following facts from
[
24
, Sect. 3.4]
: There is an Iwahori subgroup
B_{\upsilon}
in
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
and a maximal
K_{\upsilon}
-split torus
S_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
(say, of dimension
\dim S_{\upsilon}=\ell
) such that with
N_{\upsilon}=(N_{\mathbb{G}}(S_{\upsilon}))(K_{\upsilon})
equal to the normalizer of
S_{\upsilon}
, the pair
(B_{\upsilon},N_{\upsilon})
is a so-called
BN
-pair
for the group of
K_{\upsilon}
-rational points
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
. This means in particular that
H_{\upsilon}:=B_{\upsilon}\cap N_{\upsilon}
is a normal subgroup in
N_{\upsilon}
, and that there is a size
\ell+1
generating subset
\Delta_{\upsilon}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{r_{0},r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell}}}\right\}\subseteq W_{\upsilon}:=N_{\upsilon}/H_{\upsilon}
of the Weyl group
W_{\upsilon}
, corresponding to the vertices in the local Dynkin diagram of
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
, with every element of
\Delta_{\upsilon}
having order
2
. It turns out that if
B_{\upsilon}\subseteq P_{\upsilon}
for some subgroup
P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
, then there is a subset
\Theta_{\upsilon}\subseteq\Delta_{\upsilon}
of generators, and a resulting subgroup
W_{\Theta_{\upsilon}}\subseteq W_{\Delta_{\upsilon}}=W_{\upsilon}
generated by the elements of
\Theta_{\upsilon}
, such that
P_{\upsilon}=B_{\upsilon}W_{\Theta_{\upsilon}}B_{\upsilon}
. The subset
\Theta_{\upsilon}\subseteq\Delta_{\upsilon}
is then called the
type
of
P_{\upsilon}
.

From the perspective of
Bruhat–Tits buildings
, a subgroup
P=P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is parahoric if and only if it is the stabilizer of a simplex in the Bruhat–Tits building
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G},K_{\upsilon})
associated with
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
. Naturally, the correspondence between simplices and their stabilizers is inclusion-reversing. In particular, the maximal parahoric subgroups of
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
are precisely the stabilizers of individual vertices in the building
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G},K_{\upsilon})
.

A particular class of parahoric subgroups will play a central role in our subsequent analysis, namely the special and hyperspecial parahorics. We say that a parahoric subgroup
P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is
(hyper)special
if it stabilizes a point in
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G},K_{\upsilon})
, which is
(hyper)special
. Here, a point
x\in\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{G})
in an apartment of the building is
special
if every hyperplane in
\mathcal{A}
is parallel to a hyperplane that passes through
x
(see
[
1
, Prop. 10.19]
). Moreover, such a point
x
is called
hyperspecial
if it continues to be special in the Bruhat–Tits building
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G},\widehat{K_{\upsilon}})
where
\widehat{K_{\upsilon}}
denotes the maximal unramified extension of
K_{\upsilon}
. (Note that all apartments in a building are isometric. These properties therefore do not depend on the choice of an apartment containing the given point.)

For example, if
\mathbb{G}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
is defined over
{\bf Q}
, and
\upsilon
is a finite place corresponding to the prime number
p
, the subgroup
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z}_{p})\subseteq\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Q}_{p})
is a hyperspecial parahoric subgroup.

Remarks.

1)
We recall from
[
6
, Sect. 3.1]
that a parahoric subgroup
P_{\upsilon}
, which has maximal volume among all parahoric subgroups, is necessarily special; and from
[
6
, Sect. 3.2]
that a hyperspecial parahoric subgroup necessarily has maximal volume. (See also
[
6
, Prop. A.5]
.)

2)
Since we want
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
to be of minimal covolume, we claim that we are, in fact, free to assume that at each finite place
\upsilon
, the parahoric subgroup
P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
has maximal volume. Namely, this follows from the following two points: First of all, the argument in
[
5
, Sect. 4.3]
(which builds on
[
6
, Sect. 3.8]
) shows that the covolume of
\Gamma=N_{\mathbb{G}({\bf R})}(\Lambda)
can only
decrease
when a parahoric subgroup
P_{\upsilon}
in the coherent sequence of
\Lambda
is replaced with a parahoric of larger volume, in case
P_{\upsilon}
is not already maximal. (Although the statements made in
[
5
]
pertain to groups of type
D_{n}
, the proof of the inequality after
[
5
, eq. 4.(15)]
extends almost immediately to groups of type
C_{n}
as well, with only minor adjustments required.) Second, we will show that the covolume
must, in fact, decrease
in this situation. This argument will rely on the analysis that follows (where every
P_{\upsilon}
is assumed to be maximal) and, in particular, on Theorem
2.2
below, which we have not yet discussed. For this reason, we postpone the justification of this claim to Section
4
where the result is stated in the form of Lemma
4.3
.

Assume from now on that each
P_{\upsilon}
is special. This fact has at least the following useful consequences. First of all, the type
\Theta_{\upsilon}
of any parahoric
P_{\upsilon}
in our sequence has no symmetries, as is shown in
[
8
, Sect. 3.1]
. Second, the following simple characterization of hyperspecial parahoric subgroups holds: for a finite place
\upsilon
,

\displaystyle\text{$P_{\upsilon}$ is hyperspecial }\Longleftrightarrow\text{ $\mathbb{G}$ splits over $K_{\upsilon}$.}

(2.8)

Indeed, it follows immediately from the definition that
P_{\upsilon}
is hyperspecial if
\mathbb{G}
splits over
K_{\upsilon}
. Conversely, if
P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is hyperspecial, then
\mathbb{G}
must be quasisplit over
K_{\upsilon}
by
[
13
, Prop. 10.2.1]
. Accordingly,
\mathbb{G}
splits over
K_{\upsilon}
by uniqueness of the quasisplit inner form over any field.

We now proceed to the discussion of parahoric subgroups in the context of Prasad’s volume formula. Additional details are given in
[
25
, Sect. 2.2]
and
[
13
, Sect. 4.1]
.

A parahoric subgroup
P_{\upsilon}
is related to a smooth affine
\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon}
-group scheme
\textbf{G}_{\upsilon}
. In particular, the group
P_{\upsilon}
coincides with the
\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon}
-points of this scheme, i.e.
\textbf{G}_{\upsilon}(\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon})=P_{\upsilon}
. Therefore one may consider the reduction (mod
\mathfrak{m}_{\upsilon}
) of
P_{\upsilon}
in the form of the base change
\overline{\textbf{G}}_{\upsilon}:=\textbf{G}_{\upsilon}\times_{\mathcal{O}_{\upsilon}}\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}}
. (Intuitively speaking, the resulting group of points simply consists of the points of
\textbf{G}_{\upsilon}
where all of its defining equations have been reduced modulo
q_{\upsilon}
, over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}}
.) The group
\overline{\textbf{G}}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})
then admits a Levi decomposition
\textbf{M}_{\upsilon}\ltimes\mathrm{R}_{u}(\overline{\textbf{G}}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}}))
where
\textbf{M}_{\upsilon}\subseteq\overline{\textbf{G}}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})
is a maximal connected reductive subgroup (the
Levi component
), and
\mathrm{R}_{u}(\overline{\textbf{G}}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}}))
denotes the unipotent radical.

If we now let
\mathbb{G}
be a
K
-form of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
and let
\mathcal{G}
be the unique quasisplit inner
K
-form of
\mathbb{G}
, we obtain in an analogous way the maximal connected reductive subgroup
\mathcal{M}_{\upsilon}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})
. Since
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
is itself split, we have in fact
\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\upsilon}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})
.

With these details in place, we can now describe the local factors appearing in Prasad’s volume formula. Thus, we define

\displaystyle e(P_{\upsilon}):=\frac{q_{v}^{(\dim\textbf{M}_{\upsilon}\,+\,\dim\mathcal{M}_{\upsilon})/2}}{\#\textbf{M}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})},

(2.9)

and

\displaystyle e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}):=q_{\upsilon}^{(\dim\textbf{M}_{\upsilon}-\dim\mathcal{M}_{\upsilon})/2}\frac{\#\mathcal{M}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})}{\#\textbf{M}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})}=e(P_{\upsilon})\frac{\#\mathcal{M}_{\upsilon}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}})}{q_{\upsilon}^{\dim\mathcal{M}_{\upsilon}}}.

(2.10)

We note that
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})
is always a non-negative integer (cf.
[
26
, Sect. 2.5]
).

Of course, it is possible to simplify both of these expressions to some extent by using the explicit description of
\mathcal{M}_{\upsilon}
as the standard split form of the symplectic group over the residue field
\mathbb{F}_{q_{\upsilon}}
. We will do this in the next section. For now, we note (cf.
[
26
, Sect. 2.5]
) that one always has the inequality

\displaystyle e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})<e(P_{\upsilon}).

(2.11)

For later use, we also note the following equivalences (cf.
[
25
, Sect. 2.2]
):

\displaystyle e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})=1

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\dim\overline{M}_{\upsilon}=\dim\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\upsilon}\text{ and }\#\overline{M}_{\upsilon}(\mathfrak{f}_{\upsilon})=\#\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\upsilon}(\mathfrak{f}_{\upsilon})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\text{$P_{\upsilon}$ is hyperspecial.}

2.3.2
The statement of the volume formula

With the necessary preparations in place, we will now describe the main technical tool used in the paper, which is Prasad’s voluma formula. In our specific case of interest where
\mathbb{G}
has type
C_{n}
and
K
is totally real (see the end of Section
2.2
), this formula can be stated as follows. For both this result and future purposes, it will be useful to define (as we did implicitly in the introduction) the number

\displaystyle\Pi(n):=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(2j-1)!}{(2\pi)^{2j}}.

(2.12)

Theorem 2.2

(
[
25
, Thm. 3.7]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an absolutely simple, simply connected algebraic group of rank
n
and type
C_{n}
defined over a totally real number field
K
. Let
\mu_{\infty}
denote the product measure on
\mathbb{G}_{\infty}
, where (for each infinite place
\upsilon
) the measure
\mu_{\upsilon}
on
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is described in
[
25
, Sect. 1.3, Sect. 1.4]
. Let
\Lambda
be the principal arithmetic subgroup determined by a coherent collection
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{P_{\upsilon}:\upsilon<\infty}}\right\}
of parahoric subgroups
P_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
. Then we have the formula

\displaystyle\mu_{\infty}(\mathbb{G}_{\infty}/\Lambda)=D_{K}^{n(2n+1)/2}\Pi(n)^{d_{K}}\!\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{K}(2j)\prod_{\upsilon<\infty}e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}),

where the factors
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})
are given by (
2.10
), and
\zeta_{K}
denotes the Dedekind zeta function of
K
.

Remark.
The measure
\mu_{\infty}
on
\mathbb{G}_{\infty}
is normalized to give any maximal compact subgroup measure
1
. In particular, if
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is compact for any infinite place
\upsilon\neq\upsilon_{0}
, the left-hand side of Theorem
2.2
is
\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Lambda)
.

Proof.

Let
\upsilon
be any finite place of
K
. Given that the exponents
m_{i}
of
\mathbb{G}
are
m_{i}=2i-1
for
i=1,\ldots,n
(see
[
26
, Sect. 2.4]
), we observe that

\displaystyle\dim\mathbb{G}=n+2(1+3+\cdots+2n-1)=2n^{2}+n,

and, to justify the appearance of the Dedekind zeta values, that

\displaystyle e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})=e(P_{\upsilon})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{\upsilon}^{m_{j}+1}}}}\right)=e(P_{\upsilon})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{\upsilon}^{2j}}}}\right),

(2.13)

where
q_{\upsilon}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{\upsilon}=N(\mathfrak{p}_{\upsilon})
denotes the size of the residue field
\mathfrak{f}_{\upsilon}
of
K
at
\upsilon
(or, equivalently, the norm of the prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}_{\upsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
associated with
\upsilon
). Indeed, this follows from
[
26
, Sect. 2.4]
and the fact that
\mathbb{G}
is of type
C_{n}
and thus without outer forms. Upon multiplying the right-hand side above over all finite places
\upsilon
of
K
, one recognizes the reciprocal of the Euler product defining
\zeta_{K}(2)\cdots\zeta_{K}(2n)
.

As noted above, the quasisplit inner
K
-form
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathbb{G}
splits over
K
. Therefore
L=K
, and the factor involving
D_{L}/D_{K}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[L:K}}\right]}
vanishes. Moreover, since the number field
K
is totally real, its number of (inequivalent) infinite places equals
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[K:{\bf Q}}}\right]=d_{K}
, and hence the product over the infinite places of
K
can be rewritten as claimed (cf. also
[
25
, Rem. 3.8]
). The claimed formula now follows from these observations and the fact that the Tamagawa number
\tau_{K}(\mathbb{G})
is equal to
1
(see
[
16
]
).
∎

Since our approach to using Prasad’s Volume Formula involves the initial step of ignoring the contribution of the parahoric factors
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})
to the covolume
\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}}/\Lambda)
, it will be convenient for us to introduce the notation

\displaystyle S(\Lambda):=\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Lambda)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\upsilon<\infty}e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})}}\right)^{-1}=D_{K}^{n(2n+1)/2}\Pi(n)^{d_{K}}\!\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{K}(2j).

(2.14)

In terms of this number, the covolume of
\Gamma
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma}}\right)=\frac{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma:\Lambda}}\right]}S(\Lambda)\prod_{\upsilon<\infty}e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}).

(2.15)

3
Bounding the Covolume: Global considerations

In this section we estimate the right-hand side of Prasad’s volume formula from below and to different degrees of accuracy. As we described in the introduction, the assumption that
\Gamma
has smaller covolume than
\Gamma_{0}
will, in combination with the simplest bounds we prove, result in a number of numerical bounds on the arithmetic invariants related to our number field, e.g.
D_{K}
and
d_{K}
. Since many number fields are thus excluded from our list of candidates, we can use progressively finer (and more involved) bounds to rule out even more number fields and trim the list even further.

3.1
Lower bounds in terms of
d_{K}
,
D_{K}
, and
n

By Theorem
2.2
and the discussion at the end of Section
2.3.1
, we have the lower bound
\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Lambda)\geqslant S(\Lambda)
. We begin this subsection by converting this fact into a lower bound on the covolume of
\Gamma
, making use of the different number theoretic estimates mentioned in Subsection
2.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
K
be a totally real number field of degree
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[K:{\bf Q}}}\right]=d_{K}
and class number
h_{K}
, and let
D_{K}
be the absolute value of the discriminant of
K
over
{\bf Q}
. Then the covolume of
\Gamma
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma)\geqslant\frac{S(\Lambda)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{K}^{+}:U_{K}^{2}}}\right]\cdot h_{K}}\geqslant\frac{S(\Lambda)}{2^{2d_{K}-1}h_{K}},

where the subgroups
U_{K}^{+},\,U_{K}^{2}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
are defined in Subsection
2.1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the set of finite places
\upsilon
of
K
with the property that
P_{\upsilon}
is
not
hyperspecial. (Equivalently, by (
2.8
),
\mathcal{T}
is the set of all
\upsilon
such that
\mathbb{G}
does not split over
K_{\upsilon}
.) From the discussion in Section
2.3.1
we know that the types
\Theta_{\upsilon}
of the parahorics
P_{\upsilon}
are without symmetries. By
[
8
, Lemma 6.3]
we then have the bound

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma:\Lambda}}\right]\leqslant h_{K}2^{\#\mathcal{T}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{K}^{+}:U_{K}^{2}}}\right]\leqslant h_{K}2^{2d_{K}-1+\#\mathcal{T}},

(3.1)

where the last inequality is (
2.3
). To derive our claim from Theorem
2.2
, we now write

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Lambda)=S(\Lambda)\,\prod_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{T}}e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})\prod_{\upsilon\not\in\mathcal{T}}e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}).

From our discussion in Section
2.3.1
we also see that the product over
\upsilon\not\in\mathcal{T}
equals
1
, and that each factor in the other product is an integer
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})
strictly greater than
1
. In combination with the first inequality in (
3.1
), this shows that

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Lambda)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma:\Lambda}}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant S(\Lambda)2^{\#\mathcal{T}}h_{K}^{-1}2^{-\#\mathcal{T}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{K}^{+}:U_{K}^{2}}}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=S(\Lambda)h_{K}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{K}^{+}:U_{K}^{2}}}\right]^{-1}.

This proves the first bound. The second bound is proved analogously with the second inequality in (
3.1
).
∎

Remarks.

1)
We should point out that, although the result
[
8
, Lemma 6.3]
is stated and proved in the context of a different real form of the symplectic group, its proof makes no special use of the structure of
{\bf G}
at the real places of the relevant number field
k
(in the notation of
[
8
]
). Rather, the argument only relies on the structure at the non-archimedean places. As such, all types of parahorics in
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
can occur within the framework of
[
8
, Lemma 6.3]
, and its analysis is therefore exhaustive in our situation as well.

2)
In order to keep things as simple as possible, we will mainly use the weaker of the two estimates in Lemma
3.1
in the sequel, as it has the benefit that its right-hand side depends only on
d_{K}
and
h_{K}
and not on the unit groups. On the other hand, since the index
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{K}^{+}:U_{K}^{2}}}\right]
can be computed rather easily for many particular number fields, we will make use of the full strength of the lemma once our other analyses have identified a small list of candidates for
K
.

3)
For the convenience of readers unfamiliar with Galois cohomology, we wish to mention the paper
[
2
]
where a version of the bound (
3.1
) is derived in the special case
h_{K}=1
using other techniques than those in
[
8
]
and
[
6
]
. In particular, it is demonstrated here how a change in the involved parahoric subgroups can change the index
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma:\Lambda}}\right]
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose that
\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma}}\right)\leqslant\Psi(n)
where
\Psi(n)
is defined in (
1.2
). Then the discriminant of
K
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle D_{K}<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0.915\cdot 2^{2d_{K}}h_{K}\Pi(n)^{1-d_{K}}}}\right)^{1/(n^{2}+n/2)}.

(3.2)

Proof.

This follows from straightforward manipulations of the weaker estimate given in the lemma. We only need the additional observation that

\displaystyle\Psi(n)<\Pi(n)\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\zeta(2j)<1.83\cdot\Pi(n),

(3.3)

where the numerical bound for the infinite product follows from
[
15
, Lemma 1]
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

The covolume
\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma}}\right)
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma}}\right)>F(d_{K},D_{K},n):=\frac{1}{750}D_{K}^{n^{2}+n/2-3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(7.6e^{0.46}\Pi(n)}}\right)^{d_{K}}.

(3.4)

Proof.

If
R_{K}
is the regulator of
K
and
t>0
is any real number, Proposition
2.1
and Zimmert’s bound for the regulator (
2.5
) imply that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h_{K}}\geqslant\frac{R_{K}}{H(d_{K},D_{K},t)}\geqslant\frac{1}{25\cdot H(d_{K},D_{K},t)}e^{0.46\cdot d_{K}},

where

\displaystyle H(d_{K},D_{K},t)=2t(t+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2\pi^{(1+t)/2}}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t+1}{2}}}\right)}}\right)^{d_{K}}D_{K}^{(t+1)/2}\zeta_{K}(t+1).

(3.5)

With Lemma
3.1
we thus obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{2}{25}D_{K}^{n(2n+1)/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{4}e^{0.46}\Pi(n)}}\right)^{d_{K}}\frac{1}{H(d_{K},D_{K},t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{25}D_{K}^{n(2n+1)/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{0.46}\pi^{(t+1)/2}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t+1}{2}}}\right)^{\!\!-1}\Pi(n)}}\right)^{d_{K}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\frac{1}{t(t+1)D_{K}^{(1+t)/2}\zeta_{K}(t+1)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{D_{K}^{(2n^{2}+n-t-1)/2}}{25t(t+1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{0.46}\Pi(n)\alpha(t+1)}}\right)^{d_{K}},

(3.6)

where we used the classical estimate
\zeta_{K}(t+1)\leqslant\zeta(t+1)^{d_{K}}
and wrote

\displaystyle\alpha(t+1)=\pi^{(t+1)/2}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t+1}{2}}}\right)^{\!\!-1}\zeta(t+1)^{-1}=\frac{t(t+1)}{2\xi(t+1)},

where
\xi
denotes the
Riemann xi function
. Since we are free to choose the value of
t
, we take
t=4.99
which gives
\alpha(t+1)\approx 15.2199
. Then since
t(t+1)<30
, we conclude from (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma}}\right)>\frac{1}{750}D_{K}^{n^{2}+n/2-3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(7.6e^{0.46}\Pi(n)}}\right)^{d_{K}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose that
\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma}}\right)\leqslant\Psi(n)
. Then the discriminant of
K
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle D_{K}<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1372.5\cdot\Pi(n)^{1-d_{K}}\Big(7.6e^{0.46}\Big)^{-d_{K}}}}\right)^{1/(n^{2}+n/2-3)}.

(3.7)

Proof.

This follows in an analogous way to the proof of Corollary
3.2
.
∎

3.2
Monotonicity of the lower bounds

In this section we arrive at the following preliminary conclusions: if
n\geqslant 3
, then
K={\bf Q}
; and if
n=2
, then
K
is either
{\bf Q}(\sqrt{5})
or
{\bf Q}
.

We proceed by bounding the right-hand side of (
3.4
) in terms of only
d_{K}
and
n
, effectively eliminating
D_{K}
from the statement of Lemma
3.3
(for now). This will allow us to deduce upper bounds on
d_{K}
(depending on
n
) under the assumption that
\Gamma
has smaller covolume than
\Gamma_{0}
.

I. The cases
\bm{n\geqslant 4}

We proceed by bounding
D_{K}
from below in terms of
d_{K}
with Odlyzko’s estimates. In our case where
K
is totally real, inserting the bound (
2.1
) into the bound (
3.4
) from Lemma
3.3
and writing
f(n)=n^{2}+n/2-3
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma}}\right)>O(n,d_{K},A,E),

(3.8)

where

\displaystyle O(n,d_{K},A,E):=F(d_{K},A^{d_{K}}e^{-E},n)=\frac{1}{750}e^{-E\cdot f(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(7.6e^{0.46}A^{f(n)}\Pi(n)}}\right)^{d_{K}}.

For a certain choice of the pair
(A,E)
from Odlyzko’s table, which will be made more explicit later, we now make the following claims.

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists a choice of parameters
(A,E)
such that the following claims hold:

(a)

For
n\geqslant 2
, the function
n\mapsto\Pi(n)^{-1}O(n,2,A,E)
is increasing.

(b)

For
n\geqslant 3
, the function
d_{K}\mapsto O(n,d_{K},A,E)
is increasing.

(c)

We have
\Pi(4)^{-1}O(4,2,A,E)>1.83
.

Together, these claims imply that for any
n\geqslant 4
, the lattice
\Gamma
must “come from
{\bf Q}
” in the sense discussed earlier. Indeed, we know that the covolume of
\Gamma_{0}
in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
is smaller than
1.83\cdot\Pi(n)
. If
n\geqslant 4
and
K\neq{\bf Q}
, so that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[K:{\bf Q}}}\right]=d_{K}\geqslant 2
, the three claims of the lemma then imply that

\displaystyle\Pi(n)^{-1}O(n,d_{K},A,E)\geqslant\Pi(n)^{-1}O(n,2,A,E)\geqslant\Pi(4)^{-1}O(4,2,A,E)>1.83,

and consequently
O(n,d_{K},A,E)>1.83\cdot\Pi(n)
. In view of (
1.2
), (
3.3
), and (
3.8
), this implies that for
n\geqslant 4
, a number field
K
of degree greater than
1
cannot give rise to a lattice
\Gamma
of
minimal
covolume. Hence, under these assumptions,
d_{K}=1
and
K={\bf Q}
, as claimed.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

To see part
(a)
, suppose
n\geqslant 1
is arbitrary. Then we compute that

\displaystyle\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Pi(n+1)^{-1}O(n+1,2,A,E)}{\Pi(n)^{-1}O(n,2,A,E)}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-E\cdot(2n+3/2)+(4n+3)\log A+\log\frac{(2n+1)!}{(2\pi)^{2n+2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad>-E\cdot(2n+3/2)+(4n+3)\log A-(2n+2)\log 2\pi

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\log 2\pi(2n+1)+(2n+1)\log(2n+1)-(2n+1),

where we used the inequality (
2.7
). Writing
\Omega(A,E)=4\log A-2E-2\log 2\pi-2
and rearranging the terms on the right-hand side of the estimate above, we find that

\displaystyle\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Pi(n+1)^{-1}O(n+1,2,A,E)}{\Pi(n)^{-1}O(n,2,A,E)}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad>n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\log(2n+1)+\Omega(A,E)}}\right)+\tfrac{3}{2}\log(2n+1)+3\log A-\tfrac{3}{2}E-\tfrac{3}{2}\log 2\pi-1

\displaystyle\quad\quad>n(2\log(2n+1)+\Omega(A,E))+\tfrac{3}{2}\log(2n+1)+\tfrac{3}{4}\Omega(A,E).

Since our claim will follow if the logarithm we are estimating is non-negative, we derive a condition on
\Omega(A,E)
which expresses this. The right-hand side above is non-negative if and only if

\displaystyle\Omega(A,E)\cdot(n+3/4)+2n\log(2n+1)+\tfrac{3}{2}\log(2n+1)\geqslant 0,

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle 4\log A-2E\geqslant 2\log 2\pi+2-2\log(2n+1).

(3.9)

Since the right-hand side of (
3.9
) is decreasing in
n
, we conclude that our claim is justified if we can choose
A
and
E
such that

\displaystyle 2\log A-E\geqslant\log 2\pi+1-\log 5\approx 1.2284.

(3.10)

We postpone the matter of choosing
A
and
E
such that (
3.10
) is satisfied until a later point.

To prove part
(b)
, we need to show that there is a choice of
A>1
such that for
n\geqslant 3
,
7.6e^{0.46}A^{f(n)}\Pi(n)\geqslant 1
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle\log A\geqslant\frac{-\log\Pi(n)-\log 7.6-0.46}{f(n)}.

(3.11)

By computing approximations of the first values of
\Pi(n)
and using Stirling’s formula, one can easily see that
\Pi(n)
is increasing for
n\geqslant 14
. Since
f(n)
is certainly also increasing for
n\geqslant 14
, we only need to choose
A
such that (
3.11
) holds for
n=3,\ldots,14
. By using lower bounds for the values of
\Pi(n)
and inspecting the inequalities (
3.11
) for these values of
n
, we find that
A
satisfies the claim
(b)
provided that

\displaystyle A>5.66.

(3.12)

Provided that such an
A
exists which also satisfies (
3.10
), the claim is proved.

We now prove part
(c)
. By using the approximation
\Pi(4)\approx 3.9\cdot 10^{-10}
, we see that this claim is satisfied if

\displaystyle e^{-E\cdot f(4)}A^{2\cdot f(4)}\Pi(4)>1.83\cdot 750\cdot\frac{1}{7.6^{2}}\cdot e^{-0.92}\approx 9.4697.

In order for this to hold, it is sufficient that

\displaystyle-E+2\log A>\frac{\log 9.47-\log\Pi(4)}{f(4)}.

(3.13)

The proof will be concluded once we show that we can choose
A
and
E
that satisfy this inequality while also satisfying (
3.12
) and (
3.10
).

We now search through the possible pairs
(A,E)
to find a choice of
A
and
E
satisfying the three requirements (
3.13
), (
3.12
), and (
3.10
). It turns out that we may take

\displaystyle(A,E)=(6.894,2.2667),

and the lemma is proved.
∎

II. The case
\bm{n=3}

When
n=3
we are no longer able to rule out all the cases
d_{K}\geqslant 2
in a uniform way. Rather, we will have to consider different regimes of
d_{K}
and argue accordingly. Hence, our first order of business is to determine these regimes.

Initially, we ask how large
d_{K}
has to be in order that

\displaystyle\Pi(n)^{-1}O(n,d_{K},A,E)=\Pi(3)^{-1}O(3,d_{K},A,E)>1.83.

Since
\Pi(3)=45/256\pi^{12}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pi(3)^{-1}O(3,d_{K},A,E)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{750}\Pi(3)^{-1}e^{-E\cdot 7.5}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(7.6e^{0.46}A^{7.5}\cdot\Pi(3)}}\right)^{d_{K}}

\displaystyle=\frac{256\pi^{12}}{33750}\cdot e^{-7.5E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{0.46}A^{7.5}\frac{342}{256\pi^{12}}}}\right)^{d_{K}}.

This quantity exceeds
1.83
precisely when

\displaystyle d_{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(7.5\log A+0.46+\log\frac{342}{256\pi^{12}}}}\right)-7.5E>\log\frac{1.83\cdot 33750}{256\pi^{12}},

and for
A>5.65
(ensuring that the coefficient of
d_{K}
is positive), this is certainly satisfied when

\displaystyle d_{K}>\frac{7.5E-8.25}{7.5\log A-12.99}

(3.14)

because of the approximations

\displaystyle 0.46+\log\frac{342}{256\pi^{12}}\approx-12.987,\quad\quad\log\frac{1.83\cdot 33750}{256\pi^{12}}\approx-8.251.

In conclusion, (
3.14
) is a sufficient condition for the inequality
O(3,d_{K},A,E)>1.83\cdot\Pi(3)
to hold. The task now, therefore, is to choose the pair
(A,E)
from Odlyzko’s table in such a way as to
minimize
the right-hand side of (
3.14
). Using a computer algebra system, we find that the minimal value is
3.31
and is attained at
(A,E)=(13.047,3.8667)
. It follows that a number field of degree at least
4
cannot give rise to the lattice
\Gamma
.

To completely settle the case
n=3
we must now exclude the two remaining undesirable possibilities
d_{K}=2
and
d_{K}=3
. To this end, assuming that
\Gamma
has covolume smaller than
\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Sp}_{6}({\bf R})/\Gamma_{0}}}\right)=\zeta(2)\zeta(4)\zeta(6)\Pi(3)<1.83\cdot\Pi(3)
, we see from Corollary
3.4
that

\displaystyle D_{K}<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1372.5\cdot\Pi(3)\Big(7.6e^{0.46}\Pi(3)\Big)^{-d_{K}}}}\right)^{1/7.5}\approx\begin{cases}10.63&\text{ if $d_{K}=2$},\\
60.09&\text{ if $d_{K}=3$}.\end{cases}

By consulting the
L
-functions and modular forms database
[
35
]
, we find that
K
is restricted to being one of the following number fields:

•

If
d_{K}=2
, there are two possible number fields, both with class number
1
, with respective discriminants
D_{K}=5,8
. In light of the additional information that
h_{K}=1
, the estimate (
3.2
) in Corollary
3.2
can be applied to these fields to yield an even sharper bound on

. Thus, with
(n,d_{K})=(3,2)
, we find that in fact
D_{K}<5.27
, so that
D_{K}=5
and
K={\bf Q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sqrt{5}}}\right]
is the only possibility. We postpone the matter of conclusively excluding this number field until a later point when we have determined the possibilities in the case

as well.

•

If
d_{K}=3
, the only possibility is
D_{K}=49
, in which case one also has
h_{K}=1
. Knowing the class number, we can again apply Corollary
3.2
and obtain the strengthened bound
D_{K}<28.087
. This is impossible since any totally real cubic number field must have discriminant at least
49
.

\bm{n=2}

) that, when

,

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon_{0}})/\Gamma)

\displaystyle>\frac{D_{K}^{5-(t+1)/2}}{25t(t+1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3e^{0.46}}{64\pi^{6}}\pi^{(t+1)/2}\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t+1}{2}}}\right)^{\!\!-1}\zeta(t+1)^{-1}}}\right)^{d_{K}}

4.1
Estimates of the parahoric factors

For

It is possible, using Bruhat–Tits theory, to describe the Levi components

\displaystyle e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})=1\quad\text{or}\quad e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})=T(q_{\upsilon}):=\frac{q_{\upsilon}^{4}-1}{2(q_{\upsilon}+1)}.

states that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma:\Lambda}}\right]\leqslant 2^{\#\mathcal{T}}
. For the proof that

is

-split it will therefore be sufficient to show that any local factor

not equal to

does not split over

. Due to this, and since

is special, we can use the explicit formulas for

in

Lemma 4.1

(
[
8
, Lemma 4.1]
)
.

Let

be a finite place of
{\bf Q}
and

a special, non-hyperspecial parahoric subgroup. If

denotes the size of the residue field of

, then

\displaystyle e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})=\begin{cases}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{\upsilon}^{j}+(-1)^{j}}}\right)&\text{ if $n$ is odd},\\[5.0pt]
\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{\upsilon}^{4j-2}-1}}\right)&\text{ if $n=2m$ is even}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We only need to justify the formula in the case of even rank. However, this follows immediately from
[
8
, eq. (4.9)]
once we split the product in the numerator into two products depending on the parity of the indexing variable
j
.
∎

With this lemma we can easily deduce that
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})>2
for any place
\upsilon\in\mathcal{T}
. Indeed, we certainly have
q_{\upsilon}\geqslant 2
for any
\upsilon
, so if
n\geqslant 2
is odd, then
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})\geqslant 1\cdot 5\cdot 7=35
. On the other hand, if

is even, then
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})\geqslant 3
. It follows that, if
\Gamma
has minimal covolume, then
\mathbb{G}
must split at all finite places. Since
\mathbb{G}
also splits at the unique infinite place
\upsilon_{0}
, we conclude (as in the previous section) that

.

To complete the proof of Theorem 1.1, all that remains is to show that
\Lambda=\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}
, where the last equality holds up to conjugation.

For
\upsilon
an arbitrary finite place of
{\bf Q}
, the fact that the parahoric subgroup
P_{\upsilon}
is hyperspecial means that can find an element
g_{\upsilon}
in

\displaystyle\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf Q}_{\upsilon})=\Big\{g\in\mathrm{GL}_{2n}({\bf Q}_{\upsilon}):g^{\intercal}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)g=\chi(g)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)\text{ for some }\chi(g)\in{\bf Q}_{\upsilon}^{\times}\Big\},

such that
g_{\upsilon}P_{\upsilon}g_{\upsilon}^{-1}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z}_{\upsilon})
. Indeed,
\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}
acts transitively on the hyperspecial parahoric subgroups of the symplectic group (cf.
[
32
, Sect. 2.5]
). Because of the topology on the restricted product
\mathbb{A}_{\bf Q}
and the coherence of the collection
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{P_{\upsilon}:\upsilon<\infty}}\right\}
, we can assume that
g_{\upsilon}=I
for all places
\upsilon
except finitely many. Doing so, we thus obtain an element

\displaystyle g=\Bigl(1,(g_{\upsilon})_{\upsilon<\infty}\Bigr)\in\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}(\mathbb{A}_{\bf Q}).

The class number of a split reductive group is at most the class number of any maximal split torus (cf.
[
24
, Corollary to Thm. 8.11]
). Since
{\bf Q}
has class number
1
, it follows that
\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}
has class number
1
over
{\bf Q}
, and we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}(\mathbb{A}_{\bf Q})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf R})\times\prod_{\upsilon<\infty}\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf Z}_{\upsilon})}}\right)\cdot\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf Q}).

In terms of the coordinate corresponding to a finite place
\upsilon
, this means that we can write
g_{\upsilon}=g_{\upsilon}^{\prime}h
where
g_{\upsilon}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf Z}_{\upsilon})
and
h\in\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf Q})
. Since
h=(g_{\upsilon}^{\prime})^{-1}g_{\upsilon}
and conjugation by
g_{\upsilon}^{\prime}
leaves
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z}_{\upsilon})
invariant, so that
hP_{\upsilon}h^{-1}
simply equals
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z}_{\upsilon})
, we now finally obtain

\displaystyle h\Lambda h^{-1}=h\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Q})h^{-1}\cap\prod_{\upsilon<\infty}hP_{\upsilon}h^{-1}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Q})\cap\prod_{\upsilon<\infty}\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf Z}_{\upsilon})=\Gamma_{0}.

We have established that
\Lambda=h^{-1}\Gamma_{0}h
for
h\in\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf Q})
. Since
\Gamma
is the normalizer of
\Lambda
in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
, and
\Gamma_{0}=N_{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})}(\Gamma_{0})
is its own normalizer (cf.
[
3
]
), we now conclude that

\displaystyle\Gamma=\Big\{g\in\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R}):hgh^{-1}\in\Gamma_{0}\Big\}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})\cap h^{-1}\Gamma_{0}h.

In other words,
h\Gamma h^{-1}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})\cap\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma_{0}
where
h\in\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf Q})
. Taking into account the isomorphism (now,
automorphism
)
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{G}({\bf R})=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\bf R})
induced by the distinguished real place
\upsilon_{0}
of
K={\bf Q}
, we conclude that
\Gamma
is conjugate to
\Gamma_{0}
by an element of
\mathrm{GSp}_{2n}({\bf R})
(cf.
[
11
]
). This concludes the proof of Theorem 1.1.

4.3
A final remark on non-special parahorics

We have proved Theorem 1.1 under the assumption that every parahoric had maximal volume. We will now justify this assumption, as promised in Section
2.3.1
.

For the remainder of the paper, we will deviate from our previous notation and use the symbols
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
as free variables that denote, respectively,
any maximal lattice
in
\mathrm{Sp}(2n,{\bf R})
and the principal arithmetic subgroup it normalizes (by the maximality criterion
[
28
, Satz 3.5]
of Rohlfs). Likewise,
\mathbb{G}
will denote the algebraic
K
-group that defines
\Lambda
.
P_{\upsilon}(\Lambda)=P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma)
will denote the parahoric subgroup at
\upsilon
associated to the pair
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
. Finally, we will also write
\Lambda^{\mathrm{max}}
to denote a principal arithmetic subgroup for which every
P_{\upsilon}(\Lambda)
is of maximal volume; and
\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
will then denote the normalizer of
\Lambda^{\mathrm{max}}
. In the same vein, for a pair
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
, we will write
\Lambda^{\mathrm{max}}
and
\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
to denote the corresponding “maximized” principal arithmetic subgroup (as explained in
[
5
, Sect. 4.3]
and
[
6
, Sect. 3.8]
) and its normalizer in
\mathbb{G}({\bf R})
, respectively.

Up until this point, we have proved the following result in particular.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
V_{0}
denote the minimal covolume of all lattices in
\mathrm{Sp}(2n,{\bf R})
. If a lattice of the form
\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
has covolume
V_{0}
, then
\mathbb{G}
splits at every finite place of
K
, and every parahoric
P_{\upsilon}
associated to
\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
satisfies
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon})=1
.

We now prove that any lattice of minimal covolume must, in fact, be of the form
\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{Sp}(2n,{\bf R})
is a maximal lattice, associated to an algebraic
K
-group
\mathbb{G}
, with covolume
V_{0}
. Then
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
; that is, every parahoric
P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma)
has maximal volume among all parahoric subgroups of
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
.

Proof.

The discussion in Section
2.3.1
shows that also
\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
has covolume
V_{0}
. Since both
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
come from
\mathbb{G}
, Theorem
4.2
implies that
\mathbb{G}
splits at all finite places — in particular, a parahoric subgroup of
\mathbb{G}(K_{\upsilon})
is special if and only if it is hyperspecial, and consequently
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}
if and only if every
P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma)
is special. Theorem
4.2
also gives
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}))=1
, so we obtain from Theorem
2.2
and the inequality
[
5
, 4.3.(15)]
that

\displaystyle 1=\frac{\mu(\mathbb{G}({\bf R})/\Gamma)}{\mu(\mathbb{G}({\bf R})/\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}})}=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma^{\mathrm{max}}:\Lambda^{\mathrm{max}}}}\right]}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma:\Lambda}}\right]}\prod_{\upsilon<\infty}e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma))\geqslant\prod_{\upsilon<\infty}\frac{e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma))}{\#\Xi_{\Theta_{\upsilon}}},

(4.4)

where
\Xi_{\Theta_{\upsilon}}\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}(\Delta_{\upsilon})
is the subgroup of all diagram automorphisms that preserve the type
\Theta_{\upsilon}=\Theta_{\upsilon}(P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma))
of
P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma)
, and
\Delta_{\upsilon}
denotes the local Dynkin diagram of
\mathbb{G}
over
K_{\upsilon}
.

Concretely, we have
\#\Xi_{\Theta_{\upsilon}}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\}
for every
\upsilon
, and in fact
\#\Xi_{\Theta_{\upsilon}}=1
when
P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma)
is special (see
[
8
, Sect. 3.1]
or
[
6
, Sect. 3.2]
). Therefore, any factor corresponding to a special parahoric on the right-hand side of (
4.4
) must be equal to
1
. The crucial fact, as we will see below, is that the converse holds as well: If
P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma)
is not special, then the corresponding factor exceeds
1
. As this is impossible, the proof will be concluded once we demonstrate this claim. Equivalently, we must show that
e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma))>2
whenever
P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma)
is not special.

Let
\upsilon
be any
fixed
place such that
P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma)
is not special. If the residue field of
K_{\upsilon}
has degree
q_{\upsilon}=2
and
\mathbb{G}
has rank
n=2
, then our claim follows immediately from the inequality

\displaystyle e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma))\geqslant\frac{q_{\upsilon}^{4}-1}{2(q_{\upsilon}+1)}=\frac{15}{6}>2,

which follows by inspection of
[
9
, Sect. 3, Table 2]
. We therefore have either
n\geqslant 3
or
q_{\upsilon}\geqslant 3
. In this situation, we can use the
“volume rigidity estimate” proved for non-special parahorics in
[
25
, Prop. 2.10.(iv)]
together with (
2.13
) (which is independent of the assumption we are in the process of justifying) to conclude that

\displaystyle e^{\prime}(P_{\upsilon}(\Gamma))\geqslant\frac{q_{\upsilon}^{n+1}}{q_{\upsilon}+1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{\upsilon}^{2j}}}}\right)=:h(q_{\upsilon},n).

It is straightforward that
h(q_{\upsilon},n)
is increasing in both
q_{\upsilon}\geqslant 2
and
n\geqslant 1
. In consequence, as
(q_{\upsilon},n)\neq(2,2)
, we either have
h(q_{\upsilon},n)\geqslant h(2,3)\approx 3.69
or
h(q_{\upsilon},n)\geqslant h(3,2)\approx 17.75
. In any case, we arrived at the desired conclusion.
