Zeros of a growing number of derivatives of random polynomials with independent roots
Marcus Michelen, Xuan-Truong Vu

Abstract
. Let X 1 , X 2 , … X_{1},X_{2},\ldots be independent and identically distributed random variables in ℂ {\mathbb{C}} chosen from a probability measure μ \mu and define the random polynomial P n ​ ( z ) = ( z − X 1 ) ​ … ​ ( z − X n ) . \displaystyle P_{n}(z)=(z-X_{1})\ldots(z-X_{n})\,. We show that for any sequence k = k ⁡ ( n ) k=k(n) satisfying k ≤ log ⁡ n / ( 5 ​ log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) k\leq\log n/(5\log\log n) , the zeros of the k k th derivative of P n P_{n} are asymptotically distributed according to the same measure μ \mu . This extends work of Kabluchko, which proved the k = 1 k=1 case, as well as Byun, Lee and Reddy who proved the fixed k k case.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Suppose that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d. random variables with values in
{\mathbb{C}}
sampled from
\mu
, and for each
n
define the random polynomial

P_{n}(z)=(z-X_{1})\ldots(z-X_{n})\,.

By the law of large numbers if we consider the
empirical measure
of
P_{n}
, where we a put a point mass of
1/n
at each root of
P_{n}
, then we see that the empirical measure converges to
\mu
as
n
approaches infinity. Pemantle and Rivin
[
14
]
conjectured that the same holds for the derivative
P_{n}
. To make this precise, we define
\mu_{n}^{(1)}
to be the probability measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
that puts a point mass at each critical point of
P_{n}
:

\mu_{n}^{(1)}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:P_{n}^{{}^{\prime}}(z)=0}\delta_{z}.

Pemantle and Rivin conjectured that
\mu_{n}^{(1)}\to\mu
in distribution as
n\to\infty
and proved their conjecture under the assumption that
\mu
has finite
1
-energy. Subramanian
[
15
]
proved the Pemantle-Rivin conjecture in the special case when
\mu
is supported on the unit circle. In an influential work, Kabluchko
[
6
]
confirmed Pemantle and Rivin’s conjecture for
all
probability measures
\mu
. Since then, attention has been focused on higher derivatives. To this end, for each
k
define the (random) probability measure
\mu_{n}^{(k)}
via

\mu_{n}^{(k)}:=\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:P_{n}^{(k)}(z)=0}\delta_{z}\,.

Byun, Lee and Reddy
[
2
]
extended Kabluchko’s work and showed that for each fixed
k
,
1
1

1

The work
[
2
]
does not state an explicit rate at which
k
can be taken to grow. An inspection of the proof shows that it depends on the Levy concentration of the non-atomic part of the random variable
(z-X)^{-1}
as
z
varies in
{\mathbb{C}}
(see
[
2
, eq. (4.6)]
).
we have
\mu_{n}^{(k)}\to\mu
.
Looking towards very high derivatives, O’Rourke and Steinerberger
[
13
]
conjecture that for each fixed
t\in[0,1]
, one has that the random measure
\mu_{n}^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}
converges to a deterministic measure
\mu_{t}
. In the case when the underlying measure
\mu
is radial, O’Rourke and Steinerberger conjecture that the logarithmic potentials of the limiting measure
(\mu_{t})_{t}
satisfy a certain partial differential equation. This partial differential equation has been studied by analysts
[
1
,
9
]
, and O’Rourke and Steinerberger’s conjecture was proven in the special case when
\mu
has real support
[
4
]
(see also
[
3
,
7
]
). In the
t=0
case, the prediction of O’Rourke and Steinerberger suggests that the limiting measure should be the same as the underlying measure
\mu
.

We confirm this in the case that
k
grows slightly slower than logarithmically:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
and
k=k(n)
be a sequence satisfying
k\leq\frac{\log n}{5\log\log n}
. Then the sequence
\mu_{n}^{(k)}\to\mu
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Further, we show that the case of
k=o(n)
would follow from an anti-concentration conjecture for elementary symmetric polynomials evaluated at i.i.d. random variables (see Conjecture
4.1
and Remark
4.2
).

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
takes inspiration from the potential-theoretic approach of Kabluchko’s proof of the
k=1
case (as does the work of Byun, Lee and Reddy
[
2
]
); the key new step is an anti-concentration ingredient. We take a moment to sketch our proof here.

The starting point is the following classical fact from potential theory: if
f
is an analytic function not identically equal to zero then

\frac{1}{2\pi}\Delta\log\left|f\right|=\sum_{\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}:f(\zeta)=0}\delta_{\zeta}

(1)

where the Laplacian is interpreted in the distributional sense.

In particular, if we define

L_{n}^{(k)}(z):=\frac{P_{n}^{(k)}(z)}{k!P_{n}(z)}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n}Y_{i_{1}}Y_{i_{2}}\dots Y_{i_{k}},

(2)

where
Y_{i}:=\frac{1}{z-X_{i}}
for
i\in[n]
, then we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi n}\Delta\log\left|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:P_{n}^{(k)}(z)=0}\delta_{z}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:P_{n}(z)=0}\delta_{z}=\frac{n-k}{n}\mu_{n}^{(k)}-\mu_{n}^{(0)}.

(3)

By the law of large numbers, the measure
\mu_{n}^{(0)}
tends to
\mu
as
n\to\infty
, and so in order to show
\mu_{n}^{(k)}
converges to
\mu
it will be enough to show that
\frac{1}{n}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
goes to
0
in a sufficiently strong sense to guarantee that
\frac{1}{n}\Delta\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
tends to
0
.

Obtaining an upper bound on the magnitude of
\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
involves controlling two different events: when
|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
is large and when
|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
very small. The former event will not be too difficult to deal with, but the latter is trickier. This comes down to an anti-concentration problem for elementary symmetric polynomials evaluated at i.i.d. random variables.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,
be i.i.d. copies of a complex-valued non-degenerate random variable. Then for
1\leq k\leq\frac{\log n}{5\log\log n}
and each
{\varepsilon}>0
we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n}Y_{i_{1}}Y_{i_{2}}\dots Y_{i_{k}}\right|\leq e^{-{\varepsilon}n}\right)=0.

Here we think of
Y_{i}=(z-X_{i})^{-1}
as above. We will deduce Lemma
1.2
from a theorem of Meka, Nguyen and Vu
[
12
]
concerning anti-concentration of multi-affine polynomials of Bernoulli random variables.

After showing pointwise bounds on
\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
, we upgrade these bounds by showing that they hold in some uniform sense. To take care of this, we again follow Kabluchko and lean on a lemma from Tao and Vu
[
17
]
stating that tightness of an
L^{2}
-norm will be sufficient to deduce convergence in probability of the measures. For this, we use the Poisson-Jensen formula to relate values of
\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
in a disk to its values on the boundary of a larger disk, and show uniform bounds at the origin and on the boundary of this disk.

1.1.
Notation

Throughout, we use
B_{r}(z)
to be the disk of radius
r
centered at
z
and abbreviate
B_{r}(0)
as
B_{r}
. We write
m
for the standard (
2
-real-dimensional) Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
. We write
\log z=\log_{+}z-\log_{-}z
where

\log_{-}z=\begin{cases}|\log z|,&0\leq z\leq 1,\\
0,&z\geq 1,\end{cases}\quad\text{and }\quad\log_{+}z=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq z\leq 1,\\
\log z,&z\geq 1,\end{cases}

with the convention
\log_{-}0=+\infty
.

2.
Proof of the main result

We will show two main Lemmas. First we show pointwise convergence of
\frac{1}{n}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
almost everywhere:

Lemma 2.1
.

There is a set
F\subset{\mathbb{C}}
with
m(F)=0
so that for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash F
and all sequences
k\leq\frac{\log n}{5\log\log n}
we have

\frac{1}{n}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|\overset{P}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0.

(4)

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we will need to upgrade Lemma
2.1

from pointwise convergence to a more uniform mode of convergence. By a lemma of Tao and Vu
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
, it will be sufficient to show tightness of a second moment.

Lemma 2.2
.

For each
r>0
and
k\leq\frac{\log n}{5\log\log n}
, the sequence of random variables

\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{B_{r}}\left(\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|\right)^{2}dm(z)\right)_{n\geq 1}

is tight.

We now deduce Theorem
1.1
from Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

It is sufficient to show that for every smooth compactly supported function
\varphi:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:P_{n}^{(k)}(z)=0}\varphi(z)\to\int\limits_{{\mathbb{C}}}\varphi(z)\,d\mu(z)

in probability as
n\to\infty
(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 14.16]
). By, e.g.,
[
5
, Section 2.4.1]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}(\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|)\Delta\varphi(z)\,dm(z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:P_{n}^{(k)}(z)=0}\varphi(z)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:P_{n}(z)=0}\varphi(z).

(5)

Combining Lemmas
2.2
and
2.1
with
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
shows

\frac{1}{n}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}(\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|)\Delta\varphi(z)dm(z)\overset{P}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0\,.

(6)

By the law of large numbers, the right-most term of (
5
) tends to
\int\limits_{{\mathbb{C}}}\varphi(z)d\mu(z)
almost-surely, completing the proof.
∎

2.1.
Proof of Lemma
2.1

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
F=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|y-z|^{-1}d\mu(y)=\infty\}
. Then
m(F)=0
.

Proof.

Since
\mu
is a probability measure we have that
\int\limits_{y:|y-z|\geq 1}|z-y|^{-1}d\mu(y)\leq 1
for all
z
. Thus
F
is equal to the set of
z
for which
\int_{B_{1}(z)}|y-z|^{-1}\,d\mu(y)=\infty
. Apply Fubini’s theorem to compute

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{\mathbb{C}}}\int\limits_{B_{1}(z)}|z-y|^{-1}d\mu(y)dm(z)=\int\limits_{{\mathbb{C}}}\int\limits_{B_{1}(y)}|z-y|^{-1}dm(z)d\mu(y)=2\pi.

Thus
m(F)=0
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

If the random variable
X_{1}
is almost surely a constant, then the Lemma follows easily, and so we assume that
X_{1}
is non-degenerate.

First, we show that for every fixed
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus F
and every
{\varepsilon}>0
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|\geq{\varepsilon}\right]=0.

(7)

Indeed, defining
Y_{i}=(z-X_{i})^{-1}
for all
i\in[n]
, Markov’s inequality bounds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|\geq e^{{\varepsilon}n}\right]\leq e^{-{\varepsilon}n}{\mathbf{E}}[|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|]\leq e^{-{\varepsilon}n}\binom{n}{k}\left({\mathbf{E}}|Y_{1}|\right)^{k}\,.

(8)

Since
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus F
, the expectation
{\mathbf{E}}|Y_{1}|
is finite. Thus, the right-hand-side tends to zero as long as
k=o(n)
and so the limit (
7
) follows.

An application of Lemma
1.2
will provide a lower bound, i.e., we prove that for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
which is not an atom of
\mu
and every
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|\leq-{\varepsilon}\right]=0.

(9)

Write

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|\leq-{\varepsilon}\right]=\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n}Y_{i_{1}}Y_{i_{2}}\dots Y_{i_{k}}\right|\leq e^{-{\varepsilon}n}\right)

and note that Lemma
1.2
shows the right-hand-side is
o(1)
, completing the proof of the Lemma.
∎

2.2.
Proof of Lemma
2.2

To prove tightness, we will use the Poisson-Jensen formula to write
\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
in terms of a Poisson integral of
\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
along the circle of radius
R
for some
R>r
, plus a small correction in terms of the zeros and poles of
L_{n}^{(k)}(z)
. From here, showing tightness of the
L^{2}
norm will come in two steps:
\frac{1}{n}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
is tight at
0
and on the circle of radius
R
, and that the correction depending on the zeros and poles is not too large.

Note that by the proof of Lemma
2.3
we have that for any
R>0
we have that

\int_{B_{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|z-y|^{-1}d\mu(y)\,dm(z)<\infty\,.

By switching the outer integral into polar coordinates, we see that for Lebesgue-almost-all
R>0
we have that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{1}{|Re^{i\theta}-y|}\,d\mu(y)\,d\theta<\infty\,.

(10)

Throughout, we assume that
R
satisfies this assumption.

We now write the Poisson-Jensen formula for
\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|
: Let
R>r
be chosen as above, let
x_{1,n},\ldots,x_{j_{n},n}
denote the zeros of
P_{n}(z)
in the disk
B_{R}
, and let
y_{1,n},\ldots,y_{\ell_{n},n}
be the zeros of
P_{n}^{(k)}(z)
in the disk
B_{R}
. Also note that
j_{n}\leq n
and
\ell_{n}\leq n
. We take the following standard facts about the Poisson-Jensen formula from
[
11
, Chapter 8]
:

Lemma 2.4

(Poisson-Jensen formula)
.

\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|=I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{n}}\log\left|\frac{R(z-y_{\ell,n})}{R^{2}-\bar{y}_{\ell,n}z}\right|-\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}}\log\left|\frac{R(z-x_{j,n})}{R^{2}-\bar{x}_{j,n}z}\right|,

(11)

where

I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|P_{R}(|z|,\theta-\arg z)d\theta

(12)

where
P_{R}
denotes the Poisson kernel

P_{R}(r,\phi)=\frac{R^{2}-r^{2}}{R^{2}+r^{2}-2Rr\cos\phi},\;\;\;r\in[0,R],\,\phi\in[0,2\pi].

(13)

The only property of the Poisson kernel we need is that it provides only bounded distortion when
r
is uniformly bounded away from
R
.

Fact 2.5
.

For all
0<r<R
there exists
M\geq 1
so that for all
z\in B_{r}
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
we have

\frac{1}{M}\leq P_{R}(|z|,\theta)\leq M\,.

(14)

We first provide an upper bound
I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)
in probability that is uniform in
B_{r}
for all
k
and
n
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
R
satisfying (
10
) and
0<r<R
there is a constant
C>0
so that for all
t>0
and
1\leq k\leq n
we have

\mathbf{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sup_{z\in B_{r}}I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)\geq t\right)\leq\frac{C}{t}\,.

(15)

Proof.

Apply Fact
2.5
and bound

\displaystyle I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log_{+}\left|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})\right|d\theta.

(16)

Bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log_{+}\left|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\log\left(1+|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\log\left(1+\binom{n}{k}^{-1}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|\right)+1

(17)

where we used that
n^{-1}\log\binom{n}{k}\leq 1
.

Claim 2.7
.

We have

\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\mathbf{E}}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(1+\binom{n}{k}^{-1}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|\right)\,d\theta\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|Re^{i\theta}-y|^{-1}\,d\mu(y)\,d\theta\,.

Proof of Claim
2.7
.

Noting that the function
x\mapsto\log(1+x)
is concave for
x\geq 0
, Jensen’s inequality bounds

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\log\left(1+\binom{n}{k}^{-1}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(1+{\mathbf{E}}\binom{n}{k}^{-1}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|\right)\,.

Since
R
satisfies (
10
), for almost all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
we may bound

{\mathbf{E}}\binom{n}{k}^{-1}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|\leq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\left|Re^{i\theta}-y\right|^{-1}\,d\mu(y)\right)^{k}\,.

Combining the previous two displayed equations and integrating over
\theta
shows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{n}{\mathbf{E}}\log\left(1+\binom{n}{k}^{-1}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|\right)\,d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(1+\left(\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\left|Re^{i\theta}-y\right|^{-1}\,d\mu(y)\right)^{k}\right)\,d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{k}{n}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|Re^{i\theta}-y|^{-1}\,d\mu(y)\,d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|Re^{i\theta}-y|^{-1}\,d\mu(y)\,d\theta

where we used the elementary inequality
\log(1+x^{k})\leq kx
for
x\geq 0
.
∎

Combining lines (
16
) and (
17
) with the Claim and Markov’s inequality completes the proof.
∎

The Poisson-Jensen form will allow us to compare
I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)
to
I_{n}^{(k)}(0;R)
. It will be convenient to assume that
0\notin F
. If
0\in F
, we may find some
a\notin F
(recall that
F
has measure
0
) and replace each
X_{i}
with
X_{i}-a
; thus, we may assume without loss of generality that
0\notin F
.

Lemma 2.8
.

There is a constant
C_{1}
depending only on
R>0
such that for
k\leq\frac{\log n}{5\log\log n}
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}I_{n}^{(k)}(0;R)\leq-C_{1}\right]=0.

(18)

Proof.

By the Poisson–Jensen formula (
12
) at
z=0
, we have

I_{n}^{(k)}(0;R)=\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(0)|-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{n}}\log\left|\frac{y_{\ell,n}}{R}\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}}\log\left|\frac{x_{j,n}}{R}\right|.

(19)

For the first term on the right-hand side of (
19
), by (
9
),
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(0)|\leq-1\right)\right]=0.

(20)

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
19
), we get a trivial bound

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{n}}\log\left|\frac{y_{\ell,n}}{R}\right|\leq 0.

(21)

For the last term on the right-hand side of (
19
), using the strong law of large numbers, we see

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}}\log\left|\frac{x_{j,n}}{R}\right|=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log_{-}\left|\frac{X_{j}}{R}\right|\overset{a.s.}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}-{\mathbf{E}}\left[\log_{-}\left|\frac{X_{1}}{R}\right|\right].

(22)

Observe that
z\mapsto\log_{-}|z/R|-\log_{-}|z|
is a bounded function with compact support and
{\mathbf{E}}\log_{-}|X_{1}|<\infty
by the assumption
0\notin F
, so we have
{\mathbf{E}}\left[\log_{-}\left|\frac{X_{1}}{R}\right|\right]<\infty
. Combining (
19
), (
20
), (
21
) and (
22
) completes the proof.
∎

To translate Lemma
2.8
to a uniform lower bound for
z\in B_{r}
, we will use the fact that the Poisson kernel has bounded distortion, and so it is sufficient to consider the value at
0
and the boundary.

Lemma 2.9
.

For all
0<r<R
there is a constant
C
such that for all
n
,
t>0
and
k\leq\frac{\log n}{5\log\log n}
we have

\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\inf_{z\in B_{r}}I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)\leq-t\right]\leq\frac{C}{t}.

(23)

Proof.

By Fact
2.5
we may lower bound

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{n}I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|P_{R}(|z|,\theta-\arg z)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log_{+}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|P_{R}(|z|,\theta-\arg z)d\theta-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log_{-}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|P_{R}(|z|,\theta-\arg z)d\theta

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{Mn}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log_{+}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|d\theta-\frac{M}{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log_{-}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi M}{n}I_{n}^{(k)}(0;R)-\frac{1}{n}\left(M-\frac{1}{M}\right)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log_{+}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|d\theta.

(24)

By Lemma
2.8
, we have that
\frac{2\pi M}{n}I_{n}^{(k)}(0;R)\geq-C
asymptotically almost surely. By the proof of Lemma
2.6
we have

\mathbf{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log_{+}|L_{n}^{(k)}(Re^{i\theta})|d\theta\geq t\right)\leq\frac{C}{t}\,.

Combining these two bounds with (
24
) completes the Lemma.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (
11
) and dividing both sides by
n^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\log^{2}|L_{n}^{(k)}(z)|\leq\frac{3}{n^{2}}I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R)^{2}+\frac{3\ell_{n}}{n^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{n}}\log^{2}\left|\frac{R(z-y_{\ell,n})}{R^{2}-\bar{y}_{\ell,n}z}\right|+\frac{3j_{n}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}}\log^{2}\left|\frac{R(z-x_{j,n})}{R^{2}-\bar{x}_{j,n}z}\right|.

(25)

Note that for any
y\in B_{R}
and
z\in B_{r}
we have
|R^{2}-\bar{y}z|
is uniformly bounded below. Using integrability of
(\log|z|)^{2}
near
0
, this implies that

\sup_{y\in B_{R}}\int\limits_{B_{r}}\log^{2}\left|\frac{R(z-y)}{R^{2}-\bar{y}z}\right|dm(z)+\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\int\limits_{B_{r}}\log^{2}\left|\frac{R(z-x)}{R^{2}-\bar{x}z}\right|dm(z)\leq C

for some constant
C
. Bounding
j_{n}\leq n
and
\ell_{n}\leq n
shows a deterministic bound of

\frac{3\ell_{n}}{n^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{n}}\int\limits_{B_{r}}\log^{2}\left|\frac{R(z-y_{\ell,n})}{R^{2}-\bar{y}_{\ell,n}z}\right|dm(z)+\frac{3j_{n}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}}\int\limits_{B_{r}}\log^{2}\left|\frac{R(z-x_{j,n})}{R^{2}-\bar{x}_{j,n}z}\right|dm(z)\leq 3C.

Lemma
2.6
and
2.9
show that
\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int\limits_{B_{r}}(I_{n}^{(k)}(z;R))^{2}dm(z)
is tight. Since a tight sequence plus a deterministically bounded sequence is tight, the proof is complete.
∎

3.
Anticoncentration of elementary symmetric polynomials

We will deduce Lemma
1.2
from a Theorem of Meka, Nguyen and Vu concerning anti-concentration of multi-affine polynomials of Bernoulli random variables. To properly state their theorem, we will need a bit of setup.

We will consider multi-affine polynomials of the form

Q(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\sum_{S\subset[n]}a_{S}\prod_{j\in S}z_{j}

(26)

where the coefficients
a_{S}
are real and the
degree
of
Q
is the largest
|S|
so that
a_{|S|}\neq 0
. If
Q
is of degree
d
, then the
rank
of
Q
is the largest integer
r
such that there exist disjoint sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}\subset[n]
of size
d
with
|a_{S_{j}}|\geq 1
for all
j\in[r]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Theorem 1.7 of
[
12
]
)
.

There is an absolute constant
B
such that the following holds.
Let
Q
be a polynomial of the form (
26
) whose rank
r\geq 2
. Let
p
be such that
\tilde{r}:=2^{d}\alpha^{d}r=2^{d}\alpha^{d}n/k\geq 3
where
\alpha:=\min\{p,1-p\}
. Then for any interval
I
of length 1, if we let
\{{\varepsilon}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. Bernoulli
(p)
random variables we have

\mathbf{P}(Q({\varepsilon}_{1},\ldots,{\varepsilon}_{n})\in I)\leq\frac{Bd^{4/3}(\log\tilde{r})^{1/2}}{(\tilde{r})^{1/(4d+1)}}\,.

To apply this Theorem to the case of Lemma
1.2
—where the variables are complex valued and arbitrary—we will use the non-degeneracy to first sample most of the randomness of each
Y_{j}
, leaving a Bernoulli random variable’s worth of randomness behind; then we will take either the real or imaginary part of the subsequent polynomial and show that it has sufficiently high rank with high probability.

Proof of Lemma
1.2
.

Define

P(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}):=\sum_{S\subset\{1,\dots,n\};|S|=k}\prod_{j\in S}Y_{j}.

(27)

Since
Y_{j}
is non-degenerate we have that at least one of
\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}
or
\operatorname{Im}Y_{j}
is non-degenerate. By replacing each
Y_{j}
with
iY_{j}
if needed, we assume without loss of generality that
\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}
is non-degenerate. As such, there exist
t\in{\mathbb{R}},\kappa>0,q\in(0,1)
so that

\mathbf{P}(\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}-t>\kappa)\geq q\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{P}(\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}-t<-\kappa)\geq q\,.

(28)

Note that since
k=O(\log n/\log\log n)
we may bound
\kappa^{k}=o(e^{{\varepsilon}n/2})
, and so by replacing
Y_{j}
with
Y_{j}/\kappa
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\kappa=1
.

For each
j=1,\dots,n
, let
Y_{j}^{+}
and
Y_{j}^{-}
be independent random variables satisfying
\mathbf{P}(Y_{j}^{+}\in K)=\mathbf{P}(Y_{j}-t\in K|\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}-t>0)
and
\mathbf{P}(Y_{j}^{-}\in K)=\mathbf{P}(Y_{j}-t\in K|\,\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}-t\leq 0)
for all measurable subsets
K\subset{\mathbb{C}}
.
Let
\eta_{j}=\mathbbm{1}_{\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}-t\geq 0},\,j\in[n]
, and note that the collection
\{\eta_{j}\}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with parameter
p:=\mathbf{P}(\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}-t>0)
.
Let
Y_{j}^{\prime}=\eta_{j}Y_{j}^{+}+(1-\eta_{j})Y_{j}^{-}+t
, and observe that
Y_{j}^{\prime}
and
Y_{j}
have the same distribution. Therefore, it suffices show that

\mathbf{P}\left(|P(Y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,Y_{n}^{\prime})|\leq 1/2\right)=o(1)\,.

For each fixed instance of the variables
\{Y_{j}^{+},Y_{j}^{-}\}
, define the polynomial
F
in the variables
\{\eta_{j}\}
via

\displaystyle F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})

\displaystyle:=P(\eta_{1}(Y_{1}^{+}-Y_{1}^{-})+Y_{1}^{-}+t,\dots,\eta_{n}(Y_{n}^{+}-Y_{n}^{-})+Y_{n}^{-}+t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subset[n],|S|=k}\left(\prod_{j\in S}(Y_{j}^{+}-Y_{j}^{-})\right)\prod_{j\in S}\eta_{j}+Q

where
Q
is a polynomial of degree
<k
in terms of
\eta_{j}
when all the
Y_{j}^{\pm}
are fixed. For
r=\lfloor n/k\rfloor
, let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
be disjoint subsets of
[n]
of size
k
. For
S\subset[n]
, define
b_{S}:=\prod_{j\in S}(Y_{j}^{+}-Y_{j}^{-})
.

Claim 3.2
.

With probability at least
1-2\exp(-cq^{2k}n/k)
, there are at least
\frac{q^{2k}n}{2k}
coefficients
b_{S_{\ell}}
satisfying
\big|b_{S_{\ell}}\big|\geq 2^{k}
.

Proof of Claim
3.2
.

For
\ell\in[r]
, define the event
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}=\{|b_{S_{\ell}}|\geq 2^{k}\}
, and note that the events
\{\mathcal{E}_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
are independent. Further, note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{P}(\big|Y_{j}^{+}-Y_{j}^{-}\big|\geq 2,\forall j\in S_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{P}(\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}^{+}-\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}^{-}\geq 2\kappa,\forall j\in S_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{P}(\left(\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}^{+}-t\right)-\left(\operatorname{Re}Y_{j}^{-}-t\right)\geq 2,\forall j\in S_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq q^{2k}

(29)

by (
28
), since we have assumed
\kappa=1
. Let
N
denote the number of
\ell
for which
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
holds. Then by Bernstein’s inequality there is a constant
c>0
so that

\mathbf{P}(N\leq q^{2k}r/2)\leq\mathbf{P}(N\leq{\mathbf{E}}N/2)\leq 2\exp(-c{\mathbf{E}}N)\leq 2\exp\left(-cq^{2k}n/k\right)\,.

Noting that
{\mathbf{E}}N\geq q^{2k}n/k
completes the claim.
∎

Note that

\operatorname{Re}F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})=\sum_{S\subset[n],|S|=k}\left(\operatorname{Re}b_{S}\right)\prod_{j\in S}\eta_{j}+\operatorname{Re}Q,\quad\operatorname{Im}F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})=\sum_{S\subset[n],|S|=k}\left(\operatorname{Im}b_{S}\right)\prod_{j\in S}\eta_{j}+\operatorname{Im}Q

and so
\operatorname{Re}F
and
\operatorname{Im}F
are both multi-affine polynomials in the variables
\{\eta_{j}\}
with real coefficients and degree at most
k
. For each coefficient
|b_{S_{\ell}}|
with
|b_{S_{\ell}}|\geq 2^{k}
we have either
|\operatorname{Re}b_{S_{\ell}}|\geq 2^{k-1}
or
|\operatorname{Im}b_{S_{\ell}}|\geq 2^{k-1}
. Thus, if we let
\mathcal{A}
be the event that either
\operatorname{Re}F
or
\operatorname{Im}F
has rank at least
q^{2k}n/(4k)
, then Claim
3.2
implies that
\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{A}^{c})\leq 2\exp(-cq^{2k}n/k)
.

Thus, for
n
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|P(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})|\leq e^{-{\varepsilon}n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}\left(|\operatorname{Re}F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})|\leq\frac{1}{2},|\operatorname{Im}F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})|\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}\right)+2e^{-cq^{2k}n/k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(|\operatorname{Re}F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})|\leq\frac{1}{2},|\operatorname{Im}F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})|\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}\right)+o(1)

(30)

where for the second bound we used that
k=o(\log n)
.

Conditioned on the event
\mathcal{A}
, suppose that the rank of
\operatorname{Re}F
is at least
q^{2k}n/(4k)
. Then we will apply the first bound in Theorem
3.1
; if we set
\alpha=\min\{p,1-p\}
and
\widetilde{r}=2^{k}\alpha^{k}q^{2k}n/(4k)
then Theorem
3.1
implies

\mathbf{P}\left(|\operatorname{Re}F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})|\leq 1/2\,\Big|\,\text{rank}\operatorname{Re}F(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})\geq q^{2k}n/(4k)\right)\leq Bk^{4/3}\frac{(\log\widetilde{r})^{1/2}}{\widetilde{r}^{1/(4k+1)}}\,.

(31)

Write
k={\varepsilon}\log n/\log\log n
, where
{\varepsilon}\leq 1/5
and note we first note that

\log\widetilde{r}=\log n\left(1+\Theta\left(\frac{{\varepsilon}}{\log\log n}\right)\right)\,.

and so

\displaystyle\log\left(Bk^{4/3}\frac{(\log\widetilde{r})^{1/2}}{\widetilde{r}^{1/(4k+1)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\log n\left(1+\Theta\left(\frac{{\varepsilon}}{\log\log n}\right)\right)\right)-\frac{\log\log n}{8{\varepsilon}}+\Theta\left(\log\log\left({\varepsilon}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{8{\varepsilon}}\right)\log\log n+o(1)+o(\log\log n)\,.

For
{\varepsilon}\leq 1/5
, the right-hand side tends to negative infinity, completing the proof.
∎

4.
Comments and Open Problems

We believe that Lemma
1.2
is suboptimal, and that in fact the same statement should hold provided
k=o(n)
.

Conjecture 4.1
.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,
be i.i.d. copies of a complex-valued non-degenerate random variable. Then for any sequence
k=k(n)
satisfying
k=o(n)
and each
{\varepsilon}>0
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n}Y_{i_{1}}Y_{i_{2}}\dots Y_{i_{k}}\right|\leq e^{-{\varepsilon}n}\right)=0.

The sharpest general statements for anti-concentration for multi-affine polynomials are provided by the work of Meka, Nguyen and Vu
[
12
]
, for which Conjecture
4.1
lies well beyond the currently known bounds. For an example of a polynomial of very high degree for which anti-concentration is known, see Tao and Vu’s work on the permanent of a random matrix
[
16
]
(see also
[
10
]
).

Remark 4.2
.

We note that substituting a positive resolution to Conjecture
4.1
for Lemma
1.2
would immediately upgrade Theorem
1.1
to hold for all
k=o(n)
; indeed, throughout the proof the only assumptions on
k
used are that
k=o(n)
and that the conclusion of Lemma
1.2
holds. Additionally, due to Lemma
2.3
, one can further assume that the variable
Y_{1}
in Conjecture
4.1
satisfies
{\mathbf{E}}|Y_{1}|<\infty
.

We also highlight a conjecture of Kabluchko
2

2

This conjecture was stated at the Workshop on Random Functions in April, 2021.
:

Conjecture 4.3

(Kabluchko)
.

For any probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{C}}
, the sequence of random measures
\mu_{n}^{(1)}
converges to
\mu
almost surely as
n\to\infty
.

We suspect that Theorem
1.1
can also be upgraded to almost sure convergence. The main difficulty appears to be in upgrading Lemma
