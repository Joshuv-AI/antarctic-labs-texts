Algebra of Nonlocal Boxes and the Collapse of Communication Complexity
Pierre Botteron, Anne Broadbent, Reda Chhaibi, Ion Nechita, Clément Pellegrini

Abstract
Communication complexity quantifies how difficult it is for two distant computers to evaluate a function f ⁡ ( X , Y ) f(X,Y) , where the strings X X and Y Y are distributed to the first and second computer respectively, under the constraint of exchanging as few bits as possible. Surprisingly, some nonlocal boxes, which are resources shared by the two computers, are so powerful that they allow to collapse communication complexity, in the sense that any Boolean function f f can be correctly estimated with the exchange of only one bit of communication. The Popescu-Rohrlich ( 𝙿𝚁 \mathtt{PR} ) box is an example of such a collapsing resource, but a comprehensive description of the set of collapsing nonlocal boxes remains elusive. In this work, we carry out an algebraic study of the structure of wirings connecting nonlocal boxes, thus defining the notion of the “product of boxes” 𝙿 ⊠ 𝚀 \mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{Q} , and we show related associativity and commutativity results. This gives rise to the notion of the “orbit of a box”, unveiling surprising geometrical properties about the alignment and parallelism of distilled boxes. The power of this new framework is that it allows us to prove previously-reported numerical observations concerning the best way to wire consecutive boxes, and to numerically and analytically recover recently-identified noisy 𝙿𝚁 \mathtt{PR} boxes that collapse communication complexity for different types of noise models.



Nonlocal boxes (NLBs) were introduced by Popescu and Rohrlich in 1994 as a theoretical generalization of quantum correlations
[

When Alice and Bob share a pair of entangled states

Generally, nonlocal boxes are non-signalling, meaning that they respect the relativistic constraint of no faster-than-light communication between parties (although there is a recent interest for partially-signalling scenarios

\displaystyle\forall a,x\in\{0,1\},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\mathtt{P}(a,b\,|\,x,0)=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\mathtt{P}(a,b\,|\,x,1)=:\mathtt{P}(a\,|\,x)\,,

\displaystyle\forall b,y\in\{0,1\},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}}\mathtt{P}(a,b\,|\,0,y)=\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}}\mathtt{P}(a,b\,|\,1,y)=:\mathtt{P}(b\,|\,y)\,.

Interestingly, some nonlocal boxes collapse what is called
communication complexity
(CC), a notion introduced by Yao in
[
48
]

and reviewed in
[
30
,
43
]

that quantifies the difficulty of performing a distributed computation. Say we want to evaluate a Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
using two distant computers, where the first computer receives as input
X\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and the other computer receives as input
Y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
.
The CC of
f
is then defined as the minimal number of bits that the computers need to communicate in order for the first computer to output the value
f(X,Y)
.
For instance, when
n=m=2
,
X=(x_{1},x_{2})
,
Y=(y_{1},y_{2})
, the CC of
f_{1}:=x_{1}\cdot(y_{1}\oplus y_{2})
equals
1
, using the communication bit
y_{1}\oplus y_{2}
, whereas it is possible to show that the CC of
f_{2}:=x_{1}\cdot y_{1}\oplus x_{2}\cdot y_{2}
equals
2
, using communication bits
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
; therefore
f_{2}
is more complex than
f_{1}
in the sense of CC.
Yao also introduced in
[
48
]
a probabilistic version of CC, in which the computers can access shared randomness, and where for all
X,Y
the first computer has to output the correct value
f(X,Y)
with probability at least
p>1/2
(
p
being independent of
X
and
Y

Now, if a nonlocal box
\mathtt{P}
is used in the protocol to compute the value
f(X,Y)
, we say that the box
\mathtt{P}

collapses
communication complexity if there exists a fixed

for which
any
Boolean function
f
, with arbitrary input size, can be correctly computed with only
one
bit of communication and probability
p
.
In this definition, an arbitrary number of copies of the box
\mathtt{P}
can be used in the protocol.
Such a collapse is strongly believed to be unachievable in Nature since it would imply the absurdity that a single bit of communication is sufficient to distantly estimate any value of any Boolean function
f

[

10
,
13
,
6
]
.

Recent results.
Two recent papers make progress on the question above.
In

Brito, Moreno, Rai, and Chaves study correlation distillation in

Our Results.
We provide a new mathematical framework and algorithms in working towards addressing the open question. The ideas are based in part on the M.Sc. thesis of one of the authors

(i)

) and (
6
) tell us that
f_{1}
or
f_{2}
is constant in the second variable, and similarly for
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
.
Without loss of generality, up to changing the roles of both
f_{1}
,
g_{1}
with respectively
f_{2},g_{2}
, we only need to consider the following two non-exclusive cases:

•

Case 1: the functions
f_{1}(x,a_{2})
and
g_{1}(y,b_{2})
are constant in the second variable, and we denote them
f_{1}(x)
and
g_{1}(y)
;

•

Case 2: the functions
f_{2}(x,a_{1})
and
g_{1}(y,b_{2})
are constant in the second variable, and we denote them
f_{2}(x)
and
g_{1}(y)
.

In Case 1, we see that coefficients sum to one by normalization of
\mathtt{P}
and
\mathtt{Q}
:

\sum_{a,b}\mathtt{P}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{Q}(a,b\,|\,x,y)\,=\,\sum_{a_{1},b_{1}}\mathtt{P}\big(a_{1},\,b_{1}\,|\,{f_{1}(x)},\,{g_{1}(y)}\big)\times\Bigg(\small\underbrace{\sum_{a_{2},b_{2}}\mathtt{Q}\Big(a_{2},\,b_{2}\,|\,{f_{2}(x,a_{1})},\,{g_{2}(y,b_{1})}\Big)}_{=1}\Bigg)\,=\,1\,.\hskip-11.38092pt

In Case 2, using the non-signalling conditions on
\mathtt{P}
and
\mathtt{Q}
, we see that coefficients sum to one again:

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b}\mathtt{P}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{Q}(a,b\,|\,x,y)\,=\,

\displaystyle\small\sum_{a_{2},b_{1}}\!\Bigg(\!\underbrace{\sum_{a_{1}}\mathtt{P}\big(a_{1},\,b_{1}\!\,|\,\!{f_{1}(x,\!a_{2}\!)},\,{g_{1}(y)}\big)}_{=\,\mathtt{P}(b_{1}\,|g_{1}(y))}\!\Bigg)\!\Bigg(\!\underbrace{\sum_{b_{2}}\mathtt{Q}\Big(a_{2},\,b_{2}\!\,|\,\!{f_{2}(x)},\,{g_{2}(y,\!b_{1}\!)}\Big)}_{=\,\mathtt{Q}(a_{2}\,|f_{2}(x))}\!\Bigg)

\displaystyle\,=\,

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sum_{b_{1}}\mathtt{P}\big(b_{1}\,|g_{1}(y)\big)\Bigg)\,\Bigg(\sum_{a_{2}}\mathtt{Q}\big(a_{2}\,|f_{2}(x)\big)\Bigg)\,=\,1\,.

Hence
\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}\mathtt{Q}
is a conditional probability distribution.
It only remains to check that
\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}\mathtt{Q}
satisfies the non-signalling conditions (
2
) and (
3
). Fix
x,a\in\{0,1\}
. In Case 1, we have for all
y\in\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69046pt\sum_{b}\mathtt{P}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{Q}(a,b\,|\,x,y)\,=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{1},a_{2}}\mathtt{P}\big(a_{1}\,|\,f_{1}(x)\big)\,\mathtt{Q}\big(a_{2}\,|\,f_{2}(x,a_{1})\big)\,\mathbbm{1}_{a=f_{3}(x,a_{1},a_{2})}\,=:\,\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{W}\mathtt{Q}(a\,|\,x)\,,

i.e.
the result does not depend on
y
,
which means that the marginal in
b
is well-defined.
This is similar in Case 2, changing
f_{1}(x)
into
f_{1}(x,a_{2})
and
f_{2}(x,a_{1})
into
f_{2}(x)
.
Hence the first non-signalling condition (
2
) is satisfied, and the other one (
3
) follows in a similar way.
∎

1.3
Mixed Wirings

Using local randomness, one can generalize the class of deterministic wirings to the one of
mixed wirings
. The difference is that the functions
f_{i}
and
g_{j}
take values in
[0,1]
instead of
\{0,1\}
.
For instance, if
f_{1}(x,a_{1})=p\in[0,1]
for some fixed bits
x
and
a_{1}
, it means that Alice uses a Bernoulli distribution
\mathcal{B}(p)
to input the bit
1
with probability
p
, or the bit
0
with probability
1-p
.
In other words, we have
32
Bernoulli variables
(B_{1},\dots,B_{32})
, whose parameters are stored in
\mathsf{W}=\big(\text{\small$f_{1}(0,0),f_{1}(0,1),\dots,g_{3}(1,1,1)$}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{32}
, and the box product
\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{Q}
becomes the expected value of the deterministic wirings:

\mathtt{P}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{Q}\,=\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathtt{P}\underset{\{\!B_{i}\!\}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{Q}\Big]\,.

(8)

Note that this generalization of wirings does not change the definition of nonlocal boxes: the inputs and outputs of a box are still classical bits, not any real number between
0
and
1
.
In order to ensure a well-defined local order for both Alice and Bob, we will need to add a dependence relation between the variables
B_{i}
, namely the non-cyclicity condition, as for the deterministic wirings:

Definition 4
(Mixed wiring).

A
mixed wiring

\mathsf{W}
between two boxes
\mathtt{P},\mathtt{Q}\in\mathcal{N\!S}
consists in six functions
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}:\{0,1\}^{2}\to[0,1]
and
f_{3},g_{3}:\{0,1\}^{3}\to[0,1]
satisfying the
non-cyclicity conditions
(
5
) and (
6
).
Mixed wirings form a set that we denote
\mathcal{W}
.

The set of mixed wirings
\mathsf{W}
is not convex because non-cyclicity conditions (
5
) and (
6
) are non-affine equalities.
For instance, consider the wirings
\mathsf{W}
,
\mathsf{W}^{\prime}
with all coefficients
0
except the one corresponding to respectively
f_{1}(0,0)=1
,
f^{\prime}_{2}(0,0)=1
; each of these wirings satisfies the non-cyclicity conditions (
5
) and (
6
), but the mean
\mathsf{W}^{\prime\prime}=(\mathsf{W}+\mathsf{W}^{\prime})/2
does not:

\big(f^{\prime\prime}_{1}(0,0)-f^{\prime\prime}_{1}(0,1)\big)\big(f^{\prime\prime}_{2}(0,0)-f^{\prime\prime}_{2}(0,1)\big)\,=\,\big(1/2-0\big)\big(1/2-0\big)\,\neq\,0\,,

hence the non-convexity of
\mathcal{W}
.
The expression of
\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}\mathtt{Q}
is the same as before, with the convention that
\mathtt{P}(a,b\,|\,\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
means
(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\mathtt{P}(ab\,|\,00)+(1-\alpha)\beta\mathtt{P}(ab\,|\,01)+\alpha(1-\beta)\mathtt{P}(ab\,|\,10)+\alpha\beta\mathtt{P}(ab\,|\,11)
, which gives:

\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213}\mathsf{W}}\mathtt{Q}(a,b\,|\,x,y)\\
=\,\hskip-14.22636pt\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}\in\{0,1\}}\hskip-8.5359pt\Big[\mathtt{P}\big(a_{1},\,b_{1}\,|\,0,\,0\big)(1-\text{\footnotesize\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213} $f_{1}(x,a_{2})$})\,(1-\text{\footnotesize\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213} $g_{1}(y,b_{2})$})+\mathtt{P}\big(a_{1},\,b_{1}\,|\,0,\,1\big)(1-\text{\footnotesize\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213} $f_{1}(x,a_{2})$})\,\text{\footnotesize\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213} $g_{1}(y,b_{2})$}\\
+\,\mathtt{P}\big(a_{1},\,b_{1}\,|\,1,\,0\big)\,\text{\footnotesize\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213} $f_{1}(x,a_{2})$}\,(1-\text{\footnotesize\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213} $g_{1}(y,b_{2})$})+\mathtt{P}\big(a_{1},\,b_{1}\,|\,1,\,1\big)\,\text{\footnotesize\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213} $f_{1}(x,a_{2})$}\,\text{\footnotesize\color[rgb]{0.358,0.1873,0.5213} $g_{1}(y,b_{2})$}\Big]\\

(9)

Using the probabilistic point of view of

)​​

Fact
3
​​

that the set
\mathcal{N\!S}
is closed under mixed wirings:

Fact 5
.

\mathcal{N\!S}
is closed under mixed wirings.∎

Remark 6

Note that the formalism presented here is deliberately not the most general one, since this simpler version is enough to state our results in the next sections.
For a more general framework, see
[

.
For instance, here we require a deterministic local box order: knowing

Example 1

In

Non-Commutativity and Non-Associativity.

A direct consequence of the multiplication table in
Figure
4
​​

is that the algebra
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{W}_{\text{BS}}}
induced by the wiring
\mathsf{W}_{\text{BS}}
is non-commutative (
\mathtt{P}_{0}\boxtimes\mathtt{PR}\neq\mathtt{PR}\boxtimes\mathtt{P}_{0}
) and non-associative (
(\mathtt{P}_{0}\boxtimes\mathtt{P}_{1})\boxtimes\mathtt{PR}\neq\mathtt{P}_{0}\boxtimes(\mathtt{P}_{1}\boxtimes\mathtt{PR})
).
This non-associativity is at the root of interesting remarks, see drawings of the orbit of a box in the next section,
Figure
7
​​

.
Similarly, the algebra induced by the wiring
\mathsf{W}_{\text{dist}}
is both non-commutative and non-associative, but
on the contrary, the algebras induced by
\mathsf{W}\in\{\mathsf{W}_{\text{triv}},\mathsf{W}_{\oplus},\mathsf{W}_{\wedge},\mathsf{W}_{\vee\!\wedge}\}
are both commutative and associative.
One may wonder if there exist induced algebras that are associative but not commutative, and the converse.
To that end, here is a characterization of commutativity and associativity in a simple case where boxes are set in parallel and with the same input functions:

Proposition 7
(Characterization of commutativity and associativity).

Assume
\mathsf{W}
is a wiring such that
f_{1}=f_{2}=f(x)
and
g_{1}=g_{2}=g(y)
. Then:

(i)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{W}}
is commutative if and only if the functions
f_{3}(x,a_{1},a_{2})
and
g_{3}(y,b_{1},b_{2})
are “symmetric” in the last two variables, in the sense that
f_{3}(x,a_{1},a_{2})=f_{3}(x,a_{2},a_{1})
for all
x,a_{1},a_{2}
, and similarly for
g_{3}
.

If in addition
f(x)=x
and
g(y)=y
:

(ii)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{W}}
is associative if and only if the functions
f_{3}(x,a_{1},a_{2})
and
g_{3}(y,b_{1},b_{2})
are “associative” in the last two variables, in the sense that
f_{3}(x,a_{1},f_{3}(x,a_{2},a_{3}))=f_{3}(x,f_{3}(x,a_{1},a_{2}),a_{3})
for all
x,a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
, and similarly for
g_{3}
.

Proof.

(i)
First, from the expression (
7
), see that for all bits
a,b,x,y
and any boxes
\mathtt{P},\mathtt{Q}
in
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{W}}
, we have:

\mathtt{P}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{Q}(a,b\,|\,x,y)-\mathtt{Q}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{P}(a,b\,|\,x,y)\\
\,=\,\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}}\mathtt{P}\big(a_{1},b_{1}\,|\,f(x),g(y)\big)\times\mathtt{Q}\big(a_{2},b_{2}\,|\,f(x),g(y)\big)\times\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{a=f_{3}(x,a_{1},a_{2})}\mathbbm{1}_{b=g_{3}(y,b_{1},b_{2})}\\
-\mathbbm{1}_{a=f_{3}(x,a_{2},a_{1})}\mathbbm{1}_{b=g_{3}(y,b_{2},b_{1})}\Big]\,.

Hence, if
f_{3}
and
g_{3}
are both symmetric in the last two variables, then the difference is null and the algebra is commutative.
Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{W}}
is commutative, so that the left-hand side is null. Taking probability distributions
\mathtt{P}
and
\mathtt{Q}
that are always positive (such as
\mathtt{I}
), we have that the difference in the right-hand side has to be null for all
x,y,a,b,a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}
.
Fix
x,a_{1},a_{2}
and consider
a:=f_{3}(x,a_{1},a_{2})
, and similarly
fix
y,b_{1},b_{2}
and consider
b:=g_{3}(y,b_{1},b_{2})
.
We obtain
1-\mathbbm{1}_{a=f_{3}(x,a_{2},a_{1})}\mathbbm{1}_{b=g_{3}(y,b_{2},b_{1})}=0
, which means that both indicator functions are equal to
1
, and therefore both subscript equalities hold. Hence, this being true for any fixed
x,a_{1},a_{2}
and
y,b_{1},b_{2}
, we obtain that
f_{3}
and
g_{3}
are symmetric as wanted.

(ii)
From (
7
) again, we have for all bits
a,b,x,y
and any boxes
\mathtt{P},\mathtt{Q},\mathtt{R}
in
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{W}}
:

\mathtt{P}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}(\mathtt{Q}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{R})(a,b\,|\,x,y)-(\mathtt{P}\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{Q})\underset{\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{R}(a,b\,|\,x,y)\\

-\mathbbm{1}_{a=f_{3}(x,f_{3}(x,a_{1},a_{2}),a_{3})}\mathbbm{1}_{b=g_{3}(y,g_{3}(y,b_{1},b_{2}),b_{3})}\Big]\,.

A similar proof with double implication as in

2
Orbit of a Box

In this section, we study the set of all boxes that can be generated given many copies of a starting box
\mathtt{P}
and a wiring
\mathsf{W}
.
After introducing the
orbit
of a box, we provide some consequences to communication complexity. Subsequently, we study a particular example,
\mathsf{W}_{\text{BS}}
, with which we find collapsing boxes in
Subsection
4.2
​​

, and then we give some general remarks about other orbits.
Finally, we conclude this section by giving the technical proof of the theorem stating that the “best” parenthesization is the multiplication on the right.

2.1
Definition

Given multiple copies of a non-signalling box
\mathtt{P}\in\mathcal{N\!S}
and of a (mixed) wiring
\mathsf{W}
, Alice and Bob can produce many other boxes,
e.g.

(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}\mathtt{P})\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}\mathtt{P}
or
\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}\mathtt{P})
. All of these new boxes are again non-signalling because
\mathcal{N\!S}
is closed under wirings, see
Lemma
5
​​

. We call
orbit
of the box
\mathtt{P}
(induced by the wiring
\mathsf{W}
) the set of all of these possible new boxes:

\displaystyle\Orbit_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathtt{P})\,:=\,

\displaystyle\Big\{\text{\footnotesize boxes $\mathtt{Q}\in\mathcal{N\!S}$ that can be produced by using finitely many times the box $\mathtt{P}$ and the wiring $\mathsf{W}$}\Big\}

\displaystyle\,=\,

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\Orbit_{\mathsf{W}}^{(k)}(\mathtt{P})\subseteq\mathcal{N\!S}\,,

where
\Orbit^{(k)}(\mathtt{P})
is called the
orbit of depth
k
of
\mathtt{P}
(or simply
k
-orbit
), defined as:

\Orbit_{\mathsf{W}}^{(k)}(\mathtt{P})\,:=\,\Big\{\text{all possible products with $k$ times the term $\mathtt{P}$, using the multiplication $\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}$}\Big\}\,.

When the context is clear, we overload the notation and write
\Orbit
and
\Orbit^{(k)}
respectively.
In general, these
k
-orbits are not singletons for
k\geq 3
since
the algebra
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{W}}
induced by
\mathsf{W}
is not necessarily associative and commutative (see
Figure
5
​​

). Actually, up to multiplicity, the cardinal
\#\Orbit^{(k)}
is exactly the number of parenthesizations with
k
terms, which is the Catalan number
\frac{1}{k}\tiny\begin{pmatrix}2k-2\\
k-1\end{pmatrix}
, which grows exponentially fast.
Here are the
3
- and
4
- orbits:

\displaystyle\Orbit^{(3)}(\mathtt{P})\,=\,\Big\{(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\boxtimes\mathtt{P},\,\mathtt{P}\boxtimes(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle\Orbit^{(4)}(\mathtt{P})\,=\,\Big\{\text{\footnotesize$\big((\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\boxtimes\mathtt{P}\big)\boxtimes\mathtt{P},\,\big(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\big)\boxtimes\mathtt{P},\,(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\boxtimes(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P}),\,\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\big((\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\boxtimes\mathtt{P}\big),\,\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\big(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\big)$}\Big\}\,.

Note that a
k
-orbit (
k\geq 2
) can be inductively computed using orbits with lower depth:

\Orbit^{(k)}\,=\,\bigcup_{1\leq\ell\leq k-1}\Orbit^{(\ell)}\boxtimes\Orbit^{(k-\ell)}\,,

which is the same recurrence relation as that of Catalan numbers.

2.2
Consequences to Communication Complexity

Assume Alice and Bob are given infinitely many copies of a nonlocal box
\mathtt{P}
, and assume they want to distantly compute (in finite time) the value of a Boolean function
f(X,Y)
, where
X,Y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
are strings that are known by Alice and Bob respectively.
Among all the possible protocols they can try to do in order to succeed, they can wire their copies of
\mathtt{P}
in order to produce a “better” box. For example, starting from a noisy box
\mathtt{P}
, Alice and Bob can try to produce a box that is closer to the “perfect box”
\mathtt{PR}
which satisfies
a\oplus b=xy
without noise.
Such a protocol is called a
distillation protocol

[
13
]
.
We call
collapsing box
a nonlocal box that collapses CC.

Find Collapsing Boxes Using the Orbit.

Imagine Alice and Bob are able to produce a collapsing box
\mathtt{Q}
after applying wirings to copies of a starting box
\mathtt{P}
. Then they can use that new box
\mathtt{Q}
to distantly compute the value
f(X,Y)
, which means that they have a protocol to collapse communication complexity and therefore that
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing.
This point of view is particularly interesting since it implies that it is sufficient to find a single collapsing box in the union
\bigcup_{\mathsf{W}}\Orbit_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathtt{P})
to deduce that
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing as well.
See an illustration in
Figure
6
​​

(a).

Find Collapsing Boxes Using a Cone.

Once we find a collapsing box
\mathtt{P}
, we can deduce many other collapsing boxes: there is a convex cone taking origin at
\mathtt{P}
that is collapsing as well. More precisely, given a box
\mathtt{P}
, denote
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{P}}
the convex cone of boxes
\mathtt{R}
for which there exists a local correlation
\mathtt{L}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\mathtt{P}=\lambda\,\mathtt{R}+(1-\lambda)\,\mathtt{L}
, with
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
We claim that if
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing, then any
\mathtt{R}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{P}}
is collapsing as well.
Indeed, assume Alice and Bob are given copies of a box
\mathtt{R}
.
Then, they can use shared randomness to produce the wanted box
\mathtt{L}
and the wanted convex coefficient
\lambda
, so that they can generate the box
\mathtt{P}
with the relation
\mathtt{P}=\lambda\,\mathtt{R}+(1-\lambda)\,\mathtt{L}
. Now, as
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing, they have a protocol that collapses communication complexity, hence
\mathtt{R}
is collapsing as well.
See an illustration in
Figure
6
​​

(b).
In the study of collapsing boxes, notice that it is standard to assume that shared randomness is a “free” resource; for instance Brassard
et. al.
made that choice in
[
10
]
in their collapsing protocol.

Combining arguments from these last two paragraphs and by the fact that Alice and Bob can make a convex combination of boxes using shared randomness, we deduce a sufficient criterion for a box to collapse communication complexity:

Proposition 8
(Collapsing orbit).

Let
\mathtt{P}
be a box in
\mathcal{N\!S}
. If there exists a box
\mathtt{Q}\in\Conv\big(\mathcal{L}\cup\bigcup_{\mathsf{W}}\Orbit_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathtt{P})\big)
that collapses communication complexity, then
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing as well. See
Figure
6

(c)
.

Orbits that collapse communication complexity.

\forall\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in\mathcal{A},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt1-c_{3}(\mathtt{A}\boxtimes\mathtt{B})=\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{A})\big)\,\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{B})\big)\,.

Now, interestingly, we observe that the points of a given
k

Theorem 10
(Alignment).

For any
k\geq 1
and
\mathtt{P}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{}}\mathcal{A}

\Orbit^{(k)}(\mathtt{P})
are all aligned on a line
\mathfrak{L}_{k}
whose expression in convex coordinates is given by:

\mathfrak{L}_{k}\,:=\,\Big\{\mathtt{A}\in\mathcal{A}\,:\,c_{3}(\mathtt{A})=1-\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{P})\big)^{k}\Big\}\,.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
k\geq 1
that
\Orbit^{(k)}\subseteq\mathfrak{L}_{k}
.
For
k=1

1

\mathtt{P}

c_{3}(\mathtt{P})=1-\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{P})\big)
, so
\mathtt{P}
indeed belongs to
\mathfrak{L}_{1}
.
Now, assume the result holds
until
some integer
k\geq 1
, and let
\mathtt{Q}\in\Orbit^{(k)}
. By definition, the box
\mathtt{Q}
decomposes as
\mathtt{Q}=\mathtt{Q}_{1}\boxtimes\mathtt{Q}_{2}

\mathtt{Q}_{1}\in\Orbit^{(\ell)}
and
\mathtt{Q}_{2}\in\Orbit^{(k-\ell)}
for some

c_{3}(\mathtt{Q}_{1})=1-\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{P})\big)^{\ell}
and

c_{3}(\mathtt{Q}_{2})=1-\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{P})\big)^{k-\ell}\,.

Then using

​​

\displaystyle c_{3}(\mathtt{Q})\,=\,

\displaystyle 1-\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{Q}_{1})\big)\,\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{Q}_{2})\big)

\displaystyle\,=\,

\displaystyle 1-\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{P})\big)^{\ell}\,\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{P})\big)^{k-\ell}

\displaystyle\,=\,

\displaystyle 1-\big(1-c_{3}(\mathtt{P})\big)^{k}\,,

which means that
\mathtt{Q}
belongs to the line
\mathfrak{L}_{k}
.
∎

Corollary 11
(Parallelism).

The supporting line
\mathfrak{L}_{k}
of all the orbits
\Orbit^{(k)}
are parallel to the diagonal line
\mathfrak{L}_{D}:=\Aff\{\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{SR}\}

\forall k\geq 1,\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\Orbit^{(k)}||\,\mathfrak{L}_{D}\,.

In particular, all the orbits are parallel to each other:

\forall k,\ell\geq 1,\hskip 10.00002pt\Orbit^{(k)}||\Orbit^{(\ell)}\,.

∎

Moreover, looking closely at the sequence of coefficients

Assume
\mathtt{P}\notin\mathfrak{L}_{D}

tends to the line

and parallel to the diagonal
\mathfrak{L}_{D}

∎

k

\mathtt{P}

Proof of

Recall that
\mathtt{SR}:=(\mathtt{P}_{0}+\mathtt{P}_{1})/2

{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P})\,:=\,\frac{1}{4}\sum_{a\oplus b=xy}\mathtt{P}(a,b\,|\,x,y)\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt{\texttt{CHSH}}^{\prime}(\mathtt{P})\,:=\,\frac{1}{4}\hskip-11.38092pt\sum_{\tiny\begin{array}[]{c}a\oplus b\\
=(x\oplus 1)(y\oplus 1)\end{array}}\hskip-11.38092pt\mathtt{P}(a,b\,|\,x,y)\,.

For example, we have
{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{PR})=1
and
{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{SR})=\frac{3}{4}
and
{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{I})=\frac{1}{2}
.
Denote
\mathcal{A}
the affine space
\mathcal{A}:=\Aff\{\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{SR},\mathtt{I}\}

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
the set of boxes
\mathtt{P}
in the convex hull
\Conv\{\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{SR},\mathtt{I}\}
whose

We will prove our results in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}

2\mathtt{I}-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
the symmetric of
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
by
\mathtt{I}

Lemma 17
(Multiplying by
\mathtt{P}
preserves the
CHSH
-value order).

Let
\mathtt{P}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
, and let
\mathtt{Q}\neq\mathtt{R}\in\mathcal{A}
such that the line
\Aff\{\mathtt{Q},\mathtt{R}\}
is parallel to the diagonal line
\mathfrak{L}_{D}:=\Aff\{\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{SR}\}

We have:

{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{Q})\geq{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{R})\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{Q}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\geq{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{R}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\,,\\
{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{Q})\geq{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{R})\,.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

As the box
\mathtt{P}
lies in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
, it is of the form
\mathtt{P}=p_{1}\mathtt{PR}+p_{2}\mathtt{SR}+(1-p_{1}-p_{2})\mathtt{I}
for some coefficients
p_{1},p_{2}\geq 0
such that
p_{1}+p_{2}\leq 1
.
Rewrite it as
\mathtt{P}=\tiny\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix}
, and similarly denote
\mathtt{Q}=\tiny\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
q_{2}\end{pmatrix}
and
\mathtt{R}=\tiny\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}\\
r_{2}\end{pmatrix}
for some coefficients
q_{i},r_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.
By the parallelism assumption, vectors
\mathtt{Q}-\mathtt{R}
and
\mathtt{PR}-\mathtt{SR}
have to be colinear,
i.e.
there must exist some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
such that
\mathtt{Q}-\mathtt{R}=\lambda(\mathtt{PR}-\mathtt{SR})=\lambda\tiny\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
, so we may rewrite the second coefficient of
\mathtt{R}
as
r_{2}=q_{1}+q_{2}-r_{1}
.
With this notation, we can use the linearity of the function
{\texttt{CHSH}}(\cdot)
to see that condition
{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{Q})\geq{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{R})
simplifies to
(q_{1}-r_{1})\geq 0

\displaystyle\small{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{Q})\!-\!{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{R})

\displaystyle=\,\small(q_{1}\!-\!r_{1})\!\times\!{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{PR})+(q_{2}\!-\!r_{2})\!\times\!{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{SR})+\Big(\!(1\!-\!q_{1}\!-\!q_{2})\!-\!(1\!-\!r_{1}\!-\!r_{2})\!\Big)\!\times\!{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{I})

\displaystyle=\,(q_{1}-r_{1})\times 1-\textstyle(q_{1}-r_{1})\times\frac{3}{4}+0\times \frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\,\textstyle\frac{1}{4}(q_{1}-r_{1})\,.

Now, using the multiplication table from
Figure
4
​​

and bilinearity of
\boxtimes
, we may compute the following expressions:

\displaystyle{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{Q}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})-{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{R}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})

\displaystyle\,=\,\textstyle\frac{1}{8}(p_{1}+2p_{2})(q_{1}-r_{1})\geq 0\,,

\displaystyle{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{Q})-{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{R})

\displaystyle\,=\,\textstyle\frac{1}{16}(1-p_{1}+p_{2})(q_{1}-r_{1})\geq 0\,,

which gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 18
(Right multiplication gives better
CHSH
-value).

For any
\mathtt{P}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathtt{Q}\in\widetilde{\Orbit}(\mathtt{P})
, we have:

{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{Q})\geq{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P})\hskip 10.00002pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt{\texttt{CHSH}}\Big(\mathtt{Q}\boxtimes\mathtt{P}\Big)\,\geq\,{\texttt{CHSH}}\Big(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{Q}\Big).

Proof.

Use the coordinate system
(x,y)
given by the
{\texttt{CHSH}}^{\prime}
- and
CHSH
- values respectively in order to write
\mathtt{P}
and
\mathtt{Q}
as taking coordinates
(x_{\mathtt{P}},y_{\mathtt{P}})
and
(x_{\mathtt{Q}},y_{\mathtt{Q}})
.
For instance we have
\mathtt{PR}:(\frac{1}{2},1)
and
\mathtt{SR}:(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})
and
\mathtt{I}:(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})

Use the multiplication table from
Equation (
11
)​​

and apply the bilinearity of
\boxtimes

{\texttt{CHSH}}\big(\mathtt{Q}\boxtimes\mathtt{P}\big)-{\texttt{CHSH}}\big(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{Q}\big)\,=\,\textstyle\frac{1}{8}(12x_{\mathtt{P}}y_{\mathtt{Q}}-12y_{\mathtt{P}}x_{\mathtt{Q}}-7x_{\mathtt{P}}+7y_{\mathtt{P}}+7x_{\mathtt{Q}}-7y_{\mathtt{Q}})\,=:\,f_{\mathtt{P}}(x_{\mathtt{Q}},y_{\mathtt{Q}})\,.

For any fixed
\mathtt{P}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
, we want to show that
f_{\mathtt{P}}(x_{\mathtt{Q}},y_{\mathtt{Q}})\geq 0
.
By construction, we know that
\mathtt{P}\in\mathfrak{L}_{1}
and
\mathtt{Q}\in\mathfrak{L}_{k}

we have
x_{\mathtt{Q}}+y_{\mathtt{Q}}\leq x_{\mathtt{P}}+y_{\mathtt{P}}

x_{\mathtt{Q}}\leq x_{\mathtt{P}}+y_{\mathtt{P}}-y_{\mathtt{Q}}
.
As
\mathtt{P}

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathtt{Q}}}f_{\mathtt{P}}(x_{\mathtt{Q}},y_{\mathtt{Q}})=\frac{1}{8}(7-12y_{\mathtt{P}})\leq-1/4\leq 0
, which means that the function

is decreasing over
\mathbb{R}
for any fixed
y_{\mathtt{Q}}
.
It yields the following inequalities:

f_{\mathtt{P}}(x_{\mathtt{Q}},y_{\mathtt{Q}})\,\geq\,f_{\mathtt{P}}(x_{\mathtt{P}}+y_{\mathtt{P}}-y_{\mathtt{Q}},y_{\mathtt{Q}})\,=\,\textstyle\frac{3}{2}\big(y_{\mathtt{Q}}-y_{\mathtt{P}}\big)\big(x_{\mathtt{P}}+y_{\mathtt{P}}-\frac{7}{6}\big)\,\geq\,0\,,

and the second one is non-negative using

Recall that the set

Statement
(Theorem

Let

We want to show that:

{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}^{\boxtimes k+1})\geq{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{Q}\boxtimes\mathtt{P})\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}^{\boxtimes k+1})\geq{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{Q})\,,

For the other inequality, start from

and apply

in order to get
\geq{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}\boxtimes\mathtt{P}^{\boxtimes k})

Then conclude using

for any

3
Numerical Optimization on the Set of Wirings

We saw in the previous section that, given a non-signalling box
\mathtt{P}
, there may exist a wiring
\mathsf{W}
that sufficiently distills the box
\mathtt{P}
in order to collapse communication complexity.
The question we address in this section is the following: if the box
\mathtt{P}
is fixed, how to find a wiring
\mathsf{W}
good enough to collapse communication complexity (when it is possible)?
The difficulty is that, for each input
x,y\in\{0,1\}
, there are
82
possible deterministic wirings
[
45
]
, leading to a total number of
82^{4}\approx 10^{8}
possible deterministic wirings. So a naive discrete optimization over deterministic wirings seems inefficient. To that end, we present two optimization algorithms:
(i) an algorithm that tests many different combinations of wirings and that is suitable for numerical simulations, and
(ii) another one that finds a “uniform” collapsing wiring
\mathsf{W}
in a whole region of boxes, which is appropriate for deriving an analytical proof (see the next section).
This section might be skipped at first reading as it is more technical.
See our GitHub page for the details of the algorithms
[
9
]
.

Remark 19

(Comparison with
[
23
,
11
]
)
.

We now compare and contrast our methods with two recent works that also study optimization over wirings:

(i)

In
[
11
]
, the authors suggest reducing the
82^{4}
possible deterministic wirings for Alice and Bob to only
3152
by simply considering the ones that preserve the
\mathtt{PR}
box,
i.e.
wirings
\mathsf{W}
such that
\mathtt{PR}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}\mathtt{PR}=\mathtt{PR}
, and then doing a discrete optimization over that smaller set. This smaller set encompasses for instance the wirings
\mathsf{W}_{\text{BS}}
,
\mathsf{W}_{\text{dist}}

but discards
\mathsf{W}_{\oplus}
,
\mathsf{W}_{\wedge}
,
\mathsf{W}_{\vee\!\wedge}

(see definitions in
Subsection
1.4
​​

); see also
[
23
, Supplementary Material, I]
which mentions that even some optimal wirings are discarded.
This technique allows them to analytically prove that many new areas of boxes are collapsing.

(ii)

In
[
23
]
, the authors use a mix of exhaustive search and linear programming. For each of Bob’s
82^{2}
extremal half-wirings, they apply linear programming to optimize Alice’s half-wiring, and then they select the best pair of half-wirings. This allows them to numerically find optimal wirings for any pair of boxes, which leads them to discover new collapsing boxes.

(iii)

In our work, we use an efficient variant of the Gradient Descent algorithm, based on Line Search methods, frequent resets, and parallel descents.
A limitation in the method from
[
11
]
could come from the fact that many wirings are discarded; this is why we choose to take our feasible set to be the entire set of mixed wirings
\mathcal{W}\subseteq[0,1]^{32}
.
In
[
23
, Supplementary Material, II]
, the authors implement a sequence of different optimal wirings, which we do similarly in what we call later Task A, but we also implement a uniform version of it in Task B, which allows us to find a single optimal wiring for a whole region of boxes (instead of a sequence of wirings) and then to prove by hand the collapse of communication complexity for those boxes.
In this manner, we recover both the numerical results of
[
23
]
(see details in
Subsection
4.1
​​

) and the analytical results of
[
11
]
(see details in
Subsection
4.2
​​

).

3.1
Goals of the Algorithms

Task A.

To prove that a box
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing, a particular case of
Proposition
8
​​

says that it is enough to find a finite sequence of wirings
(\mathsf{W}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{W}_{N})
such that the following box is collapsing:

\mathtt{P}_{N+1}:=\Big(\big((\mathtt{P}\underset{\,\mathsf{W}_{1}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{P})\underset{\,\mathsf{W}_{2}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{P}\big)\underset{\,\mathsf{W}_{3}}{\boxtimes} \dots\Big)\underset{\,\mathsf{W}_{N}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{P}\,.

Note that we need to specify the parenthetization because the different products
\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}_{i}}
are potentially non-associative. Among the numerous possibilities, we choose the parenthesization on the left because it is easy to implement and because it is the best one when the wiring is
\mathsf{W}_{\text{BS}}
, see
Theorem
13
​​

.
This algorithm will consist in an iterative construction of the sequence
(\mathsf{W}_{i})_{i}
: first, find a wiring
\mathsf{W}_{1}
such that the
CHSH
-value of the box
\mathtt{P}_{2}:=\mathtt{P}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}_{1}}\mathtt{P}
is the highest possible, then find
\mathsf{W}_{2}
such that the
CHSH
-value of the box
\mathtt{P}_{3}:=\mathtt{P}_{2}\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}_{2}}\mathtt{P}
is the highest possible, so on and so forth until the
N
-th iteration.
If the
CHSH
-value of the box
\mathtt{P}_{N+1}
is above the threshold
\frac{3+\sqrt{6}}{6}\approx 0.91\%
, we know that communication complexity collapses
[
10
]
, so the starting box
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing as well. Otherwise, we cannot conclude whether
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing or not.

Task B.

The goal of this algorithm is essentially the same as the first one, but we add a strong constraint: we want all the
\mathsf{W}_{i}
to be the same wiring
\mathsf{W}
:

\Big(\big((\mathtt{P}\underset{\,\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{P})\underset{\,\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{P}\big)\underset{\,\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes} \dots\Big)\underset{\,\mathsf{W}}{\boxtimes}\mathtt{P}\,\,=:\,\,\mathtt{P}^{\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}N+1}\,.

In that sense, this is a “uniform” version of the first algorithm.
The interest of this algorithm is that it helps to give analytical proofs (see
Section
4
​​

): if the value
{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{P}^{\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}N})
is above the threshold
\frac{3+\sqrt{6}}{6}\approx 0.91\%
for some
N
, then by continuity of
\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}
, there is an open neighborhood around
\mathtt{P}
such that for any
\mathtt{Q}
close enough to
\mathtt{P}
we also have that
{\texttt{CHSH}}(\mathtt{Q}^{\boxtimes_{\mathsf{W}}N})
is above the threshold, and therefore the whole neighborhood of
\mathtt{P}
is collapsing.
This technique will help to discover wide collapsing areas and to provide analytical proofs by hand.

(14)

(b), and that

\mathsf{W}\leftarrow\begin{bmatrix}\min\{w_{1},1\}&\dots&\min\{w_{32},1\}\end{bmatrix}

for

Proposition 22
.

The triangle
C:=\Conv\{\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{P}_{0},\mathtt{P}_{1}\}
is a face on the boundary of
\mathcal{N\!S}
.

Proof.

First, we prove the equality between the sets

, meaning that the convex hull

is actually a slice of

The first inclusion

is trivial because the three points
\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{P}_{0},\mathtt{P}_{1}
are in
\mathcal{N\!S}
and because
\mathcal{N\!S}

Conclusion

Our new algebraic perspective on nonlocal boxes allowed us to discover a surprising structure of what we called the orbit of a box, with some strong alignment and parallelism properties (see

As a consequence, this deeper understanding of the algebraic structure of nonlocal boxes enabled us to recover in

The importance of our results is emphasized by considering the many known impossibility results

Note also that according to our present intuition of Nature

We made advances towards answering the open question of determining which nonlocal boxes do indeed collapse communication complexity, but there is still a gap to be filled: for instance, as plotted in light blue in

​​

, there are still regions of
\mathcal{N\!S}
for which it is unknown whether there is a collapse of communication complexity.

Further work includes the study of the “square root” of a box: here we introduced and studied the properties of the product

\mathtt{Q}
such that

(Alternative proof of Theorem
23
).

The set
T:=\Conv\{\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{P}_{0},\mathtt{P}_{1}\}\backslash\Conv\{\mathtt{P}_{0},\mathtt{P}_{1}\}
is collapsing.

Proof.

Consider boxes of the form
\mathtt{P}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\alpha\mathtt{PR}+\beta\mathtt{P}_{0}+(1-\alpha-\beta)\mathtt{P}_{1}
, where

\alpha,\beta\geq 0

\alpha

The triangle

\Delta\,=\,\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{K}_{n}\,,

where
\mathcal{K}_{n}:=\Delta\cap\{\alpha\geq 1/n\}
.
Denote
\boxtimes
the box product induced by the wiring
\mathsf{W}_{\text{BS}}
inspired from

13
]
.

, and fix an integer

large enough so that
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
.
We want to build a protocol that collapses communication complexity using the starting box
\mathtt{P}_{\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}}
as a resource. To do so, we will define a sequence of boxes
(\mathtt{P}_{\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}})_{k}
that are eventually collapsing for
k
large enough.
First, see that the closure
\overline{T}
of the triangle
T
is stable under
\boxtimes
, because
\overline{T}
equals
\mathcal{N\!S}\cap\Aff\{\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{P}_{0},\mathtt{P}_{1}\}
(see
Proposition
22
​​

)
and the two intersected sets are stable under
\boxtimes

8

\mathcal{N\!S}
is stable under
\boxtimes
because it is closed under wirings
[
1
]
, (ii) the affine plane
\Aff\{\mathtt{PR},\mathtt{P}_{0},\mathtt{P}_{1}\}
is stable under under
\boxtimes
as a consequence of the multiplication table in

​​

and by bilinearity of
\boxtimes
.

.

\boxtimes
and using the multiplication table in

​​

\mathtt{P}_{\alpha,\beta}\boxtimes\mathtt{P}_{\alpha,\beta}=\mathtt{P}_{\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}}\,,

with

and
\tilde{\beta}:=1-2\alpha+\alpha^{2}+\frac{5}{2}\alpha\beta-2\beta+2\beta^{2}
.
We then consider the function
F:\mathcal{K}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
F(\alpha,\beta):=(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})

(\alpha_{k+1},\beta_{k+1}):=F(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})

\alpha+\beta\leq 1

f_{\beta}
