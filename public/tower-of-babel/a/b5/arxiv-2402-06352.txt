Blockchain Bribing Attacks and the Efficacy of Counterincentives
Dimitris Karakostas, Aggelos Kiayias, Thomas Zacharias

Abstract
We analyze bribing attacks in Proof-of-Stake distributed ledgers from a game theoretic perspective. In bribing attacks, an adversary offers participants a reward in exchange for instructing them how to behave, with the goal of attacking the protocol’s properties. Specifically, our work focuses on adversaries that target blockchain safety. We consider two types of bribing, depending on how the bribes are awarded: i) guided bribing , where the bribe is given as long as the bribed party behaves as instructed; ii) effective bribing , where bribes are conditional on the attack’s success, w.r.t. well-defined metrics. We analyze each type of attack in a game theoretic setting and identify relevant equilibria. In guided bribing, we show that the protocol is not an equilibrium and then describe good equilibria, where the attack is unsuccessful, and a negative one, where all parties are bribed such that the attack succeeds. In effective bribing, we show that both the protocol and the “all bribed” setting are equilibria. Using the identified equilibria, we then compute bounds on the Prices of Stability and Anarchy. Our results indicate that additional mitigations are needed for guided bribing, so our analysis concludes with incentive-based mitigation techniques, namely slashing and dilution. Here, we present two positive results, that both render the protocol an equilibrium and achieve maximal welfare for all parties, and a negative result, wherein an attack becomes more plausible if it severely affects the ledger’s token’s market price.

Introduction

Bitcoin’s
[
44
]
paradigm for creating
decentralized databases, which combined a hash chain and a
Proof-of-Work (PoW) mechanism, enabled a solution to the consensus problem in a
setting with open participation. In exchange for maintaining the database,
participants are awarded tokens that can be sold on the open market.

Bitcoin’s protocol has been explored and expanded with a plethora of research
ideas and implementations, forming an ecosystem of distributed ledger systems.
Among them, Proof-of-Stake (PoS) systems feature prominently, mainly due to
their low environmental cost
[
59
]
. As opposed to PoW,
where participation requires consuming energy to solve moderately hard
puzzles, PoS relies on the amount of tokens that a party has. Therefore, to
participate in a PoS ledger, a party needs only to acquire and hold the
system’s tokens for a time period.

Typically, in order to argue about a ledger’s security, researchers take two
approaches.

The “cryptographic” approach assumes that a fraction of parties
is
honest
and follows the prescribed protocol faithfully, while the rest
are
Byzantine
and can deviate arbitrarily. These works show
that Bitcoin-like ledgers are secure under honest
majority
[
19
,
49
]
.

The “game-theoretic” approach assumes that all parties are
rational
,
aiming to maximize a well-defined utility function. Because distributed ledgers
typically operate in a completely open manner, without any restriction or
validation of the participants’ identities, game theoretic guarantees are
particularly useful. As such, an active line of research tries to prove that
ledgers, such as Bitcoin, are game theoretically compatible, e.g., being Nash
Equilibria
[
17
,
51
,
34
]
.

A well-known, incentive-oriented, threat to blockchain systems is

bribery
attacks
[
2
]
. Here, an adversary
\mathcal{A}

wants to disrupt the distributed ledger
via a bribing budget, which is allocated at will as bribes to protocol participants. When a party

\mathcal{P}
accepts a bribe,
\mathcal{A}
instructs
\mathcal{P}
to deviate from the
ledger protocol in a well-defined manner, i.e., perform an
infraction.
1
1

1

The details of the deviation are known to
\mathcal{P}

before choosing to get bribed.
If enough parties
accept the bribe,
\mathcal{A}
can successfully violate one of the ledger’s
properties. Moreover, if the attack is visible and the market treats it as severe,
then it can result in a reduction of the market price
(exchange rate) of the ledger’s native token. So, whether
\mathcal{P}
accepts the
bribe depends on its rationality: if
\mathcal{P}
’s utility taking everything into account increases via bribing,
compared to acting honestly, the rational choice for
\mathcal{P}
is to accept the
bribe.

Our work focuses on attacks against blockchain safety
(cf.
Section

3.3
for definitions). Such attacks are particularly
devastating, since they violate one of the two core ledger security properties
and enable real world attacks such as double spending, that has resulted in millions
in losses
[
45
,
62
]
.
Safety is also notably vulnerable during a system’s bootstrapping
period
[
50
]
, when it has not yet attracted enough parties to
safeguard it against malicious takeovers. During this period,
the participants of another ledger may attempt to attack it in order to
discredit it and claim its market share, as was observed during the infamous
Bitcoin Cash split
[
48
]
.
Therefore, an adversary could be motivated to break safety, and use bribing as
a means to succeed, for various reasons.

Several works demonstrate bribing
mechanisms
[
26
,
39
,
41
,
43
,
11
,
33
,
58
,
56
]
.
In practice, identifying whether a bribery occurred is
difficult, especially if payouts occur off-chain. Therefore, showcasing that an
incentive mechanism is resilient to bribing is a powerful tool for designing
robust protocols. This becomes more pressing in the case of PoS, where
the system does not rely on external hardware assumptions, but instead depends
on how its tokens are distributed among parties.

Because some protocol deviations are identifiable, a vulnerable protocol could be enhanced with defenses that
disincentivize bribing that leads to such identifiable attacks. One such
proposed mitigation in PoS ledgers is to hold parties
financially accountable
.
Specifically, parties are required to lock assets in
escrow before participating in the ledger protocol. If a party
misbehaves in an accountable manner, then its deposit gets
slashed
,
meaning it is confiscated. Otherwise, the party can withdraw its deposit at the
end of its participation. An alternative approach is to
dilute
a misbehaving
party’s assets by excluding it from receiving newly-issued tokens, thus
avoiding the (technical and legal) complications of locking assets. The latter approach is
taken by Cardano
[
28
]
to disincentivize abstaining, whereas
Cosmos employs a combination of slashing and
dilution
[
63
,
13
]
.
Nonetheless, both techniques have drawbacks, as slashing requires
large amounts of assets to be locked, while dilution reduces
the asset value of regular, non-participating users.
Therefore, identifying the exact cases when introducing a defense
and analyzing how effective, and under which conditions, such defenses are, is
vital for balancing the various tradeoffs.

Analysis Outline and Contributions

Our work explores the efficiency of bribing attacks and defense mechanisms in PoS systems from
a game-theoretic perspective. Note that balancing between the benefits of
accepting a bribe and the damage caused by suffering a penalty inherently
presumes rationality of the protocol participant, which renders a
game-theoretic model a natural choice. Our analysis identifies both good and
bad equilibria, depending on whether bribing leads to an attack or not. As a
result, we identify when a protocol can handle bribing on its own and when a
defense mechanism is needed, and what guarantees such defense provides.

In more detail, our approach is as follows.

First, we define the formal model of our setting (Section
4
).
We consider the relevant infraction predicate,
which captures the behavior of the parties that get bribed
and participate in the attack, e.g., by double
signing to create conflicting ledgers.
We also define the exchange rate’s behavior w.r.t. a bribing
attack. We assume that the exchange rate remains constant, if no attack is
successful, but is reduced if a successful attack is observed.
This behavior aims to capture the market’s response to such an attack.
Specifically, a bribing attack can violate one of the system’s core properties,
resulting in users losing their trust in the system, or even the system
becoming unusable. Consequently, it is expected that the market price of the
system’s token would be decreased, as its usability evaporates.

Next, we define and analyze two modes of bribing.
In the
guided
mode (Section
5
), the adversary pays
the bribes only as long as a party performs an infraction, regardless if the attack is
successful.
In the
effective
mode (Section
6
), the bribes
are paid if the attack is successful, i.e., if the exchange rate is reduced.
In both cases, the adversary instructs the bribed parties to act in a specific
manner.

First, we explore whether the extreme scenarios, where all parties either
follow the protocol or accept the bribes, are Nash Equilibria or compliant
strategies (cf. Section
3
). In guided bribing, the
protocol is not an equilibrium, whereas the “all bribed” setting is. In
effective bribing, both of these extreme scenarios are equilibria.

Second, we explore mixed scenarios, where some parties are bribed and others
behave honestly. In guided bribing, we show a family of equilibria that behave
as such and are all positive, in the sense that an attack is
unsuccessful. In effective bribing, no such mixed scenarios were identified.

Next, we analyze the aggregate utility of all
parties (“welfare”). For each setting, we identify bounds of welfare across
equilibria and then evaluate the Prices of Stability and
Anarchy (cf. Section
3.6
).

Finally, Section
7
explores counterincentives for
guided bribing. We identify two positive equilibria, each applicable to a
different set of realistic assumptions, and show that both yield
maximal welfare to parties. This result suggests that countermeasures (slashing or dilution)
can help deterring guided bribing attacks and help a
protocol become an equilibrium. Nonetheless, we also present a negative
equilibrium, where all parties are incentivized to accept a bribe. Notably, the
ability of an attacker to maintain this equilibrium depends on the attack’s
effect on the system’s exchange rate: the more destructive an
attack on the exchange rate, the lower the cost for the attacker to maintain the negative equilibrium becomes.

To make reading more effortless, we refer to Appendix
A
for all
proofs.

Related work

Bribing

Bribing attacks were first described in a peer-reviewed work by
Bonneau
[
2
]
, who offered various bribing mechanisms
and discussed potential implications. At the same time, it was shown
that such attacks can be exacerbated when miners are paid only via transaction
fees and not fixed block rewards
[
9
]
.
The first practical example was implemented on
Ethereum
[
43
]
, via smart contracts that enable
bribing both “in-bound”, i.e., denominated in the token of the attacked
system, and “out-of-band”, i.e., using a separate cryptocurrency system;
interestingly, this payout scheme enables both bribing modes explored in our work
(guided and effective).
Followup works increased the efficiency of bribing attacks, by
enabling double-spend collusion across different systems in a trustless
manner
[
26
]
, or utilized smart contracts for
payouts
[
11
]
.
Such works are complementary to ours, since we analyze player dynamics assuming
the existence of a bribing mechanism.

For a detailed research overview of PoW bribing attacks
we refer to
[
27
]
. Although we
focus on Proof-of-Stake (PoS) systems,
[
27
]
offers a broad understanding of
incentive manipulation and presents motivations for bribing attacks, e.g., double-spending
[
2
,
40
,
16
]
,
breaking consensus
[
3
,
38
,
39
]
,
censorship
[
60
]
, frontrunning
[
14
]
, or breaking Hashed Time Lock Contracts
[
33
]
.

We note that our work focuses on bribing as a means to perform safety
attacks. Although the literature on blockchain bribing primarily considers
arguably less destructive attacks, like transaction ordering manipulation or
temporary censorship, there do exist various works that explore bribing for
safety
violations
[
56
,
39
,
43
]
.

More recently, a model for bribery attacks was proposed
in
[
54
]
. This work explores attacks against longest-chain
blockchains, like Bitcoin, and assumes some miners behave honestly, whereas
others act rationally, accepting the bribe if it increases their (short-term)
utility. In addition, this work assumes in-bound bribes, i.e., which are paid via
on-chain transactions in the attacked system’s native tokens. In contrast, our
model is more general, since we assume that:

i)

all parties act rationally, without requiring some to always act honestly;

ii)

bribes are paid out-of-band, e.g., in USD, thus are not affected by
fluctuations on the blockchain’s token price (which might occur as a
result of the bribe).

Accountability and Slashing

The existence of protocol violations and attacks, such as bribing, has
highlighted the importance of
accountability
: the ability to identify,
and possibly penalize, offending parties. Accountability has been a topic of
discussion in distributed systems for many
years
[
23
,
24
]
.

In the context of BFT protocols and blockchains, a seminal
work
[
53
]
formalized the guarantees of various protocols in
terms of forensic support, i.e., identifying malicious parties in a distributed
manner. Previously, accountability was notably discussed in Casper
FFG
[
6
]
, Ethereum’s PoS proposed protocol.
Gasper
[
7
]
, a Casper variant, arguably achieved
accountable safety under a
\frac{1}{3}
adversarial
power
[
8
]
. However, a liveness attack against Gasper was
later identified
[
47
]
. That work highlighted
the availability-accountability dilemma
[
46
]
where, in an
environment with dynamic participation, no protocol can be both accountably
safe and live.
Other works explored accountability for alternative
blockchain systems, such as rollups
[
55
]
and permissioned
protocols
[
21
]
.

Finally, Kannan and Deb
[
15
]
outline a slashing analysis that
considers bribing. They argue that relying on
the market’s response and a reduction of the token’s price is insufficient
and, in the absence of such response, all parties are incentivized to accept
bribes. Our work expands this idea, by defining a formal model and
conducting a rigorous game theoretic analysis. Interestingly, our results
agree partially to the intuitive arguments of that work. Specifically, we show
that if all parties are bribed, then indeed a party is not incentivized to
unilaterally be honest. However, we also show that, under effective bribing or
under slashing, the protocol is an equilibrium, so no party is unilaterally
incentivized to get bribed, if all others are honest. Finally, the results of
Section
7
also give support to the idea that slashing is a
helpful mitigation to (guided) bribing attacks.

3.3
Distributed Ledger

Next we describe an abstraction of a distributed ledger protocol
\Pi
,
with the goal of capturing the core elements of real-world
protocols like Algorand
[
10
]
, HotStuff
[

]
,

Blocks and chains

The protocol-related messages are blocks. A block
B
contains
transactions with
application-specific information and a hash-based pointer
s
to another block.
Therefore, blocks form a tree, where each block defines a single parent and
many blocks might define the same parent, i.e., creating a fork. A single branch
of the tree is a chain
C
, with all chains starting from the same block,

B_{G}
. A ledger
L
is an array of transactions
[\mathsf{tx}_{1},\mathsf{tx}_{2},\dots]
; the length of
L
(denoted by
|L|
) is the number of
transactions in it.
L
can be extracted from a blockchain
C
by
appending the transactions in
C

the blocks.

Validity predicate

The predicate
V
defines if a block can be
appended to a chain. Given chain
C
and block
B

V
outputs
1
if
C||B

(the concatenation of
C
and
B
) is acceptable w.r.t. the protocol’s
application rules. An implementation of

should
ensure e.g., that no double-spending is possible, no assets can be forged, etc.

Parties

Each party
\mathcal{P}
that participates in
\Pi
is identifiable by a public
key
\mathsf{pk}_{\mathcal{P}}
and has some protocol power
\mu_{\mathcal{P}}
.
6
6

6

Depending on the implementation,

Each party keeps a local chain of blocks

All parties have access to function

For a given slot

7

Remark.

We assume that
a party participates in consensus at a frequency which is a linear function
of its (proportional) power.
In practice, for the real world systems explored in this work, it holds that

In particular, parties cannot participate at a higher rate by splitting their
power across multiple, seemingly independent entities (sybil behavior), or by forming
collective entities.

Security properties

A distributed ledger should guarantee two core properties, safety and

1

Definition 1
.

A secure distributed ledger protocol

•

Safety
: Let

\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}

3.5
Approximate Nash Equilibrium

An approximate Nash equilibrium is a common tool for expressing a solution to a
non-cooperative game of
n
parties
\mathcal{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Each party
\mathcal{P}_{i}

employs a strategy
S_{i}
, a set of rules and actions
the party makes depending on what has happened in the game,
i.e., it defines the part of the entire distributed protocol
\Pi
performed
by
\mathcal{P}_{i}
. There exists an “honest” strategy, defined by
\Pi
, which
parties may employ; for ease of notation,

\Pi
denotes both the distributed protocol and the honest strategy.
A
strategy profile
is a vector of all players’ strategies.
Each party
\mathcal{P}_{i}
has a game
utility

U_{i}
, which is a real
function that takes as input a strategy profile. A strategy profile is an

\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium when no party can increase its utility more than

\epsilon
by
unilaterally
changing its strategy
(Definition
2
).

Definition 2
.

Let:

i)

\epsilon
be a non-negative real number;

ii)

\mathbb{S}
be the set of strategies a party may employ;

iii)

\sigma^{*}=(S^{*}_{i},S^{*}_{-i})
be a strategy
profile of
\mathbb{P}
, where
S^{*}_{i}
is the strategy
followed by
\mathcal{P}_{i}
;

iv)

S^{*}_{-i}
denote the
n-1
strategies
employed by all parties except
\mathcal{P}_{i}
.

We say that
\sigma^{*}
is an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium
w.r.t. a
utility vector
\bar{U}=\langle U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\rangle
if:

\forall\mathcal{P}_{i}\in\mathbb{P}\;\forall S_{i}\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\{S^{*}_{i}\}:U_{i}(S^{*}_{i},S^{*}_{-i})\geq U_{i}(S_{i},S^{*}_{-i})-\epsilon
.

For simplicity, when all parties have the same utility
U
,
\sigma^{*}
is an

\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium w.r.t.
U
. Also,
\Pi
is an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium w.r.t.

when
\sigma_{\Pi}=\langle\Pi,\ldots,\Pi\rangle
, s.t. all parties follow the honest strategy.

3.6
Welfare and Prices of { Stability, Anarchy }

An important consideration in our analysis is a system’s
welfare
,
a measure of efficiency of a strategy profile.
We define the welfare of a strategy profile as the sum of (expected) utilities
of all parties under said profile:

\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)=\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma)\;,

where
U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma)

is the utility of party
\mathcal{P}
under strategy profile
\sigma
.

Two relevant notions are
Price of Stability
and
Price of Anarchy

[
37
]
, denoted by
\mathsf{PSt}
and
\mathsf{PAn}
respectively.

\mathsf{PSt}
expresses how
close an equilibrium’s welfare is to the maximum achievable welfare. For approximate equilibria,

\mathsf{PSt}
is defined as the ratio of the maximum welfare, across all
strategy profiles, over the maximum welfare achieved across all
\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
-Nash equilibria:

\mathsf{PSt}=\frac{\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}{\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{EQ}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}\;,

where
\mathbb{EQ}
is the set of all
\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
-Nash equilibria.

\mathsf{PAn}
expresses the ratio between the maximum achievable welfare and the ’worst’ equilibrium (i.e., the one with the minimum welfare), as formalized below:

\mathsf{PAn}=\frac{\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}{\min_{\sigma\in\mathbb{EQ}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}\;.

In this work, we consider a relaxation of the Price of Anarchy notion, by searching across equilibria where the parties’s strategies are in some subset
\mathbb{T}\subset\mathbb{S}
. In what we call
Price of Anarchy w.r.t.
\mathbb{T}
, denoted by
\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathsf{PAn}
, we assume that there is some level of coordination among the parties, in the sense that they agree to follow only strategies that are included in
\mathbb{T}
. Formally,

\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathsf{PAn}=\frac{\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}{\min_{\sigma\in\mathbb{EQ}\cap\mathbb{T}^{n}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}\;.

Naturally, unless
\mathbb{EQ}\cap\mathbb{T}^{n}=\emptyset
,
1\leq\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathsf{PAn}\leq\mathsf{PAn}
.

3.7
Compliance

Compliance
[
30
]
is another tool based on Nash
dynamics. The core notion in compliance is the
infraction predicate

\mathsf{IP}
, which abstracts a deviant behavior that the analysis
aims to capture. Conversely,
\mathsf{IP}
identifies the

compliant
strategies. Specifically, a strategy
S
is compliant
if for all execution traces when a party follows
S
,
\mathsf{IP}

is
0
.

Using the infraction predicate, the notion of an

(\epsilon,\mathsf{IP})
-compliant protocol
\Pi
is defined.

\epsilon
specifies the gain threshold after which a party
switches strategies. In particular,
\epsilon

specifies when a strategy profile
\sigma^{\prime}
is
directly reachable

from profile
\sigma
, where
\sigma^{\prime}
results from the
unilateral deviation of party
\mathcal{P}_{i}
from
\sigma
and,
by this deviation, the
\mathcal{P}_{i}
’s utility increases by more than
\epsilon
,
while
\sigma^{\prime}
sets a
best response
for
\mathcal{P}_{i}
.
Generally,
\sigma^{\prime}
is
reachable
from
\sigma
, if
\sigma^{\prime}
results
from a “path” of strategy profiles, starting from
\sigma
, which are
sequentially related via direct reachability. Finally, the
cone

of a profile
\sigma
is defined as the set of all strategies that are
reachable from
\sigma
, including
\sigma
itself.

Following these definitions,
\Pi
is
(\epsilon,\mathsf{IP})
-compliant if the cone of the “all honest”
profile
\sigma_{\Pi}
contains only profiles consisting of
\mathsf{IP}\mathsf{-compliant}

strategies. In a compliant protocol, parties may
(unilaterally) deviate from the honest strategy only in a compliant manner,
without violating the predicate
\mathsf{IP}
.

4
Formal Model of Bribing Attacks

In this section, we describe the model, under which the following sections will
explore bribing attacks. Briefly, we assume that an adversary offers bribes to
parties in order to perform infractions that lead to particularly harmful
attacks. Following, we first define the network setting and then discuss the
relation between infractions and attacks and offer examples of both. Next, we
describe the behavior of the exchange rate, that is the ledger’s token price,
in the presence of such attacks. Finally, we put restrictions on the adversary,
in terms of the amount of funds it can allocate for bribes.

Before we proceed, let us outline a list of assumptions that hold for
the protocols that we analyze. Assuming that all parties act honestly, i.e., follow the prescribed protocol, the following conditions should hold:

i)

the block rewards that are given by the system as a whole are
maximized;

ii)

exactly one block is created for every execution slot;

iii)

the random variable
M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}
, of ledger-related messages
that a party
\mathcal{P}
creates during the execution, respects the Chernoff concentration bounds. Namely, for any
\theta\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle{\Pr\big[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}\geq(1+\theta)\cdot E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\big]}

\displaystyle{\leq e^{-\frac{\theta^{2}}{3}\cdot E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]}}

\displaystyle{\Pr\big[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}\leq(1-\theta)\cdot E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\big]}

\displaystyle{\leq e^{-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\cdot E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]}}

4.1
Network

In this work, we assume a
synchronous
network. Specifically, the router

\mathcal{R}_{\text{sync}}
delivers all messages at the end of the round during
which they were created.

We note that various protocols have been analyzed and shown secure in
the partially synchronous setting. Here, “Global Stabilization
Time” (GST) exists, unknown to the protocol’s designers and participants.
Before GST, the router
\mathcal{R}_{\text{psync}}
can introduce arbitrary delays to
all messages. After GST,
\mathcal{R}_{\text{psync}}
has to behave as the
synchronous router. In our setting, since
\mathcal{A}
controls the router, it
can arbitrarily delay all messages before GST. Consequently, it can violate
liveness, by preventing all blocks before GST from being finalized. However,
this means that
\mathcal{A}
can prevent parties from receiving rewards for the
slots before GST. Therefore, the expected rewards that a party receives, and
hence their utility, depends on the GST and the adversarial behavior. In
addition, because the GST is unknown to participants, it is impossible to
estimate even a lower bound on utility. As a result, a game theoretic analysis,
where parties compute and compare the expected utility of each available
strategy, is not possible assuming a partially synchronous network.

4.2
Infraction Predicates

As described in Section
3.7
, we follow the model
of
[
30
]
. Briefly, a
deviant behavior is captured by an
infraction predicate

\mathsf{IP}
. For each party
\mathcal{P}
, this predicate is
0
if, for
every execution,
\mathcal{P}
follows the honest protocol.

\mathsf{IP}
is
1
if, at some execution trace,
\mathcal{P}
’s strategy
deviates from the protocol in a well-defined manner.

We define the strategy
S_{\mathsf{IP}}
as the one where

\mathcal{P}
performs the infraction defined by
\mathsf{IP}
, whenever
possible, and behaves honestly otherwise. The strategy profile where all
parties perform
\mathsf{IP}
is denoted by

\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}=\langle S_{\mathsf{IP}},\ldots,S_{\mathsf{IP}}\rangle
.
Also
\mathsf{IP}(\Im,\mathcal{P})
denotes the value of infraction predicate
\mathsf{IP}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{P}
for an execution trace
\Im
.

In this work, each predicate is parameterized by a time frame
\Delta
. This
time frame defines when the party is expected to perform an infraction. We
assume that
\Delta
is sufficiently large, s.t. if a party wants to perform the
infraction, it can do so with overwhelming probability. In other words, the
probability that a party cannot perform an infraction even though its strategy
instructs it to do so, is negligible in the security parameter
\kappa
.

Each predicate is associated with a threshold
\alpha
. This threshold
denotes the percentage of power that makes an attack against the ledger
feasible. In practice,
\alpha
sets the power needed for a subset of parties

\mathbb{A}
, s.t. if everyone in

\mathbb{A}
performs the infraction, an attack can occur with
overwhelming probability.

Predicate Examples

The compliance model allows us to capture and analyze well-defined deviations.
For instance,
predicates
1
and
2
capture the
behavior that results in violations of safety (double signing) or
liveness (censorship) (cf. Section
3
).

\mathsf{IP}_{\mathsf{ds}}(\Im,\mathcal{P})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{if in $\Im$, }\exists r\in\Delta:\mathcal{P}\mbox{ double-signs in }r\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\;

(1)

\mathsf{IP}_{\mathsf{censor}}(\Im,\mathcal{P})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{if in $\Im$, }\forall r\in\Delta:\mathcal{P}\mbox{ censors }\\
&\mbox{transaction }\mathsf{tx}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\;

(2)

In both cases, the predicate is parameterized by a value
\Delta
. This is the
time window within which the infraction takes place. In one case (double
signing), the party needs to act at least once in this window. In the other
(censorship), it needs to act for the whole duration of
\Delta
.

Attack success probability

As stated above and shown in the examples, predicates are parameterized by a
time frame
\Delta
, within which the attack should conclude.
An attack occurs if, within
\Delta
and for a duration of
rounds (which is an attack parameter), the aggregate power of parties that
perform an infraction is above the threshold
\alpha

(Definition
3
).

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathsf{IP}
be an infraction predicate with threshold

\alpha
.
Also let
\hat{r}
be a time slot and
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda,\Delta
be integers such that
\lambda^{\prime}\leq\lambda\leq\Delta
.
An
attack associated with
\mathsf{IP}
occurs
, if the following event occurs:

\displaystyle\exists\mathbb{A}\subseteq\mathbb{P}\mbox{ such that }\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\alpha:

\displaystyle\big[\exists r^{*}\geq\hat{r}:\forall r\in[r^{*},r^{*}+\lambda):\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}}\mu_{\mathbb{C},\mathcal{P}}\geq\alpha\big]\land

\displaystyle\big[\exists r^{\prime}\in[r^{*},r^{*}+\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}):\forall r\in[r^{\prime},r^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime}):\Lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{A}\big]

where
\mathbb{A}
is the set of parties that violate
\mathsf{IP}
,

\mu_{\mathbb{C},\mathcal{P}}
is the power percentage of
\mathcal{P}
within the elected committee
\mathbb{C}
,
and
\Lambda_{r}
is the leader of time slot
r
.

Note that
\lambda
is the parameter of the security property which is
violated when the infraction is performed. For example, for safety attacks it holds

\lambda=1
, since it suffices to perform a successful double signing for one
round. For liveness attacks,
\lambda
is the liveness parameter, i.e., the
number of rounds that a party waits until finalizing a published transaction.

\lambda^{\prime}
is the number of rounds for which the leader should be
adversarial. For example, for safety attacks
\lambda^{\prime}=1
. For liveness
attacks censorship is successful if
enough committee members refuse to sign blocks that contain the censored
transaction
\mathsf{tx}
, regardless of the leader’s actions, so
\lambda^{\prime}=0
.

In the rest of the paper, we assume
\hat{r},\Delta,\lambda^{\prime},\lambda
are chosen in such a manner that the event of
Definition
3
occurs with overwhelming probability.
Therefore, an attack is successful as long as enough parties, i.e., with
aggregate power above the given threshold, are willing to perform the
infraction.

4.3
Exchange Rate

We now describe how the exchange rate behaves in the presence of attacks.
In detail, assume that the parties in
\mathbb{P}
follow a strategy profile

\sigma
. Let
\mathsf{IP}
be an infraction predicate.

\mathcal{A}
instructs all parties that accept a bribe to violate

\mathsf{IP}
. Also let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be the security
threshold, i.e., the percentage of aggregate power that bribed parties need to
control, in order to perform an attack based on
\mathsf{IP}
.

The exchange rate is defined as follows:

X_{\sigma}(\Im)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{\mathsf{max}},&\mbox{if in $\Im$}:\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}:\mathsf{IP}(\Im,\mathcal{P})=1}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha\\
x_{\mathsf{min}},&\mbox{if in $\Im$}:\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}:\mathsf{IP}(\Im,\mathcal{P})=1}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\alpha\end{array}\right.\;

(3)

The values
x_{\mathsf{max}},x_{\mathsf{min}}
express the “toxicity” effect of the attack.
In particular, the difference
x=x_{\mathsf{max}}-x_{\mathsf{min}}
states the
level by which the exchange rate drops, in case of a successful attack. In the
extreme case, when the attack is catastrophic for the system, the ledger’s
token price goes to
0
(i.e.,
x_{\mathsf{min}}=0
).

We note that assuming that the market responds to an attack, s.t. the exchange
rate drops, is not always realistic. For example, if the attack is not visible
and provable, e.g., in the case of censorship attacks
[
57
]
, the
exchange rate may remain unaffected. Similarly, even if the attack is visible, but the market does not deem it
as severe,
x
might be small.
9
9

9

Historical examples of
such attacks are described in
[
30
, Section 7.3]
.

In our work, such behavior of the exchange rate affects the results where the
bound depends on
x_{\mathsf{min}}
. Typically, if
x_{\mathsf{min}}
is close to
x_{\mathsf{max}}
,
then the attack may require smaller budget, since the attacker would not need
to compensate the bribed parties for potential losses due to the price drop.

Remark.
Our work follows the approach where the
exchange rate takes one of two constant values, depending on the attackers’ power
and
\mathsf{IP}
. This enables a concrete
analysis of the studied protocol and provides evidence of its behavior against
said attack. In reality, the exchange rate may be influenced by various factors. However, to
argue concretely about an attack’s efficacy in the general setting, some
level of simplification is necessary. We capture this by bounding the
impact on the exchange rate drop across all traces. Intuitively,

x_{\mathsf{max}}
and
x_{\mathsf{min}}
act as the
bounds of the exchange rate when the attack is infeasible (
x_{\mathsf{max}}
) and when it is successful (
x_{\mathsf{min}}
).

4.4
Bribing Budget

It is easy to see that, if the adversary
\mathcal{A}
has unlimited funds for
bribes, then it can break the security of any system of rational
participants. Specifically,
\mathcal{A}
would offer every party

\mathcal{P}
10
10

10

More precisely, to enough parties that have an aggregate power above
\alpha
.

a bribe which is
\epsilon
larger than the expected rewards that the system
offers
\mathcal{P}
, when
\mathcal{P}
acts honestly. In that case,
\mathcal{P}
has an
incentive to accept the bribe and increase its utility by
\epsilon
.
Therefore, it is necessary to restrict the total amount of funds that
\mathcal{A}

can offer in bribes.

We assume that the adversary
\mathcal{A}
has a budget
\mathsf{B}

for bribes. In particular, let
\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}

denote an allocation of bribes to all parties. It should hold that

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\beta_{\mathcal{P}}=\mathsf{B}
and
\forall\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}:\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\geq 0
.

\mathcal{A}
allocates this budget arbitrarily,
without restriction on the number and the level of bribes
offered to each party.

We note that bribes are paid in the base unit of account, e.g., USD.
If a party accepts a bribe, the receivable amount does not depend on
exchange rate fluctuations, which might occur due to the attack’s success.
The exact details of how bribing takes place and how the bribes
are distributed are outside the scope of this paper.

There are various candidate bounds for the bribing budget. One could be the
aggregate amount of rewards that the system gives to all parties.
Another could be the aggregate amount of rewards for
parties that collectively control more than
\alpha
of power. In both
cases though, in various scenarios
\mathcal{A}
can trivially allocate bribes
in a way that incentivizes enough parties to deviate.
11
11

11

\mathcal{A}

would give bribes equal to each party’s expected rewards. If
x_{\mathsf{min}}>0
,
then each party would be incentivized to accept the bribe and also collect the
(reduced) rewards.

Our work assumes a tighter bribing budget. For an attack which
results from an infraction
\mathsf{IP}
, consider the following:

•

\alpha\in[0,1]
is the threshold of power proportion that parties need to control, in order to
perform
\mathsf{IP}
s.t. the attack succeeds;

•

x>0
is the difference of the exchange rate, as a result
of a successful attack;

•

N
is the total number of rounds;

•

R
is the (average) reward given per round;

•

\mathcal{S}
is the total stake in the system.

\mathcal{A}
’s bribing budget for this attack is:

\mathsf{B}_{\mathsf{IP}}\leq\alpha\cdot x\cdot(N\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}}).

Intuitively, the budget cannot exceed a percentage
\alpha
of the value of rewards and stake
that is lost as a result of the attack. Observe that, in order to offer
positive results for all reward allocations, this bound is tight. If we allowed
a larger budget,
\mathcal{A}
could trivially perform an attack in the case
when rewards are allocated to each party exactly proportionally to their power.
Specifically,
\mathcal{A}
would give to
\alpha
% of parties a bribe
equal to their expected lost rewards and stake, plus some
\epsilon
value.

We note that this restriction is useful for particularly devastating attacks.
If the bribing budget is high, a positive result shows that the system can
withstand attacks by richer adversaries. However, if the budget is low, a
positive result is weaker, since it holds only for small (less wealthy)
adversaries. Therefore,
if an attack is not particularly
destructive to the exchange rate, then real-world attackers can violate the
budget restriction assumption more easily.
This bound applies to the next two sections; in Section
7

we will revisit it to take into account counterincentives, such as slashing.

In this section, we assume that a party receives its external rewards, i.e., a
bribe, as long as it performs a specific infraction. The bribe
is issued regardless if the attack, which depends on the infraction, is
successful (s.t. the exchange rate drops to
x_{\mathsf{min}}
).
Formally, for each party
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}
, the bribe
\beta_{\mathcal{P}}
is a
non-negative real value. For some infraction predicate
\mathsf{IP}
,
the external rewards are defined as follows:

B_{\mathcal{P},\sigma}(\Im)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\beta_{\mathcal{P}},&\mbox{if }\mathsf{IP}(\Im,\mathcal{P})=1\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(4)

We call the scenario of Eq. (
4
)
guided bribing
w.r.t.
\mathsf{IP}
.

5.1
Equilibria and Compliance

The first outcome of our analysis is Theorem
1
(proof in Appendix
A.1.1
), which offers a
variety of results. In particular, the theorem assumes guided bribing w.r.t.
\mathsf{IP}
, a synchronous network,
block-proportional rewards with
R
rewards per block, and the

Reward
utility function (cf. Section
3
).

First, we show that
\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}
is a

\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
-Nash equilibrium, if no party holds more than

1-\alpha
of the participation power. The main idea is
that, if all parties deviate, no single party can prevent the attack, so
each party’s utility only increases by accepting the bribe.

Second,
\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}
is a
\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
-Nash
equilibrium if a party
\mathcal{P}
holds at least
\alpha
of
the participation power and its bribe is sufficiently large. The idea is
similar to the first result. If
\mathcal{P}
can unilaterally perform the attack
and is incentivized to do so, the other parties can only increase
their utility by getting bribed. In order for this condition to hold,

\mathcal{P}
’s bribe should substantially exceed its expected reward loss, that is
the number of blocks that it creates multiplied by
R\cdot x
.

Third, the protocol
\Pi
is not
\mathsf{IP}
-compliant, if there
exists a party
\mathcal{P}
whose participation power is less than
\alpha
and
its bribe is a positive value. The main idea here is that
\mathcal{P}
cannot
perform the attack on its own. Therefore, assuming all other parties follow

\Pi

5.3
Price of { Stability, Anarchy }

We now explore the Price of Stability
(
\mathsf{PSt}
) and the Price of Anarchy (
\mathsf{PAn}
).
These notions consider welfare loss for all parties in
two settings. First, a decentralized setting, where each party acts following their own
incentives. Second, a centrally-planned setting, where an authority chooses
each party’s strategy, aiming to maximize welfare. Intuitively,

\mathsf{PSt}
expresses the minimum such loss, which results from the best
equilibrium w.r.t. welfare, whereas
\mathsf{PAn}
expresses the maximum
loss, i.e., for the worst equilibrium. Our results for guided
bribing are formalized in two theorems.

First, Theorem
3
(proof in Appendix
A.1.3
) offers bounds for
\mathsf{PSt}
.
Intuitively, consider the case when there exists some initial coordination
between all parties. For example, the parties may initially agree to follow the
protocol and then, after the system is deployed, act rationally.
12
12

12

We
remind that the all-honest strategy profile is not an equilibrium, so
some parties are expected to deviate after the system’s
deployment.

Assuming some such initial coordination, the upper bound on
\mathsf{PSt}

expresses the minimum opportunity cost (in terms of welfare), that the parties
will suffer, i.e., if they end up in the most favorable equilibrium.
Similarly, the lower bound shows the opportunity cost that the parties will
necessarily suffer, at all times, even if they end up in the best equilibrium.
Note that both bounds are proportional to
\alpha
and

1-\frac{x_{\mathsf{min}}}{x_{\mathsf{max}}}
, so that the larger these values are, the higher both
bounds become (i.e., the Price of Stability increases).

6
Effective Bribing

We now study a stricter bribing setting, which we call
“effective”. As with guided bribing, a party needs to perform a specific
infraction, in order to receive its external rewards (bribe). In guided bribing
this was the only condition, so the bribe was paid regardless of the
infraction’s outcome. In effective bribing, the bribe is paid as long as the
infraction contributes to a successful attack. In particular, the party
receives the bribe if the exchange rate drops to
x_{\mathsf{min}}

Under

defined for some infraction predicate
\mathsf{IP}

0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\;

(5)

where

is defined in Eq. (
3

Following, we explore the equilibria formed under effective bribing, as well
as the Price of Stability and Price of Anarchy.

7
Accountable Rewards Under Guided Bribing

Our final contribution is to evaluate possible mitigations against bribing
attacks. In particular, we evaluate mechanisms that aim to protect
against visible and provable attacks, such as double signing, s.t. the protocol
can penalize a party when it observes such attack. Notably, these defenses
cannot protect against attacks when a non-interactive proof of misbehavior
cannot be produced, e.g., censorship attacks.

There are two mechanisms that we consider:

•

slashing
: In this setting, parties are required to lock a certain
amount of assets at the beginning of the execution. If a party
misbehaves in a publicly verifiable manner, that is if a
non-interactive proof of misbehavior can be created, then it forfeits
its deposit.
14
14

14

A non-interactive proof is needed due to the open
nature of such systems. In essence, if a party joins the protocol at a
later point in time, it should be able to validate that past acts of
misbehavior.
Otherwise, if no such proof is shown, the party gets back
its deposit at the end of the execution.

•

dilution
. In this setting, the system mints and distributes
rewards on a regular basis. If a party misbehaves in a publicly
verifiable manner, it is barred from receiving any newly minted
rewards. This has a dilution effect on the party’s cumulative stake in
the system (its stake percentage drops).

Under slashing, we assume that each party’s deposit is proportional to its participation power.

\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<1-\alpha
;
(ii)
\beta_{\mathcal{P}}-\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot G\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}\geq\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
;
(iii)
\beta_{\mathcal{P}}-\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot G\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}\leq\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
.

Then, it holds that
\mathsf{PAn}\geq\frac{({\mathcal{S}}+N\cdot R+G)\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}}{({\mathcal{S}}+N\cdot R)\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}+\mathsf{B}}-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
.

8
Conclusion

Our work explores if an adversary with
a limited budget can
attack a PoS blockchain system via bribes, s.t. the market price of the
ledger’s native token is affected. We
investigate two types of bribing attacks.

In guided bribing (Section
5
), a party receives the bribe
only by acting as instructed.
We show that the protocol is not an equilibrium, as
some parties are
always incentivized to accept the bribe, whereas the setting where all
parties get bribed is an equilibrium (Theorem
1
).
We then identify sufficient conditions for an equilibrium’s existence and bound the
Prices of Stability and Anarchy. Our results suggest that a protocol cannot
defend such attacks without special mechanisms
(which are explored later in Section
7
).

In effective bribing (Section
6
), a party receives the
bribe only if the attack is successful. Here,
both extreme scenarios, where (i) all parties follow the protocol or (ii) all
parties get bribed, are Nash equilibria.
Therefore, our results suggest that if a system reaches the positive
equilibrium, this can be maintained without any further defense mechanisms,
albeit a negative equilibrium is still possible.

Finally, we analyze the effect of financial penalization, like slashing or dilution, against
guided bribing (Section
7
). We identify two sets of
conditions that lead to positive equilibria, where parties achieve
maximal welfare (Price of Stability is
1
). These conditions assume an upper
bound on the bribing budget which depends on the aggregate
deposited amount. As Table
1
shows,
violating this assumption in many real world systems
requires a bribing budget of multiple millions or billions of USD.
However, we again identify a negative
equilibrium, which can be maintained with an attacking budget that is inversely
proportional to the attack’s severity on the system’s exchange rate.
Consequently, albeit defenses like slashing and dilution do help turn the
protocol into an equilibrium, they are not all-inclusive solutions since
negative equilibria can still manifest.

Our work also poses various open questions for future work.

First, we assume the adversary only wants to break safety.
However, bribes could be used to target liveness. A bribed party
could try to censor a transaction by not proposing or signing blocks that
include it. In this case, abstaining from participation or failing to create a
block, which is a liveness violation, results in lost rewards, so the
expected block rewards, hence the utility, of a party is affected by the
nature of the infraction. Analyzing such liveness attacks is particularly
complex and an interesting topic of further exploration.

Second, our results restrict the bribing budget. Although the
bounds are arguably high, they could be insufficient for the real world.
Future work can estimate bounds for deployed systems, e.g., based on
historical market data (to estimate the difference
x_{\mathsf{max}}-x_{\mathsf{min}}
) or deposits in systems with slashing (similar
to Section
7.2
).

Third, we focus on mechanisms that can be naturally explored under the standard game-theoretic model. However, there could exist attack scenarios where the adversary externally incentivizes parties and controls others (e.g., by splitting its budget in bribes and acquiring stake). In this case, some parties can be considered rational while others are Byzantine, and it would be interesting to extend our results under alternative frameworks, such as BART
[
1
]
.

Finally, we consider block-proportional rewards, but alternative
schemes, like resource-proportional
rewards
[
30
]
, have been used in practice
and are worth analyzing. Additionally, taking into account off-protocol incentives, e.g., MEV
[
42
]
, could approximate the real world better.

Following the steps in Eq. (
8
), we can show that

U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}})={\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}}+E[R_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}}]\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}+\beta_{\mathcal{P}}-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
.
The latter, along with Eq. (
10
), implies that

U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma)\leq U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}})+\mathsf{negl}(\kappa).

Therefore,
\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}
is an
\epsilon
-Nash
equilibrium w.r.t. Reward under
\mathcal{R}_{\text{sync}}
, for some

\epsilon=\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
.

(
3
)
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a party with
\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha
and

\beta_{\mathcal{P}}>0
. For every unilateral deviation of
\mathcal{P}
from

\sigma_{\Pi}
, since
\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha
and the other parties
follow the protocol
\Pi
honestly, it holds that the exchange rate
remains always
x_{\mathsf{max}}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{P}
’s stake is
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}
.

Assume that
\mathcal{P}
unilaterally deviates from
\sigma_{\Pi}
by
following
S_{\mathsf{IP}}
. Let
\sigma^{*}
be the
resulting strategy profile, where all parties are honest except from

\mathcal{P}
that follows
S_{\mathsf{IP}}
.

In any execution trace
\Im
, where
\mathcal{P}
follows
\sigma^{*}
, all
the blocks created by
\mathcal{P}
will be included in the chain of the
observer just as if
\mathcal{P}
was honest. Additionally, if
\mathcal{P}

performs an infraction w.r.t.
\mathsf{IP}
,
\mathcal{P}
will receive its
external rewards. Recall that the event
D
where

\mathcal{P}
performs an infraction when following

S_{\mathsf{IP}}
happens with
1-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)

probability. Thus, given that
\beta_{\mathcal{P}}>0
, we have that, for every
constant
\gamma\in(0,1)
:

\begin{split}&U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma^{*})=\\
=&{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot E[X_{\sigma^{*}}]}+E[R_{\mathcal{P},\sigma^{*}}\cdot X_{\sigma^{*}}]+E[B_{\mathcal{P},\sigma^{*}}]=\\
=&{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}}+E[R_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}]+\sum_{z}z\cdot\Pr[B_{\mathcal{P},\sigma^{*}}=z]\geq\\
\geq&{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}}+E[R_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}]+\sum_{z}z\cdot\Pr[\neg D]\cdot\Pr[B_{\mathcal{P},\sigma^{*}}=z|\neg D]\geq\\
\geq&{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot E[X_{\sigma_{\Pi}}]}+E[R_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}\cdot X_{\sigma_{\Pi}}]+(1-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa))\cdot\beta_{\mathcal{P}}>\\
>&U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma_{\Pi})+\gamma\cdot\beta_{\mathcal{P}}.\end{split}

(11)

Next, we show that for any unilateral deviation
\sigma
of
\mathcal{P}
from
the all-honest profile, if
\sigma
is
\mathsf{IP}
-compliant
(i.e.,
\mathcal{P}
deviates from
\sigma_{\Pi}
but never performs an
infraction w.r.t.
\mathsf{IP}
), it holds that

U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma)\leq U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma_{\Pi})
.
The latter holds because:

(i)

stake is the same in both profiles, since the exchange rate is not affected;

(ii)

the expected ledger rewards are maximized in
\sigma_{\Pi}
,
i.e.,
E[R_{\mathcal{P},\sigma}]\leq E[R_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]
;

(iii)

since
\mathsf{IP}
-compliant, the external rewards of

\mathcal{P}
in
\sigma
are always
0
.

Consequently, if there is a best response for
\mathcal{P}
with strictly
greater utility than
\sigma_{\Pi}
, then it must be not

\mathsf{IP}
-compliant.

By the above, the fact that
\sigma^{*}
is not

\mathsf{IP}
-compliant, and Eq. (
11
), we
conclude that there is a strategy profile
\sigma_{\mathsf{best}}
(not
necessarily
\sigma^{*}
) that:

(i)

is a unilateral deviation from
\sigma_{\Pi}
,

(ii)

for any constant
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma_{\mathsf{best}})>U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma_{\Pi})+\gamma\cdot\beta_{\mathcal{P}}
,

(iii)

sets a best response for
\mathcal{P}
, and

(iv)

not
\mathsf{IP}
-compliant.

So, for every constant
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\sigma_{\mathsf{best}}
is
directly
(\gamma\cdot\beta_{\mathcal{P}})
-reachable from
\sigma_{\Pi}
,
which implies that
\Pi
is not

(\gamma\cdot\beta_{\mathcal{P}},\mathsf{IP})
-compliant
w.r.t. Reward under
\mathcal{R}_{\text{sync}}
.

A.1.2
Maximal Sets and Promising Parties

Lemma

1

Assume the following:

•

\Pi
is a protocol run by parties in
\mathbb{P}
in
N
rounds, with block-proportional rewards (cf.
Subsection
3.4
) with
R

rewards per block;

•

each party’s stake is
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}=\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\mathcal{S}
;

•

\alpha
is a security threshold w.r.t.
\mathsf{IP}
;

•

for every strategy profile
\sigma
:

–

the exchange rate
X_{\sigma}
w.r.t.
\mathsf{IP},\alpha
is defined as in
Eq. (
3
);

–

the external rewards
B_{\mathcal{P},\sigma}

due to guided bribing w.r.t.
\mathsf{IP}
are
defined as in Eq. (
4
);

•

M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}
denotes the number of
blocks created by
\mathcal{P}
in all-honest strategy profile

\sigma_{\Pi}
.

Consider the following two sets of conditions:

1.

(i)
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
;

(ii)
\forall\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}\subseteq\mathbb{P}:\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{B}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\Rightarrow\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\leq\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{B}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]
;

(iii)
\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot(N\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}})\cdot x
.

2.

(i)
\forall\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}:E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]=\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\hat{N}+\frac{N-\hat{N}}{n}
, where
\hat{N}\in[0,N]
;

(ii)
\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\Big(\alpha\cdot\hat{N}+\frac{N-\hat{N}}{n}\Big)\cdot R\cdot x+{\alpha\cdot\mathcal{S}\cdot x}
, where
\hat{N}
as above.

If either the two sets of conditions holds, then there exists a subset
of parties
\mathbb{A}_{m}
that is maximal w.r.t.
\alpha,\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}
(cf. Definition
4
).

Proof.

We first show that if

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha
,
then there exists a subset of parties
\mathbb{A}_{m}
that is
maximal w.r.t.
\alpha,\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}
.
In particular, the following algorithm outputs such a maximal subset:

On input
\big(\alpha,R,x,\{{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}},}\mu_{\mathcal{P}},\beta_{\mathcal{P}},E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\}_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\big)
,
execute the following steps:

1.

Set
\mathbb{A}_{m}\leftarrow\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}:=\{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}:\beta_{\mathcal{P}}>(E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}})\cdot x\}
;

2.

Set
\mathbf{L}
as the list that contains the elements of the multiset
\{\mu_{\mathcal{P}}:\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}\}
sorted in increasing order;

3.

Set
\mathcal{P}^{*}
as the party that corresponds to the first element of
\mathbf{L}
;

4.

If
\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}_{m}\cup\{\mathcal{P}^{*}\}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha
, then
(i) set
\mathbb{A}_{m}\leftarrow\mathbb{A}_{m}\cup\{\mathcal{P}^{*}\}
,
(ii) delete
\mu_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}
from
\mathbf{L}
, and
(iii) go to Step 3.
Else, output
\mathbb{A}_{m}
.

Clearly, by the check in Step 4, the output of the algorithm satisfies Condition
1
of
Definition
4
. We will prove that it also satisfies Condition
2
of
Definition
4
. Namely, by the initialization of

\mathbb{A}_{m}
and
\mathbf{L}
in Steps 2 and 3, we have that for
every party
\mathcal{P}
that is not included in the output of the algorithm,
it holds that
\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\leq(E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}})\cdot x
.
In addition, if there is a party

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{A}_{m}
s.t.
\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}_{m}\cup\{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha
,
then for the party
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{A}_{m}

with the minimum participation power among all parties outside

\mathbb{A}_{m}
, it holds that

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}_{m}\cup\{\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}\}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha
.
By definition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}
should be included in
\mathbb{A}_{m}
at
Step 4, which leads to contradiction. Thus, for every party
\mathcal{P}^{*}

that is not included in the output of the algorithm, it also holds that

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}_{m}\cup\{\mathcal{P}^{*}\}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\alpha
.
So, the output of the algorithm is maximal w.r.t.
\alpha,\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}
.

Now assume that the first set of conditions in the lemma statement holds.
We will show that
\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha
.
For the sake of contradiction, assume that

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\alpha
.
Since
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
(condition 1.(i)), we have that

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\alpha\geq 1-\alpha

and

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}=1-\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\leq 1-\alpha

By condition 1.(ii), the latter implies that

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\leq\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}].

and

{\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}}.

Therefore, we have that

\begin{split}&(N\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}})\cdot x=\\
=&\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\Big)\cdot R\cdot x+{\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot x}=\\
=&\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]+\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\Big)\cdot R\cdot x+\\
&+{\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}+\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\Big)\cdot x}\leq\\
\leq&2\cdot\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\cdot R+{\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}}\Big)\cdot x=\\
=&2\cdot\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\Big(E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\cdot R+{\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}}\Big)\cdot x<\\
<&2\cdot\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\\
\leq&2\cdot\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\;,\end{split}

which contradicts condition 1.(iii).

Now assume that the second set of conditions in the lemma statement holds.
Again, for the sake of contradiction, assume that

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\alpha
.
By condition 2.(i), we have that

\begin{split}&\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\beta_{\mathcal{P}}>\\
>&\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}E[M_{\mathcal{P},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\Big)\cdot R\cdot x+{\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}\Big)\cdot x}=\\
=&\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\Big(\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\hat{N}+\dfrac{N-\hat{N}}{n}\Big)\Big)\cdot R\cdot x+{\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\mathcal{S}\Big)\cdot x}\geq\\
\geq&\Big(\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\hat{N}\Big)+\dfrac{N-\hat{N}}{n}\Big)\cdot R\cdot x+{\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\mathcal{S}\Big)\cdot x}\geq\\
\geq&\Big(\alpha\cdot\hat{N}+\dfrac{N-\hat{N}}{n}\Big)\cdot R\cdot x+{\alpha\cdot\mathcal{S}\cdot x}\;.\end{split}

which contradicts condition 2.(ii).

Therefore, if either of the sets of conditions holds, we have that

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}<\alpha

which implies the existence of a maximal subset w.r.t.
\alpha,\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\}_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}
.

\alpha

w.r.t.
\mathsf{IP},\alpha

3

\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}^{\mathbb{A}}

We distinguish the following cases.

Case 1.
\mathcal{P}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\mathbb{A}

\mathbb{A}

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}\cup\{\mathcal{P}^{*}\}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\alpha\mbox{ and }\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\leq(E[M_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}})\cdot x.

So, for the infraction failure event

\Pr[F_{\mathbb{A}\cup\{\mathcal{P}^{*}\}}]=\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)

\mathcal{P}^{*}

\mathsf{IP}

\sigma

\sigma

\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}^{\mathbb{A}}

\mathbb{A}

every

\mathcal{P}

\sigma

&\quad\cdot\Big(\Pr\Big[({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma})\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}+\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}=z\Big|D\wedge(\bigwedge_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{A}}\neg F_{\mathcal{P}})\Big]+\\
&\quad\quad+\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)\Big)+\\
&+\sum_{z}z\cdot\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)+\\
&+\sum_{z}z\cdot\Pr[\neg D]\cdot\Pr\big[({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma})\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}+0=z\big|\neg D\big]\leq\\
\leq&\sum_{z}z\cdot\big(\Pr[D]-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)\big)\cdot\big(\Pr\big[({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma})\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}+\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}=z\big]+\\
&\quad\quad+\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)\big)+\\
&+(N\cdot R)^{2}\cdot\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)+\\
&+\sum_{z}z\cdot\Pr[\neg D]\cdot\Pr\big[({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma})\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}=z\big]\leq\\
\end{split}

\begin{split}\leq&\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)+\sum_{z}z\cdot\Pr[D]\cdot\Pr\big[({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma})\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}+\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}=z\big]+\\
&+(N\cdot R)^{2}\cdot\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)+\\
&+\sum_{z}z\cdot\Pr[\neg D]\cdot\Pr\big[({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma})\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}=z\big]\leq\\

&+(1-\Pr[D])\cdot\big(({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+E[R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma}])\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}\big)\leq\\
\leq&\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)+\Pr[D]\cdot\big(({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+E[R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma_{\Pi}}])\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}+\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\big)+\\
&+(N\cdot R)^{2}\cdot\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)+\\

=&U_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}(\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}^{\mathbb{A}})+\mathsf{negl}(\kappa).\end{split}

Case 2.
\mathcal{P}^{*}\in\mathbb{A}
:

In this case, the probability that
\mathcal{P}^{*}
performs an infraction when

\sigma
is no more than when following

(by the definition of

). Besides, the ledger rewards of

and the exchange rate

. Therefore, we directly get that

negligible in
\kappa
s.t.
U_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}(\sigma)\leq U_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}(\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}^{\mathbb{A}})+\epsilon
,
i.e.,
\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}^{\mathbb{A}}
is an

\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium w.r.t. Reward under
\mathcal{R}_{\text{sync}}
.

\begin{split}\mathsf{PAn}&=\frac{\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}{\min_{\sigma\in\mathbb{EQ}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}\geq\\
&\geq\frac{\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)}{\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}})}\geq\\
&\geq\frac{(N\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}})\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}}{(N\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}})\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}+\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\beta_{\mathcal{P}}}-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)\geq\\
&\geq\frac{(N\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}})\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}}{(N\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}})\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}+\alpha\cdot(N\cdot R+{\mathcal{S}})\cdot x}-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)=\\
&\geq\frac{1}{\frac{x_{\mathsf{min}}}{x_{\mathsf{max}}}+\alpha\cdot\big(1-\frac{x_{\mathsf{min}}}{x_{\mathsf{max}}}\big)}-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)=\\
&\geq\frac{1}{\alpha+(1-\alpha)\cdot\frac{x_{\mathsf{min}}}{x_{\mathsf{max}}}}-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)\;.\end{split}

To show that
\mathsf{PAn}<\infty
, it suffices to prove that
\min_{\sigma\in\mathbb{EQ}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)>0
. Let
\sigma_{0}
be a strategy
profile s.t.
\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)=0
. We argue that

\sigma_{0}\notin\mathbb{EQ}
. Indeed,
\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)=0
implies
that for every party
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}
:

U_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma_{0})=0
. However,

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}}\beta_{\mathcal{P}}
is non-negligible which implies
that for at least for one party
\mathcal{P}^{*}
,
\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}
is
non-negligible. Thus, if
\mathcal{P}^{*}
unilaterally deviates from
\sigma_{0}

by following
\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}
, she will almost always get
her external rewards, so her gain of utility will be at least

\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
which is a non-negligible value. The
latter implies that
\sigma_{0}
is not a
\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
-Nash
equilibrium, i.e.,
\sigma_{0}\notin\mathbb{EQ}
.

(
2
).

If

, then

\beta_{\mathcal{P}}=\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
, for every
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}
. We argue
that the all-abstain strategy profile
\sigma_{\text{abs}}
is a

\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
-Nash equilibrium w.r.t. Reward under

\mathcal{R}_{\text{sync}}
. In particular, assume a unilateral deviation

\sigma^{*}
caused by some party
\mathcal{P}^{*}
from
\sigma_{\text{abs}}
.
Since
\mu_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}<\alpha
and all other parties abstain from the
execution in
\sigma^{*}
, there will never be a block that will be signed
by a committee with sufficiently large participation power. The latter
implies that the ledger rewards of
\mathcal{P}^{*}
in
\sigma^{*}
are
0
.
Given that
\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}=\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
, we conclude that:

U_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}(\sigma)={\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}}+0+\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)=U_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}(\sigma_{\text{abs}})+\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)\;,

which implies that
\sigma_{\text{abs}}
is a
\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
-Nash

\mathcal{R}_{\text{sync}}
.

Consequently:

\begin{split}&0\leq\min_{\sigma\in\mathbb{EQ}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)\leq\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma_{\text{abs}})={\mathcal{S}\cdot x_{\mathsf{min}}}\end{split}

Given that, as described above,
\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\mathsf{Welf}(\sigma)\geq({\mathcal{S}}+N\cdot R)\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}
,
the bound for Price of Anarchy follows directly:

\mathsf{PAn}\geq\Big(1+\frac{N\cdot R}{\mathcal{S}}\Big)\cdot\frac{x_{\mathsf{max}}}{x_{\mathsf{min}}}

&\quad+G_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma}\cdot X_{\sigma}=z\big|\neg D\big]\leq\\
\leq&\sum_{z}z\cdot\Big(\Pr[D]-\Pr\Big[D\wedge(\bigvee_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\},G}}F_{\mathcal{P}})\Big]\Big)\cdot\\

\end{split}

\begin{split}\leq&\sum_{z}z\cdot\big(\Pr[D]-\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)\big)\cdot\big(\Pr\big[{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot X_{\sigma}}+R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma}\cdot X_{\sigma}+\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}=z\big]+\\

&+\sum_{z}z\cdot\Pr[D]\cdot\Pr\big[{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot X_{\sigma}}+R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma}\cdot X_{\sigma}+\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}=z\big]+\\

\leq&{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}}+E[R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}+\mu_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot G\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}\\
&+\Pr[D]\cdot\big(\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}-({\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}+E[R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma_{\Pi}}])\cdot\\
&\quad\cdot(x_{\mathsf{max}}-x_{\mathsf{max}})-\mu_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot G\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}\big)+\\
&+\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)=\\
=&{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}}+E[R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma_{\Pi}}]\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}+\mu_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot G\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}+\\

=&U_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}(\sigma_{\mathsf{IP}}^{\mathbb{P}_{\text{prom}}^{\{\beta_{\mathcal{P}}\},G}})+\mathsf{negl}(\kappa).\end{split}

Case 2.

S_{\mathsf{IP}}

\mathcal{P}^{*}
are maximized in

and the exchange rate
is always
x_{\mathsf{max}}
. Let

be the event that
\mathcal{P}^{*}

\mathsf{IP}

\begin{split}&U_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}(\sigma)=\\
=&{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot E[X_{\sigma}]}+E[R_{\mathcal{P}^{*},\sigma}\cdot X_{\sigma}]+\Pr[D]\cdot\beta_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}+\\
&+\Pr[\neg D]\cdot\mu_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cdot G\cdot x_{\mathsf{max}}<\\

Therefore, in any case, there is an
