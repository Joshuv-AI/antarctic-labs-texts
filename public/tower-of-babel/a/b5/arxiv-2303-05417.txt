Logarithmic Comparison Theorems
F. J. Castro-Jiménez, D. Mond, L. Narváez-Macarro

Abstract
In this paper we study the comparison between the logarithmic and the meromorphic de Rham complexes along a divisor in a complex manifold. We focus on the case of free divisors, starting with the case of locally quasihomogeneous divisors, and we explain how D-module theory can be used for this comparison.



We dedicate this chapter to the memory of our colleagues Jim Damon and José Luis Vicente Córdoba.

Introduction

We survey a number of results known as
comparison theorems
, along the lines of the comparison theorem of Grothendieck. Grothendieck’s Comparison Theorem states that, for any hypersurface
D
in a complex manifold
X
, the cohomology of the meromorphic de Rham complex with respect to
D\subset X
(i.e.
h^{q}(\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D))
for
0\leq q\leq\dim X
) coincides with
\mathbb{R}^{q}j_{*}\mathbb{C}_{U}
where
j:U:=X\smallsetminus D\rightarrow X
is the inclusion and
\mathbb{C}_{U}
is the constant sheaf
\mathbb{C}
on
U
. In effect, the hypercohomology of this complex is the (topological) cohomology
H^{\bullet}(U;\mathbb{C})
.
If
D
is a normal crossing divisor (NCD), this comparison result was proved by Atiyah and Hodge, and Grothendieck’s proof reduces the general case to the case of a NCD by using Hironaka’s resolution of singularities. When
D
is an arrangement of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the Brieskorn complex
B^{\bullet}
computes the singular cohomology of the complement
U=\mathbb{C}^{n}\smallsetminus D
,
H^{\bullet}(U;\mathbb{C})
; and this is also a comparison result. There is a class of divisors, the so-called
free divisors
, introduced by Kyoji Saito, for which a certain number of comparison results can be proven. We survey these results and show how
\mathcal{D}
–module theory provides a way to deal with them.

The content of the paper is as follows. In Sect.
1.1
we first recall Grothendieck’s comparison theorem (see also Sect.
2.6
), that there is a canonical isomorphism

h^{q}(\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}j_{*}\mathbb{C}_{U}

for
0\leq q\leq\dim X
. If
X
is Stein, then the global morphisms analogous to the previous ones

h^{q}(\Gamma(X,\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D)))\rightarrow H^{q}(U;\mathbb{C})

are isomorphisms. We then recall Brieskorn’s Theorem proving that if
D
is a finite union of hyperplanes in
X=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, with equations
h_{j}=0
, then the cohomology of the complement
H^{q}(X\smallsetminus D;\mathbb{C})
can be computed as the cohomology of the so called Brieskorn complex
B^{\bullet}
, which is the
\mathbb{C}
-subalgebra of the exterior algebra
\Gamma(X,\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D))
generated by the forms
\frac{dh_{j}}{h_{j}}
.

Kyoji Saito (see Sect.
1.3
) introduced the notion of
logarithmic meromorphic form
with respect to a divisor
D
in a complex manifold
X
: a meromorphic form
\omega
has a
logarithmic pole
along the divisor
D
if both
h\omega
and
hd\omega
are holomorphic, where
h
is a local equation for
D
. Note in particular that all of Brieskorn’s forms have logarithmic poles along an arrangement of hyperplanes
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Logarithmic meromorphic forms form an
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-subcomplex of the meromorphic de Rham complex
\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D)
. A natural question asks for the class of divisors
D\subset X
for which the inclusion

\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\log D)\hookrightarrow\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D)

is a quasi-isomorphism (i.e. when the two complexes have the same cohomology). By analogy with Grothendieck’s comparison theorem, if the morphism
\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\log D)\hookrightarrow\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D)
is a quasi-isomorphism we say that the divisor
D
satisfies the
logarithmic comparison theorem
(LCT), or that LCT holds for
D
. Theorem
1.3
states that any
locally quasihomogeneous free divisor

D\subset X
satisfies LCT. In 1977 H. Terao conjectured (see Sect.
1.4
) that LCT always holds for hyperplane arrangements (which are, of course, locally quasihomogeneous), and this conjecture was finally proved by Daniel Bath in
[
2
]
.

A free divisor is
linear
if the module
\fDer(-\log D)
of logarithmic derivations with respect to
D
(as defined by K. Saito in
[
60
]
, see Sect.
1.3
) has a basis of vector fields whose coefficients are linear forms. In Sect.
1.5
we recall a number of results on linear free divisors and in particular the theorem of
[
28
]
, that LCT holds for “reductive” linear free divisors.

In Sect.
§2
we introduce the background in
\mathcal{D}
–module theory that is used in Sect.
§3
. This last section is devoted to state a
\mathcal{D}
–module criterion for the Logarithmic Comparison Theorem for free divisors. First, and following the paper of F.J. Calderón-Moreno
[
9
]
, we consider the sheaf
\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}
of logarithmic differential operators on
X
, with respect to a free divisor
D\subset X
and the logarithmic Spencer complex
{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}(\log D)
which is a locally free resolution of the
\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}
–module
\mathcal{O}_{X}
. By using
[
12
]
one generalizes the construction of this last complex to an arbitrary left
\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}
–module. This constructions are used to prove the main theorem in this context. This theorem, proved by Calderón-Moreno and Narváez-Macarro (
[
12
, Cor. 4.2]
and
[
55

), states that a free divisor
D\subset X
satisfies the Logarithmic Comparison Theorem if and only if the natural morphism

\varrho:\mathcal{D}_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{L}}}{{\otimes}}_{\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}}\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{X}(\star D)

is an isomorphism in the derived category of
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules (see Theorem
3.3
). Fixing a point
p\in D
, a reduced local equation
f
of the germ
(D,p)
and a basis
\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}\}
of
\fDer(-\log D)_{p}
, the morphism
\varrho_{p}
is an isomorphism if and only if the complex

\mathcal{D}_{X,p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{L}}}{{\otimes}}_{\mathcal{V}_{X,p}^{D}}\mathcal{O}_{X,p}(D)
is concentrated in cohomological degree 0,
\mathcal{D}_{X,p}f^{-1}=\mathcal{O}_{X,p}(\star D)
and the
\mathcal{D}_{X,p}
–annihilator of
f^{-1}\in\mathcal{O}_{X,p}(\star D)
equals the left ideal
\mathcal{D}_{X,p}(\delta_{1}+\alpha_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}+\alpha_{n})
, where
\delta_{i}(f)=\alpha_{i}f
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, see Sect.
3.3
. That gives a
\mathcal{D}
–module criterion to test if LCT holds for a given free divisor
D\subset X
. Finally we treat some examples to show how this criterion can be applied in practice.

1.1
Grothendieck’s Comparison Theorem

To calculate the cohomology of a space with constant coefficients
\mathbb{C}
, one can use a resolution of the constant sheaf
\mathbb{C}_{X}
and then calculate the hypercohomology of the resolution. For example, on a complex manifold the holomorphic Poincaré lemma (see e.g.

\textstyle{\mathbb{C}_{X}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\mathcal{O}_{X}=\Omega}^{0}_{X}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d}
\textstyle{{\Omega}^{1}_{X}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d}
\textstyle{\cdots}

(1)

is a resolution of

by locally free
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules. If

is in addition a Stein space – for example, the complement of a divisor

in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, or a convex open set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
– then all of the sheaves in the complex are
acyclic

for
j>0
and

(in effect, this is the definition of ‘Stein space’). In this case the double complex with which one calculates the hypercohomology is reduced to the complex of global sections

0{\ \rightarrow\ }{\Gamma}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}){\ \rightarrow\ }{\Gamma}(X,{\Omega}^{1}_{X}){\ \rightarrow\ }\cdots

and so one has the

(1.1) Theorem.

If

is a Stein manifold then

\textstyle{h^{q}({\Gamma}(X,{\Omega}^{\bullet}_{X}))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{H^{q}(X;\mathbb{C})}

(2)

Here if we view

as the singular cohomology group, then the arrow is given by the integration of differential forms along

. Note that this theorem already implies the non-trivial fact that the cohomology of a Stein manifold vanishes above middle dimension.

If
X
is the complement of a divisor (a hypersurface)

{\Omega}^{k}_{X}
(or more precisely

[
32

This is known as

To end this section, let us emphasize that Grothendieck’s proof of (

This fact brought conferred upon the resolution of singularities in positive characteristic a privileged place in the construction of cohomological theories, to the extent that it appeared as a matter of substance in this context.
On the other hand, Grothendieck’s comparison theorem became a completely new and unexpected way to understand regular singular points (at infinity) of integrable connections on algebraic fiber bundles (see footnote (13) in

[

Finally,

49

[
50

In this paper we would like: (1) to explain what the logarithmic comparison means, drawing some analogies with Grothendieck’s comparison theorem; (2) to survey the original proof of the Logarithmic Comparison Theorem for locally quasihomogeneous free divisors (see Theorem

\mathcal{D}
-module theory provides a characterization of those free divisors which satisfy the Logarithmic Comparison Theorem (see Sect.
3.3
). We emphasise that in all of them resolution of singularities does not play any rôle.

For any topological space

1.2
The Brieskorn Complex

For a special class of divisors, namely hyperplane arrangements in affine space
X=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, Brieskorn, in
[
6
]
generalising Arnol’d in
[
1
]
, had already given a way of calculating the cohomology of the complement. If
D
is the union of hyperplanes
H_{j}
with equations
h_{j}=0
,
j=1,{\ldots},N
, then the collection of meromorphic 1-forms
\frac{dh_{j}}{h_{j}}
generates a
\mathbb{C}
-subalgebra
B^{\bullet}
of the exterior algebra
\Gamma(X,\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D))
. Since
d\left(\frac{dh_{j}}{h_{j}}\right)=0
, all exterior derivatives on
B^{\bullet}
are zero, and
B^{\bullet}
is a subcomplex of the complex
\Gamma(X,\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D))
with derivative zero, known as the
Brieskorn complex
. Brieskorn showed

(1.3) Theorem.

The de Rham morphism
B^{p}{\ \rightarrow\ }H^{p}(U;\mathbb{C})
is an isomorphism.
\Box

Note that each of Brieskorn’s forms

\omega_{j_{1},{\ldots},j_{k}}:=\frac{dh_{j_{1}}}{h_{j_{1}}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{dh_{j_{k}}}{h_{j_{k}}}

has at most a first order pole along
D
, so in this special case his result is stronger than Grothendieck’s.

An earlier version of Brieskorn’s result, that it holds for a normal crossing divisor (NCD)(
[
34
]
) played an important role in Deligne’s mixed Hodge theory: every quasiprojective variety
U
has a projective compactification
U\hookrightarrow X
in which
D:=X\smallsetminus U
is a NCD. Similarly, every singular variety has a resolution whose exceptional divisor is once again an NCD. Deligne used the poles to define the weight filtration on the cohomology of
U
.

If
D=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\{h_{j}=0\}
is a union of more general irreducible divisors
\{h_{j}=0\}
, the analogous Brieskorn complex
B^{\bullet}
generated by the closed forms
\frac{dh_{j}}{h_{j}}
does
not
in general calculate the cohomology of the complement: for example, if
D
is irreducible, this complex reduces to
0{\ \rightarrow\ }B^{0}{\ \rightarrow\ }B^{1}{\ \rightarrow\ }0
, whereas
X\smallsetminus D
may have cohomology in higher dimensions. Deligne and Dimca
explored the relation between pole order and the Hodge filtration in
[
22
]
.

1.3
The Logarithmic Comparison Theorem

Kyoji Saito, in
[
60
]
, introduced a fruitful generalisation of the Brieskorn complex. A meromorphic form
\omega
has a
logarithmic pole
along the divisor
D
if both
h{\omega}
and
hd{\omega}
are regular, where
h
is a local equation for
D
. Note in particular that all of Brieskorn’s forms have logarithmic poles along
D
.

Denote the sheaf of germs of meromorphic
k
-forms with logarithmic poles by
\Omega^{k}_{X}(\log D)
. Then
\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\log D)
is again a subcomplex of
\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D)
. In the same article Saito also described the dual of
\Omega_{X}^{1}(\log D)
, namely the sheaf of  ‘‘logarithmic derivations’’,
\fDer(-\log D)
, whose stalk at
x\in D
consists of germs of vector fields on
X
which are tangent to
D
at its smooth points. The duality arises from the contraction pairing

\Omega_{X}^{1}(\log D)\times\fDer(-\log D){\ \rightarrow\ }\mathcal{O}_{X},\quad({\omega},\xi)\mapsto\iota_{\xi}(\omega)={\omega}(\xi).

Saito showed that this is a perfect pairing, so that
\Omega_{X}^{1}(\log D)
is also the dual of
\fDer(-\log D)
. Saito’s interest was especially in the case where
D
is a
free divisor
, that is, where
\fDer(-\log D)
(or, equivalently,
\Omega_{X}^{1}(\log D)
) is a locally free sheaf of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules. Because the dual of any
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module has depth at least 2 when
0pt\,\mathcal{O}_{X}\geq 2
(see e.g.
[
62
]
, or

[
39
, Lemma 9.2]
), it follows from this that every plane curve is a free divisor. More interesting is the fact, proved by K. Saito, that the discriminant in the base of a versal deformation of an isolated hypersurface singularity is a free divisor. Looijenga generalised this by showing in
[
39
, Corollary 6.13]
that it holds also for the discriminant in the base of a versal deformation of an isolated complete intersection singularity (ICIS), and later authors (
[
65
]
,
[
7
]
) have extended this to the discriminants of a range of non-ICIS singularities, to discriminants in quiver representation spaces
[
8
]
and more generally in prehomogeneous vector spaces
[
29
]
.

Since for any divisor
D\subset X
one has a natural morphism

\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\log D)\hookrightarrow\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D)

one can ask for the class of divisors
D\subset X
such that the previous natural morphism is a quasi-isomorphism (or an isomorphism in the derived category of sheaves of
\mathbb{C}_{X}
–vector spaces).

(1.4) Definition.

If the morphism
\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\log D)\hookrightarrow\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\star D)
is a quasi-isomorphism we say that the divisor
D
satisfies the logarithmic comparison theorem (LCT), or even that the LCT holds for
D
.

By Grothendieck’s comparison theorem, the LCT holds for a divisor
D
if and only if the morphism
h^{q}(\Omega_{X}^{\bullet}(\log D))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}j_{*}\mathbb{C}_{U}
is an isomorphism for
0\leq q\leq\dim X
.
Any NCD satisfies the LCT, after
[
21
, Cap. II, Lemme 6.9]

(1.5) Theorem.
[Logarithmic Comparison Theorem,
[
15
]
]
If

Here

Local quasihomogeneity is used twice in the proof of Theorem

(1.6) Lemma.

1.5
Linear free divisors and logarithmic comparison revisited

A free divisor
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
linear
if
\Der(-\log D)=\Gamma(\mathbb{C}^{n},\fDer(-\log D))
has a basis of vector fields whose coefficients are linear forms. Normal crossing divisors are of course the simplest examples, and are in fact the only linear free divisors among hyperplane arrangements. In
[
8
]
R.-O. Buchweitz and the second author showed that the discriminant in the representation space of a Dynkin quiver, with a root of the underlying diagram as dimension vector, is a linear free divisor. All irreducible linear free divisors are classified, though not under that name, in
[
61
]
, where the operation of
castling
derives them from four basic examples.

If
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a linear free divisor then
the set of weight zero vector fields in
\Der(-\log D)
is a Lie algebra of dimension
n
; it is isomorphic to the Lie algebra of the Lie subgroup of
G_{D}
consisting of
linear automorphisms of the pair
(\mathbb{C}^{n},D)
. For linear free divisors,
[
28
]
gives an argument for the logarithmic comparison theorem which is quite different from the proof of
[
15
]
. When D is a linear free divisor, then it turns out that
\mathbb{C}^{n}\smallsetminus D
is a single orbit of the identity component
G^{0}_{D}
of the group
G_{D}
, so
H^{*}(\mathbb{C}^{n}\smallsetminus D;\mathbb{C})
is the cohomology of
G^{0}_{D}/S_{p}
, where
S_{p}
is the isotropy subgroup of a point
p
. Since
G^{0}_{D}
is path connected, the action of
S_{p}
on
H^{*}(G^{0}_{D};\mathbb{C})
is trivial, so
H^{*}(G^{0}_{D}/S_{p};D)=H^{*}(G^{0}_{D};\mathbb{C})
. Second, in this case the weight zero subcomplex of
{\Gamma}(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\Omega}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}^{\bullet}(\log D))
coincides with the complex of Lie algebra cohomology, with complex coefficients, of the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}_{D}
of
G^{0}_{D}
. By an argument using weighted homogeneity,
{\Gamma}(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\Omega}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}^{\bullet}(\log D))
is chain homotopic to its weight zero subcomplex. Thus,
{\Gamma}(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\Omega}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}^{\bullet}(\log D))
coincides with the Lie algebra cohomology of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{D}
with complex coefficients. For compact connected Lie groups
G
, a well-known argument shows that the Lie algebra cohomology coincides with the topological cohomology of the group. For linear free divisors the group
G^{0}_{D}
is never compact, but the isomorphism also holds good for the larger class of reductive groups, and for a significant class of linear free divisors, including all those mentioned above,
G^{0}_{D}
is indeed reductive.

1.6
A pairing
\mathbf{H^{p}(X\smallsetminus D)\times H^{q}(D){\ \rightarrow\ }H^{p+q}(D)}

The paper
[
51
]
introduces a variant
\check{\Omega}^{k}_{D}
of the module of Kähler forms
{\Omega}^{k}_{D}
, defined by

\check{\Omega}^{k}_{D}:=\frac{\Omega^{k}_{X}}{h\Omega_{X}^{k}(\log D)},

where
h
is a reduced equation for
D
.
Note that
\check{\Omega}^{0}_{D}={\Omega}^{0}_{D}=\mathcal{O}_{D}
.
Since
\frac{dh}{h}
has a logarithmic pole and
{\Omega}_{X}^{k}(\log D)\supset{\Omega}^{k}_{X}
, it follows that
h{\Omega}_{X}^{k}(\log D)\supseteq dh\wedge{\Omega}^{k-1}_{X}+h{\Omega}^{k}_{X}
, so
\check{\Omega}^{k}_{D}
is a quotient of
{\Omega}^{k}_{D}
. If
D
is a free divisor then each
\check{\Omega}^{k}_{D}
is a maximal Cohen-Macaulay
\mathcal{O}_{D}
-module, and coincides with
{\Omega}^{k}_{D}
at smooth points of
D
. The
\check{\Omega}^{k}_{D}
form a complex with respect to the usual exterior derivative:
if
\omega\in{\Omega}_{X}^{k}(\log D)_{x}
then
\frac{dh}{h}\wedge\omega\in{\Omega}_{X}^{k+1}(\log D)
, from which it follows that
d(h\omega)\in h\Omega_{X}^{k+1}(\log D)
. We denote the exterior derivative on this complex by
\check{d}
.
If
D
is quasihomogeneous then
(\check{\Omega}^{\bullet}_{D},\check{d})
is a resolution of
\mathbb{C}_{D}
(
[
51
, Lemma 3.3]
).

Straightforward calculations show

1.

there is a well-defined
pairing

{\Omega}_{X}^{k}(\log D)\times\check{\Omega}^{\ell}_{D}{\ \rightarrow\ }\check{\Omega}^{k+\ell}_{D},\ \ \text{defined by }({\omega}_{k},{\omega}_{\ell})\mapsto{\omega}_{k}\wedge{\omega}_{\ell}.

(14)

Note that there is no comparable wedge pairing

{\Omega}_{X}^{k}(\log D)\times{\Omega}^{\ell}_{D}{\ \rightarrow\ }{\Omega}^{k+\ell}_{D}.

For such a pairing to be well defined, the wedge of
{\omega}_{k}\in{\Omega}_{X}^{k}(\log D)
with
\omega_{\ell}\in(dh\wedge{\Omega}^{\ell-1}_{X}+h{\Omega}^{\ell}_{X})
would have to lie in

, and in general this does not hold.
For example, if
h=h_{1}h_{2}
, and we take
\omega_{1}=\frac{dh_{1}}{h_{1}}\in\Omega_{X}^{1}(\log D)
and
{\omega}_{1}^{\prime}=dh\in dh\wedge{\Omega}^{0}_{X}
, then

{\omega}_{1}\wedge{\omega}_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{dh_{1}}{h_{1}}\wedge\bigl(h_{1}dh_{2}+h_{2}dh_{1}\bigr)=dh_{1}\wedge dh_{2}\notin\bigl(dh\wedge{\Omega}^{1}_{X}+h{\Omega}^{2}_{X}\bigr).

2.

The pairing (
14
) descends to a pairing on the homology of the two complexes,

h^{k}(\Gamma(X,{\Omega}_{X}^{\bullet}(\log D))\times h^{\ell}(\Gamma(X,\check{\Omega}^{\bullet}_{D})){\ \rightarrow\ }h^{k+\ell}(\Gamma(X,\check{\Omega}^{\bullet}_{D})).

3.

When
D
is locally quasihomogeneous then in view of the LCT and the exactness of
\check{\Omega}^{\bullet}_{D}
, this gives a pairing

H^{k}(X\smallsetminus D;\mathbb{C})\times H^{\ell}(D;\mathbb{C}){\ \rightarrow\ }H^{k+\ell}(D;\mathbb{C}).

The properties of this pairing remain to be explored.

(2.1) Definition.

Let

(2.2) Example.

(2.3) Definition.

A coherent

2.4
The Spencer complex

Let
X
be a complex manifold of dimension
n\geq 1
.

(2.6) Definition.

[
47
, Chap I, (2.1)]
The Spencer complex in
X
is the following complex of left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–modules, denoted
{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}_{X}
(or simply
{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}
):

0\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{n}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon_{-n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon_{-2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon_{-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}

where the differential
\epsilon_{\bullet}
is defined by

\begin{split}\epsilon_{-1}(P\otimes\delta)&=P\delta,\\
\epsilon_{-p}(P\otimes(\delta_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta_{p}))&=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(-1)^{i-1}P\delta_{i}\otimes(\delta_{1}\wedge\cdots\widehat{\delta_{i}}\cdots\wedge\delta_{p}))\,+\\
&\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq p}(-1)^{i+j}P\otimes([\delta_{i},\delta_{j}]\wedge\delta_{1}\wedge\cdots\widehat{\delta_{i}}\cdots\widehat{\delta_{j}}\cdots\wedge\delta_{p})\,\,\,(2\leq p\leq n).\end{split}

We denote by
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X}
(or simply
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}
) the augmented complex

{\mathrm{Sp}}_{X}^{\bullet}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon_{0}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{X}\rightarrow 0

where
\epsilon_{0}(P)=P(1)
.

(2.7) Remark.

The complex
{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}_{X}
is a locally free resolution of the left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–module
\mathcal{O}_{X}
. Since
\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})
is a locally free
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–module, each left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–module

{\mathrm{Sp}}_{X}^{p}:=\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})

is locally free (or rank
\binom{n}{p}
).

To prove that
{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}_{X}
is a resolution of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
, or equivalently that
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X}
is acyclic, we can proceed locally at each point
x\in X
. If we choose local coordinates
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
on
X
around
x
, the partial derivatives
(\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n})
– where
\partial_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
– form a basis of the free
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–module
\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})_{x}
. The differential
\epsilon_{-p,x}
can be written as

\epsilon_{-p,x}(P\otimes(\partial_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\partial_{i_{p}}))=\sum_{j=1}^{p}(-1)^{j-1}P\partial_{i_{j}}\otimes(\partial_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\widehat{\partial_{i_{j}}}\cdots\wedge\partial_{i_{p}}).

So, the complex
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X,x}
is a Koszul complex (which is denoted by
K(\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n};\mathcal{D}_{X})
in
[
44
, I.2]
. This complex is exact (see e.g.
[
24
]
). We give here a proof of the acyclicity of
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X,x}
based on
[
9
, Th. 3.1.2; Prop. 4.1.3]
(which proves a more general result and follows a suggestion of B. Malgrange
[
44
, I.2]
).

We consider a discrete increasing filtration
G^{\bullet}:=G^{\bullet}\left(\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X}\right)
on the complex
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X}
(or more precisely on the complex
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X,x}
). The discrete filtration
G^{\bullet}
is compatible with the differentials and the associated graded complex is exact. This implies that the complex
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X}
is exact since the filtration
G^{\bullet}
is discrete (i.e.
G^{k}=0
if
k<0
).

The definition of
G^{\bullet}
is as follows: for
0\leq p\leq n
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, write

G^{k,-p}:=G^{k}\left(\mathcal{D}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})\right)=F^{k-p}(\mathcal{D})\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})

where
F^{\bullet}(\mathcal{D})
is the order filtration in
\mathcal{D}
. We also write
G^{k,1}:=G^{k}(\mathcal{O}_{X})=\mathcal{O}_{X}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

For each
k,p
one has
\epsilon_{-p}(G^{k,-p})\subset G^{k,-p+1}
since for any
P\in F^{k-p}(\mathcal{D})
one has
P\partial_{i}\in F^{k-p+1}(\mathcal{D})
. Thus,
G^{k,\bullet}
is a complex of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–modules for any
k
and
(G^{k,\bullet})_{k}
is a discrete increasing exhaustive filtration (simply denoted by
G^{\bullet}
) of the complex
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}}_{X}
.

For each
0\leq p\leq n
, the family
(G^{k,-p})_{k}
is a discrete increasing exhaustive filtration of the
\mathcal{D}
–module
\mathcal{D}\otimes\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})
whose associated graded
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–module

\gr_{G^{\bullet,-p}}\left(\mathcal{D}\otimes\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})\right)

is naturally isomorphic to

\gr_{F^{\bullet}}(\mathcal{D})[-p]\otimes\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X}).

The last isomorphism follows from the fact that
\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})
is a locally free
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–module and therefore
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–flat.

Then the associated graded complex
\gr_{G^{\bullet}}(\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}})
is

0\rightarrow\gr_{F^{\bullet}}(\mathcal{D})[-n]\otimes\bigwedge^{n}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau_{-n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau_{-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\gr_{F^{\bullet}}(\mathcal{D})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau_{0}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{X}\rightarrow 0

where the differential
\tau_{\bullet}=\gr(\epsilon_{\bullet})
is acting by

\begin{split}\tau_{0}(Q)=Q_{0},\\
\tau_{-1}(Q\otimes\partial_{i})&=Q\sigma(\partial_{i}),\\
\tau_{-p}(Q\otimes(\partial_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\partial_{i_{p}}))&=\sum_{j=1}^{p}(-1)^{j-1}Q\sigma(\partial_{i_{j}})\otimes(\partial_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\widehat{\partial_{i_{j}}}\cdots\wedge\partial_{i_{p}}))\,\,\,(2\leq p\leq n),\end{split}

where the tensor product is taken over
\mathcal{O}_{X}
,
Q_{0}
is the
0-th
homogenous component of
Q\in\gr_{F^{\bullet}}(\mathcal{D})
and
\sigma(\,)
is the principal symbol of the corresponding differential operator (see (
2.1
)).
So, the last complex is nothing but the augmented Koszul complex with respect to the regular sequence
\{\sigma(\partial_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(\partial_{n})\}
in the commutative ring
\gr_{F^{\bullet}}(\mathcal{D})
. Hence this complex is acyclic.

The de Rham complex of a
\mathcal{D}_{X}
--module

We follow here
[
47
, Chap. I (2.6)]
. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–module. The left exact functor

Here

stands for the category of (left)

–modules and

–modules with bounded cohomology.

(2.8) Definition.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–module. The complex
\mathbb{R}\fHom_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{O}_{X},\mathcal{M})
in
D^{b}(\mathbb{C}_{X})

. It is denoted by
\DR(\mathcal{M})
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–module. Since

given locally by

(2.9) Proposition.

[
47
, Lemme (2.6.3)]
For any left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–module
\mathcal{M}
, the complex
\DR(\mathcal{M})
can be represented by the complex

\Omega^{\bullet}_{X}(\mathcal{M}):=0\rightarrow\mathcal{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nabla}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Omega^{1}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\mathcal{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nabla}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nabla}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Omega^{n}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow 0

concentrated in degrees
[0,n]
, where
\nabla(\omega\otimes m)=\omega\wedge\nabla(m)+(-1)^{\deg(\omega)}d\omega\otimes m.

Proof.
By Remark

the complex
\widetilde{{\mathrm{Sp}}}^{\bullet}
is a locally free resolution of the left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–module
\mathcal{O}_{X}
. Thus
\DR(\mathcal{M})
is represented by
\fHom_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}({\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet},\mathcal{M})
. Moreover, for
p=0,\ldots,n
, the
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–modules
\fHom_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X}),\mathcal{M})
and

are isomorphic. And there exists a natural quasi-isomorphism, in

, from
\fHom_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{\bullet}\fDer(\mathcal{O}_{X}),\mathcal{M})

Q.E.D.

Following
[
47
, Ch. 1, (4.1)]
, if
\mathcal{M}
is a complex in the derived category
D^{b}_{c}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
(complexes of left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–modules, with bounded and coherent cohomology), the complex

\mathbb{R}\fHom_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D}_{X})

of right
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–modules has bounded and coherent cohomology.

(2.10) Definition.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a complex in

is the complex
\mathcal{M}^{*}
in

3.1
The sheaf of logarithmic differential operators with respect to a free divisor

Let
X
be a complex analytic manifold of dimension

Let us denote

and

When

is a free divisor, there is a nice sheaf of subrings of the sheaf
\mathcal{D}_{X}
of linear differential operators in

with respect to

-algebras (see
[

; compare with Sect.

is generated by
\mathcal{O}_{X}
and
\fDer(-\log D)

\mathcal{V}^{D}_{X}
is the enveloping algebra of
\fDer(-\log D)
considered as a Lie algebroid (cf.
[
20
, §(2.1)]
). From there we deduce that
\mathcal{V}^{D}_{X}
is a left and right coherent sheaf of rings (one can proceed as in the case of
\mathcal{D}_{X}
, Sect.
2.1
, or as in
[
4
, Th. 1.2.5]
) and its stalk at each point of

is a left and right Noetherian ring of finite global homological dimension
\leq 2n
(cf.
[
4
, App. IV, Prop. 4.14 and Th. 5.1]
).

(3.15) Lemma.

Let
f
be a quasihomogeneous polynomial in
\mathbb{C}\{x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n}\}[x_{1},\dots,x_{r}]
, of strictly positive weight with respect to weights
(w_{1},\dots,w_{r},0,{\ldots},0)
with
w_{1},{\ldots},w_{r}
all strictly positive. Then, for any
p>0
and any non-zero quasihomogeneous logarithmic
p
-form
\omega
, we have
\wt\omega>-\wt f
.

Proof.
Since
f\omega
must be holomorphic, we know that
\wt\omega\geq-\wt f
.
We may assume
{\partial}f/{\partial}x_{1}\neq 0
(i.e.
f
effectively depends on
x_{1}
).

Suppose that
\omega
is a non-zero logarithmic
p
-form, and that
\wt\omega=-\wt f
. Then,
\alpha=f\omega
is a non-zero holomorphic
p
-form with
\wt\alpha=0
. That means that
\alpha
only depends on the variables
x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n}
of weight 0:

\alpha=\sum_{r+1\leq i_{1}<{\ldots}<i_{p}\leq n}\alpha_{i_{1},{\ldots},i_{p}}(x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n})dx_{i_{1}}\wedge{\ldots}\wedge dx_{i_{p}}.

Reordering the variables, we may assume
\alpha_{r+1,{\ldots},r+p}\neq 0
.

Since
\omega
is logarithmic, the
p+1
-form

df\wedge\omega
is holomorphic (this follows from the fact that
f\omega
and
fd\omega
are holomorphic). The coefficient of
dx_{1}\wedge dx_{r+1}\wedge{\ldots}\wedge dx_{r+p}
in

, which must be holomorphic, is

\frac{\alpha_{r+1,{\ldots},r+p}}{f}\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}.

So
f
divides
\alpha_{r+1,{\ldots},r+p}\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}
in the ring of power series

\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r},x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n}\}
. However,
f
and
{\partial}f/{\partial}x_{1}
are quasihomogeneous polynomials in
\mathbb{C}\{x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n}\}[x_{1},\dots,x_{r}]
. By equating homogeneous parts, we deduce that

also divides
\alpha_{r+1,{\ldots},r+p}{\partial}f/{\partial}x_{1}
in
\mathbb{C}\{x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n}\}[x_{1},\dots,x_{r}]
. Since
\wt({\partial}f/{\partial}x_{1})=\wt(f)-\wt(x_{1})
, this is impossible.
Q.E.D.

3.4
The logarithmic Spencer complex revisited

Recall that, see (
3.2
), for a free divisor
D
in a complex manifold
X
, the logarithmic Spencer complex
{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}(\log D)
is a locally free resolution of the left
\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}
-module
\mathcal{O}_{X}

[
9
, Th. 3.1.2]
. We are going to
describe the complex

\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}}{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}(\log D)
in terms of a (local) basis
\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}\}
of the free
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–module
\fDer(-\log D)
.
We can write

[\delta_{i},\delta_{j}]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}^{ij}\delta_{k}

for
1\leq i<j\leq n
where
\alpha_{k}^{ij}\in\mathcal{O}_{X}
for
k=1,\ldots,n
.
We also have

\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}}{\mathrm{Sp}}^{p}(\log D)=\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}}{\mathcal{V}_{X}^{D}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(-\log D)\simeq\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(-\log D)

for
p=0,\ldots,n
, and we also denote
\epsilon_{-p}
the differential of this complex of left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–modules. We denote this complex by
{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\log D)
.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}
we denote
\Lambda_{p}:=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{p}\,|\,1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{p}\leq n\}
. The free left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–module
\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(-\log D)\simeq\bigoplus_{{\underline{{\bf i}}}\in\Lambda_{p}}\mathcal{D}_{X}\,e_{\underline{{\bf i}}}
has rank
\binom{n}{p}
with basis
\{e_{\underline{{\bf i}}}\,|{\underline{{\bf i}}}\in\Lambda_{p}\}
, for
p=0,\ldots,n
. An isomorphism
from
\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\bigwedge^{p}\fDer(-\log D)
onto
\bigoplus_{{\underline{{\bf i}}}\in\Lambda_{p}}\mathcal{D}_{X}\,e_{\underline{{\bf i}}}
maps

1\otimes\delta_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta_{i_{p}}\,\mapsto\,e_{{\underline{{\bf i}}}}.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\underline{{\bf i}}}\in\Lambda_{p}
we fix the following notations:

•

The support of
{\underline{{\bf i}}}
is
\supp({\underline{{\bf i}}})=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\}
.

•

\overline{\supp({\underline{{\bf i}}})}=\{1,\ldots,n\}\,\,\smallsetminus\,\,\supp({\underline{{\bf i}}})
.

•

{\underline{{\bf i}}}(\widehat{k}):=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},i_{k+1},\ldots,i_{p})\in\Lambda_{p-1},
for
1\leq k\leq p
.

•

{\underline{{\bf i}}}(\widehat{k,\ell}):={\underline{{\bf i}}}(\widehat{k})(\widehat{\ell-1})\in\Lambda_{p-2},

1\leq k<\ell\leq p
.

•

\sigma(q;{\underline{{\bf i}}}):=\max\{j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}\,|\,i_{j}<q\}
and
\sigma(q;{\underline{{\bf i}}})=0
if
q<i_{1}
.

•

{\underline{{\bf i}}}(\widecheck{q})=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},q,i_{k+1},\ldots,i_{p})\in\Lambda_{p+1}
for
k=\sigma(q;{\underline{{\bf i}}})
and
q\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\smallsetminus\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\}
.

The complex
({\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\log D),\,\epsilon_{-\bullet})
can be written as the complex
(\bigoplus_{{\underline{{\bf i}}}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}}\mathcal{D}_{X}\,e_{\underline{{\bf i}}},\,\widetilde{\epsilon_{-\bullet}})
where

\widetilde{\epsilon_{-p}}:\bigoplus_{{\underline{{\bf i}}}\in\Lambda_{p}}\mathcal{D}_{X}\,e_{\underline{{\bf i}}}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{{\underline{{\bf j}}}\in\Lambda_{p-1}}\mathcal{D}_{X}\,e_{\underline{{\bf j}}}

is the morphism of left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
–modules defined by

\widetilde{\epsilon_{-p}}(e_{\underline{{\bf i}}})=\sum_{k=1}^{p}(-1)^{k-1}\delta_{i_{k}}e_{{\underline{{\bf i}}}(\widehat{k})}+\sum_{1\leq\ell<m\leq p}(-1)^{\ell+m}\sum_{q\in\overline{\supp({\underline{{\bf i}}}(\widehat{\ell,m}))}}(-1)^{\sigma(q;{\underline{{\bf i}}}(\widehat{\ell,m}))}\alpha^{i_{\ell}i_{m}}_{q}e_{{\underline{{\bf i}}}(\widehat{\ell,m})(\widecheck{q})}.

Notice that the complex
(\bigoplus_{{\underline{{\bf i}}}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}}\mathcal{D}_{X}\,e_{\underline{{\bf i}}},\,\widetilde{\epsilon_{-\bullet}})
can be viewed as a non commutative version of a Koszul complex.

Let us write down an example for
n=3
.

One has:

\begin{split}\epsilon_{-1}(e_{i})&=\delta_{i}{\mbox{ for }}i=1,2,3,\\
\epsilon_{-2}(e_{ij})&=\delta_{i}e_{j}-\delta_{j}e_{i}-\sum_{q=1}^{3}\alpha_{q}^{ij}e_{q}\,{\mbox{ for }}1\leq i<j\leq 3,\\
\epsilon_{-3}(e_{123})&=\delta_{1}e_{23}-\delta_{2}e_{13}+\delta_{3}e_{12}+(\alpha_{1}^{13}+\alpha_{2}^{23})e_{12}+(-\alpha_{1}^{12}+\alpha_{3}^{23})e_{13}+(-\alpha_{2}^{12}-\alpha_{3}^{13})e_{23}\\
&=(\delta_{3}+\alpha_{1}^{13}+\alpha_{2}^{23})e_{12}+(-\delta_{2}-\alpha_{1}^{12}+\alpha_{3}^{23})e_{13}+(\delta_{1}-\alpha_{2}^{12}-\alpha_{3}^{13})e_{23}.\end{split}

3.5
The annihilator ideal of
1/f
for a reduced equation
f
of a normal crossing divisor in
\mathbb{C}^{n}

Write
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{C}^{n},0}
and
f=x_{1}\cdots x_{r}
for
1\leq r\leq n
. One has the following equality

Ann_{\mathcal{D}}(1/f)=\mathcal{D}(\partial_{r+1},\ldots,\partial_{n},x_{1}\partial_{1}+1,\ldots,x_{r}\partial_{r}+1).

Since the inclusion
A_{n}:=A_{n}(\mathbb{C})\subset\mathcal{D}
is flat, it is enough to prove a similar equality for the Weyl algebra
A_{n}
instead of
\mathcal{D}
.
The inclusion

A_{n}(\partial_{r+1},\ldots,\partial_{n},x_{1}\partial_{1}+1,\ldots,x_{r}\partial_{r}+1)\subset\Ann_{A_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{f}\right)

is obvious since each generator of the first ideal annihilates
1/f
.
To prove the opposite inclusion, let us assume first
1=r=n
and write
x=x_{1}
and
\partial=\partial_{x}
. First of all, any
P=P(x,\partial)\in A_{1}
can be written as

P=Q(x\partial+1)+R+S

for unique
Q\in A_{1}
,
R:=R(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
and
S:=S(\partial)\in\mathbb{C}[\partial](\partial)
. If
P(\frac{1}{x})=0
and
S(\partial)
is not zero with order
s\geq 1
, then
\frac{R}{x}+S(\partial)(\frac{1}{x})=0
which is a contradiction since the order of the pole of the rational function
\frac{R}{x}+S(\partial)(\frac{1}{x})
is
s+1
. Then
S=0
and then
R=0
and
P\in A_{1}(x\partial+1)
. The general case
1\leq r\leq n
can be reduced to
r=n
by taking, for any operator
P
annihilating
\frac{1}{x_{1}\cdots x_{r}},
the remainder of the division of
P
by
\partial_{r+1},\ldots,\partial_{n}
. Finally, for the case
r=n
we proceed by division of an operator
P
annihilating
\frac{1}{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}},
with respect to
\partial_{1}x_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}x_{n}
.

The
\mathcal{D}
–module criterion for LCT gives another proof that LCT holds for normal crossing divisors. Indeed, any such divisor
D\equiv(x_{1}\cdots x_{r}=0)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is Koszul free and then the complex

\mathcal{D}\otimes_{\mathcal{V}}{\mathrm{Sp}}^{\bullet}(\log D)
is concentrated in cohomological degree
0
and
\mathcal{D}\otimes_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{O}
is locally presented as the quotient
\mathcal{D}/\mathcal{D}(\partial_{r+1},\ldots,\partial_{n},x_{1}\partial_{1}+1,\ldots,x_{r}\partial_{r}+1)
, since
\{x_{1}\partial_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\partial_{r},\partial_{r+1},\ldots,\partial_{n}\}
is a basis of
\Der(-\log D)
. Finally, one has

\frac{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{D}(\partial_{r+1},\ldots,\partial_{n},x_{1}\partial_{1}+1,\ldots,x_{r}\partial_{r}+1)}\simeq\mathcal{D}f^{-1}=\mathcal{O}(\star D).

The last equality follows from the well-know fact that the (global) Bernstein-Sato polynomial of
f=x_{1}\cdots x_{r}
is
(s+1)^{r}
.
