Abstract
The Laplacian-constrained Gaussian Markov Random Field (LGMRF) is a common multivariate statistical model for learning a weighted sparse dependency graph from given data. This graph learning problem can be formulated as a maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) of the precision matrix, subject to Laplacian structural constraints, with a sparsity-inducing penalty term. This paper aims to solve this learning problem accurately and efficiently. First, since the commonly used ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm penalty is inappropriate in this setting and may lead to a complete graph, we employ the nonconvex minimax concave penalty (MCP), which promotes sparse solutions with lower estimation bias. Second, as opposed to existing first-order methods for this problem, we develop a second-order proximal Newton approach to obtain an efficient solver, utilizing several algorithmic features, such as using Conjugate Gradients, preconditioning, and splitting to active/free sets. Numerical experiments demonstrate the advantages of the proposed method in terms of both computational complexity and graph learning accuracy compared to existing methods.



1
Introduction

Graphs are fundamental mathematical structures used in various fields to represent data, signals, and processes. Weighted graphs naturally arise when processing networked data applications, such as computer, social, sensor, energy, transportation, and biological networks
Newman, 2010
, where the data is inherently related to a graph associated with the underlying network.
As a result, graph learning is used in a wide range of applications, both for virtual, data-based networks
and for physical networks of infrastructures;
the latter include the brain
Vecchio et al., 2017
, financial analysis
Giudici and Spelta, 2016
, and electrical networks
Deka et al., 2018
.
In this context, a fundamental graph learning problem is the estimation of the Laplacian matrix, which plays a central role in spectral graph theory
Chung, 1997
and machine learning
Von Luxburg, 2007
.
Moreover, in the field of graph signal processing (GSP)
Shuman et al., 2013
, the Laplacian matrix is used to extend basic signal processing operations, such as filtering
Milanfar, 2012
, sampling
Anis et al., 2016
, and signal recovery
Narang and Ortega, 2012
;
Kroizer et al., 2022
. Therefore, accurate and efficient Laplacian learning is extremely valuable for data processing in networks.

Gaussian Markov Random Fields (GMRFs)
Rue and Held, 2005
are probabilistic graphical models that have been studied extensively over the last two decades
Banerjee et al., 2006
;
Hsieh et al., 2013
;
Hsieh et al., 2011
. In particular, there has been a significant effort to develop approaches for learning sparse precision matrices under GMRF models, where this precision represents a graph topology. The graphical LASSO (GLASSO)
Friedman et al., 2008
is a widely-used approach to estimate the sparse precision matrix using
\ell_{1}
-norm regularization.
Laplacian-constrained GMRF (LGMRF) models are a specific type of GMRF, in which the precision is a Laplacian matrix.
Laplacian constraints imply that smooth signals, which have similar values for nodes connected by large weights, have a higher probability
Ying et al., 2020b
. Smooth graph signals are common in various network applications, such as power systems
Dabush et al., 2023
.
Thus, LGMRF models
have been widely studied in semi-supervised learning frameworks
Zhu et al., 2003
, to analyze real-world datasets

Cardoso and Palomar, 2020
, and in GSP
Dong et al., 2016
;
Egilmez et al., 2017
.

The estimation of standard and Laplacian-constrained GMRF models are closely related. However, the extension of GLASSO and similar GMRF estimation algorithms to the case of LGMRF, which also considers the Laplacian constraints, is not straightforward. For example, since the Laplacian matrix is sparse in many applications, then similarly to the GLASSO approach,
\ell_{1}
-norm regularization methods were used to promote the Laplacian sparsity
Egilmez et al., 2017
;
Kumar et al., 2019
;
Liu et al., 2019
;
Zhao et al., 2019
. However, it was recently shown that the
\ell_{1}
-norm is an inappropriate penalty for promoting the sparsity of the precision matrix under the LGMRF model, as it leads to an inaccurate recovery of the connectivity pattern of the graph

Ying et al., 2020a
.
Thus, to properly address the Laplacian estimation problem, other sparsity-promoting penalty terms should be used
Ying et al., 2020a
;
Ying et al., 2020b
. However, the existing methods are of first-order (i.e., gradient-based), while in general GMRF estimation literature
Oztoprak et al., 2012
;
Hsieh et al., 2013
;
Hsieh et al., 2011
;
Treister and Turek, 2014
it is known that second-order methods, i.e., proximal Newton
(
Lee et al., 2014
)
, yield better performance in terms of run time. In such approaches, a quadratic approximation is applied on the smooth part, while the non-smooth regularization term remains intact. This exploits a connection between the gradient and Hessian of the MLE objective and hence is efficient for GMRF estimation. Therefore, there is a need for second-order methods for Laplacian learning that utilize proper regularization (to yield accurate sparsity patterns) and are efficient in run time.

Contribution.

In this paper, we present an efficient graph estimation method for the LGMRF model based on the proximal Newton approach. Our method involves solving a nonconvex penalized MLE problem with MCP penalty, instead of the commonly-used
\ell_{1}
-norm penalty. At each iteration, the smooth part of the objective is approximated by a second-order Taylor expansion around the current precision matrix, while the non-smooth penalty
and Laplacian constraints
remain intact. The resulting Newton problem is a penalized quadratic minimization under linear constraints to guarantee that the next iterate is a Laplacian matrix.
To the best of our knowledge, the proposed method is the first proximal Newton method for the LGMRF model estimation. Furthermore, our framework includes two algorithmic novelties that are nontrivial in this context.
First, the inner, constrained, Newton problem is solved by a projected nonlinear conjugate gradient method. This yields a significant computational speedup over gradient-based first-order methods. Second, we introduce a diagonal preconditioner to improve the performance further. We show some theoretical results regarding the problem and the algorithms we propose. Furthermore, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in learning sparse graphs through numerical experiments.

2.1
GMRF and Constrained MLE

In this paper, we use the formulation of the graph estimation problem based on the probabilistic
graphical model
Koller and Friedman, 2009
;
Banerjee et al., 2008
.
We data samples from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution parametrized by a positive semi-definite precision matrix,
{\bf{L}}
, i.e.,
{\bf{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({{\bf{0}},{\bf{L}}^{\dagger}}\right)
, where
\mathcal{N}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\right)
denotes the normal distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.
This defines a Laplacian-constrained improper LGMRF model
Kumar et al., 2019
with parameters
\left({{\bf{0}},{\bf{L}}}\right)
, where the precision matrix is
{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}
is defined in (
2
).

Given
n
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples
{\bf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{x}}_{n}
drawn from an LGMRF, our goal is to find the constrained MLE of
{\bf{L}}
based on these samples and
under the Laplacian constraints. The MLE can be found by solving
the following constrained minimization of the negative log-likelihood:

\hat{{\bf{L}}}_{MLE}=\underset{{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{L}}{\text{argmin}}\left\{{{\rm Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}{\bf{S}}}\right)}-\log|{\bf{L}}|_{+}}\right\},

(4)

where
{\bf{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf{x}}_{i}{\bf{x}}^{T}_{i}
is the empirical covariance matrix.

Sparsity plays an important role in high-dimensional learning, which helps avoid over-fitting and improves the identification of the relationships among data, especially where
{\bf{S}}
is low rank. In particular, the Laplacian is a sparse matrix in various applications, e.g. electrical networks
Halihal and Routtenberg, 2022
.
A sparse graph estimation problem under the LGMRF model can be formulated by adding a sparse penalty function to the estimator in (
4
), which results in

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\underset{{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{L}}{\text{minimize}}&{\rm Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}{\bf{S}}}\right)}-\log|{\bf{L}}|_{+}+\rho\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right),\end{aligned}

(5)

where
\rho\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)
is a general sparsity-promoting penalty function and
\lambda
is a tuning parameter.

The objective function in (
5
) (or in (
4
)) involves the pseudo-determinant term, since the target matrix
{\bf{L}}
is a singular matrix. As a result, the optimization problem becomes hard to solve

Holbrook, 2018
.
As a remedy to this problem,
it is demonstrated in
Egilmez et al., 2017
that if
{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{L}
, then we can use the relation

|{\bf{L}}|_{+}=|{\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}}|,

(6)

where
{\bf{J}}=\frac{1}{p}{\bf{1}}{\bf{1}}^{T}
.
By substituting (
6
) in (
5
), the problem is simplified.
In addition, we show that the constraints in (
2
) are redundant, and simplify them in the following theorem whose proof appears in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 1
.

The optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{F}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}{\bf{S}}}\right)}-\log|{\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}}|+\rho\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right),

(7)

where the feasible set is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\left\{{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{S}^{p}\Big|\begin{array}[]{ll}{\bf{L}}_{i,j}\leq 0,\forall i\neq j,~i,j=1,\ldots,p,\\
{\bf{L}}{\bf{1}}={\bf{0}}\end{array}\right\}.

is equivalent to the
optimization problem stated in (
5
).

2.2
Sparsity Promoting Penalty Functions

Recent works like
(
Egilmez et al., 2017
)

introduced the
\ell_{1}
-norm penalized MLE under the LGMRF model to estimate a sparse graph.
The choice of
\rho
in (
7
) as
\|{\bf{L}}\|_{1,\text{off}}
, the absolute sum of all off-diagonal elements of
{\bf{L}}
,
results in the same objective function as in the well-known GLASSO problem
but where the feasible set is restricted to
{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{F}
.
However, unlike in GLASSO, under Laplacian constraints the
\ell_{1}
-norm is ineffective in promoting a sparse solution. In particular, it is shown by
Ying et al., 2020a
that
substituting
\rho\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)=\lambda\|{\bf{L}}\|_{1,\text{off}}
in the objective of (
7
) and
choosing the penalty parameter
\lambda
to be large enough
leads to a fully-connected (complete) graph as the solution.
Moreover, numerical experiments in
(
Ying et al., 2020a
)
show that the number of the estimated positive edges increases as
\lambda
increases. An intuitive explanation for this phenomenon is that the
\ell_{1}
-norm promotes sparsity uniformly on all off-diagonal entrances of the matrix.
Hence, for
\lambda>0
, minimizing the
\ell_{1}
-norm separately with a positive definite constraint, as in GLASSO, leads to a diagonal matrix with a positive diagonal. However, unlike a regular GMRF model, the Laplacian constraints,
{\bf{L}}{\bf{1}}={\bf{0}}
and
{\bf{L}}_{i,j}\leq 0,
for
i\neq j
, relate the off-diagonal entries with the diagonal entries of
{\bf{L}}
. Hence, minimizing the
\ell_{1}
-norm would lead to the zeros matrix.
Therefore, as
\lambda
increases, the eigenvalues of
{\bf{L}}
get closer to zero, and the
-\log|\cdot|
term in the objective function increases significantly.
Since
\ell_{1}
-norm penalizes all off-diagonal elements in a uniform way, to prevent a significant increase of the objective function, the minimization process inserts relatively small (in absolute value) negative numbers, even for zero elements in the ground-truth Laplacian matrix. This way, both the
\ell_{1}
-norm and the expression
-\log|\cdot|
in the optimization problem remain relatively small.

Alternative approaches using nonconvex penalties, such as clipped absolute deviation (SCAD)
Fan and Li, 2001
and the minimax concave penalty (MCP)
Zhang, 2010a
, have been proposed to alleviate this issue.
These penalty functions penalize the elements in the precision matrix more selectively than the
\ell_{1}
-norm, such that elements that receive significant values from the MLE will receive insignificant penalties, while elements closer to zero will receive a greater penalty.
In this paper, we use the MCP penalty function, which is applied on the off-diagonal entries of
{\bf{L}}
as follows:

\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)=\sum\nolimits_{i\neq j}MCP\left({{\bf{L}}_{ij};\gamma,\lambda}\right),

(11)

where the
MCP(\cdot)
is a scalar function, defined as

\displaystyle MCP(x;\gamma,\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\lambda|x|-\frac{x^{2}}{2\gamma}\quad\textrm{if}\quad|x|\leq\gamma\lambda\\
\frac{1}{2}\gamma\lambda^{2}\quad\quad\quad\textrm{if}\quad|x|>\gamma\lambda\end{array}\right..

The function is constant and has a derivative of 0 for values larger than
\gamma\lambda
in magnitude, hence only elements smaller than
\gamma\lambda
are affected by
\rho_{MCP}()
.

To conclude, our goal in this paper is to develop an efficient algorithm for finding the constrained MLE of the Laplacian matrix, which is the precision matrix of the LGMRF model. This is done by solving the optimization problem in (
7
) with
\rho\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)=\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)
, defined in (
11
). I.e., the problem we solve is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\underset{{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{F}}{\text{minimize}}&{\rm Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}{\bf{S}}}\right)}-\log|{\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}}|+\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)\\
\end{aligned},

(15)

where
\mathcal{F}
is defined in (
1
).

Method

In this section, we develop the proposed proximal Newton method for the graph Laplacian estimation, named NewGLE, that aims to solve (

This constitutes the NewGLE method, which is summarized in Algorithm 1.
Following that, we present our inner solver for finding the Newton direction, which is composed of the nonlinear projected conjugate gradient that is used together with a diagonal preconditioner, in Subsection

2

3.1
Proximal Newton for Graph Laplacian Estimation

In the “proximal Newton” approach, a quadratic approximation is applied on the smooth part of the objective function, while leaving the non-smooth term intact, in order to obtain the Newton descent direction. This approach is considered to be among the state-of-the-art methods for solving the GLASSO problem
Rolfs et al., 2012
;
Hsieh et al., 2013
;
Hsieh et al., 2011
;
Mazumder and Hastie, 2012
;
Treister et al., 2016
, which is highly related to our problem in (
15
). The advantage of this method lies in the treatment of the
\log\det
term (as we show next), which appears in both problems. However, the GLASSO methods cannot be applied to our graph learning problems because we aim to estimate a precision matrix that satisfies the Laplacian constraints (and is singular). In contrast, the learned precision matrix under the GMRF model in GLASSO is a general positive definite matrix. In addition, we consider the MCP penalty, while the above-mentioned methods consider the
\ell_{1}
-norm penalty, which is not suitable for our case, as discussed in Subsection
2.2
.

In our proximal Newton approach we design a constrained Newton problem, where the smooth part of the objective function in (
15
) is

f\left({{\bf{L}}}\right)={\rm Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}{\bf{S}}}\right)}-\log{|{\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}}|},

(16)

and the penalty function,
\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)
, is the non-smooth term. At the
t
-th iteration of the proximal Newton approach, the smooth part of the objective is approximated by a second-order Taylor expansion around the current estimation
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}
.
To this end, we use the gradient and Hessian of
f({\bf{L}})
that are given by
Hsieh et al., 2014
;
Chin et al., 2013

\nabla_{{\bf{L}}}f={\bf{S}}-{\bf{Q}},\quad\nabla_{{\bf{L}}}^{2}f={\bf{Q}}\otimes{\bf{Q}},

(17)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product and
{\bf{Q}}=\left({{\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}}}\right)^{-1}
.

The gradient in (
17
) already shows the main difficulty in solving the optimization problem in (
15
): it contains
{\bf{Q}}
, the inverse of the matrix
{\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}}
, which is expensive to compute. The advantage of the proximal Newton approach for this problem is the low overhead: by calculating
{\bf{Q}}
in
\nabla_{{\bf{L}}}f({\bf{L}})
, we also get part of the Hessian computation at the same cost.

In the same spirit of the proximal Newton approach, in addition to the penalty term, we also keep the Laplacian constraint intact in the Newton problem. That is,
at iteration
t
, the Newton direction
\Delta^{(t)}

solves the constrained penalized quadratic minimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{\Delta\in\mathcal{S}^{p}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle f({\bf{L}}^{(t)})+{\rm Tr}{\left({\Delta\left({{\bf{S}}-{\bf{Q}}}\right)}\right)}+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}{\left({\Delta{\bf{Q}}\Delta{\bf{Q}}}\right)}

(18)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\|\varepsilon\cdot\Delta\|_{F}^{2}}+\rho_{MCP}({\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\Delta;\lambda)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle{\bf{\displaystyle L}}^{(t)}+\Delta\in\mathcal{F},

where
{\bf{Q}}=\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+{\bf{J}}}\right)^{-1}
.
It should be noted that for the sake of simplicity, we use the notation

{\bf{Q}}
instead of
{\bf{Q}}^{(t)}
, although
{\bf{Q}}
is updated with the iteration
t
.
Note that the gradient and Hessian of
f(\cdot)

in (
17
) at the iterate
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}
are featured in the second and third terms in (
18
), respectively. The first term of (
18
) is constant and can be ignored. Altogether, the objective function in (
18
) is quadratic with the MCP sparsity-promoting penalty. In addition, we have a constraint to guarantee that the next update of
{\bf{L}}
is feasible, i.e.,
\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\Delta}\right)\in\mathcal{F}
.
The fourth term, with a small and symmetric weight matrix
\varepsilon\geq 0
penalizing the squares of the off-diagonals of
\Delta
. This term is used for the stabilization of the iterative solution of (
18
), and to make sure that the Hessian of (
18
) is positive definite, since
\rho_{MCP}
is concave. The advantage of solving (
18
) with
\rho_{MCP}
intact, as opposed to majorizing
\rho_{MCP}
(e.g., using its gradient instead) is that the underlying gradient-based iterative solver of (
18
) has access to the curvature of
\rho_{MCP}
and to its behavior in the sub-sections in its definition (
2.2
).

Once the Newton problem is approximately solved, yielding the direction
\Delta^{(t)}
, it is added to
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}
employing a linesearch procedure to sufficiently reduce the objective in (
15
). To this end, the updated iterate is

{\bf{L}}^{(t+1)}={\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha^{(t)}\Delta^{(t)},

(19)

where the parameter
\alpha^{(t)}
is obtained by a backtracking linesearch using Armijo’s rule

. We state the following theorem on such a solution process, whose proof appears in Appendix
D
:

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\}
be a series of points produced by a proximal quadratic minimization, constrained by
\mathcal{F}
with some SPD matrices
0\prec\lambda^{{\bf{M}}}_{min}{\bf{I}}\preceq{\bf{M}}^{(t)}\preceq\lambda^{{\bf{M}}}_{max}{\bf{I}}
as the Hessian (as in (
48
)), followed by the linesearch (
19
), starting from
{\bf{L}}^{(0)}\in\mathcal{F}
. Then any limit point

of

3.3
Restricting the updates to free sets

To ease the minimization of (
15
) we limit the minimization of the Newton problem to a “free set” of variables,
while keeping the rest as zeros. This idea was suggested in
(
Hsieh et al., 2011
)
for the GLASSO problem that includes the
\ell_{1}
-norm penalty. The free set of a matrix
{\bf{L}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\textbf{Free}\left({{\bf{L}}}\right)=\left\{{k:{\bf{L}}_{i,j}\neq 0\lor\left[{{\bf{S}}-{\bf{Q}}}\right]_{i,j}>\lambda}\right\},

(26)

where
\lor

the relation between

and
k
appears in Definition (
1
).
This set comes from
the subgradient of the
\ell_{1}
-norm term, in addition to the non-positivity constraint—the zero elements that are not in

will remain zero after a (projected) coordinate descent update. Because the subgradient of the MCP penalty is identical to that of
\ell_{1}
-norm at zero, the free set is defined in the same way as for the
\ell_{1}
-norm. As

To get the Newton direction for the

More Related Works

GMRF estimation with
\ell_{1}
regularization.

The
\ell_{1}
regularized MLE under the GMRF model has been extensively studied recently (see
d’Aspremont et al., 2008
;
Friedman et al., 2008
;
Rolfs et al., 2012
;
Hsieh et al., 2013
;
Hsieh et al., 2011
;
Treister et al., 2016
;
Shalom et al., 2022
and references therein). Some of these methods include a proximal Newton approach. However, these methods cannot be directly applied to the graph learning problem, as the goal here is to learn a Laplacian precision matrix as opposed to a general positive definite matrix.

The
\ell_{1}
regularization bias and its alternatives.
One drawback of the mentioned approaches is that the
\ell_{1}
penalty causes an estimation bias, which is relevant to several variable selection problems, e.g., least squares minimization and precision estimation under
\ell_{1}
priors. To reduce the
\ell_{1}
bias, two-stage methods, nonconvex penalties, and hard-thresholding approaches have been introduced for least squares variable selection
Zhang, 2010a
;
Breheny and Huang, 2011
;
Loh and Wainwright, 2013
and GMRF estimation
Chen et al., 2018
;
Lam and Fan, 2009
;
Shen et al., 2012
;
Finder et al., 2022
.
Several of these methods use nonconvex penalties, such as SCAD, MCP, and capped
\ell_{1}
-norm
(
Zhang, 2010b
)
. Different algorithms have been proposed to solve the resulting nonconvex problems, e.g., minimization-maximization
(
Hunter and Li, 2005
)
, local quadratic and linear approximations
(
Fan and Li, 2001
;
Zou and Li, 2008
)
.

LGMRF estimation.
Recent works
Egilmez et al., 2017
;
Kumar et al., 2019
;
Liu et al., 2019
;
Zhao et al., 2019
investigate the
\ell_{1}
-norm penalized MLE under the LGMRF model to estimate a graph Laplacian from data. However, it has been shown recently that imposing an
\ell_{1}
-norm penalty to the Laplacian-constrained MLE produces an unexpected behavior: the number of nonzero graph weights grows as the regularization parameter increases
Ying et al., 2020a
.

To alleviate this issue,
nonconvex penalties, such as
the MCP, have been proposed.
For example, the work of
Koyakumaru et al., 2023
suggests adding Tikhonov regularization to convexify the cost function and use the primal-dual splitting method to solve the optimization problem. However, as the Tikhonov regularization parameter increases, the bias of the estimation increases as well. Another approach in
Ying et al., 2020a
;
Ying et al., 2020b
;
Vieyra, 2022
uses a nonconvex estimation method by solving a sequence of weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm penalized subproblems, according to a majorization-minimization framework. In each majorization step, the
\ell_{1}
-norm weights are updated, and then in the minimization step, the
\ell_{1}
-penalized function is minimized by the projected gradient descent (PGD) method.
The approach in
Ying et al., 2021
;
Tugnait, 2021
requires solving two subproblems:
The first is an initial estimator of the precision matrix, e.g., the MLE. Then, the estimation is used to define the
\ell_{1}
weights for the second subproblem. Both stages are solved by PGD.

All the abovementioned methods are gradient-based methods that do not consider Hessian information.
On the other hand, the experience with the closely related GMRF estimation problem suggests that proximal Newton approaches would result in superior computational efficiency compared to those methods.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a proximal Newton method for minimizing a regularized maximum likelihood objective function for estimating a sparse graph Laplacian precision matrix from data distributed according to an LGMRF model. Our method is based on a second-order approximation of the smooth part of the objective function, while keeping the regularization and constraints intact. Furthermore, we used the nonconvex MCP function to promote the sparsity of the resulting graph without introducing a significant bias. Beyond the quadratic approximation itself, we used three algorithmic features to treat the Newton problem at each iteration efficiently: 1) we used a free/active set splitting, (2) we used a nonlinear conjugate gradient method, and (3) we used diagonal preconditioning.
This resulted in an accurate and efficient estimation of the sparse weighted graph.

Numerical studies demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm in terms of accuracy and computational complexity. The proposed method outperformed recent methods in terms of convergence time, while achieving similar or better F-scores and relative errors, particularly for problems with a small sample size ratio (i.e.,
n/p<1
). The plausible performance of the proposed method is particularly important in high-dimensional problems, where computational efficiency is crucial, and sample scarcity is a common issue.

5

Proof.

Since the objective functions in (
5
) and in (
7
) are identical (through (
6

and
\mathcal{F}
in problems (

includes a positive semi-definite (PSD) constraint (i.e.,

includes a rank constraint (i.e.,

To address the PSD constraint in problem (

and

is a diagonally dominant matrix with positive diagonal entries. Hence, requiring symmetry is sufficient to ensure that the minimizer of (

Horn and Johnson, 2012

Next, we show that for any graph Laplacian

is non-singular under the considered objective function. To this end, we note that any

admits an eigenvalue decomposition
{\bf{U}}\Lambda_{L}{\bf{U}}^{T}
, where
{\bf{U}}
is

, and

where

are the eigenvalues of

is the number of positive eigenvalues. In addition, the first eigenvector of

(31)

and the eigenvector of

{\bf{L}}
. Hence, for the same eigenvector matrix

are

Generally, iterates of projection methods are obtained by

\delta^{(t+1)}=\Pi_{U}\left\{{\delta^{(t)}+\zeta{\bf{d}}^{(t)}}\right\},

(35)

where
\delta^{(t)}
is the current iterate,
{\bf{d}}^{(t)}

\Pi_{{\bf{v}}+{\bf{w}}\geq{\bf{0}}}\left\{{{\bf{v}}}\right\}=\textrm{max}\left\{{{\bf{v}},-{\bf{w}}}\right\}.

(36)

The step-size
\zeta>0

{\bf{d}}^{(t)}
is
given by

\displaystyle{\bf{d}}^{(t)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}-\nabla_{\delta}f_{N}\left({\delta^{(t)}}\right)&\textrm{if}\quad t=0\\
-\nabla_{\delta}f_{N}\left({\delta^{(t)}}\right)+\beta_{t}{\bf{d}}^{(t-1)}&\textrm{if}\quad t>0\end{array},\right.

where
\beta_{t}
is a scalar and
{f_{N}(\delta^{(t)})}
is the

\beta^{DY}_{t}=\textrm{max}\left\{{0,\frac{\|\nabla_{\delta}f^{(t)}_{N}\|^{2}_{2}}{\left({\nabla_{\delta}f^{(t)}_{N}-\nabla_{\delta}f^{(t-1)}_{N}}\right)^{T}{\bf{d}}^{(t-1)}}}\right\}.

(40)

One remarkable property of the DY method is that it produces a descent direction at each iteration and converges globally for convex problems in the case where linesearch is used
Dai and Yuan, 1999
.

To apply the projected NLCG, the gradient of

(defined in (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla_{\delta}f_{N}=\mathcal{P}^{*}\left({\nabla_{\Delta}f_{N}}\right)=\quad&\mathcal{P}^{*}\left({{\bf{S}}-{\bf{Q}}+{\bf{Q}}\mathcal{P}\left({{\delta}}\right){\bf{Q}}}\right)+2\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}\odot\delta+2\nabla_{\delta}\tilde{\rho}_{mcp}\left({{\bf{w}}+\delta;\lambda}\right),\end{aligned}

(41)

where
\mathcal{P}\left({{\delta}}\right)=\Delta
, and

\mathcal{P}

\left[{\mathcal{P}^{*}\left({{\bf{Y}}}\right)}\right]_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}Y_{ii}+Y_{jj}-Y_{ij}-Y_{ji}

(42)

for
k

C.1
Diagonal Preconditioning

The NLCG method that we use in this work is essentially an accelerated gradient-based method. To further accelerate it with rather minimal computational effort, a diagonal approximation to the Hessian can be used, acting as a preconditioner in the NLCG method. The aim of the diagonal preconditioner is
to properly scale the gradient and speed up the rate of convergence of the iterative inner method for the Newton direction. In this paper, we suggest using the diagonal of the Hessian with respect to
\delta
as a preconditioner with NLCG. Below we show the derivation of this preconditioner.

Our diagonal preconditioner matrix,
{\bf{D}}
, is defined as the diagonal of the Hessian of
f_{N}
. To ease the derivation, we ignore the
\varepsilon
term of (
24

f

{\bf{H}}_{N}=\mathcal{P}^{\top}\left({\bf{Q}}\otimes{\bf{Q}}\right)\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p(p-1)/2\times p(p-1)/2}.

(43)

To get its

diagonal entry for our preconditioner, we multiply
{\bf{H}}_{N}
by a unit vector from both sides:

[{\bf{D}}]_{k,k}=[{\bf{H}}_{N}]_{k,k}=\mathbf{e}_{k}^{T}{\bf{H}}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{k},

where

k

44

[{\bf{H}}_{N}]_{k,k}={\rm Tr}{\left({\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{e}_{k}){\bf{Q}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{e}_{k}){\bf{Q}}}\right)},

(45)

where the matrix
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{e}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a matrix that is essentially the reshape of the
k
-th column of the operator
\mathcal{P}
, if we consider it as a matrix. If we look at the example of (
33

k=1
corresponds to

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{e}_{1})=\begin{bmatrix}\;\;1&-1&0\\
-1&\;\;1&0\\
\;\;0&\;\;0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Note that
k=1
in (

2\times 2
sub-matrix of the indices
(1,2)
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{e}_{1})

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{e}_{k})
results in a matrix of zeros, except a

(i,j)
where
k
corresponds to the pair
(i,j)
according to Definition

is the

{\bf{Q}}
. According to (

\displaystyle[{\bf{H}}_{N}]_{k,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}{\left({(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{e}_{k}){\bf{Q}})^{2}}\right)}

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}{\left({\begin{pmatrix}[{\bf{Q}}]_{i,i}-[{\bf{Q}}]_{j,i}&[{\bf{Q}}]_{i,j}-[{\bf{Q}}]_{j,j}\\

\displaystyle\left({\left[{{\bf{Q}}}\right]_{i,i}+\left[{{\bf{Q}}}\right]_{j,j}-2\left[{{\bf{Q}}}\right]_{i,j}}\right)^{2}=[{\bf{D}}]_{k,k},

where

Appendix D
CONVERGENCE GUARANTEES: PROOF OF THEOREM
2

In this section, we provide theoretical proof for the convergence of our method to a stationary point (Theorem
2
). We note that the MCP penalty is non-convex; hence one typically gets to a local minimum using a monotone series of iterations. Some results in our convergence proof follow
Hsieh et al., 2014
(e.g., Lemma
1
and Lemma
3
). However, in the work
(
Hsieh et al., 2014
)
, the
\ell_{1}
norm is used as a penalty, and the proof heavily relies on its convexity. Here, we had to adapt the proofs for these Lemmas to the concave MCP penalty, which is far from trivial and has not been done before, to the best of our knowledge.

This section is organized as follows. In subsection
D.1
we define the considered setup of proximal methods for MCP regularized penalties. In subsection
D.2
we provide some spectral bounds on our problem, which are needed for the proofs that follow. In subsection
D.3
we provide a lemma proving that our method is a fixed point iteration. In subsection
D.4
we prove that the objective value sufficiently decreases between the iterations, which is the main point needed for the convergence proof. Lastly, in subsection
D.5
we finalize the convergence proof given the previous auxiliary lemmas.

D.1
The Proximal Methods Setup

Let us write the problem (
15
) as follows

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{F}}{\text{minimize }}F({\bf{L}})=f({\bf{L}})+\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right),

(47)

where
f
is the smooth part of the objective, defined in (
16
),

\mathcal{F}
and
\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)
are defined in (
1
) and (
11
), respectively. In the setup of general proximal methods, at each iteration
t
, we minimize the penalized and constrained quadratic objective, as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta^{(t)}=\underset{\Delta\in\mathcal{S}^{p}}{\text{argmin}}

\displaystyle G(\Delta)=f({\bf{L}}^{(t)})+\big\langle\nabla f({\bf{L}}^{(t)}),\Delta\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle_{{\bf{M}}^{(t)}}+\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\Delta;\lambda}\right)

(48)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle({\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\Delta)\in\mathcal{F},

where
{\bf{M}}^{(t)}
is some positive definite matrix that is an approximation of the true Hessian, or the true Hessian itself. In particular, Proximal Gradient Descent uses
{\bf{M}}={\bf{I}}
, while proximal Newton will have
{\bf{M}}=\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}})+\mbox{diag}(\mbox{vec}(\varepsilon))
, as in (
18
) and Algorithm
1
. Once the quadratic problem (
48
) is solved, we apply linesearch as in (
19
), finding a step size
\alpha^{(t)}
that ensures a reduction in the objective
F
. We now prove the convergence of this framework to our Laplacian estimation problem.

Optimality Conditions

We can state that by the definition of
\mathcal{F}
our optimality condition is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0\in\frac{\partial F({\bf{L}}^{*})}{\partial{\bf{L}}^{*}_{i,j}}+\mu_{i,j},&i>j\\
{\bf{L}}^{*}_{j,i}={\bf{L}}^{*}_{i,j}\geq 0,&i\neq j\\
{\bf{L}}^{*}_{i,i}=\sum_{j\neq i}-{\bf{L}}^{*}_{i,j}&i=1,...,p\end{array}\right.,

(49)

where
\frac{\partial F({\bf{L}}^{*})}{\partial{\bf{L}}_{i,j}}=[\nabla f]_{i,j}+\frac{\partial MCP({\bf{L}}^{*}_{ij};\gamma,\lambda)}{\partial{\bf{L}}_{ij}}
is the sub-differential of
F
w.r.t
{\bf{L}}_{i,j}
,
\mu_{i,j}\geq 0
is an inequality Lagrange multiplier for the constraint
{\bf{L}}_{i,j}\leq 0
, and the sub-differential of the MCP function in (
2.2
) is given by

\frac{\partial MCP(x;\gamma,\lambda)}{\partial x}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\in[-\lambda,\lambda]&x=0\\
\lambda\cdot\text{sign}\left({x}\right)-\frac{x}{\gamma}&0<|x|\leq\gamma\lambda\\
0&|x|>\gamma\lambda\end{array}\right..

(50)

If the conditions in (
49
) are fulfilled, then
{\bf{L}}^{*}
is a stationary point of (
15
). Since the MCP penalty is non-convex, we expect (
15
) to have multiple minima, and we show that Algorithm
1
converges to one of them. We initialize our algorithm with
{\bf{L}}^{(0)}\in\mathcal{F}
and keep
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{F}
in all iterations.

D.2
Spectral Bounds

Let us first note that the
-\log\det({\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}})
term in (
15
) acts as a barrier function. That is, if
{\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}}
gets closer to being singular, then
\det({\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}})\to 0
and therefore,
F({\bf{L}})\to\infty
following the
\log
term. Therefore, we can say that as long as
F({\bf{L}}^{(t)})
is bounded, there exists some
\beta_{1}>0
for which
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+{\bf{J}}\succeq\beta_{1}{\bf{I}}
for all
t
. Furthermore, by
Ying et al., 2021
, we have that for every Laplacian matrix
{\bf{L}}

\langle{\bf{S}},{\bf{L}}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle,

(51)

where
{\bf{L}}=\mathcal{P}\mathbf{w},\;\;\mathbf{w}\geq 0
, and

\mathbf{v}_{k}=[\mathcal{P}^{*}{\bf{S}}]_{k}=\left[\mathcal{P}^{*}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{q=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{q}\mathbf{x}_{q}^{T}\right]_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{q=1}^{n}\left([\mathbf{x}_{q}]_{i}-[\mathbf{x}_{q}]_{i}\right)^{2}>0

(52)

holds with a very high probability, and
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{P}
in (
42
). Following that, we have

\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle\geq\min_{j}\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}\sum_{q}{\mathbf{w}_{q}}=\frac{1}{2}\min_{j}\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}{\rm Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}}\right)}.

(53)

Now, for any
{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F({\bf{L}})\leq F({\bf{L}}^{(0)})
we have

{\rm Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}{\bf{S}}}\right)}-\log|{\bf{L}}|_{+}+\rho\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)\leq F({\bf{L}}^{(0)}),

and since
\rho\left({{\bf{L}};\lambda}\right)\geq 0
we can write:

{\rm Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}{\bf{S}}}\right)}\leq F({\bf{L}}^{(0)})+\log|{\bf{L}}|_{+}.

Furthermore, using (
53
) and
\log|{\bf{L}}|_{+}\leq(p-1)\log(\lambda_{max}({\bf{L}}))
, we get

\min_{k}\{\mathbf{v}_{k}\}\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{max}({\bf{L}})\leq\min_{k}\{\mathbf{v}_{k}\}\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}{\left({{\bf{L}}}\right)}\leq F({\bf{L}}^{(0)})+(p-1)\log(\lambda_{max}({\bf{L}})).

(54)

Now, since in (
54
) the left-had-side grows much faster than the right-hand-side, then there is and upper-bound on
\lambda_{max}({\bf{L}})
, depending on
{\bf{S}}
and
{\bf{L}}^{(0)}
, assuming (
52
). Hence, we can conclude that if the iterations
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\}
are monotonically decreasing starting from
{\bf{L}}^{(0)}\in\mathcal{F}
, there exist upper and lower bounds

\beta_{1}{\bf{I}}\preceq{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+{\bf{J}}\preceq\beta_{2}{\bf{I}}.

(55)

This also means that the level set
\mathcal{R}=\{{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{F}:F({\bf{L}})\leq F({\bf{L}}^{(0)})\}
is compact, and the Hessian is bounded, i.e.,
\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}})=({\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}})^{-1}\otimes({\bf{L}}+{\bf{J}})^{-1}\preceq\frac{1}{\beta_{1}^{2}}{\bf{I}}=\theta{\bf{I}}
, where
\theta>0
. The last upper bound comes from the properties of eigenvalues of Kronecker products.

We summarize the conclusions above in the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Assume that the iterations
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}
are monotonically deceasing with respect to the objective in (
47
), starting from a feasible
{\bf{L}}^{(0)}
. Then there exist constants
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
such that
\beta_{1}{\bf{I}}\preceq{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+{\bf{J}}\preceq\beta_{2}{\bf{I}}
, and the level-set
\mathcal{R}=\{{\bf{L}}\in\mathcal{F}:F({\bf{L}})\leq F({\bf{L}}^{(0)})\}
is compact. Also, the Hessian is bounded:
\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}})\preceq\frac{1}{\beta_{1}^{2}}{\bf{I}}=\theta{\bf{I}}

D.3
Fixed Point Iteration

The following lemma shows that if
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}
is feasible, and the quadratic minimization (
48

\Delta^{(t)}=0
, then
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}
is a minimum point of (
47
).

Lemma 2
.

Assume that
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{F}
. If
\Delta^{(t)}=0
is a minimizer of

in (

{\bf{L}}^{(t+1)}={\bf{L}}^{(t)}

in (

Proof.

Since
\Delta^{(t)}
is a minimizer of (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0\in\frac{\partial G(\Delta^{(t)})}{\partial\Delta_{i,j}}+\mu_{i,j},&i>j\\

(56)

where
\frac{\partial G(\Delta)}{\partial\Delta_{i,j}}=[\nabla f]_{i,j}+[\langle{\bf{M}}\Delta\rangle]_{i,j}+\frac{\partial MCP({\bf{L}}^{(t)}_{i,j}+\Delta_{i,j};\gamma,\lambda)}{\partial\Delta_{ij}}
, and
\langle{\bf{M}}\Delta\rangle

{\bf{M}}
with the vectorized
\Delta

\mbox{mat}({\bf{M}}\cdot\mbox{vec}(\Delta))

D.4
Sufficient Decrease in Objective

We now show that we have a decrease in the objective
F

Lemma 3

\\
\quad\quad\leq\langle\nabla f({\bf{L}}^{(t)}),\alpha\Delta^{(t)}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha\Delta^{(t)},\alpha\Delta^{(t)}\rangle_{{\bf{M}}^{(t)}}+\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta^{(t)};\lambda}\right).\end{array}

(58)

(59)

After dividing by

\big\langle\nabla f({\bf{L}}^{(t)}),\Delta\big\rangle\leq-(1+\alpha)\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle_{{\bf{M}}^{(t)}}-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\Delta;\lambda}\right)-\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta;\lambda}\right)\right).

\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta;\lambda}\right)=\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)};\lambda}\right)+\langle\nabla\rho_{MCP},\alpha\Delta\rangle-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\langle\Omega_{\alpha}\odot\alpha\Delta,\Omega_{\alpha}\odot\alpha\Delta\rangle,

\displaystyle F({\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta^{(t)})-F({\bf{L}}^{(t)})=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f({\bf{L}}^{(t)}),\alpha\Delta^{(t)}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha\Delta^{(t)},\alpha\Delta^{(t)}\rangle_{\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}}^{(t)})}+O(\|\Delta\|^{3})

\displaystyle+\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta;\lambda}\right)-\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)};\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha(1+\alpha)}{2}\big\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle_{{\bf{M}}^{(t)}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha\Delta^{(t)},\alpha\Delta^{(t)}\rangle_{\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}}^{(t)})}+O(\|\Delta\|^{3})

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left(\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\Delta;\lambda}\right)-\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta;\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta;\lambda}\right)-\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)};\lambda}\right).

Equation (

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left(\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\Delta;\lambda}\right)-\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta;\lambda}\right)\right)+\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta;\lambda}\right)-\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)};\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left((1-\alpha+\alpha)\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta;\lambda}\right)-\alpha\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\Delta;\lambda}\right)\right)-\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)};\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left((1-\alpha)\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)};\lambda}\right)-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\langle\alpha^{2}\Omega_{\alpha}^{2}-\alpha\Omega^{2}\odot\Delta,\Delta\rangle\right)-\rho_{MCP}\left({{\bf{L}}^{(t)};\lambda}\right)

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\langle\alpha^{2}\Omega_{\alpha}^{2}-\alpha\Omega^{2}\odot\Delta,\Delta\rangle\right)\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\langle\max\{\Omega_{\alpha}^{2},\Omega^{2}\}\odot\Delta,\Delta\rangle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle,

where
\Omega_{\alpha}
and

66
) we get:

\displaystyle F({\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta^{(t)})-F({\bf{L}}^{(t)})\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha(1+\alpha)}{2}\big\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle_{{\bf{M}}^{(t)}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha\Delta^{(t)},\alpha\Delta^{(t)}\rangle_{\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}}^{(t)})}+\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle+O(\|\Delta\|^{3})

(71)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{2}\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle_{({\bf{M}}^{(t)}-\gamma^{-1}{\bf{I}})}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle_{({\bf{M}}^{(t)}-\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}}^{(t)}))}+O(\|\Delta\|^{3}).

(72)

Two conditions are needed to keep the above bound negative and guarantee a decrease in the objective. One is by having
{\bf{M}}^{(t)}\succ\gamma^{-1}{\bf{I}}
, which is easy to guarantee, e.g., via the choice of
\varepsilon
in our case. The other condition, assuming that we have
{\bf{M}}^{(t)}\succ\gamma^{-1}{\bf{I}}
, can be written as

\alpha({\bf{M}}-\gamma^{-1})+\alpha^{2}\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}}^{(t)})\succ 0,

and then we have

F({\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta^{(t)})-F({\bf{L}}^{(t)})\leq-\alpha(\lambda_{min}^{{\bf{M}}}-\frac{1}{\gamma}-\alpha\theta)\|\Delta^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2}.

(73)

where
\theta
is the upper bound on the Hessian, and
\lambda_{min}^{{\bf{M}}}>\gamma^{-1}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
{\bf{M}}
. This value is negative for any

0\leq\alpha_{min}<\frac{\lambda_{min}^{{\bf{M}}}-\gamma^{-1}}{\theta}
.
∎

The result above is relevant to Algorithm
1
, where we have
{\bf{M}}^{(t)}=\nabla^{2}f({\bf{L}}^{(t)})+\mbox{diag}(\epsilon^{2})
. Hence, the second term in (
72
) almost vanishes if
\epsilon
is small. On the other hand,
\epsilon
allows us to make sure that the first term in (
72
) remains negative. We note that one can also monitor which entries are in the concave region of the MCP (smaller than
\gamma\lambda
in magnitude) and set
\varepsilon_{i,j}=\gamma^{-1}
only for them, following (
70
).

The analysis above essentially is also suitable for Algorithm
2
if we set
\beta_{t}=0
, i.e., use a projected quasi-Newton method with
{\bf{D}}
as a diagonal approximation to the Hessian.
This is because in Algorithm
2
, the Newton problem is also a smooth objective with an MCP prior and positivity constraints, and the change of variables to
\delta
is only for convenience of symmetry and does not change the problem.

D.5
Convergence

Before stating the main convergence theorem, we prove the following auxiliary lemma:

Lemma 4
.

Let
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\}
be series of points produced by (
48
) followed by the linesearch (
19
), with
\lambda^{{\bf{M}}}_{max}{\bf{I}}\preceq{\bf{M}}^{(t)}\preceq\lambda^{{\bf{M}}}_{min}{\bf{I}}\preceq 0
. Let
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t_{j})}\}
be any converging subseries of
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\}
and let
\bar{\bf{L}}
be its limit. Then
\bar{\bf{L}}
is a stationary point of
F
in (
15
).

Proof.

Since the sub-series
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t_{j})}\}
convergences to
\bar{{\bf{L}}}
, then
\{F({\bf{L}}^{(t_{j})})\}
convergences to
F(\bar{{\bf{L}}})
. Therefore,
\{F({\bf{L}}^{(t_{j}+1)})-F({\bf{L}}^{(t_{j})})\}\rightarrow 0
, which implies following (
73
) that
||{\bf{L}}^{(t_{j})}-{\bf{L}}^{(t_{j}+1)}||^{2}_{F}\rightarrow 0
and
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}{\bf{L}}^{(t_{j}+1)}=\bar{{\bf{L}}}
. According to (
48
) and the linesearch (
19
),
{\bf{L}}^{(t+1)}={\bf{L}}^{(t)}+\alpha^{(t)}\Delta^{(t)}
and because,
||{\bf{L}}^{(t_{j}+1)}-{\bf{L}}^{(t_{j})}||^{2}_{F}\rightarrow 0
and
\alpha\geq\alpha_{min}>0
, this implies
||\Delta^{(t)}||^{2}_{F}\rightarrow 0
. We know that
\Delta^{(t)}
satisfies (
56
), and by taking the limit as
j\rightarrow\infty
and using Lemma
2
we get that
\bar{{\bf{L}}}
is a stationary point of (
15
).
∎

Now, after providing the auxiliary lemmas above, we are ready to state the convergence theorem.

Theorem 2.

Let
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\}
be a series of points produced by a proximal quadratic minimization, constrained by
\mathcal{F}
with some SPD matrices
0\prec\lambda^{{\bf{M}}}_{min}{\bf{I}}\preceq{\bf{M}}^{(t)}\preceq\lambda^{{\bf{M}}}_{max}{\bf{I}}
as the Hessian (as in (
48
)), followed by the linesearch (
19
), starting from
{\bf{L}}^{(0)}\in\mathcal{F}
. Then any limit point
\bar{\bf{L}}
of
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\}
is a stationary point of (
15
).

Proof.

By Lemma
3
, the series
F({\bf{L}}^{(t)})
is monotonically decreasing, hence it is also convergent as
F
is bounded from below. Since the level set
\mathcal{R}
is compact by Lemma
1
, and
{\bf{L}}^{(t)}
is bounded in
\mathcal{R}
, there is a converging subseries
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t_{j})}\}
, which let
\bar{\bf{L}}
be its limit point. By Lemma
4
,
\bar{\bf{L}}
is a stationary point of
F
in (
15
). Since
F
is continuous then
F({\bf{L}}^{(t_{j})})\longrightarrow F(\bar{\bf{L}})
. Since the limit of
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\}
and
F({\bf{L}}^{(t)})
equal to that of any of their corresponding subseries, then
\{{\bf{L}}^{(t)}\}\longrightarrow\bar{\bf{L}}
and
F({\bf{L}}^{(t)})\longrightarrow F(\bar{\bf{L}})
.
∎
