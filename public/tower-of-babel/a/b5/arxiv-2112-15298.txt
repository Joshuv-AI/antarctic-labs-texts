Energetic Formulation of Large-Deformation Poroelasticity Preprint: To appear in International Journal for Numerical and Analytical Methods in Geomechanics Preprint: DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/nag.3326
Mina Karimi, Mehrdad Massoudi, Noel Walkington, Matteo Pozzi, Kaushik Dayal

Abstract
The modeling of coupled fluid transport and deformation in a porous medium is essential to predict the various geomechanical process such as CO2 sequestration, hydraulic fracturing, and so on. Current applications of interest, for instance, that include fracturing or damage of the solid phase, require a nonlinear description of the large deformations that can occur. This paper presents a variational energy-based continuum mechanics framework to model large-deformation poroelasticity. The approach begins from the total free energy density that is additively composed of the free energy of the components. A variational procedure then provides the balance of momentum, fluid transport balance, and pressure relations. A numerical approach based on finite elements is applied to analyze the behavior of saturated and unsaturated porous media using a nonlinear constitutive model for the solid skeleton. Examples studied include the Terzaghi and Mandel problems; a gas-liquid phase-changing fluid; multiple immiscible gases; and unsaturated systems where we model injection of fluid into soil. The proposed variational approach can potentially have advantages for numerical methods as well as for combining with data-driven models in a Bayesian framework.

Unsaturated Systems.

Consider an unsaturated medium with a solid phase, a incompressible fluid phase (e.g. water), and an compressible fluid phase (e.g. air).
The state variables for the energy are
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F},P_{0I},P_{0c},\phi_{I},\phi_{c}
,
where the subscripts
\displaystyle c

\displaystyle I

For the incompressible fluid phase, we require that the true density in the current configuration has a fixed value
\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{I}

\frac{P_{0I}}{J\phi_{I}}=\tilde{\rho}_{I}

(28)

We notice that the energy is constant for an incompressible fluid: all states that satisfy incompressibility have the same energy, and all other states are inadmissible (i.e., have infinite energy).
Since this energy is a constant, it will not appear once we take variations and consequently we can set it to
\displaystyle 0
for simplicity.

Therefore, we can write the total energy density as:

W_{0}({\mathbfit F},P_{0I},P_{0c},\phi_{I},\phi_{c})=\phi_{0s}W_{0s}({\mathbfit F})+\phi_{0c}W_{0c}(J,\phi_{c},P_{0c})

(29)

We highlight some issues.
First, if we further assume that the solid skeleton is incompressible and affinely deformed by
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
, this imposes the additional constraint
\displaystyle J=1
.
Second, we notice that the incompressibility constraint is generally not satisfied in the reference configuration, i.e.
\displaystyle\rho_{0I}\neq\tilde{\rho}_{I}
in general.
Third, it is sometimes reasonable to neglect the energy of the compressible fluid (e.g., air at low pressure in rock); in that case, we need only consider the energy of the solid.
However, we emphasize that the problem still contains the influence of the fluid through the mass transport equations (

.

Consider a porous medium with a compressible solid phase and a compressible fluid phase (Figure
3
).
Let
\displaystyle\phi_{0f}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{0s}
be the referential volume fractions of fluid and solid phases, respectively.
The deformation of the solid phase is assumed to be affine with the macroscopic deformation gradient
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
.
This implies that
\displaystyle\phi_{0s}=\phi_{s}
as follows.
We have the following relation between current and reference volume elements for the entire RVE as well as for the solid skeleton:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \mathrm{d}\Omega=J\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{0}

(30)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{s}=J\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{0s}

Further, from the definition of the volume fraction of the solid skeleton, we have:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{s}=\phi_{s}\ \mathrm{d}\Omega=\phi_{s}J\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{0}

(31)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{0s}=\phi_{0s}\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{0}

\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{0}=J\phi_{0s}\ \mathrm{d}\Omega_{0}

\displaystyle\phi_{0s}=\phi_{s}
.

Further, since
\displaystyle\phi_{f}=1-\phi_{s}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{0f}=1-\phi_{0s}

\displaystyle\phi_{0f}=\phi_{f}
.

Model Verification with Terzaghi’s and Mandel’s Problems

In this section, we verify the model by investigating its response in the model consolidation problems proposed by Terzaghi and Mandel.
The general form of the strain energy density for a saturated porous medium with an incompressible solid phase and compressible fluid phase is:

W_{0}=(1-\phi_{0f})W_{0s}({\mathbfit F})+\phi_{0f}W_{0f}(J,P_{0f},\phi_{f})

(33)

Following section
2.D
, the first Piola stress tensor can be derived as:

{\mathbfit T}=(1-\phi_{0f})\frac{\partial W_{0s}}{\partial{\mathbfit F}}+\phi_{0f}\frac{\partial W_{0f}}{\partial J}J{\mathbfit F}^{-T}

(34)

Using (
5
) and (
21
), we can write:

\frac{\partial W_{0f}}{\partial J}=-p\frac{\phi_{f}}{\phi_{0f}}

(35)

Thus, the Cauchy stress tensor can be written as follows:

\mathbfit{\sigma}=(1-\phi_{0f})J^{-1}{\mathbfit F}^{T}\frac{\partial W_{0s}}{\partial{\mathbfit F}}-\phi_{f}p{\mathbfit I}

(36)

Equation (
36
) shows that the total Cauchy stress is obtained by a summation of the solid and fluid partial stresses
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}=\mathbfit{\sigma}_{s}+\mathbfit{\sigma}_{f}
, where
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}_{f}=-\phi_{f}p{\mathbfit I}
is an isotropic stress tensor. In addition, the general expression of the Cauchy effective stress tensor is
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}^{\prime}=\mathbfit{\sigma}+p{\mathbfit I}
, which can be written as:

\mathbfit{\sigma}^{\prime}=(1-\phi_{f})\left({\mathbfit F}^{T}\frac{\partial W_{0s}}{\partial{\mathbfit F}}+p{\mathbfit I}\right)

(37)

Assuming that the deformation of the solid phase is small and that the response is isotropic, we linearize it to get:

\mathbfit{\sigma}=(1-\phi_{f})\left(2\mu\mathbfit{\epsilon}+\lambda\trace(\mathbfit{\epsilon}){\mathbfit I}\right)-\phi_{f}p{\mathbfit I}

(38)

where
\displaystyle\trace
denotes the trace operator.
We then define the renormalized Lame constants
\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}=(1-\phi_{0f})\mu
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}=(1-\phi_{0f})\lambda
.
We define
\displaystyle\tilde{p}=\phi_{f}p
and
\displaystyle p
as the total pressure and the intrinsic fluid pressure, respectively.

Following section
2.C
, the conservation of mass for the fluid phase can be written as:

\frac{\ \mathrm{d}}{\ \mathrm{d}t}P_{f}J=-J\divergence{\mathbfit q}

(39)

Following
Bor 05
, the bulk modulus of the fluid phase can be written:

K_{f}=\rho_{f}\frac{\ \mathrm{d}{p}}{\ \mathrm{d}\rho_{f}}

(40)

We recall further that:

P_{f}=\phi_{f}\rho_{f}

(41)

Using (
40
) and (
41
), we can rewrite the conservation of mass for the fluid and solid phases respectively as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ \mathrm{d}J}{\ \mathrm{d}t}\frac{\phi_{f}}{J}+\frac{\ \mathrm{d}\phi_{f}}{\ \mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\ \mathrm{d}p}{\ \mathrm{d}t}\frac{\phi_{f}}{K_{f}}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{f}}\divergence{\mathbfit q}

(42)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ \mathrm{d}J}{\ \mathrm{d}t}\frac{\phi_{s}}{J}+\frac{\ \mathrm{d}\phi_{s}}{\ \mathrm{d}t}=0

(43)

The second equation, for the solid phase, is derived in the same way as the equation (
42
) for the fluid phase, but also using that there is no transport of the solid phase and that the solid phase is incompressible (the bulk modulus
\displaystyle K_{s}\to\infty
).

Adding (
42
) and (
43
) yields the balance of mass of the porous system as follows:

\frac{\ \mathrm{d}J}{\ \mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{J}+\frac{\ \mathrm{d}p}{\ \mathrm{d}t}\frac{\phi_{f}}{K_{f}}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{f}}\divergence{\mathbfit q}

(44)

Ignoring the effect of gravity and using (
2
) and (
6

\displaystyle\displaystyle\eta_{0}=\frac{\partial W_{f}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}

(45)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial\eta_{0}}=-P_{f}

(46)

Using these equations and considering small deformations, (
44
) can be written as:

\frac{\ \mathrm{d}\trace(\mathbfit{\epsilon})}{\ \mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\ \mathrm{d}p}{\ \mathrm{d}t}\frac{\phi_{f}}{K_{f}}=\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}\nabla^{2}{p}

(47)

Concluding Remarks

In this study, we have proposed a variational energy-based continuum mechanics framework in the large deformation regime to model coupled fluid transport and deformation in a porous deformable medium.
The free energy density function of the porous medium is defined as additively composed of the free-energies of the solid and fluid phases of the medium.
The variational structure provides for the standard use of the Finite Element Method to solve the proposed model; in general, variational approaches can provide important advantages for numerical solution
OR 12
.

The proposed formulation was tested on two benchmark problems, namely Terzaghi’s 1-d and Mandel’s 2-d consolidation problems.
Further, the model enabled the modeling of porous media containing a single (gas-liquid phase transforming) and multiple immiscible fluid phases.
Furthermore, the method was used to model the behavior of an unsaturated porous medium.
As a specific application of the proposed model, we investigated fluid injection into soil.

An important advantage of the variational formulation, that we are currently investigating and will be reported in the future, is in enabling an overall variational structure to the problem of combining a physics-based approach with a data-driven approach through a Bayesian framework.
In addition, plastic effects are important in many realistic situations; it would therefore be useful to extend this model to incorporate powerful variational models for plasticity that have been proposed, e.g.
WMO 06
;
OP 04
.
