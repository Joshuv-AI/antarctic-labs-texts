Cost of quantum secret key
Karol Horodecki, Leonard Sikorski, Siddhartha Das, Mark M. Wilde

Abstract
In this paper, we develop the resource theory of quantum secret key. Operating under the assumption that entangled states with zero distillable key do not exist, we define the key cost of a quantum state, and device. We study its properties through the lens of a quantity that we call the key of formation . The main result of our paper is that the regularized key of formation is an upper bound on the key cost of a quantum state. The core protocol underlying this result is privacy dilution, which converts states containing ideal privacy into ones with diluted privacy. Next, we show that the key cost is bounded from below by the regularized relative entropy of entanglement, which implies the irreversibility of the privacy creation-distillation process for a specific class of states. We further focus on mixed-state analogues of pure quantum states in the domain of privacy, and we prove that a number of entanglement measures are equal to each other for these states, similar to the case of pure entangled states. The privacy cost and distillable key in the single-shot regime exhibit a yield-cost relation, and basic consequences for quantum devices are also provided.



Introduction
.—Quantum mechanics is a rather surprising physical theory, as it is reversible in principle, contrary to our common experience. After an arbitrary quantum operation is applied to a system, if full access to its environment is available, the system’s state can be set back to its initial form by a reversal operation.
However, in practice, reversibility is usually not possible. The system, after an operation, typically becomes entangled with an inaccessible environment in an irreversible way.

Dilution protocol

Conclusion
.—We have introduced two quantities central to the resource theory of privacy: private key cost and private key of formation. We have done so assuming the faithfulness of the distillable key (i.e., non-existance of entangled states with zero distillable key). One of our fundamental contributions is that the regularized key of formation of a bipartite state upper bounds the key cost
(K_{F}^{\infty}\geq K_{C})
. Equality holds (i.e.,
K_{F}^{\infty}=K_{C}
) if
K_{F}
is an asymptotically continuous entanglement monotone. While we have shown the monotonicity of
K_{F}
with respect to some basic operations (see Section
IV
of the SM), the monotonicity (on average) under von Neumann measurements and asymptotic continuity beyond trivial cases, remain important open problems.
The importance of these problems also stems from the fact that they would imply non-trivial results for the key cost of quantum channels.

It is striking that, while showing a function to be entanglement monotone is standard by now

is an asymptotically continuous LOCC monotone. To give one example, the method of

We have designed a privacy dilution protocol (DP) that transforms an ideal private state into a generalized private state by LOCC. Our protocol uses at least

rate of privacy in the form of pure entanglement. It remains open to determine if pure entanglement is actually needed for this task.
It is worth noticing that whenever the twisting unitaries,

.

There is an important question of how to design
practical protocols that achieve these limits.
It is also of prime importance to compute
K_{C}
for some states other than generalized private states.

We also consider it salient to generalize our findings to the settings of conference key agreement

;

.

We stress that, from a purely mathematical perspective,
rather than from an operational viewpoint, the presented results remain valid even if entangled but zero distillable key states were to
exist. The existence of entangled but zero distillable key states would result only in a redefinition
of the key cost to a novel quantity, say

Before we state the main result of this section, we introduce our definition of the asymptotic key cost

and one-shot key cost
K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho)
of a bipartite quantum state.

Definition 4
.

The asymptotic key cost
K_{C}(\rho)
and one-shot key cost
K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho)
of a state
\rho_{AB}
are defined as

\displaystyle K_{C}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\otimes n}),

(S41)

\displaystyle K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{L}\in\operatorname{LOCC},\\
\gamma_{d_{k},d_{s}}\in\operatorname{SIR}\end{subarray}}\left\{\begin{array}[c]{c}\log_{2}d_{k}:\\
\frac{1}{2}\norm{\mc{L}(\gamma_{d_k,d_s})- \rho}_{1}\leq\varepsilon\end{array}\right\},

where the infimum is taken over every LOCC channel
\mathcal{L}
and every strictly irreducible private state
\gamma_{d_{k},d_{s}}
with arbitrarily large, finite shield dimension
d_{s}\geq 1
.

To exhibit irreversibility in the privacy distillation-creation process, we use standard techniques to prove that the regularized relative entropy of entanglement bounds the key cost from below.

Theorem 2
.

For every bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
, the following bound holds

K_{C}(\rho)\geq E^{\infty}_{R}(\rho),

(S44)

where
E^{\infty}_{R}(\rho)
is defined in (
3
).

Proof.

We follow here some standard arguments used in quantum resource theories. Let us begin by giving the basic idea of the proof.
We first note that the relative entropy of entanglement of an irreducible private state
\gamma_{k}

is bounded from above by
k
, i.e.,
E_{R}(\gamma_{k})\leq k
(see Theorem 2 in
IR-pbits
).
We then note that under the action of an LOCC channel
\mathcal{L}
that approximately generates the target state
\rho^{\otimes n}
, the relative entropy does not increase, and for a separable state
\sigma
,

\mathcal{L}(\sigma)=\sigma^{\prime}\in\operatorname{SEP}
. Furthermore, asymptotic continuity
of the relative entropy of entanglement corresponds to the following inequality:

|E_{R}(\mathcal{L}(\gamma_{k}))-E_{R}(\rho^{\otimes n})|\leq f(\varepsilon,d,n),

(S45)

where

\displaystyle f(\varepsilon,d,n)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\varepsilon\log_{2}d^{n}+g(\varepsilon),

(S46)

\displaystyle g(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\varepsilon+1)\log_{2}(\varepsilon+1)-\varepsilon\log_{2}\varepsilon.

(S47)

Regularization of the considered quantity by
n
finishes
the proof.

We state these steps formally now. Consider that

\displaystyle\log_{2}d_{k}

\displaystyle\geq E_{R}(\gamma_{d_{k}})

(S48)

\displaystyle\geq E_{R}(\mathcal{L}(\gamma_{d_{k}}))

(S49)

\displaystyle\geq E_{R}(\rho^{\otimes n})-f(\varepsilon,d,n),

(S50)

where we have applied
(
IR-pbits
, Theorem 2)
for the first inequality, LOCC monotonicity of the relative entropy of entanglement for the second inequality, and asymptotic continuity of the relative entropy of entanglement
(
W16
, Corollary 8)
for the third.
Since the above inequalities hold for each
\mathcal{L}\in\operatorname{LOCC}
and
\gamma_{d_{k}}\in\operatorname{SIR}
such that
\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{L}(\gamma_{d_{k}})-\rho^{\otimes n}\right\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon
and
K_{C}^{\varepsilon}
is defined to be the infimum of
\log_{2}d_{k}
over all such
\mathcal{L}
and
\gamma_{d_{k}}
, then we conclude that

\displaystyle K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\otimes n})\geq E_{R}(\rho^{\otimes n})-f(\varepsilon,d,n).

(S51)

After dividing by
n
and applying the definition of key cost and the regularized relative entropy of entanglement, we conclude that

\displaystyle K_{C}(\rho)=\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle\geq E^{\infty}_{R}(\rho).

(S52)

This concludes the proof.
∎

As an important consequence, it follows that the process of privacy creation is irreversible.
Indeed in
CSW12
(see also
BCHW
) it was shown that there are states
\widehat{\rho}
satisfying
E_{\operatorname{sq}}\ll E_{R}^{\infty}
, where

E_{\operatorname{sq}}
is the squashed entanglement measure defined in (
S17
). We then have

K_{D}(\widehat{\rho})\leq E_{\operatorname{sq}}(\widehat{\rho})\ll E_{R}^{\infty}(\widehat{\rho})\leq K_{C}(\widehat{\rho}),

(S53)

which implies that
K_{D}(\widehat{\rho})\ll K_{C}(\widehat{\rho})
.
The above inequality is the first example of irreversibility in the case of privacy.

Remark 1
.

We note here that a simplified version of the definition of
K_{C}
is to consider only those states that take the form of the so-called Bell private states
FerraraChristandl
whenever they are strictly irreducible.
A Bell private state takes the form

\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\Phi_i}{\Phi_i}_{AB}\otimes\rho^{(i)}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}

(S54)

where
\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}^{(i)}
are arbitrary, yet mutually orthogonal states on
A^{\prime}B^{\prime}
, and the states
\outerproduct{\Phi_i}{\Phi_i}
are Bell states of local dimension
d_{k}
generated
from
|\Phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\sum_{i=0}^{d_{k}-1}|ii\rangle
by the local rotations

X^{m}{Z}^{j}=\left(\sum_{l^{\prime}=0}^{d_{k}-1}|m\oplus l^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle l^{\prime}|\right)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{d_{k}-1}e^{2\pi ijl/d_{k}}|l\rangle\!\langle l|\right).

(S55)

This simplification comes from the fact (see Lemma 1 of Ref.
FerraraChristandl
) that a one-way LOCC channel can reversibly transform any private state to a Bell private state.

IV
Key of formation

Here, we generalize the notion of entanglement of formation to the case
of the key of formation. We will need
a notion corresponding to a pure state
in the domain of private states. For this purpose,
we consider irreducible generalized private states
\gamma_{AA^{\prime}BB^{\prime}}(\psi_{AB})
(see (
S33
)). We denote such a state by
\gamma(\psi)
, where
\psi
is the pure state under consideration.

We note that subsystem
AB
, i.e., the key part of a private state, is not the same for different private states as they can differ by some product
W_{AA^{\prime}}\otimes W_{BB^{\prime}}
of local unitary
transformations, and the key part of one private state can be of a different dimension than the one for the other private state.
For this reason, by the
key

A_{K}
(similarly
B_{K}
), we will mean the one from which after a von Neumann measurement in some product basis
\{|e_{i}\rangle\}_{i}
(
\{|f_{i}\rangle\}_{i}
) the whole key of the generalized private state given in (
S33
)
is obtained.
The key of formation is then defined as follows:

Definition 5
.

The key of formation of a bipartite state
\rho
is defined as

\displaystyle K_{F}(\rho)\coloneqq\inf_{\scriptsize\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\gamma(\psi_{k})=\rho,\\
\gamma(\psi_{k})\in\operatorname{GSIR}\end{array}\right\}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}S_{A_{K}}[\gamma(\psi_{k})],

where
\gamma(\psi_{k})
is a strictly irreducible generalized private state and
S(\cdot)
denotes the von Neumann entropy. Additionally, the infimum is taken over finite, yet arbitrarily large
K
.

As a first step, we show that in the above definition, the infimum can be taken over ensembles with at most
(|A||B|)^{2}+1
elements. This technical result is crucial in proving
the main result in Theorem
4
.

Lemma 1
.

For a state
\rho_{AB}
, the infimum in Definition
5
of
K_{F}(\rho_{AB})
can
be taken on ensembles with a number of elements at most
(|A|\times|B|)^{2}+1
.

Proof.

We closely follow the idea of Proposition 3 in
Yang2005
. Consider a decomposition of
\rho_{AB}
into a finite number of states, as
\rho_{AB}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\gamma(\psi_{k})
, with
K>D+1
and
D\coloneqq(|A|\times|B|)^{2}
. We aim to find a decomposition
\rho_{AB}=\sum_{k=1}^{D+1}q_{k}\widetilde{\gamma}(\psi_{k})
, where
\{q_{k}\}_{k}
is a probability distribution. One can construct a polytope
C(S)
, which is the convex hull of the set

S\coloneqq\{(\gamma(\psi_{k}),S_{A_{K}}(\gamma(\psi_{k}))\}_{k=1}^{K}.

(S58)

The set
C(S)
is convex by definition and a proper subset of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. This holds because private states are spanned by Hermitian operators and have unit trace, and one more parameter corresponds to the value of
S_{A_{K}}(\gamma(\psi_{k}))
. Now, by definition

y\coloneqq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\gamma(\psi_{k}),\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}S_{A_{K}}(\gamma(\psi_{k}))\right)\in C(S).

(S59)

It is then important to note that the extremal points of
C(S)
are contained within the set
S
. Hence by Caratheodory’s theorem, there exist at most
D+1
extremal points
\{(\widetilde{\gamma}(\psi_{j}),S_{A_{K}}(\widetilde{\gamma}(\psi_{j}))\}_{j=1}^{D}\subseteq S
in
C(S)
such that

y=\sum_{j=1}^{D+1}q_{j}(\widetilde{\gamma}(\psi_{j}),S_{A_{K}}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}))
, which implies the statement of the lemma.
∎

Note that, while we show above that the infimum in the definition of
K_{F}
can be taken over ensembles of bounded size, it remains open to determine if the infimum is attained. In what follows, we will use that
K_{F}
, constructed by the convex-roof method, is a convex function. We prove it below for the sake of the completeness of the presentation.

Lemma 2
.

K_{F}
is a convex function.

Proof.

Let
\sum_{k}^{K}p_{k}\rho_{k}
be a convex mixture of quantum states and let
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{K}>0
. Since
K_{F}
is by the definition an infimum over ensembles into generalised strictly irreducible private states, then for each
\rho_{k}
there exist an ensemble
\{(q_{kl},\gamma_{kl})\}_{l}
such that
\sum_{l}q_{kl}\gamma_{kl}=\rho_{k}
and
\sum_{l}q_{kl}S_{A_{K}}[\gamma_{kl}]\leq K_{F}(\rho_{k})+\eta_{k}
. Now observe that
\sum_{k,l}p_{k}q_{kl}\gamma_{kl}
is a valid decomposition into generalised strictly irreducible private states of the state
\sum_{k}^{K}p_{k}\rho_{k}
. Again, since
K_{F}
is an infimum we obtain

\displaystyle K_{F}\!\left(\sum_{k}p_{k}\rho_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k,l}p_{k}q_{kl}S_{A_{K}}[\gamma_{kl}]\leq

(S60)

\displaystyle\sum_{k}p_{k}(K_{F}(\rho_{k})+\eta_{k})=\sum_{k}p_{k}K_{F}(\rho_{k})+\sum_{k}p_{k}\eta_{k}.

Since it holds true for every
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{K}>0
, it follows that

K_{F}\!\left(\sum_{k}p_{k}\rho_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k}p_{k}K_{F}(\rho_{k}),

(S61)

concluding the proof.
∎

IV.1
Monotonicity of the key of formation under some elementary operations

In this section, we prove that
K_{F}
is monotonic
under certain LOCC operations. We note first that it is easy to observe that the key of formation does not increase after local measurements on pure states.

Observation 1
.

K_{F}
does not increase on pure states under LOCC.

Proof.

We note that, for a pure state,
K_{F}=K_{D}=E_{F}
by definition. Since
E_{F}
is an LOCC monotone, we have

E_{F}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|_{AB})\geq\sum_{k}p_{k}E_{F}(\sigma_{AB}^{k}),

(S62)

where
k
is an outcome of the LOCC operation,
p_{k}
is the probability of outcome
k
, and
\sigma_{AB}^{k}
is a state given that outcome
k
was obtained. Combining the above statements, we obtain

\displaystyle K_{F}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|_{AB})

\displaystyle=E_{F}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|_{AB})

(S63)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k}p_{k}E_{F}(\sigma_{AB}^{k})

(S64)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k}p_{k}K_{F}(\sigma_{AB}^{k}).

(S65)

Here the equality comes from the fact that
|\psi\rangle
is pure; hence it cannot be expressed as a mixture of a non-pure members ensemble such as non-pure generalized private states. The first inequality follows from the monotonicity of
E_{F}
under LOCC. The second inequality follows from the definition, i.e.,
K_{F}\leq E_{F}
for all states.
∎

We note, however, that in general,
E_{F}
can be much larger than
K_{F}
for a private
state. To give an example, consider the state provided in
smallkey
and recalled explicitly below:

\gamma_{2,d_{s}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\sigma&0&0&\frac{1}{d_{s}}U^{T}\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
\frac{1}{d_{s}}U^{*}&0&0&\sigma\end{array}\right],

(S66)

where

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d_{s}-1}u_{ij}|ii\rangle\!\langle jj|,

(S67)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{s}}\sum_{i=0}^{d_{s}-1}|ii\rangle\!\langle ii|,

(S68)

with the matrix
\sum_{ij}u_{ij}|i\rangle\!\langle j|
a unitary transformation.
In
locking
, it was shown that the above state satisfies

E_{C}(\gamma_{2,d_{s}})\geq\frac{\log_{2}d_{s}}{2},

(S69)

while (as this state is a strictly irreducible private state), from Corollary
1
, its asymptotic key cost equals
\log_{2}d_{k}=1
. Hence, whenever
d_{s}>4
, the
entanglement cost exceeds the key cost of this state.
Now, since
E_{F}\geq E_{F}^{\infty}=E_{C}
, we have
that for the above state
E_{F}(\gamma_{2,d_{s}})>K_{F}(\gamma_{2,d_{s}})
for
d_{s}\geq 4
.
Due to this strict inequality holding, we cannot prove monotonicity of
K_{F}
for mixed states by the same approach given in the proof of Observation
1
.

We do not provide a general proof of monotonicity under LOCC operations; however, here we present partial results. In
Horodecki2009
, it is stated that any convex-roof type measure, to which
K_{F}
belongs to,
is montonic under LOCC if and only if it is montonic under local measurements on average. However for the sake of completness, we show here explicitly the proofs of monotonicity of
K_{F}
under all other elementary operations, including adding a local auxiliary state, local partial trace, and random unitary channels.

Observation 2
.

If
K_{F}
is non-increasing under the action of some operation
\Lambda\in\operatorname{LOCC}
on strictly irreducible generalised private states, then it is non-increasing under the action of
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be an arbitrary bipartite state. Since
K_{F}
is equal to the infimum over ensembles of
\rho
into strictly irreducible generalised private states, then for all
\eta>0
, there exists an ensemble
\{(p_{i},\gamma_{i})\}_{i}
such that

\eta+K_{F}(\rho)\geq\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{F}(\gamma_{i}).

(S70)

Let
\Lambda\in\operatorname{LOCC}
. Suppose that
K_{F}(\gamma)\geq K_{F}(\Lambda(\gamma))
for each strictly irreducible generalised private state
\gamma
. Then the following chain of inequalities holds

\displaystyle\eta+K_{F}(\rho)\geq\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{F}(\gamma_{i})\geq\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{F}(\Lambda(\gamma_{i}))\geq

\displaystyle K_{F}\!\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\Lambda(\gamma_{i})\right)=K_{F}\!\left(\Lambda\!\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\gamma_{i}\right)\right)=K_{F}(\Lambda(\rho)).

For the second inequality, we applied the assumption that
K_{F}
does not increase under the action of LOCC on generalised private states.
Since the inequality
\eta+K_{F}(\rho)\geq K_{F}(\Lambda(\rho))
holds for all
\eta>0
, then we conclude that
K_{F}(\rho)\geq K_{F}(\Lambda(\rho))
.
∎

Observation 3
.

K_{F}
is invariant under local unitary transformations.

Proof.

Let us fix
\kappa>0
, and let
\{(p_{i},\gamma(\psi_{i}))\}_{i}
be an arbitrary ensemble decomposition of
\rho_{AB}
in terms of generalized private states, such that

\sum_{i}p_{i}S_{A_{K}}(\gamma(\psi_{i}))=K_{F}(\rho)+\kappa.

(S71)

Let us note that Definition
3
for strict irreducibility of each
\gamma(\psi_{i})
implies that for each
i
there exists a unitary transformation of the form

\displaystyle W_{AB}^{(i)}\coloneqq W^{(i)}_{AA^{\prime}\rightarrow A_{K}A_{S}}\otimes W^{(i)}_{BB^{\prime}\rightarrow B_{K}B_{S}},

(S72)

such that

W_{AB}^{(i)}\gamma(\psi_{i})_{AB}{W_{AB}^{(i)}}^{\dagger}\\
=\sum_{l,m}\sqrt{\mu_{l}^{(i)}\mu_{m}^{(i)}}|e_{l}^{(i)}f_{l}^{(i)}\rangle\!\langle e_{m}^{(i)}f_{m}^{(i)}|_{A_{K}B_{K}}\otimes U_{l}^{(i)}\rho_{A_{S}B_{S}}^{(i)}{U_{m}^{(i)}}^{{\dagger}},

(S73)

where
|\psi_{i}\rangle=\sum_{l}\mu_{l}^{(i)}|e_{l}^{(i)}\rangle|f_{l}^{(i)}\rangle
, the operator
U_{l}^{(i)}
is a unitary transformation for each
l
and
i
, and
\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}^{(i)}
is a quantum state for each
(i)
.
Moreover

S_{A_{k}}(W_{AB}^{(i)}\gamma(\psi_{i}){W_{AB}^{(i)}}^{\dagger})=K_{D}(\gamma(\psi_{i})).

(S74)

We will now show that the
ensemble

\left\{(p_{i},(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})\gamma(\psi_{i})(U^{\dagger}_{A}\otimes U_{B}^{\dagger}))\right\}_{i}

(S75)

is a valid
decomposition of
\rho^{\prime}\coloneqq(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})\rho_{AB}(U_{A}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{B}^{\dagger})

into strictly irreducible generalized private states,
achieving the rate in (
S71
).
This will show that
K_{F}(\rho)\geq K_{F}(\rho^{\prime})
.
To show this, let us fix
i
arbitrarily and note that, by linearity,

\left\{(p_{i},(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})\gamma(\psi_{i})(U^{\dagger}_{A}\otimes U_{B}^{\dagger}))\right\}_{i}
is
an ensemble decomposition of
\rho^{\prime}
. We define

V_{i}\coloneqq W_{AB}^{(i)}\circ(U_{A}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{B}^{\dagger})
and observe that

\displaystyle V_{i}\rho^{\prime}V_{i}^{\dagger}=

(S76)

\displaystyle\sum_{l,m}\sqrt{\mu^{(i)}_{l}\mu^{(i)}_{m}}|e_{l}^{(i)}f_{l}^{(i)}\rangle\!\langle e_{m}^{(i)}f_{m}^{(i)}|_{A_{K}B_{K}}\otimes U^{(i)}_{l}\rho^{(i)}_{A_{S}B_{S}}(U^{(i)}_{m})^{{\dagger}}.

Moreover

S_{A_{K}}(V_{i}\rho^{\prime}V_{i}^{\dagger})=K_{D}(\gamma(\psi_{i}))\\
=K_{D}((U_{A}\otimes U_{B})\gamma(\psi_{i})(U_{A}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{B}^{\dagger}))=K_{D}(\rho^{\prime}).

(S77)

The first equality follows from (
S74
) and the second from the fact that distillable key is invariant under local unitary transformations.
Hence for every
\kappa
and ensemble decomposition of
\rho

achieving
K_{F}(\rho)+\kappa
, we constructed
an ensemble decomposition of
\rho^{\prime}
achieving the same value.
Since
\kappa>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that

K_{F}(\rho)\geq K_{F}(\rho^{\prime})
. Finally, since
\rho=(U_{A}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{B}^{\dagger})(\rho^{\prime})(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})
, repeating the above argument with the roles of
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
exchanged, we obtain
K_{F}(\rho^{\prime})\geq K_{F}(\rho)
, which implies the desired equality.

∎

Observation 4
.

K_{F}
does not increase under addition of local ancilla, i.e.
K_{F}(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime})\leq K_{F}(\rho))
, where
\rho^{\prime}
is a local state of either Alice or Bob.

Proof.

Let
\{(p_{i},\gamma(\psi_{i}))\}_{i}
be an arbitrary ensemble decomposition of
\rho
into strictly irreducible generalized private states. Then the ensemble

\{(p_{i},\gamma(\psi_{i})\otimes\rho^{\prime})\}_{i}

(S78)

is a valid ensemble decomposition of
\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}
(not necessarily optimal). Indeed, local ancilla can be treated as a part of a shield system where each unitary operator that acts on the shield is extended by identity, written explicitly as follows:

\widetilde{\gamma}(\psi_{i})=\gamma(\psi_{i})\otimes\rho^{\prime}=\\
\sum_{l,m}\sqrt{\lambda^{(i)}_{l}\lambda^{(i)}_{m}}|e_{l}^{(i)}f_{l}^{(i)}\rangle\!\langle e_{m}^{(i)}f_{m}^{(i)}|\otimes\\
\left(U^{(i)}_{l}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)\left(\sigma^{(i)}\otimes\rho^{\prime}\right)\left((U^{(i)}_{m})^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right).

(S79)

From the above formula, it is clear that

\displaystyle K_{F}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{F}(\gamma(\psi_{i}))

(S80)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{F}(\widetilde{\gamma}(\psi_{i}))

(S81)

\displaystyle\geq K_{F}(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}).

(S82)

We have used the fact that extending the shield of the strictly irreducible generalized private state
\gamma(\psi_{i})
by a local state results in a strictly irreducible generalized private state
\widetilde{\gamma}(\psi_{i})
.
∎

Observation 5
.

K_{F}
is non-increasing under the action of a local random unitary channel, i.e.

K_{F}\!\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}(U_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\gamma_{a\widetilde{A}B}(U_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1})^{{\dagger}}\right)\leq K_{F}(\gamma_{a\widetilde{A}B}).

where the channel
\sum_{x}U_{x}(.)U_{x}^{\dagger}
acts on subsystem
a
.

Proof.

\displaystyle K_{F}\!\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}\left(U_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)\gamma_{a\widetilde{A}B}\left(U_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}p_{x}K_{F}(\left(U_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)\gamma_{a\widetilde{A}B}\left(U_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)^{\dagger})

(S83)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}p_{x}K_{F}(\gamma_{a\widetilde{A}B})=K_{F}(\gamma_{a\widetilde{A}B}).

(S84)

The first inequality holds by Lemma
2
, i.e., convexity of
K_{F}
, and the second by Observation
3
.
∎

As a consequence of the above observation we notice that
K_{F}
is also non-increasing under the action of local von Neumann measurements and local partial traces. Indeed, the action of a local von Neumann measurement can written as

\gamma_{a\widetilde{A}B}\mapsto\frac{1}{d_{a}}\sum_{\ell}\left(V_{\ell}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)\gamma_{a\widetilde{A}B}\left(V_{\ell}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)^{\dagger},

where
V_{\ell}=\sum_{j}\exp{2\pi i \ell j /d}|j\rangle\!\langle j|
and local partial trace can be performed as a composition of three operations for which
K_{F}
is non-increasing: local von Neumann measurement, quantum Fourier transform, and local von Neumann measurement.

We note here that, from the above, it follows that
K_{F}
is monotonic under arbitrary local quantum operations. Indeed, we showed that
K_{F}
is monotonic under basic operations from which any quantum operation can be composed (adding auxiliary system, unitary and tracing out). What remains a challenge is to show that
K_{F}
does not increase under classical communication. This fact would imply monotonicity under LOCC in the sense given in the preliminary section, i.e.,
K_{F}(\rho)\geq K_{F}(\Lambda(\rho))
for
\Lambda\in\mathrm{LOCC}
. What is less clear is if
K_{F}
is collectively monotonic
Horodecki2009
;
Eisert2022
, that is, if
K_{F}(\rho)\geq\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{F}(\rho_{i})
where
\rho_{i}
is a post-measurement state (after certain LOCC) and
p_{i}
is the corresponding probability of its occurrence.
Let us stress here that the approach used in entanglement theory does not apply. In particular, it is not clear to us how to follow the general approach of
Vidal_2000
for establishing
K_{F}
to be an entanglement measure because the amount of key in the private state does not seem to be expressible as a direct entropic function of the reduced state of the
AB
systems (as opposed to subsystems
A_{K}B_{K}
, which can be different for each private state). We also note that

K_{F}
is not a so-called “mixed convex roof,” as introduced in
Yang_2009
. Indeed, in the latter reference, the mixed convex roof is defined as the infimum over ensembles of
all
mixed states that apply to a given state. Instead,
K_{F}
is a function of splitting only into special (in general mixed) states, the generalized private states.

V
Privacy dilution

This section is devoted to the description of how the privacy of a private state
\gamma(\Phi^{+})
can be diluted into an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
n
copies
\gamma(\psi)^{\otimes n}
of a generalized private state, with
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{a\in{\cal A}}\lambda_{a}|e_{a}\rangle|f_{a}\rangle
. The protocol is such that the error
\varepsilon
in dilution approaches zero with increasing
n
.

Before providing details of the protocol, we describe two essential subroutines: Permutation Algorithm (PA) and
Phase Error Correction (PEC). These procedures take care of the phase errors that occur during the execution of the first six steps of the Dilution Protocol. These two
procedures use some auxiliary systems. We need these systems
to return at the end of the protocol to their original state. Otherwise, they may leak some information about the key. We therefore also describe the inverse of the PA protocol (IPA), which takes care of this issue.

Since this section is rather technical, below we provide a table of notations used, with the goal of helping the reader in understanding various proofs and constructions.

{\cal A}^{n}

a set of all
n

V.1
Permutation Algorithm

The Permutation Algorithm makes use of two permutations
\pi_{\mathrm{key}}({\bf s},\hat{\bf s})
and
\pi_{\text{shield}}({\bf s},\hat{\bf s})
that allow Alice and Bob to rearrange their subsystems into a desired order.
The idea behind these permutations is as follows. By constructing a private state, given that the key takes a value
\bf s
, the shield should be rotated by the twisting unitary
U_{\bf s}
. More precisely, the shield system should be in a state
U_{\bf s}\rho^{\otimes n}U_{\bf s}^{{\dagger}}
, where

\displaystyle{\bf s}

\displaystyle=({\bf s}[1],\ldots,{\bf s}[n])\in{\cal A}^{n},

(S85)

\displaystyle U_{\bf s}

\displaystyle=U_{{\bf s}[1]}\otimes U_{{\bf s}[2]}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{{\bf s}[n]}.

(S86)

However, when a phase error occurs (as will take place in our dilution protocol described in the next section), then the unitary
U_{\widehat{\bf s}}
occurs instead.
If
\bf s
and
\widehat{\bf s}
were of the same type (with the same number of occurrences of symbols in it), then it would be sufficient to
permute the systems of the shield, i.e., transform
\widehat{\bf s}
into
\bf s
by a permutation, thereby correcting the error. However,
\widehat{\bf s}
may be of a different type. In what follows, both sequences under consideration are from the same
\delta
-typical set
T_{n}^{\delta}

(
EK11
, Section 2.4)
; that is, each symbol from the alphabet
{\cal A}
has an empirical frequency that satisfies

\absolutevalue{\lambda_a -\frac{|a({\bf s})|}{n}}\leq\delta\lambda_{a},

(S87)

where
|a({\bf s})|
is the number of occurrences of
a
in string
{\bf s}
and
\lambda_{a}
is the probability that symbol
a
occurs.
Then for all symbols in
{\cal A}
appearing in
{\bf s}
such that their number of occurrences in
\widehat{{\bf s}}
is larger, there is no problem; we can assign some entries of
\widehat{{\bf s}}
to correspond with the same values as in
\bf s
in a proper place. However, for all symbols in
\bf s
that occur a larger number of times in
{\bf s}
than in
\widehat{\bf s}
, there is a problem that cannot be resolved by a local permutation. Later on, we will see that
\widehat{\bf s}=\beta_{x}({\bf s})
where
\beta_{x}:T_{n}^{\delta}\to T_{n}^{\delta}
(defined in Lemma
7
) is a bijection.

The role of the permutation
\pi_{\text{shield}}({\bf s},\hat{\bf s})
is to correct most of the errors by using the fact that
\bf s
and
\widehat{\bf s}
are close to being related to each other by a permutation. The permutation
\pi_{\text{shield}}({\bf s},\hat{\bf s})
also swaps aside to systems
T_{A^{\prime}}T_{B^{\prime}}
all the shielding systems for which the phase error cannot be corrected by a local permutation. The number of such errors is bounded from above by
L_{\max}\coloneqq\lceil 2\delta n\rceil
. These systems will be treated later by teleportation, local correction on one side, and teleportation back with a small amount of pure form of key, i.e., the singlet state.

In spite of the fact that the permutation
\pi_{\mathrm{key}}({\bf s},\hat{\bf s})
is constructed in the same way as
\pi_{\mathrm{shield}}({\bf s},\hat{\bf s})
, its role is different. This operation, from a copy of the system
\widehat{\bf s}
and the auxiliary system
S_{\operatorname{out}}
in the state
\left|\perp\right\rangle^{\otimes(n+L_{\max})}
, creates a system
S_{\operatorname{out}}
composed of two subsystems in the states
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}
and
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{err}}
, respectively (and leaves
\widehat{\bf s}
in the state
\left|\perp\right\rangle^{\otimes n}
). The states
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}
and
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{err}}
indicate how to correct the error
on systems
B^{\prime}T_{A^{\prime}}T_{B^{\prime}}
when they are in Bob’s possession after teleportation of
T_{A^{\prime}}
by Alice. The state
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{err}}
indicates which unitary transformations should be inverted as they are incorrect. The state
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}
indicates that if the symbol
\perp
occurs at position
p_{k}
, then the unitary
U_{a}
should be applied if the symbol
a
is in position
p_{k}
in
{\bf s}
. When applied to auxiliary systems, the permutation
\pi_{\mathrm{key}}
creates a state
that is further a control system for the Phase Error Correction algorithm.
The register holding
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{err}}
indicates how to undo the errors, while the
registers holding
{\bf s}
and
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}
possess information about the correct unitary transformation that needs to be applied on system
T_{A^{\prime}}T_{B^{\prime}}
.
Since the action of the above mentioned permutations is described on a pair
({\bf s},\widehat{\bf s})
of sequences, then they are applied in a control-target manner (with control on the value of
{\bf s}
and
\widehat{\bf s}
).

At the end of this section, we describe the Permutation Algorithm in more detail. For now, we first claim the existence of the desired permutations, which we argue by proving a number of combinatorial lemmas.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
{\bf x},\widehat{\bf x}\in T_{n}^{\delta}
. Then there exists an increasing sequence of indices
\{i_{k}\}_{k}
selected from the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
{\bf x}
can be written as

{\bf x}={\bf x}_{1}{\bf x}[i_{1}]{\bf x}_{2}{\bf x}[i_{2}]\cdots{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}{\bf x}[i_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}]{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})+1},

(S88)

where
{\bf x}_{k}
are blocks (not necessarily nonempty) of contiguous elements from the string
{\bf x}
,

f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})\coloneqq\sum_{a\in{\cal A}}\big||a({\bf x})|-|a(\widehat{\bf x})|\big|\leq\lceil 2\delta n\rceil,

(S89)

and there exists a permutation
\pi
such that

\pi(\widehat{\bf x})={\bf x}_{1}\widehat{\bf x}[j_{1}]{\bf x}_{2}\widehat{\bf x}[j_{2}]\cdots{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}\widehat{\bf x}[j_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}]{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})+1},

(S90)

where
\{j_{k}\}_{k}
is an increasing sequence of indices selected from the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof.

From strong typicality, for all
a\in{\cal A}
, we have that

\big||a({\bf x})|-|a(\widehat{\bf x})|\big|\leq 2\delta\lambda_{a}n.

(S91)

Therefore
0\leq f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})\leq L_{\mathrm{max}}
, where

L_{\mathrm{max}}\coloneqq\lceil 2\delta n\rceil.

(S92)

Since
f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})
counts the difference between the occurrences of each symbol in strings
{\bf x}
and
\widehat{\bf x}
, then
{\bf x}
and
\widehat{\bf x}
have at least
n-f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})
in common. So we can group
n-f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})
elements from
\widehat{\bf x}
into at most
f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})+1
blocks (not necessarily non-empty)
\widehat{\bf x}_{1},\widehat{\bf x}_{2},\ldots,\widehat{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})},\widehat{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})+1}
which are equal to blocks
{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots,{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})},{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})+1}
of contiguous elements from
{\bf x}
such that

{\bf x}={\bf x}_{1}{\bf x}[i_{1}]{\bf x}_{2}{\bf x}[i_{2}]\ldots{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}{\bf x}[i_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}]{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})+1},

(S93)

where
i_{k}=i_{k-1}+|{\bf x}_{k}|+1
and
i_{1}=|{\bf x}_{1}|+1
. The remaining
f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})
elements from the string
\widehat{\bf x}
can be denoted as
\widehat{\bf x}[j_{1}]\ldots\widehat{\bf x}[j_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}]
, where
j_{k}
are indices of elements in the sequence
\widehat{\bf x}
. Observe that the elements
\widehat{\bf x}[j_{1}]\ldots\widehat{\bf x}[j_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}]
can be ordered in such a way that
j_{k}<j_{m}
for
k<m
. Now it is clear that there exists a permutation
\pi
that rearranges elements of
\widehat{\bf x}
into the desired order.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
{\bf x},\widehat{\bf x}\in T_{n}^{\delta}
. Then there exists a permutation
\pi
such that

\pi(\widehat{\bf x}\underbrace{\perp\ldots\perp}_{n+L_{\mathrm{max}}})=\underbrace{\perp\ldots\perp}_{n}\\
{\bf x}_{1}\perp{\bf x}_{2}\perp\ldots{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}\perp{\bf x}_{f({\bf x})+1}\\
\widehat{\bf x}[j_{1}]\ldots\widehat{\bf x}[j_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}]\underbrace{\perp\ldots\perp}_{L_{\mathrm{max}}-f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}.

(S94)

Proof.

From Lemma
3
, we know that there exists a permutation
\pi
that rearranges the string
\widehat{\bf x}\underbrace{\perp\ldots\perp}_{n+L_{\mathrm{max}}}
into

{\bf x}_{1}\widehat{\bf x}[j_{1}]{\bf x}_{2}\widehat{\bf x}[j_{2}]\ldots{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}\widehat{\bf x}[j_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})}]{\bf x}_{f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})+1}\underbrace{\perp\ldots\perp}_{n+L_{\mathrm{max}}}.

(S95)

Now it remains to compose it with
n+f({\bf x},\widehat{\bf x})
transpositions to obtain the desired permutation.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that
{\bf s},\widehat{\bf s}\in T_{n}^{\delta}
. Then there exists a unitary operation corresponding to
\pi_{\mathrm{key}}({\bf s},\widehat{\bf s})
that maps systems
\widehat{S}
and
S_{\operatorname{out}}
, which are initially in the state
|\widehat{{\bf s}}\rangle_{\widehat{S}}\left|\perp\right\rangle_{S_{\operatorname{out}}}^{\otimes(n+L_{\mathrm{max}})}
, to systems in the state
|{\bf s}_{\operatorname{all}}\rangle
:

|{\bf s}_{\operatorname{all}}\rangle=\left|\perp\right\rangle_{\widehat{S}}^{\otimes n}\otimes|{\bf s}_{1},\perp_{i_{1}},{\bf s}_{2},\perp_{i_{2}},\ldots,\\
{\bf s}_{f({\bf s},\widehat{\bf s})},\perp_{i_{f({\bf s},\widehat{\bf s})}},{\bf s}_{f({\bf s},\widehat{\bf s})+1},\widehat{\bf s}[j_{1}],\widehat{\bf s}[j_{2}],\ldots,\\
\widehat{\bf s}[j_{f({\bf s},\widehat{\bf s})}],\perp_{(f({\bf s},\widehat{\bf s})+1)},\ldots,\perp_{L_{\max}}\rangle_{S_{\operatorname{out}}}\\
\equiv|\underbrace{\perp,\ldots\perp}_{\text{$n$ times}}\rangle_{\widehat{S}}|{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}},{\bf s}_{\operatorname{err}}\rangle_{S_{\operatorname{out}}}.

(S96)

Here for abbreviation, by
\perp_{i_{k}}
we mean the state
\left|\perp\right\rangle
at position
i_{k}
.

Proof.

The desired operation is a quantum realization (i.e., a permutation of quantum systems) of a permutation from Lemma
4
. Since every permutation is a composition of transpositions, it is clear that such a quantum operation can be constructed as a composition of SWAP operations.
∎

V.2
Phase error correction algorithm

We now explain how the output of the PA (applied both by Alice and by Bob), taken as the input
of the Phase Error Correction (PEC) algorithm, is used to correct coherently the errors that remain after the PA procedure. Most of the errors have been corrected by PA.
However, the errors that came out
due to a type mismatch of
{\bf s}
and

\widehat{{\bf s}}\equiv\beta_{x}({\bf s})
,
collected in the system
T_{A^{\prime}}T_{B^{\prime}}
need to be handled separately. By notation,
|x\rangle_{j}
is equal to the
j
-th subsystem of the state
|x\rangle
. Further,
S_{\operatorname{out}}^{B}
denotes system
S_{\operatorname{out}}
obtained by applying
\tau_{\mathrm{key}}^{PA}
by Bob during the execution of PA given in Algorithm
1
. The
aforementioned errors can be corrected in two steps:

1.

untwisting incorrect unitaries,

2.

applying correct unitaries.

The first step is easy to perform because it suffices for Bob to apply the following unitary operation:

\tau_{1}^{\mathrm{PEC}}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L_{\mathrm{max}}}\Big[\sum_{a\neq\perp}|a\rangle\!\langle a|_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{err}}[i]}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{a,T_{A^{\prime}}[i]T_{B^{\prime}}[i]}+\\
\left|\perp\right\rangle\!\left\langle\perp\right|_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{err}}[i]}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{a,T_{A^{\prime}}[i]T_{B^{\prime}}[i]}\Big].

(S103)

Performing the second step requires auxiliary system
C
initially in state
|1\rangle_{C}
that will be used as a counter. To make use of this counter, we define the following unitary operation:

U_{XC}^{\mathrm{shift}}\coloneqq\sum_{a\in{\cal A}}|a\rangle\!\langle a|_{X}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{C}+\left|\perp\right\rangle\!\left\langle\perp\right|_{X}\otimes U_{C}^{\oplus 1},

(S104)

where
U^{\oplus 1}
is a unitary operation that transforms
|t\rangle
into
|(t\oplus 1)\mod L_{\mathrm{max}}\rangle
. To perform the second step, Bob has to do the following procedure. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, Bob applies

\tau_{2}^{\mathrm{PEC}}(i)\coloneqq\\
\sum_{b\in{\cal A}}\sum_{c=1}^{L_{\mathrm{max}}}\sum_{a\in{\cal A}\cup\{\perp\}}|a\rangle\!\langle a|_{{\bf s}[i]}\otimes|b\rangle\!\langle b|_{{\bf s}_{\mathrm{cor}}[i]}\otimes|c\rangle\!\langle c|_{C}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{T_{A^{\prime}}T_{B^{\prime}}}\\
+|a\rangle\!\langle a|_{{\bf s}[i]}\otimes\left|\perp\rangle\!\langle\perp\right|_{{\bf s}_{\mathrm{cor}}[i]}\otimes|c\rangle\!\langle c|_{C}\otimes U_{T_{A^{\prime}}T_{B^{\prime}}}^{a,c},

(S105)

where

U_{T_{A^{\prime}}T_{B^{\prime}}}^{a,c}\coloneqq U_{a,T_{A^{\prime}}[c]T_{B^{\prime}}[c]}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{T_{A^{\prime}}[\neq c]T_{B^{\prime}}[\neq c]}

(S106)

and
U_{a}
is a unitary operation from the definition of private states and corresponding to the symbol
a
(see (
S31
)). Next he applies
U_{{\bf s}_{\mathrm{cor}}[i]C}^{\mathrm{shift}}
, as defined in (
S104
).

V.3
Inverting PA Protocol

In the preceding two sections, we have seen that the composition of PA and PEC is, in fact, a composition of twistings, i.e., controlled-unitary operations. The first one has a control on

V.4
Privacy dilution protocol

We now introduce the privacy dilution protocol, which transforms a private state into a generalized private state.
It uses the fact that the secure one-time pad protocol is a classical correspondent of quantum teleportation.

in

(written as

). We have defined the action of partial inversion of

, called Permutation Algorithm (PA), by Algorithm

given in Section

.

Let us further denote by
U_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}}
a tensor product
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}U_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}[i]}

of unitaries
corresponding to the sequence
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}
in a way that, if in position

in
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}
, then

, and when the symbol
\perp

is at this position, then

U_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}[i]}=\mathbbm{1}
.

The mapping of the PA can be captured by a transformation
of a purification of the state from (
S131
), i.e.,

\sum_{{\bf s}\in T^{\delta}_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\bf s}}{N}}|{\bf s}{\bf s}\rangle(U_{\beta_{x}({\bf s})}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{E})|\chi^{\otimes n}\rangle_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}E}

(S133)

(where

is a purification of the state
\widetilde{\rho}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
to system

) into a state

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf s}\in T^{\delta}_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\bf s}}{N}}|{\bf s}{\bf s}\rangle_{AB}(U_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}}\otimes U_{\widehat{{\bf s}}[q_{1}]}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{\widehat{{\bf s}}[q_{f({\bf s})}]}\otimes

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{f({\bf s})+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{L_{\max}}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{E})\widetilde{|\chi\rangle}

\displaystyle\left[{\widetilde{A}}^{\prime}_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}},T_{A^{\prime}}[1:f({\bf s})],T_{A^{\prime}}^{\perp}[f({\bf s})+1:L_{\mathrm{max}}]\right.

\displaystyle\left.{\widetilde{B}}^{\prime}_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}},T_{B^{\prime}}[1:f({\bf s})],T_{B^{\prime}}^{\perp}[f({\bf s})+1:L_{\mathrm{max}}]E\right].

(S134)

In the above for ease of reading we identify
\widetilde{|\chi\rangle}_{Y}
with

By

we mean the purification of the shield systems such that whenever

(i.e., the

to the system

.
We have also omitted the systems

as they are in the state

. Moreover,
U_{\widehat{{\bf s}}[j_{k}]}
are
unitaries whose indices do not match the index of the key at position

in
|{\bf s}\rangle_{A}
(reflecting the phase error). The

Next, the PEC procedure described in Algorithm
2

states of the systems

A^{\prime}[j_{1}]\cdots A^{\prime}[j_{f({\bf s})}]\left|\perp\right\rangle_{j_{f({\bf s})}+1}\cdots\left|\perp\right\rangle_{L_{\max}},

(S135)

which are now stored in system
T_{A^{\prime}}
. There are
f({\bf s})
“phase errors”.
Namely for
k=1,\ldots,f({\bf s})
the

are the positions for which
{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}
has
\left|\perp\right\rangle

{\bf s}[i_{k}]\neq\widehat{\bf s}[j_{k}]

at this position. In other words, at position

on

. For this reason Bob

and

Indeed, this is what PEC does. The unitary
\tau_{1}^{\mathrm{PEC}}

on system

for
each

each. Further

After the phase errors are corrected, the state of (

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf s}\in T^{\delta}_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\bf s}}{N}}|{\bf s}{\bf s}\rangle_{AB}(U_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}}\otimes U_{{\bf s}[p_{1}]}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{{\bf s}[p_{f({\bf s})}]}\otimes

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{f({\bf s})+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{L_{\max}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{E})\widetilde{|\chi\rangle}

\displaystyle\left[{\widetilde{A}}^{\prime}_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}},T_{A^{\prime}}[1:f({\bf s})],T_{A^{\prime}}^{\perp}[f({\bf s})+1:L_{\mathrm{max}}]\right.

\displaystyle\left.{\widetilde{B}}^{\prime}_{{\bf s}_{\operatorname{cor}}},T_{B^{\prime}}[1:f({\bf s})],T_{B^{\prime}}^{\perp}[f({\bf s})+1:L_{\mathrm{max}}]E\right]

(S136)

which are correct “phases.”
Performing PA involves borrowing the auxiliary systems
\widetilde{A}^{\prime},T_{A^{\prime}}S_{\operatorname{out}}

and its copy

Then, there comes the Inverting PA (IPA) procedure, the task of which is to (i)

to
{A^{\prime}}{B^{\prime}}[1\ldots n]

\widehat{S}
and
{\widehat{S}}_{1}
to their original state

of the initial dimension.
The first task of IPA is rather complicated since it has to rearrange coherently the entries corrected by the PEC algorithm and conserve the order of the remaining ones. This procedure is performed in a control-target manner, where control is on systems
{\bf s}_{\mathrm{cor}}
and
C

should be swapped. Section

provides an extensive description of this procedure.
On the other hand, the second task of IPA is simple since it is completed by applying the inverse of the twisting

{\tau_{\mathrm{key}}^{PA}}^{\dagger}
. Further, the

applied twice uncomputes

to a state of the systems

Finally, the systems
{\widehat{S}}^{A}_{1}{\widehat{S}}^{B}_{1}S_{\operatorname{out}}^{A}S_{\operatorname{out}}^{B}{\widetilde{A}}T_{A^{\prime}}{\widetilde{B}}T_{B^{\prime}}
are then traced out in their original state.
The resulting state is

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf s}\in T^{\delta}_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{{\lambda_{\bf s}}}{N}}|{\bf s}{\bf s}\rangle_{AB}U_{{\bf s}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{E}|\chi\rangle_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}E}.

(S137)

The auxiliary systems were traced out in their original state. Hence, this tracing out of auxiliary systems does not disclose any further knowledge to Eve about the above state.

We have then demonstrated explicitly, by constructing the operations of the Dilution Protocol, that they are a composition of twisting unitary operations, teleportation of a state whose dimension is independent of the value of the key, and giving back the auxiliary systems in their original state. Since the teleported system is also teleported back, we can consider their composition as an identity operation on the state.
Since the states of auxiliary systems are returned to their original state, and the protocol transforms
ABA^{\prime}B^{\prime}[1\ldots n]
back to the desired state, there is no security leakage when we trace out the auxiliary systems.

We note here that mapping of Step 8 of the Dilution Protocol given in Sec.
V.4

|{\bf s}\rangle_{A}\rightarrow|e_{\bf s}\rangle_{A}
on Alice’s key part and
|{\bf s}\rangle_{B}\rightarrow|f_{\bf s}\rangle_{B}
on Bob’s one can also be done by local unitary transformations.

Finally, we observe that the system
A

|x\rangle\!\langle x|\otimes U_{\beta_{x}({\bf s})}\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}U_{\beta_{x}({\bf s})}^{\dagger}
.
However, after the protocol is done in Step

\beta_{x}
is inverted:
for every
{\bf s}
on shield there is
U_{\bf s}(\widetilde{\rho}_{AB})U_{\bf s}^{\dagger}
. Hence, the shielding systems become independent from
|x\rangle\!\langle x|_{A}

|x\rangle\!\langle x|
only at the end of the protocol since the PA procedure uses this register
to perform
U_{\beta_{x}}
.
To summarize, the resulting state of the protocol is in the desired form of the approximate twisted typical subspace of the state
|\psi\rangle^{\otimes n}
. Due to (
S117

\gamma(\psi)^{\otimes n}
. This concludes the proof of the correctness of the Privacy Dilution protocol described above
and in Theorem
3
.
∎

VI
Regularized key of formation upper bounds key cost

We now show that the regularized key of formation is an upper bound on the key cost.
For ease of writing, we will make use of the following definition:

Definition 6
.

A private state
\gamma(\Phi^{+})
is
\theta
-related to a generalized private state
\gamma(\phi)
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
from
\gamma(\Phi^{+})
one can create
\gamma(\phi)^{\otimes n}
by LOCC, up to an error
\theta
in trace distance. For ease of writing, we sometimes omit
\theta
whenever irrelevant or known from the context.

Let us note here that for each generalized private state
\gamma(\psi)
and
\theta>0
there exists a private state
\theta
-related to
\gamma(\psi)
, as assured
by Theorem
3
. We are ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 4
.

For every bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
,

K_{F}^{\infty}(\rho_{AB})\geq K_{C}(\rho_{AB}).

(S139)

Proof.

Let
\{(p_{i},\gamma(\psi_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{k}
be an optimal ensemble for
K_{F}
. For this part of the proof, we assume that
K_{F}
is attained on a finite ensemble with
k
elements. By Lemma
1
, we have that
k\leq(|{\cal A}|\times|{\cal B}|)^{2}+1
. The case when it is not attained will be considered later as a small modification of what follows.
To prove the inequality, we first note, following
Hayden_2001
, that
by typicality arguments, for all
\varepsilon,\delta_{0}>0
and sufficiently large
n
, we have that

\displaystyle\rho^{\otimes n}\approx_{\varepsilon}\rho_{n}\equiv\sum_{{\bf s}\in T^{\delta_{0}}_{n}}p_{\bf s}\gamma(\psi_{{\bf s}[1]})\otimes\gamma(\psi_{{\bf s}[2]})\otimes\cdots\otimes\gamma(\psi_{{\bf s}[n]}).

(S140)

The latter state is locally unitarily equivalent to

{\widetilde{\rho}}_{n}\equiv\sum_{{\bf s}\in T^{\delta_{0}}_{n}}p_{\bf s}\gamma(\psi_{\bf s}),

(S141)

where the string
{\bf s}
belongs to a strongly typical set
T^{\delta_{0}}_{n}
with a property that it contains each state
\gamma(\psi_{i})
, at least
\lfloor p_{i}n-\delta_{0}p_{i}n\rfloor
and at most
\lceil p_{i}n+\delta_{0}p_{i}n\rceil
times.
We note that
\delta_{0}
can be arbitrarily small for sufficiently large
n
.

Let us now fix
{\bf s}\in T^{\delta_{0}}_{n}
. Then
\gamma(\psi_{\bf s})
is such that each state
\gamma(\psi_{i})
in
\gamma(\psi_{\bf s})
occurs
l_{i}(n)\in\mathbb{N}
times, which satisfies

\lfloor p_{i}n-\delta_{0}p_{i}n\rfloor\leq l_{i}(n)\leq\lceil np_{i}+\delta_{0}p_{i}n\rceil.

(S142)

We aim to create an approximate version of
\gamma(\psi_{\bf s})
.

We will do it using a sub-protocol that has access to a private state
\gamma_{d_{n}(i)}(\Phi^{+})

\varepsilon_{i}
-related to
\gamma(\psi_{i})
; this sub-protocol will create an
\varepsilon_{i}
-approximation of the latter state. For the sake of this proof, it suffices to set
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon
. We also set
\delta_{i}>0
arbitrarily for
i\in\{1,\ldots k\}
. As noted, the existence of
\gamma_{d_{n}(i)}(\Phi^{+})
is assured by Theorem
3
. More precisely, given sufficiently large
n
we will obtain sufficiently large
d_{n}(i)
satisfying:

\log d_{n}(i)=\lceil l_{i}(n)\times(S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i})\rceil,

(S143)

(where
\eta_{i}=\delta_{i}S(A)_{\psi_{i}}
) such that the Dilution Protocol described in Section
V
creates from
\gamma_{d_{n}(i)}(\Phi^{+})
a state that
\varepsilon_{i}
-approximates in trace norm
\gamma(\psi_{i})^{\otimes l_{i}(n)}
. Let us denote this protocol as
{\cal P}_{i}
.

We note here that due to the definition of
K_{F}
, each
\gamma(\psi_{i})
is strictly irreducible. Hence by (
S120
),
\gamma_{d_{n}(i)}(\Phi^{+})
is also a strictly irreducible private state. Thus, using
{\cal P}_{i}
as a subroutine, we will create
\rho_{AB}
from a strictly irreducible private state, as demanded by the definition of
K_{C}
.

Performing protocol
{\cal P}_{i}
for each
i\in[k]
, by the triangle inequality, we obtain
\gamma_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})
that is a
\theta\coloneqq\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
-approximation of the state

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\gamma(\psi_{i})^{\otimes l_{i}(n)}.

(S144)

Let us note here that the actual amount of key needed to create
\gamma_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})
varies depending on the value of
\bf s
, due to fluctuations of the number of occurrences of states
\gamma(\psi_{i})
, i.e.,
l_{i}(n)
in sequence
\bf s
.
However, by definition of
K_{C}
, we need
a
single
private state from which
we can create our target state given in (
S141
). We will therefore choose
a private state with an amount of key exceeding
K_{F}
by a vanishing factor (proportional to
\delta_{0}\times K_{F}\leq\delta_{0}d_{AB}
by a vanishing constant) which will be ready for the worst-case (the largest) amount of key needed to create
\gamma_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})
. Namely, we will define a private state which
is
O(\epsilon)
-related to
\gamma(\psi_{\bf s})
for
every

\delta_{0}
-strongly typical
\bf s
.

To achieve this task, we choose

{\gamma}_{d_{n}^{*}}(\Phi^{+})
to be a private state
\theta
-related to a generalized private state of the form

\gamma^{+}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\gamma(\psi_{i})^{\otimes l_{i}(n)^{+}}.

(S145)

The dimension
d_{n}^{*}
of the key part of
{\gamma}_{d_{n}^{*}}(\Phi^{+})
satisfies

\log_{2}d_{n}^{*}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lceil l_{i}(n)^{+}\times(S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i})\rceil,

(S146)

where

l_{i}(n)^{+}\coloneqq\lceil np_{i}+\delta_{0}p_{i}n\rceil.

(S147)

We focus on this case, as the above state uses the largest private key. Intuitively, it can allow for the creation of
\gamma_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})
for any other
\delta_{0}
-typical
{\bf s}\neq{\bf s}_{0}
by performing partial trace operations if less key is needed to produce
\gamma(\psi_{\bf s})
.

To finalize the procedure of creating an approximate version of
\gamma(\psi_{\bf s})
, we need to permute locally (in the same way for Alice and Bob) subsystems of
\widetilde{\gamma}_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})
by some permutation
\pi_{{\bf s}}
, defined as

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{0}\equiv(\underbrace{1\ldots 1}_{\text{$l_{1}(n)$ times }},\underbrace{2\ldots 2}_{\text{$l_{2}(n)$ times }},\ldots,\underbrace{k\ldots k}_{\text{$l_{k}(n)$ times }})\rightarrow{\bf s}.

(S148)

We are ready to formalize the protocol
{\cal P}
, which takes as input
{\gamma}_{d_{n}^{*}}(\Phi^{+})
and outputs an approximation of

{\widetilde{\rho}}_{n}=\sum_{{\bf s}\in T^{\delta_{0}}_{n}}p_{s}\gamma(\psi_{\bf s})
. It
consists of the following steps:

1.

Draw
{\bf s}
at random with probability
p_{\bf s}
in order to
produce an approximation of
\gamma(\psi_{{\bf s}})
from the related private state
\gamma_{d_{n}^{*}}(\Phi^{+})
.

2.

For each
i\in[k]
, apply
{\cal P}_{i}
to produce a
\theta
-approximation
\gamma^{+}_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})
of the state
\gamma^{+}
.

3.

If
{\bf s}\neq{\bf s}_{0}
, trace out some of the subsystems of
\gamma^{+}_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})
to obtain the
\theta
-approximation
\gamma_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})
of

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\gamma(\psi_{i})^{\otimes l_{i}(n)}.

(S149)

4.

If
{\bf s}\neq{\bf s_{0}}
, apply the permutation
\pi_{\bf s}
to the local subsystems of
\gamma_{\approx}(\psi_{\bf s})

to create a
\theta
-approximation of
\gamma(\psi_{\bf s})
.

5.

Erase the symbol
{\bf s}
, producing a mixed state over typical labels
{\bf s}
.

By construction, the above protocol
{\cal P}

creates a
\theta
-approximation of the state

\widetilde{\rho}_{n}
, which is locally unitarily equivalent to
\rho_{n}
given in (
S140
). Let
V\equiv V_{A}\otimes V_{B}
denote the unitary transforming
\widetilde{\rho}_{n}
into
\rho_{n}
. Now, by the triangle inequality, the construction of
{\cal P}
, and (
S140
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|V{\cal P}({\gamma}_{d_{n}^{*}}(\Phi^{+}))V^{\dagger}-\rho^{\otimes n}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|V{\cal P}({\gamma}_{d_{n}^{*}}(\Phi^{+}))V^{\dagger}-{\rho}_{n}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\rho^{\otimes n}-{\rho}_{n}\right\|_{1}

(S150)

\displaystyle\leq\theta+\varepsilon,

(S151)

for sufficiently large
n
. We note here that
without loss of generality
\varepsilon+\theta\leq(k+1)\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon(d_{AB}^{2}+1)
(see Lemma
1
).

Since
V
is local,
{\cal P}^{\prime}\equiv V\circ{\cal P}
is an LOCC protocol that creates an approximation of
\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n}
, using
\log d_{n}^{*}
bits of private key. Hence, its rate is an upper bound on
K_{C}
. We now compute the latter rate. As we will see, it asymptotically reaches the value of
K_{F}(\rho_{AB})
.

In total, the rate of the protocol
{\cal P}^{\prime}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\log_{2}d_{n}^{*}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\lceil{l_{i}^{+}(n)}[S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i}]\right\rceil

(S152)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{l_{i}^{+}(n)}[S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i}]+\frac{k}{n}

(S153)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{l_{i}^{+}(n)}{n}}[S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i}]+\frac{d_{AB}^{2}+1}{n}

(S154)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{p_{i}(1+\delta_{0})}[S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i}]+

\displaystyle\frac{k[S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i}]}{n}+\frac{d_{AB}^{2}+1}{n}

(S155)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{p_{i}(1+\delta_{0})}[S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i}]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(d_{AB}^{2}+1)(\log(d_{AB})+3)}{n}+\frac{d_{AB}^{2}+1}{n}

(S156)

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}(1+\delta_{0})[S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+2\delta_{i}+\eta_{i}]

(S157)

\displaystyle=(1+\delta_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+(1+\delta_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}(\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i})

(S158)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+\delta_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}S(A)_{\psi_{i}}

\displaystyle\qquad+(1+\delta_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}(\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i})

(S159)

\displaystyle=K_{F}(\rho_{AB})+\delta_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}S(A)_{\psi_{i}}+(1+\delta_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}(\eta_{i}+2\delta_{i}).

(S160)

In the above, the first inequality is by an upper bound by
1
to each

Key cost of generalized private states

In this section, we prove several lemmas,
which allow us to conclude that the key
of formation, key cost, and distillable key are all equal for (irreducible) generalized private states.
We begin with a series of lemmas,
which allow us to prove the main result of this section, that is, Theorem

.

Lemma 10
.

For
\gamma(\psi)
a generalized private state, the following equality holds:

S_{A}[\gamma(\psi)]=S_{A}(\psi).

(S189)

Proof.

Consider that

\displaystyle S_{A}[\gamma(\psi)]

\displaystyle=S(\operatorname{Tr}_{BA^{\prime}B^{\prime}}[\gamma(\psi)])

(S190)

\displaystyle=S(\operatorname{Tr}_{BA^{\prime}B^{\prime}}[{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}\gamma(\psi){\widetilde{U}}])

(S191)

\displaystyle=S(\operatorname{Tr}_{BA^{\prime}B^{\prime}}[\psi\otimes\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}])

(S192)

\displaystyle=S(\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[\psi])

(S193)

\displaystyle=S_{A}(\psi).

(S194)

In the above, the first equality is by definition. The second comes from the fact that the twisting
U
is a unitary that can be substituted with one
acting only on systems
BA^{\prime}B^{\prime}
:
\widetilde{U}=\sum_{i}|i\rangle\!\langle i|_{B}\otimes U_{i}^{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
in the case of generalized private states
(for this idea, see
Horodecki_smallD
),
and because the trace does not depend on the basis in which it is performed (here, the trace over systems
BA^{\prime}B^{\prime}
does not depend on the basis
\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}
which is defined on systems
BA^{\prime}B^{\prime}
only). The last equality follows from the definition of the generalized private state.
∎

It was claimed, yet not argued explicitly in

, that generalized private states are irreducible when the conditional states

are separable for each

Lemma 11

A strictly irreducible generalized private state
\gamma(\psi)
satisfies

K_{D}(\gamma(\psi))=S_{A}(\psi)=E_{R}(\gamma(\psi))=E_{R}^{\infty}(\gamma(\psi)).

(S195)

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary generalized private state
\gamma(\psi)=U(\psi\otimes\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}})U^{\dagger}
, with
U=\sum_{i}|ii\rangle\!\langle ii|\otimes U_{i}^{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}

K_{D}(\gamma(\psi))\geq S_{A}(\psi)
and then that
E_{R}(\gamma(\psi))\leq S_{A}(\psi)
. Since
E_{R}\geq E^{\infty}_{R}\geq K_{D}

pptkey
;
keyhuge
, the claim follows by proving these two inequalities.

To show that
K_{D}(\gamma(\psi))\geq S_{A}(\psi)
, we note that, after the measurement of system
A
of
\gamma(\psi)
in the computational basis,
the system
AB
is in state
\rho_{AB}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\mu_{i}|ii\rangle\!\langle ii|

such that

.
Therefore, the Devetak–Winter protocol

applied to systems

of key, as

This proves

To show that

, which is, by the assumption of strict irreducibility of

\displaystyle D(\gamma(\psi)\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=D(\psi\otimes\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\|\sigma_{AB}\otimes\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}})

(S196)

\displaystyle=D(\psi\|\sigma_{AB})

\displaystyle=S_{A}(\psi),

(S198)

where the first equality

(S199)

and the last stems from a direct computation. By the above argument, we have by the definition of

as the choice of

may be suboptimal when taking the infimum of the relative entropy “distance” from separable states.

Due to Lemma

For every strictly irreducible generalized private state

Proof.

First, by the definition of

Theorem 6

For every bipartite state

K_{D}^{\varepsilon_{2}}(\rho)\leq K_{C}^{\varepsilon_{1}}(\rho)+\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})}\right).

(S219)

Now, taking the power of two on both sides, we get

\sup_{\sigma\in{\operatorname{SEP}}}\inf_{\Omega\in T}\operatorname{Tr}(\Omega\sigma)\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{d_{k}}.

(S220)

The above inequality follows from Theorem

Consider two LOCC channels,

Denoting now

IX
Key cost of a quantum device

We now define a quantity for a quantum device, which is analogous to the key cost
of a state. We follow the idea of introducing measures of quantum non-locality based on entanglement measures, as provided in
CFH21
(see also
HWD21
). Non-locality measures obtained in this way are called their reduced entanglement measures. For a given device, the reduced entanglement measure
E
is obtained by taking the minimal value of
E
over all quantum states that leads to the device when one can vary over measurement settings. It is natural to consider reduced key cost and reduced key of formation obtained in this way.

A device, i.e., a conditional probability distribution of two outputs
(a,b)
given two inputs
(x,y)
, denoted as
P(ab|xy)
is quantum if and only if there exists a state
\rho_{AB}
such that

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{AB}({\mathrm{M}}^{a}_{x}\otimes{\mathrm{M}}^{b}_{y}))=P(ab|xy).

(S262)

We denote
such a device as
({\mathcal{M}},\rho_{AB})
, where
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathrm{M}}^{a}_{x}\otimes{\mathrm{M}}^{b}_{y}
.
Below, we define the cost of a device for the bipartite case, with the multipartite case following along similar lines.

Definition 9
.

The key cost of a quantum device
P(ab|xy)
is equal to the reduced single-shot key cost:

K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(P(ab|xy))\equiv K_{C}^{\varepsilon\downarrow}(P(ab|xy))\coloneqq\\
\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho_{AB},\\
{\mathrm{M}}^{a}_{x}\otimes{\mathrm{M}}^{b}_{y}\end{subarray}}\{K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB}):\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{AB}{\mathrm{M}}^{a}_{x}\otimes{\mathrm{M}}^{b}_{y})=P(ab|xy)\}.

(S263)

We now establish a nontrivial bound for all devices
that admit a realization on states with a positive partial transpose (PPT states). The PPT states satisfy
\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes T_{B}(\rho_{AB})\equiv\rho_{AB}^{\Gamma_{B}}\geq 0
, where
T_{B}
is the transposition map applied to system
B
. The set of all PPT states is denoted as
\operatorname{PPT}
. We say
that
P(ab|xy)\in\operatorname{PPT}
iff
P(ab|xy)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{AB}{\mathrm{M}}^{a}_{x}\otimes{\mathrm{M}}^{b}_{y})
for some measurements
{\mathrm{M}}^{a}_{x}\otimes{\mathrm{M}}^{b}_{y}
and a state
\rho_{AB}
with positive partial transpose. We call the state
\rho_{AB}
a PPT realization of
P(ab|xy)
.

Proposition 1
.

For any
P(ab|xy)\in\operatorname{PPT}
with PPT realization
\rho_{AB}
, the following inequality holds:

K^{\varepsilon}_{C}(P(ab|xy))\leq\min\{K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB}),K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB}^{\Gamma_{B}})\}.

(S264)

Proof.

Following
CFH21
it suffices to notice that, by the identity
\operatorname{Tr}(XY)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(X^{\Gamma}Y^{\Gamma}\right)
and the fact that
\rho_{AB}^{\Gamma_{B}}\geq 0
, we have that, for every
realization
(\sigma_{AB},{\mathcal{N}})
with

{\mathcal{N}}={\mathrm{N}}^{a}_{x}\otimes{\mathrm{N}}^{b}_{y}
of a device
P(ab|xy)
, the equality

(\sigma,{\mathcal{N}})=(\sigma^{\Gamma_{B}},{\mathcal{N}}^{\Gamma_{B}})
holds.
∎

We can also define the device-independent key cost
of a state.

Definition 10
.

The device-independent key cost of a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
is defined as

K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB})\coloneqq\sup_{\mathcal{M}}K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB},{\mathcal{M}}).

(S265)

In particular, for the states
\rho
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2d_{s}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2d_{s}}
given in
smallkey
for which it is proven in
CFH21
, that device independent key is (strictly) upper bounded by
K_{\operatorname{D}}(\rho^{\Gamma})
, there is

K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB})\leq K_{C}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB}^{\Gamma})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{s}}+1}.

(S266)

These states have vanishing device-independent key cost with increasing dimension
d_{s}
. Interestingly, however, they have a device-dependent key rate equal to one. It corresponds to the fact that these states tend to be indistinguishable from their separable key-attacked version (i.e.,
K_{C}
is zero) where distinguishability is performed using LOCC operations
HorodeckiMurta
.
One can also define an asymptotic version of the key cost of a device, which, apart from an analogy of (
S266
), can be related to the device-independent key via Eqs. (2) and (5) of
CFH21
:

K_{\operatorname{DI}}(\rho)\leq K_{D}^{\downarrow}(\rho)\\
\leq K_{C}^{\downarrow}(\rho)\equiv\sup_{{\cal M}}\inf_{(\sigma,{\cal N})=(\rho,{\cal M})}K_{C}(\sigma).

(S267)

Analogous results can be obtained for the reduced key of formation, which is defined as

K_{F}(P(ab|xy))\coloneqq\\
\inf_{({\cal N},\sigma_{AB})}\{K_{F}(\sigma_{AB}):({\cal N},\sigma_{AB})=P(ab|xy)\}.

(S268)

Remark 2
.

We note here that the measure defined in (
S268
) need not coincide with the key of formation of a device, which would be an analog of
K_{F}
for quantum states. The latter analog would involve the infimum over splittings into devices that contain an ideal device-independent key (denoted as IDIK). While devices lying on the boundary of a quantum set (i.e. having quantum realization) belong to IDIK, it is unclear if they exhaust IDIK.

Proof of subadditivity of
K_{F}

It is easy to observe that the
key of formation is subadditive.
We state it in a more general form
for any convex roof measure.
Let
S
denote a subset of all finite-dimensional quantum states with the following properties:

1.

S
is closed under tensor product,

2.

every quantum state
\rho
can be written as a convex combination of elements from
S
.

Note that, as an example, the set of all pure states satisfies the conditions above. A convex-roof measure
E_{S}
, with respect to the set
S

E_{S}(\rho)=\inf_{\{(p_{i},\rho_{i})\}_{i}}\sum_{i}p_{i}f(\rho_{i})

(S269)

for some real non-negative valued function

, where the infimum is taken over every ensemble decomposition
\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{i}=\rho
and
\rho_{i}\in S
for all

.

Observation 6
.

For a convex-roof measure
E_{S}
taken with
respect to set
S
that is closed under

E_{S}
is subadditive;
i.e.,
E_{S}(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime})\leq E_{S}(\rho)+E_{S}(\rho^{\prime})

Let
\{(p_{i},\sigma_{i})\}_{i}
and

and

is closed under tensor products
and
\sigma_{i},\tau_{j}\in S

\{(p_{i}q_{j},\sigma_{i}\otimes\tau_{j})\}_{i,j}
is
a valid ensemble decomposition of
\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}
.
However, it can be suboptimal, concluding the proof.
∎
