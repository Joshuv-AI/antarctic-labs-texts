Sobolev embeddings for kinetic Fokker-Planck equations
Andrea Pascucci, Antonello Pesce

Abstract
We introduce intrinsic Sobolev-Slobodeckij spaces for a class of ultra-parabolic Kolmogorov type operators satisfying the weak Hörmander condition. We prove continuous embeddings into Lorentz and intrinsic Hölder spaces. We also prove approximation and interpolation inequalities by means of an intrinsic Taylor expansion, extending analogous results for Hölder spaces. The embedding at first order is proved by adapting a method by Luc Tartar which only exploits scaling properties of the intrinsic quasi-norm, while for higher orders we use uniform kernel estimates.

Introduction

In this paper we develop a functional framework for the study of kinetic Fokker-Planck equations.
Specifically, we introduce intrinsic Sobolev spaces suitably related to a system of Hörmander’s
vector fields: our main results are embedding, interpolation and approximation theorems that are
the basic tools in many problems concerning partial differential equations.

Let
(t,x)
denote a point in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
and, for fixed
d\leqslant N
, consider the vector fields

\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}\quad\text{ and }\quad Y:=\langle Bx,\nabla_{x}\rangle+\partial_{t},

(1.1)

where
B
is a constant
N\times N
matrix and
\nabla_{x}=(\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{N}})
. We
assume the Hörmander’s condition is satisfied:

\text{\rm rank Lie}(\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}},Y)=N+1.

(1.2)

The classical example we have in mind is the Langevin kinetic model, given by the system of
stochastic differential equations

\begin{cases}dV_{t}=dW_{t},\\
dP_{t}=V_{t}dt,\end{cases}

(1.3)

where
W
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. Here the processes
V
and
P
represent the
velocity and position of a system of
d
particles. The forward Kolmogorov (or Fokker-Planck)
operator of (
1.3
), written in terms of the variables
x=(v,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is in
the form of a sum of squares of the vector fields
\partial_{v_{1}},\dots,\partial_{v_{d}}
plus a drift (or
transport term)
Y_{0}
, precisely

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{v_{i}}^{2}-Y_{0},\qquad Y_{0}:=\langle v,\nabla_{p}\rangle+\partial_{t}.

(1.4)

In this example,
N=2d
and

B=\begin{pmatrix}0_{d}&0_{d}\\
I_{d}&0_{d}\ \end{pmatrix}

where
I_{d}
and
0_{d}
denote the
d\times d
identity and null matrices respectively. Operator
(
1.4
) satisfies the Hörmander’s condition, is hypoelliptic and has a Gaussian fundamental
solution that is the transition density of the Markov process
(V,P)
in (
1.3
).

The literature on generalized Sobolev spaces for Hörmander’s vector fields is vast (see, for
instance,
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
17
]
).
When dealing with the regularity properties of PDEs modeled on the vector fields
(
1.1
)-(
1.2
), as for example the equation in divergence form

\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}\left(a_{ij}\partial_{x_{j}}u\right)-Yu=0,

(1.5)

it is standard to assign a formal weight to each of the vector fields, that is one for the
directions
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}
of diffusion and is two for the drift
Y
; also,
consistently with the structure of the equation,
Y
should be interpreted as a
second order
derivative
in intrinsic sense. As earlier noted in
[
25
]
among others, this fact raises
a question about the role of
Y
in the definition of
first order
intrinsic Sobolev space

W^{1,p}
: indeed, in the degenerate case
d<N
, the regularity properties of (
1.5
) strongly
rely on Hörmander’s condition and involve the second order derivative
Y
in a crucial way. Many
remarkable results have been proven for weak solutions of (
1.5
), defined as functions
u

such that
Yu
belongs to
L^{2}
, in addition to the minimal assumptions

u,\partial_{x_{1}}u,\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}u\in L^{2}
needed to write the equation (
1.5
) in the sense
of distributions: we refer for instance to
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
19
]
,

[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
9
]
. In

[
22
]
a first
L^{2}-L^{\infty}
estimate has been proven by using Moser’s
approach; moreover, in
[
12
]
a Harnack inequality for kinetic Fokker-Planck equations
with rough coefficients has been proven extending the De Giorgi-Nash-Moser theory.

In Section
2
we introduce intrinsic Sobolev-Slobodeckij spaces for (
1.1
),
denoted by
W^{k,p}_{B}
, where at first order (i.e.
k=1
) the vector field
Y
appears as a
fractional derivative of order
1/2
:
this approach is coherent with the scaling properties of the Hörmander vector fields and
therefore seems suitable for the study of (
1.5
). In particular, we can give a natural
definition of weak solution
u
of (
1.5
) in the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}_{B}
without requiring

Yu\in L^{2}
as it is usually done in the literature: as far as we know, this is the first result
in this direction.

We mention that the use of fractional derivatives makes it difficult to prove embedding results by
means of representation formulas in terms of a parametrix, at least for
k=1
, as in

[
22
]
or
[
5
]
. Indeed, for the proof of our main embedding result,
Theorem
1.1
, we use a remarkable method developed by Tartar
[
28
]
, that is
only based on scaling arguments
and a characterization of Lorentz spaces given in Lemma
A.5
.

In the following statement
\mathbf{d}
denotes the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
induced by the
vector fields (
1.1
), whose precise definition is given in (
2.23
): to fix ideas,

\mathbf{d}=4d+2
for the Fokker-Planck operator (
1.4
).

Theorem 1.1

(
W^{1,p}_{B}
embeddings).

i)

For
1\leqslant p<\mathbf{d}
we have

W^{1,p}_{B}\subseteq L^{q,p},\qquad p\leqslant q\leqslant p^{\ast},\qquad\frac{1}{p^{\ast}}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\mathbf{d}},

(1.6)

where
L^{p,q}
denotes the Lorentz space. In particular,
W^{1,p}_{B}\subseteq L^{q}
for

p\leqslant q\leqslant p^{\ast}
;

ii)

for
\mathbf{d}<p<\infty
we have

W^{1,p}_{B}\subseteq C_{B}^{0,1-\frac{\mathbf{d}}{p}};

(1.7)

iii)

for
p=\mathbf{d}
we have

W^{1,\mathbf{d}}_{B}\subseteq L^{q,\mathbf{d}}\subseteq L^{q},\qquad q\geqslant\mathbf{d}.

(1.8)

Moreover, if
u\in W^{1,\mathbf{d}}_{B}
then for every
\lambda,\delta>0
we have

\int_{|u|>\delta}e^{\lambda|u(z)|^{\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}-1}}}dz<\infty.

(1.9)

The Morrey embedding (
1.7
) is given in terms of the optimal generalized Hölder
spaces
C^{k,\alpha}_{B}
only recently introduced in
[
18
]
together with an intrinsic
Taylor formula. Embeddings for higher order spaces
W^{k,p}_{B}
are provided in Theorem

7.1
. Remarkably, estimate (
1.9
) extends Trudinger’s result
[
30
]
.
Embedding results for Kolmogorov equations were also proved in
[
7
]
and more recently
in
[
11
]
.

We acknowledge that Tartar himself applied his approach to the Langevin operator (
1.4
):
according to
[
28
]
, Appendices II and III, he proved that for a function

f=f(t,x,v)
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
f,\nabla_{v}f,Y_{0}f:=(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x})f\in L^{p}
, one can first prove the “crude” embedding estimate

\|f\|_{q}\lesssim\|f\|_{p}+\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{p}+\|Y_{0}f\|_{p},

(1.10)

for some
q>p
and then get the embeddings:

-

in
L^{p^{**},p}
if
1\leqslant p<3d+1
, with
\frac{1}{p^{**}}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{3d+1}
;

-

in
L^{\infty}
if
p>3d+1
;

-

in
L^{r}
if
p=3d+1
, for any
p\leqslant r<\infty
.

As a main motivation, our study is a first step in the development of a theory of generalized
Besov spaces for possible applications to
stochastic
partial differential equations: we
mention that recent results for stochastic kinetic equations were established in
[
21
]

and
[
32
]
. Secondly, even for deterministic kinetic equations, our results improve the
known regularity estimates available in the literature by providing the natural functional
framework for weak solutions of kinetic Fokker-Planck equations.

[

]
. Crucial approximation and interpolation results, Theorems
5.2

and

, are proven in Section

. Section

, on the embeddings of

. Eventually, in

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Assumptions

We recall that Hörmander’s condition is equivalent to the well-known Kalman rank condition for
controllability of linear systems (cf., for instance, Section 9.5 in
[
20
]
); also, it
was shown in
[
13
]
that, up to a change of basis, condition
1.2
is equivalent to
the following

Assumption 2.1

(
Hörmander’s condition).

The matrix
B
takes the block-form

B=\begin{pmatrix}\ast&\ast&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
B_{1}&\ast&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
0&B_{2}&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&B_{r}&\ast\end{pmatrix}

(2.1)

where
B_{j}
is a
(d_{j-1}\times d_{j})
-matrix of rank
d_{j}
with

d\equiv d_{0}\geqslant d_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{r}\geqslant 1,\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{r}d_{j}=N.

(2.2)

In general, the
\ast
-blocks in (
2.1
) are arbitrary. Our second standing assumption is the
following

Assumption 2.2

(
Homogeneity).

All the
\ast
-blocks in (
2.1
) are null.

As proven in
[
13
]
, Assumption
2.2
is equivalent to the fact that the kinetic
Fokker-Planck operator

{\mathscr{K}}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}-Y

(2.3)

is homogeneous of degree two with respect to the family of dilations defined as follows: first of
all, consistently with the block decomposition (
2.1
) of
B
, we write
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
as the
direct sum
x=x^{[0]}+\cdots+x^{[r]}
where
x^{[i]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is defined as

x_{k}^{[i]}=\begin{cases}x_{k}&\text{ if }\,\bar{d}_{i-1}<k\leqslant\bar{d}_{i},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\bar{d}_{i}:=\sum_{j=0}^{i}d_{j},\quad\bar{d}_{-1}:=0,\quad i=0,\dots,r.

Then, we have
{\mathscr{K}}(u(D_{\lambda}))=\lambda^{2}({\mathscr{K}}u)(D_{\lambda})
where

D_{\lambda}(t,x):=(\lambda^{2}t,\hat{D}_{\lambda}x),\qquad\hat{D}_{\lambda}x:=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\lambda^{2i+1}x^{[i]}.

(2.4)

For instance, the Langevin operator (
1.4
) is homogeneous with respect to the dilation group

D_{\lambda}(t,v,p)=(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda v,\lambda^{3}p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
.

2.2
Intrinsic Hölder and Sobolev spaces

In this section we recall the definition of intrinsic Hölder space as given in
[
18
]

and introduce a notion of intrinsic Sobolev space, naturally associated to the system of
vector fields (
1.1
).

Let
h\mapsto e^{hX}z
denote the integral curve of a Lipschitz vector field
X
starting from

z\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, defined as the unique solution of

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dh}e^{hX}z=X\left(e^{hX}z\right),\qquad&h\in\mathbb{R},\\
e^{hX}z|_{h=0}=z.\end{cases}

(2.5)

For the vector fields in (
1.1
), we have

e^{h\partial_{x_{i}}}(t,x)=(t,x+h\mathbf{e}_{i}),\qquad e^{hY}(t,x)=(t+h,e^{hB}x),

(2.6)

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the
i
-th element of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
m_{X}
be a formal weight associated to the vector field
X
. For
\alpha\in\,]0,m_{X}]
, we say
that
u\in C_{X}^{\alpha}
if the quasi-norm

\|u\|_{C^{\alpha}_{X}}:=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\atop h\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left|u\left(e^{hX}z\right)-u(z)\right|}{|h|^{\frac{\alpha}{m_{X}}}}

(2.7)

is finite.

Hereafter, we set
the formal weight of the vector fields
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}
equal
to one and the formal weight of
Y
equal to two
, which is coherent with the homogeneity of the
Fokker-Planck operator
{\mathscr{K}}
with respect to the dilations
D_{\lambda}
in (
2.4
). From

[
18
]
we recall the following

Definition 2.4

(
Intrinsic Hölder spaces).

For
\alpha\in\,]0,1]
we define the Hölder quasi-norms

\displaystyle\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{B}}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}|u|+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}_{\partial_{x_{i}}}}+\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{1,\alpha}_{B}}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}|u|+\left\|{\nabla_{d}u}\right\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{B}}+\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{\alpha+1}_{Y}},

(2.9)

where
\nabla_{d}:=(\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}})
and inductively, for
n\geqslant 2
,

\displaystyle\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}|u|+\left\|{\nabla_{d}u}\right\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}_{B}}+\left\|{Yu}\right\|_{C^{n-2,\alpha}_{B}}.

(2.10)

Next we introduce the intrinsic Sobolev spaces. First, as in
[
17
]
, for any
u\in L^{p}
, with
p\geqslant 1
, we define the fractional Sobolev-Slobodeckij quasi-norm of order
s\in\,]0,1[
along a Lipschitz vector field
X
as

\displaystyle[u]_{X,s,p}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}dz\int_{{|h|\leqslant 1}}\frac{|u(e^{hX}z)-u(z)|^{p}}{|h|^{ps+1}}dh\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(2.11)

Definition 2.5
.

For
p\geqslant 1
we set

\displaystyle|u|_{1,p,B}

\displaystyle:=\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{p}+[u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p},

(2.12)

\displaystyle|u|_{2,p,B}

\displaystyle:=|\nabla_{d}u|_{1,p,B}+\|Yu\|_{p},

(2.13)

and inductively, for
n\geqslant 3
,

\displaystyle|u|_{n,p,B}

\displaystyle:=|\nabla_{d}u|_{n-1,p,B}+|Yu|_{n-2,p,B}.

(2.14)

Definition 2.6

(
Intrinsic Sobolev spaces).

For
p\geqslant 1
we define the Sobolev quasi-norms

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{1,p}_{B}}

\displaystyle:=\|u\|_{p}+|u|_{1,p,B},

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{2,p}_{B}}

\displaystyle:=\|u\|_{p}+{\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{W^{1,p}_{B}}}+\|Yu\|_{p},

(2.16)

and inductively, for
n\geqslant 3
,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{n,p}_{B}}

\displaystyle:=\|u\|_{p}+{\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{W^{n-1,p}_{B}}}+\|Yu\|_{W^{n-2,p}_{B}}.

(2.17)

The following alternative definition of Sobolev quasi-norm is sometimes useful.

Definition 2.7
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\geqslant 1
we set

\displaystyle\vvvert u\vvvert_{W^{n,p}_{B}}

\displaystyle:=\|u\|_{p}+|u|_{n,p,B}.

(2.18)

Clearly we have
\|\cdot\|_{W^{n,p}_{B}}\geqslant\vvvert\cdot\vvvert_{W^{n,p}_{B}}
. In Section

3
, Proposition
3.5
, we prove that
\|\cdot\|_{W^{n,p}_{B}}
and
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert_{W^{n,p}_{B}}
are equivalent and therefore define the same functional spaces. This means
that the intermediate orders quasi-norms are not needed to characterize
W^{n,p}_{B}
.

Remark 2.8
.

Let

\lfloor u\rfloor_{Y,s,p}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}dz\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|u(e^{h{Y}}z)-u(z)|^{p}}{|h|^{ps+1}}dh\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

and notice that

[u]_{Y,s,p}\leqslant\lfloor u\rfloor_{Y,s,p}\leqslant[u]_{Y,s,p}+c_{p,s}\|u\|_{p},\qquad c_{p,s}:=\left(\int_{|h|>1}\frac{2}{|h|^{1+ps}}dh\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Then, if we replace
[u]_{Y,s,p}
by
\lfloor u\rfloor_{Y,s,p}
in Definition
2.6
, we get
equivalent norms.

2.3
Dilation and translation groups

Besides the homogeneity with respect to
D_{\lambda}
in (
2.4
), operator
{\mathscr{K}}
in
(
2.3
) has also the remarkable property of being invariant with respect to the left translations in the group law

\displaystyle(t,x)\circ(s,{\xi})=(t+s,e^{sB}x+{\xi}),\qquad(t,x),(s,{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}.

(2.19)

Indeed, a simple computation shows that, for any
z,{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
,

{\zeta}^{-1}\circ e^{\delta Y}z=e^{\delta Y}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z),\qquad{\zeta}^{-1}\circ e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}z=e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z),\qquad i=1,\dots,d,

(2.20)

where
(t,x)^{-1}=(-t,-e^{-tB}x)
. Analogously, we have (see, for instance,
[
23
]
)

D_{\lambda}e^{\delta Y}(z)=e^{\delta\lambda^{2}Y}\left(D_{\lambda}z\right),\qquad D_{\lambda}e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}(z)=e^{\delta\lambda^{2j+1}\partial_{x_{i}}}D_{\lambda}z,\qquad i=\bar{d}_{j-1}+1,\dots,\bar{d}_{j}.

(2.21)

A
D_{\lambda}
-homogeneous norm on
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
is defined as

\|(t,x)\|_{B}=|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}+|x|_{B},\qquad|x|_{B}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}|x^{[i]}|^{\frac{1}{2i+1}},

(2.22)

and

\mathbf{d}:=2+\sum_{k=0}^{r}(2k+1)d_{k}

(2.23)

is usually called the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
with respect to
D_{\lambda}
.

Lemma 2.9

(
[
16
]
, Proposition
5.1
)
.

There exists
m=m(B)\geqslant 1
such that

\|{\zeta}^{-1}\circ z\|_{B}\leqslant m(\|{\zeta}\|_{B}+\|z\|_{B})\quad m^{-1}\|z\|_{B}\leqslant\|z^{-1}\|_{B}\leqslant m\|z\|_{B}\quad z,{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}.

(2.24)

Remark 2.10
.

Since
e^{\delta Y}z=z\circ(\delta,0)
, by (
2.24
) we have

\frac{1-mc}{{m}}\|z\|_{B}\leqslant\|e^{\delta Y}z\|_{B}\leqslant m(1+c)\|z\|_{B},

(2.25)

for any
|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant c\|z\|_{B}
with
c\in\,]0,\frac{1}{m}[
.

Remark 2.11
.

The matrix
B
is nilpotent of degree
r+1
. In particular, for any

n\leqslant r
we have

B^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{\bar{d}_{n-1}\times d_{0}}&0_{\bar{d}_{n-1}\times d_{1}}&\cdots&0_{\bar{d}_{n-1}\times d_{r-n}}&0_{\bar{d}_{n-1}\times(\bar{d}_{r}-\bar{d}_{r-n})}\\
\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}{B}_{j}&0_{d_{n}\times d_{1}}&\cdots&0_{d_{n}\times d_{r-n}}&0_{d_{n}\times(\bar{d}_{r}-\bar{d}_{r-n})}\\
0_{d_{n+1}\times d_{0}}&\prod\limits_{j=2}^{n+1}{B}_{j}&\cdots&0_{d_{n+1}\times d_{r-n}}&0_{d_{n+1}\times(\bar{d}_{r}-\bar{d}_{r-n})}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0_{d_{r}\times d_{0}}&0_{d_{r}\times d_{1}}&\cdots&\prod\limits_{j={r-n+1}}^{{r}}{B}_{j}&0_{d_{r}\times(\bar{d}_{r}-\bar{d}_{r-n})}\end{pmatrix},

(2.26)

where

\prod_{j=1}^{n}B_{j}=B_{n}B_{n-1}\cdots B_{1},

and
{B}^{n}=0
for
n>r
. Thus

e^{\delta B}=I_{N}+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{B^{j}}{j!}\delta^{j}

is a lower triangular matrix with diagonal
(1,\dots,1)
and therefore it has determinant equal
to 1.

Lemma 2.12
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
u\in L^{p}
, with
p\geqslant 1
, we have

\|u(D_{\lambda})\|_{p}=\lambda^{-\frac{\mathbf{d}}{p}}\|u\|_{p},\qquad|u(D_{\lambda})|_{n,p,B}=\lambda^{n-\frac{\mathbf{d}}{p}}|u|_{n,p,B}

(2.27)

Proof.

The first equality follows by a simple change of variable. Next, for
i=1,\dots,d
we have,
by (

)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x_{i}}u(D_{\lambda})\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}|\partial_{x_{i}}u(D_{\lambda}z)|^{p}dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}|\lambda(\partial_{x_{i}}u)(D_{\lambda}z)|^{p}dz=

(2.28)

(by the change of variable
z^{\prime}=D_{\lambda}z
)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}|(\partial_{x_{i}}u)(z^{\prime})|^{p}\lambda^{-\mathbf{d}}dz^{\prime}=\lambda^{p-\mathbf{d}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|^{p}_{p}.

(2.29)

Similarly

\displaystyle[u(D_{\lambda})]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{|u\left(D_{\lambda}(e^{hY}z)\right)-u(D_{\lambda}z)|^{p}}\frac{dh}{|h|^{1+\frac{p}{2}}}dz

(2.30)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\lambda^{2+p}|u(e^{h\lambda^{2}Y}D_{\lambda}z)-u(D_{\lambda}z)|^{p}}\frac{dh}{|\lambda^{2}h|^{1+\frac{p}{2}}}dz=

(2.31)

(by the change of variables
(h^{\prime},z^{\prime})=(\lambda^{2}h,D_{\lambda}z)
)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{|u(e^{h^{\prime}Y}z^{\prime})-u(z^{\prime})|^{p}}\frac{dh^{\prime}}{|h^{\prime}|^{1+\frac{p}{2}}}\lambda^{-\mathbf{d}}dz=\lambda^{p-\mathbf{d}}[u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}^{p}.

(2.32)

(
2.29
) and (
2.32
) give the second equality for
n=1
. The case
n=2
is analogous and
the general case
n>2
follows by induction.
∎

3
Alternative Sobolev norms and a first interpolation result

3.1
Intrinsic weak derivatives in
W^{n,p}_{B}

By definition, the quasi-norm
|\cdot|_{n,p,B}
only controls weak derivatives of order
n
and

n-1
, which are made up of compositions of
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}
and
Y
for any possible
permutation. We show that actually a function
u\in W_{B}^{n,p}
supports all the weak derivatives
of intrinsic order
l
,
l\leqslant n
, and for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
we have

Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u\in W^{n-l,p}_{B},\quad 2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}=l,

(3.1)

where

\partial^{\beta}=\partial^{\beta_{1}}_{x_{1}}\cdots\partial^{\beta_{N}}_{x_{N}},\qquad{\langle\beta\rangle_{B}:=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(2i+1)\sum_{k=1+\bar{d}_{i-1}}^{\bar{d}_{i}}\beta_{k}.}

(3.2)

Indeed these derivatives can be recovered by taking appropriate iterated commutators of the vector
fields
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}
and
Y
: exploiting these commutators, we can also rearrange
the terms appearing in
|\cdot|_{n,p,B}
and provide a more explicit characterization which only
make use of the intrinsic derivatives in the form (
3.1
).

First we recall some preliminary notions from
[
18
]
, Section 4. By the structure of the matrix
B
, for any
n=0,\dots,r
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

B^{n}v\in\bigoplus_{i=n}^{r}V_{i},\qquad V_{i}:=\{x^{[i]}\mid x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\},

(3.3)

and
B^{n}=0
for
n>r
. In particular, if
v\in V_{0}
then we have

B^{n}v\in V_{n},\qquad n=0,\dots,r.

(3.4)

Moreover there exist subspaces

V_{0,r}\subseteq V_{0,r-1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq V_{0,1}\subseteq V_{0,0}:=V_{0}

such that the linear map

\psi_{n}:V_{0,n}\longrightarrow V_{n},\qquad\psi_{n}(v):=B^{n}v

(3.5)

is bijective. For
v\in V_{0}
, we introduce the following iterated commutators

{X^{(0)}_{v}:=\sum_{k=1}^{d}v_{k}\partial_{x_{k}},}

and recursively

{X^{(n)}_{v}:=[X^{(n-1)}_{v},Y]=X^{(n-1)}_{v}Y-YX^{(n-1)}_{v},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.}

(3.6)

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u\in W^{n,p}_{B}
. Then, for any
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
2i+1\leqslant n
, and
v\in V_{0}
we have

X^{(i)}_{v}u\in W^{n-2i-1,p}_{B}.

(3.7)

Proof.

We use an induction argument on
n
. If
n\leqslant 2
there is nothing to prove because

X^{(0)}_{v}
is a linear combination of the vector fields
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}
and the
thesis follows by definition.

Assume (
3.7
) is true for some fixed
n>2
and let us prove it for
n+1
. We proceed by
induction on
i
. For
i=0
, again there is nothing to prove. We assume (
3.7
) for some

i>0
such that
2(i+1)+1\leqslant n+1
and prove it for
i+1
. If
u\in W^{n+1,p}_{B}
then

X^{(i+1)}_{v}u=X^{(i)}_{v}Yu-YX^{(i)}_{v}u.

Here
Yu\in W^{n-1,p}_{B}
by definition and therefore
X^{(i)}_{v}Yu\in W^{n-1-(2i+1),p}_{B}
by the
inductive hypothesis on
n
. On the other hand
X^{(i)}_{v}u\in W^{n+1-(2i+1),p}_{B}
by the inductive
hypothesis on
i
and therefore
YX^{(i)}_{v}u\in W^{n+1-(2i+1)-2,p}_{B}
by definition. Then
(
3.7
) holds for
n+1
, for any
i
,
2i+1\leqslant n+1
, and this concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
u\in W^{n,p}_{B}
. Then for any
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
2i+1\leqslant n
, we
have

{\partial_{x_{j}}u\in W^{n-2i-1,p}_{B},\qquad j=1+\bar{d}_{i-1},\dots,\bar{d}_{i}.}

(3.8)

Proof.

By induction it is not difficult to prove that

X^{(i)}_{v}{\varphi}=\langle B^{i}v,\nabla_{x}{\varphi}\rangle,\qquad{\varphi}\in C^{\infty}.

(3.9)

Next, since
\psi_{i}
in (
3.5
) is bijective, for every
j=1+\bar{d}_{i-1},\dots,\bar{d}_{i}
there exists
w_{j}\in V_{0,i}
such that
B^{i}w_{j}=\mathbf{e}_{j}\in V_{i}
. Then

f:=X^{(i)}_{w_{j}}u\in W^{n-2i-1,p}_{B}
is such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}f(z){\varphi}(z)dz=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}u(z)X^{(i)}_{w_{j}}{\varphi}(z)dz=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}u(z)\partial_{x_{j}}{\varphi}(z)dz,\quad{\varphi}\in C_{0}^{\infty},

which means that
f
is the weak derivative
\partial_{x_{j}}u
.
∎

By Proposition
3.2
and the definition of intrinsic Sobolev spaces we eventually infer the
following:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
u\in W^{n,p}_{B}
. Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\beta}
such that
2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}=l\leqslant n
, we have

Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u\in W^{n-l,p}_{B}.

(3.10)

Corollary 3.4
.

The following quasi-norms are equivalent:

i)

|u|_{n,p,B}
;

ii)

\sum_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}=n}\|Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u\|_{p}+\sum_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}=n-1}[Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p};

(3.11)

iii)

\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}=n-1}|Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u|_{1,p,B},&n=2l+1,\ l\in\mathbb{N},\\
\sum\limits_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}=n-1}|Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u|_{1,p,B}+\|Y^{l}u\|_{p},&n=2l,\ l\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

(3.12)

Proof.

By induction it is not difficult to check that
|u|_{n,p,B}
controls all the
L^{p}
-norms
of the
n
th-order derivatives that are compositions of
Y
and
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}
for any
possible permutation, as well as the fractional quasi-norms of the
(n-1)
th-order derivatives.
Then it suffices to note that, by (
3.6
) we have

X^{(0)}_{v}Y^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}Y^{i}X_{v}^{(n-i)},\qquad v\in V_{0};

(3.13)

then, proceeding as in the proof of Proposition
3.2
to rearrange the derivatives, we get
(
3.11
).

Moreover, by definition we have

\sum_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}=n-1}|Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u|_{1,p,B}=\sum_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}=n-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|{\partial_{x_{j}}Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}}u\|_{p}+[Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}\right),

and the fractional part of the quasi-norm coincides with (
3.11
). As for the first term in
the sum we need to distinguish two cases: if
n=2l+1
for some
l\in\mathbb{N}
, then we get an
equivalence with (
3.11
) by rearranging the derivatives as in the proof of Proposition

3.2
; indeed, compared to
W^{n-1,p}_{B}
we have the additional set of Euclidean derivatives

\partial_{x_{j}}
,
j=1+\bar{d}_{l-1},\dots,\bar{d}_{l}
which can be recovered from

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{j}}Y^{l}u
by (
3.13
), and similarly for the mixed derivatives. If

n=2l
we have the derivatives
Y^{l}u
that cannot be written as sums of iterated commutators and
thus we get (
3.12
).
∎

3.2
Interpolation inequality and equivalence of the norms
\|\cdot\|_{W^{n,p}_{B}}
and
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert_{W^{n,p}_{B}}

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
1\leqslant n{<m}
and
p\geqslant 1
. There exists
c=c(m,p,B)
such that

|u|_{n,p,B}\leqslant c\left(\varepsilon|u|_{m,p,B}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{n}{m-n}}\|u\|_{p}\right),\qquad u\in W^{m,p}_{B},\quad\varepsilon>0.

(3.14)

In particular the norms
\|\cdot\|_{W^{n,p}_{B}}
and
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert_{W^{n,p}_{B}}
are
equivalent.

Proof.

The proof is based on a two-step induction.

Step 1: case
n=1
and
m=2
. The estimate

\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|_{p}\lesssim\|u\|_{p}+\|\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}u\|_{p},\qquad i=1,\dots,d,

is standard (cf. for instance
[
2
]
, Chapter 5). On the other hand,
by Fubini’s Theorem we have

\displaystyle[u]^{p}_{{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dh}{|h|^{1+\frac{p}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}J_{p}(z,h)dz,\qquad J_{p}(z,h):=|u(e^{hY}z)-u(z)|^{p}.

(3.15)

By the mean value theorem along the vector field
Y
, for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and
h\in[-1,1]
,

h\neq 0
, there exists
|\bar{h}|\leqslant|h|
such that
|J_{p}(z,h)|\leqslant|Yu(e^{\bar{h}Y}z)|^{p}|h|^{p}
:
then, by a change of variable and recalling Remark
2.11
,

[u]^{p}_{{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}}\leqslant 2\|Yu\|^{p}_{p}\int_{0}^{1}|h|^{\frac{p}{2}-1}dh\leqslant\frac{4}{p}\|Yu\|^{p}_{p}.

Thus we obtain

|u|_{1,p,B}\lesssim|u|_{2,p,B}+\|u\|_{p}.

(3.16)

The thesis follows by a scaling argument: indeed, applying (
3.16
) to

u(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}\cdot)
, by (
2.27
) we get

\varepsilon^{1-\frac{\mathbf{d}}{p}}|u|_{1,p,B}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2-\frac{\mathbf{d}}{p}}|u|_{2,p,B}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{\mathbf{d}}{p}}\|u\|_{p}.

Step 2: induction on
n,m
with
m=n+1
.
We first prove the preliminary
interpolation inequality:

\|Yu\|_{p}\lesssim\varepsilon[Yu]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}+\varepsilon^{-1}[u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p},\qquad u\in W^{3,p}_{B},\quad\varepsilon>0.

(3.17)

We have

u(e^{Y}z)-u(z)-Yu(z)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(Yu(e^{\delta Y}z)-Yu(z)\right)d\delta,

and therefore

\displaystyle\|Yu\|^{p}_{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}}\left|u(e^{Y}z)-u(z)-\int_{0}^{1}\left(Yu(e^{\delta Y}z)-Yu(z)\right)d\delta\right|^{p}dz\leqslant

(3.18)

(by the triangular and Hölder inequalities)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}|u(e^{Y}z)-u(z)|^{p}dz+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{0}^{1}|Yu(e^{\delta Y}z)-Yu(z)|^{p}d\delta dz=:I_{1}+I_{2},

(3.19)

where

I_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|Yu(e^{\delta Y}z)-Yu(z)|^{p}}{\delta^{1+\frac{p}{2}}}\delta^{1+\frac{p}{2}}d\delta dz\leqslant\frac{1}{2}[Yu]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}

(3.20)

and

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{0}^{1}|u(e^{Y}z)-u(e^{\delta Y}z)|^{p}d\delta dz+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{0}^{1}|u(e^{\delta Y}z)-u(z)|^{p}d\delta dz=

(3.21)

(by the change of variables
z^{\prime}=e^{Y}z
and
\delta^{\prime}=\delta-1
)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{-1}^{0}|u(e^{\delta^{\prime}Y}z^{\prime})-u(z^{\prime})|^{p}d\delta^{\prime}dz^{\prime}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{0}^{1}|u(e^{\delta Y}z)-u(z)|^{p}d\delta dz\leqslant[u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p},

(3.22)

reasoning as for
I_{2}
in the last step. Then (
3.17
) follows by a scaling argument.

Next we prove that if, for some
{\bar{n}}\in\mathbb{N}
, (
3.14
)
holds with
n={\bar{n}}
,
m={\bar{n}}+1
then it also holds with
n=\bar{n}+1
and
m=\bar{n}+2
. By Step 1, (
3.12
) and
(
3.17
), if
\bar{n}+1
is
even
we have

\displaystyle|u|_{{\bar{n}}+1,p,B}

\displaystyle{\lesssim}\sum_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}={\bar{n}}}|Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u|_{1,p,B}+{\|Y^{\frac{{\bar{n}}+1}{2}}u\|_{p}}

(3.23)

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{1}\sum_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}={\bar{n}}}\left(\varepsilon\left(|Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u|_{2,p,B}+[Y^{\frac{{\bar{n}}+1}{2}}u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}\right)+\varepsilon^{-1}\left(\|Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u\|_{p}+[Y^{\frac{{\bar{n}}-1}{2}}u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}\right)\right)

(3.24)

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{1}\left(\varepsilon|u|_{{\bar{n}}+2,p,B}+\varepsilon^{-1}|u|_{{\bar{n}},p,B}\right)\leqslant

(3.25)

(by the inductive hypothesis)

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{1}\varepsilon|u|_{{\bar{n}}+2,p,B}+c_{1}c_{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\varepsilon_{1}|u|_{{\bar{n}}+1,p,B}+c_{1}c_{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\varepsilon^{-{\bar{n}}}_{1}\|u\|_{p}.

(3.26)

If
\bar{n}+1
is
odd
, by (
3.12
) we derive the same estimate only exploiting Step 1.
To conclude it suffices to take
\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2c_{1}c_{2}}
.

Step 3: backward induction on
n
.
Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m>2
, be fixed. We prove that
if (
3.14
) is true for
m
and
n={\bar{n}}
for some
\bar{n}\in\{2,\dots,m-1\}
, then it is
also true for
m
and
n=\bar{n}-1
.

By Step 2 we have

\displaystyle|u|_{{\bar{n}}-1,p,B}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}|u|_{{\bar{n}},p,B}+\varepsilon_{1}^{-({\bar{n}}-1)}\|u\|_{p}\lesssim

(3.27)

(by the inductive hypothesis)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}\left(\varepsilon_{2}|u|_{m,p,B}+\varepsilon^{-{\frac{{\bar{n}}}{m-{\bar{n}}}}}_{2}\|u\|_{p}\right)+\varepsilon_{1}^{-({\bar{n}}-1)}\|u\|_{p}.

(3.28)

Letting now
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}
and
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{m-({\bar{n}}-1)}}
, we get

|u|_{{\bar{n}}-1,p,B}\lesssim\varepsilon|u|_{m,p,B}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{{\bar{n}}-1}{m-({\bar{n}}-1)}}\|u\|_{p},

which concludes the proof.
∎

4
Taylor expansion in
W^{n,p}_{B}

According to
[
18
]
, the
n
-th order
B
-Taylor polynomial of
u
around

{\zeta}=(s,\xi)
is formally defined as

T_{n}u({\zeta},z):=\sum_{{0\leqslant 2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}\leqslant n}}\frac{(t-s)^{k}(x-e^{(t-s)B}\xi)^{\beta}}{k!\,\beta!}Y^{k}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}u(s,\xi),\qquad z=(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1},

(4.1)

with
\langle\beta\rangle_{B}
as in (
3.2
).
The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
p\geqslant 1
. There exists
c=c(n,p,B)
such that, for any
u\in W^{n+1,p}_{B}\cap C^{\infty}
we have

\displaystyle\|u-T_{n}u(\cdot\circ{\zeta},\cdot)\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant c\|{\zeta}\|^{n+1}_{B}\|u\|_{W^{n+1,p}_{B}},\qquad{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}.

(4.2)

The proof is based on an induction procedure developed in
[
18
]
to derive the

C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
estimate of the remainder. For completeness, here we give a fairly comprehensive
presentation of the main lines, and refer to
[
18
]
for the details of the construction.
To simplify the exposition we first split the proof in different steps, corresponding to
particular cases of (
4.2
).

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists
c=c(n,p)
such that, for any
u\in W^{n+1,p}_{B}\cap C^{\infty}
and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\|u(e^{\delta Y}\cdot)-\sum_{k=0}^{[n/2]}\frac{\delta^{k}}{k!}Y^{k}u\|_{p}

\displaystyle{\leqslant c}|\delta|^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\|u\|_{W^{n+1,p}_{B}},

(4.3)

where
[n/2]
denotes the integer part of
n/2
.

Proof.

We first check that, for
u\in W^{1,p}_{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\|u(e^{\delta Y}\cdot)-u\|_{p}

\displaystyle{\lesssim}|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}{[u]_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}},\qquad{|\delta|\leqslant 1}.

(4.4)

Without loss of generality, we assume
\delta{\in(0,1]}
. Adding and subtracting
u(e^{hY}\cdot)
and integrating on
h\in[0,\delta]
, we have

\displaystyle\delta\|u(e^{\delta Y}\cdot)-u\|^{p}_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\delta}\|u(e^{\delta Y}\cdot)-u(e^{hY}\cdot)\|^{p}_{p}dh+\int_{0}^{\delta}\|u(e^{hY}\cdot)-u\|^{p}_{p}dh=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

(4.5)

Then we have

I_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{|u(e^{hY}z)-u(z)|^{p}}{h^{1+\frac{p}{2}}}h^{1+\frac{p}{2}}dh\,{dz}{\leqslant\frac{1}{2}}|\delta|^{1+\frac{p}{2}}[u]^{p}_{Y,\frac{1}{2},p}.

By the change of variable
\bar{z}=e^{\delta Y}z
,
\bar{h}=h-\delta
and Remark
2.11
, the
term
I_{1}
is analogous, and thus we get (
4.4
). Similarly, we also see that if
u\in W^{2,p}_{B}
then

\displaystyle\|u(e^{\delta Y}\cdot)-u\|_{p}

\displaystyle{\lesssim}|\delta|\|Yu\|_{p}{\lesssim}|\delta|\|u\|_{W^{2,p}_{B}}.

(4.6)

More generally, for
u\in W^{n+1,p}_{B}\cap C^{\infty}
, by the mean-value theorem along the vector field
Y
, for some
\bar{\delta}
such that
|\bar{\delta}|\leqslant|\delta|
we have

u(e^{\delta Y}z)-\sum_{i=0}^{[n/2]}\frac{\delta^{i}}{i!}Y^{i}u(z)=\frac{\delta^{[n/2]}}{[n/2]!}\left(Y^{[n/2]}u(e^{\bar{\delta}Y}z)-Y^{[n/2]}u(z)\right).

(4.7)

Now, if
n=2h
for some
h\in\mathbb{N}
then
Y^{h}u\in W^{1,p}_{B}
: thus (
4.3
) follows by
combining (
4.7
) with (
4.4
) applied to
Y^{h}u
. Similarly, if
n=2h+1
for some

h\in\mathbb{N}
then (
4.3
) follows by combining (
4.7
) with (
4.6
) applied to

Y^{h}u\in W^{2,p}_{B}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists
c=c(p,B)
such that

\|u(\cdot\circ(0,{\xi}))-u\|_{p}{\leqslant c}|{\xi}|_{B}\|u\|_{W^{1,p}_{B}},\qquad u\in W^{1,p}_{B},\quad{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(4.8)

Proof.

We have the standard inequality

\|u(e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}\cdot)-u\|_{p}{\leqslant}|\delta|\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|_{p},\qquad i=1,\dots,d.

(4.9)

Notice that, for
u\in W^{1,p}_{B}
, we have
L^{p}
-bounds only on the first
d
spatial derivatives. Thus, in order to prove (
4.8
) we must exploit estimate (
4.4
) and connect any
arbitrary point
z=(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
to
z\circ(0,{\xi})=(t,x+{\xi})
through a chain of integral
curves associated only to the vector fields
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}
and
Y
.

To do so, we define a sequence of points
(z_{k}=(t,{x(k)}))_{k=0,\cdots,r}
adjusting, at any step
k
, the set of variables of the
layer
V_{k}
in (
3.3
). Following
[
18
]
, Lemma 4.22 we set

v_{0}=\frac{{\xi}^{[0]}}{|{\xi}^{[0]}|},\qquad|\delta_{0}|=|{\xi}^{[0]}|,

and

z_{-1}=z,\qquad z_{0}=\gamma^{(0)}_{v_{0},\delta_{0}}(z_{-1}):=e^{\delta_{0}\langle v_{0},{\nabla}\rangle}z=(t,{x+{\xi}^{[0]}}).

For
k=1,\dots,r
let

z_{k}=\gamma^{(k)}_{v_{k},\delta_{k}}(z_{k-1}):=e^{-\delta_{k}^{2}Y}\left(\gamma^{(k-1)}_{v_{k},-\delta_{k}}\left(e^{\delta_{k}^{2}Y}\left(\gamma^{(k-1)}_{v_{k},\delta_{k}}(z_{k-1})\right)\right)\right),\qquad\delta_{k}=|w_{k}|^{\frac{1}{2k+1}},

where
v_{k}=w_{k}/|w_{k}|
and
w_{k}
is the unique vector in
V_{0,k}\subset V_{0}
such that

B^{k}w_{k}={\xi}^{[k]}+x^{[k]}-x^{[k]}(k-1)
. Importantly, it can be proven by induction that,
for any
v\in V_{0}
we have

\gamma^{(k)}_{v,\delta}(t,x)=(t,x+S_{k}(\delta)v),\qquad S_{k}(\delta)v=(-1)^{k}\sum_{h\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\atop|h|\leqslant r}\frac{(-B)^{|h|}}{h!}\delta^{2|h|+1}v\in\bigoplus_{j=k}^{r}V_{j}.

In other words the flow
\gamma^{(k)}_{v,\delta}
only affects the set of variables
[k:r]
. Moreover,

\gamma^{(r)}_{v_{r},\delta_{r}}(z_{r-1})=(t,x+{\xi})
by construction. Notice also that at any step,
\delta_{k}
does
not depend on
x
and also, the specific choice of
w_{k}
implies
\delta_{k}\leqslant c_{B}|{\xi}|_{B}
(cf.

[
18
]
, Lemma 4.22).

We are ready to prove (
4.8
): by the Minkowski inequality we have

\|u(\cdot\circ(0,{\xi}))-u\|^{p}_{p}\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}|u(z_{k})-u(z_{k-1})|^{p}dz=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\|u(\gamma_{v_{k},\delta_{k}}^{(k)})-u\|_{p}^{{p}},

where we exploited the changes of variables

z^{\prime}=z_{k-1}\equiv z_{k-1}(z)
in the last step: here we use the fact that, by
Remark
2.11
, the Jacobian of the change of variables has determinant equal to one. Then the proof is completed once we have proved that, for any
k,i\in\{0,\dots,r\}
we have

{\|u(\gamma_{v_{i},\delta_{i}}^{(k)})-u\|_{p}\lesssim\delta_{i}\|u\|_{W^{1,p}_{B}}\lesssim|{\xi}|_{B}\|u\|_{W^{1,p}_{B}}.}

(4.10)

We proceed by induction on

(4.11)

\displaystyle+\|u(\gamma_{v_{i},-\delta_{i}}^{({k})})-u\|_{p}+\|u(e^{-\delta^{2}_{i}Y}\cdot)-u\|_{p},

(4.12)

and the thesis follows from the inductive step and (

∎

We are ready to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We prove that, for any

,
{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and

\|u(\cdot\circ{\zeta})-T_{n}u(\cdot,\cdot\circ{\zeta})\|_{p}{\leqslant c}\|{\zeta}\|^{n+1}_{B}\|u\|_{W^{n+1,p}_{B}}.

(4.13)

Estimate (

\displaystyle u(z\circ{\zeta})-T_{n}u(z,z\circ{\zeta})=\underbrace{u(z\circ{\zeta})-T_{n}u(e^{sY}z,z\circ{\zeta})}_{=:F_{1}(z,{\zeta})}+\underbrace{T_{n}u(e^{sY}z,z\circ{\zeta})-T_{n}u(z,z\circ{\zeta})}_{=:F_{2}(z,{\zeta})}.

By definition

thus we have

\displaystyle F_{2}(z,{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\langle\beta\rangle_{B}\leqslant n}\frac{{\xi}^{\beta}}{\beta!}(\partial^{\beta}u)(e^{sY}z)-\sum_{2k+\langle\beta\rangle_{B}\leqslant n}\frac{s^{k}{\xi}^{\beta}}{k!\,\beta!}(Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}u)(z)

First notice that, by a change of variable we have

\|F_{1}(\cdot,{\zeta})\|_{p}=\|u(\cdot\circ(0,{\xi}))-T_{n}u(\cdot,\cdot\circ(0,{\xi}))\|_{p}.

The case

4.3

\displaystyle u(z\circ(0,{\xi}))-T_{\bar{n}}u(z,z\circ(0,{\xi}))

Remark 4.4
.

By Theorem

, for any

Appendix A
Interpolation

We briefly recall some basic tool and notion from interpolation theory: for a comprehensive
presentation of the subject we refer, for instance, to
[
14
]
,
[
29
]
and

[
2
]
.

Given two real Banach spaces
Z_{1},Z_{2}
, we write
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
if
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
have the same
elements with equivalent norms; we write
Z_{1}\subseteq Z_{2}
if
Z_{1}
is continuously embedded in

Z_{2}
. The pair
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
is called an
interpolation pair
if both
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are
continuously embedded in some Hausdorff topological vector
space: in this case, the intersection
Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}
and the sum
Z_{1}+Z_{2}
endowed with the norms

\|u\|_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}:=\max\{\|u\|_{Z_{1}},\|u\|_{Z_{2}}\},\qquad\|u\|_{Z_{1}+Z_{2}}:=\inf_{u_{1}\in Z_{1},\,u_{2}\in Z_{2}\atop u=u_{1}+u_{2}}(\|u_{1}\|_{Z_{1}}+\|u_{2}\|_{Z_{2}}),

are Banach spaces. For any
t>0
and
u\in Z_{1}+Z_{2}
, we set

K(t,u)\equiv K(t,u;Z_{1},Z_{2}):=\inf_{u_{1}\in Z_{1},\,u_{2}\in Z_{2}\atop u=u_{1}+u_{2}}(\|u_{1}\|_{Z_{1}}+t\|u_{2}\|_{Z_{2}}).

(A.1)

Any Banach space
E
such that

Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}\subseteq E\subseteq Z_{1}+Z_{2},

(A.2)

is called an
intermediate space
. Among these, for
0<\theta<1
and
1\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
, we
have the
real interpolation space

(Z_{1},Z_{2})_{\theta,p}
consisting of
u\in Z_{1}+Z_{2}

such that

\|u\|_{\theta,p}:=\|t^{-\theta}K(t,u)\|_{L^{p}_{*}}<\infty

(A.3)

where

L^{p}_{*}=L^{p}_{*}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})

denotes the
L^{p}
space with respect to the measure
\tfrac{dt}{t}
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
L^{\infty}_{*}:=L^{\infty}
.

Proposition A.1

(
[
15
]
, Prop. 1.20)
.

For an intermediate space
E
the following conditions are equivalent:

i)

(Z_{1},Z_{2})_{\theta,1}\subseteq E
;

ii)

there exists a constant
c
such that

\|u\|_{E}\leqslant c\|u\|_{Z_{1}}^{1-\theta}\|u\|_{Z_{2}}^{\theta},\qquad u\in Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}.

(A.4)

In the very particular case
Z_{2}\subseteq Z_{1}
(for instance, if
Z_{1}
is an
L^{p}
space
and
Z_{2}
is some intrinsic Sobolev space
W^{m,p}_{B}
), we have

Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}=Z_{2},\qquad Z_{1}+Z_{2}=Z_{1},\qquad K(t,u)\leqslant{{\mathord{{\rm min}}}\{\|u\|_{Z_{1}},t\|u\|_{Z_{2}}\}}.

(A.5)

Then, since
t\mapsto K(t,u)
is bounded by (
A.5
), for
\|u\|_{\theta,p}
in
(
A.3
) to be finite, what really matters is only the behaviour of
K(t,u)
near

t=0
.

A.1
Interpolation between
L^{p}
spaces

The
distribution
of a measurable function
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{u}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\text{\rm Leb}(|u|>\lambda),\qquad\lambda\geqslant 0,

(A.6)

while

\displaystyle u^{\ast}(t)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{\lambda\geqslant 0\mid\mu_{u}(\lambda)\leqslant t\},\qquad t\geqslant 0,

(A.7)

is called the
rearranging
of
u
. Distribution and rearranging are decreasing and right
continuous functions. Since

u^{\ast}(t)>\lambda\qquad\text{\rm if and only if}\qquad\mu_{u}(\lambda)>t

we have

\text{\rm Leb}(u^{\ast}>\lambda)=\text{\rm Leb}\left(0\leqslant t<\mu_{u}(\lambda)\right)=\text{\rm Leb}(|u|>\lambda)

(A.8)

i.e.
u
and
u^{\ast}
are equimeasurable and consequently
\|u\|_{L^{p}}=\|u^{\ast}\|_{L^{p}}
.

Definition A.2

(
Weak
L^{p}
spaces).

For
1\leqslant p<\infty
, the weak
L^{p}
(or Marcinkiewicz) space is defined as the space of all
measurable functions
u
such that

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{w}}:=\sup_{\lambda>0}\lambda\mu_{u}(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty

(A.9)

and
L^{\infty}_{w}:=L^{\infty}
.

Clearly
L^{p}\subseteq L^{p}_{w}
and in
general the inclusion is strict: for instance,
u(x)=|x|^{-N/p}\in L^{p}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
but does not
belong to any
L^{q}
. On the other hand

L^{p}_{w}\cap L^{q}_{w}\subseteq L^{r},\qquad 1\leqslant p<r<q\leqslant\infty.

(A.10)

Definition A.3

(
Lorentz spaces).

For
1\leqslant p<\infty
and
1\leqslant q\leqslant\infty
, the Lorentz space
L^{p,q}
is defined as the set
of all measurable functions
u
such that the following quasi-norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p,q}}

\displaystyle:=\|t^{1/p}u^{\ast}(t)\|_{L^{q}_{\ast}}=\begin{cases}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}(t^{1/p}u^{\ast}(t))^{q}\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}&\text{if }1\leqslant q<\infty,\\
\sup\limits_{t\geqslant 0}t^{1/p}u^{\ast}(t)&\text{if }q=\infty,\end{cases}

(A.11)

is finite. We also set
L^{\infty,\infty}=L^{\infty}
.

By Hölder’s inequality

L^{p,q_{1}}\subseteq L^{p,q_{2}}
if
q_{1}\leqslant q_{2}
and more generally we have

L^{p}\equiv L^{p,p}\subseteq L^{p,q}\subseteq L^{p,\infty}\equiv L^{p}_{w},\qquad 1\leqslant p\leqslant q\leqslant\infty.

(A.12)

Lorentz spaces have a classical characterization as interpolation of
L^{p}
spaces (cf.

[
2
]
, Corollary 7.27).

Proposition A.4
.

For any
1\leqslant p_{1}<p<p_{2}\leqslant\infty
and
1\leqslant q\leqslant\infty
, we have

L^{p,q}=\left(L^{p_{1}},L^{p_{2}}\right)_{\theta,q},\qquad\frac{1}{p}=(1-\theta)\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\theta\frac{1}{p_{2}},

(A.13)

and in particular

L^{p,q}=\left(L^{1},L^{\infty}\right)_{1-\frac{1}{p},q}.

(A.14)

Another characterization of Lorentz spaces has been provided by L. Tartar with the aim of studying
improved Sobolev’s embedding theorems.

Lemma A.5

(
[
29
]
, Lemma 29.4)
.

Given a measurable function
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\mu_{u}(\lambda)<\infty
for any
\lambda>0
, we
consider a decreasing sequence
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that

u^{\ast}(e^{k})\leqslant a_{k}\leqslant u^{\ast}(e^{k}-),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(A.15)

Then, for
{1\leqslant p}<\infty
and
1\leqslant q\leqslant\infty
, we have

u\in L^{p,q}\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad e^{k/p}a_{k}\in\ell^{q}(\mathbb{Z}).

Moreover, if
a_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
, then

u\in L^{p,q}\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad e^{k/p}(a_{k}-a_{k+1})\in\ell^{q}(\mathbb{Z}).

Notice that from (
A.15
) it follows that

\mu_{u}(a_{k})\leqslant e^{k}\leqslant\mu_{u}(a_{k}-)\leqslant\mu_{u}(a_{k+1}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(A.16)
