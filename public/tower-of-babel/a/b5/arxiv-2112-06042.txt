On the fundamental solution for degenerate Kolmogorov equations with rough coefficients
Francesca Anceschi, Annalaura Rebucci

Abstract
. The aim of this work is to prove the existence of a fundamental solution associated to the Kolmogorov equation ℒ ​ u = f \mathscr{L}u=f with measurable coefficients in the dilation invariant case. Moreover, we prove Gaussian upper and lower bounds for it, and other related properties. Key words: Kolmogorov equation, weak regularity theory, ultraparabolic, fundamental solution, potential theory AMS subject classifications: 35K70, 35E05, 35D30, 35Q84, 35H20, 35B65, 35R30, 35B45



1.
Introduction

The aim of this work is to prove the existence of a fundamental solution for a
second order partial differential equation of Kolmogorov type with measurable coefficients of the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}u(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{m_{0}}\partial_{x_{i}}\left(a_{ij}(x,t)\partial_{x_{j}}u(x,t)\right)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{0}}b_{i}(x,t)\partial_{x_{i}}u(x,t)+

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{ij}x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}u(x,t)-\partial_{t}u(x,t)+c(x,t)u(x,t)=0,

where
z=(x,t)=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and
1\leq m_{0}\leq N
. In particular, the matrices
A_{0}=(a_{ij}(x,t))_{i,j=1,\ldots,m_{0}}
and
B=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,N}
satisfy the following structural assumptions.

(H1)

The matrix
A_{0}
is symmetric with real measurable entries, i.e.
a_{ij}(x,t)=a_{ji}(x,t)
, for every
i,j=1,\ldots,m_{0}
.
Moreover, there exist two positive constants
\lambda
and
\Lambda
such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{0}}a_{ij}(x,t)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2}

for every
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}
. The matrix B has constant entries.

(H2)

The
principal part operator
\mathscr{K}
of
\mathscr{L}
is hypoelliptic, where
\mathscr{K}
is defined as

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}u(x,t):=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{0}}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}u(x,t)+\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{ij}x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}u(x,t)-\partial_{t}u(x,t),

and it is dilation invariant with respect to the family of dilations
({\delta}_{r})_{r>0}
defined in (
2.10
).

Note that we allow the operator
\mathscr{L}
to be strongly degenerate whenever
m_{0}<N
. However, it is known that the
first order part of
\mathscr{L}
may induce a strong regularizing property. Indeed, under suitable assumptions on the
matrix
B
, the operator
\mathscr{L}
is hypoelliptic, namely every distributional solution
u
to
\mathscr{L}u=f
defined in
some open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
belongs to
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
and it is a classical solution to
\mathscr{L}u=f
,
whenever
f\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. We refer to Section
2
for additional information on this fact.
Lastly, when
\mathscr{L}
is uniformly parabolic (i.e.
m_{0}=N
and
B\equiv\mathbb{O}
), the assumption
(H2)
is trivially satisfied.
Indeed, in this case the heat operator is the principal part operator
\mathscr{K}
.

In order to expose our main results we first need to introduce some preliminary notation.
From now on, we consider the strip
S_{T_{0}T_{1}}:=(T_{0},T_{1})\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and in accordance with the scaling of the differential equation (see (
2.10
) below)
we split the coordinate
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
as

(1.4)

x=\big(x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(\kappa)}\big),\qquad x^{(0)}\!\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}},\quad x^{(j)}\!\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{j}},\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,\kappa\},

where every
m_{j}
is a positive integer such that

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\kappa}m_{j}=N\qquad\text{and}\qquad N\geq m_{0}\geq m_{1}\geq\ldots\geq m_{\kappa}\geq 1.

Thus, here and in the sequel we denote

\displaystyle D=(\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{N}}),\quad D_{m_{0}}=(\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{m_{0}}}),\quad\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\quad{\text{\rm div}},

the gradient, the partial gradient in the first
m_{0}
components, the inner product and the divergence in
R^{N}
, respectively.
Moreover, we introduce the matrix

\displaystyle A(x,t)=\left(a_{ij}(x,t)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N},

where

, for every

, are the coefficients appearing in (

\displaystyle Y:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{ij}x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}u(x,t)-\partial_{t}u(x,t)\quad\text{and}\quad b:=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m_{0}},0,\ldots,0).

Now, we are in a position to rewrite the operator
\mathscr{L}
in the following compact form

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}u={\text{\rm div}}(ADu)+Yu+\langle b,Du\rangle+cu

and we recall that its formal adjoint is defined as

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{*}v({\xi},{\tau})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{0}}\partial_{\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle\left(a_{ij}(\xi,\tau)\partial_{\xi_{j}}v({\xi},{\tau})\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}\partial_{\xi_{i}}(b_{i}(\xi,\tau)v({\xi},{\tau}))

\displaystyle+(c-{\text{\rm Tr}}(B))v({\xi},{\tau})+Y^{*}v(\xi,\tau)

where

Y^{*}v(\xi,\tau):=-\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{ij}\xi_{j}\partial_{\xi_{i}}v({\xi},{\tau})+\partial_{\tau}v({\xi},{\tau}).

We denote by
{\mathcal{D}}(S_{T_{0}T_{1}})
the set of
C^{\infty}
functions compactly supported in
S_{T_{0}T_{1}}
.
From now on,
H^{1}_{x^{(0)}}
denotes the Sobolev space of functions

with
distributional gradient
D_{m_{0}}u
lying in
(L^{2}({\Omega}_{m_{0}}))^{m_{0}}
, i.e.

H^{1}_{x^{(0)}}:=\left\{u\in L^{2}({\Omega}_{m_{0}}):\,D_{m_{0}}u\in(L^{2}({\Omega}_{m_{0}}))^{m_{0}}\right\},

and we set

\|u\|_{H^{1}_{x^{(0)}}}:=\|u\|_{L^{2}({\Omega}_{m_{0}})}+\|D_{m_{0}}u\|_{L^{2}({\Omega}_{m_{0}})}.

In a standard manner, see for instance
[
9
]

denotes the closure of

in the norm

that is explicitly computed as follows:

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}=\int_{{\Omega}_{N-m_{0}+1}}\|u(\cdot,y,t)\|_{H^{1}_{x^{(0)}}}^{2}dy\,dt+\int_{{\Omega}_{N-m_{0}+1}}\|Yu(\cdot,y,t)\|^{2}_{H^{-1}_{x^{(0)}}}dy\,dt,

where
y=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(\kappa)})
. In particular,
\mathcal{W}
is a Banach space,
and it was firstly introduced in
[
4

as an extension of the natural functional setting that arises in the study of the weak regularity theory for the
kinetic Kolmogorov-Fokker-Planck equation
[

17

18

19
]
.
From now on, we consider the shorthand notation
L^{2}H^{-1}
to denote
L^{2}({\Omega}_{N-m_{0}+1};H^{-1}_{c,x^{(0)}})
.
Now, we introduce the definition of weak solution we consider in our work.

Definition 1.1
.

A function
u\in\mathcal{W}
is a weak solution to (
1.1
) if for every non-negative test function
{\varphi}\in{\mathcal{D}}(S_{T_{0}T_{1}})
, we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle\int_{S_{T_{0}T_{1}}}-\langle ADu,D{\varphi}\rangle+{\varphi}Yu+\langle b,Du\rangle{\varphi}+cu{\varphi}=0.

In the sequel, we will also consider weak sub-solutions to (
1.1
), namely functions
u\in\mathcal{W}
that satisfy the following inequality

(1.9)

\displaystyle\int_{S_{T_{0}T_{1}}}-\langle ADu,D{\varphi}\rangle+{\varphi}Yu+\langle b,Du\rangle{\varphi}+cu{\varphi}\geq 0,

for every non-negative test function
{\varphi}\in{\mathcal{D}}(S_{T_{0}T_{1}})
. A function
u
is a super-solution to (
1.1
) if
-u

is a sub-solution.

Finally, we recall the definition of weak fundamental solution for the operator
\mathscr{L}
, firstly introduced by Lanconelli,
Pascucci and Polidoro in
[

Definition 1.2
.

A

\mathscr{L}
is a continuous and positive function

{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}={\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;x_{0},t_{0})
defined for

,
0\leq T_{0}<t_{0}<t<T_{1}
and any
x,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that:

(1)

{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}={\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(\cdot,\cdot;x_{0},t_{0})
is a weak solution to

\mathscr{L}u=0
in
S_{T_{0}T_{1}}
and
{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}={\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;\cdot,\cdot)
is a weak solution of
\mathscr{L}^{*}u=0

in
S_{T_{0}T_{1}}
;

(2)

for any bounded function
{\varphi}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and any

(1.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}u(x,t)=0\qquad&(x,t)\in(T_{0},T_{1})\times\mathbb{R}^{N},\\
\lim\limits_{(x,t)\to(x_{0},t_{0})\atop t>t_{0}}u(x,t)={\varphi}(x_{0})\qquad&x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}^{*}v(x_{0},t_{0})=0\qquad&(x_{0},t_{0})\in(T_{0},T_{1})\times\mathbb{R}^{N},\\
\lim\limits_{(x_{0},t_{0})\to(x,t)\atop t<t_{0}}v(x_{0},t_{0})={\varphi}(x)\qquad&x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

where

(1.11)

\displaystyle u(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;x_{0},t_{0})\,{\varphi}(x_{0})\,dx_{0},\qquad v(x_{0},t_{0}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;x_{0},t_{0})\,{\varphi}(x)\,dx.

1.1.
Motivation and background

Kolmogorov equations appear in the theory of stochastic processes as linear second order parabolic equations with non-negative characteristic form.
In its simplest form, if
\left(W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
denotes a real Brownian motion, the density
p=p(t,v,y,v_{0},y_{0})
of the stochastic process
(V_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}

(1.16)

\begin{cases}V_{t}=v_{0}+{\sigma}W_{t}\\
Y_{t}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}V_{s}\,ds\end{cases}

is a solution to one of the simplest strongly degenerate Kolmogorov equation, that is

(1.17)

\tfrac{1}{2}{\sigma}^{2}\partial_{vv}p+v\partial_{y}p=\partial_{t}p,\qquad t\geq 0,\qquad(v,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

In 1934 Kolmogorov provided us with the explicit expression of the density
p=p(t,v,y,v_{0},y_{0})
of the above equation (see
[
22
]
)

(1.18)

p(t,v,y,v_{0},y_{0})=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t^{2}}\exp\left(-\tfrac{(v-v_{0})^{2}}{t}-3\tfrac{(v-v_{0})(y-y_{0}-tv_{0})}{t^{2}}-3\tfrac{(y-y_{0}-ty_{0})^{2}}{t^{3}}\right)\quad t>0,

and pointed out it is a smooth function despite the strong degeneracy of (
1.17
).
This immediately suggested that the operator
\mathscr{L}
associated to equation (
1.17
)

(1.19)

\mathscr{L}:=\tfrac{1}{2}{\sigma}^{2}\partial_{vv}+v\partial_{y}-\partial_{t},

is hypoelliptic. Indeed, later on Hörmander considered this operator as a prototype for the family of hypoelliptic operators studied in his seminal work
[
20
]
.

Kolmogorov equations find their application in different research fields. First of all, the process in (
1.16
) is the solution to the Langevin equation

\begin{cases}dV_{t}=dW_{t}\\
dY_{t}=V_{t}\,dt,\end{cases}

and therefore Kolmogorov equations are related to every stochastic process satisfying Langevin equation. In particular, several
mathematical models involving linear and non linear Kolmogorov type equations have also appeared in finance
[
1
]
,

[
8
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
14
]
. Indeed, equations of the form (
1.17
) appear in various models for pricing of
path-dependent financial instruments (cf., for instance,
[
7
]

[
28
]
), where, for example the equation

(1.20)

\partial_{t}P+\tfrac{1}{2}{\sigma}^{2}S^{2}\partial^{2}_{S}P+(\log S)\partial_{A}P+r(S\partial_{S}P-P)=0,\qquad S>0,\,A,t\in\mathbb{R}

arises in the Black and Scholes option pricing problem

\begin{cases}dS_{t}={\mu}S_{t}dt+{\sigma}S_{t}dW_{t}\\
dA_{t}=S_{t}\,dt,\end{cases}

where
{\sigma}
is the volatility of the stock price
S
,
r
is the interest rate of a risckless bond and
P=P(S,A,t)
is the price of the Asian option depending on the price of the stock
S
, the geometric average
A
of the
past price and the time to maturity
t
. In this framework, knowing that the fundamental solution to the Kolmogorov equation exists is helpful for the study of the option pricing problem and allows us to have various advantages when dealing with numerical simulations. For further information on this topic, we refer to
[
3
,
BPV
]
.

Moreover, we recall that the Kolmogorov equation is the prototype for a family of evolution equations arising in kinetic theory of gas, which take the following general form

(1.21)

Yu=\mathcal{J}(u).

In this case, we have that
u=u(v,y,t)
is the density
of particles with velocity
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
and position
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
at time
t
.
Moreover,

Yu:=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}u+\partial_{t}u

is the so called total derivative with respect to time in the phase space

{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
, and
\mathcal{J}(u)
is the collision operator, which can be either
linear or non-linear. For instance, in the usual Fokker-Planck
equation (cf.
[
13
]
,
[
32
]
) we have a linear collision operator of the form

\mathcal{J}(u)=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\,\partial_{v_{i},v_{j}}^{2}u+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\,\partial_{v_{i}}u+au

where
a_{ij}
,
a_{i}
and
a
are functions of
(y,t)
;
\mathcal{J}(u)
can
also occur in divergence form

\mathcal{J}(u)=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{v_{i}}(a_{ij}\,\partial_{v_{j}}u+b_{i}u)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\partial_{v_{i}}u+au.

We also mention the following non-linear collision operator of the
Fokker-Planck-Landau type

\mathcal{J}(u)=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{v_{i}}\big(a_{ij}(z,u)\partial_{v_{j}}u+b_{i}(z,u)\big),

where the coefficients
a_{ij}
and
b_{i}
depend both on
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
and the unknown functions
u
through some integral expression.
Moreover, this last operator is studied as a simplified version of the Boltzmann collision operator (see for instance
[
11
]
,
[
26
]
). For the description
of wide classes of stochastic processes and kinetic models leading to equations of the previous type, we refer to the classical monographies
[
11
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
.

As first observed by Lanconelli and Polidoro in

We recall that, by

We now recall the definition of Hölder continuous function in this framework.

Definition 2.2

(Hölder continuity)

Let

Moreover, we say a function

if

for every compact subset

of
{\Omega}
.

Theorem 3.6

(Gaussian upper bound)
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}
be an operator of the form (

(H1)
-
(H3B)
. Then there exists a positive constant

Proof.

The Gaussian upper bound (

under the stronger assumption that the coefficients

The proof relies on the combination of a Caccioppoli type inequality and a Sobolev type inequality. In order to handle the more general case, we need to replace the Caccioppoli and the Sobolev inequality contained in

\displaystyle\frac{(2p-1)}{2}\,\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{r}}\langle a,D_{m_{0}}v^{2}\rangle\psi^{2}\,+2p\,\int_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{r}}\langle av^{2},D_{m_{0}}\psi\rangle\psi

\displaystyle I_{3}(z)

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be an operator of the form (
1.1
) satisfying assumptions
(H1)
-
(H3B)
. Then there exist two positive constants
R
and
c_{2}
, only dependent on
Q
and
B
, such that

(3.4)

\int_{\left|\delta_{(\sqrt{t-t_{0}})}^{0}\left(y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\right)\right|\leq R}\Gamma(x,t;y,t_{0})dx\geq c_{2},\qquad 0<t-t_{0}\leq 1,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Proof.

We first notice that for a small enough constant
c_{3}
, which depends only on
Q
and
B
, the function

v(y,t_{0}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Gamma(x,t;y,t_{0})-e^{-c_{3}(t-t_{0})},\quad t>t_{0},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

is a weak super-solution of the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}^{*}v(y,t_{0})=-e^{-c(t-t_{0})}(c-{\text{\rm Tr}}(B)+c_{3})\leq 0,\qquad&t>t_{0},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\\
v(y,t)=0,\qquad&y\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

where
\mathscr{L}^{*}
is the adjoint operator defined in (
1.6
). Hence, in virtue of the maximum principle we infer
v\geq 0
, that is

(3.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Gamma(x,t;y,t_{0})\geq e^{-c_{3}(t-t_{0})},\quad t>t_{0},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

We now observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\left|\delta_{(\sqrt{t-t_{0}})}^{0}\left(y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\right)\right|\geq R}\Gamma(x,t;y,t_{0})dx

(3.6)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\left(t-t_{0}\right)^{\frac{Q}{2}}}\int_{\left|\delta_{(\sqrt{t-t_{0}})}^{0}\left(y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\right)\right|\geq R}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{c_{1}}\left|\delta^{0}_{(t-t_{0})^{-\frac{1}{2}}}\left(y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\right)\right|^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle\quad=c_{1}\int_{|z|\geq R}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{c_{1}}|z|^{2}\right)dz,

where in the second line we have used the upper bound (
3.2
) and in the third line we have performed the change of variables
z=\delta_{(\sqrt{t-t_{0}})}^{0}\left(y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\right)
. Combining (
3.5
) and (
3
) and choosing
c_{3}
small enough we obtain the thesis.
∎

We are now in a position to state and prove the following result concerning the Gaussian lower bound of the fundamental solution.

Theorem 3.8

(Gaussian lower bound)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be an operator of the form (
1.1
) satisfying assumptions
(H1)
-
(H3B)
. Then there exists a positive constant
c_{4}
, only dependent on
Q
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and
q
, such that

(3.7)

\Gamma(x,t;y,t_{0})\geq\frac{c_{4}}{\left(t-t_{0}\right)^{\frac{Q}{2}}}e^{-c_{4}\langle C^{-1}(t-t_{0})\left(y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\right),y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\rangle}

for any
0<t-t_{0}\leq 1
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proof.

We restrict ourselves to the case where
x=0
, as the general statement can be obtained from the dilation and translation-invariance of the operator
\mathscr{L}
.
Then, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
R>0
, we set

D_{R}:=\Big\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\left|\delta_{\sqrt{\tau}}^{0}(y-e^{\tau B}\xi)\right|\leq R\Big\}

and we compute

\displaystyle{\text{\rm meas}}(D_{R})=\int_{D_{R}}d\xi

\displaystyle=R^{Q}\int_{\left|\delta_{\sqrt{\tau}}^{0}(y-e^{\tau B}\xi)\right|\leq 1}d\xi=R^{Q}\tau^{Q/2}\int_{\left|(y-e^{\tau B}\xi)\right|\leq 1}d\xi

\displaystyle=R^{Q}\tau^{Q/2}\int_{\left|\xi\right|\leq 1}d\xi=R^{Q}\tau^{Q/2}{\text{\rm meas}}(B_{1}(0))=c_{5}\tau^{Q/2},

where the constant
c_{5}
only depends on
B
and
R
.
We also note that the function
\langle C^{-1}(t-t_{0})\left(y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\right),y-e^{(t-t_{0})B}x\rangle
is bounded by a constant
M
in
D_{R}
(see
[
25
, Lemma 3.3]
).
Lastly, we now set
\tau=\frac{t-t_{0}}{2}
and apply to
\Gamma
the global Harnack inequality stated in Theorem
3.5
, which yields

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Gamma(y,t;y,t_{0})\geq c_{0}e^{-c_{0}\langle C^{-1}(\tau)\left(\xi-e^{\tau B}x\right),\xi-e^{\tau B}x\rangle}\Gamma(\xi,t+\tau;y,t_{0}),\quad y,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Hence, integrating inequality (
3.8
) over
D_{R}
, we infer

\displaystyle\Gamma(y,t;y,t_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{6}}{\tau^{Q/2}}\int_{D_{R}}\Gamma(y,t;y,t_{0})d\xi

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{6}\,c_{0}}{\tau^{Q/2}}\int_{D_{R}}e^{-c_{0}\langle C^{-1}(\tau)\left(\xi-e^{\tau B}x\right),\xi-e^{\tau B}x\rangle}\Gamma(\xi,t+\tau;y,t_{0})d\xi

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{6}\,c_{0}}{\tau^{Q/2}}\int_{D_{R}}e^{-M}\Gamma(\xi,t+\tau;y,t_{0})d\xi

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{7}}{(t-t_{0})^{Q/2}},

where the last inequality is a direct consequence of Lemma
3.7
and the constant
c_{7}
only depends on
Q
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
q
and
B
.

Setting
\tau=\frac{3}{4}(t-t_{0})
and
x=0
, we apply once again Theorem
3.5
and we get

\displaystyle\Gamma(0,t;y,t_{0})

\displaystyle\geq c_{0}e^{-c_{0}\langle C^{-1}(\tau)y,y\rangle}\Gamma(y,t+\tau;y,t_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{8}}{(t-t_{0})^{Q/2}}e^{-c_{0}\langle C^{-1}(\tau)y,y\rangle}\geq\frac{c_{9}}{(t-t_{0})^{Q/2}}e^{-c_{9}\langle C^{-1}(t-t_{0})y,y\rangle},

where the last inequality is a consequence of a property of the covariance matrix
C
(see
[
24
, Remark 4.5]
). This concludes the proof.
∎

\mathscr{L}u=0
in the set

\big\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T)\mid(x,t;x_{0},t_{0})\in{\Omega}_{2}\big\}
.

We next proceed by induction. Let us assume that the sequence
({\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!q-1,{\varepsilon}_{q-1}})_{{\varepsilon}_{q-1}}
on the set

{\Omega}_{q}
has been defined for some
q\in\mathbb{N}
. We extract from it a subsequence
({\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!q,{\varepsilon}_{q}})_{{\varepsilon}_{q}}
converging in

C^{2}({\Omega}_{q})
to some function
{\Gamma}_{q}
, satisfying (
1.5
) in
{\Omega}_{q}
and it agrees with
{\Gamma}_{q-1}
on the set
{\Omega}_{q-1}
.

Next, we define a function
{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}
in the following way: for every
(x,t),({\xi},{\tau})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T)
with

(x,t)\neq({\xi},{\tau})
we choose
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(x,t;{\xi},{\tau})\in{\Omega}_{q}
and we set
{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;{\xi},{\tau}):={\Gamma}_{q}(x,t;{\xi},{\tau})
.
This argument provides us with a non ambiguous definition of
{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}
. Indeed, if
(x,t)\in{\Omega}_{p}
, then
{\Gamma}_{p}(x,t;{\xi},{\tau})={\Gamma}_{q}(x,t;{\xi},{\tau})
.

We next check that
{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}
has the properties listed in the statement of the Theorem
1.3
. As every
{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!\!{\varepsilon}}(x,t;x_{0},t_{0})=0
whenever
t\leq t_{0}
, also
{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;x_{0},t_{0})=0
whenever
t\leq t_{0}
.
For the same reason, it satisfies Theorem
1.5
.
Moreover, for every
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T)
,
(x,t)\mapsto{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;x_{0},t_{0})\in L^{1}_{{\text{\rm loc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T))\cap C^{2}_{{\text{\rm loc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T)\setminus\{(x_{0},t_{0})\})
, and is a weak solution to
\mathscr{L}u=0
in

\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T)\setminus\{(x_{0},t_{0})\}
.

As far as we are concerned with the reproduction property
1.
of Theorem
1.3
,
we use the upper bound in (
1.5
), which yields

{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!\!{\varepsilon}}(x,t;{\xi},{\tau}){\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!\!{\varepsilon}}({\xi},{\tau};x_{0},t_{0})\leq C^{+}\,{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!\!\!{\lambda}^{+}}(x,t;{\xi},{\tau})C^{+}\,{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!\!\!{\lambda}^{+}}({\xi},{\tau};x_{0},t_{0}),

and the reproduction property

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!\!\!{\lambda}^{+}}(x,t;{\xi},{\tau}){\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!\!\!{\lambda}^{+}}({\xi},{\tau};x_{0},t_{0})\,d{\xi}\,d{\tau}={\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!\!\!{\lambda}^{+}}(x,t;x_{0},t_{0})\,<+\infty,

which allows us to use the Lebesgue convergence theorem. Thus the property holds true.

To conclude the proof of Theorem
1.3
we have to verify that for any bounded function
{\varphi}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

the function

(4.1)

u(x,t)\,=\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;{\xi},t_{0})\,{\varphi}({\xi})\,d{\xi}

is such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}u(x,t)=0\qquad\qquad&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T],\\
\lim\limits_{(x,t)\to(y,T)\atop t>T}u(x,t)={\varphi}(y)\qquad&y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.\end{cases}

By the usual standard argument, we differentiate under the integral sign

\mathscr{L}u(x,y,t)\,=\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathscr{L}{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}(x,t;{\xi},t_{0})\,{\varphi}({\xi})\,d{\xi}\,=\,0.

Thus, we are left with the proof of (
1.10
). Thanks to Theorem
2.7
applied to
the regularized operator
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}
, we have that for every
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1]
and for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

the following holds

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{(x,t)\to(y,t_{0})\atop t>t_{0}}u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)={\varphi}(y),\quad\text{where }u_{\varepsilon}(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\Gamma_{\!\!L}}^{\!\!{\varepsilon}}(x,t;y,t_{0})\,{\varphi}(y)\,dy.

Now, thanks to Theorem
1.5
we are able to apply the dominated convergence theorem, and thus for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

\displaystyle{\varphi}(y)=\lim\limits_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\lim\limits_{(x,t)\to(y,t_{0})\atop t>t_{0}}u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\lim\limits_{(x,t)\to(y,t_{0})\atop t>t_{0}}u(x,t).

We conclude the proof by adapting the same argument when considering the function
v
.

\hfill\square
