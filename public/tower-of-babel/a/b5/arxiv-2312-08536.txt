Markov Decision Processes with Noisy State Observation
Amirhossein Afsharrad, Sanjay Lall

Abstract
This paper addresses the challenge of a particular class of noisy state observations in Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), a common issue in various real-world applications. We focus on modeling this uncertainty through a confusion matrix that captures the probabilities of misidentifying the true state. Our primary goal is to estimate the inherent measurement noise, and to this end, we propose two novel algorithmic approaches. The first, the method of second-order repetitive actions, is designed for efficient noise estimation within a finite time window, providing identifiable conditions for system analysis. The second approach comprises a family of Bayesian algorithms, which we thoroughly analyze and compare in terms of performance and limitations. We substantiate our theoretical findings with simulations, demonstrating the effectiveness of our methods in different scenarios, particularly highlighting their behavior in environments with varying stationary distributions. Our work advances the understanding of reinforcement learning in noisy environments, offering robust techniques for more accurate state estimation in MDPs.

Problem Formulation

We consider a standard reinforcement learning problem defined by a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The MDP is characterized by a finite set of states, denoted as
S=\{1,\cdots,n\}
where
n
is the number of states, and a finite set of actions, represented as
A=\{1,\cdots,m\}
with
m
being the number of actions. Denoting
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
as the set of all
n\times n
transition probability matrices, the transition probability function
P:A\to{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
maps actions to
n\times n
transition probability matrices, defined as
P(a)_{ij}=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[s_{t+1}=j|s_{t}=i,a_{t}=a\right]
, where
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the time-step, and
a_{t}
is the action at time
t
. The reward function
R:S\times A\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
determines the immediate reward,
r_{t}=R(s_{t},a_{t},\eta_{t})
, with
r_{t}
as the observed reward at time
t
and
\eta_{t}
as a noise variable, incorporated in
R
to capture stochastic dependencies of the reward on state-action pairs.

This study focuses on the challenge of noisy state observation in MDPs. Considering the finite nature of the state space, we explore state observation noise by defining the probability of observing a state
{\tilde{s}}\in S
different from the actual state
s\in S
. In this context,
{\tilde{s}}
and
s
represent the
observed state
and the
true state
, respectively. This noise is characterized by the state observation confusion matrix
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
, which specifies the probabilities of observing
{\tilde{s}}
when the actual state is
s
, expressed as
C_{ij}=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\tilde{s}}_{t}=j|s_{t}=i\right]
. This matrix is assumed to be time-independent, indicating consistent observation noise characteristics across states. While
S
,
A
, and
P
are known, the reward function
R
and the confusion matrix
C
are unknown. Our objective is to estimate
C
, and given our focus on estimating
C
in settings with absent or sparse rewards, we do not assume a specific reward model, ensuring that our algorithms are not dependent on reward dynamics.

Main Results

3.1
The Method of Second-Order Repetitive Actions

Theorem 1
.

Consider an MDP with noisy observations and observation confusion matrix
C
. Define
Q^{a}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
as the observed state transition matrix at time
t
, where

Q^{a}_{t,ij}=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}=j|{\tilde{s}}_{t}=i,a_{t}=a\right].

Let
\pi_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
represent the probability distribution over the true state at time
t
, expressed as

\pi_{t}=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[s_{t}=i\right].

Then, the following relationship holds:

\mathop{\rm diag}(C^{\top}\pi_{t})Q^{a}_{t}=C^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(\pi_{t})P(a)C.

(1)

Proof provided in Section
4.1
.

Our main goal is to deduce
C
from
Q^{a}_{t}
. An empirical estimate
\hat{Q}^{a}_{t}
enables us to solve (
1
) for
C
. The primary challenge is the time-dependent nature of
Q^{a}_{t}
, as single observations per time-step are insufficient for reliable estimation of
{\hat{Q}}^{a}_{t}
.

To address this, we note that the time dependence in
Q^{a}_{t}
is solely through
\pi_{t}
. Stabilizing
\pi_{t}
over a period allows
Q^{a}_{t}
to become time-independent, enabling us to empirically estimate
Q^{a}_{t}
with
{\hat{Q}}^{a}
and then solve (
1
) for
C
. We achieve this by repeatedly selecting an action
a\in A
for a duration, leading
\pi_{t}
to converge to the stationary distribution
\pi^{a}
of
P(a)
, under the assumption that the Markov process for
P(a)
is irreducible and aperiodic. The observed state transitions then adhere to a constant matrix
Q^{a}
, defined by

\mathop{\rm diag}(C^{\top}\pi^{a})Q^{a}=C^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(\pi^{a})P(a)C.

(2)

This allows us to empirically determine
{\hat{Q}}^{a}
and estimate
C
by minimizing the loss function
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
defined as

{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(C)=\left\|C^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(\pi^{a})P(a)C-\mathop{\rm diag}\left(C^{\top}\pi^{a}\right){\hat{Q}}^{a}\right\|^{2}.

(3)

However, this approach can lead to non-unique solutions for
C
, as demonstrated with an example for
n=2
later. Different
C
matrices can produce the same
Q^{a}
, making it impossible to uniquely determine
C
from (
3
), even with the perfect esitmate
{\hat{Q}}^{a}=Q^{a}
.

To overcome this, we apply the method across various actions. While each action might yield multiple solutions, the common solution across different actions may uniquely identify the actual
C
. We select a subset
A_{0}
of
A
with
|A_{0}|=N
, and for each action in
A_{0}
, repeated for a duration
T
, we estimate
{\hat{Q}}^{a}
and solve for
C
using:

{\hat{C}}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{C}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{a\in A_{0}}\left\|C^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(\pi^{a})P(a)C-\mathop{\rm diag}\left(C^{\top}\pi^{a}\right){\hat{Q}}^{a}\right\|^{2}.

(4)

However, even with multiple actions from
A_{0}
, (
4
) may have multiple solutions. As an example, consider two actions
a,a^{\prime}
with the following probabilities

P(a)=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad Q^{a}=\begin{bmatrix}0.45&0.55\\
0.825&0.175\end{bmatrix},\quad P(a^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}0.3&0.7\\
0.7&0.3\end{bmatrix},\quad Q^{a^{\prime}}=\begin{bmatrix}0.54&0.46\\
0.69&0.31\end{bmatrix}.

Solving (
2
) for both actions, we find two possible solutions for
C
given by

C_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.1\\
0.3&0.7\end{bmatrix},\quad C_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0.3&0.7\\
0.9&0.1\end{bmatrix},

suggesting that both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are solutions of (
4
), illustrating the non-uniqueness issue. This example illustrates that multiple actions do not guarantee a unique solution for
C
. Theorem
2
addresses this issue by providing a necessary and sufficient condition for uniqueness in the
n=2
case.

Theorem 2
.

For
n=2
, a unique solution for

C^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(\pi^{a})P(a)C=\mathop{\rm diag}\left(C^{\top}\pi^{a}\right)Q^{a},\quad a\in\{a_{1},a_{2}\}

(5)

exists iff the stationary distributions of
P(a_{1})
and
P(a_{2})
are distinct, i.e.,
\pi^{a_{1}}\neq\pi^{a_{2}}
.

Proof in Section
4.2
. Theorem
3
generalizes this condition for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

Theorem 3
.

Let
B
be any non-empty strict subset of
{\mathbb{N}}_{n}=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, and define
{\mathcal{B}}=\{B\subset{\mathbb{N}}_{n}|B\neq\emptyset,{\mathbb{N}}_{n}\}
as the set of all such subsets.
Then, if

is symmetric, the set of equations

C^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(\pi^{a})P(a)C=\mathop{\rm diag}\left(C^{\top}\pi^{a}\right)Q^{a},\quad a\in A_{0}

(6)

has a unique solution if for every

, there exist two actions

such that

\sum_{i\in B}\pi^{a}_{i}\neq\sum_{i\in B}\pi^{a^{\prime}}_{i}.

(7)

Proof in Section
4.3
. Note that Theorem
3
’s uniqueness condition, while sufficient, is not strictly necessary and can be relaxed in various ways. The assumption of a symmetric

simplifies the proof but is not essential.

To conclude, the second-order repetitive actions algorithm, involving repetitive actions and recording the last two observed states, estimates
Q^{a}
and
C
. It is called a second-order algorithm as it involves recording only the last two observations and results in a second order equation in
C
. Theorems
2
and
3
ensure solution convergence to the true

. The next section introduces other methods for estimating the confusion matrix.

3.2
The Bayesian Method

In this section, we apply a Bayesian approach to estimate the confusion matrix
C
. This method involves maintaining two probability distributions: one over the unknown parameters characterized by
C
, and the other over the current state of the system given past observations. We introduce two variants of the Bayesian method and analyze their performances in Section
5
.

3.2.1
The First-Order Bayesian Algorithm

The belief state
b_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is defined as

b_{t,i}=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[s_{t}=i|{\tilde{s}}_{t},a_{t-1},\cdots,a_{1},{\tilde{s}}_{0}\right],

(8)

and the probability density function
p_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
gives the probability density at
C
at time
t
conditioned on past observations and actions,
i.e.
,

p_{t}(C_{0})=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[C=C_{0}|{\tilde{s}}_{t},a_{t-1},\cdots,a_{1},{\tilde{s}}_{0}\right].

(9)

Theorem 4
.

Given a history of observations and actions, the belief state
b_{t}
, and the probability density function
p_{t}
at
C
, the update rules are as follows:

(a)

The update rule for the probability density function of
C
:

\displaystyle p_{t+1}(C)

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{t}^{\top}PC_{{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}}}{\int_{C^{\prime}}b_{t}^{\top}PC^{\prime}_{{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}}p_{t}(C^{\prime})\mathrm{d}C^{\prime}}p_{t}(C),

(10)

where
C_{{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}}
is the
{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}
th column of
C
.

(b)

The belief state update rule:

b_{t+1,i}=\int_{C}\frac{C_{i,{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}}\sum_{j}P(a_{t})_{ji}b_{t,j}}{\sum_{k}C_{k,{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}}\sum_{j}P(a_{t})_{jk}b_{t,j}}p_{t+1}(C)\mathrm{d}C

(11)

3.2.2
The Second-Order Bayesian Algorithm

The second-order Bayesian algorithm incorporates the last two observed states into the probability computation.
It requires a modified belief state, denoted by
{\tilde{b}}_{t}
, defined as

{\tilde{b}}_{t,i}=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[s_{t}=i|{\tilde{s}}_{t-1},a_{t-2},\cdots,a_{1},{\tilde{s}}_{0}\right].

Theorem 5
.

Given a history of observations and actions, the current belief state
{\tilde{b}}_{t}
, and the probability density function
p_{t}
at
C
, the update rules are:

(a)

The update rule for the probability density function of
C
:

\displaystyle Q(C)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm diag}\left(C^{\top}{\tilde{b}}_{t}\right)^{-1}C^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}({\tilde{b}}_{t})P(a_{t})C

(12)

\displaystyle p_{t+1}(C)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q(C)_{{\tilde{s}}_{t},{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}}}{\int_{C^{\prime}}Q(C^{\prime})_{{\tilde{s}}_{t},{\tilde{s}}_{t+1}}p_{t}(C^{\prime})\mathrm{d}C^{\prime}}p_{t}(C).

(13)

(b)

The belief state update rule:

{\tilde{b}}_{t+1,i}=\int_{C}\frac{\sum_{j}P(a_{t})_{ji}C_{j,{\tilde{s}}_{t}}{\tilde{b}}_{t,j}}{\sum_{j}C_{j,{\tilde{s}}_{t}}{\tilde{b}}_{t,j}}p_{t+1}(C)\mathrm{d}C

(14)

The proof of this theorem, similar to that of Theorems
1
and
4
, is omitted for brevity. Theorems
4
and
5
provide methods for maintaining and updating probability distributions over

and the true state. Their performance will be compared in Section

Proof of Theorem

\displaystyle Q^{a}_{t,ij}

Multiplying both sides by

6
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper has tackled a particular class of the significant challenge of noisy state observations in Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) by employing a confusion matrix to model this uncertainty. We introduced two novel algorithms for noise estimation. The first, the method of second-order repetitive actions, efficiently estimates the confusion matrix
C
in a finite time frame. Moreover, our analysis reveals that system identifiability is contingent upon the dissimilarity of stationary distributions in action transition probabilities. Our second approach, grounded in Bayesian methods, focuses on maintaining and updating probability distributions over unknown parameters and states. This method provides the flexibility to choose actions freely, yet it faces practical computational challenges, particularly in maintaining distributions over large
n\times n
matrices.

Looking to the future, several avenues for research emerge from our findings. One intriguing direction is extending our approach of second-order observation recording to higher orders, examining its impact on system identifiability and efficiency. Further theoretical exploration is needed to address the general problem of system identifiability. Particularly, A key theoretical question arises from our observation that non-identifiable systems lead to limited solution spaces: the first-order Bayesian method reduces options to a line, whereas the second-order method identifies two distinct solutions. Future research could explore whether more advanced methods exist that can fully resolve this issue, or if it can be theoretically proven that no algorithm can surpass the efficacy of the second-order method. This could significantly advance our understanding of system identifiability in MDPs with noisy observations. In addition, there is substantial scope for application-oriented research. Applying our methodologies to real-world data, exploring other classes of noisy observations within our framework, and tailoring our approaches to specific challenges in different applications offer practical avenues for future exploration.
