Universal Lie Formulas for Higher Antibrackets
Marco MANETTI † and Giulia RICCIARDI ‡§

Abstract
We prove that the hierarchy of higher antibrackets (aka higher Koszul brackets, aka Koszul braces) of a linear operator Δ \Delta on a commutative superalgebra can be defined by some universal formulas involving iterated Nijenhuis–Richardson brackets having as arguments Δ \Delta and the multiplication operators. As a byproduct, we can immediately extend higher antibrackets to noncommutative algebras in a way preserving the validity of generalized Jacobi identities.

Introduction

In particle physics, the fundamental interactions of the standard model, the electroweak and the QCD interactions, are described by non-abelian gauge theories. The presence of a gauge symmetry implies the appearance of unphysical degrees of freedom in the Lagrangian, which stand in the way of the usual quantization methods. Typically, the redundant degrees of freedom are removed through a gauge-fixing procedure. Ghosts, i.e., fields with unphysical statistics, are introduced to compensate for effects of the gauge degrees of freedom and preserve unitarity. The gauge-fixed action retains a nilpotent, odd, global symmetry involving transformations of both fields and ghosts, the Becchi–Rouet–Stora–Tyutin (BRST) symmetry
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
30
]
. The BRST symmetry has played an important role in quantization, renormalization, unitarity, and other aspects of gauge theories. The Batalin–Vilkovisky formalism of antibrackets and antifields
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
18
]
retains BRST symmetry as fundamental principle while dealing with very general gauge theories, included those with open or reducible gauge symmetry algebras. The antibracket formalism covers a broad spectrum of applications, ranging from supergravity to string and topological field theories. From the mathematical point of view, probably the most convenient approach to Batalin–Vilkovisky formalism is based upon a certain hierarchy of (super)symmetric multilinear maps introduced by Koszul
[
20
]
in the framework of differential operators, Calabi–Yau manifolds and symplectic geometry.

Higher antibrackets, also known in literature as higher Koszul brackets or Koszul braces, were defined in
[
1
,
2
]
as a slight variation of Koszul construction and give a convenient generalization of the Batalin–Vilkovisky formalism working when the underlying algebra is not unitary and when the square-zero operator fails to be a differential operator of second order. Several interesting mathematical properties of higher antibrackets have been studied in
[
14
,
21
]
and more recently in
[
25
,
26
]
. In particular it is proved therein that higher antibrackets satisfy the generalized Jacobi identities, and then they provide a structure of strong homotopy Lie algebra, for every square-zero linear operator.

More recently, the formalism of higher antibrackets has been conveniently used in
[
17
]
in the framework of Poisson geometry; here one of the key points was the existence of certain relations involving the linear, bilinear and trilinear antibrackets, the multiplication maps and the Nijenhuis–Richardson bracket on the space of multilinear operators. The initial motivation of this paper is to extend these relations to any order; more precisely we prove that every higher antibracket of a linear operator
\Delta
on a commutative superalgebra can be defined by some universal formulas involving iterated Nijenhuis–Richardson brackets, having as arguments
\Delta
and the multiplication operators. In doing this we also get an explicit description of a universal gauge trivialization (Theorem
2.1
) and of some representations of the Lie algebra of vector fields of the line, which we think interesting in its own right (Section
6
). Moreover, as additional byproduct, some of the proved universal formulas for higher antibrackets,
when applied to operators in noncommutative algebras, give hierarchies of higher antibrackets satisfying generalized Jacobi identities, cf.
[
24
]
.

2
Overview of the main results

Given a commutative superalgebra
A=A^{0}\oplus A^{1}
over a field of characteristic 0 and a linear operator
f\colon A\to A
, the hierarchy of higher antibrackets of
f
is the sequence of operators
\Phi_{f}^{n}\colon A^{\odot n}\to A
,
n>0
, defined by the formulas
[
20
,
25
]
:

\displaystyle\Phi^{1}_{f}(a)=f(a),

\displaystyle\Phi^{2}_{f}(a,b)=f(ab)-f(a)b-(-1)^{|a||b|}f(b)a,

\displaystyle\Phi^{3}_{f}(a,b,c)=f(abc)-f(ab)c-(-1)^{|a|(|b|+|c|)}f(bc)a-(-1)^{|b||c|}f(ac)b

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Phi^3_f(a,b,c)=}{}+f(a)bc+(-1)^{|a||b|}f(b)ac+(-1)^{|c|(|a|+|b|)}f(c)ab,

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots

\displaystyle\Phi^{n}_{f}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\sum_{\sigma\in S(k,n-k)}\epsilon(\sigma)f(a_{\sigma(1)}\cdots a_{\sigma(k)})a_{\sigma(k+1)}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)},

(2.1)

where
A^{\odot n}
is the symmetric
n
th power of
A
, where
|a|=0,1
is the parity of a nonzero homogeneous element
a\in A
, and

S(k,n-k)
is the set of shuffles of type
(k,n-k)
, i.e., the set of permutations
\sigma
of
1,\ldots,n

such that
\sigma(1)<\cdots<\sigma(k)
and
\sigma(k+1)<\cdots<\sigma(n)
. The Koszul sign

\epsilon(\sigma)
is equal to
(-1)^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is the number of pairs
(i,j)
such that

i<j
,
\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)
and
|a_{i}||a_{j}|=1
. The same operators can be conveniently defined by the recursive formulas
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\Phi^{1}_{f}(a)=f(a),

\displaystyle\Phi^{n+1}_{f}(a_{1},\ldots,b,c)=\Phi^{n}_{f}(a_{1},\ldots,bc)-\Phi^{n}_{f}(a_{1},\ldots,b)c-(-1)^{|b||c|}\Phi^{n}_{f}(a_{1},\ldots,c)b,

(2.2)

and differ from the definition given in
[
2
,
14
]
by an extra sign factor appearing because in their set-up the operator
f
acts on the right.
It is immediate to observe that every higher antibracket is a supersymmetric operator, i.e., we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{f}^{n}(\ldots,a_{i+1},a_{i},\ldots)=(-1)^{|a_{i}||a_{i+1}|}\Phi_{f}^{n}(\ldots,a_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots)

for every index
0<i<n
and every sequence
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
of homogeneous elements.
When
A
has a unit
1\in A^{0}
, it is well known, and in any case easy to prove, that
\Phi^{n+1}_{f}=0

if and only if
f(1)=0
and
f
is a differential operator of order
\leq n

[
20
]
.

The importance of higher antibrackets relies essentially on the fact that, up to a degree shifting, they satisfy the generalized Jacobi identities
[
14
,
21
]
: this means that for every pair of operators

f
,
g
we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{n}_{[f,g]}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\Phi_{f}^{i},\Phi_{g}^{n+1-i}\big]

for every
n
, where
[-,-]
denotes the Nijenhuis–Richardson bracket (see Section
3
) on the Lie superalgebra of symmetric operators

\displaystyle D(A)=\operatorname{Hom}^{*}\big(\oplus_{n>0}A^{\odot n},A\big).

The Lie superalgebra
D(A)
contains as special even elements the multiplication operators

\displaystyle\mu_{n}\colon\ A^{\odot n+1}\to A,\qquad\mu_{n}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})=a_{0}\cdots a_{n},\qquad n\geq 0.

The associated adjoint operators

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\colon\ D(A)\to D(A),\qquad\rho_{n}(\omega)=[\mu_{n},\omega]

are even derivations. A tedious but straightforward computation shows that:

\displaystyle\bullet\ \Phi^{2}_{f}=-\rho_{1}(f)=-[\mu_{1},f];

\displaystyle\bullet\ \Phi^{3}_{f}=\dfrac{1}{2}\big(\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}\big)(f)=\dfrac{1}{2}([\mu_{1},[\mu_{1},f]]+[\mu_{2},f]);

\displaystyle\bullet\ \Phi^{4}_{f}=-\dfrac{1}{6}\big(\rho_{1}^{3}+3\rho_{1}\rho_{2}+6\rho_{3}\big)f=-\dfrac{1}{6}([\mu_{1},[\mu_{1},[\mu_{1},f]]]+3[\mu_{1},[\mu_{2},f]]+6[\mu_{3},f]);

\displaystyle\bullet\ \Phi^{5}_{f}=\left(\dfrac{1}{30}\rho_{1}^{4}+\dfrac{1}{5}\rho_{1}^{2}\rho_{2}+\rho_{1}\rho_{3}+3\rho_{4}\right)f;

\displaystyle\bullet\ \Phi^{6}_{f}=-\dfrac{1}{3}\left(\dfrac{1}{90}\rho_{1}^{5}+\dfrac{1}{9}\rho_{1}^{3}\rho_{2}+\rho_{1}^{2}\rho_{3}+7\rho_{1}\rho_{4}+28\rho_{5}\right)f.

The above expressions for
\Phi^{2}_{f}
and
\Phi^{3}_{f}
were also pointed out and conveniently used in
[
17
]
in the framework of Poisson geometry.

It is natural to ask whether similar formulas hold for every
n
: more precisely, we ask for a sequence of noncommutative polynomials
P_{n}(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n})
with rational coefficients such that, for every
f
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{f}^{n+1}=P_{n}(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n})f.

(2.3)

A first way to construct, at least in principle, the polynomials
P_{n}
, follows immediately from the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

In the above notation, for every linear operator
f\colon A\to A
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Phi_{f}^{i}=\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}\rho_{n}\right)f,

where
K_{n}
is the sequence of rational numbers defined by the recursive equations

\displaystyle K_{1}=1,\qquad K_{n}=\frac{-2}{(n+2)(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{i}K_{i},

where

\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{i}=\frac{1}{i!}\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-j}\binom{i}{j}j^{n+1}

are the Stirling numbers of the second kind.

The first 12 terms of
K_{n}
are

\displaystyle K_{1}=1,\quad K_{2}=-\frac{1}{2},\quad K_{3}=\frac{1}{2},\quad K_{4}=-\frac{2}{3},\quad K_{5}=\frac{11}{12},\quad K_{6}=-\frac{3}{4},\quad K_{7}=-\frac{11}{6},

\displaystyle K_{8}=\frac{29}{4},\quad K_{9}=\frac{493}{12},\quad K_{10}=-\frac{2711}{6},\quad K_{11}=-\frac{12406}{15},\quad K_{12}=\frac{2636317}{60},

and we refer to Table
2
for the values of
n!K_{n}
for
n\leq 16
. As kindly reported to the authors by one referee, this sequence appeared in certain computations about Hurwitz numbers contained in the paper
[
28

by Shadrin and Zvonkine, and especially in Conjecture A.9, proved later by Aschenbrenner
[
3

; more details about that conjecture and its proof will be given in Remark
4.2
.

In practice it is more convenient to calculate the polynomials
P_{n}
by using the following easy consequence of Theorem
2.1
.

Corollary 2.2
.

In the above notation, for every linear operator
f\colon A\to A
we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{1}_{f}=f,\qquad\Phi^{n+1}_{f}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{h=1}^{n}(-1)^{h}\rho_{h}\big(\Phi^{n-h+1}_{f}\big)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{h=1}^{n}(-1)^{h}\big[\mu_{h},\Phi^{n-h+1}_{f}\big].

The advantage of Corollary
2.2
with respect to standard formulas (
2.1
) and
(
2.2
) is that it gives an easy way to define higher antibrackets
also for associative noncommutative superalgebras, providing that the symmetric operators
\mu_{n}\in D_{n}(A)
are now defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n+1}}\epsilon(\sigma)a_{\sigma(0)}a_{\sigma(1)}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)}.

In fact, also in this more general situation the brackets defined in Corollary
2.2

satisfy the generalized Jacobi identities (see Remark
5.6
),
while neither the symmetrization of (
2.1
) nor the symmetrization of (
2.2
) do. Extensions of higher antibrackets to noncommutative associative algebras were discussed in
[
2
,
6
]
, and a complete description has been given, with different approaches, in
[
4
,
5
,
13
,
24
]
.

The polynomials
P_{n}(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n})
are not uniquely determined by equation (
2.3
). In fact, according to formula (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle[\rho_{n},\rho_{m}]=(n-m)\frac{(n+m+1)!}{(n+1)!(m+1)!}\rho_{n+m},

(2.4)

and therefore, by Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem, we can choose every
P_{n}
as a linear combination with rational coefficients of monomials of type

\displaystyle\rho_{1}^{i_{1}}\rho_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\rho_{n}^{i_{n}},\qquad\text{with}\qquad\sum_{h=1}^{n}hi_{h}=n.

The following theorem, which is mathematically nontrivial, tells us in particular that, in the (super) commutative case, we can restrict to monomials such that
i_{2}+\cdots+i_{n}\leq 1
.

Theorem 2.3
.

In the notation above, for every integer
n>0
, there exists an unique sequence

c_{1}^{n},\ldots,c_{n}^{n}
of rational numbers
such that, for every linear operator
f\colon A\to A
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{n+1}_{f}=\big(c_{1}^{n}\rho_{1}^{n}+c^{n}_{2}\rho_{1}^{n-2}\rho_{2}+c^{n}_{3}\rho_{1}^{n-3}\rho_{3}+\cdots+c^{n}_{n}\rho_{n}\big)f.

3
The Nijenhuis–Richardson bracket

Unless otherwise specified every (super)vector space, symmetric product, linear map etc. are considered over the base field
\mathbb{Q}
of rational numbers, although everything works in the same way over any field of characteristic 0.
Given a super vector space
V=V^{0}\oplus V^{1}
, we denote by
V^{\odot n}
its symmetric powers and by

D_{n}(V)=\operatorname{Hom}^{*}(V^{\odot n+1},V)
the super vector space of multilinear supersymmetric maps on
n+1
variables

\displaystyle f\colon\ \underbrace{V\times\cdots\times V}_{n+1}\to V.

Recall that supersymmetry means that

\displaystyle f(v_{0},\ldots,v_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n})=(-1)^{|v_{i}||v_{i+1}|}f(v_{0},\ldots,v_{i+1},v_{i},\ldots,v_{n}),

where
|v|=0,1
denotes the parity of a homogeneous element
v
.

Given
f\in D_{n}(V)
and
g\in D_{m}(V)
,
n,m\geq 0
, their Nijenhuis–Richardson bracket
[
27
]
is defined as

\displaystyle[f,g]=f\barwedge g-(-1)^{|f||g|}g\barwedge f\in D_{n+m}(V),

where

\displaystyle f\barwedge g(v_{0},\ldots,v_{n+m})=\sum_{\sigma\in S(m+1,n)}\epsilon(\sigma)f\big(g(v_{\sigma(0)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(m)}),v_{\sigma(m+1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(m+n)}\big),

and
S(m+1,n)
is the set of shuffles of type
(m+1,n)
, i.e., the set of permutations
\sigma
of
0,\ldots,n+m

such that
\sigma(0)<\cdots<\sigma(m)
and
\sigma(m+1)<\cdots<\sigma(m+n)
. The Koszul sign
\epsilon(\sigma)
is equal to
(-1)^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is the number of pairs
(i,j)
such that
i<j
,
\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)
and
|v_{i}||v_{j}|=1
.

Observe that when
f\in D_{0}(V)
the product
f\barwedge g
is the same as the composition product
f\circ g
and therefore
the Nijenhuis–Richardson bracket reduces to the usual super commutator on
D_{0}(V)
. We denote by
D(V)=\prod\limits_{n\geq 0}D_{n}(V)
; the Nijenhuis–Richardson brackets induces on
D(V)
a structure of Lie superalgebra:

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}f_{i},\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}g_{j}\right]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n}[f_{i},g_{n-i}],\qquad f_{i},g_{i}\in D_{i}(V).

Remark 3.1
.

Denoting by
\overline{S^{c}}(V)=\oplus_{n\geq 1}V^{\odot n}
the reduced symmetric coalgebra generated by
V
,
the composition with the natural projection
p\colon\overline{S^{c}}(V)\to V
gives a morphism of super vector spaces

\displaystyle P\colon\ \operatorname{Hom}^{*}\big(\overline{S^{c}}(V),\overline{S^{c}}(V)\big)\to\operatorname{Hom}^{*}\big(\overline{S^{c}}(V),V\big)=D(V),

and it is well known
[
22
]
that
P
induces an isomorphism of Lie superalgebras

\displaystyle P\colon\ \operatorname{Coder}^{*}\big(\overline{S^{c}}(V)\big)\xrightarrow{\simeq}D(V),

where the bracket on
\operatorname{Coder}^{*}(\overline{S^{c}}(V))
is the super commutator.

Assume now that
A
is a commutative superalgebra, then there exists a distinguished sequence
\mu_{n}\in D_{n}(A)
,
n\geq 0
, corresponding to multiplication in
A
:

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})=a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{n}.

We have

\displaystyle\mu_{n}\barwedge\mu_{m}=\dbinom{n+m+1}{m+1}\mu_{n+m},\qquad[\mu_{n},\mu_{m}]=(n-m)\dfrac{(n+m+1)!}{(n+1)!(m+1)!}\mu_{n+m}.

(3.1)

In fact, the binomial coefficient in the first formula is equal to the cardinality of the set of shuffles involved in the formula for the product
\barwedge
.

4
Koszul numbers

Let’s recall the notion of iterative exponential and iterative logarithm
[
16
]
. Given a formal power series
a(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]]
with rational coefficients such that
a(0)=a^{\prime}(0)=0
, the following derivation is well defined

\displaystyle a(t)\dfrac{d~}{dt}\colon\ \mathbb{Q}[[t]]\to\mathbb{Q}[[t]],

as well as its exponential

\displaystyle\exp\left(a(t)\dfrac{d~}{dt}\right)\colon\ \mathbb{Q}[[t]]\to\mathbb{Q}[[t]],

which is an isomorphism of rings. The power series

\displaystyle\operatorname{itexp}(a(t))=\exp\left(a(t)\dfrac{d~}{dt}\right)(t)=t+a(t)+\frac{1}{2}a(t)a^{\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{6}(a(t)^{2}a^{\prime\prime}(t)+a(t)a^{\prime}(t)^{2})+\cdots

is called the
iterative exponential
of
a(t)
. Conversely, for every
g(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]]
such that
g(0)=0
and
g^{\prime}(0)=1
there exists a unique formal power series
a(t)=\operatorname{itlog}(g(t))
, called the
iterative logarithm
, such that

\displaystyle a(0)=a^{\prime}(0)=0,\qquad g(t)=\operatorname{itexp}(a(t)).

In this paper we are interested to a special sequence of rational numbers
K_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
which will appear in the natural description of the infinitesimal generator of the Koszul hierarchy: with a certain lack of imagination we shall refer to this sequence as “Koszul numbers”.

Definition 4.1
.

By
Koszul numbers
we mean the sequence of rational numbers
K_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, determined by the formula

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}\frac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}=\operatorname{itlog}\big(e^{t}-1\big),

or equivalently by the equation

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}\dfrac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\dfrac{d~}{dt}\right)(t)=e^{t}-1.

Remark 4.2
.

As already mentioned in the introduction, the first 12 Koszul numbers

\displaystyle K_{1}=1,\quad K_{2}=-\frac{1}{2},\quad K_{3}=\frac{1}{2},\quad K_{4}=-\frac{2}{3},\quad K_{5}=\frac{11}{12},\quad K_{6}=-\frac{3}{4},\quad K_{7}=-\frac{11}{6},

\displaystyle K_{8}=\frac{29}{4},\quad K_{9}=\frac{493}{12},\quad K_{10}=-\frac{2711}{6},\quad K_{11}=-\frac{12406}{15},\quad K_{12}=\frac{2636317}{60},\quad\ldots

appear in certain computations of Hurwitz numbers by Shadrin and Zvonkine
[
28
]
, in particular in Conjecture A.9 of
[
28
]
, cf. also
[
29
]
. This conjecture was subsequently proved by Aschenbrenner
[
3
]
, who also fixed a couple of misprints in the original formula of Shadrin and Zvonkine. Here we briefly recall the conjecture together with the translation of the Aschenbrenner’s proof in our setup, which avoids the use of infinite matrices and combinatorial properties of the Stirling numbers.

The starting point is the following sequence of formal power series
a_{d}(z)\in\mathbb{Q}[[z]]
:

\displaystyle a_{d}(z)=\sum_{b=0}^{d}\frac{(-1)^{d-b}}{d!}\binom{d}{b}\frac{1}{1-(b+1)z}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{b=0}^{d}\frac{(-1)^{d-b}}{d!}\binom{d}{b}(b+1)^{n}z^{n},\qquad d\geq 0.

In order to show that the multiplicity of
a_{d}(z)
is
d
(cf.
[
28
, Proposition 2.1]
), it is convenient to consider the isomorphism of
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces

\displaystyle\psi\colon\ \mathbb{Q}[[z]]\to t\mathbb{Q}[[t]],\qquad\psi\left(\sum c_{n}z^{n}\right)=\sum c_{n}\frac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!},

and prove that for every
d
we have

\displaystyle\psi(a_{d}(z))=\frac{(e^{t}-1)^{d+1}}{(d+1)!}.

(4.1)

In fact

\displaystyle\psi(a_{d}(z))=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{b=0}^{d}\frac{(-1)^{d-b}}{d!}\binom{d}{b}(b+1)^{n}\frac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}=\sum_{b=0}^{d}\frac{(-1)^{d-b}}{d!}\binom{d}{b}\frac{e^{(b+1)t}-1}{b+1},

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{d\psi(a_{d}(z))}{dt}=\sum_{b=0}^{d}\frac{(-1)^{d-b}}{d!}\binom{d}{b}e^{(b+1)t}=\frac{e^{t}(e^{t}-1)^{d}}{d!}=\frac{d~}{dt}\frac{(e^{t}-1)^{d+1}}{(d+1)!}.

Thus we can define the rational numbers
a_{d,d+k}
, for
d,k\geq 0
, by setting

\displaystyle a_{d}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{d,d+k}z^{d+k}=z^{d}+\sum_{k>0}a_{d,d+k}z^{d+k}.

Consider now the ring
R=\mathbb{Q}[[t_{0},t_{1},\ldots]]
of formal power series in the pairwise distinct indeterminates
t_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. That ring
has the complete decreasing filtration

\displaystyle F^{p}R=\left\{\sum_{p\leq i_{0}+2i_{1}+3i_{2}+\cdots<+\infty}a_{i_{0},i_{1},i_{2},\ldots}t_{0}^{i_{0}}t_{1}^{i_{1}}t_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\right\},

and therefore there exists a (unique) morphism of
\mathbb{Q}
-algebras

such that

\displaystyle L(t_{d})=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{d,d+k}t_{d+k}=t_{d}+\sum_{k>0}a_{d,d+k}t_{d+k}

for every
d\geq 0
. Then the Conjecture A.9 of
[
28

is expressed by the equality:

\displaystyle L=\exp\left(\sum_{n>0}K_{n}\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{k+n+1}{n+1}t_{n+k}\frac{\partial~}{\partial t_{k}}\right).

(4.2)

In order to prove (
4.2
) notice that the vector subspace
T\subset R
of power series of type
\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t_{n}
is isomorphic to the maximal ideal of
\mathbb{Q}[[t]]
via the continuous linear isomorphism of complete
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces

\displaystyle\phi\colon\ T\to t\mathbb{Q}[[t]],\qquad\phi(t_{n})=\psi(z^{n})=\frac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.

For every
n\geq 0
the operator
\phi^{-1}\circ\frac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\frac{d~}{dt}\circ\phi\colon T\to T

is the restriction to
T
of the derivation

\displaystyle r_{n}\colon\ R\to R,\qquad r_{n}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{k+n+1}{n+1}t_{k+n}\frac{\partial~}{\partial t_{k}}.

Therefore, by (
4.1
) and the definition of Koszul numbers, for every
d\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phi\left(\exp\left(\sum_{n>0}K_{n}\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{k+n+1}{n+1}t_{n+k}\frac{\partial~}{\partial t_{k}}\right)t_{d}\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}\dfrac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\dfrac{d~}{dt}\right)\frac{t^{d+1}}{(d+1)!}\\
&\hphantom{\phi\left(\exp\left(\sum_{n>0}K_{n}\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{k+n+1}{n+1}t_{n+k}\frac{\partial~}{\partial t_{k}}\right)t_{d}\right)}{}=\frac{(e^{t}-1)^{d+1}}{(d+1)!}=\psi(a_{d}(z))=\phi(L(t_{d})),\end{split}

and this implies formula (

\Phi^{n}_{\Delta}
are the Taylor coefficients of an
L_{\infty}
structure.

Corollary 5.5
.

In the above notation, for every linear operator
f\colon A\to A
we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{1}_{f}=f,\qquad\Phi^{n+1}_{f}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{h=1}^{n}(-1)^{h}\big[\mu_{h},\Phi^{n-h+1}_{f}\big].

Proof.

The higher antibrackets of the identity
\operatorname{Id}\colon A\to A

are
\Phi^{n+1}_{\operatorname{Id}}=(-1)^{n}\mu_{n}
and then

\displaystyle 0=\Phi^{n+1}_{[\operatorname{Id},f]}=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\big[\Phi^{i}_{\operatorname{Id}},\Phi^{n-i+2}_{f}\big]=\big[\mu_{0},\Phi^{n+1}_{f}\big]+\sum_{h=1}^{n}(-1)^{h}\big[\mu_{h},\Phi^{n-h+1}_{f}\big].

The proof now follows from the trivial equality

[\mu_{0},\Phi^{n+1}_{f}]=-n\Phi^{n+1}_{f}
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

There exists a natural way to extend the definition of the operators
\mu_{n}\in D(A)
to every noncommutative associative superalgebra
A
by setting

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n+1}}\epsilon(\sigma)a_{\sigma(0)}a_{\sigma(1)}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)},

and it is easy to see that the equality

\displaystyle[\mu_{n},\mu_{m}]=(n-m)\dfrac{(n+m+1)!}{(n+1)!(m+1)!}\mu_{n+m}

still holds. The adjoint operators
\rho_{n}=[\mu_{n},-]\colon D(A)\to D(A)
are derivations and the map

\displaystyle\exp\bigg({-}\sum_{n\geq 1}K_{n}\rho_{n}\bigg)\colon\ D(A)\to D(A)

is an isomorphism of Lie superalgebras. In particular, defining the higher antibrackets by the formula

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Phi_{f}^{i}=\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}\rho_{n}\right)f,

the same argument as above shows the validity of the equalities

\displaystyle\Phi_{\operatorname{Id}}^{n+1}=(-1)^{n}\mu_{n},\qquad\Phi_{[f,g]}^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\Phi^{i}_{f},\Phi^{n-i+1}_{g}\big],

and the same proof of Corollary
5.5
works in this case as well. Further properties of this noncommutative extension of Koszul brackets are studied in
[
24
]
, where it is proved in particular that they coincide with the brackets defined by Bering and Bandiera, while they are different from the symmetrization of Börjeson’s brackets, cf.
[
26
]
.

Remark 5.7
.

For reference purpose, we point out that the sequence
K_{n}
is uniquely determined by the formula

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Phi_{\operatorname{Id}}^{i}=\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}\rho_{n}\right)\mu_{0}

in the case
A=\mathbb{Q}
. In fact
\Phi^{n+1}_{\operatorname{Id}}=(-1)^{n}\mu_{n}
,
\rho_{n}(\mu_{0})=n\mu_{n}
, and
therefore
((-1)^{n}+nK_{n})\mu_{n}
is the component in
D_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
of
\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}K_{i}\rho_{i}\right)\mu_{0}
.

6
Natural higher brackets

In order to prove the Theorem
2.3
it is useful, and we believe interesting in itself, to consider the Koszul higher brackets
as special natural derived brackets: the notion of naturality has been discussed extensively in the paper
[
26
]
; for our application we simply define a natural derived bracket of a linear endomorphism
f\colon A\to A
as an element of type

\displaystyle\sum_{i+j=n}\psi_{ij}\mu_{i}\barwedge(f\barwedge\mu_{j})\in D_{n}(A),\qquad\psi_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}.

We denote by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}=\big\{\Psi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{Q}[x],\mathbb{Q}[x])\,|\,\Psi(1)=0\big\}=\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}(x)\partial^{n+1}\,|\,p_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]\right\}

the associative algebra of linear endomorphisms of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space

\mathbb{Q}[x]
vanishing in
1
, equipped with the composition product.
Every element
\Psi\in\mathfrak{a}
is uniquely determined by its Taylor coefficients,
i.e., by the infinite double sequence
\psi_{ij}
,
i,j\geq 0
, defined by the formulas:

\displaystyle\Psi(x^{i+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{ij}\frac{x^{j}}{j!},\qquad i\geq 0,\qquad\psi_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}.

(6.1)

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\Psi\in\mathfrak{a}
be a fixed element.
For a commutative superalgebra
A
over
\mathbb{Q}
and a linear endomorphism
f\colon A\to A
,
the
\Psi
-bracket of
f
is

\displaystyle\Psi_{f}\in D(A),\qquad\Psi_{f}=\sum_{i,j\geq 0}\psi_{ij}\mu_{j}\barwedge(f\barwedge\mu_{i}),

where the
\psi_{ij}
’s are the Taylor coefficients of
\Psi
as in (
6.1
).

For every
n\geq i>0
, denote by
\Phi^{n,i}\in\mathfrak{a}
the operator

\displaystyle\Phi^{n,i}(x^{i})=\frac{x^{n-i}}{(n-i)!},\qquad\Phi^{n,i}(x^{s})=0\qquad\text{for}\quad s\not=i.

Then for every
f\colon A\to A
we have
\Phi^{n,i}_{f}=\mu_{n-i}\barwedge(f\barwedge\mu_{i-1})
:

\displaystyle\Phi^{n,i}_{f}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\sum_{\sigma\in S(i,n-i)}\epsilon(\sigma)f(a_{\sigma(1)}\cdots a_{\sigma(i)})a_{\sigma(i+1)}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)}.

In particular, setting

\displaystyle\Phi^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\Phi^{n,i}

we recover the definition of higher Koszul brackets
\Phi^{n}_{f}
.
The formulas (
2.4
), (
3.1
) and (
4.4
) suggest the introduction of the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}
over the field
\mathbb{Q}
of rational numbers with basis
l_{0},l_{1},l_{2},\ldots
and bracket

\displaystyle[l_{n},l_{m}]=(n-m)\dfrac{(n+m+1)!}{(n+1)!(m+1)!}l_{n+m}.

Notice that, setting
L_{n}=(n+1)!l_{n}
we obtain the more familiar expression

\displaystyle[L_{n},L_{m}]=(n-m)L_{n+m}.

In particular, for every commutative superalgebra
A
over
\mathbb{Q}
, the linear map

\displaystyle\mu_{A}\colon\ \mathfrak{g}\to D(A),\qquad\mu_{A}(l_{n})=\mu_{n}

is a morphism of Lie superalgebras: we denote by

\displaystyle\rho_{A}\colon\ \mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{Hom}(D(A),D(A)),\qquad\rho_{A}(l_{n})(\phi)=[\mu_{n},\phi]

the corresponding adjoint representation.

Lemma 6.2
.

The linear map
\rho\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a})
:

\displaystyle\rho(l_{n})(\Psi)=\left(\dfrac{x^{n}}{n!}-\dfrac{x^{n+1}\partial}{(n+1)!}\right)\circ\Psi-\Psi\circ\dfrac{x\partial^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}

is an injective morphism of Lie algebras.

Proof.

We first prove that

\displaystyle\phi\colon\ \mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Q}[x],\mathbb{Q}[x]),\qquad\phi(l_{n})=\dfrac{x\partial^{n+1}}{(n+1)!},

\displaystyle\psi\colon\ \mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Q}[x],\mathbb{Q}[x]),\qquad\psi(l_{n})=\dfrac{x^{n}}{n!}-\dfrac{x^{n+1}\partial}{(n+1)!}

are morphisms of Lie algebras; it is easier to make the computation in the basis
L_{n}=(n+1)!l_{n}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\phi(L_{n}),\phi(L_{m})]x^{s}&=\big[x\partial^{n+1},x\partial^{m+1}\big]x^{s}\\
&=x\partial^{n+1}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m}(s-i)\right)x^{s-m}-x\partial^{m+1}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n}(s-i)\right)x^{s-n}\\
&=(s-m)\prod_{i=0}^{m+n}(s-i)x^{s-m-n}-(s-n)\prod_{i=0}^{m+n}(s-i)x^{s-m-n}\\
&=(n-m)x\partial^{n+m+1}x^{s}=(n-m)\phi(L_{n+m})x^{s}.\end{split}

Since
\psi(L_{n})x^{s}=(n+1-s)x^{n+s}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\psi(L_{n}),\psi(L_{m})]x^{s}&=\psi(L_{n})(m+1-s)x^{m+s}-\psi(L_{m})(n+1-s)x^{n+s}\\
&=((n+1-s-m)(m+1-s)-(m+1-n-s)(n+1-s))x^{n+m+s}\\
&=(n-m)(n+m+1-s)x^{n+m+s}=(n-m)\psi(L_{n+m})x^{s}.\end{split}

Finally

\displaystyle[\rho(x),\rho(y)]\Psi

\displaystyle=\rho(x)\rho(y)\Psi-\rho(x)\rho(y)\Psi

\displaystyle=\psi(x)\rho(y)\Psi-\rho(y)\Psi\phi(x)-\psi(y)\rho(x)\Psi+\rho(x)\Psi\phi(y)

\displaystyle=\psi(x)\psi(y)\Psi+\Psi\phi(y)\phi(x)-\psi(y)\psi(x)\Psi-\Psi\phi(x)\phi(y)

\displaystyle=[\psi(x),\psi(y)]\Psi-\Psi[\phi(x),\phi(y)]

\displaystyle=\psi([x,y])\Psi-\Psi\phi([x,y])=\rho([x,y])\Psi.

The injectivity is clear.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

For every commutative superalgebra
A
and every
f\in D_{0}(A)
the map

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}\to D(A),\qquad\Psi\mapsto\Psi_{f}

is a morphism of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules. In other terms, for every
\Psi\in\mathfrak{a}
and every
l\in\mathfrak{g}
we have

\displaystyle(\rho(l)\Psi)_{f}=\rho_{A}(l)(\Psi_{f})=[\mu_{A}(l),\Psi_{f}].

(6.2)

Proof.

By linearity is sufficient to check (
6.2
) when
l=l_{k}
and
\Psi=\Phi^{n,i}
. The proof follows from the following straightforward identities:

\displaystyle\rho(l_{k})\Phi^{n,i}=\left(\binom{n-i+k}{k}-\binom{n-i+k}{k+1}\right)\Phi^{n+k,i}-\binom{k+i}{k+1}\Phi^{n+k,i+k},

\displaystyle\big[\mu_{k},\Phi^{n,i}_{f}\big]=\left(\binom{n-i+k}{k}-\binom{n-i+k}{k+1}\right)\Phi^{n+k,i}_{f}-\binom{k+i}{k+1}\Phi^{n+k,i+k}_{f}.

∎

Theorem 6.4
.

In the notation above, for every integer
k>0
let’s denote by
\rho_{k}=\rho(l_{k})\colon\mathfrak{a}\to\mathfrak{a}
.
For every integer
n>0
, there exists an unique sequence
c^{n}_{1},\ldots,c^{n}_{n}
of rational numbers such that

\displaystyle\Phi^{n+1}=\big(c^{n}_{1}\rho_{1}^{n}+c^{n}_{2}\rho_{1}^{n-2}\rho_{2}+c^{n}_{3}\rho_{1}^{n-3}\rho_{3}+\cdots+c^{n}_{n}\rho_{n}\big)\Phi^{1}.

Proof.

Denote by
V^{n}\subset\mathfrak{a}
the subspace generated by
\Phi^{n,1},\ldots,\Phi^{n,n}
; notice that
\dim V^{n}=n
and

\rho_{k}(V^{n})\subset V^{n+k}
. We claim that, for every
n\geq 2
the
n
vectors

\displaystyle\rho_{1}^{n-2}\big(\Phi^{2,2}\big),\qquad\rho_{1}^{a}\rho_{n-a}\big(\Phi^{1,1}\big),\qquad 0\leq a\leq n-2,

form a basis of
V^{n}
. This is clear for
n=2
since
\rho_{1}(\Phi^{1,1})=\Phi^{2,1}-\Phi^{2,2}
.
By induction on
n
, it is sufficient to prove that for every
n\geq 2
the linear map

\displaystyle V^{n}\oplus V^{1}\to V^{n+1},\qquad(x,y)\mapsto\rho_{1}(x)+\rho_{n}(y)

is an isomorphism. Consider first the case
n=2
: in terms of matrix multiplication we have

\displaystyle\big(\rho_{1}\big(\Phi^{2,2}\big),\rho_{1}\big(\Phi^{2,1}\big),\rho_{2}\big(\Phi^{1,1}\big)\big)=\big(\Phi^{3,3},\Phi^{3,2},\Phi^{3,1}\big)\begin{pmatrix}-3&0&-1\\
1&-1&0\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix}

and the determinant of the matrix is
\not=0
. Assume now
n\geq 3
: since

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\big(\Phi^{1,1}\big)=\Phi^{n+1,1}-\Phi^{n+1,n+1},\qquad\rho_{1}\big(\Phi^{n,n-2}\big)=-\binom{n-1}{2}\Phi^{n+1,n-1},

we can write

\displaystyle\big(\rho_{1}\big(\Phi^{n,n}\big),\ldots,\rho_{1}\big(\Phi^{n,1}\big),\rho_{n}\big(\Phi^{1,1}\big)\big)=\big(\Phi^{n+1,n+1},\ldots,\Phi^{n+1,1}\big)\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
0&C\end{pmatrix},

where
A\in M_{3,3}(\mathbb{Z})
and
C\in M_{n-2,n-2}(\mathbb{Z})
are lower triangular matrices with nonzero entries in the diagonal, and therefore the determinant of the block matrix is
\not=0
. Thus, for every
n
there exists an unique sequence of
n+1
rational numbers
c^{n}_{1},\ldots,c^{n}_{n},b_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\Phi^{n+1}=\big(c^{n}_{1}\rho_{1}^{n}+c^{n}_{2}\rho_{1}^{n-2}\rho_{2}+\cdots+c^{n}_{n}\rho_{n}\big)\Phi^{1}+b_{n}\rho_{1}^{n-1}\big(\Phi^{2,2}\big)

and we have to show that
b_{n}=0
. To this end it is sufficient to consider the representation

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}\to D(\mathbb{Q}[x]),\quad\Psi\mapsto\Psi_{\partial},

where, as usual
\partial=\frac{d~}{dt}\in D_{0}(\mathbb{Q}[x])
. Since
\partial
is a derivation we have

\Phi^{n+1}_{\partial}=[\mu_{n},\partial]=0
for every
n\geq 0
, and therefore it is sufficient to prove that
\rho_{1}^{n}(\Phi^{2,2}_{\partial})\not=0
for every
n
.
By construction
\Phi^{2,2}_{\partial}(t^{a},t^{b})=(a+b)t^{a+b-1}
and an easy induction on
n
shows that for every
n\geq 3
we have

\displaystyle\rho_{1}^{n-2}\big(\Phi^{2,2}_{\partial}\big)\big(t^{a_{1}},t^{a_{2}},\ldots,t^{a_{n}}\big)=\left[\prod_{i=3}^{n}\binom{i-1}{2}\right](a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n})t^{\sum a_{i}-1}.

∎
