Effective equidistribution of expanding translates in the space of affine lattices
Wooyeon Kim

Abstract
. We prove a polynomially effective equidistribution result for expanding translates in the space of d d -dimensional affine lattices for any d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Backgrounds

In the theory of unipotent dynamics on homogeneous spaces, a fundamental result is Ratner’s theorems
[
Rat91a
,
Rat91b
]
on measure rigidity, topological rigidity, and orbit equidistribution. They have numerous and diverse applications ranging from number theory to mathematical physics, see e.g.
[
EMS98
,
EM04
,
EO06
,
Sha09
,
Sha10
,
Mrk10
,
MS10
]
. We refer the reader to
[
Mor05
]
for expositions and references.

A limitation of Ratner’s results is that they do not tell us an explicit rate of density or equidistribution for unipotent orbits. As pointed out in
[
Mrg00
, Problem 7]
, establishing
effective
versions of Ratner’s results has been a central topic in homogeneous dynamics.

We say that a subgroup
H\subset G
is (unstable) horospherical if there exists an element
a\in G
such that

(1.1)

{H=\left\{g\in G:a^{k}ga^{-k}\rightarrow e\quad\textrm{as}\quad k\to-\infty\right\}.}

For horospherical subgroups, effective equidistribution results have been established with a
polynomially
strong error term
[
KM96
,
KM12
,
Ven10
,
DKL16
,
KSW17
,
Shi19
]
using the mixing property of
a
on
G/\Gamma
and the so-called “thickening” method, originating in Margulis’ thesis
[
Mrg04
]
. We also refer the reader to the effective equidistribution results for horocycle orbits in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma

[
Sar81
,
Bur90
,
FF03
,
Str04
,
Str13
]
via direct representation-theoretic approaches.

Here we state an effective equidistribution result of the horospherical orbits in a concrete setting as Theorem
1.1
below; it is closely related to the main results of the present paper. Let
G=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
X=G/\Gamma
for fixed
d\geq 2
. Let
\{a_{t}\}<G
be a 1-parameter diagonal subgroup
\operatorname{diag}\left(e^{nt}\operatorname{Id}_{m},e^{-mt}\operatorname{Id}_{n}\right)
, where
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
and
\operatorname{Id}_{n}
are
m\times m
and
n\times n
identity matrices, respectively, and
m+n=d
. Then the horospherical subgroup for
a:=a_{1}
in
G
is indeed expressed as

(1.2)

{H=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}\operatorname{Id}_{m}&A\\
0&\operatorname{Id}_{n}\end{matrix}\right)\in G:A\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})\right\}.}

We denote by
m_{H}
the Haar measure of
H
and
m_{X}
the
G
-invariant probability measure on
X
. In
[
KM96
]
, an effective equidistribution theorem of expanding horospherical translates in
X
was established as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
KM96
, Proposition 2.4.8]

Let
V\subset H
be a fixed neighborhood of the identity in
H
with smooth boundary and compact closure.
Then there exists a constant
\delta_{0}>0
only depending on
m
and
n
so that the following holds. For any compact set
K\subset X
, there exists a constant
T(K)\geq 0
such that

(1.3)

\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f(a_{t}ux)dm_{H}(u)=\int_{X}fdm_{X}+O(\mathcal{S}(f)e^{-\delta_{0}t})

for any
t\geq T(K)
,
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
, and
x\in K
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
is a suitable Sobolev norm of
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
and the implied constant depends only on
m
,
n
, and
V
.

For non-horospherical subgroups, several effective equidistribution results have been proved in the last decade. When the ambient group
G
is semisimple, an effective equidistribution theorem was established for closed orbits of a semisimple group in general homogeneous spaces
[
EMV09
]
(see also
[
AELM20
]
), and was recently extended to the adelic setting
[
EMMV20
]
. Also, effective results for non-horospherical unipotent subgroups are known in some concrete settings
[
Mo12
,
LM14
,
SU15
,
Ubi16
,
CY19
]
. When
G
is unipotent,
[
GT12
]
settled effective equidistribution of polynomial orbits in nilmanifolds.

In general, let
\hat{G}=G\ltimes W
be the ambient group where
G
is a semisimple group and
W
is the unipotent radical of
\hat{G}
. If
\hat{G}
is neither semisimple nor unipotent, the methods of the aforementioned results do not seem directly applicable to general homogeneous spaces. However, in special settings effective equidistribution results have been obtained e.g.
[
Str15
,
BV16
]
for
\hat{G}=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
[
SV20
]
for
\hat{G}=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\ltimes(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\oplus k}
, and
[
Pri18
]
for
\hat{G}=\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The purpose of the present paper is to establish an effective equidistribution result for
\hat{G}=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

1.2.
Main results

For fixed
d\geq 2
, denote

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\hat{G}=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z}),\quad\hat{\Gamma}=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{d}=Stab_{\hat{G}}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

We define
X=G/\Gamma
and
Y=\hat{G}/\hat{\Gamma}
. It is sometimes convenient to view
\hat{G}
as a subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})
by
\hat{G}=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}g&b\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right):g\in\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R}),b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}
. Then there exists a natural projection
\pi:Y\rightarrow X
sending
\left(\begin{matrix}g&b\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\hat{\Gamma}\in Y
to
\left(\begin{matrix}g&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\Gamma\in X
. We denote by
W\simeq\mathbb{R}^{d}
the unipotent radical of
\hat{G}
, which consists of all translations on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
W=\left\{w(b):=\left(\begin{matrix}\operatorname{Id}_{d}&b\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right):b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}
acts simply transitively on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can identify
\hat{G}/W\simeq G
. We take a lift of the element
g\in G
to
\hat{G}\subset\operatorname{SL}_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})
given by
\left(\begin{matrix}g&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)
and by abuse of notation we denote it again by
g
. Following this notation, we have a relation
gw(b)=w(gb)g
for
g\in G
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
g\Gamma\in X
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
gw(b)\hat{\Gamma}
is invariant under the integer translations of
b
. Thus, the fiber
\pi^{-1}(g\Gamma)
can be seen as
\left\{gw(b)\hat{\Gamma}\in Y:b\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\right\}
.

Let
\{a_{t}\}
be a
1
-parameter diagonal subgroup of
G
defined by

a_{t}=\operatorname{diag}\left(e^{nt}\operatorname{Id}_{m},e^{-mt}\operatorname{Id}_{n}\right),

H
be the unstable horospherical subgroup in
G
for
a=a_{1}
, and
m_{H}
be the Haar measure of
H
as before. We remark that
H
is a full horospherical subgroup in
G
,
but not in

\hat{G}
, which renders the methods in the proof of Theorem
1.1
irrelevant. We denote by
m_{X}
the
G
-invariant probability measure on
X
and
m_{Y}
the
\hat{G}
-invariant probability measure on
Y
.

Let
V\subset H
be a fixed neighborhood of the identity in
H
with smooth boundary and compact closure. For
y\in Y
and
t\geq 0
,
a_{t}Vy\subset Y
is a lift of a piece of a full horospherical orbit
a_{t}Vx\subset X
, where
x=\pi(y)\in X
. In this paper, we study the equidistribution of the expanding translates
\left\{a_{t}Vy\right\}_{t\geq 0}
in
Y
as
t\to\infty
.

For any positive integer
q
, set

{X_{q}:=\left\{gw(b)\hat{\Gamma}\in Y:g\in G,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{d},\mathbf{q}(b)=q\right\},}

where for any
b\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
,
\mathbf{q}(b)
denote its common denominator, i.e. the smallest
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
b\in q^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Clearly,
X_{q}
is a closed
G
-invariant subset of
Y
for any
q
. We denote by
m_{X_{q}}
the
G
-invariant probability measure on
X_{q}
.

We start by discussing the
ineffective
equidistribution of
\left\{a_{t}Vy\right\}_{t\geq 0}
in
Y
. For
y\in Y
and
t\geq 0
, we denote by
\mu_{y,t}\in\mathscr{P}(Y)
the normalized probability measure on the orbit
a_{t}Vy
, i.e.
\mu_{y,t}(f):=\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f(a_{t}uy)dm_{H}(u)
for any
f\in C_{b}(Y)
. The following equidistribution result is a special case of
[
Sha96
, Thm. 1.4]
:

(1.4)

{\mu_{y,t}\overset{\operatorname{w}^{*}}{\longrightarrow}m_{Y}\quad\mbox{for all }y\notin\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb{N}}X_{q},}

(1.5)

{\mu_{y,t}\overset{\operatorname{w}^{*}}{\longrightarrow}m_{X_{q}}\quad\mbox{for all }y\in X_{q},}

as
t\to\infty
. We refer to
[
MS10
, proof of Thm. 5.2]
and
[
Str15
, §1.1]
for the proof and more detailed discussions. Here we just make a remark that the proof of
[
Sha96
, Thm. 1.4]
crucially relies on Ratner’s measure classification theorem.

The main result of the present paper is Theorem
1.3
below, which is an
effective
refinement of (
1.4
). Observe that the weak
∗
limit of
\left\{\mu_{y,t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is determined by the Diophantine properties of the initial point
y
. Also, the condition
y\notin\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb{N}}X_{q}
in (
1.4
) is equivalent to
b\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, where
y=gw(b)\hat{\Gamma}
. Based on these observations, we can expect that the rate of convergence in (
1.4
) might also depend on the Diophantine properties of
y
, or equivalently that of
b
.

We say that a vector
b\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
is
of (Diophantine) type

M\geq 1
if there exists
c>0
such that
|b-\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}|>cq^{-M}
for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We first state a simplified version of the main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
V\subset H
be a fixed neighborhood of the identity in
H
with smooth boundary and compact closure.
Then there exists a constant
\delta>0
only depending on
d
such that

(1.6)

\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f(a_{t}uy)dm_{H}(u)=\int_{Y}fdm_{Y}+O\left(\mathcal{S}(f)e^{-\frac{\delta t}{M+1}}\right)

for any
t\geq 0
,
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
, and
y=gw(b)\hat{\Gamma}
, where
b\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
is of Diophantine type
M
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
is a suitable Sobolev norm of
C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
and the implied constant depends only on
d
,
V
, and
y
.

More generally, in order to express the Diophantine properties of
b
in a quantitative sense, let us define a function
\zeta:(\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{Q}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{N}
by

\zeta(b,T):=\min\left\{N\in\mathbb{N}:\displaystyle\min_{1\leq q\leq N}\|qb\|_{\mathbb{Z}}\leq\frac{N^{2}}{T}\right\},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{Z}}
denotes the supremum distance from
0\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
We note that
\zeta(b,\cdot)
is non-decreasing, unbounded, and has the following properties:

(1.7)

{\zeta(b,cT)\leq\lceil c^{\frac{1}{2}}\zeta(b,T)\rceil,}

(1.8)

{\zeta(b,\|\gamma^{-1}\|_{\textrm{op}}^{-1}T)\leq\zeta(\gamma b,T)\leq\zeta(b,\|\gamma\|_{\textrm{op}}T),}

(1.9)

{\zeta(b,T)\leq\lceil T^{\frac{d}{2d+1}}\rceil.}

for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
c>1
. Here
\|\cdot\|_{op}
denotes the operator norm of
G
w.r.t. the supremum norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The inequality (
1.9
) is equivalent to Dirichlet’s approximation theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
V
and
\mathcal{S}
be as in Theorem
1.2
. Then there exists a constant
\delta^{\prime}>0
only depending on
d
such that

(1.10)

\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f(a_{t}uy)dm_{H}(u)=\int_{Y}fdm_{Y}+O\left(\mathcal{S}(f)\zeta(b,e^{\frac{nt}{2}})^{-\delta^{\prime}}\right)

for any
t\geq 0
,
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
, and
y=gw(b)\hat{\Gamma}
with
\|g\|\leq\zeta(b,e^{\frac{nt}{2}})^{\delta^{\prime}}
and
b\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Here, the implied constant depends only on
d
and
V
.

By definition of
\zeta
, we have

(1.11)

{\zeta(b,T)\gg T^{\frac{1}{M+1}}}

for any
T>0
and
b
of Diophantine type
M
. Hence, Theorem
1.3
directly implies Theorem
1.2
with
\delta=\frac{n\delta^{\prime}}{2}
. Moreover, Theorem
1.3
implies (
1.4
) directly as
\zeta(b,T)\to\infty
as
T\to\infty
for any irrational
b\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

We give more general versions of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
for other diagonal subgroups in Section
5
.

Strömbergsson
[
Str15
]
proved Theorem
1.3
for
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2}/\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{2}
using Fourier analysis and methods of from analytic number theory, and later Prinyasart
[
Pri18
]
obtained an effective equidistribution result in
\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{3}/\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{3}
building on similar methods. In particular, their proofs rely on delicate analysis on the explicit expressions of unitary representations of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
or
\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
and cancellations from Kloosterman sums. In order to prove Theorem
1.3
for arbitrary dimension
d\geq 2
, we will use a different approach from the previous works. The method in the present paper is
geometric
in the sense that we do not rely on representation-theoretic properties of the semisimple component
G=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. It proceeds via a geometric decomposition of
X=G/\Gamma
and analysis of the Fourier coefficients of probability measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. We will use the effective equidistribution result in
X
(Theorem
1.1
above), and a strategy that is similar in spirit to the idea given in
[
BFLM11
]
.

1.3.
Sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.3

We fix an initial point
y_{0}=g_{0}w(b_{0})\hat{\Gamma}
and let
\mu_{t}\in\mathscr{P}(Y)
be the normalized probability measure on the orbit
a_{t}Vy_{0}
, for any
t\geq 0
. Then Theorem
1.3
is equivalent to obtaining an upper bound of
|\mu_{t}(f)-m_{Y}(f)|
for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
.

For any
x\in X
,
\pi^{-1}(x)\subset Y
can be identified with
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, so
Y
is a torus bundle of
X
. Moreover, if we fix a fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}\subset G
of
X
, any
y\in Y
can be uniquely parametrized as
y=gw(b)\widehat{\Gamma}
, where
g\in\mathcal{F}
and
b\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Hence, we have a bijective measurable map
y\mapsto(g\Gamma,b)
between
Y
and
X\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
. The projection to
X
of this map is the canonical projection
\pi:Y\to X
. We denote by
\sigma:Y\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
the projection to
\mathbb{T}^{d}
of this map, which depends on the choice of
\mathcal{F}
.

We first decompose the orbit
a_{t}Vy_{0}\subset Y
to small pieces so that each piece of orbit is approximately on the same fiber torus. To simplify notations assume that
b_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
is of Diophantine type
M
. Let
\left\{\omega_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
be a partition of unity of
X
such that each
\omega_{i}
is supported on a ball
B^{X}(z_{i},r)
, where
z_{i}\in X
,
r\asymp e^{-\frac{\kappa t}{M+1}}
, and
\kappa>0
will be an appropriately chosen small constant. Then the measure
\mu_{t}
is decomposed by
\sum_{i}\pi_{*}\mu_{t}(\omega_{i})\mu_{t,\omega_{i}}
, where
\mu_{t,\omega_{i}}(f)=\pi_{*}\mu_{t}(\omega_{i})^{-1}\int f\omega_{i}\circ\pi d\mu_{t}
. Considering
\mu_{t,\omega_{i}}
as a measure on
B^{X}(z_{i},r)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
, we may also look at
\nu_{t,\omega_{i}}:=\sigma_{*}\mu_{t,\omega_{i}}
which is a probability measure on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
(approximating
\mu_{t,\omega_{i}}
up to error
\ll r
).

Hence to prove the effective equidistribution in
Y
, it is enough to show that for each
\omega
, the measure
\nu_{t,\omega}
effectively equidistributes toward the Lebesgue measure on the torus. In other words, it suffices to prove that any nontrivial Fourier coefficient
\widehat{\nu_{t,\omega}}(\mathbf{m}_{0})
is small for
\mathbf{m}_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
. Most of this paper will be devoted to proving the decay of nontrivial Fourier coefficients, Proposition
4.10
.

Our basic approach to prove Proposition
4.10
is viewing the horospherical action in
Y
as the combination of the horospherical action in
X
and a discrete
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
-action on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. For example, for
s\geq 0
,
u\in V
, and
x=g\Gamma\in X
such that
g\in\mathcal{F}
, there uniquely exist
\xi_{s}(x,u)\in\mathcal{F}
and
\gamma_{s}(x,u)\in\Gamma
such that
a_{s}ug=\xi_{s}(x,u)\gamma_{s}(x,u)
. Then for
y=gw(b)\hat{\Gamma}
, a point on the horospherical orbit
a_{s}Vy
is described by
a_{s}uy=\xi_{s}(x,u)w(\gamma_{s}(x,u)b)\hat{\Gamma}
. Observe that the dynamics on the torus part is given by
\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
-action.

We already have an effective equidistribution result in
X
thanks to
[
KM96
]
: expanding horospherical translates in
X
are polynomially effectively equidistributed by Theorem
1.1
. Our main argument to deal with the dynamics on the torus is inspired by
[
BFLM11
]
, which proved a quantitative equidistribution result for
\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
-random walk on the torus. Following the strategy of
[
BFLM11
]
(see also
[
HdS19
,
HLL20
]
), we will prove:

(1)

(Proposition
3.5
) If
|\widehat{\nu_{t,\omega}}(\mathbf{m}_{0})|
is large for
\mathbf{m}_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, then for appropriately chosen
s<t
, we can find a rich family of probability measures on the torus
\left\{\nu_{t-s,j}\right\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}
such that
\mathrm{supp\,}\nu_{t-s,j}\subseteq\mathrm{supp\,}\nu_{t-s}
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
,
\mathrm{supp\,}\nu_{t-s,j}
’s are disjoint, and each
\nu_{t-s,j}
has a rich set of Fourier coefficients which are larger than a polynomial in
|\widehat{\nu_{t,\omega}}(\mathbf{m}_{0})|
.

(2)

(Proposition
4.9
) If the torus-coordinate
b_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
of the initial point
y_{0}\in Y
is not well-approximated by rationals of low denominator, then
\nu_{t-2s}
cannot be highly concentrated on a
\rho
-radius ball, i.e.
\nu_{t-2s}(B^{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(p,\rho))\ll\rho^{2\kappa^{\prime}}
for any
p\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, where
\rho\asymp e^{-\frac{\kappa^{\prime\prime}(t-2s)}{M+1}}
for an appropriately chosen constant
\kappa^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime\prime}>0
.

Then Proposition
4.10
is obtained from (1) and (2) as follows. Making use of
[
BFLM11
, Proposition 7.5.]
which is a quantitative analog of Wiener’s lemma, we deduce from Proposition
3.5
that if
|\widehat{\nu_{t,\omega}}(\mathbf{m}_{0})|
is large, then
\nu_{t-s,j}
’s are highly concentrated, namely for each
j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
there exists
p_{j}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that
\nu_{t-s,j}(B^{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(p_{j},\rho_{0}))\gg\rho_{0}^{0.1\kappa^{\prime}}
(Proposition
4.2
) for
\rho_{0}\asymp\rho^{n}
. This information is not yet sufficient to derive a contradiction. Indeed,
p_{j}
’s might vary over
j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
so it does not give high-concentration of
\nu_{t-s}
directly. Thus we shall consider
\nu_{t-2s}=\sigma_{*}\mu_{t-2s}=\sigma_{*}\big((a_{-s})_{*}\mu_{t-s}\big)
, and deduce from Proposition
4.2
that
\nu_{t-2s}(B^{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(p,\rho))\gg\rho^{\kappa^{\prime}}
for some
p\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
(Proposition
4.5
). Combining this with Proposition
4.9
, we conclude that
|\widehat{\nu_{t,\omega}}(\mathbf{m}_{0})|
must be small if
b_{0}
is not well-approximated by rationals of low denominator, i.e. Proposition
4.10
.

We now briefly explain how to prove (1) and (2) above. In the setting of random walks as
[
BFLM11
]
, the dynamics on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is determined by the law of the random walk. In the case of horospherical translates, the function
\gamma_{s}(x,u)
defined above plays a similar role as the law of the random walk. Proposition
3.3
describes the dynamics on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
induced by
\gamma_{s}(x,u)
, and is a crucial ingredient to prove both (1) and (2). It asserts that for any
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
and
x\in X
, the orbit
\gamma_{s}(x,u)^{tr}\mathbf{m}
with respect to
u\in V
is well-separated on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

For (1), the Fourier coefficient
\widehat{\nu_{t,\omega}}(\mathbf{m}_{0})
can be expressed as the average of Fourier coefficients at this
\Gamma^{tr}
-orbit, so well-separateness of such
\Gamma^{tr}
-orbit implies a rich set of large Fourier coefficients. Unlike
[
BFLM11
]
which studied the random walk on a fixed torus, a technical difficulty in our setting is that the function
\gamma_{s}(x,u)
and the fiber tori
\pi^{-1}(x)
vary with the base point
x\in X
. For this reason, we decompose the measure
\mu_{t-s}
again with finer boxes in
X
so that
\gamma_{s}(x,u)
is stable with respect to
x
in each box.

For (2), we have the well-separateness of the orbit
\gamma_{t-s}(x,u)^{tr}e_{1}
from Proposition
3.3
, and it implies the well-separateness of the first coordinate of the orbit
\gamma_{t-s}(x,u)b_{0}
under some Diophantine condition of

. It implies that the measure

cannot be highly concentrated, so we obtain (2). An effective version of Weyl’s well-known equidistribution criterion for an irrational rotation on the one-dimensional torus will be needed, and here is the place where we use the Diophantine properties of
b_{0}
.

\displaystyle(a_{s}u_{0})_{*}\mu_{y,t-s}(f)

2.10.
Dual affine lattices: reduction to the case

In the later sections, we will need an assumption

. More precisely, we will need the property that the expanding rate
e^{nt}
(when the diagonal flow
a_{t}
acts on the unipotent radical
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is not slower than the contracting rate
e^{-mt}
. In this subsection, we claim that we may assume
m\leq n
.

Recall that an element
y
in
Y=\operatorname{ASL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{ASL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
is parametrized by
y=gw(b)\hat{\Gamma}
, where
g\in G
and
b\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Let us consider the dual map
\varsigma:Y\to Y
defined by
\varsigma(gw(b)\hat{\Gamma})=(g^{tr})^{-1}w(b)\hat{\Gamma}
. Since
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
is preserved by the dual map
g\mapsto(g^{tr})^{-1}
,
\varsigma
is well-defined. Note that the Haar measure
m_{Y}
is also preserved by
\varsigma
,
\varsigma^{2}=\operatorname{id}_{Y}
, and
\varsigma(gy)=(g^{-1})^{tr}\varsigma(y)
for any
g\in G
and
y\in Y
.

Suppose that we already established Theorem
1.3
with
m\leq n
. If
n<m
, for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
let us denote
f^{*}:=f\circ\varsigma
. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f(a_{t}uy)dm_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f^{*}\big(\varsigma(a_{t}uy)\big)dm_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f^{*}\big((a_{t}^{tr})^{-1}(u^{tr})^{-1}y\big)dm_{H}(u).

Note that the push-forward of
m_{H}
by the map
H\in u\mapsto(u^{tr})^{-1}\in H^{-}
is
m_{H^{-}}
. Denoting by
V^{*}
the image of
V
by the map

,

(2.19)

{\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f(a_{t}uy)dm_{H}(u)=\frac{1}{m_{H^{-}}(V^{*})}\int_{V^{*}}f^{*}(a_{-t}uy)dm_{H^{-}}(u).}

Let us consider a permutation matrix
M=\left(\begin{matrix}0_{m,n}&\operatorname{Id}_{n}\\
\operatorname{Id}_{m}&0_{n,m}\end{matrix}\right)\in G
and define

by
f^{*}_{M}(y)=f^{*}(M^{-1}y)
. Then the conjugation of

acts on
a_{-t}
and
H^{-}
as follows:

\operatorname{Ad}_{M}(a_{-t})=\operatorname{diag}\left(e^{mt}\operatorname{Id}_{n},e^{-nt}\operatorname{Id}_{m}\right),

\operatorname{Ad}_{M}\left(\begin{matrix}\operatorname{Id}_{m}&0\\
A&\operatorname{Id}_{n}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\operatorname{Id}_{n}&A\\
0&\operatorname{Id}_{m}\end{matrix}\right)

for any
A\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n,m}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular,
\operatorname{Ad}_{M}(H^{-})
is the expanding horospherical group of
\operatorname{Ad}_{M}(a_{-1})
, and
(\operatorname{Ad}_{M})_{*}(m_{H^{-}})
is the Haar measure of the expanding horospherical group of
\operatorname{Ad}_{M}(a_{-1})
.

Observe that the role of
m
and
n
are now swapped. Writing

f^{*}(a_{-t}uy)=f^{*}_{M}\big(\operatorname{Ad}_{M}(a_{-t})\operatorname{Ad}_{M}(u)(My)\big),

we may apply the assumed Theorem
1.3
with
m\leq n
:

\frac{1}{m_{H^{-}}(V^{*})}\int_{V^{*}}f^{*}(a_{-t}uy)dm_{H^{-}}(u)=\int_{Y}f_{M}^{*}dm_{Y}+O\left(\mathcal{S}(f_{M}^{*})\zeta(b,e^{\frac{nt}{2}})^{-\delta^{\prime}}\right).

Note that
\int f^{*}_{M}dm_{Y}=\int f^{*}dm_{y}=\int fdm_{Y}
,
My=Mgw(b)\hat{\Gamma}
,
\|Mg\|\ll\|g\|
, and
\mathcal{S}(f^{*}_{M})\ll\mathcal{S}(f\circ\varsigma)
. To sum up, we obtain

(2.20)

{\frac{1}{m_{H}(V)}\int_{V}f(a_{t}uy)dm_{H}(u)=\int_{Y}fdm_{Y}+O\left(\mathcal{S}(f\circ\varsigma)\zeta(b,e^{\frac{nt}{2}})^{-\delta^{\prime}}\right).}

Regarding the Sobolev norm, suppose that the Sobolev norm in (
2.20

l
, i.e.
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{Y}_{l}
. Since
\varsigma
is an isometry on
X
, it holds that
\mathcal{S}^{X}_{l}(f\circ\varsigma)\ll\mathcal{S}^{X}_{l}(f)
. On each fiber, we have an estimate

\|gb\|\leq\|g\|_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\|g^{*}b\|\leq C_{3}^{2}C_{6}^{2}\operatorname{ht}(g\Gamma)^{2d-2}\|g^{*}b\|

for any
g\in G
and

, using (
2.3
) and (
2.15
). It follows that

and

. Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
be the Sobolev norm of degree

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}^{Y}_{(2d-1)l}

hence the error term in (

In the rest of this paper, we assume

\displaystyle=\|\iota(x)^{tr}\|_{\textrm{op}}\|u^{tr}\|_{\textrm{op}}\|a_{s}^{tr}\|_{\textrm{op}}\|(\xi(x,u)^{tr})^{-1}\|_{\textrm{op}}\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}^{3}C_{6}C_{14}\epsilon^{-1}\textrm{ht}(x)^{d-1}\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|e^{ns}.

(2) The equation
\gamma(x,u)^{tr}\mathbf{m}_{0}=\mathbf{m}
can be written

(3.4)

{a_{s}^{tr}(\xi(x,u)^{tr})^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{0}=(u^{tr})^{-1}(\iota(x)^{tr})^{-1}\mathbf{m}.}

Let
v_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
v_{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the vectors such that
\left(\begin{matrix}v_{+}\\
v_{-}\end{matrix}\right)=(\iota(x)^{tr})^{-1}\mathbf{m}
. Here,
v_{+}
and
v_{-}
only depend on
\mathbf{m}
, not on
u
.
For any
A\in\varphi^{-1}(V)
, by a straightforward computation we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle(u^{tr})^{-1}(\iota(x)^{tr})^{-1}\mathbf{m}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}v_{+}\\
-A^{tr}v_{+}+v_{-}\end{matrix}\right),

where
u=\varphi(A)
. On the other hand, the left hand side of (
3.4
) is equal to
\left(\begin{matrix}e^{ns}\mathbf{x}(x,u)\\
e^{-ms}\mathbf{y}(x,u)\end{matrix}\right)
. Combining this with (
3.5
), if
u=\varphi(A)
is a solution of (
3.4
), then

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|A^{tr}v_{+}-v_{-}\|\leq e^{-ms}\|\mathbf{y}(x,u)\|.

If
u\in V_{x,\epsilon}
, we have
\|\mathbf{x}(x,u)\|>\epsilon^{2}\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|
and

\|\mathbf{y}(x,u)\|\leq\|(\xi(x,u)^{tr})^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{0}\|\leq C_{3}\|\xi(x,u)\|\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|\leq C_{3}\epsilon^{-1}\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|

by (
3.2
) and the definition of
V_{x,\epsilon}
in Lemma
3.2
. Moreover,
\|v_{+}\|>\epsilon^{2}\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|e^{ns}
since
v_{+}=e^{ns}\mathbf{x}(x,u)
. To sum up, a solution
u=\varphi(A)
must be in a thin tube
\left\{A\in M_{m,n}(\mathbb{R}):\|A^{tr}v_{+}-v_{-}\|\leq C_{3}\epsilon^{-1}\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|e^{-ms}\right\}
, where
\|v_{+}\|>\epsilon^{2}\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|e^{ns}
. We can consider
A
as in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{mn}
, then the volume of the intersection of this tube and the ball
B^{\mathbb{R}^{mn}}(0,C_{14})
is

\ll\left(C_{14}^{-1}\|v_{+}\|^{-1}(C_{3}\epsilon^{-1}\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\|e^{-ms})\right)^{n}\ll\epsilon^{-3n}e^{-dns}
. Therefore, for any
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the
m_{H}
-measure of the set of
u\in V_{x,\epsilon}
satisfying
\gamma_{x}(u)^{tr}\mathbf{m}_{0}=\mathbf{m}
is less than
C_{16}\epsilon^{-3n}e^{-dns}
for some constant
C_{16}>1
.
∎

3.2.
A relation between Fourier coefficients of measures

In this subsection, we explore a relation between
\mu_{y_{0},t}
and
\mu_{y_{0},t-s}
, which are two probability measures of expanding translates of
y_{0}
, and their Fourier coefficients via Proposition
2.1
, where
y_{0}\in Y
and
0<s<t
.

For each
\widetilde{h}=h_{+}h_{0}\in\widetilde{H}
with
h_{+}\in H
and
h_{0}\in H^{0}
, we define a smooth diffeomorphism
\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}
of
H
onto itself by

\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)=\operatorname{Ad}(h_{0})(u)h_{+}^{-1}=h_{0}uh_{0}^{-1}h_{+}^{-1}.

In particular,
u\widetilde{h}^{-1}=h_{0}^{-1}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)
.

Let
c_{1}:=\inf\left\{\mathbf{d}^{G}(\operatorname{id},\gamma):\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\left\{\operatorname{id}\right\}\right\}
. From the discreteness of
\Gamma
, we have
c_{1}>0
. We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists a constant
C_{17}>1
such that the following holds. Let
s>0
,
0<r<c_{2}
,
0<\epsilon<r^{2}
and
\mathbf{r}=(r\epsilon,r\epsilon,r\epsilon e^{-ds})
for some constant
0<c_{2}<(2C_{9}^{4}C_{17})^{-1}
. For
x\in X
and
u\in V
, suppose that
\iota(x)\in\mathcal{F}(C_{9}^{4}r\epsilon,C_{4}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{100d^{2}}})
and
\xi(x,u)\in\mathcal{F}(r,r^{\frac{1}{d-1}})
. If
x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{B}_{C_{9}^{3}\mathbf{r}}(x)
, then there uniquely exist
\widetilde{h}\in B^{H}(\operatorname{id},C_{9}^{3}r\epsilon)B^{H^{0}}(\operatorname{id},C_{9}^{3}r\epsilon)
and
h_{-}\in B^{H^{-}}(\operatorname{id},C_{9}^{3}r\epsilon e^{-ds})
such that
x^{\prime}=\widetilde{h}h_{-}x
,
\mathbf{d}^{X}(a_{s}ux,a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)x^{\prime})\leq C_{17}r\epsilon
and
\gamma(x,u)=\gamma(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u))
.

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle a_{s}u\iota(x)=\xi(x,u)\gamma(x,u),

\displaystyle a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime})=\xi(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u))\gamma(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)).

Since
\iota(x)
is in
\mathcal{F}(C_{9}^{4}r\epsilon,C_{4}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{100d^{2}}})
,
\iota
is an isometry on
\mathsf{B}_{C_{9}^{3}\mathbf{r}}(x)
, so
\iota(x^{\prime})
is in
\mathsf{B}_{C_{9}^{3}\mathbf{r}}\iota(x)
. It follows that there uniquely exist

h_{+}\in B^{H}(\operatorname{id},C_{9}^{3}r\epsilon),\;h_{0}\in B^{H^{0}}(\operatorname{id},C_{9}^{3}r\epsilon),\;h_{-}\in B^{H^{-}}(\operatorname{id},C_{9}^{3}r\epsilon e^{-ds})

such that
\iota(x^{\prime})=\widetilde{h}h_{-}\iota(x)
, where
\widetilde{h}=h_{+}h_{0}
. Observe that

\displaystyle a_{s}u\iota(x)

\displaystyle=a_{s}uh_{-}^{-1}\widetilde{h}^{-1}\iota(x^{\prime})=(a_{s}uh_{-}^{-1}u^{-1}a_{-s})a_{s}u\widetilde{h}^{-1}\iota(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(a_{s}uh_{-}^{-1}u^{-1}a_{-s})a_{s}h_{0}^{-1}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(a_{s}uh_{-}^{-1}u^{-1}a_{-s})h_{0}^{-1}a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime}).

Note that
\mathbf{d}^{G}(\operatorname{id},h_{0}^{-1})<C_{9}^{3}r\epsilon
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{G}(\operatorname{id},a_{s}uh_{-}^{-1}u^{-1}a_{-s})

\displaystyle\asymp\|\operatorname{Ad}_{a_{s}u}(-\log h_{-})\|_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle\ll e^{ds}\|\operatorname{Ad}_{u}(-\log h_{-})\|_{\mathfrak{g}}\ll e^{ds}\mathbf{d}^{G}(\operatorname{id},h_{-})\ll r\epsilon.

Hence, for some constant
C_{17}>1
we have

(3.7)

{\mathbf{d}^{G}(a_{s}u\iota(x),a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime}))\leq C_{17}r\epsilon<r.}

Since
a_{s}u\iota(x)=\xi(x,u)\gamma(x,u)
and
\xi(x,u)\in\mathcal{F}(r,r^{\frac{1}{d-1}})
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{G}(a_{s}u\iota(x),a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{d}^{X}(\xi(x,u)\Gamma,\xi(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u))\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}^{G}(\xi(x,u),\xi(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u))).

Combining (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we get

(3.9)

{\mathbf{d}^{G}(\xi(x,u),\xi(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)))\leq C_{17}r\epsilon.}

Moreover,
\mathbf{d}^{X}(a_{s}ux,a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)x^{\prime})\leq C_{17}r\epsilon
from (

), and

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{G}

\displaystyle(\gamma(x,u),\gamma(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)))=\mathbf{d}^{G}(\xi(x,u)^{-1}a_{s}u\iota(x),\xi(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u))^{-1}a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{d}^{G}(\xi(x,u)^{-1}a_{s}u\iota(x),\xi(x,u)^{-1}a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle+\mathbf{d}^{G}(\xi(x,u)^{-1}a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime}),\xi(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u))^{-1}a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\|\xi(x,u)\|^{2}\big(\mathbf{d}^{G}(a_{s}u\iota(x),a_{s}\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u)\iota(x^{\prime}))+\mathbf{d}^{G}(\xi(x,u),\xi(x^{\prime},\Psi_{\widetilde{h}}(u))\big)

using (
2.2
) and the right-invariance of

. From (
2.15
) and

and deduce

Proposition 3.5
.

From the definitions (
3.12

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu_{t,\omega}}(\mathbf{m}_{0})

Recall that for any

2.1

(3.18)

F:G\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is given by

F(g)^{2}=\frac{(\sum_{i,j}|g_{ij}|^{2})(\sum_{i,j}|(g^{-1})_{ij}|^{2})}{\sum_{i,j}|g_{ij}|^{2}+\sum_{i,j}|(g^{-1})_{ij}|^{2}},

and satisfies
F(g)\leq\min((\sum_{i,j}|g_{ij}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}},(\sum_{i,j}|(g^{-1})_{ij}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}})\leq 2F(g)
for any
g\in G
. Since the maps
g\mapsto\sum_{i,j}|g_{ij}|^{2}
and
g\mapsto\sum_{i,j}|(g^{-1})_{ij}|^{2}
are proper,
F
is also proper. We will construct a fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}
of
X=G/\Gamma
so that it consists of every elements in
\mathcal{F}^{\circ}:=\left\{g\in G:F(g)<F(g\gamma),\;\forall\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\left\{\operatorname{id}\right\}\right\}
and properly chosen elements from its boundary. We can observe that the closure of
\mathcal{F}^{\circ}
is contained in the set
\left\{g\in G:F(g)\leq F(g\gamma),\;\forall\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\left\{\operatorname{id}\right\}\right\}
and the boundary
\overline{\mathcal{F}^{\circ}}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\circ}
has measure zero with respect to
m_{G}
.

(1) Let us first show that for any
x\in X
, there exists
g\in\overline{\mathcal{F}^{\circ}}
such that
x=g\Gamma
. For any
x\in X
, let
\Lambda_{x}
be the corresponding lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\Lambda_{x}^{*}
be the dual lattice of
\Lambda_{x}
, i.e.

{\Lambda_{x}^{*}:=\left\{v^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:v^{*}\cdot v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\quad\forall v\in\Lambda_{x}\right\}.}

We refer
[
Cas71
]
for basic properties of the dual lattice. For any
g\in\phi^{-1}(x)
, the column vectors of
g
are vectors in
\Lambda_{x}
and the row vectors of
g^{-1}
are vectors in
\Lambda_{x}^{*}
, where
\phi:G\to X
is the canonical projection.
Observe that for any
R>0
, there are only finitely many vectors in either
\Lambda_{x}
or
\Lambda_{x}^{*}
with Euclidean length
\leq R
. It follows that the function
F|_{\phi^{-1}(x)}:\phi^{-1}(x)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
that is restricted to the subset
\phi^{-1}(x)
attains its minimum. Hence, for any
x\in X
, there exists
g\in\overline{\mathcal{F}^{\circ}}
such that
x=g\Gamma
.

For each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, let
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}:=\left\{g\in G:F(g)\leq F(g\gamma)\right\}
. Note that
\overline{\mathcal{F}}=\displaystyle\bigcap_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
,
\partial\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}=\left\{g\in G:F(g)=F(g\gamma)\right\}
, and
\partial\mathcal{F}\subseteq\displaystyle\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\partial\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
. If
g
is in the interior
\mathcal{F}^{\circ}
, then
F(g)<F(g\gamma)
for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Hence, for any
x\in\phi(\mathcal{F}^{\circ})
, there uniquely exists
g\in\mathcal{F}^{\circ}
such that
x=g\Gamma
, where
\phi:G\to X
is the canonical projection map. Let
\iota(x)
be the unique element in
\mathcal{F}^{\circ}
, then
\iota:\phi(\mathcal{F}^{\circ})\to\mathcal{F}^{\circ}
is a continuous measure-preserving map satisfying
\phi\circ\iota=\operatorname{id}_{\phi(\mathcal{F}^{\circ})\to\phi(\mathcal{F}^{\circ})}
.

We can choose elements from the boundary so that for any
x\in X
there uniquely exists
g\in\mathcal{F}
such that
x=g\Gamma
. Since the
m_{G}
-measure of the boundary
\overline{\mathcal{F}^{\circ}}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\circ}
is zero, the map
\iota
is extended to be a measure-preserving map from
X
to
\mathcal{F}
. It also follows that
m_{G}(\mathcal{F})=m_{G}(\mathcal{F}^{\circ})=m_{X}(\phi(\mathcal{F}^{\circ}))=m_{X}(X)=1
.

(2) We next prove the estimate (
2.15
). Let
\lambda_{j}(\Lambda)
denote the
j
-th successive minimum of a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
i.e. the infimum of
\lambda
such that the ball
B^{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,\lambda)
contains
j
independent vectors. By Minkowski’s second theorem
[
Cas71
, Theorem I in Chapter VIII]
,
\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{x})\lambda_{2}(\Lambda_{x})\cdots\lambda_{d}(\Lambda_{x})\asymp 1
for any
x\in X
.

Since
\lambda_{j}(\Lambda_{x})\geq\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{x})\geq\textrm{ht}(x)^{-1}
for any
1\leq j\leq d-1
, we have
\lambda_{d}(\Lambda_{x})\ll\textrm{ht}(x)^{d-1}
. For
x\in\phi(\mathcal{F}^{\circ})
let
g=\iota(x)
, i.e.
g\in\mathcal{F}
is the element with
x=g\Gamma
. Then
\lambda_{d}(\Lambda_{x})\ll\textrm{ht}(x)^{d-1}
implies that there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that the Euclidean length of each column vectors of
g\gamma
is
\ll\textrm{ht}(x)^{d-1}
. Hence, We have
|g_{ij}|\leq F(g)\leq F(g\gamma)\ll\textrm{ht}(x)^{d-1}
for any
i,j
. In order to estimate the components of
g^{-1}
, we may consider the dual lattice
\Lambda_{x}^{*}
. By Mahler’s inequality
[
Cas71
, Theorem VI in Chapter VIII]
,
\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{x})\lambda_{d}(\Lambda_{x}^{*})\ll 1
, so we have
\lambda_{d}(\Lambda_{x}^{*})\ll\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{x})\leq\textrm{ht}(x)
. In other words, there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that the Euclidean length of each row vectors of
(g\gamma)^{-1}
is
\ll\textrm{ht}(x)
. It follows that
|(g^{-1})_{ij}|\leq F(g)\leq F(g\gamma)\ll\textrm{ht}(x)
for any
i,j
. Thus,
\|\iota(x)\|\ll\textrm{ht}(x)^{d-1}
for any
x\in\phi(\mathcal{F}^{\circ})
.

(3) We prove the estimate (
2.16
). We first show that

m_{G}(\left\{g\in\mathcal{F}:\textrm{dist}(g,\partial\mathcal{F})<r\right\})\ll r^{\frac{1}{2(d-1)^{2}+1}}.

For each
R>0
, denote by
\Upsilon(R)
the set of elements
g\in G
such that
(\sum_{i,j}|g_{ij}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq R
. We make use of the following volume estimate of
\Upsilon(R)
and counting estimate of
\Upsilon(R)\cap\Gamma
(See
[
DRS93
, Example 1.6 and Appendix 1]
or
[
EM93
]
):

(A.1)

{m_{G}(\Upsilon(R))\asymp R^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}},\quad|\Upsilon(R)\cap\Gamma|\asymp R^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}.}

In (2) we showed that if
\textrm{ht}(x)\leq\epsilon^{-1}
, then the column vectors of
g=\iota(x)
has Euclidean length
\ll\epsilon^{-(d-1)}
, so
(\sum_{i,j}|g_{ij}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll\epsilon^{-(d-1)}
. It means that the set
\mathcal{F}\setminus\Upsilon(R)
is contained in
\left\{g\in\mathcal{F}:\textrm{ht}(g\Gamma)\gg R^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\right\}
, hence

(A.2)

{m_{G}(\mathcal{F}\setminus\Upsilon(R))\ll R^{-\frac{d}{d-1}}.}

Observe that if
g\in\Upsilon(R)
and
\gamma\notin\Upsilon(R^{3})
, then
F(g)<F(g\gamma)
, so
g
is not on the boundary
\partial\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
. It follows that

\partial\mathcal{F}\cap\Upsilon(R)\subseteq\displaystyle\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Upsilon(R^{3})\cap\Gamma}(\partial\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}\cap\Upsilon(R)).

Denote by
\mathcal{F}(r)
and
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(r)
the set of
g
such that
\textrm{dist}(g,\partial\mathcal{F})<r
and
\textrm{dist}(g,\partial\mathcal{F}_{\gamma})<r
, respectively. Note that each
\partial\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
is the set of zeros of the equation
S_{\gamma}(g)=0
, where
S_{\gamma}(g)=F(g\gamma)-F(g)
, hence a
(\dim G-1)
-dimensional hypersurface of
G
. One can check that the first derivative
\triangledown S_{\gamma}(g):\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is bounded away from zero uniformly on
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\left\{\operatorname{id}\right\}
. Moreover, if
g\in\Upsilon(R)
and
\gamma\notin\Upsilon(R^{3})
, then the norm of the derivatives of higher degrees are bounded above by
\ll\|g\|^{2}+\|g\gamma\|^{2}\ll R^{8}
. Thus we have
S_{\gamma}(\operatorname{exp}(v)g)=S_{\gamma}(g)+\triangledown S_{\gamma}(g)\cdot v+O(R^{8}\|v\|^{2})
, hence
\partial\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
is locally approximated in the neighborhood
B^{G}(g,R^{-10})
by the zero set of the linear equation
S_{\gamma}(g)+\triangledown S_{\gamma}(g)\cdot v=0
. It follows that for
r<R^{-10}
,

m_{G}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(r)\cap\Upsilon(R))\ll\frac{r}{R^{-10}}m_{G}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(R^{-10})\cap\Upsilon(R))\ll R^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+10}r

by (
A.1
). Therefore, using (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we have

\displaystyle m_{G}(\mathcal{F}(r))

\displaystyle=m_{G}(\mathcal{F}(r)\cap\Upsilon(R))+m_{G}(\mathcal{F}(r)\setminus\Upsilon(R))

\displaystyle\leq m_{G}(\displaystyle\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Upsilon(R^{3})\cap\Gamma}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(r)\cap\Upsilon(R)))+m_{G}(\mathcal{F}\setminus\Upsilon(R))

\displaystyle\ll\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Upsilon(R^{3})\cap\Gamma}m_{G}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(r)\cap\Upsilon(R))+R^{-\frac{d}{d-1}}

\displaystyle\ll|\Upsilon(R^{3})\cap\Gamma|R^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+10}r+R^{-\frac{d}{d-1}}

\displaystyle\ll R^{2d(d-1)+10}r+R^{-\frac{d}{d-1}}

for
r<R^{-10}
. Choosing
R
to satisfy
r=R^{-10d(d-1)}
, we obtain

m_{G}(\mathcal{F}(r))\ll r^{\frac{1}{10d^{2}}}.

Combining with (
2.4
), we have shown that

(A.3)

{m_{G}(\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{0}(r,\epsilon))\ll r^{\frac{1}{10d^{2}}}+\epsilon^{d},}

where we denote
\mathcal{F}_{0}(r,\epsilon):=\left\{g\in\mathcal{F}:\textrm{ht}(g\Gamma)\leq\epsilon^{-1},\textrm{dist}(g,\partial\mathcal{F})\geq r\right\}
.

Denote by
\mathcal{E}(r)
and
\mathcal{E}^{-1}(r)
by the set of
g
such that
\operatorname{dist}(g,\mathcal{E})<r^{\frac{1}{20d}}
and
\operatorname{dist}(g^{-1},\mathcal{E})=\operatorname{dist}(g,\mathcal{E}^{-1})<r^{\frac{1}{20d}}
, respectively. It is now sufficient to estimate
m_{G}\big(\mathcal{E}^{-1}(r)\cap\mathcal{F}\big)=m_{G}\big(\mathcal{E}(r)\cap(\mathcal{F})^{-1}\big)
. Note that
r^{\frac{1}{40d}}
-neighborhood of
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(r^{\frac{1}{50d}},C_{4}r^{\frac{1}{1000d^{2}}}))^{-1}
is still contained in
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(r^{\frac{1}{4}},C_{4}r^{\frac{1}{20d}}))^{-1}
. Since
\mathcal{E}
is a
(d^{2}-2)
-dimensional analytic closed submanifold of
G
, we have

\displaystyle m_{G}\big(\mathcal{E}(r)\cap(\mathcal{F}_{0}(r^{\frac{1}{50d}},

\displaystyle C_{4}r^{\frac{1}{1000d^{2}}}))^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\ll r^{\frac{1}{40d}}m_{G}\big(\mathcal{E}(r^{\frac{1}{2}})\cap(\mathcal{F}_{0}(r^{\frac{1}{4}},C_{4}r^{\frac{1}{20d}}))^{-1}\big)\ll r^{\frac{1}{40d}}.

By (
A.3
) we also have
m_{G}\big(\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{0}(r^{\frac{1}{50d}},C_{4}r^{\frac{1}{1000d^{2}}})\big)\ll r^{\frac{1}{100d^{3}}}
. It follows that
m_{G}\big(\mathcal{E}^{-1}(r)\cap\mathcal{F}\big)\ll r^{\frac{1}{100d^{3}}}
. Let
\kappa_{3}:=\frac{1}{100d^{3}}
. Again using (
A.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle m_{G}(\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}(r,\epsilon))

\displaystyle\leq m_{G}(\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{0}(r,\epsilon))+m_{G}(\mathcal{E}^{-1}(r)\cap\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\ll r^{\frac{1}{10d^{2}}}+\epsilon^{d}+r^{\frac{1}{100d^{3}}}\ll\max\left\{r^{\kappa_{3}},\epsilon^{d}\right\}.

Appendix B
Effective Weyl’s criterion on
\mathbb{T}

This subsection is devoted to proving the estimate (
4.15
) using an effective version of Weyl’s criterion. Neither the result nor the proof is new, but we prove it here to express the estimate in a suitable form using the function
\zeta
. To prove the estimate (
4.15
) it is enough to show the following statement.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\Xi=[x_{0}-\rho,x_{0}+\rho]
be an interval in
\mathbb{T}
, where
x\in\mathbb{T}
and
0<\rho<\frac{1}{2}
. For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
and
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

(B.1)

{\frac{1}{T}|\left\{0\leq k\leq T-1:k\alpha\in\Xi\right\}|\ll\rho+\rho^{-1}\zeta(\alpha,T)^{-1},}

where
\zeta(\alpha,T)=\min\left\{N\in\mathbb{N}:\min_{1\leq q\leq N}\|q\alpha\|_{\mathbb{Z}}\leq\frac{N^{2}}{T}\right\}
.

Proof.

For any
0<\epsilon<\rho
, there exists a non-negative approximation function
\psi_{\rho}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
such that
\operatorname{Supp}\psi_{\rho}\subseteq[-\rho,\rho]
,
\int_{\mathbb{T}}\psi_{\rho}(x)dx=1
, and
\|\psi_{\rho}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll\rho^{-2}
. Then for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, we have
|\widehat{\psi_{\rho}}(m)|\ll\min(1,|m|^{-2}\rho^{-2})
. Let
\phi=\psi_{\rho}*\mathds{1}_{[x_{0}-2\rho,x_{0}+2\rho]}
, then
\phi(x)\geq\mathds{1}_{\Xi}(x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{T}
. We also have

|\widehat{\phi}(m)|\ll\rho\min(1,|m|^{-2}\rho^{-2})=\min(\rho,|m|^{-2}\rho^{-1})

for
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
. In order to get an upper bound of the left-hand side of (
B.1
), it suffices to estimate
\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq T-1}\phi(k\alpha)
. We can expand this summation as follows using the Fourier expansion of
\phi
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq T-1}\phi(k\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\phi}(m)\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq T-1}e^{2\pi imk\alpha}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\phi}(0)+\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{0<|m|<\zeta(\alpha,T)}\widehat{\phi}(m)\frac{1-e^{2\pi imT\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}

\displaystyle+\displaystyle\sum_{|m|\geq\zeta(\alpha,T)}\widehat{\phi}(m)\left(\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq T-1}e^{2\pi imk\alpha}\right).

We now estimate each term as follows:

{\widehat{\phi}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathds{1}_{[x_{0}-2\rho,x_{0}+2\rho]}(x)dx=4\rho,}

{\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{0<|m|<\zeta(\alpha,T)}\widehat{\phi}(m)\frac{1-e^{2\pi imT\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}\ll\rho\zeta(\alpha,T)^{-1},}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{|m|\geq\zeta(\alpha,T)}\widehat{\phi}(m)\left(\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq T-1}e^{2\pi imk\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\rho^{-1}\displaystyle\sum_{|m|\geq\zeta(\alpha,T)}|m|^{-2}

\displaystyle\ll\rho^{-1}\zeta(\alpha,T)^{-1},

using
|\widehat{\phi}(m)|\ll\rho
and
\left|\frac{1-e^{2\pi imT\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}\right|\ll\|m\alpha\|_{\mathbb{Z}}^{-1}\leq T(\zeta(\alpha,T)-1)^{-2}
for the second estimate, and
|\widehat{\phi}(m)|\ll\rho^{-1}|m|^{-2}
and
\left|\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq T-1}e^{2\pi imk\alpha}\right|\leq 1
for the third estimate. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}|\left\{0\leq k\leq T-1:k\alpha\in\Xi\right\}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq T-1}\phi(k\alpha)\ll\rho+\rho^{-1}\zeta(\alpha,T)^{-1}.

∎
