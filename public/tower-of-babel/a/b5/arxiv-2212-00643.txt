Quantum states in disordered media. II. Spatial charge carrier distribution
A. V. Nenashev, S. D. Baranovskii, K. Meerholz, F. Gebhard

Abstract
The space- and temperature-dependent electron distribution n ⁡ ( 𝐫 , T ) n(\mathbf{r},T) is essential for the theoretical description of the opto-electronic properties of disordered semiconductors. We present two powerful techniques to access n ⁡ ( 𝐫 , T ) n(\mathbf{r},T) without solving the Schrödinger equation. First, we derive the density for non-degenerate electrons by applying the Hamiltonian recursively to random wave functions (RWF). Second, we obtain a temperature-dependent effective potential from the application of a universal low-pass filter (ULF) to the random potential acting on the charge carriers in disordered media. Thereby, the full quantum-mechanical problem is reduced to the quasi-classical description of n ⁡ ( 𝐫 , T ) n(\mathbf{r},T) in an effective potential. We numerically verify both approaches by comparison with the exact quantum-mechanical solution. Both approaches prove superior to the widely used localization landscape theory (LLT) when we compare our approximate results for the charge carrier density and mobility at elevated temperatures obtained by RWF, ULF, and LLT with those from the exact solution of the Schrödinger equation.



I
Introduction

Short-range disorder potentials govern the opto-electronic properties of a wide
variety of disordered materials
designed for applications in modern electronics
Baranovski 2006
.
In this work we address the theoretical description of the energetically low-lying,
spatially localized states
in several classes of disordered materials.

One broad class of widely studied disordered materials
are semiconductor alloys with electronic states
localized by a short-range disorder potential.
Alloys are crystalline semiconductors, such as A
{}_{\bar{x}}
B
{}_{1-\bar{x}}
,
whose lattice sites are occupied in a given proportion
\bar{x}
by chemically
different isoelectronic atoms,
A
and
B
.
Research on semiconductor alloys currently experiences a renaissance because
alloying is one of the most efficient tools to adjust material properties for
device applications. For example, alloying permits to tune lattice constants, effective
masses of charge carriers and, most importantly, the band-structure of the underlying
semiconductor.
In particular, band gaps in alloy semiconductors are sensitive
to the mole fractions
\bar{x}
and
1-\bar{x}

of the alloy components. Since the band gap is a key property responsible for light
absorption and emission, band-gap tunability in a wide energy range opens
rich perspectives for applications of alloy semiconductors in various opto-electronic
devices.
Over recent years, this band-gap engineering has been applied
to nitride semiconductors used in modern
LEDs
Weisbuch et al. 2021
, to perovskites for applications
in photovoltaics
Hu et al. 2019
;
Baranovskii et al. 2022
,
and to two-dimensional systems, such as transition metal dichalcogenides,
desired to miniaturize the corresponding devices toward nearly atomically
thin dimensions
Chaves et al. 2020
;
Masenda et al. 2021
.

Another class of disordered materials with short-range disorder potentials are
amorphous oxide semiconductors,
such as InGaZnO, desired for applications in thin-film transistors for transparent
and flexible
flat-panel displays
Nomura et al. 2004
;
Nenashev et al. 2019
.
Also traditional amorphous semiconductors,
such as hydrogenated amorphous silicon, hydrogenated amorphous carbon,
poly-crystalline and micro-crystalline silicon, belong to disordered materials,
whose opto-electronic properties are governed by short-range disorder potentials.
Such amorphous semiconductors are
desired for applications in Schottky barrier diodes, p–i–n diodes,
thin-film transistors, and thin-film solar cells
Baranovski 2006
.

Besides their unique properties favorable for device applications,
materials with short-range disorder
are advantageous systems for developing and testing theoretical descriptions
of disorder effects.
The compositional fluctuations in alloys
and structural short-range fluctuations in oxide and amorphous semiconductors cause
spatial fluctuations of the band gap which creates
a random potential acting on electrons and holes.
The isoelectronic substitution of the alloy components and/or amorphous atomic
structure creates a short-range fluctuating crystal potential, and the
physics is not complicated by long-range effects.
The random potential leads to the spatial localization of
charge carriers in electron states at low energies that
dominate the opto-electronic properties of disordered semiconductors.
Therefore, the low-energy localized electron states require an appropriate
theoretical description.

Already in the 1960s,
a powerful theoretical tool to calculate the density of low-energy localized states (DOS)
without solving the Schrödinger equation was developed by Halperin
and Lax
Halperin and Lax 1966
.
They recognized that the spatial spread of wave functions
even in the low-energy localized states is much broader than the spatial scale
of the fluctuations in the random potential
V(\mathbf{r})
. Hence, they proposed to
apply a filter to smooth
V(\mathbf{r})
where
the absolute square of the wave function serves
as their filter function
Halperin and Lax 1966
. Later,
Baranovskii and Efros
Baranovskii and Efros 1978
addressed the same problem
by a slightly different variational technique and confirmed the
result of Halperin and Lax for the low-energy tail of the DOS.
Theoretical studies
have been performed for various kinds of disordered semiconductors
that focus on the averaged DOS,
leaving aside the temperature-dependent spatial distribution
of the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
.
However, the knowledge of
n(\mathbf{r},T)
in a random potential is required to study theoretically charge transport and light absorption/emission
in disordered semiconductors.

The distribution
n(\mathbf{r},T)
is obtained from
the solution of the Schrödinger equation in the presence of a disorder potential.
However, solving the Schrödinger equation
is extremely demanding with respect to computational time and computer memory.
It is hardly affordable for applications to realistically large chemically
complex systems.
Therefore, theoretical
tools to obtain the essential features of localized
states without solving the Schrödinger equation are highly desirable.

One of such tools is the recently introduced ‘localization landscape
theory’ (LLT)
Arnold et al. 2016
;
Filoche et al. 2017
;
Piccardo et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2017
.
The LLT is widely considered as one of the efficient theoretical approaches
to calculate the local density of states in disordered systems, for instance,
in light emitting
diodes
Chen et al. 2018
;
Di Vito et al. 2020
;
Weisbuch et al. 2021
;
Montoya et al. 2021
;
Tibaldi et al. 2021
;
Chaudhuri et al. 2021
;
Shen et al. 2021
;
Shen et al. 2022

and in photodetectors
Tsai et al. 2020
;
Chow et al. 2020
. The LLT has been used to
simulate the carrier effective
potential fluctuations induced by alloy disorder in InGaN/GaN core-shell
microrods
Liu et al. 2019
. Furthermore, the LLT has been applied to compute
the eigenstate localization length at very low energies in a two-dimensional disorder
potential
Shamailov et al. 2021
. The LLT is sometimes considered capable
to reveal errors in the finite element method software in applications to
alloys
Zhan et al. 2021
. The LLT is an important ingredient
for calculating quantum corrections in drift-diffusion
models
Bertazzi et al. 2020
;
O’Donovan et al. 2021
;
O’Donovan et al. 2022
.
It has been used for the computation of light absorption in three-dimensional
InGaN alloys of different compositions
Banon et al. 2022

and in mixed halide perovskites
Liu et al. 2022
.

The LLT is based on a mathematical
theory of quantum localization that introduces an effective
localization potential.
Using the effective potential, the quantum
mechanical problem reduces to a quasi-classical description
of charge carriers localized in the effective potential landscape.
This allows the prediction of localization regions for electrons and holes,
of their corresponding energy levels, and of the (local) densities of states.
The effective potential can be directly implemented in a drift-diffusion model
of carrier transport
and into the calculation of absorption/emission transitions
so that physical features on a mesoscopic scale become accessible.

In the preceding paper
Gebhard et al. 2023
, it was shown that the LLT
becomes equivalent to the low-pass filter
approach of Halperin and Lax
Halperin and Lax 1966

for the specific case of a Lorentzian filter when applied to the Schrödinger equation
with a constant mass.
In this work, we propose
two recipes for calculating
n(\mathbf{r},T)
in disordered systems
without solving
the Schrödinger equation, both of them prove to be superior to the LLT.

The first recipe is the random-wave-function (RWF) algorithm described
in Sect.
II.3
.
In this approach, many sets of random wave functions are generated
for a given realization
of the potential
V(\mathbf{r})

acting on the charge carriers. Such procedure has been suggested by Lu and Steinerberger
Lu and Steinerberger 2018
in order to search for the low-lying eigenfunctions of various linear operators.
After a repeated application of the thermal operator,
the temperature-dependent electron density
is found from averaging the results over different sets.
This procedure provides reliable results for
n(\mathbf{r},T)

for fairly large systems.
The RWF approach has the following advantages over the LLT.
While the output of the LLT depends on the choice of an adjustable parameter
that can be found only by comparison with the exact solution,
the RWF scheme does not contain any adjustable parameters.
Furthermore, the accuracy of the RWF approach for computing
n(\mathbf{r},T)

can be improved systematically,
whereas the accuracy of the LLT is limited by construction.
Moreover, all temperatures are accessible for the RWF algorithm,
whereas LLT is valid only at sufficiently high
T
,
as discussed in Sect.
IV
.

The second recipe employs an effective temperature-dependent potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)

that permits a quasi-classical calculation of the particle density.
The effective potential is obtained from the random potential
V(\mathbf{r})
by applying
a universal low-pass filter (ULF),
as described in Sect.
III.4.3
.
This approach resembles the one suggested by Halperin and Lax
Halperin and Lax 1966
.
In our work, we use a low-pass filter
to compute the spatial electron distribution
n(\mathbf{r},T)

while Halperin and Lax and their successors
focused on the features of the averaged DOS.
The ULF algorithm is equivalent to the LLT inasmuch it replaces the random
potential
V
by an effective potential
W
that
is used for a quasi-classical description of the electron distribution.
However, the ULF scheme of converting
V
into
W
principally
differs from the LLT scheme.
While LLT is based on solving a system of algebraic equations,
the ULF algorithm employs Fast-Fourier Transformation to calculate
the effective potential
W

so that very large systems can be addressed. More importantly, the ULF description leads to an explicit
temperature dependence of the effective potential,
W\equiv W(\mathbf{r},T)
.
On the contrary, the effective potential in LLT does not depend on
T
.
This feature is the main disadvantage of the LLT as compared to the ULF algorithm.
Consequently, the range of applicability for the ULF scheme is much
broader than that for the LLT, as illustrated in Sect.
IV
.
Typically, the ULF description
encompasses the experimentally accessible temperature regime.

In our theoretical consideration, we consider electrons as non-interacting particles. Commonly, electron-electron interactions are taken into account in the self-consistent solution of the Schrödinger equation and Poisson equation. Since we suggest to replace the solution of the Schrödinger equation in search for
n(\mathbf{r},T)
by RWF or ULF techniques, the latter methods should be exploited in a self-cosistent combination with the Poisson equation in order to simulate realistic conditions met in devices.

Our paper is structured as follows. In Sect.
II
,
we develop the RWF algorithm to calculate

n(\mathbf{r},T)
for dilute charge carriers in disordered media.

In Sect.
III
,
we derive the temperature-dependent
effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)
that permits a semi-classical description
of the particle density.
The algorithm employs a universal low-pass filter (ULF) function
and a temperature-dependent shift that we derive and analyze in detail.

The space- and temperature-dependent electron distribution
n(\mathbf{r},T)
is the key
ingredient for the theoretical treatment of charge transport.
In Sect.
IV
, we analyze the results of
different approaches for the particle density and
for the charge carrier mobility in disordered media at elevated temperatures.

Conclusions in Sect.
V
close the main part of our presentation.
Minor technical aspects of our work are deferred to two appendices.

II
Electron density in disordered media

In this section we develop the random-wave-function algorithm
to calculate the electron density in non-degenerate systems at finite temperatures.
We start with definitions in Sect.
II.1
.
Then, in Sect.
II.2
, we express the particle density
as an average over a random combination of eigenstates.
In Sect.
II.3
, we summarize the
main steps of the algorithm.
Finally, in Sect.
II.4
,
we illustrate the algorithm and demonstrate its efficacy
for disordered systems in one, two, and three dimensions.

II.1
Electron density for potential problems

Let us consider a system of non-interacting electrons
governed by a single-particle Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\hat{T}+\hat{V}\;,

(1)

where
\hat{T}
describes their kinetic energy and
\hat{V}

some potential in the specimen volume
\Omega
.
We are interested to calculate
the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
in thermal equilibrium at temperature
T
.
The eigenstates of the Hamiltonian
|\varphi_{i}\rangle
with eigenenergies
\epsilon_{i}
,

\hat{H}|\varphi_{i}\rangle=\epsilon_{i}|\varphi_{i}\rangle

(2)

have the position-space representation
\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})
,

\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})=\langle\mathbf{r}|\varphi_{i}\rangle\;,

(3)

and
|\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}
gives the probability to find the electron at
\mathbf{r}
in
the eigenstate
|\varphi_{i}\rangle
.
Correspondingly, the eigenfunctions are normalized by unity,

\int_{\Omega}|\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}=1

(4)

because the electron can be found somewhere in the specimen.

In the presence of a finite number of electrons
N
in
\Omega
,
the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
at temperature
T
is defined by

n(\mathbf{r},T)=2\sum_{i}|\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}f_{\rm FD}(\epsilon_{i})\;,

(5)

where the factor two accounts for the spin degeneracy and

f_{\rm FD}(\epsilon)
is the equilibrium (Fermi-Dirac)
distribution function,

f_{\rm FD}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}}\;,

(6)

where
\beta=1/(k_{\rm B}T)
with the Boltzmann constant
k_{\rm B}

and
\mu\equiv\mu(T)
is the chemical potential.
It is fixed by the condition

n=\frac{N}{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,n(\mathbf{r},T)\;.

(7)

In most disordered materials, the average electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
is small
so that the electrons’ Fermi statistics can be ignored
(non-degenerate case). Then, the Fermi-Dirac distribution can be replaced by
the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution

f_{\rm MB}(\epsilon)=e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\;.

(8)

In the Maxwell-Boltzmann approximation we can factor out the chemical potential,

n(\mathbf{r},T)=e^{\beta\mu}\;\tilde{n}(\mathbf{r},T)\;,

(9)

where the reduced electron density
\tilde{n}(\mathbf{r},T)
is
determined only by the eigenstates
\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})
,
their eigenenergies
\epsilon_{i}
,
and the temperature,

\tilde{n}(\mathbf{r},T)=2\sum_{i}|\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\exp(-\beta\epsilon_{i})\;.

(10)

We note in passing that the approximation of non-degenerate
electrons does not hold for band-structure theory
in solid-state physics. Therefore, our formalism below cannot be applied, e.g.,
in density-functional theory calculations.

For numerical calculations, we assume that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}

is defined on a regular grid with
L
sites. The volume assigned to a grid node
thus is
\Delta V=\Omega/L
.
A wave function takes complex amplitudes
on nodes of the lattice.
The value
\psi(\mathbf{r})
of some state

|\psi\rangle
at
\mathbf{r}
is obtained from

\psi(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta V}}\,\langle\mathbf{r}_{n}|\psi\rangle\;,

(11)

where
\mathbf{r}
is in the grid-node volume
\Delta V
around the grid point
\mathbf{r}_{n}
.

As a consequence of the discretization, the set of eigenenergies
\{\epsilon_{i}\}

is bounded within a finite range,

\forall i:\epsilon_{\text{min}}\leq\epsilon_{i}\leq\epsilon_{\text{max}}

(12)

with some lower and upper bounds
\epsilon_{\text{min}}

and
\epsilon_{\text{max}}
, respectively.
Generically,
\epsilon_{\text{max}}
is determined by the distance between grid points,
whereas
\epsilon_{\text{min}}
is a defining property of the problem itself.
We make the discretization fine enough,
L\gg 1
, so that we
can safely assume that

|\epsilon_{\text{max}}|\gg|\epsilon_{\text{min}}|\;.

(13)

For simplicity, we assume that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
can be represented
by a real symmetric matrix. To simplify the notations,
we also assume that its eigenfunctions
\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})

are real. The generalization to complex-valued Hamiltonian matrices
and eigenfunctions is straightforward.

II.2
Analysis

Consider a set
\{c_{i}^{(0)}\}
of
L
independent random variables,
one per eigenstate
|\varepsilon_{i}\rangle
.
Each variable
c_{i}^{(0)}

is taken from a normal distribution with expectation value zero and variance unity.
Thus, when we take a large number
N_{\rm R}
of realizations
{\rm R}

of the sets we obtain

\displaystyle\langle c_{i,R}^{(0)}\rangle_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\rm R}}\sum_{R=1}^{N_{\rm R}}c_{i,R}^{(0)}=0\;,

\displaystyle\langle c_{i,R}^{(0)}c_{j,R}^{(0)}\rangle_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\rm R}}\sum_{R=1}^{N_{\rm R}}c_{i,R}^{(0)}c_{j,R}^{(0)}=\delta_{i,j}\;.

(14)

For a given realization
{\rm R}
, we use the random variables
c_{i,R}^{(0)}

as coefficients to construct a wave-function
\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r})

\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{L}c_{i,R}^{(0)}\,\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})\;.

(15)

Obviously, the mean value of these wave-functions over many realizations vanishes,

\langle\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r})\rangle_{\rm R}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\langle c_{i,R}^{(0)}\rangle_{\rm R}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})=0\;.

(16)

For the calculation of the two-point correlation, we assume that
\mathbf{r}_{n}
and
\mathbf{r}_{m}

are grid nodes to write

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}_{n})\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}_{m})\rangle_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\langle c_{i,R}^{(0)}c_{j,R}^{(0)}\rangle_{\rm R}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{n})\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{r}_{m})

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{n})\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta V}\sum_{i}\langle\mathbf{r}_{n}|\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{i}|\mathbf{r}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta V}\langle\mathbf{r}_{n}|\mathbf{r}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1/\Delta V&\text{if $n=m$}\;,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\;.\end{cases}

Here we used eqs. (
11
), (
14
), (
15
),
and the fact that the eigenfunctions
|\varphi_{i}\rangle
form a complete orthonormal set.
Eq. (
17
) shows that
the values of
\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}_{n})
at different grid nodes are independent random
variables that obey a normal distribution with expectation value equal to zero
and variance equal to
1/\Delta V
.

To introduce the temperature into the problem,
we consider the wave function
\psi_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})

that is related to
\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r})
via

|\psi_{\rm R}\rangle=e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\,|\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}\rangle\;.

(18)

We expand it over the eigenfunctions
\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})
,

\psi_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i}c_{i,R}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})\;,

(19)

to find the relation between the two sets of coefficients
c_{i}
and
c_{i}^{(0)}
.
To this end we apply the operator
e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}

to both sides of eq. (
15
)
and take into account that
|\varphi_{i}\rangle
are
eigenstates of the Hamiltonian. This gives

c_{i,R}=c_{i,R}^{(0)}\exp(-\beta\epsilon_{i}/2)\;.

(20)

Using eqs. (
14
) and (
20
), we thus find that

\langle c_{i,R}c_{j,R}\rangle_{\rm R}=\delta_{i,j}\exp(-\beta\epsilon_{i})\;.

(21)

With the help of this correlation, we find the mean value of
|\psi_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\langle|\psi_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\rangle_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\langle c_{i,R}c_{j,R}\rangle_{\rm R}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{r})

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\exp(-\beta\epsilon_{i})|\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\;,

or, comparing with eq. (
10
),

\langle|\psi_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\rangle_{\rm R}=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{n}(\mathbf{r},T)\;.

(23)

Hence, the electron density can be calculated by averaging
the functions
|\psi_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}
over
many realizations
{\rm R}
.
This is the basic idea of the random-wave-function algorithm that we present
in more detail in the next subsection.

Our algorithm requires the application of the exponential
operator
\exp(-\beta\hat{H})

to a given wave function. In practical applications,
this cannot be done exactly. We use an algebraic approximation,

e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\approx(1-\alpha\hat{H})^{M}\;,

(24)

with a natural number
M
equal to

M\approx\frac{\beta}{2\alpha}\;.

(25)

The parameter
\alpha
can be chosen to minimize the residual error.
The action of operator
\exp(-\beta\hat{H}/2)
suppresses
the high-energy components in
|\psi_{\rm R}\rangle
relative to its low-energy ones.
The algebraic approximation on the right-hand side
of eq. (
24
) must have a similar effect,
i.e., the factor
(1-\alpha\epsilon_{\text{max}})^{M}

has to be small with respect to
(1-\alpha\epsilon_{\text{min}})^{M}

in absolute values, thence

|1-\alpha\epsilon_{\text{max}}|<|1-\alpha\epsilon_{\text{min}}|

(26)

must hold. The term
\alpha\epsilon_{\text{min}}
can be neglected
because
|\epsilon_{\text{min}}|\ll|\epsilon_{\text{max}}|

due to eq. (
13
).
Then, the inequality (
26
) reduces to

-1<1-\alpha\epsilon_{\text{max}}<1
.
We note that
\epsilon_{\text{max}}>0
because this quantity is determined mainly
by the maximal kinetic energy in a discretized system. Therefore,
we obtain the following constraints on
\alpha
,

0<\alpha<\frac{2}{\epsilon_{\text{max}}}\;.

(27)

On the other hand, a good approximation in eq. (
24
)
requires that the number
M
should be large.
Using eq. (
25
) and recalling
that
\beta=1/(k_{\rm B}T)
, we can rewrite the condition
M\gg 1

in the form

\alpha\ll\frac{1}{k_{\rm B}T}\;.

(28)

Usually
k_{\rm B}T\ll\epsilon_{\text{max}}
,
and, as a consequence, the stronger condition (
27
)
overrides the weaker condition (
28
).

II.3
Synthesis: the random-wave-function algorithm

The considerations presented in the previous subsection
suggest the following random-wave-function algorithm
for calculating the electron
density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
in non-degenerate non-interacting electron systems.

S1

For each grid node
\mathbf{r}_{n}
, choose a random number
\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}_{n})

from a normal distribution with expectation value zero and variance
1/\Delta V
.
All these random numbers are chosen independently,
so that the function
\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r})

itself represents a sample of Gaussian white noise.
The set of all numbers constitutes the realization
{\rm R}
.

S2

Apply the following transformation
M
times,

\underline{\psi}^{(m)}=\underline{\psi}^{(m-1)}-\alpha\underline{\underline{H}}\,\underline{\psi}^{(m-1)}

(29)

with
m=1,2,\ldots,M
. Here,
\underline{\psi}^{(m)}
is the vector that
represents the wave function on the grid, and

\underline{\underline{H}}
is the matrix representation of the Hamiltonian
on the grid. Recall that
M
is given by eq. (
25
), and
\alpha
obeys
the inequalities (
27
). In our calculations we find that

\alpha=\frac{1.5}{\epsilon_{\text{max}}}

(30)

is a suitable choice.

S3

Calculate an estimate of the reduced electron density
\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r},T)
as

\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r}_{n},T)=2\,|\psi^{(M)}(\mathbf{r}_{n})|^{2}

(31)

on each grid point
\mathbf{r}_{n}
.

The steps S1–S3 are carried out for a large number
N_{\rm R}
of
realizations
{\rm R}
of
\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r})
.
Then, the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
is the arithmetic mean
of the functions
\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})
obtained for different realizations R,
multiplied by a chemical-potential-related factor
e^{\beta\mu}
,

n(\mathbf{r},T)=e^{\beta\mu}\langle\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r},T)\rangle_{\rm R}\;.

(32)

The larger the number of realizations
N_{\rm R}
,
the more accurate is the calculated electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
.
A convenient measure of the accuracy is the relative error
\Delta(\mathbf{r},T)
,

\Delta(\mathbf{r},T)=\frac{n^{\text{rwf}}(\mathbf{r},T)-n^{\text{exact}}(\mathbf{r},T)}{n^{\text{exact}}(\mathbf{r},T)}\;,

(33)

where
n^{\text{rwf}}(\mathbf{r},T)

is calculated using the random-wave-function algorithm,
and the exact value
n^{\text{exact}}(\mathbf{r},T)

is defined by eqs. (
9
) and (
10
).
As we show in Appendix
A
,
the expectation value of
\Delta^{2}
is equal to

\left\langle[\Delta(\mathbf{r},T)]^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{2}{N_{\rm R}}\;,

(34)

i.e., the typical deviation from the exact result is not larger than

\sqrt{1/N_{\rm R}}
.

To calculate the mean value in eq. (
32
),
it is necessary to store the cumulative function

\sum_{\rm R}\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})

(35)

in the memory, and to update it after processing of each realization
{\rm R}
.

It is easy to estimate the resources of memory and computation time (or number
of operations) demanded by the random-wave-function algorithm.
It is enough to store in the memory
4L
real numbers, namely,

L
entries for the potential energy in the Hamiltonian,

2L
numbers for the wave-functions

\underline{\psi}^{(m)}
and
\underline{\psi}^{(m-1)}

at Step S2, and
L
values for the sum (
35
).
Almost all computation time is spent at Step S2.
It takes
{\cal A}\simeq LMN_{\rm R}
operations.
Indeed, applying the Hamiltonian
to a wave-function costs
{\cal O}(L)
operations due to the fact
that the potential is local and the kinetic energy is a local second derivative.
Applying the Hamiltonian repeats
M
times
for each of
N_{\rm R}
realizations.
Let us further express the quantities
L
,
M
and
N_{\rm R}

via the sample volume
\Omega
, the distance between grid points
a
,
and the desired relative error
\Delta
using eqs. (
25
),
(
30
) and (
34
),

L\simeq\frac{\Omega}{a^{d}}\,,\quad M\simeq\frac{\epsilon_{\text{max}}}{k_{B}T}\simeq\frac{\hbar^{2}}{ma^{2}k_{B}T}\,,\quad N_{\rm R}\simeq\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}}

(36)

in a
d
-dimensional system. Therefore,
the estimate for the number of arithmetical operations becomes

{\cal A}\simeq LMN_{\rm R}\simeq\frac{\hbar^{2}}{mk_{B}T}\;\frac{\Omega}{a^{d+2}\Delta^{2}}

(37)

when expressed in terms of physical quantities.
It is important to note that our algorithm is easily parallelized because
different realizations
{\rm R}
of
the wave-function
\psi_{\rm R}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r})
can be processed
completely independently.

II.4
Example: electrons in a random potential

In this subsection
we demonstrate how to use the random-wave-function algorithm.

II.4.1
White-noise potential and dimensionless units

To be definite, we consider a non-interacting electron gas in
a one-dimensional (1D), a two-dimensional (2D), and a three-dimensional (3D)
white-noise random potential. Such a potential
V(\mathbf{r})
is characterized by
the statistical properties

\langle V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})\rangle_{\rm R}=0,\qquad\langle V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})\,V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle_{\rm R}=S\,\delta(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;,

(38)

when the average is taken over many realization
{\rm R}
of the random
potential
V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})
. The strength of the disorder is characterized
by the parameter
S
.
The actual value of
S
depends on the origin of the random potential.
For instance, compositional disorder in a semiconductor
alloy
A_{\bar{x}}B_{1-\bar{x}}
leads to
Raikh et al. 1990

S=\frac{[\alpha(\bar{x})]^{2}\bar{x}(1-\bar{x})}{N}\,,

(39)

where
N
is the concentration of atoms
and
\alpha_{c}(\bar{x})=\mathrm{d}E_{c}(\bar{x})/\mathrm{d}\bar{x}
for electrons,

\alpha_{v}(\bar{x})=\mathrm{d}E_{v}(\bar{x})/\mathrm{d}\bar{x}
for holes
as a function of the band edge positions
E_{c,v}(\bar{x})
.

We discretize this system using a regular grid of points, equally spaced along
each dimension. This is a quadratic grid in 2D,
and a simple cubic grid in 3D.
The distance between neighboring grid points is
a
.
To discretize the single-particle Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
,
we use the simplest difference scheme for the Laplacian operator,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm R}\psi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\sum_{s=1}^{d}\frac{\psi(\mathbf{r}-a\mathbf{e}_{s})+\psi(\mathbf{r}+a\mathbf{e}_{s})-2\psi(\mathbf{r})}{a^{2}}

(40)

\displaystyle+V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})\psi(\mathbf{r})\;,

where
\mathbf{r}\equiv\mathbf{r}_{n}
is a grid point,
and
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d}
are unit vectors along the coordinate axes.
The realization
V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r}_{n})
of the random potential at the grid points are chosen
as independent random numbers, uniformly distributed within the range

-\sqrt{\frac{3S}{a^{d}}}<V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r}_{n})<\sqrt{\frac{3S}{a^{d}}}\;.

(41)

One can easily see that this distribution satisfies
the properties (
38
).
Indeed, the discretization converts the delta-function,

\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\to\begin{cases}1/a^{d}&\text{ if }\mathbf{r}\equiv\mathbf{r}_{n}=\mathbf{r}_{m}\equiv\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\;,\\
0&\text{ if }\mathbf{r}_{n}\neq\mathbf{r}_{m}\;,\end{cases}

(42)

and therefore eq. (
38
) takes the form

\langle V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r}_{n})\rangle_{\rm R}=0
,

\langle[V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r}_{n})]^{2}\rangle_{\rm R}=S/a^{d}
, and

\langle V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r}_{n})\,V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r}_{m})\rangle_{\rm R}=0
for
\mathbf{r}_{n}\neq\mathbf{r}_{m}
.
The uniform distribution (
41
) meets these criteria.

It is convenient to work with dimensionless quantities. We note that
it is always possible to fix the values of the Planck constant
\hbar
,
the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
, the effective mass
m
,
and the disorder strength
S
by a proper choice of physical units.
So we set all these quantities to unity,

\hbar=k_{B}=m=S=1\;.

(43)

To do this, we choose a physical unit of length

\ell_{0}=\left(\frac{\hbar^{4}}{m^{2}S}\right)^{1/(4-d)}\;,

(44)

and the corresponding unit of energy

\varepsilon_{0}=\left(\frac{m^{d}S^{2}}{\hbar^{2d}}\right)^{1/(4-d)}\;.

(45)

Then, the unit of time is
t_{0}=\hbar/\varepsilon_{0}
, and the unit of temperature
is
T_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}/k_{\rm B}
.
As an example, we mention that in a 2D system with compositional disorder,
for
\alpha=0.5\,\text{eV}
,

N=10^{15}\,\text{cm}^{-2}
,
x=0.5
, and
m=0.1\,m_{0}
,
the units of length, energy, and temperature are equal to

\ell_{0}=9.6\,\text{nm}
,
\varepsilon_{0}=8.3\,\text{meV}
, and

T_{0}=96\,\text{K}
, respectively.

In the rest of this section, the physical units are chosen such
that the equalities (
43
) hold.
The dimensionless value of the discretization step
is set to
a=0.1\ell_{0}
,
and the dimensionless value of temperature is set to
T=T_{0}\equiv 1
,
unless stated otherwise.
Periodical boundary conditions apply.

III
Effective potential

In this section we relate the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
to a (quasi-)classical,
temperature-dependent effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)
for two reasons.
First, the random-wave-function algorithm requires
matrix multiplications with (sparse)
L\times L
matrices where

L
is the number of grid points of the sample, and we are interested to find
a numerical method that scales more favorably in the system size.
Second,
W(\mathbf{r},T)
visualizes the potential landscape
in which classical particles would move so that concepts
like percolation theory can be applied.

III.1
Quasi-classical effective potential

We start from the well-known expression for the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)

of a non-degenerate electron gas, e.g.,
in the conduction band of a semiconductor,

n(T)=N_{c}\exp\left(\frac{\mu-V}{k_{\rm B}T}\right)\quad\text{(constant potential)}\;,

(46)

where
V
is the energy of the conduction band edge, i.e.,
the electrons’ potential energy, and
N_{c}
is the effective density of states,

N_{c}=2\left(\frac{mk_{\rm B}T}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{d/2}

(47)

in
d
dimensions. The relation (
46
)
remains applicable
also in the case of a coordinate-dependent potential
V(\mathbf{r})

if the potential is smooth on the scale of the de-Broglie wavelength,

n(\mathbf{r},T)=N_{c}\exp\left[\frac{\mu-V(\mathbf{r})}{k_{\rm B}T}\right]\quad\text{(smooth potential)}\;.

(48)

In other words, electrons near the position
\mathbf{r}
feel only the
local value
V(\mathbf{r})

of the potential. This approximation corresponds to the quasi-classical
description
of electron motion in a smooth potential
V(\mathbf{r})
.
However, eq. (
48
) cannot be applied when
the potential fluctuates significantly on length scales less or
equal to the de-Broglie wavelength. Since the particles are strongly
scattered by the
potential, the quasi-classical picture of a compact wave packet breaks down.

As a result of the quantum-mechanical treatment,
the electron density at some point
\mathbf{r}
is affected by the potential not only at
\mathbf{r}

but also in some extended vicinity. Therefore,
the electron density is a much smoother function of coordinates than the potential.
In this sense one might argue that the electron gas experiences
a ‘smoothed’ potential. Therefore, instead of eq. (
48
),
one might expect that the electron density obeys

n(\mathbf{r},T)=N_{c}\exp\left[\frac{\mu-W(\mathbf{r},T)}{k_{\rm B}T}\right]\;,

(49)

where
W(\mathbf{r},T)
is the quasi-classical effective potential
in the case of a strongly fluctuating potential.
This potential is obtained from the actual potential
V(\mathbf{r})
by some appropriate
operation of ‘smoothing’.

We will consider eq. (
49
) as the very
definition

of the quasi-classical effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)
. Hence,

W(\mathbf{r},T)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mu(T)-k_{\rm B}T\ln\frac{n(\mathbf{r},T)}{N_{c}}\;.

(50)

It is important to note that the
effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)
also depends on temperature.
This temperature dependence
is demonstrated numerically in Sect.
III.3

for the example of a white-noise potential.
For this reason, the effective potential introduced here
differs from that of the Localization-Landscape Theory
where the effective potential
W_{\rm LLT}(\mathbf{r})
is independent of
T
.

III.2
Linear low-pass filter

To simplify the notation, in this subsection we drop
the temperature-dependence of all quantities.
Let us consider the
response
\delta n(\mathbf{r})
of the electron gas density
to a variation
\delta V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
of the potential. To first order in
\delta V
,

\delta n(\mathbf{r})=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\Gamma_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,\delta V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})+{\cal O}(\delta V^{2})\;,

(51)

where the linear-response function
\Gamma_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

can be defined as a functional derivative,

\Gamma_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{\delta n(\mathbf{r})}{\delta V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\;.

(52)

Likewise, one can define a linear-response function
\Gamma_{W}

for the effective potential
W
,

\delta W(\mathbf{r})=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\Gamma_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,\delta V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})+{\cal O}(\delta V^{2})\;,

(53)

or, equivalently, as a functional derivative,

\Gamma_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{\delta W(\mathbf{r})}{\delta V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\;.

(54)

The relation between the response functions
\Gamma_{n}
and
\Gamma_{W}

can be readily found using the chain rule and eq. (
50
),

\Gamma_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{\mathrm{d}W(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{d}n(\mathbf{r})}\;\frac{\delta n(\mathbf{r})}{\delta V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}=-\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{n(\mathbf{r})}\,\Gamma_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;.

(55)

In Section
III.4
we show how to evaluate these response
functions with the help of perturbation theory.

The function
\Gamma_{W}
has a remarkable property: it is normalized to unity,

\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\Gamma_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=1\;.

(56)

To see this, we insert a coordinate-independent variation

\delta V(\mathbf{r})=\text{const}=\delta V

into eq. (
53
) and obtain

\delta W(\mathbf{r})=\delta V\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\Gamma_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})+{\cal O}(\delta V^{2})\;.

(57)

On the other hand, adding a constant
\delta V
to the potential gives rise to the
same shift of all electron energy levels
\epsilon_{i}
. The electron density therefore
is changed by a factor of
\exp[-\delta V/(k_{\rm B}T)]
. According
to definition (
50
), the effective potential
W(\mathbf{r})

acquires the constant shift

\delta W(\mathbf{r})=\delta V\;.

(58)

The normalization condition (
56
) then follows from
the comparison of eqs. (
57
)
and (
58
).

Since the function
\Gamma_{W}
is properly normalized, it may play
the role of a filter function that produces the effective potential
W(\mathbf{r})

from the actual potential
V(\mathbf{r})
by the operation of convolution. However,
a filter function must depend on the difference
\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
only, not on
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

separately. For a random potential
V(\mathbf{r})
this is not the case because
the right-hand
side of eq. (
54
) depends on this potential
which breaks translational invariance.
Nevertheless, in a statistical sense we may argue
that the response function
\Gamma_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
that applies for a typical realization
of the disorder potential
can be approximated by some translationally invariant function,

\Gamma_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Gamma(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;,

(59)

or, implying that the system also is isotropic in a statistical sense,

\Gamma_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Gamma(|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)\;.

(60)

Due to translational invariance, the function
\Gamma
does not depend on the
choice of the realization of the random potential. One can therefore integrate
the differential relation (
53
)
between two random realizations
V_{1}(\mathbf{r})
and
V_{2}(\mathbf{r})
which yields

U_{1}(\mathbf{r})=U_{2}(\mathbf{r})
where

U_{i}(\mathbf{r})=W_{i}(\mathbf{r})-\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\Gamma(|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)\,V_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;,

(61)

that relates the effective potentials
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
to the actual potentials

V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, respectively. Consequently,
U_{i}(\mathbf{r})\equiv C
,
whereby the constant
C
does not depend on the choice of the realization.
In sum, the hypothesis (
60
)
gives rise to the following relation between the actual potential
V(\mathbf{r})

and the effective potential
W(\mathbf{r})
,

W(\mathbf{r})=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\Gamma(|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)\,V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})+C\;.

(62)

In words,
the effective potential

W
is a convolution of the real potential

V
with the filter function
\Gamma
, plus some constant
C
.
Note that the convolution with
\Gamma
is nothing else but passing
the original potential through
the linear low-pass filter defined by
\Gamma
. For this reason,
the effective potential
W(\mathbf{r})
becomes much smoother
than the original potential
V(\mathbf{r})
.
Since the effective potential depends also on the temperature, the filter function

\Gamma
and
C
are temperature-dependent as well,

\Gamma\equiv\Gamma(|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,T)
and
C\equiv C(T)
.

In Section
III.3
, we test eq. (
62
)
numerically for the case of a white-noise random potential,
and ensure that it works surprisingly well, except for very low temperatures.
In Section
III.4
, we show that the
filter function
\Gamma
can approximately be reduced to some universal
function
\Gamma_{\text{uni}}
by the simple scaling relation

\Gamma(r)\approx\lambdabar^{-d}\,\Gamma_{\text{uni}}(r/\lambdabar)\;,

(63)

where
d
is the spatial dimension, and
\lambdabar

is the reduced de-Broglie wavelength of a charge carrier of mass
m
and
kinetic energy
k_{\rm B}T
,

\lambdabar=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2mk_{\rm B}T}}\;,

(64)

which is the thermal wave length up to a numerical factor.

Numerical study of a white-noise potential

,

For each of these samples, we calculate the reduced electron density

In 3D, the Hamiltonian matrix is too large (

)

).
Since we have a collection of random potentials

and effective potentials
W_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r},T)
,
we can test the validity of the filter function approach,
which is expressed mathematically by eq. (
62
).

Before we apply the filter function approach,
some preliminary steps must be carried out.
First, we need to determine the value of the parameter
C

in eq. (
62
).
Second, it is necessary to estimate the characteristic range
of the filter function
\Gamma(r)
. Third,
we have to determine the optimal shape of this filter function.

III.3.1
Constant parameter

To evaluate the parameter
C
, we note

in appendix

The effective potential

The contribution proportional to

III.3.2
Characteristic scale

To determine the characteristic scale

of the filter function

Sect.

and get the following estimate
similar to eq. (

(69)

where the symbol ‘var’ denotes the variance, the square of the
standard deviation.
We show the variance of

in Fig.

Our numerical data clearly demonstrate that
\text{var}\,W\sim T^{d/2}
.
Inserting this dependence into eq. (

that

This temperature dependence is the same as that of the thermal

Specifically,

III.3.3
Shape of the low-pass filter

To obtain the optimal shape of the low-pass filter
\Gamma(r)
,
we use the following variational scheme.
As a set of fitting parameters, we choose
\Gamma
at
N\simeq 10

points
r_{0}=0,r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}=2/\sqrt{T}
.
The whole function
\Gamma(r)
is restored from the values

\Gamma(r_{0}),\ldots,\Gamma(r_{N})
using a cubic spline interpolation.
Two restrictions are imposed to the fitting parameters:
(i),
\Gamma(r_{N})
is fixed to zero and, (ii), the normalization
integral
\int\Gamma(|\mathbf{r}|)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}
is fixed to unity.
The values
\Gamma(r_{0}),\ldots,\Gamma(r_{N-1})
are varied
until the minimum of the relative error of the effective potential

\mathfrak{E}[\Gamma]=\frac{\sqrt{\langle(W_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{r})-W)^{2}\rangle}}{\sqrt{\text{var}\,W}}

(71)

is reached. Here,
W
is the effective potential calculated
from the electron density using eq. (
65
),
and
W_{\Gamma}
is calculated from the potential
V
and the filter function

\Gamma
using eq. (
62
). Here, the angular brackets imply
spatial integration.

In the low-temperature limit, the electron density is dominated by contributions
of rare low-energy eigenstates.
Therefore, the shape of the electron density distribution

For this reason, the electron density distribution at very low temperatures

and the filter-function approach becomes less reliable.
On the other hand, for high temperatures the role of the fluctuating potential
becomes negligible, the system under study acquires translational
and rotational symmetry. For high temperatures,

as explained in Sect.

III.4
Universal filter function

As seen in Sect.
III.3
, the low-pass filter
representation
of the effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)
becomes more accurate
for higher temperatures.
Therefore, it is reasonable to search for a universal filter function
in the limit of infinitely
high temperature. This limit is characterized by neglecting
the random potential altogether.
Indeed, the high-temperature limit with a constant strength
of the random potential is equivalent to the limit of infinitely small strength
of the potential at constant temperature. For this reason, we develop
the universal low-pass filter (ULF) function in the case of zero potential where
the single-electron Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
reduces to the
kinetic energy operator
\hat{T}
.

for the response function
\Gamma_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
in terms of the matrix elements

perturbation theory.
Then, we consider the case of zero potential,
\hat{H}=\hat{T}

and find analytical expressions for the response functions
\Gamma_{n}

and
\Gamma_{W}

The latter plays the role of the filter function. Finally, we determine
an expression for the universal dimensionless

\Gamma_{\text{uni}}

its accuracy at finite temperatures and disorder strengths.

III.4.1
Linear response

We start from expressions (
9

and (

the electron density

in a non-interacting,
non-degenerate electron gas.
They can be rewritten in operator notation as

n(\mathbf{r},T)=2e^{\beta\mu}\langle\mathbf{r}|e^{-\beta\hat{H}}|\mathbf{r}\rangle\;,

(72)

where
\hat{H}
is the single-electron Hamiltonian, and the
position-space states
|\mathbf{r}\rangle

are normalized such that

\langle\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\rangle=\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;.

(73)

We are interested

\delta V(\mathbf{r})

The potential variation can be
represented as a small addition
\widehat{\delta V}

\widehat{\delta V}=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\delta V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,|\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\rangle\langle\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|\;.

(75)

The difference in square brackets can be evaluated via first-order
perturbation theory. Neglecting higher-order infinitesimals we find

e^{-\beta(\hat{H}+\widehat{\delta V})}-e^{-\beta\hat{H}}=-\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi\,e^{-\xi\hat{H}}\,\widehat{\delta V}\,e^{-(\beta-\xi)\hat{H}}\;.

(76)

Inserting eqs. (
74

into eq. (

\displaystyle\delta n(\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2e^{\beta\mu}\int\limits_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\langle\mathbf{r}|e^{-\xi\hat{H}}|\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\rangle

(77)

\displaystyle\hphantom{-2e^{\beta\mu} \int\limits_0^\beta\mathrm{d}\xi\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}' }\times\delta V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\langle\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|e^{-(\beta-\xi)\hat{H}}|\mathbf{r}\rangle\;.

A comparison between the eq. (

(78)

within first-order perturbation theory.

III.4.3
Effective potentials using a universal low-pass filter

Using a universal low-pass filter (ULF) provides a practical numerical method
for the
calculation of the electron density distribution
n(\mathbf{r},T)

for a given realization of the white-noise potential
V_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})
.
The method consists of the following simple steps.

(1)

Calculate the Fourier image
\hat{V}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{k})

of the random potential by means of a fast-Fourier-transform (FFT) algorithm.

(2)

Multiply the function
\hat{V}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{k})
by
\hat{\Gamma}(|\mathbf{k}|)
,
where
\hat{\Gamma}(|\mathbf{k}|)
is given by eqs. (
64
),
(
87
), and (
89
).

(3)

Perform the inverse Fourier transform of the product

\hat{V}(\mathbf{k})\hat{\Gamma}(|\mathbf{k}|)
by FFT, and add the constant
C(T)

to the result, see eq. (
67
).
The output of the third step is the effective potential
W_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r},T)

which follows from eq. (
62
) and from the fact that
inverse Fourier transform converts a product into a convolution.

(4)

Calculate the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
from the effective
potential
W_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r},T)

using eq. (
49
).

Since the numerical effort in FFT scales proportional to
L\ln(L)
,
the effective potential

W(\mathbf{r},T)
and the particle density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
can be calculated for
mesoscopically large systems from a microscopic random potential
V(\mathbf{r})
.

n=0.01
and
work with a single particle species,
the effect of the constant
C(T)
can be absorbed in the chemical potential,

\tilde{\mu}(T)\equiv\mu(T)-C(T)
.
As can be seen from Fig.
13
,
the exact result (upper plot) is accurately reproduced
by the calculation based on the universal filter function (lower plot).

Figure
14
shows an application for a 3D system
with a white-noise potential at
T=2
. The cross section of
n(x,y,z,T)

at
z=0
is plotted. The upper plot depicts the electron density distribution

n(x,y,z,T)
obtained by the random-wave-function algorithm with
relative accuracy 5%.
The lower plot depicts the result obtained by the universal filter function.
The agreement between the two plots is good.

At first glance, these results are quite surprising because
the ULF method is based on perturbation theory.
In particular, the expression (
85
)
for the universal filter function
\Gamma_{\text{uni}}(r)
is obtained
for high temperatures or small potential fluctuations.
One can expect that the perturbative approach must be restricted
to parameter sets that correspond to small density fluctuations.
However, it is seen that the approach works well even when
the density varies by more than an order of magnitude.

We surmise that the surprising success of the effective potential
in describing the local particle density at fairly low temperatures,
T\gtrsim 0.2T_{0}
,
is related to the fact that
n(\mathbf{r},T)
depends on
W(\mathbf{r},T)
exponentially.
As in statistical physics, the error
appears to be proportional to higher-order cumulants in the expansion in
V(\mathbf{r})/T
.
Cumulants often decay faster with temperature than the corresponding
coefficients of the bare series expansion because
n
th-order cumulants
describe true
n
-point correlations that cannot be factorized into
smaller subclusters.

IV
Comparison with Localization-Landscape Theory (LLT)

In this section, we compare our results to those obtained from the
Localization-Landscape Theory (LLT) for the charge carrier concentration
n(\mathbf{r},T)
.
In addition, we discuss the consequences of the different
approximate techniques of calculating
n(\mathbf{r},T)

for charge transport in disordered media at elevated temperatures.

The aim of the potential filtering with the universal low-pass filter (ULF) developed
in Sect.
III
and that of the random-wave-function (RWF)
approach developed in Sect.
II
is to speed up calculations
compared to solving the Schrödinger equation.
The same aim has been targeted in the recently developed
LLT
Arnold et al. 2016
;
Filoche et al. 2017
;
Piccardo et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Weisbuch et al. 2021
.

IV.1
Localization-Landscape Theory

In the LLT, the right-hand side of the Schrödinger equation
is replaced with a constant to arrive at the landscape equation

\hat{H}u(\mathbf{r})=\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}+V(\mathbf{r})\right)u(\mathbf{r})=1\;,

(90)

where
V(\mathbf{r})
is the disorder potential and
u(\mathbf{r})
is
localization landscape for appropriate boundary conditions.
The inverse of the landscape,
W(\mathbf{r})\equiv 1/u(\mathbf{r})
,
can be interpreted as a semi-classical effective confining potential
that determines the strength of the confinement
as well as the long-range decay of the quantum states.

IV.1.1
Positivity condition

An important condition for the applicability of the LLT is that
the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
is a positive operator
Filoche et al. 2017
.
When solving the Schrödinger equation (
2
),
the potential
V(\mathbf{r})

experienced by the quantum particle can be defined up to a
constant
K
. If one shifts the potential by
K
, then the
resulting eigenenergies
\epsilon_{i}
are shifted by the same constant
K
.
However, this invariance does not hold for the landscape
u(\mathbf{r})
. If

u(\mathbf{r})
is the solution to eq. (
90
),
then the solution
u_{K}(\mathbf{r})
corresponding to the same potential shifted by
the constant
K
satisfies
Filoche et al. 2017

-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta u_{K}(\mathbf{r})+\left[V(\mathbf{r})+K\right]u_{K}(\mathbf{r})=1\;,

(91)

and
u(\mathbf{r})\neq u_{K}(\mathbf{r})
for general
K\neq 0
.
In the LLT, the constant
K
is chosen as small as possible
in such a way that the Hamiltonian
remains a positive operator
Filoche et al. 2017
, and the
electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
should be obtained from the effective potential

W_{K}(\mathbf{r})\equiv 1/u_{K}(\mathbf{r})
via eq. (
49
).

Below we discuss that the choice of
K
drastically affects
the predictions of the LLT approach. In the calculations below, we choose

K=-1.05V_{\rm min}
, where
V_{\rm min}
is the absolute minimum of
V(\mathbf{r})
.

IV.1.2
Computational effort

The exact, complete solution of the Schrödinger equation is computationally
demanding for large systems, and approximate techniques are mandatory
for random media in two and three dimensions.
The RWF requires the repeated application of the Hamiltonian onto a random
wave function. The time consumption of the latter approach depends on the system
size and on the number of realization used for the averaging procedure.
Therefore, the accuracy of the RWF approach can be systematically improved
by increasing the number of stored wave functions
and realizations of the impurity potentials for the price of increasing
computational resources. Thus, mesoscopically large systems cannot be
treated using the RWF.

The LLT is based on a solution of a system of linear equations.
Compared to solving the Schrödinger equation
self-consistently,
the LLT speeds up the calculations by two to three orders
of magnitude
Liu et al. 2022
.
However, the ULF approach requires only Fast Fourier Transformation, which
does not consume noticeable computational time.
Below we compare the accuracy of all
three approximate approaches
in
d=1
and
d=2
and show that the ULF is superior to the LLT
not only in speed but also in precision.

IV.3.1
Transport in one dimension

First, we address the consequences of the various approaches

\rho(x,T)=\frac{1}{e\mu_{0}n(x,T)}\;.

(92)

In one dimension, the macroscopic mobility follows

V
Discussion and conclusions

The theoretical description of the opto-electronic properties of disordered media
requires an accurate knowledge of the space-dependent and temperature-dependent
charge carrier distribution
n(\mathbf{r},T)
in the presence of a random potential.
Two methods are currently available to determine
n(\mathbf{r},T)
:
(i) solving the Schrödinger equation
and (ii) utilizing the localization
landscape theory (LLT)
Arnold et al. 2016
;
Filoche et al. 2017
;
Piccardo et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2017
.
While exact, the complete solution of the Schrödinger equation is extremely demanding
with respect to computational time and computer memory.
It is hardly affordable for applications to realistically large, chemically complex systems.
As exemplified in Sect.
IV
, the LLT
is valid only in certain cases with unspecific limits.
These limits can be revealed only by comparison with the exact solution
of the Schrödinger equation for particles in a random potential
V(\mathbf{r})
.

In this work, we propose two novel theoretical tools, the random-wave-function
(RWF) algorithm and the universal low-pass filter (ULF) approach,
that permit an approximate calculation of
n(\mathbf{r},T)

without solving the Schrödinger equation.
In comparison, both methods require less computational resources than
the complete solution of the Schrödinger equation, and
both have a better accuracy and a broader range of applicability than the LLT.

As shown in this work, the RWF approach is as accurate and generally applicable to
non-degenerate electron systems as solving the Schrödinger equation.
However, for practical applications at temperatures
T\gtrsim 0.1T_{0}
,
the RWF is much less demanding
with respect to computational resources than solving the Schrödinger problem.
Nevertheless, even the RWF becomes computationally too costly for
mesoscopically large three-dimensional systems at low temperatures.
For example, the calculations of
n(\mathbf{r},T)
depicted in Fig.
14

consumed 1.5 hours on a PC in the case of the RWF approach
but only 0.15 seconds in the case of the ULF scheme.

The ULF approach employs the temperature-dependent effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)
.
In this respect it is similar to the LLT, which also relies on a quasi-classical potential
that replaces the random potential
V(\mathbf{r})
. However,
the effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)
in the ULF approach depends on temperature,
while the effective potential in LLT is temperature-independent.
In Figs.
7
and
9
, it is clearly seen that the effective potential responsible
for the distribution
n(\mathbf{r},T)
strongly depends on temperature to reproduce
the exact solution. Sect.
IV

provides evidence that ignoring the
T
-dependence of the effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)

prevents an accurate calculation of the electron distribution
n(\mathbf{r},T)
.
In the ULF, a universal low-pass filter is applied to the potential
V(\mathbf{r})

via eq. (
62
). We derive this linear filter analytically
using high-temperature perturbation theory.
The filter is
T
-dependent, while
T
does not enter Eq. (
90
) of the LLT.

The numerical tests performed in Sect.
IV
show
that the range of applicability for the ULF approach is much broader
than that for the LLT. Furthermore, the ULF scheme requires only
Fast Fourier Transformation which is computationally much less demanding
than solving the LLT eq. (
90
) so that mesoscopically large three-dimensional
disordered systems appear within reach for temperatures
T\gtrsim 0.5T_{0}
.

The space- and temperature-dependent electron distribution
n(\mathbf{r},T)

is the key ingredient for the theoretical treatment of charge transport. In our work
it is shown that RWF and ULF provide a solid basis for the theoretical treatment
of charge transport in disordered media.

In this paper, the calculation of the equilibrium carrier density in the Boltzmann approximation is considered for a disordered potential as a function of temperature. However, in practice, there are many problems where the carrier distribution at low temperature is not in equilibrium. Although neither RWF, nor ULF can be applied to non-equilibrium problems straightforwardly, there is a perspective to possible modification of RWF in order to make it applicable to non-equilibrium cases. If the number of realizations
N_{R}
decreases, the calculated occupation of energy levels by electrons gradually deviates from their equilibrium values. In principle, these deviations may mimic the non-equilibrium distributions, and one could interpret the green dashed lines in Figs.
1
and
4
as corresponding to the non-equilibrium electron densities. However, two problems arise in such interpretation of the calculated results. First, the deviations from the equilibrium occupation should be more pronounced for the low-energy states than for the high-energy levels. Some modifications of RWF should be performed to fulfill this condition. Second, the occupations of energy levels in RWF are described by
\chi^{2}
distribution, while in reality another statistics holds. For instance, the occupations should obey Poisson statistics in the approximation of non-interacting electrons. More research is necessary to overcome these obstacles.

sum is equal to the sum of variances of independent variables.

Then we recall that
\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})
is a square of random quantity

\gamma_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})\equiv\sqrt{2}\psi_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})
according to
eq. (
23
). The latter quantity is, by construction,
a linear combination of Gaussian random variables
c_{i,R}^{(0)}
,
and therefore has a Gaussian distribution with expected value zero.
The expected value of
\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})
is the second moment of

\gamma_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})
, and the expected value of
[\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})]^{2}

is the fourth moment of
\gamma_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})
. Due to Wick’s probability
theorem, the fourth moment of a normally distributed random variable is
three times larger than the square of its second moment. Hence,

\left\langle[\tilde{n}_{\rm R}(\mathbf{r})]^{2}\right\rangle=3[\tilde{n}(\mathbf{r})]^{2}.

(103)

Finally, substitution of eq. (
103
)
into eq. (
102
) gives

\left\langle[\Delta(\mathbf{r})]^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{2}{N_{\rm R}}\;.

(104)

Appendix B
Constant parameter at small discretization

In this appendix we show how to derive the scaling form (
67
)
for the constant parameter
C(T)
.

We start from eq. (
49
)
that expresses the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
through the effective potential
W(\mathbf{r},T)
,

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{c}\,e^{\beta[\mu-W(\mathbf{r},T)]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{c}e^{\beta\mu}\left[1-\beta W(\mathbf{r},T)+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}W^{2}(\mathbf{r},T)-\ldots\right]\;.

Since we consider the potential
V(\mathbf{r})
as a small perturbation, we
expand
n(\mathbf{r},T)
and
W(\mathbf{r},T)
in a power series,

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{0}(T)+n_{1}(\mathbf{r},T)+n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)+\mathcal{O}(V^{3})\;,\quad

(106)

\displaystyle W(\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mathbf{r},T)+W_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)+\mathcal{O}(V^{3})\;,\quad

(107)

where
n_{0}(T)=N_{c}\exp(\beta\mu)
. Here,

n_{1}(\mathbf{r},T)
and
W_{1}(\mathbf{r},T)
are linear in
V
,
and
n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)
and
W_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)
are quadratic in
V
.
Substituting eq. (
106
)
into eq. (
), and collecting the terms up to quadratic order
in
V
, we obtain

n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)=N_{c}\,e^{\beta\mu}\left[\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}W_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{r},T)-\beta W_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)\right]\;,

(108)

where the filter function defines the linear relation between the random potential
and the effective potential,

W_{1}(\mathbf{r},T)=\int{\rm d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\Gamma(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},T)V(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;,

(109)

see eqs. (
62
) and (
).

Eq. (
66
) shows that
C(T)=\langle W(\mathbf{r})\rangle-\langle V(\mathbf{r})\rangle
. The odd powers of the white-noise potential
V

vanish after averaging, hence

C(T)=\langle W_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)\rangle+\mathcal{O}(V^{4})\;.

(110)

We substitute
W_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)
using eq. (
108
) into
eq. (
110
) and neglect the residual term
\mathcal{O}(V^{4})

to obtain

C(T)=C^{(1)}(T)+C^{(2)}(T)

(111)

with the two contributions

\displaystyle C^{(1)}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2}\,\langle W_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{r},T)\rangle\;,

\displaystyle C^{(2)}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\,\frac{\langle n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)\rangle}{\beta N_{c}\,e^{\beta\mu}}\;.

(112)

B.1
First contribution

The mean value of
W_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{r},T)
in eq. (
112
)
depends on the statistics of the random potential
V(\mathbf{r})
.
For a white-noise potential, eq. (
38
) leads to

\langle W_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{r},T)\rangle=S\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,[\Gamma(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},T)]^{2}\;,

(113)

which is indeed independent of
\mathbf{r}
.
Here, the parameter
S
characterizes the strength of the potential fluctuations.
The Fourier image of the filter function,

\hat{\Gamma}(\mathbf{k})=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,\Gamma(\mathbf{r})\,e^{\text{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;,

(114)

permits to rewrite eq. (
113
) using the Plancherel
identity with the result

C^{(1)}(T)=\frac{\beta S}{2(2\pi)^{d}}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}\,[\hat{\Gamma}(\mathbf{k})]^{2}\;.

(115)

Here,
d=1,2,3
is the spacial dimension.
Using Eq. (
89
),
we express
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathbf{k})
via the
universal function
\hat{\Gamma}_{\text{uni}}(k)
,
which is defined by eq. (
87
).
The resulting coefficients
C^{(1)}(T)
are

\displaystyle C^{(1)}_{1d}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta S}{4\pi\lambdabar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{k}e^{-k^{2}/4}\,\text{erfi}(k/2)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}k

(116)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 0.260\,\frac{\beta S}{\lambdabar}

in
d=1
dimension,

\displaystyle C^{(1)}_{2d}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta S}{2(2\pi\lambdabar)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!2\pi k\left[\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{k}e^{-k^{2}/4}\text{erfi}(k/2)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}k

(117)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 0.146\,\frac{\beta S}{\lambdabar^{2}}

in
d=2
dimensions,
and

\displaystyle C^{(1)}_{3d}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta S}{2(2\pi\lambdabar)^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!4\pi k^{2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{k}e^{-k^{2}/4}\text{erfi}(k/2)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}k

(118)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 0.0971\,\frac{\beta S}{\lambdabar^{3}}

in
d=3
dimensions.

B.2
Second contribution

To calculate
C^{(2)}(T)
,
we need an expression for the second-order correction
n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)

to the electron density
n(\mathbf{r},T)
.
We start from eq. (
72
)
that expresses the electron density through the
one-particle Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
,

n(\mathbf{r},T)=2e^{\beta\mu}\langle\mathbf{r}|\exp(-\beta\hat{H})|\mathbf{r}\rangle\;.

(119)

Let us consider the kinetic-energy operator
\hat{T}

as the non-perturbed Hamiltonian, and the potential-energy operator

\hat{V}=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,V(\mathbf{r})\,|\mathbf{r}\rangle\langle\mathbf{r}|

(120)

as a small perturbation. Then, the perturbation expansion up to the second order yields

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{0}(T)+n_{1}(\mathbf{r},T)+n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)\;,

\displaystyle n_{0}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2e^{\beta\mu}\langle\mathbf{r}|e^{-\beta\hat{T}}|\mathbf{r}\rangle\;,

\displaystyle n_{1}(\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2e^{\beta\mu}\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi\,\langle\mathbf{r}|e^{-\xi\hat{T}}\hat{V}e^{(\xi-\beta)\hat{T}}|\mathbf{r}\rangle\;,

\displaystyle n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2e^{\beta\mu}\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}

(121)

\displaystyle\times\langle\mathbf{r}|e^{-\xi_{1}\hat{T}}\hat{V}e^{(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2})\hat{T}}\hat{V}e^{(\xi_{2}-\beta)\hat{T}}|\mathbf{r}\rangle\;.\quad

As our next step, we insert the expression for
\hat{V}
from eq. (
120
)
into eq. (
121
) to see that

n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)=\iint\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}_{2}\,\Gamma_{n}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},T)V(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{1})\,V(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{2})\;,

(122)

where the kernel
\Gamma_{n}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},T)
is defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2e^{\beta\mu}\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}\,\langle\bm{0}|e^{-\xi_{1}\hat{T}}|\mathbf{r}_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle\times\langle\mathbf{r}_{1}|e^{(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2})\hat{T}}|\mathbf{r}_{2}\rangle\langle\mathbf{r}_{2}|e^{(\xi_{2}-\beta)\hat{T}}|\bm{0}\rangle\;.

The expression (
112
) for
C^{(2)}(T)

contains the mean value of
n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)
.
Using eq. (
122
) and the statistics of the white-noise
potential
V(\mathbf{r})
from eq. (
38
) we
obtain the average value in the form

\langle n_{2}(\mathbf{r},T)\rangle=S\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\Gamma_{n}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},T)\;,

(124)

which is independent of
\mathbf{r}
.
To calculate the integral on the right-hand side,
we represent the kinetic-energy operator
\hat{T}
in Fourier space as

\hat{T}=\frac{\Omega}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}\,\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m}\,|\mathbf{k}\rangle\langle\mathbf{k}|\;,

(125)

and use eq. (
80
) for the plane-wave matrix elements
\langle\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{k}\rangle
.
Then eq. (
) leads to

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,\Gamma_{n}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2e^{\beta\mu}}{(2\pi)^{3d}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}

(126)

\displaystyle\iiint\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}_{3}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle\times e^{-\hbar^{2}\xi_{1}k_{1}^{2}/(2m)}e^{\hbar^{2}(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2})k_{2}^{2}/(2m)}

\displaystyle\times e^{\hbar^{2}(\xi_{2}-\beta)k_{3}^{2}/(2m)}e^{\text{i}(\mathbf{k}_{3}-\mathbf{k}_{1})\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;.

Here, the integration over
\mathbf{r}
is straightforward,

\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,e^{\text{i}(\mathbf{k}_{3}-\mathbf{k}_{1})\cdot\mathbf{r}}=(2\pi)^{d}\,\delta(\mathbf{k}_{3}-\mathbf{k}_{1})\;,

(127)

which allows us to perform the integral over
\mathbf{k}_{3}
.
Thence,

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,\Gamma_{n}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2e^{\beta\mu}}{(2\pi)^{2d}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}

(128)

\displaystyle\times\left(\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}_{1}\,e^{-(\beta-\xi_{2}+\xi_{1})\hbar^{2}k_{1}^{2}/2m}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}_{2}\,e^{-(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})\hbar^{2}k_{2}^{2}/2m}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2e^{\beta\mu}}{(2\pi)^{2d}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\frac{2e^{\beta\mu}}{(2\pi)^{2d}}}\times\left(\frac{2\pi m}{(\beta-\xi_{2}+\xi_{1})\hbar^{2}}\right)^{d/2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\frac{2e^{\beta\mu}}{(2\pi)^{2d}}}\times\left(\frac{2\pi m}{(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})\hbar^{2}}\right)^{d/2}.\quad

In the latter expression, the parameters
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}

appear only in the combination
\xi=\xi_{2}-\xi_{1}
.
To further simplify the integrals, we note that, for any function
\phi(\xi)
,

\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}\,\phi(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})=\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi\,(\beta-\xi)\,\phi(\xi)

(129)

holds. Hence,

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,\Gamma_{n}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2e^{\beta\mu}\left(\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{d}

(130)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi\;\xi^{-d/2}(\beta-\xi)^{1-d/2}.\qquad

We combine eqs. (
112
),
(
124
), and (
130
),
and express
N_{c}
from eq. (
47
) and
\lambdabar
from
eq. (
64
) to arrive at a simple formula
for the constant
C^{(2)}(T)
,

C^{(2)}(T)=-\frac{S\beta^{d-1}}{(2\sqrt{\pi}\lambdabar)^{d}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\xi\;\xi^{-d/2}(\beta-\xi)^{1-d/2}\;.

(131)

In
d=1
dimension, this formula is readily evaluated,

C^{(2)}_{1d}(T)=-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\;\frac{S\beta}{\lambdabar}

(132)

because the integral converges.

However, the integral in eq. (
131
) diverges
in two and three dimensions. In particular,

C^{(2)}_{2d}(T)=-\frac{S\beta}{4\pi\lambdabar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{\xi}\,.

(133)

This divergence stems from the divergent integral

I(\xi)=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}\exp\left(-\xi\frac{\hbar^{2}|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{2m}\right)\;,

(134)

that appears in eq. (
128
).
This integral becomes infinitely large in the limit
\xi\to 0
when
the integration is performed over the whole
\mathbf{k}
-space.
However, in a discretized model, the value of
|\mathbf{k}|
cannot be larger
than
\simeq a^{-1}
, where
a
is the grid lattice constant.
This shows that the lower limit of the integral in eq. (
133
)
should be set to

\xi_{\text{min}}\simeq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\text{max}}}\simeq\frac{ma^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\simeq\frac{a^{2}\beta}{\lambdabar^{2}}\;.

(135)

Therefore, in two dimensions we find

C^{(2)}_{2d}(T)=\frac{S\beta}{2\pi\lambdabar^{2}}\ln\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{2}a}{\lambdabar}\;,

(136)

where
\tilde{\mu}_{2}
arises from the unknown proportionality factor
between the left-hand side and the right-hand side of eq. (
135
).
The factor
\mu_{2}
may depend also on the type of the discretization
grid (square, hexagonal, etc.).

Similarly, we replace the lower limit in eq. (
131
)
by
\xi_{\text{min}}

also in three dimensions,

C^{(2)}_{3d}(T)=-\frac{S\beta}{(4\pi)^{3/2}\lambdabar^{3}}\left[2\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\xi_{\text{min}}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\xi_{\text{min}}}{\beta}\right)\right]\;.

(137)

Neglecting the last term in the square brackets and substituting

\xi_{\text{min}}
from eq. (
135
), we find

C^{(2)}_{3d}(T)=-\mu_{3}\frac{S\beta}{\lambdabar^{2}a}\;,

(138)

where the parameter
\mu_{3}
again depends
on the undetermined factor in eq. (
135
).

B.3
Summary

Summing up the expressions for
C^{(1)}(T)
,
eqs. (
116
)–(
118
),
and for
C^{(2)}(T)
, eqs. (
132
),
(
136
), and (
138
),
we arrive at the following results for the coefficient
C(T)
in
d=1,2,3
dimensions,

\displaystyle C_{1d}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-0.183\;\frac{S}{k_{\text{B}}T\lambdabar}\;,

(139)

\displaystyle C_{2d}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{S}{k_{\text{B}}T\lambdabar^{2}}\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\frac{\mu_{2}a}{\lambdabar}\;,

(140)

\displaystyle C_{3d}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{S}{k_{\text{B}}T\lambdabar^{3}}\left(-\mu_{3}\frac{\lambdabar}{a}+0.0971\right)\;.

(141)

The parameters
\mu_{2}
and
\mu_{3}

in these formulas are lattice specific, and can be found by fitting of the numerical data.
As shown in the main text,
\mu_{2}\approx 0.30
for the square lattice,
and
\mu_{3}\approx 0.255
for the three-dimensional cubic lattice.
Thus eq. (
67
) is shown to hold within second-order
perturbation theory for a white-noise random potential.
