Moment-Based Adjustments of Statistical Inference in High-Dimensional Generalized Linear Models Thanks: Date: August 8, 2026, Contact: sawaya@g.ecc.u-tokyo.ac.jp
Kazuma Sawaya † , Yoshimasa Uematsu ‡ , Masaaki Imaizumi †♮

Abstract
. We developed a statistical inference method applicable to a broad range of generalized linear models (GLMs) in high-dimensional settings, where the number of unknown coefficients scales proportionally with the sample size. Although a pioneering inference method has been developed for logistic regression, which is a specific instance of GLMs, we cannot apply this method directly to other GLMs because of unknown hyper-parameters. In this study, we addressed this limitation by developing a new inference method designed for a certain class of GLMs. Our method is based on the adjustment of asymptotic normality in high dimensions and is feasible in the sense that it is possible even with unknown hyper-parameters. Specifically, we introduce a novel convex loss-based estimator and its associated system, which are essential components of inference. Next, we devise a moment-based method for estimating the system parameters required by the method. Consequently, we construct confidence intervals for GLMs in a high-dimensional regime. We prove that our proposed method has desirable theoretical properties, such as strong consistency and exact coverage probability. Finally, we experimentally confirmed its validity.



1.
Introduction

For a pair
(\bm{X},Y)
of
p
-dimensional random features
\bm{X}
and random responses
Y
, we consider the following generalized linear model (GLM):

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}]=g\left(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right),~~~\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

where
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an inverse link function that monotonically increases, and
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

The GLM is one of the most popular classes of statistical models, including linear regression, logistic regression, Poisson regression, and others.

Recently, a high-dimensional regime has been studied. We are particularly interested in the proportional limit of
the coefficient dimension
p\equiv p(n)
and sample size

:

(2)

\displaystyle n,p(n)\to\infty\mbox{~~~and~~~}p(n)/n\to\exists\kappa\in(0,\infty).

\bm{\beta}\equiv\bm{\beta}(n)
in the regime of (

).
The statistical inference of

Despite the development of inferential frameworks for logistic regression, we cannot directly apply them to the other GLMs.
This is for the following reasons.
First, we need to obtain a valid SE system for GLMs but it is nontrivial.
Second, the existing estimator of hyper-parameters in the SE system is often numerically unstable in high dimensions (e.g. large
\kappa
).
This is because it relies on statistics that diverge in the large
\kappa
, i.e., the inverse of the minimum eigenvalue of the Wishart matrix and an estimator for
\bm{\beta}
.
To illustrate this, we plot errors of existing LOO-based methods, an extension of SLOE
[
51
]
, in Figure
1
.

We develop a statistical method of inference for GLMs
in the high-dimensional regime (
2
), which is applicable in the presence of unknown parameters in the SE system of GLMs.
First, we derive a new SE system for GLMs, by employing a
surrogate loss
for estimation of GLMs.
Second, we develop a
moment-based adjustment
, which estimates parameters of the SE system using moments.
Consequently, we achieve a valid statistical inference for general GLMs.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We derive the new SE system, which describes an asymptotic behavior of a general class of GLMs, by utilizing a convex surrogate loss and an
approximate message passing
approach.

•

We develop a moment-based method for estimating hyper-parameters in the SE system by using an additive form of GLMs.
This estimator is numerically stable because it depends neither on the inverse of high-dimensional matrices nor on the estimator of
\bm{\beta}
, unlike the existing methods.

•

We develop the statistical inference method for GLMs in a high-dimensional setting.
The method is an extension of the adjustment method for asymptotic normality only for logistic regression.
We also prove its asymptotic validity; specifically, the proposed confidence interval asymptotically achieves the exact coverage probability.

2.
Preliminary

We discuss the basic concept of GLM and review the statistical inference framework for logistic regression in the high-dimensional settings
2
based on a state evolution (SE) system.

2.1.
Generalized Linear Model

Suppose that we observe i.i.d.
n
pairs
\{(\bm{X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
of a feature vector
\bm{X}_{i}\equiv\bm{X}_{i}(n)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and a target variable
Y_{i}\equiv Y_{i}(n)\in\mathcal{Y}
that follow the GLM (
1
), where
\mathcal{Y}
is a response space, such as
\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}_{+},\{0,1\},\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, and so on.
Hereafter, we drop the dependence on
n
whenever it is clear from context.
We assume that the feature vector is generated independently from
\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
using the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}\equiv\bm{\Sigma}(n)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
.
The GLM can represent several models by specifying the inverse link function
g(\cdot)
and distribution of
Y_{i}
for a given
\bm{X}_{i}
: for example,
Y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(g(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}))
with
g(t)=1/(1+\exp(-t))
for the logistic regression model, and
Y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Pois}(g(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}))
with
g(t)=\exp(t)
for the Poisson regression model.

If a random variable
Y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}
has a density function (continuous case) or a mass function (discrete case)
f(\cdot\mid\bm{X}_{i})
, then the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of
\bm{\beta}
is defined as

(3)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathrm{MLE}}(n)=\argmax_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(Y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}).

In a low-dimensional setting where
n\to\infty
with fixed
p<\infty
, the MLE
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathrm{MLE}}
asymptotically obeys the normal distribution,
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathrm{MLE}}-\bm{\beta})\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\mathcal{I}_{\bm{\beta}}^{-1})
, where
\mathcal{I}_{\bm{\beta}}
is the Fisher information matrix at the true coefficient
\bm{\beta}
. Once the matrix is estimated consistently, we can consider the inference of
\bm{\beta}
conventionally.
By contrast, this classical approach is no longer valid in a proportionally high-dimensional setting (
2
), as presented in
[
42
]
.

2.2.
State Evolution (SE)-based Inference: Logistic Regression Case

For a logistic regression,
[
42
,
52
]
developed an inferential framework under the high-dimensional regime (
2
).
Specifically, the studies showed that a properly corrected MLE is asymptotically normally distributed.
That is, as
n,p(n)\to\infty
with
p(n)/n\to\kappa\in(0,1)
, we have the following for any
j=1,\dots,p
satisfying
\sqrt{n}\tau_{j}\beta_{j}=O(1)
if the MLE exists:

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\mathrm{MLE}}-\mu\beta_{j})}{\sigma{/\tau_{j}}}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\tau_{j}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(X_{ij}\mid\bm{X}_{i\setminus j})
is the conditional variance of the
j
-th element of
\bm{X}_{i}
given
\bm{X}_{i\setminus j}:=(X_{i,1},\dots,X_{i,j-1},X_{i,j+1},\dots,X_{i,p})
.
Here,
(\mu,\sigma)
are the
state evolution
(SE) parameters
that satisfy the following SE system,

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}&=\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\left[2g(Z)\left(\eta g(D)\right)^{2}\right],\\
0&=\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\left[g(Z)Z(\eta g(D))\right],\\
1-\kappa&=\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\left[2g(Z)(1+\eta g^{\prime}(D))^{-1}\right],\end{cases}

with
D=\mathrm{prox}_{\eta G}\left(\mu Z+\sqrt{\kappa}\sigma Q_{2}\right)
,
Z=\gamma Q_{1}
, and
(Q_{1},Q_{2})\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{2})
. Here, the parameter
\gamma^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\bm{X}_{i}(n)^{\top}\bm{\beta}(n))
is for
signal strength
(SS). The covariance structure of
\bm{X}_{i}
is completely captured by
\gamma^{2}
in this system.
In this logistic regression case, the inverse link and the integrated inverse link functions are defined as
g(t)=1/(1+\exp(-t))
and
G(t):=\log(1+\exp(t))
, respectively.
Here,
\mu,\sigma,\eta\in\mathbb{R}
are the SE parameters. Remarkably, SE-based inference reduces the inference problem on high-dimensional coefficients to an SE system with a small number of scalar SE parameters.

The SE system is derived as a fixed-point equation of an iterative algorithm for an optimization problem on estimators.
In the logistic regression case, we derive the SE system (
5
) from the optimization problem (
3
).
The derivation process is based on the framework of approximate message passing, the details of which are provided in
[
21
]
.

Based on the asymptotic normality of the MLE in (
4
), we may construct a confidence interval for each
\beta_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
, provided that the SE parameters are available.
Specifically, given
(\mu,\sigma)
, the MLE-centric confidence interval with confidence level
1-\alpha\in(0,1)
is formed as

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{CI}_{(1-\alpha)}:=\left[\frac{\widehat{\beta}^{\mathrm{MLE}}_{j}}{\mu}-z_{(1-\alpha/2)}\frac{\sigma{/\tau_{j}}}{\sqrt{n}\mu},\frac{\widehat{\beta}^{\mathrm{MLE}}_{j}}{\mu}+z_{(1-\alpha/2)}\frac{\sigma{/\tau_{j}}}{\sqrt{n}\mu}\right],

where
z_{\alpha/2}
is the
(\alpha/2)
-quantile of a standard Gaussian distribution.
By properly specifying of the parameters, we obtain
\mathrm{Pr}(\beta_{j}\in\mathrm{CI}_{(1-\alpha)})\to 1-\alpha
as
n,p(n)\to\infty
in the sense of (
2
).

Importantly, we need to know the SS parameter
\gamma^{2}
in advance, because the SE parameters
(\mu,\sigma)
are understood as a function of
\gamma^{2}
.

[
42
]
proposed an estimator based on the phase transitions of the existence of the MLE
[
11
]
.

[
51
]
developed the LOO-based approach for this estimation problem.

3.
Estimator and SE System for GLMs

In this section, we derive a generic estimator for the GLM and associated SE system.
It is necessary to perform SE-based inference as in
[
42
]
.

3.1.
Surrogate Estimator for Coefficient Vector
\bm{\beta}

First, we develop an estimator for the true coefficient vector
\bm{\beta}
by using a
surrogate loss
function associated with
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
.
We introduce the loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

(7)

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{b};\bm{x},y):=G\left(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{b}\right)-y\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{b},

where
G(\cdot)
is the function satisfying
G^{\prime}=g
.
Using the loss, we define the surrogate estimator

(8)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(n):=\argmin_{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\bm{b};\bm{X}_{i},Y_{i}).

The use of a surrogate estimator is justified in two ways.
First, if
g(\cdot)
monotonically increases, the surrogate loss is convex in
\bm{b}
.
This property provides computational and statistical advantages for a broader class of GLMs and plays a crucial role in the derivation of SE systems.
Second, the minimizer of surrogate risk coincides with the true coefficient
\bm{\beta}
, which is expressed as follows:

Proposition 3.1

(Lemma 1 in
[
1
]
)
.

Consider an
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathcal{Y}
-valued random element
(\bm{X},Y)
that follows GLM (
1
).
If
g(\cdot)
is increasing, then the coefficient vector
\bm{\beta}
in (
1
) satisfies
\bm{\beta}=\argmin_{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bm{b};\bm{X},Y)\mid\bm{X}\right].

These results claims that the surrogate loss estimator is a reasonable extension of MLE for GLMs.

Remark 3.1

(Connection to exponential family)
.

Modelling the distribution of
Y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}
to follow an exponential dispersion family
[
26
]
and choosing
g(\cdot)
as the inverse of the canonical link function make the surrogate estimator (
8
) equivalent to the MLE (
3
), covering logistic and Poisson regression.

3.2.
SE System for GLMs

We developed an SE system associated with the surrogate estimator (
8
).
As this estimator is given by the minimization problem of the convex loss function, we can derive the SE system for the estimator, as described in Section
2.2
.
Recall that
G(\cdot)
is a function satisfying
G^{\prime}=g
, and
\gamma^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\bm{X}_{i}(n)^{\top}\bm{\beta}(n))
is the signal strength (SS) parameter.

Proposition 3.2
.

We consider the surrogate estimator in (
8
) to minimize the surrogate loss function.
If
\kappa\in(0,1)
and the estimator is bounded, then the corresponding SE system is given by

(9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}&=\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2},\bar{Y})}\left[\left(\bar{Y}-g\left(D\right)\right)^{2}\right],\\
0&=\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2},\bar{Y})}\left[Z\left(\bar{Y}-g\left(D\right)\right)\right],\\
1-\kappa&=\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2},\bar{Y})}\left[(1+\eta g^{\prime}\left(D\right))^{-1}\right],\end{cases}

where
D=\mathrm{prox}_{\eta G}(\mu Z+\sqrt{\kappa}\sigma Q_{2}+\eta\bar{Y})
,
Z=\gamma Q_{1}
,
(Q_{1},Q_{2})\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{2})
, and

\bar{Y}\sim P_{\bar{Y}}
with
P_{\bar{Y}}
being the convergence limit of empirical distribution of
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
whose mean is
g(Z)
where
Z\overset{\rm d}{=}\lim_{n\to\infty}\bm{X}_{1}(n)^{\top}\bm{\beta}(n)
.

We demonstrate the solutions to the system for Poisson regression in Figure
3
and piecewise regression in Figure
3
.
These plots are numerical solutions to the SE system over
100
simulations with their mean and
95\%
bootstrap confidence intervals.

In view of (
9
), the SE parameters
(\mu,\sigma^{2},\eta)
depend on three components: (i)
\kappa=\lim_{n\to\infty}p(n)/n
, (ii) the SS parameter
\gamma^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\bm{X}_{i}(n)^{\top}\bm{\beta}(n))
, and (iii) the distribution of
\bar{Y}
determined by the model.
In practice, we can set
\kappa=p(n)/n
and consider it as known.
However, the estimation of
\gamma^{2}
and other parameters (if present) of the distribution of
\bar{Y}
for each GLM is highly nontrivial.
In the next section, we propose a simple method for estimating them.

Remark 3.2

(Specification of
\bar{Y}
)
.

If the distribution of
{Y}\mid\bm{X}
has a single parameter (e.g., Bernoulli, Poisson, and exponential distribution), then the model assumption
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\bm{X}]=g(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\beta})
implies that
\gamma^{2}
fully characterizes the inferential parameters
(\mu,\sigma^{2},\eta)
. Thus, in this case, the SS parameter
\gamma^{2}
is the only unknown parameter to be estimated. Meanwhile, estimating the distribution of
{Y}\mid\bm{X}
is difficult if it has multiple parameters. Even in such cases, we propose a method for their estimation, focusing on the case of
{Y}\mid\bm{X}
following a normal distribution; see Section
4.2
.

4.
Moment-Based Estimation for SS Parameter

In this section, we propose a novel estimator for the SS parameter
\gamma^{2}
and other parameters of the distribution of
\bar{Y}
to access the SE parameters
(\mu,\sigma^{2})
for inference.
Once we estimate
\gamma^{2}
and the parameters of
\bar{Y}
, we can solve system (
9
) by substituting the estimators to obtain the SE parameters.

4.1.
Additive Form of GLM

As the first step, we converted the GLM (
1
) into the
additive model
with observation
(Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
:

(10)

\displaystyle Y_{i}=g(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta})+e_{i}\quad{\rm with}\quad e_{i}=Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}],\quad i=1,\dots,n,

where
e_{i}
is a noise variable that satisfies
\mathbb{E}[e_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}]=0
.
Note that the parameters of the distribution of
e_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}
may depend on
\bm{X}_{i},~i=1,\dots,n
.
Additive model (
10
) provides a more concrete distribution structure of
Y_{i}
.
While the original GLM (
1
) highlights the conditional mean, the additive model provides a more analyzable form of the distribution by introducing the variable
e_{i}
.
This structure is essential for our estimation method.

4.2.
Moment-Based Estimation when
Y\mid\mathbf{X}
is Gaussian

We first introduce our moment-based method for the case with Gaussian output:
{Y}\mid\bm{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(g(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\beta}),\sigma_{{e}}^{2})
with an unknown variance parameter
\sigma_{{e}}^{2}>0
.
This is a typical case of GLMs including a nonlinear regression setup, but it has additional unknown parameter
\sigma_{{e}}^{2}
to be estimated, as well as the SS parameter
\gamma
.
We first consider this rather complicated case where we estimate both
\gamma
and
\sigma_{{e}}^{2}
as it serves as an appropriate introduction to our approach.

At the beginning, we characterize the parameters
\gamma^{2}
and
\sigma_{{e}}^{2}
by the moments of
Y
.
In the limit, the additive model (
10
) has the form,

(11)

\displaystyle\bar{Y}=g(Z_{\gamma})+\bar{e}\quad\text{with}\quad Z_{\gamma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{e}),

where
Z_{\gamma}
and
\bar{e}
are independent.
Then,
we obtain the condition with second and fourth moments as

(12)

\displaystyle\Psi(\gamma,\sigma_{e}):=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[g(Z_{\gamma})^{2}]-\sigma_{e}^{2}\\
\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}^{4}]-\mathbb{E}[g(Z_{\gamma})^{4}]-6\mathbb{E}[g(Z_{\gamma})^{2}]\sigma_{e}^{2}-3\sigma_{e}^{4}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

By this form, the parameter
(\gamma,\sigma_{e}^{2})
is characterized as a solution to the simultaneous equations (
12
).
The uniqueness of the solution to these equations is validated numerically.
We can identify the solutions with any
g(\cdot)
unless
g(\cdot)
is a constant function.

We consider the estimation of
(\gamma,\sigma_{e}^{2})
using an empirical analog of the equations (
12
).
Since
g(\cdot)
is known, we can simulate
h_{2}(\varsigma):=\mathbb{E}[g({Z_{\varsigma}})^{2}]
and
h_{4}(\varsigma):=\mathbb{E}[g({Z_{\varsigma}})^{4}]
by generating
{Z_{\varsigma}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\varsigma^{2})
for each
\varsigma>0
.
Using the observations
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we then define the estimators as the solution to

(13)

\displaystyle(\widehat{\gamma},\widehat{\sigma}_{e}):=\left\{(\varsigma,\varsigma_{e})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}:\Psi_{n}(\varsigma,\varsigma_{e})=\bm{0}\right\},

(14)

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(\varsigma,\varsigma_{e})=\begin{bmatrix}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{2}-h_{2}(\varsigma)-\varsigma_{e}^{2}\\
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{4}-h_{4}(\varsigma)-6h_{2}(\varsigma)\varsigma_{e}^{2}-3\varsigma_{e}^{4}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The empirical equations in (
13
) are viewed as an empirical analogue of those in (
12
), provided that the convergence
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{a}\overset{\rm a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}^{a}]
for
a\in\{2,4\}
are true under some assumptions.
The solution is obtained by root-finding algorithms, such as the Gauss-Newton method.

An advantage of the moment-based estimator is its independence from both the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
and an inverse of high-dimensional matrix
(\mathbb{X}^{\top}\mathbb{X})^{-1}
with
\mathbb{X}=(\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{n})^{\top}
, that diverge as
\kappa
increases.
Hence, the moment-based estimator is stable with any
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
.
Additionally, since this method relies only on observations through
Y
, it is computationally efficient.

4.3.
Moment-Based Estimation for Other Cases

We study moment-based estimation in other situations of GLMs with a single parameter.
The problem is simpler since we only need to estimate
\gamma
, and need not estimate additional parameters such as
\sigma_{e}^{2}
in the Gaussian case.
Examples are mentioned in Remark
3.2
.

In this case, we only consider the first moment.
By the limit form (
11
), we have

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}(\gamma):=\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}]-\mathbb{E}[g(Z_{\gamma})]=0.

Here we use the fact that
\mathbb{E}[e_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[e_{1}\mid\bm{X}_{1}]]=0
by the model’s assumption.
Thus, we define an estimator of
\gamma
as a solution to the linear equation with
h_{1}(\varsigma):=\mathbb{E}[g(Z_{\varsigma})]
:

(16)

\displaystyle\textstyle\widehat{\gamma}:=\left\{\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:\tilde{\Psi}_{n}(\varsigma)=0\right\},\quad\mbox{where~}\tilde{\Psi}_{n}(\varsigma):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}-h_{1}(\varsigma).

Similar to Section
4.2
, this estimator is stable for any
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
and computationally efficient.
If necessary, we can construct an estimator by considering higher-order moments as in Section
4.2
.

5.
Statistical Inference for GLMs

Using the result above, we construct a confidence interval with reasonable coverage, even for high dimensions. We also provide theoretical guarantees for estimators and confidence intervals.

5.1.
Confidence Interval

We construct a confidence interval for
\bm{\beta}
by using the estimators in Sections
4
and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
in (
8
).
Let
(\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\sigma}^{2},\widehat{\eta})
be the solutions to the SE system (

replaced by

We also introduce an estimator

5.2.
Theoretical Validation

We derive the theoretical results of the proposed methods.
First, we give the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

We consider the following conditions:

(A1)

\bm{X}(n)
is generated as

(A3)

(A3) is a technical requirement for the Lipschitzness of SE parameters with respect to

In the sequel, we focus only on the situation in which the estimator (

Unless it does not happen, we can consider a regularization approach to guarantee its existence; see Section

First, we obtain the asymptotic normality of the adjusted test statistics for each coordinate of

7.
Conclusion

We developed a statistical inference method for GLMs in high dimensions.
Our approach extends the SE-based inference method designed for a logistic regression model of GLMs.
Specifically, we developed a surrogate estimator for GLMs, an associated SE system, and a method to estimate the necessary parameters in the system.
Our methodology works well in terms of both theory and experimentation.
One limitation of our method is that it requires Gaussianity of the covariate; however, this limitation can be relaxed by applying studies on the universality of methodologies in terms of data, for example,
[
35
,
50
]
.

A.1.
Construction of
\widehat{\tau}_{j}^{2}

To construct the corrected confidence interval (
17
) with correlated features, we have to estimate the conditional variance parameter,
\tau_{j}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\bm{X}_{ij}|\bm{X}_{i\setminus j})
, for each
j=1,\ldots,p
. In this article, we follow
[
52
]
. They consider the residual sum of squares
\mathrm{RSS}_{j}
obtained by regression of
\bm{X}_{j}=(\bm{X}_{1j},\ldots,\bm{X}_{nj})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto a sub-vector of the input
\bm{X}_{\setminus j}=(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{j-1},\bm{X}_{j+1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p-1)}
.
Owing to the Gaussianity of
\bm{X}_{i}
, it satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{RSS}_{j}=\bm{X}_{j}^{\top}\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{\setminus j}}^{\perp}\bm{X}_{j}\sim\tau_{j}^{2}\chi_{n-p+1}^{2},

where
\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{\setminus j}}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the orthogonal complement of column space spanned by
\bm{X}_{\setminus j}
. Then, we immediately obtain an unbiased estimator of
\tau_{j}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{j}^{2}=\frac{1}{n-p+1}\mathrm{RSS}_{j}.

A.2.
Master Theorem of Generalized Approximate Message Passing (GAMP)

We present the GAMP algorithm by
[
37
]
and its associated theoretical result, which are key tools to inspect the limiting distributional behavior of the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
.

First, we provide the GAMP algorithm, which generate a sequence of parameters
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{k}
for an index

based on the minimization problem in (
8
) and a limit of the sequence corresponds to the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

Let

\bm{X}=(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}

\breve{\bm{X}}=\bm{X}/\sqrt{n}

\bar{\eta}_{0}>0,\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{0}

(19)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\eta}_{k+1}}{\kappa}\breve{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left\{\bm{Y}-g\left(\mathrm{prox}_{\bar{\eta}_{k}G}(\tilde{\bm{\xi}}^{k}+\bar{\eta}_{k}\bm{Y})\right)\right\}+\frac{\bar{\eta}_{k+1}}{\bar{\eta}_{k}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{k},

(20)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\breve{\bm{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{k+1}-\bar{\eta}_{k+1}\left\{\bm{Y}-g\left(\mathrm{prox}_{\bar{\eta}_{k}G}(\tilde{\bm{\xi}}^{k}+\bar{\eta}_{k}\bm{Y})\right)\right\}.

Here,

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\kappa\bar{\eta}_{k}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2},U)}\left[\frac{1}{1+\bar{\eta}_{k}g^{\prime}\left(d_{k}\right)}\right]\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\eta}_{k+1}}{\gamma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2},U)}\left[\gamma Q_{1}\left\{h(\gamma Q_{1},U)-g\left(d_{k}\right)\right\}\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\eta}_{k+1}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1},Q_{2},U)}\left[\left\{h(\gamma Q_{1},U)-g\left(d_{k}\right)\right\}^{2}\right],

Proof of Lemma

.

We prove this lemma by the general proof strategy discussed in Section 4.4 in

It consists of three steps: (i) find a fixed point of the GAMP recursion; (ii) consider the stationary version of the GAMP recursion; (iii) show that the stationary version of the GAMP iterate converges to the estimator

Following the strategy, the step (i) and the step (ii)
are simply achieved by the convexity and smoothness of the surrogate loss function by the assumption.
Given the fixed point

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{k+1}

Denote

are some positive constants.
Here, we use
\omega(\|t\widehat{\bm{\beta}}+(1-t)\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\|)\geq\omega(\max(\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\|,\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\|))
since
\omega(\cdot)
is non-increasing.
Using optimality of the estimator
\ell(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\geq\ell(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality as
(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})^{\top}\nabla\ell(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\geq-\|{\nabla\ell(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\|}\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\|
yields, with probability at least
1-c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}n)
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\|\leq\frac{2}{\omega\left(\max\left\{\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\|,\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\|\right\}\right)}\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\nabla\ell(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\right\rVert\leq\frac{2}{\omega(\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\|)\omega(\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\|)}\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\nabla\ell(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\right\rVert,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
0<\omega(\cdot)<1
and
\omega
is non-increasing. Next, we consider controlling
\|\nabla\ell(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\|
. We have

(26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{prox}_{\bar{\eta}_{*}G}\left(\bm{\xi}^{k-1}+\bar{\eta}_{*}\bm{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{\xi}^{k-1}+\bar{\eta}_{*}\bm{Y}-\bar{\eta}_{*}g\left(\mathrm{prox}_{\bar{\eta}_{*}G}\left(\bm{\xi}^{k-1}+\bar{\eta}_{*}\bm{Y}\right)\right)

(27)

\displaystyle=\bm{\xi}^{k-1}-\bm{\xi}^{k}+\breve{\bm{X}}\bm{\beta}^{k}

(28)

\displaystyle=\bm{\xi}^{k-1}-\bm{\xi}^{k}+\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k},

by the definition of the proximal operator and (
25
). Thus,

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{k}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\eta}_{*}}{\kappa}\breve{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left\{\bm{Y}-g\left(\mathrm{prox}_{\bar{\eta}_{*}G}(\bm{\xi}^{k-1}+\bar{\eta}_{*}\bm{Y})\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\eta}_{*}}{\kappa}\breve{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left\{\bm{Y}-g\left(\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}+\bm{\xi}^{k-1}-\bm{\xi}^{k}\right)\right\},

by (
24
). Using this and triangle inequalities give

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\ell(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\bm{X}^{\top}\left\{g(\bm{X}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})-\bm{Y}\right\}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\bm{X}^{\top}\left\{\bm{Y}-g(\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}+\bm{\xi}^{k-1}-\bm{\xi}^{k})\right\}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\quad+\left\lVert\bm{X}^{\top}\left\{g(\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}+\bm{\xi}^{k-1}-\bm{\xi}^{k})-g(\bm{X}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\right\}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{\bar{\eta}_{*}}\left\lVert\bm{\beta}^{k}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\rm op}\left\lVert g(\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}+\bm{\xi}^{k-1}-\bm{\xi}^{k})-g(\bm{X}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{\bar{\eta}_{*}}\left\lVert\bm{\beta}^{k}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\right\rVert+L_{g}\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\rm op}\left\lVert\bm{\xi}^{k-1}-\bm{\xi}^{k}\right\rVert,

where
L_{g}=\sup_{z}g^{\prime}(z)
.
This establishes, with probability at least
1-c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}n)
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\|\leq c\left\{\frac{\kappa}{\bar{\eta}_{*}}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k-1}\right\rVert+\frac{C_{g}}{n}\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\rm op}\left\lVert\bm{\xi}^{k-1}-\bm{\xi}^{k}\right\rVert\right\},

with
c={2}/\left(\omega(\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\|)\omega(\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\|)\right)
. Finally, Lemma
C.2
and the Borel-Cantelli lemma implies

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}\right\rVert=_{\rm a.s.}0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
A.1
provides a statement on the convergence of the distance between 1-dimensional distributions, which is computed by averaging over the coordinates of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. However, for statistical inference purposes, it is necessary to investigate the limiting behavior of the marginal distributions of each coordinate. To achieve this, we utilize a property of the surrogate loss function.

Lemma A.2
.

For any invertible matrix
\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
satisfying
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top}
,
\bm{L}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
minimizes the surrogate loss function in (
8
) for the true coefficient
\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{\beta}
and the covariate
\bm{L}^{-1}\bm{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{p})
.

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Since the surrogate loss depends on
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
only through their inner product
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{b}
, we have
\ell(\bm{b};\bm{X},Y)=\ell(\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{b};\bm{L}^{-1}\bm{X},Y)
.
∎

Using this in reverse, once we show Lemma A.1 for a design with identity covariance, we can rewrite the estimator corresponding to the unit covariance in
\bm{L}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
to obtain the general covariance result.

A.2.1.
Limit of Estimation and Classification Errors

As the consequence of the master theorem of GAMP (Lemma
A.1
), we obtain the convergence limits of the mean squared error (MSE) and cosine similarity-like classification error. If we set
\psi(s,t)=(s-t)^{2}
, then Lemma
A.1
implies the MSE limit

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}(\mu-1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\beta}^{2}]+\sigma^{2}.

By setting
\psi(s,t)=(s-\mu t)^{2}
, we have a corrected MSE limit

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\mu\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\sigma^{2}.

For classification errors, by taking the ratio of
\psi(s,t)=st
and
\psi(s,t)=t^{2}
, we can obtain the convergence limit of a cosine similarity-like measure

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{\beta}}{\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\mu.

A.3.
Approximation of Inverse Link Functions

In this section, we explore a way to approximate an inverse link function that does not satisfy Assumption
1
with an inverse link function that falls within a manageable range.

Firstly, when dealing with a function
g(\cdot)
that lacks Lipschitzness or smoothness, we consider the approach of clipping either its value or derivative by a suitably large threshold. This enables us to handle functions that are otherwise difficult to work with. As an example, we approximate the exponential inverse link function
g(t)=e^{t}
using the following formulation:

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(t)=e^{t}1\{t\leq\log(50)\}+50(t+1-\log(50))1\{t>\log(50)\}.

Here,
\tilde{g}
satisfies
L^{\prime}
-Lipschitz continuity and
L^{\prime}
-smoothness with
L^{\prime}=50
. In our numerical experiments conducted in Section
6
on Poisson regression, we constructed an estimate for the data generated from the inverse link function
g(t)=e^{t}
, assuming that
\tilde{g}(\cdot)
above serves as an approximation of the inverse link function. This construction yielded satisfactory results.

Next, we consider the case where
g(\cdot)
does not satisfy the asymmetry assumption, which is essential for the existence and uniqueness of our estimation for the SS parameter
\gamma^{2}
.
We consider a non-odd approximation of an odd inverse link.
Note that our approximation here directly applies to the data-generating process.
For example, if
g(\cdot)
is not exactly linear but follows the form
g(t)=\min(1.1t,0.9t)
, we can consistently estimate the SS parameter for this model.
The same holds true when
g(\cdot)
is not precisely sigmoid but instead follows
g(t)=1/(1+\exp(-t+0.01))
.

A.4.
GLM extension of the SLOE estimator

This section provides an extension of the SLOE estimator
[
51
]
from logistic regression to the GLM. First, it considers another representation of the state evolution parameters by using
corrupted signal strength

\gamma^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}&=\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{(Q_{1}^{\prime},Q_{2}^{\prime},\bar{Y})}\left[\left(\bar{Y}-g\left(\mathrm{prox}_{\eta G}(Q_{2}^{\prime})\right)\right)^{2}\right],\\

.

Suppose that
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

1

[

Unfortunately, the estimator

is unstable when

is to 1. This property inherits from two components:

and

A.5.
Regularized Estimator

In this section, we consider the following regularized estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\lambda}\in\argmin_{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\bm{b};\bm{X}_{i},Y_{i})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}J(b_{j})\right\},

GLM Form with

In this section, we discuss the other form of GLM used in (A3) in Assumption

(35)

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle=h\left(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta},\varepsilon_{i}\right),

.

\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}

35

introduced in (
9
).

\bullet

Bernoulli case (binary choice model)
. When we use a model
\bar{Y}|Z\sim\mathrm{Ber}(g(Z))
with some
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
, the inverse transformation method yields

\displaystyle\bar{Y}=1\{g(Z)\leq\bar{\varepsilon}\},\quad\bar{\varepsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1].

\bullet

Exponential case
. If
\bar{Y}|Z\sim\mathrm{Exp}(g(Z))

\displaystyle\bar{Y}=-\frac{1}{g(Z)}\log(\bar{\varepsilon}),\quad\bar{\varepsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1].

Poisson case

\displaystyle\bar{Y}=\min\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}\bar{\varepsilon}_{l}>g(Z)\right.\right\},\quad\bar{\varepsilon}_{l}\overset{\rm iid}{\sim}\mathrm{Exp}(1).

\bullet

Gaussian case

\displaystyle\bar{Y}=g(Z)+\bar{\varepsilon},\quad\bar{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\bar{\varepsilon}}^{2}).

As evident from the aforementioned examples, in the case of modeling using a one-parameter distribution, the distribution of
\bar{\varepsilon}
can be fully determined without any additional parameters.

Proof of Lemma
A.3
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(Z_{\varsigma})]=\frac{1}{\varsigma}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)\phi\left(\frac{x}{\varsigma}\right)dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(\varsigma y)\phi\left(y\right)dy=2\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{0}(\varsigma y)\phi\left(y\right)dy,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(Z_{\varsigma_{1}})]-\mathbb{E}[g(Z_{\varsigma_{0}})]=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{g_{0}(\varsigma_{1}y)-g_{0}(\varsigma_{0}y)\right\}\phi\left(y\right)dy.
