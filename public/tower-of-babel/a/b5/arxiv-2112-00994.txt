Transforming Radical Differential Equations to Algebraic Differential Equations
Sebastian Falkensteiner, J.Rafael Sendra

Abstract
. In this paper we present an algorithmic procedure that transforms, if possible, a given system of ordinary or partial differential equations with radical dependencies in the unknown function and its derivatives into a system with polynomial relations among them by means of a rational change of variables. The solutions of the given equation and its transformation correspond one-to-one. This work can be seen as a generalization of previous work on reparametrization of ODEs and PDEs with radical coefficients.

1.
Introduction

Some differential equations appear to be easier solvable than others.
A common method for finding solutions is to first transform the given differential equation into another one where solution methods are known, find its solutions, and transform them back accordingly.
In applied mathematics, often the given equation is transformed into an explicit differential equation where the highest-order derivatives are explicitly written as a function of the independent variables, dependent variables, and lower order derivatives.
Then classic results such as the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem are applied in order to show existence and uniqueness of solutions with given initial data fulfilling certain criteria.
Various other approaches for computing exact solutions exist for algebraic differential equations (shortly ADEs).
They are polynomials in the unknowns and their derivatives, and the coefficients of the polynomial are rational in the independent variables, see e.g.
[
11
]
.
In this paper, we show a strategy to transform differential equations involving radical expressions to such ADEs.
For the sake of simplicity we mainly focus on ordinary differential equations, but also give insight into the partial case.

In
[
6
]
, the authors focus on AODEs whose coefficients involve radical expressions of the independent variable
x
, and they study the possible transformation of the equation into another with only rational expressions of
x
.
Moreover, they show how to relate the solutions of both equations.
For instance, the method in
[
6
]
transforms

\sqrt{x}\,y^{\prime 3}y^{\prime\prime}+y+x=0

into

z\,Y^{\prime 3}Y^{\prime\prime}-Y^{\prime 4}+8z^{3}\,Y+8z^{5}=0

by using the change
r(z)=z^{2},y(z^{2})=Y(z)
.
In this paper, we take a step forward and we analyze differential equations where the unknown function and its derivatives can appear as radical expressions as for instance

\sqrt{y^{\prime 2}+y^{2}}+x\,\sqrt[3]{y}^{2}-yy^{\prime}=2x.

More precisely, we present a method to find, if they exist, rational changes of variables that convert systems of ODEs and PDEs of this type into systems of algebraic differential equations.
Similarly as in
[
6
]
, the underlying idea is to associate to the differential system a radical parametrization such that the possible change of variable can be derived from the tower variety; we refer to
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
for further details on radical varieties.

Combining the results in this paper with that in
[
6
]
, we can analyze mixed types of equations where radical expressions occur in the differential indeterminates and, separately, in the differential determinates.
An example of a single equation of this type is the Thomas-Fermi equation, cf. also Example
5.5
below.
In this type of situations, one can successively apply the two transformations, the one that affects the independent variables and the one that involves the dependent ones, to solve the radicalness of the equations.
One may apply the transformations in any of the two orders. Nevertheless,
the transformation on the independent variables keeps the number of equations, while the transformation on the dependent variable
will introduce, in general, additional equations. Thus, the suggestion would be to proceed first to resolve the radicals involving the independent variables.

The main contribution of the paper is the development of a method that transforms the given equation into a system of algebraic differential equations. Clearly the benefits of considering algebraic differential
equations instead of differential equations, with radical dependencies, depend on the particular type of algebraic differential equations that the method generates in each particular application. Nevertheless, the expected benefit is that the existing techniques for computing exact solutions of algebraic differential equations will be available (see e.g.
[
8
]
). In addition,
there have been several approaches in order to transform the equation and approximate its solutions, see for instance
[
4
,
22
]
.
By our method, symbolic solutions of the transformed equations can be computed by well-known techniques for AODEs such as the Newton polygon method
[
5
]
. In Example
5.3
, we show a differential equation where the application of the method in this paper generates more satisfactory expressions of the solutions than the direct treatment on the given equation; for this, the example has been developed with the aid of the computer algebra system Maple 2023.

The problem that we study is the following.
Define the radical tower

(1.1)

\mathbb{F}_{0}=\mathbb{C}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{m},

where
\mathbb{F}_{i}=\mathbb{F}_{i-1}(\delta_{i})=\mathbb{F}_{0}(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{i})
with
\delta_{i}^{e_{i}}=\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{i-1},e_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
.
In other words,
\mathbb{F}_{m}
is the field of functions built using several (possibly nested) radicals.
Consider the system of differential equations

(1.2)

\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})})=0,\ldots,F_{N}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})})=0\}

with
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{\ell}+\ell
,
F_{i}(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)
.
In other words, the independent variable
x
appears rationally and the dependent variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell}
and their derivatives possibly appear inside radicals.
We may assume that
F_{i}
do not have any denominators.
Otherwise we consider their numerators
\num(F_{i})
and we assume that the solutions are not zeros of the denominators.
Our goal is to find, if it exists, a rational change of variables of the form

(1.3)

z_{1}=r_{1}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}),\ldots,z_{n}=r_{n}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})

such that the new equations

(1.4)

G_{i}(x,w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}):=F_{i}(x,r_{1}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}),\ldots,r_{n}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}))=0

have rational expressions in
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
and polynomials in
x
as coefficients.
The denominator of
G_{i}
is a multiple of the denominators of
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
.
When speaking about a solution of
G_{i}=0
, we again consider

\num(G_{i})(x,w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})=0

and we assume that the denominator of
G_{i}
does not vanish when substituting in the solution.

The paper is structured as follows.
The next section describes the algebraic preliminaries on radical varieties that will be used later.
Section
3
describes the results on systems of ordinary differential equations.
Section
4
deals with special cases of the given system.
In Section
5
the process for transforming the differential equation, with radicals, into a system of ADEs is summarized in a procedure.
In the situations described in Section
4
, this procedure is indeed algorithmic.
We underline this by giving some examples.
Section
6
treats the case of systems of partial differential equations.

2.
Preliminaries on Radical Varieties

In this section we recall some results on radical varieties; for further details see
[
19
]
.
For the basic notions and results on algebraic geometry, which are fundamental here, see for example
[
7
]
.

We will express tuples of variables or functions by using bars such as
\bar{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
.
Let
\mathbb{F}_{m}
and
\delta_{i}
be defined as in (
1.1
).
A radical parametrization of (
1.1
) is a tuple

\mathcal{P}=(P_{1}(\bar{z}),\ldots,P_{k}(\bar{z}))

of elements of
\mathbb{F}_{m}
whose Jacobian has rank
n
.
For a suitable election of branches we obtain a (usually non-rational) function with domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and whose image is Zariski dense in an
n
-dimensional variety.
The Zariski closure of the image
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{P})
is the (irreducible) radical variety determined by
\mathcal{P}
and will be denoted by
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})

[
19
, Theorem 3.11 (iii)]
.

The function
\mathcal{P}
can be factored as
R\circ\psi
, where
\psi(\bar{w})=(\bar{w},\bar{\delta}(\bar{w}))
,
\bar{\delta}
are the radicals in the construction of the tower (
1.1
), and
R
is a rational function.
Another relevant algebraic variety in our construction is the tower variety of
\mathcal{P}
, defined as the Zariski closure of
\mathrm{Im}(\psi)
, denoted by
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
.
It is also irreducible of dimension
n
.
Therefore, the map
R:\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})\dashrightarrow\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
is rational.
The tower variety contains useful information about
\mathcal{P}
and its properties are essential in this work.
One more ingredient is an incidence variety, the Zariski closure of the map
\bar{z}\mapsto(\bar{z},\bar{\delta},\bar{q})
, where
\bar{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})
represents the coordinates of the points in
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
, that we denote as
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})
.
This provides the projections
\pi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})\dashrightarrow\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\pi^{*}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})\dashrightarrow\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
.
In
[
19
]
, generators of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})
are given and hence, using the Closure theorem (see
[
7
]
), one may compute generators of the varieties
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
.
We define the tracing index of
\mathcal{P}
as the degree of the map
\pi
, that is the cardinality of the generic fiber of
\pi
.
The calculation of the tracing index is described in
[
19
]
.
The case where the tracing index is equal to one is of particular interest, because in this case
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
are birationally equivalent
[
19
, Theorem 4.11]
.

3.
Differential Equations

In this section, we will use some of the notation introduced previously.
In particular,
\mathbb{F}_{m}
is the last field of a radical tower over
\mathbb{C}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
, introduced in (
1.1
), and
\bar{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m})
is the tuple of algebraic elements used in the construction of the tower.
We also consider the system of differential equations introduced in (
1.2
).
After the change of variables

\bar{z}=\bar{r}(\bar{w})=(\bar{r}_{1}(\bar{w}),\ldots,\bar{r}_{\ell}(\bar{w})),

where

\bar{r}_{i}=(r_{i,0}(\bar{w}),\ldots,r_{i,n_{i}}(\bar{w})),

we consider
G_{i}(x,\bar{w})=F_{i}(x,\bar{r}(\bar{w}))
as in (
1.4
).
By a change of variable we allow mappings
\bar{r}(\bar{w})
whose Jacobian is generically non-singular, so that, via the Inverse Function Theorem, the transformation can be inverted.
Recall that
G_{i}=0
is an algebraic equation depending on
x,\bar{w}
and no derivatives, and involves fractional expressions in
\bar{w}
.
The differential relations among the variables are kept by additionally imposing, for
1\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq j<n_{i}
, the equations

r_{i,j+1}(\bar{w})=J_{r_{i,j}}(\bar{w})\cdot(\bar{w}^{\prime})^{T}=J_{r_{i,j}}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\cdot(w_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,w_{n}^{\prime})^{T}

where
J_{r_{i,j}}
denotes the Jacobian of
r_{i,j}
.
Then we obtain the differential system

(3.1)

\mathcal{G}=\begin{cases}G_{1}(x,\bar{w})=0,\ldots,G_{N}(x,\bar{w})=0\\
r_{i,j+1}(\bar{w})=J_{r_{i,j}}(\bar{w})\cdot(\bar{w}^{\prime})^{T},\ \ 1\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq j<n_{i}\end{cases}

Remark 3.1
.

The algebraic equations
G_{i}(x,\bar{w})=0
, and the differential equations
r_{j+1}(\bar{w})=J_{r_{j}}(\bar{w})\cdot(\bar{w}^{\prime})^{T}
in (
3.1
) might have denominators.
In this case, all denominators depend only on
\bar{w}
and are independent of
x
.
Moreover, the denominator of
G_{i}
arises from the change of variables in the
F_{i}
and hence, it is a product of powers of the denominators of the
r_{i,j}
.
Consequently, for a given function
\bar{w}(x)
, if the substitution
r_{j}(\bar{w}(x))
is well–defined for every
0\leq j\leq n
, then the denominator of
G_{i}(x,\bar{w}(x))
is non-zero as well.

Remark 3.2
.

After possibly clearing denominators in (
3.1
), the system
\mathcal{G}
might be simplified by various differential elimination methods such as the Thomas decomposition or differential regular chains.
For a description of these two methods see
[
12
,
3
]
.

Remark 3.3
.

For given
\mathcal{F}
as in (
1.2
), an alternative approach to find a system of ADEs would be to directly replace every radical expression by a new variable.
More precisely, let
\bar{H}(\bar{z})
be the tuple of non-rational coefficients of the
F(x,\bar{z})\in\mathcal{F}
w.r.t.
x
and set
\bar{w}:=\bar{H}(\bar{z})
.
Let
G_{i}(x,\bar{z},\bar{w})
be the resulting ADEs after replacing
\bar{H}(\bar{z})
by
\bar{w}
.
Additionally, by taking suitable powers, the equations
\bar{w}=\bar{H}(\bar{z})
can be transformed into algebraic equations of the form
\bar{T}(\bar{z},\bar{w})=0
.
Then

\mathcal{G}=\{G_{1}(x,\bar{z},\bar{w}),\ldots,G_{N}(x,\bar{z},\bar{w}),\bar{T}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\}

is a system of ADEs.
An obstacle with this approach is that the number of variables increases from
n
to
n+\#(\bar{H})
.
In the end, we want to solve
\mathcal{G}
only for
\bar{z}
.
For this purpose, it would be reasonable to apply a differential elimination method to
\mathcal{G}
(with respect to an ordering where the
z_{i}
are ranked lower than the
w_{i}
).
In most examples, however, this elimination step does not terminate in reasonable time.

In contrast to this more direct approach, we use parametric expressions of the
\bar{w}=\bar{H}(\bar{z})
in order to keep the number of variables.
In this way, the resulting system (
3.1
) does not involve more variables than the original system
\mathcal{F}
.

The next theorem states sufficient conditions of
\bar{r}(\bar{w})
such that (
3.1
) is a system of AODEs.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)
be as in (
1.2
), let
\bar{r}(\bar{w})\in\mathbb{C}(\bar{w})^{n}
be a rational change of variables, and let
\bar{H}(\bar{z})\in\mathbb{F}_{m}^{\rho}
be the tuple of non-rational (function) coefficients of the
F(x,\bar{z})\in\mathcal{F}
w.r.t.
x
, taken in any order.
The following are equivalent.

(1)

(
3.1
) is a system of AODEs.

(2)

All the components of
\bar{H}(\bar{r}(\bar{w}))
are rational.

Proof.

“(1)
\implies
(2)”: Since (
3.1
) is a system of AODEs, in particular, the
G_{i}(x,\bar{w})
are algebraic.
Thus
F_{i}(x,\bar{r}(\bar{w}))=0
is an algebraic equation for every
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
.
So its coefficients, and in particular
\bar{H}(\bar{r}(\bar{w}))
, are rational functions.

“(2)
\implies
(1)”: After the substitution
\bar{z}=\bar{r}(\bar{w})
, every
F_{i}(x,\bar{r}(\bar{w}))
has coefficients which are rational in
\bar{w}
and polynomial in
x
, so
G_{i}(x,\bar{w})
is algebraic.
Moreover, since
\bar{r}
is a tuple of rational functions, the remaining equations of (
3.1
) are AODEs.
∎

In the sequel we associate to the differential system (
1.2
) a radical parametrization, namely

(3.2)

\mathcal{P}=(\bar{z},\bar{H}(\bar{z}))

with
\bar{H}
as in Theorem
3.4
, and we consider its associated radical variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
as well as its tower variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
, see Section
2
; observe that, as required, the rank of the Jacobian of
\mathcal{P}
is
n
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
be as above.
Then the following statements hold.

(1)

\dim(\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T}))=\dim(\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P}))=n
.

(2)

If
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
is unirational, then
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
is unirational.
Moreover, if the tracing index of
\mathcal{P}
is one and
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
is rational, then
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
is rational.

Proof.

By Theorem 3.11(iii) and Theorem 4.6 in
[
19
]
, the dimension of both,
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
, is equal to
n
.
The second item follows from Theorem 4.11 in the same reference.
∎

In the case where
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
is a unirational curve or surface, by Lemma
3.5
,
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
is unirational and, by Lüroth’s Theorem for curves and surfaces,
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
are rational.
Indeed, there exist algorithmic approaches to compute such birational parametrizations
[
21
,
14
]
.
In Lemma
3.5
we have shown that the dimension of the tower variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P})
is equal to
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{\ell}+\ell
, where
\ell
is the number of differential indeterminates and
n_{i}
is the order with respect to
y_{i}
, but as we will show in Section
5
, in various situations the dimension can be reduced and the case where these varieties are curves or surfaces might occur.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be as in (
1.2
),
\mathcal{G}
be as in (
3.1
) and let
\mathcal{Q}(\bar{w})=(\bar{r}(\bar{w}),\bar{\delta}(\bar{r}(\bar{w})))
be a rational parametrization of
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
.
It holds that

(1)

For every solution
\bar{w}(x)
of
\mathcal{G}
,

(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}(x)):=(r_{1,0}(\bar{w}(x)),\ldots,r_{\ell,0}(\bar{w}(x)))

is a solution of the differential system
\mathcal{F}
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{Q}
is invertible,
(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}(x))
is a solution of
\mathcal{F}
,

\bar{w}(x):=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x),\bar{\delta}(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x)))

and
\mathcal{Q}(\bar{w}(x))
are well–defined, then
\bar{w}(x)
is a solution of
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Using the differential equations in
\mathcal{G}
, one gets for
1\leq i\leq\ell,1\leq j<n_{i}
that
r_{i,j}(\bar{w}(x))
is the
j
-th derivative of
r_{i,0}(\bar{w}(x))
.
So,
\bar{r}(\bar{w}(x))=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x))
.
Moreover, for every
1\leq k\leq N
, since
\num(G_{k})(x,\bar{w}(x))=0
, and, by Remark
3.1
, the denominator of
G_{k}
specialized at
(x,\bar{w}(x))
is non-zero, so

F_{k}(x,\bar{r}(\bar{w}(x)))=G_{k}(x,\bar{w}(x))=0.

Conversely, let
(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}(x))
be a solution of
\mathcal{F}
, and let
T_{1},\ldots,T_{t}
be generators of the tower variety; that is,
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
is the zero-set of
\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{t}\}
.
Let
\bar{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
and
\bar{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
be such that
(\bar{u},\bar{v})\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
and such that
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(\bar{u},\bar{v}))
is well–defined.
Then,

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(\bar{u},\bar{v}))=(\bar{u},\bar{v})\,\,\mod\,\,I(\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})).

In particular,

\bar{r}(\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(\bar{u},\bar{v}))=\bar{u}\,\,\mod\,\,I(\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})).

Therefore, there exist polynomials
M_{i,1},\ldots,M_{i,t}
, for
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, such that

\displaystyle\bar{r}(\bar{w}(x))

\displaystyle=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x))

\displaystyle+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{t}M_{0,j}(\bar{\sigma}(x))\,T_{j}(\bar{\sigma}(x)),\ldots,\sum_{j=1}^{t}M_{n,j}(\bar{\sigma}(x))\,T_{j}(\bar{\sigma}(x))\right).

where
\bar{\sigma}(x):=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x),\bar{\delta}(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x)))
.
Furthermore,
\bar{\sigma}(x)=\psi(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x))\in\mathrm{Im}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
for all
x
.
Thus,
T_{i}(\bar{\sigma}(x))=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, and hence

\bar{r}(\bar{w}(x))=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x)).

In this situation, we first observe that since
\mathcal{Q}(\bar{w}(x))
is well–defined, then the denominators of
\mathcal{Q}
do not vanish at
\bar{w}(x)
and, by Remark
3.1
,
G_{k}(x,\bar{w}(x))
is also well–defined for every
1\leq k\leq N
.
In addition,

G_{k}(x,\bar{w}(x))=F_{k}(x,\bar{r}(\bar{w}(x)))=F_{k}(x,y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x))=0.

Now, clearly
\num(G_{k})(x,\bar{w}(x))=0
.
The remaining equations in
\mathcal{G}
hold by the chain rule applied to the components of
\bar{r}_{i}(\bar{w}(x))
, i.e. for
1\leq i\leq\ell
,
0\leq j<n_{i}

r_{i,j+1}(\bar{w}(x))=\frac{d}{dx}(y_{i}^{(j)}(x))=\frac{d}{dx}(r_{i,j}(\bar{w}(x)))=J_{r_{i,j}}(\bar{w}(x))\cdot(\bar{w}^{\prime}(x))^{T}.

∎

Note that in Theorem
3.6
we assume that
\bar{r}(\bar{w}(x))
is well–defined.
In the case that the tower variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
admits a polynomial parametrization
\mathcal{Q}
, this is always the case.
For details on polynomial parametrizations see e.g.
[
16
,
9
]
.

In the following proposition we analyze the particular case where the system contains a single equation of the form
F(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})=0
or
F(y_{1},y_{2})=0
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be as in (
1.2
),
\mathcal{G}
be as in (
3.1
) and let
\mathcal{Q}(\bar{w})=(\bar{r}(\bar{w}),\bar{\delta}(\bar{r}(\bar{w})))
be a rational parametrization of
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
.
If
\mathcal{F}=\{F\}
consists only of an autonomous equation,
n=2
, and
\bar{w}(x)=(w_{1}(x),w_{2}(x))
is a zero of the denominator of
r_{1}(w_{1},w_{2})
or
r_{2}(w_{1},w_{2})
and of the numerator of
G
, then
\bar{w}(x)
is constant.

Proof.

Let
d_{j}\in\mathbb{C}[w_{1},w_{2}]
be the denominator of
r_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2\}
.
Since
F
is independent of
x
, also
G
and its numerator are autonomous.
Hence,
\num(G)(\bar{w}(x))=d_{j}(\bar{w}(x))=0
if and only if either
\bar{w}(x)
is constant or
\num(G),d_{j}\in\mathbb{C}[w_{1},w_{2}]
share a common component.
In the latter case, since
\denom(G)
is a multiple of the
d_{j}
, also
\num(G)
and
\denom(G)
have a common factor, which can be neglected.
∎

4.
Special Cases

In the substitution (
1.3
), the number of new variables is equal to the sum of the orders of the given differential equations in the system (
1.2
) with respect to the differential indeterminates.
In many cases the radicals in

In these cases, one may take a projection of the tower variety where the dimension is smaller than the order.
In the following, we deal with these special cases.

Special case I.
We start with the case where there exist some variables

Let

be as in Theorem

.
Let us say that

We will use the following notation:

•

\begin{array}[]{lccc}\pi_{I}:&\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+m}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}^{s+m}\\
&(\bar{u},\bar{v})&\longmapsto&(\bar{u}^{c},\bar{v})\end{array}

Observe that

and hence, by Lemma

,
\dim(\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})_{I})=s<n

Furthermore, note that for

\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{p})=\{(\bar{a}^{e}(\bar{p}),\bar{b})\,|\,\bar{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{n-s}\}.

For an

, and for

Then, if
\bar{r}(\bar{w}):=(\bar{r}^{I}(\bar{w}^{I}))^{e}(\bar{w}^{I^{*}}),
and if

(4.1)

\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}(\bar{w}^{I}):=(\bar{r}^{I}(\bar{w}^{I}),\bar{\delta}(\bar{r}^{I}(\bar{w}^{I})))

is a rational (resp. birational) parametrization of

, it holds that

(4.2)

\mathcal{Q}(\bar{w}):=\pi_{I}^{-1}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}(\bar{w}^{I}))=(\bar{r}(\bar{w}),\bar{\delta}(\bar{r}(\bar{w})))

is a rational (resp. birational) parametrization of
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
.
Now, since
\bar{H}
does not depend on
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in I^{*}}
, Theorem
3.4
can be adapted as follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)
be as in (
1.2
),
\mathcal{G}
be as in (
3.1
),
\bar{H}(\bar{z})\in\mathbb{F}_{m}^{\rho}
be the tuple of non-rational coefficients of the polynomials

w.r.t.
x
depending on the variables

with

, and let

\bar{r}(\bar{w})=(\bar{r}^{I}(\bar{w}^{I}))^{e}(\bar{w}^{I^{*}})\in\mathbb{C}(\bar{w})^{n}

be a rational change of variables. The following are equivalent.

(1)

(
3.1
) is a system of AODEs.

(2)

All the components of
\bar{H}(\bar{r}^{I}(\bar{w}^{I}))
are rational.

Moreover, Theorem
3.6
(1) can be adapted directly.
Let us analyze the applicability of Theorem

For this purpose, we assume that

)).
This is the case if and only if

)).
Moreover, for
\bar{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}),

\bar{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})

(4.3)

\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(\bar{u},\bar{v})=\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}(\bar{u}^{I},\bar{v})^{e}(\bar{u}^{I^{*}}).

In this situation, Theorem

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)
be as in (

) involving radicals in the variables

with

,

be as in (

It holds that

(1)

For every solution

of

\bar{w}(x):=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x),\bar{\delta}(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\ell}^{(n_{\ell})}(x)))

Special case II.

Now, let us deal with the case where the radicals in the differential equations in
\mathcal{F}
can be considered separately.
More precisely, let
\mathcal{F}
be as in (
1.2
), and let
\bar{H}(\bar{z})\in\mathbb{F}_{m}^{\rho}
be the tuple of non-rational (function) coefficients of
F(x,\bar{z})
w.r.t.

.
We assume that the entries of
\bar{H}
can be grouped separately in terms of the

.
That is, there exist

,
I_{i}\cap I_{j}=\emptyset
for

\bar{H}=(h_{i})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,\rho\}}
can be re-ordered as

\bar{H}=(\bar{H}^{1},\ldots,\bar{H}^{s})

where
\bar{H}^{j}
collects the entries in
\bar{H}
depending on
\{z_{d}\,|\,d\in I_{i}\}
.
Let
I_{i}^{*}=\bigcup_{j>i}I_{j}
.
Furthermore we use the notation
\bar{p}^{K}
introduced in the special case I.
In this situation, we will proceed recursively.

Firstly, let us study the initial step.
We consider the radical tower

\mathbb{T}^{1}=\{\mathbb{L}_{1}:=\mathbb{C}(\bar{z}^{I_{1}^{*}},\bar{H}^{2},\ldots,\bar{H}^{s}),\mathbb{F}_{0}=\mathbb{L}_{1}(\bar{z}^{I_{1}})\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{m_{1}}\}

where
\mathbb{F}_{i}=\mathbb{F}_{i-1}(\delta_{1i})

\delta_{1i}^{e_{1i}}=\alpha_{1i}\in\mathbb{F}_{i-1}\setminus\mathbb{L}_{1},e_{1i}\in\mathbb{N}

Let

be the tuple collecting the

\mathbb{T}^{1}
.
Then, the polynomials
F
defining (

) can be seen as polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{m_{1}}(x)
.
In addition, we consider the radical parametrization

\mathcal{P}^{1}:=(\bar{z}^{I_{1}},H^{1}(\bar{z}^{I_{1}}))

as well as the radical variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P}^{1})
and its associated tower variety

\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T}^{1})\subset\mathbb{C}^{\#(I_{1})+m_{1}}.

By Lemma
3.5
, we have that
\dim(\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T}^{1}))=\dim(\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{P}^{1}))=\#(I^{1})
.
Now, let us assume that
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T}^{1})
is rational, and let
\bar{r}^{I_{1}}(\bar{w}^{I_{1}})
be the corresponding rational change of variables.
Then we consider the associated system (
3.1
) where
r_{k,d}
for

are the dependent variables not treated yet in the recursive process.

For

and after

\mathbb{T}^{k}=\{\mathbb{L}_{k}:=\mathbb{C}(\bar{z}^{I_{k}^{*}},\bar{H}^{k+1},\ldots,\bar{H}^{s}),\mathbb{F}_{0}=\mathbb{L}_{k}(\bar{z}^{I_{k}})\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{m_{k}}\}

\mathbb{F}_{i}=\mathbb{F}_{i-1}(\delta_{ki})

Again, the radical variety

For a rational change of variables

Eventually, if all tower varieties

and Theorem

Note that by the structure of the radicals in case II, the change of variables can be performed also in parallel.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that special case I can be seen as a particular instance of special case II:
In the notation from above, let us define
I_{1}
as the index set of variables

which occur in radical expressions in
\mathcal{F}
and set

.
Then the radical tower
\mathbb{T}^{2}

5.
Algorithmic Treatment

The previous ideas are in principle algorithmic, except that for arbitrary dimension it cannot be decided whether the tower variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
is rational or not.
Therefore, we present the following procedure in a non-algorithmic way.
In some special situations, however, the procedure is indeed an algorithm.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a system of ordinary differential equations as in (
1.2
).

(1)

Collect in the tuple
\bar{H}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
all radical expressions in (
1.2
).

(2)

If it exists, compute a partition
I_{1},\ldots,I_{s}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\bar{H}=(\bar{H}^{1},\ldots,\bar{H}^{s})
such that the radicals are separated (cf. Special case II in Section
4
).

(3)

For every tower variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T}^{k})
, compute a rational parametrization
\mathcal{Q}_{k}(\bar{r}^{I_{k}},\bar{\delta}^{I_{k}}(\bar{r}^{I_{k}}))
if possible.

(4)

Perform the change of variables
\bar{z}=\bar{r}(\bar{w})
in order to obtain a system of AODEs
\mathcal{G}
as in (
3.1
).

Remark 5.1
.

In the case that all the dimensions of the tower varieties
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T}^{k})
, are either one or two, namely when the varieties
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T}^{k})
define a curve or surface, it can be decided whether a rational parametrization
\mathcal{Q}
exists.
In the affirmative case, compute such a proper (i.e. invertible) rational parametrization (see
[
21
,
14
]
).
In this way, the above procedure is indeed an algorithm for this special situation.

Remark 5.2
.

Usually, one is interested in working, if possible, with real solutions.
With the method described in this paper, if the system
\mathcal{G}
has real solutions, and the tower variety can be parametrized over the field of the real numbers, then the deduced solutions of the system
\mathcal{F}
are also real.
If the tower variety is a curve, there exist algorithms to either directly compute a real rational parametrization (see
[
20
]
) or to reparametrize complex parametrizations into real parametrizations (see
[
10
]
).
If the tower variety is a surface the situation is not so complete as in the case of curves, but still there exist algorithms to compute real parametrizations for certain types of surfaces (see e.g.
[
2
,
1
,
13
,
15
]
).

We finish this section illustrating the previous ideas by some examples.

Example 5.3
.

We consider the ordinary differential equation

F:=\sqrt{y\!\left(x\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{d}{dx}y\!\left(x\right)\right)^{2}}+y\!\left(x\right)^{5}=0.

First, we try to solve the equation using the command
dsolve
of the computer algebra system Maple 2023. Maple provides huge implicit expressions for the solutions, involving integrals, that look hard to be used; we omit here the output.

Let us apply the method developed in the paper. The radical in the equation in

\sqrt{z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}}
and the corresponding radical parametrization is

\mathcal{P}(z_{1},z_{2})=\left(z_{1},z_{2},\sqrt{z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}}\right).

The tower variety is

\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})=\left\{(u_{1},u_{2},\Delta)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\,|\,\Delta^{2}-u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}=0\right\}

which is a rational surface that can be parametrized as

\mathcal{Q}(w_{1},w_{2})=\left(\frac{w_{2}\left(w_{1}^{2}-1\right)}{w_{1}^{2}+1},\frac{2w_{2}w_{1}}{w_{1}^{2}+1},w_{2}\right).

So, the differential system (
3.1
), associated to the equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
via
\mathcal{Q}
, is

\left.\begin{array}[]{r}w_{2}\!\left(x\right)^{3}\left(w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{10}w_{2}\!\left(x\right)^{2}+w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{10}-5w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{8}w_{2}\!\left(x\right)^{2}+5w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{8}\right.\\
\left.+10w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{6}w_{2}\!\left(x\right)^{2}+10w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{6}-10w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{4}w_{2}\!\left(x\right)^{2}+10w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{4}\right.\\
\left.+5w_{2}\!\left(x\right)^{2}w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{2}+5w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{2}-w_{2}\!\left(x\right)^{2}+1\right)=0\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr-w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{4}\left(\frac{d}{dx}w_{2}\!\left(x\right)\right)+2w_{1}\!\left(x\right)^{3}w_{2}\!\left(x\right)-4w_{2}\!\left(x\right)w_{1}\!\left(x\right)\left(\frac{d}{dx}w_{1}\!\left(x\right)\right)\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr+2w_{2}\!\left(x\right)w_{1}\!\left(x\right)+\frac{d}{dx}w_{2}\!\left(x\right)=0\end{array}\right\}

Now, applying the Maple command
dsolve
to the system we get the solutions

(\pm i,0),(\pm 1,0),(w_{1}(x),0),(0,\pm 1),\left(-\tan\!\left(\dfrac{x}{3}+\dfrac{c_{1}}{3}\right),\pm b(x)\right)

where

b(x)=\frac{\left(\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x}{3}+\frac{c_{1}}{3}\right)\right)\sqrt{-\left(\sec^{2}\left(\frac{x}{3}+\frac{c_{1}}{3}\right)\right)\left(\sec^{2}\left(\frac{x}{3}+\frac{c_{1}}{3}\right)-2\right)}}{{\left(2\left(\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x}{3}+\frac{c_{1}}{3}\right)\right)-1\right)}^{3}}.

Let

r(w_{1},w_{2})=\dfrac{w_{2}\left(w_{1}^{2}-1\right)}{w_{1}^{2}+1}.

Then, for each solution above, if the substitution in
r
is well–defined, we get a solution of the initial equation. More precisely

\begin{array}[]{ccc}(w_{1}(x),w_{2}(x))&&y(x)\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr\hline\cr\vskip 2.84526pt\cr(\pm\imath,0)&\longrightarrow&\text{no solution}\\
(\pm 1,0)&\longrightarrow&0\\
(w_{1}(x),0)&\longrightarrow&0\\
(0,\pm 1)&\longrightarrow&0\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr\left(-\tan\!\left(x+c_{1}\right),b(x)\right)&\longrightarrow&-\dfrac{1}{{\left(2\left(\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x}{3}+\frac{c_{1}}{3}\right)\right)-1\right)}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\end{array}

The solution

\left(-\tan\!\left(x+c_{1}\right),-b(x)\right)

of (
3.1
) provides the solution

y(x)=\frac{1}{{\left(2\left(\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x}{3}+\frac{c_{1}}{3}\right)\right)-1\right)}^{\frac{3}{2}}}

of the conjugated equation

-\sqrt{y\!\left(x\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{d}{dx}y\!\left(x\right)\right)^{2}}+y\!\left(x\right)^{5}=0.

.

Example 5.5
.

Let us consider the Thomas-Fermi equation

\sqrt{x}\,y^{\prime\prime}-y^{3/2}=0.

By the methods from
[
6
]
, the given differential equation gets transformed by
x=z^{2}
into the differential equation

F=z\,Y^{\prime\prime}-Y^{\prime}-4z^{2}\,Y^{3/2}=0.

The only radical expression in
F
is
Y^{1/2}
(special case I), leading to a tower variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T})
implicitly defined by the polynomial
\Delta^{2}-Y
and parametrized by
(Y,\Delta):=(W^{2},W)
.
By using the transformation
Y=W^{2}
, and directly set
Y^{\prime}=2WW^{\prime},Y^{\prime\prime}=2(WW^{\prime\prime}+W^{\prime 2})
, the above equation is equivalent to

G=z\,W\,W^{\prime\prime}+z\,W^{\prime 2}-W\,W^{\prime}-2z^{2}\,W^{3}=0.

By using the Newton polygon method
[
5
]
, we obtain for the given initial value
y(0)=W(0)=0
the solution family

W(z)=cz+\frac{c^{2}z^{5}}{12}+\frac{c^{3}z^{9}}{360}+\mathcal{O}(z^{13}),

where
c>0
is an arbitrary constant, and hence,

y(x)=\sqrt{W(\sqrt{x})}=\sqrt{c}\sqrt{x}+\frac{c^{3/2}x^{9/2}}{24}+\frac{c^{5/2}x^{17/2}}{1920}+\mathcal{O}(x^{25/2}).

For given
c>0
, the terms of order
25/2
are uniquely given and no field extension of the coefficients is necessary anymore.
Hence, existence and uniqueness of the solution is guaranteed and
y(x)
is a convergent Puiseux series in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{c})((x^{1/2}))
.

Let us note that for the frequently used initial values
y(0)=1,y(\infty)=0
there is no formal Puiseux series solution and other solution methods as the Newton polygon method have to be used for finding solutions of
G=0
and hence, of the Thomas-Fermi equation itself.

6.
Partial Differential Equations

In this section we treat systems of partial differential equations
\mathcal{F}
in the differential indeterminates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}
.
More precisely, let
\mathcal{F}
consist of differential equations of order at most
s
and possibly involving radicals in
\Theta(\alpha)y_{k}:=\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}y_{k}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{q}^{\alpha_{q}}}
with
1\leq k\leq\ell
,
|\alpha|\leq s
.
Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
enumerate the
\Theta(\alpha)y_{k}
and consider a radical tower (
1.1
).
We assume that
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{F}_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})
.

We are looking for a change of variables (
1.3
) such that
F(\bar{x},\bar{r}(\bar{w}))
is an algebraic equation for every
F
in
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
z_{i}=\Theta(\alpha)y_{k},z_{j}=\Theta(\alpha+e_{p})y_{k}
for some
|\alpha|<s
and a unit vector
e_{p}
which is one exactly at the
p
-th position.
The differential relations are kept by additionally imposing all such

(6.1)

\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\,r_{i}}{\partial w_{t}}(\bar{w})\cdot\frac{\partial\,w_{t}}{\partial x_{p}}=r_{j}(\bar{w}).

In this way, Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
hold in the expected way as for the case of ODEs (where
q=1
).
Let us illustrate this in the following example.

Example 6.1
.

Let us consider the system of partial differential equations

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\begin{aligned} F_{1}&=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt{z_{1}-1}+z_{3}}+x_{1}\,(z_{1}+\sqrt{z_{5}})=0,\\
F_{2}&=\sqrt{z_{2}+1}^{3}+x_{2}^{2}\,z_{1}z_{4}=0,\\
F_{3}&=x_{1}\,\sqrt{z_{1}z_{4}-z_{2}z_{3}}+x_{2}\,z_{5}^{2}-1=0\end{aligned}\right.

where
(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4},z_{5})=(y_{1},\frac{\partial\,y_{1}}{\partial x_{1}},\frac{\partial\,y_{1}}{\partial x_{2}},\frac{\partial^{2}\,y_{1}}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}},y_{2})
.
The radical coefficients are
collected in

\bar{H}=\left(\sqrt[3]{\sqrt{z_{1}-1}+z_{3}},\sqrt{z_{5}},\sqrt{z_{2}+1}^{3},\sqrt{z_{1}z_{4}-z_{2}z_{3}}\right).

We set
I_{1}=\{1,2,3,4\}
and
I_{2}=\{5\}
.
The tower variety
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{T}^{1})
is given by
