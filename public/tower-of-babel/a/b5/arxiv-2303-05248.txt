Primordial black hole isocurvature modes from non-Gaussianity
Sam Young

Abstract
Primordial black holes (PBHs) are black holes that might have formed in high density regions in the early universe. The presence of local-type non-Gaussianity can lead to large-scale fluctuations in the PBH formation rate. If PBHs make up a non-negligible fraction of dark matter, these fluctuations can appear as isocurvature modes, and be used to constrain the amplitude of non-Gaussianity. Assuming that the parameters of non-Gaussianity are constant over all scales, we build upon the results of previous work by extending the calculation to include peaks theory and making use of the compaction C C for the formation criteria, accounting for non-linearities between C C and the curvature perturbation ζ \zeta . For quadratic models of non-Gaussianity, our updated calculation gives constraints that are largely unaltered compared to those previously found, while for cubic models the constraints worsen significantly. In case all of the DM is made up of PBHs, the parameters of non-Gaussianity are − 2.9 ⋅ 10 − 4 < f < 3.8 ⋅ 10 − 4 -2.9\cdot 10^{-4}<f<3.8\cdot 10^{-4} and − 1.5 ⋅ 10 − 3 < g < 1.9 ⋅ 10 − 3 -1.5\cdot 10^{-3}<g<1.9\cdot 10^{-3} for quadratic and cubic models respectively.

2
Non-Gaussianity and primordial black holes

2.1
Non-Gaussianity

Primordial fluctuations created during inflation are predicted and observed to follow a distribution that is very close to Gaussian on the CMB scales
[
48
]
.
However, many models of inflation predict the emergence of some levels of non-Gaussianity (see e.g. ref.
[
49
]
).
The detection of Non-Gaussianity therefore offers important insights into the workings of inflation.
The presence of even small amounts of primordial non-Gaussianity can also have a large impact on the amount of PBHs that are formed
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
.

The curvature perturbation
\zeta
appears as a perturbative quantity in the FLRW metric

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-c^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\mathrm{e}^{2\zeta}a^{2}\left({\mathrm{d}x^{2}+\mathrm{d}y^{2}+\mathrm{d}z^{2}}\right),

(2.1)

with
a
the scale factor.
Local-type non-Gaussianity can be modeled by expanding the curvature perturbation as a polynomial of a Gaussian distributed curvature perturbation
\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}

\displaystyle\zeta=\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}+f\left({\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\right)+g\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{3}+\mathcal{O}\left({\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{4}}\right),

(2.2)

where
f=3f_{\mathrm{NL,local}}/5
and
g=9g_{\mathrm{NL,local}}/25
describe the amplitude of non-Gaussianity.
In eq. (
2.2
) the variance
\sigma^{2}=\left<{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{2}}\right>
is subtracted to ensure that the average value
\left<{\zeta}\right>
vanishes.
Because the abundance of PBHs is sensitive to non-Gaussianity, it is important to take non-Gaussianity into account when calculating PBH abundances.
However, as we will see later, this can also result in strong constraints on the non-Gaussianity parameters if PBHs make up a non-negligible fraction of dark matter.

2.2
Primordial black hole formation and the effect of non-Gaussianity

PBHs do not form at a single time and the PBH density parameter can be expressed as
[
37
,
50
]

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{PBH}}=\int\limits_{M_{\mathrm{min}}}^{M_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}\ln(M_{\mathrm{H}})\,\left({\frac{M_{\mathrm{eq}}}{M_{\mathrm{H}}}}\right)^{1/2}\beta(M_{\mathrm{H}}),

(2.3)

with
M_{\mathrm{min}}
and
M_{\mathrm{max}}
the smallest and largest horizon masses at which PBHs form and
M_{\mathrm{eq}}
the horizon mass at matter-radiation equality. Note that this equation assumes pure radiation domination right up until matter-radiation equality.
The term
\left({M_{\mathrm{eq}}/M_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)^{1/2}\propto 1/a_{\mathrm{H}}
accounts for the redshift of the PBH density parameter during radiation domination, where
a_{\mathrm{H}}
is the scale factor at the time of PBH formation.
With the horizon mass serving as a parameter of time, this integral integrates the PBH abundance over the whole period in which PBHs form.

Assuming that PBHs form over a short time-interval, parameterised by a single horizon mass, a good estimate of the PBH density parameter can be found as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{PBH}}\sim\left({\frac{M_{\mathrm{eq}}}{M_{\mathrm{H}}}}\right)^{1/2}\beta(M_{\mathrm{H}}).

(2.4)

This assumption can be valid in the case that the power spectrum peaks sharply at this scale (which will be assumed throughout this paper) — meaning that PBH formation at other scales is negligible. This also coincides with the condition that the local-type expansion, equation
2.2
, gives a valid description of the statistics of the compaction (see ref.
[
43
]
for more discussion).

We use
M_{\mathrm{eq}}=2.8\cdot 10^{17}M_{\odot}

[
37
,
51
]
and choose
M_{\mathrm{H}}=M_{\odot}
throughout this paper, and note that the final constraints on the non-Gaussianity parameters depend only very weakly on this choice (see also the discussion in ref.
[
45
]
), justifying also the approximation done in eq. (
2.4
).
Using the definition of the fraction
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}
, eq. (
1.4
), and
\Omega_{\mathrm{CDM}}=0.26

[
52
]
, we then find

\displaystyle\beta\sim\left({\frac{M_{\mathrm{\odot}}}{M_{\mathrm{eq}}}}\right)^{1/2}\Omega_{\mathrm{CDM}}f_{\mathrm{PBH}}\sim 10^{-10}f_{\mathrm{PBH}}.

(2.5)

In addition to the already mentioned effect on the total abundance of PBHs
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
, non-Gaussianity can also lead to the production of isocurvature modes in the early universe. The modal coupling which can arise as a result of the non-Gaussianity means that the amplitude of small-scale perturbations can be coupled to a long wavelength perturbation. This means that PBH formation can be enhanced (reduced) in regions where the small-scale perturbations are larger (smaller). These fluctuations in the PBH formation rate therefore appear as dark matter isocurvature perturbations. Refs.
[
45
,
53
]
calculated the amplitude of these isocurvature perturbations, and used costraints from the
Planck Collaboration

[
46
]
on isocurvature modes to find constraints on the non-Gaussianity parameters (as a function of the PBH abundance). The authors used a Press-Schechter approach, and applied the statistics of the curvature perturbation, derived from eq. (
2.2
), (see e.g. ref.
[
41
]
).

It has since been argued that using peaks theory is more suitable for calculating the PBH abundance
[
54
,
55
,
56
]
.
As with Press-Schechter theory, peaks theory assumes that PBHs form in regions where perturbations exceed a critical value, but it also introduces the condition that PBHs form in regions where the perturbations are at a maximum.
Additionally, instead of using the curvature perturbation or density contrast to describe when a region forms a PBH, refs.
[
57
,
58
]
argue that the compaction

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{x},r)\equiv 2\frac{M(\mathbf{x},r,t)-M_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x},r,t)}{R(\mathbf{x},r,t)},

(2.6)

is a more appropriate parameter use.
In the above,
M(\mathbf{x},r,t)
is the Misner-Sharp mass, with
M_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x},r,t)
its background value.
The Misner-Sharp mass gives the mass within a sphere of areal radius
R(\mathbf{x},r,t)=a(t)\exp(\zeta(\mathbf{x}))r
with spherical coordinate radius
r
, centred around position
\mathbf{x}
and evaluated at time
t
. Refs.
[
47
,
43
]
have recently considered the effect of local-type non-Gaussianity on the statistics of the compaction, and we will apply their methods here.

As with the density contrast, PBHs form in regions where the compaction exceed a critical value.
Furthermore, like the density contrast, the compaction directly measures the overabundance of mass in a region and is therefore better suited than the curvature perturbation for determining when a region collapses.
The compaction is time-independent on super-horizon scales, and can also be expressed as the time-independent component of the top-hat smoothed density contrast
\delta_{\mathrm{TH}}

[
58
]

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm{TH}}=\epsilon(t)^{2}C(\mathbf{x},r),

(2.7)

where
\epsilon=r/r_{\mathrm{H}}
, the ratio between the perturbation scale
r
and the Hubble scale
r_{\mathrm{H}}
. See ref.
[
58
]
for more discussion on the compaction and its use as the formation criterion.

In light of these developments, ref.
[
47
]
has evaluated the abundance of PBHs in the presence of local-type non-Gaussianity using peaks theory and the compaction, in addition to using the correct mass scaling in eq. (
1.1
).
In this work, we will use these same methods to calculate the PBH abundance and use these methods to update the constraints on non-Gaussianity parameters previously found in ref.
[
45
]
from the isocurvature modes.
For clarity, we will refer to the results of ref.
[
45
]
as the results from
Young and Byrnes
.

3
Compaction and peaks theory

In this section we will discuss how using the compaction, eq. (
2.6
), can be used to determine the abundance of PBHs.
We will follow the discussion in ref.
[
47
]
in this section.

3.1
Compaction

The compaction has a form similar to the density contrast.
Indeed, the compaction can be written in terms of the density contrast as

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{x},r)=\frac{2}{R(r,t)}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\rho_{\mathrm{b}}\delta(\mathbf{x},t),

(3.1)

where the integral of the density contrast over volume evaluates precisely the mass difference in eq. (
2.6
).
On super-horizon scales, the density contrast
\delta
is related to the curvature perturbation in real space by the non-linear relation

\displaystyle\delta(\mathbf{x},t)=-\frac{2(1+w)}{5+3w}\left({\frac{1}{aH}}\right)^{2}e^{-2\zeta(\mathbf{x})}\left({\nabla^{2}\zeta(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\zeta(\mathbf{x}))^{2}}\right).

(3.2)

We shall take the equation of state parameter to be
w=1/3
for the radiation dominated regime, and will take the high peak limit throughout.
The high-peak limit assumes that PBHs form in the high positive tail of the density distribution (or correspondingly, that peaks in the density fluctuation must have a high amplitude for PBH formation).
In the high peak limit, perturbations can be approximated as spherically symmetric
[
54
]
, and we find

\displaystyle\delta(r,t)=-\frac{4}{9}\left({\frac{1}{aH}}\right)^{2}e^{-2\zeta(r)}\left({\zeta^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{2}{r}\zeta^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{\prime}(r)^{2}}\right),

(3.3)

where
\zeta^{\prime}\equiv\mathrm{d}\zeta/\mathrm{d}r
.
Eq. (
3.1
) then becomes

\displaystyle C=\frac{8\pi}{R(r,t)}\rho_{\mathrm{b}}\int_{0}^{R}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{R}\,\widetilde{R}(r,t)^{2}\delta(r,t)=-\frac{4}{3}r\zeta^{\prime}(r)\left({1+\frac{1}{2}r\zeta^{\prime}(r)}\right),

(3.4)

having used
R=a\exp(\zeta)r
.
Taking the linear component
C_{1}=-4r\zeta^{\prime}(r)/3
, this gives the relation

\displaystyle C=C_{1}-\frac{3}{8}C_{1}^{2}.

(3.5)

This quadratic equation is sketched in figure
2
and has a maximum at
C_{1}=4/3\equiv C_{\mathrm{1,to}}
, which we define as a turnover point, denoted by the subscript ‘to’.
The corresponding value of
C
is
C_{\mathrm{to}}\equiv C(C_{\mathrm{1,to}})=2/3
.
Perturbations with
C_{1}<C_{\mathrm{1,to}}
are referred to as type I perturbations, while perturbations with
C_{1}>C_{\mathrm{1,to}}
are referred to as type II perturbations.
Type II perturbations are exponentially suppressed compared to type I perturbations as we will later see in eq. (
3.35
) and section
3.3
, and the formation mechanism of PBHs from type II perturbations is not well understood
[
47
,
59
]
.
We will therefore limit our discussion to PBHs formed from type I perturbations.

3.2
The effect of non-Gaussianity on the compaction

The effect of non-Gaussianity on the compaction can be described by consulting eq. (
2.2
) to find that in the presence of non-Gaussianity, the linear term of the compaction becomes

\displaystyle C_{1}=-\frac{4}{3}r\zeta^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{4}{3}r\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{\prime}(r)\left({1+2f\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}(r)+3g\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}(r)^{2}}\right).

(3.6)

The
-4r\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{\prime}(r)/3
term arises from the smoothing of the density contrast over a top-hat smoothing function, whilst the
\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}(r)
term arises from the surface term, corresponding to a surface smoothing function. The top-hat and surface smoothing functions are defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle W(\mathbf{x},r)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{3}{4\pi r^{3}}\theta_{\mathrm{H}}(r-x),

(3.7)

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbf{x},r)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{4\pi r^{2}}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(x-r),

(3.8)

with
\theta_{\mathrm{H}}(x)
the Heaviside step function and
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(x)
the Dirac delta function.
Their respective Fourier transforms are

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(k,r)

\displaystyle=3\frac{\sin(kr)-kr\cos(kr)}{(kr)^{3}},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{\mathrm{s}}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(kr)}{kr}.

(3.10)

We can use the smoothing functions to construct the following quantities which appear in the compaction, in eq. (
3.6
):

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{G}}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{4}{9}r^{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{y}\,\nabla^{2}\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}(\mathbf{y})W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},r)=-\frac{4}{3}r\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{\prime}(r),

(3.11)

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}(\mathbf{x})=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{y}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}(\mathbf{y})W_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},r)=\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}(r).

(3.12)

In each case, we have made the assumption of spherical symmetry for the second equality.

We also define the following correlation functions;

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{16}{81}\int_{0}^{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}k}{k}\,(kr)^{4}\widetilde{W}^{2}(k,r)k^{2n}P_{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\sigma_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}k}{k}\,\widetilde{W}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(k,r)P_{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}},

(3.14)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0r}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{4}{9}\int_{0}^{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}k}{k}\widetilde{W}(k,r)\widetilde{W}_{\mathrm{s}}(k,r)P_{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}},

(3.15)

with
P_{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}}
the power spectrum of
\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}
.

To describe the PDF of
C_{\mathrm{G}}
and
\zeta_{r}
, we define the following quantities, which are normalised to their variance,

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{C_{\mathrm{G}}}{\sigma_{0}},

(3.16)

\displaystyle\nu_{r}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\zeta_{r}}{\sigma_{r}}.

(3.17)

The PDF is then given by the two-variate Gaussian distribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\mathrm{det}(\Sigma)}}\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{Y}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y},

(3.18)

with
\mathbf{Y}=(\nu,\nu_{r})
and
\Sigma
the covariance matrix.
To diagonalise this PDF, we introduce the variable

\displaystyle z_{r}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\nu_{r}-\gamma_{0r}\nu}{\sqrt{1-\gamma_{0r}^{2}}},

(3.19)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{0r}\equiv\frac{\sigma_{0r}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}\sigma_{r}}

(3.20)

is the correlation function of
\nu
and
\nu_{r}
.
Like
\nu
and
\nu_{r}
, the parameter
z_{r}
follows a Gaussian distribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(z_{r})\mathrm{d}z_{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\gamma_{0r}^{2})}}\exp\left({-\frac{(\nu_{r}-\gamma_{0r}\nu)^{2}}{2(1-\gamma_{0r}^{2})}}\right)\mathrm{d}\nu_{r}.

(3.21)

This gives the diagonalised PDF

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y}=\mathcal{N}(\nu)\mathcal{N}(z_{r})\mathrm{d}\nu\mathrm{d}z_{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\nu^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}z_{r}^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\mathrm{d}z_{r}.

(3.22)

The high peak limit implies
\gamma_{0r}\nu\gg\sqrt{1-\gamma_{0r}^{2}}
.
This implies that the Gaussian distribution in eq. (
3.21
) has a mean that is much greater than its variance.
Such distributions can be estimated as Dirac delta functions (see figure
1
), and we therefore approximate

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(z_{r})\mathrm{d}z_{r}\approx\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\nu_{r}-\gamma_{0r}\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu_{r}.

(3.23)

Integrating the PDF over
\nu_{r}
therefore results in the substitution
\nu_{r}=\gamma_{0r}\nu
, or equivalently

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}(r)=\gamma_{0r}\frac{\sigma_{r}}{\sigma_{0}}C_{\mathrm{G}}.

(3.24)

For brevity we define
\gamma\equiv\gamma_{0r}\sigma_{r}/\sigma_{0}
.
From eq. (
3.6
) we then find

\displaystyle C_{1}=C_{\mathrm{G}}+\widetilde{f}C_{\mathrm{G}}^{2}+\widetilde{g}C_{\mathrm{G}}^{3},

(3.25)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}

\displaystyle\equiv 2\gamma f,

(3.26)

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}

\displaystyle\equiv 3\gamma^{2}g.

(3.27)

In this paper, to ensure that the local-type expansion considered is valid, we will consider only narrowly peaked power spectra, which can be well approximated by a Dirac delta function

\displaystyle P_{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}}(k)=\mathcal{A}k\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(k-k_{\mathrm{p}}),

(3.28)

with
\mathcal{A}
the amplitude.
Although such a power law is unphysical, it justifies the use of the high peak limit and thus also spherical symmetry and is an accurate approximation of the lognormal power law often used in the literature
[
58
,
47
,
60
]
.
Using this expression of the power law and eqs. (
3.13
) and (
3.15
) we can calculate

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{16}{81}(k_{\mathrm{p}}r)^{4}\widetilde{W}(k_{p},r)^{2}\mathcal{A}\approx 2.01\mathcal{A},

(3.29)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0r}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{9}(k_{\mathrm{p}}r)^{2}\widetilde{W}(k_{p},r)\widetilde{W}_{s}(k_{p},r)\mathcal{A}\approx 0.200\mathcal{A},

(3.30)

having used
r=2.74/k_{p}
, which relates the peak of the power spectrum to the amplitude scale (and maximises
\sigma_{0}
)
[
58
]
.
Using eq. (
3.20
), we then also find

\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma_{0r}\frac{\sigma_{r}}{\sigma_{0}}\approx 0.0995,

(3.31)

which is actually independent of
\sigma_{r}
.

3.3
Primordial black hole abundance in peaks theory

Young and Byrnes
used Press-Schechter theory to calculate the abundance of PBHs, which assumes that PBHs form at points where the perturbation exceeds a critical value.
Peaks theory expends on this condition by adding the requirement that PBHs form at the point where the perturbation is at a local maximum.

The number density of peaks can be found to be
[
37
]

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{pk}}(C_{\mathrm{G}})=\frac{1}{3^{3/2}(2\pi)^{2}}\left({\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{0}}}\right)^{3}\left({\frac{C_{\mathrm{G}}}{\sigma_{0}}}\right)^{3}\exp\left({\frac{-C_{\mathrm{G}}^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}}\right).

(3.32)

(a)

Figure 1
:
The PDF of a Gaussian distribution where the average value is much larger than the standard deviation. Such distributions approach Dirac delta distributions.

PBHs form in regions where the compaction exceeds a critical value
C_{\mathrm{c}}
.
The abundance of PBHs can be found as

\displaystyle\beta=(2\pi)^{3/2}r^{3}\int\mathrm{d}C_{\mathrm{G}}\,\frac{M_{\mathrm{PBH}}(C_{\mathrm{G}})}{M_{\mathrm{H}}}n_{\mathrm{pk}}(C_{\mathrm{G}}).

(3.33)

4.1
Peak-background split

Under a peak-background split, perturbations could be split into a small-scale “peak” component
\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}
and a large-scale “background” component

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}=\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}+\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}.

Up to first order in Fourier space, eq. (

\displaystyle\delta(\mathbf{k},t)=\frac{2(1+w)}{5+3w}\left({\frac{k}{aH}}\right)^{2}\zeta(\mathbf{k}).

Because large-scale fluctuations are suppressed by a factor

[
62
]

In the presence of local-type non-Gaussianity, they do however contribute to PBH formation indirectly.
The amplitude of small wavelengths will be boosted around peaks of long wavelengths, increasing the probability that the total perturbation exceeds the critical perturbation
[
45
]
(see figure
5
).
Small-scale perturbations need to be much larger than those with CMB scale wavelengths in order for a significant number of PBHs to be formed.
Following the approach in
Young and Byrnes

and analyse the effect that this has on the abundance of PBHs under quadratic and cubic expansion of non-Gaussianity.

, the quadratic term by
\mathcal{O}(\zeta_{\mathrm{l}})
, while the cubic term remains unperturbed.
Because
b
is determined from perturbations in
\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}\ll 1
, we can conclude that the quadratic term in eq. (
4.23
) has the most dominant effect on
b
, and therefore also on
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}
by eq. (
4.19
).
The same could be said for the quadratic term in eq. (
4.22
).
However, while the prefactor of the quadratic term in eq. (
4.22
) is
3g\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}
, that of the quadratic term in eq. (
4.23
) is
6g\gamma\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}
and is thus smaller by a factor
2\gamma=\mathcal{O}(0.1)
.

Because the constraints in our work relied on perturbations in
C_{1}
, while those found in
Young and Byrnes
relied on perturbations in
\zeta
, this leads to a broadening of roughly magnitude
\mathcal{O}(10)
for the constraints found in this work as we need
g
to be larger by the same order
\mathcal{O}(10)
to compensate for this smaller prefactor.
From figure
7
we can indeed see that for lower values of
g
, the constraints indeed worsen by a factor
\sim 10
.
For larger values of
g
non-linearity starts to have a more pronounced effect and constraints worsen even more.

This effect does not occur for the quadratic expansion.
The reason for that is that in a peak-background split,
\zeta
transformed as
[
45
]

\displaystyle\zeta=(1+2f\zeta_{\mathrm{l}})\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}+f\left({\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}-\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)

(4.24)

and
C_{1}
as eq. (
4.3
)

\displaystyle C_{1}=(1+2f\zeta_{\mathrm{l}})C_{\mathrm{G}}+\widetilde{f}C_{\mathrm{G}}^{2}.

(4.25)

In both cases, the perturbation lies only in the linear term with identical prefactor
2f\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}
and suppression by a factor
\gamma
is absent here.
Therefore we expect that the constraints on
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}
found in this work would be similar to those found by
Young and Byrnes
.

Symmetrisation of constraints for cubic models

To explain why the constraints for cubic expansions are more symmetric for positive and negative values of
g
, we instead need to consider that the abundance of PBHs was found by integration.
In this work, this integration was performed over
C_{\mathrm{G}}
, while in the work of
Young and Byrnes
this was done over
\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}
.
The range of integration in this work was determined from
C_{1}=C_{\mathrm{1,c}}
and
C_{1}=C_{\mathrm{1,to}}
, while that of
Young and Byrnes
was determined from
\zeta=\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}
.
This is the critical value of
\zeta
above which PBHs form, equivalent to the critical value of the density perturbation
\delta_{\mathrm{c}}
.
It was found in ref.
[
63
,
62
]
, that this takes the value
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}=1
.

Because
\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}\ll 1
, we can ignore all terms that depend on
\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}
in order to find the solutions for
\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}
and
C_{\mathrm{G}}
of eqs. (
4.22
) and (
4.23
) that correspond to these values.
We find that for a given
\zeta=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
C_{1}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the solution for
\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}
and
C_{\mathrm{G}}
are found respectively by good approximation as

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\approx\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}+g\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}^{3},

(4.26)

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle\approx C_{\mathrm{G}}+\widetilde{g}C_{\mathrm{G}}^{3}.

(4.27)

Although these equations have the same form, there is again suppression in the equation of
C_{1}
since
\widetilde{g}
is smaller than
g
by a factor
3\gamma^{2}=\mathcal{O}(0.01)
.
Therefore, for
|g|\lesssim 1
, the solution to
C_{\mathrm{G}}
will be dominated by the linear term of eq. (

), with the cubic term offering only a slight perturbation.
This means that for a given magnitude
\left|{g}\right|
, solutions for
C_{\mathrm{G}}
for
g>0
will be very close to those for
g<0
, which symmetrises the constraints.
The main difference between
g>0
and
g<0
in the constraints then comes from the different magnitude of the bounds of
b
in eq. (
4.17
) for positive and negative values of
b
, or equivalently positive and negative values of

.

Meanwhile, the cubic term of eq. (
4.26
) only depends on
g
.
Thus, for
|g|\lesssim 1
the cubic term could have a more appreciable effect on the solutions

, and solutions for
g>0
could deviate significantly from those for

, which asymmetrises the constraints.
Given that our work relies on perturbations in
C_{1}
, while that of
Young and Byrnes
relies on perturbations in
\zeta
, we find that our constraints on cubic expansions is more symmetric between positive and negative values of
g
.

The same symmetrisation can actually also be observed in the quadratic model in figure
7
, albeit with a lesser effect.
Solutions to
\zeta
and
C_{1}
for quadratic models can roughly be found by solving the equations

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\approx\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}+f\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}^{2},

(4.28)

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle\approx C_{\mathrm{G}}+\widetilde{f}C_{\mathrm{G}}^{2}.

(4.29)

Similar to before,

is smaller than

4.3.1
Constraints for higher order terms

With the knowledge of the broadening of the cubic expansion we are now also in the position to understand what the effect of adding higher order terms to the non-Gaussian expansion of
C_{1}
would be.
Following the patterns of eq. (
2.2
) and (
3.6
) we find that
\zeta
and
C_{1}
transform for an
n
-th order term as

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}+h\left({\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{n}-\left<{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{n}}\right>}\right),

(4.30)

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{G}}\left({1+nh\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{n-1}}\right),

(4.31)

with
h
the prefactor of the
n
-th order non-Gaussianity term and we ahve subtracted
\left<{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{n}}\right>
to ensure that
\left<{\zeta}\right>=0
.
Expanding
\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}=\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}+\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}
in short and long wavelength modes, Taylor expanding the resulting equations for small
\zeta_{l}
and ignoring again any terms that depend exclusively on
\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}
gives for these two equations

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}+nh\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}\left({\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}^{n-1}-\left<{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{n-1}}\right>}\right)+h\left({\zeta_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}-\left<{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{n}}\right>}\right),

(4.32)

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{G}}+n(n-1)h\gamma^{n-2}\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}C_{\mathrm{G}}^{n-1}+h^{\prime}C_{\mathrm{G}}^{n},

(4.33)

where
h^{\prime}=nh\gamma^{n-1}
.
In the first of these equations we have subtracted
\left<{\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}^{n-1}}\right>
to again ensure that
\left<{\zeta}\right>=0
, in case
n
is even.

The bias factor
b
is determined by the leading order term in
\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}
.
Having ignored all higher order terms in
\zeta_{\mathrm{l}}

\gamma^{n-2}
, the second term in eq. (
4.33
) has an increasingly less dominating effect for higher orders

As with the cubic term, the term
\gamma^{n-2}
ensures that higher order terms will be subject to more intense broadening as larger values of

This effect leads to particularly significant deviation from the constraints found using the approach of
Young and Byrnes
as the second term in eq. (
4.32

4.33

\gamma^{2-n}=\mathcal{O}(10^{n-2})
.

5
Conclusion

PBHs offer very tight constraints on non-Gaussianity even if they make up only a small portion of the DM.
Because non-Gaussianity is an important prediction of many models of inflation, PBHs are a great window to study the early universe.

Previous work by
Young and Byrnes

[
45
]
and
Tada and Yokoyama

[
53
]
considered how the local-type non-Gaussianity parameters could be constrained from isocurvature modes appearing from fluctuations in the formation rate of PBHs, if PBHs make up a non-negligible fraction of dark matter.
This was done by assuming that PBHs form in regions where the curvature perturbation exceeds a critical value and using Press-Schechter theory to calculate the abundance of PBHs.
By considering modal coupling arising from non-Gaussianity, the peak-background split was applied to calculate the isocurvature modes appearing from fluctuations in the PBH formation rate.
By then using constraints from the
Planck Collaboration

[
46
]
on isocurvature modes they were able to constrain the non-Gaussianity parameters
f=3f_{\mathrm{NL,local}}/5
and
g=9g_{\mathrm{NL,local}}/25
for a given fraction of DM that is made up of PBHs,
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}
.

In this work, we have updated these constraints by using recent developments in the field, improving the calculation of the PBH abundance, and providing more accurate results.
These include the use of peaks theory instead of Press-Schechter theory, the use of the compaction instead of the curvature perturbation to describe when PBHs form and the correct mass scaling of the PBH mass (which depends on the scale and amplitude of the perturbation which formed the PBH).
Applying again non-Gaussianity when calculating the PBH abundance and applying the peak-background split to calculate the isocurvature modes, we found updated constraints on the non-Gaussianity parameters by using again the
Planck
data
[
46
]
. Based on refs.
[
47
,
43
]
, we consider only a narrow peak in the power spectrum to be responsible for PBH formation, ensuring the validity of the local-type expansion considered. The calculation could be extended to predict the isocurvature modes for specific models, such as the curvaton model considered in
[
42
,
43
]
.

Similar to the results found by
Young and Byrnes
and
Tada and Yokoyama
, our updated calculation for quadratic models of non-Gaussianity offers tight constraints of
\left|{f}\right|\lesssim 10^{-4}
for
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}=1
, when all of the DM is made up of PBHs.
This means that the constraints earlier found by
Young and Byrnes
for quadratic models through Press-Schechter theory still provide a reasonable approximation in case the easier approach of Press-Schechter theory is desired.

For cubic models of non-Gaussianity the constraints in this work weaken significantly compared to those found by
Young and Byrnes
, however.
This is due to additional suppression of cubic order non-Gaussian terms by a factor
\gamma\sim 0.1
.
For smaller values of
g
this broadens constraints by a factor
\mathcal{O}(10)
, while for larger values of
g
the equations start to become non-linear, and constraints worsen even more.
Even so, for large values of
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}
, even cubic models of non-Gaussianity can still be tightly constrained by the updated calculation, with
\left|{g}\right|\lesssim 10^{-3}
for
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}=1
.

We emphasise that the constraints calculated in this paper assume that the local model of non-Gaussianity is accurate over a large range of scales; from the large scales visible in the CMB to the small scales at which PBHs form.
The constraints therefore apply specifically to the local-type non-Gaussianity parameters.
A local-type bispectrum peaks in the squeezed-limit, implying a strong correlation between large and small scales — giving strong constraints on the non-Gaussianity parameter. However, other bispectrum shapes (such as equilateral and orthogonal) do not peak in this limit, implying a weak(er) correlation — which would result in much weaker constraints.

In the case of models predicting varying levels of non-Gaussianity on different scales, the constraints calculated here would not be accurate. For example, if the curvaton model is considered, then the small-scale perturbations can be sourced by the curvaton, which can be decoupled from the inflaton, which sources the large-scale perturbations. This would imply a much weaker correlation between the small and large scales, and correspondingly weaker constraints on the non-Gaussianity parameters. Such constraints could be computed on a model-by-model basis using the methods presented here, although such consideration is beyond the scope of this paper.
However, the constraints on the non-Gaussianity parameters from PBH isocurvature modes could still be competitive with those available from other observations (such as the CMB) even if weaker by several orders of magnitude.

We also briefly considered the constraints on higher-order non-Gaussianity parameters.
Young and Byrnes
previously found that the isocurvature constraints applied (almost) equally at all orders of non-Gaussianity and would essentially rule out non-Gaussianity at all orders if PBHs are detected. However, we have demonstrated here that this is not the case, and that the constraints become significantly weaker as higher order terms are considered.
