A Bounded Regret Strategy for Linear Dynamics with Unknown Control
Jacob Carruth

Abstract
We consider a simple linear control problem in which a single parameter b b , describing the effect of the control variable, is unknown and must be learned. We work in the setting of agnostic control: we allow b b to be any real number and we do not assume that we have a prior belief about b b . For any fixed time horizon, we produce a strategy whose expected cost is within a constant factor of the best possible.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider a simple linear control problem with a single unknown parameter
b
. We do not assume that we are given a prior belief about
b
, and we seek to bound a quantity called the regret, which compares our expected cost to that of an opponent with perfect knowledge of
b
.

This falls within the purview of adaptive control. In the adaptive control literature, one typically seeks a control strategy that minimizes the regret as the time horizon tends to infinity. Here, we explore a variant of adaptive control called agnostic control, in which one seeks to minimize the regret over a fixed, potentially short, time horizon; see also
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

Let
q(t)
denote the position of a particle at time
t
. Given some starting position
q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the particle evolves according to the stochastic ODE

dq(t)=(aq(t)+bu(t))dt+dW(t),\qquad q(0)=q_{0}.

(1.1)

Here
a
and
b
are real numbers,
W(t)
is a standard Brownian motion (here “standard” means that
W(0)=0
and the variance of
W(t)
is
t
), and
u
is the control variable. We treat the parameter
a
as a fixed, known quantity, but we treat
b
as an unknown parameter about which we have no prior belief. We choose
u(t)
depending on the history up to time
t
, i.e.,
(q(\tau))_{0\leq\tau\leq t}
. The control
u(t)
is allowed to depend on
a
, but not on the unknown parameter
b
.

More precisely, a
strategy
is a choice of
u(t)
for every
t
and every history
(q(\tau))_{0\leq\tau\leq t}
, subject to certain technical conditions that allow us to make sense of solutions to (
1.1
) for arbitrary
b\in\mathbb{R}
(a precise definition is given in Section
3
). For a given strategy
\sigma
, we let
q^{\sigma}(t,b)
and
u^{\sigma}(t,b)
denote the corresponding solutions to (
1.1
) (note that these are random variables). Fix a time horizon
T>0
. We define the cost of
\sigma
to be the random variable

\textsc{Cost}(\sigma,b)=\int_{0}^{T}\big((q^{\sigma}(t,b))^{2}+(u^{\sigma}(t,b))^{2}\big)dt,

and we write
\textsc{ECost}(\sigma,b)
for the expected value of
\textsc{Cost}(\sigma,b)
.

For a given value of
b
, there exists a strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(b)
that minimizes the quantity
\textsc{ECost}(\sigma,b)
over all strategies
\sigma
(this is well-known, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
). We write

\textsc{ECost}_{\text{opt}}(b)=\textsc{ECost}(\sigma_{\text{opt}}(b),b).

It is helpful to think of
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(b)
as the strategy of an opponent who knows the value of
b
and plays optimally—this leads to the notion of
regret
.

We define the (multiplicative)
regret

of a strategy
\sigma
by

\text{MReg}(\sigma,b)=\frac{\textsc{ECost}(\sigma,b)}{\textsc{ECost}_{\text{opt}}(b)},

and the
worst-case regret
of
\sigma
by

\text{MReg}^{*}(\sigma)=\sup_{b\in\mathbb{R}}\text{MReg}(\sigma,b).

We seek a strategy that minimizes the
worst-case regret
, and we refer to such a strategy as an
optimal agnostic strategy
.

In
[
2
,
4
]
, we produced almost optimal agnostic strategies for the dynamics (
1.1
) in the case in which the parameter
a
is unknown and
b
is known (for simplicity, in those papers we took
b=1
). The problem considered here, in which
b
is unknown, appears to be more difficult. This is because it has the following feature: If we set
u(t)=(\Delta t)^{-1/2}
for a very short time increment
\Delta t
, then we learn a lot about the parameter
b
and only incur a finite cost. Therefore, we may want to take
u=\pm\infty
; this had better be done carefully.

In this paper, we tackle the more modest problem of exhibiting a strategy with
bounded
multiplicative regret. Specifically, we prove the following theorem

Theorem 1
.

Fix
|q_{0}|\geq 1
and
T>0
as above. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
the strategy
\emph{BR}
defined in Sections
4
–
7
satisfies

\textsc{ECost}(\emph{BR},b)\leq C\cdot\textsc{ECost}_{\emph{opt}}(b)\;\text{for any}\;b\in\mathbb{R}.

Clearly, Theorem
1
implies that

\text{MReg}^{*}(\text{BR})\leq C.

We emphasize that the constant
C
in Theorem
1
depends on
q_{0}
and
T
but is independent of the parameter
a
.

We note that our assumption that
|q_{0}|\geq 1
in Theorem
1
can be replaced by
|q_{0}|\geq q_{\text{small}}
for any
q_{\text{small}}>0
. Unfortunately, however, the resulting constant
C
in Theorem
1
then tends to infinity as
q_{\text{small}}
tends to zero. We think that with a bit of care this can be avoided, and we are currently working on producing a bounded regret strategy for small
q_{0}
.

We now give an overview of some of the ideas behind the strategy
BR
. We will assume that
q_{0}\geq 1
. By symmetry, this also defines the strategy for
q_{0}\leq-1
.

We begin with a high-level overview of our strategy. Our strategy is different in each of the cases
a>A
,
|a|\leq A
, and
a<-A
, where
A>1
is large real number.

First, we describe our strategy when
a<-A
. In this case, the position
q
of the particle will decay to zero rapidly if we just set
u=0
. This will achieve bounded regret unless
|b|\gg|a|
, in which case the system will decay to zero much faster if we set
u=\pm q
(depending on the sign of
b
). Therefore, our strategy begins by immediately testing whether
|b|
is huge. If we find that it is, then we set
u=\pm q
; if not, we set
u=0
.

Next, we describe our strategy when
|a|\leq A
. In this case, we incur a bounded expected cost by simply setting
u=0
. As in the previous case, this achieves bounded regret unless
|b|
is huge, in which case we should again set
u=\pm q
. Therefore, when
a
is bounded, our strategy is essentially the same as in the case in which
a
is large and negative.

Last, suppose that
a>A
. As in the previous two cases, we’d like to set
u=\pm q
if
|b|\gg|a|
. On the other hand, if
|b|
is extremely small, then we stand to gain little by controlling the system and we are happy setting
u=0
. For
|b|
neither huge nor tiny, we can achieve bounded regret by exercising a control whose order of magnitude is
|u|\approx\pm(a/b^{2})|q|
(again, the sign here is determined by the sign of
b
).

We thus proceed as follows. We begin by testing whether
|b|\gg a
. If we detect that it is, then we set
u=\pm q
(depending on the sign of
b
). If do not detect
|b|\gg a
, then we attempt to learn the order of magnitude of
|b|
. We accomplish this via a series of
Testing Epochs
, in which we exercise increasingly large control over extremely short successive time intervals. If during one of these Testing Epochs we register a significant change in
q
, then we have a good guess for the order of magnitude of
b
and we control the system by setting
u\approx(a/|b|)|q|
. If, on the other hand, we undergo many Testing Epochs without registering a significant change in
q
, we conclude that
|b|
is extremely small and we set
u=0
for the remainder of the game. We now discuss the Testing Epochs in a bit more detail.

We continue to assume that
a
is large and positive. Suppose that we have determined that
|b|\gg a
; our goal now is to determine the order of magnitude of
|b|
. We enter a Testing Phase, consisting of a series of Testing Epochs indexed by an integer
\nu\geq 1
. The goal of each Testing Epoch is to determine whether
|b|\approx e^{-\nu}a
. Only the first of these Testing Epochs is guaranteed to occur.

Let
\tau>0
be a small real number depending on
a
. During Testing Epoch 1, we set
u\approx e(a\tau)^{-1/2}|q|
. If we register a change in
q
on the order of magnitude of
(a\tau)^{1/2}
within time
\tau
, then with high probability
|b|\approx e^{-1}a
. Since we have a good guess for
|b|
, we exit the Testing Phase and control the system by setting
u\approx\pm(a/|b|)|q|\approx\pm e^{\nu}|q|
. If, on the other hand, we fail to register a sufficiently large change in
q
within time
\tau
, then we assume that
|b|\ll e^{-1}a
and we enter Testing Epoch 2.

If we enter Testing Epoch 2, then we set
u\approx e^{2}(a\tau)^{-1/2}|q|
. As above, if we register a change in
q
on the order of magnitude of
(a\tau)^{1/2}
within time
\tau
, then with high probability
|b|\approx e^{-2}a
. We then exit the Testing Phase and control the system by setting
u\approx\pm e^{2}|q|
. If, on the other hand, we fail to detect a significant change in
q
within time
\tau
, then we enter Testing Epoch 3.

In general, if we enter Testing Epoch
\nu
, then we set
u\approx e^{\nu}(a\tau)^{-1/2}|q|
. If we register a change in
q
on the order of magnitude of
(a\tau)^{1/2}
within time
\tau
, then with high probability
|b|\approx e^{-\nu}a
. We then exit the Testing Phase and set
u\approx\pm e^{\nu}|q|
. If, on the other hand, we fail to detect a significant change in
q
within time
\tau
, then we enter Testing Epoch
(\nu+1)
.

If we pass through
\nu^{*}
Testing Epochs for some large, positive integer
\nu^{*}
(depending on
a
) without detecting a significant change in
q
, then we conclude that
|b|\ll e^{-\nu^{*}}a
. We then achieve bounded regret by setting
u=0
for the remainder of the game.

This concludes our high-level overview of the strategy
BR
. In order to keep the discussion simple, we’ve left out some details, e.g., we haven’t said how our strategy handles rare events or how we determine the sign of
b
. For details, see Sections
5
–
7
.

We now outline the contents of the remainder of this paper. In Section
2
, we prove some preliminary lemmas about stochastic processes. In Section
3
, we establish some basic properties of strategies and of the function
\textsc{ECost}_{\text{opt}}(b)
. In Sections
4
–
7
, we prove Theorem
1
.

Remark 1

Suppose

Lemma 2

Let

dq(t)=\alpha q(t)dt+dW_{t},\qquad q(0)=Q_{0}.

For any

(A)

If

\emph{Prob}[q(t)<2Q_{0}\;\emph{forall}\;t\in[0,(\eta\alpha)^{-1}]]\leq C\exp(-c_{\eta}Q_{0}^{2}\alpha)

(ii)

\emph{Prob}[\exists t\geq 0:q(t)\leq Q_{0}/2]\leq C\exp(-cQ_{0}^{2}\alpha)

(B)

\alpha<0

(i)

\emph{Prob}[q(t)>Q_{0}/2\;\emph{forall}\;t\in[0,(\eta|\alpha|)^{-1}]]\leq C\exp(-c_{\eta}Q_{0}^{2}|\alpha|)

(ii)

\alpha\neq 0
, then

\emph{Prob}[\exists t\in[0,\eta|\alpha|^{-1}]:q(t)\geq 2Q_{0}\;\emph{OR}\;q(t)\leq Q_{0}/2]\leq C\exp(-c_{\eta}Q_{0}^{2}|\alpha|).

Proof.

We first remark that Part (B.ii) is an easy consequence of Lemma 4.3 in
[
2
]
.

Motivated by Remark
1
, we define

X(t)=e^{-\alpha t}q(t)-Q_{0},

and we recall that
X(t)
is a normal random variable with mean 0 and

\text{Var}[X(t)]=\frac{1-e^{-2\alpha t}}{2\alpha}.

(2.22)

Assume until further notice that
\alpha>0
. If
q((\eta\alpha)^{-1})<2Q_{0}
, then

X((\eta\alpha)^{-1})<e^{-1/\eta}2Q_{0}-Q_{0}<-c_{\eta}Q_{0}.

Therefore

\text{Prob}[q(t)<2Q_{0}\;\text{for all}\;t\in[0,(\eta\alpha)^{-1}]]\leq\text{Prob}[X((\eta\alpha)^{-1})<-c_{\eta}Q_{0}].

By (
2.22
),

\text{Var}[X((\eta\alpha)^{-1})]\approx\alpha^{-1}.

(2.23)

Since
X((\eta\alpha)^{-1})
is a normal random variable with mean 0, we conclude that

\text{Prob}[q(t)<2Q_{0}\;\text{for all}\;t\in[0,(\eta\alpha)^{-1}]]\leq C\exp(-c_{\eta}Q_{0}^{2}\alpha).

(2.24)

This proves (A.i)

To prove (A.ii), it suffices (e.g., by Fatou’s Lemma) to show that for any sufficiently large
\hat{T}>0
we have

\text{Prob}[\exists t\in[0,\hat{T}]:q(t)\leq Q_{0}/2]\leq C\exp(-cQ_{0}^{2}\alpha).

(2.25)

If
q(t)\leq Q_{0}/2
for some
t\in[0,\hat{T}]
, then
X(t)\leq-Q_{0}/2
. Combining this with (
2.4
) (i.e., the reflection principle for
X(t)
), we see that

\text{Prob}[\exists t\in[0,\hat{T}]:q(t)\leq Q_{0}/2]\leq 2\cdot\text{Prob}[X(\hat{T})<-Q_{0}/2].

To prove (
2.25
), we note that
\text{Var}[X(\hat{T})]\approx\alpha
and that
X(\hat{T})
is a normal random variable with mean 0.

Now observe that if
q(t)\geq 2Q_{0}
for some
t\in[0,\eta\alpha^{-1}]
, then

X(t)\geq e^{-\alpha t}2Q_{0}-Q_{0}\geq c_{\eta}Q_{0}.

By (
2.4
) (i.e., the reflection principle for
X(t)
), we have

\text{Prob}[\exists t\in[0,\eta\alpha^{-1}]:q(t)\geq 2Q_{0}]\leq 2\cdot\text{Prob}[X(\eta\alpha^{-1})\geq c_{\eta}Q_{0}].

Observe that
\text{Var}[X(\eta\alpha^{-1})]\approx_{\eta}\alpha^{-1}
; combining this with the fact that
X(\eta\alpha^{-1})
is a normal random variable with mean 0 gives

\text{Prob}[\exists t\in[0,\eta\alpha^{-1}]:q(t)\geq 2Q_{0}]\leq C\exp(-c_{\eta}Q_{0}^{2}\alpha).

(2.26)

We combine (
2.25
) and (
2.26
) to get

\begin{split}\text{Prob}[\exists t\in[0,\eta\alpha^{-1}]:(q(t)\geq 2Q_{0})\;\text{OR}\;&(q(t)\leq Q_{0}/2)]\\
&\leq C\exp(-c_{\eta}Q_{0}^{2}\alpha)\;\text{for any}\;\alpha>0.\end{split}

(2.27)

This proves (C) when
\alpha>0
.

We omit the proofs of (B.i), and (C) when
\alpha<0
; they are essentially the same as the proofs of (
2.24
) and (
2.27
) (respectively).
∎

3
Agnostic Control Strategies

We begin this section by establishing some conventions and notation:

•

We introduce a
starting position

Q_{0}\neq 0
, a
time horizon

T>0
, and a
growth parameter

a\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

Constants
c,C,C^{\prime},
etc. denote absolute constants.

•

Recall that
W(t)
denotes a copy of standard Brownian motion,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\text{Prob})
denotes the corresponding probability space, and, for any
t\geq 0
, we write
\mathcal{F}_{t}
to denote the sigma-algebra determined by the history
(W(s))_{s\in[0,t]}
.

A
strategy
(for starting position
Q_{0}
, time horizon
T
, and growth parameter
a
) is a collection of random variables
q(t,b)
and
u(t,b)
defined for all
t\in[0,T]
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following properties:

(S.1)

For every
b\in\mathbb{R}
,
q(t,b)
is a continuous function of
t
with probability 1 and
u(t,b)
is an
L^{2}
function of
t
with probability 1.

(S.2)

For every
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[0,T]
the maps
(s,\omega)\mapsto q(s,b,\omega)
and
(s,\omega)\mapsto u(s,b,\omega)
, defined on
[0,t]\times\Omega
, are measurable as functions on
[0,t]\times(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{t},\text{Prob})
. Intuitively, this means that
q,u
are determined by the past.

(S.3)

For every
b\in\mathbb{R}
,

\text{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}(q(t,b))^{2}+(u(t,b))^{2}\ dt\bigg]<\infty.

(S.4)

For almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, we have that for all
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, and for all
t\in[0,T]
, if
q(s,b,\omega)=q(s,b^{\prime},\omega)
for all
s\in[0,t]
, then
u(s,b,\omega)=u(s,b^{\prime},\omega)
for almost all
s\in[0,t]
. This tells us that
u
does not depend on the unknown
b
.

(S.5)

For every
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[0,T]
, we have

q(t,b)=Q_{0}+W(t)+\int_{0}^{t}[aq(s,b)+bu(s,b)]\ ds

with probability 1.

For a given
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we refer to
q(t,b)
and
u(t,b)
as, respectively, the
particle trajectory
and the
control variable
at time
t
.

Let
\sigma
denote an arbitrary strategy. We then write
q^{\sigma}(t,b)
,
u^{\sigma}(t,b)
for the particle trajectories and control variables associated with
\sigma
. Often, the parameter
b
is implicit and we just write
q^{\sigma}(t)
,
u^{\sigma}(t)
.

We define the
cost
of a strategy
\sigma
to be the random variable

\textsc{Cost}(\sigma,b)=\int_{0}^{T}\big((q^{\sigma}(t,b))^{2}+(u^{\sigma}(t,b))^{2}\big)\ dt.

We then define the
expected cost
of a strategy
\sigma
by

\textsc{ECost}(\sigma,b)=\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}(\sigma,b)].

Given any smooth function
v:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we can define a strategy
\tilde{\sigma}
by setting

u^{\tilde{\sigma}}(t,b)=-v(t)q^{\tilde{\sigma}}(t,b)

(note that
q^{\tilde{\sigma}}
is then determined by Property (S.5)). We refer to such a strategy
\tilde{\sigma}
as a
simple feedback strategy
with
gain function

v
.

We now establish some upper bounds on the expected cost of simple feedback strategies.

Let
\tilde{\sigma}
denote the simple feedback strategy with gain function
v
. One can show (as in
[
2
]
) that for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\textsc{ECost}(\tilde{\sigma},b)=\varphi(0,b)\cdot Q_{0}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(t,b)\ dt,

where

\varphi(t,b)=\int_{t}^{T}(1+v^{2}(\tau))\cdot\exp\bigg(2\int_{t}^{\tau}\big(\alpha-bv(s)\big)\ ds\bigg)\ d\tau.

This implies the following remark.

Remark 2
.

Let
\emph{CG}(\alpha)
denote the simple feedback strategy with constant gain function
v\equiv\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
(so that
u^{\emph{CG}(\alpha)}(t)=-\alpha\cdot(q^{\emph{CG}(\alpha)}(t))
). Then the following hold.

(A)

If
a<b\alpha
, then

\textsc{ECost}(\emph{CG}(\alpha),b)\leq\frac{(Q_{0}^{2}+T)(1+\alpha^{2})}{|a-b\alpha|}.

(B)

Let
A=\max\{|\alpha|,|a-b\alpha|\}
. Then

\textsc{ECost}(\emph{CG}(\alpha),b)\leq C_{A}(Q_{0}^{2}+T).

(C)

If
a>0
and
\alpha=0
, then

\textsc{ECost}(\emph{CG}(0),b)\leq(Q_{0}^{2}+T)\frac{e^{2aT}}{2a}\;\text{for any}\;b\in\mathbb{R}.

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we define a simple feedback strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\beta)
via the control variable

u^{\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\beta)}(t)=-\beta\cdot\kappa(T-t,\beta)\cdot q^{\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\beta)}(t),

where
\kappa:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is defined by

\kappa(t,\beta)=\kappa(t,\beta;a)=\begin{cases}t&\text{if }a=\beta=0,\\
\frac{\tanh(t\sqrt{a^{2}+\beta^{2}})}{\sqrt{a^{2}+\beta^{2}}-a\tanh(t\sqrt{a^{2}+\beta^{2}})}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

It is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
) that for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
the strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(b)
minimizes the quantity
\textsc{ECost}(\sigma,b)
among simple feedback strategies. (In fact, by a straightforward modification of an argument in
[
2
]
, one can show that
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(b)
minimizes
\textsc{ECost}(\sigma,b)
among
all
strategies.) For
b\in\mathbb{R}
we define

\textsc{ECost}_{\text{opt}}(b)=\textsc{ECost}(\sigma_{\text{opt}}(b),b).

Of course, both the strategies
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\beta)
and the quantity
\textsc{ECost}_{\text{opt}}(b)
depend on the parameters
a
,
T
,
Q_{0}
.

It is well known (again, see
[
1
]
) that
\textsc{ECost}_{\text{opt}}(b)
is of the form

\textsc{ECost}_{\text{opt}}(b)=\kappa(T,b)\cdot Q_{0}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\kappa(t,b)\ dt,

(3.2)

where
\kappa(t,b)
is defined in (
3.1
). We use (
3.2
) to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

We have the following lower bounds on
\textsc{ECost}_{\emph{opt}}(b)
.

(A)

If
a\geq 1
, then

\textsc{ECost}_{\emph{opt}}(b)\geq c_{T}\cdot Q_{0}^{2}\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{e^{2aT}}{a}&\text{for any}\;|b|\leq ae^{-aT},\\
\frac{a}{b^{2}}&\text{for any}\;ae^{-aT}\leq|b|\leq a,\\
\frac{1}{|b|}&\text{for any}\;|b|\geq a.\end{cases}

(B)

If
a\leq 1
, then

\textsc{ECost}_{\emph{opt}}(b)\geq\frac{c_{T}\cdot Q_{0}^{2}}{1+|a|+|b|}\;\text{for any}\;b\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

Observe that

\kappa(t,b)\geq 0\;\text{for any}\;t\in[0,T],

and therefore

\textsc{ECost}_{\text{opt}}(b)\geq Q_{0}^{2}\cdot\kappa(T,b).

(3.3)

It is straightforward to verify that

\kappa(T,b)>\frac{c_{T}}{1+|a|+|b|}\;\text{for any}\;a,b\in\mathbb{R};

combining this with (
3.3
) implies Part (B) of the Lemma as well as the case
|b|\geq a
in Part (A).

We now remark that

\begin{split}&\tanh(x)\geq(1-Ce^{-2x})\;\text{for any}\;x\geq 0.\end{split}

(3.4)

Assume that
a\geq 1
. Then clearly

\kappa(T,b)\geq\frac{c_{T}}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-a\tanh(T\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}})}\;\text{for any}\;b\in\mathbb{R}.

Combining this with (
3.4
) gives

\kappa(T,b)\geq\frac{c_{T}}{(b^{2}/a)+aCe^{-2T\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}}.

From this inequality and (
3.3
), we easily deduce the remaining two cases of Part (A).
∎

(for

to be absolute constants. We fix

Recall that we defined the event

as

\mathcal{E}_{\nu,m}=\{\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\text{ AND }(\mathcal{E}_{\mu.\text{ii}}\;\text{for exactly }m\text{ integers }\mu\text{ with }0\leq\mu<\nu)\}.

The purpose of the present section is to prove the following estimates:

(P.I)

For any integers
\mu,m
satisfying
2\leq\mu\leq(\nu^{*}+1)
and
0\leq m\leq\mu

\text{Prob}[\mathcal{E}_{\mu,m}]\leq C\exp(-c4^{2m}e^{\mu}|b|)\;\text{for any}\;|b|\geq ae^{-\mu+n_{0}+1}.

(P.II)

For any integers
\mu,m
satisfying

and

\text{Prob}[\mathcal{E}_{\mu,m}]\leq C\exp(-c4^{2m}a)\;\text{for any}\;|b|\leq ae^{-\mu-m_{0}+1}.

(P.III)

For any integers
\mu,m
satisfying
1\leq\mu\leq\nu^{*}
and
0\leq m\leq\mu
, we have

\text{Prob}[\mathcal{E}_{\mu.\text{ii}}\;\text{AND}\;\mathcal{E}_{\mu,m}]\leq C\exp(-c4^{2m}a)\;\text{for any}\;|b|\leq ae^{-\mu-m_{0}}.

We first prove (
P.III
). Let
1\leq\mu\leq\nu^{*}
and
0\leq m\leq\mu
. Recall that if

, i.e., if Subepoch
\mu.\text{i}

•

during Subepoch
\mu.\text{i}
.

•

we enter Subepoch

.

we enter Epoch

1
implies that

\text{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mu.\text{ii}}}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{\mu}}]\leq C\exp(-c|q(t_{\mu})|^{2}a)\;\text{for any}\;|b|\leq ae^{-\mu-m_{0}}.

5.2

P.I
). Let

Assume that

Now assume that
