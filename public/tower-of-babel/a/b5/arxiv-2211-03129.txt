Maximum size of C ≤ k C_{\leq k} -free strong digraphs with out-degree at least two
Bin Chen, Xinmin Hou, Email: cb03@ustc.edu.cn, xmhou@ustc.edu.cn

Abstract




Abstract
:
Let
\mathscr{H}
be a family of digraphs. A digraph
D
is
\mathscr{H}
-free
if it contains no isomorphic copy of any member of
\mathscr{H}
. For
k\geq 2
, we set
C_{\leq k}=\{C_{2},C_{3},\ldots,C_{k}\}
, where
C_{\ell}
is a directed cycle of length
\ell\in\{2,3,\ldots,k\}
. Let
D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
denote the family of
{C}_{\leq k}
-free
strong digraphs on
n
vertices with every vertex having out-degree at least
\xi
and in-degree at least
\zeta
, where both
\xi
and
\zeta
are positive integers. Let
\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\max\{|A(D)|:\;D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)\}
and
\Phi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\{D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta):|A(D)|=\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)\}
.
Bermond et al. (1980) verified that
\varphi_{n}^{k}(1,1)=\binom{n-k+2}{2}+k-2
. Chen and Chang (2021) showed that
\binom{n-1}{2}-2\leq\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)\leq\binom{n-1}{2}
. This upper bound was further improved to
\binom{n-1}{2}-1
by Chen and Chang (DAM, 2022), furthermore, they also gave the exact values of
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)
for
n\in\{7,8,9\}
. In this paper, we continue to determine the exact values of
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)
for
n\geq 10
, i.e.,
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)=\binom{n-1}{2}-2
for
n\geq 10
.

AMS
: 05C20; 05C35; 05C38.

Keywords
:
C_{\leq k}
-free
strong digraph, girth, Turán number, out-degree

1
Introduction

For terminology not explicitly introduced here, we refer to
[
1
]
. All digraphs considered here are finite and simple. Let
D=(V,A)
be a digraph with vertex set
V=V(D)
and arc set
A=A(D)
. The
order
(
size
) of
D
is the number of vertices (arcs) in
D
.

Given a family of digraphs
\mathscr{H}
, a digraph
D
is called
\mathscr{H}
-free
if it has no isomorphic copy of any member of
\mathscr{H}
. The
Turán number
of
\mathscr{H}
, denoted
ex(n,\mathscr{H})
, is defined to be the maximum size of
\mathscr{H}
-free digraphs on
n
vertices.

The study of
Turán-type problems
is one of the central topics in extremal graph theory and it can be traced back to the work of Turán
[
27
]
in 1941, known as Turán’s Theorem, which is a generalization of Mantel’s Theorem due to Mantel
[
21
]
in 1907. Since then, Turán-type problems have received considerable attention and have developed extremely rapidly, and a large number of classical and meaningful results for undirected graphs have been given by researchers. Brown and Harary
[
7
]
were the first to investigate such problems for digraphs, see, e.g.,
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
for details. However, only a few results have been obtained for digraphs.

A
directed path
of length
\ell
of
D
is a list
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}
of distinct vertices such that
(x_{i},x_{i+1})\in A(D)
for
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
. A digraph
D
is
strongly connected
(
strong
for short) if every two vertices are mutually reachable by directed paths. A
directed cycle
of length
\ell
in
D
is a list
y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell-1}
of distinct vertices such that
(y_{i},y_{i+1})\in A(D)
for
0\leq i\leq\ell-2
and
(y_{\ell-1},y_{0})\in A(D)
. The
girth

g(D)
of
D
is the length of the shortest cycles in
D
. All
paths
(resp.,
cycles
) considered in this paper are directed paths (resp., directed cycles).

Thomassen raised an interesting problem as follows: What is the least number
m(n,k)
such that every strong digraph with
n
vertices and size at least
m(n,k)
has a cycle of length at most
k
(see
[
1
]
, Chapter
8
, p.331 for details)?
In 1980, Bermond et al.
[
5
]
resolved the above problem by showing the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

For
n\geq k+1
and
k\geq 2
,

m(n,k)=\frac{n^{2}+(3-2k)n+k^{2}-k}{2}.

Moreover, a family of extremal digraphs with size
m(n,k)-1
containing no cycle of length at most
k
has been recursively constructed.

Let
C_{\leq k}=\{C_{2},C_{3},\ldots,C_{k}\}
, where
k\geq 2
and
C_{i}
is a cycle of length
i
for
i\in\{2,3,\ldots,k\}
. By
D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
we mean a family of
C_{\leq k}
-free
strong digraphs on
n
vertices such that every digraph
D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
satisfies
\delta^{+}(D)\geq\xi
and
\delta^{-}(D)\geq\zeta
, where both
\xi
and
\zeta
are positive integers. Let

\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\max\{|A(D)|:D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)\},

which can be viewed as the Turán number of
C_{\leq k}
in the host family of strong digraphs on
n
vertices with
\delta^{+}(D)\geq\xi
and
\delta^{-}(D)\geq\zeta
.
Denote by

\Phi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\{D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta):|A(D)|=\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)\}

and call graphs
D\in\Phi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
the extremal graphs of
C_{\leq k}
. Note that for a given collection
\{n,k,\xi,\zeta\}
, it is possible that
D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\O
and we define
\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=0
if it occurs. Clearly,
\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\varphi_{n}^{k}(\zeta,\xi)
for all collections of
\{n,k,\xi,\zeta\}
.
Theorem
1.1
tells us that

\varphi_{n}^{k}(1,1)=m(n,k)-1=\frac{n^{2}+(3-2k)n+k^{2}-k-2}{2}

for
n\geq k+1
and
k\geq 2
. For
k=2
, it is easy to see that
\Phi_{n}^{2}(1,1)=\{T_{n}\}
for
n\geq 3
, where
T_{n}
is a strong tournament on
n
vertices, and
\Phi_{n}^{2}(1,1)=\O
for
n\leq 2
.

One motivation of the study of the function
\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
comes from the following two well-known conjectures proposed by Behzad et al.
[
3
]
in 1970
and Caccetta and Häggkvist
[
10
]
in 1978.

Conjecture 1.2

(Behzad, Chartrand and Wall, 1970,
[
3
]
)
.

Every
r
-regular digraph on
n
vertices has a cycle of length at most
\lceil n/r\rceil
.

Conjecture 1.3

(Caccetta and Häggkvist, 1978,
[
10
]
)
.

Every digraph on
n
vertices of minimum out-degree at least
r
has a cycle of length at most
\lceil n/r\rceil
.

The two conjectures remain widely open. Many results on these two conjectures have been obtained, see, e.g.,
[
6
,
15
,
18
,
23
,
25
,
13
,
19
,
14
,
2
,
4
,
16
,
20
,
17
,
22
,
24
]
.
The following is a simple observation.

Observation
.

(1) Behzad-Chartrand-Wall Conjecture is true if and only if
\varphi_{n}^{\lceil n/r\rceil}(r,r)=0
, i.e.,
D_{n}^{\lceil n/r\rceil}(r,r)=\O
.

(2) Caccetta-Häggkvist Conjecture is true if and only if
\varphi_{n}^{\lceil n/r\rceil}(r,1)=0
, i.e.,
D_{n}^{\lceil n/r\rceil}(r,1)=\O
.

The above observation implies that determining the exact values of
\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
seems quite difficult.
A special case of Conjecture
1.3
, which has received substantial attention, states that
\varphi_{n}^{3}(n/3,1)=0
.
The best known result for this special case, given by Hladký et al.
[
19
]
, is
\varphi_{n}^{3}(0.3465n,1)=0
.
As a straightforward corollary of Theorem
1.1
for
k=3
, we have

Corollary 1.4

(
[
5
]
)
.

For
n\geq 4
,

\varphi_{n}^{3}(1,1)=\binom{n-1}{2}+1.

Note that
\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi^{{}^{\prime}},\zeta^{{}^{\prime}})\leq\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
if
1\leq\xi\leq\xi^{{}^{\prime}}
and
1\leq\zeta\leq\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}
. Thereby,
\varphi_{n}^{3}(\xi,1)\leq\varphi_{n}^{3}(1,1)=\binom{n-1}{2}+1
for every
\xi\geq 1
and
n\geq 4
. Moreover,
\varphi_{n}^{3}(1,1)=0
for
n\leq 3
and
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)=0
for
n\leq 6
. Recently, Chen and Chang
[
11
]
bounded the values of
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)
by presenting
\binom{n-1}{2}-2\leq\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)\leq\binom{n-1}{2}
for all
n\geq 7
. The lower bound is obtained by constructing a family of digraphs that meet the requirements and the upper bound is obtained by using the structure property of
\Phi_{n}^{3}(1,1)
. The same authors
[
12
]
improved the above upper bound to
\binom{n-1}{2}-1
for
n\geq 7
and further gave the exact values of
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)
for
7\leq n\leq 9
.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
12
]
)
.

For
n\geq 7
,

\binom{n-1}{2}-2\leq\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)\leq\binom{n-1}{2}-1.

Moreover,
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)=\binom{n-1}{2}-1
when
n=7,8
and
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)=\binom{n-1}{2}-2
when
n=9
.

In this paper, we continue the work of Chang and Chen
[
12
]
and determine the exact values of
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)
for all
n\geq 10
.

Theorem 1.6
.

For
n\geq 10
,

\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)=\binom{n-1}{2}-2.

Consequently, by combining Theorem
1.5
and
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)=0
for
n\leq 6
, we obtain the exact values of
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)
for all
n
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we first introduce some notation, and then we present several auxiliary results that will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.6
.
In Section 3, we mainly verify Theorem
1.6
. We end in Section 4 with a few concluding remarks.

2
Preliminary

In this section, we introduce some notation and present several auxiliary results to verify our main result.

2.1
Notation

The main aim of this subsection is to establish some notation, see e.g.,
[
1
]
.

Let
D
be a digraph. If
(u,v)
is an arc of
D
, then we say
u
dominates
v
or
v
is dominated by
u
, and
u
(resp.,
v
) is its
tail
(resp.,
head
). For convenience, we use
u\rightarrow v
to mean an arc
(u,v)
. Every two vertices
u
and
v
are
adjacent
if
u\rightarrow v
or
v\rightarrow u
, otherwise they are nonadjacent.

Let
v
be a vertex of
D
. The
out-neighborhood
of
v
is the set
N^{+}_{D}(v)=\{u\in V(D):v\rightarrow u\}
, and the
out-degree
of
v
is
d^{+}_{D}(v)=|N^{+}_{D}(v)|
. The
in-neighborhood
of
v
is the set
N^{-}_{D}(v)=\{u\in V(D):u\rightarrow v\}
, and the
in-degree
of
v
is
d^{-}_{D}(v)=|N^{-}_{D}(v)|
. The
neighborhood
of
v
is the set
N_{D}(v)=\{u\in V(D):u,v\;\text{are}\;\text{adjacent}\}
, and the
degree
of
v
is
d_{D}(v)=|N_{D}(v)|
. Note that
N_{D}(v)=N^{+}_{D}(v)\cup N^{-}_{D}(v)
. The vertices in
N^{+}_{D}(v),N^{-}_{D}(v)
and
N_{D}(v)
are called the
out-neighbors
,
in-neighbors
and
neighbors
of
v
, respectively. By an
(\alpha,\beta)
-vertex
we mean a vertex with out-degree
\alpha
and in-degree
\beta
, where both
\alpha
and
\beta
are integers.

Let
\Delta^{+}(D)
be the
maximum out-degree
,
\Delta^{-}(D)
be the
maximum in-degree
, and
\Delta(D)
be the
maximum degree
. Similarly, we denote by
\delta^{+}(D)
the
minimum out-degree
,
\delta^{-}(D)
the
minimum in-degree
and
\delta(D)
the
minimum degree
.

A digraph
H
is a
subdigraph
of
D
if
V(H)\subseteq V(D)
and
A(H)\subseteq A(D)
. For every such
H
, we denote
N^{+}_{H}(v)=N^{+}_{D}(v)\cap V(H)
(resp.,
N^{-}_{H}(v)=N^{-}_{D}(v)\cap V(H)
) and
d^{+}_{H}(v)=|N^{+}_{H}(v)|
(resp.,
d^{-}_{H}(v)=|N^{-}_{H}(v)|
). For any
W\subseteq V(D)
, let
D\langle W\rangle
be the subdigraph induced by
W
in
D
, and we denote the digraph
D\langle V(D)\backslash W\rangle
by
D-W
. If
W
contains only one vertex say
w
, then we will omit the set brackets and write
D\langle w\rangle
for
D\langle\{w\}\rangle
. By
D-w
we mean a subdigraph obtained from
D
by deleting
w
and all arcs incident with
w
. Let
X
and
Y
be two disjoint vertex sets. The arc set from
X
to
Y
will be denoted by
A(X,Y)
, i.e., each arc
(x,y)\in A(X,Y)
satisfies that
x\in X
and
y\in Y
.

A maximal strong subdigraph of
D
is called a
strong component
(
component
for short) of
D
. The components of
D
have an
acyclic ordering
, namely, they can be labeled
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{h}
such that all arcs are from
D_{i}
to
D_{j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq h
(see, e.g.,
[
1
]
for details).

Definition 2.1.
For a digraph
D
,
\gamma(D)
is the number of unordered pairs
\{u,v\}
of distinct vertices
u,v
such that they are nonadjacent in
D
. Let
Q
and
K
be vertex disjoint subdigraphs of
D
. By
\gamma(Q,K)
we denote the number of unordered pairs of nonadjacent vertices
\{u,v\}
in
D
with
u\in V(Q)
and
v\in V(K)
. Particularly, if
Q
contains a unique vertex
w
, then we shall use
\gamma(w,K)
to mean
\gamma(Q,K)
.

2.2
Characterization of
\Phi_{n}^{3}(1,1)

Note that
D_{n}^{3}(1,1)=\O
for
n\leq 3
. It is clear that
D_{n}^{3}(1,1)=\{C_{4}\}
when
n=4
.
In 2021, Chen and Chang
[
11
]
characterized all the extremal digraphs in
\Phi_{n}^{3}(1,1)
, and there are
5
families of such extremal digraphs for
n\geq 5
, denoted by
\mathscr{D}_{i}
, where
i\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
, as shown in Fig.1.

Figure 1:
\mathscr{D}_{1},\mathscr{D}_{2},\mathscr{D}_{3},\mathscr{D}_{4}
, and
\mathscr{D}_{5}
are arranged in the order of top to bottom, left to right

Theorem 2.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

For
n\geq 5
,
\Phi_{n}^{3}(1,1)=\cup_{i=1}^{5}\mathscr{D}_{i}
.

Next, we describe each
\mathscr{D}_{i}\subseteq\Phi_{n}^{3}(1,1)
in detail to make this paper self-contained, where
i\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
and
n\geq 5
.

Let
D
denote a digraph of
\Phi_{n}^{3}(1,1)
for
n\geq 5
. For any fixed
v\in V(D)
with
d_{D}(v)\leq n-3
, the subdigraph
D-v
has
h\geq 2
components, denoted by
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{h}
, such that they have an acyclic ordering. Additionally,
|V(D_{i})|=n_{i}
for

.

Characterization I. Orders of
D_{i}
for
2\leq i\leq h-1

(1).
If
D\in\mathscr{D}_{1}
, then
D-v
contains two components and
n_{i}\geq 4
for
i=1,2
.

(2).
If
D\in\mathscr{D}_{i}
for
i\in\{2,3,4,5\}
, then
h\geq 3
. Each component
D_{i}
contains a unique vertex, namely,
n_{i}=1
holds, where
2\leq i\leq h-1
. In addition, we enumerate the orders of
D_{1}
and
D_{h}
as below:

(2.1).
If
D
is a member of
\mathscr{D}_{2}
, then
n_{1}\geq 4
and
n_{h}\geq 4
;

(2.2).
If
D
is a member of
\mathscr{D}_{3}
, then
n_{1}\geq 4
and
n_{h}=1
;

(2.3).
If
D
is a member of
\mathscr{D}_{4}
, then
n_{1}=1
and
n_{h}\geq 4
;

(2.4).
If
D
is a member of
\mathscr{D}_{5}
, then
n_{1}=1
and
n_{h}=1
.

Characterization II. Subdigraphs
D_{i}
and
\widetilde{D_{i}}
for
i=1,h

Let
\widetilde{D_{i}}
denote
D\langle V(D_{i})\cup\{v\}\rangle
, where
i\in\{1,h\}
. We now present the structures of
D_{i}
and
\widetilde{D_{i}}
for
i=1,h
as follows:

(1).
If
n_{i}=1
for

, then
v\rightarrow x
and

, where
V(D_{1})=\{x\}
and

.

(2).
If
n_{i}\geq 4

, then

and

. This means that

and

, respectively. Furthermore, we have

,

,

and

.

Characterization III. Neighbors of

in
D_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq h-1

Let
U
be the union of vertex set of

for
2\leq i\leq h-1
, i.e.,

. By

we mean the subdigraph of

induced by

, namely,
\widetilde{D}\langle U\rangle=D\langle U\cup\{v\}\rangle
. In addition, we denote

and

.

(1).
Every vertex

lies in front of every vertex

in this sequence of vertices.

(2).
The arc set

consists of all arcs from
D_{i}
to
D_{j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq h
, except the following three types of arcs:

(2.1).
The arc
(x,y)
, where
x\in N^{+}_{\widetilde{D_{1}}}(v)
and
y\in N^{-}_{\widetilde{D_{h}}}(v)
;

(2.2).
All arcs
(x,r)
, where
x\in N^{+}_{\widetilde{D_{1}}}(v)
and

;

(2.3).
All arcs
(s,y)
, where

and

.

We end in this subsection by establishing a proposition with respect to
\Phi_{n}^{3}(1,1)
as follows:

Proposition 2.2

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
D
be a digraph of
\Phi_{n}^{3}(1,1)
for

. Then

contains at least two vertices of out-degree one.

Concretely, we have the following:

(1).
If

has order at least

, i.e.,

, then such two vertices of out-degree one belong to

that differ from

, where

(2).

, then either

and

, then

and

2.3
Characterization of

This subsection mainly elaborates the specific structure of

for

that will be used in a key step in the proof of Theorem

.

I. Structure of
\Phi_{7}^{3}(2,1)

It is not difficult to deduce that
D_{n}^{3}(2,1)=\O
for
n\leq 6
by applying the following lemma:

Lemma 2.3

(
[
10
]
)
.

Every digraph on
n
vertices of minimum out-degree
2
has a cycle of length at most
\lceil n/2\rceil
.

Let
n\geq 2
denote an integer and
S
denote a subset of
\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
. The
circulant digraph

C_{n}(S)
is defined with the vertex set and arc set as follows (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
, Chapter
2
, p.80 for details):

(1). Vertex set:

V(C_{n}(S))=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
;

(2). Arc set:

A(C_{n}(S))=\{(i,i+j\pmod{n}):1\leq i\leq n,\;j\in S\}
.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
D
be a digraph of
D_{7}^{3}(2,1)
. Then
D
is isomorphic to the circulant digraph
C_{7}(1,2)
.

Lemma
2.4
tells us that up to isomorphism
D_{7}^{3}(2,1)
has only one member
C_{7}(1,2)
and thus
\Phi_{7}^{3}(2,1)=D_{7}^{3}(2,1)=\{C_{7}(1,2)\}
. It is clear that the unique digraph
D
of

Claim 1

Case 2.

3.2
The minimum degree of
D
is at most
n-4
, i.e.,
\delta(D)\leq n-4
.

Proof.
Let
w
be a vertex of
D
with minimum degree. Then
d_{D}(w)=\delta(D)\leq n-4
. In the remaining proof in this subsection, we address
D-w
by distinguishing two parts as follows, namely,
D-w
is strong and
D-w
is not strong:

Part I.
D-w
is strong

Notice first that
D-w\in D_{n-1}^{3}(1,1)
. Hence,
|A(D-w)|\leq\varphi_{n-1}^{3}(1,1)=\binom{n-2}{2}+1
. Together with
d_{D}(w)\leq n-4
, we have
|A(D)|=|A(D-w)|+d_{D}(w)\leq\binom{n-1}{2}-1
. Recall that
|A(D)|=\binom{n-1}{2}-1
, which implies that both
|A(D-w)|=\binom{n-2}{2}+1
and
d_{D}(w)=n-4
hold. For convenience, we denote
G=D-w
.
One easily sees that
G\in\Phi^{3}_{n-1}(1,1)
. In the rest of proof in this part, we will use the structure of digraphs of
\Phi^{3}_{n-1}(1,1)
. By applying Theorem
2.1
, one can separate
G
into five families of digraphs on
n-1
vertices, namely, {
\mathscr{D}_{1},\mathscr{D}_{2},\mathscr{D}_{3},\mathscr{D}_{4},\mathscr{D}_{5}
}, as shown in Fig.1 (see Subsection 2.2 for details).

Let
v
be a vertex of
G
such that
d_{G}(v)\leq n-4
and
G-v
has
h
components, denoted by
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{h}
, satisfying that there is no arc from
D_{j}
to
D_{i}
for
1\leq i<j\leq h
, where
h\geq 2
. Denote by
|V(D_{i})|=n_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,h\}
. We divide the discussion into five cases as follows:

Case 1.
G\in\mathscr{D}_{1}

Note first that
G-v
contains exactly two components
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
such that
n_{1}\geq 4
and
n_{2}\geq 4
. By
Characterization II
, we have
d^{+}_{D\langle V(D_{2})\cup\{v\}\rangle}(v)=n_{2}-2\geq 2
and
d^{-}_{D\langle V(D_{2})\cup\{v\}\rangle}(v)=1
. Moreover,
D\langle V(D_{i})\cup\{v\}\rangle\in\Phi^{3}_{n_{i}+1}(1,1)
for

. Applying Proposition
2.2
, there are two vertices of out-degree ones that differ from
y
of
D_{2}
, where
N^{-}_{D\langle V(D_{2})\cup\{v\}\rangle}(v)=\{y\}
. Hence, one easily finds a vertex
u\in N^{+}_{D\langle V(D_{2})\cup\{v\}\rangle}(v)
such that
d^{+}_{G}(u)=1
. Since
d^{+}_{D}(u)\geq 2
, we thus obtain
u\rightarrow w
.
Characterization III
gives us that every vertex of
D_{1}
dominates
u
. It is not difficult to check that
w
does not dominate any vertex of
D_{1}
and
w\nrightarrow v
as
D
is
\emph{$C_{\leq 3}$-free}
. It follows that
N^{+}_{D}(w)\subseteq V(D_{2})
. Denote by
D_{2}^{*}=D\langle V(D_{2})\cup\{v,w\}\rangle
. One easily sees that
D_{2}^{*}\in D^{3}_{n_{2}+2}(2,1)
. Since
d^{-}_{D_{2}^{*}}(v)=1
, by Lemma
2.4
, we obtain
n_{2}\geq 6
. We only need to consider the following two subcases:

Subcase 1.1.
n_{2}=6

Combining Lemma
2.5
and
d^{-}_{D_{2}^{*}}(v)=1
, it is not difficult to check that
D_{2}^{*}\notin\Phi^{3}_{8}(2,1)
. This implies that
\gamma(D_{2}^{*})\geq 9
. Additionally,
\gamma(D\langle V(D_{1})\cup\{v\}\rangle)=n_{1}-1

\gamma(D)\geq\gamma(D\langle V(D_{1})\cup\{v\}\rangle)+\gamma(D_{2}^{*})+\gamma(D_{1},D_{2})\geq n+1
, contradicting the fact that
\gamma(D)=n
.

Subcase 1.2.
n_{2}\geq 7

Obviously, we have

. By the inductive hypothesis,
|A(D_{2}^{*})|\leq\varphi^{3}_{n_{2}+2}(2,1)=\binom{n_{2}+1}{2}-2

\gamma(D_{2}^{*})\geq n_{2}+3
. Together with
\gamma(D\langle V(D_{1})\cup\{v\}\rangle)=n_{1}-1

\gamma(D)\geq\gamma(D\langle V(D_{1})\cup\{v\}\rangle)+\gamma(D_{2}^{*})+\gamma(D_{1},D_{2})\geq n+1
, also a contradiction.

Case 2.
G\in\mathscr{D}_{2}

Indeed,
G-v
has
h\geq 3
components

satisfying that
n_{i}\geq 4
for

for

. Moreover,
d^{+}_{D\langle V(D_{h})\cup\{v\}\rangle}(v)=n_{h}-2\geq 2
and

. By Proposition
2.2
, one easily checks that
D_{h}
contains two vertices of out-degree ones that are different from

, where

, which follows that
u\rightarrow w
. By

, we obtain that each vertex of
D_{i}
dominates
u
, where

. It yields that
w
does not dominate any vertex of
D_{i}
for

and

since
D
is
\emph{$C_{\leq 3}$-free}
. This means
N^{+}_{D}(w)\subseteq V(D_{h})
. Denote by
D_{h}^{*}=D\langle V(D_{h})\cup\{v,w\}\rangle

. Analogously, we also consider the subcases
n_{h}=6
and
n_{h}\geq 7

Subcase 2.1.
n_{h}=6

Since

2.5
, it is easily seen that

. That is,
\gamma(D_{h}^{*})\geq 9
holds. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma(D\langle V(D_{1})\cup\{v\}\rangle)+\gamma(D_{h}^{*})+\gamma(v,K)+\gamma(D_{1},K)+\gamma(K,D_{h})+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{1}-1+9+n-n_{1}-6-2+1

\displaystyle=n+1,

Subcase 2.2.

Note once again that

\displaystyle\geq\gamma(D\langle V(D_{1})\cup\{v\}\rangle)+\gamma(D_{h}^{*})+\gamma(v,K)+\gamma(D_{1},K)+\gamma(K,D_{h})+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

for

Part II.

is

From now on, we shall use the following notation. Let

and

are positive integers since
D
is strong. One easily checks that
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq k_{1}\ell_{h}

\emph{$C_{\leq 3}$-free}

is
\emph{$C_{\leq 3}$-free}
, one checks that

and

\gamma(w,U)=z

\gamma(D_{1},U)\geq k_{1}y

\gamma(U,D_{h})\geq x\ell_{h}

Case 1. Both
G_{1}
and
G_{h}
are strong

. In addition,

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{1})+\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{1}-1+9+n-n_{1}-8+1

\displaystyle=n+1.

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{1})+\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{1}-1+n_{h}+2+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-1+1

\displaystyle=n+1.

The above inequality also contradicts the assumption that
\gamma(D)=n

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{1})+\gamma(D_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma(w,D_{h})

\displaystyle=n+n_{h}-5

contradicts the assumption that
\gamma(D)=n
.

Subcase 1.3.
k_{h}=1
and
\ell_{h}\geq 3

. In addition, it is obvious that

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{1})+\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{1}-1+n_{h}+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-1+3

\displaystyle=n+1,

a contradiction to the fact that
\gamma(D)=n
too.

Case 2.
G_{1}
is strong and
G_{h}
is not strong

It is straightforward that
k_{h}=0
and
\ell_{h}\geq 1
, which follows that
\gamma(w,D_{h})=n_{h}-\ell_{h}
. Since
d^{+}_{D}(w)\geq 2
, we have
|N^{+}_{D}(w)\cap(V(D_{1})\cup V(U))|\geq 2
. We now consider the sum of
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})
and
\gamma^{*}
. If

k_{1}\geq 2
, then
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq 2\ell_{h}
. Hence, we obtain
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma^{*}\geq 2\ell_{h}+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-1
. If
k_{1}=1
, then there is a vertex
t
of
U
such that
w\rightarrow t
. Consequently,
\gamma(t,D_{h})\geq\ell_{h}
since
D
is
\emph{$C_{\leq 3}$-free}
. It is not difficult to check that
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma^{*}\geq\ell_{h}+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-2+\ell_{h}=2\ell_{h}+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-2
. In summary,
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma^{*}\geq 2\ell_{h}+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-2
holds. Similarly, as shown above,
\gamma(G_{1})\geq n_{1}-1
and
\gamma(D_{h})\geq n_{h}-2
hold. Combining the above conclusions, we deduce that

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{1})+\gamma(D_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma(w,D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{1}-1+n_{h}-2+2\ell_{h}+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-2+n_{h}-\ell_{h}

\displaystyle=n+\ell_{h}+n_{h}-5.

Recall that
n_{h}\geq 4
. Next, we state that
\ell_{h}+n_{h}\geq 7
. Obviously, if
n_{h}\geq 6
, then we are done. Due to
\delta^{+}(D)\geq 2
, it is easily seen that
\ell_{h}=4
if
n_{h}=4
, yielding that
\ell_{h}+n_{h}=8
. Hence, we assume
n_{h}=5
. One easily checks that
D_{h}\in D^{3}_{5}(1,1)
, implying that
|A(D_{h})|\leq 7
. There exist at least three vertices of out-degree one in
D_{h}
and we have
\ell_{h}\geq 3
. Therefore, we obtain
\ell_{h}+n_{h}\geq 8
. As a consequence, we obtain that
\ell_{h}+n_{h}\geq 7
. This follows
\gamma(D)\geq n+2
, a contradiction.

Case 3.
G_{1}
is not strong and
G_{h}
is strong

Recall that
D_{1}
contains either exactly one vertex or at least
4
vertices since
D
is a
\emph{$C_{\leq 3}$-free}
strong digraph. Here to proceed with this case, we present the proof by distinguishing the following two subcases, namely,
n_{1}=1
and
n_{1}=4
:

Subcase 3.1.
D_{1}
has a unique vertex, namely,
n_{1}=1

Clearly, we have
k_{1}=1
. Note that
K_{h}\neq\O
since
G_{h}
is a strong subdigraph of
D
. Analogous to the proof of Case 1, we divide the discussion into the subcases as follows:

(1).
k_{h}\geq 2
. Similar to the previous proof, one easily sees that
G_{h}\in D_{n_{h}+1}^{3}(2,1)
. As
G_{h}
is a
\emph{$C_{\leq 3}$-free}
strong subdigraph of
D
, Lemma
2.3
gives us that
n_{h}\geq 6
.

(1.1).
n_{h}=6
. Note that
G_{h}
is isomorphic to the circulant digraph
C_{7}(1,2)
by Lemma
2.4
. It is straightforward to check that
\ell_{h}=2
and
\gamma(G_{h})=7
. Moreover,
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq\ell_{h}=2
. Together with
\gamma^{*}\geq n-8
, one easily sees that

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq 7+n-8+2

\displaystyle=n+1,

contradicts to the fact that
\gamma(D)=n
.

(1.2).
n_{h}=7
. We only need to consider
G_{h}\in\Phi_{8}^{3}(2,1)
and
G_{h}\notin\Phi_{8}^{3}(2,1)
.

(a)
. By Lemma
2.5
, it is obvious that
G_{h}
is isomorphic to
F_{8}
if
G_{h}\in\Phi_{8}^{3}(2,1)
. This yields that
\ell_{h}\geq 2
and
\gamma(G_{h})=8
. Additionally, we have
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq 2
. Combining with
\gamma^{*}\geq n-9
, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq 8+n-9+2

\displaystyle=n+1,

a contradiction to
\gamma(D)=n
too.

(b)
. One checks that
\gamma(G_{h})\geq 9
if
G_{h}\notin\Phi_{8}^{3}(2,1)
by applying Lemma
2.5
. It is clear that
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq 1
and
\gamma^{*}\geq n-9
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq 9+n-9+1

\displaystyle=n+1,

a contradiction again.

(1.3).
n_{h}\geq 8
. Observe first that
9\leq n_{h}+1<n
and
G_{h}\in D_{n_{h}+1}^{3}(2,1)
. By the induction hypothesis, we obtain that
|A(G_{h})|\leq\varphi_{n_{h}+1}^{3}(2,1)=\binom{n_{h}}{2}-2
. Thus,
\gamma(G_{h})\geq n_{h}+2
holds. Recall that
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq 1
and
\gamma^{*}\geq n-n_{h}-2
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{h}+2+n-n_{h}-2+1

\displaystyle=n+1,

a contradiction.

(2).
k_{h}=1
and
\ell_{h}\leq 2

Notice once again that
D_{h}\in D^{3}_{n_{h}}(1,1)
and
|A(D_{h})|\leq\varphi^{3}_{n_{h}}(1,1)=\binom{n_{h}-1}{2}+1
holds. Hence, we have
\gamma(D_{h})\geq n_{h}-2
. Analogous to the above discussion in Case 1,
n_{h}\geq 6
holds. It is not difficult to check that
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq\ell_{h}
and
\gamma(w,D_{h})=n_{h}-\ell_{h}-1
. We can derive the following by combining with
\gamma^{*}\geq n-n_{h}-2
:

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(D_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma(w,D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{h}-2+n-n_{h}-2+\ell_{h}+n_{h}-\ell_{h}-1

\displaystyle=n+n_{h}-5

\displaystyle\geq n+1.

a contradiction.

(3).
k_{h}=1
and
\ell_{h}\geq 3

It is not hard to see that
n_{h}\geq 5
and
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq 3
. Moreover,
G_{h}\in D^{3}_{n_{h}+1}(1,1)
and
n_{h}+1\geq 6
. Clearly,
G_{h}
contains a unique vertex of out-degree one. Using Proposition
2.2
, it is straightforward that
G_{h}\notin\Phi^{3}_{n_{h}+1}(1,1)
, implying that
|A(G_{h})|\leq\binom{n_{h}}{2}
by Corollary
1.4
. Thus,
\gamma(G_{h})\geq n_{h}
holds. Due to
\gamma^{*}\geq n-n_{h}-2
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{h}+n-n_{h}-2+3

\displaystyle=n+1.

This contradicts our assumption that
\gamma(D)=n
.

Subcase 3.2.
D_{1}
has at least
4
vertices, i.e.,
n_{1}\geq 4

Note once again that
D_{1}\in D^{3}_{n_{1}}(1,1)
. Thus, we obtain
|A(D_{1})|\leq\binom{n_{1}-1}{2}+1
, which follows that
\gamma(D_{1})\geq n_{1}-2
. Since
G_{1}
is not strong, it is clear that
\gamma(w,D_{1})=n_{1}-k_{1}
. In addition,
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq k_{1}\ell_{h}\geq k_{1}
and
\gamma^{*}\geq n-n_{1}-n_{h}-1
. Moreover, by the previous discussion, one easily checks that
\gamma(G_{h})\geq n_{h}
. By combining these conclusions, one can deduce that

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(D_{1})+\gamma(G_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma(w,D_{1})

\displaystyle\geq n_{1}-2+n_{h}+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-1+k_{1}+n_{1}-k_{1}

\displaystyle=n+n_{1}-3

\displaystyle\geq n+1,

contradicts the fact that
\gamma(D)=n
.

Case 4. Both
G_{1}
and
G_{h}
are not strong

Note once again that
n_{1}=1
or
n_{1}\geq 4
, and
n_{h}\geq 4
. In the remaining proof of this case, we divide the discussion into the following two subcases:

Subcase 4.1.
D_{1}
contains only one vertex, i.e.,
n_{1}=1

One easily sees that
k_{1}=1
as
D
is strong. Since
G_{h}
is not strong, then
k_{h}=0
. Recall that
X=N^{+}_{D}(w)\cap V(U)
and
|X|=x
. It is obvious that
x\geq 1
due to
d^{+}_{D}(w)\geq 2
. It is not difficult to check that
\gamma^{*}\geq n-n_{h}+\ell_{h}-3
and
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq\ell_{h}
. Together with
\gamma(D_{h})\geq n_{h}-2
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(D_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma(w,D_{h})

\displaystyle\geq n_{h}-2+n-n_{h}+\ell_{h}-3+\ell_{h}+n_{h}-\ell_{h}

\displaystyle=n+\ell_{h}+n_{h}-5.

For the same reason presented in the proof of Case 2, it is not hard to check that
\ell_{h}+n_{h}\geq 7
. Consequently, we have
\gamma(D)\geq n+2
, contradicting the fact that
\gamma(D)=n
.

Subcase 4.2.
D_{1}
contains at least
4
vertices, i.e.,
n_{1}\geq 4

Obviously,
k_{1}
and
\ell_{h}
are positive integers and
\ell_{1}=k_{h}=0
. Notice once again that
n_{i}\geq 4
and
D_{i}\in D^{3}_{n_{i}}(1,1)
for
i\in\{1,h\}
. Hence, we have
|A(D_{i})|\leq\binom{n_{i}-1}{2}+1
, yielding that
\gamma(D_{i})\geq n_{i}-2
, where
i=1,h
. Because
G_{1}
(resp.,
G_{h}
) is not strong, one can check that
\gamma(w,D_{1})=n_{1}-k_{1}
(resp.,
\gamma(w,D_{h})=n_{h}-\ell_{h}
). Additionally,
\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})\geq k_{1}\ell_{h}
and
\gamma^{*}\geq n-n_{1}-n_{h}-1
. Combining these conclusions, one can deduce that

\displaystyle\gamma(D)

\displaystyle=\gamma(D_{1})+\gamma(D_{h})+\gamma^{*}+\gamma(D_{1},D_{h})+\gamma(w,D_{1})+\gamma(w,D_{1})

\displaystyle\geq n_{1}-2+n_{h}-2+n-n_{1}-n_{h}-1+k_{1}\ell_{h}+n_{1}-k_{1}+n_{h}-\ell_{h}

\displaystyle=n+n_{1}+n_{h}-6+(k_{1}-1)(\ell_{h}-1)

\displaystyle\geq n+n_{1}+n_{h}-6

\displaystyle\geq n+2,

a contradiction to
\gamma(D)=n
.
\blacksquare

Combining the previous proofs, we thus complete the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

4
Concluding remarks

Recall that a digraph is
C_{\leq k}
-free
if it contains no cycle of length at most
k
. For positive integers
\xi
and
\zeta
,
D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
is a family of
C_{\leq k}
-free
strong digraphs on
n
vertices such that each digraph
D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
satisfies
\delta^{+}(D)\geq\xi
and
\delta^{-}(D)\geq\zeta
. Let
\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\max\{|A(D)|:D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)\}
. Denote
\Phi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\{D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta):|A(D)|=\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)\}
. Particularly,
\varphi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=0
if
\Phi_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)=\O
for some collections of
\{n,k,\xi,\zeta\}
.

In this paper, we prove that
\varphi^{3}_{n}(2,1)=\binom{n-1}{2}-2
for
n\geq 10
. Combining Theorem
1.5
and
\varphi_{n}^{3}(2,1)=0
for
n\leq 6
, we thus completely determine the values of
\varphi^{3}_{n}(2,1)
for all
n
. Proposition
Observation
establishes the equivalence relation between the problem to determine the exact values of
\varphi^{k}_{n}(\xi,\zeta)
and the famous Caccetta-Häggkvist Conjecture. As a consequence, it will be interesting to investigate ordinary
\varphi^{k}_{n}(\xi,\zeta)
except
\varphi^{k}_{n}(1,1)
and
\varphi^{3}_{n}(2,1)
, where
k\geq 3
and
\xi,\zeta
are positive integers.

Furthermore, we believe Lemma
2.8
may be of independent interest since we observe that if one can find a vertex of a digraph
D\in D_{n}^{k}(\xi,\zeta)
of small degree such that the removal of this vertex does not destroy the strong connectedness, then the upper bounds of general
\varphi^{k}_{n}(\xi,\zeta)
might be improved by repeatedly applying this lemma.
