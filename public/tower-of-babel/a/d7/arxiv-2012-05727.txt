Implicit-explicit multistep formulations for finite element discretisations using continuous interior penalty
Erik Burman, Johnny Guzmán

Abstract
. We consider a finite element method with symmetric stabilisation for the discretisation of the transient convection–diffusion equation. For the time-discretisation we consider either the second order backwards differentiation formula or the Crank-Nicolson method. Both the convection term and the associated stabilisation are treated explicitly using an extrapolated approximate solution. We prove stability of the method and the τ 2 + h p + 1 2 \tau^{2}+h^{p+{\frac{1}{2}}} error estimates for the L 2 L^{2} -norm under either the standard hyperbolic CFL condition, when piecewise affine ( p = 1 p=1 ) approximation is used, or in the case of finite element approximation of order p ≥ 1 p\geq 1 , a stronger, so-called 4 / 3 4/3 -CFL, i.e. τ ≤ C ​ h 4 / 3 \tau\leq Ch^{4/3} . The theory is illustrated with some numerical examples.



1.
Introduction

In the computational solution of convection–diffusion problems it is
highly advantageous to treat the convection term explicitly and the
diffusion term implicitly. Although there is an important literature
on the topic there seems to be very few works that show that
implicit-explicit (IMEX)
methods are robust under finite element discretisation both in the convection and the diffusion dominated
regimes. Indeed most works on IMEX methods with finite element
discretisations assume that the diffusion
dominates, typically leading to an assumption that the product of the
Courant number and the Peclet number is small
[
12
,
19
,
3
,
1
,
2
]
. Most other works on
IMEX methods typically consider a stability region type analysis that
is unsuitable for a quantitative finite element analysis
[
5
,
4
,
15
,
16
]
. To pass from the analysis of the
semi-discrete case to a fully discrete case typically requires the use
of energy methods as advocated in
[
17
]
. This was the route taken in

[
8
]
where a second order implicit explicit Runge-Kutta method was
considered for
H^{1}
-conforming finite element approximations of convection–diffusion
equations using interior penalty to guarantee stability in the high
Peclet regime and more recently
[
21
]
where a local discontinuous
Galerkin methods with 2nd and 3rd order Runge-Kutta IMEX schemes are considered. Explicit Runge-Kutta methods have been very successful for the
approximation of hyperbolic equations often in combination with
discontinuous Galerkin methods and for this case there is a very large
literature
[
11
,
22
,
9
,
23
]
, to name a few. In particular the mass matrix
is block diagonal allowing for high order explicit time stepping, this
however is no longer the case for IMEX methods where the system matrix
associated to diffusion couples globally. Moreover if the target application is
the incompressible Navier-Stokes’ equations, the explicit methods are
unsuitable and methods with many implicit
stages may become costly. It is not clear that the analysis of

[
8
]
generalises to this case, since it is assumed there that
the operator treated implicitly is symmetric and
elliptic. For a second order scheme the RK IMEX method has three
stages and is known to impose compatibility
conditions on the exact solution for consistency to
hold (see
[
8
, Section 3]
, for a discussion in the case of
convection–diffusion equations). Finite element-IMEX methods with extrapolation were considered as early as 1970 by Douglas and Dupont
[
13
]
, however, they considered diffusion dominated problems.
Implicit-explicit methods with extrapolation for the
incompressible Navier-Stokes equations and backward differentiation
used for time discretisation were analysed in
[
6
]
, and some
other multi-step IMEX methods together with the local Galerkin method
was considered in
[
20
]
,
but the mesh Peclet number, defined by

Pe:=\frac{|u|h}{\mu}

where
u
denotes the fluid velocity,
\mu
the viscosity coefficient
and
h
the local mesh size,
is assumed to be small. In many applications such as
large eddy simulation or under resolved DNS it may not be possible
to satisfy such a condition, nevertheless for such problems, in which
convective effects are strong, the use of IMEX schemes is very
attractive, since the nonlinearity and stabilization terms are
handled explicitly, whereas the velocity-pressure coupling, which is
implicit can be solved using optimized methods for saddle point
problems.
As a first step towards IMEX schemes for the equations of
incompressible flow we will in this paper consider the
convection-diffusion equation and analyse some known IMEX schemes with
respect to their stability properties for varying mesh Peclet number.
For an IMEX scheme to be stable for high mesh
Peclet number it has to degenerate to a stable explicit scheme in the
limit of vanishing diffusion. Such time integrator are typically
characterized by nontrivial imaginary stability boundary. Examples are
given by Adams-Bashforth (AB) integrators of higher order such as AB3,
AB4, AB7 and AB8 (see
[
14
]
). Unfortunately IMEX schemes
designed using methods popular for the solution of incompressible flow
problems such as the Crank-Nicolson scheme or the second order
backward differentiation scheme do not enjoy this
property, if the convection is treated explicitly using
extrapolation. Indeed in this case the limit schemes are the second order Adams-Bashforth scheme for pure
transport and the extrapolated Gear scheme
[
19
]
(BDF2 with
extrapolated convcetion). Both have
trivial imaginary stability boundary and would therefore seem
unsuitable candidates for Peclet robust IMEX methods. Nevertheless in
this contribution we will consider these two schemes with a finite
element space discretization stabilized using continuous interior
penalty. Observe that the explicit treatment of the stabilization
is appealing since it avoids having to
handle the extended stencil on the level of the linear solver
[
10
]
.
We use energy methods to prove that they are stable, irrespective of
the Peclet number,
under suitable CFL conditions. The stability however depends on the space
discretization. Both the polynomial order of the approximation space
and the stabilization of the convection operator come into play. Using
the additional stability of the gradient penalty operator we show
optimal error estimates for the material derivative and
L^{2}
-error
error estimates with the classical order
O(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}})
(where
p

is the polynomial degree), which is known
to be the best that can be obtained for continuous FEM in the general case.
Observe also that since our results are robust with respect to the Peclet
number they remain valid for the case of vanishing diffusion, i.e. the pure transport
equation. So the present work also give the first analysis of these
explicit methods together with stabilized FEM for the transport equation.

We only consider the case of continuous approximation spaces herein,
but the analysis carries over to the case of discontinuous Galerkin
symmetric interior penalty methods, with upwind fluxes in a straightforward fashion (we
refer to
[
9
]
for a uniform treatment of continuous and
discontinuous Galerkin methods in the case of explicit Runge-Kutta
methods).

The outline of the paper is as follows. In the next section we
introduce our model problem, define the finite element spaces and
prove some technical results. In section
3.1
we introduce
the BDF2-IMEX method and derive stability results in all regimes
and for all polynomial orders. This allows us to prove a priori error
estimates in section
4
. Here we consider the high
Peclet regime only and derive error estimates for the
L^{2}
-error at
final time and the error in the material derivative over the
space-time domain. In section
5
we introduce the Crank-Nicolson IMEX
scheme and prove stability estimates in all regime and for all
polynomial orders. Note that it is then straightforward to derive similar error
estimates as for the BDF2-IMEX scheme in the high Peclet regime for
the Crank-Nicolson scheme and
also optimal estimates in
l^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
, or

l^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
for both methods, but to keep the length of the paper reasonable these
results have not been included here. The paper finishes with some
numerical experiments (section
6
), validating the theory and
showing the robustness of the methods in the presence of non-smooth data.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Convection–diffusion problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=1,2,3
be an open polygonal
domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
and outward pointing normal
n
. Let
I:=(0,T)
and denote the space time domain by
Q=\Omega\times I
.
We consider the convection diffusion equation,

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\beta\cdot\nabla u-\mu\Delta u=

\displaystyle f\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }Q

(2.1b)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,0)=

\displaystyle u_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega

(2.1c)

\displaystyle u=

\displaystyle 0\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

Here
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
\beta\in[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)]^{d}
, with
\nabla\cdot\beta=0
,
\beta\cdot n|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. This is a parabolic
problem and it is known to admit a unique solution in

L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
We define the forms

(u,v)_{\Omega}:=\int_{\Omega}uv~\mbox{d}x,\quad c(w,v):=\int_{\Omega}\beta\cdot\nabla wv~\mbox{d}x,\quad a(w,v):=\int_{\Omega}\mu\nabla w\cdot\nabla v~\mbox{d}x.

Assuming sufficient smoothness of the solution the equation may then
be cast on the weak formulation,

(2.2a)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}u,v)_{\Omega}+c(u,v)+a(u,v)=

\displaystyle(f,v)_{\Omega},\quad

\displaystyle\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\,t>0,

(2.2b)

\displaystyle(u(\cdot,0),v)_{\Omega}=

\displaystyle(u_{0},v)_{\Omega},\quad

\displaystyle\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

We will use the following two norms
\|\cdot\|^{2}=(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Omega}
and
\|v\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}|v(x)|
.

2.2.
Finite element spaces and bilinear forms

Let
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h}
denote a family of shape regular, quasi
uniform, triangulation of
\Omega
into simplices. The set of interior faces
of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
will be denoted
\mathcal{F}
. Let
V_{h}
denote the
space of continuous finite element functions of polynomial degree less
than or equal to
p
:

V_{h}:=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):v\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(T),\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

We also consider the space with homogenuous boundary conditions
\mathring{V}_{h}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap V_{h}
. We let
\pi_{h}
be the
L^{2}
projection onto
\mathring{V}_{h}
given by

(2.3)

(\pi_{h}w,v_{h})_{\Omega}=(w,v_{h})_{\Omega}\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h}.

We recall the following approximation estimate that holds on
quasi-uniform meshes

(2.4)

\|u-\pi_{h}u\|+h\|\nabla(u-\pi_{h}u)\|\leq Ch^{p+1}|u|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}.

We will also make use of the piece-wise constant space

W_{h}:=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):v\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(T),\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

We let
P_{0}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow W_{h}
be the
L^{2}
orthogonal projection:

(P_{0}w,v_{h})_{\Omega}=(w,v_{h})_{\Omega}\quad\forall v_{h}\in W_{h}.

In order to stabilize a FEM we need the following bilinear form (see for example
[
7
]
)

(2.5)

s(w_{h},v_{h}):=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\int_{F}h_{F}^{2}(|\beta\cdot n|+\varepsilon_{\beta^{\perp}})\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\nabla w\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\cdot\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\nabla v\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]~\mbox{d}s,

where we introduce the jump of the gradient

\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\nabla w\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]|_{F}:=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\nabla w(x-\epsilon n_{T})\cdot n_{T}+\nabla w(x-\epsilon n_{T^{\prime}})\cdot n_{T^{\prime}},\mbox{ with }x\in F\quad\mbox{ and }F=\bar{T}\cap\bar{T}^{\prime}

and
\varepsilon_{\beta^{\perp}}\geq 0
is a coefficient that
introduces some weakly consistent cross wind diffusion when non-zero.
We will also apply the jump to scalar quantities below in which case
it is defined by

\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[w\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]|_{F}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}w(x-\epsilon n_{F})-w(x+\epsilon n_{F})\mbox{ with }x\in F

where
n_{F}
is a fixed but arbitrary normal to the face
F
.
We may then define the semi-norm

|v|_{s}:=s(v,v)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We let let
\beta_{0}
be the Raviart-Thomas projection of
\beta
to the lowest order Raviart-Thomas space. Since
\nabla\cdot\beta=0
we have that
\beta_{0}
is piecewise constant. Thus, we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|\beta-\beta_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq Ch\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}.

Then recall a critical approximation result that exhibits the
importance of the stabilization term, this follows from the local
estimate of
[
7
, Lemma 5.3]
:

(2.7)

\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\|\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}-v_{h}\|^{2}\leq C\,\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}h_{F}\|\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|^{2}_{F}.

Note that since
\beta_{0}\cdot n|_{\partial\Omega}=\beta\cdot n|_{\partial\Omega}=0
we have
\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
and therefore (
2.7
) holds taking the
infimum over the space
\mathring{V}_{h}
, i.e.

(2.8)

\inf_{v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h}}\|\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}-v_{h}\|^{2}\leq C\,\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}h_{F}\|\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|^{2}_{F}.

Using (
2.7
) together with (
2.6
) it is
straighforward to show that

(2.9)

\inf_{v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h}}\|\beta\cdot\nabla w_{h}-v_{h}\|\leq C\,(\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}\|w_{h}\|+\left(\frac{\|\beta\|_{\infty}}{h}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}|w_{h}|_{s})

Indeed, we first add and subtract
\beta_{0}
and apply the triangle inequality

\|\beta\cdot\nabla w_{h}-v_{h}\|\leq\|(\beta-\beta_{0})\cdot\nabla w_{h}\|+\|\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}-v_{h}\|.

Then using (
2.6
), an inverse inequality and (
2.7
)

\|(\beta-\beta_{0})\cdot\nabla w_{h}\|+\|\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}-v_{h}\|\leq C(\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}\|w_{h}\|+(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}h_{F}\|\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|^{2}_{F})^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Adding and subtracting
\beta
in the second term and using a trace
inequality followed by (
2.6
),

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}h_{F}\|\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\beta_{0}\cdot\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|^{2}_{F}\leq

\displaystyle C(\|(\beta-\beta_{0})\cdot\nabla w_{h}\|^{2}+\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}\|w_{h}\|^{2}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}h_{F}\|\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\beta\cdot\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}\|w_{h}\|^{2}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}h_{F}\|\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\beta\cdot\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|_{F}^{2}).

Using the continuity of
w_{h}
in the last term of the right hand side
we see that

\|\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\beta\cdot\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|_{F}=\|\beta\cdot n\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|_{F}\leq\|\beta\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}}\||\beta\cdot n|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\hskip-1.8063pt\left[\nabla w_{h}\right]\hskip-1.8063pt\right]\|_{F}.

Hence, we have shown (
2.9
).

We can then defined the stabilised convection form

c_{h}(w_{h},v_{h}):=c(w_{h},v_{h})+\gamma s(w_{h},v_{h}).

Introducing
\tau
as the time step size, we also define Courant
number
Co
that will either be the standard hyperbolic CFL,
Co:=(\|\beta\|_{\infty}+1)\frac{\tau}{h}
,
or a slightly stronger
4/3
-Courant number (see
[
22
,
9
]

where it was used in the context of second order Runge-Kutta methods),
Co_{4/3}:=\tau(\|\beta\|_{\infty}/h)^{\frac{4}{3}}
, that will apply for
finite element spaces or polynomial degrees higher than
1
.
Observe that
Co
is a free parameter that can be made
as small as we like by making
\tau
small relative to
h
and

\beta
. The crucial point is that certain time residual terms from
the convection term can be made as small as necessary by fixing
Co

to be small enough. This is expressed in boundedness properties of the
convection and the associated stabilization that we now summarize.
First note that by the skew symmetry of the convection we have the positivity

(2.10)

\gamma|v|_{s}^{2}=c_{h}(v,v),\quad\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{\frac{3}{2}+\epsilon}(\Omega)+\mathring{V}_{h},

and by skew-symmetry followed by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, an
inverse inequality and the definition of
Co
we have the positivity

(2.11)

\tau c(v,w_{h})\leq C_{i}Co\|v\|\|w_{h}\|\quad\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),w_{h}\in V_{h},

where
C_{i}
the constant of an inverse inequality.
Similarly for the stabilisation norm we have the bound

(2.12)

\tau^{\frac{1}{2}}|w_{h}|_{s}\leq C_{i}Co^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{h}\|\quad\forall w_{h}\in V_{h}.

For the analysis we introduce a projection operator
C_{h}:H^{1}(\Omega)\mapsto\mathring{V}_{h}
defined by

(C_{h}v,v_{h})_{\Omega}=c(v,v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h}.

Note that by the definition of
c
,
(C_{h}w_{h},v_{h})_{\Omega}=-(w_{,}C_{h}v_{h})_{\Omega}

for
w_{h},v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h}
. Using (
2.11
) it is straightforward to show that the
C_{h}
operator satisfies the bound

(2.13)

\tau\|C_{h}v\|\leq C_{i}\tau^{\frac{1}{4}}Co^{\frac{3}{4}}_{4/3}\|v\|.

Proof.

(inequality (
2.13
))

\tau^{2}(C_{h}v,C_{h}v)=\tau^{2}c(v,C_{h}v)=-\tau^{2}c(C_{h}v,v)\leq\tau^{2}h^{-1}\|\beta\|_{\infty}C_{i}\|C_{h}v\|\|v\|.

Therefore,

\tau\|C_{h}v\|\leq\tau h^{-1}\|\beta\|_{\infty}C_{i}\|v\|,

but
\tau=\tau^{\frac{1}{4}}Co_{4/3}^{\frac{3}{4}}h/\|\beta\|_{\infty}
by which the claim follows.
∎

We also notice that

(2.14)

\tau\|C_{h}v\|\leq C_{i}Co\|v\|.

2.3.
Operators for time discretization

We define the second order backward differentiation operator

(2.15)

D_{\tau}v^{n+1}:=\frac{3v^{n+1}-4v^{n}+v^{n-1}}{2\tau}.

We recall the second order extrapolation
\tilde{v}^{n+1}=2v^{n}-v^{n-1}
, and the increment operator
\delta
such that
\delta v^{n+1}:=v^{n+1}-v^{n}
. Observe that there holds

(2.16)

\tilde{v}^{n+1}-v^{n+1}=\delta v^{n}-\delta v^{n+1}=-\delta\delta v^{n+1}.

We also recall that

(2.17)

\tau D_{\tau}v^{n+1}=\delta v^{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta\delta v^{n+1}.

Finally, we also observe that

(2.18)

\|\delta^{m}v^{n}\|\leq 2\sum_{i=0}^{m}\|v^{n-i}\|,\quad m=1,2,\,\mbox{ and }2\leq n\leq N.

As we will describe in a later section, for the Crank-Nicolson method the approximation of the time derivative
is given by the scaled increment operator

\tau^{-1}\delta v^{n+1}
. The extrapolation is taken to the time
level
t^{n+1/2}
, in order to approximate the central difference in
time that is the key feature of the Crank-Nicolson scheme,
\hat{v}^{n+1}=\frac{3}{2}v^{n}-\frac{1}{2}v^{n-1}
.

For the time discretization part of the error analysis we need some well known results on truncation
error analysis of finite difference operators that we collect in the
following proposition for future reference. These results are
standard and can be found for instance in the monography
[
18
]
, but for
completeness we sketch the proofs.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u^{n}:=u(t^{n})
and
y:=\beta\cdot\nabla u
then there holds

(2.19)

\|D_{\tau}u^{n+1}-\partial_{t}u^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq C\tau^{3}\|u_{ttt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n+1};L^{2}(\Omega))};

(2.20)

\|\beta(t^{n+1})\cdot\nabla u^{n+1}-\tilde{y}^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq C\tau^{3}\|(\beta\cdot\nabla u)_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n+1};L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Proof.

We first consider the bound (
2.19
)
We wish to bound

\|D_{\tau}u^{n+1}-\partial_{t}u^{n+1}\|.

Using Taylor development we may write

u(t)=u^{n-1}+(t-t^{n-1})\partial_{t}u^{n-1}+\frac{1}{2}(t-t^{n-1})^{2}\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t}(t-s)^{2}u^{(3)}(s)\,ds}_{R(t)}=Q(t)+R(t).

Deriving and evaluating at
t^{n+1}
we see that

Q^{\prime}(t)=\partial_{t}u^{n-1}+(t-t^{n-1})\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1},\quad Q^{\prime}(t^{n+1})=\partial_{t}u^{n-1}+2\tau\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}.

We also have

\tau^{-1}\delta Q^{n+1}=\partial_{t}u^{n-1}+\frac{1}{2\tau}((t^{n+1}-t^{n-1})^{2}-(t^{n}-t^{n-1})^{2})\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}=\partial_{t}u^{n-1}+\frac{3\tau}{2}\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}

and

\tau^{-1}\delta^{2}Q^{n+1}=\underbrace{((t^{n+1}-t^{n-1})-2(t^{n}-t^{n-1}))\partial_{t}u^{n-1}}_{=0}\\
+\frac{1}{2\tau}((t^{n+1}-t^{n-1})^{2}-2(t^{n}-t^{n-1})^{2})\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}=\tau^{2}\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}.

Using (
2.17
) we see that

D_{\tau}Q^{n+1}=\partial_{t}u^{n-1}+\frac{3\tau}{2}\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}+\frac{\tau}{2}\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}=\partial_{t}u^{n-1}+2\tau\partial^{2}_{t}u^{n-1}=Q^{\prime}(t^{n+1}).

Therefore

\|D_{\tau}u^{n+1}-\partial_{t}u^{n+1}\|^{2}=\|\underbrace{D_{\tau}Q-Q^{\prime}}_{=0}+D_{\tau}R^{n+1}-\partial_{t}R^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq\frac{C}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{k=n-1}^{n+1}\|R^{k}\|^{2}+2\|\partial_{t}R^{n+1}\|^{2}.

By the definition of
R
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{k=n-1}^{n+1}\|R^{k}\|^{2}\leq C\tau^{3}\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n+1}}\|u^{(3)}\|^{2}~ds.

Finally

\partial_{t}R^{n+1}=\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n+1}}(t^{n}-s)u^{(3)}(s)~ds

and therefore in a similar fashion

\|\partial_{t}R^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq\tau^{3}\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n+1}}\|u^{(3)}\|^{2}~ds,

which gives (
2.19
).
The result (
2.20
) easily follows after we apply the Cauchy-Shwarz inequality

(2.21)

\|y^{n+1}-\tilde{y}^{n+1}\|^{2}=\|\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n}}\int_{t}^{t+\tau}y_{tt}(s)ds\,dt\|^{2}\leq\tau^{3}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t^{n+1}}\|y_{tt}(s)\|^{2}ds.

∎

3.
The BDF2-IMEX Method

We may write the BDF2-IMEX finite element method as follows.
Find
u_{h}^{n+1}\in\mathring{V}_{h}
such that for
n\geq 1
,

(3.1)

(D_{\tau}u_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})_{\Omega}+c_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})+a(u_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})=L^{n+1}(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h},

where
u_{h}^{0},u_{h}^{1}
are given. Here
\{L^{n}\}
are a bounded linear operator on
\mathring{V}_{h}
.

3.1.
Stability of BDF2-IMEX

In the diffusion dominated (
Pe<1
) regime the BDF2-IMEX method is
stable under the standard hyperbolic CFL condition.
In this section we prove in addition to this, that BDF2-IMEX the method is stable
indepdendent of the Peclet number with a standard hyperbolic CFL
condition when
p=1
and under the
4/3
-CFL when
p>1
.

Let us define some norms. We start by defining the natural dissipation of the spatial variables.

E(v)^{2}:=\gamma|v|_{s}^{2}+\|\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla{v}\|^{2}.

We see that
E(v)^{2}=c_{h}(v,v)+a(v,v)
when
v\in\mathring{V}_{h}
. An
immediate consequence of (
2.12
) and an inverse inequality
is that for all

,

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\gamma|v|_{s}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C\gamma(\|\beta\|_{\infty}+1)}{h}\|v\|^{2},

(3.2b)

\displaystyle\|\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla{v}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C\mu}{h^{2}}\|v\|^{2}.

Hence,

(3.3)

\tau E(v)^{2}\leq C\left(\gamma Co+\frac{Co}{Pe}\right)\|v\|^{2},

where we recall that the definition of the Peclet number
Pe
:

Pe:=\frac{\|\beta\|_{\infty}h}{\mu}.

For a linear operator
L
defined for
\mathring{V}_{h}
we define

(3.4)

\|L\|_{h}=\sup_{v\in\mathring{V}_{h}}\frac{L(v)}{\sqrt{E(v)^{2}+\|v\|^{2}}}.

We introduce the triple norm, measuring the dissipation in the system,

|||v|||^{2}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}(\tau E(v^{n+1})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|v^{n+1}-\tilde{v}^{n+1}\|^{2}).

The following elementary relationship will be useful.

(3.5)

\tau(D_{\tau}v^{n+1},v^{n+1})_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{4}\big(\|v^{n+1}\|^{2}+\|\tilde{v}^{n+2}\|^{2}-(\|v^{n}\|^{2}+\|\tilde{v}^{n+1}\|^{2})+\|v^{n+1}-\tilde{v}^{n+1}\|^{2}\big).

We will also make use of the following summation by parts formulas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
r(\cdot,\cdot)
denote a bilinear form on
V_{h}\times V_{h}
. Then
the following summation by parts formulas holds

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}r(\delta v^{n+1},w^{n+1})=r(v^{N},w^{N})-r(v^{1},w^{2})-\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}r(v^{n},\delta w^{n+1}).

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}r(\delta v^{n+1},w^{n+1})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}r(v^{n+1},w^{n+1})-\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}r(v^{n},w^{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r(v^{N},w^{N})+\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}r(v^{n},w^{n})-\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}r(v^{n},w^{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r(v^{N},w^{N})-r(v^{1},w^{2})-\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}r(v^{n},\delta w^{n+1}).

∎

A priori error estimate for BDF2-IMEX in the case

In this section we will study the error in the BDF2-IMEX method. We focus on the case
Pe>1
for simplicity. If
u
solves (
2.2
), its approximation is given by:

Find
u_{h}^{n+1}\in\mathring{V}_{h}

,

(4.1)

(D_{\tau}u_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})_{\Omega}+c_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})+a(u_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})=(f,v_{h})_{\Omega},\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h},

with
u_{h}^{i}=\pi_{h}u(\cdot,t_{i})

We can prove an error estimate in the case of

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
u

2.2
) and
\{u_{h}^{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}

(
4.1
). Let

. Furthermore, suppose that
Co

\max\{Co,Co_{4/3}\}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\|\pi_{h}u(T)-u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}\leq C(h^{2(p+1)}(\|u(t_{0})\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u(t_{1})\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2})+\mathsf{G})\mathsf{M}

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}:=

\displaystyle\big(\tau^{4}\|\partial_{t}^{3}u\|_{L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}\tau^{4}\|\partial_{t}^{2}u\|_{L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle+(\mu h^{2p}+\gamma\|\beta\|_{\infty}h^{2(p+1/2)}+\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}h^{2(p+1)})\tau\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\|u^{n+1}\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

and

\mathsf{M}=\Big(1+cT(\tau\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}+1)e^{cT(\tau\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}+1)}\Big).

Proof.

Let
w_{h}^{n}=\pi_{h}u^{n}
and let
e_{h}^{n}=w_{h}^{n}-u_{h}^{n}
where
\pi_{h}
is
defined by (
2.3

\eta_{h}^{n}=w_{h}^{n}-u^{n}

(D_{\tau}e_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})_{\Omega}+c_{h}(\tilde{e}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})+a(e_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})=L^{n+1}(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h},

\displaystyle L^{n+1}(v_{h}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{5}\Psi_{j}(v_{h}),

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(v_{h}):=

\displaystyle(D_{\tau}u^{n+1}-\partial_{t}u^{n+1},v_{h})_{\Omega},\quad

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(v_{h}):=c(\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h}),

\displaystyle\Psi_{3}(v_{h}):=

\displaystyle\gamma s(\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h}),\quad

\displaystyle\Psi_{4}(v_{h}):=a(\eta_{h}^{n+1},v_{h}),

\displaystyle\Psi_{5}(v_{h}):=

\displaystyle c(\tilde{u}^{n+1}-u^{n+1},v_{h}).\quad

All the terms above can easily be bounded. However, we have to pay
special care to
\Psi_{2}(v_{h})
. Using the skew-symmetry of

, and

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(v_{h})=

\displaystyle-c(v_{h},\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1})=-(\beta\cdot\nabla v_{h}-z_{h},\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1})_{\Omega}\leq\inf_{z_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h}}\|\beta\cdot\nabla v_{h}-z_{h}\|\|\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1}\|.

\displaystyle|\Psi_{2}(v_{h})|\leq

\displaystyle C(\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}\|v_{h}\|+\frac{\sqrt{\|\beta\|_{\infty}}}{\sqrt{h}\sqrt{\gamma}}\sqrt{\gamma}|v_{h}|_{s})\|\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1}\|.

As a consequence of this bound for
\Psi_{2}
and by bounding all the

\Psi_{i}

\displaystyle\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}\leq

\displaystyle\|D_{\tau}u^{n+1}-\partial_{t}u^{n+1}\|+\|\beta\cdot\nabla(\tilde{u}^{n+1}-u^{n+1})\|

\displaystyle+C(\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}+\frac{\sqrt{\|\beta\|_{\infty}}}{\sqrt{h}\sqrt{\gamma}})\|\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1}\|+C\sqrt{\gamma}|\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1}|_{s}+C\|\sqrt{\mu}\nabla\eta_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using (
2.19
), (
2.20
) and (
2.20
), the square of the first four terms of the right hand side can be bounded as follows.

\displaystyle\|D_{\tau}u^{n+1}-\partial_{t}u^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\tau^{3}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}^{3}u(\cdot,s)\|^{2}ds,\quad

\displaystyle\|\beta\cdot\nabla(\tilde{u}^{n+1}-u^{n+1})\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\|\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}\tau^{3}\|\partial_{t}^{2}\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(t_{n-1},t_{n+1};L^{2}(\Omega))},\quad

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\eta}_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\gamma\|\beta\|_{\infty}h^{2(p+1/2)}\|\tilde{u}^{n+1}\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\mu h^{2p}\|u^{n+1}\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Therefore, combining the above inequalities we get

\displaystyle\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\big(\tau^{4}\|\partial_{t}^{3}u\|_{L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}\tau^{4}\|\partial_{t}^{2}u\|_{L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\big)

(4.4)

\displaystyle+C(\mu h^{2p}+\gamma\|\beta\|_{\infty}h^{2(p+1/2)}+\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}h^{2(p+1)})\tau\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\|u^{n+1}\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

The result now follows if we apply (
3.18
) and (
3.19
).

4.1.
Error estimate for the material derivative

In this section we prove error estimates for the material derivative. We start with a lemma that shows that the projection of the material derivative superconverges.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
u
be the solution of (
2.2
) and
\{u_{h}^{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
be the solution of
(
4.1
). Let

,

,
\gamma>0
and

. Furthermore, suppose that

is sufficiently small. If

then

\displaystyle Ch((\gamma+1)\|\beta\|_{\infty}+1)\big(1+T(\tau\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}+1)\big)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\quad\times\big(h^{2(p+1)}(\|u(t_{0})\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u(t_{0})\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2})+\mathsf{G}\big)M

Using (
4.3

\displaystyle h\|\pi_{h}m_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle h(m_{h}^{n+1},\pi_{h}m_{h}^{n+1})_{\Omega}=S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3},

where

\displaystyle S_{1}=

\displaystyle-h\,a(e_{h}^{n+1},\pi_{h}m_{h}^{n+1}),

\displaystyle S_{2}=

\displaystyle h\|\pi_{h}m_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq

.

(4.6)

\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}h\|\pi_{h}m_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq Ch((\gamma+1)\|\beta\|_{\infty}+1)(|||e_{h}|||^{2}+\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}).

\displaystyle||||e_{h}|||^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\big(1+T(\tau\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}+1)\big)\big(\|e_{h}^{0}\|^{2}+\|e_{h}^{1}\|^{2}+\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}\big)M.

(4.7)

\displaystyle||||e_{h}|||^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\big(1+T(\tau\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2}+1)\big)\big(h^{2(p+1)}(\|u(t_{0})\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u(t_{0})\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2})+\mathsf{G}\big)M.

∎

5.
Crank Nicolson IMEX scheme

In this section we will define the Crank Nicolson-IMEX method and prove that it is stable.

The Crank Nicolson-IMEX method will read:
Find
u_{h}^{n+1}\in\mathring{V}_{h}
such that for
n\geq 1
,

(5.1)

(\delta u_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})_{\Omega}+\tau c_{h}(\hat{u}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})+\tau a(\overline{u}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})=\tau L^{n+1}(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h},

where
u_{h}^{0}
and
u_{h}^{1}
are given. Here we use the notation

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{h}^{n+1}:=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}u_{h}^{n}-\frac{1}{2}u_{h}^{n-1}=\frac{\tilde{u}_{h}^{n+1}+u_{h}^{n}}{2}.

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{h}^{n+1}:=

\displaystyle\frac{u_{h}^{n+1}+u_{h}^{n}}{2}.

We see that

(5.2)

\hat{u}_{h}^{n+1}=\overline{u}_{h}^{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}.

Here we define the triple norm as:

|||v|||^{2}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau E(v^{n+1})^{2}.

In order to prove a stability result for the Crank-Nicoloson IMEX method we need to we need two different bounds for
\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}
. One for the
Pe>1
and one for
Pe\leq 1
. The first is as follows.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
u_{h}
solve (
5.1
). If
Pe>1
and
Co
is sufficiently small, then the following estimate holds

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq CCo\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta u_{h}^{n+1}-P_{0}(\delta u_{h}^{n+1})\|^{2}+C\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}+CCo|||\overline{u}_{h}^{n+1}|||^{2}.

Moreover, if
Pe>1
and
\max\{Co_{4/3},Co\}
is sufficiently small then we have the following estimate

(5.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq C\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}+CCo|||\overline{u}_{h}^{n+1}|||^{2}+\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}.

Proof.

We see from (
5.1
) that

(\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})_{\Omega}+\tau c_{h}(\delta\hat{u}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})+\tau a(\delta\overline{u}_{h}^{n+1},v_{h})=\tau\delta L^{n+1}(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathring{V}_{h},

Therefore, we easily have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}=S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}+S_{4},

where

(5.5a)

\displaystyle S_{1}=

\displaystyle-\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}c(\delta\hat{u}_{h}^{n+1},\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1})

(5.5b)

\displaystyle S_{2}=

\displaystyle-\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\gamma s(\delta\hat{u}_{h}^{n+1},\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1})

(5.5c)

\displaystyle S_{3}=

\displaystyle-\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a(\delta\overline{u}_{h}^{n+1},\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1})

(5.5d)

\displaystyle S_{4}=

\displaystyle\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\delta L^{n+1}(\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}).

We start with an estimate of
S_{2}
. Using (
5.2
) and inverse estimates followed by Young’s inequality we get:

\displaystyle S_{4}\leq\frac{1}{8}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+C\tau(\frac{\sqrt{Co}}{\sqrt{Pe}}+\sqrt{\gamma Co}+\sqrt{\tau})^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}.

\displaystyle-\tau^{2}\gamma s(\hat{u}_{h}^{n},\psi_{h}^{n+1}),

\displaystyle M_{3}:=

\displaystyle-\tau^{2}a(\overline{u}_{h}^{n},\psi_{h}^{n+1}),

\displaystyle S_{1}\leq

The following alternative estimate will be useful when

Lemma 5.2

\displaystyle-\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}c(\delta u_{h}^{n},\delta(u_{h}^{n+1}-u_{h}^{n}))+\frac{\tau}{2}\big(c(\delta u_{h}^{N},u_{h}^{N})-c(\delta u_{h}^{1},u_{h}^{2})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}c(\delta u_{h}^{n},\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1})+\frac{\tau}{2}\big(c(\delta u_{h}^{N},u_{h}^{N})-c(\delta u_{h}^{1},u_{h}^{2})\big).

We easily have

-\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}c(\delta u_{h}^{n},\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1})\leq CCo\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta u_{h}^{n+1}-P_{0}(\delta u_{h}^{n+1})\|^{2}+CCo\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}.

The next term can similarly be bounded:

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{2}\big(c(\delta u_{h}^{N},u_{h}^{N})-c(\delta u_{h}^{1},u_{h}^{2})\big)\leq

\displaystyle CCo\|\delta u_{h}^{N}-P_{0}(\delta u_{h}^{N})\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+CCo\|\delta u_{h}^{1}-P_{0}(\delta u_{h}^{1})\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{2}\|^{2}.

Hence, we arrive at

D_{1}\leq CCo\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta u_{h}^{n+1}-P_{0}(\delta u_{h}^{n+1})\|^{2}+CCo\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}.

Which combined with the estimate for
D_{2}
gives

S_{1}\leq CCo\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta u_{h}^{n+1}-P_{0}(\delta u_{h}^{n+1})\|^{2}+CCo\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{2}\|^{2}.

Using (
5.16
) and combining the above inequalities we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|u_{h}^{1}\|^{2}+|||\overline{u}_{h}|||^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16}|||\overline{u}_{h}|||^{2}+8\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}+\frac{c_{L}}{32}\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+CCo(1+\gamma)\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta\delta u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+CCo\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|\delta u_{h}^{n+1}-P_{0}(\delta u_{h}^{n+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle+CCo\|u_{h}^{2}\|^{2}.

Applying (
5.9
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|u_{h}^{1}\|^{2}+|||\overline{u}_{h}|||^{2}\leq

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{16}+CCo^{2})|||\overline{u}_{h}|||^{2}+(8+CCo)\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle+(\frac{c_{L}}{32}+\tau CCo\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2})\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}+CCo\|u_{h}^{2}\|^{2}.

Taking
Co
sufficiently small we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\|u_{h}^{N}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|||\overline{u}_{h}|||^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|u_{h}^{1}\|^{2}+9\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|L^{n+1}\|_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle+(\frac{c_{L}}{32}+\frac{\tau}{4}\|\nabla\beta\|_{\infty}^{2})\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|u_{h}^{2}\|^{2}.

The inequality (
5.12
) follows from the above inequality and the discrete Gronwall’s inequality (
3.9
).

7.
Concluding remarks

In this paper we have considered the use of implicit-explicit time
integrators together with symmetric stabilization methods. An
important advantage is that the often nonlinear convection term is
handled explicitly as well as the stabilization, which otherwise is
known to extend the system matrix. Two second order methods were
considered that are appealing in applications for their simplicity,
but that have trivial imaginary stability boundary in the limit of
vanishing diffusion. We prove that nevertheless these methods can be
used together with stabilized FEM (or upwind discontinuous Galerkin
method) under CFL conditions that allow for an optimal matching of
errors in space and time. The present work opens for several
interesting research venues such as the use of predictor-corrector
methods
[
14
]
in combination with stabilized FEM for first
order pde, or higher
order IMEX-schemes based on Adams-Bashforth/Adams-Moulton combinations
for singularly perturbed second order systems such as
convection–diffusion or the Navier-Stokes’ equations.
