HEMPPCAT: MIXTURES OF PROBABILISTIC PRINCIPAL COMPONENT ANALYSERS FOR DATA WITH HETEROSCEDASTIC NOISE
Alec S. Xu, Laura Balzano, Jeffrey A. Fessler

Abstract
Mixtures of probabilistic principal component analysis (MPPCA) is a well-known mixture model extension of principal component analysis (PCA). Similar to PCA, MPPCA assumes the data samples in each mixture contain homoscedastic noise. However, datasets with heterogeneous noise across samples are becoming increasingly common, as larger datasets are generated by collecting samples from several sources with varying noise profiles. The performance of MPPCA is suboptimal for data with heteroscedastic noise across samples. This paper proposes a heteroscedastic mixtures of probabilistic PCA technique (HeMPPCAT) that uses a generalized expectation-maximization (GEM) algorithm to jointly estimate the unknown underlying factors, means, and noise variances under a heteroscedastic noise setting. Simulation results illustrate the improved factor estimates and clustering accuracies of HeMPPCAT compared to MPPCA.

Introduction

PCA
is a well-known unsupervised dimensionality reduction method for high-dimensional data analysis.
It has been extended to capture a mixture
of low-dimensional affine subspaces.
When this mixture model is derived through a probabilistic perspective,
it is called Mixtures of Probabilistic PCA (MPPCA)
[
1
]
.
MPPCA models
are a statistical extension
of union-of-subspace models

[
2
,
3
,
4
]

and are also related to subspace clustering methods

[
5
,
6
]
.

One can apply MPPCA
to many engineering and machine learning tasks,
such as image compression and handwritten digit classification.

However, a limitation
of PPCA and MPPCA is that
they both model the noise
as independent and identically distributed (IID) with a shared
variance, i.e., homoscedastic.
Consequently, the performance of PPCA and MPPCA
can be suboptimal for heteroscedastic noise conditions.
Heterogeneous datasets are increasingly common,
e.g.,
when combining samples
from several sources

[
7
]
, or
samples collected under
varying ambient conditions

[
8
]
.
Recently an algorithm called
HePPCAT was developed
to extend PPCA
to data with
heteroscedastic noise across samples

[
9
]
,
but no corresponding methods exist
for a
mixture
of PPCA models.
This paper generalizes MPPCA
to introduce a
He
teroscedastic
MPPCA

T
echnique (HeMPPCAT):
an MPPCA method for data with heteroscedastic noise.
This paper presents the statistical data model,
a GEM algorithm,
and results with both synthetic data
and motion segmentation
from real video data.

2
Related Work

2.1
MPPCA

The original MPPCA approach

[
1
]

models
n
data samples in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

as arising from
J
affine subspaces:

\bm{y}_{i}=\bm{F}_{j}\bm{z}_{i}+\bm{\mu}_{j}+\bm{\epsilon}_{i}

(1)

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

and some class index

j=j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J\}
.
Here

\bm{F}_{1},\dots,\bm{F}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
are deterministic factor matrices to estimate,

\bm{z}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{k},\bm{I}_{k})
are IID factor coefficients,

\bm{\mu}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are unknown deterministic mean vectors to estimate,

\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{d},v_{j}\bm{I}_{d})
are IID noise vectors,
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{J}
are unknown noise variances to estimate.

MPPCA assumes all samples from mixture component
j
have the same noise variance
v_{j}
.
In contrast,
our proposed
HeMPPCAT allows each sample from mixture
j

to come from one of
L\leq n
noise groups,
where
L\neq J
in general.

2.2
Probabilistic PCA for Heteroscedastic Signals

The authors of

[
10
]

developed a Riemannian optimization method to perform an MPPCA-like method on signals with heterogeneous power levels. They model
n
data samples in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
as

\bm{y}_{i}=\sqrt{\tau_{j}}\bm{U}_{j}\bm{z}_{i}+\bm{\epsilon}_{i}

(2)

for all
i=\{1,\dots,n\}
and some
j=j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J\}
.
The signal powers
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
known as
signal textures
, are factors to estimate,

\bm{U}_{1},\dots,\bm{U}_{J}\in\mathrm{St}_{d,k}
are orthonormal subspace bases to estimate
(
\mathrm{St}_{d,k}
denotes the
d\times k
Stiefel manifold),

\bm{z}_{i}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{k},\bm{I}_{k})
are IID subspace coefficients,
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{d},\bm{I}_{d})
are IID noise vectors.

The model
(
2
)
assumes all signals in subspace
j
have the same texture value
\tau_{j}
.
That assumption is somewhat analogous
to how the MPPCA model assumes all samples in mixture
j

have the same noise variance
v_{j}
.
Our proposed HeMPPCAT model instead allows samples in the same mixture component
to have different noise variances,
and allows different signal components
to have different signal strengths,
rather than a common scaling factor

\sqrt{\tau}
.

2.3
Covariance-Guided MPPCA

Covariance-Guided MPPCA (C-MPPCA)
[
11
]
is an MPPCA variant that estimates the factor matrices and noise variances using a pooled sample covariance matrix, rather than a global sample covariance matrix, in each GEM iteration.

C-MPPCA uses the original MPPCA model (
1
) for each sample. As a result, C-MPPCA also assumes all samples in mixture component
j
share a unique noise variance
v_{j}
.

2.4
Mixtures of Robust PPCA

Mixtures of Robust PPCA (MRPPCA)
[
12
]
is another MPPCA variant that is more robust to outlier samples. It also models each sample using (
1
), but assumes
\bm{z}_{i}\sim t_{\nu_{j}}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{d})
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\sim t_{\nu_{j}}(\bm{0},v_{j}^{-1}\bm{I}_{d})
, where
t_{\nu}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda})
denotes the multivariate
t
-distribution with degrees of freedom
\nu
, mean
\bm{\mu}
, and scale matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
. The degrees of freedoms
\nu_{j}
are additional parameters to estimate.

Again, like MPPCA and C-MPPCA, MRPPCA assumes all samples in mixture component
j
have variance
v_{j}
.

3
HeMPPCAT Data Model

We assume
there are
n_{1}+\dots+n_{L}=n
data samples in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

from
L
different noise groups
with model

\bm{y}_{\ell,i}=\bm{F}_{j}\bm{z}_{\ell,i}+\bm{\mu}_{j}+\bm{\epsilon}_{\ell,i}

(3)

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n_{\ell}\}
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
,
and some
j=j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J\}
.

\bm{F}_{1},\dots,\bm{F}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
are unknown factor matrices to estimate
(
not
constrained to the Stiefel manifold,
so different signal components
can have different amplitudes),

\bm{z}_{\ell,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{k},\bm{I}_{k})
are IID coefficients,
and
\bm{\mu}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are unknown mean vectors to estimate.
To model heteroscedastic noise, we assume

\bm{\epsilon}_{\ell,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{d},v_{\ell}\bm{I}_{d})
,
where
v_{1},\dots,v_{L}
are unknown noise variances to estimate.
Importantly,
the noise model associates noise variance with data sample,
regardless of the underlying affine subspace.

The joint log-likelihood of the samples is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{F},\bm{\mu},\bm{v},\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\mathrm{ln}\big\{p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i})\big\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\mathrm{ln}\Bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\pi_{j}p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}\>|\>j)\Bigg\},

(4)

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}\>|\>j)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\mathrm{det}(\bm{C}_{\ell,j})^{-1/2}\mathrm{exp}(-E_{\ell ij}^{2}/2),

\displaystyle E_{\ell ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j})^{T}\bm{C}_{\ell,j}^{-1}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j}),

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{\ell,j}

\displaystyle=\bm{F}_{j}\bm{F}^{T}_{j}+v_{\ell}\bm{I}_{d},

where
\bm{F}=[\bm{F}_{1},\dots,\bm{F}_{J}]
,
\bm{\mu}=[\bm{\mu}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{J}]
,
\bm{v}=[v_{1},\dots,v_{L}]
,
and
\bm{p}=[\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{J}]
,
with
\pi_{j}
being the
j
th
mixing proportion
,
such that
\pi_{j}\geq 0
and
\sum_{j}\pi_{j}=1
.

Conceptually,
to draw samples from this model,
one first picks a class
j

according to the categorical distribution
with parameters
\bm{p}
,
and then picks random coefficients
to multiply the factor matrix
\bm{F}_{j}
,
and then adds noise,
where the noise variance
depends on the noise group.

4
GEM algorithm

The joint log-likelihood
(
3
) is nonconvex with respect to
the parameters of interest.
MPPCA
[
1
]

maximized a similar expression
using an EM method.
We derived
a GEM
algorithm
to maximize (
3
) with respect to the parameters
\bm{F}
,
\bm{\mu}
,
\bm{v}
, and
\bm{p}
,
using a natural

complete-data log-likelihood
formulation.

Let
\bm{z}_{\ell ij}
denote the coefficients associated with mixture
j
for sample
\bm{y}_{\ell,i}
,
and let
g_{\ell ij}

denote random variables having a
categorical distribution
where
g_{\ell ij}=1
indicates “mixture
j
generated sample
\bm{y}_{\ell,i}
.”
Treating
\bm{z}_{\ell ij}
and
g_{\ell ij}
as missing data,
the complete-data log-likelihood is

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{L}_{C}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}g_{\ell ij}\mathrm{ln}\big\{\pi_{j}p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i},\bm{z}_{\ell ij})\big\},\\
p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i},\bm{z}_{\ell ij})=p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}|\bm{z}_{\ell ij})p(\bm{z}_{\ell ij})\\
\bm{y}_{\ell,i}|\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{F}_{j}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}+\bm{\mu}_{j},v_{\ell}\bm{I})\\
p(\bm{z}_{\ell ij})=(2\pi)^{-k/2}\mathrm{exp}\{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\},\end{gathered}

(5)

where
\bm{\theta}=[\bm{F},\bm{\mu},\bm{v},\bm{p}]

is shorthand for all the parameters to estimate.

4.1
Expectation step

For the E-step of the GEM algorithm,
we compute the expectation of (
5
)
conditioned on the current iterate’s parameter estimates.
Ignoring irrelevant constants,
one can show this expression is

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{L}_{C}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}R_{\ell ij}\Big\{\mathrm{ln}(\pi_{j})-\frac{d}{2}\mathrm{ln}(v_{\ell})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\Big(\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle\Big)-\frac{1}{2v_{\ell}}\|\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle^{T}\bm{F}_{j}^{T}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2v_{\ell}}\mathrm{tr}\Big(\bm{F}^{T}_{j}\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle\Big)\Big\},

(6)

where

\bm{\theta}^{(t)}=[\bm{F}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}^{(t)},\bm{v}^{(t)},\bm{p}^{(t)}]

denotes the parameter estimates at iteration
t
,

\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes conditional expectation,
the conditional moments
\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle
and
\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle
are given by

\begin{gathered}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle=\bm{M}_{\ell,j}^{(t)\>-1}\bm{F}^{(t)\>T}_{j}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}^{(t)}_{j}),\\
\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle=v^{(t)}_{\ell}\bm{M}_{\ell,j}^{(t)\>-1}+\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle^{T},\\
\bm{M}_{\ell,j}^{(t)}=v^{(t)}_{\ell}\bm{I}_{k}+\bm{F}^{(t)\>T}_{j}\bm{F}^{(t)}_{j},\end{gathered}

(7)

and
R_{\ell ij}
is the posterior
mixing responsibility

of mixture component
j

generating sample
\bm{y}_{\ell,i}
:

R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}=\frac{p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}\>|\>j)\,\pi_{j}}{p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i})},

(8)

where the probabilities are evaluated
at the current parameter estimates

\bm{\theta}^{(t)}
.

4.2
Maximization step

For the M-step,
it appears impractical
to maximize (
4.1
)
over all parameters simultaneously,
so we adopt a
GEM approach

[
13
]

where we update subsets of parameters
in sequence.

Maximizing (
4.1
)
with respect to
\pi_{j}
,
v_{\ell}
,
\bm{\mu}_{j}
, and
\bm{F}_{j}
,
in that sequence,
results in the following M-step update expressions
(derivations omitted due to page limits):

\displaystyle\pi^{(t+1)}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}

\displaystyle v^{(t+1)}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}\Bigg[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}\|\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}^{(t)}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle^{T}\bm{F}^{(t)\>T}_{j}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}^{(t)}_{j})

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}\mathrm{tr}\bigg(\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle\bm{F}^{(t)\>T}_{j}\bm{F}^{(t)}_{j}\bigg)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{(t+1)}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v^{(t+1)}_{\ell}}[\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{F}^{(t)}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle]}{\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v^{(t+1)}_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{j}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v^{(t+1)}_{\ell}}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}^{(t+1)}_{j})\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle^{T}\Big)

\displaystyle\cdot\Big(\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v^{(t+1)}_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle\Big)^{-1}.

These expressions naturally generalize
those in
[
1
]
.
For the subsequent results,
we initialized the parameter estimates
by using final MPPCA estimates. MPPCA was initialized using 1000 iterations of
K
-Planes
[
14
]

[
15
]
.

Synthetic Datasets

We generated 25 separate synthetic datasets.
Each dataset contained
n=10^{3}

d=10^{2}

),
where there were

k=3

J=3

The factor matrices were generated as

\bm{F}_{j}=\bm{U}_{j}\mathrm{Diag}^{1/2}(\bm{\lambda})
for

,
where
\bm{U}_{j}\in\mathrm{St}_{d,k}

and
\bm{\lambda}=(16,9,4)

The elements of the mean vectors

In all 25 datasets, the first

Mixtures 1 and 2 each generated 250 of these samples,

The other

,

We applied K-Planes,
MPPCA, and HeMPPCAT
to compute estimates

\hat{\bm{F}}_{j}

of the underlying factors

\bm{F}_{j}

across all 25 datasets.
In every dataset,
we recorded
the normalized estimation errors

.

,

5.2
Hopkins 155 Dataset

In computer vision,
motion segmentation is
the task of segmenting
moving objects in a
video sequence into several
independent regions, each
corresponding to a different motion.
The Hopkins 155 dataset is a
series of 155 video sequences containing
bodies in motion.
The dataset has the coordinates of

n
feature points
\bm{x}_{f,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

that were tracked across
F
video frames.
A feature point
trajectory

is formed by stacking
the feature points across the frames:

\bm{y}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1,i}^{T}&\dots&\bm{x}_{F,i}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
.
These trajectories are clustered
to segment the motions
using affine
subspace clustering methods.
A single body’s trajectories lie
in an affine subspace with dimension
of at most 4
[
16
]
,
so the trajectories of
J
bodies
lie in a union of

J
affine subspaces.
The dataset
includes the ground-truth cluster
assignments for each trajectory.

Many motion segmentation methods
assume the moving bodies are
rigid
.
In practice, many common objects of interest
do not satisfy
rigid body motion.
For instance, feature points
on a walking human’s legs
move with different velocities
than feature points on the torso.
These differences can be modeled
as heteroscedastic noise:
trajectories of the leg feature points
may lie in a different noise group
than those of the torso.

Each video sequence
contains either
J=2
or
J=3
moving bodies.
To simulate nonrigid body motion,
we added synthetic Gaussian noise
to the trajectories.
In each sequence,
we created
L=3
synthetic
noise groups with variances

v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}

corresponding to
signal to noise ratio (SNR) values of

-30,-25,
and
-20
dB
relative to
that sequence’s maximum trajectory

\ell_{2}
norm.
Noise groups 1, 2, and 3 contained
50%, 35%, and 15%
of all trajectories, respectively.

For each sequence,
we divided the dataset of trajectories
into train and test sets
using an 80/20 split.
We applied K-Planes, MPPCA, and HeMPPCAT on the
train set, and then used
parameter estimates
from all methods
to classify trajectories in the test set.
The test trajectories were
classified based on nearest affine subspace
using the K-Planes estimates,
and by
maximum likelihood
using MPPCA and HeMPPCAT estimates,
i.e., the
predicted body for a test point
\bm{y}
was

\operatorname{argmax}\limits_{j}\hat{\pi}_{j}p(\bm{y}\>|\>j;\hat{\bm{\theta}})
,
where
\hat{\pi}_{j}
is the estimated mixing proportion
of body
j
.
We computed

p(\bm{y}\>|\>j;\hat{\bm{\theta}})

according to the approaches’
respective data models
(
1
) and (
3
).

6
Conclusion

This paper generalized MPPCA to jointly estimate
the underlying factors, means, and noise variances
from data with heteroscedastic noise.
The proposed EM algorithm sequentially updates the mixing proportions,
noise variances, means, and factor estimates.
Experimental results
on synthetic and the Hopkins 155 datasets
illustrate the benefit of accounting for heteroscedastic noise.

There are several possible interesting directions for future work.
We could generalize this approach even further
by accounting for other cases of heterogeneity,
e.g., missing data or heteroscedastic noise across features.
There may be faster convergence variants of EM
such as
a space-alternating generalized EM (SAGE) approach

[
17
]

that could be explored.
Another direction could be jointly estimating
the number of noise groups
L
and mixtures
J
along with the other parameters.

Appendix A
Complete-Data Log-Likelihood Expansion

We first compute the expression for
p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i},\bm{z}_{\ell ij})=p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}\>|\>\bm{z}_{\ell ij})p(\bm{z}_{\ell ij})
. Given the coefficients
\bm{z}_{\ell ij}
, the only random component in sample
\bm{y}_{\ell,i}
is due to the Gaussian noise vector
\bm{\epsilon}_{\ell,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{k},v_{\ell}\bm{I})
:

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}\>|\>\bm{z}_{\ell ij})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\mathrm{det}(v_{\ell}\bm{I})^{-1/2}\mathrm{exp}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{F}_{j}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}-\bm{\mu}_{j})^{T}(v_{\ell}\bm{I})^{-1}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{F}_{J}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}-\bm{\mu}_{j})\Big\}

(9)

\displaystyle=(2\pi v_{\ell})^{-d/2}\mathrm{exp}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2v_{\ell}}\|\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{F}_{j}-\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}.

(10)

The coefficients
\bm{z}_{\ell ij}
are assumed to be standard Gaussian vectors:

\displaystyle p(\bm{z}_{\ell ij})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-k/2}\mathrm{det}(\bm{I})^{-1/2}\mathrm{exp}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\bm{I}^{-1}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\Big\}

(11)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-k/2}\mathrm{exp}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\Big\}.

(12)

Multiplying the two expressions above results in the following joint distribution:

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{\ell,i},\bm{z}_{\ell ij})=(2\pi v_{\ell})^{-d/2}\mathrm{exp}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2v_{\ell}}\|\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{F}_{j}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}-\bm{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}(2\pi)^{-k/2}\mathrm{exp}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\Big\}.

(13)

Substituting (
13
) into (
5
) results in the following complete-data log-likelihood expression:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

and
\bm{F}_{j}
, respectively, makes the expression match the corresponding equation in Section
4.2
.

C.3
Means

Deriving the update expression for each mean
\bm{\mu}_{j}
is equivalent to maximizing

f_{3}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}R_{\ell ij}\bigg\{-\frac{1}{2v_{\ell}}\|\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle^{T}\bm{F}^{T}_{j}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu})\bigg\}

(22)

with respect to
\bm{\mu}_{j}
. Choosing an arbitrary
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
:

\begin{gathered}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{j}}f_{3}=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}}{v_{\ell}}\bigg\{\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j}-\bm{F}_{j}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bigg\},\\
\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}}{v_{\ell}}\bigg\{\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j}^{(t+1)}-\bm{F}_{j}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bigg\}=0,\\
\bm{\mu}_{j}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}[\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle]}{\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}}.\end{gathered}

This holds for all
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
. Using the current iterate’s estimate
\bm{F}_{j}^{(t)}
for
\bm{F}_{j}
, and the new estimate
v^{(t+1)}_{\ell}
for
v_{\ell}
, makes the expression match the corresponding equation in Section
4.2
.

C.4
Factor Matrices

Deriving the update expression for each factor matrix
\bm{F}_{j}
is equivalent to maximizing

f_{4}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}R_{\ell ij}\bigg\{\frac{1}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle^{T}\bm{F}^{T}_{j}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu})-\frac{1}{2v_{\ell}}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{F}^{T}_{j}\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle)\bigg\}

(23)

with respect to
\bm{F}_{j}
. Choosing an arbitrary
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
:

\displaystyle f_{4}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}\bigg\{\frac{1}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle^{T}\bm{F}^{T}_{j}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu})-\frac{1}{2v_{\ell}}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{F}^{T}_{j}\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\bigg\{\mathrm{tr}\Big((\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j})^{T}\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle\Big)-\mathrm{tr}(\bm{F}^{T}_{j}\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\bigg\{\mathrm{tr}\Big(\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j})^{T}\Big)-\mathrm{tr}(\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle\bm{F}^{T}_{j})\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\Bigg(\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j})^{T}-\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\bm{F}_{j}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle\bm{F}^{T}_{j}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\Bigg(\bm{F}_{j}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j})^{T}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{F}_{j}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle\bm{F}^{T}_{j}\Bigg).

Let
\bm{B}_{j}:=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j})^{T}
and
\bm{K}_{j}:=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle
.

\displaystyle f_{4}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\big(\bm{F}_{j}\bm{B}_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{F}_{j}\bm{K}_{j}\bm{F}^{T}_{j}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\big(\bm{F}_{j}\bm{K}_{j}^{1/2}\bm{K}_{j}^{-1/2}\bm{B}_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{F}_{j}\bm{K}_{j}^{1/2}\bm{K}_{j}^{1/2}\bm{F}^{T}_{j}\big),

where
\bm{A}^{1/2}
denotes the principal square root of a positive semidefinite matrix
\bm{A}
, and
\bm{A}^{-1/2}
denotes the inverse
\bm{A}^{1/2}
if it is positive definite. Now let
\bm{X}_{j}=\bm{F}_{j}\bm{K}_{j}^{1/2}
and
\tilde{\bm{B}}_{j}=\bm{K}_{j}^{-1/2}\bm{B}_{j}
.

\begin{gathered}f_{4}=\mathrm{tr}\big(\bm{X}_{j}\tilde{\bm{B}}_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{X}_{j}\bm{X}_{j}^{T}\big),\\
\nabla_{\bm{X}_{j}}f_{4}=\tilde{\bm{B}}_{j}-\bm{X}_{j},\\
\tilde{\bm{B}}_{j}-\hat{\bm{X}}_{j}=0,\\
\bm{F}^{(t+1)}_{j}\bm{K}_{j}^{1/2}=\bm{B}_{j}^{T}\bm{K}_{j}^{-1/2},\\
\bm{F}^{(t+1)}_{j}=\bm{B}_{j}^{T}\bm{K}_{j}^{-1}=\bigg[\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}(\bm{y}_{\ell,i}-\bm{\mu}_{j})\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\rangle^{T}\bigg]\bigg[\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\frac{R_{\ell ij}^{(t)}}{v_{\ell}}\langle\bm{z}_{\ell ij}\bm{z}_{\ell ij}^{T}\rangle\bigg]^{-1}.\end{gathered}

This holds for all
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
. Using the new estimates
v^{(t+1)}_{\ell}
and
\bm{\mu}_{j}^{(t+1)}
for
v_{\ell}
and
\bm{\mu}_{j}
, respectively, makes this expression match the corresponding equation in Section
4.2
.
