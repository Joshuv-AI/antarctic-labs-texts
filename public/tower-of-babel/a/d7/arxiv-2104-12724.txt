Quantum explosions of black holes and thermal coordinates
Irina Aref’eva and, Igor Volovich

Abstract
The Hawking temperature for Schwarzschild black hole T H = 1 / 8 ​ π ​ M T_{H}=1/8\pi M is singular in the limit of vanishing mass M → 0 M\to 0 . However, the Schwarzschild metric itself is regular when the black hole mass M M tends to zero, it is reduced to the Minkowski metric and there are no reasons to believe that the temperature becomes infinite. It is pointed out that this discrepancy may be due to the fact that the Kruskal coordinates are singular in the limit of the vanishing mass of the black hole. To improve the situation, new coordinates for the Schwarzschild metric are introduced, called thermal coordinates, which depend on the black hole mass M M and the parameter b b . The thermal coordinates are regular in the limit of vanishing black hole mass M M . In this limit, the Schwarzschild metric is reduced to the Minkowski metric, written in coordinates dual to the Rindler coordinates. Using the thermal coordinates the Schwarzschild black hole radiation is reconsidered and it is found that the Hawking formula for temperature is valid only for large black holes while for small black holes the temperature is T = 1 / 2 ​ π ​ ( 4 ​ M + b ) T=1/2\pi(4M+b) . The thermal observer in Minkowski space sees radiation with temperature T = 1 / 2 ​ π ​ b T=1/2\pi b , similar to the Unruh effect with non-constant acceleration. Now, during evaporation, in the thermal coordinates the black hole mass is decreasing inverse proportional to time and the black hole life time is infinite. The thermal coordinates for more general spherically symmetric metrics, including the Reissner-Nordstrom, de Sitter and anti-de Sitter are also considered. In these coordinates one sees a Planck distribution with constant temperature. One obtains that the property to have a temperature distribution for quantum fields in classical gravitational background is not restricted to the cases of black holes or constant acceleration, but is valid for any spherically symmetric metric written in thermal coordinates. Implications for primordial black holes and for the information loss problem are mentioned.

4.1
E-coordinates

We define here an extension of E-coordinates to the arbitrary static metric of the form

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+f(r)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}

(95)

Define also

r_{*}=r_{*}(r)=\int\frac{dr}{f(r)}

(96)

and general Eddington-Finkilstein coordinates

(97)

ds^{2}_{2}=-f(r)dudv.

(98)

E-coordinates

are defined by the relations

{\mathscr{U}}=-e^{-u/B},\,\,{\mathscr{V}}=e^{v/B},\,\,B>0.

(99)

Now the the 2-dim part of the metric (

e^{2r_{*}/B}=-{\mathscr{U}}{\mathscr{V}}.

(101)

By introducing the coordinates
{\mathscr{T}}=({\mathscr{V}}+{\mathscr{U}})/2
and
{\mathscr{X}}=({\mathscr{V}}-{\mathscr{U}})/2
the metric can be written in the form

ds^{2}_{2}=f(r)B^{2}\frac{-d{\mathscr{T}}^{2}+d{\mathscr{X}}^{2}}{-{\mathscr{T}}^{2}+{\mathscr{X}}^{2}},

(102)

where
{\mathscr{X}}^{2}-{\mathscr{T}}^{2}>0.

Note that if
r_{*}
and
r
are constants, then from (
101
)
rewritten as

{\mathscr{X}}^{2}-{\mathscr{T}}^{2}=e^{2r_{*}/B}=const,

(103)

It follows that we deal with the motion on a hyperbola (
103
).

In the same way as in Sect.
2.3
, one can show that in the E-coordinates introduced above

the E-observer will see the Planck distribution of quanta with temperature

5.2
Acceleration along trajectories
\xi=\xi_{0}
in black hole backgrounds

Let us consider an observer moving along this hyperbola, i.e. along the trajectory (
147
)
with
\xi=\xi_{0}
in
(t,r_{*})
-plane. One can parametrize this trajectory as

\displaystyle r_{*}=r_{*}(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{a\xi_{0}}\cosh(a\eta)}{a},

(151)

\displaystyle t=t(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{a\xi_{0}}\sinh(a\eta)}{a}.

(152)

This parametrization means that
r_{*}>0,
or, more precisely, that
r_{*}>\frac{e^{a\xi_{0}}}{a}
.

One can find velocity and acceleration along this trajectory.
Indeed, along this trajectory the interval is

\displaystyle ds=\sqrt{f(r)}e^{a\xi_{0}}d\eta,\quad\mbox{here}\quad ds=|ds|,

(153)

and components of the velocity along this trajectory are

\displaystyle u^{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{dt}{ds}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{f(r)}}\cosh(a\eta),

\displaystyle u^{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{dr}{ds}=\sqrt{f(r)}\sinh(a\eta).

(154)

We see
that the square of velocity is equal to -1,
-f(u^{0})^{2}+f^{-1}(u^{1})^{2}=-1
.

The components of acceleration are

\displaystyle w^{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{du^{0}}{ds}+\Gamma^{0}_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=\frac{\sinh(a\eta)}{f}\left(ae^{-a\xi_{0}}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\cosh a\eta\right),

(155)

and

\displaystyle w^{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{du^{1}}{ds}+\Gamma^{1}_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=\cosh(a\eta)\left(\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\cosh(a\eta)+ae^{-a\xi_{0}}\right),

(156)

\displaystyle w^{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\vartheta}_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=0,

(157)

\displaystyle w^{\varphi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\varphi}_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=0,

(158)

and

\displaystyle w^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-f(w^{0})^{2}+\frac{1}{f}(w^{1})^{2}=\frac{a^{2}e^{-2a\xi_{0}}}{f}\left(\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}r_{*}(r)+1\right)^{2}.

(159)

One can also check the orthogonality condition

-fu^{0}w^{0}+f^{-1}u^{1}w^{1}=0
.

5.4
Temperature in L-coordinates

Here we show that the accelerated observer moving along special trajectories defined by requirement
\xi=\xi_{0}

[[specified this condition in term of the original coordinates, the form of trajectory essentially depends on the blackening function
f(r)
]] see Hawking radiation with temperature, defined by only by parameter
a
.

T=\frac{a}{2\pi}.

(161)

Indeed, comparing the solution of the wave equation in
(u,v)
and
(\nu,\vartheta)
coordinates related as in (
140
)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\vartheta}\phi=0,\,\,\nu,\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}

(162)

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\partial_{u}\,\Phi=0,\quad u<0,\,v>0

(163)

we get relation between corresponding creation and annihilation operators. For this purpose we write as usual, the representation
for solutions of 2-dimensional wave equations as combinations of the left and right modes,

\phi(\nu,\vartheta)=\phi_{R}(\nu)+\phi_{L}(\vartheta)
,
\Phi(u,v)=\Phi_{R}(u)+\Phi_{L}(v)
.
For the real right mode (for the left mode all consideration is similar) one has

\displaystyle\phi_{R}(\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega(f_{\omega}B_{\omega}+f_{\omega}^{*}B^{+}_{\omega}),\quad f_{\omega}(\nu)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\omega}}e^{-i\omega\nu},

(164)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{R}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mu\Big(\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}(u)+\mathfrak{B}^{+}_{\mu}\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}^{*}(u)\Big),\quad\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\mu}}e^{-i\mu u},

where

[\mathfrak{B}_{\mu},\mathfrak{B}^{+}_{\mu^{\prime}}]=\delta(\mu-\mu^{\prime})
.

Right (and left) modes in different coordinate system are related as

\phi_{R}(\nu)=\Phi_{R}(u)
and therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega(f_{\omega}B_{\omega}+f_{\omega}^{*}B^{+}_{\omega})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mu(\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}+\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}^{*}\mathfrak{B}^{+}_{\mu}).

(166)

Multiplying (
40
) on

\displaystyle B_{\omega}=\int d\mu\Big(\beta^{*}_{\omega\,\mu}\mathfrak{B}^{+}_{\mu}+\alpha^{*}_{\omega\,\mu}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}\Big),\qquad B^{+}_{\omega}

6
Characteristic times in the black holes space times

6.1
Time of black hole evaporation

The change of the black hole mass due to evaporation is described by equation

\frac{dM(t)}{dt}=-L

(174)

where
L
is the luminosity,

L=C\,T^{4}\cdot{\mbox{Area}}
and

C
is a constant. In our case

T=1/2\pi(b+4M),
and

\mbox{Area}=16\pi M^{2}

so

L=\frac{C\,M^{2}}{\pi^{3}(b+4M)^{4}}

(175)

and we have the equation

\displaystyle\frac{dM}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{C\,M^{2}}{\pi^{3}(b+4M)^{4}},\quad M(0)=M_{0}

(176)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{M_{0}}^{M}\,\frac{(b+4M^{\prime})^{4}}{M^{\prime 2}}\,dM^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{C}{\pi^{3}}\,t,

(178)

Taking the integral we get

\displaystyle-\frac{b^{4}}{M}+8b^{3}\log(M)+96b^{2}M+128bM^{2}+\frac{256M^{3}}{3}=\frac{C}{\pi^{3}}\,(t_{0}-t),

(179)

where
t_{0}
is

t_{0}=\frac{\pi^{3}}{C}\Big(-\frac{b^{4}}{M_{0}}+8b^{3}\log(M_{0})+96b^{2}M_{0}+128bM_{0}^{2}+\frac{256M_{0}^{3}}{3}\Big)

(180)

The evaporation time
t=t_{evap.time}
is the time, when
M(t_{evap.time})=\epsilon\to 0
. In our case the leading terms are the first two terms in the LHS of (
179
) and

-\frac{b^{4}}{\epsilon}+8b^{3}\log(\epsilon)=\frac{C}{\pi^{3}}\,(t_{0}-t_{evap.time}),

(181)

so we obtain

t_{evap.time}\sim\frac{\pi^{3}}{C}(\frac{b^{4}}{\epsilon}-8b^{3}\log\epsilon)+t_{0}\to\infty.

(182)

Or, in other words

M(t)=\frac{\pi^{3}}{C}\frac{b^{4}}{t}\to 0\qquad\mbox{when}\qquad t\to\infty

(183)

Note that the leading term is independent on the initial mass of the black hole.
Note also that if we set
b=0

then the evaporation time is

t_{evap.time}=\frac{256\pi^{3}}{3C}M_{0}^{3}.

(184)

6.2
Small black holes and free falling observer

Light falling on black hole in the Schwarzschild spacetime is described by equation

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r_{h}}^{r}\,\frac{dr}{(1-\frac{r_{h}}{r})},

(185)

where
r_{h}=2M
. Here the integral is divergent at
r=r_{h}
as

r_{h}\ln(r-r_{h})
. Usually one concludes an asymptotics of approaching to the horizon is

\displaystyle r-r_{h}=const\,e^{-\frac{t}{r_{h}}}.

(186)

We consider the question about the limit
r_{h}\to 0
in more detailed. The solution of the equation

-dt=\frac{dr}{(1-\frac{r_{h}}{r})},\quad r(0)=r_{0}>r_{h}

(187)

is

-t+r_{0}-r=2M\log(\frac{r-2M}{r_{0}-2M}).

(188)

When
M\to 0
one gets

r=r_{0}-t
, as it should be.

From the other site if we rewrite equation (
188
) in the form

r=2M+(r_{0}-2M)e^{\frac{r_{0}-t-r}{2M}}

(189)

then it is not obvious how to take the limit
M\to 0
.

Let us discuss the leading term when the regularization parameters
B_{!}

and
B_{2}
are introduced

\displaystyle t=\int_{r_{h}+B_{1}}^{r}\,\frac{dr}{(1-\frac{r_{h}+B_{2}}{r})},\qquad B_{1}>B_{2}>0.

(190)

We have

\displaystyle r=r_{h}+B_{2}+(B_{1}-B_{2})e^{\frac{-t+r-r_{h}-B_{1}}{r_{h}+B_{2}}}

(191)

and for
r
near
r_{h}
and large
t
one obtains

\displaystyle r=r_{h}+B_{2}+(B_{1}-B_{2})e^{-\frac{t}{r_{h}+B_{2}}}

(192)

If
r_{h}
is large we can set
B_{2}=0
and we get the asymptotic formula (
186
). However for small black holes we can take the limit
r_{h}\to 0
in
(
191
) to get

\displaystyle r=B_{2}+(B_{1}-B_{2})e^{-\frac{t}{B_{2}}}

(193)

This consideration was a motivation to introduce the E-coordinates.

7
Discussions and Conclusions

It is shown that the property to have a temperature distribution for quantum fields in classical gravitational background is not restricted to the cases of black holes or constant acceleration, but is valid for any spherically symmetric metric written in thermal coordinates.

The Hawking temperature for Schwarzschild black hole
T_{H}=1/8\pi M
is singular in the limit of vanishing mass
M\to 0
. So, as the result of evaporation one gets an explosion of black hole. This is clearly unphysical since the Schwarzschild metric in the original coordinates is regular when the black hole mass
M
tends to zero. It is reduced to the Minkowski metric and there are no reasons to believe that the temperature becomes infinite.

To improve the situation, new coordinates, called thermal coordinates, which depend on the black hole mass
M
and the parameter
b
that defines the semi-axis of a hyperbola along which an observer is moving
are used. Using the thermal coordinates the Schwarzschild black hole radiation is reconsidered and it is found that the Hawking formula for temperature is valid only for large black holes while for small black holes the temperature is
T=1/2\pi(4M+b)
.
The thermal coordinates are regular in the limit of vanishing black hole mass
M
. In this limit, the Schwarzschild metric is reduced to the Minkowski metric, written in coordinates dual to the Rindler coordinates.
The thermal observer in Minkowski space
sees radiation with temperature
T=1/2\pi b
, similar to the Unruh effect but in our case the acceleration is not a constant.

During evaporation, in the thermal coordinates with
b\neq 0
the black hole mass is decreasing inverse proportional to time and the black hole life time is infinite. This is in a contrast with the case
b=0
when the time life of black hole is finite. Sometimes the information paradox is formulated as follows.
A collapsing black hole, described by a wave function, completely evaporates and leaves only radiation, described by a dense matrix.
So one gets a transformation of pure state to a mixed state, that contradicts to the unitary evolution in quantum mechanics.
Thus, a transformation of a pure state into a mixed one is occurred, which contradicts the unitary evolution in quantum mechanics, and means
the loss of information. This formulation of the information lost paradox has a meaning only if the black hole time life is finite. In the case of using the
E-coordinates the black hole life time is infinite. Therefore, formally speaking, the information loss paradox disappears.

It would be interesting
to estimate the entropy balance during the evaporation in the thermal coordinates.
It would also be interesting to study the fate of primordial black holes in the thermal coordinates and to use the thermal coordinates for investigation of massive fields of various spins in different dimensions in gravitational backgrounds.
