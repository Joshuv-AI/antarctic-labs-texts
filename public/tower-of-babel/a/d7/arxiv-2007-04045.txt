Abstract
In the work of Mukhin and Varchenko from 2002 there was introduced a Wronskian map from the variety of full flags in a finite dimensional vector space into a product of projective spaces. We establish a precise relationship between this map and the Plücker map. This allows us to recover the result of Varchenko and Wright saying that the polynomials appearing in the image of the Wronsky map are the initial values of the tau-functions for the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili hierarchy.

Introduction

Let
G={\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
T\subset B_{-}\subset G
the subgroups of diagonal and lower triangular
matrices,
X=B_{-}\backslash G
the variety of full flags in
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

We have the
Plücker embedding

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}=(\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{n-1})\colon\ X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}^{C_{n}^{i}-1},

where
C_{n}^{i}=\binom{n}{i}
.
On the other hand, in
[
9
]
there was introduced a map

\mathfrak{W}\colon\ X\longrightarrow\big(\mathbb{P}^{N}\big)^{n-1}

(
N
being big enough), which we call the
Wronskian map
since its definition uses a lot of Wronskians. This map has been studied in
[
10
]
. We will see below that
\mathfrak{W}
lands in a subspace

\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}^{i(n-i)-1}\subset\big(\mathbb{P}^{N}\big)^{n-1},

so we will consider it as a map

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}=(\mathfrak{W}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{W}_{n-1})\colon\ X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\prime}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}^{i(n-i)-1}.

(1.1)

The present note, which may be regarded as a postscript to
[
10
]

\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}
and
\mathfrak{W}
.

We define for each
1\leq i\leq n-1
a linear

c_{i}\colon\ \mathbb{P}^{C_{n}^{i}-1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{i(n-i)-1},

such that

\mathfrak{W}_{i}=c_{i}\circ\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{i},

see Theorem

.

For

; let

2
Wronsky map

We fix a base commutative ring
\mathbf{k}\supset\mathbb{Q}
.
Let

\mathfrak{f}=(f_{1}(t),\dots,f_{n}(t))

be a sequence of rational functions
f_{i}(t)\in\mathbf{k}(t)
. Its Wronskian matrix
is by definition an
n\times n
matrix

\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{f})=\big(f_{i}^{(j)}(t)\big),

where
f^{(j)}(t)
denotes the
j
-th derivative.

The determinant of
\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{f})
is
the
Wronskian
of
\mathfrak{f}
:

W(\mathfrak{f})=\det(\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{f})).

If
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbf{k})
is a scalar matrix,

\displaystyle W(\mathfrak{f}A)=\det(A)W(\mathfrak{f}),

\displaystyle W(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W(f_{1},\dots,f^{\prime}_{i},\dots,f_{n}).

2.1
The map
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{W}}

Let

M=(b_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n,m}(\mathbf{k})

be a rectangular matrix. Let us associate to it a sequence of polynomials of degree

m-1

\mathfrak{b}(M)=(b_{1}(M,t),\dots,b_{n}(M,t)),\qquad b_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}b_{i,j+1}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}.

In general we identify the space of polynomials of degree
\leq m-1
with the space
of
\mathbf{k}
-points of an affine space:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}[t]_{\leq m-1}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{A}^{m+1}(\mathbf{k}),\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}b_{j}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\mapsto(b_{0},\dots,b_{m-1}).

For
1\leq j\leq n
let
\mathfrak{b}_{\leq j}(M)
denote the truncated sequence

\mathfrak{b}(M)_{\leq j}=(b_{1}(M,t),\dots,b_{j}(M,t)).

We define a sequence of polynomials

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}(M)=(y_{1}(M),\dots,y_{n-1}(M)):=(W(\mathfrak{b}(M)_{\leq 1}),\dots,W(\mathfrak{b}(M)_{\leq n-1}))\in\mathbf{k}[t]^{n-1}.

Note that if
n=m
then

W(\mathfrak{b}(M))=\det M,

it is a constant polynomial.

If
A\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(k)
then

\mathfrak{b}(AM)=\mathfrak{b}(M)A^{t}.

It follows that if
e_{ij}(a)
,
i>j
, is a lower triangular elementary matrix then

\mathfrak{b}(e_{ij}(a)M)=(b_{1}(M),\dots,b_{j}(M)+ab_{i}(M),b_{j+1}(M),\dots),

whence

\mathfrak{W}(e_{ij}(a)M))=\mathfrak{W}(M).

It follows that for any
A\in N_{-}(\mathbf{k})
(a lower triangular with
1
’s on the diagonal)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}(AM)=\mathfrak{W}(M).

(2.1)

On the other hand, if

D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbf{k})

then

\mathfrak{W}(DM)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{W}(M).

In other words, if
A\in B_{-}(n,\mathbf{k})
(the lower Borel),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}(AM)=\det(A)\mathfrak{W}(M),

(2.2)

which of course is seen immediately.

2.2
Degrees and Bruhat decomposition

Suppose that
n=m
. We will denote by
G={\rm GL}_{n}
,
B_{-}\subset G
the lower triangular Borel,
N_{-}\subset B_{-}
etc.

For a matrix
g\in G(\mathbf{k})
let
\mathfrak{W}(g)=(y_{1}(g),\dots,y_{n-1}(g))
,
and consider the vector of degrees

d(g)=(d_{1}(g),\dots d_{n-1}(g))=(\deg y_{1}(g),\dots,\deg y_{n-1}(g))\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}.

It turns out that
d(g)
can take only
n!
possible values
situated in vertices of a permutohedron.

Namely, consider the Bruhat decomposition

G(\mathbf{k})=\cup_{w\in W}B_{-}(\mathbf{k})wB_{-}(\mathbf{k}),

W=S_{n-1}=W(G,T)
being the Weyl group.

Identify
\mathbb{N}^{n-1}
with the root lattice
Q
of
G^{s}:={\rm SL}_{n}
using the standard base
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}\}\!\subset\!Q
of simple roots.

Then it follows from
[
9
, Theorem 3.12]
, that for
g\in B(w):=B_{-}(\mathbf{k})wB_{-}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle d(g)=w*\mathbf{0},

where
\mathbf{0}=(0,\dots,0)
and
*
denotes the usual shifted Weyl group action

w*\alpha=w(\alpha-\rho)+\rho.

In other words, if

w*\mathbf{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{i}(w)\alpha_{i}

then

d_{i}(g)=d_{i}(w),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n-1.

Example 2.1
.

Let
n=3
. For
g=(a_{ij})\in{\rm GL}_{3}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle y_{1}(g)=a_{11}+a_{12}x+a_{13}\frac{x^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle y_{2}(g)=\Delta_{11}(g)+\Delta_{13}(g)x+\Delta_{23}(g)\frac{x^{2}}{2}.

Here
\Delta_{ij}(g)
denotes the
2\times 2
minor of
g
picking the first two rows
and
i
-th and
j
-th columns.

We have two simple roots
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
.

For
g\in{\rm GL}_{3}(\mathbf{k})
the vector
d(g)
can take
6
possible values:

(0,0)
,
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
,
(1,2)
,
(2,1)
, and
(2,2)
, these vectors forming a hexagon, cf.
[
9
, Section 3.5]
.

One checks directly that

B_{-}=d^{-1}(0,0).

This means that

g\in B_{-}
if and only if
a_{12}=a_{13}=\Delta_{23}(g)=0
.

Similarly

\displaystyle B_{-}(12)B_{-}=d^{-1}(1,0),\qquad B_{-}(23)B_{-}=d^{-1}(0,1),\qquad B_{-}(123)B_{-}=d^{-1}(2,1),

i.e.,

g\in B_{-}(123)B_{-}
if and only if
\Delta_{23}(g)=0
;

\displaystyle B_{-}(132)B_{-}=d^{-1}(2,1),\qquad B_{-}(13)B_{-}=d^{-1}(2,2)

(the big cell). This means that

g\in B_{-}(13)B_{-}
if and only if
a_{12}\neq 0
,
a_{13}\neq 0
,
\Delta_{23}(g)\neq 0
.

These formulas may be understood as a criterion for recognizing the Bruhat cells in
{\rm GL}_{3}
, cf.
[
5
]
.

Therefore the Wronskian map induces maps

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}(w)\colon\ B(w)\longrightarrow\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}^{d_{i}(w)}

for each
w\in W
.

2.3
Induced map on the flag space

Let
D\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
d_{i}(g)\leq D
for all

g\in G
,
i\in[n-1]:=\{1,\dots,n-1\}
.

The invariance (
2.1
) implies that
\mathfrak{W}
induces a map from the
base affine space

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}_{{\tilde{\mathcal{F}\ell}}_{-}}\colon\ {\tilde{\mathcal{F}\ell}}_{-}:=N_{-}\backslash G\longrightarrow\mathbf{k}[t]_{\leq D}^{n-1}\overset{\sim}{=}\big(\mathbb{A}^{D+1}\big)^{n-1}(\mathbf{k}),

while (
2.2
) implies that
\mathfrak{W}
induces a map from the full flag space

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}_{\operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}_{-}}\colon\ \operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}_{-}:=B_{-}\backslash G\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{k}[t])_{\leq D}^{n-1}\overset{\sim}{=}\big(\mathbb{P}^{D}\big)^{n-1}(\mathbf{k}).

More explicitly:
we can assign to an arbitrary matrix
g=(b_{ij})\in G
a flag in
V=\mathbf{k}^{n}

F(g)=V_{1}(g)\subset\dots\subset V_{n}(g)=V,

whose
i
-th space
V_{i}(g)
is spanned by the first
i
row vectors of
g

v_{j}(g)=(b_{j1},\dots,b_{jn})\in V,\qquad 1\leq j\leq i.

It is clear that
F(g)=F(ng)
for
n\in B_{-}
, and the map

\displaystyle F\colon\ G\longrightarrow\operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}(V),

where
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}(V)
is the space of full flags in
V
,
induces an isomorphism

B_{-}\backslash G\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}(V).

On the other hand consider the restriction of
F
to the upper triangular group

\displaystyle F_{N}\colon\ N\hookrightarrow\operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}(V);

this map is injective and its image is the big Schubert cell.

We may also consider the composition

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}_{N}\colon\ N\hookrightarrow\operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}_{-}\overset{\mathfrak{W}_{\operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}_{-}}}{\longrightarrow}\big(\mathbb{P}^{D}\big)^{n-1}.

We will see below (cf. Section
3.6
) that this map is an embedding.

2.4
Partial flags

More generally, for any unordered partition

\lambda\colon\ n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{p},\qquad n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{Z}>0}

we define in a similar way a map

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}\colon\ \operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}_{\lambda,-}:=P_{\lambda,-}\backslash G\longrightarrow\big(\mathbb{P}^{D}\big)^{p-1}.

For example, for
p=2
(Grassmanian case) corresponding to a partition

\lambda=i+(n-i)

\mathfrak{W}\colon\ \operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}_{\lambda,-}=\operatorname{Gr}_{n}^{i}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{D}.

Plücker map

3.1.1
Schubert cells in a Grassmanian

For
i\in[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
let
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}
denote the set of all
i
-element subsets of
[n]
.

The natural action of
W=S_{n}
on
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}
identifies

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}\overset{\sim}{=}S_{n}/S_{i}.

Let
P_{i}\subset G={\rm GL}_{n}
denote the stabilizer of the coordinate subspace
\mathbb{A}^{i}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
, so that

G/P_{i}\overset{\sim}{=}\operatorname{Gr}^{i}_{n},

the Grassmanian of
i
-planes in
\mathbb{A}^{n}
.
The Bruhat lemma gives rise to an isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}\overset{\sim}{=}B\backslash G/P_{i}.

This set may also be interpreted as “the set of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-points”

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}=\operatorname{Gr}_{n}^{i}(\mathbb{F}_{1}),

whose cardinality is a binomial coefficient

|\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}|=C_{n}^{i}=\binom{n}{i}.

3.1.2
A Plücker map

Consider a matrix

M=(b_{ij})_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n,m}(\mathbf{k})

with
n\leq m
. For any
j\in[n]

M_{\leq j}
will denote the truncated matrix

M_{\leq j}=(b_{ip})_{i\in[j],p\in[m]}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{j,m}(\mathbf{k}).

We suppose that
\operatorname{rank}(M)=n
.

For any
j\in[n]
consider the set of
j\times j
minors of
M

p_{j}(M)=(\Delta_{[j],I})_{I\in\mathcal{C}_{m}^{j}}\in\mathbb{A}^{C_{m}^{j}}(\mathbf{k})

or the same set up to a multiplication by a scalar

{\bar{p}}_{j}(M)=\pi(p_{j}(M))=\mathbb{P}^{C_{m}^{j}-1}(\mathbf{k}),

where
\pi\colon\mathbb{A}^{C_{m}^{j}}(\mathbf{k})\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{C_{m}^{j}-1}(\mathbf{k})
is the canonical projection.

We will use notations

{\tilde{\mathcal{P}\ell}}(M)=(p_{1}(M),\dots,p_{m}(M))\in\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{A}^{C_{m}^{j}}(\mathbf{k})

and

\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}(M)=({\bar{p}}_{1}(M),\dots,{\bar{p}}_{m}(M))\in\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}^{C_{m}^{j}-1}(\mathbf{k}).

Suppose that

, so we get maps

{\tilde{\mathcal{P}\ell}}=(\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{n})\colon\ {\rm GL}_{n}\longrightarrow\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{A}^{C_{n}^{j}}

and

\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}\colon\ {\rm GL}_{n}\longrightarrow\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{C_{n}^{j}-1}.

It is clear that
\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}(zM)=\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}(M)
for
z\in N_{-}\subset G={\rm GL}_{n}
and

\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}(bM)=\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}(M)
for
b\in B_{-}\subset G
, so
{\tilde{\mathcal{P}\ell}}
,
\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}
induce maps

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{{\tilde{\mathcal{F}\ell}}}\colon\ {\tilde{\mathcal{F}\ell}}_{-}=N_{-}\backslash G\longrightarrow\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{A}^{C_{n}^{j}}

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{\operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}}\colon\ \operatorname{\mathcal{F}\ell}_{-}=B_{-}\backslash G\longrightarrow\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{C_{n}^{j}-1}.

Schubert cells in Grassmanians

For
i\in[n]
consider the

\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{i}\colon\ G\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{C_{n}^{i}}.

We will compare it with the

\mathfrak{W}_{i}\colon\ G\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{d_{i}+1},\qquad g\mapsto y_{i}(g).

(3.1)

3.3
From
\boldsymbol{\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}}
to
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{W}}
: a contraction

Theorem 3.3
.

\displaystyle y_{i}(g)(t)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}}\Delta_{[i],I}(g)m(I)\frac{t^{\mathfrak{l}(I)}}{\mathfrak{l}(I)!}=\sum_{j=0}^{d_{i}}\biggl(\sum_{I\in\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}(j)}m(I)\Delta_{[i],I}(g)\biggr)\frac{t^{j}}{j!},

In other words, the map

\displaystyle c((a_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{C}_{n}^{i}})=\sum_{j=0}^{d_{i}}\biggl(\sum_{I\in\mathfrak{l}^{-1}(j)}m(I)a_{I}\biggr)\frac{t^{j}}{j!}.

Then

\mathfrak{W}_{i}=c_{i}\circ\operatorname{\mathcal{P}\ell}_{i}.

We will also use the reciprocal polynomials

5.1

identity

Recall a formula from
[
10

.
Let

\mathfrak{f}=(f_{1}(x),f_{2}(x),\dots)

be a sequence of functions. For a totally ordered finite subset

we denote

\mathfrak{f}_{A}=(f_{i_{1}},\dots,f_{i_{a}}),\qquad W(A):=W(\mathfrak{f}_{A}).

We write
[a]=\{1,\dots,a\}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

A=[a+1],\qquad B=[a]\cup\{a+2\}.

Then

\displaystyle W(W(A),W(B))=W(A\cap B)\cdot W(A\cup B).

(5.1)

This is a particular case of
[
9
, Section 9]
.

Example 5.2
.

W(W(f_{1},f_{2}),W(f_{1},f_{3}))=f_{1}W(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}).

5.2
Desnanot–Jacobi, aka Lewis Carrol

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix.
Denote by
A_{1n,1n}
the
(n-2)\times(n-2)
submatrix obtained from

by deleting the first and the
n
-th row
and the first and the
n
-th column, etc.
Then

\displaystyle\det A\det A_{1n,1n}=\det A_{1,1}\det A_{n,n}-\det A_{1,n}\det A_{n,1}.

(5.2)

Let us rewrite (

W(A\cap B)\cdot W(A\cup B)=W(A)W(B)^{\prime}-W(A)^{\prime}W(B);

we see that it resembles (

5.3
Example: Lusztig vs Wronskian mutations

Recall the situation (
1.1
), where we suppose that
m=n
.
The main result of
[
10
]
describes the compatibility of the map
\mathfrak{W}
with multiplication by an
upper

e_{i,i+1}(c)=I_{n}+ce_{ij},\qquad e_{ij}=(\delta_{pi}\delta_{qj})_{p,q}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbf{k}).

Let

,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}(M)=(b_{1}(M)(x),b_{2}(M)(x),b_{3}(M)(x),\dots),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}(M)=(y_{1}(M)(x),y_{2}(M)(x),y_{3}(M)(x),\dots).

The following example is taken from
[
10
, proof of Theorem 4.4]
.
We have

\mathfrak{W}(e_{23}M)=(y_{1}(e_{23}M),y_{2}(e_{23}M),\dots),

where

\mathfrak{b}(e_{23}M)=(b_{1}(M),b_{2}(M)+b_{3}(M),\dots).

Thus

y_{1}(e_{23}(c)M)=y_{1}(M),

whereas

y_{2}(e_{23}(c)M)=y_{2}(M)+cW(b_{1}(M),b_{3}(M)).

The
W5
identity implies

\displaystyle W(y_{2}(M),y_{2}(e_{23}(c)M))=cW(y_{2}(M),W(b_{1}(M),b_{3}(M)))

\displaystyle\hphantom{W(y_2(M), y_2(e_{23}(c)M))}{}=cW(W(b_{1}(M),b_{2}(M),W(b_{1}(M),b_{3}(M)))

\displaystyle\hphantom{W(y_2(M), y_2(e_{23}(c)M))}{}=cb_{1}W(b_{1}(M),b_{2}(M),b_{3}(M))=cy_{1}(M)y_{3}(M).

The resulting equation

\displaystyle W(y_{2}(M),y_{2}(e_{23}(c)M))=cy_{1}(M)y_{3}(M)

is called a
Wronskian mutation
equation. It is regarded in
[
10
]
as a differential equation
of the first order on an unknown function
\tilde{y}_{2}(c,M)=y_{2}(e_{23}(c)M)
;
together with some initial conditions it determines the polynomial
\tilde{y}_{2}(c,M)
uniquely from given

,
1\leq i\leq 3
.

This equation is a modification of a similar differential equation from
[
9
]
whose solution is a function

\tilde{y}^{\prime}_{2}(c,M)=y_{2}(e^{\prime}_{23}(c)M),

where

e^{\prime}_{23}(c)=ce_{23}\big(c^{-1}\big)=cI_{n}+e_{23}.
