Local and global blow downs of transport twistor space
Jan Bohr, François Monard, Gabriel P. Paternain

Abstract
. Transport twistor spaces are degenerate complex 2 2 -dimensional manifolds Z Z that complexify transport problems on Riemannian surfaces, appearing e.g. in geometric inverse problems. This article considers maps β : Z → ℂ 2 \beta\colon Z\to\mathbb{C}^{2} with a holomorphic blow-down structure that resolve the degeneracy of the complex structure and allow to gain insight into the complex geometry of Z Z . The main theorems provide global β \beta -maps for constant curvature metrics and their perturbations and local β \beta -maps for arbitrary metrics, thereby proving a version of the classical Newlander–Nirenberg theorem for degenerate complex structures.



1.
Introduction

Transport twistor spaces are degenerate complex
2
-dimensional manifolds that can be associated to any oriented Riemannian surface and whose complex geometry is closely linked to the geodesic flow of the surface. In the last few years, these twistor spaces have become a useful device to organise and reinterpret various questions arising in geometric inverse problems and dynamical systems
[
3
,
1
]
.
Vice versa, they give rise to an array of intriguing complex geometric questions that are amenable to tools of the aforementioned areas.

This article is an instance of the latter—using results from X-ray tomography and microlocal analysis, we address some fundamental issues pertaining to the complex structure of transport twistor space. In particular, we
show that locally the degeneracy of the complex structure can always be resolved. Using this, we derive an identity principle for biholomorphisms between twistor spaces, which features as an important ingredient in our follow-up work on biholomorphism rigidity
[
2
]
.

Our strategy consists in finding appropriate blow-down maps
\beta
from twistor space into
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, first explicitly in constant curvature, then more systematically for simple metrics. We prove that the blow down structure persists for small perturbations of the constant curvature models and derive our local results by comparing small geodesic balls with nearly Euclidean disks.

1.1.
Transport twistor spaces

Let
(M,g)
be an oriented Riemannian surface, possibly with non-empty boundary
\partial M
. We denote its unit tangent bundle with
SM=\{(x,v)\in TM:g(v,v)=1\}
and write
X
for the geodesic vector field on
SM
. The
transport twistor space
of
(M,g)
is the unit disk bundle

Z=\{(x,v)\in TM:g(v,v)\leq 1\},

equipped with a natural complex structure that degenerates at
SM\subset\partial Z
. To describe this complex structure, first note that
g
and the orientation endow the fibre
Z_{x}\subset Z
over
x\in M
with a complex structure, such that the anti-holomorphic tangent bundle
T^{0,1}Z_{x}
is well defined. The complex structure on
Z
can then itself be described in terms of its anti-holomorphic tangent bundle
\mathscr{D}
:

Theorem
.

There exists a unique involutive, orientation compatible
2
-plane distribution
\mathscr{D}\subset T_{\mathbb{C}}Z=TZ\otimes\mathbb{C}
such that
T^{0,1}Z_{x}\subset\mathscr{D}
for all
x\in M
and such that

(1.1)

\mathscr{D}\cap\overline{\mathscr{D}}=0\text{ on }Z\backslash SM\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathscr{D}\cap\overline{\mathscr{D}}=\spn_{\mathbb{C}}(X)\text{ on }SM.

Remark 1.1
.

An explicit description of
\mathscr{D}
in the conformally Euclidean case is discussed in §
2.5
below. The general construction of
\mathscr{D}
is carried out in
[
3
]
, and the uniqueness – which is not needed here – will be addressed elsewhere.

In general
Z
is a manifold with corners and we write
Z^{\circ}
for its interior, where all boundary hypersurfaces have been removed. On
Z^{\circ}
, which is a real
4
-manifold without boundary, condition (
1.1
)
implies that
\mathscr{D}=\ker(J+i)
for an almost complex structure
J
. Involutivity of
\mathscr{D}
is equivalent to the integrability of
J
and thus
(Z^{\circ},J)
is a classical complex surface with anti-holomorphic tangent bundle
T^{0,1}Z^{\circ}=\mathscr{D}|_{Z^{\circ}}
. Orientation compatibility means that
J
induces the canonical orientation on
Z^{\circ}
.

The distribution
\mathscr{D}
is an instance of an
involutive structure
as discussed in
[
34
]
.

1.1.1.
Twistor correspondences

In the articles
[
3
,
1
]
several correspondence principles have been set up that link the geodesic flow (in the form of the transport equation
Xu=f
) with the complex geometry of the transport twistor space:

Geodesic flow

Twistor space

Invariant functions (=
\ker X|_{C^{\infty}(SM)}
)

Holomorphic functions on
Z

that are ‘fibrewise holomorphic’

Invariant distributions (=
\ker X|_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(SM)}
)

Holomorphic functions on
Z^{\circ}

that are ‘fibrewise holomorphic’

with polynomial growth at
SM

Connections and matrix potentials on
M

Holomorphic vector bundles on
Z

(=
0

)

Here holomorphic functions on
Z
are functions
f\colon Z\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
that are smooth up to the boundary and holomorphic in the interior (equivalently,
df|_{\mathscr{D}}=0
on
Z
). Similarly, holomorphic vector bundles on
Z
are smooth up to the boundary in a suitable sense.

These correspondence principles have, at least in the view of the authors, become tremendously helpful in organising and reinterpreting various developments in geometric inverse problems and dynamical systems in two dimensions. For example, Pestov–Uhlmann’s proof of boundary rigidity for simple surfaces
[
28
]
admits a natural formulation in terms of the algebra
\mathcal{A}(Z)
of holomorphic functions on
Z
and a Cartan extension operator
\mathcal{A}(M)\to\mathcal{A}(Z)
; moreover, the influential notion of
holomorphic integrating factors
that was introduced by Salo–Uhlmann
[
30
]

is underpinned by an Oka–Grauert principle for holomorphic line bundles on
Z
—for more examples we refer to
[
3
,
1
]
.

It seems intriguing to further explore this link and possibly
use the power of complex geometry to gain insights into the geodesic flow. Doing this requires both a better understanding of the degeneracy of the complex structure and of the complex geometry of twistor space—the present article aims at making a start on both objectives.

1.1.2.
Projective twistor spaces

Quotienting
Z
by the antipodal action
(x,v)\mapsto(x,-v)
, one obtains another degenerate complex surface that depends only on the projective class of the geodesic flow. This surface – that we shall refer to as
projective twistor space
and denote
Z_{\mathbb{P}}
– is in fact the more classical object and has been studied by several other authors
[
14
,
17
,
5
,
25
,
18
,
19
,
21
,
20
]
. The quotient map

Z\rightarrow Z_{\mathbb{P}}

is a branched
2:1
covering and thus locally near a boundary point of
Z_{\mathbb{P}}
the complex structure degenerates in the same way as
\mathscr{D}
. This degeneracy has been explicitly addressed in the work of LeBrun and Mason
[
18
,
19
]
, who – in the case of Zoll surfaces – construct a holomorphic map
\beta_{\mathrm{LM}}\colon Z_{\mathbb{P}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}P^{2}
that is an embedding in the interior and maps
\partial Z_{\mathbb{P}}
onto a totally real surface
P\subset\mathbb{C}P^{2}
, thereby collapsing all geodesics. As observed in
[
29
]
, there is a diffeomorphism
Z_{\mathbb{P}}\cong[\mathbb{C}P^{2};P]
with the Melrosian blow-up of
P
under which
\beta_{\mathrm{LM}}
becomes the usual blow-down map.

This picture has been an inspiration for the maps
\beta\colon Z\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
in this article, which recover many of the features of
\beta_{\mathrm{LM}}
, while being constructed by completely different means. By analogy, we refer to them also as blow-down maps, though the geometry of
\beta(Z)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
is more complicated and does not readily fit into the setting of Melrosian blow-ups.

1.1.3.
The Euclidean
\beta
-map

If
M=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, equipped with the Euclidean metric, the transport twistor space is given by

(1.2)

Z=\{(z,\mu)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|\mu|\leq 1\},\quad\mathscr{D}=\spn_{\mathbb{C}}(\mu^{2}\partial_{z}+\partial_{\bar{z}},\partial_{\bar{\mu}}).

The complex geometry of
Z
can be completely understood by means of the map

(1.3)

\beta\colon Z\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2},\quad\beta(z,\mu)=(z-\mu^{2}\bar{z},\mu),

which is holomorphic and maps the interior
Z^{\circ}
diffeomorphically onto the poly-disk
\{(w,\xi)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|\xi|<1\}
, with inverse explicitly given by

(1.4)

\beta^{-1}(w,\xi)=\left((w+\xi^{2}\bar{w})/(1-|\xi|^{4}),\xi\right),\quad|\xi|<1.

The existence of such a map immediately implies that there is a wealth of holomorphic functions (in fact, if
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is any open domain, then
Z^{\circ}
is a Stein surface). Moreover,
\beta
can be viewed as desingularisation of the complex structure. Indeed, introducing
w
and
\xi
as new coordinates on
Z^{\circ}
, we simply have
\mathscr{D}=\spn_{\mathbb{C}}(\partial_{\bar{w}},\partial_{\bar{\xi}})
.

The Euclidean
\beta
-map serves as a prototype for the type of desingularisation that we seek out in this article. To capture the characteristic properties of
\beta
, we propose the notion of
holomorphic blow down structure
that we shall discuss after a brief intermezzo regarding Hermitian structures on twistor space.

1.1.4.
Hermitian structure

One of the characteristic properties of the Euclidean
\beta
-map
is that it restricts to an embedding on the interior
Z^{\circ}
. Unfortunately, the space of embeddings

\mathrm{Emb}(Z^{\circ},\mathbb{C}^{2})\subset C^{\infty}(Z^{\circ},\mathbb{C}^{2})

is
not open
in the
C^{\infty}
-topology, which is problematic for the purpose of perturbation theory
1
1

1

If
X
is a smooth manifold, the space
C^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be equipped with either the
weak
or the
strong
topology and if
X
is non-compact these topologies are genuinely different. In the present article we exclusively use the weak topology, which gives
C^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R}^{n})
its standard Fréchet space structure, and simply refer to it as the
C^{\infty}
-topology. The strong topology, while being a differential topologist’s favourite, is less suitable for us, as the restriction map
C^{\infty}(Z,\mathbb{C}^{2})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(Z^{\circ},\mathbb{C}^{2})
fails to be continuous in the strong topology. See Chapter 2 in
[
13
]
for more details.
To illustrate the failure of openness in
C^{\infty}
-topology, one can consider the following toy example: On
X=(0,2\pi)
define
f_{\epsilon}(x)=\exp(i(1+\epsilon)x)
, then
f_{0}\in\mathrm{Emb}(X,\mathbb{C})
is not an interior point, as it can be approximated by the non-injective immersions
(f_{\epsilon}:\epsilon>0)
.

. This problem can be solved by additionally keeping track of metric data and for this reason, we equip
Z^{\circ}
with a Hermitian metric.

Recall that a Hermitian metric on the complex surface
Z^{\circ}
is the data of a
(1,1)
-form
\Omega\in C^{\infty}(Z^{\circ},\Lambda^{1,1}Z^{\circ})
that, expressed in a frame where
\Lambda^{1,1}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
, corresponds to
iH
for a Hermitian matrix
H
. Since
\Lambda^{1,1}Z^{\circ}
is globally trivial (irrespective of the topology of
M
, see §
2.4
), there is a
1:1
correspondence

\text{Hermitian metric $\Omega$}\qquad\leftrightarrow\qquad H\in C^{\infty}(Z^{\circ},\mathrm{Her}_{+}^{2}),

where
\mathrm{Her}_{+}^{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
is the space of positive definite Hermitian
2\times 2
matrices.
Under this correspondence, we pick out a distinguished metric
{\underline{\Omega}}
:

(1.5)

{\underline{\Omega}}\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\underline{H}(x,v)=\begin{bmatrix}(1-|v|_{g}^{4})^{2}&\\
&1\end{bmatrix}\in C^{\infty}(Z^{\circ},\mathrm{Her}_{+}^{2})

In the Euclidean case, where
\Lambda^{0,1}Z^{\circ}
is canonically framed by
\Xi^{\vee}=(d\bar{z}-\bar{\mu}^{2}dz)/(1-|\mu|^{4})
and
d\bar{\mu}
this metric is given by

{\underline{\Omega}}=i(1-|\mu|^{4})^{2}\bar{\Xi}^{\vee}\wedge\Xi^{\vee}+id\mu\wedge d\bar{\mu}.

The significance of
{\underline{\Omega}}
stems from the observation that, at least in the Euclidean case, it degenerates in the same way as the Jacobian of the
\beta
-map. Precisely, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(1.6)

C^{-1}{\underline{\Omega}}\leq\beta^{*}\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\leq C\penalty\ {\underline{\Omega}},

where
\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}=idw\wedge d\bar{w}+id\xi\wedge d\bar{\xi}
is the standard Hermitian structure on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and ‘
\leq
’ is understood as inequality between the associated Hermitian matrices. The verification of (
1.6
) is elementary and the curious reader is invited to check the inequalities at once—we will come back to them in §
4
and give the required computations alongside the constant curvature case.

1.1.5.
Holomorphic blow-down structure

Let now
(M,g)
be a compact oriented Riemannian surface with non-empty boundary
\partial M
. Let
\nu
be the inward pointing unit normal to
\partial M
and define
influx
(+)
and outflux
(-)
boundaries

(1.7)

\partial_{\pm}SM=\{(x,v)\in SM:\pm g(v,\nu(x))\geq 0\}.

If
\partial M
is strictly convex and
(M,g)
is non-trapping (i.e. geodesics reach
\partial M
in finite time), then
\partial_{+}SM
can be thought of as
parameter space for the geodesics of
M
.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that a map
\beta\in C^{\infty}(Z,\mathbb{C}^{2})
has a
holomorphic blow-down structure
if it has the following properties:

(i)

\beta|_{\partial_{+}SM}:\partial_{+}SM\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a totally real
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}
-embedding;

(ii)

the restriction of
\beta
to
Z^{\circ}
is a biholomorphism onto its image;

(iii)

\beta^{*}\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\geq c\,{\underline{\Omega}}
for some constant
c>0
.

Let us comment on the three properties: The first property implies in particular that
\beta
separates geodesics and
is the strongest embedding property one can expect. Note that
\beta
being holomorphic implies that
X\beta|_{SM}=0
and since the geodesic vector field
X
is tangent to the boundary of
\partial_{+}SM
, this enforces the Jacobian of
\beta|_{\partial_{+}SM}
to drop rank. To address this issue, we change the smooth structure near the boundary of
\partial_{+}SM
and refer to embeddings with respect to this new smooth structure as
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}
-embeddings
(see also Definition
2.2
). Further, if
S
is a smooth surface (such as
\partial_{+}SM
equipped with the adjusted smooth structure), then an embedding
f\colon S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
is called
totally real
, if

df_{x}(T_{x}S)\cap J_{f(x)}df_{x}(T_{x}S)=0\text{ for all }x\in S.

Here
J
is the complex structure of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. This aspect parallels LeBrun–Mason’s paper on Zoll surfaces
[
18
]
, where the space of geodesics (the analogue of
\partial_{+}SM
) is realised as a totally real submanifold of
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
.

The second property is equivalent to
\beta\colon Z^{\circ}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
being both holomorphic and injective. In this case the image
\beta(Z^{\circ})
is automatically open and the Jacobian has full rank. To ensure that this property is preserved under small perturbations, it is supplemented by the third property, which corresponds to a uniform lower bound on the singular values of the Jacobian in an
{\underline{\Omega}}
-orthonormal frame. If we were merely interested in
\beta
being an immersion on
Z^{\circ}
, it would suffice to keep track of the determinant of this Jacobian, which is encoded in the
2
-form
d\beta_{1}\wedge d\beta_{2}
. In fact,

(1.8)

\beta^{*}\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\geq c\,{\underline{\Omega}}\penalty\ \text{ for some }c>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad d\beta_{1}\wedge d\beta_{2}\neq 0\text{ pointwise on }Z

(see Remark
7.5
) and requiring the stronger property on the left is
precisely what ensures that also injectivity of
\beta
persists under small perturbations. As will be shown later, the reverse inequality in
(iii)
holds automatically due to holomorphicity and thus having a holomorphic blow-down structure ensures that
\beta\colon Z^{\circ}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a bi-Lipschitz map.

The definition of holomorphic blow down structure has been chosen such that it captures the characteristic behaviour of
\beta
-maps in special geometries, while at the same time being an open condition with respect to simultaneous variations of
\beta
and
g
, see Theorem
3.10
below.

1.2.
Main theorems

The work on this article started with the question of how to appropriately generalise the Euclidean
\beta
-map from §
1.1.3
to other geometries and how these maps behave under small perturbations.
We discuss our approach to this systematically in §
3
below—first, however, we shall present some facets and consequences that can be discussed without further preparation.

1.2.1.
Global blow-downs

Let
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
be the closed unit disk. Our first result concerns constant curvature metrics on
\mathbb{D}
(see also §
4
for a more precise formulation, allowing also curvatures with
|\kappa|\geq 1
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M=\mathbb{D}
and
g=(1+\kappa|z|^{2})^{-2}|dz|^{2}

(-1<\kappa<1)
. On transport twistor space
Z=\{(z,(1+\kappa|z|^{2})\mu)\}\subset T\mathbb{D}
with coordinates
z,\mu\in\mathbb{D}
, the map

\beta_{\kappa}(z,\mu)=\left(\frac{z-\mu^{2}\bar{z}}{1+\kappa\bar{z}^{2}\mu^{2}},\mu\frac{1+\kappa|z|^{2}}{1+\kappa\bar{z}^{2}\mu^{2}}\right),\quad(z,\mu)\in\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}

has a holomorphic blow-down structure.

By means of a perturbation argument, we also obtain global blow-downs for nearby metrics. For simplicity we state the following theorem as an existence result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
M=\mathbb{D}
and suppose that
g
is a Riemannian metric which is sufficiently close (in
C^{\infty}
-topology) to the Euclidean metric (or one of the constant curvature models from the preceding theorem). Then there exists a map
\beta\in C^{\infty}(Z,\mathbb{C}^{2})
with holomorphic blow-down structure.

It turns out that the
\beta
-map in the theorem arises from a
canonical construction
that works for any simple surface (see Definition
3.1
). It is canonical in the sense that it is obtained by an energy minimisation and a subsequent Szegő projection. This energy minimiser can be expressed via the geodesic X-ray transform and the inverse of its normal operator, see Definition
3.5
. The underlying invertibility result for the normal operator was proved in
[
23
]
.

1.2.2.
Local blow-downs

Applying the preceding theorem to small geodesic disks, we deduce the following:

Theorem 1.5
.

(Transport Newlander–Nirenberg theorem) Let
(M,g)
be an oriented Riemannian surface with twistor space
Z
. Then any point
p\in Z
admits an open neighbourhood
U\subset Z
and a holomorphic map
\beta\colon U\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that:

(i)

d\beta_{1}\wedge d\beta_{2}\neq 0
pointwise on
U
;

(ii)

if
q_{1},q_{2}\in U
are two distinct points with
\beta(q_{1})=\beta(q_{2})
, then both points lie on a common geodesic on
U\cap SM
—in particular,
\beta
is injective on
U\backslash SM
.

Consequentially
\mathcal{O}(U)=\{f\in C^{\infty}(U):\,f\text{ holomorphic}\}
separates points in
U\backslash SM
.

In the context of involutive structures (cf.
[
34
]
) the existence of local solutions to
d\beta|_{\mathscr{D}}=0
with property
(i)
is referred to as
local integrability
. For
p\in Z\backslash SM
local integrability is guaranteed by the classical Newlander–Nirenberg theorem (e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 2.6.19]
) and in this case property
(ii)
is a consequence of
(i)
and the inverse function theorem. The crux of the preceding theorem is that we obtain local integrability together with the separation property
(ii)
also near points
p\in SM
, where
\mathscr{D}
degenerates and the inverse function theorem cannot be applied.

Since this is a local result, it is also valid on the projective twistor space
Z_{\mathbb{P}}
and it appears like this is the first such integrability result beyond LeBrun-Mason’s work in the Zoll setting.

1.2.3.
Application: Identity principles

Let
Z
be the twistor space of an oriented Riemannian surface
(M,g)
and let
\Sigma
be a compact connected Riemann surface with non-empty boundary. We consider holomorphic maps

f\colon\Sigma\rightarrow Z

and refer to these as
holomorphic curves
parametrised by
\Sigma
. Here holomorphic means that
f
is smooth up to the boundary and satisfies
f_{*}(T^{0,1}\Sigma)\subset\mathscr{D}
.

An example of a holomorphic curve is the embedding of the closed unit disk
\mathbb{D}=\{\mu\in\mathbb{C}:|\mu|\leq 1\}
as fibre of
Z
. Precisely, if
(x,v)\in SM
, then the fibre over
x
is parametrised by
f(\mu)=(x,\mu\cdot v)
. The local point separation of Theorem
1.5
has the following consequence:

Corollary 1.6

(Identity principle for holomorphic curves)
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\colon\Sigma\rightarrow Z
be holomorphic curves with
f_{1}=f_{2}
on
\partial\Sigma
. Then
f_{1}=f_{2}
on all of
\Sigma
.

Let now
(M_{i},g_{i})
(
i=1,2
) be two oriented Riemannian surfaces and
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
their associated twistor spaces. We say that a map
\Phi\colon Z_{1}\rightarrow Z_{2}
is holomorphic, if it is smooth up to the boundary and satisfies
\Phi_{*}(\mathscr{D}_{1})\subset\mathscr{D}_{2}
. Such a map
\Phi
is determined by its action on the fibres of
Z_{1}
and hence Corollary
1.6
implies:

Corollary 1.7

(Identity principle for holomorphic maps)
.

Suppose
\Phi,\Psi\colon Z_{1}\rightarrow Z_{2}
are two holomorphic maps with
\Phi|_{SM_{1}}=\Psi|_{SM_{1}}
. Then
\Phi=\Psi
on
Z_{1}
.

This identity principle is one of the key ingredients of our follow-up work
[
2
]
, where we prove that biholomorphisms between transport twistor spaces exhibit rigidity: if the underlying metrics are simple or Anosov, then every biholomorphism is, up to constant rescaling and the antipodal map, the lift of an orientation preserving isometry. As the restriction of a biholomorphism to unit tangent bundles always induces an orbit equivalence,
this is closely related to questions such as conjugacy rigidity and lens rigidity (see e.g.
[
4
,
8
,
11
,
12
]
for some recent related works). We refer to
[
2
]
for further details.

1.2.4.
Strategy for the proof of Theorem
1.4

As already pointed out, the map
\beta_{g}
in Theorem
1.4
is canonically constructed using the geodesic X-ray transform together with the inverse of its normal operator. The first step in the proof consists of showing that this canonical construction agrees with the explicit maps from Theorem
1.3
; here we exploit the rotational symmetry to avoid the difficult task of inverting normal operators. Once this is achieved the core idea of the proof is simple—we show that

g\mapsto\beta_{g}
is continuous in a suitable sense and ensure that the notion of holomorphic blow-down structure (Definition
1.2
) survives small perturbations. Implementing this idea is technically challenging. The continuous dependence involves many moving pieces, such as changes in the
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}
-structure or changes of the inverse of the normal operator in the Grubb–Hörmander framework used in
[
23
]
. At the same time, the perturbation theory of the holomorphic blow-down structure takes place on the non-compact manifold
Z^{\circ}
and this requires dealing with a metric-dependent degeneracy of the complex structure.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Fibrewise Fourier analysis

Let
(M,g)
be an oriented Riemannian surface. The vertical vector field
V
on
SM
is defined as the infinitesimal generator of the circle action
(x,v)\mapsto(x,e^{it}v)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Defining

\Omega_{k}=\{f\in C^{\infty}(SM):Vf=ikf\},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},

any
u\in C^{\infty}(SM)
has a unique decomposition
u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{k}
into its vertical Fourier modes
u_{k}\in\Omega_{k}
. Functions that only have non-trivial Fourier modes in degrees
k\geq 0
are called
fibrewise holomorphic
.
There are natural isomorphisms

(2.1)

\Omega_{k}\cong C^{\infty}(M,\otimes^{k}(T^{1,0}M)^{*})\quad\text{ and }\quad\Omega_{-k}\cong C^{\infty}(M,\otimes^{k}(T^{0,1}M)^{*}),\quad k\geq 0,

where a
k
-tensor is pulled back to a function in
\Omega_{k}\subset C^{\infty}(SM)
by inserting the velocity variable
k
-times.

Let now
Z
be the twistor space of
(M,g)
and consider the algebra of holomorphic functions on
Z
, assumed to be smooth up to the boundary:

\mathcal{A}(Z)=\{f\in C^{\infty}(Z):f\text{ holomorphic}\}

As mentioned in §
1.1.1
, there is an isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}(Z)

(2.3)

2.2.
X-ray transforms and normal operator

Let
(M,g)
be a dimension
d\geq 2
that is non-trapping
and has a strictly convex boundary.
The

geodesic X-ray transform
on
(M,g)
is defined as map

I\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(SM^{\circ})\rightarrow C^{\infty}_{c}(\partial_{+}SM^{\circ}),\quad If(x,v)=\int_{0}^{\tau(x,v)}f(\varphi_{t}(x,v))dt,

where
(\varphi_{t})
is the geodesic flow on
SM
and
\tau(x,v)\geq 0
is the hitting time of the orbit
\varphi_{t}(x,v)
with
\partial SM
. Let
\rho
be a boundary defining function of
M
(that is,
\rho=0
and
d\rho\neq 0
on
\partial M
) and consider this as function on
SM
. Then the X-ray transform extends to a continuous map

(2.5)

I\colon\rho^{-1/2}C^{\infty}(SM)\rightarrow C_{\alpha}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM),

where
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)
denotes the space of smooth maps
h\colon\partial_{+}SM\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
for which the flow-invariant extension
h^{\sharp}
is smooth on all of
SM
(i.e.
h^{\sharp}\in C^{\infty}(SM),Xh^{\sharp}=0
and
h^{\sharp}=h
on
\partial_{+}SM
). If
\pi\colon SM\rightarrow M
is the base point projection, then we define
I_{0}=I\circ\pi^{*}
and the
normal operator
by

N_{0}\colon\rho^{-1/2}C^{\infty}(M)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(M),\quad N_{0}f(x):=\pi_{*}(I_{0}f)^{\sharp}(x)=2\int_{S_{x}M}\!\!\!dv\int_{0}^{\tau(x,v)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!f\circ\varphi_{t}(x,v)dt.

We may view
N_{0}
as
I_{0}^{*}I_{0}
, where
I_{0}^{*}
is the formal adjoint with respect to the
symplectic measure
on
\partial_{+}SM
. This is given by
g(\nu(x),v)\,d\Sigma^{2d-2}
, where
d\Sigma^{2d-2}
is the volume form for the Sasaki metric. For the corresponding norm, we use the notation

||h||_{\mathrm{sym}}^{2}=\int_{\partial_{+}SM}|h(x,v)|^{2}\cdot g(v,\nu(x))d\Sigma^{2d-2}(x,v),\quad h\in C_{\alpha}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM).

(This is frequently denoted
||\cdot||_{\mu}
or
||\cdot||_{L^{2}_{\mu}(\partial_{+}SM)}
, but we avoid the
\mu
-notation, as to not cause confusion with the canonical variables on twistor space.)

2.2.1.
Two-dimension case

If
d=2
, we may restrict the X-ray transform to the
k
th Fourier mode to obtain maps

(2.6)

I_{k}\colon\rho^{-1/2}\Omega_{k}\rightarrow C_{\alpha}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM),\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

For
k=0
this corresponds to
I_{0}
, as defined above, if we identify
\Omega_{0}
with
C^{\infty}(M)
. Writing
(\cdot)_{k}
for the projection onto the

th mode, we define normal operators

(2.7)

N_{k}\colon\rho^{-1/2}\Omega_{k}\rightarrow\Omega_{k},\quad N_{k}f=((I_{k}f)^{\sharp})_{k}.

2.3.
The
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}
-structure

Let
(M,g)
be a non-trapping manifold with strictly convex boundary. The space
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)
, defined below (
2.5
), can be viewed as space of
all
smooth functions with respect to a modified smooth structure on
\partial_{+}SM
.
To see this, we embed
M
into the interior of a slightly larger manifold
(\hat{M},g)
which is also non-trapping and has a strictly convex boundary. Let
\hat{\tau}\in C^{\infty}(M,[0,\infty))
denote the exit time of
\hat{M}
and consider the smooth map

(2.8)

F_{g}\colon\partial SM\rightarrow\partial S\hat{M},\quad F_{g}(x,v)=\hat{\varphi}_{-\hat{\tau}(x,-v)}(x,v),

where
(\hat{\varphi}_{t})
is the geodesic flow of
(\hat{M},g)
. As discussed in
[
27
, §5.2]
this map restricts to an embedding on
\partial_{+}SM^{\circ}
and has the structure of a
Whitney fold
near every point
(x_{0},v_{0})\in\partial_{0}SM
. The latter property means that there are local coordinates
(u_{1},\dots,u_{2d-2})
and
(v_{1},\dots,v_{2d-2})
, centred at
(x_{0},v_{0})
and
F_{g}(x_{0},v_{0})
, respectively, in which
F_{g}
has the following normal form:

(2.9)

F_{g}(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{2d-2})=(u_{1}^{2},u_{2},\dots,u_{2d-2})

As an immediate consequence,
S_{g}=F_{g}(\partial_{+}SM)\subset\partial S\hat{M}
is a smooth submanifold with boundary and
F_{g}\colon\partial_{+}SM\rightarrow S_{g}
is a homeomorphism.

Definition 2.1
.

On
\partial_{+}SM
a new smooth structure is defined by transferring the smooth structure of
S_{g}
via the homeomorphism
F_{g}\colon\partial_{+}SM\rightarrow S_{g}
. The structure will be referred to as
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}
-structure
and its maximal atlas will be denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
.

The space of
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
-smooth functions
C^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM,\mathcal{A}_{\alpha})
coincides with
C^{\infty}_{\alpha}(\partial_{+}SM)
, as we can verify by means of the following chain of equalities:

C_{\alpha}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)=F_{g}^{*}C^{\infty}(\partial S\hat{M})=F_{g}^{*}C^{\infty}(S_{g})=C^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM,\mathcal{A}_{\alpha})

The first equality is a consequence of Theorem C.4.4 in
[
15
]
, the second one follows from
S_{g}\subset\partial S\hat{M}
being a smooth submanifold and the last one holds by definition.

Definition 2.2
.

A map
f\colon\partial_{+}SM\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}
-embedding
, if it is an injective
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
-smooth immersion. (Equivalently, if it is a smooth embedding of
(\partial_{+}SM,\mathcal{A}_{\alpha})
.)

Remark 2.3
.

In the local coordinate system from (
2.9
) any
f\in C_{\alpha}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM,\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfies
\partial_{u_{1}}|_{u_{1}=0}f=0
and thus – using the standard smooth structure – it cannot be an immersion up to the boundary. For
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
-smooth immersions one instead considers rescaled Jacobians, where
\partial_{u_{1}}f
is replaced by
u_{1}^{-1}\partial_{u_{1}}f
.

2.4.
Hermitian manifolds

A Hermitian metric on a complex
n
-manifold
(X,J)
is a Riemannian metric
G
for which
J
is an isometry. The real
(1,1)
-form
\Omega(\cdot,\cdot)=G(J\cdot,\cdot)
is called the
fundamental
2
-form
of
G
, and with respect to a local frame
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}
of
\Lambda^{1,0}X
it is given by

(2.10)

\Omega=i\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}H_{jk}\cdot\eta_{j}\wedge\bar{\eta}_{k}

for a positive definite Hermitian
n\times n
-matrix
H=(H_{jk})
. We also refer to
\Omega
itself as the Hermitian metric, with the understanding that
G=\Omega(\cdot,J\cdot)
is the actual metric tensor. The associated Riemannian quantities will be labelled with
\Omega
, e.g.
d_{\Omega}
is the Riemannian distance function and
|\cdot|_{\Omega}^{2}
is the induced inner product on forms.
Given two Hermitian metrics
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
we write
\Omega\leq\Omega^{\prime}
if and only if
G^{\prime}-G
is positive semi-definite (or equivalently if
H^{\prime}-H\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is positive semi-definite in any coordinate chart).

2.4.1.
Hermitian metrics on twistor space

Let
(M,g)
be an oriented Riemannian surface with twistor space
Z
. To study twistor space invariantly, it is convenient to consider
Z
as the base of the principal circle bundle

\mathsf{p}\colon SM\times\mathbb{D}\rightarrow Z,\quad\mathsf{p}(x,v,\omega)=(x,\omega\cdot v),

where the product
\omega\cdot v
is defined by means of the complex structure of
T_{x}M
.
It turns out that
\mathsf{p}^{*}(\Lambda^{0,1}Z^{\circ})
has a global frame consisting of

1
-forms

\tau,\gamma\in\Omega^{1}(SM\times\mathbb{D}^{\circ},\mathbb{C}).

Let
\mathbf{V}
be the vector field on
SM\times\mathbb{D}
that generates the circle action
(x,v,\omega)\triangleleft e^{it}=(x,e^{it}v,e^{-it}\omega)
(
t\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
) and denote with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{V}}
the associated Lie derivative. Then
\tau,\gamma\in\ker(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{V}}-i)
and thus
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{V}}(\bar{\tau}\wedge\tau)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{V}}(\bar{\tau}\wedge\gamma)=\dots=0
. As a consequence, the
\mathsf{p}
-push-forwards of these
2
-forms exist and yields a global frame

\{\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\tau}\wedge\tau),\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\tau}\wedge\gamma),\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\gamma}\wedge\tau),\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\gamma}\wedge\gamma)\}\subset\Lambda^{1,1}Z^{\circ}.

Using this frame, we associate to any
H=(H_{ij})\in C^{\infty}(Z^{\circ},\mathrm{Her}_{2}^{+})
a Hermitian metric

\Omega=i\left\{H_{11}\,\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\tau}\wedge\tau)+H_{12}\,\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\tau}\wedge\gamma)+H_{21}\,\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\gamma}\wedge\tau)+H_{22}\,\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\gamma}\wedge\gamma)\right\}

on
Z^{\circ}
and this gives the
1:1
-correspondence from §
1.1.4
. In this article, we only work with the distinguished metric
{\underline{\Omega}}
defined in (
1.5
).
For further details on this invariant approach to twistor spaces and the definition of
\tau,\gamma
, we refer to
[
3
,
1
]
.

2.5.
Twistor space of conformally Euclidean disks

If

We refer to

\bar{\Xi}_{\sigma}^{\vee}\wedge\Xi_{\sigma}^{\vee}=\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\tau}\wedge\tau),\bar{\Xi}_{\sigma}^{\vee}\wedge\partial_{\bar{\mu}}^{\vee}=\mathsf{p}_{*}(\bar{\tau}\wedge\gamma),\dots

3.
Canonical
\beta
-maps and their perturbation theory

The Euclidean
\beta
-map arises in a rather systematic way. We will demonstrate this in the context of simple surfaces, where we define the notion of
\beta
-extensions
and study their behaviour under perturbations of the metric.

Definition 3.1
.

A compact Riemannian surface
(M,g)
is called
simple
if it is non-trapping, free of conjugate points and has a strictly convex boundary
\partial M
.

3.1.
Canonical
\beta
-maps for simple surfaces

3.1.1.
Holomorphic extensions

The Euclidean
\beta
-map in (
1.3
) has the components
\beta^{(0)}(z,\mu)=z-\mu^{2}\bar{z}
and
\beta^{(1)}(z,\mu)=\mu
. In terms of the spaces
\mathcal{A}_{m}(Z)
and
\mathcal{H}_{m}
and the maps
\pi_{m*}
defined in §
2.1
we may express this as:

\displaystyle\beta^{(0)}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}(Z)\quad\text{ and }\quad\pi_{0,*}\beta^{(0)}=z\in\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle\beta^{(1)}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\quad\text{ and }\quad\pi_{1,*}\beta^{(1)}=dz\in\mathcal{H}_{1}

That is,
\beta
is a holomorphic extension of the tuple
(z,dz)\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\times\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Due to a classical result of Pestov-Uhlmann
[
28
]
this type of holomorphic extension exists for general simple surfaces (see
[
3
,
1
]
for a discussion in the context of twistor spaces).

Theorem

(Holomorphic extensions)
.

Let
(M,g)
be simple and
Z
its transport twistor space. Then the following sequence is exact:

0\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{m+1}(Z)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{m}(Z)\xrightarrow{\pi_{m*}}\mathcal{H}_{m}\rightarrow 0

Of course, the holomorphic extension of
(z,dz)
is far from unique, but we shall see below that there is a canonical choice that is consistent with the Euclidean
\beta
-map.

3.1.2.
Canonical first integrals

Let us first discuss a canonical choice of first integral on
SM
, that is based on the following result from
[
23
]
.

Proposition 3.2

(Isomorphism property)
.

Let
(M,g)
be simple and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then the normal operator
N_{k}\colon\rho^{-1/2}\Omega_{k}\to\Omega_{k}
(defined in §
2.2
) is an isomorphism.

Proof.

For
k=0
this is part of Theorem 2.2 of
[
23
]
. For
k\neq 0
, we introduce the attenuation
a=e^{-ik\theta}X(e^{ik\theta})\in\Omega_{-1}\oplus\Omega_{1}
, where the angle
\theta
is chosen with respect to a trivialisation of
SM
. One can check that
N_{k}
fits into the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho^{-1/2}\Omega_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho^{-1/2}\Omega_{k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{k},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N_{a,0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\times e^{ik\theta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\times e^{ik\theta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N_{k}}

where
N_{a,0}=I_{a,0}^{*}I_{a,0}
is the normal operator of the attenuated X-ray transform
I_{a,0}
considered in §12.2 of
[
27
]
. It is then enough to prove the corresponding isomorphism property for
N_{a,0}
and this can be done by the same methods as in
[
23
]
.
∎

Proposition 3.3

(Canonical first integrals)
.

Let
(M,g)
be simple and
f\in\Omega_{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then there exists a solution
u\in C^{\infty}(SM)
to the problem

(3.1)

Xu=0\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{k}=f

and this solution is unique, if one requires any of the following equivalent conditions:

(i)

||u|_{\partial_{+}SM}||_{\mathrm{sym}}
is minimal amongst all solutions to (
3.1
);

(ii)

u|_{\partial_{+}SM}
lies in the range of
I_{k}\colon\rho^{-1/2}\Omega_{k}\rightarrow C_{\alpha}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)
;

(iii)

u=[I_{k}N_{k}^{-1}f]^{\sharp}
.

Proof.

Let
h=u|_{\partial_{+}SM}\in C^{\infty}_{\alpha}(\partial_{+}SM)
, then
I_{k}^{*}h=f
. Suppose that
h=I_{k}a
for some
a\in\rho^{-1/2}\Omega_{k}
, then we must have
N_{k}a=I_{k}^{*}I_{k}a=f
and hence
a=N_{k}^{-1}f
. This shows that
(ii)
and
(iii)
are equivalent. Let
h^{\prime}=u^{\prime}|_{\partial_{+}SM}
for any other solution
u^{\prime}
to (
3.1
). Then

\langle h,h-h^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathrm{sym}}=\langle I_{k}a,h-h^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathrm{sym}}=\langle a,I_{k}^{*}(h-h^{\prime})\rangle_{L^{2}(SM)}=0
and hence

||h||_{\mathrm{sym}}^{2}=|\langle h,h^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathrm{sym}}|\leq||h||_{\mathrm{sym}}\cdot||h^{\prime}||_{\mathrm{sym}},

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Hence

(ii)
implies
(i)
. Vice versa, if
h^{\prime}
is also an energy minimiser, then in the previous display we have equality, and thus
h
and
h^{\prime}
have to be linearly dependent, which is only possible if
u=u^{\prime}
.
∎

As discussed in §
3.1.1
, the Euclidean
\beta
-map from (

) is a holomorphic extension of
(z,dz)
. In view of the preceding proposition, we now see that it is singled out amongst all such extensions by having minimal
||\cdot||_{\mathrm{sym}}
-energy.

Proposition 3.4

(Extremality of Euclidean
\beta
-map)
.

Let
M=\mathbb{D}
, equipped with the Euclidean metric. Then the map
\beta(z,\mu)=(z-\mu^{2}\bar{z},\mu)
satisfies

\beta(z,\mu)=([I_{0}N_{0}^{-1}(z)]^{\sharp},[I_{1}N_{1}^{-1}(dz)]^{\sharp}),\quad(z,\mu)\in S\mathbb{D}.

This proposition is proved together with the constant curvature case in §
4
– see also Theorem
4.3
, where it is restated in context. When the metric is rotation-invariant, the proof relies on the unique identifiability of certain geodesic first integrals which are also rotation-equivariant, see Lemma
4.4
below.

3.1.3.
Definition of
\beta
-maps on simple surfaces

Let
(M,g)
be a simple surface. Guided by the preceding considerations, we now describe how to canonically extend a pair
(f,df)\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\times\mathcal{H}_{1}
to a holomorphic map
\beta\colon Z\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
on twistor space. As the first integrals in Proposition
3.3
might not always be fibrewise holomorphic (even if

) we introduce the Szegő projectors
\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{ev}},\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{odd}}\colon C^{\infty}(SM)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(SM)
:

(3.2)

\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{ev}}u=\sum_{k\geq 0}u_{2k}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{odd}}u=\sum_{k\geq 0}u_{2k+1}

The Szegő projections of an invariant function remain invariant if the lowest mode (i.e.
u_{0}
or
u_{1}
) is in the kernel if
\eta_{-}
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
(M,g)
be a simple surface and
f\colon M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
a holomorphic map. The
\beta
-extension
of
f
is defined as the holomorphic map
\beta\colon Z\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
given by

\beta|_{SM}=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{ev}}(I_{0}N_{0}^{-1}f)^{\sharp},\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{odd}}(I_{1}N_{1}^{-1}df)^{\sharp}\right).

Remark 3.6
.

The
\beta
-map in Theorem
1.4
is precisely the
\beta
-extension of an appropriate holomorphic embedding
f\colon M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. If
g
is already known to be conformally Euclidean, then one can simply take
f(z)=z
.

This leads to the following question:

Question 3.7
.

Does every simple surface
(M,g)
admit an embedding
f\colon M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that the
\beta
-extension
\beta\colon Z\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
has a holomorphic blow-down structure?

3.2.
Perturbation theory for
\beta
-maps

Instead of addressing Question
3.7
directly, we prove that the canonical
\beta
-maps depend continuously on the metric and that the property of having a holomorphic blow-down structure is preserved under perturbations. In view of the Riemann mapping theorem (or rather its version with continuous dependence on the metric, discussed in §
A.1
) it suffices to consider conformally Euclidean metrics and we shall do this from now on.

3.2.1.
Continuous dependence on the conformal factor

We now consider the
\beta
-extensions from Definition
3.5
in the conformally Euclidean case. For this, write

\Sigma_{\mathrm{s}}=\{\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{R}):e^{2\sigma}|dz|^{2}\text{ is simple}\}

and note that this is an open set in the
C^{\infty}
-topology.
As perturbing
\sigma
does not change the complex structure of
M
, the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{m}
of holomorphic
m
-differentials remain unchanged and
(z,dz)\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\times\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Further, via the isomorphism in (
2.12
), the
\beta
-extensions of
f(z)=z
for varying
\sigma
’s can all be considered as maps on the same space
Z=\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}
:

\beta_{\sigma}\colon Z\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2},\quad\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{s}}

We collect the relevant properties of these maps in the following theorem:

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
Z=\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{s}}
. Then:

(i)

\beta_{\sigma}\colon Z\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
is
\mathscr{D}_{\sigma}
-holomorphic, i.e.
\Xi_{\sigma}\beta_{\sigma}=\partial_{\bar{\mu}}\beta_{\sigma}=0
;

(ii)

if
\sigma=-\log(1+\kappa|z|^{2})
, then
\beta_{\sigma}
equals the map
\beta_{\kappa}
from Theorem
1.3
;

(iii)

the following map is continuous:

\sigma\mapsto\beta_{\sigma},\quad\Sigma_{\mathrm{s}}\rightarrow C^{\infty}(Z,\mathbb{C}^{2}).

Of course,
(i)
follows directly by construction and
(ii)
is a consequence of Proposition
3.4
(or rather, its extension to constant curvature). The novel part of the theorem is thus the continuity claim and this is proved by keeping track of the metric dependencies at each step in the construction in §
3.1
.

Remark 3.9
.

There is an alternative way to obtain a continuous family as in Theorem
3.8
(iii)
, at least in the vicinity of a fixed background map, say
\beta_{0}
. Consider the vector field
\mathbb{X}_{\sigma}=\bar{\mu}\Xi_{\sigma}|_{S\mathbb{D}}=e^{-\sigma}(X+\lambda_{\sigma}V)
on
S\mathbb{D}
. This generates the geodesic flow of
e^{2\sigma}|dz|^{2}
when projecting it onto
S\mathbb{D}
(see Remark

). Similar to Proposition 3.1 in

(x,v)\notin\partial_{0}SM
) or collapes it to a point (if
(x,v)\in\partial_{0}SM

The purpose of this section is to show that the
\Upsilon
-map depends continuously on the underlying metric. From this, further continuity statements about the geodesic X-ray transform and conjugacies between geodesic flows are derived—these are used both in §

5.1.
Set-up for perturbation theory

Let us define

\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}\subset C^{\infty}(M,\otimes_{S}^{2}T^{*}M)

as the space of all Riemannian metrics
g
such that
(M,g)
is non-trapping and
\partial M
is stricly convex. This is an open set of the ambient
C^{\infty}
-space and we equip it with the subspace topology. It will we useful to embed
M
into a closed manifold

of the same dimension and extend all metrics to

(and Remark

E\colon\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}\rightarrow C^{\infty}(N,\otimes^{2}_{S}T^{*}N),\quad Eg|_{M}=g.

To simplify notation, the extended metric is also written as
g
.
Fix a background metric
g_{0}\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}
and denote with
SM\equiv SM_{g_{0}}
(resp.
SN\equiv SN_{g_{0}}
) the corresponding unit tangent bundle.
The rescaling map

\phi_{g}\colon SN\xrightarrow{\sim}SN_{g},\quad(x,v)\mapsto(x,v/|v|_{g}),\quad g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}

can be used to transfer all objects of interest to a fixed manifold.
For instance, if
(\varphi^{g}_{t})
denotes the
g
-geodesic flow on
SN_{g}
, we define a rescaled flow by

\psi^{g}\in C^{\infty}(SN\times\mathbb{R},SN),\quad\psi_{t}^{g}=\phi_{g}^{-1}\circ\varphi_{t}^{g}\circ\phi_{g}.

The infinitesimal generator of
\psi^{g}_{t}
will be denoted with
\mathbb{X}_{g}\in C^{\infty}(SN,T(SN))
.

Remark 5.1
.

If
d=2
, then one can show that
\mathbb{X}_{g}=a_{g}(X+\lambda_{g}V)
for smooth functions

having non-zero Fourier modes only in the degrees

and

(see Section 3.1 in
[

For
(x,v)\in SM_{g}
the

of the

and we write its pull-back to

\tilde{\tau}_{g}=(\tau_{+}^{g}+\tau_{-}^{g})\circ\phi_{g}\in C^{\infty}(SM,\mathbb{R}).

Additionally define

5.1.1.
\Upsilon
-map revisited

We can now re-define the

-map:

\Upsilon_{g}\colon\partial_{+}SM\times[0,1]\rightarrow SM,\quad\Upsilon_{g}(x,v,u)=\psi^{g}_{u\tilde{\tau}_{g}(x,v)}(x,v)

and recall from

Proposition 5.2

(Properties of the
\Upsilon

.

For all

(i)

(ii)

and

∎

5.1.2.

The X-ray transform of the

-flow is given by:

I_{g}\colon\rho^{-1/2}C^{\infty}(SM)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM),\quad I_{g}f(x,v)=\int_{0}^{\tilde{\tau}_{g}(x,v)}f\circ\psi_{t}(x,v)dt.

I_{g}(\rho^{-1/2}h)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Upsilon_{g}^{*}h}{F_{g}^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{du}{\sqrt{u(1-u)}},\quad h\in C^{\infty}(SM)

Further, the extension to

.

5.2.
Collection of continuous dependences

In the just described setting, all objects depend continuously on the metric.

Proposition 5.3

All of the following maps are well-defined and continuous:

5.3.
Conjugacies

The geodesic flows of any two metrics in
\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}
are smoothly conjugate up to a time change. We first show this for metrics in the closed subset

(5.3)

\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}(K)=\{g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}:\supp(g-g_{0})\subset K\},\quad K\subset M^{\circ}\text{ compact}.

Here the canonical choice of conjugacy (flow back along the orbits of
g_{0}
and then forward along the orbits of
g
) is smooth and can be written in terms of
\Upsilon
-maps.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
K\subset M^{\circ}
be compact and
g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}(K)
. Then there exists a unique solution
(\Phi_{g},a_{g})\in\Diff_{+}(SM)\times C^{\infty}(SM,\mathbb{R})
to

(5.4)

\Phi_{*}\mathbb{X}_{g_{0}}=a\mathbb{X}_{g},\quad\mathbb{X}_{g}a=0\text{ on }SM\quad\text{ and }\quad\Phi=\mathrm{Id}\text{ on }\partial_{+}SM.

For
g=g_{0}
we have
(\Phi,a)=(\mathrm{Id},1)
and moreover, the following map is continuous:

g\mapsto(\Phi_{g},a_{g}),\quad\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}(K)\rightarrow\Diff_{+}(SM)\times C^{\infty}(SM,\mathbb{R})

Proof.

Suppose
(\Phi,a)
is a solution to (
5.4
) and
(x,v)\in\partial_{+}SM
. Then
\Phi
necessarily maps the
g_{0}
-orbit emerging from
(x,v)

to the corresponding
g
-orbit, with a constant time change
a(x,v)=\tilde{\tau}_{g}(x,v)/\tau_{g_{0}}(x,v)
. Hence there is the representation

\Phi=\tilde{\Upsilon}_{g}\circ\tilde{\Upsilon}_{g_{0}}^{-1},\quad a=[\tilde{\tau}_{g}/\tilde{\tau}_{g_{0}}]^{\sharp_{g}},

which at once implies uniqueness and that
\Phi
is a homeomorphism. To show that this is a valid solution, we need to verify smoothness. Since
g=g_{0}
on
M\backslash K
there is a neighbourhood
U\subset\partial_{+}SM
of
\partial_{0}SM
such that
\Upsilon_{g}=\Upsilon_{g_{0}}
on
U\times[0,1]
. Hence

\Phi=\mathrm{Id}\text{ on }\Upsilon_{g_{0}}(U\times[0,1])\quad\text{ and }\quad\Phi=\Upsilon_{g}\circ\Upsilon_{g_{0}}^{-1},\quad\text{ on }SM\backslash\partial_{0}SM

and
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
must be smooth. Consider the spaces

C_{\alpha,\pm}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)=\{h\in C^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM):A_{\pm}^{g}h\in C^{\infty}(\partial SM)\},

where
u=A_{\pm}^{g}h\in L^{\infty}(\partial SM)
is the unique function with
u\circ\alpha_{g}=\pm u
and
\alpha_{g}(x,v)=\Upsilon_{g}(x,v,1)
is the scattering relation of
g
. Since
g=g_{0}
on
M\backslash K
, we have
\alpha_{g}=\alpha_{g_{0}}
in a neighbourhood of
\partial_{0}SM
. This makes the spaces
C_{\alpha,\pm}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)
independent of the choice
g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}(K)
used in their definition. Moreover,
m(x,v)={g(\nu(x),v))}
is a smooth function on
\partial SM
that is independent of
g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}(K)
. By
[
27
, Section 3.2]
,

\tilde{\tau}_{g},\tilde{\tau}_{g_{0}}\in C^{\infty}_{\alpha,-}(\partial_{+}SM),\quad\tilde{\tau}_{g}/m,\tilde{\tau}_{g_{0}}/m\in C^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)

and as a consequence
\tilde{\tau}_{g}/\tilde{\tau}_{g_{0}}\in C^{\infty}_{\alpha,+}(\partial_{+}SM).

Finally, since
\sharp_{g}
maps
C^{\infty}_{\alpha,+}(\partial_{+}SM)
into
C^{\infty}(SM)
, also
a
must be smooth.

To verify that
\Phi=\Phi_{g}\in\Diff_{+}(SM)
depends continuously on the metric, it suffices to verify continuity of the restrictions

g\mapsto\Phi_{g}|_{V},\quad\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}(K)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(V,SM),\quad V\subset SM\text{ open}.

For
V=\Upsilon_{g_{0}}(U\times[0,1])
this restriction is equals the identity and so there is nothing to prove. For
V=SM\backslash\partial_{0}SM
continuity follows from Proposition
5.3
(iv). Next, by Proposition
5.3
(iii), the map

g\mapsto a_{g}|_{\partial_{+}SM},\quad\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}(K)\rightarrow C_{\alpha,+}^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)

is continuous and hence also
\tilde{a}_{g}=(a_{g}|_{\partial_{+}SM})^{\sharp_{g_{0}}}
, as a function in
C^{\infty}(SM)
, depends continuously on
g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}(K)
. Since
a_{g}=\tilde{a}_{g}\circ\Phi_{g}^{-1}
, the proof is complete.
∎

For metrics without compact support condition, smoothness might be lost at the glancing region. This can be dealt with by moving the boundary condition to a slightly larger manifold.

Proposition 5.5
.

There is a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}
of
g_{0}
and a continuous map

g\mapsto(\Phi_{g},a_{g}),\quad\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\Diff_{+}(SM)\times C^{\infty}(SM,\mathbb{R})

such that
(\Phi_{g},a_{g})
solves
\Phi_{*}\mathbb{X}_{g_{0}}=a\mathbb{X}_{g}
and
\mathbb{X}_{g}a=0
. Moreover,
(\Phi_{g_{0}},a_{g_{0}})=(\mathrm{Id},1)

Proof.

Recall that
M
is embedded into a closed manifold
N
of the same dimension. Let
M^{\prime}\subset N
be a slightly larger manifold with boundary, containing
M
in its interior and such that
(M^{\prime},g_{0})
remains non-trapping with strictly convex boundary. By modifying the extension map

from (

) if necessary, we may assume that
\supp(Eg-g_{0})\subset K
for a compact set

and all

. Let
\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}^{\prime}(K)\subset C^{\infty}(M^{\prime},\otimes^{2}_{S}T^{*}M^{\prime})

by applying the preceding proposition on

and then restricting to

.
∎

5.4.
Parametrising the
C_{\alpha}^{\infty}
-structure

The rescaling map restricts to a homeomorphism of the influx boundaries:

\phi_{g}|_{\partial_{+}SM}\colon\partial_{+}{SM}\to\partial_{+}SM^{g},\quad g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{ntc}}

This is automatically a diffeomorphism with respect to the standard

that is sensitive to the boundary behaviour of

. Equivalently, this smooth structure can be obtained by first rescaling the fold map from (

f_{g}\colon\partial_{+}SM\to\partial S\hat{M},\quad f_{g}=\phi_{g}^{-1}\circ F_{g}\circ\phi_{g}

in some open set, for which we can choose a common extension
\hat{M}\subset N
of

\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{W}^{\partial_{0}SM}(\partial SM,\partial S\hat{M}),\quad g\mapsto f_{g}

6.1.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
g_{0}\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}

\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}

Further, the following map is continuous:

g\mapsto P^{g},\quad\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\Psi^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(N),

Proof.

First, by Lemma
A.5
there is a continuous extension map

E\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow C^{\infty}(N,\otimes^{2}_{S}T^{*}N),\quad Eg|_{M}=g

such that

is a Riemannian metric on

6.2.
The Grubb–Hörmander framework

Consider the space

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(M)=\{e_{M}\rho^{\mu}u:u\in C^{\infty}(M)\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(N),\quad\mu\in\mathbb{C},\ \operatorname{Re}\mu>-1,

which is isomorphic to

and becomes a Fréchet space in this way. An operator
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(N)
is said to satisfy the
\mu
-transmission condition
, if

(6.4)

r_{M}Au\in C^{\infty}(M)\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(M).

This property has been studied extensively by Grubb and Hörmander (see
[

), and is equivalent to a certain symmetry of the local symbol of

at
\partial M
—we refer to the discussion in
[
23
, pp.27]
for more details. As observed in
[
23
, Lemma 4.2]
, the operators

from the preceding section do satisfy the
\mu
-transmission condition for
\mu=-1/2
. This leads to a number of mapping properties, which ultimately allow to establish the isomorphism property.

We retrace the argument from
[
23
]
with a view on continuous
g
-dependence. To this end it is useful to introduce a scale of Hilbert spaces

(

, referred to as

. For their definition, which is intricate, we refer to
[
23
, p.29]
and the references therein. For us it suffices to know the following properties:

(i)

H^{\mu(0)}(M)=L^{2}(M)
and
H^{\mu(s+t)}(M)\subset H^{\mu(s)}(M)
for all

(ii)

\bigcap_{s\in\mathbb{R}}H^{\mu(s)}(M)=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(M)
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(M)
carries the initial topology with respect to the inclusions
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(M)\hookrightarrow H^{\mu(s)}(M)

(iii)

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(M)\subset H^{\mu(s)}(M)
is a dense subspace for all

; see
[

;

(iv)

if

satisfies the
\mu
-transmission condition, then it has the continuous mapping property

r_{M}A\colon H^{\mu(s)}(M)\rightarrow H^{s-m}(M),\quad s>\operatorname{Re}\mu-1/2;

see
[

.

Proposition 6.4
.

Any

(

A\mapsto r_{M}A,\quad\Psi_{\mathrm{ev}}^{m}(N)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(H^{\mu(s)}(M),H^{s-m}(M))

The novelty of this proposition is the continuity claim, which is proved by means of the following application of the closed graph theorem.

Lemma 6.5

(Continuity upgrade)
.

Suppose
E
and
F
are Fréchet spaces and

is a linear map. Let
\iota\colon F\rightarrow G
be a continuous linear injection into another topological vector space. Then

\iota\circ T\colon E\rightarrow G\text{ continuous}\quad\Rightarrow\quad T\colon E\rightarrow F\text{ continuous}.

Proof of Lemma
6.5
.

As
T^{\prime}=\iota\circ T
is continuous,
\mathrm{Graph}(T^{\prime})=\{(x,T^{\prime}x):x\in E\}
is a closed subset of
E\times G
. Define
j\colon E\times F\rightarrow E\times G
by
j(x,y)=(x,\iota(y))
. Let
(x,y)\in E\times F
, then due to the injectivity of
\iota
it holds that

j(x,y)\in\mathrm{Graph}(T^{\prime})\penalty\ \Leftrightarrow\penalty\ \iota(y)=\iota(Tx)\penalty\ \Leftrightarrow\penalty\ y=Tx\penalty\ \Leftrightarrow\penalty\ (x,y)\in\mathrm{Graph}(T).

This means that
\mathrm{Graph}(T)=j^{-1}(\mathrm{Graph}(T^{\prime}))
and as

is continuous, the graph of
T
must be closed in
E\times F
. The continuity of
T
then follows from the closed graph theorem for Fréchet spaces.
∎

We wish to apply the preceding lemma with
G
given by a space of continuous linear operators between non-normed spaces.
For two topological vector spaces
E
and

we may equip the space
\mathcal{L}(E,F)
of continuous linear maps

with the

and denote the resulting topological vector space with

.

Proof of Proposition
6.4
.

The fact that operators in

(

In order to show continuity of the map (

We need to check two things:

(1)

is continuous as a map

;

(2)

the map

to

For (1), we consider

is continuous as map
f

For (2), continuity is clear and it remains to show injectivity. This however follows from the density

∎

Proof of Theorem

Recall the following elementary result:

Lemma 6.6
.

Let

and

be normed vector spaces. Then the subset

Remark 6.7
.

In general the lemma fails for non-normed topological vector spaces. Consider e.g.

and

in

Proof of Lemma

.

If

. Let

\displaystyle T^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{0}^{-1}(\mathrm{Id}-H)^{-1}=T_{0}^{-1}\sum_{k\geq 0}H^{k},

\displaystyle\|T^{-1}-T_{0}^{-1}\|

Consequently, also the following map is continuous:

7.1.
Lipschitz maps on Hermitian manifolds

Let

We assume for simplicity that the bundles

(7.2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\Lambda^{1,0}Y\oplus\Lambda^{0,1}Y\right)_{f(x)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\Lambda^{1,0}X\oplus\Lambda^{0,1}X\right)_{x}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{m}}

and

and

Proposition 7.2
.

Suppose

is a smooth map whose differential is given with respect to orthonormal frames as

(i)

is globally Lipschitz from

into

and its Lipschitz constant satisfies

\Lip(f)\leq\|S\|_{L^{\infty}(X)}+\|T\|_{L^{\infty}(X)}.

(ii)

then

(7.3)

\Lip(f)\leq 1+\epsilon\quad\text{ and }\quad\Lip(f^{-1})\leq(1-\epsilon)^{-1},

or equivalently,

(7.4)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,x^{\prime}\in X\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left|1-\frac{d_{\Omega_{Y}}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))}{d_{\Omega_{X}}(x,x^{\prime})}\right|\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

For
(i)
it suffices to uniformly bound the operator norm of

, which satisfies (writing

and

(7.5)

|df_{x}|^{2}=|df_{x}^{T}|^{2}\leq\left|\begin{bmatrix}S&\bar{T}\\
T&\bar{S}\end{bmatrix}\right|^{2}=\sup\left\{|Sv+\bar{T}w|^{2}+|Tv+\bar{S}w|^{2}\right\},

where the supremum is taken over all

with
|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}\leq 1
. The desired norm estimate then follows from Young’s inequality:

\displaystyle|Sv+\bar{T}w|^{2}+|Tv+\bar{S}w|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|S|^{2}(|v|^{2}+|w|^{2})+4|S||T||v||w|+|T|^{2}(|v|^{2}+|w|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(|S|+|T|)^{2}(|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}),

For
(ii)
, the first bound in (
7.3
) follows immediately from
(i)
. The differential of
f^{-1}
at

is given as

\begin{bmatrix}S&\bar{T}\\
T&\bar{S}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}F&F\bar{G}\\
\bar{F}G&\bar{F}\end{bmatrix},\penalty\ \text{where}\penalty\ \penalty\ F=(S-\bar{T}\bar{S}^{-1}T)^{-1},G=-TS^{-1}.

Writing

\displaystyle|Q|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon}{9}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\epsilon/3}=\frac{\epsilon}{3-\epsilon},\quad|F|\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}|Q|^{k}\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{\epsilon}{3-\epsilon}}=\frac{3-\epsilon}{3-2\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

is holomorphic, then
h=g\circ f_{1}-g\circ f_{2}
is holomorphic on
V
and satisfies
h(\mu_{n})=0
for infinitely many
n
. By the identity principle
h\equiv 0
on
V
and, again using point separation,
f_{1}=f_{2}
on
V
. This shows that
\mu_{*}\in\Sigma
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.7
.

Let
x\in M_{1}
and let

be a holomorphic curve whose image is the fibre

over

. Then both

and
\Psi\circ f
are holomorphic curves and by assumption they have the same boundary values. Hence
\Phi\circ f=\Psi\circ f
, which means that
\Phi
and
\Psi
agree on the fibre

. This holds for all

∎

Appendix A
Continuity theorems in geometry

We recapitulate some aspects from geometric analysis with a focus on continuous dependence on the underlying geometry.

A.1.
Riemann mapping theorem

Define:

\displaystyle\Riem(\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D},\otimes_{S}^{2}T^{*}\mathbb{D}):g\text{ is a Riemannian metric}\}

\displaystyle\Bel(\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{m\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{C}):|m|<1\}

These are the spaces of
Riemannian metrics
and
Beltrami coefficients
on
\mathbb{D}
, respectively, and they both lie open in the ambient
C^{\infty}
-spaces and as such they are Fréchet manifolds. Each
m\in\Bel(\mathbb{D})
encodes an orientation compatible complex structure on
\mathbb{D}
with anti-holomorphic tangent bundle

(A.1)

\mathscr{D}_{m}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}(\partial_{\bar{z}}+m\partial_{z})\subset T_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{D},

and vice versa, every complex structure on
\mathbb{D}
that is compatible with the standard orientation is given in terms of such a Beltrami coefficient. In particular, the complex structure determined by
g\in\Riem(\mathbb{D})
is given by

m_{g}(z)=\frac{\langle dz,v+iv^{\perp}\rangle}{\langle d\bar{z},v+iv^{\perp}\rangle},\quad\text{ where }\{v,v^{\perp}\}\subset T_{z}\mathbb{D}\text{ is an oriented $g$-ONB}.

Let
\Diff_{+}(\mathbb{D})
be the orientation preserving diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{D}
, equipped with the
C^{\infty}
-topology. Moreover, let
\Diff_{+}(\mathbb{D},\{0,1\})\subset\Diff_{+}(\mathbb{D})
be the closed subset of diffeomorphisms with
\psi(0)=0
and
\psi(1)=1
.
Then the following version of the Riemann Mapping Theorem was proved by
Earle–Schatz in
[
6
, Theorem on p.171]
:

Theorem A.1

(RMT with continuous dependence)
.

For any
m\in\Bel(\mathbb{D})
there exists a unique
\psi\in\Diff_{+}(\mathbb{D},\{0,1\})
such that
\psi\colon(\mathbb{D},\mathscr{D}_{m})\rightarrow(\mathbb{D},\mathscr{D}_{0})
is holomorphic (
\psi_{*}\mathscr{D}_{m}=\mathscr{D}_{0}
). The map
m\mapsto\psi
is a homeomorphism
\Bel(\mathbb{D})\cong\Diff_{+}(\mathbb{D},\{0,1\})
.

Corollary A.2
.

There is a continuous map
P\colon\Riem(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{R})
such that:

(i)

P(e^{2\sigma}|dz|^{2})=\sigma
for all
\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{R})
;

(ii)

(\mathbb{D},g)\cong(\mathbb{D},e^{2\sigma}|dz|^{2})
isometric for
\sigma=P(g)
and
g\in\Riem(\mathbb{D})
.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\Diff_{+}(\mathbb{D},\{0,1\})
the image of
m_{g}
under the homeomorphism in Theorem
A.1
. Write
\phi=\psi^{-1}
, let
\mathbf{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D},T\mathbb{D})
be a vector field with
|\mathbf{1}|_{|dz|^{2}}\equiv 1
and define

P(g):=\log|\mathbf{1}|_{\phi^{*}g}.
Using that
\Diff_{+}(\mathbb{D},\{0,1\})
is a topological group that acts continuously on
\Riem(\mathbb{D})
by pull-backs, one readily verifies that
P
is continuous.
Let us verify the two properties: If
g=e^{2\sigma}|dz|^{2}
, then
m_{g}=0
and
\psi=\phi=\mathrm{Id}
, such that
P(g)=\log(e^{\sigma}|\mathbf{1}|_{|dz|^{2}})=\sigma
. For general
g\in\Riem(\mathbb{D})
, the map
\phi=\psi^{-1}\colon(\mathbb{D},|dz|^{2})\rightarrow(\mathbb{D},g)
is conformal, which is to say that
\phi^{*}g=e^{2\sigma}|dz|^{2}
for
some

\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{R})
. Clearly
P(g)=\sigma
, and the proof is complete.
∎

A.2.
Integration of vector fields

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a closed
d
-dimensional manifold and
T\geq 1
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{T}=\mathcal{M}\times[0,T]
. Then an
isotopy
on
\mathcal{M}
is a smooth map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{M}_{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}

such that
\Phi_{t}(x)=\Phi(x,t)
defines a diffeomorphism of
\mathcal{M}
for all
0\leq t\leq T
, with
\Phi_{0}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{M}}
. We denote the set of all isotopies on
\mathcal{M}
by

\mathrm{Iso}_{T}(\mathcal{M})\subset\{\Phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}_{T},\mathcal{M}):\Phi(\cdot,0)=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{M}}\}.

The set on the right lies closed inside the ambient
C^{\infty}
-space and contains
\mathrm{Iso}_{T}(\mathcal{M})
as an open subset.
To every isotopy
\Phi
we can associate a time-dependent vector field
X
, i.e., an element of
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}_{T},T\mathcal{M})
, via the relation

\partial_{t}\Phi(x,t)=X(\Phi_{t}(x),t),\quad(x,t)\in\mathcal{M}_{T}.

Vice versa, every such vector field
X
can be integrated to an isotopy
\Phi^{X}
by solving this ordinary differential equation. We record here the – certainly well-known – observation that the map
X\mapsto\Phi^{X}
is continuous in the
C^{\infty}
-topology.

Theorem A.3
.

The map
X\mapsto\Phi^{X}
is a homeomorphism
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}_{T},T\mathcal{M})\cong\mathrm{Iso}_{T}(\mathcal{M})
.

Proof (Sketch).

The result can be reduced to the continuity of the exponential map

X\mapsto\Phi^{X}_{1},\quad C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M},T\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathrm{Diff}(\mathcal{M})

for time-independent vector fields, which is proved by Ebin–Marsden in
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
. Precisely, the authors prove continuity into
\Diff^{s}(\mathcal{M})
, the space of diffeomorphisms of Sobolev
H^{s}
-regularity, for all
s>d/2+2
. Since
\Diff(\mathcal{M})
carries the initial topology with respect to the inclusions into
\Diff^{s}(\mathcal{M})
, this gives the desired continuity.

To obtain continuity of the full isotopy, one can embed
\mathcal{M}_{T}
into a closed manifold
\mathcal{N}
of dimension
d+1
and consider a smooth vector field
Y
on
\mathcal{N}
with
Y(x,t)=tX(x)
for
(x,t)\in\mathcal{M}_{T}\subset\mathcal{N}
. The isotopy
\Psi^{Y}\in\mathrm{Iso}_{1}(\mathcal{N})
leaves
\mathcal{M}_{T}
invariant and satisfies

\Psi_{s}^{Y}(x,t)=(\Phi_{s}^{tX}(x),t)=(\Phi^{X}_{st}(x),t),\quad(x,t)\in\mathcal{M}_{T}\subset\mathcal{N},0\leq s\leq 1.

The full isotopy
\Phi^{X}\in\mathrm{Iso}_{T}(\mathcal{M})
can be recovered from
\Psi_{1}^{Y}
, and after constructing a continuous extension map
X\mapsto Y
, this allows to derive continuity of the map

X\mapsto\Phi^{X},\quad C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M},T\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathrm{Iso}_{T}(\mathcal{M}).

The passage to time-dependent vector fields is done similarly, by extending
X
to a time-independent vector field on
\mathcal{N}
and then applying the preceding result. This proves that also the following map is continuous:

X\mapsto\Phi^{X},\quad C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}_{T},T\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathrm{Iso}_{T}(\mathcal{M}).

It is clear that this is a bijection, with inverse provided by differentiating a given isotopy. This is clearly continuous and so we indeed obtain a homeomorphism.
∎

For convenience, we record the following result as a corollary (it could also be derived using variation of constants):

Corollary A.4
.

Let
M
be a closed manifold and
M_{T}=M\times[0,T]
. For
A\in C^{\infty}(M_{T})
and
a_{0},b_{0}\in C^{\infty}(M)
, denote with
f_{A}\in C^{\infty}(M_{T})
the unique solution to the initial value problem

\partial_{t}^{2}f+Af=0\text{ on }M_{T},\quad(f,\partial_{t}f)|_{t=0}=(a_{0},b_{0}).

Then the map
A\mapsto f_{A}
is continuous from
C^{\infty}(M_{T})
into
C^{\infty}(M_{T})
.

Proof.

On
\mathcal{M}=M_{x}\times\mathbb{R}_{a}\times\mathbb{R}_{b}
consider the time-dependent vector field
X(x,a,b,t)=b\,\partial_{a}-A(x,t)a\,\partial_{b}

, whose isotopy satisfies
\Phi^{X}(x,a_{0}(x),b_{0}(x),t)=(x,f_{A}(x,t),\partial_{t}f_{A}(x,t))
for all
(x,t)\in M_{T}
. Projecting onto the second component, we retrieve
f_{A}
and the corollary follows from the preceding theorem.
∎

A.3.
Riemannian extensions

Lemma A.5
.

Let
M
be a compact manifold with boundary, embedded into a closed manifold
N
of the same dimension. Then for any Riemannian metric
g_{0}
there is a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
inside

and a continuous map

E\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow C^{\infty}(N,\otimes^{2}_{S}T^{*}N),\quad Eg|_{M}=g

such that
Eg
is a Riemannian metric on
N
for all
g\in\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

First, there is a continuous
linear
extension operator

E_{0}\colon C^{\infty}(M,\otimes_{S}^{2}T^{*}M)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(S,\otimes_{S}^{2}T^{*}N),\quad E_{0}f|_{M}=f,

this is proved e.g. by Seeley in
[
31
]
. Let
h
be an arbitrary metric on
N
and let
\epsilon>0
be so small that the symmetric
2
-tensor
g_{0}-2\epsilon h
is positive definite on
M
. For
f\in C^{\infty}(M,\otimes_{S}^{2}T^{*}M)
, define

U(f,\delta)=\{x\in N:(E_{0}f-\delta h)_{x}\text{ is positive definite}\},\quad\delta>0.

Then
U_{0}=U(g_{0},2\epsilon)
is an open neighbourhood of
M
in
N
. Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}(S,[0,1])
be
\equiv 1
in a neighbourhood of
M
and
\equiv 0
on
N\backslash U_{0}
. We claim that

\mathcal{U}:=\{g\in C^{\infty}(M,\otimes^{2}_{S}T^{*}M):U(g,\epsilon)\supset\bar{U}_{0}\},\quad Eg:=\chi E_{0}g+(1-\chi)h

have the desired properties. Clearly,
\mathcal{U}
is open and contains

. It remains to show that

is positive definite for all

. If
\chi(x)=0
, this is obvious. If
\chi(x)>0
, then

, and hence each term in

(Eg)_{x}=\chi(x)(E_{0}g-\epsilon h)_{x}+(1+\epsilon\chi(x)-\chi(x))h_{x}

A.5.
Whitney folds

Let
X
and
Y
be closed orientable
n
-manifolds and
S\subset Y
a closed embedded hypersurface. We wish to consider smooth maps and involutions

(f,\alpha)\in C^{\infty}(Y,X)\times\Diff(Y)\quad\text{ with }f\circ\alpha=f\text{ and }\alpha\circ\alpha=\mathrm{Id},

such that
f
has a

at every point in

(see also §

), having
\alpha
as its non-trivial involution. The purpose of this section is to describe a setting where the local theory from
[
15

can be globalised and to supplement the theory with adequate continuity theorems.

To ensure that
Y
supports global involutions with fixed point set

, we will from now on impose the following condition:
Y
is the union of two connected submanifolds
Y_{1},Y_{2}\subset Y
having disjoint interiors and a common boundary
\partial Y_{1}=\partial Y_{2}=S
. To further ease discussion we fix two volume forms
\omega_{X}
and
\omega_{Y}
and, given a smooth map
f\colon Y\to X
, write
\lambda_{f}\in C^{\infty}(Y,\mathbb{R})
for the function defined by
f^{*}\omega_{X}=\lambda_{f}\,\omega_{Y}

Definition A.8
.

Define the following sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{S}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{f\in C^{\infty}(Y,X):\penalty\ \begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm(1)}&\lambda_{f}=0\text{ on }S\\
{\rm(2)}&|d\lambda_{f}|+|df|>0\text{ on }TY|_{S}\\
{\rm(3)}&f|_{S}\in\mathrm{Emb}(S,X)\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{S}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{f\in\mathcal{W}^{S}_{\mathrm{loc}}(Y,X):\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm(4)}&|\lambda_{f}|>0\text{ on }Y\backslash S\\
{\rm(5)}&f|_{Y_{k}}\colon Y_{k}\to X\text{ is a topological}\\
&\text{embedding for }k=1,2\\
{\rm(6)}&f(Y_{1})=f(Y_{2})\end{array}\right\}

Proposition A.9

(Local and global folds and their involutions)
.

(i)

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{S}(Y,X)
is an open subset of
\{f\in C^{\infty}(Y,X):\lambda_{f}=0\text{ on }S\}
;

(ii)

for all
f\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{S}(Y,X)
there exists an
\epsilon>0
and embeddings

\varphi_{X}^{f}\colon S\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\to X\quad\text{ and }\quad\varphi_{Y}^{f}\colon S\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\to Y

such that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y\mapsto(y,0)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(y,t)\mapsto(y,t^{2})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi^{f}_{Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi^{f}_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

(iii)

every

has an open neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
such that for
f\in\mathcal{U}
one can choose

(iv)

\mathcal{W}^{S}(Y,X)\subset\mathcal{W}^{S}_{\mathrm{loc}}(Y,X)
is open;

(v)

for every

there exists a unique
\alpha\in\Diff(Y)\backslash\{\mathrm{Id}\}

and

. The following map is continuous:

f\mapsto\alpha,\quad\mathcal{W}^{S}(Y,X)\to\Diff(Y)

Proof.

Part (i) is obvious. For (ii) we may choose
\varphi_{X}\colon S\times[-1,1]\to X
to be any embedding that extends
f
in the sense that
\varphi_{X}(y,0)=f(y)
for all

.
Then in a neighbourhood

of

is a submersion with

being transversal to

. Hence, after shrinking

if necessary,

is a fibre bundle with fibre
\mathbb{R}
. As
S\subset U
is two-sided, there exists a trivialisation

\tau\colon S\times\mathbb{R}\xrightarrow{\sim}U

with
\tau(y,0)=y
for all

. Define

by
F(y,s)=\mathrm{pr}_{2}\circ\varphi_{X}^{-1}\circ f\circ\tau(y,s)
. Then for all

we have

, the last ‘

we have

\partial_{s}^{2}F(y,s)\geq c
(

possibly after flipping a sign in the definition of

\varphi_{X}
and
\varphi_{Y}
. The definition of
\varphi_{X}
relies on extending the smooth embedding

\varphi_{Y}
relies only on elementary calculus and as such is continuous in
f
.

f_{0}\in\mathcal{W}^{S}(Y,X)
and let
\mathcal{U}
and
\epsilon>0
be as in (iii). Then the sets

K_{k}=Y_{k}\backslash\varphi_{Y}^{f_{0}}\left(S\times(-\epsilon/2,\epsilon/2)\right),\quad k\in\{1,2\}

are compact and connected. Consider the following conditions on
f\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle{\rm(a)}

\displaystyle\penalty\ Y_{k}\backslash K_{k}^{\circ}\subset\varphi_{Y}^{f}\left(S\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\right),\quad

\displaystyle{\rm(b)}\penalty\ \inf_{K_{k}}|\lambda_{f}|>0,

\displaystyle{\rm(c)}

\displaystyle\penalty\ f(K_{k})\cap f(S)=\emptyset,\quad

\displaystyle{\rm(d)}\penalty\ f(K_{1}^{\circ})\cap f(K_{2}^{\circ})\neq\emptyset.

These conditions are all satisfied for

and further they define an open subset of
\mathcal{U}

, proving that

is an interior point.

To prove the claim suppose that
f\in\mathcal{U}
satisfies conditions (a)–(d). Then property (4) can be checked pointwise on the sets

and a tubular neighbourhood of
S
, where it follows from (b) or the normal form of
f
, respectively.
Due to (c) and (d) there is a unique connected component

of

that contains both

f(K_{2})

.

Finally, for (v) the involution

can be defined by
\alpha|_{Y_{k}}=f|_{Y_{j}}^{-1}\circ f|_{Y_{k}}
(

leaves

, because it is explicitly given in terms of

\alpha\circ\varphi_{X}^{f}(y,t)=\varphi_{X}^{f}(y,-t)

.

The main theorem about pull-backs by Whitney folds – stated here in its global form – asserts that the following non-linear sequence is exact:

(A.4)

\mathcal{W}^{S}(Y,X)\times C^{\infty}(X)\to\mathcal{W}^{S}(Y,X)\times C^{\infty}(Y)\to C^{\infty}(Y)

Here we first map
(f,w)\mapsto(f,f^{*}w)
and then
(f,h)\mapsto h\circ\alpha-h
, where
\alpha
is the involution associated to

. Exactness means that the image of the first map equals the

preimage of the second map. For the latter we also write

\mathcal{X}=\{(f,h)\in\mathcal{W}^{S}(Y,X)\times C^{\infty}(Y):h=h\circ\alpha\}.

Theorem A.10
.

The non-linear sequence (
A.4
) admits a continuous left-split. Precisely, there exists a continuous map

(f,h)\mapsto w,\quad\mathcal{X}\to C^{\infty}(X)

such that

.

Proof (Sketch).

The assertion is non-trivial only near the fold, where one may pass to the normal form provided in the preceding proposition. Writing

\mathcal{X}_{0}=\{h\in C^{\infty}\left(S\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\right):h(y,t)=w(y,-t)\}

it then suffices to find a continuous
linear
map

h\mapsto w,\quad\mathcal{X}_{0}\to C^{\infty}\left(S\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\right)

such that
w(y,t^{2})=h(y,t)
for all
(y,t)\in S\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
. This however can be achieved by the same methods as in Theorem C.4.4 of
[
15
]
, using the open mapping theorem to establish continuity.
∎

Appendix B
Continuous dependence of the normal operator

We recapitulate the proof that the

of a simple manifold (defined in §
2.2
) is a classical pseudodifferential operator and show that as such it depends continuously on the metric.

B.1.
Classical
\psi
do and their topology

A smooth function
a\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\xi}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is called a
classical symbol
of order
m\in\mathbb{R}
, if

(B.1)

a(x,\xi)=|\xi|^{m}\hat{a}(x,\xi/|\xi|,1/|\xi|),\quad|\xi|>0,

for some
\hat{a}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[0,\infty))
. The space of all such symbols is denoted with
S_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
and it is turned into a Fréchet space by the identification with

\{(a,\hat{a})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\times C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[0,\infty)):\text{\eqref{classical2} holds}\},

which is closed in the
C^{\infty}
-topology. A symbol
a\in S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
is called
even
, if the function
\hat{a}
from (
B.1
) has a smooth extension to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
with the property

\hat{a}(x,-\omega,h)=\hat{a}(x,\omega,-h),\quad(x,\omega,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}.

The set of even symbols defines a closed subspace
S^{m}_{\mathrm{ev}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
of
S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Remark B.1
.

Taylor expanding
\hat{a}(x,\omega,h)
in
h=0
yields an asymptotic expansion

a(x,\xi)\sim\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}(x,\xi),\quad a_{k}(x,\xi)=|\xi|^{m-k}\partial_{h}^{k}\hat{a}(x,\xi/|\xi|,0)/k!,\quad|\xi|>0,

which is frequently taken as equivalent definition of classicality (cf.
[
35
,
32
]
). From this point of view, evenness of
a
is the assertion that
a_{k}(x,-\xi)=(-1)^{k}a_{k}(x,\xi)
for all
k\geq 0
(cf.
[
23
, Definition 4.6]
).

For
a\in S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
the
quantisation map

\Op\colon S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\Op(a)(x,y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x-y)}a(x,\xi){d\xi},

interpreted as oscillatory integral, is well-defined, continuous and injective. Identifying operators with their Schwartz kernels, the space of classical pseudodifferential operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can then be defined as

\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\Op(S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}))+C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

To topologise this we give an alternative definition, valid on any open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We define
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U\times U)
to consist of those Schwartz kernels
A(x,y)
for which

(a)

A
is smooth away from the diagonal
\Delta_{U}=\{(x,y)\in U\times U:x=y\}
;

(b)

if
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
, then
A_{\varphi}(x,y)=\varphi(x)A(x,y)\varphi(y)
lies in
\Op(S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

For
U=\mathbb{R}^{d}
this is equivalent to the preceding definition.
Writing
A_{\varphi}=\Op(a_{\varphi})
, we equip
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)
with the initial topology for the collection of maps

(B.2)

\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)\xrightarrow{A\mapsto A|_{U\times U\backslash\Delta_{U}}}C^{\infty}(U\times U\backslash\Delta_{U}),\quad\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)\xrightarrow{A\mapsto a_{\varphi}}S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\varphi
runs through all elements of
C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
. In other words, a sequence of operators in
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)
converges if and only if their kernels converge in the
C^{\infty}
-topology away from the diagonal, and all local symbols converge in
S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
With this topology,
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)
is a Fréchet space—as the completeness is crucial in this article, we include a brief sketch of this well-known fact.

Lemma B.2
.

\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(U)
is a Fréchet space.

Proof (Sketch).

Let
U=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}V_{k}
be a locally finite open cover with
\bar{V}_{k}\subset U
(
k=1,2,\dots
). Define
W_{0}=U\times U\backslash\Delta_{U}
,
W_{k}=V_{k}\times V_{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, as well as

\tilde{\Psi}^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)\subset\{(A_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\in C^{\infty}(W_{0})\times\prod_{k\geq 1}S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}):(\ref{dagger})\text{ holds}\},

where the tuples are subjected to the constraint

(B.3)

A_{j}=A_{k}\text{ on }W_{j}\cap W_{k}\penalty\ \penalty\ (j,k\geq 0),\quad\text{ where }A_{k}=\Op(a_{k})\penalty\ \penalty\ (k\geq 1).

If (
B.3
) is satisfied, then there is a Schwartz kernel
A\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U\times U)
with
A=A_{k}
on
W_{k}
(
k\geq 0
)
and sending a given tuple to this kernel yields a map

\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(U)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U\times U).

One checks that this map induces a homeomorphism
\tilde{\Psi}^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)\cong\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(U)
and as the former space is a closed subspace of a countably infinite product of Fréchet spaces, also
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)
is a Fréchet space—we leave the details to the reader.
∎

On a manifold
X
of dimension
d
, the space
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(X)\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X\times X)
is defined by replacing condition (b) above with:

(b’)

if
\psi\colon V\xrightarrow{\sim}U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a local chart and
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
, then
A_{\varphi,\psi}(x,y)=\varphi(x)A(\psi^{-1}(x),\psi^{-1}(y))\varphi(y)
lies in
\Op(S^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

Analogous considerations then give a natural Fréchet space structure on
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(X)
that coincides with the one above when
X=U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By replacing classical symbols at each step with even symbols we also obtain the closed subspace
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{ev}}(X)\subset\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(X)
of
even pseudodifferential operators
.

B.2.
Fourier transform of homogeneous functions

Suppose
Q\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{x}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{z}\backslash 0)\times\mathbb{R}_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is smooth and satisfies, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the homogeneity property

Q(x,tz,h)=t^{\lambda}Q(x,z,th),\quad t>0.

For later use, we wish to consider the inverse Fourier transform of
Q
in the
z
-variable, restricted to frequencies
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Formally this yields a function

TQ(x,\omega,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{iz\omega}Q(x,z,h)dz,\quad(x,\omega,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}.

Due to the homogeneity,
Q
may be recovered from its restriction to
|z|=1
as
Q=E_{\lambda}(Q|_{|z|=1})
, where the extension operator
E_{\lambda}
is defined by

E_{\lambda}q(x,z,h)=|z|^{\lambda}q(x,z/|z|,|z|h),\quad q\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}).

Lemma B.3
.

If
\lambda>-d
, then
T_{\lambda}=TE_{\lambda}
defines a continuous linear map

T_{\lambda}\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}).

Proof (Sketch).

It is enough to show continuity
T_{\lambda}\colon C_{K}^{\infty}\to C^{\infty}
for all compacts
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, where
C^{\infty}_{K}
stands for smooth functions supported in
K
. Showing continuity into
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2d+1})
is straightforward and, by virtue of Lemma
6.5
, this can be upgraded to continuity into a
C^{\infty}
-space, if one shows that
T_{\lambda}Q
is smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash 0)\times\mathbb{R}
. The latter assertion is standard if
Q
is independent of the parameters
x
and
h
(see e.g. §7.4 in
[
33
]
) and the arguments carry over to the present setting.
∎

B.3.
A continuous family of pseudodifferential operators

Let
\Psi^{m}_{\mathrm{cl}}(M^{\circ})
denote the space of classical pseudodifferential operators of order
m\in\mathbb{R}
on the interior
M^{\circ}
of
M
. It is well known (see e.g. Theorem 8.1.1 in
[
27
]
) that

N_{0}^{g}\in\Psi^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}(M^{\circ}),

provided
g
is simple. Here we show that
as classical pseudodifferential operator
, it depends continuously on the metric
g
.

Proposition B.4
.

The following map is continuous:

g\mapsto N_{0}^{g},\quad\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}\rightarrow\Psi^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}(M^{\circ})

Here
\Psi^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}(M^{\circ})
carries its natural Fréchet space topology.

In the setting of closed Anosov manifolds a similar continuity statement was proved in
[
10
, Proposition 4.1]
. Near the diagonal of
M^{\circ}\times M^{\circ}
their analysis essentially carries over to the present setting and yields continuity into
\Psi^{-1}(M^{\circ})
, that is, into the weaker topology of non-classical pseudodifferential operators. Keeping this in mind, we will keep the following discussion brief and focus on classicality.

Proof of Proposition
B.4

Simple manifolds are diffeomorphic to a compact ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so without loss of generality we assume that
M=\overline{U}
, where
U=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|<1\}
, with metric
g=(g_{ij})
given in terms of Euclidean coordinates. By
[
27
, Lemma 8.1.10]
, the Schwartz kernel of
N^{g}_{0}
equals

N^{g}_{0}(x,y)=\frac{1}{d_{g}(x,y)^{d-1}}\times 2\,J_{g}(x,y)\sqrt{g(y)},\quad(x,y)\in U\times U,

where
d_{g}
is the geodesic distance and
J_{g}
is a Jacobian factor. They depend continuously on the metric in the sense that the following map is continuous:

(B.4)

g\mapsto(d_{g}^{2},J_{g}),\quad\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}\rightarrow C^{\infty}(U\times U)^{2}

Proving this fact requires revisiting some fairly standard constructions in Riemannian geometry—this tedious but elementary exercise is omitted. Further:

Lemma B.5
.

For every
g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
there exists a smooth function
G^{g}\colon U\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, taking values in symmetric matrices, such that:

(i)

G^{g}(x,x)=g(x)
for
x\in U
;

(ii)

d_{g}(x,y)^{2}=(x-y)^{j}G^{g}_{jk}(x,y)(x-y)^{k}
for
(x,y)\in U\times U
;

(iii)

g\mapsto G^{g},

\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}\rightarrow C^{\infty}(U\times U,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
is continuous.

Proof.

Let
H_{ij}^{g}(x,y)=\partial_{y_{i}}\partial_{y_{j}}d^{2}_{g}(x,y)
, then Taylor expanding
d_{g}^{2}(x,\cdot)
about
y=x
yields

d_{g}^{2}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)}{2}H^{g}_{ij}(x,x+t(y-x))dt\cdot(x-y)^{i}(x-y)^{j}

and defining
G^{g}_{ij}
as this integral expression, the continuity claim (iii) follows directly from (
B.4
). The other two properties are well known (see e.g.
[
27
, Lemma 8.1.12]
).
∎

We now proceed with the proof that
N^{g}_{0}\in\Psi^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}(U)
depends continuously on
g\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
, using the characterisation of the topology in (
B.2
).
Clearly, the kernel
N^{g}_{0}
is smooth away from the diagonal and due to (
B.4
) the map

g\mapsto N^{g}_{0}|_{U\times U\backslash\Delta_{U}},\quad\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}\rightarrow C^{\infty}(U\times U\backslash\Delta_{U})

is continuous. Next, if
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
then following
[
27
, Chapter 8.1]
,

N^{g}_{0,\varphi}(x,y):=\varphi(x)N^{g}_{0}(x,y)\varphi(y)=\Op(a^{g}_{\varphi}),\quad a_{\varphi}^{g}(x,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{iz\xi}N^{g}_{0,\varphi}(x,x-z)dz.

As the integrand in the definition of
a^{g}_{\varphi}
is supported in the compact set
|z|\leq 2
and depends continuously on the metric, one can derive continuity of the following map

g\mapsto a^{g}_{\varphi},\quad\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Classicality is the assertion that the symbol at infinity, defined by

\hat{a}_{\varphi}^{g}(x,\omega,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{e^{iz\omega}q_{\varphi}^{g}(x,x-hz)dz}{[z^{i}G_{ij}(x,x-hz)z^{j}]^{\frac{d-1}{2}}},\quad q_{\varphi}^{g}(x,y)=2J_{g}(x,y)\varphi(x)\sqrt{g(y)}\varphi(y),

is smooth up to
h=0
. This, however, together with the continuity of the map

g\mapsto\hat{a}_{\varphi}^{g},\quad\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{s}}\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[0,\infty)),

follows from Lemma
B.3
. This completes the proof of Proposition
B.4
. ∎
