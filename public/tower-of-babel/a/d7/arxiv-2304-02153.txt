Real moments of the logarithmic derivative of characteristic polynomials in random matrix ensembles
Fan Ge

Abstract
. We prove asymptotics for real moments of the logarithmic derivative of characteristic polynomials evaluated at 1 − a N 1-\frac{a}{N} in unitary, even orthogonal, and symplectic ensembles, where a > 0 a>0 and a = o ⁡ ( 1 ) a=o(1) as the size N N of the matrix goes to infinity. Previously, such asymptotics were known only for integer moments (in the unitary ensemble by the work of Bailey, Bettin, Blower, Conrey, Prokhorov, Rubinstein and Snaith [ BBB+19 ] , and in orthogonal and symplectic ensembles by the work of Alvarez and Snaith [ AS20 ] ), except that in the odd orthogonal ensemble real moments asymptotics were obtained by Alvarez, Bousseyroux and Snaith [ ABS23 ] . Our proof is new and does not make use of the known integer moments results in [ BBB+19 ] and [ AS20 ] , and is different from the method in [ ABS23 ] for the odd orthogonal ensemble.



1.
Introduction

Zeros of the Riemann zeta-function have been of central importance in number theory. One way to investigate zeta zeros is to study the logarithmic derivative
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
, which encodes information of zeta zeros via the Hadamard factorization formula. This brings considerable interest to the study of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
. Besides its relation to zeta zeros, the logarithmic derivative function
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
also offers connections between the zeros and the critical points of zeta; see for instance
[
LM74
,
Sou98
,
Zha01
,
GY07
,
Ki08
,
FK12
,
Rad14
,
Ge17b
,
Ge17a
]
in which the logarithmic derivative plays a key role in the study of critical points. In addition,
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
has intimate connections to the prime numbers. Indeed, Riemann’s original plan for proving the Prime Number Theorem makes use of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
in a crucial way. Futhermore, the mean values of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
are closely related to primes in short intervals; see
[
Sel43
,
GGM01
]
.

As is often the case, one seeks for simple quantities that can more or less represent important properties of the function itself, and in the theory of the Riemann zeta-function, mean values or moments often serve such purposes. To this end, let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{K}(\sigma,T)=\int_{T}^{2T}\left|\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}\left(\sigma+it\right)\right|^{2K}dt.

It turns out that the range of
\sigma
has a crucial impact on the bahavior of
\mathcal{I}_{K}(\sigma,T)
. We separate the range of
\sigma
into the following four cases. (Note that the average gap between zeta zeros near height
T
is of order
1/\log T
.)

•

The
macroscopic
range:
\sigma_{0}\leq\sigma\leq 1
for some constant
\sigma_{0}>1/2
.

•

The
mesoscopic
range:
\sigma=\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{a}{\log T}
, where
a=o(\log T)
and
a\to\infty
as
T\to\infty
.

•

The
microscopic
range:
\sigma=\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{a}{\log T}
, where
a
is of constant size.

•

The
nanoscopic
range:
\sigma=\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{a}{\log T}
, where
a\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.

An important and well-known formula of Selberg
[
Sel46
]
expresses
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
in terms of a prime sum plus a typically controllable error, provided that
\sigma
is not too close to the critical line. In the macroscopic and mesoscopic range, it follows from Selberg’s formula that
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
can be approximated by a short prime sum for most
t
. If we assume the Riemann Hypothesis (RH) then such approximation holds for all
t
, and as a result, the
2K
-th moment
\mathcal{I}_{K}
of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
can be approximated by the
2K
-th moment of the prime sum, which can be computed for positive integers
K
using the mean value theorem of Montgomery and Vaughan
[
MV74
]
. Moreover, in the mesoscopic range the distribution of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
obeys a central limit theorem; see Lester
[
Les14
]
and Guo
[
Guo96
]
.

It seems that in the macroscopic and mesoscopic range, the behavior of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
is not much affected by the vertical distribution of zeta zeros, and thus it reflects less such information of them. The situation changes dramatically when we move closer to the critical line, that is, to the microscopic range. In this range, the behavior of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
is largely affected by the distribution of zeta zeros. In fact, Goldston, Gonek, and Montgomery
[
GGM01
]
showed that an asymptotic estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
in this range is
equivalent
to Montgomery’s Pair Correlation Conjecture. See also
[
Sel43
,
GM87
,
Gol88
]
for connections to primes in short intervals. Therefore, obtaining asymptotic behavior for moments of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
in the microscopic range is highly important, but we also expect it to be extremely difficult. Indeed, it is also known that
\mathcal{I}_{K}
has close connections to other important properties of zeta and primes (see Farmer, Gonek, Lee and Lester
[
FGLL13
]
for a connection to higher correlation functions of zeta zeros, Baluyot
[
Bal16
]
for a discussion of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
under the Alternative Hypothesis, and Ki
[
Ki08
]
for an estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
under an assumption on zeros of
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
, as well as
[
Sou98
,
Zha01
,
Ge17a
]
for implication of Ki’s assumption to small gaps between zeta zeros), yet obtaining an unconditional asymptotic estimate for
\mathcal{I}_{K}
in the microscopic range seems to be far out of reach. In fact, even assuming knowledge on correlation functions of zeta zeros, it is still not clear how to obtain such asymptotic estimates for a general integer
K
; see
[
FGLL13
]
for some relevant discussion.

The extent to which the behavior of
\mathcal{I}_{K}
reflects the distribution of typical sizes of zero gaps fades down when we move even closer to the critical line, namely, to the nanoscopic range. The first result illustrating such type of phenomena is a result of Goldston et al
[
GGM01
]
who showed that an asymptotic for
\mathcal{I}_{1}
is equivalent to the Essential Simplicity hypothesis, which roughly speaking asserts that there are not too many small gaps between zeta zeros. Therefore, the second moment of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
is only capable of recognizing certain information of very small zero gaps, rather than their typical sizes. This result was generalized by the author
[
Ge23
]
to all even integer moments, in which we established asymptotic estimates for
\mathcal{I}_{K}
for all integers
K
conditional on RH and a so-called
2K
-tuple Essential Simplicity hypothesis. This result also provides a conditional proof of a conjecture of Bailey, Bettin, Blower, Conrey, Prokhorov, Rubinstein and Snaith
[
BBB+19
]
. Thus, in the nanoscopic range, on one hand the quantities
\mathcal{I}_{K}
capture only partial information on vertical gaps between zeros, but on the other hand the asymptotics for
\mathcal{I}_{K}
become available under reasonable assumptions.

The above mentioned conjecture of Bailey et al was motivated by their investigation of analogous problems in the setting of random unitary matrices (also called the Circular Unitary Ensemble, or CUE for short). It is widely believed that the characteristic polynomials
P
in the unitary ensemble
U(N)
model the Riemann zeta-function regarding zero statistics, value distribution, and more (for example, see
[
Mon73
]
and
[
KS00
]
). In particular, it is expected that the logarithmic derivative
P^{\prime}/P
of the characteristic polynomials models
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
and is therefore also important and interesting. What is good about CUE is that, unlike zeta, the distribution of zeros of
P
is known, and thus, it is often hopeful to obtain unconditional results whose analogues are formidable in the zeta case. Corresponding to the moments of
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta
, let us define for
a\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{K}=\mathcal{J}_{K}(a,N)=\int_{U(N)}\left|\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}\Big(1-\frac{a}{N}\Big)\right|^{2K},

where the integral is taken with respect to the Haar measure on
U(N)
. Note that in the unitary ensemble the distribution of eigenvalues is invariant under rotation, and therefore, the integral remains unchanged if we introduce an extra averaging over the circle centered at the origin with radius
1-a/N
, which would look more directly like an analogue of
\mathcal{I}_{K}
in the zeta case. The four ranges in this context are as follows.

•

The
macroscopic
range:
0<\frac{a}{N}<1
is of constant size.

•

The
mesoscopic
range:
a=o(N)
and
a\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
.

•

The
microscopic
range:
a
is of constant size.

•

The
nanoscopic
range:
a\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.

An important set of closed form formulas, known as the ratios theorems (see
[
CS08
,
CFZ08
]
), is useful for evaluating integrals like
\mathcal{J}_{K}
. Indeed, in the macroscopic and mesoscopic ranges the leading term of
\mathcal{J}_{K}
for positive integers
K
may be obtained by a direct application of the ratios formula. Moreover, like zeta, in the mesoscopic range we have a central limit theorem for
P^{\prime}/P
as proved in
[
Ge24
]
.
The microsopic range is again the most difficult to deal with: while we have the powerful ratios theorems, deriving asymptotic estimates in the microscopic range for
\mathcal{J}_{K}
from the ratios theorems is not easy. In fact, the situation becomes significantly more complicated as
K
grows, and it is still an unsolved problem how to obtain asymptotics for
\mathcal{J}_{K}
for a general
K\in\mathbb{N}
. See
[
BBB+19
,
FGLL13
]
for some relevant discussion. Now let us move closer to the unit circle and consider the nanoscopic range. In this range, although we may lose finer information of typical zero gaps (as suggested by the zeta case), it becomes possible to obtain unconditional asymptotics for
\mathcal{J}_{K}
. Indeed, in
[
BBB+19
]
Bailey et al proved that if
K
is a positive integer and
a>0
with
a\to 0
as
N\to\infty
, then

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{K}\sim\dfrac{N^{2K}}{(2a)^{2K-1}}\cdot\binom{2K-2}{K-1}

as
N\to\infty
. Based on this, they also conjectured a similar estimate for
\mathcal{I}_{K}
in the zeta case, and this is the aforementioned conjecture of theirs which was studied in
[
Ge23
]
.

The conjectural connection between zeta and the unitary ensemble was developed further by Katz and Sarnak
[
KS99
]
into a more general philosophy that families of
L
-functions can be modelled by random matrices of certain symmetry types. This brings considerable interest to the study of orthogonal and symplectic ensembles as well. In
[
AS20
]
Alvarez and Snaith proved analogous results of (
1
) for orthogonal and symplectic random matrices. More precisely, for even orthogonal ensemble
SO(2N)
their result is

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{SO(2N)}\left(\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}\Big(1-\frac{a}{N}\Big)\right)^{K}\sim(-1)^{K}\dfrac{2N^{K}}{a^{2K-1}}\cdot\dfrac{(2K-3)!!}{(K-1)!}

for integers
K\geq 2
,
while for odd orthogonal ensemble
SO(2N+1)
they showed that

(3)

\displaystyle\int_{SO(2N+1)}\left(\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}\Big(1-\frac{a}{N}\Big)\right)^{K}=(-1)^{K}\left[\left(\frac{N}{a}\right)^{K}-\frac{N^{K}}{a^{K-1}}K\right]+O\left(\frac{N^{K-1}}{a^{K-1}}+\frac{N^{K}}{a^{K-2}}\right)\quad

for integers
K\geq 1
.
For symplectic ensemble
USp(2N)
their result is

(4)

\displaystyle\int_{USp(2N)}\left(\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}\Big(1-\frac{a}{N}\Big)\right)^{K}\sim(-1)^{K}\frac{2}{3}\dfrac{N^{K}}{a^{K-3}}\cdot\dfrac{(2K-5)!!}{(K-1)!}

for integers
K\geq 4
.
We remark that in their work Alvarez and Snaith also obtained asymptotic results for
K=1
in
SO(2N)
and for
K=1,2,3
in
USp(2N)
, but these results have different shapes from the above.

It is desirable to extend the above asymptotics to real moments (see the introduction of
[
ABS23
]
for some discussion on this matter). The method used in proving (
1
), (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
) in
[
BBB+19
,
AS20
]
makes use of ratios theorems to transform the integral, and therefore is capable of treating integer moments but does not generalize to real moments directly.
Very recently, Alvarez, Bousseyroux and Snaith
[
ABS23
]
extended the corresponding result for the odd orthogonal ensemble to real moments. Precisely, they proved that (
3
) is true for all real
K>0
. The reason that they can treat real moments for the odd orthogonal ensemble is that in
SO(2N+1)
all matrices have a fixed eigenvalue at
1
. It turns out that this fixed eigenvalue at
1
gives the main contribution in moments, and they are able to control the smaller order terms by making use of the integer moments result (
3
). Note that in other ensembles we do not have any fixed eigenvalues, and as a result, the method in
[
ABS23
]
does not work directly for unitary, even orthogonal, or symplectic ensembles.

The purpose of this paper is to prove real moments asymptotics for unitary, even orthogonal, and symplectic ensembles, as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K>1
be a real number, and
a>0
with
a\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{U(N)}\left|\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}\Big(1-\frac{a}{N}\Big)\right|^{K}

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{N}{2\pi}\cdot\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\dfrac{1}{\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)^{2}+\theta^{2}}\right)^{\frac{K}{2}}d\theta

(5)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{N}{2\pi}\cdot\left(\dfrac{N}{a}\right)^{K-1}\cdot\sqrt{\pi}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{K-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{K}{2}\right)}\ .

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
K>1
be a real number, and
a>0
with
a\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{SO(2N)}\left|\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}\Big(1-\frac{a}{N}\Big)\right|^{K}

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{2N}{\pi}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\dfrac{2\cdot{\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)}}{\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)^{2}+\theta^{2}}\right)^{K}d\theta

(6)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2N}{\pi}\cdot 2^{K}\cdot\left(\dfrac{N}{a}\right)^{K-1}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(K-\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(K\right)}\ .

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
K>3
be a real number, and
a>0
with
a\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{USp(2N)}\left|\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}\Big(1-\frac{a}{N}\Big)\right|^{K}

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{2N^{3}}{3\pi}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\dfrac{2\cdot{\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)}}{\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)^{2}+\theta^{2}}\right)^{K}\cdot\theta^{2}d\theta

(7)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2N^{3}}{3\pi}\cdot 2^{K}\cdot\left(\dfrac{N}{a}\right)^{K-3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(K-\frac{3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(K\right)}\ .

Remark 1
.

In Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
we consider
|P^{\prime}/P|^{K}
rather than
(P^{\prime}/P)^{K}
as in (
2
) and (
4
).
For
K>1
in the case of
SO(2N)
and
K>3
in the case of
USp(2N)
, the difference between
|P^{\prime}/P|^{K}
and
(P^{\prime}/P)^{K}
is minor. This is because from the proof of the theorems we can see that, for such
K
, the main contributing quantities in the integrals have the same sign. We choose to work with
|P^{\prime}/P|^{K}
because it avoids unnecessary complication caused by complex logarithm.

Remark 2
.

Our proofs are new and do not make use of integer moments results (
1
), (
2
) or (
4
). By evaluating the gamma functions at half integers explicitly, one easily checks that our theorems agree with the known integer moments.

Remark 3
.

Our method is robust for obtaining asymptotics for real moments when the parameter
K
is not too small. The reason we require
K
to be not too small is that, roughly speaking, the smaller
K
is, the less influential large values of
P^{\prime}/P
are on the
K
-th moment. As our method eventually extracts the large values and uses them to compute moments, there is a threshold for
K
above which we are able to do so. While this approach does not seem to work for
|P^{\prime}/P|^{K}
when
K
is small, the results for
(P^{\prime}/P)^{K}
for small integers
K
are known (see the remark following equation (
4
)).

Remark 4
.

Some ideas in our proofs could be adapted to treat nanoscopic real moments in the zeta case, extending the integer moments results in
[
Ge23
]
. The
2K
-tuple essential simplicity hypothesis may be replaced by a similar hypothesis for
2\lceil K\rceil
-tuples. Such hypotheses are used to control error terms in the context of zeta. We also note that the method in
[
Ge23
]
for zeta may be adapted to treat integer moments in the case of the unitary ensemble, by introducing an additional average over the unit circle (and this is because the unitary ensemble is rotation-invariant). However, this approach does not work for orthogonal or symplectic ensembles.

We shall give a complete proof for the unitary case, and indicate necessary changes in other ensembles.

2.
Unitary ensemble

Recall that
a>0
and
a=o(1)
as
N\to\infty
. Let

\displaystyle z_{0}=1-\frac{1}{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad z=1-\frac{a}{N}.

We will need to choose a suitable parameter
c
such that

(8)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=o(1),

(9)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=o(c),

(10)

\displaystyle\text{and }\quad c^{-K}

\displaystyle=o(a^{1-K}).

For example, we may take

\displaystyle c=a^{\frac{K-1}{2K}}.

Note that these restrictions of
c
require
K>1
.

Let
z_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}}
be the eigenvalues and write

\displaystyle\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}(z)=\sum_{|\theta_{j}|<\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{z-z_{j}}+X_{1}+X_{2}-X_{3},

where

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}(z_{0})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{z_{0}-z_{j}},

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\theta_{j}|\geq\frac{c}{N}}\left(\frac{1}{z-z_{j}}-\frac{1}{z_{0}-z_{j}}\right),

\displaystyle X_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\theta_{j}|<\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{z_{0}-z_{j}}.

We require the following three lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Then

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|X_{1}|^{2\ell}\ll_{\ell}N^{2\ell}.

This is Propostion 2.1 in
[
Ge24
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have

\displaystyle X_{2}\ll\frac{1}{c}\cdot(N+|X_{1}|)

Proof
. This is obtained implicitly in the proof of Proposition 2.2 in
[
Ge24
]
. We sketch the argument here. First we write

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\theta_{j}|\geq\frac{c}{N}}\left(\frac{1}{z-z_{j}}-\frac{1}{z_{0}-z_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\theta_{j}|\geq\frac{c}{N}}\frac{z_{0}-z}{(z-z_{j})(z_{0}-z_{j})}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|\theta_{j}|\geq\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{|z-z_{j}|\cdot|z_{0}-z_{j}|}.

Now by definitions of
z
and
z_{0}
and the assumption
|\theta_{j}|\geq c/N
we have

\displaystyle c|z_{0}-z_{j}|\ll|z-z_{j}|.

It follows that

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{cN}\sum_{|\theta_{j}|\geq\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{|z_{0}-z_{j}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{cN}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|z_{0}-z_{j}|^{2}}.

By a change of variable, transforming the unit circle into the imaginary axis, one can show that (see equation (8) in
[
Ge24
]
)

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|z_{0}-z_{j}|^{2}}\ll N(N+|X_{1}|).

This gives

\displaystyle X_{2}\ll\frac{1}{c}\cdot(N+|X_{1}|).

∎

Lemma 2.3
.

We have

\displaystyle X_{3}\ll N+|X_{1}|.

This is obtained in the proof of Proposition 2.3 in
[
Ge24
]
.

From the above three lemmas we can write

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{P^{\prime}}{P}(z)=M+E,

where the main term

(14)

\displaystyle M=\sum_{|\theta_{j}|<\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{z-z_{j}}

and the error term

(15)

\displaystyle E=X_{1}+X_{2}-X_{3}\ll\frac{1}{c}\cdot(N+|X_{1}|).

From (
15
) and (
11
) it is clear that for every
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
we have

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|E|^{2\ell}\ll_{\ell}\left(\frac{N}{c}\right)^{2\ell}.

A simple application of Jensen’s (or Hölder’s) inequality extends this to all real moments, as follows.

Proposition 2.4
.

For all
K>0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|E|^{K}\ll_{K}\left(\frac{N}{c}\right)^{K}.

We will also prove a moment estimate for the main term
M
, as follows.

Proposition 2.5
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|M|^{K}

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{N}{2\pi}\cdot\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\dfrac{1}{\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)^{2}+\theta^{2}}\right)^{\frac{K}{2}}d\theta

\displaystyle=\dfrac{N}{2\pi}\cdot\left(\dfrac{N}{a}\right)^{K-1}\cdot\sqrt{\pi}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{K-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{K}{2}\right)}.

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.
We let

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}=\{U\in U(N):U\text{ has no eigenvalues in }|\theta|<c/N\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}=\{U\in U(N):U\text{ has exactly $1$ eigenvalue in }|\theta|<c/N\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}=\{U\in U(N):U\text{ has at least $2$ eigenvalues in }|\theta|<c/N\},

and so,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|M|^{=}\left(\int_{\mathcal{T}_{0}}+\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}+\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}\right)\left|\sum_{|\theta_{j}|<\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{z-z_{j}}\right|^{K}dU.

The
\mathcal{T}_{0}
integral is trivial
0
since the integrand is
0
.

Consider the
\mathcal{T}_{1}
integral. By definition

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{z-e^{i\theta}}\right|^{K}dU,

where
e^{i\theta}
is the unique eigenvalue of
U
in the region
|\theta|<c/N
. We may rewrite this as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}=\int_{-c/N}^{c/N}\frac{1}{|z-e^{i\theta}|^{K}}\cdot f(\theta)\cdot P(\theta)d\theta,

where

\displaystyle f(\theta)=f_{U(N)}(\theta):=\text{ the likelihood that }\theta\text{ is an eigenangle of some }U\in U(N)

and

\displaystyle P(\theta):=\Pr\left(\text{conditional on }\theta\text{ is an eigenangle, there is exactly one eigenangle in }\left[\dfrac{-c}{N},\dfrac{c}{N}\right]\right).

Here the notation
\Pr(\cdot)
means probability.
From the standard
1
-level density estimates we know that

(17)

\displaystyle f(\theta)=\frac{N}{2\pi}.

Next, we show

(18)

\displaystyle P(\theta)=1+o(1)

for
\theta\in[-c/N,c/N]
.
This is intuitively true since the condition (
8
)
c=o(1)
implies that it is not likely to see two or more eigenvalues in
[-c/N,c/N]
. To argue rigorously, we shall show that for
|\theta|<c/N
and for every positive integer
m
,

\displaystyle P_{m}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\Pr(\text{exactly }m\text{ eigenangles in }[-c/N,c/N],\text{ and one in }[\theta,\theta+d\theta])

(19)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N}{2\pi}d\theta\cdot m\left(\frac{c}{\pi}\right)^{m-1}.

To prove this, recall the
m
-level density formula for
U(N)
is (see
[
Con05
]
)

\displaystyle\int_{U(N)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subset\{1,...,N\}\\
J=\{j_{1},...,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}F(\theta_{j_{1}},...,\theta_{j_{n}})dU

(20)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}n!}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{n}}F(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})\det_{n\times n}S_{N}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n},

where

\displaystyle S_{N}(x)=\dfrac{\sin\frac{Nx}{2}}{\sin\frac{x}{2}}.

Apply this with

F=\mathbbm{1}_{[\theta,\theta+d\theta]}\times\mathbbm{1}_{[-c/N,c/N]}^{m-1}+\mathbbm{1}_{[-c/N,c/N]}\times\mathbbm{1}_{[\theta,\theta+d\theta]}\times\mathbbm{1}_{[-c/N,c/N]}^{m-2}+\cdots+\mathbbm{1}_{[-c/N,c/N]}^{m-1}\times\mathbbm{1}_{[\theta,\theta+d\theta]}

for
|\theta|<c/N
, and (
2
) follows since the left-hand side of (
2
) is at least
P_{m}(\theta)
while the right-hand side of (
2
) is

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N}{2\pi}d\theta\cdot m\left(\frac{c}{\pi}\right)^{m-1},

where the last assertion can be seen by expanding the determinant as a permutation sum. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle P_{2}(\theta)+P_{3}(\theta)+\cdots+P_{N}(\theta)

\displaystyle\ll Nd\theta\cdot\sum_{m\geq 2}mc^{m-1}

\displaystyle\ll(Nd\theta)c,

while

\displaystyle P_{1}(\theta)+P_{2}(\theta)+P_{3}(\theta)+\cdots+P_{N}(\theta)=f(\theta)d\theta=\frac{N}{2\pi}d\theta.

Since
c=o(1)
, it follows that

\displaystyle P_{1}(\theta)\sim f(\theta)d\theta,

and thus, the conditional probability

\displaystyle P(\theta)=\frac{P_{1}(\theta)}{f(\theta)d\theta}\sim 1.

Therefore, with
f(\theta)
and
P(\theta)
estimated, we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\sim\int_{-c/N}^{c/N}\frac{1}{|z-e^{i\theta}|^{K}}\cdot\frac{N}{2\pi}d\theta.

Recall that
z=1-\frac{a}{N}
. Thus,

\displaystyle|z-e^{i\theta}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{a}{N}-\cos\theta\right)^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta

\displaystyle\sim\left(-\frac{a}{N}+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}+O(\theta^{4})\right)^{2}+\theta^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)^{2}+\theta^{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\sim\int_{-c/N}^{c/N}\left(\frac{1}{(a/N)^{2}+\theta^{2}}\right)^{K/2}\cdot\frac{N}{2\pi}d\theta.

By a change of variable and the condition (
9
) that
a=o(c)
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\sim\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(a/N)^{2}+\theta^{2}}\right)^{K/2}\cdot\frac{N}{2\pi}d\theta.

It is now straightforward to compute the above integral and we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\sim\dfrac{N}{2\pi}\cdot\left(\dfrac{N}{a}\right)^{K-1}\cdot\sqrt{\pi}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{K-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{K}{2}\right)}

which gives the leading term in Proposition
2.5
.

It remains to show that

\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}=o\left(\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\right).

A familiar inequality says that for
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}
and
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we have

\displaystyle m\cdot(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m})^{s}\leq(ma_{1})^{s}+\cdots+(ma_{m})^{s}.

This inequality holds essentially because when
s\geq 1
the function
x^{s}
is convex, and one can prove it easily by first proving for
m=2
and then applying two-term averaging repeatedly.
This inequality implies

\displaystyle(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m})^{s}\leq(a_{1}^{s}+\cdots+a_{m}^{s})\cdot m^{s-1}.

Now in the
\mathcal{T}_{2}
integral we first apply the triangle inequality and then the above inequality to the integrand , and we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}\left|\sum_{|\theta_{j}|<\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{z-z_{j}}\right|^{K}dU

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}\left(\sum_{|\theta_{j}|<\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{|z-z_{j}|}\right)^{K}dU

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}\left(\sum_{|\theta_{j}|<\frac{c}{N}}\frac{1}{|z-z_{j}|^{K}}\right)m^{K-1}dU,

where
m=m(U)
is the number of
\theta
’s in
[-c/N,c/N]
for
U
. As we did in the
\mathcal{T}_{1}
integral, we interchange summation in the last integral and arrive that

(21)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}\leq\int_{-c/N}^{c/N}\left(\frac{1}{|z-e^{i\theta}|^{K}}\cdot\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2},\text{ and }\theta\text{ is an eigenangle}}m^{K-1}d_{\theta}U\right)\cdot f(\theta)d\theta,

where
d_{\theta}U
is the conditioning Haar measure on the subset of
U(N)
when
\theta
is an eigenangle. Now recall (
2
) that for
\theta\in[-c/N,c/N]
,

\displaystyle\Pr(\text{exactly }m\text{ eigenangles in }[-c/N,c/N],\text{ and one in }[\theta,\theta+d\theta])\leq\frac{N}{2\pi}d\theta\cdot m\left(\frac{c}{\pi}\right)^{m-1}.

Thus, for
\theta\in[-c/N,c/N]
we have

\displaystyle\Pr(\text{conditional on an eigenangle at }\theta,\text{ there are exactly }m\text{ eigenangles in }[-c/N,c/N])

\displaystyle\quad\ll mc^{m-1}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2},\text{ and }\theta\text{ is an eigenangle}}m^{K-1}d_{\theta}U

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{m=2}^{N}m^{K-1}\cdot\Pr\big(\text{conditional on an eigenangle at }\theta,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{ there are exactly }m\text{ eigenangles in }[-c/N,c/N]\big)

\displaystyle\quad\ll\sum_{m=2}^{N}m^{K}\cdot c^{m-1}

\displaystyle\quad\ll_{K}c=o_{K}(1),

where in the last line we recall again that
c=o(1)
by (
8
).
Plugging this into (
21
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}

\displaystyle=o_{K}(1)\cdot\int_{-c/N}^{c/N}\frac{1}{|z-e^{i\theta}|^{K}}f(\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=o_{K}(1)\cdot\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}.

This finishes the proof of Proposition
2.5
. ∎

Now recall the condition (
10
) that
c^{-K}=o(a^{1-K})
. Thus, by Propositions
2.4
and
2.5
we see that

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|E|^{K}=o\left(\mathbb{E}|M|^{K}\right).

From this we shall deduce the following proposition, which together with Proposition
2.5
gives Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.6
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|M+E|^{K}\sim\mathbb{E}|M|^{K}.

Proof of Proposition
2.6
.
We write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|M+E|^{K}=\int_{|M|>3|E|}|M+E|^{K}dU+\int_{|M|\leq 3|E|}|M+E|^{K}dU.

For the second integral we have

\displaystyle\int_{|M|\leq 3|E|}|M+E|^{K}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|M|\leq 3|E|}(|M|+|E|)^{K}dU

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|M|\leq 3|E|}(4|E|)^{K}dU

\displaystyle\ll_{K}\int_{U(N)}|E|^{K}dU

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}|E|^{K}

\displaystyle=o\left(\mathbb{E}|M|^{K}\right)

by (
22
).

For the first integral we write

\displaystyle A=|M|^{2},\quad B=|E|^{2}+M\overline{E}+\overline{M}E,

and so

\displaystyle\int_{|M|>3|E|}|M+E|^{K}dU

\displaystyle=\int_{|M|>3|E|}(|M|^{2}+|E|^{2}+M\overline{E}+\overline{M}E)^{K/2}dU

\displaystyle=\int_{|M|>3|E|}(A+B)^{K/2}dU

\displaystyle=\int_{|M|>3|E|}|M|^{K}\left(1+\frac{B}{A}\right)^{K/2}dU

\displaystyle=\int_{|M|>3|E|}|M|^{K}\left(1+\frac{K}{2}\frac{B}{A}+O_{K}\left(\frac{B^{2}}{A^{2}}\right)\right)dU,

where in the last line we have used the Taylor expansion with remainder that for real
\alpha
and
|x|<\frac{7}{9}
, say,

\displaystyle(1+x)^{\alpha}=1+\alpha x+O_{\alpha}(x^{2}),

and note that the condition
|M|>3|E|
guarantees
|B/A|<7/9
. Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{|M|>3|E|}|M+E|^{K}dU

\displaystyle=\int_{|M|>3|E|}|M|^{K}dU+O_{K}\left(\int|M|^{K-1}|E|+|M|^{K-2}|E|^{2}dU\right).

As before we have

\displaystyle\int_{|M|\leq 3|E|}|M|^{K}=o\left(\mathbb{E}|M|^{K}\right),

so that

\displaystyle\int_{|M|>3|E|}|M|^{K}dU\sim\mathbb{E}|M|^{K}.

For the
O_{K}
-terms we use Hölder’s inequality together with Propositions
2.4
and
2.5
to obtain again an upper bound
o\left(\mathbb{E}|M|^{K}\right)
. This finishes the proof of Proposition
2.6
. ∎

Orthogonal and Symplectic ensembles

In this section we give a proof sketch for Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. We shall only indicate the differences from the unitary case.

The three lemmas cited from
[
Ge24
]
can be used or easily adapted. For example, Lemma
2.1
(that is, even integer moments bounds for
X_{1}
) is proved by applying a ratios theorem in
[
CS08
]
together with a discretization argument. The analogous results for orthogonal and symplectic ensembles can be deduced by applying the ratios theorems in
[
MS18
]
for these ensembles together with a similar discretization argument as in
[
Ge24
]
.

In
SO(2N)
, eigenvalues appear in conjugate pairs, and we shall only label eigenvalues in the upper half plane, as
z_{1},...,z_{N}
. This explains why the integral in (
1.2
) is from
0
rather than
-\infty
.
Also, in the corresponding summation (
14
) for
M
we group conjugate pairs and so each summand looks like

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z-z_{j}}+\frac{1}{z-\overline{z_{j}}}=2\Re\frac{1}{z-z_{j}},

and if
\theta_{j}<c/N
the above is

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{2\cdot{\left(-\frac{a}{N}+O(\theta^{2})\right)}}{\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)^{2}+\theta_{j}^{2}}.

The
O
-term makes a small contribution toward the integral, and this explains the shape of the integrand in the integral in (
1.2
). The likelihood function
f_{SO(2N)}
can be calculated using
1
-level density estimate for
SO(2N)
and the result is

\displaystyle f_{SO(2N)}\sim\frac{2N}{\pi},

and this should replace (
17
). The
P(\theta)
and
P_{m}(\theta)
terms in (
18
) and (
2
) can be estimated in the same way and the results are similar as the unitary case. The main term in (
1.2
) is of order
N^{K}a^{1-K}
, and so we require
K>1
in order for the parameter
c
to exist.

In
USp(2N)
, eigenvalues again appear in conjugate pairs, and so the integral in (
1.3
) is from
0
.
As in
SO(2N)
, summands in the corresponding summation (
14
) for
M
are

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{2\cdot{\left(-\frac{a}{N}+O(\theta^{2})\right)}}{\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)^{2}+\theta_{j}^{2}}.

if
\theta_{j}<c/N
, and again the
O
-term is negligible since its contribution toward the integral is small. The likelihood function
f_{USp(2N)}
can be calculated using
1
-level density estimate for
USp(2N)
and the result is

\displaystyle f_{USp(2N)}\sim\frac{2N^{3}\theta^{2}}{3\pi}.

The
P_{m}(\theta)
terms can be treated in a similar way (with a bit more care dealing with Taylor expansions) and the bounds look like

\displaystyle N^{3}\theta^{2}d\theta\cdot m(c^{3})^{m-1}.

From this we again have
P(\theta)\sim 1
.
This time the main term in (
1.3
) is of order
N^{K}a^{3-K}
, so we require
K>3
to guarantee the existence of
c
.
