1.
Overview

In this paper we establish the weak (1,1) boundedness of Fourier integral operators with complex phases and of order
-(n-1)/2.
Our approach combines two methods. First, we adapt to our setting the factorisation procedure developed by Tao
[
46
]
in the proof of the weak (1,1) inequality of these operators in the setting of real-valued phase functions, and the other one came from the global parametrisation of the canonical relation associated to the operator with an appropriate complex phase as observed by the second author in
[
39
]
, when extending to Fourier integral operators with complex phases the
H^{1}
-
L^{1}
boundedness result established by Seeger, Sogge and Stein in
[
44
]
. To present our main Theorem
1.7
we are going to introduce some preliminaries. We consider
\mathbb{R}^{n},
with
n\geq 2,
and we denote by

f\mapsto\widehat{f},\,\widehat{f}(\xi):=\smallint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}f(x)dx,\,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

the Fourier transform of an integrable function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

1.1.
Outline

When working with the real methods of the harmonic analysis one can find three substantial categories related to the boundedness properties of operators: maximal averages, singular integrals, and oscillatory integrals. In the latter category, oscillatory integrals provide a necessary tool for exploiting the geometric properties related to the notions of
curvature
and of
orthogonality
. As it was pointed out by Stein in
[
43
]
, this family of objects is not easily classified and appears in multiple forms: Bochner-Riesz operators, variants of pseudo-differential operators and of Fourier multipliers, and in the main object treated here,
Fourier integral operators
.

Fourier integral operators appear as solutions to hyperbolic differential equations and then their boundedness properties have a direct relation with the validity of
a priori estimates
for these equations. As it was shown e.g. in
[
39
,
44
,
46
]
, the geometry and the singularities of the canonical
relation of an operator are reflected in the boundedness properties of operators in
L^{p}
-
spaces. Due to the compatibility of the space
H^{1}
with the Littlewood-Paley theory, see Fefferman and Stein
[
17
]
, and with the complex interpolation of operators, a main step in the proof of the
L^{p}
-boundedness properties of Fourier integral operators (and for other operators arising in harmonic analysis), is the proof of a suitable criterion for its
H^{1}
-
L^{1}
-boundedness which is usually interpolated with its corresponding
L^{2}
-theory. However, as in the case of pseudo-differential operators, once proved the
H^{1}
-
L^{1}
-continuity criterion one expects that the corresponding class of
H^{1}
-
L^{1}
-bounded Fourier integral operators must satisfy the
L^{1}
-
L^{1,\infty}
boundedness property. This last property is called the
weak (1,1) inequality/boundedness
for these operators.

The purpose of this paper is to prove the weak (1,1) boundedness for the class of Fourier integral operators associated to symbols of order
m=-(n-1)/2
and of complex phases (our main result is Theorem
1.7
below). This order is expected from the weak (1,1) inequality for Fourier integral operators with real-valued phase functions proved by Tao in
[
46
]
, where it was applied a ‘second decomposition’ on the symbol of the operator adapted to a suitable family of ellipsoids with a smooth variation of eccentricity and orientation that avoid Kakeya-type covering lemmas, see Theorem
1.2
. This approach was combined with a factorisation strategy. The order
m=-(n-1)/2
also provides bounded Fourier integral operators with real-valued phase functions from the Hardy space
H^{1}
into
L^{1}
, see Segger, Sogge, and Stein
[
44
]
, as well as in the case of complex phase functions as proved by the second author in
[
39
]
, see Theorems
1.3
and
1.6
, respectively.

The extension of the theory of Fourier integral operators due to Hörmander and Duistermaat to the case of complex-valued phase functions was systematically developed by Melin and Sjöstrand in
[
30
]
motivated by the problem of the construction of parametrices, or fundamental solutions for operators of principal type with non-real principal symbols, see for instance
[
31
]
.

The algebraic properties for the theory of Fourier integral operators with complex phases are well developed but their regularity has
not been much studied, with the exception, e.g. of
[
39
]
. In some sense, the use of complex phases provides a more natural
approach to Fourier integral operators. Indeed, when working with the complex phase approach, the geometric obstructions of the global theory with real phase functions can be avoided and it is a remarkable fact that every Fourier integral operator with a real phase can be globally parametrized
by a single complex phase, see e.g.
[
39
]
. We also refer to Laptev, Safarov, and Vassiliev
[
29
]
for this construction.

1.2.
Fourier integral operators with real-phase functions

In order to present our main result, we record the definition of Fourier integral operators based on the real-phase function approach. So, we present the following simplified version which is suitable for our purposes.

Definition 1.1
.

A Fourier integral operator
T
of order
m\in\mathbb{R}
and with real phase function
\Phi,
is a continuous linear operator
T:C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of the form

(1.1)

Tf(x)=\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\Phi(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi,

where
\widehat{f}
is the Fourier transform of
f.
The symbol
a:=a(x,\xi)
is of order
m\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
that is
a
satisfies the symbol inequalities

(1.2)

\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,\forall\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,\,|\partial_{x}^{\beta}\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}a(x,\xi)|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}(1+|\xi|)^{m-|\alpha|}.

The symbol
a
has compact support in
x.
We assume that in an open neighbourhood
V
of
\pi_{1}(\textnormal{supp}(a))=\{x:(x,\xi)\in\textnormal{supp}(a)\},
(with
\overline{V}
being a compact set) the real-valued phase function
\Phi
is homogeneous of degree
1
in
\xi\neq 0,
smooth in
(x,\xi)\in{V}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}),
and satisfies the non-degeneracy condition

(1.3)

\forall(x,\xi)\in\overline{V}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}),\,\textnormal{det}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi(x,\xi)}{\partial{x_{i}}\partial{\xi_{j}}}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\neq 0.

The following result provides the weak(1,1) inequality for a Fourier integral operator of order
m=-(n-1)/2,
or equivalently its boundedness from
L^{1}
into (the weak
L^{1}
-space)
L^{1,\infty}.

Theorem 1.2

(Tao
[
46
]
, 2004)
.

Let
T
be a Fourier integral operator of order
m=-(n-1)/2
with a real phase function. Then
T
is of weak
(1,1)
type.

The corresponding
L^{p}
-theory for these operators as developed in
[
44
]
, can be summarised as follows.

Theorem 1.3

(Seeger, Sogge and Stein
[
44
]
, 1991)
.

Let
1<p<\infty.
Let
T
be a Fourier integral operator of order
m=-(n-1)|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}|,
with a real phase function. Then
T
is bounded from
L^{p}
to
L^{p}.
When
p=1,
then
T
is bounded from
H^{1}
to
L^{1}.

The case
p=2
in Theorem
1.3
was proved by Duistermaat and Hörmander
[
15
]
1
1

1

See also Èskin
[
16
]
.
using the
T^{*}T
-method and extended independently to the setting of Fourier integral operators with complex phases by Hörmander in
[
25
]
and by Melin and Sjöstrand in
[
31
]
. One of the fundamental differences when working on the boundedness of operators in the setting of complex-valued phase functions, compared with the real-valued setting is that one has to deal with the boundedness properties of pseudo-differential operators in the class
S^{0}_{1/2,1/2}.
One reason for this is the analysis of the oscillatory term
e^{\pm(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi)},
see Melin and Sjöstrand

1.3.
Fourier integral operators with complex-phase functions

Let
X
and
Y
be smooth manifolds of dimension
n
and let us denote by
\widetilde{T^{*}(X\times Y)\setminus 0}
the almost analytic continuation of
T^{*}(X\times Y)\setminus 0.
For this notion see Hörmander
[
22
]
and Nirenberg
[
34
]
, see also Subsection
2.2
. Let

C\equiv C_{\Phi}=\{(x,d_{x}\Phi,y,d_{y}\Phi):d_{\theta}\Phi=0\}\subset\widetilde{T^{*}(X\times Y)\setminus 0}

be a smooth positive homogeneous canonical relation parametrised by a phase function of
positive type
, namely, it satisfies
\textnormal{Im}(\Phi)\geq 0,
see Subsections
2.2
and
2.4
for details.

The class
I^{\mu}_{\rho}(X,Y;C)
of Fourier integral operators is determined modulo
C^{\infty}
by those integral operators
T
that locally have an integral kernel of the form

(1.4)

\displaystyle A(x,y)=\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{i\Phi(x,y,\theta)}a(x,y,\theta)d\theta,

where
a(x,y,\theta)
is a symbol of order
\mu+\frac{N-n}{2}.

By the equivalence-of-phase-function theorem, we can always microlocally assume that
N=n,
and that the symbol
a
satisfies the Kohn-Nirenberg type estimates

(1.5)

|\partial_{x,y}^{\beta}\partial_{\theta}^{\alpha}a(x,y,\theta)|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}(1+|\theta|)^{\mu-|\alpha|},

locally uniformly in
(x,y).
According to Lemma 2.1 of
[
31
]
, one can always re-write the kernel in (
1.4
) as

(1.6)

\displaystyle A(x,y)=\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{2\pi i(\Phi(x,\theta)-y\cdot\theta)}a(x,y,\theta)d\theta,

where the function
\Phi
is of positive type. Note that the amplitude
a
in (
1.6
) is probably different from the one in (
1.4
), however, we keep the same notation for simplicity. To be able to use the Fourier inversion formula we will assume that
a(x,y,\theta)
is independent of
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
and compactly supported in
x.
So, we will work with symbols instead of amplitudes, in order to recover the simplified form

(1.7)

Tf(x)=\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\Phi(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi.

To analyse the boundedness of a Fourier integral operator associated to the phase
\Phi
we require the following
local graph condition
.

Assumption 1.4
.

There exists
\tau\in\mathbb{C},
such that

(1.8)

\Phi_{\tau}:=\textnormal{Re}(\Phi)+\tau\textnormal{Im}(\Phi)\textnormal{ defines }\textnormal{ a }\textnormal{ local }\textnormal{ graph }\textnormal{ in }T^{*}(\widetilde{X\times Y})\setminus 0.

Locally, this means that

(1.9)

\textnormal{det}\partial_{x}\partial_{\xi}(\textnormal{Re}(\Phi(x,\xi))+\tau\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi)))\neq 0,

on the support of the symbol
a,
for all
\xi\neq 0.

Remark 1.5
.

According to Lemma 1.8.1 of
[
39
]
, the local graph condition (
1.8
) in Assumption
1.4
is equivalent to the same assumption for almost every
\tau\in\mathbb{C}.
Moreover, the determinant in (
1.9
) is a polynomial function in
\tau
which means that if (
1.8
) holds for some
\tau\in\mathbb{C},
then it holds for all but finitely many
\tau\in\mathbb{C}.
To summarise this discussion, Assumption
1.4
is equivalent to the existence of the same condition for some, and then for almost every
\tau\in\mathbb{C}.

Before presenting our main result we record the
L^{p}
-theory of Fourier integral operators with complex phases as developed by the second author in
[
39
, Theorems 1.7.1 and 1.7.2]
including the endpoint case
p=1.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
T
be a Fourier integral operator of order
\mu=-(n-1)|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}|
associated with a canonical relation parametrised by a complex phase
\Phi
of positive type and satisfying the local graph condition (
1.8
). If
p=1
then
T
is bounded from
H^{1}_{comp}
to
L^{1}_{loc},
and for
1<p<\infty,

T
is bounded from
L^{p}_{comp}
to
L^{p}_{loc}.

1.4.
Main result

The following is the main result of this paper. We will assume that the complex phase function is of
positive type
, in such a way that
\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))>0,
when
|\xi|\neq 0.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
T
be a Fourier integral operator of order
\mu=-(n-1)/2
associated with a canonical relation parametrised by a complex phase
\Phi
of positive type in such a way that
\textnormal{Im}[\Phi(x,\xi)]>0
when
|\xi|\neq 0,
and satisfying the local graph condition (
1.8
). Then
T
is of weak
(1,1)
type.

Remark 1.8
.

We observe that the condition on the phase function in Theorem
1.7
, that
\textnormal{Im}[\Phi(x,\xi)]>0
when
|\xi|\neq 0,
is not restrictive, see Laptev, Safarov, and Vassiliev
[
29
]
.

Below we briefly discuss some aspects of the main differences between the regularity properties of Fourier integral operators in the setting of complex-valued phase functions in comparison with the setting of real-valued phases.

Remark 1.9
.

There is a simple argument to show that our main Theorem
1.7
does not follow from its real counterpart in Theorem
1.2
. Indeed, any Fourier integral operator
T\in I^{\mu}(X,Y;C)
in the form

(1.10)

Tf(x)=\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(\textnormal{Re}(\Phi(x,\xi))+\tau\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi,

with real
\tau\neq 0
and with real phase function

\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi):=\textnormal{Re}(\Phi(x,\xi))+\tau\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))

has the symbol

a_{\tau}(x,\xi)=a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}

in the class
S^{\mu}_{1/2,1/2}
if
a\in S^{\mu},
see Melin and Sjöstrand
[
31
, Page 394]
. Note that
T
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),
with
1<p<\infty
if, for example,

\mu\leq-\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)|1/p-1/2|.

However when
p\rightarrow 1^{+}
this order is not good enough as the one in Theorem
1.2
, or compared with the order
-(n-1)/2
for the
H^{1}
-
L^{1}
-boundedness proved by Seeger, Sogge and Stein in
[
44
]
for real phase functions or even as proved in
[
39
]
for complex phase functions.
In order to improve this order in Theorem
1.7
we need to avoid the reduction to a real phase function, making some strategic modifications to the argument given in Tao
[
46
]
, in particular taking into account that we have to deal with dyadic components of the symbol
a_{\tau}(x,\xi)=a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}.

Remark 1.10
.

We remark that in Tao
[
46
]
it has been introduced a
factorisation approach
for the proof of the weak (1,1) inequality of Fourier integral operators, see Section
4
for details. When
T
has a real-valued phase function, according to such an approach a portion of the operator, the non-degenerate part
T_{\textnormal{non-deg}},
is factorised as
T_{\textnormal{non-deg}}=SA
modulo an error operator
E=T_{\textnormal{non-deg}}-SA.
Here,
S
is a pseudo-differential operator of order zero and its symbol is essentially
|\xi_{n}|^{(n-1)/2}a(x,\xi),
when
|\xi_{n}|\gg 1,
where
a
is the symbol of
T.
Note that the weak (1,1) boundedness of
S
is a consequence of the Calderón-Zygmund theory.
However, compared with the case of Fourier integral operators with complex phases, one has to redefine the operator
S,
because in this setting
|\xi_{n}|^{(n-1)/2}a_{\tau}(x,\xi)\in S^{0}_{1/2,1/2},
for
|\xi_{n}|\gg 1.
Nevertheless, according to the
L^{1}
-theory for the Hörmander classes
S^{m}_{1/2,1/2},

m\in\mathbb{R},
see Álvarez and Hounie
[
1
]
, a pseudo-differential operator with a symbol in these classes is of weak (1,1) type when
m\leq-n/4.
In order to avoid the loss of regularity caused by the order
-n/4
, we will keep the symbol of the operator
a
in the pseudo-differential part
S,
and we will absorb the oscillating term
e_{\Phi}=e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}
into the average operator
A.
Essentially, this term will modify the argument in
[
46
]
, where the
L^{1}
-boundedness of the averaging operator
A
depends on the uniformly
L^{1}
-boundedness of the Littlewood-Paley operators, but even, perturbating this part with the oscillating symbol
e_{\Phi}
we are able to preserve the
L^{1}
-boundedness of
A.
In order to follow this strategy, we will make a fundamental reduction of our Theorem
1.7
to the case of phase functions of
strictly positive type
in Section
3
, see Theorem
3.1
. We present the proof of Theorem
1.7
in Section
3
. The authors thank Terence Tao for suggesting the factorisation approach used in this manuscript.

In the next Section
2
, we present the preliminaries and the notation used in this work. We will continue the discussion about the regularity properties of Fourier integral operator in Section
3
, its main result is Theorem
3.1
and its proof is presented in Section
4
. We finish this manuscript with Section
5
where we present an application of our main theorem and also we provide a bibliographic discussion.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
M,

X
and
Y
be paracompact smooth real manifolds. We assume that
X
and
Y
are manifolds of dimension
n.
Here, we can take
M=X,Y
or
M=X\times Y.
When
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

L^{1}(Y)
is defined in the usual form, namely,

f\in L^{1}(Y)\Longleftrightarrow\|f\|_{L^{1}(Y)}:=\smallint_{Y}|f(x)|dx<\infty,
where
dx
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
The measure of a Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
|A|,
ans its characteristic function is denoted by
1_{A}
or by
\chi_{A}.
We also consider the weak
L^{1}
-space
L^{1,\infty}(Y)
defined by the seminorm

\|f\|_{L^{1,\infty}(Y)}:=\sup_{t>0}t|\{y\in Y:|f(y)|>t\}|.

If
X
is a compact manifold without boundary we define the spaces
L^{1}(X)
and
L^{1,\infty}(X)
by using partitions of unity (subordinated to local coordinate systems). We say that an operator
T:L^{1}(Y)\rightarrow L^{1,\infty}(Y)
is of
weak (1,1) type
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
f\in L^{1}(Y),
the inequality
\|Tf\|_{L^{1,\infty}}(Y)\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}(Y)}
holds. A prototype of operators of weak (1,1) type is the family of Calderón-Zygmund singular integral operators, see Calderón and Zygmund
[
8
]
.

Below we precise the notation and the fundamental notions related to the theory of Fourier integral operators of importance for this work. We start with the properies of
conic Lagrangian manifolds
in the next subsection.

2.1.
Basics on symplectic geometry

Now, let us follow
[
39
, Chapter I]
.
We recall that a
2
-form
\omega
is called
symplectic
on
M
if
d\omega=0,
and for all
x\in M,
the bilinear form
\omega_{x}
is antisymmetric and non-degenerate on
T_{x}M
. The canonical symplectic form
\sigma_{M}
on
M
is defined as follows. Let
\pi:=\pi_{M}:T^{*}M\rightarrow M,
be the canonical projection. For any
(x,\xi)\in T^{*}M,
let us consider the linear mappings

d\pi_{(x,\xi)}:T_{(x,\xi)}(T^{*}M)\rightarrow T_{x}M\textnormal{ and, }\xi:T_{x}M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

The composition
\alpha_{(x,\xi)}:=\xi\circ d\pi_{(x,\xi)}\in T^{*}_{(x,\xi)}(T^{*}M),
that is

\xi\circ d\pi_{(x,\xi)}:T_{(x,\xi)}(T^{*}M)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
defines a 1-form
\alpha
on
T^{*}M.
With the notation above, the canonical symplectic form
\sigma_{M}
on
M
is defined by

(2.1)

\sigma_{M}:=d\alpha.

Since
\sigma_{M}
is an exact form, it follows that
d\sigma_{M}=0
and then that
\sigma_{M}
is symplectic. If
M=X\times Y,
it follows that
\sigma_{X\times Y}=\sigma_{X}\oplus-\sigma_{Y}.
Now we record the family of submanifolds that are necessary when one defines the canonical relations.

•

Let
M
be of dimension
n.
A submanifold
\Lambda\subset T^{*}M
of dimension
n
is called
Lagrangian
if

T_{(x,\xi)}\Lambda=(T_{(x,\xi)}\Lambda)^{\sigma}:=\{v\in T_{(x,\xi)}(T^{*}M):\sigma_{M}(v,v^{\prime})=0,\,\forall v^{\prime}\in T_{(x,\xi)}\Lambda\}.

•

We say that
\Lambda\subset T^{*}M\setminus 0
is
conic
if
(x,\xi)\in\Lambda,
implies that
(x,t\xi)\in\Lambda,
for all
t>0.

•

Let
\Sigma\subset X
be a smooth submanifold of
X
of dimension
k.
Its conormal bundle in
T^{*}X
is defined by

(2.2)

N^{*}\Sigma:=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}X:\,x\in\Sigma,\,\,\xi(\delta)=0,\,\forall\delta\in T_{x}\Sigma\}.

The following facts characterise the conic Lagrangian submanifolds of
T^{*}M.

•

Let
\Lambda\subset T^{*}M\setminus 0,
be a closed sub-manifold of dimension
n.
Then
\Lambda
is a conic Lagrangian manifold if and only if the 1-form
\alpha
in (
2.1
) vanishes on
\Lambda.

•

Let
\Sigma\subset X,
be a submanifold of dimension
k.
Then its conormal bundle
N^{*}\Sigma
is a conic Lagrangian manifold.

The Lagrangian manifolds have the following property.

•

Let
\Lambda\subset T^{*}M\setminus 0,
be a conic Lagrangian manifold and let

(2.3)

d\pi_{(x,\xi)}:T_{(x,\xi)}\Lambda\rightarrow T_{x}M,

have constant rank equal to
k,
for all
(x,\xi)\in\Lambda.
Then, each
(x,\xi)\in\Lambda
has a conic neighborhood
\Gamma
such that

1.

\Sigma=\pi(\Gamma\cap\Lambda)
is a smooth manifold of dimension
k.

2.

\Gamma\cap\Lambda
is an open subset of
N^{*}\Sigma.

The Lagrangian manifolds have a local representation defined in terms of
phase functions
that can be defined as follows. For this, let us consider a local trivialisation
M\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus 0),
where we can assume that
M
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition 2.1

(Real-valued phase functions)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a cone in
M\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus 0).
A smooth function
\phi:M\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus 0)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

(x,\theta)\mapsto\phi(x,\theta),
is a real
phase function
if, it is homogeneous of degree one in
\theta
and has no critical points as a function of
(x,\theta),
that is

(2.4)

\forall t>0,\,\phi(x,t\theta)=t\phi(x,\theta),\textnormal{ and }d_{(x,\theta)}\phi(x,\theta)\neq 0,\forall(x,\theta)\in M\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus 0).

Additionally, we say that
\phi
is a
non-degenerate phase function
in
\Gamma,
if for any
(x,\theta)\in\Gamma
such that
d_{\theta}\phi(x,\theta)=0,
one has that

(2.5)

d_{(x,\theta)}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta_{j}}(x,\theta),\,\,1\leq j\leq N,

is a system of linearly independent vectors on
\mathbb{R}
.

The following facts describe locally a Lagrangian manifold in terms of a phase function.

•

Let
\Gamma
be a cone in

•

Let

Remark 2.2

Remark 2.3

2.2.
Almost analytic continuation of real manifolds

To define the almost analytic continuation of a manifold we require some preliminaries. We record that a function
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
defined on an open subset
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is called
almost analytic
in
U_{\mathbb{R}}:=U\cap\mathbb{R}^{n},
if
f
satisfies the Cauchy-Riemann equations on
U_{\mathbb{R}},
that is, if
\overline{\partial}f
and all its derivatives vanish in
U_{\mathbb{R}}.
Here, as usual,
\overline{\partial}=1/2(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}).

One defines an
almost analytic extension
of a manifold
M
requiring that the corresponding coordinate functions are almost analytic in
M.
Here, we are going to present this notion carefully.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\rho:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a non-negative and Lipschitz function. A function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
\rho
-flat in
\Omega
if for every compact set
K\subset\Omega,
and for every integer
N\geq 0,
one has the growth estimate
|f(x)|\lesssim_{K,N}\rho(x)^{N},
for all
x\in K.
This notion defines an equivalence relation on the space of mapping on
\Omega:

f\textnormal{ and }g\textnormal{ are }\rho\textnormal{-equivalent if }f-g\textnormal{ is }\rho\textnormal{-flat on }\Omega.

If
K_{0}\subset\Omega
is a compact set, we will say that
f
is
flat
on
K_{0},
if it is
\rho
-flat with
\rho(x):=\textnormal{dist}(x,K_{0}).
One has the following property:

•

let
f\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
be
\rho
-flat. Then all its derivatives are
\rho
-flat and
f
is flat on
K_{0}
if and only if
D^{\alpha}f=0
for all
x\in K_{0}
and all
\alpha.

Let
G
be an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
K
be a closed subset of
G.
A function
f\in C^{\infty}(G)
is
almost analytic
on
K
if the functions
\partial_{j}f
are flat on
K,
for all
1\leq j\leq n.
For an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
we will denote

(2.9)

\tilde{\Omega}:=\Omega+i\mathbb{R}^{n}.

One can make the identification
\Omega\cong\tilde{\Omega}\cap\{z:\textnormal{Im}(z)=0\}.
Note that
every function
f\in C^{\infty}(G_{\mathbb{R}})
defines an equivalence class of almost analytic functions on
G_{\mathbb{R}},
which consist of functions in
C^{\infty}(G)
which are almost analytic in
G_{\mathbb{R}},
modulo functions being flat on
G_{\mathbb{R}}.
Any representative of this class is called an
almost analytic continuation of
f
in
G.

Let
O
be an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}.
Let
M
be a smooth submanifold of codimension
2k
of
O,
and let
K
be a closed subset of
O.
Then,

is called
almost analytic
on

if for any point

there exists an open set

and
k
-functions

such that every
f_{j}
is almost analytic on
K\cap U,

and such that in

is defined by the zero-level sets

with the differentials
df_{j},

1\leq j\leq k,
being linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}.

Two almost analytic submanifolds
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
of

are

if they have the same dimension and the same intersection with
\mathbb{R}^{n},
namely, that

M_{1,\mathbb{R}}=M_{1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=M_{2}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=M_{2,\mathbb{R}}=:M_{\mathbb{R}},

and locally

are flat functions on
M_{\mathbb{R}}
where
f_{j}
and
g_{j}
are the functions that define
M_{1}
and
M_{2},
respectively.

A real manifold
\Omega
defines an equivalence class of almost analytic manifolds in
\tilde{\Omega}.
A representative of this class is called an
almost analytic continuation
of
\Omega
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Now, let us consider:

•

a real symplectic manifold

of dimension
d=2n,
and let
\tilde{M}
be (modulo its equivalence class) its almost analytic continuation in
\mathbb{C}^{2n}.

•

Let
\Lambda\subset\tilde{M}
be an almost analytic sub-manifold containing the real point

and let

be its real symplectic coordinates in a neighbourhood

of

•

Let

be almost analytic continuations of the coordinates

in

in such a way that
(\tilde{x},\tilde{\xi})
maps

•

Let

in

and such that

Then, an almost analytic manifold

2.3.
Fourier integral operators with real-valued phases

We can assume that
X,Y
are open sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. One defines the class of
Fourier integral operators
T\in I^{\mu}_{\rho}(X,Y;\Lambda)
by
the (microlocal) formula

(2.10)

Tf(x)=\smallint\limits_{Y}\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{i\Psi(x,y,\theta)}a(x,y,\theta)f(y)d\theta\;dy,

where the symbol
a
is a smooth function locally in the class
S^{\mu}_{\rho,1-\rho}(X\times Y\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus 0)),
with
1/2\leq\rho\leq 1.
This means that
a
satisfies the symbol inequalities

|\partial_{x,y}^{\beta}\partial_{\theta}^{\alpha}a(x,y,\theta)|\leq C_{\alpha\beta}(1+|\theta|)^{\mu-\rho|\alpha|+(1-\rho)|\beta|},

for
(x,y)
in any compact subset
K
of
X\times Y,
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus 0,
while the real-valued phase function
\Psi
satisfies
the following properties:

1.

\Psi(x,y,\lambda\theta)=\lambda\Psi(x,y,\theta),
for all
\lambda>0
;

2.

d\Psi\not=0
;

3.

\{d_{\theta}\Psi=0\}
is smooth
(e.g.
d_{\theta}\Psi=0
implies

d_{(x,y,\theta)}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{j}}
are linearly
independent).

Here
\Lambda\subset T^{*}(X\times Y)\setminus 0
is a Lagrangian manifold locally parametrised by the phase function
\Psi,

\Lambda=\Lambda_{\Psi}=\{(x,d_{x}\Psi,y,d_{y}\Psi):d_{\theta}\Psi=0\}.

Remark 2.6
.

The canonical relation associated with
T

is the conic Lagrangian manifold in

T^{*}(X\times Y)\backslash 0
, defined by

\Lambda^{\prime}=\{(x,\xi,y,-\eta):(x,\xi,y,\eta)\in\Lambda\}.

In view of the Hörmander equivalence-of-phase-functions theorem (see e.g. Theorem 1.1.3 in
[
39
, Page 9]
), the notion of Fourier integral operator becomes independent of the choice of a particular phase function associated to a Lagrangian manifold
\Lambda.
Because of the diffeomorphism
\Lambda\cong\Lambda^{\prime},
we do not distinguish between
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
by saying also that
\Lambda
is the canonical relation associated with
T.

2.4.
Fourier integral operators with complex-valued phases

Now we record the following result for canonical relations, see e.g. Theorem 1.2.1 in
[
39
]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\Psi
be a phase function of the positive type that is defined in a conical neighbourhood. Let
\tilde{\Psi}
be an almost analytic homogeneous continuation of
\Psi
in a canonical neighbourhood in
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times({\mathbb{C}^{n}}\setminus\{0\}).
Let

(2.11)

C_{\tilde{\Psi}}=\{(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{\theta})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times({\mathbb{C}^{n}}\setminus\{0\}):\partial_{\theta}\tilde{\Psi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{\theta})=0\}.

Then, the image of the set
C_{\tilde{\Psi}}
under the mapping

(2.12)

C_{\tilde{\Psi}}\ni(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{\theta})\mapsto(\tilde{x},\partial_{\tilde{x}}\tilde{\Psi},\partial_{\tilde{y}}\tilde{\Psi})|_{(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{\theta})}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times({\mathbb{C}^{n}}\setminus\{0\})\times({\mathbb{C}^{n}}\setminus\{0\})

is a local positive Lagrangian manifold.

Let
X
and
Y
be smooth manifolds of dimension
n
and let us denote by
\widetilde{T^{*}(X\times Y)\setminus 0}
the almost analytic continuation of
T^{*}(X\times Y)\setminus 0.
Let

C\equiv C_{\Phi}=\{(x,d_{x}\Phi,y,d_{y}\Phi):d_{\theta}\Phi=0\}\subset\widetilde{T^{*}(X\times Y)\setminus 0}

be a
smooth positive homogeneous canonical relation.
This means that
C
is locally parametrised by a complex phase function
\Phi(x,y,\theta),
satisfying the following properties:

1.

\Phi
is homogeneous of order one in
\theta:

\Phi(x,y,t\theta)=t\Phi(x,y,\theta),
for all
t>0,

2.

\Phi
has no critical points on its domain, i.e.
d\Phi\neq 0;

3.

\{d_{\theta}\Phi(x,y,\theta)=0\}
is smooth (e.g.
d_{\theta}\Phi=0
implies that

are independent over
\mathbb{C}
),

4.

\textnormal{Im}(\Phi)\geq 0.

When the last condition is satisfied one says that the complex phase function
\Phi
is of
positive type
. Then, the class
I^{\mu}_{\rho}(X,Y;C)
of Fourier integral operators
T
is determined modulo
C^{\infty}
by those integral operators
T
that locally have an integral kernel of the form

\displaystyle A(x,y)=\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{i\Phi(x,y,\theta)}a(x,y,\theta)d\theta,

where
a(x,y,\theta)
is a symbol of order
\mu+\frac{N-n}{2}.

By the equivalence-of-phase-function theorem, we can always assume that

and that the symbol

satisfies the type
(\rho,1-\rho)
-estimates

(2.13)

|\partial_{x,y}^{\beta}\partial_{\theta}^{\alpha}a(x,y,\theta)|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}(1+|\theta|)^{\mu-\rho|\alpha|+(1-\rho)|\beta|},\,\,\frac{1}{2}\leq\rho\leq 1,

locally uniformly in
(x,y).

2.5.
Asymptotics for oscillatory integrals

Now, we recall two principles used in this work: the principle of non-stationary phase and the principle of stationary phase, respectively.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
43
]
, Page 342)
.

Suppose that
a
is smooth and supported in the unit ball, also let
\phi
be real-valued so that for some multi-index
\alpha
with
k=|\alpha|>0,
we have that

|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\phi|\geq 1

throughout the support of
a.
Then,

\left|\smallint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\lambda\phi(\xi)}a(\xi)d\xi\right|\leq C_{k,\phi}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{k}}(\|a\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla a\|_{L^{1}}).

The constant
C_{k,\phi}
is independent of
\lambda>0,
and of
a,
and remains bounded as long as the
C^{k+1}

norm of
a
remains bounded.

Proposition 2.9

(
[
48
]
, Proposition 6.4)
.

Let
\phi
be a smooth function and assume that
\nabla\phi(p)=0.
Assume also that the Hessian
H_{\phi}(p)=(\partial_{ij}^{2}\phi(\xi))|_{\xi=p}
of
\phi
at
\xi=p,
is an invertible matrix. Let
\mu
be the signature of
H_{\phi}(p),
and let
\Delta=2^{-n}\det(H_{\phi}(p)).
Let
a:=a(\xi)\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be supported in a small neighbourhood of
\xi=p.
Then for any
N\in\mathbb{N},
the integral

I(\lambda)=\smallint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\pi\lambda\phi(\xi)}a(\xi)d\xi,

satisfies the asymptotic expansion

(2.14)

I(\lambda)=e^{\pi i\lambda\phi(p)}e^{-\frac{\mu\pi i}{4}}\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{-\frac{n}{2}}\left(a(p)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda^{-j}D_{2j}a(p)+O(\lambda^{-(N+1)})\right),

where
D_{2j}
are differential operators of order
2j,
with smooth coefficients depending on
\phi,
and bounds for finitely many derivatives of
a.

3.
Reduction to phases of strictly positive type

We say that a complex phase function is of
strictly positive type
, if
\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\theta))>0,
when
|\theta|\sim 1.

The aim of this section is to show that our main Theorem
1.7
follows from the following reduced version.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
T
be a Fourier integral operator of order
-(n-1)/2
associated with a canonical relation parametrised by a complex phase
\Phi
of strictly positive type, that is, there exists
\varkappa_{0}>0
such that for all
(x,\theta)\in\overline{V}\times\overline{\{\theta:|\theta|\sim 1\}},
one has the lower bound

\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\theta))>\varkappa_{0},

and additionally satisfying the local graph condition (
1.8
). Then
T
is of weak type (1,1).

The proof of Theorem
3.1
will be addressed in Section
4
. Assuming this statement we give a proof of our main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Since
\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))>0,\,\,|\xi|\neq 0,
the compactness of the closed annulus
\overline{\{\theta:|\theta|\sim 1\}},
and of
\overline{V},
imply that there exists
\varkappa_{0}>0
such that for all
(x,\theta)\in\overline{V}\times\overline{\{\theta:|\theta|\sim 1\}},
one has the lower bound

\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\theta))>\varkappa_{0}.

In view of Theorem
3.1
we end the proof.
∎

Notation

We consider the dimension
n\geq 2
and the parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1.
We will write
2^{C\varepsilon k},
and the constant
C>0
will be independent of
\varepsilon,
and probably it is not the same in different parts of the manuscript. Always we will keep in mind that the constant
C\varepsilon
can be chosen small enough by allowing

to be arbitrarily small.

Since

we can define the projective system of co-ordinates

via

(3.1)

\lambda:=\xi_{n},\,\omega:=\overline{\xi}/\xi_{n},\,\textnormal{ where }\,\xi=(\overline{\xi},\xi_{n}),\,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\,\overline{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

In the same way we will denote

(3.2)

\lambda^{\prime}=\xi_{n}^{\prime},\,\omega^{\prime}=\overline{\xi^{\prime}}/\xi^{\prime}_{n},\,\,\xi^{\prime}=(\overline{\xi}^{\prime},\xi_{n}^{\prime}),\,\xi^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\,\overline{\xi^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},

when

We will use the standard notation for Littlewood-Paley decompositions. So, we fix a non-negative radial test function
\phi(\xi)=\phi_{0}(\xi),
supported on the ball

and for any

\phi_{k}(\xi):=\phi(\xi/2^{k}),\,\,\eta_{k}(\xi)=\phi_{k}(\xi)-\phi_{k-1}(\xi).

Even, when these functions are defined on

4.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

The aim of this section is to prove the reduced version of our main Theorem
1.7
, namely, the one in Theorem
3.1
for phase functions of strictly positive type. To do this we will combine the following two approaches:

•

The first one, is a strategy traced back to the works of Melin and Sjöstrand
[
30
,
31
]
, where any Fourier integral operator
T\in I^{\mu}(X,Y;C)
can be written in the form

(4.1)

Tf(x)=\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(\textnormal{Re}(\Phi(x,\xi))+\tau\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi,

with the real parameter
\tau\neq 0
as in the local graph condition (
1.8
), the real phase function
\Phi_{\tau}
is given by

(4.2)

\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi):=\textnormal{Re}(\Phi(x,\xi))+\tau\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi)),

and the symbol

a_{\tau}(x,\xi)=a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}

belongs to the class
S^{-(n-1)/2}_{1/2,1/2}.
This formula for the operator has shown to be effective when dealing with its
H^{1}
-
L^{1}
-boundedness, see
[
39
]
.

•

The second one, introduced by Tao
[
46
]
where for the proof of the weak (1,1) inequality one decomposes the operator
T
into its degenerate and non-degenerate components,
T_{\textnormal{deg}}
and
T_{\textnormal{non-deg}}.
Indeed, this is done using the
curvature
notion, which measures the extent to which the phase function fails to be linear. The portion where the curvature is small on each dyadic component of the operator defines the degenerate component
T_{\textnormal{deg}}
of
T
, and its ‘complement’
T-T_{\textnormal{deg}},
defines the non-degenerate component
T_{\textnormal{non-deg}}.
Among other things, the following are the key points in the factorisation approach in
[
46
]
: (i) to prove that
T_{\textnormal{deg}}
is bounded on
L^{1}
; (ii) to construct a pseudo-differential operator
S
of order zero, and then of weak (1,1) type, according to the Calderón-Zygmund theory and an average operator
A,
which is bounded on
L^{1},
and essentially has the same phase function that
T_{\textnormal{non-deg}},
in such a way that the error operator
E=T_{\textnormal{nondeg}}-SA
is bounded on
L^{1}.

The aim of the next subsections is to show that the two previous approaches are compatible, allowing to factorise the operator
T
with a complex phase satisfying the conditions in Theorem

, into more manageable operators.

4.1.
Degenerate and non-degenerate components

Let us use the notation
\phi_{k}
in (

) for the Littlewood-Paley partition of unity. Typically,

runs over the set of integers, but also we are going to consider the functions
\phi_{-\varepsilon k}
where

runs over the set of integers and

is fixed, and then
\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(\xi):=\phi(2^{\varepsilon k}\xi).

In terms of the projective co-ordinates
(\lambda,\omega)
in (

), in view of the homogeneity of the phase function
\Phi_{\tau}
as defined in (

), we can write
\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)=\lambda\Phi_{\tau}(x,(\omega,1)),
when
\xi_{n}=\lambda\neq 0.
In such a case we are going to simplify the notation by writing
\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega):=\Phi_{\tau}(x,(\omega,1)).

Using the Littlewood-Paley decomposition for the Fourier integral operator

we have

Tf(x)=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\Phi(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)\eta_{k}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi,

where the inequality

with

is justified because the symbol

is supported on

Now, we decompose

T_{\textnormal{deg}}f(x)=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega))\eta_{k}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi,

where, as above,

(4.3)

\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi):=\textnormal{Re}(\Phi(x,\xi))+\tau\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi)),

and

(4.4)

J_{\tau}(x,\omega)=\textnormal{det}(\nabla_{\omega}^{2}(\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega))),\,\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega):=\Phi_{\tau}(x,(\omega,1)),

where
\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)=\lambda\Phi_{\tau}(x,(\omega,1)).
Here,

is the

associated to the real-valued phase function
\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega).
Since
\tau\neq 0,
sometimes we also write

for simplicity.
Note then that
T_{\textnormal{nondeg}}
is given by

(4.5)

T_{\textnormal{nondeg}}f(x)=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega)))\eta_{k}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi.

4.1.1.
Symbol classes in projective co-ordinates

The aim of this subsection is to analyse the behaviour of symbols in the frequency variable with respect to the projective co-ordinates
(\lambda,\omega),
defined via

(4.6)

\lambda=\xi_{n},\,\omega=\overline{\xi}/\xi_{n},\,\,\xi:=(\overline{\xi},\xi_{n}),\,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\,\overline{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

Although
\lambda
and
\xi_{n}
are equal in value, the radial derivative
\partial_{\lambda}
keeps

fixed, and the vertical derivative
\partial_{\xi_{n}}
keeps
\overline{\xi}
fixed. In the next lemma we analyse the derivatives of a symbol in terms of the radial and of the angular variables. We note that
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},
and we write
\omega^{J}=\omega_{1}^{J_{1}}\cdots\omega_{n-1}^{J_{n-1}},
when

and also
\omega^{T}=(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{n-1})
for a row vector.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let us assume that the domain of
a
contains an open neighborhood of the region
\Omega_{M}=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi_{n}|>M\},
where
M>0
is fixed. Assuming
a:=a(x,\xi)
smooth in
\Omega_{M},
we have that

(4.7)

\partial^{\ell}_{\lambda}a=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}C_{J}\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell-|J|}a\cdot\omega^{J},

for some family of coefficients
C_{J}
. Also,

(4.8)

\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}a=\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{\beta}a\cdot\lambda^{|\beta|},

on
\Omega_{M}.

Proof.

Let us prove (
4.7
). When
\ell=1,

the chain rule gets

(4.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}a=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\partial_{\xi_{j}}a\times\partial_{\xi_{j}}/\partial{\lambda}+\partial_{\xi_{n}}a\times\partial{\xi_{n}}/\partial\lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\omega_{j}\partial_{\xi_{j}}a+\partial_{\xi_{n}}a=\omega^{T}\cdot\nabla_{\overline{\xi}}a+\partial_{\xi_{n}}a.

When
\ell=2,
observe that

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}^{2}a

\displaystyle=\partial_{\lambda}(\omega^{T}\cdot\nabla_{\overline{\xi}}a+\partial_{\xi_{n}}a)=\omega^{T}\cdot\partial_{\lambda}\nabla_{\overline{\xi}}a+\omega^{T}\cdot\nabla_{\overline{\xi}}(\partial_{\xi_{n}}a)+\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{2}a

\displaystyle=\omega^{T}\nabla^{2}_{\overline{\xi}}a\cdot\partial{\overline{\xi}}/\partial\lambda+\omega^{T}\cdot\nabla_{\overline{\xi}}(\partial_{\xi_{n}}a)+\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{2}a

\displaystyle=\omega^{T}\cdot[\nabla^{2}_{\overline{\xi}}a\cdot\omega]+\omega^{T}\cdot\nabla_{\overline{\xi}}(\partial_{\xi_{n}}a)+\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{2}a.

Mathematical induction on
\ell\in\mathbb{N},

implies that
\partial^{\ell}_{\lambda}a
can be written in terms of some coefficients
C_{J},

J\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
as

(4.10)

\partial^{\ell}_{\lambda}a=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}C_{J}\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell-|J|}a\cdot\omega^{J}.

For the proof of (
4.10
)
observe that assuming this identity valid for
\ell>2,
we have

\displaystyle\partial^{\ell+1}_{\lambda}a=\partial_{\lambda}\partial^{\ell}_{\lambda}a=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}C_{J}\partial_{\lambda}(\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell-|J|}a)\cdot\omega^{J}.

With
b_{J}=\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell-|J|}a,
we apply (
4.9
) to deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}C_{J}\partial_{\lambda}(\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell-|J|}a)\cdot\omega^{J}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}C_{J}\partial_{\lambda}(b_{J})\cdot\omega^{J}=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}C_{J}\partial_{\xi_{j}}b_{J}\omega_{j}\omega^{J}+\partial_{\xi_{n}}b_{J}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}C_{J}\partial_{\xi_{j}}\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell-|J|}a\omega_{j}\omega^{J}+\partial_{\xi_{n}}\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell-|J|}a\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}C_{J}\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J+e_{j}}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell+1-|J+e_{j}|}a\omega^{J+e_{j}}+\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell+1-|J|}a\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\tilde{J}|\leq\ell+1}C_{\tilde{J}}\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{\tilde{J}}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell+1-|\tilde{J|}}a\cdot\omega^{\tilde{J}},

for some family of coefficients
C_{\tilde{J}}.
The proof of (
4.7
) is complete. For the proof of (
4.8
) note that for any
j=1,2,\cdots,n-1,

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega_{j}}a=\partial_{\xi_{j}}a\cdot\partial\xi_{j}/\partial\omega_{j}=\partial_{\xi_{j}}a\cdot\lambda,

which verifies (
4.8
) when
|\beta|=1.
Assuming (
4.8
) with
\beta
fixed, namely, that
\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}=\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{\beta}a\cdot\lambda^{|\beta|},
note that for any
1\leq j\leq n-1,

\partial_{\omega}^{\beta+e_{j}}a=\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}a=\partial_{\xi_{j}}(\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}a)\cdot\lambda=\partial_{\xi_{j}}\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{\beta}a\cdot\lambda^{|\beta|}\cdot\lambda=\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{\beta+e_{j}}a\cdot\lambda^{|\beta+e_{j}|},

from where the mathematical induction proves
(
4.8
)
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}.

∎

For
1/2\leq\rho\leq 1,
let
S^{m}_{\rho,\mathscr{C}}
be the class of symbols that consists of all functions
a:=a(x,\xi)
which are Borel measurable in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
supported in the cone bundle

(4.11)

\mathscr{C}=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}:\xi_{n}\gg 1,\,|\overline{\xi}|\leq\lambda\},

and satisfying the Hörmander type estimates

(4.12)

|\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}a(x,\xi)|\leq C_{\alpha}|\xi|^{m-\rho|\alpha|},\,\,(x,\xi)\in\textnormal{supp}(a).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
k\gg 1,

C>0
and let
\varepsilon>0.
Let
a\in S^{m}_{\rho,\mathscr{C}}.
Consider the symbol

(4.13)

a_{k}(x,\xi)=a(x,\xi)\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega))\eta_{k}(\xi),

supported in the region

\mathscr{C}_{\lambda}=\{(x,\xi)\in\textnormal{supp}(a):\lambda\sim 2^{k};\,|J_{\tau}(x,\xi)|\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k}\}.

Then, we have the following symbol estimates

(4.14)

|\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}\partial_{\lambda}^{\gamma}a_{k}(x,\xi)|\lesssim_{\beta,\gamma,C}2^{mk}2^{-\rho|\gamma|k+(1-\rho)|\beta|k}2^{C\varepsilon k|\beta|},

for
(x,\xi)
in the support of
a:=a(x,\xi).

Proof.

Using that

a
satisfies the estimates in (
4.12
) and the identity (
4.7
) we have that

\displaystyle\forall\ell\geq 1,\,\,|\partial_{\lambda}^{\ell}a|\lesssim\sup_{|J|\leq\ell}|\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{J}\partial_{\xi_{n}}^{\ell-|J|}a|\lesssim|\xi|^{m-\rho\ell}.

Observing that, on the region
\mathscr{C}_{\lambda},

|\xi|\sim|\lambda|\sim 2^{k}
we have proved that

(4.15)

|\partial_{\lambda}^{\ell}a|\lesssim 2^{k(m-\rho\ell)}.

By the Leibniz rule we have that

(4.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}a_{k}=\sum_{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}=\beta}C_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{2}}\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{1}}a\times\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{2}}(\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega)))\times\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{3}}\eta_{k}.

In view of (
4.8
),
we can estimate

(4.17)

|\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{1}}a|=|\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{\beta_{1}}a||\lambda|^{|\beta_{1}|}\lesssim|\xi|^{m-\rho|\beta_{1}|}|\lambda|^{|\beta_{1}|}\sim|\lambda|^{m-\rho|\beta_{1}|}|\lambda|^{|\beta_{1}|}\sim 2^{km}2^{k(1-\rho)|\beta_{1}|}

Since the sequence
\eta_{k}
belongs uniformly in
k
to
S^{0},
we have that

(4.18)

|\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{3}}\eta_{k}|=|\partial_{\overline{\xi}}^{\beta_{3}}\eta_{k}||\lambda|^{|\beta_{3}|}\lesssim|\xi|^{-|\beta_{3}|}|\lambda|^{|\beta_{3}|}\sim 1.

Now, let us analyse the behaviour of the cutt-off
\psi_{-\varepsilon k}
at the “curvature” term
J_{\tau}(x,\omega).
Observe that for any
j=1,\cdots,n-1,

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega_{j}}(\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega)))

\displaystyle=\phi_{-\varepsilon k}^{\prime}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega)))\times\partial_{\omega_{j}}J_{\tau}(x,\omega).

Then, we have the equalities

\displaystyle|\partial_{\omega_{j}}(\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega)))|

\displaystyle=|\phi^{\prime}_{-\varepsilon k}(t)|_{t=J_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\times\partial_{\omega_{j}}J_{\tau}(x,\omega)|

\displaystyle=|\partial_{t}[\phi(t/2^{-\varepsilon k})]|_{t=J(x,\omega)}\times\partial_{\omega_{j}}J(x,\omega)|

\displaystyle=|(\partial_{t}\phi)(t/2^{-\varepsilon k})2^{k\varepsilon}|_{t=J_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\times\partial_{\omega_{j}}J_{\tau}(x,\omega)|.

There is
C_{\phi}>0
such that

|\partial_{t}\phi(\lambda)|\leq C_{\phi}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle|(\partial_{t}\phi)(t/2^{-\varepsilon k})|_{t=J_{\tau}(x,\omega)}2^{k\varepsilon}\lesssim 2^{k\varepsilon}.

Thus, the mathematical induction gives the estimate

(4.19)

|\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{2}}(\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega)))|\lesssim 2^{k\varepsilon|\beta_{2}|}.

We have proved the estimates

|\partial_{\lambda}^{\gamma}a|\lesssim 2^{km}2^{-k\gamma},\,|\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{1}}a|\lesssim 2^{km}2^{k(1-\rho)|\beta_{1}|},

and

(4.20)

|\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{3}}\eta_{k}|\lesssim 1,\,|\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{2}}(\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega)))|\lesssim 2^{k\varepsilon|\beta|}.

Note that when
\gamma=\beta_{1}=0,
we recover the information that
a
has order
m.
Indeed, let
a_{\beta_{1}}:=\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{1}}a.
Since
\lambda\sim 2^{k},
note that the order of
a_{\beta_{1}}
with respect to
\lambda
is
m_{\beta_{1}}:=m+(1-\rho)|\beta_{1}|.
If we replace the analysis above with the symbol
a_{\beta_{1}}
instead of
a,
then
m_{\beta_{1}}
takes the place of
m,
and we get the estimate

|\partial_{\lambda}^{\gamma}a_{\beta_{1}}|\lesssim 2^{km_{\beta_{1}}}2^{-k\gamma}=2^{km+k(1-\rho)|\beta_{1}|-k\gamma}.

Therefore we have proved that

|\partial_{\lambda}^{\gamma}\partial_{\omega}^{\beta_{1}}a|\lesssim 2^{km_{\beta_{1}}}2^{-k\gamma}=2^{km+k(1-\rho)|\beta_{1}|-k\gamma}.

The estimates for the derivatives of
a
in
\lambda
and in
\omega
above, the inequality in (
4.20
) and the Leibniz rule (
4.16
) conclude the proof of (
4.14
).
∎

4.2.
Boundedness of the degenerate component

In this section we prove that the operator

(4.21)

T_{\textnormal{deg}}f(x)=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega))\eta_{k}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi,

is bounded on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Before continuing with the proof of the
L^{1}
-boundedness of
T_{\textnormal{deg}},
we discuss the second dyadic partition that will be employed in our further analysis.

Remark 4.3

(About discrete vs continuous ‘second’ dyadic decompositions)
.

In
[
44
]
, a discrete ‘second dyadic decomposition’ was introduced where the variable
\omega
is decomposed in a finite family of disks
\{D\}_{D\in\mathcal{I}},
see Figure
1
. At first glance, for the analysis of the degenerate component
T_{\textnormal{deg}},
one could apply this discrete ‘second dyadic partition’ on the symbol:

a_{k}(x,\xi)=a(x,\xi)\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega))\eta_{k}(\xi),

generating the new family of symbols
a_{k,D},
in such a way that
a_{k}=\sum_{D}a_{k,D}.
However, as it was explained in
[
46
]
, this discrete decomposition provides the estimate

(4.22)

\left\|\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)}a_{k}(x,\xi)e^{-2\pi iy\cdot\xi}d\xi\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\lesssim 1,

for the
L^{1}
-norm of the kernel associated with each dyadic part
a_{k}.
This estimate is not enough to deduce the
L^{1}
boundeness of
T_{\textnormal{deg}}.
The reason is that one needs a more accurate decay of this
L^{1}
-norm. Indeed, this is proved in Lemma
4.6
improving the
O(1)
term in (
4.22
), by the smaller one
2^{-\varepsilon k}.
In order to make this improvement, we will use the degeneracy condition
|J(x,\omega)|\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k}.
To have the control of lower terms in the Taylor expansion of the phase
\Phi_{\tau}-y\cdot\xi
at
\omega_{0}=\omega_{D},
especially if
\omega-\omega_{D}
is in the direction where
\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}
degenerates, the disks
D
will be replaced by a suitable family of ellipsoids, that allows us to decompose the
(x,\xi)
-variable in a family of tubular regions where we gain the decay
2^{-\varepsilon k}.

Figure 1.
Here we illustrate the partition of the
\omega
variable smoothly into about
2^{(n-1)k/2}
disks
D
is radius
2^{-k/2}
when
|\omega|\lesssim 1.
First, in the system of coordinates
(\overline{\xi},\xi_{n}),
the projective coordinates
(\lambda,\omega)
determine each point
(\overline{\xi},\xi_{n})\in\mathscr{C}.
The unit ball
B_{n-1}=\{|\omega|\leq 1\}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is saturated to the ‘segment’
\overline{AB}.
Each partition of the ball
B_{n-1}
in a family of disks
\{D\}_{D\in\mathcal{I}}
is saturated to a partition of the segment
\overline{AB}.
In particular if the radii of the disks are proportional to
r=2^{-k/2},
one can estimate
|\mathcal{I}|\sim 2^{(n-1)k/2}.

As it was pointed out in
[
46
]
, there is an apparent difficulty in the case when
n\geq 3,
where the ellipsoid around
\omega_{0}=\omega_{D},
depends on the spectrum of
\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega_{D}).
Then, the eccentricity and the orientation of this ellipsoid vary in function of
\omega_{D}.
However, by adapting to the setting of the complex phases, the approach in
[
46
]
, we will define a continuous ‘second dyadic partition’ in
\omega,
using a family of functions
\psi_{x,\omega_{D}}
with a family of corresponding supports determined by a family of ellipsoids with a smooth variation of eccentricity and orientation, centred at the points
\omega_{D}
that avoid Kakeya-type covering lemmas. According to this partition the part of the support of each symbol
a_{k,D}
on the hyperplane
\xi_{n}=\lambda
is illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
Note that the dyadic partition of the variable

The following is the main result of this subsection.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
S^{-(n-1)/2}_{\rho,\mathscr{C}}
be the symbol of the Fourier integral operator
T
of order
m=-(n-1)/2
and let
\Phi
be its complex phase function. Then, the degenerate component
T_{\textnormal{deg}}
in (
4.21
) extends to a bounded operator from
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
into
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Remark 4.5
.

For the proof of Proposition
4.4
,
it is suffices to prove the estimate

(4.23)

\exists C>0,\forall f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\,\|T_{\textnormal{deg}}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Note that the kernel of
T_{\textnormal{deg}}
is given by

K_{\textnormal{deg}}(x,y):=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega))\eta_{k}(\xi)e^{-2\pi iy\cdot\xi}d\xi.

By the triangle inequality, for the proof of (
4.23
), it suffices to prove that

(4.24)

\left\|\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega))\eta_{k}(\xi)e^{-2\pi iy\cdot\xi}d\xi\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k},

in view of Minkowski integral inequality. Indeed, from (
4.24
) one deduces that

(4.25)

C:=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|K_{\textnormal{deg}}(x,y)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\lesssim\sum_{k\gg 1}2^{-\varepsilon k}<\infty.

Consequently,

\|T_{\textnormal{deg}}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\smallint K_{\textnormal{deg}}(x,y)f(y)dy\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}=\smallint\left|\smallint K_{\textnormal{deg}}(x,y)f(y)dy\right|dx

\leq\smallint\smallint|K_{\textnormal{deg}}(x,y)|dx|f(y)|dy\leq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\smallint|K_{\textnormal{deg}}(x,y)|dx\smallint|f(y)|dy

=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\smallint|K_{\textnormal{deg}}(x,y)|dx\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

from where one deduces (
4.23
) with
C>0
defined in (
4.25
).

For the proof of Proposition
4.4
we require some preliminary results. We start with the following estimates for radial and angular derivatives of symbols in the class
S^{-(n-1)/2}_{\rho,\mathscr{C}}
and with an estimate of the
L^{1}
-norm of the kernel of
T_{\textnormal{deg}}.
This is our first step in the proof of (
4.24
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
k\gg 1,

\frac{1}{2}\leq\rho\leq 1,

C>0,
and let
\varepsilon>0.
Let
a\in S^{-(n-1)/2}_{\rho,\mathscr{C}}.
Consider the symbol

(4.26)

a_{k}(x,\xi)=a(x,\xi)\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J_{\tau}(x,\omega))\eta_{k}(\xi).

Then, we have the following symbol estimates

(4.27)

|\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}\partial_{\lambda}^{\gamma}a_{k}(x,\xi)|\lesssim_{\beta,\gamma}2^{-\frac{(n-1)k}{2}}2^{-\rho|\gamma|k+(1-\rho)|\beta|k}2^{C\varepsilon k|\beta|},

for
(x,\xi)
in the support of
a.
Moreover, any function
a_{k}:=a_{k}(x,\xi)\in S^{-(n-1)/2}_{\rho,\mathscr{C}}
supported in the region

(4.28)

\{(x,\xi)\in\textnormal{supp}(a):\lambda\sim 2^{k};\,|J_{\tau}(x,\xi)|\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k}\}

satisfying the symbol estimates in (
4.27
) defines a Fourier integral operator whose kernel satisfies the inequality

(4.29)

\left\|\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)}a_{k}(x,\xi)e^{-2\pi iy\cdot\xi}d\xi\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\lesssim_{\rho}2^{-\varepsilon k},

uniformly in
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

The estimate in (
4.27
) for
a_{k}
as defined in (
4.26
) is a consequence of Lemma
4.2
applied to the order
m=-(n-1)/2.

Now, let us consider a symbol
a_{k}:=a_{k}(x,\xi)
satisfying the estimates in (
4.27
) whose support is contained in (
4.28
). For the proof of (
4.29
) we need a preliminary construction. Indeed, let us define a positive-definite replacement
Q(x,\omega)
of
\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega).
Note that
\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)
is the associated matrix to the quadratic form

\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{T}\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)\zeta=:\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)(\zeta,\zeta).

Let

\lambda_{1}(x,\omega),\cdots,\lambda_{n-1}(x,\omega)

be the family of eigenvalues of
\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega).

In an appropriate bases
B
of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
we can write

\displaystyle\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)(\zeta,\zeta)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}(x,\omega)|\zeta_{j}|^{2},\,\,\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

Define

(4.30)

Q(x,\omega):=(2^{-2\varepsilon k}I+(\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega))^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
I=I_{n-1}
is the identity matrix of size
n-1.

In the same basis
B,
we can write

\displaystyle Q(x,\omega)=\textnormal{diag}[2^{-2\varepsilon k}+\lambda_{j}(x,\omega)^{2}]_{1\leq j\leq n-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Observe that

(4.31)

\det(Q(x,\omega))=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}[2^{-2\varepsilon k}+\lambda_{j}(x,\omega)^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq 2^{-Ck\varepsilon},\,C=n-1.

On the other hand

\displaystyle\det(Q(x,\omega))

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}[2^{-2\varepsilon k}+\lambda_{j}(x,\omega)^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}[2^{-\varepsilon k}+|\lambda_{j}(x,\omega)|]

\displaystyle=2^{-(n-1)\varepsilon k}+\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}|\lambda_{j}(x,\omega)|+O(2^{-\varepsilon k})

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k}+|J_{\tau}(x,\omega)|.

The matrix
Q(x,\omega)
has determinant satisfying the estimates

(4.32)

\displaystyle 2^{-Ck\varepsilon}\leq\det(Q(x,\omega))\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k}+|J_{\tau}(x,\omega)|.

For any
(x,\omega_{D})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1},
with the property that when
\lambda\sim 2^{k},

(x,\lambda(\omega_{D},1))=(x,\overline{\xi}_{D},\lambda)\in\mathscr{C},\,\,\overline{\xi}_{D}:=\omega_{D}\lambda,

define the function
\psi_{x,\omega_{D}}
by

(4.33)

\displaystyle\psi_{x,\omega_{D}}(\omega):=\det(Q(x,\omega_{D}))^{\frac{1}{2}}{2^{\frac{(n-1)k}{2}}\psi_{-k}(Q(x,\omega_{D})(\omega-\omega_{D},\omega-\omega_{D}))}.

Since on
\mathscr{C},

|\overline{\xi}_{D}|\lesssim\lambda
we necessarily have that
|\omega_{D}|\lesssim 1
and so
\omega_{D}
is contained in a compact subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

Note that

\displaystyle Q(x,\omega_{D})(\omega-\omega_{D},\omega-\omega_{D})\leq\sup_{x\in\pi_{1}[\textnormal{supp}(a)],\,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega}\|Q(x,\omega^{\prime})\|_{\textnormal{op}}\|\omega-\omega_{D}\|^{2}\lesssim_{\textnormal{supp}(a),\Omega}\|\omega-\omega_{D}\|^{2}.

In consequence, if

\|\omega-\omega_{D}\|\lesssim 2^{-(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})k},

then

Q(x,\omega)(\omega-\omega_{D},\omega-\omega_{D})\leq 2^{-(2-\varepsilon)k}\lesssim 2^{-k},

and consequently
\textnormal{supp}(\psi_{x,\omega_{D}})
contains a small disk
\{\omega=\omega_{D}+z:|z|\lesssim 2^{-(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})k}\},
namely, we have the inclusion

(4.34)

\displaystyle\{\omega=\omega_{D}+z:|z|\lesssim 2^{-(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})k}\}\subset\textnormal{supp}(\psi_{x,\omega_{D}}).

By writing
Q(x,\omega_{D})
in the basis
B,
we have

\displaystyle Q(x,\omega_{D})(\omega-\omega_{D},\omega-\omega_{D})=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(2^{-2\varepsilon k}+\lambda_{j}(x,\omega_{D})^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}(\omega_{j}-\omega_{D,j})^{2}

from where one can deduce that
\psi_{x,\omega_{D}}(\omega)
is supported in an ellipsoid centred at
\omega=\omega_{D}.

Observe that, since

(4.35)

\displaystyle Q(x,\omega_{D})(\omega-\omega_{D},\omega-\omega_{D})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}2^{-\varepsilon k}(\omega_{j}-\omega_{D,j})^{2},

the support of
\psi_{x,\omega_{D}}
contains the disk
\{\omega=\omega_{D}+O(2^{-k/2})\}.
Indeed, on the support of
\psi_{x,\omega_{D}},

Q(x,\omega_{D})(\omega-\omega_{D},\omega-\omega_{D})\in\textnormal{supp}(\psi_{-k})
which says that

Q(x,\omega_{D})(\omega-\omega_{D},\omega-\omega_{D})\lesssim 2^{-k}.

In view of (
4.35
) the inequality

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}2^{-\varepsilon k}(\omega_{j}-\omega_{D,j})^{2}\lesssim 2^{-k}

implies that

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\omega_{j}-\omega_{D,j})^{2}\lesssim 2^{-k}2^{\varepsilon k}=2^{-(1-\varepsilon)k}.

In consequence,

(4.36)

\displaystyle\textnormal{supp}(\psi_{x,\omega_{D}})\subset\{\omega=\omega_{D}+z:|z|\lesssim 2^{-(1-\varepsilon)k}\}.

In view of the inclusions (
4.34
) and (
4.36
) for any
\omega\in\textnormal{supp}(\psi_{x,\omega_{D}})
we have

(4.37)

\displaystyle 2^{-(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})k}\lesssim|\omega-\omega_{D}|\lesssim 2^{-(1-\varepsilon)k}.

Now, define the averaged function
\psi_{x}(\omega)

Therefore, we have that

\left\|\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}}e^{2\pi i\lambda(\Phi_{\tau}(x,T(\zeta))-y\cdot(T(\zeta),1))}b_{k,\omega_{D},x}(\lambda,\zeta)d\lambda d\zeta\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k}2^{-(n-1)k/2}

as desired. We have proved (

). In consequence, the proof of Lemma

is complete.
∎

4.3.

Now, we are going to prove the weak (1,1) boundedness of the non-degenerate component

bounded on

4.3.1.
Construction of the operators

and

Let us consider the projection
\pi_{1}:\textnormal{supp}(a)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the first component

and let
V
be an open subset, such that
\overline{V}
is compact with
\pi_{1}(\textnormal{supp}(a))\subset V.
Let
1_{\overline{V}}
be the characteristic function of
\overline{V}.

The operator
A
will be defined as follows

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\smallint e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4}

\displaystyle(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}

\times 1_{\overline{V}}(x)|J(x,\omega)|^{1/2}\widehat{f}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}d\omega,

where
\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)
denotes the value of
\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi)
at
\xi=(\omega,1),
and

\psi:=\psi(x,\omega)
is a bump function to be chosen later. The integer quantities
\mu(x,\omega)
are such that they satisfy, according to the principle of stationary phase, the asymptotic expansion (see Proposition
2.9
)

\displaystyle\smallint e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\psi(x,\omega)d\omega=e^{2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime})}\psi(x,\omega^{\prime})e^{\mu(x,\omega^{\prime})\pi i/4}(\lambda^{\prime})^{-(n-1)/2}|J(x,\omega^{\prime})|^{-1/2}+\cdots,

when
|\xi^{\prime}|\gg 1.
Since
\mu(x,\omega)
are smooth functions in the region of integration they are constant on each connected component of this region.

Remark 4.8
.

When
\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))\equiv 0,
that is if
\Phi
is real-valued, the definition of
A
purely involves the phase function
\Phi_{\tau}
and the
curvature term

the

-boundedness of

follows from the argument for real-valued phase functions in Tao
[
46
, Pages 14-15]
.

Lemma 4.9
.

A
extends to a bounded operator on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
provided that there exists
\varkappa_{0}>0,
such that for all
(x,\theta)\in\overline{V}\times\overline{\{\theta:|\theta|\sim 1\}},
the lower bound

\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\theta))>\varkappa_{0},

holds.

Proof.

First, assume that
\widehat{f}(\xi)
is smooth and compactly supported and that
f
vanishes when
|\xi|\leq 1.
Note that the support of
\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})
is contained in the region
|\xi^{\prime}|\sim 2^{k}.

By applying summation by parts, because of the properties of the support of
f,
boundary terms vanish and we can write

\displaystyle Af(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\smallint_{|\xi^{\prime}|\sim 2^{k}}e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4}(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle\times|J(x,\omega)|^{1/2}\widehat{f}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}d\omega

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\smallint_{|\xi^{\prime}|\sim 2^{k}}e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4}(\phi_{-\varepsilon(k+1)}(J(x,\omega))-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))

\displaystyle\times\phi_{k}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}1_{\overline{V}}(x)|J(x,\omega)|^{1/2}\widehat{f}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}d\omega.

In view of the triangle inequality, it suffices to prove the estimate

(4.49)

\|A_{k}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

where

\displaystyle A_{k}f(x)

\displaystyle=-\smallint\smallint_{|\xi^{\prime}|\sim 2^{k}}e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4})(\phi_{-\varepsilon(k+1)}(J(x,\omega))-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))

\displaystyle\times\phi_{k}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}1_{\overline{V}}(x)|J(x,\omega)|^{1/2}\widehat{f}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}d\omega.

Let us denote by

\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(\xi^{\prime}):=\phi_{k}(\xi^{\prime})\chi_{\{\xi^{\prime}:|\xi^{\prime}|\sim 2^{k}\}}(\xi^{\prime}).

Then,

\displaystyle A_{k}f(x)

\displaystyle=-\smallint\smallint e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4})(\phi_{-\varepsilon(k+1)}(J(x,\omega))-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))

\displaystyle\times\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}1_{\overline{V}}(x)|J(x,\omega)|^{1/2}\widehat{f}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}d\omega.

Using the Fourier inversion formula, we have that

\displaystyle\smallint e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}1_{\overline{V}}(x)\widehat{f}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=1_{\overline{V}}(x)[e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,D))}\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(D)f](\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)).

Since
\textnormal{supp}[(\phi_{-\varepsilon(k+1)}(J(x,\omega))]
is contained in the set where
J(x,\omega)\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k},
the difference
\phi_{-\varepsilon(k+1)}(J(x,\omega))-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)),
is supported on the region
J(x,\omega)\sim 2^{-k\varepsilon}.
Since
|\phi_{k}(t)|\lesssim\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}},

\forall t>0,
we have that

|(\phi_{-\varepsilon(k+1)}(J(x,\omega))-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega))||J(x,\omega)|^{1/2}\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k/2},

and consequently

\displaystyle\|A_{k}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\smallint|A_{k}f(x)|dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\smallint\smallint|1_{\overline{V}}(x)[e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,D))}\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(D)f](\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega))|2^{-\varepsilon k/2}|\phi(x,\omega)|dxd\omega.

Note that the operator

\Omega(x,D)=1_{\overline{V}}(x)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,D))}\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(D)

is uniformly bounded in
k
on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Indeed, its kernel
\Omega(x,D)\delta(y),
given by

\Omega(x,D)\delta(y)=\smallint e^{2\pi i(x-y)\xi}1_{\overline{V}}(x)\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}d\xi

belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{x}^{n})
uniformly in
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Since the phase function
\Phi
is of strictly positive type, that is,
\textnormal{Im}(\phi)>0,
and
|\phi_{k}(\xi)|\lesssim 1,
note that

\displaystyle\smallint|\smallint e^{2\pi i(x-y)\xi}1_{\overline{V}}(x)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}d\xi|dx

\displaystyle=\smallint|\smallint_{|\xi|\sim 2^{k}}e^{2\pi i(x-y)\xi}1_{\overline{V}}(x)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(\xi)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi))}d\xi|dx

\displaystyle\leq\smallint 1_{\overline{V}}(x)\sup_{|\xi^{\prime}|\sim 2^{k}}e^{-\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}dx\smallint_{|\xi|\sim 2^{k}}|\phi_{k}(\xi)|d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim|\overline{V}|\sup_{x\in\overline{V}}\sup_{|\xi^{\prime}|\sim 2^{k}}e^{-\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}\smallint_{|\xi|\sim 2^{k}}d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim|\overline{V}|\sup_{x\in\overline{V}}\sup_{|\xi^{\prime}|\sim 2^{k}}e^{-2^{k}\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,2^{-k}\xi^{\prime}))}2^{kn}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\overline{V}|\sup_{x\in\overline{V}}\sup_{|\theta|\sim 1}e^{-2^{k}\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\theta))}2^{kn}<\infty.

Indeed, since
\textnormal{Im}(\phi)>0,
when
|\xi|\neq 0,
the compactness of the closed annulus
\overline{\{\theta:|\theta|\sim 1\}},
and of
\overline{V},
imply that there exists
\varkappa_{0}>0
such that for all

one has the lower bound

Therefore,

Thus, we have proved that
\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\Omega(x,D)\delta(y)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}<\infty,
and Schur’s lemma gives the boundedness of

on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Note also, that if

is an smooth change of coordinates on

is also bounded on

Moreover, since

by following the previous argument, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{y}\|\Omega(F^{-1}(z),D)\delta(y)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{z})}\lesssim|F(\overline{V})|\sup_{z\in F(\overline{V})}\sup_{|\theta|\sim 1}e^{-2^{k}\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(F^{-1}(z),\theta))}2^{kn}<\infty.

Now, since for any

is a diffeomorphism on

taking

we have that

\displaystyle\|A_{k}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\smallint_{|\omega|\leq R}\smallint|1_{\overline{V}}(x)[\phi_{k}^{\bullet}(D)e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,D))}f](\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega))|2^{-\varepsilon k/2}|\phi(x,\omega)|dxd\omega

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\phi(x,\omega)\|_{L^{\infty}}\smallint_{|\omega|\leq R}\smallint|\Omega(x,D)f(F_{\omega}(x))|2^{-\varepsilon k/2}dxd\omega

\displaystyle=\|\phi(x,\omega)\|_{L^{\infty}}\smallint_{|\omega|\leq R}\smallint|\Omega(F^{-1}_{\omega}(z),D)f(z)|2^{-\varepsilon k/2}|\det(DF_{\omega}^{-1}(z))|dzd\omega

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k/2}\smallint_{|\omega|\leq R}\|\Omega(F^{-1}_{\omega}(z),D)f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{z})}d\omega

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k/2}\sup_{|\omega^{\prime}|\leq R}\|\Omega(F^{-1}_{\omega^{\prime}}(z),D)f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{z})}\smallint_{|\omega|\leq R}d\omega

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-\varepsilon k/2}\sup_{|\omega^{\prime}|\leq R}|F_{\omega^{\prime}}(\overline{V})|\sup_{z\in F_{\omega^{\prime}}(\overline{V})}\sup_{|\theta|\sim 1}e^{-2^{k}\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(F_{\omega^{\prime}}^{-1}(z),\theta))}2^{kn}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=2^{-\varepsilon k/2}\sup_{|\omega^{\prime}|\leq R}|F_{\omega^{\prime}}(\overline{V})|\sup_{x\in\overline{V}}\sup_{|\theta|\sim 1}e^{-2^{k}\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\theta))}2^{kn}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\mathscr{K}}2^{-\varepsilon k/2}\sup_{|\omega^{\prime}|\leq R}|F_{\omega^{\prime}}(\overline{V})|\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Since

is

in
\omega,
and
\overline{V}
is compact,
\sup_{|\omega^{\prime}|\leq R}|F_{\omega^{\prime}}(\overline{V})|<\infty.
So we have the estimate

(4.50)

\|A_{k}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim 2^{-k\varepsilon/2}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

as desired proving the

-boundedness of
A.
The proof of Lemma
4.9
is complete.
∎

4.4.
Control of the error operator

In this subsection we prove that the error operator
E
is bounded on
L^{1}.
The idea behind the proof of this fact can be understood from the calculus of Fourier integral operators. The operator
A
is essentially a Fourier integral operator with the same phase function that
T,
in the sense that its phase
2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)
evaluated at the stationary point
\omega=\omega^{\prime}
is
2\pi i\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime}),
see
[
46
, Page 19, Eq. (16)]
. So, intuitively, if
A
would have had the form in (
1.1
), namely,
Af(x)=\smallint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi_{A}(x,\xi)}\sigma_{A}(x,\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi,
one could compute an asymptotic expansion for the symbol
\sigma(x,\xi)
of
SA
as follows

(4.53)

\sigma(x,\xi)=\smallint\smallint\sigma_{A}(x,\xi)s(x,\zeta)e^{2\pi i(\phi_{A}(y,\xi)-\phi_{A}(x,\xi)+(x-y)\cdot\zeta)}d\zeta dy,

and satisfying the asymptotic expansion, see e.g.
[
27
, Page 17]
,

(4.54)

\sigma(x,\xi)=s(x,\nabla\phi_{A}(x,\xi))\sigma_{A}(x,\xi)+\sum_{0<|\alpha|<N}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\sigma_{\alpha}(x,\xi)+r_{N}(x,\xi),

and the symbols
\sigma_{\alpha}
satisfying the estimates (under suitable conditions on the phase
\phi_{A}
)

|\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}\partial_{x}^{\gamma}\sigma_{\alpha}(x,\xi)|\lesssim_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(1+|\xi|)^{-\frac{n-1}{2}-(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon^{\prime})|\alpha|-\frac{|\beta|}{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\gamma|},

for any
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<1/2.

Below we will prove that essentially
T_{\textnormal{nondeg}}
and
SA
have the same principal symbol
s(x,\nabla\phi_{A}(x,\xi))\sigma_{A}(x,\xi),
and then the order
\tilde{\mu}
of the error operator

should be, at least heuristically, the order of the
\sigma_{\alpha}

that is
\tilde{\mu}\leq-\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon^{\prime}.
This order

is good enough to deduce the

-boundedness of
E.
However, since the definition of
A
also involves integration with respect to
\omega,
the structure of this operator is not as the one in (
1.1
), and one has to use the stationary phase method to prove that each dyadic component of the error

has a kernel with a

-norm satisfying uniformly in

a decay proportional to

Then, if proved this we are allowed to use Proposition 6.1 in
[
46
, Page 17]
to deduce the boundedness of

even under the presence of the oscillating term
e_{\Phi}(x,\xi)=e^{-(1+i\tau)\Phi(x,\xi)}.

First, let us calculate the kernel
E\delta_{z}(x)=T_{\textnormal{nondeg}}\delta_{z}(x)-SA\delta_{z}(x)
of the error operator

Note that

\displaystyle A\delta_{z}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\smallint e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)}\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4})(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\smallint e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)-2\pi iz\cdot\xi^{\prime}}\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4}(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))

\displaystyle\times\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}|J(x,\omega)|^{1/2}d\xi^{\prime}d\omega.

Consequently,

\displaystyle SA\delta_{z}(x)

\displaystyle=\smallint e^{2\pi ix\cdot\zeta}s(x,\zeta)\widehat{A\delta_{z}}(\zeta)d\zeta

\displaystyle=\smallint\smallint e^{2\pi i(x-y)\cdot\zeta}s(x,\zeta)A\delta_{z}(y)dyd\zeta

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\smallint\smallint\smallint e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(y,\omega)-2\pi iz\cdot\xi^{\prime}+2\pi i(x-y)\cdot\zeta}s(x,\zeta)\psi(y,\omega)e^{-\mu(y,\omega)\pi i/4}

\displaystyle\times(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(y,\omega)))\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(y,\xi^{\prime}))}|J(y,\omega)|^{1/2}d\xi^{\prime}d\omega dyd\zeta

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint\smallint\smallint\smallint e^{2\pi i[\xi^{\prime}\cdot(\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(y,\omega)-z)+(x-y)\cdot\zeta]}s(x,\zeta)\psi(y,\omega)e^{-\mu(y,\omega)\pi i/4}

\displaystyle\times(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(y,\omega)))\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(y,\xi^{\prime}))}|J(y,\omega)|^{1/2}d\xi^{\prime}d\omega dyd\zeta.

On the other hand, the kernel of
T_{\textnormal{nondeg}}
is given by

\displaystyle T_{\textnormal{nondeg}}\delta_{z}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint e^{2\pi i[\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime})-\xi^{\prime}\cdot z]}a(x,\xi^{\prime})e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega^{\prime})))\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\gg 1}\smallint e^{2\pi i[\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime})-\xi^{\prime}\cdot z]}s(x,\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime}))(\lambda^{\prime})^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle\times(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega^{\prime})))\eta_{k}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}.

In order to prove the
L^{1}
boundedness of

\displaystyle\|

In accordance with the principle of stationary phase, we now look at where the phase is stationary in
\omega.
From the homogeneity of the phase function we have that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{\overline{\xi}}\Phi_{\tau}(x,(\omega,1)),\partial_{\xi_{n}}\Phi_{\tau}(x,(\omega,1)))

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{\omega}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega),\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)-\omega\cdot\nabla_{\omega}\Phi(x,\omega)).

Hence

\displaystyle\nabla_{\omega}(\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega))

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\omega}(\lambda^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\omega}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)+\lambda^{\prime}(\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)-\omega\cdot\nabla_{\omega}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)))

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega).

So, the only stationary points occurs when
\omega=\omega^{\prime},
and the Hessian at this stationary point is given by

(4.57)

\det(\nabla_{\omega}^{2}(\xi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)))|_{\omega=\omega^{\prime}}=\det(-\lambda^{\prime}\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega))|_{\omega=\omega^{\prime}}=(-\lambda^{\prime})^{n-1}J(x,\omega^{\prime}).

Consequently, on the support of
\phi_{-(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)k}(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

\displaystyle 2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot(\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)-\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime}))=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{\prime}\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi(x,\omega^{\prime})(\omega-\omega^{\prime},\omega-\omega^{\prime})+O(2^{C\varepsilon}2^{-k/2}).

\displaystyle\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4}(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))|J(x,\omega)|^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\psi(x,\omega^{\prime})e^{-\mu(x,\omega^{\prime})\pi i/4}(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega^{\prime})))|J(x,\omega^{\prime})|^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(2^{C\varepsilon}2^{-k/2}),

\displaystyle s(x,\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime}))e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle\times\smallint_{\omega-\omega^{\prime}=O(2^{\varepsilon k}2^{-k/2})}e^{2\pi i\xi^{\prime}\cdot(\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\omega)-\nabla_{\xi}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime}))}\psi(x,\omega)e^{-\mu(x,\omega)\pi i/4}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega)))|J(x,\omega)|^{\frac{1}{2}}d\omega

\displaystyle=s(x,\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\tau}(x,\xi^{\prime}))e^{-(1+i\tau)\textnormal{Im}(\Phi(x,\xi^{\prime}))}\psi(x,\omega^{\prime})e^{-\mu(x,\omega^{\prime})\pi i/4}(1-\phi_{-\varepsilon k}(J(x,\omega^{\prime})))|J(x,\omega^{\prime})|^{\frac{1}{2}}

In view of the stationary phase principle

\displaystyle\smallint_{\omega-\omega^{\prime}=O(2^{\varepsilon k}2^{-k/2})}e^{\pi i\lambda^{\prime}\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\Phi(x,\omega^{\prime})(\omega-\omega^{\prime},\omega-\omega^{\prime})}\phi_{-(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)k}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})d\omega

\displaystyle W_{x,k,z}(\xi^{\prime})^{\mathscr{R}_{1}}

Since

In this way, the previous estimate says that, omitting the errors

is essentially a symbol of order

T_{\textnormal{deg}}
is bounded on
L^{1}.
As for the non-degenerate operator
T_{\textnormal{non-deg}},
we have constructed in Subsubsection
4.3.1
a Fourier integral operator
A,
which is an average operator, of the same order that
T
and a pseudo-differential operator
S
of order zero. We have proved, that the factorisation approach introduced in Tao
[
46
]
also operates in our case, namely, that
A
is bounded on
L^{1},
(see Proposition
4.9
)
S
is of weak (1,1) type in view of the standard Calderón-Zygmund theory, and that the error operator
E=T_{\textnormal{nondeg}}-SA
is bounded on
L^{1},
see Subsection
4.4
. Since
T=T_{\textnormal{deg}}+SA+E,
then
T
is of weak (1,1) type. The proof of Theorem
3.1
is complete.
∎

5.
Final remarks

5.1.
Applications

In order to illustrate the applications of Theorem
3.1
to
a priori
estimates for the Cauchy problem with complex characteristics we consider the case where the associated operator is a classical pseudo-differential operator of order one. Let us introduce some required notation. Let
(X,g)
be a compact Riemmanian manifold of dimension
n
and let us consider
T>0.
Denote by
\Delta_{g}
the positive Laplacian associated to the metric
g.

Denote the space
L^{1}_{s}(X)
defined by the norm

\|f\|_{L^{1}_{s}(X)}=\|(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}f\|_{L^{1}(X)}.

We also consider the
weak

L^{1}_{s}
-space defined by the seminorm

\|f\|_{L^{1,\infty}_{s}(X)}=\|(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}f\|_{L^{1,\infty}(X)},

and by
L^{1,\infty}_{s,\textnormal{loc}}(X),
its local version, namely the family of functions
g
such that for any compactly supported function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(X),

[(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}g]\phi\in L^{1,\infty}(X).

Denote
\partial_{t}
the partial derivative with respect to the time variable
t\in[0,T],
and let
D_{t}=-i\partial_{t}.
Let us consider the Cauchy problem for a first-order classical pseudo-differential operator
A,

(5.1)

\begin{cases}D_{t}v-Av=0,&(t,x)\in[0,T]\times X,\\
v(0,x)=f(x).&\end{cases}

We assume that its principal symbol
a(t,x,\xi),\,(t,x,\xi)\in[0,T]\times(T^{*}X\setminus\{0\}),
has non-negative imaginary part, namely,
a
satisfies

\textnormal{Im}[a(t,x,\xi)]>0,\,|\xi|\neq 0.

The solution operator
U(t)
of (
5.1
) can be realised as a Fourier integral operator of order zero with a global complex phase function of positive type, see
[
39
, Page 117]
and also Laptev, Safarov and Vassiliev
[
29
]
for details. Applying (essentially) Theorem
3.1
to the operator

(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}U(t)(1+\Delta_{g})^{-\frac{n-1}{4}-\frac{s}{2}}

of order
-(n-1)/2,
we have that for any compactly supported initial datum
f\in L^{1}_{s+\frac{n-1}{2}}(X)
, and for a fixed time
t\in[0,T],
the solution
v
of the Cauchy problem (
5.1
) satisfies
v(t,\cdot)\in L^{1,\infty}_{s,\textnormal{loc}}(X).
This analysis proves the following consequence of Theorem
3.1
.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R},
and let
f\in L^{1}_{s+\frac{n-1}{2}}(X)
be compactly supported. Then, for a fixed
t\in[0,T]
, the solution
v
of the Cauchy problem (
5.1
) satisfies
v(t,\cdot)\in L^{1,\infty}_{s,\textnormal{loc}}(X).

Proof.

We write
v(x,t)=U(t)f(x),
namely, the solution
v
of (
5.1
) in terms of the solution operator
U(t).
Since
f\in L^{1}_{s+\frac{n-1}{2}}(X),
we have that

(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{n-1}{4}+\frac{s}{2}}f\in L^{1}(X).

We can use the factorisation

\displaystyle(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}v=(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}U(t)(1+\Delta_{g})^{-\frac{n-1}{4}-\frac{s}{2}}(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{n-1}{4}+\frac{s}{2}}f,

and the fact that
(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}U(t)(1+\Delta_{g})^{-\frac{n-1}{4}-\frac{s}{2}}
is a Fourier integral operator with complex phase of order
-(n-1)/2
3
3

[
39
, Page 117]
and also Laptev, Safarov and Vassiliev
[
29
]
for details.
to prove that
v(t,\cdot)\in L^{1,\infty}_{s,\textnormal{loc}}(X).
Indeed, if
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denotes a partition of unity subordinated to a finite open covering
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of the compact manifold
X,
then
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}=1,
and
\textnormal{supp}[\phi_{i}]\subset O_{i}.
We can assume that the covering
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is an atlas for the manifold
X,
and then each
O_{i}
is diffeomorphic to an Euclidean open set
\tilde{O}_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

n=\dim(X).
Note that
(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}U(t)(1+\Delta_{g})^{-\frac{n-1}{4}-\frac{s}{2}}M_{\phi_{i}}
is a Fourier integral operator of order
-(n-1)/2
that can be microlocalised to a Fourier integral operator whose symbol is compactly supported in the spatial variable. Here,
M_{\phi_{i}}g=\phi_{i}g
denotes the multiplication operator by the function
\phi_{i}.
Then, Theorem
3.1
imply that for any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(X),
we have that

\displaystyle\|\psi(1+\Delta_{g})^{\frac{s}{2}}v\|_{L^{1,\infty}(X)}

5.2.
Conclusions and bibliographical discussion

In this work we have analysed the weak (1,1) boundedness of Fourier integral operators with complex-valued phase functions. As in the setting of real-valued phase functions regarding the weak (1,1) boundedness due to Tao in
[
46
]
, our estimate is local in the sense that we have considered the symbol
a
to be compactly supported in the
x
-variable, following the previous approaches, see Seeger, Sogge and Stein
[
44

, Beals
[
3
]
, and the monograph of the second author about the mapping properties of Fourier integral operators with complex phases
[
39

-(n-1)/2
for the weak (1,1) inequality of elliptic Fourier integral operators has been discussed by the authors in
[
9
]
, and we also refer to
[
38

for the sharpness of Seeger-Sogge-Stein orders regarding the
L^{p}
-boundeness of these operators.

There has been in the last decades a wide interest in the analysis of the global continuity properties for Fourier integral operators. This problem can be traced back to the work of Asada and Fujiwara
