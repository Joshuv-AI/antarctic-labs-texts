On odd parameters in geometry
Dimitry Leites

Abstract
1) In 1976, looking at the list of simple finite-dimensional complex Lie superalgebras, J. Bernstein and I, and independently M. Duflo, observed that some divergence-free vectorial Lie superalgebras have deformations with odd parameters and conjectured that no other simple Lie superalgebras have such deformations. Here, I prove this conjecture and overview the known classification of simple finite-dimensional complex Lie superalgebras, their presentations, realizations, and relations with simple Lie (super)algebras over fields of positive characteristic. 2) Any ringed space of the form (a manifold M M , the sheaf of sections of the exterior algebra of a vector bundle over M M ) is called split supermanifold. Gawȩdzki (1977) and Batchelor (1979) proved that every smooth supermanifold is split. In 1982, P. Green and Palamodov showed that a complex-analytic supermanifold can be non-split. So far, researchers considered only even obstructions to splitness. This lead them to the conclusion that any supermanifold of superdimension m | 1 m|1 is split. I’ll show there are non-split supermanifolds of superdimension m | 1 m|1 ; e.g., certain superstrings, the obstructions to their splitness depend on odd parameters.

1.3
Simple Lie superalgebras. First definitions and examples

First examples of Lie superalgebras, actually Lie super
rings
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
, appeared in 1941, in topology as the sets of homotopy groups with the Whitehead product, see
[
Wh
]

[

Weibel writes: “Homological algebra had its origins in the 19-th century, via the work of Riemann (1857) and Betti (1871) on “homology numbers”, and the rigorous development of the notion of homology numbers by Poincaré in 1895. An observation of Emmy Noether in 1925 shifted the attention to the “homology groups” of a space, and algebraic techniques were developed for computational purposes in the 1930’s. Yet, homology remained a part of the realm of topology until about 1945.”, see
[
We
, pp. 797–836]
.

The Lie superalgebras or super rings that thus appeared in topology over
{\mathbb{Z}}
or finite fields were nilpotent (hence, “not very interesting” and nobody tried to find out any simple (without ideals) examples), and did not draw much attention until it was discovered that the representations of the quantum groups

1.7
Deformations with odd parameters

Some of simple Lie superalgebras — over fields of any characteristic — have deformations thus resembling simple modular Lie algebras.

Here, I give a complete classification of deformations of simple finite-dimensional Lie superalgebras over
{\mathbb{C}}
, with proofs for the first time. In order to do this, I need to introduce the adequate definition of Lie superalgebra and deformations with odd parameters. Help of A. Lebedev in clarifying these notions was invaluable.

4
Deformations

4.1
Deformations and deforms. Odd parameters

Which of the infinitesimal deformations can be extended to
a global one is a separate much tougher question, usually solved

ad hoc
. Let me give two graphic examples.

1)
Deformations of representations
.
The tangent space of the moduli superspace of deformations of a representation
{\rho:{\mathfrak{g}}\longrightarrow{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)}

is isomorphic to
H^{1}({\mathfrak{g}};V\otimes V^{*})
. For example, if
{\mathfrak{g}}
is
the
0|n
-dimensional (i.e., purely odd) Lie superalgebra (with the
only bracket possible: identically equal to zero), its only
irreducible representations are the 1-dimensional trivial one,

\mathbbmss{1}
, and
\Pi(\mathbbmss{1})
, where

\Pi
is the
change of parity functor
, i.e.

\Pi(V)_{i}:=V_{i+{\bar{1}}}
for any superspace
V
. Clearly,

\mathbbmss{1}\otimes\mathbbmss{1}^{*}\simeq\Pi(\mathbbmss{1})\otimes\Pi(\mathbbmss{1})^{*}\simeq\mathbbmss{1},

and, because the Lie superalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
is
commutative
(the bracket of any two elements is identically equal to zero), the
differential in the cochain complex is zero. Therefore

H^{1}({\mathfrak{g}};\mathbbmss{1})\simeq{\mathfrak{g}}^{*},

so there is a
(0|\dim\,{\mathfrak{g}})
-dimensional space of odd parameters of deformations of the
trivial representation. If we consider
{\mathfrak{g}}
“naively”, all of
these odd parameters will be lost.

2)
Deformations of the brackets
.
Let
C
be a supercommutative superalgebra.

After
[
Ru
]
, where the non-super case is considered, we say that a
deformation
of
a Lie superalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
over
\mathop{\mathrm{Spec}}\nolimits C
, is a Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{G}}
such that
{\mathfrak{G}}\simeq{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes C
, as spaces. The deformation is
trivial
if
{\mathfrak{G}}\simeq{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes C
, as Lie superalgebras over
C
, not just as
C
-modules, and
non-trivial
otherwise.

Generally, the
deforms
— the results of deformations — of
a given Lie superalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
over
{\mathbb{K}}
are Lie superalgebras
{\mathfrak{G}}\otimes_{I}{\mathbb{K}}
, where
I
is any closed point in
\mathop{\mathrm{Spec}}\nolimits C
.

In particular, consider a deformation with an odd parameter
\tau
. This is a Lie superalgebra
{\mathfrak{G}}
isomorphic to
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes{\mathbb{K}}[\tau]
as a
superspace
. If, moreover,
{\mathfrak{G}}\simeq{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes{\mathbb{K}}[\tau]
as a
Lie superalgebra
, i.e.,

[a\otimes f,b\otimes g]=(-1)^{p(f)p(b)}[a,b]\otimes fg\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ for any $a,b\in{\mathfrak{g}}$ and $f,g\in{\mathbb{K}}[\tau]$},

then the deformation is considered
trivial
(and
non-trivial
otherwise). Observe that
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes\tau
is not an ideal of
{\mathfrak{G}}
: the ideal should be a free
{\mathbb{K}}[\tau]
-module.

Comment
. In a sense, the people who ignore odd parameters of deformations have a point: we (rather they) consider classification of simple Lie superalgebras (or whatever other problem) over the ground field
{\mathbb{K}}
, not over
{\mathbb{K}}[\tau]
, right? However, the odd parameters of deformations are no less natural, actually, than the odd part of the Lie superalgebra itself. In order to see these parameters, we have to consider whatever we are deforming not over
{\mathbb{K}}
, but over
{\mathbb{K}}[\tau]
.

We do the same, actually, when
\tau
is even and we consider formal deformations over
{\mathbb{K}}[[\tau]]
. If
the formal series in
\tau
converges in a domain
U
, we can evaluate
\tau
for any
\tau\in U
and consider copies
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\tau}
, where
\tau\in U
(of the same dimension as that of
{\mathfrak{g}}
if
\dim{\mathfrak{g}}<\infty
). If the parameter is formal or odd, such an evaluation is only possible trivially:
\tau\mapsto 0
.

5.2
The retract of the supermanifold

Let

be a supermanifold,
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{M}}

To any supermanifold

(8)

6
Finite-dimensional simple Lie superalgebras considered naively

Certain simple Lie superalgebras (or their relatives, like
{\mathfrak{gl}}(n|n)
,
{\mathfrak{pgl}}(n|n)
, and
{\mathfrak{sl}}(n|n)
) were easy to describe meaningfully, by some properties of these algebras. Even before a matrix realization of
{\mathfrak{osp}}(m|2n)
and its interpretation as a Lie superalgebra preserving a non-degenerate even bilinear form were discovered it was natural to call it “ortho-symplectic”, just by looking at its even part. Two series of simple Lie superalgebras were considered “strange” until the interpretations of their non-simple relatives as preserving a tensor was discovered, hence their first notation and certain awkward names (e.g., “di-spin algebra of Mitchell—Gell-Mann—Radicati”) were soon abandoned; certain confusing names —
F(4)
and
G(3)
instead of
ad hoc
but not confusing (with the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{f}}_{4}
and some object on the line of exceptional Lie algebras on the Vogel plane, see
[
MSV
]
, respectively) names given by their discoverer, Kaplansky) still remain in some papers. The notation “
{\mathfrak{psl}}
” was also being avoided for a long time, although it was already usual in the modular setting.

6.1
Linear (matrix) Lie superalgebras

Let
\text{Size}:=(p_{1},\dots,p_{|\text{Size}|})
, where
|\text{Size}|:=\dim V
, be an ordered
collection of parities of the basis vectors of a superspace
V
for which we take only vectors
homogeneous with respect to parity
. The
general linear
Lie
superalgebra of all supermatrices of size
Size
corresponding to linear operators in the superspace
V=V_{\bar{0}}\oplus V_{\bar{1}}
over the ground field
{\mathbb{K}}
is denoted by

{\mathfrak{gl}}(\text{Size})
.
Usually, for the
standard
(a simplest from a certain point of view) format,
{\mathfrak{gl}}({\bar{0}},\dots,{\bar{0}},{\bar{1}},\dots,{\bar{1}})
is abbreviated to
{\mathfrak{gl}}(\dim V_{\bar{0}}|\dim V_{\bar{1}})
. Any non-zero supermatrix in
{\mathfrak{gl}}(\text{Size})
can
be uniquely expressed as the sum of its even and odd parts; in the
standard format, this is the following block expression; on non-zero summands the parity is defined:

\text{\small$\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}$}=\text{\small$\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&D\end{pmatrix}$}+\text{\small$\begin{pmatrix}0&B\\
C&0\end{pmatrix}$},\quad p\left(\text{\small$\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&D\end{pmatrix}$}\right)={\bar{0}},\;p\left(\text{\small$\begin{pmatrix}0&B\\
C&0\end{pmatrix}$}\right)={\bar{1}}.

The
trace
on the Lie superalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
(or
supertrace
for emphasis) is any map
{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits:{\mathfrak{g}}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{K}}}
that vanishes on the first derived Lie (super)algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
. In particular, on
{\mathfrak{gl}}(\text{Size})
, there is just one (up to a non-zero scalar) such map; it is given by the formula

\mathop{\mathrm{str}}\nolimits(X):=\sum(-1)^{p_{i}}X_{ii}.

(12)

Thus, in the standard format,

\mathop{\mathrm{str}}\nolimits\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits A-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits D.

Observe that for the Lie superalgebra
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\mathcal{C}}(a|b)
over a supercommutative superalgebra
{\mathcal{C}}
, i.e., for supermatrices with homogeneous (with respect to parity) elements in
{\mathcal{C}}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathop{\mathrm{str}}\nolimits X=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits A-(-1)^{p(X)}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits D\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ for any $X=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}$,}\\
\text{where $p(X)=p(A_{ij})=p(D_{kl})=p(B_{il})+{\bar{1}}=p(C_{kj})+{\bar{1}}$},\\
\end{array}

(13)

so on odd supermatrices with entries in
{\mathcal{C}}
such that

Since
\mathop{\mathrm{str}}\nolimits[x,y]=0

There are, however, at least two super versions of

{\mathfrak{q}}(n):=\mathrm{C}(J)=\{X\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(n|n)\mid[X,J]=0\},\text{ where }J^{2}=-\mathop{\mathrm{id}}\nolimits.

It is clear that over an algebraically closed field, we can by a change of basis reduce

to the normal form (shape)

in the standard

and then the elements of

{\mathfrak{q}}(n):=\left\{(A,B):=\text{\footnotesize$\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B&A\end{pmatrix}$},\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ where $A,B\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(n)$ and $J_{2n}:=\text{\footnotesize$\begin{pmatrix}0&1_{n}\\
-1_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}$}$}\right\}.

(14)

(Over any

On

6.1.1
Supermatrices of operators

To a linear map of superspaces
{F:V\longrightarrow W}
there corresponds the
dual map
F^{*}:W^{*}\longrightarrow V^{*}
between the dual superspaces. In bases
consisting of homogeneous vectors
v_{i}\in V
of parity
p(v_{i})
, and
w_{j}\in W
of parity
p(w_{j})
, the formula

F(v_{j})=\mathop{\sum}_{i}w_{i}X_{ij}
assigns to
F
the
supermatrix
X
. In the dual bases, the
supertransposed

matrix
X^{st}
corresponds to
F^{*}
:

(X^{st})_{ij}:=(-1)^{(p(v_{i})+p(w_{j}))p(w_{j})}X_{ji}.

In the standard supermatrix format, we have

\footnotesize X=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\longmapsto X^{st}:=\begin{pmatrix}A^{t}&(-1)^{p(X)}C^{t}\\
-(-1)^{p(X)}B^{t}&D^{t}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}A^{t}&C^{t}\\
-B^{t}&D^{t}\end{pmatrix}&\text{if $p(X)={\bar{0}}$},\\
\begin{pmatrix}A^{t}&-C^{t}\\
B^{t}&D^{t}\end{pmatrix}&\text{if $p(X)={\bar{1}}$}.\end{cases}

6.1.2
Supermatrices of bilinear forms

Having selected a basis (by definition consisting of vectors homogeneous with respect to parity) of the superspace
V
, we define the Gram matrix
B=(B_{ij})
of the
bilinear form
B^{f}
on

by the formula

B_{ij}:=(-1)^{p(B)p(v_{i})}B^{f}(v_{i},v_{j})\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ for the basis vectors $v_{i}\in V$.}

(15)

This formula for the Gram matrix of

allows us
to identify any bilinear form

, where
V
and

are superspaces, with an operator, an element of
\mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits(V,W^{*})
, see
[

, Ch.1]
.

Recall that the

of bilinear forms

is given by the formula

u(B^{f})(w,v):=(-1)^{p(v)p(w)}B^{f}(v,w)\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ for any $v\in V$ and $w\in W$.}

(16)

Let now

, and
\mathop{\mathrm{Bil}}\nolimits(V):=\mathop{\mathrm{Bil}}\nolimits(V,V)
. The shape of the Gram matrix

of a homogeneous form

in the standard format of

is as follows

B^{u}:=\text{\small$\begin{pmatrix}R^{t}&(-1)^{p(B)}T^{t}\\
(-1)^{p(B)}S^{t}&-U^{t}\end{pmatrix}$}\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ for $B=\text{\small$\begin{pmatrix}R&S\\
T&U\end{pmatrix}$}$,}

(17)

The form

B^{f}
is said to be

if

, and

if

.
In particular, the form on

(resp.

) given by the respective trace is symmetric:

\begin{array}[]{l}(X,Y):=\mathop{\mathrm{str}}\nolimits(XY)\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ for any $X,Y\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)$}\\
(\text{resp. $(X,Y):=\mathop{\mathrm{qtr}}\nolimits(XY)$ for any $X,Y\in{\mathfrak{q}}(V)$)}.\end{array}

Clearly, the
upsetting

B\mapsto B^{u}
of Gram matrices of bilinear forms is
not
supertransposition.

Observe
that
the passage from

to

turns every symmetric

\text{${\mathcal{B}}^{\Pi}(\Pi(x),\Pi(y)):=(-1)^{p(B)+p(x)+p(x)p(y)}{\mathcal{B}}(x,y)$ for any $x,y\in V$}.

B^{f}(w,v):=(-1)^{p(v)p(w)}B^{f}(v,w)\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ for any $v,w\in V$}

(18)

which is sometimes given as their definition just reflects braiding in the category of superspaces and has nothing to do with the symmetry of bilinear forms. (Therefore, the title of

]

7.6.1
Induced
and coinduced modules

Let
{\mathfrak{h}}\subset{\mathfrak{g}}
be a Lie superalgebra
and its subsuperalgebra,

{\mathfrak{h}}
-module. The induced
and coinduced

-modules are constructed from

\mathop{\mathrm{Ind}}\nolimits^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{h}}(M):=U({\mathfrak{g}})\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{h}})}M,\quad\quad\mathop{\mathrm{Coind}}\nolimits^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{h}}(M):=\mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{U({\mathfrak{h}})}(U({\mathfrak{g}}),M).

-modules.

For any

-module

[

Note that for the Lie superalgebra

, where

, or

, or

, except for

which is generated by
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
.

For any
{\mathbb{Z}}
-graded simple vectorial Lie superalgebras

on

-module

-module

— sections of the vector bundle with fiber

{\mathfrak{g}}
-module

, let

denote the submodule of

-invariants. We have

(due to Losik) in Fuchs’ book

If this action is irreducible, then
L
is what Kac calls “classical”; it is isomorphic to one of the following:
B(m,n)
,
D(m,n)
,
F(4)
,
G(3)
,
Q(n)
or
D(2,1;\alpha)
. This is proved in
Theorem 2 on p. 44
. Proof is performed by means of the representation theory of semisimple Lie algebras.

If the
L_{\bar{0}}
-action on
L_{\bar{1}}
is reducible, we can take a maximal proper subalgebra
L_{0}\subset L
containing
L_{\bar{0}}
(there can be several such subalgebras, take any of them). The subalgebra
L_{0}
induces a filtration of
L
:

L=L_{-1}\supset L_{0}\supset L_{1}\supset\dots

Set
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L:=\oplus_{i\geq-1}\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{i}L
.

Then, (
Proposition 1.3.2, p. 24
)
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L
has the following properties:

1.
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L
is transitive (this is a direct corollary of simplicity of
L
);

2.
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading of
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L
is compatible with
{\mathbb{Z}}/2
-grading a.k.a. parity (this is a corollary of the fact
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{-1}L\subset\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L_{\bar{1}}
because
L_{0}\supset L_{\bar{0}}
).

3.
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L
is irreducible, i.e.,
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{0}L
irreducibly acts on
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{-1}L
(since
L_{0}
is maximal).

4.
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{1}L\neq 0
(since
L_{\bar{0}}
-action on
L_{\bar{1}}
is reducible).

Theorem 4, p. 71
claims that any
{\mathbb{Z}}
-graded Lie superalgebra satisfying the above conditions 1–4 is one of the following:

I.
A(m,n),C(n),P(n)
;

II.
W(n),S(n),\widetilde{H}(n):={\mathfrak{h}}(0|n),H(n)
;

III.
H^{\xi}=H\otimes\Lambda(\xi)+{\mathbb{K}}\cdot\partial_{\xi}
, where
H
is a simple Lie algebra, with the grading given by setting
\deg\xi=-1
.

IV.
{\mathfrak{g}}+{\mathbb{K}}\cdot d
, where
{\mathfrak{g}}
is of type I, II, III, the center of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\bar{0}}
is trivial and
d
is the grading operator.

Let the
L_{\bar{0}}
-action on
L_{\bar{1}}
be reducible. Then,
Theorem 4
implies
Theorem 5
by case-by-case checking of these four possibilities for
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L
.

III. If
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=H^{\xi}
, then
L
is not simple. To prove this Kac uses
Proposition 2.2.2, p. 35
. In this particular case, one can directly see why:
L_{0}=H\oplus{\mathbb{K}}\cdot\partial_{\xi}
(as a linear space). Hence,
L_{\bar{0}}=H
. Since
H
is a simple Lie algebra, it acts on the 1-dimensional space
{\mathbb{K}}\cdot\partial_{\xi}
by zero, so
L^{\prime}\neq L
.

IV. This case, and the case where
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=W(n)
or
A(m|n)
or
C(n)
, are dealt with by
Proposition 1.3.1, p. 24
, which claims

If for a finite-dimensional transitive filtered Lie superalgebra
L
the representation of
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{0}L
in
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{-1}L
is irreducible, and the even part of the center of
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{0}L
is non-zero, then
L\simeq\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L
.

This claim is practically obvious. Indeed, let us normalize the element
z
of the center of
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{0}L
so as to act as a grading operator on
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L
. Then, its pre-image in
L
is diagonalizable and defines a grading on
L
itself.

Thanks to this Proposition, the case IV does not contribute to the classification: the Lie superalgebra
L
is not simple, whereas if
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=W(n)
or
A(m|n)
or
C(n)
, then
L=W(n)
or
A(m|n)
or
C(n)
, respectively.

The case
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=P(n)
(as well as with many other cases) is dealt with by
Proposition 2.1.4, p. 34
which claims

If
L=L_{\bar{0}}\oplus L_{\bar{1}}
is a simple finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra such that the
L_{\bar{0}}
-action on
L_{\bar{1}}
is the same as that in one of the Lie superalgebras
A(m,n)
,
B(m,n)
,
C(n)
,
D(m,n)
where
(m,n)\neq(2,1)
,
F(4)
,
G(3)
,
P(n)
,
Q(n)
, then
L
is isomorphic to the corresponding Lie superalgebra.

The hypothesis of this Proposition is obvious if
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=P(n)
or
A(m|n)
or
C(n)
: indeed,
L_{0}=(L_{0})_{\bar{0}}\oplus(L_{0})_{\bar{1}}
, where
{(L_{0})_{\bar{0}}=\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{0}L=L_{\bar{0}}}
and
(L_{0})_{\bar{1}}=\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{1}L
. Since
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits_{0}L=L_{\bar{0}}
is reductive, any its finite-dimensional representation is completely reducible, in particular, the
L_{\bar{0}}
-action on
L_{\bar{1}}
.

The key point in the proof of
Proposition 2.1.4
is
Proposition 2.1.3
, which claims that for the Lie superalgebras in question, the
L_{\bar{0}}
-module
S^{2}(L_{\bar{1}})
contains
L_{\bar{0}}
with multiplicity 1.

The cases where
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=\widetilde{H}(n):={\mathfrak{h}}(0|n)
, or
H(n)={\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}(0|n)
are considered in
Proposition 3.3.7, p. 66
. Since
{\mathfrak{h}}(0|n)
is not simple, there remains only
H(n)
.

Finally, the case of
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=S(n)
is dealt with by
Theorem 3.3.5, p. 64
. In both these cases, the following two arguments are key ones.

The first is
Proposition 3.1.3, p.59
which claims that if

{L=L_{-1}\supset L_{0}\supset\dots}
is a transitive filtered Lie superalgebra such that
L_{0}\supset L_{\bar{0}}
and
{\dim L_{-1}/L_{0}=(0|n)}
, then
L
can be embedded into
W(n)
.

The second is the Levi-Malcev theorem for Lie algebras claiming that any semisimple subalgebra of the Lie algebra
L
can, by means of conjugations, be embedded into a fixed Levi subalgebra (i.e., the complement to the radical) of
L
. Hence, in the case
where
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=\widetilde{H}(n)
or
H(n)
, the Lie superalgebra
L
contains
{\mathfrak{o}}(n)
, whereas in the case where
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits L=S(n)
, the Lie superalgebra
L
contains
{\mathfrak{sl}}(n)
. The rest is a routine application of the representation theory of simple Lie algebras.
