Structured Nonsmooth Optimization Using Functional Encoding and Branching Information
Fengqiao Luo

Abstract
We develop a novel gradient-based algorithm for optimizing nonsmooth nonconvex functions where nonsmoothness arises from explicit nonsmooth operators in the objective’s analytical form. Our key innovation involves encoding active smooth branches of these operators, enabling both branch function extraction at arbitrary points and transition detection through branch tracking. This approach yields a Branch-Information-Driven Gradient Descent (BIGD) method for encodable piecewise-differentiable functions, with an enhanced version achieving local linear convergence under appropriate conditions. The computationally efficient encoding mechanism is straightforward to implement. The power of using branch information has been proved via substantial numerical experiments compared to some existing nonsmooth optimization methods on standard test problems. Most importantly, for piecewise-smooth problems given analytical expressions, implementation of functional encoding can be integrated into a wide range of existing nonsmooth optimization methods to improve the bundle points management, reduce the complexity of the quadratic programming sub-problems, and improve the efficiency of line search.



keywords
nonsmooth optimization, analytical structure exploitation, functional encoding, branching information

1
Introduction

We study the unconstrained optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x),

(1.1)

where
f
is continuous but potentially nonconvex and nonsmooth. While general nonsmooth functions may be non-differentiable on dense subsets, we focus on functions whose nonsmoothness stems from explicit nonsmooth operators, including
\max(\cdot)
,
\min(\cdot)
,
|\cdot|
,
(\cdot)_{+}
, and various rule-based operators that appear in
f
’s analytical form.
This important class of piecewise-differentiable functions covers numerous practical applications such as nonsmooth penalized objectives in signal processing and machine learning, piecewise nonlinear regression problems, control systems, and deep neural networks.
Throughout this work, we assume algorithms have direct access to
f
’s analytical form, enabling exploitation of its underlying piecewise structure.

1.1
Motivation

Existing methods for nonsmooth optimization include subgradient methods, bundle methods, trust region methods, gradient sampling methods, and quasi-Newton methods. These approaches typically treat the objective as a general non-differentiable function (e.g., locally Lipschitz continuous) where subgradient information is available almost everywhere. While this general framework makes the methods broadly applicable, it fails to fully exploit the analytical structure present in many practical problems of interest.

For the problem class in (
1.1
), each nonsmooth operator may induce multiple branch functions, potentially containing further nested nonsmooth operators. Crucially, we can numerically track the active branches of each nonsmooth operator to determine the locally smooth function at any point
x
, with the branching sequence being encodable. This suggests an algorithmic opportunity: by recording the active branch codes at previous iterates, could we develop more efficient optimization methods?

This paper systematically addresses this question through theoretical analysis, algorithmic development, and practical implementation. We demonstrate how branch-coding information can significantly enhance optimization efficiency for piecewise-smooth functions.

1.2
Review of related works

Bundle methods are in the category of the most efficient methods for nonsmooth optimization,
which were initiated in
Lemaréchal (1977)
, and have been developed to incorporate more ingredients
to increase stability and efficiency since then. The key idea of bundle methods is to utilize the subgradient
information obtained from previous iterations to build the current local model and determine the next searching direction.
A typical bundle method often consists of five major steps in an iteration: construction of the local model,
solving the local model to determine the searching direction, line search for getting an efficient while reliable step size,
checking the termination criteria and updating the set of bundle points for the next iteration
Bagirov
et al. (2014)
.
The quality and reliability of the local model, as well as the complexity of constructing it
play an essential role in determining the algorithm performance. To improve the quality
of the local model, primary bundle methods have been integrated with other methods to develop advanced
methods such as proximal bundle

Hintermüller (2001)
;
Kiwiel (2006)
;
Hare and
Sagastizábal (2010)
;
Sagastizábal (2013)
;
Hare
et al. (2015)
;
Monjezi and
Nobakhtian (2022)
,
quasi-Newton bundle
Lukšan and Vlček (1998)
;
Maleknia and
Shamsi (2022)
,
and trust-region bundle methods
Monjezi (2023)
, etc.
In a proximal bundle method, it manages a bundle of subgradients (often obtained from previous iterations)
to generate a convex piecewise linear local model, while a quadratic penalty term can be incorporated to control
the moving distance from the current point
Bagirov
et al. (2014)
.
Recently the proximal bundle algorithm has been generalized with different assumptions on the objective
function
Hare and
Sagastizábal (2010)
;
Dao
et al. (2016)
;
Hare
et al. (2015)
;
Monjezi and
Nobakhtian (2022)
;
Kiwiel (1996)
,
which extended the application domain of this type of methods.
Proximal bundle method has also been extended for handling nonsmooth, nonconvex constrained optimization

Dao (2015)
;
Monjezi and
Nobakhtian (2019)
;
Monjezi and
Nobakhtian (2021)
;
Lv

.
In Quasi-Newton bundle methods
Mifflin

,
BFGS updating rules are used to approximate the inverse Hessian matrix
for deriving the search direction. In trust-region bundle methods
Monjezi (2023)
,
the trust region management is used as an alternative to proximity control and
it is a tighter control on the step size.

Gradient (subgradient) sampling (GS) methods

Burke
et al. (2005)
;
Curtis and
Que (2015)
;
Kiwiel (2007)

are in another category of effective methodology for nonsmooth optimization.
The key difference between this type of methods and bundle methods
is that the algorithm proactively draws samples in a neighborhood of the current point to access the subgradient
information nearby. As an advantage, the sampling technique can help collecting sufficient subgradient information
for improving the accuracy of the local model. However, it also takes additional computational time for sampling and selecting
useful subgradients in each iteration.
Another side effect of a large sample size is that it leads to
a high-complexity constrained quadratic program as a local model at every iteration, which is the most time-consuming step
in a contemporary GS method. Inexact subproblem solutions are proposed to mitigate this shortcoming

Curtis and
Li (2021)
.
The motivation of sampling technique is based on the assumption that
the objective function can be non-differentiable at the current point. But if the point is in the domain of a smooth piece of the objective,
sampling is not necessary and it can save much computational effort. Since the algorithm is not informed by the macroscopic structural
information of the objective function, it will miss the opportunities of skipping the sampling procedure or reducing the sample size.
As a consequence, it is difficult to establish a uniform rule of determining the an adaptive sample size
based on the dimension of the problem and the performance of previous iterations.
Gradient sampling methods are often coupled with quasi-Newton methods
Lewis and
Overton (2013)

for utilizing the neighborhood subgradient information in building a high-quality quadratic local model

Curtis and
Que (2015)
.

An interesting research stream for nonsmooth nonconvex optimization in the recent decade
is based on the assumption that the analytical form of the objective function is given and the
non-differentiability is only caused by operators of
\max()
,
\min()
,
|\cdot|
and
(\cdot)^{+}
.
Given this information,

;

;

A local subproblem is parameterized by a binary vector indicating the branch of each nonsmooth operator.
First and second order optimality conditions can be derived for the constrained local problem

Walther (2016)
. Successive piecewise-linearization
algorithm has been developed for solving these local subproblems by exploiting the kink structure resulted
by the branches of nonsmooth operators

;

et al. (2016)
. The algorithm can

Linear rate of convergence can be achieved using exact second-order information under the linear independent
kink qualification (LIKQ)

Walther (2019)
.
There are a few shortcomings of this approach. First, adding auxiliary variables extends the dimension of
an instance and increases complexity.
Second, it requires intensive reformulation process and artificial intervention,
which makes it difficult to implement for automatically transforming the original problem and solving nonsmooth
optimization problems at scale.

On the rate of convergence, Han and Lewis
Han and
Lewis (2023)
proposed a survey
descent algorithm that achieves a local linear rate of convergence for the family of max-of-smooth functions
with each component function being strongly convex. Mifflin et al.

has developed a quasi-Newton bundle method for nonsmooth convex optimization. The method consists
of a quasi-Newton outer iterations and using bundle method as inner iterations, and it has been shown

to find a solution with a certain accuracy level required by the outer iterations.
Charisopoulos and Davis
Charisopoulos and Davis (in
press)

have recently developed an advanced subgradient method that achieves superlinear convergency
for objective functions satisfying sharp growth and semi-smooth conditions
defined in the paper. The convergence rate of proximal bundle methods has

;

We refer to the monograph

Encoding of piecewise-differentiable functions

We begin by giving a formal definition of piecewise-differentiable functions that
are analyzed in this paper.

Definition 2.1
.

A continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
piecewise-differentiable
if there exists a finite set of functions

\{f_{\theta}:\;\theta\in\Theta\}
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

The function
f_{\theta}:\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable on the open domain
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a
\theta\in\Theta
depending on
x
such that
x\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
and
f(x)=f_{\theta}(x)
.

3.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
f_{\theta}(x)=f(x)
, then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists an open set
C\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
such that

\overline{C}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(x,\epsilon)
,
f(y)=f_{\theta}(y)\;\forall y\in{C}
but

f(y)\neq{f}_{\theta^{\prime}}(y)\;\forall\theta^{\prime}\neq\theta\;\forall y\in{C}\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta^{\prime}}
, where
\mathbb{B}(x,\epsilon)
is the open ball with radius
\epsilon
centered at
x
.

4.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a small neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
\mathcal{M}(y)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x),\;\forall{y}\in{U}
.

5.

If
f
is differentiable at
x
, then
\nabla{f}(x)=\nabla{f}_{\theta}(x)
for every
\theta
satisfying
x\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}

and
f(x)=f_{\theta}(x)
.

For a piecewise-differentiable function
f
,

\Theta
is the set of
branches
,
f_{\theta}
is called the
branch function
indexed by
\theta\in\Theta
,

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x):=\{\theta\in\Theta:\;x\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}\}
is the set of
feasible branches
at
x
,
and
\mathcal{M}(x):=\{\theta\in\Theta:x\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta},\;f_{\theta}(x)=f(x)\}
is set of
active branches
at
x
.
The subset
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}:=\{x:\theta\in\mathcal{M}(x)\}
is called the
active domain
of the
branch
\theta
(assuming
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
).
Note that one has
\mathcal{M}(x)\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)
by definition.
The
multiplicity
of
f
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the cardinality of
\mathcal{M}(x)
.

To provide insights into Definition
2.1
, let us analyze its four five conditions:
Condition 1 ensures that each branch function is differentiable on its domain.
Condition 2 states that the value of
f
at
x
is determined by at least one branch function.
Condition 3 requires that every active branch at
x
has a
characteristic region
within
any arbitrarily small neighborhood of
x
. Here, a
characteristic region
of a branch
\theta

is the set where
\theta
is the only active branch.
This condition plays a crucial role in preventing branch redundancy. Specifically, if a branch
\theta\in\mathcal{M}(x)

violates this condition, there must exist an arbitrarily small open neighborhood
C
of
x
such that for
every
y\in{C}
, some other branch
\theta^{\prime}
(depending on
y
) satisfies
f_{\theta}(y)=f_{\theta^{\prime}}(y)
.
Intuitively, this means
\theta
is redundant near
x
, and its domain can be refined–by redefining

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}\leftarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}\setminus\overline{C}
–without altering the function’s behavior.
Condition 4 ensures that the set of active branches in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
x

can only be a subset of
\mathcal{M}(x)
. Without this condition, it is possible to construct a branch function
f_{\theta}

with domain
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
in the way that
x
is at the boundary of
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
but the function value
satisfies
\lim_{y\to{x}}f_{\theta}(y)=f(x)
and there exists a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
satisfying
\theta\in\mathcal{M}(x_{n})
.
In this case, the branch
\theta
has active points in any sufficiently small neighborhood of
x
, but
x
as the limit
point of
\{x_{n}\}
is not in
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
. This ill situation can be resolved by adjusting the definition of
f_{\theta}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
.
Condition 5 states that if
x
is at a characteristic region of a branch
\theta
, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}(x)=\{\theta\}
,
then
\nabla{f}(x)=\nabla{f}_{\theta}(x)
.

Definition 2.2

(encodablility)
.

A piecewise-differentiable function
f
is
encodable
if for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
set
\mathcal{M}(x)
of active branches is accessible and the gradient
\nabla{f}_{\theta}(x)
is accessible
for any
\theta\in\mathcal{M}(x)
.

We emphasize that encodability constitutes an algorithmic property of piecewise-differentiable functions,
which relies on having access to
f
’s analytical expression or numerical representation.
This property enables exploitation of
f
’s analytical structure.
Crucially, determining
\mathcal{M}(x)
requires knowledge of the branching rules for each nonsmooth operator in
f
.
In general, a piecewise-differentiable function cannot be encoded if only its function values are accessible.

Definition 2.3

(Clarke differential)
.

Given a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, for every
x
, the Clarke differential
\partial{f}(x)
is defined as:

\partial{f(x)}:=\mathrm{conv}(\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists\{x_{i}\}^{\infty}_{i=1}\;\textrm{s.t. }x_{i}\to{x},\;\nabla{f(x_{i})}\to{s}\}).

(2.1)

Assumption 2.1
.

Assume that each branch function
f_{\theta}
of the encodable function
(f,\mathcal{M})

is
L_{\Theta}
-smooth for a uniform Lipschitz coefficient
L_{\Theta}
.

Given an encodable function
(f,\mathcal{M})
, a point
x
and a nonempty subset
\mathcal{C}
of
\Theta
,
we will frequently refer to the following quadratic program:

\min_{\lambda}\;\left\lVert\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda_{\theta}\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\quad\textrm{s.t. }\;\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda_{\theta}=1,\quad\lambda_{\theta}\geq 0.

(QP-
x
-
\mathcal{C}
)

Denote
\mathrm{sol(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}
as the optimal solution
\lambda^{*}
of
(\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}})
,
and
\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}
as the optimal vector
d^{*}=\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda^{*}_{\theta}\nabla f_{\theta}(x)
.
This vector is referred as the
joint gradient
of branch functions in
\mathcal{C}
at the point

.
Joint gradients are intensively used in Section
4
to develop an algorithm that can achieve
linear convergence. A similar notion has been used in the gradient sampling method

Burke
et al. (2005)

In the following, we provide two examples to illustrate the notions defined above and the motivation
of functional encoding.

Example 2.1
.

Consider the function
f(x)=\max\big\{-x+1,\;\frac{1}{4}x,\;x-6\big\}

,

\theta=1,2,3

,

Our methods are particularly effective for problem instances where the number of branches
near local minimizers remains relatively small–specifically,
cases where the branch count does not grow exponentially with problem dimension.
For example, consider objectives of the form

).
In such cases, the

For the case that the multiplicity at

exponentially depends on

Furthermore, while the issue of exponentially many branch functions near minimizers poses a challenge
for all nonsmooth optimization methods, it manifests differently across approaches.
In conventional methods, this challenge remains implicit, whereas our method explicitly addresses it.
Crucially, our computational results show that even in such demanding cases–where the number
of branch functions grows exponentially near minimizers–our method consistently outperforms competing approaches.

3
A gradient descent method with branch information

We developed a branch-information-driven gradient descent method (BIGD)
to optimize an encodable piecewise-differentiable objective function.
The method is presented in Algorithm
1
.
The key innovation of our approach lies in dynamically maintaining a set
\Theta_{\mathrm{disc}}
of branches that have been visited
by the algorithm and a corresponding set
\mathcal{Z}^{n}=\{z^{n}_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{\mathrm{disc}}\}
of representative points in the
active domain of these branches. Here, each
z^{n}_{\theta}\in\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
serves as the unique representative
point for branch
\theta
at iteration
n
, which may be updated in subsequent iterations.
During optimization,
\Theta_{\mathrm{disc}}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{n}
are leveraged to generate a candidate set of
descent directions. These directions are then evaluated by a customized line-search method within our
framework to select the most promising descent direction and step size at each iteration
n
.

The algorithm consists of outer and inner iterations. The outer iteration (indexed by
n
in the algorithm)
generates the sequence
\{x_{n}\}
of points which converges to a Clarke stationary point. All inner iterations
(indexed by
k
in the algorithm) within each outer iteration are designed to test the efficiency of candidate descent directions.
There are three functions involved in an inner iteration:

,
GradientComputation
and

. The function

selects a subset
\mathcal{T}^{n}_{k}
of branches based on the distance from

to their representative points

.
The function

computes a candidate gradient ascent direction

.

The

Note that all the trial points generated in the line search are also used to update

is the main result of the section.
It shows that the sequence

generated by the algorithm converges to a Clarke stationary point.
We first present a few technical results that are used in proving this theorem.
The following proposition is a standard result of Taylor expansion for a same branch function.
The result is the foundation for the step size analysis.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any point
x
, any subset of branch functions
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)
,
let
d=\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}
. Then, for all
\theta\in\mathcal{C}
and step size
\alpha
satisfying
x-\alpha d\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}

the following inequality holds:

f_{\theta}(x)-f_{\theta}(x-\alpha d)\geq\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{2}L_{\Theta}\alpha^{2}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2},

(3.2)

where
L_{\Theta}
is the Lipschitz constant associated with the branch functions.

Proof.

This is a direct application of the fact
\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta}(x),d\right\rangle\geq\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}
for any
\theta\in\mathcal{C}

from Proposition
A.1
, and the Taylor expansion and
L_{\Theta}
-smoothness.
∎

The following proposition concerns the ratio between
f(x)-f(x-\alpha{d})
and
\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}
.
Note that the active branch at
x-\alpha{d}
may be different from the one at
x
. This subtlety
needs to be considered in the analysis.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a non-empty subset of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)
at any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and let
d=\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}
satisfying
\left\lVert d\right\rVert>0
.
Suppose
\theta_{1}\in\mathcal{M}(x)\cap\mathcal{C}
and
\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(x-\alpha{d})

for a step size

If
\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
and
f_{\theta_{1}}(x)\geq{f}_{\theta_{2}}(x)
,
the following inequality holds:

\frac{f(x)-f(x-\alpha{d})}{\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha{L_{\Theta}}.

(3.3)

Proof.

By definition we have

\displaystyle\frac{f(x)-f(x-\alpha{d})}{\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}=\frac{f_{\theta_{1}}(x)-f_{\theta_{2}}(x-\alpha{d})}{\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{f_{\theta_{2}}(x)-f_{\theta_{2}}(x-\alpha{d})}{\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha{L_{\Theta}},

where Proposition
3.1
is applied to derive the last inequality.
∎

Our convergence analysis points out that just using the current active branch functions to compute
a gradient ascent direction at each
x^{n}
can not guarantee a lower-bounded step size in the line search.
As a consequence, this approach cannot make
\{x^{n}\}
converge to a Clarke stationary point.
Instead, we should take into account branches with
z^{n}_{\theta}
that are very close to
x^{n}
but are not
active at
x^{n}
, i.e., the branches with the representative point of visit in some small neighborhood of
x^{n}
.
This motivates us to consider the quadratic program (
3.4
). The following lemma
quantifies the quality of its solution when
z_{\theta}
’s are very close to the point
x
.
The result of this lemma is directly used in analyzing the step size.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(f,\mathcal{M})
be an encodable function,
x
be a point with
\left\lVert\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{M}(x)}\mathrm{)}\right\rVert>0
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{M}(x)
and
z_{\theta}\in\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
for all
\theta\in\mathcal{C}
.
Let
\lambda
be the optimal solution of the following quadratic program

\min_{\lambda}\left\lVert\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda_{\theta}\nabla f_{\theta}(z_{\theta})\right\rVert^{2}\;\mathrm{s.t.}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda_{\theta}=1,\;\lambda_{\theta}\geq 0\;\forall\theta\in\mathcal{C},

(3.4)

and
d=\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda_{\theta}\nabla f_{\theta}(z_{\theta})
.
Then for any constant
0<\rho<1
, there exist constants
\alpha_{0},\epsilon_{0}>0
such that
the following inequality holds if
\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}
and
\max_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\left\lVert z_{\theta}-x\right\rVert<\epsilon_{0}
:

\frac{f(x)-f_{\theta}(x-\alpha d)}{\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}\geq\rho\quad\forall\theta\in\mathcal{C}.

(3.5)

Proof.

Let
d_{0}=\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}
. Note that
\left\lVert d_{0}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{M}(x)}\mathrm{)}\right\rVert>0
.
Consider any
\theta\in\mathcal{C}
. Using the Taylor expansion and Lipschitz condition
we can derive

\displaystyle f(x)-f_{\theta}(x-\alpha d)\geq\alpha\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta}(x),d\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta}(z_{\theta}),d\right\rangle-\alpha\left\lVert\nabla f_{\theta}(x)-\nabla f_{\theta}(z_{\theta})\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert d\right\rVert-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta}(z_{\theta}),d\right\rangle-\alpha{L_{\Theta}}\left\lVert x-z_{\theta}\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert d\right\rVert-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}-\alpha{L_{\Theta}}\left\lVert x-z_{\theta}\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert d\right\rVert-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2},

where the last inequality follows from Proposition

.
By Proposition
A.2
, we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{\theta^{\prime}}\to x\\
\forall\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\end{subarray}}\left\lVert d\right\rVert=\left\lVert d_{0}\right\rVert.

(3.7)

Using (
3.7

3.6

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{\theta^{\prime}}\to x\\

(3.8)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{\theta^{\prime}}\to x\\
\forall\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\end{subarray}}\frac{f(x)-f_{\theta}(x-\alpha d)}{\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}\geq\frac{\alpha\left\lVert d_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\alpha\left\lVert d_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}=1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha L_{\Theta}.

(3.9)

Therefore, the following inequality holds for some small enough

if

and
\epsilon_{0}
are sufficiently small

\left\lVert z_{\theta^{\prime}}-x\right\rVert<\epsilon_{0}\;\forall\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\frac{f(x)-f_{\theta}(x-\alpha d)}{\alpha\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha{L_{\Theta}}-\delta>\rho,

4
An enhanced algorithm and analysis of the convergence rate

In this section, we develop and analyze an enhanced branch-information-driven
gradient descent algorithm (EBIGD). See Algorithm
2
for details.
This algorithm can achieve local linear convergence under certain conditions.
To analyze the rate of convergence, we consider ecodable piecewise-smooth functions that are locally-max representable
defined as follows:

Definition 4.1

(locally-max representability)
.

An encodable function
(f,\mathcal{M})
is
locally-max-representable
in an open neighborhood
A

of a local minimizer
x^{*}
if for any
x\in{A}
, the objective
f
has the representation
f(x)=\max\{f_{\theta}(x):\theta\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)\}
.

A notion that is similar to Definition
4.1
has been used in
Han and
Lewis (2023)
(Definition 3) to analyze the convergence rate
of a survey descent method. We make the following two additional assumptions throughout the section:

Assumption 4.1
.

Every branch function is twice differentiable in its domain of definition, i.e., the Hessian matrix

\nabla^{2}{f}_{\theta}(x)
exists for any
x\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
and
\theta\in\Theta
.

Assumption 4.2
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is possible for an algorithm to verify if a given branch
\theta
is in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)
.

The above assumption is used in Algorithm
2
.
The motivation of it is to verify if the current point
x
is in the definition domain

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
of a branch
\theta
that is not active at
x
.
If
x\notin\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
, the function value
f_{\theta}(x)
and gradient
\nabla{f}_{\theta}(x)

are not well defined, and hence cannot be used in the algorithm. However, in a small enough
neighborhood
A
of
x^{*}
, all the active branches in this region belong to
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)
for any
x\in{A}
.

It can be shown that in a small neighborhood of a local minimizer
x^{*}
,
the distance from a point
x
to
x^{*}
is bounded by
\left\lVert\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{M}(x^{*})}\mathrm{)}\right\rVert+\mathrm{Gap}(x,\mathcal{M}(x^{*}))

up to a constant factor, where
\mathrm{Gap}(x,\mathcal{C})=\max_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}f_{\theta}(x)-\min_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}f_{\theta}(x)

for any set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)
of branch functions. Therefore, it amounts to reduce the norm
of the joint gradient and the gap. However, the set
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
is not fully known
at a point
x\neq{x^{*}}
, which is a challenge to the algorithm design.

Based on the above motivation, the EBIGD algorithm works as follows: At each iteration, the algorithm collects
a set
\mathcal{C}
of branch functions sequentially through the function
BranchSelection
.
It first initializes
\mathcal{C}
to just include an active
branch at the current point
x
, and computes a corresponding joint gradient
d=\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}
.
Using a sufficiently small constant step size
\alpha
, it then checks if the function value
f(x-\alpha{d})

has a sufficient decrease compared to
f(x)
. If sufficient decrease is not
met while
x-\alpha{d}
is in an active branch
\theta^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{C}
, the algorithm
adds
\theta^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{C}
and computes a new joint gradient.
This procedure is repeated until the sufficient decrease is satisfied.
The function
BranchSelection
returns an eventual set
\mathcal{C}
of branches
encountered in this process and the corresponding joint gradient
d
.
It can be shown that the set
\mathcal{C}
contains a complete basis (Definition
4.2
)
of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
if
x
is in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
x^{*}
.
Using this information, the algorithm then executes two functions
JointGradientReduction

and
GapReduction
separately to compare which one leads to larger function value reduction.
The
JointGradientReduction
returns a candidate point
x-\alpha{d}
, and it can be proved that
the norm
\left\lVert\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}\right\rVert
of the joint gradient will decrease if the solution
x
moves to this candidate point.
The function
GapReduction
is designed to reduce the gap
\mathrm{Gap}(x,\mathcal{C})
by solving a
convex program involving a simple quartic term to determine a descent direction.

A local minimizer
x^{*}
is Clarke stationary. In general, it is possible to find a proper subset
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})

such that
\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathrm{conv}(\{\nabla{f}_{\theta}(x^{*}):\;\theta\in\mathcal{C}\})
. In this case,
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
is more than necessary
to characterize the Clarke stationarity of
x^{*}
. This motivates the definition of a complete basis as follows:

Definition 4.2
.

For an encodable function
(f,\mathcal{M})
and a local minimizer
x^{*}
,
A subset
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
is a
complete basis
of branch functions if
it satisfies the following two conditions:

(a)

\left\lVert\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x^{*}}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}\right\rVert=0
;

(b)

either
|\mathcal{C}|=1
or
\left\lVert\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x^{*}}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\mathrm{)}\right\rVert>0
for any
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subsetneq\mathcal{C}
.

In words, a complete basis is a minimal subset
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
satisfying
\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathrm{conv}(\{\nabla{f}_{\theta}(x^{*}):\;\theta\in\mathcal{C}\})
.

Observation 4.1
.

If
x^{*}
is a local minimizer of
f
, there exists at least one complete basis of branch functions at
x^{*}
.
Furthermore, in a sufficiently small neighborhood
A
of
x^{*}
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\left\lVert\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\mathrm{)}\right\rVert>c
for any
x\in{A}
and any
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
that does not contain a complete basis of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
.

The following observation is useful in gap reduction analysis.

Observation 4.2
.

Given
\{\nabla f_{i}(x^{*}):i\in\mathcal{M}(x^{*})\}
for a local minimizer
x^{*}
, there exists a constant
c>0

such that for any complete basis
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
at
x^{*}
with
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 2
,
and any vector
v\in\mathrm{span}\{\nabla f_{i}(x^{*}):i\in\mathcal{C}\}
with
\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1
,
there exist
i,j\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\left\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{*}),v\right\rangle\geq{c}

and
\left\langle\nabla f_{j}(x^{*}),v\right\rangle\leq{-c}
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
is a subset that contains a complete basis. Consider
the subspace
S=\mathrm{span}\{\nabla{f}_{\theta}(x^{*}):\theta\in\mathcal{C}\}
. If the dimension of
S

is smaller than
n
, we need to impose a regularity condition
on the behavior of function
f_{\theta}(x^{*}+\alpha{v})
for
v\in{S}^{\perp}
and
0<\alpha<\ll 1
,
which is needed for linear convergence. This regularity condition is defined as follows:

Definition 4.3

(complementary positive-definite condition)
.

The complementary positive-definite condition is satisfied at a local minimizer
x^{*}
of an encodable function

(f,\mathcal{M})
if for any subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
that contains a complete basis at
x^{*}
,
the inequality
v^{\top}\nabla^{2}f_{\theta}(x^{*})v>0
holds for every
\theta\in\mathcal{C}

and all
v\in{V}^{\perp}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
, where
V=\mathrm{span}\{\nabla{f}_{\theta}(x^{*}):\theta\in\mathcal{C}\}
.

The necessity of the complementary positive-definite condition can be seen from an extremal case.
Suppose
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})=\{\theta\}
. It follows that
f
behaves like a smooth branch function
f_{\theta}

in a small neighborhood of
x^{*}
. One can verify that the complementary positive-definite condition
reduces to
\nabla^{2}f_{\theta}(x^{*})\succ{0}
in this case, and the later one is a well known sufficient condition
that ensures an algorithm can be designed to achieve local linear convergence.

Definition 4.4

(dimension consistency condition)
.

The dimension consistency condition is satisfied at a local minimizer
x^{*}
of an encodable function

(f,\mathcal{M})
if there exists a complete basis
\mathcal{C}
at
x^{*}
such that

\mathrm{span}\{\nabla f_{\theta}(x^{*}):\theta\in\mathcal{C}\}=\mathrm{span}\{\nabla f_{\theta}(x^{*}):\theta\in\mathcal{M}(x^{*})\}
.

The following proposition provides a complexity analysis of the function
BranchSelection
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(f,\mathcal{M})
be an encodable locally-max-representable function and
x^{*}
be a local minimizer
of
f
. Let
x
be a point in a small neighborhood of
x^{*}
.
For a sufficient small step size
\alpha
,
the while-loop in the function
BranchSelection
(
x,\alpha,\rho_{0}
)

of Algorithm
2
terminates in a bounded number of iterations,
and the set
\mathcal{C}
of branches returned by this function satisfies
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
.

Proof.

Since the definition domain
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
is an open set for each branch function
f_{\theta}
.
By selecting a
r>0
such that
\mathbb{B}(x^{*},r)\subseteq\cap_{\theta\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x^{*})}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\theta}
,
one can guarantee that
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x^{*})
for every
x\in\mathbb{B}(x^{*},r)
.
Since
x
is in a small neighborhood of
x^{*}
,
\alpha
is sufficiently small
and
\left\lVert d_{i}\right\rVert\leq{G_{f}}
, by the condition 4 of Definition
2.1
, one has

\mathcal{M}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
and
\mathcal{C}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
for every iteration
i

in the function
BranchSelection
.
It implies that after at most
|\mathcal{M}(x^{*})|
iterations
in the while-loop of
BranchSelection
, the following
condition should be satisfied:
\theta_{i+1}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{i}
with
i\leq|\mathcal{M}(x^{*})|
.

Since
\theta_{i},\theta_{i+1}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
d_{i}=\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}_{i}}\mathrm{)}
,
one has
\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta_{i}}(x),d_{i}\right\rangle\geq\left\lVert d_{i}\right\rVert^{2}
and

\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta_{i+1}}(x),d_{i}\right\rangle\geq\left\lVert d_{i}\right\rVert^{2}
by Proposition
A.1
.
Let
\theta_{1}
be the active branch at

.
If
\left\lVert d_{i}\right\rVert=0
, the loop ends, otherwise, one has

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{f(x)-f(x-\alpha{d_{i}})}{\alpha\left\lVert d_{i}\right\rVert^{2}}=\frac{f_{\theta_{1}}(x)-f_{\theta_{i+1}}(x-\alpha{d_{i}})}{\alpha\left\lVert d_{i}\right\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{f_{\theta_{i+1}}(x)-f_{\theta_{i+1}}(x-\alpha{d_{i}})}{\alpha\left\lVert d_{i}\right\rVert^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}{\alpha}L_{\Theta},

where we use the property of a locally-max-representable function to get the first inequality.
It indicates that

if

\alpha<2(1-\rho_{0})/L_{\Theta}
.
This shows that the while-loop should terminate within

iterations.
∎

Suppose

is in a small neighborhood of
x^{*}
and let
d=x-x^{*}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a complete basis of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})

returned by

.
Note that

. The
GapReduction
is designed to reduce the contribution
of
\mathrm{Proj}_{V}d
in the objective value in the case that
\left\lVert\mathrm{Proj}_{V}d\right\rVert=O(\left\lVert d\right\rVert)
.
The quartic convex program (
4.1

recover
\mathrm{Proj}_{V}d
. Specifically, if
(z^{*},s^{*})
is an optimal solution of (

),
the linear expression

approximates the first-order Taylor expansion of

near

,
where
z^{*}\approx{f^{*}}
and
s^{*}\approx\mathrm{Proj}_{V}d
.
Since
\left\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),s^{*}\right\rangle\approx\left\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),s^{*}+u\right\rangle
for a

,
a quartic term
\left\lVert s\right\rVert^{4}
is added to the convex program to penalize component in

that lies in
{V}^{\perp}
.
Intuitively, moving along

should reduce the objective value contributed by
\mathrm{Proj}_{V}d
.
This result is formally shown in the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

(f,\mathcal{M})
be an encodable locally-max-representable function and

be a local minimizer
of

. Let

be a point in a neighborhood of

. Let

and

be an optimal solution of

\min_{z\in\mathbb{R},s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}(z+\left\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),s\right\rangle-f_{i}(x))^{2}+\left\lVert s\right\rVert^{4},

(4.1)

where
\mathcal{C}
is a subset of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
that contains a complete basis.
Then the optimal solution satisfies
\left\lVert s^{*}\right\rVert\leq{O}(\left\lVert d\right\rVert)
and
|z^{*}-f^{*}|\leq{O}(\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2})
as

.
Furthermore, the following inequality holds for any

is nonempty:

f(x)-f(x-\gamma{s^{*}})\geq\gamma\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta}(x^{*}),d\right\rangle+O(\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2})\quad\mathrm{as}\;\;x\to{x^{*}},

(4.2)

where
\theta

\displaystyle\left\langle g_{\theta_{3}},d\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle g_{\theta_{3}},\mathrm{Proj}_{V_{0}}d\right\rangle+\left\langle g_{\theta_{3}},\mathrm{Proj}_{V^{\perp}_{0}}d\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq a_{3}\left\lVert\mathrm{Proj}_{V_{0}}d\right\rVert,

it follows that
\left\lVert\mathrm{Proj}_{V_{0}}d\right\rVert\leq{O}(\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2})
, and hence

|\left\langle g_{i},d\right\rangle|\leq\left\lVert g_{i}\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert\mathrm{Proj}_{V_{0}}d\right\rVert\leq{O}(\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2})
for every
i\in\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
.
This concludes the proof of the claim.

It is ready to prove the theorem. Since the step size
\alpha

used in
BranchSelection
is small enough, by Proposition
4.1
,
the set
\mathcal{C}
returned by this function satisfies
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
.
Then there are two possible cases:
Case 1.
\mathcal{C}
does not contain a complete basis of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
; Case 2. It contains a complete basis of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
.
In Case 1, using Observation
4.1
, we conclude that
\left\lVert\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}\right\rVert>c
for a constant
c>0
.
Then by the termination criterion of
BranchSelection
, the reduction of objective value for this iteration
must be greater than
\rho_{0}\alpha{c^{2}}
, which is a constant. In this case, the convergence is better than linear rate.

We now analyze Case 2. First, using Taylor expansion the function value satisfies

f(x)-f^{*}\leq\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2},

(4.15)

where
\theta
is an active branch at the current point
x
and
d=x-x^{*}
.
We consider two sub-cases.

Case 2.1.
\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle\geq{M}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}

for some large enough constant
M>0
. Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the set of branches
after completing Step 2 in
GapReduction
.
(Note that Step 2 can be finished in a bounded number of iterations due to the finiteness of
\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
.)
It follows that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{M}(x-\gamma{s^{*}})
is nonempty.
Since
\mathcal{C}
contains a complete basis due to the hypothesis of Case 2, so does
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
We can then apply Lemma
4.1
to the set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and conclude that
the function-value reduction obtained from the
GapReduction
has the following lower bound:

f(x)-f(x-\gamma{s^{*}})\geq\gamma\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle-c_{0}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2},

for some constant
c_{0}\geq 0
.
Then it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{f(x)-f(x-\gamma{s^{*}})}{f(x)-f^{*}}\geq\frac{\gamma\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle-c_{0}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle+L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}/2}\geq\frac{\gamma\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle/2}{\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle+L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}/2}\geq\frac{\gamma}{4},

where we use the hypothesis that
M
is large enough.

Case 2.2
\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle\leq{M}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}
. Using Claim 2, we conclude
that
|\left\langle g_{i},d\right\rangle|\leq{O}(\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2})
for every
i\in\mathcal{M}(x^{*})
.
In particular, we have
\left\lVert\mathrm{Proj}_{V}d\right\rVert\leq{O}(\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2})
where
V=\mathrm{span}\{g_{i}:i\in\mathcal{C}\}
by Claim 2.
Let
d^{\prime}=\mathrm{vec(}\mathrm{QP}\textrm{-}{x}\textrm{-}{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{)}
.
Applying Lemma
4.2
gives
\left\lVert d^{\prime}\right\rVert\geq\sigma\left\lVert d\right\rVert

for some constant
\sigma>0
. The function-value reduction obtained from the

JointGradientReduction
has the following lower bound:

f(x)-f(x-\alpha{d^{\prime}})\geq\rho_{0}\alpha\left\lVert d^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}\geq\rho_{0}\alpha\sigma^{2}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}.

Therefore, the reduction ratio is lower bounded by:

\frac{f(x)-f(x-\alpha{d^{\prime}})}{f(x)-f^{*}}\geq\frac{\rho_{0}\alpha\sigma^{2}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\langle g_{\theta},d\right\rangle+L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}/2}\geq\frac{\rho_{0}\alpha\sigma^{2}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}}{M\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}+L_{\Theta}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}/2}\geq\frac{\rho_{0}\alpha\sigma^{2}}{M+L_{\Theta}/2}.

We therefore have proved that the reduction of function value in Case 2 is in linear rate.
Combining Case 1 and Case 2 concludes the proof.
∎

Numerical investigation

A practical implementation of the branch-information-driven gradient descent method is
is given in Algorithm
3
, which has been used in the numerical study.
This implementation mostly aligns with the gradient sampling algorithm developed in

Burke
et al. (2005)
. The difference is on the
bundle points collection and line search. Specifically, in each iteration,
the bundle points used in Algorithm
3
for gradient computation are
representative points of visited branches within a ball,
whereas those in
Burke
et al. (2005)
are randomly sampled within a ball.
The line search in Algorithm
3
employs a custom analysis tailored for this work,
whereas that in
Burke
et al. (2005)

relies on the standard Armijo condition, which is less effective for piecewise-differentiable objectives.
We consider 9 standard test problems from
Haarala
et al. (2004)

with 6 different dimensions:
n=25,50,75,100,125,150,175,200
in our experiments.
Their definition and properties are summarized in Table
3
.

We compare the performance of our branch-information-driven gradient descent method
with other three methods for non-smooth optimization developed in literature.
The methods and their notations are listed as follows:

-

BIGD: the branch-information-driven gradient descent method (Algorithm
3
)
with parameters
\epsilon_{0}=0.1
,
\nu_{0}=1.0\times 10^{-3}
,
\gamma=0.5
,

\epsilon_{\textrm{opt}}=1.0\times 10^{-5}
,
\nu_{\textrm{opt}}=1.0\times 10^{-4}
,
\theta_{\epsilon}=0.1
,
\theta_{\nu}=0.9

and
\rho=1.0\times 10^{-2}

-

GS: the gradient sampling method
Burke
et al. (2005)

-

TRB: a trust-region bundle method
Monjezi (2023)

-

QNS: a quasi-Newton method with neighorhood sampling
Curtis and
Que (2015)
.

For the methods GS, TRB and QNS, the parameters are selected following the corresponding references.
Algorithm
3
and all algorithms used in the comparison
are implemented in Julia, gradient calculation is via the automatic differentiation package ForwardDiff
Revels et al. (2016)
,
and the solver for quadratic programs called in each algorithm is OSQP
Stellato et al. (2020)
.
The code can be accessed via the following link:

https://github.com/luofqfrank/CID-JGD.git

for reproduction. All computations were performed on a system equipped with a 2.3 GHz Quad-Core Intel Core i7 CPU and 32GB of memory.
The computational time limit per problem instance is 5 minutes.
Overall, the experiments results reveal that the BIGD method has the following three advantages compared to other methods:

-

Reduced number of function and gradient evaluations:
This results from the efficient representation of visited branches, particularly compared to sampling-based methods.

-

Faster quadratic program (QP) solving per iteration (especially for high-dimensional problems):
Each bundle point in the QP uniquely represents a visited branch near the current iterate.
The QP size depends only on the number of local branches—not on dimension or sample size.

-

More effective and informative line-search: This property is implied by Proposition
3.2
. If a trial point belongs to a
branch
\theta
that is included in the set
\mathcal{T}
bundle point the search direction computed based on
\mathcal{T}
has incorporated the
information of
\nabla{f_{\theta}}(x^{k})
which can lead to a quality decrease of the objective. If
\theta\notin\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{T}
can be updated to include
\theta
, refining the QP and search direction until the condition
\theta\in\mathcal{T}
is satisfied.

It is worth to remark that utilization of branch information can be integrated into any existing nonsmooth optimization method that is
based on neighborhood sampling and bundle points to benefit from the above three advantages when applied to encodable piecewise-smooth
functions. The BIGD method is just an example demonstrating the power of using the branch information.

The initial points used in the comparison are specified in

.
For each problem instance, if the method terminates within 5 mins, the computational time is reported.
Similarly, Table

gives the comparison under randomly generated initial points (but consistent used for all methods).
For both cases, the BIGD are not terminated by the time limit in only 2

instances for the other three methods. Within terminated instances, the computational time of BIGD is systematically lower than the other three methods.
When the termination criterion is met, the BIGD method consistently obtains an optimality gap

at the scale of

which is superior to the GS and TRB methods. For the setting of random initial points (Table

For the setting of recommended initial points (Table

in computational time, number of iterations and optimality gap. In particular, the QNGS does not perform well
on some higher dimensional problem instances, i.e.,
n\geq 75
.
Given that BIGD is a first-order method while QNGS is a second-order method,
these results further demonstrate the remarkable advantage of incorporating branch information in optimization.
Figure

is the visualization of the optimality gap versus the computational time corresponding
to the four methods, plotted for 8 selected problem instances of dimension
n=200
. The figure shows that the BIGD
leads to the fastest decrease of objective values in the last 6 problem instances, while the BIGD is on par with the QNGS in
the first 2 problem instances.

Table

provides a breakdown analysis of average computational time spending on two key steps of

compared among the BIGD, GS and QNGS methods. The comparison between the BIGD and GS methods
clearly shows the three advantages of using the branch information: The BIGD spends 1
\sim
2 magnitude
less computational time on function evaluation, solving the quadratic program and execution of each iteration.
The BIGD also shows systematically advantage over the QNGS on the iteration time, function evaluation time
and QP-solving time with a few exceptions, but the advantage is not as big as when compared with the GS method.
This similarity arises because BIGD and GS share identical procedural structures and both are first-order methods,
implying that BIGD’s advantage stems solely from its incorporation of branch information.
In contrast, QNGS employs a second-order approach with BFGS updates,
which inherently provides more sophisticated optimization capabilities than first-order methods.
Moreover, QNGS selectively performs gradient sampling only when the objective function fails to achieve sufficient decrease.
Consequently, the benefits of branch information utilization in BIGD are counterbalanced by QNGS’s advanced algorithmic framework.

We can get more insights on the mechanism of the BIGD method by investigating the dynamics of the bundle size
|\mathcal{T}|

which is the number of effective branches used in the QP (
6.1
) to generate a search direction,
and the number
|\Theta_{\mathrm{disc}}|
of visited branches in the algorithm.
Figure
5
shows the plots of
|\mathcal{T}|
and
|\Theta_{\mathrm{disc}}|
versus the iteration index for 7 problem instances
of dimension
n=200
. In each plot, the blue line and red line respectively represent the number of
effective branches used in the QP at each iteration and number of visited branches by the end of each iteration,
with the scale of the two lines displayed on the left and right axis respectively.
We observed that the number of effective branches fluctuates periodically within a certain range which is
much smaller than and does not increase with the accumulated number of visited branches, indicating that
the number of effective branches within the neighborhood of the current point is relatively stable.
The periodic pattern of fluctuation reveals a cycle of neighborhood exploration and progressing.
During the increasing phase of
|\mathcal{T}|
, the algorithm explored the current neighborhood with small step sizes
to encounter and collect all the ‘blocking’ branches
(branches that restrict the direction of making a large enough step) in several iterations.
At a moment, all blocking branches were collected and included in
\mathcal{T}
,
an efficient search-direction was identified, and a substantial decrease of objective
was achieved, which corresponds to the sudden drop of
|\mathcal{T}|
, concluding the progressing phase of a cycle.

6
Conclusion

This paper demonstrates the effectiveness of functional encoding and branch information
in designing algorithms for piecewise-smooth optimization.
Numerical results provide strong evidence that incorporating branch information reduces
the computational complexity of solving quadratic programs for search direction generation
while enhancing the efficiency and informativeness of line-search procedures.
Future research could explore the integration of branch information into existing
nonsmooth optimization methods to further improve their performance.
Additionally, methodological extensions to different problem settings warrant further investigation.
For instance, if certain branch functions take a stochastic form, such as
f_{\theta}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\psi(x,\xi)]
,
the evaluation of function values and gradients becomes stochastic.
This stochasticity introduces new challenges and opportunities,
potentially enriching both algorithmic design and convergence analysis.

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

Proposition A.1
.

For the set
\{v_{i}\}^{k}_{i=1}
of
k
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, consider the quadratic programming problem

\min_{\lambda}\left\lVert\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}v_{i}\right\rVert^{2}\;\mathrm{s.t.}\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}=1,\;\lambda_{i}\geq 0\;\forall i\in[k].

(A.1)

Let
\lambda^{*}_{i}
be an optimal solution of (
A.1
)
and
d^{*}=\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{*}_{i}v_{i}
.
Then the inequality
\left\langle v_{i},d^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert^{2}
holds for each
i\in[k]
.

Proof.

Let
C=\mathrm{conv}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
and then
d^{*}
can is the projecting point of
\boldsymbol{0}

on the convex set
C
. Using the projection theorem on a convex set, any point

x\in{C}
can be represented as
x=d^{*}+u
with
\left\langle u,d^{*}\right\rangle\geq 0
.
Therefore, one has
\left\langle x,d^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert^{2}
, and in particular,

\left\langle v_{i},d^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert^{2}
for all
i\in[k]
.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\{v_{i}\}^{k}_{i=1}
,
\lambda^{*}
and
d^{*}
be defined the same as in Proposition
A.1
.
Suppose for each
i\in[k]
, the is a sequence
\{u^{j}_{i}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
of vectors satisfying

\lim_{j}u^{j}_{i}=v_{i}
. Let
\lambda^{j}
be an optimal solution of the quadratic program (
A.1
)
after replacing
v_{i}
with
u^{j}_{i}
for
i\in[k]
, and let
d^{j}=\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{j}u^{j}_{i}
. Then
the following limit holds:
\lim_{j}\left\lVert d^{j}\right\rVert=\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert
.

Proof.

We can derive an upper bound of
\|d^{j}\|
as follows:

\displaystyle\|d^{j}\|

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{j}_{i}u^{j}_{i}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{*}_{i}u^{j}_{i}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{*}_{i}v_{i}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{*}_{i}u^{j}_{i}-\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{*}_{i}v_{i}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert+\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{*}_{i}\left\lVert u^{j}_{i}-v_{i}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert+\max_{i}\left\lVert u^{j}_{i}-v_{i}\right\rVert,

where the first inequality follows from the optimality of
d^{j}
.
On the other hand,
\|d^{j}\|
can be lower bounded as:

\displaystyle\|d^{j}\|

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{j}_{i}u^{j}_{i}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{j}_{i}v_{i}\right\rVert-\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{j}_{i}\left\lVert u^{j}_{i}-v_{i}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert-\sum^{k}_{i=1}\lambda^{j}_{i}\left\lVert u^{j}_{i}-v_{i}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert-\max_{i}\left\lVert u^{j}_{i}-v_{i}\right\rVert,

where the second inequality is due to the optimality of
d^{*}
.
Since
\lim_{j}u^{j}_{i}=v_{i}
, the above two inequalities imply
\lim_{j}\|d^{j}\|=\left\lVert d^{*}\right\rVert
.
∎
