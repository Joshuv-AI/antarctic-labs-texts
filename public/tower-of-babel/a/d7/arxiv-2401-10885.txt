Thermodynamic limit for the magnetic uniform electron gas and representability of density-current pairs
Mihály A. Csirik and Andre Laestadius, Erik I. Tellgren

Abstract
. Although the concept of the uniform electron gas is essential to quantum physics, it has only been defined recently in a rigorous manner by Lewin, Lieb and Seiringer. We extend their approach to include the magnetic case, by which we mean that the vorticity of the gas is also held constant. Our definition involves the grand-canonical version of the universal functional introduced by Vignale and Rasolt in the context of current-density-functional theory. Besides establishing the existence of the thermodynamic limit, we derive an estimate on the kinetic energy functional that also gives a convenient answer to the (mixed) current-density representability problem.



1.
Introduction

Following
[
14
]
, we view the uniform electron gas (UEG) as a continuous system of (interacting or not) electrons with prescribed constant density everywhere.
In the non-interacting case,
one can readily write down the ground state of the UEG via a fully occupied Fermi sphere. One can consider the classical UEG by neglecting the kinetic energy of the
electrons
[
14
]
.

In contrast, the Jellium is a continuous system of interacting electrons in a constant neutralizing potential
[
21
]
. In the classical case, it was
recently shown that the Jellium and the UEG energies are the same
[
15
]
. In the quantum case, the problem is still open. We will solely focus on the UEG in this article.

The article
[
14
]
pioneered the use of universal density functionals of density-functional theory (DFT) as a theoretical tool in quantum statistical mechanics, namely
for the
definition
of the UEG. In a later article
[
16
]
, the same authors established an even more elegant (and equivalent)
definition of the UEG, and we will restrict ourselves to this latter approach.

When magnetic fields are present, the situation is much more delicate
[
5
]
. Even the noninteracting UEG is more difficult to analyze
in the magnetic case
[
4
,
25
,
26
]
, even though the ground state can be given exactly.
The celebrated Laughlin wave function has also received attention
from the mathematics community
[
28
,
27
]
, which is a rather precise trial state (with almost constant density in the thermodynamic limit)
for the Jellium in a constant magnetic field.

2.
Setting

2.1.
States

In the first half of this section, we will fix the particle number
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
The
N
-particle fermionic Hilbert space
is the antisymmetric power

\mathfrak{H}^{N}=\bigwedge^{N}\mathfrak{H}
(endowed with the usual inner product), where
\mathfrak{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

is the
one-particle Hilbert space
. Note that
\mathfrak{H}^{1}=\mathfrak{H}
.

Generally speaking, a density matrix (or state) on a Hilbert space is self-adjoint, non-negative trace class operator that has trace one. We first consider the Hilbert space to be the fermionic
N
-particle space
\mathfrak{H}^{N}
.
To make notations more compact, we henceforth restrict our attention to states with finite kinetic energy, which we call
H^{1}
-regular states
.
The
set of
H^{1}
-regular (fermionic)
N
-particle states
is defined as

\mathcal{D}_{N}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \Gamma&\in\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\mathfrak{H}^{N}):\Gamma=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\mu_{j}\Gamma_{\Psi_{j}},\;\text{for some}\;\mu_{j}\geqslant 0\;\text{with}\;\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\mu_{j}=1,\,\text{and}\\
&\{\Psi_{j}\}\subset\mathfrak{H}^{N}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{dN})\,\text{is}\,L^{2}\text{-orthonormal with}\;\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\mu_{j}\|\bm{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\|^{2}<\infty\end{aligned}\right\},

where
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
the trace class.
Then clearly
0\leqslant\Gamma=\Gamma^{\dagger}
and
\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{N}}\Gamma=1
for all
\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
. It is not hard to show that the operator
\Gamma
can be given by the Hermitian integral kernel

\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\times\mathbb{R}^{dN}\to\mathbb{C}
(denoted with the same symbol),

\Gamma(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\mu_{j}\Psi_{j}(\mathbf{X})\overline{\Psi_{j}(\mathbf{Y})},

so that its action on an element
\Phi\in\mathfrak{H}^{N}
is given by
\Gamma\Phi(\mathbf{X})=\int\Gamma(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})\Phi(\mathbf{Y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y}
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathfrak{H}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a nonnegative quadratic form with
domain in
\mathfrak{H}^{N}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{dN})
, and let
A:\mathfrak{H}^{N}\to\mathfrak{H}^{N}
be its self-adjoint realization. Then for any
\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, we define

\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{N}}A\Gamma:=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\mu_{j}\mathcal{A}(\Psi_{j}),

which is a nonnegative number or
+\infty
. Choosing
A=-\bm{\Delta}
, i.e.
\mathcal{A}(\Psi)=\|\bm{\nabla}\Psi\|^{2}
, we find that
\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{N}}(-\bm{\Delta}\Gamma)=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\mu_{j}\|\bm{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\|^{2}<\infty

for any
\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
.

Next, we consider states on Fock space. For convenience, we introduce the notation
\mathfrak{H}^{0}=\mathbb{C}
. The
fermionic Fock space

\mathfrak{F}
is then the Hilbert space given by direct sum of the Hilbert spaces
\mathfrak{H}^{n}
,
\mathfrak{F}=\mathfrak{H}^{0}\oplus\mathfrak{H}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{H}^{2}\oplus\ldots
endowed with the usual inner product.
A
Fock space state

{\bm{\Gamma}}
is a positive self-adjoint, trace-class operator on
\mathfrak{F}
with unit trace:
{\bm{\Gamma}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\mathfrak{F})
,
0\leqslant{\bm{\Gamma}}={\bm{\Gamma}}^{\dagger}
and

\Tr_{\mathfrak{F}}{\bm{\Gamma}}=1
.
We will not need this generality, however, and it will suffice to consider states
{\bm{\Gamma}}
which commute with the number operator.
It is easy to show by induction that such states are
block-diagonal:

{\bm{\Gamma}}=\Gamma_{0}\oplus\Gamma_{1}\oplus\ldots,

where
\Gamma_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\mathfrak{H}^{n})
is such that
0\leqslant\Gamma_{n}=\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}
.
Note in particular that
\Gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and that the normalization constraint now reads
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\Tr_{\mathfrak{F}}\Gamma_{n}=1
.
Again, we will further restrict attention to states with finite kinetic energy, which we called
H^{1}
-regular states. All this leads to the definition

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\begin{aligned} {\bm{\Gamma}}=\Gamma_{0}\oplus\Gamma_{1}\oplus\ldots\in\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\mathfrak{F}):&\,\Gamma_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\mathfrak{H}^{n})\,\text{and}\,0\leqslant\Gamma_{n}=\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}\,\text{for all}\,n\geqslant 0,\\
&\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\Tr\Gamma_{n}=1\;\text{and}\;\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{n}}(-\bm{\Delta}\Gamma_{n})<\infty\end{aligned}\right\}

of the set of
H^{1}
-regular block-diagonal Fock space states
. Each
\Gamma_{n}
may be given by an integral kernel
\Gamma_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n};\mathbf{Y}_{n})
,
like before (of course these will not be normalized to have unit trace, in general).
Note also that by linearity, the one-, and the two-particle reduced density matrices of
{\bm{\Gamma}}=\Gamma_{0}\oplus\Gamma_{1}\oplus\ldots\in\mathcal{D}
are given by

\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}}=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\gamma_{\Gamma_{n}},\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma^{(2)}_{\bm{\Gamma}}=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\gamma^{(2)}_{\Gamma_{n}}.

We note in passing that all derived quantities, such as
\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}}
, may be defined by linearity.

2.2.
One-particle quantities

In this section, we introduce various quantities which may be defined using the one-particle density matrix.
The
one-particle reduced density matrix
\gamma\in\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\mathfrak{H})
of
\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
is defined as (
N
times) the partial trace of
\Gamma
via duality:

\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}}(\gamma B)=N\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{N}}(\Gamma(B\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\mathfrak{H}^{N-1}}))\quad\text{for all}\quad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{H}).

In symbols, this operation is sometimes written as
\gamma=N\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{N-1}}\Gamma
. Note the choice of normalization, so that we have
\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}}\gamma=N
.
It is easy to see that
\gamma
can be given by the kernel

\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N-1)}}\Gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N};\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{2}\cdots\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{N}.

It is also clear that
\gamma
is a trace class operator on the one-particle Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}
such that
0\leqslant\gamma=\gamma^{\dagger}\leqslant\mathbb{1}
.
By the spectral theorem
\gamma
has the decomposition

\gamma=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}\ket{\varphi_{j}}\!\!\bra{\varphi_{j}}
,
which in terms of its kernel can be written as

\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\overline{\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{y})}

for some
0\leqslant\lambda_{j}\leqslant 1
with
\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}=N
and
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j\geqslant 1}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

L^{2}
-orthonormal.
It is clear that
\gamma
has finite kinetic energy, i.e. it is
H^{1}
-regular:
\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}\|\bm{\nabla}\varphi_{j}\|^{2}<\infty
.

The
density

\rho_{\gamma}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the one-particle density matrix
\gamma
may also be defined via duality,
i.e. by requiring that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\gamma}\varphi=\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}}(\gamma\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This gives

\rho_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}|\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}.

The kinetic energy
\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}}(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma):=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}\|\bm{\nabla}\varphi_{j}\|^{2}<\infty
may be expressed using the kernel
\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
as

\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{y}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Here, the integrand
\tau_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}):=\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{y}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}
is called the
kinetic energy density
, and more generally

{\bm{\tau}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}):=\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{y}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}\bm{\nabla}\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\otimes\overline{\bm{\nabla}\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})}

the
kinetic energy tensor
. Clearly,
0\leqslant{\bm{\tau}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})={\bm{\tau}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})^{\dagger}
and
\Tr_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\bm{\tau}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\tau_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})
.

A further useful quantity is the
complex current density

{\bm{\zeta}}_{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
, which is given by

(1)

{\bm{\zeta}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}):=\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}\bm{\nabla}\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\overline{\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})}.

A straightforward calculation shows that

(2)

\Real{\bm{\zeta}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nabla}\rho_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}):=\Imag{\bm{\zeta}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\Imag\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}},

where
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called the
paramagnetic current density
.

The following theorem is based on
[
3
,
29
,
13
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
\gamma
is fermionic one-particle density matrix composed of sufficiently smooth orbitals. Then the following hold true.

(i)

|\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}+\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}}{\rho(\mathbf{x})}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\left|\mathbf{D}_{a}\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})}{\rho_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})}\right|\leqslant\tau_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})
for a.a.
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(ii)

|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})|\leqslant|{\bm{\zeta}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})|\leqslant\tau_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})^{1/2}\rho_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})^{1/2}
for a.a.
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, hence
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
.

Here, the symmetric-, and antisymmetric derivatives
\mathbf{D}_{s}
and
\mathbf{D}_{a}
will be used at some occasions, given by

\mathbf{D}_{s}\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u})^{\top}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{D}_{a}\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}-(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u})^{\top}}{2},

for any differentiable vector field
\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is its derivative. Further, we denote by
\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{B}=\Tr(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{B})
the Frobenius inner product, and by
|\mathbf{A}|=\sqrt{\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{A}}

the Frobenius norm.

Let us consider the case
d=3
. Then
\left|\mathbf{D}_{a}\mathbf{u}\right|=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}|\rot\mathbf{u}|
and part (i) improves the usual Hoffmann–Ostenhof bound
[
10
]
as

(3)

\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma)\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}|^{2}}{\rho_{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\gamma}\left|{\bm{\nu}}_{\gamma}\right|,

for any
H^{1}
-regular density matrix
\gamma
, where
{\bm{\nu}}_{\gamma}=\rot\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}
is the (gauge-invariant, see below)
vorticity
.
We call the terms on the r.h.s. the
von Weizsäcker-
, the
gauge
-, and the
vorticity term
, respectively.
Note that
Eq.

3
implies that for
H^{1}
-regular density one-particle matrices
\gamma
, we have
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}|^{2}<\infty
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}|^{2}}{\rho_{\gamma}}<\infty

and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\gamma}\left|{\bm{\nu}}_{\gamma}\right|<\infty
, quantities which play important roles later. Finally, we note that for
d=3
the constant in front of vorticity term in
Eq.

3
can be improved to 1
[
29
]
.

Remark 1
.

Part (ii) of
Proposition

2.1
implies that the support of
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}
is contained in the support of
\rho
, in particular, the quotients
\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}}{\sqrt{\rho}}
and

\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}
are meaningful on
\supp\rho
. Moreover, if
\tau\equiv 0
in some ball, then by (i),
\rho
is constant and

\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}\equiv 0
on that ball.

2.3.
Gauge transformation

We now discuss a very important tool in the study of magnetic systems.
Given an
N
-particle state
\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, consider the
gauge-transformed
state

\widetilde{\Gamma}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=e^{i\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}(g(\mathbf{y}_{j})-g(\mathbf{x}_{j}))}\Gamma(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

where
g
is called the
gauge function
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\gamma
be a fermionic one-particle density matrix. Then the gauge transformation of
\gamma
is given by

\widetilde{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=e^{i(g(\mathbf{y})-g(\mathbf{x}))}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

In particular,
\rho_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=\rho_{\gamma}
.
Furthermore,
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}\bm{\nabla}g
and

(4)

\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\widetilde{\gamma})=\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma)-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}\cdot\bm{\nabla}g+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\gamma}|\bm{\nabla}g|^{2}.

We did not specify what function space the gauge function
g
is coming from. In order for the
r.h.s. of
Eq.

4
to make sense, it is enough to assume that
\bm{\nabla}g\in L_{\rho_{\gamma}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
, using the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality and

Eq.

3
.

Note that the gauge term
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}
transforms exactly like the kinetic energy
Eq.

4
. More precisely,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}|^{2}}{\rho_{\widetilde{\gamma}}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}|^{2}}{\rho_{\gamma}}-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}\cdot\bm{\nabla}g+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\gamma}|\bm{\nabla}g|^{2},

therefore the inequality
Eq.

3
is “consistent” with a gauge transformation in the sense that all the terms involving
g
will cancel.
We call such inequalities
gauge-invariant
.

2.4.
Representability

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that the set of
pure (or mixed) state
N
-representable densities
may be written as

\mathcal{I}_{N}=\Bigg\{\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+}):\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d},\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho=N\Bigg\}.

according to
[
9
]
and
[
19
, Theorem 1.2]
. For later purposes, we also introduce the set of
Fock space-representable densities
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\left\{\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+}):\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\right\}.

For density-paramagnetic current density pairs, the situation is more complicated. Consider the set

\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\mathrm{pure}}=\Bigl\{(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):\rho=\rho_{\Psi},\,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\Psi},\;\text{for some}\;\Psi\in\mathfrak{H}^{N}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{dN}),\;\|\Psi\|=1\Bigr\}

of all
pure state
N
-representable density-current pairs
, and

\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\mathrm{mixed}}=\{(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):\rho=\rho_{\Gamma},\,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\Gamma},\;\text{for some}\;\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{N}\},

the set of
mixed state
N
-representable density-current pairs
.
In this more general setting it is yet unknown whether
\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\mathrm{pure}}
is equal to
\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\mathrm{mixed}}
or not.
Unfortunately, no explicit characterization of either
\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\mathrm{pure}}
or
\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\mathrm{mixed}}
exists up to date. We can specify a larger set by setting

(5)

\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\prime}=\Bigg\{(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):\rho\in\mathcal{I}_{N},\;\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d},\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}<\infty\Bigg\},

so that by
Eq.

3
,
\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\mathrm{pure}},\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\mathrm{mixed}}\subset\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\prime}
.
It can be shown that
\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\prime}
is also convex
[
12
]
. We remark that the finiteness of the vorticity term in
Eq.

3

may also be added to the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\prime}
.

Whether any
(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})\in\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\prime}
can be represented with a Slater determinant
\Phi\in\mathfrak{H}^{N}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
such that
\rho_{\Phi}=\rho
and
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\Phi}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}

(the
determinantal density-current
N
-representability problem
)
is still not settled completely. The following result gives a partial answer, however.

Theorem 2.3
.

(Lieb–Schrader
[
23
]
)
Let
d=3
and suppose that
(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})\in\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\prime}
and set
{\bm{\nu}}=\rot\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}}{\rho}
. Then the following holds true.

(i)

(Zero vorticity) If
{\bm{\nu}}=0
, then there exists a Slater determinant
\Phi\in\mathfrak{H}^{N}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, such that
\rho_{\Phi}=\rho
,
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\Phi}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}
and

\|\bm{\nabla}\Phi\|^{2}\leqslant C_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho}|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}

for some constant
C_{N}>0
.

(ii)

If
N\geqslant 4
, then there exists a Slater determinant
\Phi\in\mathfrak{H}^{N}
, such that
\rho_{\Phi}=\rho
,
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\Phi}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}
.
Moreover, if there is a
\delta>0
such that the growth conditions

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(\mathbf{x})|{\bm{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})|<\infty,\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(\mathbf{x})|\mathbf{D}{\bm{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})|<\infty

hold true, then
\|\bm{\nabla}\Phi\|<\infty
. Here, we have set
f(\mathbf{x})=(1+x_{1}^{2})^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}(1+x_{2}^{2})^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}(1+x_{3}^{2})^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}
.

Moreover,
[
23
]
also exhibits an example
(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
in the case
N=2
(and of course
{\bm{\nu}}\neq 0
), when there is no
C^{1}
-solution
to the representability problem. Unfortunately, the restriction
N\geqslant 4
and the fact that no control over the kinetic energy is retained in (ii) renders the preceding result inapplicable for our case.

The following theorem establishes mixed representability under different assumptions, now for general
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

(Tellgren–Kvaal–Helgaker
[
30
]
)
Let
d=3
and suppose that
(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})\in\mathcal{J}_{N}^{\prime}
and that

(6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|\mathbf{x}|^{2})\rho\Bigl|\mathbf{D}\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}}{\rho}\Bigr|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}<\infty.

Then there exists a constant
C>0
and a fermionic one-particle density matrix
\gamma
such that
\rho_{\gamma}=\rho
,
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}
and

(7)

\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma)\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho}|^{2}+CN+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}+C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|\mathbf{x}|^{2})\rho\Bigl|\mathbf{D}\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}}{\rho}\Bigr|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Representability holds also true without the integer particle number constraint on the density, i.e. when
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho\not\in\mathbb{N}
, but the representing state will live in
the Fock space
\mathfrak{F}
.
Analogously to the
N
-particle case, we define the set of
Fock space-representable density-current pairs
as

\mathcal{J}:=\Bigg\{(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):\rho=\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}},\,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}\;\text{for some}\;{\bm{\Gamma}}\in\mathcal{D}\Bigg\}.

Then the conditions of
Theorem

2.4
provide a smaller set, but we will use an improved version below.

2.5.
Definition of the grand-canonical current-density functionals

In this section, we define the current-density functionals relevant to our study. These are well-known in CDFT,
but we will also employ them in the definition of the uniform electron gas. In this section we restrict ourselves to the physically most
relevant 3D case.

We begin by introducing the
grand-canonical Vignale–Rasolt functional

[
31
]
via

(8)

\boxed{F(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\Gamma}}\in\mathcal{D}\\
\rho_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}=\rho\\
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{n}}\Biggl(-\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\bm{\Delta}_{\mathbf{x}_{j}}+\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{k}|}\Biggr)\Gamma_{n}}.

Omitting the Coulomb interaction in the above definition, we obtain the
mixed-state kinetic energy functional

T(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
.
By definition,
T
and
F
is (jointly) convex on
\mathcal{J}
.
In our analysis we will consider the
indirect energy

(9)

\boxed{E(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):=F(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})-D(\rho)}

where for any finite measures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, we define the
Coulomb inner product
via

D(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\mu_{2}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|},

and additionally, as an abuse of notation, we set
D(\mu):=D(\mu,\mu)
for the
direct energy of
\mu
. If the measures
\mu_{j}
come from densities
\rho_{j}
,
i.e.
\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}=\rho_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
(
j=1,2
), then we will use the notations
D(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and
D(\rho)
.

For convenience, we introduce the
gauge-corrected indirect energy

\boxed{\overline{E}(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):=E(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}}

and similarly
\overline{T}(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
. We will also consider the
exchange-correlation functional

\boxed{E^{\mathrm{xc}}(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):=E(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})-T(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})}

which is more common in the physics and chemistry literature.

The reason for the introduction of the “gauge-corrected” quantities is the following. Note that under a gauge transformation
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}-\rho\bm{\nabla}g
, the functional
F
transforms as

(10)

F(\rho,\widetilde{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}})=F(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}g+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho|\bm{\nabla}g|^{2},

i.e. like the kinetic energy (see
Proposition

2.2
). Analogous relations holds for
T(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
and
E(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
.
It follows that the gauge-corrected indirect-, and kinetic energy
\overline{E}(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
and
\overline{T}(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
, and exchange-correlation functional
E^{\mathrm{xc}}(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
are all
gauge-invariant
.

By replacing the
N
-particle Hilbert space with the corresponding Fock space, the next result follows along similar lines as
[
11
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})\in\mathcal{J}
. Then the infima in the definitions
Eq.

8
of the Vignale–Rasolt functional
F
and the kinetic energy functional
T
are both attained.

The grand-canonical Levy–Lieb functional
F(\rho)
(no paramagnetic current density argument) and the kinetic energy functional
T(\rho)
is defined similarly to
F(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
and
T(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
, only without any constraint on
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}
in the infimum.

Proposition 2.6

(Correspondence with the nonmagnetic case)
.

For any
\rho\in\mathcal{I}
, the relations
T(\rho,0)=T(\rho)
and
F(\rho,0)=F(\rho)
hold true. In particular,

E(\rho,0)=E(\rho)\quad\text{and}\quad E^{\mathrm{xc}}(\rho,0)=E^{\mathrm{xc}}(\rho),

where
E(\rho)=F(\rho)-D(\rho)
and
E^{\mathrm{xc}}(\rho)=E(\rho)-T(\rho)
.

Proof.

By convexity and time-reversal symmetry, we find
F(\rho,0)\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}F(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})+\tfrac{1}{2}F(\rho,-\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})=F(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
. Hence,
F(\rho)\leqslant F(\rho,0)\leqslant\inf_{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}}F(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})=F(\rho)
as desired. The proof of the relation between the kinetic energy functionals is similar.
∎

3.
Main results

3.1.
The magnetic uniform electron gas

This section is devoted to the precise definition of the various energetic quantities of the 3D uniform electron gas (UEG) with constant density and constant vorticity.

Recall that the vorticity of the ground state of the
noninteracting
UEG in a constant magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{0}
is
{\bm{\nu}}_{0}=-\mathbf{B}_{0}
.
Physically, we can generate a constant vorticity
{\bm{\nu}}_{0}
in the noninteracting UEG by subjecting it to an external magnetic field
-\mathbf{B}_{0}
.
Moreover, linear response theory suggests the same holds true to the first order in the
interacting
case
[
5
]
.
Instead of referring to the external magnetic field, we will simply demand the UEG to have constant density
and
constant vorticity.

A function
0\leqslant\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
\supp\eta\subset B_{1}
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{x}\eta(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\eta=1
will be
called a
regularization function
. Note that this is a slightly more general definition than the one in
[
14
,
16
]
,
where it is required that a regularization function be radial. We also introduce the notation
\eta_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=\delta^{-3}\eta(\delta^{-1}\mathbf{x})
.

Define the
indirect gauge-corrected energy per volume
of the (interacting) UEG at constant density
\rho_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and constant vorticity
{\bm{\nu}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
inside a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
smeared with
\eta
as

(11)

\boxed{e_{\Omega,\eta}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0}):=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\overline{E}\big((\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta)(\rho_{0},\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{0}{\bm{\nu}}_{0}\times\cdot)\big)}

Note that
\rot\left(\tfrac{1}{2}{\bm{\nu}}_{0}\times\mathbf{x}\right)={\bm{\nu}}_{0}
and that
e_{\Omega,\eta}(0,{\bm{\nu}}_{0})=e_{\Omega,\eta}(0,0)=0
. The smearing of the indicator
\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}
is necessary, since
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}}|^{2}=\infty
and this would render the energy
E
infinite (see
Eq.

3
).
Recall that
\overline{E}
contains a term of the form
-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}
, which we call the
gauge correction term
,
and it needs to be subtracted otherwise the energy per volume would diverge
in the thermodynamic limit. In fact, the gauge correction term grows faster than the volume,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\ell\Omega|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\mathbb{1}_{\ell\Omega}*\eta)(\mathbf{x})|{\bm{\nu}}_{0}\times\mathbf{x}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=C_{\Omega,\eta,{\bm{\nu}}_{0}}\ell^{2},

where
C_{\Omega,\eta,{\bm{\nu}}_{0}}>0
is a constant depending only on
\Omega
,
\eta
and
{\bm{\nu}}_{0}
.
Hence, by
Eq.

3
, the energy per volume diverges as
\ell\to\infty
. Notice that replacing

\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{0}{\bm{\nu}}_{0}\times\cdot\to\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{0}{\bm{\nu}}_{0}\times\cdot+\rho_{0}\bm{\nabla}g

in the definition
Eq.

11
above would yield the same value for
\overline{E}
due to the gauge transformation rule
Eq.

10
.
In particular, one can choose the
Landau gauge
instead of the
symmetric gauge

\tfrac{1}{2}{\bm{\nu}}_{0}\times\mathbf{x}
above without changing anything.

By replacing
\overline{E}
with
\overline{T}
on the r.h.s. of
Eq.

11
, we can also define the
kinetic energy per volume

\tau_{\Omega,\eta}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0})
.
Finally, we define the
exchange-correlation energy per volume

\displaystyle e_{\Omega,\eta}^{\mathrm{xc}}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0}):=e_{\Omega,\eta}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0})-\tau_{\Omega,\eta}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0}).

Note that, in this case the gauge correction terms cancel.

Following Fischer, we say that a sequence of domains
\{\Omega_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
has a
uniformly regular boundary
if

|\partial\Omega_{N}+B_{r}|\leqslant Cr|\Omega_{N}|^{2/3}\quad\text{for all}\quad r\leqslant\frac{|\Omega_{N}|^{2/3}}{C},

for some
C>0
. For instance, one may take
\Omega_{N}=N\Omega
for some convex, bounded and open set
[
8
]
.

The following theorem is the main result of the paper.

Theorem 3.1

(Uniform electron gas)
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
{\bm{\nu}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

and let
\{\Omega_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a sequence of bounded domains such that
|\Omega_{N}|\to\infty
and it has a uniformly regular boundary.
Let
\delta_{N}>0
be any sequence such that
\delta_{N}/|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}\to 0
and
\delta_{N}|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}\to\infty
.
Then the following thermodynamic limit exists

e_{\mathrm{UEG}}(\rho_{0},\nu_{0}):=\lim_{N\to\infty}e_{\Omega_{N},\eta_{\delta_{N}}}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0})

and is independent of the choice of the sequences
\{\Omega_{N}\}
,
\{\delta_{N}\}
and the regularization function
\eta
.
Analogous results hold true for
\tau_{\mathrm{UEG}}(\rho_{0},\nu_{0})
and
e_{\mathrm{UEG}}^{\mathrm{xc}}(\rho_{0},\nu_{0})
.

We see using
Corollary

3.4
(see below) for the upper bound and the Lieb–Thirring and Lieb–Oxford inequalities for the lower bound (see
Corollaries

4.2
and
4.3
below),

|e_{\mathrm{UEG}}(\rho_{0},\nu_{0})|\leqslant c_{\mathrm{TF}}\rho_{0}^{5/3}+c_{\mathrm{LO}}\rho_{0}^{4/3}+c^{\prime}\rho_{0}\nu_{0}.

Note that in
e_{\mathrm{UEG}}(\rho_{0},\nu_{0})
only the magnitude of
{\bm{\nu}}_{0}
matters due to rotational invariance in the thermodynamic limit (see
Lemma

4.5
(ii)).
It is clear from
Proposition

2.6
that we recover the nonmagnetic case by putting
\nu_{0}=0
, i.e.
e_{\mathrm{UEG}}(\rho_{0},0)=e_{\mathrm{UEG}}(\rho_{0})
.
Moreover,
Eq.

3
and the Lieb–Oxford inequality implies that
e_{\mathrm{UEG}}(\rho_{0},\nu_{0})\geqslant c\rho_{0}\nu_{0}-c_{\mathrm{LO}}\rho_{0}^{4/3}
,
so
e_{\mathrm{UEG}}(\rho_{0},\nu_{0})\to+\infty
as
\nu_{0}\to\infty
for every fixed
\rho_{0}>0
.

Remark 2
.

Unfortunately, we cannot offer a definition of the
classical
UEG in the magnetic case, akin to
[
14
]
in the nonmagnetic case.
The reason is that
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}
uses the phase of the wavefunction in an essential way, and we have no obvious classical probabilistic interpretation of that.

3.2.
Upper bound on the kinetic energy functional and representability

In this section, we describe a technical tool that is needed in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, but it is also of independent interest mainly because
it improves previous results on the (mixed) representability problem for density-current density pairs (see
Section

2.4
).

If
\rho_{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}
comes from an
H^{1}
-regular one-particle density matrix
\gamma
, we know from
Eq.

3
that we necessarily have
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\gamma}\left|\mathbf{D}_{a}\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\right|<\infty.

Note that if
d=3
, the the condition is simply
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\gamma}|{\bm{\nu}}_{\gamma}|<\infty
, where
{\bm{\nu}}_{\gamma}=\rot\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}
is the vorticity.

Using the method of proof of
[
16
, Theorem 3]
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
q
be the number of spin states. Fix
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that
\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
.
Let
\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
and suppose that
\mathbf{v}
admits a decomposition
\mathbf{v}=\bm{\nabla}g+\mathbf{w}
, where
\bm{\nabla}g\in L^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mathbf{w}\in L^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
,
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}\in L^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d\times d}
.

Then there exists a one-particle density matrix
\gamma
with finite kinetic energy, such that
\rho_{\gamma}=\rho
,
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}:=\rho\mathbf{v}
and

(12)

\displaystyle\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma)

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\kappa_{1}\varepsilon)q^{-2/d}c_{\mathrm{TF}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{1+2/d}+\frac{\kappa_{2}(1+\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}+C_{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}|

for any
\varepsilon>0
, and for some universal constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
. Here, the (dimension-dependent)
Thomas–Fermi constant

c_{\mathrm{TF}}
is given by

c_{\mathrm{TF}}:=\frac{d}{d+2}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{|B_{1}|^{2/d}}
and
C_{d}=1+\frac{d^{3}}{4}
.

The inequality
Eq.

12
is
not
gauge-invariant, to see this, take
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})=(0,x_{1},0)
(Landau gauge) and
\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{3}\times\mathbf{x}
(symmetric gauge),
with
g(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}x_{1}x_{2}
and some appropriate
\rho
.
Then
\mathbf{D}_{a}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{D}_{a}\mathbf{w}
,
\mathbf{D}_{s}\mathbf{v}\neq 0
but
\mathbf{D}_{s}\mathbf{w}=0
. Clearly,
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{v}
and
g=0
is also a possible choice. The lack of gauge invariance in
Eq.

12
solely comes from the presence of the symmetric derivative
\mathbf{D}_{s}\mathbf{w}
,
which we discuss further below. The utility of the formulation of
Theorem

3.2
is that with a proper choice of gauge, we can hope to make
\mathbf{D}_{s}\mathbf{w}
vanish.

The condition
Eq.

6
of
Theorem

2.4
is easily seen to imply
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}\in L^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d\times d}
(up to a gauge) via the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality.
We also note that for
\mathbf{v}=0
, we
exactly
recover the statement (and proof) of
[
16
, Theorem 3]
, since that construction actually yields a state
\gamma
with
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}=0
. Based on the kinetic energy of the ground state of the noninteracting electron gas, we conjecture that the
optimal constant in 3D is
C_{3}=1
.

The last term on the r.h.s. of
Eq.

12
has similar role as the von Weizsäcker term. It can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho\sqrt{|\mathbf{D}_{s}\mathbf{w}|^{2}+|\mathbf{D}_{a}\mathbf{w}|^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho\sqrt{|\mathbf{D}_{s}\mathbf{w}|^{2}+|\mathbf{D}_{a}\mathbf{v}|^{2}},

where we used the fact
\mathbf{D}_{a}\bm{\nabla}p=0
for any
C^{2}
scalar field
p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, due to Young’s theorem.
Hence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}|\simeq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho|\mathbf{D}_{s}\mathbf{w}|+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho|\mathbf{D}_{a}\mathbf{v}|,

in the sense of norm equivalence. In 3D, the second term on the r.h.s. is proportional to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho|{\bm{\nu}}|
, which is gauge-invariant.

The first term on the r.h.s. of the preceding equation (with
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{\gamma}
)—which we call the
strain term
—is not necessarily finite for
H^{1}
-regular
\gamma
.
However, if we assume that
\gamma
is
H^{2}
-regular (and the gauge function has
\mathbf{D}^{2}g\in L^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d\times d}
), then it is not hard to show that the strain term is finite.
Recall that the eigenstates of the magnetic Schrödinger Hamiltonian are in
H^{2}
.
Although
Theorem

3.2
does not provide the complete solution to the mixed density-current
N
-representability problem (it also does not provide an
H^{2}
-regular
\gamma
without further assumptions, sadly), it seems to provide weak enough assumptions that any practically
relevant
(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
pair verifies it.
That being said, it is our belief that the presence of the strain term is only an artifact of the method of the proof of both
Theorem

2.4
and
Theorem

3.2
.
It remains a challenge to devise a gauge-invariant analogue of
Eq.

12
.

Motivated by
Theorem

3.2
, we define the
smaller
set
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\subset\mathcal{J}
, via

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} (\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}):&\;\rho\in\mathcal{I},\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}|^{2}}{\rho}<\infty,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho\left|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}\right|<\infty,\;\text{where}\;\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}}{\rho}=\bm{\nabla}g+\mathbf{w},\\

With this notation at hand, we can provide an upper bound on the kinetic energy functional
T(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})
.

Corollary 3.3
.

For any
(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
, we have
T(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})\leqslant\text{r.h.s. of \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{repineq}}
.

Moreover, using the fact
[

]
that for every one-particle density matrix
\gamma
there exists a quasi-free state
{\bm{\Gamma}}
, such that

.

For any

, we have

In fact, for a quasi-free state
{\bm{\Gamma}}

Proof of
Theorem

Step 0.

First, define the ground-state one-particle density matrix

It is immediate that

is Hermitian and
0\leqslant f_{t}\leqslant\mathbb{1}

f_{t}(0)=t

Moreover,
\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}f_{t}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}=\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{y}}f_{t}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}}=0
. Furthermore, the kinetic energy density is also constant

\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{y}}f_{t}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}=c_{\mathrm{TF}}t^{1+2/d}.

Step 1.

As the first derivative of
f_{t}
vanishes on the diagonal, this state has zero paramagnetic current density.
We would like to construct a state
f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
which has a constant paramagnetic current density proportional to a fixed vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
To achieve this, we simply shift the Fermi sphere
|\mathbf{k}|^{2}\leqslant\frac{d+2}{d}c_{\mathrm{TF}}t^{2/d}
by
\mathbf{u}
. More precisely, we consider the translation-invariant kernel
f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
, where

\displaystyle f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\int_{|\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{u}|^{2}\leqslant\frac{d+2}{d}c_{\mathrm{TF}}t^{2/d}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{z}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}=e^{i\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}}f_{t}(\mathbf{z}).

Clearly,
f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}
is Hermitian and
0\leqslant f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}\leqslant\mathbb{1}

f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(0)=t

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=it\mathbf{u},

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{y}}f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=-it\mathbf{u}.

Therefore, this state has paramagnetic current density

\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}(\mathbf{x})=\Imag\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}=t\mathbf{u}

as desired.
Further, the kinetic energy density is again constant, but has an additional term

\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}f_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}=t|\mathbf{u}|^{2}+c_{\mathrm{TF}}t^{1+2/d}.

q=1
. We are now ready to construct a fermionic one-particle density matrix
\gamma
with prescribed density
\rho(\mathbf{x})
and paramagnetic current density of the form
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}=\rho\mathbf{v}
.
Let us first assume that
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}
. Following
[
16
]
, we define

Note that this state reduces to the cited one by taking

and

4.2.
Further bounds

We start by recalling a simple version of the magnetic Lieb–Thirring inequality, which can be used to derive a gauge invariant bound.
Let
\mathbf{A}\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{3}
and denote the corresponding
magnetic Sobolev space
as

, see
[

Theorem 4.1

(Magnetic Lieb–Thirring kinetic energy inequality)
.

[
24
]

Fix
\mathbf{A}\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. For any fermionic one-particle reduced density matrix
\gamma
in
H^{1}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, the bound

\Tr(-i\bm{\nabla}+\mathbf{A})^{2}\gamma\geqslant C_{\mathrm{LT}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\gamma}^{5/3}

holds true for some universal constant
C_{\mathrm{LT}}>0
.

Using this, the usual Lieb–Thirring kinetic energy inequality can be cast in a gauge-invariant form.

Corollary 4.2

(Gauge-invariant Lieb–Thirring inequality)
.

For any fermionic one-particle reduced density matrix
\gamma
with finite kinetic energy, the bound

\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma)\geqslant c_{\mathrm{LT}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\gamma}^{5/3}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}|^{2}}{\rho_{\gamma}}

holds true for some universal constant
c_{\mathrm{LT}}>0
.

Proof.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we set
\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}+\varepsilon\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}
. Then
\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
since
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}|^{2}}{\rho_{\gamma}}<\infty
. Note that

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{LT}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\gamma}^{5/3}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Tr(-i\bm{\nabla}+\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon})^{2}\gamma=\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma)+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\gamma}|\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\gamma}|^{2}\frac{\rho_{\gamma}+2\varepsilon\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{(\rho_{\gamma}+\varepsilon\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}})^{2}}.

An application of Fatou’s lemma finishes the proof.
∎

For any
{\bm{\Gamma}}\in\mathcal{D}
let us introduce the notations

\mathcal{T}({\bm{\Gamma}})=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{n}}(-\bm{\Delta}_{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\Gamma_{n}),\quad\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Gamma}})=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n}\Tr_{\mathfrak{H}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{k}|}\Gamma_{n}\right)

for the

Coulomb energy of the state

{\bm{\Gamma}}
. Of course,
\mathcal{T}({\bm{\Gamma}})=\Tr(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}})
, so

4.2
provides a gauge-invariant lower bound on

.

Theorem 4.3

(Lieb–Oxford inequality)
.

[

,
22
,

There exists a universal constant

{\bm{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Gamma}})-D(\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}})\geqslant-c_{\mathrm{LO}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}}^{4/3}

4.3.
Transformation properties

In this section, we discuss the behavior of the density functionals under various transformations of the density-current density pair.

First, we consider general affine transformations of states. Let
{\bm{\Gamma}}=\Gamma_{0}\oplus\Gamma_{1}\oplus\ldots\in\mathcal{D}
and for any
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
nonsingular and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{a}
, and
define the transformed state
\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}{\bm{\Gamma}}
by setting

(\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}\Gamma_{n})(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})=(\det\mathbf{M})^{n}\Gamma_{n}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}_{n});\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y}_{n})).

Then the reduced one-particle density matrix of

is given by
\gamma_{\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}{\bm{\Gamma}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(\det\mathbf{M})\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y}))

\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}}
denotes the one-particle density matrix of
{\bm{\Gamma}}
.
We have
\rho_{\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}{\bm{\Gamma}}}(\mathbf{x})=(\det\mathbf{M})\rho_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}))

\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}{\bm{\Gamma}}}(\mathbf{x})=(\det\mathbf{M})\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}))

\gamma^{(2)}_{\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}{\bm{\Gamma}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(\det\mathbf{M})^{2}\gamma^{(2)}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y});\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y}))
.
We immediately obtain that for any
{\bm{\Gamma}}\in\mathcal{D}
the following relations hold true:

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}{\bm{\Gamma}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Tr_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\top}(\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{y}})\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}{\bm{\Gamma}})

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\gamma^{(2)}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Motivated by the above considerations, for any
(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})\in\mathcal{J}

(19)

\boxed{\Upsilon_{\mathbf{T}}(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}})(\mathbf{x}):=(\det\mathbf{M})\big(\rho(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}))\big)}

as a slight abuse of notation.
Using
Eq.

17

18

the transformed gauge-corrected indirect energy may be expressed as

\overline{E}(\Upsilon_{\mathbf{M}}(\rho,\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}))=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\Gamma}}\in\mathcal{D}\\
\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}}=\rho\\
\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}=\mathbf{j}^{\mathrm{p}}\end{subarray}}\Bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Tr_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\top}\overline{{\bm{\tau}}}_{\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}}}(\mathbf{x})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\mathcal{C}(\Upsilon_{\mathbf{M}}{\bm{\Gamma}})\Bigg]-D(\Upsilon_{\mathbf{M}}\rho),

which follows by a simple reparametrization of the infimum, using the fact that the map

20

The lower bound is based on well-known localization techniques, and for that, smoothing is needed.

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{24}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(\cdot-\mathbf{x}-\ell\mathbf{z})\eta_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\eta_{\delta}\equiv 1

Proof of
Theorem

3.1

The proof is very similar to the one in
[
16
]
. Let
\{\Omega_{N}\}
be a sequence of domains such that
|\Omega_{N}|\to\infty
,
|\partial\Omega_{N}+B_{r}|\leqslant Cr|\Omega_{N}|^{2/3}
for
r\leqslant|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}/C

and
\delta_{N}/|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}\to 0
,
\delta_{N}|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}\to\infty
.

We can define the index sets
J
and
J_{0}
for the domain
\Omega_{N}
in an obvious manner,

J:=\left\{\begin{aligned} (\mathbf{z},j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\times\{1,\ldots,24\}:&\supp((\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}*\eta_{\delta})(\mathbf{R}\cdot-\ell\mathbf{z}-{\bm{\tau}}))\cap\supp(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega_{N}}*\eta_{\delta_{N}})\neq\emptyset,\\
&\text{for some}\,\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\,\text{and}\,{\bm{\tau}}\in C_{\ell}\end{aligned}\right\},

which collects all the small tetrahedra
\mathbf{R}^{\top}(\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell\mathbf{z}+{\bm{\tau}})
that possibly intersect the smearing of
\Omega_{N}
. Furthermore, define

J_{0}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} (\mathbf{z},j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\times\{1,\ldots,24\}:&\supp((\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}*\eta_{\delta})(\mathbf{R}\cdot-\ell\mathbf{z}-{\bm{\tau}}))\subset\Omega_{N}\smallsetminus(\partial\Omega_{N}+B_{\delta_{N}}),\\
&\text{for some}\,\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\,\text{and}\,{\bm{\tau}}\in C_{\ell}\end{aligned}\right\},

which contains small smeared tetrahedra which are well inside
\Omega_{N}
.

The argument for the tetrahedron
\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}
works exactly the same for the general domain
\Omega_{N}
,
we only need to bound the quantities
M_{\ell}
and
M_{\ell}^{\prime}
to obtain the analog of
Eq.

37
.
If
e_{\Omega_{N},\delta_{N}}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0})\leqslant 0
, then we can use the trivial bound
M_{\ell}/|\Omega_{N}|\leqslant 1
.
The boundary tetrahedra indexed by
J\smallsetminus J_{0}
are at a distance
r=\ell+\delta+\delta_{N}

M_{\ell}^{\prime}=C|\partial\Omega_{N}+B_{r}|\leqslant C(\ell+\delta+\delta_{N})|\Omega_{N}|^{2/3}

for sufficiently large
N
, since
r\leqslant|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}/C

\delta_{N}/|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}\to 0

But then
M_{\ell}\geqslant|\Omega_{N}|-C(\ell+\delta+\delta^{\prime})|\Omega_{N}|^{2/3}
, so we arrive at the bound

\displaystyle e_{\Omega_{N},\delta_{N}}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0})

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-C\sigma\frac{\delta}{\ell}-C\sigma\frac{\ell+\delta+\delta_{N}}{|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}}\right)\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}e_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta},\delta}(\rho_{0},\mathbf{R}{\bm{\nu}}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}

\delta

\ell=|\Omega_{N}|^{1/6}

as
\delta_{N}/|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}\to 0
.

For the upper bound, we use the analog of
Eq.

42
, with
\alpha=1/2
,

\displaystyle e_{\Omega_{N},\delta_{N}}(\rho_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{0})

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(1+C\sigma\frac{\ell+\delta+\delta_{N}}{|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}}\right)\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{1/2}^{3/2}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}e_{t\ell\mathbb{\Delta},t\delta}(\rho_{0},\mathbf{R}{\bm{\nu}}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t^{4}}+\frac{C\rho_{0}}{\ell}\big(\delta\rho_{0}^{2/3}+\ell^{-1}\big)\right)

\displaystyle+C\frac{\ell+\delta+\delta_{N}}{|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}}\rho_{0}\left(\rho_{0}^{2/3}+(\ell\delta)^{-1}+\nu_{0}\right)+\frac{C\rho_{0}}{\delta_{N}|\Omega_{N}|^{1/3}}+C\delta^{2}\rho_{0}^{2}.

Part (i).

Note first that

(44)

\displaystyle\frac{{\bm{\omega}}(\mathbf{x})-{\bm{\omega}}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}}{2i}=\frac{1}{2i}({\bm{\pi}}_{\mathbf{x}}\otimes\overline{{\bm{\pi}}}_{\mathbf{y}}-\overline{{\bm{\pi}}}_{\mathbf{y}}\otimes{\bm{\pi}}_{\mathbf{x}})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}}
