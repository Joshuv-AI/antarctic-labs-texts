Scaling limit for a second-order particle system with local annihilation Keywords and phrases: second-order system; scaling limit; annihilating particles; local interaction; hypoellipticity. AMS subject classification (2020): 60K35, 82C21, 82C40, 35J70.
Ruojun Huang

Abstract
For a second-order particle system in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} subject to locally-in-space pairwise annihilation, we prove a scaling limit for its empirical measure on position and velocity towards a degenerate elliptic partial differential equation. Crucial ingredients are Green’s function estimates for the associated hypoelliptic operator and an Itô-Tanaka trick.



1
Introduction

Second-order particle systems comprising of dynamics of positions and velocities, in the spirit of Newtonian mechanics, give a more complete description of interacting particles. When the behavior of a first-order system of positions only is understood, or is insufficient for one’s purpose, it is natural to look at a second-order system. The technical difficulties associated with second-order systems are in general greater, some coming from the degeneracy of the associated operators. Here, we explore a simplest model of interacting particles
with local interactions
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
written in second-order, where the interaction occurs not in the equation of the motion but in pairwise annihilation. Besides being well-related to a large body of works on annihilating (or proliferating, coagulating, colliding etc) particle systems and their scaling limits (e.g.
[
21
,
32
,
25
,
26
,
22
,
28
,
16
,
14
,
12
]
) and generally relevant for reaction-diffusion phenomena, one motivation is also from the technical side, namely, we aim to derive a scaling limit when the interaction range between particles is truly local (see Condition
2
).

Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, for any positive integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\geq 1
, consider a system of particles, whose cardinality at time
t=0
is
N(0)=N
, and with positions and velocities at any time
t\geq 0
denoted by
x_{i}^{N}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
v_{i}^{N}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,...,N(t)
, and the interaction comes from the possibility of pairwise annhilation when spatial positions are close. More specifically, the position and velocity of each particle, while active, obey the second-order dynamics

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{i}^{N}(t)=x_{i}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}v_{i}^{N}(s)ds\\
v_{i}^{N}(t)=v_{i}(0)+B_{i}(t)\end{cases},\quad t\geq 0,\,i\in\mathcal{N}(t),

(1)

where
\mathcal{N}(t)
denotes the set of indices of active particles at time
t
whose cardinality
|\mathcal{N}(t)|=N(t)\leq N
,
\{B_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a collection of independent standard Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the initial conditions
(x_{i}(0),v_{i}(0))_{i=1}^{\infty}
are random, chosen i.i.d. with some given probability density
f_{0}(x,v):\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying the condition below, independent of the Brownian motions.

Condition 1

For every
d\geq 1
, we assume that there exist some finite constants
\Gamma
and
R
such that
\|f_{0}(x,v)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}\leq\Gamma
and supp
(f_{0})\subset\mathbb{B}_{E}((0,0),R)
, the closed Euclidean ball of radius
R
centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.

The probability space is endowed with the canonical filtration

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma\big\{\{B_{i}(s):0\leq s\leq t\}_{i=1}^{\infty},(x_{i}(0),v_{i}(0))_{i=1}^{\infty}\big\},\quad t\geq 0.

Interaction between different particles comes by means of pairwise annhilation, informally described as follows. Let
\theta(x):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be given, nonnegative, of class
C^{\infty}
, with
\int\theta dx=1
,
\theta(0)=0
,
\theta(x)=\theta(-x)
and compact support in
\mathbb{B}_{E}(0,1)
. Then, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, denote by

\displaystyle\theta^{\epsilon}(x):=\epsilon^{-d}\theta(\epsilon^{-1}x).

(For the necessity of the condition
\theta(0)=0
, see Remark
6
.)
A configuration of the particle system is given by

\displaystyle\zeta=(x_{1},v_{1},x_{2},v_{2},...,x_{N},v_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\emptyset)^{2N}

where we adopt the convention that if a particle
i_{0}
has been annihilated, we set
x_{i_{0}}=v_{i_{0}}=\emptyset
. It will no more participate in the dynamics of the system. Now suppose the current configuration is
\zeta
, then for any pair of particles
i,j
with
i\neq j
(each ranging over the index set
\mathcal{N}(\zeta)
of active particles), with an infinitesimal rate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}(x_{i}-x_{j})

we remove both particles
i,j
from the system (i.e. they annihilate with each other) and obtain a new configuration, denoted
\zeta^{-ij}
which is equal to
\zeta
in all other slots except for
x_{i},v_{i},x_{j},v_{j}
which have been set to
\emptyset
. Since the support of the function
\theta^{\epsilon}
has radius
\epsilon
, interaction happens for the pair
i,j
only when their spatial distance is within the critical distance
\epsilon
. In fact, our interest is in the case where
\epsilon=\epsilon(N)
depends on
N
and converges to zero as
N\to\infty
. An important case to cover is the regime of local interaction, corresponding to
\epsilon=N^{-1/d}
in which case an individual particle typically interacts with a bounded number of others at any given time. Contrary to lattice models
[
6
,
19
]
where nearest-neighbor or bounded-range interactions are standard, in our continuum case such local interaction has to be enforced by a potential (i.e.
\theta^{\epsilon}
), which is a source of technical difficulty. One of the key techniques available to date to handle such local interaction, or sometimes even more stringently the mean-free path interaction, is due to Hammond and Rezakhanlou
[
16
]
, see also
[
21
,
32
,
34
,
33
]
for other techniques. On the other hand, mean-field type models correspond to choosing
\epsilon
independent of
N
, and in that setting techniques for proving scaling limits of (both first and second-order) particle systems have become very advanced, cf. the survey
[
18
]
,
[
4
]
, even when interactions are very singular and influence directly the stochastic differential equations. Clearly, there is a big technical gap between mean-field and more local cases, which serves as another motivation for this research.

Condition 2

For any
d\geq 1
, we assume that
\epsilon=\epsilon(N)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
and

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\epsilon^{-d}}{N}\leq 1.

When the equality is achieved, we call the interaction local.

We now give formally the infinitesimal generator of the jointly Markov process
(x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t))_{i=1}^{\mathcal{N}{(t)}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}=\mathcal{L}_{N}^{D}+\mathcal{L}_{N}^{J}

(2)

where the diffusion and respectively, jump (i.e. annhilation) part of the generator are (acting on test functions
F
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}^{D}F(\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(\zeta)}\big[v_{i}\cdot\nabla_{x_{i}}F(\zeta)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v_{i}}F(\zeta)\big]

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}^{J}F(\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(\zeta)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(\zeta):j\neq i}\frac{1}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}(x_{i}-x_{j})\big[F(\zeta^{-ij})-F(\zeta)\big].

For every
N
and
T
, denote the process of joint empirical measure of positions and velocities of active particles

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{N}(dx,dv):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\delta_{\left(x_{i}^{N}(t),\,v_{i}^{N}(t)\right)}(dx,dv),\quad t\in[0,T].

(3)

For each
t
,
\mu_{t}^{N}(dx,dv)
is a subprobability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Denote by
\mathcal{M}_{+,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
the space of subprobability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
endowed with the weak topology, and by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{T}(\mathcal{M}_{+,1}):=\mathcal{D}\big([0,T];\mathcal{M}_{+,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\big)

(4)

the space of càdlàg functions on
[0,T]
taking values in
\mathcal{M}_{+,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
endowed with the Skorohod topology
[
8
, Chapter 3]
. Our goal is to prove that the
N\to\infty
limit of (
3
) is given by the unique weak solution (see Definition
23
) of the following degenerate elliptic partial differential equation (
pde
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)&=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}f-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x,v)f(t,x,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}\\
f|_{t=0}&=f_{0}(x,v)\end{cases},\quad(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(5)

Theorem 3

Under Conditions
1
and
2
, for any finite
T
and
d\geq 1
, the sequence of empirical measures
\{\mu^{N}_{t}:t\in[0,T]\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability in the space
\mathcal{D}_{T}(\mathcal{M}_{+,1})
towards a limit
\{\overline{\mu}_{t}:t\in[0,T]\}
. For every
t\in[0,T]
, the measure
\overline{\mu}_{t}(dx,dv)
has a density
f(t,x,v)
with respect to Lebesgue measure and
\{f(t,x,v):t\in[0,T],x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
is the unique weak solution to the
pde
(
5
) in the sense of Definition
23
.

Remark 4

Our setting (
1
) of a second-order system is almost the simplest possible. The motivation and challenge is to handle the local interaction. One may notice that, if substituting the velocity into the first equation (
1
), then we get a closed expression for the dynamics of the position

\displaystyle x_{i}^{N}(t)=x_{i}(0)+v_{i}(0)t+\int_{0}^{t}B_{i}(s)ds.

(6)

One may ask why not study directly a first-order system of
N
locally interacting particles with dynamics given by (
6
), especially if one is mainly interested in the limiting density of positions alone

\displaystyle\rho(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x,v)dv.

However, it is not hard to check that
t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}B(s)ds
is not a Markov process, hence working directly with the first-order dynamics is not necessarily easier. Consistent with that, if we integrate the
pde
(
5
) formally in variable
v
, we do not obtain a closed equation for
\rho(t,x)
. It remains unclear what (if any) equation
\rho
should satisfy.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we derive an identity satisfied by the empirical measure, and identity the nonlinear term whose convergence is the main issue. For this purpose, in Section
3
we set up the Itô-Tanaka trick which involves the solution of an auxiliary
pde
with a degenerate Kolmogorov (or Fokker-Planck) operator, see that section for more on the meaning of this manouvre. It turns out that key to our proof are pointwise estimates of the Green’s function associated with this operator, which are recalled and developed in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we apply these estimates to show that various terms coming from the Itô-Tanaka trick are negligible. In Section
6
, we check the tightness of the laws of our particle system, which together with previous sections completes the proof of subsequential convergence. In the last Section
7
, we prove the uniqueness of the limit
pde
. The techniques used here, which are very influenced by the paper of Hammond-Rezakhanlou
[
16
]
on particle approximation of the Smoluchowski coagulation equation, share elements with the author’s previous works
[
12
,
13
]
, hence certain arguments are made more succinct.

2
Identity from the empirical measure

Fixing
T<\infty
and any
\phi_{t}(x,v)=\phi(t,x,v)\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, we consider the process

\displaystyle F_{1}(t,\zeta(t)):=\langle\phi_{t},\mu_{t}^{N}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big),\quad t\geq 0,

(7)

where
\langle f,\mu\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(x,v)\mu(dx,dv)
. Applying Itô’s formula to (
7
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{T},\mu_{T}^{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=F_{1}(T,\zeta(T)=F_{1}(0,\zeta(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{N}^{D}+\mathcal{L}_{N}^{J}\right)F_{1}(t,\zeta(t))dt+M_{T}^{N,\phi,D}+M_{T}^{N,\phi,J}

(8)

\displaystyle=\langle\phi_{0},\mu_{0}^{N}\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\partial_{t}\phi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\phi,\,\mu_{t}^{N}\right\rangle dt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(t):j\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\left[\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)+\phi\big(t,x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\right]\,dt

\displaystyle+M_{T}^{N,\phi,D}+M_{T}^{N,\phi,J}.

More precisely, (
7
) is a time-dependent functional of the particle configuration
\zeta(t)
, hence we may act the Markov generator (
2
) on (
7
). The diffusion part of the generator acts individually on each
\phi\left(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\right)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}^{D}\phi\left(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\right)=\left(v_{i}^{N}(t)\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\right)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big),

hence

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{N}^{D}\right)F_{1}(t,\zeta(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\left(\partial_{t}+v_{i}^{N}(t)\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\right)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\partial_{t}\phi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\phi,\,\mu_{t}^{N}\right\rangle.

Turning to the jump part of the generator, for each distinct pair
i\neq j\in\mathcal{N}(t)
, we know that at an exponential rate

\displaystyle q_{ij}^{N}(t):=\frac{1}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big),

(9)

they annihilate and hence the sum (
7
) decreases by the amount

F_{1}(t,\zeta^{-ij}(t))-F_{1}(t,\zeta(t))=-N^{-1}\big[\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)+\phi\big(t,x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\big].

Therefore,

\mathcal{L}_{N}^{J}F_{1}(t,\zeta(t))=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}\frac{1}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\frac{1}{N}\big[\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)+\phi\big(t,x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\big].

(From now on, for the ease of notation, all sums are only over active particles hence we sometimes drop
\mathcal{N}(t)
.)
Any inactive particle is assigned a cemetery state
\emptyset
; it stays put and does not contribute to the dynamics. We note in passing that even though
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an unbounded variable,
v\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi
is bounded continuous since here
v
is in the compact support of
\phi
, thus in the first line of (
8
), we have the form of
\mu_{t}^{N}
integrated against a bounded continuous test function.

To compensate for the action of the generator,
M_{t}^{N,\phi,D},M_{t}^{N,\phi,J}
are two martingales due to diffusion and jump (i.e. annhilation), respectively. We can bound the martingales via quadratic variation: indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|M_{T}^{N,\phi,D}|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\nabla_{v}\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\cdot dB_{i}(t)\big|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\big|\nabla_{v}\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\big|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}^{2}}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{N}(t)dt\leq\frac{\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}^{2}T}{N},

(10)

and by
[
5
, Proposition 8.7]
(the quantity
\alpha
therein, so-called carré-du-champ, is the sum over all possible jumps of the particle configuration
\zeta(t)
, of the squared change of the value of the functional
F_{1}(t,\zeta(t))
, weighted by the rate
q_{ij}^{N}(t)
of such a change), see also
[
19
, Appendix 1, Lemma 5.1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|M_{T}^{N,\phi,J}|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}q_{ij}^{N}(t)\left[F_{1}(t,\zeta^{-ij}(t))-F_{1}(t,\zeta(t))\right]^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\frac{1}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\left[\frac{1}{N}\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)+\frac{1}{N}\phi\big(t,x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\right]^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}}{N^{3}}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)dt.

To bound the remaining quantity, applying Itô’s formula to
\mathcal{N}(t)
and then taking expectation, we obtain (cf.
[
16
, Proof of Lemma 3.1]
for a similar context)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{N}(T)=\mathbb{E}\mathcal{N}(0)-\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\frac{2}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)dt

hence we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)dt\leq\frac{N^{2}}{2},

(11)

whereby

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|M_{T}^{\phi,J}|^{2}\big]\leq\frac{\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}}{2N}.

(12)

To handle the nonlinearity, we first fix another test function
\psi(x,v)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and consider

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt.

(13)

Let us denote by
K=K^{\phi,\psi}
the maximal radius of the compact supports of
\phi
and
\psi
, and by

\displaystyle\chi^{K}(\xi):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]

(14)

a compactly-supported function of class

that is identically

for
|\xi|\leq 2K
and

|\xi|\geq 4K
. Then, (

) is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\chi^{K}\big(v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt

Indeed, since

are in the arguments of
\phi,\psi
, their moduli are less than

, whereby

otherwise one of

In the next two sections we concentrate on proving

Proposition 5

For any finite
T
and
d\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\lim_{|z|\to 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}

\displaystyle\big[\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)+z\big)-\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\big]

\displaystyle\cdot\chi^{K}\big(v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt\Big|=0.

This continuity claim should be seen as a form of local equilibrium (cf.
[
19
]
) for our continuum particle system at two macroscopically separated sites (here
z
is a macroscopic variable whereas
\epsilon
is microscopic or mesoscopic). Since for
\epsilon
small, the function
\theta^{\epsilon}
is very close to a Dirac delta function (hence singular), this continuity claim is difficult to prove directly. The main bulk of subsequent work is to apply the so-called Itô-Tanaka trick to (

), so that instead of working with the function
\theta^{\epsilon}
, we work equivalently with a more regular function

(more regular in the sense not for fixed
\epsilon
but for

tending to zero). Roughly speaking, it is given by a convolution of
\theta^{\epsilon}
with the Green’s function of a hypoelliptic operator, see (
38
). Since the Green’s function is not integrable at infinity and
\theta^{\epsilon}
is a function of

only, we seek additional localization in the variable

. This is the reason we introduce the function
\chi^{K}
, as it will be part of an auxiliary
pde
(

) we set up for

.

Remark 6

By the assumption
\theta(0)=0
, we can have a version of Proposition
5
where the double sum is over all

(including repeated indices

). Indeed, there are at most

By the assumption

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\eta^{\delta}(z_{1})\eta^{\delta}(z_{2})dz_{1}dz_{2}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)-z_{1}+z_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cdot\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt+\text{Err}(N,\delta).

Indeed, any
z_{1},z_{2}
in the support of
\eta^{\delta}
in the last line have modulus less than
\delta
, hence the stochastic error depends only on
N
and
\delta
. Next, since
\phi,\psi
are test functions, shifting their
x

respectively causes a stochastic error on the order of
\delta
(here recall (
11
)), hence the preceding display is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\eta^{\delta}(z_{1})\eta^{\delta}(z_{2})dz_{1}dz_{2}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)-z_{1}+z_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cdot\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t)-z_{1},v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t)-z_{2},v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt+\text{Err}(N,\delta).

By a change of variables
w_{1}=x_{i}^{N}(t)-z_{1}
,
w_{2}=x_{j}^{N}(t)-z_{2}
, we can rewrite the above as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\eta^{\delta}\left(x_{i}^{N}(t)-w_{1}\right)\eta^{\delta}\big(x_{j}^{N}(t)-w_{2}\big)\theta^{\epsilon}\big(w_{1}-w_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cdot\phi\big(t,w_{1},v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(w_{2},v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dw_{1}dw_{2}dt+\text{Err}(N,\delta).

Since
|w_{1}-w_{2}|<\epsilon
in the support of
\theta^{\epsilon}
, we shift the
w_{2}
in
\psi\big(w_{2},v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)
to
w_{1}
causing an error on the order of

. Since in our claim

before
\delta\to 0
, this error is negligible and can be combined into Err
(N,\delta)
. Similar, shifting the
w_{2}
in the argument of
\eta^{\delta}\big(x_{j}^{N}(t)-w_{2}\big)
to
w_{1}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\eta^{\delta}\left(x_{i}^{N}(t)-w_{1}\right)\eta^{\delta}\big(x_{j}^{N}(t)-w_{1}\big)\theta^{\epsilon}\big(w_{1}-w_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cdot\phi\big(t,w_{1},v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(w_{1},v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,)dw_{1}dw_{2}dt+\text{Err}(N,\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\eta^{\delta}\left(x_{i}^{N}(t)-w_{1}\right)\eta^{\delta}\big(x_{j}^{N}(t)-w_{1}\big)\phi\big(t,w_{1},v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(w_{1},v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dw_{1}dt+\text{Err}(N,\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}dw_{1}\left\langle\eta^{\delta}(x_{1}-w_{1})\eta^{\delta}(x_{2}-w_{1})\phi(t,w_{1},v_{1})\psi(w_{1},v_{2}),\;\mu_{t}^{N}(dx_{1},dv_{1})\mu_{t}^{N}(dx_{2},dv_{2})\right\rangle dt+\text{Err}(N,\delta),

where one of the integral in

is removed using the property
\int\theta^{\epsilon}(w_{1}-w_{2})dw_{2}=1
for any

.
The last equality is a rewritting in terms of the empirical measure

.

3
The Itô-Tanaka trick

In this section, we apply the so-called Itô-Tanaka trick to (
15
). The trick itself is a classical one in stochastic analysis, related to Tanaka’s formula in local time, see e.g.
[
11
, pp. 4]
for an account, nevertheless its use in a particle system context may be quite recent, which we learned from Hammond and Rezakhanlou
[
16
]
. Roughly speaking, its aim is to replace a term of low regularity equivalently by a series of other terms involving functions of better regularity, by applying inversely the Itô’s formula.

For the above-fixed test functions
\phi,\psi
, and for any fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, consider the process

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{t}:=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(t):\,j\neq i}H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big),\quad t\geq 0

(16)

which is also a functional of the configuration
\zeta(t)
,
where

\displaystyle H^{\epsilon,z}(x,v):=U^{\epsilon}(x+z,v)-U^{\epsilon}(x,v)

(17)

and
U^{\epsilon}(x,v):\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function of class
C^{\infty}
to be defined in (
38
). Applying Itô’s formula to
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{T}-\mathcal{Y}_{0}

(18)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\big(v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\cdot\nabla_{x}H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt

(19)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\Delta_{v}H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt

(20)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(s)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\partial_{t}\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(s),v_{i}^{N}(s)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(s),v_{j}^{N}(s)\big)H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,ds

(21)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\Big[v_{i}^{N}(t)\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi+v_{j}^{N}(t)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\Big]H^{\epsilon,z}\,dt

(22)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\Big[\Delta_{v}\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)+\Delta_{v}\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\Big]H^{\epsilon,z}\,dt

(23)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{2}{N^{3}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{k:k\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\sum_{j:j\neq i,k}H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt

(24)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{2}{N^{3}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{k:k\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\sum_{j:j\neq i,k}H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{j}^{N}(t)-x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)-v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt

(25)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{2}{N^{3}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{k:k\neq i}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t)\big)H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\,dt

(26)

\displaystyle+\widetilde{M}_{T}^{D}+\widetilde{M}_{T}^{J},

(27)

where we omitted some arguments for brevity as they are self-evident. Specifically, in (
24
)-(
26
) we used the fact that if a pair
(i,k)
of distinct particles coagulate at time
t
(which occurs at rate
q_{ik}^{N}(t)
(
9
)), the double sum (
16
) decreases by an amount of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq i,k}H^{\epsilon,z}\left(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\right)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq i,k}H^{\epsilon,z}\left(x_{k}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{k}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\right)\phi\big(t,x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq i,k}H^{\epsilon,z}\left(x_{j}^{N}(t)-x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)-v_{i}^{N}(t)\right)\phi\big(t,x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq i,k}H^{\epsilon,z}\left(x_{j}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)-v_{k}^{N}(t)\right)\phi\big(t,x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{k}^{N}(t)\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}}H^{\epsilon,z}\left(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{k}^{N}(t)\right)\phi\big(t,x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{k}^{N}(t)\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}}H^{\epsilon,z}\left(x_{k}^{N}(t)-x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{k}^{N}(t)-v_{i}^{N}(t)\right)\phi\big(t,x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big).

Since
\theta
is symmetric, hence
\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t)\big)=\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{k}^{N}(t)-x_{i}^{N}(t)\big)
, after summing over all distinct pairs
(i,k)
of the above six terms, we can switch the indices of
i
and
k
and regroup them into the claimed three sums.

The martingale associated with diffusion is

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{T}^{D}:=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}(\nabla_{v}H^{\epsilon,z}\phi\psi+H^{\epsilon,z}\psi\nabla_{v}\phi)\,dB_{i}(t)+(-\nabla_{v}H^{\epsilon,z}\phi\psi+H^{\epsilon,z}\phi\nabla_{v}\psi)\,dB_{j}(t),

hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[|\widetilde{M}_{T}^{D}|^{2}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{N^{4}}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\Big|\sum_{j:j\neq i}(\nabla_{v}H^{\epsilon,z}\phi\psi+H^{\epsilon,z}\psi\nabla_{v}\phi)\Big|^{2}dt

(28)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{N^{4}}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\Big|\sum_{i:i\neq j}(-\nabla_{v}H^{\epsilon,z}\phi\psi+H^{\epsilon,z}\phi\nabla_{v}\psi)\Big|^{2}dt.

(29)

The martingale associated with jumps can be bounded via

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\big[|\widetilde{M}_{T}^{J}|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{k:k\neq i}\frac{1}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\Big[\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j:j\neq i,k}H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\psi\Big]^{2}dt

(30)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{k:k\neq i}\frac{1}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\Big[\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j:j\neq i,k}H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{j}^{N}(t)-x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)-v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\psi\Big]^{2}dt

(31)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{k:k\neq i}\frac{1}{N}\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\Big[\frac{1}{N^{2}}H^{\epsilon,z}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{k}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{k}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\psi\Big]^{2}dt,

(32)

cf.
[
5
, Proposition 8.7]
.

The only term with second derivatives of
H^{\epsilon,z}
from the above development is (
20
). In order to substitute the highest derivative (also together with (
19
)), we set up the following subelliptic equation for
U^{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle v\cdot\nabla_{x}U^{\epsilon}(x,v)+\Delta_{v}U^{\epsilon}(x,v)=-\theta^{\epsilon}(x)\chi^{K}(v).

(33)

We will choose in (
38
) a particular solution of (
33
), which is the convolution of the
rhs
with the subelliptic Green’s function. Recall in (
17
) we have defined

H^{\epsilon,z}(x,v):=U^{\epsilon}(x+z,v)-U^{\epsilon}(x,v).

(
33
) yields, by linearity, the equation satisfied by
H^{\epsilon,z}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
, for any fixed
z
:

\displaystyle v\cdot\nabla_{x}H^{\epsilon,z}(x,v)+\Delta_{v}H^{\epsilon,z}(x,v)=\big[\theta^{\epsilon}(x)-\theta^{\epsilon}(x+z)\big]\chi^{K}(v).

(34)

Consequently, we can substitute (
19
)-(
20
) as follows

\displaystyle\eqref{2nd-order-1}+\eqref{2nd-order-2}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\sum_{j:j\neq i}

\displaystyle\cdot\big[\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)\big)-\theta^{\epsilon}\big(x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)+z\big)\big]\chi^{K}\big(v_{i}^{N}(t)-v_{j}^{N}(t)\big)\phi\big(x_{i}^{N}(t),v_{i}^{N}(t)\big)\psi\big(x_{j}^{N}(t),v_{j}^{N}(t)\big).

The
rhs
is precisely the expression appearing in Proposition
5
.

We remark at this stage that
\nabla_{x}H^{\epsilon,z}
does not appear any more in the rest of terms coming from the Itô’s formula applied to
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
, after it is absorbed into the operator on the
lhs
of (
34
). Its estimate is expected to be bad due to the degeneracy of the subellitic operator in the direction of
x
. What do appear in the rest of the terms are
\nabla_{v}H^{\epsilon,z}
and
H^{\epsilon,z}
itself, whose estimates are nice enough. We will show in the next sections that all the other terms (
18
), (
21
)-(
27
) (i.e. apart from (
19
)-(
20
)) are negligible in
L^{1}(\Omega)
in the order of limits
N\to\infty
followed by
|z|\to 0
, as required for Proposition
5
.

We can thus summarise the use of Itô-Tanaka trick (
33
) in our context as follows. It allows to transfer proving a continuity claim Proposition
5
involving a nearly singular function
\theta^{\epsilon}
(for
\epsilon
small), into proving similar claims involving more regular functions
\nabla_{v}H^{\epsilon,z}
and
H^{\epsilon,z}
. The estimates for the latter can be equivalently obtained from estimates for
U^{\epsilon}
, which is a convolution of
\theta^{\epsilon}(x)\chi^{K}(v)
with a subelliptic Green’s function. This convolution is a sort of regularization. Indeed, in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
,
U^{\epsilon}
does not tend to a singular object, but rather to a pointwise evaluation (in
x
) of the Green’s function (suitably integrated in
v
). This is the reason why pointwise estimates and gradient estimates (in
v
) of the associated Green’s function are key to our analysis.

4
The hypoelliptic Green’s function

Denote the operator

\displaystyle L:=v\cdot\nabla_{x}+\Delta_{v}

(35)

acting on appropriate functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, and we rewrite the
pde
(
33
) more compactly as

\displaystyle LU^{\epsilon}=-\theta^{\epsilon}(x)\chi^{K}(v).

(36)

It is well-known that
L
(
35
) is an hypoelliptic operator in the sense of Hörmander
[
17
]
, and its parabolic version (i.e.
\partial_{t}-L
) was earlier studied by Kolmogorov
[
20
]
who wrote down its kernel explicitly. cf.
[
1
]
for a recent survey. Crucial for our purpose are pointwise estimates for the Green’s function of
-L
.

To briefly recall that
L
is hypoelliptic, let us write

\displaystyle L=A_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}^{2}

(37)

where

\displaystyle A_{i}=\partial_{v_{i}},\quad i=1,...,d;\quad A_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}.

Since the commutators

\displaystyle[A_{0},A_{i}]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}(\partial_{v_{i}})-\partial_{v_{i}}(\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}\partial_{x_{j}})=-\partial_{x_{i}},\quad i=1,...,d

we see that at every point in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

\displaystyle\text{span}\big\{A_{i},[A_{0},A_{i}],\;i=1,...,d\big\}=\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

Thus
L
satisfies Hörmander’s condition
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
,
[
24
, pp. 128]
, implying that it is an hypoelliptic operator; that is, if
u
is a distribution such that
Lu
is
C^{\infty}
, then
u
must be
C^{\infty}
as well, cf.
[
17
, pp. 147]
,
[
24
, pp. 129]
.
Since the
rhs
of (
36
) is of class
C^{\infty}
with compact support (for every fixed
\epsilon>0
), we can find a solution of the form

\displaystyle U^{\epsilon}(x,v)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}G\big((x,v),(y,w)\big)\theta^{\epsilon}(y)\chi^{K}(w)dydw,

(38)

where
G=G_{L}
is a distribution of class
C^{\infty}
away from the diagonal, called the Green’s function of
-L
, see e.g.
[
30
, pp. 143]
,
[
10
, pp. 247]
. The definition of hypoellipticity implies that
U^{\epsilon}
is of class
C^{\infty}
for every fixed
\epsilon
; in particular, this is the function that is used in Section
3
to define
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
.

To state the classical results of
[
23
,
30
,
10
]
on the estimates for the Green’s function of general hypoelliptic operators, we recall that they are expressed in terms of a control metric (or Carnot-Carathéodory metric)
\rho_{L}(X,Y)
canonically attached to
L
(to avoid confusion, we use capital letters
X=(x,v),Y=(y,w),...
to denote variables in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
). We also set here

\displaystyle D:=2d.

Let (in our case)
q=1+2d
and
\{V_{j},j=1,...,q\}
are an enumeration of

\displaystyle V_{1}:=A_{0},\;V_{i+1}:=A_{i},\;V_{i+d+1}:=[A_{0},A_{i}],\quad i=1,...,d.

As explained in
[
23
, pp.107]
,
[
29
, Section 18]
, for operators of the form (
37
) which are not just a sum-of-squares but with
A_{0}
, the degree of
A_{0}
counts
2
while the degree of each
A_{i}
counts
1
, and likewise for their commutators. Hence, in our case we have that

\displaystyle d_{1}=2,\;d_{i+1}=1,\;d_{i+d+1}=3,\quad i=1,...,d,

where
d_{j}
is the degree of
V_{j}
.

Definition 8
(
[
23
, Definition 1.1]
)

Let
C(\delta)
denote the class of absolutely continuous mappings
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
which almost everywhere satisfy the differential equation

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}a_{j}(t)V_{j}(\phi(t))

(39)

with
|a_{j}(t)|\leq\delta^{d_{j}}
. Then define

\displaystyle\rho_{L}(X,Y):=\inf\big\{\delta>0:\;\exists\phi\in C(\delta),\phi(0)=X,\phi(1)=Y\big\}.

Remark 9

The geometric meaning of this definition is as follows. The ball of radius
\delta
around the point
X
in the control metric
\rho_{L}
consists of those points
Y
that can be reached at time
t=1
by a “particle” (whose trajectory is denoted
\phi(t)
,
t\in[0,1]
), that starts at
t=0
from
X
and travels with velocity the
rhs
of (
39
). The velocity at time
t
has direction controlled by the vector fields
V_{j}
evaluated at “particle” position, and modulus controlled together by the strength of the vector fields and the scalar multipliers
a_{j}(t)
.

It is known that
\rho_{L}
is a metric and in our case, it compares with the Euclidean distance
|\cdot|
in the following way
[
23
, Proposition 1.1]
: for any compact set
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, there are constants
C_{i}=C_{i}(\mathbb{K},d)
,
i=1,2
, such that for any
X,Y\in\mathbb{K}
we have that

\displaystyle C_{1}|X-Y|\leq\rho_{L}(X,Y)\leq C_{2}|X-Y|^{1/3}.

(40)

Given the control metric
\rho_{L}
we define balls in this metric of radius
\delta>0
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{L}(X,\delta):=\big\{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:\rho_{L}(X,Y)\leq\delta\big\}.

These balls are locally volume doubling as proved in
[
23
]
.

Theorem 10
(
[
23
, Theorem 1, pp. 110]
)

For every compact set
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, there exist constants

Lemma 13

6
Tightness and subsequential convergence

We show that for any finite

, the laws of the sequence

Proposition 19

If (a). there exists constant finite
C_{1}
such that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\big(|x|+|v|\big)\mu_{t}^{N}(dx,dv)\leq C_{1};

and (b). there exists some

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big|\langle\mu_{\tau+\sigma}^{N},\,\phi\rangle-\langle\mu_{\tau}^{N},\,\phi\rangle\big|\leq C(\phi)\,\gamma^{\beta};

Proof.
Tightness of a collection of laws on a Skorohod space

E
is a complete separable metric space, is well-studied in the literature; it builds on the characterization of compact sets in these spaces, e.g.
[
3

[
8

[
19

Specializing to our case,

[

[
13

\displaystyle\delta(\mu,\nu):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left[\left|\langle\mu,\phi_{k}\rangle-\langle\nu,\phi_{k}\rangle\right|\wedge 1\right],\quad\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{+.1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}),

(61)

where
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})

Recall
[
19

that a sequence of probability law

\iota>0

\displaystyle\sup_{N}\mathsf{P}_{N}\left(\mu_{t}\not\in K(t,\iota)\right)<\iota.

(62)

(b’). For every
\iota>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{T},\,\sigma\leq\gamma}\mathsf{P}_{N}\left(\delta(\mu_{\tau},\mu_{\tau+\sigma})>\iota\right)=0,

(63)

where

is the set of stopping times

Now recall that given

59

7
Uniqueness of weak solution

Definition 23

Assume
f_{0}\in L^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
(the space of nonnegative integrable functions) satisfies Condition
1
, we say that

\displaystyle f\in L^{\infty}\big([0,T],L^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\big)

(68)

is a weak solution to (
5
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)&=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}f-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x,v)f(t,x,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}\\
f|_{t=0}&=f_{0}(x,v)\end{cases},\quad(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

if for any
\phi(t,x,v)\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and
t\in[0,T]
we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(t,x,v)\phi(t,x,v)dxdv

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{0}(x,v)\phi(0,x,v)dxdv

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\big(\partial_{s}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\big)\phi(s,x,v)f(s,x,v)dxdvds

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3d}}f(s,x,v)f(s,x,v^{\prime})\phi(s,x,v)dxdvdv^{\prime}dt.

Proposition 24

For every finite
T
and
d\geq 1
, weak solitions to (
5
) are unique.

Proof.
As in Lemma
13
, we denote the operator

\displaystyle L^{*}=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v},

and by
\{P(t)\}_{t>0}
the Markov semigroup associated with
L^{*}
. It is a strongly continuous semigroup on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, for
1\leq p<\infty
, see
[
15
, Corollary 2.6]
, which we also denote by
P(t)=e^{tL^{*}}
. Let
f_{1},f_{2}
be two weak solutions of (
5
) in the sense of Definition
23
, and set

\overline{f}:=f_{1}-f_{2}.

Then,
\overline{f}
satisfies in weak form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}-L^{*})\overline{f}(t,x,v)&=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{1}(t,x,v)\overline{f}(t,x,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}(t,x,v)f_{2}(t,x,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}\\
\overline{f}|_{t=0}&=0.\end{cases}

That is, for any
\phi(t,x,v)\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and
t\in[0,T]
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\overline{f}(t,x,v)\phi(t,x,v)dxdv

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\big(\partial_{s}+L\big)\phi(s,x,v)\overline{f}(s,x,v)dxdvds

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3d}}\left[f_{1}(s,x,v)\overline{f}(s,x,v^{\prime})+\overline{f}(s,x,v)f_{2}(s,x,v^{\prime})\right]\phi(s,x,v)\,dv^{\prime}dxdvds.

A weak solution is also a mild solution. Indeed, for each
t
taking
\phi(s,x,v)=\left(e^{(t-s)L}\widetilde{\phi}\right)(x,v)
for
\widetilde{\phi}(x,v)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\overline{f}(t,x,v)\widetilde{\phi}(x,v)dxdv=-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3d}}\left[f_{1}(s,x,v)\overline{f}(s,x,v^{\prime})+\overline{f}(s,x,v)f_{2}(s,x,v)\right]\left(e^{(t-s)L}\widetilde{\phi}\right)(x,v)dxdvdv^{\prime}ds

\displaystyle=-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}e^{(t-s)L^{*}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{1}(s,\cdot,\cdot)\overline{f}(s,\cdot,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}(s,\cdot,\cdot)f_{2}(s,\cdot,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}\right)(x,v)\,\widetilde{\phi}(x,v)\,dxdvds.

By the arbitrariness of
\widetilde{\phi}
, we see that
\overline{f}
satisfies

\displaystyle\overline{f}(t,x,v)=-2\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)L^{*}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{1}(s,\cdot,\cdot)\overline{f}(s,\cdot,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}(s,\cdot,\cdot)f_{2}(s,\cdot,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}\right)(x,v)ds.

(69)

Since
P(t)=e^{tL^{*}}
is a contraction in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, cf.
[
27
, pp. 255, last line]
,
[
15
, Lemma 2.4 (iv)]
, in particular when
p=1
, we have by taking
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
-norm of (
69
) that for any
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|\overline{f}(t,x,v)\right|dxdv

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{t}\left\|e^{(t-s)L^{*}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{1}(s,\cdot,\cdot)\overline{f}(s,\cdot,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}(s,\cdot,\cdot)f_{2}(s,\cdot,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}ds

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{1}(s,\cdot,\cdot)\overline{f}(s,\cdot,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}(s,\cdot,\cdot)f_{2}(s,\cdot,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}ds

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3d}}f_{1}(s,x,v)\left|\overline{f}(s,x,v^{\prime})\right|dxdvdv^{\prime}ds+2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3d}}\left|\overline{f}(s,x,v)\right|f_{2}(s,x,v^{\prime})dxdvdv^{\prime}ds.

(70)

By a simple comparison, we have that

\displaystyle 0\leq f_{i}(t,x,v)\leq g(t,x,v),\quad i=1,2,

where
g
is the unique (weak) solution to

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-L^{*})g(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=0.

\displaystyle g|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=f_{0}(x,v).

Further,
g(t)=P(t)f_{0}
is precisely the function
\mathsf{p}(t,x,v)
in (
50
) hence, since
f_{0}
satisfies Condition

, we have that for some finite constant
C(T,\Gamma,R,d)
and uniformly for all

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{i}(t,x,v)dv\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,x,v)dv\leq C(T,\Gamma,R,d),\quad i=1,2.

(71)

Hence, we obtain by (
7

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{f}(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|\overline{f}(s,x,v^{\prime})\right|dxdv^{\prime}ds+C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|\overline{f}(s,x,v)\right|dxdvds

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\overline{f}(s)\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}ds.

By Gronwall’s lemma, we have that

Since any weak subsequential limit obtained in Proposition

to the unique weak solution of the

. Since the limit is deterministic, we actually have convergence in probability.
