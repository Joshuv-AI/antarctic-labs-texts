Superized Leznov-Saveliev equations as the zero-curvature condition on a reduced connection
Dimitry Leites

Abstract
. The equations of open 2-dimensional Toda lattice (TL) correspond to Leznov-Saveliev equations (LSE) interpreted as zero-curvature Yang-Mills equations on the variety of O ⁡ ( 3 ) O(3) -orbits on the Minkowski space when the gauge algebra is the image of 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 2 ) {\mathfrak{sl}}(2) under a principal embedding into a simple finite-dimensional Lie algebra 𝔤 ⁡ ( A ) \mathfrak{g}(A) with Cartan matrix A A . The known integrable super versions of TL equations correspond to matrices A A of two different types. I interpret the super LSE of one type 1 as zero-curvature equations for the reduced connection on the non-integrable distribution on the supervariety of O ​ S ​ p ​ ( 1 | 2 ) OSp(1|2) -orbits on the N = 1 N=1 -extended Minkowski superspace; the Leznov-Saveliev method of solution is applicable only to 𝔤 ⁡ ( A ) \mathfrak{g}(A) finite-dimensional and admitting a superprincipal embedding 𝔬 ​ 𝔰 ​ 𝔭 ​ ( 1 | 2 ) ⟶ 𝔤 ⁡ ( A ) \mathfrak{osp}(1|2)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}(A) . The simplest LSE1 is the super Liouville equation; it can be also interpreted in terms of the superstring action. Olshanetsky introduced LSE2 — another type of equations of super TL. Olshanetsky’s equations, as well as LSE1 with infinite-dimensional 𝔤 ⁡ ( A ) \mathfrak{g}(A) , can be solved by the Inverse Scattering Method. To interpret these equations remains an open problem, except for the super Liouville equation — the only case where these two types of LSE coincide. I also review related less known and less popular mathematical constructions involved.

1.
Introduction

The zero curvature gauge field equations were superized in two ways yielding solvable equations. Their meaning was not, however, explained except for the simplest case of super Liouville equation whose interpretation is not related with gauge fields, but with a superstring action, see
[
Po
]
.

Here, I recall two approaches to solutions of zero-curvature gauge field equations. I review the subtleties of their superizations needed to understand what is going on, e.g., appearance of integrable forms, see their definition (
9
). Vital in the
super
version of gauge field (a.k.a. Yang-Mills) equations is a
non-integrable distribution
on the supermanifold considered, such as Minkowski supermanifold; this was never explained before. The methods of solution in these approaches are different and the ranges of their applicability intersect in one case only.

1.1.
From the Yang–Mills equations to the variety of
\text{O}(3)
-orbits in the Minkowski space and further to zero-curvature equations with the gauge algebra
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)\subset{\mathfrak{g}}(A)
. Two methods to solve these equations

In physics, a
gauge theory
is a type of field theory in which the dynamics of the system is invariant under local transformations performed by Lie groups, or — infinitesimally — Lie algebras.

The equations of gauge theories — called
Yang–Mills
equations — are too complicated to be explicitly solved; solutions are found only under some additional restrictions.

1) The Leznov–Saveliev method
. Under additional spatial
\text{O}(3)
-symmetry, the 4-dimensional Minkowski space reduces to a 2-dimensional cylinder with 2 parameters: the radius
r
of the
\text{O}(3)
-orbit (the sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
) and the time
t
. In the simplest case of zero-curvature conditions on the gauge field, these equations — called
Leznov–Saveliev
equations (LSE) — can be solved; the method of solution requires the complexified gauge Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
to be of finite dimension and admitting a principle embedding of
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)
. The Leznov–Saveliev equations do not, however, consider abelian theories, they are applicable only to simple finite-dimensional gauge Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}
, hence, of the form
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{g}}(A)
with Cartan matrix
A
; so the LSE are of interest as integrable systems, but are hardly applicable to certain current models of particle physics with abelian summand in the gauge algebra.

Explicitly, the
Leznov-Saveliev equations
constitute the system (
1
) of equations for a vector-valued function
F=(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
, where
n=\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\nolimits{\mathfrak{g}}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{g}}(A)
is a complex Lie algebra with Cartan matrix
A:=(A_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
, each
F_{i}
depends on
x
and
y
which are complexified linear combination of
r
and
t
:

(1)

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.25pt}{$\dfrac{\partial^{2}F_{i}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{0.25pt}{$\dfrac{\partial^{2}F_{i}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\tfrac{\partial^{2}F_{i}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\tfrac{\partial^{2}F_{i}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}=\exp\left(\sum_{j}A_{ij}F_{j}\right),\text{~~where $i=1,\dots,n$}.

Sometimes, the term
Leznov-Saveliev equations
is applied to the following system of equations for a vector-valued function
F=(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
, where each
F_{i}
depends on
x
and
y
:

(2)

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.25pt}{$\dfrac{\partial^{2}G_{i}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{0.25pt}{$\dfrac{\partial^{2}G_{i}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\tfrac{\partial^{2}G_{i}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\tfrac{\partial^{2}G_{i}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}\exp(G_{j}),\text{~~where $i=1,\dots,n$}.

If the matrix
A
is invertible (in the simplest case,
{\mathfrak{g}}(A)
is finite-dimensional and simple), then the system (
1
) is equivalent to the system (
2
): set
G=AF
, and hence
F=A^{-1}G
.

If
A
is the
n\times n
Cartan matrix of a simple finite-dimensional Lie algebra (over
{\mathbb{C}}
), then the general solution of eq. (
1
) is a vector depending on (the logarithm in)
2n
polynomials in 2 arbitrary analytic functions each (one function depending on
x
, the other one on
y
) and their derivatives, see
[
LS
,
LSb
]
.

2) The Inverse Scattering Method
. This powerful method was discovered earlier than the LS-method; it is applicable not only to finite-dimensional gauge algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}(A)
but also to algebras of infinite-dimension (namely, affine Kac-Moody algebra a.k.a.
double extension
of the loop algebra, see the last two Sections in
[
BLS
]
), see, e.g.,
[
AC
,
AN
]
. This method allows one to solve versions of Leznov–Saveliev equations (sine-Gordon and sinh-Gordon equations in the simplest cases) to which the Leznov–Saveliev method is inapplicable: there are no principal embeddings of
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)
into the infinite-dimensional Lie algebras.

If
A
is the Cartan matrix of an infinite-dimensional Kac-Moody Lie algebra, the solution is obtained via ISM in terms of theta-functions, see
[
AN
]
.

1.2.
Superization of Leznov-Saveliev equations via the LS-approach

It seems natural to consider equations (
1
) and (
2
) where
A
is any Cartan matrix of any finite-dimensional or Kac-Moody Lie superalgebra. However, to this day only two types of Cartan matrices were considered in order to get a solution of these equations, and only one of these two types of equations has an interpretation: it is given in this paper.

1.2.1.
Non-integrable distributions.

Recall that a
distribution

{\mathcal{D}}
on the manifold or supermanifold
M
is a subbundle of the tangent bundle
TM
; it is given by selecting a subspace
{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
of the tangent space
T_{m}M
at every point
m\in M
; we assume that all spaces
{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
are of the same dimension

and smoothly depend on

. The distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
is

at a point

k

containing

such that

.
For integrability of the distribution, there is the following criterion true on supermanifolds as well.

The Frobenius Criterion for integrability of the distribution (
[
Lg
, Ch. VI]
)

The distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
is integrable if and only if its sections form a Lie subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{vect}}(M)
closed under the Lie bracket of vector fields
.

1.3.
Olshanetsky’s approach: superization LSE2

M. Olshanetsky suggested a completely different approach to superizing TL equations, see
[
O
]
. Olshanetsky solved these LSE2 by means of the Inverse Scattering Method. Interestingly, the ISM is applicable to infinite-dimensional gauge Lie superalgebra, as in periodic TL (provided it is “not too big”: namely be not of exponential growth, as hyperbolic algebras or almost affine superalgebras as described in
[

(5)

not necessarily finite-dimensional, can be
{\mathbb{Z}}
-graded of polynomial growth, and its Cartan matrix should have only 2s and 1s on its main diagonal

Observe that such Lie superalgebras have various properties almost identical to those of finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras — unlike properties of Lie superalgebras with Cartan matrices of different shape or without Cartan matrix at all.

Observe that the equations suggested by Olshanetsky are more complicated than eq. (

]

Following

2.1.
The Yang–Mills equation

The
Yang-Mills equation
is

(7)

\nabla^{\mathop{\mathrm{End}}\nolimits(V)}((F_{\nabla})^{*})=0.

Observe that the “zero curvature condition”
F_{\nabla}=0
is invariant under any changes of coordinates
and does not depend on the choice of one of the two definitions of the connection. The “zero curvature condition” is the simplest version of YME.

On the
n
-dimensional manifold
M
, we have

(8)

(\Omega^{i})^{*}\simeq\Omega^{n-i},

where the dualization is considered over the algebra
{\mathcal{F}}=\Omega^{0}
of functions, not over the ground field. Although the isomorphism (
8

Similarly, on the supermanifold
{\mathcal{M}}
, the dual to the space of
differential
i
-forms
defined to be elements of the space
\Omega^{i}:=T(E^{i}(V))
, where

is the operator of raising to the

is the tautological
{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)={\mathfrak{gl}}(\dim({\mathcal{M}}))
-module, is the space of
integrable
(-i)
-forms

(9)

\Sigma_{-i}({\mathcal{M}}):=(\Omega^{i}({\mathcal{M}}))^{*}\otimes_{\mathcal{F}}\mathop{\mathrm{{V\hskip-2.36806pto\hskip-0.08612ptl\/}}}\nolimits({\mathcal{M}})\simeq T(E^{i}(V^{*})\otimes\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits),

where
\mathop{\mathrm{{V\hskip-2.36806pto\hskip-0.08612ptl\/}}}\nolimits({\mathcal{M}})

-module of volume forms (a.k.a. Berezinian), and

2.2.
The Leznov-Saveliev equations

Here is how Yang-Mills equations turn into LSE.

Recall that the Minkowski space
M
is, locally,
{\mathbb{R}}^{3,1}
on which the orthogonal groups
\text{O}(3)
naturally acts rotating its
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
part. The variety of orbits are 2-dimensional “cylinders”, each being “
\text{sphere $S^{2}$}\times\text{Time}
”, with coordinates being the radius of the sphere
r
and the Time parameter
t
. Clearly, the Poincaré group preserving the Lorentz metric contains
\text{O}(3)
. Now, let us complexify everything and consider the linear approximation, i.e., pass from Lie groups to their Lie algebras.
Consider a connection whose gauge Lie algebra is the image of the principal embedding
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)\longrightarrow{\mathfrak{g}}
into the Lie algebra of the gauge group
G
. The LSE are the zero curvature conditions on the variety of
\text{O}(3)
-orbits.

Recall that an embedding
i:{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)\longrightarrow{\mathfrak{g}}
, where
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a simple finite-dimensional Lie algebra, is called
principal
, if
{\mathfrak{g}}
splits into
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\nolimits{\mathfrak{g}}
irreducible
i({\mathfrak{sl}}(2))
-submodules. Every simple finite-dimensional Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
admits a principal embedding, all these embeddings are isomorphic under the Lie group of automorphisms of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
After complexification and passage to the “light cone coordinates”, i.e., instead of d’Alembertian in
r
and
t
we consider the Laplacian in
x
and
y
, we arrive at the LSE (
1
).

The 1-dimensional Toda lattice (TL) is related to its version described by the Leznov-Saveliev equations as explained on the first page of
[
Ki
]
; both TL and LSE depend on 2 variables, but — for reasons unknown — only LSE is called “2-dimensional”.

2.2.1.
The simplest example.

Consider the following basis of
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{sl}}(2)
:

X^{-}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ H:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\ \ X^{+}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

In terms of the first definition of connection, Leznov and Saveliev (see
[
LS
,
LSb
]
) considered the following
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)
-valued connection

(11)

\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla_{\partial_{x}}:=\partial_{x}+aH+bX^{+},\\
\nabla_{\partial_{y}}:=\partial_{y}+AH+BX^{-}.\end{array}

The zero-curvature condition takes the form

[\partial_{x}+aH+bX^{+},\ \ \partial_{y}+AH+BX^{-}]=0,

which is the same as (here
f_{x}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
and
f_{y}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
)

A_{x}H+B_{x}X^{-}-a_{y}H-b_{y}X^{+}-2aBX^{-}-2bAX^{+}+bBH=0

equivalent to the system of equations

\begin{array}[]{l}A_{x}-a_{y}=-bB,\\
B_{x}=2aB,\\
b_{y}=-2bA,\\
\end{array}\Longleftrightarrow\begin{array}[]{l}A_{x}-a_{y}=-bB,\\
(\ln B)_{x}=2a,\\
(\ln b)_{y}=-2A,\\
\end{array}

wherefrom we derive the Liouville equation (
13
)

(\ln b)_{xy}+(\ln B)_{xy}=2bB\Longleftrightarrow F_{xy}=2\exp(F),\text{~~where $F=bB$}.

2.2.2.
General LSE and zero-curvature equations.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}=\oplus{\mathfrak{g}}_{i}

-orbits in the Minkowski space:

(12)

\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla_{\partial_{x}}:=\partial_{x}+\sum(a_{i}H_{i}+b_{i}X_{i}^{+}),\\
\nabla_{\partial_{y}}:=\partial_{y}+\sum(A_{i}H_{i}+B_{i}X_{i}^{-}).\end{array}

The zero-curvature equation in this case leads to LSE only if
{\mathfrak{g}}
has a Cartan matrix; this LSE can be explicitly (resp. implicitly) solved only if
{\mathfrak{g}}
is finite-dimensional (resp. of polynomial growth in Chevalley generators), see
[

,
LSb
]
.

2.2.3.
The Liouville equation.

The
Liouville equation
can be written in various forms (see
[
Li
]
), in particular, as the simplest of Leznov–Saveliev equations:

(13)

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.25pt}{$\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{0.25pt}{$\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\tfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\tfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial_{x}\partial_{y}}$}}=\exp(2F).

Its general solution depends on two arbitrary analytic functions
f
and
g
:

(14)

F(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\ln\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.25pt}{$\dfrac{f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(y)}{(f(x)+g(y))^{2}}$}}{\raisebox{0.25pt}{$\dfrac{f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(y)}{(f(x)+g(y))^{2}}$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\tfrac{f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(y)}{(f(x)+g(y))^{2}}$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\tfrac{f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(y)}{(f(x)+g(y))^{2}}$}}.

This fact is obvious if we somehow find one solution (for example,
f(x)=x
and
g(y)=y
) and observe the conformal symmetry of the Liouville equation under the action of the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{witt}}(x)\oplus{\mathfrak{witt}}(y)
, where
{\mathfrak{witt}}(x):={\mathfrak{der}}{\mathbb{C}}[x^{-1},x]
, called the
Witt algebra
, is the Lie algebra of vector fields on the circle with coefficients expandable in Laurent polynomials, i.e.,
x:=\exp(i\varphi)
, where
\varphi
is the angle parameter on the circle, see
[
L
,
IvKr
]
. (Observe that it is absolutely not obvious that the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{vir}}(x)\oplus{\mathfrak{vir}}(y)
, where
{\mathfrak{vir}}(x)
is the
Viraroso algebra
, the only non-trivial central extension of
{\mathfrak{witt}}(x)
, also acts on the Liouville equation, but this is true, see
[
Top
]
. Physicists call this phenomenon a
classical anomaly
.)

2.3.1.
The simplest example.

Recall, see
[

(17)

\nabla:{\mathcal{D}}\times V\longrightarrow V

which is
{\mathcal{F}}
-linear in the first argument, additive in the second argument and satisfies the following version of the Leibniz rule, where
\nabla_{D}(v):=\nabla(D,v)

\nabla_{D}(fv)=D(f)v+(-1)^{p(f)p(D)}f\nabla_{D}(v)\text{~~for any $f\in{\mathcal{F}}$, $v\in V$, $D\in{\mathfrak{vect}}$}.

Observe that the supervariety of orbits of the
\text{OSp}(1|2)
-action on Minkowski supermanifold is of superdimension
2|2
, this supervariety inherits a non-integrable distribution induced by the distribution given on the Minkowski supermanifold, and the connection on the supervariety of orbits is determined by a reduced connection on the
(0|2)

Consider the following basis of

\begin{array}[]{l}X^{-}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\

1&0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ H:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\

\hskip 56.9055pt\delta^{-}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\

Let
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\bar{1}}
and

for an auxiliary supercommutative superalgebra
{\mathcal{C}}
which will automatically disappear at the final stage.

After simplification similar to the one performed in Subsection
2.2.1
, the
reduced zero-curvature condition

[\nabla_{+},\nabla_{-}]=0
for the

takes the form
equivalent to the system of equations

\begin{array}[]{l}D^{+}(\beta)+D^{-}(\alpha)=-ab,\\
D^{+}(b)=-\alpha b,\\
D^{-}(a)=a\beta,\\
\end{array}\Longleftrightarrow\begin{array}[]{l}D^{+}(\beta)+D^{-}(\alpha)=-ab,\\
D^{+}(\ln b)=-\alpha,\\
D^{-}(\ln a)=\beta,\\
\end{array}

wherefrom we derive the

(
