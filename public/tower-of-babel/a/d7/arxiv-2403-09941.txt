Abstract
. We solve constrained optimal transport problems in which the marginal laws are given by the laws of solutions of stochastic differential equations (SDEs). We consider SDEs with irregular coefficients, making only minimal regularity assumptions. We show that the so-called synchronous coupling is optimal among bicausal couplings, that is couplings that respect the flow of information encoded in the stochastic processes. Our results provide a method to numerically compute the adapted Wasserstein distance between laws of SDEs with irregular coefficients. We show that this can be applied to quantifying model uncertainty in stochastic optimisation problems. Moreover, we introduce a transformation-based semi-implicit numerical scheme and establish the first strong convergence result for SDEs with exponentially growing and discontinuous drift.

1.
Introduction

A fundamental question in stochastic modelling is that of quantifying the effects of model uncertainty. In this context it is of interest to compute an appropriate distance between different stochastic models. In particular, we focus on models that are described by stochastic differential equations (SDEs), potentially having irregular coefficients.
The main contribution of this paper is to provide a methodology to efficiently compute such a distance between the laws of SDEs, by solving an optimal transport problem. Our approach successfully brings together optimal transport and numerical analysis of SDEs. Moreover, for SDEs with exponentially growing and discontinuous drift, we prove strong well-posedness and strong convergence of a novel numerical scheme.

In order to compare stochastic models, a reasonable choice of distance is a modification of the Wasserstein distance on the space of probability measures. The Wasserstein distance arises from an optimal transport problem and is defined as the infimum of an expected cost over the set of couplings
\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)
between probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
, that is the set of probability measures on the product space having marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. This distance metrises the weak topology. However, in many situations the weak topology is unsuitable for comparing stochastic processes, since it does not take into account the information structure that is encoded in the filtrations generated by the processes. As a remedy, the adapted Wasserstein distance and associated bicausal optimal transport problem has been proposed. This constitutes the main object of study in this paper.

We consider stochastic models described by scalar SDEs of the form

(1.1)

\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],

with
X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, where
T\in(0,\infty)
,
b\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
are measurable functions, and
W=(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a standard scalar Brownian motion.
Now consider two different models with data
(b,\sigma,x_{0},W)
, respectively
(\bar{b},\bar{\sigma},x_{0},\bar{W})
.
For
\Omega=C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
,
p\geq 1
, and
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
being the set of all probability measures on
\Omega
with finite
p
th
moment, denote the law of the solutions of these SDEs by
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
, respectively
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
. Let
(\omega,\bar{\omega})
denote the canonical process on
\Omega\times\Omega
.
Then the adapted Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined by

(1.2)

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\omega_{t}-\bar{\omega}_{t}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!,

where we optimise over the set
\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)
of bicausal couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
, that is the set of couplings that satisfy an additional constraint that encodes the filtrations of the processes. Informally, this bicausality condition imposes that for any
t\in[0,T]
, the value
X_{t}
is conditionally independent of the process
\bar{X}
given
(\bar{X}_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
, with the symmetric condition when exchanging the roles of
X
and
\bar{X}
. In the context of optimisation problems for stochastic processes, such as those that arise in mathematical finance and mathematical biology, the causality condition appears natural. One is typically only able to make decisions based on information contained in the filtration of the process at the present time.

We aim to solve such a bicausal transport problem under minimal regularity assumptions on the coefficients of the SDE (
1.1
).
For one-dimensional SDEs (
1.1
) with smooth time-homogeneous coefficients,
Bion-Nadal and Talay 2019
exploit PDE methods to show that the so-called synchronous coupling attains the adapted Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{2}
. This coupling is the joint law of the SDEs when they are driven by a common Brownian motion. Following this work,
Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2024
show that the synchronous coupling attains
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
,
p\geq 1
, between the laws of SDEs with globally Lipschitz coefficients. The result of
[
8
]
holds also for continuous linearly growing coefficients, provided that pathwise uniqueness holds. However, no explicit conditions for this are given in
[
8
]
.

We show that optimality of the synchronous coupling holds in much greater generality, considering also SDEs with irregular coefficients.
We consider two classes of irregularity:

–

discontinuous drift that grows up to exponentially, degenerate diffusion;

–

bounded measurable drift, bounded diffusion that is
\alpha
-Hölder, with
\alpha\in [1/2,1]
.

Our main result can be stated informally as follows:

Main result
.

For models with data
(b,\sigma,x_{0},W)
, respectively
(\bar{b},\bar{\sigma},x_{0},\bar{W})
, each potentially having any one of the above irregularities, the synchronous coupling is optimal. Furthermore, one can compute the adapted Wasserstein distance by approximating each scalar SDE with any numerical scheme for which strong convergent rates are known.

Given that the synchronous coupling attains the adapted Wasserstein distance, the problem of computing this distance reduces to that of simulating two one-dimensional SDEs, using the same noise for each, and applying (multilevel-)Monte Carlo methods. Such computational efficiency is not usually expected, even in the case of classical optimal transport, without some regularisation. Moreover, this distance provides a computationally efficient measure of model uncertainty for optimal stopping problems.

The methods of proof in our paper are based on transformation and discretisation of SDEs. In the first case, for SDEs with discontinuous and exponentially growing drift, we introduce a transformation-based semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme for which we prove strong convergence. We design this scheme such that it is stochastically monotone. For measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying such a monotonicity condition, it is known that the well-studied Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement is an optimiser for the bicausal transport problem between them. Applying the strong convergence of the scheme and a stability result for bicausal optimal transport, we prove the optimality of the synchronous coupling. In the case of bounded and measurable drift, we apply the Zvonkin transformation to obtain SDEs with sufficiently regular coefficients, for which optimality results are already known. We show that such a transformation preserves the optimality property of the synchronous coupling. The novel strong convergence result in the first case can be stated informally as follows:

Auxiliary result
.

For the SDE (
1.1
) with drift
b
that has exponential growth and satisfies mild piecewise continuity conditions, and with diffusion
\sigma
that may be degenerate except at the points of discontinuity of
b
,

–

there exists a unique strong solution with bounded moments of all orders;

–

a transformation-based drift-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme converges strongly;

–

we provide convergence rates.

2.
Optimal transport and applications to stochastic processes

Here we introduce the concepts from optimal transport that will be needed for our applications. The field has a long history, originating with
Monge 1781
in 1781 and taken up again by
Kantorovich 1942
in 1942. For the interested reader, modern accounts can be found, for example, in
Villani 2003
,
Ambrosio et al. 2008
, and
Santambrogio 2015
, with the latter taking a more applied point of view.

2.1.
Introduction to optimal transport

Given separable metric spaces
(\mathcal{X},d)
and
(\mathcal{Y},\rho)
, let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
,
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y})
denote the respective Borel sigma-algebras, and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
the spaces of probability measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
,
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y}))
, respectively. Further, let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
denote the set of probability measures on the product space equipped with the product Borel sigma-algebra. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
,
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
, define the set of
couplings

\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)
by

(2.1)

\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\colon\;\pi(A,\mathcal{Y})=\mu(A),\;\pi(\mathcal{X},B)=\nu(B),\;\text{for all}\;A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y})\}.

Consider a lower semicontinuous
cost function

C\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.

The
optimal transport problem

[
40
]
is to find

(OT)

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)\\
\mathrm{Law}(X,Y)=\pi\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}[C(X,Y)],

supposing sufficient conditions on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\mu)
,
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y}),\nu)
and
C
such that the expectation in (
OT
) is well-defined for all
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)
.

2.1.1.
Brenier’s theorem

Central to the theory of optimal transport is the following key result of Brenier
[
15
]
:
an optimiser for the quadratic cost is given by the gradient of a convex function
.
More precisely, let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
be equipped with the Euclidean metric
|\cdot|
and set
c(x,y)\coloneqq|x-y|^{2}
. Suppose that
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Then (
OT
) admits a unique optimiser
\pi^{\mathrm{mono}}
and there exists a convex function
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\pi^{\mathrm{mono}}=(\mathrm{id},\nabla v)_{\#}\mu
, the pushforward of the measure
\mu
by the map
(\mathrm{id},\nabla v)\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In the one-dimensional case,
\nabla v=F^{-1}_{\nu}\circ F_{\mu}
, where
F_{\eta},F^{-1}_{\eta}
denote the cumulative distribution function and quantile function of a measure
\eta
, and the coupling
\pi^{\mathrm{mono}}
is known as the
monotone rearrangement
(or the
Hoeffding–Fréchet coupling
).

2.1.2.
Wasserstein distance

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}
be equipped with some metric
d
such that
(\mathcal{X},d)
is a Radon space. Then, following
[
3
, Section 7.1]
, a metric is induced on the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
, for any
p\geq 1
, by taking the cost function
C=d^{p}
in (

\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
the

-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)
is defined by

(2.2)

\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)\\
\mathrm{Law}(X,Y)=\pi\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}[d(X,Y)^{p}].

The
p
-Wasserstein distance metrises the weak topology on
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
, see
[
3
, Proposition 7.1.5]
, and is thus a natural candidate to compare probability measures on

.

2.2.
Optimal transport applied to stochastic processes

We now consider optimal transport between the laws of real-valued stochastic processes.
First we treat discrete-time processes.

Set
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Equip this space with the

norm, defined by
\|x\|_{p}^{p}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}
for

, for some

, and with the corresponding Borel sigma-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let

denote the canonical process on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
(x,\bar{x})

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}}

As stated in

2.2.1.
Bicausal optimal transport

We have seen that the drawback of the Wasserstein distance for comparing stochastic processes is that this distance is not able to distinguish different information structures. To rectify this shortcoming, we consider Wasserstein distances with the additional constraint that the couplings must respect the flow of information. This constraint has been formalised in various ways, including
the
Markov constructions
of
Rüschendorf 1985
, the
nested distances
of
Pflug and Pichler 2012
,
Pflug and Pichler 2014
, a stochastic control problem of
Bion-Nadal and Talay 2019
, the
causal transport plans
of
Lassalle 2018
, and the
adapted Wasserstein distance
introduced by
Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2020a
. Here we will use the terminology
adapted Wasserstein distance
, which we define below. Although there are other candidates for distances between stochastic processes, many such distances have been shown to be topologically equivalent to the adapted Wasserstein distance; see, for example,
[
7
,
14
]
.

Analogously to the classical Wasserstein distance, the adapted Wasserstein distance is induced by a
bicausal optimal transport
problem.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{F})
and let
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)
. The coupling
\pi
is
causal
if, for any random variables
X,Y
with
\mathrm{Law}(X,Y)=\pi
, we have the conditional independence,

(2.5)

\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{k}\;\text{is independent of}\;\mathcal{F}^{X}_{n}\;\text{under $\pi$ conditional on}\;\mathcal{F}^{X}_{k},

for all
k\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
. If
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)
is causal, and the coupling
\theta_{\#}\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\nu,\mu)
is causal, where
\theta(x,y)=(y,x)
, then we say that
\pi
is
bicausal
and we write
\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)
for the set of all such couplings.

In words,
\pi=\mathrm{Law}(X,Y)
is causal if the present value of
Y
is independent of the future of the process
X
, conditional on the past of
X
.

We assume that the cost function
C\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
in (
OT
) takes the following form. Suppose that there exist continuous functions
c_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, for

. For each

, suppose that the function
c_{k}
has

; i.e. for some
p\geq 1
, there exists
K\geq 0
such that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
,

(2.6)

|c_{k}(x,y)|\leq K[1+|x|^{p}+|y|^{p}],

and that
c_{k}
is
quasi-monotone
1

1

(2.7)

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the

is then to find (

\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)

\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)

(2.8)

\inf_{\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}(x_{k},\bar{x}_{k})\right]\!.

Definition 2.3

(adapted Wasserstein distance – discrete time)
.

Let

\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then the

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)
is defined by

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}|^{p}\right]\!.

Remark 2.4
.

The term

2.2.2.
Optimality of bicausal couplings

Under the following condition of stochastic monotonicity,

\eta

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote by
\eta_{1}
the first one-dimensional marginal of

\eta_{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k-1}}

conditional marginal of
\eta

(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k-1})

Definition 2.6

(stochastic co-monotonicity

]
)

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}}

x_{k}\mapsto\mu_{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k-1},x_{k}}
is increasing (resp. decreasing) in first order stochastic dominance, for each
k\in\{1,\dotsc,N-1\}
.
Two such processes or measures are
stochastically co-monotone
if they are both stochastically increasing, both stochastically decreasing, or one is stochastically increasing and decreasing while the other is arbitrary.

As we shall see below, the optimal bicausal coupling under this monotonicity condition is the Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement, introduced independently by
Rosenblatt 1952
and
Knothe 1957
. This coupling is a multi-dimensional extension of the monotone rearrangement described in
Section

2.1.1
. Under the Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement, the first marginals are coupled monotonically and then, inductively, the conditional marginals are coupled monotonically; see
[
8
, Figure 1]
for an illustration.

Definition 2.7

(Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement)
.

Given probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
U_{1},\dotsc,U_{n}
be independent uniform random variables on
[0,1]
, define
X_{1}=F_{\mu_{1}}^{-1}(U_{1})
,
Y_{1}=F_{\nu_{1}}^{-1}(U_{1})
and, for
k\in\{2,\dotsc,n\}
, define inductively the random variables

(2.10)

X_{k}=F^{-1}_{\mu_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k-1}}}(U_{k}),\quad Y_{k}=F^{-1}_{\nu_{Y_{1},\dotsc,Y_{k-1}}}(U_{k}).

The Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement between the marginals
\mu
and
\nu
is then given by

(2.11)

\pi^{\mathrm{KR}}_{\mu,\nu}=\mathrm{Law}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n},Y_{1},\dotsc,Y_{n}).

We will make use of the following equivalent definition, which is proved in
Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2017
. Similarly to
Definition

2.6
of stochastic co-monotonicity, we say that two functions on
\mathbb{R}
are
co-monotone
if they are both increasing or both decreasing.

Proposition 2.8
.

A coupling
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)
is equal to
\pi^{\mathrm{KR}}_{\mu,\nu}
if and only if
\pi=\mathrm{Law}(X,Y)
, where

(2.12)

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=(T_{1}(U_{1}),T_{2}(U_{2};X_{1}),\dotsc,T_{n}(U_{n};X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n-1}),

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=(S_{1}(U_{1}),S_{2}(U_{2};Y_{1}),\dotsc,S_{n}(U_{n};Y_{1},\dotsc,Y_{n-1}),

for
U_{1},\dotsc,U_{n}
independent uniform random variables on
[0,1]
, and functions
T_{i},S_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{i}\to\mathbb{R}
that are co-monotone in
U_{i}
, for each
i\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
.

The following optimality result is a straightforward modification of
[
8
, Proposition 3.4]
, which generalises earlier results from
Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2017
,
Rüschendorf 1985
. We note that, although
[
8
, Proposition 3.4]
considers a single function
c\equiv c_{k}
for all
k\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
, only minor changes to the proof are required for our setting and we do not repeat the proof here.

Proposition 2.9
.

For
p\geq 1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the laws of Markov processes. Consider the bicausal optimal transport problem (
2.8
) with continuous functions
c_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
, satisfying (
2.6
) and (
2.7
). If
\mu
and
\nu
are stochastically co-monotone, then the Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement attains the infimum in (
2.8
).

Remark 2.10
.

We show in
Example

5.11
that, when first order stochastic dominance is replaced by second order stochastic dominance for one of the marginals in the definition of stochastic co-monotonicity, optimality of the Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement may fail.

2.2.3.
Continuous-time bicausal optimal transport

The notions of bicausality and adapted Wasserstein distance extend to continuous-time processes.

Fix a finite time horizon
T>0
and let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\Omega\coloneqq C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
be the space of real-valued continuous paths on the time interval
[0,T]
. Analogously to the discrete-time setting, equip the space
\Omega
with the
L^{p}
norm, defined by
\|\gamma\|_{p}^{p}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}|\gamma_{t}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t
for
\gamma\in\Omega
, and with the corresponding Borel sigma-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
. Let
\omega=(\omega_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denote the canonical process on
\Omega
and
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
the canonical filtration, noting that
\mathcal{F}_{T}=\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
. Let
(\omega,\bar{\omega})=((\omega_{t},\bar{\omega}_{t}))_{t\in[0,T]}
denote the canonical process on the product space
\Omega\times\Omega
, and equip this space with the product filtration. As in the discrete-time case, for a random variable
X
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T})
with law
\eta
, the completion of its natural filtration under
\eta
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{X}=(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
, for some
p\geq 1
. We say that a coupling
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)
is bicausal if it satisfies the continuous-time analogue of
Definition

2.2
; i.e.
\pi
is causal if (
2.5
) holds for all
t\in[0,T]
, for any random variables
X,Y
with
\pi=\mathrm{Law}(X,Y)
, and
\pi
is bicausal if (
2.5
) also holds with the roles of
X
and
Y
reversed. Again we write
\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)
for the set of bicausal couplings. Note that, by
[
45
, Example 1(v), Lemma 4, and Proposition 2]
, our definition is equivalent to
Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2020a
and
Lassalle 2018
.

In the continuous-time setting, we consider cost functions
C\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
in (
OT
) that take the form
C(\omega,\bar{\omega})=\int_{0}^{T}c_{t}(\omega_{t},\bar{\omega}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t
, for any
\omega,\bar{\omega}\in\Omega
, where
c\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function. Additionally, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 2.11
.

Suppose that
c\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function and that, for some
p\geq 1
, there exists
K\geq 0
such that, for each fixed
t\in[0,T]
,
c_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and satisfies (
2.6
) and (
2.7
).

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
, the continuous-time
bicausal transport problem
between
\mu
and
\nu
is to find the value

(BCOT)

V_{c}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}c_{t}(\omega_{t},\bar{\omega}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!.

We define the adapted Wasserstein distance in the continuous-time setting as follows.

Definition 2.12

(adapted Wasserstein distance – continuous time)
.

For
p\geq 1
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
, the
adapted Wasserstein distance

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)
is defined by

(2.13)

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\omega_{t}-\bar{\omega}_{t}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!.

The adapted Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{2}
, in essentially the form of
Definition

2.12
, was first studied by
Bion-Nadal and Talay 2019
via a stochastic control formulation and associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, and
Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2024
studied
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
as defined in
Definition

2.12
, for general
p\geq 1
. We will see in
Section

7
that this distance is amenable to efficient computation, and in
Section

8
we show that this distance can be applied to robust stochastic optimisation. For semimartingales,
Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2020a
consider an alternative definition of adapted Wasserstein distance based on the Doob decomposition. While this definition also yields stability of various stochastic optimisation problems, we are not aware of efficient methods for the numerical computation of such a distance.

We consider the problem (
BCOT
) between the laws of stochastic processes that are described by SDEs. To the best of our knowledge, for such continuous-time problems,
Bion-Nadal and Talay 2019
,
Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2020a
were the first to find an explicit optimiser, the so-called
synchronous coupling
, which we define after the following preparations.

Definition 2.13
.

For an adapted process
\rho=(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
taking values in
[-1,1]
, we call
(W,\bar{W})
a
\rho
-correlated Brownian motion
if
W
,
\bar{W}
are each one-dimensional Brownian motions and, for each
t\in[0,T]
, their correlation is given by
\textrm{corr}(W_{t},\bar{W}_{t})=\rho_{t}
.

Let
b,\bar{b}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma,\bar{\sigma}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be measurable functions, and let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that there exists a unique strong solution
(X,\bar{X})
of the system of SDEs

(2.14)

\begin{cases}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},&X_{0}=x_{0},\\
\,\mathrm{d}\bar{X}_{t}=\bar{b}_{t}(\bar{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bar{\sigma}_{t}(\bar{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{W}_{t},&\bar{X}_{0}=x_{0},\end{cases}

on the time interval
[0,T]
, where
(W,\bar{W})
is a
\rho
-correlated Brownian motion, and write
\mu,\nu
for the laws of
X,\bar{X}
, respectively. It is shown in
[
8
, Proposition 2.2]
that the set of bicausal couplings
\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\nu)
is equal to the set of all couplings of the form
\pi=\mathrm{Law}(X,\bar{X})
, where (
2.14
) is driven by some correlated Brownian motion. In particular, the synchronous coupling is defined as follows.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
(X,\bar{X})
be the unique strong solution of (
2.14
) with
W=\bar{W}
. Then the
synchronous coupling
is defined as
\pi^{\mathrm{sync}}_{\mu,\nu}\coloneqq\mathrm{Law}(X,\bar{X})
, the joint law of the solutions when the SDEs are driven by a common Brownian motion.

2.3.
Numerical methods for SDEs

In order to solve SDEs of the form (
1.1
), one usually has to resort to numerical methods. The simplest of these is the
Euler–Maruyama scheme
. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
h=T/N
. We suppose here and throughout the paper that
N>T
so that
h<1
. The Euler–Maruyama scheme
(X^{h,\mathrm{EM}}_{kh})_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}
is defined by
X^{h,\mathrm{EM}}_{0}=x_{0}
and, for all
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}

(2.15)

X^{h,\mathrm{EM}}_{(k+1)h}=X^{h,\mathrm{EM}}_{kh}+b_{kh}(X^{h,\mathrm{EM}}_{kh})h+\sigma_{kh}(X^{h,\mathrm{EM}}_{kh})\Delta W_{k+1},

where
\Delta W_{k+1}\coloneqq W_{(k+1)h}-W_{kh}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h)
. One can also define a
semi-implicit
Euler–Maruyama scheme
(X^{h,\mathrm{IEM}}_{kh})_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}
by
X^{h,\mathrm{EM}}_{0}=x_{0}
and, for all
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
,

(2.16)

X^{h,\mathrm{IEM}}_{(k+1)h}=X^{h,\mathrm{IEM}}_{kh}+b_{kh}(X^{h,\mathrm{IEM}}_{(k+1)h})h+\sigma_{kh}(X^{h,\mathrm{IEM}}_{kh})\Delta W_{k+1}.

Under global Lipschitz conditions on the coefficients, both of the above schemes converge in the strong
L^{p}
-sense to the true solution of the SDE; see, for example,
Kloeden and Platen 1992
.
In recent years, numerical methods for SDEs with irregular (non-globally Lipschitz continuous) coefficients have been studied intensively. We refer to
Remarks

4.4
and
5.2
and
Examples

7.3
and
7.4
for references to relevant results.

The
monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme
is introduced in
[
8
, Definition 3.10]
. This scheme is defined analogously to (
2.15
) with the Brownian increments
\Delta W_{k+1}
replaced by
truncated Brownian increments

\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}
, defined in
Definition

2.15
below. Similarly, we define the
monotone semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme
by replacing the Brownian increments in (
2.16
) by truncated Brownian increments.

Definition 2.15

(truncated Brownian motion; cf.
[
54
, §2.1]
)
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a standard Brownian motion, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and set
h=T/N
. Define
A_{h}=4\sqrt{-h\log h}
and, for each
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
, define the stopping time
\tau^{h}_{k}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geq kh\colon\,|W_{t}-W_{kh}|>A_{h}\}
. The
truncated Brownian motion

(W^{h}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is defined by
W^{h}_{0}=0
and
W^{h}_{t}\coloneqq W^{h}_{kh}+W_{t\wedge\tau^{h}_{k}}-W_{kh}
, for
t\in(kh,(k+1)h]
,
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
. The
truncated Brownian increments
are defined by
\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}\coloneqq W^{h}_{(k+1)h}-W^{h}_{kh}=W_{(k+1)h\wedge\tau^{h}_{k}}-W_{kh}
,
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
.

If the coefficients
b
and
\sigma
of the SDE (
1.1
) are time-homogeneous and globally Lipschitz continuous,
[
8
, Lemma 3.11]
shows that, in contrast to (
2.15
), the monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme is stochastically increasing for sufficiently small step size. The scheme also converges strongly to the solution of (
1.1
); in particular,
[
8
, Proposition 3.13]
shows that the linear interpolation of the monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme converges in
L^{p}
uniformly in time. Combined with a stability argument, these properties are key to the proof of optimality of the synchronous coupling in
[
8
, Proposition 3.15]
. Similar numerical schemes based on truncated Brownian increments are also studied in
Liu and Pagès 2022
and
Jourdain and Pagès 2023
, with applications to (monotone) convex ordering of SDEs.

A different perspective on the interplay between optimal transport and numerical methods for SDEs is given by
Davie 2022
.

4.
Discontinuous drift with exponential growth

Consider the SDE (
1.1
) with time-homogeneous coefficients. We allow the drift coefficient
b
to exhibit both
discontinuities
and
exponential growth
. We will impose only mild piecewise continuity assumptions on
b
, and we will assume that the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
is globally Lipschitz continuous and that
\sigma
is non-zero only at the points of discontinuity of
b
. In this setting we will prove:

–

existence and uniqueness of strong solutions of (
1.1
);

–

strong
L^{p}
-convergence of a transformed semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme;

–

optimality of the synchronous coupling between the laws of two such SDEs for the problem (
BCOT
).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to treat SDEs with both discontinuous and exponentially growing drift. Precisely, we make the following assumptions on the coefficients
(b,\sigma)
.

Assumption 4.1
.

Let
(b,\sigma)
be time-homogeneous; i.e.
b_{t}\equiv b\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma_{t}\equiv\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
, for all
t\in[0,T]
. Suppose that there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, with
-\infty=\xi_{0}<\xi_{1}<\xi_{1}<\dotsc,\xi_{m}<\xi_{m+1}=\infty
, and there exist constants
L_{b},K_{b},L_{\sigma},\gamma,\eta\in(0,\infty)
such that
(b,\sigma)
satisfy the following:

(A1)

on each interval
(\xi_{k},\xi_{k+1})
,
k\in\{0,\dotsc,m\}
,

(i)

b
is absolutely continuous,

(ii)

b
is one-sided Lipschitz; i.e.
(x-y)(b(x)-b(y))\leq L_{b}|x-y|^{2}
, for all
x,y\in(\xi_{k},\xi_{k+1})
,

(iii)

b
is locally Lipschitz with exponential growth; i.e.
|b(x)-b(y)|\leq K_{b}(\exp\{\gamma|x|^{\eta}\}+\exp\{\gamma|y|^{\eta}\})|x-y|
for all
x,y\in(\xi_{k},\xi_{k+1})
;

(A2)

\sigma
is globally Lipschitz; i.e.
|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|\leq L_{\sigma}|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
;

(A3)

\sigma(\xi_{k})\neq 0
, for all
k\in\{1,\dotsc,m\}
.

Remark 4.2
.

We note that we do not impose any uniform non-degeneracy condition on the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
. This is in contrast to the setting of
Dareiotis et al. 2023
, for example.

Example 4.3
.

Consider the introductory example from
[
35
]
:

(4.1)

\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-X_{t}^{3}\,\mathrm{d}t+\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

and a modification of the example in
[

, Section 2]
:

(4.2)

\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\!\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\mathrm{sign}(X_{t}-1)\right)\!\,\mathrm{d}t+|X_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

The coefficients of the SDEs (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) each satisfy
4.1
.

Remark 4.4
.

(i)

In the case that
m=0
, the drift coefficient
b
is continuous, and there is no non-degeneracy condition on
\sigma
. In this setting, convergence in probability and almost sure convergence of the explicit Euler–Maruyama scheme were shown by
Gyöngy and Krylov 1996
and
Gyöngy 1998
, respectively. However,
Hutzenthaler et al. 2011
shows that, for the SDE (
4.1
), this scheme has unbounded moments and diverges in the strong
L^{p}
sense. Two approaches to recover moment bounds and strong convergence have been taken in the literature.

(a)

Tamed numerical schemes, in which polynomially growing coefficients are appropriately rescaled, have been studied, for example, by
Hutzenthaler et al. 2012
,
Sabanis 2013
. These are explicit schemes, which are computationally efficient. However, the technique of taming does not readily yield a scheme satisfying the monotonicity property
Item

(iv)
and to our knowledge has not been applied to exponentially growing coefficients.

(b)

For a semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme,
Hu 1996
proves
L^{2}
-convergence of order
1/2
at the terminal time. When
b
has at most polynomial growth,
Higham et al. 2002
prove uniform-in-time
L^{2}
-convergence at the same rate. Under additional assumptions,
Mao and Szpruch 2013a
,
Mao and Szpruch 2013b
,
Hutzenthaler and Jentzen 2015
also study strong convergence of this scheme. However, under
4.1
with
m=0
, we are not aware of any existing results on uniform-in-time
L^{p}
-convergence.

(ii)

Strengthening
Item

(A1)
to piecewise Lipschitz continuity, we are in the setting of
Leobacher and Szölgyenyi 2016
,
Leobacher and Szölgyenyi 2017
, who were the first to consider transformation-based schemes for SDEs with piecewise-Lipschitz drift. Under these assumptions, the authors proved strong well-posedness of the SDEs and strong
L^{p}
-convergence of a transformation-based Euler–Maruyama scheme.
Leobacher and Szölgyenyi 2018
,
Müller-Gronbach and Yaroslavtseva 2020
also prove strong convergence rates for the classical Euler–Maruyama scheme. Higher-order transformation-based schemes and adaptive schemes have been introduced in
Müller-Gronbach and Yaroslavtseva 2022
,
Przybyłowicz et al. 2024
,
Yaroslavtseva 2022
,
Neuenkirch et al. 2019
.
Przybyłowicz and Szölgyenyi 2021
,
Przybyłowicz et al. 2021
additionally allow for processes with jumps. Overviews of this line of research can be found in
Leobacher and Szölgyenyi 2017
,
Szölgyenyi 2021
.

(iii)

Recent results of
Müller-Gronbach et al. 2022
,
Spendier and Szölgyenyi 2024
, as well as
Hu and Gan 2022
, prove existence and uniqueness of strong solutions and convergence of tamed Euler–Maruyama schemes for SDEs with drift coefficients that are both discontinuous and superlinearly growing. However, these results impose a polynomial growth condition on the drift coefficient, whereas we allow for exponential growth. We note, however, that
[
59
]
allows for both the drift and diffusion coefficient to have polynomial growth.

4.1.
A transformed SDE

In order to remove the discontinuities form the drift coefficient of the SDE (
1.1
), we define a transformation of space
G\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as in
Leobacher and Szölgyenyi 2017

First we define
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(4.3)

\phi(u)=\begin{cases}(1+u)^{3}(1-u)^{3}&|u|\leq 1,\\
0&|u|>1.\end{cases}

Then, for constants

and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dotsc,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

\bar{\phi}_{k}(x)\coloneqq\phi\!\left(\frac{x-\xi_{k}}{c_{0}}\right)\!(x-\xi_{k})|x-\xi_{k}|,\quad\text{for}\;x\in\mathbb{R},

and

(4.5)

Before fixing the constants in the definition of

Remark 4.5
.

For any

is absolutely continuous on the open interval

and, in particular, there exist finite one-sided limits

exist and are finite.

By
Remark

4.5
and

(A3)

we can now define

(4.6)

\alpha_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sigma(\xi_{k})^{-2}(b(\xi_{k}-)-b(\xi_{k}+)),

and choose

(4.7)

c_{0}<\min_{k\in\{1,\dotsc,m\}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{6|\alpha_{k}|}\bigg\}\wedge\min_{k\in\{1,\dotsc,m-1\}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}(\xi_{k+1}-\xi_{k})\bigg\}.

We will see that this choice of constants ensures that the transformation
G

in

Proposition 4.12
.

Suppose that
(b,\sigma)
satisfy
4.1
. Then the SDE (
4.8
) admits a unique strong solution
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. Moreover, for any

, there exist constants

,
C_{1}(p,T)
,

such that

(4.11)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Z_{t}|^{p}\right]\!\leq C_{0}(p,T)

and, for all

with

,

(4.12)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{r\in[s,t]}|Z_{r}-Z_{s}|^{p}\right]\!\leq C_{1}(p,T)(t-s)^{\frac{p}{2}}

and

(4.13)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{r\in[s,t]}|\tilde{b}(Z_{r})-\tilde{b}(Z_{s})|^{p}\right]\!\leq C_{2}(p,T)(t-s)^{\frac{p}{2}}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.11
,
(\tilde{b},\tilde{\sigma})
satisfy (
4.9
),
\tilde{b}
is one-sided Lipschitz, and
\tilde{\sigma}
is Lipschitz. Under these conditions, the classical result of Krylov
[

shows existence and uniqueness of a strong solution

of (

).
The monotonicity condition (

) also implies moment bounds (

) on the solution, as shown, for example, in
[

, Lemma 3.2]
.

Given that

also satisfies the exponential growth condition (

to prove the bounds (

) and (

). We note that

assumes that the coefficients

∎

We now prove our existence and uniqueness result, following the proof of
[

.

Theorem 4.13
.

Suppose that the coefficients
(b,\sigma)
satisfy
4.1
. Then there exists a unique strong solution
X
of (
1.1
) with coefficients
(b,\sigma)
and initial condition
X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, for any
p\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C(p,T)>0
such that

(4.14)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{t}|^{p}\right]\!\leq C(p,T).

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.12
, there exists a unique strong solution
Z
of the SDE (
4.8
). Now define
X_{t}=G^{-1}(Z_{t})
, for all
t\in[0,T]
.
By
Lemma

4.7
,
G^{-1}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
L_{G^{-1}}
, and by
Proposition

4.12
,
Z
satisfies the one-step error bound (
4.12
). Hence, for
p\geq 1
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{t}|^{p}\right]\!&\leq 2^{p-1}L_{G^{-1}}^{p}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Z_{t}-Z_{0}|^{p}\right]\!+2^{p-1}|x_{0}|^{p}\leq C(p,T),\end{split}

where
C(p,T)\coloneqq 2^{p-1}L_{G^{-1}}^{p}C_{1}(p,T)T^{\frac{p}{2}}+2^{p-1}|x_{0}|^{p}
.

By
Lemma

4.7
,

is continuously differentiable and has a second derivative almost everywhere, and so we may apply Itô’s formula (see, e.g.
[

, §VI Theorem 1.5 and Remarks]
). Hence
X
satisfies the SDE (
1.1
) with coefficients
(b,\sigma)
, and
X_{0}=G(Z_{0})=x_{0}
. Since
Z
is a strong solution, and

is a deterministic transformation of

at each time,
X
is also a strong solution. Conversely, for any strong solution
X^{\prime}
of (
1.1
) with coefficients
(b,\sigma)
and with

, the process

defined by taking

for

is a strong solution of (

). By pathwise uniqueness,
Z^{\prime}_{t}=Z_{t}
for all

almost surely. Thus,
X^{\prime}_{t}=G^{-1}(Z^{\prime}_{t})=G^{-1}(Z_{t})=X_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
almost surely. We conclude that strong existence and pathwise uniqueness hold for (

) with coefficients
(b,\sigma)
.
∎

4.2.
Transformed semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme

We now define a numerical scheme via the transformation introduced in the previous section.

Definition 4.14

(transformed (monotone) semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme)
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
h=T/N
. For the SDE (
1.1
) with coefficients
(b,\sigma)
satisfying
4.1
, let

be defined as in (

Define the (discrete time)

where

We may define the (discrete time)

Remark 4.15
.

(i)

Similarly to the monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme discussed in

(ii)

[

,

.

For the scheme defined in

, we will prove moment bounds and strong

-convergence for

. In particular, for the semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme, we generalise the moment bounds from
Higham et al. 2002
and the

. We note that
[

,

]
also assume continuous differentiability of

We will repeatedly make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.16
.

Suppose that
b
is one-sided Lipschitz with coefficient
L_{b}
and let
h\in(0,1/L_{b})
. Then the map
(\mathrm{id}-hb)
is strictly increasing and invertible with strictly increasing inverse.

Proof.

Suppose that
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
x<y
. By the one-sided Lipschitz condition,

\begin{split}h(x-y)(b(x)-b(y))\leq hL_{b}|x-y|^{2}<|x-y|^{2}.\end{split}

Then, since
x-y<0
,

\begin{split}h(b(x)-b(y))>(x-y)^{-1}|x-y|^{2}=x-y,\end{split}

or equivalently

(4.16)

(\mathrm{id}-hb)(x)<(\mathrm{id}-hb)(y).

Hence
(\mathrm{id}-hb)
is strictly increasing, and thus invertible with strictly increasing inverse.
∎

Lemma 4.17
.

4.1
. Then for all

is one-sided Lipschitz with coefficient

h<1/L_{\tilde{b}}
, the map
(\mathrm{id}-h\tilde{b})
is invertible by
Lemma

4.16
. We can rewrite (
4.15
) as

(4.17)

(\mathrm{id}-h\tilde{b})(G(X^{h}_{(k+1)h}))=G(X^{h}_{kh})+(\tilde{\sigma}\circ G)(X^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1},

and similarly in the case of non-truncated Brownian increments. Since
G
is also invertible by
Lemma

4.7
, we may conclude.
∎

Proposition 4.18
.

Suppose that
b
is one-sided Lipschitz with coefficient
L_{b}
, and that the monotonicity condition (
4.9
) holds with constants
\alpha,\beta
. Let
N>4\beta T\vee 2L_{b}T
and
h=T/N
, and let
p\geq 1
. Then, for the scheme
X^{h}
defined via (
4.15
), there exists a constant
C(p,T)>0
such that

(4.18)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}|X^{h}_{kh}|^{p}\right]\!\leq C(p,T).

Analogous bounds hold for the scheme
\tilde{X}^{h}
defined with respect to non-truncated Brownian increments.

Proof.

First suppose that
p>4
.

For
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}
, define
Z^{h}_{kh}\coloneqq G(X^{h}_{kh})
. By
Lemma

4.7
,
G^{-1}
is Lipschitz with constant
L_{G^{-1}}
, and so

(4.19)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}|X^{h}_{kh}|^{p}\right]\!\leq 2^{2(p-1)}L_{G^{-1}}^{p}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}|Z^{h}_{kh}|^{p}\right]\!+2^{2(p-1)}L_{G^{-1}}^{p}|G(x_{0})|^{p}+2^{p-1}|x_{0}|^{p}.

Thus we aim to bound the moments of
\sup_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}|Z^{h}_{kh}|
.
Define
K\coloneqq(1-2\beta h)^{-1}\leq 2
and, for
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}
, define
Y^{h}_{kh}\coloneqq(\mathrm{id}-hb)(Z^{h}_{kh})
. Then, as in
[
52
, Lemma 3.2]
, the monotonicity condition (
4.9
) implies that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(4.20)

|x|^{2}\leq K(|(\mathrm{id}-hb)(x)|^{2}+2\alpha h).

Therefore it suffices to bound the moments of
\sup_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}|Y^{h}_{kh}|
. Observe that, for any
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
,

(4.21)

Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}=Z^{h}_{kh}+\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1},

with

(4.22)

Z^{h}_{kh}=Y^{h}_{kh}+h\tilde{b}(Z^{h}_{kh}),

and so
(Y^{h}_{kh})_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}
coincides with the so-called
split-step backward Euler method
described in
[
31
, Section 3.3]
, started from
Y^{h}_{0}=x_{0}-h\tilde{b}(x_{0})
and with the increments
(\Delta W_{k+1})_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}}
replaced by
(\Delta W^{h}_{k+1})_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}}
. We now closely follow the proof of
[
31
, Lemma 3.7]
to obtain moment bounds.

To ensure finite moments, for
R>0
, define the stopping time

(4.23)

\theta_{R}\coloneqq\inf\{k\in\{0,\dotsc N\}\colon|Y^{h}_{kh}|>R\},

with the convention that
\inf\emptyset\coloneqq\infty
. For
k\in\{0,\dotsc,\theta_{R}-1\}
, we have the bound
|Y^{h}_{kh}|\leq R
. To bound the moments of
|Y^{h}_{\theta_{R}h}|
we first note that, by (
4.20
),

(4.24)

|Z^{h}_{(\theta_{R}-1)h}|^{p}\leq 2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}K^{\frac{p}{2}}(|Y^{h}_{(\theta_{R}-1)h}|^{p}+(2\alpha h)^{\frac{p}{2}})\leq 2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}K^{\frac{p}{2}}(R^{p}+(2\alpha)^{\frac{p}{2}})\eqqcolon C(p,R).

Then, combining (
4.21
) with
Definition

2.15
and the monotonicity condition (
4.9
) proved in
Lemma

4.11
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[|Y^{h}_{\theta_{R}h}|^{p}]&\leq 2^{p-1}\mathbb{E}[|Z^{h}_{(\theta_{R}-1)h}|^{p}]+2^{p-1}A_{h}^{p}\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\sigma}(Z_{(\theta_{R}-1)h})|^{p}]\\
&\leq 2^{p-1}\mathbb{E}[|Z^{h}_{(\theta_{R}-1)h}|^{p}]+2^{p-1}2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}A_{h}^{p}(\alpha^{\frac{p}{2}}+\beta^{\frac{p}{2}}\mathbb{E}[|Z_{(\theta_{R}-1)h}|^{p}])\\
&\leq 2^{p-1}C(p,R)^{p}(1+2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\beta^{\frac{p}{2}}A_{h}^{p})+2^{\frac{3p}{2}-2}\alpha^{\frac{p}{2}}A_{h}^{p}\eqqcolon\tilde{C}(p,R).\end{split}

Hence

(4.25)

\sup_{k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}\mathbb{E}[|Y^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}]\leq\max\{R^{p},\tilde{C}(p,R)\}<\infty.

Combining (
4.20
) with (
4.21
) gives

\begin{split}|Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}|^{2}&=|Z^{h}_{kh}|^{2}+2Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}+|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{2}\\
&\leq K|Y^{h}_{kh}|^{2}+2\alpha hK+2Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}+|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{2},\end{split}

for any
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
.
For all
\ell\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}
, noting that
K=1+2\beta hK
, and summing the above expressions, we have

\begin{split}|Y^{h}_{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{2}&\leq|Y^{h}_{0}|^{2}+2\beta hK\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|Y^{h}_{kh}|^{2}+2\alpha TK+2\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{jh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{2}.\end{split}

We now raise each side to the power
p/2
and use the inequality
(a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}+a_{4})^{\frac{p}{2}}\leq 4^{\frac{p}{2}-1}(|a_{1}|^{\frac{p}{2}}+|a_{2}|^{\frac{p}{2}}+|a_{3}|^{\frac{p}{2}}+|a_{4}|^{\frac{p}{2}})
, for any
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\in\mathbb{R}
. Setting
c_{0}(p,T)\coloneqq 4^{\frac{p}{2}-1}(|x_{0}-h\tilde{b}(x_{0})|^{2}+2\alpha TK)^{\frac{p}{2}}
, we have

\begin{split}|Y^{h}_{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}&\leq c_{0}(p,T)+2^{\frac{3p}{2}-2}\bigg(\beta hK\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|Y^{h}_{kh}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{2}}+2^{\frac{3p}{2}-2}\bigg|\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}\bigg|^{\frac{p}{2}}\\
&\quad+2^{p-2}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{2}}.\end{split}

For all
M\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}
, we seek a bound for
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}|Y^{h}_{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}]
. We will treat each term in the sum (
4.2
) in turn. First, since
p>2
, an application of Hölder’s inequality gives

(4.26)

2^{\frac{3p}{2}-2}\bigg(\beta hK\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|Y^{h}_{kh}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{2}}\leq c_{1}(p,T)h\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}|Y^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p},

where
c_{1}(p,T)\coloneqq 2^{\frac{3p}{2}-2}(\beta K)^{\frac{p}{2}}T^{\frac{p}{2}-1}
.

To bound the second term in (
4.2
), we will apply the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality, noting that the quadratic variation
\langle W^{h}\rangle
of the truncated Brownian motion
W^{h}
satisfies the bound
\langle W^{h}\rangle_{t}\leq\langle W\rangle_{t}=t
, for all
t\geq 0
. Thus there exists a constant
c_{2}(p,T)>0
such that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}\bigg|\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}\bigg|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\!&=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}\bigg|\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\,\mathrm{d}W^{h}_{s}\bigg|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\!\\
&\leq c_{2}(p,T)\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{(M\wedge\theta_{R})-1}h|Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{4}}\right]\!.\end{split}

Now noting that
p>4
, Hölder’s inequality gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{(M\wedge\theta_{R})-1}h|Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{4}}\right]\!&\leq T^{{\frac{p}{4}}-1}h\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h})|^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big].\end{split}

Using the monotonicity condition (
4.9
) and the inequality
a\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+a^{2})
for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we find a constant
c_{3}(p,T)>0
such that

\begin{split}|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h})|^{\frac{p}{2}}&\leq|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{\frac{p}{2}}(\alpha+\beta|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{2})^{\frac{p}{4}}\\
&\leq c_{3}(p,T)|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{\frac{p}{2}}(\alpha^{\frac{p}{4}}+\beta^{\frac{p}{4}}|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{\frac{p}{2}})\\
&\leq c_{3}(p,T)\!\left[\frac{1}{2}+\Big(\frac{\alpha^{\frac{p}{2}}}{2}+\beta^{\frac{p}{4}}\Big)|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}\right]\!,\end{split}

for all
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}
.
Combining the previous estimates and setting
c_{4}(p,T)\coloneqq 2^{-1}T^{\frac{p}{4}}c_{2}(p,T)c_{3}(p,T)
and
c_{5}(p,T)\coloneqq(2^{-1}\alpha^{\frac{p}{2}}+\beta^{\frac{p}{4}})T^{\frac{p}{4}-1}c_{2}(p,T)c_{3}(p,T)
, we arrive at

(4.27)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}\bigg|\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}\bigg|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\!\leq c_{4}(p,T)+c_{5}(p,T)h\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}[|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}].

For the final term in (
4.2
), note that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\!&\leq N^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{p}\right]\!\\
&=N^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\sum_{k=0}^{(M\wedge\theta_{R})-1}\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})|^{p}|\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{p}].\end{split}

For each
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
, by independence of increments, we have
\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})|^{p}|\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{p}]=\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})|^{p}]\mathbb{E}[|\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{p}]
. The monotonicity condition (
4.9
) implies that
\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})|^{p}]\leq c_{6}(p,T)(\alpha^{\frac{p}{2}}+\beta^{\frac{p}{2}}\mathbb{E}[|Z^{h}_{kh}|^{p}])
, for some constant
c_{6}(p,T)>0
. By definition of the truncated Brownian motion
W^{h}
, there exists a constant
c_{7}(p,T)>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[|\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{p}]\leq\mathbb{E}[|\Delta W_{k+1}|^{p}]\leq c_{7}(p,T)h^{\frac{p}{2}}
. Hence, by (
4.2
), we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\!&\leq c_{8}(p,T)+c_{9}(p,T)h\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}[|Z^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}],\end{split}

where
c_{8}(p,T)\coloneqq c_{6}(p,T)c_{7}(p,T)\alpha^{\frac{p}{2}}T^{\frac{p}{2}}
and
c_{9}(p,T)\coloneqq c_{6}(p,T)c_{7}(p,T)\beta^{\frac{p}{2}}T^{\frac{p}{2}-1}
.

Combining (
4.20
) with (
4.27
) and (
4.2
) gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}\bigg|\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}Z^{h}_{kh}\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}\bigg|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\!&\leq\tilde{c}_{4}(p,T)+\tilde{c}_{5}(p,T)h\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}[|Y^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}],\\
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})-1}|\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\!&\leq\tilde{c}_{8}(p,T)+\tilde{c}_{9}(p,T)h\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}[|Y^{h}_{(k\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}],\end{split}

for constants
\tilde{c}_{4}(p,T)=c_{4}(p,T)+2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}(2\alpha hK)^{\frac{p}{2}}Tc_{5}(p,T)
,
\tilde{c}_{5}(p,T)=2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}K^{\frac{p}{2}}c_{5}(p,T)
,
\tilde{c}_{8}(p,T)=c_{8}(p,T)+2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}(2\alpha hK)^{\frac{p}{2}}Tc_{9}(p,T)
,
\tilde{c}_{5}(p,T)=2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}K^{\frac{p}{2}}c_{9}(p,T)
. Further combining this with (
4.2
) and (
4.26
), we find that

(4.28)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,M\}}|Y^{h}_{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}\right]\!\leq\tilde{c}_{0}(p,T)+\tilde{c}_{1}(p,T)h\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{j\in\{0,\dotsc,k\}}|Y^{h}_{(j\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}\right]\!,

where
\tilde{c}_{0}(p,T)=c_{0}(p,T)+2^{\frac{3p}{2}-2}\tilde{c}_{4}(p,T)+2^{p-2}\tilde{c}_{8}(p,T)
and
\tilde{c}_{1}(p,T)=c_{1}(p,T)+2^{\frac{3p}{2}-2}\tilde{c}_{5}(p,T)+2^{p-2}\tilde{c}_{9}(p,T)
.

In light of (
4.25
), we can apply the discrete Grönwall inequality (see, e.g.
[
51
, Lemma 3.4]
) to conclude that

(4.29)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}|Y^{h}_{(\ell\wedge\theta_{R})h}|^{p}\right]\!\leq\tilde{c}_{0}(p,T)e^{\tilde{c}_{1}(p,T)T}.

We can now take the limit
R\to\infty
and apply Fatou’s lemma to find

(4.30)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}|Y^{h}_{\ell h}|^{p}\right]\!\leq\tilde{c}_{0}(p,T)e^{\tilde{c}_{1}(p,T)T}.

A further application of (
4.20
) yields

(4.31)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{\ell\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}}|Z^{h}_{\ell h}|^{p}\right]\!\leq 2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}K^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big(\tilde{c}_{0}(p,T)e^{\tilde{c}_{1}(p,T)T}+(2\alpha h)^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big).

Set
C_{0}(p,T)\coloneqq 2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}K^{\frac{p}{2}}(\tilde{c}_{0}(p,T)e^{\tilde{c}_{1}(p,T)T}+(\frac{\alpha}{\beta})^{\frac{p}{2}})
and
C(p,T)\coloneqq 2^{2(p-1)}L_{G^{-1}}^{p}(C_{0}(p,T)+|G(x_{0})|^{p})+2^{p-1}|x_{0}|^{p}
. Applying the bound (
4.19
), we conclude that (
4.18
) holds.

Given the bound (
4.18
) for some
p=\bar{p}>4
, we deduce that (
4.18
) also holds for any
p\in[1,4]
with
C(p,T)=C(\bar{p},T)^{\frac{p}{\bar{p}}}
, by applying Jensen’s inequality.

The result for
\tilde{X}^{h}
is proved analogously.
∎

We prove the following strong convergence rates.

Theorem 4.19
.

Suppose that the coefficients of (
1.1
) satisfy
4.1
, and let
X
be the unique strong solution of (
1.1
). For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large and
h=T/N
, denote by
X^{h}
the associated transformed monotone semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme defined by (
4.15
). Then, for any
p\geq 1
there exists
C\in(0,\infty)
such that

(4.32)

\sup_{k\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}}\mathbb{E}[|X_{kh}-X^{h}_{kh}|^{p}]^{\frac{1}{p}}\vee\mathbb{E}{\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}|X_{t}-X^{h}_{kh}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!}^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\begin{cases}Ch^{\frac{1}{2}},&p\in[1,2],\\
Ch^{\frac{1}{p(p-1)}},&p\in[2,\infty).\end{cases}

Analogous estimates hold for the scheme
\tilde{X}^{h}
defined with respect to non-truncated Brownian increments.

Remark 4.20
.

(i)

To our knowledge, this is the first result on strong convergence of an implicit scheme in the presence of
both
discontinuities
and
exponential growth in the drift coefficient.

(ii)

For
p\in[1,2]
, we obtain the strong
L^{p}
convergence rate of
1/2
that we expect. For
p>2
, we leave the question of improving the strong
L^{p}
convergence rate for future research.

(iii)

In the case that
b
is continuous, our result still strengthens
Hu 1996
, since we relax the continuous differentiability assumption on the coefficients and consider general
p\in[1,\infty)
, not only
p=2
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.19
.

We first consider
p=2
.

As in the proof of
Theorem

4.13
,
X_{t}=G^{-1}(Z_{t})
, for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
Z
is the unique strong solution of (
1.1
) with coefficients
(\tilde{b},\tilde{\sigma})
, as defined in (
4.1
), and
Z_{0}=G(x_{0})
. Now, for
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}
, define
Z^{h}_{kh}\coloneqq G(X^{h}_{kh})
, so that
Z^{h}_{0}=Z_{0}
and, for
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
,

(4.33)

Z^{h}_{(k+1)h}=Z^{h}_{kh}+h\tilde{b}(Z^{h}_{(k+1)h})+\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}.

Then, using the Lipschitz property of
G^{-1}
proved in
Lemma

4.7
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[|X_{kh}-X^{h}_{kh}|^{2}]&\leq L_{G^{-1}}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|Z_{kh}-Z^{h}_{kh}|^{2}],\\
\mathbb{E}{\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}|X_{t}-X^{h}_{kh}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!}&\leq L_{G^{-1}}^{2}\mathbb{E}{\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}|Z_{t}-Z^{h}_{kh}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!}.\end{split}

Therefore it suffices to analyse the
L^{2}
error between
Z^{h}
and
Z
.

For any
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N-1\}
, we can write

(4.34)

Z_{(k+1)h}=Z_{kh}+\tilde{b}(Z_{(k+1)h})h+\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh})\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}+B_{k+1}+\Sigma_{k+1}+\Sigma^{h}_{k+1}+\mathcal{E}_{k+1},

where we define the error terms

(4.35)

\displaystyle B_{k+1}\coloneqq\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}(\tilde{b}(Z_{s})-\tilde{b}(Z_{(k+1)h}))ds,\qquad\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}(\tilde{\sigma}(Z_{s})-\tilde{\sigma}(Z_{kh}))dW_{s},

\displaystyle\Sigma^{h}_{k+1}\coloneqq(\tilde{\sigma}(Z_{kh})-\tilde{\sigma}(Z^{h}_{kh}))\Delta W^{h}_{k+1},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tilde{\sigma}(Z_{kh})[\Delta W_{k+1}-\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}].

Note that, if we define
\tilde{Z}^{h}_{kh}\coloneqq G(\tilde{X}^{h}_{kh})
, for
k\in\{0,\dotsc,N\}
, then
\tilde{Z}^{h}
satisfies analogous estimates to (
4.2
) and also satisfies (
4.33
) with the truncated increment
\Delta W^{h}_{k+1}
replaced by
\Delta W_{k+1}
. Thus we can write

(4.36)

Z_{(k+1)h}=Z_{kh}+\tilde{b}(Z_{(k+1)h})h+\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{Z}^{h}_{kh})\Delta W_{k+1}+B_{k+1}+\Sigma_{k+1}+\tilde{\Sigma}_{k+1},

with
\tilde{\Sigma}_{k+1}\coloneqq\big(\tilde{\sigma}(Z_{kh})-\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{Z}^{h}_{kh})\big)\Delta W_{k+1}
. We can bound the second moment of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{k+1}
in exactly the same way as we do for
\Sigma^{h}_{k+1}
below. Thus the estimates that we prove for
Z^{h}
also hold for
\tilde{Z}^{h}
with only a minor modification in the proof.

Now we consider

(4.37)

\displaystyle Z_{(k+1)h}-Z^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle=Z_{kh}-Z^{h}_{kh}+(\tilde{b}(Z_{(k+1)h})-\tilde{b}(Z^{h}_{(k+1)h}))h+B_{k+1}+\Sigma_{k+1}+\Sigma^{h}_{k+1}+\mathcal{E}_{k+1}.

By
Lemma

4.11
,
\tilde{b}
is absolutely continuous and one-sided Lipschitz with constant
L_{\tilde{b}}
. Therefore the Lebesgue-almost everywhere derivative
\tilde{b}^{\prime}
exists and satisfies
\tilde{b}^{\prime}\leq L_{\tilde{b}}
. The fundamental theorem of calculus shows that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(4.38)

\tilde{b}(x)-\tilde{b}(y)=\int_{y}^{x}\tilde{b}^{\prime}(s)ds=\!\left(\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{b}^{\prime}(ux+(1-u)y)du\right)\!(x-y).

Hence

(4.39)

Hence, by (

5.
SDEs with bounded measurable drift

We now consider a second class of irregular coefficients. We only require the drift coefficient to be bounded and measurable, and we allow the diffusion coefficient to be Hölder continuous in space rather than Lipschitz, but we do require a uniform non-degeneracy condition and boundedness of the diffusion, which was not needed in
Section

4
. The coefficients may be time-dependent. In this setting, we again obtain optimality of the synchronous coupling for (

) between the laws of SDEs.
Similarly to

4

4

of the SDE (

]

5.1.
Lack of monotonicity and counterexamples

In the proof of
Theorem

5.8
, we consider transformed SDEs with Hölder continuous diffusion coefficients that may not be Lipschitz. Recall the monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme from
Section

2.3
that was introduced in
[
8
, Definition 3.10]
. We show that this scheme is not stochastically monotone for SDEs whose diffusion coefficient is not Lipschitz. Thus we could not apply
Proposition

with the monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme to prove optimality in this case.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
be
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for some
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
, bounded from above, and bounded away from zero. Consider the SDE
\,\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\sigma(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
, with
Z_{0}=z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\sigma
is not Lipschitz continuous, then the monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme for
Z
is not stochastically monotone for any step size.

Remark 5.10
.

In the setting of
Proposition

5.9
, the monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme coincides with the transformed monotone semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme, since there is no drift term.

Proof of
Proposition

5.9
.

The conditions of the proposition are sufficient to guarantee the existence of a unique strong solution
Z
of the SDE by, e.g.
[
87
, Theorem 4]
. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
h={\frac{T}{N}}
, let
Z^{h}
denote the monotone Euler–Maruyama scheme for
Z
with step size
h
, and let
A_{h}
be the truncation level given in
Definition

2.15
. Note that, since
\sigma
is not Lipschitz continuous, there exist
z,\bar{z}\in\mathbb{R}
with

such that

(5.7)

|\sigma(\bar{z})-\sigma(z)|>A_{h}^{-1}(\bar{z}-z)>0.

Now consider the random variables
Y\coloneqq z+\sigma(z)\xi^{h}
,
\bar{Y}\coloneqq\bar{z}+\sigma(\bar{z})\xi^{h}
, where

is a random variable with the same law as an increment of the truncated Brownian motion
W^{h}
. For all

(5.8)

\mathbb{P}[Y\leq a]=\mathbb{P}\!\left[\xi^{h}\leq\frac{a-z}{\sigma(z)}\right]\!=\begin{cases}0,&a\leq z-A_{h}\sigma(z),\\
2\Phi\!\left(h^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{a-z}{\sigma(z)}\right)\!,&a\in(z-A_{h}\sigma(z),z+A_{h}\sigma(z)),\\
1,&a\geq z+A_{h}\sigma(z),\end{cases}

where
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is the distribution function of a standard Gaussian, which is strictly increasing. The same holds for
\bar{Y}
with

replaced by
\bar{z}
. We aim to show that there exist

such that
\mathbb{P}[\bar{Y}\leq a_{\ast}]<\mathbb{P}[Y\leq a_{\ast}]
and
\mathbb{P}[Y\leq a^{\ast}]<\mathbb{P}[\bar{Y}\leq a^{\ast}]
. This would imply that

is neither stochastically decreasing nor increasing. Suppose first that

. Then rearranging (

) gives

\bar{z}-A_{h}\sigma(\bar{z})<z-A_{h}\sigma(z).

So there exists

, and we see that

(5.10)

\mathbb{P}[Y\leq a^{\ast}]=0,\;\text{while}\quad\mathbb{P}[\bar{Y}\leq a^{\ast}]>0.

Set
a_{\ast}=z+A_{h}\sigma(z)
and note that
a_{\ast}<\bar{z}+A_{h}\sigma(\bar{z})
. Therefore
\mathbb{P}[Y\leq a_{\ast}]=1
and
\mathbb{P}[\bar{Y}\leq a_{\ast}]<1
.

On the other hand, if
\sigma(\bar{z})<\sigma(z)
, then
\bar{z}+A_{h}\sigma(\bar{z})<z+A_{h}\sigma(z)
, and so there exists
a^{\ast}
such that
\mathbb{P}[\bar{Y}\leq a^{\ast}]=1
and
\mathbb{P}[Y\leq a^{\ast}]<1
. Now setting
a_{\ast}=\bar{z}-A_{h}\sigma(\bar{z})>z-A_{h}\sigma(z)
gives
\mathbb{P}[\bar{Y}\leq a_{\ast}]=0
and
\mathbb{P}[Y\leq a_{\ast}]>0
. Hence

is not stochastically monotone.
∎

A natural generalisation of stochastic monotonicity is monotonicity with respect to second order stochastic dominance. We now provide an example to show that, for two marginals that are increasing in second order stochastic dominance, the Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement may fail to be optimal for the bicausal transport problem, if one of the marginals is not stochastically increasing.

In the previous sections we have identified different classes of coefficients of the SDEs (

.

Suppose that

7.
Computation of the adapted Wasserstein distance

Our results immediately suggest an efficient method for computing the value of a bicausal optimal transport problem. This is in sharp contrast to the general situation in classical and constrained optimal transport problems, when the optimiser is not known. Therefore our results contribute to the efficiency of computations in applications, such as in the calculation of robust bounds for stochastic optimisation problems, as discussed in
Section

8
.

In classical optimal transport, entropic regularisation is used in computation. Sinkhorn’s algorithm can be used to efficiently compute the value of the regularised problem, but not of the original unregularised problem; see
Cuturi 2013
,
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
.
For bicausal optimal transport in discrete time,
Pichler and Weinhardt 2021
,
Eckstein and Pammer 2024
introduce two different adapted analogues of Sinkhorn’s algorithm. However, each of these algorithms either include a discretisation of the marginal measures or work only on a discrete state space, and generalising them to both continuous time and space would be prohibitively costly.
Under rather strong assumptions on the coefficients,
Bion-Nadal and Talay 2019
consider a stochastic control problem for SDEs, which is shown to be equivalent to finding the adapted
2
-Wasserstein distance in
[
8
, Proposition 2.2]
. This control formulation gives rise to a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, which one could solve numerically.

7.1.
Computation via the synchronous coupling

Let
c\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy
2.11
. Suppose that
(b,\sigma)
satisfies one of
3.7
,
4.1
, and
5.1
, and that
(\bar{b},\bar{\sigma})
also satisfies one of these assumptions. Then, by
Theorem

6.1
, the synchronous coupling is optimal for
V_{c}(\mu,\nu)
. Writing
(X,\bar{X})
for the solution of the SDEs (
2.14
) driven by a common Brownian motion
W
, we thus have

(7.1)

V_{c}(\mu,\nu)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}c_{t}(X_{t},\bar{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!.

In particular, taking
c_{t}(x,y)=|x-y|^{p}
, for all
t\in[0,T]
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, the adapted Wasserstein distance is given by

(7.2)

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}|X_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, set
h=T/N
and take
X^{h}
,
\bar{X}^{h}
to be càdlàg processes adapted to the natural filtration of
W
such that
X^{h}
(resp.
\bar{X}^{h}
) approximates
X
(resp.
\bar{X}
) strongly in
L^{p}
. Define
S^{h}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{T}|X^{h}_{t}-\bar{X}^{h}_{t}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t]^{\frac{1}{p}}
. Then

(7.3)

|\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)-S^{h}|\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}|X_{t}-X^{h}_{t}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!^{\frac{1}{p}}+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\bar{X}_{t}-\bar{X}^{h}_{t}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\!^{\frac{1}{p}}\xrightarrow{h\to 0}0.

Note that each term in the sum on the right hand side of (
7.3
) depends on only one of the processes
X,\bar{X}
and so these terms can be estimated separately. Thus the problem of computing the adapted Wasserstein distance is reduced to the well-studied problem of numerically solving two one-dimensional SDEs. Monte Carlo methods can then be applied to compute these approximations.

There are now two error sources to consider in the numerical computation of
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)
: the convergence rate of the schemes
X^{h},\bar{X}^{h}
, and the computational cost of Monte Carlo estimation for
S^{h}
. Given numerical schemes for which we have strong convergence rates, one can efficiently implement a multi-level Monte Carlo (MLMC) method to reduce the overall computational cost; see, for example,
Giles and Szpruch 2013
.

Remark 7.1
.

The bound (
7.3
) shows that, for each SDE, we may take any numerical scheme that converges strongly in
L^{p}
. For example, for any SDE with Lipschitz coefficients, we can apply the Euler–Maruyama scheme defined in
Section

2.3
.
In the case of discontinuous and exponentially growing drift, we proved strong

convergence of the transformed (monotone) semi-implicit Euler–Maruyama scheme in

4.19
, which allows us to compute the adapted Wasserstein distance when one of the SDEs has coefficients satisfying

. When additional conditions hold, however, we may exploit the existing convergence results that are discussed in
Remark

4.4
to improve computational efficiency. While stochastic monotonicity of the scheme is essential to the proof of optimality in
Theorem

4.23
, this condition is not needed for the computation of the adapted Wasserstein distance. Under
5.1
, suitable numerical schemes are discussed in
Remark

5.2
.

8.
Application to robust optimisation

We finally turn to applications in the robust approach to stochastic optimisation. Several results have been proven on stability of various stochastic optimisation problems
[
1
,
6
]
and (Lipschitz) continuity of optimal stopping problems
[
1
,
7
,
2
]
, in both discrete and continuous time, with respect to the adapted Wasserstein distance. In a discrete-time setup, the adapted Wasserstein distance has also been applied to quantify model-sensitivity of multi-period optimisation problems in
[
9
,
4
]
and
[
37
]
, with the latter also treating model-sensitivity of continuous-time problems.

As a concrete application of the adapted Wasserstein distance to robust optimisation, we consider optimal stopping problems for continuous-time stochastic processes.
Such problems are often analytically intractable and the numerical approximation of their solution may be computationally intensive; see, e.g.
[
10
]
and the references therein.
In
Theorem

8.1
we prove Lipschitz continuity of optimal stopping problems with respect to the adapted Wasserstein distance, which admits an efficient numerical approximation, following the approach of
Section

7.1
.
Hence, our methods provide computationally feasible bounds on the discrepancy between the values of optimal stopping problems under different probability measures, making such problems tractable for applications.

8.1.
Optimal stopping

We consider the following class of optimal stopping problems. For an
\mathcal{F}
-adapted process
L\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
p\geq 1
, define the value function
v^{L}\colon\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
of the associated optimal stopping problem for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
by

(8.1)

v^{L}(\mu)\coloneqq\inf_{\tau}\mathbb{E}^{\mu}[L(\tau(\omega),\omega)],

where the infimum is taken over all stopping times with respect to
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}
, the completion of the canonical filtration under
\mu
.
We prove the following Lipschitz estimate, which quantifies the model uncertainty in the choice of the measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
p\geq 1
, let
L\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be
\mathcal{F}
-adapted and suppose that there exists a Lipschitz constant
C_{L}>0
such that, for all
t\in[0,T]
,
\gamma,\bar{\gamma}\in\Omega
,

(8.2)

|L(t,\gamma)-L(t,\bar{\gamma})|\leq C_{L}\|\gamma-\bar{\gamma}\|_{p}.

Then, for any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
, we have the bound

(8.3)

|v^{L}(\mu)-v^{L}(\nu)|\leq C_{L}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

Example 8.2
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz and define
L\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, for any
t\in[0,T]
,
\gamma\in\Omega
, by

(8.4)

L(t,\gamma)\coloneqq f\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big).

Then
L
satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

8.1
for any
p\geq 1
. In this example, the value
v^{L}
of the corresponding optimal stopping problem has a financial interpretation as an American-style Asian option.

This result could also be derived from
[
1
, Proposition 4.4]
, but we rather provide a direct proof, following a similar approach to the proof of the analogous discrete-time result
[
7
, Lemma 7.1]
.

Proof of
Theorem

8.1
.

Let
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mu,\nu)
and let
(X,Y)
be a pair of random variables on
\Omega\times\Omega
with joint law
\pi
. As before, let
\mathcal{F}^{X}
and
\mathcal{F}^{Y}
denote the completions of the natural filtrations of
X
and
Y
under the measures
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and take an
\varepsilon
-optimal
\mathcal{F}^{Y}
-stopping time
\tau^{Y}
; i.e.

(8.5)

\mathbb{E}[L(\tau^{Y},Y)]\leq v^{L}(\nu)+\varepsilon.

For each
u\in[0,1]
, define
\theta_{u}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geq 0\colon\;\pi(\tau^{Y}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T})\geq u\}
. We claim that
\theta_{u}
is an
\mathcal{F}^{X}
-stopping time. By continuity of the paths of processes
X
and
Y
, we know that, for any
u\in[0,1]
and
t\in[0,T]
,

(8.6)

\{\theta_{u}\leq t\}=\{\pi(\tau^{Y}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T})\geq u\}.

So it suffices to show that, for each
t\in[0,T]
, the conditional probability
\pi(\tau^{Y}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T})
is
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}
-measurable. Since
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mu,\nu)
, the causality condition (
2.5
) states that, under
\pi
,
\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}
is conditionally independent of
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}
, given
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}
. An equivalent formulation of this conditional independence condition (see, e.g.
Kallenberg 1997
) is that for any
F\in\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}
,

(8.7)

\pi(F\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}\vee\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})=\pi(F\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}).

In fact,
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}\subset\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}
, and so
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}\vee\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}=\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}
. By the definition of a stopping time,
\{\tau^{Y}\leq t\}\in\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}
. Therefore (
8.7
) gives us

(8.8)

\pi(\tau^{Y}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T})=\pi(\tau^{Y}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}),

which is then
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}
-measurable. This proves our claim that
\theta_{u}
is an
\mathcal{F}^{X}
-stopping time for each
u\in[0,1]
. Thus

(8.9)

v^{L}(\mu)\leq\inf_{u\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}[L(\theta_{u},X)]\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[L(\theta_{u},X)]\,\mathrm{d}u.

Applying the tower property of conditional expectation and Fubini’s theorem to exchange the order of integration, we arrive at

(8.10)

v^{L}(\mu)\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[L(\theta_{u},X)\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}]\,\mathrm{d}u\right]\!.

Now fix a path
\gamma\in\Omega
of the process
X
. We can define a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma}
on
[0,T]
by
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma}([0,t))=\pi(\tau^{Y}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T})(\gamma)
, for all
t\in[0,T]
. Then
u\mapsto\theta_{u}(\gamma)
is the quantile function of
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma}
, and we see that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[L(\tau^{Y},X)\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}](\gamma)&=\int_{0}^{T}L(t,\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\gamma}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}L(\theta_{u}(\gamma),\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[L(\theta_{u},X)\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}](\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}u.\end{split}

Taking the expectation with respect to
\pi
and substituting into (
8.10
), we find

(8.11)

v^{L}(\mu)\leq\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[L(\tau^{Y},X)\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}](\omega)\right]\!=\mathbb{E}\!\left[L(\tau^{Y},X)\right]\!.

By (
8.5
), the Lipschitz continuity of
L
in the second argument, and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\begin{split}v^{L}(\mu)-v^{L}(\nu)&\leq C_{L}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|X-Y\|_{p}\right]\!+\varepsilon\leq C_{L}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|X-Y\|_{p}^{p}\right]\!^{\frac{1}{p}}+\varepsilon.\end{split}

Exchanging the roles of
\mu
and
\nu
and noting that
\varepsilon>0
and
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mu,\nu)
were chosen arbitrarily completes the proof.
∎
