Remarks on stationary and uniformly-rotating vortex sheets: Flexibility results
Javier Gómez-Serrano, Jaemin Park, Jia Shi and Yao Yao

Abstract
. In this paper, we construct new, uniformly-rotating solutions of the vortex sheet equation bifurcating from circles with constant vorticity amplitude. The proof is accomplished via a Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction and a second order expansion of the reduced system. Keywords: incompressible, vortex sheet, uniformly-rotating, bifurcation, Lyapunov-Schmidt



1.
Introduction

Let us consider a vortex sheet
\omega
: a weak solution of the 2D Euler equation concentrated on a simple closed curve
\Gamma:=\left\{z(\theta,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta\in\mathbb{T}\right\}
with vortex-sheet strength
\gamma(\theta,t)
, that is, for all test functions
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)d\omega(x,t)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\varphi(z(\theta,t))\gamma(\theta,t)d\theta.

Note that the evolution of
z
and
\gamma
is described by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z(\theta,t)=\mathbf{v}(z,t)+c(\theta,t)\partial_{\theta}z(\theta,t)

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\gamma(\theta,t)=\partial_{\theta}\left(c(\theta,t)\gamma(\theta,t)\right),

where
\text{curl}(\mathbf{v})=\omega
and
c(\theta,t)
represents the reparametrization freedom of the curve
[
6
,
25
,
29
,
39
]
. Recall that the (discontinuous) velocity
\mathbf{v}
on the curve, generated by the vortex sheet on
\Gamma
is given by the Birkhoff-Rott integral:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(z(\theta,t),t):=BR(z,\gamma)(z(\theta,t)):=\frac{1}{2\pi}PV\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{(z(\theta,t)-z(\eta,t))^{\perp}}{|z(\theta,t)-z(\eta,t)|^{2}}\gamma(\eta,t)d\eta.

For simplicity, we will omit the word
PV
in the notation for principal value integral from now on. We will also denote by
\mathbf{v^{\pm}}
the respective limits of the velocity on the two sides of
\Gamma
(with
\mathbf{v}^{+}
being the limit on the side that
\mathbf{n}
points into).

The main goal of this paper is to find a class of uniformly-rotating vortex sheets, concentrated on a closed curve which is not a circle. This is the first proof of existence of a family of solutions of such kind. We will say that a vortex sheet is uniformly-rotating with angular velocity
\Omega
if it is stationary in the rotating frame with angular velocity
\Omega
. See Lemma
2.1
for the exact equations satisfied by a uniformly-rotating vortex sheet. The existence of such solutions is not evident a priori since there are rigidity results by Gómez-Serrano–Park–Shi–Yao
[
15
]
ruling out their existence in the case
\Omega\leq 0
and
\gamma>0
.
These solutions are also important since they show that one cannot expect any asymptotic stability of the radial vortex sheet with constant strength. See the recent work by Ionescu–Jia
[
20
]
for an asymptotic stability result when the vorticity is made out of a Dirac delta part and a Gevrey smooth part. Previously, similar solutions (uniformly rotating, non-radial) had been found for the 2D Euler equations in the context of vortex patches or smooth, compactly-supported functions
[
9
,
8
,
4
,
18
,
13
,
16
]
.

Despite the complexity of the solutions shown in numerical/actual experiments
[
24
,
40
,
27
]
, there have been significant efforts to prove the existence of solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) in various settings. For a
L^{2}_{loc}
initial velocity whose vorticity has a definite sign, it turns out that there exists a global weak solution
[
11
,
28
]
by the works of Delort, and Majda. In case that the vorticity does not have a definite sign, the existence was proved by Lopes Filho–Nussenzveig Lopes–Xin
[
26
]
under the assumption that the initial vorticity satisfies a reflection symmetry. For analytic initial data, local-in-time existence of analytic solutions was proved by Sulem–Sulem–Bardos–Frisch
[
39
]
.

The singularity formation was conjectured by Birkhoff–Fisher and Birkhoff
[

,
2
]
. For analytic initial data, the possibility that the curvature may blow up in finite time was supported by asymptotic analysis of Moore
[

and also verified by numerical simulations by Krasny and Meiron–Baker–Orszag in
[

,

. Note that the system (

1.1.
Steady solutions of the vortex sheet

There are very few known examples of nontrivial steady solutions, and in fact, other than the circle or the line, the list only comprises the segment of length
2a
and density

(1.4)

\gamma(x)=\Omega\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}},\qquad x\in[-a,a],

which is a rotating solution with angular velocity
\Omega

[
1
]
and the family found by Protas–Sakajo
[
36
]
, made out of segments rotating about a common center of rotation with endpoints at the vertices of a regular polygon. We remark that none of these are supported on a closed curve.

Numerically, O’Neil
[
32
,
33
]
used point vortices to approximate the vortex sheet and compute uniformly rotating solutions and Elling
[
12
]
constructed numerically self-similar vortex sheets forming cusps. O’Neil
[
34
,
35
]
also found numerically steady solutions which are combinations of point vortices and vortex sheets.

1.2.
Main strategy

The main strategy to prove Theorem
2.2
, the Main Theorem of this paper, is to employ bifurcation theory and try to bifurcate from the simple eigenvalue
b:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\gamma(x,t)dx=2
. However, the standard methods (Crandall-Rabinowitz
[
10
]
) fail since the linearized operator around the circle does not satisfy the transversality condition: in other words, the nontrivial zero set is not transversal to the trivial one (disks with constant vorticity amplitude). This phenomenon is usually known in the literature as a
degenerate bifurcation

[
22
,
21
]
. Graphically, this can be seen in Figure
1
. The problem is that we no longer have a single branch emanating from the disk, but two, and therefore the linearized operator fails to describe the local behaviour at the bifurcation point. To overcome this issue, we first reduce the nonlinear problem to a suitable finite dimensional space by means of a Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction since the restriction of
D\mathcal{F}
is an isomorphism between Ker
(D\mathcal{F})^{\perp}
and Im
(D\mathcal{F})
. After having done so, we are left with a finite dimensional system and it is there where we perform a higher order expansion around the bifurcation point, since, as expected by the failure of the transversality condition, the first order approximation is identically zero. We obtain that in suitable coordinates, the zero sets of
\mathcal{F}
behave as
x^{2}-y^{2}=0
and thus two bifurcation branches emanate from the bifurcation point. The last part of the proof is devoted to handle the higher order terms, which can be controlled if we restrict the bifurcation domain to a suitable small enough neighbourhood. We mention here that this technique had been successfully employed by Hmidi–Mateu
[
17
]
(in the hyperbolic case) and Hmidi–Renault
[
19
]
(in the elliptic case).

2.
The equations and the functional spaces

Let
\omega(\cdot,t)=\omega_{0}(R_{\Omega t})
be a stationary/rotating vortex sheet solution to the incompressible 2D Euler equation, where
\omega_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Here
\Omega=0
corresponds to a stationary solution, and
\Omega\neq 0
corresponds to a rotating solution. Assume that
\omega_{0}
is concentrated on
\Gamma
. Throughout this paper we will assume that
\Gamma
is a simple closed curve and
\Omega>0
. Following
[
15
, Lemma 2.1]
, we have that:

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume
\omega(\cdot,t)=\omega_{0}(R_{\Omega t}x)
is a stationary/uniformly-rotating vortex sheet with angular velocity
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\omega_{0}
is concentrated on
\Gamma
, with
z
and
\gamma
defined as above. Then the Birkhoff-Rott integral
BR
(
1.3
) and the strength
\gamma
satisfy the following two equations:

(2.1)

(BR-\Omega x^{\perp})\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{v}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{v}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

and

(2.2)

(BR(z(\alpha))-\Omega z^{\perp}(\alpha))\cdot\mathbf{s}(z(\alpha))\,\frac{\gamma(\alpha)}{|z^{\prime}(\alpha)|}=C.

Note that (
2.2
) can be written as

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left(I-P_{0}\right)\left[\left(BR(z,\Gamma)(z(\theta))-\Omega z(\theta)^{\perp}\right)\cdot\frac{z^{\prime}(\theta)\gamma(\theta)}{|z^{\prime}(\theta)|^{2}}\right]=0,

where
P_{0}
is a projection to the mean, that is,
P_{0}f:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\theta)d\theta
. For simplicity, we also denote
\fint f(\theta)d\theta:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\theta)d\theta
. Now plugging
z(\theta)=(1+r(\theta))(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))
and
\gamma(\theta):=b+g(\theta)
into (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) yields that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(b,g,r):=(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2})=(0,0),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}(b,g,r):=\fint_{-\pi}^{\pi}(b+g(\eta))\frac{\left(r^{\prime}(\theta)\cos(\theta-\eta)-(1+r(\theta))\sin(\theta-\eta)\right)(1+r(\eta))-(1+r(\theta))r^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+r(\theta))^{2}+(1+r(\eta))^{2}-2(1+r(\theta))(1+r(\eta))\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\Omega r^{\prime}(\theta)(1+r(\theta)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}(b,g,r):=\left(I-P_{0}\right)\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(b,g,r),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(b,g,r):=\fint_{-\pi}^{\pi}(b+g(\eta))\frac{(1+r(\theta))^{2}-(r^{\prime}(\theta)\sin(\theta-\eta)+(1+r(\theta))\cos(\theta-\eta))(1+r(\eta))}{(1+r(\theta))^{2}+(1+r(\eta))^{2}-2(1+r(\theta))(1+r(\eta))\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\times\frac{(b+g(\theta))}{r^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}+(1+r(\theta))^{2}}-\Omega(1+r(\theta))^{2}\frac{b+g(\theta)}{r^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}+(1+r(\theta))^{2}}.

Throughout the paper we will work with the following analytic function spaces. Let
c>0
be a sufficiently small parameter and let
\mathcal{C}_{w}(c)
be the space of analytic functions in the strip
|\Im(z)|\leq c
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, denote

\displaystyle X^{k}_{c}:=\left\{f(\theta)\in\mathcal{C}_{w}(c),\quad f(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\cos(2n\theta),\quad\sum_{\pm}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(\theta\pm ic)|^{2}+|\partial^{k}f(\theta\pm ic)|^{2}d\theta<\infty\right\}

\displaystyle\quad Y^{k}_{c}:=\left\{f(\theta)\in\mathcal{C}_{w}(c),\quad f(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\sin(2n\theta),\quad\sum_{\pm}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(\theta\pm ic)|^{2}+|\partial^{k}f(\theta\pm ic)|^{2}d\theta<\infty\right\},

From now on, due to scaling considerations, we will fix
\Omega=1
and
b
will play the role as bifurcation parameter. It is clear that
\mathcal{F}(b,0,0)=(0,0)
for all
b\in\mathbb{R}
since
\mathcal{F}_{1}(b,0,0)=0
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(b,0,0)
is constant. Our main theorem in this paper is the following:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
k\geq 3
, and let
c>0
be sufficiently small. Then, there exists a curve of solutions
(b,g,r)
of
\mathcal{F}=(0,0)
, belonging to
\mathbb{R}\times X^{k}_{c}\times X^{k+1}_{c}
and a neighbourhood of
(b,g,r)=(2,0,0)
, bifurcating from
(b,g,r)=(2,0,0)
such that
(g,r)\neq(0,0)
.

3.
Proof of the Main Theorem

The goal of this section is to prove the existence of non-radial uniformly-rotating vortex sheets. To do so, we will split the proof into the following steps: first we will prove that the functional
\mathcal{F}
is
C^{3}
, next we will study
D\mathcal{F}
to show that, as mentioned in the introduction, it is a Fredholm operator of index 0, with dim(Ker
(D\mathcal{F}))=1
. The next step is to apply Lyapunov-Schmidt theory and reduce the problem to a finite (2) dimensional one. In those coordinates, linear expansions fail to be conclusive (all the linear terms vanish) since 2 nontrivial branches emanate from the bifurcation point (as opposed to 1). Instead, we perform a quadratic expansion to determine that locally the bifurcation branches look like two pairs of straight lines (specifically as
x^{2}-y^{2}=0
in some well-chosen coordinates) and hence the bifurcation does not trivialize (as if it had been of the type
x^{2}+y^{2}=0
). We conclude the proof by handling the higher order terms and showing that they don’t alter the quadratic behaviour in a sufficiently small neighbourhood of the bifurcation point.

3.1.
Continuity of the functional

In this subsection, we will check the regularity of
\mathcal{F}
. As explained above, we will reduce the infinite dimensional problem to a finite dimensional problem and investigate its Taylor expansion up to quadratic order. Hence, we need to check if the functional is regular enough to do so. To this end, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
. Then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
(2,0,0)\in\mathbb{R}\times X^{k}_{c}\times X^{k+1}_{c}
such that
\mathcal{F}\in C^{3}\left(U;Y^{k}_{c}\times X^{k}_{c}\right)
.

Proof.

Since the stream function,
\omega*\mathcal{N}
, is invariant under rotations, it follows immediately that
\mathcal{F}
is also invariant under rotation by
\pi
-radians, hence
\mathcal{F}
has only even Fourier modes. Also the oddness of
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and evenness of
\mathcal{F}_{2}
follow from the invariance under reflection.

To prove the regularity, we briefly sketch the idea. We impose
k\geq 3
to ensure that
H^{k}
is a Banach algebra. It is clear that
\mathcal{F}
is smooth in
b
. It is also straightforward that, for example, for all
(g,r)
near
(0,0)\in X^{k}_{c}\times X^{k+1}_{c}
,

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{1}

\displaystyle(b,g+th_{1},r+th_{2})\bigg|_{t=0}=PV\fint h_{1}(\eta)\frac{\left(r^{\prime}(\theta)\cos(\theta-\eta)-(1+r(\theta))\sin(\theta-\eta)\right)(1+r(\eta))-(1+r(\theta))r^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+r(\theta))^{2}+(1+r(\eta))^{2}-2(1+r(\theta))(1+r(\eta))\cos(\theta-\eta)}

\displaystyle\quad+(b+g(\eta))\left[\frac{(h_{2}^{\prime}(\theta)\cos(\theta-\eta)-h_{2}(\theta)\sin(\theta-\eta))(1+r(\eta))-h_{2}(\theta)r^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+r(\theta))^{2}+(1+r(\eta))^{2}-2(1+r(\theta))(1+r(\eta))\cos(\theta-\eta)}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{\left(r^{\prime}(\theta)\cos(\theta-\eta)-(1+r(\theta))\sin(\theta-\eta)\right)h_{2}(\eta)-(1+r(\theta))h_{2}^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+r(\theta))^{2}+(1+r(\eta))^{2}-2(1+r(\theta))(1+r(\eta))\cos(\theta-\eta)}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\left[\left(r^{\prime}(\theta)\cos(\theta-\eta)-(1+r(\theta))\sin(\theta-\eta)\right)(1+r(\eta))-(1+r(\theta))r^{\prime}(\theta)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times\frac{\left[2(1+r(\theta)h_{2}(\theta)+2(1+r(\eta)h_{2}(\eta)-2\cos(\theta-\eta)(h_{2}(\theta)(1+r(\eta))+h_{2}(\eta)(1+r(\theta)))\right]}{((1+r(\theta))^{2}+(1+r(\eta))^{2}-2(1+r(\theta))(1+r(\eta))\cos(\theta-\eta))^{2}}\right]d\eta+\Omega h_{2}^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=:D\mathcal{F}_{1}(b,g,r)[h_{1},h_{2}],

and
D\mathcal{F}_{1}:\mathbb{R}\times X^{k}_{c}\times X^{k+1}_{c}\mapsto\mathcal{L}(X^{k}_{c}\times X^{k+1}_{c};Y^{k}_{c}\times X^{k}_{c})
is continuous. A similar derivation can be performed for
D\mathcal{F}_{2}
. For the higher derivatives, we refer to
[
7
,
8
,
14
,
18
,
37
]
for the method to deal with the singular integrals arising throughout the calculations.
∎

3.2.
Fredholm index of the linearized operator
D\mathcal{F}

This subsection is devoted to show that
D\mathcal{F}
is Fredholm of index zero. We can make all the calculations explicit, moreover the operator diagonalizes in Fourier modes. We have the following lemmas:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
g(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\cos(2n\theta)
and
r(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\cos(2n\theta)
. Then we have that

D\mathcal{F}(b,0,0)\left[g,r\right]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{g}(\theta)\\
\hat{r}(\theta)\end{array}\right),

where

\hat{g}(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{a}_{n}\sin(2n\theta),\quad\hat{r}(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{b}_{n}\cos(2n\theta),

and the coefficients satisfy, for any
n\geq 1
:

M_{n}\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}\\
b_{n}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}&-2n\left(\Omega-\frac{b}{2}\right)\\
\frac{b}{2}-\Omega&b^{2}(n-1)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}\\
b_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}_{n}\\
\hat{b}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

We use (
A.3
) in Lemma
A.2
and obtain

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\theta)=\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{1}(b,tg,tr)

\displaystyle=-\fint\frac{g(\eta)\sin(\theta-\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta+\left(\Omega-\frac{b}{2}\right)r^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\fint\frac{\cos(2n\eta)\sin(\theta-\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-2n)\left(\Omega-\frac{b}{2}\right)b_{n}\sin(2n\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{a_{n}}{2}+(-2n)\left(\Omega-\frac{b}{2}\right)b_{n}\right)\sin(2n\theta),

where the last equality follows from (
A.29
). Similarly, we apply (
A.4
) in Lemma
A.2
and (
A.30
) to obtain

\displaystyle\hat{r}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b}{2}-\Omega\right)g(\theta)+b^{2}\left(\fint\frac{r(\theta)-r(\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta-r(\theta)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{b}{2}-\Omega\right)a_{n}\cos(2n\theta)+b^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\left(\fint\frac{\cos(2n\theta)-\cos(2n\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta-\cos(2n\theta)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\left(\frac{b}{2}-\Omega\right)a_{n}+b^{2}\left(n-1\right)b_{n}\right)\cos(2n\theta).

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let us fix

and
\Omega=1
. We also denote

and

. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\text{Ker}\left(D\mathcal{F}(2,0,0)\right)=\text{span}\left\{v\right\}\subset{X^{k}_{c}\times X^{k+1}_{c}},

\displaystyle\text{Im}\left(D\mathcal{F}(2,0,0)\right)^{\perp}=\text{span}\left\{w\right\}\subset Y^{k}_{c}\times X^{k}_{c}.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle M_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&4(n-1)\end{pmatrix},

for all
n\geq 1
.
For all
n\geq 2
,

is clearly an isomorphism, while
\text{Ker}(M_{1})=\text{Im}(M_{1})^{\perp}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\

. By orthogonality of Fourier modes, this proves the lemma.

3.3.
Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction

In this subsection, we will aim to derive a finite dimensional system which is equivalent to (
2.4
). From Lemma
3.3
, we have the following orthogonal decompositions of the function spaces:

\displaystyle X:=X^{k}_{c}\times X^{k+1}_{c}=\text{span}\left\{v\right\}\oplus\text{Ker}\left(D\mathcal{F}(2,0,0)\right)^{\perp}=:\text{span}\left\{v\right\}\oplus\mathcal{X},\quad v\in\text{Ker}\left(D\mathcal{F}(2,0,0)\right),

\displaystyle Y:=Y^{k}_{c}\times X^{k}_{c}=\text{span}\left\{w\right\}\oplus\text{Im}\left(D\mathcal{F}(2,0,0)\right)=:\text{span}\left\{w\right\}\oplus\mathcal{Y},\quad w\in\text{Im}\left(D\mathcal{F}(2,0,0)\right)^{\perp},

where
v
and
w
are as defined in Lemma
3.3
. Let us consider the orthogonal projections

P:X\rightarrow\text{span}\left\{v\right\},\quad Q:Y\rightarrow\text{span}\left\{w\right\}.

More precisely, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle P(g(\theta),r(\theta))=\left(0,\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\int r(\eta)\cos(2\eta)d\eta\right)\cos(2\theta)\right)\quad\text{ for all }(g,r)\in X^{k}_{c}\times X^{k+1}_{c},

(3.2)

\displaystyle Q(G(\theta),R(\theta))=\left(0,\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\int R(\eta)\cos(2\eta)d\eta\right)\cos(2\theta)\right)\quad\text{ for all }(G,R)\in Y^{k}_{c}\times X^{k}_{c}.

We remark that we will sometimes abuse notation and identify
\mathcal{F}(b,g,r)
with
\mathcal{F}(b,(g,r))
, where
(g,r)\in X
. Let us define
G:\mathbb{R}\times\text{span}\left\{v\right\}\times\mathcal{X}\mapsto{Y}
as follows:

\displaystyle G(b,f,x):=\mathcal{F}(b,f+x),\quad\text{ for }\quad b\in\mathbb{R},\quad f\in\text{span}\left\{v\right\},\quad x\in\mathcal{X}.

Then (
2.4
) is equivalent to (for
(g,r)=f+x
)

(3.3)

\displaystyle QG(b,f,x)=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\left(I-Q\right)G(b,f,x)=0.

However, it follows from Lemma
3.3
that

(3.4)

\displaystyle D_{x}\left((I-Q)G\right)(b,0,0)=\left(I-Q\right)D\mathcal{F}(b,0)P:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}

is an isomorphism, consequently, the implicit function theorem yields that there is an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}\times\text{span}\left\{v\right\}
near
(b,0)
and a function
\varphi:U\mapsto\mathcal{X}
such that

\displaystyle\left(I-Q\right)G(b,f,\varphi(b,f))=\left(I-Q\right)\mathcal{F}(b,f+\varphi(b,f))=0.

Note that from
\mathcal{F}(b,0)=(0,0)
for any

, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\varphi(b,0)=0,

and thus (
3.3
) is equivalent to

(3.6)

\displaystyle 0=QG(b,f,\varphi(b,f))=Q\mathcal{F}(b,f+\varphi(b,f)),\quad(b,f)\in U.

Since
\text{span}\left\{v\right\}
is one dimensional, we have
f=tv
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
, therefore the system (
3.6
) can be written in terms of the variables
b
and
t
as

\displaystyle 0=Q\mathcal{F}(b,tv+\varphi(b,tv))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\left(Q\mathcal{F}(b,stv+\varphi(b,stv))\right)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}QD\mathcal{F}(b,stv+\varphi(b,stv))(tv+t\partial_{f}\varphi(b,stv)v)ds,

where we used (
3.5
) to obtain the second equality.
Dividing the right-hand side by
t
to get rid of the trivial solutions, we are led to solve the following two dimensional problem:

(3.7)

\displaystyle 0=F_{red}(b,t):=\int_{0}^{1}QD\mathcal{F}(b,stv+\varphi(b,stv))(v+\partial_{f}\varphi(b,stv)v)ds,\quad(b,tv)\in U.

3.4.
Quadratic expansion of the reduced functional

Proposition 3.4
.

[

Let

(a)

First derivatives:

\displaystyle\partial_{b}F_{red}(2,0)=Q\partial_{b}D\mathcal{F}(2,0)v,

3.5.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Now we are ready to prove the main theorem of this section.

Proof.

From (
3.7
), it suffices to show that there exist
(b,t)
such that
t\neq 0
and
F_{red}(b,t)=0
. To do so, we expand
F_{red}
up to quadratic terms and obtain that for all
(b,t)
near
(2,0)

\displaystyle F_{red}(b,t)

\displaystyle=\left[F_{red}(2,0)+\partial_{b}F_{red}(2,0)(b-2)+\partial_{t}F_{red}(2,0)t\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ +\frac{1}{2}\partial_{bb}F_{red}(2,0)(b-2)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{tt}F_{red}(2,0)t^{2}+\partial_{tb}F_{red}(2,0)(b-2)t+\left((b-2)^{2}+t^{2}\right)\epsilon(b,t)\right]w,

where

is a continuous function such that
\lim_{(b,t)\to(2,0)}\epsilon(b,t)=\epsilon(2,0)=0
. From Proposition
3.5
, it follows that (we drop

for simplicity)

\displaystyle F_{red}(b,t)=(b-2)^{2}-4t^{2}+\left((b-2)^{2}+t^{2}\right)\epsilon(b,t).

Now we use the change of variables
b:=x+2
and
t=xy
, so that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\hat{F}(x,y):=\frac{F_{red}(x+2,xy)}{x^{2}}=\left(1-4y^{2}\right)+(1+y^{2})\epsilon(x+2,xy).

Clearly,
\hat{F}\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)=0
and
\partial_{y}\hat{F}\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)=-4\neq 0
.
Therefore the implicit function theorem implies that there exists a continuous function
\phi
near
0
such that
\hat{F}(x,\phi(x))=0
and
\phi(0)=\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore it follows from (
3.13
) that there exists a pair
(b,t)
such that
t\neq 0
and
F_{red}(b,t)=0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}=\fint A_{1}A_{2}A_{3}d\eta+\Omega A_{4},

Lemma A.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}P_{2}\frac{d^{3}}{dt^{3}}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(2,tv)=4\cos(2\theta),

which implies (
A.20
).

To prove (
A.21
), we use (
A.17
) and (
A.8
) in Lemma
A.4
with
g=0,\ \tilde{g}=2\cos(2\theta),\ r=\cos(2\theta)
and
\tilde{r}=0
and obtain

\displaystyle P_{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dtds}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(2,tv+s\tilde{v})

\displaystyle=P_{2}\left(-4\cos^{2}(2\theta)+4\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)+\cos(2\eta))(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+8\fint\frac{\cos(2\eta)\sin(2\theta)\sin(\theta-\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta\right)

\displaystyle=P_{2}\left(-4\cos^{2}(2\theta)+4\cos^{2}(2\theta)+4\left(-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cos(4\theta)\right)+4\sin^{2}(2\theta)\right)

\displaystyle=0,

where the second equality follows from (
A.30
), (
A.29
) and (
A.9
). This implies (
A.21
).

To prove (
A.22
), it follows from (
A.18
) and Lemma
A.2
that

\displaystyle\partial_{b}D\mathcal{F}(2,0)\left(-8\cos(4\theta),\frac{3}{2}\cos(4\theta)\right)=(3\sin(4\theta),2\cos(4\theta)).

By projecting it to the image of
Q
, we obtain (
A.22
).

To prove (
A.23
), we note that
Q\partial_{b}D\mathcal{F}(2,0)\tilde{v}=\left(0,P_{2}\partial_{b}\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(2,t\tilde{v})\right)\bigg|_{t=0}
. Hence it follows from (
A.4
) in Lemma
A.2
and (
A.17
) that
P_{2}\partial_{b}\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(2,t\tilde{v})\bigg|_{t=0}=\cos(2\theta)
. This implies (
A.23
).
∎

A.3.
Basic Integrals

Lemma A.8
.

For
\mathbb{N}\ni m\geq 1
, it holds that

(A.29)

\displaystyle\fint\frac{\cos(m\eta)\sin(\theta-\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta=\frac{1}{2}\sin(m\theta),

(A.30)

\displaystyle\fint\frac{\cos(m\theta)-\cos(m\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta=\frac{m}{2}\cos(m\theta).

Proof.

For (
A.29
), it is clear that
\fint\frac{\cos(m\eta)\sin(\theta-\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta=\frac{1}{2}\fint\cos(m\eta)\cot{\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}d\eta=\frac{1}{2}H(\cos(m\theta))(\theta)
, where
H
denotes the Hilbert transform in the periodic domain. Therefore the result follows immediately since
H(\cos(m\theta))(\theta)=\sin(m\theta)
.

For (
A.30
), we recall that
\fint\frac{f(\theta)-f(\eta)}{1-\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta=\Lambda f(\theta)=:(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}f(\theta)
. Thus (
A.30
) follows immediately.
∎

Lemma A.9
.

(A.31)

\displaystyle\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))\cos(4\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta=-\frac{1}{2}\cos(2\theta)+\frac{1}{2}\cos(6\theta),

(A.32)

\displaystyle\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))\cos(2\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cos(4\theta),

(A.33)

\displaystyle\fint\frac{(\cos(4\theta)-\cos(4\eta))\cos(2\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta=\cos(6\theta),

(A.34)

\displaystyle\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))\sin(2\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta=\frac{1}{2}\sin(4\theta).

Proof.

We will show (
A.31
) only. (
A.32
), (
A.33
) and (
A.34
) can be proved in the same way. For (
A.31
), one can write

\displaystyle\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))\cos(4\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Lambda(\cos(2\theta)\cos(4\theta))(\theta)-\frac{1}{2}\cos(2\theta)\Lambda(\cos(4\theta))(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\cos(2\theta)+\frac{1}{2}\cos(6\theta),

where the last equality follows from (
A.30
).
∎

Lemma A.10
.

\displaystyle\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))^{3}}{(2-2\cos(\theta-\eta))^{2}}d\eta=\frac{9}{4}\cos(2\theta)-\cos(6\theta).

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\fint

\displaystyle\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))^{3}}{(2-2\cos(\theta-\eta))^{2}}d\eta=\fint\frac{(-2\sin(\theta-\eta)\sin(\theta+\eta))^{3}}{\left(4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}d\eta=4\fint-\frac{\cos^{3}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\sin^{3}(\theta+\eta)}{\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}d\eta

\displaystyle=4\fint\frac{\cos^{3}\frac{\eta}{2}\sin^{3}(2\theta+\eta)}{\sin\frac{\eta}{2}}d\eta=4\fint\frac{\cos^{3}\frac{\eta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(3\sin^{2}(2\theta)\cos(2\theta)\cos^{2}\eta\sin\eta+\cos^{3}(2\theta)\sin^{3}\eta\right)d\eta

\displaystyle=12\sin^{2}(2\theta)\cos(2\theta)\fint\frac{\cos^{3}\frac{\eta}{2}\cos^{2}\eta\sin\eta}{\sin\frac{\eta}{2}}d\eta+4\cos^{3}(2\theta)\fint\frac{\cos^{3}\frac{\eta}{2}\sin^{3}\eta}{\sin\frac{\eta}{2}}d\eta=\frac{21}{4}\sin^{2}(2\theta)\cos(2\theta)+\frac{5}{4}\cos^{3}(2\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{9}{4}\cos(2\theta)-\cos(6\theta),

which proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma A.11
.

\displaystyle\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))^{2}\sin(\theta-\eta)}{(2-2\cos(\theta-\eta))^{2}}d\eta=-\sin(4\theta).

Proof.

Using the integration by parts, we compute

\displaystyle\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))^{2}\sin(\theta-\eta)}{(2-2\cos(\theta-\eta))^{2}}d\eta=-2\fint\frac{(\cos(2\theta)-\cos(2\eta))\sin(2\eta)}{2-2\cos(\theta-\eta)}d\eta.

Therefore the result follows from (
A.34
).
∎
