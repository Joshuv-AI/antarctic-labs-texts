Ergodicity for the hyperbolic P ​ ( Φ ) 2 P(\Phi)_{2} -model
Leonardo Tolomeo

Abstract
. We consider the problem of ergodicity for the P ​ ( Φ ) 2 P(\Phi)_{2} measure of quantum field theory under the flow of the singular stochastic (damped) wave equation u t ​ t + u t + ( 1 − Δ ) ​ u + : p ⁡ ( u ) := 2 ​ ξ u_{tt}+u_{t}+(1-\Delta)u+{:}\,p(u)\mspace{2mu}{:}=\sqrt{2}\xi , posed on the two-dimensional torus 𝕋 2 \mathbb{T}^{2} . We show that the P ​ ( Φ ) 2 P(\Phi)_{2} measure is ergodic, and moreover that it is the unique invariant measure for (the Markov process associated to) this equation which belongs to a fairly large class of probability measures over distributions. The main technical novelty of this paper is the introduction of the new concepts of asymptotic strong Feller and asymptotic coupling restricted to the action of a group. We first develop a general theory that allows us to deduce a suitable support theorem under these hypotheses, and then show that the stochastic wave equation satisfies these properties when restricted the action of translations by shifts belonging to the Sobolev space H 1 − ε × H − ε H^{1-\varepsilon}\times H^{-\varepsilon} . We then exploit the newly developed theory in order to conclude ergodicity and (conditional) uniqueness for the P ​ ( Φ ) 2 P(\Phi)_{2} measure.

1.1.
The strong Feller property and its failure for stochastic wave equations

It turns out that from a technical point of view, the stochastic quantisation program for wave equations such as (
SDNLW
) is much harder to achieve than in the parabolic case.
While important milestones for the local well posedness theory for canonical stochastic quantisation equations are progressively been achieved, global well posedness and ergodicity results are both very rare. Indeed, local well posedness for equation (
SDNLW
) has been proven in
[
20
]
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, in
[
40
,
35
]
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and in
[
34
]
on a general 2-dimensional compact manifold. A series of 3-dimensional results have been proven in
[
21
]
and
[
33
]
for the equation with quadratic nonlinearity,
in
[
32
,
3
,
36
]
for cubic nonlinearities under the addition of some smoothing in the equation, and finally in
[
4
]
for the canonical stochastic quantisation equation for the
\Phi^{4}_{3}
measure.

When an invariant measure is available, often global well-posedness for a.e. initial data sampled according to the invariant measure follows via an application of Bourgain’s invariant measure argument
[
2
]
(see also
[
17
, Theorem 6.1]
for a general formulation).
However, this is more-or-less the only globalisation argument that has been shown to work for
singular
stochastic wave equations. The only exceptions that the author is aware of, in which it is actually possible to show some appropriate (pathwise) energy estimates for the solutions, are the results by the author
[
40
]
and Gubinelli, Koch, Oh and the author
[
22
]
.
While in principle good energy estimates are not necessary in order to prove (unique) ergodicity, they are a fundamental tool in many applications. Nevertheless, the main difficulty in showing ergodicity for stochastic wave equations comes from a different issue, which is the failure of the strong Feller property.

Before we can discuss in details the techniques developed in this paper to get around this problem, it is instructive to move back to the case of parabolic SPDEs (such as (
SQE
)), and describe the general strategy to proving unique ergodicity in that case.
Let us denote the solution of a SPDE at time
t\geq 0
with initial data
u_{0}
and driven by a noise
\xi
by
\Phi_{t}(u_{0},\xi)
. Suppose that on an appropriate space
X
of initial data, the solution
\Phi_{t}(u_{0},\xi)
exists for every
t>0
.
Under reasonable assumptions on the equation, the noise
\xi
, and the local well posedeness theory, this defines a Markov process on the space
X
. In particular, for any bounded measurable function
F:X\to\mathbb{R}
, we can define

P_{t}F:=\mathbb{E}[F(\Phi_{t}(u_{0},\xi))],

and
P_{t}
will be a Markov semigroup. We denote its dual by
P_{t}^{*}
. Unique ergodicity for the SPDE then corresponds to having a unique invariant measure for the semigroup
P_{t}
. Typically, the main ingredients for showing such a statement are the following.

(Long time estimates):

Show good long time estimates for the flow, i.e. estimates of the form

\mathbb{E}\|\Phi_{t}(u_{0},\xi)\|\leq C(u_{0})\text{ for every }u_{0}\in X,

where
\|\cdot\|
is some appropriate norm of the solution
1
1

1

There is actually no need for this to be a norm, and one can consider situations in which
\|\cdot\|
is replaced by an appropriate “size function”
r:X\to\mathbb{R}
such that
r(\Phi_{t}(u_{0},\xi))\to\infty
as
t\uparrow t^{*}
implies blowup at time
t^{*}
(or more typically, is the
definition
of blowup at time
t^{*}
).
, and the constant
C(u_{0})
is allowed to depend on the initial data
u_{0}
, but not on time.

(Irreducibility):

Fix a base point
u_{*}
, and show that for every small ball
B_{\varepsilon}(u_{*})
, for every
R>0
, and for every
u_{0}
belonging to
B_{R}(u_{*})
, we have that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(\{\Phi_{t}(u_{0},\xi)\in B_{\varepsilon}\})\geq 2\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon,R)>0.

(Coupling):

Show that for every
\delta>0
and for some appropriate distance on probability measures
d
, there exists some
\varepsilon>0
, such that for every
u_{0}\in B_{\varepsilon}(u_{*})
,

\limsup_{t\to\infty}d(P_{t}^{*}\delta_{u_{0}},P_{t}^{*}\delta_{u_{*}})\leq\delta.

The proof of (unique) ergodicity then goes roughly as follows: starting from
u_{0}
, by irreducibility, after some time
t_{1}>0
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{t_{1}}(u_{0},\xi)\in B_{\varepsilon})\geq\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon,\|u_{0}\|)
. Then from the coupling property, the evolution starting from the ball
B_{\varepsilon}
will be “close in law” to the evolution of
{u_{*}}
. For the part of the evolution that at time
t_{1}
is outside the ball
B_{\varepsilon}
, we repeat the same process: after a (random) time
t_{2}
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{t_{2}}(u_{0},\xi)\in B_{\varepsilon}|\Phi_{t_{1}}(u_{0},\xi)\not\in B_{\varepsilon})\geq\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon,\|\Phi_{t_{1}}(u_{0},\xi)\|)
. Iterating this process, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}(\{\Phi_{t_{j}}(u_{0},\xi)\not\in B_{\varepsilon}\text{ for every }j\leq J)\leq\prod_{j=1}^{J}(1-\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon,\|\Phi_{t_{j-1}}(u_{0},\xi)\|)).

This is where the long time estimates come into play: up to possibly extending the times
t_{1},t_{2},\dotsc
, we can guarantee that
\|\Phi_{t_{j-1}}(u_{0},\xi)\|
remains under control, and so
\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon,\|\Phi_{t_{j-1}}(u_{0},\xi)\|)
diverges. Therefore, we obtain that the evolution starting from
u_{0}
is “close in law” (with respect to some appropriate distance) to the evolution starting from
u_{*}
with high probability, and this allows us to conclude uniqueness of the invariant measure.
If one then has better control over the quantities
C(u_{0}),\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon,R),
and
\varepsilon
as a function of
\delta
, it is also possible to extract a convergence rate from this argument, which will typically be exponential.
It is also possible to slightly weaken the three properties above, at the cost of getting a worse convergence rate.

In practice, however, one needs not to perform this complicated analysis and show tight control over the various quantities, but there are instead a number of pre-confectioned results that can be used in order to obtain exponential convergence to equilibrium. A classical example of such results is Harris theorem (see
[
29
]
for a satisfying proof of this result). In this context of “pre-pacakged” results, a very successful approach has been to rely on the
strong Feller property
. In short, we say that a semigroup has the strong Feller property if, for some time
t>0
,

F\text{ measurable and bounded}\Rightarrow P_{t}F\text{ is continuous.}

This is essentially an infinitesimal smoothing property of the semigroup
P_{t}
, and in the case of finite dimensional systems of SDEs, checking this property is in most situation a simple consequence of Hörmander’s hypoellipticity theorem.
It is easy to check that, in the finite dimensional setting, both (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) always satisfy Hörmander’s condition, hence under extremely general assumptions on the potential
V
, the associated semigroups do possess the strong Feller property. Since the invariant measures (
1.1
), (
1.4
) trivially have full support, the strong Feller property automatically implies unique ergodicity (see
[
30
, Corollary 3.9]
).
This approach has had incredible success in infinite dimension as well, with the result in
[
30
]
showing that an incredibly large class of parabolic SPDEs has the strong Feller property.

Even in situations where the strong Feller property fails or it is otherwise hard to prove, we do possess an alternative theory, developed by Hairer and Mattingly in their seminal work
[
26
]
.
In their work, they introduced the notion of “asymptotic strong Feller property”. Morally spealing, this property does not require
P_{t}F
to be continuous for any positive time
t>0
, but requires the continuity to hold in the limit
t\to\infty
.
2
2

2

For the actual definition, we refer the reader to
[
26
, Section 3.2]
.
The strength of both the strong Feller property and the asymptotic strong Feller property is encapsulated in the following support theorem.

Proposition 1.2

(Theorem 3.16 in
[
26
]
)
.

Suppose that the semigroup
P_{t}
has the (asymptotic) strong Feller property. Let
\mu,\nu
be two invariant measures with
\mu\perp\nu
. Then we have

\supp(\mu)\cap\supp(\nu)=\emptyset.

It is fairly easy to see that the conclusion of this proposition and the irreducibility property above are in contradiction. Therefore, we have that irreducibility and the (asymptotic) strong Feller property imply unique ergodicity, if at least one invariant measure is shown exists.
3
3

3

More precisely, without this extra assumption, they imply that there exists
at most
one invariant measure.

The combined toolbox of strong Feller property and asymptotic strong Feller property has been extremely successful in showing ergodicity for several classes of SPDEs, including the above mentioned results for singular parabolic SPDEs
[
41
,
30
]
, Navier-Stokes equations
[
15
,
26
,
28
]
, and in many situations with degenerate noise
[
27
,
8
,
10
,
19
]
.

However, such techniques do not seem to be easily applicable to wave equations, and more in general dispersive SPDEs.
Indeed, the only results known to the author that prove ergodicity for stochastic dispersive PDEs are
[
1
,
5
,
13
,
18
,
39
,
16
]
. While some advances towards the low-regularity regime has been achieved in the results by the author and by Forlano and the author
[
39
,
16
]
, none of these results can deal with the singular regime.

The main observation to explain this discrepancy is that dispersive (stochastic) PDEs seem to
never
have the strong Feller property on a connected state space, as it was firstly observed by the author in
[
39
]
.
The reason is the following. By expressing the solution of a stochastic dispersive PDE using the Duhamel/variation of constants formula, we obtain that

u(t)=S(t)u_{0}+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}S(t-t^{\prime})\xi(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}+\mathcal{N}_{t}(u),

where
S(t)
denotes the linear propagator for the equation at hand,
\xi
is the particular choice of the noise and
\mathcal{N}_{t}(u)
denotes a nonlinear remainder.
We expect the regularity of the solutions (and hence the space in which the invariant measures are concentrated) to be dictated by the stochastic convolution
\psi=\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}S(t-t^{\prime})\xi(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime},
which will typically belong to some Sobolev space
H^{s_{0}-\varepsilon}
for some
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and every
\varepsilon>0
,
but not
belong to
H^{s_{0}}
. This forces us to take a state space
X
for the Markov semigroup to be rich enough to contain functions that belong to
H^{s_{0}-\varepsilon}\setminus H^{s_{0}}
, but not are not any smoother.
As it is common in this business, we expect the nonlinear remainder to be smoother than the linear solution.
4
4

4

As far as the author is aware, this is the case for every (stochastc) dispersive equation that has a satisfactory local well posedness theory.

The main difference with the parabolic case, is that the linear propagator is
invertible
in the Sobolev spaces
H^{\sigma}
for every
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, the linear propagator preserves the regularity of the initial data.
Therefore, one can test the definition of the strong Feller property on the following indicator functions

(1.8)

F(u)=\mathbb{1}_{H^{s_{0}-\varepsilon}\setminus H^{s_{0}}}(u).

From the discussion abobe, one obtains that

P_{t}F(u)=F(S(t)u)=F(u),

which is also an indicator function, hence it is not continuous (as long as the space is connected, and there exists at least one element of the state space that also belongs to
H^{s_{0}}
).

In principle, one could try to shrink the state space in order to avoid this kind of counterexamples, but in many applications this seems to be a fool’s errand.
The reason is that one can replicate the counterexample above by replacing
H^{s_{0}-\varepsilon}\setminus H^{s_{0}}
with any set
S
which is both invariant for the linear propagator
S(t)
and by (relatively) smooth perturbations. In the case of dispersive equations, this is an extremely rich family.

Of course, as per the discussion above, one could try to completely avoid relying on the strong Feller property, and instead attempt building the theory using the asymptotic strong Feller property instead (or the related concept of asymptotic couplings, see
[
28
,
19
,
8
]
). In principle, this seems to be a reasonable approach, since the linear equation associated to (
SDNLW
)

\begin{cases}u_{t}=v\\
v_{t}=-v+(1-\Delta)u\end{cases}

has a propagator
S(t):\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\mapsto S(t)\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}
which satisfies

\|S(t)\|_{H^{s}\times H^{s-1}\righttoleftarrow}\lesssim e^{-\frac{t}{2}}.

However, in order to show such property, one would need a good long-time estimate on the difference of two solutions
\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi_{1})-\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\xi_{2})
, where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
are two copies of the noise with the same law as
\xi
.
5
5

5

One actually just needs some kind of control of the signed measures
\Law(\xi_{j})-\Law(\xi)
in total variation. This generalisation is extremely useful in many applications, and we will make use of similar ideas in the following sections.

This is how Forlano and the author achieved the ergodicity result for
2k=4
in the non-singular case
[
16
]
. However, dealing with the singular case seems to be beyond the current technology.
The reason is that, since the nonlinearity
u^{3}
has a controlled modulus of continuity only on bounded set, one would need to show some good global estimates for a
single
solution
\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi)
to begin with.
As discussed earlier in the introduction, this seems to be extremely hard in the singular case, and such an estimate is not known for any singular wave equation
6
6

6

The estimates in
[
40
,
22
]
grow with a double exponential in time, which is way too fast for this argument to work - and their proof works only in the case
2k=4
.
.
The main reason is that, contrarily to the parabolic case, the only “useful” quantity to control the global evolution for wave equations is the energy

E(u,u_{t})=\frac{a_{2k}}{2k}\int u^{2k}+\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int u^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int|u_{t}|^{2},

up to some refinements. It should not be a surprise that when solutions became rougher and rougher, the quantity above gives progressively less and less information on the growth of solutions, up to a point in which the argument breaks down completely.
It is interesting that for
2k=4
, the threshold of regularity for obtaining energy estimates corresponds exactly to the threshold for singularity of the equation (
SDNLW
).
It is unclear to the author if this is just an accident due to the proof techniques, or there is a deeper connection between the two.

1.2.
Asymptotic couplings restricted to the action of a group

In view of the discussion in Section
1.1
, one might wonder how it is possible to get a positive result in Theorem
1.1
to begin with. The starting point is the following observation by the author in
[
39
]
, that now we adapt to (
SDNLW
). By the analysis in
[
20
,
22
]
, we know that the solution of (
SDNLW
) can be written as

\mathbf{u}(t)=S(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}+\vec{\psi}(t,\xi)+\mathbf{v}(t),

where
S(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}+\vec{\psi}(t,\xi)
denotes the (vector) solution
(\psi,\psi_{t})
to the
linear
equation

(1.9)

\begin{cases}\psi_{tt}+\psi_{t}=\sqrt{2}\xi,\\
(u,u_{t})(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0},\end{cases}

and
\mathbf{v}(t)\in H^{1-\varepsilon}\times H^{-\varepsilon}=:\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
is a smoother nonlinear reminder. For simplicity of notation, denote
\mathcal{H}^{s}:=H^{s}\times\mathcal{H}^{s-1}
. Inspired by the form of the functionals (
1.8
) that were used to disprove the strong Feller property, we consider

F(\mathbf{u})=\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\setminus\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}.

We remark here that
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\setminus\mathcal{H}^{0}
is the typical regularity of solutions with initial data sampled according to (
1.5
). As discussed in Section 1.1, we have that

P_{t}F(\mathbf{u}_{0})=F(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi))=F(S(t)\mathbf{u}_{0})=F(\mathbf{u}_{0}).

As a consequence,
P_{t}F
is not continuous in the topology of
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
. However, if
\mathbf{u}_{0}^{1},\mathbf{u}_{0}^{2}
are such that
\mathbf{u}_{0}^{1}-\mathbf{u}_{0}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
, then we have

P_{t}F(\mathbf{u}_{0}^{1})-P_{t}F(\mathbf{u}_{0}^{2})=0.

In particular, if we consider the distance

d_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{u}_{0}^{1},\mathbf{u}_{0}^{2})=\|\mathbf{u}_{0}^{1}-\mathbf{u}_{0}^{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}},

one has that
P_{t}F
is
continuous
in the topology induced by
d_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}
.
While in this way the state space is not connected, the change of topology removes the main obstruction to showing the strong Feller property.
If then we are able to show a (stronger) version of Proposition
1.2
, we can deduce that if two invariant measures
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
are such that
\nu_{1}\perp\nu_{2}
, then there exists a set
E
such that

\nu_{1}(E)=1,\hskip 10.0pt\nu_{2}(E)=0,\hskip 25.0ptE=E+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}

Finally, one can show that such properties are in contradiction with the extra property
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\ll\rho
. From this, we deduce that the measure
\rho
must be ergodic.
The reason why the contradiction holds, is that the family
\{E=E+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\}
is contained in the
\sigma
-algebra generated by sure events for the Gaussian measure
\rho_{0}
(see Lemma
3.15
and
[
39
, Remark 5.8]
), and so for any such set
E
we must have
\nu_{j}(E)=\rho_{0}(E)
.

The strategy described above is essentially how ergodicity for (
SDNLW
) when
2k=4
was shown on the one dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}
in the previous work by the author
[
39
]
. However, there are a series of issues in extending this strategy to the 2 dimensional case.
The most important of these, is that it is unclear if the strong Feller property holds after the change of topology induced by
d_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}
.
The technical reason for this, is the fact that the space
\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
is strictly bigger than the Cameron-Martin space for the Gaussian measure
\rho_{0}
, as opposed to the analogous property for the 1-dimensional flow, which does hold. This element was used crucially in
[
39
]
.

Nevertheless, we can still show the following coupling property: for every
\mathbf{u}_{0}
such that the solution
\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi)
does not grow too fast, and for every
\mathbf{u}_{0}^{2}
such that
\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{u}_{0}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
, there exist a noise
\xi^{\prime}
which satisfies
\Law(\xi^{\prime})\ll\Law(\xi)
and

\mathbb{P}\big(\{\|\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0}^{1},\xi)-\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0}^{2},\xi^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\lesssim e^{-\frac{t}{4}}\}\big)>\frac{1}{2}.

This is essentially the content of Lemma
3.9
below, and can be shown via a carefully chosen Girsanov shift argument. While we cannot show that the growth assumption on
\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi)
holds for every initial data
\mathbf{u}_{0}
due to the difficulties described in Section 1.1,
we can show that it holds almost surely according to any invariant measure in the class
W^{1}_{{:}\,p\mspace{2mu}{:}}
. The main novelty of this paper then consists in codifying the correct support theorem that holds under the very weak coupling assumption above.

To this scope, it is convenient to put everything into an abstract framework, and show a support theorem that holds in a general setting. We consider the space
Y
of “good” initial data (such that the solution has controlled growth as
t\to\infty
), and we define the state space to be

X=Y+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}.

Then we see the space
\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
as a group
\mathscr{G}
acting on
X
by translations, i.e.

\mathscr{G}\times X\ni(\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{0})\mapsto\tau_{\mathbf{v}_{0}}(\mathbf{u}_{0})=\mathbf{u}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{0}.

This action allows us to define a new topology on the space
X
, which is induced by the distance

d_{\mathscr{G}}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})=\inf\{1\wedge\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}:\tau_{\mathbf{v}_{0}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})=\mathbf{u}_{2}\}=1\wedge\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}

Under this distance, the space
X
loses most of the “good” measure-theoretical properties of Polish spaces, namely, the space
(X,d_{\mathscr{G}})
is not separable, it has uncountably many connected components,
and the invariant measure
\rho
will not be a Radon measure on this space. Nevertheless, thanks to this definition, the coupling property above can be codified in the following way: for every
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in X
, for every
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathscr{G}
and for every
\varphi:(X,d_{\mathscr{G}})\to\mathbb{R}
Lipschitz, we have that

\big|P_{t}\varphi(\mathbf{u}_{0})-P_{t}\varphi(\tau_{\mathbf{v}_{0}}(\mathbf{u}_{0}))\big|\leq 2\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0})\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+e^{-\frac{t}{4}}C(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0})\|\varphi\|_{\Lip}

for some
\varepsilon_{0}<1
. This is the basis for the concept of
asymptotic coupling property restricted to the action of a group
that we will introduce in Section 2. Similarly, when
\varepsilon_{0}\to 0
as
\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|\to 0
, we say that the semigroup has the asymptotic
strong Feller
property restricted to the action of the group
\mathscr{G}
.
One needs to be very careful with measurability issues here, since for a function
\varphi:(X,d_{\mathscr{G}})\to\mathbb{R}
, being Lipschitz does not automatically imply being measurable. Nevertheless, we can still deduce a support theorem in the guise of Proposition
1.2
, which we formulate in Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
2.7
.
The latter states that under an appropriate asymptotic coupling assumption, if
\mu,\nu
are two invariant measures with
\mu\perp\nu
, and
X/\mathscr{G}
is the space of orbits of the action of
\mathscr{G}
, then
\pi_{\sharp}\mu\perp\pi_{\sharp}\nu
as well, where
\pi:X\to X/\mathscr{G}
is the canonical projection. Together with properties of the linear evolution for the equation (
1.9
), this support theorem allows us to conclude the result of Theorem
1.1
.

Structure of the paper

Further remarks

Remark 1.3
.

The main part of the analysis for equation (
SDNLW
) will happen on the state space
X
defined in (
3.34
).
Unfortunately, at this stage we are not able to show that this space is a Borel subset of
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
(even if we believe it should be), but only that it has full measure according to any invariant measure in
W^{1}_{{:}\,p\mspace{2mu}{:}}
.
While this seems to be a minor point, due to the unusual setting of the theory in Section 2, we choose to take an extremely cautious approach,
and slowly check that by performing all the straightforward modifications to the definition of the measures and of the semigroup, one is still able to apply the theory of Section 2 to conclude Theorem
1.1
. This is the role of the map
\iota^{\ast}
introduced in Section 3.5. Unfortunately, the addition of this map makes the proofs of Section 3.5 and 3.6 notationally heavy. On a first reading, the author would suggest that the reader assumes that the set
X
is a Borel subset of
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
, in which case
\iota^{\ast}
is simply the identity map (after restricting the measure to
X
).

Remark 1.4
.

In the result of Theorem
1.1
, the particular choice of
\varepsilon_{k}
is such that the Sobolev inequality

\|u^{2k-1}\|_{H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}^{2k-1},

holds, and does not play a major role in the proof (see Lemma
3.4
). It is likely possible to push the value of
\varepsilon
to
\varepsilon<\min(\frac{2}{2k-2},\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2k-2})
, which corresponds to the local well posedness theory for the equation (
SDNLW
) (see
[
20
]
). However, such an extension would make the technical part of the proof significantly harder to digest, without substantially affecting the result of Theorem
1.1
. For sake of exposition, we decided to avoid this further complication.

Remark 1.5
.

The (conditional) uniqueness result in Theorem
1.1
depends on the particular definition of the flow
\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi)
of (
SDNLW
).
More precisely, whenever the initial data
\mathbf{u}_{0}
of (
SDNLW
) does not satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
3.3
, the equation does not admit a satisfying local well posedness theory, and so the definition of the flow starting from these data is somewhat arbitrary. The choice that we make in this paper is to declare that if
\mathbf{u}_{0}
does not satisfy such hypotheses, then the flow “blows up immediately”, which prevents the existence of pathological invariant measures which are concentrated on a set where the flow is not well defined. See (
3.14
) for the precise definition.

2.
Restricted asymptotic strong Feller and restricted asymptotic coupling properties

In this Section, we introduce the abstract concepts of asymptotic strong Feller and asymptotic coupling restricted to the action of group, and show how they imply a support theorem in the same vein as Proposition
1.2
. The main results of this section are Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
2.7
.

2.1.
Assumptions

Throughout this section, we will assume the following.

Assumption 1
.

Let
X
be a metric space, with distance
d_{X}
. Let
\mathscr{B}(X)
be its Borel sigma-algebra, and let

\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X):=\{f:X\to\mathbb{R}\text{ Borel}:\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|<\infty\},

equipped with the sup-norm, denoted by
\|f\|_{\infty}
.

Assumption 2
.

It is given a Markov semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)
. More precisely,

(i)

For every
t\geq 0
,
P_{t}:\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)\to\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)
is linear and bounded.

(ii)

For every
t,s\geq 0
,
P_{t+s}=P_{t}P_{s}=P_{s}P_{t}
.

(iii)

For every
t\geq 0
and for every
f
Borel with
f(x)\geq 0

\forall x\in X
, then
P_{t}f(x)\geq 0

\forall x\in X
.

(iv)

Denoting by
\mathbb{1}
the constant function
\mathbb{1}(x)=1

\forall x\in X
, for every
t\geq 0
we have that
P_{t}\mathbb{1}=\mathbb{1}
.

Assumption 3
.

We have a topological group
\mathscr{G}
with identity
e
, whose topology is induced by a left-invariant distance. More precisely, there exists a function
|\cdot|:\mathscr{G}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(i)

for every
g\in\mathscr{G}
,
|g|\geq 0
, and
|g|=0
if and only if
g=e
,

(ii)

for every
g\in\mathscr{G}
,
|g^{-1}|=|g|
,

(iii)

for every
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathscr{G}
,

|g_{1}g_{2}|\leq|g_{1}|+|g_{2}|,

and the distance
d_{\mathscr{G}}(g_{1},g_{2})
between two elements
g_{1},g_{2}
is given by

d_{\mathscr{G}}(g_{1},g_{2})=|g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}|=|g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}|.

Given
r\geq 0
, we denote by
B_{r}
the closed ball with centre
e
and radius
r
:

B_{r}:=\{g\in\mathscr{G}:|g|\leq r\}.

We remark that Assumption
3
holds if and only if the group
\mathscr{G}
is metrisable. See
[
24
, Theorem 8.3]
.

Assumption 4
.

We have a group action
\tau:\mathscr{G}\times X\to X
. More precisely, by denoting
\tau_{g}(u):=\tau(g,u)
, we have that

(i)

for every
x\in X
,
\tau_{e}(x)=x
,

(ii)

for every
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathscr{G},x\in X
,
\tau_{g_{1}g_{2}}(x)=\tau_{g_{1}}(\tau_{g_{2}}(x))
.

Assumption 5
.

For every compact set
K\subseteq X
, the map
g\mapsto\tau_{g}(x)
is equicontinuous in
e
for
x\in K
. More precisely,

(2.1)

\lim_{r\to 0}\sup_{x\in K}\sup_{g\in B_{r}}d_{X}(x,\tau_{g}(x))=0.

Moreover, for every
r\geq 0
and for every compact set
K\subset X
, we have that

(2.2)

\tau(B_{r}\times K)\in\mathscr{B}(X).

We note that the assumptions above are very general, and we do not require many of the usual properties of the space
X
and the action
\tau
. For instance, the space
X
does not need to be complete, nor separable, and the action
\tau
does not need to be continuous in the
X
-variable.
The only compatibility conditions between the topologies of
X
and
\mathscr{G}
respectively is delineated in Assumption
5
.
However, we will only work with Radon probabilities, which recovers a number of the usual properties of measures on Polish spaces.

The goal of being so general in the settings is not (only) being able to provide the most comprehensive statement possible.
In order to obtain the result in Theorem
1.1
, we will need to consider a space
X
which is merely a subset of the Banach space
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
, without any clear connection with the topology of
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}

(and actually, it is not even clear if the state space is going to be a Borel subset of
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
).
This prevents us from exploiting most of the “usual” assumptions on the space
X
. While the group action we will consider is going to be continuous with respect to the topology of
\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}
, it is convenient to allow for discontinuous actions in order to relate the results of this section with the existing theory, hence our choice of “minimal” compatibility conditions in Assumption
5
.
See Example
2.8
for more details.

2.2.
Definitions

As discussed in Section 1.2, the group action will induce a new distance on the space
X
.

Definition 2.1
.

The action
\tau
allows us to define another distance on the space
X
, that with a slight abuse of notation, we denote with
d_{\mathscr{G}}
. For
u_{1},u_{2}\in X
, we define
d_{\mathscr{G}}:X^{2}\to[0,\infty]
by

d_{\mathscr{G}}(u_{1},u_{2}):=\inf\{|g|:g\in\mathscr{G},u_{1}=\tau_{g}(u_{2})\}.

It is easy to check that this is indeed a metric (here (
2.1
) guarantees that
d_{\mathscr{G}}(u_{1},u_{2})=0
implies
u_{1}=u_{2}
). For a function
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}
, we say that
\varphi
is
\mathscr{G}
-Lipschitz
if there exists a constant
c\geq 0
such that

|\varphi(u_{1})-\varphi(u_{2})|\leq cd_{\mathscr{G}}(u_{1},u_{2}),

and we denote

\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{G}-\Lip}:=\sup_{u_{1},u_{2}\in X,u_{1}\neq u_{2}}\frac{|\varphi(u_{1})-\varphi(u_{2})|}{d_{\mathscr{G}}(u_{1},u_{2})}.

We are now ready to introduce the main new properties in this work, the asymptotic strong Feller and asymptotic coupling properties restricted to the action of
\mathscr{G}
.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that
P_{t}
has the
asymptotic strong Feller
property
restricted to the action of
\mathscr{G}
on a set
S\subseteq X
, in short
\mathrm{(rASF)_{S}}
,
if there exists a sequence of times
t_{n}\geq 0
and a sequence of positive real numbers
\delta_{n}\to 0
such that for every
u_{0}\in S
and every
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)
with
\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{G}-\Lip}<\infty
, we have

(rASF)

\big|P_{t_{n}}\varphi(u_{0})-P_{t_{n}}\varphi(\tau_{g}(u_{0}))\big|\leq 2\varepsilon_{0}(u_{0},g)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\delta_{n}C(u_{0},g)\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{G}-\Lip},

where
C(u_{0},g)<\infty
for every
u_{0}\in S,g\in\mathscr{G}
, and for every
u_{0}\in S
,

\lim_{|g|\to 0}\varepsilon_{0}(u_{0},g)=0.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that
P_{t}
has
asymptotic coupling
property
restricted to the action of
\mathscr{G}
on a set
S\subseteq X
, in short
\mathrm{(rAC)_{S}}
,
if there exists a sequence of times
t_{n}\geq 0
and a sequence of positive real numbers
\delta_{n}\to 0
, such that for every
u_{0}\in S
, there exist
r=r(u_{0})>0
so that for every
g\in B_{r(u_{0})}
and for every
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)
with
\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{G}-\Lip}<\infty
, we have

(rAC)

\big|P_{t_{n}}\varphi(u_{0})-P_{t_{n}}\varphi(\tau_{g}(u_{0}))\big|\leq 2\varepsilon_{0}(u_{0},g)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\delta_{n}C(u_{0},g)\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{G}-\Lip},

where
\varepsilon_{0}(u_{0},g)<1
and
C(u_{0},g)<\infty
for every
u_{0}\in S
and
g\in B_{r(u_{0})}
.

Remark 2.4
.

By definition, we have that the property
\mathrm{(rASF)_{S}}
implies the the
\mathrm{(rAC)_{S}}
property with an arbitrarily small constant
\varepsilon_{0}(u_{0},g)
.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
X
is
invariant
for
P_{t}
if for every
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)
and for every
t\geq 0
, we have

(2.3)

\int_{X}P_{t}\varphi(u)d\mu(u)=\int_{X}\varphi(u)d\mu(u).

2.3.
Support theorems

We are now able to state our main support theorem, which should be seen as a generalisation of Proposition
1.2
to our setting.

Theorem 2.6

(Support theorem I)
.

Suppose that
P_{t}
satisfies
\mathrm{(rAC)_{X}}
, and let
\mu,\nu
be
Radon
probability measures which are invariant for
P_{t}
and such that
\mu\perp\nu
.
Then there exist two disjoint sets
U_{0},U_{1}\subseteq X
, both open with respect to the topology induced by
d_{\mathscr{G}}
and measurable with respect to the Borel sigma-algebra on
X
, such that

\mu(U_{0})=1,\nu(U_{0})=0,\hskip 5.0pt\text{and }\mu(U_{1})=0,\nu(U_{1})=1.

Moreover,

(i)

if
\varepsilon_{0}(u_{0},g)<\frac{1}{2}
for every
u_{0}\in X,|g|\leq r(u_{0})
, then the closures of
U_{0},U_{1}
in the topology induced by
d_{\mathscr{G}}
are also disjoint. In particular, this holds if
P_{t}
satisfies
\mathrm{(rASF)_{X}}
.

(ii)

if
r(u_{0})=\infty
for every
u_{0}\in X
, we can choose
U_{0}
to be
\mathscr{G}
-invariant, i.e.
\tau(\mathscr{G}\times U_{0})=U_{0}
, and
U_{1}=(U_{0})^{c}
.

In the particular setting of this paper, it is convenient to repackage (ii) of this support theorem in the following statement, which will have a direct application in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 2.7

(Support theorem II)
.

Let
X/\mathscr{G}
be the set of the orbits for the action of
\mathscr{G}
, i.e.

X/\mathscr{G}=\{\tau(\mathscr{G}\times\{x\}):x\in X\},

and let
\pi:X\to X/\mathscr{G}
be the canonical projection. Let
\pi_{\sharp}\mathscr{B}
be the projected sigma algebra over
X/\mathscr{G}
, i.e.

E\in\pi_{\sharp}\mathscr{B}\Longleftrightarrow\pi^{-1}(E)\in\mathscr{B}(X).

Suppose that the semigroup
P_{t}
satisfies
\mathrm{(rAC)_{X}}
with
r(u_{0})=\infty
for every
u_{0}\in\mathscr{G}
. Then two Radon probability measures
\mu,\nu
which are invariant with respect to
P_{t}
satisfy

\mu\perp\nu\iff\pi_{\sharp}\mu\perp\pi_{\sharp}\nu,

and similarly

\mu\ll\nu\iff\pi_{\sharp}\mu\ll\pi_{\sharp}\nu.

Example 2.8
.

Let
X
be a Polish space, and let
P_{t}
be a Markov semigroup on
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)
such that for an appropriate sequence of times
t_{n}
, for every
x\in X
and every
\eta>0
, there exists a radius
r=r(x,\eta)>0
and a sequence
\delta_{n}=\delta_{n}(x)\to 0
such that

(2.4)

\sup_{y\in B(x,r)}|P_{t_{n}}\varphi(x)-P_{t_{n}}\varphi(y)|\leq\eta\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\delta_{n}(x)\|\varphi\|_{\Lip}.

It is well known that this condition, together with the assumption that
P_{t}
is Feller, implies the asymptotic strong Feller property for
P_{t}
(see for instance
[
26
, Proposition 3.12]
).
As we saw in the introduction, the main consequence of the asymptotic strong Feller property is that for every two invariant measures
\mu,\nu
with
\mu\perp\nu
, we have that
\supp(\mu)\cap\supp(\nu)=\emptyset
(as in Proposition
1.2
). We can derive this result as a consequence of Theorem
2.6
as well. We consider

\mathscr{G}=S^{\infty}(X):=\{f:X\to X\text{ bijective }:\sup_{x\in X}d(x,f(x))\},

with

|f|:=\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in X}d(x,f(x)),

\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}
. Notice that the set where the sequence of simple functions converges is measurable, and will contain

by construction. We will use this fact (and more generally, the consequences of this construction) liberally throughout the rest of the paper.
Moreover, by invariance of

, we have that for every
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in X

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi))]=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\varphi}(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi))].

Therefore, for

\{u_{0}\in X:\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi))]\leq s\}=\{\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}:\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\varphi}(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi))]\leq s\}\cap X,

which is the intersection of a Borel set in
\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}
(see Remark

) and
X
, so it is Borel in
X
.
Therefore, we can define

(3.36)

P_{t}\varphi(\mathbf{u}_{0}):=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi))].

In view of Lemma

, we have that
P_{t}
is a Markov semigroup on
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)
, thus the space

together with the semigroup

satisfies Assumptions
1
,
2
. In order to put ourselves in the framework of Section

\mathscr{G}=\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon},

with

|\mathbf{v}_{0}|:=\min(\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}},1),

and for
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in X
,
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathscr{G}=\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}

(3.38)

\tau_{\mathbf{v}_{0}}(\mathbf{u}_{0}):=\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{0}&\text{ if }\mathbf{u}_{0}\neq\infty\\
\infty&\text{ if }\mathbf{u}_{0}=\infty.\end{cases}

It easy to check that Assumptions
1
–
5
hold, with the possible exception of the measurability condition (

). In order to show that
\tau(B_{r}\times K)
is measurable, we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
be a closed set in
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
. Then for every
r\geq 0
,

(3.39)

C+\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq r\}

is closed in
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
is a metric space, it is enough to show that if
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in
C
,
\|\mathbf{v}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq r
, and

\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}+\mathbf{v}_{n}=x,

then
x\in C+\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq r\}.
By compactness of Sobolev embeddings, the ball
\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq r\}
is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
, so there exists a subsequence
\mathbf{v}_{n_{k}}
such that for some
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbf{v}_{n_{k}}=\mathbf{v},

and
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq r
. Therefore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n_{k}}=x-\mathbf{v}.

Since
C
is closed, we have that
x-\mathbf{v}\in C
. Therefore,

x=\overbrace{x-\mathbf{v}}^{\in C}+\hskip 5.0pt\mathbf{v}\hskip 5.0pt\in C+\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq r\}.

∎

For a compact set

, we have that

\tau(B_{r}\times K)=\begin{cases}K+\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq r\}&\text{ if }r<1,\\
K+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K+\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq n\}&\text{ if }r\geq 1.\end{cases}

Since

.

In conclusion, we have that

defined in (

.

Proposition 3.12
.

The semigroup

on

(i)

The semigroup
P_{t}
has the asymptotic strong Feller property restricted to the action of
\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
on the set

.

(ii)

on the set

, with

Proof.

Both statements follow from the estimates (

) and the respective definitions, except for the technical issue that Proposition

which are Borel-measurable in

Therefore, the proposition is proven if we show that for every

,

, there exists

\displaystyle\overline{F}(\mathbf{u}_{0})=F(\mathbf{u}_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\widetilde{F}(\mathbf{u}_{0})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}F(\mathbf{u}_{0}+\mathbf{v})+\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}-\Lip}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\geq F(\mathbf{u}_{0})+\inf_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}-|F(\mathbf{u}_{0}+\mathbf{v})-F(\mathbf{u}_{0})|+\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}-\Lip}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\geq F(\mathbf{u}_{0})+\inf_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}-\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}-\Lip}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}+\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}-\Lip}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}

Remark 3.13
.

In what follows, we are never going to use the fact that the semigroup has the
\mathrm{(rASF)_{Y}}
property, and we will only focus on the consequences of the
\mathrm{(rAC)_{X}}
property. The reason is self-evident from the support statements of Theorem
2.6
and
2.7
, that require the relevant property to hold on the whole space.

If on top of the
\mathrm{(rAC)_{X}}
property, we also had the
\mathrm{(rASF)_{X}}
property, it would actually be possible to show that the flow is globally well posed on
X\setminus\{\infty\}
, in the sense that for every
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in X
,
\mathbf{u}_{0}\neq\infty
, then for every
t\geq 0
, that
\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi)\neq\infty
a.s.
This would provide a global well posedness statement for every initial data of the form
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in Y+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
. Unfortunately, the techniques of this paper are not strong enough to provide such a result (and it is unclear if it is true to begin with). The technical reason is that, in the construction of the Girsanov shift of Lemma
3.9
, we have no way to control exponential moments of
h
when
\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\gg 1
.
Indeed, it is in principle possible that for initial data not on the set
Y
, the solution blows up with positive probability. What the Girsanov shift guarantees, that is encoded in the
\mathrm{(rAC)_{X}}
property, is that the solution starting from
\mathbf{u}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{0}
will follow (in law) the trajectory of the flow starting from
\mathbf{u}_{0}
with strictly positive probability.

Nevertheless, we decided to include the definition of
\mathrm{(rASF)_{S}}
property (and to show it on the set
Y
) in order to make it is easier to draw comparisons with the existing theory.

3.5.
Ergodicity of the
P(\Phi)_{2}
measure

We now move to proving the ergodicity of the measure
\rho
.
In order to be able to apply the theory described in Section
2
, we need to a procedure to associate to a measure
\mu
defined on
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
a measure
\iota^{\ast}\mu
defined on
X
.
We recall that from the definition (
3.34
), it is not clear if the space
X
is measurable, so we cannot simply define
\iota^{\ast}\mu
as the restriction of
\mu
to
X
. However, we have the following.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
\mu
be a finite, nonnegative measure on
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
. Then there exists a unique Radon measure
\iota^{\ast}\mu
such that for every compact set
K\subseteq X
,

(3.42)

\iota^{\ast}\mu(K)=\mu(K).

Moreover, if
\mu
is concentrated in
\widetilde{X}=\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}
and it is an invariant probability measure in the sense that (
3.16
) holds, and
\iota^{\ast}\mu(X)=1
, then

\iota^{\ast}\mu
is an invariant probability measure for
P_{t}
defined in (
3.36
).

Proof.

Since Radon measures are uniquely determined by their values on compact sets, uniqueness follows from (
3.42
), so we just need to show existence. Let

\lambda:=\sup_{K\subseteq X\text{ compact}}\mu(K)\leq\mu(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})<\infty,

and let
K_{n}\subseteq X
be an increasing sequence of compact sets so that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(K_{n})=\lambda
. For
E\subseteq X
Borel, we define

\iota^{\ast}\mu(E):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(E\cap K_{n}).

Notice that since
E
is a Borel set in
X
, there exists a Borel set
E^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
so that

E=E^{\prime}\cap X,

so
E\cap K_{n}=E^{\prime}\cap K_{n}
is a Borel set in
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
, and
\iota^{\ast}\mu(E)
is well defined. By monotone convergence,
\iota^{\ast}\mu
is
\sigma
-additive, so
\iota^{\ast}\mu
actually defines a measure. We now check that it is indeed a Radon measure. Recalling that finite measures on a compact metric space are Radon, it is enough to show tightness, or more specifically that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\iota^{\ast}\mu(X\setminus K_{n})=0.

Noticing that by definition
\iota^{\ast}\mu(K_{n})=\mu(K_{n})
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\iota^{\ast}\mu(X\setminus K_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(\iota^{\ast}\mu(X)-\iota^{\ast}\mu(K_{n})\big)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mu(K_{m})-\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(K_{n})=0,

so
\iota^{\ast}\mu
is a Radon measure. Finally, we show (
3.42
). For
K\subseteq X
compact, recalling the definition of
\lambda
, we have that

\displaystyle 0\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(K\setminus K_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(K\cup K_{n})-\mu(K_{n})\leq\lambda-\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(K_{n})=0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\iota^{\ast}\mu(K)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(K\cap K_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(K)-\mu(K\setminus K_{n})=\mu(K).

We now assume (
3.16
) and that
\iota^{\ast}\mu(X)=1
, and show that
\iota^{\ast}\mu
is invariant for
P_{t}
. We first show that
for every function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
,

(3.43)

\int f(\mathbf{u}_{0})d\iota^{\ast}\mu(\mathbf{u}_{0})=\int f(\mathbf{u}_{0})d\mu(\mathbf{u}_{0}).

Since simple functions are dense in
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
, it is enough to show that this holds for
f=\mathbb{1}_{E^{\prime}}
,
where
E^{\prime}
is a Borel subset of
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
. In this setting, (
3.43
) reduces to showing that

\iota^{\ast}\mu(E\cap X)=\mu(E).

By definition of
\iota^{\ast}\mu
, we have that
\iota^{\ast}\mu(E\cap X)=\lim_{n}\mu(E\cap K_{n})
. Moreover, since
\iota^{\ast}\mu(X)=1
,
we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(K_{n}^{c})=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu(E)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(E\cap K_{n})+\mu(E\setminus K_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(E\cap K_{n})

\displaystyle=\iota^{\ast}\mu(E\cap X),

so we have
3.43
. In order to prove invariance, fix
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(X)
, and let
\widetilde{\varphi}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
be such that
\widetilde{\varphi}|_{X}=\varphi
. By (
3.36
), (
3.43
), and (
3.16
), we have that

\displaystyle\int P_{t}\varphi(\mathbf{u}_{0})d\iota^{\ast}\mu(\mathbf{u}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\varphi}(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi))]d\iota^{\ast}\mu(\mathbf{u}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\varphi}(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi))]d\mu(\mathbf{u}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\widetilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{u}_{0})d\mu(\mathbf{u}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\varphi(\mathbf{u}_{0})d\iota^{\ast}\mu(\mathbf{u}_{0}).

∎

The next lemma, even if technically fairly simple, is one of the central pieces of the proof strategy for Theorem
1.1
. It essentially states that the support Theorem
2.7
is enough to obtain a contradiction further down the line. In our setting, the statement should be interpreted as a kind of “irreducibility under the action of
\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
” for the Gaussian measure
\rho_{0}
(and as a consequence, for the invariant measure
\rho
).

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
\pi:X\to X/\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
be the canonical projection, and consider the measure
\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{0}
, where
\rho_{0}
is the gaussian measure (
1.7
). For every measurable set
E\in\pi_{\sharp}\mathscr{B}(X)
, we have that

\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{0}(E)=0\hskip 10.0pt\text{ or }\hskip 10.0pt\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{0}(E)=1.

Proof.

We start by showing this property for the measure
\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
instead of
\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{0}
, where

\pi^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}/\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
denotes the canonical projection.
By (
3.1
), we can see
\mu
as the law of the random variable
\mathbf{U}=(U,V)
, with

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Re{\Big(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{g_{n}}{\langle n\rangle}e^{in\cdot x}\Big)},

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Re{\Big(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}{h_{n}}e^{in\cdot x}\Big)},

where
g_{n}
,
h_{n}
, are i.i.d., centred, complex valued gaussian random variables, with
\mathbb{E}g_{n}^{2}=\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{2}=0
,
\mathbb{E}|g_{n}|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|h_{n}|^{2}=1
.
Call
\mathbf{U}_{>N}=(U_{>N},V_{>N})
, with

\displaystyle U_{>N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Re{\Big(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},|n|_{\infty}>N}\frac{g_{n}}{\langle n\rangle}e^{in\cdot x}\Big)},

\displaystyle V_{>N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Re{\Big(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},|n|_{\infty}>N}{h_{n}}e^{in\cdot x}\Big)},

and let
\mathbf{U}_{\leq N}=\pi_{N}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{U}_{>N}
. Since
e^{in\cdot x}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
for every

, we have that
\mathbf{U}_{\leq N}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
.
By definition, for a set
E\in\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
, we have

\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\rho_{0}(E)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{U}\in(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)).

Moreover, since
(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)=(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
, we have the equivalence

\mathbf{U}\in(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)\iff\mathbf{U}_{>N}\in(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E).

Therefore,

\{\mathbf{U}\in(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)\}=\{\mathbf{U}_{>N}\in(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)\}.

This shows that for every

, the event
\{\mathbf{U}\in(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)\}
belongs to the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{g_{n},h_{n}:n>|n|_{\infty}\}
. By Kolmogorov’s zero-one law, this implies that
\mathbb{P}(\{\mathbf{U}\in(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)\})=0
or
\mathbb{P}(\{\mathbf{U}\in(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(E)\})=1
. Let now
F\in\pi_{\sharp}\mathscr{B}(X/\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
. By definition, this means that there exists a set
\widetilde{F}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
such that

(3.44)

\pi^{-1}(F)=\widetilde{F}\cap X,\quad\pi^{-1}(F^{c})=\widetilde{F}^{c}\cap X.

By Lemma
3.7
,
\rho_{0}(Y)=1
. Therefore, there exists a
\sigma

\widetilde{Y}\subseteq Y
such that
\rho_{0}(\widetilde{Y})=1
as well. In particular, by Lemma
3.11

\widetilde{Y}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
, and clearly
\rho_{0}(\widetilde{Y}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=1

(3.45)

\displaystyle\iota^{\ast}\mu(\pi^{-1}(F))

\displaystyle=\iota^{\ast}\mu(\widetilde{F}\cap X)=\mu(\widetilde{F})=\mu(\widetilde{F}\cap(\widetilde{Y}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})).

Morever, by (
3.44
), since
\widetilde{Y}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\subseteq Y+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\subseteq X

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}\cap(\widetilde{Y}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=(\widetilde{F}\cap X)\cap(\widetilde{Y}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=\pi^{-1}(F)\cap(\widetilde{Y}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=\pi^{-1}(F\cap\pi(\widetilde{Y})).

Notice that, since

is invariant under the action of
\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
over
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
, for every set

∎

We would like to point out that, in the space

, measures that take only values

do not necessarily correspond to measures concentrated in one point.
In the particular case of

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\})=\mathbb{P}(\{\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\})=\mathbb{P}(\{\pi(\mathbf{U}_{1})=\pi(\mathbf{U}_{2})\})

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on

on the

However, we have that
\iota^{\ast}\rho_{j}\ll\iota^{\ast}\rho\ll\iota^{\ast}\rho_{0}
for
j=1,2
,
so by Lemma
3.16
,
\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{1}=\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{0}=\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{2}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

3.6.
Conditional uniqueness of the
P(\Phi)_{2}
measure

In this final subsection, we are going to derive the uniqueness result of Theorem
1.1
as a consequence of Theorem
2.7
. The main element of the proof is the following proposition.

Proposition 3.17
.

Consider the class
W^{1}_{{:}\,p\mspace{2mu}{:}}
defined in (
1.6
), and suppose that
\mu\in W^{1}_{{:}\,p\mspace{2mu}{:}}
is an invariant measure for (
3.7
). Let
\iota^{\ast}\mu
be the measure defined in Lemma
3.14
. Finally, let
\pi:X\to X/\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
be the canonical projection, and let
\rho_{0}
be the gaussian measure (
1.7
). Then
\iota^{\ast}\mu(X)=1
, and

\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\mu=\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{0}.

In order to be able to show this, we need a couple of preparatory lemmas.

Lemma 3.18
.

For every
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
,

\Law(S(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}+\vec{\psi}[\xi](t))\rightharpoonup\rho_{0}

as
t\to\infty
, where the limit is intended as the weak limit of probability measures over
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

By the estimate
\|S(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}}\lesssim e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}},
we obtain that
\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}=0
in
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
. Therefore, it is enough to show that

\Law(\vec{\psi}[\xi](t))\rightharpoonup\rho_{0}.

Since both
\vec{\psi}[\xi](t)
and
\rho_{0}
are Gaussian measures concentrated on
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
, it is enough to check that the covariance operator of
\vec{\psi}[\xi](t)
converges to the covariance operator of
\rho_{0}
(as trace-class operators over
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
). Denoting by
C(t)
the covariance operator of
\vec{\psi}[\xi](t)
and by
C
the covariance operator of
\rho_{0}
,
and writing
[\nabla]:=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}-\Delta}
, we have that

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\Delta)^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle C(t)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{t}e^{-t^{\prime}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sin(t^{\prime}[\nabla])^{2}}{[\nabla]^{2}}&\frac{\sin(t^{\prime}[\nabla])}{[\nabla]}\left(\cos(t^{\prime}[\nabla])-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sin(t^{\prime}[\nabla])}{[\nabla]}\right)\\
\frac{\sin(t^{\prime}[\nabla])}{[\nabla]}\left(\cos(t^{\prime}[\nabla])-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sin(t^{\prime}[\nabla])}{[\nabla]}\right)&\left(\cos(t^{\prime}[\nabla])-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sin(t^{\prime}[\nabla])}{[\nabla]}\right)^{2}\end{pmatrix}dt^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-t})\begin{pmatrix}(1-\Delta)^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{-2[\nabla]\sin(2t[\nabla])+\cos(2t[\nabla])-1}{(3-4\Delta)(1-\Delta)}&\frac{\sin(t[\nabla])^{2}}{[\nabla]^{2}}\\
\frac{\sin(t[\nabla])^{2}}{[\nabla]^{2}}&\frac{2[\nabla]\sin(2t[\nabla])+\cos(2t[\nabla])-1}{3-4\Delta}\end{pmatrix}.

From these formulas, it is easy to check that
C(t)\to C
as
t\to\infty
in trace class over
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
.
∎

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be two probability measures on
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
such that
\iota^{\ast}\mu_{1}(X)=\iota^{\ast}\mu_{2}(X)=1
. Let
\pi^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}/\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
be the canonical projection. Suppose moreover that
\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\mu_{1}=\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\mu_{2}
. Then

\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\mu_{1}=\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\mu_{2}.

Proof.

Since
\iota^{\ast}\mu_{1}(X)=\iota^{\ast}\mu_{2}(X)=1
, by definition of the measures
\iota^{\ast}\mu_{j}
, there exists a
\sigma
-compact set
\widetilde{K}\subseteq X
such that

\mu_{1}(\widetilde{K})=\mu_{2}(\widetilde{K})=1.

By Lemma
3.11
,
\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon})
, and clearly
\mu_{j}(\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=1
as well. Let
E
be a set in
\pi_{\sharp}\mathscr{B}(X/\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})
. Then, by definition of the
\sigma
-algebra
\pi_{\sharp}\mathscr{B}(X/\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})
, there exists a set
\widetilde{E}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\cup\{\infty\})
such that

(3.46)

\pi^{-1}(E)=\widetilde{E}\cap X.

Therefore, by (
3.43
),

\iota^{\ast}\mu_{j}\pi^{-1}(E)=\iota^{\ast}\mu_{j}(\widetilde{E}\cap X)=\mu_{j}(\widetilde{E})=\mu_{j}(\widetilde{E}\cap(\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})).

Moreover, by (
3.46
),

\widetilde{E}\cap(\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=(\widetilde{E}\cap X)\cap(\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=\pi^{-1}(E)\cap(\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=\pi^{-1}(E\cap\pi(\widetilde{K})).

Therefore,

\iota^{\ast}\mu_{j}\pi^{-1}(E)=\mu_{j}(\widetilde{E}\cap(\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}))=\mu_{j}(\pi^{-1}(E\cap\pi(\widetilde{K})))=\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\mu_{j}(E\cap\pi^{\prime}(\widetilde{K})),

and by hypothesis, the last term in the equality does not depend on
j
. Therefore, we obtain that

\iota^{\ast}\mu_{1}\pi^{-1}(E)=\iota^{\ast}\mu_{2}\pi^{-1}(E).

∎

Proof of Proposition
3.17
.

Recall the decomposition

\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\xi)=S(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}+\vec{\psi}[\xi](t)+\mathbf{v}(t),

where
\mathbf{v}(t)
solves the equation (
3.12
). Let
\mathbf{U}_{0}
be a
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
-valued random variable with
\Law(\mathbf{U}_{0})=\mu
, and for
t\geq 0
, define

(3.47)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(t)

\displaystyle:=S(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}+\vec{\psi}[\xi](t),

(3.48)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{v}(t).

By invariance of
\mu
, we have that
\Law(\mathbf{X}(t)+\mathbf{V}(t))=\mu
for every
t\geq 0
. We want to show that (up to subsequences),
\Law(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{V}(t))
has a weak limit as a probability measure over
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\times\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
.
By Prokhorov’s theorem, we just need to show tightness of the couple
(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{V}(t))
. By Lemma
3.18
, the family
{\Law(\mathbf{X}(n))}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight, so there exists a family of compact sets
K_{\delta}\subset\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
such that

(3.49)

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}(n)\not\in K_{\delta})\leq\delta.

We now move to the tightness estimate for
\mathbf{V}
. From (
3.12
), we have that
\mathbf{V}
solves the equation

\mathbf{V}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}S(t-t^{\prime})\begin{pmatrix}0\\
{:}\,p(X+V(t^{\prime}))\mspace{2mu}{:}\end{pmatrix}dt^{\prime},

where
X,V
are respectively the first component of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{V}
.
From this and Hölder, we obtain that

\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{t-t^{\prime}}{2}}\|{:}\,p(X+V(t^{\prime}))\mspace{2mu}{:}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}}dt^{\prime}

Recalling that
\mu\in W^{1}_{{:}\,p\mspace{2mu}{:}}
, and that
\mu
is invariant, we obtain that

(3.50)

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{V}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq C(\mu),

where

C(\mu)\sim\int\|{:}\,p(u)\mspace{2mu}{:}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}}d\mu(u).

Therefore, from Markov’s inequality, we obtain that

(3.51)

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{V}(n)\not\in\{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq\delta^{-1}C(\mu)\}\leq\delta.

Putting (
3.49
) and (
3.51
) together, we obtain

(3.52)

\mathbb{P}\big(\big\{(\mathbf{X}(n),\mathbf{V}(n))\not\in K_{\delta/2}\times\{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq 2\delta^{-1}C(\mu)\}\big\}\big)\leq\delta.

Since the embedding
\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
is compact, this shows tightness for
\Law(\mathbf{X}(n),\mathbf{V}(n))
. Therefore, up to subsequences, we have that
\Law(\mathbf{X}(n),\mathbf{V}(n))\rightharpoonup\nu
as
n\to\infty
, where
\nu
is a Borel measure on
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\times\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
. Moreover, by (
3.52
), we have that

(3.53)

\nu(\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\times\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=1.

We define the map

\oplus(x,y):=x+y,

by invariance of
\mu
, we have that

\displaystyle\oplus_{\sharp}\Law(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{Y}(t))=\Law(\mathbf{X}(t)+\mathbf{Y}(t))=\mu.

Since
\oplus:\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\times\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
is continuous, this property passes to limit, and we obtain that

(3.54)

\oplus_{\sharp}\nu=\mu.

Let
\pi^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}/\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
be the canonical projection. We observe that on the set
\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}\times\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
, we have that

\pi^{\prime}\circ\oplus(x,y)=\pi(x).

Therefore, by (
3.53
), (
3.54
), and Lemma
3.18
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\mu

\displaystyle=\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\oplus_{\sharp}\nu

\displaystyle=\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\lim_{t\to\infty}\Law(\mathbf{X}(t))

\displaystyle=\pi^{\prime}_{\sharp}\rho_{0}.

In view of Lemma
3.19
, in order to show the analogous statement for
\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\mu
and
\pi_{\sharp}\iota^{\ast}\rho_{0}
, we just need to show that
\iota^{\ast}\mu(X)=1
. Let
\widetilde{K}
be a
\sigma
-compact set such that
\widetilde{K}\subseteq Y
and
\rho_{0}(K)=1
, where
Y
is defined in (
3.17
). The existence of such a set follows from Lemma
3.7
. In view of (
3.54
), (
3.53
) and Lemma
3.18
, we have that

\displaystyle\mu(\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{1}_{\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})d\nu(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle\geq\int\mathbb{1}_{\widetilde{K}}(\mathbf{u})\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{v})d\nu(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=1,

hence
\mu(\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon})=1.
Moreover, recalling that the embedding
\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{H}^{-\varepsilon}
is compact, we have that
\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}
is a
\sigma
-compact set as well, and
\widetilde{K}+\mathcal{H}^{1-\varepsilon}\subseteq X
by definition of
X
. Therefore,
\iota^{\ast}\mu(X)=1
.
∎

We are finally ready to show the uniqueness statement of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of conditional uniqueness in Theorem
1.1
.

Our goal is to apply Theorem

. In order to do so, suppose by contradiction that the
P(\Phi)_{2}

\rho
is not unique in the class

\mu

and

\iota^{\ast}\mu(X)=1
. Therefore, by (

), we obtain that
\iota^{\ast}\mu\perp\iota^{\ast}\rho

∎
