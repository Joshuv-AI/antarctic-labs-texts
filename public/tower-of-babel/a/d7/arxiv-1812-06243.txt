Algorithmic Theory of ODEs and Sampling from Well-conditioned Logconcave Densities
Yin Tat Lee, Zhao Song, Santosh S. Vempala

Abstract
Sampling logconcave functions arising in statistics and machine learning has been a subject of intensive study. Recent developments include analyses for Langevin dynamics and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC). While both approaches have dimension-independent bounds for the underlying continuous processes under sufficiently strong smoothness conditions, the resulting discrete algorithms have complexity and number of function evaluations growing with the dimension. Motivated by this problem, in this paper, we give a general algorithm for solving multivariate ordinary differential equations whose solution is close to the span of a known basis of functions (e.g., polynomials or piecewise polynomials). The resulting algorithm has polylogarithmic depth and essentially tight runtime — it is nearly linear in the size of the representation of the solution. We apply this to the sampling problem to obtain a nearly linear implementation of HMC for a broad class of smooth, strongly logconcave densities, with the number of iterations (parallel depth) and gradient evaluations being polylogarithmic in the dimension (rather than polynomial as in previous work). This class includes the widely-used loss function for logistic regression with incoherent weight matrices and has been subject of much study recently. We also give a faster algorithm with polylogarithmic depth for the more general and standard class of strongly convex functions with Lipschitz gradient. These results are based on (1) an improved contraction bound for the exact HMC process and (2) logarithmic bounds on the degree of polynomials that approximate solutions of the differential equations arising in implementing HMC.

1.3
Techniques

In this paper we give bounds on the Collocation Method for solving ODEs. To ensure the algorithm is applicable to the ODE’s that arise in the sampling application, we need to show that the solution of the ODE is close to a low-rank basis. Given only bounds on the Hessian of a function, we do this by approximating the solution of the ODE with a piecewise degree two polynomial. For smooth functions, we can use low-degree polynomials.

The proofs of the degree bounds go via the Cauchy-Kowalevsky method, by showing bounds on all derivatives at the initial point. To do this for multivariate ODE’s, we use the method of majorants, and reduce the problem to bounding the radius of convergence of one-variable ODEs.

The improved convergence guarantees for exact HMC are also based on better analysis of the underlying ODE, showing that a larger step size than previously known is possible.

For many optimization and sampling methods, there are corresponding customized versions that deal with decomposable functions by sampling terms of the functions. These algorithms usually take nearly linear time with the number of iterations being polynomial in the dimension. Often, an improvement in the general case would lead to an improvement in the decomposable case. To limit the length of this paper, we focus only on results with polylogarithmic depth. Therefore, in Table

2.1
Picard-Lindelöf theorem

In general, we can rewritten the first-order ODE as an integral equation

x(t)=v+\int^{t}_{0}F(x(s),s)\mathrm{d}s\quad\text{for all }0\leq t\leq T.

(1)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \int_{0}^{h}\|F(\T^{\circ(j-1)}x(s),s)-F(\T^{\circ(j-1)}y(s),s)\|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\penalty\ L\int_{0}^{h}\|\T^{\circ(j-1)}x(s)-\T^{\circ(j-1)}y(s)\|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\text{\penalty\ by\penalty\ }f\text{\penalty\ is\penalty\ $L$\penalty\ Lipschitz}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ L\int_{0}^{h}\frac{L^{j-1}s^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\|x-y\|\mathrm{d}s

by the induction statement

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{L^{j}}{j!}h^{j}\|x-y\|.

This completes the induction.

2.2
Intuition of collocation method

To make the Picard-Lindelöf theorem algorithmic,
we need to discuss how to represent a function in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
One standard way is to use a polynomial
p_{i}(t)
in
t
for each
coordinate
i\in[d]
. In this section, we assume that there is
a basis
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{D}\subset\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
such
that for all
i\in[d]
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}
is approximated by some
linear combination of
\varphi_{j}(t)
.

For example, if
\varphi_{j}(t)=t^{j-1}
for
j\in[d]
, then our assumption is
simply saying
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}
is approximated by a degree
D-1

polynomial. Other possible basis are piecewise-polynomial and Fourier
series. By Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, we can always pick nodes
point
\{c_{i}\}_{j=1}^{D}
such that

\varphi_{j}(c_{i})=\delta_{i,j}\quad\text{for }i,j\in[D].

The benefit of such basis is that for any
f\in\text{span}(\varphi_{j})
,
we have that
f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}f(c_{j})\varphi_{j}(t).

For polynomials, the resulting basis are the Lagrange polynomials

\varphi_{j}(t)=\prod_{i\in[D]\backslash\{j\}}\frac{t-c_{i}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}\quad\text{for }j\in[D].

The only assumption we make on the basis is that its integral is bounded.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a
D
dimensional subspace
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R})

and node points
\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{D}\subset[0,T]
. For any
\gamma_{\varphi}\geq 1
, we call a basis

\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{D}\subset\mathcal{V}
is
\gamma_{\varphi}

bounded if
\varphi_{j}(c_{i})=\delta_{i,j}
and
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{D}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{j}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\gamma_{\varphi}T\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T].

Note that if the constant function
1\in\mathcal{V}
, then we have

1=\sum_{j=1}^{D}1(c_{j})\varphi_{j}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\varphi_{j}(t).

Hence, we have

T=\int^{T}_{0}1\mathrm{d}s\leq\sum_{j=1}^{D}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\varphi_{j}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\gamma_{\varphi}T.

Therefore,
\gamma_{\varphi}\geq 1
for most of the interesting basis. This is the reason why we simply put it as an assumption to shorten some formulas.

In the section
2.5
, we prove that for the space of low degree polynomial
and piece-wise low degree polynomial, there is a basis on the Chebyshev
nodes that is
O(1)
bounded.

Assuming that
\frac{dx}{dt}
can be approximated by some element
in
\mathcal{V}
, we have that

\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)\sim\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(c_{j})\varphi_{j}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}F(x(c_{j}),c_{j})\varphi_{j}(t).

Integrating both sides, we have

x(t)\approx v+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{D}F(x(c_{j}),c_{j})\varphi_{j}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(2)

This inspires us to consider the following operator from
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})

to
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\mathcal{T}_{\varphi}(x)(t)_{i}=v_{i}+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{D}F(x(c_{j}),c_{j})_{i}\varphi_{j}(s)\mathrm{d}s\quad\text{for }i\in[d].

(3)

Equation (
2
) can be written as
x\approx\mathcal{T}_{\varphi}(x).

To find
x
to satisfies this, naturally, one can apply the fix point
iteration and this is called the collocation method.

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{T}F(x^{*}(0),s)\mathrm{d}s\right\|+\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\left(F(x^{*}(s),s)-F(x^{*}(0),s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{T}F(x^{*}(0),s)\mathrm{d}s\right\|+L\int_{0}^{t}\|x^{*}(s)-x^{*}(0)\|\mathrm{d}s.

Solving this integral inequality (see Lemma
A.6
), we have that

\|x^{*}(t)-x^{*}(0)\|\leq e^{Lt}\left\|\int_{0}^{T}F(x^{*}(0),s)\mathrm{d}s\right\|.

Now, we use
LT\leq\frac{1}{2}
and get

\|x^{*}(t)-x^{*}(0)\|\leq 2\left\|\int_{0}^{T}F(x^{*}(0),s)\mathrm{d}s\right\|.

Picking
N=\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(\frac{T}{\epsilon}\max_{s\in[0,T]}\left\|F(x^{*}(0),s)\right\|\right)\right\rceil
, (
10
) shows that the error is less than
20\gamma_{\varphi}\epsilon
.

∎

2.5
A basis for piece-wise polynomials

In this section, we discuss how to construct a bounded basis for
low-degree piece-wise polynomials. We are given
n
intervals
\{[I_{i-1},I_{i}]\}_{i=1}^{n}

where
I_{0}=0
and
I_{n}=T
. In the
i^{th}
interval
[I_{i-1},I_{i}]
,
we represent the function by a degree
D_{i}
polynomial. Formally,
we define the function subspace by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{V}_{i}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{V}_{i}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\left\{\left(\sum_{j=0}^{D_{i}}\alpha_{j}t^{j}\right)\cdot 1_{[I_{i-1},I_{i}]}:\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(11)

The following Lemma shows we can construct the basis for
\mathcal{V}

by concatenating the basis for
\mathcal{V}_{i}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

For
i\in[n]
, we are given a
\gamma_{i}

bounded basis
\{\varphi_{j,i}\}_{j=0}^{D_{i}}
for the subspace

\mathcal{V}_{i}\subset\mathcal{C}([I_{i-1},I_{i}],\mathbb{R})
on nodes
point
\{c_{j,i}\}_{j=0}^{D_{i}}
. Then,
\{\varphi_{j,i}\}_{i,j}

is a
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(I_{i}-I_{i-1})
bounded basis for the subspace

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{V}_{i}\subset\mathcal{C}([I_{0},I_{n}],\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{D_{i}}\left|\int_{I_{0}}^{t}\varphi_{i,j}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{D_{i}}\left|\int_{I_{i-1}}^{t}\varphi_{i,j}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right|1_{t\geq I_{i-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(I_{i}-I_{i-1})

where we used that
\varphi_{i,j}
is supported on
[I_{i-1},I_{i}]

in the first inequality.
∎

Next, we note that the boundedness for basis is shift and scale invariant.
Hence, we will focus on obtaining a basis for
(t-1)
-degree polynomial
on
[-1,1]
for notation convenience.

For
[-1,1]
, we choose the node points
c_{j}=\cos(\frac{2j-1}{2t}\pi)

and the basis are

\varphi_{j}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{1-c_{j}^{2}}\cos(t\cos^{-1}x)}{t(x-c_{j})}.

It is easy to see that
\varphi_{j}(c_{i})=\delta_{i,j}
. To bound
the integral, Lemma 91 in
[
LV17
]
shows that

\left|\int_{-1}^{y}\varphi_{j}(x)dx\right|\leq\frac{2000}{t}\text{ for all }y\in[-1,1].

Summing it over
t
basis functions, we have that
\gamma_{\varphi}\leq 2000
.
Together with Lemma
2.9
, we have the following
result:

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a subspace
of piecewise polynomials on
[0,T]
with fixed nodes. Then, there is a
2000
bounded
basis
\{\varphi\}
for
\mathcal{V}
. Furthermore, for any vector
v
, it takes
O(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+D_{i})^{2})
time to compute
v^{\top}A_{\varphi}
where
D_{i}
is the maximum degree of the
i
-th piece.

Alternatively, one can find
u
such that
\|u-v^{\top}A_{\varphi}\|\leq\epsilon T\|v\|_{\infty}
in time

O\left(\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{V})\log(\frac{\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{V})}{\epsilon})\right).

Proof.

The bound follows from previous discussion. For the computation cost, note that

(v^{\top}A_{\varphi})_{(i,j)}=\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{c_{(i,j)}}v_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}\varphi_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

where
(i,j)
is the
j
-th node at the
i
-th piece.
For any
i\neq i^{\prime}
, the support of
\varphi_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}(s)
is either
disjoint from
[0,c_{(i,j)}]
(if
i^{\prime}>i
) or included in
[0,c_{(i,j)}]

(if
i^{\prime}<i
). Hence, we have that

\int_{0}^{c_{(i,j)}}\varphi_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}(s)\mathrm{d}s=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i^{\prime}>i\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}(s)\mathrm{d}s&\text{if }i^{\prime}<i\end{cases}.

Therefore, we have

(v^{\top}A_{\varphi})_{(i,j)}=\sum_{i^{\prime}<i,j^{\prime}}v_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}\cdot\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}(s)\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{j^{\prime}}v_{(i,j^{\prime})}\cdot\int_{0}^{c_{(i,j)}}\varphi_{(i,j^{\prime})}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Note that
\int_{0}^{c_{(i,j)}}\varphi_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}(s)\mathrm{d}s
can precomputed.
Since there are
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+D_{i})
many pairs of
(i,j)
, the first
term can be computed in
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+D_{i})
time. Since there are

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+D_{i})^{2}
many pairs of
(i,j,j^{\prime})
, the second term
can be computed in
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+D_{i})^{2}
time.

Theorem
2.12
gives another way to compute the integration and its runtime is

O\left(\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{V})\log(\frac{\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{V})}{\epsilon})\right).

∎

Improved Contraction Bound for HMC

In this section, we give an improved contraction bound for HMC (Algorithm
1
). Each iteration of Algorithm
1
solve the HMC dynamics approximately.
In the later sections, we will discuss how to solve this ODE.

To give a contraction bound for the noisy HMC, we first analyze the
contraction of the ideal HMC. We reduce the problem of bounding the
contraction rate of the ideal HMC to the following lemma involving a matrix ODE.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given a symmetric matrix
H(t)
such
that
0\prec m_{2}\cdot I\preceq H(t)\preceq M_{2}\cdot I
for all
t\geq 0
.
Consider the ODE

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-H(t)\cdot u(t),

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=0.

Let
\alpha(t)=\frac{1}{\|u(0)\|_{2}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\|H(s)u(0)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s
.
For any
0\leq T\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{M_{2}}}
such that
\alpha(T)\leq\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}
,
we have that

\|u(T)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(1-\max\left(\frac{1}{4}m_{2}T^{2},\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}\cdot\alpha(T)\right)\right)\cdot\|u(0)\|_{2}^{2}.

Using this lemma, we prove both parts of the main contraction bound, Lemma
1.8
.

Proof of Lemma
1.8
.

Let error function
e(t)=y(t)-x(t)
. The definition of HMC shows that

e^{\prime\prime}(t)=-(\nabla f(y(t))-\nabla f(x(t)))=-H(t)\cdot e(t)

where
H(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(x(t)+s(y(t)-x(t)))\mathrm{d}s
. By the
strong convexity and the Lipschitz gradient of
f
, we have that

m_{2}\cdot I\preceq H(t)\preceq M_{2}\cdot I.

Hence, we can apply Lemma
3.1
.

To get the first bound, we bound the
\alpha(t)
defined in Lemma

3.1
as follows

\alpha(t)=\frac{1}{\|e(0)\|_{2}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\|H(s)e(0)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq M_{2}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\mathrm{d}s=M_{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}.

Therefore, for
0\leq t\leq\frac{m_{2}^{1/4}}{2M_{2}^{3/4}}
, we have that

\alpha(t)\leq\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}
and hence Lemma
3.1

gives the first bound.

To get the second bound, we note that
\alpha(t)
is increasing and
hence there is
t
such that
\alpha(t)=\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}
.
Using such
t
in Lemma
3.1
gives the second
bound.
∎

Now, we prove the main technical lemma of this section, a contraction
estimate for matrix ODE. We note that not all matrix ODEs come from
some HMC and hence it might be possible to get a better bound by directly
analyzing the HMC.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Let
e_{1}
denote the basis vector that it is
1
in the first coordinate and
0
everywhere else.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
\|u(0)\|_{2}=1
and
u(0)=e_{1}
.

The proof involves first getting a crude bound on
\|u(t)\|_{2}
.
Then, we boast the bound by splitting the movement of
u(t)
into
one parallel to
e_{1}
and one orthogonal to
e_{1}
.

Crude bound on
\|u(t)\|_{2}
:

Integrating both sides of
u^{\prime\prime}(t)=-H(t)\cdot u(t)
twices and using
u^{\prime}(0)=0
gives

u(t)=u(0)-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)H(s)u(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(12)

We take the norm on both sides and use
0\preceq H(s)\preceq M_{2}\cdot I

to get

\|u(t)\|_{2}\leq 1+M_{2}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\|u(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Applying Lemma
A.5
to this equation and using
t\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{M_{2}}}

gives

\|u(t)\|_{2}\leq\cosh(\sqrt{M_{2}}t)\leq\frac{6}{5}

Putting it back to (
12
) gives

\|u(t)-e_{1}\|_{2}\leq\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\|H(s)\cdot u(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot M_{2}\cdot\frac{6}{5}\mathrm{d}s=\frac{6}{10}M_{2}t^{2}.

In particular, for any
0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{M_{2}}}
, we have
that

\frac{5}{6}\leq u_{1}(t)\leq\frac{7}{6}.

(13)

Improved bound on
\|u(t)\|_{2}
:

Let
P_{1}
be the orthogonal projection to the first coordinate
and
P_{-1}=I-P_{1}
. We write
u(t)=u_{1}(t)+u_{-1}(t)
with
u_{1}(t)=P_{1}u(t)

and
u_{-1}(t)=P_{-1}u(t)
, namely,
u_{1}(t)
is parallel to
e_{1}

and
u_{-1}(t)
is orthogonal to
e_{1}
.

Fix any
0\leq t\leq T
. Let
\beta(t)=e_{1}^{\top}u(t)
. By the
definition of
u
, we have

u^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\beta(t)\cdot H(t)e_{1}-H(t)u_{-1}(t).

(14)

Integrating both sides twice and using
u_{-1}(0)=0
, we have

\displaystyle u_{-1}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)P_{-1}u^{\prime\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\beta(s)\cdot P_{-1}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)P_{-1}H(s)u_{-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Taking norm on both sides and using that
0\preceq H(t)\preceq M_{2}\cdot I

and
\frac{5}{6}\leq\beta(s)\leq\frac{7}{6}
, we have that

\displaystyle\|u_{-1}(t)\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\beta(s)\cdot\|H(s)e_{1}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s+M_{2}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\|u_{-1}(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{7}{6}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\|H(s)e_{1}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s+M_{2}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\|u_{-1}(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{7}{6}\alpha(T)+M_{2}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\|u_{-1}(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

(15)

where
\alpha(T)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{T}(T-s)\cdot\|H(s)e_{1}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Solving this integral inequality (Lemma
A.5
), we get

\|u_{-1}(t)\|_{2}\leq\frac{7}{6}\alpha(T)\cdot\cosh(\sqrt{M_{2}}t)\leq\frac{7}{6}\alpha(T)\cdot\cosh(1/2)\leq\frac{4}{3}\alpha(T)

(16)

where the second step follows from
t\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{M_{2}}}
, and the last step follows from
\cosh(1/2)\leq\frac{8}{7}
.

Next, we look at the first coordinate of (
14
) and get

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta(t)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}-e_{1}^{\top}H(t)u_{-1}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{5}{6}e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}+\|H(t)e_{1}\|_{2}\cdot\|u_{-1}(t)\|_{2}.

(17)

To bound the last term, we note that

\displaystyle\|H(t)e_{1}\|_{2}=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sqrt{e_{1}^{\top}H^{2}(t)e_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sqrt{M_{2}\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}}

\displaystyle\text{\penalty\ by\penalty\ }H^{2}(t)\preceq M_{2}\cdot H(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sqrt{\frac{M_{2}}{m_{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}}}e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sqrt{\frac{M_{2}}{m_{2}}}\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}

\displaystyle\text{\penalty\ by\penalty\ }m_{2}\leq e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}.

(18)

Using this into (
17
) and
\alpha(T)\leq\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}
, we have

\beta^{\prime\prime}(t)\leq-e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{5}{6}-\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{\frac{M_{2}}{m_{2}}}\alpha(T)\right)\leq-\frac{2}{3}e_{1}^{\top}H(t)e_{1}.

Hence, we have that

\beta(t)\leq 1-\frac{2}{3}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s.

(19)

Using (
19

\beta(t)\geq\frac{5}{6}
and (
16
)
gives

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}(t)+\|u_{-1}(t)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s+\left(\frac{4}{3}\int_{0}^{T}(T-s)\cdot\|H(s)e_{1}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s+2\sqrt{\frac{M_{2}}{m_{2}}}\int_{0}^{T}(T-s)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s\cdot\alpha(T)

\displaystyle=1-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s\left(1-2\sqrt{\frac{M_{2}}{m_{2}}}\alpha(T)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s

where we used (
3
) at the second inequality and
\alpha(T)\leq\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}

at the end.

Finally, we bound the last term in two way. One way simply uses

and get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s\geq\frac{m_{2}}{2}t^{2}

which implies

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1-\frac{m_{2}}{4}t^{2}.

For the other way, we apply (
3

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot e_{1}^{\top}H(s)e_{1}\mathrm{d}s\geq\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\cdot\|H(t)e_{1}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}\mathrm{d}s=\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}\cdot\alpha(T).

where the last step follows by the definition of

. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}}{M_{2}}}\alpha(T).

∎

Finally, we analyze the contraction of the noisy HMC.

Theorem 3.2

(Contraction of noisy HMC)
.

Suppose
f
is
m_{2}
strongly convexity
with
M_{2}
Lipschitz gradient. For any step-size
h\leq\frac{m_{2}^{1/4}}{2M_{2}^{3/4}}
, let
X\sim\textsc{HMC}(x^{(0)},f,\epsilon,h)
and
Y\sim e^{-f}
. Then, we have that

W_{2}(X,Y)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{m_{2}}}.

In addition, the number of iterations is

\displaystyle N=O\left(\frac{1}{m_{2}h^{2}}\right)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x^{(0)})\|_{2}^{2}/m_{2}+d}{\epsilon}\right).

Proof.

To prove the
W_{2}
distance, we let
x^{(k)}
be the iterates
of the algorithm
HMC
. Let
y^{(k)}
be the
k
-th step of the
ideal HMC starting from a random point
y^{(0)}\sim e^{-f}
with
the random initial direction identical to the algorithm
HMC
.
Let
x^{*(k)}
be the 1 step ideal HMC starting from
x^{(k-1)}
with the same initial direction as
y^{(k)}
. Lemma
1.8

shows that

\|x^{*(k)}-y^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{m_{2}h^{2}}{4}\right)\|x^{(k-1)}-y^{(k-1)}\|_{2}^{2}.

Let
\theta=\frac{m_{2}h^{2}}{8}
, then
\theta\leq\frac{1/(4\kappa^{3/4})}{8}\leq 1/32
. By the assumption of the
noise, we have
\|x^{(k)}-x^{*(k)}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon\theta}{2\sqrt{m_{2}}}
. Hence,
we have

\displaystyle\|x^{(k)}-y^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \|(x^{*(k)}-y^{(k)})+(x^{(k)}-x^{*(k)})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \left(1+\theta\right)\|x^{*(k)}-y^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}+(1+1/\theta)\|x^{(k)}-x^{*(k)}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \left(1+\theta\right)(1-2\theta)\|x^{(k-1)}-y^{(k-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+(1+1/\theta)\|x^{(k)}-x^{*(k)}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \left(1-\theta\right)\|x^{(k-1)}-y^{(k-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+(1+1/\theta)\|x^{(k)}-x^{*(k)}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \left(1-\theta\right)\|x^{(k-1)}-y^{(k-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+(2/\theta)\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{2}\theta^{2}}{4m_{2}}.

where the second step follows by
(a+b)^{2}\leq(1+\theta)a^{2}+(1+1/\theta)b^{2}
, the third step follows by
\|x^{*(k)}-y^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1-2\theta)\|x^{(k-1)}-y^{(k-1)}\|_{2}^{2}

, the fifth step follow by

and

.

Applying this bound iteratively gives

\|x^{(k)}-y^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(1-\theta\right)^{k}\|x^{(0)}-y^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2m_{2}}.

(20)

Let
x^{(\min)}
be the minimum of
f
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\|x^{(0)}-y^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|x^{(0)}-x^{(\min)}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|y^{(0)}-x^{(\min)}\|_{2}^{2}.

(21)

For the first term, the strong convexity of
f
shows that

\|x^{(0)}-x^{(\min)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\|\nabla f(x^{(0)})\|_{2}^{2}.

(22)

For the second term, Theorem 1 in
[
DM16
]
shows that

\E\left[\|y^{(0)}-x^{(\min)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{d}{m_{2}}.

(23)

Combining (

)

\E\left[\|x^{(k)}-y^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\left(1-\theta\right)^{k}\left(\frac{2\|\nabla f(x^{(0)})\|_{2}^{2}}{m_{2}^{2}}+\frac{2d}{m_{2}}\right)+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2m_{2}}.

Picking

\displaystyle k=\frac{1}{\theta}\cdot\log\left(\frac{\frac{2\|\nabla f(x^{(0)})\|_{2}^{2}}{m_{2}^{2}}+\frac{2d}{m_{2}}}{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2m_{2}}}\right)=\frac{1}{\theta}\cdot\log\left(\frac{4}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x^{(0)})\|_{2}^{2}}{m_{2}}+d\right)\right),

we have that

\E\left[\|x^{(k)}-y^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{m_{2}}.

This proves that
W_{2}(X,Y)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{m_{2}}}
.
∎

4
Strongly Convex functions with Lipschitz Gradient

In this section, we give a faster sampling algorithm for strongly convex functions
with Lipschitz gradient. The purpose of this section is to illustrate
that our contraction bound and ODE theorem are useful even for functions that are not infinitely differentiable. We believe that our bound can
be beaten by algorithms designed for this specific setting.

4.1
Bounding the ODE solution

First, we prove that the HMC dynamic for these functions can be well
approximated by piece-wise degree-2 polynomials. Note that this only requires that the Hessian has bounded eigenvalues.

Lemma 4.1

(Smoothness implies the existence of degree-2 polynomial approximation)
.

Let
f
be a twice-differentiable function such that
-M_{2}\cdot I\preceq\nabla^{2}f(x)\preceq M_{2}\cdot I

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
0\leq h\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{M_{2}}}
.
Consider the HMC dynamic

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}x}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla f(x(t))\text{ for }0\leq t\leq h,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)

\displaystyle=v_{1},

\displaystyle x(0)

\displaystyle=v_{0}.

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}(t)-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}x}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}(t)\right\|_{2}

Lemma 4.2

(Smoothness implies expected gradient is upper bounded)
.

Let
f
be a function such that

-M_{2}\cdot I\preceq\nabla^{2}f(x)\preceq M_{2}\cdot I

for all

be the starting point
of the
k^{th}

\overline{\epsilon}
is the error in solving the HMC defined in Algorithm
1
.

Proof.

Consider one step of the HMC dynamic. Note that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f(x(t))=\nabla f(x(t))^{\top}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}f(x(t))=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}^{\top}\nabla^{2}f(x(t))\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}+\nabla f(x(t))^{\top}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}x}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}^{\top}\nabla^{2}f(x(t))\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}-\|\nabla f(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle\text{\penalty\ by\penalty\ }\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}x}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}=-\nabla f(x(t))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ M_{2}\cdot\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\nabla f(x(t))\|^{2}.

\displaystyle\text{\penalty\ by\penalty\ }\nabla^{2}f(x(t))\preceq M_{2}\cdot I

(25)

In Lemma
4.1
, we proved that
\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\|_{2}\leq 2\left(\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\|_{2}+\|\nabla f(x(0))\|_{2}\cdot h\right)

for all
0\leq t\leq h
. Using this, we have that

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x(t))-\nabla f(x(0))\|\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ M_{2}\|x(t)-x(0)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ M_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ 2M_{2}h\cdot\left(\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\|_{2}+\|\nabla f(x(0))\|_{2}\cdot h\right)

Using our choice of

Putting these estimates into (

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}f(x(t))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ 2M_{2}\left(\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\|^{2}+\|\nabla f(x(0))\|^{2}h^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla f(x(0))\|^{2}+2M_{2}h\cdot\|\nabla f(x(0))\|\cdot\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ 2M_{2}\left(\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\|^{2}+\|\nabla f(x(0))\|^{2}h^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla f(x(0))\|^{2}+2\cdot(\frac{1}{8}\|\nabla f(x(0))\|)^{2}+2\cdot(16M_{2}h\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\|)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\penalty\ (2M_{2}+512M_{2}^{2}h^{2})\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\|^{2}-(\frac{1}{4}-2M_{2}h^{2}-\frac{1}{32})\|\nabla f(x(0))\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ 10M_{2}\cdot\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\|^{2}-\frac{1}{8}\|\nabla f(x(0))\|^{2}

where we used that
h\leq\frac{1}{8\sqrt{M_{2}}}
at the last two

Since

\displaystyle\penalty\ f(x(0))+\int_{0}^{h}(h-t)\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}f(x(t))\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\penalty\ f(x(0))+5M_{2}\cdot h^{2}\cdot\E\left[\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}x(0)\|^{2}\right]-\frac{h^{2}}{16}\|\nabla f(x(0))\|^{2}

\displaystyle\penalty\ f(x(0))+5M_{2}\cdot h^{2}\cdot d-\frac{h^{2}}{16}\|\nabla f(x(0))\|^{2}

For the ODE starting from

T\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m_{2}}}
.

Note that we bounded how much each iteration of the HMC dynamic can change the solution.
Writing it differently, we have

\displaystyle s_{i}^{(j+1)}=(1-\frac{1}{2}m_{2}T^{2})s_{i}^{(j)}+{s_{i}^{(j)}}^{\prime}(0)\cdot T+\beta

where

\displaystyle|\beta|\leq\frac{1}{4}m_{2}T^{2}|s_{i}^{(j)}(0)|+\sqrt{m_{2}}T^{2}\cdot|{s_{i}^{(j)}}^{\prime}(0)|+\tau M\cdot T^{2}.

Note that

and that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(A^{\top}A)=\lambda_{\max}(AA^{\top})\leq\|AA^{\top}\|_{\infty\rightarrow\infty}=\tau.

Therefore,
{s_{i}^{(j)}}^{\prime}(0)T\sim\alpha_{i}\cdot{\cal N}(0,1)
with
0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\sqrt{\tau}T
.
Now, we simplify the dynamic to

\displaystyle s_{i}^{(j+1)}=(1-\delta)s_{i}^{(j)}+\alpha_{i}N^{(j)}+\beta

where
\delta:=\frac{1}{2}m_{2}T^{2}
,
\alpha_{i}\leq a:=\sqrt{\tau}T
, and

\displaystyle|\beta|\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{\delta}{2}|s_{i}^{(j)}(0)|+b|N^{(j)}|+c

with

and
c:=\tau M\cdot T^{2}
.

Applying Lemma

, we have that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\max_{j\in[N]}|s_{i}^{(j)}-s_{i}^{(0)}|\geq C\cdot\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{\delta}}+\frac{c+b}{\delta}\right)\log(N/\epsilon)\right]\leq\epsilon

for some constant

. Taking union bound
over
i\in[d]

\displaystyle\frac{a}{\sqrt{\delta}}+\frac{c+b}{\delta}=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{\sqrt{2\tau}T}{\sqrt{m_{2}}T}+\frac{\tau M\cdot T^{2}+\sqrt{m_{2}\tau}\cdot T^{2}}{2m_{2}T^{2}}\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{m_{2}}}+\frac{\tau M}{m_{2}}.

∎

Lemma 5.2

(Bounding the Martingale)
.

Let
X^{(i)}
be a sequence of random
variable such that

X^{(i+1)}=(1-\delta)X^{(i)}+\alpha_{i}N^{(i)}+\beta

where
N^{(i)}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
are independent,
\alpha_{i}\leq a
,
\beta\leq\frac{\delta}{2}|X^{(i)}|+b|N^{(i)}|+c

with positive
a,b,c
and
0<\delta\leq 1
. For some constant universal

C>0
, we have that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\max_{i\in[k]}|X^{(i)}-X^{(0)}|\geq C\cdot\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{\delta}}+\frac{c+b}{\delta}\right)\log(k/\epsilon)\right]\leq\epsilon

for any
0<\epsilon<1
.

Proof.

We will first show that
X^{(i)}
cannot grow to large. The proof of the other direction is similar. Consider the potential

\Phi^{(i)}=\E\left[e^{\lambda X^{(i)}}\right]
. Note that

\displaystyle\Phi^{(i+1)}\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \E\left[e^{\lambda((1-\delta)X^{(i)}+\alpha_{i}N^{(i)}+b|N^{(i)}|+c+\frac{\delta}{2}|X^{(i)}|)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ e^{\lambda c}\E\left[e^{\lambda(1-\delta)X^{(i)}+\frac{\delta}{2}|X^{(i)}|}\right]\E\left[e^{\lambda(\alpha_{i}N^{(i)}+b|N^{(i)}|)}\right],

where we used that
N^{(i)}
are independent. Picking
\lambda\leq\frac{1}{a+b}

and using
N^{(i)}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
and
|\alpha_{i}|\leq a
, we have that

\displaystyle\E\left[e^{\lambda(\alpha_{i}N^{(i)}+b|N^{(i)}|)}\right]\leq e^{O(\lambda b+\lambda^{2}a^{2})}.

Therefore, for any
\eta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{(i+1)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{O(\lambda c+\lambda b+\lambda^{2}a^{2})}\cdot\left(\E\left[e^{\lambda(1-\delta)X^{(i)}+\frac{\delta}{2}|X^{(i)}|}1_{X^{(i)}\leq\eta}\right]+\E\left[e^{\lambda(1-\frac{\delta}{2})X^{(i)}}1_{X^{(i)}>\eta}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{O(\lambda c+\lambda b+\lambda^{2}a^{2})}\cdot\left(e^{\lambda\eta}+\E\left[e^{\lambda X^{(i)}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\delta\eta}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{O(\lambda c+\lambda b+\lambda^{2}a^{2}+\lambda\eta)}+e^{O(\lambda c+\lambda b+\lambda^{2}a^{2})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\delta\eta}\Phi^{(i)}.

Choose
\eta=\Theta(\frac{c+b+\lambda a^{2}}{\delta})
such that

O(\lambda c+\lambda b+\lambda^{2}a^{2})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\delta\eta\leq 0
.
Hence, we have

\Phi^{(i+1)}\leq e^{O(\lambda c+\lambda b+\lambda^{2}a^{2}+\lambda\eta)}+\Phi^{(i)}\leq e^{O(\frac{\lambda c+\lambda b+\lambda^{2}a^{2}}{\delta})}+\Phi^{(i)}.

Picking an appropriate
\lambda
, we have the result.
∎

A.3
Cauchy Estimates and Method of Majorants

In order to prove the solution of the HMC dynamic can be approximated by a low degree polynomial, we first give a general bound the
k^{th}
derivative of the second order ODE
u^{\prime\prime}(t)=F(u(t))
, by reducing it to bounding derivatives of a one-variable ODE. The low degree result then follows from: first, we take the Taylor expansion of the original function; second, truncate it at a certain degree; finally we can claim that the low-degree truncation provides a good approximation to the original function.

Theorem A.7
.

Given vectors
v_{1},v_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
neighborhood of
v_{0}
and that

is real
analytic near
v_{0}
. Suppose that

\|D^{(k)}F(v_{0})[\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\cdots,\Delta_{k}]\|\leq k!\cdot a\cdot c^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\|\Delta_{j}\|\text{ for all }k\geq 0.

Then, the
ODE

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}u(t)=F(u(t)),\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}u(0)=v_{1},u(0)=v_{0}

(39)

has a unique real analytic solution around
t=0
. Furthermore, we have

\|u^{(k)}(0)\|\leq\frac{k!\alpha^{k}}{c}\quad\forall k\geq 1

with
\alpha=\max(\sqrt{\frac{4}{3}ac},2\|v_{1}\|c)
.

Theorem
A.7
involves two steps. The first step (Lemma
A.9
) involves bounding the derivatives of the solution of the multivariate ODE (
39
) by its scalar version. The second step (Lemma
A.10
) involves bounding the scalar ODE directly.

The first step follows directly from the majorization proof of Cauchy–Kowalevski theorem. See
[
vdH03
]
for an introduction of the method of majorants. This theorem usually stated qualitatively without an explicit bound. For completeness, we include a proof for the first step.

\displaystyle\frac{d^{k}}{\mathrm{d}t^{k}}x(t)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{i}}{\mathrm{d}t^{i}}x(0)

Lemma D.3
