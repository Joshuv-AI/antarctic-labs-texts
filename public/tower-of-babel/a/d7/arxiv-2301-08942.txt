A central limit theorem for a sequence of conditionally centered random fields
Abdollah Jalilian, Arnaud Poinas, Ganggang Xu, Rasmus Waagepetersen

Abstract
A central limit theorem is established for a sum of random variables belonging to a sequence of random fields. The fields are assumed to have zero mean conditional on the past history and to satisfy certain conditional α \alpha -mixing conditions in space or time. Exploiting conditional centering and the space-time structure, the limiting normal distribution is obtained for increasing spatial domain, increasing length of the sequence, or both of these. The theorem is very well suited for establishing asymptotic normality in the context of unbiased estimating function inference for a wide range of space-time processes. This is pertinent given the abundance of space-time data. Two examples demonstrate the applicability of the theorem. Keywords: Central limit theorem; conditional α \alpha -mixing; conditional centering; estimating function; random field; space-time; spatio-temporal; vector autoregression MSC2020 subject classifications: 60F05; 60G55; 60G60; 62M30



1
Introduction

While the literature on central limit theorems for
spatial stochastic processes is extensive
(
Bolthausen, 1982
;
Jensen and Künsch, 1994
;
Guyon, 1995
;
Comets and Janz̧ura, 1998
;
Karácsony, 2006
;
Biscio and Waagepetersen, 2019
, to mention a few)
, the literature on central limit theorems
for spatio-temporal processes seems much less developed. The theoretical foundation for statistical analysis of
space-time data is thus incomplete which is unfortunate since such
data are increasingly common.
The objective of this paper is to derive a
central limit theorem for a sequence of random fields that satisfy a
certain conditional centering condition. This condition is
motivated by applications of estimating functions in
space-time statistics (see Section
2
)
where the estimating function based on a random field at
one time point is unbiased (has zero mean expectation at the true
parameter value) conditional on the past.

Our central limit theorem is based on increasing spatial
domain, increasing time horizon or both but does not require stationarity in space nor in time. To prove the theorem we use a unified framework that more specifically covers both a fixed length
sequence of random fields each with increasing spatial
domain, a sequence of random fields with unconstrained
time horizon and fixed spatial domain, and a combination of these
asymptotic settings.
The proof requires to bound
a large variety of covariances. In the case of spatial random fields,
this is typically done by imposing appropriate weak spatial dependence
conditions like
m
-dependence
(
Leonenko, 1975
;
Heinrich, 2013
, e.g.)
or
\alpha
-mixing
(
Bolthausen, 1982
)
.
Leonenko, 1975
considers
a central limit theorem for a sequence of statistics each obtained as
a sum of variables for a random field in a sequence of increasing domain
m
-dependent random
fields. Instead of using mixing conditions as an intermediate step to
bound covariances,
Doukhan and Lang, 2016
propose a notion of weak
dependence where they impose decay
conditions directly on covariances.
Jensen and Künsch, 1994
and
Comets and Janz̧ura, 1998
use a different strategy for spatial random fields of conditionally centered random variables that have expectation zero given all other
variables. By exploiting the conditional centering, they are able to
dispense with spatial mixing assumptions.

Different from
Jensen and Künsch, 1994
and
Comets and Janz̧ura, 1998
, we assume
conditional centering given the past history in time, which is useful for eliminating
covariances involving variables at distinct time points. This is related
to the key property of uncorrelated increments for martingales. In
addition we exploit the space-time structure and introduce a novel
assumption of
\alpha
-mixing for a random field at a given point in time
conditional on the past history. This is natural to consider when the distribution of a space-time process is specified in
terms of a sequence of conditional distributions given the past, see
Section
2
. Referring again to martingale terminology, this
type of
\alpha
-mixing is convenient since it essentially only
pertains the spatial mixing properties of innovations.
We note that our notion of conditional

\alpha
-mixing is very different from the notion of conditional

m
-dependence in
Biscio and Svane, 2022
for spatial point processes
where conditioning is on an underlying random field.

To give a concise statement of the various conditions needed, we
introduce the novel concept of a space-time filtration which is an
increasing sequence of
\sigma
-algebras that are indexed both by time
points and
subsets of space.

2
Examples of applications

To motivate our central limit we provide some illustrative examples of applications where we try to strike a balance between
accessibility and practical relevance. The targets for our central
limit are so-called score functions. These play a key role in
statistical inference where parameter estimates are obtained by
solving estimating equations where score functions involving unknown
parameters are equated to zero. We use the first example as a simple illustration of the kind of localization and mixing conditions that we impose for our central limit theorem.

2.1
A vector autoregression of order one

Let
X_{0}=(X_{0}(\mathbf{l}))_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}}
denote an initial
condition and
let
\nu_{k}=(\nu_{k}(\mathbf{l}))_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
k\geq 1
, denote a sequence
of independent and identically distributed zero-mean stationary processes. Then for
n=1,2,\ldots
we for consistency of notation
let
X_{0,n}(\mathbf{l})=X_{0}(\mathbf{l})
,
\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and define for
k>0
,

X_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})=a\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})+\nu_{k}(\mathbf{l}),\quad\mathbf{l}=1,\ldots,n,

where
\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})
is an average of
X_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})
and its immediate neighbours and
a\in\mathbb{R}
is
an autogression coefficient. More specifically,
\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(1)=[X_{k-1,n}(1)+X_{k-1,n}(2)]/2
,
\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})=[X_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l}-1)+X_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})+X_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l}+1)]/3
for
1<\mathbf{l}<n
, and
\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(n)=[X_{k-1,n}(n-1)+X_{k-1,n}(n)]/2
. Suppose the
X_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})
are observed for
k=0,\ldots,K
and
\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We may then estimate
a
by solving
T_{K,n}=0
with respect to
a
where
T_{K,n}
is the least squares score function

T_{K,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{l}=1}^{n}\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})[X_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})-a\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})].

Despite the simple formulation of this model, the space-time
correlation structure is rather complicated. For a given
time point
k
, spatial dependence between two variables
X_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})

and
X_{k,n}(\mathbf{j})
arise to begin with from the spatial dependence in
X_{0}
and the

\nu_{l}
processes for any
1\leq l\leq k
. In addition, spatial
dependence may be due to common previous terms
X_{0}(\mathbf{k})
,
\nu_{l}(\mathbf{k})
,
1\leq l<k
,
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
appearing in the sums defining
X_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})
and

X_{k,n}(\mathbf{j})
.

The model is closely related to the network vector
autoregression (NAR) introduced in
Zhu et al., 2017
for large-scale (large
n
) networks, and its numerous variants, e.g.,
Zhu et al., 2023
and
Liu et al., 2024
. It is
simpler than the NAR in the sense that we consider a very specific neighbour
structure and do not consider covariates. We on the
other hand allow the innovation terms

\nu_{k}(\mathbf{l})
,
\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}
, to be correlated while
Zhu et al., 2017
consider independent innovations. Using our central limit
theorem it turns out to be relatively straightforward to obtain
asymptotic normality for
T_{K,n}
and our theorem seems useful as
well for more general NAR models with correlated innovations.

2.2
A discrete time spatial birth-death point process

Consider a sequence of point processes

X_{0},\ldots,X_{K}
and spatial covariates
Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{K}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
where the latter are considered non-random. For each
k=0,\ldots,K
, we observe
X_{k}\cap W
and
Z_{k}(v)
,
v\in W,
where
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded observation window. We consider a model for the
X_{k}

corresponding to a discrete-time spatial birth-death process that mimics the seasonal behaviour of a plant community.

Given
X_{k-1}
and
Z_{k-1}
,
X_{k}=S_{k}\cup B_{k}\cup I_{k}
where
B_{k}=\cup_{u\in X_{k-1}}Y^{k}_{u}
is a
union of offspring clusters
Y^{k}_{u}
given by independent Poisson
processes. For each
u\in X_{k-1}
,
Y_{u}^{k}
has an intensity function given by
\alpha k_{u}(\cdot)
where
\alpha>0
and
k_{u}(\cdot)
is the uniform density on the disc
b(u,\omega)
with center
u
and
radius
\omega
. The intensity function depends on
X_{k-1}
only through
u
. The point process
S_{k}
represents plants from
X_{k-1}
that survived to time
k
and is
given by
S_{k}=\{u\in X_{k-1}|R_{u}=0\}
, where the
R_{u}
are
Bernouilli variables conditionally independent given
X_{k-1}
and

Z_{k-1}
and with ‘survival’ probability

\mathbb{P}(R_{u}=0|X_{k-1},Z_{k-1})
depending on
X_{k-1}
and
Z_{k-1}

only through
Z_{k-1}(u)
. The ‘immigrant’ point processes

I_{k}
,
k\geq 1
, are independent Poisson processes of intensity

\rho\geq 0
. Given
X_{k-1}
, the process
B_{k}\cup I_{k}
of new trees in generation
k
is a Poisson point process with intensity function given by
\rho+\alpha\sum_{u\in X_{k-1}}k_{u}(v)
,
v\in W
.

Suppose parametric models are imposed for the conditional distributions of
B_{k}\cup I_{k}
and
S_{k}
given
Z_{k-1}
and
X_{k-1}
. For each
k
we let
T_{k}^{W}
denote the concatenation of likelihood score functions for the conditional distributions of
B_{k}\cup I_{k}
and
S_{k}
. Then
T_{k}^{W}
can be additively
decomposed as

T_{k}^{W}=\sum_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}}E_{{k}}(\mathbf{l}),

where
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:c(\mathbf{l})\cap W\neq\emptyset\}
,
c(\mathbf{l})

is the unit cube centered at
\mathbf{l}
and the likelihood score component

E_{k}(\mathbf{l})
depends on

Z_{k-1},X_{k-1},B_{k}\cup I_{k},S_{k}
only through
Z_{k-1}(v)
,
v\in c(\mathbf{l})\cap W
and through the intersections of
X_{k-1},B_{k}\cup I_{k},

and
S_{k}
with
c(\mathbf{l})\cap W
. Due to boundary effects, the definition of
the variables
E_{{k}}(\mathbf{l})
may depend on
W
(when
c(\mathbf{l})
is not
entirely contained in
W
). Therefore we will later on, when
considering a sequence of expanding windows
W_{n}
, add the index
n

and write
E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{l})
.

Parametric inference is based on the accumulated score function

T^{W}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}T_{k}^{W}

which has zero mean (is unbiased) since each conditional score
function has zero conditional mean by the first Bartlett identity. For asymptotic inference it is essential to establish asymptotic normality of
T^{W}
. Here relevant asymptotic regimes can be expanding
time horizon
K\rightarrow\infty
, expanding observation window
W

or a combination of these.

The case of increasing window asymptotics
is pertinent for rain forest point pattern data sets covering large
study regions, see
Jalilian et al., 2024
. Here tree positions are registered in
censuses conducted a moderate number of times. For example, for the
Barro Colorado Island data set
(
Condit et al., 2019
)
, censuses are
available at just eight time points which does not justify large
K

asymptotics.

2.3
Graphical autoregressive model

In this section, we consider a spatio-temporal autoregressive model. The model can also be viewed
as a multivariate time series with graphical interactions at each time
instance
(
Dahlhaus, 2000
)
.
Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite lattice and
G=(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E})
be an undirected graph with vertices in
\mathcal{D}
and edges
\mathcal{E}
where

\mathbf{l}\neq\mathbf{j}
in
\mathcal{D}
are connected if
\{\mathbf{l},\mathbf{j}\}\in\mathcal{E}
.
For example, elements of
\mathcal{D}
may represent sub-regions (provinces, counties or municipalities) of a specific geographical region and edges in
\mathcal{E}
determine interconnections between these sub-regions due to e.g. adjacency or transportation routes.

Let
X_{k}=\left(X_{k}(\mathbf{l})\right)_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, be a sequence of random fields where for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}
, we assume that given the history
X_{0},\ldots,X_{k-1}
and the current neighbourhood
{X_{k}(-\mathbf{l})=\{X_{k}(\mathbf{j})\}_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{\mathbf{l}\}}}
,
X_{k}(\mathbf{l})
follows the conditional distribution

X_{k}(\mathbf{l})\big|X_{0},\ldots,X_{k-1},X_{k}(-{\mathbf{l}})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\beta\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}(\mathbf{l})+\gamma\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{spat}}(\mathbf{l}),1/a(\mathbf{l})\right),

(1)

where for some
r\geq 1
,

\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}(\mathbf{l})=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{D}}b_{j}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{j})X_{k-j}(\mathbf{j}),

with
X_{k-j}(\mathbf{l})=0
for
k<j
, and

\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{spat}}(\mathbf{l})=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{D}\backslash\{\mathbf{l}\}}b_{0}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{j})\left(X_{k}(\mathbf{j})-\beta\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}(\mathbf{j})\right).

Let
\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}=(\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}(\mathbf{l}))_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}}
,
\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{spat}}=(\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{spat}}(\mathbf{l}))_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}}
,
A=\mathrm{diag}\left(a(\mathbf{l}):\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}\right)
, and
B_{j}=\left[b_{j}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{j})\right]_{\mathbf{l},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{D}}
,
j=0,\ldots,r
, with diagonal entries
b_{0}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{l})=0
for
B_{0}
.
Then,

\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}B_{j}X_{k-j}\quad\text{ and }\quad\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{spat}}=B_{0}\left(X_{k}-\beta\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}\right).

We assume that
b_{0}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{j}),\ldots,b_{p}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{j})
and
a_{n}(\mathbf{l})
are
known and
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
are unknown parameters. Based on
observations of
X_{k}
for
k=0,\ldots,K
, a score function for estimation of
\beta
and
\gamma
is given by

T=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}}E_{k}(\mathbf{l}),

(see Section 5.2 of the supplementary material
Jalilian et al., 2023
, for
details)
where

E_{k}(\mathbf{l})=\varepsilon_{k}(\mathbf{l})\left(\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}(\mathbf{l}),\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{spat}}(\mathbf{l})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}

and
\varepsilon_{k}=A(X_{k}-\beta\xi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{temp}}-\gamma\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{spat}})
.

We can asympotically approximate the complicated distribution of
T
by a normal distribution as
K
tends to infinity.

3
The space-time central limit theorem

Consider for each
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
a random field
E_{{k,n}}=(E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{l}))_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}
, on
\mathcal{D}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, where

E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{l})=\left(E^{(1)}_{k,n}(\mathbf{l}),\ldots,E^{(q)}_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})\right)^{\mathsf{T}},

q\geq 1
, and
(\mathcal{D}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
sequence of finite observation lattices while
k
can be viewed as a time
index. As explained in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
, the index
n
for the variables
E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{l})
may be needed due to
edge effects when the variables are constructed in terms of an autoregression or from an underlying
process on a continuous observation window
W_{n}
.

Depending on the context (see examples in Section
2
), it may or may not be reasonable to
assume properties like stationarity or mixing in time for the sequence

E_{{k,n}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. If the sequence

E_{{k,n}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, can not be viewed as a part of a stationary
sequence, it is convenient to consider it conditional on an
initial condition represented by a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, see
Section
3.2
.
Defining for
K,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

T_{K,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{l}),

(2)

we formulate in Section
3.4
a central limit theorem for

\Sigma_{K,n}^{-1/2}T_{K,n}
with
\Sigma_{K,n}={\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[T_{K,n}]
, that is, the variance conditional on the initial condition
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Conditions for the theorem are specified in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Initial remarks in relation to vector autoregression

Before going deeply into technical definitions and conditions we
return for a moment to the vector autoregression in Section
2.1
to
give an informal motivation and explanation of various technical
concepts introduced in the next sections.

Letting
E_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})=\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})[X_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})-a\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})]

it is clear that the
E_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})
are conditionally
centered,
\mathbb{E}[E_{k,n}(\mathbf{l})|X_{k-1.n}]=0
. For
Y^{k,K,\mathbf{j}}=f[E_{k,n}(\mathbf{j}),E_{k+1}(\mathbf{j}),\ldots,E_{K,n}(\mathbf{j})]
given as a function of
E_{k,n}(\mathbf{j})
and future variables
E_{l,n}(\mathbf{j})
,
k<l\leq K
, we consider further
the conditional expectation of
Y^{k,K,\mathbf{j}}
given the past

X_{0},\nu_{1}\ldots,\nu_{k-1}
. We will formulate such a conditional expectation in terms of a space-time filtration introduced in the next section. For the vector autoregression we for example
define the past
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
as the
\sigma
-algebra generated
by
\mathbf{X}_{0}
and
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{k-1}
and

\mathcal{H}_{0,k-1,\{\mathbf{j}-K,\ldots,\mathbf{j}+K\}}
as the smaller
\sigma
-algebra
generated by
X_{0}(\mathbf{l})
and
\nu_{l}(\mathbf{l})
for
0<l\leq k-1
and

\mathbf{l}\in\{\mathbf{j}-K,\ldots,\mathbf{j}+K\}
. Then the conditional expectation is ‘localized’
(or Markovian) in the sense that

\mathbb{E}[Y^{k,K,\mathbf{j}}|\mathcal{H}_{k-1}]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{k,K,\mathbf{j}}|\mathcal{H}_{0,k-1,\{\mathbf{j}-K,\ldots,\mathbf{j}+K\}}]
.

Spatial localization is
important when we need to control spatial dependence between for
example
\mathbb{E}[Y^{k,K,\mathbf{l}}|\mathcal{H}_{k-1}]
and
\mathbb{E}[Y^{k,K,\mathbf{j}}|\mathcal{H}_{k-1}]
,

\mathbf{l}\neq\mathbf{j}
, using spatial
\alpha
-mixing coefficients (next section). This is because the mixing coefficients are
concerned with dependence between parts of the stochastic process
where the parts are defined in terms of subsets of space or time or both.

For
the toy example, if the correlations
{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{orr}}[\nu_{k}(\mathbf{l}),\nu_{k}(\mathbf{l}+h)]

are sufficiently fast decaying as a function of
h
, then
\nu_{k}
is

\alpha
-mixing
(
Doukhan, 1994
)
. This immediately implies that stochastic processes involving
\nu_{k}
and the past are
\alpha
-mixing given the past. For example,
E_{k,n}
with components
\bar{X}_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{l})\nu_{k}(\mathbf{l})
is

\alpha
-mixing conditional on
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
. When we in the next section introduce a mixing coefficient for
\mathcal{H}_{0,k,A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0,k,B}
given
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
this essentially involves mixing between
\nu_{k}(\mathbf{l}),\mathbf{l}\in A
, and
\nu_{k}(\mathbf{j})
,
\mathbf{j}\in B
.

For fixed
n
and increasing
K
we might instead consider
localization in time. In this case we may define the past

\mathcal{H}_{0,k-1,\mathcal{D}_{n}}
as the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X_{n,l}(\mathbf{j})
,

0\leq l\leq k-1
,
\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{k-1,k-1,\mathcal{D}_{n}}
as the

\sigma
-algebra generated by
X_{k-1,n}(\mathbf{j})
,
\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, in which case the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[Y^{k,K,\mathbf{j}}|\mathcal{H}_{0,k-1,\mathcal{D}_{n}}]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{k,K,\mathbf{j}}|\mathcal{H}_{k-1,k-1,\mathcal{D}_{n}}]
is localized in time.

3.2
Notation, space-time filtration and

\alpha
-mixing

We define
\mathrm{d}(x,y)=\max\{|x_{i}-y_{i}|:1\leq i\leq d\},\ x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and, reusing notation,

\mathrm{d}(A,B)=\inf\{\mathrm{d}(x,y):x\in A,\ y\in B\},\ A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}.
For a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
\left|A\right|
the
cardinality or Lebesgue
measure of
A
. The meaning of
|\cdot|
and
\mathrm{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
will
be clear from the context. For
a\in\mathbb{R}
we use the brief notation

\mathbb{E}[\cdots]^{a}
for the less ambiguous
\left(\mathbb{E}[\cdots]\right)^{a}
.

Conditioning on the past history plays a crucial role for our result. We
represent the history by what we call a space-time filtration
whose definition not only involves time points but also spatial
regions. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be the underlying
probability space on which all random objects in this paper are
defined. Recalling
\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, we coin a set
\mathcal{H}=\{\mathcal{H}_{l,k,A}\mid l\leq k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
of

\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{H}_{l,k,A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
a space time
filtration if
\mathcal{H}_{l,k,A}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{l^{\prime},k^{\prime},A}
for
l^{\prime}\leq l\leq k\leq k^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{H}_{l,k,A}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{l,k,B}
for
A\subseteq B
.
For brevity we let
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathcal{H}_{0,k,\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. As discussed in Section
3.1

one can think of the
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{H}_{l,k,A}

as being generated by a stochastic process restricted to the space-time domain

\{l,l+1,\ldots,k\}\times A
. More generally, this could also include
space-time exogeneous variables serving as covariates in a
space-time model. This in turn makes the
E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{l})

measurable when defined in terms of the underlying processes. We consider further examples in
Sections
4.2
and

4.3
.

For any
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{G}_{0},\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, the conditional
\alpha
-mixing
coefficient of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
given

\mathcal{G}_{0}
is defined in
Prakasa Rao, 2009
as

\alpha_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}F_{1}\in\mathcal{G}_{1}\\
F_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}\end{subarray}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(F_{1}\cap F_{2})-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(F_{1})\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(F_{2})\big|,

where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(F)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}[I_{F}]
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}
denotes the conditional expectation with respect
to
\mathcal{G}_{0}
,
I_{F}(\omega)=I[\omega\in F]
and
I[\,\cdot\,]

is the indicator function. Similarly,
{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}

and
{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}
denote variance and covariance
conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Note that

For

\displaystyle\alpha_{K,c_{1},c_{2}}(m)

\displaystyle=\sup_{1\leq k\leq K}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\\

\mathrm{d}(A,B)\geq m\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}(\mathcal{H}_{0,k,A},\mathcal{H}_{0,k,B}).

Moreover, for

\displaystyle{\alpha_{r}(m)}

\displaystyle{=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\\

where

. Finally, we combine (

obtain for

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,k\leq K\\
l-k\geq m\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
|A|\leq c_{1},|B|\leq c_{2}\\
\mathrm{d}(A,B)\geq m^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}(\mathcal{H}_{0,k,A},\mathcal{H}_{{(l-r+1)^{+}},l,B}).

This extends the previous mixing coefficients since (

)
is
\alpha_{\infty;K,c_{1},c_{2}}({0;m})
if we restrict

to

.

Remark 1
.

The definitions (

) and (

) involve in a standard way

over
space lags as well as over sizes of spatial regions or over time

involves conditional
\alpha
-mixing for events up to time

see also Section

. For (

3.3
Conditions

so that the following conditional centering

Condition

Moreover, it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[T_{K,n}]=0
and

\Sigma_{K,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\Big[\sum_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{l})\Big]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{l},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\left[{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}[E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{l}),E_{{k,n}}(\mathbf{j})]\right].

Next, we introduce the following conditional
\alpha
-mixing
conditions for the filtration

:

(
\mathcal{A}
3)

there exists a real number

such
that one of the following holds

is defined in

).

(
\mathcal{A}

for a fixed

, there exists a function

, where

.

(
\mathcal{A}

for a fixed

.

.

(
\mathcal{A}

there exist an

and a constant

(

\mathcal{A}

(

The conditional centering condition

is
trivially satisfied when the theorem is applied in the context of
conditionally unbiased estimating functions for space-time

(

while

\alpha
-mixing
(

. This may be quite easy to
verify when considering stochastic processes specified in terms of
conditional distributions, see for example

2.1
and
2.2
. We elaborate further in

below. The conditions of bounded

(

A

and non-vanishing variance

(

A

are typical for
central limit theorems. The more difficult condition among these
is
(

A

which is often simply
left as an assumption. However, we are able to give a practically
verifiable sufficient condition for
(

A

in Section
4.3
.

3.3.1
Criteria for
\alpha
-mixing

The condition of
\alpha
-mixing is translated into bounds for
covariances using inequalities originally due to
Davydov, 1968
and later on

Rio, 1993
and
Doukhan, 1994
where we use the conditional
version provided in
Yuan and Lei, 2013
. It is possible to
alternatively use
\beta
-mixing to bound covariances

(
Heinrich and Pawlas, 2013
)
or to impose decay
conditions directly on covariances as in
Doukhan and Lang, 2016
. We nevertheless find that
\alpha
-mixing is a well-established, concise and intuitively
appealing measure of spatial dependence. Specific examples of

\alpha
-mixing processes are
m
-dependent processes, Gaussian
processes with sufficiently fast decay of the correlation function

(
Doukhan, 1994
)
, Neyman-Scott processes with sufficiently fast
decay of the cluster density
(
Waagepetersen and Guan, 2009
)
and Cox
point processes with
\alpha
-mixing random intensity
function. Recently
Poinas et al., 2019
further established

\alpha
-mixing for associated point processes which include the
important class of determinantal point processes. In our setting, for example, for the
iterated point process in Section
2.2
, we
may relax the assumption that the
B_{k}
are Poisson conditional on the
past by the assumption that the
B_{k}
are conditionally

\alpha
-mixing Neyman-Scott, Cox, or determinantal point
processes.

Our central limit theorem is

.

(

,

(

A

, and
(

A

A

5-1)

hold,

\lim_{n\to\infty}|\mathcal{D}_{n}|=\infty

(

A

A

,
(

A

3)(

A

3-2)
,

(

A

A

, and
(

A

,

are provided in

Remark 2
.

When

increases in case

, dependence may
propagate over space which may dilute the effect of increasing spatial domain. This
is particularly reflected by the localization condition

(

A

2)(

A

2-3)
. Therefore,
to exploit spatial mixing in case

we need to temper increase of time

relative to increase of space

.

Remark 3
.

The conclusion of

is the same for all cases

(i)
-
(iii)
and in practice we may not need the
more complex setting of
(iii)
with
n
and
K
jointly tending to
infinity. The case of
K
fixed,
n\rightarrow\infty
, is for example relevant in rain forest ecology where spatially large data sets are collected at a modest number of census times.

Remark 4
.

There exists some measurable function
X_{0}
so that
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\sigma(X_{0})
.
Write
\phi_{K,n}(x_{0})
for
\mathbb{E}[\exp(it^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{K,n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}T_{K,n})|X_{0}=x_{0}]
where expectation is with respect to a regular conditional
distribution of the
E_{{k,n}}
,
{1\leq}k\leq K
,

, given

. Then
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\exp(it^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{K,n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}T_{K,n})]=\phi_{K,n}(X_{0})
almost
surely. In our proof we thus show that
\phi_{K,n}(x_{0})
converges to

\exp(\|t\|^{2}/2)
for almost all values
x_{0}
of
X_{0}
.

Remark 5
.

The result in case (ii) (
K\rightarrow\infty
,
n
fixed) could
alternatively under the same conditions be established using existing martingale central limit
theorems
(
Brown, 1971
;
Hall et al., 2014
, e.g.)
. To apply
these theorems it is required to control a sum of conditional
variances. This, however, involves all the results from
Sections
5.1
-
5.2
,
Section 3 in the supplementary material, and derivations similar to those used
to handle the term
A_{1}
in our proof. We therefore find it more
natural to use our unified framework for all cases
(i)
-
(iii)
.

Remark 6
.

In the proof of
Theorem 1
, we need to bound a large
number, of the order
K^{4}|\mathcal{D}_{n}|^{4}
, of certain covariances, see
Section
5.3
and the supplementary material. The
conditional centering property
(

A

is crucial in
this regard since it immediately reduces the number of the non-zero
covariances to be of the order
K^{3}|\mathcal{D}_{n}|^{4}
. However, it does not
affect the order
|\mathcal{D}_{n}|^{4}
. This is why spatial mixing assumptions
are required in case
(i)
and
(iii)
.
