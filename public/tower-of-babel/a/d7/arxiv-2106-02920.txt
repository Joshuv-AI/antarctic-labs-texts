The perturbed energy-momentum tensor:

All the components of the perturbed energy-momentum tensor up to first order can be expressed in matrix form as,

{T^{\mu}}_{\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}-\rho&\delta{\partial_{1}}q&\delta{\partial_{2}}q&\delta{\partial_{3}}q\\
-a^{-2}\delta{\partial^{1}}q+a^{-1}(p^{(0)}+\rho^{(0)}){\partial^{1}}B&p&0&0\\
-a^{-2}\delta{\partial^{2}}q+a^{-1}(p^{(0)}+\rho^{(0)}){\partial^{2}}B&0&p&0\\
-a^{-2}\delta{\partial^{3}}q+a^{-1}(p^{(0)}+\rho^{(0)}){\partial^{3}}B&0&0&p\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(3.2)

Coupled density-momentum equations:

By solving the Einstein field equations using (
3.1
) and (

\Phi_{B}

\delta\dot{q}+3H\delta{q}=-\delta{p}-(p^{(0)}+{\rho}^{(0)})\left[\Phi_{B}+\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\Phi_{B}}{H}\right)\right].

(3.4)

These equations in the Fourier space originate from the linear metric perturbations.

Classical evolution equation of inflaton perturbation minimally coupled to gravity:

We consider that the evolution of matter perturbations given by (

\ddot{\phi}^{(0)}(k,t)+3H\dot{\phi}^{(0)}(k,t)+\frac{\partial V(\phi^{(0)}(k,t))}{\partial\phi^{(0)}(k,t)}=0,

(3.5)

\begin{split}\delta\ddot{\phi}(k,t)+3H\delta\dot{\phi}(k,t)+\frac{{\partial}^{2}V(\phi^{(0)}(k,t))}{\partial{\phi^{(0)}(k,t)}^{2}}\delta\phi(k,t)+\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}\delta\phi(k,t)&\\
=\dot{\phi}^{(0)}(k,t)\left[(\dot{\Phi}_{B})(k,t)+\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\left(\frac{(\Phi_{B})(k,t)}{H}\right)+\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}\left(\frac{(\Phi_{B})(k,t)}{H}\right)\right]\\
-2\left[(\Phi_{B})(k,t)+\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{(\Phi_{B})(k,t)}{H}\right)\right]\frac{\partial V(\phi^{(0)}(k,t))}{\partial\phi^{(0)}(k,t)}.\end{split}

(3.6)

In (

Quantization of the inflaton field:

In order to study the quantum modes of the perturbations we will now, proceed to quantise the inflaton field. Now this field will behave as an operator when it is quantized canonically,

\hat{\phi}(t,\vec{X})=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}[\phi(k,t)\hat{a}(\vec{k})e^{i\vec{k}.\vec{x}}+{{\phi}^{*}}(k,t){\hat{a}^{\dagger}}(\vec{k})e^{-i\vec{k}.\vec{x}}],

(3.7)

with the commutation,

[\hat{a}(\vec{k}),{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}(\vec{k^{\prime}})]=(2\pi)^{3}\delta(\vec{k}-\vec{k^{\prime}}).

(3.8)

The mode-dependent inflaton field operator can be expressed as,

\hat{\phi}(\vec{k},t)=\phi(k,t)\hat{a}_{\vec{k}}+\phi^{*}(-k,t){\hat{a}}^{\dagger}_{-{\vec{k}}}.

(3.9)

The power spectra:

A two-point correlation function in a quasi de-Sitter universe is the vacuum expectation value of a product of first-order perturbations in the quantum mode functions (
3.9
), as given by,

\left\langle\delta\phi(\vec{k},t)\delta\phi(\vec{k^{\prime}},t)\right\rangle=\frac{4\pi^{3}}{k^{3}}\delta(\vec{k}+\vec{k^{\prime}})H^{2}.

(3.10)

In the spatially flat gauge, the curvature perturbation
\mathcal{R}
is related to the inflaton perturbation
\delta\phi

\mathcal{R}=H\frac{\delta\phi}{\dot{\phi}}.

(3.11)

The quantum fluctuations in the inflaton field are microscopically induced in the curvature perturbation and thus the curvature correlation function can be written as,

\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\vec{k},t)\mathcal{R}(\vec{k^{\prime}},t)\right\rangle=\frac{H^{2}}{\dot{\phi}^{2}}\left\langle\delta\phi(\vec{k},t)\delta\phi(\vec{k^{\prime}},t)\right\rangle

(3.12)

which can also be written as,

\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\vec{k},t)\mathcal{R}(\vec{k^{\prime}},t)\right\rangle=\frac{16\pi^{5}}{k^{3}}\delta(\vec{k}+\vec{k^{\prime}})\Delta_{\mathcal{R}}(t)

(3.13)

where

is a dimensionless curvature power spectrum,

\Delta_{\mathcal{R}}(t)=\frac{H^{4}}{(2\pi)^{2}{\dot{\phi}^{2}}}.

(3.14)

Similarly, for tensor perturbation we have a dimensionless power spectrum for single polarization state,

\Delta_{h}(t)=\frac{1}{{M_{Pl}}^{2}}\left(\frac{H}{\pi}\right)^{2}.

(3.15)

(For two polarization states, the customary notation is

The horizon crossing is a dynamical process, as different modes cross the horizon at different times satisfying the condition

k

The fact that different modes exit the horizon at different times, is buried in the quantum fluctuations and this will be our theme in the subsequent analysis of the mode-dependent cosmological parameters.

\Delta_{t}(k)=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}{M_{Pl}}^{2}}{\left(H^{2}\right)}_{k=aH}

(3.17)

where, in (

5
Conclusions

In conclusion, we have done detailed mode analysis of cosmological perturbations including quantum fluctuations during inflation in the sub-Planckian region, with the chaotic
\alpha
-attractor potentials. We have mainly focused on the long wavelength (
k\longrightarrow 0
) regions of the modes which drive the late time evolution of the universe
[
43
]
. The role of the short wavelength modes, which are responsible for reheating, is not examined in this paper.

In this paper, we project out the possible experimentally favoured values of the
\alpha
parameter through chaotic inflationary mode analysis. In addition to selecting the participating modes during inflation, our calculation has another aspect of constraining
\alpha
, which may have quite intricate relationship (see (
3.50
)) with high-scale microscopic scenario, as follows.

Geometrically
\alpha
is the inverse curvature of
SU(1,1)/U(1)
Kähler manifold (see (
1.3
)), as it also controls the tensor-to-scalar ratio
r
. If
\alpha\longrightarrow 0
, i.e. for an infinitely curved manifold,
r\longrightarrow 0
and if
\alpha\longrightarrow\infty
, i.e. for a flat manifold
r\longrightarrow\infty
. Therefore the values of
\alpha
have direct correspondence to those of
r
and thereby to the geometry (shape and size) of the internal manifold. Now this interplay is complicated both from the theoretical and the experimental point of view. Finding out the exact volume of the internal manifold is an outstanding task for string theoretic models such as Large Volume Scenario (LVS) in the context of moduli stabilisation, since it is related to finding a particular Calabi-Yau manifold out of a large number of possibilities, which are themselves related by dualities such as mirror symmetry. And as stated in Section
1
, finding
r
is the aim of the ongoing and the future CMB experiments (Background Imaging of Cosmic Extragalactic Polarization(BICEP/Keck), Suborbital polarimeter for Inflation, Dust, and Epoch of Reionization(Spider), POLARization of the Background Radiation(PolarBear), Atacama Cosmology Telescope(ACT), South Pole Telescope(SPT), Simons Observatory(SO), Lite (Light) satellite for the studies of B-mode polarization and Inflation from cosmic background Radiation Detection(LiteBIRD), CMB-S4)
[
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
]
, through the possible detection of
B
-mode polarisation of gravitational waves with accuracy never achieved before. It is challenging enough because primordial gravitational waves are very weak and
B
-modes are also very feeble to detect. So we believe that the results of our calculation may throw some light on these issues in future. Interestingly, we have reproduced, in the present study, the values of
\alpha
and corresponding values of
r
as predicted by maximal supersymmtery models with
B
-mode target
[
32
]
.

In contrast to our microscopic approach, the statistical model calculations yield a continuous range of values of
\alpha
. For example,
\alpha<19.95
(
E
-Model) and
\alpha<10
(
T
-Model) obtained by Bayesian analysis
[
38
]
and
\alpha=7.56\pm 5.15
by MCMC analysis
[
12
]
. However, our findings of discrete values of
\alpha
conform to the predicted values from the supergravity
[
32
,
40
]
and the type IIB string theory
[
61
]
models, at least partially.
