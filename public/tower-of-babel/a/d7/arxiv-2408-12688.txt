Shadowing in the hyperspace of continua Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification : Primary 37D10, 37B40, 37B45, Secondary 37B99.
Bernardo Carvalho and Udayan Darji

Abstract
. We discuss whether classical examples of dynamical systems satisfying the shadowing property also satisfy the shadowing property for the induced map on the hyperspace of continua, obtaining both positive and negative results. We prove that transitive Anosov diffeomorphisms, or more generally continuum-wise hyperbolic homeomorphisms, do not satisfy the shadowing property for the induced map on the hyperspace of continua. We prove that dendrite monotone maps satisfy the shadowing property if, and only if, their induced map on the hyperspace of continua also satisfies it. We give some algorithms that show that there are abundant dynamical systems satisfying the shadowing property with dendrites (compact metric trees) as the underlying space. As a consequence, we show that the universal dendrite of order n n admits a homeomorphism with the shadowing property.



1.
Introduction

The dynamical systems theory studies the behavior of orbits generated by some evolution law. We can think of a discrete dynamical system as a map
f\colon X\to X
where
(X,d)
is a compact metric space. In this case, the orbit of a point
x\in X
is the sequence
(f^{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
f^{n}
denotes the composition of
f
with itself
n

(f^{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
when
n\to+\infty
? In many cases, it is necessary to approximate or truncate the orbit, yielding what are called
pseudo-orbits
. They do not describe the real evolution of the system, therefore it would be useful to obtain a real orbit (hence the real evolution of the system) following them through time. In this way, the pseudo-orbit errors can be disregarded. This property in a dynamical system is called the
shadowing property
(see definition
2.1
). Its importance comes from distinct research directions, such as the stability theory
[
55
]
, recurrence theory
[
23
]
, chaotic and hyperbolic dynamics
[
2
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
53
]
, and it is a significant part of the qualitative study of dynamical systems that contains several important and deep results (see the monographs
[
42
]
and
[
45
]
).

Many distinct notions of shadowing properties were introduced and discussed extensively in the literature. They either assume distinct notions of pseudo-orbits or distinct notions of shadowing orbits (see for example
[
6
]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

[

). In this article, we discuss pseudo-orbits where elements are not necessarily singles points but are sets of the space (not necessarily small). We restrict ourselves to the case the sets are continua (compact and connected subsets) obtaining for every pseudo-orbit of continua, a continuum shadowing it through time. This can be easily explained using the induced map on the hyperspace of continua as follows.

Let
f\colon X\to X
be a continuous map of a compact metric space
(X,d)
,
\mathcal{C}(X)
be the set of all subcontinua of

be the map defined by

. We call

the induced map of

to the hyperspace of continua
\mathcal{C}(X)
. Endowing
\mathcal{C}(X)
with the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}(F,G)=\inf\{\varepsilon>0;F\subset B(G,\varepsilon)\,\,\text{and}\,\,G\subset B(F,\varepsilon)\}
, where for a compact set
A

B(A,\varepsilon)=\{x\in M;d(x,A)<\varepsilon\}
, we obtain a compact metric space where
C(f)
is a continuous self-map. In the set
\mathcal{C}(X)
, points are continua of
X
so the shadowing property of the system
(\mathcal{C}(X),C(f))
describes precisely the property of shadowing pseudo-orbit of continua we described above.

The study of dynamics in hyperspaces comes from the idea of understanding the collective dynamics and the literature is extensive relating chaotic properties of

and its induced map such as topological entropy, sensitivity to initial conditions, recurrence properties, and specification like properties (see
[

. Chaotic properties of

have a few differences but there are many similarities. However, the system
(\mathcal{C}(X),C(f))
can contain a much more different dynamics even being a subsystem of
(2^{X},2^{f})
.

Regarding the shadowing property, it is proved in
[
28
]
that
f
has the shadowing property if, and only if,
2^{f}
has the shadowing property. It is easy to prove that if

has the shadowing property, then

has the shadowing property

. As far as the converse is concerned, in

are the simplest possible dynamical systems where there are (up to a finite iterate) only a finite number of fixed points that are either sinks or sources and the orbit of every other point converges to these fixed points in the future and in the past. This was generalized in
[

but it is still open in the case of general Morse-Smale diffeomorphisms (even on the Sphere).

Thus, it is interesting to understand what are the maps that induce the shadowing property in
\mathcal{C}(X)
and the ones that do not. In this work, we discuss this problem for a few classes of systems satisfying the shadowing property, obtaining both positive and negative results.

Compact metric trees, also known as dendrites, arise in a variety of situations. Some of the earliest results concerning dendrites were obtained by Ważewski who constructed and investigated properties of the universal dendrites. Dendrites appear as Julia sets of quadratic polynomials in the plane
[
17

[

[

f\colon D\to D

has the shadowing property if, and only if,

A classical result in hyperbolic dynamics is that hyperbolic sets satisfy the shadowing property. This was proved independently by Anosov
[

and Bowen
[

,
[

Some generalizations of hyperbolicity also imply the shadowing property. This is the case, for example, of the continuum-wise hyperbolicity considered in
[
7

that generalizes hyperbolicity with respect to the continuum theory. We prove that if
f\colon M\to M
is a transitive Anosov diffeomorphism of a closed manifold, or more generally a continuum-wise hyperbolic homeomorphism of a Peano continuum, then the induced map
C(f)
does not satisfy the shadowing property.

2.
Continuum-wise expansive/hyperbolic homeomorphisms

The main result of this section is Theorem
2.10
where we prove that
C(f)
does not have the shadowing property when
f
is a transitive cw-hyperbolic homeomorphism (this includes the case of Anosov diffeomorphisms). Before stating the results of this section, we state all necessary definitions.

Definition 2.1

(Shadowing)
.

We say that a homeomorphism
f\colon X\rightarrow X
of a compact metric space
(X,d)
satisfies the
shadowing property
if given
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that for each sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset X
satisfying

d(f(x_{n}),x_{n+1})<\delta\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,n\in\mathbb{Z}

there is
y\in X
such that

d(f^{n}(y),x_{n})<\varepsilon\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,n\in\mathbb{Z}.

In this case, we say that
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a
\delta-
pseudo orbit of
f
and that
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is

\varepsilon-
shadowed by
y
.

Definition 2.2

(Local stable/unstable sets/continua)
.

For each
x\in X
and
c>0
, let

W^{s}_{c}(x):=\{y\in X;\,\,d(f^{k}(y),f^{k}(x))\leq c\,\,\,\,\textrm{for every}\,\,\,\,k\geq 0\}

be the
c-stable set
of
x
and

W^{u}_{c}(x):=\{y\in X;\,\,d(f^{k}(y),f^{k}(x))\leq c\,\,\,\,\textrm{for every}\,\,\,\,k\leq 0\}

be the
c-unstable set
of
x
. Denote by
C^{s}_{c}(x)
the
c
-stable continuum of
x
, that is the connected component of
x
on
W^{s}_{c}(x)
, and by
C^{u}_{c}(x)
the
c
-unstable continuum of
x
, that is the connected component of
x
on
W^{u}_{c}(x)
.

Definition 2.3

(Sets of stable/unstable continua)
.

Let
\operatorname{diam}(A)
denotes the diameter of the set
A
defined by
\operatorname{diam}(A)=\sup\{d(x,y);x,y\in A\}
,

\mathcal{C}^{s}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}(X)\;;\;\operatorname{diam}(f^{n}(C))\to 0\,\text{ when }\,n\to+\infty\},\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}

\mathcal{C}^{u}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}(X)\;;\;\operatorname{diam}(f^{-n}(C))\to 0\,\text{ when }\,n\to\infty\}.

Continua in
\mathcal{C}^{s}
are called stable and continua in
\mathcal{C}^{u}
are called unstable. Let

\mathcal{C}^{s}_{\varepsilon}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}(X)\;;\;\operatorname{diam}(f^{n}(C))\leq\varepsilon\,\text{ for every }\,n\geq 0\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}

\mathcal{C}^{u}_{\varepsilon}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}(X)\;;\;\operatorname{diam}(f^{-n}(C))\leq\varepsilon\,\text{ for every }\,n\geq 0\}.

These sets contain exactly the
\varepsilon
-stable and
\varepsilon
-unstable continua of
f
, respectively.

Definition 2.4

(Continuum-wise expansiveness)
.

For each
x\in X
and
c>0
let

\Gamma_{c}(x)=W^{u}_{c}(x)\cap W^{s}_{c}(x)

be the dynamical ball of
x
with radius
c
. We say that
f
is
expansive
if there exists
c>0
such that

\Gamma_{c}(x)=\{x\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,x\in X.

We say that
f
is
continuum-wise expansive
if there exists
c>0
such that
\Gamma_{c}(x)
is totally disconnected for every
x\in X
. The number
c
is called a
cw-expansive constant
of
f
.

Cw-expansiveness was first considered by Kato in
[
33
]
and
[
32
]
. It was proved there that if
c>0
is a cw-expansive constant of
f
and
\varepsilon<\frac{c}{2}
, then

\mathcal{C}^{s}_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{C}^{s}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,\mathcal{C}^{u}_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{C}^{u}.

The following result contains a mechanism that rule out the shadowing property for
C(f)
when
f
is a cw-expansive homeomorphism.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
f\colon X\rightarrow X
be a cw-expansive homeomorphism of a compact metric space
(X,d)
. If for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a pair of non-trivial stable/unstable continua
(S_{n},U_{n})
satisfying

(1)

d_{H}(S_{n},U_{n})\to 0
when
n\to+\infty
and

(2)

there exists
r>0
such that

\operatorname{diam}(S_{n})>r\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,\operatorname{diam}(U_{n})>r\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,n\in\mathbb{N},

then
C(f)
does not have the shadowing property.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that
C(f)
has the shadowing property. Let
c\in(0,r)
be a cw-expansive constant of
f
,
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{c}{4})
, and
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
, given by the shadowing property of
C(f)
, be such that every
\delta
-pseudo-orbit of
C(f)
is
\varepsilon
-shadowed. Choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d_{H}(S_{n},U_{n})<\delta
. The shadowing property of
C(f)
ensures the existence of
C\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying

d_{H}(f^{k}(C),f^{k}(S_{n}))\leq\varepsilon\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,k\geq 0\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}

d_{H}(f^{-k}(C),f^{-k}(U_{n}))\leq\varepsilon\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,k\geq 0.

Since
S_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{s}
and
U_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{u}
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\operatorname{diam}(f^{k}(S_{n}))\leq\varepsilon\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,\operatorname{diam}(f^{-k}(U_{n}))\leq\varepsilon\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,k\geq k_{0}

and, consequently,

\operatorname{diam}(f^{k}(C))\leq 2\varepsilon\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,\operatorname{diam}(f^{-k}(C))\leq 2\varepsilon\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,k\geq k_{0}.

It follows from the choice of
\varepsilon
that

f^{k_{0}}(C)\in\mathcal{C}^{s}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,f^{-k_{0}}(C)\in\mathcal{C}^{u}

and, consequently,
C\in\mathcal{C}^{s}\cap\mathcal{C}^{u}
. Note that
C
is not a singleton since, in this case,
d_{H}(C,S_{n})<\varepsilon
would imply
\operatorname{diam}(S_{n})\leq\varepsilon<r
, contradicting the assumption.
By
[
40
, Theorem 1.21]
we can choose a subcontinuum
C^{\prime}\subset C
such that
C^{\prime}
is not a singleton and
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{s}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{C}^{u}_{\varepsilon}
, contradicting the cw-expansiveness of
f
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

It is important to note that in a cw-expansive homeomorphism it is never possible to find
S_{n}
and
U_{n}
accumulating as in Theorem
2.5
and being local stable/unstable continua. Indeed, if
S_{n}
and
U_{n}
are
c
-stable and
c
-unstable continua, then any continua that is accumulated by subsequences of both
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is both
c
-stable and
c
-unstable, contradicting cw-expansiveness. This is more easily seen on Anosov diffeomorphisms, where local stable/unstable continua are contained in stable/unstable manifolds and their transversality does not allow such an accumulation.

In what follows we prove the hypothesis of Theorem
2.5
in some well known cases: among them are the Anosov diffeomorphisms and the continuum-wise hyperbolic homeomorphisms. To create the stable/unstable continua accumulating as in Theorem
2.5
, we use global stable/unstable manifolds in the case of Anosov diffeomorphisms, while in the case of cw-hyperbolic homeomorphisms we use the global stable/unstable continua.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
f\colon X\rightarrow X
be a cw-expansive homeomorphism of a non-trivial compact metric space
(X,d)
,
c>0
be a cw-expansive constant of
f
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{c}{2})
. If there exist
x,y\in X
such that

C^{s}(x):=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f^{-n}(C^{s}_{\varepsilon}(f^{n}(x)))\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,C^{u}(y):=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f^{n}(C^{u}_{\varepsilon}(f^{-n}(y)))

are dense in
X
, then
C(f)
does not have the shadowing property.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the existence of stable/unstable continua satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
2.5
. First, note that
C^{s}(x)
and
C^{u}(y)
are connected sets, since they are increasing unions of connected sets, and by hypothesis
\overline{C^{s}(x)}=X=\overline{C^{u}(y)}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, choose
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

d_{H}\left(\overline{C^{s}(x)},\bigcup_{i=1}^{k_{n}}f^{-i}(C^{s}_{\varepsilon}(f^{i}(x)))\right)<\frac{\operatorname{diam}(X)}{n}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}

d_{H}\left(\overline{C^{u}(y)},\bigcup_{i=1}^{k_{n}}f^{i}(C^{u}_{\varepsilon}(f^{-i}(y)))\right)<\frac{\operatorname{diam}(X)}{n}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

S_{n}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k_{n}}f^{-i}(C^{s}_{\varepsilon}(f^{i}(x)))\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,U_{n}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k_{n}}f^{i}(C^{u}_{\varepsilon}(f^{-i}(y))).

Thus,

S_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{s}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,U_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{u}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,n\in\mathbb{N},

d_{H}(S_{n},U_{n})<\frac{2\operatorname{diam}(X)}{n}\to 0\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{when}\,\,\,\,\,\,n\to+\infty

and

\operatorname{diam}(S_{n})>\frac{\operatorname{diam}(X)}{2}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,\operatorname{diam}(U_{n})>\frac{\operatorname{diam}(X)}{2}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,n>1,

concluding the proof.
∎

We are able to prove the hypothesis of this theorem in the case of transitive cw-hyperbolic homeomorphisms.

Definition 2.8

(Continuum-wise hyperbolicity)
.

We say that
f
satisfies the
cw
-local-product-structure if for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

C^{s}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap C^{u}_{\varepsilon}(y)\neq\emptyset\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{ whenever }\,\,\,\,\,\,d(x,y)<\delta.

The
cw
-expansive homeomorphisms satisfying the
cw
-local-product-structure are called
cw
-hyperbolic.

For more information about cw-hyperbolicity, see
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
20
]
.

Definition 2.9

(Transitivity)
.

A map
f\colon X\to X
is called
transitive
, if for any pair
U,V\subset X
of non-empty open subsets, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\emptyset.

The following is the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.10
.

If
f\colon X\to X
is a transitive cw-hyperbolic homeomorphism, then
C(f)
does not have the shadowing property. In particular, if
f\colon M\to M
is a transitive Anosov diffeomorphism, then
C(f)
does not have the shadowing property.

Proof.

We will prove that
C^{s}(x)
and
C^{u}(x)
are dense in
X
for every
x\in X
(the proof follows the proof of the similar result in the case of topologically hyperbolic homeomorphisms
[
39
, Theorem 3.2.6]
). Theorem
2.7
ensures then that
C(f)
does not have the shadowing property. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
f^{n}
is topologically mixing (see
[
7
, Corollary 2.2]
). To prove that
C^{s}(x)
is dense in
X
, we consider any
z\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
, and prove the existence of a point in
C^{s}(x)\cap B(z,2\varepsilon)
. Let
\delta>0
, given by the cw-local-product-structure of
f
, be such that

C^{s}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap C^{u}_{\varepsilon}(y)\neq\emptyset\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{ whenever }\,\,\,\,\,\,d(x,y)<2\delta

and choose a finite set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{j}\}
such that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{j}B(x_{i},\delta)
covers
X
. Since
f^{n}
is topologically mixing, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

f^{nk}(B(z,\varepsilon))\cap B(x_{i},\delta)\neq\emptyset\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,i\in\{1,\dots,j\}.

Since
f^{nk}(x)\in B(x_{i},\delta)
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,j\}
and there exists
w\in f^{nk}(B(z,\varepsilon))\cap B(x_{i},\delta)
, the cw-local-product-structure ensures the existence of
y\in C^{s}_{\varepsilon}(f^{nk}(x))\cap C^{u}_{\varepsilon}(w)
. Thus,

f^{-nk}(y)\in f^{-nk}(C^{s}_{\varepsilon}(f^{nk}(x)))\subset C^{s}(x)

and also

f^{-nk}(y)\in C^{u}_{\varepsilon}(f^{-nk}(w))
, which implies
f^{-nk}(y)\in B(z,2\varepsilon)
. This concludes the proof. The statement for Anosov diffeomorphisms follows noting that any Anosov diffeomorphism is cw-hyperbolic.
∎

We also discuss the relation between transitivity of
f
and of
C(f)
. It is proved in
[
44
]
that
2^{f}
is transitive if, and only if,
f
is weakly mixing, that is,
f\times f
is transitive. The case of
C(f)
is not clear since
C(f)
is not transitive when
f
is defined on dendrites but it is when
f
is the shift map on
[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}}

[
1
]
. We prove that
C(f)
is not transitive when
f
is a cw-expansive homeomorphism of a compact metric space with positive topological dimension.

Theorem 2.11
.

If
f\colon X\to X
is a cw-expansive homeomorphism of a compact metric space with positive topological dimension, then
C(f)
is not transitive.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
[
33
, Proposition 2.2]
which states that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that if a continuum
C
satisfies
\operatorname{diam}(C)\leq\delta
and there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{diam}(f^{k}(C))>\varepsilon
, then

\operatorname{diam}(f^{n}(C))\geq\delta\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,n\geq k.

Let
A\subset X
be a non-trivial continuum with
\varepsilon=\operatorname{diam}(A)>0
(which exists since
X
has positive topological dimension) and choose
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
as in the result of Kato above for this
\varepsilon
. Thus, if a continuum
C\subset X
has a dense future orbit in
\mathcal{C}(X)
, then it has to accumulate at trivial continua of the form
\{x\}
, so there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{diam}(f^{k}(C))<\delta
, and also at the non-trivial continuum
A
, so there exists
k^{\prime}>k
such that
\operatorname{diam}(f^{k^{\prime}}(C))>\varepsilon
. But since the future orbit of
C^{\prime}
accumulates at
\{x\}
, there exists
k^{\prime\prime}>k^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{diam}(f^{k^{\prime\prime}}(C))<\delta
contradicting Kato’s result.
∎

3.
Dendrites and the shadowing property for
C(f)

The goal of this section is two-fold: prove that for a dendrite monotone map
f:D\rightarrow D
,
f
has the shadowing property if and only if
C(f)
does and give algorithms which can be used to show that abundant of dendrites admit homeomorphisms with the shadowing property, including the universal dendrite of order
n
. We begin by stating necessary definitions.

Definition 3.1

(Dendrites)
.

A
dendrite
is a Peano continuum which does not contain a simple closed curve.

Dendrites enjoy a variety of characterizations and properties. We refer the reader to
[
40
, Chapter X]
for further general information on the topic. A characterization that will be useful for us is the following:

Theorem 3.2
.

[
40
, Charactrization of Dendrites, Thm 10.10]

A continuum
X
is a dendrite if, and only if, the intersection of any two connected subsets of
X
is connected.

We will also need pointwise behavior of dendrites, in particular the following definition.

Definition 3.3

(Order of a point)
.

Let
X
be a dendrite and
p\in X
. The
order of
p
in
X
is the number of connected components of
X\setminus\{p\}
. We will say that
p
is a branch point of
X
if the order of
p
in
X
is at least 3.

Each point of a dendrite has a countable order. Moreover, the set of branch points of a dendrite is countable
[
40
, Thm 10.23]
.

Definition 3.4

(Monotone map)
.

A mapping
f:X\rightarrow X
is said to be
monotone
if
f^{-1}(p)
is connected for all
p\in X
. When
X
happens to be a compact metric space and
f
is a surjection, this is equivalent to saying that
f^{-1}(A)
is connected for all connected sets
A\subseteq X

[
40
, Ex 8.46]
.

We refer the reader to
[
40
, Chapter XIII]
for further information on monotone maps. Clearly, every homeomorphism is a monotone map, but we do not restrict ourselves to the case of invertible maps. Thus, it is important to note that in Definition
2.1
of the shadowing property, when
f
is not invertible, we consider pseudo-orbits indexed by the positive integer numbers
\mathbb{N}
while the shadowing orbit is a future orbit. It is a classical result in the shadowing theory that positive shadowing, finite shadowing, and shadowing are equivalent when the underlying space is compact (see
[
45
]
for more details). We also recall the following theorem of Fernandez-Good that will be used in the proof of Theorem
3.6
below.

Theorem 3.5
.

[
28
, Theorem 6]

If
X
is a compact metric space, then
f:X\rightarrow X
has the shadowing property if, and only if,
2^{f}:2^{X}\rightarrow 2^{X}
has the shadowing property.

The following is one of the main results of this section.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
D
be a dendrite and
f:D\rightarrow D
be a monotone map. Then,
f
has the shadowing property if, and only if,
C(f)
does.

Proof.

That the shadowing property for
C(f)
implies the shadowing property for
f
follows from
[
28
, Thm 3.2]
.

Let us now show the converse.
Let
\varepsilon>0
. By the fact that
D
is locally connected, we may choose a finite cover
\mathcal{G}
of
X
consisting of open connected sets such that each
g\in\mathcal{G}
has diameter less than
\varepsilon/4
. Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\frac{\varepsilon}{4})
be a Lebesgue number associated with the open cover
\mathcal{G}
, i.e., if
A\subseteq X
has diameter less than
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, then
A\subseteq g
for some
g\in\mathcal{G}
. By Theorem
3.5
, there is a
\delta>0
which witnesses the fact that
2^{f}
has the shadowing property with respect to
\varepsilon^{\prime}
.
We will show that the same
\delta
witnesses that
C(f)
has the shadowing property with respect to
\varepsilon
. To this end, let
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a
\delta
-pseudo-orbit of
C(f)
in
C(X)
. As
C(X)\subseteq 2^{X}
, we have that
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a pseudo-orbit in
2^{X}
. By our choice of
\delta
, we have that there is a compact set
A\subseteq X
such that

d_{H}(f^{n}(A),K_{n})<\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for every}\,\,\,\,\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

Now, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\{g\in\mathcal{G};g\cap K_{n}\neq\emptyset\}.

We next observe that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
covers
f^{n}(A)
. Indeed, let
x\in f^{n}(A)
. As
d_{H}(f^{n}(A),K_{n})<\varepsilon^{\prime},
there is an open ball
U
with center
x
with diameter less than
\varepsilon^{\prime}
which intersects
K_{n}
. As
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is a Lebesgue number of
\mathcal{G}
, there is
g\in\mathcal{G}
such that
U\subseteq g
. As
g\cap K_{n}\neq\emptyset
, we have that
g\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, implying that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
covers
f^{n}(A)
. Now let

L_{n}=\overline{\bigcup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}g}.

We note that
L_{n}
is a continuum as
K_{n}
is a continuum and each
g\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
is connected. Moreover, we have that

f^{n}(A)\cup K_{n}\subseteq L_{n}\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in K_{n}}B(x,\varepsilon/4),

implying that

\displaystyle d_{H}(f^{n}(A),L_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{H}(f^{n}(A),K_{n})+d_{H}(K_{n},L_{n})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

As
f
is monotone, we have that
f^{-n}(L_{n})
is a continuum containing
A
. By Theorem
3.2
, we have that

K=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f^{-n}(L_{n})

is a continuum containing
A
. Now, as

f^{n}(A)\subseteq f^{n}(K)\subseteq L_{n}\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,d_{H}(f^{n}(A),L_{n})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

we have that
d_{H}(f^{n}(K),f^{n}(A))<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Hence,

\displaystyle d_{H}(f^{n}(K),K_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{H}(f^{n}(K),f^{n}(A))+d_{H}(f^{n}(A),K_{n}))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\varepsilon,

verifying that
K
is a continuum which
\varepsilon
-shadows the pseudo-orbit
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

We now explore which dendrites admit homeomorphisms with the shadowing property.
The simplest possible dendrite is the interval
[0,1]
, so the above result ensures that homeomorphisms of

with shadowing are also examples where

have shadowing. In what follows, let
\mathcal{H}([0,1])
be the set of homeomorphisms
f\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
and
\mathcal{H^{+}}([0,1]),\mathcal{H^{-}}([0,1])
denote the groups of homeomorphisms of

which are increasing and decreasing, respectively. Using
[
8
]
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.7
.

A typical

has the property that

has the shadowing property.

Proof.

It was shown
[
8
]
that there is
h\in\mathcal{H^{+}}([0,1])
whose conjugacy class is comeager and

has the shadowing property. Using a symmetric argument, one can show there is a corresponding such map for
\mathcal{H^{-}}([0,1])
. Hence, we have that a typical

has the shadowing property. Now as

is a dendrite, Theorem

ensures that

has the shadowing property for a typical

.
∎

Given a dendrite
D
, it is well-known that a generic continuous self-map of
D
has the shadowing property (see
[
14
,
34
,
38
]
). Unfortunately, a generic map of the dendrite usually fails to be montone. The hypothesis of

being monotone is important in the proof of Theorem
3.6
and there are dendrites whose only homeomorphism is the identity map. Such dendrites can easily be constructed by making branch points dense in the dendrite and the order of each branch point is distinct. In this article, we prove that a large class of dendrites admit homeomorphisms with the shadowing property. This will follow from a series of propositions we prove below. These propositions will give general methods for constructing dendrites which admit homeomorphisms with the shadowing property from simpler dendrites with such properties. Before we state our results, we first need the following definition. We use use
\mathcal{H}(X)
to denote the group of homeomorphisms of
X
.

Definition 3.8

(Quasi-attractors)
.

Suppose that
X
is a compact metric space and take

.
A compact
f
-invariant subset

is a
quasi-attractor
if for every
open neighborhood
U
of
A

there is an open subset
V\subset U
such that
A\subset V
and

\clos(f(V))\subset V
.
If, in addition,

has the shadowing property we say that

is a

. We say that
A
is a

if it is a quasi-attractor for the inverse map

.

The following proposition
[
7
]
will be useful for us.

Proposition 3.9
.

[
7
, Prop 3]

If every point of

belongs to a quasi-attractor with shadowing, then

has the shadowing property.

The following proposition is a rather well-known fact. It also follows from the characterization of homeomorphisms of the interval with the shadowing property given in
[

]
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Consider

defined by

. Then,

has the shadowing property.

Next we define star union of countably many metric spaces with exactly one point in common.

Definition 3.11

(Star union of metric spaces)
.

Let
((X_{n},d_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of compact metric spaces and

be such that

for all

Note that if

as

is a compact metric space. In particular, we define the

n

\omega

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose
\star(X_{n},p)

and assume that

is a homeomorphism with the shadowing property such that

As

and the

Definition 3.14

Definition 3.18

(Combs)
.

Let
X
be a continuum,
D=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots\}\subset X
be a countable set, and

. We define the
(X,D,n)
-comb
as the following subset of

Above,

denotes the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We endow

with the product metric given by the maximum between the metric on
X
and the metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We note that

is a closed subset of

, and hence a continuum, as

and

are continua. One can think of

as

with a decreasing sequence of stars

attached at each point of

. We note that there is a natural projection map
\Pi:Z\rightarrow X
defined by

.

Definition 3.19
.

We call a dynamical system
(X,f)
simple if it has a quasi-attractor fixed point
p
and a quasi-repeller fixed point
q
such that for all
x\in X\setminus\{p,q\}
we have that
lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f^{n}(x)=p
and
lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f^{-n}(x)=q
.

The following proposition is well-known when
(X,f)
is a simple Morse-Smale diffeomorphism of a manifold
[
41
]
or an increasing/decreasing continuous function of the interval
[
43
]
. We could not find a proof in the general case of homeomorphisms of compact metric spaces, so we include a proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3.20
.

If
(X,f)
is a simple homeomorphism of a compact metric space, then
f
has the shadowing property.

Proof.

Let
(X,f)
be a simple homeomorphism with quasi-attractor fixed point
p
, quasi-repeller fixed point
q
, and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
U_{p},U_{q}
be open sets with diameters less than
\varepsilon/2
containing
p,q
, respectively, such that
\overline{f(U_{p})}\subseteq U_{p}
,
\overline{f^{-1}(U_{q})}\subseteq U_{q}
, and
U_{p}\cap U_{q}=\emptyset
. As
p
is a quasi-attractor fixed point of
f
, for each
x\in X\setminus(U_{p}\cup f^{-1}(U_{q}))
, there is a positive integer
N_{x}
such that
f^{N_{x}}(x)\in f(U_{p})
. Note that for
n>N_{x}
,
f^{n}(x)\in f(U_{p})
. Using the compactness of
X\setminus(U_{p}\cup f^{-1}(U_{q}))
and the continuing of
f
, we may assume that there is a positive integer
N
such that
N_{x}<N
for all
x\in X\setminus(U_{p}\cup f^{-1}(U_{q}))
.

Let

\eta>0
be such that

\eta<1/2\cdot\min\{\varepsilon,d_{H}(\overline{f(U_{p})},X\setminus U_{p}),d_{H}(\overline{f^{-1}(U_{q})},X\setminus U_{q})\}.

Note that if
x\in f(U_{p})
and
y
is
\eta
-close to
x
, then
f(y)\in f(U_{p})
. Analogously, if
x\in f^{-1}(U_{q})
and
y
is
\eta
-close to
x
, then
f^{-1}(y)\in f^{-1}(U_{q})
. Using these observations, we have that if
(x_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is any
\eta
-pseudo-orbit of
f
with
x_{0}\in U_{p}
, then
x_{n}\in U_{p}
for all
n\geq 0
. Analogously, if
(x_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is any
\eta
-pseudo-orbit of
f^{-1}
with
x_{0}\in U_{q}
, we have that
x_{n}\in U_{q}
for all
n\geq 0
.

Let
0<\delta<\eta
be such that if
x_{0}\in X\setminus(U_{p}\cup f^{-1}(U_{q}))
and
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{N}
is any
\delta
-pseudo-orbit of
f
, then
d(x_{j},f^{j}(x_{0}))<\eta
for all
0\leq j\leq N
. This may be done using the uniform continuity of
f,\ldots,f^{N}
. Moreover, we also require that if
d(x,y)<\delta
, then
d(f^{-1}(x),f^{-1}(y))<\eta
.

Now, let
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
be a
\delta
-pseudo-orbit of
f
. We need to show that it is
\varepsilon
-shadowed by a real orbit. If
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}\subseteq U_{p}
, then the fixed point
p

\varepsilon
-shadows
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
. Similarly, if
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}\subseteq U_{q}
, then the fixed point
q

\varepsilon
-shadows
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
. Otherwise, we may choose the least
n\geq 0
such
x_{n}\notin f^{-1}(U_{q})
. As
\delta
is sufficiently small, it follows that
x_{n}\notin U_{p}
. As
d(f(x_{n-1}),x_{n})<\delta
, by our choice of
\delta
we have that
d(x_{n-1},f^{-1}(x_{n}))<\eta
. Since
x_{n-1}\in f^{-1}(U_{q})
, by our choice of
\eta
we have that
f^{-1}(x_{n})\in U_{q}
. Hence, for all
k\geq 1
, we have that
f^{-k}(x_{n})\in U_{q}
. Now it is easy to verify that
f^{-n}(x_{n})

\varepsilon
-shadows
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
. Indeed, for
0\leq j<n
, we have that
x_{j}
and
f^{j}(f^{-n}(x_{n}))
belong to
U_{q}
. For
n\leq j\leq n+N
we have that
d(x_{j},f^{j}(f^{-n}(x_{n})))<\varepsilon
by our choice of
\delta
. Finally, for
j>n+N
, we have that
x_{j}
and
f^{j}(f^{-n}(x_{n}))
both belong to
U_{p}
.
∎

Proposition 3.21
.

Let
X
be a continuum,
D=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots\}\subseteq X
be a countable set, and
1\leq n\leq\omega
. Let
h_{1}\colon X\rightarrow X
be such that
(X,h_{1})
is a simple dynamical system with quasi-attractor and quasi-repeller fixed points
p,q
, respectively, with
p,q\in X\setminus D
, and
h_{1}(D)=D
. Let
h_{2}\colon\operatorname{st_{n}}\rightarrow\operatorname{st_{n}}
be a homeomorphism such that
h_{2}(0)=0
. Then, there is a homeomorphism
h:Z\rightarrow Z
,
Z=(X,D,n)
-comb, such that

•

\Pi\circ h=h_{1}\circ\Pi
where
\Pi:Z\rightarrow X
is the projection map, and

•

(Z,h)
is a simple dynamical system with quasi-attractor and quasi-repeller points
(p,0)
and
(q,0)
, respectively.

Proof.

Define
h:Z\rightarrow Z
on
Z=(X,D,n)
-comb by

h(x,y)=\begin{cases}(h_{1}(x),0)&\textit{ if }x\notin D\\
(h_{1}(x),\frac{1}{j}\cdot h_{2}(i\cdot y))&\textit{ if }x=d_{i},\ h_{1}(x)=d_{j}.\end{cases}

It is easy to check that
\Pi\circ h=h_{1}\circ\Pi
. Moreover, by definition, we have that
\Pi
is a monotone map.

As
p,q
are quasi-attractor and quasi-repeller points of
h_{1}
and for each
x\in D
we have that
\{f^{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is infinite, we have that the diameter of “teeth” attached at
f^{n}(x)
goes to zero when
n\rightarrow\pm\infty
. Hence, we have that
(p,0)
and
(q,0)
are quasi-attractor and quasi-repeller points of
h
, respectively, and that
(Z,h)
is a simple system.

∎

Remark 3.22

Definition 3.24

(Inverse limit systems)
.

Let
((X_{i},h_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence (possibly finite) of topological dynamical systems and for each

\varprojlim\{X_{i},\varphi_{i}\}:=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\in\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}X_{i};\,\,\varphi_{i}(x_{i+1})=x_{i}\right\}

and the induced dynamical system on
\varprojlim\{X_{i},\varphi_{i}\}

h:\varprojlim\{X_{i},\varphi_{i}\}\rightarrow\varprojlim\{X_{i},\varphi_{i}\}

by

h(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)=(h_{1}(x_{1}),h_{2}(x_{2}),\ldots).

We have the following well-known result (see
[
31
,
11
]
for information and generalizations).

Proposition 3.25
.

Suppose we are in the setting above. If each of
h_{i}
has the shadowing property, then so does
h
.

The following is the last result of this article.

Theorem 3.26
.

The universal dendrite
U_{n}
admits a homeomorphism with the shadowing property for every
3\leq n\leq\infty
.

Proof.

Let
Y
be
(n-2)
-star in the plane emanating from the origin
0
. By Corollary
3.13
, there is a homeomorphism
f:Y\rightarrow Y
with the shadowing property such that
f(0)=0
. We will construct
U_{n}
with an induction process which we begin by letting
X_{0}=[0,1]
,
h_{0}:X_{0}\rightarrow X_{0}
be a simple homeomorphism with quasi-attractor and quasi-repeller fixed points
0,1
, and
D_{0}
be a countable dense subset of
(0,1)
such that
h_{0}(D_{0})=D_{0}
.

Suppose we are at step
k\geq 1
, a dendrite
X_{k}
, a simple homeomorphism
h_{k}:X_{k}\rightarrow X_{k}
with quasi-attractor point
p_{k}
and quasi-repeller point
q_{k}
, and a countable dense set
D_{k}\subseteq X_{k}\setminus\{p_{k},q_{k}\}
with
h_{k}(D_{k})=D_{k}
are defined so that

•

the set of branch points of
X_{k}
does not intersect
D_{k}
,

•

each branch point of
X_{k}
has order
n
and

•

each
x\in D_{k}
has order 2 in
X_{k}
.

Applying Proposition
3.21
to
X_{k}
,
h_{k}
,
D_{k}
,
Y
, and
f
, we obtain
X_{k+1}
,
h_{k+1}
, and a surjective map
\varphi_{k}:X_{k+1}\rightarrow X_{k}
such that

•

X_{k+1}=(X_{k},D_{k},n-2)
-comb,

•

h_{k+1}:X_{k+1}\rightarrow X_{k+1}
is a simple homeomorphism with quasi-attractor fixed point
(p_{k},0)
and quasi-repeller fixed point
(q_{k},0)
,

•

\varphi_{k}:X_{k+1}\rightarrow X_{k}
is a monotone map such that
\varphi_{k}\circ h_{k+1}=h_{k}\circ\varphi_{k}
, and

•

each point of
D_{k}\times\{0\}
is a branch point of
X_{k+1}
of order
n
.

We now let
D_{k+1}\subseteq X_{k+1}
be any countable set dense in
X_{k+1}
such that for all
(x,y)\in D_{k+1}
,
x\in D_{k}
,
(x,y)
is a point of order 2 of
X_{k+1}
and
h_{k+1}(D_{k+1})=D_{k+1}
. This can easily be accomplished by choosing a countable set dense in each “tooth” of the comb and closing the union of these sets under the orbit of
h_{k+1}
. This finishes the induction step.

Now we consider the inverse limit space
X:=\varprojlim\{X_{i},\varphi_{i}\}
, a continuum. Indeed, as
\varphi_{i}
’s are monotone, we have that
X
is a dendrite (see
[
40
, Thm 10.36]
).
Now consider the induced map
h\colon X\rightarrow X
given by
h(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)=(h_{1}(x_{1}),h_{2}(x_{2}),\ldots)
. By Proposition
3.20
, we have that each
h_{k}
has the shadowing property. By Proposition
3.25
, we have that
h
is a homeomorphism of
X
with the shadowing property. It only remains to show that
X
is the universal dendrite of order
n
, that is, each branch point of
X
has order
n
and the set of branch points of
X
is dense in
X
.

We let
\Pi_{k}
be the projection of
X
onto the
X_{k}
coordinate. As each
\varphi_{i}
is monotone,
\Pi_{k}
is monotone
[
40
, Ex 8.47]
.
We will first show that each branch point of
X
has order
n
, or, equivalently, the order of each point of
X
is either 1, 2, or
n
. To this end, consider
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in X
. We observe that by construction we have that if
x_{k}\notin D_{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
x_{k+1}=(x_{k},0)
and
x_{k+1}\notin D_{k+1}
. Hence, for such
k
, we have that
x_{l}\notin D_{l}
for every
l\geq k
. Thus, we have two cases to consider, either there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{l}\notin D_{l}
for every
l\geq k
, or
x_{k}\in D_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
In the first case, by hypothesis, we have that
x_{k}
has order
j
in
X_{k}
where
j\in\{1,2,n\}
. As such, we have
X_{k}\setminus\{x_{k}\}
has
j
components, labeled
A_{1},\ldots,A_{j}
. As
\Pi_{k}
is monotone, we have that each
\Pi_{k}^{-1}(A_{i})
is connected in
X
. As
x_{l}\notin D_{l}
for all
l\geq k
, we have that
\varphi_{l}^{-1}(x_{l})
has exactly one element for each
l\geq k
. Hence, we have that

\Pi_{k}^{-1}(x_{k})=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\ldots),

i.e,
X\setminus\{(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\}
is the union of
j
connected sets, namely,
\Pi_{k}^{-1}(A_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq j
. This ensures that
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\ldots)
has order
j
in
X
. Let us now consider the case
x_{k}\in D_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In this case, it suffices to show that
X\setminus\{(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\}
has a component dense in
X
, implying that the order of
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)
is 1 in
X
as
X
is a dendrite. The density follows from the fact that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a component of
X_{k+1}\setminus\{x_{k+1}\}
which contains
X_{k}\times\{0\}
as a subset. This is the case as all elements of
D_{k+1}
are chosen from the “teeth” of
X_{k+1}
.

Finally, to see that the set of branch points of order
n
is dense in
X
, we observe that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x\in D_{k}
,
(x,0)
is a branch point of order
n
of
X_{k+1}
and hence if we consider a point
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in X
such that
x_{k}\in D_{k}
and
x_{l+1}=(x_{l},0)
for all
l\geq k
, then
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in X
is a branch point of order
n
in
X
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Open questions

The examples of systems with shadowing for
C(f)
in this article involve dendrites, one-dimensional continua. All other known results concerning the shadowing property for
C(f)
are negative, leading to the following question.

Question 4.1
.

Does there exist a topological dynamical system
(X,f)
,
X
a continuum with dimension greater than one, such that
C(f)
has the shadowing property?

We still cannot decide whether
C(f)
has the shadowing property in the case of general Morse-Smale diffeomorphisms. The following question was stated in
[
4
]
and is still open:

Question 4.2
.

Does there exist a Morse-Smale diffeomorphism
f\colon M\to M
of a closed manifold such that
C(f)
has the shadowing property?
