An Overview of Asymptotic Normality in Stochastic Blockmodels: Cluster Analysis and Inference
Joshua Agterberg and Joshua Cape

Abstract
This paper provides a selective review of the statistical network analysis literature focused on clustering and inference problems for stochastic blockmodels and their variants. We survey asymptotic normality results for stochastic blockmodels as a means of thematically linking classical statistical concepts to contemporary research in network data analysis. Multiple different forms of asymptotically Gaussian behavior arise in stochastic blockmodels and are useful for different purposes, pertaining to estimation and testing, the characterization of cluster structure in community detection, and understanding latent space geometry. This paper concludes with a discussion of open problems and ongoing research activities addressing asymptotic normality and its implications for statistical network modeling.



1
Introduction

Stochastic blockmodels (SBMs)
((
Holland et al. 1983
;
Wasserman and Faust 1994
)
are generative models for random graphs in which network connectivity is determined by community (block) structure among nodes. In the simplest setting, nodes (vertices) belong to communities, and connectivity, as quantified via the presence or absence of links (edges) between nodes, is probabilistically governed solely by nodes’ community memberships. Stochastic blockmodels and their variants serve as workhorse generative models in the statistical analysis of networks, particularly in the vertex-centric, networks-as-graphs paradigm.

Stochastic blockmodels, instances of which are sometimes referred to as
planted partition models

((
Schaeffer 2007
;
Lancichinetti and Fortunato 2009
)
, have received significant attention within the mathematics, statistics, computer science, and physics research communities, finding applications in social network analysis, neuroimaging, and elsewhere. Popular variants and extensions include mixed membership
((
Airoldi et al. 2008
)
and degree-corrected stochastic blockmodels
((
Karrer and Newman 2011
)
, among numerous others
((
Arroyo and Levina 2022
;
Noroozi et al. 2021b
;
Noroozi et al. 2021a
;
Sengupta and Chen 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
. Historical developments pertaining to the study of networks and SBMs are cataloged in, for example,
Fortunato 2010
;
Fortunato and Hric 2016
;
Lee and Wilkinson 2019
;
Wasserman and Faust 1994
. Stochastic blockmodels have themselves already been the subject of survey-length treatment, notably in
Abbe 2018
which summarizes phase transition phenomena for community recovery. Stochastic blockmodels also arise in survey-length treatments of research focused on dynamic networks
((
Kim et al. 2018
)
as well as latent space modeling and analysis
((
Athreya et al. 2018
;
Matias and Robin 2014
)
, among others
((
Gao and Ma 2021
;
Goldenberg et al. 2009
;
Zhao 2017
)
.

The study of networks at times challenges convention and intuition developed in classical statistical inference settings. Typically, one observes a single or multiple networks, each describing
pairwise interactions
between vertices, in contrast to observing independent Euclidean-valued feature vectors for each sample unit. When modeling network data, several natural questions arise. Do multiple networks (graphs) constitute multiple observations, or are individual vertices within a single network the units of interest? Correspondingly, is the inference task focused on vertex-specific behavior by, for example, seeking to infer individual edge probability parameters, or does one wish to understand the behavior of certain summary statistics computed from the entire network? Further, where or how could asymptotic normality appear, and to what end? Possible answers depend on how statistical units of interest are conceptualized. This survey contributes to the existing literature by providing a unified treatment of asymptotic normality results for stochastic blockmodels, as a means of contextualizing and detailing advances in
statistical network analysis
.

A sizable body of literature considers spectral-based matrix factorization approaches to network analysis by leveraging the convenient representation of networks in terms of their adjacency or Laplacian matrix. Such approaches have led to widespread use of
spectral embeddings
, namely eigendecomposition-based low-dimensional Euclidean representations of vertices in graphs. This survey discusses existing asymptotic normality results for different matrix factorization approaches to network analysis as well as applications of these results to parameter estimation and hypothesis testing. In part, to the extent possible, this survey seeks to elucidate the interplay between underlying matrix structure and asymptotic normality behavior. Loosely speaking, a tug-of-war exists between network sparsity, the antagonist, and network size, the protagonist. The emergence of asymptotic normality depends on the extent of community separation (between-community heterogeneity), sample size, and sparsity, with rate of convergence, covariance matrix, and centering determined by these aspects.

Numerous existing works in the literature address statistical consistency under SBMs and in more general network settings, without addressing asymptotic distributional properties. To maintain focus and a reasonable page count, this survey restricts its attention to only the most closely related works. In particular, this review does not delve into the rich literature on information-theoretical thresholds and phase transition phenomena for random graphs
((
Abbe 2018
)
nor does it consider graphons as graph limit objects
((
Lovász 2012
)
. Many of the results covered herein hold for more general models, and while we aim to mention this when appropriate, we emphasize that the focus herein is deliberately on stochastic blockmodels.

2
Stochastic Blockmodels

This section briefly reviews the basics of stochastic blockmodeling wherein graphs (networks) constitute the observable data. Arising at the confluence of
blockmodeling
and
stochastic modeling
, SBMs date back several decades to at least
Holland et al. 1983
;
Wasserman and Faust 1994
. Significant attention has been devoted to SBMs as concrete instances of more general
latent space network models

((
Hoff et al. 2002
)
.

Definition 1

(Stochastic blockmodel — undirected, hollow)
.

Let
B\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
be a symmetric matrix of connectivity probabilities, let
\rho=\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
, and let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K})^{\top}\in\mathcal{S}_{K-1}
. We say that
A
is the adjacency matrix for a stochastic blockmodel graph on
n
vertices with sparsity term
\rho
if the following hold. First, the block assignment map
\tau:[n]\to[K]
satisfies
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq K
and for each
1\leq i\leq n
. Second, conditional on
\tau
, the upper triangular entries
A_{ij}
are independently generated in the manner

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho B_{\tau(i),\tau(j)})&\textnormal{~for~}i<j,\\
0&\textnormal{~for~}i=j,\\
A_{ji}&\textnormal{~for~}i>j.\end{cases}

In words, vertex
i
belongs to community
l
with probability
\pi_{l}
, and conditional on the community memberships, the probability of an edge occurring between vertices
i
and
j
is determined solely by the community memberships, namely the
(\tau(i),\tau(j))
entry of
B
. Throughout this work, we implicitly assume that
B
has distinct rows which prevents multiple communities from exhibiting identical, indistinguishable connectivity behavior, thereby mitigating a potential source of non-identifiability.

Remark 1

(Presence or absence of self-edges)
.

In
Definition

1
, self-edges (loops) are not permitted (i.e., hollowness is enforced), since
A_{ii}=0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. If instead self-edges are permitted (i.e., hollowness is not enforced), then the main diagonal entries
A_{ii}
are typically taken to be independent random variables satisfying
A_{ii}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho B_{\tau(i),\tau(i)})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Remark 2

(Fixed community memberships and the expected adjacency matrix)
.

When the community memberships are taken to be fixed (nonrandom), the matrix of edge probabilities for generating an adjacency matrix
A
, denoted by
P\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
, can be expressed as
P\equiv\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
provided the matrix
Z\in\{0,1\}^{n\times K}
satisfies
Z_{ij}=1
if and only if
\tau(i)=j
. Depending on whether or not the graph corresponding to
A
is permitted to have self-edges, per
Definition

1
, either
\mathbb{E}[A]=P-\operatorname{diag}(P)
or
\mathbb{E}[A]=P
. In certain instances, the distinction between allowing or forbidding the presence of self-edges has a negligible impact on statistical performance guarantees and procedures, while at other times the distinction cannot be ignored. The latter point is addressed further in subsequent sections when needed.

Irrespective of whether or not self-edges are permitted,
A
can be viewed as a perturbation of the (approximately) low-rank matrix
P
. Here, low-rankness plays a crucial role in the subsequent analysis of spectral embeddings of
A
.

2.1
Generalizations of Blockmodels

The stochastic blockmodel per
Definition

1
permits heterogeneous nodal connectivity across different blocks (communities) but simultaneously imposes stochastic equivalence on all nodes belonging to the same block. In other words, vertices in a SBM graph can be viewed as each possessing a latent attribute that governs edge formation. A more flexible and arguably realistic modeling approach would allow vertices to possess shared characteristics, to various extents, from among a common set of possible characteristics. Enter the
mixed-membership stochastic blockmodel
(MMSBM)
((
Airoldi et al. 2008
)
.

Definition 2

(Mixed-membership stochastic blockmodel — undirected, loopy)
.

We say that
A
is the adjacency matrix for a mixed-membership stochastic blockmodel graph on
n
vertices with sparsity term
\rho=\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
when the upper triangular entries
A_{ij}
are independently generated in the manner

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho\eta_{i}^{\top}B\eta_{j})&\textnormal{~for~}i\leq j,\\
A_{ji}&\textnormal{~for~}i>j,\end{cases}

where
B\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
is a symmetric matrix of connectivity probabilities and
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
is a collection of non-negative mixed membership vectors
\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
satisfying
\|\eta_{i}\|_{1}=1
. The unobserved vectors
\eta_{i}
could be specified as deterministic or could be modeled in a generative fashion, for example, i.i.d.
\eta_{i}\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\alpha)
for some concentration parameter vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
.

The MMSBM requires that the membership vectors
\eta_{i}
belong to the unit simplex and
reduces to the SBM when all nodes in the graph are
pure nodes
, namely when their mixed membership vectors are all standard basis vectors (i.e., the vertices of the unit simplex). A different approach to generalizing SBMs is to permit further node-specific connectivity properties, modeled by permitting heterogeneous vertex degree parameters interpretable as a measure of importance in a network, at the expense of introducing additional parameters and model complexity. Thus arises the
degree-corrected stochastic blockmodel
(DCSBM)
((
Dasgupta et al. 2004
;
Karrer and Newman 2011
)
.

Definition 3

(Degree-corrected stochastic blockmodel — undirected, hollow)
.

We say that
A
is the adjacency matrix for a degree-corrected stochastic blockmodel graph on
n
vertices with sparsity term
\rho=\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
when the upper triangular entries
A_{ij}
are independently generated in the manner

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho\theta_{i}\theta_{j}B_{\tau(i),\tau(j)})&\textnormal{~for~}i<j,\\
0&\textnormal{~for~}i=j,\\
A_{ji}&\textnormal{~for~}i>j,\end{cases}

where
B\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
is a symmetric matrix of connectivity probabilities,
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})^{\top}
is a vector of node-specific nonnegative degree parameters, and
(\tau(1),\dots,\tau(n))^{\top}
is the vector of node block labels. The unobserved degree parameters
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
could be specified as deterministic or could alternatively be modeled in a generative fashion, such as i.i.d.
\theta_{i}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}[a,b]
.

Further generalizations beyond those discussed above include but are not limited to the
popularity-adjusted stochastic blockmodel
and the
degree-corrected mixed-membership blockmodel

((
Jin 2015
;
Karrer and Newman 2011
;
Sengupta and Chen 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
as well as
bipartite stochastic blockmodels
and
directed stochastic blockmodels
. For a more in-depth account of these different models, see for example
Noroozi and Pensky 2022
.

The aforementioned models can be viewed as particular instances of the
generalized random dot product graph

Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
if one imposes a distribution on the underlying parameters (such as the degree corrections or mixed memberships). In all cases,
P
is (approximately) low-rank, which facilitates the analysis of spectral algorithms.
Definition

5
provides further discussion and details.

3
Community Detection in Stochastic Blockmodels

In network analysis, the problem of
community detection
is to cluster vertices or nodes into
communities
according to their connectivity properties. For stochastic blockmodels, in which nodes belong to latent ground-truth communities or blocks, link connection probabilities are determined solely by their community memberships. Traditionally, edges are observed in stochastic blockmodel graphs, whereas nodes’ community memberships and their connectivity probabilities are unobserved, hence the impetus for community detection. As previously remarked, there are at least two competing aspects at play. One aspect is the number of vertices, a measure of sample size, whereby having more vertices leads to observing more pairwise edges which improves inference and community recovery, while the second aspect is the level of global network sparsity, determining how many edges are present in the graph, whereby the presence of few edges amounts to sparsely observed data, making it difficult or even impossible to recover communities, say, better than chance. Various approaches exist for addressing the community detection problem in networks, including but not limited to maximum likelihood estimation, modularity maximization, and spectral clustering. Beyond the present paper, a discussion of these and other methods can be found in the existing literature. See
Zhao 2017
for a theoretically focused survey, and see
Fortunato 2010
for a discussion of practical considerations.

Matrix factorization approaches to community detection are frequently framed as two-step procedures to obtain estimated community memberships. First, starting with an
n\times n
(similarity) matrix
S
, such as an observed adjacency matrix, obtain an
n\times d
spectral embedding of
S
by taking the
d\ll n
leading eigenvectors, perhaps scaled by their eigenvalues, and second, cluster the
n
rows of this spectral embedding using a clustering method, such as
K
-means.
Algorithm

1
provides a representative example of spectral clustering algorithms but is by no means exhaustive. For a tutorial on spectral clustering and extensive additional discussion, see
von Luxburg 2007
.

The term
spectral embedding
refers to obtaining a low-dimensional representation of the matrix
S
, often through its
spectral decomposition
, namely its eigenvalues and eigenvectors. We will survey different procedures for obtaining spectral embeddings and their statistical properties. Notably, different spectral embeddings lead to different-yet-related forms of asymptotic normality as quantified through the centering, scaling, and associated asymptotic covariance matrices.
Section

3.1
discusses the properties of spectral embeddings obtained from the population-level probability matrix with fixed community memberships, and
Section

3.2
addresses eigenvector estimation and spectral embeddings obtained from observed adjacency matrices, focusing on consistent estimation of population leading eigenvectors. Discussion of consistent eigenvector estimation and clustering serves as a stepping stone for the presentation of asymptotic normality results in
Section

4

4.6

3.1
Population Properties of Spectral Embeddings

For succinctness and pertinence, this section includes only the adjacency and Laplacian input variants to
Algorithm

1
for which asymptotic normality is discussed in
Section

4
. Here and in the remainder of
Section

3
, we consider community memberships to be fixed
a priori
, with
K
communities and each community
1\leq k\leq K
having size
n_{k}
. In other words, we condition on the community memberships and associated community sizes.

Consider the population adjacency matrix, that is, the matrix
\mathbb{E}[A]=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}=P
whose independent upper triangular entries satisfy
\mathbb{P}[A_{ij}=1]=P_{ij}
for all
i\leq j
. Self-edges are permitted for simplicity, and the surrounding discussion is not materially different for this section if self-edges are disallowed. Recall that
Z
is the
n\times K
matrix whose entries satisfy
Z_{ik}=1
when vertex
i
belongs to community
k
, and zero otherwise. Write the (skinny) eigendecomposition
P=U\Lambda U^{\top}
, where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is a matrix whose columns are orthonormal eigenvectors for
P
, and
\Lambda
is the
d\times d
diagonal matrix whose main diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of
P
. If each community has at least one vertex, then the rank of
P
is
d=\operatorname{rank}(B)
. Consequently, the eigenvectors of
P
reveal the population-level community memberships as follows.

Lemma 1

(Restatement of Lemma 2.1 of
Lei and Rinaldo 2015
)
.

Suppose
B\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
is rank
K
, and let
U
be the leading
K
eigenvectors of
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
. There exists a
K\times K
matrix
R
such that
U=ZR
. Furthermore, in Euclidean norm,
\|R_{k*}-R_{l*}\|=\sqrt{n_{k}^{-1}+n_{l}^{-1}}
for all
k\neq l
.

Given two vertices
i
and
j
, it follows that the row differences of
U
satisfy

\displaystyle\|U_{i*}-U_{j*}\|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sqrt{n_{\tau(i)}^{-1}+n_{\tau(j)}^{-1}}&\text{~if~}\tau(i)\neq\tau(j),\\
0&\text{~if~}\tau(i)=\tau(j).\end{cases}

(1)

When
B
is instead rank-degenerate, the row differences can at times be shown to satisfy a similar property.

Lemma 2

(Restatement of Lemma 2 of
Zhang et al. 2022b
)
.

Suppose that
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, where
B
satisfies

\displaystyle\min_{k,l}\left\{B_{kk}+B_{ll}-2B_{kl}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\eta>0.

If
\tau(i)\neq\tau(j)
, then

\displaystyle\|U_{i*}-U_{j*}\|\geq\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{n}\eta}{\lambda_{\max}(P)}}.

(2)

More general conditions for rank-degenerate matrices
B
are possible such that a lower bound of the form
Eq.

2
holds. See Lemma 1 of
Zhang et al. 2022b
for further details. If
B
is fixed as
n\to\infty
, then
\lambda_{\max}(P)\asymp n\rho_{n}
and
\eta\asymp 1
, so in this regime the bound is of order
n^{-1/2}
, which matches the lower bound in
Lemma

1
for full-rank matrices
B
.

In either the full rank or rank-degenerate case, there are
K
unique rows in
U
corresponding to each of the different communities. Consequently, there are only
K
unique rows of the matrix
U|\Lambda|^{1/2}
, hence
U|\Lambda|^{1/2}=Z\widetilde{R}
for some
K\times d
matrix
\widetilde{R}
with unique rows corresponding to community memberships.

An alternative approach is to consider the normalized Laplacian matrix, with population version given by
\mathcal{L}(P)=(\operatorname{diag}(P1))^{-1/2}

P(\operatorname{diag}(P1))^{-1/2}
.
The normalized Laplacian has a long history in spectral graph theory, capturing relationships between spectral matrix properties and graph connectivity
((
Chung and Graham 1997
)
. Alternatively,
I-(\operatorname{diag}(P1))^{-1/2}P(\operatorname{diag}(P1))^{-1/2}
, but this formulation simply shifts the eigenvalues without affecting the eigenspaces. Writing
\mathcal{L}(ZBZ^{\top})
with eigendecomposition
U^{(L)}\Lambda^{(L)}(U^{(L)})^{\top}
, the following result provides an analogue of
Lemma

1
for the normalized Laplacian.

Lemma 3

(Lemma 3.1 of
Rohe et al. 2011
)
.

If
B
is full rank, then there exists
R^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
such that
U^{(L)}=ZR^{(L)}
.

The Euclidean norm of the difference between the rows of the matrix
R^{(L)}
is not explicitly given above, but examining the proof, matching notation, and comparing it to
Lemma

1
reveals that
Eq.

1
holds. Namely,
\|R_{k*}^{(L)}-R_{l*}^{(L)}\|=\sqrt{n_{k}^{-1}+n_{l}^{-1}}
. Hence, for vertices
i
and
j
,

\displaystyle\|U_{i*}^{(L)}-U_{j*}^{(L)}\|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sqrt{n_{\tau(i)}^{-1}+n_{\tau(j)}^{-1}}&\textnormal{~if~}\tau(i)\neq\tau(j),\\
0&\textnormal{~if~}\tau(i)=\tau(j).\end{cases}

(3)

In summary, knowledge of the eigenvector matrix
U^{(L)}
is again sufficient to recover the community memberships, since there are
K
distinct rows of the eigenvector matrix
U^{(L)}
and repeated rows corresponds to the same community. As with the population adjacency matrix, directly examining the proof also reveals that
U^{(L)}|\Lambda^{(L)}|^{1/2}=Z\widetilde{R}^{(L)}
, where
\widetilde{R}^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
.

The above observations together show that the spectral embedding of each population (expected) similarity matrix reveals community information, namely that the population spectral embedding is a fixed transformation of the community membership matrix
Z
. Provided the empirical similarity matrices approximate their population counterparts, in a sense to be quantified in the following subsection, then the corresponding empirical spectral embeddings will approximate their population counterparts.

3.2
Subspace Perturbation Approach to Community Detection

Several concepts of consistent community detection are studied in the literature on large graphs.
Partial recovery
refers to recovering vertex memberships better than chance with probability tending to one.
Weak recovery
refers to recovering all but a vanishing fraction of vertex memberships with probability tending to one.
Exact recovery
refers to (asymptotically) recovering all vertex memberships exactly with probability tending to one. For additional context,
Gao and Ma 2021
surveys minimax rates of estimation and community detection in networks, while
Abbe 2018
surveys the information-theoretic and computational limits of community detection. The present survey focuses on the exact recovery regime which admits a rich landscape of asymptotic normality results.

The spectral embeddings of population-level similarity matrices distinguish community memberships, so consistency can be achieved provided that the empirical spectral embeddings are sufficiently close to their respective population counterparts. We will first focus on the setting where the spectral embedding is the
n\times d
matrix of leading
d
eigenvectors of the similarity matrix, though the underlying methods used to prove some of the results here can also be used to derive results for scaled eigenvectors.

Below, let
\widehat{U}
denote the leading eigenvectors computed from an observed, generic similarity matrix, and let
U
denote the leading eigenvectors of the underlying population similarity matrix. The population and empirical eigenvectors of the adjacency matrix are of dimension
n\times d
, where
d
is the rank of
B
with
d\ll n
, where
d\leq K
though
B
need not be full rank. Consequently, the matrix of eigenvectors grows in size as
n
increases, therefore the convergence of empirical eigenvectors must be defined with respect to an appropriate metric. Since orthogonal projection matrices uniquely represent orthogonal projections onto lower-dimensional subspaces, metrics on projection matrices offer a natural distance between subspaces. At the same time, cluster information is contained in the rows of the eigenvector matrix
U
. It is therefore natural to consider
\inf_{W\in\mathbb{O}(d)}\|\widehat{U}-UW\|_{\eta}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\eta}
denotes a (often unitarily invariant) norm on matrices such as the spectral or Frobenius norm, and
\mathbb{O}(d)
denotes the set of
d\times d
orthogonal matrices, namely square matrices
W
satisfying
WW^{\top}=I_{d}
. For both the spectral norm and Frobenius norm, classical matrix perturbation theory
Bhatia 1997
provides upper and lower bounds of the form

\displaystyle c_{\eta}\|\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-UU^{\top}\|_{\eta}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{W\in\mathbb{O}(d)}\|\widehat{U}-UW\|_{\eta}\leq C_{\eta}\|\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-UU^{\top}\|_{\eta},

(4)

where
c_{\eta}
and
C_{\eta}
are universal constants depending only on the choice of norm
\eta
. For example,
c_{\operatorname{F}}=1/\sqrt{2}
and
C_{\operatorname{F}}=1
. See Lemma 1 in
Cai and Zhang 2018
or Lemma 2.1.3 in
Chen et al. 2021
.
Of note, these distances are also closely related to the
\sin\Theta
distances between subspaces, which are defined via
\|\sin\Theta(\widehat{U},U)\|_{\eta}=\|({I}-UU^{\top})\widehat{U}\|_{\eta}
. Similar to (
4
), classical matrix perturbation theory shows the equivalence between
\sin\Theta
distances and the metrics above. In the setting herein, all of these metrics are equivalent up to constant factors, meaning they generate the same metric topology on the set of
K
-dimensional subspaces.

The Frobenius and spectral norm differences between
\widehat{U}
and
U
quantify overall error. Bounding these differences can be shown to yield weak recovery (also called weak consistency) in certain SBMs when using
(1+\epsilon)
approximate
k
-means clustering, based on the paradigm in
Lei and Rinaldo 2015
. By employing bounds of the above form,
Lei and Rinaldo 2015
proves that a vanishing fraction of vertices are misclustered with high probability for certain large SBM graphs.

To establish strong recovery or perfect clustering, one requires uniform control over the rows of the difference between
\widehat{U}
and
U
. Such uniform control can be established using the
\ell_{2,\infty}
norm and the entrywise
\max
norm. Traditionally, the
\ell_{2,\infty}
norm of a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is defined as
\|M\|_{2,\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{\|x\|_{2}=1}\|Mx\|_{\infty}\equiv\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|M_{i*}\|
, i.e., the maximum row-wise Euclidean norm. Consequently, numerous works seek to obtain high-probability bounds for the expression

\displaystyle\inf_{W\in\mathbb{O}(d)}\|

\displaystyle\widehat{U}-UW\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=\inf_{W\in\mathbb{O}(d)}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|(\widehat{U}-UW)_{i*}\|

(5)

which is typically achieved indirectly by bounding the proxy quantity

\displaystyle\|\widehat{U}-UW^{\star}\|_{2,\infty},\quad

\displaystyle\text{~where~}\quad W^{\star}=\underset{W\in\mathbb{O}(d)}{\arg\inf}\|\widehat{U}-UW\|_{\operatorname{F}}.

Above,
W^{\star}
denotes the solution to the orthogonal Procrustes problem under the Frobenius norm.

The recent monograph
Chen et al. 2021
presents a broadly applicable entrywise bound which is restated here in the particular setting of stochastic blockmodels.

Theorem 1

(Specialization of Theorem 4.2.1 in
Chen et al. 2021
for SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
B={\nu\nu}^{\top}
for some
K\times d
matrix
\nu
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
and that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq l,k\leq K
. Suppose
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, and let
\widehat{U}
and
U
denote the leading eigenvectors of
A
and
P
, respectively. There exists an orthogonal matrix
W
such that with probability exceeding
1-O(n^{-5})
,

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|(\widehat{U}-UW)_{i*}\|=O\left(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(n)}}{n\sqrt{\rho_{n}}}\right).

Obtaining the above result follows from matching notation, observing that the eigenvalues of
\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
grow at order
n\rho_{n}
, noting that the standard deviation of each entry is bounded by
\rho_{n}^{1/2}
, and the fact that
U
is
\mu
-incoherent with
\mu=O(1)
when
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
(see
Eq.

1
). A slightly stronger result can also be stated which yields exact recovery down to the optimal information-theoretic threshold including explicit constants; see Theorem 4.5.1 in
Chen et al. 2021
.

The sparsity term
\rho_{n}
is used to control graph density, balanced against graph size, where often
\rho_{n}\to 0
but
n\rho_{n}\to\infty
. If
n\rho_{n}\ll\log(n)
, then it is well-known that the adjacency matrix will no longer concentrate about
P
in spectral norm due to disconnectedness of the underlying graph, whereas if
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
, then
\|A-P\|\lesssim\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}
with high probability (see
Lei and Rinaldo 2015
or Remark 3 of
Bandeira and Handel 2016
. See also
Lu and Peng 2013
for results concerning the Laplacian). Furthermore, the information-theoretic limit to achieve perfect clustering is in the regime
n\rho_{n}\asymp\log(n)
with explicit constant determined in some important special cases
((
Abbe 2018
)
.

A host of works have studied eigenvector perturbations in statistical models, often in the context of network analysis
((
Abbe et al. 2020
;
Abbe et al. 2022
;
Agterberg et al. 2022
;
Cai et al. 2021
;
Cape et al. 2019a
;
Cape et al. 2019c
;
Chaudhuri et al. 2012
;
Eldridge et al. 2018
;
Jin et al. 2023
;
Lei 2019
;
Lei et al. 2020b
;
Rohe and Zeng 2023
;
Sarkar and Bickel 2015
;
Yan et al. 2024
;
Zhang and Tang 2022
)
. For the setting of stochastic blockmodels with fixed
B
matrix, these results all translate to similar statistical consequences. If
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
k,l
, and
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
, then the row-wise error between
\widehat{U}
and
U
, after proper orthogonal alignment, is exceedingly small. Since the rows of
U
reveal the community memberships per
Eq.

1
, these results suggest exact community recovery with probability tending rapidly to one for sufficiently dense graphs, namely when
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
. Moreover, consistent estimation of the
i
-th row of
U
uniformly over all rows
1\leq i\leq n
paves the way for further downstream inference and cluster analysis.

4
Asymptotic Normality in Stochastic Blockmodels

Asymptotic normality provides a more refined characterization of cluster geometry and spectral embeddings beyond consistency. Further, distributional theory is conventionally a precursor of inference.

4.1
Asymptotic Normality of the Adjacency Matrix Eigenvectors

We begin by highlighting row-wise asymptotic normality for the
n\times K
matrix
\widehat{U}
of leading orthonormal eigenvectors of the stochastic blockmodel adjacency matrix
A
.

Theorem 2

(Restatement of Theorem 4.4 in
Xie 2024
for positive semidefinite SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
B={\nu}{\nu}^{\top}
for some
K\times d
matrix
{\nu}
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
and that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l\leq K
. Suppose
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, and let
\widehat{U}
and
U
denote the leading eigenvectors of
A
and
P
, respectively. Suppose further that there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that
\min_{1\leq i\leq n}(n\rho_{n})^{-1}\sum_{j}P_{ij}\geq\delta
. Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{(n)}(k)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Delta_{n}^{-3/2}\Gamma^{(k)}_{n}\Delta_{n}^{-3/2},

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{K}\frac{n_{l}}{n}{\nu}_{l*}{\nu}_{l*}^{\top},\qquad\Gamma^{(k)}_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{K}\frac{n_{l}}{n}B_{kl}(1-\rho_{n}B_{kl}){\nu}_{l*}{\nu}_{l*}^{\top}.

Then, there exist sequences of orthogonal matrices
\widetilde{W}
and
W
depending on
n
and a constant
C>0
depending only on
d
such that, for each fixed index
i
, for any convex measurable set
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that for
n
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{P}\bigg\{n\rho_{n}^{1/2}\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{(n)}(k)\big)^{-1/2}\widetilde{W}^{\top}

\displaystyle\big((\widehat{U}W)_{i*}-U_{i*}\big)\in\mathcal{A}\bigg\}-\mathbb{P}\{G\in\mathcal{A}\}\bigg|\leq\frac{C\log(n\rho_{n})}{(n\rho_{n})^{1/2}}

where
G
denotes a
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector.

Theorem

2
establishes that the rows of the empirical eigenvectors
\widehat{U}
are approximately Gaussian about the rows of
U
and with covariance determined by
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{(n)}(k)
. The rate of convergence of the cumulative distribution functions clarifies the role of
n\rho_{n}
as the effective sample size. For classical finite-dimensional Berry–Esseen theorems, the rate of convergence to the Gaussian cumulative distribution function is of order
n^{-1/2}
, whereas in
Theorem

2
, it is of order
(n\rho_{n})^{-1/2}
modulo logarithmic terms. Of note, the full result in
Xie 2024
is more general, permitting positive semidefinite
P
matrices outside of blockmodels, and allowing both
d
and
B
to change with
n
.

A key property of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(n)}_{U}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is that it depends only on the membership
\tau(i)=k
, the community probabilities
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and the block probability matrix
B
. Per
Section

3.1
, there are at most
K
unique values of
U_{i*}
, and here, each distinct row
U_{i*}
gives rise to a potentially different block-specific covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(n)}_{U}(k)
. Therefore, unconditionally, the random vector
\widehat{U}_{i*}
asymptotically behaves like a mixture of Gaussians with component distributions associated to the different community centroids. In particular, when the communities are stochastically generated according to
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
, then the matrices
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
and
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{(k)}_{n}
in
Theorem

2
have well-defined limits with probability one by the law of large numbers.
Theorem

2
generalizes an earlier result in
Cape et al. 2019a
which is applicable to slightly denser SBMs and directly assumes that that
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
and
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{(k)}_{n}
have well-defined limits, hence so too does
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{(n)}(k)
. More recently, further generalization is available in
Xie and Zhang 2024
.

When all community sizes are comparable (which holds when relative community membership proportions are held fixed), then per
Eqs.

1
and
2
, the row norms of the population eigenvectors all necessarily converge to zero and at the same rate, thereby reflecting
incoherence

((
Chen et al. 2021
)
. This shrinking-to-zero property is reflected in the scaling
n\rho_{n}^{1/2}
required to obtain a Gaussian limit. Informally, this scaling decomposes as the product
n^{1/2}\times(n\rho_{n})^{1/2}
, where the term
n^{1/2}
accounts for incoherence and the term
(n\rho_{n})^{1/2}
is the square root of the order of each node’s expected degree. In the absence of sparsity, namely in the fully dense regime
\rho_{n}\equiv 1
, the classical
n^{1/2}
parametric rate is obtained after adjusting for the eigenvector incoherence.

The
k
-th entry of the
i
-th row of
\widehat{U}
is expressible as the inner product of the
k
-th eigenvector
\widehat{U}_{*k}
with the standard basis vector
e_{i}
, namely
e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{U}e_{k}=e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{U}_{*k}
. Recently,
Fan et al. 2022a
develops asymptotic theory for general linear forms
x^{\top}\widehat{U}_{*k}
where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a user-specified deterministic unit vector. Theorem 2 therein establishes that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s_{x,k}}\bigg\{t_{k}\bigg(x^{\top}\widehat{U}_{*k}-\mathbf{A}_{x,k,t_{k}}\mathcal{P}_{k,t_{k}}^{1/2}\bigg)-\mathbb{E}\left[(b_{x,k,t_{k}}-x^{\top}U_{*k}U_{*k}^{\top})(A-\mathbb{E}[A])U_{*k}\right]\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(6)

where

\displaystyle s_{x,k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\left[(b_{x,k,t_{k}}^{\top}-x^{\top}U_{*k}U_{*k}^{\top})(A-\mathbb{E}[A])U_{*k}\right]

with
b_{x,k,t_{k}}
,
\mathbf{A}_{x,k,t_{k}}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{k,t_{k}}
defined according to population parameters and the additional terms
x
,
k
, and
t_{k}
. Explicitly,
t_{k}
solves the fixed-point equation

\displaystyle 1+\lambda_{k}\big\{\mathcal{R}(U_{*k},U_{*k},z)-\mathcal{R}(U_{*k},U_{-k},z)\big[\Lambda_{-k}^{-1}+\mathcal{R}(U_{-k},U_{-k},z)\big]^{-1}\mathcal{R}(U_{-k},U_{*k},z)\big\}\overset{\textnormal{set}}{=}0

(7)

over an interval
z\in[a_{k},b_{k}]
that contains
\lambda_{k}
but no other eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}[A]
, thereby precluding the possibility of repeated eigenvalues. Here,
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(M_{1},M_{2},z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\sum_{l=0,l\neq 1}^{L}z^{-(l+1)}M_{1}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[(A-P)^{l}\right]M_{2},

where
L
denotes a sufficiently large integer per the proof, and where
U_{-k}
and
\Lambda_{-k}
denote the matrices
U
and
\Lambda
but with the entries associated to the
k
-th eigenvector and eigenvalue removed, respectively. See
Section

4.5
for further discussion of the quantity
t_{k}
. Lemma 3 of
Fan et al. 2022a
shows that
t_{k}
exists, is unique, and satisfies
t_{k}/\lambda_{k}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. The definitions of
b_{x,k,t_{k}}
,
\mathbf{A}_{x,k,t_{k}}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{k,t_{k}}
appearing in
Eq.

6
are involved yet in special cases admit simple asymptotic expressions. Namely, take
x=U_{*k}
, in which case the following simplification describes the cosines of the angles between the empirical and population eigenvectors.

Theorem 3

(Restatement of Part 2 of Theorem 2 in
Fan et al. 2022a
for SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
B
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{\eta}
for some constant
\eta>0
and that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l\leq K
. Suppose
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, and let
\widehat{U}
and
U
denote the leading eigenvectors of
A
and
P
, respectively. Suppose further that
P
has
K
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{K}
satisfying
\min_{i\neq j}|\lambda_{i}|/|\lambda_{j}|\geq 1+c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
for
\lambda_{i}\neq-\lambda_{j}
. Let
t_{k}
be the solution to the fixed-point equation in
Eq.

7
.
Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{2t_{k}^{2}\left(U_{*k}^{\top}\widehat{U}_{*k}+A_{U_{*k},k,t_{k}}\mathcal{P}_{k,t_{k}}^{1/2}\right)}{\operatorname{Var}\left[U_{*k}^{\top}(A-P)^{2}U_{*k}\right]^{1/2}}\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where the centering term has the expansion

\displaystyle A_{U_{*k},k,t_{k}}\mathcal{P}_{k,t_{k}}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=-1+\frac{1}{2t_{k}^{2}}U_{*k}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[(A-P)^{2}\right]U_{*k}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{(n\rho_{n})^{3/2}}\bigg).

Theorem

3
demonstrates that the cosines of the angles between empirical and population eigenvectors are asymptotically Gaussian with mean one minus a bias term stemming from second-order moment properties via
U_{*k}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[(A-P)^{2}\right]U_{*k}
and variance determined by fourth-order moment properties via
\operatorname{Var}\left[U_{*k}^{\top}(A-P)^{2}U_{*k}\right]
. The presentation in
Theorem

3
differs from the original statement in
Fan et al. 2022a
but holds by observing that the eigenvalues of
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
are of order
n\rho_{n}
when
B
is fixed, the parameter
\alpha_{n}
appearing in
Fan et al. 2022a
is of order
(n\rho_{n})^{1/2}
, and that the eigenvectors
U_{*k}
are incoherent when
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
. Of note,
Fan et al. 2022a
covers other situations, including demonstrating different asymptotic phenomena based on whether the deterministic vector
x
is incoherent.

To elucidate
Theorem

3
, we remark that Lemma 3 of
Fan et al. 2022a
together with the present assumptions implies that
t_{k}
is of order
n\rho_{n}
. Furthermore, by a slight modification of Remark 1 in
Fan et al. 2022a
, it follows that the asymptotic variance quantity
\operatorname{Var}[U_{*k}^{\top}(A-P)^{2}U_{*k}]
is of order
n\rho_{n}
. Thus, the variance quantity satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Var}\big[U_{*k}^{\top}(A-P)^{2}U_{*k}\big]}{4t_{k}^{4}}\asymp\frac{n\rho_{n}}{(n\rho_{n})^{4}}=\frac{1}{(n\rho_{n})^{3}}.

Informally,
U_{*k}^{\top}\widehat{U}_{*k}-1
is approximately Gaussian with bias of order
(n\rho_{n})^{-1}
and variance of order
(n\rho_{n})^{-3}
.

By
Eq.

4
, the eigenvector difference
\widehat{U}-UW
is closely related to the projection norm difference, and hence, its distributional asymptotics are closely related to those of the matrix
\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-UU^{\top}
. The theory in
Fan et al. 2022a
reflects this relationship, namely the limit results therein for the linear form
x^{\top}\widehat{U}_{*k}
are based on first developing asymptotic expansions for the term
x^{\top}(\widehat{U}_{*k}\widehat{U}_{*k}^{\top}-U_{*k}U_{*k}^{\top})y
for sequences of deterministic unit vectors
x
and
y
, which is the bilinear form determined by the difference of projections onto the
k
-th dominant eigenspace, in contrast to the row-wise focus jointly for the collection of leading eigenvectors in
Theorem

2
.

The results in
Fan et al. 2022a
permit
P
to have negative eigenvalues but require that the eigenvalues be distinct so as to explicitly quantify the fluctuations of the individual eigenvectors
\widehat{U}_{*k}
. While
Theorem

2
also requires that
P
is positive semidefinite, it permits repeated eigenvalues, hence it can only quantify the fluctuations of the rows (as opposed to entries) of the matrix of leading eigenvectors
\widehat{U}
up to orthogonal transformation (as opposed to individual eigenvectors
\widehat{U}_{*k}
). The results in
Fan et al. 2022a
require the sparsity condition
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{\eta}
for some (fixed)
\eta>0
, whereas the results in
Theorem

2
require
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
.

Rather than investigating the rows or entries of
\widehat{U}
or
\widehat{U}_{*k}\widehat{U}_{*k}^{\top}
,
Li and Li 2018
considers the asymptotic behavior of a Frobenius norm difference statistic between
\widehat{U}
and
U
, modulo orthogonal transformation. For balanced homogeneous stochastic blockmodels, simple analytical expressions hold for the centering and scaling terms, and the overall expression is closely related to the
\sin\Theta
distance.

Theorem 4

(Restatement of Theorem 3 in
Li and Li 2018
)
.

Fix
K
, and suppose that the
K\times K
matrix
B
has the form

\displaystyle B_{kl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a&\textnormal{~if~}k=l;\\
b&\textnormal{~if~}k\neq l,\end{cases}

where
0<b<a<1
are constants. Assume that
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim n^{1/2+\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Suppose
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, and let
\widehat{U}
and
U
denote the leading eigenvectors of
A
and
P
, respectively. Further suppose that each community has precisely
n/K
vertices. Then, there exists a sequence of orthogonal matrices
W
such that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{Kn}\left\|\left(\widehat{U}W-U\right)\Lambda\right\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{2}-\mu(a,b)}{\sigma(a,b)}\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\mu(a,b)
and
\sigma(a,b)
are given by

\displaystyle\mu(a,b)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\rho_{n}b(1-b\rho_{n})+\frac{\rho_{n}a(1-a\rho_{n})-\rho_{n}b(1-b\rho_{n})}{K},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(a,b)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{2}{nK}\bigg[(b\rho_{n}(1-b\rho_{n}))^{2}+\frac{(a\rho_{n}(1-a\rho_{n}))^{2}-(b\rho_{n}(1-b\rho_{n}))^{2}}{K}\bigg].

In fact,
Li and Li 2018
allows
a
and
b
to change with
n
, though this is omitted above for ease of presentation. Whether
Theorem

4
can be generalized to the setting of general
B
matrices and sparsity of order
O(n^{\eta})
for
0<\eta\leq 1/2
, or even
n\rho_{n}=O(\log(n))
, remains an open problem.

The random variable
\|(\widehat{U}W-U)\Lambda\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{2}
can be viewed as a columnwise re-weighted Frobenius
\sin\Theta
distance with respect to the magnitude of the eigenvalues. While
Theorem

3
demonstrates that the cosines of the eigenvectors between
\widehat{U}_{*k}
and
U_{*k}
have an asymptotically normal distribution after appropriate centering and scaling, the results of
Fan et al. 2022a
do not imply those in
Li and Li 2018
. In particular, the former result requires distinct eigenvalues, whereas the latter model setting gives rise to repeated population eigenvalues. Yet, both theorems reflect similar asymptotic normality properties for the (rescaled) sines or cosines between the empirical and true eigenvectors, suggesting that extensions to the regime
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\operatorname{polylog}(n)
or even
n\rho_{n}\asymp\log(n)
might be possible.

4.2
Asymptotic Normality of the Scaled and Laplacian Eigenvectors

Another graph embedding approach is to scale the leading eigenvectors by the square roots of the corresponding eigenvalue magnitudes. This scaling has a convenient interpretation via
latent space modeling
, namely that each vertex has a latent low-dimensional Euclidean representation (i.e., vector or position). The general latent space model posits that the entries of the
P
matrix are obtained by first generating
n
independent latent vectors
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
from a distribution
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and then setting
P_{ij}=\rho_{n}\kappa(X_{i},X_{j})
, where
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
is a symmetric kernel function. The special case
\kappa(X_{i},X_{j})=\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle
, yields the so-called
random dot product graph
(model)
((
Young and Scheinerman 2008
)
, presented below.

Definition 4

(Random dot product graph – undirected, loopy)
.

We say that a symmetric adjacency matrix
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
is a random dot product graph on
n
vertices with sparsity term
\rho=\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
if, conditional on
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
satisfying
XX^{\top}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
, its upper triangular entries are generated independently in the manner

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho X_{i*}^{\top}X_{j*})&\textnormal{~if~}i\leq j;\\
A_{ji}&\textnormal{~if~}i>j.\end{cases}

The rows of
X
may be deterministic or modeled in a generative fashion via i.i.d. vectors
X_{i*}\sim F_{X}
for a distribution
F_{X}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
X_{i\star}^{\top}X_{j\star}\in[0,1]
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
.

Random dot product graphs have the property that
P=\rho_{n}XX^{\top}
, where each row of
X
is interpreted as a latent position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this setting,
U\Lambda^{1/2}=\rho_{n}^{1/2}X\widetilde{W}
for some orthogonal matrix
\widetilde{W}
(not necessarily identifiable). Consequently, the scaled eigenvectors correspond to an isometric linear transformation and scaling of the original latent vectors. The latent-space interpretation avoids the previous shrinking-to-zero phenomenon from the eigenvectors in
n
and introduces additional regularity into the definition of
P
.

Here,
\mathbb{P}[A_{ij}=1]=\rho_{n}X_{i*}^{\top},X_{j*}
leads to having matrices
B
that are necessarily positive semidefinite. More recently,
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
introduces the
generalized random dot product graph
model, which replaces the inner product with a pseudo-inner product to model large-in-magnitude negative eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix
A
and hence allow for indefinite
B
matrices.

Definition 5

(Generalized random dot product graph — undirected, loopy)
.

We say that a symmetric adjacency matrix
A
is a generalized random dot product graph on
n
vertices with sparsity term
\rho=\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
if, conditional on
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
satisfying
X{I}_{p,q}X^{\top}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
, its upper triangular entries are generated independently in the manner

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho X_{i*}^{\top}{I}_{p,q}X_{j*}),&\textnormal{~for~}i\leq j;\\
A_{ji}&\textnormal{~for~}i>j.\end{cases}

Here,
{I}_{p,q}
is the matrix
\operatorname{diag}(I_{p},-I_{q})
with
p+q=d
. The rows of
X
may be deterministic or modeled in a generative fashion via i.i.d. vectors
X_{i*}\sim F_{X}
for a distribution
F_{X}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
X_{i*}^{\top}{I}_{p,q}X_{j*}\in[0,1]
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
.

For GRDPGs, as for RDPGs, the latent positions can be viewed as following a mixture distribution of point masses, namely
X_{i*}={\nu}_{k*}
if
\tau(i)=k
, defined by
B={\nu}{I}_{p,q}{\nu}^{\top}
, with non-negative integers
p
and
q
representing the number of positive and negative eigenvalues of
B
, respectively. Under the generalized random dot product graph model, the matrix
P
can be decomposed as
\rho_{n}X{I}_{p,q}X^{\top}
, but the definition induces an additional nonidentifiability. If
Q
satisfies
Q{I}_{p,q}Q^{\top}={I}_{p,q}
, namely if
Q
is a so-called
indefinite orthogonal matrix
, then
X
and
XQ
generate the same probability matrix, implying that the latent positions are only identifiable up to such transformations
Q
. Consequently, analyzing the generalized dot product graph requires additional considerations with respect to these transformations. For random dot product graphs, namely when
q=0
, these matrices
Q
are simply
orthogonal matrices
.

A survey of statistical inference under the random dot product graph can be found in
Athreya et al. 2018
. Our focus herein is on asymptotic normality for (generalized) random dot product graphs through the lens of stochastic blockmodels.

4.2.1
Latent Space Interpretation of the Stochastic Blockmodel

Interpreting the stochastic blockmodel as a latent space model amounts to imposing an additional assumption on the distribution
F
if the communities are stochastic or on the rows of the matrix
X
if the communities are deterministic. Namely, either
F
corresponds to a mixture of
K
point masses in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d=\operatorname{rank}(B)
, or
X
has at most
K
unique
d
-dimensional rows, with each row corresponding to community membership in the latter if the communities are fixed. One approach to specify the distribution
F
is via the eigendecomposition of the matrix
B
. If
B
is positive semidefinite, write its eigendecomposition as
B=VDV^{\top}
, and define the matrix
{\nu}\coloneqq VD^{1/2}
. Then, the rows of
X
can be drawn via
\mathbb{P}[X_{i*}=\nu_{k*}]=\pi_{k}
hence
\rho_{n}XX^{\top}=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, so the stochastic blockmodel is a random dot product graph.

If
B
is not positive semidefinite, then one must consider a generalized random dot product graph, with
p
and
q
equal to the number of positive and negative eigenvalues of
B
, respectively. In this case, take
{\nu}\coloneqq V|D|^{1/2}{I}_{p,q}
so that
B=V|D|^{1/2}{I}_{p,q}|D|^{1/2}V^{\top}
. In fact, the latent space interpretation also applies to the stochastic blockmodel extensions mentioned in
Section

3.2
. For the mixed-membership stochastic blockmodel, the latent space interpretation is that the latent positions belong to a simplex in
d
dimensions, and for the degree-corrected stochastic blockmodel, the latent positions are one of
K
rays emanating from the origin, where each ray corresponds to a different community and location along the ray is governed by the node degree parameters.

Denote the spectral decomposition of
A
by
A=\widehat{U}\widehat{\Lambda}\widehat{U}^{\top}+\widehat{U}_{\perp}\widehat{\Lambda}_{\perp}\widehat{U}_{\perp}^{\top}
, where
\widehat{\Lambda}
is the
d\times d
diagonal matrix of leading eigenvalues in magnitude of
A
, and where
\widehat{U}
is the
n\times d
matrix whose columns are the orthonormal eigenvectors corresponding to
\widehat{\Lambda}
. Similarly, write
P=U\Lambda U^{\top}
. Under certain conditions, existing row-wise uniform error bounds establish that
\widehat{U}|\widehat{\Lambda}|^{1/2}\approx U|\Lambda|^{1/2}W
for a suitable orthogonal transformation
W
. In the special case when
P
is positive semidefinite with
P=\rho_{n}XX^{\top}
, then
U\Lambda^{1/2}=\rho_{n}^{1/2}X\widetilde{W}
for some orthogonal matrix
\widetilde{W}
, which in particular suggests that
\widehat{U}|\widehat{\Lambda}|^{1/2}\approx\rho_{n}^{1/2}XW^{\prime}
, where
W^{\prime}
is a possibly different orthogonal transformation representing the product of different, underlying orthogonal transformations.

In fact, the matrix of scaled eigenvectors
\widehat{X}\coloneqq\widehat{U}|\widehat{\Lambda}|^{1/2}
exhibits similar uniform row-wise convergence guarantees to the unscaled eigenvectors
((
Athreya et al. 2018
;
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
)
, as follows.

Theorem 5

(Restatement of Theorem 1 in
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
)
.

Fix
K
and
B=\nu{I}_{p,q}\nu^{\top}
. Assume that each
X_{i}
is drawn independently according to
\mathbb{P}[X_{i}=\nu_{k*}]=\pi_{k}
, with
\nu_{k*}
denoting the
k
-th row of
\nu
and
d=\operatorname{rank}(\nu)
.
Suppose
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}X{I}_{p,q}X^{\top}=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
. Let
P
have eigendecomposition
U\Lambda U^{\top}
, let
\widetilde{X}=U|\Lambda|^{1/2}
, and let
\widehat{U}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}
denote the leading eigenvectors and eigenvalues (in magnitude) of
A
. There exists a universal constant
c>1
and an orthogonal matrix
W
such that provided
n\rho_{n}\gg\log^{4c}(n)
, then

\displaystyle\|\widehat{X}W-\widetilde{X}\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

holds with probability at least
1-O(n^{-M})
for any prespecified choice of constant
M>0
. The value of
M
influences the implicit constant in the upper bound.

Under the GRDPG model, there exists an indefinite orthogonal matrix
Q
such that
XQ=\widetilde{X}
.
For sufficiently dense graphs, the convergence of
\widehat{X}
to
XQ
resembles the convergence of
\widehat{U}
to
U
after accounting for the order of magnitudes. For sufficiently dense graphs,
n\rho_{n}\gg\operatorname{polylog}(n)
, there exist sequences of orthogonal transformations
W^{X}
and
W^{U}
depending on
n
such that with high probability,

\displaystyle\frac{\|\widehat{X}W^{X}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}X{Q}\|_{2,\infty}}{\|\rho_{n}^{1/2}XQ\|_{2,\infty}}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}}\right),\qquad\frac{\|\widehat{U}W^{U}-U\|_{2,\infty}}{\|U\|_{2,\infty}}=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}}\right),

establishing that the relative row-wise error for both
\widehat{U}
and
\widehat{X}
decays to zero as the number of vertices grows.
Theorem

5
also holds for RDPGs per
Xie 2024
under the milder assumption
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
.

4.2.2
Asymptotic Normality of the Scaled Eigenvectors

The scaled eigenvectors
\widehat{X}
exhibit a similar central limit theorem as the unscaled eigenvectors, as demonstrated in the following result.

Theorem 6

(Restatement of Theorem 2 in
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
for SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
B=\nu{I}_{p,q}\nu^{\top}
. Assume that each
X_{i}
is drawn independently according to
\mathbb{P}[X_{i}=\nu_{k*}]=\pi_{k}
, with
\nu_{k*}
denoting the
k
-th row of
\nu
and
d=\operatorname{rank}(\nu)
. Suppose
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}X{I}_{p,q}X^{\top}=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
. Let
P
have eigendecomposition
U\Lambda U^{\top}
, let
\widetilde{X}=U|\Lambda|^{1/2}
, and let
\widehat{U}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}
denote the leading eigenvectors and eigenvalues (in magnitude) of
A
.
There exist sequences of orthogonal matrices
W
and indefinite orthogonal matrices
Q
and a universal constant
c>1
such that, if
n\rho_{n}\gg\log^{4c}(n)
, then as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg(n^{1/2}Q^{\top}\big((\widehat{X}W)_{i*}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}{\nu}_{k*}\big)\bigg)\mid\tau(i)=k

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{S}(k))&\textnormal{~if~}\rho_{n}\equiv 1,\\
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{S,o(1)}(k))&\textnormal{~if~}\rho_{n}\to 0,\end{cases}

where the asymptotic covariance matrices satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{S}(k)={I}_{p,q}\Delta^{-1}\Gamma^{(k)}\Delta^{-1}{I}_{p,q},\qquad\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{S,o(1)}(k)

\displaystyle={I}_{p,q}\Delta^{-1}\Gamma^{(k)}_{o(1)}\Delta^{-1}{I}_{p,q},

(8)

where

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{K}\pi_{l}{\nu}_{l*}{\nu}_{l*}^{\top},\qquad\Gamma^{(k)}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{K}\pi_{k}B_{kl}\big(1-B_{kl}\big){\nu}_{l*}{\nu}_{l*}^{\top},\qquad\Gamma^{(k)}_{o(1)}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{K}\pi_{k}B_{kl}{\nu}_{l*}{\nu}_{l*}^{\top}.

A precursor to
Theorem

6
appears in
Athreya et al. 2016
which proves a central limit theorem for the scaled eigenvectors of random dot product graphs (
q=0
) under the assumption that the latent positions are sampled from a distribution
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that the second moment matrix
\mathbb{E}_{X_{1*}\sim F}[X_{1*}X_{1*}^{\top}]
has distinct eigenvalues, and that
\rho_{n}=1
. This result is extended in
Athreya et al. 2018
to the setting where the second moment matrix is permitted to have repeated eigenvalues. For positive semidefinite stochastic blockmodels with equal community membership probabilities, the assumption of distinct eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}[X_{1*}X_{1*}^{\top}]
is equivalent to the assumption that
B
has distinct eigenvalues, but for unequal community membership probabilities the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}[X_{1*}X_{1*}^{\top}]
need not be the same as those of
B
. In fact,
\mathbb{E}[X_{1*}X_{1*}^{\top}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}{\nu}_{k*}{\nu}_{k*}^{\top}
, where the matrix
{\nu}
is the
K\times d
matrix of distinct latent vectors associated to the corresponding stochastic blockmodel.
These results were later extended to the case
\rho_{n}\to 0
in
Tang and Priebe 2018
prior to the further extension in
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
.

In
Theorem

6
, the asymptotic covariance matrices for both
\rho_{n}=1
and
\rho_{n}\to 0
are extensions of those in the positive semidefinite case, reflecting the roles of the leading eigenvalue signs through the matrix
{I}_{p,q}
and introducing an indefinite orthogonal matrix
Q
. A key property of indefinite orthogonal matrices is that they preserve
volume
, namely
|\det(Q_{X}{I}_{p,q}Q_{X}^{\top})|=|\det({I}_{p,q})|=1
, so that the asymptotic Gaussianity is retained in the sense that the volume of the ellipse generated by these asymptotic covariance matrices is invariant to these transformations. See
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
for more discussion about these matrices and properties pertaining to latent position random graphs.

Theorem

6
demonstrates that the rows of the matrix
\widehat{X}
are approximately Gaussian and centered around the rows of
\rho_{n}^{1/2}Q^{\top}\nu_{k*}
. As before, there are only
K
unique centroids corresponding to the possible community memberships. Results presented earlier only involve orthogonal transformations and so the cluster shape (ellipse) is determined by the asymptotic covariance matrix in the statement of the result, whereas in
Theorem

6
the cluster shape is influenced by both the limiting asymptotic covariance as well as the indefinite orthogonal matrix
Q
.

As in
Theorem

2
for the unscaled eigenvectors, the asymptotic covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(k)
in
Theorem

6
depends on the community probabilities
\pi_{k}
, the membership of the
i
-th vertex, and the matrix
B
. Provided
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(k)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{(n)}(k)
exists, then
Theorem

2
has asymptotic covariance matrix of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(k)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-3/2}\Gamma^{(k)}\Delta^{-3/2}.

Consequently, when
B
is positive semidefinite,
Theorem

6
demonstrates that the asymptotic covariance matrices for the scaled and unscaled eigenvectors are related in the manner

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(k)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{1/2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(k)\Delta^{1/2},

which reflects the additional scaling of the eigenvectors by the eigenvalues.

More recently,
Xie 2024
extends this result to the regime
n\rho_{n}\asymp\log(n)
, building upon work in
Xie and Xu 2021
for sequences of deterministic matrices
P=\rho_{n}XX^{\top}
, rather than stochastically generated community memberships.

Theorem 7

(Restatement of Theorem 4.4 in
Xie 2024
for SBMs)
.

Suppose the conditions of
Theorem

2
hold. Write

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}^{(n)}(k)\coloneqq\Delta_{n}^{-1}\Gamma^{(k)}_{n}\Delta_{n}^{-1},

where
\Gamma^{(k)}_{n}
and
\Delta_{n}
are defined in
Theorem

2
. Suppose
\tau(i)=k
with
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l\leq n
. There exist sequences of orthogonal matrices
\widetilde{W}
and
W
depending on
n
and a constant
C>0
depending only on
d
such that for any convex measurable set
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{P}\bigg\{n^{1/2}\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}^{(n)}(k)

\displaystyle\big)^{-1/2}\widetilde{W}^{\top}\big((\widehat{X}W)_{i*}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}{\nu}_{k*}\big)\in\mathcal{A}\bigg\}-\mathbb{P}(G\in\mathcal{A})\bigg\}\bigg|\leq\frac{C\log(n\rho_{n})}{(n\rho_{n})^{1/2}},

where
G
denotes a
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector.

Taken together,
Theorems

6
and
7
demonstrate a similar phenomenon as for the unscaled eigenvectors, namely that the estimated scaled eigenvectors are approximately Gaussian around block-specific centroids after appropriate (indefinite) orthogonal transformation. For the scaled eigenvectors, the cluster centroids do not depend on
n
besides through the sparsity factor
\rho_{n}
, which, for practical purposes, could be estimated consistently in certain settings after imposing an identifiability condition, for example,
\max_{i}B_{ii}=1
or
\|B\|=1
.

4.2.3
Asymptotic Normality of the Laplacian Eigenvectors

A central limit theorem for the scaled eigenvectors

\textstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=6.83331pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=9.06943pt}

\textstyle X

of the Laplacian under the random dot product graph model is illustrated in the following theorem.

Theorem 8

(Restatement of Corollary 3.3 in
Tang and Priebe 2018

4.3
Relationships Between Limiting Results for Eigenvectors

The results in
Sections

4.1
and
4.2
illustrate a common theme, namely that each row vector of the embedding is (marginally) approximately Gaussian around one of
K
different centroids in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the location of the centroid depends on the particular embedding specification. In addition, aggregating across the embedding also yields asymptotic normality, per
Theorems

3
and
4
. Underlying many of the proofs in the aforecited works, as well as the entrywise perturbation results discussed in
Section

3.2
, are the perturbation expansions

\displaystyle\widehat{U}W-U

\displaystyle=(A-\mathbb{E}[A])U\Lambda^{-1}+R_{U}

(9)

\displaystyle\widehat{U}\widehat{\Lambda}^{1/2}W^{\prime}-U\Lambda^{1/2}

\displaystyle=(A-\mathbb{E}[A])U\Lambda^{-1/2}+R_{X},

where
R_{U}
and
R_{X}
are residual matrices whose
\ell_{2,\infty}
norm is comparatively small with high probability for large graphs. Similar characterizations hold for Laplacian spectral embeddings for networks (e.g., Theorem 3.1 in
Tang and Priebe 2018
or Theorem of 3.1 of
Ke and Wang 2022
) and beyond network models (e.g., Lemma 4.18 in
Chen et al. 2021
). The primary technical difficulty is that isolating leading-order terms requires care, though there are several different approaches for obtaining these and related expansions. For example,
Fan et al. 2022a
uses techniques based on contour integration and resolvents in random matrix theory,
Cape et al. 2019a
uses deterministic matrix perturbations and series expansions, and
Xie 2024
uses a
leave-one-out technique
; see also
Abbe et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
.

The appearance of the orthogonal matrix
W
stems from the fact that the eigenvalues
\Lambda
need not always be distinct. If the eigenvalues are distinct, then Lemma 9 in
Fan et al. 2022b
shows that a similar entrywise expansion holds for the individual eigenvectors (see also Theorem 3 in
Fan et al. 2022a
).

Lemma 4

(Restatement of Lemma 9 in
Fan et al. 2022b
for SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
B
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim n^{\eta}
for some constant
\eta>0
, and suppose that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l\leq K
. Let
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, where
P
has
K
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{K}
satisfying
\min_{i\neq j}|\lambda_{i}|/|\lambda_{j}|\geq 1+c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
for
\lambda_{i}\neq-\lambda_{j}
. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle t_{k}(e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{U}_{*k}-e_{i}^{\top}U_{*k})

\displaystyle=e_{i}^{\top}(A-\mathbb{E}[A])U_{*k}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2}),

where
t_{k}
is the solution to the fixed-point equation in
Eq.

7
.

The expansions in
Eq.

9
can be understood as holding for a diagonal (orthogonal) matrix
W
with main diagonal elements
\pm 1
when the eigenvalues are sufficiently separated. Explicitly, this leading-order expansion demonstrates that, under certain conditions, the entries of the
k
-th eigenvector have a variance that is asymptotically proportional to the
k
-th eigenvalue, a phenomenon that has also been shown in non-network settings (e.g.,
Agterberg et al. 2022
).
Lemma

4
demonstrates that when the eigenvalues are distinct, then the asymptotic variance is proportional to the quantity
t_{k}^{2}
, while the difference between
\lambda_{k}^{2}/t_{k}^{2}
and
1
is asymptotically negligible for sufficiently dense networks. Additional details regarding
t_{k}
are provided in
Section

4.5
.

The convergence of
\|(\widehat{U}W-U)\Lambda\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{2}
to a Gaussian random variable is established in
Li and Li 2018
. This expression can be viewed as a modification of the
\sin\Theta
(Frobenius) distance between the eigenvectors, cf.
Section

4.1
. Though there do not appear to be existing distributional results for
\sin\Theta
distances customized to network problems, existing works do give concentration inequalities for the random variable
\|\widehat{U}|\widehat{\Lambda}|^{1/2}W-U|\Lambda|^{1/2}\|_{\operatorname{F}}
. Namely, see
Tang et al. 2017a
;
Tang and Priebe 2018
;
Agterberg et al. 2020
for applicable results to (generalized) random dot product graphs.

Asymptotic multivariate normality results by themselves do not address consistent estimation of the limiting covariance matrix for each row, which are needed to devise asymptotically valid confidence regions or provide data-driven uncertainty quantification. In
Section

5
, we review hypothesis testing settings where consistent estimation of the limiting covariance is possible.

4.4
Cluster Structure: A Simple Numerical Example

The asymptotic normality results presented in previous subsections have limiting covariance matrices that depend on the
B
matrix and the community membership of the
i
-th vertex. The resulting cluster geometry reflects the particular form of these covariance matrices. Here, consider for example the setting where
B_{11}=B_{22}=a
,
B_{12}=B_{21}=b
, for
0<b<a<1
, and with equal size communities. In this setting, the leading eigenvector of
P=ZBZ^{\top}
is proportional to the constant vector of all ones. In addition, the second (un-normalized) leading eigenvector has entries
\pm 1
, with positive and negative signs distinguishing between the two communities. Consequently, since (unit-norm) eigenvectors are only unique up to sign, by symmetry it follows that
\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(1)\big)_{\ell\ell}=\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(2)\big)_{\ell\ell}
for
\ell=1
or
2
, and that
\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(1)\big)_{12}=-\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(2)\big)_{12}
, where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(k)
denotes the asymptotic covariance obtained from
Theorem

2
. Consequently, the covariance matrices are of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(1)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&\gamma\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(2)=\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}\alpha&-\beta\\
-\beta&\phantom{-}\gamma\end{pmatrix},

and written in terms of underlying, implicit parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
.
Fig.

1
showcases this phenomenon, where the covariance for Community One is positive for dimensions one and two, whereas the covariance between dimensions one and two for Community Two is negative. However, this observation does not necessarily result in nearly spherical covariance matrices.

Consider two possible connectivity matrices

B^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}.02&.01\\
.01&.02\end{pmatrix},\qquad B^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}.9&.85\\
.85&.9\end{pmatrix},

with
n/2=5000
vertices in each community. The leading eigenvectors of
P^{(1)}=ZB^{(1)}Z^{\top}
and
P^{(2)}=ZB^{(2)}Z^{\top}
are the same, but the resulting covariance matrices are different as they depend on the entries of
B
. In
Fig.

2
, we plot the first
n/2
rows of
n(\widehat{U}W-U)
for
B^{(1)}
and
B^{(2)}
respectively. Here,
B^{(1)}
has a condition number of
3
, whereas
B^{(2)}
has a condition number of
35
, resulting in covariance condition numbers of
10.36
and
1236.59
, respectively. The cluster associated to
B^{(2)}
is visibly elongated, even more than the matrix
B^{(1)}
which consists of smaller connectivity probabilities. Computing the theoretical limiting covariance numerically yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}65.556&-64.667\\
-64.667&590\end{pmatrix}&\textnormal{~for~}B^{(1)},\\
\begin{pmatrix}0.142&0.857\\
0.857&174\end{pmatrix}&\textnormal{~for~}B^{(2)},\end{cases}

which showcases the effect of ill-conditioned matrices

on cluster structure. While the presented asymptotic normality results hold for fixed
B

, the limiting covariance structure depends highly on the matrix

4.5
Empirical Eigenvalues

The eigenvalues of large-dimensional matrices are well-studied in the field of random matrix theory. This survey focuses on asymptotic normality concerning the top
d
in magnitude eigenvalues of large adjacency matrices
A
. Among existing results in the literature, the following classical result establishes the limiting behavior of the leading eigenvalue of Erdős–Rényi random graphs.

Theorem 10

(Restatement of Theorem 1 in
Füredi and Komlós 1981
)
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
. Let
A
be a symmetric random matrix with i.i.d. upper triangular entries
A_{ij}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)
,
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
. Let
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}
denote the largest eigenvalue of
A
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}_{1}-np\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(1-p,2p(1-p)).

Observe that
np
is the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbb{E}[A]=p11^{\top}
.

More recently,
Athreya et al. 2021
extended
Theorem

10
to the general rank
d
setting for stochastic blockmodels, as follows.

Theorem 11

(Restatement of Theorem 3 in
Athreya et al. 2021
for SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
B={\nu}{I}_{p,q}{\nu}^{\top}
. Suppose that
A\sim P
with
P=ZBZ^{\top}
and that
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
. Let
\mathbf{v}_{i}
denote the unit norm eigenvector satisfying

\displaystyle\big({\nu}^{\top}Z^{\top}Z{\nu}\big)^{1/2}{I}_{p,q}\big({\nu}^{\top}Z^{\top}Z{\nu}\big)^{1/2}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i},

where
\lambda_{i}
is the
i
-th nonzero eigenvalue of
ZBZ^{\top}
.
Define the vector
\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{i}\coloneqq\big({\nu}\ Z^{\top}Z{\nu}\big)^{-1/2}\mathbf{v}_{i}
. Let
{\eta}
denote the
d
-dimensional vector with components

\displaystyle{\eta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\sum_{l=1}^{K}n_{l}\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{i}^{\top}{\nu}_{l}{\nu}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{K}B_{lk}(1-B_{lk}),

and let
\mathbf{C}=(\mathbf{C}_{ij})
denote the
d\times d
matrix with elements

\displaystyle 2\bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}n_{l}\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{i}^{\top}{\nu}_{l*}{\nu}_{l*}^{\top}\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{j}\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{i}^{\top}{\nu}_{k*}{\nu}_{k*}^{\top}\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{j}B_{kl}(1-B_{kl})\bigg).

Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{-1/2}\big(\widehat{\lambda}_{1}-\lambda_{1}-{\eta}_{1},\widehat{\lambda}_{2}-\lambda_{2}-{\eta}_{2},\dots,\widehat{\lambda}_{d}-\lambda_{d}-{\eta}_{d}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

In fact, the vector
{\eta}
is also shown to converge when the eigenvalues of
\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\mathbf{\nu}_{k*}\mathbf{\nu}_{k*}^{\top}{I}_{p,q}
are assumed distinct (see Theorem 2 therein). Similar to
Theorem

10
, it can be shown that
{\eta}
and
\mathbf{C}
are bounded bias and covariance terms. Explicitly,
\sigma_{i}^{2}
is the limiting value of the variance of the random variable
U_{*i}^{\top}(A-\mathbb{E}[A])U_{*i}
after unconditioning on the latent vectors
X_{i*}
. Here
\lambda_{i}
is order
n
as the authors assume that
\rho_{n}=1
. The results are derived under the general latent-space framework of the generalized random dot product graph described in
Section

4.2
.

A closely related result for individual eigenvalues is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 12

(Restatement of Theorem 1 in
Fan et al. 2022a
for SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
B
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{\eta}
for some
\eta>0
and that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l\leq K
. Suppose
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, where
P
has
K
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{K}
satisfying
\min_{i\neq j}|\lambda_{i}|/|\lambda_{j}|\geq 1+c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
for
\lambda_{i}\neq-\lambda_{j}
. Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}-t_{k}}{\operatorname{Var}\left[U_{*k}^{\top}(A-P)U_{*k}\right]^{1/2}}\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
t_{k}
is the unique solution to the fixed-point equation defined in
Eq.

7
.

Theorems

11
and
12
, while related, are difficult to directly compare with one another. For one, unlike
Athreya et al. 2021
,
Fan et al. 2022a
does not consider a latent-space framework, so the eigenvalues of
n^{-1}P
need not converge. Since
P=X{I}_{p,q}X^{\top}
, and the nonzero eigenvalues of a generic matrix product
MN
are the same as the nonzero eigenvalues of the matrix product
NM
, the nonzero eigenvalues of
P
are the same as those of
X^{\top}X{I}_{p,q}
. If each row of
X
is drawn from a latent position distribution, then
n^{-1}X^{\top}X\to\mathbb{E}[X_{1*}X_{1*}^{\top}]
almost surely, so the eigenvalues of
n^{-1}P
are converging to the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}[X_{1*}X_{1*}^{\top}]{I}_{p,q}
. As a consequence,
Theorem

12
allows for more sparsity by only specifying a sequence of matrices
P=P_{n}
. Here,
n\rho_{n}=\omega(n^{\eta})
for some
\eta>0
, which is a more stringent condition than the
\log(n)
information-theoretic threshold required for consistency in community detection but is significantly sparser than the fully dense regime studied in
Athreya et al. 2021
. A further important distinction is that
Theorem

11
is a
joint
convergence result when the eigenvalues are almost surely asymptotically distinct, whereas
Theorem

12
holds marginally, for each individual eigenvalue. That being said, the result in
Fan et al. 2022a
allows for significantly more general forms of the low-rank probability matrix
P
.

Another impediment to explicitly comparing the above results is that, while the bias (centering) term in
Athreya et al. 2021
is computed directly, the bias term in
Fan et al. 2022a
is expressed implicitly through the solution of a complex analytic fixed-point equation, which is of order
n\rho_{n}
from the discussion immediately following
Theorem

3
. On the other hand, the limiting variances are seen to be comparable since both stem from
U_{*k}^{\top}(A-\mathbb{E}[A])U_{*k}
(which is of order
\rho_{n}
).

Theorems

11
and
12
both consider the fluctuations of the eigenvalues at the scaling
O(1)
. Under the latent space framework, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}=n^{-1}X{I}_{p,q}X^{\top}
can be viewed as the eigenvalues of an empirical kernel matrix with kernel
X_{i*}^{\top}{I}_{p,q}X_{j*}
. The results of
Koltchinskii and Gine 2000
imply that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}
are approximately Gaussian about the eigenvalues of a corresponding integral operator, which in this case are simply the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}[X_{1*}X_{1*}^{\top}]{I}_{p,q}
. Therefore, the fluctuations of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}
may be approximated by the eigenvalues of the normalized matrix
n^{-1}A
or
(n\rho_{n})^{-1}A
. These eigenvalues are studied in the context of a general low-rank random graph in
Lunde and Sarkar 2023
.

Theorem 13

(Restatement of Theorem 3.1 in
Lunde and Sarkar 2023
for SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
B
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{1/2}
and that
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
. Let
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
. Suppose that the eigenvalues
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\lambda}_{d}
of
\mathbb{E}[X_{1*}X_{1*}^{\top}]{I}_{p,q}
are distinct. Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\bigg(\frac{\widehat{\lambda}_{1}}{n\rho_{n}}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\frac{\widehat{\lambda}_{d}}{n\rho_{n}}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{d}\bigg)\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{S}),

where
\mathbf{S}
denotes the asymptotic covariance matrix.

In
Lunde and Sarkar 2023
, the probabilities are allowed to be drawn from a general latent position distribution but with
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{1/2}
. Therein, the covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
is not discussed in detail, though it is the same as in
Koltchinskii and Gine 2000
, due to the fact that the fluctuations of
(n\rho_{n})^{-1}A-\mathcal{K}
are of smaller order than the fluctuations of
\mathcal{K}
and its corresponding integral operator. In other words, the leading
d
eigenvalues of
(n\rho_{n})^{-1}A
and
(n\rho_{n})^{-1}P
have the same limiting distribution. In contrast, the results in
Athreya et al. 2021
;
Fan et al. 2022a
concern the leading eigenvalues of
A
.

The aforementioned results illustrate that for sufficiently dense graphs, the eigenvalues of
A
are asymptotically biased estimators of the (population) eigenvalues of
P
, but, the eigenvalues of
(n\rho_{n})^{-1}A
are asymptotically unbiased estimators of those of the corresponding kernel operator (not necessarily unbiased for finite
n
). In all cases, these results hold under more general models, namely for both
Theorems

11
and
13
the edge probability matrix
P
can be drawn from a generalized random dot product graph, and in
Theorem

12
the edge probability matrix need only be low-rank. Whether these results can be extended to various sparsity regimes or used explicitly for inference is a topic of ongoing investigation.

4.6
Parameter Estimation

When the community memberships are viewed as stochastic, and when both
\rho
and
K
are assumed known, then the model parameters are encapsulated in
(B,\pi)
. Absent additional structural assumptions,
B
consists of
(K+1)K/2
unconstrained parameters in
[0,1]
, while
\pi
consists of
K
nonnegative parameters in
[0,1]
that sum to unity. Community memberships are unique only up to a global permutation of community labels (i.e., global relabeling), so strictly speaking, estimation of the matrix
B
holds up to simultaneous row/column permutation, while estimation of the vector
\pi
holds up to a permutation of its coordinates.

The likelihood function of
(A,\tau)
and of
A
when permitting self-edges are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(A,\tau;B,\pi)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\pi_{\tau_{i}}\right)\times\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}(\rho B_{\tau_{i},\tau_{j}})^{A_{ij}}(1-\rho B_{\tau_{i},\tau_{j}})^{1-A_{ij}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(A;B,\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in[K]^{n}}\mathcal{L}(A,\tau;B,\pi).

(10)

For stochastic blockmodel graphs
A
governed by parameters
(B,\pi)
, where
\rho
and
K
are assumed known, the maximum likelihood estimate of
B
and
\pi
is given by

\displaystyle(\widehat{B}^{(\operatorname{MLE})},\widehat{\pi}^{(\operatorname{MLE})})=\underset{B\in[0,1]^{K\times K},\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{K-1}}{\operatorname{\textnormal{arg max}}}\mathcal{L}(A;B,\pi)

(11)

The asymptotic normality of the maximum likelihood estimate was obtained in Lemma 1 of
Bickel et al. 2013
in the asymptotic regime
n\rho_{n}\gg\log n
, with implicit limiting variances (see
Tang et al. 2022
for additional discussion).

Theorem 14

(Restatement of Lemma 1 in
Bickel et al. 2013
)
.

Fix
K
and
B
. Suppose
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
with
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
. Let
\widehat{B}^{(\operatorname{MLE})}
denote the MLE of
B
obtained from
A
with
\rho_{n}
assumed known. If
n\rho_{n}\gg\log(n)
, then as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

n\rho_{n}^{1/2}\big(\widehat{B}^{(\operatorname{MLE})}-B\big)\hskip-5.0pt\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\hskip-5.0pt\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B})&\textnormal{~if~}\rho_{n}\equiv 1,\\
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B,o(1)})&\textnormal{~if~}\rho_{n}\to 0,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B})_{kl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2B_{kk}(1-B_{kk})}{\pi_{k}^{2}}&\textnormal{~if~}k=l,\\
\frac{B_{kl}(1-B_{kl})}{\pi_{k}\pi_{l}}&\textnormal{~if~}k\neq l.\end{cases}

(12)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B,o(1)})_{kl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2B_{kk}}{\pi_{k}^{2}}&\textnormal{~if~}k=l,\\
\frac{B_{kl}}{\pi_{k}\pi_{l}}&\textnormal{~if~}k\neq l.\end{cases}

(13)

In both cases,
\{n\rho_{n}^{1/2}~(\widehat{B}^{(\operatorname{MLE})}_{kl}-B_{kl})\}_{k\leq l}
are asymptotically independent.

The maximum likelihood estimator computed using the likelihood
Eq.

10
can be computationally expensive and is in general NP-hard to compute
((
Amini et al. 2013
)
. Furthermore, as discussed in
Section

4
, when specifying a stochastic blockmodel
B
matrix having dimension
K\times K
, there is no
a priori
reason to assume that
B
has full matrix rank equal to
K
. This observation is important, since the presence or absence of a rank constraint can influence the number of free parameters in the SBM parameter estimation problem. For
d\coloneqq\textrm{rank}(B)\leq K
, by taking the spectral decomposition of
B
, there are at most
d(2K-d+1)/2\leq K(K+1)/2
total parameters, via the eigendecomposition of
B
.

Example 1

(SBM with two communities and rank one
B
matrix)
.

Consider a
K=2
SBM with

B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p^{2}&pq\\
pq&q^{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
where
p,q\in(0,1),p\neq q
. The matrix
B
can be written as the outer product of the vector
(p,q)
with itself, hence
\textrm{rank}(B)=1
. Here, there are two parameters, rather than
K(K+1)/2=3
parameters, namely
p
and
q
, with
B_{12}
depending on
B_{11}
and
B_{22}
.

The paper
Tang et al. 2022
considers entrywise estimation of
B
by via spectral estimators
\widehat{B}_{k\ell}^{(S)}
formed by averaging over the truncated eigendecomposition
\widehat{U}\widehat{\Lambda}\widehat{U}^{\top}
of the adjacency matrix
A
in the following manner. Assume that
d=\textrm{rank}(B)
,
K
, and
\rho_{n}
are all known. Then, clustering the rows of the eigenvector matrix
\widehat{U}
via
K
-means (or Gaussian mixture modeling) as in
Algorithm

1
yields an estimated block membership vector
\widehat{\tau}
and corresponding assignment vectors
\widehat{s}_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and block sizes
\widehat{n}_{k}
for all
k\in[K]
, where

\widehat{B}_{k\ell}^{(S)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\widehat{n}_{k}\widehat{n}_{\ell}\rho_{n}}\widehat{s}_{k}^{\top}\widehat{U}\widehat{\Lambda}\widehat{U}^{\top}\widehat{s}_{\ell}.

(14)

The following result demonstrates the entrywise asymptotic normality of
\widehat{B}^{(S)}
when
B
is rank
K
.

Theorem 15

(Restatement of Corollary 2 in
Tang et al. 2022
)
.

Fix
K
and
B
where
\textrm{rank}(B)=K
. Suppose that
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
with
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
. Let
\widehat{B}^{(S)}
be defined as in
Eq.

14
with
\rho_{n}
assumed known. If
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{1/2}
, then as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle n\big(\widehat{B}^{(\operatorname{S})}_{kl}-B_{kl}\big)\hskip-5.0pt\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\hskip-5.0pt\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}(0,(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B})_{kl})&\textnormal{~if~}\rho_{n}\equiv 1,\\
\mathcal{N}(0,(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B,o(1)})_{kl})&\textnormal{~if~}\rho_{n}\to 0,\end{cases}

for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B,o(1)}
as in
Theorem

14
.

Consequently, when
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{1/2}
and
B
is full rank, the spectral estimators are (marginally) asymptotically efficient, meaning that the variances in
Theorem

14
coincide with the variances in
Theorem

15
.
More is true, namely Remark 1 in
Tang et al. 2022
shows that the half-vectorization of

is asymptotically multivariate (jointly) normal, with given bias, variance, and covariance terms.

When
B
is not full rank, then the spectral estimator is no longer asymptotically efficient. In particular,
Tang et al. 2022
also establishes that
\widehat{B}^{(S)}
is biased and has an inflated asymptotic variance when

is rank degenerate, providing explicit expressions for the bias and variance terms. To ameliorate the efficiency gap,
Tang et al. 2022
further develops a one-step update to obtain efficiency in parameter estimation. Using the (debiased) spectral embedding-based estimate as an initial estimate, the one-step estimator is obtained by performing a single Newton–Raphson step in the negative gradient direction with respect to the likelihood of
B
(considering
\tau

Tang et al. 2022
demonstrates that this procedure yields an asymptotically efficient estimator. A similar result was also obtained in

for an alternative parametrization and different initial estimator to yield asymptotic efficiency in a modified parameter space. The results therein are in terms of an underlying
d(2K-d+1)/2
-dimensional parameter.

In
Xie and Xu 2021
the authors consider a notion of

with respect to the random dot product graph model which includes stochastic blockmodels with positive semidefinite

Theorem 16

(Restatement of Theorems 2 and 5 in
Xie and Xu 2021
)
.

Fix
K
and
B
. Suppose that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
with
n_{k}/n\to\pi_{k}\in(0,1)
for all
1\leq k,l\leq n
. Let
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
. Suppose that there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that
B={\nu}{\nu}^{\top}
satisfies
\delta\leq B_{kl}\leq 1-\delta
for all
k,l
. Let
P=\rho_{n}XX^{\top}
, and suppose that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
with
n_{k}/n\to\pi_{k}\in(0,1)
for all
1\leq k,l\leq n
. Define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}(\delta)\coloneqq\left\{x:x^{\top}u\in[\delta,1-\delta]\text{ for all $u\in\mathcal{X}(\delta)$}\right\}.

Define

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{i*}^{(\operatorname{MLE})}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathop{\rm arg\max}_{x\in\mathcal{X}(\delta)}\sum_{j\neq i}\{A_{ij}\log(x^{\top}X_{j*})+(1-A_{ij})\log(1-x^{\top}X_{j*})\};

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{i*}^{(\operatorname{OS})}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widehat{X}_{i*}+\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\widehat{X}_{j*}\widehat{X}_{j*}^{\top}}{\widehat{X}_{i*}^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j*}(1-\widehat{X}_{i*}^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j*})}\bigg\}^{-1}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(A_{ij}-\widehat{X}_{i*}^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j*})\widehat{X}_{j*}}{\widehat{X}_{i*}^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j*}(1-\widehat{X}_{i*}^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j*})}\bigg\},

where
\widehat{X}_{i*}^{(\operatorname{MLE})}
is computed assuming
\{X_{j*}\}_{j\neq i}
are known, and
\widehat{X}
are the scaled eigenvectors of
A
.
Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}(k)\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{K}\frac{\pi_{l}}{B_{kl}(1-B_{kl})}{\nu}_{l*}{\nu}_{l*}^{\top}.

Then, as
n\to\infty
, if
\rho_{n}\equiv 1
,

\displaystyle\bigg(n^{1/2}\big(\widehat{X}_{i*}^{(MLE)}-{\nu}_{k*}\big)\bigg)\mid\tau(i)=k\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}\big(0,\mathbf{G}(k)^{-1}\big).

Similarly, if
n\rho_{n}^{5}\gg\log^{2}(n)
, then there exists a sequence of orthogonal matrices
W
such that

\displaystyle\bigg(n^{1/2}\big((

\displaystyle\widehat{X}^{(\operatorname{OS})}W)_{i*}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}{\nu}_{k*}\big)\bigg)\mid\tau(i)=k\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{G}(k)^{-1}).

Finally, define
\Sigma_{S}(k)\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}^{(n)}(k)
, with
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}^{(n)}(k)
as in
Theorem

7
(which exists under the present assumptions). Then,

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(k)-\mathbf{G}(k)^{-1}
is positive semidefinite for all
x\in\mathcal{X}(\delta)
.

The estimator
\widehat{X}_{i*}^{(\operatorname{MLE})}
is obtained by maximizing the random dot product graph likelihood assuming that
X_{j*}
is known for
j\neq i
, which is not possible in practice. On the other hand, the vector
\widehat{X}_{i*}^{(\operatorname{OS})}
is obtained by starting with
\widehat{X}
as an initial estimate and then performing a Newton–Raphson step update for
\widehat{X}_{i*}
with respect to the random dot product graph likelihood. In both cases, these procedures yield estimators for
{\nu}_{k*}
, modulo orthogonal transformation, that achieve the limiting variance
\mathbf{G}(k)^{-1}
which is no larger than the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{X}_{i*}
with respect to the Loewner order. For the particular rank one stochastic blockmodel with
B
matrix

\displaystyle B\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}p^{2}&pq\\
pq&q^{2}\end{pmatrix},

the authors show that the one-step covariance in the first community is strictly smaller than that of the covariance in

unless

. However, these results require stronger sparsity assumptions than other results, and it is not clear how the asymptotic covariance matrix is affected with either indefinite stochastic blockmodels or considering the update with respect to the SBM likelihood function, as opposed to the RDPG likelihood function. The above results were later extended in
Xie 2024
to the setting

.

4.7
Subgraph Counts

For network data, the probabilistic concept of
moments
can be understood in the language of
subgraph counts
, namely counts and frequencies of specified subgraphs appearing in a network. The study of the so-called
method of moments
for network data was initiated in
Bickel et al. 2011
with numerous follow-on works in recent years. Considerable effort in this direction has centered around the latent position random network, defined as follows.

Definition 6

(Latent position network – undirected, loopy)
.

We say that an adjacency matrix
A
is a latent position random network on
n
vertices with sparsity term
\rho=\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
if, conditional on
\{X_{i\star}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, its upper triangular entries are generated independently in the manner

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\rho\cdot f\left(X_{i*},X_{j*}\right)\right),&\textnormal{~for~}i\leq j,\\
A_{ji}&\textnormal{~for~}i>j,\end{cases}

where
X_{i*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
satisfies
f(x,y)=f(y,x)
. The variables
\{X_{i*}\}_{i=1}^{n}
may be deterministic or modeled in a generative fashion, e.g., i.i.d.
X_{i*}\sim F_{X}
for a distribution
F_{X}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The latent position network with
d=1
and
F_{X}
being the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
is often referred to as a so-called
graphon
, though in some settings the function
f
is itself referred to as a graphon. When
f
is the (pseudo) inner product, one arrives at the (generalized) random dot product graph.
Definition

6
therefore encompasses a broad family of models beyond the low-rank network models discussed in previous sections. A latent position network can be associated to an infinite-dimensional version of a generalized random dot product graph via its so-called
graph root distribution
as studied in
Lei 2021
.

Next, we highlight the method of moments for networks when, for simplicity,
\rho_{n}
is known. Suppose
G
is a subgraph of interest with
r
vertices, that is,
G\subset\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq n\}
with
|G|=r
. Let
A(G)
denote the subgraph of
A
induced by the vertices in
G
. The corresponding network moment are defined as

\displaystyle U_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\rho_{n}^{-r}\mathbb{P}\left[E(A(G))=G\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{(i,j)\in G}f(X_{i},X_{j})\prod_{(i,j)\in\widetilde{G}}(1-\rho_{n}f(X_{i},X_{j}))\right\},

where
E(\cdot)
denotes the edge set of a graph, and
\widetilde{G}
is the set of edges that do not appear in
G
but are incident to vertices in
G
.
Define the (normalized) empirical network moment as

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\rho_{n}^{r}N(G)}{n\choose r}^{-1}\sum_{H\subset A}\mathbb{I}_{\{H\sim G\}},

where the notation
H\sim G
indicates that the graph
H
is isomorphic to
G
, the summation is over all subgraphs
H
of
A
of size
r
, and
N(G)
denotes the number of graphs isomorphic to
G
. The asymptotic normality of
\widehat{U}_{n}
was obtained in Theorem 1 of
Bickel et al. 2011
.

Theorem 17

(Theorem 1 in
Bickel et al. 2011
)
.

Suppose that
\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f^{2}(u,v)\operatorname{d}v\operatorname{d}u<\infty
. Suppose that
G
is fixed with
\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f^{2r}(u,v)\operatorname{d}u\operatorname{d}v<\infty
. If
G
is acyclic and
n\rho_{n}\to C\in(0,\infty]
, then

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\widehat{U}_{n}-U_{n}\big)\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}(G)),

(15)

where
\sigma^{2}(G)
depends on
C
if
C<\infty
, and otherwise does not. If instead
G
is cyclic and
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{1-2/r}
, then
Eq.

15
continues to hold.

The asymptotic variance,
\sigma^{2}(G)
, can be difficult to compute and in general depends on the variance of the associated
U
-statistic

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N(G)}{n\choose r}^{-1}\sum_{H\sim G}\Bigg\{\prod_{(i,j)\in H}f(X_{i},X_{j})\prod_{(i,j)\in\widetilde{H}}(1-\rho_{n}f(X_{i},X_{j}))\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{U}_{n}\mid\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\big).

When
n\rho_{n}
diverges,
\sigma^{2}(G)
is equal to the asymptotic variance of
\sqrt{n}(\widetilde{U}_{n}-U_{n})
, implying that the contribution of the Bernoulli noise of the network
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{U}_{n}-\widetilde{U}_{n})
is asymptotically negligible relative to the contribution of
\sqrt{n}(\widetilde{U}_{n}-U_{n})
.

In the time since
Bickel et al. 2011
, a number of papers have studied the theoretical properties of network moments for bootstrapping
((
Bhattacharyya and Bickel 2015
;
Levin and Levina 2019
;
Lunde and Sarkar 2023
;
Lin et al. 2020
;
Green and Shalizi 2022
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Xia 2022
)
and for hypothesis testing
((
Gao and Lafferty 2017
;
Maugis et al. 2020
;
Maugis 2023
;
Mitra et al. 2024
;
Shao et al. 2024
)
.
For example,
Zhang and Xia 2022
develops Edgeworth expansions for network moments, showing that the additional Bernoulli noise contributes a so-called smoothing effect to the higher-order accuracy of the Gaussian distribution.
Levin and Levina 2019
considers low-rank networks and shows that by leveraging the low-rank property it is possible to develop a consistent bootstrap procedure for subgraph counts.

5
Hypothesis Testing and Multiple-Network Normality

Network hypothesis testing takes many forms depending on how the statistical unit of interest is specified. Units of interest can include, for example, individual (or several) vertices, representing microscale (local) structure, or individual (or several) entire networks, representing macroscale (global) structure. Each scale or level of resolution has different practical applications and interpretations, and, depending on the inference task at hand, the asymptotic normality results presented in the previous section may be adaptable to designing and analyzing test statistics tailored to inference problems. This section focuses primarily on test statistics motivated by the asymptotic normality results presented in the previous sections.

5.1
Vertex-Level Tests

One problem of interest is testing whether two vertices share certain properties such as latent community memberships. In this vein,
Fan et al. 2022b
considers the problem of testing whether two vertices from a general degree-corrected mixed-membership model have the same membership parameters, where the null hypothesis for nodes
i,j
is

\displaystyle H_{0}:Z_{i*}=Z_{j*}.

The following vertex-level testing result is obtained.

Theorem 18

(Restatement of Theorems 1 and 2 in
Fan et al. 2022b
)
.

Fix
K
and
B
, where
B
has full rank. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\geq n^{\eta}
for some
\eta>0
and that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l,\leq K
. Let
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
, where
P
has
K
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{K}
satisfying
\min_{i\neq j}|\lambda_{i}|/|\lambda_{j}|\geq 1+c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
for
\lambda_{i}\neq-\lambda_{j}
. Suppose also that the eigenvalues of
(\rho_{n})^{-1}\operatorname{Cov}((e_{i}-e_{j})^{\top}(A-P)U)
are bounded away from zero and infinity. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(i,j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\operatorname{Cov}\big((e_{i}-e_{j})^{\top}(A-P)U\Lambda^{-1}\big).

Define

\displaystyle T^{2}_{U}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big(\widehat{U}_{i*}-\widehat{U}_{j*}\big)^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{-1}(i,j)\big(\widehat{U}_{i*}-\widehat{U}_{j*}\big).

Then, under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:Z_{i*}=Z_{j*}
with
\tau(i)=k
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, it holds that

\displaystyle T^{2}_{U}\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\chi^{2}_{K},

where
\chi^{2}_{K}
denotes the chi-squared distribution with
K
degrees of freedom. In addition, under the fixed alternative
Z_{i*}\neq Z_{j*}
, for any constant
C>0
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T^{2}_{U}>C\right]\to 1.

Finally, these results continue to hold for the plug-in test statistic

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{2}_{U}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\widehat{U}_{i*}-\widehat{U}_{j*})^{\top}\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{U}^{-1}(i,j)(\widehat{U}_{i*}-\widehat{U}_{j*}),

provided
\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{U}(i,j)
satisfies

\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{-1}\|\Lambda(\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{U}(i,j)-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(i,j))\Lambda\|\to 0

in probability as
n\to\infty
.

The proofs of the distributional convergence of
T^{2}_{U}
and
\widehat{T}^{2}_{U}
depend on the asymptotics for eigenvectors and eigenvalues from
Fan et al. 2022a
as discussed in
Sections

4.1
and
4.5
. The test statistic
T^{2}_{U}
and its empirical estimate
\widehat{T}^{2}_{U}
can be understood as the analogue of the classical Hotelling
T^{2}
statistic, considering the rows of
U
as the means, and considering
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(i,j)
as the asymptotic covariance of the
i
-th row and
j
-th row of the leading eigenvectors discussed in
Section

4
. In fact, under the additional conditions in
Theorem

2
the limiting matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(k)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{(n)}(k)
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(\tau(i)))+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(\tau(j)).

Theorem

18
does not explicitly define an estimator
\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{U}(i,j)
. The sample eigenvalues are biased as shown in
Fan et al. 2022a
(see also
Athreya et al. 2021
and the requisite discussion in
Section

4.5
), hence their estimator is based on a debiasing procedure for the eigenvalues that can be viewed as a single fixed-point iteration for the fixed-point equation that determines proper centering for the empirical eigenvalues from
Fan et al. 2022a
. See
Eq.

7
.

The aforecited paper also prove a local power result for mixed-membership blockmodels, allowing
Z_{i*}-Z_{j*}
to converge to zero, and it also extends the result to account for degree corrections using the normalization procedure first proposed in
Jin 2015
.

In
Du and Tang 2023
, the authors propose using the scaled eigenvectors to perform a similar test. Under the latent position framework, they study a hypothesis test for the more general hypothesis

\displaystyle H_{0}:X_{i}=X_{j}.

Theorem 19

(Restatement of Theorem 3.2 in
Du and Tang 2023
)
.

Fix
K
and
B
, and suppose that
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gg\log(n)
and that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l,\leq K
. Suppose that there exists a constant
c>0
such that
B_{kl}\geq c
for all
k
and
l
, and that
B
has
p
positive and
q
negative eigenvalues with
p+q=d
. Define

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{2}_{S}\coloneqq(\widehat{X}_{i*}-\widehat{X}_{j*})^{\top}\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}^{-1}_{S}(i,j)(\widehat{X}_{i*}-\widehat{X}_{j*}),

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{S}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\widehat{X}_{i*})+\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\widehat{X}_{j*}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\widehat{X}_{i*})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widehat{\Lambda}^{-1}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{X}_{k*}\widehat{X}_{k*}^{\top}\widehat{X}_{i*}^{\top}{I}_{p,q}\widehat{X}_{k*}\big(1-\widehat{X}_{i*}^{\top}{I}_{p,q}\widehat{X}_{k*}\big)\bigg]\widehat{\Lambda}^{-1}.

Then, under the null hypothesis, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{2}_{S}\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\chi^{2}_{d},

and under the fixed alternative, for any choice of constant
C>0
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{T}^{2}_{S}>C\right]\to 1.

The authors also prove a local power result for
\|X_{i*}-X_{j*}\|\to 0
and provide analogous results for the Laplacian spectral embedding and for degree-corrected stochastic blockmodels. For the latter, they normalize each row by its Euclidean norm instead of the first component of the leading eigenvector. Furthermore,
Du and Tang 2023
extends the central limit theorem for
\widehat{X}
in
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
(
Theorem

6
) to deterministic sequences
P=\rho_{n}X{I}_{p,q}X^{\top}
as opposed to rows
X_{i}
drawn from a latent position distribution.

The matrix
\mathbf{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{S}(i,j)
appearing in
Theorem

19
can be viewed as the plug-in estimate of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(\tau(i))+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(\tau(j))
stemming from the limiting results of
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
. Similarly, the limiting covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(i,j)
in
Theorem

18
corresponds to the asymptotic covariance matrix stemming from the distributional theory for the eigenvectors (e.g.,
Lemma

4
or
Theorem

2
). Moreover, estimating
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(i,j)
requires an additional step to debias the empirical eigenvalues, whereas in
Du and Tang 2023
, the authors show that estimating
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(i,j)
requires no additional debiasing procedure for the eigenvalues. Informally, they show that the matrix
\widehat{X}=\widehat{U}|\widehat{\Lambda}|^{1/2}
already contains information about the spectrum via the eigenvalue scaling, so there is no further need to incorporate additional information to estimate the covariance. Finally, in
Du and Tang 2023
, the authors allow for repeated eigenvalues of the matrix
P=\rho_{n}X{I}_{p,q}X^{\top}
, and sparsity of order
n\rho_{n}=\omega(\log(n))
, whereas in
Fan et al. 2022b
the authors require that
P=\operatorname{diag}(\theta)ZBZ^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\theta)
has distinct eigenvalues and that the sparsity regime satisfies
n\rho_{n}=\omega(n^{\eta})
for some
\eta>0
.

Theory for two-vertex testing was later extended in
Xie 2024
to the setting that
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
, and then further modified to use the one-step updated spectral embeddings as proposed in
Xie and Xu 2021
. Explicitly, using the one-step updated term, the author proposes a similar Hotelling-type
T^{2}
test statistic using the asymptotic variance
\mathbf{G}(k)^{-1}
per
Section

4.6
. Since
\mathbf{G}(k)^{-1}
is no larger than the limiting variance
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(k)
,
Xie 2024
shows that the test statistic using the one-step updated embedding is asymptotically more powerful than the test statistic using
\widehat{T}^{2}_{S}
.

5.2
One-Sample and Two-Sample Network Tests

Network-level tests can be designed to determine whether the network itself or its expected adjacency matrix exhibits certain properties. For low-rank SBM networks, one natural goodness-of-fit consideration is the null hypothesis

\displaystyle H_{0}^{(\operatorname{gof})}:\operatorname{rank}(B)=d_{0}

for a fixed positive integer
d_{0}
, against the alternative
H_{A}:\operatorname{rank}(B)>d_{0}
. The asymptotic normality results presented in
Section

4
all require that
\operatorname{rank}(B)=d
is known and fixed (or can be consistently estimated).
Han et al. 2023
, proposes a test statistic for the rank of the matrix of interest under generic noise model assumptions that include stochastic blockmodels. Similar to
Fan et al. 2022b
, the limiting distribution of the test statistic is closely related to the asymptotics considered in
Fan et al. 2022a
which further emphasizes the need for deriving limit theory for graph embeddings to study test statistics. Their test statistic is motivated as follows. If the underlying matrix
P
is known, then, the matrix
E\coloneqq A-\mathbb{E}[A]
is mean zero and has independent entries. If self-edges are permitted, then the diagonals of
E
constitute
n
independent random draws from a centered Bernoulli distribution, and therefore a rescaled test statistic using the diagonal entries of
E
would yield asymptotic normality. In practice, one does not observe the matrix
P
so it needs to be estimated.
Han et al. 2023
proposes taking the truncated eigendecomposition of
A
and estimating
E
as the residual. More specifically, define

\displaystyle\widehat{P}^{(d_{0})}\coloneqq\widehat{U}_{[1:d_{0}]*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{[1:d_{0}]}\widehat{U}_{[1:d_{0}]*}^{\top},

that is,
\widehat{U}_{[1:d_{0}]*}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{[1:d_{0}]}
consist of the top
d_{0}
eigenvectors and eigenvalues, respectively. From the preceding discussion, under the null hypothesis that
d=d_{0}
, the residual
\widehat{E}^{(d)}\coloneqq A-\widehat{P}^{(d)}
is anticipated to behave approximately like the mean-zero matrix
E
, and test statistics can be constructed using the entries of
\widehat{E}^{(d)}
. Moreover, under the alternative
d_{0}<d
, the matrix
\widehat{E}^{(d_{0})}
should contain some information about the matrix
P
and will not necessarily be approximately mean zero.

A technical challenge is that the entries of the matrix
\widehat{E}^{(d)}
are not independent, and therefore the classical central limit theorem does not apply by simply summing up the entries of
\widehat{E}^{(d)}
. The approach in
Han et al. 2023
is to subsample the edges of
\widehat{E}^{(d)}
in an independent fashion. In particular, consider the test statistic

\displaystyle T_{n,d_{0},m}\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{m}\sum_{i\neq j}\widehat{E}^{(d_{0})}_{ij}Y_{ij}}{\sqrt{2\sum_{i\neq j}\left(\widehat{E}^{(d_{0})}_{ij}\right)^{2}}},

(16)

where
Y_{ij}
are Bernoulli random variables satisfying
\mathbb{P}[Y_{ij}=1]=m^{-1}
for
i<j
and
Y_{ij}=Y_{ji}
otherwise. The following result establishes the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n,d_{0},m}
.

Theorem 20

(Restatement of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 in
Han et al. 2023
)
.

Fix
K
and
B
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim n^{\eta}
for some
\eta>0
and that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for
1\leq k,l,\leq K
. Let
A\sim P
with
P=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
where
P
has
K
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{K}
satisfying
\min_{i\neq j}|\lambda_{i}|/|\lambda_{j}|\geq 1+c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
for
\lambda_{i}\neq-\lambda_{j}
. Further suppose that

\displaystyle n^{1-\eta/2}\ll m\ll\frac{n^{1+\eta}}{\log(n)}.

Under the null hypothesis of
\operatorname{rank}(B)=d_{0}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle T_{n,d_{0},m}\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1).

If the one-sided alternative hypothesis
d>d_{0}
holds and

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{k=d_{0}+1}^{d}\lambda_{k}U_{ik}U_{jk}\right|\ll n\rho_{n},

then as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle T_{n,d_{0},m}-\frac{\sqrt{m}\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{k=d_{0}+1}^{d}\lambda_{k}U_{ik}U_{jk}Y_{ij}}{\sqrt{2\sum_{i\neq j}\left(\widehat{E}^{(d_{0})}_{ij}\right)^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Finally, if instead

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{k=d_{0}+1}^{d}\lambda_{k}U_{ik}U_{jk}\right|\gg\sqrt{m}\left(\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}+\sum_{d_{0}=1}^{d}|\lambda_{k}|\right),

then
\mathbb{P}\left[|T_{n,d_{0},m}|>C\right]\to 1
for any pre-specified constant
C>0
.

The authors also determine more general conditions on
m
such that asymptotic normality holds. The centering in
Theorem

20
reflects the fact that
\widehat{E}^{(d_{0})}
contains information from the low-rank matrix when
d_{0}<d
, as exhibited by the appearance of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
for
d_{0}+1\leq k\leq d
. Specialized to the setting of full-rank stochastic blockmodels, their results enable accurately estimating the number of communities
K
, namely the sequential testing procedure
d_{0}=1,2,\dots,d_{\max}
asymptotically rejects the null hypothesis at a fixed level
\alpha
unless
d=d_{0}
.

The paper
Han et al. 2023
tests whether the rank of
P
is
d_{0}
but does not include a goodness-of-fit test for specific submodels such as testing for stochastic blockmodelness. Earlier,
Lei 2016
proposes a test specifically designed for stochastic blockmodels by estimating the matrix
E
using the estimated communities and then computing the largest eigenvalue of an appropriately rescaled version. The associated limiting null distribution is shown to be Tracy–Widom, though
Han et al. 2023
points out that this test empirically performs poorly for sparser graphs. Similarly,
Wang and Bickel 2017
studies a likelihood-based approach for model selection, though their focus is on estimation (not hypothesis testing).

The related work
Zhang and Amini 2023
proposes a goodness-of-fit test for degree-corrected stochastic blockmodels under the assumption that entries of
A
are Poisson random variables. This work leverages high-dimensional asymptotics for testing multinomials to establish that their proposed test statistic converges in distribution to a Gaussian random variable under the null hypothesis that there are
K
communities. Again, while the considerations there somewhat outside the scope of this survey, the analysis therein showcases how network asymptotics can differ significantly from classical asymptotics, as demonstrated through the interplay between sample size (
n
), sparsity
(\rho_{n})
, and
other model parameters including
K
.

Via
Tang et al. 2022
or
Xie 2023
, it is also possible to devise single-network tests for specific stochastic blockmodel hypotheses. Consider, for example, the setting where the block memberships are known. To test the null hypothesis
B=B^{(0)}
for some fixed full rank matrix
B^{(0)}
, the results in
Tang et al. 2022
imply that the spectral estimator for
B
is asymptotically efficient. Moreover, provided the asymptotic covariance can be estimated consistently, a simultaneous confidence interval for the vectorization of
B
will be asymptotically valid.

In classical two-sample testing, the user tests whether two sets of observations plausibly arise from the same distribution or have similar distributional properties. By analogy, two-graph testing seeks to determine whether two graphs have similar network-centric distributional properties. One version of the two-graph hypothesis test assumes that there is a known
a priori
correspondence between vertices, which is equivalent to testing whether
P^{(1)}=P^{(2)}
, where
P^{(1)}
and
P^{(2)}
denote the probability matrices for two observed adjacency matrices
A^{(1)}
and
A^{(2)}
, respectively. If the correspondence is not known, then this test is equivalent to testing whether
P^{(1)}=\Pi P^{(2)}\Pi^{\top}
, where
\Pi
is a permutation matrix. If one assumes the underlying
P
matrix comes from a stochastic blockmodel with random community assignments, one can test whether the two graphs have the same underlying distributions; this corresponds to testing the hypothesis
(B^{(1)},\pi^{(1)})=(B^{(2)},\pi^{(2})
.

In many application settings, multiple graphs are vertex-matched, namely graphs are observed on the same vertex set with known vertex correspondences. For example, in time series of graphs, one might observe connectivity of the same vertex set evolving over time. Given a specific vertex of interest, its connectivity profile or community membership may change over time. When
B^{(1)}=\gamma B^{(2)}
for some constant
\gamma
,
Li and Li 2018
considers testing whether the community memberships between two networks are equal, with null hypothesis

\displaystyle H_{0}:Z^{(1)}=Z^{(2)}.

Their test statistic can be viewed as a re-weighted
\sin\Theta
distance between the estimated eigenvectors.

Theorem 21

(Restatement of Theorem 5 and Theorem 7 in
Li and Li 2018
)
.

Fix
K
.
Suppose that
A^{(1)}\sim P^{(1)}
and
A^{(2)}\sim P^{(2)}
with
P^{(1)}=\rho_{n}Z^{(1)}B(Z^{(1)})^{\top}
and
P^{(2)}=\rho_{n}\gamma Z^{(2)}B(Z^{(2)})^{\top}
, where
B
has the form

\displaystyle B_{kl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a&\textnormal{~if~}k=l,\\
b&\textnormal{~if~}k=l,\end{cases}

with constant
a
,
b
, and
\gamma
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\geq n^{1/2+\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\widehat{U}^{(1)}
be the eigenvectors of
A^{(1)}
and let
\widehat{U}^{(2)}
be the eigenvectors of
A^{(2)}
. Let
\widehat{\Lambda}_{2}
be the eigenvectors of
A^{(2)}
. Define

\displaystyle T_{n,K}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Kn}\|(\widehat{U}^{(1)}W_{1,2}-\widehat{U}^{(2)})\widehat{\Lambda}_{2}\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{2},

where
W_{1,2}=\argmin_{W\in\mathbb{O}(d)}\|\widehat{U}^{(1)}W-\widehat{U}^{(2)}\|_{\operatorname{F}}
is the Frobenius optimal orthogonal matrix aligning
\widehat{U}^{(1)}
and
\widehat{U}^{(2)}
. Then, under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:Z^{(1)}=Z^{(2)}
, and provided each community size is order
n/K
, in the large-sample limit,

\displaystyle\frac{T_{n,K}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\mu_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}
have the form

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\rho_{n}b(1-\rho_{n}b)+\rho_{n}b(1-\rho_{n}b)+\frac{1}{K}\bigg(\gamma\rho_{n}a(1-\rho_{n}a)+\rho_{n}a(1-\rho_{n}a)-\big(\gamma^{2}\rho_{n}b(1-\rho_{n}b)+\rho_{n}b(1-\rho_{n}b)\big)\bigg),

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{nK}\bigg(\big(\gamma^{2}\rho_{n}b(1-\rho_{n}b)+\rho_{n}b(1-\rho_{n}b)\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{\big(\gamma^{2}\rho_{n}a(1-\rho_{n}a)+\rho_{n}a(1-\rho_{n}a)\big)^{2}}{K}-\frac{\big(\gamma^{2}\rho_{n}b(1-\rho_{n}b)+\rho_{n}b(1-\rho_{n}b)\big)^{2}}{K}\bigg)+o(1).

Let
q_{\alpha/2}
and
q_{1-\alpha/2}
denote quantiles of the standard Gaussian distribution. Under the local alternative
\|Z^{(1)}-Z^{(2)}\|_{0}\geq\frac{CK}{n^{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}}
, with exactly
n/K
vertices in each community, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[q_{\alpha/2}<\frac{T_{n,K}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}<q_{1-\alpha/2}\bigg]\to 1.

The result in
Li and Li 2018
allows
a
and
b
to change with
n
provided
\frac{a-b}{a}\gtrsim\frac{K\log(n)}{n}
, reflecting the setting in which perfect community recovery is information-theoretically possible
((
Abbe 2018
)
. The stated asymptotic normality continues to hold when replacing the parameters with suitable estimators, namely
\mu_{n}\mapsto\widehat{\mu}_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}\mapsto\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
. While
Li and Li 2018
does not explicitly provide such estimators, it is mentioned that they exist using previously established results such as the methodology in
Tang et al. 2022
.

The proof of
Theorem

21
applies the analysis leading to
Theorem

4
, demonstrating that
\|(\widehat{U}^{(1)}W_{1}-U^{(1)})\Lambda^{(1)}\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{2}
is asymptotically Gaussian after appropriate normalization and scaling. This argument exploits the particular structure of
B
together with the approximation

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{U}^{(1)}W-U^{(1)})\Lambda^{(1)}\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\|(A^{(1)}-P^{(1)})U^{(1)}\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{2},

which follows from the discussion in
Section

4.3
. See also
Eq.

9
.

The paper
Tang et al. 2017a
proposes a similar test statistic to
Li and Li 2018
based on Procrustes alignment of the scaled eigenvectors of each graph and shows that their test statistic concentrates about a constant quantity determined by the distributions of each graph. This constant can also be viewed as the analogue of
\mu_{n}
in the limit theorem for
Li and Li 2018
.
Tang et al. 2017a
does not study the explicit limiting distribution of their test statistic, and their main results depend primarily on a concentration inequality for the Frobenius norm difference between the scaled eigenvectors. See also
Agterberg et al. 2020
;
Athreya et al. 2023
for similar concentration results.

Several other tests have been proposed for two graphs
((
Agterberg et al. 2020
;
Ghoshdastidar et al. 2017
;
Ghoshdastidar et al. 2020
;
Tang et al. 2017a
;
Tang et al. 2017b
)
, though either they do not have associated asymptotic normality guarantees or are not focused on stochastic blockmodels.

5.3
Multiple-Graph Asymptotics

The two-sample testing results in
Theorem

21
are closely related to the asymptotic theory derived for a single network in
Theorem

4
. Analogously, testing procedures relying on similar theory will typically require first embedding each individual network separately and then aligning them, say via Procrustes rotations. In many practical situations networks may share some structure such as communities but may contain some network-level differences such as edge probabilities, so a
joint graph embedding
may be desirable, namely, an embedding that includes both shared information from each network and individual vertex information within each network.

Assuming each graph has the same probability matrix,
Levin et al. 2019
studies a joint embedding constructed from the scaled eigenvectors of the so-called
omnibus
matrix. Given
L
independent vertex-matched graphs, the
omnibus matrix

\widehat{M}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A^{(1)}&\frac{1}{2}(A^{(1)}+A^{(2)})&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}(A^{(1)}+A^{(L)})\\
\frac{1}{2}(A^{(1)}+A^{(2)})&A^{(2)}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}(A^{(2)}+A^{(L)})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{2}(A^{(1)}+A^{(L)})&\frac{1}{2}(A^{(2)}+A^{(L)})&\cdots&A^{(L)}\end{pmatrix}.

Obtaining an embedding for
\widehat{M}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
does not require additional Procrustes alignment that would otherwise be needed if each graph were separately embedded. Here,
Levin et al. 2019
derives asymptotic normality guarantees for the scaled eigenvectors of
\widehat{M}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
when all the block probability matrices are the same.

Theorem 22

(Restatement of Theorem 1 in
Levin et al. 2019
)
.

Fix
K,L
and
B={\nu\nu}^{\top}
. Let
A^{(l)}\sim P^{(l)}
, with
P^{(l)}\equiv ZBZ^{\top}
with
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
. Let
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
denote the
Ln\times d
and
d\times d
matrices of leading eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\widehat{M}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
respectively. There exists a sequence of orthogonal matrices
W_{*}
such that as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg(n^{1/2}\big((\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}\widehat{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}^{1/2}W_{*})_{(n-1)l+i*}-{\nu}_{k*}\big)\bigg)\mid\tau(i)=k

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,\tfrac{L+3}{4L}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(k)),

where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{S}(k)
is defined in
Theorem

6
with
q=0
.

The coefficient
\frac{L+3}{4L}
equals one when
L=1
, and so the resulting asymptotic covariance matrix here matches the asymptotic covariance matrix for the rows of
\widehat{X}
for a single network, as in
Section

4
. More generally,
Pantazis et al. 2022
shows that this coefficient is determined by the choice of weights assigned to the diagonal and off-diagonal block matrices. For example, if, instead of weighting each block-diagonal element by the average of each corresponding matrix (i.e.,
\frac{1}{2}
weighting to both
A^{(i)}
and
A^{(j)}
), one simply uses the average
\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}A^{(l)}
, then the limiting variance is instead scaled by
\frac{L^{2}+L-1}{L^{3}}\asymp\frac{1}{L}
, reflecting the fact that variability shrinks as more networks
L
are observed for this particular choice of weighting. See
Pantazis et al. 2022
for further discussion on how the choice of weighting induces correlation between rows of the associated embedding.

Theorem

22
requires
P^{(l)}=ZBZ^{\top}
for all
l
, which can be restrictive. The asymptotic normality of the omnibus embedding and associated bias was further studied in
Draves and Sussman 2021
when
P_{ij}^{(l)}=X_{i*}^{\top}C^{(l)}X_{j*}
, where
C^{(l)}
is a diagonal matrix with positive entries, giving the following result.

Theorem 23

(Restatement of Theorem 1 in
Draves and Sussman 2021
for Stochastic Blockmodels)
.

Fix
K
and the
K\times K
matrix
\nu
. Let
A^{(l)}\sim P^{(l)}
with
P^{(l)}=ZB^{(l)}Z^{\top}
with
B^{(l)}={\nu}C^{(l)}{\nu}^{\top}
for some diagonal matrix
C^{(l)}
with nonnegative diagonal elements, and suppose that
\min_{i}\max_{l}C^{(l)}_{ii}>0
, with
A^{(l)}
independent of
A^{(l^{\prime})}
.
Suppose also that
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
and suppose that
\sum_{k}\pi_{k}{\nu}_{k*}{\nu}_{k*}^{\top}
is diagonal and full rank. Let
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
be as in
Theorem

22
. There exists an orthogonal matrix
W_{*}
such that with high probability it holds that

\displaystyle\bigg\{\bigg(\big(\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}\widehat{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}^{1/2}W_{*}\big)_{n(l-1)+i*}-\sqrt{C^{(l)}}{\nu}_{k*}\bigg)\mid\tau(i)=k\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\big(\widetilde{C}^{(l)}-\sqrt{C^{(l)}}\big){\nu}_{k*}+N_{n(l-1)+i},

with
\widetilde{C}^{(l)}=\frac{1}{2}\big[C^{(l)}C^{-1/4}+C^{1/4}\big]
,
C=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(C^{(l)})^{2}
, and
N_{n(l-1)+i}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|N\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{L^{3/2}\log(nL)}{n^{1/2}}.

The above decomposition resembles the discussion in
Section

4.3
, though there is an additional term of the form
\big(\widetilde{C}^{(l)}-\sqrt{C^{(l)}}\big){\nu}_{k*}
appearing on the right hand side, and the term
N
behaves as a first-order error, similar to the bounds in
Theorem

5
. Succinctly, the rows of the scaled eigenvectors of
\widehat{M}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}
are biased away from
\sqrt{C^{(l)}}{\nu}_{k*}
, with bias depending on the additional scaling factor
C^{(l)}
associated to the
l
-th network. The authors also prove a normality result for the rows of
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}\widehat{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{OMNI}}^{1/2}
using this decomposition, via additional expansions for the concentration of the noisy bias about its mean, and the expansion of the term
N_{(l-1)+i}
.

For the purpose of jointly clustering based on multiple graphs, observing more networks improves inference and clustering by boosting signal. Consider the setting where
A^{(1)},\dots,A^{(L)}
all satisfy
\mathbb{E}[A^{(l)}]=\rho_{n}ZBZ^{\top}
for some
\rho_{n}
,
Z
, and
B
not depending on
l
. Let
\bar{A}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l}A^{(l)}
, and let
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{ave}}
denote the leading
d
eigenvectors of
\bar{A}
. By an application of the
\ell_{2,\infty}
bound in
Chen et al. 2021
(see
Theorem

1
), the concentration of the leading eigenspace improves by a factor of
L^{-1/2}
. Similarly, using the proof methods mentioned in previous sections, asymptotic normality guarantees can be derived for this averaged matrix as both the number of vertices and the number of graphs tends to infinity, thereby providing an initial justification for using more networks in jointly clustering vertices.

The argument in the previous paragraph requires that the networks have the same block probability matrices.
The
multilayer stochastic blockmodel
posits that between graphs (layers) the memberships remain constant while the probabilities of community membership are allowed to vary between graphs. Various algorithms have been proposed for clustering the networks jointly based on spectral methods
((
Chen et al. 2022
;
Lei and Lin 2022
;
Paul and Chen 2016
;
Paul and Chen 2020
)
or tensor decompositions
((
Jing et al. 2021
;
Lei et al. 2020a
;
Lyu et al. 2023
)
, though the focus of these is often on weak recovery.

Pertaining to asymptotic normality,
Jones and Rubin-Delanchy 2021
;
Arroyo et al. 2021
study algorithms for embeddings in specific multiple graph models that respectively subsume particular instantiations of the multilayer stochastic blockmodel. In
Arroyo et al. 2021
the authors study an algorithm for subspace recovery under the assumption that each graph probability matrix shares a common subspace, in the sense that it can be factored as
US^{(l)}U^{\top}
, where
S^{(l)}
is a so-called score matrix corresponding to the
l
-th graph, and
U
is an orthonormal matrix representing the common subspace. For a multilayer stochastic blockmodel, one may take
U=Z(Z^{\top}Z)^{-1/2}
with
S^{(l)}=(Z^{\top}Z)^{1/2}B^{(l)}(Z^{\top}Z)^{1/2}
.

Let
\widehat{U}^{(l)}
denote the matrix of leading
d
eigenvectors of
A^{(l)}
, and define
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{MASE}}
to be the leading
d
eigenvectors of the matrix
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\widehat{U}^{(l)}(\widehat{U}^{(l)})^{\top}
. Note that
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{MASE}}
can equivalently be expressed as the left singular vectors of the matrix
[\widehat{U}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{U}^{(L)}]
. Define

\displaystyle\widehat{S}^{(l)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{MASE}}^{\top}A^{(l)}\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{MASE}}.

The authors prove the following asymptotic normality result for their estimates
\widehat{S}^{(l)}
.

Theorem 24

(Restatement of Theorem 11 in
Arroyo et al. 2021
for MLSBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
L
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gg\log(n)
and that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l\leq K
. Suppose that
A^{(l)}\sim P^{(l)}
with
P^{(l)}=\rho_{n}ZB^{(l)}Z^{\top}
, with
B^{(l)}
full rank and fixed. Define

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(l)}_{\frac{2k+q(q-1)}{2},\frac{2k+q(q-1)}{2}}=\sum_{s=1}^{n-1}\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}P_{st}^{(l)}(1-P_{st}^{(l)})\big[(U_{sk}U_{tq}+U_{tk}U_{sq})(U_{sk^{\prime}}U_{tq^{\prime}}+U_{sk^{\prime}}U_{tq^{\prime}})\big].

Let
\widehat{S}^{(l)}
be defined as above.
There exists a sequence of orthogonal matrices
W
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg(\Sigma^{(l)}_{\frac{2k+q(q-1)}{2},\frac{2k^{\prime}+q^{\prime}(q^{\prime}-1)}{2}}\bigg)^{-1/2}\big(W\widehat{S}^{(l)}W^{\top}-S^{(l)}+H^{(l)})_{kq}

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where

is a stochastic offset term which satisfies
\mathbb{E}\|H^{(l)}\|_{\operatorname{F}}=O(dL^{-1/2})
.

The authors study the vectorization of
\widehat{S}^{(l)}
when the matrix
\Sigma^{(l)}
is assumed to have its smallest eigenvalue bounded away from zero.
Theorem

24
can be compared to, for example
Theorem

12
or

, which demonstrate that the empirical eigenvalues of a single random graph are asymptotically biased.
When
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{1/2}
,
Zheng and Tang 2022
consider the explicit form of the bias term

.

Theorem 25

(Restatement of Theorem 3.3 in
Zheng and Tang 2022
)
.

Instate the conditions of
Theorem

24
, and let
D^{(l)}
denote the
{n+1\choose 2}\times{n+1\choose 2}

for

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{ii}^{(l)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}^{(l)}(1-P_{ij}^{(l)}).

Let

denote the vector

\displaystyle\mu^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{vech}\bigg(\frac{1}{L}U^{\top}\widetilde{D}^{(l)}U(S^{(l)\top})^{-1}-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{L}S^{(l)}(S^{(l^{\prime})})^{-1}U^{\top}\widetilde{D}^{(l^{\prime})}U(S^{(l^{\prime})\top})^{-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\operatorname{vech}\bigg(\frac{1}{L}(S^{(l)\top})^{-1}U^{\top}\widetilde{D}^{(l)}U-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{L}(S^{(l^{\prime})\top})^{-1}U^{\top}\widetilde{D}^{(l^{\prime})}U(S^{(l^{\prime})})^{-1}S^{(l)}\bigg),

where

is the half vectorization operation that extracts the upper triangle of a matrix, represented as a column vector. There exists a sequence of orthogonal transformations

such that, as

,

\displaystyle\big(\Sigma^{(l)}\big)^{\dagger/2}

\displaystyle\bigg(\operatorname{vech}\big(W\widehat{S}^{(l)}W^{\top}-S^{(l)}\big)-\mu^{(l)}\bigg)\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,\Pi^{(l)}),

where
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse and
\Pi^{(l)}
denotes a symmetric idempotent matrix. If
\Sigma^{(l)}
is invertible, then
\Pi^{(l)}=I
.

In other words, the stochastic bias term
H^{(l)}
can be replaced with a deterministic bias term
\mu^{(l)}
when
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{1/2}
. The additional condition
n\rho_{n}\gg n^{1/2}
is used to establish that the random bias term concentrates sufficiently about its expectation
\mu^{(l)}
. A similar requirement appears in
Theorem

15
. Informally, this condition is required in order to demonstrate that residual terms of order
(n\rho_{n})^{-1}
are of smaller order than
n^{-1/2}
, the latter of which is the rate of concentration about the expectation and the parametric rate of convergence to asymptotic normality. The authors also uses this result to obtain a Hotelling-type
T^{2}
test statistic for testing if
R^{(l)}=R^{(l^{\prime})}
, namely, if
B^{(l)}=B^{(l^{\prime})}
.

The authors in
Zheng and Tang 2022
also demonstrate the following result for the estimates
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{MASE}}
obtained via the eigenvectors of
\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\widehat{U}^{(l)}\widehat{U}^{(l)^{\top}}
.

Theorem 26

(Restatement of Theorem 3.2 in
Zheng and Tang 2022
)
.

Instate the conditions of
Theorem

24
, and suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gg\log^{2}(n)
with
L=O(1)
. There exists a sequence of orthogonal matrices
W_{*}
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg(L^{1/2}n\rho_{n}^{1/2}\big((\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{MASE}}W_{*})_{i*}-U_{i*}\big)\bigg)\mid\tau(i)=k

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\operatorname{MASE}}(k))

with asymptotic covariance matrix given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\operatorname{MASE}}(k)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{n^{2}\rho_{n}}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\big(S^{(l)}\big)^{-1}U^{\top}\Xi^{(l,i)}U\big(S^{(l)}\big)^{-1},

where
\Xi^{(l,i)}
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal elements
\Xi^{(l,i)}_{kk}=P_{ik}^{(l)}(1-P_{ik}^{(l)})
.

The matrices
S^{(l)}
,
U
, and
\Xi^{(l,i)}
change with
n
, but, due to the regularity conditions imposed in
Zheng and Tang 2022
, the matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\operatorname{MASE}}(k)
is well-defined. It is also possible to show that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\operatorname{MASE}}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(k),

where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}(k)
is the (asymptotic) covariance matrix appearing in
Theorem

2
. To prove
Theorem

26
,
Zheng and Tang 2022
demonstrates that
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{MASE}}
behaves as an empirical average of the first-order perturbations of each of the individual networks. Namely, with high probability,

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{MASE}}W-U

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(A-P)U^{(l)}(\Lambda^{(l)})^{-1}+R_{\operatorname{MASE}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(A-P)U(S^{(l)})^{-1}+R_{\operatorname{MASE}},

where
R_{\operatorname{MASE}}
is a residual term similar to the residual terms in
Eq.

9
.

The authors in
Jones and Rubin-Delanchy 2021
consider a multiple random graph model that subsumes the multilayer stochastic blockmodel by allowing for general edge probabilities of the form
P^{(l)}=X\Lambda^{(l)}X^{\top}
, where
\Lambda^{(l)}
is a general symmetric matrix for the
l
-th graph. For SBMs, one parameterization is
X_{i}=Z_{i*}
and
\Lambda^{(l)}=B^{(l)}
, where
B^{(l)}
is the block probability matrix for the
l
-th graph. Therein, the so-called unfolded adjacency matrix
A_{\operatorname{unfold}}
is given by

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{unfold}}\coloneqq[A^{(1)},A^{(2)},\dots,A^{(L)}]\in\{0,1\}^{n\times nL},

with the goal of estimating the scaled left and right singular vectors of the matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{unfold}}
, defined analogously.

Theorem 27

(Restatement of Theorem 3 in
Jones and Rubin-Delanchy 2021
for Multilayer SBMs)
.

Fix
K
and
L
. Suppose that
n\rho_{n}\gg\log^{c}(n)n^{1/2}
for some universal constant
c>0
, and suppose
\mathbb{P}[\tau(i)=k]=\pi_{k}
. Let
A^{(l)}\sim P^{(l)}
with
P^{(l)}=\rho_{n}ZB^{(l)}Z^{\top}
, where each
B^{(l)}=\nu^{(l)}{I}_{p,q}(\nu^{(l)})^{\top}
. Let
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{unfold}}\widehat{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{unfold}}\widehat{V}_{\operatorname{unfold}}^{\top}
be the top
r
truncated singular value decomposition of
A_{\operatorname{unfold}}
above. There exists a sequence of invertible linear transformations
T
depending on
n
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg(n^{1/2}\big((\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{unfold}}\widehat{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{unfold}}^{1/2}T)_{i*}-e_{k}\big)\bigg)\mid\tau(i)=k

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\operatorname{unfold}}(k)),

where the asymptotic covariance matrix is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\operatorname{unfold}}(k)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\Delta_{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\mathbf{B}(\Gamma^{(k,1)}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Gamma^{(k,L)})\mathbf{B}^{\top}\Delta_{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}&\textnormal{~if~}\rho_{n}\equiv 1,\\
\Delta_{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\mathbf{B}(\Gamma^{(k,1)}_{o(1)}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Gamma^{(k,L)}_{o(1)})\mathbf{B}^{\top}\Delta_{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}&\textnormal{~if~}\rho_{n}\to 0,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle\equiv B_{\operatorname{unfold}}\coloneqq[B^{(1)},\dots,B^{(L)}],

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{B}(\operatorname{diag}(\pi)\otimes\mathbf{I}_{L})\mathbf{B}^{\top},

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(k,l)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{r=1}^{K}\pi_{r}B^{(l)}_{kr}(1-B^{(l)}_{kr})\nu^{(l)}(\nu^{(l)})^{\top},

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(k,l)}_{o(1)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{r=1}^{K}\pi_{r}B^{(l)}_{kr}\nu^{(l)}(\nu^{(l)})^{\top},

where
\oplus
denotes the direct sum operator, and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

The paper
Jones and Rubin-Delanchy 2021
also establishes similar results for the scaled right singular vectors, and the results therein hold more generally, including for directed, bipartite, and different dimension networks, and for different amounts of individual network sparsity.

Theorem

27
requires that
n\rho_{n}\gg\log^{c}(n)n^{1/2}
, which is a stronger requirement than the minimal requirement
n\rho_{n}\gtrsim\log(n)
for perfect clustering when specialized to the single graph setting. The appearance of the matrices
\Delta_{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{B}
in the definition of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\operatorname{unfold}}(k)
may informally be viewed as a matrix-weighted average of the individual network covariances. In fact, under the condition that each matrix
B^{(l)}
is the same, the covariance in
Theorem

27
matches the corresponding variance in the single-network setting (e.g.,
Theorem

6
), though now scaled by a factor of
L^{-1}
, which coincides with the setting where adjacency spectral embedding is applied on the averaged graph.

Many existing asymptotic normality results require that the absolute value of the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
P
tends to infinity at a rate faster than
\log(n)
. For the
K
-block balanced homogeneous stochastic blockmodel with
n/K
vertices in each community, its smallest eigenvalue is
n(a-b)/K
, and hence this requires
n(a-b)/K\gtrsim\log(n)
. In contrast,
Huang et al. 2023
considers the less-studied setting
n(a-b)/K\ll 1
, wherein the
K
-means clustering error need not decrease to zero as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 28

(Restatement of Proposition 3 in
Huang et al. 2023
)
.

Fix
K
and
L
. Suppose there are precisely
n/K
vertices in each community. Let
A^{(l)}\sim P^{(l)}
, with
P^{(l)}=ZB^{(l)}Z^{\top}
for
1\leq l\leq L
, where each
B^{(l)}
is of the form

\displaystyle B^{(l)}_{kk^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a^{(l)}&\textnormal{~if~}k=k^{\prime},\\
b^{(l)}&\textnormal{~if~}k\neq k^{\prime},\end{cases}

where
a^{(l)}
and
b^{(l)}
are allowed to depend on
n
. For a given nonnegative vector
w
, define

\displaystyle\tau_{n}^{w}\coloneqq\frac{n\left(\sum_{l}w_{l}(a^{(l)}-b^{(l)})\right)^{2}}{\sum_{l}w_{l}^{2}\left(a^{(l)}(1-a^{(l)})+(K-1)b^{(l)}(1-b^{(l)})\right)}.

(17)

Let
\tau_{\infty}^{w}\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n}^{w}
, and suppose that
0<\tau_{\infty}^{w}<\infty
. Further suppose that

\displaystyle\sum_{l}w_{l}^{2}\left(a^{(l)}(1-a^{(l)})+(K-1)b^{(l)}(1-b^{(l)})\right)\gtrsim\frac{\log^{4}(n)}{n},

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{l}w_{l}^{2}a^{(l)}(1-a^{(l)})\right]\left[\sum_{l}w_{l}^{2}b^{(l)}(1-b^{(l)})\right]^{-1}\to 1.

Let
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{K}
be
(K-1)
-dimensional vectors such that the matrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}&\eta_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}&\eta_{K}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}

is orthogonal. Define the vectors
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{K}
as

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\sqrt{\frac{K(\tau_{\infty}^{w}-K)}{\tau_{\infty}^{2}}}\eta_{k}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}.

Let
\widehat{U}^{(w)}
denote the leading
K
eigenvectors of
\sum_{l}w_{l}A^{(l)}
. There exists a sequence of orthogonal matrices
W
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg(n^{1/2}\big(\widehat{U}^{(w)}W\big)_{i*}\bigg)\mid\tau(i)=k\quad\xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}}\quad\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},\mathbf{\Sigma}^{w}),

where
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{w}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}^{w}\coloneqq\frac{K}{\tau_{\infty}^{w}}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&I_{K-1}\end{pmatrix}.

The primary difference between the normality result in
Theorem

28
and the previous asymptotic normality for the unscaled eigenvectors presented in
Theorem

2
is that the probability matrix asymptotically degenerates to that of an Erdős–Rényi random graph (here
a-b\to 0
). The paper
Huang et al. 2023
states a general scenario where the technical conditions are that
a^{(l)}\gtrsim\frac{\log^{4}(n)}{n}
, and
\frac{n(a^{(l)}-b^{(l)})^{2}}{a^{(l)}}\asymp 1
, which reflects sufficiently dense networks with average expected degree of order at least
\log^{4}(n)
but where the communities are nearly indistinguishable. On the other hand, for the setting
a^{(l)}-b^{(l)}\asymp a^{(l)}\asymp b^{(l)}
, the literature typically emphasizes sparse graphs with distinguishable communities. For the regime studied in
Huang et al. 2023
, the optimal misclustering error need not vanish in the limit, so their methodology is based on optimizing over weight vectors
w
that minimize the asymptotic Bayes-optimal misclustering error determined by this limiting distribution. Further, any two rows of
\widehat{U}^{(w)}
are asymptotically independent.

The regime considered in
Huang et al. 2023
is notably different from those most commonly studied in the statistics literature. Several extensions are possible. For example, it remains open to determine whether similar results hold for other types of stochastic blockmodels or multiple stochastic blockmodels with different block probability matrices. The averaging procedure therein requires that
a^{(l)}>b^{(l)}
for all
l
, so it is not clear how to extend their procedure to settings with
b^{(l)}>a^{(l)}
. More generally, it is an open problem to determine optimal clustering procedures in regimes where the cluster separation vanishes but the networks remain dense. See
Kadavankandy and Couillet 2019
for related considerations in a similar model setting.

The order
n^{1/2}
scaling in
Theorem

28
differs from the
n\rho_{n}^{1/2}
scaling in
Theorem

2
. Further, the limiting covariance matrix is degenerate. When
\tau_{\infty}^{w}<\infty
, the leading eigenvalues besides the first eigenvalue are separated from the other eigenvalues but with smaller gaps between distinct eigenvalues, since
n\rho_{n}\ll\log(n)
. Therefore, unlike the settings discussed in
Section

4
, the matrix
A
now consists of three parts: the first is a rank-one matrix corresponding to the (large) leading eigenvalue, the second part is a rank
K-1
matrix corresponding to the bottom (small)
K-1
nonzero eigenvalues, and the third part is a mean-zero matrix. The rank-one component associated to the leading eigenvalue contributes to the degeneracy in the asymptotic covariance. Since the rank-one component is well-separated from the other eigenvalues, it exhibits qualitatively similar phenomena to the leading eigenvectors when all the eigenvalues are spiked, and hence it is plausible for the leading eigenvector to converge in probability to the all ones vector after scaling by
n^{1/2}
. The second part of the matrix contributes to the centering, namely scaling by
n^{1/2}
stabilizes these expectations, though they are now slightly biased away from the population eigenvectors. The remaining term in the decomposition of
A
is a mean-zero (inhomogeneous Wigner) matrix, and the final scaling by
n^{1/2}
is determined by the appropriate scaling from the literature on inhomogeneous Wigner matrices in random matrix theory(e.g.,
Bai and Pan 2012
). All components contribute to the asymptotic normality in
Theorem

28
.

In preceding discussion, the number of layers
L
is assumed fixed as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and thus might not apply to the case that
L\asymp n
.
Several works consider settings where all the network-level block probability matrices
B
are positive semidefinite, implying that an averaging procedure will suffice
((
Bhattacharyya and Chatterjee 2018
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Paul and Chen 2016
;
Paul and Chen 2020
)
. In this setting, developing asymptotic normality theory for scaled and unscaled eigenvectors follows with only slight modifications to previous works as long as
Ln\rho_{n}\gg\log(n)
. For example, focusing on the unscaled eigenvectors, one may develop an analogous first-order expansion to (
9
) via

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{ave}}W-U_{\operatorname{G}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{L}(A^{(l)}-\mathbb{E}A^{(l)})U_{\operatorname{ave}}\Lambda_{\operatorname{ave}}^{-1}+R_{U},

(18)

where
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{ave}}
are the leading eigenvectors of
\sum_{l}A^{(l)}
, and
U_{\operatorname{ave}}
and
\Lambda_{\operatorname{ave}}
are the leading eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\sum_{l}P^{(l)}
, respectively. Here,
R_{U}
is a residual matrix with comparatively small
\ell_{2,\infty}
norm with high probability provided
Ln\rho_{n}\gg\log(n)
.

The statistical theory in the setting that each
B^{(l)}
has a different number of positive and negative eigenvalues has primarily focused on consistency to date
((
Jing et al. 2021
;
Lei and Lin 2022
)
. For example,
Lei and Lin 2022
focuses on spectral clustering via the matrix
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathcal{H}\{(A^{(l)})^{2}\}
, where
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
is the
hollowing operator
that sets the diagonal of a matrix to zero. The empirical eigenvectors of this matrix are shown to be consistent with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{F}
when
\sqrt{L}n\rho_{n}\gg\sqrt{\log(n+L)}
. However, there do not appear to exist asymptotic normality results in the literature for this setting, seeing as the hollowing operation introduces non-trivial technicalities in the requisite perturbation analysis. Seemingly the closest such result is for asymmetric matrices from
Yan et al. 2024
, who use the algorithm
HeteroPCA
proposed in
Zhang et al. 2022a
and also analyzed in
Agterberg et al. 2022
. We omit a detailed discussion for brevity, but in essence the algorithm impute the diagonals starting from the initialization
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathcal{H}\big\{(A^{(l)})^{2}\big\}
, where
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
is the
hollowing operator
that sets the diagonal of a matrix to zero.

Theorem 29

(Restatement of Theorem 5 in
Yan et al. 2024
for SBMs)
.

Fix
K
.
Suppose that
n_{k}\asymp n_{l}
for all
1\leq k,l\leq K
, that
\sqrt{L}n\rho_{n}\gg\log(n)
, that
Ln\rho_{n}\gg\log(n)
, and that
L\leq Cn
.
Let
A^{(l)}\sim P^{(l)}
consist of independent entries (directed networks), where
P^{(l)}=\rho_{n}ZB^{(l)}Z^{\top}
, and where each
B^{(l)}
satisfies
c\leq|\lambda_{\min}(B^{(l)})|\leq\lambda_{\max}(B^{(l)})\leq C
, for some fixed constants
c,C>0
.
Let
\widehat{U}_{\operatorname{HP}}
denote the output of the
HeteroPCA
algorithm for
t_{0}
iterations, with
t_{0}\geq\log(C\sqrt{L}n\rho_{n})
, and let
U_{\operatorname{HP}}
denote the leading
K
eigenvectors of
\sum_{l}(P^{(l)})^{2}
.
Finally, define
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq[P^{(1)},\cdots P^{(L)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times nL}
and suppose that
\mathcal{P}
has singular value decomposition
U_{{\operatorname{HP}}}\Lambda_{{\operatorname{HP}}}V_{{\operatorname{HP}}}^{\top}
. Then, there exists an orthogonal matrix
W
such that with probability at least
1-O(\max(n,L)^{-10})
,

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{{\operatorname{HP}}}W-U_{{\operatorname{HP}}}=(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{P})V_{{\operatorname{HP}}}\Lambda_{{\operatorname{HP}}}^{-1}+\mathcal{H}\{(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{P})(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{P})^{\top}\}U_{{\operatorname{HP}}}\Lambda_{{\operatorname{HP}}}^{-2}+R,

where the matrix
R
satisfies

\displaystyle\|R\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{L}n^{2}\rho_{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{\log(n)}}{n\sqrt{Ln\rho_{n}}}.

The above presentation differs significantly from the original statement in
Yan et al. 2024
but can be established using the fact that the squared singular values of
\mathcal{P}
are equivalent to the eigenvalues of
\sum_{l}(P^{(l)})^{2}
, and hence are of order
Ln^{2}\rho_{n}^{2}
, by taking
n_{2}
therein to be
nL
, and by using the fact that the eigenvectors of
\sum_{l}(P^{(l))^{2}}
are incoherent. Furthermore, the results in
Yan et al. 2024
are for a very general model with independent heteroskedastic noise, a special case of the setting considered herein.
For simplicity, we have focused on the setting that
L\leq Cn
, but a similar result can be stated even when
L\gg n
provided one replaces
\log(n)
with
\log(L)
and one modifies the bound on the residual term appropriately. In particular,
Theorem

29
holds with slight modifications even when
L\to\infty
. However, unlike the asymptotic expansion in (
9
),
Theorem

29
contains two terms, the first of which is linear in the noise
\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{P}
, and the second of which is quadratic in this difference. Hence, any subsequent normality result will depend on both the first-order and second-order fluctuations.

6
Discussion

This survey illustrates how properties of the underlying block probability matrix
B
influence the emergence and behavior of asymptotic normality phenomena. For not-too-sparse networks, this survey highlights that the row vectors of data-derived embeddings are asymptotically Gaussian about their population counterparts after proper orientation, with asymptotic covariance affected by the limiting behavior of the sparsity term
\rho_{n}
. Notably, cluster geometry and point cloud shape in low-dimensional Euclidean space are driven by characteristics of underlying network connectivity. Networks with communities that are difficult to distinguish on the basis of connectivity patterns yield highly elliptical cluster geometry compared to settings where communities are well-separated with sufficiently distinct connectivity properties.

Outside of cluster geometry, aggregating information from spectral embeddings also yields asymptotic normality. Furthermore, spectral embeddings and related quantities are useful for hypothesis testing and for parameter estimation. By understanding the geometry of spectral embeddings, one can devise test statistics with known limiting distributions or asymptotically efficient estimators of population parameters. Information-geometric properties also interface with latent space geometry, in the sense that spectral estimators of population parameters, without refinement, can be statistically inefficiency in rank-degenerate stochastic blockmodels. For multiple networks, the particular form of the asymptotic distribution can also depend on the relationships between different block probability matrices. Latent space geometry can substantially change based on the choice of spectral embedding and data aggregation, manifest in the diverse expressions for resulting asymptotic covariance matrices. For example, contrast
Theorems

27
and
26
.

Numerous open problems remain in the study of stochastic blockmodels. Below, we mention several pertinent topics and areas of ongoing investigation.

•

Vanishing or weak signals
. Aside from
Huang et al. 2023
;
Kadavankandy and Couillet 2019
, comparatively few works in the statistics literature address the asymptotic distributional properties of spectral embeddings when the signal strength in the model is either weak or vanishing. Such settings require the further treatment of regularization and normalization when constructing matrix representations of data and specifying the embedding method. More remains to be done.

•

Asymptotic normality of other embeddings
. This survey focuses on embeddings based on eigenvectors and eigenvalues of adjacency or Laplacian matrices. Other graph embeddings are also of interest, both theoretically and in applications, such as the eigenvectors of the
regularized Laplacian
matrix
((
Qin and Rohe 2013
)
, or the
Node2Vec
embedding procedure
((
Grover and Leskovec 2016
)
. Several recent works in this direction include
Barot et al. 2021
;
Cape 2024
;
Kloepfer et al. 2021
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Zhang and Tang 2024
.

•

Choosing between embedding methods
. Different choices of embedding yield different forms of asymptotic normality, depending on whether or not the eigenvectors are scaled by the eigenvalues, or whether one uses the adjacency matrix or Laplacian matrix. Several works have studied the pros and cons of different embedding choices in the presence of network structure (e.g., core-periphery; affinity) per the underlying
B
matrix, including but not limited to
Cape et al. 2019b
;
Tang and Priebe 2018
;
Priebe et al. 2019
, though they are limited to asymptotic regimes wherein perfect node clustering is possible. Deeper theoretical guidance to inform practice is needed.

•

Multiple testing problems for networks
. Throughout the natural and physical sciences, analysts frequently encounter or construct multiple networks corresponding to different populations, for example, brain graphs for healthy subjects, high-risk subjects, and individuals diagnosed with a particular disease. Multi-sample testing, discrimination, and comparison are consequently of interest beyond the two-vertex or two-graph problems discussed in this survey. To date, ongoing efforts in the study of multilayer or multiplex networks are paving the way for a deeper statistical understanding and applicable statistical toolkit for multiple graph inference
((
Fan et al. 2022c
;
MacDonald et al. 2022
;
Pensky and Wang 2024
;
Pensky 2019
;
Pensky and Zhang 2019
;
Zheng and Tang 2022
;
Jones and Rubin-Delanchy 2021
;
Jing et al. 2021
;
Lei and Lin 2022
;
Lei et al. 2020a
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Lyu et al. 2023
)
.

•

Trade-offs between sparsity and the number of networks
. Several works study community detection with a large or growing number of sparse networks
((
Bhattacharyya and Chatterjee 2018
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Jing et al. 2021
;
Lei et al. 2020a
;
Lei and Lin 2022
;
Lyu et al. 2023
;
Paul and Chen 2020
)
. Beyond consistency, it is of interest to study joint network embeddings and understand how resulting cluster geometry reflects network sparsity and sample size.

•

Covariates, dependencies, and model generalizations
. In various applied domains, networks often have vertex covariates or features, and edges need not be independent
Abbe et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2020
;
Ma and Nandy 2023
;
Mele et al. 2022
;
Mu et al. 2022
;
Razaee et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2019
. It will be interesting to further study models and methods that incorporate and leverage such additional information when available.
