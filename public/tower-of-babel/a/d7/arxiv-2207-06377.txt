Tilt-then-Blur or Blur-then-Tilt? Clarifying the Atmospheric Turbulence Model
Stanley H. Chan

Abstract
Imaging at a long distance often requires advanced image restoration algorithms to compensate for the distortions caused by atmospheric turbulence. However, unlike many standard restoration problems such as deconvolution, the forward image formation model of the atmospheric turbulence does not have a simple expression. Thanks to the Zernike representation of the phase, one can show that the forward model is a combination of tilt (pixel shifting due to the linear phase terms) and blur (image smoothing due to the high order aberrations). Confusions then arise between the ordering of the two operators. Should the model be tilt-then-blur, or blur-then-tilt? Some papers in the literature say that the model is tilt-then-blur, whereas more papers say that it is blur-then-tilt. This paper clarifies the differences between the two and discusses why the tilt-then-blur is the correct model. Recommendations are given to the research community.

Introduction

Over the past decade, there is a significant growth of image processing research focusing on mitigating atmospheric turbulence effects present in images and videos taken by long-range cameras

However, unlike many restoration problems such as deconvolution, atmospheric turbulence does not have a simple equation that can describe how images are distorted. In fact, optics textbooks tell us that the turbulent effect is caused by the changing index of refraction along the optical path

From an image processing perspective, we all understand the importance of the forward model but we also realize the difficulty of using wave propagation theory. Thus, in the image processing literature, we often see the so-called “tilt + blur” model
[

,

,

]

shift

. Shall we tilt the image first and then add the blur, or shall we blur the image first and then add the tilt? Or, perhaps it does not matter?

The confusion between the tilt-then-blur and the blur-then-tilt is not a light one. To the best of the author’s knowledge, at least in the literature of image processing, both models have been used. For the tilt-then-blur model, one of the earlier papers is by Shimizu et al. published in CVPR 2008

]

. On the other side of the spectrum, there is a consistent emphasis of the blur-then-tilt model. One of the earlier work is by Mao and Gilles in 2012

The goal of this paper is to clarify the difference between the tilt-then-blur model and the blur-then-tilt model. There are three main conclusions:

•

The blur-then-tilt model is unfortunately wrong.

•

The tilt-then-blur model is correct.

•

For natural images, the difference is less noticeable because many regions are smooth. For point sources, the difference can be significant.

II
Modeling Atmospheric Turbulence

As a wave propagates through the atmospheric turbulence, the phase is distorted by the changing index of refraction. From the image formation point of view, if the clean image is
J(\mathbf{x})
where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
specifies the coordinate in the 2D space, the distortion can be modeled by a spatially varying point spread function (PSF)
h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{u})
. If the image
J(\mathbf{x})
is discretized over a grid of
N
pixels
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\}
, the observed image is

I(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}h_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})J(\mathbf{u}_{j}),\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

(1)

Here,
\mathbf{x}_{i}
specifies the output coordinate at which the distorted pixel should be located, and
\mathbf{u}_{j}
is a running index for the weighted average. (
1
) is the general form of a spatially
varying
convolution. In the special case where the PSF is spatially invarying,
h_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})
can be written as
h(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})
which is the usual convolution kernel.

For atmospheric turbulence, if the source is incoherent (e.g., passive imaging systems without any laser transmitters), the PSF is generated by taking the Fourier transform of the phase
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
via
[
6
,
7
]

h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{u})=|\mathcal{F}(e^{-j2\pi\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})})|^{2}.

(2)

Here, the phase function
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
is defined
per pixel
at each coordinate
\mathbf{x}
. The variable
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the phase coordinate, which is a 2D coordinate in the Fourier space.

The randomness of
h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{u})
comes from the randomness of the phase
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})

[
10
]
.
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
is constructed by cropping and propagating the wave through a sequence of phase screens sampled from the Kolmogorov power spectral density
[
9
,
26
,
8
,
27
]
. This is a computationally expensive process, but recent work has alleviated the difficulty by modeling
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
at the
aperture

[
28
,
29
]
. Using the Zernike polynomials
\{Z_{m}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\,|\,m=1,\ldots,M\}
as the basis representation, Noll
[
30
]
states that we can write
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
as

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_{\mathbf{x},m}Z_{m}(\boldsymbol{\rho}),

(3)

where
\{a_{\mathbf{x},m}\,|\,m=1,\ldots,M\}
denote the
M
Zernike coefficients at coordinate
\mathbf{x}
. The Zernike coefficients are sampled from a zero-mean Gaussian random process

a_{\mathbf{x},m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),

(4)

where the
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},m,m^{\prime})
th component of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is
[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}]_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},m,m^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}[a_{\mathbf{x},m}a_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime},m^{\prime}}]
. The joint expectation follows from Chanan
[
31
]
, Takato and Yamaguchi
[
32
]
, and Chimitt and Chan
[
28
]
.

The representation of
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
in the Zernike space gives us a way to decouple the
shift
(ie, tilt) and the
smoothing
(ie, blur). These two operations can be summarized as follows:

•

Tilt
\mathcal{T}
: The tilt is encoded by the first two Zernike bases via
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\sum_{m=1}^{2}a_{\mathbf{x},m}Z_{m}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
;

•

Blur
\mathcal{B}
: The blur is encoded by the remaining Zernike bases via
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}a_{\mathbf{x},m}Z_{m}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
.

However, how do we combine these two operations? There are a few options, based on the ordering of the tilt and the blur:

\displaystyle\text{Blur-then-tilt}:\;\;\;

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{x})=[\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}](J(\mathbf{x}))=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{B}(J(\mathbf{x}))),

(5)

\displaystyle\text{Tilt-then-blur}:\;\;\;

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{x})=[\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}](J(\mathbf{x}))=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}(J(\mathbf{x}))),

(6)

\displaystyle\text{Full model}:\;\;\;

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{H}(J(\mathbf{x})),

(7)

where “
\circ
” denotes the functional composition. For reference, the full turbulence model
I(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{H}(J(\mathbf{x}))
uses all the Zernike coefficients
simultaneously
without decoupling them into tilts and blurs.

III
Which One is Correct?

The simplest way to compare the models is to run a numerical simulation and see which one is correct. Consider a grid of points as shown in Figure
1
. We apply the three respective models
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathcal{H}
to the points and observe the resulting point spread functions (PSFs). The numerical experiment shows that
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}\not=\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
. Let’s discuss the reasons.

III-A

Analysis from Matrices and Vectors

We discretize the image so that the operations by
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{B}
can be written in terms of matrices and vectors. Specifically, the operator
\mathcal{T}
can be written as a shifting matrix
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
with the
(i,j)
th entry being
[\mathbf{T}]_{ij}=t_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})
, where
t_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})=1
if a pixel located at
\mathbf{u}_{j}
is relocated to coordinate
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, and
t_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})=0
if otherwise. For example, if
J(\mathbf{x})
is a 1D signal and
\mathbf{T}
shifts the signal by one pixel, then
\mathbf{T}
takes the form

\mathbf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&1&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

The operator
\mathcal{B}
is a collection of tilt-free but spatially varying blurs. In the matrix notation, we can define a matrix
\mathbf{B}
where
[\mathbf{B}]_{ij}=b_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})
where
b_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
is the tilt-free blur located at
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. As before,
b_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
is generated by the phase distortion at
\mathbf{x}_{i}
using high-order Zernike coefficients. The overall structure of the matrix
\mathbf{B}
is

\mathbf{B}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})&b_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})\\
b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})&b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})&b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})\end{bmatrix}.

(9)

At this point, it is easy to understand why
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
cannot be the same because matrices do not commute, i.e.,
\mathbf{T}\mathbf{B}\not=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{T}
. In fact, if the tilt matrix is the one shown in (
8
), then
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{T}
shifts
\mathbf{B}
to the left

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})&0\\
b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})&0\end{bmatrix},

whereas
\mathbf{T}\mathbf{B}
shifts
\mathbf{B}
upwards

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}\mathbf{B}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})&b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})&b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})\\
0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Now, the question is: which one is correct? Let us go back to Figure
1
. Imagine that there is only one point source located at
\mathbf{x}
. This point source is a delta function
J(\mathbf{u})=\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})
which will be distorted by the PSF
h(\mathbf{u})
via
I(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}h(\mathbf{u}_{j})\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}_{j})=h(\mathbf{x})
. Suppose that
h(\mathbf{x})
is shifted by an amount
\mathbf{t}
. Physically, the shift is caused by a phase offset in the exponential as can be seen in (
2
). The tilt changes
I(\mathbf{x})=h(\mathbf{x})
to
I(\mathbf{x})=h(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t})
.

Using the tilt example shown in (
8
), the overall operator
\mathcal{H}
is to replace the blur
b_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})
by
b_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j+1})
. Therefore, in terms of matrices and vectors,
\mathcal{H}
can be written as a matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
where

\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})&0\\
b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})&\ldots&b_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathbf{u}_{N})&0\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{T}.

(10)

Notice that the shift occurs
horizontally
in the matrix because we are shifting the blur. We are not relocating the output value to a different pixel location.

III-B

Analysis from Geometry

For readers who like a geometric explanation, here is the analysis. Assume that the blur is spatially invariant so that each blur can be written as

b_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u})=g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{u}).

(11)

Also, for notation simplicity, let us rewrite the tilt operator
\mathcal{T}
as a set of tilt vectors
\{\mathbf{t}_{i}\;|\;i=1,\ldots,N\}
. Each tilt vector
\mathbf{t}_{i}
is used to relocate pixel
J(\mathbf{x}_{i})
to a new coordinate
\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{t}_{i}
:

I_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{t}_{i})=J(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

This new notation can avoid the complication arising from the matrix-vector multiplication.

To clarify different combination of operations, we define the intermediate results
I_{\mathcal{B}}
and
I_{\mathcal{T}}
for blur and tilt only, and the final results
I_{\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}}
and
I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}
for the two operators.

Blur-then-Tilt
. When a blur is applied to the clean image
J(\mathbf{u})
, the intermediate result
I_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x})
is

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})J(\mathbf{u}_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})J(\mathbf{u}_{j}).

This is simply a blurred point source. Now consider the tilt. The tilt
\mathbf{t}_{i}
assigns
I_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
to a new pixel location
\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{t}_{i}
of the final image
I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}
. That is,

I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{t}_{i})=I_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}).

Since
\mathbf{t}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{t}_{N}
is a dense field,
\mathcal{T}
will move
every pixel
individually to a new location. As a result, the blur is not shifted uniformly to a new coordinate but each pixel is shifted to a different coordinate. Thus, the blur is
destroyed
, as shown in Figure
2
.

Fig. 2:
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
moves the center to a new location, whereas
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
moves the points of the blur to different locations.

To prepare for a follow up discussion, we let
\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{t}_{i}
and so
I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{v}_{i})=I_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{t}_{i})
. Since
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is a dummy variable,
I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}
becomes

I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{t}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})J(\mathbf{u}_{j}).

(12)

Tilt-then-Blur
. When tilt is applied to the clean image
J(\mathbf{u})
, the intermediate result
I_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{x})
is

I_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{t}_{j})=J(\mathbf{u}_{j}).

(13)

If now a blur is applied to
I_{\mathcal{T}}
, the blur will simply move to the new location
\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{t}_{j}
. The shape of the blur is
preserved
.

To complete the discussion. we let
\mathbf{v}_{j}=\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{t}_{j}
. Then
I_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{v}_{j})=J(\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{j})
, and

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{v}_{j})I_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{v}_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{j})J(\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{j}).

Replacing
\mathbf{u}_{j}=\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{j}
, it follow that

I_{\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{t}_{j}-\mathbf{u}_{j})J(\mathbf{u}_{j}).

(14)

Comparing (
12
) and (
14
), the difference between the two equations is that for
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
, the tilt is
\mathbf{t}_{i}
whereas for
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
the tilt is
\mathbf{t}_{j}
. In the case of
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
, the tilt
\mathbf{t}_{i}
is applied to the
output
. That is, we blur the image (drawn as a circle in Figure
2
) and move the output by
\mathbf{t}_{i}
. Since each output pixel experiences a different
\mathbf{t}_{i}
, the blur is destroyed. For
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
, although the situation is more complicated because there are
N
tilts
\{\mathbf{t}_{j}\;|\;j=1\ldots,N\}
, they perturb the location of the
input
. Therefore, while each
\mathbf{x}_{i}
sees
N
tilts, the
N
tilts are common for every
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. The shape of the blur is thus preserved.

IV
Impact to Natural Images

Although the two operators
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
are theoretically different, their impacts to real images are less so if the images do not contain any point sources.

To see why this is the case, rewrite (
12
) and (
14
) as

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})J(\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{i}),

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})J(\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{j}).

Then, by approximating
J(\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{i})
and
J(\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{j})
to the first order, the pointwise difference between
I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
and
I_{\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
can be evaluated as

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-I_{\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{j=1}^{N}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})\Big[J(\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{i})-J(\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{j})\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\approx\sum_{j=1}^{N}\underset{\text{convolution}}{\underbrace{g(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})}}\;\;\;\underset{\text{distorted image gradient}}{\underbrace{\underset{\text{image gradient}}{\underbrace{\nabla J(\mathbf{u}_{j})^{T}}}\;\;\;\underset{\text{random tilt}}{\underbrace{(\mathbf{t}_{i}-\mathbf{t}_{j})}}}}.

(15)

An intuitive argument here is that
\mathbf{t}_{i}-\mathbf{t}_{j}
is the difference between two tilt vectors. Since each tilt is a zero-mean Gaussian random vector, the difference remains a zero-mean Gaussian random vector. Although they are not white Gaussian, they are nevertheless
noise
. If there is a large ensemble average of these noise vectors, the result will be close to zero.

So, where does the average comes from? There is a convolution by
g(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})
. If the support of this blur kernel is large, then many of the noise vectors will be added and this will result in a small value. However, for an image with a large field of view, the relative size of the blur kernel
g
is usually not big (at most
30\times 30
for a
256\times 256
image). So, there must be another source that makes the error small.

The main reason why natural images tend to show a less difference between
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
is that the image gradient
\nabla J(\mathbf{u}_{j})
is typically
sparse
. For most parts, the gradient is zero except for edges. (Textures are less of a problem because they will be smoothed by the blur.) When
\nabla J(\mathbf{u}_{j})
is multiplied with the noise vector
\mathbf{t}_{i}-\mathbf{t}_{j}
, the result is an edge map with noise multiplied to every pixel. Convolving
\nabla J(\mathbf{u}_{j})^{T}(\mathbf{t}_{i}-\mathbf{t}_{j})
with a blur kernel
g
will further smooth out the variations. Figure
3
shows a typical example with some standard optical configurations. The difference is not noticeable.

V
Recommendations

This paper illustrated the validity of the tilt-then-blur model and issues of the blur-then-tilt model. The recommendation here is that when describing the imaging through turbulence problem, statements such as

“
… The image formation of atmospheric turbulence follows the equation
I(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{B}(J(\mathbf{x})))
…
”

should be avoided because they are wrong. Instead, it is more appropriate to state that the image formation model is
I(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}(J(\mathbf{x})))
and comment that for natural images it can be approximated by
I(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{B}(J(\mathbf{x})))
.

From a simulation point of view, the computational complexity of implementing
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
are identical if one chooses the Zernike-based multi-aperture model
[
28
]
. Since
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{T}
is
the
correct model, there is no reason to go with an inferior model.

When solving inverse problems, however, there is a bit more freedom. One can choose to recover
\mathcal{T}
first, or one can choose to recover
\mathcal{B}
first, or simultaneously. Speaking of the author’s own experience, recovering
\mathcal{B}
is less recommended because it is spatially varying. Therefore, even though the correct forward model is
I(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}(J(\mathbf{x})))
so that the proper inversion is
J(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{T}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}^{-1}(I(\mathbf{x})))
, it is often easier to estimate
\mathcal{T}
first. The residue error is usually not a problem when deep neural networks are used
[
33
,
34
]
.

In any case, the author hopes that the confusion between blur-then-tilt and tilt-then-blur is settled.
