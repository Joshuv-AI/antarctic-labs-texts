Continuum effective Hamiltonian for graphene bilayers for an arbitrary smooth lattice deformation from microscopic theories
Oskar Vafek, Jian Kang

Abstract
We provide a systematic real space derivation of the continuum Hamiltonian for a graphene bilayer starting from a microscopic lattice theory, allowing for an arbitrary inhomogeneous smooth lattice deformation, including a twist. Two different microscopic models are analyzed: first, a Slater-Koster like model and second, ab-initio derived model. We envision that our effective Hamiltonian can be used in conjunction with an experimentally determined atomic lattice deformation in twisted bilayer graphene in a specific device to predict and compare the electronic spectra with scanning tunneling spectroscopy measurements. As a byproduct, our approach provides electron-phonon couplings in the continuum Hamiltonian from microscopic models for any bilayer stacking. In the companion paper we analyze in detail the continuum models for relaxed atomic configurations of magic angle twisted bilayer graphene.

e^{i(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{K})\cdot\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})}
at
\boldsymbol{K}
corresponds to Bessel functions at high indices with arguments set by the
\partial_{\mu}\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})
, which are exponentially small. Inspecting the tight binding spectra analyzed in the companion paper, which contain the intervalley scattering terms, and comparing them with the spectra obtained from the continuum models which neglect them, indeed justifies neglecting the intervalley scattering terms over the experimentally relevant energy scale.

Different from the earlier works
KoshinoPRB20
, our derivation does not distinguish the intralayer and interlayer Hamiltonians of the continuum theory. Consequently, the inter-layer tunneling obtained from Eqn.
15
depends not only on the asymmetric lattice displacement
\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{t,S}-\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{b,S^{\prime}}
, but also on the gradient of the symmetric part
\boldsymbol{\nabla}(\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{t,S}+\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{b,S^{\prime}})
.

II.1
Bond Orientation Dependent Hopping

In the previous section we derived the effective continuum Hamiltonian when the hopping depends only on the separation of two carbon atoms
\boldsymbol{X}_{j,S}-\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime},S^{\prime}}
, as is the case for Slater-Koster type models
Ando2001
;
Uryu2004
;
SlaterKoster1954
;
MagaudNL10
;
KoshinoPRB12
;
KangVafekPRX
. In such models, the Wannier states are essentially the atomic
p_{z}
orbitals on each carbon atom, and therefore the full azimuthal symmetry is retained making the inter-layer hoppings in-plane isotropic (see Eq.
7
), with no dependence on the three nearest neighbor bond vectors
\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha)}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{X})
at the position of
\boldsymbol{X}_{j,S}
where
\alpha=1,2
, or
3
(see Fig.
1
); and similarly no dependence on
\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime},S^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})
. In the more detailed microscopic model derived from DFT determined Wannier states of the monolayer (and untwisted bilayer) graphene’s conduction and valance bands
KaxirasPRB16
, the localized state indeed has a dominant
p_{z}
character, but the azimuthal symmetry is lost due to the trigonal crystal field of the neighboring atoms. The localized state is therefore a superposition of several lattice harmonics with angular momenta
L_{z}=0
,
3
,
6
, etc. As a consequence, the inter-layer hopping part of
t
acquires the dependence on the relative orientation of the atomic separation vector
\boldsymbol{X}_{j,S}-\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime},S^{\prime}}
and
\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha)}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{X})
,
\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime},S^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})
.

Here we generalize Eq.(
15
) to include such effects on the effective continuum Hamiltonian.
In Eq.(
4
) therefore replace

\displaystyle t(\boldsymbol{X}_{j,S}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j^{\prime},S^{\prime}}^{\prime})\rightarrow

\displaystyle t(\boldsymbol{X}_{j,S}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j^{\prime},S^{\prime}}^{\prime},\{\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha)}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{X})\},\{\boldsymbol{n}_{j^{\prime},S^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})\}),

(16)

where the
\{\}
denotes the dependence on each term in the set, i.e.
\alpha=1,2,3
.
Next, from the definition of the nearest neighbor vectors we can write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha)}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}_{j,\bar{S}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{S}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{(\alpha)})-\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{r}_{S}),

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{(\alpha)}
are the three nearest neighbor bond vectors of the undistorted lattice, that can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(2\pi(\alpha-1)/3)\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{(1)},

(18)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\bar{S}}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{S}\equiv\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S},

(19)

where

is the two-dimensional rotation matrix with the angle
\omega

\displaystyle R(\omega)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\omega&-\sin\omega\\
\sin\omega&\cos\omega\end{array}\right).

With our choice of the coordinate system,
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{A}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{B}=\frac{1}{3}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}+\boldsymbol{a}_{2})
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{A}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{2}-2\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\right)
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{A}^{(3)}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-2\boldsymbol{a}_{2}\right)
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{B}^{(\alpha)}=-\boldsymbol{\delta}_{A}^{(\alpha)}
.

We will consider only the atomic configurations which are varying smoothly not only within a sublattice but also between the two sublattices.
All configurations examined in the companion paper are of this type.
Therefore, we can drop the
S
subscript in Eq.(
3
)

\boldsymbol{X}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{r})\equiv\boldsymbol{X}_{j}(\boldsymbol{r})=\boldsymbol{r}+\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{j}(\boldsymbol{X}_{j})+\boldsymbol{U}^{\perp}_{j}(\boldsymbol{X}_{j}).

(23)

Correspondingly, the bond vectors in Eq.(
17
) become
\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha)}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{X}_{j}^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{r}_{S}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{\alpha})-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{r}_{S})
. Introducing a continuum variable
\boldsymbol{r}
and changing the integration variable to
\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}=\boldsymbol{r}+\boldsymbol{u}^{\parallel}_{j}(\boldsymbol{r})
for each
j
and
S
, as in the previous section makes the bond vectors
\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha)}_{j,S}
a function of
\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}
.
Therefore,
t
in Eq.(
14
) gains an additional dependence on
\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel})
and
\boldsymbol{n}_{j^{\prime},S^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime\parallel})
.
For a smooth atomic displacement fields we can then write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{\parallel}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}-\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{\parallel}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}\right)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{\alpha}\right)-\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle\simeq\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{\alpha}+\delta_{S,\mu}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{\parallel}}{\partial r_{\mu}}\simeq\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{\alpha}+\delta_{S,\mu}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}\right)}{\partial X^{\parallel}_{\mu}},

because

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{j,\nu}^{\parallel}}{\partial r_{\mu}}=\frac{\partial\left(X_{\nu}^{\parallel}-r_{\nu}\right)}{\partial r_{\mu}}=\frac{\partial X_{\nu}^{\parallel}}{\partial r_{\mu}}-\delta_{\mu\nu}=\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}}\right)_{\nu\mu}^{-1}-\delta_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}-\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{j}\right)}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}}\right)_{\nu\mu}^{-1}-\delta_{\mu\nu}\simeq\frac{\partial U^{\parallel}_{j,\nu}}{\partial X^{\parallel}_{\mu}}.

(25)

By going to center-of-mass and relative coordinates, and keeping only term up the first order derivative of
\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}
, we find

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}^{(\alpha)}_{j,S}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\pm\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{y}\right)\simeq\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{(\alpha)}+\delta_{S,\mu}^{(\alpha)}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)}{\partial x_{\mu}}.

(26)

Following the arguments that led to the Eq.(
14
), we find that
H^{\mathbf{K}}_{eff}
can be obtained from Eq.(
15
) if for each layer index
j
,
j^{\prime}
, we drop the sublattice index on
\boldsymbol{U}
i.e. we replace
\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel,\perp}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{x})\rightarrow\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel,\perp}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})
and similarly for
j^{\prime}
,
S^{\prime}
, and we replace

\displaystyle t\left[\boldsymbol{d}_{S,S^{\prime}}\right]\rightarrow

(27)

\displaystyle t\left[\boldsymbol{d},\left\{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{(\alpha)}+\delta_{S,\mu}^{(\alpha)}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)}{\partial x_{\mu}}\right\},\left\{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}+\delta_{S^{\prime},\mu}^{(\alpha)}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{j^{\prime}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)}{\partial x_{\mu}}\right\}\right].

With these replacements, Eq.(
15
) gives the effective continuum Hamiltonian for the bond orientation dependent inter-layer hopping for an arbitrary, sublattice independent, smooth atomic deformation. The additional configuration dependent on-site term is discussed in the next subsection.

II.2
Bond Dependent On-Site Energy

The onsite terms in the tight binding model need to be considered separately because in practice they may not be accounted for accurately by the continuous interpolation function
t
in the expression (
27
). We assume that the difference between the full configuration dependence of the on-site term and the contribution from
t
at
\boldsymbol{d}=0
can be approximated by the form

\displaystyle H_{onsite}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,S}\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}_{S}}\epsilon\left(\left\{|\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{X}_{j,S})|\right\}\right)c^{\dagger}_{j,S,\boldsymbol{r}_{S}}c_{j,S,\boldsymbol{r}_{S}},

(28)

where the onsite energy
\epsilon
is assumed to depend on the length of three nearest bonds
\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{X})
, defined in Eq.
24
. Applying the same methods, we can write

\displaystyle H_{onsite}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A_{mlg}}\sum_{j,S,\boldsymbol{G}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\ e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{S})}\epsilon\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{X}_{j,S})\right\}\right)c^{\dagger}_{j,S,\boldsymbol{r}}c_{j,S,\boldsymbol{r}}.

(29)

Next, we introduce the field operator
\psi_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{r})
via the Eq.
9
in order to obtain the correction to the effective Hamiltonian at the valley
\boldsymbol{K}
from the on-site term. Changing the integration variable
\boldsymbol{r}
to
\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}
introduces the Jacobi determinant
|J(\partial\boldsymbol{r}/\partial\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel})|
. As shown in Eq.
12
, this factor is absorbed by the redefinition of the field operator
\Psi_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel})
. In addition,
e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}=e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}-U^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}))}
. Thus, the onsite term at the valley
\boldsymbol{K}
can be written as

\displaystyle H^{\mathbf{K}}_{onsite}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,S,\boldsymbol{G}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{S}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}\ e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}-U^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}))}

\displaystyle\epsilon\left(\left\{|\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel})|\right\}\right)\Psi^{\dagger}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel})\Psi_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}).

(30)

If
\boldsymbol{G}\neq 0
, the factor
e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}}
oscillates around zero on the scale of the carbon-carbon distance and because it multiplies much more slowly varying functions of
\boldsymbol{X}^{\parallel}
the integral vanishes. Therefore, we can keep only the term with
\boldsymbol{G}=0
in the above sum and obtain

\displaystyle H^{\mathbf{K}}_{eff,onsite}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,S}\int{\rm d}^{2}\boldsymbol{x}\ \epsilon\left(\left\{|\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{x})|\right\}\right)\Psi^{\dagger}_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{x})\Psi_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(31)

To the linear order of gradients of
\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}
, the length of the distorted nearest neighbor bond is

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{x})|\approx|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{\alpha}|+\delta_{S,\mu}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial U^{\parallel}_{j,\mu}}{\partial x_{\nu}}\delta_{S,\nu}^{\alpha}/|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{\alpha}|,

(32)

where the length of the undistorted nearest neighbor bond vectors is the same
|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{S}^{\alpha}|=a/\sqrt{3}
. Thus, to leading order gradient expansion, the onsite energy
\epsilon
is

\epsilon\left(\left\{|\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{x})|\right\}\right)\approx\epsilon\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\right)+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{a}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\frac{\partial\epsilon_{0}}{\partial|\delta_{S}^{\alpha}|}\delta_{S,\mu}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial U^{\parallel}_{j,\mu}}{\partial x_{\nu}}\delta_{S,\nu}^{\alpha}.

(33)

Due to
C_{3}
and space inversion symmetries,
\partial\epsilon/\partial|\delta_{S}^{\alpha}|
is independent of
\alpha
and
S
. In addition,
\sum_{\alpha}\delta_{S,\mu}^{\alpha}\delta_{S,\nu}^{\alpha}=\delta_{\mu\nu}a^{2}/2
. Introducing
\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon(a/\sqrt{3})
and
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a\left(\partial\epsilon/\partial|\delta_{S}^{\alpha}|\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\epsilon\left(\left\{|\boldsymbol{n}_{j,S}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{x})|\right\}\right)\approx\epsilon_{0}+\kappa\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{\parallel}\ .

(34)

Therefore, the contribution of the on-site term to the effective continuum Hamiltonian at
\boldsymbol{K}
is

\displaystyle H_{eff,onsite}^{\mathbf{K}}=\sum_{j,S}\int{\rm d}^{2}\boldsymbol{x}\ \left(\epsilon_{0}+\kappa\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}^{\parallel}_{j}\right)\Psi_{j,S}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{x})\Psi_{j,S}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(35)

thus correcting the value of the deformation potential obtained from
t
alone.

III
Discussion

In our derivation of the continuum effective Hamiltonians
H_{eff}^{\mathbf{K}}
for graphene bilayers, we have not made use of symmetries. Although this might seem reasonable given that we are considering arbitrary smooth inhomogeneous atomic configurations which would remove any remaining symmetries, as pointed out by Balents
Balents19
, the form of the leading order terms in the effective Hamiltonian can nevertheless be further constrained. That is because
H_{eff}^{\mathbf{K}}
must be invariant under symmetry operations of the undistorted lattice (i.e. AA-stacked bilayer) that leave a valley invariant if we
simultaneously
transform the fermion operators
and
the atomic displacement fields
Balents19
.

Although we postpone the detailed analysis of the symmetry, here and in the Appendix
B
we would like to highlight some of its consequences. The symmetries of interest to us will be
C_{3}
,
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
,
C_{2x}
, and
\mathcal{R}_{y}
. Here
C_{3}
is the three-fold rotation along
z
axis.
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
is the time reversal followed by the two-fold rotation along
z
axis with the two sublattices interchanged.
\mathcal{R}_{y}
is the mirror reflection along
xz
plane bisecting the nearest neighbor carbon bond so that
(x,y,z)\rightarrow(x,-y,z)
and the sublattice index
A\leftrightarrow B
. And
C_{2x}
is
\mathcal{R}_{y}
followed by the interchange of the two layers i.e. followed by the
xy

\mathcal{R}_{z}
.

The consequences of
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{R}_{y}
at
\mathbf{G}=0
for the contact interlayer tunneling term –independent of the spatial gradients of the atomic displacement i.e. to zeroth order in
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{U}
– were worked out in Ref.
Balents19
. There it was shown that, when combined with
C_{3}
, only two independent real parameters are allowed for the first shell of wavectors
\mathbf{G}=0,-4\pi\boldsymbol{a}_{2}\times\hat{z}/\sqrt{3}a^{2},4\pi(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})\times\hat{z}/\sqrt{3}a^{2}
. Physically, these correspond to the interlayer tunneling through the AA region and the AB region, and are the only interlayer tunneling terms kept in the Bistritzer-MacDonald model
BMModel
;
Balents19
.

As mentioned in the introduction, the anomalous decrease of the bandwidth near the magic twist angle promotes the importance of the next-to-leading order terms in setting the anisotropies, thus selecting from the nearly degenerate manifold of correlated states that are obtained if only the leading order terms are kept. Instead of listing all of the consequences of the above symmetries on such higher order terms, here we only mention in passing that
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
and the combined operation
C_{2x}\mathcal{R}_{y}
will be seen to allow for a particularly interesting inter-layer tunneling contact term which, as shown in the companion paper, is the main source of the particle-hole symmetry breaking in the model of Ref.
KaxirasPRB16
, but which is altogether absent in the Slater-Koster type models
MagaudNL10
;
KoshinoPRB12
;
KangVafekPRX
