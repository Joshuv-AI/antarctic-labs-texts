Classical and semi-classical limits in phase space
Clay D. Spence

Abstract
A semiclassical approximation is derived by using a family of wavepackets to map arbitrary wavefunctions into phase space. If the Hamiltonian can be approximated as linear over each individual wavepacket, as often done when presenting Ehrenfest’s theorem, the resulting approximation is a linear first-order partial differential equation on phase space, which will be referred to as the Schrödinger-Ehrenfest or SE equation. This advectively transports wavefunctions along classical trajectories, so that as a trajectory is followed in time the amplitude remains constant while the phase changes by the action divided by ℏ \hbar . The wavefunction’s squared-magnitude is a plausible phase space density and obeys Liouville’s equation for the classical time evolution. This is a derivation of the Koopman-von Neumann (KvN) formulation of classical mechanics, which previously was postulated but not derived. With the time-independent SE equation, continuity of the wavefunction requires the change of phase around any closed path in the torus covered by a classical trajectory to be an integer multiple of 2 ​ π 2\pi , giving a standing wave picture of old quantum mechanics. While this applies to any system, for separable systems it gives Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization.



I
Introduction

Semiclassical approximations are important not only for their
conceptual value in showing the relationship between classical and
quantum mechanics, but also for their practical applications

Heller 2018
. A standard semiclassical approach is
to set
\psi=A\exp(\frac{i}{\hbar}S)

Keller 1958
,
(
Messiah 1970
, Section VI.4)
or
\psi=\exp(\frac{i}{\hbar}S)

(
Heller 2018
, Section 7.5)
,
insert into Schrödinger’s equation, cancel the common exponential
factor and expand in powers of
\hbar
, considering only the
lowest-order terms. The results include classical flow in position
space, the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, and quantization.

In prior work
Torres-Vega and Frederick 1990
;
Harriman 1994
;
Ban 1998
that might appear to be unrelated, wavefunctions in
the usual Hilbert space are mapped into a larger Hilbert space
representable by wavefunctions on phase space. That prior work focuses
on developing quantum mechanics in terms of these phase-space
wavefunctions.

In this paper phase-space wavefunctions are used to provide another
semiclassical approximation. When Schrödinger’s equation is mapped
into phase space, this approximation changes it to a first-order
partial differential equation (
9
), which will be
referred to as the Schrödinger-Ehrenfest or SE equation. This
transports the wavefunction along classical trajectories, with the
amplitude remaining constant while the phase changes by the action
divided by
\hbar
. This is equivalent to the Koopman-von Neumann
(KvN) formulation of classical mechanics

Mauro 2002
;
Koopman 1931
;
von Neumann 1932
,
except the phase plays no role in the latter. The time-independent SE
equation gives quantization, since continuity of the wavefunction
restricts the phase to change by an integer multiple of
2\pi
around
any closed path, whether it is a classical trajectory or not. While
that applies to any system, for separable systems the Bohr-Sommerfeld
quantization formula is derived. This lacks the zero-point
term. It will be shown that for the harmonic oscillator this term is
given by the second-order corrections in the SE approximation, so that
is the case for at least some systems.

II
Approximating Schrödinger’s equation on phase space

In this section we give a fairly simple derivation of the first-order
Schrödinger-Ehrenfest equation. This implies Liouville’s
equation. It is not quite the same as the KvN formulation of classical
mechanics, but we show that it can be changed into that form. We then
discuss the fact that the set of wavefunctions on phase space forms a
superspace of the conventional Hilbert space. We skipped this when first
deriving the SE equation for the sake of simplicity. The mapping
between conventional wavefunctions and wavefunctions on phase space is
mostly in the literature, and we review those sources. We end this
section with a presentation of second-order terms in the SE
approximation to Schrödinger’s equation.

II.1
The first-order SE equation

Consider a single particle in
D
spatial dimensions with no internal
state like spin. In the notation used here when a variable with
multiple components is written without an index it indicates the
collection of all components. Schrödinger’s equation is

i\hbar\,\partial\psi(x,t)/\partial t=H(x,\hat{p},t)\psi(x,t).
Suppose that we measure position
x
and momentum
\hat{p}

simultaneously, with some measurement errors. If this measurement is
otherwise ideal it must project the state onto one with properties
consistent with the measurement results, so that the position and
momentum are concentrated around the observed values. Use
q
for the
position coordinate in phase space to distinguish it from the argument

x
of the usual wavefunction. Note that up until
Section
III
Cartesian coordinates are being used,
so through the sections before that
q
are also Cartesian. Suppose
that after observing the position
q
and momentum
p
the resulting
state is the wave packet

u_{qp}(x)=u_{0}(x-q)\exp\bigl(\tfrac{i}{\hbar}p\cdot(x-q)\bigr).

(1)

Here
u_{0}
is a wave packet with zero mean position and momentum and
variances
\sigma_{x}^{2}
and
\sigma_{p}^{2}
, and the notation
a\cdot b=\sum_{i}a_{i}b_{i}
is used. The
u_{qp}
have
q
and
p
for
means and the same variances as
u_{0}
.

The wavefunction can be expanded in a set of
u_{qp}
as

\eta(q,p,t)=\int\!d^{D}\!x\,u^{*}_{qp}(x)\psi(x,t).

(2)

We can show that the
u_{qp}
’s are self-inverting, i.e.,

\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,u_{qp}(x)u^{*}_{qp}(x^{\prime})=\delta^{D}(x-x^{\prime}),

(3)

by integrating
p
first to get the delta function. So this expansion
is invertible, and
\psi
can be recovered from
\eta
through

\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,u_{qp}(x)\eta(q,p,t)=\psi(x,t).

(4)

From Schrödinger’s equation and the Hermiticity of
H
, the time
derivative of
\eta
is

\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\!dx\,[H(x,\hat{p},t)u_{qp}(x)]^{*}\psi(x,t).

(5)

If
H
varies slowly over the widths
\sigma_{q}
and
\sigma_{p}
of the

u_{qp}
it can be expanded in powers of
x-q
and
\hat{p}-p
. To
first order this gives

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\eta(q,p)}{\partial t}&=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl\{H(q,p,t)\eta(q,p)\\
+&\sum_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}}\int\!d^{D}\!x\,[(x_{i}-q_{i})u_{qp}(x)]^{*}\psi(x,t)\\
+&\sum_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}\int\!d^{D}\!x\,[(\hat{p}_{i}-p_{i})u_{qp}(x)]^{*}\psi(x,t)\Bigr\},\end{split}

(6)

where the sum is over all coordinate-momentum pairs, as usual. From
(
1
) it can be shown that

\displaystyle(x_{i}-q_{i})u_{qp}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}u_{qp}(x)

(7)

and

\displaystyle(\hat{p}_{i}-p_{i})u_{qp}(x)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(-\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}}-p_{i}\Bigr)u_{qp}(x).

(8)

Inserting these into (
6
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl(p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}-H\Bigr)\eta+\sum_{i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}}\,\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}\,\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial q_{i}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl(p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}-H\Bigr)\eta-\{\eta,H\}

(9)

using the Poisson bracket
(
Goldstein 1980
, Sec. 9-4)

\{B,C\}=\sum_{i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial B}{\partial q_{i}}\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial{p_{i}}}-\frac{\partial B}{\partial{p_{i}}}\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial{q_{i}}}\Bigr).

(10)

The equation (
9
) and its derivation will be referred
to as the Schrödinger-Ehrenfest (
SE
) equation or
approximation, because approximating the Hamiltonian as linear across
a wavepacket is often done when presenting Ehrenfest’s theorem. When
we are discussing second-order terms from the expansion of
H
we will
qualify (
9
) as the first-order SE equation.

Since
\eta(q,p)
is the amplitude for observing the values
q
and

p
, the probability density on phase space is

\rho(q,p,t)=|\eta|^{2}.

(11)

From (
9
) the time evolution of
\rho
is Liouville’s
equation for the classical time evolution of phase space density

(
Goldstein 1980
, Sec. 9-8)
,

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-\{\rho,H\}.

(12)

Note the qualitative properties of the solutions of the SE equation
(
9
). If a classical solution
q(t),p(t)
is
inserted, the terms with partial derivatives of
\eta
form the total
time derivative of
\eta

(
Goldstein 1980
, Sec. 9-5)
. This then can be
written as

\frac{d\eta}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl(p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}-H\Bigr)\eta.

(13)

Since the coefficient of
\eta
is pure imaginary, if we followed

\eta
along this classical trajectory only the phase changes while
the amplitude is constant. The change in phase from time
t_{0}
to

t_{1}
is

\Delta\arg(\eta)=\hbar^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\!dt\,\Bigl(p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}-H\Bigr).

(14)

The expression
p\cdot\partial H/\partial p-H
is
numerically equal to the Lagrangian, so
\Delta\arg(\eta)
is

\hbar^{-1}
times the action, specifically Hamilton’s principal
function
S

(
Goldstein 1980
, Sec. 10-1)
, though as a function of
different arguments. Here it is a function of
q,p
and
t
, and
has a value equal to Hamilton’s principal function for the classical
trajectory that arrives at
q,p
at time
t
from some starting
time.

Given that the phase changes along a trajectory by the action divided
by
\hbar
, we might expect some form of action quantization from the
time-independent SE equation. We will show this in
Section
III
.

II.2
A generalization and a derivation of the KvN formulation of
classical mechanics

The wavepackets used in the preceding expansion can be generalized to
give a derivation of the KvN formulation of classical mechanics.
Alter the wavepackets
u_{qp}
with an additional phase
\phi(q,p,t)
,
independent of
x
. The wavepackets become

u_{qp}(x,t)=u_{0}(x-q)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}p\cdot(x-q)+i\phi(q,p,t)}.

(15)

The time derivative of
\eta
changes to

\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}=-i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\!dx\,[H(x,\hat{p},t)u_{qp}(x)]^{*}\psi(x,t).

(16)

The expressions (
7
) and (
8
) change to

\displaystyle

\displaystyle(x_{i}-q_{i})u_{qp}(x)=\frac{\hbar}{i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}-i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial p_{i}}\Bigr)u_{qp}(x)\qquad\text{and}

(17)

\displaystyle(\hat{p}_{i}-p_{i})u_{qp}(x)=\Bigl[-\frac{\hbar}{i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}}-i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial q_{i}}\Bigr)-p_{i}\Bigr]u_{qp}(x).

The SE equation (
9
) becomes

\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl[p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}-H-\hbar\Bigl(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\{\phi,H\}\Bigr)\Bigr]\eta-\{\eta,H\}.

(18)

For example, we could choose
\phi=-H(q,p)t/\hbar
, and the term in
square brackets multiplying
\eta
would reduce to
(i/\hbar)p\cdot\partial H/\partial p
.

To get the KvN formulation of classical mechanics, choose
\phi=\hbar^{-1}S(q,p,t)
, with
S
equal in value to Hamilton’s principal
function as described above. Since the terms
\partial\phi/\partial t+\{\phi,H\}
equal the total time derivative of
\phi
if
classical solutions are inserted for
q
and
p
, for
\phi=\hbar^{-1}S(q,p,t)
these equal the Lagrangian divided by
\hbar
and
cancel the other terms in square brackets in
Eq. (
18
), leaving

\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}=-\{\eta,H\}.

(19)

This and Liouville’s equation (
12
) that follows from
it are the KvN formulation of classical mechanics

Mauro 2002
;
Koopman 1931
;
von Neumann 1932
.
Koopman and von Neumann postulated this rather than deriving it.

II.3
Wavefunctions on phase space as a larger Hilbert space

One might suspect that the set of wavefunctions
\eta(q,p)
on phase
space represents a larger Hilbert space than the usual wavefunctions

\psi(x)
. In the preceding this was ignored in order to give a simple
derivation of the basic equation (
9
). In this
section the relation between the usual and larger Hilbert spaces is
described in some detail. This is mostly a review, as will be
discussed in Section
II.4
. The images
\eta(q,p)
of
wavefunctions
\psi(x)
under (
2
) will be shown to form a
subspace of the set
\mathcal{C}
of
L^{2}
functions on phase space. There is
a natural projection operator from
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathcal{Q}
, so that an
\eta\notin\mathcal{Q}
can be close to
\mathcal{Q}
, and therefore a good approximation
to some
\psi\in\mathcal{Q}
. This superspace-subspace relationship will be
constructed. While these ideas weren’t strictly needed for the
preceding presentation, they will be necessary for semiclassical
quantization.

Consider a Hilbert space
\mathcal{C}
representable with complex-valued
L^{2}

functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2D}
. Use
q,p
for the
2D
coordinates,
and
|q,p\rangle
for joint eigenvectors of the operators for those
coordinates, scaled so that

\langle q^{\prime},p^{\prime}|q,p\rangle=(2\pi\hbar)^{D}\delta^{D}(q-q^{\prime})\delta^{D}(p-p^{\prime}).

(20)

A resolution of the identity in
\mathcal{C}
is

\hat{I}_{\mathcal{C}}=\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}|q,p\rangle\langle q,p|.

(21)

With the functions
u_{qp}(x)
from (
1
), construct the
set of vectors

|x\rangle=\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}u^{*}_{qp}(x)|q,p\rangle.

(22)

Multiplying this on the left with
\langle q^{\prime},p^{\prime}|
, using
(
20
), and taking the conjugate gives

u_{qp}(x)=\langle x|q,p\rangle.

(23)

Multiplying (
22
) by its conjugate and using the
self-inverting property (
3
) of the
u_{qp}
gives

\langle x|x^{\prime}\rangle=\delta^{D}(x-x^{\prime}).

(24)

Therefore these
|x\rangle
can be used as the position basis for
the usual Hilbert space
\mathcal{Q}
of quantum mechanics, here as a subspace
of
\mathcal{C}
. (See
Ban 1998
for a procedure that starts with

\mathcal{Q}
and constructs
\mathcal{C}
as a superspace. This might seem more
natural but is somewhat more involved.)

With these relations the expression (
2
) for mapping

\psi(x)
into
\eta(q,p)
becomes

\eta(q,p)=\int\!d^{D}\!x\,\langle q,p|x\rangle\langle x|\psi\rangle.

(25)

In these terms this is obvious since
\int\!d^{D}x\,|x\rangle\langle x|
is a resolution of the identity in
\mathcal{Q}
and
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{Q}
. It then simply says
\eta(q,p)=\langle q,p|\psi\rangle
, i.e.,

\eta(q,p)
is
|\psi\rangle
expressed in the
|q,p\rangle

basis. Note that while

\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int\!d^{D}\!x\,|x\rangle\langle x|

(26)

is a resolution of the identity in
\mathcal{Q}
, within
\mathcal{C}
it is an
expression for a projection operator from
\mathcal{C}
to

\mathcal{Q}
.

Equations (
3
) and (
4
) for the
mapping of
\eta(q,p)
back to
\psi(x)
become

\displaystyle\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,\langle x|q,p\rangle\langle q,p|x^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta^{D}(x-x^{\prime})

(27)

and

\displaystyle\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,\langle x|q,p\rangle\langle q,p|\psi,t\rangle

\displaystyle=\psi(x,t).

(28)

These follow trivially from the identity operator
\hat{I}_{\mathcal{C}}
expressed
in (
21
).

Equation (
4
) expressed the fact that a wavefunction

\psi(x)
can be recovered from its mapping into
\eta(q,p)
, but we
can replace
|\psi,t\rangle\in\mathcal{Q}
in (
28
) with any
vector
|\eta\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
to get

\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,\langle x|q,p\rangle\langle q,p|\eta\rangle=\psi(x).

(29)

This expresses the projection of an arbitrary vector
|\eta\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
onto
\mathcal{Q}
in the
|x\rangle
basis.

If
\mathcal{Q}
is a subspace of
\mathcal{C}
, the projection operator
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}

should have a null space
\mathcal{N}
. The projection operator onto this null
space should be the identity minus
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}
, i.e.,
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{N}}=\hat{I}_{\mathcal{C}}-\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}
, which we can write as

\hat{P}_{\mathcal{N}}=\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}|q,p\rangle\langle q,p|-\int\!d^{D}\!x\,|x\rangle\langle x|.

(30)

It is easy to see that
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{N}}|x\rangle=0
using
(
22
), the construction of
|x\rangle
. Similarly we
can show that

\langle q,p|\hat{P}_{\mathcal{N}}|q_{0},p_{0}\rangle=(2\pi\hbar)^{D}\,\delta^{D}(q-q_{0})\delta^{D}(p-p_{0})\\
-\int\!d^{D}\!x\,u^{*}_{qp}(x)u_{q_{0}p_{0}}(x).

(31)

We can work this out for a particular choice of the
u_{qp}(x)
, such
as Gaussians, and verify that it is non-zero. Thus none of the
|x\rangle
have components in the null space, while all
|q_{0},p_{0}\rangle
have non-zero projection onto the null space, so none lie in

\mathcal{Q}
.

It is perhaps obvious that
q
and
p
are not exact position and
momentum eigenvalues, but particular kinds of approximations. There
are, however, relationships between the position and momentum
operators on
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{Q}
. On
\mathcal{C}
the natural choices for
classical mechanics are

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\mathcal{C},i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,|qp\rangle q_{i}\langle qp|\qquad\text{and}

(32)

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\mathcal{C},i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,|qp\rangle p_{i}\langle qp|.

(33)

Clearly these commute, so their action on elements of
\mathcal{Q}
can’t be
the same as the usual quantum position and momentum
operators. However, we can show that the projections onto
\mathcal{Q}
,
namely
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{q}_{\mathcal{C},i}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{p}_{\mathcal{C},i}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}
, are the usual quantum operators

\hat{x}_{\mathcal{Q},i}
and
\hat{p}_{\mathcal{Q},i}
. For example, from the
definition of
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}
above

\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{p}_{\mathcal{C},i}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}=\\
\int\!d^{D}\!x\,d^{D}\!x^{\prime}\,|x\rangle\Bigl[\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\langle x|qp\rangle p_{i}\langle qp|x^{\prime}\rangle\Bigr]\langle x^{\prime}|.

(34)

The factor in square brackets is

\displaystyle\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,u_{0}(x-q)u^{*}_{0}(x^{\prime}-q)\,p_{i}\,e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{i}(x_{i}-x^{\prime}_{i})}

\displaystyle=\int\!d^{D}\!q\,u_{0}(x-q)u^{*}_{0}(x^{\prime}-q)\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\delta^{D}(x-x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\delta^{D}(x-x^{\prime}).

(35)

Re-inserting into (
34
) gives

\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{p}_{\mathcal{C},i}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}=\int\!d^{D}\!x\,|x\rangle\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\langle x|=\hat{p}_{Q,i}.

(36)

More general classical observables are functions of
q,p
, so the
corresponding operators in
\mathcal{C}
are

\hat{O}_{\mathcal{C}}=\int\Bigl(\frac{dq\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}\,|qp\rangle O(q,p)\langle qp|.

(37)

In general the projection of this onto
\mathcal{Q}
is not the same as
replacing
q,p
in
O(q,p)
with
\hat{x},\hat{p}
to get
O(\hat{x},\hat{p})
.

II.4
Relation to previous work

Much of Section
II.3
and parts of
Section
II.1
are in the previous literature on phase space
wavefunctions. In this section the similarities and differences are
presented in more detail.

In the earliest of these papers
Torres-Vega and Frederick 1990
, Torres-Vega
and Frederick use wavepackets of the same form as (
1
),
relate them to the inner product of phase space basis vectors and
coordinate basis vectors as in (
23
), map a
wavefunction in the coordinate representation into the phase space
representation with (
2
), and use the expressions
(
7
) for
u_{qp}
multiplied by
x_{i}-q_{i}
and

\hat{p}_{i}-q_{i}
, though all in their notation. They then proceed to
express Schrödinger’s equation in phase space for Hamiltonians of
the form
\|p\|^{2}/2m+V(q)
, solve for energy eigenstates of the
harmonic oscillator, and examine the time evolution of coherent
states. They note the resemblance of the resulting equation for the
time evolution of the phase space density of coherent states to
Liouville’s equation.

Harriman
Harriman 1994
has a similar presentation with a
more general approach to the matrix elements or kernels
(
23
). Early in the paper he narrows his discussion to
Gaussian kernels or coherent states of the harmonic oscillator, for
the sake of treating position and momentum similarly. This choice
results in an eigenvalue equation satisfied by any element of
\mathcal{Q}
,
which discriminates between elements of
\mathcal{Q}
and other elements of

\mathcal{C}
. He then proceeds to discuss the phase space forms of various
position-space wavefunctions and momentum-space wavefunctions.

The SE equation (
9
) was not derived in that previous
work. This approach of deriving the classical limit directly, rather
than first formulating quantum mechanics on phase space, allows
considerable simplification, more general Hamiltonians, and more
general wavepacket families. To the author’s knowledge this and the
results in the remainder of the paper are new.

II.5
Second-order terms

In this section the summation convention is used. The second-order
terms dropped from (
6
) are

\begin{split}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\biggl(&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\int\!dx\bigl[(\hat{p}_{i}-p_{i})(\hat{p}_{j}-p_{j})u_{q,p}(x)\bigr]^{*}\psi\\
+&\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial q_{i}\partial p_{j}}\int\!dx\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2}[x_{i}-q_{i},\hat{p_{j}}-p_{j}]_{+}u_{q,p}(x)\Bigr\}^{*}\psi\\
+&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial q_{i}\partial q_{j}}\int\!dx\bigl[(x_{i}-q_{i})(x_{j}-q_{j})u_{q,p}(x)\bigr]^{*}\psi\biggr).\end{split}

(38)

(Here the anticommutator
\frac{1}{2}[x_{i}-q_{i},\hat{p}_{j}-p_{j}]_{+}
is used to make
the operator Hermitian.) Like (
7
) and (
8
), it
can be shown that

(\hat{p_{i}}-p_{i})(\hat{p_{j}}-p_{j})u_{q,p}(x)=\\
\Bigl(-\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}}-p_{i}\Bigr)\Bigl(-\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}-p_{j}\Bigr)u_{q,p}(x),

(39)

\frac{1}{2}[x_{i}-q_{i},\hat{p}_{j}-p_{j}]_{+}u_{q,p}(x)=\\
\Bigl[\bigl(-\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}-p_{j}\bigr)\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\hbar}{2i}\delta_{i,j}\Bigr]u_{q,p}(x),

(40)

and

(x_{i}-q_{i})(x_{j}-q_{j})u_{q,p}(x)=-\hbar^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}u_{q,p}(x).

(41)

Inserting these into (
38
) and then adding to
(
9
) gives

\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl(p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}-H\Bigr)\eta-\{\eta,H\}\\
+\Bigl[\frac{-i}{2\hbar}p_{i}p_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial q_{i}\partial p_{i}}\Bigr]\eta\\
+p_{i}\Bigl[\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial q_{j}}-\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial q_{j}}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial p_{j}}\Bigr]\\
+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\Bigl[\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial q_{i}\partial q_{j}}-2\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial q_{j}}\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial q_{i}\partial p_{j}}+\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial q_{i}\partial q_{j}}\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\Bigr].

(42)

The new terms couple the phase and amplitude, and thus the time
derivative of the density
\rho=\lvert\eta\rvert^{2}
no longer
involves only terms with
\rho
and its derivatives.

III
Semi-classical quantization

Here we first derive the time-independent SE equation. We then use
this to derive the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization formula
J_{i}=hn_{i}

(
Messiah 1970
, Section I.15)
for separable systems. This is
followed by a discussion of the exact harmonic oscillator energy
eigenfunctions mapped into phase space. These obey the first-order SE
equation with the Bohr-Sommerfeld result
E=\hbar\omega n
. We then
show that the ground-state term
\hbar\omega/2
comes from the
second-order terms in the SE approximation.

III.1
The approximate time-independent Schrödinger-Ehrenfest equation

We can derive equations for semi-classical quantization using the
time-independent form of the SE equation
Eq. (
9
). Assume that the Hamiltonian doesn’t depend
explicitly on time, and consider an energy eigenstate

\psi(x)\exp(-iEt/\hbar)
in
\mathcal{Q}
. The partial time derivative of

\eta
is
-\frac{i}{\hbar}E\eta
. Inserting this into the SE equation
(
9
) gives

E\eta=\Bigl(H-p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Bigr)\eta+\frac{\hbar}{i}\{\eta,H\}.

(43)

Represent
\eta
with an amplitude and phase,

\eta(q,p,t)=A(q,p)\exp\Bigl\{\frac{i}{\hbar}\bigl[\beta(q,p)-Et\bigr]\Bigr\},

(44)

where
A
and
\beta
are real-valued. The real and
imaginary parts of (
43
) become, after dividing
out some factors,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\{A,H\}\qquad\qquad\text{and}

(45)

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=H-p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}+\{\beta,H\}.

(46)

The amplitude and phase are independent, as pointed out
in
Mauro 2002
for the KvN formulation.

If a phase-space function
B
has no explicit time dependence and
classical solutions
q(t),p(t)
are inserted,
\{B,H\}
equals

dB/dt
, so (
45
) and (
46
) relate the
values of
A
or
\beta
along any classical trajectory, including any
manifold covered by a trajectory. In particular (
45
)
requires
A
to be constant on such a manifold. Since they follow
classical trajectories the expressions (
45
) and
(
46
) can’t relate
A
or
\beta
across different values
of the constants of the motion, in particular
H
.

One might try to simplify (
46
) by using the KvN equation
(
19
), derived by adding the phase
S(q,p,t)/\hbar
to
the
u_{qp}
. However, introducing an extra phase
\phi
changes
the partial time derivative of
\eta
to
(-i/\hbar)(E+\hbar\partial\phi/\partial t)\eta
. Inserting this into
(
18
), the terms involving
\partial\phi/\partial t
cancel, leaving
\{\phi,H\}\eta
as the only term involving

\phi
. Therefore using
\phi=S(q,p,t)/\hbar
does not give the
total time derivative of the action, and does not cancel the terms
H-p\cdot\partial H/\partial p
.

There is nothing in the preceding that requires
H=E
. In
Section
II.3
it was shown that wavefunctions on phase
space represent a Hilbert space
\mathcal{C}
, and that the usual Hilbert
space
\mathcal{Q}
is a subspace of
\mathcal{C}
. In Appendix
A
it
is shown that solutions with
H\neq E
approach the null space of the
projection from
\mathcal{C}
onto
\mathcal{Q}
as
\lvert E-H\rvert
grows, and
therefore they cannot be good approximations to an eigenfunction in

\mathcal{Q}
. The scale for which
\lvert E-H\rvert
is small enough to
contribute is related to the system, the spatial width
\sigma
of the
wavepackets
u_{qp}
, and
\hbar
. Points with
H=E
make the
dominant contribution. Assuming the wavefunction has support only for

H(q,p)=E
, there are three equations for an approximate energy
eigenstate and eigenvalue
E
: The equation (
45
) for
the amplitude,
H=E
, and

0=-p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}+\{\beta,H\}.

(47)

III.2
Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization

Quantization follows from the time-independent SE equation
(
47
) and the continuity of
\eta
, which in turn requires
that the phase
\beta/\hbar
changes by an integer multiple of
2\pi

as a closed path is followed on a torus given by particular values of
the constants of motion. For a separable system we will compute the
change of phase
\beta/\hbar
around suitable closed paths. These must
be integer multiples of
2\pi
as described above, and the result is
Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization.

First, the preceding was all in Cartesian coordinates, but in general
a system is separable in some other coordinates. So we need to see how
the equation (
47
) governing
\beta
changes. It is shown
in Appendix
B
that
p\cdot\partial H/\partial p
is
invariant under coordinate transformations, so Eq. (
47
)
is also invariant, since the Poisson bracket is a canonical invariant.

In separable coordinates
q,p
, compute the indefinite integral of
(
47
) to get an expression for
\beta
. Consider a
classical trajectory parameterized by a time-like variable
t
. Using

\partial H/\partial p_{i}=\dot{q}_{i}
and
\{\beta,H\}=d\beta(q,p)/dt
, integrate (
47
) over
t
to get

\int\!dt\,p\cdot\dot{q}=\beta.

(48)

The left-hand side is Hamilton’s characteristic function,
W

(
Goldstein 1980
, Sec. 10-3)
, so
\beta
is numerically equal to

W
. They are, however, functions of different variables. The
arguments of
W
are the coordinates
q
and the
D
constants of the
motion
\alpha
, but not the momenta, while the arguments of
\beta

are the coordinates and momenta but not
\alpha
.
W
and
\beta
can
be equal since
\alpha
and
q,p
are not independent. We can express
the constants of motion as functions
c(q,p)
, with values
\alpha=c(q,p)
when
q,p
satisfies those constants of motion. With
c

inserted for the
\alpha
arguments we can write

\beta(q,p)=W\bigl(q,c(q,p)\bigr).

(49)

There is another important difference:
W
is multiple-valued, since

\partial W(q,\alpha)/\partial q_{i}=p_{i}
, and a particular value of

q
does not completely fix the momenta
p
. For example, with a
harmonic oscillator in one dimension a given value of
q
fixes the
magnitude of
p
but not its sign. In general there must be different

W
’s for different parts of the trajectory. In
\beta
these are
disambiguated since
\beta
depends on both
q
and
p
. Thus at any
particular point in phase space (
49
) holds with one of the
values of
W
, while
\beta
is a single-valued function on all of
phase space.

We can use (
49
) to evaluate the change in
\beta
as the
system follows a closed path that is the intersection of the torus for
given values of the constants of the motion and the plane of a
particular
q_{i},p_{i}
pair with the other variables held fixed. We’ll
show that this intersection is followed by a path satisfying

dq_{i}/dt_{i}=\partial H/\partial p_{i}
and
dp_{i}/dt_{i}=-\partial H/\partial q_{i}
with parameter
t_{i}
, and all
q_{j},p_{j}
for
j\neq i

held fixed, i.e., it preserves the constants of motion, even though it
is not a solution of the full set of equations of motion. Then
(
49
) will determine how
\beta
changes along this path.

For brevity use

\{B,C\}_{i}=\frac{\partial B}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial C}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial C}{\partial q_{i}},

(50)

for the terms in the Poisson bracket, so
\{B,C\}=\sum_{i}\{B,C\}_{i}
.
In Appendix
C
it is shown that, for all
i
and
j
,

\{c_{i},H\}_{j}=0.

(51)

On the path mentioned above, with a specific
q_{i},p_{i}
pair completing
an orbit while
q_{j},p_{j}
for all
j\neq i
are fixed, because of
(
51
) all of the constants of the motion are satisfied, so
the path lies in the solution torus. The derivative of
\beta
with
respect to
t_{i}
is

\displaystyle\frac{d\beta}{dt_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{dq_{i}}{dt_{i}}+\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{dp_{i}}{dt_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial p_{i}}\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}}.

(52)

Use the relation (
49
) between
\beta
and
W
to get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial q_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial W}{\partial q_{i}}+\sum_{j}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\frac{\partial c_{j}}{\partial q_{i}}

\displaystyle=p_{i}+\sum_{j}\frac{\partial W}{\partial{\alpha_{j}}}\frac{\partial c_{j}}{\partial q_{i}}\qquad\text{and}

(53)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial p_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\frac{\partial c_{j}}{\partial p_{i}}.

(54)

Inserting these into (
52
) and using (
51
)
gives

\displaystyle\frac{d\beta}{dt_{i}}

\displaystyle=p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}+\sum_{j}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\{c_{j},H\}_{i}

\displaystyle=p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}.

(55)

Define
\Delta_{i}\beta
as the change in
\beta
after completing one
orbit on this path. Integrating (
55
) for one
period of
t_{i}
gives

\Delta_{i}\beta=\oint p_{i}\,dq_{i}=J_{i}.

(56)

Since the phase
\beta/\hbar
can only change by an integer multiple
of
2\pi
, this requires

J_{i}=n_{i}h,

(57)

i.e., Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization.

III.3
The harmonic oscillator

As a simple example, consider the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator.
We’ll show that the exact energy eigenstates mapped into
\mathcal{C}
using a
particular set of
u_{qp}
obey the first-order SE equation if the
energy eigenvalue is the Bohr-Sommerfeld value
E_{n}=\hbar\omega n
. The ground-state energy term
\hbar\omega/2
is given by the
second-order terms in the SE approximation.

These eigenstates are

\psi_{n}(x)=\Bigl(\frac{m\omega}{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{1/4}H_{n}\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}x\Bigr)e^{-m\omega x^{2}/2\hbar}

(58)

where
H_{n}
is the
n
-th Hermite polynomial with normalization such
that the highest-order term has coefficient
2^{n}
. We’ll map this
into
\eta(q,p)
using the wavepackets

u_{qp}(x)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-1/4}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(x-q)^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p(x-q)\Bigr]

(59)

The mapping into
\mathcal{C}
is

\eta_{n}(q,p)=\Bigl(\frac{m\omega}{2^{2n+1}(n!)^{2}\pi^{2}\hbar\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)^{1/4}\int\!dx\,H_{n}\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}x\Bigr)\\
\times\exp\Bigl(-\frac{m\omega x^{2}}{2\hbar}-\frac{(x-q)^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}-\frac{i}{\hbar}p(x-q)\Bigr).

(60)

Defining

\zeta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}}

(61)

shortens this a little, to

\eta_{n}(q,p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{(2n+1)/2}n!\pi\zeta\sigma}}\int\!dx\,H_{n}(x/\zeta)\\
\times\exp\Bigl(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\zeta^{2}}-\frac{(x-q)^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}-\frac{i}{\hbar}p(x-q)\Bigr).

(62)

Define further

\alpha\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{4\sigma^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+\zeta^{2}}}.

(63)

Letting
y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}x/\alpha\zeta
the wavefunction (
62
) becomes

\eta_{n}(q,p)=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha^{2}\zeta}{2^{(2n+1)/2}n!\pi\sigma}}\\
\times\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}q^{2}+\frac{i\alpha^{2}pq}{2\hbar}-\frac{\alpha^{2}\zeta^{2}p^{2}}{4\hbar^{2}}\Bigr)\\
\times\int\!dy\,H_{n}(\alpha y)\exp\Bigl(-\Bigl[y-\frac{\alpha\zeta}{2}\Bigl(\frac{q}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{i}{\hbar}p\Bigr)\Bigr]^{2}\Bigr).

(64)

We can use the result
(
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 1965
, entry 7.374.8)

\int\!dx\,e^{-(x-z)^{2}}H_{n}(\alpha x)=\\
\pi^{1/2}(1-\alpha^{2})^{n/2}H_{n}\Bigl(\frac{\alpha z}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}\Bigr)

(65)

to get

\eta_{n}(q,p)=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha^{2}\zeta}{2^{(2n+1)/2}n!\sigma}}(1-\alpha^{2})^{n/2}\\
\times H_{n}\Bigl[\frac{\alpha^{2}\zeta}{2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}\Bigl(\frac{q}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{i}{\hbar}p\Bigr)\Bigr]\\
\times\exp\Bigl[-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\Bigl(\frac{q^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-2\frac{i}{\hbar}pq+\frac{\zeta^{2}p^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr].

(66)

For
\alpha>1
we can replace
\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}
with

i\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}
.

The rest is greatly simplified if we let
\alpha
approach
1
, or
equivalently to let
2\sigma^{2}
approach
\zeta^{2}
. The resulting
wavepackets
u_{qp}
are the coherent states for this Hamiltonian.
The factor
(1-\alpha^{2})^{-1/2}
in the argument to
H_{n}
goes to
infinity, so
H_{n}
will go to infinity as
(1-\alpha^{2})^{-n/2}
, but

H_{n}
is multiplied by
(1-\alpha^{2})^{n/2}
. The leading term then
is constant in
1-\alpha^{2}
while all sub-leading terms go to zero,
so

(1-\alpha^{2})^{n/2}H_{n}\Bigl[\frac{\alpha^{2}\zeta}{2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}\Bigl(\frac{q}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{ip}{\hbar}\Bigr)\Bigr]\\
\rightarrow(1-\alpha^{2})^{n/2}2^{n}\Bigl[\frac{\alpha^{2}\zeta}{2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}\Bigl(\frac{q}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{ip}{\hbar}\Bigr)\Bigr]^{n}

(67)

=\Bigl(\frac{q}{\zeta}-\frac{ip\zeta}{\hbar}\Bigr)^{n}

(68)

With this (
66
) becomes

\eta_{n}(q,p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}\Bigl(\frac{q}{\zeta}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\zeta p\Bigr)^{n}\\
\times\exp\Bigl[-\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(\frac{q^{2}}{\zeta^{2}}-2\frac{i}{\hbar}pq+\frac{\zeta^{2}p^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr]

(69)

Inserting the definition of
\zeta
gives

\eta_{n}(q,p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\Bigl(\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\Bigr)^{n/2}\Bigl(q-\frac{ip}{m\omega}\Bigr)^{n}\\
\times\exp\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2\hbar\omega}\Bigl(\frac{m\omega^{2}q^{2}}{2}-i\omega pq+\frac{p^{2}}{2m}\Bigr)\Bigr].

(70)

Let us insert this into the first-order SE equation,
(
43
). The Poisson bracket is

\{\eta,H\}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl(\hbar\omega n+\frac{p^{2}}{2m}-\frac{m\omega^{2}q^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\eta.

(71)

The first-order time-independent SE equation is

\displaystyle E\eta

\displaystyle=\Bigl(H-p\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Bigr)\eta-i\hbar\{\eta,H\}

(72)

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega n\eta.

(73)

This lacks the ground-state energy term,
\hbar\omega/2
, which must
come from higher-order terms in this approximation. For the harmonic
oscillator the only higher-order terms are second-order.

For a one-dimensional system with
\partial^{2}H/\partial q\partial p=0
the second order form for the
time-independent SE equation is, from Eq. (
42
),

E\eta=\Bigl(H-p\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Bigr)\eta-i\hbar\{\eta,H\}\\
+\frac{1}{2}p^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p^{2}}+i\hbar p\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p^{2}}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial q}\\
-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2}\Bigl[\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial q^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial q^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial p^{2}}\Bigr].

(74)

For the harmonic oscillator these terms are

E\eta=-\Bigl(\frac{p^{2}}{2m}-\frac{m\omega^{2}q^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\eta+\frac{\hbar}{i}\Bigl(\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial q}-m\omega^{2}q\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial p}\Bigr)\\
+\frac{p^{2}}{2m}\eta+i\hbar\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial q}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial q^{2}}-\frac{m\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial p^{2}}.

(75)

With the exact eigenfunction (
70
) the second-order terms
(the second line of (
75
)) equal

(\hbar\omega/2)\eta.

So at least in this case the terms to first order give the
Bohr-Sommerfeld result, without a ground-state energy term, while the
latter comes from the second-order terms.

IV
Discussion

The first-order Schrödinger-Ehrenfest (SE) equation
(
9
) gives classical mechanics in a fairly intuitive
form: Wavefunctions on phase space evolve in time by being transported
along classical trajectories, with the amplitude remaining constant
while the phase changes by the action divided by
\hbar
. This applies
to any wavefunction, not just wavepackets. The squared-magnitude of
the corresponding wavefunction in phase space is a plausible
probability density, and obeys Liouville’s equation, in this
approximation.

This is a derivation of the KvN formulation of classical mechanics
(Section
II.2
), which previously was postulated but not
derived. There is a difference, since the phase plays essentially no
role in the KvN formulation, and is unchanging along a
trajectory. Here its form is necessary to project correctly back to
the usual Hilbert space
\mathcal{Q}
, and its change along a trajectory is
important for quantization.

The version of semiclassical quantization derived here has an
intuitive interpretation as standing waves in phase space on the torus
covered by a classical trajectory. For separable systems the
Bohr-Sommerfeld formula follows, including its lack of a zero-point
term. For the case of the harmonic oscillator and its exact energy
eigenstates it was shown that the ground-state energy term comes from
the second-order terms of the SE approximation. We have not yet given
a procedure for computing zero-point terms from the second-order
approximation.

We can compare the time-independent SE approximation’s picture of
quantization with Einstein-Brillouin-Keller (EBK) quantization

Keller 1958
;
Stone 2005
. There, the quantization formula arises from
the wavefunction ansatz
\psi=\sum_{k}A_{k}\exp(\frac{i}{\hbar}S_{k})
. The use of multiple terms differs
from the form
\psi=A\exp(\frac{i}{\hbar}S)
given in the
introduction. In
(
Messiah 1970
, Section VI.4)
an odd
feature of the single-term version is noted, namely that
\nabla S

gives only one value for the momentum at each position in
configuration space. The multiple terms in the EBK ansatz are used
precisely to represent all of the possible values of momentum at each
position. These terms are conceived of as wavefunctions on different
parts of a torus. It is the matching of the phases of these terms at
the boundaries of the classically-accessible region, or edges of the
torus, that gives zero-point terms. This matching of terms can also
be attributed to caustics, which are an artifact of representing the
state in configuration space

Littlejohn 1986
. Representing the wavefunction in
momentum space, for example, gives a different approximation to the
wavefunction, since matching must now be done at the momentum turning
points instead of the position turning points. The matching of terms
can only be done for separable systems, otherwise there is a
continuous set of momenta for a given position, and there is no method
to relate the terms in this continuous set.

In the SE approximation the torus appears naturally as the phase-space
torus covered by a classical trajectory. Because the treatment is in
phase space the wavefunction does not have multiple terms, so no
matching is required. In particular it still applies to non-separable
systems.

Applying the SE approximation to non-separable systems remains to be
done. As usual solving those equations is difficult. At best it seems
likely that some of the eigenvalues can be obtained by integrating the
phase part (
47
) of the SE equation on any periodic orbits
that might be known.

Appendix A
H\approx E
for eigenstates close to

\mathcal{Q}

Here it is shown that the solutions of (
43
)
approach the null space of the projection onto
\mathcal{Q}
as
H
is farther
from
E
. The projection will be expressed in the
|q,p\rangle

basis. Since
\hbar
is not zero, solutions with
H\neq E
can
contribute to an energy eigenfunction in
\mathcal{Q}
, but only if
H
is
sufficiently close to
E
.

Starting with an eigenfunction
\eta
, compute the projection onto

\mathcal{Q}
by applying (
4
), the mapping from
\eta
to

\psi
, and then (
2
), the mapping back to the
\lvert q,p\rangle
basis. Let the dimensionality of the phase space be

2D
. The projection is

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}(q,p)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\!\int\!\Bigl(dx\frac{dq^{\prime}\,dp^{\prime}}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}u^{*}_{qp}(x)u_{q^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(x)\eta(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\Bigl(\frac{dq^{\prime}\,dp^{\prime}}{2\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{D}K(q,p,q^{\prime},p^{\prime})\eta(q^{\prime},p^{\prime}),

(76)

where the kernel
K
is

K(q,p,q^{\prime},p^{\prime})=\int\!d^{D}x\,u^{*}_{qp}(x)u_{q^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(x)\\
=\int\!d^{D}x\,u^{*}_{0}(x-q)u_{0}(x-q^{\prime})\\
\times\exp\bigl(i\hbar^{-1}[-p\cdot(x-q)+p^{\prime}\cdot(x-q^{\prime})]\bigr).

(77)

Remember that
q
is a vector of Cartesian coordinates. The exact
form of
K
depends on the form of
u_{0}
. For convenience use the
Gaussian

u_{0}(x)\approx\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{1/4}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}/4\sigma^{2}}.

(78)

If
u_{0}
is not a Gaussian, this can be viewed as an approximation,
where we write
u_{0}(x)
as
\exp(\log u_{0}(x))
and replace
\log u_{0}(x)
with its second-order Taylor expansion around the
x
that
maximizes it. This assumes that
u_{0}
is sufficiently
well-behaved. With this form the kernel works out to

K(q,p,q^{\prime},p^{\prime})=\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{\|q^{\prime}-q\|^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\|p^{\prime}-p\|^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}\sigma^{-2}}\\
-\frac{i(p^{\prime}+p)\cdot(q^{\prime}-q)}{2\hbar}\Bigr\}.

(79)

Evaluate the integral (
76
) for
q,p
on the
manifold satisfying the constants of motion, and for a segment of
trajectory passing through
q,p
. Parameterize this segment with a
time-like variable
\tau
, so that, for small
\tau
,

\displaystyle q^{\prime}

\displaystyle\approx q+\frac{\partial q}{\partial\tau}\tau

\displaystyle=q+\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\tau

(80a)

and

\displaystyle p^{\prime}

\displaystyle\approx p+\frac{\partial p}{\partial\tau}\tau

\displaystyle=p-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\tau.

(80b)

Inserting into
K
gives

K(q,p,q^{\prime},p^{\prime})=\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{T^{2}}-\frac{i}{\hbar}p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\tau\Bigr\}

(81)

where

T=\biggl[\frac{1}{8\sigma^{2}}\Bigl\|\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Bigr\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}\Bigl\|\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\Bigr\|^{2}\biggr]^{-1/2}.

(82)

Notice that
T
depends on
q,p
. It is non-zero except at
possible stationary points, i.e., where both
\partial H/\partial q

and
\partial H/\partial p
are zero.

Parameterize the dimensions other than
\tau
with a
2D-1

dimensional vector
s
, scaled so that
d\tau\,d^{2D-1}s=(dq\,dp)^{D}
. A local part of a wavefunction with support only on this
segment of trajectory is

\eta(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})=(2\pi\hbar)^{D}Ae^{i\beta(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})/\hbar}\delta^{2D-1}(s)

(83)

where
A
is a constant. Expanding
\beta
around
q,p
gives

\displaystyle\beta(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\beta(q,p)+\Bigl(\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial q}\cdot\frac{\partial q}{\partial\tau}+\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial p}\cdot\frac{\partial p}{\partial\tau}\Bigr)\tau

\displaystyle=\beta(q,p)+\Bigl(\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial q}\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}-\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial p}\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\Bigr)\tau

\displaystyle=\beta(q,p)+\{\beta,H\}\tau.

(84)

Using Eq. (
46
), this is

\beta(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})\approx\beta(q,p)+\Bigl(E-H+p\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Bigr)\tau.

(85)

Inserting (
81
), (
83
), and
(
85
) into (
76
), the projection is

\tilde{\eta}(q,p)\approx Ae^{i\beta(q,p)/\hbar}\\
\times\int\!d\tau\exp\Bigl[-\frac{\tau^{2}}{T^{2}}+\frac{i}{\hbar}(E-H)\tau\Bigr]

(86)

=\sqrt{\pi}ATe^{i\beta(q,p)/\hbar}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(E-H)^{2}T^{2}}{4\hbar^{2}}\Bigr].

(87)

Therefore, where the original function
\eta
had support, the
projected wavefunction
\tilde{\eta}
is maximal if
H
equals the
energy eigenvalue
E
. This then is the condition for a wavefunction
like (
83
) to best approximate an eigenfunction in

\mathcal{Q}
. A more general approximate eigenfunction can involve manifolds
with other values of
H
, but those values must be close to
E
, where
close means

\lvert E-H\rvert\lesssim\hbar/T.

(88)

In fact as
\lvert E-H\rvert
grows the projection approaches zero,
i.e., it converges to an element of the null space of the projection
operator.

Appendix B
Invariance of
p\cdot dq
under coordinate transformations

Suppose that we have some old variables
q,p
and want to transform
to new variables
Q,P
. A coordinate transformation
Q_{i}=f_{i}(q)

can be implemented as a canonical transformation with generating
function
(
Goldstein 1980
, Sec. 9-2)

F(q,P)=\sum_{i}f_{i}(q)P_{i}.

(89)

This doesn’t depend explicitly on time, so the Hamiltonian does not
change values. The old momenta can be written as

p_{i}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_{i}}=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial q_{i}}P_{j}.

(90)

Use this and the fact that the
q
are independent of
P
to express

\partial H/\partial P_{i}
as a function of the
Q,P
, i.e., the
derivative of
H\bigl(q(Q,P),p(Q,P)\bigr)
, getting

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial P_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\Bigl[\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}\frac{\partial q_{j}}{\partial P_{i}}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial P_{i}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial q_{j}}.

(91)

We can now write

\sum_{i}P_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial P_{i}}=\sum_{i,j}P_{i}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial q_{j}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}=\sum_{j}p_{j}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}.

(92)

This and the fact that Poisson brackets are canonical invariants show
that (
47
) keeps its form under this kind of
transformation.

Appendix C
Proof that
\{c_{i},H\}_{j}=0

If
q,p
are separable, they must be isolated in a function within the
Hamiltonian, so it can be expressed as

H(q_{\bar{\imath}},p_{\bar{\imath}},f_{i}(q_{i},p_{i}))

(
Goldstein 1980
, Section 10-4)
. Here

q_{\bar{\imath}},p_{\bar{\imath}}
indicates all of the
q,p
pairs
except the
i
-th. It is easy to show that
f_{i}(q_{i},p_{i})
is itself a
constant of the motion, and so a function of a corresponding
c
, say

c_{i}
. For a completely separable system this procedure is repeated
with successive
q,p
pairs, each isolated in its own function within

H
though possibly dependent on previously separated
q,p
pairs
through their corresponding constants of motion.

Put the indices of the
q,p
pairs in the order of their
separation. For brevity use

\{B,C\}_{i}=\frac{\partial B}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial C}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial C}{\partial q_{i}},

(93)

so the Poisson bracket
\{B,C\}=\sum_{i}\{B,C\}_{i}
. Because they are
constants of the motion,
\dot{c}_{i}=0
for all
i
, and for
c_{1}

this implies

0=\dot{c}_{1}=\{c_{1},H\}=\{c_{1},H\}_{1},

(94)

since
c_{1}
doesn’t depend on
q_{i},p_{i}
for
i>1
.
c_{2}

depends on
q_{2},p_{2}
and possibly
c_{1}
, so

0=\dot{c}_{2}=\{c_{2},H\}=\{c_{2},H\}_{2}+\frac{\partial c_{2}}{\partial c_{1}}\{c_{1},H\}_{1}\\
=\{c_{2},H\}_{2}.

(95)

Continuing this for successive
c
’s gives

0=\{c_{i},H\}_{i}

for all
i
. We can generalize this. For
i<j
,
c_{i}
is
independent of
q_{j},p_{j}
, so
0=\{c_{i},H\}_{j}
. For
j<i
the
expression
\{c_{i},H\}_{j}
does not depend on constants
c_{k}
with
k<j
, since it only has derivatives with respect to
q_{j}
and
p_{j}
. For

k
with
j\leq k<i
, the constant of motion
c_{i}
can depend on

c_{k}
, directly or through one or more
c_{l}
with
k<l<i
. This
leads to

\{c_{i},H\}_{j}=\sum_{k=j}^{i-1}\frac{\partial c_{i}}{\partial c_{k}}\{c_{k},H\}_{j}.

(96)

We can show that this is zero by induction on
i
. Therefore
Eq. (
51
), i.e.,
\{c_{i},H\}_{j}=0
, holds for all
i
and

j
.
