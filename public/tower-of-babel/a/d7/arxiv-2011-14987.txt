Abstract
. Orthogonal polynomials P n ​ ( λ ) P_{n}(\lambda) are oscillating functions of n n as n → ∞ n\to\infty for λ \lambda in the absolutely continuous spectrum of the corresponding Jacobi operator J J . We show that, irrespective of any specific assumptions on coefficients of the operator J J , amplitude and phase factors in asymptotic formulas for P n ​ ( λ ) P_{n}(\lambda) are linked by certain universal relations found in the paper. Our approach relies on a study of operators diagonalizing Jacobi operators. Diagonalizing operators are constructed in terms of orthogonal polynomials P n ​ ( λ ) P_{n}(\lambda) . They act from the space L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}) of functions into the space ℓ 2 ​ ( ℤ + ) \ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+}) of sequences. We consider such operators in a rather general setting and find necessary and sufficient conditions of their boundedness.

Main result

Jacobi operators

2.
Jacobi operators and orthogonal polynomials

2.1.
Basic facts

Let us briefly recall necessary results about
Jacobi operators
J
. They act in the space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
by the formula

(Ju)_{n}=a_{n-1}u_{n-1}+b_{n}u_{n}+a_{n}u_{n+1},\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\quad a_{-1}=0,

where the sequences
a_{n}>0
and
b_{n}=\bar{b}_{n}
are given and
u=\{u_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. A minimal symmetric operator
J_{0}
is
defined on a set of vectors with only a finite number of non-zero components
u_{n}
. The spectra of all self-adjoint extensions
J
of
J_{0}
are simple with
e_{0}=(1,0,0,\ldots)^{\top}
being a generating vector. The spectral measure of
J
is defined by the relation
d\rho_{J}(\lambda)=d(E_{J}(\lambda)e_{0},e_{0})
where
E_{J}(\lambda)
is the spectral family of the operator
J
.
If the Carleman condition

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-1}=\infty

(2.1)

is satisfied, then the operator
J_{0}
is essentially self-adjoint.

Orthonormal polynomials
P_{n}(z)
can be defined as solutions of the difference equation

a_{n-1}u_{n-1}(z)+b_{n}u_{n}(z)+a_{n}u_{n+1}(z)=zu_{n}(z),\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}},

(2.2)

satisfying the boundary condition
P_{0}(z)=1
. Clearly,
P_{n}(z)
is a polynomial of degree
n
:

P_{n}(z)=p_{n}z^{n}+\cdots
with
p_{n}>0
. It can be shown that
these polynomials are orthogonal and normalized in the spaces
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};d\rho)
:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}(\lambda)P_{m}(\lambda)d\rho(\lambda)=\delta_{n,m};

as usual,
\delta_{n,n}=1
and
\delta_{n,m}=0
for
n\neq m
. Here
d\rho=d\rho_{J}
is the spectral measure of an arbitrary self-adjoint extension
J
of the minimal operator
J_{0}
.
Alternatively, given a probability measure
d\rho(\lambda)
, the polynomials
P_{0}(\lambda),P_{1}(\lambda),\ldots,P_{n}(\lambda),\ldots
can be obtained by the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization of the monomials
1,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda^{n},\ldots
in the space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};d\rho)
.

Recall that mapping (
1.1
) is unitary, that is,

{\bf U}^{*}{\bf U}=I,\quad{\bf U}{\bf U}^{*}=I

(we denote by
I
the identity operator in different spaces)
and enjoys the intertwining property

({\bf U}^{*}Ju)(\lambda)=\lambda({\bf U}^{*}u)(\lambda)
.

Let
\Lambda
be an interval of the absolutely continuous spectrum of the operator
J
where the spectral measure is given by relation (
1.3
).
On the subspace
L^{2}(\Lambda;d\rho)
, mapping (
1.1
) can be reduced (if one sets
\sqrt{\tau(\lambda)}{\bf f}(\lambda)=f(\lambda)
) to the operator
U=U_{\Delta}:L^{2}(\Lambda)\to\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
defined by the relation

(Uf)_{n}=\int_{\Lambda}P_{n}(\lambda)\sqrt{\tau(\lambda)}f(\lambda)d\lambda,\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\quad f\in L^{2}(\Lambda).

(2.3)

Note that the operator
U
is isometric on the space
L^{2}(\Lambda)
.

Below we discuss asymptotic formula (
1.2
) for
\lambda\in\Lambda
. All our estimates are uniform in
\lambda
from compact subintervals of an open interval
\Lambda
.

2.2.
Classical polynomials

The Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}(\lambda)=P_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)
are parametrized by two indices
\alpha>-1
and

\tau(\lambda)=k(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}(1+\lambda)^{\beta},\quad\alpha,\beta>-1\quad\lambda\in\Lambda=(-1,1).

(2.4)

The constant

\rho({\mathbb{R}})=\rho((-1,1))=1

Explicit expressions for

17

According to formula (8.21.10) in the book
[

]

satisfy relation (
1.2

,

,

The Laguerre polynomials

P_{n}(\lambda)=P_{n}^{\alpha}(\lambda)

a_{n}=\sqrt{(n+1)(n+1+\alpha)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad b_{n}=2n+\alpha+1.

The corresponding Jacobi operators

According to formula (10.15.1) in

The Hermite polynomials
are determined by the Jacobi coefficients

a_{n}=\sqrt{(n+1)/2},\quad b_{n}=0

The corresponding Jacobi operator

has the absolutely continuous spectrum
coinciding with the whole axis

According to formula (10.15.18) in

Remark 2.1
.

Theorem

3.
Boundedness of integral operators

3.1.
Continuous case

To motivate our results on semidiscrete operators (
1.6
), let us previously consider the continuous case where the role of
V
(or of (
1.11
) for
d=1
) is played by the operator

({\sf V}f)(x)=v(x)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\theta(x)\omega(\lambda)}w(\lambda)f(\lambda)d\lambda

(3.1)

with sufficiently arbitrary functions
\theta(x)
and
\omega(\lambda)
. We suppose that these functions are monotone and tend to
\mp\infty
(or
\pm\infty
) as
x\to\mp\infty
and
\lambda\to\mp\infty
. Operators (
3.1
) can be reduced to the sandwiched Fourier transforms by unitary transformations corresponding to the changes of variables

y=\theta(x),\quad\mu=\omega(\lambda)
.
Indeed, let us set

(F_{1}u)(y)=\theta^{\prime}(x)^{-1/2}u(x),\quad(F_{2}f)(\mu)=\omega^{\prime}(\lambda)^{-1/2}f(\lambda).

(3.2)

Then

F_{1}{\sf V}=\widetilde{\sf V}F_{2}

where the operator
\widetilde{\sf V}
acts as

(\widetilde{\sf V}\tilde{f})(y)=\tilde{v}(y)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iy\mu}\tilde{w}(\mu)\tilde{f}(\mu)d\mu

with
\tilde{f}=F_{2}f
,

\tilde{v}(y)=\theta^{\prime}(x)^{-1/2}v(x),\quad\tilde{w}(\mu)=\omega^{\prime}(\lambda)^{-1/2}w(\lambda).

Since the operator
\widetilde{\sf V}
is bounded if the functions
\tilde{v}
and
\tilde{w}
are bounded, we can state the following elementary result.

Lemma 3.1
.

The operator
{\sf V}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
is bounded if

(\theta^{\prime})^{-1}|v|^{2}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad(\omega^{\prime})^{-1}|w|^{2}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}).

Of course the space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
can be replaced here by
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. For example, the operator
{\sf V}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
is bounded if

\theta(x)=x^{s},\quad|v(x)|\lx@nobreakspace\leq Cx^{(s-1)/2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\omega(\lambda)=\lambda^{t},\quad|w(\lambda)|\leq C\lambda^{(t-1)/2}.

Note that if
s>1
or
t>1
, then the functions
v(x)
or
w(\lambda)
may be unbounded at infinity.

The case of operators acting from
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
into
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
is essentially more difficult because there is no unitary operator in the space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
playing the role of the operator
F_{1}
defined by formula (
3.2
). Nevertheless, to a some extent, Lemma
3.1
remains true in this case – see Theorem
3.11
, below.

3.2.
Discrete Fourier transforms

It is convenient to study boundedness of operators (
1.6
) in a more general setting. Thus, we consider operators (
1.11
) where
\Phi
is the Fourier transform (
1.10
) and
dM(x)

is a locally finite measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(x)
be the ball of radius

satisfying the condition

Theorem 3.2
.

If condition (
3.3
) is satisfied and

|w(\xi)|\leq C(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-\ell/2}

(3.4)

for some integer

\|\lx@nobreakspace A\|\leq C\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}M({\mathbb{B}}_{1}(x)).

Conversely, if

is the characteristic function of some ball and the operator

is bounded, then condition (

Proof.

Let us consider the lattice of unit cubes
\Pi_{n}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. By the Sobolev inequality, it follows from condition (
3.3
) that, for every

\int_{\Pi_{n}}|u(x)|^{2}dM(x)\leq M(\Pi_{n})\max_{x\in\Pi_{n}}|u(x)|^{2}\leq C\int_{\Pi_{n}}\big(|\nabla^{(\ell)}u(x)|^{2}+|u(x)|^{2}\big)dx.

Summing these estimates over all cubes
\Pi_{n}
, we see that

\|u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};dM)}^{2}\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big(|(\nabla^{(\ell)}u)(x)|^{2}+|u(x)|^{2}\big)dx.

(3.6)

It means that operator (
1.11
) is bounded and estimate (
3.5
) holds.

Conversely, take any not identically zero function

such that

. There exist a ball
{\mathbb{B}}_{r_{0}}(x_{0})
and a number

such that the Fourier transform of

satisfies

|\hat{\varphi}(x)|\geq c>0\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in{\mathbb{B}}_{r_{0}}(x_{0}).

(3.7)

If condition (
3.3
) is violated, then there exists a sequence
x_{n}
,
x_{n}\to\infty
, such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}M({\mathbb{B}}_{r_{0}}(x_{n}))=\infty.

(3.8)

Set

f_{n}(\xi)=e^{i(x_{0}-x_{n})\xi}\varphi(\xi).

(3.9)

Since

\hat{f}_{n}(x)=\hat{\varphi}(x-x_{n}+x_{0}),

it follows from (
3.7
), (
3.8
) that

\|Af_{n}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};dM)}^{2}\geq\int_{{\mathbb{B}}_{r_{0}}(x_{n})}|\hat{f}_{n}(x)|^{2}dM(x)\geq c^{2}M({\mathbb{B}}_{r_{0}}(x_{n}))\to\infty

as

∎

Although Theorem

is quite elementary, we were unable to localize it in the literature. On the other hand, in the case of absolutely continuous measures, it is equivalent to Theorem 3 in
[
18
]
. In the general case, the proof is practically the same.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that boundedness of operator (

satisfying estimate (

Remark 3.4
.

Suppose now that a measure

Remark 3.5

F(\mu)
– by formula (
4.18
),
{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)
– by (
4.8
) and
\Psi_{n}(t)
– by (
4.9
).
Then, as
t\to+\infty
,

(e^{-i\Theta(J)t}u)_{n}=\sqrt{2\pi}v_{n}e^{-\pi i\tau/4}e^{i\Psi_{n}(t)}t^{-1/2}{\mathcal{F}}(x_{n}/t)\mathbbm{1}(x_{n}/t\in{\mathcal{I}})+\varrho_{n}(t),

(4.20)

where
{\mathcal{I}}=\theta^{\prime}(\omega(\Lambda))
,
\tau=\operatorname{sgn}\theta^{\prime\prime}
and the remainder
\varrho_{n}(t)
satisfies (
4.16
).

According to (
4.20
) the sequence
(e^{-i\Theta(J)t}u)_{n}
“lives” in the region of
n
such that
x_{n}/t\in{\mathcal{I}}
. Below, the phase factor
e^{i\Psi_{n}(t)}
is not very important. Since

\|e^{-i\Theta(J)t}u\|=\|u\|=\|Uf\|=\|f\|,

(4.21)

we can state

Corollary 4.6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.5
we have

2\pi\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\!\!\!\sum_{n:x_{n}/t\in{\mathcal{I}}}v_{n}^{2}\,|{\mathcal{F}}(x_{n}/t)|^{2}=\|f\|^{2}.

(4.22)

Remark 4.7
.

It suffices to make assumptions on only one derivative of functions
\tau,\kappa
and two derivatives of functions
\omega,\Phi_{n}
. However in this case one has to demand (
4.13
) for
k=1
.

Remark 4.8
.

The stationary phase method does not necessarily require that the function
\Theta(\lambda)
be defined by formula (
4.17
). If
\omega^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
, we can also take
\Theta(\lambda)=\lambda
and if
\omega(\lambda)=\omega_{0}\lambda
, any function
\Theta(\lambda)
with
\Theta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
is admissible. Asymptotic formula (

) remains essentially the same in all these cases. On the contrary if

\omega(\lambda)=\omega_{0}\lambda
and
\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta_{0}\lambda
, then the evolution
e^{-i\Theta(J)t}u
reduces to shifts; see Appendix B.

4.3.
Main result

Relations (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) are deduced from formula (
4.22
). To that end, we need
the following auxiliary result. It is a direct consequence of the definition of an integral of a continuous function as a limit of the corresponding Riemann sums.

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
is a bounded interval and a function
{\mathcal{F}}\in C({\mathcal{I}})
. Let
a sequence
X_{n}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, be such that
X_{n+1}(t)>X_{n}(t)
,
X_{n}(t)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
for all
t
and

\lim_{t\to\infty}\max_{n:X_{n}(t)\in{\mathcal{I}}}(X_{n+1}(t)-X_{n}(t))=0.

(4.23)

Then

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{n:X_{n}(t)\in{\mathcal{I}}}(X_{n+1}(t)-X_{n}(t))|{\mathcal{F}}(X_{n}(t))|^{2}=\int_{{\mathcal{I}}}|{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi.

(4.24)

Now we can state our main result.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let Assumption
4.4
be satisfied. Suppose additionally that

\lim_{n\to\infty}(x_{n+1}-x_{n})/x_{n}=0.

(4.25)

Define a sequence
\sigma_{n}
by equality (
3.11
)
and assume that a limit
(
possibly, infinite
)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}=\sigma

(4.26)

exists. Then necessarily
0<\sigma<\infty

and

2\pi\sigma\tau(\lambda)\kappa^{2}(\lambda)=\omega^{\prime}(\lambda).

(4.27)

Proof.

Let us set
{\mathcal{I}}=\theta^{\prime}(\omega(\Lambda))
,
X_{n}(t)=x_{n}/t
and observe that the sequence

X_{n+1}(t)-X_{n}(t)=((x_{n+1}-x_{n})/x_{n})X_{n}(t)

satisfies condition (
4.23
).
Indeed, the first factor tends to zero in view of (
4.25
) and the second one is bounded if
X_{n}(t)\in{\mathcal{I}}
.
Note also that
n\to\infty
if
X_{n}(t)\in{\mathcal{I}}
and
t\to\infty
.
Applying relation (
4.24
) to a function
{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)
defined by equalities (
4.8
) and (
4.18
) and using condition (
4.26
), we find that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{n:X_{n}(t)\in{\mathcal{I}}}\sigma_{n}(X_{n+1}(t)-X_{n}(t))|{\mathcal{F}}(X_{n}(t))|^{2}=\sigma\int_{{\mathcal{I}}}|{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\\
=\sigma\int_{{\mathcal{I}}}|h^{\prime}(\xi)||F(h(\xi))|^{2}d\xi=\sigma\int_{\omega(\Lambda)}|F(\mu)|^{2}d\mu=\sigma\int_{\Lambda}\omega^{\prime}(\lambda)^{-1}\varkappa(\lambda)^{2}|f(\lambda)|^{2}d\lambda.

(4.28)

Since

t^{-1}v_{n}^{2}=\sigma_{n}(X_{n+1}(t)-X_{n}(t)),

it follows from Corollary
4.6
that the left-hand side of (
4.28
) equals
(2\pi)^{-1}\|f\|^{2}
.
Therefore relation (
4.28
) ensures that

\int_{\Lambda}|f(\lambda)|^{2}d\lambda=2\pi\sigma\int_{\Lambda}\omega^{\prime}(\lambda)^{-1}\varkappa^{2}(\lambda)|f(\lambda)|^{2}d\lambda

(4.29)

for an arbitrary function
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Lambda)
. Thus,
\sigma
is necessary finite,
\sigma\neq 0
and

2\pi\sigma\omega^{\prime}(\lambda)^{-1}\varkappa^{2}(\lambda)=1
. This yields (
4.27
).
∎

Remark 4.11
.

Condition (
4.25
) imposes a mild restriction on the growth of
x_{n}
as
n\to\infty
. However it is satisfied for
x_{n}=n^{s}
with an arbitrary
s>0
.

Appendix A
An elementary inequality

As a by-product of our considerations, we obtain a simple inequality for functions in the Sobolev space
{\sf H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. First, we note that

|u(0)|^{2}\leq c_{0}\int_{0}^{1}(|u^{\prime}(x)|^{2}+|u(x)|^{2})dx

(A.1)

where we can choose
c_{0}=2(\sqrt{5}-1)^{-1}
. We check the following result.

Proposition A.1
.

Let a sequence
x_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, be such that
x_{n}\to\pm\infty
as
n\to\pm\infty
and
x_{n}<x_{n+1}<x_{n}+\delta
for some
\delta>0
and all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(x_{n+1}-x_{n})|u(x_{n})|^{2}\leq c_{0}\max\{1,\delta^{2}\}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(|u^{\prime}(x)|^{2}+|u(x)|^{2})dx

(A.2)

for all
u\in{\sf H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

Applying estimate (
A.1
) to the function
u_{\varepsilon}(x)=u(\varepsilon x)
and making the change of variables
y=\varepsilon x
, we see that

|u(0)|^{2}\leq c_{0}\int_{0}^{1}(\varepsilon^{2}|u^{\prime}(\varepsilon x)|^{2}+|u(\varepsilon x)|^{2})dx=c_{0}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon|u^{\prime}(y)|^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}|u(y)|^{2})dy

whence

\varepsilon|u(x_{n})|^{2}\leq c_{0}\int_{x_{n}}^{x_{n}+\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{2}|u^{\prime}(y)|^{2}+|u(y)|^{2})dy.

(A.3)

Let us set here
\varepsilon=x_{n+1}-x_{n}
. Then
\varepsilon\leq\delta
and (
A.3
) yields

(x_{n+1}-x_{n})|u(x_{n})|^{2}\leq c_{0}\max\{1,\delta^{2}\}\int_{x_{n}}^{x_{n+1}}(|u^{\prime}(y)|^{2}+|u(y)|^{2})dy.

It remains to take the sum of these estimates over all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
∎

Inequality (
A.2
) is quite elementary but, surprisingly, we were unable to find it in the literature. Of course
(
A.2
) implies again inequalities (
3.12
) and (
3.13
).

Appendix B
Dispersionless evolution

If
x_{n}=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
(which excludes the linear growth of
x_{n}
) and the phases
\Phi_{n}
do not depend on
\lambda

) for the case

excluded in Sect. 4.2. Making the change of variables (

(e^{-i\omega(J)t}u)_{n}=\sqrt{2\pi}v_{n}\big(e^{i\Phi_{n}}\widehat{F}(x_{n}-t)+e^{-i\Phi_{n}}\widehat{F}(-x_{n}-t)\big)+\varrho_{n}(t)

where
\widehat{F}(x)

. The remainder
\varrho_{n}(t)
is given by (

) is also negligible. Thus instead of Theorem

Theorem B.1
.

Let Assumption
4.4
be satisfied, and let

. Then

(e^{-i\omega(J)t}u)_{n}=\sqrt{2\pi}v_{n}e^{i\Phi_{n}}\widehat{F}(x_{n}-t)+\varrho_{n}(t)

where the remainder
\varrho_{n}(t)
obeys condition (
4.16
).

It follows from (

) that

\|e^{-i\omega(J)t}u\|^{2}=2\pi\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}v_{n}^{2}|\widehat{F}(x_{n}-t)|^{2}+o(1),\quad t\to\infty,

Note that formula (
4.20
) is not true now because

\theta(\lambda)=\lambda

\theta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=0

On the other hand, one can calculate the left-hand side of (

X_{n}(t)=x_{n}-t

\sigma_{n}
