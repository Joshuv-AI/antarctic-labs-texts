Huber Loss-Based Penalty Approach to Problems with Linear Constraints Thanks: Submitted to the editors on ……
Angelia Nedić, Tatiana Tatarenko

Abstract
We consider an optimization problem with many linear inequalities constraints. To deal with a large number of constraints, we provide a penalty reformulation of the problem, where the penalty is a variant of the one-sided Huber loss function. We study the infeasibility properties of the solutions of penalized problems for nonconvex and convex objective functions, as the penalty parameters vary with time. Then, we propose a random incremental penalty method for solving convex problem, and investigate its convergence properties for convex and strongly convex objective functions. We show that the iterates of the method converge to a solution of the original problem almost surely and in expectation for suitable choices of the penalty parameters and the stepsize. Also, we establish convergence rate of the method for appropriately defined weighted averages of the iterates for the expected function values. We establish O ⁡ ( ln 1 / 2 + ϵ ⁡ k / k ) O(\ln^{1/2+\epsilon}k/{\sqrt{k}}) -convergence rate when the objective function is convex, and O ⁡ ( ln ϵ ⁡ k / k ) O(\ln^{\epsilon}k/k) -convergence rate when the objective function is strongly convex with ϵ \epsilon being any small positive number. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first results on the convergence rate for the penalty-based incremental subgradient method with time-varying penalty parameters.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the problem of minimizing a

convex
function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
over a convex and closed set
X

that is the intersection of finitely many sets
X_{i}
, represented by linear inequalities,
i=1,\ldots,m,

where
m\geq 2
is large,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\hbox{min}\,f(x),\quad\hbox{s.t.}\,x\in X=\cap_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}.\vskip-8.5359pt

(1)

Optimization problems of the form (
1
) arise in many areas of research,
such as digital filter settings in communication systems
[
1
]
, energy consumption in Smart Grids
[
7
]
, convex relaxations of various combinatorial optimization problems in machine learning applications
[
19
,
39
]
.

Our interest is in case when
m
is large, which prohibits us from using
projected gradient and
augmented Lagrangian methods
[
2
,
38
]
,
that require either computation of the (Euclidean) projection or
an estimation of the gradient for the sum of many functions, at each iteration.
To reduce the complexity, one may consider a method that operates on a single set
X_{i}

from the constraint set collection
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
at each iteration.
Algorithms using random constraint sampling for general convex optimization problems (
1
) have been first considered in
[
22
]
and were extended in
[
36
]
to a broader class of randomization over the sets of constraints. The convergence rate analysis in
[
22
,
36
]

demonstrates that the expected optimality error (in terms of function values) diminishes to zero with the rate of
O(1/\sqrt{k})

which is the optimal convergence rate for merely convex functions.
The work
[
26
]
further extends the results from
[
22
,
36
]
to non asymptotic analysis of problems with potentially infinitely many constraints. On the other hand, a primal-dual method updating a random coordinate of the dual variable pro iteration was proposed for generalized linear programming with equality constraints in
[
32
]
.

A possible reformulation of the problem (
1
) is through the use of the indicator functions of
the constraint sets, resulting in the following unconstrained problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\{\frac{1}{m}f(x)+\chi_{i}(x)\right\},

(2)

where
\chi_{i}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is the indicator function of the set
X_{i}

(taking value
0
at the points

x\in X_{i}
and, otherwise, taking value
+\infty
).
The advantage of this reformulation is that the objective function is the sum of convex functions and incremental methods can be employed that compute only a (sub)-gradient of one of the component functions at each iteration.
The traditional incremental methods do not have memory, and their origin can be traced back to work of Kibardin
[
15
]
. They have been studied for
smooth least-square problems
[
3
,
18
]
, for training the neural networks
[
10
,
11
]
, for smooth convex problems
[
31
,
35
]
and
for non-smooth convex problems
[
23
,
9
,
12
,
14
,
37
]

(see
[
5
]
for a more comprehensive survey of these methods).
However, no rate of convergence to the exact solution has been obtained for such procedures.
Reformulation (
2
) has been considered in
[
16
]
as a departure point
toward an exact penalty reformulation using the set-distance functions.
This exact penalty formulation has been motivated by a simple exact penalty model proposed in
[
4
]
(using only the set-distance functions)
and a more general penalty model considered in
[
5
]
.
In
[
16
]
, a lower bound on the penalty parameter
has been identified guaranteeing that the optimal solutions of the penalized problem are also optimal solutions of the original problem (
2
). However, this bound depends on a so-called regularity constant for the constraint set, which might be difficult to estimate. Moreover, the proposed approaches in
[
16
]
do not utilize
incremental processing, but rather primal-dual approaches where a full (sub)-gradient of the penalized function
is used.
On a broader scale, our work is related to random methods for solving linear feasibility problems
[
21
,
33
]
, and their extensions to solving convex inequality systems
[
20
]
.

In contrast to the penalized formulation in (
2
) and the
works mentioned above, this paper deals with a penalized reformulation of the problem (
1
), where the penalized problems vary with time.
This is done by varying penalty parameters so as to gradually decrease the infeasibility of the iterates. In this way, we can guarantee convergence of the
single time scale
procedure incremental procedure
to an exact solution of the original problem (
1
). Our choice of the penalty functions is a variant of the one-sided Huber losses
[
17
]
, which have Lipschitz continuous gradients.
In the work
[
34
]
, existence of the fixed penalty choices for this penalized reformulation has been shown under which the fast incremental algorithms can be applied to achieve convergence to a feasible point in a predefined neighborhood of the optimal solution of the original problem, with a linear convergence rate. However, to guarantee this convergence, some problem specific parameters need to be known, which are difficult to estimate in practice. The recent paper
[
25
]
deals with the penalty parameters which vary with time. However, that work considers a strongly convex objective function
f(\cdot)
exclusively.
In this present paper, we
show that some of the results in
[
25
]
extend to nonconvex objective functions
f(\cdot)
and to merely convex functions, as given in Section
3.1
and
Section
3.2
, respectively.

The random incremental penalty-based gradient method has been proposed in
[
25
]
and analyzed for a strongly convex objective function
f(\cdot)
with Lipschitz continuous gradients. In this paper, however,
we consider a subgradient variant of the method in Section
4
for both convex and strongly convex
f(\cdot)
.
Therein, we analyze its almost sure convergence and show its convergence rate of the order
O(\ln^{1/2+\epsilon}k/\sqrt{k})
for merely convex objective function, where
\epsilon>0
is arbitrarily small. Moreover, for strongly convex objective function, we establish convergence rate
of the order
O(\ln^{\epsilon}k/{k})
for arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
, which improves the convergence rate of
O(1/\sqrt{k})
provided in
[
25
]
.
Note that these rates possess better dependence on the logarithmic term than the rates obtained in
[
8
]
. In that work the authors focus on an optimization problem over a convex set with a special structure and present a penalty-based method with a double loop structure, where at each iteration the corresponding penalized problem has
to be solved up to some accuracy, and prove the convergence rates of the order
O(\ln k/{\sqrt{k}})
and
O(\ln k/{k})
for the purely convex and strongly convex cases respectively.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
,
we provide the penalty based formulation of the original problem (
1
)
and some basic properties of the chosen penalty functions.
In Section
3
, we investigate the relations for the solutions of the penalized problems, as we vary the penalty parameters, for continuous nonconvex objective function
f(\cdot)
and for convex objective function
f(\cdot)
.
In Section
4
, we propose a random incremental penalty method and show that its iterates converge almost surely and in expectation to a solution of the original problem, under suitable assumptions on the penalty parameters and the stepsize. In Section 4, we also provide convergence rate estimates using appropriate weighted averages of the iterates. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first results on the convergence rate for the penalty-based incremental subgradient method with time-varying penalty parameters.
In Section
6
, we conclude the paper.

2
Problem Formulation and its Penalty-based Reformulation

We consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\hbox{min}\,f(x),\quad\hbox{s.t.}\,\langle a_{i},x\rangle-b_{i}\leq 0,\ i\in[m],

(3)

where
[m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
the vectors
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[m]
, are nonzero.
We will assume that the problem is
feasible
.
Throughout the paper, we use
X_{i}
to denote the set of points satisfying the
i
-th inequality constraint, i.e.,

X_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle a_{i},x\rangle-b_{i}\leq 0\}\quad\hbox{for all }i\in[m],

and
X
to denote the nonempty intersection of the sets
X_{i}
,
i\in[m]
, i.e.,
X=\cap_{i\in[m]}X_{i}
.
Associated with problem (
3
), we
consider a penalized problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F_{\gamma\delta}(x),

(4)

where

F_{\gamma\delta}(x)=f(x)+\frac{\gamma}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{\delta}\left(x;a_{i},b_{i}\right).

(5)

Here,
\gamma>0
and
\delta\geq 0
are penalty parameters.
The vectors
a_{i}
and scalars
b_{i}
are the same as those characterizing the constraints in problem (
3
).
For a given nonzero vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
, the penalty function

h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
is given by

\displaystyle h_{\delta}(x;a,b)=\begin{cases}\frac{\langle a,x\rangle-b}{\|a\|}&\text{ if }\langle a,x\rangle-b>\delta,\\
\frac{(\langle a,x\rangle-b+\delta)^{2}}{4\delta\|a\|}&\text{ if }-\delta\leq\langle a,x\rangle-b\leq\delta,\\
0&\text{ if }\langle a,x\rangle-b<-\delta,\end{cases}

(6)

(see Figure
1
for an illustration).

\begin{overpic}[plot_h_new.eps]\put(22.0,34.0){\scriptsize{$\delta=1$}}
\put(22.0,31.4){\scriptsize{$\delta=\frac{1}{2}$}}
\put(22.0,28.5){\scriptsize{$\delta=\frac{1}{4}$}}
\put(51.0,-1.0){\Large{$x$}}
\put(4.0,17.0){\rotatebox{90.0}{\Large$h_{\delta}$}}
\end{overpic}

Figure 1:
Penalty functions
h_{\delta}(x;1,1)
for the constraint
x-1\leq 0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
with
\delta\in\left\{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2},1\right\}
.

The penalty function
h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
is a variant of the one-sided Huber loss functions.
Originally, such functions have been introduced in applications of robust regression models
to make them less sensitive to outliers in data in comparison with the squared error loss
[
17
]
.
In contrast, here,
we use this type of penalty function to smoothen the distance-based penalties (the average distance to the sets
X_{i}
)
proposed in
[
5
]
.
Furthermore, an appropriate choice of the parameter
\delta\geq 0
allows us to increase the penalty values as compared to the penalties based on the squared distances to the sets
X_{i}
, which have a small penalty values around an optimum lying close to the boundary of the constraint set
[
30
]
.

For any
\delta\geq 0
, the function
h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
satisfies the following relations:

\displaystyle h_{\delta}(x;a,b)\geq 0\qquad\hbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(7)

\displaystyle h_{\delta}(x;a,b)\leq\frac{\delta}{4\|a\|}\qquad\hbox{for all $x$ satisfying }\langle a,x\rangle\leq b,

(8)

\displaystyle h_{\delta}(x;a,b)>\frac{\delta}{4\|a\|}\qquad\hbox{for all $x$ satisfying }\langle a,x\rangle>b.

(9)

For a vector
a\neq 0
, the function
h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
is a composition of
a scalar function

\displaystyle p_{\delta}(s)=\begin{cases}s&\text{ if }\quad s>\delta,\\
\frac{(s+\delta)^{2}}{4\delta}&\text{ if }\quad-\delta\leq s\leq\delta,\\
0&\text{ if }\quad s<-\delta,\end{cases}

(10)

with a linear function
x\mapsto\langle a,x\rangle-b
, scaled by
\frac{1}{\|a\|}
, i.e.,

h_{\delta}(x;a,b)=\frac{1}{\|a\|}p_{\delta}(\langle a,x\rangle-b)\qquad\hbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(11)

The function
p_{\delta}(\cdot)
is convex on
\mathbb{R}
for any
\delta\geq 0
,
implying that the function
h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any
\delta\geq 0
.

Furthermore, the function

p_{\delta}(\cdot)
is twice differentiable for any
\delta>0
,
with the first and second derivatives given by

\displaystyle p^{\prime}_{\delta}(s)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\quad s>\delta,\\
\frac{(s+\delta)}{2\delta}&\text{ if }\quad-\delta\leq s\leq\delta,\\
0&\text{ if }\quad s<-\delta,\end{cases}

(12)

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}(s)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\delta}&\text{ if }\quad-\delta\leq s\leq\delta,\\
0&\text{ if }\quad s<-\delta\quad\text{or}\quad s>\delta.\end{cases}

Thus, for
\delta>0
, the function
p_{\delta}(\cdot)
has Lipschitz continuous derivatives with a constant
\frac{1}{2\delta}
.
Hence, the function
h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
is differentiable for any
\delta>0
, and
its gradient is given by

\nabla h_{\delta}(x;a,b)=\frac{1}{\|a\|}\,p^{\prime}_{\delta}(\langle a,x\rangle-b)a\qquad\hbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(13)

Moreover,
\nabla h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
is Lipschitz continuous with a constant
\frac{\|a\|}{2\delta}
, i.e.,
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\|\nabla h_{\delta}(x;a,b)-\nabla h_{\delta}(y;a,b)\|\leq\frac{\|a\|}{2\delta}\,\|x-y\|.

(14)

In view of the definition of the penalty function
F_{\gamma\delta}
in (
5
)
and relation (
13
),
we can see that the magnitude of the “slope” of the penalty function is controlled by the parameter
\gamma>0
,
while the ratio of the parameters
\gamma
and
\delta
is controlling the “curvature” of the penalty function.
Finally, in the following lemma, we provide some additional properties of the gradients
\nabla h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider the function
h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
as given in (
6
).
Then, we have
\|\nabla h_{\delta}(x;a,b)\|\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Additionally,
if
\delta_{1}\geq\delta_{2}
, then

\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla h_{\delta_{1}}(x;a,b)-\nabla h_{\delta_{2}}(x;a,b)\|\leq\frac{\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}}{2\delta_{1}}.

Proof.

Can be found in
[
34
]
.

Throughout the rest of the article,
we let
\Pi_{Y}[x]
denote the (Euclidean) projection of a point
x
on a convex closed set
Y
,
i.e.,

{\textrm{dist}}(x,Y)=\|x-\Pi_{Y}[x]\|.

Also, the smallest norm of the vectors
a_{i}
,
i\in[m]
, is denoted by
\alpha_{\min}
, i.e.,

\alpha_{\min}=\min_{i\in[m]}\|a_{i}\|
.

3
Time-varying Penalty Parameters

Consider positive scalar sequences
\{\delta_{k}\}
and
\{\gamma_{k}\}

and, for each
k
, let
F_{k}
denote the penalty function
F_{\delta_{k}\gamma_{k}}(x)
, i.e.,

F_{k}(x)=f(x)+\frac{\gamma_{k}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{k}\left(x;a_{i},b_{i}\right),

(15)

where we use
h_{k}\left(\cdot;a_{i},b_{i}\right)
to denote
h_{\delta_{k}}\left(\cdot;a_{i},b_{i}\right)
for each
i
.
We let
X_{k}^{*}
denote the optimal set for the penalized problem
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F_{k}(x)
and
X^{*}
denote the optimal set for the original problem (
3
). We next investigate the properties of the optimal solutions of the penalized problems for the case of a general function
f(\cdot)
and the case when
f(\cdot)
is convex.

3.1
General Function
f

When
f(\cdot)
is continuous and has bounded lower-level sets,
the optimal set
X^{*}
for the original problem is nonempty and compact. Also, for each
k
,
the optimal set
X_{k}^{*}
of the penalized problem is also nonempty and compact.

As a solution
x_{k}^{*}\in X_{k}^{*}
need not be feasible, we next estimate the distance of any
x_{k}^{*}\in X_{k}^{*}

from the feasible set
X
.
To simplify the notation, we denote by
H_{k}(\cdot)
the average of the penalty functions
h_{k}(\cdot;a_{i},b_{i})
,
i\in[m]
,

H_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{k}(x;a_{i},b_{i})\qquad\hbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(16)

Thus, the penalty function in (
15
)
is written as

F_{k}(x)=f(x)+\gamma_{k}H_{k}(x)\qquad\hbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(17)

Regarding the average penalty
H_{k}(\cdot)
, we have an upper bound on its values at feasible points
x\in X
via relation (
8
).
Specifically, setting
\delta=\delta_{k}
in relation (
8
), we have
h_{k}(x;a_{i},b_{i})\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{4\|a_{i}\|}\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{4\alpha_{\min}}
for all
x\in X
and
i\in[m]
,
implying that

H_{k}(x)\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{4\alpha_{\min}}\qquad\hbox{for all $x\in X$ and all }k\geq 1.

(18)

To lower bound the value
H_{k}(x)
at any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we use
the following result regarding the intersection of linear sets.

Lemma 2
(Hoffman lemma
[
13
]
).

Given a collection of sets
X_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle a_{i},x\rangle-b\leq 0\}
,
i\in[m]
,
with a nonempty intersection
X=\cap_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}
,
there exists a scalar

\beta=\beta(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})>0
such that

\beta\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\textrm{dist}}(x,X_{i})\geq{\textrm{dist}}(x,X)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The following lemma and its corollary provide some additional
properties of the penalty function
h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
that we will use later on.
The proof can be found in Lemma 1 in
[
34
]
.

Lemma 3
.

Given a nonzero vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a scalar
b\in\mathbb{R}
,
consider the penalty function
h_{\delta}(\cdot;a,b)
defined in (
6
) with
\delta\geq 0
.
Let
Y=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle a,x\rangle-b\leq 0\}.

Then, we have for
\delta=0
,
h_{0}(x;a,b)={\textrm{dist}}(x,Y)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and for any
0\leq\delta\leq\delta^{\prime}
,

h_{\delta}(x;a,b)\leq h_{\delta^{\prime}}(x;a,b)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Lemma
3
,
which shows that any
feasible point
\hat{x}\in X
can be used to construct non-empty level sets of

F_{\gamma\delta}(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot)
.
The proof can be found in Corollary 2 in
[
34
]
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\gamma>0
and
\delta\geq 0
be arbitrary, and let

\hat{x}
be a feasible point for the original problem (
3
).
Then, for the scalar
t_{\gamma\delta}(\hat{x})
defined by

t_{\gamma\delta}(\hat{x})=f(\hat{x})+\gamma\delta/(4\alpha_{\min}),

the level set

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid F_{\gamma\delta}(x)\leq t_{\gamma\delta}(\hat{x})\}
is nonempty and

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid F_{\gamma\delta}(x)\leq t_{\gamma\delta}(\hat{x})\}\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)\leq t_{\gamma\delta}(\hat{x})\}
.
Moreover, the solution set
X^{*}_{\gamma\delta}
of
the penalized problem (
4
) is contained in the level set

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)\leq t_{\gamma\delta}(\hat{x})\}
.

Now, a lower bound on the value
H_{k}(x)
at any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
follows from
Hoffman Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
, as follows.
By Lemma
3
with
\delta=0
,
\delta^{\prime}=\delta_{k}
,
a=a_{i}
,
b=b_{i}
, and
Y=X_{i}
, we obtain
h_{k}(x;a_{i},b_{i})\geq h_{0}(x;a_{i},b_{i})={\textrm{dist}}(x,X_{i})\quad\hbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
i\in[m]
.
Therefore, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

H_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{k}(x;a_{i},b_{i})\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\textrm{dist}}(x,X_{i})
.
By using Hoffman Lemma
2
, we obtain

H_{k}(x)\geq\frac{1}{m{\beta}}{\textrm{dist}}(x,X)\quad\hbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(19)

The following result provides an upper bound on the distance of
x_{k}^{*}
from the feasible set
X
,
and shows that this distance goes to 0 if
\gamma_{k}
tends to infinity and
\delta_{k}
tends to 0.

Proposition 1
.

Let
f(\cdot)
be continuous with bounded lower-level sets,
and let
\gamma_{k}>0
,
\delta_{k}>0
, and
\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}\leq c
for all
k
and for some
c>0
.
Then, for arbitrary
\hat{x}\in X
and all
k
,

{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k}^{*},X)\leq\frac{m{\beta}}{\gamma_{k}}(f(\hat{x})-f(x_{k}^{*}))+\frac{m{\beta}\delta_{k}}{4\alpha_{\min}},

where
\beta
is the Hoffman constant from Lemma
2
. In particular,

\lim_{k\to\infty}{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k}^{*},X)=0
as
\gamma_{k}\to\infty
and
\delta_{k}\to 0
,
with the convergence rate of the order
O(\gamma_{k}^{-1}+\delta_{k})
.

We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for the proof.

3.2
Convex Function
f
not Necessarily Differentiable

Here, we consider the case when
f(\cdot)
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since the domain of
f(\cdot)
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the function
f
is continuous. The subdifferential set
\partial f(x)
is nonempty at every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

since
f(\cdot)
is convex and
{\textrm{dom}}(f)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The function
f(\cdot)
is strongly convex with a constant
\mu>0
if and only if
for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all subgradients
\tilde{\nabla}f(x)\in\partial f(x)
, we have

f(v)+\langle\tilde{\nabla}f(v),u-v\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|u-v\|^{2}\leq f(u).

(20)

Moreover, when
f(\cdot)
is strongly convex with a constant
\mu
, we also have for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and all subgradients
\tilde{\nabla}f(x)\in\partial f(x)

and
\tilde{\nabla}f(y)\in\partial f(y)
,

\mu\|x-y\|^{2}\leq\langle\tilde{\nabla}f(x)-\tilde{\nabla}f(y),x-y\rangle.

(21)

If
f(\cdot)
is just convex relations (
20
) and (
21
) hold with
\mu=0
.
To capture both cases when
f
is strongly convex and when
f
is just convex,
we will sometimes abuse the definition of strong convexity in (
20
) by allowing
the possibility that
\mu=0
.

When
f(\cdot)
is strongly convex with
\mu>0
, then so is every penalty function
F_{k}(\cdot)
in (
15
) with the same
\mu>0
.
In this case,
the original problem (
3
) and each penalized problem

, respectively.
Moreover, under mild conditions on the penalty parameters

and

Lemma 4
.

Let

be continuous and have bounded lower-level sets.
Let
\gamma_{k}>0
,

\delta_{k}>0
, and
\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}\leq c
for all

Then, the optimal set

for the penalized problem

X_{k}^{*}\subseteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)\leq t_{c}(\hat{x})\right\}
for all

t_{c}(\hat{x})=f(\hat{x})+c/(4\alpha_{\min})
, with
\hat{x}\in X
.
In particular, the set sequence
\{X_{k}^{*}\}
is uniformly bounded.

Remark 1
.

When the conditions of Lemma

are satisfied, the optimal solutions of the penalized problems

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F_{k}(x)
, for all
k\geq 1
,
are uniformly bounded, i.e., there exists
D>0
such that

\|x_{k}^{*}\|\leq D
for all
x_{k}^{*}\in X_{k}^{*}
and all
k\geq 1
.
Therefore, the projections
\Pi_{X}[x_{k}^{*}]
of these optimal solutions on the feasible set

are also uniformly bounded, i.e., there exists
R>0
such that

\|\Pi_{X}[x_{k}^{*}]\|\leq R
for all
x_{k}^{*}\in X_{k}^{*}
and all
k\geq 1
.
Hence, the subgradients
\tilde{\nabla}f(x)\in\partial f(x)
, for all
x
with
\|x\|\leq R
,
are bounded, i.e.,

L=\max_{\|x\|\leq R}\{\|\tilde{\nabla}f(x)\|\,\mid\tilde{\nabla}f(x)\in\partial f(x)\}<\infty.

(22)

We next provide a set of conditions on parameters
\delta_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}
ensuring
that the sequence
\{x_{k}^{*}\}
converges to
x^{*}
as
k\to\infty
when

is strongly convex.
When
f(\cdot)
is just convex, we obtain a special bound on
{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k}^{*},X)
for any solution
x_{k}^{*}\in X_{k}^{*}
to the penalized problem.
This bound yields an improved convergence rate of

compared to
that for a general function

provided in Proposition

Proposition 2
.

Let
f(\cdot)
be strongly convex with
\mu\geq 0
. If

, assume that
f(\cdot)

has bounded lower-level sets.
Let
\gamma_{k}>0
,
\delta_{k}>0
, and
\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}\leq c
for all
k
and some

.
Then, for all

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}\|x^{*}-x_{k}^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x^{*}-\Pi_{X}[x_{k}^{*}]\|^{2}+\left(\frac{\mu}{2}+\frac{\gamma_{k}}{m{\beta}}-L\right){\textrm{dist}}(x_{k}^{*},X)\leq\frac{\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{4\alpha_{\min}},

where

is given by (

The proof is in Appendix
C

Proposition
2
indicates that, when
\mu>0
,
by letting
\gamma_{k}\to\infty
,
we will have
\frac{\gamma_{k}}{4m{\beta}}\geq L
for all large enough

implying

\displaystyle{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k}^{*},X)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{4\alpha_{\min}\left(\frac{\mu}{2}+\frac{\gamma_{k}}{4m{\beta}}-L\right)}\approx O(\delta_{k}).

(23)

Thus, if
\delta_{k}\to 0
, the distance of
x_{k}^{*}
to the feasible set

will go to 0 asymptitically
at the rate of
O(\delta_{k})
, independent of
\gamma_{k}
.
The preceding relation holds with

is merely convex.
The convergence rate order is better than

obtained for a general
f(\cdot)

in Proposition

When
f(\cdot)
is strongly convex with
\mu>0
,
Proposition
2
shows that
\|x^{*}-x_{k}^{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\mu\alpha_{\min}}
for large enough

If additionally

, then the solutions

of the penalized problems converge to
the optimal solution
x^{*}
of the original problem, with the rate in the order of

.

The common requirement for
{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k}^{*},X)\to 0

and Proposition

is that

.
The main difference between these propositions is in the requirement for the penalty parameter
\gamma_{k}
.
Specifically,
to ensure

has to increase to

(Proposition

In contrast,

However, determining such a value of

is challenging as it is hard to obtain upper estimates for the subgradient norm bound

and the Hoffman constant

. Estimating

requires knowing a region that contains the projections of the solutions to the penalized problems on the feasible set (see (

is also a difficult problem, which has recently been addressed in
[
27

via computational approaches.

Based on Proposition
2
, one can construct a two-loop iterative approach to compute
the optimal point

of the original problem in the case of strongly convex

where the penalty values
\gamma_{k}
and
\delta_{k}
are set.
For any given

, the inner loop of iterations compute the optimal point

for the penalized problem
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F_{k}(x)
.
This naive two-loop approach is quite inefficient. Later in Section

, we propose a more efficient single-loop algorithm,
where at each iteration

adjust the parameters
\gamma_{k}
and
\delta_{k}
, and use a (stochastic) gradient of the penalty function
F_{k}(\cdot)
for the update.

4
Random Incremental Penalty Algorithm

Assuming that the function
f(\cdot)
is convex over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we consider an algorithm that takes one gradient step for minimizing
F_{k}(\cdot)
at iteration
k
,
as opposed to determining
x^{*}_{k}
for each function
F_{k}(\cdot)
.
To deal with the large number of component functions
h_{k}(\cdot;a_{i},b_{i})
involved in

F_{k}(\cdot)
, we consider a random incremental subgradient algorithm using only
one randomly chosen constraint (indexed by
i_{k}
) to estimate
a subgradient
\tilde{\nabla}F_{k}(x_{k})\in\partial F_{k}(x_{k})
at iteration
k
, when
x_{k}
is available.
This estimation is employed to construct
x_{k+1}
via
random penalty
corresponding to subgradient sampling,
as opposed to determining a full subgradient of
F_{k}(x_{k})
.

We apply random incremental update to the penalty function (
15
)
represented in the following form:

F_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(f(x)+\gamma_{k}h_{k}\left(x;a_{i},b_{i}\right)\right),

and the random incremental penalty method is:
for
k\geq 1
,

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-s_{k}[\tilde{\nabla}f(x_{k})+\gamma_{k}\nabla h_{k}(x_{k};a_{i_{k}},b_{i_{k}})],

(24)

where
s_{k}>0
is a stepsize,
\tilde{\nabla}f(x_{k})
is a subgradient of
f(\cdot)
at
x=x_{k}
,
and the index
i_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
is chosen uniformly at random at every iteration
k
.

The algorithm is initiated with a random initial point
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for which we assume that

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{1}\|^{2}}\right]<\infty
.

Note that
\tilde{\nabla}f(x_{k})+\gamma_{k}\nabla h_{k}(x_{k};a_{i_{k}},b_{i_{k}})
is an unbiased estimate of
a subgradient
\tilde{\nabla}F_{k}(x_{k})\in\partial F_{k}(x_{k})
,
since by the uniform distribution of
i_{k}
we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\tilde{\nabla}f(x_{k})+\gamma_{k}\nabla h_{k}(x;a_{i_{k}},b_{i_{k}})|\EuScript F_{k}}\right]=\tilde{\nabla}F_{k}(x_{k}),

(25)

where
\EuScript F_{k}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables

\{i_{j},\,1\leq j\leq k-1\}
and the random initial iterate
x_{1}
, which is equivalent to
the
\sigma
-algebra
\EuScript F_{k}=\sigma(\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{k})
for all
k\geq 1
.

Unlike the standard random incremental method,
the random incremental penalty method in (
24
) selects one
random component from the time-varying function
F_{k}(\cdot)
.
This makes the analysis of the method more challenging since the iterates
\{x_{k}\}
need not be feasible for the original problem,
which poses difficulties, and the penalty parameters
\gamma_{k}
and
\delta_{k}
have to be carefully tuned
to ensure the convergence of the iterates.

, where
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
continuous function and
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex set. Assume that
the solution set
Z^{*}
of the problem is nonempty. Let

\{z_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random sequence,

\{a_{k}\}
and
\{c_{k}\}
be random nonnegative scalar sequences, and
\{b_{k}\}
and
\{b^{\prime}_{k}\}
be deterministic nonnegative scalar sequences
such that
a.s.
for all
z^{*}\in Z^{*}
and for all
k\geq 1,

\mathbb{E}[\|z_{k+1}-z^{*}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq(1+a_{k})\|z_{k}-z^{*}\|^{2}-b_{k}\left(\phi(\Pi_{Z}[z_{k}])-\phi^{*}\right)+c_{k},

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b^{\prime}_{k}{\textrm{dist}}(z_{k},Z_{k})<\infty\qquad\emph{a.s.}

where
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\}
for all
k
,

\phi^{*}=\min_{x\in Z}\phi(z)
, while the scalar sequences satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}<\infty
and

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}<\infty

a.s.
, and

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b^{\prime}_{k}=\infty
.
Then, the sequence
\{z_{k}\}
converges to some (random) optimal solution
z^{*}\in X^{*}
almost surely. Moreover, if the solution set
Z^{*}
is bounded, then there exists a scalar
M_{0}
such that
\|z_{k}\|\leq M_{0}
for all
k\geq 0

a.s.
and convergence to
z^{*}
is also in expectation.

Proof
:
We note that the conditions of Lemma
7
are satisfied with
v_{k}=\|z_{k}-z^{*}\|^{2}
, for every
z^{*}\in Z^{*}
, and
u_{k}=b_{k}\left(\phi(\Pi_{Z}[z_{k}])-\phi^{*}\right)
, and by this lemma
we obtain the following statements:

\hbox{$\{\|z_{k}-z^{*}\|^{2}\}$ converges \emph{a.s.}\ for each $z^{*}\in Z^{*}$},

(38)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}\left(\phi(\Pi_{Z}[z_{k}])-\phi^{*}\right)<\infty\qquad\emph{a.s.}.

(39)

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}=\infty
, it follows from (
39
) that
a.s.
,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\phi(\Pi_{Z}[z_{k}])=\phi^{*}
.
The conditions
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b^{\prime}_{k}{\textrm{dist}}(z_{k},Z_{k})<\infty

a.s.
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b^{\prime}_{k}=\infty

imply that

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|z_{k}-\Pi_{Z}[z_{k}]\|=0.

Let
\{z_{k_{\ell}}\}
be a subsequence of
\{z_{k}\}
such that
a.s.
,

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\phi(\Pi_{Z}[z_{k_{\ell}}])=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\phi(\Pi_{Z}[z_{k}])=\phi^{*},

(40)

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\|z_{k_{\ell}}-\Pi_{Z}[z_{k_{\ell}}]\|=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|z_{k}-\Pi_{Z}[z_{k}]\|=0.

(41)

Now, relation (
38
) implies that the sequence
\{z_{k}\}
is bounded
a.s.
, so
without loss of generality, we can assume that
\{z_{k_{\ell}}\}
is converging
a.s.
to some
random point
\tilde{z}
(for otherwise, we
can in turn select
a.s.
convergent subsequence of
\{z_{k_{\ell}}\}
). Since the projection mapping
z\mapsto\Pi_{Z}[z]
is continuous, it follows that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\Pi_{Z}[z_{k_{\ell}}]=\Pi_{Z}[\tilde{z}]\qquad\emph{a.s.}

The preceding relation and relation (
41
)
imply that

0=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\|z_{k_{\ell}}-\Pi_{Z}[z_{k_{\ell}}]\|=\|\tilde{z}-\Pi_{Z}[\tilde{z}]\|\qquad\emph{a.s.}

Therefore, we have that
\tilde{z}\in Z

a.s.
Moreover, by the continuity of
\phi(\cdot)
,

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\phi(z_{k_{\ell}})=\phi(\tilde{z})\qquad\emph{a.s.},

which by relation (
40
) and the fact
\tilde{z}\in Z

a.s.
implies that
\tilde{z}\in Z^{*}

a.s.
By letting
z^{*}=\tilde{z}
in (
38
)
we obtain that
\{z_{k}\}
converges to
\tilde{z}

a.s.

When the set
Z^{*}
is bounded, the convergence point
\tilde{z}
of the iterates
\{z_{k}\}
is bounded by a (deterministic) scalar almost surely, implying that
\{\|z_{k}\|\}
is
a.s.
bounded by some deterministic scalar
M_{0}
. Finally, using Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we conclude that
\{z_{k}\}
converges to
\tilde{z}
also in expectation.

Having Lemma
6
,
Lemma
7
, and Lemma
8
in place, we show next the convergence of the method
under some conditions on the stepsize
s_{k}
and the penalty parameters
\gamma_{k},\delta_{k}
, as given in the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

Let
\gamma_{k}>0
,
\delta_{k}>0
, and
s_{k}>0
for all
k
, and assume that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\gamma_{k}=\infty
,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}=\infty
,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}<\infty
,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}<\infty
.

The intuition behind the conditions in Assumption
3
is as follows. The condition
\lim_{k\to\infty}\gamma_{k}=\infty

ensures that the penalty function pushes the iterations
into the feasible set as time runs, whereas
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}=\infty
allows the algorithm to make sufficient progress toward an optimal solution of the original problem. The last two conditions,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}<\infty
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}<\infty
, keep the perturbations caused by the penalty function under control.

In the following proposition, we establish almost sure iterates’ convergence
and their boundedness properties. For this purpose we define the sigma-algebra
\EuScript F_{k}
relevant to the random method (
24
) as follows:

\EuScript F_{k}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\qquad\hbox{for all }k\geq 1.

Proposition 3
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
3
hold. Then, the iterates of the random method (
24
) converge to a (random) optimal solution
a.s.
and in expectation. Moreover, there exists a scalar
M>0
such that

\|x_{k}\|\leq M
for all
k\geq 1

a.s.

Proof
: We use Lemma
6
, according to which we surely have for all
x^{*}\in X^{*}
, all
\rho\in[0,1]
, and all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq(1+4s_{k}^{2}M_{1}^{2})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\ \ +2s_{k}\left((1-\rho)B+\rho(M_{1}B+M_{2})\right){\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)

\displaystyle\ \ +2s_{k}\rho(f^{*}-f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}]))+2s_{k}\rho M_{1}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}-2s_{k}\gamma_{k}{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X_{i_{k}})+4s_{k}^{2}(M_{1}^{2}B^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}),\qquad

We will take the conditional expectation in the preceding relation with respect to
\EuScript F_{k}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
. In doing so, we note that, given
x_{k}
, the index

i_{k}
is uniformly distributed over
\{1,\ldots,m\}
, for all
k\geq 1
, implying that

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X_{i_{k}})\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X_{i}).

By using Hoffman’s Lemma (Lemma
2
), we obtain

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X_{i_{k}})\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{{\beta}m}\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X).

Therefore, it follows that
a.s.
for all
x^{*}\in X^{*}
, all
\rho\in[0,1]
, and all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]\leq(1+4s_{k}^{2}M_{1}^{2})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle\ \ +2s_{k}\left((1-\rho)B+\rho(M_{1}B+M_{2})\right){\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)

(43)

\displaystyle\ \ +2s_{k}\rho(f^{*}-f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}]))+2s_{k}\rho M_{1}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)

(44)

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}-\frac{2s_{k}\gamma_{k}}{{\beta}m}\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)+4s_{k}^{2}(M_{1}^{2}B^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).\qquad

(45)

The proof proceeds in two major steps both using relation (
42
): (Step 1) we show that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)<\infty

a.s.
, and (Step 2) we show the almost sure convergence of the method by means of Lemma
8
.

Step 1
:
In relation (
42
), we let
x^{*}\in X^{*}
be arbitrary but fixed, and we set

\rho=0
. In the resulting relation, we group the common coefficients with

{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)
and obtain
a.s.
for all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]\leq(1+4s_{k}^{2}M_{1}^{2})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}

\displaystyle\ \ -2s_{k}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k}}{{\beta}m}-B\right)\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)+4s_{k}^{2}(M_{1}^{2}B^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).\qquad

By Assumption
3
, we have that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\gamma_{k}=\infty
, implying that
for some sufficiently large
k_{1}\geq 1
, we have
\frac{\gamma_{k}}{m{\beta}}-B\geq B
. Therefore,
for all
k\geq k_{1}

a.s.
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]\leq(1+4s_{k}^{2}M_{1}^{2})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}

\displaystyle\ \ -2s_{k}B{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)+4s_{k}^{2}(M_{1}^{2}B^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).

Under Assumption
3
,
the preceding relation
satisfies all the conditions of Lemma
7
, starting at time

,
with
v_{k}=\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}
,

\alpha_{k}=4s_{k}^{2}M_{1}^{2}
,

, and

{\beta}_{k}=\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}+4s_{k}^{2}(M_{1}^{2}B^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2})
.
Thus, by Lemma
7
,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)<\infty,\qquad\emph{a.s.}

(46)

Step 2
:
We now use relation (
42
) with
\rho=1

and obtain that
a.s.
for all
x^{*}\in X^{*}
and all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]\leq(1+4s_{k}^{2}M_{1}^{2})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\ \ +2s_{k}(f^{*}-f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}]))+2s_{k}M_{1}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}-2s_{k}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k}}{{\beta}m}-(M_{1}B+M_{2})\right)\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)+4s_{k}^{2}(M_{1}^{2}B^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).\qquad

By Assumption

, we have that

. Thus,

for all

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+4s_{k}^{2}M_{1}^{2}+s_{k}M_{1}\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)\right)\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}

(47)

\displaystyle\ \ +2s_{k}(f^{*}-f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}]))+s_{k}M_{1}\,{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)

(48)

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}+8s_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}.\qquad

(49)

By Assumption

we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}=\infty
.
By Assumption

(see (

)),

with

,

, and

Hence, the results follow from Lemma

.

Next we provide some sufficient conditions for the parameters to satisfy Assumption
3
and formulate their properties which we will use in the forthcoming convergence rate analysis.

Lemma 9
.

Let us consider the following setting for the parameters:

\mbox{$s_{k}=O\left(\frac{1}{k^{c}\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(k+1)}\right)$, $\gamma_{k}=O\left(\ln^{g}(k+1)\right)$, $\delta_{k}=O\left(\frac{1}{k^{d}}\right)$ with $c,g,d>0$}.

Then, if
c\in[1/2,1)
and
d>1/2
, Assumption
3
holds. Moreover, given this choice of the parameters,

\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}\geq\frac{(t+1)^{1-c}-1}{(1-c)\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(t+1)}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{t}s^{2}_{k}=O(1)
,
\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}=O(1)
,
\sum_{k=1}^{t}s^{2}_{k}\gamma^{2}_{k}=O(1)
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Obviously, given the settings,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\gamma_{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\ln^{g}(k+1)=\infty
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{k^{c}\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(k+1)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(t+1)}\int_{1}^{t+1}\frac{dx}{x^{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(t+1)^{1-c}-1}{(1-c)\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(t+1)}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{k^{c}\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(k+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(2)}\left(1+\int_{1}^{t}\frac{dx}{x^{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{1-c}-c}{(1-c)\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(2)}.

Thus,
\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}=O\left(\frac{t^{1-c}}{\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(2)}\right)
, which implies also the condition
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}=\infty
. Next,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}s^{2}_{k}\gamma^{2}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{k^{2c}\ln^{1+g}(k+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\ln^{1+g}2}+\int_{1}^{t}\frac{dx}{x\ln^{1+g}(x+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\ln^{1+g}2}+\frac{g}{\ln^{g}2}.

Hence,
\sum_{k=1}^{t}s^{2}_{k}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t}s^{2}_{k}\gamma^{2}_{k}=O(1)
and the corresponding series converge.
Finally,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{k^{c+d}\ln^{(1+g)/2}(k+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\ln^{(1+g)/2}(2)}\left(1+\int_{1}^{t}\frac{dx}{x^{c+d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{1-c-d}-c-d}{(1-c-d)\ln^{(1+3g)/2}(2)}.

Thus, taking into account that
c+d>1
, we conclude that
\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}=O(1)
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}<\infty
.

4.3
Convergence Rate Results

In this section,
we establish convergence rate results for the iterates of the method (
24
), under
the following condition:
there is a scalar
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\nabla}f(x_{k})\|\leq M,\qquad\|\tilde{\nabla}f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}])\|\leq M\qquad\hbox{for all $k\geq 1$}\ \ \emph{a.s.}

This condition is satisfied under Assumptions

–
3

bounded by a constant.
As a consequence, the subgradients
\tilde{\nabla}f(x_{k})

is strongly convex and when it is just convex.

Using relation (

.

Lemma 11

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-y\|^{2}\leq(1-\mu s_{k})\|x_{k}-y\|^{2}+2s_{k}(f(y)-f(x_{k}))

\displaystyle+\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}-2s_{k}\gamma_{k}{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X_{i_{k}})+2s_{k}^{2}(M^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).\qquad

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-y\|^{2}\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]\leq(1-\mu s_{k})\|x_{k}-y\|^{2}+2s_{k}(f(y)-f(x_{k}))

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}-2s_{k}\gamma_{k}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X_{i_{k}})\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]+2s_{k}^{2}(M^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).

Given

is uniformly distributed over
\{1,\ldots,m\}

Therefore, we have
a.s.
for all
y\in X
and all
k\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-y\|^{2}\mid\EuScript F_{k}}\right]\leq(1-\mu s_{k})\|x_{k}-y\|^{2}+2s_{k}(f(y)-f(x_{k}))

(53)

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}-\frac{2s_{k}\gamma_{k}}{m{\beta}}{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)+2s_{k}^{2}(M^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).

Let
\eta\in[0,1]
and lets write

f(y)-f(x_{k})=(1-\eta)(f(y)-f(x_{k}))+\eta(f(y)-f(x_{k})).

Further, we write

\eta(f(y)-f(x_{k}))=\eta(f(y)-f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}]+f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}])-f(x_{k})).

By Lemma

(a), we have

f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}])-f(x_{k})\leq M{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)-\frac{\mu}{2}{\textrm{dist}}^{2}(x_{k},X).

Combining the preceding relations yields

\displaystyle f(y)-f(x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\eta)(f(y)-f(x_{k}))+\eta(f(y)-f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}])

\displaystyle+\eta M{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)-\frac{\eta\mu}{2}{\textrm{dist}}^{2}(x_{k},X).

By substituting the preceding estimate back in (

\displaystyle\leq(1-\mu s_{k})\|x_{k}-y\|^{2}+2s_{k}(f(y)-f(x_{k}))+\frac{s_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}}{2\alpha_{\min}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2s_{k}\gamma_{k}}{m{\beta}}{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)+2s_{k}^{2}(M^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).

By taking the total expectation and dividing with
s_{k}^{2}
, we obtain for all
y\in X
and
k\geq 1

\displaystyle s_{k}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-y\|^{2}}\right]\leq s_{k}^{-2}(1-\mu s_{k})\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k}-y\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle+2s_{k}^{-1}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{f(y)-f(x_{k})}\right]-\frac{2s_{k}^{-1}\gamma_{k}}{m{\beta}}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)}\right]+c_{k},\qquad

where

c_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}s_{k}^{-1}}{2\alpha_{\min}}+2(M^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}).

Note that for the given stepsize

,
we have for sufficiently large

s_{k}^{-2}(1-\mu s_{k})=\frac{\mu^{2}k(k-2)}{4}\leq\frac{\mu^{2}(k-1)^{2}}{4}=s_{k-1}^{2}.

Therefore, we obtain for all
y\in X
and sufficiently large

\displaystyle s_{k}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k+1}-y\|^{2}}\right]\leq s_{k-1}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{k}-y\|^{2}}\right]

(65)

\displaystyle+2s_{k}^{-1}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{f(y)-f(x_{k})}\right]-\frac{2s_{k}^{-1}\gamma_{k}}{m{\beta}}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)}\right]+c_{k}.\qquad\qquad

(66)

By summing the relations in (

and omitting the terms with
\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)}\right]
,
after re-arranging the terms,
we find that for all

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}^{-1}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{f(x_{k})}\right]-f(y)\right)+s_{t}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{t+1}-y\|^{2}}\right]\leq s_{0}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{1}-y\|^{2}}\right]+\sum_{k=1}^{t}c_{k},

where we define

.
By dividing with
2\bar{S}_{t}
, where

Next, we establish the estimate for

\displaystyle s_{k}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}^{2}(x_{k+1},X)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq s_{k-1}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}^{2}(x_{k},X)}\right]+2s_{k}^{-1}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{f(\Pi_{X}[x_{k}])-f(x_{k})}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2s_{k}^{-1}\gamma_{k}}{m{\beta}}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)}\right]+c_{k}.\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle s_{k}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}^{2}(x_{k+1},X)}\right]\leq s_{k-1}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}^{2}(x_{k},X)}\right]

\displaystyle-2s_{k}^{-1}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k}}{m{\beta}}-M\right)\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{{\textrm{dist}}(x_{k},X)}\right]

\displaystyle+c_{k}.\qquad\qquad

Proposition 5
.

Let
f(\cdot)
be strongly convex with
\mu>0
, and
let Assumption
2
hold.
Assume
s_{k}=\frac{2}{\mu k}
,
\delta_{k}=\frac{1}{k^{d}}
with
d>1
, and

\gamma_{k}=\ln^{g}k
with
g>0
for all
k
.
Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the iterate sequence generated by method (
24
).
Then, the following estimate is valid for the
s^{-1}
-weighted averages
{\bar{x}_{t}^{\textrm{a}v}}
,
t\to\infty

|\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{f({\bar{x}_{t}^{\textrm{a}v}})}\right]-f^{*}|=O\left(\frac{\ln^{2g}t}{t}\right).

Proof.

Given the choice

,

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\mu k}{2}=\frac{\mu}{2}\frac{(1+t)t}{2}.

(68)

Next, taking into account that
\gamma_{k}=\ln^{g}k
and
\delta_{k}=\frac{1}{k^{d}}
,

, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}s^{-1}_{k}\gamma_{k}\delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\mu\ln^{g}k}{2k^{d-1}}

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu\ln^{g}t}{2}[\int_{1}^{t}\frac{dx}{x^{d-1}}+1]=\begin{cases}\frac{\mu\ln^{g}t}{2}\left(\frac{t^{2-d}-1}{2-d}+1\right),\,\mbox{if $d\neq 2$},\\
\frac{\mu\ln^{g}t}{2}\left(\ln t+1\right),\,\mbox{if $d=2$}.\end{cases}

(70)

By combining the inequalities in (
68
) and (
69
) with
the estimate for the function values
from
Lemma
13
, namely,

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{f({\bar{x}_{t}^{\textrm{a}v}})}\right]-f^{*}\leq\frac{s_{0}^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{\|x_{1}-x^{*}\|^{2}}\right]}{2\bar{S}_{t}}+\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{t}c_{k}}{2\bar{S}_{t}},

where

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\left[{f({\bar{x}_{t}^{\textrm{a}v}})}\right]-f^{*}\leq\frac{D_{1}\ln^{2g}t}{t}.

Note that

depends on

of the subgradients defined in relation (
50
), the value

To determine a lower bound for

Remark 4
.

As seen in Propositions

and
5

6
Conclusion

This work deals with penalty reformulation of the optimization problems subjected to linear constraints, where the penalty is a variant of the one-sided Huber loss function. The infeasibility properties of the solutions of penalized problems for nonconvex and convex objective functions under time-varying penalty parameters are analyzed.
A random incremental penalty method for solving convex problem is proposed. This method is proven to converge to a solution of the original problem almost surely and in expectation for suitable choices of the penalty parameters and the stepsize. Moreover,
O(\ln^{1/2+\epsilon}k/{\sqrt{k}})
-convergence rate when the objective function is convex, and
O(\ln^{\epsilon}k/k)
-convergence rate when the objective function is strongly convex are established, given any positive
\epsilon
.

Some interesting questions for the future work include applicability of
accelerated incremental algorithms for the proposed penalty
reformulation in the case of both strongly and non-strongly
convex optimization as well as extension of the presented penalty approach to other structures of constraints.

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

X_{k}^{*}

F_{k}(\cdot)
for all

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid F_{k}(x)\leq t_{k}(\hat{x})\}\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)\leq t_{k}(\hat{x})\},
