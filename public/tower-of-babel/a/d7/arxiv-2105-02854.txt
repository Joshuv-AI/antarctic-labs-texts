Fine-scale distribution of roots of quadratic congruences Research supported by EPSRC grant EP/S024948/1
Jens Marklof, Matthew Welsh

Abstract
We establish limit laws for the distribution in small intervals of the roots of the quadratic congruence μ 2 ≡ D mod m \mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m , with D > 0 D>0 square-free and D ≢ 1 mod 4 D\not\equiv 1\bmod 4 . This is achieved by translating the problem to convergence of certain geodesic random line processes in the hyperbolic plane. This geometric interpretation allows us in particular to derive an explicit expression for the pair correlation density of the roots.



1
Introduction

The object of this paper is the fine-scale distribution of solutions (the “roots”)
\mu
of the quadratic congruence
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
, where
D
is a fixed non-zero integer and
m
runs through the positive integers. For each
m
, we consider the set of normalised roots in
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
,

Q_{D}(m)=\bigg\{\frac{\mu}{m}\in{\mathbb{T}}:\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m\bigg\}.

(1.1)

We order the points in
Q_{D}(m)
in a specified (but arbitrary) finite sequence, and then construct an infinite sequence
(\xi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathbb{T}}
by first listing the points in
Q_{D}(1)
, then those in
Q_{D}(2)
, and so on. The first significant result in this setting is due to Hooley
[
14
]
, who proved uniform distribution modulo one if
D
is not a perfect square. That is, for any
0\leq a<b\leq 1
we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\big\{j\leq N:\xi_{j}\in[a,b)+{\mathbb{Z}}\big\}=b-a.

(1.2)

Hooley later extended this result to general polynomial congruences of degree two or higher
[
13
]
.
His method has since been generalised and extended, see
[
18
]
and
[
31
]
.

Having established uniform distribution, it is natural to ask for finer measures that capture the pseudo-random properties of the sequence. The simplest of these is the pair (or two-point) correlation measure
R_{2,N}
, which for a finite interval
I
is defined by

R_{2,N}(I)=\frac{1}{N}\#\big\{i,j\leq N:i\neq j,\;\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}\in N^{-1}I+{\mathbb{Z}}\big\}.

(1.3)

Our first principal result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
D>0
is square-free and
D\not\equiv 1\bmod 4
. Then there is an even and continuous function
w_{D}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, such that
for every finite interval
I
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{2,N}(I)=\int_{I}w_{D}(v)\differential v.

(1.4)

In other words, the pair correlation measure
R_{2,N}
converges vaguely to a limit with bounded continuous density
w_{D}
. Our geometric approach will allow us to derive an explicit formula for
w_{D}
; three illustrations are given in figures
1
–
3
. Compare this with the case of independent and uniformly distributed random variables in
{\mathbb{T}}
where the limit density is
w(v)=1
; or with the eigenphases of large random unitary matrices where the limiting pair correlation is
w(v)=1-(\sin(\pi v)/\pi v)^{2}
.

The conditions that
D
is squarefree and
D\not\equiv 1\bmod 4
in Theorem
1.1
ensure that the associated quadratic order
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is maximal, which will provide some simplifications in the proofs.

Figure 1:
Pair correlation density
w_{2}
compared to experiments with
N=10^{6}
. The ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]
has both class and narrow class number
1
.

Figure 2:
Pair correlation density
w_{3}
compared to experiments with
N=10^{6}
. The ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
has class number
1
and narrow class number
2
.

Figure 3:
Pair correlation density
w_{10}
compared to experiments with
N=10^{6}
. The ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{10}]
has both class and narrow class number
2
.

Our techniques are in fact strong enough to produce results for all higher-order correlation measures. Let
\xi
be a random variable in
{\mathbb{T}}
which is distributed according to a Borel probability measure
\lambda
, and consider the random counting measure (random point process)

\Xi_{N,\lambda}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\delta_{N(\xi_{j}-\xi+k)}

(1.5)

on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Here
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac mass at
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. For example, for a given interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
and integer
k
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}(\Xi_{N,\lambda}(I)=k)=\lambda(\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I}(x,N)=k\})

(1.6)

with

{\mathcal{N}}_{I}(x,N)=\#\{j\leq N:\xi_{j}\in x+N^{-1}I+{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

(1.7)

The scaling of the interval by
N^{-1}
ensures that we expect a finite number of points for typical
x
. The following theorem describes the precise distribution.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume
D>0
is square-free and
D\not\equiv 1\bmod 4
. Then there exists a random point process
\Xi
depending only on
D
so that, for every Borel probability measure
\lambda
on
{\mathbb{T}}
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we have convergence

\Xi_{N,\lambda}\to\Xi
in distribution as
N\to\infty
.

Specifically, for all
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
and finite intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{r}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\big(\big\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{i}}(x,N)=k_{i}\;\forall i\big\}\big)={\mathbb{P}}\big(\Xi(I_{i})=k_{i}\;\forall i\big)

(1.8)

and the limit is a continuous function of the endpoints of
I_{i}
.

We furthermore establish the uniform upper bound
{\mathcal{N}}_{I}(x,N)\ll\log N
, where the applied constant is independent of
x\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
N
. This generalises Fouvry and Iwaniec’s bound
[
9
]
in the case
D=-1
.

We provide an explicit description of the limit process
\Xi
in terms of tops of random geodesics in the hyperbolic plane, as well as an alternative description as entry times for a certain Poincaré section of the horocycle flow. A striking feature is that
\Xi
is independent of the choice of
\lambda
. Theorem
1.2
implies the convergence of various popular fine-scale statistics of the sequence
(\xi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
, including the gap distribution. Furthermore, we will prove the convergence of moments and higher correlation densities.

The above theorems in fact remain true (with different limit distributions) if we further restrict the roots to
m\equiv 0\bmod n
, and
\mu\equiv\nu\bmod n
, for fixed
n>0
and
\nu\bmod n
such that
\nu^{2}\equiv D\bmod n
.

The results of this paper also hold for negative
D
. Here the proofs reduce to existing results
[
23
,
26
]
on the fine-scale distribution of the real parts of hyperbolic lattice points, rather than the tops of geodesics. The studies of real parts of hyperbolic lattice points are closely related to the distribution of angles in hyperbolic lattices
[
3
,
4
,
17
,
23
,
27
]
.
We also remark that results on counting the tops of geodesics can be found in
[
24
,
25
]
as well as applications of a similar flavor.
Their methods also apply in a general setting of negative curvature, where the notion of ‘top’ is naturally replaced by that of a common perpendicular between a fixed horosphere and geodesic.

1.1
Previous work on roots

In this section we review previous techniques used to study the sequence of roots
\frac{\mu}{m}
and the results produced.
As previously mentioned, Hooley
[
14
]
proved the equidistribution of this sequence.
In fact, he produced a power-saving bound on the Weyl sums

\sum_{m\leq M}\sum_{\mu^{2}\equiv D(m)}e\left(\frac{h\mu}{m}\right),\qquad e(x):={\mathrm{e}}^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}x},

(1.9)

for non-zero integer
h
.
These results are obtained by transforming (
1.9
) into a sum of Kloosterman sums, which is then estimated by an application of the Weil bound.

In
[
15
]
, Iwaniec follows a similar strategy to bound the Weyl sum (
1.9
) with
D=-1
but with various additional arithmetic constraints on
m
and
\mu
, most notably the restriction to
m\equiv 0\bmod n
.
Iwaniec obtains enough uniformity in
n
so that a sieve can be applied, resulting in a proof that
l^{2}+1
is infinitely often a product of at most two primes.

In light of the appearance of a sum of Kloosterman sums in these works,
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
, one could in retrospect already predict the relevance of the spectral theory of
\mathrm{SL}(2)
automorphic forms.
However, the first application to analysing the sequence of
\frac{\mu}{m}
, found in the work of Bykovskiĭ,
[
5
]
, proceeds by a more direct method and only applies to negative
D
.
Roughly speaking, Bykovskiĭ’s method exploits the classical connection between the roots
\mu\mod m
and the
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
orbits of Heegner points in the Poincaré upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
.
For example, the images of
{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathbb{H}
under
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
have the form

\gamma{\mathrm{i}}=\frac{\mu}{m}+\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{m},

(1.10)

where
\mu
satisfies
\mu^{2}\equiv-1\bmod{m}
(see section
5.3
for more details).
In this way, Bykovskiĭ turns the Weyl sum (
1.9
) into a finite sum of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-Poincaré series, which, after a spectral expansion, can be controlled using estimates for sums of Fourier coefficients of automorphic forms. These spectral techniques were extended substantially by Good
[
11
]
, and similar problems were considered in a very general context by Parkkonen and Paulin
[
24
]
,
[
25
]
, although no reference to roots of quadratic congruences are made.

In
[
6
]
, Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec extend Bykovskiĭ’s approach to apply to the Weyl sums (
1.9
) restricted to
m\equiv 0\bmod n
, analogously to Iwaniec’s earlier extension of Hooley’s work.
Here Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec obtain enough uniformity in
h
and
n
to apply a sieve method and conclude the spectacular result that the sequence
\frac{\mu}{m}
with
m
restricted to be prime is still equidistributed modulo one.

The first use of automorphic forms to understand the sequence of
\frac{\mu}{m}
in the case with
D>0
came in the work of Hejhal,
[
12
]
.
Hejhal approximates the Weyl sum (
1.9
) by an integral of a fixed automorphic form (related to the automorphic Green’s function) over some closed geodesics in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}
.
The results obtained in
[
12
]
only apply for select
D
and are not as strong as those previously mentioned, perhaps due to a lack of a clear geometric interpretation of the roots.
Nevertheless, in
[
30
]
Toth manages to extend the results of Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec,
[
6
]
to the positive
D
setting.
Here the geometric question is avoided by returning to the original methods of Hooley and Iwaniec, which transform the Weyl sum directly into a sum of Kloosterman sums.

It is also worth mentioning the approach to this equidistribution question outlined by Sarnak in
[
28
]
.
Sarnak suggests relating the Weyl sum (
1.9
) to the Fourier coefficients of certain half integral weight automorphic forms by means of Salie sums.
As shown by Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec in
[
7
]
, this kind of method is capable of producing savings in the discriminant of the polynomial
F
itself.
None of the previously mentioned methods, nor the new method we develop below, seem to be capable of this.

Other than equidistribution, the only statistics about the sequence
\frac{\mu}{m}
that appear in the literature, as far as we are aware, are upper bounds for the number that can lie in very short intervals.
For
D=-1
, Fouvry and Iwaniec
[
9
]
found that the number of
\frac{\mu}{m}
with
m\leq M
in an interval of length
\frac{1}{M}
is
O(\log M)
. Our approach reproduces this bound for general
D
, cf. lemma
2.8
.
In
[
9
]
and in Friedlander and Iwaniec’s breakthrough
[
10
]
, this bound was a key input into a sieve method for producing primes of the form
l^{2}+p^{2}
and
l^{2}+k^{4}
, respectively.

1.2
Outline of paper

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete subgroup of the group of isometries
G
acting on the complex upper half-plane
{\mathbb{H}}
, and let
\bm{c}_{1},\ldots,\bm{c}_{h}
be a finite collection of geodesics in
{\mathbb{H}}
that project to closed geodesics on the hyperbolic surface
\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}}
. In
section
2
we study the distribution of the geodesic lines
g^{-1}\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
where
\gamma
runs through
\Gamma
and
l=1,\ldots,h
. This becomes a geodesic random line process if
g
is taken to be a random isometry with respect to a suitable probability measure. In
section
2.1
we prove limit theorems for sequences of random geodesics that are randomly translated, followed by the proof of convergence of moments in
section
2.2
. Key ingredients of the proofs are the equidistribution of long horocycles, a Siegel-style mean value formula and no-escape-of-mass estimates to control the moments. A convenient parametrisation of a geodesic is given by its “top”, i.e., the point on the geodesics with largest imaginary part. In
section
2.3
we project the tops of the random geodesics to the real line, which produces one-dimensional point processes. The convergence of these processes to a limit will eventually lead to the proof of theorem
1.1
for the roots of quadratic congruences.

The objective of
section
3
is to prepare the ground for geodesic line process conditioned so that at least one geodesic has a top in the imaginary axis. To this end, we introduce in
section
3.1
a Poincaré section of the horocycle flow, and prove various equidistribution results for the intersection points of horocycles with the section, with an interesting variant in
section
3.2
that considers the intersection points reduced to a closed geodesic.

These equidistribution results are then applied in
section
4
to establish limit theorems for the conditioned geodesic line processes. In analogy with section
2
we prove convergence in distribution in
section
4.1
, work out out a highly non-trivial Siegel-type volume formula in
section
4.2
, and prove convergence of moments in
section
4.3
. Projecting the tops to the real line as in section
2.3
yields a point process
\Xi^{0}
which is distributed according the Palm distribution of
\Xi
. The corresponding limit theorems are stated in
section
4.4
. We give an alternative description of the processes
\Xi
and
\Xi^{0}
in terms of the entry and return times for the horocycle flow in
section
4.5
.

The final
section
5
of this paper expresses the roots
\mu
of quadratic congruences in terms of tops of geodesics and hence makes the connection with the distribution results established thus far. This is detailed in
section
5.1
for the setting described in the introduction, where the relevant discrete subgroup
\Gamma
is the modular group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. In
section
5.2
we extend the setting to allow additional restrictions
\mu\equiv\nu\bmod n
, which leads us to the congruence subgroups
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(n)
. In
section
5.3
we indicate how our results can be modified in the case of negative
D
, leading to random hyperbolic lattices, rather than geodesic line processes.

2
Geodesic line processes

We recall that
G=\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
acts on the complex upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}=\{x+{\mathrm{i}}y:y>0\}
by fractional linear transformations and that
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})=G/\{\pm I\}
is identified with the unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{H}
via

g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\left(\frac{a{\mathrm{i}}+b}{c{\mathrm{i}}+d},\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{(c{\mathrm{i}}+d)^{2}}\right),

(2.1)

where we have written the unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{H}
as
(z,w)
with
z\in\mathbb{H}
and
w\in\mathbb{C}
,
|w|=\mathrm{Im}(z)
.
This identification is nicely expressed in terms of the Iwasawa decomposition of
G
: if

g=\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&y^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix},

(2.2)

then
\pm g
is identified with
(x+{\mathrm{i}}y,{\mathrm{i}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\theta}y)
.
We note that multiplying
g
by
-I
corresponds to changing
\theta
in (
2.2
) to
\theta+2\pi
, and so does not change the point
(x+{\mathrm{i}}y,{\mathrm{i}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\theta}y)
.
In what follows we work with
G
directly rather than
G/\{\pm I\}
, and we only emphasise the difference when there is a chance of confusion.

Given
g\in G
, we set

\bm{c}=\bm{c}(g)=\left\{g\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}{\mathrm{i}}:t>0\right\}\subset\mathbb{H},

(2.3)

which defines, via the identification of
G/\{\pm I\}
with the unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{H}
, an oriented geodesic in
\mathbb{H}
.
Every geodesic has two limit points on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.
We say that
\bm{c}
has positive orientation if both endpoints are in
\mathbb{R}
and the endpoint corresponding to
t\to 0
is less than the endpoint corresponding to
t\to\infty
. This means that
\bm{c}
is a semicircle traced from left to right. (Of course we could also assign an orientation to geodesics with one endpoint at
\infty
, but it is convenient not to include them in this definition.) For a positively oriented geodesic
\bm{c}
we define
z_{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{H}
, the top of the geodesic
\bm{c}
, to be the point in
\mathbb{H}
on
\bm{c}
with largest imaginary part.
That is, the point on
\bm{c}
closest (with respect to the hyperbolic metric) to a horocyclic neighborhood of the boundary point
\infty\in\partial\mathbb{H}
.
We see that for every
z\in\mathbb{H}
there is a unique positively oriented geodesic
\bm{c}
such that
z=z_{\bm{c}}
. (Note that this would fail for geodesics with endpoint
\infty
). The aim is now study an infinite set of geodesics in
\mathbb{H}
generated by a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
.

Let
\Gamma
be a lattice in
G
which is not co-compact, and we assume (without loss of generality) that
\Gamma
is scaled so that
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
has a cusp of width one at
\infty
, and also that
-I\in\Gamma
.
This means that

\Gamma_{\infty}=\Gamma\cap\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:x\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

(2.4)

is a subgroup of
\Gamma
; it is the stabiliser in
\Gamma
of
\infty\in\partial\mathbb{H}
.

In our applications to roots of quadratic congruences, we will work with the modular group
\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and, more generally, the congruence subgroups

\Gamma_{0}(n)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}):c\equiv 0\bmod n\right\}.

(2.5)

Consider the finite collection of positively oriented geodesics
\bm{c}_{1}=\bm{c}(g_{1}),\ldots,\bm{c}_{h}=\bm{c}(g_{h})
with
g_{1},\dots,g_{h}\in G
so that no two
\bm{c}_{l}
are
\Gamma
-equivalent (i.e.
\Gamma\bm{c}_{l^{\prime}}\neq\Gamma\bm{c}_{l}
for
l^{\prime}\neq l
) and that each
\bm{c}_{l}
projects to a closed geodesic in
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
.
This last assumption implies that the stabiliser
\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}
of
\bm{c}_{l}
in
\Gamma
,

\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}=\Gamma\cap\left\{\pm g_{l}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}g_{l}^{-1}:t>0\right\},

(2.6)

is conjugate to the infinite (projectively) cyclic group

g_{l}^{-1}\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}g_{l}=\left\langle\pm\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{l}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{l}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle

(2.7)

for suitable
\varepsilon_{l}>1
.

Let us now turn to the distribution of the infinite collection of geodesics

\bigcup_{l=1}^{h}\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}}\gamma\bm{c}_{l}

(2.8)

by studying their tops,

{\mathcal{S}}=\bigcup_{l=1}^{h}\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}}z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}.

(2.9)

Note that in general
gz_{\bm{c}_{l}}\neq z_{g\bm{c}_{l}}
. However we have
\gamma z_{\bm{c}_{l}}=z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}
for
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}
. Thus for such
\gamma
we have
\gamma{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}
, i.e.
{\mathcal{S}}
is invariant under translations by 1, and we can therefore reduce the problem to
{\mathcal{S}}
modulo one, with
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}
.
Specifically, we are interested in the fine-scale statistics of the real parts of geodesic tops with imaginary part in the interval
[\alpha y,\beta y)
,

X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)=\biguplus_{l=1}^{h}X_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}(y)

(2.10)

with

X_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}(y)=\Big\{\real(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\bmod 1:\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}},\;\alpha y\leq\imaginary(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})<\beta y\Big\}\subset{\mathbb{T}},

(2.11)

in the limit
y\to 0
. Here
X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
,
X_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}(y)
are defined as multisets, i.e., we list points with their multiplicity. To quantify their distribution, define the number of points in small intervals by

{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=\#\Big(X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\cap(x+yI+{\mathbb{Z}})\Big),

(2.12)

where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is a fixed finite interval, and
1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty
. With

B_{I,\alpha,\beta}=\{u+\mathrm{i}v\in\mathbb{H}:u\in I,\alpha\leq v<\beta\},

(2.13)

we have

{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\Big\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}:z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}\in x+yB_{I,\alpha,\beta}+{\mathbb{Z}}\Big\}.

(2.14)

In fact

\begin{split}{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)&=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\Big\{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}:z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}\in x+yB_{I,\alpha,\beta}\Big\}\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\Big\{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}:z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}\in n(x)a(y)B_{I,\alpha,\beta}\Big\}\end{split}

with

n(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad a(y)=\begin{pmatrix}y^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&y^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.15)

Define for
g\in G
and bounded
B\subset{\mathbb{H}}
the counting function

{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}:z_{g^{-1}\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}\in B\}.

(2.16)

Using the fact that
gz_{\bm{c}}=z_{g\bm{c}}
for
g=n(x)a(y)
we see that

{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)={\mathcal{N}}_{B}(n(x)a(y)).

(2.17)

where
B=B_{I,\alpha,\beta}
is given by (
2.13
). We furthermore note that
{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(\gamma g)={\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
This motivates the definition of the following random line processes in
{\mathbb{H}}
.

Define

\Theta_{y,\lambda}=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}}\delta_{z_{(n(\xi)a(y))^{-1}\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}}

(2.18)

with the random variable
\xi
distributed according to the Borel probability measure
\lambda
on
{\mathbb{T}}
.

We also define

\Theta=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}}\delta_{z_{g^{-1}\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}}

(2.19)

with the random element
g
distributed with respect to Haar probability measure
\mu_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma\backslash G
(
\mu_{\Gamma}
is the Haar measure of
G
normalised so that
\mu_{\Gamma}(\Gamma\backslash G)=1
).
Since
\Theta_{y,\lambda}
and
\Theta
describe the distribution of random geodesics in
{\mathbb{H}}
, we refer to them as
geodesic line processes
. Of course, in the present parametrisation they can also be viewed as point processes describing the location of the geodesic tops in
{\mathbb{H}}
.

2.1
Convergence of geodesic line processes

We will now investigate the convergence of our random line processes, first in distribution, then (in the next section) the convergence of its moments.

Theorem 2.1
.

For every Borel probability measure
\lambda
on
{\mathbb{T}}
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we have convergence

\Theta_{y,\lambda}\to\Theta
in distribution as
y\to 0
.

In particular, for all
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, finite intervals
I_{i}
and
1\leq\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}\leq\infty
, we have that

\lim_{y\to 0}\lambda\big(\big\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}}(x,y)=k_{i}\;\forall i\big\}\big)={\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta(B_{I_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}})=k_{i}\;\forall i\big)

(2.20)

and the limit is a continuous function of
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
and the endpoints of
I_{i}
.

As an example of theorem
2.1
, let us consider the special case of the void distribution, where
r=1
and
k_{1}=0
. Here, for
1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty
we have that for any finite interval
I

\lim_{y\to 0}\lambda\big(\big\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=0\big\}\big)={\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta(B_{I,\alpha,\beta})=0\big),

(2.21)

with the formula

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta(B)=0\big)=\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\bigg(\prod_{l=1}^{h}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\chi_{B}(z_{g^{-1}\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\Big)\bigg)\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g),

(2.22)

where
\chi_{B}
denotes the characteristic function of the set
B=B_{I,\alpha,\beta}
.

Our proof of theorem
2.1
follows similar lines as the arguments in section 4 of
[
23
]
.
The main difference here is that the points
z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}
do not form a
\Gamma
-orbit in
\mathbb{H}
.
Both here and in
[
23
]
the main ingredient for the proof is the equidistribution of long horocycles.
The precise statement we use here can be found in theorem 5.6 of
[
22
]
, which is based on ideas going back to
[
20
]
and
[
8
]
.

We first need to compute the average of the functions
{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)
, which gives the intensity measure
{\mathbb{E}}\Theta
of the process
\Theta
. The analogue of this calculation in
[
23
]
is just a straightforward application of the unfolding technique, but here it is more involved due to the fact that the
z_{\gamma\bm{c}}
are not an orbit of a point.
This obstacle is overcome by an appropriate change of variables from the Iwasawa coordinates (
2.2
) which has a notably simple Jacobian as seen in the proof below.
Set

\kappa_{\Gamma}=\frac{\ell}{2\pi\,\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})},\qquad\ell=2\sum_{l=1}^{h}\log\varepsilon_{l},

(2.23)

where
2\log\varepsilon_{l}
and
\ell
represent the individual and total lengths of the geodesics
\bm{c}_{1},\ldots,\bm{c}_{h}
in
\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any Borel set
B\subset{\mathbb{H}}
,

{\mathbb{E}}\Theta(B)=\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g)=\kappa_{\Gamma}\,\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}(B).

(2.24)

Proof.

We have

{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}}\chi_{S_{B}}(g^{-1}\gamma g_{l}),

(2.25)

where
\chi_{S_{B}}
is the characteristic function of the set

S_{B}=\{g\in G:z_{g\bm{c}_{0}}\in B\}

(2.26)

with

\bm{c}_{0}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}{\mathrm{i}}:t>0\right\}

(2.27)

defining an upward oriented geodesic.
Unfolding shows that

\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g)=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\int_{\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}\backslash G}\chi_{S_{B}}(g^{-1}g_{l})\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g).

(2.28)

Changing variables
g\leftarrow g_{l}g
, the region of integration changes from
\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}\backslash G
to

(g_{l}^{-1}\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}g_{l})\backslash G=\left\langle\pm\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{l}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{l}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\backslash G,

(2.29)

and so the integral becomes

\sum_{l=1}^{h}\int_{\mathcal{F}_{l}}\chi_{S_{B}}(g^{-1})\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g),

(2.30)

where
\mathcal{F}_{l}
is a fundamental domain for the action of
\left\langle\pm\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{l}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{l}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle
on
G
.

To compute this integral, we introduce a coordinate chart on
G
in which both
S_{B}
and a fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}_{l}
are easy to describe.
The region of
G
where this chart applies is the open set of
g
such that
g^{-1}\bm{c}_{0}
is positively oriented.
Clearly
S_{B}
is a subset of this region, as we only define
z_{\bm{c}}
for positively oriented geodesics, and in what follows we only consider the subset of
\mathcal{F}_{l}
in this region.

The choice of coordinates we use is explicitly

g^{-1}=n(u)a(v)k(\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(s)^{-1},

(2.31)

where

k(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}

(2.32)

and
u,v,s\in\mathbb{R}
with
v,s>0
.
Since
k(\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(s)^{-1}\bm{c}_{0}
is the positively oriented geodesic with top
i
, we see that in these coordinates
z_{g^{-1}\bm{c}_{0}}=u+\mathrm{i}v
.
Moreover, we observe that in this coordinate chart the action of
\pm\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{l}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{l}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
is simply to multiply
s
by
\varepsilon_{l}^{2}
,
and so

\mathcal{F}_{l}=\{a(s)k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(v^{-1})n(u)^{-1}:1\leq s<\varepsilon_{l}^{2},v>0,u\in\mathbb{R}\}

(2.33)

is a valid region for use in the integration (
2.30
).

We now express the Haar measure on
G
with respect to the coordinate chart (
2.31
).
To this end, we write the matrix on the right in (
2.31
) in Iwasawa coordinates.
We have

g^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2sv}}(u+v)&\frac{\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{2v}}(u-v)\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2sv}}&\frac{\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{2v}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.34)

and so we find that the Iwasawa coordinates (
2.2
) of
g^{-1}
are

\displaystyle\ignorespaces\ignorespaces x

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2sv}(u+v)+\frac{s}{2v}(u-v)\right)\left(\frac{1}{2sv}+\frac{s}{2sv}\right)^{-1}=u-\frac{s^{2}-1}{s^{2}+1}v

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2sv}+\frac{s}{2v}\right)^{-1}=\frac{2sv}{s^{2}+1}

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=2\arctan(s^{-1}).

We note that the Jacobian matrix of this change of coordinates,

J=\begin{pmatrix}\partialderivative{x}{u}&\partialderivative{x}{v}&\partialderivative{x}{s}\\
\partialderivative{y}{u}&\partialderivative{y}{v}&\partialderivative{y}{s}\\
\partialderivative{\theta}{u}&\partialderivative{\theta}{v}&\partialderivative{\theta}{s}\end{pmatrix},

(2.35)

is upper triangular, and so we only need to compute the diagonal entries.
We have

\partialderivative{x}{u}=1,\quad\partialderivative{y}{v}=\frac{2s}{s^{2}+1},\quad\partialderivative{\theta}{s}=-\frac{2}{s^{2}+1},

(2.36)

and so

\differential x\frac{\differential y}{y^{2}}\differential\theta=\differential u\frac{\differential v}{v^{2}}\frac{\differential s}{s}.

(2.37)

Including the normalizing factor that makes
\mu_{\Gamma}
a probability measure on
\Gamma\backslash G
, we have that the integral (
2.30
) is

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\int_{1}^{\varepsilon_{l}^{2}}\underset{u+\mathrm{i}v\in B}{\int\int}\differential u\frac{\differential v}{v^{2}}\frac{\differential s}{s},

(2.38)

and (
2.24
) follows.
∎

Alhtough not strictly necessary for our purposes, we note that we can extend proposition
2.2
to the following mean value formula.

Proposition 2.3
.

For every measurable
f:{\mathbb{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
(or
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{H}},\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}})
),

\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{h}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}}f(z_{g^{-1}\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\,\bigg)\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g)=\kappa_{\Gamma}\int_{\mathbb{H}}f(z)\differential\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}(z).

(2.39)

Proof.

As
\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}
is a Borel measure, we may reduce to the case when
f
is the characteristic function of a Borel set, which is exactly the content of proposition
2.2
.
∎

Following the technique in
[
23
]
, we derive from proposition
2.2
the following estimates.

Lemma 2.4
.

We have

\mu_{\Gamma}\left(\{g\in\Gamma\backslash G:{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)\geq 1\}\right)\leq\kappa_{\Gamma}\,\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}(B).

(2.40)

In particular, if
\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}(\partial B)=0
, then

\mu_{\Gamma}\left(\partial\{g\in\Gamma\backslash G:{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)=k\}\right)=0.

(2.41)

Proof.

Markov’s inequality implies

\mu_{\Gamma}\left(\{g\in\Gamma\backslash G:{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)\geq 1\}\right)\leq\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g),

(2.42)

and so proposition
2.2
implies (
2.40
).
We now observe that
g^{\prime}\in\partial\{g\in\Gamma\backslash G:{\mathcal{N}}_{B}(g)=k\}
implies
{\mathcal{N}}_{\partial B}(g^{\prime})\geq 1
, and so (
2.41
) follows from (
2.40
).
∎

Proof of theorem
2.1
.

We now have all the ingredients to apply the equidistribution of long horocycles, theorem 5.6 of
[
22
]
.
This theorem states in particular that, for
\lambda
absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, if
S\subset\Gamma\backslash G
has boundary of
\mu_{\Gamma}
-measure
0
, then

\lim_{y\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\chi_{S}(n(x)a(y))\differential\lambda(x)=\mu_{\Gamma}(S),

(2.43)

where
\chi_{S}
is the indicator function of
S
.
Now (
2.41
) shows that the relevant set
S
for our theorem
2.1
does indeed have boundary of
\mu_{\Gamma}
-measure zero. This furthermore implies that the limit distribution is continuous as stated.
∎

2.2
Moments

We now turn to the moments of the counting functions
{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)
.
For
y>0
,
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
,
\mathcal{I}=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{r}
,
I_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}
a finite interval, and
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
,
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r})
satisfying
1\leq\alpha_{j}<\beta_{j}\leq\infty
,
define the probabilities (cf. theorem
2.1
)

P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}^{\lambda}(\bm{k},y)=\lambda(\{x\in\mathbb{T}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}}(x,y)=k_{j}\;\forall j\}),

(2.44)

P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k})={\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta(B_{I_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}})=k_{i}\;\forall i\big),

(2.45)

where
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
with
k_{j}
non-negative integers;
the moments

M_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha}}^{\lambda}(\bm{\kappa},y)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\prod_{1\leq j\leq r}{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}}(x,y)^{\kappa_{j}}\differential\lambda(x)=\underset{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0}{\sum}k_{1}^{\kappa_{1}}\cdots k_{r}^{\kappa_{r}}P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}^{\lambda}(\bm{k},y),

(2.46)

and

M_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\kappa})=\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\prod_{1\leq j\leq r}{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}}(g)^{\kappa_{j}}\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g)=\underset{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0}{\sum}k_{1}^{\kappa_{1}}\cdots k_{r}^{\kappa_{r}}P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k}),;

(2.47)

where
\bm{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with
\kappa_{j}\geq 0
;
and the moment generating functions

\begin{split}G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}^{\lambda}(\bm{s},y)&=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\exp( \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} s_j \scrN_{I_j, \alpha_j, \beta_j}(x, y))\differential\lambda(x)\\
&=\underset{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0}{\sum}\exp( \sum_{1\leq j\leq r} k_j s_j )P^{\lambda}_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k},y),\end{split}

and

\begin{split}G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s})&=\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\exp( \sum_{1\leq j\leq r} s_j \scrN_{I_j, \alpha_j, \beta_j}(g))\differential\mu_{\Gamma}(g)\\
&=\underset{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0}{\sum}\exp( \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} k_j s_j)P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k}).\end{split}

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\lambda
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{T}
with bounded density (with respect to Lebesgue measure), and
\mathcal{I}
,
\bm{\alpha}
,
\bm{\beta}
as above.
Then there is a constant
c_{0}>0
such that for
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
satisfying
\sum_{j}\max\{\mathrm{Re}(s_{j}),0\}<c_{0}
the function
G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s})
is analytic and we have

\lim_{y\to 0}G^{\lambda}_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s},y)=G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s}).

(2.48)

By a standard argument, theorem
2.5
implies the convergence as
y\to 0
of the mixed moments
M^{\lambda}_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\kappa},y)
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\lambda
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{T}
with bounded density, and
\mathcal{I}
,
\bm{\alpha}
,
\bm{\beta}
as above.
Then for all
\bm{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with
\kappa_{j}\geq 0
, we have
M_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\kappa})
is finite and

\lim_{y\to\infty}M^{\lambda}_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\kappa},y)=M_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\kappa}).

(2.49)

We also record the following corollary, which is simply an application of corollary
2.6
to
r=1
and
\kappa=1
together with the calculation in proposition
2.2
.

For
0\leq a<b\leq 1
and
y>0
, define the counting function for the distribution of the real parts of
z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}
modulo one,

N_{\alpha,\beta}(a,b,y)=\#\Big(X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\cap([a,b]+{\mathbb{Z}})\Big).

(2.50)

Corollary 2.7

(Equidistribution modulo one)
.

For a finite interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
,
1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty
and probability measure
\lambda
with bounded density, we have

\lim_{y\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)\differential\lambda(x)=\kappa_{\Gamma}\bigg(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta}\bigg)\mathrm{length}(I)

(2.51)

and, for
0\leq a<b\leq 1
,

\lim_{y\to 0}yN_{\alpha,\beta}(a,b,y)=\kappa_{\Gamma}\bigg(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta}\bigg)(b-a).

(2.52)

Proof.

As mentioned above, (
2.51
) follows from corollary
2.6
and proposition
2.2
.
Equation (
2.52
) follows from the observation that given any
\epsilon>0
, we have for
0<y<\epsilon
and
I=[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]

\int_{a+\epsilon}^{b-\epsilon}{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)\differential x\leq yN_{\alpha,\beta}(a,b,y)\leq\int_{a-\epsilon}^{b+\epsilon}{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(x,y)\differential x.

(2.53)

Indeed, from (
2.12
), the left side (resp. right side) counts each point in
\xi\in X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
with
\xi\in[a,b]+\mathbb{Z}
with weight at most (resp. at least)
y\mathrm{length}(I)
.
In view of (
2.51
) (choose
\lambda
to be the uniform measure on the intervals
[a+\epsilon,b-\epsilon]
and
[a-\epsilon,b+\epsilon]
, respectively), the limits of the left and right hand side exist for all
\epsilon>0
. Taking
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain the right hand side of (
2.52
).
∎

Our proof of theorem
2.5
follows the strategy of the proof of theorem 8 in
[
23
]
.
As in the proof of theorem
2.1
, our setting is somewhat complicated by the fact that our points
z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}
do not form a
\Gamma
-orbit of a point in
\mathbb{H}
.
However there is a point in
w_{l}\in\mathbb{H}

z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}

This fact gives us sufficient control over the points

for the purposes of proving theorem

and

.

Lemma 2.8
.

For every

, there is a constant

I

,

(2.54)

(ii)

for

and

(2.55)

(iii)

for

,

(2.56)

(2.57)

(The constant
C_{\delta}

\delta
,
\Gamma
and the geodesics

Proof.

Consider the number of hyperbolic lattice points in
B_{I,\alpha,\beta}

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(g)=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma:g^{-1}\gamma g_{l}{\mathrm{i}}\in B_{I,\alpha,\beta}\}.

(2.58)

The set
g^{-1}\Gamma g_{l}
is uniformly discrete, and the minimum distance between any two points is independent of
g
, since
G
acts by isometries. Thus there is a constant
C_{\delta}
such that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{[0,1],1,\delta}(g)\leq C_{\delta}
for all
g\in G

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{[t,t+\alpha],\alpha,\beta}(g)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{[t,t+\alpha],\alpha,\delta\alpha}(g)=\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{[0,1],1,\delta}(gn(t)a(\alpha))\leq C_{\delta}.

This bound is evidently uniform in

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha,\beta}(g)\leq C_{\delta}\bigg(1+\frac{\mathrm{length}(I)}{\alpha}\bigg).

(2.60)

\gamma

Indeed,
\gamma g_{l}{\mathrm{i}}
is on the geodesic
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
, and
\gamma\Gamma_{l}\gamma^{-1}

\gamma
can be chosen so that

is a distance at most

from

2.55
) follows by adjusting

\varepsilon_{l}
.
Next, (
2.54
) follows trivially from (
2.55
) in view of (
2.17
).

\gamma

\begin{split}{\mathcal{N}}_{I,1,\infty}(x,y)&=\sum_{0\leq j\leq\log_{2}(Y/y)}{\mathcal{N}}_{I,2^{j},2^{j+1}}(x,y)\\

).

which in view of (

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\lambda

\mathcal{I}
,

,
\bm{\beta}

\bm{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})

\sup_{y>0}P^{\lambda}_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k},y)\leq\sup_{y>0}\lambda\big(\big\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}}(x,y)\geq k_{i}\;\forall i\big\}\big)\ll\exp( - c_0 \max_{1\leq j\leq r} k_j)

(2.61)

and

P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k})\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta(B_{I_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}})\geq k_{i}\;\forall i\big)\ll\exp( -c_0 \max_{1\leq j \leq r} k_j ).

(2.62)

Proof.

The inequalities “
\leq
” are trivial. As to the upper bounds “
\ll
”, it is sufficient to establish them for
[\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})=[1,\infty)
. Let
j
be so that
k_{j}=\max_{i}k_{i}
.
We may assume that

, since (
2.61
) is otherwise evident.
Now recalling that

{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},1,\infty}(x,y)=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}:\mathrm{Re}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\in x+yI_{j},\mathrm{Im}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\geq y\}

(2.63)

and that by lemma
2.8

\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}:\mathrm{Re}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\in x+yI_{j},y\leq\mathrm{Im}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\leq Yy\}\ll\log Y

(2.64)

for

(note that this is not the same

that appears in the proof of lemma

), we conclude that for

such that
{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},1,\infty}(x,y)\geq k_{j}

\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}:\mathrm{Re}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\in x+yI_{j},\mathrm{Im}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\geq Cy\exp( c_0 k_j)\}\geq 1

for some positive

independent of

and

\{x\in\mathbb{T}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},1,\infty}(x,y)\geq k_{j}\}\subset\{x\in\mathbb{T}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},Y,\infty}(x,y)\geq 1\}.

(2.66)

By Markov’s inequality, the
\lambda

) is at most

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},Y,\infty}(x,y)\differential\lambda(x)\ll\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{j},Y,\infty}(x,y)\differential x\\
&\ll y\;\mathrm{length}(I_{j})\sum_{l=1}^{h}\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{j}}:0\leq\mathrm{Re}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\leq 1,\mathrm{Im}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\geq yY\},\end{split}

where the first inequality follows from

It follows from lemma
2.8
that the quantity on the right of (
2.2
) is

\ll Y^{-1}\ll\exp( - c_0 k_j)

(2.67)

with the implied constant independent of
y
(but depending on
I_{j}
, the geodesics
\bm{c}_{l}
, etc.).
This establishes (
2.61
), and (
2.62

2.61
) and theorem

.
∎

Proof of theorem
2.5
.

Having lemma
2.9
, the proof of theorem
2.5
is easy to finish.
To verify that
G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s})
is analytic in the set of
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
satisfying
\sum_{j}\max\{\mathrm{Re}(s_{j}),0\}<c_{0}
, we start by arranging

G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0\\
\max_{j}k_{j}=k\end{subarray}}{\sum}\exp( \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} k_j s_j)P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k}).

(2.68)

Applying (
2.62
) we find that

\begin{split}&\sum_{k\geq K}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0\\
\max_{j}k_{j}=k\end{subarray}}{\sum}\bigg|\exp( \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} k_j s_j )P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k})\bigg|\\
&\ll\sum_{k\geq K}(k+1)^{r}\exp( -\left( c_0 - \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} \max\{ \mathrm{Re}(s_j), 0\}\right) k ).\end{split}

This bound clearly goes to
0
uniformly as
K\to\infty
for
\bm{s}
in compact subsets of the region
\sum_{j}\max\{\mathrm{Re}(s_{j}),0\}<c_{0}
, and so

is analytic in this region.

To verify that

\lim_{y\to 0}G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s},y)=G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s}),

(2.69)

we recall that

G_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{s},y)=\underset{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0}{\sum}\exp( \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} k_j s_j )P^{\lambda}_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k},y).

(2.70)

Proceeding as above, we arrange this sum according to
k=\max_{j}k_{j}
, and split the sum into
k<K
and
k\geq K
.

For the terms
k<K
, we apply theorem
2.1
to obtain

\begin{split}&\lim_{y\to\infty}\sum_{0\leq k<K}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0\\
\max_{j}k_{j}=k\end{subarray}}{\sum}\exp( \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} k_js_j)P^{\lambda}_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k},y)\\
&=\sum_{0\leq k<K}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0\\
\max_{j}k_{j}=k\end{subarray}}{\sum}\exp( \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} k_js_j)P_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k}).\end{split}

Using (
2.2
), we see that this is equal to

up to an error that goes to

.
For the terms

2.61
) to obtain

\begin{split}&\sum_{k\geq K}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0\\
\max_{j}k_{j}=k\end{subarray}}{\sum}|\exp( \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} k_js_j)P^{\lambda}_{\mathcal{I},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\bm{k})|\\
&\ll\sum_{k\geq K}(k+1)^{r}\exp( - \left( c_0 - \sum_{1\leq j \leq r} \max\{ \mathrm{Re}(s_j), 0\} \right) k).\end{split}

As before, this goes to

).
∎

2.3
A one-dimensional point process

We will now restate theorem
2.1
(in the case
\alpha=1
,
\beta=\infty
) in a form closer to the setting of the introduction. The family of multisets

X_{1,\infty}(y)=\biguplus_{l=1}^{h}\big\{\real(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\bmod 1:\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}},\;\imaginary(z_{\gamma\bm{c}})\geq y\big\}\subset{\mathbb{T}},

(2.71)

forms a nested subsequence as

. We list its elements (with multiplicity) as the sequence
(\xi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
, where we fix any ordering such that if
\xi_{i}\in X_{1,\infty}(y^{\prime})
,
\xi_{j}\in X_{1,\infty}(y)
with

, then
y^{\prime}\geq y
. So in particular, the elements in

are listed as
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}
with

. As in the introduction, we are interested in the number
{\mathcal{N}}_{I}(x,N)={\mathcal{N}}_{I_{i},1,\infty}(x,N)
of points in the interval

. We define the corresponding random point process

\Xi_{N,\lambda}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\delta_{N(\xi_{j}-\xi+k)}

(2.72)

on
{\mathbb{R}}
, with
\xi\in{\mathbb{T}}
distributed according to
\lambda
.
Define the one-dimensional point process process
\Xi
by

\Xi(I)=\Theta(B_{\kappa_{\Gamma}^{-1}I,1,\infty})

(2.73)

with
\kappa_{\Gamma}
as in (
2.23
).
With this scaling,

(2.74)

and hence the intensity measure of

is the normalised Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Furthermore, since the distribution of

is invariant under translations

of
{\mathbb{H}}

We have the following limit theorem.

Theorem 2.10
.

For every Borel probability measure
\lambda
on
{\mathbb{T}}
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we have convergence

\Xi_{N,\lambda}\to\Xi
in distribution as

.

Specifically, for all
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, finite intervals

, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\big(\big\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{i}}(x,N)=k_{i}\;\forall i\big\}\big)={\mathbb{P}}\big(\Xi(I_{i})=k_{i}\;\forall i\big).

(2.75)

Proof.

This follows directly from theorem
2.1
. The only difference is the scaling by

rather than
y^{-1}
. The equivalence of the convergence in the two scalings follows from the asymptotics
yN(y)\to\kappa_{\Gamma}
from (
2.52

∎

3
Equidistribution along discrete orbits

Theorems
2.1
and
2.5
already give the existence of limits for many interesting fine-scale distributions of the the real parts of
z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}
mod 1. There are standard combinatorial arguments that allow the extension of the results to other statistics, such as gap distribution and pair correlation. See for instance
[
23
]
, where the pair correlation is computed from second mixed moments. Here we will use a geometric approach, the surface of section method, from which the gap distribution and pair correlation follow more directly.
This is similar to the approach in
[
1
]
for the statistics of Farey sequences, but with a different section, see section
4.5
for details.
We refer the reader to
[
21
]
for more background on the connection between statistics of entry and return times for a given Poincaré section on one hand, and point processes and their Palm distributions on the other.

3.1
A Poincaré section for the horocycle flow

For
1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty
, define the two-dimensional section

S_{\alpha,\beta}=\{a(s)k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(v^{-1}):s>0,\alpha\leq v<\beta\}\subset G.

(3.1)

We will show below that for
1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty
the sets

\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}=\Gamma\backslash\Gamma g_{l}S_{\alpha,\beta}=\Gamma\backslash\Gamma g_{l}\{a(s)k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(v^{-1}):s\in[1,\varepsilon_{l}^{2}),\alpha\leq v<\beta\}

(3.2)

and

\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta}=\bigcup_{l=1}^{h}\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}

(3.3)

are measurable subsets of
\Gamma\backslash G
.
Define the finite Borel measures
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
,
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}
on
\Gamma\backslash G
by

\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}f(g)\,\differential\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}(g)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{H}}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\int_{1}^{\varepsilon_{l}^{2}}f(\Gamma g_{l}a(s)k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(v^{-1}))\;\frac{\differential s}{s}\;\frac{\differential v}{v^{2}},

(3.4)

and furthermore

\nu_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{l},

(3.5)

so that the support of
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
is
\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta}
, and that of
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}
is
\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}
.

Recall the definition of the multiset
X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
in (
2.10
).

Theorem 3.1
.

For
1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty
and
f:\mathbb{T}\times\Gamma\backslash G\to\mathbb{C}
bounded continuous, we have

\lim_{y\to 0}y\sum_{\xi\in X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)}f(\xi,\Gamma n(\xi)a(y))=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}f(x,g)\differential\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(g)\differential x.

(3.6)

The plan for the proof of this statement is to show that
\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta}
is a Poincaré section for the horocycle flow
\Gamma g\mapsto\Gamma gn(t)
on
\Gamma\backslash G
such that the return times for the periodic orbit
\{\Gamma a(y)n(t):t\in{\mathbb{T}}\}
have the form
y^{-1}\mathrm{Re}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})
. We then introduce an
\epsilon
-thickening of

in the direction of the horocycle flow, and theorem
3.1
then follows by applying the equidistribution of long horocycles in
\Gamma\backslash G
.

Let us first investigate the structure of
\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta}^{l}
.
Recall
\{\Gamma g_{l}a(s):s>0\}
is a closed geodesic in
\Gamma\backslash G
for each

, and in particular the curves are not self-intersecting and are disjoint for different values of
l
.
Therefore
\{\Gamma g_{l}a(s):s>0\}k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(v^{-1})

\Gamma\backslash G
, which are not self-intersecting and are disjoint for different values of
l
. In fact, they do not intersect even after a slight thickening, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
1\leq\alpha<\beta<\infty
and
\epsilon>0
define

S^{\epsilon}_{\alpha,\beta}=\{a(s)k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(v^{-1})n(u):s>0,\alpha\leq v<\beta,|u|<\epsilon\}\subset G.

(3.7)

Then there is
\delta>1
sufficiently small (depending only on
\Gamma
and the
g_{l}

sufficiently small (depending only on
\delta
,
\Gamma
and the

), such that for
\beta<\delta\alpha
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
and

\gamma g_{l_{1}}S^{\epsilon}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap g_{l_{2}}S^{\epsilon}_{\alpha,\beta}=\emptyset

unless
l_{1}=l_{2}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l_{1}}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\gamma\in\Gamma
is such that

\gamma g_{l_{1}}a(s_{1})k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(v_{1})^{-1}n(u_{1})=g_{l_{2}}a(s_{2})k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(v_{2})^{-1}n(u_{2}),

(3.9)

for some
s_{1},s_{2}>0
,
\alpha\leq v_{1},v_{2}<\beta
, and
|u_{1}|,|u_{2}|<\epsilon
. Replacing
\gamma
with
\gamma_{2}\gamma\gamma_{1}
for suitable
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{i}}
,
we may assume furthermore that
1\leq s_{1}<\varepsilon_{l_{1}}^{2}

.
This implies that

g_{l_{2}}^{-1}\gamma g_{l_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}s^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}+O(\beta\epsilon+(\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}}-\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}\beta^{-\frac{1}{2}}))

where

The condition that (

The discreteness of
\Gamma
then implies that for
\beta/\alpha<\delta
sufficiently close to

\beta\epsilon
sufficiently small, if (

for some

Since the geodesics

∎

The lemma implies that

well defined as Borel measures. This observation will be key in the application of the surface-of-section method below.

Lemma 3.3

Let
1\leq\alpha<\beta<\delta\alpha<\infty

X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\to\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta},\qquad\xi\mapsto\Gamma n(\xi)a(y)

n(\mathrm{Re}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}))a(\mathrm{Im}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}))k(\tfrac{\pi}{2})=\gamma g_{l}a(s)

Note that the previous results require

Lemma 3.4

For

Proof of theorem

Since the sum over

.
We write

3.2
Equidistribution of intersection points on a closed geodesic

The following corollary of the discussion in the previous section may be of independent interest. It concerns the joint equidistribution of the points
\mathrm{Re}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})+{\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathbb{T}
(with respect to Lebesgue measure) and the points

on the closed geodesic
\Gamma\backslash\Gamma\bm{c}_{l}

To be precise, for
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}

as the unique

\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}\gamma^{-1}n(\mathrm{Re}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}))a(\mathrm{Im}(z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}))=\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}g_{l}a(\mathrm{e}^{t})k(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})

We remark that in the context of the roots

Proposition 3.5

For
1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty

4.1
Convergence in distribution

The key result of this section is the following analogue of theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty
,
0\leq a<b\leq 1
, we have convergence

\Theta_{y,\alpha,\beta}^{0}\to\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}^{0}
in distribution as

.

In particular, for all
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, finite intervals

,
0\leq a<b\leq 1
, and
1\leq\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}\leq\infty
, we have that

\begin{split}&\lim_{y\to 0}y\#\big\{\xi\in X_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\cap([a,b)+{\mathbb{Z}}):{\mathcal{N}}_{I_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}}(\xi,y)=k_{i}\;\forall i\big\}\\
&=\kappa_{\Gamma}(b-a)\bigg(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta}\bigg){\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}^{0}(B_{I_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}})=k_{i}\;\forall i\big)\end{split}

and the limit is a continuous function of
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
and the endpoints of
I_{i}
.

In the special case of the void distribution (recall theorem
2.1
) we have for instance that for any finite interval
I
(or finite unions of finite intervals),

\lim_{y\to 0}y\#\big\{\xi\in X_{\alpha,\beta}(y):{\mathcal{N}}_{I,\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}(\xi,y)=0\big\}=\kappa_{\Gamma}\bigg(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta}\bigg){\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}^{0}(B_{I,\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}})=0\big)

(4.4)

with the formula

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}^{0}(B)=0\big)=\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\bigg(\prod_{l=1}^{h}\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}}\Big(1-\chi_{B}(z_{g^{-1}\gamma\bm{c}_{l}})\Big)\bigg)\differential\widehat{\nu}_{\alpha,\beta}(g)\\
&=\kappa_{\Gamma}\bigg(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta}\bigg)\sum_{l_{0}=1}^{h}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\int_{1}^{\varepsilon_{l_{0}}^{2}}\bigg(\prod_{l=1}^{h}\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}}\Big(1-\chi_{S_{B}}(a(v)k(\tfrac{\pi}{2})a(s)g_{l_{0}}^{-1}\gamma g_{l})\Big)\bigg)\frac{\differential s}{s}\;\frac{\differential v}{v^{2}}.\end{split}

In particular, the choice

yields the probability that the gap between consecutive elements is greater than

, and

yields the probability that the distance to the nearest neighbour is greater than

.

The proof of theorem

follows the same route as that of theorem
2.1
, except that, rather than equidistribution of closed long horocycles, we now use the discrete averages established in theorem
3.1
. We will not repeat the proof, other than to establish the following lemma which guarantees the regularity of the limit distribution.

.

For any finite interval

,

,

(4.5)

(4.6)

and

Proof.

has measure zero with respect to

has measure zero for every

,
l_{0}

). Analogously, to prove (

∎

4.5
Entry and return times

Athreya and Cheung
[

constructed a Poincaré section (different from

) for the horocycle flow on

In fact, the return times for the horocycle flow for our section

generate the gaps between the roots of quadratic congruences when

To see this, define for initial condition

the

for the section

by the
\Gamma

\tau_{\alpha,\beta}(g)=\inf\{u>0:\Gamma gn(u)\in\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,\beta}\}.

.

,

is a measurable function. It is a general fact, see
[

]
, that the ergodicity of the horocycle flow

.

5
Geodesics and roots of quadratic congruences

The aim of this section is to express the roots
\frac{\mu}{m}
in terms of the tops of certain geodesics in the Poincaré upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
. The key result is the following theorem
5.1

2

4
yield the convergence of all fine-scale statistics for the roots of quadratic congruences, including moments, gap distributions and pair correlation measures. In particular, the limit theorems

and
1.2
in the introduction for the special choice

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
D>0
be square-free and
D\not\equiv 1\bmod 4
. Fix

and
\nu\bmod n
such that
\nu^{2}\equiv D\bmod n

Then there exists a finite set of geodesics
\{\bm{c}_{1},\dots,\bm{c}_{h}\}
, all of which are closed and have the same length in
\Gamma_{0}(n)\backslash\mathbb{H}
, with the following properties:

(i)

For any integer
m>0
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfying
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
,
m\equiv 0\bmod n
, and
\mu\equiv\nu\bmod n

and double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(n)/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}
such that

z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}\equiv\frac{\mu}{m}+{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}\bmod{\Gamma_{\infty}}.

(5.1)

(ii)

Conversely, given
l
and double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(n)/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}
with
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
positively oriented, there exist unique
m>0
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfying
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
,
m\equiv 0\bmod n
, and
\mu\equiv\nu\bmod n
such that (
5.1
) holds.

We will see below that the number
h
of the closed geodesics is equal to the narrow class number
h^{+}(D)
of the quadratic number field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
. The narrow class group is defined as the quotient of the group of fractional ideals in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
modulo the group of fractional ideals of the form
\xi\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
with
\xi\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
totally positive; here
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is a maximal order by our assumption on
D
. Moreover, the stabiliser subgroups
\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}
are all conjugate in
G
to the (projectively) cyclic subgroup generated by
\pm\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{0}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
, where
\varepsilon_{0}
is the generator of the totally positive units of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, chosen so that
\varepsilon_{0}>1
.

The remainder of this section is dedicated to the proof of theorem
5.1
. It is based on the amalgamation of two well-known correspondences.
The first is that given an integral binary quadratic form
F(X,Y)
with positive discriminant, the geodesic in
\mathbb{H}
connecting the two roots in
\mathbb{R}
of
F(X,1)
projects to a closed geodesic in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}
. The second is that if
F(X,Y)=mX^{2}-2\mu XY+cY^{2}
has discriminant
4D
, then
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
, so then we see that the sum of the roots of
F(X,1)
is
2\frac{\mu}{m}
, the difference of the roots is
2\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}
, and thus the top of the geodesic corresponding to
F(X,Y)
is
\frac{\mu}{m}+{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}
.
In the remainder of this section we re-establish these connections in our own notation, but using ideals in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
instead of binary quadratic forms. We then examine them further in the case when
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is replaced by the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(n)
.

\sqrt{D}I
has basis

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}\beta_{1}\\
\sqrt{D}\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&D\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(5.4)

I
being an ideal is equivalent to

\frac{1}{b_{1}b_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&D\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{3}&-b_{2}\\
0&b_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{b_{1}b_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}b_{2}b_{3}&-b_{2}^{2}+Db_{1}^{2}\\
b_{3}^{2}&-b_{2}b_{3}\end{pmatrix}

(5.5)

having integer entries.
From the first column and last row, we see that for
I
to be an ideal, it is necessary that
b_{1}\mid b_{2}
and
b_{1}\mid b_{3}
.
Writing
b_{2}=\mu b_{1}
and
b_{3}=mb_{1}
, we see from the top right entry of (
5.5
) that
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod{m}
is necessary for
I
to be an ideal.
We note that, if
I
is an ideal not divisible by rational integers greater than
1
, then
b_{1}=1
, and we obtain the first part of proposition
5.2
.
For the converse part, we simply note that if
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
, then the above shows that the sublattice
I
with basis given by (
5.2
) satisfies
\sqrt{D}I\subset I
as required.
∎

We now produce different bases for ideals
I\subset\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
than those in (
5.2
). Any new basis is related to (
5.2
) by an element of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
We fix representatives
I_{l}
,
1\leq l\leq h^{+}(D)
, of the narrow class group of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
.
This implies that for any ideal
I\subset\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
there is a totally positive
\xi\in I_{l}^{-1}
such that
I=\xi I_{l}
.
To obtain a basis for
I
in this way, we first embed
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
via
a+b\sqrt{D}\mapsto(a+b\sqrt{D},a-b\sqrt{D})
, so that
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
(Here we have fixed
\sqrt{D}
to be the positive square root of
D
.)
We adopt the notation that for
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
we use
\xi^{(1)}
and
\xi^{(2)}
to denote these coordinates.
With this notation, in (
5.2
) we replace

\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}\leftarrow\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}^{(1)}&\beta_{1}^{(2)}\\
\beta_{2}^{(1)}&\beta_{2}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\leftarrow\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(5.6)

We fix
\mathbb{Z}
-bases
\{\beta_{l1},\beta_{l2}\}
for each of the
I_{l}
so that

\mathfrak{B}_{l}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{l1}^{(1)}&\beta_{l1}^{(2)}\\
\beta_{l2}^{(1)}&\beta_{l2}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}

(5.7)

has positive determinant.
Then
\{\xi\beta_{l1},\xi\beta_{l2}\}
is a basis for
\xi I_{l}
, and the corresponding basis matrix is

\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}\beta_{l1}^{(1)}&\xi^{(2)}\beta_{l1}^{(2)}\\
\xi^{(1)}\beta_{l2}^{(1)}&\xi^{(2)}\beta_{l2}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.8)

For
m>0
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfying
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
, we let
I\subset\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
be the corresponding ideal via proposition
5.2
.
There is some
I_{l}
and totally positive
\xi
such that
I=\xi I_{l}
, and hence there is a
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(5.9)

We observe that considering
\mu
as a residue class modulo
m
is equivalent to considering the coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma
.
More significantly, since
\xi
is only determined up to a multiple of a totally positive unit, we also need only consider the coset
\gamma\Gamma_{l}
, where

\Gamma_{l}=\left\{\pm\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{0}^{k}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{0}^{-k}\end{pmatrix}\mathfrak{B}_{l}^{-1}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

(5.10)

with
\varepsilon_{0}
, chosen to be greater than
1
, generating the group of totally positive units in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
.
Thus equation (
5.9
) defines a unique double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{l}
. Furthermore
\Gamma_{l}
satisfies

\Gamma_{l}=\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\cap\left\{\pm\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathfrak{B}_{l}^{-1}:t>0\right\}

(5.11)

exactly because
\varepsilon I_{l}=I_{l}
if and only if
\varepsilon
is a unit in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
.

Conversely, suppose that there is a totally positive
\xi
(necessarily in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
), and
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(5.12)

We claim that then we in fact have

\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(5.13)

where
m>0
and
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
.

To verify this, we first note that if
\xi\in I_{l}^{-1}
, then the left side of (
5.12
) is the basis for an ideal
I\subset\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, so then (
5.13
) follows by an application of proposition
5.2
.
Now to show
\xi\in I_{l}^{-1}
follows from (
5.12
), we fix a basis
\{\overline{\beta}_{l1},\overline{\beta}_{l2}\}
of
I_{l}^{-1}
and write
\xi=c_{1}\overline{\beta}_{l1}+c_{2}\overline{\beta}_{l2}
.
Our goal then is to show that
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are in fact integers.
Since
I_{l}^{-1}I_{l}=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, we can define integers
b_{lijk}
by

\overline{\beta}_{li}\beta_{lj}=b_{lij1}\sqrt{D}+b_{lij2},

(5.14)

and we let

B_{li}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{li11}&b_{li12}\\
b_{li21}&b_{li22}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.15)

It follows that

\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=(c_{1}B_{l1}+c_{2}B_{l2})\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(5.16)

and so, under the assumption (
5.12
), if
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
, then

B_{l}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d\\
-c\end{pmatrix},

(5.17)

where

B_{l}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{l111}&b_{l211}\\
b_{l121}&b_{l221}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.18)

The integrality of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
is now an immediate consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

For all
l
, we have
B_{l}\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

We first observe that if
c_{1}^{\prime},c_{2}^{\prime}
were such that

B_{l}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{\prime}\\
c_{2}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(5.19)

then, setting
\xi^{\prime}=c_{1}^{\prime}\overline{\beta}_{l1}+c_{2}^{\prime}\overline{\beta}_{l2}
, we would have from (
5.16
) that
\xi I_{l}\subset\mathbb{Z}
.
This is a contradiction if
\xi^{\prime}\neq 0
, and so we must have
\det B_{l}\neq 0
.

Now suppose that
\xi^{\prime\prime}=c_{1}^{\prime\prime}\overline{\beta}_{l1}+c_{2}^{\prime\prime}\overline{\beta}_{l2}
is a primitive vector in
I_{l}^{-1}
, meaning
a^{-1}\xi^{\prime\prime}\notin I_{l}^{-1}
for all rational integers
a>1
, or equivalently
\gcd(c_{1}^{\prime\prime},c_{2}^{\prime\prime})=1
.
Then the ideal
\xi^{\prime\prime}I_{l}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is not divisible by any rational integer greater than
1
.
Now by the construction of the
B_{li}
, see (
5.16
), the rows of

(c_{1}^{\prime\prime}B_{l1}+c_{2}^{\prime\prime}B_{l2})\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

(5.20)

form a basis of
\xi^{\prime\prime}I_{l}
.
Putting
c_{1}^{\prime\prime}B_{l1}+c_{2}^{\prime\prime}B_{l2}
into Hermite normal form, proposition
5.2
implies that the entries in the first column of
c_{1}^{\prime\prime}B_{l1}+c_{2}^{\prime\prime}B_{l2}
, i.e. the entries of
B_{l}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{\prime\prime}\\
c_{2}^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}
, are coprime.
Hence
B_{l}
maps pairs of coprime integers to coprime integers, and thus
B_{l}\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

We record the above observations in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
m
be a positive integer and
\mu\bmod m
satisfy
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
.
Then there exists a unique
l
,
1\leq l\leq h^{+}(D)
, and a unique double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{l}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma_{l}
such that

\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\mu}{m}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}=\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}

(5.21)

for some totally positive
\xi
.

Conversely, if for
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{l}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma_{l}
there exists a totally positive
\xi
such that

\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(5.22)

then in fact

\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\mu}{m}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

(5.23)

with
m
a positive integer and
\mu\bmod m
satisfying
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
.
Moreover such
m
and
\mu\bmod m
, if they exist, are unique for a given
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{l}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma_{l}
.

Using the identification of
G/\{\pm I\}
with the unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{H}
, we interpret proposition
5.4
geometrically in proposition
5.5
below.
We define oriented geodesics
\bm{c}_{l}
,
1\leq l\leq h^{+}(D)
by

\bm{c}_{l}=\left\{(\det\mathfrak{B}_{l})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}:t>0\right\}.

(5.24)

In this case
\bm{c}_{l}
defines a closed geodesic in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}
and
\Gamma_{l}
is the stabilizer in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
of the geodesic
\bm{c}_{l}
, i.e.
\Gamma_{l}=\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}
in the general notation introduced in section
2
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
m>0
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfy
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
.
Then there is a unique
l
and double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{l}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma_{l}
such that

z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}\equiv\frac{\mu}{m}+{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}

(5.25)

modulo
\Gamma_{\infty}
.

Conversely, given
l
and a double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{l}
such that
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
is positively oriented, there exist unique
m
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfying the above.

Proof.

We observe that in view of (
2.2
), the matrices

\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
0&*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}&-\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\\
\sin\frac{\pi}{4}&\cos\frac{\pi}{4}\end{pmatrix}

(5.26)

are identified in the unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{H}
with points having tangent vectors pointing directly to the right, and so the intersection of the set of these matrices with the set of matrices having the form

\gamma(\det\mathfrak{B}_{l})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}*&0\\
0&*\end{pmatrix}

(5.27)

is exactly the point on the geodesic
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
having horizontal tangent vector pointing to the right, if such a point exists, and otherwise this intersection is empty.
We note that such a point on
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
exists exactly when
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
is positively oriented and in this case gives the point on
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
with largest imaginary part.
Scaling and rewriting the right side of (
5.21
) as

\displaystyle(2\sqrt{D})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\mu}{m}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&m^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\quad=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\mu}{m}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\left(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\left(\frac{m}{\sqrt{D}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}&-\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\\
\sin\frac{\pi}{4}&\cos\frac{\pi}{4}\end{pmatrix},

we finish the proof by appealing to proposition
5.4
.
∎

5.2
Congruence subgroups

So far we have only considered the orbit of geodesics under the action of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, however, as reflected in theorem
5.1
, the orbits under
\Gamma_{0}(n)
also give interesting arithmetic information.
Indeed, by picking cosets
\Gamma_{0}(n)\gamma_{l}\in\Gamma_{0}(n)\backslash\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
correctly, one can obtain all
m
and roots
\mu\bmod m
satisfying
m\equiv 0\bmod n
and
\mu\equiv\nu\bmod n
, for a fixed root
\nu\bmod n
, by considering the
\Gamma_{0}(n)
-orbits of the geodesics
\gamma_{l}\bm{c}_{l}
.

To establish this, we first show that if a root
\mu_{1}\bmod{m_{1}}
satisfies
m_{1}\equiv 0\bmod n
and
\mu_{1}\equiv\nu\bmod n
, and if
\mu_{2}\bmod{m_{2}}
is another root in the same
\Gamma_{0}(n)
-orbit as
\mu_{1}\bmod{m_{1}}
, then
\mu_{2}\bmod{m_{2}}
also satisfies
m_{2}\equiv 0\bmod n
,
\mu_{2}\equiv\nu\bmod n
.
We then show that if roots
\mu_{1}\bmod{m_{1}}
and
\mu_{2}\bmod{m_{2}}
in the same
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-orbit satisfy
m_{1}\equiv m_{2}\equiv 0\bmod n
and
\mu_{1}\equiv\mu_{2}\equiv\nu\bmod n
, then in fact the roots are in the same
\Gamma_{0}(n)
-orbit.
Finally, for sake of completeness, we show that for every
1\leq l\leq h^{+}(D)
there is
\gamma_{l}=\gamma_{l}(n,\nu)
such that the top of
\gamma_{l}\bm{c}_{l}
satisfies the congruence conditions.

Although it may not be the quickest route to demonstrating the first point, we proceed by recording the following corollary of proposition
5.4
.

Corollary 5.6
.

As
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
runs through representatives of the double cosets in
\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma_{l}
such that
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
is positively oriented,

\begin{pmatrix}\mu\\
m\end{pmatrix}=(\det B_{l})\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{l112}b_{l211}-b_{l212}b_{l111}&b_{l112}b_{l221}-b_{l212}b_{l121}\\
b_{l122}b_{l211}-b_{l111}b_{l222}&b_{l122}b_{l221}-b_{l121}b_{l222}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c\\
d\end{pmatrix},

(5.28)

parametrise all
\mu\bmod{m}
satisfying
m>0
and
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m
.

This corollary recovers the classic parametrisation of roots of
\mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod{m}
in terms of binary quadratic forms with discriminant
4D
.
Indeed, (
5.28
) gives
m
as a binary quadratic form evaluated at
c,d
, and one can check that this form has discriminant
4D
.
This binary quadratic form automatically comes with a Bhargava cube, see
[
2
]
, given by the matrices
B_{l1},B_{l2}
, which is associated with the ideals
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}],I_{l},I_{l}^{-1}
.
We also note that (
5.28
) gives a criterion on
\gamma
for the geodesic
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
to be positively oriented, namely that the
m
given by (
5.28
) is in fact positive.

Proof.

We recall that for
\xi=c_{1}\overline{\beta}_{l1}+c_{2}\overline{\beta}_{l2}
,

\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\gamma(c_{1}B_{l1}+c_{2}B_{l2})\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(5.29)

and so (
5.21
), (
5.23
) give

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}=\gamma(c_{1}B_{l1}+c_{2}B_{l2}).

(5.30)

Equating the first columns, we see that

\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}=B_{l}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}d\\
-c\end{pmatrix},

(5.31)

from which (
5.28
) follows from the second column of (
5.30
).
∎

Having (
5.28
), we immediately see that if
\gamma
is replaced by
\gamma_{1}\gamma
for some
\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma_{0}(n)
,
m\bmod n
is replaced by a multiple, and, if
m\equiv 0\bmod n
,
\mu\bmod n
is unchanged.
Hence the conditions
m\equiv 0\bmod n
and
\mu\equiv\nu\bmod n
are invariant under
\Gamma_{0}(n)
, thus finishing the first part of our examination of the
\Gamma_{0}(n)
orbits.
The second part is completed by the following lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
be positive integers and
\mu_{1}\bmod{m_{1}}
,
\mu_{2}\bmod{m_{2}}
satisfy
\mu_{1}^{2}\equiv D\bmod{m_{1}}
,
\mu_{2}^{2}\equiv D\bmod{m_{2}}
.
Suppose that
m_{1}\equiv m_{2}\equiv 0\bmod n
and that
\mu_{1}\equiv\mu_{2}\equiv\nu\bmod n
where
\nu\bmod n
is a fixed root of
\nu^{2}\equiv D\bmod n
.
Then, if there exists
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu_{1}\\
0&m_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\gamma\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu_{2}\\
0&m_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

(5.32)

for some totally positive
\xi
, then in fact
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(n)
.

Proof.

Let
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
be the ideals corresponding to
\mu_{1}\bmod{m_{1}}
,
\mu_{2}\bmod{m_{2}}
.
Then since

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu_{j}\\
0&m_{j}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&k_{j}\\
0&\frac{m_{j}}{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\nu\\
0&n\end{pmatrix}

(5.33)

for some integers
k_{j}
, we have
I_{1},I_{2}\subset I
, where
I
is the ideal corresponding to
\nu\bmod{n}
.
Similarly, we have

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu_{1}\\
0&m_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\
0&n\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\nu_{1}\\
0&n_{1}\end{pmatrix},

(5.34)

where
n_{1}=\frac{m_{1}}{n}
and
\nu_{1}\bmod{n_{1}}
is the root corresponding to the ideal
I_{1}I^{-1}
.
Now (
5.32
) implies that
\xi I_{1}=I_{2}
, so
\xi I_{1}I^{-1}=I_{2}I^{-1}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, whence

\begin{pmatrix}1&\nu_{1}\\
0&n_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=A\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

(5.35)

for some integer matrix
A
.
Rewriting the left side of (
5.35
) as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\
0&n\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu_{1}\\
0&m_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\
0&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\quad=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\
0&n\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\gamma\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu_{2}\\
0&m_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

we obtain

\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\
0&n\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\gamma\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu_{2}\\
0&m_{2}\end{pmatrix}=A.

(5.36)

Since
A
has integer entries, an inspection of the
(2,1)
entry of (
5.36
) shows that
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(n)
.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

For each
1\leq l\leq h^{+}(D)
, there exists
\gamma_{l}=\gamma_{l}(n,\nu)
such that

z_{\gamma_{l}\bm{c}_{l}}=\frac{\mu}{m}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}

(5.37)

with
m\equiv 0\bmod n
and
\mu\equiv\nu\bmod n
.

Proof.

By proposition
5.2
, there exists an ideal
I_{0}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
corresponding to the root
\nu\bmod n
.
As
I_{1}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
ranges over the narrow ideal class of
I_{l}I_{0}^{-1}
, the integers

We let

Letting

\ignorespaces\ignorespaces I_{0}I_{1}=(n,\nu+\sqrt{D})(m_{1},\mu_{1}+\sqrt{D})=(m_{1}n,m_{1}\nu+m_{1}\sqrt{D},n\mu_{1}+n\sqrt{D},\mu_{1}\nu+D+(\mu_{1}+\nu)\sqrt{D})\\

therefore corresponds to the root

Corollary

, lemma

, and lemma

show that for a fixed

In fact we show that the closed geodesic in
\Gamma_{0}(n)\backslash\mathbb{H}

has the same length as the closed geodesic in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}
coming from
\bm{c}_{l}

This is not a trivial observation; in general the splitting behaviour of geodesics in finite covering surfaces is quite complicated, being analogous to the splitting of primes in number fields, see
[

Understanding the length of

in

in

It is clear that there is some positive integer

\gamma_{l}\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{0}^{k}&0\\

(5.39)

and the smallest such

in

Lemma 5.9
.

For all

, we have

\gamma_{l}\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{0}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathfrak{B}_{l}^{-1}\gamma_{l}^{-1}\in\Gamma_{0}(n).

(5.40)

Proof.

We let
\gamma_{0}
be the matrix on the left side of (

\gamma_{0}

\gamma_{0}\gamma_{l}\mathfrak{B}_{l}=\gamma_{l}\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{0}&0\\

(5.41)

From the way

was chosen, for some totally positive

\gamma_{l}\mathfrak{B}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{(1)}&0\\

∎
