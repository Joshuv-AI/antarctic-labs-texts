Probabilistic fine-tuning of pruning masks and PAC-Bayes self-bounded learning
Soufiane Hayou, Bobby He, Gintare Karolina Dziugaite

Abstract
We study an approach to learning pruning masks by optimizing the expected loss of stochastic pruning masks, i.e., masks which zero out each weight independently with some weight-specific probability. We analyze the training dynamics of the induced stochastic predictor in the setting of linear regression, and observe a data-adaptive L 1 L_{1} regularization term, in contrast to the data-adaptive L 2 L_{2} regularization term known to underlie dropout in linear regression. We also observe a preference to prune weights that are less well-aligned with the data labels. We evaluate probabilistic fine-tuning for optimizing stochastic pruning masks for neural networks, starting from masks produced by several baselines (namely, magnitude pruning ( Han et al. 2015 ) , SNIP ( Lee et al. 2018 ) , and random masks). In each case, we see improvements in test error over baselines, even after we threshold fine-tuned stochastic pruning masks. Finally, since a stochastic pruning mask induces a stochastic neural network, we consider training the weights and/or pruning probabilities simultaneously to minimize a PAC-Bayes bound on generalization error. Using data-dependent priors ( Dziugaite et al. 2021 ) , we obtain a self-bounded learning algorithm with strong performance and numerically tight bounds. In the linear model, we show that a PAC-Bayes generalization error bound is controlled by the magnitude of the change in feature alignment between the “prior” and “posterior” data.

Introduction

Modern deep neural networks (DNNs) are
massively overparametrized, which presents problems both during training and deployment. Recently, the demand to reduce the size of networks during and after training has
led to a rush of work in network pruning (
Han et al. 2015
;
Frankle & Carbin 2018
;
Lee et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Hayou et al. 2021
, etc.),
much of it building on pioneering efforts carried out decades ago
(
Mozer & Smolensky 1989
;
LeCun et al. 1990
;
Hassibi et al. 1993
)
. Many approaches to pruning begin by masking a subset of weights to zero. In approaches applied after training, this step typically causes a drop in both train and test accuracy, which is then addressed by fine-tuning (or retraining).

Intuitively, fine-tuning can be seen as looking for
locally optimal weights
given the mask.
In this work, we consider replacing adhoc rules for selecting pruning masks with optimization to learn the masks.
In doing so, we observe that the masks used by state-of-the-art approaches to pruning are far from locally optimal.

To approach this learning problem, we introduce a stochastic optimization framework for masks, based on the Gumbel–Softmax trick
(
Eric 2017
;
C.. & Teh 2017
)
.
Rather than viewing the application of a mask as a deterministic procedure,
we view it as a stochastic operation, where each weight
w_{i}
is pruned with some probability
1-\lambda_{i}
such that
D-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=sD
, where
D
is the total number of weights and
s\in[0,1]
is the desired sparsity level.
We then consider optimizing
the
expected
empirical risk of this induced randomized predictor
with respect to the pruning probabilities.
Finally, to obtain a deterministic mask, we threshold the probabilities at a fixed level or at a level chosen to achieve a desired sparsity.
We call this entire procedure
probabilistic fine-tuning
(PFT).

We investigate probabilistic fine-tuning both analytically and empirically.
To gain some analytical insight, we study the case of linear regression in
Section

3
, where we find that optimizing the pruning probabilities of a stochastic pruning mask has a data-dependent regularizing effect similar to that of dropout
(
Srivastava et al. 2014
)
.
For linear regression, it is well known that dropout leads to data-adaptive
L_{2}
regularization of the weights
(
Wager et al. 2013
)
. We show that optimizing the pruning probabilities looks like training the mean network subject to data-and-weight-adaptive
L_{1}
regularization of the pruning probabilities. In the appendix, we discuss how this analysis of a linear model can be extended to wide DNNs in the Neural Tangent Kernel regime
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
.

We evaluate probabilistic fine-tuning on several benchmark vision tasks
in
Sections

4
and
7
. In this setting, the regularizing effects of dropout on weight training are well documented. On the other hand, the dynamics of optimizing only the dropout probabilities has not received close attention,
although the sparsifying effects of variational dropout (when both weights and Gaussian dropout parameters are trained) were noted by
Molchanov et al. 2017
.
We consider optimizing stochastic pruning masks, starting from initial pruning probabilities derived from
magnitude pruning
(
Han et al. 2015
)
, SNIP
(
Lee et al. 2018
)
, and random pruning. Across the board, we observe improved accuracy as a result of probabilistic fine-tuning, demonstrating that existing heuristics are, in some cases, far from optimal.

Finally, after observing that pruned networks are also more robust to random perturbations of their weights, we consider replacing deterministic fine-tuning with PAC-Bayes bound optimization.
Our approach, dubbed PAC-Bayes pruning, uses spike-and-slab prior and posterior distributions, which were previously investigated by
Louizos 2015
in the context of variational inference.
Different from earlier work, we employ data-dependent priors to achieve tighter bounds (and prevent underfitting), as advocated recently by
Dziugaite et al. 2021
.
On MNIST
(
LeCun & Cortes 2010
)
, Fashion-MNIST
(
Xiao et al. 2017
)
, and CIFAR-10
(
Krizhevsky 2009
)
, we obtain competitive predictors along with numerically tight, nonvacuous generalization bounds.

Contributions

We propose to learn pruning masks by truncating stochastic pruning masks that have been optimized to minimize expected error using stochastic optimization.
We study this idea analytically and empirically and describe several extesions.

•

We introduce
Probabilistic Fine-Tuning
(PFT), whereby we learn a pruning mask for a trained predictor.

•

Theoretically, PFT trains a mean predictor subject to implicit data-and-weight dependent L1 regularization.
Empirically,
PFT improves upon popular heuristics for choosing pruning masks, demonstrating these approaches are far from locally optimal.

•

Based on the observation that pruned networks are more robust to perturbations of the weights, we propose
PAC-Bayes Pruning
(PBP), whereby we prune a neural network by optimizing a PAC-Bayes bound, using data-dependent, spike-and-slab priors and posteriors on the weights.

•

Theoretically, PBP performs constrained feature realignment in linear models. Empirically, it delivers strong predictors for standard benchmarks along with numerically tight generalization bounds.

Stochastic pruning

Consider a class of predictors
\{f_{W}\}
, indexed by weight vectors
W\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
Let
S
denote the training data,
consisting of
N
i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution
\mathcal{D}
. For a given loss function
\ell
,
let
\hat{R}_{S}(W)
denote the empirical risk, i.e.,
the loss
of the predictor
f_{W}
on average over the training set
S
.
Let
R_{\mathcal{D}}(W)
denote the risk, i.e., the expectation of the empirical risk, regarding the weights as fixed.

Pruning involves the application of a binary mask
b\in\{0,1\}^{D}

to the weights of a predictor
f_{W}
producing the predictor
f_{W^{\prime}}
, where
W^{\prime}=b\circ W
is the Hadamard (i.e., element-wise) product.
We say weight
i\in[D]
is pruned if
b_{i}=0
.
We assume our class of predictors
f_{W}
are such that
b_{i}=0
implies
\frac{\partial f_{W^{\prime}}(x)}{\partial w_{i}}=0
for all
x\in X
. That is, a pruned weight plays no role in training.

Pruning Masks

A standard approach to generating masks is to give each weight
w_{i}
a score
g_{i}
according to some criterion.
The mask is then created by keeping the top
k
weights by score, where
k
is chosen to meet some desired level of sparsity.
Examples of pruning criterion include:

•

Magnitude
: weights are scored by magnitude, i.e.,
g_{i}=|W_{i}|

(
Han et al. 2015
)
;

•

Sensitivity
: weights are scored based on how much zero’ing them will impact empirical risk
(
LeCun et al. 1990
;
Lee et al. 2018
)
.
For example, SNIP
(
Lee et al. 2018
)
uses a first-order approximation of the training objective

\tilde{\ell}_{W}\approx\tilde{\ell}_{W=0}+W\frac{\partial\tilde{\ell}}{\partial W}
,
yielding the score
g_{i}=|W_{i}\frac{\partial\tilde{\ell}}{\partial W_{i}}|
.

Stochastic Pruning Masks

In this work, we consider
stochastic
pruning masks, i.e.,
vectors
b
of Bernoulli random variables.
We focus on the case where elements of the mask are independent, so that the distribution
of the mask is determined by probabilities
\lambda_{i}=\Pr\{b_{i}=1\}
.
For a fixed set of weights
W
,
a stochastic mask induces a distribution, say
P
, on pruned weights

W^{\prime}=(w^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{D}
.
In particular, conditional on
W
, the components of
W^{\prime}
are independent and
w^{\prime}_{i}
has distribution
(1-\lambda_{i})\delta_{0}+\lambda_{i}\delta_{w_{i}}
, where
\delta_{v}
denotes the Dirac mass on
v
.

Optimizing stochastic pruning masks

Besides inducing a distribution
P
on weights, stochastic pruning masks correspond to randomized predictors:
We can think of such a distribution as a stochastic predictor that, on each input
x
, samples
W\sim P
and then outputs
f_{W}(x)
. Such a stochastic predictor is known as a
Gibbs
predictor.
The
empirical Gibbs risk

and
Gibbs risk
of
P
are, respectively,

\hat{R}_{S}(P)=\mathbb{E}_{W\sim P}[\hat{R}_{S}(W)]

and

R_{\mathcal{D}}(P)=\mathbb{E}_{W\sim P}[R_{\mathcal{D}}(W)].

We can find stochastic pruning masks that produce sparse networks with small empirical risk by minimizing the empirical Gibbs risk with respect to the pruning probabilities.
A discussion on how we initialize the pruning probabilities is provided in
Section

A.1
.

Stage 1: Pre-train the dense model.

Using a variant of stochastic gradient descent, minimize

Obtain a deterministic predictor with weights

Stage 2: Fine-tune the masks.

1.

Initialize the pruning probabilities.
If a criterion

2.

Continue minimizing the empirical Gibbs risk.
The minimization is done w.r.t. the pruning probabilities (performed using the Gumbel–Softmax trick. See

) and the weights.

3.

The trained pruning probabilities are used to create a new
deterministic
mask by keeping the weights with the largest
\lambda_{i}
values.

Learning prior pruning probabilities.

We now investigate the effect of optimizing the empirical risk of the stochastic predictor
P
with respect to
\lambda^{0}_{i}
’s, as one does in stage 2 of PFT.
According to the following result, this optimization is equivalent to optimizing the empirical risk of the mean predictor
\overline{W}
with an explicit regularization term that encourages pruning.
(See

Lemma 0

(Informal)
.

Gradient descent on the empirical Gibbs error (
Eq.

1
)
with respect to pruning probabilities
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}
performs implicit

regularization of
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}
.

Proof sketch.

One can show
\frac{\partial\hat{R}_{S_{P}}(P)}{\partial\lambda^{0}_{i}}{-}\frac{\partial\hat{R}_{S_{P}}(\overline{W})}{\partial\lambda^{0}_{i}}{=}r_{i}^{0},

where
r_{i}^{0}=\frac{1}{2}(w^{P}_{i})^{2}(\Sigma_{P})_{ii}

For fixed weights

∎

Unlike optimizing with respect to the deterministic pruning weight, optimizing with respect to the stochastic pruning mask accounts for the effect of dropping the weight.
While we do not optimize the weights in Stage 2 of the simplified algorithm above, we can apply a similar analysis as in

We find

5
PAC-Bayes: Preliminaries

In the previous section, we saw that pruned networks were more robust to weight perturbation, and that this effect was true also for probabilistically fine-tuned masks. This insensitivity-to-weight-perturbation is suggestive that an application of the PAC-Bayes theorem may yield a generalization bound.
In this section, we introduce data-dependent PAC-Bayes bounds and propose to optimize them to prune and fine-tune the network.
We extend our linear model analysis to this approach and find that it carries out constrained feature realignment. We then evaluate this approach empirically, and find that it produces predictors with both high accuracy and extremely tight generalization guarantees, without the need for extra data.

5.1
Data-dependent PAC-Bayes Bounds

PAC-Bayes theorems provide
confidence intervals for Gibbs risks
R_{\mathcal{D}}(Q)
in terms of an empirical Gibbs risk
\hat{R}_{S}(Q)

Many forms of PAC-Bayes theorems exist.
In our work we consider a PAC-Bayes bound with a data-dependent prior, meaning that the prior

8
Related Work

Recent work by
Elesedy et al. 2020
has shown that, in the context of linear models, magnitude pruning zeros out the weights based on the magnitude of feature alignment under certain assumptions on the feature covariance matrix. Our analysis is complementary to these findings.

Other pruning methods.

Our work uses a class of pruning
approaches that identify the set of weights to prune based on a criterion applied to each weight by the end of a single training run. We refer to such methods as one-shot pruning (OSP). These include magnitude pruning
(
Han et al. 2015
)
and SNIP
(
Lee et al. 2018
)
, as well as many others (e.g., see GraSP
(
Wang et al. 2020a
)
and SynFlow
(
Tanaka et al. 2020
)
for pruning at initialization, and
L_{0}
regularization-based
(
Savarese et al. 2019
;
Gale et al. 2019
;
Louizos et al. 2017
)
and dropout-based
(
Molchanov et al. 2017
;
Zhu & Gupta 2017
)
for pruning during training).
SNIP was proposed as a technique to prune at initialization, with the remaining weights trained as usual. In contrast, magnitude pruning was proposed as a technique to apply after training, following by fine-tuning.
However, state-of-the-art applications of magnitude pruning now employ iterative magnitude pruning (IMP)
(
Frankle & Carbin 2018
;
Frankle et al. 2020
)
, where a fraction of the smallest weights are pruned on each round, followed by rewinding training back to an earlier iteration
(
Frankle et al. 2020
)
or simply rewinding the learning rates
(
Renda et al. 2020
)
.
Gale et al. 2019
;
Frankle et al. 2021
show that none of the OSP techniques dominate one another, and IMP outperforms them all
(
Renda et al. 2020
)
.

The pruning method that is most related to PFT is an extension of variational dropout due to
Molchanov et al. 2017
.
In variational dropout, the weights are multiplied by Gaussian variables instead of Bernoulli variables. Although the variances of the Gaussian mask can be forced to be small or large, the corresponding model is never sparse (with probability one), and another thresholding step is required to make the model sparse (e.g., remove the weight with the highest Gaussian mask variance). Another major difference is the choice of the prior; Gaussian dropout consider the improper log-scale uniform prior since it is the only prior that makes variational inference consistent with Gaussian dropout. As a result, this prior can only yield vacuous PAC-Bayes bounds.

9
Discussion and Limitations

In this work, we introduced a new algorithmic framework for stochastic pruning. The generality of this framework allowed us to derive two algorithms, PFT and PBP, that act on the same input network and return different outputs. We elucidate the non-optimality of OSP pruning methods by showing that probabilistic fine-tuning (PFT) finds better local masks, starting from popular OSP methods. To depict this gain in performance, we analyze PFT in the context of linear models and show that PFT learns pruning probabilities based on feature alignment. Further, we show a regularization effect on pruning probabilities arising from minimizing risk of a stochastic predictor, rather than an average one. This is complementary to
(
Wager et al. 2013
)
, where the authors demonstrate a similar regularization effect on the weights when training a stochastic predictor. On small vision tasks, we notice that PFT consistently boosts the accuracy of existing One-Shot Pruning methods (
Section

4
), however, the computational cost of PFT is no less than ordinary training and so scales with network size. Adapting PFT for large scale tasks is an interesting topic for future work.

Motivated by the apparent robustness of pruned networks to perturbations of the weights, we introduced PAC-Bayes Pruning (PBP), which is a self-bounded learning algorithm with data-dependent priors, aimed at training a sparse
stochastic
network. In the linear model context, we demonstrate that the tightness of a data-dependent PAC-Bayes bound obtained via PBP depends on how the feature alignment changes as we add more data to the learning problem.
In our experiments, we obtained tight numerical bounds for sparse DNNs on a variety of datasets as demonstrated in
Section

7
. However, we observe that stochastic predictors seem to consistently under-perform their deterministic counterparts. The role of product prior distributions on the weights and pruning probabilities is worth investigating. The consistent drop in performance seen when pruning weights highlights the importance of considering the interaction between weights when pruning.

Posterior probability mapping.

Optimizing the posterior probabilities

\lambda_{i}=\textrm{Sig}(\lambda_{i}^{\prime})

•

The Clamp function:

\breve{\ell}

For some

\tilde{R}_{S}(Q)=\lambda_{i}e_{i}(1)+(1-\lambda_{i})e_{i}(0)

this yields

\partial_{\lambda_{i}}\tilde{R}_{S}(Q)=e_{i}(1)-e_{i}(0)

and

B.3
Reparameterization trick and Gumbel-Softmax

The empirical risk
\tilde{R}_{S}(Q)
depends on
\lambda
in an implicit way, thus, it is not straightforward to compute the gradient
\partial_{\lambda}\tilde{R}_{S}(Q)
using Monte Carlo samples.
A classic approach is the
reparameterization trick
which consists of finding a mapping that makes the dependence on
\lambda
explicit.
In the case of a discrete distribution (Bernoulli in our case), a common reparameterization trick is Gumbel–Max (GM) trick
(
Eric 2017
;
C.. & Teh 2017
)
, which can be traced back to
Luce 1959
.
It was introduced as a method for sampling from discrete random variables using explicit dependence on the probabilities of each state.
More precisely, consider a discrete representation
d\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=1
and let
(\lambda^{0}_{1},..,\lambda^{0}_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{n}
denote an unnormalized probability vector.
The GM trick can then be described by the following steps:

1.

sample
U_{k}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
i.i.d. for
k\in[n]
;

2.

find
k=\arg\max_{k}\{\log(\lambda^{0}_{k})-\log(-\log(U_{k}))\}
, set
D_{k}=1
and
D_{i}=0
for
i\neq k
.

Then, we have

\mathbb{P}(D_{k}=1)=\frac{\lambda^{0}_{k}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{0}_{i}}.

The name Gumbel–Max trick was inspired by the fact that the random variable
-\log(-\log(U))
has a Gumbel distribution.

Although the GM trick allows straightforward simulation of discrete variables, it is not practical for gradient computing since the the differentiation of the
\max
function might cause numerical issues. For this purpose,
Maddison et al. 2017
introduced the Gumbel–Softmax (GS) trick which relaxes the discrete distribution to the
concrete
distribution. It proceeds as follows:

•

fix
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
and sample
G_{k}\sim\textrm{Gumbel}
i.i.d.;

•

set
X_{k}=\frac{\exp{((\log(\lambda^{0}_{k})+G_{k})/\beta)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp{((\log(\lambda^{0}_{i})+G_{i})/\beta)}}.

Then,
X
has a concrete distribution which we denote
Concrete(\lambda^{0}_{1},...,\lambda^{0}_{n})
.
Further, it follows that

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\mathbb{P}(X_{k}>X_{i}\textrm{ for all }i\neq k)=\frac{\lambda^{0}_{k}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{0}_{i}}.

The relaxed problem.

In our case, given a sequence of independent random variables
X_{i}\sim Concrete(\lambda_{i},1-\lambda_{i})
,
we optimize the relaxed empirical risk, given by

\hat{R}_{S}((X_{i},W_{i})_{1\leq i\leq p}).
We take
\beta=0.5
throughout this work.

B.4

With the spike and slab prior

and posterior

, the KL divergence is tractable and is given by

\begin{split}\mathrm{KL}(Q\,\|\,P)&=\sum_{i=1}^{D}KL(q_{i}||p_{i})\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})+\lambda_{i}\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{N}(W^{f}_{i},s_{i}^{2})\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(W^{0}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}))}_{\gamma_{i}},\end{split}

where for all

,

\displaystyle\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda_{i})\log\left(\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{1-\lambda^{0}_{i}}\right)+\lambda_{i}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda^{0}_{i}}\right),

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(W_{i}^{f}-W_{i}^{0})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\log(\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}})-1\right).

C.1.1
Pruning probabilities are aligned with weight magnitude growth

Here we show that early stopping induces a pruning criterion that is aligned with the magnitude of the weights. In linear regression with diagonal feature covariance, the induced pruning criterion is based on variance-normalized feature alignment.

From
Eq.

8
, we can conclude the following: the first gradient term increases
\lambda_{i}
when
the gradient of the empirical risk of
\overline{W}
pushes the weight magnitude up, and decreases otherwise.
The regularization term drives
\lambda_{i}
to zero.

Note that the weights minimizing the empirical risk
\hat{R}_{S}(w)
under square loss are

w_{i}=\frac{1}{|S|}\Sigma^{-1}(X)\psi_{i}(X)^{T}Y,

(9)

In linear regression, the weight gradient depends on the feature alignment – large feature alignment magnitude yields large magnitude weights (up to rescaling based on
\Sigma_{P}
), as seen from
Eq.

9
.
When
\Sigma_{P}
is diagonal,
at convergence

w_{i}=(\Sigma_{P})_{ii}^{-1}A_{i}(P)
and
\frac{\partial\hat{R}_{S_{P}}(P)}{\partial\lambda^{0}_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}(w_{i}^{P})^{2}
.
If we start by initializing the pruning probabilities all to the same value,
then
\lambda^{0}_{i}
’s with larger (always non-negative) gradient increase more rapidly, and thus can be interpreted as being more important.
With early stopping, this yields a pruning criterion based on the magnitude of feature alignment, normalized by
(\Sigma_{P})^{2}_{ii}
.
In other words, the weights that have the highest feature alignment (after adjusting for the variance), are the ones that are not pruned. Magnitude-based pruning and feature alignment connections were recently described by
Elesedy et al. 2020
.

Non-diagonal covariance.

We extend the analysis when the features are correlated. Assume
(\Sigma_{P})_{ij}=(\Sigma_{P})_{ji}\neq 0
for some
i\neq j
and assume
(\Sigma_{P})_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=0
for all other
i^{\prime}\neq i,j^{\prime},\,j^{\prime}\neq j
. We now compute the ratio of
{\lambda^{0}_{i}}/{\lambda^{0}_{j}}
, indicating the relative importance of
w_{i}
and
w_{j}
.

Setting the gradient
\frac{\partial\hat{R}_{S_{P}}(P)}{\partial\lambda^{0}_{i}}
to 0, if
w_{i}\neq 0
, we have

\lambda^{0}_{j}=\frac{2A_{i}(P)-w_{i}(\Sigma_{P})_{ii}}{2w_{j}(\Sigma_{P})_{ij}}.

(10)

Using
Eq.

9
, we can also write
w_{i}
for this special

case as

w_{i}=(\Sigma_{P})_{ii}A_{i}(P)-(\Sigma_{P})_{ij}A_{j}(P).

(11)

Then for
(\Sigma_{P})_{ii}=(\Sigma_{P})_{jj}=1
, by substituting
Eq.

11
to
Eq.

10
, we can express the ratio as

\frac{\lambda^{0}_{i}}{\lambda^{0}_{j}}=\frac{(c_{ji}+(\Sigma_{P})_{ij})(1/c_{ji}-(\Sigma_{P})_{ij})}{(c_{ji}-(\Sigma_{P})_{ij})(1/c_{ji}+(\Sigma_{P})_{ij})},

(12)

where
c_{ji}=\frac{A_{j}(P)}{A_{i}(P)}
.
7
7

7

Note, that in
Eq.

12
, to obtain valid values, we need to take into account that the magnitudes of
(\Sigma_{P})_{ij}
and

are dependent.

Studying
Eq.

, we conclude the following:

•

if
\mathrm{sign}(A_{i}(P))\cdot\mathrm{sign}(A_{j}(P))=\mathrm{sign}(\Sigma_{P})_{ij}

•

otherwise, the weight with larger feature alignment is more likely to be pruned.

Intuitively, if the correlation

C.2
Optimizing the PAC-Bayes bound as constrained Feature Realignment

In this section, we analyze a three-stage process, the first two steps being probabilistic fine-tuning:

1.

Train the linear model with
(X_{P},Y_{P})
.
Let
w^{P}
denote the trained weights.

2.

Learn pruning probabilities
\{\lambda^{0}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}
by minimizing
\hat{R}_{S}(P)
, where
P
is a product distribution
\prod_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}
, with
p_{i}=(1-\lambda^{0}_{i})\delta_{\{0\}}+\lambda^{0}_{i}\delta_{\{w^{P}_{i}\}}
.

3.

Optimize a PAC-Bayes bound with prior P over the
D
weights with respect to a product posterior
Q
, where the posterior distribution on each weight
i\in[D]
is of the form

q_{i}=(1-\lambda_{i})\delta_{\{0\}}+\lambda_{i}\delta_{\{w^{P}_{i}\}}
.
(That is, we optimize over the
\lambda_{i}
’s.)

To ease our analysis, we consider a linear PAC-Bayes bound due to
(
Catoni 2007
)
.
The resulting optimization objective for our choice of prior and posterior is

B(\lambda)=\hat{R}_{S}(Q)+\frac{\zeta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\mathrm{KL}(q_{i}\,\|\,p_{i}),

(13)

where
\mathrm{KL}(q_{i}\,\|\,p_{i})=\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})
(see
Section

B.4
).

Using
Eq.

8
, we express

\frac{\partial B}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=w^{P}_{i}\frac{\partial\hat{R}_{S_{\smash{\bar{P}}}}(\overline{W})}{\partial\overline{W}_{i}}+r^{0}_{i}+r^{\lambda^{0}}_{i},

(14)

where
r^{\lambda^{0}}_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}
is the KL penalty.
The penalty term acts as a regularizer, forcing
\lambda_{i}
to stay close to its prior value
\lambda^{0}_{i}
.

Fig.

5
illustrates this effect: when
\lambda_{i}>\lambda^{0}_{i}
, the penalty term is positive, driving the value of
\lambda_{i}
to be lower, and vice versa. This is in addition to the regularization coming from
r^{0}_{i}
, which drives
\lambda_{i}
to zero.

In contrast to the analysis in
Section

3.1
, here the gradient of the empirical risk of
\overline{W}
is computed on the new data
S_{\smash{\bar{P}}}
. If the sign of the gradient on
S_{\smash{\bar{P}}}
disagrees with the sign of the weight (that was optimized on
S_{P}
),
\lambda_{i}
will be driven to zero. Otherwise,
\lambda_{i}
will increase.

When
\Sigma(X_{\bar{P}})=\mathbb{I}
, this translates to the sign of the feature alignment
A_{i}(\bar{P})
matching the sign of
w^{P}_{i}
.
Further, if
w^{P}_{i}
is the minimizer of the empirical risk of a deterministic predictor, then
w_{i}=A_{i}(P)
.
In this case,
\lambda_{i}
is driven to zero if
\mathrm{sign}(A_{i}(P))\neq\mathrm{sign}(A_{i}(\bar{P}))
, and to 1, otherwise.

C.2.1
Closed-form solution for uncorrelated features

Assume
\Sigma(X_{\bar{P}})=\Sigma(X_{P})=\mathbb{I}
and let
\kappa=\frac{\zeta}{N-M}
.
The gradient of
\lambda_{i}
’s when minimizing
Eq.

13
is given by

\frac{d\lambda_{i,t}}{dt}=-\mu\left(\Delta_{i}+\kappa\,\frac{\partial\mathrm{KL}(q_{i}\,\|\,p_{i})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\right),

(15)

where
\mu>0
is the learning rate

The KL divergence above is reduced to that of a Bernoulli variable, and for each
i\in[D]
is given by

\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})=\lambda_{i}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda^{0}_{i}}\right)+(1-\lambda_{i})\log\left(\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{1-\lambda^{0}_{i}}\right),

with derivative

\frac{\partial\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda^{0}_{i}}\right)-\log\left(\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{1-\lambda^{0}_{i}}\right).

The penalty in the objective
Eq.

13
coming from this KL term and its derivative are plotted in
Fig.

5
as a function of
\lambda_{i}
’s, posterior pruning probabilities, for a few different values of
\lambda^{0}_{i}
.

:

The gradient flow of
\lambda_{i}
minimizing the bound converges to the unique solution given by

\lambda_{i,\infty}=\frac{\lambda^{0}_{i}}{\lambda^{0}_{i}+(1-\lambda^{0}_{i})\exp(\kappa^{-1}\Delta_{i})},

(16)

where
\Delta_{i}=r_{i}^{0}-w^{P}_{i}A_{i}(\bar{P})
, i.e., the first two terms in

For
\Delta_{i}=0
, we recover the value
\lambda_{i,\infty}=\lambda^{0}_{i}
.

Approximations.

For particular values of the drift,
\lambda_{i,\infty}
can be approximated with Taylor expansion:

•

|\Delta_{i}|\ll 1
:
\lambda_{i,\infty}\approx\lambda^{0}_{i}(1+\kappa^{-1}\Delta_{i}(1-\lambda^{0}_{i}))
;

•

\Delta_{i}\ll-1

\lambda_{i,\infty}\approx(1-(\lambda^{0}_{i})^{-1}(1-\lambda^{0}_{i})\exp(\kappa^{-1}\Delta_{i}))
;

•

\Delta_{i}\gg 1
:
\lambda_{i,\infty}\approx\lambda^{0}_{i}(1-\lambda^{0}_{i})^{-1}\exp(-\kappa^{-1}\Delta_{i})
.

In words, when the drift magnitude is large, the bias coming from the value of
\lambda^{0}_{i}
learned on the data
X_{P}
diminishes as
N-M
or
|\Delta_{i}|
grow:

\lambda_{i,\infty}
tends to 1 (large negative drift) or 0 (large positive drift).

C.2.2
Concentration of feature alignment as a measure of data distribution complexity

The tightness of the PAC-Bayes generalization guarantee is determined by the size of
\kappa\sum_{i}\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})
.
Here we state the dependence of the KL term on the drift term.

Lemma 0
.

Under certain approximations, the tightness of the data-dependent PAC-Bayes bound on the generalization error scales with the drift term:

•

with
|\Delta_{i}|\ll 1
, the bound is quadratic in
\Delta_{i}
;

•

with
|\Delta_{i}|\gg 1
, the bound is linear in
\Delta_{i}
.

For the details, see
Section

C.3
.

C.3
The dependence of KL on the drift term in a linear model

The tightness of the PAC-Bayes generalization guarantee is determined by the size of
\kappa\sum_{i}\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})
.
Substituting the optimal
\lambda_{i}
from
Eq.

16
yields

\begin{split}\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})=&-\log(\exp(\eta_{i})(1-\lambda^{0}_{i})+\lambda^{0}_{i})\\
&+\eta_{i}\frac{\exp(\eta)}{\exp(\eta_{i})+\frac{\lambda^{0}_{i}}{1-\lambda^{0}_{i}}},\end{split}

(17)

where
\eta_{i}=\kappa^{-1}\Delta_{i}

We bound
Eq.

17
separately for small and large drift
\Delta_{i}
.

When
\Delta_{i}
, and therefore
\eta_{i}
, is large positive, then

\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})\approx\Delta_{i}\lambda^{0}\kappa^{-1}.

When
\Delta_{i}
is large negative, then

\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})\approx\Delta_{i}\kappa^{-1}\frac{\lambda^{0}_{i}}{1-\lambda^{0}_{i}}-\log\lambda^{0}_{i}.

In both cases, large magnitude of the drift terms results in a linear dependence of the bound on
\Delta_{i}
.

When the magnitude of the drift term is small, up to the error introduced by a first order Taylor approximation,
\lambda_{i,\infty}=\lambda^{0}_{i}(1+\kappa^{-1}\Delta_{i}(1-\lambda^{0}_{i}))
. This yields

\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})=\lambda^{0}_{i}a_{i}\log a_{i}-(1-\lambda^{0}_{i})b_{i}\log b_{i},

(18)

where
a_{i}=1-\eta_{i}\lambda^{0}_{i}+\eta_{i}
and
b_{i}=1-\eta_{i}\lambda^{0}_{i}
.
Assuming
\eta_{i}<1
, we can bound
\log a_{i}\leq\eta_{i}(1-\lambda^{0}_{i})
and, similarly,
\log b_{i}\leq\eta_{i}\lambda^{0}_{i}
. Substituting these log bounds to
Eq.

18
, we obtain

\mathrm{kl}(\lambda_{i}\,\|\,\lambda^{0}_{i})\leq\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\lambda^{0}_{i}(1-\lambda^{0}_{i}).

(19)

In summary, the tightness of the data-dependent PAC-Bayes bound on the generalization error scales with the drift term:

•

with
|\Delta_{i}|\ll 1
, the bound is quadratic in
\Delta_{i}
;

•

with
|\Delta_{i}|\gg 1
, the bound is linear in
\Delta_{i}
.

C.4
Overparameterized neural networks as linear models

Recent work by
Jacot et al. 2018
has shown that training a depth
L
neural networks with gradient flow is equivalent to a functional gradient flow with respect to the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) defined by

\Theta^{L}_{t}(x,x^{\prime})=\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta_{t})\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\theta_{t})^{T},

where
f
is the network output,
\theta
are the parameters, and
t
is the training time.
In the infinite width limit, the kernel
\Theta^{L}
becomes independent of
t
and training the network with quadratic loss is equivalent to the following linear model

f_{t}(x)=f_{0}(x)+\gamma(x,X_{P})(I-e^{-\frac{1}{N}\hat{\Theta}^{L}t})(Y_{P}-f_{0}(X_{P})),

(20)

where
\hat{\Theta}^{L}=\Theta^{L}(X_{P},X_{P})
,
f_{0}
is the network output at initialization, and
\gamma(x,X_{P})=\Theta^{L}(x,X_{P})(\hat{\Theta}^{L})^{-1}
.
Consider a zero init, i.e.,
f_{0}=0
, then, in the limit of infinite width, training the network with gradient flow on
S_{P}
is equivalent to training the linear model with the feature map given by
\Psi(x)=\{\Theta^{L}(x,x^{\prime})\}_{x^{\prime}\in S_{P}}
. Thus, by considering this alternative linear model, all the results on the linear model in this section apply. Note that the most practically relevant setting for pruning is the wide-but-finite DNN setting, where it has been shown that the linear DNN approximation holds around parameter initialization under a certain parameterization or learning rate regime
(
Lee et al. 2019
)
.

Performance–risk bound tightness trade-off

The KL divergence term plays a crucial role in the PAC-Bayes bound optimization.
To capture the impact of the KL term, we run multiple experiments where we down-weigh the KL term in the bound, i.e., we multiply the KL term by a factor

Q
is able to move away from
P
.

F.4
PairPlot analysis for PBP

Here we seek to evaluate how various quantities appearing in PBP optimization interact with each other.
Pairplot analysis is a valuable tool to depict such interactions.
Fig.

15
shows a pairplot of 4 quantities: PAC-Bayes bound , test error of the posterior
Q
, the fraction of the weight used to train the prior
P
, and the normalized KL divergence.
The first noticeable effect is how the choice of alpha impacts the normalized KL divergence. Indeed, the histogram of the normalized KL divergence shows two bulks of values, each corresponding to a value of alpha. Overall, larger alpha tends to yield smaller KL divergence. This can be attributed to the fact that using more data to train the prior would allow the posterior not to move far from the prior in order to find a good trade-off between the KL term and the empirical risk.

Another interesting observation is the almost perfect linear relationship between the posterior test error and the PAC-Bayes bound. This suggests that the KL term is usually negligible in comparison with posterior risk, showing that posterior training favors localized optimization (i.e., concentrated around the prior).
This is also confirmed by the plot of the PAC-Bayes bound as a function of the normalized KL divergence; on average, high KL divergence yields worse PAC-Bayes bounds, confirming that fact that PBP optimizes posterior weights and pruning probabilities in the neighborhood of the prior.

F.5
Is the deterministic mask really deterministic?

It is well known that with standard pruning methods, the resulting sparse architecture depends on the initialization of the weights and data reshuffling seed. The dependence on the initialization is natural and expected: a weight that was initialized to be very small compared to others will likely remain small after training, especially in sufficiently overparameterized neural networks trained with small learning rates.
However, the dependence of the mask on the data shuffling seed is interesting and suggests that there may be no single optimal sparse architecture, and a randomized mask might be better.
We train a network multiple times with a fixed initialization. Each run has a different data shuffling seed.
We keep track of the probability of the weight being pruned.

(0,1)
.
We show the standard deviation of these weights as a function of their average magnitude across the runs.
