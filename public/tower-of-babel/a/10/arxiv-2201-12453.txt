The Arithmetic Partial Derivative


Abstract
The arithmetic partial derivative (with respect to a prime p p ) is a function from the set of integers that sends p p to 1 and satisfies the Leibniz rule. In this paper, we prove that the p p -adic valuation of the sequence of higher order partial derivatives is eventually periodic. We also prove a criterion to determine when an integer has integral anti-partial derivatives. As an application, we show that there are infinitely many integers with exactly n n integral anti-partial derivatives for any nonnegative integer n n .

Introduction

Let
p
be a prime number. We recall the
p
-adic valuation
\mathrm{ord}_{p}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
. For any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)=\begin{cases}\max\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:p^{\ell}\mid x\},&\text{if $x\neq 0$};\\
+\infty,&\text{if $x=0$.}\end{cases}

In other words, if
x\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)
is the largest integer
\ell
such that
p^{\ell}\mid x
. We sometimes denote this by
p^{\ell}\;||\;x
.

The arithmetic derivative of a nonzero integer
x
is a function
D:\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}\to\mathbb{Z}
defined as

D(x)=x\sum_{p|x}\frac{\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)}{p}.

If
x=p^{k}
is a prime power, then

D(p^{k})=p^{k}\cdot\frac{k}{p}=kp^{k-1}.

This shows that
D
mimics the power rule for differentiable functions on prime powers. One can check that
D
satisfies the Leibniz rule
D(xy)=xD(y)+yD(x)
, and sends every prime to
1
. By the Leibniz rule, we are able to extend
D
uniquely to
\mathbb{Z}
by defining
D(0)=0
.

Ufnarovski and Åhlander
[
6
]
made several conjectures related to this arithmetic derivative, two of which we will investigate here. The first conjecture relates to the dynamical system by considering higher order arithmetic derivatives of an integer. We denote by
D^{i}(x)
the
i
-th arithmetic derivative of
x
for
i\geq 1
.

We note that the first conjecture that we would like to address is not correct as stated. The original conjecture
[
6
, Conjecture 2]
states that for any positive integer
x
, exactly one of the following could happen: either
D^{i}(x)=0
for sufficiently large
i
,
\displaystyle\lim_{i\to+\infty}D^{i}(x)=+\infty
, or
x=p^{p}
for some prime
p
. The fact that
D(1647082)=823543=7^{7}
means that it is not quite true. We modify it slightly here to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

For any positive integer
x
, exactly one of the following could happen: either
D^{i}(x)=0
or
p^{p}
for some prime
p
for sufficiently large
i
, or
\displaystyle\lim_{i\to+\infty}D^{i}(x)=+\infty
.

The second conjecture relates to the number of anti-derivatives of an integer. An integer
y
is said to be an (integral) anti-derivative of
x
if
D(y)=x
. Unlike anti-derivatives of functions, for any positive integer
x>1
,
x
has only finitely many anti-derivatives
[
6
, Corollary 3]
. We denote by
i(x)
the number of anti-derivatives of an integer
x
.

Conjecture 2
.

For each nonnegative integer
n
, the number of solutions to the equation
i(x)=n
is infinite.

Part of what makes investigating the function
D(x)
challenging is that the derivative of an integer
x
will often have prime factors that are not factors of
x
itself. For instance, if
x=72=2^{3}\,3^{2}
, then
D(x)=156=2^{2}\,3^{1}\,13
, and we see that while the multiplicities of the prime factors
2
and
3
each decreased by
1
, the derivative
D(x)
has a factor of
13
. In particular, when investigating the dynamical system
D^{i}(x)
, we would have to keep track of new prime factors that may arise following each successive differentiation. If
x
has more than one prime factor, predicting new prime factors of
D(x)
relies on the ability of predicting prime factors of
a+b
when knowing the prime factors of
a
and
b
. A difficult conjecture that tries to tackle a question in this direction is the famous
abc
conjecture. There is still ongoing debate in the mathematics community about whether
abc
conjecture has been proved
[
5
]
. Loosely speaking, the
abc
conjecture implies that when two numbers
a
and
b
are divisible by large powers of small primes, then
a+b
tends to be divisible by small powers of large primes. Because of difficulties like these, we start by investigating these conjectures in the case of arithmetic partial derivatives with respect to a prime
p
, which was formally introduced by Kovič
[
3
]
.

For any nonzero
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, the (arithmetic) partial derivative (with respect to
p
) is defined as

D_{p}(x)=\frac{x}{p}\cdot\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x).

One can check that
D_{p}
satisfies the Leibniz rule
D_{p}(xy)=D_{p}(x)y+xD_{p}(y)
. And to extend the definition of
D_{p}
to
0
, we observe that for any nonzero
x\in\mathbb{Z}

D_{p}(0)=D_{p}(0\cdot x)=D_{p}(0)\cdot x+0\cdot D_{p}(x)=D_{p}(0)\cdot x.

This implies that
D_{p}(0)=0
. If
p
does not divide
x
, then
D_{p}(x)=0
. For every integer
x
, the arithmetic derivative of
x
is a finite sum of arithmetic partial derivative of
x
, that is,

D(x)=\sum_{p\mid x}D_{p}(x).

The main advantage that we have in exploring the dynamical system
D_{p}^{i}(x)
over
D^{i}(x)
is that now we do not need to keep track of any new primes that may arise when we differentiate. This is because if
x=ap^{\ell}
, where
\ell=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)
, then
D_{p}(x)=aD_{p}(p^{\ell})
, and so upon repeated differentiation, we only need to keep track of
\mbox{ord}_{p}(x)
and its partial derivatives with respect to
p
. Any new prime factors that may arise when differentiating get absorbed into the coefficient.

Because we do not have to deal with new primes occurring in our dynamical system, we are able to prove a stronger version of Conjecture
1
for the arithmetic partial derivative. Theorem
8
provides an exact prescription of the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of
x
when
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)\geq p
, and as a corollary we prove Theorem
10
, which is analogous to Conjecture
1
; see Section
2
for the definition of
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence. We go on to prove in Theorem
11
that given any possible
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence as prescribed in Theorem
8
, we are able to construct an integer
x
with this
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence.

For each integer
x
, we say that an integer
y
is an (integral) anti-partial derivative of
x
if
D_{p}(y)=x
. Let
i_{p}(x)
be the number of anti-partial derivatives of
x
. Theorem
21
and Example
13
together show that for each nonnegative integer
n
, the number of solutions to the equation
i_{p}(x)=n
is infinite, which is analogous to Conjecture
2
. We think the natural next step is to study analogous conjectures for the arithmetic subderivative
D_{S}
defined by Haukkanen, Merikoski, and Tossavainen
[
2
]
in the following way:

D_{S}(x):=x\sum_{p\in S}\frac{\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)}{p}=\sum_{p\in S}D_{p}(x),

where
S
is a finite set of primes.

The definition of arithmetic derivative and arithmetic partial derivative can be generalized to rational numbers
[
6
,
1
]
. Haukkanen, Merikoski, and Tossavainen
[
1
, Conjecture 27]
conjecture that for any nonzero rational number
x
, there are at most four rational anti-partial derivatives if
p=2
, and at most two rational anti-partial derivatives if
p>2
. As a result of Theorem
21
, we prove that this conjecture is false; see Remark
22
. This also confirms Pandey and Saxena’s earlier observations
[
4
]
that
[
1
, Conjecture 27]
might be false.

2
Period of the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence

We fix a prime
p
. For any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we refer to the sequence
x,D_{p}(x),D_{p}^{2}(x),\ldots
as the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
, and the sequence
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x),\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}(x)),\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}^{2}(x)),\ldots
as the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of
x
. In this section, we will study properties of
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequences and show that they are eventually periodic. For the sake of completeness, we first give the definition of eventually periodic.

Definition 3
.

Given a sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
, we say that it is
eventually periodic
if there exist integers
m,L\geq 1
such that for all
0\leq j\leq L-1
and
i\geq 1
, we have
x_{m+j+iL}=x_{m+j}
. If a sequence is eventually periodic with
m=1
, then we say the sequence is
periodic
. The smallest possible
L
is called the
period
of the sequence.

If the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
is periodic (resp., eventually periodic), then the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of
x
is periodic (resp., eventually periodic), but not vice versa.

Lemma 4
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
x=0
, then the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
(and thus the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of
x
) is periodic with period 1. If
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)<p
, then the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
(and thus the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of
x
) is eventually periodic with period 1.

Proof.

If
x=0
, then
D_{p}(x)=0
and thus
D_{p}^{i}(x)=0
for all
i\geq 0
. Therefore the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
is periodic of period
1
.

Suppose
x\neq 0
. We write
x=ap^{\ell}
where
p\nmid a
and
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)=\ell<p
. We can compute that
D_{p}^{\ell}(x)=a\ell!
. Since
0\leq\ell<p
, we know that
p\nmid a\ell!
. This implies that
D_{p}^{\ell+1}(x)=0
and thus
D_{p}^{i}(x)=0
for all
i\geq\ell+1
. Therefore the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
is eventually periodic of period
1
.
∎

Suppose
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}^{i}(x))\neq+\infty
for all
i\geq 1
. To understand how each term in
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence changes, we study the sequence of increments (of consecutive terms) of the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence

\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}(x))-\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x),\;\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}^{2}(x))-\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}(x)),\;\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}^{3}(x))-\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}^{2}(x)),\;\ldots

and we call this the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
. Recall that for any integer
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
x\bmod p
is the unique integer between 0 and
p-1
that is congruent to
x
modulo
p
. We denote
\lfloor x\rfloor_{p}:=x-(x\bmod p)
. In other words,
\lfloor x\rfloor_{p}
is the largest multiple of
p
that is less than or equal to
x
.

We are ready to handle the case
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)\geq p
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
be a nonzero integer with
p\nmid ab
and
k\geq 1
. Define
k^{\prime}:=(k-1\bmod p)+1
. The first
k^{\prime}
terms of the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
are

(k-1,\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{(k-1\bmod p)\text{ copies}}).

Proof.

Since
D_{p}(x)=abp^{bp^{k}+k-1}
and
p\nmid ab
, we know
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}(x))=bp^{k}+k-1
. If
k^{\prime}\geq 2
, then
D_{p}^{2}(x)=ab(bp^{k}+k-1)p^{bp^{k}+k-2}
. Since
p\nmid ab(bp^{k}+k-1)
, we know
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D^{2}_{p}(x))=bp^{k}+k-2
. Following this pattern, we know that when
1\leq i\leq k^{\prime}
, the
(i+1)
-th term of the
D_{p}
sequence is

ab\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(bp^{k}+k-j)p^{bp^{k}+k-i}

because
p\nmid ab\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(bp^{k}+k-j)
for any
1\leq i\leq k^{\prime}
. The first
k^{\prime}+1
terms of the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence are

(bp^{k},bp^{k}+k-1,bp^{k}+k-2,\ldots,bp^{k}+k-k^{\prime}).

Therefore the first
k^{\prime}
terms of the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
are

(k-1,\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{(k-1\bmod p)\text{ copies}}).\qed

Corollary 6
.

Let
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
be a nonzero integer with
p\nmid ab
and
1\leq k\leq p
. The
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence and the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
is periodic of period
k
.

Proof.

If
1\leq k\leq p
, then
k^{\prime}=(k-1\bmod p)+1=k-1+1=k
. The first
k+1
terms of the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence are

(bp^{k},bp^{k}+k-1,bp^{k}+k-2,\ldots,bp^{k}).

It is now clear that the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence and the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
is periodic of period
k
.
∎

Definition 7
.

For any integer
k\geq 1
, we call the following sequence

\mathcal{S}_{k}:=(k-1,\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{(k-1\bmod p)\text{ copies}})

the
k
-segment
.

For any integer
\ell\geq p
, we define
\ell_{1}:=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor\ell\rfloor_{p})
and for
i\geq 2

\ell_{i}:=\begin{cases}\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor\ell_{i-1}-1\rfloor_{p}),&\text{if $\ell_{i-1}<+\infty$};\\
+\infty,&\text{if $\ell_{i-1}=+\infty$}.\end{cases}

(1)

It is clear that if
\ell_{i}<+\infty
, then
\ell_{i+1}<\log_{p}(\ell_{i})
. For any integer
\ell\geq p
, there exists a unique positive integer
N=N(\ell)
such that
1\leq\ell_{N}\leq p
, and
\ell_{i}=+\infty
for all
i>N
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
x=ap^{\ell}
be a nonzero integer with
\ell\geq p
and
p\nmid a
.The
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
is of the form

(\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{(\ell\bmod p)\text{ copies}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{1}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{2}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{3}},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\ldots).

As a result, the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence and the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
are eventually periodic of period
\ell_{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell^{\prime}_{0}=\ell\bmod p
. Then the first
\ell^{\prime}_{0}+1
terms of the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
are

(ap^{\ell},a\ell p^{\ell-1},a\ell(\ell-1)p^{\ell-2},\ldots,a\prod_{i=1}^{\ell^{\prime}_{0}}(\ell-i)p^{\ell-\ell^{\prime}_{0}}).

The first
\ell^{\prime}_{0}+1
terms of the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of
x
are

(\ell,\ell-1,\ell-2,\ldots,\ell-\ell^{\prime}_{0}).

Hence the first
\ell^{\prime}_{0}
terms of the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
are

(\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{\ell^{\prime}_{0}\text{ copies}}).

Note that
\ell-\ell^{\prime}_{0}=\lfloor\ell\rfloor_{p}
and
\ell_{1}=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor\ell\rfloor_{p})
, we can write
D_{p}^{\ell^{\prime}_{0}}(x)=a_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}}
with
p\nmid a_{0}b_{0}
. We can compute
D_{p}^{\ell_{0}^{\prime}+1}(x)=a_{0}b_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}+\ell_{1}-1}
. Note that
b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}+\ell_{1}-1\bmod p=\ell_{1}-1\bmod p
.

Let
\ell^{\prime}_{1}:=(\ell_{1}-1)\bmod p
. The
(\ell^{\prime}_{0}+1)
-th term to the
(\ell^{\prime}_{0}+\ell^{\prime}_{1}+2)
-th term of the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
are

(a_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}},a_{0}b_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}+\ell_{1}-1},\ldots,a_{0}b_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell^{\prime}_{1}}(b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}+\ell_{1}-i)p^{b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}+\ell_{1}-1-\ell_{1}^{\prime}}).

The
(\ell^{\prime}_{0}+1)
-th term to the
(\ell^{\prime}_{0}+\ell^{\prime}_{1}+2)
-th term of the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of
x
are

(b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}},b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}+\ell_{1}-1,\ldots,b_{0}p^{\ell_{1}}+\ell_{1}-1-\ell_{1}^{\prime}).

The next
\ell_{1}^{\prime}+1
terms of the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
is the
\ell_{1}
-segment

(\ell_{1}-1,\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{\ell^{\prime}_{1}\text{ copies}})=\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{1}}.

As
\ell_{1}-1-\ell_{1}^{\prime}=\lfloor\ell_{1}-1\rfloor_{p}
and
\ell_{2}=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor\ell_{1}-1\rfloor_{p})
, we can write
D_{p}^{\ell_{0}^{\prime}+\ell_{1}^{\prime}+1}(x)=a_{1}p^{b_{1}p^{\ell_{2}}}
with
p\nmid a_{1}b_{1}
. There exists a positive integer
N
such that
1\leq\ell_{N}\leq p
. Using induction, we know that the initial terms of the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
is of the form

(\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{(\ell\bmod p)\text{ copies}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{1}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{2}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{3}},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}}).

Corollary
6
implies that if
a_{N-1}p^{b_{N-1}p^{\ell_{N}}}
, where
p\nmid a_{N-1}b_{N-1}
, is a term in the
D_{p}
sequence of
x
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}}
will appear repeatedly in the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

We summarize our results of Lemma
4
and Theorem
8
into the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

The
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of any integer
x
is eventually periodic of period at most
p
.

As an application of Theorem
8
, we can prove the following theorem that has a similar structure as Conjecture
1
.

Theorem 10
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, exactly one of the following three cases will happen:

(i)

D_{p}^{i}(x)=0
for sufficiently large
i
;

(ii)

D_{p}^{i}(x)=ap^{p}
where
\gcd(a,p)=1
for sufficiently large
i
;

(iii)

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to+\infty}D_{p}^{i}(x)=\pm\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose
x=0
or
0\leq\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)<p
. Lemma
4
implies that
D_{p}^{i}(x)=0
for sufficiently large
i
.

Suppose
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)\geq p
. Theorem
8
implies that the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence of
x
is eventually periodic of period
\ell_{N}
. Let
x_{1}=ap^{bp^{k}}
be the first term of the periodic cycle where
1\leq k\leq p
and
p\nmid ab
. If
b=k=1
, then
D_{p}(x_{1})=D_{p}(ap^{p})=ap^{p}=x_{1}
. We know that
D_{p}^{i}(x)=ap^{p}
for sufficiently large
i
. Suppose
b>1
or
k>1
. If
x_{1}>0
, then

x_{1}<D_{p}(x_{1})<D_{p}^{2}(x_{1})<\cdots<D_{p}^{\ell_{N_{0}}-1}(x_{1})<D_{p}^{\ell_{N_{0}}}(x_{1})=\alpha x_{1},

for some
\alpha>1
. Therefore

\lim_{i\to+\infty}D_{p}^{i}(x)=\lim_{i\to+\infty}\alpha^{i}x_{1}=+\infty.

Similarly, if
x_{1}<0
, then

x_{1}>D_{p}(x_{1})>D_{p}^{2}(x_{1})>\cdots>D_{p}^{\ell_{N_{0}}-1}(x_{1})>D_{p}^{\ell_{N_{0}}}(x_{1})=\alpha x_{1},

for some
\alpha>1
. Therefore

\lim_{i\to+\infty}D_{p}^{i}(x)=\lim_{i\to+\infty}\alpha^{i}x_{1}=-\infty.\qed

Theorem
8
gives the form of the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of any integer
x
such that
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)\geq p
. It is a sequence of nonnegative integers that rapidly decreases until it is less than
p
, after which it is constant, where the first term determines all subsequent terms. This sequence can be interrupted by strings of
-1
’s, where the length of the string is equal to the previous nonnegative integer modulo
p
.

The question remains about whether all sequences of the form

(\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{(\ell\bmod p)\text{ copies}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{1}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{2}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{3}},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\ldots)

has an
x
such that this is its
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence. Since the initial
\ell_{1}
determines all the subsequent
\ell_{i}
’s, the answer is no. But we do have some control over the length of the interrupting
-1
’s and the number of jumps
N
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
N>0
be an integer. For any
N+1
integers
0\leq i_{0},i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{N}\leq p-1
, there exists a positive integer
\ell
such that the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x=p^{\ell}
is of the form

(\underbrace{-1,-1,\ldots,-1}_{i_{0}\text{ copies}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{1}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{2}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{3}},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{N}},\ldots),

such that for all
1\leq j\leq N
,
\mathcal{S}_{\ell_{j}}
is the
\ell_{j}
-segment with
i_{j}
consecutive copies of
-1
.

Proof.

Given any sequence of
N+1
nonnegative integers
0\leq i_{0},i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{N}\leq p-1
, we construct a sequence
k_{N},k_{N-1},\dots,k_{1},k_{0}
as follows. Let
k_{N}=i_{N}+1
and
k_{j}=p^{k_{j+1}}+i_{j}+1
for
0\leq j\leq N-1
. Let
\ell:=k_{0}-1
and
x=p^{k_{0}-1}
. We first show that
\ell_{j}=k_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq N
. By definition of
\ell_{1}
, we can compute

\ell_{1}=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor\ell\rfloor_{p})=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor k_{0}-1\rfloor_{p})=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor p^{k_{1}}+i_{0}\rfloor_{p})=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(p^{k_{1}})=k_{1}.

Suppose for
2\leq j\leq m-1<N
, we have
\ell_{j}=k_{j}
. Then we have

\ell_{m}=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor\ell_{m-1}-1\rfloor_{p})=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor k_{m-1}-1\rfloor_{p})=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(\lfloor p^{k_{m}}+i_{m-1}\rfloor_{p})=\mathrm{ord}_{p}(p^{k_{m}})=k_{m}.

It remains to show that the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
is of the desired form. As
k_{0}-1=p^{k_{1}}+i_{0}
, it is clear that the first
i_{0}
terms of the
\mathrm{inc}_{p}
sequence of
x
are
-1
and
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D^{i_{0}}_{p}(x))=p^{k_{1}}
. Suppose
D_{p}^{i_{0}}(x)=\alpha p^{p^{k_{1}}}
. Set
k_{1}^{\prime}:=(k_{1}-1\bmod p)+1=(i_{1}\bmod p)+1=i_{1}+1
. By Lemma

terms of the

sequence is a

-segment

. The number of consecutive
-1
in this

-segment
\mathcal{S}_{k_{1}}
is equal to
k_{1}^{\prime}-1=i_{1}
as desired. The proof is complete by induction.
∎

For any nonzero integer
x=ap^{\ell}
with
p\nmid a
and
\ell\geq 1
, Theorem

implies that the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
sequence increases (not necessarily consecutively) exactly
N-1
times before it reaches the periodic cycle. Therefore it is impossible for the
\mathrm{ord}_{p}
-sequence to continue to increase indefinitely. However, we see that the number of jumps can be made arbitrarily large. And as the following corollary tells us, we can even prescribe these jumps to be consecutive.

Corollary 12

For any integer

, there exists

such that

\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)<\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}(x))<\cdots<\mathrm{ord}_{p}(D_{p}^{N}(x)).

Use Theorem

∎

3
Anti-partial derivatives

Not every integer has an anti-partial derivative with respect to
p
. We would like to have a criterion to determine when an integer does have an anti-partial derivative (Question 1). If we know an integer has an anti-partial derivative, a natural extension of Question 1 is how many anti-partial derivatives can it have (Question 3)? In order to answer that, it would be useful to know when two integers have the same partial derivatives (Question 2). Furthermore, if we know that there exists at least one integer that has exactly
n
anti-partial derivatives, then we can also ask how many integers there are that have exactly
n
anti-partial derivatives (Question 4). In this section, we will attempt to answer these four questions:

1.

When does an integer have an anti-partial derivative?

2.

When do two integers have the same partial derivatives?

3.

How many anti-partial derivatives can an integer have?

4.

For any positive integer
n
, how many integers that have exactly
n
anti-partial derivatives?

For any nonzero integer
x
, if
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)>0
, then there exist unique integers

,

, and
k\geq 0
with
p\nmid ab
such that
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
. We call this the
standard form
of

.

3.1
When does an integer have an anti-partial derivative?

We start this subsection with an example of a family of integers that does not have an anti-partial derivative.

Example 13
.

Suppose
y=a_{0}p^{p-1}
for some integer
a_{0}
where
p\nmid a_{0}
. If
D_{p}(x)=y
, then it is clear that
x\neq 0
and
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)>0
. Let

be in standard form such that

abp^{bp^{k}+k-1}=a_{0}p^{p-1}

. Therefore

and this contradicts to

a_{0}p^{p-1}
does not have an (integral) anti-partial derivative (with respect to

The main goal of this subsection is to determine when an integer
y
has an anti-partial derivative. We first consider two simple cases.

•

If

for all

with

.

•

If
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(y)=0
, then
D_{p}(x)=y
if and only if
x=yp
.

Now we assume that
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(y)>0
. If

, then
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)>0
.

Lemma 14

Let

where

and
\ell_{0}>0
. For any

in standard form,
D_{p}(x)=y
if and only if

\displaystyle ab

\displaystyle=a_{0},

(2)

\displaystyle bp^{k}+k-1

\displaystyle=\ell_{0}.

(3)

Proof.

Since
D_{p}(x)=abp^{bp^{k}+k-1}
, it is clear that
abp^{bp^{k}+k-1}=a_{0}p^{\ell_{0}}
if and only if (
2
) and (

) hold.
∎

Using Lemma
14
, we can show that any nonzero integer has finitely many anti-partial derivatives. This is already known as a result of
[

.

Corollary 15

For each nonzero

,

Proof.

If
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(y)=0
, then

if and only if

. If
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(y)>0
, then (

k\leq\log_{p}(\ell_{0})
. Since

is determined by

by (

) and

is determined by

such that (

∎

Corollary 16
.

For any two nonzero integers
x_{1}
and
x_{2}

\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x_{1}),\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x_{2})>0
, let
x_{1}=a_{1}p^{b_{1}p^{k_{1}}}
and
x_{2}=a_{2}p^{b_{2}p^{k_{2}}}
be in standard form such that

\text{(i) }k_{1}<k_{2}\qquad\text{(ii) }b_{1}p^{k_{1}}>b_{2}p^{k_{2}}\qquad\text{(iii) }b_{1}>b_{2}

If
D_{p}(x_{1})=D_{p}(x_{2})>0
, then the above three statements are further equivalent to the following two statements.

\text{(iv) }a_{1}<a_{2}\qquad\text{(v) }x_{1}<x_{2}

Proof.

If
D_{p}(x_{1})=D_{p}(x_{2})
, then we have

\displaystyle a_{1}b_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{2}b_{2}

(4)

\displaystyle b_{1}p^{k_{1}}+k_{1}-1

\displaystyle=b_{2}p^{k_{2}}+k_{2}-1,

(5)

(
5

) implies that (i) holds.

Now suppose

. Hence

If

3.2
When do two integers have the same partial derivatives?

Anti-partial derivatives do not exist uniquely. For example

D_{p}(p^{p^{p+1}+p})=D_{p}((p^{p}+1)p^{p^{p+1}}).

The main goal of this subsection is to determine when two integers have the same partial derivatives. If
D_{p}(x)=0
, then
x=0
or
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)=0
. This means that the set of anti-partial derivatives of 0 is

\{x\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,p\nmid x\}\,\cup\,\{0\}.

Now we suppose that
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(x)>0
and let
y=D_{p}(x)
. By Corollary
16
, there is an element
x_{0}
in the set of all anti-partial derivative of
y
with the smallest value
k
. We call
x_{0}
the
primitive
anti-partial derivative of
y
and denote
x_{0}=a_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{k_{0}}}
in the standard form.

Theorem 17
.

Let
x_{0}=a_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{k_{0}}}\in\mathbb{Z}
be the standard form of the primitive anti-partial derivative of
y=D_{p}(x_{0})
. Define

C=C(x_{0}):=\Big\{c\in[0,b_{0})\cap\mathbb{Z}\,:\,p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;(b_{0}-c),\;\frac{b_{0}-c}{p^{p^{k_{0}}c}}\mid a_{0}b_{0}\Big\}.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between
C
and the set of all anti-partial derivatives of
y
. Furthermore, if
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
(in its standard form) is an anti-partial derivative of
y
, then there exists a unique
c\in C
such that

k=p^{k_{0}}c+k_{0},\quad b=\frac{b_{0}-c}{p^{k-k_{0}}}=\frac{b_{0}-c}{p^{p^{k_{0}}c}}\in\mathbb{Z},\quad a=\frac{a_{0}b_{0}}{b}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

We show that any anti-partial derivative
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
of
y
is associated with a unique
c\in C
. If
x=x_{0}
, then we associated
x
with
c=0
. If
x\neq x_{0}
, since
x_{0}
is the primitive anti-partial derivative, we know that
k>k_{0}
. Then
p^{k_{0}}(b_{0}-bp^{k-k_{0}})=k-k_{0}
. Since
p\nmid bb_{0}
, we know that
p\nmid(b_{0}-bp^{k-k_{0}})
. Therefore
p^{k_{0}}\;||\;(k-k_{0})
. Let
k-k_{0}=p^{k_{0}}c
where
c>0
and
p\nmid c
. By plugging
k-k_{0}=p^{k_{0}}c
into
p^{k_{0}}(b_{0}-bp^{k-k_{0}})=k-k_{0}
, we get
b_{0}-bp^{k-k_{0}}=c
. Since
bp^{k-k_{0}}>0
, we know that
c<b_{0}
. Since
p\nmid b
, we know that
p^{k-k_{0}}\;||\;(b_{0}-c)
. Since
a\in\mathbb{Z}
, we also have
b\mid a_{0}b_{0}
, that is,
\frac{b_{0}-c}{p^{p^{k_{0}}c}}\mid a_{0}b_{0}
.

Then we show that for each
c\in C
, we can define a unique
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
such that
D_{p}(x)=y
. Since
c<b_{0}
and
p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;(b_{0}-c)
, we know that
b>0
is an integer and
\text{ord}_{p}(b)=0
. Since
b\mid a_{0}b_{0}
, we know that
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\text{ord}_{p}(a)=0
as well. We can compute

bp^{k}+k-1=\frac{b_{0}-c}{p^{k-k_{0}}}p^{k}+k-1=(b_{0}-c)p^{k_{0}}+p^{k_{0}}c+k_{0}-1=b_{0}p^{k_{0}}+k_{0}-1.

Therefore

D_{p}(x)=abp^{bp^{k}+k-1}=a_{0}b_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{k_{0}}+k_{0}-1}=D_{p}(x_{0})=y.\qed

3.3
How many anti-partial derivatives can an integer have?

By Corollary
15
, we know that every nonzero integer can only have finitely many anti-partial derivatives. This partially answers Question 3 but we can further ask for any given positive integer
n
, is there an integer
x
with exactly
n
anti-partial derivatives? The answer is yes. In order to prove that, we will construct
k_{0}
such that
x_{0}
is the primitive anti-partial derivative of
D_{p}(x_{0})
and then construct
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
so that
D_{p}(x_{0})
has exactly
n
anti-partial derivatives. We first show that for certain values of
k_{0}
,
x_{0}
is always the primitive anti-partial derivative of
D_{p}(x_{0})
no matter how
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
are defined as long as
p\nmid a_{0}b_{0}
.

Lemma 18
.

Fix an integer
m\geq 2
and let
k_{0}:=p+p^{2}+p^{3}+\cdots+p^{m}
. For any integers
a_{0},b_{0}
with
b_{0}>0
such that
p\nmid a_{0}b_{0}
, let
x_{0}:=a_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{k_{0}}}
. Then
x_{0}
is the primitive anti-partial derivative of
D_{p}(x_{0})
.

Proof.

Suppose
x_{0}
is not the primitive anti-partial derivative of
D_{p}(x_{0})
. Let
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
be an anti-partial derivative of
D_{p}(x_{0})
such that
0\leq k<k_{0}
. Since
D_{p}(x)=D_{p}(x_{0})
, we get
bp^{k}+k=b_{0}p^{k_{0}}+k_{0}
. This means that
p^{k}\;||\;k_{0}-k
. It is clear that
k\neq 0
because
p\;||\;k_{0}-0
. It is also clear that
k\neq 1
because
p\nmid k_{0}-1
. Suppose
k\geq 2
. If
p^{k}\;||\;k_{0}-\ell
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
\ell\geq p+p^{2}+\cdots p^{k-1}>k
. Therefore there does not exist an anti-partial derivative
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
with
k<k_{0}
. This means that
x_{0}
is the primitive anti-partial derivative of
D_{p}(x_{0})
.
∎

We now construct
b_{0}
such that there are exactly
n-1
different possible values of
c\in(0,b_{0})\cap\mathbb{Z}
such that
p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;(b_{0}-c)
for any
k_{0}\geq 0
. This means that the set
C(x_{0})
(as defined in Theorem
17
) has at most
n
elements (including
0
).

Lemma 19
.

Fix integers
n>0
and
k_{0}\geq 0
. Let
c_{1}=0
. For
2\leq i\leq n+1
, let
c_{i}:=p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{i-1}}+c_{i-1}
and
b_{0}:=c_{n+1}
. We have

\{c\in(0,b_{0})\cap\mathbb{Z}\;:\;p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;b_{0}-c\}=\{c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\}.

Proof.

First, we prove
\supseteq
. By the definition of
c_{j}
, we know that
c_{j+1}>c_{j}
and
p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{j}}\;||\;c_{j+1}-c_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq n
. For
1\leq i\leq n
, we have

b_{0}-c_{i}=\sum_{j=i}^{n}(c_{j+1}-c_{j})=\sum_{j=i}^{n}p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{j}}.

Therefore
p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{i}}\;||\;b_{0}-c_{i}
.

Second, we prove
\subseteq
. Let
c\in(0,b_{0})
be an integer such that
p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;b_{0}-c
. If
c\not\in\{c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\}
, then there exists a unique
3\leq i\leq n+1
such that
c_{i-1}<c<c_{i}
. As

b_{0}-c=\sum_{j=i}^{n}(c_{j+1}-c_{j})+(c_{i}-c)=\sum_{j=i}^{n}p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{j}}+(c_{i}-c).

Since
c<c_{j}
for all
i\leq j\leq n
, we have
p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\mid p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{j}}
. Since
p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;b_{0}-c
, we know that
p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;c_{i}-c
. Let
\ell>0
with
p\nmid\ell
such that
c_{i}=\ell p^{p^{k_{0}}c}+c
. By definition of
c_{i}
, we know that
c_{i}=p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{i-1}}+c_{i-1}
. This means that

\ell p^{p^{k_{0}}c}+c=p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{i-1}}+c_{i-1}.

Since
c_{i-1}<c
, we know that
\ell p^{p^{k_{0}}c}<p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{i-1}}
. But this is not possible as
\ell\geq 1
and
c>c_{i-1}
. Hence there does not exist an integer
c\in(0,b_{0})
such that
p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;b_{0}-c
and
c\notin\{c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\}
. This completes the proof of
\subseteq
.
∎

In order for
D_{p}(x_{0})
to have exactly
n
anti-partial derivatives, we want to construct
a_{0}
so that

\frac{b_{0}-c}{p^{p^{k_{0}}c}}\mid a_{0}b_{0}

for every
0<c<b_{0}
satisfying
p^{p^{k_{0}}c}\;||\;b_{0}-c
. This can be achieved by the next lemma.

Lemma 20
.

Fix integers
n>0
and
k_{0}\geq 0
. Let
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n+1}=b_{0}
be defined as in Lemma

. For each
2\leq i\leq n

a_{i}:=(b_{0}-c_{i})/p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{i}}
and let

. Then

(as defined in Theorem

) has exactly

Proof.

Lemma
19
shows that
C(x_{0})\subseteq\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\}
. For each
2\leq i\leq n
, we know that
(b_{0}-c_{i})/p^{p^{k_{0}}c_{i}}=a_{i}
divides

by the definition of
a_{0}
. Hence it divides

for

Remark 22
.

For any integer
x_{0}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{Z},p}(x_{0})
(resp.,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{Q},p}(x_{0})
) be the set of all integral (resp., rational) anti-partial derivatives of

implies that

is false.

Theorem

remains true even if we allow rational anti-partial derivatives. We give a brief explanation as follows. Let
x_{0}:=a_{0}p^{b_{0}p^{k_{0}}}
be defined as in Lemma
18
, so
x_{0}
is the primitive anti-partial derivative of
D_{p}(x_{0})
(even among all the rational anti-partial derivatives) with
b_{0}>0
. Let
x\neq x_{0}
be a rational anti-partial derivative of
D_{p}(x_{0})
. We can write
x=ap^{bp^{k}}
where

,

,

,

, and

. Since

is the primitive anti-partial derivative of

, we have

. As

, we have

, or

. If

, then

. This is a contradiction, so

. This is saying that if
x_{0}
is the primitive rational anti-partial derivative of

with

, then any other rational anti-partial derivative

of

also satisfies

. In this case, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of all rational anti-partial derivatives of

and the following set

If we compare

x_{0}
such that

has exactly
n

rational
anti-partial derivatives.
