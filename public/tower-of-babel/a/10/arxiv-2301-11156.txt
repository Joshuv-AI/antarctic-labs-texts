On Unifying Randomized Methods for Inverse Problems
Jonathan Wittmer, C G Krishnanunni, Hai V. Nguyen, Tan Bui-Thanh

Abstract
This work unifies the analysis of various randomized methods for solving linear and nonlinear inverse problems by framing the problem in a stochastic optimization setting. By doing so, we show that many randomized methods are variants of a sample average approximation. More importantly, we are able to prove a single theoretical result that guarantees the asymptotic convergence for a variety of randomized methods. Additionally, viewing randomized methods as a sample average approximation enables us to prove, for the first time, a single non-asymptotic error result that holds for randomized methods under consideration. Another important consequence of our unified framework is that it allows us to discover new randomization methods. We present various numerical results for linear, nonlinear, algebraic, and PDE-constrained inverse problems that verify the theoretical convergence results and provide a discussion on the apparently different convergence rates and the behavior for various randomized methods.

Introduction

Solving large-scale ill-posed inverse problems that are governed by
partial differential equations (PDEs), though tremendously
challenging, is of great practical
importance in science and engineering. Classical deterministic inverse methodologies, which provide
point estimates of the solution, are not capable of
accounting for the uncertainty in the inverse solution in a principled way. The Bayesian
formulation provides a systematic quantification of uncertainty by
posing the inverse problem as one of
statistical inference.
The Bayesian framework for inverse problems proceeds as follows: given
observational data
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and their uncertainty, the governing forward
problem and its uncertainty, and a prior probability density function
describing uncertainty in the parameters
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the solution of the inverse problems is the posterior
probability distribution
{\pi\left({\boldsymbol{u}}|\mathbf{d}\right)}
over the
parameters. Bayes’ Theorem explicitly gives the posterior density as

\displaystyle{\pi\left({\boldsymbol{u}}|\mathbf{d}\right)}\propto{\pi_{\text{like}}\left(\mathbf{d}|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)}\times{\pi_{\text{prior}}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)}

which updates the prior knowledge
{\pi_{\text{prior}}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)}
using the
likelihood
{\pi_{\text{like}}\left(\mathbf{d}|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)}
. The prior encodes any
knowledge or assumptions about the parameter space that we may wish to
impose before any data are observed, while the likelihood explicitly represents
the probability that a given set of parameters
{\boldsymbol{u}}
might give
rise to the observed data
\mathbf{d}
.

Even when the prior and noise probability distributions are Gaussian,
the posterior need not be Gaussian, due to possible nonlinearity embedded
in the likelihood. For large-scale inverse problems, exploring
non-Gaussian posteriors in high dimensions to compute statistics is a
grand challenge since evaluating the posterior at each point
in the parameter space requires a solution of the parameter-to-observable map,
including a potentially expensive forward model solve.
Using numerical quadrature to compute the mean and covariance matrix,
for example, is generally infeasible in high dimensions.
Usually the method of choice for
computing statistics is Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), which
judiciously samples the posterior distribution, so that sample
statistics can be used to approximate the exact ones.

The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm, first developed by
Metropolis
et al.
[
1
]
and then
generalized by Hastings
[
2

method. Its popularity and attractiveness come from the ease of
implementation and minimal requirements on the target density and the
proposal density
[
3

4
]
.
The problem, however, is
that standard MCMC methods often require millions of samples for
convergence; since each sample requires an evaluation of the
parameter-to-observable map, this could entail millions of expensive
forward PDE simulations—a prohibitive proposition.
On one hand, with the rapid development of parallel computing,
parallel MCMC methods
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,

]

are studied to accelerate the computation.
While parallelization allows MCMC algorithms to produce more samples in a
shorter time with multiple processors, such accelerations typically do not
improve the mixing and convergence of MCMC algorithms.
More sophisticated MCMC methods that exploit
the gradient and higher derivatives of the log posterior (and hence
the parameter-to-observable map)

One approach to addressing the computational challenge in high-dimensional

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\left(\left|X_{1}\right|^{2/3}\left|X_{2}\right|^{2/3}\left|X_{3}\right|^{2/3}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{{X_{1}}^{2}}{3}+\frac{{X_{2}}^{2}}{3}+\frac{{X_{3}}^{2}}{3}\right)\quad\left(\text{Young's inequality for 3 variables \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Youngs3Variables}{}{}]}}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{{X_{1}}^{2}}{3}\right)\exp\left(\frac{{X_{2}}^{2}}{3}\right)\exp\left(\frac{{X_{3}}^{2}}{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{3}\left[\exp\left({X_{1}}^{2}\right)+\exp\left({X_{2}}^{2}\right)+\exp\left({X_{3}}^{2}\right)\right]\quad\left(\text{Young's inequality again}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2.

∎

Corollary 5
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a zero-mean random vector such that
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{T}]={\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

with
\alpha
-subexponential entries. Define

\zeta_{\alpha}\left(N,\beta\right):=\exp\left(-c\beta^{\alpha}N^{\alpha/2}\right)
.
Then

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{i}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\beta\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\right]\geq 1-2n\zeta_{\alpha}\left(N,\beta\right).

(14)

Note that while inequality (
14
) has the dimension
n
in front of the exponential,
this is fixed and the probability of committing an error greater than some tolerance
still decreases exponentially in the number of
samples
N
. Though the decaying is at the slower rate
\propto\exp\left(-N^{\alpha/2}\right)
compared to
the subgaussian error rate
\propto\exp\left(-N\right)
, it is not surprising because
subgaussian is a special case of
\alpha
-subexponential when
\alpha=2

[
40
]
.
These results can be combined to derive a probabilistic non-asymptotic error bound for
nonlinear inverse problems.

Lemma 6

(Non-asymptotic error analysis for randomized nonlinear inverse problems)

Let
\vect\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right)
denote a vectorization of a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}

\vect\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right),\vect\left({\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right),{\boldsymbol{e}}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Define the function

g\left(\boldsymbol{P};{\boldsymbol{u}}\right):=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathcal{F}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)\left[{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)-\mathbf{d}\right)-{\boldsymbol{e}}\right]+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right)-{\boldsymbol{z}}.

Assume that the problem
g\left(\boldsymbol{P};{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)=0
, with
\boldsymbol{P}
as parameters and
{\boldsymbol{u}}
as solution, is Lipschitz well-posed
[
44
]

with Lipschitz constant
\mathcal{L}
, and we define
\mathcal{G}\left(\boldsymbol{P}\right)
as the solution
{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
Let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{\text{{MAP}}}\,\;

\displaystyle:=\left[\vect\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right);\;\vect\left({\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right);\;0;\;0\right],

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}_{N}\quad\;

\displaystyle:=\left[\vect\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\right);\;\vect\left(\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\right);\;\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N};\;\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}_{N}\right],

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{N}\quad\;

\displaystyle:=\left[\vect\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\right);\;\vect\left(\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\right);\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{{{\varepsilon}}}}^{i}\left({\boldsymbol{{{\varepsilon}}}}^{i}\right)^{T}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{i};\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{i}\left({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{i}\right)^{T}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{i}\right],

where
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{T}]={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{T}]={\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\text{{MAP}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\beta\mathcal{L}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}+\left(1+\beta\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}+\left(1+\beta\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\text{ w.p. at least }1-10\zeta\left(N,\beta\right),

and

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\text{{MAP}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\beta\mathcal{L}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle\text{ w.p. at least }1-4\zeta\left(N,\beta\right)-2k\zeta_{2/3}\left(N,\beta\right)-2n\zeta_{2/3}\left(N,\beta\right).

Proof.

Noting that
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}=\mathcal{G}\left(\boldsymbol{P}^{\text{{MAP}}}\right)
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\mathcal{G}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}_{N}\right)
,
we have by the Lipschitz well-posedness assumption,

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\text{{MAP}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}=\left\|\mathcal{G}\left(\boldsymbol{P}^{\text{{MAP}}}\right)-\mathcal{G}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}_{N}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mathcal{L}\left\|\boldsymbol{P}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}\big(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\big).

We can bound
\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}
(and similarly for
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}_{N}
) as follows

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{S}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}-\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{I}\right){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{S}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}-\mathcal{I}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}.

Note that
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}
is the sample average of a mean-zero subgaussian random variable
with identity covariance and
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{S}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}]=\mathcal{I}
.
Therefore, applying Proposition
3
to

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{S}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}-\mathcal{I}\right\|_{\infty}
and Proposition

2
to
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}
along with the union bound, we obtain

\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\beta^{2}+\beta\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\quad\text{w.p. at least }1-3\zeta\left(N,\beta\right).

The proof of the bound
\left\|\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\beta^{2}+\beta\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}

\text{w.p. at least }1-3\zeta\left(N,\beta\right)
follows analogously.
Applying Proposition
3
to the terms
\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{N}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}
and

\left\|\boldsymbol{L}_{N}-{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}
along with the union bound, inequality
(
15
) follows immediately.

To prove the bound in (
16
) for
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\text{{MAP}}}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}
, it is sufficient
to bound
\left\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\right\|_{\infty}
according to the definition of
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{N}

since the bounds for
\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{N}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}
and

\left\|\boldsymbol{L}_{N}-{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}
have already been proven.
By Proposition
4
,
{\boldsymbol{{{\varepsilon}}}}^{i}\left({\boldsymbol{{{\varepsilon}}}}^{i}\right)^{T}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{i}
is a zero-mean,

2/3
-subexponential random variable with covariance
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}
.
Then by Corollary
5
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\beta\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\right]\geq 1-2k\zeta_{2/3}\left(N,\beta\right).

Similarly, applying Corollary
5
to
\left\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\right\|_{\infty}
yields

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\beta\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\right]\geq 1-2n\zeta_{2/3}\left(N,\beta\right).

Using the union bound to combine the bounds on
\left\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\right\|_{\infty}
with
the bounds previously
\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{N}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}
and

, inequality (

∎

Lemma
6
provides a general strategy for analyzing the convergence of various randomized methods. When a variable is not randomized, we simply
drop the corresponding terms in equations (
15
) and (
16
), and adjust the probability accordingly.
Additionally, as will be discussed later, the Lipschitz constant may be affected by the choice of randomization strategy.
By choosing to randomize only some variables, the inverse solution may have lower worst-case sensitivity (Lipschitz constant).
Note that since we are mainly concerned with small deviations,
it is sufficient to consider well-posed inverse problems where the
solution depends continuously on
\mathbf{d}
everywhere and
depends on
\boldsymbol{P}
in a locally Lipschitz manner in a neighborhood of

\left[\vect\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right);\;\vect\left({\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right);\;0;\;0\right]
.
Note that it is unlikely for the solution to depend continuously on
\boldsymbol{P}
everywhere since
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}

acts as regularization. Otherwise, the original problem would not be ill-posed.
In particular, the regularizing role that
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
plays may cause the solution of the inverse problem to be
especially sensitive to perturbations of
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
, greatly increasing the (local) Lipschitz constant
compared to the case where
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
is not randomized.
This is clear from the linear case where
the condition number of the problem takes the place of the Lipschitz constant. It is well-known that
the choice of
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
has a significant impact on the condition number
[
45
,
46
]
.
Lastly, note that in the linear case, standard perturbation theory for linear systems can be used to find an explicit
bound for the relative error in terms of the condition number of
\mathcal{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathcal{A}+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
.

Proposition 7

When the PtO map is linear, i.e.

under the conditions that

and

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RMA+RMAP${}_{1}$}}}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|{{\boldsymbol{{{\varepsilon}}}}^{i}}^{T}\left(\mathbf{d}+{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{i}-\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{u}})\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{i}\right\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}}\right].

(28)

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RMA+RMAP${}_{2}$}}}_{N,M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|{{\boldsymbol{{{\varepsilon}}}}^{j}}^{T}\left(\mathbf{d}+{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{i}-\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{u}})\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{i}\right\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}}\right].

(29)

We would like to point out (
29
) is perhaps the most intuitive
way to combine RMA and RMAP. Randomization of the
noise covariance matrix acts as a random projection (left sketching) while the
randomized prior mean and data aid in sampling from the posterior. Note that (
29
) arises as a variant of the loss function defined in equation (
10
) by
exchanging the optimization and expectation of only
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.
On the other hand, (
28
) would likely yield inaccurate results as
it is the sum of solutions where the PtO map and data have been projected
onto a one dimensional subspace: thus the prior dominates each solution.
In the linear case,
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RMA+RMAP}}_{2}}_{N,M}
can be written as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RMA+RMAP}}_{2}}_{N,M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{j}\right)^{T}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{j}+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{j}\right)^{T}\left({\boldsymbol{{{\varepsilon}}}}^{j}\right)^{T}\mathbf{d}^{i}+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}^{i}\right).

Clearly we also have convergence to the MAP point of other combinations,
such as randomizing only one of the data or prior mean.
To avoid a combinatorial explosion in the number of corollaries,
we omit all the possibilities here.

3.3
Randomized prior

Here we propose a randomization scheme based on randomizing
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
though
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\sim\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right]=0
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]={\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
. Let

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS\_U1}}}:

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{J}}\left({\boldsymbol{u}};{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\mathbf{d},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\right]

(30)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{u}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{d}-\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{u}})\right\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}\right].

The reason for designating this method “RS_U1” is due to its relationship with the right sketching approach (see Section
4.1
).
Then the SAA reads

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS\_U1}}}_{N}:

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}\left({\boldsymbol{u}};{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\mathbf{d},{\boldsymbol{{{\varepsilon}}}}^{i}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{d}-\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{u}})\right\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\left({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{i}\right)^{T}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}.

Corollary 12

(Asymptotic convergence of randomized prior)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS\_U1}}}_{N}
be as defined above. Then

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS\_U1}}}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

In the linear case, the optimality condition yields the following solution

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS\_U1}}}=\left(\mathcal{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathcal{A}+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]\right)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{d}+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right).

This approach can be thought of as sketching the prior from the left.

4
Optimize, transform, then randomize

An important observation about
the methods discussed so far
is that the linear settings are
solved using
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
given in (
17a
).
That is, to show the equivalence of the solution of the randomized cost function
to the solution of the corresponding method in the literature,
one only needs to consider the optimal solution
(
17a
).
Additionally, the sample average approximation of the cost function is
exactly the same as replacing the expectations in form
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
with their
respective sample average approximations.
The next methods require form
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
in (
17b
)
to be used to see the equivalence of the randomized solution
and the corresponding method given in the literature – where the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury
formula is applied to the optimality condition before making sample average approximations.

As
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
is only equivalent to
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
in the linear case, we
will restrict the following discussion to linear inverse problems.

4.1
Right sketching and the Ensemble Kalman filter

Since we are now considering schemes derived from randomizing
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
,
we introduce a new random variable,
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, defined such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right]=0
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}\right]={\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
.
By taking advantage of the asymptotic convergence of the SAA of

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}\right]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{i}\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{i}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}\mathcal{A}^{T}\right]={\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\mathcal{A}^{T},

(32a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{i}\right)\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{i}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\left[\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}\mathcal{A}^{T}\right]=\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\mathcal{A}^{T}.

(32b)

Combining (
17b
) and (
32
) gives

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}_{N}:={\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}+\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{i}\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{i}\right)^{T}\right)\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{i}\right)\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{i}\right)^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right),

(33)

which is the same as sketching the PtO map
\mathcal{A}
from the
right or sketching the transpose of the PtO map from the left.

Lemma 13

(Asymptotic convergence of right sketching)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}_{N}
be defined in (
33
) and assume that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]
(where
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is defined in
Section
2
) is invertible. Then

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

Proof.

Beginning with equation (
2
) and randomizing
only
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
through
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, the optimality condition is

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}=\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathcal{A}+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]\right)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{d}+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right).

Since
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]
is assumed to be invertible, this can
be rewritten using the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula in the form

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}+\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]\right)^{-1}\mathcal{A}^{T}\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}+\mathcal{A}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]\right)^{-1}\mathcal{A}^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right).

Before making a sample average approximation, note that

\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]\right)^{-1}=\left({\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}={\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}\right].

Then,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}={\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}\right]\mathcal{A}^{T}\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}+\mathcal{A}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}\right]\mathcal{A}^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right).

since matrix multiplication and matrix inversion are continuous functions,

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}={\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}.

by the continuous mapping theorem
[
56
, Theorem 2.3]
.
∎

The key step here is recognizing that, asymptotically, sampling from
\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}

and solving using form
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
(
17a
)
gives the same results as sampling from
\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}
and solving using
form
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
(
17b
). However, Lemma
13
does
not imply that
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}_{N}
is equivalent to sampling from
\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
and
solving using form
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
for a finite
N
. Indeed, when
N<\dim\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)
,

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}_{N}
cannot be rewritten in the form
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
since

\text{rank}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{j}\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{j}\right)^{T}\right)\leq N<\dim\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right).

This implies that the sample average of
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}\right]
is not
invertible, breaking an assumption of Lemma
13
and
showing that
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{RS}}}_{N}
does not satisfy the optimality condition of (
2
).
Here, it is important that
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
is invertible, otherwise

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}\neq{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
and the randomized schemes discussed here have no hope of
converging to
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}
.

If in addition to right sketching we use (
19
)
to randomize
\mathbf{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}
in (
33
), and define

\left({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{ENKF}}}_{N}\right)^{i}:={\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}^{i}+\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{j}\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{j}\right)^{T}\right)\left({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{j}\right)\left(\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{j}\right)^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}^{i}-\mathcal{A}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}^{i}\right),

(34)

we rediscover the well-known ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) update formula
for a single member of the ensemble
[
57
]
.
Notice here that the sketching of
\mathcal{A}
from the right is
fixed for each random sample
\mathbf{d}^{i}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}^{i}
.
In the language of the EnKF, the sample prior covariance
matrix is fixed for all members of the ensemble.

Corollary 14

(Asymptotic convergence of EnKF)
.

Let

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{ENKF}}}_{M,N}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{ENKF}}}_{N}\right)^{i}.

(35)

Then

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{ENKF}}}_{M,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}\quad\text{as}\quad M,N\to\infty.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from Theorem
1
,
Lemma
13
, and the interchange of expectation and integration used in
Corollary
9
.
∎

As with right sketching, this result only holds asymptotically,
with special sensitivity to
N
, since the validity of
\left({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{ENKF}}}_{N}\right)^{i}
as an optimal solution
of (
2
) requires
N
to be large enough to ensure invertibility
of all matrices involved. It should be noted that the EnKF is often used as part of
an iterative method for solving inverse problems rather than used directly

[
33
]
. The reason for this can be understood by investigating what right sketching
(and thus the EnKF) is doing to the prior covariance matrix and viewing this
through the lens of regularization.

4.1.1
Right sketching from the left as randomized regularization

Consider again the form
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
given in (
17a
):

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}=\left(\mathcal{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathcal{A}+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{d}+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\right).

While the randomized prior (
31
),
right sketching (
33
)
and ensemble Kalman filter (
34
)
methods still fall under the asymptotic analysis given in Section

2.1
for linear inverse problems,
a practical and theoretical issue arises due to the regularizing role that
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
plays.
The inverse of the prior covariance,
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
, can be considered to be
a regularization operator when viewed through the lens of deterministic
inverse problems and is indeed equivalent to a Tikhonov regularization
strategy
[
58
]
. In the deterministic setting, the role of regularization
is often to “damp out” highly oscillatory modes caused by the rapidly
decaying spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
— modes that are highly polluted by noise.
While asymptotic analysis (see Theorem
1
) establishes the convergence of these aforementioned methods, it is incapable of explaining why these methods could fail for finite sample size
N
. This is where non-asymptotic analysis shines.
Indeed, Lemma
6
shows that
the successful (small error) probability requires quite a large number of samples.
According to Remark 4.7.2 of
[
37
]
, the number of samples
required to accurately estimate the covariance matrix is proportional to
n/\beta^{2}
where

n
is the dimension of the matrix and
\beta
is the tolerance.
This is not surprising from a regularization point of view as the sample covariance needs to closely approximate the true covariance
in order to adequately perform its role as a regularizer.

As a concrete example, consider the simple case where
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}=\alpha\mathcal{I}

with
\alpha>0
.
Letting
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{A}={\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}
be the SVD of the whitened PtO map,
the first term of
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
can be written

\left({\boldsymbol{V}}{\boldsymbol{S}}^{2}{\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}=\left({\boldsymbol{V}}\left({\boldsymbol{S}}^{2}+\alpha\mathcal{I}\right){\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}\right)^{-1}={\boldsymbol{V}}\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{V}}^{T},

where
\mathbf{D}
is the diagonal matrix with the
i
th diagonal element given by
\mathbf{D}_{ii}=\frac{1}{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{ii}^{2}+\alpha}
.
Comparing to the case of no regularization, we can see that the inverse of the prior
covariance shifts the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
upward by the constant
\alpha
.
Furthermore, upon inverting,
\alpha>0
ensures that the denominator of

\frac{1}{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{ii}^{2}+\alpha}
is not too close to 0, keeping the
inverse solution from blowing up as
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{ii}^{2}\to 0
.
Now, consider the RS_U1 randomization of
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
proposed in (
31
) —
the same randomization as right sketching
when viewed in the
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
form (sketching the prior from the left):

{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\right]\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{i}\right)\left({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{i}\right)^{T}.

As we saw before, this randomization converges as
N\to\infty
,
but the convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
of a
SAA is notoriously slow. So how
does this slow convergence affect the regularization strategy?
Clearly when
N<\dim\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)
, the regularization is not full rank and
there may be
\dim\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)-N
modes of
\mathcal{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathcal{A}

left unregularized, assuming the random matrix has linearly independent columns.
Even in the case when
N\geq\dim\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)
,
slow convergence of the SAA leaves modes underregularized leading to oscillatory solutions as seen in
Figures
17(d)
and
17(e)
for the 1D deconvolution problem. This can also be
seen explicitly in Figure
1(a)
where the spectrum of the sample average
inverse covariance is plotted against the spectrum of the true prior inverse covariance for various
N
.

In Figure
1(a)
, we consider the case
where
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}=\mathcal{I}
and
\dim({\boldsymbol{u}})=1000
.

Because randomizing the inverse of the prior covariance results

)

{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}
.

5
Numerical results

In this section we show numerical results for a variety of inverse problems demonstrating the asymptotic convergence of
various methods. As the possible number of randomized variants would be unnecessarily burdensome to enumerate,
we will focus on a few key methods:
randomized misfit approach (
24
),
randomized MAP (
21
),
the combination of RMA and RMAP (
29
),
right sketching (
33
),
the ensemble Kalman filter (
35
),
and randomizing everything (
10
) (listed as ALL).
It is important to keep in mind
that we are not advocating for or against the use of any particular method — this section is to serve as
numerical validation of the asymptotic convergence of each method.
Additionally we discuss the differing convergence behavior of each method for different problems.
In particular, we find empirically that
methods randomizing
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-1}

such as right sketching, the EnKF, and ALL generally have very poor performance and require
many more samples than the dimension of the problem in order to provide suitable
results for several problems.
The reason for this has been discussed at length in Section
4.1.1
.
These methods do however exhibit asymptotic convergence to the MAP solution as predicted by our theoretical results.

To explore the performance and convergence of the various methods, we consider a variety of
prototype problems with different characteristics. The 1D deconvolution problem
with scaled identity prior covariance is a relatively simple inverse problem
that provides easily digestible visualizations of the convergence
for each method. X-ray tomography is a mildly ill-posed two dimensional imaging problem with fewer
observations than parameters. The fact that it is only mildly ill-posed exposes interesting effects
in the context of randomization.
We also show the convergence of each method for a linear time dependent PDE-constrained
inverse problem with PDE-based prior covariance on a domain with a hole. Finally, we conclude with an
example demonstrating convergence on a non-linear elliptic PDE-constrained inverse problem.

In problems with more than one randomization, such as EnKF and RMA+RMAP,
each expectation can be approximated
by a separate sample average. However, exploring the effect of choosing a different number of samples
for each random variable is outside the scope of this paper and serves only to obscure
the asymptotic convergence property that we aim to show in this section. Therefore, all methods
assume that the number of random samples is the same for all random variables. To be more concrete, we set

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{ENKF}}}_{M,N}={\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{ENKF}}}_{N,N}
in (
35
).
In addition, the relative errors presented are with respect to
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}
, not the true solution,
emphasizing the errors induced by randomization rather than errors due to other effects.
This is due to the fact that the theory presented shows convergence to
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\text{{MAP}}}
.

are usually easier to work with, this can present a challenge for randomized methods, particularly
methods such as RMA that randomize the misfit term. Recall that for any two matrices
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
,

\Rank(\boldsymbol{XY})\leq\Rank(\boldsymbol{X})
. By projecting the misfit term onto a lower dimensional
subspace, important information is lost in the case where
\mathcal{A}
is has effective
rank close to the dimension of the data. This indicates that such a method is better suited for problems
that are severely ill-posed.

In previous problems, we have used a direct linear solver to find the solution to the stochastic optimization problem.

6
Conclusions

By viewing the randomized solution of inverse problems through the lens of stochastic programming and
the sample average approximation, we developed a unified framework though which we can analyze the asymptotic
convergence of randomized solutions of linear inverse problems to the solution obtained with its deterministic counterpart.
This framework allowed us to prove the asymptotic and non-asymptotic convergence of the minimizer of a general stochastic cost function
to the minimizer of the expected value of the stochastic cost function.
Several well-known methods for introducing randomness into linear and nonlinear inverse problems were recovered as special cases of
this general framework.
Viewing the solution to randomized inverse problems through the lens
of the sample average approximation also allowed us to prove a novel
non-asymptotic error analysis that applies to all randomized methods discussed.
We also show that while all of the methods presented converge asymptotically, the results
can be quite poor if an insufficient number of samples are drawn.
While this observation is easily understood through our non-asymptotic
error analysis, it is not possible from an asymptotic view point.
In particular, we showed that randomizing the prior
covariance matrix may not be a good idea for certain priors due to the regularizing role that the prior plays
in the solution of inverse problems.
This is due to the potentially slow convergence of random matrices to their expected value,
depending on the spectrum of the expected value matrix.
The convergence of all schemes was shown numerically for a variety of linear and nonlinear inverse problems,
including 1D and 2D problems governed by algebraic and PDE constraints.
