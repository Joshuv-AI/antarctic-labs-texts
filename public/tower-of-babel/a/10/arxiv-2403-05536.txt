Long-range competition on the torus
Bas Lodewijks and Neeladri Maitra

Abstract
We study a competition between two growth models with long-range correlations on the torus 𝕋 n d \mathbb{T}_{n}^{d} of size n n in dimension d d . We append the edge set of the torus 𝕋 n d \mathbb{T}_{n}^{d} by including all non-nearest-neighbour edges, and from two source vertices, two first-passage percolation (FPP) processes start flowing on 𝕋 n d \mathbb{T}_{n}^{d} and compete to cover the sites, where the cost of traversing an edge depends on the torus distance between its endpoints. The FPP processes we consider are long-range first-passage percolation processes, as studied by Chaterjee and Dey in [ 17 ] . Here, we have two types, say Type- 1 1 and Type- 2 2 , and the Type- i i transmission time of an edge e e equals λ i − 1 ​ ‖ e ‖ α i ​ E e \lambda_{i}^{-1}\|e\|^{\alpha_{i}}E_{e} for i ∈ { 1 , 2 } i\in\{1,2\} , where ( E e ) e (E_{e})_{e} is a family of i.i.d. rate-one exponential random variables, λ 1 , λ 2 > 0 \lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0 are the global rate parameters, and α 1 , α 2 ≥ 0 \alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0 are the long-range parameters. In particular, we consider the instantaneous percolation regime, where α 1 , α 2 ∈ [ 0 , d ) \alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,d) , and we allow all parameters to depend on n n . We study coexistence , the event that both types reach a positive proportion of the graph, and identify precisely when coexistence occurs. In the case of absence of coexistence, we outline several phase transitions in the size of the losing type, depending on the relation between the rates of both types. One of the consequences of our results is that for constant intensity competition, i.e. when the long-range parameters of the two processes are the same, while their rates differ by a constant multiplicative factor, coexistence of the two processes at the scale of the torus volume happen if and only if their global rates are equal. On the other hand, when the long-range parameters differ, it is possible for one of the types, e.g. Type- 2 2 , to reach a significant number of vertices, even when its global rate parameter λ 2 \lambda_{2} is much smaller than λ 1 \lambda_{1} .



Keywords:
First-passage percolation, long-range first-passage percolation, competition, competing first-passage percolation, continuous-time branching processes.

Introduction

Suppose that a virus spreads through a population, when suddenly a mutated variant appears. This mutated variant is less contagious, but has a longer incubation period (the time until symptoms develop). As such, though the variant spreads slower than the original virus locally, its increased incubation period may result in infections to occur over greater distances. People who carry the virus but show no symptoms may still go into work, visit friends or family, or travel large distances. As such, the variant can more easily spread along greater distances compared to the original virus.

An important question is which of the two viruses is the more dangerous one. Indeed, in the outbreak of, for example, the COVID virus, it was observed that mutated variants showed behaviour different to that of the original strain
[
25
,
47
]
, thus requiring a shift in focus on how to combat the outbreak. Depending on the difference in properties and behaviour of the two viruses (e.g. their contagiousness and incubation periods), which of the two viruses infects only a negligible part of the population, compared to the other, by the time the viruses are endemic? Or, do both viruses infect a positive proportion of the population and thus both form a threat?

The aim of this paper is to study a mathematical model of competing viral infections in a population spread out through space, where the viral infections can differ not only in the
local intensity
at which they spread, but also in the strength of their respective
long-range
spreading rate. As far as the authors are aware, this initiates the study of such competition models where long-range effects are considered. We model the spread of infections as two long-range first-passage percolation processes on the torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
of size
n
in dimension
d
. To describe the model, let us briefly review the models of first-passage percolation and the competition variants of them.

First-passage percolation (FPP) was first introduced by Hammersley and Welsh in 1965
[
26
]
. It has then been studied extensively as a prototypical model of fluid-flow through porous media; we refer the interested reader to the survey
[
6
]
and references therein. Informally, one considers a graph
G=(V,E)
, and associates i.i.d. copies
\gamma_{e}
of some random variable
\gamma
to every edge
e\in E
. We interpret
\gamma_{e}
as the time it takes for a fluid to traverse the edge
e
. For the special case when
\gamma
is an exponentially distributed random variable with mean
1/\lambda
, we say that the FPP process ‘flows at rate
\lambda
’.

A model where two FPP processes compete to occupy sites in the two-dimensional integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
was first introduced as the two-type Richardson model in
[
36
]
. In this model, initially two different kinds of FPP processes start flowing from two different sources, at potentially different rates, and they compete to cover the entire lattice. The main interest is in understanding when both processes cover infinitely many sites with positive probability; an event known as
coexistence
. In this direction, the main conjecture from
[
36
]
is that coexistence occurs if and only if the rates of the two competing processes are equal. Note that by a straightforward scaling, one can without loss of generality consider the case where the first fluid flows at rate
1
, while the second at rate
\lambda\geq 1
. Many results related to this conjecture have been obtained. The original paper
[
36
]
introducing this model showed coexistence occurs for
\lambda=1
,
[
37
]
showed non-coexistence for almost all parameter values of
\lambda
, and
[
4
]
showed coexistence if and only if
\lambda=1
, on the half-plane
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}
. We refer the interested reader to the survey
[
19
]
and the references therein for more in-depth information.

A model of
long-range first-passage percolation
(LRFPP) that inspired the model of the present paper, was introduced by Chatterjee and Dey in
[
17
]
. Here, the underlying graph
\mathbb{G}_{\infty}=(V(\mathbb{G}_{\infty}),E(\mathbb{G}_{\infty}))
has the
d
-dimensional integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as its vertex set, and each vertex is connected to every other vertex; thus creating an ‘infinite complete graph embedded in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
’. Every edge
e
is assigned a transmission time
T_{e}:=\|e\|^{\alpha}E_{e}
, where
\|e\|
denotes the spatial length of the edge (one can take any
p
-norm to determine the length),
\alpha\geq 0
is the
long-range
parameter, and
(E_{e})_{e\in E(\mathbb{G}_{\infty})}
is a family of i.i.d. rate-one exponential random variables. One can view this setting as a first-passage percolation model where transmission times are penalised by the lengths of the edge, where long edges receive heavier penalties and
\alpha
controls the discrepancy between penalties of short and long edges. Note that
\alpha=\infty
can be interpreted as the case of nearest-neighbour FPP on the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

In this paper, we study a competition variant of the LRFPP model on the torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
of size
n
in dimension
d
obtained by including the non-nearest-neighbor edges (instead of the entire lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as in the previous paragraph). Here, initially from two different source vertices of
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, two different LRFPP processes start, say a Type-
1
and Type-
2
process, attempting to reach the vertices of the graph. Here, Type-
i
spreads through the graph according to transmission times
T_{e}^{(i)}:=\lambda_{i}^{-1}\|e\|^{\alpha_{i}}E_{e}
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
are
global
intensities and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0
are the long-range parameters. Referring back to the real-world setting that served as motivation at the start, we can view the different global intensities as the
contagiousness of the viruses, and the long-range parameters as the strength of the long-range spread of the viruses (possibly determined by e.g. different incubation periods). Our main interest is in understanding the size of the set of vertices infected by a particular infection, when all the vertices of
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
have been infected.

We restrict our attention to long-range parameters strictly less than the dimension
d
, which is the
instantaneous percolation regime
for LRFPP studied by Chatterjee and Dey
[
17
]
and can be thought of as a setting with weak spatial dependence. We remark that, by
[
17
, Theorem
1.2
]
, a LRFPP process on
\mathbb{G}_{\infty}
with long-range parameter
\alpha<d
has the following property: for any time
t>0
, the set of vertices that can be reached from the origin equals
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
almost surely. Hence, any vertex is essentially reached instantaneously. This clarifies the choice to restrict the competition process to the finite torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. Indeed, such a process on
\mathbb{G}_{\infty}
is not well-defined in the instantaneous percolation regime, since one cannot distinguish which infection reaches a vertex first. For long-range parameters exceeding
d
, this process can be studied on
\mathbb{G}_{\infty}
and is ongoing future work. In fact, as remarked before, for the case when one has
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\infty
, and
\lambda_{1}=1,\lambda_{2}=\lambda\geq 0
, we recover the two-type Richardson model. This link served as one of the motivations to consider the model of the present paper. One particularly interesting question to understand is to determine how large the long-range parameters have to be, so that the model is essentially in the same
universality class
as the two-type Richardson’s model. That is, when do most qualitative aspects of the competing LRFPP model behave in a similar manner to the two-type Richardson model? Though this is beyond the scope of this paper, it serves as a first attempt to understand the competing LRFPP model.

In this paper we are generally concerned with questions related to (absence of) coexistence at a linear scale: after all vertices are infected, have both LRFPP processes infected a positive proportion of the graph, or does one process infect almost all vertices, leaving only
o(n)
many for the other process? In the case of non-coexistence, i.e. one type winning almost all vertices of the torus, what can further be said about the size of the losing type? We provide a complete picture in terms of the intensities
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
, and the long-range parameters
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
. In particular, we allow these parameters to depend on
n
, which uncovers more subtle behaviour in certain cases that cannot be observed from fixed parameters, independent of
n
, which is common in most of the literature.

1.1
Model definition

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional torus of size
n
, i.e.
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}=

[-n^{1/d}/2,n^{1/d}/2]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where we identify vertices on opposing boundaries to be the same. Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}(n),\lambda_{2}(n)>0
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{1}(n),\alpha_{2}(n)\in[0,d)
. Throughout the paper, when there is no confusion, we omit the argument
n
and write
\lambda_{i}
(resp.
\alpha_{i}
) for
\lambda_{i}(n)
(resp.
\alpha_{i}(n)
), for
i=1,2
. Let
\|\cdot\|
denote the torus norm, which we can allow to be any
\ell^{p}
distance on the discrete box
Q_{n}:=[-n^{1/d}/2,n^{1/d}/2]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, with appropriate periodicity conditions that come from identifying the boundaries of
Q_{n}
. Let us define the quantities

R_{n}^{(1)}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\|v\|^{-\alpha_{1}},\qquad R_{n}^{(2)}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\lambda_{2}\|v\|^{-\alpha_{2}}.

(1.1)

R_{n}^{(1)}
and
R_{n}^{(2)}
are key quantities for the analysis of the process, and thus we state them together. However, we do not need
R_{n}^{(2)}
in the model definition, while
R_{n}^{(1)}
appears as a scaling factor that does not influence the behaviour of the model, but is used for convenience.

We append the edge set of the torus with all the non-nearest-neighbor edges, and call this set of ‘complete’ edges
\mathrm{E}_{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{n}:=\{\{x,y\}:x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d},{x\neq y}\}.

To each edge
e\in\mathrm{E}_{n}
, we associate a pair
(E^{(1)}_{e},E^{(2)}_{e})
of independent exponential random variables with rate parameter
1
, where the collection of the pairs over all the edges also forms an independent collection, and we define

T_{e}^{(i)}:=R_{n}^{(1)}\lambda_{i}^{-1}\|e\|^{\alpha_{i}}E^{(i)}_{e}

(1.2)

to be the time it takes for the Type-
i
infection to traverse the edge
e
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
. For our results, it is not important that the random variables
E^{(1)}_{e}
and
E^{(2)}_{e}
are independent for a fixed edge
e
(in fact, we could use the
same
random variable for both transmission times). We still assume this independence, as this simplifies some techniques that we employ. It is crucial, however, that for different edges
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
,
(E^{(1)}_{e_{1}},E^{(2)}_{e_{1}})
and
(E^{(1)}_{e_{2}},E^{(2)}_{e_{2}})
are independent.

Let us now define the Long-Range Competition (LRC) process. Fix two distinct vertices
v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. Initially, the two vertices
v^{(1)}
and
v^{(2)}
are infected with two distinct infection types, which we call the Type-
1
and Type-
2
infection, respectively. Infections start traversing edges in
\mathrm{E}_{n}
, incident to already infected vertices, according to the transmission times in (
1.2
). When the Type-
i
infection traverses an edge
e
and encounters an uninfected vertex
v
, it infects
v
with Type-
i
and starts to propagate to other vertices from
v
according to the times
T_{e^{\prime}}^{(i)}
of edges
e^{\prime}\neq e
incident to
v
. If
v
has already been infected by either Type-
1
or Type-
2
, nothing happens when the Type-
i
infection reaches
v
.

There is a natural continuous-time Markovian description of this process, which we adopt from
[
5
]
. Let
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a Markov process with state space the set of all pairs of disjoint subsets of
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\Omega=\{(A,B):A,B\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d},A\cap B=\emptyset\}.

At
t=0
, we initialise the process by setting
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(0),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(0)=(\{v^{(1)}\},\{v^{(2)}\})
for the two distinct vertices
v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. At any time
t\geq 0
, the process evolves by including a vertex
v\in(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t))^{c}
in the set
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)
at rate
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)}(R_{n}^{(1)})^{-1}\lambda_{1}\|v-x\|^{-\alpha_{1}}
, and to the set
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)
at rate
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)}(R_{n}^{(1)})^{-1}\lambda_{2}\|v-x\|^{-\alpha_{2}}
. This Markov process describes the evolution of the sets of the two infection types. We define the stopping times

T_{k}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)|=k\},\qquad k\in\{2,\ldots,n\},

(1.3)

where, by convention,
T_{2}=0
, and let
T_{\mathrm{cov}}=T_{n}
be the
cover time
, when all vertices have been infected.

It is useful to also describe the corresponding discrete-time process
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k}),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k}))_{k=2}^{n}
, which is the continuous-time process evaluated at the stopping times
(T_{k})_{k=2}^{n}
. We set
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{2})=\{v^{(1)}\}
and
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{2})=\{v^{(2)}\}
. For any
k\in\{2,\ldots,n-1\}
, conditionally on
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k}),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k}))
, the pair
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k+1}),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k+1}))
is obtained by first sampling a vertex
v\in(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k})\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k}))^{c}
with probability

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k})}\lambda_{1}\|x-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k})}\lambda_{2}\|y-v\|^{-\alpha_{2}}}{\sum_{z\in(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k})\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k}))^{c}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k})}\lambda_{1}\|x-z\|^{-\alpha_{1}}+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k})}\lambda_{2}\|y-z\|^{-\alpha_{2}}\right)}.

Then, set
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k+1}),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k+1}))=(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k})\cup\{v\},\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k}))
, conditionally on both
v
and on
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k}),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k}))
, with probability

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k})}\lambda_{1}\|x-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k})}\lambda_{1}\|x-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k})}\lambda_{2}\|y-v\|^{-\alpha_{2}}},

and
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k+1}),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k+1}))=(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k}),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k})\cup\{v\})
otherwise.

Standard notations.

For sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
b_{n}>0
for all
n
, we let
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
and
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(b_{n})
denote
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|a_{n}|}{b_{n}}=0
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big|\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}\big|<\infty
, respectively. For a sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables and a random variable
X
, we write
X_{n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}X,X_{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}X,X_{n}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}X
to denote convergence of
X_{n}
to
X
in distribution, in probability, and in the almost sure sense, respectively. For random variables
X
and
Y
, we let
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y
denote equality in distribution. We say that a sequence of events
\mathcal{E}_{n}
holds
with high probability
, if
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

1.2
Statements of main results

The main quantities of interest are the final sizes
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{\mathrm{cov}})|
and
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{\mathrm{cov}})|
of the two infections when the entire torus is infected. In particular, we are interested whether both infections reach a linear portion of the vertices in the graph, or if just one of the infections reaches all but a sub-linear portion of the vertices. To this end, we define notions
coexistence
and
absence of coexistence
as follows:

Definition 1.1

((Absence of) coexistence)
.

We say that
absence of coexistence
occurs when, for some
i\in\{1,2\}
, it holds that
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(T_{\mathrm{cov}})|/n\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
. In this case, we call the Type-
i
infection the
losing
type, whereas the Type-
(3-i)
infection is called the
winning
type.

We say that
coexistence
occurs when

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(T_{\mathrm{cov}})|/n>\epsilon\right)=1,

(1.4)

for both
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Our main theorems describe precisely when (absence of) coexistence occurs. We recall
R_{n}^{(1)}
and
R_{n}^{(2)}
from (
1.1
) and define

Z_{n}:=\frac{R_{n}^{(2)}}{R_{n}^{(1)}}.

(1.5)

Looking at the transmission times defined in (
1.2
), we see that a Type-
1
vertex spreads at rate
1
(assuming no other vertices are infected), whereas a vertex of Type-
2
spreads at rate
Z_{n}
. The ratio
Z_{n}
is crucial in understanding and formulating the behaviour of the LRC process. For the presentation of the main results, it is convenient to further define

c_{n}:=(Z_{n}-1)\log n,\quad\text{or, equivalently,}\quad Z_{n}=1+\frac{c_{n}}{\log n}.

(1.6)

The behaviour of the process can then by characterised fully by the behaviour of
c_{n}
. We work under the following assumptions:

Assumption 1.2
.

•

Without loss of generality, we set
\lambda_{1}=1
, and write
\lambda_{2}(n)=\lambda(n)
.

•

Without loss of generality,
Z_{n}\geq 1
.

•

We assume that both
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{1}(n),\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{2}(n)\in[0,d)
.

The final assumption allows us to avoid certain technical difficulties that can arise when
\alpha_{i}\uparrow d
as
n\to\infty
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Finally, we say that, for
p\in[0,1]
, a random variable
X
is
geometric distributed with success parameter
p
, and write
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(p)
, when it has distribution

\mathbb{P}\left(X=k\right)=p(1-p)^{k-1},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(1.7)

We then have the following result, pertaining to absence of coexistence:

Theorem 1.3

(Absence of coexistence)
.

Let
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
denote a LRC process on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, where
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(0)=v^{(i)}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, as defined in Section
1.1
. We write
N_{n}^{(i)}:=|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(T_{\mathrm{cov}})|
. Recall
Z_{n}
from (
1.5
) and
c_{n}
from (
1.6
). Under Assumption
1.2
, when
c_{n}
diverges,
N_{n}^{(1)}/n\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
. Furthermore:

(i)

If
c_{n}/(\log n)^{2}\to\infty
, then
N^{(1)}_{n}=1
with high probability.

(ii)

If
c_{n}=(c+o(1))(\log n)^{2}
for some
c>0
, then
N_{n}^{(1)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}X
, where
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\mathrm{e}^{-1/c})
.

(iii)

If
c_{n}=o((\log n)^{2})
, then
\log(N_{n}^{(1)})\frac{Z_{n}}{\log n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1
.

(iv)

If
c_{n}=o(\log n)
but
c_{n}
diverges, then
c_{n}^{-1}(\log n-\log N_{n}^{(1)})\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1
.

The following result identifies when coexistence occurs, showing that we have a complete characterisation:

Theorem 1.4

(Coexistence)
.

Consider the same definitions and notation as in Theorem
1.3
. When
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{n}<\infty
, then
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(N_{n}^{(i)}/n>\epsilon)=1
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Remark 1.5

(Non-spatial results)
.

In the case that
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e. when all transmission times
T_{e}^{(1)}
and
T_{e}^{(2)}
are i.i.d. exponential random variables with rate
1
and
\lambda(n)
, respectively, more precise results can be proved. Indeed, as the process can then be exactly described by a Pólya urn, it follows from
[
39
, Theorem
1.4
]
that the absence of coexistence result and the results in Theorem
1.3

(iii)
and
(iv)
, as well as Theorem
1.4
, hold. Moreover, let
E_{1},E_{2}
be i.i.d. rate-one exponential random variables. When
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda(n)=\lambda^{*}\geq 1
such that
(\lambda(n)-1)\log n
diverges, then

N_{n}^{(1)}n^{-1/\lambda(n)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}E_{1}E_{2}^{-1/\lambda^{*}},

(1.8)

and when
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\lambda(n)-1)\log n=c\in[0,\infty)
, then

\frac{N_{n}^{(1)}}{n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\frac{E_{1}}{E_{1}+E_{2}\mathrm{e}^{c}}.

(1.9)

In particular, when
c=0
, the limit has the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. We refer the interested reader to Section
1.4.3
for more details.

Remark 1.6

(Choice of the distance and negative long-range parameters)
.

The torus norm
\|\cdot\|
is some (unspecified)
\ell^{p}
distance on the torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. By the equivalence of all
p
-norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, changing the distance to other
p
-norm metrics
introduces only a constant factor in the intensity of the infections, so that the characterisation of
c_{n}
does not change. That is, changing
\lambda
to
\lambda^{\prime}=a\lambda
for some constant
a>0
does not change whether
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{n}
is finite or whether
c_{n}
diverges.

Further, one could consider
negative
long-range parameters. As long as one assumes that
\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{1}(n),\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{2}(n)>-\infty
, the analysis for non-negative long-range parameters carries over with only minor adjustments to this more general setting. When the long-range parameters diverge to
-\infty
, penalising short edges significantly more than long edges, similar technical issues arise as when
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\uparrow d
.

\blacktriangleleft

1.2.1
Examples

Let us discuss couple of examples in this section by taking some particular choices of
\alpha_{1}(n)
,
\alpha_{2}(n)
, and
\lambda(n)
, for which we can apply Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.

Equal and fixed long-range parameters.

Take
\alpha_{1}(n)=\alpha_{2}(n)=\alpha\in[0,d)
. In this case,
Z_{n}=\lambda(n)
and so
c_{n}=(\lambda(n)-1)\log{n}
. In this case, we observe absence of coexistence when
\log(n)(\lambda(n)-1)
diverges. Moreover,

(i)

N_{n}^{(1)}=1
with high probability when
\log{n}=o(\lambda(n))
;

(ii)

N_{n}^{(1)}
converges to a geometric random variable when
\lambda(n)/\log{n}
converges;

(iii)

N_{n}^{(1)}=n^{(1+o(1))/\lambda(n)}
when
\lambda(n)=o(\log{n})
;

(iv)

N_{n}^{(1)}=n\mathrm{e}^{-(1+o(1))(\lambda(n)-1)\log n}
when
\lambda(n)\downarrow 1
but
(\lambda(n)-1)\log n
diverges;

N_{n}^{(1)}
is linear in
n
with high probability, when
(\lambda(n)-1)\log{n}
is bounded in
n
. In particular, when
\lambda(n)=\lambda>1
fixed, then
N_{n}^{(1)}=n^{1/\lambda+o(1)}
, while when
\lambda(n)=1
,
N_{n}^{(1)}
is linear. We remark that the particular case where
\lambda(n)=\lambda\geq 1
is fixed, is, in general, the setting of competing first-passage percolation models most often studied in the literature.

Unequal and fixed long-range parameters.

Take
\alpha_{1}(n)=\alpha_{1}\in[0,d)
and
\alpha_{2}(n)=\alpha_{2}\in[0,d)
constant, with
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
. The sum
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d},x\neq 0}\|x\|^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha\in[0,d)
equals
(R+o(1))n^{1-\alpha/d}
for some constant
R=R(\alpha,d,p)
depending on
\alpha
, the dimension
d
, and
p
signifying the
\ell^{p}
-norm on the torus that we work with, see
[
31
, Appendix]
. When
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
, we thus obtain that

Z_{n}=\lambda(n)\Big(\frac{R(\alpha_{2},d,p)}{R(\alpha_{1},d,p)}+o(1)\Big)n^{(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})/d}.

(1.10)

Let us first consider the case
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}
. The same conclusions as in the three cases
(i)
through
(iii)
in the previous example can be made, when substituting
\lambda(n)
by
\lambda(n)n^{(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})/d}
. Case
(iv)
, as well as the case of coexistence, are slightly more subtle, as one would need to understand the precise order of the
o(1)
term in (
1.10
). Rather, we have the more general statement that
N_{n}^{(1)}=n\mathrm{e}^{-(1+o(1))(Z_{n}-1)\log n}
when
Z_{n}\downarrow 1
but
(Z_{n}-1)\log n
diverges, and that coexistence occurs when
(Z_{n}-1)\log n
is bounded in
n
. Here, in contrast to the previous example, we see that the global intensity
\lambda(n)
of the Type-
2
infection can tend to zero at a polynomial rate, and still the Type-
2
infection would reach almost all vertices before the Type-
1
infection. We thus observe that the long-range advantage that the Type-
2
infection has via
\alpha_{2}<\alpha_{1}
, which yields a less severe penalisation for the transmission times of non-nearest neighbour edges, results in a very strong advantage.

When
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}
, similar conclusions can be drawn for
N_{n}^{(2)}
, but now when
\lambda(n)
diverges at a polynomial rate
n^{(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})/d}
.

Converging long-range parameters.

Take
\alpha_{2}(n)=\alpha_{2}\in[0,d)
fixed and
\alpha_{1}(n)\downarrow\alpha_{2}
as
n\to\infty
. That is,
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}+d\mathrm{e}_{n}
for some sequence
\mathrm{e}_{n}\downarrow 0
. In this case, one can again obtain the same expression for
Z_{n}
as in (
1.10
), which we can now write as
Z_{n}=(1+o(1))\lambda(n)n^{\mathrm{e}_{n}}
. In particular, absence of coexistence or coexistence now depends on both
\lambda(n)
and the sequence
\mathrm{e}_{n}
, in particular at which rate
\mathrm{e}_{n}
tends to zero. The two processes may or may not coexist depending on how fast
\mathrm{e}_{n}\downarrow 0
. We now have that

(i)

N_{n}^{(1)}=1
with high probability, when
n^{-\mathrm{e}_{n}}\log{n}=o(\lambda(n))
;

(ii)

N_{n}^{(1)}
converges to a geometric random variable when
\lambda(n)n^{\mathrm{e}_{n}}/\log{n}
converges;

(iii)

N_{n}^{(1)}=n^{(1+o(1))n^{-\mathrm{e}_{n}}/\lambda(n)}
when
\lambda(n)=o(n^{-\mathrm{e}_{n}}\log{n})
;

(iv)

N_{n}^{(1)}=n\mathrm{e}^{-(1+o(1))(Z_{n}-1)\log n}
when
\lambda(n)n^{\mathrm{e}_{n}}\downarrow 1
but
(Z_{n}-1)\log n
diverges;

We observe coexistence when
(Z_{n}-1)\log n
is bounded in
n
. In this example, we see a weaker domination compared to the second example, where the two long-range parameters are strictly bounded away from each other.

1.3
Overview of proof

Let us discuss, at a heuristic level, the main ideas that we use to prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.

1.3.1
Coexistence

Let us start with the main proof idea of Theorem
1.4
, as this is more straightforward to describe. The key tool we deploy is a coupling of the competing infection process with two independent branching processes. That is, we couple the FPP flows from the vertices
v^{(1)}
and
v^{(2)}
with two independent Yule processes (see Section
2
for definitions)
\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t)
and
\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t)
with rates
1
and
Z_{n}
respectively, such that the sets of the infections can be seen as subsets of the respective branching processes.

This roughly works in the following way. When vertices are infected, other infected vertices can no longer spread to these newly infected vertices, which decreases the rate at which the infection spreads. To counter this depletion of vertices, we augment the graph with
artificial
vertices to which the infections can spread, so that the rate at which either the Type-
1
or Type-
2
infection spreads is linear its current size. In a Yule process, the rate at which individuals are born is also linear in the size of the process, we can couple the augmented graph process to two Yule processes.

This yields an upper bound on the sizes of the infection sets at any time
t\geq 0
by the respective sizes of the branching processes. We express the total size of the Type-
i
infection at time
t
as
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(t)|=|\mathrm{BP}^{(i)}(t)|-C^{(i)}((0,t])
, where the quantity
C^{(i)}((0,t])
is to be interpreted as the ‘defect’ in coupling the infection sets with branching processes. This defect arises by allowing infected vertices to ‘re-infect’ other, already infected, vertices. These re-infections correspond to births in the branching process whilst such re-infections do not actually occur in the LRC process, thus causing an error or defect in the coupling. Using the condition
\sup_{n}c_{n}<\infty
of Theorem
1.4
and using a point-process representation of the defect, we show that with high probability,

a.

by the time
t_{n}\approx\log(n)/Z_{n}
, the size of each Yule process is
\delta n
for some
\delta>0
, and

b.

the defect
C^{(i)}((0,t_{n}])
is smaller than
\delta^{\prime}n
for some
\delta^{\prime}<\delta
and for both
i\in\{1,2\}
.

As a result, the size of the FPP clusters
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|
and
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|
themselves are both at least
\epsilon n
for some
\epsilon=\epsilon(\delta,\delta^{\prime})\in(0,\delta)
. In particular, the final sizes
N_{n}^{(1)}
and
N_{n}^{(2)}
are both at least
\epsilon n
, which implies coexistence.

1.3.2
Absence of coexistence

Let us now discuss how to prove
N_{n}^{(1)}/n\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
when
c_{n}\to\infty
, which is the main statement of Theorem
1.3
. To prove this, we divide the time interval
(0,T_{\mathrm{cov}}]
, which starts from the initial infections and ends when all the vertices are infected, into two phases, namely
(0,t_{n}]
and
(t_{n},T_{\mathrm{cov}}]
, where
t_{n}\approx\log(n)/Z_{n}
. We use the coupling of the competition process to two Yule processes, as described above, to show that at the end of the first phase,
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|\approx\exp{\log(n)/Z_{n}}
, while
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|\geq\xi n
for some constant
\xi\in(0,1)
. Again, this follows by using the growth rate of the respective Yule processes, combined with showing that the defect
C^{(i)}((0,t_{n}])
is not ‘too large’ for both
i\in\{1,2\}
. It is readily checked that
\exp{\log(n)/Z_{n}}=o(n)
when
c_{n}\to\infty
, so that, at the end of the first phase, the Type-
1
infection has covered a negligible portion of the vertices, while the Type-
2
has covered a positive fraction of the vertices.

For the second phase
(t_{n},T_{\mathrm{cov}})
, we use a martingale argument to show that the significant advantage achieved by the Type-
2
infection at the end of the first phase is strong enough to ensure that the size of the Type-
1
infection does not increase beyond a multiplicative factor of its size at the end of the first phase. Overall, at time
T_{\mathrm{cov}}
, the size of the Type-
1
infection is thus still
o(n)
, which shows there is no coexistence of the two types. The martingale argument is based on the discrete description of the competition process, as outlined in Section
1.1
, and is comparable to a Pólya urn argument, where the initial configuration of the urn (which are the infection sizes at the end of phase 1), grows with
n
.

1.3.3
Size of the losing type

Finally we describe the more detailed results pertaining the exact asymptotic size of
N_{n}^{(1)}
in the different cases of Theorem
1.3
.

For Theorem
1.3

(i)
, we use a result from
[
31
, Theorem 1.1]
, which tells us that a single Type-
2
infection takes roughly
2\log(n)/Z_{n}
amount of time to reach all vertices. On the other hand, the time for vertex
v^{(1)}
, which is infected by the Type-
1
infection at the start, to reach
any
other vertex, is an exponential random variable with rate roughly
1
. Hence, when
c_{n}/(\log n)^{2}
diverges or, equivalently,
Z_{n}/\log n
diverges, it follows that the Type-
2
infection reaches all vertices by time
2\log(n)/Z_{n}=o(1)
, whilst the Type-
1
infection does not spread to any vertex in that time, with high probability. As a result
N_{n}^{(1)}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1
.

Theorem
1.3

(ii)
uses almost the same approach, now using another result from
[
31
, Theorem 1.1]
, namely that by time
\log(n)/Z_{n}
, a Type-
2
infection reaches
almost all
vertices, i.e. only a negligible proportion of vertices are not reached. At the same time, since
c_{n}=(c+o(1))(\log n)^{2}
or, equivalently,
Z_{n}=(c+o(1))\log n
, it follows that in time
\log(n)/Z_{n}=c^{-1}+o(1)
, the Type-
1
infection can reach a (random but finite) number of vertices, since it initially infects other vertices at rate approximately
1
. To obtain the exact limiting distribution of the size of the Type-
1
infection, we use the coupling with the two independent Yule processes, and show that up to time
t_{n}:=(1-\delta_{n})\log(n)/Z_{n}
, for some
\delta_{n}=o(1)
, the coupling of the Type-
1
infection with a rate-one Yule process is in fact
exact
with high probability. That is, the defect
C^{(1)}((0,t_{n}])
equals zero with high probability. We then use a similar Pólya urn argument as discussed in Section
1.3.2
to show that the uninfected vertices remaining at time
t_{n}
(of which there are
o(n)
many) are then
all
infected by the Type-
2
infection, since it has such an overwhelming advantage.

For Theorems
1.3

(iii)
and
(iv)
, the proof of absence of coexistence discussed above, which uses that the Type-
1
infection is of size at most
An^{1/Z_{n}}
for a large constant
A=A(\epsilon)
with probability at least
1-\epsilon
, yields a sufficient upper bound for
N_{n}^{(1)}
. A matching lower bound follows by once more using the coupling to the Yule processes. Here we again show that up to time
t_{n}\approx\log(n)/Z_{n}
, the defect
C^{(1)}((0,t_{n}])
of the coupling of the Type-
1
infection with a Yule process is not ‘too large’. Hence, we can bound the size of the Type-
1
infection from below by the size of the Yule process at time
t_{n}
(which is roughly
n^{1/Z_{n}}
), minus a negligible term coming from the defect. This implies a lower bound on
N_{n}^{(1)}
of the right order.

1.4
Discussion

1.4.1
Related literature

As far as the authors are aware, the LRC model studied in this paper is the first of its kind, in the sense that it is the first competing first-passage percolation model where the edge-weights depend on the spatial embedding of the vertices in the graph. It is inspired by the LRFPP model of Chatterjee and Dey
[
17
]
, where the behaviour of a single type infection on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is studied. This behaviour is not limited to
\alpha<d
, and the study of the competition version of this model for
\alpha\geq d
is future work. The qualitative results in
[
17
]
for the regime
\alpha<d
, however, play no role in the analysis here. Instead, the quantitative results of the first author and Van der Hofstad
[
31
]
regarding the single type LRFPP on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
are used to a greater extent. Moreover, a variation on the coupling of the first-passage percolation process to a branching process (a Crump-Mode-Jagers (CMJ) branching process, to be precise), as in
[
11
]
, underpins the analysis here, too.

Competing first-passage percolation on finite random graphs, has received a significant interest, where many well-known models have been studied. These include, regular random graphs
[
5
]
, the configuration model with finite-variance degrees
[
3
]
, infinite-variance and finite-mean degrees
[
20
,
28
]
(see also the work of Baroni, Van der Hofstad, and Komjáthy on competing FPP with deterministic edge-weights
[
7
,
29
]
), and infinite-mean degrees
[
21
]
. The preprint
[
28
]
shows a universal non-coexistence result for CMs with finite-mean but infinite-variance degrees under general assumptions. The literature on first-passage percolation processes with a single type on random graphs extends to many other models as well. This includes, but does not limit itself, to the complete graph
[
38
,
1
,
12
,
34
,
35
,
11
,
15
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
, the Erdős-Rényi random graph
[
14
,
32
,
33
]
, the configuration model
[
2
,
8
,
9
,
13
,
16
]
, preferential attachment models
[
40
,
41
]
, and spatial models such as scale-free percolation and geometric inhomogeneous random graphs
[
30
,
42
,
18
]
. Noteworthy is the recent work of Komjáthy et al.
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
, who extensively study a degree-penalised variation of first-passage percolation on geometric inhomogeneous random graphs. In this model, edges incident to high-degree vertices typically have higher transmission times. This is, at least in spirit, similar to the spatial penalisation in LRFPP, though the underlying random graph model is very different.

1.4.2
Main contributions

In many of the works mentioned above, both for single-type FPP processes and competition processes, approximating first-passage percolation with branching processes is a very useful and commonly used technique. We highlight here, in particular, the work of Bhamidi and Van der Hofstad on FPP on the complete graph
[
11
]
, where a precise coupling between an FPP process on the complete graph and a CMJ branching process is provided. The main contribution of the current work is an adaptation of this coupling to branching processes in both a competition setting and a spatial environment, as well as a more in-depth analysis of the defect of the coupling. This analysis is crucial to many of the proofs in this paper.

In
[
11
]
, it is proved that the coupling between the FPP process and the CMJ branching process approximation is
exact
up to a certain time
T
, with high probability. This result allows the authors to obtain precise asymptotic results for the typical weighted distance in the complete graph, as well as the hopcount (the number of edges on the shortest edge-weighted path). The analysis of a similar coupling carried out here, additionally identifies the
growth rate
of the coupling defect. That is, when the coupling is no longer exact, we control the error between the FPP processes and the CMJ branching process approximation over time. In particular, we show that, up to some time
T^{\prime}>T
, this error is negligible compared to the size of the branching process. This allows us to use the coupling to a higher degree and obtain useful quantitative bounds, much further into the competition process than previously possible.

The work of Ahlberg, Deijfen, and Janson on competing FPP on the configuration model with finite-variance degrees
[
3
]
is another example where branching-process approximations up to the time that the approximations are exact, are used. From that point onwards, their analysis shows that the competition dynamics are essentially deterministic, in the sense that the ratio of the sizes of the two competing infections stays roughly the same. These results are harder to prove for the model studied here, due to the spatial embedding of the vertices, even when the spatial dependence is weak (long-range parameters close to zero), see also Problem
1.9
below. Instead, our improved control of the coupling to branching processes, still allows us to obtain the desired results, despite not being able to solve Problem
1.9
.

1.4.3
Related models and open problems

Competition without spatial dependence.

When
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
, the spatial embedding of the vertices plays no role, and the competition process takes place on the complete graph where the Type-
1
(resp. Type-
2
) infects through edges at rate
1
(resp.
Z_{n}
, which now simply equals
\lambda(n)
). As claimed in Remark
1.5
, this setting can be exactly represented as a Pólya urn. In particular, adopting the setting of generalized Pólya Urns of Janson
[
39
]
, we consider a Pólya Urn with two types of balls - Type-
1
and Type-
2
balls, where upon drawing a ball of some type, it is added back to the urn along with some additional balls according to the replacement matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&Z_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

This means, upon drawing a Type-
1
ball (resp. Type-
2
ball), one extra Type-
1
ball is (resp.
Z_{n}
extra Type-
2
balls are) added back to the urn, along with the drawn ball. In this description, we implicitly think of
Z_{n}
as being a positive integer, but that need not be, and in particular, the process has a description as a Markov process on the state space
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x,y\geq 0,x+y>0\}
, see
[
39
, Remark
1.11
]
. However, following Janson
[
39
]
, we use phrases like ‘
Z_{n}
balls are drawn’ for ease of understanding.

Let
M^{(i)}_{n}
denote the number of Type-
i
balls after
n
draws, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, where we initialise the urn with
M^{(1)}_{0}=1,M^{(2)}_{0}=Z_{n}
. It readily follows that
M_{n-2}^{(1)}\overset{d}{=}N_{n}^{(1)}
and
M^{(2)}_{n-2}/Z_{n}\overset{d}{=}N_{n}^{(2)}
when
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
, following the discrete description of the competition process in Section
1.1
. It thus follows that, when
Z_{n}\equiv Z
for some fixed
Z\geq 1
, the results of
[
39
, Theorem
1.4
]
can be applied to yield Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, as well as the more in-depth results stated in Remark
1.5
. The additional results of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
and Remark
1.5
, for
Z_{n}
depending on
n
, can be obtained with minor adjustments only, as well. Though we are unable to prove the results of Remark
1.5
when
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
are not equal zero, we conjecture these to be true:

Conjecture 1.7

(Distributional convergence for (absence of) coexistence)
.

Consider the same definitions and notation as in Theorem
1.3
and let
E_{1},E_{2}
be two i.i.d. rate-one exponential random variables. If
c_{n}
diverges, but
\lim_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}=Z\geq 1
, then
N_{n}^{(1)}n^{-1/Z_{n}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}E_{1}E_{2}^{-1/Z}
. If, instead,
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}=c\geq 0
exists, then
N_{n}^{(1)}/n\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\frac{E_{1}}{E_{1}+E_{2}\mathrm{e}^{c}}
.

In fact we are even unable to show the following weaker result, which we leave as a problem:

Problem 1.8

(Distributional asymmetry of the final sizes)
.

Consider the same definitions and notations as in Theorem
1.3
. If
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}=c\geq 0
exists,
then
p(c):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(N_{n}^{(1)}<N_{n}^{(2)})
exists and satisfies

\begin{cases}p(c)>\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }c>0,\\
p(c)=\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }c=0.\end{cases}

(1.11)

It is clear that Conjecture
1.7
implies Problem
1.8
. Towards proving Conjecture
1.7
, using a branching process approximation, for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, we can check that for
t_{\epsilon}:=\log(\epsilon n)
,
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{\epsilon})|
and
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{\epsilon})|
are roughly
E_{1}\epsilon n
and
E_{2}\epsilon\mathrm{e}^{c}n
respectively, where
E_{1},E_{2}
are two i.i.d. rate-one Exponential random variables. Note that this implies that not all vertices of the torus are infected by time
t_{\epsilon}
, for very small
\epsilon
, although both the infections have reached linearly many vertices. We were unable to apply similar techniques as used in e.g.
[
3
]
, to show that, at this stage, the ratios of the two infections remain roughly unchanged until the end of the process. We leave this as an open problem:

Problem 1.9
.

Consider the same definitions and notations as in Theorem
1.3
. Assume
c:=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}
exists. Suppose for two arbitrary subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
with
|A|=an
and
|B|=bn
for
a,b>0
with
A\cup B\neq\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
and
A\cap B=\emptyset
, we set
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(0)=A
and
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(0)=B
. Then
\frac{N_{n}^{(1)}}{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{a}{a+b}
, and
\frac{N_{n}^{(2)}}{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{b}{a+b}
.

This problem, coupled with our earlier observation on the infection sizes up to time
t_{\epsilon}
, resolves Conjecture
1.7
in the following way: we divide time into intervals
[0,t_{\epsilon})
and
[t_{\epsilon},T_{\mathrm{cov}}]
. At the end of the first time interval both the processes reach a linear size, with some arbitrary infection sets. Applying the Markov property at time
t_{\epsilon}
and using problem
1.9
shows that
N_{n}^{(1)}/n
and
N_{n}^{(2)}/n
converge in distribution to
\frac{E_{1}}{E_{1}+\mathrm{e}^{c}E_{2}}
and
\frac{e^{c}E_{2}}{E_{1}+\mathrm{e}^{c}E_{2}}
, respectively. Using Pólya urns, as discussed in Section
1.4.3
, one can check Problem
1.9
is true for
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
. When the long-range parameters are bounded away from
0
, we were unable to resolve this problem.

Long-range parameters bounded away from
d
.

The main assumption that we make is that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{1}(n),\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{2}(n)<d
. This is necessary for parts of the analysis carried out in the paper. When either
\alpha_{1}{(n)}
or
\alpha_{2}{(n)}
converges to
d
, estimates of the quantities
R_{n}^{(1)}
and
R_{n}^{(2)}
, as in (
1.1
), fail to hold, which play a crucial role in the analysis. The case when either or both of
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{1}(n)
and
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{2}(n)
equals
d
, remains open. In particular, we leave the following conjecture, which is a first step beyond
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,d)
into more unconstrained choices for the values of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0
:

Preliminary results

In this section we collect a number of minor technical preliminary results that we need throughout the paper. They consist of estimating the quantities

2.1
Rate estimation

Recall the quantities
R_{n}^{(1)}
and
R_{n}^{(2)}
from (
1.1
) (with
\lambda_{2}=\lambda(n)
). In this section we provide quantitative bounds and asymptotic results for these quantities. To this end, we define, with
\alpha=\alpha(n)\in[0,d)
,

R_{n}(\alpha):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
v\neq\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\|v\|^{-\alpha}.

(2.1)

It follows from
[
31
, Appendix]
that, by switching from summation to integration and applying appropriate variable substitutions, if
\alpha
is fixed and independent of
n
, then
R_{n}=(R+o(1))n^{1-\alpha/d}
as
n\to\infty
, where

R=R(\alpha,d,p)=2^{\alpha}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\|x\|^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x=2^{d}\int_{[0,\frac{1}{2}]^{d}}\|x\|^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.2)

Here,
R
depends on
\alpha,d
, and the
\ell_{p}
-norm that is used, for
p\in[1,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
. As we are only interested in the dependence on
\alpha
in what follows, we omit the arguments
d
and
p
. The final representation on the right-hand side follows from a variable substitution. This representation, however, allows us to directly conclude that
R(\alpha)
is increasing in
\alpha
, regardless of which
\ell^{p}
-norm is used. It is also readily verified that the asymptotic result
R_{n}=(R+o(1))n^{1-\alpha/d}
also holds when
\alpha=\alpha(n)\in[0,d)
depends on
n
, as long as
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(n)<d
is satisfied. Moreover, it holds along any diverging sub-sequence
(j(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, in the sense that

R_{j(n)}(\alpha(n))=(R(\alpha(n))+o(1))j(n)^{1-\alpha(n)/d},\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

(2.3)

where
R(\alpha(n))
as in (
2.2
) now also depends on
n
. We have the following useful bounds for
R_{j}(\alpha(n))
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, as well as bounds for the sum of the rates between disjoint sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, based on
R_{n}
:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha=\alpha(n)\in[0,d)
be such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(n)<d
. There exist constants
0<\underline{c}<\overline{c}<\infty
, such that, uniformly over
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\underline{c}\leq\frac{R_{j}(\alpha(n))}{j^{1-\alpha(n)/d}}\leq\overline{c}.

(2.4)

Proof.

Set
\overline{\alpha}:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(n)
. It follows from (
2.3
) that
R_{j}(\alpha(n))=(R(\alpha(n))+o(1))j^{1-\alpha(n)/d}
as
j\to\infty
, where
j
can either be independent of
n
and tend to infinity, or
j
can depend on
n
such that
n\to\infty
and
j(n)\to\infty
. Then, since
R
is increasing in
\alpha
, we bound
1=R(0)\leq R(\alpha(n))\leq R(\overline{\alpha})<\infty
, uniformly over the sequence
(\alpha(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. As a result, taking
\underline{c}
sufficiently small and
\overline{c}
sufficiently large, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,d)
, fix
A,B\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
such that
A\cap B=\emptyset
, and write
A^{c}:=\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus A
for the complement of
A
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in A}\sum_{u\in B}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\min\{|A|R_{|B|}(\alpha),|B|R_{|A|}(\alpha)\},

(2.5)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in A}\sum_{u\in B}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq\max\{|A|(R_{n}(\alpha)-R_{|B^{c}|}(\alpha)),|B|(R_{n}(\alpha)-R_{|A^{c}|}(\alpha))\}.

(2.6)

In particular, for
B=A^{c}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in A}\sum_{u\in A^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\min\{|A|R_{|A^{c}|}(\alpha),|A^{c}|R_{|A|}(\alpha)\}\leq\min\{|A|,|A|^{c}\}R_{n}(\alpha),

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in A}\sum_{u\in A^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq\max\{|A|(R_{n}(\alpha)-R_{|A|}(\alpha)),|A^{c}|(R_{n}(\alpha)-R_{|A^{c}|}(\alpha))\}.

(2.8)

Proof.

For the upper bound we observe that for each
v\in A
, the sum of the inverse distances from
v
to all vertices
u\in B
is always smaller than the sum of the inverted distances of the
|B|
many closest vertices to
v
. Hence,

\sum_{v\in A}\sum_{u\in A^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha}\leq\sum_{v\in A}R_{|B|}(\alpha)=|A|R_{|B|}(\alpha).

(2.9)

By symmetry the upper bound
|B|R_{|A|}(\alpha)
also holds.
The final upper bound (with
B=A^{c}
) directly follows since
R_{k}(\alpha)
is increasing in
k
.

For the lower bound we observe that for each
v\in A
,

\sum_{u\in B}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha}=R_{n}(\alpha)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in B^{c}\\
u\neq v\end{subarray}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha}\geq R_{n}(\alpha)-R_{|B^{c}|}(\alpha),

(2.10)

where we again use that the sum over
u\in B^{c},u\neq v
, is at most the sum of the inverted distances of the
|B^{c}|
many closest vertices to
v
. Since the lower bound does not depend on
v
, we obtain the first argument of the maximum. Symmetry again concludes the proof.
∎

2.2
Elementary results on branching processes

Let us start by defining a Yule process:

Definition 2.3

(Yule process)
.

Let
\lambda>0
. We define a rate-
\lambda

Yule process

(\mathcal{Y}(t))_{t\geq 0}
as a branching process in continuous time, started from a root
\varnothing
at time
t=0
. Each individual born into the system
(
including
\varnothing)
has an associated i.i.d. exponential clock of rate
\lambda
. Every time the clock of an individual rings, it gives birth to a child
(
receiving its own associated exponential clock
)
and resets its clock. Children are connected to their parent by an edge.

The following is a useful lemma on the size of a Yule process. To this end, recall the distribution of a geometric random variable
X
from (
1.7
):
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(p)
, when it has distribution
\mathbb{P}\left(X=k\right)=p(1-p)^{k-1},k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix
\lambda>0
and consider a rate-
\lambda
Yule process
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
.

1.

For any
t\geq 0
, it holds that
|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t})
.

2.

It holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{A\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\{\sup_{t>0}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\leq A\}\cap\{\inf_{t>0}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\geq A^{-1}\}\right)=1.

We do not prove Lemma
2.4
(1); it is quite standard, and we refer the reader to
[
27
, Section 3.4.1]
for a proof.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
(2).

Since a Yule process of rate
\lambda
, evaluated at time
t
, is equal in distribution to a Yule process of rate
1
evaluated at time
\lambda t
, we can without of generality set
\lambda=1
in the proof. It is well known that, with
E\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
,

|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}E,

(2.11)

see e.g.
[
27
, Exercise 3.16]
. Moreover, we have by
(1)
that
\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\right]=\mathrm{e}^{t}
for any
t\geq 0
. We use both results in the following. First, we can bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\geq 0}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}>A\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\leq\log(A)/2}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}>A\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\geq\log(A)/2}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}>A\right).

(2.12)

For the first probability, we bound the exponential term from above by
1
and observe that
|\mathrm{BP}(t)|
is monotone increasing in
t
. As a result, using Markov’s inequality yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\leq\log(A)/2}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}>A\right)\leq A^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{BP}(\log(A)/2)|\right]=A^{-1/2}.

(2.13)

For the second probability, we use the almost sure convergence in (
2.11
) to obtain the upper bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|\sup_{t\geq\log(A)/2}\!\!\!\!|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}-E\Big|>1\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(E>A-1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|\sup_{t\geq\log(A)/2}\!\!\!\!|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}-E\Big|>1\right)+\mathrm{e}^{1-A}.

(2.14)

The first term vanishes with
A
by (
2.11
). Thus, for any
\epsilon>0
we can choose
A
sufficiently large such that the probability on the right-hand side is at most
\epsilon/6
, as well as
\mathrm{e}^{1-A}<\epsilon/6
and
A^{-1/2}<\epsilon/6
, to arrive at the desired bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\geq 0}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}>A\right)<\epsilon/2.

(2.15)

For the infimum, we first observe that
|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-t}>A^{-1}
for any
t<\log A
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left(\inf_{t\geq 0}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}<A^{-1}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\inf_{t\geq\log A}|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}<A^{-1}\right).

(2.16)

Similar calculations as for the supremum yield that for any
\epsilon>0
, we can choose
A=A(\epsilon)
sufficiently large such that the right-hand side is at most
\epsilon/2
. Combined with the bound for the supremum and since
\epsilon
is arbitrary, we obtain the desired result.
∎

3
Coupling the infection growths to Yule processes

In this section, we introduce and analyse a coupling between the LRC process and two Yule processes, that underpins the analysis carried out in the upcoming sections. This coupling is inspired by the work of Bhamidi and Van der Hofstad
[
11
]
, where a similar coupling for first-passage percolation (FPP) on the complete graph is used to determine precise results regarding the typical distance between vertices and the hopcount (the number of edges on the edge-weighted shortest path between two vertices). Here, we extend the definition coupling to a
spatial setting
with two
different
FPP processes. Moreover, we extend the analysis of the coupling beyond the point when the coupling is no longer exact, and instead derive quantitative bounds on the defect made in the coupling.

We rigorously define the coupling in Section
3.1
and carry out the analysis of the coupling defect in Section
3.2
.

3.1
Coupling definition

Let us use the notation
\mathcal{K}_{n}
for the embedding of the complete graph obtained by taking the torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
and adding all the non-nearest-neighbor edges, where we associate to each edge
e
a pair of exponential random variables
E_{e}^{(1)}
and
E_{e}^{(2)}
, with means
R_{n}^{(1)}\|e\|^{\alpha_{1}}
and
R_{n}^{(1)}\lambda^{-1}\|e\|^{\alpha_{2}}
, respectively. We think of the infections starting from the initial vertices
v^{(1)}
and
v^{(2)}
as the flow of two types of fluid from these vertices, say Type-
1
and Type-
2
. Here, we think of the variable
E_{e}^{(i)}
as the time it takes for the Type-
i
flow to traverse the edge
e
once it reaches either of the vertices incident to
e
. We continue the process until the time that all vertices have been reached by the two fluids. At this time, letting
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}
denote the set of vertices that has been reached by the Type-
i
fluid, it is immediate to see that
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}|
has the same distribution as
N_{n}^{(i)}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

To study the sets
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}
, let us first write
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(t)
for the set of vertices in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, reached by the Type-
i
fluid at time
t\geq 0
. We couple the LRC processes
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
to two independent Yule processes
(\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
with intensities
1
and
Z_{n}
, respectively. The coupling is such that the total population sizes of the branching processes dominate the individual growths of the infections at all time, i.e.
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(t)\subseteq\mathrm{BP}^{(i)}(t)
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and all
t\geq 0
.

To rigorously set up this coupling, we need the following randomization ingredients:

a.

The complete graph
\mathcal{K}_{n}
with random edge weights
(E_{e}^{(1)},E_{e}^{(2)})
for each edge
e
.

b.

For each
x\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
, an infinite i.i.d. sequence
\mathbf{A}_{x}=(A_{x}^{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where, for each
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
,
A_{x}^{j}
is an i.i.d. random vector of length
n-1
with independent coordinates, which correspond to the edges incident to
x
. We think of the coordinates of these vectors as being indexed by the vertices in
\mathcal{K}_{n}\setminus\{x\}
, where each coordinate represents the tuple of random variables associated to the corresponding edge emanating from
x
. That is,
A_{x}^{j}(y)
denotes a tuple of random variables associated with the edge
\{x,y\}
, with
y\in\mathcal{K}_{n}\setminus\{x\}
. This tuple consists of independent copies
E_{(x,y)}^{(1),j}
and
E_{(x,y)}^{(2),j}
of the exponential random variables
E_{(x,y)}^{(1)}
and
E_{(x,y)}^{(2)}
, with means
R_{n}^{(1)}\|x-y\|^{\alpha_{1}}
and
R_{n}^{(1)}\lambda^{-1}\|x-y\|^{\alpha_{2}}
, respectively, as described above. Further, for fixed
x,y\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
, the collection
(A_{x}^{j}(y))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is an i.i.d. collection, and the collection
\mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{A}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}
is an independent collection among the vertices
x
.

c.

For each
x\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
, an infinite i.i.d. sequence of rate-one Yule processes
\mathrm{BP}_{x}^{(1)}=(\mathrm{BP}_{x,j}^{(1)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence of rate-

Yule processes
\mathrm{BP}_{x}^{(2)}=(\mathrm{BP}_{x,j}^{(2)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. Further, the collection
\mathrm{BP}=(\mathrm{BP}_{x}^{(1)},\mathrm{BP}_{x}^{(2)})_{x\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}
is an independent collection.

Roughly, one should have the following idea in mind. In the FPP process, an infection cannot travel from an infected vertex to another infected vertex simply because the latter is already infected. This is called
depletion
and causes the rate at which the infection reaches uninfected vertices from some fixed infected vertex to decrease over time. To couple the infection growth to a branching process, we counter the depletion by creating artificial ‘proxy’ vertices and edges for already infected vertices, to which the infection can travel through these artificial edges from infected vertices. This involves augmenting the network with these extra vertices and edges throughout the processes, which yields a domination of the total size of the infection growth in the FPP process, which is exactly described by the total population size of a Yule process. Keeping this heuristic in mind, we recall the tuple
A_{x}^{j}(y)=(E^{(1),j}_{(x,y)},E^{(2),j}_{(x,y)})
. One should think of
E^{(i),j}_{(x,y)}
as the time it takes for the Type-
i
flow to traverse the edge
\{x,y\}
, when it enters this edge for the
j^{\mathrm{t}h}
time (using a ‘real’ edge if
j=0
, or using an ‘artificial’ edge if
j>0
).

We now describe this intuitive idea in a rigorous manner. To this end, recall that
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(t)
is the set of vertices first reached by the Type-
i
fluid within time
t
.

Initialization step.

We describe the initialisation of the process starting from two vertices
v^{(1)}
and
v^{(2)}
. First, we remove the edge
\{v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\}
, since the vertices
v^{(1)}
and
v^{(2)}
are already infected, so that the fluids cannot flow along this edge to reach the other vertex. We also create two
inactive artificial vertices

v^{(1)}_{1}
and
v^{(2)}_{1}
, and create
artificial edges
between
v^{(i)}_{1}
and all vertices in
\mathcal{K}_{n}\setminus\{v^{(i)}\}
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
. The removal of the edge
\{v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\}
and construction of the two artificial vertices and edges is to ensure that the flows from vertices
v^{(1)}
and
v^{(2)}
occur at rates exactly equal to
1
and
Z_{n}
, respectively, which we achieve by associating appropriate edge weights to the edges. Further, through the creation of these artificial edges, it is possible for the Type-

v^{(i)}
to traverse to a ‘proxy’

of the ‘original’ vertex

, for

.

Let us now describe the edge weights. To all edges

(but not

) flow to traverse the edge

. Here, the first (resp. second) argument of the tuple encodes the time it takes the Type-
1

Let us recall the standard

property of the exponential distribution. For two i.i.d. exponential random variables

, conditionally on the event
\{E>t\}
,

has the same distribution as
t+E^{\prime}
. Using this fact, and from the definition of the coupling, the following result follows.

Proposition 3.1
.

1.

(\mathrm{BP}^{(i)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is equal in distribution to a Yule process with rate
1
if
i=1
or
Z_{n}
if
i=2
.

2.

(\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
are independent.

Proof.

Fix
t\geq 0
and condition on
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t),\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(t),\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(t)
, as well as
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)
. Each real vertex
v\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)
is infected with the Type-
1
infection, and spreads this to the uninfected real vertices in
(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t))^{c}
at rate

\frac{1}{R_{n}^{(1)}}\sum_{u\in(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t))^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}.

(3.1)

Furthermore, for each infected vertex
v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)
with
v^{\prime}\neq v
, there exists a unique artificial vertex
v^{\prime}_{j}
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
v
is connected to
v^{\prime}_{j}
and spreads to
v^{\prime}_{j}
at rate
(R_{n}^{(1)})^{-1}\|v^{\prime}-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}
. As a result, by the definition
R_{n}^{(1)}
in (
1.1
), the rate at which
v
spreads the Type-
1
infection equals

\frac{1}{R_{n}^{(1)}}\sum_{u\in(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t))^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}+\frac{1}{R_{n}^{(1)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)\\
v^{\prime}\neq v\end{subarray}}\|v^{\prime}-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}=\frac{1}{R_{n}^{(1)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
u\neq v\end{subarray}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}=1.

(3.2)

Note that the sum of the two terms is equal to one, irrespective of which vertices are in
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)
, so the rate at which the Type-
1
infection spreads from
v
equals one, independently of
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)
.

Then, consider the activated artificial vertices, activated by the Type-
1
flow, and the descendants of Type-
1
activated artificial vertices in
\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(t)
and
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(t)
, respectively. We know, by the construction of the coupling, that such vertices give birth to children at rate
1
, as they are either the root of or a child in a rate-one Yule process. As all individuals in
\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t)=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(t)
give birth at rate one for any
t\geq 0
, it thus follows that
(\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is equal in distribution to a rate-one Yule process.

For
\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t)
, we substitute
\alpha_{2}
for
\alpha_{1}
and introduce a factor
\lambda
to each rate. Recalling
R_{n}^{(2)}
from (
1.1
) and
Z_{n}
from (
1.5
), applying the same argument yields that each real vertex in
\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t)
spreads the infection at rate
Z_{n}
. Similarly, each Type-
2
activated artificial vertex and each descendant of a Type-
2
activated artificial vertex in
\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t)
gives birth to children at rate
Z_{n}
, by definition. As a result,
(\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is equal in distribution to a rate
Z_{n}
Yule process, proving
1
.

The independence of
(\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
follows from the fact that the infections spread along disjoint sets of edges, whose transmission times are independent, and by the rerouting of flow along edges to artificial edges, once vertices are infected. Finally, as the Yule processes
\mathrm{BP}^{(i)}_{v,j}
for
i\in\{1,2\},v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, and
j\in\mathbb{N}
, started when inactive artificial vertices are activated, are also independent, we arrive at
2
., which concludes the proof.
∎

3.2
Tail bounds on the coupling defect

To be able to accurately apply the coupling introduced in the previous section, it is important to keep track of the
defect
we incur while coupling CLR process with the two Yule processes
\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}
and
\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}
. That is, we require control of the size of the sets
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(t)\cup\mathcal{R}^{(i)}(t)
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and
t\geq 0
. In this section we provide bounds for this defect, which form the main technical tool used throughout the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. Controlling this defect is essential to be able to use quantitative results for Yule processes, like some of the preliminary results stated in Section
2
.

Analysis of a similar kind has been done in
[
11
]
, up to the point when defects first start to appear in the coupling - an event the authors of
[
11
]
call
collisions
. At this point in time, the defect is of constant order. We move significantly beyond this, by also controlling the size of the defect when it is of linear order and show that it is still ‘sufficiently small’ to be of use for our purposes.

We let
\tau^{(1)}_{0}=\tau^{(2)}_{0}=0
and, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\tau^{(i)}_{k}:=\inf\{t>\tau^{(i)}_{k-1}:|\mathcal{R}^{(i)}(t)|=k\}.

(3.3)

So, the number of Type-
i
artificial vertices increase by one at times
\tau_{k}^{(i)}
. Furthermore, let
(\mathrm{BP}_{j}^{(i)}(\cdot))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be an i.i.d. collection of Yule processes with rate
1
if
i=1
and with rate
Z_{n}
if
i=2
, and let us use the convention that
|\mathrm{BP}_{j}^{(i)}(a)|=0
for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
when
a\leq 0
. Then, define the random measure
C^{(i)}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and
0\leq a\leq b<\infty
, such that

C^{(i)}((a,b])=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{j}^{(i)}\in(a,b]\}}|\mathrm{BP}_{j}^{(i)}(b-\tau_{j}^{(i)})|.

(3.4)

Observe that the total size
|\mathcal{R}^{(i)}(t)\cup\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(t)|
of Type-
i
activated artificial vertices and their descendants at time
t
is exactly distributed as
C^{(i)}((0,t])
. That is,

|\mathcal{R}^{(i)}(t)\cup\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(t)|\overset{d}{=}C^{(i)}((0,t]).

(3.5)

As such, to show that the Yule process
\mathrm{BP}^{(i)}(t)
is a good approximation for the FPP cluster
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(t)
at some time
t
, is equivalent to showing that
C^{(i)}((0,t])
is not too big. To this end, we have the following result:

Proposition 3.2
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and
\delta\in(0,\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\min\{1-\alpha_{1}(n)/d,1-\alpha_{2}{(n)}/d\})
, and recall
Z_{n}
from (
1.5
). If
Z_{n}
does not converge to one, then for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
N=N(m)\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for all
n\geq N
,

\mathbb{P}\left(C^{(1)}\Big(\Big(0,\frac{\log{n}-m}{Z_{n}}\Big]\Big)>n\mathrm{e}^{-m(1+\delta)}\right)<\epsilon.

(3.6)

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}=1
instead, then there exist
M=M(\epsilon,\delta)\in\mathbb{N}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
independent
of
M
, such that for all
m\geq M
and
n\geq N
, the inequality (
3.6
) holds. Moreover,

\mathbb{P}\left(C^{(2)}\Big(\Big(0,\frac{\log{n}-m}{Z_{n}}\Big]\Big)>n\mathrm{e}^{-m(1+\delta)}\right)<\epsilon.

(3.7)

Furthermore, if
c_{n}
diverges, let
\delta_{n}
be such that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\delta_{n}<1
and
\delta_{n}\log n
diverges. Then,

C^{(1)}\Big(\Big(0,\frac{(1-\delta_{n})\log n}{Z_{n}}\Big]\Big)\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\delta_{n})\log(n)/Z_{n}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(3.8)

To prove Proposition
3.2
, we first define the
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{F}=\sigma\{(\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(s),\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(s)):0<s<\infty\}.

(3.9)

Clearly,
C^{(i)}
depends on
\mathcal{F}
, as artificial vertices are activated via the flow from infected real vertices. Towards proving Proposition
3.2
, let us start with an upper bound on the expected value of
C^{(i)}((a,b])
for some interval
(a,b]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the
\sigma
-algebra in (
3.9
), and recall
C^{(i)}
from (
3.4
). Then, for any
0\leq a\leq b<\infty
and
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[C^{(i)}((a,b])\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}\right]=\int_{a}^{b}\Bigg(\frac{1}{R_{n}^{(1)}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(t)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t)\\
v\neq u\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!(\mathbbm{1}_{\{i=1\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{i=2\}}\lambda)\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{i}}\Bigg)\mathrm{e}^{b-t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(3.10)

Proof.

We recall the sequence
(\tau_{j}^{(i)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
defined in (
3.3
); the stopping times at which artificial vertices are activated by the Type-
i
flow. By conditioning on both
\mathcal{F}
and
(\tau_{j}^{(i)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we can use Lemma
2.4
to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[C^{(i)}((a,b])\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F},(\tau_{j}^{(i)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{j}^{(i)}\in(a,b]\}}\mathrm{e}^{b-\tau_{j}^{(i)}}.

(3.11)

We now want to take the expected value with respect to the sequence
(\tau_{j}^{(i)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. We first define the function
f_{b}(t):=\mathrm{e}^{b-t}
, and note that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{j}^{(i)}\in(a,b]\}}\mathrm{e}^{b-\tau_{j}^{(i)}}=\int_{a}^{b}f_{b}(t)\,\eta^{(i)}(\mathrm{d}t),\quad\text{where}\quad\eta^{(i)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\delta_{\tau_{j}^{(i)}},

(3.12)

with
\delta
a Dirac measure. Recall the sequence of stopping times
(T_{k})_{k=2}^{n}
from (
1.3
), and let
T_{n+1}=\infty
. We observe that the sequence
(T_{k})_{k=2}^{n+1}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}
. For any
k\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
, the point process
\eta^{(i)}
, restricted to the interval
[T_{k},T_{k+1})
, is distributed as a Poisson point process
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}_{k}
, restricted to the interval
[T_{k},T_{k+1})
, with constant intensity

\frac{1}{R_{n}^{(1)}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(T_{k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{k})\cup\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{k})\\
v\neq u\end{subarray}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{i=1\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{i=2\}}\lambda)\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{i}}.

(3.13)

Moreover, as
[0,T_{3})=[T_{2},T_{3}),[T_{3},T_{4}),\ldots,[T_{n},T_{n+1})=[T_{n},\infty)
partitions
[0,\infty)
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[C^{(i)}((a,b])\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}\right]

4
Absence of coexistence

In this section, we prove an upper bound on
N_{n}^{(1)}
when
c_{n}\to\infty
, implied by the following key proposition:

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,d)
such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{i}(n)<d
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and recall

from (
1.5
). Let

denote the size of the Type-
1
infection of a LRC process, started from two distinct initial vertices

c_{n}
diverges,
(N_{n}^{(1)}n^{-1/Z_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a tight sequence of random variables.

To prove Proposition
4.1

\mathrm{I}

1
infection grows to a sub-linear size of the ‘correct order’, and the Type-
2
infection grows linearly large. In phase
\mathrm{II}
, the Type-
1
infection only increases its size up to a multiplicative constant, that is, it stays of the same order, whereas the Type-
2

\mathcal{E}_{n}(A,m,\xi):=\bigg\{\Big|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}\Big(\frac{\log n-m}{Z_{n}}\Big)\Big|\leq A\exp\Big(\frac{\log n-m}{Z_{n}}\Big)\bigg\}\cap\bigg\{\Big|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}\Big(\frac{\log n-m}{Z_{n}}\Big)\Big|\geq\xi n\bigg\}.

(4.1)

We can then formulate the following result, regarding phase
\mathrm{I}

Lemma 4.2

(Phase
\mathrm{I}
)
.

diverges, it follows that

Proposition
4.1
then readily follows:

4.1

4.2

vertices are exactly infected so far, due to the overwhelming advantage that the Type-
2
infection already has.

From here on out, we work conditionally on the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}(A,m,\xi)
, and omit its arguments for ease of writing. Furthermore, let
\ell_{n}:=n-(|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|+|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|)
and let
(A_{j})_{j=0}^{\ell_{n}},(B_{j})_{j=0}^{\ell_{n}}
denote the sets of Type-
1
and Type-
2
vertices when exactly
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|+|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|+j
many vertices have been infected. In particular,
A_{0}=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})
and
B_{0}=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})
. The evolution of these sets can be described using the discrete analogue of the competition process, as introduced in Section
1.1
.

For ease of writing, we let
S_{j}:=|A_{j}|
, for
j\in\{0,\ldots,\ell_{n}\}
, with
S_{0}=|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|\leq k_{n}^{(1)}
, conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. We then have the following result:

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{j}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the sets
(A_{i})_{i=0}^{j}
and
(B_{i})_{i=0}^{j}
. Then
(M_{j})_{j\geq 0}
, given by

M_{j}:=S_{j}\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\Big(1+\frac{R^{(1)}_{|(A_{i}\cup B_{i})^{c}|}}{|(A_{i}\cup B_{i})^{c}|(R^{(2)}_{n}-R^{(2)}_{|B_{i}^{c}|})}\Big)^{-1},\qquad j\in\{0,\ldots,\ell_{n}\},

(4.8)

is a supermartingale with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{j})_{j\geq 0}
.

Proof.

We observe that

\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j+1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{j}\right]=S_{j}+\frac{\sum_{v\in A_{j}}\sum_{u\in(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}}{\sum_{v\in A_{j}}\sum_{u\in(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}+\sum_{v\in B_{j}}\sum_{u\in(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}}\lambda\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{2}}}.

(4.9)

We first bound the denominator from below by omitting the first sum. Then, using Lemma
2.2
, we bound the numerator from above by
|A_{j}|R^{(1)}_{|(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}|}=S_{j}R_{|(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}|}^{(1)}
and the remaining term in the denominator from below by
|(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}|(R_{n}^{(2)}-R_{|B_{j}^{c}|}^{(2)})
. Overall, we obtain the upper bound

\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j+1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{j}\right]\leq S_{j}\Big(1+\frac{R_{|(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}|}^{(1)}}{|(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}|(R^{(2)}_{n}-R^{(2)}_{|B_{j}^{c}|})}\Big).

(4.10)

It then readily follows that
M_{i}
is a supermartingale, as desired.
∎

With Lemma
4.4
at hand, we can prove Lemma
4.3
:

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

First, we use the following estimate. Since, conditionally on the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,
|B_{0}^{c}|\leq(1-\xi)n
and as
|B_{j}^{c}|
is non-increasing in
j
, it follows that, uniformly in
j
,

R_{n}^{(2)}-R_{|B_{j}^{c}|}^{(2)}\geq R_{n}^{(2)}-R_{|B_{0}^{c}|}^{(2)}\geq\gamma R_{n}^{(2)},

(4.11)

almost surely, where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is a constant. We now aim to bound the expected value of
N_{n}^{(1)}
, conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. We write

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n}^{(1)}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\ell_{n}}\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[M_{\ell_{n}}\prod_{j=0}^{\ell_{n}-1}\Big(1+\frac{R^{(1)}_{|(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}|}}{|(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}|(R^{(2)}_{n}-R^{(2)}_{|B_{j}^{c}|})}\Big)\,\Bigg|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right].

(4.12)

By combining the the deterministic lower bound in (
4.11
), the fact that
|(A_{j}\cup B_{j})^{c}|=n-(|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|+|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|)-j=\ell_{n}-j
, and that
S_{0}\leq k_{n}^{(1)}
, on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, together with Lemma
4.4
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n}^{(1)}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right]\leq k_{n}^{(1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{\ell_{n}-1}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\gamma R^{(2)}_{n}}\frac{R^{(1)}_{\ell_{n}-j}}{\ell_{n}-j}\Big)\,\Bigg|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right]=k_{n}^{(1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\gamma R_{n}^{(2)}}\frac{R_{j}^{(1)}}{j}\Big)\,\Bigg|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right].

(4.13)

Since, conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we have that
\ell_{n}\leq n-k_{n}^{(2)}\leq(1-\xi)n
, we arrive at the deterministic upper bound

k_{n}^{(1)}\exp\bigg(\frac{1}{\gamma R^{(2)}_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{(1-\xi)n}\frac{R^{(1)}_{j}}{j}\bigg)\leq k_{n}^{(1)}\exp\bigg(\frac{K_{1}}{Z_{n}n^{1-\alpha_{1}/d}}\sum_{j=1}^{(1-\xi)n}j^{-\alpha_{1}/d}\bigg),

(4.14)

for some constant
K_{1}>0
. In the last step we use the inequalities in Lemma
2.1
. Now, we fix
\eta>0
. By bounding the sum from above by an integral, we obtain, for some constants
K_{2}>0
and
C>\mathrm{e}^{K_{2}}/\eta
, and using that
Z_{n}\geq 1
, the upper bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n}^{(1)}\geq Ck_{n}^{(1)}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n}^{(1)}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right]}{Ck_{n}^{(1)}}\leq C^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{K_{2}/Z_{n}}\leq C^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{K_{2}}<\eta.

As a result, conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,
N_{n}^{(1)}/k_{n}^{(1)}
is tight. Since
k_{n}^{(1)}=A\exp((\log n-m)/Z_{n})\leq An^{1/Z_{n}}
, it follows that
N_{n}^{(1)}n^{-1/Z_{n}}
is tight, conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, as well, so that the desired result follows.
∎

Coexistence and asymptotic behaviour of
N_{n}^{(1)}

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. We remark that Proposition
4.1
in Section
4
already implies that the size
N_{n}^{(1)}
of the Type-
1
infection is
o(n)
when
c_{n}
diverges, which partially proves Theorem
1.3
. We verify this in the proof of Theorem
1.3
, in Section
5.3
. To obtain the asymptotic results of Theorems
1.3

(i)
and
(ii)
, we improve on Proposition
4.2
in Sections
5.1
and
5.2
, respectively, by using the approximation of the competition process to two Yule processes, as described in Section
3
. Though we already used this to prove Proposition
4.1
, we use it in a more refined way here to prove that
N_{n}^{(1)}
converges in distribution in these two cases. Next, we prove a lower bound for

for Theorems
1.3

and
(iv)

.

5.1
The winner takes it all but one

To be able to show that the Type-
2
infection reaches all but a finite number of vertices in this section and in Section
5.2
, we require some notation. Recall from (
1.2
) that
T^{(i)}_{e}
denotes the transmission time of the Type-
i
infection to traverse the edge
e
, which is an exponential random variable with rate
(R_{n}^{(1)})^{-1}\|e\|^{-\alpha_{1}}
when
i=1
and rate
(R_{n}^{(1)})^{-1}\lambda\|e\|^{-\alpha_{2}}
when
i=2
. We then let
X_{u,v}^{(i)}
denote the edge-weighted distance between the vertices
u
and
v
, formally defined as

X_{u,v}^{(i)}:=\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma_{u,v}}\sum_{e\in\pi}T_{e}^{(i)},

(5.1)

where
\Gamma_{u,v}
is the set that consists of all self-avoiding paths from
u
to
v
in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. This was the object of study in a recent article of the first author and Van der Hofstad
[
31
]
. We generalise this to the following extent. Let
V\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
such that
u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus V
, and let
\Gamma_{u,v}^{(V)}
be the set of self-avoiding paths from
u
to
v
in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus V
. Then, define

X^{(i),(V)}_{u,v}:=\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma^{(V)}_{u,v}}\sum_{e\in\pi}T^{(i)}_{e},

(5.2)

as the length of the shortest edge-weighted path from
u
to
v
in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus V
. With this at hand, we prove Theorem
1.3

(i)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

(i)
.

Fix two distinct vertices
v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, set
V=\{v^{(1)}\}
, and start the Type-
i
infection from
v^{(i)}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. For
\beta>2
fixed, we introduce the following two events:

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)}:=\Big\{\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
u\neq v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\end{subarray}}T_{v^{(1)},u}^{(1)}>\frac{\beta\log n}{Z_{n}}\Big\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)}:=\Big\{\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
u\neq v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\end{subarray}}X_{v^{(2)},u}^{(2),(V)}<\frac{\beta\log n}{Z_{n}}\Big\}.

(5.3)

In words,
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)}
states that it takes the Type-
1
infection more than
\beta\log n/Z_{n}
amount of time to traverse any edge incident to
v^{(1)}
, and thus the Type-
i
infection potentially reaches its first vertex after time
\beta\log n/Z_{n}
. On the other hand,
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)}
states that the maximal weighted distance from
v^{(2)}
in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus V=\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus\{v^{(1)}\}
among all other vertices (not including
v^{(1)}
) for the Type-
2
infection is at most
\beta\log n/Z_{n}
. As a result, the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)}\cap\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)}
implies that the Type-
2
infection reaches
all
vertices in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus\{v^{(1)}\}
before the Type-
1
infection spreads to any vertex. Hence, it suffices to prove that the probability of
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)}\cap\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)}
converges to one to prove Case
(i)
.

We observe that the two events
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)}
are independent. Indeed, the two events consider two disjoint sets of edges (and thus disjoint sets of independent edge-weights). It hence suffices to show that either event has a probability converging to one. We can (almost) directly use
[
31
, Theorem
1.1
]
to obtain that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)})=1-o(1)
, as
\beta>2
. Indeed, this result tells us that the
flooding time
,
\max_{u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d},u\neq v^{(2)}}X^{(2)}_{v^{(2)},u}
, the time to reach all vertices in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
of a Type-
2
infection, starting from any initial vertex
v^{(2)}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, is of the order
(2+o(1))\log n/Z_{n}
. As the required result considers this same time, but on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus\{v^{(1)}\}
rather than
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, we refer the reader to
[
31
]
to see that all details indeed carry through rather than provide an almost identical proof here. Moreover, we note that
[
31
, Theorem
1.1
]
also holds with
\alpha_{2}
that can depend on
n
such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{2}(n)<d
, rather than being fixed (as in the assumption in
[
31
]
). Finally, note that a single-type LRFPP model on the complete graph, as studied in
[
31
]
, does not include the time scaling
R_{n}^{(1)}
in the rates of the exponential random variables, which here leads to the term
Z_{n}
rather than
R_{n}^{(2)}
in the denominator.

For the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)}
we use that the minimal edge-weight among
(T_{v^{(1)},u}^{(1)})_{u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus\{v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\}}
is an exponential random variable with rate
1-\|v^{(1)}-v^{(2)}\|^{-\alpha_{1}}/R_{n}^{(1)}\leq 1
, to arrive at

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)}\right)=\exp\Big(-\big(1-\|v^{(1)}-v^{(2)}\|^{-\alpha_{1}}/R_{n}^{(1)}\big)\frac{\beta\log n}{Z_{n}}\Big)\geq\exp\Big(-\frac{\beta\log n}{Z_{n}}\Big).

(5.4)

If
c_{n}/(\log n)^{2}
diverges or, equivalently,
Z_{n}/\log n
diverges, it follows that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)})=1-o(1)
, which yields the desired result.
∎

5.2
The winner takes all but finitely many

In Section
5.1
, we saw that the proof of Theorem
1.3

(i)
is based on the fact that the Type-
2
infection can spread to all vertices within time
\beta\log(n)/Z_{n}
with high probability for any
\beta>2
, since
c_{n}\gg(\log n)^{2}
and thus
Z_{n}\gg\log n
. In this section, we discuss Theorem
1.3

(ii)
, where
c_{n}=(c+o(1))(\log n)^{2}
and thus
Z_{n}=(c+o(1))\log n
. In this case, it is not sufficient to run the process up to time
2\log(n)/Z_{n}
. Instead, the aim is to run it up to time
(1+\delta_{n})\log(n)/Z_{n}
, for some small
\delta_{n}
, and show that by this time the Type-
2
infection has reached all but a negligible proportion of the vertices, whilst the approximation of the slower Type-
1
infection by the corresponding Yule process, as described in Section
3
, is in fact
exact
with high probability.

5.2.1
Typical distance on the torus with a small set removed

To be able to show that the Type-
2
infection reaches all but a small set of vertices in time
(1+\delta_{n})\log(n)/Z_{n}
, we need to strengthen a part of the first result in
[
31
, Theorem
1.1
]
. This result is concerned with
X^{(2)}_{v,U}
, as in (
5.1
), the edge-weighted distance between a fixed vertex
v
and a uniform vertex
U
in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, in a single-type LRFPP process on the torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, with long-range parameter
\alpha\in[0,d)
. Theorem
1.1
in
[
31
]
states that
X^{(2)}_{v,U}\frac{Z_{n}}{\log n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1
. In particular, for any
\epsilon>0
, the left-hand side is at most
1+\epsilon
with high probability. Here, we generalise this result by, first, allowing
\alpha
to depend on
n
, and by considering
X^{(2),(V)}_{v,U}
, as in (
5.2
), for sets
V\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
such that
|V|\leq\log n
. We also strengthen it by allowing
\epsilon=\epsilon(n)
to tend to zero with
n
. This is summarised in the following result.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
such that
|V|\leq\log n
, and take
v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash V
, and let
U
be a vertex selected uniformly at random from
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash(V\cup\{v\})
. Run a single Type-2 LRFPP on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash V
, started from
v
, with a distance metric as in (
5.2
). Finally let
t_{n}:=(1+\delta_{n})\log(n)/Z_{n}
, where
\delta_{n}
is such that
\delta_{n}=o(1)
and
\delta_{n}\log n\to\infty
. Then, there exists a constant
K>0
such that, uniformly over all sets
V
of size at most
\log n
and vertices
v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus V
,

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{v,U}^{(2),(V)}\geq t_{n}\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-K\delta_{n}\log n}.

(5.5)

Proof.

We can bound the probability in the statement of the lemma from above by replacing
U
with a supremum over all vertices in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus V
that are not
v
. That is,

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{v,U}^{(2),(V)}\geq t_{n}\right)\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash V\\
w\neq v\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{v,w}^{(2),(V)}\geq t_{n}\right).

(5.6)

We bound
X_{v,w}^{(2),(V)}
from above by the time it takes two independent LRFPP processes on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash V
, started at
v
and
w
, to reach
a_{n}:=\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
many individuals, plus the shortest transmission time
T^{(2)}_{(x,y)}
, among vertices
x
and
y
in the process started at
v
and
w
, respectively. We let
\tau_{j}^{(v)}
denote the time it takes for the infection started from
v
to reach
j\in[n-2]
many vertices, and let
B_{v}(\tau_{j}^{(v)})
denote the set of vertices, reach by
v
at time
\tau_{j}^{(v)}
. We then have the upper bound

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash V\\
w\neq v\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{(v)}+\tau_{a_{n}}^{(w)}+\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B_{w}(\tau^{(w)}_{a_{n}}),y\in B_{v}(\tau^{(v)}_{a_{n}})\end{subarray}}T^{(2)}_{(x,y)}\geq t_{n}\right).

(5.7)

Indeed, if the two sets
B_{v}(\tau^{(v)}_{a_{n}})
and
B_{w}(\tau^{(w)}_{a_{n}})
are disjoint, then the sum of these three terms provides the edge-weighted distance of one possible path from
w
to
v
, and hence is an upper bound for
X_{v,w}^{(2),(V)}
. If the two sets are not disjoint, then the minimum equals zero and the first two terms already are an upper bound for
X_{v,w}^{(2),(V)}
, since
v
and
w
can be connected within time
\tau_{a_{n}}^{(v)}+\tau_{a_{n}}^{(w)}
. We further split this probability into the two terms

2\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{(v)}\geq\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta_{n}}{4}\Big)\frac{\log n}{Z_{n}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B_{w}(\tau^{(w)}_{a_{n}}),y\in B_{v}(\tau^{(v)}_{a_{n}})\end{subarray}}T^{(2)}_{(x,y)}\geq\frac{\delta_{n}}{2}\frac{\log n}{Z_{n}}\right),

(5.8)

where the multiplicative factor in front of the first term is due to symmetry for
v
and
w
. Let us start by bounding the second term from above. As a minimum of exponential random variables is an exponential random variable, whose rate equals the sum of all the individual rates, we can condition on
B_{w}(\tau^{(w)}_{a_{n}})
and
B_{v}(\tau^{(v)}_{a_{n}})
to obtain

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B_{w}(\tau^{(w)}_{a_{n}}),y\in B_{v}(\tau^{(v)}_{a_{n}})\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!T^{(2)}_{(x,y)}\geq\frac{\delta_{n}\log n}{2Z_{n}}\bigg)=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\exp\bigg(\!-\frac{\delta_{n}}{2}\frac{\log n}{R_{n}^{(2)}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{x\in B_{w}(\tau^{(w)}_{a_{n}})}\sum_{y\in B_{v}(\tau^{(v)}_{a_{n}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\lambda\|x-y\|^{-\alpha_{2}}\bigg)\Bigg].

(5.9)

We now bound
\|x-y\|^{-\alpha_{2}}\geq Cn^{-\alpha_{2}/d}
for some large constant
C>0
, since we can bound the distance between
x
and
y
from above by a constant times
n^{1/d}
for any
x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. Using this, and the fact that
R_{n}^{(2)}\lambda^{-1}=\Theta(n^{1-\alpha_{2}/d})
by Lemma
2.1
, we obtain for some constants
C,C^{\prime},\kappa_{1}>0
,

\exp\Big(-\frac{\delta_{n}}{2}\frac{\log n}{R^{(2)}_{n}}\lambda a_{n}^{2}Cn^{-\alpha_{2}/d}\Big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{\delta_{n}}{2}C^{\prime}\log n\Big)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{1}\delta_{n}\log n},

(5.10)

for some constant
\kappa_{1}>0
. We then turn to the first term in (
5.8
). First, we observe that the rate
\Lambda_{j}^{(V)}
at which the infection reaches the next vertex, given that the infection consists of
j\in[a_{n}-1]
many vertices, satisfies the lower bound
\Lambda_{j}^{(V)}\geq j(R_{n}^{(1)})^{-1}(R_{n}^{(2)}-R_{|V|+(j-1)}^{(2)})\geq jZ_{n}(1-d_{n})
almost surely, by Lemma
2.2
, where
d_{n}:=R^{(2)}_{2a_{n}}/R_{n}^{(2)}
. As a result, with
(E_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of i.i.d. rate-one exponential random variables, we can almost surely bound

\tau_{a_{n}}^{(v)}=\sum_{j=1}^{a_{n}-1}\frac{E_{j}}{\Lambda_{j}^{(V)}}\leq\frac{1}{Z_{n}(1-d_{n})}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{n}-1}\frac{E_{j}}{j}.

(5.11)

We now observe that

d_{n}=(1+o(1))\Big(\frac{2a_{n}}{n}\Big)^{1-\alpha_{2}/d}\leq 2n^{-(1-\alpha_{2}/d)/2}=o(\delta_{n}),

(5.12)

where the last step holds since
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{2}(n)<d
and
\delta_{n}\log n\to\infty
. We then use the upper bound in (
5.11
) together with a Chernoff bound with
s\in(0,1)
, to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{(v)}\geq\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta_{n}}{4}\Big)\frac{\log n}{Z_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(s\sum_{j=1}^{a_{n}-1}\frac{E_{j}}{j}\geq s\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta_{n}}{4}\Big)(1-d_{n})\log n\right)

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{s}{2}\Big(1+\frac{\delta_{n}}{2}\Big)(1-d_{n})\log n\Big)\prod_{j=1}^{a_{n}-1}\frac{j}{j-s}.

We bound the product from above by

\prod_{j=1}^{a_{n}-1}\frac{j}{j-s}\leq\exp\bigg(s\sum_{j=1}^{a_{n}-1}\frac{1}{j-s}\bigg)=\exp(s\log(a_{n})+\mathcal{O}(1))=\exp\Big(\frac{s}{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)\Big).

(5.14)

Combined, we thus arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{(v)}\geq\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta_{n}}{4}\Big)\frac{\log n}{Z_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\frac{s}{2}\log n-\frac{s}{2}\Big(1+\frac{\delta_{n}}{2}\Big)(1-d_{n})\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)\Big)

(5.15)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-s\delta_{n}\log(n)\Big[\frac{1}{4}-\frac{d_{n}}{2\delta_{n}}-\frac{d_{n}}{4}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(1)\Big).

Since
d_{n}=o(\delta_{n})
, it follows that the terms within the brackets are strictly positive for all
n
sufficiently large. Moreover, since
\delta_{n}\log n
diverges with
n
, we obtain that the probability on the left-hand side tends to zero with
n
as
\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{2}\delta_{n}\log n}
for some constant
\kappa_{2}>0
. Combining this with (
5.10
) in (
5.8
), we obtain the desired result for any
K<\kappa_{1}\wedge\kappa_{2}
and
n
sufficiently large.
∎

5.2.2
Proof of Theorem
1.3

(ii)

Proof of Theorem
1.3

(ii)
.

The aim of the proof is to show that by the time all vertices have been infected, the size of the Type-
1
infection is equal in distribution to the size of a rate-one Yule process stopped at time
1/c
, which by Lemma
2.4
has the desired distribution. To this end, we use the coupling to two independent Yule processes, as in Section
3.1
.

We define a non-negative sequence
(\delta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
and
\delta_{n}\log n\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. It follows that
n^{1-\delta_{n}}=o(n)
. We also define
s_{n}:=(1-\delta_{n})\log(n)/Z_{n}
and
t_{n}:=(1+\delta_{n})\log(n)/Z_{n}
. Finally, we define the events

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,n}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(s_{n})=\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(s_{n})=\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n}),\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})\leq\log n\},

(5.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2,n}

\displaystyle:=\{|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(s_{n})|\leq n^{1-\delta_{n}/2},|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|\geq n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3,n}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{\mathrm{cov}})=\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})\},

where
\xi>0
is small and to be determined later, and where we recall that
T_{\mathrm{cov}}
is the time when all vertices of the torus are reached by either the Type-
1
or the Type-
2
infection. The aim is to prove that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{3,n}\right)
tends to
1
with
n
. Indeed, this is sufficient, since
t_{n}\to 1/c
as
Z_{n}=(c+o(1))\log n
implies that
\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(1/c)
. As a result, we have
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{\mathrm{cov}})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(1/c)
, and hence
N_{n}^{(1)}=|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{\mathrm{cov}})|\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(1/c)|
. The latter limiting random variable is equal in distribution to a geometric random variable with success probability
p=\mathrm{e}^{-1/c}
, as desired, which follows from Lemma
2.4
.

Let us start by observing that the event
\mathcal{E}_{1,n}
implies that
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(s_{n})=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})=\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})
, whereas the event
\mathcal{E}_{2,n}
implies that, by time
s_{n}
, there are still uninfected vertices, and by time
t_{n}
the Type-
2
infection has reached almost all vertices. Hence, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,n}
, the event
\mathcal{E}_{3,n}
holds when all uninfected vertices after time
t_{n}
(if any) are reached by the Type-
2
infection. We start by proving that the events
\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,n}
hold with high probability, and then turn to the event
\mathcal{E}_{3,n}
.

To prove that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\right)
tends to one, we recall that

\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(s_{n})=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(s_{n})\cup\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(s_{n})\cup\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(s_{n}),

(5.17)

so that the first equality in the event holds when
|\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(s_{n})\cup\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(s_{n})|=0
. We use Lemma
3.2
to show this holds with high probability, since

|\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(s_{n})\cup\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(s_{n})|=C^{(1)}((0,s_{n}]),

(5.18)

where we recall
C^{(1)}
from (
3.4
). Further, the second equality in the event
\mathcal{E}_{1,n}
is implied if no vertex in
\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(s_{n})
gives birth to a child in the time interval
(s_{n},t_{n}]
. As a result, by a union bound and Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1,n}^{c}\right)\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(C^{(1)}((0,s_{n}])\geq 1\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\exists v\in\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(s_{n}):v\text{ has child in }(s_{n},t_{n}]\right)

(5.19)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})|>\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(C^{(1)}((0,s_{n}])\geq 1\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(s_{n})|\right]\big(1-\mathrm{e}^{-(t_{n}-s_{n})}\big)+\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})|\right]\frac{1}{\log n}.

The probability on the right-hand side tends to zero by Lemma
3.2
, which states that

C^{(1)}((0,s_{n}])\mathrm{e}^{-s_{n}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(5.20)

Since
Z_{n}=(c+o(1))\log n
for some
c>0
, so that
s_{n}
converges to
1/c
, it in fact holds that
C^{(1)}((0,s_{n}])\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
. The two expected values equal
\mathrm{e}^{s_{n}}
and
\mathrm{e}^{t_{n}}
, respectively, by Lemma
2.4
, both of which converge to
\mathrm{e}^{1/c}
. Furthermore, as
t_{n}-s_{n}=2\delta_{n}\log(n)/Z_{n}=o(1)
, the entire right-hand side of (
5.19
) tends to zero with
n
.

We then show that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{2,n}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\right)
tends to zero with
n
. First, we use that
|\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(s_{n})|\mathrm{e}^{-Z_{n}s_{n}}
converges almost surely (as
Z_{n}s_{n}
diverges with
n
) by Lemma
2.4
. As a result,

|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(s_{n})|\leq|\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(s_{n})|\leq\mathrm{e}^{Z_{n}s_{n}+\delta_{n}\log(n)/2}=n^{1-\delta_{n}/2},

(5.21)

with high probability. Now, we prove that
|\mathcal{N}^{(2)}(t_{n})|\geq n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}
holds with probability tending to one for any
\xi
sufficiently small. Here, we use the following approach. On the event
\mathcal{E}_{1,n}
, we know that
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})=\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})
, which has size at most
\log n
. By conditioning on
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})
, we can dominate
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})
as follows: we let a single Type-
2
infection spread from
v^{(2)}
on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})
for the time interval
[0,t_{n}]
to yield a set of infected vertices
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})
. The single Type-
2
infection on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})
has fewer vertices to spread to compared to the Type-
2
infection in the competition process between Type-
1
and Type-
2
during the time interval
[0,t_{n}]
. Indeed,
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})
and thus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t)
for any
t\in[0,t_{n}]
, almost surely. It is hence clear that we can couple the two infection processes, so that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})
almost surely, conditionally on
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})
. As a result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\{|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|<n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{1,n}}\mathbb{P}\Big(|\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|<n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n}),\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})\Big)\Big]

(5.22)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{1,n}}\mathbb{P}\left(|\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|<n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})\right)\right],

where we can omit
\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})
from the conditioning since, conditionally on
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})
, the event
\{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|<n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\}
does not depend on
\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})
(and on the event
\mathcal{E}_{1,n}
, we have
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})=\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})
, too). We can bound the expected value from above by

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}D\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
|D|\leq\log n\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(|\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|<n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})=D\right).

(5.23)

Then, using the distance metric in (
5.2
), we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|<n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})=D\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus D}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{v^{(2)},u}^{(2),(D)}\leq t_{n}\}}<n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\right)

(5.24)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus D}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{v^{(2)},u}^{(2),(D)}>t_{n}\}}>n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}-|D|\right).

Here, we note that
n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}-|D|
tends to infinity with
n
, since
\delta_{n}=o(1)
and
|D|\leq\log n
. By a Markov inequality, we then obtain the upper bound

\frac{n-|D|}{n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}-|D|}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{v^{(2)},U}^{(2),(D)}\geq t_{n}\right),

(5.25)

where
U
is a vertex selected uniformly at random from
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\setminus D
. Using Lemma
5.1
, the probability can be bounded from above, uniformly in the set
D
and the vertex
v^{(2)}
, by
\mathrm{e}^{-K\delta_{n}\log n}
for some
K>0
. The fraction
\frac{n-|D|}{n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}-|D|}
equals
(1+o(1))n^{\xi\delta_{n}}
, uniformly in the choice of
D
, so that choosing
\xi<K
yields that the upper bound tends to zero with
n
. Combining this with (
5.21
), we obtain that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{2,n}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\right)=o(1)
, as desired.

Finally, we turn to
\mathcal{E}_{3,n}
. Recall the discrete description of the LRC process in Section
1.1
. As discussed after (
5.16
), the event
\mathcal{E}_{3,n}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,n}
implies that all vertices that remain uninfected at time
t_{n}
(if any) are all reached by the Type-
2
infection. We prove this using the discrete version of the process. Here, we also use the event
\mathcal{E}_{2,n}
, which provides a quantitative lower bound on the number of vertices reached by the Type-
2
infection at time
t_{n}
.

We define

\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\{|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|\leq\log n,\ |\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|\geq n-n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}\},

(5.26)

and note that
\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,n}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n}
, as follows from (
5.16
). We then bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{3,n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{3,n}\cap\mathcal{C}_{n}\right)

(5.27)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\{\text{Type-$2$ infects all remaining }n-|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|-|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|\text{ vertices}\}\cap\mathcal{C}_{n}).

We write
A_{0}:=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})
and let
B_{i}
be the set of vertices infected by Type-
2
when there are exactly
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|+|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|+i
many vertices infected, for
i\in\{0,\ldots,n-|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|-|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|\}
. In particular,
B_{0}=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})
. We also set
\ell_{n}:=n-|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|-|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|=n-|A_{0}|-|B_{0}|
and define the events
\mathcal{S}_{i}:=\{|B_{i}|=|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(t_{n})|+i\}
for
i\in[\ell_{n}]
. We can then write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{\text{Type-$2$ infects all remaining }\ell_{n}\text{ vertices}\}\cap\mathcal{C}_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell_{n}}\mathcal{S}_{i}\Big)\right)

(5.28)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell_{n}}\mathcal{S}_{i}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\right)\right].

We now investigate the conditional probabilities

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{S}_{i}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\right),\qquad j\in[\ell_{n}].

(5.29)

Fix
j\in[\ell_{n}]
. We write the conditional probability as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{S}_{i}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,|\,A_{0},B_{0},(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}})_{k\in[j-1]}\right]\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\right].

(5.30)

There is a positive contribution to the outer expectation only when the indicators
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
equal one for all
k\in[j-1]
. As a result, we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}{}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,|\,A_{0},B_{0},(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}})_{k\in[j-1]}\right]

(5.31)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\frac{\sum_{v\in A_{0}}\sum_{u\in(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}}{\sum_{v\in B_{j-1}}\sum_{u\in(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}}\lambda\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{2}}}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0},(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}})_{k\in[j-1]}\right],

so that we arrive at the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{}

\displaystyle\Bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{S}_{i}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\Bigg)

(5.32)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\frac{\sum_{v\in A_{0}}\sum_{u\in(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}}{\sum_{v\in B_{j-1}}\sum_{u\in(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}}\lambda\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{2}}}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0},(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}})_{k\in[j-1]}\right]\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\right].

Again, there is a positive contribution to the outer expectation only when the indicators
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
equal one for all
k\in[j-1]
, in which case
|B_{j-1}^{c}|=n-(|B_{0}|+(j-1))=\ell_{n}+|A_{0}|-(j-1)
and
|(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}|=n-|A_{0}|-|B_{j-1}|=\ell_{n}-(j-1)
. Using Lemma
2.2
, we can then bound,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in A_{0}}\sum_{u\in(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}}\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq|A_{0}|R^{(1)}_{|(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}|}=|A_{0}|R^{(1)}_{\ell_{n}-(j-1)},

(5.33)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in B_{j-1}}\sum_{u\in(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}}\lambda\|u-v\|^{-\alpha_{2}}

\displaystyle\geq|(A_{0}\cup B_{j-1})^{c}|(R_{n}^{(2)}-R^{(2)}_{|B_{j-1}^{c}|})

(5.34)

\displaystyle=(\ell_{n}-(j-1))(R_{n}^{(2)}-R^{(2)}_{\ell_{n}+|A_{0}|-(j-1)}).

(5.35)

This yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{S}_{i}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\Big(1-\frac{|A_{0}|R^{(1)}_{\ell_{n}-(j-1)}}{(\ell_{n}-(j-1))(R_{n}^{(2)}-R^{(2)}_{\ell_{n}+|A_{0}|-(j-1)})}\Big)\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\right]

(5.36)

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{|A_{0}|R^{(1)}_{\ell_{n}-(j-1)}}{(\ell_{n}-(j-1))(R_{n}^{(2)}-R^{(2)}_{\ell_{n}+|A_{0}|-(j-1)})}\Big)\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{j-1}\mathcal{S}_{i}\,\Bigg|\,A_{0},B_{0}\right).

Applying this lower bound recursively in (
5.28
), we thus arrive at the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{}

\displaystyle(\{\text{Type-$2$ infects all remaining }\ell_{n}\text{ vertices}\}\cap\mathcal{C}_{n})

(5.37)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\Big(1-\frac{|A_{0}|R^{(1)}_{\ell_{n}-(j-1)}}{(\ell_{n}-(j-1))(R_{n}^{(2)}-R^{(2)}_{\ell_{n}+|A_{0}|-(j-1)})}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\Big(1-\frac{\log(n)R^{(1)}_{\ell_{n}-(j-1)}}{(\ell_{n}-(j-1))(R_{n}^{(2)}-R^{(2)}_{\ell_{n}+\log n-(j-1)})}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\bigg(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\frac{\log(n)R^{(1)}_{\ell_{n}-(j-1)}}{(\ell_{n}-(j-1))(R_{n}^{(2)}-R^{(2)}_{\ell_{n}+\log n-(j-1)})}\bigg)\right].

Here, we use that
R^{(2)}_{n}
is increasing in
n
and that
|A_{0}|\leq\log n
by the event
\mathcal{C}_{n}
in the final step. We now also use that
\ell_{n}=n-|A_{0}|-|B_{0}|\leq n-|B_{0}|\leq n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}
by the event
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, and let
i=\ell_{n}-(j-1)
, to obtain the lower bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}\right)\bigg(1-\sum_{i=1}^{n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}}\frac{\log(n)R^{(1)}_{i}}{i(R^{(2)}_{n}-R^{(2)}_{i+\log n})}\bigg).

(5.38)

Since
\delta_{n}
is such that
\delta_{n}\log n
diverges, it follows that
n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}+\log n=o(n)
, for any
\xi>0
. Hence,
R^{(2)}_{n}-R^{(2)}_{i+\log n}=R_{n}^{(2)}(1-o(1))=Z_{n}R_{n}^{(1)}(1-o(1))
. Moreover, we can bound
\underline{c}i^{1-\alpha_{i}/d}\leq R_{i}^{(1)}\leq\overline{c}i^{1-\alpha_{1}/d}
for some constants
\underline{c},\overline{c}>0
, which are independent of
i
, using Lemma
2.1
. As a result, we have the lower bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}\right)\bigg(1-\frac{\overline{c}\log n}{Z_{n}\underline{c}n^{1-\alpha_{1}d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{1-\xi\delta_{n}}}i^{-\alpha_{1}/d}\bigg)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}\right)\Big(1-C\frac{\log n}{Z_{n}}n^{-(1-\alpha_{1}/d)\xi\delta_{n}}\Big).

(5.39)

Since
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{1}(n)<d
,
\delta_{n}\log n
diverges, and
Z_{n}=(c+o(1))\log n
, we find that this lower bound equals
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}\right)(1-o(1))
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,n}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n}
and we already proved that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1,n}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,n}\right)=1-o(1)
, we thus finally use (
5.27
) to derive that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{3,n}\right)=1-o(1)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

5.3
Lower bound for
N_{n}^{(1)}

In this section we prove a lower bound for the size of the Type-
1
infection in Theorem
1.3

(iii)
and
(iv)
. This is summarised in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.2
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,d)
such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{i}(n)<d
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and recall
Z_{n}
from (
1.5
). Let
N_{n}^{(1)}
denote the size of the Type-
1
infection of an LRC process, started from two distinct initial vertices
v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. Fix
\xi\in(0,1)
and define
\epsilon_{n}:=\xi(\frac{c_{n}}{\log n}\wedge 1)
. When
c_{n}=o((\log n)^{2})
and
c_{n}
diverges, the event
\{N_{n}^{(1)}\geq n^{(1-(1+\xi)\epsilon_{n})/Z_{n}}\}
holds with high probability.

Proof.

We couple the infection processes with two Yule processes, as in Section
3.1
. We define
t_{n}:=(1-\epsilon_{n})\log(n)/Z_{n}
, and

\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(1)}:=\{|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|\geq n^{(1-(1+\xi)\epsilon_{n})/Z_{n}}\}.

(5.40)

It follows that
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(1)}
implies the desired result, since the size of the Type-
1
infection is monotone increasing in time and stays constant when all vertices have been infected, so that
N_{n}^{(1)}\geq|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|\geq n^{(1-(1+\xi)\epsilon_{n})/Z_{n}}
. We thus aim to show that this event holds with high probability.

Since
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}<1
and
c_{n}
diverges, we can apply Lemma
3.2
with
\delta_{n}=\epsilon_{n}
. We use the second result in Lemma
3.2
, which states that
C^{(1)}((0,t_{n}])\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
. Since
Z_{n}=o(\log n)
(that is,
c_{n}=o((\log n)^{2}
), it follows that
t_{n}
diverges with
n
, so that

\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})|\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}E_{1},

(5.41)

where
E_{1}
is a rate-one exponential random variable. Combined, we thus find that

\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(t_{n})|=\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}\big(|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t_{n})|-C^{(1)}((0,t_{n}])\big)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}E_{1}.

(5.42)

As a result, since
c_{n}
diverges but
c_{n}=o((\log n)^{2})
, and thus

n^{(1-(1+\xi)\epsilon_{n})/Z_{n}}=\mathrm{e}^{t_{n}}n^{-\xi\epsilon_{n}/Z_{n}}=\exp\Big(t_{n}-\xi^{2}\frac{(c_{n}\wedge\log n)\log n}{\log n+c_{n}}\Big)=o\big(\mathrm{e}^{t_{n}}),

(5.43)

it follows that the event
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(1)}
holds with high probability, which concludes the proof.
∎

We conclude this section by giving the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We start by proving the most general result that, when
c_{n}
diverges, it follows that
N_{n}^{(1)}/n\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
. This directly follows from the fact that

n^{1/Z_{n}}=n^{1-(Z_{n}-1)/Z_{n}}=n\exp\Big(-\frac{c_{n}\log n}{\log n+c_{n}}\Big)=o(n),

(5.44)

where the final step holds since
c_{n}
diverges. Hence, since
(N_{n}^{(1)}n^{-1/Z_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a tight sequence by Proposition
4.1
, it follows that
N_{n}^{(1)}/n\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
.

We then prove the more precise results on the size of
N_{n}^{(1)}
in the four cases in the theorem. We note that the proofs of Cases
(i)
and
(ii)
follow from Sections
5.1
and
5.2
, respectively. To prove Cases
(iii)
and
(iv)
, fix
\epsilon>0
and let
A=A(\epsilon)
be a sufficiently large constant, such that
\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n}^{(1)}\geq An^{1/Z_{n}}\right)<\epsilon
, which is possible by Proposition
4.1
. Then, in Case
(iii)
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
,

\frac{\log(N_{n}^{(1)})Z_{n}}{\log n}\leq 1+\log(A)\frac{Z_{n}}{\log n}.

(5.45)

Since
c_{n}=o((\log n)^{2})
in Case
(iii)
, so that
Z_{n}=o(\log n)
, it follows that the right-hand side is smaller than
1+\xi
for
n
sufficiently large with probability at least
1-\epsilon
, for any
\xi>0
. Since we can take
\epsilon
arbitrarily small, independently of
\xi
, it follows that
\log(N_{n}^{(1)})Z_{n}/\log n
is at most
1+\xi
with high probability for any
\xi>0
. To prove a matching lower bound, we use Proposition
5.2
to conclude that
N_{n}^{(1)}\geq n^{(1+(1+\zeta)\epsilon_{n})/Z_{n}}
, where
\epsilon_{n}:=\zeta(\frac{c_{n}}{\log n}\wedge 1)
, holds with high probability for any
\zeta>0
. By taking the logarithm on both sides and rearranging terms, we directly obtain that

\log(N_{n}^{(1)})\frac{Z_{n}}{\log n}\geq 1-(1+\zeta)\epsilon_{n}.

(5.46)

As
\epsilon_{n}\leq\zeta
, the right-hand side equals
1-(1+\zeta)\zeta=1-\xi
by letting
\xi:=\zeta(1+\zeta)
. Since
\zeta
is arbitrary, we obtain that
\log(N_{n}^{(1)})Z_{n}/\log n
is at most
1-\xi
with high probability for any
\xi>0
. Combined with the upper bound, this concludes the proof of Case
(iii)
.

In Case
(iv)
we can be more precise in (
5.45
) by observing that
Z_{n}=1+c_{n}/\log n
, so that

c_{n}^{-1}(\log n-\log N_{n}^{(1)})\geq\frac{\log n}{\log n+c_{n}}-\frac{\log A}{c_{n}}.

(5.47)

Since, in Case
(iv)
,
c_{n}
diverges such that
c_{n}=o(\log n)
, it follows that the right-hand side is at least
1-\xi
for
n
sufficiently large with probability at least
1-\epsilon
, for any
\xi>0
. Again, we obtain that
c_{n}^{-1}(\log n-\log N_{n}^{(1)})\geq 1-\xi
with high probability for any
\xi>0
. We then rewrite (
5.46
) to obtain, with high probability,

c_{n}^{-1}(\log n-\log N_{n}^{(1)})\leq\frac{\log n}{\log n+c_{n}}(1+\zeta(1+\zeta))\leq 1+\xi,

(5.48)

when we again set
\xi:=\zeta(1+\zeta)
. Since
\zeta
is arbitrary, we obtain a matching upper bound, which proves Case
(iv)
and concludes the proof.
∎

5.4
Coexistence: Proof of Theorem
1.4

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Our aim is to check (
1.4
) for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Note that this is implied by the following: There exists a function
f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}f(x)=0
, such that for any
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(T_{n})|}{n}\geq f(\epsilon)\right)\geq 1-\epsilon,

(5.49)

for some suitable stopping time
T_{n}
. Here note that the stopping time
T_{n}
need not be
T_{\mathrm{cov}}
, and can be something strictly smaller, since infected vertices stay infected forever and thus
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(t)|
is non-decreasing in
t
. To this end, let us fix
\epsilon>0
and take
m>0
. Consider the events

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}(m)

\displaystyle:=\{\inf_{t>0}|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-t}>m^{-1}\}\cap\{\inf_{t>0}|\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-Z_{n}t}>m^{-1}\}.

By Lemma
2.4
, we can choose an
M_{1}=M_{1}(\epsilon)
large enough so that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}_{1}(m)\right)>1-\epsilon/2
for all
m\geq M_{1}
. Further, let us fix
\delta\in(0,\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\min\{1-\alpha_{1}(n)/d,1-\alpha_{2}(n)/d\})
. By using Proposition
3.2
, we can choose
M_{2}=M_{2}(\epsilon,\delta)
large enough so that for all
m\geq M_{2}
, the event

\mathcal{G}_{2}(m):=\{C^{(1)}((0,\log n-m])\leq n\mathrm{e}^{-m(1+\delta)}\}\cap\{C^{(2)}((0,\log n-m])\leq n\mathrm{e}^{-m(1+\delta)}\}

(5.50)

holds with probability at least
1-\epsilon/2
, and that
\mathrm{e}^{-m}m^{-1}>\mathrm{e}^{-m(1+\delta)}
holds for all
m\geq M_{2}
. Consider
M=M(\epsilon)=\max\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
, define the (deterministic) stopping time
T_{n}:=\log{n}-M
, and set
f(\epsilon):=\mathrm{e}^{-M}M^{-1}-\mathrm{e}^{-M(1+\delta)}
. Further, let
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{1}(M)\cap\mathcal{G}_{2}(M)
. We aim to show that the event
\mathcal{G}
implies the desired bound
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(i)}(T_{n})|/n\geq f(\epsilon)
for both
i=1
and
i=2
. Let us start with
i=1
. First, by the event
\mathcal{G}_{1}(M)
, we have
|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(T_{n})|>\mathrm{e}^{T_{n}}M^{-1}=n\mathrm{e}^{-M}M^{-1}
. Second, by the event
\mathcal{G}_{2}(M)
, we have

|\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(T_{n})|+|\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(T_{n})|\overset{d}{=}C^{(1)}((0,\log n-M])\leq n\mathrm{e}^{-M(1+\delta)}.

(5.51)

Combined, by the choice of
M
, we obtain that

|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{n})|=|\mathrm{BP}^{(1)}(T_{n})|-(|\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(T_{n})|+|\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(T_{n})|)\geq n(\mathrm{e}^{-M}M^{-1}-\mathrm{e}^{-M(1+\delta)})=nf(\epsilon).

(5.52)

For
i=2
, we similarly have that

|\mathrm{BP}^{(2)}(T_{n})|\geq M^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{Z_{n}T_{n}}=nM^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{c_{n}-MZ_{n}}\geq nM^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-MZ_{n}}.

(5.53)

Since
c_{n}
is bounded in
n
and thus
Z_{n}=1+o(1)
, we can bound the right-hand side from below by
\mathrm{e}^{-M(1+\delta)}
for all
n
sufficiently large by our choice of
M
. This yields the same conclusions in (
5.52
), but for the Type-
2
infection, so that also
|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{n})|\geq nf(\epsilon)
is implied by
\mathcal{G}
. Hence, we arrive at

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\big\{|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(1)}(T_{n})|/n\geq f(\epsilon)\big\}\cap\big\{|\mathcal{N}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{n})|/n\geq f(\epsilon)\big\}\right)\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}\right)\geq 1-\epsilon,

(5.54)

which concludes the proof.

∎
