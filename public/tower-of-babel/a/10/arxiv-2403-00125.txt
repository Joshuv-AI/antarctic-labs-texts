Penalty-free discontinuous Galerkin method
Jan Jaśkowiec, N. Sukumar

Abstract
In this paper, we present a new high-order discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method, in which neither a penalty parameter nor a stabilization parameter is needed. We refer to this method as penalty-free DG (PF-DG). In this method, the trial and test functions belong to the broken Sobolev space, in which the functions are in general discontinuous on the mesh skeleton and do not meet the Dirichlet boundary conditions. However, a subset can be distinguished in this space, where the functions are continuous and satisfy the Dirichlet boundary conditions, and this subset is called admissible. The trial solution is chosen to lie in an augmented admissible subset, in which a small violation of the continuity condition is permitted. This subset is constructed by applying special augmented constraints to the linear combination of finite element basis functions. In this approach, all the advantages of the DG method are retained without the necessity of using stability parameters or numerical fluxes. Several benchmark problems in two dimensions (Poisson equation, linear elasticity, hyperelasticity, and biharmonic equation) on polygonal (triangles, quadrilateral and weakly convex polygons) meshes as well as a three-dimensional Poisson problem on hexahedral meshes are considered. Numerical results are presented that affirm the sound accuracy and optimal convergence of the method in the L 2 L^{2} norm and the energy seminorm.

In this paper, we introduce a penalty-free discontinuous Galerkin (PF-DG) method that is applicable to standard finite elements as well as general polygonal and polyhedral meshes. Use of such general meshes has appeared in polygonal and polyhedral finite element methods,
[

DG methods on polytopes,

Unlike classical DG methods and the virtual element method that require stabilization, the PF-DG method proposed herein does not need a penalty nor stabilization parameter, while retaining the flexibility of being applicable on polytopal meshes.
In the PF-DG method, the finite-dimensional broken Sobolev space is used to search for approximate solutions in which some equality constraints are defined, which allows one to choose only those functions that are continuous and satisfy the inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. These constraints are applied using the least squares approach on the mesh skeleton as well as the outer boundary, and as a consequence, a subset of admissible functions is obtained,
which consists only of continuous functions that meet the boundary conditions on the Dirichlet boundary. The admissible functions are constructed by solving a least-square constraints problem. However, numerical computation of these constraints on polygonal meshes proves to be overly restrictive, which causes numerical instabilities.
Thus, an
augmented
admissible subset is used so that very small discontinuities on the mesh skeleton are allowed. The constraint problem leads to a linear system of equations that is solved prior to the main discrete problem. The matrix in the constraints problem is rectangular and singular, which admits a nonunique solution, and the null-space method
[
58
,
59
]
is applied to obtain the complete solution. It should be noted here that in Oden at al.
[
48
]
, a penalty-free DG method is proposed, but this method has not been widely adopted due to its low level of accuracy when compared to penalty-based approaches. In this paper, we compare the penalty-free approach of Oden et al. to the one proposed herein on one benchmark problem.
Another DG method without penalty parameters was presented by John et al.
[
39
]
, where the lifting operator on the mesh skeleton is introduced in the discrete model. The method exploits the properties of the piecewise Raviart–Thomas–Nédélec element in which the lifting operator increases the polynomial space by one.
Other penalty-free versions of the DG method are available
[
44
,
54
]
, but these are limited to Stokes flow. Continuity of the approximate solution is enforced using the method of Lagrange multipliers
[
30
,
13
,
42

Even though there are some commonalities, the approach pursued in the PF-DG method is distinct from the enhanced strain method (ESM)
[
49
,
64
]
. In both these methods, special approximation subspaces are selected—in PF-DG the trial space must be (nearly) continuous and in the ESM the stress and enhanced strain subspaces are mutually orthogonal.
The PF-DG method presented in this paper is applicable on high-order finite elements and can be applied to different classes of boundary-value problems.

.
∎

The admissible space is the null space of the operator
D
, which consists of continuous functions that vanish on the Dirichlet boundary. We consider an augmented admissible space where some small levels of discontinuities are allowed and
\overline{\ker}(D)
is defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\ker}(D)=\left\{\phi\in V:\lvert d(\phi,{v})\rvert<\epsilon\lVert\phi\rVert_{V}\ \ \forall{v}\in V\right\},

(18)

where
\epsilon>0
is a small number. It is evident that

\ker(D)\subset\overline{\ker}(D)\subset V
.

The bilinear operator
a(\cdot,\cdot)
consists of two components: the first on the domain and the second on the mesh skeleton and Dirichlet boundary, respectively:

\displaystyle{a}(u,{v})=a_{\varOmega}(u,{v})-a_{\varGamma}(u,{v}).

(19)

The operator for the Poisson problem with the two components is presented in (
8b
). The component
a_{\varGamma}
vanishes for
d(u,{v})=0
, which implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{{v}\in V}\frac{\lvert a_{\varGamma}(u,{v})\rvert}{\lVert{v}\rVert_{V}}\leq\gamma(\epsilon)\lVert u\rVert_{V}\quad\forall\,u\in\overline{\ker}(D)\,,\quad\gamma(\epsilon)\xrightarrow[\epsilon\rightarrow 0]{}0.

(20)

The number
\gamma>0
depends on
\epsilon
from (
18
) and
\gamma
goes to zero when
\epsilon
is zero. The operator
a
is coercive in the admissible space, but can also remain coercive for the augmented admissible space for some small values of
\epsilon
, which is shown in the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

When the operator
a(u,{v})
is coercive in
\ker(D)
with the constant
c_{1}
then the operator remains coercive in
\overline{\ker}(D)
for some small
\epsilon
.

Proof.

It can be observed that

\displaystyle\overline{\ker}(D)\xrightarrow[\epsilon\rightarrow 0]{}\ker(D)\,,\quad\overline{\ker}(D)\xrightarrow[\epsilon\rightarrow\infty]{}V

Consequently, the coercivity of the
a_{\varOmega}
operator changes with
\epsilon
in the following manner:

\displaystyle a_{\varOmega}(u,u)\geq c(\epsilon)\lVert u\rVert_{V}^{2}\,,\quad c(\epsilon)\xrightarrow[\epsilon\rightarrow 0]{}c_{1}\,,\quad c(\epsilon)\xrightarrow[\epsilon\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

The function
\c{(}\epsilon)
is monotonically decreasing while the function
\gamma(\epsilon)
is monotonically increasing as
\epsilon
increases. According to (
19
), for any
u\in\overline{\ker}(D)
we have

\displaystyle{a}(u,u)=a_{\varOmega}(u,u)-a_{\varGamma}(u,u)\geq a_{\varOmega}(u,u)-\lvert a_{\varGamma}(u,u)\rvert\geq c(\epsilon)\lVert u\rVert_{V}^{2}-\gamma(\epsilon)\lVert u\rVert_{V}^{2}=\bigl(c(\epsilon)-\gamma(\epsilon)\bigr)\lVert u\rVert_{V}^{2}.

The operator
a(u,u)
is coercive in
\overline{\ker}(D)
so long as
\epsilon
is small enough such that
c(\epsilon)>\gamma(\epsilon)
. ∎

In the next section, the algorithm to compute the discrete bilinear form in the augmented admissible space is presented. The coercivity of the discrete bilinear
form is numerically confirmed through the numerical example in Section (
6.1
) where we show that the stiffness matrix is well-conditioned.

2.2
Approximate solution in the PF-DG method

Now we apply the approach presented in the previous section to find an approximate solution of the boundary-value problem in (
14
). First, we need to rewrite constraint equations (
10
) in algebraic form. Then we present the construction of the finite-dimensional constraints and obtain the discrete system of equations that correspond to the weak form in (
14
).

The domain
\varOmega
is covered by a finite element mesh
\varOmega_{h}
, and the elements in the mesh
\varOmega^{e}\in\varOmega_{h}
can be arbitrary polygons in 2D or polyhedrons in 3D with affine edges and planar faces, respectively. The approximate solution is to be found in the approximation space
{V_{p}}\subset V
, which is constructed on a set of complete, polynomial basis functions up to order
p
in each element
{V_{p}^{e}}
, where
{V_{p}}=\bigcup\limits_{e}{V_{p}^{e}}
. The linear subspace
{V_{p}^{e}}
is constructed by the basis functions
\{{\Psi}^{e}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m_{p}}
, where
{\Psi}^{e}_{k}
are the basis functions in the
e
-th element and the support of the basis functions are limited to the
e
-th element. The numbers of basis functions depend on the approximation order
p
and the spatial dimension:
m_{p}=p+1
in 1D,
m_{p}=\frac{1}{2}(p+1)(p+2)
in 2D, and
m_{p}=\frac{1}{6}(p+1)(p+2)(p+3)
in 3D. The polynomial basis functions can be: monomials, Legendre, Chebyshev, Lagrange polynomials, or any other basis. The approximation of a function
u
in this space is given by

\displaystyle u\approx u^{h}={\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{q},

(21)

where

{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}
is a row vector that consists of all the basis functions in the mesh and
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}
is the vector of degrees of freedom for this approximation. In general, this approximation is not continuous on the mesh skeleton and does not satisfy the Dirichlet boundary conditions. The discrete equivalents of the sets
{V^{c}_{0}}
and
{V^{c}_{b}}
are defined as the following vector sets
{Z^{c}_{0p}}
and
{Z^{c}_{bp}}
, respectively:

\displaystyle{Z^{c}_{0p}}=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\,:[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\bm{v}=0\ \text{on}\ \varGamma_{s}\ \text{and}\ {\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{v}=0\ \text{on}\ \varGamma_{D}\right\},

(22a)

\displaystyle{Z^{c}_{bp}}=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\,:[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\bm{v}=0\ \text{on}\ \varGamma_{s}\ \text{and}\ {\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{v}=\mathcal{P}_{p}{u}_{b}\ \text{on}\ \varGamma_{D}\right\}{.}

(22b)

where the vector
\bm{v}
in (
22
) represents all vectors that meet the particular conditions. In addition,
\mathcal{P}_{p}{u}_{b}
is the projection of the function
{u}_{b}
to
{V_{p}}\Bigr|_{\varGamma_{D}}
, such that
\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{p}{u}_{b}-{u}_{b}\right)\,dS=\bm{0}
.

The vector set
{Z^{c}_{bp}}
, in combination with the basis functions, constitutes the finite admissible subset. In order to find the vector sets
{Z^{c}_{bp}}
and
{Z^{c}_{0p}}
, the discrete form of (
10
) has to be solved, which reads

\displaystyle\bm{D}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\bm{b},\qquad\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\,,\,\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\,,\,\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}},

(23a)

where

\displaystyle\bm{D}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{s}}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\,dS+\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\,dS,\quad\bm{b}=\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}{u}_{b}\,dS{.}

(23b)

The discrete version of Theorems
1
and
2
is presented in the two following Theorems.

Theorem 4
.

There is a vector
\bm{v}\in{Z^{c}_{bp}}

if and only if

\bm{D}\bm{v}=\bm{b}
.

Proof.

1.

\bm{v}\in{Z^{c}_{bp}}

\Longrightarrow

\bm{D}\bm{v}=\bm{b}
.
The vector is applied to (
23
) and we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{s}}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\,dS\bm{v}+\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\,dS\bm{v}=\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}{u}_{b}\,dS,

or

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{s}}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\bm{v}\,dS+\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}\left({\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{v}-{u}_{b}\right)\,dS=0,

and since
[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\bm{v}=0

and
{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{v}=\mathcal{P}_{p}{u}_{b}
and from (
22b
), the equality is met.

2.

\bm{D}\bm{v}=\bm{b}

\Longrightarrow

\bm{v}\in{Z^{c}_{bp}}
.

Since the vector
\bm{v}
satisfies (
23
), so after some simple operations we can write

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{s}}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\bm{v}\,dS+\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}\left({\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{v}-{u}_{b}\right)\,dS=0{.}

Both integrals are independent of each other since
\varGamma_{s}\cap\varGamma_{D}=\emptyset
, so we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{s}}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\bm{v}\,\,dS=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\bm{v}=0\ \ \text{on }\varGamma_{s},

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}\left({\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{v}-{u}_{b}\right)\,dS=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{v}=\mathcal{P}_{p}{u}_{b}\ \ \text{on }\varGamma_{D}{.}\qed

Theorem 5
.

There is a vector
\bm{v}\in{Z^{c}_{0p}}

if and only if

\bm{D}\bm{v}=\bm{0}
.

Now, the definitions of the sets
{Z^{c}_{0p}}
and
{Z^{c}_{bp}}
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle{Z^{c}_{0p}}=\left\{\bm{v}:\,\bm{v}\in\ker{(\bm{D})}\right\},

(24a)

\displaystyle{Z^{c}_{bp}}=\left\{\bm{v}:\,\lVert\bm{D}\bm{v}-\bm{b}\rVert=0\right\}{.}

(24b)

The system of equations in (
23
) is square but is singular. The rank of the left-hand side matrix is much smaller in comparison to its dimension. This implies that the system of equations has many linearly dependent equations. The solution of this system of equations is performed as described in Appendix
A
and expressed in the form shown in (
88
), which is

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\bm{W}\bm{z}+\bm{v}_{b}\ \ \forall\bm{z},\qquad\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}-r}\,,\,\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}-r}\,,\,\bm{v}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}},

(25)

where
r
is the rank of
\bm{D}
matrix,
\bm{W}
is the matrix that consists of basis vectors in
\ker{(\bm{D})}
and the vector
\bm{v}_{b}\in{Z^{c}_{bp}}
, which is stated via the following theorem:

Theorem 6
.

If there is a vector
\bm{v}\in{Z^{c}_{bp}}
and
\bm{v}=\bm{W}\bm{z}+\bm{v}_{b}

\forall\bm{z}
, then
\bm{w}=\bm{W}\bm{z}\in{Z^{c}_{0p}}
and
\bm{v}_{b}\in{Z^{c}_{bp}}
.

Proof.

When
\bm{v}\in{Z^{c}_{bp}}
, then
\bm{v}
satisfies (
23
) and we have

\displaystyle\bm{D}\bm{W}\bm{z}+\bm{D}\bm{v}_{b}=\bm{b}\ \ \forall\bm{z}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bm{D}\bm{W}\bm{z}=\bm{0}\ \ \forall\bm{z}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bm{w}\in{Z^{c}_{0p}},

\displaystyle\bm{D}\bm{v}_{b}=\bm{b}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bm{v}_{b}\in{Z^{c}_{bp}}{.}\qed

Equation (
23
) defines constraints for the vectors, and therefore to enforce continuity on the mesh skeleton and to satisfy the Dirichlet boundary conditions, we need to first solve this singular system of equations. In the calculations, these restrictions prove to be overly restrictive, especially for polygonal meshes, which as a consequence can lead to numerical instabilities that can lead to an inaccurate final solution. Hence, we
loosen
these restrictions to realize a numerically stable approach. To this end, instead of (
23
) we propose to use the following relation:

\displaystyle{\overline{\bm{D}}}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{b}},\qquad\overline{\bm{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\overline{p}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{\overline{p}}}\,,\,\overline{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\overline{p}}},

(26a)

where

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{D}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{s}}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\,dS+\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\,dS,\quad\overline{\bm{b}}=\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}^{\mathrm{T}}{u}_{b}\,dS,

(26b)

with
{\overline{p}}\leq p
. In the computations, we choose
{\overline{p}}=p-1
. The matrix
{\overline{\bm{D}}}
is rectangular with the same number of columns as
\bm{D}
matrix, but with fewer number of rows and so
\rank(\bm{D})>\rank(\overline{\bm{D}})\,\Rightarrow\,\ker(\bm{D})\subset\ker(\overline{\bm{D}})
. The set of all possible solutions of (
26
) is larger in comparison to the one in (
23
), allowing for some level of discontinuities or variation of the boundary conditions. The solution of the system in (
26
) has the following form:

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\overline{\bm{W}}\bm{z}+\bm{q}_{b}\ \ \forall\bm{z}\,,\qquad\overline{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{\overline{p}}-\overline{r}}\,,\,\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\overline{p}}-\overline{r}}\,,\,\bm{q}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}},

(27)

where
\overline{r}=\rank(\overline{\bm{D}})
.

Consequently, the sets of vectors in (
24
) now become

\displaystyle{\overline{Z}}^{c}_{0p}=\left\{\bm{v}:\,\bm{v}\in\ker{(\overline{\bm{D}})}\right\},

(28a)

\displaystyle{\overline{Z}}^{c}_{bp}=\left\{\bm{v}:\,\lVert\overline{\bm{D}}\bm{v}-\overline{\bm{b}}\rVert=0\right\},

(28b)

where
{\overline{Z}}^{c}_{bp}
together with the basis functions define the augmented admissible subset.

When we have the solution as expressed in(
27
), then the discrete version of the weak problem formulation in (
14
) leads to the following problem:
For the given
\overline{\bm{W}}
and
\bm{q}_{b}
, find
\bm{q}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{W}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{K}\overline{\bm{W}}\bm{q}_{0}=\overline{\bm{W}}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{f}-\bm{K}\bm{q}_{b}\right)\,,\qquad\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\,,\,\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}

(29)

where
K_{ij}=a({\Psi}_{j},{\Psi}_{i})
and
f_{i}=l({\Psi}_{i})
and the final solution vector is

\displaystyle\bm{q}=\overline{\bm{W}}\bm{q}_{0}+\bm{q}_{b}{.}

(30)

The matrix
\overline{\bm{W}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{K}\overline{\bm{W}}
is nonsymmetric and well-conditioned and can be solved by many solvers for finding the
\bm{q}_{0}
vector and later on to obtain the global vector
\bm{q}
. However, the matrix
\overline{\bm{W}}
and the vector
\bm{q}_{b}
have to be constructed a priori using the procedure presented in the following section. The well-posedness of the problem (
29
) is shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 7
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3
and Conjecture
1
for any closed subspace
{V_{p}}\subset V
, there exist a unique solution
u^{h}={\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\bm{q}\in{V_{p}}
of the problem (
29
).

Proof.

Consider the basis
{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}
in
{V_{p}}
, i.e.
{V_{p}}=\Span\{{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\}
. Because
\ker(\overline{\bm{D}})=\Span\{\overline{\bm{W}}\}
, so the function set
{\bm{\varPhi}}_{p}={\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\overline{\bm{W}}
is the basis functions of the space
\overline{\ker}(D)\Bigr|_{{V_{p}}}=\Span\{{\bm{\varPhi}}_{p}\}
. The following matrix is constructed

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{K}}=a({\bm{\varPhi}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}},{\bm{\varPhi}}_{p})=\overline{\bm{W}}^{\mathrm{T}}a({\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}^{\mathrm{T}},{\bm{{\Psi}}})\overline{\bm{W}}=\overline{\bm{W}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{K}\overline{\bm{W}}{.}

(31)

From the coercivity condition in Conjecture
1
, we obtain that the matrix
\overline{\bm{K}}
is positive-definite, and hence it is invertible.
∎

3
Construction of the constraint equations

In the PF-DG method presented herein, the constraints (
26
) that enforce the continuity and boundary conditions are the critical component of the approach. The solution of (
27
) is nonunique due to the vector of free variables
\bm{z}
(which is part of the vector
\bm{v}
when using the approach described in Appendix
A.1
) whose choice is in general arbitrary, and as a consequence, construction of
\overline{\bm{W}}
and
\bm{q}_{b}
depend on the choice of the free variables. In the case when we deal with a relatively small problem with few low-order elements, the final result is independent of the choice of
\bm{z}
. However, for larger problems, choice of
\bm{z}
becomes important, since otherwise truncation errors can accumulate in
\overline{\bm{W}}
and
\bm{q}_{b}
which can influence the correctness of the solution. Hence, special attention needs to be paid to construct this particular matrix and vector.

The construction of the solution as shown in (
27
) can be done using two approaches. In the first approach, the system in (
26
) is solved globally, as shown in Appendix
A
. In the second approach, the solution is obtained by sequential procedure along the mesh skeleton and outer boundary segments, which are presented in this section. The solution in (
29
) guarantees that the approximate solution is both continuous and meets the boundary conditions. In order to construct the constraint components, i.e.,
\overline{\bm{W}}
and
\bm{q}_{b}
, the procedure that involves integration along the mesh skeleton and Dirichlet boundary is utilized. It is performed sequentially for each segment of the mesh using the procedure described in Section
A.2
. We first present the part of the algorithm to satisfy continuity on the mesh skeleton and then for the boundary conditions.

This procedure is performed for each skeleton segment to obtain the global final solution. Each skeleton segment has exactly two adjacent elements. We want the jump in the approximation on each skeleton segment to be zero. Consider the
k
-th skeleton segment with
i
and
j
adjustment elements, where the
i
-th element is on the ’+’ side and
j
-th element is on the ’–’ side of the segment. The approximation of the discontinuity jump on the
k
-th segment is:

\displaystyle[\negthinspace[u_{h}]\negthinspace]=[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\bm{q}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{{\Psi}}}^{i}_{p}&-{\bm{{\Psi}}}^{j}_{p}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{q}^{i}\\
\bm{q}^{j}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{\Phi}^{k}_{p}\bm{a}^{k}\quad\text{on }\varGamma_{s}^{k},

(32)

where
{\bm{{\Psi}}}^{i}_{p}
is the vector of basis function for the
i
-th element,
\bm{q}^{i}
is the dof vector for the
i
-th element,
\bm{\Phi}^{k}
and
\bm{a}^{k}
are the relevant quantities for the
k
-th segment. The vector
\bm{a}^{k}
has to satisfy the following equation:

\displaystyle\bm{M}^{k}\bm{a}^{k}=\bm{0},\quad\bm{M}^{k}=\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}^{k}}{\bm{\Phi}^{k}_{{\overline{p}}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\Phi}^{k}_{p}\,dS.

(33)

The matrix
\bm{M}^{k}
is rectangular and additionally is singular. The dependent rows are removed from the matrix using the procedure described in Appendix
A
, which yields the
\overline{\bm{M}}^{k}
matrix. The current version of the matrix
\overline{\bm{W}}
and the current version of the vector of free variables
\bm{z}
, obtained from the previous skeleton segment, can be used for expressing the vector
\bm{q}
. The vector
\bm{a}^{k}
is the part of the vector
\bm{q}
, as shown in (
32
) and so the vector
\bm{a}^{k}
can be expressed with the same vector
\bm{z}
as the
\bm{q}
:

\displaystyle\bm{q}=\overline{\bm{W}}\bm{z}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\bm{a}^{k}=\overline{\bm{W}}^{k}\bm{z}{.}

(34)

Applying the relation in (
34
) to the (
33
), we have

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{M}}^{k}\overline{\bm{W}}^{k}\bm{z}=\bm{0}{.}

(35)

The solution of (
35
) results in the current version of the
\bm{z}
vector, which can be written in the recurrence form

\displaystyle\bm{z}=\overline{\bm{W}}^{k}\bm{z}{.}

(36)

In order to obtain this solution, the pivoting variables in the Gauss-Jordan procedure have to be chosen in the solution procedure. For the best choice for the pivots,
\overline{\bm{W}}
is analyzed, in such a way that the number of nonzero components in each column of this matrix is counted. Then the pivoting variables are chosen with the minimum nonzero components in the column of the
\overline{\bm{W}}
and which also has the leading component in (
35
). When the solution (
36
) is obtained the matrix
\overline{\bm{W}}
is updated:

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{W}}=\overline{\bm{W}}\overline{\bm{W}}^{k}{.}

(37)

The procedure is repeated for all the skeleton segments, and finally, we obtain the
\overline{\bm{W}}
that defines the
{Z^{c}_{0p}}
set. The procedure for the boundary conditions is quite similar, remembering that there is only a single element connected to a single outer boundary segment. Finally, at the end of the procedure the matrix
\overline{\bm{W}}
and vector
\bm{q}_{b}
are calculated that are applied in the approach presented in (
29
).

4
PF-DG for linear elasticity

The analysis of the mechanical problem starts with the standard equilibrium equations (momentum balance) in the domain
\varOmega
and the appropriate boundary conditions on the outer boundary
\varGamma
:

\displaystyle\diverg\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}\quad\text{in }\varOmega,

(38a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{t}}_{b}\quad\text{on }\varGamma_{N},

(38b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{u}}_{b}\quad\text{on }\varGamma_{D},

(38c)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the Cauchy stress tensor,
\mathbf{b}
is the body force vector per unit volume,
{\mathbf{t}}_{b}
is the prescribed traction vector,
\mathbf{u}
is the displacement field and
{\mathbf{u}}_{b}
is the prescribed displacement boundary data on
\varGamma_{D}
, and
\varGamma_{N}
and
\varGamma_{D}
are the parts of the outer boundary on which the Neumman and essential boundary conditions are prescribed, respectively.

Equation (
38
) is supplemented with the generalized Hooke’s law:

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}=E_{ijkl}\,\varepsilon_{kl},

(39)

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is the small-strain tensor and
\mathbf{E}
is the fourth-order material moduli tensor. We adopt small-strain kinematics:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}={\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{s}\mathbf{u}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{u}+({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{u})^{\mathrm{T}}\Bigr]\quad\text{in }\varOmega{.}

(40)

In the PF-DG method, the displacement field is approximated using the same formula as in (
21
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\approx{\bm{{\Psi}}}\mathbf{\check{u}},

(41)

where now
{\bm{{\Psi}}}
consists of three rows in 3D and two rows in the 2D case.

To find the approximate solution of the elasticity problem the system of equations in the form presented in (
29
) have to be solved, where in this case the definitions of the stiffness matrix and the loading vector are as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{K}=\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{sh}{\bm{{\Psi}}}^{\mathrm{T}}:\mathbf{E}:{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{sh}{\bm{{\Psi}}}\,d\bm{x}+\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{s}}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{n}:{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{sh}{\bm{{\Psi}}}\,dS-\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{D}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{n}:{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{sh}{\bm{{\Psi}}}\,dS,

(42)

\displaystyle\bm{F}=\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\bm{{\Psi}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{b}\,d\bm{x}+\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{N}}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\mathbf{t}}_{b}\,dS,

(43)

where
\mathbf{E}_{n}=\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{n}
. The stiffness matrix
\bm{K}
is nonsymmetric due to the last two components on the mesh skeleton and Dirichlet boundary that appear in (
42
).

5
PF-DG for biharmonic problem

In this section, the DG method is developed for the fourth-order biharmonic equation. The strong form of the model problem is:

\displaystyle\Delta(m_{1}\Delta u)+{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}^{\mathrm{T}}(m_{2}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u)+m_{3}u

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\varOmega,

(44a)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle={u}_{b}\quad\text{on }\varGamma,

(44b)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle={q}_{b}\quad\text{on }\varGamma,

(44c)

where
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
,
m_{3}
, are parameter functions. The boundary conditions in (
44b
) are of Dirichlet type. Other possible type of boundary conditions are connected with the second and third derivatives on the boundary, which are of Neumann type. Dirichlet type boundary conditions are particularly challenging in DG methods.

In the weak form, the Laplace operator
\Delta
and the nabla operator
{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}
are viewed to be element-wise and for the sake of clarity the subscript ’h’ is omitted. The construction of the weak form starts by multiplying (
44a
) with a test function and integrating over the domain:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}\,\Delta(m_{1}\Delta u)\,d\bm{x}+\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}\,{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}^{\mathrm{T}}(m_{2}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u)\,d\bm{x}+\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}\ m_{3}u\,d\bm{x}-\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}f=0{.}

(45)

The first integral in (
45
) is integrated by parts to yield

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}\,\Delta(m_{1}\Delta u)\,d\bm{x}&=\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\left({v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\right)\,d\bm{x}-\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\,d\bm{x}\\
&=\int\limits_{\varGamma}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\,d\bm{x}{.}\end{aligned}

(46)

The standard decomposition for the jump of two function is applied:

\displaystyle[\negthinspace[{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}=[\negthinspace[{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}+\left\{{v}\right\}\cancelto{0}{[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}}\qquad\text{on }\,\varGamma_{s}{.}

(47)

It is assumed that the jump of the gradient of Laplacian is zero. Furthermore, the last integral in (
46
) is integrated by parts to yield

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\,d\bm{x}&=-\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\left({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}\,m_{1}\Delta u\right)\,d\bm{x}+\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{1}\,\Delta{v}\,\Delta u\,d\bm{x}\\
&=-\int\limits_{\varGamma}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}\,m_{1}\Delta u\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}\,m_{1}\Delta u]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS+\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{1}\,\Delta{v}\,\Delta u\,d\bm{x}{.}\end{aligned}

(48)

After the standard decomposition, it is assumed (as in the finite element method) that the jump of the Laplacian on the mesh skeleton is set to zero:

\displaystyle[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}\,m_{1}\Delta u]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}=[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{m_{1}\Delta u\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}+\left\{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}\right\}\cancelto{0}{[\negthinspace[m_{1}\Delta u]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}}\qquad\text{on }\,\varGamma_{s}{.}

(49)

Finally, (
46
) is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}\,\Delta(m_{1}\Delta u)\,d\bm{x}&=\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{1}\,\Delta{v}\,\Delta u\,d\bm{x}\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varGamma}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}\,m_{1}\Delta u\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\\
&-\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}+\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{m_{1}\Delta u\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS{.}\end{aligned}

(50)

The second integral in (
45
) is

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}\,{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}^{\mathrm{T}}(m_{2}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u)\,d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\left(m_{2}\,{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\right)\,d\bm{x}-\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{2}\,{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\,d\bm{x}

(51)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\varGamma}m_{2}\,{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}m_{2}\,[\negthinspace[{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{2}\,{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\,d\bm{x}{.}

(52)

It is assumed, similar to arriving at (
7
), that the normal derivative on the mesh skeleton is zero:

\displaystyle[\negthinspace[{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}=[\negthinspace[{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}+\left\{{v}\right\}\cancelto{0}{[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u]\negthinspace]\cdot\mathbf{n}}\qquad\text{on }\,\varGamma_{s}{.}

(53)

The weak form in (
45
) can now be written as:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{1}\,\Delta{v}\,\Delta u\,d\bm{x}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{2}\,{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\,d\bm{x}+\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}\ m_{3}u\,d\bm{x}-\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}f\\
&-\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{m_{1}\Delta u\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}m_{2}\,[\negthinspace[{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\\
&+\int\limits_{\varGamma}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varGamma}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}\,m_{1}\Delta u\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma}m_{2}\,{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}f\,d\bm{x}=0{.}\end{aligned}

(54)

In this case, the definitions of the bilinear and linear forms from (
7
) are:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} a({v},u)&=\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{1}\,\Delta{v}\,\Delta u\,d\bm{x}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varOmega}m_{2}\,{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\,d\bm{x}+\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}\ m_{3}u\,d\bm{x}-\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}f\\
&-\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{m_{1}\Delta u\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}m_{2}\,[\negthinspace[{v}]\negthinspace]\left\{{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\right\}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\\
&+\int\limits_{\varGamma}{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS-\int\limits_{\varGamma}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{v}\,m_{1}\Delta u\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma}m_{2}\,{v}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\end{aligned}

(55a)

\displaystyle l({v})=\int\limits_{\varOmega}{v}f\,d\bm{x}{.}

(55b)

The definitions of matrix
\overline{\bm{D}}
and the vector
\overline{\bm{b}}
from the constraints (
26
) are now as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \overline{\bm{D}}&=\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}[\negthinspace[{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\,dS\\
&+\int\limits_{\varGamma_{s}}h^{2}[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}]\negthinspace]^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\,[\negthinspace[{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}]\negthinspace]\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma}h^{2}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{p}\,dS,\end{aligned}

(56a)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{b}}=\int\limits_{\varGamma}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}^{\mathrm{T}}{u}_{b}\,dS+\int\limits_{\varGamma}h^{2}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{\bm{{\Psi}}}_{\overline{p}}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,{q}_{b}\,dS{.}

(56b)

In the definition of the bilinear form in (
55a
), the third-order component
{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)
on the mesh skeleton and the outer boundary have to be evaluated. In this application of the DG method, Chebyshev basis functions are used, for which the second- or third-order derivatives are readily computed. The component expands to

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(m_{1}\Delta u)=\begin{bmatrix}{m_{1}}_{,x}\cdot\left(u_{,xx}+u_{,yy}\right)+m_{1}\cdot\left(u_{,xxx}+u_{,xyy}\right)\\
{m_{1}}_{,y}\cdot\left(u_{,xx}+u_{,yy}\right)+m_{1}\cdot\left(u_{,xxy}+u_{,yyy}\right)\end{bmatrix}{.}

(57)

6
Numerical examples

In this section, seven numerical examples affirming the accuracy and robustness of the PF-DG method are presented. Six problems are chosen in 2D and one example in 3D is solved over the biunit cube. Three Poisson problems are first solved. In the fourth example, a 2D elasticity problem on the L-shaped domain is considered. In the fifth example, the hyperelastic Cook’s membrane problem is analyzed. The sixth example is a fourth-order biharmonic problem on the square.

In the 2D problems, polygonal meshes are used for the analyses, and in the first example, additionally, triangular and quadrilateral meshes are used. For the 3D problem, regular hexahedral meshes are used. Numerical integration has to be performed in the numerical computations. In the case when standard finite elements are used, i.e., triangular, quadrilateral, or hexagonal shapes, the symmetric cubatures presented in the paper by Witherden and Vincent
[
63
]
are applied. The integration over polygonal elements is performed using the scaled boundary cubature scheme proposed by Chin and Sukumar.
[
17
]

In these examples, the results are mainly shown in the form of convergence plots using the relative error measures over the whole domain, along the mesh skeleton and the Dirichlet boundary:

\displaystyle\eta=\frac{\lVert u^{h}-u\rVert_{L^{2}(\varOmega)}}{\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\varOmega)}},\quad\eta_{E}=\frac{\lVert u^{h}-u\rVert_{E(\varOmega)}}{\lVert u\rVert_{E(\varOmega)}},\quad\eta_{s}=\frac{\lVert[\negthinspace[u^{h}]\negthinspace]\rVert_{L^{2}(\varGamma_{s})}}{\lvert\varGamma_{s}\rvert},\quad\eta_{D}=\frac{\lVert u^{h}-{u}_{b}\rVert_{L^{2}(\varGamma_{D})}}{\lvert\varGamma_{D}\rvert},

(58)

where
\lVert u\rVert_{E(\varOmega)}=a(u,u)
is the energy norm. The measures show the global error, the discontinuity level on the mesh skeleton of the approximate solution, and how well the Dirichlet boundary conditions are met, respectively.

The theoretical rates of the convergence for the approximate solution for the
L^{2}
norm and energy seminorm are:

\displaystyle\lVert u^{h}-u\rVert_{L^{2}(\varOmega)}\leq C(\varOmega,\mu)h^{p+1}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{p+1}}\,,\quad\lVert u^{h}-u\rVert_{E(\varOmega)}\leq C(\varOmega,\mu)h^{p}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{p+1}}

(59)

where the constant
C=C(\varOmega,\mu)
depends on the domain shape of the problem and the mesh regularity parameter
\mu\in(0,1]
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\forall\varOmega^{e}\in\varOmega_{h}\quad\mu h_{e}\leq 2\rho_{e}\leq h_{e}

where
h_{e}
is the diameter of the finite element and
\rho_{e}
is the radius of the largest sphere contained in
\varOmega^{e}
.

6.4
Example 4: Linear elasticity problem on L-shaped domain

In this example, a benchmark elasticity problem on an L-shaped domain is considered. Plane strain conditions are assumed. The governing equations are stated in (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u_{r}(r,\theta)&=\frac{1}{2\mu}r^{\beta}\Bigl[-(\beta+1)\cos\left((\beta+1)\theta\right)+(C_{2}-\beta-1)C_{1}\cos\left((\beta-1)\theta\right)\Bigr],\\
u_{\theta}(r,\theta)&=\frac{1}{2\mu}r^{\beta}\Bigl[(\beta+1)\sin\left((\beta+1)\theta\right)+(C_{2}+\beta-1)C_{1}\sin\left((\beta-1)\theta\right)\Bigr]{.}\end{aligned}

(63)

C_{1}=-\cos\left((\beta+1)\omega\right)/\cos\left((\beta-1)\omega\right)
,

C_{2}=2\left(\lambda+2\mu\right)/\left(\lambda+\mu\right)
,

\omega=3\pi/4
and the critical exponent
\beta
is the positive solution of the equation

\beta\sin(2\omega)+\sin(2\omega\beta)=0

\displaystyle\psi=\frac{\mu}{2}\left(\Tr\bm{C}-3\right)+\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(J^{2}-1\right)-\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}+\mu\right)\ln J,

(67)

where
\lambda

is the determinant of the deformation gradient
\bm{F}={\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{u}+\bm{I}
, and
\bm{C}=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\bm{F}}
is the right Cauchy-Green tensor. The second Piola Kirchhoff stress tensor and the material tangent tensor are

\displaystyle\bm{S}=2\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{C}}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(J^{2}-1\right)\bm{C}^{-1}+\mu\left(\bm{I}-\bm{C}^{-1}\right),

(68)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}=2\frac{\partial\bm{S}}{\partial\bm{C}},

(69)

where symbolic computations are used to compute the tensor
\mathbb{C}
.

The momentum balance, in the absence of the body forces, in the initial configuration can be expressed in terms of the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor as:

\displaystyle\diverg\bm{P}=\bm{0}\quad\text{in }\varOmega,

(70a)

\displaystyle\bm{P}\cdot\mathbf{N}={\mathbf{t}}_{b}\quad\text{on }\varGamma_{N},

(70b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\bm{0}\quad\text{on }\varGamma_{D},

(70c)

where
\mathbf{N}
denotes the unit outward normal.

The weak form of (
70
) is: Find
\mathbf{u}\in{V^{c}_{0}}
such that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\varOmega}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}:\bm{P}\,d\bm{x}-\int\limits_{{\varGamma_{N}}}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}{\mathbf{t}}_{b}\,dS=0\ \ \forall\mathbf{v}\in{V^{c}_{0}}{.}

(71)

The Green-Lagrange strain tensor and its variation are

\displaystyle\bm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{F}-\bm{I}\right),

(72)

\displaystyle\delta\bm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{F}+\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\delta\bm{F}\right),\quad\delta\bm{F}=\frac{\partial\delta\mathbf{u}}{\partial\bm{X}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial\bm{X}}={\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}

(73)

Using the relation between the first and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensors,
\bm{P}=\bm{F}\bm{S}
, the inner product (
71

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}:\bm{P}={\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}:\left(\bm{F}\cdot\bm{S}\right)=\left({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\bm{F}\right):\bm{S}

(74)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\bm{F}+\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}\right):\bm{S}+\cancelto{0}{\frac{1}{2}\left({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\bm{F}-\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\mathbf{v}\right):\bm{S}}=\delta\bm{E}(\mathbf{v}):\bm{S}{.}

(75)

The tensor
\bm{S}

\displaystyle\bm{S}=\bm{S}+\Delta\bm{S}{.}

(76)

The tensor increment

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{S}\approx\mathbb{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\Delta\mathbf{u}),\quad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\Delta\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\left({\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\Delta\mathbf{u}+{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\Delta\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{T}}\right){.}

In this procedure, the external force multiplier

\bm{P}
at every iterative step. The tangent tensor
\mathbb{C}

7
Conclusions

In this paper, a new DG method is presented, in which no penalty or stability parameter is used, and hence the method is referred to as the penalty-free DG method. Generally, in DG methods, the global approximations are not continuous a priori on the mesh skeleton and continuity has to be enforced by penalty-like parameters. A similar scenario arises when imposing Dirichlet boundary conditions. An alternative approach is proposed in this paper where the consistency of the approximation field, i.e., continuity on the mesh skeleton and satisfaction of the Dirichlet boundary conditions, is obtained by adding least-squares constraint relations. In the discrete version, the constraints require solving a singular system of equations. Such an approach allows for the construction of the discrete, continuous approximation fields over the whole domain, independent of the basis functions within the elements, and at the same time, the Dirichlet boundary conditions are satisfied. In the implementation, the consistent approximation field is augmented (especially needed on polygonal meshes), which allows for small levels of discontinuities on the mesh skeleton.

The augmented consistent approximation field consists of basis functions in all finite elements, and the constraints are applied to the degrees of freedom, which guarantee the consistency of the approximation field. The constraints are expressed via a rectangular system of equations that is singular. In this paper, the method for solving such a singular system of equations is proposed, which can be solved globally, or the solution is constructed using a special assembly-free approach. The constraint relations are similar to the ones used in the DG method with Lagrange multipliers. However, in the case of the PF-DG method, there is no need to construct an additional space for the Lagrange multipliers.

The PF-DG method is verified on several benchmark problems where various types of boundary-value problems are analyzed in 2D and for one problem in 3D. In each example, the convergence plots are presented for the approximation orders up to
p=10
, and optimal convergence rates in the
L^{2}
norm and the energy seminorm are obtained. In finite-dimensional space, small jumps (discontinuities) are observed in the approximate solution, but it has been shown via the examples that the jumps converge to zero with similar rates as the global error. The method can be especially effective in the nonlinear regime since the constraint problem is solved only once before the incremental iterative procedure. It is shown that the method works well for standard elliptic problems, such as the Poisson equation or linear and nonlinear elasticity. It was also tested for the biharmonic problem, where the approximation in a standard Galerkin method must be
C^{1}
continuous. As part of future research, application of the PF-DG method to other classes of boundary-value problems will be pursued.

Appendix A
Solver for the PF-DG method

The following algebraic system of equations is considered:

\displaystyle\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b},

(81)

where
\bm{A}
is an
n\times m
matrix,
\bm{b}
is a
n
-component column vector and
\bm{x}
is an
m
-component column vector. The vector
\bm{x}
belongs to the set that consists of all possible solutions:

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in S=\left\{\bm{y}:\lVert\bm{A}\bm{y}-\bm{b}\rVert=0\right\}{.}

(82)

It is assumed that the system of equations (
81
) is consistent, which means that
S\neq\emptyset
. Since we have a linear, consistent system of equations, there exists one or infinite number of solutions. The matrix
\bm{A}
can be a square or rectangular matrix, i.e.,
n=m
or
n<m
. The matrix
\bm{A}
can be singular in the sense that the rows in the matrix are linearly dependent. The solution of the system of (
81
) can be unique, i.e., there exists only one
\bm{x}
, otherwise the set
S
consists of an infinite number of certain vectors.

In this paper, such a general linear system of equations as defined above is solved using the Gauss-Jordan approach as presented in Valco and Vajda.
[
62
]
Here the approach is presented in a more general way so that it can be applied to an arbitrarily large system of equations. For the sake of clarity, the nonsingular matrices are firstly considered, which is extended to singular matrices in
A.3
.

The procedure for solving the square (consistent) singular system of equations is presented in Farhat and Roux,
[
31
]
where the solver is applied to the floating subdomains as a result of domain partitioning in parallel calculations. This approach is referred to as the null-space method,
[
9
,
51
]
which is used to solve the saddle-point problem.
[
58
]
. This method has successfully been applied to solve problems in incompressible linear and nonlinear elasticity.
[
46
]
In this approach, the singular stiffness matrix is partitioned concerning the principal and redundant quantities, which is performed during the factorization process. Then the null space of the stiffness matrix can be identified, which contains the rigid-body modes of the subdomain. Then the final solution is presented as the linear combination of the null space basis vectors plus the vector constructed as the right-hand side vector multiplied by the pseudo-inverse stiffness matrix. However, the procedure presented here is more general, since it can also handle rectangular and singular matrices.

The rectangular system of equations can be solved using the
LQ
factorization, which is presented in Slobodkins and Tausch.
[
60
]
In this method, the matrix is decomposed as
\bm{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{L}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{Q}^{1}&\bm{Q}^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}
, where the matrix
\bm{L}
is lower triangular and matrix
\bm{Q}
consists of orthogonal vectors where the vectors in
\bm{Q}^{2}
form the basis of the null space of matrix
\bm{A}
. Another approach that can be applied to solve the linear equation with a rectangular matrix is the singular value decomposition (SVD),
[
2
]
and after some modifications, the SVD can also handle the solution of the singular system of equations.

A.1
Global approach

The variables in
\bm{x}
are divided into basic variables vector
\bm{x}_{b}
and free variables vector
\bm{x}_{f}
. For the sake of clarity, it can be assumed without loss of generality, that basic and free variables are contiguous in the
\bm{x}
vector. The matrix
\bm{A}
is decomposed on:

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{b}\\
\bm{x}_{f}\end{bmatrix}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{b}&\bm{A}_{f}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{b}\\
\bm{x}_{f}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{b}{.}

(83)

The division into the basic and free parts is done in such a way that the matrix
\bm{A}_{b}
is square and well-conditioned. On multiplication both sides of (
83
) by
\bm{A}_{b}^{-1}
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\tilde{\bm{A}}_{f}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{b}\\
\bm{x}_{f}\end{bmatrix}=\tilde{\bm{b}},

(84)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{A}}_{f}=\bm{A}_{b}^{-1}\bm{A}_{f}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\bm{b}}=\bm{A}_{b}^{-1}\bm{b}{.}

(85)

The system of equations shown in (
84
), is derived by multiplication both sides of (
83
) by
\bm{A}_{b}^{-1}
. However, the same results can be obtained using the Gauss-Jordan (GJ) elimination procedure. The GJ approach can be applied when the leading components of the matrix
\bm{A}_{b}
are located on the diagonal. In other cases the equations in (
83
) can be rearranged to place the leading components on the diagonal or the leading component can be searched in every row of the matrix
\bm{A}_{b}
. In this approach, the matrix
\bm{A}_{b}
is substituted by the matrix
\bar{\bm{I}}
which is the zero-one matrix, but the ones are not located on the diagonal. In this situation, it can be observed that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{I}}&\bar{\bm{A}}_{f}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{b}\\
\bm{x}_{f}\end{bmatrix}=\bar{\bm{b}}\,\Bigr|\,\bar{\bm{I}}^{\mathrm{T}}\quad\rightarrow\quad\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\tilde{\bm{A}}_{f}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{b}\\
\bm{x}_{f}\end{bmatrix}=\tilde{\bm{b}}{.}

(86)

Using (
84
) the basic variables can be expressed by the free variables:

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{b}=-\tilde{\bm{A}_{f}}\bm{x}_{f}+\tilde{\bm{b}}{.}

(87)

Using the relation in (
87
) the vector
\bm{x}
can be expressed by the free variable vector
\bm{z}
:

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\bm{W}\bm{z}+\bm{s},

(88)

where

\displaystyle\bm{W}=\begin{bmatrix}-\tilde{\bm{A}_{f}}\\
\bm{I}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{b}}\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{z}=\bm{x}_{f}{.}

(89)

Theorem 8
.

is defined as

, then

\displaystyle\bm{y}\in S\ \ \forall\bm{z}{.}

When
\bm{y}\in S
that means that the vector satisfies the equation (

). Let us suppose that there exists a vector
\bm{z}
for which
\bm{y}\notin S

\bm{r}

\displaystyle\bm{r}=\bm{A}\bm{y}-\bm{b}=\bm{A}\bm{W}\bm{z}+\bm{A}\bm{s}-\bm{b}{.}

(90)

Using the definitions of the vector
\bm{y}
and in the (
85
) and (
89
) and applying the matrix division as in (
83
), we have

\displaystyle\bm{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{b}&\bm{A}_{f}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\bm{A}_{b}^{-1}\bm{A}_{f}\\
\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{b}&\bm{A}_{f}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{b}^{-1}\bm{b}\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}-\bm{b},

(91)

\displaystyle\bm{r}=(-\bm{A}_{f}+\bm{A}_{f})\bm{z}+\bm{b}-\bm{b}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\bm{r}=\bm{0}{.}

(92)

It was shown that for every vector
\bm{z}
the residual vector is zero.

∎

The solution in (
88
) represents the hypersurface in which
\bm{s}
represents the point on that surface and the vectors of the matrix
\bm{W}
are the basis vectors for that surface. But in the case when
n=m
, the number of columns of the matrix
\bm{W}
is reduced to zero and the vector
\bm{s}
is the unique solution of the problem in (
81
). When
n<m
the vector
\bm{x}
represents the points belonging to the hypersurface defined by the pair
(\bm{W},\bm{s})
. In this hypersurface, a vector
\bm{x}_{m}
can be distinguished that is the shortest among all vectors in
S

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{x}_{m}\rVert=\min_{\bm{x}\in S}\lVert\bm{x}\rVert{.}

(93)

In order to identify the
\bm{x}_{m}
vector the relevant
\bm{z}_{m}
has to be found by solving the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{m}\,:f(\bm{z}_{m})=\min_{\bm{z}}f(\bm{z}),

(94)

where

A.2
Recursive approach

The approach presented in the previous section can be applied to obtain the recursive solution of the set of equations (
81
). For the recursive approach, this set of equations is divided into
s
subproblems. For the sake of clarity, firstly let
s=2
:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}\\
\bm{A}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\bm{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{b}_{1}\\
\bm{b}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(98)

where
\bm{A}_{i}
and
\bm{b}_{i}
are the appropriate parts of the matrix
\bm{A}
and
\bm{b}
, respectively. Now, the problem defined in (
81
) is reformulated: find the vector
\bm{x}
that satisfy the following equations:

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{1}\bm{x}=\bm{b}_{1},

(99a)

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{2}\bm{x}=\bm{b}_{2}{.}

(99b)

The solution of (
99a
) leads to the following

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\bm{W}_{1}\bm{z}_{1}+\bm{s}_{1}{.}

(100)

When applying this solution to (

\displaystyle\bm{A}\bm{W}_{1}\bm{z}_{1}=\bm{b}_{2}-\bm{A}_{2}\bm{s}_{2}{.}

The solution of (

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{1}=\bm{W}_{2}\bm{z}_{2}+\bm{s}_{2}{.}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\bm{s}=\bm{W}\bm{s}_{i}+\bm{s},\\

and

.

The recursive procedure presented in this section has the same complexity as the global approach in (
A.1
), i.e., the number of multiplications in these two approaches is the same. However, the recursive approach can be applied when the system of equations is constructed by segments. Then the solution can be applied to each of the segments sequentially without the need to construct the full set of equations.

A.3
Singular system of equations

When the matrix
\bm{A}
in the problem (
81
), then the dependent equations should be removed from the system of equations, and then the solution shown in (
88
) can be achieved. The linearly dependent equations can be identified when applying the Gauss-Jordan elimination procedure to calculate the quantities in (
87
). The Gauss-Jordan procedure is performed using the leading position in each row to locate the pivot variable. When reaching a linear dependent equation all the elements in that record become zero, so this equation is deleted from the set of equations. There may be multiple such equations, and each time these equations are eliminated.
