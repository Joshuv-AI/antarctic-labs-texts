Webster Sequences, Apportionment Problems, and Just-In-Time Sequencing
XIAOMIN LI

Abstract
Given a real number α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) , we define the Webster sequence of density α \alpha to be W α = ( ⌈ ( n − 1 / 2 ) / α ⌉ ) n ∈ ℕ W_{\alpha}=(\lceil(n-1/2)/\alpha\rceil)_{n\in\mathbb{N}} , where ⌈ x ⌉ {\lceil x\rceil} is the ceiling function. It is known that if α \alpha and β \beta are irrational with α + β = 1 \alpha+\beta=1 , then W α W_{\alpha} and W β W_{\beta} partition ℕ \mathbb{N} . On the other hand, an analogous result for three-part partitions does not hold: There does not exist a partition of ℕ \mathbb{N} into sequences W α , W β , W γ W_{\alpha},W_{\beta},W_{\gamma} with α , β , γ \alpha,\beta,\gamma irrational. In this paper, we consider the question of how close one can come to a three-part partition of ℕ \mathbb{N} into Webster sequences with prescribed densities α , β , γ \alpha,\beta,\gamma . We show that if α , β , γ \alpha,\beta,\gamma are irrational with α + β + γ = 1 \alpha+\beta+\gamma=1 , there exists a partition of ℕ \mathbb{N} into sequences of densities α , β , γ \alpha,\beta,\gamma , in which one of the sequences is a Webster sequence and the other two are “almost” Webster sequences that are obtained from Webster sequences by perturbing some elements by at most 1. We also provide two efficient algorithms to construct such partitions. The first algorithm outputs the n n th element of each sequence in O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) time and the second algorithm gives the assignment of the m m th positive integer to a sequence in O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) time. We show that the results are best-possible in several respects. Moreover, we describe applications of these results to apportionment and optimal scheduling problems.



1
Introduction

1.1
Webster Sequences

A classical problem that arises in many contexts is the sequencing problem (see, for example, Kubiak
[
21
, p. vii]
). Given a finite alphabet and a list of frequencies for these letters, we seek to construct a sequence over the given alphabet in which each letter occurs with its prescribed frequency as evenly as possible. This problem is equivalent to partitioning the set of natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
into sequences with prescribed densities as evenly as possible.

For a single sequence with density
\alpha
, the “ideal” position of the
n
th element is
n/\alpha
. However,
n/\alpha
is in general not an integer, so the best one can do is to assign the
n
th element of the sequence to the nearest integer below or above
n/\alpha
, i.e., to
{\lfloor n/\alpha\rfloor}
or
{\lceil n/\alpha\rceil}
. This leads to the sequences

B_{\alpha}=\left({\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha}\right\rfloor}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\overline{B}_{\alpha}=\left({\left\lceil\frac{n}{\alpha}\right\rceil}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

In fact, the former sequence is known as the
Beatty sequence of density
\alpha

[
6
]
. More generally, given a real number
\theta
, the
non-homogeneous Beatty sequence of density
\alpha
and phase
\theta

[
30
]
is defined as

B_{\alpha,\theta}=\left({\left\lfloor\frac{n+\theta}{\alpha}\right\rfloor}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

Note that
B_{\alpha}=B_{\alpha,0}
and (assuming
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Q}
)
\overline{B}_{\alpha}=B_{\alpha,\alpha}
, so
B_{\alpha}
and
\overline{B}_{\alpha}
are both special cases of
B_{\alpha,\theta}
.

In this paper, we focus on another special case, namely the sequence (assuming
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Q}
)

(1.1)

W_{\alpha}=B_{\alpha,\alpha-1/2}=\left({\left\lfloor\frac{n+\alpha-1/2}{\alpha}\right\rfloor}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=\left({\left\lceil\frac{n-1/2}{\alpha}\right\rceil}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}},

which we call the
Webster sequence of density
\alpha
. The terminology is motivated by the connection with the Webster method of apportionment (see Section
1.2
). The Webster sequence represents, in a sense, the “fairest” sequence among all sequences
B_{\alpha,\theta}
when measured by the quantity

(1.2)

\Delta_{\alpha,\theta}(m)=\#\{m^{\prime}\leq m:m^{\prime}\in B_{\alpha,\theta}\}-m\alpha.

Indeed, it is easy to see (cf. Lemma
3.4
(ii) below) that for the Webster sequence (i.e., when
\theta=\alpha-1/2
), the quantity
\sup_{m}|\Delta_{\alpha,\theta}(m)|
is minimal (and equal to
1/2
).

As mentioned, the problem of sequencing letters from a given alphabet of size
k
is equivalent to the problem of constructing a partition of
\mathbb{N}
into
k
sequences with prescribed densities. In the case
k=2
, such partitions can be obtained using either Beatty or Webster sequences.

Theorem 1.1

(Beatty’s Theorem: Partition into two Beatty sequences
[
6
]
)
.

Given positive irrational numbers
\alpha,\beta
with
\alpha+\beta=1
, the Beatty sequences
B_{\alpha},B_{\beta}
partition
\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 1.2

(Partition into two Webster sequences
[
10
,
30
]
)
.

Given positive irrational numbers
\alpha,\beta
with
\alpha+\beta=1
, the Webster sequences
W_{\alpha},W_{\beta}
partition
\mathbb{N}
.

It is known that these theorems do not generalize to three sequences if their densities are irrational
[
41
,
33
,
12
]
. For rational densities
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, a partition into non-homogeneous Beatty sequences exists only if
\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}=\{1/7,2/7,4/7\}
(see
[
11
]
and
[
28
]
). This result is a special case of Fraenkel’s Conjecture (see
[
11
]
or
[
21
, Conjecture 6.22]
), which characterizes the
k
-tuples of densities
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
for which a partition into
k
non-homogeneous Beatty sequences with the given densities exists. Fraenkel’s Conjecture has been proven for
k=3,4,5,6,7
(see Altman
[
1
]
, Tijdeman
[
40
,
39
]
, Morikawa
[
28
,
29
]
, and Barát & Varjú
[
4
]
), but the general case remains open.

In
[
14
]
, we considered partitions of
\mathbb{N}
into three Beatty sequences or small perturbations of Beatty sequences and proved the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
14
, Theorem 4]
)
.

Given positive irrational numbers
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
such that

\max(\alpha,\beta)<\gamma\text{ and }\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1,

there exists a partition of
\mathbb{N}
into sequences
B_{\alpha},\widetilde{B_{\beta}},\widetilde{B_{\gamma}}
, where
B_{\alpha}
is an exact Beatty sequence,
\widetilde{B_{\beta}}
is obtained from the Beatty sequence
B_{\beta}
by perturbing some elements by at most 1, and
\widetilde{B_{\gamma}}
is obtained from the Beatty sequence
B_{\gamma}
by perturbing some elements by at most 2.

The perturbation bounds 1 and 2 in this result are best-possible
[
14
, Theorem 8]
.

In this paper, we consider the analogous question for Webster sequences and prove an analog of Theorem
1.3
. Our main result, Theorem
2.2
, shows that when using Webster sequences, perturbations of at most 1 are needed to obtain a three-part partition of
\mathbb{N}
. Thus, Webster sequences are more efficient in generating partitions than Beatty sequences which require perturbations of up to 2 to obtain a partition.

We also provide two efficient algorithms, Algorithm
2.3
and Algorithm
2.4
, to generate the partitions. The first algorithm outputs the
n
th element of each of the three sequences and the second algorithm outputs the assignment of the
m
th natural number to a sequence, both in
O(1)
time.

Our results have natural interpretations in the context of apportionment problems and Just-In-Time sequencing problems. In the following sections, we describe some of these applications.

1.2
Apportionment Methods

Suppose we have a union of
k
states, with relative populations
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}
, respectively (so that
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\alpha_{i}=1
). We want to distribute
n
seats in the house to
n
representatives selected from these
k
states. Denote by
s_{i}
the number of representatives selected from state
i
. The numbers
s_{i}
must be nonnegative integers and satisfy
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}s_{i}=n
. This problem is called the
Discrete Apportionment Problem
. It has its origins in the problem of seat assignments to the house of representatives in the United States. The goal is to assign the seats in the fairest possible manner. While the “fairness” can be measured by various objective functions, most measures in the literature involve the differences between the fair share of representatives and the actual count of assigned representatives for each of the
k
states (i.e., the quantity
|s_{i}-n\alpha_{i}|
). Some desirable properties of an apportionment method are the following. Details and more properties can be found in Balinski & Young
[
3
, Appendix A]
, Kubiak
[
21
, Chapter 2.3]
, Pukelsheim
[
31
]
, Jozefowska
[
18
, Chapter 2]
, or Thapa
[
35
, Section 2.3]
.

House Monotone
: An apportionment method should have the property that the number of seats of each state is non-decreasing when
n
increases. This prevents the Alabama paradox, where increasing the number of seats in the house can result in a state losing seats
[
3
,
17
,
5
]
.

Population Monotone
: For any state
i
, the
number of representatives from state
i
should not decrease if
\alpha_{i}
increases.

Quota Condition
:
The “fair share” of seats for state
i
is
n\alpha_{i}
. We call this number the
quota
for state
i
. We say an apportionment satisfies the
quota condition
if
s_{i}\in\{{\lfloor n\alpha_{i}\rfloor},{\lceil n\alpha_{i}\rceil}\}
for each
i
, i.e., if the actual number of seats assigned is within
\pm 1
of the fair share.

The impossibility theorem of Balinski and Young
[
2
,
3
]
states that it is in general not possible to satisfy all the desirable requirements (including the three above) .

An important class of apportionment methods are divisor methods, which assign seats according to the formula

s_{i}=d\left(\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda}\right),

where
d(x)
is a rounding function and
\lambda
is a divisor chosen such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}=n
(see
[
3
, Appendix A]
,
[
31
, Section 4.5]
,
[
19
, Section 2.6]
, or
[
16
, Appendix A]
). All divisor methods are house and population monotone
[
3
, Theorem 4.3 & Corollary 4.3.1]
, but in general they do not satisfy the quota condition
[
3
, Chapter 10]
.

A particular divisor method is
Webster’s method
, which specifies
d(x)={\lceil x-1/2\rceil}
as the rounding function, i.e.,
\alpha_{i}/\lambda
is rounded to the nearest integer
[
2
]
. Webster’s method has several unique properties. It is the only divisor method that satisfies the quota condition for
k=3

[
2
, Theorem 6]
. It is the fairest method judged by natural criteria suggested by real-life problems
[
2
]
. In the case
k=2
and irrational densities
\alpha,\beta
, Webster’s method generates the partition given by Theorem
1.2
into the sequences
W_{\alpha}
and
W_{\beta}
defined in (
1.1
) (see
[
21
, Section 5.6]
). This justifies calling these sequences
Webster sequences
.

1.3
The Chairman Assignment Problem

A related problem is the
Chairman Assignment Problem

[
38
,
24
,
7
,
32
,
37
]
: Suppose a union is formed by
k
states, with associated weights
\alpha_{i}
(so that
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\alpha_{i}=1
). In each year, a union chair is selected from one of these
k
states. We try to construct an assignment of chairs such that the number of chairs selected from each state
i
in the first
n
years should be as close as possible to its expected count
n\alpha_{i}
. More precisely, we seek an assignment
\omega
that minimizes the quantity

(1.3)

D(\omega)=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{i=1,\dots,k}|A_{\omega}(i,n)-n\alpha_{i}|,

where
A_{\omega}(i,n)
denotes the number of chairs selected by
\omega
from state
i
in the first
n
years.

It is known
[
24
,
38
]
that
\inf_{\omega}D(\omega)=1-1/(2k-2)
and Tijdeman
[
38
]
gave an algorithm that generates, in linear time, the assignment that achieves this bound. Schneider
[
32
]
gives an improvement of this bound in some special cases. In particular, when
k=2
,
\inf_{\omega}D(\omega)=1/2
, and this bound is achieved by a partition into Webster sequences.

1.4
Just-In-Time Sequencing

Another closely related problem is the Just-In-Time (JIT) Sequencing Problem (see Jozefowska
[
18
]
and Kubiak
[
21
]
). In this problem, we seek to produce
k
types of products with demands
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, respectively, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}=N
. Assume that it takes one unit of time to produce one product of any kind. Then, the production rate for product
i
is
\alpha_{i}=d_{i}/N
and the “ideal” production for product
i
at time
n
is
n\alpha_{i}
. To measure the deviation of the actual productions of products from their ideal productions, Miltenburg
[
25
]
proposed the function

(1.4)

\max_{n=1}^{N}\max_{i=1}^{k}|a_{in}-n\alpha_{i}|,

where
a_{in}
denotes the actual production of product
i
in time
n
. Note that this is an analog of (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). Other authors
[
9
,
27
,
15
,
26
,
5
]
considered various types of average deviations such as

(1.5)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{in}-n\alpha_{i})^{2}

and

(1.6)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{n=1}^{d_{i}}\left(y_{in}-\frac{n-1/2}{\alpha_{i}}\right)^{2},

where
y_{in}
denotes the time when the
n
th copy of product
i
is produced. More general measures have been proposed by Dhamala & Kubiak
[
8
]
, Kubiak
[
20
]
, Thapa & Silvestrov
[
36
]
, and Steiner & Yeomans
[
34
]
. Efficient algorithms to minimize the different types of deviations defined above have been given by Kubiak & Sethi
[
22
]
, Steiner & Yeomans
[
34
]
, and Inman & Bulfin
[
15
]
. Kubiak
[
21
, Theorem 3.1]
showed that, if one disregards the possibility of conflicting assignments, then assigning the
n
th element of the
i
th sequence to position
{\lceil(n-1/2)/\alpha_{i}\rceil}
is the optimal solution to a very general class of minimization problems. Note that
{\lceil(n-1/2)/\alpha_{i}\rceil}
is exactly the
n
th term of the Webster sequence
W_{\alpha i}
defined in (
1.1
).

There are close connections between the JIT sequencing problem and the apportionment problem. For example, the Inman-Bulfin algorithm for minimizing (
1.6
) is equivalent to the Webster divisor method (see
[
5
]
and
[
19
]
). Moreover, the quota condition described in Section
1.2
is equivalent to requiring (
1.4
) to be bounded by 1. Also, the “house monotone” condition in the JIT problem means that the total number of products of each type produced up to time
n
is a monotone function of
n
and thus is a natural requirement in this problem.

Main Results

2.1
Notations and Terminology

We use
\mathbb{N}
to denote the set of positive integers, and we
use capital letters
A,B,\dots
, to denote subsets of
\mathbb{N}
or,
equivalently, strictly increasing sequences of positive integers. We denote
the
n
th elements of such sequences by
a(n)
,
b(n)
, etc. and we will usually use the letter
m
to denote the positions of these elements within
\mathbb{N}
, i.e., we may write
m=b(n)
.

We denote the floor and ceiling of a real number
x
by

\displaystyle{\lfloor x\rfloor}

\displaystyle=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n\leq x\},

\displaystyle{\lceil x\rceil}

\displaystyle=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n\geq x\}.

We denote the fractional part of
x
by

\{x\}=x-{\lfloor x\rfloor}.

Given a set
A\subset\mathbb{N}
, we denote the counting function of A by

(2.1)

\quad A(m)=\#\{m^{\prime}\leq m:m^{\prime}\in A\}\quad(m\in\mathbb{N}).

For convenience, we also define
A(0)=0
.

Definition 2.1

(Webster sequences and almost Webster sequences)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

(i)

We define the
Webster sequence of density
\alpha
as

(2.2)

\displaystyle W_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(a(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad a(n)={\left\lceil\frac{n-1/2}{\alpha}\right\rceil}.

(ii)

We call a sequence
\widetilde{W_{\alpha}}=(\widetilde{a}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
an
almost Webster sequence of density
\alpha
if, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
n
th element of
\widetilde{W_{\alpha}}
and
n
th element of
W_{\alpha}
differ by at most 1. That is, a sequence
\widetilde{W_{\alpha}}
is an
almost Webster sequence of density
\alpha
if

(2.3)

|\widetilde{a}(n)-a(n)|\leq 1\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Note that any Webster sequence is trivially an almost Webster sequence of the same density.

2.2
Statement of Main Theorem

Theorem 2.2

(Partition into one exact Webster sequence and two almost
Webster sequences)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
satisfy

(2.4)

\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\mathbb{Q},\quad\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1,\quad\beta<1/2,\quad\alpha<\gamma.

Then there exists a partition of
\mathbb{N}
into a Webster sequence,
W_{\alpha}
, of density
\alpha
and two almost Webster sequences,
\widetilde{W_{\beta}}
and
\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}
, of densities
\beta
and
\gamma
, respectively. That is, there exist sequences
\widetilde{W_{\alpha}}=(\widetilde{a}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\widetilde{W_{\beta}}=(\widetilde{b}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}=(\widetilde{c}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that partition
\mathbb{N}
and satisfy

(2.5)

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}(n)

\displaystyle=a(n)\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}),

(2.6)

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}(n)-b(n)

\displaystyle\in\{-1,0,1\}\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}),

(2.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}(n)-c(n)

\displaystyle\in\{-1,0,1\}\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}),

where
(a(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(b(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(c(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are the Webster sequences of densities
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, respectively.

In Section
7
, we will show that this result is best-possible in the sense that the conditions in (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) cannot be replaced by stronger conditions (see Theorem
7.3
). In particular, it is, in general, not possible to obtain a partition of
\mathbb{N}
into two exact Webster sequences and one almost Webster sequence with prescribed densities (see Theorem

).

2.3
Partition Algorithms

In the following, we provide two equivalent algorithms that generate the partition in Theorem
2.2
.
We assume that
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
satisfy condition (
2.4
). Note that condition (
2.4
) implies

(2.8)

\alpha,\beta<1/2\text{ and }\gamma>1/4

and

(2.9)

\alpha+\beta/2<1/2<\gamma+\beta/2.

We introduce the following notation that will appear frequently in the remainder of this paper:

(2.10)

u_{m}=\{m\alpha+1/2\},\quad v_{m}=\{m\beta+1/2\},

where
\{x\}
denotes the fractional part of
x
. Note that our assumption
\alpha,\beta\notin\mathbb{Q}
implies

(2.11)

u_{m}\notin\alpha\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Z},\quad v_{m}\notin\beta\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Z}.

To prove (
2.11
), suppose
u_{m}\in\alpha\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Z}
. Then
m\alpha+1/2=\alpha q+k
for some
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
q=m
, then
1/2=k
, which contradicts
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
q\neq m
, then
\alpha=(k-1/2)/(m-q)
, which contradicts
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Q}
.

The partition algorithms receive inputs
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, and output almost Webster sequences
\widetilde{W_{\alpha}},\widetilde{W_{\beta}},\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}
that partition
\mathbb{N}
. Our first algorithm generates the
n
th element in each of the sequences
\widetilde{W_{\alpha}},\widetilde{W_{\beta}},\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}
.

Algorithm 2.3
.

Let
\widetilde{W_{\alpha}}=(\widetilde{a}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\widetilde{W_{\beta}}=(\widetilde{b}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be defined by

(2.12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}(n)=a(n),

(2.13)

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}(n)=\begin{cases}b(n)&\text{if $u_{m}>\alpha$;}\\
b(n)-1&\text{if $u_{m}<\alpha$ and $v_{m}>\beta/2$;}\\
b(n)+1&\text{if $u_{m}<\alpha$ and $v_{m}<\beta/2$,}\end{cases}

where
m=b(n)
, and let
\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}=\mathbb{N}\setminus(W_{\alpha}\cup\widetilde{W_{\beta}})=(\widetilde{c}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Note that the three conditions in (
2.13
) are mutually exclusive and exhaust all possibilities, since by (
2.11
), the cases
u_{m}=\alpha
and
v_{m}=\beta/2
cannot occur. Moreover, by (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), the sequences
(\widetilde{a}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\widetilde{b}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Algorithm
2.3
clearly satisfy the conditions (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) of Theorem
2.2
and thus, in particular, are almost Webster sequences. In Proposition
6.1
below, we will show that the sequence
(\widetilde{c}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies condition (
2.7
) and thus is also an almost Webster sequence.

We remark that Algorithm
2.3
gives the
n
th element of the sequences
W_{\alpha},\widetilde{W_{\beta}},\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}
in
O(1)
time. This is clear from (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) in the case of the sequences
W_{\alpha}
and
\widetilde{W_{\beta}}
. In the case of the sequence
\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}
, a similar characterization of
\widetilde{c}(n)
proved in Proposition

\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}
in

.

Our second algorithm outputs, for a given

, the sequence to which

is assigned.

Algorithm 2.4
.

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let

(2.14)

m\in\begin{cases}\widetilde{W_{\alpha}}&\text{if $u_{m}<\alpha$;}\\
\widetilde{W_{\beta}}&\text{if ($u_{m}>\alpha$ and $v_{m}<\beta$) or ($u_{m}>1-\alpha$ and $v_{m}>1-\beta/2$)}\\
&\text{\hskip 10.22217ptor ($\alpha<u_{m}<2\alpha$ and $\beta<v_{m}<3\beta/2$);}\\
\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Again, noting that
\alpha<1/2
(see (
2.8
)), the three cases in (
2.14
) are mutually exclusive, and exhaust all possibilities, so the algorithm indeed generates a partition of
\mathbb{N}
.

In Section
4
, we will prove that the two algorithms are equivalent.

Proposition 2.5
.

Algorithm
2.3
and Algorithm
2.4
generate the same sequences.

In Section
5

Proposition 2.6

Lemma 3.1

(Generalized Weyl’s Theorem)
.

Let
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k}
be real numbers such that
the numbers
1,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k}
are linearly independent over

\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}
be arbitrary real numbers.
Then the
k
-dimensional sequence
(\{n\theta_{1}+\eta_{1}\},\dots,\{n\theta_{k}+\eta_{k})\})

is
uniformly distributed modulo

in

. That is,
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\{n\leq N:\{n\theta_{i}+\eta_{i}\}<t_{i}\text{ for $i=1,\dots,k$}\}=t_{1}\cdots t_{k}\quad(0\leq t_{i}\leq 1).

Proof.

This is a special case of Theorem 6.3 in Chapter 1 of Kuipers and Niederreiter
[
23
]
(see Example 6.1).
∎

Lemma 3.4

(Elementary properties of Webster sequences (cf.
[
14
, Lemma 10]
))
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, and let

W_{\alpha}=(a(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(\lceil(n-1/2)/\alpha\rceil)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=({\lfloor(n-1/2)/\alpha\rfloor}+1)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the Webster sequence of density
\alpha
.

(i)

Membership criterion:

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

m\in W_{\alpha}\Longleftrightarrow\{m\alpha+1/2\}<\alpha.

(ii)

Counting function formula:

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

W_{\alpha}(m)={\lfloor m\alpha+1/2\rfloor},

where
W_{\alpha}(m)
is the counting function of
W_{\alpha}
.

(iii)

Gap criterion:

Given
m\in W_{\alpha}
, let

m^{\prime}
denote the successor to
m
in the sequence

W_{\alpha}
. Let
k={\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor}
. Then,
m^{\prime}=m+k
or
m^{\prime}=m+k+1
. Moreover, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle m\in W_{\alpha}\text{ and }m^{\prime}=m+k

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\{1/\alpha\}\alpha<\{m\alpha+1/2\}<\alpha;

(3.2)

\displaystyle m\in W_{\alpha}\text{ and }m^{\prime}=m+k+1

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\{m\alpha+1/2\}<\{1/\alpha\}\alpha.

Note that by (
2.11
), equality cannot hold in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).

Proof.

(i) We have

\displaystyle m\in W_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow m={\lfloor(n-1/2)/\alpha+1\rfloor}\text{ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow m<(n-1/2)/\alpha+1<m+1\text{ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(m-1)\alpha<(n-1/2)<m\alpha\text{ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(m-1)\alpha+1/2<n<m\alpha+1/2\text{ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\{m\alpha+1/2\}<\alpha.

(ii) We have

\displaystyle W_{\alpha}(m)=n

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow{\lfloor(n-1/2)/\alpha\rfloor}+1\leq m\leq({\lfloor(n+1/2)/\alpha\rfloor}+1)-1

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(n-1/2)/\alpha+1<m+1<(n+1/2)/\alpha+1

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(n-1/2)<m\alpha<(n+1/2)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow n<m\alpha+1/2<n+1

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow{\lfloor m\alpha+1/2\rfloor}=n.

(iii)
Suppose
m=a(n)
and
m^{\prime}=a(n+1)
are successive elements in
W_{\alpha}
. We have

\displaystyle m^{\prime}-m

\displaystyle=a(n+1)-a(n)={\left\lfloor\frac{n+1-1/2}{\alpha}\right\rfloor}-{\left\lfloor\frac{n-1/2}{\alpha}\right\rfloor}

\displaystyle={\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}\right\rfloor}\text{ or }{\left\lceil\frac{1}{\alpha}\right\rceil}.

Hence,
m^{\prime}
must be equal to
m+k
or
m+k+1
, where
k={\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor}
.
Note that

k\alpha=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\left\{\frac{1}{\alpha}\right\}\right)\alpha=1-\left\{\frac{1}{\alpha}\right\}\alpha.

Thus, using the results of part (i) we have

\displaystyle m\in W_{\alpha}\text{ and }m^{\prime}=m+k

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow m\in W_{\alpha}\text{ and }m+k\in W_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\{m\alpha+1/2\}<\alpha\text{ and }\{m\alpha+1/2+k\alpha\}<\alpha

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\{m\alpha+1/2\}<\alpha\text{ and }\{m\alpha+1/2-\{1/\alpha\}\alpha\}<\alpha

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\{1/\alpha\}\alpha<\{m\alpha+1/2\}<\alpha.

This proves the equivalence (
3.1
) in (iii). Observe that, by (i),
m\in W_{\alpha}
is equivalent to

\{m\alpha+1/2\}\in(0,\alpha)
. The
equivalence (
3.2
) in (iii) then follows from the fact that
m^{\prime}
is either
m+k
or
m+k+1
.
∎

Lemma 6.8

Given

Case II:

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(\widetilde{m})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}(\widetilde{m})-W_{\gamma}(\widetilde{m})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}(\widetilde{m})-W_{\gamma}(m-1)

\displaystyle=n-(n-1)

\displaystyle=1.

Case III:

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(\widetilde{m})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}(\widetilde{m})-W_{\gamma}(\widetilde{m})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}(\widetilde{m})-W_{\gamma}(m+1)

\displaystyle=0\text{ or }-1.

Note that by Proposition

if and only if

7.5
), we then have
\widetilde{b}(n)\in\{m,m+1\}
and
\widetilde{c}(k)\in\{m,m+1,m+2\}
. Since
m,m+2\in W_{\alpha}
, this implies
\widetilde{b}(n)=m+1=\widetilde{c}(k)
, contradicting the partition property. Therefore, it suffices to prove that there exists an
m
satisfying (
7.6
).

Lemma
3.4
(i) gives that

m\in W_{\beta}\Longleftrightarrow v_{m}<\beta.

We have
m+1\in W_{\gamma}\Longleftrightarrow w_{m}>1-\gamma
. From (
5.2
) we get

\displaystyle m+1\in W_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow 1-\{u_{m}+v_{m}-1/2\}>1-\gamma

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\text{$1/2<u_{m}+v_{m}<\gamma+1/2$}

or
3/2<u_{m}+v_{m}<3/2+\gamma
.

By Lemma
3.4
(i) and (iii),

m,m+2\in W_{\alpha}\Longleftrightarrow\{1/\alpha\}\alpha<u_{m}<\alpha.

Note here we have used that
\alpha<\gamma<1/2
, so the gap between any two successive elements in
W_{\alpha}
is at least 2. Therefore (
7.6
) is equivalent to

(7.7)

\begin{cases}&\{1/\alpha\}\alpha<u_{m}<\alpha,\\
&1/2<u_{m}+v_{m}<\gamma+1/2\text{ or }3/2<u_{m}+v_{m}<3/2+\gamma,\\
&v_{m}<\beta.\end{cases}

By Corollary
3.3
and the linear independence of
1,\alpha,\beta
, for general
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the pairs
(u_{m},v_{m})
are uniformly distributed modulo 1. By choosing
u_{m}>\alpha-\epsilon,v_{m}>\beta-\epsilon
for a suitable positive
\epsilon
, one can check that
the regions inside the unit square defined by
the three conditions in (
7.7
) have a nonempty intersection. Hence, there exists an
m
satisfying (
7.6
). This completes the proof.
∎

Concluding Remarks

In this section, we discuss some possible generalizations and extensions of our results.

Necessity of the conditions in Theorem
2.2
.

The conclusion of Theorem
2.2
does not necessarily hold for the sequences generated by Algorithm
2.3
if the condition
\beta<1/2
or
\alpha<\gamma
in Theorem
2.2
is not satisfied. For example, a computer search shows that when
(\alpha,\beta)=(\sqrt{2}/10,3\sqrt{3}/10)=(0.14142\dots,0.51961\dots)
, the sequences generated by Algorithm
2.3
satisfy
|\widetilde{c}(n)-c(n)|=2
for
n=692
. Similarly, when
(\alpha,\beta)=(3\sqrt{2}/10,\sqrt{3}/10)=(0.42426\dots,0.17320\dots)
(which does not satisfy the condition
\alpha<\gamma
), the sequences generated by Algorithm
2.3
satisfy
|\widetilde{c}(n)-c(n)|=2
for
n=4787
. However, it may still be possible that, with a different construction, Theorem
2.2
remains valid under more general conditions on
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma
.

Partitions into two exact Webster sequences and one
almost Webster sequence.

By Theorem
7.2
, in general it is not possible to partition
\mathbb{N}
into two Webster sequences and one almost Webster sequence with given densities
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
. Nevertheless, for certain special irrational triples
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, we can obtain such a partition. In fact, we have the following theorem, which is analogous to Theorem 1 in
[
14
]
and can be proved using similar methods.

Theorem 8.1
.

Suppose
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
satisfy
(
2.4
) and there exist positive integers
r,s
such that

(8.1)

r\alpha+s\beta=1,\quad r\equiv s\mod 2,\quad r,s\geq 2

If we define
\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}=\mathbb{N}\setminus(W_{\alpha}\cup W_{\beta})
, then
W_{\alpha},W_{\beta},\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}
partition
\mathbb{N}
. Moreover,

\widetilde{c}(n)-c(n)\in\{-1,0,1\}\quad(n\in\mathbb{N})

and thus
\widetilde{W_{\gamma}}
is an almost Webster sequence.

The case of finite sequences and rational densities.

In real-life applications, we seek to partition a finite sequence
\{1,2,\dots,N\}

Partitions into
k
sequences.

A natural extension of our results would be partitions of
\mathbb{N}
into more than 3 sequences. However, by Theorem
7.2
, in general at most one of them can be a Webster sequence. In fact, as pointed out by the referee, in general, perturbations of size at least

are necessary to obtain a partition of
\mathbb{N}
into
k
sequences. This is obvious in the case when all densities
\alpha_{i}
are equal, i.e.,
\alpha_{i}=1/k
for
i=1,\dots,k
, since in this case, the Webster sequence
W_{\alpha_{i}}=W_{1/k}=\{{\lceil k/2\rceil},{\lceil 3k/2\rceil},{\lceil 5k/2\rceil},\dots\}
has gaps of size
k
