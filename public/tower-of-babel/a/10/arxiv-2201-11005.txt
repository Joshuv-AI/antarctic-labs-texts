1.
Introduction

The technique of mollifying various
L
-functions has proven to be extremely useful for obtaining information about their zeros. For instance, in the case of the Riemann zeta function, Levinson
[
15
]
showed how one may obtain a lower bound on the proportion of the non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
lying on the critical line by evaluating the mollified second moment

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)M_{N}(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)|^{2}dt.

(1.1)

Here, the mollifier
M_{N}(s)
is a Dirichlet polynomial of length
N
which should approximate
\zeta(s)^{-1}
. Levinson was able to obtain an asymptotic formula as
T\to\infty
for the mollified moment in (
1.1
) for
N=T^{\theta}
with
\theta<1/2
. As a consequence, he proved that at least
1/3
of the non-trivial zeros of the zeta function satisfy the Riemann Hypothesis. Subsequently, Conrey
[
9
]
computed the mollified second moment for all
\theta<4/7
and hence showed that the proportion of the non-trivial zeros on the critical line is at least
2/5
. By using different choices of mollifier, the proportion has been improved to
\geq 0.4105
in
[
3
]
and
\geq 0.417293
in
[
19
]
.

Another problem in analytic number theory where the mollifier method has been successfully applied is the non-vanishing of
L
-functions at the central point. A famous conjecture of Chowla
[
6
]
states that
L(\frac{1}{2},\chi)\neq 0
for all Dirichlet
L
-functions
L(s,\chi)
with
\chi
a primitive character. While this conjecture remains open, there are now numerous results in the literature on the non-vanishing of various families of
L
-functions at
s=1/2
. In the case of Dirichlet
L
-functions, it was shown by Iwaniec and Sarnak
[
12
]
that for at least
1/3
of primitive characters
\chi
to a sufficiently large modulus
q
, the
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
does not vanish at
s=1/2
. This proportion was improved to
\geq 0.341
with the use of a two piece mollifier by Bui in
[
2
]
.

In the case of the family of quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions
L(s,\chi_{d})
, Soundararajan
[
20
]
computed both the first and second mollified moments for a sufficiently short mollifier. This allowed Soundararajan to show that
L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{d})\neq 0
for at least
7/8
of quadratic characters
\chi_{d}
. By studying the one-level density of zeros of
the
L
-functions
L(s,\chi_{d})
, Özlük and Snyder
[
18
]
showed, assuming GRH, that the proportion of non-vanishing at
s=1/2
is at least
15/16
.

In general, the way to obtain stronger results on the zeros and non-vanishing of
L
-functions using the mollifier method is to increase the length of the mollifier. However, this is often a very difficult problem involving intricate analysis. The Ratios Conjecture of Conrey, Farmer and Zirnbauer
[
8
]
provides a relatively easy way to compute asymptotic formulae for mollified moments. In particular, one may write the mollified moments as certain contour integrals involving the mean values of ratios of
L
-functions and then apply the Ratios Conjecture to yield an asymptotic formula. Conrey and Snaith
[
10
]
gave multiple examples of computing mollified moments in this way. They show that the Ratios Conjecture implies formulae for the mollified moments with arbitrarily long mollifiers and that these formula agree with known results in the literature for sufficiently short mollifiers.

The method of mollifying may also be applied to derivatives of
L
-functions as well. For instance, it is known that RH implies that all the zeros of
\xi^{(k)}(s)
, where
\xi(s)
is Riemann’s
\xi
-function, lie on the line
\textup{Re}(s)=1/2
. By considering the second mollified moment of
\xi^{(k)}(s)
, Conrey
[
7
]
showed that the proportion of zeros of
\xi^{(k)}(s)
on the critical line is
1+O(k^{-2})
as
k\to\infty
. Importantly, this result does not require an arbitrarily long mollifier to be used.

A similar approach to that employed by Soundararajan in
[
20
]
can be used to yield non-vanishing results for the derivatives of
L
-functions at the central point. In
[
14
]
, Kowalski, Michel and VanderKam gave asymptotic formulae for the mollified first and second moments of the derivatives
\Lambda^{(k)}(\tfrac{1}{2},f)
where
\Lambda(s,f)
is the completed
L
-function attached to a primitive Hecke eigenform
f
of weight 2. This is an orthogonal family of
L
-functions so half of the family have an even functional equation and the other half odd. Consequently, the strongest result one can hope to obtain is that
50\%
of
\Lambda^{(k)}(\tfrac{1}{2},f)\neq 0
for any
k
. The key result of
[
14
]
is that this proportion can be shown to tend to
50\%
as
k\to\infty
, regardless of the length of the mollifier. This is analogous to the result of Conrey
[
7
]
on the proportion of zeros of
\xi^{(k)}(s)
on the critical line. Using similar techniques, Michel and VanderKam
[
17
]
computed the mollified moments of the derivatives
\Lambda^{(k)}(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi)
where now
\Lambda(s,\chi)
is a completed Dirichlet
L
-function attached to the character
\chi
. They show that as
k\to\infty
, the proportion of non-vanishing of
\Lambda^{(k)}(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi)
approaches
1/2
if using the “standard” mollifier and
2/3
if they use a two-piece mollifier.

In this paper, we consider the mollified moments of the family of quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions in the function field setting. To set some notation, let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field with
q
odd and let
\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
denote the hyperelliptic ensemble, i.e. the set of monic, square-free polynomials of degree
2g+1
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. For each
D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
, let
\chi_{D}
be the quadratic character to the modulus
D
and let
L(s,\chi_{D})
be the associated Dirichlet
L
-function. Using a geometric argument, Li
[
16
]
showed that infinitely many of the
L
-functions
L(s,\chi_{D})
do vanish at
s=1/2
and so the analogue of Chowla’s conjecture in this setting does not hold. However, the density conjectures of Katz and Sarnak
[
13
]
predict that
L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})\neq 0
for
100\%
of discriminants
D
. Bui and Florea
[
4
]
, by computing the one-level density of zeros in the family, proved that the proportion of
L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})
which do not vanish is greater than
94\%
. Ellenberg, Li and Shusterman
[
11
]
obtained an upper bound, depending on
q
, on the proportion of
L(s,\chi_{D})
which do vanish at
s=1/2
. Their bound tends to
0
as
q\to\infty
and thus improves on the bound given in
[
4
]
for sufficiently large
q
.

2.
Background and statement of results

2.1.
Quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions over function fields

Here we will cover the necessary background on quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions over function fields. Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field with
q
odd and let
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
be the ring of polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}_{n}
the sets of monic polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and the monic polynomials of degree
n
, respectively. The polynomial
P
is said to be prime if it is both monic and irreducible and we denote the set of prime polynomials by
\mathcal{P}
.

Throughout, we will write
d(f)
for the degree of a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. The norm of the polynomial
f
is defined to be
|f|=q^{d(f)}
if
f\neq 0
and
|f|=0
if
f=0
. For
\textup{Re}(s)>1
, the zeta function of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
is defined by the Dirichlet series and Euler product

\zeta_{q}(s)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{|f|^{s}}=\prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{|P|^{s}}\bigg)^{-1}.

(2.1)

Since there are
q^{n}
monic polynomials of degree
n
, we have that

\zeta_{q}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n}}{q^{ns}}=\frac{1}{1-q^{1-s}},

(2.2)

which immediately provides a meromorphic continuation of
\zeta_{q}(s)
to the complex plane with a simple pole at
s=1
.

We denote by
\mathcal{H}
the set of monic, square-free polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and by
\mathcal{H}_{n}
the set of monic, square-free polynomials of degree
n
. The cardinality of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is

\displaystyle|\mathcal{H}_{n}|=\begin{cases}1&n=0,\\
q^{n}(1-q^{-1})&n\geq 1,\end{cases}

(2.3)

as can be seen by considering the coefficient of
q^{-ns}
in the series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}{q^{ns}}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{|f|^{s}}=\frac{\zeta_{q}(s)}{\zeta_{q}(2s)}.

(2.4)

For
D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
, we define the quadratic character
\chi_{D}
using the Kronecker symbol over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
by

\chi_{D}(f)=\bigg(\frac{D}{f}\bigg).

(2.5)

The quadratic Dirichlet
L
-function attached to the character
\chi_{D}
is defined for
\textup{Re}(s)>1
by

L(s,\chi_{D})=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}=\prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\bigg(1-\frac{\chi_{D}(P)}{|P|^{s}}\bigg)^{-1}.

(2.6)

With the change of variables
u=q^{-s}
, we define
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})=L(s,\chi_{D})
which is then given by the power series

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\chi_{D}(f)u^{d(f)}=\prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\big(1-\chi_{D}(P)u^{d(P)}\big)^{-1}.

(2.7)

By the orthogonality relations for Dirichlet characters over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, we have that
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})
is in fact a polynomial in
u
of degree
2g
and furthermore, it satisfies the functional equation

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})=(qu^{2})^{g}\mathcal{L}\bigg(\frac{1}{qu},\chi_{D}\bigg).

(2.8)

Equivalently, we have that
L(s,\chi_{D})
is a polynomial of degree
2g
in
q^{-s}
and satisfies

L(s,\chi_{D})=q^{g(1-2s)}L(1-s,\chi_{D}).

(2.9)

We define the completed
L
-function
\Lambda(s,\chi_{D})=q^{\frac{g}{2}(2s-1)}L(s,\chi_{D})
which satisfies the symmetric functional equation

\Lambda(s,\chi_{D})=\Lambda(1-s,\chi_{D}).

(2.10)

Finally, for
D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
, the hyperelliptic curve
C_{D}
given by the affine equation
y^{2}=D(x)
is a smooth, projective and connected curve of genus
g
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The zeta function of the curve
C_{D}

Z_{C_{D}}(u)=\exp\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}N_{n}(C_{D})\frac{u^{n}}{n}\bigg),

(2.11)

where
N_{n}(C_{D})
is the number of points on
C_{D}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
, including the point at infinity. Weil
[
21
]
showed that
Z_{C_{D}}(u)
is a rational function of the form

Z_{C_{D}}(u)=\frac{P_{C_{D}}(u)}{(1-u)(1-qu)},

(2.12)

where
P_{C_{D}}(u)\in\mathbb{Z}[u]
is a polynomial of degree
2g
. It was proven in Artin’s thesis that
P_{C_{D}}(u)=\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})
. By the Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields, also proven by Weil
[
21
]
, all of the zeros of
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})
lie on the circle
|u|=q^{-1/2}
.

2.2.
The Ratios Conjecture

The Ratios Conjecture for the family of quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions over function fields was formulated by Andrade and Keating in
[
1
]
. For simplicity, we state the conjecture below only in the case where the number of
L
-functions in the numerator and denominator is the same.

Conjecture 2.1

(The Ratios Conjecture)
.

For
|\textup{Re}(\alpha_{j})|<1/4
and
1/g\ll\textup{Re}(\beta_{j})<1/4
for
j=1,\dots,k
, we have as
g\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha_{j},\chi_{D})}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma_{j},\chi_{D})}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\epsilon_{j}\in\{-1,1\}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}q^{g(\epsilon_{j}\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j})}YA(\epsilon_{1}\alpha_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{k}\alpha_{k};\gamma)+O(q^{-\delta g})

(2.13)

for some
\delta>0
, where

Y(\alpha;\beta)=\frac{\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k}\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j})\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\zeta_{q}(1+\beta_{i}+\beta_{j})}{\prod_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j})},

(2.14)

and

\displaystyle A(\alpha;\beta)=

\displaystyle\prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k}\left(1-\frac{1}{|P|^{1+\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}}}\right)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\left(1-\frac{1}{|P|^{1+\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}}}\right)}{\prod_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\left(1-\frac{1}{|P|^{1+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}}}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{|P|}{|P|+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{|P|^{1/2+\beta_{j}}}\right)}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{|P|^{1/2+\alpha_{j}}}\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1+\frac{1}{|P|^{1/2+\beta_{j}}}\right)}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1+\frac{1}{|P|^{1/2+\alpha_{j}}}\right)}+\frac{1}{|P|}\right).

(2.15)

The conditions on the real parts of the shifts
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
are there to ensure that the Euler products appearing in the conjectured formula are convergent. However, the conjecture may be extended to a wider ranger so long as the Euler products are convergent. As mentioned in the introduction, the above conjecture has recently been proven for certain ranges of the parameters when
k\leq 3
by Bui, Florea and Keating in
[
5
]
. Specifically, they prove the following.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
0<\textup{Re}(\beta_{j})<1/2
for
1\leq j\leq k
. Denote
\alpha=\max\{|\textup{Re}(\alpha_{1})|,\dots,|\textup{Re}(\alpha_{k})|\}
and
\beta=\min\{\textup{Re}(\beta_{1}),\dots,\textup{Re}(\beta_{k})\}
. Then Conjecture
2.1
holds for
1\leq k\leq 3
with the error term
E_{k}
, where

E_{1}\ll_{\varepsilon}\begin{cases}q^{-g\beta(3+2|\textup{Re}(\alpha)|)+\varepsilon g\beta}\ \text{if}\ 0\leq\textup{Re}(\alpha)<1/2\ \text{and}\ \beta\gg g^{-1/2+\varepsilon},\\
q^{-g\beta(3-4|\textup{Re}(\alpha)|)+\varepsilon g\beta}\ \text{if}\ -1/2<\textup{Re}(\alpha)<0\ \text{and}\ \beta\gg g^{-1/2+\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(2.16)

and

E_{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{-g\beta\min\{\frac{1-4\alpha}{1+\beta},\frac{1-2\alpha}{2+\beta}\}+\varepsilon g\beta}\ \text{if}\ \alpha<1/4\ \text{and}\ \beta\gg g^{-1/4+\varepsilon},

(2.17)

E_{3}\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{-g\beta\min\{\frac{1/4-4\alpha}{\beta},\frac{1/2-4\alpha}{3+\beta}\}+\varepsilon g\beta}\ \text{if}\ \alpha<1/16\ \text{and}\ \beta\gg g^{-1/6+\varepsilon}.

(2.18)

We will make use of Theorem
2.2
in the cases
k=1
and
k=2
. We will see in the proof of our main results on the mollified moments that because Theorem
2.2
does not allow us to take
\beta\gg 1/g
as in the Ratios Conjecture, it is the size of the bound on the error term that determines how long a mollifier we may take. For this reason, we also include the following result which improves the bound on the error
E_{2}
in Theorem
2.2
.

Proposition 2.3
.

With notation as in Theorem
2.2
, suppose
\alpha<1/2
and
\beta\gg g^{-1/4+\varepsilon}
. Then, the error term
E_{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle E_{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}\begin{cases}q^{-2g\beta(1+2\min\{\textup{Re}(\alpha_{1}),\textup{Re}(\alpha_{2})\})+\varepsilon g\beta}\ &\textup{if}\ \textup{Re}(\alpha_{1}),\textup{Re}(\alpha_{2})\geq 0,\\
q^{-2g\beta(1-2\max\{|\textup{Re}(\alpha_{1})|,|\textup{Re}(\alpha_{2})|\})+\varepsilon g\beta}\ &\textup{if}\ \textup{Re}(\alpha_{1}),\textup{Re}(\alpha_{2})<0,\\
q^{-2g\beta(1+2\min\{0,\textup{Re}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\})+\varepsilon g\beta}&\textup{if}\ \textup{Re}(\alpha_{i})\geq 0\ \textup{and}\ \textup{Re}(\alpha_{j})<0\ \textup{for}\ i\neq j.\end{cases}

(2.19)

The strategy used to prove Theorem
2.2
in
[
5
]
is to first expand the
L
-functions in the denominator and write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha_{j},\chi_{D})}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\beta_{j},\chi_{D})}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mu(f_{j})}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}|f_{j}|^{1/2+\beta_{j}}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha_{j},\chi_{D})\bigg)\chi_{D}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}\bigg).

(2.20)

They then truncate this series and set

S_{k,\leq X}=\sum_{f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq X}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(f_{j})}{|f_{j}|^{1/2+\beta_{j}}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha_{j},\chi_{D})\bigg)\chi_{D}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}\bigg),

(2.21)

where
X
is a parameter to be chosen. Also,
S_{k,>X}
is defined to be the sum of the terms in (
2.2
) where at least one of the
f_{j}
’s has degree larger than
X
. Then, by proving upper bounds for negative moments of these
L
-functions, it is shown in
[
5
]
that

S_{k,>X}\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{-(1-\varepsilon)X\beta},

(2.22)

for
\beta\gg g^{-1/2k+\varepsilon}
. Also proven in
[
5
]
are asymptotic formulae for the twisted, shifted moments appearing in (
2.2
) for
k\leq 3
. Inserting the main terms of these formulae into
S_{k,\leq X}
, extending the sums over
f_{j}
to be over all
f_{j}\in\mathcal{M}
(which introduces a negligible error) and then performing an Euler product computation yields the main terms as predicted by the Ratios Conjecture.

For the contribution of the error terms in the twisted moment formulae to
S_{k,\leq X}
in the case
k=2
or
k=3
, Bui, Florea and Keating use their overall bounds for the error and bound the sums over
f_{j}
trivially. An optimum value for the parameter
X
is then chosen to yield the bound on the error terms
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
in Theorem
2.2
. However, for
k=1
, they keep the error terms in the first twisted moment explicit and make use of the cancellation coming from the Möbius function in the sum over
f_{1}
. This leads to a better error term and a wider range for the shift parameter
\alpha
. The proof of Proposition
2.3
is similar to the case of
k=1
covered in
[
5
, Section 4.1]
and so we omit the details.

2.3.
The mollification

For our choice of mollifier, we take the standard notion of a mollifier in the number field setting and translate it to the function field setting in the natural way. The mollifier is a Dirichlet polynomial whose purpose is to approximate
L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})^{-1}
so we begin with the Dirichlet series

\frac{1}{L(s,\chi_{D})}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{\mu(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}},

(2.23)

which is convergent for
\textup{Re}(s)>1/2
. Truncating this series and multiplying by a smoothing function leads us to the mollifier

M_{y}(\chi_{D},P)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}\\
|f|\leq y\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{1/2}}P\left(\frac{\log(y/|f|)}{\log y}\right),

(2.24)

where
P(x)
is a polynomial satisfying
P(0)=0
and
y=(q^{2g})^{\theta}
for some
\theta>0
. Provided that the mollifier is not too long, i.e.
\theta
is sufficiently small, we will prove asymptotic formula for the first and second mollified moments

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P),

(2.25)

and

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}|L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P)|^{2}.

(2.26)

Our main results are now stated below.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
Q
be an even polynomial and
P
a polynomial satisfying
P(0)=0
. Then for
\theta<3/2
, we have as
g\to\infty
,

\displaystyle Q\left(\frac{1}{g\log q}\frac{d}{d\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\Lambda(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P)\bigg|_{\alpha=0}=P(1)Q(1)+\frac{1}{2\theta}P^{\prime}(1)\int_{0}^{1}Q(t)\,dt+O(1/g).

(2.27)

Assuming Conjecture
2.1
, the result holds for any
\theta>0
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
Q_{1},Q_{2}
be even polynomials and
P_{1},P_{2}
polynomials satisfying
P_{j}(0)=P_{j}^{\prime}(0)=0
for
j=1,2
. Then for
\theta<1/2
, we have as
g\to\infty
,

\displaystyle Q_{1}\left(\frac{1}{g\log q}\frac{d}{d\alpha}\right)Q_{2}\left(\frac{1}{g\log q}\frac{d}{d\beta}\right)\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\Lambda(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})\Lambda(\tfrac{1}{2}+\beta,\chi_{D})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P_{1})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P_{2})\bigg|_{\alpha=\beta=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\theta}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{\theta}P_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)\tilde{Q}_{1}(u)-4\theta P_{1}(r)Q_{1}^{\prime}(u)\bigg)\left(\frac{1}{\theta}P_{2}^{\prime\prime}(r)\tilde{Q}_{2}(u)-4\theta P_{2}(r)Q_{2}^{\prime}(u)\right)\,du\,dr

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}P_{1}^{\prime}(1)\tilde{Q}_{1}(1)+2P_{1}(1)Q_{1}(1)\right)\left(\frac{1}{\theta}P_{2}^{\prime}(1)\tilde{Q}_{2}(1)+2P_{2}(1)Q_{2}(1)\right)+O(1/g),

(2.28)

where

\tilde{Q}(u)=\int_{0}^{u}Q(t)\,dt.

(2.29)

Assuming Conjecture
2.1
, the result holds for any
\theta>0
.

Remark 2.6

(2.30)

2.4.
Applications to non-vanishing

As an application of our results on the mollified moments, we are able to obtain non-vanishing results on the derivatives
\Lambda^{(2k)}(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})
. For
Q(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}
an even polynomial, we define the differential operator

\widehat{Q}=Q\left(\frac{1}{g\log q}\frac{d}{ds}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{(g\log q)^{n}}\frac{d^{n}}{ds^{n}}.

(2.33)

Then we define the first and second mollified moments

\mathcal{S}_{1}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\widehat{Q}\big(\Lambda(s,\chi_{D})\big)(\tfrac{1}{2})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P),

(2.34)

and

\mathcal{S}_{2}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}|\widehat{Q}\big(\Lambda(s,\chi_{D})\big)(\tfrac{1}{2})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P)|^{2}.

(2.35)

By Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
, we have

\mathcal{S}_{1}(P,Q)=P(1)Q(1)+\frac{1}{2\theta}P^{\prime}(1)\tilde{Q}(1)+O(1/g),

(2.36)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\bigg(P(1)Q(1)+\frac{1}{2\theta}P^{\prime}(1)\tilde{Q}(1)\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{8\theta}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{\theta}P^{\prime\prime}(r)\tilde{Q}(u)-4\theta P(r)Q^{\prime}(u)\bigg)^{2}\,du\,dr+O(1/g).

(2.37)

By applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the usual way, we have

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}|\{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}:\widehat{Q}\big(\Lambda(s,\chi_{D})\big)(\tfrac{1}{2})\neq 0\}|\geq\frac{\mathcal{S}_{1}(P,Q)^{2}}{\mathcal{S}_{2}(P,Q)}.

(2.38)

Using the above two expression for
\mathcal{S}_{1}(P,Q)
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}(P,Q)
on the right-hand side of this inequality then gives us the following general non-vanishing result.

Theorem 2.7
.

For
Q
an even polynomial, we have that as
g\to\infty
,

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}|\{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}:\widehat{Q}\big(\Lambda(s,\chi_{D})\big)(\tfrac{1}{2})\neq 0\}|\geq\sup_{P,\theta}\frac{1}{1+\mathcal{R}(P,Q)}+o(1),

(2.39)

where the supremum is over all polynomials
P
with
P(0)=P^{\prime}(0)=0
and real numbers
0<\theta<1/2
. Also, the ratio
\mathcal{R}(P,Q)
is given by

\mathcal{R}(P,Q)=\frac{(2\theta)^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\big(P^{\prime\prime}(r)\tilde{Q}(u)-4\theta^{2}P(r)Q^{\prime}(u)\big)^{2}\,du\,dr}{\big(2\theta P(1)Q(1)+P^{\prime}(1)\tilde{Q}(1)\big)^{2}}.

(2.40)

Remark 2.8
.

(1)

The ratio
\mathcal{R}(P,Q)

(2)

The expression for the ratio

By the continuity of

are absolutely convergent on
[0,1]
.

Finally, we examine in more detail the non-vanishing of

3.
The mollified first moment

In this section we will prove Theorem
2.4
. We begin by considering the shifted mollified moment

\mathcal{M}(\alpha,P):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P).

(3.1)

Lemma 3.1
.

For
\theta<3/2
, we have

\mathcal{M}(\alpha,P)=\frac{1+q^{-2g\alpha}}{2}P(1)+\frac{1-q^{-2g\alpha}}{2\alpha\log y}P^{\prime}(1)+O(1/g),

(3.2)

uniformly for
\alpha\ll 1/g
. Assuming Conjecture
2.1
, the result holds for any
\theta>0
.

Proof.

First, we write the mollifier in the form of a contour integral by using the fact that for
|f|\leq y
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left(\frac{\log(y/|f|)}{\log y}\right)^{n}=\frac{n!}{\log^{n}y}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\left(\frac{y}{|f|}\right)^{z}\frac{dz}{z^{n+1}},

(3.3)

where
c>0
. This can be seen by moving the contour to
-\infty
and computing the residue of the pole at
z=0
. Therefore, by writing
P(x)=\sum_{n\geq 1}p_{n}x^{n}
, we have

M_{y}(\chi_{D},P)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}n!}{\log^{n}y}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}\\
|f|\leq y\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{1/2}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\left(\frac{y}{|f|}\right)^{z}\frac{dz}{z^{n+1}}.

(3.4)

Now, if
|f|>y
, by moving the contour far to the right we see that the integral above vanishes. Thus, we may drop the condition that
|f|\leq y
from the sum over
f
. Then, since
\textrm{Re}(z)>0
, the resulting series converges and we have

M_{y}(\chi_{D},P)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}n!}{\log^{n}y}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\frac{y^{z}}{z^{n+1}}\frac{dz}{L(\tfrac{1}{2}+z,\chi_{D})}.

(3.5)

Using this expression for the mollifier in (
3.1
) yields

\mathcal{M}(\alpha,P)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}n!}{\log^{n}y}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\frac{y^{z}}{z^{n+1}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\frac{L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})}{L(\tfrac{1}{2}+z,\chi_{D})}\,dz.

(3.6)

By Theorem
2.2
, for
|\textup{Re}(\alpha)|<1/2
and
g^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\ll c<1/2
, the mean value of the ratio of
L
-functions appearing in the integrand is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\frac{L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})}{L(\tfrac{1}{2}+z,\chi_{D})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta_{q}(1+2\alpha)}{\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+z)}A(\alpha;z)+q^{-2g\alpha}\frac{\zeta_{q}(1-2\alpha)}{\zeta_{q}(1-\alpha+z)}A(-\alpha;z)

\displaystyle\qquad+O(q^{-gc(3-4|\textup{Re}(\alpha)|)+\varepsilon cg}),

(3.7)

where
A(\alpha;z)
is the Euler product defined in (
2.1
). As
y=q^{2g\theta}
and
\alpha\ll 1/g
, by bounding the integral by absolute values, the contribution of the error term above to (
3.6
) will be

\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{cg(2\theta-3+\varepsilon)}.

(3.8)

This error is
\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{-\varepsilon g}
for some
\varepsilon>0
if and only if
\theta<3/2
. On the other hand, if we assume the Ratios Conjecture, then (
3
) holds for
1/g\ll c<1/2
with an error that is
O(q^{-\varepsilon g})
uniformly. In this case, we may take
c\asymp 1/g
and then the contribution of the error term to (
3.6
) will be
\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{-\varepsilon g}
for any
\theta>0
. Thus, unconditionally for
\theta<3/2
and conditionally for any
\theta>0
, we may write

\mathcal{M}(\alpha,P)=I(\alpha,P)+q^{-2g\alpha}I(-\alpha,P)+O(q^{-\varepsilon g}),

(3.9)

where

I(\alpha,P)=\zeta_{q}(1+2\alpha)\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}n!}{\log^{n}y}J_{\alpha}(y),

(3.10)

with

J_{\alpha}(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\frac{y^{z}}{z^{n+1}}\frac{A(\alpha;z)}{\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+z)}\,dz.

(3.11)

By moving the contour to
\textup{Re}(z)=-\delta
, where
\delta>0
is sufficiently small so that the Euler product is absolutely convergent, we have that
J_{\alpha}(y)
is given by the residue at
z=0
plus the new integral along the line
\textup{Re}(z)=-\delta
. We write the residue at
z=0
as an integral where the contour is a circle around zero and we bound the new integral trivially by
O(y^{-\delta})
. We therefore have that

J_{\alpha}(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{y^{z}}{z^{n+1}}\frac{A(\alpha;z)}{\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+z)}\,dz+O(q^{-\varepsilon g}),

(3.12)

where the contour is now a circle of radius
\asymp 1/g
around zero. On this circular contour, for
\alpha,z\ll 1/g
, we use the Taylor expansion

\frac{A(\alpha;z)}{\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+z)}=(\alpha+z)A(0;0)\log q+O(g^{-2}).

(3.13)

From the definition in (
2.1
), we have that
A(0;0)=1
which gives us

J_{\alpha}(y)=\frac{\log q}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{y^{z}}{z^{n+1}}\left((\alpha+z)+O(g^{-2})\right)\,dz=\frac{\log q}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{y^{z}}{z^{n+1}}(\alpha+z)\,dz+O(g^{n-2}).

(3.14)

Next, by computing the residue at
z=0
, we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}n!}{\log^{n}y}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{y^{z}}{z^{n+1}}dz=\sum_{n\geq 1}p_{n}=P(1)

(3.15)

and

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}n!}{\log^{n}y}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{y^{z}}{z^{n}}dz=\frac{1}{\log y}\sum_{n\geq 1}np_{n}=\frac{1}{\log y}P^{\prime}(1).

(3.16)

Therefore, by combining (
3.10
), (
3.14
), (
3.15
) and (
3.16
), we have

I(\alpha;P)=\zeta_{q}(1+2\alpha)\log q\left(\alpha P(1)+\frac{1}{\log y}P^{\prime}(1)+O(g^{-2})\right).

(3.17)

Lastly, for
\alpha\asymp 1/g
, we have the Laurent expansion

\zeta_{q}(1+2\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\alpha\log q}+O(1)=O(g),

(3.18)

and so we have

I(\alpha;P)=\frac{1}{2}P(1)+\frac{1}{2\alpha\log y}P^{\prime}(1)+O(g^{-1}),

(3.19)

uniformly on any fixed annulus
|\alpha|\asymp 1/g
. Plugging this back into (
3.9
) yields

\mathcal{M}(\alpha;P)=\frac{1+q^{-2g\alpha}}{2}P(1)+\frac{1-q^{-2g\alpha}}{2\alpha\log y}P^{\prime}(1)+O(g^{-1}),

(3.20)

with the assumption that
\alpha\asymp 1/g
. But since
\mathcal{M}(\alpha;P)
and the main term above are holomorphic for
\alpha\ll 1/g
, the error term is also holomorphic in this region. By the maximum modulus principle, the bound on the error term also holds uniformly for
\alpha\ll 1/g
which completes the proof.
∎

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.4

We define

\mathcal{N}(\alpha;P)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\Lambda(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P).

(3.21)

By definition,
\Lambda(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})=q^{g\alpha}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})
and so by Lemma
3.1
, we have

\mathcal{N}(\alpha;P)=\frac{q^{g\alpha}+q^{-g\alpha}}{2}P(1)+\frac{q^{g\alpha}-q^{-g\alpha}}{2\alpha\log y}P^{\prime}(1)+O(g^{-1}),

(3.22)

uniformly for
\alpha\ll 1/g
. We now write
\alpha=\frac{a}{g\log q}
. Then, as
y=(q^{2g})^{\theta}
, the above can be rewritten as

\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{a}{g\log q};P\right)=P(1)\cosh{a}+P^{\prime}(1)\frac{\sinh{a}}{2a\theta}+O(g^{-1}).

(3.23)

For an even polynomial
Q
, we have that

Q\left(\frac{d}{da}\right)\left.\cosh{a}\right|_{a=0}=Q(1),

(3.24)

and

Q\left(\frac{d}{da}\right)\left.\frac{\sinh{a}}{a}\right|_{a=0}=Q\left(\frac{d}{da}\right)\left.\int_{0}^{1}\cosh{at}\,dt\right|_{a=0}=\int_{0}^{1}Q(t)\,dt.

(3.25)

Thus, we have

Q\left(\frac{d}{da}\right)\left.\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{a}{g\log q};P\right)\right|_{a=0}=P(1)Q(1)+\frac{1}{2\theta}P^{\prime}(1)\int_{0}^{1}Q(t)\,dt+O(g^{-1}).

(3.26)

By noting that
\frac{d}{da}=\frac{1}{g\log q}\frac{d}{d\alpha}
, we see that this is precisely the statement of Theorem
2.4
.

4.
The mollified second moment

In this section we will prove Theorem
2.5
on the mollified second moment. Similarly to the first moment, we will first obtain a formula for the shifted moment

\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta;P_{1},P_{2}):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\beta,\chi_{D})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P_{1})M_{y}(\chi_{D},P_{2}).

(4.1)

Lemma 4.1
.

For polynomials
P_{1},P_{2}
satisfying
P_{j}(0)=P_{j}^{\prime}(0)=0
for
j=1,2
, and for
\theta<1/2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta;P_{1},P_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta(1-q^{-2g(\alpha+\beta)})}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{\alpha\beta(q^{-2g\alpha}-q^{-2g\beta})}{\alpha-\beta}\right)\log y\int_{0}^{1}P_{1}(r)P_{2}(r)dr

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}(1+q^{-2g\alpha})(1+q^{-2g\beta})\int_{0}^{1}\big(P_{1}(r)P_{2}^{\prime}(r)+P_{1}^{\prime}(r)P_{2}(r)\big)dr

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{(1+q^{-2g\alpha})(1-q^{-2g\beta})}{\beta}+\frac{(1-q^{-2g\alpha})(1+q^{-2g\beta})}{\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{0}^{1}P_{1}^{\prime}(r)P_{2}^{\prime}(r)dr

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1-q^{-2g(\alpha+\beta)}}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{q^{-2g\beta}-q^{-2g\alpha}}{\alpha-\beta}\right)\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{0}^{1}\big(P_{1}(r)P_{2}^{\prime\prime}(r)+P_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)P_{2}(r)\big)dr

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(1-q^{-2g\alpha})(1-q^{-2g\beta})}{4\alpha\beta}\frac{1}{\log^{2}y}\int_{0}^{1}\big(P_{1}^{\prime}(r)P_{2}^{\prime\prime}(r)+P_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)P_{2}^{\prime}(r)\big)dr

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4\alpha\beta}\left(\frac{1-q^{-2g(\alpha+\beta)}}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{q^{-2g\alpha}-q^{-2g\beta}}{\alpha-\beta}\right)\frac{1}{\log^{3}y}\int_{0}^{1}P_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)P_{2}^{\prime\prime}(r)dr+O(g^{-1})

(4.2)

uniformly for
\alpha,\beta\ll 1/g
. Assuming Conjecture
2.1
, the result holds for all
\theta>0
.

Proof.

We write
P_{1}(x)=\sum_{m\geq 2}p_{1,m}x^{m}
and
P_{2}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 2}p_{2,n}x^{n}
and use (
3.5
) which gives us

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta;P_{1},P_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\geq 2}\frac{p_{1,m}m!p_{2,n}n!}{\log^{m+n}y}\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\int_{(c)}\int_{(c)}\frac{y^{w+z}}{w^{m+1}z^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\frac{L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha,\chi_{D})L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\beta,\chi_{D})}{L(\tfrac{1}{2}+w,\chi_{D})L(\tfrac{1}{2}+z,\chi_{D})}\,dw\,dz,

(4.3)

where
c>0
. For
\alpha,\beta\ll 1/g
and
g^{-1/4+\varepsilon}\ll c<1/2
, Proposition
2.3
gives us that the mean value of the ratio of
L
-functions in the integrand is given by Conjecture
2.1
with an error that is
\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{-2cg+\varepsilon cg}
. By bounding the integral with absolute values, the contribution of this error to (
4
) will be

\ll_{\varepsilon}y^{2c}q^{-2cg+\varepsilon cg}\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{2cg(2\theta-1+\varepsilon/2)}.

(4.4)

For
\theta<1/2
, we may bound this error by
O(q^{-\varepsilon g})
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. Assuming Conjecture
2.1
, the error in the ratio of
L
-functions is
\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{-\varepsilon g}
and we may take
c\asymp 1/g
. This allows us to bound the error in (
4
) by
O(q^{-\varepsilon g})
for any
\theta>0
. In either case, by inserting the main terms of the Ratios Conjecture into (
4
), we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta;P_{1},P_{2})

\displaystyle=I(\alpha,\beta)+q^{-2g\alpha}I(-\alpha,\beta)+q^{-2g\beta}I(\alpha,-\beta)+q^{-2g(\alpha+\beta)}I(-\alpha,-\beta)+O(q^{-\varepsilon g}),

(4.5)

where

\displaystyle I(\alpha,\beta)=\zeta_{q}(1+2\alpha)\zeta_{q}(1+2\beta)\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+\beta)\sum_{m,n\geq 2}\frac{p_{1,m}m!p_{2,n}n!}{(\log y)^{m+n}}J_{\alpha,\beta}(y),

(4.6)

with

\displaystyle J_{\alpha,\beta}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\int_{(c)}\int_{(c)}\frac{y^{w+z}}{w^{m+1}z^{n+1}}\frac{\zeta_{q}(1+w+z)A(\alpha,\beta;w,z)}{\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+w)\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+z)\zeta_{q}(1+\beta+w)\zeta_{q}(1+\beta+z)}\,dw\,dz.

(4.7)

To evaluate the contour integral
J_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
, we would like to move the contours to the left of 0 but the factor of
\zeta_{q}(1+w+z)
with its pole at
w+z=0
makes this somewhat tricky. To deal with this, we use the fact that for
\textup{Re}(w+z)>0
, we may write

\frac{y^{w+z}}{(w+z)}=\int_{0}^{y}u^{w+z}\frac{du}{u}.

(4.8)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle J_{\alpha,\beta}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\int_{1}^{y}\int_{(c)}\int_{(c)}\frac{u^{w+z}}{w^{m+1}z^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{(w+z)\zeta_{q}(1+w+z)A(\alpha,\beta;w,z)}{\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+w)\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+z)\zeta_{q}(1+\beta+w)\zeta_{q}(1+\beta+z)}\,dw\,dz\frac{du}{u},

(4.9)

with the integration in
u
only over
1\leq u\leq y
since if
u<1
, we can move the contours far to the right to see that the integrals in
w
and
z
vanish. As
(w+z)\zeta_{q}(1+w+z)
is analytic at
w+z=0
, the poles of the integrand are now at
w=0
or
z=0
only. So, by moving the contours to
\textup{Re}(w)=\textup{Re}(z)=-\delta
where
\delta>0
is sufficiently small to ensure that the Euler product is absolutely convergent, we get that
J_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
is given by the residue at
w=z=0
plus the new integral over the lines
\textup{Re}(w)=\textup{Re}(z)=-\delta
whose contribution may be bounded by

\int_{1}^{y}u^{-2\delta}\frac{du}{u}\ll 1.

(4.10)

We express the residue at
w=z=0
as an integral with the contours being circles of radius
\asymp 1/g
around the zero. Then, for
w,z,\alpha,\beta\ll 1/g
, we approximate the integrand on these contours via the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\frac{(w+z)\zeta_{q}(1+w+z)A(\alpha,\beta;w,z)}{\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+w)\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+z)\zeta_{q}(1+\beta+w)\zeta_{q}(1+\beta+z)}

\displaystyle\qquad=(\alpha+w)(\alpha+z)(\beta+w)(\beta+z)A(0,0;0,0)\log^{3}q+O(g^{-5}).

(4.11)

Since
A(0,0;0,0)=1
as can be seen by the definition, this gives us

\displaystyle J_{\alpha,\beta}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log^{3}q}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\int_{1}^{y}\oint\oint\frac{u^{w+z}}{w^{m+1}z^{n+1}}\left((\alpha+w)(\alpha+z)(\beta+w)(\beta+z)+O(g^{-5})\right)\,dw\,dz\frac{du}{u}

By generalising (

) and (

and

Hence, by combining (

\displaystyle=\zeta_{q}(1+2\alpha)\zeta_{q}(1+2\beta)\zeta_{q}(1+\alpha+\beta)\log^{3}q

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg[\int_{1}^{y}\left(\alpha\beta P_{1}\left(\frac{\log u}{\log y}\right)+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\log y}P_{1}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\log u}{\log y}\right)+\frac{1}{\log^{2}y}P_{1}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\log u}{\log y}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\alpha\beta P_{2}\left(\frac{\log u}{\log y}\right)+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\log y}P_{2}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\log u}{\log y}\right)+\frac{1}{\log^{2}y}P_{2}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\log u}{\log y}\right)\right)\frac{du}{u}+O(g^{-4})\Bigg].

By making the change of variables

, the integral in the above becomes

\displaystyle\log y\int_{0}^{1}\left(\alpha\beta P_{1}(r)+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\log y}P_{1}^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{\log^{2}y}P_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)\right)\left(\alpha\beta P_{2}(r)+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\log y}P_{2}^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{\log^{2}y}P_{2}^{\prime\prime}(r)\right)dr.

(4.15)

Now, for

(4.16)

Hence, we have that

\displaystyle I(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log y}{4\alpha\beta(\alpha+\beta)}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\alpha\beta P_{1}(r)+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\log y}P_{1}^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{\log^{2}y}P_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)\right)

(4.17)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{(1+q^{-2g\alpha})(1-q^{-2g\beta})}{\beta}+\frac{(1-q^{-2g\alpha})(1+q^{-2g\beta})}{\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{0}^{1}P_{1}^{\prime}(r)P_{2}^{\prime}(r)dr

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1-q^{-2g(\alpha+\beta)}}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{q^{-2g\beta}-q^{-2g\alpha}}{\alpha-\beta}\right)\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{0}^{1}\big(P_{1}(r)P_{2}^{\prime\prime}(r)+P_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)P_{2}(r)\big)dr

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4\alpha\beta}\left(\frac{1-q^{-2g(\alpha+\beta)}}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{q^{-2g\alpha}-q^{-2g\beta}}{\alpha-\beta}\right)\frac{1}{\log^{3}y}\int_{0}^{1}P_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)P_{2}^{\prime\prime}(r)dr+O(g^{-1}).

(4.18)

Finally, since both

which completes the proof of the lemma.

\displaystyle\frac{1+Y^{2}}{16}

\displaystyle\Big(2(2\theta)^{-1}I(\tilde{Q}^{2})(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}-4\theta I(\tilde{Q}Q^{\prime})\big((\alpha+i\beta)^{2}+(\alpha-i\beta)^{2}\big)+16\theta^{3}I(Q^{\prime 2})\Big)\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\alpha x}\,dx.

(5.20)

Also as
(\alpha\pm i\beta)^{2}
are the roots of the polynomial (
5.7
), we have that

4\theta I(\tilde{Q}Q^{\prime})(\alpha\pm i\beta)^{2}=\frac{1}{2\theta}I(\tilde{Q}^{2})(\alpha\pm i\beta)^{4}+8\theta^{3}I(Q^{\prime 2}),

(5.21)

and substituting this into (
5.20
) gives us

\frac{1+Y^{2}}{16}\frac{I(\tilde{Q}^{2})}{2\theta}\left(2(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}-(\alpha+i\beta)^{4}-(\alpha-i\beta)^{4}\right)\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\alpha x}\,dx.

(5.22)

Evaluating the integral and then simplifying this expression then yields

\frac{e^{2\alpha}}{2\alpha}\frac{1+Y^{2}}{2\theta}I(\tilde{Q}^{2})(\alpha\beta)^{2}\left(1+O(e^{-2\alpha})\right).

(5.23)

Lastly, using
\alpha=2\theta(2k+1)+O(k^{-1})
,
\beta=\theta+O(k^{-1}
),
1+Y^{2}=(\cos\beta)^{-2}+O(k^{-1})
and plugging in

I(\tilde{Q}^{2})=\frac{1}{(4k+3)(2k+1)^{2}},

(5.24)

gives us the final expression

\frac{e^{2\alpha}\theta^{2}}{2\cos^{2}\beta}\frac{1}{(4k+3)(2k+1)}\left(1+O(k^{-1})\right)=\frac{e^{2\alpha}\theta^{2}}{16k^{2}\cos^{2}\beta}\left(1+O(k^{-1})\right)

(5.25)

for the numerator of
\mathcal{R}(P,x^{2k})
. Recalling that the denominator of
\mathcal{R}(P,x^{2k})
is given by the square of (
5
), we therefore have that

\mathcal{R}(P,x^{2k})=\frac{1}{64k^{2}}\left(1+O(k^{-1})\right),

(5.26)

as required.
∎

The proof of Theorem
2.9
is completed by recalling the statement of Theorem
2.7
with

and applying the result of Lemma
5.1
.
