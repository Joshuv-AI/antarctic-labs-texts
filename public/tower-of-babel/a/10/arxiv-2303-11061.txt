Convergence in distribution of the Bernstein-Durrmeyer kernel and pointwise convergence of a generalised operator for functions of bounded variation
Mohammed Taariq Mowzer

Abstract
We study the convergence of Bernstein type operators leading to two results. The first: The kernel K n K_{n} of the Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator at each point x ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) x\in(0,1) — that is K n ​ ( x , t ) ​ d ​ t K_{n}(x,t)dt — once standardised converges to the normal distribution. The second result computes the pointwise limit of a generalised Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator applied to — possibly discontinuous — functions f f of bounded variation.

Introduction

Bernstein operators
B_{n}(f)(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{n,k}(x)f(k/n)
, where
p_{n,k}(t)=\binom{n}{k}t^{k}(1-t)^{n-k}
, were introduced by S.Bernstein
[
4
]
to give a constructive proof of the Weierstrass approximation theorem. The Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator below was constructed by Durrmeyer
[
7
]
and independently by Lupaş
[
10
]
:

Definition 1.1
.

The Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator
M_{n}
applied to an integrable function
f
on
[0,1]
is given by

M_{n}(f)(x)=\int_{0}^{1}f(t)K_{n}(x,t)\,dt,\quad x\in[0,1],

where
K_{n}
is a Bernstein-Durrmeyer kernel given by

K_{n}(x,t)=(n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{n,k}(x)p_{n,k}(t),\quad x,t\in[0,1].

In his original proof, Bernstein used the binomial distribution and the weak law of large numbers to constructively prove the Weierstrass approximation theorem.

In this paper, we use the fact that the binomial distribution converges to the normal distribution as the number of trials increases to show that the kernel of the Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator tends to the normal distribution. To be precise:

Theorem 1.2
.

For
x\in(0,1)
fixed

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}K_{n}\left(x,\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}+x\right)dt\text{ converges in distribution to }N(0,2x(1-x))\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

where
N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
is the normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Equivalently, if
X_{n}
is a random variable with distribution
K_{n}(x,t)dt
then

\sqrt{n}(X_{n}-x)\text{ converges in distribution to }N(0,2x(1-x))\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

The Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator can be generalised to arbitrary measure. Here, we limit ourselves to arbitrary weights.

Definition 1.3
.

The Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator with non-negative weight
w
is given by

M_{n,w}(f)(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{n,k}(x)\frac{\int_{0}^{1}f(t)p_{n,k}(t)w(t)\,dt}{\int_{0}^{1}p_{n,k}(t)w(t)\,dt},

(1)

where
w
is chosen so that the terms are well defined,
x\in[0,1]
, and
f
is a function on
[0,1]
integrable with respect to
w(t)dt
.

In the particular case when
w
is a Jacobi weight
w(t)=t^{\alpha}(1-t)^{\beta}
for
\alpha,\beta>-1
, the operator
M_{n,w}
is well known. For example, Berens and Xu (e.g.
[
3
]
) investigated the Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator with Jacobi weights, and Ditzian (e.g.
[
6
]
) studied the multidimensional Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator with Jacobi weights. The case of arbitrary measure — replace
w(t)dt
with
d\rho(t)
in (
1
) — and its multivariate analogue was, to our knowledge, for the first time studied by Berdysheva and Jetter
[
2
]
. Berdysheva
[
1
]
showed that the Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator with arbitrary measure when applied to
f
at
x
converges to
f(x)
if
f
is essentially bounded, continuous at
x
and the support of
\rho
contains
x
.

Herzog and Hill proved in
[
9
]
that for
f
defined on
\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
,
f
bounded and having left and right limits
f(x-)
and
f(x+)
at a given
x\in(0,1)
, for the classical Bernstein operator

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}B_{n}(f)(x)=\frac{1}{2}f(x-)+\frac{1}{2}f(x+).

Guo
[
8
]
showed when
f
is of bounded variation that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}M_{n}(f)(x)=\frac{1}{2}f(x-)+\frac{1}{2}f(x+)

(2)

holds for the Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator and gave an estimate of the rate of convergence.

In this paper we find the limit of the Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator with arbitrary weights when applied to a function
f
of bounded variation. To be precise, we show:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
w
be a non-negative function of bounded variation on
[0,1]
. Let
x\in(0,1)
be fixed. Assume that either the left limit
w(x-)
or the right limit
w(x+)
at
x
is non-zero. For any function
f
of bounded variation on
[0,1]
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}M_{n,w}(f)(x)=(1-\nu)f(x-)+\nu f(x+),

(3)

where

\nu=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w(x+)(1-u)}{w(x-)u+w(x+)(1-u)}\,du.

When
w(x-)=w(x+)
,
\nu
evaluates to
\frac{1}{2}
. Otherwise,

\nu=\frac{r^{2}-r[1+\ln r]}{(r-1)^{2}},\quad\text{for }r=\frac{w(x+)}{w(x-)}\text{ when }r\in(0,\infty)\text{ and }r\neq 1.

Theorem
1.4
is an extension of (
2
) — consider
w=1
. In fact (
2
) holds even if only
w(x+)=w(x-)
, that is,
w
continuous at
x
.

Prerequisites and useful results

Henceforth, we denote the binomial distribution with
n
trials and probability
p
of success by
Bin(n,p)
, the normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
by
N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, and the beta distribution with
\alpha
and
\beta
parameters by
Beta(\alpha,\beta)
. The symbol
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}
denotes convergence in distribution and
\sim
denotes having the same distribution. We denote the probability measure by
\mathbb{P}
and expectation of a random variable by
\mathbb{E}
.

The function
p_{n,k}
is extended onto
\mathbb{R}
, and
k
is allowed on
[0,n]
so that

p_{n,k}(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(n-k+1)}t^{k}(1-t)^{n-k}&\text{ if }t\in[0,1],\\
0&\text{ if }t\not\in[0,1].\end{cases}

For a function
f
,
f(t-)
and
f(t+)
denote the left and right limit of
f
at
t
, and
\mathcal{D}(f)
is the set of discontinuities of
f
. Usually
x
will be fixed in
(0,1)
. It is safe to define
\Psi
as the cumulative distribution function (cdf) of
N(0,x(1-x))
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
C_{n}
have a
Bin(n,p)
distribution for fixed
p\in(0,1)
. Then

\sqrt{n}(C_{n}/n-p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,p(1-p)),\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}
have distribution
Beta(r_{1},r_{2})
. Then

\sqrt{r_{1}+r_{2}}\left(\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}-\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}+r_{2}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\gamma(1-\gamma)),

(4)

as
r_{1},r_{2}\rightarrow\infty
and
\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}+r_{2}}\rightarrow\gamma
for
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Theorem
2.1
is a direct consequence of the central limit theorem and is well known. Theorem
2.2
is a generalisation of the well known approximation that the standardised
Beta(an,bn)
distribution tends to the normal distribution as
n\rightarrow\infty
. As far as we know, Theorem
2.2
is not well known. We supply a proof in the appendix.

The Corollary below will be the most useful form of Theorem
2.2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose
B_{n,k}
has a
Beta(k+1,n-k+1)
distribution, and let
x\in(0,1)
be fixed. Then

\sqrt{n}\left(B_{n,k}-x\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(\alpha,x(1-x))

(5)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
and
\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)\rightarrow\alpha
.

Proof.

Notice
\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)\rightarrow\alpha
implies
\frac{k}{n}\rightarrow x
. By Theorem
2.2
,

{\overbrace{\frac{\sqrt{n+2}}{\sqrt{n}}}^{M}}\left[\sqrt{n}\left(B_{n,k}-\frac{k}{n}\right)\right]+{\overbrace{\sqrt{n+2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-\frac{k+1}{n+2}\right)}^{C}}=\sqrt{n+2}\left(B_{n,k}-\frac{k+1}{n+2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,x(1-x))

as
n\rightarrow\infty
and
\frac{k}{n}\rightarrow x
. Notice
M\rightarrow 1
and
C\rightarrow 0
in the limit. Therefore, by Slutsky’s Theorem,
\sqrt{n}\left(B_{n,k}-\frac{k}{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,x(1-x))
. Another application of Slutsky’s Theorem gives

\sqrt{n}(B_{n,k}-x)=\sqrt{n}\left(B_{n,k}-\frac{k}{n}\right)+\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(\alpha,x(1-x))

as
n\rightarrow\infty
and
\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)\rightarrow\alpha
.
∎

The following gives a straightforward way to show uniform convergence on compact intervals using sequences.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
11
, Chapter 3]
.
Let
f
and
(f_{n})
be functions defined on a compact subset
K
of
\mathbb{R}
. The following are equivalent

i)

(f_{n})
converges uniformly to
f
, and
f
is continuous on
K
.

ii)

For any sequence
(\alpha_{n})\subset K
, if
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}=\alpha
then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}(\alpha_{n})=f(\alpha)
.

Proof.

See for example
[
11
, Chapter 3]
section on continuous convergence.
∎

The next Theorem allows us to use convergence in distribution to show convergence in expectation.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of real-valued random variables which converge to the random variable
X
in distribution. Let
(h_{n})
be a sequence of uniformly bounded measurable functions from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
which converge uniformly on compact intervals to a measurable function
h
whose set of discontinuities
\mathcal{D}(h)
is closed and satisfies
\mathbb{P}(X\not\in\mathcal{D}(h))=1
. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(X_{n}))=\mathbb{E}(h(X)).

Proof.

By Skorokhod’s representation theorem, see for example
[
5
, Chapter 3.5]
, there exists a sequence of random variables
(Y_{n})
such that each
Y_{n}
has the same distribution as
X_{n}
, and
(Y_{n})
converges almost surely to a random variable
Y
having the same distribution as
X
. Let
\Omega_{0}
be the almost sure set where
(Y_{n})
converges pointwise to
Y
and let
\Omega_{1}
be the almost sure set
\{Y\not\in\mathcal{D}(h)\}
. For
\omega
in the almost sure set
\Omega_{0}\cap\Omega_{1}
,
Y(\omega)
is in the open set
\mathcal{D}(h)^{c}
. Hence,
h
is continuous on a compact neighbourhood of
Y(\omega)
. By Lemma
2.4
, uniform convergence of
h_{n}
to
h
on the compact neighbourhood yields
h_{n}(Y_{n}(\omega))\rightarrow h(Y(\omega))
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore,
h_{n}(Y_{n})\rightarrow h(Y)
almost surely. Since
(h_{n})
is uniformly bounded, the sequence
(h_{n}(Y_{n}))
is dominated by some constant. By Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(X_{n}))=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(Y_{n}))=\mathbb{E}(h(Y))=\mathbb{E}(h(X)).

∎

3
Normality of Bernstein-Durrmeyer kernel

For the rest of this section
x\in(0,1)
is fixed. We construct a probabilistic representation of the Bernstein-Durrmeyer kernel
K_{n}(x,\cdot)
. Consider the distribution generated from the samples of the following process.

Step 1.

Obtain a coin with probability
x
of obtaining heads.

Step 2.

Flip the coin
n
times recording the number of heads.

Step 3.

If
k
heads were recorded, the sample is randomly drawn from a
Beta(k+1,n-k+1)
distribution.

Suppose
C_{n}\sim Bin(n,x)
and
B_{n,k}~\sim~Beta(k~+~1,~n~-~k~+~1)
form an independency of random variables. The random variable

X_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{\{C_{n}=k\}}B_{n,k}

describes samples from the above process. Here,
\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{F}
is the indicator function on a set
F
that is

\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{F}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }x\in F,\\
0&\text{ if }x\not\in F.\end{cases}

We show
X_{n}
has distribution
K_{n}(x,\cdot)dt
. First, we note the probability density function (pdf) of
B_{n,k}
is

f_{B_{n,k}}(t)=\frac{\Gamma(n+2)}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(n-k+1)}t^{k}(1-t)^{n-k}=(n+1)p_{n,k}(t)\quad\text{for }t\in[0,1]

and the probability mass function (pmf) of
C_{n}
is

p_{C_{n}}(k)=\binom{n}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{n-k}=p_{n,k}(x)\quad\text{ for }k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}.

Now, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\leq z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\leq z\,|\,C_{n}=k)]

(6)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(B_{n,k}\leq z\,|\,C_{n}=k)]

(7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{n,k}(x)\mathbb{P}(B_{n,k}\leq z)

(8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{n,k}(x)\int_{0}^{z}(n+1)p_{n,k}(t)\,dt

(9)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}K_{n}(x,t)\,dt.

(10)

Independence of
C_{n}
and
B_{n,k}
is used in the third equality. Hence,
X_{n}
has distribution
K_{n}(x,t)dt
.

Now, the transformation formula says that the distribution of
\sqrt{n}(X_{n}-x)
is

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}K_{n}\left(x,\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}+x\right)dt.

Therefore we can prove Theorem
1.2
by showing the equivalent statement:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
x\in(0,1)
be fixed. Suppose
X_{n}
is defined as above. Then

\sqrt{n}(X_{n}-x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,2x(1-x)).

Both the binomial and beta distribution, when standardised, converge to the normal distribution. It makes sense that
X_{n}
— which is a mixture of a binomial distribution with beta distributions — when standardised, also converges to the normal distribution. Indeed, Corollary
2.3
says
\sqrt{n}(B_{n,k}-x)
is approximately
N(\alpha,x(1-x))
where
\alpha=\sqrt{n}\left(k/n-x\right)
for large
n
and
k
. In the random process
X_{n}
,
k
equals
C_{n}\sim Bin(nx,nx(1-x))
. Now
\alpha
is the random variable
C^{*}_{n}=\sqrt{n}\left(C_{n}/n-x\right)
which is approximately
N(0,x(1-x))
for large
n
. So, intuitively, for large enough
n

\sqrt{n}(X_{n}-x)\sim N(\alpha,x(1-x))=\alpha+N(0,x(1-x))=N(0,x(1-x))+N(0,x(1-x))=N(0,2x(1-x)).

Most of the challenge of the proof comes from the fact that as
|\alpha|
increases much larger values of
n
are required for the approximation
\sqrt{n}(B_{n,k}-x)\sim N(\alpha,x(1-x))
to hold. However, it turns out that large
|\alpha|
is improbable since
\alpha
is approximately
N(0,x(1-x))
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed. Let
C_{n}^{*}=\sqrt{n}(C_{n}/n-x)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}(X_{n}-x)\leq z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}(B_{n,k}-x)\leq z\,|\,C_{n}=k)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}(B_{n,xn+\alpha\sqrt{n}}-x)\leq z\,|\,\sqrt{n}(C_{n}/n-x)=\alpha)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}(B_{n,xn+\alpha\sqrt{n}}-x)\leq z\,|\,C_{n}^{*}=\alpha)]=\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(C_{n}^{*})),

(11)

where the last equality follows from the independence of
C_{n}
and
B_{n,k}
, and

h_{n}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}(B_{n,xn+\alpha\sqrt{n}}-x)\leq z)&\text{ if }xn+\alpha\sqrt{n}\in[0,n],\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
K
be any non-empty compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Let
(\alpha_{n})\subset K
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}=\alpha
. Then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{xn+\alpha_{n}\sqrt{n}}{n}-x\right)=\alpha
. Corollary
2.3
then gives
\sqrt{n}(B_{n,xn+\alpha_{n}\sqrt{n}}-x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(\alpha,x(1-x))
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Let
A
be a random variable with distribution
N(\alpha,x(1-x))
and recall
\Psi
is the cdf of
N(0,x(1-x))
. Convergence in distribution yields,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}h_{n}(\alpha_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}(B_{n,xn+\alpha_{n}\sqrt{n}}-x)\leq z)=\mathbb{P}(A\leq z)=\Psi(z-\alpha).

(12)

Lemma
2.4
gives that
h_{n}
converges uniformly to
\Psi(\cdot-\alpha)
on
K
. Since
K
was arbitrary, uniform convergence holds on all compact intervals.

Let
U
and
V
be independent random variables with distribution
N(0,x(1-x)).
By Theorem
2.1
,
C_{n}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}V
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Equations (
11
), (
12
) and Theorem
2.5
give

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}(X_{n}-x)\leq z)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(C_{n}^{*}))=\mathbb{E}(\Psi(z-V))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}(U\leq z-V\,|\,V))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(U\leq z-V)=\mathbb{P}(U+V\leq z).

Since
U
and
V
are independent, the sum
U+V
has distribution
N(0,2x(1-x))
. Hence, the cdf of
\sqrt{n}(X_{n}-x)
converges pointwise to the cdf of
N(0,2x(1-x))
.
∎

4
Limit of the generalised Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator

We introduce the Lupaş Beta operator
[
10
]
for integrable functions
f
on
[0,1]

L_{n}(f)(y)=\int_{0}^{1}f(\theta)\frac{\theta^{ny}(1-\theta)^{n(1-y)}}{\mathcal{B}(ny+1,n(1-y)+1)}\,d\theta,

where
\mathcal{B}
is the Beta function. Notice that the kernel of the Lupaş Beta operator is the
Beta(ny+1,n(1-y)+1)
distribution. Specifically, for
B_{n,k}\sim Beta(k+1,n-k+1)
,
0\leq k\leq n
, we have that

L_{n}(f)(k/n)=\mathbb{E}(f(B_{n,k})).

(13)

For the rest of this section
x\in(0,1)
is fixed. We can express the generalised Bernstein-Durrmeyer operator as

M_{n,w}(f)(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{n,k}(x)\frac{\int_{0}^{1}f(t)p_{n,k}(t)w(t)\,dt}{\int_{0}^{1}p_{n,k}(t)w(t)\,dt}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{n,k}(x)\frac{L_{n}(fw)(k/n)}{L_{n}(w)(k/n)}.

Using (
13
), the Lupaş Beta operators can be represented as an expected value of a beta distribution. The summation can be represented as the expected value of a binomial distribution. Using these probabilistic representations we can decompose the proof of Theorem
1.4
into first evaluating the Lupaş Beta operator, then evaluating the binomial part. In both cases, Theorem
2.5
will be used to evaluate the limit.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be of bounded variation. Let
k_{n}(\alpha)=xn+\alpha\sqrt{n}
. The limit

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}L_{n}(f)(k_{n}(\alpha)/n)=\Psi(-\alpha)f(x-)+[1-\Psi(-\alpha)]f(x+),

(14)

holds uniformly in
\alpha
on all compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We look to apply Lemma
2.4
. Let
K
be a non-empty compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Let
(\alpha_{n})\subset K
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}=\alpha
. Clearly,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{k_{n}(\alpha_{n})}{n}-x\right)=\alpha
. Hence, Corollary
2.3
yields

B_{n,k_{n}(\alpha_{n})}^{*}:=\sqrt{n}\left(B_{n,k_{n}(\alpha_{n})}-x\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\rightarrow}}N(\alpha,x(1-x))\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

(15)

Using (
13
), we represent the Lupaş Beta operator probablistically and get that

L_{n}(f)(k_{n}(\alpha_{n})/n)=\mathbb{E}(f(B_{n,k_{n}(\alpha_{n})}))=\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(B_{n,k_{n}(\alpha_{n})}^{*})),

(16)

where

h_{n}(t)=f\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}+x\right)\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{[0,1]}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}+x\right)=f\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}+x\right)\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{[-x\sqrt{n},(1-x)\sqrt{n}]}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

From the decomposition

\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{[-x\sqrt{n},(1-x)\sqrt{n}]}=\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{[-x\sqrt{n},0)}+\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{\{0\}}+\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{(0,(1-x)\sqrt{n}]},

we notice
h_{n}
limits uniformly to
h=f(x-)\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{(-\infty,0)}+f(x)\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{\{0\}}+f(x+)\mbox{\large$\chi$}_{(0,\infty)}
on
K
. Since
f
is of bounded variation,
(h_{n})
is uniformly bounded. Let
A
be a random variable with distribution
N(\alpha,x(1-x))
. The set of discontinuities
\mathcal{D}(h)\subset\{0\}
is closed, and is a null set of the distribution of
A
. Hence, equations (
16
), (
15
) and Theorem
2.5
give

L_{n}(f)(k_{n}(\alpha_{n})/n)=\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(B_{n,k_{n}(\alpha_{n})}^{*}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{E}(h(A))=\Psi(-\alpha)f(x-)+[1-\Psi(-\alpha)]f(x+).

(17)

If we consider the sequence of functions
\left(L_{n}(f)(k_{n}(\cdot)/n)\right)_{n}
and the converging sequence
(\alpha_{n})
, an application of Lemma
2.4
on (
17
) gives that convergence of (
14
) is uniform in
\alpha
on
K
. Since
K
was arbitrary, (
14
) holds uniformly in
\alpha
on all compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Notice

M_{n,w}(f)(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{n,k}(x)c_{n}(k)=\mathbb{E}(c_{n}(C_{n})),

(18)

where
C_{n}\sim Bin(n,x)
and

c_{n}(k)=\frac{L_{n}(fw)(k/n)}{L_{n}(w)(k/n)}\text{ if }k\in[0,n],\text{ otherwise }c_{n}(k)=0.

We transform
