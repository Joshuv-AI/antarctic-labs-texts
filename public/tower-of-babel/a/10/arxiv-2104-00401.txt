Non-vanishing of theta components of Jacobi forms with level and an application
Pramath Anamby

Abstract
. We prove that a non–zero Jacobi form of arbitrary level N N and square–free index m 1 ​ m 2 m_{1}m_{2} with m 1 | N m_{1}|N and ( N , m 2 ) = 1 (N,m_{2})=1 has a non–zero theta component h μ h_{\mu} with either ( μ , 2 ​ m 1 ​ m 2 ) = 1 (\mu,2m_{1}m_{2})=1 or ( μ , 2 ​ m 1 ​ m 2 ) ∤ 2 ​ m 2 (\mu,2m_{1}m_{2})\nmid 2m_{2} . 1.
Introduction

The invariance of Jacobi forms with respect to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
gives us the decomposition of a Jacobi form into
theta components
, which is a collection of one variable holomorphic functions with automorphic properties under
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. These theta components act as a bridge between different type of modular forms. For example, through Fourier–Jacobi coefficients and the Eichler–Zagier map, they give an important map between the Siegel modular forms of degree
2
and the half integral weight modular forms. The theta decomposition also gives us the mappings from the space of Jacobi forms to the spaces of vector–valued modular forms and the half–integral weight modular forms. Moreover, the knowledge about the theta components is equivalent to knowing the Jacobi form itself. Thus it is important to understand the properties of these theta components.

One such interesting problem is the non–vanishing of theta components of a Jacobi form. In
[
13
]
, a non–vanishing result was proved for the theta components of Jacobi forms of full level and index
p
,
p^{2}
or
pq
, where
p
and
q
are primes. In particular, for such indices, by analysing the structure of square classes, it was shown that the Jacobi form is uniquely determined by one of the associated theta components. The study of these objects has many important arithmetic consequences. For example, in
[
3
]
, non–vanishing of theta components of Jacobi forms of full level and matrix index M with discriminant of M square-free, proved to be a crucial step in studying the fundamental Fourier coefficients of Siegel modular forms, which further had applications in establishing the functional equation of the spinor zeta function of degree 3.

In this article, we study the non–vanishing of theta components of Jacobi forms for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(N)
. For Jacobi cusp forms of even weight, square–free level
N
and index
p
, a prime with
(p,2N)=1
, it was proved by Martin in
[
9
]
that all the theta components are non–zero. Using the techniques from
[
3
]
and the properties of generalized quadratic Gauss sums, we prove the following non–vanishing result for the theta components of a Jacobi form of general level
N
and square–free index.

Theorem 1
.

Let
N
be an odd integer and
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
be square–free integers such that
m_{1}|N
and
(N,m_{2})=1
. For any non–zero
\phi\in J_{k,m_{1}m_{2}}(N)
there exists a
\mu\text{ mod }2m_{1}m_{2}
with either
(\mu,2m_{1}m_{2})=1
or
(\mu,2m_{1}m_{2})\nmid 2m_{2}
such that the theta component
h_{\mu}\neq 0
.

Theorem
1
is a generalization of the non–vanishing result of
[
3
]
for Jacobi forms with level in degree 1. It will be interesting to investigate this non–vanishing question for non square–free indices and also for higher degree Jacobi forms.

As an application, we prove the following result for the Siegel cusp forms of degree
2
in the Atkin–Lehner type newspace (see section
2.5
). For positive integers
k
and
N
, let
S_{k}^{2}(N)
denote the space of Siegel cusp forms of degree
2
, weight
k
and level
N
.
S_{k}^{2,new}(N)\subseteq S_{k}^{2}(N)
be the subspace of Atkin–Lehner type newforms (see section
2.5
). The Fourier coefficients
A(F,T)
of any
F\in S_{k}^{2}(N)
are supported on
\Lambda_{2}^{+}
, the set of
2\times 2
half–integral, symmetric, positive definite matrices.

Theorem 2
.

Let
k>2
and
N=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}...p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
be an odd integer. Suppose
F\in S_{k}^{2,new}(N)
is non–zero, then there exist infinitely many
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–inequivalent
T\in\Lambda_{2}^{+}
such that

(1)

A(F,T)\neq 0
.

(2)

4\mathrm{det}(T)
is of the form
\prod_{1}^{t}p_{j}^{r_{j}}n
where
n
is odd and square–free,
(n,N)=1
and
0\leq r_{j}\leq\alpha_{j}
.

In particular, when
N
is an odd, square–free integer we see that
F
is in fact determined by its fundamental Fourier coefficients. When
k
is even, this was known from
[
10
]
and Theorem
2
provides an alternative proof of this fact.

The proof of Theorem
2
uses the quite useful technique of reducing the question to half–integral weight cusp forms by using the Jacobi forms as an intermediate bridge. The passage to the case of Jacobi forms is aided by the Fourier–Jacobi expansion. To get to the desired half–integral case cusp form, instead of using the Eichler–Zagier map (which is defined in
[
8
]
), we directly use the theta components of the Jacobi forms as in
[
3
]
. Although this passage to half–integral weight forms is inspired by the calculations in
[
3
]
, we are in a somewhat more technically challenging situation due to the existence of level and thus requires a fresh set of calculations. The use of theta components instead of the Eichler–Zagier map (as in
[
10
]
) lets us prove the result for all weights
k>2
,
k
odd included.

By virtue of Corollary
3.1
, the problem reduces to half–integral weight cusp forms on the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{1}(4L)
for some
L\geq 1
. We then make use of the following result to finish the proof of Theorem
2
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\kappa\geq 5/2
be a half–integer and
L=\prod_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
. Suppose
f\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))
is non–zero and
a(f,n)=0
for all
(n,L_{f})>1
, for an even divisor
L_{f}
of
4L
. Then there exist infinitely many odd and square–free integers
n
such that

(1)

For
p_{i}|L
,
(n,p_{i})=1
.

(2)

a(f,p_{1}^{i_{1}}...p_{r}^{i_{r}}n)\neq 0
, where
(p_{j},L_{f})=1
and
0\leq i_{j}\leq\alpha_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq r
.

On our way to prove Theorem
3
, we prove certain auxiliary results (e.g., Lemma
4.1
) for half–integral forms on
\Gamma_{1}(4L)
. Even though these results are known for the group
\Gamma_{0}(4L)
from
[
12
]
, it seems such results are not readily available in the literature for the group
\Gamma_{1}(4L)
. So we give a brief account of them in section
4
.

2.
Notations and preliminaries

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we write
e(z):=e^{2\pi iz}
,
e^{m}(z):=e^{2\pi imz}
and
e_{m}(z):=e^{2\pi iz/m}
.

2.1.
Jacobi forms with level

In this article we encounter Jacobi forms with level. For more details about Jacobi forms for congruence subgroups, see
[
6
]
.

A holomorphic function
\phi:\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a Jacobi form of weight
k
, index
m
and level
N
if:

(1)

\phi\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\frac{z}{c\tau+d}\right)(c\tau+d)^{-k}e^{m}\left(\frac{-cz^{2}}{c\tau+d}\right)=\phi(\tau,z)
for all
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(N)
.

(2)

\phi(\tau,z+\lambda\tau+\mu)e^{m}(\lambda^{2}\tau+2\lambda z)=\phi(\tau,z)
for all
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

(3)

\phi
satisfies the boundedness condition at the
cusps
.

We denote by
J_{k,m}(N)
the space of Jacobi forms of weight
k
, index
m
and level
N
. As a consequence of condition (3) above, any
\phi\in J_{k,m}(N)
has a Fourier expansion of the form (at the cusp
i\infty
)

(2.1)

\phi(\tau,w)=\sum\nolimits_{4nm-r^{2}\geq 0}c(n,r)e(n\tau)e(rw).

A similar Fourier expansion holds at other cusps too. If the Fourier coefficients corresponding to
4nm-r^{2}=0
vanish at all the cusps, then we say that
\phi
is a Jacobi cusp form. We denote the space of Jacobi cusp forms by
J_{k,m}^{cusp}(N)
.

Like in the case of full level (
[
4
]
), any
\phi\in J_{k,m}(N)
admits a
theta expansion
:

(2.2)

\phi(\tau,w)=\sum\nolimits_{\mu\text{ mod }2m}h_{\mu}(\tau)\cdot\Theta_{\mu,m}(\tau,w),

where
\Theta_{\mu,m}
is the theta series given by

(2.3)

\Theta_{\mu,m}(\tau,w)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e\Big(\frac{(2mn-\mu)^{2}}{4m}\tau+(2mn-\mu)w\Big)

and
h_{\mu}
are modular forms of weight
k-1/2
for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma(4mN)
(see
[
3
]
for a proof) and have a Fourier expansion as below.

(2.4)

h_{\mu}(\tau)=\sum\nolimits_{4mn-\mu^{2}\geq 0}c(n,\mu)e\Big(\frac{4mn-\mu^{2}}{4m}\tau\Big).

As in
[
3
]
, we refer to the theta components corresponding to
(\mu,2mN)=1
as
primitive
theta components.

For
0\leq\mu<2m
,
\Theta_{\mu,m}
and
h_{\mu}
satisfy the following transformation properties (see
[
6
]
). Let
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, then

(2.5)

\Theta_{\mu,m}\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\frac{z}{c\tau+d}\right)(c\tau+d)^{-1/2}e\left(\frac{-mcz^{2}}{c\tau+d}\right)=\sum_{\nu\text{ mod }2m}\varepsilon_{m}(\nu,\mu;\gamma)\Theta_{\nu,m}(\tau,z).

Here we take the branch of square root having argument in
(-\pi/2,\pi/2]
.

From (
2.5
) and the transformation properties of
\phi
under
\Gamma_{0}(N)
(see conditions (1) and (2) above) we get,

(2.6)

(-N\tau+1)^{-k+1/2}h_{\mu}\Big(\frac{\tau}{-N\tau+1}\Big)=\sum_{\nu\text{ mod }2m}\varepsilon_{m}(\nu,\mu;\gamma)h_{\nu}(\tau),

where
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
N&1\end{pmatrix}
and
\varepsilon_{m}(\nu,\mu;\gamma)
is the generalized quadratic Gauss sum and is given by

(2.7)

\varepsilon_{m}(\nu,\mu;\gamma)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{\eta\text{ mod }2m}e_{4m}\left(N\eta^{2}+2\eta(\nu-\mu)\right).

For the sake of simplicity we write
\varepsilon_{m}(\nu,\mu;\gamma)=\varepsilon_{m}(\nu,\mu)
.

2.2.
Generalized quadratic Gauss sums

The generalized quadratic Gauss sum is defined as

(2.8)

G(a,b,c):=\sum\nolimits_{l\text{ mod }c}e_{c}(al^{2}+bl),\quad\text{ where }a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}.

G(a,b,c)
has the following properties (see
[
2
]
for more details):

(2.9)

G(a,b,c_{1}c_{2})=G(c_{2}a,b,c_{1})G(c_{1}a,b,c_{2}),\text{ when }(c_{1},c_{2})=1.

Moreover,
G(a,b,c)=0
if
(a,c)>1
and
(a,c)
does not divide
b
and when
(a,c)|b
, we have

(2.10)

G(a,b,c)=(a,c)G\left(a(a,c)^{-1},b(a,c)^{-1},c(a,c)^{-1}\right)

and for
(a,c)=1

(2.11)

G(a,b,c)=\begin{cases}\epsilon_{c}\sqrt{c}\big(\frac{a}{c}\big)e_{c}(-\psi(a)b^{2})&\mbox{ if }c\equiv 1\text{ mod }2,4a\psi(a)\equiv 1\text{ mod }c;\\
2G(2a,b,c/2)&\mbox{ if }c\equiv 2\text{ mod }4,b\equiv 1\text{ mod }2;\\
0&\mbox{ if }c\equiv 2\text{ mod }4,b=0;\\
(1+i)\epsilon_{a}^{-1}\sqrt{c}\big(\frac{a}{c}\big)&\mbox{ if }c\equiv 0\text{ mod }4,b=0;\\
0&\mbox{ if }c\equiv 0\text{ mod }4,b\equiv 1\text{ mod }2.\end{cases}

Here
\epsilon_{m}=1
or
i
depending on
m\equiv 1\text{ mod }4
or
3\text{ mod }4
respectively.

Splitting the congruence class
\text{ mod }4m
into two congruence classes
\text{ mod }2m
we see from (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) that
\varepsilon_{m}(\mu,\nu)=0
when
(N,4m)\nmid(\nu-\mu)
and when
(N,4m)|(\nu-\mu)
, we have

(2.12)

\varepsilon_{m}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{4m}G(N,2(\nu-\mu),4m)=\frac{(N,4m)}{4m}G\left(\frac{N}{(N,4m)},\frac{2(\nu-\mu)}{(N,4m)},\frac{4m}{(N,4m)}\right).

2.3.
Siegel modular forms

The Siegel’s upper half space of degree
2
is given by

(2.13)

\mathbb{H}_{2}=\{Z\in M(2,\mathbb{C})|Z=Z^{t},\mathrm{Im}(Z)>0\}.

The symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(2,\mathbb{R})
acts on
\mathbb{H}_{2}
by
Z\mapsto\gamma\langle Z\rangle=(AZ+B)(CZ+D)^{-1}
. For any integer
k
, the stroke operator on functions
F:\mathbb{H}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is defined as

(2.14)

(F|_{k}\gamma)(Z):=\text{ det}(CZ+D)^{-k}F(\gamma\langle Z\rangle).

For any positive integer
N
, the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}^{2}(N)\subset\mathrm{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is given by

(2.15)

\Gamma_{0}^{2}(N)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z})\;|\;C\equiv 0\text{ mod }N\right\}.

A Siegel modular form of degree
2
, weight
k
and level
N
is a holomorphic function
F:\mathbb{H}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying
F|_{k}\gamma=F
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}^{2}(N)
. Any such form admits a Fourier expansion of the form

(2.16)

F(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{2}}A(F,T)e(\mathrm{Tr}(TZ)),

where
\Lambda_{2}
is the set of
2\times 2
half–integral, symmetric, positive semi–definite matrices. Denote the subset of positive definite matrices by
\Lambda^{+}_{2}
. If
A(F,T)
non–zero for only
T\in\Lambda^{+}_{2}
we say that
F
is a cusp form. The space of Siegel modular forms of degree
2
, weight
k
and level
N
is denoted by
M_{k}^{2}(N)
and the subspace of cusp forms is denoted by
S_{k}^{2}(N)
.

For a
T=\begin{pmatrix}a&b/2\\
b/2&c\end{pmatrix}\in\Lambda_{2}
, define the content
c(T)
as below:

(2.17)

c(T)=\max\{d\in\mathbb{N}\;|\;d^{-1}T\text{ is half--integral}\}.

When
c(T)=1
, we say that
T
is
primitive
. If
-D=b^{2}-4ac(<0)
is a fundamental discriminant, then we say that
T
is
fundamental
and the corresponding Fourier coefficient
A(F,T)
is called a fundamental Fourier coefficient. The same analogy applies to primitive Fourier coefficients.

2.4.
Fourier–Jacobi expansion

For any
Z\in\mathbb{H}_{2}
consider the following decomposition:

(2.18)

Z=\begin{pmatrix}\tau&w\\
w&z\end{pmatrix};\text{ with }w\in\mathbb{C},\tau,z\in\mathbb{H}\text{ (the complex upper half plane)}.

Any
F\in M_{k}^{2}(N)
has a Fourier–Jacobi expansion with respect to the above decomposition as below.

(2.19)

F(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{m\geq 0}\phi_{m}(\tau,w)e(mz).

Here
\phi_{m}
are the Jacobi forms of weight
k
, index
m
and level
N
defined in section
2.1
. If
F
is a cusp form then
\phi_{m}
are also cusp forms.

For a
\phi\in J_{k,m}(N)
that appears in the Fourier–Jacobi expansion of a Siegel modular form
F
as in (
2.19
), the Fourier coefficients of
\phi
are given by the Fourier coefficients of
F
:

(2.20)

c_{\phi}(n,r)=A\left(F,\begin{pmatrix}n&r/2\\
r/2&m\end{pmatrix}\right).

2.5.
A newspace of Siegel cusp forms

In this article we deal with the Atkin-Lehner type space of newforms introduced in
[
5
]
. We give a brief description of the space below.

Let
N
be any positive integer and
d(>1)
,
M
be positive integers such that
dM|N
. Consider any
F\in S_{k}^{2}(M)
, then
F(dZ)\in S_{k}^{2}(N)
. Denote by
S_{k}^{2,old}(N)
the subspace of
S_{k}^{2}(N)
spanned by

(2.21)

\{F(dZ)|F\in S_{k}^{2}(M),dM|N\}.

The orthogonal complement of
S_{k}^{2,old}(N)
with respect to the Petersson inner product is denoted by
S_{k}^{2,new}(N)
and this is the subspace of
S_{k}^{2}(N)
that we refer to as the Atkin–Lehner type
newspace
in this article.

3.
Theta components of Jacobi forms with level

Let
N
be an odd integer and
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
be square–free integers such that
m_{1}|N
and
(N,m_{2})=1
. For any
\phi\in J_{k,m_{1}m_{2}}(N)
, let
h_{\mu}
denote the
theta
components of
\phi
(see section
2.1
). We also have that
h_{\mu}
is a modular form of weight
k-1/2
for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma(4m_{1}m_{2}N)
and the Fourier expansion of
h_{\mu}
in (
2.4
) can be rewritten as

(3.1)

h_{\mu}(\tau)=\sum\nolimits_{n}c_{\phi}\Big(\frac{n+\mu^{2}}{4m_{1}m_{2}},\mu\Big)q^{n/4m_{1}m_{2}},

where we follow the convention that
c_{\phi}\Big(\frac{n+\mu^{2}}{4m_{1}m_{2}},\mu\Big)=0
if
n\not\equiv-\mu^{2}\text{ mod }4m_{1}m_{2}
.

For any
\phi\in J_{k,m_{1}m_{2}}(N)
, suppose that
h_{\mu}=0
for all
(\mu,2m_{1}m_{2})=1
. Then from (
2.6
) we get that for
(\mu,2m_{1}m_{2})=1
:

(3.2)

\underset{(\nu,2m_{1}m_{2})>1}{\sum\nolimits_{\nu\text{ mod }2m_{1}m_{2}}}\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)h_{\nu}(\tau)=0.

First we evaluate the Gauss sums
\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)
. Since
(N,m_{2})=1
and
m_{1}|N
, we have that
(N,4m_{1}m_{2})=m_{1}
. Thus with
N=Mm_{1}
, we see from (
2.12
) that (for
m_{1}|(\nu-\mu)
)

(3.3)

\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)=\frac{1}{4m_{2}}G\left(M,\frac{2(\nu-\mu)}{m_{1}},4m_{2}\right).

Lemma 3.1
.

If
m_{1}|(\nu-\mu)
, then

(3.4)

\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m_{2}}}(1+i)\epsilon_{M}^{-1}\left(\frac{M}{4m_{2}}\right)e_{4m_{2}}\left(\frac{-\overline{M}(\nu-\mu)^{2}}{m_{1}^{2}}\right),

where
\overline{M}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
M\overline{M}\equiv 1\text{ mod }4m_{2}
.

When
m_{1}|(\nu-\mu)
, the Gauss sum on the RHS can be evaluated using (
2.11
) as shown below:

(3.5)

\begin{split}G(M,\tfrac{2(\nu-\mu)}{m_{1}},4m_{2})&=\sum_{\eta\text{ mod }4m_{2}}e_{4m_{2}}\Big(M\eta^{2}+\tfrac{2\eta(\nu-\mu)}{m_{1}}\Big)=e_{4m_{2}}\Big(\tfrac{\overline{M}(\nu-\mu)^{2}}{m_{1}^{2}}\Big)G(M,0,4m_{2})\\
&=(1+i)\epsilon_{M}^{-1}\sqrt{4m_{2}}\Big(\tfrac{M}{4m_{2}}\Big)e_{4m_{2}}\Big(\tfrac{-\overline{M}(\nu-\mu)^{2}}{m_{1}^{2}}\Big).\end{split}

Lemma 3.2
.

If
(\nu,m_{1})>1
, then
\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)=0
.

Proof.

First, note that the RHS of (
3.3
) is zero whenever
m_{1}\nmid(\nu-\mu)
(see section
2.2
). On the other hand, we also have that
(\mu,2m_{1}m_{2})=1
and
(\nu,2m_{1}m_{2})>1
. Now if
(\nu,m_{1})>1
, since
(\mu,m_{1})=1
, we have
m_{1}\nmid(\nu-\mu)
. Thus
\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)=0
in this case.
∎

Now coming back to (
3.2
), using the transformation
\tau\mapsto\tau+t
we get

(3.6)

\underset{(\nu,2m_{1}m_{2})>1}{\sum\nolimits_{\nu\text{ mod }2m_{1}m_{2}}}\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)e_{4m_{1}m_{2}}(-\nu^{2}t)h_{\nu}(\tau)=0.

Using the linear independence of pairwise different characters (see
[
3
, pp. 15]
for a detailed argument) we see that it is enough to consider
\nu
with
(\nu,2m_{1}m_{2})>1
in square classes. That is,
\nu^{2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{2}\mod 4m_{1}m_{2}
for fixed
\nu_{0}\mod 2m_{1}m_{2}
. Thus from (
2.7
) and Lemma
3.2
we get:

(3.7)

\underset{(\nu_{0},m_{1})=1}{\underset{\nu^{2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{2}\text{ mod }4m_{1}m_{2}}{\sum_{\nu\text{ mod }2m_{1}m_{2}}}}e_{4m_{2}}\Big(\frac{-\overline{M}(\nu-\mu)^{2}}{m_{1}^{2}}\Big)h_{\nu}(\tau)=0.

For
(\nu_{0},m_{1})=1
and any
l
with
(l,2m_{2})=1
, consider the matrix

(3.8)

M_{\nu_{0}}=\left(\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)\right)_{\nu,\mu},

where
\varepsilon_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\nu,\mu)=e_{4m_{2}}\left(\frac{l(\nu-\mu)^{2}}{m_{1}^{2}}\right)
when
m_{1}|(\nu-\mu)
and is zero otherwise,
(\mu,2m_{1}m_{2})=1
and
\nu
runs over the set

(3.9)

S_{\nu_{0}}=\{\nu\text{ mod }2m_{1}m_{2}:\nu^{2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{2}\text{ mod }4m_{1}m_{2}\}.

We prove that the only possible solution to (
3.7
) is the trivial solution (i.e.,
h_{\nu}=0
) by showing that the matrix
M_{\nu_{0}}
has maximal rank. When
N=1
, this reduces to the arguments presented in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(\nu_{0},m_{1})=1
. Then the matrix
M_{\nu_{0}}
has maximal rank.

Proof.

Let
m_{1}=p_{1}p_{2}...p_{r}
and
m_{2}=q_{1}q_{2}...q_{s}
. Write
t=r+s
. The set

(3.10)

\{\nu\text{ mod }2m_{1}m_{2}:\nu^{2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{2}\text{ mod }4m_{1}m_{2}\}

has the cardinality
2^{t^{\prime}}
, where
t^{\prime}
is the number of primes dividing
m_{1}m_{2}
but not
\nu_{0}
. But the additional condition
(\nu_{0},m_{1})=1
gives us that the cardinality of the above set is at least
2^{r}
. We prove by induction on
t
.

When
t=0
,
|S_{\nu_{0}}|=1
and the lemma is trivially true. Consider the case
t=1
. Then either
r=1
and
s=0
or
r=0
and
s=1
.

Case 1:

r=1
and
s=0
. In this case
|S_{\nu_{0}}|=2
with
\nu_{0}
and
-\nu_{0}
being the solutions. Choose
\mu_{1}=\nu_{0}+2p_{1}
(
\nu_{0}+p_{1}
if
\nu_{0}
is even) and
\mu_{2}=-\nu_{0}+2p_{1}
(
-\nu_{0}+p_{1}
if
\nu_{0}
is even). Then clearly
(\mu_{i},2p_{1})=1
for
i=1,2
. Clearly the diagonal matrix

(3.11)

\begin{pmatrix}e_{4}\big(\frac{l(\nu_{0}-\mu_{1})^{2}}{p_{1}^{2}}\big)&0\\
0&e_{4}\big(\frac{l(-\nu_{0}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{p_{1}^{2}}\big)\end{pmatrix}

has non–zero determinant. Thus
M_{\nu_{0}}
has rank
2
.

Case 2:

r=0
and
s=1
. If
q_{1}|\nu_{0}
, then
|S_{\nu_{0}}|=1
and the matrix
M_{\nu_{0}}
is a non–zero column and thus the lemma follows. If
(\nu_{0},q_{1})=1
, then
|S_{\nu_{0}}|=2
. Determinant of the matrix

(3.12)

\begin{pmatrix}e_{4q_{1}}\big(l(\nu-1)^{2}\big)&e_{4q_{1}}\big(l(\nu+1)^{2}\big)\\
e_{4q_{1}}\big(l(\nu-\mu)^{2}\big)&e_{4q_{1}}\big(l(\nu+\mu)^{2}\big)\end{pmatrix}

is given by
e_{4q_{1}}\left(l(2\nu^{2}+\mu^{2}+2\nu(\mu-1))\right)\left(1-e_{q_{1}}\left(l\nu(\mu-1)\right)\right)
. Choosing
\mu\text{ mod }2q_{1}
with
(\mu,2q_{1})=1
and different from
1
, we see that this determinant is non-zero. Thus the matrix
M_{\nu_{0}}
has rank
2
.

Now coming to the induction step
t\implies t+1
, first we assume that
r>0
and write
m_{1}=m_{3}p
and decompose
\nu_{0}\text{ mod }2m_{1}m_{2}
as
\nu_{0}=2m_{3}m_{2}\nu_{0}^{\prime}+p\nu_{0}^{\prime\prime}
with
\nu_{0}^{\prime}\text{ mod }p
and
\nu_{0}^{\prime\prime}\text{ mod }2m_{3}m_{2}
. Decompose
\nu
and
\mu
similarly. Note that

(1)

m_{1}|(\nu-\mu)
iff
m_{3}|(\nu^{\prime\prime}-\mu^{\prime\prime})
and
p|(\nu^{\prime}-\mu^{\prime})
.

(2)

\nu^{2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{2}\text{ mod }4m_{1}m_{2}
iff
\nu^{\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime 2}\text{ mod }p
and
\nu^{\prime\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime\prime 2}\text{ mod }4m_{3}m_{2}
.

Moreover, we also have

(3.13)

\frac{(\nu-\mu)^{2}}{4m_{1}^{2}m_{2}}=\frac{m_{2}(\nu^{\prime}-\mu^{\prime})^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{(\nu^{\prime\prime}-\mu^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{4m_{3}^{2}m_{2}}\text{ mod }\mathbb{Z}.

As
\nu^{\prime}
and
\nu^{\prime\prime}
run over the sets
\{\nu^{\prime}\text{ mod }p:\nu^{\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime 2}\text{ mod }p\}
and
\{\nu^{\prime\prime}\text{ mod }2m_{3}m_{2}:\nu^{\prime\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime\prime 2}\text{ mod }4m_{3}m_{2}\}
respectively, write
\varepsilon^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})=e\left(\frac{lm_{2}(\nu^{\prime}-\mu^{\prime})^{2}}{p^{2}}\right)=1
if
p|(\nu^{\prime}-\mu^{\prime})
and is zero otherwise and
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\nu^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})=e_{4m_{2}}\left(\frac{l(\nu^{\prime\prime}-\mu^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{m_{3}^{2}}\right)
if
m_{3}|(\nu^{\prime\prime}-\mu^{\prime\prime})
and zero otherwise. Let

(3.14)

A:=\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\right)_{\nu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}\text{ mod }p}\quad\text{ and }\quad B:=\left(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\nu^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})\right)_{\nu^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime}\text{ mod }2m_{3}m_{2}},

From the induction hypothesis,
B
has maximal rank. To see that
A
also has maximal rank, first note that
(\nu_{0}^{\prime},p)=1
since
(\nu_{0},m_{1})=1
. Thus
\nu^{\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime 2}\text{ mod }p
has two solutions (i.e., the rank of
A
is at most
2
). Denote them by
\nu_{1}^{\prime}=\nu_{0}^{\prime}
and
\nu_{2}^{\prime}=-\nu_{0}
. Let
\mu_{1}^{\prime}=\nu_{1}^{\prime}+p
and
\mu_{2}^{\prime}=\nu_{2}^{\prime}+p
. Clearly
(\mu_{i}^{\prime},p)=1
and
p|(\nu_{i}^{\prime}-\mu_{j}^{\prime})
iff
i=j
. Thus
A
has a sub-matrix of the form
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
.
This shows that
A
is indeed of rank
2
. The lemma is true in this case by noting that
M_{\nu_{0}}=A\otimes B
.

Now we turn to the case when
r=0
. Here
s=t+1
and we write
m_{2}=m_{4}q
, where
m_{4}
has
t=s-1
prime factors. Note that in this case
m_{1}=1
. As in the previous case decompose
\nu_{0}\text{ mod }2m_{2}
as
\nu_{0}=2m_{4}\nu_{0}^{\prime}+q\nu_{0}^{\prime\prime}
with
\nu_{0}^{\prime}\text{ mod }q
and
\nu_{0}^{\prime\prime}\text{ mod }2m_{4}
. Decompose
\nu
and
\mu
similarly. Note that
\nu^{2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{2}\text{ mod }4m_{2}
iff
\nu^{\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime 2}\text{ mod }q
and
\nu^{\prime\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime\prime 2}\text{ mod }4m_{4}
. We also have

(3.15)

\frac{(\nu-\mu)^{2}}{4m_{2}}=\frac{m_{4}(\nu^{\prime}-\mu^{\prime})^{2}}{q}+\frac{q(\nu^{\prime\prime}-\mu^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{4m_{4}}\text{ mod }\mathbb{Z}.

As before, we write

(3.16)

A:=\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\right)_{\nu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}\text{ mod }q}\quad\text{ and }\quad B:=\left(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\nu^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})\right)_{\nu^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime}\text{ mod }2m_{4}},

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})=e_{q}\left(lm_{4}(\nu^{\prime}-\mu^{\prime})^{2}\right)
and
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\nu^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})=e_{4m_{4}}\left(lq(\nu^{\prime\prime}-\mu^{\prime\prime})^{2}\right)
. As in the previous case,
\nu^{\prime}
runs over the set
\{\nu^{\prime}\text{ mod }q:\nu^{\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime 2}\text{ mod }q\}
and
\nu^{\prime\prime}
runs over the set
\{\nu^{\prime\prime}\text{ mod }2m_{4}:\nu^{\prime\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime\prime 2}\text{ mod }4m_{4}\}
.

Since
(l,2m_{4})=1
, the matrix
B
has maximal rank by induction hypothesis. To see that
A
has maximal rank, note that
\nu^{\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime 2}\text{ mod }q
has one solution if
q|\nu_{0}^{\prime}
and in this case
A
is a non–zero column and thus this is true.

If
(\nu_{0}^{\prime},q)=1
, then
\nu^{\prime 2}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\prime 2}\text{ mod }q
has two solutions. The matrix

(3.17)

\begin{pmatrix}e_{q}\big(lm_{4}(\nu^{\prime}-1)^{2}\big)&e_{q}\big(lm_{4}(\nu^{\prime}+1)^{2}\big)\\
e_{q}\big(lm_{4}(\nu^{\prime}-\mu)^{2}\big)&e_{q}\big(lm_{4}(\nu^{\prime}+\mu)^{2}\big)\end{pmatrix}

has determinant
e_{q}\left(lm_{4}(2\nu^{\prime 2}+\mu^{\prime 2}+1+2\nu^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime}-1))\right)\left(1-e_{q}\left(4lm_{4}\nu^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime}-1)\right)\right)
. By choosing
\mu
mod
q
with
(\mu,q)=1
and different from
1
, we see that the above determinant is non-zero. Thus the matrix
A
has rank
2
. By noting that
M_{\nu_{0}}=A\otimes B
, the lemma is now complete.
∎

Now define the relation
\nu\sim\nu^{\prime}
iff
\nu^{2}\equiv\nu^{\prime 2}\text{ mod }4m_{1}m_{2}
. This defines an equivalence relation among
\nu\text{ mod }2m_{1}m_{2}
with
(\nu,m_{1})=1
and
(\nu,2m_{1}m_{2})|2m_{2}
. Thus from the above discussion we see that
h_{\nu}=0
for all such indices. This gives us the proof of Theorem
1
.

When
m_{1}=1
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
N
be an odd integer and
m
be a square–free integer with
(N,m)=1
. For any non–zero
\phi\in J_{k,m}(N)
there exists a
\mu\text{ mod }2m
with

such that the theta component

.

4.
Half–integral weight forms for
\Gamma_{1}(4L)

For any positive integer
L
and a positive half–integer
\kappa
(i.e.,
\kappa=l/2
for some odd
l>0
) denote by
S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))
the space of half–integral weight cusp forms for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{1}(4L)
. Let
\chi
be any Dirichlet character modulo
4L
, then the space
S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))
decomposes as below.

(4.1)

S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))=\oplus_{\chi\text{ mod }4L}S_{\kappa}(4L,\chi),

where
S_{\kappa}(4L,\chi)
denotes the space of half–integral weight cusp forms for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(4L)
and character
\chi
.

Let
L
be as above and
L_{f}
an even divisor of
4L
. Consider a non–zero
f\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))
such that
a(f,n)=0
for all
(n,L_{f})>1
. Write

(4.2)

L=\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\;,\;L^{\prime}:=\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}\;,\;L_{f}^{\prime}=\prod\nolimits_{p|L_{f}}p\;,\;L^{\prime}=M_{f}L_{f}^{\prime}.

Our aim is to construct a new modular form
g
such that
a(g,n)=0
for all
(n,L^{\prime})>1
. To proceed further, we need the following two results. The first one is an analogue of Lemma 7 in
[
12
]
for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{1}(4L)
. The arguments used here are similar in spirit to those used to prove a similar result in the integral weight case (see
[
7
]
) with some additional care.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
h\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))
be non–zero. For an odd prime
p|L
, suppose
a(h,n)=0
for all
(n,p)=1
, then
g(\tau):=h(\tau/p)
belongs to
S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L/p))
.

Proof.

Consider the operators
V_{p}
and
U_{p}
given as below (see
[
12
]
for more about these operators).

\begin{split}&(f|V_{p})(\tau)=f(p\tau)=p^{-\kappa/4}f|\left\{\begin{pmatrix}p&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},p^{-1/4}\right\}=\sum a(f,n)q^{np},\\
&(f|U_{p})(\tau)=p^{\kappa/4-1}\sum\nolimits_{j\text{ mod }p}f|\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&j\\
0&p\end{pmatrix},p^{1/4}\right\}=\sum a(f,np)q^{n}.\end{split}

Then the condition on
h
is equivalent to
h|(1-U_{p}V_{p})=0
.

Next we consider the projection
\pi_{\chi}:S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))\rightarrow S_{\kappa}(4L,\chi)
which is given by

(4.3)

\pi_{\chi}(f)=\frac{1}{\phi(4L)}\sum_{a\text{ mod }4L,(a,4L)=1}\overline{\chi(a)}f|\sigma_{a}^{*},

\sigma_{a}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\sigma_{a}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}a^{-1}&0\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}\text{ mod }4L
. Also note that if
\sigma_{a}
and
\sigma_{a}^{\prime}
are any two such matrices, then
\sigma_{a}\sigma_{a}^{\prime-1}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\text{ mod }4L
and thus we have that
f|\sigma_{a}^{*}=f|\sigma_{a}^{\prime*}
. If we write
h=\sum h_{\chi}
, then we have that
h_{\chi}=\pi_{\chi}(h)
.

Now we claim that
h|(1-U_{p}V_{p})=0
would imply
h_{\chi}|(1-U_{p}V_{p})=0
for every
\chi\text{ mod }4L
. To see this, we show that actions of
U_{p}V_{p}
and
\sigma_{a}^{*}
commute. Let
\sigma_{a}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
with
a_{1}\equiv a^{-1}\text{ mod }4L
,
b\equiv c\equiv 0\text{ mod }4L
and
d\equiv a\text{ mod }4L
. First note that

(4.4)

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}p&j\\
0&p\end{pmatrix},1\right\}\sigma_{a}^{*}=\sigma_{a}^{\prime*}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}p&j^{\prime}\\
0&p\end{pmatrix},1\right\},

where
\sigma_{a}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}+jc&(b+jd)-j^{\prime}(a_{1}+jc)\\
c&d-j^{\prime}c\end{pmatrix}
. By choosing
j^{\prime}\equiv a_{1}^{-1}dj\text{ mod }4L
, we have
f|(U_{p}V_{p})|\sigma_{a}^{*}=f|\sigma_{a}^{\prime*}|(U_{p}V_{p})
. Thus

(4.5)

h_{\chi}|(1-U_{p}V_{p})=\frac{1}{\phi(4L)}\sum_{a\text{ mod }4L,(a,4L)=1}\overline{\chi(a)}h|(1-U_{p}V_{p})|\sigma_{a}^{*}=0.

Thus we get that for every
\chi\text{ mod }4L
,
h_{\chi}\in S_{\kappa}(4L,\chi)
such that
a(h_{\chi},n)=0
for all
(n,p)=1
.

Now
g(\tau)=h(\tau/p)=\sum h_{\chi}(\tau/p)
. If
h_{\chi}
is non–zero, then from
[
12
, Lemma 7]
,
h_{\chi}(\tau/p)\in S_{\kappa}(4L/p,\chi\chi_{p})
. Thus we see that
g\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L/p))
.
∎

The second result is the following lemma from
[
3
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
h\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))
. Suppose
a(h,n)=0
for all
(n,p)=1
for an odd prime
p\nmid L
, then
h=0
.

We now construct the new cusp form
g
using Lemma
4.1
and
4.2
and an inductive argument. These arguments are similar in essence to the ones used in
[
11
]
for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(4N)
and in
[
1
]
for the integral weight cusp forms.

Let
M_{f},L_{f}^{\prime}
be the co–prime square–free integers as in (
4.2
). Write
M_{f}=p_{1}p_{2}....p_{r}
.

(1)

Consider

g_{0}(\tau)=\sum\nolimits_{(n,L_{f}^{\prime})=1}a(f,n)q^{n}.

Clearly
g_{0}\neq 0
and
g_{0}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}))
. We construct
g_{1}
such that
a(g_{1},n)=0
for
(n,p_{1}L_{f}^{\prime})>1
.

(a)

Let
g_{1,0}(\tau)=\sum\nolimits_{(n,p_{1})=1}a(g_{0},n)q^{n}.
Then
g_{1,0}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}p_{1}^{2})
.

(b)

If
g_{1,0}=0
, then we set
g_{1,0}^{\prime}(\tau)=g_{0}(\tau/p_{1})
. Clearly
g_{1,0}^{\prime}\neq 0
, since
g_{0}\neq 0
. Since
g_{1,0}=0
, we have from Lemma
4.1
that
g_{1,0}^{\prime}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}/p_{1}))
. Now we set
g_{1,1}(\tau)=\sum\nolimits_{(n,p_{1})=1}a(g_{1,0}^{\prime},n)q^{n}
and we have
g_{1,1}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}p_{1})
.

(c)

Now suppose
g_{1,i}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}p_{1}^{2-i})
has been constructed as in step (b) for some
0\leq i\leq\alpha_{1}
. If
g_{1,i}=0
, then we go back to step (b) and construct
g_{1,i+1}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}p_{1}^{2-(i+1)})
with
g_{1,0}
and
g_{0}
replaced by
g_{1,i}
and
g^{\prime}{1,i-1}
respectively. The first
i_{0}
for which
g_{1,i_{0}}\neq 0
, we set
g_{1}=g_{1,i_{0}}
.

If all of
g_{1,0},\cdots,g_{1,\alpha_{1}}
are zero, since
g^{\prime}_{1,\alpha_{1}-1}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}/p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}))
and
p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}||L
, we see from the Fourier coefficients of
g_{1,\alpha_{1}}
and Lemma
4.2
that
g^{\prime}_{1,\alpha_{1}-1}=0
. This gives us that
g_{0}=0
, a clear contradiction. Hence, the process must stop for some
1\leq i\leq\alpha_{1}
. Thus we get
g_{1}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}p_{1}^{2-i_{0}})
for some
1\leq i_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}
such that
a(g_{1},n)=0
for
(n,p_{1}L_{f}^{\prime})>1
and
a(g_{1},n)=a(g_{0},p^{i_{0}}n)
.

(2)

Now suppose
g_{j}\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}p_{1}^{2-i_{1}}p_{2}^{2-i_{2}}...p_{j}^{2-i_{j}}))
has been constructed for some
1\leq j<r
, then we construct
g_{j+1}
as in step (1) with
g_{0}
,
L_{f}^{\prime}
and
M_{f}
replaced by
g_{j}
,
L_{f}^{\prime}p_{1}p_{2}...p_{j}
and
p_{j+1}...p_{r}
respectively.

(3)

Finally, we set
g=g_{r}
and we have
g\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}p_{1}^{2-i_{1}}p_{2}^{2-i_{2}}...p_{r}^{2-i_{r}}))
. Moreover,
a(g,n)=0
if
(n,L_{f}^{\prime}p_{1}...p_{r})>1
and
a(g,n)=a(f,p_{1}^{i_{1}}...p_{r}^{i_{r}}n)
otherwise.

We summarize the discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
L=\prod_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
and
L_{f}
be an even divisor of
4L
. Suppose
f\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))
is non–zero and
a(f,n)=0
for all
(n,L_{f})>1
. Then there exists a
g\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4LL_{f}^{\prime 2}p_{1}^{2-i_{1}}...p_{r}^{2-i_{r}}))
with the following properties.

(1)

a(g,n)=0
whenever
(n,4L)>1
.

(2)

a(g,n)=a(f,p_{1}^{i_{1}}...p_{r}^{i_{r}}n)
when
(n,4L)=1
.

(3)

0\leq i_{j}\leq\alpha_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq r
.

Now we use the following result for half–integral weight cusp forms from
[
3
, Theorem 4.5]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\kappa\geq 5/2
be a half–integer and
L\geq 1
be an integer. Suppose
g\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L))
is such that
a(g,n)=0
whenever
(n,4L)>1
. Then there exists infinitely many odd and square-free integers
n
such that
a(g,n)\neq 0
. More precisely, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\#\{n\leq X,n\text{ square--free }:\;a(g,n)\neq 0\}\gg_{g,\epsilon}X^{5/8-\epsilon}.

To complete the proof of Theorem
3
, first note that for any such
f
as in the statement of Theorem
3
we get from Proposition
4.1
a
g\in S_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{1}(4L_{1}))
whose Fourier coefficients are supported away from the level. Now using Theorem
4.1
for
g
and noting the relation between the Fourier coefficients of
f
and
g
we get Theorem
3
.∎

5.
Fourier coefficients of Siegel cusp forms

Using the corresponding results for Jacobi forms (Theorem
1
) and half integral weight forms (Theorem
3
) we now give the proof of Theorem
2
.
First we need the following result due to Ibukiyama and Katsurada (see
[
5
]
).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
F\in S_{k}^{2,new}(N,\chi)
be non–zero. Then there exists a primitive
T
such that
A(F,T)\neq 0
.

For any
M\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, we have that
A(F,T)=\mathrm{det}(M)^{k}A(F,M^{t}TM)
. Thus if
A(F,T)\neq 0
, we can say that
A(F,M^{t}TM)\neq 0
. Now coming to the case at hand, the quadratic form associated to
T
represents infinitely many primes (see
[
14
]
). Since
T
is primitive, we can choose
M
such that the matrix
T_{0}=M^{t}TM
has the right lower entry to be an odd prime, say
p
(see
[
11
, Lemma 2.1]
for exact arguments). Since the quadratic form defined by
T
represents infinitely many odd primes, we can choose
p
such that
(p,N)=1
. Let
T_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{0}&\mu_{0}/2\\
\mu_{0}/2&p\end{pmatrix}
and we have
A(F,T_{0})\neq 0
. Thus we get a non–zero Fourier–Jacobi coefficient
\phi_{p}\in J_{k,p}(N)
of
F
. Let
\phi_{p}(\tau,z)=\sum\nolimits_{4pn>r^{2}}c(n,r)e(n\tau)e(rz),

where
c(n,r)=A\left(F,\begin{pmatrix}n&r/2\\
r/2&p\end{pmatrix}\right)
. Since
A(F,T_{0})\neq 0
, we see that
c(n_{0},\mu_{0})\neq 0
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
F\in S_{k}^{2,new}(N)
be non–zero. Then there exists an odd prime
p
with
(p,N)=1
such that the Fourier–Jacobi coefficient
\phi_{p}\in J_{k,p}^{cusp}(N)
of
F
is non–zero.

Write
N=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}...p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
. Then from Proposition
5.1
and Corollary
3.1
we get a
\mu\text{ mod }2p
with
(\mu,2p)=1
such that
h_{\mu}\neq 0
. Let
f(\tau)=h_{\mu}(4p\tau)
, then
f\in S_{k-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{1}((4p)^{2}N))
. From the Fourier expansion of
h_{\mu}
in (
3.1
), we see that
f(\tau)=\sum\nolimits_{n\geq 1}a(f,n)q^{n},
where
a(f,n)=c\Big((n+\mu^{2})/4p,\mu\Big)
.

Now using the Theorem
3
