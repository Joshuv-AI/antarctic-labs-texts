Homotopy Classification of loops of Clifford unitaries
Roman Geiko, Yichen Hu

Abstract
. Clifford quantum circuits are elementary invertible transformations of quantum systems that map Pauli operators to Pauli operators. We study periodic one-parameter families of Clifford circuits, called loops of Clifford circuits, acting on 𝖽 {\mathsf{d}} -dimensional lattices of prime p p -dimensional qudits. We propose to use the notion of algebraic homotopy to identify topologically equivalent loops. We calculate homotopy classes of such loops for any odd p p and 𝖽 = 0 , 1 , 2 , 3 {\mathsf{d}}=0,1,2,3 , and 4 4 . Our main tool is the Hermitian K-theory, particularly a generalization of the Maslov index from symplectic geometry. We observe that the homotopy classes of loops of Clifford circuits in ( 𝖽 + 1 ) ({\mathsf{d}}+1) -dimensions coincide with the quotient of the group of Clifford Quantum Cellular Automata modulo shallow circuits and lattice translations in 𝖽 {\mathsf{d}} -dimensions.

Unitary dynamics of many-body quantum systems constitute one of the core components of quantum dynamics in the theory of quantum computation and information processing. Recent works have made tremendous progress in understanding the entanglement generation

The notion of a topological phase of matter colloquially means a family of systems whose elements can be connected by adiabatic evolution. A mathematically rigorous definition of a topological phase involves sophisticated functional analysis and is an area of active research, see
[

In this paper, we use algebraic tools to study homotopy groups (in a sense explained below) of loops of Clifford unitary dynamics bypassing functional analysis. We assume that the unitary dynamics is strictly locality-preserving, Clifford, and translation invariant. By strict locality-preserving dynamics, we mean operator algebra automorphisms that map operators with finite support to operators with finite support. Clifford dynamics stands for automorphisms of the operator algebra normalizing the group of Pauli operators. A natural way to study Clifford unitary dynamics is by analyzing its action on stabilizer Hamiltonians. As was demonstrated by J. Haah, stabilizer Hamiltonians admit a description in terms of certain submodules of the so-called Pauli module, a module over the ring of Laurent polynomials
[
17
]
, while Clifford QCA correspond to automorphisms of the Pauli module. We shall be interested in specifically simple Lagrangian submodules of the Pauli module. In the language of stabilizer Hamiltonians, the set of Lagrangian submodules, the Lagrangian Grassmannian, corresponds to a subset of invertible Hamiltonians, i.e., Hamiltonians having a unique ground state on a lattice of any topology.

We argue that the phases of Clifford unitary dynamics can be analyzed by studying the homotopy theory of the Lagrangian Grassmannian. In particular, we analyze topological phases of loops of Clifford circuits by studying periodic paths in the Lagrangian Grassmanian. The group structure on the set of loops of Clifford circuits is induced by stacking corresponding families of invertible Hamiltonians. This group law differs from the obvious group structure on the set of topological phases of unitary dynamics, which is induced by the composition of QCA. We analyze periodic paths in the Lagrangian Grassmannian using a generalization of the classical Maslov index, which is a homotopy invariant of loops in the real Lagrangian Grassmannian. The version of the Maslov index used in this paper is a manifestation of the fundamental theorem of Hermitian K-theory
[
18
]
of Karoubi in the interpretation of Barges and Lannes
[
19
]
. Since the fundamental theorem of Hermitian K-theory is applicable only to the Laurent polynomial rings with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with
p>2
, our results do not apply to lattices of qubits.

The main result of this paper is a classification of homotopy classes of loops of Clifford circuits for translationally invariant

\displaystyle\Omega\mathfrak{C}(4,p)\cong\begin{cases}{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\,,\quad p\equiv 1\;\mbox{mod}\;4\,,\\

2.
Review of symplectic formalism

Throughout this letter, we assume that the lattice systems under consideration are translation-invariant and unitaries acting on lattice systems are translation invariant as well. We focus on Hamiltonians made of products of Pauli operators and unitaries mapping products of Pauli operators in products of Pauli operators, also known as Clifford unitaries. In this section we review the formalism of symplectic vector spaces and symplectic modules as a convenient framework for translation-invariant Pauli Hamiltonians and Clifford unitaries.

Single qudit.
We begin our review with the case of a single site. The Pauli operators acting on a prime
p
-dimensional qudit form the Pauli group, which we denote by
\mbox{P}_{p}
. This group is defined through generators and relations:

(1)

\displaystyle\mbox{P}_{p}\coloneqq\langle X,Z,\omega\;|\;X^{p}=Z^{p}=\omega^{p}=1,\;XZ=\omega ZX\rangle\,.

Let us pick an orthonormal basis
\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0}^{p-1}
for
\mathbb{C}^{p}
, then the minimal faithful unitary representation of
\mbox{P}_{p}
, i.e., a homomorphism into the unitary group of
\mathbb{C}^{p}
, denoted by
\mbox{U}_{p}
, is given by

\displaystyle\omega|n\rangle=e^{2\pi i/p}|n\rangle\,,\quad X|n\rangle=|n+\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{305082}{393216}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{305082}{393216}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{347455}{393216}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{415257}{393216}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}}}}\,\mbox{mod}\;p\rangle\,,\quad Z|n\rangle=e^{2\pi in/p}|n\rangle\,.

In this paper we are concerned with automorphisms of algebras of operators acting on lattice systems. For the case of a single qudit, all automorphisms of the algebra of observables are given by conjugations with some unitary operator. The group of Clifford unitaries
\mbox{CU}_{p}
is a subgroup of the unitary group that normalizes
\mbox{P}_{p}
(more accurately, the image of
\mbox{P}_{p}
in
\mbox{U}_{p}
):

\displaystyle\mbox{CU}_{p}\coloneqq\{x\in\mbox{U}_{p}\,|\,x\,\mbox{P}_{p}\,x^{-1}=\mbox{P}_{p}\}\,.

In other words, Clifford unitaries generate the automorphisms of the qudit operator algebra that preserve the Pauli group.

Let us recall that the adjoint action of the unitary group is not faithful – the kernel of this action is the center of
\mbox{U}_{p}
and it is isomorphic to
\mbox{U}_{1}
. In its turn, the adjoint action of the quotient group
\mbox{U}_{d}/\mbox{U}_{1}
, also known as the projective unitary group
\mbox{PU}_{d}
, on the matrix algebra is faithful. In order to get rid of this redundancy, we define the projective Clifford group
\mbox{PCU}_{p}
:

\displaystyle\mbox{PCU}_{p}\coloneqq\{x\in\mbox{PU}_{p}\,|\,x\,\mbox{P}_{p}\,x^{-1}=\mbox{P}_{p}\}\,.

whose adjoint action on
\mbox{P}_{p}
is faithful. There is an alternative description
of
\mbox{PCU}_{d}
, which will be important for future applications, as the normalizer of the abelianization of
\mbox{P}_{d}
within
\mbox{PU}_{p}
. Let
\mbox{AP}_{p}\coloneqq\mbox{P}_{p}/[\mbox{P}_{p},\mbox{P}_{p}]\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{p}

{\mathsf{d}}>0

{\mathbb{Z}}^{{\mathsf{d}}}

N

p

N

{\mathbb{Z}}^{{\mathsf{d}}}

\mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{{\mathsf{d}}})

\mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbb{Z}})

a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}

2.1.
Stabilizer modules

This paper is devoted to the analysis of
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitaries and it is convenient to study them by their action on submodules of Pauli modules, more precisely, on Lagrangian submodules. Thus, we briefly digress on the so-called stabilzer modules following
[

Let us consider a translation invariant Hamiltonian

(7)

H=-\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{{\mathsf{d}}}}(h_{1,t}+\cdots+h_{v,t})

where
h_{1,t},\dots,h_{v,t}
are terms corresponding to the interaction types that satisfy a number of properties: we assume that the terms
h_{1,t},\dots,h_{v,t}
are made of Pauli operators, mutually commute, and

is frustration-free – such Hamiltonians are called
stabilizer Hamiltonians
. Since the Hamiltonian in (

\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}}
-module. The module generated by

is called

.

Example

As an example, let us consider a one-dimensional spin-chain with a single
p
-dimensional qudit per site. Here, the Pauli algebra is
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{1}=\mathbb{F}_{p}[x,x^{-1}]
and the Pauli module is two-dimensional. Let
H
be a generalized
{\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}}
cluster Hamiltonian:

(8)

\displaystyle H=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}X_{x-1}Z_{x}X_{x+1}+h.c.

The stabilizer module for such a Hamiltonian is generated by
\begin{pmatrix}x^{-1}+x^{+1}\\
1\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}-x^{-1}-x^{+1}\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
, which is, in fact, one-dimensional.

The commutation relations between Pauli operators are encoded by the pairing (
5
). By definition, all elements of the stabilizer module mutually commute what means that the stabilizer module is
isotropic
, i.e., pairing (
5
) degenerates on such a submodule. Further, we can ask – what Pauli operators commute with the terms in
H
, i.e., what are the local symmetries admitted by
H
? Let us denote the stabilizer module by

, then the operators commuting with

form the module
S^{\perp}
where orthogonal complement is taken with respect to the pairing (
5

is implied by the definition of the stabilizer module, while the absence of local symmetries is equivalent to the condition
S^{\perp}\subset S
, i.e.,

is
coisotropic

condition. There is a special class of locally topologically ordered Hamiltonians having a unique ground state on any topology – we call such Hamiltonians invertible. Even more restricted is the class of Hamiltonians corresponding to Lagrangian stabilizer modules defined in Section
3.5
: they are isotropic, coisotropic and direct summands in the Pauli module. One checks that the module from the example above is indeed a Lagrangian. We recommend
[
17
]
and
[
22
]
for further reading on how properties of stabilizer modules translate into physical properties of Hamiltonians. The upshot of this discussion is that Lagrangian submodules of the Pauli module correspond to invertible Hamiltonians, though not all invertible systems can be encoded by a Lagrangian submodule.

2.2.
Clifford QCA

A quantum cellular automaton (QCA) is a locality-preserving automorphism of the observables algebra: it maps operators localized in finite regions to operators localized in finite regions. Clifford QCA (CQCA) are QCA which map product of Pauli operators to products of Pauli operators. According to the discussion above, we identify the groups of Clifford QCA and the group of
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitaries.

The most elementary CQCA are given by conjugations with Clifford gates – local unitary transformations whose adjoint action maps Pauli operators to Pauli operators. An operator evolution generated by mutually commuting Clifford gates is called Clifford quantum circuit (CQC). A finite number of layers of CQCs is called Clifford finite-depth (shallow) quantum circuit (CFDQC), which is a prototypical example of a CQCA. While the group of CQCA correspond to the group of
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitaries, CFDQCs correspond to the normal subgroup of elementary
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitaries, see, e.g.,
[
17
]
.

Example.

In the setting of the previous example, let us provide an elementary
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitary that maps the product state Hamiltonian
H_{0}=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}X_{x}+h.c.
, which corresponds to a stabilizer module generated by
\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
, to the cluster Hamiltonian (
8
):

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&x^{-1}+x^{+1}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

This example demonstrates that there exists a shallow Clifford circuit preparing the cluster state from the product state.

Together with shallow circuits, there is another important class of CQCA which is given by generalized translations (we shall equally use the term “shifts”). A translation along one of the lattice directions is obviously a CQCA, yet it cannot be presented by a finite number of layers of Clifford circuits. Indeed, if we try to do so, then we either loose the locality, or we end up with an infinite number of layers. Together with the ordinary translations, we are interested in generalized translations –
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitaries defined by a property that they do not mix the generalized Pauli
X
and Pauli
Z
operators. In other words, a generalized translation consists of a simultaneous translation and a mixing of species of qudits. Generalized translations correspond to the subgroup of “hyperbolic”
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitaries defined in (
11
).

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{L}}_{R^{n+1}}(R)\,

\displaystyle{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\Lambda}\oplus R\,

Thus defined

\displaystyle{\Lambda}_{1}\pitchfork{\Lambda}_{2}\Longleftrightarrow{\Lambda}_{1}+{\Lambda}_{2}={\mbox{H}}^{-}({\mbox{L}})\,.

Obviously,

\displaystyle{\Lambda}\pitchfork{\mbox{L}}\Longleftrightarrow{\Lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}1&q_{1}\\

Serre-Suslin-Swan theorem.
We aim to apply our results to the rings of Laurent polynomials with coefficients in a prime field

-unitary

5.
An interlude

Let us comment more on the correspondence between stabilizer modules and stabilizer Hamiltonians. Any concrete stabilizer Hamiltonian like (
7
) generates an isotropic submodule of the Pauli module. Since the set of generators for any module is highly non-unique, many stabilizer Hamiltonians may generate the same stabilizer module. In the opposite direction, if two stabilizer Hamiltonians generate the same module, then they are homotopy-equivalent, i.e., there exists a path of gapped Hamiltonians connecting them, see Proposition 2.1 of
[
17
]
, which is formulated for qubits but can be adjusted to qudits of any prime dimension. Thus, there is a 1-1 correspondence between the stabilizer modules and homotopy equivalence classes of stabilizer Hamiltonians. We denote the homotopy class of the Hamiltonian
H
by
\{H\}
.

It turns out that such properties as the ground state degeneracy are characteristics of the stabilizer module and not of a specific Hamiltonian
[
17
]
. We focus on special classes of Hamiltonians corresponding to Lagrangian stabilizer modules as they are particularly simple. Let
{\Lambda}_{1}
and
{\Lambda}_{2}
be a pair of Lagrangians in the Pauli module that can be connected by an elementary
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitary, and let
\{H_{1}\}
and
\{H_{2}\}
be a pair of corresponding classes of Hamiltonians, then, we have an equivalence:

\displaystyle\{H_{1}\}\xleftrightarrow{\text{CFDQC}}\{H_{2}\}\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\Lambda}_{1}\xleftrightarrow{{\mathsf{EU}}^{-}}{\Lambda}_{2}

In the previous section we have discussed the algebraic homotopy relation between Lagrangian submodules. We can think of such a homotopy as a time-dependent Hamiltonian interpolating between the end points of the path. Note that the time parametrizing the path is not on the same footing as the spatial coordinates. The algebraic homotopies are defined through the polynomial extension of the Pauli algebra. Monomials in the non-extended Pauli algebra generate lattice translations, while the new time variable generates translations only in one direction. We observed that if two Lagrangians are connected by an elementary
{\lambda}^{-}

5.1.
Classical Maslov index.

The main tool of the next section is an algebraic generalization of the Maslov
index, which is well-known in symplectic geometry. Before we proceed to the algebraic version in the next section, we wish to demonstrate how the index works in the simplest case of a two-dimensional real phase space. As we pointed out in the introduction, the Pauli module is a direct analog of the phase space of Hamiltonian mechanics. The classical Maslov index of a loop of Lagrangian subspaces in a real phase space is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-valued invariant that can be thought of as the winding number of such a loop.

Le us consider the setting of Hamiltonian mechanics: the Pauli algebra is
R={\mathbb{R}}
and the Pauli module is a real phase space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. It is a classical observation that
\mbox{U}_{n}/\mbox{O}_{n}
acts freely and transitively on the space of all Lagrangian subspaces
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}})
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. Therefore, by picking a base-point, we obtain a diffeomorphism of manifolds

(27)

\displaystyle\mbox{U}_{n}/\mbox{O}_{n}\xrightarrow{\sim}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}})\,,\quad u\mapsto u\cdot({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\oplus 0)\,.

Since the determinant of any matrix in
\mbox{O}_{n}
is
\pm 1
, there is a well-defined map:

(28)

\displaystyle\mbox{det}^{2}:{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}})\to\mbox{U}_{1}\,

whose fiber is the subspace of
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}})
corresponding to
(\mbox{det}\,u)^{2}=1
under
(
27
)
. Let us denote the fiber of (
28
) by
S{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}})
, then we have a fibration

\displaystyle\mbox{SO}_{n}\to\mbox{SU}_{n}\to S{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}})

The long exact sequence of homotopy groups for this fibration together with that for
S{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}})\to\mbox{U}_{1}
gives us an isomorphism
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathbb{R}}))\cong{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The classical Maslov index is a tool calculating the degree of a loop of Lagrangians. Let us work out the simplest possible example of a loop of Lagrangians in the real two-dimensional phase space. The Lagrangians in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
are straight lines such that

(29)

\displaystyle\alpha(T)=\begin{pmatrix}4(T+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})^{3}-6(T+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})^{2}+1\\
12(T+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})^{2}-12(T+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad T\in[0,1]\,.

The curve
\{\alpha(T)\,,\,T\in[0,1]\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
defines a loop in
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}}({\mathbb{R}})\cong{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree one.

Let us describe an algebraic method of computing degrees of loops in
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}}({\mathbb{R}})
using Sturm functions
[
19
,
29
]

of the form

\textstyle\begin{pmatrix}P(T)\\
P^{\prime}(T)\end{pmatrix}

where

is a polynomial of degree

with simple roots and such that
P(0),P(1)\neq 0
. We use the Euclidean algorithm to divide

by
P^{\prime}(T)
which results in a sequence of residues

where each element is a linear polynomial and

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}P(T)\\

To this end, we define a bilinear form with the following matrix representation:

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}(T)&-1&&&&&&\\
-1&q_{2}(T)&-1&&&&&\\

&&&&&&&\\

For the case of a real phase space, abstract algebraic loops of Lagrangians correspond to actual geometric loops like the ones considered above. In the next section we shall use an analog of the Maslov index for Lagrangian submodules inside the Pauli module. One should be aware that the Lagrangian Grassmannian for

a^{*}\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{622585}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\circ\,$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{622585}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\circ\,$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{776920}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\circ\,$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{982102}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\circ\,$}}}}}\mbox{st}(\phi)\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{622585}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\circ\,$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{622585}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\circ\,$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{776920}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\circ\,$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{982102}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\circ\,$}}}}}a=\mbox{st}(\psi)

∎

The main result of this section is the classification of loops of Lagrangians based at the standard Lagrangian
{\mbox{L}}\subset{\mbox{H}}^{-}({\mbox{L}})

7.
Fundamental ideal

In this section, we analyze the fundamental ideal of the free Witt group of
+
hermitian forms for the ring of Laurent polynomials
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}}\coloneqq\mathbb{F}_{p}[x_{1},x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{{\mathsf{d}}},x_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{243794}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathsf{d}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{243794}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle{\mathsf{d}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{243793}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathsf{d}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{243795}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{d}}$}}}}}}^{-1}]
with odd
p
. Note that
I^{lib}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
is isomorphic to
I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
due to the Serre-Suslin-Swan theorem. Thus, it suffices to compute
I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
.

Recall, the Witt group of
+
hermitian forms
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
, defined in (
13
), is the quotient of the Grothedieck-Witt group of
+
hermitian forms on finite-dimensional free
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}}
-modules modulo hyperbolic forms. The fundamental ideal
I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
consists of elements from
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
corresponding to classes of even-dimensional forms. Thus,
I
fits into the following exact sequence

(56)

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})\longrightarrow{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/2\longrightarrow 0

such that
I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
is a subgroup of
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
of order two.

It is instructive to calculate
I(\mathbb{F}_{p})
explicitly. It is well-known, see, e.g.,
[
33
]
, that the Witt group
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is a group of order four generated by +hermitian forms on
1
-dimensional modules. Let us denote those forms by
\langle 1\rangle
and
\langle\theta\rangle
, where
\theta\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}/\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2\times}
is a square non-residue. Then, the Witt group is given by the following generators and relations depending on
p
mod
4
:

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

\displaystyle\cong\langle\;\langle 1\rangle,\langle\theta\rangle\,|\,2\langle 1\rangle=2\langle\theta\rangle=0\;\rangle\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/2\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}/2\,,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\;\;p\equiv 1\;\mbox{mod}\;4\,,\;

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

\displaystyle\cong\langle\;\langle 1\rangle,\langle\theta\rangle\,|\,2\langle 1\rangle=2\langle\theta\rangle,\langle 1\rangle+\langle\theta\rangle=0\;\rangle\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/4\,,\quad\quad\quad\;\;\;\;\;p\equiv 3\;\mbox{mod}\;4\,.\;

Thus,
I(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/2
is generated by
\langle 1\rangle+\langle\theta\rangle
for
p\equiv 1
mod
4
and by
2\langle\theta\rangle
for
p\equiv 3
mod
4
. This can also be seen directly from (
55
) after noting that
V(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong\mathbb{F}^{\times}_{p}
.

We are interested in the fundamental ideal
I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
for
{\mathsf{d}}=1,2,3
, and
4
. The calculation of
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})
was done by A. Ranicki in
[
34
]
and recently reviewed by J. Haah in
[
12
]
. Due to the Serre-Suslin-Swan theorem, Ranicki’s
V
- and
U
-theories for
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}}
coincide. Also, theories of
\pm
hermitian forms coincide with theories of
\pm
quadratic forms since we focus on
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}}
with odd
p
. We adopt the notation used by Haah:

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{L}}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})=\begin{cases}{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})\,,\quad n\equiv 0\;\mbox{mod}\;4\,,\\
{\mathfrak{U}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})\,,\quad\,\,n\equiv 1\;\mbox{mod}\;4\,,\\
{\mathfrak{W}}^{-}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})\,,\quad n\equiv 2\;\mbox{mod}\;4\,,\\
{\mathfrak{U}}^{-}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})\,,\quad\,\,n\equiv 3\;\mbox{mod}\;4\,.\end{cases}

These L-groups satisfy the following recursion relation
[
34
,
12
]
:

(57)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{L}}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}})\cong{\mathfrak{L}}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}-1})\oplus{\mathfrak{L}}_{n-1}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}-1})\,.

The base of recursion is given by
{\mathfrak{L}}_{0}(\mathbb{F}_{p})={\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
and by known isomorphisms:

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong 0\,\quad{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong 0\,\quad{\mathfrak{L}}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong 0\,.

We immediately obtain:

(58)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{1})\cong{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2})\cong{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3})\,,\quad{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4})\cong{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\oplus{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\,,

and obvious isomorphisms

(59)

\displaystyle I(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{1})\cong I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2})\cong I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3})\,.

In order to calculate
I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4})
, we need a closer look at how isomorphism (
57
) works. We always have a ring homomorphism of evaluation at
1
, which induces a group homomorphism
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4})\to{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3})
whose kernel is isomorphic to
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. Moreover, one copy of
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is present in
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathsf{d}})
for all
{\mathsf{d}}
and it comes from
{\mathsf{d}}=0
. The second copy of
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is the image of
{\mathfrak{U}}^{-}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3})
under a map
{\mathsf{B}}^{\uparrow}_{3}
:

(60)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{B}}^{\uparrow}_{3}:{\mathfrak{U}}^{-}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3})\to{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4})

defined in Section 5.8 of
[
12
]
. In short,
{\mathsf{B}}^{\uparrow}_{3}
maps a
\lambda^{-}
-unitary
u\in{\mathsf{U}}^{-}({\mbox{L}};R)
to a
+
hermitian form on the induced
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4}
-module
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}}({\mbox{L}}\oplus{\mbox{L}}^{*})
. Explicitly, the non-trivial classes of forms in
{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4})
can be obtained by applying
{\mathsf{B}}^{\uparrow}_{3}
to the non-trivial classes of
{\mathfrak{U}}^{-}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3})
which are provided in Section IV of
[
35
]
. This simple observation leads to a conclusion that the whole image of
{\mathsf{B}}^{\uparrow}_{3}
consists of classes of even-dimensional hermitian forms and we obtain

(61)

\displaystyle I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/2\oplus{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\,.

Note that the first term
{\mathbb{Z}}/2
is also present in the zero-dimensional case, i.e., there is an inclusion
\iota:I(\mathbb{F}_{p})\to I(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathsf{d}})
for
{\mathsf{d}}=1,2,3
. Clearly, any loop of Lagrangians over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
defines a loop of Lagrangians over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{{\mathsf{d}}}
. In order to get rid of the zero-dimensional loops, we define
\tilde{I}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathsf{d}})=\mbox{coker}\,\iota
. A similar reduction of the space of invertible lattice systems is discussed in Section VI of
[
36
]
. As a result, we obtain that the homotopy classes of loops of free Lagrangians modulo shifts and zero-dimensional loops are given by

(62)

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathsf{d}})\cong 0\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;{\mathsf{d}}=0,1,2,3\,,\quad\mbox{and}\mbox{\quad}\tilde{I}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4})\cong{\mathfrak{W}}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\,.

This concludes our calculation and we arrive at the result announced in Section
1
.

8.
Discussion

In this paper, we studied homotopy classes of periodic Clifford unitaries through the analysis of stabilizer Hamiltonians they act on. We found that there are non-trivial homotopy classes of periodic Clifford unitary dynamics in four spatial dimensions.

1.
Our calculations heavily relied on the classical results in Hermitian K-theory, in particular, the fundamental theorem by Karoubi
[
18
]
and its interpretation by Barges and Lannes
[
19
]
. The fundamental theorem applies only to rings with involution containing an element
\lambda
such that
{\lambda}+\bar{\lambda}=2
. In our case, this condition restricts us to prime
p
-dimensional qudits with
p>2
. However, from the physics perspective, we expect that similar results can be obtained for
p=2
. Namely, we expect that the group of homotopy classes of reduced loops of Clifford unitaries for four-dimensional lattices of qubits is isomorphic to the Witt group of symmetric bilinear forms on
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector spaces. The reason to believe it is true is described in the next paragraph.

2.
The results obtained in this letter confirm the general correspondence between
{\mathsf{d}}
-dimensional QCA and
({\mathsf{d}}+1)
-dimensional Floquet circuits, also known as the bulk-boundary correspondence, as mentioned in
[
12
]
and analyzed for
{\mathsf{d}}=1
in
[
37
]
. A heuristic argument towards the bulk-boundary correspondence for QCA is a variant of the famous argument by Kitaev about loops of short-range entangled phases
[
38
]
, reviewed, in particular, in
[
39
]
. Colloquially speaking, we should replace the
{\mathsf{d}}
-dimension SRE state in Kitaev’s argument with a
{\mathsf{d}}
-dimensional QCA to obtain a
({\mathsf{d}}+1)
-dimensional Floquet circuit parametrized by the QCA. In our approach, the bulk-boundary correspondence arises as an incarnation of the fundamental theorem of hermitian K-theory.

3.
As we reviewed in the introductory section, the framework of Clifford unitaries admits a formulation in terms of
{\lambda}^{-}
-unitaries and is naturally connected to the Hermitian K-theory. Of course, there are QCA beyond Clifford QCA, see examples in
[
40
]
, for which the Hermitian K-theory does not immediately apply. A novel approach to the general QCA in terms of invertible subalgebras can be found in
[
41
]
and
C^{*}
-algebraic approach to symmetric QCA is discussed in
[
42
]
. One of the possible candidates describing the structure of general QCA is a Hermitian K-theory of dg categories in the sense of
[
43
]
.
