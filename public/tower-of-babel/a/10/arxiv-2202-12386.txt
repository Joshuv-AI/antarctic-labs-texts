Limits and colimits of synthetic ∞ \infty -categories
César Bardomiano Martínez

Abstract
We develop the theory of limits and colimits in ∞ \infty -categories within the synthetic framework of simplicial Homotopy Type Theory developed by Riehl and Shulman. We also show that in this setting, the limit of a family of spaces can be computed as a dependent product.



1
Introduction

Defining the notion of
\infty
-category is always difficult a task. There have been many different proposed definitions, notably to mention a few, by Boardman and Vogt
[
BV73
]
with the idea of quasi-categories, later exploited by Joyal
[
Joy08a
]
and Lurie
[
Lur09
]
, or by Rezk in
[
Rez01
]
in the form of
complete Segal spaces
. In the case of
(\infty,1)
-categories, most of known definitions have been shown to be equivalent in an appropriate homotopy theoretic sense, see for example the work by Toën
[
Toë05
]
, Bergner
[
Ber07
]
and Joyal-Tierney
[
JT07
]
.

Recent work by Riehl and Shulman
[
RS17
]
develops a
synthetic theory of
(\infty,1)
-categories
. They achieve this by implementing an extension of Homotopy Type Theory. Moreover, Riehl explores in
[
Rie23
]
the novelty and advantages that support the philosophy behind this approach. We highlight some of this points here. Firstly, all construction are by design invariant under equivalence. Another aspect of this synthetic theory is that by construction compatible with the Univalence Axiom. Moreover, the standard model of the theory, the category of bisimplicial sets, has a well-known homotopy theory. Furthermore, it is possible to carry this synthetic theory internally to any Grothendieck
\infty
-topos (see
Remark

6.5
). Finally, another pleasant consequence of this synthetic account is that somewhat simplifies some definitions and constructions, and therefore lightens the development of the theory of
(\infty,1)
-categories. To begin with, the synthetic definition of
(\infty,1)
-category is fairly succinct in comparison with any other given before.

Previously, Voevodsky’s simplicial model for HoTT
[
KL21
]
interpreted types as
\infty
-groupoids. However, we do not have a similar interpretation of types as
\infty
-categories, where by
\infty
-category we mean
(\infty,1)
-category. To overcome such difficulty
[
RS17
]
introduce a new type theory by adding to HoTT a strict interval object and the novel idea, due to Shulman and Lumsdaine (unpublished), of a new type former called
extension type
. We call this
simplicial Homotopy Type Theory
or sHoTT for short. This type theory can be seen as an instance of a cubical type theory, however, different to that presented in
[
CCHM15
]
.

In this setting, it is possible to define
Rezk types
as certain special types which play the role of
\infty
-categories. In this synthetic treatment they retrieve some expected results from higher categories, including a theory for adjunctions, the analog of left (right) fibrations, which are called
(contravariant) covariant families
, and the Yoneda lemma for these fibrations. Additional work by Buchholtz and Weinberger
[
BW23
]
studies
synthetic cocartesian fibrations
as a generalization of covariant families. Further treatment can be found in Weinberger’s PhD thesis
[
Wei21
]
, this includes
two-sided fibrations
.

Exploiting results from
[
Shu15
]
it is shown in
[
RS17
]
that sHoTT has a model in bisimplicial sets where Rezk types correspond to complete Segal spaces (also called Rezk spaces).

However, we are not in the position to claim that sHoTT is indeed able to capture all category theory of
(\infty,1)
-categories. For example, the construction of the opposite of an
(\infty,1)
-category is not implemented yet, and we lack of a Yoneda embedding in which an
(\infty,1)
-category is embedded into an
(\infty,1)
-category of presheaves of spaces. Another important problem is that the type of
discrete types
is a
Segal type
that does not coincide with the
\infty
-category of
\infty
-groupoids in the bisimplicial sets model.

Despite the current inherent limitations of the type theory, we have tried to explore which other categorical properties can be obtained with the theory as is. Throughout this paper we intend to present a reasonable theory of limits and colimits of diagrams of
(\infty,1)
-categories. In this setting, we can prove most of the expected properties that are familiar from category theory. Under the mild assumption of the existence of a type-theoretic universe of spaces, we can compute the limit of a space-valued diagram as a dependent product (see
Remark

5.11
).

1.1
Limits and colimits

Limits and colimits have been studied extensively for quasi-categories in
[
Lur09
]
, and for Segal spaces. A short presentation appears in
[
Ras23
]
. We introduce the definitions of limits and colimits within this synthetic theory and verify that they are consistent with the current definition for Rezk spaces in the bisimplicial model of sHoTT. We also prove some expected results like
Proposition

3.7
, which can be phrased as the universal property of colimits. We also present the interaction between limits and colimits with
adjoints
,
Theorem

3.8
shows that “right adjoints preserve limits.”

We prove in
Corollary

4.6
that in a Rezk type the
type of all limits
of a given diagram is a proposition. This can be understood as a uniqueness property. Lastly, in
Section

5
we show that in an appropriate sense any limit of spaces can be computed simply as a dependent product. The goal is to replicate the fact that for any diagram of spaces
G:I\to\infty\text{-}\mathbf{Gpd}
where
I
is a set,
\lim_{I}G=\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}
. The difficulty carrying this computation in sHoTT is that we are unable to construct the correct type of spaces (discrete types) within the theory. We implement this using a directed formulation of the Univalence Axiom due to Cavallo, Riehl, and Sattler which allows to assume the existence of a type with the desired properties.

The reader will appreciate that the study of limits and colimits in the synthetic setting is relatively simple. Our only prerequisite is some knowledge of Homotopy Type Theory. This is in line with the goal of having a synthetic theory of
\infty
-categories where these are somewhat basic objects out of which we can effortlessly develop a robust synthetic theory.

2
Extension types, Segal types and covariant families

In this section we introduce the necessary material about the type theory that we need in the paper. We refrain from providing detailed proofs. The best reference for this is the original paper
[
RS17
]
where simplicial Homotopy Type Theory was first introduced. We indicate precisely where each result can be found in
Ibidem
.

2.1
Extension types

Essentially, simplicial Homotopy Type Theory is ordinary Homotopy Type Theory augmented with some axioms postulating the existence of a strict interval object
\mathbbm{2}
. That is, a type
\mathbbm{2}
equipped with a total order, a smallest element
0
and a distinct largest element
1
. In a general type
X
, an arrow in
X
can be defined as a map
f:\mathbbm{2}\to X
, with the source and target of
f
being the image of the endpoints
0,1:\mathbbm{2}
. However, one would like to be able to talk about the family of hom types from
x
to
y
, for
x,y:X
. This would not be achievable in ordinary type theory, it can be done thanks to a new type former called an
extension type
. However, the inner workings of extension types force us to single out the interval
\mathbbm{2}
by putting it in a separate layer of a layered type theory.

More concretely, simplicial type theory is built as a three layer type theory: the layer of
cubes
, the layer of
topes
, and the layer of
types
. All rules for each layer can be found in greater detail in our main reference
[
RS17
]
, here we just give a small account together with some of the fundamental results we will need.

The first layer is a type theory with finite products of types. Some rules of this type theory include the following:

\textup{{1}}\textup{ cube}\quad\quad\Gamma\vdash\star:\textup{{1}}

I\times J\textup{ cube}.\lx@proof@logical@and I\,\textup{cube}J\,\textup{cube}

The second layer is an intuitionistic logic over the layer of cubes. Its types are called
topes
. Topes can be regarded as polytopes embedded in a cube. They admit operations of finite conjunction and disjunction, but negation, implication or quantifiers are not part of this theory. The complete rules for topes can be found in
[
RS17
, Figure 2]
. There is a “tope equality” that appears in the third layer as a “strict equality.” For example, this tope equality is used to define some
shapes
below, and it is denoted with the symbol “
\equiv
.”

In simplicial type theory, the
cubes
layer is given as follows: It starts by postulating the cube
\mathbbm{2}
, which has two elements
0:\mathbbm{2}
and
1:\mathbbm{2}
. This cube also comes with a inequality tope

(x\leq y)\,\textup{{tope}}\lx@proof@logical@and x:\mathbbm{2}y:\mathbbm{2}.

In addition, there are axioms that turn the inequality tope into a total order relation on
\mathbbm{2}
with distinct endpoints
0
and
1
. Recall that these axioms are:

\vdash x\leq xx:\mathbbm{2}\quad\quad(x\leq y),(y\leq z)\vdash(x\leq z)x:\mathbbm{2},y:\mathbbm{2},z:\mathbbm{2}\quad\quad(x\leq y),(y\leq x)\vdash(x\equiv y)x:\mathbbm{2},y:\mathbbm{2}

\vdash(x\leq y)\vee(y\leq x)x:\mathbbm{2},y:\mathbbm{2}\quad\quad\vdash(0\leq x)x:\mathbbm{2}\quad\quad\vdash(x\leq 1)x:\mathbbm{2}\quad\quad\vdash\bot(0\equiv 1).

The rest of the cubes are generated by finite products of the cube
\mathbbm{2}
. Using cubes and topes we can introduce
shapes
as follows:

\{t:I\>|\>\phi\}\quad\textup{{shape}}.\lx@proof@logical@and I\quad\textup{{ cube}}t:I\vdash\phi\quad\textup{{tope}}

Some relevant shapes are:

\Delta^{0}:\equiv\{t:\textup{{1}}|\top\},

\Delta^{1}:\equiv\{t:\mathbbm{2}|\top\},

\Delta^{2}:\equiv\{\langle t_{1},t_{2}\rangle:\mathbbm{2}\times\mathbbm{2}|t_{2}\leq t_{1}\},

\partial\Delta^{1}:\equiv\{t:\mathbbm{2}|(t\equiv 0)\vee(t\equiv 1)\},

\partial\Delta^{2}:\equiv\{\langle t_{1},t_{2}\rangle:\Delta^{2}|(0\equiv t_{2}\leq t_{1})\vee(t_{1}\equiv t_{2})\vee(t_{2}\leq t_{1}\equiv 1)\},

\Lambda_{1}^{2}:\equiv\{\langle t_{1},t_{2}\rangle:\Delta^{2}|(t_{1}\equiv 1)\vee(t_{2}\equiv 0)\}.

More can be said about this strict interval: The category of simplicial sets is the classifying topos for such strict intervals
[
MM94
]
. Furthermore, as explained in
[
RS17
]
, by embedding simplicial sets into the category of bisimplicial sets it is possible to show that the later presents the “classifying (
\infty
,1)-topos” of strict intervals.

Finally, there is a third layer of types that has all the ordinary type formers of Homotopy Type Theory and one additional type former, the
extension type
, that involves the previous layers. Its formation rule is:

\Xi\>|\>\Phi\>|\>\Gamma\vdash\left\langle\prod_{t:I\>|\>\psi}A(t)\middle|_{a}^{\phi}\right\rangle\,\textup{{type}}.\lx@proof@logical@and\Xi\>|\>\Phi\vdash\Gamma\,\textup{{context}}\{t:I\>|\>\phi\}\,\textup{{shape}}\Xi,t:I\>|\>\Phi,\psi\>|\>\Gamma\vdash A\,\textup{{type}}\{t:I\>|\>\psi\}\,\textup{{shape}}\Xi,t:I\>|\>\Phi,\phi\>|\>\Gamma\vdash a:At:I\>|\>\phi\vdash\psi

We refer to
[
RS17
]
for the precise formulation of its rules. The name extension types is suggestive on how to construe them. We can think of
\{t:I\>|\>\phi\}
as a “sub-shape” of
\{t:I\>|\>\psi\}
and read the judgment
\Xi,t:I\,\,|\,\,\Phi,\phi\>|\>\Gamma\vdash a:A

as a function
\phi\rightarrow A
. We could represent a point in an extension type with a dashed arrow in the commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi.}

This dashed arrow does not have to be unique in any sense. The benefit of this diagrammatic representation of extension types will be obvious later on when we introduce
Segal types
. Note that the
hom
type in
Definition

2.6
is a special case of an extension type. As noted in
[
BW23
]
, it is conceivable to obtain a similar theory by considering HoTT plus a strict interval theory, and replace extension types using identity types (see
[
RS17
, Pag. 119]
and
[
BW23
, Section 2.4]
), but this makes the theory harder to use. In this paper we will follow the original Riehl-Shulman formulation of sHoTT using extension types.

2.1.1
Relative function extentionality axiom

In Homotopy Type Theory we use the function extensionality axiom for dependent functions. We recall this formulation.

Axiom 2.4
.

Let
B:A\to\mathcal{U}
be a dependent type family over a type
A
. For
f,g:\prod_{a:A}B(a)
, the canonical map
f=g\to\prod_{a:A}(fa=ga)
is an equivalence.

Note that the equality on the left is in the dependent product. An element of
\prod_{a:A}(fa=ga)
is usually refer to as a homotopy, and often this type is written as
f\sim g
. There is a version of function extensionality for extension types.

Axiom 2.5
.

Suppose
t:I\;|\;\phi\vdash\psi
and that
A:\{t:I\;|\;\psi\}\to\mathcal{U}
is such that each
A(t)
is contractible, and moreover there is an element
a:\prod_{t:I\;|\;\phi}A(t)
, then
\left\langle\prod_{t:I\;|\;\psi}A(t)|_{a}^{\phi}\right\rangle
is contractible.

Further discussion and different formulations on this axiom can be found in
[
RS17
, 4.4]
. Moreover, taking
\phi
to be
\bot
in
2.5
gives us function extensionality. In the paper, we assume
2.5
, hence we also have function extensionality, and we use the notation
f\sim g
from above.

2.2
Segal types

With extension types and simplices at hand it is feasible to introduce “morphisms” of a type as “points” in a “hom space.” The coherences that
witness “composition” between these morphisms are elements in another suitable type. We can use the above to define
Segal types
.

Let us recall first that from the rules presented in
[
RS17
, Figure 3]
it is possible to prove the following: Given a type
A
, in order to construct an element
a:A
in context
\partial\Delta^{1}
we can just give two elements
x,y:A
, and vice versa, any two elements
x,y:A
determine another element
a:A
in context
\partial\Delta^{1}
. Similarly, the construction of an element
a:A
in context
\partial\Delta^{2}
is equivalent to give three terms
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}:A
in context
t:\mathbbm{2}
such that
a_{0}[0/t]\equiv a_{1}[0/t],

a_{0}[1/t]\equiv a_{2}[0/t]
and
a_{1}[1/t]\equiv a_{2}[1/t]
. For more details on this we refer to
[
RS17
, Section 3.2]
. The last construction can be seen as the “boundary of a triangle in
A
”, see
Definition

2.7
below.

Definition 2.6
.

Given
x,y:A

[x,y]:A
in context
\partial\Delta^{1}

\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y):\equiv\left\langle\Delta^{1}\rightarrow A\middle|_{[x,y]}^{\partial\Delta^{1}}\right\rangle
.

A term in this type is called an
arrow
in
A
.

Note that this is just an extension type where the type family has constant value
A

f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)
has the property that
f(0)\equiv x
and
f(1)\equiv y
. Thus, such an element captures the idea of what an arrow in
A
from
x
to

f

for such term. This composition of arrows is associative whenever it is defined. For any type
A
we can define
\textup{{id}}_{x}:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,x)

\textup{{id}}_{x}(t)\equiv x
for all

[
RS17
, Section 5.2 and 5.3]
). We can also get a useful characterization of Segal types:

Theorem 2.9
.

(
[
RS17
, Theorem 5.5]
)
A type
A
is a Segal type if and only if the restriction map

(\Delta^{2}\to A)\to(\Lambda_{1}^{2}\to A)

is an equivalence.

Moreover, this allows to show that if
A:X\rightarrow\mathcal{U}

X
such that for all

the type
A(x)
is a Segal type then the dependent product of the family
A
over
X
is again a Segal type
[
RS17
, Corollary 5.6]
. Plainly this means that the dependent product of Segal types is again a Segal type.

Let
A,B
be types and
\phi:A\rightarrow B
a function. For any
x,y:A
there is an induced function

\phi_{\#}:\hom_{A}(x,y)\rightarrow\hom_{B}(\phi(x),\phi(y))

defined by precomposition. We use
\phi
for this induced function instead of
\phi_{\#}
when there is no risk of confusion. We have:

Proposition 2.10
.

Any function
\phi:A\rightarrow B
between Segal types preserves identities and composition. This is, for all
a,b,c,d:A
and
f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,b),\,g:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(b,c)
we have
\phi(g\circ f)=\phi(g)\circ\phi(f)
and
\phi(\textup{{id}}_{d})=\textup{{id}}_{\phi(d)}
.

Proof.

See
[
RS17
, Proposition 6.1]
.
∎

This proposition makes patent the functorial behaviour of functions between Segal types. From
Theorem

2.9
the type
of functions
A\rightarrow B
is a Segal type when
B
is itself a Segal type and
A
is a shape or type. Given two functions
f,g:A\rightarrow B
we can select
a term
\alpha:\textup{{hom}}_{A\rightarrow B}(f,g)
, spelling out the definition of such term we get for all
s:\mathbbm{2}
a function

\alpha(s):A\rightarrow B
such that
\alpha(0)\equiv f
and
\alpha(1)\equiv g
. Additionally, for all
a:A
we have

\lambda(s:\mathbbm{2}).\alpha(s)(a):\textup{{hom}}_{B}(fa,ga).

We denote this function as
\alpha_{a}
and we refer to it as the component of
\alpha
at
a:A
. An element
\alpha:\textup{{hom}}_{A\rightarrow B}(f,g)
is called

natural transformation
between
f
and
g
. Such terminology is supported by the following remarks:

Remark 2.11
.

Let
A
be a type or shape,
B
a Segal type and
f,g:A\rightarrow B
. Then the induced function

\textup{{hom}}_{A\rightarrow B}(f,g)\rightarrow\prod\limits_{a:A}\textup{{hom}}_{B}(fa,ga)

is an equivalence
[
RS17
, Proposition 6.3]
. And also
[
RS17
, Proposition 6.5]
, if
\alpha:\textup{{hom}}_{A\rightarrow B}(f,g),\beta:\textup{{hom}}_{A\rightarrow B}(g,h)

and
x:A
then

(\beta\circ\alpha)_{x}=\beta_{x}\circ\alpha_{x}\text{ and }(\textup{{id}}_{f})_{x}=\textup{{id}}_{f(x)}.

This is to say that natural transformations are component-wise defined and their composition is performed point-wise.

Remark 2.12
.

Under the same assumptions of the previous remark, if
\alpha:\textup{{hom}}_{A\rightarrow B}(f,g)
and
k:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)
then
\alpha_{y}\circ fk=gk\circ\alpha_{x}
.

Hence we can say that a natural transformation is truly natural in the categorical sense.

2.2.1
Discrete types

Definition 2.13
.

[
RS17
, Definition 7.1]

For any type
A
, there is map

\mathsf{idtoarr}:\prod_{x,y:A}(x=_{A}y)\to\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)

given by path induction
\mathsf{idtoarr}_{x,x}(\textup{{refl}}_{x}):\equiv\textup{{id}}_{x}
. We say that the type
A
is discrete if
\mathsf{idtoarr}_{x,y}
an equivalence for all
x,y:A
.

The first aspect to note about discrete types is that they are also Segal types
[
RS17
, Proposition 7.3]
.

2.2.2
Rezk types

In the category of bisimplicial sets, Segal spaces are, intuitively, bisimplicial sets containing categorical and homotopical information. This data is not necessarily compatible. Complete Segal spaces fix this problem. More precisely, adopting the terminology and notation of
[
Rez01
]
, we have
X_{\text{hoequiv}}
as the
space of homotopy equivalences
of
X
. This space
X_{\text{hoequiv}}
is contained in
X_{1}
and consist of
homotopy equivalences
. Then there is an obvious map
u:X_{0}\to X_{\text{hoequiv}}
, if
u
is an equivalence of spaces we say that the Segal space is
complete
. The map
u
is not always a weak equivalence, an example of such Segal space can be found in
[
Ras18
, Example 2.57]
. A similar phenomenon occurs for Segal types. These have an associative, unital composition but are not “univalent” just as Segal spaces are not necessarily complete. To amend this problem and obtain “better behaved” types we need to introduce
Rezk types
.

Let
A
be a Segal type and
f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)
, we define the type

\textsf{{isiso}}(f):\equiv\left(\sum\limits_{g:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(y,x)}g\circ f=\textup{{id}}_{x}\right)\times\left(\sum\limits_{h:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(y,x)}f\circ h=\textup{{id}}_{y}\right)

and say that
f
is an
isomorphism
if this type is inhabited. The reader might note that we do not ask for the left and right inverse of an isomorphism to be equal. The reason behind this can be found in
[
RS17
, Section 10.1]
There is an immediate characterization of isomorphisms:

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
A
be a Segal type and
f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)
. Then
f
is an isomorphism if and only if we have
g:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(y,x)
with
g\circ f=\textup{{id}}_{x}
and

f\circ g=\textup{{id}}_{y}
.

Proof.

This is
[
RS17
, Proposition 10.1]
.
∎

The type
\textsf{{isiso}}(f)
is a proposition
[
RS17
, Proposition 10.2]
, hence we can define the type of isomorphisms between any
two terms
x,y
in a Segal type
A
as

x\cong y:\equiv\sum\limits_{f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)}\textsf{{isiso}}(f).

Given a Segal type
A
and
x:A
is clear that
\textup{{id}}_{x}
is an isomorphism. We get a family of functions

\textup{{idtoiso}}_{A}:\prod\limits_{x,y:A}\left(x=y\rightarrow x\cong y\right)

constructed by path induction via
\textup{{idtoiso}}_{A}(\textup{{refl}}_{x}):\equiv\textup{{id}}_{x}
. Although the function
\textup{{idtoiso}}_{A}
is dependent of the type
A
, we will drop
A
from the notation since it is always clear over which type the function is defined.

Definition 2.15
.

A Segal type
A
is a
Rezk type
if
idtoiso
is an equivalence. We denote the inverse by
rezk
.

We will need the following fact about Rezk types from
[
RS17
, Proposition 10.9]

Proposition 2.16
.

If
B
is a Rezk type then for any type or shape
X
, the type
B^{X}
is a Rezk type.

2.3
Covariant families

Left fibrations were introduced by Joyal in
[
Joy08b
]
, and further studied in
[
Lur09
]
. These are the quasi-categorical version of functors cofibered in groupoids. In turn, the category of bisimplicial sets also have a well-known theory of left fibrations (see
[
Ras23
]
). In sHoTT we study them as

covariant families
, and they will be an important tool in later sections.

Let
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
be a type family over a type
A
. Two elements
x,y:A
,
f:\textup{{hom}}_{a}(x,y)
and
u:C(x),v:C(y)
define the type

\textup{{hom}}_{C(f)}(u,v):\equiv\left\langle\prod\limits_{t:\mathbbm{2}}C(f(t))\middle|_{[u,v]}^{\partial\Delta^{1}}\right\rangle.

This type can be thought of as the type of arrows from
u
to
v
over
f
in the total type of
C
.

Definition 2.17
.

A type family
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
over a Segal type
A
is
covariant
if for any
f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)
and

u:C(x)
the type

\sum\limits_{v:C(y)}\textup{{hom}}_{C(f)}(u,v)

is contractible. Similarly, if for any
f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)
and
v:C(y)
the type

\sum\limits_{u:C(x)}\textup{{hom}}_{C(f)}(u,v)

is contractible we say that
C
is
contravariant
.

In the situation of the definition above, we denote the center of contraction of each type as
(f_{*}u,\textup{{trans}}_{f,u})
and
(f^{*}v,\textup{{trans}}_{f,v})
, respectively.

From
[
RS17
, Remark 8.3]
we have that if
g:B\rightarrow A
is a function and
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is a covariant type family, then

\lambda b.C(g(b)):B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is also covariant. This just means that a covariant family is stable under substitution.

The first important example of a covariant family is given by the
hom
type (see
[
RS17
, Proposition 8.13]
):

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
A
be a type and fix
a:A
. Then the type family
\lambda x.\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,x):A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is covariant if and only if
A
is a Segal type.

We can show that if
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is a covariant family over a Segal type
A
then the total type of
C

\sum\limits_{x:A}C(x)

is a Segal type
[
RS17
, Theorem 8.8]
. Also, as previously advertised, if the covariant family is defined over a Segal type then it is functorial in the following sense:

Proposition 2.19
.

Suppose
A
is a Segal type and
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is covariant. Then given
f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)
,
g:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(y,z)
, and
u:C(x)
, we have

g_{*}(f_{*}u)=(g\circ f)_{*}u
and
(\textup{{id}}_{x})_{*}u=u
.

Proof.

This is
[
RS17
, Proposition 8.16]
.
∎

Furthermore, naturality holds:

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
C,D:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
be two covariant families and a fiber-wise map
\phi:\prod\limits_{x:A}C(x)\rightarrow D(x)
.
Then for any
f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(x,y)
and
u:C(x)
,

\phi_{y}(f_{*}u)=f_{*}(\phi_{x}(u)).

Proof.

See
[
RS17
, Proposition 8.17]
.
∎

If we have a covariant family
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
over a Segal type, a path
p:x=y
and
u:C(x)
then the element

(\textup{{idtoiso}}(p))_{*}u:C(y)
can be understood as transporting
u
along the arrow
\textup{{idtoiso}}(p)
. The covariant transport and the usual type-theoretic transport along paths coincide, by this we mean that they are propositional equal:

Lemma 2.21
.

If
A
is Segal and
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is covariant, while
e:x=_{A}y
, then for any
u:C(x)
we have

\textup{{idtoiso}}(e)_{*}u=\textup{{transport}}^{C}(e,u)
.

Proof.

[
RS17
, Lemma 10.7]
.
∎

2.3.1
Yoneda lemma

The Yoneda lemma is with no doubt a fundamental result of category theory. In the context of
\infty
-categories it has been proved for different models, such as, for quasi-categories in
[
Lur09
]
,
[
Joy08a
]
, for Segal spaces in
[
Ras23
]
and
\infty
-cosmoi in
[
RV17
]
. We can think of the result in simplicial type theory, as commented in
[
RS17
]
, as a directed version of path transport in type theory. Also, there is a dependent version of Yoneda lemma which can be seen as the analogous to path induction for covariant families.
Although we will need only one of its consequences, namely
representability
, there is no harm if we quickly review it in full generality.

Let
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
a covariant family and a fix term
a:A
. Then there are functions

\textup{{evid}}_{a}^{C}:\equiv\lambda\phi.\phi(a,\textup{{id}}_{a}):\left(\prod\limits_{x:A}\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,x)\rightarrow C(x)\right)\rightarrow C(a),

\textup{{yon}}_{a}^{C}:\equiv\lambda u.\lambda x.\lambda f.f_{*}u:C(a)\rightarrow\left(\prod\limits_{x:A}\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,x)\rightarrow C(x)\right).

Theorem 2.22
.

(Yoneda lemma,
[
RS17
, Theorem 9.1]
)
If
A
is a Segal type, then for any covariant family
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
and
a:A
the functions
\textup{{yon}}_{a}^{C}
and
\textup{{evid}}_{a}^{C}
are mutual inverses.

We have the dependent version of Yoneda:

Theorem 2.23
.

(
[
RS17
, Theorem 9.5]
)
If
A
is a Segal type,
a:A
and
C:\prod\limits_{x:A}(\textup{{hom}}_{a}(a,x)\rightarrow\mathcal{U})
is covariant then the function

\textup{{evid}}_{a}^{C}:\equiv\lambda\phi.\phi(a,\textup{{id}}_{a}):\left(\prod\limits_{x:A}\prod_{f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,x)}C(x,f)\right)\rightarrow C(a,\textup{{id}}_{a})

is an equivalence.

One particular instance of this equivalence is when
C:\equiv\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a^{\prime},-)
, where
A
is a Segal type and
a^{\prime}:A
, gives the so-called
Yoneda embedding
. The concept of representability is closely related to this embedding.

Definition 2.24
.

A covariant family
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
over a Segal type is
representable
is there exist
a:A
and a family of equivalences:

\prod\limits_{x:A}(\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,x)\simeq C(x))
.

To finish this section we state the result that for us is of special interest:

Definition 2.25
.

A term
b:B
is
initial
if for any
x:B
the type
\textup{{hom}}_{B}(b,x)
is contractible. That is, if the type

\textup{{isinitial}}(b):\equiv\prod\limits_{x:B}\textup{{iscontr}}(\textup{{hom}}_{B}(b,x))

is inhabited.
Also, a term
b:B
is
terminal
if for any
x:B
the
type
\textup{{hom}}_{B}(x,b)
is contractible. That is, if the type

\textup{{isterminal}}(b):\equiv\prod\limits_{x:B}\textup{{iscontr}}(\textup{{hom}}_{B}(x,b))

is inhabited.

In a Segal type initial and terminal elements are unique up to isomorphism.

Proposition 2.26
.

Let
A
be a Segal type. The following is true:

1.

If
a:A
is an initial element, then it is unique up to isomorphism.

2.

If
a:A
is a terminal element, then it is unique up to isomorphism.

Proof.

Assume that
A
is a Segal type and let
a,b:A
be initial elements. The types
\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,b),\textup{{hom}}_{A}(b,a)
are contractible, in particular, inhabited say by
f,g
, respectively. We get the terms
f\circ g:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(b,b),g\circ f:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,a)
and
\textup{{id}}_{b}:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(b,b),\textup{{id}}_{a}:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,a)
. Again, these hom types are contractible so
f\circ g=\textup{{id}}_{b}
and
g\circ f=\textup{{id}}_{a}
. By
Proposition

2.14

f

is an isomorphism between
a
and
b
. Therefore, initial terms in Segal types are unique up to isomorphism. Similarly, a terminal element is unique up to isomorphism.
∎

Proposition 2.27
.

A covariant type family
C:A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
over a Segal type
A
is representable if
and only if there exists an initial element
(a,u)
in the type
\sum\limits_{x:A}C(x)
, in which case

\textup{{yon}}_{a}^{C}(u):\prod\limits_{x:A}(\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,x)\rightarrow C(x))

defines an equivalence.

Proof.

This is
[
RS17
, Proposition 9.10]
.
∎

The majority of the results we have cited are stated in terms of covariant families. However, for contravariant families we have similar results. The proofs for these statements are completely analogous.

3
Limits and colimits

There is a well-known path to define limits and colimits in
\infty
-categories, as in
[
Lur09
]
for quasi-categories or in Segal spaces
[
Ras23
]
. This strategy
is first to establish an adequate notion of cones and cocones. The second step is to define initial and terminal objects. Then a
limit
is a “terminal cone” and a
colimit
is an “initial cocone.” We follow the same approach to define them in sHoTT using the machinery of covariant families.

For
A,B
types and
b:B
define the constant function

\triangle b:\equiv\lambda a.b:A\rightarrow B.

Remark 3.1
.

If
B
is a Segal type then by
Theorem

2.9
,
B^{A}:\equiv A\rightarrow B
is also a Segal type. Let
f:A\rightarrow B
, from
Proposition

2.18
and the fact that covariance and contravariance are stable under substitution (precomposition) then the type families
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda b.\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(f,\triangle b):B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{U}}

and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda b.\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(\triangle b,f):B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{U}}

are covariant and contravariant, respectively.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
f:A\rightarrow B
be a function. Define the type of
cocones
of
f
as the type

\textup{{cocone}}(f):\equiv\sum\limits_{b:B}\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(f,\triangle b),

and the type of
cones
of
f
as the type

\textup{{cone}}(f):\equiv\sum\limits_{b:B}\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(\triangle b,f).

Observation 3.3
.

From
Remark

2.11
it follows that

\textup{{cocone}}(f)\simeq\sum\limits_{b:B}\prod\limits_{a:A}\textup{{hom}}_{B}(fa,b)

and

\textup{{cone}}(f)\simeq\sum\limits_{b:B}\prod\limits_{a:A}\textup{{hom}}_{B}(b,fa).

We can recognize the type of cones and cocones of a function as direct analogues to cones and cocones in category theory. In
Section

6.1
we will verify how the definition above, and the following, are consistent with the semantics.

Definition 3.4
.

A
colimit
for
f:A\rightarrow B
is an initial term of the type:

\textup{{cocone}}(f)\equiv\sum\limits_{x:B}\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(f,\triangle x).

A
limit
for
f:A\rightarrow B
is an terminal term of the type:

\textup{{cone}}(f)\equiv\sum\limits_{x:B}\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(\triangle x,f)

Remark 3.5
.

Since the total space of a covariant family over a Segal type is a Segal type then from
Remark

3.1
it follows that the type of cocones of a function

f:A\to B
is a Segal type. Similarly, we also get that the type of cones is a Segal type. We will make use of these two facts where needed without explicit mention.

We can show the following:

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
B
be a Segal type and
f:A\rightarrow B
is a function. Limits and colimits are unique up to a unique isomorphism if they exist.

Proof.

It follows immediately from
Remark

3.5
and
Proposition

2.26
above.
∎

We will obtain in
Corollary

4.6
uniqueness property up to equality under the additional assumption that
B
is a Rezk type. The following characterization is more flexible in terms of computations. We can think of it as the universal property of limits and colimits.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
B
a Segal type and a function

. There exists a colimit
(b,\beta)
for
f
if and only if

\prod\limits_{x:B}(\textup{{hom}}_{B}(b,x)\simeq\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(f,\triangle x))
.

Similarly, there exists a limit
(a,\alpha)
for
f
if and only if

\prod\limits_{x:B}(\textup{{hom}}_{B}(x,a)\simeq\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(\triangle x,f))
.

Proof.

The existence of a colimit
(b,\beta)
for the function
f

i.e.
(b,\beta)
is an initial term of the type
\sum\limits_{x:B}\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(f,\triangle x)
,
by
Proposition

2.27
is equivalent to say that the covariant family

\textup{{hom}}_{B^{A}}(f,\triangle-):B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}

is representable. The proof for the second part is similar.
∎

In the situation of

, it is common practice to say that “

” is the colimit and that “

” is the limit of
f:A\to B
, respectively. This leaves implicit the datum of the cocone and the cone, respectively.

3.1
Preservation of limits and colimits

The main goal of this section is to reproduce the classical result that limits are preserved under right adjoints. The same argument will show that left adjoints preserve colimits.
Different presentation of adjunction are studied in
[
RS17
]
, namely
transposing
and
diagrammatic
adjunctions, and
it is shown that they are equivalent. The first type of adjunction is the analogous definition of adjunction in category theory
in terms of hom sets, whereas the second resembles the triangle identities. For us, it will be enough to know that a
quasi-transposing adjunction
between types

A,B
consists of functions
f:A\rightarrow B
and
u:B\rightarrow A
and a family of maps

\phi:\prod\limits_{a:A,b:B}\textup{{hom}}_{B}(fa,b)\rightarrow\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,ub)

with quasi-inverses

\psi:\prod\limits_{a:A,b:B}\textup{{hom}}_{A}(a,ub)\rightarrow\textup{{hom}}_{B}(fa,b)

and homotopies

\xi:\prod_{a,b,k}\phi_{a,b}(\psi_{a,b}(k))=k,\quad\zeta:\prod_{a,b,l}\psi_{a,b}(\phi_{a,b}(l))=l.

In this situation
u
, together with the data above is a
quasi-transposing right adjoint
for
f
. Using this notation we have:

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
A,B
be Segal types and functions
g:J\rightarrow B
,
f:A\rightarrow B
,
u:B\rightarrow A
. If
g
has a limit
(b,\beta)
and
u
is a quasi-transposing right adjoint of
f
, then
(u(b),u\beta)
is a limit for
ug:J\rightarrow A
.

Proof.

Denote by

D:\equiv\textup{{hom}}_{A^{J}}(\triangle-,ug):A\rightarrow\mathcal{U}\text{ and }C:\equiv\textup{{hom}}_{B^{J}}(\triangle-,g):B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}.

Consider the term
(u(b),u\beta):\textup{{cone}}(ug)
. For any
(y,\alpha):\textup{{cone}}(ug)
we have:

\textup{{hom}}_{D(k)}(\alpha,u\beta)\equiv\left\langle\prod\limits_{s:\mathbbm{2}}D(k(s))\middle|_{[\alpha,u\beta]}^{\partial\Delta^{1}}\right\rangle

and

\prod\limits_{s:\mathbbm{2}}D(k(s))\equiv\prod\limits_{s:\mathbbm{2}}\textup{{hom}}_{A^{J}}(\triangle k(s),ug)\simeq\prod\limits_{s:\mathbbm{2}}\prod\limits_{j:J}\textup{{hom}}_{A}(k(s),ug(j)).

For any
h:\textup{{hom}}_{B}(f(y),b)
and
k:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(y,ub)
we have a map:

\textup{{hom}}_{D(k)}(\alpha,u\beta)\rightarrow\textup{{hom}}_{C(h)}(f\alpha,\beta)

defined by
\lambda l.\lambda s.\lambda j.\psi_{k(s),g(j)}(l(s)(j))
. And also

\textup{{hom}}_{C(h)}(f\alpha,\beta)\rightarrow\textup{{hom}}_{D(k)}(\alpha,u\beta)

is defined by
\lambda m.\lambda s.\lambda j.\phi_{m(s),g(i)}(m(s)(j))
. These maps compose to the identities because of the coherence given by

\xi
and
\zeta
, thus

\textup{{hom}}_{D(k)}(\alpha,u\beta)\simeq\textup{{hom}}_{C(h)}(f\alpha,\beta).

Hence:

\displaystyle\textup{{hom}}_{\textup{{cone}}(ug)}((y,\alpha),(u(b),u\beta))

\displaystyle\simeq\sum\limits_{k:\textup{{hom}}_{A}(y,ub)}\textup{{hom}}_{D(k)}(\alpha,u\beta)

\displaystyle\simeq\sum\limits_{h:\textup{{hom}}_{B}(fy,b)}\textup{{hom}}_{C(h)}(f\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\simeq\textup{{hom}}_{\textup{{cone}}(g)}((fy,f\alpha),(b,\beta))

The third equivalence follows from the adjunction and the equivalence above. This last type is contractible since
(b,\beta)
is a limit for
g
.
In conclusion,
(u(b),u\beta)
is a limit for
ug
.
∎

We also have:

Corollary 3.9
.

If
A,B
are Segal types and
f:A\rightarrow B
has left quasi-transposing adjunct then
f
preserves colimits of functions
g:J\rightarrow A
.

Proof.

Similar to the previous theorem.
∎

5
Limit of spaces as dependent product

Let
\{G_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of
\infty
-groupoids indexed by a set
I
. Denote by
G
to the obvious diagram
I\to\infty\text{-}\textbf{Gpd}
then we have that

\lim_{I}\,G_{i}=\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}.

We can think for simplicity that the calculation above takes place in bisimplicial sets
i.e.
this is a family of complete Segal spaces which are homotopically constant. Or could also be happening in simplicial sets.

Given the previous result, it seems natural to expect that a similar result is true in sHoTT. However, such computation is not straightforward: The obstruction is that in our type theory we do not have an
\infty
-category of
\infty
-groupoids in which we can take this limit. The way we go around this issue is by using the notion of
univalent covariant families
originally due to Cavallo-Riehl-Sattler
[
CRS
]
to sufficiently axiomatize the properties of the
\infty
-category of spaces.

Let
B
a Segal type. For any covariant family
E:B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
and
a,b:B
, using the notation of
Definition

2.17
we obtain a function

\textup{{arrtofun}}:\textup{{hom}}_{B}(a,b)\rightarrow\big(E(a)\rightarrow E(b)\big)

where for any
f:\textup{{hom}}_{B}(a,b)
we set

\textup{{arrtofun}}(f):\equiv f_{*}\equiv\lambda u.f_{*}u.

Definition 5.1
.

Given
E:B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
a covariant family over a Segal type
B
. We say that
E
is
univalent
if for all
a,b:B

the map
arrtofun
is an equivalence. Denote its inverse by

\textup{{dua}}:\big(E(a)\rightarrow E(b)\big)\rightarrow\textup{{hom}}_{B}(a,b).

We can understand the type
B
as satisfying a “directed univalent axiom.” The prime example of a
univalent fibration
in the semantics should be the
\infty
-category of
\infty
-groupoids. In
[
RS17
, Remark A.27]
it is shown that covariant families correspond to left fibrations. Cavallo, Riehl, and Sattler announced in
[
CRS
]
(unpublished) the existence of a fibrant universe (bisimplicial set) of left fibrations. This implies the consistency of
Definition

5.1
, see also
Remark

5.11
.

The result in
[
RS17
, Proposition 8.18]
shows that if
E:B\to\mathcal{U}
is a covariant type family over a Segal type
B
, then for any
b:B
the type
E(b)
is discrete. Furthermore, if
E
is univalent we could think of
B
as a “Segal type that has terms discrete types.” We will make this idea precise in
Corollary

5.5
. In this part of the paper, we make use freely of the extensionality axiom, and the notation introduced in
Section

2.1.1
. In particular, we use
f\sim g
introduced there.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
B
a Segal type and
E:B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
a univalent covariant family and
a,b:B
. If
\delta:E(a)\rightarrow E(b)
is an equivalence then
\textup{{dua}}(\delta)
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

By
Proposition

2.19
for any
x:B
and
u:E(x)
we have
(\textup{{id}}_{x})_{*}(u)=u
. Therefore,
\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{id}}_{x})=\textup{{id}}_{E(x)}
. This also implies that

\textup{{dua}}(\textup{{id}}_{E(x)})=\textup{{dua}}(\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{id}}_{x}))=\textup{{id}}_{x}.

Treat
\delta:E(a)\rightarrow E(b)
as a bi-invertible function so there is
\gamma:E(b)\rightarrow E(a)
such that
\delta\gamma\sim\textup{{id}}_{E(b)}
and
\gamma\delta\sim\textup{{id}}_{E(a)}
.
From the extensionality axiom we can further assume
\delta\gamma=\textup{{id}}_{E(b)}
and
\gamma\delta=\textup{{id}}_{E(a)}
. Hence,
\textup{{dua}}(\delta\gamma)=\textup{{id}}_{b}
and
\textup{{dua}}(\gamma\delta)=\textup{{id}}_{a}
.
Observe that again from
Proposition

2.19
, for any
f:\textup{{hom}}_{B}(x,y)
,
g:\textup{{hom}}_{B}(y,z)
, and
u:E(x)
,
we have
g_{*}(f_{*}u)=(g\circ f)_{*}u
, and as a consequence

\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{dua}}(\delta)\circ\textup{{dua}}(\gamma))=\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{dua}}(\delta))\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{dua}}(\gamma)))=\delta\gamma=\textup{{id}}_{E(b)}

it follows that
\textup{{dua}}(\delta)\circ\textup{{dua}}(\gamma)=\textup{{dua}}(\delta\gamma)=\textup{{id}}_{b}
. Similarly

\textup{{dua}}(\gamma)\circ\textup{{dua}}(\delta)=\textup{{dua}}(\gamma\delta)=\textup{{id}}_{a}.

Which concludes the proof of the statement.
∎

Definition 5.3
.

Given a type
A
and a covariant family
E:B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
over a type
B
, we define the type

\textup{{issmall}}^{E}_{B}(A):\equiv\sum\limits_{b:B}\big(E(b)\simeq A\big).

We say that
A
is
B
-small if
\textup{{issmall}}^{E}_{B}(A)
is inhabited.

A related notion appears in
[

, Definition 17.1.3]
where they define what it means for a type

to be small with respect to a univalent universe
\mathcal{U}
; A type

is said to be
\mathcal{U}
-

if there is type

and an equivalence between

and

. The same definition appear in
[

, Section 2.19]
under the name

\mathcal{U}
-small
. From our definition, since we require a covariant family, we can express whether a discrete type

is
\mathcal{U}
-small. This is because if
A:\mathcal{U}
and
E:\textup{{1}}\to\mathcal{U}
is a covariant family with value

, then
A
must be discrete. And it is obvious that
A
is
\mathcal{U}
-small. A more general and concrete example of this can be seen in the semantics. Given a Grothendieck universe

, one could build a sub-universe

(an
\infty
-category) which consists of
\kappa
-small
\infty
-groupoids, where
\kappa
is a regular cardinal. By construction, elements of

are exactly the

-small elements of

. Of course, this would be one way to justify the consistency of
Definition

5.1
.

The idea in

5.3
is that
B
has an element representing the type
A
in question. We can think of
A
as “a type in
B
”, hence the name
B
-small. To make sense of this, we need to note some simple facts. The first lemma simply computes
\textup{{transport}}^{D}
for the type family
D:B\to\mathcal{U}
as defined below.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
E:B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
be a univalent covariant family over a Rezk type
B
. For a type
A
, define
D:B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
as
D(b):\equiv E(b)\simeq A
.
Then for any
b,b^{\prime}:B
,
p:b=b^{\prime}
and
\sigma:E(b)\simeq A

\textup{{transport}}^{D}(p,\sigma)=\sigma(\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(p^{-1}))).

Proof.

By path induction we can assume that
p\equiv\textup{{refl}}_{b}:b=b
. In this case

\textup{{transport}}^{D}(\textup{{refl}}_{b},\sigma)\equiv\textup{{id}}_{D(b)}(\sigma)=\sigma.

Whereas on the right-hand side we have

\displaystyle\sigma(\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(p^{-1})))

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma(\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(\textup{{refl}}_{b})))

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma(\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{id}}_{b}))

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma\textup{{id}}_{E(b)}

\displaystyle=\sigma

hence we can simply use
\textup{{refl}}_{\sigma}:\sigma=\sigma
.
Additionally, path induction also shows that for any
p:b=b^{\prime}

\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(p^{-1}))=\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(p))^{-1}

since if
p\equiv\textup{{refl}}_{b}
then both sides of the equality are
\textup{{id}}_{E(b)}
. Here,
\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(p))^{-1}
abusively denotes an inverse for
\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(p))
.
∎

This has the following consequence:

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
E:B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
be a univalent covariant family over a Rezk type
B
. Then for any type
A
,
\textup{{issmall}}^{E}_{B}(A)
is a proposition.

Proof.

Let
(b,\sigma),(b^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}):\sum\limits_{b:B}\big(E(b)\simeq A\big)
. We have

\big((b,\sigma)=(b^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\big)\simeq\sum\limits_{p:b=b^{\prime}}\sigma=^{p}\sigma^{\prime}

therefore it is enough to give a term of the right-hand side. We realize
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
as a bi-invertible functions, let
\delta:A\rightarrow E(b)
,
\delta^{\prime}:A\rightarrow E(b^{\prime})
be their inverses, respectively. We get an equivalence
\delta^{\prime}\sigma:E(b)\simeq E(b^{\prime})
, by
Lemma

5.2

\textup{{dua}}(\delta^{\prime}\sigma):b\cong b^{\prime}
. Since
B
is a Rezk type,
\textup{{rezk}}(\textup{{dua}}(\delta^{\prime}\sigma)):b=b^{\prime}
. From the
Lemma

5.4
above and the fact that
((\textup{{arrtofun}})(\textup{{idtoiso}}))((\textup{{rezk}})(\textup{{dua}}))=\textup{{id}}_{E(b^{\prime})^{E(b)}}
we obtain:

\displaystyle\textup{{transport}}^{D}(\textup{{rezk}}(\textup{{dua}}(\delta^{\prime}\sigma)),\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(\textup{{rezk}}(\textup{{dua}}(\delta^{\prime}\sigma))^{-1}))

\displaystyle=\sigma(\textup{{arrtofun}}(\textup{{idtoiso}}(\textup{{rezk}}(\textup{{dua}}(\delta^{\prime}\sigma))))^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\delta^{\prime}\sigma)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\delta\sigma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\textup{{id}}_{A}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}

where again
(\delta^{\prime}\sigma)^{-1}
denotes an inverse for
\delta^{\prime}\sigma
, but since
\delta\sigma^{\prime}
is also an inverse these are equal. ∎

Remark 5.6
.

In the situation of
Corollary

5.5
, if a type
A
is
B
-small then
\textup{{issmall}}^{E}_{B}(A)
is contractible. This entails the existence of a center of contraction
(b_{0},\sigma_{0})
where
b_{0}
is a term of type
B
and
\sigma_{0}
is an equivalence between
E(b_{0})
and
A
. Hence,
A
is uniquely related to a term in
B
.

Therefore, given a directed univalent family of types

, one can think of

as being the
\infty
-category of “small spaces”, or at least a full subcategory of it. However, note that we do not assume that
B
is closed under limits. The next proposition shows that as long as a dependent product of small types is itself small, it corresponds to a limit in
B
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
B
a Rezk type, a function
f:D\rightarrow B
and assume that
E:B\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is a univalent covariant family such that
\prod\limits_{d:D}E(f(d))
is
B
-small. If
(b_{0},\sigma_{0})
is the center of contraction of
\textup{{issmall}}^{E}_{B}(\prod\limits_{d:D}E(f(d)))
then
b_{0}
is the limit for the function
f
.

Proof.

Indeed, using
Proposition

3.7
it is enough
to show that for all
b:B

\textup{{hom}}_{B}(b,b_{0})\simeq\textup{{hom}}_{B^{D}}(\triangle b,f).

For the right-hand side, we have:

\textup{{hom}}_{B^{D}}(\triangle b,f)\simeq\left(\prod\limits_{d:D}\textup{{hom}}_{B}(b,f(d))\right)\simeq\left(\prod\limits_{d:D}E(b)\rightarrow E(f(d))\right)

The last equivalence follows because

is univalent. For the same reason together with the assumption
\sigma_{0}:E(b_{0})\simeq\prod\limits_{d:D}E(f(d))

the left-hand side is equivalent to

\big(E(b)\rightarrow E(b_{0})\big)\simeq\left(E(b)\rightarrow\prod\limits_{d:D}E(f(d))\right).

And certainly we have the equivalence

\left(E(b)\rightarrow\prod\limits_{d:D}E(f(d))\right)\simeq\left(\prod\limits_{d:D}E(b)\rightarrow E(f(d))\right).

∎

6
The bisimplicial sets semantics of sHoTT

In this this section, we verify that our synthetic definitions of limit an colimit are semantically correct. We use the fact the semantics of sHoTT is the category of bisimplicial sets
\mathbf{ssSet}.

In
[
RS17
, Appendix A]
it is shown that sHoTT can be interpreted in the category
\mathbf{ssSet}
. Let us first recall the following result from
[
Shu15
]
:

Theorem 6.1
.

For any elegant Reedy category
\mathcal{C}
, the Reedy model structure on
\mathbf{sSet}^{\mathcal{C}^{op}}
supports a model of intentional type theory with dependent sums and products, identity types and as many univalent universes as there are inaccessible cardinals greater than
|\mathcal{C}|
, where
|\mathcal{C}|
is the cardinal of the set of objects of the category

Remark 6.4
.

In

we defined discrete types. However, the terminology might be confusing. According to
[

, Proposition 10.10]
a type

[

gives a similar characterization for complete Segal spaces, such simplicial spaces are

Remark 6.5
.

The interpretation of sHoTT can be done in greater generality. This is essentially a consequence of the main result in

Now we have the semantical overview of sHoTT. This gives us the opportunity to say a few words on the necessity of contrasting the definitions we make in the type theory with the existing ones in the bisimplicial sets model.

Remark 6.6
.

As we mentioned above, sHoTT can be interpreted the category of bisimplicial sets. However, note that a type theoretic statement translates internally to the bisimplicial sets model. A concrete example of this phenomena is the characterization of Segal types. In general, each type is interpreted as a Reedy fibrant bisimplicial set. On the one hand, the equivalence
A^{\Delta^{2}}\to A^{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}
from

is interpreted as a trivial fibration in the Reedy model structure on
\mathbf{ssSet}
. On the other hand, Segal spaces are defined by the Segal condition: A bisimplicial set

is a Segal space if for all

is a trivial fibration in the Fallen-Kan model structure on
\mathbf{sSet}

[

show that both conditions are equivalent.

Informally speaking, an internal statement in the model could be stronger under interpretation in the intended semantics. The results we present in the following sections show that this is not the case

the internal and external notions coincide.
