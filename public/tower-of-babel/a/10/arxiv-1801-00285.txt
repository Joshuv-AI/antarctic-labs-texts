A Light Modality for Recursion
Paula Severi

Abstract
. We investigate a modality for controlling the behaviour of recursive functional programs on infinite structures which is completely silent in the syntax. The latter means that programs do not contain “marks” showing the application of the introduction and elimination rules for the modality. This shifts the burden of controlling recursion from the programmer to the compiler. To do this, we introduce a typed lambda calculus à la Curry with a silent modality and guarded recursive types. The typing discipline guarantees normalisation and can be transformed into an algorithm which infers the type of a program.

1.
Introduction

The quest of finding a typing discipline that
guarantees that functions on coinductive data types
are productive has prompted a variety of works
that rely on a modal operator
[
23
,
18
,
30
,
7
,
1
,
8
,
4
,
13
]
.
Typability in these systems guarantees normalization and non-normalizing programs
such as
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}I}
where
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}I}=\lambda x.x
are not typable.
All these works
except for Nakano’s
[
23
]

have explicit constructors and destructors in the syntax
of programs

[
18
,
30
,
7
,
8
,
13
]
.
This has the advantage that type checking and/or type inference are
easy but it has the disadvantage that
they do not really liberate
the programmer from the task of controlling recursion
since one has
to know when to apply the introduction and elimination rules
for the modal operator.

We present a typed lambda calculus with a temporal
modal operator
\bullet
called
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}

which
has the advantage of having the modal operator silent in
the syntax of programs
The system
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
does not need to make use of
a subtyping relation as Nakano’s.
Even without a subtyping relation,
the modal operator is still challenging to deal with because types are intrinsically
non-structural, not corresponding to any expression form in the
calculus.

The modal operator
\bullet
, also called
the delay operator
,
indicates that the data on the recursive calls
will only be available later.
Apart from the modal operator, we also include guarded
recursive types
which generalize the recursive equation

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{t}=t\times\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{t}

for streams

[
18
,
30
]
.
This allows us to type productive functions
on streams such as

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}skip}~xs=\langle\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fst}~xs\texttt{,}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}skip}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~xs))\rangle

which deletes the elements at even positions of a stream
using the type
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{{\rm Nature}}\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str2}_{{\rm Nature}}

where

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str2}_{t}=t\times\bullet\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str2}_{t}
.
The temporal modal operator
\bullet
allows to type many productive functions
which are rejected by the proof assistant Coq

[
9
]
.

Lazy functional programming is acknowledged as a paradigm that fosters
software modularity
[
14
]
and enables programmers to specify
computations over possibly infinite data structures in elegant and
concise ways.
We give some examples that show how modularization
and compositionality can be
achieved using the modal operator.
An important recursive pattern used in functional programming for modularisation
is
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}foldr}

defined by

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}foldr}~f~xs=f~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fst}~xs)~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}foldr}~f~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~xs))

The type of
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}foldr}
which is

(t\to\bullet s\to s)\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{t}\to s
, is telling us
what is the safe way to build functions.
While it is possible to type

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}iterate}~f=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}foldr}~(\lambda xy.{\langle f~x\texttt{,}y\rangle})

by assigning the type
t\to t
to
f
, and assuming
s=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{t}
,
it is not possible to type the unproductive function

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}foldr}~(\lambda xy.{y})
.
This is because
\lambda xy.{y}
does not have type

(t\to\bullet s\to s)
.

In spite of the fact that
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{{\rm Nature}}\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str2}_{{\rm Nature}}

is the type of
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}skip}
with the minimal number of bullets,
it does not give enough information to
the programmer to know that the composition of
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}skip}

with itself is still typeable.
The type inference algorithm for
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
can
infer a more general type for
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}skip}
,
which looks like

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Stream}({N},{X})\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Stream}({N+1},{X})

where

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Stream}({N},{X})=X\times\bullet^{N}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Stream}({N},{X})

and

N
and
X

are integer and type variable, respectively.
It is clear now that from
the above type, a programmer can deduce that
it is possible to do
the composition of
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}skip}
with itself.

2.
The Typed Lambda Calculus with the Delay Modality

This section defines the type discipline for
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
.
Following
[
23
]
, we introduce the
delay
type
constructor
\bullet
, so that an expression of type
\bullet t

denotes a value of type
t
that is available “at the next moment
in time”.
This constructor is key to control recursion and attain normalisation
of expressions.

2.1.
Syntax

The syntax for
expressions
and

types

is given by the following grammars.

\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{\quad\quad\quad}c@{}}\begin{array}[t]{r@{~~}c@{~~}l@{\quad}l}e&::=^{ind}&&\textbf{Expression}\\
&&{x}&\text{(variable)}\\
&|&\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}&\text{(constant)}\\
&|&\lambda{x}.e&\text{(abstraction)}\\
&|&ee&\text{(application)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[t]{r@{~~}c@{~~}l@{\quad}l}t&::=^{coind}&&\textbf{Pseudo-type}\\
&&X&\text{(type variable)}\\
&|&{\rm Nature}&\text{(natural type)}\\
&|&t\times t&\text{(product)}\\
&|&t\to t&\text{(arrow)}\\
&|&\bullet t&\text{(delay)}\end{array}\end{array}

In addition to the usual constructs of the
\lambda
-calculus,
expressions include constants, ranged over by
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}
. Constants are the
constructor for pairs
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}pair}
, the projections
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fst}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}

and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}0}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}succ}\
.
We do not need a primitive constant for the fixed point operator
because it can be expressed and typed inside the language.
Expressions are subject to the usual conventions of the

\lambda
-calculus. In particular, we assume that the bodies of
abstractions extend as much as possible to the right, that
applications associate to the left, and we use parentheses to
disambiguate the notation when necessary.
We write
\langle e_{1}\texttt{,}e_{2}\rangle
in place of

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}pair}~e_{1}~e_{2}
.

The syntax of
pseudo-types
is
defined co-inductively.
A type is
a possibly infinite tree, where each internal node is labelled by a
type constructor
\rightarrow
,

\times
or
\bullet

and has as many children as the arity of the
constructor.
The leaves of the tree (if any) are labelled by basic types which in this case
are type variables or
{\rm Nature}
.
The type variables are needed in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
to express the general type of functions such as the identity.
We use a co-inductive syntax to describe infinite data structures (such
as streams).
The syntax for pseudo-types include the types of the simply typed lambda
calculus (
arrows
and
products
)
and the
delay
type
constructor
\bullet

[
23
]
.

For simplicity, we only include
{\rm Nature}
and type variables as basic types.
One could easily add other basic types such as
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Bool}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Unit}

together with constants for their values.

Definition 2.1

(Types)
.

We say that a pseudo-type
t
is

(1)

regular
if
its tree representation
has finitely many distinct sub-trees;

(2)

guarded
if
every infinite path in its tree
representation has infinitely many
\bullet
’s;

(3)

a
type
if
it is regular and guarded.

The regularity condition implies that we only consider types
admitting a finite representation.
It is equivalent to representing types
with
\mu
-notation and a strong equality
which allows for an infinite number of unfoldings.
This is also called the
equirecursive approach
since it views
types as the unique solutions
of recursive equations
[
11
]

[
25
, Section 20.2]
.
The existence and
uniqueness of a solution for a pseudo-type satisfying condition
1

follow from known
results (see
[
10
]
and also
Theorem 7.5.34 of
[
6
]
). For example, there
are unique pseudo-types
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str}^{\prime}_{{\rm Nature}}
,
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{{\rm Nature}}
, and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
that respectively
satisfy the equations
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str}^{\prime}_{{\rm Nature}}={\rm Nature}\times\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str}^{\prime}_{{\rm Nature}}
,

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{{\rm Nature}}={\rm Nature}\times\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{{\rm Nature}}
, and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}=\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
.

The guardedness condition intuitively means that not all parts of an
infinite data structure can be available at once: those whose type is
prefixed by a
\bullet
are necessarily “delayed” in the
sense that recursive calls on them must be deeper.
For example,
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{{\rm Nature}}
is a type
that denotes streams of natural numbers, where each subsequent element
of the stream is delayed by one
\bullet
compared to its
predecessor. Instead
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str}^{\prime}_{{\rm Nature}}
is not a type:
it
would denote an infinite stream of natural numbers, whose elements are
all available right away.
If the types are written in
\mu
-notation, the guardedness condition means that
all occurrences of
X
in the body
t
of
\mu X.t

are in the scope of
a
\bullet
.

The type
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
is somehow degenerated in that it contains no
actual data constructors. Unsurprisingly, we will see that
non-normalising terms such as

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\Upomega}=(\lambda{x}.{x}\ {x})(\lambda{x}.{x}\ {x})
can
only be typed with
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}

(see Theorem
3.13
). Without condition
2
,

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\Upomega}
could be given any type
since the recursive pseudo-type

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}D}=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}D}\rightarrow t

would become a type.

We adopt the usual conventions regarding arrow types (which associate
to the right) and assume the following precedence among
type constructors:

\to
,
\times
and
\bullet
with

\bullet
having the highest precedence.
Sometimes we will write
\bullet^{n}t
in place of

\smash{\underbrace{\bullet\cdots\bullet}_{n\text{-times}}t}
.

2.2.
Operational Semantics

Expressions reduce according to a standard
call-by-name

semantics:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{}{(\lambda{x}.e_{1})~e_{2}\longrightarrow e_{1}[e_{2}/{x}]}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{r-beta}}{\text{\scriptsize[{r-beta}]}}}\quad\frac{}{\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fst}~{\langle e_{1}\texttt{,}e_{2}\rangle}\longrightarrow e_{1}}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{r-first}}{\text{\scriptsize[{r-first}]}}}\quad\frac{}{\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~{\langle e_{1}\texttt{,}e_{2}\rangle}\longrightarrow e_{2}}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{r-second}}{\text{\scriptsize[{r-second}]}}}\quad\frac{e\longrightarrow f}{\mathrm{E}[e]\longrightarrow\mathrm{E}[f]}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{r-ctxt}}{\text{\scriptsize[{r-ctxt}]}}}\end{array}

where the
evaluation contexts
are

\mathrm{E}::=[~]\mid({\mathrm{E}}~{e})\mid(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fst}~\mathrm{E})\mid(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~\mathrm{E})\mid(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}succ}~\mathrm{E})
.

Normal forms
are defined as usual
as expressions that do not reduce.
The reflexive and transitive closure of
\longrightarrow
is denoted by
\longrightarrow^{*}
.

Note that

\longrightarrow
does not allow to reduce expressions which are inside the body of an abstraction or
component of a pair as the usual
\beta
-reduction of lambda calculus does.
In the standard terminology of lambda calculus,

\longrightarrow
actually corresponds to
weak head reduction
and
the normal forms of
\longrightarrow
are actually called
weak head normal forms
.

2.3.
Type System

First we assume a set
\kappa
containg the types for the constants:

\begin{array}[]{l@{\,\,\,}l}\begin{array}[t]{l@{\!\,\,\,}l@{\!\,\,\,}l}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}pair}&:&t\to s\to t\times s\\
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fst}&:&t\times s\to t\\
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}&:&t\times s\to s\end{array}&\begin{array}[t]{l@{\!\,\,\,}l@{\!\,\,\,}l}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}0}&:&{\rm Nature}\\
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}succ}&:&{\rm Nature}\to{\rm Nature}\end{array}\end{array}

The
type assignment system

\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
is
defined by the following rules.

\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\frac{{x}:t\in\Upgamma}{\Upgamma\vdash{x}:t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{axiom}}{\text{\scriptsize[{axiom}]}}}\qquad\frac{\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}:t\in\kappa}{\Upgamma\vdash\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}:t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{const}}{\text{\scriptsize[{const}]}}}\quad\frac{\Upgamma\vdash e:t}{\Upgamma\vdash e:\bullet t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\bullet$I}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\bullet$I}]}}}\quad\frac{\Upgamma,{x}:\bullet^{n}t\vdash e:\bullet^{n}s}{\Upgamma\vdash\lambda{x}.e:\bullet^{n}(t\to s)}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$I}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$I}]}}}\quad\frac{\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}:\bullet^{n}(t\to s)\ \ \ \Upgamma\vdash e_{2}:\bullet^{n}t}{\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}e_{2}:\bullet^{n}s}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$E}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$E}]}}}\end{array}

The rule
[
\bullet
I
]

introduces the modality.
The rule is unusual in the sense
that the expression remains
the same. We do not have a constructor for
\bullet
at the level
of expressions.

The rule
[
\bullet
I
]
does not have any restrictions, i.e.
if
it is known that a value will only be available with delay
n
, then
it will also be available with any delay
m\geq n
, but not earlier.
Using rule
[
\bullet
I
]
and the recursive type

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}D}=\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}D}\rightarrow t
,
we can derive that the fixed point
combinator

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}=\lambda{y}.(\lambda{x}.{y}\ ({x}\ {x}))(\lambda{x}.{y}\ ({x}\ {x}))

has type
(\bullet t\rightarrow t)\rightarrow t

by assigning
the type
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}D}\rightarrow t

to the first occurrence of
\lambda{x}.{y}\ ({x}\ {x})

and
\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}D}\rightarrow t

to the second one
[
23
]
.

The rules
[
\to
I
]
and
[
\to
E
]

allow for an arbitrary delay in front of the
types of the entities involved. Intuitively, the number of

\bullet
’s represents the delay at which a value becomes
available. So for example, rule
[
\to
I
]
says that a
function which accepts an argument
{x}
of type
t
delayed by

n
and produces a result of type
s
delayed by the same
n
has
type
\bullet^{n}(t\to s)
, that is a function delayed
by
n
that maps elements of
t
into elements of
s
.

2.4.
Inductive data types

The temporal modal operator can only be used for defining co-inductive data types.
Inductive data types need to be introduced separately into the calculus.
For example, we can introduce primitive recursion on
{\rm Nature}

by means of a function

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}natrec}:t\to({\rm Nature}\to t\to t)\to{\rm Nature}\to t

with the reduction rule:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}natrec}~{f_{1}}~f_{2}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}0}\longrightarrow f_{1}\quad\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}natrec}~{f_{1}}~f_{2}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}succ}\ ~e)\longrightarrow f_{2}~e~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}natrec}~{f_{1}}~f_{2}~e)\end{array}

We can also introduce a type
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}List}_{t}
for the set of finite lists as an inductive
data type with
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}nil}:\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}List}_{t}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}consl}:t\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}List}_{t}\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}List}_{t}

and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}lrec}:s\to(t\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}List}_{t}\to s\to s)\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}List}_{t}\to s

with the reduction rule:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}lrec}~{f_{1}}~f_{2}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}nil}\longrightarrow f_{1}\quad\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}lrec}~{f_{1}}~f_{2}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}consl}~e_{1}~e_{2})\longrightarrow f_{2}~e_{1}~e_{2}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}lrec}~{f_{1}}~f_{2}~e_{2})\end{array}

2.5.
Disjoint union

We could add the disjoint union
t_{1}+t_{2}
of two types
t_{1}
and
t_{2}

and add constants:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}inl}:t_{1}\to t_{1}+t_{2}\\
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}inr}:t_{2}\to t_{1}+t_{2}\\
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}case}:(t_{1}+t_{2})\to(t_{1}\to s)\to(t_{2}\to s)\to s\end{array}

the reduction rules:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}case}~{(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}inl}~e)}~{f_{1}}{f_{2}}\longrightarrow f_{1}~e\quad\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}case}~{(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}inr}~e)}~{f_{1}}{f_{2}}\longrightarrow f_{2}~e\end{array}

and the evaluation contexts are extended with
\mathrm{E}::=...\mid\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}case}~{\mathrm{E}}~{f_{1}}{f_{2}}
.

Using the disjoint union, we can define the type of finite and infinite lists as:

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}coList}_{t}=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Unit}+\bullet t\times\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}coList}_{t}

2.6.
Type of conatural numbers

We can also define the type of conatural numbers as
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}coNat}=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Unit}+\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}coNat}
.
Then
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}z}=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}inl}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}unit}
has type
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}coNat}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}s}=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}inr}
has type
\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}coNat}\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}coNat}
.
The set of conatural numbers include
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}s}^{\infty}=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}s}
.
The usual definition of addition given by the equations:

n+0=n\quad n+(m+1)=(n+m)+1

can be written as

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}add}~{x}~{y}=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}case}~{y}~(\lambda{y}_{1}.{{x}})~(\lambda{y}_{1}.{\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}s}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}add}~{x}~{y}_{1})})

and it can be typed in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
.
However, the usual definition of substraction given by the equations

n-0=n\quad 0-n=0\quad(n+1)-(m+1)=n-m

which is expressed as:

3.
Properties of Typable Expressions

This section proves the two most relevant
properties of typable expressions, which are subject reduction
(reduction of expressions preserves their types) and normalisation.
As informally motivated in
Section

2
, the type constructor

\bullet
controls recursion and guarantees normalisation of any
expression that has a type different from
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
.
This section also proves that any typable expression
has a Lèvy-Longo and Böhm tree without
\bot
if the type is
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
-free and
tail finite respectively.

3.1.
Inversion and Subject Reduction

Lemma 3.1

(Weakening)
.

If

\Upgamma\vdash e:t
and
\Upgamma\subseteq\Upgamma^{\prime}

then

\Upgamma^{\prime}\vdash e:t
.

Proof.

By an easy induction on the derivation.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Delay)
.

If

\Upgamma_{1},\Upgamma_{2}\vdash e:t

then

\Upgamma_{1},\bullet\Upgamma_{2}\vdash e:\bullet t
.

Proof.

By induction on the derivation. We only show the
case for the rule
[
\to
I
]
.

\frac{\Upgamma_{1},\Upgamma_{2},{x}:\bullet^{n}t_{1}\vdash e:\bullet^{n}t_{2}}{\Upgamma_{1},\Upgamma_{2}\vdash\lambda{x}.e:\bullet^{n}(t_{1}\to t_{2})}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$I}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$I}]}}}

By induction hypothesis,

\Upgamma_{1},\bullet\Upgamma_{2},{x}:\bullet^{n+1}t_{1}\vdash e:\bullet^{n+1}t_{2}
.
By applying the rule
[
\to
I
]
,
we conclude that

\Upgamma_{1},\bullet\Upgamma_{2}\vdash\lambda{x}.e:\bullet^{n+1}(t_{1}\to t_{2})
.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Inversion)
.

(1)

If
\Upgamma\vdash\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}:t
then

t=\bullet^{n}t^{\prime}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}:t^{\prime}\in\kappa
.

(2)

If
\Upgamma\vdash{x}:t
then

t=\bullet^{n}t^{\prime}
and

{x}:t^{\prime}\in\Upgamma
.

(3)

If

\Upgamma\vdash\lambda{x}.e:t
then

t=\bullet^{n}(t_{1}\to t_{2})
and

\Upgamma,{x}:\bullet^{n}t_{1}\vdash e:\bullet^{n}t_{2}

(4)

If

\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}e_{2}:t
then

t=\bullet^{n}t_{2}
and

\Upgamma\vdash e_{2}:\bullet^{n}t_{1}
and

\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}:\bullet^{n}(t_{1}\to t_{2})

Proof.

By case analysis and induction on the derivation.
We only show
Item

3
.
A
derivation of

\Upgamma\vdash\lambda{x}.e:t

ends with an
application of either
[
\to
I
]
or

[
\bullet
I
]
.
The proofs for
[
\to
I
]
is immediate.
If the last applied rule is
[
\bullet
I
]
then

t=\bullet t^{\prime}
and

\Upgamma\vdash\lambda{x}.e:t^{\prime}
.
By induction,

t^{\prime}=\bullet^{n}(t_{1}\to t_{2})
and

\Upgamma,{x}:\bullet^{n}t_{1}\vdash e:\bullet^{n}t_{2}
.
Hence,

t=\bullet t^{\prime}=\bullet^{n+1}(t_{1}\to t_{2})
and

\Upgamma,{x}:\bullet^{n+1}t_{1}\vdash e:\bullet^{n+1}t_{2}

by
Lemma

3.2
.
∎

Lemma 3.4

(Substitution)
.

If

\Upgamma,{x}:s\vdash e:t
and

\Upgamma\vdash f:s
then

\Upgamma\vdash e[f/{x}]:t
.

Proof.

By induction on the structure of expressions.
We only show the case when
e=\lambda{y}.e^{\prime}
.
It follows from
Item

3
of
Lemma

3.3
that

t=\bullet^{n}(t_{1}\to t_{2})
and

\Upgamma,{x}:s,{y}:\bullet^{n}t_{1}\vdash e^{\prime}:\bullet^{n}t_{2}
.
By induction hypothesis,

\Upgamma,{y}:\bullet^{n}t_{1}\vdash e^{\prime}[f/{x}]:\bullet^{n}t_{2}
.
By applying the rule
[
\to
I
]
,

\Upgamma\vdash\lambda{y}.e^{\prime}[f/{x}]:\bullet^{n}(t_{1}\to t_{2})
.
∎

Lemma 3.5

(Subject Reduction)
.

If

\Upgamma\vdash e:t

and
e\longrightarrow e^{\prime}
then

\Upgamma\vdash e^{\prime}:t
.

Proof.

By induction on the definition of
\longrightarrow
. We only do the case

(\lambda{x}.e_{1})~e_{2}\longrightarrow e_{1}[e_{2}/{x}]
.
Suppose

\Upgamma\vdash(\lambda{x}.e_{1})~e_{2}:t
.
By
Item

4
of
Lemma

3.3
we have that:

t=\bullet^{n}t_{2}\quad\quad\Upgamma\vdash e_{2}:\bullet^{n}t_{1}\quad\quad\Upgamma\vdash(\lambda{x}.e_{1}):\bullet^{n}(t_{1}\to t_{2})

It follows from
Item

3
of
Lemma

3.3
that

\Upgamma,{x}:\bullet^{n}t_{1}\vdash e_{1}:\bullet^{n}t_{2}
.
By applying Substitution Lemma,
we deduce that

\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}[e_{2}/{x}]:\bullet^{n}t_{2}
.
∎

Neither Nakano’s type system nor ours is closed under
\eta
-reduction.
For example,

{y}:\bullet(t\to s)\vdash\lambda{x}.{\mbox{$({y}\ {x})$}}:(t\to\bullet s)

but
{y}:\bullet(t\to s)\not\vdash{y}:(t\to\bullet s)
.
The lack of subject reduction for
\eta
-reduction is natural
in the context of lazy evaluation where programs are closed terms and only
weak head normalised.

3.2.
Normalization

We prove that any expression
which has a type
t
such that
t\not=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}

reduces to a normal form (Theorem
3.13
).
For this, we define a type interpretation indexed on the
set of natural numbers for dealing with the temporal operator

\bullet
.
The time is discrete and represented using the set
of natural numbers.
The semantics reflects the fact that one

\bullet
corresponds to one unit of time
by shifting
the interpretation from
i
to
i+1
.

Before introducing
the type interpretation, we give a few definitions.
Let
\mathbb{E}
be the set of expressions.
We define the following subsets of
\mathbb{E}
:

\begin{array}[]{r@{~}l}\mathbb{WN}&=\{e\mid e\longrightarrow^{*}f\ \&\ f\mbox{ is a normal form}\}\\
\mathbb{HV}&=\{e\mid e\longrightarrow^{*}\mathrm{E}[{x}]\ \&\ {x}\mbox{ is a variable}\}\end{array}

We will do induction on the rank of types.
For
{\rm Nature}
and type variables, the rank is always 0.
For the other types, the rank measures the depth of all what we can observe
at time
0
. We could also compute it by taking
the maximal
0
-length of all the paths in the
tree representation of the type, where the

0
-length of a path is the number
of type constructors different from
\bullet

from the root to a leaf or
\bullet
.

Definition 3.6

(Rank of a Type)
.

The rank
of a type
t
(notation
\mathit{rank}(t)
) is defined as
follows.

\begin{array}[]{r@{~}ll}\mathit{rank}({\rm Nature})&=\mathit{rank}(X)=\mathit{rank}(\bullet t)=0\\
\mathit{rank}(t\times s)&=max(\mathit{rank}(t),\mathit{rank}(s))+1\\
\mathit{rank}(t\rightarrow s)&=max(\mathit{rank}(t),\mathit{rank}(s))+1\end{array}

The rank is well defined (and finite) because
the tree representation of
a type cannot have an infinite branch with
no
\bullet
’s at all
(Condition
2

in Definition
2.1
)
and
\mathit{rank}(\bullet t)
is set to
0
.

We now define the type interpretation

{(\![{t}]\!)}\in\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{E})
, which is
an indexed set, where
\mathbb{N}
is the set of natural numbers and
\mathcal{P}
is the powerset constructor.

Definition 3.7

(Type Interpretation)
.

We define

{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{E}
by induction on

(i,\mathit{rank}(t))
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{(\![{{\rm Nature}}]\!)}_{i}&=&\mathbb{HV}\cup\{e\mid e\longrightarrow^{*}n\}\\[3.0pt]
{(\![{X}]\!)}_{i}&=&\mathbb{HV}\\[3.0pt]
{(\![{t\times s}]\!)}_{i}&=&\mathbb{HV}\cup\{e\mid e\longrightarrow^{*}\langle e_{1}\texttt{,}e_{2}\rangle\mbox{ and }e_{1}\in{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i}\mbox{ and }\ ~e_{2}\in{(\![{s}]\!)}_{i}\}\\[3.0pt]
{(\![{t\to s}]\!)}_{i}&=&\mathbb{HV}\cup\{e\mid e\longrightarrow^{*}\lambda x.f\mbox{ and }ee^{\prime}\in{(\![{s}]\!)}_{j}\ \ \forall e^{\prime}\in{(\![{t}]\!)}_{j},j\leq i\}\\[3.0pt]
&&{}\cup\{e\mid e\longrightarrow^{*}\mathrm{E}[\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}]\mbox{ and }ee^{\prime}\in{(\![{s}]\!)}_{j}\ \ \forall e^{\prime}\in{(\![{t}]\!)}_{j},j\leq i\}\\[3.0pt]
{(\![{\bullet t}]\!)}_{0}&=&\mathbb{E}\\[3.0pt]
{(\![{\bullet t}]\!)}_{i+1}&=&{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i}\end{array}

Note that
{(\![{\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}}]\!)}_{i}=\mathbb{E}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
In the interpretation of the arrow type,
the requirement “for all
j\leq i
” (and not just
“for all
i
”) is crucial
for dealing with the contra-variance of the arrow type in the proof of
Item

2
of
Lemma

3.9
.

Lemma 3.8
.

(1)

{(\![{\bullet^{n}t}]\!)}_{i}=\mathbb{E}
if
i<n
.

(2)

{(\![{\bullet^{n}t}]\!)}_{i}={(\![{t}]\!)}_{i-n}
if
i\geq n
.

Proof.

Both items are proved by induction on
n
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

For all types
t
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1)

\mathbb{HV}\subseteq{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i}
.

(2)

{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i+1}\subseteq{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i}
.

Proof.

(
Item

1
) follows by
induction on
i
and doing case analysis on
the shape of the type.
(
Item

2
) follows by induction on

(i,\mathit{rank}(t))
.
Suppose
e\in{(\![{t\to s}]\!)}_{i+1}
.
Then,

ee^{\prime}\in{(\![{s}]\!)}_{j}

for
j\leq i+1
.
This is equivalent to saying that

ee^{\prime}\in{(\![{s}]\!)}_{j^{\prime}+1}

for
j^{\prime}\leq i
.
By induction hypothesis,
{(\![{s}]\!)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\subseteq{(\![{s}]\!)}_{j^{\prime}}
.
Hence,

e\in{(\![{t\to s}]\!)}_{i}
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

If
t\not=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}

then

\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{WN}
.

Proof.

Suppose
t=\bullet^{n}t_{0}
and
t_{0}

is either
{\rm Nature}
,
X
,
t_{1}\to t_{2}
or

t_{1}\times t_{2}
. Then, for all
i\geq n
,

{(\![{\bullet^{n}t_{0}}]\!)}_{i}={(\![{t_{0}}]\!)}_{i-n}\subseteq\mathbb{WN}

by Lemma
3.8
and the definition of type interpretation.
∎

In order to deal with open expressions we resort
to substitution functions, as usual.
A substitution function is
a mapping from (a finite set of) variables to
\mathbb{E}
. We use
\rho
to range over substitution functions.
Substitution functions allows us to
extend the semantics to typing judgements
(notation
\Upgamma\models_{i}e:t
).

Definition 3.11

(Typing Judgement Interpretation)
.

Let

be a substitution function.

(1)

\rho\models_{i}\Upgamma

if
\rho({x})\in{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i}

for all
{x}:t\in\Upgamma
.

(2)

\Upgamma\models_{i}e:t

if
\rho(e)\in{(\![{t}]\!)}_{i}

for all
\rho\models_{i}\Upgamma
.

As expected we can show the soundness of our type system
with respect to
the indexed semantics.

Theorem 3.12

(Soundness)
.

If
\Upgamma\vdash e:t

then
\Upgamma\models_{i}e:t
for all

.

The proof of the above theorem can be found in
Appendix

Theorem 3.13

(3.1)

for all

&\subseteq{(\![{s}]\!)}_{i}&\mbox{by {\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lemma:wnvar}} of {\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:A}}.}\end{array}

This means that

for all

From

It follows from

Notice that there are normalising expressions that cannot be typed, for example
\lambda x.\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\Upomega}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}I}

Notice also that there are normalizing expressions of type

Lemma 3.14
.

If

and

Proof.

By Theorems

Then,

3.3.
Lévy-Longo and Böhm Trees

A simple way to give meaning to a computation of lambda calculus is to consider
Lévy-Longo and Böhm trees
[
2
,
21
,
22
]
.
Consider, for example, a procedure for computing the decimal expansions of
\pi
;
if implemented appropriately, it can provide partial output as it runs and this ongoing output
is a natural way to assign meaning to the computation. This is in contrast to a program
that loops infinitely without ever providing an output. These two procedures have very different
intuitive meanings.
This section gives a nice characterization of computations that never produce meaningless information by means of types.

In lambda calculus, expressions such as
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}~x
or
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}~(\lambda xy.x)

have no finite normal form though they are intuitively meaningfull
and should be distinguished from meaningless expressions such as
fix~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}I}
.
By analogy with
\pi
,
the Lévy-Longo (as well as the Böhm) tree of a term is obtained as the limit of these partial
outputs.
If in the process of computing the Lévy-Longo or Böhm tree of a term, we find a subexpression
that has no meaning such as
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}I}
then this is recorded by replacing

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}I}
by
\bot
.
Lévy-Longo and Böhm trees differ on the notion of
meaningless expressions: weak head normal forms for the former and
head normal forms for the latter.

Definition 3.15

(Lèvy-Longo Tree)
.

Let
e
be an expression (it may be untypable).
The Lèvy-Longo tree of
e
, denoted as
\mathit{LLT}(e)
is
defined coinductively as follows.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathit{LLT}(e)&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x~\mathit{LLT}(e_{1})\ldots\mathit{LLT}(e_{n})&\mbox{if }e\longrightarrow^{*}x~e_{1}\ldots e_{n}\\
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}~\mathit{LLT}(e_{1})\ldots\mathit{LLT}(e_{n})&\mbox{if }e\longrightarrow^{*}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}~e_{1}\ldots e_{n}\\
\lambda x.\mathit{LLT}(e^{\prime})&\mbox{if }e\longrightarrow^{*}\lambda x.e^{\prime}\\
\bot&\mbox{ otherwise, i.e. }e\mbox{ has no (weak head) normal form }\end{array}\right.\end{array}

Definition 3.16

(Böhm Tree)
.

Let
e
be an expression (it may be untypable).
The Böhm tree of
e
, denoted as
\mathit{BT}(e)
is
defined coinductively as follows.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathit{BT}(e)&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{k}.x~\mathit{BT}(f_{1})\ldots\mathit{BT}(f_{n})&\mbox{ if }\mathit{hnf}(e)=\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{k}.x~f_{1}\ldots f_{n}\\
\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{k}.\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}~\mathit{BT}(f_{1})\ldots\mathit{BT}(f_{n})&\mbox{ if }\mathit{hnf}(e)=\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{k}.\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}~f_{1}\ldots f_{n}\\
\bot&\mbox{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.\end{array}

where
\mathit{hnf}(e)
is the
head normal form
of
e

defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathit{hnf}(e)=&\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{k}.x~f_{1}\ldots f_{n}&\mbox{ if }e=e_{0}\mbox{ and }e_{k}\longrightarrow^{*}x~f_{1}\ldots f_{n}\\
&\mbox{ and }e_{i}\longrightarrow^{*}\lambda x_{i+1}.e_{i+1}\mbox{ for }0\leq i\leq k-1\\
\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{k}.\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}~f_{1}\ldots f_{n}&\mbox{ if }e=e_{0}\mbox{ and }e_{k}\longrightarrow^{*}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}~f_{1}\ldots f_{n}\\
&\mbox{ and }e_{i}\longrightarrow^{*}\lambda x_{i+1}.e_{i+1}\mbox{ for }0\leq i\leq k-1\\
\bot&{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\end{array}

Lévy-Longo and Böhm trees are obtained as possible infinite normal forms
of possible infinite
\beta
-reduction sequences.
Infinitary normalization formalizes the idea of productivity, i.e. an infinite reduction
sequence which always produces part of the result
[
16
,
17
,
30
,
20
]
.
Besides the
\beta
-rule,
the infinitary lambda calculus of Lévy-Longo trees has a reduction rule defined as

M\longrightarrow\bot
if
M
has no weak head normal form while the
one of Böhm trees has a similar rule using the condition that
M
has no head normal form.

Definition 3.17
.

We say that
t
is
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
-free
if it does not contain
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
.

For example,
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{{\rm Nature}}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}E}_{{\rm Nature}}
are

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
-free where

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}E}_{t}=t\to\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}E}_{t}
.
But
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}}
and
{\rm Nature}\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
are not
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
-free.

Theorem 3.18

(Productivity I: Lèvy-Longo Trees without
\bot
)
.

Let
t
and all types in
\Upgamma
be
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
-free.
If
\Upgamma\vdash e:t
then
the Lèvy-Longo tree of

e
has no
\bot
’s.

Proof.

We construct the Lèvy-Longo tree depth by depth.
By
Theorem

3.13
,
e
reduces to a
(weak head) normal form
e_{0}
which is typable by
Lemma

3.5
.
Suppose
e_{0}
is
\lambda x.e^{\prime}

(the cases when
e_{0}=x~e_{1}\ldots e_{n}

or
e_{0}=\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}~e_{1}\ldots e_{n}
are similar).
The Lèvy-Longo tree of
e
contains
\lambda x
at depth
0
.
It only remains to show that we can construct the Lèvy-Longo tree of
e^{\prime}
which will be at depth
n>0
.
It follows from Inversion Lemma that
\Gamma,x:t_{1}\vdash e^{\prime}:t_{2}

and both
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
-free.
Hence, we can repeat the process to obtain the Lèvy-Longo tree of
e^{\prime}
.
∎

Definition 3.19
.

(1)

An
infinite alternation of
\bullet
’s and
\to
’s
is a type of the form:

(2)

We say that

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}
-free and it does not contain

For example,

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}G}={\rm Nature}\to\bullet({\rm Nature}\times\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}G})
is tail finite but

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}F}={\rm Nature}\to\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}F}
is not.

Theorem 3.20

(Productivity II: Böhm Trees without
\bot
)
.

Let all the types in
\Upgamma
and
t
be tail finite.
If
\Upgamma\vdash e:t

’s.

Proof.

By Theorem

, the Lévy-Longo tree of
e
has no
\bot
’s.
Suppose the Lévy-Longo tree of

contains a subtree of the form

\lambda x_{1}\lambda x_{2}\lambda x_{3}.\ldots
.
It is not difficult to prove using
Lemma

3.3
and

3.5

that the type should contain an infinite alternation of

’s and
\to
’s.
∎

Let

. Then

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}E}_{{\rm Nature}}

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}{\bullet^{\infty}}}

For example,
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fib}

4.
Formal Comparision with Other Type Systems

This section stablishes a formal relation between
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
and the typed lambda calculi
of Krishnaswami and Benton
[
18
]
and Nakano
[
23
]
.

4.1.
Embedding the Type System by Krishnaswami and Benton into
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}

The typing rules of

\lambda^{\text{K}B}
defined by Krishnaswami and Benton
[
18
]
are:

\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\frac{}{\Upgamma,{x}:_{i}t\vdash{x}:_{j}t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{axiom}}{\text{\scriptsize[{axiom}]}}}~~j\geq i\qquad\frac{\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}:t\in\kappa}{\Upgamma\vdash\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}:_{i}t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{const}}{\text{\scriptsize[{const}]}}}\quad\frac{\Upgamma\vdash e:_{i+1}t}{\Upgamma\vdash\bullet e:_{i}\bullet t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\bullet$I}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\bullet$I}]}}}\qquad\frac{\Upgamma\vdash e:_{i}\bullet t}{\Upgamma\vdash\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}await}~e:_{i+1}t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\bullet$E}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\bullet$E}]}}}\\
\\
\frac{\Upgamma,{x}:_{i}t\vdash e:_{i}s}{\Upgamma\vdash\lambda{x}.e:_{i}(t\to s)}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$I}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$I}]}}}\qquad\frac{\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}:_{i}(t\to s)\hskip 8.19447pt\Upgamma\vdash e_{2}:_{i}t}{\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}e_{2}:_{i}s}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$E}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$E}]}}}\end{array}

\lambda^{\text{K}B}
has only the recursive types
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{t}=t\times\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{t}

for infinite lists with only one
\bullet
.
Since
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}
cannot be expressed
in
\lambda^{\text{K}B}
, we need to add it to the set
\kappa

of constants:

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}:(\bullet t\to t)\to t

The mapping
\mathit{itob}
replaces the subindex
i
by
\bullet^{i}
in typing contexts.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathit{itob}(\emptyset)&=\emptyset\\
\mathit{itob}(\Upgamma,{x}:_{i}t)&=\mathit{itob}(\Upgamma),{x}:\bullet^{i}t\end{array}

The function
\mathit{clear}
removes the constructor and destructor of

\bullet
from
e
, i.e.

\mathit{clear}(\bullet e^{\prime})=e^{\prime}
and

\mathit{clear}(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}await}~e^{\prime})=e^{\prime}
.

Lemma 4.1

(Embedding
\lambda^{\text{K}B}
into
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
)
.

If
\Upgamma\vdash e:_{i}t
in
\lambda^{\text{K}B}

then

\mathit{itob}(\Upgamma)\vdash\mathit{clear}(e):\bullet^{i}t
in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
.

Proof.

By an easy induction on the derivation. ∎

If only recursive types for lists are available, one cannot create other recursive types such as
trees but having only one bullet
also limits the amount of functions on streams we can type.
For example, the functions
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}skip}
(see
Eq.

2.1
),

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}ones}^{\prime}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}pairup}
are not typable in
\lambda^{\text{K}B}
where

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}ones}^{\prime}=\langle 1\texttt{,}\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}interleave}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}ones}^{\prime}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}ones}^{\prime})\rangle\\
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}pairup}~xs=\langle\langle(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fst}~xs)\texttt{,}(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fst}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~xs))\rangle\texttt{,}(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~(\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}snd}~xs))\rangle\end{array}

The functions
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}skip}
,

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}ones}^{\prime}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}pairup}
are all typeable in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
because
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
has more flexibility
in the location and number of
\bullet
’s which can be inserted in the types.
We can derive that
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}ones}^{\prime}
has type
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{X}
in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}

by assigning the type
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{X}\to\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{X}\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{X}

to
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}interleave}

and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}pairup}
has type
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}StrHalf}_{X}\to\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}Str1}_{X}

where
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}StrHalf}_{X}=X\times X\times\bullet\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}StrHalf}_{X}
.

4.2.
Embedding
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
into the Type System by Nakano

We consider the type system by Nakano of
[
23
]
without
\top

and call it
\lambda_{\text{N}ak}
.

Types in
\lambda_{\text{N}ak}
are finite, written in
\mu
-notation and denoted by

T
,
S
.
Types in
\lambda_{\text{N}ak}
are
guarded
which means that
the type variable
X
of
\mu X.T
can occur free in
T

only under the scope of a
\bullet
.

Let
\simeq
the equivalence between types which allows for an infinite number of unfolding
and

\Sigma
be a set
\{X_{1}\leq Y_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\leq Y_{n}\}
of subtype assumptions
between type variables which cannot contain twice the same variable.
The subtyping relation of

\lambda_{\text{N}ak}
is defined as the smallest relation on types closed under
the following rules:

\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\frac{}{\Sigma\vdash T\leq\bullet T}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\leq\bullet$}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\leq\bullet$}]}}}\quad\frac{}{\Sigma\vdash T\to S\leq\bullet T\to\bullet S}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\bullet\to$}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\bullet\to$}]}}}\quad\frac{}{\Sigma\vdash\bullet T\to\bullet S\leq\bullet(T\to S)}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to\bullet$}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to\bullet$}]}}}\quad\frac{}{\Sigma,X\leq Y\vdash X\leq Y}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{var}}{\text{\scriptsize[{var}]}}}\\
\\
\frac{T\simeq S}{\Sigma\vdash T\leq S}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\simeq$}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\simeq$}]}}}\quad\frac{\Sigma,X\leq Y\vdash T\leq S}{\Sigma\vdash\mu X.T\leq\mu Y.S}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\mu$}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\mu$}]}}}\quad\frac{\Sigma\vdash T\leq S}{\Sigma\vdash\bullet T\leq\bullet S}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\bullet$}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\bullet$}]}}}\quad\frac{\Sigma_{1}\vdash S_{1}\leq T_{1}\hskip 8.19447pt\Sigma_{2}\vdash T_{2}\leq S_{2}}{\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}\vdash T_{1}\to T_{2}\leq S_{1}\to S_{2}}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$}]}}}\end{array}

In
\Sigma\vdash T\leq S
,
we assume that all the free type variables of
T
and
S
are in
\Sigma

and that
the
X
’s cannot occur on the right hand side of
\leq
and
the
Y
’s cannot occur on the left.
The typing rules of
\lambda_{\text{N}ak}
are defined as follows.

\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{\qquad}c@{}}\frac{{x}:T\in\Upgamma}{\Upgamma\vdash{x}:T}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{axiom}}{\text{\scriptsize[{axiom}]}}}&\frac{\Upgamma\vdash e:T\ \ \ T\leq T^{\prime}}{\Upgamma\vdash e:T^{\prime}}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\leq$}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\leq$}]}}}\\
\frac{\bullet\Upgamma\vdash e:\bullet T}{\Upgamma\vdash e:T}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\bullet$ E}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\bullet$ E}]}}}\frac{\Upgamma,{x}:T\vdash e:S}{\Upgamma\vdash\lambda{x}.e:(T\to S)}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$I}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$I}]}}}&\frac{\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}:\bullet^{n}(T\to S)\ \ \ \Upgamma\vdash e_{2}:\bullet^{n}T}{\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}e_{2}:\bullet^{n}S}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$E}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$E}]}}}\end{array}

We define the function
\mathit{nf}_{\!\!\!\mu}

5.

This section gives a denotational semantics
for

where types and expressions are interpreted as

We give a self-contained description of this topos

The

Exponentials

The functor

6.
A Type Inference Algorithm

In this section, we define a
type inference algorithm
for
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
.
Apart from the usual complications
that come from having no type declarations,
the difficulty of finding an appropriate type inference algorithm for

\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}

is due to the fact that
the expressions do not have a constructor and destructor for
\bullet
.
We do not know which sub-expressions need to be delayed as
illustrated by the type derivation of
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}fix}
where the first
occurrence of

(\lambda{x}.{y}\ ({x}\ {x}))

has a different derivation from the second one
since
[
\bullet
I
]
is applied in different places
[
23
]
.
Even worse,
in case a sub-expression has to be delayed, we do not know
how many times needs to be delayed to be able
to type the whole expression.

6.1.
A Syntax-directed Type System

We obtain a syntax-directed type system by eliminating the rule

[
\bullet
I
]

from
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
.
The
type assignment system

(\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow})^{-}
is defined by the following rules.

\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\frac{{x}:t\in\Upgamma}{\Upgamma\vdash{x}:\bullet^{n}t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{axiom}}{\text{\scriptsize[{axiom}]}}}\qquad\frac{\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}:t\in\kappa}{\Upgamma\vdash\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}k}:\bullet^{n}t}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{const}}{\text{\scriptsize[{const}]}}}\quad\quad\frac{\Upgamma,{x}:\bullet^{n}t\vdash e:\bullet^{n}s}{\Upgamma\vdash\lambda{x}.e:\bullet^{n}(t\to s)}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$I}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$I}]}}}\quad\quad\frac{\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}:\bullet^{n}(t\to s)\ \ \ \Upgamma\vdash e_{2}:\bullet^{n}t}{\Upgamma\vdash e_{1}e_{2}:\bullet^{n}s}\quad\textsc{\hypertarget{rule:{$\to$E}}{\text{\scriptsize[{$\to$E}]}}}\end{array}

Lemma 6.1

(Equivalence between
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
and
(\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow})^{-}
)
.

\Upgamma\vdash e:t
in
(\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow})^{-}

iff
\Upgamma\vdash e:t
in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
.

Proof.

The direction from left to right follows by induction on the derivation.
The direction from right to left follows by induction on

using Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Though
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
and
(\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow})^{-}

are equivalent in the sense they type the same expressions, they do not have the same
type derivations. The derivations
of
(\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow})^{-}
are more restrictive than the ones of

\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
since one can only introduce
\bullet
’s at the beginning of the derivation with the

[

]
and
[
const
]
.

6.2.
Meta-types

The type inference algorithm infers
meta-types

which are a generalization of types
where the
\bullet
can be exponentiated
with integer expressions, e.g.

\bullet^{N_{1}}X\rightarrow\bullet^{N_{2}-N_{1}}X
.
The syntax for

is defined below.
A meta-type can contain type variables and
(non-negative) integer expressions with variables.
In this syntax,

\bullet T
is written as
\bullet^{1}T
.
We identify
\bullet^{0}T
with
T
and

\bullet^{E}\bullet^{E^{\prime}}T
with

\bullet^{E+E^{\prime}}T
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\qquad}l@{}}\begin{array}[t]{r@{~~}c@{~~}l@{\quad}l}E&::=^{ind}&&\textbf{Integer Expression}\\
&&N&\text{(integer variable)}\\
&|&\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}n}&\text{(integer number)}\\
&|&E+E&\text{(addition)}\\
&|&E-E&\text{(substraction)}\end{array}\quad\begin{array}[t]{r@{~~}c@{~~}l@{\quad}l}T&::=^{coind}&&\textbf{Pseudo Meta-type}\\
&&X&\text{(type variable)}\\
&|&{\rm Nature}&\text{(natural numbers)}\\
&|&T\times T&\text{(product)}\\
&|&T\to T&\text{(arrow)}\\
&|&\bullet^{E}T&\text{(delay)}\end{array}\end{array}

Definition 6.2
.

We say that

is

if

\bullet

are natural numbers.

We identify a positive pseudo meta-type with the pseudo-type
obtained from replacing the type constructor

in the syntax of pseudo meta-types with

Definition 6.3

(Integer Expression and Type Substitution)
.

Let

Let

.

.

6.7.
Typability and Type Checking

Typability (finding out if the program is typable or not)
in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
can be solved by checking whether

\mathtt{infer}(\mathtt{True},e)\not=\emptyset
.
By
Theorem

6.16
, if

\mathtt{infer}(\mathtt{True},e)=\emptyset
then there is no

t
such that

\vdash e:t
in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
.
By
Theorem

6.16
, if

\mathtt{infer}(e)\not=\emptyset
then there exists

t
such that

\vdash e:t
in
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
.

Type checking is usually an easier problem than type inference.
In the case of
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
(given an an expression
e
and a type
t
check if
\vdash e:t
), type checking
can easily be solved
by inferring the (finite) set of meta-types for
e
and
checking whether one of these meta-types unifies with
t
.

The type inference algorithm is exponential in the size of the input
because of the unification algorithm which branches (case
1
of

Algorithm

1
).
If we are only interested in typability (and not type inference), then the complexity could be reduced to

{\bf NP}
by “guessing the solutions”.
Instead of giving the union of the results
in
Algorithm

1
of
Algorithm

1
, we could
choose one of the options non-deterministically and
call
\mathtt{unify}
only “once”.

Related Work

This paper is an improvement over past typed lambda calculi with a
temporal modal operator like
\bullet
in two respects.
Firstly, we do not need any subtyping relation as in

[
23
]
and secondly programs are not cluttered
with constructs for the introduction and elimination of individuals of
type
\bullet
as in

[
18
,
30
,
19
,
1
,
7
,
8
,
4
,
13
]
.

Another type-based approach for ensuring productivity
are sized types
[
15
]
.
Type systems using size types do not always have neat properties:
strong normalisation
is gained by contracting the fixed point operator
inside a case and they lack the property of
subject reduction
[
28
]
.
Another disadvantage of size types is that they
do not include negative occurrences of the recursion variable

[
15
]
which are useful for some applications
[
31
]
.

The proof assistant Coq does not ensure productivity through
typing but by means of a syntactic guardedness condition
(the recursive calls should be guarded by
constructors)
[
12
,
9
]

which is somewhat restrictive since it rules out
some interesting functions
[
30
,
8
]
.

A sound but not complete type inference algorithm for Nakano’s
type system is presented in
[
27
]
.
This means that the expressions typable by the algorithm
are also typable in Nakano’s system but the converse
is not true.
Though this algorithm is tractable, it is not clear
to which type system it corresponds.

8.
Conclusions and Future Work

The typability problem is trivial in
\lambda\mu

because all expressions are typable
using
\mu X.(X\rightarrow X)
.
In
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
, this problem turns out to be interesting
because it gives us
a way of filtering programs that do not satisfy certain properties, i.e.
normalization, having a Lèvy-Longo or a Böhm tree without
\bot
.
It is also challenging because it involves the generation of
integer constraints.

Due to the high complexity of the type inference algorithm, it will be important
to find optimization techniques (heuristics, use of concurrency, etc) to make it feasible.

It will be interesting to investigate the interaction
of this modal operator with dependent types.
As observed in Section
2
, our type system is not
closed under
\eta
-reduction and this property may be useful for establishing a better
link with the denotational semantics.
We leave the challenge of
attaining a normalising and decidable type system closed under
\beta\eta
-reduction
for future research.
It will also be interesting to investigate ways of extending
\lambda^{\bullet}_{\rightarrow}
to be able
to type the programs
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}get}
and
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}take}
of Section
2.8
.
