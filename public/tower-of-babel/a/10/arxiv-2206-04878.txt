Projecting onto rectangular hyperbolic paraboloids in Hilbert space
Heinz H. Bauschke, Manish Krishan Lal, Xianfu Wang

Abstract
In ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , a hyperbolic paraboloid is a classical saddle-shaped quadric surface. Recently, Elser has modeled problems arising in Deep Learning using rectangular hyperbolic paraboloids in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . Motivated by his work, we provide a rigorous analysis of the associated projection. In some cases, finding this projection amounts to finding a certain root of a quintic or cubic polynomial. We also observe when the projection is not a singleton and point out connections to graphical and set convergence.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification:

Primary 41A50, 90C26; Secondary 15A63, 46C05.

Keywords:

cross, graphical convergence, rectangular hyperbolic paraboloid, projection onto a nonconvex set.

Introduction

Throughout this paper, we assume that

X
is
a real Hilbert space with an inner product

\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
,

and induced norm
\|\cdot\|
, and that

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\{0\}
and
\beta>0
.
Define the
\beta
-weighted norm on
the product space
X\times X\times\mathbb{R}
by

(\forall(x,y,\gamma)\in X\times X\times\mathbb{R})\ \|(x,y,\gamma)\|:=\sqrt{\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}+\beta^{2}|\gamma|^{2}}.

Now define the set

{C_{\alpha}}:=\big\{{(x,y,\gamma)\in X\times X\times\mathbb{R}}~\big|~{\left\langle{x},{y}\right\rangle=\alpha\gamma}\big\}.

(1)

The set
C_{\alpha}
is a special bilinear constraint set in optimization,
and it corresponds to a rectangular (a.k.a. orthogonal)
hyperbolic paraboloid in geometry
[
8
]
.
Motivated by Deep Learning, Elser recently presented in
[
6
]
a formula for
the projection
{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})
when
x_{0}\neq\pm y_{0}
. However, complete mathematical justifications were not presented, and the case when
x_{0}=\pm y_{0}
was not considered.
The goal of this paper is to provide a complete analysis of
{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}
that is applicable to all possible cases.

The paper is organized as follows.
We collect auxiliary results in

Section

2
. Our main result is proved in
Section

3
which also
contains a numerical illustration.
The formula for the projection onto the set
{C_{\alpha}}
is presented in

Section

4
.

As usual, the distance function and projection mapping associated to

{C_{\alpha}}
are denoted by

d_{{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0}):=\inf_{(x,y,\gamma)\in{C_{\alpha}}}\|(x,y,\gamma)-(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})\|

and

{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0}):=\operatorname{argmin}_{(x,y,\gamma)\in{C_{\alpha}}}\|(x,y,\gamma)-(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})\|
,
respectively. We say that
x,x_{0}\in X
are

conically dependent
if there exists
s\geq 0
such that

x=sx_{0}
or
x_{0}=sx
.

2
Auxiliary results

We start with some elementary properties of
{C_{\alpha}}
, and justify the
existence of projections onto these sets.

Proposition 2.1
.

The following hold:

(i)

The set

{C_{\alpha}}
is closed.
If
X
is infinite-dimensional, then
{C_{\alpha}}

is not weakly closed; in fact,
\overline{{C_{\alpha}}}^{\text{\rm weak}}=X\times X\times\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

{C_{\alpha}}
is prox-regular in
X\times X\times\mathbb{R}
. Hence, for every point in
(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})\in{C_{\alpha}}
,
there exists a neighborhood such that the projection mapping
{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}
is single-valued.

Proof
.
Item

(i)
:
Clearly,
{C_{\alpha}}
is closed.
Thus assume that
X
is infinite-dimensional.
By
[
3
, Proposition 2.1]
, for every
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\overline{\{(x,y)\in X\times X|\ \left\langle{x},{y}\right\rangle=\alpha\gamma\}}^{\text{\rm weak}}=X\times X
. Thus,

\displaystyle X\times X\times\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\big(\overline{\{(x,y)\in X\times X|\ \left\langle{x},{y}\right\rangle=\alpha\gamma\}}^{\text{\rm weak}}\times\{\gamma\}\big)

\displaystyle\subseteq\overline{\{(x,y,\gamma)\in X\times X\times\mathbb{R}|\ \left\langle{x},{y}\right\rangle=\alpha\gamma\}}^{\text{\rm weak}}\subseteq X\times X\times\mathbb{R}.

Item

(ii)
:
Set
F\colon X\times X\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\colon(x,y,\gamma)\mapsto\left\langle{x},{y}\right\rangle-\alpha\gamma
.
Then
{C_{\alpha}}=F^{-1}(0)
and
\nabla F(x,y,\gamma)=(y,x,-\alpha)\neq(0,0,0)
because
\alpha\neq 0
.
The prox-regularity of
{C_{\alpha}}
now follows from
[
9
, Example 6.8]
when
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}

or from
[
4
, Proposition 2.4]
in the general case.
Finally, the single-valuedness of the projection locally around every point
in
{C_{\alpha}}
follows from
[
4
, Proposition 4.4]
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

To study the projection onto
{C_{\alpha}}
, it is convenient to introduce

{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}:=\big\{{(u,v,\gamma)\in X\times X\times\mathbb{R}}~\big|~{\lVert u\rVert^{2}-\lVert v\rVert^{2}=2\alpha\gamma}\big\},

(2)

which is the standard form of a rectangular hyperbolic paraboloid.
Define a linear operator
A:X\times X\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow X\times X\times\mathbb{R}
by sending
(u,v,\gamma)
to
(x,y,\gamma)
, where

x=\frac{u-v}{\sqrt{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad y=\frac{u+v}{\sqrt{2}}.

In terms of block matrix notation, we have

\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
\gamma\end{bmatrix}=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}}\right]\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\\
\gamma\end{bmatrix}\Leftrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\\
\gamma\end{bmatrix}=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&0\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}}\right]\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
\gamma\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, we may and do identify
A
with its block matrix representation

A=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{Id}&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},

and we denote the adjoint of
A
by
A^{\intercal}
.
Note that
A
corresponds to a rotation by
\pi/4
about
the
\gamma
-axis.
The relationship between
{C_{\alpha}}
and
{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}
is summarized as follows.

Proposition 2.2
.

The following hold:

(i)

A
is a
surjective isometry (i.e., a unitary operator):
AA^{\intercal}=A^{\intercal}A=\operatorname{Id}
.

(ii)

A{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}={C_{\alpha}}
and
{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}=A^{\intercal}{C_{\alpha}}
.

(iii)

{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}=A{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}A^{\intercal}.

Proof
. It is straightforward to verify
Item

(i)
and
Item

(ii)
.
To show
Item

(iii)
, let
(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})\in X\times X\times\mathbb{R}
.
In view of
Item

(i)
and
Item

(ii)
,
we have

(x,y,\gamma)\in{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})
if and
only if
(x,y,\gamma)\in{C_{\alpha}}
and

\|(x,y,\gamma)-(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})\|=d_{{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})=d_{A{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})=d_{{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}(A^{\intercal}[x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0}]^{\intercal}),

and this is equivalent to

\|A^{\intercal}[x,y,\gamma]^{\intercal}-A^{\intercal}[x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0}]^{\intercal}\|=d_{{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}(A^{\intercal}[x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0}]^{\intercal}).

Since
A^{\intercal}[x,y,\gamma]^{\intercal}\in{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}
, this gives

A^{\intercal}[x,y,\gamma]^{\intercal}\in{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}(A^{\intercal}[x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0}]^{\intercal})
, i.e.,

[x,y,\gamma]^{\intercal}\in A{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}(A^{\intercal}[x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0}]^{\intercal})
.
The converse inclusion is proved similarly.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Exploiting the structure of
{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}
is crucial for showing the existence
of
{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}(u_{0},v_{0},\gamma_{0})
for every

(u_{0},v_{0},\gamma_{0})\in X\times X\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

(existence of the projection)

Let

(u_{0},v_{0},\gamma_{0})\in X\times X\times\mathbb{R}
.
Then the minimization problem

\displaystyle\textrm{minimize}\quad f(u,v,\gamma)

\displaystyle:=\|u-u_{0}\|^{2}+\|v-v_{0}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}|\gamma-\gamma_{0}|^{2}

(3a)

\displaystyle\textrm{subject to}\quad\ h(u,v,\gamma)

\displaystyle:=\lVert u\rVert^{2}-\lVert v\rVert^{2}-2\alpha\gamma=0

(3b)

always has a solution, i.e.,
{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}(u_{0},v_{0},\gamma_{0})\neq\varnothing
.
If
(u,v,\gamma)\in{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}}(u_{0},v_{0},\gamma_{0})
, then

u,u_{0}
are conically dependent,
and
v,v_{0}
are also conically dependent.

Proof
. We only illustrate the case
when
u_{0}\neq 0,v_{0}\neq 0
, since
the other cases are similar.
We claim that the optimization problem is essentially

3
-dimensional. To this end, we expand

\displaystyle f(u,v,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\|u\|^{2}-2\left\langle{u},{u_{0}}\right\rangle+\|u_{0}\|^{2}}+\underbrace{\|v\|^{2}-2\left\langle{v},{v_{0}}\right\rangle+\|v_{0}\|^{2}}+\beta^{2}|\gamma-\gamma_{0}|^{2}.

(4)

The constraint

h(u,v,\gamma)=\lVert u\rVert^{2}-\lVert v\rVert^{2}-2\alpha\gamma=0

means that for the variables
u,v

only the norms
\|u\|
and
\|v\|
matter.
With
\|u\|
fixed, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in Hilbert space
(see, e.g.,
[
7
]
),
shows that
-2\left\langle{u},{u_{0}}\right\rangle
in the left underbraced part of

Eq.

4

will be smallest when

u,u_{0}
are conically dependent.
Similarly, for fixed
\|v\|
, the second underlined part in

f
will be smaller when

v=tv_{0}
for some
t\geq 0
. It follows that the optimization
problem given by
Eq.

3
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\textrm{minimize}\quad g(s,t,\gamma)

\displaystyle:=(1-s)^{2}\|u_{0}\|^{2}+(1-t)^{2}\|v_{0}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}|\gamma-\gamma_{0}|^{2}

(5a)

\displaystyle\textrm{subject to}\quad g_{1}(s,t,\gamma)

\displaystyle:=s^{2}\lVert u_{0}\rVert^{2}-t^{2}\lVert v_{0}\rVert^{2}-2\alpha\gamma=0,\quad s\geq 0,t\geq 0,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}.

(5b)

Because
g
is continuous and coercive, and
g_{1}
is
continuous, we conclude that the optimization problem
Eq.

5

has a solution.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Proposition 2.4
.

The following hold:

(i)

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}=\widetilde{C}\times\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}{C_{\alpha}}=C\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof
.
Item

(i)
:
First we show that
\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}\subseteq\widetilde{C}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Let
(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha},\gamma_{\alpha})\rightarrow(u,v,\gamma)
and
(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha},\gamma_{\alpha})\in{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}
with
\alpha\rightarrow 0
. Then

\|u_{\alpha}\|^{2}-\|v_{\alpha}\|^{2}=2\alpha\gamma_{\alpha}

, so

Next we show

. Let
(u,v,\gamma)\in\widetilde{C}\times\mathbb{R}
,
i.e.,
\|u\|^{2}-\|v\|^{2}=0
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
We consider three cases:

Case 1:
\gamma=0
. Then
(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha},0)=(u,v,0)\in{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}
for
every
\alpha
.

Case 2:
\gamma\neq 0
but
(u,v)=(0,0).
If
\alpha\gamma>0
, take

(u_{\alpha},0,\gamma)
with
\|u_{\alpha}\|^{2}-0=\alpha\gamma
so that

(u_{\alpha},0,\gamma)\in C_{\alpha}
; if
\alpha\gamma<0
, take

(0,v_{\alpha},\gamma)
with
0-\|v_{\alpha}\|^{2}=\alpha\gamma
so that

(0,v_{\alpha},\gamma)\in C_{\alpha}
. Then

\|(u_{\alpha},0,\gamma)-(0,0,\gamma)\|=\|u_{\alpha}\|=\sqrt{|\alpha\gamma|}<\varepsilon,

or

\|(0,v_{\alpha},\gamma)-(0,0,\gamma)\|=\|v_{\alpha}\|=\sqrt{|\alpha\gamma|}<\varepsilon,

if
|\alpha|<\varepsilon^{2}/|\gamma|.

Case 3:
\gamma\neq 0
and
(u,v)\neq(0,0)
.
Take
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that

|\alpha|<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon\|(u,v)\|}{|\gamma|},\frac{\|(u,v)\|^{2}}{|\gamma|}\right\},

and set

\lambda:=\frac{\alpha\gamma}{\|(u,v)\|^{2}}.

Then

|\lambda|=\frac{|\alpha\gamma|}{\|(u,v)\|^{2}}<1.

Now set

u_{\alpha}:=\sqrt{1+\lambda}u,\quad v_{\alpha}:=\sqrt{1-\lambda}v.

Then

\displaystyle\|u_{\alpha}\|^{2}-\|v_{\alpha}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+\lambda)\|u\|^{2}-(1-\lambda)\|v\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda(\|u\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2})=\alpha\gamma,

so that
(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha},\gamma)\in{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}

and

\displaystyle\|(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha},\gamma)-(u,v,\gamma)\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\sqrt{1+\lambda}-1)^{2}\|u\|^{2}+(\sqrt{1-\lambda}-1)^{2}\|v\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(1+\sqrt{1+\lambda})^{2}}\|u\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda})^{2}}\|v\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda^{2}(\|u\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2})}=|\lambda|\|(u,v)\|<\varepsilon.

Item

(ii)
: This follows from
(i)
because that
{C_{\alpha}}=A{\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}
and
C\times\mathbb{R}=A(\widetilde{C}\times\mathbb{R})
and that
A
is an isometry.
See also
[
9
, Theorem 4.26]
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

(Rockafellar–Wets)

Recall that

Armed with

,

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})\in X\times X\times\mathbb{R}
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

If
x_{0}\neq\pm y_{0}
, then

{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},y_{0},\gamma_{0})=\bigg\{\Big(\dfrac{x_{0}-\lambda y_{0}}{1-\lambda^{2}},\dfrac{y_{0}-\lambda x_{0}}{1-\lambda^{2}},\gamma_{0}+\dfrac{\lambda\alpha}{\beta^{2}}\Big)\bigg\}

for a unique
\lambda\in\left]-1,1\right[
that solves the
(essentially) quintic equation

g(\lambda):=\frac{(\lambda^{2}+1)p-2\lambda q}{(1-\lambda^{2})^{2}}-\frac{2\lambda\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}-2\alpha\gamma_{0}=0,

where
p:=2\left\langle{x_{0}},{y_{0}}\right\rangle
and
q:=\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2}+\lVert y_{0}\rVert^{2}.

(ii)

If
y_{0}=-x_{0}\neq 0
, then we have the following:

a) When
\alpha(\gamma_{0}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}})<-\frac{\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{4}
,
then

{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},-x_{0},\gamma_{0})=\bigg\{\Big(\dfrac{x_{0}}{1-\lambda},\dfrac{-x_{0}}{1-\lambda},\gamma_{0}+\dfrac{\lambda\alpha}{\beta^{2}}\Big)\bigg\}

for a unique
\lambda\in\left]-1,1\right[
that solves

g_{1}(\lambda):=\frac{2\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{2\lambda\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+2\alpha\gamma_{0}=0.

b) When
\alpha(\gamma_{0}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}})\geq-\frac{\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{4}
, then

\displaystyle{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},-x_{0},\gamma_{0})=

\displaystyle\quad\bigg\{{\Big(\dfrac{x_{0}}{2}+\dfrac{u}{\sqrt{2}},-\dfrac{x_{0}}{2}+\dfrac{u}{\sqrt{2}},\gamma_{0}-\dfrac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}}\Big)}~\bigg|~{\|u\|=\sqrt{2\alpha\Big(\gamma_{0}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}}\Big)+\frac{\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{2}},~u\in X}\bigg\}

which is a singleton if and only if

\alpha(\gamma_{0}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}})=-\frac{\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{4}
.

(iii)

If
y_{0}=x_{0}\neq 0
,
then we have the following:

a) When
\alpha(\gamma_{0}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}})>\frac{\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{4}
,
then

{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},x_{0},\gamma_{0})=\bigg\{\Big(\dfrac{x_{0}}{1+\lambda},\dfrac{x_{0}}{1+\lambda},\gamma_{0}+\frac{\lambda\alpha}{\beta^{2}}\Big)\bigg\}

\lambda\in\left]-1,1\right[
that solves
the (essentially) cubic equation

g_{2}(\lambda):=\frac{2\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{(1+\lambda)^{2}}-\frac{2\lambda\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}-2\alpha\gamma_{0}=0.

b) When
\alpha(\gamma_{0}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}})\leq\frac{\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle{\thinspace P\thinspace}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace{C_{\alpha}}}(x_{0},x_{0},\gamma_{0})=

\alpha(\gamma_{0}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}})=\frac{\|x_{0}\|^{2}}{4}

(iv)

x_{0}=y_{0}=0
