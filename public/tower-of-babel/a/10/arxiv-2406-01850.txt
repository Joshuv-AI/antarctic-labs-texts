Abstract
Cell-free massive MIMO, where each user equipment is connected to multiple access points, is emerging as an important component for 5G and 6G cellular systems. Accurate channel models based on measurements are required to optimize their design and deployment. This paper presents an extensive measurement campaign for cell-free massive MIMO in an urban environment. A new “virtual access point” technique measures channels between 80 80 UE locations and more than 20,000 20,000 possible microcellular access point locations. Measurements are done at 3.5 3.5 GHz carrier frequency with 350 350 MHz bandwidth. The paper describes the measurement setup and data processing, shows sample results and their physical interpretation, and provides statistics for key quantities such as pathloss, shadowing, delay spread, and delay window. We find pathloss coefficients of 2.9 2.9 and 10.4 10.4 for line-of-sight and non line-of-sight, respectively, where the high line-of-sight coefficient is mainly because larger distance leads to more grazing angle of incidence and thus lower antenna gain in our setup. Shadowing standard deviations are 5.1 / 16.6 5.1/16.6 dB, and root mean squared delay spreads of − 80.6 / − 72.6 -80.6/-72.6 dBs. The measurements can also be used for parameterizing a CUNEC-type model, which will be reported in future work.

II-B

Calibration Procedure

Evaluation of the measurements requires calibration of the sounder. This occurs in two steps: (i) back-to-back calibration, and (ii) antenna calibration. For the former, the output of the transmitter switch is connected not to the uniform linear array, but rather, via a suitable attenuator, to the receiver input (note that the receiver includes the cables and the switch). The transmitter then sends its sounding sequence, and the receiver digitizes this signal, thus establishing the transfer function. Since both transmitter and receiver have 8 antenna ports each, we have a set of
64
back-to-back calibrations.

In a separate step, we calibrated the frequency-dependent antenna gain characteristics of the transmitter antenna, as well as of all the receiver antennas. This calibration was done in the anechoic chamber at University of Southern California, with a precision rotor rotating the antenna in
4^{\circ}
(in azimuth) and
5^{\circ}
(in elevation) steps, while the other link end is a calibrated standard gain antenna, and a vector network analyzer to measure at the different frequencies. For the transmitter antennas, we measured

II-D

Clock drift correction

The movement of the access point may lose the locking of GPS timing signals, pushing the Rubidium clocks at the transmitter and receiver into free-running mode with increased phase drift. We performed a delay domain
2
-stage correction based on the channel status. For line-of-sight channels, we use the locations of access points and user equipments acquired from the synchronized camera recordings from the real-time video recordings to compute the access point-user equipment distance as the true reference to compute the delay correction offset; for non line-of-sight channels, we assume,
based on the line-of-sight measurements, that the phase drift is a linear function of time and compute the correction offset by linearly interpolating between the phases at the first/last LOS point next to a NLOS region. To estimate the residual error, we also perform a linear interpolation in the LOS region, and compare this interpolation against the true phase drift that we obtain as described above. The root mean squared error has an expected value of
0.43
m, and the standard deviation is
0.64
m. The CDF of the correlation distance of the error (i.e., the distance between two points such that the correlation between their distance offsets is
\frac{1}{e}
) from different line-of-sight channels shows a mean value of
10
m and for the
10\%
worst cases a correlation distance of
3
m, see Fig.
4
. This indicates that the (unpredictable) deviations of the clock phase from the linear drift change relatively slowly.

III
Data Processing

This section describes the post-processing of the measured data to extract the channel impulse responses and condensed parameters derived from them.

III-A

Pre-processing of data

The received data are the temporal samples at the receiver, where each burst (receiver cycling through the receiver antennas, lasting for
5.12
ms) is stored as a separate file named with the snapshot index (which can be related to the timestamps and thus locations in post-processing). We first perform a fast Fourier Transformation over the captured time-domain data; since each such file represents a
10
-times repetition of the sounding sequence, we then perform an averaging over those repetitions, resulting in a
10
dB SNR gain. We divide the result by the fast Fourier Transformation of the sounding sequence, providing the “raw” transfer function of the channel
H_{{\rm raw},m,j,k}
, where
m
is the snapshot (time) index,
j
is the index of the receiver, and
k
is the frequency index.

From the digitizer (which has a sampling rate of 1.25 GS/s), we crop the
350
MHz IF band corresponding to the desired
3.325-3.675
GHz passband. The cropped transfer function is then divided by the (cropped) B2B calibration transfer function
H_{{\rm cal},k}
. Note that the current measurements are not directionally resolved and all presented results contain the impact of both the channels and the antennas. Consequently, the transfer functions of both transmitter and receiver antennas, which show some dependence on the considered direction (see, e.g., Fig.
2a
), are contained in the transfer functions. The calibrated transfer function is then filtered with a Kaiser-Bessel filter with parameter
\beta=3
to reduce sidelobes in the delay domain. While the 3-dB bandwidth is reduced by about a factor of
2
through this filtering, it is still larger than the typical band assignment for a 5G operator, i.e.,
100
MHz. The transfer function is then furthermore zero-padded (extending the bandwidth by a factor of
10
), resulting in oversampling of the impulse response (its Fourier transform) as described below; this is important for accurate computation of the delay spread
[
29
]
.

From the calibrated, cropped, filtered, zeroes-extended transfer function
H_{m,j,k}
, we then obtain the impulse response via Inverse Fast Fourier Transformation, and from that the power-delay profile:

\displaystyle P_{{\rm h,}{m,j,\tau}}=\Big|\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{k}\big\{H_{m,j,k}\big\}\Big|^{2}

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\cdot)
denotes the inverse Fourier transform with respect to
k
,
\tau
is the index in the Fourier dual domain (i.e., delay), and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big\{H_{m,j,i}(f)\big\}
represents the complex CIR.
We use here
\tau
to denote any delay indexing scheme, irrespective of whether it is oversampled or not; the meaning is clear from the context.
We then apply small-scale averaged to all the power-delay profiles using a sliding window with a width of
0.5
m (corresponding to
6
wavelengths). Note that this window length is a compromise between residual small-scale fading and the need not to obscure actual changes in the small-scale averaged statistics; in particular, the delay-domain change of a multi-path component within this window should be smaller than the resolvable delay bin.

We subsequently introduce thresholding at a level
\theta_{m^{\prime},j}
(on a dB scale) that is the maximum of a threshold
\theta_{{\rm n},m^{\prime},j}
that is
\Delta_{{\rm n}}
(set to 7 dB) above the average noise level
P_{{\rm n},m^{\prime},j}
of this small-scale fading averaging window
m^{\prime}
, and a threshold that is
\Delta_{{\rm dr}}
(set to 20 dB) below the peak of the power-delay profile in this window:

\displaystyle\theta_{m^{\prime},j}=\max\Big(P_{{\rm n},m^{\prime},j}+\Delta_{{\rm n}},\ \underset{\tau}{\max}\big(P_{{\rm h},\tau}\big)-\Delta_{{\rm dr}}\Big)

(2)

We furthermore apply delay gating to the impulse response, such that all delay bins corresponding to a distance of
>343
m are set to zero. This was done since, in the considered geometry, no significant multi-path components can be expected for longer delays.
The combined operation of noise thresholding and delay gating can be written as
[
30
]

\displaystyle P_{\rm h}^{\rm DG;NT}(\tau)=\Big[P_{\rm h}(\tau):(\tau\leq\tau_{\rm gate})\cap(P_{\rm h}(\tau)\geq\theta_{m^{\prime},j})\Big]

(3)

A further important processing step is the elimination of impulse response precursors. In our measurements, we observed significant energy arriving
before
the line-of-sight component (whose delay could be easily determined from the geometrical location of access point and user equipment). These precursors were not wraparound components (this could be excluded from the fact that such components would require a run length of more than
2
km). Rather, they are direct “talk through” from the transmitter antenna into the receiver device, without the detour via the receiver antenna and cable. This explanation was confirmed by the fact that the precursors showed up at all user equipments irrespective of their locations, and were furthermore exactly at the delay (which changed with transmitter location) corresponding to the distance between the transmitter antenna and the receiver sounder box. After having confirmed the source of the precursors, we could thus simply eliminate them in post-processing, i.e., all components arriving more than a “guard interval” (accommodating the Kaiser pulse corresponding to the LOS component) of 4 delay bins before the LOS component are set to zero. Note that this issue did not occur in NLOS situations, due to the large separation between transmitter and receiver box.

Definition of parameters

III-B
1
Pathloss and shadowing

The path gain, which is the inverse of the pathloss, is computed as

\displaystyle PG=\sum_{\tau}P_{\rm h}(\tau)

(4)

For the path gain we furthermore perform an averaging over a window of size
20
wavelength; such a larger window (compared to the window used for eliminating the SSF in the PDP) is permissible here because multi-path components moving from one resolvable delay bin to another is not a concern anymore; it has furthermore the advantage of better averaging out residual small-scale fading fluctuations.

We will further use an
\alpha-\beta
model to describe the pathloss as a function of distance between AP and UE:

\displaystyle PL_{\rm dB}(d)=\alpha\cdot 10\log_{10}(d)+\beta+S

(5)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are the linear fitting coefficients, and
S
is a random variable describing shadowing; it is commonly assumed to be zero-mean Gaussian random variable (i.e., lognormal on a linear scale), and is characterized by its shadowing distribution. We will parameterize this model from our measurements because it is widely used for system simulations of cell-free massive MIMO
[
4
]
. However, it is worth noting that it does not reproduce important effects in urban microcells. The CUNEC model recently introduced by us
[
23
]
is better suited for such systems, but is more complex, so that its parameterization from our measurement campaign is left for future work.

We finally note that although the distance between access points can be non-uniform due to acceleration/deceleration of the cherry picker, the density changes are small. Thus, there is no need to perform a
weighted
pathloss model fitting as proposed in
[
31
,
32
]
.

2
Delay dispersion parameters

The delay spread is the most common condensed parameter for the description of the delay dispersion. It is defined as the second central moment of the power-delay profile

\displaystyle\sigma_{\tau}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\tau}P_{\rm h}(\tau)\tau^{2}}{\sum_{\tau}P_{\rm h}(\tau)}-\Bigg(\frac{\sum_{\tau}P_{\rm h}(\tau)\tau}{\sum_{\tau}P_{\rm h}(\tau)}\Bigg)^{2}}~.

(6)

The delay spread depends significantly on the dynamic range. In order to avoid distortions of the statistics, we therefore only consider those power-delay profile windows that have
20
dB dynamic range for the computation of the delay spread statistics.

While the delay spread is commonly used, it gives excessive weight to long-delayed multi-path components. From a system design point of view, a better measure is the Q-window and the Q-tap number
[
5
, Chapter 6]
. Defining a threshold
\gamma=\frac{SIR_{\rm ISI}}{SIR_{\rm ISI}+1}
(where
SIR_{\rm ISI}
is the desired signal-to-intersymbol-interference ratio) the Q-window is defined as

Q_{\rm win}=\underset{x}{\min}~x,~s.t.~\underset{T_{0}}{\max}\sum_{\tau=T_{0}}^{T_{0}+x}P_{\rm h}(\tau)\geq\gamma PG.

(7)

We furthermore define the Q-tap number, using the
sorted
power in the resolvable delay bins
PDP_{\rm sort}(\tau_{m})
as:

Q_{\rm tap}=\underset{x}{\min}~x,~s.t.~\sum_{m=1}^{x}PDP_{\rm sort}(\tau_{m})\geq\gamma PG,

(8)

Q-window describes the length of an equalizer with contiguously-placed taps (or cyclic prefix in an OFDM system) that is necessary to achieve a certain
SIR_{\rm ISI}
. Q-tap describes the number of taps in an equalizer (or Rake fingers in a Rake receiver) that can place the taps at arbitrary delays.
7
7

7

By length of an equalizer, we mean the length of the impulse response that the equalizer has to compensate. This may be different from the actual number of taps used in the linear filter that constitutes the equalizer
[
33
]
.

VI
Conclusions

We have performed an extensive measurement campaign for determining the pathloss and delay dispersion of wideband cell-free massive MIMO channels. The dataset encompasses channels between more than
20,000
access point locations and
80
user equipment locations, which is orders of magnitude larger than previous multi-BS outdoor measurements. After describing the measurement setup that is based on a combination of virtual and switched array, we presented sample results that explain the peaks in the measured power-delay profiles in terms of the surrounding geometry and the specific propagation processes leading to each peak. Observations of the evolution of the multi-path components from the plot of the power-delay profile over time further confirm the measurement results and show the significant variations with the time/location of the access points. Finally, we extracted the statistics of the “classical” channel models, namely slope and intercept of the
\alpha-\beta
model, shadowing standard deviation, delay spread, Q-win, and Q-tap.

The sample results give insights not only into the propagation channels, but also how cell-free massive MIMO systems would perform with different densities of access points, and number of access points belonging to one cluster. The differences in pathloss between line-of-sight and non line-of-sight are significant, namely up
50
dB (for the same distance AP-), indicating that deployment should be dense enough to guarantee that each user equipment has line-of-sight to at least one access point. Obstruction of the line-of-sight by vegetation is also a significant factor. At the same time, the strong difference between line-of-sight and non line-of-sight indicates that interference from nodes that have non line-of-sight might be limited, which might enable smaller access point clusters connected to each user equipment. It is also remarkable that not only the channel transfer function, but also the power-delay profile, and thus the frequency correlation function, can differ significantly for the links from one user equipment to different access points, which might complicate acquisition and exploitation of second-order channel statistics.

Finally, the traditional channel statistics such as
\alpha-\beta
pathloss model and delay spread are valuable for system simulations when the underlying generic channel model is based on those specific quantities, as is the case in most theoretical investigations of cell-free massive MIMO systems. However, the experimental results in this paper confirm that there are significant variations between statistics of different streets, and that the long-term correlations must be taken into account on a per-street basis. Future work will thus focus on the development of a wideband channel model that can properly quantify these effects, generalizing our CUNEC approach that we previously introduced for the modeling of pathloss.
