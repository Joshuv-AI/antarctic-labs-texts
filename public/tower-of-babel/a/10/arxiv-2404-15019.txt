Analytical prediction for the steady-state behavior of a confined drop with interface viscosity under shear flow Preprint: APS/123-QED
F. Guglietta, F. Pelusi

Abstract
The steady-state behavior of a single drop under shear flow has been extensively investigated in the limit of small deformation and negligible inertia effects. In this work, we combine the calculations proposed by Flumerfelt [R. W. Flumerfelt, J. Colloid Interface Sci. 76 , 330 (1980)] for unconfined drops with interface viscosity, with those by Shapira and Haber [M. Shapira and S. Haber, Int. J. Multiph. Flow 16 , 305 (1990)] for confined drops without interface viscosity. By merging these two approaches, we provide comprehensive analytical predictions for steady-state drop deformation and inclination angle across a wide range of physical conditions, from confined to unconfined droplets, including or excluding the effect of interface viscosity. The proposed analytical predictions are also robust concerning variations in the viscosity ratio, making our model general enough to include any of the above conditions.

Introduction

Complex fluids, such as blood, emulsions, and immiscible polymer blends, are familiar to people ordinary-life since they are implemented in several engineering applications, ranging from pharmaceutical

;
Gao and Zhang 2015
;

During their processing, the micro-constituents of these systems, namely red blood cells, drops, and single polymers, may undergo deformation, turning into a specific system morphology, up to the interface rupture. Studying the steady-state shape of the system is crucial in defining the mechanical properties and rheology of such systems
Guido and Villone 1998
;
Narsimhan 2019
;
Aouane et al. 2021
;
Guglietta et al. 2023

The most streamlined situation consists in a single drop undergoing a shear flow at low Reynolds numbers, which has garnered extensive scrutiny through analytical

;
Taylor 1934
;
Chaffey and Brenner 1967
;
Rallison 1980
;

In particular, under the same conditions, the pioneering work of Taylor

;
Sibillo et al. 2006
;
Vananroye et al. 2007
;

;
Vananroye et al. 2008

In this scenario, Shapira & Haber

provided an analytical prediction for steady-state drop deformation as a function of the confinement degree and the relative distance between the drop’s center of mass and the lower wall, but no claim has been made on the inclination angle. Their analytical prediction has also been confirmed by experiments

;
Vananroye et al. 2007

Later on, confinement degree has been numerically and experimentally observed to play an important role in drop breakup, influencing not only the threshold and dynamics of breakup but also the resulting daughter-drop size distribution under different confinement, viscosity ratios and flow conditions

Barai and Mandal 2016

;

;

In this landscape, a comprehensive analytical expression predicting the steady-state value of deformation and inclination angle of a confined drop with interface viscosity in shear flow is still missing, despite the necessity coming from practical applications involving drops and suspensions of droplets.

II
Numerical method

Given the inherent challenges of precisely controlling all possible parameter combinations in experiments is challenging, numerical simulations are crucial to address this scope.
In this work, we performed immersed boundary-lattice Boltzmann numerical simulations. Such a method has been largely benchmarked and employed for investigating the dynamics of single drops
Li and Zhang 2019
;
Guglietta et al. 2020
;
Pelusi et al. 2023
;
Taglienti et al. 2023
;
Taglienti et al. 2024
and capsules
Zhang et al. 2007
;
Krüger et al. 2011
;
Krüger et al. 2014
;
Guglietta et al. 2023
;
Silva et al. 2024
;
Owen et al. 2023
with and without interface viscosity. In particular, the in-house GPU code we employed has already been extensively validated in previous works of some of the authors
Guglietta et al. 2020
;
Guglietta et al. 2021a
;
Guglietta et al. 2021b
;
Taglienti et al. 2023
;
Taglienti et al. 2024
.

Instead of directly simulating the Navier-Stokes equations, the lattice Boltzmann (LB) method simulates the evolution of some probability distribution functions
\mbox{n}_{i}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)
, representing the probability of finding fluid particles at position
\bm{\bm{x}}
and time
t
, moving with a discrete velocity
\bm{\bm{c}}_{i}
. In particular, we implemented the so-called D3Q19 LB scheme, in which 19 discrete velocities are considered on a 3D regular lattice (see Ref.
Krüger et al. 2017
for details).
The dynamics of such functions
\mbox{n}_{i}
is given by the LB equation:

\mbox{n}_{i}(\bm{\bm{x}}+\bm{\bm{c}}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)-\mbox{n}_{i}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)=-\Delta t\frac{\mbox{n}_{i}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)-\mbox{n}_{i}^{\mbox{\tiny eq}}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)}{\tau_{\mbox{\tiny LB}}}+\left(1-\frac{\Delta t}{2\tau_{\mbox{\tiny LB}}}\right)\frac{w_{i}}{c_{s}^{2}}\left[\left(\frac{\bm{\bm{c}}_{i}\cdot\bm{\bm{u}}}{c_{s}^{2}}+1\right)\bm{\bm{c}}_{i}-\bm{\bm{u}}\right]\cdot\bm{\bm{F}}\ ,

(1)

being
\mbox{n}_{i}^{\mbox{\tiny eq}}
the equilibrium distribution function

\mbox{n}_{i}^{\mbox{\tiny eq}}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)=w_{i}\rho\left(1+\frac{\bm{\bm{u}}\cdot\bm{\bm{c}}_{i}}{c_{s}^{2}}+\frac{(\bm{\bm{u}}\cdot\bm{\bm{c}}_{i})^{2}}{2c_{s}^{4}}-\frac{\bm{\bm{u}}\cdot\bm{\bm{u}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}c_{s}^{2}}\right)\ ,

(2)

\bm{\bm{F}}
the force density,
w_{i}
suitable weights (
w_{1}=1/3
,
w_{2-7}=1/18
,
w_{8-19}=1/36
),
c^{2}_{s}=1/3
the speed of sound,
\Delta x=\Delta t=1
the lattice spacing and the discrete time-step, respectively, and
\tau_{\mbox{\tiny LB}}=1
is the LB relaxation time
Krüger et al. 2017
.
The hydrodynamics fields (fluid density
\rho
and velocity
\bm{\bm{u}}
) can be computed as:

\rho(\bm{\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{19}\mbox{n}_{i}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)\ ,\qquad\qquad\qquad\rho\bm{\bm{u}}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{19}\bm{\bm{c}}_{i}\mbox{n}_{i}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bm{\bm{F}}\ .

(3)

Notice that
\tau_{\mbox{\tiny LB}}
provides the link between the kinetic approach of the LB method and the hydrodynamic world of the Navier-Stokes equation via the fluid viscosity

\mu=\rho c_{s}^{2}\left(\tau_{\mbox{\tiny LB}}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)
.
Further technical details can be found in textbooks
Krüger et al. 2017
;
Succi 2018
or in previous works by some of the authors, in which the same method (and the same numerical code) was employed
Guglietta et al. 2020
;
Guglietta et al. 2021a
;
Guglietta et al. 2021b
;
Taglienti et al. 2023
;
Pelusi et al. 2023
;
Taglienti et al. 2024
.
In the present work, the fluid domain is a three-dimensional rectangular box with sizes
\text{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize x}}=\text{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize y}}=128\ \Delta x
and
\text{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize z}}=H
, the latter varying to explore different degrees of confinement.
The immersed boundary method handles the two-way coupling between the fluid and the drop interface
Krüger et al. 2017
;
Peskin 2002
.
The drop interface is modeled as a 3D triangular mesh with radius
R=20\ \Delta x
, made of
1.8\times 10^{4}
faces. On each mesh Lagrangian node
k
at position
\bm{\bm{r}}_{k}
, the interface force
\bm{\bm{\varphi}}_{k}(t)
is computed (details on the interface model are given below), and then, according to the immersed boundary method, it is spread over the neighboring Eulerian (fluid, LB) nodes with coordinates
\bm{\bm{x}}
:

\bm{\bm{F}}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{k}\bm{\bm{\varphi}}_{k}(t)\delta(\bm{\bm{r}}_{k}-\bm{\bm{x}})\ .

(4)

The function
\delta(\bm{\bm{x}})
is the discretized Dirac delta. In particular, we implemented the 4-points stencil for the Dirac delta (see Refs.
Krüger et al. 2017
;
Peskin 2002
). The immersed boundary method is also used to interpolate the fluid velocity on the
k-
th Lagrangian node:

\bm{\bm{\dot{r}}}_{k}(t)=\sum_{\bm{\bm{x}}}\Delta x^{3}\bm{\bm{u}}(\bm{\bm{x}},t)\delta(\bm{\bm{r}}_{k}-\bm{\bm{x}})\ .

(5)

Once we have the velocity
\bm{\bm{\dot{r}}}_{k}
, the position of the Lagrangian node is integrated with a forward-Euler scheme.

Concerning the interface model, we compute on each element of the triangular mesh the viscoelastic stress tensor
\bm{\bm{\tau}}=\bm{\bm{\tau}}_{s}+\bm{\bm{\tau}}_{v}
, that is made of the sum of the surface-tension stress tensor
\bm{\bm{\tau}}_{s}=\sigma\mathcal{I}_{2}

Li and Zhang 2019
;
Guglietta et al. 2020
;
Taglienti et al. 2023
;
Taglienti et al. 2024
, with
\mathcal{I}_{2}
being the 2D identity matrix, and the viscous tensor
\bm{\bm{\tau}}_{v}
, which is given by the Boussinesq-Scriven tensor
Scriven 1960
:
\bm{\bm{\tau}}_{v}=2\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize int}}\bm{\bm{e}}
, where
\bm{\bm{e}}=\frac{1}{2}[\nabla\bm{\bm{u}}+(\nabla\bm{\bm{u}})^{T}]
is the strain rate tensor and

\nabla\bm{\bm{u}}
is the velocity gradient at the interface
Li and Zhang 2019
;
Barthès-Biesel and Sgaier 1985
;
Gounley et al. 2016
.

In the most general case, the Boussinesq-Scriven tensor can be split into shear and dilatational contributions, characterized by the corresponding values of surface viscosity (
\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
and
\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
, respectively):
\bm{\bm{\tau}}_{v}=\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}[2\bm{\bm{e}}-\mbox{tr}(\bm{\bm{e}})\mathcal{I}_{2}]+\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}\mbox{tr}(\bm{\bm{e}})\mathcal{I}_{2}
.
For the sake of simplicity, as already done in other works
Barthès-Biesel and Sgaier 1985
;
Yazdani and Bagchi 2013
, we assume the surface shear and dilatational viscosities to be the same, allowing us to characterize viscous effects with a single parameter:
\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}=\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}=\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize int}}
.

Numerical details on how to compute the nodal force
\bm{\bm{\varphi}}_{i}
starting from the stress tensor
\bm{\bm{\tau}}
can be found in Refs.
Li and Zhang 2019
;
Guglietta et al. 2020
.

Concerning the setup,
we consider a drop with initial radius
R
characterized by a surface tension
\sigma
, an interface viscosity
\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize int}}
, and immersed in a fluid with density
\rho
and viscosity
\mu
, resulting in a viscosity ratio between inner and outer fluid viscosity
\lambda
(see Fig.
1
). The drop is placed between two walls in the
xy
plane, located at
z=\pm H/2
and separated by a distance
H
. The drop’s center of mass is fixed at a distance
h
from the lower wall.
We apply a shear rate
\dot{\gamma}=2u_{\mathrm{wall}}/H
by moving the walls with a velocity
u_{w}(x,y,z=\pm H/2)=(\pm u_{\mathrm{wall}},0,0)
(see Fig.
1
). The Reynolds number
\text{Re}=\rho\dot{\gamma}R^{2}/\mu
keeps values smaller than
10^{-2}
to avoid inertial effects. We measure the drop steady-state deformation
\text{D}_{\infty}
and the inclination angle
\theta_{\infty}
by varying the capillary number
\text{Ca}=\dot{\gamma}\mu R/\sigma
, the Boussinesq number
\text{Bq}=\mu_{\mbox{\scriptsize int}}/\mu R
, the degree of confinement
\chi=2R/H
, and the relative height of the drop’s center of mass
\hat{h}=h/H
.

III
Steady-state deformation

To derive an analytical prediction for the steady-state drop deformation under shear flow, we considered the one computed by Shapira & Haber
Shapira and Haber 1990
for confined pure drops, which reads

\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize SH}}(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi,\text{Ca})=\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}(\lambda,\text{Ca})\Psi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{SH}}}(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi)\ ,

(6)

where
\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}(\lambda,\text{Ca})=\text{Ca}(19\lambda+16)/(16\lambda+16)
is the steady-state drop deformation computed by Taylor for an unconfined pure drop
Taylor 1932
, and

\Psi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{SH}}}(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi)=1+C_{s}(\hat{h})\left(\frac{\chi}{2}\right)^{3}\frac{1+2.5\lambda}{1+\lambda}\ ,

(7)

is a function accounting for the degree of confinement. The coefficient
C_{s}(\hat{h})
in Eq. (
7
) represents the shape factor
1
1

1

The values of
C_{s}(\hat{h})
used in this work are
C_{s}(\hat{h}=0.25)=25.026798
,
C_{s}(\hat{h}=0.35)=9.9646144
and
C_{s}(\hat{h}=0.5)=5.6996174
and are taken from Ref.
Shapira and Haber 1990
. It is worth noting that, when
\chi=0
(unconfined drop), then
\Psi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{SH}}}=1
and
\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize SH}}=\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
. To include the effect of the interface viscosity, we considered the steady-state deformation of an unconfined drop with interface viscosity computed by Flumerfelt
Flumerfelt 1980
:

\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize F}}(\lambda,\text{Ca},\text{Bq})=\frac{19\lambda+16+32\text{Bq}}{(16\lambda+16+32\text{Bq})\sqrt{\text{Ca}^{-2}+\left[\frac{19\mathcal{F}}{20}(\lambda+2\text{Bq})\right]^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}}}}\ ,

(8)

with

\mathcal{F}=1-\frac{9\lambda+18\text{Bq}-2}{8(\lambda+2\text{Bq})^{2}}\ .

(9)

Based on this observation and intending to provide a
comprehensive
analytical prediction for confined droplets with interface viscosity, we replaced
\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}(\lambda,\text{Ca})
with
\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize F}}(\lambda,\text{Ca},\text{Bq})
in Eq. (
6
), thus obtaining

\mbox{D}(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi,\text{Ca},\text{Bq})=\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize F}}(\lambda,\text{Ca},\text{Bq})\Psi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{SH}}}(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi)\ .

(10)

In the case of
\chi=0
(i.e.,
\Psi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{SH}}}=1
), Eq. (
10
) recovers both Flumerfelt’s result (when
\text{Bq}>0
) and Taylor’s result (when
\text{Bq}=0
).
In this way, we implicitly assume that
\Psi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{SH}}}(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi)
does not depend on the interface viscosity and that the effects of confinement and interface viscosity separately contribute to the overall drop deformation.
On the other hand, one can consider the Oldroyd’s model
Oldroyd 1955
to account for the effect of membrane viscosity, as also shown in Refs.
Danov 2001
;
Narsimhan 2019
.
This results in considering an effective viscosity ratio
\lambda^{*}=\lambda+2\text{Bq}

Danov 2001
;
Narsimhan 2019
;
Flumerfelt 1980
;
Gounley et al. 2016
, that can be substituted in Eq. (
7
), leading to:

\Psi(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi,\text{Bq})=1+C_{s}(\hat{h})\left(\frac{\chi}{2}\right)^{3}\frac{1+2.5\lambda+5\text{Bq}}{1+\lambda+2\text{Bq}}\ .

(11)

However, Eq. (
11
) represents a small correction to Eq. (
7
), which can be only appreciated for high degrees of confinement. To verify it
, we measured the steady-state value of the deformation
\text{D}_{\infty}
from numerical simulations, and we estimated the ratio
\text{D}_{\infty}/\text{D}_{\mbox{\scriptsize F}}
as a function of
Bq
. Results are reported in Fig.
2
for
\lambda=1
: different panels refer to different combinations of
\chi
and
\hat{h}
, while different symbols and colors refer to different
Ca
. In each panel,
the corresponding values of
\Psi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{SH}}}(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi)
(cfr. Eq. (
7
)) and
\Psi(\lambda,\hat{h},\chi,\text{Bq})
(cfr. Eq. (
11
)) are reported with dashed and solid black lines, respectively. Our analysis shows that at small degrees of confinement (
\chi<0.7
),
\Phi_{\mathrm{SH}}
and
\Psi
are qualitatively the same, with
\Psi
losing its dependence on
Bq
for values of

\chi

IV
Steady-state inclination angle

We now move our attention to the drop inclination angle with respect to the flow direction. According to Maffettone & Minale (MM)
Maffettone and Minale 1998
, who described the shape evolution of an ellipsoidal drop in terms of
two functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, the steady-state deformation and inclination angle in the limit of small
Ca
can be computed as

\displaystyle\mbox{D}=\text{Ca}\frac{f_{2}}{2f_{1}}\ ,

(13a)

\displaystyle\theta=\frac{1}{2}\arctan{\left(\frac{f_{1}}{\text{Ca}}\right)}\ ,

(13b)

respectively.
Since they considered an unconfined pure drop (
\chi=0
and
\text{Bq}=0
),
the functions
f_{i}
have been chosen to recover the first-order expansion in
Ca
of the shape-evolution equation computed by Rallison
Rallison 1980
:

\displaystyle f_{1}(\lambda)=f_{1}^{(\mbox{\tiny MM})}(\lambda)=\frac{40(\lambda+1)}{(2\lambda+3)(19\lambda+16)}\ ,

(14a)

\displaystyle f_{2}(\lambda)=f_{2}^{(\mbox{\tiny MM})}(\lambda)=\frac{5}{2\lambda+3}\ .

(14b)

Since Rallison’s result has been recently extended by Narsimhan (N)
Narsimhan 2019
to include the effect of the interface viscosity, we matched the functions
f_{i}
with the first-order expansion of the shape-evolution equation computed in Ref.
Narsimhan 2019
, thus obtaining
f_{i}(\lambda,\text{Bq})=f_{i}^{(\mbox{\tiny N})}(\lambda,\text{Bq})
(cfr. Eqs. (
22

V
Conclusions

In this work,
we present a comprehensive analytical prediction describing the steady-state deformation (cfr. Eq. (
12
)) and inclination angle (cfr. Eq. (
21
)) of a confined/unconfined drop with/without interface viscosity, in the limit of small deformation and negligible inertia effects.

Since there are no available experiments for this system, to validate the quality and robustness of the proposed predictions, we performed immersed boundary-lattice Boltzmann simulations, which have already been validated in a series of previous works
Li and Zhang 2019
;
Guglietta et al. 2020
;
Taglienti et al. 2023
;
Taglienti et al. 2024
;
Pelusi et al. 2023
. We also compared Eqs. (
12
) and (
21
) with the already known analytical predictions computed by Flumerfelt
Flumerfelt 1980
for the unconfined drop with interface viscosity, showing that the accounting for confinement effects in Flumerfelt’s prediction is fundamental to quantitatively describe the correct deformation of a drop under these conditions. The robustness of our analytical predictions is strengthened by its application to different values of the drop viscosity ratio. We hope this result can open a new route to experiments of confined/unconfined drops with/without interface viscosity.
