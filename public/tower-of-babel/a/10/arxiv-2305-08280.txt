Self-adjointness criteria and self-adjoint extensions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on α \alpha -Grushin manifolds
Ivan Beschastnyi, Hadrian Quan

Abstract
. The Grushin plane serves as one of the simplest examples of a sub-Riemannian manifold whose distribution is of non-constant rank. Despite the fact that the singular set where this distribution drops rank is itself a smoothly embedded submanifold, many basic results in the spectral theory of differential operators associated to this geometry remain open, with the question of characterizing self-adjoint extensions being a recent question of interest both in sub-Riemannian geometry and mathematical physics. In order to systematically address these questions, we introduce an exotic calculus of pseudodifferential operators adapted to the geometry of the singularity, closely related to the 0-calculus of Mazzeo arising in asymptotically hyperbolic geometry. Extending results of [ 7 , 8 , 35 ] , this calculus allows us to give a criterion for essential self-adjointness of the Curvature Laplacian, Δ − c ​ S \Delta-cS for c > 0 c>0 (here S S is the scalar curvature). When this operator is not essentially self-adjoint, we determine several natural self-adjoint extensions. Our results generalize to a broad class of differential operators which are elliptic in this calculus.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Problem statement and previous results

In this article, we study the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
(n+1)
-dimensional
\alpha
-Grushin manifolds, which have recently garnered much attention in both the sub-Riemannian and mathematical physics community
[
8
,
9
,
18
,
6
,
7
,
21
,
20
,
35
,
16
]
. These manifolds are defined as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a real number
\alpha
, an
\alpha
-Grushin manifold is a triple
(M,\mathcal{Z},g)
consisting of a smooth manifold
M
of dimension
n+1
, a co-orientable embedded submanifold
\mathcal{Z}\subset M
of codimension one, which we call the singular set, and a metric
g
which in a tubular neighborhood of
\mathcal{Z}
takes the form

(1)

g_{\alpha}=dx^{2}+\frac{1}{|x|^{2\alpha}}g_{x,\mathcal{Z}},

where
g_{x,\mathcal{Z}}
is a smooth family of Riemannian metric on the level sets
x=const
and
x
is the distance to
\mathcal{Z}
.

These manifolds have a naturally associated volume form
\omega
and the Riemannian Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
. However, both of those objects exhibit a singularity at
\mathcal{Z}
. For this reason, a priori we can define
\Delta
only on
C^{\infty}_{c}(M\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})
. The animating goal of this work began with the following two natural questions, inspired by the mathematical physics literature above, which considers using the scalar curvature
S
as a geometrically natural singular potential to define an associated Schrödinger operator.

Question 1: If
\Delta
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator of an
\alpha
-Grushin manifold,
S
its scalar curvature and
c\in\mathbb{R}
a constant, when is the operator
\Delta-cS
essentially self-adjoint on
C^{\infty}_{c}(M\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})
with respect to the Riemannian
L^{2}
-space?

Question 2: If
\Delta-cS
is not essentially self-adjoint, what are its self-adjoint extensions?

The motivation for those questions comes from the unusual properties of
\alpha
-Grushin manifolds. For values of
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
these
\alpha
-Grushin manifolds are well-defined metric spaces and whose geodesics can cross
\mathcal{Z}
without developing any singularity. All such geodesics are projections of a Hamiltonian system endowed with an energy Hamiltonian, suggesting the dynamics of these spaces enjoy a form of classical completeness. However, if we try to consider a corresponding quantization of this Hamiltonian system, we find that the Laplace-Beltrami operator can become self-adjoint, as was first proven in
[
8
]
. Physically this corresponds to a situation when a classical particle can pass through a singularity, while a quantum particle can not. This phenomenon is now known as
quantum confinement
. Moreover, quantum properties depend significantly on the particular choice of quantization, parameterized here by the constant
c
. In the literature one can find values
c=1/3

[
13
,
39
]
,
c=2/3

[
13
]
,
c\in[0,2/3]

[
39
]
. It should also be noted that for
n\geq 3
and
c=(n-2)/(4(1-n))
, the operator
\Delta-cS
is conformally invariant and is referred to as the conformal Laplacian. In
[
7
]
the first author with his collaborators proved that the quantum confinement is absent for
\alpha=n=1
and
c>0
. The present work continues the investigation of this phenomenon.

Most of the known results for these types of spaces were developed for flat models, which can be described as
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
\mathcal{Z}=\{x=0\}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a metric

(2)

g_{\alpha}=dx^{2}+\frac{1}{|x|^{2\alpha}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}dy_{i}^{2}\right).

An orthonormal frame in this case is given by

X_{0}=\partial_{x},\qquad X_{i}=|x|^{\alpha}\partial_{y_{i}},\qquad i=1,\dots,n

and the Laplace-Beltrami operator has the form

(3)

\Delta^{G}=\partial_{x}^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}-\frac{\alpha n}{x}\partial_{x},

where
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is the Euclidean Laplacian.

We first consider when the operator (
3
) is essentially self-adjoint on
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\smallsetminus\{x=0\})
with respect to its Riemannian
L^{2}
-space. In this case, the volume form is given by

\omega=\frac{dx\wedge dy_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dy_{n}}{|x|^{\alpha n}}.

We would like to transform this to the standard Euclidean volume. For this reason, we make a unitary transform:

(4)

u\mapsto|x|^{\frac{\alpha n}{2}}u.

The Laplace-Beltrami operator in these new coordinates becomes

P=|x|^{-\frac{\alpha n}{2}}\Delta^{G}|x|^{\frac{\alpha n}{2}}=\partial_{x}^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}-\frac{\alpha(\alpha n+2)}{4x^{2}}.

Performing a Fourier transform on the
y
variables we obtain

\hat{P}(\xi)=\partial_{x}^{2}-|x|^{2\alpha}|\xi|^{2}-\frac{\alpha n(\alpha n+2)}{4x^{2}}.

It is clear that self-adjointness and self-adjoint extensions can be determined by this family of one-dimensional operators. One expects that
\Delta^{G}
should be self-adjoint if
\hat{P}(\xi)
are all self-adjoint as well. Each
\hat{P}(\xi)
is a Schrödinger operator with an inverse square potential, whose self-adjoint properties are well-known
[
12
]
. This strategy was used in
[
20
]
, where the authors proved the following theorem for
n=1
:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
G
be the
\alpha
-Grushin plane of dimension two. Depending on
\alpha
the following statements hold:

(1)

If
\alpha\in(-\infty,-3]\cup[1,+\infty)
, then
\Delta^{G}
is essentially self-adjoint on
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})
;

(2)

If
(-3,1)
, then
\Delta^{G}
is not essentially self-adjoint on
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})
and

(a)

For
\alpha\in(-3,-1]
the deficiency index is equal to two;

(b)

For
\alpha\in(-1,1)
the deficiency index is infinite.

A precise definition of deficiency indices will be given later. For now it is just important that they measure how far a given operator is being from essentially self-adjoint.

A similar result was proven earlier in
[
9
]
for Grushin cylinders. Using exactly the same idea one can prove an analogous result for all
n\geq 1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be the
\alpha
-Grushin plane of dimension
n+1
. Depending on
\alpha
the following statements hold:

(1)

\Delta^{G}
is essentially self-adjoint on
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})
if and only if
\alpha\in(-\infty,-3/n]\cup[1/n,+\infty)
.

(2)

Assume that
\Delta^{G}
is not essentially self-adjoint. If
n=1
or
n=2
and
\alpha\in(-3/n,-1]
then deficiency index is equal to two. In all the remaining cases the deficiency index is infinite.

There is no conceptual change if we add a scalar curvature term to
\Delta^{G}
. The scalar curvature for the
\alpha
-Grushin plane is given by (see Appendix A)

(5)

S=-\frac{\alpha n(\alpha n+\alpha+2)}{x^{2}},

thus
S
, as a multiplication operator, commutes with the unitary transformation (
4
). Hence after we apply the latter and the partial Fourier transform in the
y
variable, the resulting operator will have exactly the same form as
\hat{P}(\eta)
but with a different constant in the numerator of the inverse square term. Consequently, we can indicate three regions on the space
(\alpha,n,c)
of parameters: where
\Delta^{G}
has deficiency index equal to zero, two and infinity.

We should also mention that unsurprisingly the properties of
\Delta-cS
are closely related to the Schrödinger operator with the inverse square potential. Its natural domains were previously studied in
[
12
]
.

Apart from the already mentioned work
[
8
]
, there are scant results in the case of general
\alpha
-Grushin manifolds, and even fewer for more general sub-Riemannian structures. In
[
35
]
sufficient conditions for self-adjointness were obtained for a large class of singular manifolds which are even more general than the
\alpha
-Grushin manifolds we study here. Later the same group of authors generalized their results to sub-Riemannian structures
[
17
]
. Compared to their work, the method presented here can determine not only when
\Delta
is not essentially self-adjoint, but can further explicitly construct many of its self-adjoint extensions. This generalizes the results of
[
21
]
beyond the flat model
\Delta^{G}
. Also, operators in
[
36
]
can be studied using the
\alpha
-calculus we develop in this paper.

1.2.
Main tools and results

In this paper we make the following set of assumptions:

(A1)

The manifold
M
is closed;

(A2)

The singular set
\mathcal{Z}
has only one connected component;

(A3)

\alpha>-1
.

All of these assumptions are technical. We need the closeness of
M
in order to not consider the behavior of our operators at infinity, but adding the appropriate analysis can deal also with non-compact cases. Similarly,
\mathcal{Z}
can have more than one component with different orders of singularity
\alpha
. All the results will be local and thus each singularity can be treated separately.

The assumption that
\alpha>-1
is the most important one since it is the result of the method we use. We build a pseudo-differential calculus, the
\alpha
-calculus, that is closely related to the
0
-calculus
[
30
]
. In particular, when
\alpha=0
our calculus reduces to the
0
-calculus. This construction allows us to find a left parametrix for
\Delta-cS
and determine quite explicitly the minimal and maximal domains. Papers
[
22
,
6
]
use this technique for studying elliptic wedge operators and can be compared to other works where suitable adapted PDO calculi are constructed, see for example
[
33
,
30
,
27
,
14
]
and especially
[
3
]
which featured a calculus adapted to the geometry of general contact sub-Riemannian manifolds.

The cases
\alpha=-1
and
\alpha<-1
require a separate consideration, which is absent from this work. When
\alpha=-1
the
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)
is a
b
-operator and one can use the
b
-calculus to obtain similar results. For
\alpha<-1
one needs to develop a different calculus which seems, however, to be very similar to the cusp calculus (see
[
29
]
and more generally
[
38
]
). This is one setting we plan to pursue in future works, as much less is known for Grushin-type manifolds in this range of
\alpha
.

It should be noted that there also exists an alternative technique to prove the existence of left parametrices that uses the notion of Lie groupoids
[
10
]
. It is certainly possible to find the minimal domain this way, however, it is not very clear how to obtain a good description of the maximal domain in this general and abstract setting.

\alpha
-Grushin manifolds have some properties that simplify their study. For example, in Appendix A we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 1.3
.

Consider a
\alpha
-Grushin manifold of dimension
n+1
with
\alpha>-1
and compact singular set
\mathcal{Z}
. Then

(6)

S=-\frac{\alpha n(\alpha n+\alpha+2)}{x^{2}}+o(|x|^{-2}),\qquad x\to 0.

Thus we see that the main term in the asymptotics of the curvature depends only on the order of singularity and the dimension of the manifold, but does not depend on the geometry of the manifold
M
or the singular set
\mathcal{Z}
. This is a very useful property for the parametrix construction.

A central role in all of the constructions and results is played by the
indicial operator
of
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)
that we denote by
I(x^{2}(\Delta-cS))
. It can be defined as follows. Consider the tubular neighborhood from Definition
1.1
and start stretching it by applying the change of variables
x\mapsto sx
, where
s>0
is a parameter. Then the coefficient in front of the principal term will be an operator of the form

(7)

I(x^{2}(\Delta-cS))=(x\partial_{x})^{2}-(1+\alpha n)x\partial_{x}-c\alpha n(\alpha n+\alpha+2).

Note that this is true only for
\alpha>-1
. If we formally replace
x\partial_{x}
with
\lambda
, we obtain the
indicial polynomial

p(\lambda)

(8)

p(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-(1+\alpha n)\lambda-c\alpha n(\alpha n+\alpha+2).

Thanks to Proposition
1.3
, this polynomial does not depend on the
y
variable nor the geometry of the singular set. In this case, one says that
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)
has
constant indicial roots
, which is an important condition in the parametrix construction in many of the classical geometric calculi.

All of the properties related to the self-adjointness can be read off the indicial polynomial and its roots
\lambda_{\pm}
. Let

\mu=(1+\alpha n)^{2}+4c\alpha n(\alpha n+\alpha+2)

be the discriminant of the indicial polynomial. It turns out that the value of the discriminant codifies very well the situation when
\Delta-cS
is an essentially self-adjoint operator.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{Z},g_{\alpha})
be a
\alpha
-Grushin manifold of dimension
n+1
with
\alpha>-1
and let
c\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\mu\neq 4
then the operator
\Delta-cS
with domain
C^{\infty}_{c}(M\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})
is essentially self-adjoint on
L^{2}(M,\omega)
if and only if

\mu>4.

When
\Delta-cS
is not essentially self-adjoint, it has infinite deficiency indices.

The idea of the proof is to characterize as explicitly as possible the closure (or the minimal domain) and the adjoint (or the maximal domain) of
\Delta-cS
. Recall that
D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)
is described as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(M\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})
in the operator norm

\|u\|_{\Delta-cS}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(M,\omega)}+\|(\Delta-cS)u\|_{L^{2}(M,\omega)},

while the domain of the adjoint for a real symmetric differential operator coincides with

D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)=\{u\in L^{2}(M,\omega)\,:\,(\Delta-cS)\in L^{2}(M,\omega)\}.

The operator
\Delta-cS
will be essentially self-adjoint if and only if

D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)=D_{\max}(\Delta-cS).

In this case, the closure is the unique self-adjoint extension of our operator.

By the classical theorem of Von Neumann
[
23
, Theorem 3.1]
, we know that

D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)=D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)\oplus\ker((\Delta-cS)^{*}+i)\oplus\ker((\Delta-cS)^{*}-i).

The spaces
\ker((\Delta-cS)^{*}\pm i)
are known as
deficiency spaces
and their dimensions, usually referred to as
deficiency numbers
, are a measure of how far the closure of an operator is from being self-adjoint.

Using the
\alpha
-calculus we are able to find explicitly for
\mu\neq 4
both the closure and the adjoint of
\Delta-cS
. Comparison between the two gives exactly the result of Theorem
1.4
. The problem with
\mu=4
is that the left-parametrix construction fails in this case. We still can say something about both the closure and the adjoint of
\Delta-cS
(see Theorems
3.5
and (
37
)), however, the information we are able to retrieve is not enough to determine whether this operator is essentially self-adjoint.

If we compare Theorem
1.4
to Theorem
1.2
when
c=0
, we get exactly the same result except the value
\alpha=1/n
, which is not covered by Theorem
1.4
. In Theorem
3.5
we show that for all possible values of the discriminant
\mu\neq 4
the closure of
\Delta-cS
is a single functional space, which we describe explicitly. For
\mu=4
our technique does not work, but it is known that in the critical case for the Schrödinger operator with the inverse square potential its closure becomes slightly bigger
[
12
]
, so that the resulting operator is essentially self-adjoint. For
\alpha=n=1
,
\mu=4
self-adjointness of
\Delta-cS
was proven in
[
7
]
using the fact that this operator is positive. One can expect that a similar proof should hold for all the other critical cases.

Now that we have established that
\Delta-cS
is not essentially self-adjoint when
\mu<4
, we would also like to construct all of its self-adjoint extensions. It is known that any self-adjoint extension must lie between the minimal and maximal domains

(9)

D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)\subset D_{s.a.}(\Delta-cS)\subset D_{\max}(\Delta-cS).

Another classical Theorem by Von Neumann
[
23
, Theorem 3.4]
states that all of the self-adjoint extensions of
\Delta-cS
are in one-to-one correspondences with unitary operators between the two deficiency spaces
\ker((\Delta-cS)^{*}\pm i)
. In the case of Grushin manifolds this can be made much more explicit. The following theorem plays a central role in this description.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{Z},g_{\alpha})
be a
\alpha
-Grushin manifold of dimension
n+1
with
\alpha>-1
. Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu\neq 4
. Denote

(10)

\Theta=\{(1+\alpha)i+j\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,:\,i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}

Suppose
u\in x^{\delta}L^{2}(M,\omega_{\alpha})
, then solutions of

(\Delta-cS)u=0,

admit a weak asymptotic expansion of the form

(11)

u=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\Theta\\
p\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}x^{\lambda_{+}+\theta}\,(\log x)^{p}\,u_{\theta,p}^{+}(y)\;+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\Theta\\
p\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}x^{\lambda_{-}+\theta}\,(\log x)^{p}\,u_{\theta,p}^{-}(y),\quad u_{\theta,p}^{\pm}\in H^{-r_{\pm,\theta}}(\mathcal{Z})

where
r_{\pm,\theta}=\lambda_{\pm}+(i+j)-\delta+\tfrac{1}{2}
. Here we say that this is a weak asymptotic expansion in the sense that there is an expansion of the integral
\int_{\mathcal{Z}}u(x,y)\chi(y)dy
for any test function. A similar asymptotic expansion with
x
replaced by
-x
holds when
x\to 0-
.

This theorem tells us that the elements of
\ker(\Delta-cS)
have relatively ‘nice’ asymptotics. A priori there is no reason to believe that
u_{\pm,\theta,p}
are smooth. However, if the principal coefficient in this asymptotic expansion is a smooth function of
\mathcal{Z}
, then, in fact, all the remaining coefficients will be smooth in turn. We will show, in fact, that the graph closure of functions admitting such asymptotics are dense in the domain of the adjoint (see Theorem
3.6
). This explicit characterization of the domain of the adjoint is what allows us to use the method of asymmetry forms to find many natural families of self-adjoint extensions
[
23
, Section 3.4]
. It turns out that the coefficients of (
11
) in front of the
x^{\lambda_{\pm}}
terms will completely determine these extensions.

There are various approaches for construction of self-adjoint extensions. One popular method is Krein-Višik-Birman theory
[
19
]
; this approach was used in
[
21
]
to construct self-adjoint extensions to the Laplace operator on Grushin cylinder for
\alpha\in(0,1)
. However, this approach requires that the operator of interest is positive-definite. While this is true for the Laplacian itself, it may not be true for perturbations via the scalar curvature. Another approach uses the theory of asymmetry forms (see, for example,
[
23
]
) and unitary operators. This method can also handle non-positive operators. For example, self-adjoint extensions of the Schrödinger operator with an inverse square potential were completely classified in
[
23
]
. When dealing with PDEs instead, it can be difficult to apply it directly even in the case of Riemannian manifolds with boundary
[
26
]
. A particular case where this method was successfully applied is self-adjoint extensions of the Laplace operator on a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
15
]
. To deal with functional analytic problems, the authors introduce a generalized Green’s identity that can be used for functions in
D(\Delta^{*})
, but which does not extend easily to our setting.

Instead of classifying all possible self-adjoint extensions, we recover those extensions which seem to be the most important, as was done in
[
21
]
. Even though there is an infinite number of self-adjoint extensions, most of the time in the literature one encounters Dirichlet, Neumann, or mixed boundary conditions. In
[
21
]
, the authors find a slightly bigger family that may be viewed as their natural generalizations. Here, we use a similar idea but via different techniques and find that the general manifold case is far richer and admits a greater variety of self-adjoint extensions. The key idea is in our study is that whenever
\Delta-cS
has two distinct indicial roots the elements of
D_{max}(\Delta-cS)/D_{min}(\Delta-cS)
locally must be of the form

u=u^{r}_{+}+u^{r}_{-}+u^{l}_{+}+u^{l}_{-}\mod D_{\min}(\Delta-cS).

where

\displaystyle u^{r}_{\pm}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a^{r}_{\pm}(y)x^{\lambda_{-}}(1+o(1)),&x>0,\\
0,&x<0;\end{cases}

\displaystyle u^{l}_{\pm}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a^{l}_{\pm}(y)(-x)^{\lambda_{+}}(1+o(1)),&x<0,\\
0,&x>0.\end{cases}

Here
u^{r,l}_{\pm}\in\ker(\Delta-cS)
have the weak asymptotic expansions as in Theorem
1.5
and
a^{r,l}_{\pm}
are their principal coefficients, belonging to certain Hilbert spaces on the singular set
\mathcal{Z}
. This description of the adjoint allows us to prove the following theorems.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{Z},g_{\alpha})
be an
\alpha
-Grushin manifold of dimension
n+1
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
\mu<0
. Then, for every unitary
U:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, one can assign a self-adjoint extension as follows. Let
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the ordered family of eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{0,\mathcal{Z}}
, which is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the singular set with respect to the metric
g_{0,\mathcal{Z}}
from Definition
1.1
. Let also
a^{r,l}_{\pm,k}
be the coefficients of the decomposition of the principal terms
a^{r,l}_{\pm}
in the basis
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and define

a_{\pm,k}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{r}_{\pm,k}\\
a^{l}_{\pm,k}\end{pmatrix}.

Then, given a unitary operator
U:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, one can construct a self-adjoint extension by taking its domain to be a subspace of
D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)
satisfying

a_{+,k}=Ua_{-,k}.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{Z},g_{\alpha})
be an
\alpha
-Grushin manifold of dimension
n+1
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that

(1)

\alpha\geq 0
;

(2)

\mu\in(0,4)
and
\mu\neq 2
;

(3)

function
h:=\left.\sqrt{\mu}+(\dive_{|x|^{\alpha n}\omega}(\partial_{x})\lambda_{-}-c\partial_{x}(x^{2}S))/p(\lambda_{-})\right|_{\mathcal{Z}}
is sign-definite.

Then, for every unitary operator
U:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, one can assign a self-adjoint extension as follows. Let
\{\hat{\varphi}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the ordered family of eigenfunctions of
\hat{\Delta}_{0,\mathcal{Z}}
, which is a Laplace-Beltrami operator on the singular set with respect to the metric
h^{2}g_{0,\mathcal{Z}}
. Let also
a^{r,l}_{\pm,k}
be the coefficients of the decomposition of the principal terms
a^{r,l}_{\pm}
in the basis
\{\hat{\varphi}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and define

A_{1,k}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{r}_{+,k}+ia^{r}_{-,k}\\
a^{l}_{+,k}+ia^{l}_{-,k}\end{pmatrix},\qquad A_{2,k}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{r}_{+,k}-ia^{r}_{-,k}\\
a^{l}_{+,k}-ia^{l}_{-,k}\end{pmatrix}

Then, given a unitary operator
U:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, one can construct a self-adjoint extension by taking its domain to be a subspace of
D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)
for which

A_{2,k}=UA_{1,k}.

Let us comment on the assumptions of the last theorem. The strategy in proving both theorems will be to use a limiting version of Green’s identity at the singular set (
49
) (sometimes referred to as the
boundary pairing formula
) by exploiting the weak asymptotic expansion of the functions
u^{r,l}_{\pm}
. However, when
\mu>0
, the two roots
\lambda_{\pm}
are real and different. Hence we need expansion of
u^{r,l}_{-}
up to terms of order
o(x^{\lambda_{+}})
. The first condition guarantees that all of such terms are multiples of the first term, which is used in the derivation of the pairing formula. The next assumption simply removes log terms for simplicity. Finally, the last assumption guarantees that the measure in this pairing formula comes from a Riemannian metric on the singular set
\mathcal{Z}
, which we use to construct our Fourier basis. The sub-leading coefficient in the expansion of
u^{r,l}_{-}
is given by
\sqrt{\mu}-ha^{r,l}_{-}
.

In the case of Grushin cylinder, when
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
c=0
,
n=1
, we should be able to recover the results of
[
21
]
. And indeed, in this case, there is no scalar curvature term, and
\partial_{x}
is divergent-free. Hence
h=\sqrt{\mu}>0
. Even though
\mu=1
, for this concrete example, due to the particularity of the problem, there are no log terms. All self-adjoint extensions that the authors of

]
find correspond to gluing individually
k
-th harmonics, as we do in our work. The difference is that they use the Krein-Višik-Birman theory, which requires the operator of interest to be bounded from below. In our work, we do not make this assumption.

Next, we list analogues of the self-adjoint extensions from
[

, Theorem 1.3]
and their relation to the unitary operator
U
from the Theorem

:

(1)

The analogue of the Friedrichs extension:

U=\id_{2},\qquad a^{r}_{-}=a^{l}_{-}=0;

(2)

For
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
:

U=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\gamma-i}{\gamma+i}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a^{l}_{-}=0,\\
a^{r}_{+}=\gamma a^{r}_{-};\end{array}\right.

(3)

For
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
:

U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{\gamma-i}{\gamma+i}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a^{l}_{+}=\gamma a^{l}_{-},\\
a^{r}_{-}=0;\end{array}\right.

(4)

For
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and

:

U=\frac{1}{1+|b|^{2}-i\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}1-|b|^{2}-i\gamma&-2b\\
-2\overline{b}&-1+|b|^{2}-i\gamma\end{pmatrix},\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a^{r}_{-}=ba^{l}_{-},\\
a^{l}_{+}+\overline{b}a^{r}_{+}=\gamma a^{l}_{-};\end{array}\right.

(5)

If
\Gamma
is a Hermitian
2\times 2
matrix, then the last extension is given by

U=\frac{\Gamma-i\id_{2}}{\Gamma+i\id_{2}},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}a^{r}_{+}\\
a^{l}_{+}\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma\begin{pmatrix}a^{r}_{-}\\
a^{l}_{-}\end{pmatrix}.

Even though the authors exclude the previously studied case
\alpha=0
, where there is no singularity, the present self-adjoint extensions are valid in this case as well. In particular, the first listed self-adjoint extension (

) represents Dirichlet boundary conditions at

, while the fifth case with
\Gamma=0
(

) are the von Neumman conditions at

.

In general, it is not possible to reformulate the obtained self-adjointness criteria as boundary value problems, because one has to recover principal terms of both
u^{r,l}_{\pm}
which can be separated by several terms of the asymptotic expansion. However, there is a simple case in the previous theorem, namely, when

. This would force
a^{r}_{-}=a^{l}_{-}=0
. Since we know that those are the principal terms in the asymptotic expansion of

, we obtain this particular self-adjoint extension as a boundary condition of the form

\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1}{|x|^{\lambda_{-}}}u(x,y)=0,

which corresponds to the Friedrichs extension. This way, we extend the results from
[

]
to a much more general class of manifolds.

2.
Principal results of the
\alpha
-calculus

In this section we discuss concepts related to the construction of the
\alpha
-calculus. Let
M
be an
\alpha
-Grushin manifold. We cut this manifold along the singular set
\mathcal{Z}
and double the boundary to obtain a new manifold with the boundary
\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{Z}
. Abusing the notation we call this manifold again
M
.

We define the
\alpha
-vector fields
as follows: if
M
is endowed with a metric as in (
1
), we say

\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}(M)=\{W\in C^{0}(M;TM)\,|\,W\in C^{\infty}(M^{\circ};TM^{\circ}),\;|W|_{g_{\alpha}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(x^{2})\}

i.e., they are continuous vector fields on
M
, which are smooth in the interior, vanish at the boundary, and those which are
g_{\alpha}
-orthogonal to the boundary unit normal
N
vanish to order
(1+\alpha)
, (our assumption that
\partial M=\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{Z}
is co-orientable is equivalent to the boundary unit normal being trivialized). The
\alpha
-calculus is a tool to study operators in the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}(M)
, which in local coordinates near
\partial M
can be expressed as

(12)

\sum_{j,\beta}a_{j,\beta}(x,y)(x\partial_{x})^{j}(x^{1+\alpha}\partial_{y})^{\beta},

where
(x,y)
are such that
x
is a boundary defining function for
\partial M
(i.e. that
\partial M=\{x=0\}
and
dx|_{\partial M}\neq 0
) and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
are coordinates on
\partial M
. From this form of the operator, we see that a local frame for the Lie algebra
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}(M)
is given by

\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}(M)=\text{span}_{C^{\infty}(M^{\circ})}\{x\partial_{x},\,x^{1+\alpha}\partial_{y_{j}}\}.

In general the “nice” operators in our calculus will be elliptic combinations of these vector fields, which have some control at infinity.

The animating idea between various geometric pseudodifferential calculi is that one should consider the kernels of differential operators not as distributions living on
M^{2}
, but as certain distributions living on its desingularization
M_{\alpha}^{2}
called the double stretched product. In order to pass from
M^{2}
to
M^{2}_{\alpha}
we need to perform a blow-up of a certain submanifold. Note that
M^{2}
is a manifold with corners and hence the correct notion of blow-up is given by quasi-homogeneous blow-up of
p
-submanifolds. This blow-up procedure was thoroughly studied in
[
5
]
, where the orders were assumed to be integer. We define the quasi-homogeneous blow-up for non-integer orders at the cost of the blow-down map being not smooth (while remaining manageable).

As
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
is a finitely generated projective module over
C^{\infty}(M),
we can use the
Serre-Swan theorem, or proceed directly as in
[
31
, §8.2]
,
and find that there is a vector bundle

(13)

{}^{\alpha}TM\to M,

together with a bundle map
j_{\alpha}:{}^{\alpha}TM\to TM
such that

(j_{\alpha})_{*}C^{\infty}(M;{}^{\alpha}TM)=\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}\subseteq C^{0}(M;TM).

Eliding the map
j_{\alpha}
, we say that the space of sections of
{}^{\alpha}TM
is
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}.

We will refer to
{}^{\alpha}TM
as the
\alpha
-tangent bundle
. One connection between these last two geometric constructions is that
{}^{\alpha}TM
arises now as the normal bundle to the diagonal in the desingularized
M_{\alpha}^{2}
.

We discuss the blow-up construction in Subsection
2.1
. The double-stretched product
M_{\alpha}^{2}
is defined in Subsection
2.2
. Finally, the polyhomogeneous distributions are discussed in Subsection
2.3
and the parametrix construction is given in Subsection
2.4
. The parametrix construction lies at the center of our proofs. It is adapted for studying elliptic operators of the form (
12
),
where due to the singularity at
x=0
, ellipticity is not sufficient for having a good parametrix. Most of the proofs from this section are postponed till Section
5
.

2.1.
Manifolds with corners and quasi-homogeneous blow-up

In this subsection we review the notion of quasi-homogeneous blow-ups. We illustrate the definitions via some simple examples to underline the main differences with homogeneous blow-ups. However, the construction conceptually is the same. For a deeper look see the thesis

The natural differential geometric structure for many geometric pseudodifferential operator calculi is given by smooth manifolds with corners. These are second-countable Hausdorff topological spaces modeled by the standard Euclidean corners

Definition 2.3
.

Let

be the blow-up data, where

Proposition 2.1
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

is a smooth manifold with corners;

(2)

The construction does not depend on the choice of complements, i.e. two blow-ups using two different complements are diffeomorphic;

(3)

Blow-down map

(4)

As in the case of homogeneous blow-ups we have two good coordinate systems. One system generalizes the spherical coordinates; the other system is an analog of projective coordinates. Note that we can extend
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{l}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by gluing together several of its copies. Let

be a positive-definite quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\|x\|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q(x_{i})^{\frac{1}{a_{i}}}}.

This pseudo-norm is well defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{l}
as well. Now we can define coordinates on

as follows. We take the level set

(15)

S(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{l})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{l}:\|x\|=1\}

which is a smooth manifold by construction. Then we can describe the blown-up space as

[\mathbb{R}^{n}_{l};0]_{(\mathcal{F},\Lambda)}=S(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{l})\times\mathbb{R}^{+},

where
\mathbb{R}^{+}

and thus one can construct charts from the charts of

Let
\theta
be a point in

\beta(r,\theta)=(r^{\lambda_{1}}\theta_{1},\dots,r^{\lambda_{k}}\theta_{k}).

To construct the blow-up as a singular change of variables, we can define

(r,\theta)(x)=\left(\|x\|,\frac{x_{i}}{\|x\|^{\lambda_{i}}}\right).

It is often more convenient to use projective coordinates instead. By slight abuse of notation let

\xi_{j}=\begin{cases}\pm|x_{i}|^{\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}},&\text{if }j=i,\\
\frac{x_{j}}{|x_{i}|^{\lambda_{i}/\lambda_{j}}},&\text{if }j\neq i;\end{cases}

where we use two signs

is a line and only plus if the same subset is a semi-line.

Repeating over all possible

Similarly, if we wish to blow-up along submanifolds, we need to be careful that the normal bundle is well-defined, since the normal cone can vary from point to point. Thus again we need to restrict to a special class of submanifolds.

Definition 2.4
.

Given
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}

L_{I}=\{x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{l}:x_{i}=0,i\in I\}.

A
p

(U,\varphi)
around

and an index set

\varphi(P)=\varphi(U)\cap L_{I}.

Every

p

is defined as smoothly varying family of

Whenever we have a blow-up data
(\mathcal{F},\Lambda)
, where
\mathcal{F}
is a

and we can use charts of

to construct some charts of

2.2.
The double stretched product

We now construct the double stretched product
M_{\alpha}^{2}
, where the kernels of the constructed pseudo-differential calculus live. The double stretched product in the
0
-calculus is just a special case for
\alpha=0
.

Consider a
\alpha
-Grushin manifold. By cutting along the singular set
\mathcal{Z}
we obtain a compact manifold
M
with boundary
\partial M=\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{Z}
.
\mathcal{Z}
is co-orientable, and hence the normal vector field
X
at
\mathcal{Z}
is well defined. Essentially we want to blow-up along this vector field with order
1
and along the boundary with order
1+\alpha
. In order to make everything consistent with the definition of blow-up given previously, we consider two separate cases: when
-1<\alpha<0
and
\alpha\geq 0
. Both cases are very similar, and for this reason we explain the second one in-depth and only sketch the first one.

Assume that
\alpha\geq 0
. Consider the annihilator of a vector field
X
of the normal to a boundary component. It is given by a collection of
n-1

\sigma
, which will define
X
uniquely modulo a scale factor due to the relation

. Close to the boundary we can always choose coordinates
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

. Then
\sigma=dy
locally.

We define the double-stretched product via the following construction. Denote by
\Delta
the diagonal of

. First note that
\partial\Delta\subset M\times M
is a
p
-submanifold. Indeed, in the local coordinates it is given by the set
(0,y,0,y)
. Let
\pi_{L},\pi_{R}:M\times M\to M
be projections to the left and right factors. We can lift
\sigma
naturally to a form on
M\times M
by defining

\sigma_{2}=\pi_{l}^{*}\sigma-\pi_{r}^{*}\sigma.

This is a vector-valued one-form that lives in the annihilator of
\partial\Delta
, which is the dual space to the normal bundle
N(\partial\Delta)
. Thus we can define a subbundle
\ker\sigma_{2}\subset N(\partial\Delta)
and assign to it weight
1
and the weight
1+\alpha
to all
N(\partial\Delta)
. The filtration
\mathcal{F}

{0}\subset\ker\sigma_{2}\subset N(\partial\Delta)

is indeed a
p
-filtration over
\partial\Delta
. We assign to this filtration the order vector
\Lambda=(1,1+\alpha)
. This gives the blow-up data
(\mathcal{F},\Lambda)
.

Definition 2.5
.

\alpha
-double stretched product is defined as

M^{2}_{\alpha}=[M^{2};\partial\Delta]_{(\mathcal{F},\Lambda)}.

The structure of this resolved space is more clear in various coordinate systems. As previously mentioned, close to the boundary we have coordinates
(x,y)
, where

N(\partial\Delta)=\spann\{\partial_{x},\partial_{\tilde{x}},\partial_{y}-\partial_{\tilde{y}}\}.

In these coordinates the one form

is written as

\sigma_{2}=dy-d\tilde{y}.

Hence
\ker\sigma_{2}=\spann\{\partial_{x},\partial_{\tilde{x}}\}
.

Thus centering our coordinates at a specific point in
\partial\Delta

as
(r,\theta)

(18)

r=(x^{2(1+\alpha)}+|y-\tilde{y}|^{2}+\tilde{x}^{2(1+\alpha)})^{\frac{1}{(2(1+\alpha))}},\quad\theta=\left(\frac{x}{r},\frac{y-\widetilde{y}}{r^{(1+\alpha)n}},\frac{\widetilde{x}}{r}\right)=(\theta_{x},\theta_{y},\theta_{\widetilde{x}}).

and

Recall that a blow-up produces a new boundary face. Thus

Definition 2.6
.

An

(s_{j},p_{j})\in E,\qquad|(s_{j},p_{j})|\to\infty\Rightarrow\Re(s_{j})\to+\infty.

for all

with

.

The index sets are used to define the space of functions with good asymptotic properties. Assume that
X
is a manifold with a single boundary component, with boundary defining function
r
, and with associated index set
E
. Given these definitions, the space of
conormal polyhomogeneous distributions with index set

consists of distributions on

which have the following asymptotic expansions. Given

a defining function for
\partial X
we say that given

(20)

u(r,y)\sim\sum_{(s,p)\in E}r^{s}(\log r)^{p}\,u_{sp}(y),\quad u_{sp}\in C^{\infty}(\partial X)

where this asymptotic is interpreted to mean that for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

(21)

u-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,p)\in E\\
\Re(s)\leq N\end{subarray}}r^{s}(\log r)^{p}\,u_{s,p}(y)\in\dot{C}^{N}\left([0,1),C^{\infty}(\partial X)\right)

We denote this space of distributions by
\mathcal{A}^{E}_{phg}(X)
. If there are more boundary components, then we can impose the previous asymptotics at each boundary component with their own index set.

More generally, assume that manifold
X
is a manifold with corners and has

codimension one boundary faces
\mathcal{B}_{j}
,

. Let

be the corresponding defining functions and for each

. Denote
\mathcal{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{k})
and
\mathcal{E}(k)
for the collection of index sets from the boundaries intersecting
\mathcal{B}_{k}
. The space of conormal polyhomogeneous distributions
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{E}}(X)
is constructed inductively as a space of distributions which close to the boundary faces
\mathcal{B}_{j}
have expansions

(22)

u\sim\sum_{(s,p)\in E_{k}}r_{k}^{s}(\log r_{k})^{p}u_{sp},

where
u_{sp}\in\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{\mathcal{E}(k)}(\mathcal{B}_{k})
. More generally we denote the space of functions of
all possible
polyhomogeneous conormal expansions with respect to some arbitrary smooth index set by
\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{*}(X)
.

Remark 1
.

Note that higher order coefficients
u_{sp}
are not invariantly defined in general. Nevertheless, the principal term of the expansion is invariant.

For example, the space of smooth extendable functions across the boundary is a subspace of
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{phg}(X)
, the spaces of smooth functions which vanish up to order
k
is a subspace of
\mathcal{A}^{k}_{phg}(X)
. Smooth functions which vanish to infinite order form a subspace of conormal polyhomogeneous distributions with the empty index set. But by the analogy with the previous example, we denote this space as
\mathcal{A}^{\infty}_{phg}(X)
.

Because the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{\mathcal{E}}(X)
is defined inductively, in order to check that a function

belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{\mathcal{E}}
it is often simplest to apply the following criterion, proven in
[
32

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume

, and at every boundary face
\mathcal{B}_{k}
of

there is an expansion of

of the form (

), with coefficients merely belonging to
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}(\mathcal{B}_{k})
, but with exponents belonging to

. Then

in fact belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{\mathcal{E}}(X)
.

Remark 2
.

The spaces
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{E}}_{phg}(M)
are very natural for the goal of this article, mainly for studying the operator
\Delta-cS
. As we have already mentioned in the introduction, the indicial operator
I(x^{2}(\Delta-cS))
plays an important role in all of our constructions. As formula (

) shows, this is just a second-order ODE with a regular singular point. The classical theory of such equations
[
11

implies that solutions to

). Later we will see that this kind of asymptotics holds also for the solution of the equation

and, more generally, for
\alpha
-elliptic differential operators under certain additional assumptions.

Another useful property of the spaces
\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{\mathcal{E}}(X)
is that, much like the standard classes of symbol functions, they obey a form of asymptotic completeness. As in the classical case this is a consequence of a lemma of Borel which here can be written in the following way.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X
be a compact manifold with boundary, and
E
a corresponding index set. If
u_{s,p}\in C^{\infty}(X)
is given for each

then there exists

satisfying (
20
). Further, if
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{E}(X)
is another element with the same asymptotic expansion then
u-u^{\prime}\in\dot{C}^{\infty}(X)
.

Proof.

Choose
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
with
\chi|_{\{t<1/2\}}=1
and
\chi|_{\{t>1\}}=0
. We shall prove the existence of a sequence of constants
\epsilon_{s,p}\in(0,1)
which decrease sufficiently fast to ensure that for each

the series

(23)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,p)\in E\\
\Re(z)>N\end{subarray}}\chi\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon_{s,p}}\right)\,r^{s}(\log r)^{k}\,u_{s,p}\;\text{converges absolutely in }\dot{C}^{N}(X).

Namely, for each
N
, there exists a sequence of constants
\epsilon_{s,p}^{(N)}
such that (
23
) holds for that

whenever
\epsilon_{s,p}<\epsilon_{s,p}^{(N)}
for all
\Re(z)>N
. From the definition of an index set, we know that (
19
) holds, thus any ‘left segment’

is a finite set for each
N
. From this fact, all these conditions imposed on the sequence of constants
\epsilon_{s,p}
is a finite number of conditions for each

; choosing a diagonal subsequence these constants can be chosen such that (

)) to an element

which satisfies (

\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{E}(X)
and the fact that

\dot{C}^{\infty}(X)=\bigcap_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\dot{C}^{N}(X).

∎

Remark 3

We observe for later purposes that if

Arguing similarly as in the lemma also proves the asymptotic completeness for manifolds with corners, and the spaces

The space
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{phg}
of such functions with unrestrained asymptotics will be used to construct parametrices to singular differential operators. But first, we need to understand how those spaces change under various mappings. These mappings, however, must have certain properties in order to send polyhomogeneous distributions to polyhomogeneous distributions. First, we consider those maps that can be used as pull-backs.

Definition 2.7

Remark 4
.

Note that in our definition a

Moreover, the definition of polyhomogeneous expansions already includes non-integer weights. This means that there will be no problem in defining kernels and the construction of parametrices.

2.4.
\alpha
-calculus and existence of a parametrix

Recall that from the construction of the double space, we have the blow-down map
\beta^{2}_{\alpha}:M^{2}_{\alpha}\to M^{2}
. This allows us to lift the diagonal from
M^{2}
to the double space as

\Delta_{\alpha}=\overline{(\beta_{\alpha}^{2})^{-1}(\Delta_{int})},

where
\Delta_{int}
is the restriction of the diagonal to the interior of
M^{2}
. Similarly as in the case of the zero-calculus we associate to
M
a density bundle

\Omega_{\alpha}(M)=(\overline{r})^{-1-n-\alpha n}\Omega(M),

where
n=\dim M-1
and
\overline{r}
is the product of defining functions of all boundaries of
M
. This multiplicative factor is determined by the determinant of the Jacobian of the blow-down map
\beta_{\alpha}^{2}
, and is computed in Lemma 5.3. It is also convenient to introduce a different density bundle

\Omega_{b}(M)=\overline{r}^{-1}\Omega(M),

which will be used to understand the mapping properties of operators from the
\alpha
-calculus. From here we can define the half-density bundles
\Omega_{\alpha}^{1/2}
and
\Omega_{b}^{1/2}
, which are useful when studying Schwartz kernels of linear operators. Recall that we can multiply two half-densities which gives a density. Moreover, half-density bundles are one-dimensional and trivializable. So we can choose a reference non-vanishing section of this bundle.

For example, in our case close to the singular set
\mathcal{Z}
we have local coordinates
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We can take a reference section of the half-density bundle
\Omega^{1/2}(M)
in these coordinates to be

\sqrt{\gamma}=\sqrt{dxdy}.

Any other section would be given by
f\sqrt{\gamma}
, where
f
is a smooth function. Thus, if we have two sections
f\sqrt{\gamma}
and
g\sqrt{\gamma}
, their product will be a section of the density bundle
(fg)\gamma\in\Omega(M)
, which can be integrated over
M
. In particular, we can define the space square-integrable sections of
\Omega^{1/2}
as

L^{2}(M,\Omega^{1/2})=\left\{\sigma\in\Gamma(\Omega(M))\,:\,\int_{M}\sigma^{2}<\infty\right\}.

If we take a reference half-density
\sqrt{\gamma}
then a section
\sigma=f\sqrt{\gamma}
will be square integrable if and only if
f
is square integrable with respect to
\gamma
. Thus we have a natural isomorphism

(25)

L^{2}(M,\Omega^{1/2})\simeq L^{2}(M,\gamma).

We work with half-densities to prove all the main results in the calculus, but in its concrete applications, it is much more convenient to fix a trivializing section as we will see later.

We begin by recalling the characterization of classical pseudodifferential operators to see how they are generalized in the
\alpha
-calculus. Recall that the classical pseudo-differential operators
\Psi^{s}(M)
on smooth compact manifolds without boundary we have a principal symbol map

\sigma:\Psi^{r}(M)\to C^{\infty}(T^{*}M),\quad\text{defined by }\sigma(L)(x,\xi)=\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-r}e^{-itf}Le^{itf},\;\;df_{x}=\xi,

which provides an invariant definition of this algebra homomorphism. The homomorphism takes the highest order part of the operator and produces a function which is degree
r
-homogeneous in the fibers of
T^{*}M
. Choosing coordinates, it admits a particularly intuitive description for differential operators, namely if we have a differential operator with smooth coefficients

L=\sum_{|I|\leq s}a_{I}(x)\partial_{x}^{I},

where
I
is a multi-index, then its principal symbol is given by

\sigma(L)(x,\xi)=\sum_{|I|=r}a_{I}(x)(i\xi)^{I}.

The symbol map is a part of a short exact sequence

(26)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi^{r-1}(M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi^{r}(M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma}

which is the central property for an iterative parametrix construction. It allows us to construct an inverse modulo
\Psi^{-\infty}(M)
, whenever the principal symbol
\sigma(L)
is an invertible operator. It is well-known that in this situation that
\Psi^{-\infty}(M)
consists of compact operators
[
37
]
.

In the case of
\alpha
-calculus we are interested in inverting differential operators of the form

L=\sum_{j+|\beta|\leq m}a_{j,\beta}(x,y)(x\partial_{x})^{j}(x^{1+\alpha}\partial_{y})^{\beta},

which is an element of
\Psi^{m}_{\alpha}(M)

{}^{\alpha}\sigma_{m}(L)=\sum_{j+|\beta|=m}a_{j,K}(x,y)(i\xi)^{j}(i\eta)^{\beta}.

Principal symbols in this calculus are also smooth functions on the
\alpha

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

and correspondingly we say an operator is
\alpha

\alpha

{}^{\alpha}T^{*}M\smallsetminus\{0\}
. Classical pseudodifferential constructions, depending only on the

Now we are ready to give a definition of the operators in the

Definition 2.10
.

The large calculus is defined as comprised of linear operators whose Schwartz kernels satisfy precisely

A\in\Psi^{s,\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(M)\iff\mathcal{K}_{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{\mathcal{E}}I^{s}(M^{2}_{\alpha},\Delta_{\alpha};\Omega_{\alpha}^{1/2}),

for any index set
\mathcal{E}=\{E_{10},E_{01},E_{11}\}
. This is the space of all linear operators with distributional kernels with conormal singularities at the lifted diagonal, which have polyhomogeneous expansions with index set
\mathcal{E}
. The small
\alpha
-calculus is a special case defined as

A\in\Psi^{s}_{\alpha}(M)\iff\mathcal{K}_{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{phg}^{(\infty,\infty,\mathbb{N}_{0})}I^{s}(M^{2}_{\alpha},\Delta_{\alpha};\Omega_{\alpha}^{1/2}).

i.e. its Schwartz kernel vanishes to infinite order at the side faces and has a ‘Taylor expansion’ at the front face.

One example is the identity operator, whose Schwartz kernel is a distributional section of
\Omega^{1/2}(M^{2})
, expressible in coordinates near
\partial M_{\text{diag}}
as,

\mathcal{K}_{\id}(x,y,\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})=\delta(x-\widetilde{x})\delta(y-\widetilde{y})\mu

where
\mu=\sqrt{dxdyd\widetilde{x}d\widetilde{y}}\in\Omega^{1/2}(M^{2})
. Now the half-density
\mu
pulls back along
\beta_{\alpha}^{(2)}:M_{\alpha}^{2}\to M^{2}
to the half-density
r^{1+(1+\alpha)n}\nu
(a direct consequence of Lemma
5.3
), where
\nu
is a smooth half-density on
\Omega^{1/2}(M_{\alpha}^{2})
, i.e.
\nu=\sqrt{drd\theta d\widetilde{y}}
. Thus our operator lifts to one of the form,

(\beta_{\alpha}^{(2)})^{*}\mathcal{K}_{\id}=\delta(\theta_{x}-\theta_{\widetilde{x}})\delta(\theta_{y})\,r^{-1-(1+\alpha)n}\nu=\delta(\theta_{x}-\theta_{\widetilde{x}})\delta(\theta_{y})\widetilde{\nu}

in the coordinates (

), and
\widetilde{\nu}\in\Omega_{\alpha}^{1/2}
.

In order to construct a good parametrix, we need to take into account the behavior at the front face. So we consider the restriction of the lift of
L
to the front face of
M_{\alpha}^{2}

(27)

(s,u,\tilde{x},\tilde{y}):=\left(\frac{x}{\tilde{x}},\frac{y-\tilde{y}}{\tilde{x}^{1+\alpha}},\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\right),

N(L)=\sum_{j+|\beta|\leq m}a_{j,\beta}(0,\tilde{y})(s\partial_{s})^{j}(s^{1+\alpha}\partial_{u})^{\beta}.

For each

fixed, this operator is called the

of

. In Section

L
is invertible modulo a compact operator if it is elliptic in the interior and all

are invertible between suitable Hilbert spaces. Note that each

\partial_{u}\mapsto-i\eta

\hat{\eta}=\eta/|\eta|

(y,\hat{\eta})\in S^{*}(\partial M)

\widehat{N(L)}_{(y,\hat{\eta})}=\sum_{j+|\beta|\leq m}a_{j,\beta}(0,y)(s\partial_{s})^{j}(is^{1+\alpha}\hat{\eta})^{\beta}.

In Theorem
2.10
we prove that the invertibility of those operators is mostly determined by the behavior of these operators close to

. More precisely we can define the indicial operators as mentioned in the introduction, by rescaling
s\mapsto\lambda s
and writing the invariant term, which will be given by

I(L;y)=\sum_{j\leq m}a_{j,0}(0,y)(s\partial_{s})^{j}.

This is family of scale invariant differential operators on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and as such can be reduced to a polynomial via the Mellin transform:

Definition 2.11
.

The Mellin transform of a function
f(s)
is an integral transform of the form

\mathcal{M}[f](\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{-\lambda}f(s)\frac{ds}{s}.

The inverse Mellin transform is given by

(29)

\mathcal{M}^{-1}[F](x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Re(\lambda)=c}x^{\lambda}F(\lambda)d\lambda.

where we integrate over a horizontal line.

We use a slightly non-standard definition of Mellin transform different from
[
27
]
, but which is closer to one that can be found, for example, in
[
34
]
. It has the following mapping property.

Theorem 2.7
.

Mellin transform
\mathcal{M}
is an isomorphism between

x^{\delta}L^{2}([0,\infty])
and
L^{2}(\left\{\Re(z)=\delta-\tfrac{1}{2}\right\})
.

Straight from the definition, we get the following property

\mathcal{M}[s\partial_{s}u]=\lambda\mathcal{M}[u].

Thus if we apply the Mellin transform to the indicial operators, we get a family of indicial polynomials, which in turn should be invertible away from their roots.

This motivates the following definition:

Definition 2.12
.

The boundary spectrum, or
indicial roots
, at
y\in\partial M
of an operator

L=\sum_{j+|\beta|\leq m}a_{j,\beta}(x,y)(x\partial_{x})^{j}(x^{1+\alpha}\partial_{y})^{\beta},

is the set

\spec_{b}(L;y)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\,\biggr|\,\lambda\text{ is a root of the polynomial }I_{\zeta}(L;y)=\sum_{j\leq m}a_{j,0}(0,y)\zeta^{j}\right\}.

If these roots are independent of the choice of basepoint
y\in\partial M
then we say that
L
has

.

In general, we shall make hypotheses of the type that we can find a line
\{\Re{\zeta}=c\}
which does not meet any point in
\text{Spec}_{b}(L;y)
. Because the inverse of

Before stating the main parametrix theorem, let us also discuss the mapping properties of elements in the small and large calculi. The first important result is that operators from the large calculus preserve polyhomogeneous conormality.

Proposition 2.8

If

\mathcal{A}^{*}_{phg}(M)
to itself.

A more refined version of this result is given in Theorem

Another important ingredient is the adapted Sobolev spaces.

Definition 2.13

The Sobolev spaces

Similarly, we define the weighted Sobolev spaces

where

Theorem 2.9

Given

In order to construct a parametrix the following assumption is usually made

Assumption 1

The indicial polynomial does not depend on the boundary, i.e. the indicial roots are constant.

Finally we come back to the Fourier transforms

\displaystyle\underline{\delta}

We are now ready to state our main consequence of the

Theorem 2.10

Suppose that

This result is in fact a consequence of a more extensive Theorem

3.
Natural domains for the Laplacian on
\alpha
-Grushin manifolds

Our goal is to use the results of the previous section to find the domain of the closure and of the adjoint of the operators
\Delta-cS
. All of the essential information needed for studying self-adjointness and self-adjoint extensions is encoded in the description of the mentioned domains close to the singular set. Indeed, in an open neighborhood around
q\in M\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z}
the operator
\Delta-cS
is elliptic and smooth and hence morally both its closure and adjoint locally should be standard Sobolev space. The difficulty is understanding the behaviour of elements of the kernel close to the singular set. This is done using a parametrix construction from Theorem
5.7
that is used in its simplified version given by Theorem
2.10
. Note that in order to prove all of the results of this section we can use this theorem as a blackbox.

Let us explicitly write down operators
\Delta-cS
. In the tubular neighborhood of
\mathcal{Z}
we introduce a companion metric

\tilde{g}=dx^{2}+g_{(x,\mathcal{Z})},

which simply removes the singularity. The metric is defined only locally, and we will use it to study local behavior of
P_{\alpha,c}
. Let
\tilde{\omega}
be the associated volume form on
M
and let
\Delta_{x,\mathcal{Z}}
be the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the level sets
x=\const
with respect to
g_{(x,\mathcal{Z})}
.

If in the tubular neighborhood of
\mathcal{Z}
we have chosen a basis of orthonormal vector fields
X_{0},\dots,X_{n}
and
\omega
the Riemannian volume associated to the original metric
g
. We can write the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
locally as

\Delta=\sum_{i=0}^{n}X_{i}^{2}+\dive_{\omega}(X_{i})X_{i}.

Let us choose
X_{0}=\partial_{x}
and the remaining
X_{i}=x^{\alpha}Y_{i}
, where
Y_{i}
is a family of vector fields tangent to the level sets of the distance function
x
. Note that this implies that
Y_{i}
applied to the function
x
is zero.

To calculate these divergence terms we notice that by given definitions the volume form is

\omega=\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha n}}\tilde{\omega}=\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha n}}\omega_{(x,S)}\wedge dx,

where
\tilde{\omega}
is the volume form of the companion metric
\tilde{g}
and
\omega_{(x,S)}
are volume forms on the level sets
x=\const
, which can be identified with a smooth family of
n
-forms on
S
. We have by definition of divergence

\dive_{\omega}(\partial_{x})\omega=\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{x}}(|x|^{-\alpha n}\tilde{\omega})=-\alpha n|x|^{-\alpha n-1}\sign(x)\tilde{\omega}+\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{x}}\tilde{\omega}}{|x|^{\alpha n}}=\left(\dive_{\tilde{\omega}}(\partial_{x})-\frac{\alpha n}{x}\right)\omega.

For us the explicit form of
\dive_{\tilde{\omega}}(\partial_{x})
is not important. We will only need to know that it is smooth. For the remaining fields we find using Cartan’s magic formula

\displaystyle\dive_{\omega}(x^{\alpha}Y_{j})\omega

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{x^{\alpha}Y_{j}}(|x|^{-\alpha n}\omega_{(x,S)}\wedge dx)=|x|^{-\alpha n}d(|x|^{\alpha}(Y_{j}\lrcorner\;\omega_{(x,S)})\wedge dx)=

\displaystyle=|x|^{\alpha}\dive_{\omega_{(x,S)}}(Y_{j})\omega.

Combining everything we get

\Delta=\partial_{x}^{2}+\left(\dive_{\tilde{\omega}}(\partial_{x})-\frac{\alpha n}{x}\right)\partial_{x}+|x|^{2\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}^{2}+\dive_{\omega_{(x,\mathcal{Z})}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\right)

or in a compact form

(30)

\Delta=\partial_{x}^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}\Delta_{x,\mathcal{Z}}+\left(\dive_{\tilde{\omega}}(\partial_{x})-\frac{\alpha n}{x}\right)\partial_{x},

where
\Delta_{x,\mathcal{Z}}
are the Laplacians on the level sets of the distance function
x
.

From now on we focus only on the behavior from right and remove the modulus sign in
|x|
. Left side is handled in the same manner.

3.1.
Closure of
\Delta-cS

We can find the closure of
\Delta-cS
using the following result proved in
[
22
, Proposition 4.6]

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be Hilbert spaces, and let
D
be a Banach subspace of
H_{1}
equipped with a norm
\|\cdot\|_{D}
such that the inclusion map
(D,\|\cdot\|_{D})\to(H_{1},\|\cdot\|_{H_{1}})
is continuous in
H_{1}
. Let
A:D\to H_{2}
be a continuous operator and assume that:

(1)

The range of
A
is closed.

(2)

The kernel
\ker A\subset H_{1}
is closed with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{H_{1}}
.

Then the operator
A
with domain
D
is closed, i.e.,
D
is complete with respect to the graph norm
\|u\|_{A}=\|u\|_{H_{1}}+\|Au\|_{H_{2}}
.

In particular, we will take
H_{1}=H_{2}=L^{2}(M,\omega)
and
D
to be a certain weighted Sobolev space, so that the operator
\Delta-cS:D\subset L^{2}(M,\omega)\to L^{2}(M,\omega)
will be left semi-Fredholm. In this case the conditions of the Proposition
3.1
are satisfied.

We can rewrite
\Delta-cS
as a weighted operator
Q:=x^{2}(\Delta-cS)
:

(31)

\Delta-cS=x^{-2}Q:=\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(x^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+x^{2(1+\alpha)}\Delta_{x,\mathcal{Z}}+\left(x\dive_{\tilde{\omega}}(\partial_{x})-\alpha n\right)x\partial_{x}-cx^{2}S\right).

If we look at the closure of
\Delta-cS
as an operator from
L^{2}(M,\omega)
to itself, we can equivalently look at the closure of

Q:L^{2}(M,\omega)\to x^{2}L^{2}(M,\omega).

We have

\omega=x^{1+n}\omega_{\alpha}

where
\omega_{\alpha}\in\Omega_{\alpha}
is a compatible density. Consequently

L^{2}(M,\omega)=L^{2}(M,x^{1+n}\omega_{\alpha})\simeq x^{-\frac{1+n}{2}}L^{2}(M,\omega_{\alpha}).

We see that
Q\in\text{Diff}^{2}_{\alpha}(M)
and thus continuous as an operator

Q:x^{2-\frac{1+n}{2}}H^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha})\to x^{2-\frac{1+n}{2}}L^{2}(M,\omega_{\alpha}).

If
Q
is also left semi-Fredholm, then the operator
\Delta-cS=x^{-2}Q
will be also left semi-Fredholm on the same space. Note that by definition we have that
x^{2-\frac{1+n}{2}}H^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha})\simeq x^{2}H_{\alpha}^{2}(M,\omega)

is continuously embedded into
x^{-\frac{1+n}{2}}L^{2}(M,\omega_{\alpha})\simeq L^{2}(M,\omega)
and contains
C^{\infty}_{c}(M)
functions as a dense set. Hence proving that
Q
is left semi-Fredholm would imply that the closure of
\Delta-cS
on the core
C^{\infty}_{c}(M)
is given by
x^{2-\frac{1+n}{2}}H^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha})
. The rest of this subsection is dedicated to the proof of this fact (this is the content in part of Theorem
3.5
).

In order to apply Theorem
2.10
we need to check that the operators
\widehat{N(Q)}
are injective on
x^{\delta}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},dx)
with

\delta=2-\frac{1+n}{2}+\frac{1+\alpha n+n}{2}=2+\frac{\alpha n}{2}.

We can write down
\widehat{N(Q)}
explicitly using asymptotics of the scalar curvature (
6
). They are given by

(32)

\widehat{N(Q)}=x^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-\alpha n\,x\partial_{x}+c\alpha n(\alpha n+\alpha+2)+\sigma_{\mathcal{Z}}(y,\hat{\eta})x^{2(1+\alpha)},

where
\sigma_{\mathcal{Z}}<0
is the symbol map of
\Delta_{0,\mathcal{Z}}
.

Before studying this operator let us consider a more general operator of the form

(33)

T:=x^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+ax\partial_{x}+b-hx^{2\beta},

with
\beta>0
,
h>0
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
constants. Define the following quantities

\mu=(a-1)^{2}-4b

and

\nu=\frac{\sqrt{|\mu|}}{2\beta}.

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
I(x,p)
and
K(x,p)
be modified Bessel functions of order
p\geq 0
. For imaginary orders define

\tilde{I}(x,p)=\Re I(x,ip),\qquad\tilde{K}(x,p)=K(x,ip).

Then equation

(34)

Tu=0,

has two independent solutions of the form

(35)

u_{1}=x^{\frac{1-a}{2}}I\left(\frac{\sqrt{h}x^{\beta}}{\beta},\nu\right),\qquad u_{2}=x^{\frac{1-a}{2}}K\left(\frac{\sqrt{h}x^{\beta}}{\beta},\nu\right),

when
\mu\geq 0
. When
\mu<0
, replace
I,K
in the formulas with
\tilde{I},\tilde{K}
.

Proof.

The proof is just a change of variables of the form

y=\frac{\sqrt{h}}{\beta}x^{\beta},\qquad v(y)=y^{(a-1)/2\beta}u(y),

which reduces (
33
) to a modified Bessel equation.
∎

We need to understand when
T
is injective as an operator from
x^{\delta}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},dx)
to itself for some real parameter
\delta
. We can reduce this question to
\delta=0
by considering the conjugation

T\mapsto x^{-\delta}Tx^{\delta}.

In this case

is transformed to an operator of the same form where one has to replace

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\mapsto 2\delta+a,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\mapsto\delta^{2}+\delta(a-1)+b.

After this transformation the elements of the kernel are the same functions (
35

.

Proposition 3.3
.

The operator (
33
) mapping

T:x^{\delta}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},dx)\to x^{\delta}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},dx)

has a non-trivial kernel if and only if

\Re\left(\frac{1-a}{2}-\delta-\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{2}\right)>-\frac{1}{2}.

Remark 6
.

One can give a different interpretation of Proposition 3.2. Consider the operator
T
and write down its indicial polynomial

(36)

p(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}+(a-1)\lambda+b.

Then we can reformulate Proposition
3.3
as follows. Operator
T
is injective on
x^{\delta}L^{2}
if and only if

\Re\lambda_{-}-\delta\leq-1/2,

where
\lambda_{-}
is the root of the indicial polynomial with the smaller real part. Indeed, the exponential decay of the solutions at infinity imply that the injectivity questions are completely determined by the restriction of the operator to a neighborhood of zero. But in this case we can essentially use Mellin transform to solve approximately the equation
Tu=0
.

Proof.

As already mentioned without any loss of generality we can assume that
\delta=0
. We have the following well-known asymptotics for modified Bessel functions for
\nu\geq 0

[
1
]
:

\displaystyle I(x,\nu)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}x^{-1/2}e^{x},

\displaystyle K(x,\nu)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}x^{-1/2}e^{-x}

as
x\to+\infty
and

\displaystyle I(x,\nu)\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{(x/2)^{|\nu|}}{|\nu|!},&\nu\in\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\},\\
\dfrac{(x/2)^{\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu+1)},&\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\},\end{cases}

\displaystyle K(x,\nu)\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu)}{2}\left(\dfrac{2}{x}\right)^{\nu},&\nu>0,\\
-\ln(x/2)+\gamma,&\nu=0,\end{cases}

where
\gamma
is the Euler constant and
x\to 0+
. For the imaginary order the asymptotics at infinity are the same, while the asymptotics as
x\to 0+
are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(x,\nu)

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{\sinh(\pi\nu)}{\pi\nu}\right)^{1/2}\cos\left(\nu\ln\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)-\gamma_{\nu}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(x,\nu)

\displaystyle\sim-\left(\frac{\pi\nu}{\sinh(\pi\nu)}\right)^{1/2}\sin\left(\nu\ln\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)-\gamma_{\nu}\right),

where
\gamma_{\nu}
is a real continuous function of the order
\nu
.

From the asymptotics it follows immediately that the
I
-solution of (
33
) is never in
L^{2}
for both real and imaginary orders because of the exponential growth. So the kernel of this operator can be only a multiple of the
K
-solution. Since the
K
-solution decreases exponentially at infinity, we only need to understand when it is square integrable close to zero. From the given asymptotics it follows that this is the case, if

\frac{1-a}{2}-\beta\sqrt{\frac{(a-1)^{2}}{4\beta^{2}}-\frac{b}{\beta^{2}}}>-\frac{1}{2},

when the order of the modified Bessel functions is real and

\frac{1-a}{2}>-\frac{1}{2},

when the order is imaginary.

∎

Remark 7
.

Note first that there is a unique critical value of
\delta
, such that for all

. Secondly, this value does not depend on the constant

We can now analyse the operators

. First note that as discussed in Remark

for all

simultaneously. As discussed, we can look at the root of the indicial polynomial (

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=2+\frac{\alpha n}{2},

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=-\alpha n,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=c\alpha n(\alpha n+\alpha+2).

The two roots are given by the expression:

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\alpha n\pm\sqrt{(1+\alpha n)^{2}-4c\alpha n(2+\alpha+\alpha n)}\right).

The condition for injectivity is given by

but this is always satisfied, because after simplifying the parameters above we always have

Thus we have proven

Proposition 3.4
.

The operators
\widehat{N(Q)}_{(y,\hat{\eta})}
are injective for all possible
\alpha>-1
,

,

Thus by Theorem

This can be false for certain values of the parameter

is a continuous monotone non-increasing function of the parameter

To include this important class of generic almost-Riemannian structures, we would also like to say something about the closure of those operators for the forbidden values of the parameter

. To do this, we consider the closure of smooth functions

on a slightly bigger space than

. The resulting space will contain the original closure as a subspace. So let us consider the closure of compactly supported smooth functions in

. Similarly we can consider the closure in

, which is given by
x^{2+\varepsilon-\frac{1+n}{2}}H^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha})
. The inclusions

are continuous. Exactly the same discussion as before proves the following result.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
M
be a
\alpha
-Grushin manifold of dimension

. If
\mu\neq 4
, then the closure of
\Delta-cS
or the minimal domain is given by

D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)=x^{2-\frac{1+n}{2}}H^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha}).

If
\mu=4
, then

\bigcup_{\varepsilon>0}x^{2+\varepsilon-\frac{1+n}{2}}H^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha})\subset D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)\subset\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}x^{2-\varepsilon-\frac{1+n}{2}}H^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha}).

3.2.
Adjoint of
\Delta-cS

The domain of the adjoint, or the maximal domain, of
\Delta-cS
is given by

D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(M,\omega)\,:\,(\Delta-cS)u\in L^{2}(M,\omega)\right\}.

In order to find when
\Delta-cS
is essentially self-adjoint and construct its self-adjoint extensions, we need a better description of this space modulo functions in the minimal domain.

Here again, we use our left parametrix. However, we need a slightly refined version given in Theorem
5.7
, which compared to Theorem
2.10
, states that one can invert
\alpha
-elliptic operators modulo not simply compact operators, but modulo operators which lie in the large calculus. Assume first
\mu\neq 4
. By the main parametrix Theorem
5.7
we have a left parametrix
G
and a very residual harmonic projector
\pi_{ker}
such that

Gx^{2}(\Delta-cS)=\id-\pi_{ker}.

In particular, if
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)u=f
, then we obtain

u=Gf+\pi_{ker}u.

If
u\in D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)
, by definition
u\in L^{2}(M,\omega)
and
f\in x^{2}L^{2}(M,\omega)
, thus by construction
Gf
will lie in the closure. We are thus reduced to studying
\pi_{ker}u
. This implies that

(37)

D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)=\left(\ker(\Delta-cS)\cap L^{2}(M,\omega)\right)+D_{\min}(\Delta-cS).

A consequence of (
9
) is that
\Delta-cS
is essentially self-adjoint if and only if

\left(\ker(\Delta-cS)\cap L^{2}(M,\omega)\right)\subset D_{\min}(\Delta-cS).

We will determine the precise form of elements in the kernel by considering them as limits of functions with polyhomogeneous expansions. Namely, by Theorem
1.5
elements of
\ker(\Delta-cS)
have a weak asymptotic expansion (
11
), which we repeat here

(38)

u\sim\sum_{\lambda\in\emph{Spec}_{b}(L)}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{p=0}^{p_{\lambda}}x^{\lambda+\theta}(\log x)^{p}\,u_{\lambda,\theta,p}(y),\qquad x\to 0+,\quad u_{\lambda,\theta,p}\in H_{\alpha}^{-r_{\lambda,\theta}}(\mathcal{Z})

where
\lambda
are roots of the indicial polynomial, and the order of negative regularity is
r_{\lambda,\theta}=\Re(\lambda)+(i+j)-\delta+\tfrac{1}{2}
for the value
\theta=(1+\alpha)i+j\in\Theta
. Here we say that this is a weak asymptotic expansion in the sense that there is such an expansion of the integral
\int_{\mathcal{Z}}u(x,y)\chi(y)dy
for any test functions on
\mathcal{Z}
. As mentioned in the introduction, we give the proof of this technical result in Section
5
.

Let us first focus only on the polyhomogeneous elements of the kernel, i.e., functions for which the coefficients
u_{\lambda,\theta,p}(y)
in the expansion (
38
) are actually smooth. Notice that in this case the remainder can be an element of the the minimal domain. It is clear that
x^{2-\frac{1+n}{2}}H^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha})\subset x^{2-\frac{1+n}{2}}L^{2}_{\alpha}(M,\omega_{\alpha})
. If
\mu\neq 4
, then Theorem
3.5
implies that any function
u\in D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)
must have asymptotics

(39)

u(x)=o\left(x^{\frac{3+\alpha n}{2}}\right),\qquad x\to 0+.

Thus we need all of the expansions modulo these terms. On the other hand, if
u\in D_{\max}
, then
u\in L^{2}(M,\omega)
. Thus functions
u\in D_{\max}
must have asymptotics satisfying

(40)

u(x)=o\left(x^{\frac{-1+\alpha n}{2}}\right),\qquad x\to 0+.

Let us use asymptotic expansion (
38
) as an ansatz assuming in addition that all of the coefficients are smooth functions. This is essentially the Frobenius method and we are reduced to distinguishing between two cases

(1)

The roots of the indicial polynomial are such that
\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}\notin\Theta
(see equation (
10
));

(2)

The roots of the indicial polynomial are separated by an element of
\Theta
.

In the first case, there are no log terms and we get a pure power series asymptotics.

To obtain the desired asymptotic expansions the first step is to rewrite operator
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)
as a power series with powers in
\Theta
using its indicial part and lower order terms. To do so, we expand formally all coefficients of
s^{2}\Delta
in Taylor series and use Proposition
1.3
of the curvature term. Recall the explicit form of
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)
given by (
31
). We get:

\displaystyle\dive_{\tilde{\omega}}(\partial_{x})(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\dive_{\theta}(y)x^{\theta},

\displaystyle S(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{0}}{x^{2}}+\sum_{\theta\in\Theta\smallsetminus\{0\}}S_{\theta}(y)x^{-2+\theta},

\displaystyle\Delta_{x,\mathcal{Z}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\Delta_{\theta,\mathcal{Z}}.

Note that
\Delta_{0,\mathcal{Z}}
, as discussed previously, is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the singular set. For brevity we will often omit the dependence of the coefficients on
y
until the rest of this subsection.
Now we rewrite
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)
as

x^{2}(\Delta-cS)=I(x^{2}(\Delta-cS))+\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}x^{1+\theta}\dive_{\theta}x\partial_{x}-\sum_{\theta\in\Theta\smallsetminus\{0\}}cS_{\theta}x^{\theta}+\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}x^{2(1+\alpha)+\theta}\Delta_{\theta,\mathcal{Z}}.

Note that in the non-indicial part of the exrepssion all of the powers of
x
belong to
\Theta\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

We can now plug-in the asymptotics ansatz

(41)

u=\sum_{\varphi\in\Theta}a_{\varphi}(y)x^{\lambda+\varphi}

into
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)u=0
. Recall that

I(x^{2}(\Delta-cS))x^{\lambda}=p(\lambda)x^{\lambda},

where
p
is the indicial polynomial of
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)
. Thus

(42)

\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\Theta}a_{\varphi}p(\lambda+\varphi)x^{\lambda+\varphi}+\sum_{\varphi\in\Theta}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta\smallsetminus\{0\}}R_{c,\theta}(a_{\varphi})x^{\lambda+\varphi+\theta}=0,

where
R_{c,\theta}
are differential operators on
\mathcal{Z}
obtained by combining all of the terms of the same order. We solve this equation term by term starting with the principal one:

p(\lambda)a_{0}x^{\lambda}=0.

Thus
\lambda
must be one of the two roots
\lambda_{\pm}
of the indicial polynomial. Note that the first coefficient
a_{0}
can be an arbitrary smooth function on
\mathcal{Z}
. We then proceed inductively solving for all of the remaining coefficients. For each term we get an equation of the type

p(\lambda+\varphi)a_{\varphi}+\text{ lower order terms }=0,

which can be solved for
a_{\varphi}
under the assumption that
\lambda+\varphi
is not an indicial root. This inductive procedure shows that if the first coefficient of the weak expansion is smooth, then all the remaining coefficients are also smooth and
u
is, in fact, polyhomogeneous.

If we are in one of the critical cases and the two indicial roots are separated by an element of
\Theta
, then clearly we can solve recursively (
42
) with
\lambda=\lambda_{+}
. This gives a family of solutions
u_{+}
. In order to find the second family of solutions
u_{-}
we can use a trick from the classical Frobenius method, namely we take

u_{-}=Cu_{+}\log x+\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}a^{-}_{\theta}x^{\lambda_{-}+\theta}.

Where
C\neq 0
is a constant that must be determined. If we plug this ansatz into
x^{2}(\Delta-cS)u_{-}=0
, we obtain a modification of (
42
) and we proceed in a similar manner. And there are no other solutions of the form (
11
).

Remark 8
.

The coefficient
a_{\varphi}^{-}
, which could not be determined from the recurrence relations can be taken to be any smooth function. It is accustomed to take them to be zero, since a non-zero choice would correspond to a different choice of of the principal coefficient

, then

The following characterization of the maximal domain holds.

Theorem 3.6

The maximal domain of
\Delta-cS
is given by

(43)

D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)=\overline{(\spann\{u_{+}\}\oplus\spann\{u_{-}\})\cap L^{2}(M,\omega)}^{||\cdot||_{\emph{Gr}}}+D_{\min}(\Delta-cS),

where the span is taken over an arbitrary choice of the leading term coefficients as smooth functions on the singular set
\mathcal{Z}
and the closure is in graph norm of the operator
\Delta-cS
.

Proof.

Since the minimal domain by definition is closed in the graph norm, and all elements of
(\spann\{u_{+}\}\oplus\spann\{u_{-}\})\cap L^{2}
belong to the maximal domain, it is clear that the space on the right hand side of (
43

D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)
. Hence, we only need to prove the other direction.

For this, we will show that any element of
\mathcal{D}_{\text{max}}(\Delta-cS)
can be approximated in the graph norm by polyhomogeneous elements of
\mathcal{D}_{\text{max}}(\Delta-cS)
. Consider any
v\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{max}}(\Delta-cS)
and let
\phi
be local cut off function. Namely, assume that
\phi(x,y)=\phi_{1}(x)\phi_{2}(y)
where
\phi_{1}\equiv 1
in a neighborhood of
x=0
, and
\phi_{2}
a smooth bump function supported in a coordinate chart
(x,y)
centered at a point
(0,y_{0})=p\in\mathcal{Z}

v\ast\psi(x,y)=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}v(x,y-y^{\prime})\psi(y^{\prime})dy^{\prime},

which has an asymptotic expansion as

induced by the expansion of

, hence

. Further, by definition of the weak expansion of

38

thus

Now choose

to be a smooth bump function as above, compactly supported in a coordinate chart centered around

\phi_{\varepsilon}(y):=\varepsilon^{-n}\phi(y/\varepsilon)

||\hat{v}_{\varepsilon}-\hat{v}||_{L^{2}}\to 0\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0

(\Delta-cS)v_{\varepsilon}=(\Delta-cS)v\ast\phi_{\varepsilon}+[(\Delta-cS),\phi_{\varepsilon}\ast(-)]v
is in

(since

. This completes the proof of the claim of graph norm convergence.

Now, having shown this graph norm approximation of

by polyhomogeneous elements

of the maximal domain, by projecting such elements into

∎

Proof of Theorem

Since

(2)

4.
Self-adjoint extensions

4.1.
Asymmetry forms method and the symplectic linear algebra of self-adjoint extensions

Let us recall the asymmetry form method which can be used to construct self-adjoint extensions of an operator. Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
A:D(A)\subset H\to H
a densely defined symmetric operator. As discussed in the introduction, we can associate to
A
two natural extensions: the closure
\overline{A}
and the adjoint
A^{*}
defined as usual with

D(A)\subset D(\overline{A})\subset D(A^{*}).

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\pm}
be the two deficiency spaces of
A
, i.e.

\mathcal{D}_{\pm}=\ker(A^{*}{\pm}iI).

Then a result due to Von Neumann
[
23
, Theorem 3.4]
states that

D(A^{*})=D(\overline{A})\oplus\mathcal{D}_{+}\oplus\mathcal{D}_{-}

The asymmetry forms of an operator
B
are defined as:

\omega_{B}(x,y):=(x,By)-(Bx,y),\qquad\omega_{B}(x):=\omega_{B}(x,x),\qquad\forall x,y\in D(B).

If
B=A
, then
\omega_{A}=0
thanks to the assumption of
A
symmetric, so we instead consider
B=A^{*}
. In this case
\omega_{A^{*}}
measures how far
A^{*}
is far from being a symmetric operator.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
x\in D(\overline{A})
, then
\omega_{A^{*}}(x,\cdot)=0
.

Proof.

We know that
\overline{A}=(A^{*})^{*}
and thus by definition if
z=\overline{A}x
, then

(x,A^{*}y)-(z,y)=0,\qquad\forall y\in D(A^{*}).

But
D(\overline{A})\subset D(A^{*})
and hence
z=\overline{A}x=A^{*}x
and

\omega_{A^{*}}(x,y)=(x,A^{*}y)-(A^{*}x,y)=0,\qquad\forall y\in D(A^{*}).

∎

We can now see that the only obstruction for
A^{*}
to be symmetric is given by the deficiency spaces, and all symmetric extensions are given by subspaces of
D(A^{*})/D(\overline{A})\simeq\mathcal{D}_{+}\oplus\mathcal{D}_{-}
on which
\omega_{A^{*}}
vanishes. We can write explicitly the quadratic form
\omega_{A}
using the definition of the deficiency spaces:

(44)

\omega_{A^{*}}(x_{+}+x_{-})=2i(\|x_{+}\|^{2}-\|x_{-}\|^{2}),\qquad x_{\pm}\in\mathcal{D}_{\pm}.

In particular, if one of the deficiency spaces is trivial and the other is not, there are no non-trivial symmetric extensions.

A self-adjoint extension neccesarily must be a maximal symmetric extension. However, this is not sufficient. The form
\omega_{A^{*}}
must also be non-degenerate on
D(A^{*})/D(\overline{A})

[
23
]
. For example, if the deficiency indexes were different, then the form
\omega_{A^{*}}
would have a kernel on
D(A^{*})/D(\overline{A})
. If the form
\omega_{A^{*}}
is non-degenerate, then indeed, every self-adjoint extension is characterized by a
Lagrangian space
inside
D(A^{*})/D(\overline{A})
, i.e., a maximal subspace on which the asymmetry form vanishes. This allows us to classify all of the self-adjoint extensions of real-differential operators. Namely, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
A:D(A)\subset H\to H
a densely defined symmetric operator. If
\omega_{A^{*}}
is non-degenerate on
D(A^{*})/D(\overline{A})
, then

(1)

(D(A^{*})/D(\overline{A}),\omega_{A^{*}})
is a symplectic space;

(2)

there is a natural bijection between self-adjoint extensions of
A
and Lagrangian subspaces of
(D(A^{*})/D(\overline{A}),\omega_{A^{*}})
.

4.2.
Construction of self-adjoint extensions

We are now ready to construct the self-adjoint extensions of
\Delta-cS
in a number of cases. We separate the discussion into two parts: when
\mu<0
and when
0\leq\mu<4
. Both cases are studied using an explicit characterization of the asymmetry form.

Consider a tubular neighborhood around the singular set
\mathcal{Z}=\{x=0\}
. From the proof of Theorem
3.6
it follows that when
\mu<4
the adjoint
D_{\max}(\Delta-cS)
consists of functions

u(x,y)=(u^{r}_{+}(x,y)+u^{r}_{-}(x,y)+u^{l}_{+}(x,y)+u^{l}_{-}(x,y))\chi(|x|)\mod D_{\min}(\Delta-cS),

where
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is a smooth cut-off function satisfying
\chi|_{|t|<1/2}\equiv 1
and
\chi|_{|t|>1}\equiv 0
. Further, the coefficient functions are given by

u^{r}_{\pm}(x,y):=\begin{cases}u_{\pm}(x,y),&x>0,\\
0,&x<0.\end{cases}

and similarly

u^{l}_{\pm}(x,y):=\begin{cases}u_{\pm}(-x,y),&x<0,\\
0,&x>0.\end{cases}

Here
u_{\pm}
are functions with asymptotic expansion (
41
). Assume first that their principal terms are smooth functions of
y\in\mathcal{Z}
. Functions
u^{l,r}_{\pm}
can have all principal coefficients different from each other. For the sake of brevity, we denote the coefficients of the principal term as
a_{\pm}^{r,l}
omitting the zeros. Also since those functions are compactly supported in a small tubular neighborhood around
\mathcal{Z}
we extend them by zero.

Using the Green’s identity we can write down explicitly the asymmetry form

\displaystyle\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Z}}(\overline{u}\Delta v-v\Delta\overline{u})\omega=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\int_{|x|\geq\varepsilon}(\overline{u}\Delta v-v\Delta\overline{u})\omega=

(45)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\left(-\int_{x=\varepsilon}(\overline{u}\partial_{x}v-v\partial_{x}\overline{u})\sigma_{\varepsilon}-\int_{x=-\varepsilon}(\overline{u}(-\partial_{x})v-v(-\partial_{x})\overline{u})\sigma_{-\varepsilon})\right),

where
\sigma_{\pm\varepsilon}

are induced measures on submanifolds
\{x=\pm\varepsilon\}
. Note that all of them are diffeomorphic to
\mathcal{Z}
.

4.2.1.
Case
\mu<0

In this case the indicial roots are complex conjugates
\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
. For
x>0
let us write the asymptotic expansions for
u
and
v
:

(46)

\displaystyle u(x,y)

\displaystyle=a^{r}_{-}(y)x^{\bar{\lambda}}+a^{r}_{+}(y)x^{\lambda}+o(x^{\Re(\lambda)}),

(47)

\displaystyle v(x,y)

\displaystyle=b^{r}_{-}(y)x^{\bar{\lambda}}+b^{r}_{+}(y)x^{\lambda}+o(x^{\Re(\lambda)}),

For
x<0
we have a similar expression, which we omit. Assume for now that coefficients
a^{r}_{\pm}
and
b^{r}_{\pm}
are smooth functions on the singular set
\mathcal{Z}
. We can then substitute these asymptotic expansions into (
45
). From this we have

(48)

\int_{x=\varepsilon}(\overline{u}\partial_{x}u-u\partial_{x}\overline{u})\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\int_{x=\varepsilon}2i\Im(\lambda)\left((\bar{a}^{r}_{+}b^{r}_{+}-\bar{a}^{r}_{-}b^{r}_{-})+o(1)\right)\varepsilon^{2\Re(\lambda)-1}\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y).

Next we write a similar expression for
x<0
and take the limit when
\varepsilon\to 0+
. This limit exists by an application of the dominated convergence theorem since
2\Re(\lambda)-1=\alpha n
, and simultaneously

\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-\alpha n}\tilde{\sigma}_{\varepsilon},

leading to cancelation of the singular orders of
\varepsilon
. Here
\tilde{\sigma}_{\varepsilon}
is the restriction of the companion volume form
\tilde{\omega}
to
x=\varepsilon
, which can be viewed as a family of smooth volume forms on
\mathcal{Z}
with a well-defined limit at
\varepsilon\to 0
. Doing this procedure gives us the final formula for the assymetry form

\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)=i\sqrt{|\mu|}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\left(\bar{a}^{r}_{+}b^{r}_{+}-\bar{a}^{r}_{-}b^{r}_{-}+\bar{a}^{l}_{+}b^{l}_{+}-\bar{a}^{l}_{-}b^{l}_{-}\right)\tilde{\sigma}(y).

We introduce new variables

a_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{r}_{\pm}\\
a^{l}_{\pm}\end{pmatrix},\qquad b_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}b^{r}_{\pm}\\
b^{l}_{\pm}\end{pmatrix},

to shorten the last expression to

(49)

\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)=i\sqrt{|\mu|}\left(\langle a_{+},b_{+}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}-\langle a_{-},b_{-}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}\right).

Note that this expression can be extended to the case when these principal coefficients are functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
. However, Theorem
1.5
tells us that this is not enough, because these coefficients can lie in some negative order Sobolev spaces and there is no natural extension of the form above to a pair of such functions. Nevertheless, if either
a
or
b
is smooth, then this pairing indeed is well-defined. To see this, consider the operator
-\Delta_{0,\mathcal{Z}}
, the regularized Laplacian operator on the singular set (see (
30
)). This is a non-negative elliptic operator on a closed manifold and hence has a discrete spectrum
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots
with the corresponding eigenfunctions
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, which are smooth functions by the classical elliptic regularity. These functions
\varphi_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
and we get the usual analogue of the classical Fourier theory. In particular, every element can be represented with respect to this basis as

f(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}\varphi_{k}(y)

and one can characterize Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathcal{Z})
as distributions
f
on
\mathcal{Z}
for which

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+\lambda_{k})^{s}|f_{k}|^{2}<\infty.

For smooth functions
f
the series will converge for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now assume that
a_{\pm}
and
b_{\pm}
are smooth. Set
a_{\pm,k}
and
b_{\pm,k}
be their Fourier coefficients in the above sense. We can rewrite expression (
49
) as

(50)

\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)=i\sqrt{|\mu|}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\langle a_{+,k},b_{+,k}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}-\langle a_{-,k},b_{-,k}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\right).

The crux of the proof is that if we keep one of the functions smooth, then by a density argument, we can assume that the other function belongs to any
H^{s}(\mathcal{Z})
, as the corresponding series remains convergent. Hence the above formula holds as long as one of the two functions is assumed to be smooth. So we can proceed as follows. First we find a reasonable candidate for a self-adjoint extension, i.e. one that is represented by a linear subspace of
D_{max}(\Delta-cS)/D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)
. Then we prove that this subspace is in fact Lagrangian, by testing against functions with a smooth principal term via (
50
).

Using the formula for the asymmetry form we are now ready to construct self-adjoint extensions of
\Delta-cS
. We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
\mu<0
the form
\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}
is non-degenerate on
D_{max}(\Delta-cS)/D_{\min}(\Delta-cS)
.

Proof.

It suffices to check that for any
u\in D_{max}(\Delta-cS)
outside the closure there exists
v\in D_{max}(\Delta-cS)
, such that
\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)\neq 0
. By our characterization of the adjoint,
u
must have an asymptotic expansion of the form (
51
), with the principal coefficient being in some Sobolev space by Theorem
1.5
. Since
u
is not in the closure, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that at least one of the
k
-th harmonics,
a_{+,k}
or
a_{-,k}
, is not zero. Then we choose
v\in D_{max}(\Delta-cS)
with an asymptotic expansion with principal term in (
52
) given by
b^{r,l}_{\pm}(y)=\pm a^{r,l}_{\pm,k}\varphi_{k}(y)
, which exists by Lemma
2.3
. Then we have

\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)=i\sqrt{|\mu|}\left(|a_{+,k}|^{2}+|a_{-,k}|^{2}\right)>0.

∎

Now we can construct some natural self-adjoint extensions of
\Delta-cS
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We start by noting that the bilinear form
\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}
is completely determined by its quadratic form. We have from (
50
)

\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u)=i\sqrt{|\mu|}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(|a_{+,k}|^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}-|a_{-,k}|^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\right).

So if
U:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a unitary operator, we can consider a closed subspace
\Lambda_{U}
of
D_{max}(\Delta-cS)
determined by the relations
a_{+,k}=Ua_{-,k}
. First note that this space is isotropic. Indeed, by definition
\mathcal{A}_{phg}(M\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})\cap\Lambda_{U}
is dense in
\Lambda_{U}
. For this space we can use formula (
50
) to check that
\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}|_{\mathcal{A}_{phg}(M\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z})\cap\Lambda_{U}}=0
. Thus, we can extend it to whole
\Lambda_{U}
by continuity. It only remains to prove that this subspace is maximal. If it is not maximal, then there should exist
v\notin\Lambda_{U}
such that
\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)=0
for all
u\in\Lambda_{U}
. Inverting this statement we see that maximality will follow, if for any
v\in D_{max}(\Delta-cS)
such that
v\notin\Lambda_{U}
, one can find
u\in\Lambda_{U}
such that
\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)\neq 0
.

The proof of this fact is similar to the proof of Lemma
4.4
. If
v\notin\Lambda_{U}
, then there must exist at least one harmonic
b_{k}
such that
b_{+,k}\neq Ub_{-,k}
. Choose
u\in\Lambda_{U}
be a polyhomogeneous function whose first coefficient
a_{-}(y)=a_{-,k}\varphi_{k}(y)
and
a_{+}(y)=Ua_{-,k}\varphi_{k}(y)
. Then we have

\omega_{(\Delta-cS)^{*}}(u,v)=i\sqrt{|\mu|}\left(\langle a_{+,k},b_{+,k}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}-\langle a_{-,k},b_{-,k}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\right).

The quadratic form on the right is a symplectic form on
\mathbb{C}^{4}
, and the graph of
U
given by
a_{+,k}=Ua_{-,k}
determines a Lagrangian subspace
\Lambda_{k}
, because every isotropic subspace of dimension two is Lagrangian. But by construction,
(b_{+,k},b_{-,k})
does not belong to
\Lambda_{k}
, and hence, by maximality there must exist
(Ua_{-,k},a_{-,k})
for which the expression above is not zero.
∎

Finally, the boundedness result follows essentially from Schur’s inequality. Using Cauchy-Schwartz in the second inequality and Fubini’s theorem in the last equality below, we find

\displaystyle|\langle A^{\prime\prime}u\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}},v\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{M}(A^{\prime\prime}u\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}})v\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}\right|=\left|\int_{M_{\alpha}^{2}}\beta_{L}^{*}\left((A^{\prime\prime}u\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}})v\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}\right)\right|=

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{M_{\alpha}^{2}}\mathcal{K}_{A^{\prime\prime}}(\beta_{R}^{*}u)(\beta_{L}^{*}v)\mu_{\alpha}\right|\leq\int_{M_{\alpha}^{2}}|\mathcal{K}_{A^{\prime\prime}}||\beta^{*}_{R}u||\beta^{*}_{L}v|\mu_{\alpha}\leq

\displaystyle(\beta_{R})_{*}(|\mathcal{K}_{A^{\prime\prime}}|\mu_{\alpha})\leq C\gamma_{\alpha}

Compactness follows from the compactness of embeddings of weighted Sobolev spaces on manifolds of bounded geometry (see, for example,

5.3.
Construction of a Parametrix

The goal of this section is to finish the proof that an
\alpha
-elliptic differential operator admits an
\alpha
-pseudodifferential inverse modulo compact operators, acting on weighted Sobolev spaces with weights governed by the indicial roots of the operator.

Consider a differential operator
P
which in local coordinates can be written as

P=\sum_{j+|\beta|\leq m}a_{j,\beta}(x,y)(x\partial_{x})^{j}(x^{1+\alpha}\partial_{y})^{\beta},

which belongs to
\Psi^{m}_{\alpha}(M)
. Its principal symbol in this coordinates is given by

{}^{\alpha}\sigma_{m}(P)=\sum_{j+|\beta|\leq s}a_{j,\beta}(x,y)\xi^{j}\eta^{\beta}.

We define the normal operator
N(P)
at a point

to be defined as an operator obtained by restricting to the front face the kernel of the lift of
P
to
M^{2}_{\alpha}
. Using local coordinates

(s,u,\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\left(\frac{\tilde{x}}{x},\frac{y-\tilde{y}}{x^{1+\alpha}},\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\right),

we obtain

(57)

N(P)=\sum_{j+|\beta|\leq m}a_{j,\beta}(0,\tilde{y})(s\partial_{s})^{j}(s^{1+\alpha}\partial_{u})^{\beta}.

We also need for the parametrix construction the family of indicial family
I_{\zeta}(P)
, which is defined as

P(x^{z}(\log x)^{p}f(x,y))=x^{z}(\log x)^{p}I_{\zeta}(P;y)f(0,y)+O(x^{z}(\log x)^{p-1}),

for all
f\in C^{\infty}(M),\zeta\in\mathbb{C},p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In local coordinates this arises as the Mellin transform of the operator

I(P;y)=\sum_{j\leq m}a_{j,0}(0,y)(s\partial_{s})^{j},

thus

I_{\zeta}(P;y)=\sum_{j\leq m}a_{j,0}(0,y)\zeta^{j}

Definition 5.2
.

Recall from Definition
2.12
the definition of the boundary spectrum. We also have a refined notion of boundary spectrum which keeps track of multiplicities; this is defined as

\widetilde{\text{Spec}}_{b}(P,y)=\{(z,l)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}:I_{\zeta}(P;y)^{-1}\textit{ has a pole at $z$ of order $\geq l+1$ }.\}

As before we say that
P
has
constant indicial roots
if the discrete set
\text{Spec}_{b}(P,y)\subset\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}
is independent of the choice of
y\in\partial M
.

To finally state the result we introduce some sets which will arise as the index sets of the parametrix and projectors: let
C\in\mathbb{R}
and denote

\displaystyle\Sigma^{+}(C)

\displaystyle=\{(\gamma,p)\in\widetilde{\textrm{Spec}}_{b}(P):\Re\gamma>C\},

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-}(C)

\displaystyle=\{(-\gamma,p)\in\widetilde{\textrm{Spec}}_{b}(P):\Re\gamma<C\},

\displaystyle\Sigma(C)

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{+}(C)\cup\Sigma^{-}(C).

The reason such sets arise is because when constructing the parametrix we proceed in a way formally similar to the construction of an elliptic parametrix on a closed manifold: in constructing an Neumann series for
PQ-\id-R
, i.e. asymptotically summing
\id+R+R^{2}+R^{3}+...
, with each term improving the error, both symbolically and by improving decay. In order for these compositions of
R
to even be defined in the
\alpha
-calculus, we refer to Theorem
5.5
; we observe that
R
must vanish to infinite order on at least one of the side faces in order for this sum to converge even asymptotically. But further, each composition of
R
produces more complicated index sets at the front face via the extended union; the above sets are introduced merely to account for every possible term in the asymptotic expansion.

Appendix A: Scalar curvature computations

In this Appendix we prove Proposition
1.3
and compute the scalar curvature for the model space from the introduction. We use Cartan’s method of moving frames for computing the scalar curvature in an orthonormal frame. The resulting formula is likely well-known to experts, but for the sake of completeness we give a derivation here. We will use Einstein summation convention over repeated indices unless it is stated explicitly otherwise.

Let
M
be a smooth
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold. Unless stated otherwise all lower-case letter indices range from one to
n
. Let
X_{i}
be a local orthonormal frame of a Riemannian manifold and
\theta^{i}
be the dual frame. Given a Levi-Cevita connection
\nabla
, we can define the Christofel symbols
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
as

\nabla_{X_{i}}X_{j}=\Gamma^{k}_{ij}X_{k}.

We can equivalently define a connection as a family of one forms
\theta^{i}_{j}
, via

\nabla_{X}(X_{i})=\theta_{i}^{j}(X)X_{j},

which translates to

(63)

\theta^{i}_{j}=\Gamma^{i}_{kj}\theta^{k}.

The metric compatibility condition in terms of Christofel symbols reads as

\Gamma^{i}_{jk}+\Gamma^{k}_{ji}=0,

which in the language of forms is equivalent to

\theta^{i}_{j}+\theta^{j}_{i}=0.

Note that in particular
\theta^{i}_{i}=0
and
\Gamma^{i}_{ji}=0
.

The following theorem is the base of Cartan’s moving frame method.

Theorem 5.10

(
[
24
]
, Theorem 2.1)
.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold. Suppose that
\theta^{i}
is a co-frame dual to an orthonormal frame and that
\theta^{i}_{j}
are one-forms which define the Levi-Cevita connection of this metric. Then

(64)

d\theta^{i}=\theta^{j}\wedge\theta^{i}_{j}.

Using the connection forms
\theta^{i}_{j}
we can compute the curvature. Define

(65)

\Omega^{j}_{i}=d\theta^{j}_{i}+\theta^{j}_{k}\wedge\theta^{k}_{i}.

Proposition 5.11

(
[
24
]
, Proposition 2.3f)
.

We have

\Omega^{i}_{j}=R^{i}_{jkl}\theta^{k}\wedge\theta^{l},

where
R^{i}_{jkl}
are the components of the Riemann tensor in the orthonormal frame
X_{i}
.

This correspondence allows us to compute the scalar curvature of
M
as

(66)

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Omega^{j}_{i}(X_{j},X_{i}).

Lemma 5.12
.

Scalar curvature of
M
can be computed as

(67)

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}2X_{j}[\Gamma^{j}_{ii}]+\Gamma^{j}_{ki}\Gamma^{k}_{ij}+\Gamma^{j}_{jk}\Gamma^{k}_{ii}-\Gamma^{j}_{ki}\Gamma^{k}_{ji}-\Gamma^{j}_{ik}\Gamma^{k}_{ji}.

Proof.

Using (
65
) and (
63
) we have

\Omega^{j}_{i}=d(\Gamma^{j}_{ki}\theta^{k})+\Gamma^{j}_{kl}\Gamma^{k}_{mi}\theta^{l}\wedge\theta^{m}=X_{l}[\Gamma^{j}_{ki}]\theta^{l}\wedge\theta^{k}+\Gamma^{j}_{ki}d\theta^{k}+\Gamma^{j}_{lk}\Gamma^{k}_{mi}\theta^{l}\wedge\theta^{m}.

Combining this with (
64
) and (
63
) we obtain

\displaystyle\Omega^{j}_{i}

\displaystyle=X_{l}[\Gamma^{j}_{ki}]\theta^{l}\wedge\theta^{k}+\Gamma^{j}_{ki}\Gamma^{k}_{ml}\theta^{l}\wedge\theta^{m}+\Gamma^{j}_{lk}\Gamma^{k}_{mi}\theta^{l}\wedge\theta^{m}=

\displaystyle=(X_{l}[\Gamma^{j}_{mi}]+\Gamma^{j}_{ki}\Gamma^{k}_{ml}+\Gamma^{j}_{lk}\Gamma^{k}_{mi})\theta^{l}\wedge\theta^{m}.

So we find

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}[\Gamma^{j}_{ii}]-X_{i}[\Gamma^{j}_{ji}]+\Gamma^{j}_{ki}\Gamma^{k}_{ij}+\Gamma^{j}_{jk}\Gamma^{k}_{ii}-\Gamma^{j}_{ki}\Gamma^{k}_{ji}-\Gamma^{j}_{ik}\Gamma^{k}_{ji}.

Using the skew-symmetry of Christofel symbols gives us exactly the formula (
67
).
∎

\alpha
