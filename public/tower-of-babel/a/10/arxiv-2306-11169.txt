Abstract
. The category of compact Hausdorff locales is a pretopos which is filtral, meaning that every object is covered by one whose subobject lattice is isomorphic to the lattice of filters of complemented elements. We show that any filtral pretopos satisfying some mild additional conditions can be embedded into the category of compact Hausdorff locales. This result is valid in the internal logic of any topos. Assuming the principle of weak excluded middle and the existence of copowers of the terminal object in the pretopos, the image of the embedding contains all spatial compact Hausdorff locales. The notion of filtrality was introduced by V. Marra and L. Reggio (Theory Appl. Categ., 2020) to characterise the category of compact Hausdorff spaces within the class of pretoposes. Our results can be regarded as a constructive extension of the aforementioned characterisation, avoiding reference to points. If the ambient logic is classical, i.e. it satisfies excluded middle, and the prime ideal theorem for Boolean algebras holds, we obtain as a corollary the characterisation of compact Hausdorff spaces in op. cit .



1.
Introduction

A purely category theoretic characterisation of a class of mathematical objects, when available, can be regarded as a way to identify the essential properties of such a class, thus paving the way for an axiomatic study of categories sharing similar properties. A prime example is Lawvere’s Elementary Theory of the Category of Sets, see
[
18
,
19

, which was later adapted by Schlomiuk to capture the class of topological spaces
[

In a similar vein, Marra and Reggio
[
22

recently characterised the category
{\bf CHSp}
of compact Hausdorff spaces as the unique, up to (weak) equivalence, non-trivial well-pointed pretopos that is
filtral
and admits set-indexed copowers of its terminal object (see Theorem
3.3
).

Given the important role played by compact Hausdorff
locales
in the development of mathematics internally in a topos,

We hasten to point out that, as is well known, if we work in classical logic and assume the axiom of choice, or even a weaker principle such as the prime ideal theorem for Boolean algebras, then all compact Hausdorff locales are spatial, i.e. have enough points. Thus, the categories

Equivalently, the object of truth values

From the standpoint of constructive locale theory, a major shortcoming of the approach in

In the remainder of this introductory section we offer a more detailed overview of the content of the article. In Section

Preliminaries

2.1.
Frames and locales

We recall some basic (constructively valid) facts about frames and locales that will be needed in the remainder of the article. Classical references include e.g.
[
13
,
23
,
25
]
; for a constructive treatment, see
[
15
, §C1]
.

A
frame
is a complete lattice
L
satisfying the infinite distributive law

u\wedge\bigvee_{i\in I}{v_{i}}=\bigvee_{i\in I}{(u\wedge v_{i})}

for all subsets
\{u\}\cup\{v_{i}\mid i\in I\}\subseteq L
. It follows that, for each
u\in L
, the monotone map
u\wedge-\colon L\to L
preserves all suprema and thus has a right adjoint
u\to-\colon L\to L
. In particular, every element
v\in L
admits a
pseudocomplement

\neg v\coloneqq v\to 0
, the largest element of
L
disjoint from
v
.
A
frame homomorphism
is a map
L\to M
between frames that preserves finite infima and arbitrary suprema (in particular, frame homomorphisms preserve
0
and
1
). The category of frames and their homomorphisms is denoted by
{\bf Frm}
, and is complete and cocomplete; moreover, the underlying-set functor
{\bf Frm}\to{\bf Set}
is right adjoint and thus preserves limits.

The category
{\bf Loc}
is defined as the opposite of
{\bf Frm}
; the objects of
{\bf Loc}
are referred to as
locales
and its morphisms as
localic maps
.
If
X
is a locale, we write
OX
to denote the same object, this time regarded as a frame; we often refer to
OX
as the frame corresponding to
X
. Accordingly, the frame homomorphism corresponding to a localic map
f\colon X\to Y
is denoted by

f^{*}\colon OY\to OX.

Since
f^{*}
preserves (finite infima and) all suprema, it has a right adjoint
f_{*}
:

f^{*}\dashv f_{*}\colon OX\to OY.

As with any adjunction between posets, we have
f^{*}\cdot f_{*}\cdot f^{*}=f^{*}
and
f_{*}\cdot f^{*}\cdot f_{*}=f_{*}
. Thus, the following conditions are equivalent:

•

f^{*}
is surjective;

•

f_{*}
is injective.

If any of the previous equivalent conditions is satisfied, we say that
f
is a localic
injection
. Similarly,
f
is a localic
surjection
if any of the following equivalent conditions is satisfied:

•

f^{*}
is injective (equivalently, it reflects the order);

•

f_{*}
is surjective.

This terminology suggests that properties of a localic map
f
are reflected by the right adjoint
f_{*}
to the corresponding frame homomorphism. In fact, this viewpoint turns out to be far-reaching and has been adopted systematically e.g. in
[
23
]
. In the following, we shall occasionally exploit this perspective and refer to the right adjoint to a frame homomorphism as a “localic map”.

Sublocales

Localic injections are precisely the extremal monomorphisms in
{\bf Loc}

2.1.2.
Compact Hausdorff locales

The notion of compactness for locales is a direct abstraction of the one for topological spaces, while the localic notion of Hausdorffness is based on the characterisation of Hausdorff spaces as those whose diagonal is closed in the product topology. Let us recall that a localic map
f\colon X\to Y
is
closed
if it satisfies the dual Frobenius law

(1)

\forall u\in OX,\forall v\in OY,\ \ f_{*}(u\vee f^{*}v)=f_{*}u\vee v.

With this terminology, a locale
X
is

compact

if whenever a directed supremum
\bigvee_{i\in I}u_{i}
, with
\{u_{i}\mid i\in I\}\subseteq OX
, equals the top
element
1\in OX
, then
u_{i}=1
for some
i\in I
.

Hausdorff

if its diagonal
X\to X\times X
is closed.
3
3

3

This notion is sometimes referred to as
strongly Hausdorff
or
I-Hausdorff
in the literature, cf. e.g.
[
11

13
]
and
[
23
]
, respectively.

The full subcategory of
{\bf Loc}
defined by compact Hausdorff locales is denoted by
{\bf CHLoc}
. An important fact is that every localic map
f
between compact Hausdorff locales is
proper
, i.e.
f
is closed and
f_{*}
preserves directed suprema. More generally, every localic map from a compact locale to a Hausdorff one is proper, see
[
30
, Corollary 4.4]
.

This yields a useful criterion for a morphism in
{\bf CHLoc}
to be a localic surjection. In fact, as an immediate consequence of eq. (
1

is surjective whenever it is
dense
, i.e.
f_{*}(0)=0
.
Therefore,

Lemma 2.1
.

Any dense localic map between compact Hausdorff locales is surjective.

Remark 2.2
.

Although we shall not need this fact, we point out that a proper localic map is surjective precisely when it is dense, hence the converse implication in Lemma
2.1
holds as well.

Throughout, we shall rely on the following characterisation of monomorphisms and regular epimorphisms in
{\bf CHLoc}

[

Lemma 2.3
.

The following statements hold for any morphism

{\bf CHLoc}
:

(a)

is a monomorphism if, and only if, it is a localic injection.

(b)

is a regular epimorphism if, and only if, it is a localic surjection.

Remark 2.4
.

We will see in Section
2.2.2
below that
{\bf CHLoc}
is a pretopos and every epimorphism in a pretopos is regular; hence, all epimorphisms in
{\bf CHLoc}
are regular and they coincide with the localic surjections.

2.1.3.
Frames of ideals

Let
L
be a (bounded) distributive lattice. Recall that an
ideal
on
L
is a subset
I\subseteq L
such that

(i)

I
is closed under finite suprema, i.e.
0\in I
and
x\vee y\in I
whenever
x,y\in I
;

(ii)

I
is downward closed, i.e. for all
x,y\in L
such that
x\leq y
, if
y\in I
then
x\in I
.

The set
\Idl(L)
of all ideals on
L
, ordered by set-theoretic inclusion, is a frame. Further, if
h\colon L\to M
is a lattice homomorphism between distributive lattices, then the map

\Idl(h)\colon\Idl(L)\to\Idl(M),\ \ I\mapsto\mathop{\downarrow}h[I]\coloneqq\{y\in M\mid\exists x\in I\text{ such that }y\leq h(x)\}

is a frame homomorphism. If
{\bf DLat}
denotes the category of (bounded) distributive lattices and lattice homomorphisms, these assignments yield a functor

(2)

\Idl\colon{\bf DLat}\to{\bf Frm}

which is left adjoint to the forgetful functor
{\bf Frm}\to{\bf DLat}
. The unit
L\to\Idl(L)
of the adjunction sends an element
x\in L
to the principal ideal
\mathop{\downarrow}x
, and is clearly injective. Thus, if
L
is a distributive lattice and
M
is a frame, any lattice homomorphism
L\to M
admits a unique extension to a frame homomorphism
\Idl(L)\to M
.

Consider now the restriction of the functor in eq. (
2
) to the full subcategory
{\bf BA}
of
{\bf DLat}
defined by Boolean algebras. The frames (isomorphic to one) of the form
\Idl(B)
, with
B
a Boolean algebra, are known as
Stone frames
. These can be characterised as the distributive lattices
L
such that the map

(3)

\varphi_{L}\colon L\to\Idl(\B(L)),\ \ x\mapsto\{c\in\B(L)\mid c\leq x\}

is an order isomorphism, where
\B(L)
denotes the
Boolean center
of
L
, i.e. the Boolean algebra of complemented elements of
L
. The locales corresponding to Stone frames are referred to as
Stone locales
.

Remark 2.6
.

Although we shall not need this fact, let us mention in passing that the category
{\bf BA}
of Boolean algebras is dually equivalent to the full subcategory of
{\bf Loc}
defined by Stone locales. This is a localic version of the celebrated Stone duality between Boolean algebras and Stone spaces (i.e., zero-dimensional compact Hausdorff spaces)
[
28
]
.

The inclusion functor
{\bf CHLoc}\hookrightarrow{\bf Loc}
has a left adjoint
\beta\colon{\bf Loc}\to{\bf CHLoc}
, known as the
localic Stone-Čech compactification
functor. When
X
is a locale whose corresponding frame is a Boolean algebra
B
, the frame
O(\beta X)
is isomorphic to
\Idl(B)
, see e.g.
[
1
, p. 310]
. For an arbitrary locale
Y
, the frame
O(\beta Y)
can be identified with a
subframe
of
\Idl(OY)
, cf.
[
1
, Proposition 3]
.
It follows that
{\bf CHLoc}
is a complete and cocomplete category: in fact,
{\bf CHLoc}
is closed under limits in
{\bf Loc}
, and colimits in
{\bf CHLoc}
can be computed by first taking the colimit in
{\bf Loc}
and then applying the functor
\beta
.

2.2.
Regular and coherent categories, and pretoposes

Fix an arbitrary category
\mathcal{K}
. Given an object
X\in\mathcal{K}
, we denote by
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
the poset of subobjects of
X
.
We will often identify a subobject of
X
with any of its representatives
m\colon S\rightarrowtail X
, and sometimes even with just the domain
S
of such a representative. Recall that

(m_{1}\colon S_{1}\rightarrowtail X)\leq(m_{2}\colon S_{2}\rightarrowtail X)

in
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
if, and only if, there is a morphism
u\colon S_{1}\to S_{2}
satisfying
m_{2}\cdot u=m_{1}
.

There is, a priori, no reason why
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
should be a set, as opposed to a proper class. Categories in which every object has only a set of subobjects are called
well-powered
. For instance, the category
{\bf Loc}
is not well-powered, see e.g.
[
23
, p. 70]
. However, all the (generic) categories considered in this paper are assumed to be well-powered.

If
\mathcal{K}
has pullbacks, each poset
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
is a
\wedge
-semilattice with top element, where the meet of two subobjects is given by their pullback (recall that monomorphisms are stable under pullback), and the top element by the identity arrow
X\to X
.
Moreover, every morphism
f\colon X\to Y
in
\mathcal{K}
induces a
\wedge
-semilattice homomorphism

f^{-1}\colon\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y}}\right)\to\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)

preserving the top element, which sends a subobject
m\colon S\rightarrowtail Y
to the pullback of
m
along
f
. In fact, this assignment yields a functor

\Subobjects\colon\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\bf SLat}

into the category of
\wedge
-semilattices and
\wedge
-semilattice homomorphisms preserving the top elements.
As suggested by the notation,
f^{-1}
may be thought of as the
preimage
map associated with
f
.

Example 2.7
.

In
{\bf CHLoc}
, the subobjects of a compact Hausdorff locale can be identified with its closed sublocales (see e.g.
[
29
, p. 92]
). Further, the preimage map
f^{-1}\colon\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y}}\right)\to\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
corresponding to an arrow
f\colon X\to Y
in
{\bf CHLoc}
can be described, in terms of nuclei, as the order-dual of the map
f^{-}\colon NY\to NX
(defined in Section
2.1.1
) restricted to closed nuclei. In other words, for all
v\in OY
,
f^{-1}
sends the closed sublocale of
Y
corresponding to the nucleus
\mathfrak{c}_{v}
to the closed sublocale of
X
corresponding to
\mathfrak{c}_{f^{*}v}
.

2.2.1.
Regular and coherent categories

We shall now focus on two classes of categories, namely regular and coherent categories, defined by properties of the corresponding functors of subobjects
\Subobjects\colon\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\bf SLat}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{K}
is a category admitting pullbacks.
For every arrow
f\colon X\to Y
in
\mathcal{K}
, the preimage map
f^{-1}\colon\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y}}\right)\to\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
is right adjoint if, and only if, for every subobject
m\colon S\rightarrowtail X
there exists a smallest subobject of
Y
through which
f\cdot m
factors. If such a subobject of
Y
exists, we shall denote it by
f[S]
and refer to it as the
image of
S
under
f
. Suppose for a moment that
f^{-1}
is right adjoint. In that case, we shall write

f[-]\colon\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)\to\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y}}\right)

for its left adjoint—the
image
map associated with
f
. In particular, considering the identity
X\to X
, we see that
f
admits an image factorisation

(4)

X\to f[X]\rightarrowtail Y.

We say that
f
has a
pullback-stable image
if, for any morphism
g\colon Z\to Y
, taking the pullback of diagram (
4
) along
g
yields the image factorisation of the pullback of
f
along
g
.

Definition 2.8
.

A category is
regular
if it has finite limits and every morphism has a pullback-stable image.

Given an arrow
f\colon X\to Y
in a regular category, the morphism
X\to f[X]
in eq. (
4
) is a regular epimorphism. In fact, every regular category admits a (regular epimorphism, monomorphism) factorisation system given by image factorisations.
The following properties of regular categories will be useful in the following; for a proof, see e.g.
[
3
, Propositions 3.8 and 3.9, p. 142]

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a regular category and let
f
be a morphism in

\mathcal{K}
.

(a)

If
f
is a monomorphism then
f[-]
is
injective.

(b)

If
f

is
surjective.

We shall now look at the case where the functor of subobjects corestricts to the category
{\bf DLat}

Definition 2.10
.

A

is a regular category in which every poset (in fact,
\wedge

Example 2.11
.

The category

For every object

Every coherent category has an initial object
\mathbf{0}
and the latter is

2.2.2.
Pretoposes

A central notion for this paper is that of pretopos, which can be regarded as a special type of coherent category. Recall that a category is said to be

positive

\mathbf{0}

effective

5
5

5

The

of a morphism

[

or
[

Definition 2.12

A

is a positive and effective coherent category.

Equivalently, a pretopos can be defined as an effective category with finite coproducts that is
extensive

universal
, i.e. the pullback of a coproduct diagram

[
6

Example 2.13
.

The category
{\bf CHLoc}

In the remainder of this section, we recall some well known facts about Cartesian functors and pretopos morphisms. A

Moreover, recall that a functor is

A

For the following characterisation of pretopos morphisms, cf.
[

Lemma 2.14

A Cartesian functor between pretoposes is a pretopos morphism precisely when it preserves finite coproducts and regular epimorphisms.

The following conditions are equivalent for any pretopos morphism

(2)

(3)

for all objects

Just observe that, since faithful functors reflect epimorphisms and monomorphisms, a faithful functor defined on a balanced category is conservative. Hence,

Let us say that a pretopos morphism is an

[

Lemma 2.15

A pretopos morphism is an embedding provided it is bijective on subobjects.

With regard to essential surjectivity, let us say that a pretopos morphism

Let

Recall that a functor is a

Combining Lemmas

3.2.
Closed images of compact Hausdorff locales are compact Hausdorff

It is well known that the localic image of a compact locale is compact. The next proposition shows that the image of a compact Hausdorff locale under a closed localic map is also Hausdorff.
7
7

7

As we were not able to find this result in the literature, we offer a proof which essentially amounts to showing that subfitness is preserved under closed localic surjections; classically, the latter fact was observed e.g. in

Proposition 3.6
.

If

is a closed localic surjection and

Proof.

The localic image of a compact locale is compact, see e.g.

is subfit whenever

Let
j=\mathfrak{o}_{u}

\displaystyle f_{+}f^{-}j(w)

\displaystyle\leq j(w)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ f_{*}(f^{*}u\to f^{*}w)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ f_{*}(f^{*}u\to f^{*}w)\wedge u

\displaystyle\leq w

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ f^{*}f_{*}(f^{*}u\to f^{*}w)\wedge f^{*}u

3.3.
The functor

As promised, we show that the functor

{\bf CHLoc}\hookrightarrow{\bf Loc}
.
To avoid confusion, throughout we shall denote by

dual to

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a filtral category. The functor

in Lemma

into the category of compact Hausdorff locales. Moreover,

preserves monomorphisms and regular epimorphisms.

Proof.

Recall that the image of a morphism

is the localic map

The localic map

is closed because, in any regular category, the Frobenius law

4.
Faithfulness and preservation of finite limits

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a filtral category. Our aim in this section consists in identifying conditions on
\mathcal{K}
ensuring that the functor
\mathscr{S}\colon\mathcal{K}\to{\bf CHLoc}
is Cartesian; equivalently, that it preserves equalisers, binary products, and the terminal object.

4.1.
Enough subobjects and preservation of equalisers

To start with, we shall postulate that
\mathscr{S}
is faithful; this can be stated as a first-order axiom in the language of categories in the following way:

(7)

\forall f,g\colon X\to Y\;((\forall i\colon U\rightarrowtail X,\;f[U]=g[U])\Rightarrow f=g)).

Definition 4.1
.

A regular category
has enough subobjects
if it satisfies condition (
7
).

Example 4.2
.

The category
{\bf CHLoc}
has enough subobjects. Just observe that, for every localic map

between compact Hausdorff locales, since a subobject

of

can be identified with a closed sublocale of
X

for some

, the image

corresponds to the nucleus
f_{+}\mathfrak{c}_{u}
. As
f
is closed, the latter nucleus coincides with
\mathfrak{c}_{f_{*}u}
. Thus, given another localic map

,
f[U]=g[U]
implies
\mathfrak{c}_{f_{*}u}=\mathfrak{c}_{g_{*}u}
, which in turn entails
f_{*}u=g_{*}u
. It follows that, if
f[U]=g[U]
for all subobjects

of

, then
f=g
.

The following easy observation will imply that
\mathscr{S}
preserves equalisers:

Let

If

4.2.
Binary products: compatible filtrality

We now discuss the preservation of binary products by the functor
\mathscr{S}\colon\mathcal{K}\to{\bf CHLoc}

\mathcal{K}
satisfies an additional property, namely

Remark 4.8
.

If the category

Thus, by Lemma

preserves binary products, we can reduce ourselves to showing that the canonical maps

The following statements are equivalent for any filtral category

(2)

Note that, for any distributive lattice

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\bf BA}}

4.3.
Terminal object: non-triviality

Concerning the preservation of the terminal object, we note the following easy fact:

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be a functor. Suppose
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
admit a terminal object and
F
preserves binary products. Then the unique arrow
F(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})\to\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}}
is a monomorphism.

Proof.

Note that the unique morphism
F(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})\to\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}}
is monic if, and only if, any two morphisms into
F(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})
are equal. In
\mathcal{C}
, the pair of identity arrows
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\leftarrow\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}
is a product diagram, hence the pair of identity arrows

F(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})\leftarrow F(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})\rightarrow F(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})

is a product diagram in
\mathcal{D}
. It follows that, for any two arrows
u,v\colon T\to F(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})
, their pairing
\langle u,v\rangle\colon T\to F(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})
satisfies
u=\langle u,v\rangle=v
.
∎

Let
\Omega
denote the frame of truth values, which is also the initial object of
{\bf Frm}
(see e.g.
[
29
, Lemma 1.5.1]
). Because
{\bf CHLoc}
is closed in
{\bf Loc}
under finite limits,
\Omega
is also the frame corresponding to the terminal object of
{\bf CHLoc}
. To avoid confusion, we shall write
\mho
for the terminal object of
{\bf CHLoc}
(equivalently, of
{\bf Loc}
); that is,
O\mho=\Omega
.
When applied to the functor
\mathscr{S}\colon\mathcal{K}\to{\bf CHLoc}
, for
\mathcal{K}
a compatibly filtral category with enough subobjects, Lemma
4.11
shows that the unique map

t\colon\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{1})\to\mho

is a localic injection.
From the intuitionistic point of view, the sufficiently strong way to say that a filtral category is
non-trivial
is to say that
t
is a surjection, i.e.
t^{*}\colon\Omega\to\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
is injective, cf.
[
7
, §2.21]
. That is, according to the usual terminology in constructive locale theory, a filtral category is non-trivial if
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
is a
positive
locale.

Example 4.12
.

Clearly,
{\bf CHLoc}
is non-trivial because the unique frame homomorphism
\Omega\to\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mho}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
is an isomorphism.

An application of Lemma
4.11
, combined with Propositions
4.4
and
4.10
, yields the following result.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a non-trivial compatibly filtral category with enough subobjects. The functor
\mathscr{S}\colon\mathcal{K}\to{\bf CHLoc}
is Cartesian.

5.
A pretopos embedding into compact Hausdorff locales

In the previous section we saw that, if
\mathcal{K}
is a non-trivial compatibly filtral category with enough subobjects,
\mathscr{S}\colon\mathcal{K}\to{\bf CHLoc}
is Cartesian.
In this section we shall prove that, if we further assume that
\mathcal{K}
is a pretopos, the functor
\mathscr{S}
is a pretopos embedding. In other words,
\mathcal{K}
is equivalent to a sub-pretopos of
{\bf CHLoc}
. The essential image of this embedding is studied in Section
6
.

The key ingredient to establish that
\mathscr{S}
is a pretopos morphism (see Theorem
5.2
below) consists in showing that finite coproducts are preserved:

Proposition 5.1
.

For a filtral pretopos
\mathcal{K}
, the functor
\mathscr{S}\colon\mathcal{K}\to{\bf CHLoc}
preserves finite coproducts.

Proof.

The initial object in a coherent category is strict, hence
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{0}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
is the one-element frame and the corresponding locale
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{0})
is initial in
{\bf CHLoc}
.
Now, consider a coproduct diagram

]
and states that, for any distributive lattice
D
and
a\in D
, if
f(a)=0
for all lattice homomorphisms
f\colon D\to\Omega
, then
a=0
. Constructively, CPIT is equivalent to the existence of enough points for compact Hausdorff locales; moreover, in the presence of the law of excluded middle, it is equivalent to the prime ideal theorem for distributive lattices. See
[
29
, §1.8]
. Thus, by Lemma
7.1
, every topos satisfying CPIT is Boolean and satisfies the prime ideal theorem.

Even in the presence of excluded middle, in order to derive Theorem
3.3
from Theorem
6.3
we need to show that compatible filtrality follows from the remaining assumptions.
To this end, borrowing terminology from topos theory, let us say that an object of a category is an
atom
if it has precisely two (distinct) subobjects.
We shall see that a filtral category with enough subobjects is compatibly filtral whenever its terminal object
\mathbf{1}
is an atom (Proposition
7.4
).
We start with a technical lemma:

Lemma 7.3
.

Consider a product diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}\times X_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}

whose terminal object is an atom. The following statements hold:

(a)

\forall\,U\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
, either
U=1
or
\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]=0\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
.

(b)

If
\mathcal{K}
is filtral, for all complemented elements
T_{1}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
and
T_{2}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
,

\pi_{1}^{-1}(T_{1})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (T_{1}=0\ \text{ or }\ T_{2}=0).

Proof.

(a)
We prove the order-dual statement.
Fix a subobject
m\colon U\rightarrowtail X_{1}
and let
U\twoheadrightarrow V\rightarrowtail\mathbf{1}
be the (regular epi, mono) factorisation of the composite

!_{1}\cdot m\colon U\rightarrowtail X_{1}\to\mathbf{1}.

Since
\mathbf{1}
is an atom, either
V\cong\mathbf{0}
or
V\cong\mathbf{1}
. If
V\cong\mathbf{0}
we have
U\cong\mathbf{0}
because the initial object of a coherent category is strict, and so
U=0\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}}}\right)
. It remains to show that if
V\cong\mathbf{1}
, then
\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]=1\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{2}}}\right)
.

Suppose
V\cong\mathbf{1}
. Then
!_{1}\cdot m
is a regular epimorphism and so
!_{1}[U]=1\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{1}}}\right)
. The map
!_{2}^{-1}\colon\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{1}}}\right)\to\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{2}}}\right)
preserves the top element because it is a bounded lattice homomorphism, hence
!_{2}^{-1}!_{1}[U]=1\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{2}}}\right)
. Now, consider the following pullback square:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}\times X_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces!_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces!_{2}}

The Beck–Chevalley condition

\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(-)]={!}_{2}^{-1}!_{1}[-]\colon\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}}}\right)\to\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{2}}}\right)

holds in any regular category (cf. e.g.

), and so
\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]=!_{2}^{-1}!_{1}[U]=1
.

(b)
The right-to-left direction is immediate, since the frame homomorphisms
\pi_{1}^{-1}
and
\pi_{2}^{-1}
preserve

.
For the left-to-right direction, suppose that

\pi_{1}^{-1}(T_{1})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})=0

and denote by
U\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
the complement of
T_{1}

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{-1}(T_{1})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ (\pi_{1}^{-1}(T_{1})\sqcup\pi_{1}^{-1}(U))\sqcap(\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})\sqcup\pi_{1}^{-1}(U))

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ \pi_{1}^{-1}(T_{1}\sqcup U)\sqcap(\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})\sqcup\pi_{1}^{-1}(U))

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ 1\sqcap(\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})\sqcup\pi_{1}^{-1}(U))

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ \pi_{2}[\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})\sqcup\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]

\displaystyle=\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]

(14)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ T_{2}\sqcup\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]

\displaystyle=\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]

where the last implication follows from the fact that, since
\mathcal{K}
is filtral, the localic map

\pi_{2}[-]=\mathscr{S}(\pi_{2})\colon\mathscr{S}(X_{1}\times X_{2})\to\mathscr{S}(X_{2})

is closed.
By item

, either
U=1
or
\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]=0
. If

then
T_{1}=0
, whereas if
\pi_{2}[\pi_{1}^{-1}(U)]=0

.
∎

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a filtral category with enough subobjects. If its terminal object is an atom, then
\mathcal{K}
is compatibly filtral.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{1}
is an atom. In view of Lemma
4.9
, in order to show that
\mathcal{K}
is compatibly filtral it suffices to prove that, for all filtral objects
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
, the canonical map

p\colon\mathscr{S}(S_{1}\times S_{2})\to\mathscr{S}(S_{1})\times\mathscr{S}(S_{2})

is a localic surjection.

Since
{\bf CHLoc}
is closed under limits in the category of locales (see Section
2.1.3
),
p^{*}
is the unique frame homomorphism making the following diagram commute,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}+\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{1}\times S_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{*}}

where the top row is a coproduct diagram in
{\bf Frm}
.
By Lemma
2.1
,
p
is a localic surjection provided it is dense, i.e.
p_{*}(0)=0
.

Now, every element of the frame
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}+\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
is a join of generators of the form
T_{1}\oplus T_{2}=u_{1}(T_{1})\wedge u_{2}(T_{2})
with
T_{1}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
and
T_{2}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
(see e.g.
[
23
, IV.5.2]
). Note that, in the frame
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{1}\times S_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
, we have

(15)

p^{*}(T_{1}\oplus T_{2})=p^{*}(u_{1}(T_{1})\wedge u_{2}(T_{2}))=\pi_{1}^{-1}(T_{1})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2}).

Hence, in the frame
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}+\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{2}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
,

\displaystyle p_{*}(0)

\displaystyle=\bigvee{\{T_{1}\oplus T_{2}\mid T_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}},\ T_{1}\oplus T_{2}\leq p_{*}(0)\}}

p^{*}\dashv p_{*}

\displaystyle=\bigvee{\{T_{1}\oplus T_{2}\mid T_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}},\ p^{*}(T_{1}\oplus T_{2})\sqsubseteq 0\}}

eq. (
15
)

\displaystyle=\bigvee{\{T_{1}\oplus T_{2}\mid T_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}},\ \pi_{1}^{-1}(T_{1})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})=0\}}.

For each
T_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}
, consider the inhabited set

\mathcal{U}_{T_{i}}\coloneqq\{U\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}\mid U\sqsubseteq T_{i}\text{ and $U$ is complemented}\}.

As each
S_{i}
is a filtral object,
\mathscr{S}(S_{i})
is a Stone locale, and so
T_{i}=\displaystyle\bigsqcup{\mathcal{U}_{T_{i}}}
. Using the fact that
\oplus
distributes over suprema (see again
[
23
, IV.5.2]
), we get

\displaystyle\bigvee{\{T_{1}\oplus T_{2}\mid T_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}},\ \pi_{1}^{-1}(T_{1})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(T_{2})=0\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigvee{\{U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\mid T_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}},\ U_{i}\in\ \mathcal{U}_{T_{i}},\ \pi_{1}^{-1}(\bigsqcup{\mathcal{U}_{T_{1}}})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(\bigsqcup{\mathcal{U}_{T_{2}}})=0\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigvee{\{U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\mid T_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}},\ U_{i}\in\ \mathcal{U}_{T_{i}},\ \bigsqcup{\{\pi_{1}^{-1}(U_{1})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(U_{2})}\mid U_{i}\in\ \mathcal{U}_{T_{i}}\}=0\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigvee{\{U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\mid U_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}},\ U_{i}\text{ is complemented},\ \pi_{1}^{-1}(U_{1})\sqcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(U_{2})=0\}}

Lemma
7.3
(b)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigvee{\{U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\mid U_{i}\in\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{i}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}},\ U_{1}=0\text{ or }U_{2}=0\}}

which is equal to
0
because
U_{1}\oplus U_{2}=0
provided that at least one of
U_{1}
and

is

. Thus

, which implies that
\mathcal{K}
is compatibly filtral.
∎

Remark 7.5
.

The existence of a non-trivial filtral category with enough subobjects such that its terminal object is an atom implies the law of excluded middle.

To see this, note that if
\mathcal{K}
is a filtral category satisfying the conditions above, then it is compatibly filtral by Proposition
7.4
, and so
\Subobjects\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{1}}}\right)^{\mathrm{op}}\cong\Omega
by Proposition
4.13
. Since
\mathbf{1}
is an atom of
\mathcal{K}
, it follows that
\Omega
is a two-element frame, and in particular a Boolean algebra. Thus, the law of excluded middle holds.

The next result is an immediate consequence of Theorem
6.3
and Proposition
7.4
. Note that its assumptions entail the law of excluded middle (cf. Lemma
7.1
and Remark
7.5
).

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a non-trivial filtral pretopos with enough subobjects such that its terminal object is an atom and admits all set-indexed copowers. If compact Hausdorff locales have enough points, the functor
\mathscr{S}\colon\mathcal{K}\to{\bf CHLoc}
is a weak equivalence.

Classically, assuming the existence of enough points for compact Hausdorff locales, the category
{\bf CHLoc}
is equivalent to the category
{\bf CHSp}
of compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps; cf. e.g.
[
13
, §III.1.10]
. Thus, the characterisation of
{\bf CHSp}
(up to
weak
equivalence) in Theorem
3.3
can be deduced from Corollary
7.6
. Just observe that every filtral pretopos satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
3.3
has enough subobjects (since points are special types of subobjects) and its terminal object is an atom; for the latter statement, cf.
[
22
, Lemmas 3.2 and 5.7]
.
