The L p L^{p} convergence of Fourier series on triangular domains
Ryan L. Acosta Babb

Abstract
We prove L p L^{p} norm convergence for (appropriate truncations of) the Fourier series arising from the Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunctions on three types of triangular domains in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} : (i) the 45 45 - 90 90 - 45 45 triangle, (ii) the equilateral triangle and (iii) the hemiequilateral triangle (i.e. half an equilateral triangle cut along its height). The limitations of our argument to these three types are discussed in light of Lamé’s Theorem.



1
Introduction

In one dimension, there is only one way to truncate the partial sums of a Fourier series

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(n)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}nx},

namely

S_{N}(x):=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\widehat{f}(n)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}nx}.

Then the Fourier series converges if, and only if,
S_{N}(x)
converges as
N\to\infty
.

When moving to higher dimensions, we have

\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(m,n)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(mx+ny)}

and an ambiguity arises.

Since the eigenvalue for
\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(mx+ny)}
is proportional to
m^{2}+n^{2}
,
the “natural choice” of truncation for the partial sums is

\sum_{m^{2}+n^{2}\leqslant N^{2}}\widehat{f}(m,n)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(mx+ny)};

that is, we cut off the sum once we have picked out all eigenfunctions with eigenvalues

\left|\lambda_{m,n}\right|\lesssim N^{2}
.
(Geometrically, this procedure corresponds to using a “circular cutoff” in frequency space,
by choosing frequencies
(m,n)
in the ball of radius
N
.)
A celebrated result of
Fefferman, 1971
implies,
with the help of standard transference results
(
Grafakos, 2014
, Chap. 4)
,
that such “eigenvalue truncations” of Fourier expansions
fail, in general, to converge to
f
in the
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
norm when
p\neq 2
.

We may, instead, truncate according to the labelling of the indices (or frequencies)
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
:

\sum_{\left|m\right|,\left|n\right|\leqslant N}\widehat{f}(m,n)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(mx+ny)}

Happily, these “truncations by label” always converge back to
f
in
all

L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
spaces
(provided that
1<p<\infty
).

(See
Grafakos, 2014
, for a proof of this classical result.)

Recently,
Feffermal et al., 2021
have noted that the general problem of
finding “well-behaved” truncations of eigenfunction expansions in
L^{p}
-based spaces is still open
for more general bounded Euclidean domains.

A natural starting point is to consider the next “simplest” domains, such as discs or triangles.
The eigenfunctions of the disc are products of trigonometric and Bessel functions
and so share a similar product structure to the classical Fourier series on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
Specifically, the eigenfunctions are of the form

\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta n}J_{\left|n\right|}(j_{m}^{\left|n\right|}r)

giving rise to the multidimensional Bessel–Fourier series

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m,n}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n\theta}J_{\left|n\right|}(j_{m}^{\left|n\right|}r).

(1)

(Here,
J_{\left|n\right|}
denotes a Bessel function of the first kind and
j_{m}^{\left|n\right|}
its non-negative zeros.)
The best result we were able to find in the literature is due to
Balodis and Córdoba, 1999
,
who proved norm convergence in the mixed norm space
L^{p}_{\mathrm{rad}}(L^{2}_{\mathrm{ang}})

defined by the condition

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{p,2}:=\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k}\left|f_{k}(r)\right|^{2}\right)^{p/2}r\,\mathrm{d}{r}\right]^{1/p}<\infty,

where
f_{k}(r)
is the
k
th
Fourier coefficient of the angular function
f(r,\cdot)
for fixed
r
:

f_{k}(r):=\int_{0}^{1}f(r,\theta)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k\theta}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}.

By truncating the series (
1
) in the ranges
\left|n\right|\leqslant N
,
m\leqslant M
,
they were able to show that there is a constant
A>0
such that the Bessel–Fourier series
of
f\in L^{p}_{\mathrm{rad}}(L^{2}_{\mathrm{ang}})
converges to
f
in the
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{p,2}
norm
provided that
M\geqslant AN+1
and
4/3<p<4
.
Furthermore, the endpoints for the range of
p
are sharp.

(See
Balodis and Córdoba, 1999
, Theorem 2, of which our discussion is a special case when
d=2
.)

By modifying their proof, we were able to improve the result to
L^{p}
convergence with respect to the usual measure
r\,\mathrm{d}{r}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}

on the disc, provided that
2\leqslant p<4
and

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{p,q}:=\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k}\left|f_{k}(r)\right|^{q}\right)^{p/q}r\,\mathrm{d}{r}\right]^{1/p}<\infty,\quad\text{where}\quad\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

As far as we know, the problem of
L^{p}
convergence for functions
f\in L^{p}(r\,\mathrm{d}{r}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta})

in the range
4/3<p<4
is still open; see
Acosta Babb, 2022
.

We therefore turn our attention to triangular domains, which turn out to be much more amenable to analysis.
Following early work of
Lamé, 1833
, other authors such as
Práger, 1998
and
McCartin, 2003
have derived
explicit trigonometric expressions for Dirichlet eigenfunctions on the equilateral triangle.
Neither of these authors, however, consider questions of convergence for the associated eigenfunction series,
and subsequent work by
Adcock, 2009
,
Huybrechs et al., 2011
, and
Sun and Li, 2005
,
restricts attention to numerical methods or convergence in
L^{2}
-based spaces such as
H^{k}
.

(See
Grebenkov and Nguyen, 2013
, for a comprehensive survey of the literature on the Laplacian and its eigenfunctions.)

To our knowledge, there is no treatment of the
L^{p}
convergence of series of eigenfunctions on the triangle for
p\neq 2
.

In this paper we establish
L^{p}
convergence for trigonometric series of eigenfunctions for the Dirichlet Laplacian
on three types of triangular domains:
(i) the
45
-
90
-
45
triangle,
(ii) the equilateral triangle and
(iii) the hemiequilateral triangle (i.e. half an equilateral triangle cut along its height).

Eigenfunctions for the later types, (ii) and (iii), were obtained in the abovementioned work of
Práger, 1998
and
McCartin, 2003
.
Using their insights, we prove that, on each of these domains, any
L^{p}
function
can be written as a norm-convergent series of eigenfunctions.
We also give a complete treatment of these issues for the simpler case (i).
As far as we know, the result for type-(i) triangles is new.

In
Section

2
we derive the eigenfunctions for the type-(i) triangles,
prove their completeness and establish
L^{p}
convergence of the associated series.
This serves as a useful prototype for the arguments we will develop in the following sections.

The result for equilateral domains is obtained by a decomposition of a function into
a symmetric and antisymmetric part with respect to the line
x=0
.
This reduces the problem to type-(iii) domains, which, once solved, easily yields the equilateral case.

Thus, in
Sections

3
and
5
we separately derive the antisymmetric and symmetric modes,
following the ingenious approach of
Práger, 1998
(see
Fig.

2
).
The hemiequilateral triangle
T_{1}
is tiled into the rectangle
{R=[0,\sqrt{3}]\times[0,1]}
,
where we exploit the classical
L^{p}
convergence of “double-sine” and “cosine-sine” series on
R

to derive convergence on
T_{1}
.
This is our main contribution, and is worked out in
Sections

4
and
6
.
These results are combined in
Section

7
to obtain
L^{p}
convergence on the equilateral triangle.

In
Section

8
we conclude with some remarks about the limitations of this argument:
owing to a theorem of Lamé, the three domains (i)–(iii) listed above are the “only” ones amenable to this procedure.

2
The 45-90-45 triangle

Taking our cue from Práger’s analysis of the equilateral case,
we reflect the 45-90-45 triangle along the hypotenuse to obtain a square;
see
Fig.

1
.

Figure 1
:
Reflecting
T
into the square
[0,1]^{2}
along the diagonal
y=1-x
.

Placing the triangle,
T
, at vertices
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
and
(0,0)
, the reflections have the simple from

x=1-\eta\quad\text{and}\quad y=1-\xi\quad\text{where}\quad(\xi,\eta)\in T.

Given a function
f:T\to\mathbb{R}
we define the
prolongation
of

\mathscr{P}f(x,y):=\begin{cases}f(x,y),&\text{if }(x,y)\in T;\\
-f(1-y,1-x),&\text{if }(x,y)\in T^{\prime};\end{cases}

where
T^{\prime}
denotes the reflected triangle, so that
[0,1]^{2}=T\cup T^{\prime}
.

We now expand
\mathscr{P}f
as a double-sine series on
[0,1]^{2}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n)

\displaystyle:=4\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathscr{P}f(x,y)\sin(\pi mx)\sin(\pi ny)\,\mathrm{d}{x}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=4\int_{T}f(\xi,\eta)\bigg[\sin(\pi m\xi)\sin(\pi n\eta)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}-\sin(\pi m(1-\eta))\sin(\pi n(1-\xi))\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}{\eta}

\displaystyle=4\int_{T}f(\xi,\eta)u_{m,n}(\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}{\eta},

(2)

where we defined the functions

u_{m,n}(\xi,\eta):=\sin(\pi m\xi)\sin(\pi n\eta)-\sin(\pi m(1-\eta))\sin(\pi n(1-\xi))

for all points
(\xi,\eta)\in T
.

Lemma 1
.

We record the following facts about the functions
u_{m,n}
(indexed by
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
).

(i)

For all
m\neq n
:

u_{m,n}=\sin(\pi m\xi)\sin(\pi n\eta)-(-1)^{m+n}\sin(\pi n\xi)\sin(\pi m\eta),

and clearly
u_{m,n}\equiv 0
when
n=m
.

(ii)

Symmetry of the indices:

u_{n,m}=-(-1)^{m+n}u_{m,n}\quad\text{for all}\quad m\neq n;

therefore, it suffices to consider the set with
0<m<n
.

(iii)

Letting
(x,y)
range over
[0,1]^{2}
,

\mathscr{P}\left[u_{m,n}|_{T}\right](x,y)=u_{m,n}(x,y)\quad\text{for all}\quad m\neq n.

In other words: restricting
u_{m,n}
from
[0,1]^{2}
to
T
and prolonging by
\mathscr{P}
yields
u_{m,n}
once more,
as a function on
[0,1]^{2}
.

(iv)

The set
\{u_{m,n}:0<m<n\}
is a complete, orthogonal set in
L^{2}(T)
and

\left\lVert u_{m,n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}({T})}=\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

Statements (i)-(iii) are easily verified. For the completeness claim in (iv), suppose that
f\in L^{2}(T)
is such that

\int_{T}fu_{m,n}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad 0<m<n.

It follows from (i), (iii) and (
2
) that the Fourier coefficients of
\mathscr{P}f
on
[0,1]^{2}
vanish,
so
\mathscr{P}f=0
in
L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
, and therefore
f=\mathscr{P}f|_{T}=0
.
This proves the completeness of the set.

From (iii) and (
2
), it is clear that

\int_{T}u_{m,n}u_{k,l}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}u_{m,n}(x,y)\sin(\pi kx)\sin(\pi ly)\,\mathrm{d}{x}\,\mathrm{d}{y}.

Ordering the indices as
0<m<n
and
0<k<l
, it follows from the formula in (i) that
the functions
u_{m,n}
and
u_{k,l}
are pairwise orthogonal; setting
m=k
and
n=l
easily yields
the norm of
u_{m,n}
.
∎

Corollary 2
.

The function
u_{m,n}
with
0<m<n
is an eigenfunction of (minus) the Dirichlet Laplacian on
T

with eigenvalue
\pi^{2}(m^{2}+n^{2})
.

Proof.

It is immediate from (i) and (iii) in
Lemma

1
that

-\Delta u_{m,n}(x,y)=\pi^{2}(m^{2}+n^{2})u_{m,n}(x,y)

pointwise for all
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, and
u_{m,n}\equiv 0
on the boundary of the square.
Thus, restricting to
T\subset[0,1]^{2}
yields eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on
T

that clearly vanish along the edges parallel to the axes.
It remains to show that
u_{m,n}
also vanishes on the hypotenuse
y=1-x
,
but this is immediately verified on substitution into the formula from (i) above.
∎

We thus have a complete, orthogonal set of eigenfunctions for the Dirichlet Laplacian on
T
.
We can define the Fourier coefficients as usual:

f^{\triangle}(m,n):=\frac{1}{\left\lVert u_{m,n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}({T})}^{2}}\int_{T}fu_{m,n}=\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n);

the last equality holds by
Section

2
.

The partial sum operators, truncated by indices, for the Fourier series on
T
and
[0,1]^{2}
are

\displaystyle S_{N}^{T}f

\displaystyle:=\sum_{0<m<n\leqslant N}f^{\triangle}(m,n)u_{m,n},\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle S_{N}^{[0,1]^{2}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{0<m,n\leqslant N}\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n)\sin(\pi mx)\sin(\pi ny),

respectively.

The next proposition tells us that the prolongation operation commutes with the Fourier partial sums,
allowing us to“push forward” the
L^{p}
convergence results from the square to the triangle.

Proposition 3
.

Let
f\in L^{p}(T)
. Then:

\mathscr{P}\left[S_{N}^{T}f\right](x,y)=S_{N}^{[0,1]^{2}}\left[\mathscr{P}f\right](x,y)\quad\text{for all}\quad(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}.

Proof.

Break up the lattice
[1,N]^{2}\cap\mathbb{N}^{2}
into the following regions:

I:0<m<n\leqslant N\quad\text{and}\quad II:0<n<m\leqslant N.

(Recall that, by (i) in
Lemma

1
, the diagonal
m=n
yields no eigenfunctions.)
Hence:

\displaystyle S_{N}^{[0,1]^{2}}\left[\mathscr{P}f\right](x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n)\sin(\pi mx)\sin(\pi ny)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+\sum_{II}\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n)\sin(\pi mx)\sin(\pi ny)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n)\bigg[\sin(\pi mx)\sin(\pi ny)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}-(-1)^{m+n}\sin(\pi nx)\sin(\pi my)\bigg]

Lemma

1
(ii)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n)u_{m,n}(x,y)

Lemma

1
(i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n)\mathscr{P}u_{m,n}(x,y)

Lemma

1
(iii)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{P}\left[S_{N}^{T}f\right](x,y),

where in the last line we make use of the linearity of
\mathscr{P}
and the identity

f^{\triangle}(m,n)=\widehat{\mathscr{P}f}(m,n)
for all
(m,n)
in
I
.
∎

We now have everything we need to prove our result.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f\in L^{p}(T)
with
1<p<\infty
. Then,
S_{N}^{T}f\to f
in
L^{p}(T)
.

Proof.

Note that, for
f\in L^{p}
, we have

\left\lVert\mathscr{P}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({[0,1]^{2}})}^{p}=2\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T})}^{p}.

Thus, by successively applying this equality and
3
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert S_{N}^{T}f-f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T})}^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathscr{P}\left[S_{N}^{T}f\right]-\mathscr{P}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({[0,1]^{2}})}^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert S_{N}^{[0,1]^{2}}\mathscr{P}f-\mathscr{P}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({[0,1]^{2}})}^{p}\to 0,

by the
L^{p}
convergence of Fourier double-sine series on
[0,1]^{2}
.
∎

3
Antisymmetric eigenfunctions on the equilateral triangle

To tackle convergence on the equilateral triangle
T
, we divide it along its midline
x=0

into two congruent hemiequilateral triangles, and call the right-hand one
T_{1}

(see
Fig.

2
).
Any function
f:T\to\mathbb{R}
may be decomposed into a symmetric and antisymmetric part
with respect to
x
, i.e.
f=f_{a}+f_{s}
where

f_{a}(x,y):=\frac{f(x,y)-f(-x,y)}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{s}(x,y):=\frac{f(x,y)+f(-x,y)}{2}

for all
(x,y)\in T
.
Clearly each of
f_{a}
and
f_{s}
is determined by its values on
T_{1}\subset T
.

Let us begin with the
L^{p}
convergence of the Fourier series of
f_{a}
on the triangle
T_{1}
.
Denote by
(\xi,\eta)
the coordinates of a point in
T_{1}
.
Then, for each coordinate pair
(x,y)
in the rectangle
R=[0,\sqrt{3}]\times[0,1]
,
we write
(following
Práger, 1998
, p. 312)

(x,y)=(x_{i}(\xi,\eta),y_{i}(\xi,\eta))\quad\text{for exactly one}\quad(x_{i},y_{i})\in T_{i}.

Note that the
i
subscript serves just as a reminder that the point
(x_{i},y_{i})

lies in the region
T_{i}
of the Cartesian plane.

The
antisymmetric prolongation
of
u\colon T_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
to the rectangle
R
is defined by

\mathscr{P}_{a}u(x,y):=\begin{cases}u(\xi,\eta)&\text{if }(x,y)=(x_{1},y_{1})\in T_{1},\\
-u(\xi,\eta)&\text{if }(x,y)=(x_{2},y_{2})\in T_{2},\\
u(\xi,\eta)&\text{if }(x,y)=(x_{3},y_{3})\in T_{3},\\
u(\xi,\eta)&\text{if }(x,y)=(x_{4},y_{4})\in T_{4},\\
-u(\xi,\eta)&\text{if }(x,y)=(x_{5},y_{5})\in T_{5},\\
u(\xi,\eta)&\text{if }(x,y)=(x_{6},y_{6})\in T_{6}.\\
\end{cases}

:
The equilateral triangle
T
(shaded), the right-angled triangle
T_{1}

and six congruent copies
T_{i}

R=[0,\sqrt{3}]\times[0,1]
.
The “
\pm

\mathscr{P}_{a}
.
Assuming zero boundary conditions on
T_{1}

\mathscr{P}_{a}

R

More succinctly (albeit less precisely) we express this relation as

\mathscr{P}_{a}u(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{6}c_{i}u(\xi(x_{i},y_{i}),\eta(x_{i},y_{i})),

(3)

where
c_{i}
are the appropriate “
\pm

From
Fig.

\mathscr{P}_{a}u

R
.
We therefore express
\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}
as a double-sine series on

:

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m,n)\sin\left(\frac{\pi mx}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi ny\right).

The Fourier coefficients of
\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}
are:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m,n)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\sqrt{3}}\int_{R}\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}(x,y)\sin\left(\frac{\pi mx}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi ny\right)\,\mathrm{d}{x}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\sqrt{3}}\int_{T_{1}}f_{a}(\xi,\eta)\left[\sum_{i=1}c_{i}\sin\left(\frac{\pi mx_{i}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi ny_{i}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}{\eta}

by
(
3
)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{{\sqrt{3}}}\int_{T_{1}}f_{a}(\xi,\eta)u_{m,n}(\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}{\eta},

(4)

where we defined the antisymmetric eigenfunctions as

u_{m,n}(\xi,\eta):=\sum_{i=1}c_{i}\sin\left(\frac{\pi mx_{i}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi ny_{i}\right).

(Recall that each pair
(x_{i},y_{i})\in R
is a function of
(\xi,\eta)\in T_{1}
.)

By explicit computation using the parametrisations of the
T_{i}
, Práger obtained the following.

Lemma 5
.

For integers
0<m<n
with the same parity (i.e. both odd or both even),

\displaystyle u_{m,n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2\sin\left(\frac{\pi mx}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi ny\right)

\displaystyle\quad-2(-1)^{(m+n)/2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{2\sqrt{3}}(m+3n)\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi y}{2}(m-n)\right)

(5)

\displaystyle\quad+2(-1)^{(m-n)/2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{2\sqrt{3}}(m-3n)\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi y}{2}(m+n)\right).

Furthermore,
u_{m,n}\equiv 0
whenever
n
and
m
have different parity
or

n=m

or

m=3n
.
Finally, with
{0<m<n}
as above, define the pairs

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} m^{\prime}&=\tfrac{1}{2}(3n-m),\\
n^{\prime}&=\tfrac{1}{2}(m+n);\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} m^{\prime\prime}&=\tfrac{1}{2}(m+3n),\\

Then,

u_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}=-(-1)^{(m-n)/2}u_{m,n}\quad\text{and}\quad u_{m^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}=(-1)^{(m+n)/2}u_{m,n}.

(6)

The next technical result is all that stands between us
and the
L^{p}
convergence of a “
u_{m,n}
”-expansion of
f_{a}
on
T_{1}
.

Lemma 6
.

The functions
\{u_{m,n}:0<m<n\text{ and }m\equiv n\mod 2\}
are pairwise orthogonal.
Furthermore,

\mathscr{P}_{a}u_{m,n}(x,y)=u_{m,n}(x,y)\quad\text{for all}\quad(x,y)\in R,

(7)

from which it follows that

\left\lVert u_{m,n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}({T_{1}})}^{2}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.

(8)

Corollary 7
.

The functions
u_{m,n}
are eigenfunctions of (minus) the Dirichlet Laplacian on
T_{1}
,
with eigenvalue
\pi^{2}(\tfrac{m^{2}}{3}+n^{2})
.

Proof.

Since each
\mathscr{P}_{a}u_{m,n}
is a finite combination of eigenfunctions on
R
, we have

-\Delta\mathscr{P}_{a}u_{m,n}(x,y)=\pi^{2}\left(\frac{m^{2}}{3}+n^{2}\right)\mathscr{P}_{a}u_{m,n}(x,y)\quad\text{for all}\quad(x,y)\in R

by direct calculation.
This equation holds pointwise owing to the smoothness of the functions;
therefore, it holds when restricted to
T_{1}
.
But
\mathscr{P}_{a}u_{m,n}\equiv u_{m,n}
on
T_{1}
, whence

-\Delta u_{m,n}(\xi,\eta)=\pi^{2}\left(\frac{m^{2}}{3}+n^{2}\right)u_{m,n}(\xi,\eta)\quad\text{for all}\quad(\xi,\eta)\in T.

These functions clearly vanish on the boundary of the triangle by construction.
∎

Práger, 1998
,
although we have added a more explicit proof of
7

We will now use these results to obtain
L^{p}
convergence of the eigenfunctions.

4
L^{p}
Convergence of the Fourier series of
f_{a}

Eq.

8
tells us that the “Fourier coefficients” of
f_{a}
(with respect to
u_{m,n}
!) are

f_{a}^{\triangle}(m,n):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\int_{T_{1}}f_{a}(\xi,\eta)u_{m,n}(\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}{\eta},

and it follows from (
3
) that

\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m,n)=2f_{a}^{\triangle}(m,n).

(9)

To help keep track of our indices, we will introduce the sets

\mathscr{U}_{N}:=\left\{(m,n):0<m<n\leqslant N\text{ and }m\equiv n\mod 2\right\}.

Denote the
N
-th “component-wise” partial sums on the triangle
T_{1}
and rectangle
R
, respectively, by

\displaystyle S^{T_{1}}_{N}f_{a}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathscr{U}_{N}}f_{a}^{\triangle}(m,n)u_{m,n}(\xi,\eta),

\displaystyle S^{R}_{N}\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{0<m,n\leqslant N}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m,n)\sin\left(\frac{\pi m}{\sqrt{3}}x\right)\sin\left(\pi ny\right).

The next theorem is a key ingredient in the proof of our main result.

Theorem 8
.

Let
f\in L^{p}(T)
with
1<p<\infty
, and denote its antisymmetric part by
f_{a}
.
Then
f_{a}\in L^{p}(T_{1})
and
S^{T_{1}}_{N}f_{a}\to f_{a}
in
L^{p}(T_{1})
.

Proof.

Since
f\in L^{p}(T)
and
f_{a}
is a linear combination of
f
and its reflections, clearly
f_{a}\in L^{p}(T)

and, by symmetry (and abuse of notation),
{f_{a}=f_{a}|_{T_{1}}\in L^{p}(T_{1})}
.

Next, we break up the set of indices with
0<m,n\leqslant N
into three sets:

0<m<n,\quad n<m<3n\quad\text{and}\quad 3n<m,

noting that the coefficients when
m=n
and
m=3n
vanish.
From
Lemma

5
, we may label these groups as
(m,n)
,
(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
and
(m^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime\prime})
,
respectively.
Note that such labelling divides
[1,N]\times[1,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
into three disjoint regions,
which we label as
I=\mathscr{U}_{N}
,
II
and
III
; see
Fig.

3
.

It follows from (
6
) that

\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m,n)=-(-1)^{(m-n)/2}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})=(-1)^{(m+n)/2}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime\prime}).

Hence,

\displaystyle S^{R}_{N}\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathscr{U}_{N}}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m,n)\sin\left(\frac{\pi mx}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi ny\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sum_{(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})\in II}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})\sin\left(\frac{\pi m^{\prime}x}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi n^{\prime}y\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sum_{(m^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime\prime})\in III}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime\prime})\sin\left(\frac{\pi m^{\prime\prime}x}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi n^{\prime\prime}y\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathscr{U}_{N}}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m,n)\biggl[\sin\left(\frac{\pi mx}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi ny\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-(-1)^{(m+n)/2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{2\sqrt{3}(m+3n)}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi y}{2}(m-n)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+(-1)^{(m-n)/2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{2\sqrt{3}}(m-3n)\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi y}{2}(m+n)\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathscr{U}_{N}}\frac{\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}}(m,n)}{2}u_{m,n}(x,y)\quad\text{by \eqref{eqn:umn}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathscr{U}_{N}}f_{a}^{\triangle}(m,n)\mathscr{P}_{a}u_{m,n}(x,y).

The last line follows from (
7
) and (
9
).
Since
\mathscr{P}_{a}
is a linear operator, we have therefore proved that

\mathscr{P}_{a}\left[S^{T_{1}}_{N}f_{a}\right](x,y)=S^{R}_{N}\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}(x,y).

Furthermore, since each transformation
T_{1}\to T_{i}
is an isometry, we have

\left\lVert\mathscr{P}_{a}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({R})}=6^{1/p}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T_{1}})}\quad\text{for all}\quad u\in L^{p}(T_{1}).

Combining the last two displayed equations with the
L^{p}
convergence of double-sine series on
R
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert S_{N}^{T_{1}}f_{a}-f_{a}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T_{1}})}^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\left\lVert\mathscr{P}_{a}\left[S_{N}^{T_{1}}f_{a}\right]-\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({R})}^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\left\lVert S_{N}^{R}\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}-\mathscr{P}_{a}f_{a}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({R})}^{p}\to 0

as
N\to\infty
.
∎

When

, the Fourier coefficients of

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}}(m,n)

By explicit computation, Pragér obtained the following.

Lemma 9
.

For integers
0\leqslant m<n
with the same parity,

\displaystyle v_{m,n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2\cos\left(\frac{\pi mx}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi ny\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2(-1)^{(m+n)/2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi x}{2\sqrt{3}}(m+3n)\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi y}{2}(m-n)\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\quad-2(-1)^{(m-n)/2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi x}{2\sqrt{3}}(3n-m)\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi y}{2}(m+n)\right).

Furthermore,
v_{m,n}\equiv 0
whenever
n
and

Finally, with

as above, define the pairs

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} m^{\prime\prime}&=\tfrac{1}{2}(m+3n),\\

and

Theorem 12
.

Let

with
1<p<\infty
, and denote its symmetric part by

.

f_{s}\in L^{p}(T_{1})
and
S^{T_{1}}_{N}f_{s}\to f_{s}
in

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as that of

As before, we break up the set of indices with

0<m<n,\quad n<m<3n\quad\text{and}\quad 3n<m

9
, we may label these groups as
(m,n)
,
(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})

(m^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime\prime})
,
respectively, dividing the lattice
{[0,N]\times[1,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}

It follows from (

\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}}(m,n)=-(-1)^{(m-n)/2}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})=(-1)^{(m+n)/2}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}}(m^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime\prime}),

\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}}(0,n)=-(-1)^{n/2}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}}(3n/2,n/2)\quad\text{for all}\quad n>0.

Recall that we identify

We begin by grouping the partial sums on

\displaystyle S_{N}^{R}\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}(x,y)

(21)

\displaystyle\quad{}+\biggl[\sum_{(0,n)}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}}(0,n)\sin(\pi ny)

\displaystyle\quad{}+\sum_{(0^{\prime},n^{\prime})}\widehat{\mathscr{P}_{s}f_{s}}(0^{\prime},n^{\prime})\cos\left(\frac{0^{\prime}\pi x}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sin\left(\pi n^{\prime}y\right)\biggr].

7
Main Results for the Equilateral Triangle

We now wrap up with the main results as they apply to the original equilateral triangle,
T
.

Theorem 13

(
Práger, 1998
)
.

The functions

\displaystyle u_{m,n}:\quad m,n=1,2,\ldots,\quad

\displaystyle m\equiv n\mod 2,\quad 0<m<n;

\displaystyle v_{m,n}:\quad m=0,1,2,\ldots,n=1,2,\ldots,\quad

\displaystyle m\equiv n\mod 2,\quad 0\leqslant m<n,

form a complete, orthogonal system on
T
, consisting of eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet Laplacian.
Furthermore, each eigenfunction,
u_{m,n}
or
v_{m,n}
, corresponds to the eigenvalue
\pi^{2}\left(\tfrac{m^{2}}{3}+n^{2}\right)
.

As for the
L^{p}
theory, we can combine our
Theorems

8
and
12
to obtain the full result on
L^{p}(T)
.
As before, we use the following notation to keep track of our indices in the asymmetric and symmetric parts of the sums:

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{N}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(m,n):0<m<n\leqslant N\text{ and }m\equiv n\mod 2\right\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}_{N}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(m,n):0\leqslant m<n\text{ and }m\equiv n\mod 2\right\}.

Theorem 14
.

Let
f\in L^{p}(T)
with
1<p<\infty
. With the notation of the previous sections, let

S_{N}^{T}f:=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathscr{U}_{N}}f_{a}^{\triangle}(m,n)u_{m,n}+\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathscr{V}_{N}}f_{s}^{\triangle}(m,n)v_{m,n},

Then,
S_{N}^{T}f\to f
in
L^{p}(T)
.

Proof.

For any function
f
on
T
, write its decomposition into a symmetric and antisymmetric part with respect to the
y
-axis:

f=f_{a}+f_{s}.

Then clearly

\left\lVert f_{\nu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T})}^{p}=2\left\lVert f_{\nu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T_{1}})}^{p}\quad\text{for}\quad\nu=a,s,

whence

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T})}\leqslant 2^{1/p}\left(\left\lVert f_{a}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T_{1}})}+\left\lVert f_{s}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T_{1}})}\right).

Hence, since

\left[S_{N}^{T}f\right]_{\nu}=S_{N}^{T_{1}}f_{\nu}\quad\text{for}\quad\nu=a,s,

it follows that

\left\lVert S_{N}^{T}f-f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T})}\leqslant 2^{1/p}\left(\left\lVert S_{N}^{T_{1}}f_{a}-f_{a}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T_{1}})}+\left\lVert S_{N}^{T_{1}}f_{s}-f_{s}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({T_{1}})}\right)\to 0

by
Theorems

8
and
12
.
∎

8
Concluding remarks

Our argument made use of Práger’s cunning triangle-to-rectangle transformation
in order to reduce the convergence problem on the triangle to the well-known convergence on the rectangle.
In a similar, but more direct way, we were able to obtain these results for the 45-90-45 triangle.

There are, however, limitations to this approach, as a consequence of the following theorem due to
Lamé, 1833

and reported as Theorem 3.1 in
McCartin, 2003
.

Theorem 15

(Lamé’s Fundamental Theorem)
.

Suppose that
f(x,y)
can be represented by the trigonometric series

f(x,y)=\sum_{i}A_{i}\sin(\lambda_{i}x+\mu_{i}y+\alpha_{i})+B_{i}\cos(\lambda_{i}x+\mu_{i}y+\beta_{i})

with
\lambda_{i}^{2}+\mu_{i}^{2}=k^{2}
.
Then
f
is antisymmetric about any line about which it vanishes.

(a)

In general, the diagonal is not a line of

symmetry, so it cannot be a nodal line.

(b)

Mimicking the Práger construction with an angle of
\pi/2n
(
n\neq 2,3
) does not tile a rectangle.

Figure 5
:
Limitations of the triangle-to-rectangle constructions.

The implication for our argument is that, assuming Dirichlet boundary conditions on the hypothenuse of
T_{1}
,
we will generate a nodal (i.e. vanishing) line along the diagonal of the rectangle (see, e.g.,
Fig.

2
).
Lamé’s Theorem then requires the eigenfunctions to have a line of anti-symmetry along the diagonal.
The only possibilities are symmetry along the diagonal line itself, as in the square (see
Fig.

5
,(a));
or an arrangement of smaller triangles inside the rectangle
as in Práger’s construction with three hemiequilateral triangles.
In this case, the upper right-angle of the rectangle is cut into three angles of
\pi/6
.
Attempts to replace this decomposition by
n>3
triangles with angles
\pi/2n

will not tile a rectangle (see
Fig.

5
,(b)).

A more general treatment of eigenfunction expansions for the Dirichlet Laplacian in arbitrary triangular domains
requires further research.
