The Multiplicative Formula of Langlands for Orbital Integrals in G ​ L ​ ( 2 ) GL(2)
Malors Espinosa

Abstract
. In [ 11 ] , Langlands introduces a formula for a specific product of orbital integrals in GL ​ ( 2 , ℚ ) \mbox{GL}(2,\mathbb{Q}) . Altuğ, in [ 1 ] , employs this formula to manipulate the regular elliptic part of the trace formula, with the aim of eliminating the contribution of the trivial representation from the spectral side. In [ 4 ] , Arthur predicts that this formula coincides with a product of polynomials associated with zeta functions of orders developed by Zhiwei Yun in [ 13 ] . In [ 7 ] , we determined the explicit polynomials for the relevant quadratic orders. This paper demonstrates how these polynomials can effectively generalize Langlands’ formula to G ​ L ​ ( 2 , K ) GL(2,K) , for general algebraic number fields K K . Furthermore, we also use this formula to extend a well-known formula of Zagier to any algebraic number field and explain its applications in the contexts of the strategy of Beyond Endoscopy proposed by Langlands. 1.
Introduction and Motivation

1.1.
Context of the problem

The main conjecture of the Langlands Program is the Principle of Functoriality. Despite the progress that has been achieved in the study of this problem, and the implications it has in number theory, representation theory and the theory of the trace formula, it has not been settled in full generality. In
[
11
]
, Langlands proposes a strategy to prove this conjecture which is now known as the strategy of Beyond Endoscopy or simply BE. It requires the construction of a new trace formula in which the poles of automorphic
L-
functions play a prominent role.

The present article is motivated by precise questions in BE which we discuss below. To see the concrete nature of the problems we will consider, as well as the motivation and importance they have, we must briefly review the strategy of BE.

Recall that a trace formula is an identity equating two distributions built from different information of a reductive algebraic group, for example
G=\mbox{GL}(n)
, which is the only case we will consider below. Each side of the trace formula has its main part. For the geometric side, the main part is the regular elliptic part

\displaystyle\sum_{[\gamma]}\mbox{vol}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f),

where
[\gamma]
runs over all regular elliptic conjugacy classes in
GL(n,\mathbb{Q})
and
f
is a test function. Furthermore,
\mbox{vol}(\gamma)
refers to the adelic volume of the centralizer
G_{\gamma}
of
\gamma
and
\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f)
is the orbital integral of
f
.

On the spectral side, the main term is the discrete part which is

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}m_{\pi}\mbox{Tr}(\pi(f)).

It is the part of the spectral side of the trace formula that decomposes as a discrete sum. Here
f
is again a test function and
\pi
runs over the representations supporting the discrete part.

The strategy of BE postulates that there must be a
new
trace formula whose corresponding discrete part is

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\mu_{\pi,r}m_{\pi}\mbox{Tr}(\pi(f)),

where
\mu_{\pi,r}
is the order of the pole at
s=1
for the automorphic
L-
function
L(s,\pi,r)
associated to
\pi
and to some irreducible representation
r
of
GL(n,\mathbb{C})
. This new trace formula changes as we vary the representation
r
(
i.e.
we would have an
r-
trace formula for each
r
). The objective is to exploit this variation to classify representations, since proper functorial transfers of representations should be those with poles for some
r
.

Nevertheless, that these poles exist and behave in the desired way to make this definition valid is a consequence of Functoriality and cannot be taken for granted. Instead, we suppose it and work backwards. We do this for two reasons: firstly, we want an expression that does not rely on Functoriality for its definition. Secondly, it is not clear how the main geometric part (
i.e.
the regular elliptic part) should be modified to become the dominant part of the geometric side of this conjectural
r-
trace formula. We expect to obtain an expression for this out of this process.

For the manipulations that follow, Langlands supposes
\pi
to vary only in the representations that are unramified at all finite places and which, consequently, have trivial central character. Tauberian limit theory then implies that, supposing Functoriality, we have

(3)

\mu_{\pi,r}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\sharp\{p\mid p\leq n\}}\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq n}\log(p)\mbox{Tr}(r(c(\pi_{p})))

where the sum varies over primes and
c(\pi_{p})
is the semisimple conjugacy class associated to
\pi_{p}
where
\pi=\bigotimes_{p}\pi_{p}
is the decomposition of
\pi
into local representations. Substituting this back into equation (
2
), and exchanging the order of summation, we get

(4)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\sharp\{p\mid p\leq n\}}\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq n}\log(p)\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}m_{\pi}\mbox{Tr}(r(c(\pi_{p})))\mbox{Tr}(\pi(f)).

If we now suppose our test function
f
factorizes, that is, it can be written as

f=\displaystyle\prod_{p<\infty}f_{p}\times f_{\infty},

then we can construct for each fixed prime
q
a smooth function
h_{q}
of
GL(2,\mathbb{Q}_{q})
such that the new function
f^{q}
defined by

f^{q}=\displaystyle\prod_{p<\infty,p\neq q}f_{p}\times(h_{q}f_{q})\times f_{\infty},

satisfies

\mbox{Tr}(r(c(\pi_{q})))\mbox{Tr}(\pi(f))=\mbox{Tr}(\pi(f^{q})).

Concretely,
h_{q}
is the unramified spherical function on
GL(2,\mathbb{Q}_{q})
such that

\mathcal{S}(h_{q})(c_{q})=\mbox{Tr}(r(c_{q})),

for any Satake parameter
c_{q}
in
GL(2,\mathbb{Q}_{q})
and where
\mathcal{S}
is the Satake transform.
Using such functions, as we vary the prime
p
in the sum (
4
), we get that the discrete part of the
r
-trace formula should be

(5)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\sharp\{p\mid p\leq n\}}\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq n}\log(p)\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}m_{\pi}\mbox{Tr}(\pi(f^{p})).

Notice that the inner sum of equation (
5
) above is the discrete spectral side of a classical trace formula for the test function is
f^{p}
. With this observation we realize that the
r
-trace formula must be a weighted average of classical trace formulas. We remark that this expression does not depend on Functoriality to be defined.

The main parts of each side of the trace formula are not equal as distributions. However, for purposes of motivation we can treat them as if they were. In such case, the regular elliptic part of the
r-
trace formula must be

(6)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\sharp\{p\mid p\leq n\}}\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq n}\log(p)\displaystyle\sum_{[\gamma]}\mbox{vol}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f^{p}).

With this, we have obtained a candidate for the main term of the geometric side of the
r
-trace formula.

At this stage, the supposed equality between the regular elliptic part and discrete part of the desired
r-
trace formula manifests in the equality of the previous two limits in equations (
5
) and (
6
). Both make sense by themselves without Functoriality but present the serious analytic issue of whether they converge. Due to the presence of nontempered representations the spectral side limit will not converge. In the situation where the
\pi
are assumed to be unramified at all finite places, the only such nontempered representation is the trivial one. Hence, we could isolate it from the sum and merge it with the geometric side limit (
i.e.
pass it to the other side). We get
the equality between

(7)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\sharp\{p\mid p\leq n\}}\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq n}\log(p)\left(\displaystyle\sum_{[\gamma]}\mbox{vol}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f^{p})-\mbox{Tr}(\mathbf{1}(f^{p}))\right)

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\sharp\{p\mid p\leq n\}}\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq n}\log(p)\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\neq\mathbf{1}}m_{\pi}\mbox{Tr}(\pi(f^{p}))

We expect that what remains in the discrete spectral side converges since we have taken away the nontempered terms, so what is on the geometric side
must
converge. Hence, the following is unavoidable:
it should be possible to isolate the trace of the trivial representation and cancel its contribution from the spectral side, so that what remains when plugged into the limit converges. Furthermore, both geometric and spectral limits must converge to the same value.

Thus, for the strategy of BE to hold, the first problem of interest is the isolation of the nontempered spectrum from the regular elliptic part. In
[
9
]
the idea that the isolation process can be achieved by applying an additive Poisson Summation was introduced. This is exactly what Altuğ did in
[
1
]
in the case where
f_{p}
is the indicator of the standard maximal open compact subgroup at each finite prime
p
and
r
is the standard representation.

In
[
1
]
, Altuğ works with
\mbox{GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
. He also takes
r
to be the
k
-th symmetric power of the standard representation. Concretely, in
[
1
]
, he gives an expression for

\displaystyle\sum_{[\gamma]}\mbox{vol}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f^{p})-\mbox{Tr}(\mathbf{1}(f^{p})),

in terms of the trace and the determinant of
[\gamma]
. In order to perform the Poisson Summation required, and then the isolation of the trace of the trivial representation, two steps are introduced in
[
1
]
and that are of relevance for any future work that plans to follow these steps.

The first one is the application of the approximate functional equation to a zeta function which has been introduced by Zagier in
[
6
]
. This step is necessary to smooth the singularities of orbital integrals so that the hypothesis necessary for Poisson summation are satisfied. The second one is the evaluation of some Kloosterman sums, that appear once the Poisson summation has been performed, and that have very specific values. It is these values which are responsible for the isolation of the trace of the trivial representation. Both of these steps build on work that Langlands introduced in
[
11
]
. Concretely, he gives an explicit formula for the product of the orbital integrals over the nonarchimidean primes. This formula turns out to be the value at
s=1
of the aforementioned formula of Zagier. This realization is what drives the whole work of Altuğ in
[
1
]
.

Finally, in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
it is proven that the limit in equation (
7
) converges to the correct value.

1.2.
The work of this paper

It is of interest to generalize the work of Altuğ to other contexts. One of the most natural directions to extend these results is to change the base field, that is, to work over an arbitrary number field
K
as opposed to
\mathbb{Q}
. This is being carried out in the article
[
8
]
. In this paper, our work will be in the efforts of making this generalization possible.

Our main goal in this paper is to generalize the zeta function of Zagier, that Altuğ uses in
[
1
]
over the rational numbers, to an arbitrary number field. In the process of doing so, we will also prove the analogous formula of Langlands for the product of local orbital integrals.

These two perspectives, the zeta function of Zagier and the orbital integrals, are connected by the work of Yun in
[
13
]
. Concretely, he constructs zeta functions of certain orders and proves that at
s=1
they recover the values of the orbital integrals. This lead Arthur to conjecture in
[
4
]
that the zeta functions of these orders are the nonarchimedean Euler factors of Zagier’s zeta function. We will prove that this is indeed the case.

It follows easily from the definition of

For a

\mathcal{O}_{K}[\gamma]=\{a+b\gamma\mid a,b\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\},\;\;\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}}[\gamma]=\{a+b\gamma\mid a,b\in\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}}\}.

2.
Explicit Zeta Functions of Certain Orders

In the paper
[
13
]
a zeta function for general orders of number fields is defined. Furthermore, a local version of such zeta function is also defined for orders of reduced
K
- algebras where

is a local field. This general version is necessary to account for the diversity of algebras that appear when considering extensions by polynomials since an irreducible polynomial over a global number field may not remain irreducible over the completion at a prime.

We begin our brief discussion of

with the local case.

Definition 1
.

Let

be a non-Archimedean local field of characteristic zero and
\mathcal{O}\subseteq O_{K}
be a monogenic order, where

is the ring of integers of

where the sum runs over all ideals

of finite index.

In

a more general zeta function is defined, but it is not the one we have defined above. For general orders, and not only monogenic ones, a more careful construction is needed in order to make sure the results proven hold true. In the case of the orders we will deal with the previous definition is correct and coincides with the zeta function defined in
[
13

.

The main result of
[
13
]
is the existence of a functional equation for the zeta functions of orders. The proof goes by studying them locally, and verifying that the local versions satisfy the functional equation when they are properly modified.

For now, we are interested in the local situation so we will restrict to it. In this case, we have a non-Archimedean local field of characteristic zero
K
and a finite dimensional reduced
K-
algebra

which can be written as product of

The residue field of each one of these fields has cardinality

and the prime ideal

in

satisfies

(\pi_{i}O_{L_{i}})^{e_{i}}=pO_{L_{i}},

where
\pi_{i}
is a uniformizer of

and

is one of
O_{K}
. Here

and

are the ramification and inertia degrees of the extension

. With this at hand we define the following function of a complex parameter

\displaystyle V(s)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{g}(1-q^{-f_{i}s}),

Considering now an order
\mathcal{O}
of

Notice that

does not depend on the order
\mathcal{O}
selected. Now we can state the following proposition which is proved in
[
13

as theorem 2.5.

Proposition 2
.

Let
K
be a non-Archimedean local field of characteristic zero,
L
be a finite dimensional reduced
K
-algebra and
\mathcal{O}\subseteq O_{L}
. In the context of the previous discussion, define the function

\tilde{J}(s)=q^{ns}V(s)\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(s).

Then there exists a polynomial
P(x)\in 1+x\mathbb{Z}[x]
of degree
2n
such that

V(s)\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(s)=P(q^{-s}).

Furthermore,
\tilde{J}
satisfies the functional equation

\tilde{J}(s)=\tilde{J}(1-s),

or equivalently, the polynomial
P
satisfies

(qx^{2})^{n}P\left(\dfrac{1}{qx}\right)=P(x).

When
K
is a non-Archimedean local field of characteristic zero field and

is a quadratic reduced
K-
algebra there are three possible scenarios: either

is a field extension, which can be ramified or unramified, or

7

we have restricted to these three cases and computed explicitly what are the polynomials predicted by the previous proposition for certain family of orders.

The setup is as follows: if
\mathcal{O}_{L}
is the integral closure of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
in
L
, we get that the standard theory of discrete valuation rings guarantees that there exists an integral element
\Delta\in\mathcal{O}_{L}
such that

\mathcal{O}_{L}=\mathcal{O}_{K}[\Delta].

We define a sequence of orders
\mathcal{O}_{0}\supset\mathcal{O}_{1}\supset\mathcal{O}_{2}\supset...
by

\mathcal{O}_{n}=\mathcal{O}_{K}[p^{n}\Delta].

It is an easy exercise to verify that this sequence does not depend on the specific
\Delta
chosen. Each of these orders has a zeta function as defined above. Let us call such zeta function
\zeta_{n}
.

With this notation now settled we can state the result:

Theorem 2
.

For each
n\geq 0
define the following polynomials:

\displaystyle R_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=1+qX^{2}+q^{2}X^{4}+...+q^{n}X^{2n},

and for
n\geq 1
define

\displaystyle U_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(1+X)R_{n-1}(X)+q^{n}X^{2n},

\displaystyle S_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(1-X)R_{n-1}(X)+q^{n}X^{2n}.

Finally, also put
U_{0}(X)=S_{0}(X)=1
. Explicitly, these polynomials are

\displaystyle R_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=1+qX^{2}+q^{2}X^{4}+...+q^{n}X^{2n},

\displaystyle U_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=1+X+qX^{2}+qX^{3}+...+q^{n-1}X^{2n-2}+q^{n-1}X^{2n-1}+q^{n}X^{2n},

\displaystyle S_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=1-X+qX^{2}-qX^{3}+...+q^{n-1}X^{2n-2}-q^{n-1}X^{2n-1}+q^{n}X^{2n}.

Then we have

\displaystyle(1-q^{-s})\zeta_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=R_{n}(q^{-s}),

\displaystyle(1-q^{-2s})\zeta_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=U_{n}(q^{-s}),

\displaystyle(1-q^{-s})^{2}\zeta_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=S_{n}(q^{-s}).

That is,
R_{n},U_{n}
and

are the polynomials predicted by proposition
2
in the ramified, unramified and split case, respectively.

We refer the reader to

Associated to the quadratic extension

Colloquially, we might say the Hecke character allow us to merge the polynomials into a single family.

Finally, evaluating at
s=1
and making the change of variable
\mathfrak{d}
for
\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}
(both go over the divisors of
S_{\gamma}
), we obtain

\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{q}}\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f_{\mathfrak{q}})=\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{d}|S_{\gamma}}N_{K}(\mathfrak{d})\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{p}|\mathfrak{d}}\left(1-\dfrac{\chi_{\gamma}(\mathfrak{p})}{N_{K}(\mathfrak{p})}\right)

∎

With this we have recovered Langlands’ formula
9
, this time for a general number field. Furthermore, this settles Arthur’s Conjecture
1
, from the introduction, by using the previous theorem on
\mathbb{Q}
and letting
k_{p}=0
for all primes

of
\mathbb{Z}
except for one arbitrary one. This settles the first of our two goals.

4.
The Congruence Conditions

Our objective in this section is to change the condition
d\mid S_{\gamma}
for a different one which is more useful for applications. We begin with two lemmas.

Lemma 1
.

Let
E/F
be an extension of local fields. Let
\mathfrak{p}
the prime of
F
,
\mathfrak{P}
that of
E
and let
e
be the ramification exponent of this extension. If
E/F
is tamely and totally ramified then the exponent with which
\mathfrak{p}
divides
\mbox{Disc}(E/F)
is
e-1
.

Proof.

This follows from standard theory of local fields. Concretely, if
\mathfrak{D}
is the different of the extension then we know that the ideal norm satisfies

N_{E/F}(\mathfrak{D})=\mbox{Disc}(E/F).

We also know that by definition

N_{E/F}(\mathfrak{P})=\mathfrak{p}^{f(E/F)},

where
f(E/F)
is the inertia degree. Since
\mathfrak{p}
totally ramified, the inertia degree is
1
. Thus

N_{E/F}(\mathfrak{P})=\mathfrak{p}.

Hence, the exponent with which
\mathfrak{p}
divides
\mbox{Disc}(E/F)
coincides with the exponent with which
\mathfrak{P}
divides
\mathfrak{D}
. In the tamely ramified case this exponent is known to be
e-1
. (For a proof of this see
[
10
]
, proposition 8, chapter III). This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
E/\mathbb{Q}_{2}
be a finite field extension with ramification degree
e
. Let
\pi
be a uniformizer of
E
and
U
any unit of
\mathcal{O}_{E}
. Then, for any odd integer
1\leq t\leq 2e-1
, there is no solution in
\mathcal{O}_{E}
to the congruence

X^{2}-\pi^{t}U\equiv Y^{2}\pmod{4}.

Proof.

Suppose such
X
and
Y
exist and rewrite the congruence as

(X+Y)(X-Y)\equiv X^{2}-Y^{2}\equiv\pi^{t}U\pmod{4}.

If
\pi^{e}\mid X+Y
or
\pi^{e}\mid X-Y
, then using that

X+Y=X-Y+2Y,

and that
\pi^{e}\mid 2
, we deduce
\pi^{e}\mid X\pm Y
. In such situation, we have

\pi^{2e}|X^{2}-Y^{2},

which implies (since
4
is
\pi^{2e}
up to a unit) that

\pi^{2e}\mid\pi^{t},

but this is impossible for the range of values of
t
. Hence
\mbox{val}_{\pi}(X\pm Y)<e
.

Now, using this last inequality, and the nonarchimidean triangle inequality for valuations we deduce

\displaystyle\mbox{val}_{\pi}(X+Y)

\displaystyle=\mbox{val}_{\pi}(X-Y+2Y)

\displaystyle\geq\min\{\mbox{val}_{\pi}(X-Y),e+\mbox{val}_{\pi}(Y)\},

but since the valuation of
X-Y
and the
2Y
are different (one being strictly smaller than
e
, and the other at least as big as
e
) we deduce there is equality. Hence

\mbox{val}_{\pi}(X+Y)=\mbox{val}_{\pi}(X-Y)

Let us say this valuation is
0\leq m<e
and let us write

X+Y=\pi^{m}V,X-Y=\pi^{m}W,

for some units
V,W
in
\mathcal{O}_{E}
.

Then we have
\pi^{2m}VW
and
\pi^{t}U
have different valuations, because one is even and the other is odd. Also, both are smaller than
2e
. This implies that

\mbox{val}_{\pi}(\pi^{2m}VW-\pi^{t}U)=\min\{2m,t\}<2e,

but this contradicts that

\pi^{2m}VW-\pi^{t}U\equiv X^{2}-Y^{2}-\pi^{t}U\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

With this at hand we can now prove the following equivalence:

Proposition 9
.

Let
\gamma
be a regular elliptic element in
GL(2,K)
. Then, for an ideal
I
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, the following facts are equivalent

(1)

I
divides
S_{\gamma}
.

(2)

I^{2}
divides
\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)
and for each prime
\mathfrak{q}
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
above
2
we have

\dfrac{\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)}{\pi_{\mathfrak{q}}^{2\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(I)}}\in\square_{K}(4).

Here
\square_{K}(4)
is the set of squares classes modulo
4\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}}
and
\pi_{\mathfrak{q}}
is a uniformizer of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}}
.

Proof.

Recall the equality

(17)

(\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma))=S_{\gamma}^{2}\Delta_{\gamma}.

Let
\mathfrak{q}
be any prime of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
\pi
a uniformizer of
K_{\mathfrak{q}}
. In this proof we will write
\pi
instead of
\pi_{\mathfrak{q}}
to simplify notation. Taking valuation on both sides of the above equality, we deduce

\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma))=2\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(S_{\gamma})+\mbox{val}\mathfrak{q}(\Delta_{\gamma}).

To simplify notation we will write
r\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(I)
.
We prove both directions separately.

(1) implies (2):

If
I
divides
S_{\gamma}
then the equation (
17
) above implies
I^{2}
divides
\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)
.

What remains to be proven is the extra fact about the primes above
2
. To that end, let
\mathfrak{q}
be such a prime.
If
\mathfrak{q}
splits in the extension
K(\gamma)/K
then in
K_{\mathfrak{q}}
the characteristic polynomial of
\gamma
splits. That is,

\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)

is an integral square. Hence

\dfrac{\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)}{\pi^{2\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(I)}},

is a square itself and thus also modulo
4
.

If
\mathfrak{q}
is not split, let
\Delta_{\mathfrak{q}}
be a generator of the rings of integers of the extension

. Suppose further that it satisfies a quadratic equation

for some

. We thus know that for some unit

we have

\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)=\pi^{2n_{\mathfrak{q}}}u^{2}(A^{2}-4B),

where

. Using that
(1)
holds we get
2r\leq 2n_{\mathfrak{q}}
. Hence

\dfrac{\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)}{\pi^{2r}}=\pi^{2(n_{\mathfrak{q}}-r)}u^{2}(A^{2}-4B)\equiv(\pi^{n_{\mathfrak{q}}-r}uA)^{2}\pmod{4},

which is a square modulo
4
. This concludes the proof of this direction.

(2) implies (1):
Let
I
be an ideal that satisfies
(2)
. We must prove that for each prime ideal
\mathfrak{q}
we have

(18)

r\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(I)\leq\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(S_{\gamma}).

We have three possibilities for
\mathfrak{q}
:

(a):

It is unramified.

(b):

It is ramified but not above
2
.

(c):

It is ramified and above
2
.

For case (a), we have
\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\Delta_{\gamma})=0
. Using that (2) holds and equation
4
above we get

2r\leq\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma))=2\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(S_{\gamma}).

From this,
r\leq\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(S_{\gamma})
as desired.

For case (b), we have that
\mathfrak{q}
is tamely and totally ramified. Thus the inertia degree of
K_{\mathfrak{q}}(\gamma)/K_{\mathfrak{q}}
is
1
. Using lemma
1
we get

\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\Delta_{\gamma})=1.

Thus, from equation (
4
), we have

2r\leq\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma))=2\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(S_{\gamma})+1.

Since all the terms in the above equation are integers we have

2r\leq 2\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(S_{\gamma}),

and so
r\leq\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(S_{\gamma})
. This concludes this case.

For case (c) we proceed as follows: once more
\mathfrak{q}
is totally ramified. As such, we have that there exists a uniformizer of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}(\gamma)}
such that

(1)

it generates the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}(\gamma)}
,

(2)

it satisfies an Eisenstein polynomial, which we denote once more as

X^{2}-AX+B=0.

We write
A=\pi^{t}A_{0}
and
B=\pi B_{0}
, with
A_{0},B_{0}
units and
t\geq 1
. We can do this by definition of Eisenstein polynomial.

(For a proof of this fact, as well as of a discussion of Eisenstein polynomials, see
[
10
, section 5 chapter II]
).

Using this uniformizer and its Eisenstein polynomial we get

\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)=\pi^{2n_{\mathfrak{q}}}u^{2}(A^{2}-4B)=\pi^{2n_{\mathfrak{q}}}u^{2}(A_{0}^{2}\pi^{2t}-v^{2}\pi^{2e+1}B_{0}),

In the above equation we used
2=\pi^{e}v
for some unit
v
and where
e
is the ramification exponent of
\mathfrak{q}
above
2
. Notice that

2r\leq\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma))=2n_{\mathfrak{q}}+\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(A_{0}^{2}\pi^{2t}-v^{2}\pi^{2e+1}B_{0})=2n_{\mathfrak{q}}+\min\{2t,2e+1\},

where the last equality follows from the ultrametirc inequality, which is an equality in this case because the valuations of the terms are different.

Let us suppose for a contradiction that
m\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=r-n_{\mathfrak{q}}\geq 1
. Hence

2m\leq\min\{2t,2e+1\}.

In particular
2m
is not bigger than either of
2t
or
2e+1
. We thus have

\dfrac{\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)}{\pi^{2r}}=u^{2}(A_{0}^{2}\pi^{2t-2m}-\pi^{2e+1-2m}vB_{0}^{2})

Given that (2) holds, and that
\mathfrak{q}
is a prime above
2
, we know this quotient is a square modulo
4
. That is

u^{2}(A_{0}^{2}\pi^{2t-2m}-\pi^{2e+1-2m}vB_{0}^{2})\in\square_{K}(4),

and since
u
is invertible, then

(19)

A_{0}^{2}\pi^{2t-2m}-\pi^{2e+1-2m}vB_{0}^{2}\in\square_{K}(4).

Notice that
2e+1-2m
is an odd integer between
1
and
2e-1
and that

is a unit. Hence, lemma
2
implies the above inclusion (
19
) cannot occur. This contradiction shows
r\leq n_{\mathfrak{q}}
. This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.
Generalization of Zagier’s Zeta Function

We begin with two lemmas that will aid in our construction. The first one is as follows:

Lemma 3
.

Let
K
be a number field and
\delta\in\square_{K}(4)
be a nonsquare algebraic integer of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Then

(i)

There exists a regular elliptic matrix
\gamma\in GL(2,K)
with entries in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)=\delta
.

(ii)

The matrix
\gamma
in (i) is a contributing matrix for

f=\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{q}}f_{\mathfrak{q},\;k_{\mathfrak{q}}},

with
k_{\mathfrak{q}}=\mbox{val}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\det(\gamma))
for each prime
\mathfrak{q}
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

Proof.

We begin with the proof of
(i).
Because
\delta\in\square_{K}(4)
there exist integers
m
and
r
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
such that

\delta=4m+r^{2}.

We have
m\neq 0
because
\delta
is itself not a square. Consider the matrix

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}r&1\\
m&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of this matrix is

\mbox{Trace}(\gamma)^{2}-4\det(\gamma)=r^{2}-4(-m)=\delta.

We conclude it is regular elliptic since
\delta
is not a square, so the characteristic polynomial is indeed irreducible.
(ii) is immediate by the definition.
∎

We also have:

Lemma 4
.

Let
K
be a number field and
\delta\in\square_{K}(4)
be a nonsquare algebraic integer of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
be two regular elliptic matrices such that

\mbox{Trace}(\gamma_{1})^{2}-4\det(\gamma_{1})=\mbox{Trace}(\gamma_{2})^{2}-4\det(\gamma_{2})=\delta.

Also, let
f_{1},f_{2}
be functions to which
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
contribute respectively. Then we have the following equalities

(1)

\chi_{\gamma_{1}}=\chi_{\gamma_{2}}
,

(2)

S_{\gamma_{1}}=S_{\gamma_{2}}
,

(3)

\mathcal{O}(s,\gamma_{1},f_{1})=\mathcal{O}(s,\gamma_{2},f_{2})
.

(4)

\mathcal{O}_{K}[\gamma_{1}]=\mathcal{O}_{K}[\gamma_{2}]
.

Proof.

The quadratic extension generated by
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
coincide. Concretely, it is
K(\sqrt{\delta})/K
. The character depends on the extension itself and not in the polynomial that generates it. This proves (i).

Using the definition of
S_{\gamma_{1}}
and
S_{\gamma_{2}}
, we have

(\mbox{Trace}(\gamma_{1})^{2}-4\det(\gamma_{1}))=S_{\gamma_{1}}^{2}\Delta_{\gamma_{1}},

and

(\mbox{Trace}(\gamma_{2})^{2}-4\det(\gamma_{2}))=S_{\gamma_{2}}^{2}\Delta_{\gamma_{2}}.

We know that
\Delta_{\gamma_{1}}=\Delta_{\gamma_{2}}
because it is the relative discriminant of the extension
K(\sqrt{\delta})/K
which depends only on
\delta
. Thus, from the equations above, we deduce

S_{\gamma_{1}}^{2}=\dfrac{(\delta)}{\Delta_{\gamma_{1}}}=\dfrac{(\delta)}{\Delta_{\gamma_{2}}}=S_{\gamma_{2}}^{2}.

From this (ii) follows.

Using theorem
3
, and parts
(1)
and
(2)
of this lemma, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(s,\gamma_{1},f_{1})

\displaystyle=N_{K}(S_{\gamma_{1}})^{s}\displaystyle\sum_{I|S_{\gamma_{1}}}N_{K}(I)^{(1-2s)}\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{p}|I^{\prime}}\left(1-\dfrac{\chi_{\gamma_{1}}(\mathfrak{p})}{N_{K}(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=N_{K}(S_{\gamma_{2}})^{s}\displaystyle\sum_{I|S_{\gamma_{2}}}N_{K}(I)^{(1-2s)}\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{p}|I^{\prime}}\left(1-\dfrac{\chi_{\gamma_{2}}(\mathfrak{p})}{N_{K}(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(s,\gamma_{2},f_{2}).

Finally, using proposition
7
and
(2)
, we know
\mathcal{O}_{K}[\gamma_{1}]
and
\mathcal{O}_{K}[\gamma_{2}]
localize to the same local orders at every prime. Hence,

\mathcal{O}_{K}[\gamma_{1}]=\mathcal{O}_{K}[\gamma_{2}].

∎

Remark 4
.

One might be tempted to guess that if
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are as above, then one can construct
f_{1}=f_{2}
. This not true because the functions are built out of the determinants which are different.

For example, consider
K=\mathbb{Q}
and
\delta=45
. The matrices

\gamma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\
9&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\;,\gamma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}5&1\\
5&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

have characteristic polynomials
X^{2}-3X-9
and
X^{2}-5X-5
, respectively. Both generate the extension
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})/\mathbb{Q}
. Nevertheless,
f_{1}\neq f_{2}
. They differ at
p=3
and
p=5
. Indeed
f_{1}
has local factor at
5
equal to
f_{5,0}
while
f_{2}
has local factor at
5
equal to
f_{5,1}
. On the other hand, at
p=3
the local factor of

is
f_{3,2}
while that of
f_{2}
is
f_{3,0}
. They coincide at every other place.

For the prime
p=3
, the extension
\mathbb{Q}_{3}(\sqrt{5})/\mathbb{Q}_{3}
is unramified and
\sqrt{5}
generates te ring of integers. Expanding
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
with respect to the corresponding integral basis we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{3}{2}+\dfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{5},

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{5}{2}+\dfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{5}.

Since
\mbox{val}_{3}(3/2)=1
, we deduce their contribution at the prime

is the same. Furthermore, as we know from proposition
7
, we must have

\mbox{val}_{3}(S_{\gamma_{1}})=\mbox{val}_{3}(S_{\gamma_{2}})=1.

Indeed, the relative discriminant of the extension

is
(5)

S_{\gamma_{i}}^{2}=\dfrac{(45)}{(5)}=(3)^{2},\;i=1,2.

Hence

. A similar analysis can be done at the other primes.

Remark 5

Let

Remark 6

We have the following result relating to the properties of this function.

Theorem 4
.

The function

(1)

It can be analytically continued to an entire function

\Lambda(s,\delta)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=N_{K}(S_{\delta})^{s}L_{\infty}(s,\chi_{\delta})L(s,\delta).

(3)

We have the functional equation

(4)

We have the following relationship between the different global zeta functions

\Lambda(s,\delta)=\Lambda(s,\chi_{\delta})\cdot\dfrac{\Lambda_{\mathcal{O}_{\delta}}(s)}{\Lambda_{K(\sqrt{\delta})}(s)}.

For a given ideal

We have

\displaystyle L(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{I^{2}|\delta}^{\prime}\dfrac{1}{N_{K}(I)^{2s-1}}L\left(s,\chi_{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{I^{2}|\delta}^{\prime}\dfrac{1}{N_{K}(I)^{2s-1}}\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{q}|I^{\prime}}\left(1-\dfrac{\chi_{\delta}(\mathfrak{q})}{N_{K}(\mathfrak{q})^{s}}\right)L(s,\chi_{\delta})

\displaystyle=L(s,\chi_{\delta})\displaystyle\sum_{I|S_{\delta}}\dfrac{1}{N_{K}(I)^{2s-1}}\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{q}|I^{\prime}}\left(1-\dfrac{\chi_{\delta}(\mathfrak{q})}{N_{K}(\mathfrak{q})^{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=N_{K}(S_{\delta})^{-s}L(s,\chi_{\delta})N_{K}(S_{\delta})^{s}\displaystyle\sum_{I|S_{\delta}}\dfrac{1}{N_{K}(I)^{2s-1}}\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{q}|I^{\prime}}\left(1-\dfrac{\chi_{\delta}(\mathfrak{q})}{N_{K}(\mathfrak{q})^{s}}\right)

Notice that we have used that

satisfies the congruence conditions for

to change the index of summation. Multiplying by

we get

\Lambda(s,\delta)=\Lambda(s,\chi_{\delta})\mathcal{O}(s,\delta).

From this equation we get the four claims. (2) is the statement of the above equation. Furthermore, both factors on the right are entire and satisfy the functional equation, one by standard theory of characters and the other by theorem
3
above.

Finally, (4) follows immediately from (2) and proposition
3
above.
∎

Remark 7
.

We have spent a considerable amount of effort in this section to prove that the choices of
\gamma
, and thus of
f
, do not change the definitions associated to a given nonsquare
\delta\in\square_{K}(4)
. This might misled one to think that
f
is not really important as it is specified by the given
\gamma
, but this is imprecise.

In the applications of this section, to construct the generalization of Zagier zeta function, we have started out from some appropriate
\delta
, then constructed
\gamma
and then
f
. With all these, we can invoke the properties of
\mathcal{O}(s,\gamma,f)
and prove the choices were not relevant.

Nevertheless, in other applications, as those we shall see in the next section, the process goes in the opposite direction: we are given a test function, which then sifts those
\gamma
that contribute to it. Usually the test function is constructed to satisfy properties desirable to the appropriate problem at hand.

In summary, neither parameter is superfluous, they are just in different ways according to the purposes at hand.

With this at hand we can now discuss how the above construction recovers the original Zagier zeta function when
K=\mathbb{Q}
. Zagier’s original function is

(21)

L(s,\delta)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\displaystyle\sum_{f^{2}|\delta}^{\prime}\dfrac{1}{f^{2s-1}}L\left(s,\left(\dfrac{\frac{\delta}{f^{2}}}{\cdot}\right)\right).

The sum is taken only over those
f
such that
\delta/f^{2}\equiv 0,1\pmod{4}
. Notice that
\square_{\mathbb{Q}}(4)=\{0,1\}.
In here

\left(\dfrac{\cdot}{\cdot}\right),

is the Kronecker Symbol. This symbol allows us to write explicitly the quadratic sign character of quadratic extensions over
\mathbb{Q}
. Concretely, we have

\chi_{\gamma}(\cdot)=\left(\dfrac{D_{\gamma}}{\cdot}\right),

where
D_{\gamma}
is the fundamental discriminant of the extension generated by
\gamma
(i.e. the specific generator of
\Delta_{\gamma}
obtained by computing with an integral basis). Hence, for each
f|S_{\gamma}
we have

\chi_{f}(\cdot)=\left(\dfrac{\frac{\delta}{f^{2}}}{\cdot}\right),

so that equation (
21
) is indeed the case for
K=\mathbb{Q}
of definition
11
.
Furthermore, Zagier’s zeta function has a completion, as follows:

(22)

\Lambda_{O}(s,\delta)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left(\dfrac{|\delta|}{\pi}\right)^{s/2}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{z+i_{\delta}}{2}\right)L(s,\delta),

where
i_{\delta}
is
0
or
1
according to whether
\delta
is positive or negative, respectively. We have used the subindex
O
to denote
original
as opposed to the completion we are defining above via theorem
4
.

Notice that
\Lambda_{O}
depends only on the sign
\delta
itself without using the character
\chi_{\delta}
. Instead, in theorem
4
we used the properties of the character to define the completion. What we must verify is that both of these approaches coincide (as opposed of one being an entire multiple of the other). Indeed we have

Proposition 11
.

In the context of the discussion above, both completions of Zagier’s zeta function coincide. That is,

\Lambda_{O}(s,\delta)=\Lambda(s,\delta).

Proof.

The computations above, and the Euler Factor expansion of the
L
-function of a character, imply for
K=\mathbb{Q}

(23)

L(s,\chi_{\gamma})O(s,\gamma,f)=S_{\gamma}^{s}\displaystyle\sum_{d^{2}\mid\;\delta}^{{}^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{d^{2s-1}}L\left(s,\left(\dfrac{\frac{\delta}{d^{2}}}{\cdot}\right)\right)=S_{\gamma}^{s}L\left(s,\delta\right).

Notice that the
L
function on the left hand side is one associated with a quadratic sign character
\chi_{\gamma}
while that on the right is associated to the discriminant
\tau^{2}-4\det(\gamma)=\delta
.

In order to get the equality let us complete the right hand side following equation (
22
) above. We have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{O}\left(s,\delta\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{|\delta|}{\pi}\right)^{s/2}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{s+i_{\delta}}{2}\right)L(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{|S_{\gamma}^{2}D_{\gamma}|^{s/2}}{\pi^{s/2}}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{s+i_{\delta}}{2}\right)L(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=S_{\gamma}^{s}|D_{\gamma}|^{s/2}\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{s+i_{\delta}}{2}\right)L(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=S_{\gamma}^{s}|D_{\gamma}|^{s/2}\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{s+i_{\delta}}{2}\right)S_{\gamma}^{-s}L(s,\chi_{\gamma})O(s,\gamma,f)

\displaystyle=|D_{\gamma}|^{s/2}\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{s+i_{\delta}}{2}\right)L(s,\chi_{\gamma})O(s,\gamma,f).

Recall that the completion of the
L-
function associated to
\chi_{\gamma}
is

\left(\dfrac{|D_{\gamma}|}{\pi}\right)^{s/2}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{s+i_{\gamma}}{2}\right)L(s,\chi_{\gamma}),

with
i_{\gamma}=0
or
1
according to
\chi_{\gamma}(-1)=1
or
\chi_{\gamma}(-1)=-1
. By definition the Kronecker Symbol satisfies for an integer
n

\left(\frac{n}{-1}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&n\geq 0,\\
-1&n<0.\end{array}\right.

Since
\delta=S_{\gamma}^{2}D_{\gamma}
, it follows
\delta
and
D_{\gamma}
have the same sign. Consequently the conditions for the value of
i_{\delta}
and
i_{\gamma}
coincide. We conclude

\Gamma\left(\dfrac{s+i_{\delta}}{2}\right)=\Gamma\left(\dfrac{s+i_{\gamma}}{2}\right).

Using this in the computations above, we conclude

\Lambda_{O}\left(s,\delta\right)=\Lambda(s,\chi_{\gamma})O(s,\gamma,f)=\Lambda(s,\gamma),

as desired.
∎

An immediate consequence of the above is the recovery of the well known properties of the original Zagier’s zeta function.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\delta\equiv 0,1\pmod{4}
be a nonsquare integer, then
\Lambda(s,\delta)
is entire and satsfies the functional equation

\Lambda(s,\delta)=\Lambda(1-s,\delta).

Proof.

This is immediate from theorem
4
and the equality
\Lambda_{O}(s,\delta)=\Lambda(s,\delta)
.
∎

6.
Relevance of our work in the strategy of BE

The results we prove are very relevant for the generalization to arbitrary number fields of the methods in
[
1
]
. We will discuss their use only over
\mathbb{Q}
, to simplify the exposition. The use in arbitrary number fields is the same and the role our results play is the strategy is in proving the required results hold. We refer to the interested reader to
[
8
]
for a deeper discussion over other number fields.

We will fix the prime
p
in this subsection and define

f=\displaystyle\prod_{q<\infty}f_{q}\times f_{\infty},

where at finite primes
q\neq p
we have
f_{q}
is the indicator function of the maximal open compact in
\mbox{GL}(2,\mathbb{Q}_{q})
and

f_{p}=p^{-k/2}\mathbf{1}\left(X\in\mbox{Mat}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{p})\mid|\det(X)|_{p}=p^{-k}\right),

where
k
is a nonnegative integer. In the notation used above,
f_{p}=p^{-k/2}f_{p,\;k}
while for
p\neq q
,
f_{q}=f_{q,0}
. Using theorem
1
we get

(24)

\displaystyle\prod_{q<\infty}\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f_{q})=p^{-k/2}\displaystyle\sum_{d|S_{\gamma}}d\displaystyle\prod_{q|d}\left(1-\dfrac{\left(\frac{D_{\gamma}}{q}\right)}{q}\right).

Formula (
24
) is useful because it allows the finite part of the orbital integral to merge with the volume term. Concretely, it can be proven that

\mbox{vol}(\gamma)=\sqrt{|D_{\gamma}|}L(1,\chi_{\gamma}),

where
\chi_{\gamma}(\cdot)=\left(\frac{D_{\gamma}}{\cdot}\right)
. Hence, each of the summands

\mbox{vol}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f).

of the regular elliptic part becomes

p^{-k/2}\sqrt{|D_{\gamma}|}L(1,\chi_{\gamma})\left(\displaystyle\sum_{d|S_{\gamma}}d\displaystyle\prod_{q|d}\left(1-\dfrac{\left(\frac{D_{\gamma}}{q}\right)}{q}\right)\right)\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f_{\infty})

Upon manipulation with the definition of
S_{\gamma}
this gets transformed into

S_{\gamma}^{-1}L(1,\chi_{\gamma})\left(\displaystyle\sum_{d|S_{\gamma}}d\displaystyle\prod_{q|d}\left(1-\dfrac{\left(\frac{D_{\gamma}}{q}\right)}{q}\right)\right)\cdot p^{-k/2}|\tau^{2}\mp 4p^{k}|^{1/2}\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f_{\infty}).

The Poisson Summation we desire to apply should be performed over the trace
\tau
. In our present case,
\tau
is an integer so we could proceed if we knew two things. First, that we actually had a complete lattice as required by the Poisson Summation formula. Secondly, that we are dealing with a sum over this complete lattice of a Schwartz function evaluated at the points of the lattice. The whole point of
[
1
]
is to overcome these two issues and then perform Poisson Summation to find the contribution of the trivial representation.

Altuğ proves is that after several nontrivial manipulations, the term

p^{-k/2}|\tau^{2}\mp 4p^{k}|^{1/2}\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f_{\infty})

is indeed a function evaluated at the integer
\tau
as required. The issue is that it is not a Schwartz function as it has the singularities introduced by the orbital integral. Furthermore,

S_{\gamma}^{-1}L(1,\chi_{\gamma})\left(\displaystyle\sum_{d|S_{\gamma}}d\displaystyle\prod_{q|d}\left(1-\dfrac{\left(\frac{D_{\gamma}}{q}\right)}{q}\right)\right)

is just a number, not an actual Schwartz function evaluated at some integer. Altuğ overcomes these two issues by invoking Zagier’s zeta function. What Altuğ realizes is

L(1,\tau^{2}\mp 4p^{k})=S_{\gamma}^{-1}L(1,\chi_{\gamma})\left(\displaystyle\sum_{d|S_{\gamma}}d\displaystyle\prod_{q|d}\left(1-\dfrac{\left(\frac{D_{\gamma}}{q}\right)}{q}\right)\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{d^{2}|\;\delta}^{\prime}\dfrac{1}{d}L\left(1,\left(\dfrac{\frac{\delta}{d^{2}}}{\cdot}\right)\right).

This is how Zagier’s zeta function appears in the strategy. We proved the corresponding equation for general number fields in section
5
above.
The regular elliptic part becomes

(25)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pm\tau}p^{-k/2}L(1,\tau^{2}\mp 4p^{k})|\tau^{2}\mp 4p^{k}|^{1/2}\mathcal{O}(\gamma,f_{\infty}),

where the sum goes over the choices of
\pm
and
\tau
that give irreducible characteristic polynomials
X^{2}-\tau X\pm p^{k}
.
Altuğ then proceeds to prove this can indeed be modified into an appropriate sum over complete lattices of Schwartz functions to which one can perform Poisson Summation. In order to do so, what remains is to smooth the singularities of the orbital integral and fix the conditional convergence introduce by taking the limit at
s=1
above. He achieves this by invoking the approximate functional equation.

Recall that the approximate functional equation is a tool to understand the behaviour of
L-
functions in the critical strip, where the Dirichlet Series expansion is not valid. In order to carry this out, the classical functional equation for the
L
function must already be known for its completion
\Lambda
. This is an instance of corollary
1
which we proved in section
5
.

Once the approximate functional equation is performed, the previous sum (
25
) takes the form

(26)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\displaystyle\sum_{\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}\neq\square}\displaystyle\sum_{d^{2}|\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}}^{{}^{\prime}}\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}G(\pm,\tau,a,d),

for some complicated function
G
that depends on the parameters
\pm,\tau,a,d
. The sum over
a
is introduced by the approximate functional equation. Notice that the terms that index the sum are all terms we have found before: first,
\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}\neq\square
means this is not square, but it is a discriminant. The third sum is the one of Zagier’s zeta function and includes the congruence conditions.

The function
G(\pm,\tau,a,d)
, complicated as it might be, makes sense for all choices of
\pm,\tau,a,d
. Due to this fact, we can define the complementary sum

(27)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\displaystyle\sum_{\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}=\square}\displaystyle\sum_{d^{2}|\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}}^{{}^{\prime}}\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}G(\pm,\tau,a,d),

The congruence conditions, denoted by
′
, mean exactly the same. That is,

(1)

d^{2}|\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}
,

(2)

\dfrac{\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}}{4}\equiv 0,1\pmod{4}
.

Notice how these make sense even if
\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}
is a square. In such case we could not define
S_{\gamma}
because there is no associated regular elliptic matrix nor quadratic extension. This shows the importance of the congruence conditions and their equivalence with the divisibility condition
d|S_{\gamma}
. Without them, we do not know what should the complementary sum be. Thus, we wouldn’t be able to complete a lattice nor perform Poisson summation.

In this way, we can add these two sums to
complete
the lattice of integers
\tau
to get the completed sum

(28)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{Z}}\displaystyle\sum_{d^{2}|\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}}^{{}^{\prime}}\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}G(\pm,\tau,a,d).

Rearranging the order of summation one gets

(29)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}\displaystyle\sum_{d^{2}|\tau^{2}\pm 4p^{k}}^{{}^{\prime}}G(\pm,\tau,a,d).

At this stage one studies the inner sum and proves that Poisson Summation can be performed on it. What follows after this is a long analysis of the main term of the spectral side of the Poisson Summation to find in there the contribution of the trivial representation.

The congruence conditions are important once more after Poisson summation as follows. Once it has been performed, it is necessary to simplify a Dirichlet Series that naturally stems from it, and whose coefficients are Kloosterman Sums associated to the character
\chi_{\gamma}
. Concretely, it is important to evaluate exactly

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{d^{2s+1}}\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{K_{a,d}}{a^{s+1}},

where

K_{a,d}=\displaystyle\sum_{m\pmod{4ad^{2}}}^{\prime}\left(\dfrac{(m^{2}\pm 4p^{k}/d^{2})}{a}\right).

Here
′
means once more the congruence conditions for a set of representatives of
\mathbb{Z}
modulo
4ad^{2}
. That is, the sum runs over those classes
m
for which
d
satisfies the congruence conditions for
m^{2}\pm 4p^{k}
. It turns out that these sums can be evaluated precisely by local methods because the congruence conditions, and the Kloosterman sums they define as above, are multiplicative. That is, one can decompose the above sum into an Euler product and evaluate each factor independently. It is unclear how would one proceed here if one needed the condition
d|S_{\gamma}
instead. It is even unclear what the definition of the Kloosterman sum would be.

In these computations, at the prime
p
, the Euler factor becomes the contribution, from the finite places, of the trace of the trivial representation (see
[
11
]
, section 2.1).

To appreciate the congruence conditions, one could remove the
′
in the above Dirichlet Series and simplify. The new Dirichlet series still factors into an Euler product. For odd primes there is no change in them, but for
p=2
one instead gets that the Euler factor when evaluated at
s=1
either does not simplify to a simple fraction or it simplifies to a

\dfrac{1-2^{-(k+3)}}{1-2^{-1}}.

This is
not
what comes from the trivial representation.

What we develop in this paper allows us to repeat for general number fields the steps of the process in which the congruence conditions were relevant. This is being done in
[
8
]
. All the stages where the congruence conditions were the only obstacle to generalize, as discussed above for
\mathbb{Q}
, can be carried out now with this generalization for
K
.

7.
Questions and future work

Our work in this paper highlights complications that will likely appear when generalizing to other contexts of importance. Furthermore, we see that some of these problems can be studied independently of their relationships to the strategy of BE.

1. What regularity do the polynomials of local orders have?
One of the main observations of our previous discussion is that all of the calculations are possible because of veracity of Arthur’s conjecture
1
above. In particular, because we know the explicit left hand side of the equation.

Thanks to the work of Yun in
[
13
]
we have, at least for
GL(n,K)
, the existence of the polynomials in the right hand side. However, we do not know them explicitly for
n>2
as we do for
GL(2)
. It is of relevance then to know these values, or at least enough of their properties to deduce similar results as the ones used in the case of
GL(2)
.

Significantly, we see that in the case the local polynomials are produced from a regular elliptic matrix, the Hecke character of the global quadratic allows us to describe uniformly these polynomials which otherwise distribute themselves different families according to the reduced algebra one is working with.

We could repeat the construction with regular elliptic matrices in higher rank. The nature of the local orders is related to the splitting of primes in the associated global extension. Since we know reciprocity laws are more complicated in higher degree, as well as less explicit, is there such a similar description possible in higher rank?

2. What are the concrete congruence conditions?
As we have pointed out above, the zeta functions of orders exist for
GL(n)
. Thus their product is a candidate to play the role that Zagier’s Zeta Function plays here. However, in the process of constructing the global product, what appears naturally as the indexing set is a divisibility condition
I|S_{\gamma}
. If what we have done for
GL(2)
is to serve as our guide, it should be expected that these conditions are equivalent to other more explicit conditions which allow us to complete the lattice for Poisson Summation and to split the Kloosterman Sums into local Euler Factors. What are these equivalent conditions?

3. The congruence conditions and the nontempered spectrum?
As we have mentioned, the congruence conditions allow for the evaluation of the Kloosterman sums and lead to the local trace of the trivial representation to be found. We have seen that Zagier´s zeta function satisfies two properties. First, its Euler factors are related to the orbital integrals from the geometric side of the trace formula. Second, in its global expression, it is indexed with a congruence condition that precisely allows the detection of information from the spectral side of the trace formula. How general is this and is there a deeper reason for it? The author does not know a satisfactory answer to this question.

4. What applications can the generalizations of Zagier’s zeta function have?

Over the rational numbers this function has been used by Zagier to construct concrete modular forms (see
[
6
, proposition 3.(iii), section 4, page 130]
) associated to quadratic fields. It has also been used by Bykovskii in
[
5
, equation 1.5]
to settle a conjecture of Iwaniec on the asymptotics of the distribution of prime geodesics related to Selberg’s zeta function. It is also used, in the same paper, to study the asymptotics of the class numbers of imaginary quadratic fields as well as on the number of representations of a number as a sum of three squares. More recently it has been used by Soundararajan and Young to study the prime geodesic theorem and its asymptotics (
[
12
, equations (4) and (5)]
). It is an interesting question if our generalization has similar applications. The author hopes to return to this question at a later date.
