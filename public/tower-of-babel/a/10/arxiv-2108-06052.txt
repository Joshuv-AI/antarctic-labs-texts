No breather theorems for the mean curvature flow Thanks: Liang Cheng’s Research partially supported by Natural Science Foundation of Hubei 2019CFB511
Liang Cheng and Yongjia Zhang

Abstract
. In this article we study the breathers of the mean curvature flow in the Euclidean space. A breather is a solution to the mean curvature flow which repeats itself up to isometry and scaling once in a while. We prove several no breather theorems in the noncompact category, that is, under certain conditions, a breather of the mean curvature flow must be a solitonic solution (self-shrinker, self-expander, or translator).



1.
Introduction

A breather of the mean curvature flow is a solution which is self-similar at two different times. Precisely, it is defined as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
x:M^{n}\times I\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be a smooth and immersed solution to the mean curvature flow. If there exist two time instances
t_{1},t_{2}\in I
,
t_{1}<t_{2}
, a constant
\alpha>0
, an isometry
F:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, and a self-diffeomorphism
\phi:M^{n}\rightarrow M^{n}
, such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle x(p,t_{2})=\alpha F\circ x(\phi(p),t_{1})\quad\text{ for all }\quad p\in M,

then
x
is called a breather. If
\alpha=1
,
\alpha<1
, or
\alpha>1
, then the breather is called steady, shrinking, or expanding, respectively.

Clearly, the
solitonic solutions
(by this term we specifically mean the
self-shrinkers
, the
self-expanders
, and the
translators
) can be regarded as the most trivial examples of the breather. Our goal in this article is to prove some no breather theorems for the mean curvature flow, that is, under certain conditions, the breathers must be these trivial ones—the solitonic solutions.

The no breather theorems were first proved by Perelman
[
19
]
for the Ricci flow. He proved that a closed breather of the Ricci flow must be a gradient Ricci soliton; these results are the first applications of his entropy formula. Later developments have generalized the no breather theorems to the noncompact Ricci flow under diverse conditions; see, for instance,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
. Among them the strongest one for the shrinking case is proved by the authors
[
6
]
, where they have shown that a shrinking breather with Ricci curvature bounded from below must be a shrinking gradient Ricci soliton. The result in
[
6
]
is recently generalized to the harmonic Ricci flow in
[
3
]
.
Since the mean curvature flow is also a parabolic geometric evolution equation bearing many similar properties as the Ricci flow, it is natural to ask whether the same results also hold for the mean curvature flow. One may immediately think that Huisken’s monotonicity formula
[
13
]
would have some implications, and this is indeed how Magni-Mantegazza
[
15
, Theorem 2.3]
proved that a closed shrinking breather of the mean curvature flow must be a self-shrinker. Applying a method similar to that of Perelman, they considered the supremum of Huisken’s entropy, which is always attained in the closed case; see
[
15
]
or Remark
3.4
for a brief discussion of this method. In the closed case, Magni-Mantegazza also proved the no steady and expanding breather theorems for the closed mean curvature flow. Their argument goes as follows. A closed mean curvature flow (with any codimension) always develops finite-time singularity. But a steady or expanding breather gives rise to an immortal solution; see section 4 and section 5 below. Hence closed steady or expanding breather does not exist. However, Magni-Mantegazza’s methods can not be applied to the noncompact case. In this paper, we will prove some no steady and expanding breather theorems in the noncompact case.

We remark that our study looks more interesting in light of the examples of general self-similar mean curvature flows. Recall that a self-similar
solution is a mean curvature flow
x:M^{n}\times I\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
satisfying

\displaystyle x(M,t)=\alpha(t)F(t)\circ x(M)\quad\text{ for all }\quad t\in I,

where
\alpha(t)
is a positive function with
\alpha(0)=1

and
F(t)
is a one-parameter family of isometries in
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with
F(0)=\operatorname{id}
. Note that except for the solitonic solutions, there are other self-similar solutions such as rotators and those of mixed types (they rotate while at the same time shrink/expand). The self-similar solutions to the curve shortening flow in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are classified by Halldorsson
[
11
]
. These examples show that one can not hope the no breather theorem to hold without any assumption; we shall include them as counterexamples in the remarks after our main theorems.

As our first main result, we state the no shrinking breather theorem, since, among all of our results, its conclusion is the most strong.
In the statements of Theorem
1.2
—
1.5
, the time instances
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
correspond to the ones in Definition
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
x:M^{n}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, be a complete immersed shrinking breather of the mean curvature flow. Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be the constant in (
1.1
). Furthermore, assume

(1.2)

\int_{M}e^{-\gamma|x(\cdot,t_{1})|^{2}}d\mu_{t_{1}}<\infty,

where
\gamma
is an arbitrary constant satisfying
\gamma<\tfrac{1}{4(t_{2}-t_{1})}(1-\alpha^{2})
and
\mu_{t}
, where
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, is the Riemannian measure induced by the immersion
x(\cdot,t):M^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
. Then
x
must be a self-shrinker.

Remark 1.3
.

(1)

There exist self-similar solutions to the curve shortening flow which rotate and shrink at the same time (see
[
11
, Figures 12—19]
). None of these examples satisfies (
1.2
), since they all wind infinitely many times near a circle with finite radius. This shows that the condition (
1.2
) can not be removed.

(2)

Theorem
1.2
can also be proved for the
Brakke flow breathers
. The details are left to the readers; note that the condition (
1.2
) is necessary. A Brakke flow breather should be defined as follows. Let
\mu_{t}
, where
t\in I
, be an
n
-dimensional Brakke flow in
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
. Then,
\mu_{t}
is a Brakke flow breather, if there exists
t_{1}<t_{2}
,
\alpha>0
, and isometry
F:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
as in Definition
1.1
, such that

\mu_{t_{2}}=\alpha\cdot F_{\#}\mu_{t_{1}},

where
F_{\#}\mu
stands for the push-forward of
\mu
by
F
.

(3)

If there is no translation part in the isometry
F
in (
1.1
), that is, if
F
can be written as
F(x)=\mathcal{R}x
for some
\mathcal{R}\in O(n+m)
, then Theorem
1.2
also holds if the constant
\gamma=\tfrac{1}{4(t_{2}-t_{1})}(1-\alpha^{2})
; one may check this fact with the proof of Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
.

Next, we state the no steady and no expanding breather theorems. The examples in
[
11
]
indicate that (
1.2
) is not sufficient for a no steady or expanding breather theorem. The examples in
[
11
, Figures 8—9]
are curve shortening flows evolving by rotation, and the examples in
[
11
, Figures 10—11]
evolve by rotation along with expansion. The reader can easily check that all these examples satisfy (
1.2
). Hence, in the noncompact case, the no steady or expanding breather theorems are not expected to hold unless under strong conditions.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
x_{0}:M^{n}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a complete, noncompact, and weakly convex expanding breather of the mean curvature flow with bounded second fundamental form. Then
x_{0}
is a self-expander.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
x_{0}:M^{n}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a complete, noncompact, and weakly convex steady breather of the mean curvature flow with bounded second fundamental form. Moreover, assume
either
one of the following is true.

(1)

There exists a point
p_{0}\in M
such that
\displaystyle\max_{M}H(\cdot,t_{1})
is attained at
p_{0}
.

(2)

There exists a point
p_{0}\in M
such that

(1.3)

\text{either }\quad\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\,x(\phi^{-j}(p_{0}),t_{1})\,\right|<\infty\quad\text{ or }\quad\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\,x(\phi^{j}(p_{0}),t_{1})\,\right|<\infty.

Then
x_{0}
must be a translator.

Remark 1.6
.

The examples in
[
11
, Figures 10—11]
evolve by rotation along with expansion. They are not weakly convex. This shows that without the weakly convex condition, Theorem
1.4
does not hold.
The Altschuler’s yin-yang spiral in
[
2
]
is a rotator satisfying (
1.3
); to see this, one needs only to observe that this curve passes through the origin, and then check the definition of rotator in section 2; see (
2.4
). However, it is not weakly convex. This shows that without the weakly convex condition, Theorem
1.5
(2) does not hold.

An immediate application of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.4
is the following
corollary.

Corollary 1.7
.

There exists no complete self-similar solution to the mean curvature flow satisfying (
1.2
) which rotates and shrinks at the same time. Moreover, there exists no complete weakly convex self-similar solution to the mean curvature flow with bounded second fundamental form which rotates and expands at the same time.

Though Theorem
1.5
has a corollary similar to Corollary
1.7
, we have a stronger result based on a more direct proof.

Theorem 1.8
.

Assume that
x_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a properly immersed weakly mean convex rotator. Then
x_{0}
must be a minimal hypersurface. If we further assume
x_{0}
to be weakly convex, then it must be a hyperplane.

Finally, let us recall the statements of the different versions of no breather theorems for the Ricci flow. All of them, except for the diverse conditions, are formulated in almost the same way, namely, a breather, under certain conditions, must be the critical point of certain monotonicity formula, ergo a gradient Ricci soliton. In other words, under these conditions all breathers must be “trivial”. Therefore, it remains interesting to find examples of nontrivial breathers. Obviously, there are “semi-nontrivial” examples—the non-gradient solitons; see
[
14
]
for an example of complete non-gradient expanding soliton. The canonical form of a non-gradient Ricci soliton flows by self-diffeomorphism and hence satisfies the definition of the breather, but it is not a gradient soliton, that is, a critical point of a monotonicity formula (Perelman’s entropy or Hamilton’s Harnack). Nevertheless, the non-gradient solitons still move by self-diffeomorphism. Recently, Topping
[
21
]
has constructed a “truly” nontrivial expanding breather of the Ricci flow, that is, he has proved there exists an expanding breather of the Ricci flow which is not the Ricci soliton. As we have seen above, the same issue arises when studying the breathers of the mean curvature flow. It remains interesting to ask whether there exists non-self-similar mean curvature flow breather, namely, mean curvature flow solution with nontrivial periodicity. We conjecture that there at least exists a non-self-similar expanding breather of the mean curvature flow as in the case of the Ricci flow.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Huisken’s monotonicity formula

Huisken’s monotonicity formula was originally defined for compact mean curvature flows. Nevertheless, there are multiple ways to generalize it to the noncompact case. We shall include a version that is proper to our end. First of all, Let us define the backward heat kernel
\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\times(-\infty,t_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x,t):=(4\pi(t_{0}-t))^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{|x-x_{0}|^{2}}{4(t_{0}-t)}}.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
x:M^{n}\times I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be a smooth and complete immersed solution to the mean curvature flow. Given
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\times\mathbb{R}
, let
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,t_{0})\cap I
with
t_{1}<t_{2}
. If

\int_{M}\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x,0)d\mu_{0}<\infty,

then we have

\int_{M}\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x,t)d\mu_{t}\leq\int_{M}\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x,0)d\mu_{0}<\infty

for all
t>0
under the mean curvature flow.
Moreover we have the following Huisken’s monotonicity holds

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x(\cdot,t_{2}),t_{2})d\mu_{t_{2}}-\int_{M}\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x(\cdot,t_{1}),t_{1})d\mu_{t_{1}}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{M}\left|\vec{H}+\frac{(x(\cdot,t)-x_{0})^{\perp}}{2(t_{0}-t)}\right|^{2}\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x(\cdot,t),t)d\mu_{t}dt,

where
\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}
is the backward heat kernel defined in (
2.1
) and
\mu_{t}
stands for the Riemannian measure induced by the immersion
x(\cdot,t):M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
.

Proof.

In order to avoid the integration by parts which may depend on extra conditions,
we consider the following drifted mean curvature flow as in
[
5
]

(2.3)

\partial_{t}x=\vec{H}-\frac{(x-x_{0})^{\top}}{2(t_{0}-t)},

Notice that (
2.3
) differs from the original mean curvature flow only by the tangential tangential flow
generated by the vector fields
-\frac{(x-x_{0})^{\top}}{2(t_{0}-t)}
.
We denote by
\{\nu_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=n+1}^{n+m}
an orthonormal frame of the normal bundle.
Then we may compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=2\partial_{i}\left(\vec{H}-\frac{(x-x_{0})^{\top}}{2(t_{0}-t)}\right)\cdot\partial_{j}x

\displaystyle=-2\vec{H}\cdot\vec{h}_{ij}-\frac{1}{t_{0}-t}\partial_{i}\left((x-x_{0})-\sum\limits_{\alpha}\langle x-x_{0},\nu^{\alpha}\rangle\nu^{\alpha}\right)\partial_{j}x

\displaystyle=-2\vec{H}\cdot\vec{h}_{ij}-\frac{1}{t_{0}-t}g_{ij}-\frac{1}{t_{0}-t}\sum\limits_{\alpha}\langle x-x_{0},\nu^{\alpha}\rangle h^{\alpha}_{ij},

where
h^{\alpha}_{ij}=\nu_{\alpha}\cdot\partial_{i}\partial_{j}x
,
\vec{h}_{ij}=h^{\alpha}_{ij}\nu_{\alpha}
, and
\vec{H}=g^{ij}\vec{h}_{ij}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}d\mu_{t}=\left(-|\vec{H}|^{2}-\frac{n}{2(t_{0}-t)}-\frac{1}{2(t_{0}-t)}\langle x-x_{0},\vec{H}\rangle\right)d\mu_{t}.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x(\cdot,t),t)=\left(-\frac{|(x-x_{0})^{\perp}|^{2}}{4(t_{0}-t)^{2}}+\frac{n}{2(t_{0}-t)}-\frac{1}{2(t_{0}-t)}\langle x-x_{0},\vec{H}\rangle\right)\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x(\cdot,t),t).

Hence
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x(\cdot,t),t)d\mu_{t})=-\left|\vec{H}+\frac{(x(\cdot,t)-x_{0})^{\perp}}{2(t_{0}-t)}\right|^{2}\Phi_{x_{0},t_{0}}(x(\cdot,t),t)d\mu_{t}\leq 0.

Note that the flow (
2.3
) is only a reparametrization of the original one, which does not affect the computation of Huisken’s functional. The conclusion then follows from Tonelli’s theorem.
∎

2.2.
Self-similar solutions

We briefly introduce different kinds of self-similar solutions to the mean curvature flow. For a more detailed treatment, refer to
[
1
, Section 6.2]
.

Translators.
If
x_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
is an immersed submanifold satisfying

\vec{H}=e^{\perp},

where
\vec{H}
is the mean curvature vector and
e\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
is a fixed unit vector, then this immersion generates a mean curvature flow moving by translation. In other words,
x:M^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
defined by

x(p,t):=x_{0}(\phi(p,t))+et,

where
\phi
is the flow generated by
V:=-dx_{0}^{-1}(e^{\top})
, evolves by the mean curvature. Here and below, we use
dx_{0}:\Gamma(TM)\to C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
to denote the push-forward induced by the immersion
x_{0}
.

Self-shrinkers.
Let
x_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be an immersed submanifold satisfying

\vec{H}=-\frac{1}{2}x_{0}^{\perp},

then
x:M^{n}\times(-\infty,0)\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
defined by

x(p,t):=\sqrt{-t}x_{0}\left(\phi\left(p,\tfrac{1}{2}\log(-t)\right)\right),

where
\phi
is the flow generated by
V:=-dx_{0}^{-1}(x_{0}^{\top})
, is a solution to the mean curvature flow. It is well known that a self-shrinker is a critical point of Huisken’s monotonicity formula.

Self-expanders.
Self-expanders are similar to self-shrinkers, since their definitions differ only by a sign. Let
x_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be an immersed submanifold satisfying

\vec{H}=\frac{1}{2}x_{0}^{\perp},

then
x:M^{n}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
defined by

x(p,t):=\sqrt{t}x_{0}\left(\phi\left(p,\tfrac{1}{2}\log(t)\right)\right),

where
\phi
is the flow generated by
V:=-dx_{0}^{-1}(x_{0}^{\top})
, is a solution to the mean curvature flow.

Rotators.
Let
x_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be an immersed submanifold and
J\in\mathfrak{so}(n+m)
, satisfying

\vec{H}=(Jx_{0})^{\perp},

then
x:M^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
defined by

x(p,t):=e^{tJ}\cdot x_{0}(\phi(p,t)),

where
\phi
is the flow generated by

(2.4)

V:=-dx_{0}^{-1}\left((Jx_{0})^{\top}\right),

is a rotating self-similar solution to the mean curvature flow.

Mixed type.
There also exist self-similar solutions which evolve by more complex self-similarities, for instance, rotation together with shrinking/expansion. For these examples, refer to
[
11
]
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The idea of proving Theorem
1.2
is similar as that which was applied in
[
6
]
and
[
23
]
. We will first of all implement the method in
[
18
]
to construct an ancient solution using the shrinking breather, by splicing properly scaled and properly located copies of the breather head-to-tail. Then, this ancient solution admits a blow-down limit, which must be a critical point of Huisken’s monotonicity formula, and hence a self-shrinker. Finally, because of the construction of the ancient solution, the limit must be identical to the original breather up to a scale and an isometry. This is the idea of the proof.

After parabolic scaling and translating in time, we let
x:M^{n}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, where
t_{1}=0
and
t_{2}=1
, be the breather in the statement of Theorem
1.2
. Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be the constant therein. According to the definition of breather, we can find an isometry
F:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
and a self-diffeomorphism
\phi:M\to M
, such that

x(p,1)=\alpha F\circ x(\phi(p),0)\quad\text{ for all }\quad p\in M.

For the sake of convenience, let
y_{0}(p,\tau)=x(p,t)
with
\tau(t)=1-t
, and the backward time
\tau
shall be considered instead throughout this section. Since
F
is an isometry on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, there exist an orthogonal matrix
\mathcal{R}\in O(n+m)
and a constant vector
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
such that

(3.1)

y_{0}(p,1)=F^{-1}\left(\alpha^{-1}y_{0}\left(\phi^{-1}(p),0\right)\right)=\alpha^{-1}\mathcal{R}y_{0}\left(\phi^{-1}(p),0\right)+V\quad\text{ for all }\quad p\in M.

For each
j\geq 0
we define

(3.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{j}_{k=0}\alpha^{-2k},

\displaystyle y_{j}(p,\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{-j}\mathcal{R}^{j}y_{0}\left(\phi^{-j}(p),\alpha^{2j}(\tau-\tau_{j-1})\right)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V,\ \ \tau\in[\tau_{j-1},\tau_{j}].

Then we obviously have
\tau_{j}\nearrow\infty
. As mentioned at the beginning of the section, we define the spliced ancient solution

\displaystyle y(p,\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{0}(p,\tau),&\tau\in[0,1],\\
y_{j}(p,\tau),&\tau\in[\tau_{j-1},\tau_{j}].\end{array}\right.

Lemma 3.1
.

y(p,\tau)
defined in (
3
) is a smooth ancient solution to the backward mean curvature flow.

Proof.

Clearly we only need to check that
y(p,\tau)
is smooth at each
\tau_{j}
.
Applying (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we get

\displaystyle y_{j}(p,\tau_{j-1})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-j}\mathcal{R}^{j}y_{0}\left(\phi^{-j}(p),0\right)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-(j-1)}\mathcal{R}^{j-1}\left(y_{0}\left(\phi^{-(j-1)}(p),1\right)-V\right)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-(j-1)}\mathcal{R}^{j-1}y_{0}\left(\phi^{-(j-1)}(p),\alpha^{2(j-1)}(\tau_{j-1}-\tau_{j-2})\right)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-2}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V

\displaystyle=y_{j-1}(p,\tau_{j-1}).

By the definition of
y
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{+}}{\partial\tau}y(p,\tau_{j-1})=\frac{\partial_{+}}{\partial\tau}y_{j}(p,\tau_{j-1})=\alpha^{j}\mathcal{R}^{j}\frac{\partial_{+}}{\partial\tau}y_{0}\left(\phi^{-j}(p),0\right)=-\alpha^{j}\mathcal{R}^{j}\vec{H}_{0}\left(\phi^{-j}(p),0\right),

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{-}}{\partial\tau}y(p,\tau_{j-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{-}}{\partial\tau}y_{j-1}(p,\tau_{j-1})=\alpha^{j-1}\mathcal{R}^{j-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}y_{0}\left(\phi^{-(j-1)}(p),1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha^{j-1}\mathcal{R}^{j-1}\vec{H}_{0}\left(\phi^{-(j-1)}(p),1\right)=-\alpha^{j}\mathcal{R}^{j}\vec{H}_{0}(\phi^{-j}(p),0).

Hence, we have

\frac{\partial_{+}}{\partial\tau}y(p,\tau_{j-1})=\frac{\partial_{-}}{\partial\tau}y(p,\tau_{j-1}).

Similarly, it is straightforward to check

\frac{\partial^{k}_{+}}{\partial\tau^{k}}y(p,\tau_{j-1})=\frac{\partial^{k}_{-}}{\partial\tau^{k}}y(p,\tau_{j-1})\quad\text{ for all }\quad k\geq 2.

This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

One important ingredient of the proof is to show that the ancient solution
y
has bounded entropy. The first step is to show that this holds for the original breather
x
. For all
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we define the backward heat kernel
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
as

(3.6)

\displaystyle\tau_{0}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(\alpha^{-2}-1)^{-1},

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(4\pi(\tau+\tau_{0}+\varepsilon))^{-\frac{n}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4(\tau+\tau_{0}+\varepsilon)}\right).

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a positive constant
\bar{\varepsilon}
depending only on the constant
\gamma
in (
1.2
), such that for all
\varepsilon\in[0,\bar{\varepsilon})
, it holds that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(y(\cdot,\tau),\tau)d\mu_{\tau}\leq C\quad\text{ for all }\quad\tau\in[0,1],

where
y
is defined in (
3
),
\mu_{\tau}
is the Riemannian measure induced by
y(\cdot,\tau):M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, and
C
is a constant depending only on the quantity in (
1.2
).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.1
, we need only to show that (
3.7
) holds at
\tau=1
. Since
\gamma<\tfrac{1}{4}(1-\alpha^{2})
, by (
3.6
), we have

\frac{1}{4(1+\tau_{0}+\varepsilon)}=\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{4(1+\varepsilon(1-\alpha^{2}))}\geq\gamma,

if
\bar{\varepsilon}
is taken to be small enough. It then follows from (
1.2
) that (
3.7
) holds when
\tau=1
. The lemma then follows from Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

There is a constant
C
, such that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\Phi(y(\cdot,\tau),\tau)d\mu_{\tau}\leq C\quad\text{ for all }\quad\tau>0,

where
y
,
\mu_{\tau}
are as defined before, and

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,\tau):=\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{\frac{n}{2}}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4\tau}}.

Proof.

Let us fix an arbitrary
\tau>0
and assume
\tau\in[\tau_{j-1},\tau_{j}]
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
j>0
, for otherwise we already have the bound (
3.7
) for
\tau\in(0,1]
. Then
y(\cdot,\tau)=y_{j}(\cdot,\tau)
, where the latter is defined in (
3.2
). Since a time-independent reparametrization does not affect the mean curvature flow equation or the integral, we may instead consider

\displaystyle y_{j}(\phi^{j}(\cdot),\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{-j}\mathcal{R}^{j}y_{0}\left(\cdot,\alpha^{2j}(\tau-\tau_{j-1})\right)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\alpha^{-j}\left(\mathcal{R}^{j}y_{0}\left(\cdot,\tilde{\tau}\right)+v_{i}\right),

where we have defined

(3.10)

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2j}(\tau-\tau_{j-1})\in[0,1],

\displaystyle v_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\alpha^{j}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}.

It is easy to verify by straightforward computation that
|v_{j}|\leq c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}
independent of
j
. Hence, letting
\delta
be a small positive constant to be fixed, we have

|y_{j}(\phi^{j}(\cdot),\tau)|^{2}\geq\alpha^{-2j}\Big((1-\delta)|y_{0}\left(\cdot,\tilde{\tau}\right)|^{2}-C_{\delta}\Big),

where
C_{\delta}
is a constant depending on
c_{0}
and
\delta
, but independent of
j
.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\int_{M}\Phi(y(\cdot,\tau),\tau)d\mu_{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\int_{M}\frac{1}{(4\pi(\alpha^{-2j}\tilde{\tau}+\tau_{j-1}))^{\frac{n}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{-2j}\Big((1-\delta)|y_{0}\left(\cdot,\tilde{\tau}\right)|^{2}-C_{\delta}\Big)}{4(\alpha^{-2j}\tilde{\tau}+\tau_{j-1})}\right)\alpha^{-nj}d\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\int_{M}\frac{1}{(4\pi(\tilde{\tau}+(1-\alpha^{2j})\tau_{0}))^{\frac{n}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\delta)|y_{0}\left(\cdot,\tilde{\tau}\right)|^{2}}{4(\tilde{\tau}+(1-\alpha^{2j})\tau_{0})}\right)d\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\int_{M}\frac{1}{(4\pi(\tilde{\tau}+\tau_{0}+\varepsilon(\delta)))^{\frac{n}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{|y_{0}\left(\cdot,\tilde{\tau}\right)|^{2}}{4(\tilde{\tau}+\tau_{0}+\varepsilon(\delta))}\right)d\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\int_{M}\Phi_{\varepsilon(\delta)}(y(\cdot,\tilde{\tau}),\tilde{\tau})d\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}.

Note that in the above computation the constant
C_{\delta}
varies from line to line, and that
\varepsilon(\delta)
depends only on
\delta
and satisfies
\varepsilon(\delta)\searrow 0
as
\delta\searrow 0
. Therefore, taking
\delta
small enough such that
\varepsilon(\delta)<\bar{\varepsilon}
, the conclusion follows from (
3.7
).
∎

Let us scale the ancient solution
y:M\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with the factors
\left\{\tau_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
and obtain the sequence of mean curvature flows
\{\tilde{y}_{j}(p,\tau)\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
. More precisely, we define

\tilde{y}_{j}(p,\tau):=\tau_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}y(\phi^{j+1}(p),\tau_{j}\tau)\quad\text{ for }\quad\tau\in\left[1,\tfrac{\tau_{j+1}}{\tau_{j}}\right],

where
y
is defined in (
3
). Note that the reparametrization does not affect anything. Then we have

(3.11)

\tilde{y}_{j}(p,\tau)=\tau_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\alpha^{-(j+1)}\mathcal{R}^{j+1}y_{0}\left(p,\alpha^{2(j+1)}\tau_{j}(\tau-1)\right)+\tau_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V

for all
\tau\in\left[1,\frac{\tau_{j+1}}{\tau_{j}}\right]
. Here we remark that

\frac{\tau_{j+1}}{\tau_{j}}\searrow\alpha^{-2}.

By the definition (
3.2
) of
\tau_{j}
, we estimate

(3.12)

\displaystyle\left|\tau_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V\right|

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\alpha^{-k}|V|\leq c|V|,

where
c
is a constant independent of
j
. Since the scaling factors in (

) are all controlled, that is,

\alpha^{2(j+1)}\tau_{j}\rightarrow c_{0}:=(\alpha^{-2}-1)^{-1},

we have that
\tilde{y}_{j}
and
y_{0}
differ only by a rotation, a scaling with a controlled factor, and a transportation by a controlled vector. Next, since
O(n+m)
is a compact Lie group, after passing to a subsequence, we have

\mathcal{R}^{j+1}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}^{\infty}\in O(n+m),\quad\tau_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\alpha^{-k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V\rightarrow V^{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}.

If we write

\tilde{y}_{\infty}\big(p,\tau)=c_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{R}^{\infty}y_{0}(p,c_{0}(\tau-1)\big)+V^{\infty}\quad\text{ for all }\quad(p,\tau)\in M\times[1,\alpha^{2}],

then it is trivial to see that
\tilde{y}_{j}\to\tilde{y}_{\infty}
pointwise smoothly, that is,

\nabla^{m}\tilde{y}_{j}(p,\tau)\to\nabla^{m}\tilde{y}_{\infty}(p,\tau)\quad\text{ for all }\quad m\in\mathbb{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad(p,\tau)\in M\times[1,\alpha^{2}].

Now, it remains only to show that
\tilde{y}_{\infty}
is a critical point of Huisken’s monotonicity formula.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be as defined in (
3.9
). We shall use the notations
\tilde{\mu}_{j,\bar{\tau}}
to denote the Riemannian measure induced by

. For any

, we have

\displaystyle\int_{M}\Phi(\tilde{y}_{j}(\cdot,\bar{\tau}_{2}),\bar{\tau}_{2})d\tilde{\mu}_{j,\bar{\tau}_{2}}-\int_{M}\Phi(\tilde{y}_{j}(\cdot,\bar{\tau}_{1}),\bar{\tau}_{1})d\tilde{\mu}_{j,\bar{\tau}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\int_{M}\Phi(y(\cdot,\bar{\tau}_{2}\tau_{j}),\bar{\tau}_{2}\tau_{j})d\mu_{\bar{\tau}_{2}\tau_{j}}-\int_{M}\Phi(y(\cdot,\bar{\tau}_{1}\tau_{j}),\bar{\tau}_{1}\tau_{j})d\mu_{\bar{\tau}_{1}\tau_{j}}

\displaystyle\to\,

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{ as}\quad j\to\infty,

where we have used the boundedness (Lemma
3.3
) and the monotonicity (Theorem
2.1
) of
\int_{M}\Phi(y(\cdot,\tau),\tau)d\mu_{\tau}
. Since

\left|\vec{\tilde{H_{j}}}+\frac{\tilde{y}_{j}^{\perp}(\cdot,\tau)}{2\tau}\right|^{2}\Phi(\tilde{y}_{j}(\cdot,\tau),\tau)d\tilde{\mu}_{j,\tau}\to\left|\vec{\tilde{H}}_{\infty}+\frac{\tilde{y}_{\infty}^{\perp}(\cdot,\tau)}{2\tau}\right|^{2}\Phi(\tilde{y}_{\infty}(\cdot,\tau),\tau)d\tilde{\mu}_{\infty,\tau}

pointwise on

, Fatou’s lemma implies

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\tau}_{1}}^{\bar{\tau}_{2}}\int_{M}\left|\vec{\tilde{H}}_{\infty}+\frac{\tilde{y}_{\infty}^{\perp}(\cdot,\tau)}{2\tau}\right|^{2}\Phi(\tilde{y}_{\infty}(\cdot,\tau),\tau)d\tilde{\mu}_{\infty,\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\bar{\tau}_{1}}^{\bar{\tau}_{2}}\int_{M}\left|\vec{\tilde{H}}_{j}+\frac{\tilde{y}_{j}^{\perp}(\cdot,\tau)}{2\tau}\right|^{2}\Phi(\tilde{y}_{j}(\cdot,\tau),\tau)d\tilde{\mu}_{j,\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left(\int_{M}\Phi(\tilde{y}_{j}(\cdot,\bar{\tau}_{2}),\bar{\tau}_{2})d\tilde{\mu}_{j,\bar{\tau}_{2}}-\int_{M}\Phi(\tilde{y}_{j}(\cdot,\bar{\tau}_{1}),\bar{\tau}_{1})d\tilde{\mu}_{j,\bar{\tau}_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle 0.

Hence,
\tilde{y}_{\infty}
satisfies

\vec{\tilde{H}}_{\infty}+\frac{\tilde{y}_{\infty}^{\perp}(\cdot,\tau)}{2\tau}=0.

This finishes the proof of Theorem Theorem
1.2
.

Remark 3.4
.

The no shrinking breather theorem in the closed case is proved in
[
15
, Theorem 2.3]
by using the supremum of Huisken’s functional.
For the sake of completeness, we give their proof below.
For a closed mean curvature flow
x:M^{n}\times I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, let us define

H_{x_{0},T}\left(M_{t}\right)=\int_{M}\frac{1}{(4\pi T)^{-\frac{n}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x(\cdot,t)-x_{0}|^{2}}{4T}\right)d\mu_{t},\quad t\in I,

and

(3.13)

\lambda_{t_{0}}(t)=\sup\limits_{x_{0}}H_{x_{0},t_{0}-t}\left(M_{t}\right),\quad t\in(-\infty,t_{0})\cap I.

Since
M
is closed,
H_{x_{0},T}\left(M_{t}\right)\to 0
for as
x_{0}\to\infty
. Hence
\lambda_{t_{0}}(t)
can be achieved for any
t
. Moreover, Huisken’s monotonicity formula implies that
\lambda_{t_{0}}(t)
is finite and is monotonically non-increasing in
t
. If
x
is also a shrinking breather, letting
t_{1}<t_{2}
, and
\alpha
be as in the statement of Definition
1.1
, we may set
t_{0}=\frac{t_{2}-\alpha^{2}t_{1}}{1-\alpha^{2}}
. Then

t_{0}-t_{2}=\alpha^{2}(t_{0}-t_{1})
. Assume
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
is the point where
\lambda_{t_{0}}(t_{2})
is attained, that is,
\lambda_{t_{0}}(t_{2})=H_{x_{2},t_{0}-t_{2}}(M_{t_{2}})
, then

\displaystyle\lambda_{t_{0}}(t_{2})

\displaystyle=H_{x_{2},t_{0}-t_{2}}\left(M_{t_{2}}\right)\leq H_{x_{2},t_{0}-t_{1}}\left(M_{t_{1}}\right)=H_{x_{2},\alpha^{-2}(t_{0}-t_{2})}\left(\alpha^{-1}F\circ M_{t_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=H_{F^{-1}(\alpha x_{2}),t_{0}-t_{2}}\left(M_{t_{2}}\right)\leq\lambda_{t_{0}}(t_{2}),

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
is an isometry. It follows that
H_{x_{2},t_{0}-t_{1}}\left(M_{t_{1}}\right)=H_{x_{2},t_{0}-t_{2}}\left(M_{t_{2}}\right)
, and hence
x
is a self-shinker.

Proof of Theorem

After parabolic scaling and translating in time, we let

1.4
, where

and

, an isometry

As before, we can find an orthogonal matrix
\mathcal{R}\in O(n+1)
and a constant vector

(4.1)

x_{0}(p,1)=\alpha F\big(x_{0}(\phi(p),0)\big)=\alpha\mathcal{R}x_{0}(\phi(p),0)+V\quad\text{ for all }\quad p\in M.

1.2
and define an immortal solution. Precisely, for each

, let

(4.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle t_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{j}_{k=0}\alpha^{2k},

\displaystyle x_{j}(p,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{j}\mathcal{R}^{j}x_{0}\big(\phi^{j}(p),\alpha^{-2j}(t-t_{j-1})\big)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha^{k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V,\ \ t\in[t_{j-1},t_{j}].

Obviously, we have

. By the same argument as in the proof of Lemma

, we also have that the following spliced immortal solution is smooth.

\displaystyle x(p,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{0}(p,t),&t\in[0,1],\\
x_{j}(p,t),&t\in[t_{j-1},t_{j}].\end{array}\right.

As in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we consider the rescaled sequence
\{\tilde{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
defined as

(4.6)

\tilde{x}_{j}(p,t):=t_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}x\big(\phi^{-(j+1)}(p),t_{j}t\big)\quad\text{ for }\quad t\in\left[1,\tfrac{t_{j+1}}{t_{j}}\right].

Then

(4.7)

\tilde{x}_{j}(p,t)=t_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\alpha^{j+1}\mathcal{R}^{j+1}x_{0}\big(p,\alpha^{-2(j+1)}t_{j}(t-1)\big)+t_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\alpha^{k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V.

By (

\displaystyle\left|t_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\alpha^{k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V\right|

\displaystyle\leq t_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\alpha^{k}|V|\leq c|V|,

where

is a constant independent of

, and the scaling factor in

is controlled, that is

(4.9)

\displaystyle\alpha^{-2(j+1)}t_{j}\to c_{0}:=(\alpha^{2}-1)^{-1}.

From the computation above, we see that

and

differ only by a rotation, and a scaling with a controlled factor, and a transportation by a controlled vector. Hence, all the conditions of
[
1

are satisfied (it is obvious that this theorem holds true for noncompact mean curvature flows with higher codimension also, the reader can check this point easily). Note that the local area bound and the properness of immersion are both provided by the convexity assumption. Hence, we may obtain a mean curvature flow
\tilde{x}_{\infty}:M_{\infty}\times(1,\alpha^{2}]\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that a subsequence of
\{\tilde{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
converges to

on compact sets of

(see
[
1

). By the compactness of

, and by (

), me may find
\mathcal{R}^{\infty}
and

, such that, after passing to a further subsequence, we have

\mathcal{R}^{j+1}\to\mathcal{R}^{\infty}\quad\text{ and }\quad t_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\alpha^{k}\mathcal{R}^{k}V\to V_{\infty}.

Hence we have

M_{\infty}=M^{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{x}_{\infty}(p,t)=c_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{R}_{\infty}x_{0}(p,c_{0}(t-1))+V_{\infty}.

It remains to show that
\tilde{x}_{\infty}
is a self-expander.

In the argument below, if the notation is with tilde, then we are referring to the scaled mean curvature flow
\tilde{x}_{j}
, otherwise we are referring to the immortal solution
x
. Since

x_{0}
has bounded mean curvature
C
, we have

\sup\limits_{p\in M}|H|(p,t)\leq\alpha^{-(j+1)}C\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}\quad\text{ for all }\quad t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}].

It follows that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{p\in M}|H|(p,t)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}\quad\text{ for all }\quad t>0.

Let us fix a point
p_{0}\in M^{n}
. Then, for all
j\geq 1
, we have

\big|\,x(p_{0},t)-x(p_{0},1)\,\big|\leq\int_{1}^{t_{j+1}}|H(p_{0},t)|dt\leq\int_{1}^{t_{j+1}}Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq Ct_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{ for all }\quad t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}].

Hence, by (

\displaystyle\big|\,\tilde{x}_{j}(\phi^{j+1}(p_{0}),t)\,\big|\leq C\quad\text{ for all }\quad t\in\left[1,\tfrac{t_{j+1}}{t_{j}}\right],

where

is a constant independent of

. It then follows from the definition of convergence on compact sets of

that, after passing to a further subsequence, we can find a point

\displaystyle\nabla^{m}\tilde{x}_{j}(\phi^{j+1}(p_{0}),t)\to\nabla^{m}\tilde{x}_{\infty}(p_{\infty},t)\quad\text{for all}\quad m\in\mathbb{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad t\in(1,\alpha^{2}].

Recall Hamilton’s matrix Harnack estimate for weak convex mean curvature flow,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\frac{H}{2t}+2V_{i}\nabla_{i}H+h_{ij}V_{i}V_{j}\geq 0,

where

is any vector field on

m\in\mathbb{N}
, and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{H}_{\infty}(p_{\infty},0)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{H}_{j}(\phi^{-j}(p_{0}),0)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}H(p_{0},-j)=0,

where the last equality is because of Hamilton’s Harnack estimate
\partial_{t}H(p_{0},t)\geq 0
for the ancient solution defined in (
5.3
) and the fact that
H\geq 0
.
We may without loss of generality assume that
\tilde{x}_{\infty}
is strictly convex as above. Taking
V_{j}=-(\tilde{h}_{\infty})^{-1}_{ij}\nabla_{j}\tilde{H}_{\infty}
in (
5.4
), we get
\nabla\tilde{H}_{\infty}(p_{\infty},0)=0
.
The rest of the proof is not different from that of Theorem
1.4
; the computation is the same as
[
12
]
. This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Concerning the case where

(5.8)

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\,x(\phi^{j}(p_{0}),0)\,\right|<\infty

holds, we may define

\displaystyle x_{j}(p,t)=F^{j}\circ x_{0}\big(\phi^{j}(p),t-j\big)\quad\text{ for }\quad t\in[j,j+1],

and consider the eternal solution

\displaystyle x(p,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{-j}(p,t)&t\in[-j,-j+1],\\
x_{j}(p,t)&t\in[j,j+1],\end{array}\right.

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, where
x_{-j}
is defined in (
5.2
) and (
5.7
). Obviously, on this eternal solution Hamilton’s Harnack is still true. Then, writing

x_{j}(p,t)=\mathcal{R}^{j}x_{0}(\phi^{j}(p),t-j)+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{R}^{k}V\quad\text{ for }\quad t\in[j,j+1],

we may consider the sequence of mean curvature flows
\tilde{x}_{j}:M^{n}\times[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, defined by
\tilde{x}_{j}(p,t)=x(\phi^{-j}(p),t+j)-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{R}^{k}V
, that is,

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{j}(p,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{R}^{j}x(p,t)&t\in[0,1],\\
\mathcal{R}^{j-1}x(\phi^{-1}(p),t+1)-\mathcal{R}^{j-1}V&t\in[-1,0].\end{array}\right.

We may then apply the same argument to the sequence
\{\tilde{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
as before and use the condition (
5.8
) in place of (
5.5
). The conclusion follows similarly.

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.8

We show that there exists no nontrivial weakly mean convex rotator. Arguing by contradiction, we assume that
x_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a weakly mean convex rotator, satisfying

(6.1)

\displaystyle H=-\langle Jx_{0},n\rangle

for some
J\in\mathfrak{so}(n+1)
. Obviously,
x_{0}
cannot be closed, since otherwise it generates a closed immortal solution
x:M^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, which does not exist.

Assume that
x_{0}
is noncompact. Since it is properly embedded, we have
x_{0}(p)\to\infty
whenever
p\to\infty
. Hence, we can find a point
p_{0}\in M
, such that
|x_{0}|
attains its minimum at
p_{0}
. If
x_{0}(p_{0})
is the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, then by (
6.1
) we immediately have that
H(p_{0})=0
. If
x_{0}(p_{0})
is not the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, then at
p_{0}
we compute

2x_{0}^{\top}=2Dx_{0}\cdot x_{0}=D|x_{0}|^{2}=0.

Hence
n(p_{0})=cx_{0}(p_{0})
for some constant
c
. Since
J=-J^{T}
, by (
6.1
) we have

H(p_{0})=-\langle Jx_{0}(p_{0}),n(p_{0})\rangle=-c\langle Jx_{0}(p_{0}),x_{0}(p_{0})\rangle=0.

The strong maximum principle immediately implies that
H\equiv 0
on the mean curvature flow
x
generated by
x_{0}
. If
x_{0}
is weakly convex, we also have that
x_{0}
is totally geodesic, and hence it is a hyperplane.

Remark 6.1
.

We remark that any minimal surface can be view as a trivial rotator if we
take
J=0
in (
6.1
).
